Screening for irritable bowel syndrome and its associated factors among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus attending outpatient clinics in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Sarah Mohamed Hussein, Sara Okasha, Noura Omair, Fatima Bakarshoom, Lamiaa Marghalani, Amina Rajab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a major public health problem in Saudi Arabia. Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is considered as the most often diagnosed gastrointestinal disease. So, this study aims to screen the prevalence of IBS and its associated factors among patients with T2DM.Materials and Methods:This cross-sectional study was performed in the outpatient clinics of a private hospital in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, among 245 patients with T2DM enrolled in the study by a convenient sampling technique. Data was collected by a structured interview questionnaire. This questionnaire was composed of personal data, diabetes history, IBS screening questions, family history, and other clinical symptoms.Results:Among 245 patients with T2DM, 37.14% were classified as probably having IBS, while 1.22% were likely to have IBS. The mean IBS score was 11.17 ± 6.48. Female gender, younger age, unmarried status, family history of IBS or celiac diseases, rectal bleeding, and nocturnal gastrointestinal symptoms that wake up the patient were significantly associated with IBS likelihood (P < 0.05). Logistic regression identified younger age (OR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.95–1.00), family history of celiac disease (OR = 2.83, 95% CI: 1.35–5.97), rectal bleeding (OR = 4.15, 95% CI: 1.13–15.19), and nocturnal symptoms (OR = 3.56, 95% CI: 1.69–7.47) as independent predictors of IBS.Conclusion:Probability of having IBS is common among patients with T2DM. Younger age, family history of celiac disease, rectal bleeding, and nocturnal symptoms were significant independent predictors of IBS likelihood. These findings emphasize the need for targeted gastrointestinal assessment in diabetic patients to ensure timely diagnosis and appropriate management.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a major and rapidly growing global health challenge. Its burden is closely linked to population ageing, urbanization, and lifestyle changes, and it is a leading driver of other chronic diseases and healthcare costs worldwide. Recent estimates from the 11th edition of the international diabetes federation (IDF) Diabetes Atlas indicate that diabetes affects a substantial proportion of the adult population and will continue to expand over the coming decades.[1] According to the IDF Diabetes Atlas 11th edition, approximately 589 million adults aged 20–79 years were living with diabetes in 2024, representing about 11.1% of the global population in this age group.[1] Based on demographic projections alone, the number of adults with diabetes is expected to rise by about 45%, reaching around 853 million by 2050.[1] These alarming figures highlight the urgent need to better understand diabetes and its complications, including its association with gastrointestinal (GI) disorders such as irritable bowel syndrome (IBS).

With regards to Saudi Arabia, recent data confirm that diabetes represents a major and growing public health and economic challenge. In a five-year longitudinal cohort of 638 adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) from 15 centers across the Kingdom, almost half of the patients were overweight or obese at diagnosis and the projected lifelong direct medical cost per patient ranged from 201,377 to 437,371 Saudi Riyal, underscoring the substantial clinical and economic burden of T2DM in Saudi Arabia.[2] Furthermore, a nationwide systematic review and meta-analysis of 19 population-based studies including 258,283 individuals estimated an overall pooled prevalence of T2DM of 16.4% (95% CI 11.6–17.5) in the Saudi general population between 2000 and 2020, with higher prevalence in older age groups and marked regional variation.[3]

Irritable bowel syndrome is a common chronic disorder of gut–brain interaction characterized by recurrent abdominal pain associated with altered bowel habits. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of population-based studies that applied contemporary Rome criteria reported pooled global IBS prevalences of 9.2% with Rome III and 3.8% with Rome IV, indicating that roughly one in eleven to one in twenty-six adults worldwide meet diagnostic criteria for IBS; women had about 1.5-fold higher odds of having IBS than men, and most patients were younger than 50 years.[4] In high-income countries such as the United States, IBS also represents a substantial public health problem. A large nationwide cross-sectional survey of more than 88,000 adults conducted in 2020 showed that 6.1% of respondents met Rome IV criteria for IBS, with mixed, constipation-predominant, and diarrhea-predominant subtypes occurring in similar proportions; most affected individuals reported seeking medical care for their symptoms and described notable impacts on daily functioning.[5]

Regarding Saudi Arabia, recent evidence indicates that IBS is highly prevalent and associated with important effects on quality of life. A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis of 38 Saudi studies including more than 26,000 participants estimated a pooled IBS prevalence of 20.7% in the Saudi population, with similar rates among the general population (21%), students (22%), and health-care workers (18.3%).[6] In a population-based survey of adults in Qatif, Eastern Saudi Arabia, 17.6% of respondents met diagnostic criteria for IBS, and participants with IBS reported significantly lower physical and overall health-related quality of life scores than those without.[7] At the wider regional level, a 2024 systematic review of 52 studies from seven Arab countries found that IBS prevalence in the Arab world ranged from about 7.6% to 54.8%, with an average prevalence of approximately 27% and consistently higher rates among women; some of the highest estimates were reported in studies from Saudi Arabia, Lebanon.[8] A recent multicenter Saudi study further confirmed that IBS has a marked negative effect on multiple domains of health-related quality of life across different regions of the Kingdom, particularly among patients with more frequent symptoms and higher health-care utilization.[9]

Gastrointestinal involvement is increasingly recognized as an important complication of diabetes. A cross-sectional study from northern Iran that compared 255 diabetic patients with 255 non-diabetic controls found that more than 90% of individuals with diabetes reported at least one GI symptom, with flatulence, retrosternal pain, belching, postprandial fullness and constipation being particularly common; GI symptoms were more frequent in women and were related to poorer glycemic control.[10] In a recent cross-sectional study from the Aseer region of Saudi Arabia, GI manifestations were likewise common among patients with T2DM and were associated with longer disease duration and poorer glycemic control, underlining the contribution of diabetes to gastrointestinal morbidity in this population.[11] Large prospective cohort studies further suggest that functional bowel disorders such as IBS may be part of this gastrointestinal burden. In a population-based UK Biobank cohort, Zhou et al.[12] reported that individuals with T2DM had about a 39% higher risk of incident IBS than those without T2DM, and that people with IBS had an 18% higher risk of subsequently developing T2DM, demonstrating a bidirectional association between the two conditions. Consistent with this, a recent large-scale UK Biobank analysis by Liu et al.[13] found extensive bidirectional associations between endocrine diseases and GI disorders, with IBS being one of several GI conditions linked to an increased risk of subsequent T2DM and vice versa. Mutually, these findings indicate that T2DM and IBS frequently co-occur and that their relationship may have important implications for metabolic and gastrointestinal health.

Despite the high prevalence of both T2DM and IBS in Saudi Arabia, most local studies have examined these conditions separately, focusing either on the epidemiology of diabetes or on IBS in the general population, students, or health-care workers. There is limited evidence regarding the likelihood of having IBS and determinants of IBS among patients with T2DM in Saudi Arabia. A better understanding of the prevalence, symptom profile, and associated factors of IBS in Saudi adults with T2DM can help to improve clinical recognition, guide targeted screening, and inform integrated management strategies for this high-risk group. Therefore, this study was conducted to screen the prevalence of IBS through identifying its likelihood among patients with T2DM and examining factors that can affect the likelihood of IBS.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted to screen the prevalence of IBS and its associated factors among patients with T2DM. Data was collected from January 2024 to June 2025.

Study setting and participants

This study was conducted among patients with T2DM, who attended outpatient clinics at Dr. Soliman Fakeeh Hospital in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia.

Sample size and sampling technique

Sample size: the sample size has been calculated by using the Raosoft sample size calculator based on the gastrointestinal symptoms among diabetic patients (20.1%),[14] confidence interval of 95%, and error of margin 5%. The estimated sample size was 245 patients with T2DM.

Sampling and procedure: A convenient sampling method was used to enroll the participants.

Data collection tool

Data was collected by a structured interview questionnaire in the English language. The researchers were trained to ask the patients in the same manner to avoid interviewer bias. Before starting the questionnaire, the participants must read the informed consent; the researchers explained it, and the participants must accept to participate in the study. The informed consent clarified the title and objectives of the study and confirmed anonymity, confidentiality, and voluntary participation.

Screening of irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)

Screening of irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)

This questionnaire has been developed by the World Gastroenterology Organization (WGO) and is used to determine whether a patient has IBS. Patients are asked to recall any discomfort or pain in their abdominal region in the previous 3 months. Then ask them to describe their discomfort or pain. The description is based on their bowel movement manifestations and characteristics. The score was calculated by the sum of the scores of all questions. If the total score ranged from 25 to 30, the patient is likely to be suffering from IBS. However, if the score was 15–24, the patient may suffer from IBS, but other conditions are also possible. Finally, if the score was <15, the patient’s symptoms may not be due to IBS, and other conditions should be considered.

The patient was then asked additional questions about additional representations such as the presence of specific diseases in the family, antibiotic use, unintended weight loss, blood in the stool, or symptoms that cause them to wake up at night. The answers to questions 1–16 should be added to determine the most likely diagnosis, while questions 17–21 should be discussed if yes.[15]

To establish content and face validity, this questionnaire was sent to two experts in gastroenterology to assess the questions, and 10 patients with T2DM were examined to ensure clarity of questions asked by the researchers. The Cronbach’s Alpha was examined to ensure reliability, and it was 0.88, indicating a high level of internal consistency and demonstrating good internal consistency.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Before data collection, this study obtained the ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 407/IRB/2023). The informed consent was explained to the participants before participating in the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation in the study.

Data management

Data was entered and statistically analyzed by using the Package for Social Science (SPSS) software program for Windows version 23.00 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Qualitative variables age, gender, residency, diabetes history questions and IBS questions, and likelihood of having IBS were presented as frequencies and percentages. Age and IBS score were expressed as mean and standard deviation, median and interquartile range, and range. To identify factors affecting likelihood of having IBS, a Chi-square test was performed except for age as a Mann Whitney U test was conducted. Logistic regression was performed to identify predictors of the likelihood of having IBS. Statistical significance will be set at P < 0.05.

Discussion

This study aims to screen the prevalence of IBS by identifying the likelihood of having IBS and examining its associated factors among patients with T2DM. It is a cross-sectional study performed in the outpatient clinics of a private hospital in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, among 245 patients with T2DM. The present study found that 37.14% of type 2 diabetic patients attending the outpatient clinics fell under the category of “probably having IBS” as per the WGO questionnaire. This is significantly higher compared to the prevalence rates of IBS among the general population, which are usually in the range of 4.6-9.0%, depending on the diagnostic criteria utilized. Palsson et al. (2020)[16] demonstrated that when Rome IV criteria (4.6%) were utilized instead of Rome III (9.0%), prevalence was about half as high, largely because of more stringent pain frequency criteria in Rome IV. In another study, the prevalence rates for IBS showed wide variation depending on the diagnostic methodologies adopted, indicating that diagnostic tools strongly influence prevalence assessments.[4] According to Almasary et al. (2023),[6] the overall prevalence of IBS in Saudi Arabia was 20.7%, but varied by population group: 21% in the general population, 22% among students, and 18.3% among healthcare workers. In addition, Makkawy et al. (2023)[17] documented even wider prevalence ranges of 7.9–49.3%, with significant heterogeneity across the studies.

The increased risk of IBS noted within the study correlates with evidence emanating from population studies that show that adults with T2DM have a greater incidence of gastrointestinal symptoms than people without diabetes. In the largest epidemiologic study, Bytzer et al.[18] investigated the influence of diabetes on symptomatology in 8,657 participants, selected from a random sample of 15,000 people, and showed that all 16 gastrointestinal symptoms, as well as five symptom subgroups, occurred significantly more often by 94.8% of patients with T2DM, after adjusting for age and gender. This increase showed a graded relationship with the severity of glycemic control, without any link to the duration or type of therapy used.

Such observations are consistent with findings in subsequent studies, in which gastrointestinal symptoms were significantly more prevalent in diabetic individuals compared with controls. Moreover, another study reported that the prevalence rate of upper gastrointestinal symptoms was 50% among diabetic cases compared with 38% among controls.[19] Whereas Kim (2010) observed that gastrointestinal symptoms was 72% among diabetic cases compared with 62% among nondiabetic controls, in which upper gastrointestinal symptoms were most significantly different (43% vs. 31%).[20] Even more recent observations show similar increased symptom prevalence in diabetic cases, with a difference in reports ranging from 10% to 20% above controls. This body of data supports the idea that functional gastrointestinal symptoms are a common manifestation in diabetic illness and help explain why there was such a high prevalence of IBS identified in our study. Such consistency may indicate that diabetes mellitus predisposes the population to functional gastrointestinal disorder. The following pathophysiological mechanisms contribute to explaining the high probability of IBS observed in the current study patients with T2DM: various gut microbiota dysbiosis, autonomic neuropathy, and inflammatory processes are implicated in gastrointestinal dysfunction in T2DM. According to Du et al.[21] (2022), as many as 75% of T2DM patients with autonomic neuropathy of the gastrointestinal tract exacerbate gut microbiota dysbiosis. In turn, Kant et al.(2023)[22] explained the main features of dysbiosis, including disturbance in the balance of Firmicutes versus Bacteroidetes and a decrease in Akkermansia muciniphila, which provoke several pathological mechanisms: increased gut permeability, bacterial translocation, lipopolysaccharide production, and the activation of pro-inflammatory cytokines.

The Tao et al. (2025)[23] study emphasized that the “microbiota-enteric nervous system axis” plays a central role in the disruption of sensorimotor control and underlined how these mechanisms commonly impair gastrointestinal function in T2DM patients. These described pathophysiological pathways give biological plausibility to the observed association between diabetes and increased symptoms of IBS in our study population.

With regards to factors affecting the likelihood of having IBS among patients with T2DM, the present study revealed that female participants were significantly more likely to be categorized as probably having IBS compared with males, 65.93% versus 34.07%, respectively (P = 0.023). This finding is in concordance with the literature on gender differences reported in IBS. According to Lovell and Ford (2012), the odds ratio for IBS in women compared with men was 1.67.[24] In T2DM patients, Dickman et al. (2014)[25] found a higher prevalence of gastrointestinal symptoms such as nausea, early satiety, and loss of appetite among females, especially obese women with longer disease duration.

In addition, a study by Choghakhori et al. (2017)[26] demonstrated that women have more severe symptoms, increased abdominal distension, rumbling, and flatulence, along with higher levels of inflammatory markers and lower quality of life scores. However, in our multivariate analysis, gender is not a significant determinant of the likelihood of having IBS, indicating that other confounding variables in the model largely explain the gender association. Furthermore, the reviewed sources do not consider the full degree of psychological and lifestyle confounder adjustments required to attain conclusive evidence for the persistence of the gender association after complete statistical control.

Regarding age, the present study showed that younger age was significantly associated with higher IBS likelihood, with the mean age being significantly lower for patients probably having IBS, 52.70 ± 13.86 years, compared to those likely not having IBS, 57.55 ± 12.17 years (P = 0.009). Logistic regression confirmed age as a significant independent predictor, OR = 0.97, P = 0.028, indicating that with every increase of one year in age, the odds of having IBS decreased by 3%.

Several mechanisms might explain the lack of a consistent age-related pattern in the prevalence of IBS in patients with T2DM because age has a greater effect on symptom perception and reporting than on true disease occurrence. Also, psychological distress is considered an important confounder; Thakur et al. (2016)[27] have demonstrated that anxiety and depressive symptoms are more evident among younger adults with medical comorbidities, which may amplify the reporting of gastrointestinal symptoms, thereby increasing the apparent likelihood of IBS in younger patients.

Importantly, existing evidence differentiates between symptom severity and prevalence. Algera et al.[28] (2024) found that lower age is related to more severe symptoms and a poorer quality of life. Although, Minocha et al. (2006)[29] did not find younger age to significantly influence the prevalence of IBS compared to older age (9.9% vs. 7.6%). Moreover, in their coping styles and social functioning, older patients may be more resilient and therefore show milder symptoms. All these competing factors may prevent any clear age-related trends in IBS prevalence among T2DM populations from appearing.

Addressing the influence of family history of celiac disease on IBS, the current study demonstrated that a positive family history of celiac disease manifested as the most imperative risk factor for IBS, with an odds ratio of 2.83, 95% CI 1.35-5.97, and a P value of 0.006. This concurs with evidence of complex interactions between IBS and celiac disease, hence making it unlikely to be explained by a single underlying pathway.

According to Ford et al. (2009),[30] the prevalence of celiac disease in IBS patients is four times that of controls. Barbara et al. (2014)[31] indicated shared contributing factors to include genetic, microbiological, epithelial, and immunological elements. However, another study identified specific genetic susceptibility loci for IBS that had strong colocalization with mood disorders, thus indicating neurological links shared between these disorders.[32]

Quigley (2005) believed that observable overlap may indicate misdiagnosis, given that IBS and celiac disease share many clinical characteristics. The association between family history of celiac disease and risk of IBS appears to be multifactorial, including such possibilities as genetic predisposition, shared immune interaction, and diagnostic challenges rather than a cause-and-effect relationship. A high degree of suspicion should thus exist among clinicians for celiac disease in patients with symptoms of IBS who have a family history of this disorder.[33]

Concerning the alarming symptoms, the present study stated two alarm symptoms were considered as significant independent predictors of the likelihood of IBS: nocturnal symptoms, with an OR of 3.56, 95% CI: 1.69–7.47, P = 0.001, and loss of blood with stool, with an OR of 4.15, 95% CI: 1.13–15.19, P = 0.032. These findings are thus interpreted cautiously because classically, alarm symptoms suggest organic pathology rather than functional disorders such as IBS.

The literature provides important context for the clinical significance of alarm features in patients presenting with symptoms of IBS. Of patients screened positive for IBS with symptoms of alarm features, 10–28% have underlying organic disease; studies vary. Patel et al. (2015)[34] reported that 27.7% of alarm IBS patients have organic disease compared to 15.4% without any alarm feature. Asghar et al. (2022)[35] reported the overall prevalence of organic diseases, including IBD, colorectal cancer, and microscopic colitis, as 12%. Yang et al. (2022)[36] found that 10.41% of suspected IBS patients had organic diseases in their 2022 article. Cash et al. (2007)[37] found that 6.3% of suspected IBS patients had organic diseases in their 2008 article.

The association of alarm symptoms with the likelihood of IBS remains a cause of great clinical concern in our study. Those with these symptoms in our cohort may be considered for further diagnostic testing, including colonoscopy with random biopsies, testing for inflammatory markers, and celiac antibodies.[38] The finding of alarm symptoms in this diabetic population also draws attention to the need for an adequate clinical examination requisite in ruling out organic pathology before diagnosing functional gastrointestinal disorders as causes of symptoms.

Contrary to what might have been expected, our study did not find an association between the risk of IBS and some diabetes-related variables like the duration of diabetes diagnosis, HbA1c, the type of antidiabetic medication, or the presence of complications in patients with diabetes (P > 0.05 for all comparisons).

However, this lack of association with glycemic control in our study deviates from previous studies, which concluded that poor glycemic control, as determined by self-report and HbA1c, was independently associated with upper gastrointestinal symptoms.[1838] Several reasons can account for this discrepancy: our study assessed the risk of IBS rather than general gastrointestinal symptoms, utilized a different diagnostic tool, namely the WGO questionnaire versus symptom questionnaires, and analyzed a clinic-based sample rather than a population-based sample.

Regarding microvascular complications, several studies have established the association between gastrointestinal symptoms and diabetic complications. Bytzer et al. (2002)[18] reported that diabetic complications were independently associated with symptom complexity; in most symptom groups, peripheral neuropathy accounted for this association.[37] Similarly, a study by Ghadiri-Anari et al.[39] (2019) concluded that gastrointestinal symptoms were independently associated with diabetic complications; among such complications, retinopathy was one of them. Lack of similar associations in the current study could point toward the fact that IBS may be a unique entity because it falls under the larger spectrum of gastrointestinal dysfunction or might be due to sample characteristics and study design.

Further, a study by Du et al. (2018)[40] reported the association of symptoms with glycemic control was still “poorly defined” and required further study based on specific diagnostic criteria. Our findings support the assertion and point out the complexity of GI manifestations in diabetic patients.

Limitations

There are several limitations that should be considered in this study; being a cross-sectional character study, no causal relationship could be established between diabetes and IBS. Although the calculated sample size, having convenience sampling from a single private hospital in Jeddah, limited the generalizability of the results to the broader Saudi diabetic population. In addition, while the questionnaire can serve as a screening tool for IBS, there is a need to use a diagnostic tool to confirm the findings. Although internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and a pilot study was conducted, the use of a non-validated questionnaire may affect the external validity and comparability of the results with other studies. Finally, even though researchers were trained to administer questionnaires uniformly, interviewer-administered surveys can introduce reporting bias compared with self-administered questionnaires, as participants are likely to underreport symptoms of a socially sensitive nature or overreport symptoms perceived to be expected by the interviewer.

Conclusion and Recommendations

More than one-third (37.14%) of studied patients with type 2 diabetes were classified as probably having IBS, indicating a substantial burden of gastrointestinal symptoms. Female gender, younger age, unmarried status, positive family history of inflammatory bowel diseases or celiac diseases, rectal bleeding, and nocturnal symptoms were associated with higher IBS likelihood. Multivariable analysis identified younger age, family history of celiac disease, rectal bleeding, and nocturnal symptoms as independent predictors.

These findings highlight the need for careful routine screening for gastrointestinal symptoms, particularly in younger patients. A detailed family history, especially of celiac disease, is essential when evaluating diabetic patients with gastrointestinal complaints. The presence of alarm features, including rectal bleeding and nocturnal symptoms, necessitates immediate clinical evaluation to rule out organic gastrointestinal disorders prior to confirming an IBS diagnosis. Integrating gastrointestinal symptom assessment into routine diabetes care and adopting a multidisciplinary approach may facilitate early detection, appropriate referral, and improved patient management. In addition, there is a dire need for large-scale, multicenter studies based on standardized validation for the determination of actual estimates of the prevalence of IBS in diabetic patients of various regions of Saudi Arabia. Such studies that incorporate nondiabetic controls matched for age, gender, and sociodemographic variables would enable comparison of the rates of IBS between diabetic and nondiabetic populations.
