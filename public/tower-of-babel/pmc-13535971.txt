Comparison of depressive symptoms and anxious distress between major depressive disorder and bipolar disorder using the CUDOS‐A
Authors: Kazufumi Oishi, Yasuyuki Matsumoto, Yumi Aoki, Yoshikazu Takaesu, Masami Murao, Taku Maruki, Takashi Tsuboi, Masahiro Nakano, Koichiro Watanabe, Hitoshi Sakurai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimAnxious distress is a clinically important specifier associated with adverse outcomes in mood disorders. This study aimed to compare the prevalence of anxious distress and overall depressive symptoms between patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) and bipolar disorder (BD) using validated self‐report measures.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study included 202 psychiatric outpatients (133 MDD and 69 BD) recruited from seven outpatient clinics in Japan. Participants completed the Clinically Useful Depression Outcome Scale supplemented with the DSM‐5 anxious distress specifier (CUDOS‐A) and the Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology, Self Report (QIDS‐SR). Anxious distress was operationally defined using the CUDOS‐A items corresponding to the DSM‐5 anxious distress specifier. Group differences in prevalence and mean scores were examined using chi‐square tests and independent samples t‐tests, respectively.ResultsThe prevalence of anxious distress was 42.9% in patients with MDD and 43.4% in those with BD, with no significant difference between groups (p = 0.95). No significant differences were observed in the mean CUDOS‐A total scores (28.0 ± 19.9 vs. 27.0 ± 17.7, p = 0.57) or the QIDS‐SR total scores (13.6 ± 7.6 vs. 12.1 ± 7.4, p = 0.18). Furthermore, all individual items of the CUDOS‐A showed no significant differences between the two groups.ConclusionsBoth anxious distress and overall depressive symptom severity assessed by self‐report measures appear to be comparable between MDD and BD. Although the diagnostic utility of anxious distress for differentiating between the two disorders has not been established, its high prevalence in both groups underscores its clinical significance and supports the need for routine assessment in mood disorders.Keywords: anxious distress, bipolar disorder, CUDOS‐A, depression, QIDS‐SR

INTRODUCTION

Anxious distress is a specifier introduced in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM‐5), for major depressive episodes. Previous studies have shown that 50%–75% of patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) meet the criteria for this specifier, rendering it one of the most frequently observed DSM‐5 specifiers. 1 The presence of anxious distress is associated with a higher risk of suicide, prolonged illness duration, and inferior treatment outcomes. 2 , 3 Accordingly, accurately identifying anxious distress and its severity holds significant clinical value for both treatment planning and monitoring of therapeutic response.

Although anxiety symptoms in mood disorders have often been conflated with comorbid anxiety disorders, anxious distress represents a distinct clinical construct. 2 While this distinction is increasingly recognized, whether the prevalence and symptom profile of anxious distress differ between major depressive disorder (MDD) and bipolar disorder (BD) remains unclear. Previous literature has yielded inconsistent findings: some studies report a higher prevalence in BD, 4 others find no significant difference between the two conditions, 5 and still others suggest a stronger association with MDD. 6

The Clinically Useful Depression Outcome Scale supplemented with items for the DSM‐5 anxious distress specifier (CUDOS‐A) is a self‐report instrument developed to assess not only core depressive symptoms but also additional features such as anxious distress, social function, and quality of life (QOL). 7 As a self‐report instrument, it offers the advantages of easy administration and comprehensive assessment of patients' subjective experiences, while facilitating the efficient collection of patient‐reported outcomes in both clinical and research settings. Despite previous studies evaluating anxious distress in patients with MDD and BD using various assessment instruments, to our knowledge, no study has directly compared the two disorders using the CUDOS‐A. Therefore, whether the CUDOS‐A can identify differences in depressive symptoms and anxious distress between MDD and BD remains unclear.

The present study aims to compare the total and item‐level scores on the CUDOS‐A between patients with MDD and BD, and to examine the proportion of individuals meeting the criteria for anxious distress in each group. Through these analyses, we aim to explore the potential utility of the CUDOS‐A for differentiating between MDD and BD.

METHOD

Design and setting

This cross‐sectional study was conducted between January and December 2021 at seven psychiatric outpatient clinics in Tokyo, Japan. The methodological details have been described previously. 8 The study protocol was approved by the School of Medicine Research Ethics Committee, Kyorin University (Approval number: 786–01). All participants received a comprehensive explanation of the study procedures and provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Participants

Eligible participants were outpatients aged between 20 and 65 years who met the DSM‐5 diagnostic criteria for either MDD or BD. 9 Exclusion criteria included the presence of manic or psychotic symptoms, substance use disorders including alcohol and illicit drugs, a history of dementia, severe physical illness, and active suicidal ideation.

Rating scale

Participants completed the CUDOS‐A, a self‐administered questionnaire designed to evaluate depressive symptoms, anxious symptoms, social function, and QOL. The scale consists of 15 items (Items 1–15) assessing core depressive symptoms, 5 items (Items 16–20) corresponding to the DSM‐5 anxious distress specifier, 1 item assessing social function (Item 21), and 1 item assessing QOL (Item 22). Each item is rated on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (almost every day), with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. Anxious distress was defined as a score of ≥2 or higher on at least two of CUDOS‐A Items 16–20, which correspond to the DSM‐5 anxious distress specifier. Depressive symptom severity was concurrently assessed using the Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology, Self‐Report (QIDS‐SR), a widely validated self‐report scale for evaluating depressive symptom severity based on the DSM criteria.

Data analysis

Differences in demographic and clinical characteristics between the MDD and BD groups were analyzed using chi‐square tests for categorical variables and independent samples t‐tests for continuous variables. These variables included age, sex, race, marital status, educational attainment, comorbid psychiatric and physical illnesses, medication status, family history of mental illness, and the QIDS‐SR scores. The total CUDOS‐A scores were compared between the MDD and BD groups using an independent samples t‐test, whereas individual item‐level scores were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test, given their ordinal nature. The proportion of those meeting the criteria for anxious distress was analyzed using a chi‐square test. Furthermore, independent samples t‐tests were used to compare QIDS‐SR scores between those with and without anxious distress within each diagnostic group. All statistical analyses were performed with the significance level set at p < 0.05. Additionally, demographic and clinical characteristics were compared specifically within the subgroup of those with MDD and BD who met the threshold for anxious distress. To verify the robustness of our findings in individuals actively experiencing depressive symptoms, all primary analyses were re‐evaluated in a restricted sample with QIDS‐SR total scores ≥6.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics

A total of 202 patients participated in this study (mean age: 45.3 ± 13.6 years; 51.5% female). The diagnostic distribution comprised 133 patients with MDD and 69 patients with BD. There were no significant differences between the two groups in demographic and clinical variables, with the exception of sex, where the proportion of women was significantly higher in the BD group than in the MDD group (62.3% vs. 45.9%, p = 0.03) (Table 1). The mean QIDS‐SR score was 10.6 ± 5.7 in the MDD group and 9.8 ± 5.6 in the BD group, showing no significant difference between the groups (p = 0.36).

Comparison of the CUDOS‐A scores by diagnosis

The mean total CUDOS‐A score was 28.0 ± 19.9 in the MDD group and 27.0 ± 17.7 in the BD group (Table 2). An independent samples t‐test revealed no statistically significant difference between the groups (p = 0.57). Additionally, no significant differences were observed between the groups for any of the 22 individual CUDOS‐A items. Based on the CUDOS‐A assessment of the DSM‐5 criteria for anxious distress specifier, 43.4% of participants in the BD group met the threshold for anxious distress, compared to 42.9% in the MDD group. A chi‐square test indicated no statistically significant difference in the prevalence of anxious distress between the two groups (p = 0.95).

Association between anxious distress and depressive symptom severity

Those with anxious distress showed significantly higher QIDS‐SR scores than those without anxious distress in both the MDD and BD groups (MDD: 15.0 ± 4.4 vs. 7.3 ± 4.2, p < 0.001; BD: 13.9 ± 4.2 vs. 6.6 ± 4.4, p < 0.001).

Comparison of MDD and BD within the anxious distress subgroup

Among those meeting the criteria for anxious distress, no significant differences were observed between the MDD and BD groups regarding age (44.3 ± 13.2 vs. 40.9 ± 13.8 years, p = 0.29), the proportion of females (47.4% vs. 66.7%, p = 0.09), depressive symptom severity (14.8 ± 4.5 vs. 13.9 ± 4.1, p = 0.33), or the prevalence of comorbid anxiety disorders (22.2% vs. 6.9%, p = 0.12).

Analysis in the non‐remitted subgroups

The results restricted to non‐remitted participants were virtually identical to those of the primary analysis, with no significant differences observed between the MDD and BD groups (Tables S1). Those with anxious distress showed significantly higher QIDS‐SR scores than those without anxious distress in both the MDD and BD groups (MDD: 15.2 ± 4.2 vs. 9.8 ± 2.9, p < 0.001; BD: 13.9 ± 4.2 vs. 9.7 ± 3.9, p < 0.001).

DISCUSSION

This study is the first to directly compare depressive symptom profiles, including items related to anxious distress, between MDD and BD using the CUDOS‐A. Our results revealed no significant differences between MDD and BD in overall symptom severity, individual anxiety‐related items, or the prevalence of anxious distress. These findings suggest that anxious distress represents a shared clinical feature across the mood disorder spectrum, rather than a disorder‐specific marker. Although these symptoms do not serve for diagnostic differentiation, their high prevalence and association with greater depressive severity underscore the clinical importance of routinely monitoring anxious distress, given its reported association with poorer clinical outcomes, including increased suicidality and reduced treatment response, in patients with MDD. 2 , 10

In the present study, the proportion of patients meeting the criteria for anxious distress was 42.9% in the MDD group and 43.4% in the BD group. These prevalence rates are notably lower than those reported in many previous studies. For example, a clinical study of 418 outpatients and inpatients in the United States utilizing the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM Disorders (SCID) found high prevalence rates of 79.6% in BD and 76.4% in MDD, without significant diagnostic differences. 5 Similarly, an Italian inpatient study of 206 patients based on psychiatrists' clinical judgments using the DSM‐5 specifiers indicated that 66.5% presented with anxious distress. 6 Another Italian multicenter cohort study involving 241 patients in Italy used semi‐structured or DSM‐5–compliant interview‐based methods and reported prevalence rates of 66.9% in BD and 51.2% in MDD, which were also higher than those observed in the present study. 4 While such interview‐based studies consistently report high prevalence rates typically ranging from 50% to 80%, our results using the self‐administered CUDOS‐A yielded more conservative estimates. This discrepancy may reflect differences between self‐report instruments and clinician‐administered assessments. While self‐report measures capture patients' subjective experiences of anxious distress, clinician‐administered assessments additionally incorporate behavioral observations and clinical judgment. These methodological differences may have contributed to the variation in prevalence estimates across studies.

Beyond these methodological differences, differences in clinical settings may also have contributed to the variation in prevalence estimates. Our study focused on relatively stable outpatients, whereas several previous studies included acutely ill inpatients. 6 Because anxious distress is often associated with greater depressive severity, 3 the inclusion of individuals in remission might be thought to mask potential group differences. However, even when our analysis was restricted to non‐remitted patients actively experiencing a depressive episode, the findings remained consistent, with no significant differences observed between MDD and BD. This suggests that while overall symptom severity may influence prevalence rates across settings, the inclusion of milder or remitted cases in our outpatient sample did not alter the core comparison between the two disorders.

Anxious distress was associated with greater depressive severity in both MDD and BD. In patients with MDD, this finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating an association between anxious distress and greater depressive symptom severity, 3 including findings from the psychometric evaluation of the Japanese version of the CUDOS‐A. 11 The present findings extend these observations by demonstrating a similar association between anxious distress and depressive severity in patients with BD. These findings suggest that anxious distress may serve as a marker of greater depressive burden across mood disorders. In this context, use of concise self‐report measures like the CUDOS‐A is highly valuable in routine practice, as it allows clinicians to efficiently identify anxiety‐related symptoms and facilitate appropriate clinical assessment and management.

Several limitations of this study should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the present study relied on a cross‐sectional design, which precludes conclusions regarding causality between anxious distress and clinical outcomes. Second, diagnoses were based on routine clinical interviews rather than structured diagnostic instruments such as the SCID, potentially affecting diagnostic precision. Third, the current mood state in patients with BD (e.g., depressive, hypomanic, or euthymic) was not systematically assessed, which may have influenced the observed severity of anxious distress. Fourth, the relatively smaller BD sample size may have reduced the statistical power to detect subtle group differences. Fifth, potential confounders such as medication status, duration of illness, and psychosocial stressors were not comprehensively evaluated. Sixth, the exclusive focus on outpatients from a single metropolitan academic hospital in Japan may limit the generalizability of our findings to inpatient populations or other geographical and cultural settings. Finally, anxious distress was operationally defined using the self‐reported CUDOS‐A rather than a clinician‐administered DSM‐5 assessment. Consequently, the concordance between this operational definition and formal DSM‐5 clinical diagnoses has not been established, nor did this approach permit evaluation of specific severity levels specified in the DSM‐5 specifier.

In conclusion, the comparable prevalence of anxious distress between MDD and BD suggests that these symptoms represent shared transdiagnostic features across mood disorders. Although this shared prevalence limits the utility of anxious distress for diagnostic differentiation, its association with greater depressive severity and high rate underscores its clinical significance and the necessity of routine assessment to inform risk management. The CUDOS‐A serves as a practical tool for efficiently capturing this burden in everyday clinical practice.
