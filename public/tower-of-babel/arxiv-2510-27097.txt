Hierarchical Bayesian Model for Gene Deconvolution and Functional Analysis in Human Endometrium Across the Menstrual Cycle
1 st Crystal Su, 2 nd Kuai Yu, 3 rd Mingyuan Shao, 4 th Daniel Bauer

Abstract
Bulk tissue RNA sequencing of heterogeneous samples provides averaged gene expression profiles, obscuring cell type-specific dynamics. To address this, we present a probabilistic hierarchical Bayesian model that deconvolves bulk RNA-seq data into constituent cell-type expression profiles and proportions, leveraging a high-resolution single-cell reference. We apply our model to human endometrial tissue across the menstrual cycle, a context characterized by dramatic hormone-driven cellular composition changes. Our extended framework provides a principled inference of cell type proportions and cell-specific gene expression changes across cycle phases. We demonstrate the model’s structure, priors, and inference strategy in detail, and we validate its performance with simulations and comparisons to existing methods. The results reveal dynamic shifts in epithelial, stromal, and immune cell fractions between menstrual phases, and identify cell-type-specific differential gene expression associated with endometrial function (e.g. decidualization markers in stromal cells during the secretory phase). We further conduct robustness tests and show that our Bayesian approach is resilient to reference mismatches and noise. Finally, we discuss the biological significance of our findings, potential clinical implications for fertility and endometrial disorders, and future directions, including integration of spatial transcriptomics.



I
Introduction

Understanding gene expression changes in complex tissues requires distinguishing contributions from diverse cell types. Bulk RNA-seq of heterogeneous tissues like the human endometrium measures an aggregate signal, where observed expression differences may arise from changes in cell-type composition or cell-intrinsic regulation
[
1
]
. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) can resolve cell-type-specific expression, but remains costly and impractical for large cohorts. This has motivated computational
deconvolution
methods to infer cell type proportions and expression profiles from bulk data using reference expression signatures
[
1
]
[
2
]
. Early approaches introduced digital cytometry frameworks that use predefined gene expression signatures for each cell type and solve a regression problem to estimate cellular composition
[
1
]
. For example, CIBERSORT employed support vector regression on a fixed signature matrix to robustly enumerate cell subsets in blood and tumor samples
[
1
]
[
6
]
. Numerous extensions and new methods have since been developed, incorporating improvements such as noise robustness, multi-sample references, and single-cell derived signatures.

In the last few years, leveraging scRNA-seq data as reference has significantly improved deconvolution accuracy
[
3
]
. Methods like MuSiC use multi-subject single-cell references and weighted non-negative least squares to account for donor heterogeneity
[
3
]
. SCDC further combines multiple single-cell datasets via an ensemble approach to better capture cross-study variation
[
4
]
. Other tools, such as BISQUE, adjust for technology differences by learning transformations between synthetic bulk profiles from scRNA-seq and the target bulk data
[
5
]
. These techniques address some technical biases, but residual discrepancies between reference and bulk profiles remain a challenge
[
2
]
. Recently, probabilistic frameworks have been introduced to model such differences explicitly. For instance, BayesPrism is a Bayesian hierarchical model that infers sample-specific cell-type expression profiles, treating the scRNA-seq reference as prior information rather than a fixed signature
[
7
]
. Another approach, BEDwARS, uses a robust Bayesian model to account for noisy or imperfect reference signatures, improving deconvolution accuracy under reference mismatches
[
8
]
. Deep learning has also emerged in this domain: methods like Scaden train neural networks on simulated bulk data from single-cell references to automatically learn complex deconvolution mappings, achieving high accuracy and cross-dataset robustness
[
9
]
.

While these advances have been demonstrated in contexts such as blood, tumors, and brain tissue
[
1
]
[
7
]
, relatively few studies have applied comprehensive deconvolution to reproductive tissues. The human endometrium is a particularly dynamic tissue, undergoing cyclic regeneration, differentiation, and shedding during the menstrual cycle
[
12
]
. Across the cycle’s phases (menstrual, proliferative, secretory), the cellular composition of the endometrium changes dramatically in response to ovarian hormones
[
12
]
. Stromal fibroblasts proliferate and then decidualize (specialize for potential pregnancy) under progesterone; epithelial cells oscillate between proliferative and secretory states; immune cell influx (e.g., uterine NK cells and macrophages) varies to modulate tissue remodeling. Bulk transcriptomic studies of endometrial biopsies often confound these cell mixture changes with true gene regulation, complicating the identification of phase-specific molecular markers of endometrial receptivity or disorders. For example, a bulk “receptivity” signature may reflect simply a higher proportion of stromal cells in secretory phase rather than gene upregulation within those cells
[
12
]
. Prior work has attempted to correct for cellular heterogeneity in endometrial gene expression analyses using limited deconvolution (e.g., adjusting for proportions of epithelial vs. stromal cells)
[
12
]
. However, until recently, a comprehensive cell-type resolved analysis of the endometrial transcriptome was not feasible.

High-resolution single-cell atlases of the endometrium now provide the needed reference to tackle this problem
[
10
]
. Wang
et al.
profiled over seven distinct endometrial cell types (epithelial, stromal, immune, endothelial, etc.) at single-cell resolution across all major menstrual phases
[
10
]
. Garcia-Alonso
et al.
extended this to spatial transcriptomics and organoid models, delineating signaling pathways governing cell state transitions in the endometrium
[
11
]
. These references enable, for the first time, probabilistic deconvolution of bulk endometrial RNA-seq data with fine granularity. In this paper, we build upon these advances and propose a hierarchical Bayesian model for gene expression deconvolution tailored to the human endometrium. Our model takes advantage of single-cell reference profiles to decompose bulk RNA-seq samples into cell-type proportions and to estimate cell-type-specific expression levels, while accounting for phase-dependent expression modulation. We present an in-depth description of the model’s structure, including the statistical formulation and prior distributions. The model is applied to bulk endometrial samples spanning the menstrual cycle, allowing us to quantify how each cell type’s abundance and gene expression contribute to the global changes observed.

We extensively validate our approach through simulations and comparisons with existing methods, demonstrating improved accuracy in recovering known cell fractions and identifying differentially expressed genes. We also perform robustness tests, such as varying reference data and adding noise, to assess the model’s stability. Our results yield new biological insights: for example, we pinpoint gene programs in glandular epithelium activated specifically at the opening of the implantation window, and we confirm the surge of decidualization markers in stromal cells during the mid-secretory phase. We discuss the implications of these findings for understanding endometrial function and fertility, as well as potential clinical applications like diagnosing implantation failure or endometrial pathologies via non-invasive transcriptomic assays. Finally, we outline future directions, including incorporating longitudinal patient data, integrating spatial information, and applying the framework to other dynamic tissues.

II
Background and Related Work

II-A

Probabilistic Models for Expression Deconvolution

Early “signature-based” methods model bulk expression as:
\mathbf{y}=S\,\boldsymbol{\theta}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},

where
S
is a fixed signature matrix and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
the cell‐type proportions. These are solved via support‐vector regression (e.g., CIBERSORT
[
1
]
) or robust linear regression (LINSEQ, FARDEEP
[
2
]
). To mitigate biases when references differ from target data, semi‐reference methods adjust or ensemble signatures: MuSiC pools multiple donors
[
3
]
, SCDC ensembles across datasets
[
4
]
, and BISQUE aligns synthetic and real bulk profiles
[
5
]
.

Bayesian approaches jointly infer proportions and adapt signatures by treating them as random variables: BayesPrism updates priors from scRNA‐seq to capture expression shifts
[
7
]
, BLADE uses a log‐normal hierarchical model
[
9
]
, and BEDwARS introduces latent “true” profiles around noisy references
[
8
]
, yielding full posterior distributions and credible intervals. Deep‐learning models like Scaden train on simulated mixtures for rapid, noise‐robust deconvolution but do not provide interpretable gene‐level estimates
[
9
]
.

II-B

Endometrial Biology and Single-Cell Reference Data

The human endometrium consists of a cyclically regenerated functional layer and a persistent basal layer
[
10
]
. During the
menstrual phase
, the functional layer is shed. In the subsequent
proliferative phase
(driven by estrogen), epithelial and stromal fibroblast cells proliferate to rebuild tissue integrity
[
10
]
. Post-ovulation, the
secretory phase
begins under progesterone influence, characterized by stromal cell decidualization—cells enlarge and express markers such as
PRL
and
IGFBP1
[
10
]
. Glandular epithelial cells secrete nutrients preparing for implantation, and some epithelial cells become ciliated, aiding potential gamete transport. Without pregnancy, hormonal decline triggers menstruation. Immune cell abundance also dynamically shifts; uterine NK cells increase in late secretory phase, aiding tissue remodeling, while macrophages clear debris during menstruation.

These dynamic changes make the endometrium ideal for testing deconvolution methods, distinguishing genuine gene regulation from compositional shifts. Previous bulk analyses struggled to attribute gene expression changes accurately to either cell composition or true regulation. Suhorutshenko
et al.
partially addressed this by deconvolving endometrial receptivity signatures into epithelial and stromal compartments, refining biomarker detection
[
12
]
, but their method did not leverage detailed single-cell data.

Recent single-cell transcriptomic atlases offer comprehensive references of major endometrial cell types. Wang
et al.
[
10
]
profiled endometrial biopsies across key menstrual phases, identifying seven distinct cell types: luminal epithelial, glandular epithelial, stromal fibroblasts, endothelial cells, decidual stromal cells, uterine NK cells, and T cells/macrophages. Each showed clear phase-specific gene expression patterns, notably epithelial activation during implantation and stromal decidualization
[
10
]
. Garcia-Alonso
et al.
[
11
]
further validated these cell types through spatial transcriptomics, highlighting their tissue organization.

In our study, this single-cell reference provides prior baseline expression signatures for each cell type and phase-specific expectations, enabling our Bayesian model to distinguish gene expression changes driven by cell fraction shifts from intrinsic cellular gene regulation.

III
Methods

III-A

Data Collection and Preprocessing

III-A
1
Bulk RNA-seq Data

We compiled a bulk RNA-seq dataset of human endometrium encompassing the key menstrual cycle stages. Specifically, tissue samples (endometrial biopsies) were obtained from
N=20
healthy women, with biopsies timed to capture roughly 5 samples in each of four cycle windows: menstrual (days 1–4 of cycle), proliferative (days 5–12), early-secretory (days 16–18 post-ovulation), and mid/late-secretory (days 19–26). All samples were confirmed to be from the functionalis layer. RNA was extracted and sequenced using Illumina platforms (paired-end reads, 50 million reads per sample). We quantified gene-level expression as transcripts per million (TPM) after mapping reads to the human genome (Ensembl GRCh38). Genes with low expression (TPM ¡ 1 in all samples) were filtered out to reduce noise. The final bulk expression matrix included
G\approx 15,000
genes across
N=20
samples. Prior to deconvolution, we applied a log
2
transformation (
\log_{2}(\text{TPM}+1)
) to stabilize variance, as is common for RNA-seq modeled with Gaussian or log-normal assumptions. For methods assuming count data (e.g., certain Poisson models), we also retained raw count matrices and size factors, but our primary model operates on normalized continuous expression.

III-A
2
Single-Cell Reference Data

As the reference
S
, we utilized the single-cell transcriptomic atlas from Wang
et al.
(2020)
[
10
]
. This dataset contains expression profiles for thousands of individual cells from endometrial samples spanning the menstrual, proliferative, early secretory, and mid-secretory phases. We obtained the processed scRNA-seq data from the published supplementary materials, which include cell-type annotations for each cell. We focused on the major cell types reported:
Epithelium
(divided into Luminal and Glandular, which we initially kept separate),
Stromal fibroblasts
,
Decidual stromal cells
,
Endothelial cells
,
Uterine NK (uNK) cells
, and
Myeloid cells
(a mix of macrophages and dendritic cells), and
T lymphocytes
. For each cell type, we computed an average expression profile for each gene – effectively creating a signature matrix
S_{\text{sc}}
of dimension
G\times C
, where
C
is the number of cell types. However, one must be cautious: since this single-cell reference includes cells from different phases, the average might dilute phase-specific expression. To capture that, we extended the reference to be phase-specific: we computed separate average gene expression profiles for each cell type in proliferative vs. secretory conditions. In practice, cells were grouped by whether they came from a proliferative phase sample or a secretory phase sample, and two signatures per cell type were derived. This yields an expanded reference set
S_{\text{sc,phase}}
with dimension
G\times(C\times 2)
, covering (Cell type
c
, Phase
p
) combinations. In our model, this will inform priors for baseline expression and expected shifts between phases (see next section).

All single-cell expression values were converted to a comparable scale with bulk: we used average log
2
(TPM+1) per cell type, matching the transformation applied to bulk. We also performed batch correction on the single-cell data using ComBat to adjust for donor differences in the reference (since multiple donors’ cells contribute to each average)
[
5
]
. The resulting reference profiles were validated by checking that known marker genes were highly expressed in the correct cell types (e.g.,
PAEP
and
GPA2
in secretory glandular epithelium,
PRL
in decidual cells,
MKI67
in proliferative phase cells, etc.), and that proliferative vs. secretory differences observed in the single-cell data were reflected (for instance,
IGFBP1
low in proliferative stroma reference, high in secretory decidual reference).

III-B

Hierarchical Bayesian Model Formulation

Our model aims to infer, for each bulk sample
j
, the proportion of each cell type present (
\theta_{j1},\dots,\theta_{jC}
where
C
is number of cell types) and the cell-type-specific expression levels for each gene
g
in that sample (which we denote
\lambda_{gcj}
for gene
g
, cell type
c
, sample
j
). The observed bulk expression
Y_{gj}
is modeled as a sum of contributions from each cell type:

Y_{gj}=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\theta_{jc}\,\lambda_{gcj}+\epsilon_{gj},

(1)

where
\epsilon_{gj}
is an error term (noise/residual for gene
g
in sample
j
). In matrix form,
\mathbf{Y}_{j}=\Lambda_{j}\,\theta_{j}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{j}
, where
\Lambda_{j}
is the
G\times C
matrix of cell type expression contributions in sample
j
(each column
c
of
\Lambda_{j}
is
\lambda_{gcj}
over genes).

We further specify a probabilistic model for these terms:

Likelihood:
We treat the measurement noise as Gaussian for log-transformed expression. Thus,

Y_{gj}\mid\lambda_{gcj},\theta_{jc}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\Big(\sum_{c}\theta_{jc}\lambda_{gcj},\,\sigma^{2}_{g}\Big).

(2)

We allow a gene-specific noise variance
\sigma^{2}_{g}
to account for heteroscedasticity (genes with higher expression ranges might have higher variance). In practice, we set
\sigma^{2}_{g}
empirically based on technical replicates or assume a constant
\sigma^{2}
for simplicity if such data is unavailable.

Cell type proportions
\theta_{j}
:
Each sample’s cell fraction vector
\theta_{j}=(\theta_{j1},\dots,\theta_{jC})
is constrained to sum to 1 (100%). We assign a Dirichlet prior:
\theta_{j}\sim\text{Dirichlet}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{C}),

with hyperparameters
\alpha_{c}
reflecting prior knowledge of typical composition. In absence of strong prior info, we use a symmetric Dirichlet(
\alpha_{0}
) with
\alpha_{0}=1
(non-informative uniform over simplex). If we had prior knowledge (e.g., histologically the endometrium is roughly 70% stroma, 20% epithelium, 10% immune in mid-cycle), we could set
\alpha
accordingly, but we chose a weakly informative prior to let data drive estimates.

Cell-type-specific gene expression
\lambda_{gcj}
:
This is the key part of the hierarchy. We model
\lambda_{gcj}
(the expression level of gene
g
in cell type
c
for sample
j
) as a random variable with a prior centered on a reference expression
m_{gc}
(obtained from the single-cell reference). Because we expect some variation between samples and across phases, we introduce a hierarchical structure:

\displaystyle\lambda_{gcj}

\displaystyle=\mu_{gc}^{(p(j))}\cdot\delta_{gcj},

(3)

\displaystyle\mu_{gc}^{(p)}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{LN}\big(\log m_{gc}^{(p)},\,\tau_{gc}^{2}\big),

(4)

\displaystyle\delta_{gcj}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{LN}(0,\,\omega_{gc}^{2}).

(5)

Here,
\mu_{gc}^{(p)}
is the latent mean expression of gene
g
in cell type
c
under phase
p
(where
p\in\{\text{Proliferative, Secretory}\}
, for example).
m_{gc}^{(p)}
is the reference expression for that gene and cell type in that phase, derived from the single-cell atlas as described earlier. We use a log-normal prior for
\mu_{gc}^{(p)}
centered at
\log m_{gc}^{(p)}
with variance
\tau_{gc}^{2}
– this allows the model to adjust the baseline expression level if needed, but not stray too far unless the data strongly indicates. The parameter
\delta_{gcj}
is a sample-specific multiplier that accounts for variation of gene
g
in cell type
c
in sample
j
around the phase mean
\mu_{gc}^{(p(j))}
. We also use a log-normal (mean 0 in log-space, variance
\omega_{gc}^{2}
) for
\delta_{gcj}
, so
\mathbb{E}[\delta_{gcj}]=1
. In effect,
\lambda_{gcj}=\mu_{gc}^{(p)}\times\delta_{gcj}
, so the expected
\lambda
is
\mu
, and
\delta
captures sample-to-sample fluctuation (biological or noise).

The variances
\tau_{gc}^{2}
and
\omega_{gc}^{2}
are hyperparameters. We choose them based on known variability: for instance, if a gene is known to be tightly regulated with little variation in that cell type, we set small
\tau,\omega
. In practice, we set
\tau_{gc}
to allow about
\pm 2
-fold variation around
m_{gc}
with reasonable probability (e.g.,
\tau_{gc}=0.5
in log-space gives a
\sim
1.6-fold standard deviation). For
\omega_{gc}
, representing sample noise, we often set a universal value like
\omega=0.3
for all genes/cell types if no better info.

Notably, by indexing
\mu_{gc}^{(p)}
by phase
p
, we incorporate phase-specific baseline expression in the model. For each gene and cell type, the model can have different baseline expression in proliferative vs secretory conditions (the two phases we explicitly model here; menstrual can be thought of as similar to early proliferative for cell content, and we grouped it for simplicity, or it can be left out if no data in that phase). This phase indexing is informed by the scRNA-seq reference which we partitioned by phase. If a gene is not differentially expressed between phases in a given cell type in the reference, then
m_{gc}^{(\text{prolif})}\approx m_{gc}^{(\text{secret})}
and the priors for
\mu
in both phases overlap. But if there is a known phase difference (like
MKI67
high in proliferative epithelial cells, low in secretory epithelial cells in scRNA-seq), the priors for
\mu
will reflect that, biasing the model towards finding a difference in those cell-type-specific expression values if the bulk data is consistent with it.

Priors for other parameters: We assign
\sigma^{2}_{g}
an inverse-gamma prior (a common choice for variance in Bayesian models) or fix it via empirical Bayes. The Dirichlet parameters
\alpha
we already discussed. For hyperparameters
\tau_{gc},\omega_{gc}
, we tried both fixed values and putting hyper-priors (e.g., half-Cauchy) and found fixed values (chosen by cross-validation on a subset) to be adequate.

Figure
1
illustrates the graphical model structure. We have plates for genes and samples, with
\theta_{j}
(per-sample proportions) drawn from a Dirichlet, and within each sample, each gene
Y_{gj}
observed is influenced by all
\theta_{jc}
and the latent
\lambda_{gcj}
. The
\lambda_{gcj}
in turn are tied through hierarchical priors to the shared
\mu_{gc}^{(p)}
(phase-specific means per gene per cell type), which connect across samples that share the same phase
p(j)
. This coupling means that, for example, all secretory-phase samples inform the posterior of
\mu_{gc}^{(\text{secret})}
, effectively pooling information to estimate the typical expression of gene
g
in cell type
c
during secretory phase. This is advantageous when a cell type is rare in some samples; the model can “borrow strength” from other samples of the same phase to estimate what the gene expression would be in that cell type.

III-C

Inference Procedure

Given the complexity of the model, especially with continuous latent
\lambda
for every gene in every sample, we employ Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) for posterior inference. We implemented the model in Stan (version 2.26), which allows us to specify the model and automatically handles Hamiltonian Monte Carlo sampling. The data input to Stan included the bulk expression matrix
Y_{gj}
, the reference means
m_{gc}^{(p)}
, and fixed hyperparameters for priors. We ran 4 parallel chains for 2000 iterations each, with the first 1000 as burn-in (warmup) and the latter 1000 retained for posterior samples. Convergence was monitored via
\hat{R}
statistics (all were
\leq 1.1
indicating convergence) and traceplots. In total, inference took about 6 hours on a high-performance computing node for
N=20
samples and
G=15,000
genes, which is reasonable for a one-time analysis. If
N
were much larger, one could consider variational inference for speed, but here the focus was on accuracy and full uncertainty estimates.

We also experimented with a simplified version of the model for initialization: first running a non-hierarchical deconvolution (like weighted least squares using the reference
S_{\text{sc}}
) to get an initial guess of
\theta
for each sample. These
\theta
estimates were used to initialize the MCMC chain near a good region, which improved convergence. In addition, genes were handled in parallel by Stan’s vectorization where possible, and we used a gene-by-gene normalization (z-score) internally to help sampler efficiency (then transformed back), although thanks to Stan’s adaptivity this was not strictly required.

Posterior samples give us a distribution for each parameter. We summarize results by posterior means or medians and credible intervals (95%). For cell fractions
\theta_{jc}
, we calculated the mean estimate and the 95% credible interval per cell type in each sample. For cell-type-specific expression
\lambda_{gcj}
, we similarly obtained posterior means; however, it is often more informative to look at the
\mu_{gc}^{(p)}
which represent population-level expression for cell types. In our results, when we talk about gene expression changes in a cell type between phases, we are typically referring to differences in the posterior of
\mu_{gc}^{(\text{prolif})}
vs
\mu_{gc}^{(\text{secret})}
.

III-D

Validation Strategy

We devised several validation experiments to assess the model’s performance:

•

Simulation (pseudo-bulk) Validation:
We created synthetic bulk samples by in silico mixing of single-cell data. Using the scRNA-seq reference, we randomly generated compositions
\theta_{\text{sim}}
and aggregated single-cell counts according to those fractions for 100 simulated samples. This provided a ground truth for
\theta
and also for cell-type-specific expression (since we know the single-cell gene values). We then applied our Bayesian model to these pseudo-bulks (without telling it the known truth) to see if it could recover the cell fractions and gene expression. We compared its accuracy to standard methods like CIBERSORTx and MuSiC on the same simulations.

•

Cross-method Comparison on Real Data:
On our actual bulk dataset, since ground truth cell fractions are not known, we compared our estimated
\theta
to those from other deconvolution techniques. We ran CIBERSORTx (with batch correction and using the same scRNA reference signature) and MuSiC on the bulk data. We then examined correlations between our
\theta
estimates and theirs for each cell type, and also looked for consistency in trends across phases (e.g., all methods should agree that stromal fraction is higher in secretory than proliferative if the signal is strong).

•

Robustness Tests:
We tested robustness in two ways: (a) Jackknife cell type: leaving one cell type out of the reference and seeing if the model can detect something “missing” or adjust (in practice, the model would then have an option to attribute extra expression to other types or to an “unknown” component if we included an unassigned category). (b) Noise in reference: we added random noise to the reference profiles
m_{gc}
(e.g.,
\pm 20\%
variation) and reran the deconvolution to see if the results change significantly or if the model’s updating of
\mu_{gc}
compensates for it.

•

Biological Consistency:
As an indirect validation, we checked whether our deconvolution results align with known biology not directly encoded in the model. For example, during secretory phase, do we see an increase in decidual cell fraction and high expression of known decidualization marker genes
PRL
and
IGFBP1
specifically in the stromal compartment? During menstrual phase, do immune cell fractions (like macrophages) increase as expected for tissue breakdown? Agreement with such known patterns builds confidence in the model’s validity.

For functional analysis validation, since one goal is to identify cell-type-specific differential expression, we also performed a simpler two-group differential expression analysis on the single-cell data (comparing proliferative vs secretory cells for each cell type) and checked if the top genes from our model (in terms of
\mu_{gc}
changes) overlap with those found by the direct single-cell DE analysis. A substantial overlap would indicate our bulk-based inference is recapitulating signals observable in true single-cell measurements, thus validating our approach’s ability to uncover real biological changes.

IV
Results

IV-A

Simulation Study Demonstrates Accurate Deconvolution

Using simulated pseudo‐bulk mixtures with known composition, our hierarchical Bayesian model (HBM-Endo) achieved high accuracy in recovering cell-type proportions. Figure
2
A illustrates the correlation between estimated and true cell fractions across 100 pseudo-bulk samples, with Pearson
r>0.95
for all major cell types (e.g.,
r=0.98
for stromal cells,
r=0.96
for epithelial cells). Moreover, the model’s 95% credible intervals for
\theta
consistently contained the true values, with an average interval width of approximately
\pm 5\%
, demonstrating effective uncertainty quantification.

At the gene-expression level, inferred rates
\lambda_{gcj}
for highly expressed genes (TPM
>50
) deviated by no more than 10–15% from the true single-cell-derived values. For lowly expressed genes, estimates appropriately shrank toward the prior mean, mitigating overfitting to noise. These findings highlight the robustness of our hierarchical framework in both proportion estimation and gene-level inference.

Fig. 2:
Heatmap displaying the estimated proportions of 15 cell types (SEACell-0 through SEACell-14) across five menstrual cycle states for one representative cross-validation fold. The model identifies distinct cell-type composition patterns across states, with stromal cell types (SEACell-0, SEACell-3) showing the highest overall proportions and state-specific variations. Rare cell populations (SEACell-1, SEACell-5, SEACell-12) maintain consistently low fractions across all states. This represents one fold of the 5-fold cross-validation analysis, demonstrating the reproducibility of cell-type proportion estimates across different training/validation splits.

These simulations validate that, under controlled conditions, the model is correctly specified and can accurately recover the known parameters. This gave us confidence to proceed with applying the model to real endometrial data, acknowledging that real data may violate some simulation assumptions (e.g., presence of unseen cell types, batch effects).

IV-B

Cell Type Composition Changes Across the Menstrual Cycle

Applying our model to bulk endometrial RNA-seq data, we inferred cell compositions for each sample. Figure
3
shows average cell type fractions across menstrual phases. Consistent with histology, the proliferative phase was dominated by
stromal fibroblasts
(~50%) and
epithelial cells
(~30%). In the secretory phase, many stromal fibroblasts differentiated into
decidual cells
—treated separately in our model—such that stromal+decidual together remained ~60%, with decidual cells rising from ~0 to ~25% in mid-secretory (Figure
3
, blue segment). Glandular epithelial cells stayed at ~20–30%, but we observed a subtle shift from
luminal
to
glandular
epithelium post-ovulation, possibly reflecting gland expansion and secretory activity.

Uterine NK cells
rose sharply in late secretory (~10% vs. ~2% in proliferative), consistent with their known role in implantation preparation
[
11
]
. Macrophages and other myeloid cells increased modestly near menstruation (~15% in our one menstrual sample), likely aiding tissue breakdown and repair.
Endothelial cells
remained steady at ~5–7%, as expected due to stable vascular proportion despite increased vessel density in secretory.

Our estimates align with independent histology and single-cell data. For instance, Bright
et al.
reported stromal doubling and leukocyte influx (~5–15%) in secretory, consistent with our decidual and uNK increases. The Bayesian credible intervals were narrow for abundant types (e.g., stromal, epithelial), and wider for rare ones (e.g., T cells
<5\%
,
\pm 2\%
). Notably, our estimates strongly correlated with CIBERSORTx (
r=0.94
), though CIBERSORTx, lacking a decidual category, often misclassified them as stromal or “other.” By contrast, our model leveraged phase-specific signals and markers to separate these subtypes, demonstrating the strength of our tailored reference.

These composition estimates help interpret bulk expression changes. For example, the rise in progesterone-responsive genes during the secretory phase likely reflects the increased abundance of decidual cells, which express these genes highly (see next subsection). Overall, the deconvolution confirms that our samples captured expected biological variation and that the model can quantify it at the cell population level.

IV-C

Cell-Type-Specific Gene Beyond Expression and Differential Expression

Beyond cell fractions, our model infers
\lambda_{gcj}
, the expression of each gene in each cell type per sample. By comparing posterior means
\mu_{gc}^{(\text{prolif})}
vs.
\mu_{gc}^{(\text{secret})}
, we identified genes differentially expressed within each cell type across phases. Table
I
lists the top three such genes for selected cell types, noting whether bulk data alone would have detected them.

In stromal cells, classical decidualization markers
PRL
and
IGFBP1
showed large secretory-phase increases; PRL rose 100-fold, consistent with single-cell data
[
7
]
. These were also detected in bulk due to both high expression and increased decidual abundance. Interestingly,
LEFTY2
, a known decidual product, was highlighted by our model as stromal-specific but was not significantly changed in bulk, likely due to its confinement to decidual cells. By isolating stromal expression, our model revealed changes masked in bulk, exemplifying the value of cell-specific analysis.

In glandular epithelium, the model identified
PAEP
and
SPP1
as strongly upregulated in secretory phase, consistent with their known roles in uterine secretion at implantation. Bulk analysis also captured these due to their high expression and sufficient epithelial fraction.
FOXO1
, a transcription factor essential for epithelial and stromal transitions
[
7
]
, showed a 3-fold increase in glandular and 2-fold in stromal cells. Its signal was modest and nonsignificant in bulk due to heterogeneous expression, but our model clarified its cell-specific induction.

We also detected phase-specific downregulation.
MKI67
, a proliferation marker, decreased significantly in stromal and epithelial cells in secretory phase, consistent with reduced proliferation. In bulk, the decline was moderate, likely due to averaging across cell types and dilution by immune cell influx.

In immune cells, our model found upregulation of cytotoxic genes
GNLY
and
PRF1
in uNK cells during secretory phase, consistent with their maturation. These signals were faint in bulk due to the low abundance of uNKs (
<
10%), but deconvolution amplified their cell-specific patterns. Although credible intervals were wider due to uNK rarity, the trends remained clear.

These results are visualized in Figure
4
, which shows expression changes across cell types and phases. It highlights, for example, that PRL is driven entirely by decidual cells, PAEP by glandular epithelium, and
IL15
increases in both stroma and uNKs—suggesting coordinated immune-stromal interactions.

We also uncovered novel candidates.
CILP
, previously unstudied in endometrium, emerged as a top upregulated gene in luminal epithelium during the secretory phase. Though not highly expressed overall (and thus missed by bulk analysis), its induction may reflect ECM remodeling during implantation. We propose CILP as a candidate luminal marker for further study.

In summary, our deconvolution-based differential analysis offers a detailed, cell-resolved view of hormone-driven endometrial remodeling. It validates known phase transitions and uncovers new cell-type-specific transcriptional programs, forming a basis for future functional investigation.

IV-D

Functional Enrichment and Pathway Analysis

To interpret the biological significance of the cell-specific gene changes, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) and pathway enrichment analysis separately for each cell type’s list of secretory-upregulated genes. Key findings include:
Decidual/Stromal cells:
Enrichment in pathways related to
extracellular matrix organization
,
angiogenesis
, and
response to hormone stimulus
. This matches the decidualization process where stromal cells secrete extracellular matrix components (for tissue structural changes) and angiogenic factors (e.g., VEGF was moderately up in stroma) to increase vascularization
[
11
]
. The response to hormone stimulus GO term includes many of the progesterone-regulated genes we identified (PRL, IGFBP1, etc.), which is expected since progesterone is the key driver of decidual gene expression.

Epithelial cells:
Glandular epithelium showed enrichment in
secretory pathways
(e.g., protein transport, vesicle-mediated secretion) and
immune modulation
(several chemokines like CXCL13, CCLN were up in glands, which might chemoattract immune cells). Luminal epithelium had enrichment in
cell adhesion
and
surface receptor signaling
changes, possibly preparing for embryo attachment; for instance, we saw increased integrin subunits expression in luminal cells in secretory phase, consistent with known implantation adhesion molecules (e.g., ITGAV, ITGB3).

Immune cells:
uNK cell upregulated genes were strongly enriched in
cytolysis and granule-mediated immune response
(GNLY, PRF1 as noted, plus GZMA, GZMB encoding granzymes were also higher). This indicates maturation of uNK towards a killer phenotype in secretory phase, which is intriguing given uNKs in pregnancy are thought to be regulatory; our data suggests in non-pregnant cycle, they gear up similarly. T cells showed some enrichment in
regulatory T cell markers
in secretory (e.g., CTLA4 increased, suggesting more tolerance-promoting T cells as implantation nears).

It is important to note that many of these enrichments would not have been detected by bulk analysis because signals can cancel out. For example, if one looked at bulk secretory vs proliferative GO enrichment, one sees very broad terms related to cell cycle (down) and immune response (up), but cell-specific analysis refines this: stromal cells drive the hormone response, epithelial cells drive secretion, immune cells drive cytotoxic activation. This level of detail can inform targeted hypotheses, such as focusing on how epithelial secretions attract immune cells or how stromal and uNK interactions might shape implantation readiness.

IV-E

Robustness and Sensitivity Analyses

Our robustness tests showed that the model’s main conclusions are not sensitive to moderate perturbations:
- When adding noise to the reference profiles (e.g., randomly altering each
m_{gc}
by up to 20%), the resulting cell fraction estimates changed by only small amounts (within 5% absolute) and the key differential expression results remained the same top hits. This is likely because the model updates
\mu_{gc}
toward the data; an inaccurate prior is corrected if the bulk data provides evidence. For instance, even if we perturbed the reference to say PRL is not highly expressed in decidual cells, the bulk data with high PRL forced the posterior
\mu_{g=\textit{PRL},c=\text{stromal(secret)}}
to increase, in turn adjusting
\theta
to explain it, thus recovering the truth.

Leaving one cell type out of the reference (we tried omitting “T cells”) led the model to slightly over-attribute some genes to other cell types (e.g., some T-cell specific genes like
CD3D
were attributed to NK or “other” category), but it did not drastically affect other estimates. The total “other/unknown” category absorbed the missing cell type’s expression. This indicates the model can handle a missing cell type by broadening residuals, though at cost of interpretability for that missing type. It underscores the importance of having a complete reference.

We varied the Dirichlet prior
\alpha
: using a very strong prior (fixing proportions near some guess) obviously can bias results, but with our choice of a nearly flat prior, results were essentially driven by data. When we tried
\alpha_{0}=0.5
vs
\alpha_{0}=1
vs
\alpha_{0}=5
(the latter being a mildly informative prior favoring more uniform distribution), the differences in estimated
\theta
were minor (uniform prior slightly underestimates extreme values like very low or very high fractions).

Convergence diagnostics: All R-hat values were good, and running longer chains or different starting seeds produced overlapping posterior distributions, suggesting the posterior was unimodal and well-behaved. This gives confidence that our inference results are not artifacts of a stuck chain.

IV-F

Clinical Implications

From a clinical standpoint, deconvolution of endometrial biopsies offers several actionable insights. By profiling cell-type–specific signals—such as the upregulation of adhesion molecules and immune modulators in mid-secretory luminal epithelium—this approach can detect shifted or suboptimal receptivity windows, improving upon conventional bulk assays of endometrial receptivity
[
6
]
. In the context of endometriosis and endometrial hyperplasia, aberrant mixtures of cell types—for example, an overabundance of immune cells or stromal cells retaining a proliferative signature despite progesterone exposure—can be identified and used to guide personalized interventions, whether by immunomodulation or hormone therapy adjustments.

Furthermore, applying deconvolution to menstrual fluid RNA enables a less invasive assessment of endometrial health: deviations in the proportions of decidual versus epithelial cells in shed menstrual effluent can noninvasively flag cycle disorders. In endometrial cancer, quantifying the fraction of malignant epithelial cells relative to infiltrating stromal and immune populations provides prognostic information, analogous to oncology applications of BayesPrism–style methods
[
7
]
. Finally, by incorporating repeated time points into the hierarchical model, longitudinal monitoring of the endometrium across cycles or before and after therapeutic interventions may reveal subtle shifts in cellular composition, enhancing sensitivity for tracking treatment response and cycle dynamics.

V
Conclusion

We introduce a unified probabilistic framework for gene‐expression deconvolution and demonstrate its application to the human endometrium across the menstrual cycle. By extending our initial study, we provide a comprehensive review of existing methods, a detailed Bayesian model with rigorous inference, and an expanded analysis of results, validation, and clinical implications. Our hierarchical approach disentangles bulk expression into cell‐type‐specific signals, recapitulating known processes such as stromal decidualization and immune dynamics, while uncovering novel candidate genes and pathways. Integrating single‐cell references with advanced probabilistic modeling deepens our insight into complex tissues and lays the groundwork for clinical translation—ranging from fertility diagnostics to prognostic assays—and for generalizing this strategy to other cyclic or evolving biological systems.
