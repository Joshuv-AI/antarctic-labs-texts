Ergodic properties of
concurrent systems

Abstract
A concurrent system is defined as a monoid action of a trace monoid on a finite set of states. Concurrent systems represent state models where the state is distributed and where state changes are local. Starting from a spectral property on the combinatorics of concurrent systems, we prove the existence and uniqueness of a Markov measure on the space of infinite trajectories relatively to any weight distributions. In turn, we obtain a combinatorial result by proving that the kernel of the associated Möbius matrix has dimension 1 1 ; the Möbius matrix extends in this context the Möbius polynomial of a trace monoid. We study ergodic properties of irreducible concurrent systems and we prove a Strong law of large numbers. It allows us to introduce the speedup as a measurement of the average amount of concurrency within infinite trajectories. Examples are studied. Keywords: concurrent systems; Markov measures; ergodicity; partial order semantics

Introduction

In this paper we build on previous results to study the theory of probabilistic concurrent systems.
Our main goal is to obtain ergodicity results, in particular a Strong law of large numbers with an adequate notion of convergence.
The latter point is central since “probabilistic concurrent processes” are not given as standard probabilistic processes.
Indeed, the very nature of concurrent systems is the absence of a global clock at the scale of the system.

We first recall some background on the theory of trace monoids, on concurrent systems and on their probabilistic counterparts.
Then we establish a spectral property which will play a central role.
We study the structure of probabilistic valuations; probabilistic valuations describe Markov measures for concurrent systems.
Then we apply these results to the study of ergodic properties, deriving in particular a Strong law of large numbers.
Examples illustrating some specific aspects of the theory are given in a separate section.

Some of the constructions and results of this paper are generalizations of statements proved in earlier works
[
1
,
3
]
.

Motivations and framework.

Markov chains are, among other things, a powerful and versatile model for describing the evolution of state systems through time.
Today, a number of important real-life systems have their state distributed over space.
As a result, the evolution of their state through time is most adequately captured by a
partially ordered
collection of time instants, rather than by a
sequence
of time instants.
This renders the concurrency of local changes of state, but does not comply well with Markov chains models.

Before we come to their probabilistic part, let us first quickly describe the underlying dynamics of the models that we consider.
The basic dynamics is handled by a
trace monoid
, alternatively called in the literature a
heap monoid
or a
free partially commutative monoid

[
9
,
8
,
6
,
16
]
; it also corresponds to the monoid counterpart of a
right-angled Artin-Tits group
.
Its elements, called
traces
, are sequences of actions modulo commutation of concurrent actions;
the concurrency feature of the model is obtained through the commutativity of some generators of the monoid.
Given a trace monoid
\mathcal{M}
and a set of states
X
, we assume given a right monoid action
X\times\mathcal{M}\to X
, which may be only partially defined.
The whole data defines a
concurrent system
, a model that combines the intrinsic concurrency features of trace monoids with a natural notion of state.

Combinatorial encoding.

A key point is the existence of a normal form for traces
[
6
]
.
As a result, the traces identify with the paths of some finite digraph (directed graph).
This combinatorial encoding of elements is a feature shared by all monoids with general Artin-Tits presentations
[
7
]
.
The interplay between the intrinsic properties of the concurrent system and the properties of its combinatorial encoding is one of the most interesting aspects of this theory.
We insist on that point on several occasions throughout the paper.

The probabilistic dynamics of a concurrent system is defined through a probability measure on the space of infinite traces.
Specifically, we study the notion of
Markov measure
, which must satisfy an intrinsic chain rule;
“intrinsic” meaning “without reference to the combinatorial encoding of traces”.
The corresponding dynamics of the system is
not
sequential: it is not the result of a sequence of random actions.

The mere existence of Markov measures is not obvious;
in particular, they admit natural probabilistic parameters subject to polynomial normalization conditions.

It is known that a Markov measure induces on the combinatorial encoding of traces a Markov chain dynamics, namely the
Markov chain of state-and-cliques

[
1
]
.
Yet, even for an irreducible concurrent system, the digraph that supports the representation of its trajectories may very well be non-strongly connected, and in particular it may have several final components.
This is intriguing, and it is also an obstacle to transferring in a straightforward way ergodicity results from the Markov chain of state-and-cliques (which is not ergodic in general), to the concurrent process itself (which is expected to be ergodic in some sense).

Contributions and outline of the paper.

We build on previous results on probabilistic concurrent systems; hence, the characterization of a Markov measure, the existence of the Markov chain of state-and-cliques and its characteristics are recalled without proof.

After the background on trace monoids and concurrent systems has been recalled in Section
2
, we prove in Section
3
a spectral property for irreducible concurrent systems (Theorem
3.1
).
It is combined in Section
4
with some properties of
reducible
non-negative matrices to derive information on the combinatorial encoding.
In particular, we show that the Markov chain of state-and-cliques actually lives on a sub-digraph of the encoding digraph.
Hence, some of the state-and-cliques are immaterial from the probabilistic point of view—they are called
non-stable
.
Interestingly, these non-stable state-and-cliques do not depend on the particular Markov measure considered (Corollary
4.15
).

An existence and uniqueness result on Markov measures for concurrent systems is proved (Theorem
4.4
).
Any Markov measure is shown to be the limit of Boltzmann-like probability distributions.
With the existence and uniqueness result come a series of corollaries; in particular, the Möbius matrix of the concurrent system (which extends to concurrent systems the classical notion of Möbius polynomial) has a one-dimensional kernel generated by a positive vector (Theorem
4.19
).

Building on the previous results, Section
5
is devoted to the ergodic properties of irreducible concurrent systems.
We proceed in two steps:
we first prove that shift-invariant functions are almost surely constant (Theorem
5.2
);
then we derive a Strong law of large numbers (Theorem
5.6
).
Again, a challenge is to formulate the ergodic properties in an intrinsic manner, without reference to the combinatorial representation of trajectories.
A key tool is a custom notion of stopping time, well suited for concurrent systems.

The Strong law of large numbers that we obtain is formulated through a smooth notion of convergence.
We consider
test functions
, that is, functions which are either non-decreasing and sub-additive along finite traces, or simply additive along finite traces.
The ratios with the length of traces define the ergodic means of our test functions.
We show that, for almost surely every trajectory, the ergodic means converge toward a constant, whatever the “way” of convergence of finite traces toward their limit infinite trajectory.

As an application, we introduce the notion of
speedup
for an irreducible concurrent system.
It characterizes the amount of parallelism observed on average along an infinite trajectory of the given concurrent system.
Almost surely, this quantity does not depend on the infinite trajectory.
The stationary probability distribution of the Markov chain of state-and-cliques provides a way to compute the speedup (Proposition
5.11
).

Throughout the paper we introduce small examples where all the constructions are illustrated.
In Section
6
we also introduce more elaborated examples.
The first one, in §
6.1
, proves that
the combinatorial encoding of an irreducible concurrent system can have several non-isomorphic basic components.
Finally, we explicitly determine in §
6.2
the uniform measure for a family of models which directly originate from concurrency theory.

Trace monoids and concurrent systems

2.1
Trace monoids (
[
6
,
16
,
8
,
7
]
)

Algebraic description of traces.

A
trace monoid
is a presented monoid of the form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\Sigma,I)=\langle\Sigma\,|\,\forall(a,b)\in I\quad ab=ba\rangle

where
\Sigma
is a finite set of
letters
and
I
is an irreflexive and symmetric binary relation on
\Sigma
.
Hence,
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}(\Sigma,I)
is the quotient monoid
\mathcal{M}=\Sigma^{*}/\mathcal{I}
where
\mathcal{I}
is the smallest congruence on
\Sigma^{*}
generated by pairs of the form
(ab,ba)
for
(a,b)
ranging over
I
.

\mathcal{M}
is
trivial
if
\Sigma=\emptyset
,
non-trivial
otherwise.

Let
R=(\Sigma\times\Sigma)\setminus I
.
The monoid
\mathcal{M}
is
irreducible
if the graph
(\Sigma,R)
is connected;
this is equivalent to saying that
\mathcal{M}
is not isomorphic to a direct sum of the form
\mathcal{M}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{M}_{2}
, where
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
are two non-trivial trace monoids.

Elements of
\mathcal{M}
are called
traces
.
The unit element of
\mathcal{M}
is the
empty trace
, denoted by
\varepsilon
.
The
length
of a trace
x
, denoted by
|x|
, is the length of every representative word of
x
.

The left division relation on
\mathcal{M}
is a partial order, denoted by
\leqslant
, and defined by
x\leqslant y
if and only if there exists a trace
z\in\mathcal{M}
such that
y=xz
.
Since trace monoids are left-cancellative, when this trace
z
exists, it is unique, and it is denoted by
x^{-1}y
.

Trace monoids are also right-cancellative, and therefore, when
x
right-divides
y
, the unique trace
z\in\mathcal{M}
such that
y=xz
is denoted by
yx^{-1}
.

Furthermore, both the left and right divisibility orders satisfy the two following properties:

(P1)

Every non-empty set of traces has a greatest lower bound.

(P2)

Every bounded, non-empty, finite set of traces has a least upper bound.

Combinatorial description of traces.

A
clique
of
\mathcal{M}
is a trace of the form
a_{1}\ldots a_{k}
, where the
a_{i}
s are letters such that
(a_{i},a_{j})\in I
for all
i\neq j
.
The empty trace is the only clique of length
0
, and the letters of
\Sigma
form the cliques of length
1
.
There exist cliques of length greater than
1
if and only if
\mathcal{M}
is not a free monoid.
Let
\mathcal{C}
denote the set of cliques, and
\mathfrak{C}
the set of non-empty cliques, which are both finite sets.

By construction, a clique is entirely determined by the letters it carries; since a clique
c
is the product of its letters, regardless of their order.
It is thus legitimate to identify cliques with subsets of
\Sigma
, and to consider set-theoretic operations on cliques.

A pair
(c_{1},c_{2})\in\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
is
normal
if, for every letter
b\in c_{2}
, there is a letter
a\in c_{1}
such that
(a,b)\notin I
; this is denoted by
c_{1}\to c_{2}
.
For every trace
x\in\mathcal{M}
, there exists a unique integer
n\geqslant 0
and a unique sequence of non-empty cliques
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
such that
x=c_{1}\cdots c_{n}
and
c_{i}\to c_{i+1}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
.
The sequence
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
is the
Cartier-Foata normal form
, or
normal form
for short, of
x
.
The integer
n
is the
height
of
x
, denoted by
n=0ptx
.

If
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
are two traces such that
x\leqslant y
, the normal form of
x
is not, in general, a prefix of the normal form of
y
, and the normal form of
x^{-1}y
is not a suffix of the normal form of
y
.
For instance, in
\mathcal{M}=\langle a,b,c\,|\,ab=ba\rangle
,
x=a
and
y=ab
satisfy
x\leqslant y
, but both traces have height
1
; the normal form of
x
, which if
(a)
, is not a prefix of the normal form of
y
, which is
(ab)
.

In order to characterize the relation
x\leqslant y
on the normal forms of
x
and
y
, it is useful to introduce this notion: two cliques
c
and
d
are
parallel
, which is denoted by
c\parallel d
, whenever, as subsets of
\Sigma
, they satisfy:
c\cap d=\emptyset
and
c\cup d\in\mathcal{C}
.
In this case, the monoid concatenation
cd
is then the clique
c\cup d
.

Now, if
x
and
y
are two traces with normal forms
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
and
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{p})
, respectively, then
x\leqslant y
if and only if (
[
2
, Lemma 4.1]
):
n\leqslant p
, and there exist cliques
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}
such that, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
:

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}\parallel c_{i},\ldots,c_{n}\text{\quad and\quad}d_{i}=c_{i}\gamma_{i}

(1)

As a consequence, let
x
be a trace, of height
n
.
For every trace
z
with height
0ptz\geqslant n
and with normal form
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{p})
, let
z_{n}=d_{1}\cdots d_{n}
.
Then we have:

\displaystyle x\leqslant z\iff x\leqslant z_{n}

(2)

Generalized normal form of traces and boundary at infinity of the trace monoid.

For each trace
x
with normal form
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
, let us define
c_{i}
for
i>n
by putting
c_{i}=\varepsilon
.
The infinite sequence
(c_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
thus obtained is the
generalized normal form
of
x
.
By construction, it satisfies:

1:

c_{i}\to c_{i+1}
for all
i\geqslant 1
; and

2:

there exists an integer
N
such that
c_{N}=\varepsilon
(and then, necessarily,
c_{j}=\varepsilon
for all
j\geqslant N
).

An infinite sequence of cliques
x=(c_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
satisfying
c_{i}\to c_{i+1}
for all
i\geqslant 1
is called a
generalized trace
.
We define:
C_{i}(x)=c_{i}
for all integers
i\geqslant 1
.
Consider the digraph
(\mathcal{C},\to)
, with cliques as vertices and with edge relation defined by all normal pairs of cliques.
Then each generalized trace identifies in a unique way with an infinite path in the digraph
(\mathcal{C},\to)

The set of generalized traces is denoted by
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
.
As a closed subset of the infinite product
(\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\times\cdots)
,
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
is a compact metric space.
We identify the monoid
\mathcal{M}
with a subset of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
; namely, each trace
x\in\mathcal{M}
identifies with its generalized normal form.

The set
\partial\mathcal{M}=\overline{\mathcal{M}}\setminus\mathcal{M}
is the
boundary at infinity
of
\mathcal{M}
.
Its elements are called
infinite traces
.
By contrast with traces, an infinite trace is a sequence
\omega=(c_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
of
non-empty
cliques such that
c_{i}\to c_{i+1}
for all
i\geqslant 1
.

If
x\in\mathcal{M}
and
u\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}
, we define the concatenation
xu
as follows.
Let
u=(c_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
with
c_{i}\in\mathcal{C}
for all
i\geqslant 1
.
Consider the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
defined by
x_{n}=xc_{1}\dots c_{n}
.
Let
(d_{n,i})_{i\geqslant 1}
be the generalized normal form of
x_{n}
.
For each
i\geqslant 1
, the sequence
(d_{n,i})_{n\geqslant 1}
is eventually constant, say equal to
d_{i}
, and then
d_{i}\to d_{i+1}
holds for all
i\geqslant 1
.
By definition, the concatenation
xu
is the generalized trace
(d_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
.
This extends the monoid concatenation
xu
if
u\in\mathcal{M}
.

Let
\xi,\omega\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}
.
We define a relation
\leqslant
on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
by:

\displaystyle\xi\leqslant\omega\iff\bigl(\forall n\geqslant 1\quad C_{1}(\xi)\cdots C_{n}(\xi)\leqslant C_{1}(\omega)\cdots C_{n}(\omega)\bigr)

(3)

The following proposition gathers results that mostly belong to folklore and derive essentially from the equivalence (
2
).

\bullet

Proposition

2.1—

For every generalized trace
\xi
and every integer
n\geqslant 0
, let
\xi_{n}
denote the trace of height
n
defined by
\xi_{n}=C_{1}(\xi)\cdots C_{n}(\xi)
.

1.

The relation
\leqslant
on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
defined by (
3
) is a partial ordering that extends the left-divisibility order
(\mathcal{M},\leqslant)
.

2.

If
u\in\mathcal{M}
is of height
n
and if
\xi\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}
, then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

u\leqslant\xi
;

(ii)

u\leqslant\xi_{n}
;

(iii)

there exists
\zeta\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}
such that
\xi=u\zeta
.

3.

Every bounded subset
Y
of
(\overline{\mathcal{M}},\leqslant)
has a least upper bound in
(\overline{\mathcal{M}},\leqslant)
, denoted by
\bigvee Y
.

4.

For every
\xi\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}
:
\xi=\bigvee\{x\in\mathcal{M}\,:\,x\leqslant\xi\}=\bigvee\{\xi_{n}\,:\,n\geqslant 1\}
.

If a finite trace
u
and
\xi\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}
are such that
u\leqslant\xi
, then the generalized trace
\zeta
such that
\xi=u\zeta
stated in point
2
above is unique; it is denoted by
\zeta=u^{-1}\xi
.
In general, the cliques of
\zeta
are not obtained by a translation from the cliques of
\xi
.

Combinatorics of trace monoids;
valuations.

A
valuation
on
\mathcal{M}
is a function
\lambda:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that
\lambda(xy)=\lambda(x)\lambda(y)
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
.
Valuations are in bijection with finite families of positive numbers of the form
(\lambda(a))_{a\in\Sigma}
.

A
uniform valuation
is a valuation
\lambda
such that
\lambda(a)
is constant, say equal to
t
, for
a
ranging over
\Sigma
;
hence,
\lambda(x)=t^{|x|}
.
The
counting valuation
corresponds to
t=1
.

Let
G_{\lambda}(z)
denote the generating series:

\displaystyle G_{\lambda}(z)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\lambda(x)z^{|x|}

and let
\rho_{\lambda}
be its radius of convergence.

The series
G_{\lambda}(z)
, with non-negative coefficients, is actually a rational series;
its inverse is the
\lambda
-Möbius polynomial
:

\displaystyle\mu_{\lambda}(z)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{C}}\lambda(\gamma)(-1)^{|\gamma|}z^{|\gamma|}

(4)

If
\mathcal{M}
is non-trivial, the polynomial (
4
) has a unique complex root of smallest modulus;
this root is positive and lies in
(0,1]
, and coincides with
\rho
.
Furthermore, this root is simple if
\mathcal{M}
is irreducible
[
11
,
12
]
.

Without further precision, the
Möbius polynomial

\mu(z)
of
\mathcal{M}
corresponds to the counting valuation;
it is thus given by
\mu(z)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{C}}(-1)^{|\gamma|}z^{|\gamma|}
.

Möbius transform.

The
Möbius transform
of a function
f:\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{R}
is the unique function
h:\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{R}
such that:

\displaystyle\forall\gamma\in\mathcal{C}\quad f(\gamma)=\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}\,:\,\gamma\leqslant\gamma^{\prime}}h(\gamma^{\prime})

(5)

It is given by:

\displaystyle\forall\gamma\in\mathcal{C}\quad h(\gamma)=\sum_{\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}\,:\,\gamma\leqslant\gamma^{\prime}}(-1)^{|\gamma^{\prime}|-|\gamma|}f(\gamma^{\prime})

(6)

This is a particular instance of the general notion of Möbius transform introduced by Rota
[
13
]
.

Remark 2.2

(connecting Möbius transform and Möbius polynomial)
.

Let
\lambda
be a valuation, and let
f_{z}(x)=\lambda(x)z^{|x|}
, which is also a valuation.
Let
h_{z}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{R}
be the Möbius transform of
f_{z}
.
Then the
\lambda
-Möbius polynomial (
4
) coincides with
\mu_{\lambda}(z)=h_{z}(\varepsilon)
.

Normal and visual cylinders.

Let
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
be the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
.
By definition,
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
is generated by the
normal cylinders
, of the following form:

\displaystyle\overline{C}_{x}=\{\xi\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}\,:\,C_{i}(\xi)=C_{i}(x),\ 1\leqslant i\leqslant 0ptx\}\quad(x\in\mathcal{M})

(7)

But
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
is also generated by the countable collections of
full visual cylinders
:

\displaystyle\Uparrow\!x=\{\xi\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}\,:\,x\leqslant\xi\}\quad(x\in\mathcal{M}).

Similarly, the set
\partial\mathcal{M}
is equipped with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}
, which has two natural families of generators.
On the one hand, we have the collection of
normal cylinders
:

\displaystyle C_{x}=\{\omega\in\partial\mathcal{M}\,:\,C_{i}(\omega)=C_{i}(x),\ 1\leqslant i\leqslant 0ptx\}\qquad(x\in\mathcal{M})

(8)

and on the other hand, we have the collection of
visual cylinders
:

\displaystyle\uparrow\!x=\{\omega\in\partial\mathcal{M}\,:\,x\leqslant\omega\}\qquad(x\in\mathcal{M}).

Let us justify that
\mathcal{F}=\sigma\langle\uparrow\!x,\ x\in\mathcal{M}\rangle
; the argument is similar to see that
\overline{\mathcal{F}}=\sigma\langle\Uparrow\!x,\ x\in\mathcal{M}\rangle
.
Let
x\in\mathcal{M}
, say of height
n=0ptx
, let
\omega\in\partial\mathcal{M}
and let
z=C_{1}(\omega)\ldots C_{n}(\omega)
.
Then, by Proposition
2.1
, point
2
:
x\leqslant\omega
if and only if
x\leqslant z
, and therefore:

\displaystyle\uparrow\!x

\displaystyle=\bigsqcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathcal{M}\,:\,0ptz=0ptx\\
\ x\leqslant z\end{subarray}}C_{z}

\displaystyle C_{x}=\,\uparrow\!x\setminus\Bigl(\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathcal{M}\,:\,0ptz=0ptx\\
z\leqslant x,\ z\neq x\end{subarray}}\uparrow\!z\Bigr)

(9)

Memoryless measures and probabilistic valuations.

Since the collection
\{\uparrow\!x\,:\,x\in\mathcal{M}\}\cup\{\emptyset\}
is closed under finite intersections (this is called a
\pi
-system) and generates the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}
, any probability measure
m
on
\partial\mathcal{M}
is entirely determined by the function
\lambda:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0},\quad x\mapsto m(\uparrow\!x)
.

\bullet

Definition

2.3—

A probability measure
m
on
\partial\mathcal{M}
is a
memoryless measure
if the function
\lambda:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0},\quad x\mapsto m(\uparrow\!x)
is a valuation.
Such a valuation
\lambda
is said to be
probabilistic
.

By definition, memoryless measures are thus the probability measures on
\partial\mathcal{M}
that satisfy
m\bigl(\uparrow\!(xy)\bigr)=m(\uparrow\!x)m(\uparrow\!y)
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
.
Hence, this notion extends to trace monoids the standard notion of memoryless measures on infinite words.
It is a sort of “coin tossing with partial commutations”.

Let
\lambda
be a valuation on
\mathcal{M}
, and let
h:\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{R}
be the Möbius transform of
\lambda
(more precisely, of the restriction of
\lambda
to
\mathcal{C}
).
Then
\lambda
is probabilistic if and only if (
[
2
, Th. 2.4]
):

\displaystyle h(\varepsilon)=0,\qquad\text{and\quad}\forall\gamma\in\mathfrak{C}\quad h(\gamma)\geqslant 0

(10)

In particular, there is a unique valuation which is both uniform and probabilistic.
It is given by
\lambda(x)=\rho^{|x|}
, where
\rho
is the root of smallest modulus of the Möbius polynomial
\mu(z)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{C}}(-1)^{|\gamma|}z^{|\gamma|}
.
The associated memoryless measure is the
uniform measure
on
\partial\mathcal{M}
.

Markov chain of cliques.

Let
m
be a memoryless measure on
\partial\mathcal{M}
.
Then the sequence of cliques
(C_{i}(\omega))_{i\geqslant 1}
of a random infinite trace
\omega
is a random process with values in the finite set
\mathfrak{C}
.
It happens to have a rather simple structure.
Indeed (
[
2
, Th. 2.5]
), the sequence

Concurrent systems and their trajectories.

We define a
concurrent system
as a pair
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{M},X)
where
\mathcal{M}
is a trace monoid and
X
is a finite set of
states
, together with a right monoid action
X\sqcup\{\bot\}\times\mathcal{M}\to X\sqcup\{\bot\}
where
\bot
is a special symbol not in
X
, which is a sink state for the action.
Hence, we require
\alpha\cdot\varepsilon=\alpha
,
\alpha\cdot(xy)=(\alpha\cdot x)\cdot y
and
\bot\cdot x=\bot
.

We say that
\mathcal{S}
is
trivial
if
\alpha\cdot x=\bot
for all
\alpha\in X
and
x\in\mathcal{M}
;
and that
\mathcal{S}
is
non-trivial
otherwise.

We introduce the following notations, for
(\alpha,\beta)\in X\times X
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\{x\in\mathcal{M}\,:\,\alpha\cdot x\neq\bot\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{M}_{\alpha},

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle=\{x\in\mathcal{M}\,:\,\alpha\cdot x=\beta\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\beta},

\displaystyle\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\hbox to0.0pt{$\{\omega\in\partial\mathcal{M}\,:\,\text{for all~$x\in\mathcal{M}$, if~$x\leqslant\omega$, then~$x\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}$}\}$\hss}

A pair
(\alpha,x)
such that
\alpha\in X
and
x\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
is called a
trajectory
of
\mathcal{S}
, and a pair
(\alpha,\omega)
such that
\alpha\in X
and
\omega\in\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
is called an
infinite trajectory
.

The concurrent system
\mathcal{S}
is
transitive
if
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\beta}\neq\emptyset
for every pair
(\alpha,\beta)\in X\times X
.
This is similar to the transitivity property for group actions.

\bullet

Definition

2.5—

A concurrent system
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{M},X)
is
irreducible
if the three following conditions are fulfilled:

1:

\mathcal{S}
is transitive and non-trivial;

2:

the monoid
\mathcal{M}
is irreducible;
and

3:

for every state
\alpha
and every letter
a\in\Sigma
, there exists a trace
u\in\mathcal{M}
such that
\alpha\cdot(ua)\neq\bot
.

The two first conditions are natural requirements for a notion of irreducibility;
the last condition is a
liveness
property, since it requires that every letter
a
can eventually be played starting from any state
\alpha
.
It is the spectral property stated below (Theorem
3.1
) that fully justifies that this definition is relevant.

Combinatorics of concurrent systems.

We extend the notion of valuation to concurrent systems as follows.

\bullet

Definition

2.7—

A
valuation
of a concurrent system
(\mathcal{M},X)
is a collection
\lambda=(\lambda_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in X}
of functions
\lambda_{\alpha}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
such that:

\displaystyle\forall(\alpha,x)\in X\times\mathcal{M}\quad\lambda_{\alpha}(x)>0\iff\alpha\cdot x\neq\bot

(13)

\displaystyle\forall(\alpha,x,y)\in X\times\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}\quad\lambda_{\alpha}(xy)=\lambda_{\alpha}(x)\lambda_{\alpha\cdot x}(y)

(14)

The
counting valuation
is defined by
\lambda_{\alpha}(x)=\mathbf{1}_{\{\alpha\cdot x\neq\bot\}}
.

A valuation is entirely determined by the finite collection of values
(\lambda_{\alpha}(a))_{(\alpha,a)\in X\times\Sigma}
.

To each valuation
\lambda
we attach a matrix of generating series
G
, and a
Möbius matrix

\mathsf{M}
, which is a matrix of polynomials;
both are square matrices of size
X\times X
, defined by:

\displaystyle G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\beta}}\lambda_{\alpha}(x)z^{|x|}

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}_{\alpha,\beta}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}}\lambda_{\alpha}(c)(-1)^{|c|}z^{|c|}.

Then
\mathsf{M}
is the formal inverse of
G
(
[
1
, Th. 5.6]
), hence all the entries of
G
are rational series.

Let
\rho_{\alpha,\beta}
denote the radius of convergence of the
(\alpha,\beta)
entry of
G
.
The
characteristic root
of
\mathcal{S}
(together with the valuation
\lambda
) is defined as:

\displaystyle\rho=\min_{(\alpha,\beta)\in X\times X}\rho_{\alpha,\beta}

(15)

Remark 2.8
.

If the system is transitive, then
\rho_{\alpha,\beta}
is actually independent of
(\alpha,\beta)
, hence
\rho=\rho_{\alpha,\beta}
for all pairs
(\alpha,\beta)\in X\times X
.
If, furthermore, the system is non-trivial, then
\rho<\infty
.

Markov measures and probabilistic valuations.

In the following definition, we extend to concurrent systems the notions of memoryless measure and of probabilistic valuation, defined previously for trace monoids.

\bullet

Definition

2.9—

A
Markov measure
on a concurrent system
(\mathcal{M},X)
is a collection
m=(m_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in X}
, where each
m_{\alpha}
is a probability measure on
\partial\mathcal{M}
, and such that:

\displaystyle\forall\alpha\in X\quad m_{\alpha}(\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha})=1

(16)

\displaystyle\forall(\alpha,x)\in X\times\mathcal{M}\quad x\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\iff m_{\alpha}(\uparrow\!x)>0

(17)

\displaystyle\forall(\alpha,x,y)\in X\times\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}\quad m_{\alpha}\bigl(\uparrow\!(xy)\bigr)=m_{\alpha}(\uparrow\!x)m_{\alpha\cdot x}(\uparrow\!y).

(18)

A valuation
\lambda=(\lambda_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in X}
is a
probabilistic valuation
if there exists a Markov measure
m=(m_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in X}
such that
\lambda_{\alpha}(x)=m_{\alpha}(\uparrow\!x)
for all
(\alpha,x)\in X\times\mathcal{M}
.

The conditions (
17
) and (
18
) are equivalent to saying that
\lambda_{\alpha}(x)=m_{\alpha}(\uparrow\!x)
defines a valuation on
\mathcal{S}
.

The values
m_{\alpha}(\uparrow\!a)
, for
(\alpha,a)
ranging over
X\times\Sigma
, entirely determine the corresponding valuation.
These finitely many values are the natural probabilistic parameters of
m
.
Their normalization condition is given in (
19
) below.

Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in X}
be a valuation and let
h=(h_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in X}
be its Möbius transform;
namely, each
h_{\alpha}
is the Möbius transform (see §
2.1
) of the restriction
\lambda_{\alpha}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
.
Then
\lambda
is a probabilistic valuation if and only if, for all
\alpha\in X

[
1
, Th. 4.8]
:

\displaystyle h_{\alpha}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\text{and\quad}\forall c\in\mathfrak{C}_{\alpha}\quad h_{\alpha}(c)

\displaystyle\geqslant 0

(19)

Examples.

Let
\mathcal{M}
act on a singleton set
X=\{*\}
by
*\cdot x=*
and
\bot\cdot x=\bot
for all
x\in\mathcal{M}
.
Then the probabilistic valuations for the concurrent system
(\mathcal{M},X)
correspond to the probabilistic valuations, in the sense of §
2.1
, of
\mathcal{M}
.

Markov chains can be realized as concurrent systems with respect to a free monoid
\mathcal{M}=\Sigma^{*}
.
Indeed, if
E
is the state space of a Markov chain, the corresponding concurrent systems is
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{M},X)
with
X=E
,
\mathcal{M}=E^{*}
and
\alpha\cdot(x_{1}\ldots x_{n})=x_{n}
.
Markov measures on
\mathcal{S}
correspond to the probability measures on the standard sample space associated with the Markov chain.

To continue the analysis of the toy example of concurrent system previously introduced and depicted on Figure
2
, the following is checked to define a probabilistic valuation
\lambda
; that is to say, to satisfy (

):

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \lambda_{0}(a)&=0.5&\lambda_{0}(b)&=1&\lambda_{0}(c)&=0\\
\lambda_{1}(a)&=0.5&\lambda_{1}(b)&=1&\lambda_{1}(c)&=0\\

Indeed, if

is the Möbius transform of

, one has for instance:

\displaystyle h_{0}(\varepsilon)=1-\lambda_{0}(a)-\lambda_{0}(b)-\lambda_{0}(c)+\lambda_{0}(a)\lambda_{1}(b)=0.

The Markov chain of state-and-cliques.

For trace monoids, we have seen that a memoryless measure gives rise to a Markov chain on cliques.

The analogous for a concurrent system equipped with a Markov measure
\mu=(\mu_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in X}
is a Markov chain on the
DSC
, relatively to every probability measure
\mu_{\alpha}
.
Hence, if
\alpha\in X
is the initial state, the random sequence
(\alpha_{i},C_{i+1})_{i\geqslant 0}
of state-and-cliques corresponding to trajectories of
\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
is a Markov chain, characterized as follows
[
1
, Th. 4.5]
.

Let
f
be the valuation associated to
\mu
, let
h=(h_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in X}
be the Möbius transform of
f
, and for each
\beta\in X
let
g_{\beta}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
be defined by

\displaystyle g_{\beta}(c)=\sum_{d\in\mathcal{C}_{\beta}\,:\,c\to d}h_{\beta}(d)

(21)

Then, relatively to
\mu_{\alpha}
, the initial distribution of the Markov chain of state-and-cliques is
\delta_{\{\alpha\}}\otimes h_{\alpha}
, and the transition matrix
P
is:

\displaystyle P_{(\alpha,c),(\beta,d)}=\mathbf{1}_{\{\beta=\alpha\cdot c\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{c\to d\}}\frac{h_{\beta}(d)}{g_{\alpha\cdot c}(c)}\qquad\text{if~$g_{\alpha\cdot c}(c)>0$},

(22)

whereas
P_{(\alpha,c),(\beta,d)}
is undefined if
g_{\alpha\cdot c}(c)=0
(see below).

Furthermore, for any valuation
f
with Möbius transform
h
, if one considers the function
g
defined by (
21
) and if
h_{\alpha}(\varepsilon)=0
for all
\alpha\in X
(in particular, this holds if
f
is a probabilistic valuation), then the three functions
f
,
g
and
h
are related by the following identity
[
1
, Lemma 4.7]
:

\displaystyle\forall\alpha\in X\quad\forall c\in\mathcal{C}\quad h_{\alpha}(c)=f_{\alpha}(c)\,g_{\alpha\cdot c}(c)

(23)

This has two consequences regarding the transition matrix
P
.
Firstly,
g_{\alpha\cdot c}(c)=0\iff h_{\alpha}(c)=0
, and thus if the
(\alpha,c)
line of
P
is not defined, it corresponds to an
(\alpha,c)
column of
P
filled with
0
s and to an initial state with
0
probability; hence,
P
is well defined by (
22
) as the transition matrix of a Markov chain.
Secondly, an alternative form of
P
is given by:

\displaystyle P_{(\alpha,c),(\beta,d)}=\mathbf{1}_{\{\beta=\alpha\cdot c\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{c\to d\}}f_{\alpha}(c)\frac{h_{\beta}(d)}{h_{\alpha}(c)}

(24)

\bullet

Definition

2.10—

Let
(\mathcal{M},X)
be a concurrent system equipped with a Markov measure
m
.
For each initial state
\alpha\in X
, the associated
Markov chain of state-and-cliques
is the probabilistic process
(\alpha_{i},C_{i+1})_{i\geqslant 0}
defined on the probability space
(\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha},m_{\alpha})
.
It takes its values in the

, its initial distribution is
\delta_{\{\alpha\}}\otimes h_{\alpha}
, and its transition matrix is given by (

), or equivalently by (

).

The next results in the theory of probabilistic valuations now rest upon a spectral property that we need to establish before further developments.

3
The spectral property for irreducible concurrent systems

Let
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}(\Sigma,I)
be a trace monoid.
For every letter
a\in\Sigma
, let
\Sigma^{a}=\Sigma\setminus\{a\}
and
I^{a}=I\cap(\Sigma^{a}\times\Sigma^{a})
, and let
\mathcal{M}^{a}
be the submonoid of
\mathcal{M}
generated by
\Sigma^{a}
, which is isomorphic to
\mathcal{M}(\Sigma^{a},I^{a})
.

If
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{M},X)
is a concurrent system with
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}(\Sigma,I)
, equipped with a valuation
\lambda
, and if
a
is a letter of
\Sigma
, we define
\mathcal{S}^{a}=(\mathcal{M}^{a},X)
as the restriction of the monoid action to
\mathcal{M}^{a}
.
It is equipped with the restriction of
\lambda
to
\mathcal{S}^{a}
.
Furthermore, let
\rho^{a}
be the characteristic root of
\mathcal{S}^{a}
, minimum radius of convergence of the rational series
G^{a}_{\alpha,\beta}
.

From the inclusions
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\beta}^{a}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\beta}
derives the termwise inequalities
G^{a}_{\alpha,\beta}(z)\leqslant G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)
, which in turn induces the inequality of radii:

\rho\leqslant\rho^{a}
.
The spectral property gives conditions for this inequality to be strict, which we shall see has many applications in the probabilistic theory of concurrent systems: see Theorem
4.4
, Theorem
4.19
and the results from Section
4.3
.

\bullet

Theorem

3.1—

Let
\mathcal{S}
be an irreducible concurrent system.
Then
\rho<\rho^{a}
for every letter
a
of the base alphabet of the trace monoid.

The proof uses the custom notion of link and of linking sequence.
A
link
is a trace
a_{1}\ldots a_{k}
such that:

1:

every letter of
\Sigma
is a letter
a_{i}
;
and

2:

no two consecutive letters
a_{i}
and
a_{i+1}
satisfy
(a_{i},a_{i+1})\in I
;
and

3:

a_{1}=a_{k}
.

A
linking sequence
is a trace
\tau
for which there exists a link
\ell
such that
\ell^{2}
is a (possibly scattered) subword of
\tau
.
Note that, since no trace commutation relation swaps letters of
\ell
, containing
\ell
or a power of
\ell
as a subword is indeed a trace property.

\bullet

Lemma

3.2—

For every linking sequence
\tau
and every letter
a\in\Sigma
, the function
\varphi:(u,v)\in\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}^{a}\mapsto u\tau v\in\mathcal{M}
is one-to-one.

Proof.

For each
u\in\mathcal{M}
, let
\gamma(u)
denote the largest clique of
\mathcal{M}
that right-divides
u
and let

u_{a}
denote the largest right-divisor of
u
that lies in
\mathcal{M}^{a}
, which are both well-defined thanks to Property (P
2
) of §
2.1
.

We claim that:

(†)

u_{a}
is the only trace
x\in\mathcal{M}^{a}
that right-divides
u
and such that
\gamma(ux^{-1})\in\{\varepsilon,a\}

Indeed, let
x=u_{a}
;
if
\gamma(ux^{-1})
were divisible by a letter
b\neq a
, the trace
bx
would also be a right divisor of
u
in
\mathcal{M}^{a}
:
a contradiction.
Conversely, let
x\in\mathcal{M}^{a}
be a right divisor of
u
such that
\gamma(ux^{-1})\in\{\varepsilon,a\}
.
Then
x
right-divides
u_{a}
, and if
x
were a strict right divisor of
u_{a}
, there would exist a letter
b\in\Sigma^{a}
such that
bx
right-divides
u_{a}
;
hence,
bx
would also right-divide
u
, and
b
would divide
\gamma(ux^{-1})
:
a contradiction.
The claim (†) is proved.

Now, let
x
be the least left divisor of
\tau
that contains
\ell
as a subword, which exists according to Property (P
1
) of §
2.1
, and let
z=(x^{-1}\tau)_{a}
.
We claim that, for every trace
u\in\mathcal{M}
:

\displaystyle z=(u\tau)_{a}

(25)

Let
y=x^{-1}\tau z^{-1}
.
By construction, and since
\tau
contains
\ell^{2}
as a subword,
yz
also contains
\ell
as a subword, and
y
contains an occurrence of all letters of
\ell
, including
a_{1}
, until the rightmost occurrence of
a
in
\ell
.
Furthermore, the claim (†) applied to
x^{-1}\tau
yields
\gamma(y)=a
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{M}
.
Since
x
contains an occurrence of every letter of
\Sigma
, one has
\gamma(ux)=\gamma(x)
.
In addition, if
\gamma(x)
were divisible by a letter
b\neq a_{k}
, the trace
xb^{-1}
would be a shorter left-divisor of
\tau
containing
\ell
as a subword, contradicting the definition of
x
.
It follows that
\gamma(x)=a_{k}=a_{1}
.

Hence, the well known property
\gamma(ww^{\prime})=\gamma(\gamma(w)w^{\prime})
for all traces

, yields:

\displaystyle\gamma(uxy)=\gamma(\gamma(ux)y)=\gamma(\gamma(x)y)=\gamma(a_{1}y)

(26)

But
y
contains an occurrence of
a_{1}
, hence (
26
) yields:

\gamma(uxy)=\gamma(y)=a
.
Hence,
\gamma(u\tau z^{-1})=\gamma(uxy)=a
, and then (†) yields (

).

Since (

) is true for every trace
u
, it follows that

and therefore that
(u\tau v)_{a}=\tau_{a}v
for every

.
Consequently, given a trace
\varphi(u,v)=u\tau v
, we have

and then

, which implies that

is one-to-one.
∎

Let
\rho[N,\alpha]
denote, for every subset

and every

\displaystyle\mu[N,\alpha]=\sum_{x\in N}\lambda_{\alpha}(x)t^{|x|}

We shall prove:

\displaystyle\forall a\in\Sigma\quad\forall\alpha\in X\quad\rho[\mathcal{M},\alpha]<\rho[\mathcal{M}^{a},\alpha]

(27)

\bullet

Lemma

3.3—

Let
n
be a positive integer.
For every state
\alpha\in X
, denoting by

the set of traces of length divisible by

\alpha

\rho[\mathcal{M},\alpha]=\min\{\rho[Q_{\beta},\beta]\,:\,\beta\in\alpha\cdot\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\}
.

Proof.

First, for each trace
u\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
, the inclusion
uQ_{\alpha\cdot u}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
proves that
\rho[\mathcal{M},\alpha]\leqslant\rho[Q_{\alpha\cdot u},\alpha\cdot u]
, and therefore that
\rho[\mathcal{M},\alpha]\leqslant\min\{\rho[Q_{\beta},\beta]\,:\,\beta\in\alpha\cdot\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\}
.

Then, we identify every trace
u\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
with its lexicographically minimal representation as a word
w
in
\Sigma^{*}
, and with the path
p
induced by the action of
w
on
\alpha
.
That path admits a factorization, of minimal length, as a product

\displaystyle p=p_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot p_{\ell}

where
|p_{\ell}|\leqslant n
and each path
p_{i}
, when

, is either of length
n
or is a cycle of length divisible by
n
.
Since
\ell
is minimal, any two paths

and

such that

start from distinct states, except if

and

is a cycle.
It follows that

and that

is a concatenation of up to
|X|
paths of length at most

and
|X|
cycles belonging to a set

, for pairwise distinct states
\beta
.

Setting
\Lambda=\max\{\lambda_{\beta}(a)\,:\,(\beta,a)\in X\times\Sigma\}
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mu[\mathcal{M},\alpha](t)\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{n|X|}(\Lambda t)^{k}\prod_{\beta\in\alpha\cdot\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}}\mu[Q_{\beta},\beta](t)

and therefore that
\rho[\mathcal{M},\alpha]\geqslant\min\{\rho[Q_{\beta},\beta]\,:\,\beta\in\alpha\cdot\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem

4
Probabilistic valuations

From now on, we consider a concurrent system
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{M},X)
equipped with a valuation
f=(f_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in X}
, and we assume that
\mathcal{S}
is transitive and non-trivial.

4.1
Weak convergence of Boltzmann-like distributions

\bullet

Definition

4.1—

Let
X
be a set.
A
cocycle
on
X
is a function
\Delta:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
such that
\Delta(\alpha,\gamma)=\Delta(\alpha,\beta)\Delta(\beta,\gamma)
for all
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in X
.
If
\Delta
takes only positive values, we say that
\Delta
is a
positive cocycle
.

For every positive real
r
and for every positive cocycle
\Delta
on
X
, the following defines another valuation
\widetilde{f}
on
\mathcal{S}
:

\displaystyle\forall(\alpha,x)\in X\times\mathcal{M}\quad\widetilde{f}_{\alpha}(x)=r^{|x|}\Delta(\alpha,\alpha\cdot x)f_{\alpha}(x)

(28)

We are looking for conditions on
r
and
\Delta
for the new valuation
\widetilde{f}
to be probabilistic.
This will prove the existence of probabilistic valuations, and thus of Markov measures.

For each
\alpha\in X
, let
G_{\alpha}(s)
be the generating function

G_{\alpha}(s)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}}f_{\alpha}(x)s^{|x|}
, of radius of convergence
\rho
;
and for
s\in[0,\rho)
let
\nu_{\alpha,s}
be the Boltzmann-like probability distribution on
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
defined by

\displaystyle\nu_{\alpha,s}=\frac{1}{G_{\alpha}(s)}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}}f(x)s^{|x|}\delta_{\{x\}}

(29)

\bullet

Lemma

4.2—

For each
\alpha\in X
, and as
{s\longrightarrow\rho^{-}}
, the following weak convergence holds:

\displaystyle\nu_{\alpha,s}\xrightarrow{\ \textsf{w}\ }\mu_{\alpha}

where
\mu_{\alpha}
is the probability measure on
\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
entirely characterized by :

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\quad\mu_{\alpha}(\uparrow\!x)=f_{\alpha}(x)\rho^{|x|}\Delta(\alpha,\alpha\cdot x)

(30)

and
\Delta:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is the positive cocycle given by

\displaystyle\Delta(\alpha,\beta)=\lim_{s\to\rho^{-}}\frac{G_{\beta}(s)}{G_{\alpha}(s)}

Proof.

For each pair
(\alpha,\beta)\in X\times X
and for each
s\in[0,\rho)
, define
H_{\alpha,\beta}(s)
by:

\displaystyle H_{\alpha,\beta}(s)=\frac{G_{\beta}(s)}{G_{\alpha}(s)}

which is well defined since
G_{\alpha}(s)>0
on
[0,\rho)
.
Let
x\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
be such that
\beta=\alpha\cdot x
.
Then, since
f
is a valuation and is non-negative:

\displaystyle G_{\alpha}(s)\geqslant f_{\alpha}(x)s^{|x|}G_{\beta}(s)

(31)

Since
f_{\alpha}(x)>0
, it entails that
H_{\alpha,\beta}
is bounded on
[\rho/2,\rho)
.
Since
H_{\alpha,\beta}(s)
is a rational function, it has thus a limit
\Delta(\alpha,\beta)\in[0,+\infty)
as
s\to\rho^{-}
.
But
H_{\beta,\alpha}(s)=\bigl(H_{\alpha,\beta}(s)\bigr)^{-1}
for
s\in[0,\rho)
and also has a limit
\Delta(\beta,\alpha)\in[0,+\infty)
.
This entails that
\Delta(\alpha,\beta)>0
.
So far we have proved that the cocycle
\Delta
is well defined and positive.

Since
\mathcal{M}
identifies with a subset of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
, we see the probability measures
\nu_{\alpha,s}
as defined on the compact space
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
rather than on
\mathcal{M}
.
From the definition (
29
), we derive:

\displaystyle\forall(\alpha,x)\in X\times\mathcal{M}\quad\nu_{\alpha,s}(\Uparrow\!x)=s^{|x|}f_{\alpha}(x)H_{\alpha,\alpha\cdot x}(s)

(32)

and therefore:

\displaystyle\forall(\alpha,x)\in X\times\mathcal{M}\quad\lim_{s\to\rho^{-}}\nu_{\alpha,s}(\Uparrow\!x)=f_{\alpha}(x)\rho^{|x|}\Delta(\alpha,\alpha\cdot x)

(33)

The family
\mathcal{H}=\{\emptyset\}\cup\{\Uparrow\!x,\quad x\in\mathcal{M}\}
is a
\pi
-system generating the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
;
and all the elements of
\mathcal{H}
are subsets of empty topological boundary in
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
.
Hence, by the compactness of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
on the one hand and using the Portemanteau theorem
[
5
]
on the other hand, (
33
) ensures the weak convergence of
\nu_{\alpha,s}
, as
s\to\rho^{-}
, toward a probability measure
\mu_{\alpha}
on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
satisfying:

\displaystyle\forall(\alpha,x)\in X\times\mathcal{M}\quad\mu_{\alpha}(\Uparrow\!x)=f_{\alpha}(x)\rho^{|x|}\Delta(\alpha,\alpha\cdot x)

(34)

Every singleton
\{x\}
for
x\in\mathcal{M}
is of empty topological boundary in
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
.
By the Portemanteau theorem, its measure is thus given by:

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}(\{x\})=\lim_{s\to\rho^{-}}\nu_{\alpha,s}\bigl(\{x\}\bigr)=\lim_{s\to\rho^{-}}\frac{f_{\alpha}(x)s^{|x|}}{G_{\alpha}(s)}=0

since the system is non-trivial.
Since
\mathcal{M}
is countable, we deduce first that
\mu_{\alpha}
is indeed a probability measure on
\partial\mathcal{M}
, and second that in (
34
), one may read
\uparrow\!x
rather than
\Uparrow\!x
, which is thus (
32
)

So far
\mu_{\alpha}
is a probability measure on
\partial\mathcal{M}
; it remains to see that it is concentrated on
\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
.
Since
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}
is a closed subset of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
, passing to the limit in
\nu_{\alpha,s}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha})=1
yields
\mu_{\alpha}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha})\geqslant 1
, and thus
\mu_{\alpha}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha})=1
and finally
\mu_{\alpha}(\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha})=1
since
\mu_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}_{\alpha})=0
.
∎

Discussion 4.3
.

Lemma
4.2
shows in particular that, when starting from a valuation
f
, there exists an adequate pair
(r,\Delta)
making the new valuation
\widetilde{f}_{\alpha}(x)=r^{|x|}\Delta(\alpha,\alpha\cdot x)f_{\alpha}(x)
probabilistic.
This raises two natural questions: is the pair
(r,\Delta)
unique? and
is any Markov measure the weak limit of Boltzmann-like distributions? The first question is the topic of the next section;
the second question will be given a positive answer in §
4.3
.

4.2
Stable state-and-cliques and a uniqueness result

The following result is a key step for the analysis of probabilistic valuations.

\bullet

Theorem

4.4—

Let an irreducible concurrent system
(\mathcal{M},X)
be equipped with a valuation
f
.
Then there is a unique pair
(r,\Gamma)
where
r>0
and
\Gamma:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is a positive cocycle, such that the valuation
\widetilde{f}
defined by:

\displaystyle\forall\alpha\in X\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\quad\widetilde{f}_{\alpha}(x)=r^{|x|}\Gamma(\alpha,\alpha\cdot x)f_{\alpha}(x)

(35)

is a probabilistic valuation;
this is the pair
(\rho,\Delta)
where
\rho
is the common radius of convergence of the series
G_{\alpha}(s)
, for
\alpha
ranging over
X
, and
\Delta
is the positive cocycle introduced in Lemma
4.2
.

Applying in particular Theorem
4.4
with the counting valuation yields the following definition.

\bullet

Definition

4.5—

For an irreducible concurrent system
(\mathcal{M},X)
, let
(r,\Delta)
be the unique pair where
r>0
and
\Delta:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
is a positive cocycle, such that the valuation
f_{\alpha}(x)=r^{|x|}\Delta(\alpha,\alpha\cdot x)
, is a probabilistic valuation.
The associated Markov measure is the
uniform measure
of
(\mathcal{M},X)
.

The existence part of Theorem
4.4
follows from Lemma
4.2
.
The proof of uniqueness relies on two new ingredients.
The first ingredient is some properties of reducible non-negative matrices (matrices with non-negative coefficients).
Recall that a component (or access class) of a non-negative matrix
[
14
,
4
,
15
]
is
basic
if it is of maximal spectral radius;
and that it is
final
if it does not have access to any other component.

\bullet

Definition

4.6—

A non-negative matrix
F
is an
umbrella matrix
if its basic components exactly coincide with its final components.

It is known that for a non-negative matrix
F
to be an umbrella matrix, it is necessary and sufficient that there exists a positive right eigenvector (
[
14
, § 10.3, Fact 12(b)]
).

The second ingredient is a study, based on the result of Theorem
3.1
, of state-and-cliques, in particular a characterization of those which we call
stable
.

\bullet

Definition

4.7—

Let
(\alpha,c)
be a state-and-clique (see §
2.2
).
A trace

is a

of
(\alpha,c)
if:

\displaystyle\forall z\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\quad x\leqslant z\implies C_{1}(z)=c

A state-and-clique
(\alpha,c)
is
stable
if there exists a protection of
(\alpha,c)
.

The sub-digraph of
DSC
with stable state-and-cliques as vertices will be denoted by
\text{{DSC}}^{+}
.

.

for

; but no such

On the contrary,

4.3
Universal construction of probabilistic valuations and other consequences

We now come to a series of corollaries concerning the structure and the properties of probabilistic valuations, which all derive from the spectral property of Theorem
3.1
and from the previous result (Theorem
4.4
).
Next section (§
4.4
) will be devoted to another interesting consequence of Theorem

\bullet

Corollary

4.12—

Assume that the system is irreducible and that
f
is a probabilistic valuation.
Then
\rho=1

\displaystyle\forall(\alpha,\beta)\in X\times X\quad\lim_{s\to 1^{-}}\frac{G_{\beta}(s)}{G_{\alpha}(s)}=1

Proof.

According to Lemma

also works since

Hence, by Theorem

,
\rho=1
and
\Delta=1
, which is (

∎

The next result shows that the construction of probabilistic valuations that we have studied so far, and which was introduced in Lemma

, actually covers the range of all possible probabilistic valuations, and is thus universal.

\bullet

Corollary

Assume that the concurrent system is irreducible, equipped with a Markov measure
\nu=(\nu_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in X}
.

\alpha\in X

\rho=1

\nu_{\alpha}

∎

Recall that, for any letter

and let:

\displaystyle x\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha\cdot x}^{a}=\{x\omega\,:\,\omega\in\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha\cdot x}^{a}\}

The next result shows that, after any finite trajectory, all letters will be almost surely used; this is easily shown to be false if the system is not irreducible, for instance if the underlying trace monoid itself is not irreducible.

\bullet

The next result gives a probabilistic characterization of stable state-and-cliques. An interesting point is that this characterization does not depend on the particular probabilistic valuation.

\bullet

The next result brings a probabilistic argument for the claim that the unstable state-and-cliques are an “encoding artefact”. Of course, some trajectories need them to be correctly encoded; but all together, these trajectories have probability zero to occur—independentely of the chosen Markov measure.

\bullet

Corollary

Assume that the system is irreducible.

Derives at once from Corollary

∎

4.4

Given a valuation

\Delta

\widetilde{f}

Recall that

\mathsf{M}_{\alpha,\beta}(t)=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{\alpha,\beta}}f_{\alpha}(c)(-1)^{|c|}t^{|c|}

\displaystyle\theta(t)=\det\mathsf{M}(t)

In the next result, we prove in particular that the kernel of the Möbius matrix

evaluated at the root
\rho

\Delta(\alpha,\beta)=U_{\beta}/U_{\alpha}

(‡)

5
Ergodic properties of irreducible concurrent systems

5.1
An ergodic property

We now come to the study of the ergodic properties of irreducible concurrent systems.
Since the Markov chain of state-and-cliques is not ergodic in general, as its digraph may not be strongly connected, the ergodic properties must rely on the intrinsic concurrent systems itself, and not on its combinatorial representation.

At the scale of the intrinsic system, there is however no global time available.
Henceforth the standard formulation of ergodicity does not apply in a straightforward way, whence the following definition.

\bullet

Definition

5.1—

Let
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{M},X)
be a concurrent system equipped with a Markov measure
\nu
, and let
Z=(Z_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in X}
be a family of measurable functions
Z_{\alpha}:\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\to X
.
It is
shift-invariant
if for all
\alpha\in X
and for
\nu_{\alpha}
-
a.s.
every
\omega\in\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
:

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\quad x\leqslant\omega\implies Z_{\alpha}(\omega)=Z_{\alpha\cdot x}(x^{-1}\omega)

(50)

where
x^{-1}\omega
is the unique
\xi\in\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha\cdot x}
such that
\omega=x\xi
.

\bullet

Theorem

5.2—

Let
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{M},X)
be an irreducible concurrent system equipped with a Markov measure
\nu
.
If
Z=(Z_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in X}
is a family of non-negative or bounded functions, and if
Z
is shift-invariant, then there is a constant
z
such that
Z_{\alpha}(\omega)=z
for all
\alpha\in X
and for
\nu_{\alpha}
-
a.s.
every
\omega\in\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

The proof proceeds in three parts.
We first establish a theory of stopping times for concurrent systems, yielding to a Strong Markov property for concurrent systems.
The second part introduces hitting times and a new Markov chain attached to the irreducible concurrent system.
The last part applies these results to conclude the proof.

First part:
stopping times and the Strong Markov property.
Define a
stopping time
as a family
T=(T_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in X}
of mappings
T_{\alpha}:\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\to\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}
such that, for all
\alpha\in X
:

\displaystyle\forall\omega\in\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\quad T_{\alpha}(\omega)\leqslant\omega

(51)

\displaystyle\forall\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\quad T_{\alpha}(\omega)\leqslant\omega^{\prime}\implies T_{\alpha}(\omega^{\prime})=T_{\alpha}(\omega)

(52)

We say that
T
is
\nu
-
a.s.
finite if
T_{\alpha}(\omega)\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
for
\nu_{\alpha}
-
a.s.
every
\omega\in\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
.

If
T
is
\nu
-
a.s.
finite, the
shift operator
associated to
T
is the family of mappings
\theta_{T}
defined, for
\alpha\in X
and
\omega\in\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
, by
\theta_{T}(\omega)=(T(\omega))^{-1}\omega
, where the dependence to
\alpha
is dropped to shorten the notation.

If
S
and
T
are two stopping times, the stopping time
S\circ T
is defined, for
\alpha\in X
, on
\{T_{\alpha}(\omega)\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\}
, by:

\displaystyle(S\circ T)_{\alpha}(\omega)=T_{\alpha}(\omega)S_{\alpha\cdot T(\omega)}\bigl(\theta_{T}(\omega)\bigr)

(53)

In particular, the iterates
T^{n}
of a stopping time
T
are defined, for all
n\geqslant 0
, by:

\displaystyle T^{0}=\varepsilon,\qquad\forall n\geqslant 0\quad T^{n+1}=T\circ T^{n}

(54)

If
T
is a stopping time, let
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}=\{T_{\alpha}(\omega)\,:\,\omega\in\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\}
.
The family of
\sigma
-algebras associated with
T
is the family
\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in X}
where
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the at most countable collection of visual cylinders of the form
\uparrow\!x
, for
x
ranging over
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}\cap\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
.

The
Strong Markov property
then states as follows:
if
Z=(Z_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in X}
is a collection of mappings
Z_{\alpha}\to\mathbb{R}
, either bounded or non-negative, and if
T
is a stopping time, then:

\displaystyle\forall\alpha\in X\quad\text{$\nu_{\alpha}$-\text{a.s.}\quad}\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}(Z_{\alpha\cdot T_{\alpha}}\circ T_{\alpha}|\mathcal{F}_{\alpha})=\mathbb{E}_{T_{\alpha}}(Z_{\alpha\cdot T_{\alpha}})

(55)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}
denotes the expectation with respect to
\nu_{\alpha}
, and where both members are conventionally
0
where
T_{\alpha}
is not finite.
The proof is straightforward from the definitions, hence we omit it.

Second part:
hitting times.
In Markov chain theory, one is accustomed with the notion of hitting time of a state.
Here, there is no natural notion of “first hitting time of a state”;
for, if
x,y\leqslant\omega
are such that
\alpha\cdot x=\beta
and
\alpha\cdot y=\beta
, nothing ensures that
x\wedge y
would satisfy
\alpha\cdot(x\wedge y)=\beta
.
Hence, there is no well-defined notion of “first hitting” a given state.
By contrast, hitting a letter of the base alphabet of the monoid is a well-defined notion.

Let
a\in\Sigma
.
For
\omega\in\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
, if there is at least some
x\leqslant\omega
with an occurrence of
a
, then we define the first hitting time of
a
by:

\displaystyle T^{a}_{\alpha}(\omega)=\min\{x\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\,:\,x\leqslant\omega\text{ and }|x|_{a}>0\}

(56)

where
|x|_{a}
denotes the number of occurrences of
a
in
x
, which is indeed a trace quantity;
Then
T^{a}=(T^{a}_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in X}
is a stopping time in the sense of (
51
) and (
52
).

\bullet

Lemma

5.3—

Assume that
\mathcal{S}
is irreducible and equipped with a Markov measure
\nu
.
For any
a\in\Sigma
,
T^{a}
and all its iterates
(T^{a})^{n}
are
\nu
-
a.s.
finite, and:

\displaystyle\forall\alpha\in X\quad\forall k\geqslant 0\quad\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}\bigl[|T^{a}_{\alpha}|^{k}\bigr]<\infty

Furthermore, if
\mathcal{F}^{n}
is the
\sigma
-algebra associated with
(T^{a})^{n}
, then:

\displaystyle\bigvee_{n\geqslant 0}\mathcal{F}^{n}=\mathcal{F}

(57)

And for every
\alpha\in X
and for
\nu_{\alpha}
-
a.s.
every
\omega\in\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
:

\displaystyle\omega=\bigvee\bigl\{(T^{a})^{n}(\omega)\,:\,n\geqslant 0\bigr\}

(58)

Proof.

If
T_{\alpha}^{a}(\omega)\notin\mathcal{M}
then
a
does not occur in
\omega
.
Hence,
\{T_{\alpha}^{a}\notin\mathcal{M}\}\subseteq\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{a}
, but
\nu_{\alpha}(\partial\mathcal{M}^{a})=0
by Corollary
4.14
, hence
T_{\alpha}^{a}
is
\nu_{\alpha}
-
a.s.
finite.
It follows by induction, and with the Strong Markov property (
55
), that all the iterates
(T_{\alpha}^{a})^{n}
are
\nu_{\alpha}
-
a.s.
finite as well.

Let
\alpha\in X
, and let
\Omega\subseteq\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
such that
\nu_{\alpha}(\Omega)=1
, and
T_{\alpha}^{a}
and all its iterates are finite on
\Omega
.
Define
\mathcal{T}^{a}_{\alpha}=\{T^{a}_{\alpha}(\omega)\,:\,\omega\in\Omega\}
, the set of finite values of
T_{\alpha}^{a}
on
\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
.
Then the stopping time properties (
51
) and (
52
) imply that
\Omega
can be written as the disjoint union:

\displaystyle\Omega=\bigsqcup_{x\in\mathcal{T}^{a}_{\alpha}}\uparrow\!x

Henceforth, for every integer
k\geqslant 0
, the
k^{\text{th}}
moment of
|T^{a}_{\alpha}|
is:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}\bigl[|T^{a}_{\alpha}|^{k}\bigr]=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}^{a}_{\alpha}}{|x|}^{k}f_{\alpha}(x)

(59)

where
f=(f_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in X}
is the probabilistic valuation associated to the Markov measure
\nu
.
Every
x\in\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}^{a}
writes as
x=ya
with
y\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{a}
.
Therefore (
59
) implies:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}\bigl[|T^{a}_{\alpha}|^{k}\bigr]\leqslant\sum_{y\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{a}}(1+|y|)^{k}f_{\alpha}(y)<\infty

Indeed, the series converges by the following argument:

1:

the radius of convergence of the series
\sum_{y\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}}s^{|y|}f_{\alpha}(y)
is
1
by Corollary
4.12
; and

2:

the radius of convergence of the series
\sum_{y\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}^{a}}s^{|y|}f_{\alpha}(y)
is thus greater than
1
, thanks to Theorem
3.1
.

We now prove (
58
).
For
\omega\in\Omega
, let
\xi(\omega)=\bigvee_{n\geqslant 0}(T_{\alpha}^{a})^{n}(\omega)
, which is well defined thanks to Proposition
2.1
, point
3
.
We wish to prove that
\xi=\omega
for
\nu_{\alpha}
-
a.s.
every
\omega\in\Omega
.
Let
N=\inf\{n\geqslant 0\,:\,C_{n}(\omega)\neq C_{n}(\xi)\}
.
Then
N<\infty
on
\{\xi\neq\omega\}
, and there is a letter
b\in C_{N}(\omega)
such that
b\notin C_{N}(\xi)
.
We claim that:

b\notin C_{n}(\xi)
for all
n\geqslant N

(60)

Indeed, let
n\geqslant N
.
Referring to the definition (
3
) of
\xi\leqslant\omega
, one has
C_{1}(\xi)\cdots C_{n}(\xi)\leqslant C_{1}(\omega)\cdots C_{n}(\omega)
, and thus:

\displaystyle C_{N}(\xi)\cdots C_{n}(\xi)\leqslant C_{N}(\omega)\cdots C_{n}(\omega)

From the characterization (
1
) of the order
(\mathcal{M},\leqslant)
follows the implication
b\notin C_{N}(\omega)\implies b\notin C_{N+1}(\omega),\ldots,C_{n}(\omega)
, which proves (
60
).
Consequently:

\displaystyle\{\xi\neq\omega\}\subseteq\bigcup_{(x,b)\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\times\Sigma}x\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha\cdot x}^{b}

a countable collection of
\nu_{\alpha}
-null sets according to Corollary
4.14
, and thus
\xi=\omega
,
\nu_{\alpha}
-
a.s.
, which proves (
58
).

Finally, to prove (
57
), let
x\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
.
Define the stopping time
S_{\alpha}
by

\displaystyle R_{\alpha}(\omega)=(T^{a})^{N(\omega)}(\omega),\quad N(\omega)=\inf\{n\geqslant 0\,:\,x\leqslant(T^{a})^{n}(\omega)\}

if
N(\omega)<\infty
, and by
R_{\alpha}(\omega)=\omega
otherwise.
Then (
58
) shows that
N(\omega)<\infty
for
\nu_{\alpha}
-
a.s.
every
\omega\in\uparrow\!x
.
Therefore
\uparrow\!x
can be written as a countable and disjoint union of the form:

\displaystyle\uparrow\!x=\bigsqcup_{y\in\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}^{a}}\uparrow\!y

where
\mathcal{R}^{a}_{\alpha}=\{R_{\alpha}(\omega)\,:\,\omega\in\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha},\quad N(\omega)<\infty\}
.
Hence,
\uparrow\!x\in\bigvee_{n\geqslant 0}\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}^{n}
and thus
\mathcal{F}=\bigvee_{n\geqslant 0}\mathcal{F}^{n}
since
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}=\sigma\langle\uparrow\!x\,:\,x\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\rangle
.

∎

We now introduce a new Markov chain induced by the system.
With the letter
a\in\Sigma
still fixed, pick an initial state
\alpha\in X
and let
(X_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
be defined by:

\displaystyle\forall n\geqslant 0\quad X_{n}=\alpha\cdot(T^{a})^{n}

(61)

which is
\nu_{\alpha}
-
a.s.
well defined.
In particular
X_{0}=\alpha
, and then
X_{n}
is the state reached by the process at its
n^{\text{th}}
hitting of the letter
a
.

\bullet

Lemma

5.4—

The process
(X_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
defined by (
61
) is a Markov chain with a unique closed class.

Proof.

For
\alpha,\beta\in X
, let
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}^{\beta}=\{T^{a}(\omega)\,:\,\omega\in\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha},\ \alpha\cdot T^{a}(\omega)=\beta\}
.
For any integer
n\geqslant 0
and any sequence
x_{0}=\alpha
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}\in X
, we have:

\displaystyle\nu_{\alpha}(X_{0}=x_{0},\ldots,X_{n+1}=x_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{x_{i-1}}^{x_{i}},\;1\leqslant i\leqslant n+1\end{subarray}}\nu_{\alpha}\bigl(\uparrow\!(t_{1}\cdots t_{n+1})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{x_{i-1}}^{x_{i}},\;1\leqslant i\leqslant n+1\end{subarray}}\nu_{\alpha}\bigl(\uparrow\!(t_{1}\cdots t_{n})\bigr)\nu_{x_{n}}(\uparrow\!t_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\nu_{\alpha}(X_{0}=x_{0},\ldots,X_{n}=x_{n})\nu_{x_{n}}(X_{1}=x_{n+1})

which proves that
(X_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
is a Markov chain, with transition probability from
\alpha
to
\beta
given by
\nu_{\alpha}(X_{1}=\beta)=\nu_{\alpha}(\alpha\cdot T_{\alpha}^{a}=\beta)
.

To prove that the chain
(X_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
has a unique closed class, it is enough to prove the following confluence property:
for every two states
\alpha,\beta\in X
, there is a state
\gamma\in X
such that both
\alpha
and
\beta
lead to
\gamma
in the graph of the chain.
Indeed, let
x\in\mathcal{M}_{\beta}
such that
\beta\cdot x=\alpha
.
It follows from (
58
) that there is a trace
y\in\mathcal{M}_{\beta}
of the form
y=(T^{a})^{n}
such that
x\leqslant y
.
And then
\gamma=\beta\cdot y
is suitable since
x^{-1}y\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
is also of the form
(T^{a})^{m}
.
∎

\bullet

Corollary

5.5—

For every letter
a\in\Sigma
, there exists at least a state
\alpha_{0}\in X
such that, for every
\alpha\in X
and for
\nu_{\alpha}
-
a.s.
every
\omega\in\partial\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
, the sequence
((T^{a})^{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
satisfies
\alpha\cdot(T^{a})^{n}=\alpha_{0}
for infinitely many

.

Proof.

The Markov chain
(X_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
associated to
a
has a unique closed class according to Lemma
5.4
.
Then any state
\alpha_{0}
of this class is recurrent and is reached infinitely often by
a.s.
every trajectory, hence it is suitable.
∎

Third part:
conclusion.
Assume that
Z=(Z_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in X}
is a shift-invariant family of non-negative or bounded random variables.
Pick
a\in\Sigma
an arbitrary letter and let
((T^{a})^{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
be the sequence of iterated hitting times of
a
.
Let also
\alpha_{0}\in X
be chosen according to Corollary
5.5
, and introduce the stopping time

\displaystyle S(\omega)=(T^{a})^{N(\omega)}(\omega)\qquad\text{with }N(\omega)=\inf\{n\geqslant 0\,:\,\alpha\cdot(T^{a})^{n}(\omega)=\alpha_{0}\}

It follows from Corollary
5.5
that
S
and all its iterates
S^{n}
are
\nu_{\alpha}
-
a.s.
finite.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{n}
be the
\sigma
-algebra associated to the stopping time
S^{n}
.
By the Strong Markov property (

) on the one hand, and by using the shift invariance of

on the other hand, one has:

\displaystyle\text{$\nu_{\alpha}$-\text{a.s.}}\quad\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}(Z_{\alpha}|\mathcal{G}_{n})=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha\cdot S^{n}}(Z_{\alpha\cdot S^{n}})

(62)

And since
\alpha\cdot S^{n}=\alpha_{0}
by construction of

, we obtain
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}(Z_{\alpha}|\mathcal{G}_{n})=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha_{0}}(Z_{\alpha_{0}})
.
Since

as a consequence of (

\nu_{\alpha}

5.2
A Strong Law of Large Numbers

Let us call a
test function
any family of functions
\varphi=(\varphi_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in X}
with
\varphi_{\alpha}:\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\to\mathbb{R}
such that, either:

1.

\varphi
is additive, meaning:
\forall\alpha\in X\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\quad\forall y\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha\cdot x}\quad\varphi_{\alpha}(xy)=\varphi_{\alpha}(x)+\varphi_{\alpha\cdot x}(y)
;
or

2.

\varphi
is non-negative, non-decreasing and sub-additive, the latter meaning, for all
\alpha\in X
,
x\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
and
y\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha\cdot x}
:

\displaystyle\varphi_{\alpha}(x)\leqslant\varphi_{\alpha}(xy)\leqslant\varphi_{\alpha}(x)+\varphi_{\alpha\cdot x}(y)

Any additive function
\varphi
is entirely determined by the values
\varphi_{\alpha}(a)
for
\alpha\in X
and
a\in\Sigma
such that
\alpha\cdot a\neq\bot
, and provided that
\varphi_{\alpha}(a)+\varphi_{\alpha\cdot a}(b)=\varphi_{\alpha}(b)+\varphi_{\alpha\cdot b}(a)
whenever
ab\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
and
ab=ba
.
An interesting example of a sub-additive function is the height function
\varphi_{\alpha}(x)=0ptx
.

The
ergodic means
associated to a test function
\varphi
are defined, for any non-empty trace
x\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
, by:

\displaystyle M\varphi(x)=\frac{\varphi_{\alpha}(x)}{|x|}

We say that the ergodic means associated to a test function
\varphi

converge
a.s.
toward
L=(L_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in X}
if, for every
\alpha\in X
:

\displaystyle\text{$\nu_{\alpha}$-\text{a.s.}}\quad\lim_{x\in\mathcal{M},\ x\to\omega}M\varphi(x)=L_{\alpha}(\omega)

To make the definition of the limit explicit:

\displaystyle\forall\varepsilon>0\quad\exists x\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\quad\forall y\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\quad(x\leqslant y\leqslant\omega\implies|M\varphi(y)-L_{\alpha}(\omega)|<\varepsilon)

(63)

In particular, for every sequence
(x_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
of traces converging in
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
toward
\omega
, one has
M\varphi(x_{n})\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}L_{\alpha}(\omega)
.
Indeed, for a given
\varepsilon>0
and
x\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
as in (
63
), there exists an integer
N
such that
x\leqslant x_{n}
for all
n\geqslant N
.

\bullet

Theorem

5.6—

Assume that the concurrent system is irreducible and equipped with a Markov measure
\nu
.

1.

For every test function
\varphi
, there is a constant
k_{\varphi}
such that the ergodic means
M\varphi
converge
a.s.
toward the constant
L=(L_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in X}
where
L_{\alpha}=k_{\varphi}
for all
\alpha\in X
.

2.

If
\varphi
is additive, the limit
k_{\varphi}
can be computed as follows.
Let
J
be any final component of
\text{{DSC}}^{+}
.
Let
Q
be the restriction to
J
of the transition matrix of the Markov chain of state-and-cliques, and let
\pi_{J}
be the unique invariant probability distribution of
Q
.
Then:

\displaystyle k_{\varphi}=\Bigl(\sum_{(\alpha,c)\in J}\pi_{J}(\alpha,c)\,|c|\Bigr)^{-1}\sum_{(\alpha,c)\in J}\pi_{J}(\alpha,c)\varphi_{\alpha}(c)

(64)

We first establish three lemmas.

\bullet

Lemma

5.7—

Let
\varphi
be a test function.
For any letter
a\in\Sigma
, there is a constant

6.2
Doubled trace monoids

The structure of a given concurrent system can give short ways of describing the uniform measure.
Doubled trace monoids are an example of this kind.

Consider an irreducible trace monoid
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}(\Sigma,I)
, put
X=\mathcal{C}
the set of cliques of
\mathcal{M}
, and define a mapping
X\times\Sigma\to X\cup\{\bot\},\quad(\gamma,a)\mapsto\gamma\cdot a
as follows:

\displaystyle\gamma\cdot a=\begin{cases}\gamma\cup\{a\},&\text{if~$a\notin\gamma$ and~$\gamma\cup\{a\}\in\mathcal{C}$}\\
\gamma\setminus\{a\},&\text{if~$a\in\gamma$}\\
\bot,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

This mapping extends in a unique way to an action
X\times\mathcal{M}\to X\cup\{\bot\}
making
(\mathcal{M},X)
an irreducible concurrent system.

For instance, if
\Sigma=\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{4}\}
with
a_{i}a_{j}=a_{j}a_{i}
when
i-j\neq\pm 1\mod 5
, the concurrent system corresponds to the so-called “five dining philosophers” model introduced by Dijkstra in concurrency theory
[
10
]
.

The probabilistic valuation
f_{\alpha}(x)=\rho^{|x|}\Delta(\alpha,\alpha\cdot x)
associated with the uniform measure has a simple expression for these models:

(†)

Let
r
be the root of smallest modulus of the Möbius polynomial of the monoid
\mathcal{M}
.
Then
\rho=\sqrt{r}
and
\Delta(\alpha,\beta)=r^{\frac{1}{2}(|\beta|-|\alpha|)}
.

Proof.

Let
\nu
be the uniform measure of the concurrent system, and let
f
be the corresponding probabilistic valuation.
Let
\gamma\in X
, hence
\gamma
is a clique of
\mathcal{M}
.
It follows from Corollary
4.14
that
\nu_{\gamma}(\uparrow\!\gamma)=1
;
indeed, otherwise some letter would be never played.
Therefore
f_{\gamma}(\gamma)=1
, and since
\gamma\cdot\gamma=\varepsilon
in the action, it yields
\rho^{|\gamma|}\Delta(\gamma,\varepsilon)=1
and therefore, using the cocycle property of
\Delta
:

\Delta(\varepsilon,\gamma)=\rho^{|\gamma|}
, and finally
\Delta(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})=\rho^{|\gamma^{\prime}|-|\gamma|}
.
As a result,
f_{\varepsilon}(\gamma)=\rho^{2|\gamma|}
.

It suffices now to prove that
\rho=\sqrt{r}
.
Let
\psi
be the Möbius transform of the uniform valuation
\varphi(x)=\rho^{2|x|}
of the monoid
\mathcal{M}
.
Given the form already found for the cocycle
\Delta
, the condition (
19
) for
f
being probabilistic, used at state
\varepsilon
, shows that
\rho^{2}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\psi(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall\gamma\in\mathfrak{C}\quad\psi(\gamma)

\displaystyle>0

But only the root of smallest modulus
r
has this property (short proof:
by the uniqueness of Theorem
4.4
, since it allows to construct a uniform measure on
\partial\mathcal{M}
).
Hence,
r=\rho^{2}
, which completes the proof of (†).
