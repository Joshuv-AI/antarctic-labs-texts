A canonical approach to quantum fluctuations
Joanna Ruhl, Vanja Dunjko, Maxim Olshanii

Abstract
We present a canonical formalism for computing quantum fluctuations of certain discrete degrees of freedom in systems governed by integrable partial differential equations with known Hamiltonian structure, provided these models are classical-field approximations of underlying many-body quantum systems. We then apply the formalism to both the 2-soliton and 3-soliton breather solutions of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation, assuming the breathers are created from an initial elementary soliton by quenching the coupling constant. In particular, we compute the immediate post-quench quantum fluctuations in the positions, velocities, norms, and phases of the constituent solitons. For each case, we consider both the white-noise and correlated-noise models for the fluctuation vacuum state. Unlike previous treatments of the problem, our method allows for analytic solutions. Additionally, in the correlated-noise case, we consider the particle-number-conserving (also called U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) -symmetry-conserving) Bogoliubov modes, i.e., modes with the proper correction to preserve the total particle number. We find that in most (but not all) cases, these corrections do not change the final result.



I
Introduction

The study of integrable systems is important both conceptually—because they are exactly solvable through methods such as inverse scattering
Gardner et al. 1967
;
Ablowitz et al. 1973
;
Ablowitz and Segur 1981
;
Ablowitz et al. 1974
;
Novikov et al. 1984
;
Lax 1968
—and experimentally, because many of these models can be realized in the laboratory. Indeed, some even apply to naturally occurring phenomena. The best-known examples include the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation, which describes waves on the surface of shallow water and supports soliton solutions
Zabusky and Kruskal 1965
; the sine-Gordon equation, which models long Josephson junctions
Mazo and Ustinov 2014
; and the nonlinear Schroödinger equation (NLSE), which has numerous applications
Malomed 2005
, including in nonlinear optics
Hasegawa and Tappert 1973
;
Haus and Wong 1996
;
Lai and Haus 1989a
;
Lai and Haus 1989b
and Bose-Einstein condensates (BECs)
Strecker et al. 2003
;
Khaykovich et al. 2002a
.

An atomic gas forms a BEC when it is cooled to such an extent that the de Broglie wavelength of the atoms becomes comparable to the interparticle distance. At the level of the Hartree-Fock approximation, a macroscopic number of atoms then occupy the single quantum state of lowest energy
Leggett 2001a
. This fact necessitates a quantum description for accurate modeling of the system, despite the condensate being a macroscopic object.

The classical NLSE provides only a mean-field description of BECs, and thus cannot account for quantum fluctuations on its own. A widely used method for describing small quantum excitations is the linearization method proposed by Bogoliubov for superfluid quantum liquids
Bogolubov 1947
and later applied extensively to ultracold gases
Leggett 2001a
. Eventually, it was applied to fundamental solitons in optical fibers
Haus and Lai 1990
;
Lai 1993
and then to center-of-mass degrees of freedom in soliton breathers
Yeang 1999
.

More recently, we and collaborators applied it to the constituent solitons of NLSE 2-soliton breathers
Marchukov et al. 2020
. The approach taken in that work relied on intermediate calculations of so-called quasi-inner products. That method, however, is computationally expensive, and in several cases the calculations could only be completed numerically.

Here we present a canonical formalism for the calculation of quantum fluctuations in systems which, in addition to continuous degrees of freedom, also have discrete ones, and in which it is these discrete degrees of freedom that are of principal interest. Importantly, the system should have a Hamiltonian structure with a canonical system of coordinates that includes, in addition to continuous variables, also a certain number of discrete variables. These should correspond to the discrete degrees of freedom of interest.

The principal difference between the present work and that of
Marchukov et al. 2020
is in the classical part of the problem. As in
Marchukov et al. 2020
, we also need to model the source of quantum fluctuations; and as in that work, we consider two such models: one with simple white noise, and a more realistic one with a type of correlated noise. Having said that, we have improved the evaluation of the field fluctuations due to correlated vacuum in two respects: (i) Following
Castin 2009
;
Gardiner 1997
;
Castin and Dum 1998
;
Sinatra et al. 2007
, we use the particle-number-conserving Bogoliubov modes, which include additional correction terms compared to those obtained in the standard
U(1)
-symmetry-breaking approach. Surprisingly, it turned out that most final results are not affected by this, which we discuss further in Appendix
A
. (ii) We have significantly streamlined the computation of these field fluctuations.

In Sec.
III
, we use this formalism to exactly calculate the quantum fluctuations in the NLSE model for the 2- and 3-soliton breathers.

II
Canonical treatment of relationships between fluctuations

A standard strategy for deriving equations valid for a continuous system is to first obtain the corresponding equations for a related discrete system and then take the continuum limit; see, e.g., Ch. 13 of
Goldstein et al. 2002
or Sec. 2.1 of
Sterman 1993
. We will adopt this approach as well.

II.1
Discrete coordinates

II.1.1
Quantities of interest: fluctuations

Let
\{q_{i}
,
p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
\{Q_{i}
,
P_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
be two sets of canonical coordinates and their conjugate momenta, related by a canonical transformation. As is customary, we will sometimes refer to these as the ‘old’ and ‘new’ sets, or denote them by
(q,\,p)
and
(Q,\,P)
, respectively. For simplicity, we will assume that all canonical transformations are restricted, i.e., without explicit time dependence, although this restriction is not essential.

For any quantity
x

\delta x
denote any kind of small change in
x
. For us, these changes will come from quantum fluctuations, but, for now, they could also come from classical stochastic fluctuations or any other kind of perturbation. We write
x=x_{0}+\delta x
, where
x_{0}
is the unperturbed value of
x
.

In the context of the Bogoliubov decomposition (see Sec.
II.3
), the perturbation will be a quantum operator,
\delta\hat{x}
. We will also assume this operator to be ‘small’, in the sense that in any expression, only the lowest-order terms in
\delta\hat{x}
need be retained.

We will be interested in the fluctuations in the ‘new’ variables. Expanding to first order in the fluctuations of the ‘old’ variables yields:

\displaystyle\delta Q_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\partialderivative{Q_i}{q_j}\delta q_{j}+\partialderivative{Q_i}{p_j}\delta p_{j}\right),

(1)

\displaystyle\delta P_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\partialderivative{P_i}{q_j}\delta q_{j}+\partialderivative{P_i}{p_j}\delta p_{j}\right).

In the partial derivatives above, each
Q_{i}
and
P_{i}
is regarded as a function of all the ‘old’ variables
(q,\,p)
, as given by the canonical transformation. Accordingly, each partial derivative is itself a function of the variables
(q,\,p)
, and is to be evaluated at the unperturbed values
(q_{j})_{0}
and
(p_{j})_{0}
for all
j=1,\,\ldots,\,N
. Here
q_{j}=(q_{j})_{0}+\delta q_{j}
and
p_{j}=(p_{j})_{0}+\delta p_{j}
.

As it turns out, in our case we will face the following difficulty: the partial derivatives above will be difficult to evaluate because it will be difficult to derive explicit expressions for the
(Q,\,P)
-variables as functions of the
(q,\,p)
-variables. On the other hand, the inverse relationships—i.e., the expressions for the variables
(q,\,p)
in terms of the variables
(Q,\,P)
—will be known explicitly. Therefore, we need to ‘invert’ the partial derivatives in Eq. (
1
), so that the derivatives of
Q_{i}
and
P_{i}
with respect to
q_{j}
and
p_{j}
are expressed in terms of the derivatives of
q_{j}
and
p_{j}
with respect to
Q_{i}
and
P_{i}
.

For a general change of variables, this can always be done at least in principle, by finding the inverse of the Jacobian matrix of the transformation. However, for large values of
N
, the resulting expressions are generally unwieldy: usually, the best one can do is to write each ‘inverted’ partial derivative as the ratio of a cofactor of the Jacobian matrix and the Jacobian determinant. Fortunately, the fact that our transformation is canonical simplifies matters dramatically, as we will now recall.

II.1.3
Lagrange brackets

Because we will be employing the ‘inverted’ partial derivatives, it is the Lagrange brackets, rather than the Poisson brackets, that will naturally appear in our calculations (for example, in the derivations of Eqs. (
30
) and (
31
)). We now briefly review their definition, following Sec. 9.5 of
Goldstein et al. 2002
. Let
\{q_{i},\,p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, or
(q,\,p)
for short, be a set of canonical coordinates and their conjugate momenta. Let
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2N}
be a set of
2N
independent functions of the variables
(q,\,p)
. We assume that these functions define an invertible change of variables,
y_{i}=y_{i}(q_{1},\,\ldots,\,q_{N},\,p_{1},\,\ldots,\,p_{N})
. This change of variables is not assumed to be canonical (though it may be). The inverse transformation expresses each
q_{i}
and
p_{i}
as a function of
y_{1},\,\ldots,\,y_{2N}
. To simplify notation, let us introduce
u
and
v
as two of the
y
-variables, e.g.,
u=y_{\alpha}
and
v=y_{\beta}
. Then the Lagrange bracket of
u
and
u
with respect to the variables
(q,\,p)
is defined as

\{u,\,v\}_{(q,\,p)}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\partialderivative{q_k}{u}\partialderivative{p_k}{v}-\partialderivative{p_k}{u}\partialderivative{q_k}{v}\right)\,.

(3)

Like the Poisson bracket, the Lagrange bracket is a canonical invariant. Thus, the subscript
(q,\,p)
can be omitted and we can write
\{u,\,v\}
without ambiguity.

Next, suppose that the two functions
y_{\alpha}
and
y_{\beta}
(which define
u
and
v
) are trivial, so that each of
u
and
v
is simply one of the original
(q,\,p)
variables. There are three possibilities: both
u
and
v
can be
q
’s, or both can be
p
’s, or one can be a
q
and the other a
p
. Plugging each case into Eq. (
3
), we obtain the fundamental Lagrange brackets:

\displaystyle\{q_{i},\,q_{j}\}=\{p_{i},\,p_{j}\}=0,

(4)

\displaystyle\{q_{i},\,p_{j}\}=\delta_{ij}\,.

As we have seen, the properties of the Lagrange bracket in many respects parallel those of the Poisson bracket. However, the Lagrange bracket does not satisfy the Jacobi identity. Instead, it satisfies a different relation (which we will not use here); see Derivation 19 in Ch. 9 of
Goldstein et al. 2002
.

II.1.4
Fluctuations in terms of inverted partial derivatives

Applying the ‘direct conditions’ of Eq. (
2
) to Eq. (
1
), the latter becomes

\displaystyle\delta Q_{i}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\partialderivative{q_j}{P_i}\delta p_{j}-\partialderivative{p_j}{P_i}\delta q_{j}\right),

(5)

\displaystyle\delta P_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\partialderivative{q_j}{Q_i}\delta p_{j}-\partialderivative{p_j}{Q_i}\delta q_{j}\right).

This is the form that will enable us to evaluate the fluctuations
\delta Q_{i}
and
\delta P_{i}
.

As an illustration of the properties of the Lagrange brackets, let us use them to verify the correctness of Eq. (
5
). For example, to verify the first relation in Eq. (
5
), we start from its right-hand side, and substitute the following first-order expansions:

\displaystyle\delta q_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\partialderivative{q_j}{Q_k}\delta Q_{k}+\partialderivative{q_j}{P_k}\delta P_{k}\right)

and

\displaystyle\delta p_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\partialderivative{p_j}{Q_k}\delta Q_{k}+\partialderivative{p_j}{P_k}\delta P_{k}\right).

In the double sum that appears, we first exchange the order of summation. Then, for each
k
, we group the terms with a common
\delta Q_{k}
, which we factor out. We do the same for the terms with a common
\delta P_{k}
. We get

\begin{split}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[\left(\partialderivative{q_j}{Q_k}\partialderivative{p_j}{P_i}-\partialderivative{p_j}{Q_k}\partialderivative{q_j}{P_i}\right)\delta Q_{k}\right.\hskip 50.00008pt&\\
\left.+\left(\partialderivative{q_j}{P_k}\partialderivative{p_j}{P_i}-\partialderivative{p_j}{P_k}\partialderivative{q_j}{P_i}\right)\delta P_{k}\right]\hskip 30.00005pt&\\
&\hskip-200.0003pt=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left[\{Q_{k},\,P_{i}\}\delta Q_{k}+\{P_{k},\,P_{i}\}\delta P_{k}\right]\\
&\hskip-200.0003pt=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left[\delta_{ki}\delta Q_{k}+0\,\delta P_{k}\right]\\
&\hskip-200.0003pt=\delta Q_{i},\end{split}

where in the next-to-last step we used the properties of the fundamental Lagrange brackets, Eq. (
4
). The second relation in Eq. (
5
) can be verified the same way.

II.2
Fields

In our field-theoretic system of interest, the discrete set of ‘old’ canonical variables
\{q_{i}
,
p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is replaced by ‘old’ canonical fields: a one-dimensional coordinate field
q(x)
and its conjugate momentum field
p(x)
.

The ‘new’ canonical variables, on the other hand, appear in a hybrid form. First, there is a pair of continuous functions of a certain spectral parameter, which are a ‘new’ canonical coordinate and its ‘new’ conjugate momentum. These will not be important in what follows, so we need not discuss them further. Second, in addition to these continuous ‘new’ variables, there is also a certain number of discrete pairs of ‘new’ coordinates and their conjugate momenta. Our main interest will lie in some of these discrete ‘new’ variables.

The most straightforward way to handle the continuous degrees of freedom is to first discretize space onto a regular one-dimensional lattice with spacing
\Delta x
, reproduce the relevant relationships for the discrete variables
\tilde{q}_{i}\approx q(x)/\Delta x,\,\tilde{p}_{i}\approx p(x)/\Delta x
, and finally replace any spatial sums by integrals.

Applying this procedure to Eq. (
5
), the fluctuations for the ‘new’ discrete coordinates and momenta can be written as

\displaystyle\delta Q_{i}

\displaystyle=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\partialderivative{q(x)}{P_i}\delta p(x)-\partialderivative{p(x)}{P_i}\delta q(x)\right)\,dx,

(6)

\displaystyle\delta P_{i}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\partialderivative{q(x)}{Q_i}\delta p(x)-\partialderivative{p(x)}{Q_i}\delta q(x)\right)\,dx.

Here, each of the fields
q(x)
and
p(x)
is, in general, a function of all the ‘new’ variables, both discrete and continuous. We will not need to handle (and therefore will not explain) the partial derivatives with respect to the ‘new’ continuous variables.

II.3
Vacuum fluctuations around a one-dimensional Bose condensate

From now on, we will be interested in the quantum fluctuations of macroscopic variables characterizing localized states of the one-dimensional Bose condensates. It will turn out that these macroscopic variables can be expressed in terms of the ‘new’ discrete canonical variables.

We will make use of the Bogoliubov decomposition
Bogolubov 1947
, in which a bosonic quantum field is written as the sum of a classical bulk and small quantum fluctuations on top of it:

\hat{\Psi}=\Psi_{0}+\delta\hat{\Psi}.

(7)

A natural choice for ‘old’ canonical coordinate and momentum fields is

\displaystyle q(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\hbar}\real[\Psi(x,t)]

(8)

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\hbar}\imaginary[\Psi(x,t)],

The corresponding definitions for the small fluctuations are

\displaystyle\delta\hat{q}(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\hbar}\,\frac{\delta\hat{\Psi}(x)+\delta\hat{\Psi}^{\dagger}(x)}{2}

(9)

\displaystyle\delta\hat{p}(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\hbar}\,\frac{\delta\hat{\Psi}(x)-\delta\hat{\Psi}^{\dagger}(x)}{2i}\,.

Now, as far as quantum fluctuations are concerned, it turns out we already know how to compute four basic correlators of the form
\bra{0}\delta\hat{u}(x)\delta\hat{v}(y)\ket{0}
, for some appropriate vacuum state
\ket{0}
, where each of
\delta\hat{u}(x)
and
\delta\hat{v}(x)
is either
\delta\hat{q}(x)
or
\delta\hat{p}(x)
.

To take advantage of this, we will be computing quantities such as
\bra{0}\delta\hat{Q}_{i}^{2}\ket{0}
, which we will be denoting by
\expectationvalue{\delta\hat Q_i^2}
when it is clear which vacuum state is meant. In that example, we use the first relation in Eq. (
6
) twice, obtaining

\expectationvalue{\delta\hat Q_i^2}=\\
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\Biggl(\partialderivative{p(x^\prime)}{P_i}\partialderivative{p(y^\prime)}{P_i}\bra{0}\delta\hat{q}(x^{\prime})\delta\hat{q}(y^{\prime})\ket{0}\\
-\partialderivative{p(x^\prime)}{P_i}\partialderivative{q(y^\prime)}{P_i}\bra{0}\delta\hat{q}(x^{\prime})\delta\hat{p}(y^{\prime})\ket{0}\\
-\partialderivative{q(x^\prime)}{P_i}\partialderivative{p(y^\prime)}{P_i}\bra{0}\delta\hat{p}(x^{\prime})\delta\hat{q}(y^{\prime})\ket{0}\\
+\partialderivative{q(x^\prime)}{P_i}\partialderivative{q(y^\prime)}{P_i}\bra{0}\delta\hat{p}(x^{\prime})\delta\hat{p}(y^{\prime})\ket{0}\Biggr)\differential x^{\prime}\differential y^{\prime}.

(10)

It will turn out that such integrals can be evaluated analytically, in closed form.

III
A concrete setting: the nonlinear Schrödinger equation

We will now show how to apply the canonical formalism to the
n
-soliton breathers of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NLSE).

III.1
The underlying quantum many-body model

Underlying our classical field is a many-body quantum mechanical system, which we will now describe.

This underlying quantum model can be introduced in either the first- or the second-quantized form. Although we will briefly describe the first-quantized form as well, for our present purposes, the second-quantized form will be more important, because its connection to the classical NLSE is more manifest.

Let us recall the basic ingredients of any 1D, nonrelativistic, bosonic, spin-0 quantum field theory (QFT). The basic objects are the quantum field
\hat{\Psi}(x,t)
and the Fock vacuum
\ket{0_\text{F}}
. The Fock vacuum is assumed to be the unique (up to overall phase) quantum state of unit norm that is annihilated by
\hat{\Psi}
for all
x
and
t
:

\hat{\Psi}(x,t)\ket{0_\text{F}}=0.

(11)

This is why the field
\hat{\Psi}
is called the annihilation operator field. Its hermitian conjugate
\hat{\Psi}^{\dagger}
is called the creation operator. The two fields are postulated to satisfy the standard canonical equal-time bosonic commutation relations:

\displaystyle\left[\hat{\Psi}(x,t),\,\hat{\Psi}^{\dagger}(y,t)\right]=\delta(x-y)

(12)

\displaystyle\left[\hat{\Psi}(x,t),\,\hat{\Psi}(y,t)\right]=\left[\hat{\Psi}^{\dagger}(x,t),\,\hat{\Psi}^{\dagger}(y,t)\right]=0.

For most of the remainder of this subsection, we will assume that everything happens at a fixed moment of time, and omit explicitly showing the argument
t
.

The operator
\widehat{N}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\Psi}^{\dagger}(x)\hat{\Psi}(x)\,dx
is, for reasons that will become apparent shortly, called the particle-number operator. Any finite eigenvalue of
\widehat{N}
must be a non-negative integer (for these and other basic QFT facts reviewed here, see Sec. A.3 of
Huang 1987
).

Let
\ket{\psi_N}
be a unit-norm eigenstate of
\widehat{N}
with eigenvalue
N
. Then we have the theorem that for all unit-norm states
\ket{\phi}
, the inner product
\bra{\phi}\hat{\Psi}(x_{1})\cdots\hat{\Psi}(x_{N})\ket{\psi_N}
does
not
vanish if and only if, up to an overall phase,
\ket{\phi}=\ket{0_\text{F}}
. Motivated by this, we consider the function of
N
variables

\psi_{N}(x_{1},\,\ldots,\,x_{N})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bra{0_\text{F}}\hat{\Psi}(x_{1})\cdots\hat{\Psi}(x_{N})\ket{\psi_N}.

(13)

As the reader can anticipate, in the first-quantized theory that is equivalent to the second-quantized theory we are currently discussing,
\psi_{N}(x_{1},\,\ldots,\,x_{N})
will be the wavefunction that corresponds to
\ket{\psi_N}
.

Because the field operators commute,
\psi_{N}
is completely symmetric with respect to permutations of its arguments. Moreover, it is normalized to unity, and it can be used to represent
\ket{\psi_N}
in the following way:

\ket{\psi_N}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx_{1}\cdots dx_{N}\,\psi_{N}(x_{1},\,\ldots,\,x_{N})\\
\times\hat{\Psi}^{\dagger}(x_{1})\cdots\hat{\Psi}^{\dagger}(x_{N})\ket{0}.

(14)

Now we specialize to a system whose Hamiltonian is given by

\widehat{H}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\partialderivative{\hat{\Psi}^\dagger(x)}{x}\partialderivative{\hat{\Psi}(x)}{x}\right.\\
\left.+\frac{g}{2}\hat{\Psi}^{\dagger}(x)\hat{\Psi}^{\dagger}(x)\hat{\Psi}(x)\hat{\Psi}(x)\right)dx,

(15)

where
m>0
and
g
are constants (see, e.g., Sec. I.1 of
Korepin et al. 1997
).

This Hamiltonian gives rise to the following Heisenberg equation of motion for the quantum fields:

i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{\Psi}(x,t)=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\hat{\Psi}(x,t)+g\hat{\Psi}^{\dagger}(x,t)\hat{\Psi}^{2}(x,t),

(16)

This is the quantum nonlinear Schrödinger equation. We will return to it in the next subsection.

In the remainder of the present subsection, we will discuss the equivalent first-quantized model and the connection to experiments.

The particle-number operator
\widehat{N}
commutes with the Hamiltonian. Fix a positive integer
N
, and consider the space of all eigenstates of
\widehat{N}
with eigenvalue
N
. Physically, this subspace is the Hilbert space of states for a system of
N
identical bosons. Within that subspace of states, the quantum field theory we have been discussing is equivalent to the
N
-body first-quantized system known as the Lieb-Liniger model
Lieb and Liniger 1963
. The equivalence is established
Korepin et al. 1997
by showing that a state of the form (
14
) will be an eigenstate of
\widehat{H}
from Eq. (
15
), with eigenenergy
E_{N}
, if and only if the function
\psi_{N}
is an eigenstate of the Lieb-Liniger Hamiltonian
\widehat{H}_{N}=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\partialderivative{^2}{x_{j}^2}+g\,\sum_{j<k}\delta(x_{j}-x_{k})
, with the same eigenenergy.

The Lieb-Liniger model describes a system of
N
identical bosonic particles of spin 0 and mass
m
, whose pairwise interactions are described by the zero-range potential
V(x,y)=g\,\delta(x-y)
. The functions
\psi_{N}
are the
N
-body time-independent wavefunctions of the quantum-mechanical system. This model is exactly solvable through the Bethe ansatz
Lieb and Liniger 1963
.

While Refs.
Korepin et al. 1997
and
Lieb and Liniger 1963
restrict themselves to the case
g>0
, we will be interested in the case
g<0
, corresponding to attractive interactions between the particles. This case is also solvable through the Bethe ansatz
Yurovsky et al. 2017
.

The Lieb-Liniger model provides a very good description of experiments with ultracold Bose gases, including self-attractive gases
Strecker et al. 2002
;
Khaykovich et al. 2002b
;
Yurovsky et al. 2008
. Such systems consist of a gas of bosonic atoms, usually alkali metals, located in an elongated, cigar-shaped harmonic trap with transverse trapping frequency
\omega_{\perp}
. The particles each have a mass
m
and their interactions are pairwise, with an s-wave scattering length
a_{\text{sc}}<0
. The systems can be prepared so that individual atoms have a kinetic energy of less than
\hbar\omega_{\perp}
, so that they are effectively 1D particles. The connection to the Lieb-Liniger coupling constant is
g=-2\hbar\omega_{\perp}a_{\text{sc}}

Olshanii 1998
. The scattering length can be
tuned using a magnetic field, through the mechanism of the Feshbach resonance
Chin et al. 2010
.

In experiments, there is usually also a weak longitudinal harmonic trapping, which means that an external harmonic potential must be added to
\widehat{H}_{N}
. On the one hand, such a term breaks the integrability of the model. On the other hand, since the longitudinal trapping is weak, the results on the integrable case can be used as ingredients to more general but approximate solutions; see, e.g.,
Dunjko et al. 2001
. In our case, we will assume that the longitudinal trapping is so weak that it can be ignored.

III.2
The classical field model and its breather solutions

Let us apply the Bogoliubov decomposition from Eq. (
7
) to the quantum NLSE field,

\hat{\Psi}(x,t)=\Psi(x,t)+\delta\hat{\Psi}(x,t).

(17)

The classical field
\Psi(x,t)
, which is also variously referred to as the mean field approximation and the order parameter, represents the Bose-condensed part of the gas
Leggett 2001b
. It satisfies the (classical) NLSE, which, in this context, is also called the Gross-Pitaevskii equation:

i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Psi(x,t)=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\Psi(x,t)\\
+g\left|\Psi(x,t)\right|^{2}\Psi(x,t).

(18)

This simple derivation ignores some subtleties, particularly the fact that it breaks the
U(1)
symmetry of the underlying problem (i.e., the fact the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
15
) is invariant under a global phase change of
\hat{\Psi}
). Methods of fixing this have been developed
Gardiner 1997
;
Castin and Dum 1998
, and they will play a role when we discuss the ‘correlated-noise vacuum’ in Sec.
IV.2
.

The classical Hamiltonian that corresponds to the classical NLSE is

H=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left(\absolutevalue{\pdv{x} \Psi}^{2}+\frac{g}{2}|\Psi|^{4}\right)\differential x\,.

(19)

When
g<0
, we say that we have an attractive, or focusing, case of the NLSE. This case supports bright soliton solutions
Faddeev and Takhtajan 2007
, including multi-soliton ‘breathers’. These are so called because their density profiles oscillate in time with a well-defined period. As we will see below, there is a sense in which an NLSE breather is a ‘nonlinear superposition’ of
n
elementary solitons.

Consider two NLSEs, 1 and 2, whose coupling constants are
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
, respectively. Suppose
g_{1}=g_{2}/n^{2}
, with
n
an integer. It turns out that there is a relation between the elementary soliton solution of NLSE 1 and the so-called
odd-norm-ratio
(ONR) breather solution of NLSE 2. The latter is an
n
-soliton breather such that the norms of its constituent solitons are in the ratio
1:3:5:\cdots:(2n-1)
. Assume that the total norm is the same for both the elementary soliton and the ONR breather. Remarkably, the wavefunction of the elementary soliton is identical to the wavefunction of the ONR breather at the point of time when its density profile is the widest
Satsuma and Yajima 1974
.

This makes it possible to experimentally realize ONR breathers, by first creating an elementary soliton and then using the Feshbach resonance to quench the coupling constant by a factor of
n^{2}

Di Carli et al. 2019
.

We have mentioned previously that in a breather, the relative velocities and positions of the constituent solitons must be zero. In the context of the quench protocol, the symmetry of the situation makes it obvious that this must be so. After all, the elementary soliton is completely symmetric about its center of mass, and both NLSE 1 and NLSE 2 respect this symmetry. Thus, the post-quench field must have the same symmetry. But if any relative velocities or positions of the constituent solitons were non-zero, that symmetry would be violated.

III.3
The mathematical structure of breather solutions

Chapter 3 §7 of
Faddeev and Takhtajan 2007
provides a reversible canonical transformation for the NLSE based on the so-called inverse scattering data. It will be convenient to switch to the system of units used there, in which

\displaystyle\hbar

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2},

\displaystyle gN

\displaystyle=-2\kappa,

(20)

so that the NLSE becomes

i\partialderivative{\psi}{t}=-\partialderivative[2]{\psi}{x}+2\kappa\absolutevalue{\psi}^{2}\psi\,.

(21)

A generic solution of the NLSE is, in a sense which will become clearer below, a ‘nonlinear superposition’ of a finite number of constituent simple solitons plus a non-solitonic component. The latter is also called the
radiation
component, because it spreads—radiates—quickly, becoming more and more spatially diluted. In terms of the discussion in Sec.
II.2
, the constituent solitons give rise to the ‘new’ discrete canonical variables, while the non-solitonic component gives the two ‘new’ continuous variables. When we talk about
n
-soliton solutions, we mean purely solitonic solutions, without any non-solitonic component.

In an
n
-soliton solution, each constituent soliton is characterized by four parameters which form two canonical pairs:
\{q_{j},p_{j}\}
, which are proportional respectively to the position and velocity of the
j
th constituent soliton; and
\{\rho_{j},\phi_{j}\}
, which are proportional to the norm (i.e., the number of particles) and phase of the same constituent soliton. In fact, the positions are given by
x_{j}=2q_{j}/\rho_{j}
, and velocities by
v_{j}=p_{j}
. As one might expect, the total norm (i.e., the total number of particles) of an
n
-soliton is given by the sum of all the
\rho_{j}
s.

Breather solitons are the
n
-solitons in which all the constituent solitons have the same positions and the same velocities, but no two have the same norm
Zakharov and Shabat 1972
.

Let us introduce the scattering data
\lambda_{j}
and
\gamma_{j}
, which will become important shortly. The following relations hold:

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\kappa}{2}\left(p_{j}+i\rho_{j}\right)

(22)

\displaystyle\gamma_{j}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(q_{j}-i\phi_{j}\right)\exp\left(-it\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)\exp\left(ix\lambda_{j}\right)\,.

Note that for each
j
, we have that
\lambda_{j}
and
\gamma_{j}
are associated only with the
j
th constituent soliton.

Multi-soliton solutions for this system are then found by first solving the following linear system for the auxiliary fields
\psi_{k}(x,t)
:

\sum_{k=1}^{n}M_{jk}(x)\psi_{k}(x,t)=-\gamma_{j}

(23)

Here
n
is the number of constituent solitons, and

M_{jk}(x)=\frac{\gamma^{*}_{k}\gamma_{j}+1}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda^{*}_{k}},

(24)

with
∗
indicating complex conjugation.

Note that the individual auxiliary fields
\psi_{k}(x,t)
have no simple interpretation. In particular, it is not the case that each is associated with a different constituent soliton, since each depends on all the
\lambda_{j}
and
\gamma_{j}
, for all
j
.

The full
n
-soliton solution is then constructed as

\psi(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\sum_{k}^{n}\psi_{k}(x,t).

(25)

When we say that an
n
-soliton is a ‘nonlinear superposition’ of its constituent solitons, we mean that each constituent soliton is characterized by its own parameters
\lambda_{j}
and
\gamma_{j}
, which enter nonlinearly into the solution (
25
).

Details of this derivation and proofs can be found in Ch. 2 §5 of
Faddeev and Takhtajan 2007
; similar results, albeit without canonical structure, are found in
Zakharov and Shabat 1972
and
Gordon 1983
.

III.4
The problem to be solved

As we mentioned, the ONR
n
-soliton breathers can be realized by first creating an elementary soliton (the ‘mother’ soliton), which may be undergoing substantial center-of-mass motion (i.e., it might be ‘hot’). Next, we quench the coupling constant by a factor of
n^{2}
. This produces an ORB
n
-soliton breather (whose constituent solitons we will call ‘daughter’ solitons), created at the point of its breathing cycle when its width is maximal. The actual field
\Psi(x,t)
does not change at the moment of quench; only its subsequent evolution does. Since the daughter solitons constitute a breather, their relative velocities are exactly zero, even though the center-of-mass motion of the breather might be substantial.

Any nonzero relative velocities are a signature of the presence of fluctuations in the mother soliton. The present-day experiments are so well controlled that the dominant source of fluctuations is the intrinsic quantum fluctuations of the mother soliton. Thus, any observed post-quench separation of the constituent solitons would be a beyond-mean-field, quantum-many-body effect.

In addition to nonzero relative velocities, the quantum fluctuations of the mother soliton also cause the post-quench constituent solitons to be created already slightly displaced from each other, and to have norms whose ratios deviate from the ratios of consecutive odd integers. The latter implies that the field will have a small non-solitonic component
Satsuma and Yajima 1974
, which, however, will quickly radiate away (i.e., it will quickly reach the edges of the physical trap and be mostly lost from the system). Finally, the relative phases of the constituent solitons will also deviate from their classical-field predictions.

The problem we want to solve is to compute the immediate post-quench variances of the distributions of the constituent solitons’ relative velocities and relative positions around their classical-field zero values (which are zero). We also want to compute the variance of relative phases and norms. Any non-zero values for these variances are due to the quantum fluctuations of the matter field
\Psi
at the moment of quench.

The basic principle that makes this calculation possible is this: although, under the quench, the Hamiltonian is changed instantaneously and discontinuously (well, very quickly and sharply, at any rate), the post-quench change in the wavefunction is much slower and smoother. This is because that change is due solely to the regular time evolution under the post-quench Hamiltonian; the fact that the new Hamiltonian was arrived at through a sudden change in one of its parameters does not matter. This is true at both the classical and the quantum-many-body levels.

Thus, the following procedure is valid: in an expression such as Eq. (
10
), the quantum fluctuation—i.e., the factors such as
\bra{0}\delta\hat{q}(x^{\prime})\delta\hat{p}(y^{\prime})\ket{0}
—are computed for the mother soliton, using results that already exist in the literature. However, the partial derivatives such as
\partialderivative{q(x^\prime)}{P_i}
are computed assuming
q(x^{\prime})
corresponds, via Eq. (
8
), to the breather
\Psi
. Again, as a function of
x
, the field
\Psi
just before and just after the quench is the same, and so the ‘old’ canonical variables (
8
) are the same. However, the ‘new’ canonical variables just before the quench are different from those just after the quench.

In more detail: before the quench, there is only a single soliton and thus
q(x)
and
p(x)
are functions of (i.e., parameterized by) only
4
discrete ‘new’ variables:
q_{1}
,
p_{1}
,
\rho_{1}
, and
\phi_{1}
. This parameterization is the one in Eq. (S-27) of the Supplemental Material for
Marchukov et al. 2020
, where our

p_{1}
,
\rho_{1}
, and
\phi_{1}
correspond to (and are simple functions of) the parameters
B
,
V
,
N
, and
\Theta
in that equation.

To get Eq. (S-27), one starts from Eqs. (

)-(

n=2

Immediately after the quench, even though
q(x)

p(x)

IV
The computation of fluctuations

The causes of the fluctuations in the post-quench constituent solitons’ relative velocities and other quantities of interest are the quantum fluctuations of the field
\hat{\Psi}
. They enter our formalism through factors such as
\bra{0}\delta\hat{q}(x^{\prime})\delta\hat{p}(y^{\prime})\ket{0}
. To evaluate these factors, we need a model for the quantum fluctuations.

As in
Marchukov et al. 2020
, we will be considering two different models. The first one is a simple white-noise model. The other is a more realistic model with a particular kind of correlated noise.

IV.1
White-noise vacuum

From Eqs. (
12
) and (
17
), it follows that the quantum-fluctuation field
\delta\hat{\Psi}(x,t)
also satisfies the canonical equal-time commutation relations,

\displaystyle\quantity[\delta\hat{\Psi}(x,t),\,\delta\hat{\Psi}^\dagger(y,t)]=\delta(x-y)

(26)

\displaystyle\quantity[\delta\hat{\Psi}(x,t),\,\ \delta\hat{\Psi}(y,t)]=\quantity[\delta\hat{\Psi}^\dagger(x,t),\,\ \delta\hat{\Psi}^\dagger(y,t)]=0.

On the other hand, from Eqs. (
17
) and (
11
), it follows that if any Bose-condensed atoms are present, then
\delta\hat{\Psi}(x,t)
cannot annihilate the Fock vacuum
\ket{0_\text{F}}
. We will refer to the state it does annihilate as the
white-noise vacuum
,
\ket{0_\text{W}}
:

\displaystyle\delta\hat{\Psi}(x)\ket{0_\text{W}}

\displaystyle=0

(27)

\displaystyle\bra{0_\text{W}}\delta\hat{\Psi}^{\dagger}(x)

\displaystyle=0.

Although both
\ket{0_\text{F}}
and
\ket{0_\text{W}}
are referred to as ‘vacua’, they correspond to vastly different physical situations. The Fock vacuum corresponds to there being no actual particles present, i.e., no atoms. In contrast, the white-noise vacuum corresponds to there being no Bogoliubov quasi-particles, which are just excitations of the macroscopic number of atoms that
are
present.

Next, we apply Eqs. (
9
), (
26
), and (
27
) to Eq. (
10
), where all expectation values (in other words,
\bra{0}\delta\hat{q}(x^{\prime})\delta\hat{p}(y^{\prime})\ket{0}
, etc.) are taken with respect to the white-noise vacuum. We find that the expression reduces to a single integral,

\expectationvalue{\delta\hat Q^2}=\frac{\hbar}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\quantity[\qty(\pdv{q(x^\prime)}{P})^2+\qty(\pdv{p(x^\prime)}{P})^2]\differential x^{\prime}.

(28)

The same procedure can be done to find also the initial fluctuations in
P
as well as all required covariances. For completeness, the initial fluctuation in
P
is

\expectationvalue{\delta\hat P^2}=\frac{\hbar}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\quantity[\qty(\pdv{q(x^\prime)}{Q})^2+\qty(\pdv{p(x^\prime)}{Q})^2]\differential x^{\prime}.

(29)

It turns out that for covariances, the middle terms in (
10
) with imaginary coefficients form a Lagrange bracket, and will vanish for all covariances except
\expectationvalue{\delta Q \delta P}
and
\expectationvalue{\delta P \delta Q}
. Therefore, for covariances one must only calculate

\expectationvalue{\delta Q_1 \delta Q_2}=\frac{\hbar}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left(\partialderivative{q(x^\prime)}{P_1}\partialderivative{q(x^\prime)}{P_2}\right.\\
\left.+\partialderivative{p(x^\prime)}{P_1}\partialderivative{p(x^\prime)}{P_2}\right)\differential x^{\prime}

(30)

or

\expectationvalue{\delta Q \delta P}=\frac{i\hbar}{2}-\frac{\hbar}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left(\partialderivative{q(x^\prime)}{P}\partialderivative{q(x^\prime)}{Q}\right.\\
\left.+\partialderivative{p(x^\prime)}{P}\partialderivative{p(x^\prime)}{Q}\right)\differential x^{\prime}\,.

(31)

\hat{b}_{k}^{\dagger}
operators:

\delta\hat{\psi}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\quantity(u_k(x)\hat{b}_k + v^*_k(x)\hat{b}^\dagger_k)\differential k.

(35)

Here
u_{k}(x)
and
v_{k}(x)
are the ‘corrected’ expressions for the Bogoliubov modes
U_{k}(x)
and
V_{k}(x)
of the fundamental soliton, at position
x
and wavenumber
k
:

\displaystyle\ket{u_k}

\displaystyle=\quantity(1 - \dyad{\phi_0}{\phi_0} )\ket{U_k}

(36)

\displaystyle\ket{v_k}

\displaystyle=\quantity(1 - \dyad{\phi_0}{\phi_0} )\ket{V_k},

where
\ket{\phi_0}
is the pure single soliton solution. It is the presence of the projectors
\outerproduct{\phi_0}{\phi_0}
which ensures that the
U(1)
symmetry of the problem is preserved
Castin and Dum 1998
. The Bogoliubov modes
\ket{U_k}
and
\ket{V_k}
are known exactly
Kaup 1990
:

\displaystyle U_{k}(x)=e^{iKX}\hskip 110.00017pt

(37)

\displaystyle\times\frac{1+(K^{2}-1)\cosh^{2}{X}+2iK\sinh{X}\cosh{X}}{(K-i)^{2}\cosh^{2}{X}},

\displaystyle V_{k}(x)=e^{iKX}\frac{1}{(K-i)^{2}\cosh^{2}{X}},

where

X=\frac{mgN}{2\hbar^{2}}x\,,\qquad K=\frac{2\hbar^{2}}{mgN}k.

(38)

Note that in
Marchukov et al. 2020
, the analog of equation (
35
) uses the uncorrected expressions for the Bogoliubov modes; in other words, instead of
u_{k}(x)
and
v^{*}_{k}(x)
, it uses
U_{k}(x)
and
V^{*}_{k}(x)
(see Sec. V of the Supplemental Material for that reference). Further details on the role of these corrections are discussed in Appendix
A
.

Next, we follow the same procedure as for the white noise vacuum, where the fluctuations in canonical coordinates are given in (
6
) and we use the same definitions of
\delta q(x)
and
\delta p(x)
as stated in (
9
). Using the commutation relations (
34
), we find

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\delta\hat{\psi}(x)\delta\hat{\psi}(y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u_{k}(x)v^{*}_{k}(y)\differential k\,,

(39)

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\delta\hat{\psi}(x)\delta\hat{\psi}^\dagger(y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u_{k}(x)u^{*}_{k}(y)\differential k\,,

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\delta\hat{\psi}^\dagger(x)\delta\hat{\psi}(y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v_{k}(x)v^{*}_{k}(y)\differential k\,,

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\delta\hat{\psi}^\dagger(x)\delta\hat{\psi}^\dagger(y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v_{k}(x)u^{*}_{k}(y)\differential k\,,

so that the expectation values are now triple integrals with 16 terms each. However, the complexity of this calculation can be reduced by defining

\displaystyle F(x,k)

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{q(x)}{P}\quantity( \vphantom{\frac{}{1}} U_k(x)-V_k(x)-2\braket{\phi_0}{U_k}\phi_0)

(40)

\displaystyle-i\partialderivative{p(x)}{P}\quantity( \vphantom{\frac{}{1}} U_k(x)+V_k(x))\,,

\displaystyle F^{*}(x,k)

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{q(x)}{P}\quantity( \vphantom{\frac{}{1}} U^*_k(x)-V^*_k(x)-2\braket{\phi_0}{U_k}\phi_0)

\displaystyle+i\partialderivative{p(x)}{P}\quantity( \vphantom{\frac{}{1}} U^*_k(x)+V^*_k(x))\,,

and exchanging the order of integration. Note the terms
2\innerproduct{\phi_0}{U_k}\phi_{0}
; these appear because we use the ‘corrected’ expressions for the Bogoliubov modes. Then,
\expectationvalue{\delta\hat Q^2}
becomes two one-dimensional integrals which can be evaluated analytically using software. Specifically,

\expectationvalue{\delta\hat Q^2}=\frac{\hbar}{4\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\absolutevalue{F(k)}^{2}\differential k\,,

(41)

where

F(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(x,k)\differential x.

(42)

The variances for
\expectationvalue{\delta\hat P^2}
can be similarly simplified, defining instead

G(x,k)=\partialderivative{q(x)}{Q}\quantity( \vphantom{\frac{}{1}} U_k(x)-V_k(x)-2\braket{\phi_0}{U_k}\phi_0)\\
-i\partialderivative{p(x)}{Q}\quantity( \vphantom{\frac{}{1}} U_k(x)+V_k(x))

(43)

and its complex conjugate. Note again the appearance of the ‘correction terms’
2\innerproduct{\phi_0}{U_k}\phi_{0}
. We obtain

\expectationvalue{\delta\hat P^2}=\frac{\hbar}{4\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\absolutevalue{G(k)}^{2}\differential k\,.

(44)

Finally, for the relevant covariances, we have

\expectationvalue{\delta Q\delta P}=\frac{\hbar}{4\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(k)G^{*}(k)\differential k\,.

(45)

V
Conclusion

Small quantum excitations of integrable PDEs with known Hamiltonian structure are computable using the canonical bracket formalism developed in this paper. The canonical approach reduces the computational complexity of the required calculations, which allows results which were previously computed numerically to be calculated analytically. Further, it allows for calculations of new results, for larger systems. We have applied the formalism to the problems of 2- and 3-soliton breathers arising from a quench of the coupling constant in the nonlinear Schrödinger equation. In future work, we hope to identify a different integrable PDE to which our formalism can be applied.

Appendix A

In this appendix, we will discuss the derivation of the ‘correction terms’
2\innerproduct{\phi_0}{U_k}\phi_{0}
in Eqs. (
40
) and (
43
), which appear because we use the corrected expressions for the Bogoliubov modes. In the process, we will explain why our results for the 2-soliton breather match those in
Marchukov et al. 2020
despite the fact that we included the corrections and
Marchukov et al. 2020
did not.

We first need to calculate the inner products
\innerproduct{\phi_0}{U_k}
and
\innerproduct{\phi_0}{V_k}
.

The Bogoliubov-de Gennes equations are

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}U_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\quantity[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\pdv[2]{x}+2gN\abs{\phi_0(x)}^2-\mu_0]U_{k}(x)\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\hskip 100.00015pt+gN\phi_{0}^{2}V_{k}(x)

(46)

\displaystyle-\epsilon_{k}V_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\quantity[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\pdv[2]{x}+2gN\abs{\phi_0(x)}^2-\mu_0]V_{k}(x)

\displaystyle\hskip 100.00015pt+gN\phi_{0}^{*^{2}}U_{k}(x)\,\,,

(47)

where
\mu_{0}
is the chemical potential.

First, taking the inner product with
\ket{\phi_0}
to obtain
\innerproduct{\phi_0}{U_k}
, we obtain

\epsilon_{k}\innerproduct{\phi_0}{U_k}=\\
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential x\,\phi_{0}\quantity[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\pdv[2]{x}+2gN\abs{\phi_0(x)}^2-\mu_0]U_{k}(x)\\
+gN\phi_{0}^{3}V_{k}(x).

(48)

The first term of the integrand can be integrated by parts twice. The boundary terms vanish in both cases since in order to be a soliton solution,
\phi_{0}
must go to zero at
\pm\infty
. Thus we have

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential x\,\quantity( -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\phi_0 \pdv[2]{x}U_k(x))\\
=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential x\,\quantity( -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\qty(\pdv[2]{x}\phi_0 )U_k(x))\,.

(49)

But we know that
\phi_{0}
is a solution of the NLSE, so we can substitute

-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\partialderivative[2]{x}\phi_{0}=\mu_{0}\phi_{0}-gN\absolutevalue{\phi_0(x)}^{2}\phi_{0}

(50)

into (
48
) for the first term. We find

\epsilon_{k}\innerproduct{\phi_0}{U_k}=\\
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential x\left[\mu_{0}\phi_{0}-gN\absolutevalue{\phi_0(x)}^{2}\phi_{0}+2gN\absolutevalue{\phi_0(x)}^{2}\phi_{0}\right.\\
\left.\vphantom{\absolutevalue{\phi_0(x)}^{2}}-\mu_{0}\phi_{0}\right]U_{k}(x)+gN\phi_{0}^{3}V_{k}(x)\,\,.

(51)

When
\phi_{0}
is wholly real, as is the case for the soliton breather model,
\absolutevalue{\phi_0}^{2}=\phi_{0}^{2}
and (
51
) reduces to

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}\innerproduct{\phi_0}{U_k}=gN\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi_{0}^{3}\quantity[U_k(x) + V_k(x)]\differential x\,\,.

(52)

We now repeat the calculation for the inner product with
V_{k}(x)
. Again, when
\phi_{0}
is real,

-\epsilon_{k}\innerproduct{\phi_0}{V_k}=\\
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential x\phi_{0}\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\partialderivative[2]{x}+2gN\absolutevalue{\phi_0(x)}^{2}-\mu_{0}\right]V_{k}(x)\\
+gN\phi_{0}^{3}U_{k}(x)\,\,.

(53)

Again integrate the first term by parts twice, and make the substitution in (
50
). The expression reduces to

\displaystyle-\epsilon_{k}\innerproduct{\phi_0}{V_k}=gN\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi_{0}^{3}\quantity[U_k(x) + V_k(x)]\differential x.

(54)

Thus, for a real
\phi_{0}
, we have demonstrated that

\displaystyle\innerproduct{\phi_0}{U_k}=-\innerproduct{\phi_0}{V_k}\,\,,

(55)

and so the corrected modes are

\displaystyle\ket{u_k}

\displaystyle=\ket{U_k}-\innerproduct{\phi_0}{U_k}\ket{\phi_0}

(56)

\displaystyle\ket{v_k}

\displaystyle=\ket{V_k}+\innerproduct{\phi_0}{U_k}\ket{\phi_0}\,.

Because the corrections to
U_{k}(x)
and
V_{k}(x)
are equal in magnitude but opposite in sign, when we insert the corrected modes into the expressions in (
39
) and factor the resulting integrand, we find the correction term is simply

-2\innerproduct{\phi_0}{U_k}\phi_{0}\partialderivative{q(x)}{X},

(57)

where
X
is either
P
for
F(x,k)
or
Q
for
G(x,k)
. This means that for any fluctuations where
\partialderivative{q(x)}{X}=0
, there will be no contribution from the correction term. For the two-soliton with the initial conditions listed in Table
1
,
\partialderivative{q(x)}{\phi_i}=\partialderivative{q(x)}{p_i}=0
, so there are no corrections to any
\rho_{i}
or
q_{i}
fluctuations. However,
\partialderivative{q(x)}{q_i}
and
\partialderivative{q(x)}{\rho_i}
are not zero.

Nevertheless, when we actually go through with the computations, for both
\expectationvalue{p_i^2}
and
\expectationvalue{p_1 p_2}
we find that the result does not depend on whether corrected or uncorrected modes are used—despite the fact that
\partialderivative{q(x)}{q_i}
is not zero in those cases. The reason for this is as yet unclear.

Interestingly, the differences in fluctuations in
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
calculated using corrected modes compensate for one another: when considering fluctuations in the relative coordinate, there is zero net difference between using the corrected and the uncorrected modes. When using the corrected modes, the fluctuation for
\phi_{1}
is increased by
\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}
relative to the result using the uncorrected mode, while the fluctuation for
\phi_{2}
, relative to the result from the uncorrected mode, decreases by the same amount.
