Experience-driven perceptions misalign with assessed heat risk in the United States
Authors: Jennifer R. Marlon, Nicolas Begotka, Annie Preston, Max Steifel, Jesse M. Keenan, Cal Inman, Natasha P. Malmin, Jennifer Carman, Emily Goddard, John Kotcher, Edward Maibach, Seth A. Rosenthal, Anthony Leiserowitz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Extreme heat poses a risk to public health, yet illness and death from heat exposure are largely preventable through behavioral adaptation. However, individuals’ perceptions of local heat risks often diverge from expert assessments, limiting protective behaviors. Here we introduce the Risk Analysis – Perception Framework, which quantifies the alignment between assessed risk, based on a standardized public health metric from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Heat and Health Index, and perceived risk, modeled using a multi-year national survey (2018–2022). Applying the framework across US counties, we find substantial misalignments between assessed and perceived risk, particularly in the Pacific Northwest and in Appalachia. These gaps highlight how personal experience, contextual cues, and mental shortcuts shape risk perceptions that often diverge from expert assessments of vulnerability. Through systematic risk alignment mapping, the Risk Analysis – Perception Framework provides an empirical approach to target climate risk communication, adaptation strategies, and policy interventions.

Introduction

Extreme heat is one of the most direct and widespread threats to human health from climate change. Recent heat waves, intensified by global warming, have killed thousands of people, and the risks associated with heat exposure are increasing1,2. Heat-related deaths are preventable with appropriate preparation, but many members of the public do not adequately understand the risks associated with extreme heat and many are unprepared, underscoring the characterization of heat waves as “silent killers”3. Moreover, while the acute effects of heat waves are being intensively studied, chronic impacts have received much less attention4–6. This disconnect between the scientific understanding of heat risks and public awareness creates a fundamental challenge for climate adaptation: how can communities prepare for threats they don’t fully perceive, and how can risk communication bridge the gap between expert assessments and lived experience?

Addressing this disconnect between assessed hazards and public understanding requires frameworks that carefully integrate how climate risks are both scientifically assessed and subjectively perceived7,8. The Risk Analysis – Perception (RAP) framework provides a systematic approach for explicitly quantifying gaps between expert risk assessments and public perceptions across diverse populations. Drawing on dual-process theories of cognition9–11, the RAP framework recognizes that people’s responses to climate hazards often rely more heavily on experiential, affect-driven processes rather than the deliberative analysis that characterizes scientific risk assessment12,13. This mismatch between experiential and analytic risk evaluations may create systematic patterns of public misalignment with assessed risks that vary across communities and geographic regions14–16.

Risk perceptions are crucial for motivating responses to warnings and promoting adaptation behaviors for diverse threats17–19. The catastrophic damages from Hurricanes Katrina and Sandy, for example, motivated major investments of over US$10 bn in flood risk-reduction measures20,21. Similarly, as Americans’ experiences with extreme heat increase, improvements in public awareness, outreach, and policymaking can help stimulate protective measures. Unlike hurricanes, extreme heat can occur in virtually all regions of the U.S., necessitating comprehensive, nationwide geographic public health metrics on heat vulnerability and perceived risk to support preparedness and culturally informed risk communication efforts.

Worry represents the affective dimension of risk perceptions, reflecting a person’s engagement with potential future hazards. Often rooted in underlying beliefs, values, and lived experiences, high levels of worry about threats can drive adaptive behaviors and policy support, fostering mitigation efforts and promoting community resilience22,23. Conversely, low levels of worry may hinder proactive measures, leaving communities more vulnerable to extreme heat impacts and reducing the effectiveness of risk communication efforts. However, the relationship between worry and action is not always direct or causal. As noted in prior work on the risk perception paradox, individuals may perceive high risk but remain unable or unwilling to act due to structural, social, or personal barriers—such as limited financial resources, physical ability, or trust in institutions24,25. These dynamics are particularly relevant for vulnerable populations, such as older or low-income adults, who may experience high perceived risk but lack the capacity to respond effectively. Therefore, understanding assessment-perception alignment is necessary, but not sufficient for effective preparedness; it must inform targeted interventions that address both psychological and structural barriers to action.

A heat risk perception ranking of census tracts developed for the summer of 2015 found that residents in northern areas of the U.S. had lower risk perceptions than those in the south; it also identified the western Mountain region has having high exposure to extreme heat yet relatively low heat risk perceptions. The index included a measure of worry, but the study only produced relative rankings for one summer26. Similarly, Allan et al.27 mapped variations in risk perceptions of eight hazards, including extreme heat, across 115 National Weather Service County Warning Areas (CWA). Yet, CWAs are not widely used by risk managers and communicators as practical units of analysis. Furthermore, neither study systematically tested predictors of community-level risk perceptions nor examined alignment between perceived and assessed risks–key components of the RAP framework that we advance here.

Understanding the spatial variations in worry about extreme heat, and how these perceptions align with assessed hazards, is crucial for developing psychologically informed communication strategies that enhance community preparedness, resilience, and adaptive capacity. To support these efforts, we advance methods in multilevel regression with poststratification (MRP) to model the percentage of people worried about heat in each state and county across the contiguous U.S. (CONUS), producing maps showing the geographic variation in perceived heat risk. We compare these estimates with the national Heat and Health Index (HHI), a comprehensive measure of heat-related health outcomes and social vulnerability developed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) (Supplementary Table 1)28. We refer to the HHI as assessed heat risk because heat risk ultimately manifests through its impact on health. We calculate the difference between assessed and perceived heat risks to identify alignment gaps in each state and county (Fig. 1), providing a concrete application of the RAP framework. Prior research has shown that sociodemographic data (gender, age, race, income, disability status) and contextual environmental factors (temperature, land cover) are predictive of heat-related risk perceptions26. Drawing on this literature and the RAP framework’s emphasis on integrating assessed and perceived risk factors, we employ recent advancements in MRP that leverage machine learning and model ensembling29 to develop estimates of the percentage of residents in each state and county who are worried about extreme heat (see “Methods”). Our model employs five years of time-aggregated cross-sectional surveys (N = 11,113) where each respondent is geolocated and matched with local sociodemographic and physical data (Supplementary Tables 1–2). We build five alternative models to predict perceived heat risk, ensemble estimates from the three best performing models, and map the results to identify spatial variations in perceived heat risk and their alignment with expert hazard assessments across the CONUS.

Results

Geographic patterns of perceived and assessed risk

Our model estimates that 35.3% of Americans in the CONUS are worried about extreme heat, which is 2.8 percentage points lower than the 38.1% raw survey data before modeling adjustments. The state-level worry estimates varied from 20% in Wisconsin to 57% in Texas (Fig. 2a). Only five states (AZ, TX, OK, CA, NV) have majority populations who are worried about extreme heat. At the county level, values range from 17% in Iron County, Wisconsin to 65% in Hidalgo County, Texas (Fig. 2b). Only 4% (115 of 3109) of CONUS counties have majorities who are at least moderately worried about extreme heat affecting their local area. Residents of southern areas are more worried about extreme heat than those to the north. In general, counties in interior northern areas, especially in northern Michigan and Wisconsin, have the lowest levels of worry. County quadrant analysis reveals distinct geographic patterns (Fig. 5, Supplementary Fig. 1). Most counties fall into Quadrant I (high assessed/low perceived risk; 1442 counties) or Quadrant IV (low assessed/low perceived risk; 1552 counties), reflecting generally low perceived risk nationwide. Quadrant II (high assessed/high perceived risk) contains 113 counties concentrated in the South and Midwest, particularly in Texas and California. Only two counties fall in Quadrant III (high perceived/low assessed risk): Bossier Parish, Louisiana and Tom Green County, Texas.

Urban-rural differences are pronounced: urban counties ( > 1 million residents) are over 10 times more likely than rural counties ( < 10,000 residents) to have majority heat worry. Geographically, Quadrant I counties are widespread, Quadrant II counties cluster along the southwestern border, and Quadrant IV counties concentrate in the upper Midwest and Northeast (Supplementary Fig. 1). These patterns provide the foundation for targeted risk communication and adaptation planning through the RAP framework.

Demographic and socioeconomic influences on risk perception

Regression analysis reveals several significant predictors of the alignment gap between assessed and perceived heat risk, with an adjusted R2 value of 0.21 (Table 1). Counties with higher proportions of residents over 65 (e.g., in the rural upper Midwest) tend to underestimate heat risk relative to assessments (coefficient 8.523, p < 0.01), as do counties with higher proportions of Hispanic residents (coefficient 6.076, p < 0.01). However, the interaction between Hispanic population and poverty rates (e.g., counties in southern Texas) shows an inverse relationship (−7.045, p < 0.05). Counties with higher proportions of Black residents tend to overestimate heat risk relative to assessments (−5.553, p < 0.01). Higher educational attainment (proportion with a bachelor’s degree) is also associated with overestimating heat risk (−11.542, p < 0.01), in the coastal metropolitan regions of the Northeast and Mid-Atlantic, for example (Fig. 3f). Conversely, higher poverty rates (e.g., in Appalachia, Fig. 4) are associated with underestimating heat risk (10.260, p < 0.01). Additional three-way interaction effects reveal that counties with the highest proportions of older and lower-income Americans, whether predominantly White (−8.634, p < 0.01) or non-White (−5.371, p < 0.05 for non-White), tend to overestimate heat risk relative to assessments. Model performance and robustness

The heat risk perception estimates from the five models all produced Area Under the Curve (AUC) values of approximately 0.66 when evaluated on held-out splits of the survey data (Supplementary Table 3). These AUC values reflect the inherent challenges of predicting complex social attitudes at small geographic scales, where individual and contextual factors create substantial heterogeneity. AUC values here serve primarily as a diagnostic check for model learning, with the close match between cross-validated and final test/holdout AUCs indicating that all models captured meaningful signal from the survey data rather than overfitting (Supplementary Table 3, see “Methods”). For small area estimation applications, predictive performance is best evaluated by comparing modeled estimates to the observed survey data. We measured performance using the Mean Absolute Error (MAE), calculated as the difference between the weighted mean percentage of worried residents in the five most surveyed counties (n ≥ 112) and the modeled estimates (Supplementary Table 5). We compared five individual models and two ensemble approaches: one combining all five models (MAE = 2.01) and another using only the machine learning models (MAE = 1.57). The machine learning ensemble performed best and was selected for final analysis (Supplementary Tables 6, 7, Supplementary Fig. 2). This MAE of 1.57 indicates improved performance compared to prior MRP studies of climate and hazard-related opinions26,30. The top-performing individual models consistently identified temperature, latitude, and CDC social vulnerability measures of racial and ethnic minority status as the predictors with the greatest influence on heat risk perception (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

The Risk Analysis – Perception (RAP) framework provides a systematic approach for understanding misalignments between scientifically assessed risk and public perceptions. Our findings reveal distinct spatial patterns with important implications for targeted climate adaptation strategies.

Across most of the country, particularly in northern and eastern regions, public risk perceptions remain low despite increasing temperatures. Regional correlations between perceived and assessed risk vary dramatically—from R² = 0.21 in the West to R² = 0 in the Northeast—demonstrating how geographic contexts shape risk perceptions31,32. The stronger correlation in the South suggests that residents’ experiential knowledge aligns better with heat vulnerability, while patterns in the Northeast indicate fundamental disconnects between scientific assessments and public understanding.

The four risk alignment quadrants reveal distinct challenges requiring tailored interventions:

Quadrant I (“Danger Zone”: High assessed/Low perceived risk) encompasses many northwestern, Appalachian, Michigan, and Maine counties characterized by high White populations and poverty rates. These regions face adaptation challenges including limited historical exposure, older housing stock, healthcare access barriers, and cultural self-reliance norms that may resist public health advisories1. Public awareness campaigns, expanded cooling infrastructure, and integrated heat risk messaging are crucial for bridging perception gaps in these vulnerable communities.

Quadrant 2 (High assessed/High perceived risk) includes areas like Arizona and California where alignment between assessed and perceived risk suggests successful integration of analytical and experiential knowledge. Efforts to better account for extreme heat morbidity and mortality are underway in places such as Pima County, Arizona, where worry about heat is very high33. However, these regions also have distinct vulnerabilities, as some individuals may feel overconfident in their ability to handle extreme heat or assume they are already prepared due to past experience. This could lead to complacency, either from a sense of knowing how to handle the heat or from an underestimation of how quickly the climate is changing.

Quadrant 3 (Low assessed/High perceived risk) contains only two counties: Bossier Parish, Louisiana and Tom Green County, Texas. This rarity suggests that public heat risk perceptions rarely exceed scientifically assessed health risks nationwide, indicating that under-perception of risk is the dominant concern for heat risk communication efforts.

Quadrant 4 (Low assessed/Low perceived risk), primarily in the Northern Midwest, New England, and Southern Appalachians, represents regions with historically cooler climates but rapidly warming temperatures. Limited cooling infrastructure and preparedness reduce adaptive capacity, creating vulnerability to climate surprises and heat waves, with some states projected to have high future per-capita heat mortality rates despite current low risk classifications34,35. Public officials and households in these areas are also less likely to be prepared for extreme heat events due to their limited historical and cultural experience with them36.

Our results emphasize how poverty and demographics influence both assessed and perceived heat risks37. Higher perceived risk among Black communities likely reflects greater vulnerability and cultural knowledge systems that prioritize environmental health threats, illustrating how cultural knowledge systems from redlining and other forms of systemic racism can enhance, rather than bias, risk perception38. Such communities may benefit from support and resources that directly address systemic failures and build trust in authorities and institutions, helping them for and mitigate the effects of extreme heat25. The interaction effects between age, poverty, and race capture complex dynamics, where counties with the highest proportions of older and lower-income Americans—whether predominantly White or non-White—tend to perceive high risks from extreme heat relative to assessments, likely including many southern counties where heat concern is widespread and resources for staying cool are limited.

Lower perceived risks among older residents, despite their physiological vulnerability, illustrates reliance on outdated experiential heuristics based on historical climate conditions. Older populations have unique physiological concerns, such as reduced ability to sweat, greater reliance on multiple medications, and more mobility challenges that may limit access to cooling infrastructure39,40. As a result, older Americans need tailored outreach strategies that directly address these concerns while building on existing community knowledge systems.

A major barrier across all regions is inadequate identification and reporting of heat-related deaths compared to other hazards, contributing to public complacency. Federal agencies’ failure to categorize prolonged heat waves as disasters further limits awareness, monitoring, and communication efforts. The largely invisible impacts of extreme heat require enhanced national heat risk communication strategies.

While the HHI provides a robust health-focused risk assessment, it does not capture dynamic adaptations like improved warning systems or infrastructure upgrades (e.g., white roofs and green infrastructure). Our county-level analysis does not reveal finer-scale urban variations, involve stakeholders37, or directly measure behavioral responses to heat risk. Similar variables used in both worry prediction and risk assessment may lead to conservative gap estimates. Longer-term longitudinal studies could better illuminate changes in public concern and adaptive behaviors. Further research is also needed to examine how structural inequalities shape both perceived and actual vulnerability.

Extreme heat remains a growing public health threat with significant spatial variation in alignments between perceived and assessed risk. Our quadrant framework highlights the need for regionally tailored communication and adaptation strategies. Future efforts must focus on increasing awareness where heat risks are underestimated, leveraging existing concern for proactive adaptation, and integrating heat risk into broader disaster response frameworks. Given anticipated increases in extreme heat events, targeted communication efforts will be critical for enhancing community resilience and reducing heat-related health impacts.

Methods

Data

We used three types of data to model perceived heat risk—survey data, poststratification data, and aggregate (geographic-level) predictors.

Survey data

The survey is based on 10 nationally-representative “waves” collected each spring and fall from 2018–2022 via the IPSOS KnowledgePanel (Table 2). Despite the “KnowledgePanel” name, we do not employ panel data, but rather aggregate 10 cross-sectional surveys using this representative online panel of US adults ages 18 and older who are recruited and paid to complete self-administered questionnaires in a web-based environment. Through the survey, we collected demographic information (sex, age, race), location (latitude, longitude), and risk perceptions of various climate and weather-related hazards and their impacts. To protect respondent anonymity, individual locations were randomly displaced between 100 and 2000 feet depending on the local population density, ensuring privacy while preserving spatial accuracy for analysis. To measure extreme heat risk perceptions, survey respondents were asked, “How worried are you that extreme heat might harm your local area?” Prior research demonstrates that worry as a measure of perceived heat risk is highly correlated with perceived health harms from heat waves to one’s community, family, and self26. Responses to the worry question were dichotomized for modeling, with respondents who answered “Very worried” or “Moderately worried” coded as 1, indicating perceived heat risk. Respondents who answered “A little worried,” “Not at all worried,” or “Refused” were coded as 0, indicating little or no perceived heat risk. Excluding “A little worried” from the “positive” response category was a deliberate choice. We argue that this threshold represents a minimal level of perceived risk and is unlikely to drive the adaptive behaviors needed to effectively address the escalating public health risks of extreme heat. By focusing on stronger expressions of worry, our approach ensures that the resulting estimates are both meaningful and actionable for understanding public concern and guiding policy responses. In addition, by dichotomizing the response variable, we can estimate population percentages for every state and county in the CONUS (rather than a relative risk index) thereby providing a direct measure of where majorities or minorities of people are worried about heat—an actionable metric that can easily be used to support decision making.

Poststratification data

We used the 2022 5-year American Community Survey (ACS) data from the U.S. Census to construct a poststratification data frame for modeling across all survey years from 2018 to 2022. Custom race by education by sex population crosstabs were prepared for all U.S. states and all CONUS counties and county-equivalents. Demographic categories in the poststratification frame align exactly with those collected in the survey. The ACS does not directly provide race by education by sex cross-tabulations because of non-mutually exclusive relationships between race and ethnicity membership. We were able to use the ACS data to construct count crosstabs for ‘Hispanic or Latino’, ‘White, non-Hispanic or Latino’, ‘African American’, ‘Other, non-Hispanic or Latino’ racial categories. This approach generates some error since Americans who identify as ‘African American, Hispanic or Latino’ will be double counted in both the ‘African American’ and the ‘Hispanic or Latino’ categories; in practice, however, this error is minimal since this group is extremely small. Perceived heat risk is modeled at both the state and county levels using separate poststratification frames for each level. The state-level frame includes state and Census Region 9 information, while the county-level frame additionally includes county information. Thus, each row in the poststratification frame contains the population counts of a specific demographic subgroup within a defined geographic area.

Model predictors

Predictors used in modeling are described in Supplementary Table 1. Individual-level predictors included race, age, sex, and state of residence, consistent with prior MRP studies26. Year and U.S. census Region 9 were also included at the individual level to account for increasing perceived heat risk over time and spatial coherence in extreme heat events, respectively (Table 1).

Geographic-level (aggregate) predictors, including climatological, sociodemographic, and partisanship indicators were selected based on the literature and were further refined through exploratory data analysis and variable selection algorithms. Heat-specific measures were obtained from the PRISM Climate Group and the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA). Socioeconomic, housing, and additional demographic data were obtained from the CDC and the ACS. Historical state- and county-level voting behavior data are from the MIT Election Data and Science Lab given the partisan nature of climate change beliefs in the U.S41. Additional contextual predictors, including the mean area of a state or county covered by impervious surfaces and the latitude centroid for each state or county, were obtained using ArcGIS Pro (ESRI, v3.4)42. The former provides information about urbanicity and urban heat island effects and the latter captures temperature gradients.

Geographic-level predictors were appended to respondents in the state- and county-level poststratification frames. All predictors were either originally obtained at the county level and aggregated to the state level or were available at both geographic levels, ensuring consistent data coverage. Summary statistics for the county-level data are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Modeling perceived heat risk

We use a multilevel regression with poststratification (MRP) framework to estimate the percentage of residents worried about extreme heat in each county. MRP proceeds in two phases (Fig. 6): hierarchical modeling to generate predictions for demographic-geographic subgroups, followed by poststratification using Census population weights. MRP is considered the gold standard for small area estimation of public opinions26,43,44. Process diagram for Machine Learning and Multilevel Regression with Poststratification (MRP).

Five models were built to estimate the percentage of Americans worried about extreme heat at the national, state, and county levels. In phase 1, we appended demographic, climatological, and political covariates to the survey dataset (N = 11,113) and modeled the probabilities of being worried about extreme heat for all unique demo-geo-subgroups (e.g., African American females age 34–45 in Alabama were one demo-geo-subgroup). In phase 2, we summed the predicted probabilities for all demo-subgroups in each geographic area and divided by the total population count for that geography to obtain the percentage of all residents who are worried about extreme heat in that geographic unit.

Three models employed machine learning algorithms with hyperparameter optimization: extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), Random Forest (RF), and Elastic Net. Two hierarchical (multilevel) models incorporated systematic feature selection using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) or best subsets selection. PCA reduced dimensionality by converting correlated predictors into uncorrelated components, while best subsets regression evaluated all predictor combinations within a set.

Implementation

We refined predictors by removing variables with >95% identical observations and pairwise correlations >0.8, reducing multicollinearity and enhancing model performance. All models included demographic variables (state, region, race, age, sex) as random effects and year as a fixed effect. Machine learning models utilized all 16 predictors, while hierarchical models employed variable selection to identify optimal subsets. The baseline model was hierarchical and only included the demographic and year variables (Supplementary Equations 1–2).

Data were split 70–30 into training and test sets, stratified by state. Numerical predictors were Yeo-Johnson transformed to reduce skewness and then normalized. Hyperparameter tuning employed Latin hypercube sampling (500–1000 combinations) with 10-fold cross-validation using an area under the curve (AUC) metric. For hierarchical models, we tested predictor combinations using identical cross-validation procedures. The tuned models (Supplementary Tables 8–9) were then trained on the full training set and evaluated on the test set. Similar cross-validated and test AUCs indicate good model fit without over- or underfitting, as observed in our results (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Model selection and validation

Final models were trained on all survey data and applied to poststratification frames, with results weighted by Census demographics and averaged across 2018–2022 to minimize seasonal effects. Model validation compared predictions against survey data from the five most populous states and counties using mean absolute error (MAE).

The ensemble of machine learning models (XGBoost, Random Forest, Elastic Net) achieved the lowest error (Supplementary Table 6) compared with a baseline model that included only individual-level and geographic predictors. Predictions were ensembled as weighted averages of individual model predictions, with weights determined by MAE rank such that lower-MAE (higher-ranked) models had greater influence (Eq. 1). Weights were then normalized to sum to 1 (Eq. 2) and applied to predictions before calculating the ensemble MAEs (Eq. 3). Bootstrap confidence intervals (n = 10,000) confirmed ensemble reliability (Supplementary Table 7).

Alignment gap analysis

Assessed heat risk was defined using the Heat-Health Index (HHI), which integrates data from four modules: (1) Historical Heat and Health Burden (past heat wave occurrences and heat-related mortality); (2) Sensitivity (pre-existing health conditions increasing heat susceptibility); (3) Sociodemographic Characteristics (poverty, unemployment, social isolation affecting adaptive capacity); and (4) Natural and Built Environment (vegetation, land cover, urban infrastructure, and air pollution) (Supplementary Table 10). The HHI was generated at the ZIP code level and aggregated to county and state levels using HUD crosswalk data, weighted by residential address proportions.
