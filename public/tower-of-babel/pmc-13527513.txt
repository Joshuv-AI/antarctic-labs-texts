Video dataset for surgical phase, keypoint, and instrument recognition in laparoscopic surgery (PhaKIR)
Authors: Tobias Rueckert, Raphaela Maerkl, David Rauber, Leonard Klausmann, Max Gutbrod, Daniel Rueckert, Hubertus Feussner, Dirk Wilhelm, Christoph Palm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis data article describes the Surgical Procedure Phase, Keypoint, and Instrument Recognition (PhaKIR) dataset, a collection of eight complete laparoscopic cholecystectomy videos acquired during routine minimally invasive procedures at three German medical centers. The recordings were captured with different monocular endoscopic camera systems at 25 frames per second and a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels, with durations ranging from 28 to 58 min. Segments showing regions outside the abdominal cavity were removed for anonymization, and the corresponding cut indices are provided.The dataset offers unified annotations for three interrelated tasks on the same complete surgical sequences: surgical phase recognition, annotated for every frame at 25 fps (485,875 frames), instrument keypoint estimation (19,435 frames), and instrument instance segmentation (19,435 frames), the latter two annotated at one frame per second (every 25th frame). Surgical phases follow the seven-phase Cholec80 scheme, extended by an undefined label for transitional frames. The instrument annotations distinguish 19 instrument classes as well as individual instances of the same class, with keypoint annotations comprising two to four class-dependent points labeled with COCO-style visibility states. Segmentation and keypoint labels were created manually using the Computer Vision Annotation Tool (CVAT), whereas phase labels were derived from documented phase-transition timestamps, and all annotations underwent a multi-stage review by a medically trained team. The data are provided in open formats (MP4, CSV, JSON, PNG) together with a frame-extraction script under the CC BY-NC-SA license and are available through controlled access upon request via the Zenodo platform.Because the dataset combines procedural context, instrument pose, and pixel-accurate instance segmentations within full-length recordings from multiple institutions, it can be reused for single-task or multi-task model development, for temporally aware approaches that exploit motion continuity, and for cross-institutional generalization protocols such as leave-one-hospital-out evaluation. The dataset served as the training resource for the PhaKIR Challenge at the Endoscopic Vision (EndoVis) Challenge at MICCAI 2024.Keywords: Cholecystectomy, Endoscopy, Robot-assisted interventions, Surgical workflow analysis, Instance segmentation, Pose estimation, Temporal modeling

Background

This data article documents the Surgical Procedure Phase, Keypoint, and Instrument Recognition (PhaKIR) dataset, compiled for the PhaKIR sub-challenge organized within the Endoscopic Vision (EndoVis) Challenge at MICCAI 2024 and reported in the companion research article [1]. The challenge addressed a recurring limitation in surgical data science: methods for instrument recognition and surgical workflow analysis are typically trained and evaluated on datasets that target a single task, omit complete procedure sequences, and originate from a single medical center, which constrains the study of temporal context and cross-institutional generalization [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. A tabular comparison of the related challenge datasets is provided in Table 1 of the companion research article [1], and a comprehensive review of the datasets available in this field is given in [2]. The dataset was therefore designed to combine three interrelated tasks — surgical phase recognition, instrument keypoint estimation, and instrument instance segmentation — within full-length laparoscopic cholecystectomy recordings from several institutions. Whereas the companion article [1] presents the challenge design and the comparative evaluation of the submitted methods, the present article focuses on the data resource itself. It provides a detailed account of the acquisition setup, the annotation protocols for all three tasks, the ground-truth generation, the file organization, and the access conditions, thereby supporting transparent reuse of the data independently of the original challenge.

Data Description

The PhaKIR dataset comprises eight complete laparoscopic cholecystectomy videos recorded at three medical centers, accompanied by frame-level annotations for three interrelated tasks: surgical phase recognition, instrument keypoint estimation, and instrument instance segmentation. An overview of the source video data and the corresponding annotations for the three tasks across the three contributing centers is shown in Fig. 1. All videos were acquired at 25 frames per second (fps) and a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels, with durations between 28 and 58 min. Surgical phase labels are provided for every frame (485,875 frames in total), whereas instrument keypoint and instance segmentation annotations are provided for every 25th frame, corresponding to 1 fps (19,435 annotated frames per task). All annotation types cover the identical, complete surgical sequences. The dataset is available through controlled access upon request via the Zenodo platform [9]. The access procedure is described in the section "Publication and access".

Extracted frames are written to subfolders of 1000 images each (named 0, 1000, …), with filenames zero-padded to six digits (e.g., frame_055649.png). Frame indices are zero-based throughout the dataset: the first frame of a video has index 0, and the same index is used in the extracted frame filenames, the mask filenames, the phase CSV files, the keypoint JSON files, and the cut-index CSV files, so that all annotation types can be aligned by frame index without any offset. All data are provided in open formats (CSV, JSON, PNG, MP4).

Each Video_xx_Phases.csv lists, for every frame, the frame index and the assigned phase label. The phase labels comprise the seven phases defined in the Cholec80 dataset [10] — Preparation, Calot Triangle Dissection, Clipping and Cutting, Gallbladder Dissection, Gallbladder Packaging, Cleaning and Coagulation, and Gallbladder Retraction — together with an additional undefined label for transitional frames. Frames carrying this label were excluded from the evaluation in the PhaKIR challenge [1]; we recommend adopting this convention for results intended to be comparable with the challenge. Each Video_xx_Keypoints.json stores keypoint annotations in JSON format, structured per frame and, within each frame, per instrument instance, with each entry providing the keypoint coordinates and visibility state. Each keypoint additionally carries a visibility state following the COCO protocol [11] (visible, occluded, or not available); keypoints that are not applicable to a given instrument are marked as not available. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The PhaKIR dataset was created following the four-stage workflow summarized in Fig. 6: acquisition of the surgical videos, manual annotation of the three tasks, multi-stage validation of the annotations, and publication of the dataset under a controlled-access procedure. The subsections below follow this order and specify the tools, software, and scripts used in each stage.

Creation pipeline of the PhaKIR dataset, from video acquisition and anonymization through manual annotation of the three tasks and multi-stage validation to publication under controlled access. The lower row indicates the data artifacts resulting from each stage.

Data acquisition

A total of n = 8 videos were recorded during routine minimally invasive cholecystectomies performed on adult patients of different genders. Recordings were acquired with different endoscopic cameras at 25 frames per second (fps) and a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels, with durations ranging from 28 to 58 min. The videos originated from three German hospitals: TUM University Hospital Rechts der Isar (MRI; n = 6), Heidelberg University Hospital (UKHD; n = 1), and Weilheim-Schongau Hospital (KWS; n = 1). The single Heidelberg recording corresponds to HeiChole2.mp4, originally released as part of the HeiChole benchmark [8], and was re-annotated for protocol consistency. Sequences depicting regions outside the abdominal cavity were removed during anonymization; the corresponding cut indices are provided for each video in Video_xx_Cuts.csv. The source hospital, duration, and per-task annotation counts of all videos are listed in Table 1.

Annotation

Annotation team

Annotations were created by four annotators with medical expertise: one senior surgeon and three medically trained students.

Frame extraction for annotation

For surgical phase recognition, every frame was annotated at the native rate of 25 fps, resulting in 485,875 labeled frames. For instrument instance segmentation and instrument keypoint estimation, one frame per second (every 25th frame) was annotated, resulting in 19,435 labeled frames per task. All three tasks cover the identical and complete surgical sequences.

Task 1 - surgical phase recognition

Each frame was assigned to one of the seven surgical phases defined in the Cholec80 dataset [10], with an additional undefined label to capture transitions. Phase-transition timestamps were documented during manual review of the full-length recordings, without timeline restrictions. The start of a phase was defined as the first appearance of the characteristic instruments performing the essential actions of that phase, and its end as the disappearance of all such instruments. The documented timestamps were converted into CSV files, and all frames between the start and end markers of a phase were automatically assigned to the corresponding label, yielding the frame-level phase annotations stored in Video_xx_Phases.csv. The Heidelberg recording was fully re-annotated according to this protocol.

Task 2 - instrument instance segmentation

Instruments were segmented in every 25th frame and assigned to one of 19 instrument classes, with each pixel labeled as either instrument or background, and multiple instances of the same class distinguished. Segmentation was performed with the Computer Vision Annotation Tool (CVAT) [12] using polygon contours; only the visible parts of the instruments were labeled. During annotation, different instances of the same class were temporarily separated using pseudo-classes to support ground-truth generation. The manual annotations were subsequently converted into segmentation masks by means of Python scripts. Each mask is encoded as a three-channel PNG image in which the red and green channels encode the instrument class and the blue channel encodes the instrument instance; instances of the same class therefore share identical red and green values and differ only in the blue channel. The per-class RGB codes are listed in Table 4, and the resulting masks are provided in Video_xx_Masks.zip.

Task 3 - instrument keypoint estimation

For every 25th frame, keypoints describing the instrument pose were annotated together with their visibility status. For each instrument, a set of predefined keypoints was specified: the End Point, where the instrument enters the image border; the Shaft Point, marking the shaft-to-tip junction; and the instrument tip, which for opening tools was represented by two separate points (Tip 1 and Tip 2). The number of keypoints was class-dependent and ranged from two to four per instrument; the number and type of keypoints associated with each instrument class are listed in Table 3. Keypoints were annotated in CVAT [12] using the point function. Each keypoint was assigned a visibility status following the COCO protocol [11] (visible, occluded, or not available), and keypoints not applicable to a given instrument were marked as not available. When placing the keypoints, the annotators had access to the full video sequences, including both preceding and subsequent frames; the temporal continuity and expected motion of the instruments were taken into account to refine the placement, particularly when the target region was partially occluded. The annotations were stored in JSON format, structured per frame and, within each frame, per instrument instance, in Video_xx_Keypoints.json.

Validation of Annotations

General approach

Prior to annotation, the team was provided with a detailed annotation protocol containing task-specific guidelines and illustrative examples, developed jointly with medical experts and refined during the annotation process. Each video was first annotated by a single annotator, who then re-inspected the annotations to correct potential oversights. Two additional team members subsequently verified the annotations sequentially according to the protocol, so that each video was reviewed three times after its initial annotation. The annotation protocol is published as Appendix B (Supplementary file S1) of the companion research article [1], which is openly accessible. Adjudication followed a sequential rather than a consensus scheme: each reviewer applied corrections directly to the annotations of the preceding pass, with the protocol serving as the binding reference, so that the state after the third review pass constitutes the released ground truth. The task-specific rules governing ambiguous cases are part of the protocol, which was refined during the annotation process whenever a case was not yet covered.

Task 1 - surgical phase recognition

The annotated timestamps were repeatedly checked following the procedure described above. In addition, plausibility checks were performed by verifying the presence of the expected instruments in the corresponding phases, as certain instrument classes occur only in specific phases. No quantitative correction statistics were recorded for this task.

Task 2 - instrument instance segmentation

The verification of the original annotations was supplemented by visual inspection. The annotations were visualized and combined side by side with the original frames into composite images, from which a correction video was generated and subjected to the three-stage validation procedure. Monitoring the color-coded instrument representations over time allowed inconsistencies to be detected, such as changes in the assigned class of an instrument between consecutive frames. Between the second and third review passes, correction rates were quantified using the multi-instance, multi-class Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) applied analogously to the PhaKIR challenge evaluation [1], which yielded an annotation agreement of 83.64%. The principal source of disagreement was the classification of instruments, for which a misclassification entered the result as a DSC of zero for the affected instrument instance.

Task 3 - instrument keypoint estimation

Keypoint annotations were likewise verified by visual inspection. As the keypoints were annotated in CVAT on top of the segmentation labels, the segmentations and their classifications were implicitly re-examined during this step. The keypoints were annotated by a team member who had not contributed to the original segmentations or to the segmentation correction runs. The input images were combined with the keypoint annotations, compiled into videos, and subjected to the three-stage validation procedure. No quantitative correction rates were tracked for this task; quality control relied on repeated visual inspection and temporal consistency checks under occlusion.

Community use during the challenge

The dataset served as the training resource of the PhaKIR challenge at MICCAI 2024 [1], where it was downloaded and inspected by 66 registered teams. Rare annotation errors identified and reported by participants during the challenge were corrected, and the dataset was updated accordingly. No systematic errors were reported during the challenge.

Publication and Access

Assembly of the released dataset

For each video, the raw video file, the cut indices, and the annotations for all three tasks were bundled into a single archive (Video_xx.zip). To reduce storage requirements, the complete videos were distributed rather than pre-extracted frames; a separate frame-extraction script (see below) allows users to control the frame compression. The resulting root-level organization of the dataset is shown in Fig. 2.

Access conditions and licensing

Because the recordings constitute sensitive surgical patient data, the dataset is not offered as an unrestricted download but as a restricted-access record on Zenodo [9]. Access is requested through the request function of the record, stating the requesting institution and the intended use. Institutional affiliation is recorded for documentation purposes but is not an approval criterion. Requests are granted if the stated purpose is non-commercial research consistent with the CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 license [13]; no separate data-use agreement is required beyond this statement. Requests are reviewed by the corresponding authors and are typically processed within one working day, after which approved users obtain download access to the complete dataset. Access control and licensing serve complementary purposes: the request step documents who receives the data, whereas the license defines the permitted reuse — non-commercial use only, citation of this article, the companion research article [1], and the HeiChole benchmark [8], and distribution of derived material under identical terms. Incomplete requests are not declined outright but returned for clarification and may be resubmitted.

A single access request covers the complete record, that is, all video files, phase annotations, keypoint annotations, segmentation masks, and cut indices; no individual component is subject to a separate request. The frame-extraction script is not part of the restricted record and is available without registration from the public GitHub repository (see "Source code and frame extraction"). Apart from this script, no further software is required to use the dataset; the evaluation metrics and the evaluation protocol applied in the PhaKIR challenge are defined in the companion research article [1]. The dataset is archived on Zenodo, which provides persistent identification and long-term availability. The DOI cited in this article is the concept DOI, which always resolves to the most recent version of the record. Individual versions additionally receive their own DOI, so that revised or extended releases can be published without invalidating existing references.

Source code and frame extraction

The frame-extraction script split_video_in_frames.py is provided in a dedicated GitHub repository (https://github.com/remic-othr/PhaKIR_Dataset) rather than within the Zenodo archive, so that updates to the script do not trigger a new dataset version. It splits the video sequences into individual frames and allows the compression rate of the resulting frames to be controlled. The script is written in Python and requires no external dependencies beyond the standard library. Applied to Video_xx.mp4, it writes the extracted frames into subfolders of 1000 images each, with filenames zero-padded to six digits (e.g., frame_055649.png); the segmentation masks in Video_xx_Masks.zip follow the same subfolder organization and filename convention (e.g., 055650_mask.png). The script is released under the same license as the dataset (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0) [13].

Limitations

Several limitations of the dataset should be considered. First, the number of procedures is limited to eight complete recordings, which may not capture the full range of variability present in surgical practice. Second, all recordings depict laparoscopic cholecystectomies, so other types of surgical procedures are not represented. Third, the data originate exclusively from German medical centers, and surgical practices for cholecystectomy may differ between countries. Fourth, the dataset contains only RGB video; no further multimodal information (e.g., depth, kinematic, or sensor data) is included. Fifth, the frequency of occurrence of the instrument classes is highly imbalanced, with some classes appearing in several thousand frames and others in fewer than one hundred. Sixth, while surgical phase labels are provided for every frame, instrument keypoint and instance segmentation annotations are available only at one frame per second (every 25th frame). Finally, for the instrument instance segmentation task, only the visible parts of the instruments were annotated.

These limitations also delineate the directions in which the resource can be extended. The most immediate one is a broader institutional basis: in the PhaKIR challenge, the submitted methods performed consistently worse on the test recordings from the two centers that were less represented in the training data, which was attributed to the predominance of a single center in that data [1]. Extending the dataset with recordings from additional centers, including centers outside Germany, and with a more balanced number of procedures per center would therefore allow leave-one-hospital-out protocols to be evaluated with several recordings per held-out institution. A second direction is the inclusion of further laparoscopic procedure types beyond cholecystectomy. The annotation protocol, the mask encoding, and the keypoint definitions are not specific to cholecystectomy and can be transferred to other interventions, requiring only an extension of the instrument vocabulary, so that cross-procedure transfer and domain shift could be studied within a consistent annotation scheme. Beyond these two aspects, the targeted acquisition of sequences containing rarely observed instrument classes would mitigate the class imbalance, a higher annotation frequency for the segmentation and keypoint tasks would support dense temporal modeling and instrument tracking, and complementary modalities such as depth or kinematic data would broaden the range of addressable research questions.

Ethics Statement

The dataset comprises endoscopic video recordings acquired during routine laparoscopic cholecystectomies. The underlying research project was approved by the institutional ethics committee of the Technical University of Munich (TUM; approval code 337/21 S-EB) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participating patients for the anonymized use of their recordings for research purposes.

Only fully anonymized data were released. The recordings capture exclusively the intra-abdominal surgical field; no patient-identifying information, demographic records, or other clinical metadata were collected or stored. To further protect the anonymity of patients and operating-room staff, all segments recorded outside the abdominal cavity were removed prior to publication, with the corresponding frame indices documented in the Video_xx_Cuts.csv files. The released data therefore contain no personal data within the meaning of applicable data-protection regulations.

The authors have read and follow the ethical requirements for publication in Data in Brief.
