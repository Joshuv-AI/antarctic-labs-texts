Citric acid alters Arabidopsis root morphology and development through ROS-dependent and ROS-independent mechanisms
Authors: Tao Zhang, Jesus T Peng, Jie Chu, Shihong Luo, Jihyeon Lee, Diana Belén Sánchez Rodríguez, Katreena Gundran, Xuan Xia, Adriana Garay-Arroyo, Jocelyn A Richardson, Alexandra J Dickinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCitric acid is an integral component of primary metabolism and cellular energetics and plays extensive roles in other cellular processes, such as signaling, chelating, and exudation. Here, we characterized the unique effects that citric acid has on Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) root structure and development. In particular, we investigated how citric acid modifies 2 root types in opposing ways: by inhibiting primary root growth while promoting anchor root growth. To understand the mechanisms driving these different growth patterns within the same organism, we analyzed nutrient and transcriptomic responses to citric acid treatment in anchor roots and primary roots. High-spatial resolution elemental analysis revealed that root meristems and the root–hypocotyl junction are regions of strong nutrient enrichment, but that citric acid treatment has little effect on nutrient levels in these regions. Transcriptional analysis revealed major differences between primary roots and anchor roots in response to citric acid. In particular, citric acid acted as a reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenger through increased Class III peroxidase transcription, effectively reducing H2O2 levels both in vitro and in vivo. Altering the ROS balance at the root–hypocotyl junction was sufficient to induce anchor root formation. Citric acid treatment also differentially upregulated lignin biosynthesis, lignin assembly, and ETHYLENE RESPONSE FACTOR 115 expression in primary roots and anchor roots. ETHYLENE RESPONSE FACTOR 115 regulates the quiescent center and root columella, and we found that citric acid treatment induces developmental defects in this tissue. Overall, this study reveals that a vital organic acid produced and secreted at relatively high concentrations has both widespread and specific effects on plant development and root architecture.

Introduction

Plant root systems are vital first-responders to the local environment. The highly flexible nature of plant development allows them to integrate many fluctuating molecular and environmental signals. Arabidopsis thaliana root architecture is typically comprised of an apically dominant primary root (PR) with lateral roots protruding along its length, anchor roots (ARs) emerging from the root–hypocotyl junction, and adventitious roots branching from shoot tissue under specific conditions. During root cell differentiation, cells acquire specialized features. For example, vascular cells develop structural components like cellulose, lignin, and suberin, which contribute to the root's mechanical properties and impermeability. External stimuli can directly and indirectly affect different stages of root development. For instance, mechanical and chemical signals can regulate gene expression pathways that control cell growth, elongation, differentiation, and branching, ultimately modifying the root system architecture (López-Bucio et al. 2003; Braam 2005; Lynch 2007; Coomey et al. 2024). Flexible control of root structures allows plants to effectively regulate the uptake of water, acquisition of nutrients, and responses to environmental and pathogenic stress (López-Bucio et al. 2002; Julkowska et al. 2014; Couzigou and Combier 2016).

Intermediate metabolites of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle provide an exciting avenue for understanding how root development is orchestrated. Not only are these small molecules directly responsible for maintaining cellular energetics, but certain intermediates can also undergo noncyclic flux and cataplerosis to produce a diverse array of vital secondary metabolites such as fatty acids, nucleotides, and porphyrins. Organic acids are an important component of the matrix secreted by plant roots into the surrounding soil, which forms a chemically and biotically distinct environment known as the rhizosphere. Among the primary metabolites, citric acid (CA) is of prominence in plant research due to its high exudation rate and known roles as a nutrient and metal chelator and rhizosphere effector (Tahjib-Ul-Arif et al. 2021).

As an integral component of the TCA cycle, CA is essential for almost all respiring organisms. Beyond the TCA cycle, CA has been found to play various biological roles in different systems. For instance, CA is critical for chelating calcium and is an important structural component involved in bone formation and regeneration (Franklin et al. 2014; Granchi et al. 2019). CA has also been used for various medicinal purposes, including as an anticoagulant (Ma et al. 2018). In mice, it has been shown to decrease inflammation in response to oxidative stress (Abdel-Salam et al. 2014). In plants, exogenous CA treatment has been shown to ameliorate heat, drought, salinity, and heavy metal stresses, which has been attributed to an increase in the activities of antioxidant enzymes (Gao et al. 2010; Sun and Hong 2011; Yeh and Lin 2012; Ehsan et al. 2014; Phan et al. 2017; Mahmud et al. 2018; Tahjib-Ul-Arif et al. 2021; Xie et al. 2022; Ali et al. 2024). Exogenous CA promotes the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, including flavonoids (Preciado-Rangel et al. 2018), anthocyanins (Kaur et al. 2017), and phenolic compounds (Limón et al. 2015; Li et al. 2019). CA can mitigate heavy metal stress by forming complexes with ions including lead, cadmium, nickel, and aluminum. Furthermore, naturally exudated CA can increase the acidification of local soil, thereby increasing the bioavailability of vital nutrients such as inorganic phosphorus and insoluble iron (Du et al. 2024). The internal transport of iron also relies on CA, which forms complexes with ferric ions to facilitate long distance iron transport along the xylem and phloem. In Arabidopsis, the efflux of CA by MULTIDRUG and TOXIN EFFLUX and ferric reductase defective 3 is critical for the delivery of iron to the aerial portions of the plant (Roschzttardtz et al. 2011; Chao and Chao 2022; Xu et al. 2022). CA is also an important allosteric regulator for enzymes involved in primary and secondary metabolite regulation. For example, in glycolysis, CA acts as an inhibitor of phosphofructokinase-1 (Underwood and Newsholme 1965). Recently, CA was discovered to play a role in strigolactone signaling pathway by inducing a conformational switch in the SCF-D3/MAX2 ubiquitin ligase complex (Tal et al. 2022). All in all, CA is an important naturally secreted compound that plays multifaceted roles affecting plant growth.

In Arabidopsis, there are 5 CITRATE SYNTHASE (CSY) genes, which catalyze the condensation of oxaloacetate and acetyl-Coenzyme A (acetyl-CoA) into citrate. Of these genes, AtCSY2 and AtCSY3 are localized to the peroxisome and play crucial roles in seed germination and fatty acid respiration (Pracharoenwattana et al. 2005). AtCSY4 is primarily localized in mitochondria, although it has also been detected in the cell wall, a notable observation because this structure is not normally associated with primary metabolism (Slabas et al. 2004). The Cys326 residue in AtCSY4 is important for redox-dependent oligomerization and enzyme activity (Nishio and Mizushima 2020; Schmidtmann et al. 2014). Furthermore, AtCSYs have been identified as targets for thioredoxins, a redox regulatory protein (Balmer et al. 2004). Investigations into the function of CSY have revealed its importance in growth and development. Knockdown mitochondrial CSY in tomato and potato showed impaired changes in growth rate and flowering development respectively (Landschütze et al. 1995; Sienkiewicz-Porzucek et al. 2008), suggesting a tissue-specific role for CA. Moreover, overexpression of CSY and exogenous CA treatment in yeast and plants increases CA exudation and improves growth in high aluminum stress and low phosphate conditions (Koyama et al. 1999; Koyama et al. 2000; Deng et al. 2009; Han et al. 2009; Mora-Macías et al. 2017; Yan et al. 2023).

While there has been substantial effort to examine the downstream pathways of CA and its roles in altering the root–soil environment, little is known about how CA may regulate plant growth. Previously, we found that CA treatment inhibits the development and growth of PR, lateral roots, and root hairs in a concentration-dependent manner, but induces the formation of ARs (Zhang et al. 2023). However, the mechanisms underlying these phenotypic changes remain unknown. The stark contrast in CA response between these genetically identical root types provides a unique opportunity to better understand the mechanisms governing both root development and CA functions. We previously observed that exogenous CA application can dramatically alter ROS homeostasis by reducing hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) levels and increasing superoxide (O2•−) in the root. Here, we found that a key difference between AR and PR response to CA was in the regulation of H2O2 scavenging processes. We observed that CA upregulates Class III peroxidases and lignin synthesis genes in a tissue-specific manner—notably, both of these types of enzymes consume H2O2. In addition, we found that altering the balance of H2O2 in the root–hypocotyl junction is sufficient to trigger AR growth. Furthermore, we found that CA affects root stem cell and quiescent cell development, partially through changes in ERF115 expression. Examining how this foundational metabolite contributes to developmental decisions provides key insights into plant physiology and offers a valuable tool for understanding how a single stimulus can induce alternative developmental responses within the same root system.

Results

CA treatment elicits tissue-specific phenotypes in Arabidopsis roots

We previously reported that Arabidopsis exhibits different and sometimes opposing tissue-specific responses to TCA cycle metabolite treatments (Zhang et al. 2023). For instance, isocitrate treatment induces PR growth, but its direct downstream intermediate, alpha-ketoglutarate, inhibits PR growth. In the case of CA treatment, we observed a dose-dependent set of phenotypes, including inhibition in root hair development, PR length, and lateral root number. However, CA significantly promotes AR number (Fig. 1). ARs emerge specifically from the most mature region of the PR—directly below the root–hypocotyl junction. AR emergence is a normal developmental feature of an Arabidopsis root system, although their emergence is increased by certain types of environmental stress, such as nitrogen deficiency (Jia et al. 2019). Notably, the only 2 TCA cycle metabolites that affected AR growth were CA and its precursor, oxaloacetate. Of the 2, CA is the strongest inducer of AR growth (Zhang et al. 2023). Previously, we found that CA inhibition of root hair growth (Fig. 1e) was consistent with its suppression of H2O2, as reduction in cytosolic and apoplastic H2O2 has been shown to decrease root hair length (Dunand et al. 2007). However, the mechanisms underpinning the structural root phenotypes caused by CA remained uncharacterized. Despite the role of CA as a key metabolite in central metabolism and cellular energetics, we previously found that its exogenous application does not significantly disrupt the level of detectable ATP in the root (Zhang et al. 2023).

To further investigate the mechanisms leading to the specific effects of CA treatment on root morphology, we treated Arabidopsis seeds with 0 to 5 mm CA. These concentrations are consistent with those commonly used in plant and mammalian research (Hu et al. 2016; Balmer et al. 2018; Zhao et al. 2022) and are comparable with amounts found endogenously in Arabidopsis tissue (Doireau et al. 2024). In order to mitigate the pronounced morphological effects associated with low pH and isolate the phenotypes caused by the CA metabolite itself, we used a sodium citrate–CA solution buffered to the growth media pH of 5.7. We confirmed that the effect of CA on PR length is dose dependent (Zhang et al. 2023): as the concentration of CA is increased, PRs shorten. This is due to a reduced number of meristem cells and a decreased length of mature root cells (Figs. 1b and S1). Concentrations from 1 to 5 mm CA significantly decrease the number of lateral roots per plant (Fig. 1c). However, these same concentrations significantly increase the number of ARs (Fig. 1d), indicating that the development of ARs may be resistant to the stress induced by CA treatment. Similarly, although root hair elongation is severely inhibited in PRs upon CA treatment, root hairs remain normal in ARs of the same plant (Fig. 1e). These results demonstrate that ARs and PRs have dramatically different developmental responses to CA.

To investigate if exogenous CA affects root development by altering endogenous TCA metabolism, we conducted HPLC-MS/MS analysis. We found that exogenous 5 mm CA treatment increases endogenous levels of fumarate, succinate, malate, and α-ketoglutarate (Fig. 1f), while levels of cis-aconitate remain unchanged. Endogenous CA levels decreased, suggesting that exogenously supplied CA is not elevating endogenous citrate after 2 wk of treatment (Figs. 1f and S2a). This may be due to CA exclusion from the root, secretion at a faster rate than uptake, conversion to other metabolites, or some combination of all 3. When we examined the pH of the Arabidopsis media after CA treatment, we found that the pH initially matched that of the buffered citrate/CA treatment (pH 5.7). However, after 1 wk, the media surrounding the roots had become notably more acidic, indicating that buffered CA treatment is resulting in acidification of the rhizosphere over time (Fig. S2b).

Because CA acidity has been shown to increase the bioavailability of nutrients like phosphorous and iron in the soil, we wanted to determine if CA treatment was changing the nutrient profile in Arabidopsis roots when grown in ½ MS media. To test this, we performed X-ray spectromicroscopy to analyze phosphorous (P), iron (Fe), zinc (Zn), and manganese (Mn) at high-spatial resolution across the entire root. We found that P, Fe, and Zn are consistently and strongly enriched in PR meristems, AR meristems, and root–hypocotyl junctions under control and CA treatments (Figs. 1g and S3a). Notably, this spatial enrichment information could be useful for engineering strategies to enhance metal or nutrient storage in plants.

The enrichment of nutrients in AR meristems and the contiguous root–hypocotyl junction suggests that ARs may be involved in nutrient uptake or storage. However, despite the increase of ARs caused by CA, we find that CA treatment did not increase the levels of any nutrients in the PR meristem or at the root–hypocotyl junction (Figs. 1g and S3b and c). The levels of Fe and P remained relatively constant across all measured regions, despite CA's known role as a transporter of these nutrients, suggesting that CA treatment does not enhance their levels in the roots in ½ MS media. Furthermore, CA treatment did not affect nutrient (Fe, Cu, Zn, P, S, K, Ca, Mn, and Ni) levels in the PR tip. The only significant effect that we measured is at the root–hypocotyl junction, where CA selectively reduces potassium (K) levels while leaving other nutrient levels unchanged (Fig. S3b and c). However, bulk inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) analysis of shoots revealed that CA treatment significantly reduces concentrations of manganese (Mn), iron (Fe), copper (Cu), potassium (K), and calcium (Ca) in treated plants, but causes no significant difference in phosphorus (P), zinc (Zn), sulfur (S), or nickel (Ni) compared with the controls (Fig S4). This indicates that CA influences the concentration of certain nutrients in the shoot tissue. Overall, this suggests that our CA treatment regime does not increase nutrient levels in the shoot or root, as might be expected, but can reduce levels of certain nutrients in the shoot.

RNA-seq data reveal that ROS-related pathways are differentially expressed in CA-treated ARs and PRs

To understand how CA triggers 2 different developmental responses in genetically and morphologically identical roots, we performed bulk RNA-seq analysis comparing PRs with ARs. Plants were continuously grown in 5 mm CA or in control media for 7 d. For PRs, RNA-seq data revealed 1,121 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in CA treatment compared with control treatment. In CA treatment, we uncovered 2,196 DEGs between ARs and PRs (Fig. 2a). This is a striking result that suggests substantial transcriptional differences between primary and ARs during CA treatment. Furthermore, analysis of the DEGs that were unique to each experimental condition revealed a large proportion of genes were unique to the CAAR vs CAPR condition (1,778 out of 2,196, ∼81%), whereas the proportion of genes unique to the CAPR vs control PR (ContPR) condition was far lower (703 out of 1,121, ∼32%) (Fig. 2b). This further suggests that CAAR are more transcriptionally distinct from CAPR than CAPR and ContPR are from each other. To explore the connection between genes consistently affected by CA treatment, we examined the correlation between the 418 DEGs shared by both datasets. Here, we found that the majority of the genes that significantly change during CA treatment exhibited opposite regulation (347 out of 418, ∼83%). In other words, most of the genes upregulated by CA in the PR are downregulated in CA-treated ARs, when compared with control roots (Fig. 2b and c). Taken together, the opposing effects of CA on AR and PR growth are supported by distinct and often opposing transcriptomic regulation.

To understand what types of genes are influenced by CA, we performed gene ontology (GO) term analysis of the DEGs in CA-treated PRs compared with ContPRs. This revealed enrichment of genes in a number of pathways, including a notable enrichment of functional terms associated with stress response and ROS (Fig. 2d). These included processes such as H2O2 catabolism, tetrapyrrole and heme binding, oxidoreductase activities, and oxidative stress response (Fig. 2d). We next performed GO term analysis of the DEGs in ARs compared with PRs during CA treatment. Again, we found significant enrichment in ROS-related, cell wall-related, and stress response pathways among these DEGs (Fig. S5a and b). To explore the relationships between all the treatment conditions and root types, we performed a Venn diagram analysis of all upregulated and downregulated genes. We found that there was only 1 single gene (AT4G07195) that was downregulated in both the CAAR–CAPR data set and the CAPR–ContPR data set (Fig. S5c and d). There were no genes that were upregulated in both data sets. This indicates that although CA is changing transcription in similar types of genes—particularly ROS, cell wall, and stress-related genes—in both the primary and AR, there are substantial differences in the exact genes and the direction of these changes.

Altering the balance of H2O2 at the root–hypocotyl junction is sufficient to induce AR growth

To explore the role of ROS in the differential effects of CA on root structure and development, we examined H2O2 levels in the PR and the root/shoot junction, where ARs originate, using 35S-HyPer, a highly specific genetically encoded fluorescent probe for H2O248,49. We found that 5 mm CA significantly reduced the 488/405 nm ratio in 35S-HyPer, indicating a decrease in in vivo H2O2 levels (Fig. 3a). In addition, CA reduces H2O2 in the hypocotyl, right above the root junction. However, the reduction was most pronounced in the most mature regions of the root tissue, directly below the hypocotyl. In contrast, the hypocotyl itself exhibited the weakest reduction (Fig. 3b). This phenotype raised the question: is the ratio of H2O2 between the hypocotyl and root important for AR development? To test this, we altered the H2O2 ratio at the root–hypocotyl junction by selectively applying exogenous H2O2 directly to the hypocotyl or to the mature root. We also tested the effect of applying exogenous H2O2 to the PR tips (Fig. 3c). We observed that the only H2O2 treatment at the hypocotyl, directly above the root junction, significantly induces adventitious and ARs (Fig. 3d). In contrast, H2O2 treatment on the mature root, directly below the root–hypocotyl junction, inhibits growth of all roots (Fig. 3d). Notably, H2O2 treatment in any of the regions tested caused inhibition of PR growth (Fig. 3d). This data shows that altering the spatial distribution of H2O2 is sufficient to induce AR emergence, suggesting that H2O2 homeostasis is important for AR patterning.

CA treatment leads to drastic differences in root hair elongation between the PR and ARs. We previously observed a positive relationship between root hair length and H2O2 levels in exogenous metabolite treatments (Zhang et al. 2023), consistent with the known role of apoplastic H2O2 in promoting root hair elongation (Dunand et al. 2007). CA treatment represses root hair elongation in the PR but has minimal impacts on the AR. Since this repression in PRs is associated with a decrease in H2O2, we investigated whether ARs also exhibit reduced H2O2 levels. Our findings reveal that while CA reduces H2O2 content in the PR, it does not have the same effect on ARs (Fig. S6a and c), suggesting a differential regulation of H2O2 that may underlie the variation in root hair development between these root types.

CA reduces the H2O2 levels in vitro and in vivo by upregulating Class III peroxidases

In order to understand how CA influences H2O2 levels in roots, we conducted a detailed analysis of H2O2 related genes in the RNA-seq data. We discovered that a large number of genes responsible for the controlled transformation of ROS into more stable compounds were upregulated in CA-treated PRs when compared with ARs in the same treatment. We observed that CA treatment increases the transcription of various superoxide dismutases (FeSOD1 and MnSOD2) (Fig. S6d), which are directly responsible for reducing O2•− into H2O2 (Wang et al. 2018). Furthermore, we found that CA had a notably strong effect on peroxidases, particularly on Class III peroxidases (Fig. 4a). Class III peroxidases are remarkable for their versatility, catalyzing the oxidation of a wide range of compounds by using H2O2 as the electron acceptor, including during the polymerization of cell wall components (Shigeto et al. 2016). Previous studies in Arabidopsis have shown that Class III peroxidases play an important a role in responding to various abiotic stresses (Eljebbawi et al. 2022). In our study, 34 of the 72 Class III peroxidases found in Arabidopsis were significantly upregulated in the CA-treated PR, compared with ContPRs, while 43 of them were downregulated in the CA-treated ARs compared with the CA-treated PRs (Fig. 4a). Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis confirmed a significant increase in the expression of a Class III peroxidase gene PRX52 in PRs following treatment with 5 mm CA (Fig. S5e).

To further explore CA's effect on peroxidase activity, we performed a series of broad range peroxidase activity assays in 7-d root tissue treated with 5 mm CA or a control. We discovered that CA treatment significantly increases total root peroxidase activity (Fig. 4b) independent of the oxidatively coupled dye used. These results are both consistent with previous findings of the H2O2 lowering effects of CA in other species (Hu et al. 2016; Tahjib-Ul-Arif et al. 2021; Ali et al. 2024) and corroborate the decreased H2O2 levels we observe upon CA treatment. In summary, our results suggest that exogenous CA treatment reduces H2O2 in PRs largely through the upregulation of Class III peroxidases, leading to an increase in peroxidase activity, particularly in the PRs.

To further investigate the relationship between peroxidases, H2O2 levels, and root growth, we examined known reducing agents responsible for scavenging H2O2. Potassium iodide (KI), glutathione (GSH), and ascorbic acid (AsA) can act as direct chemical scavengers of ROS, as well as by acting as substrates in peroxidase-catalyzed H2O2 reduction. We found that KI, GSH, and AsA displayed many phenotypic similarities compared with CA when treated constitutively. At the different concentrations of each scavenger tested, the amount of H2O2 reduction was comparable between the different known scavengers and CA (Fig. 4c). All the treatments produce a shorter PR (Fig. S6f) and significantly increase the incidence of elongated ARs (Fig. 4d). Given the overall phenotypic similarities between CA and the 3 H2O2 scavengers, these data provide further evidence that altering H2O2 levels is sufficient to reproduce many of the CA treatment effects on root architecture.

CA increases lignin levels in the roots by upregulating the expression of lignin synthesis genes

Previous research has shown that some Class III peroxidases use H2O2 as a substrate to oxidatively polymerize lignin (Herrero et al. 2013). In our RNA-seq data, we found that lignin synthesis genes and cell wall pathways are enriched in DEGs upregulated in PRs under CA treatment (Fig. 5a). In particular, CA induces the expression of key MYB transcription factors that enhance lignification (MYB15 and MYB63) as well as a number of genes in the phenylpropanoid (PP) pathway (most notably PAL3 and CAD8), which is critical for lignin biosynthesis (Kim et al. 2020). As expected, those same genes were relatively downregulated in the CA-treated ARs, compared with the CA-treated PRs (Fig. 5a). In total, these results suggest that lignin synthesis may be altered under CA treatment. Therefore, we measured the total lignin content of the roots in the presence and absence of CA treatment. Quantification of total lignin content and Wiesner staining for lignin (phloroglucinol-HCl) shows that CA treatment dramatically increases the degree of lignification (Figs. 5b and S7). CA induction of lignification is consistent with its upregulation of Class III peroxidases and depletion of H2O2 in roots.

To examine the role of lignin in the CA phenotype, we tested CA treatment in combination with trans-cinnamic acid (t-CNA), the ubiquitous precursor of the PP pathway. The combination of t-CNA and CA treatments results in significantly shorter PRs compared with t-CNA or CA treatment alone (Fig. 5c, ANOVA interaction P-value < 0.0001). Furthermore, we also measured total lignin content, finding that CA and the combination of CA and t-CNA treatment significantly increase lignin levels compared with control roots (Fig. 5b). Additionally, t-CNA alone also induces ARs, and the combination of t-CNA and CA treatments induces more ARs than either treatment alone (Fig. 5d). Interestingly, these results suggest that lignin biosynthesis alone is sufficient to increase AR growth, although it remains unclear how it promotes ARs while inhibiting PR growth.

To further test whether the effect of CA is dependent on the PP pathway, we tested mutants in the PP pathway involved in the committed steps of lignin biosynthesis. Specifically, we treated mutants of key genes in the lignin biosynthesis pathway, C4H (Schilmiller et al. 2009), 4CL1 and 4CL3 (Vanholme et al. 2012), with CA and t-CNA and measured the effects on PR growth. We found that these mutants were overall more resistant to the root growth inhibition induced by CA, t-CNA, and the combination of both (Fig. 5e). These genetic data indicate that lignin biosynthesis plays a major role in the phenotypes generated by CA treatment.

CA induces ETHYLENE RESPONSE FACTOR 115 expression through a ROS-independent pathway

Previously, Zluhan-Martínez et al. (2025) showed that H2O2 treatment promotes the differentiation of the root columella, decreasing columella stem cell number cell layers and increasing the number of differentiated columella cell layers at the root tip. To decipher the relationship between CA, redox, and columella development, we examined the effect of CA treatment on the PR columella cell differentiation using starch accumulation as a marker. We found that CA treatment affected the number of both columella stem cells (CSCs) and differentiated columella cells (DCCs) in the columella region (Fig. 6a to c), which is consistent with its effects on H2O2 levels. In addition, we observed CA treatment caused abnormal quiescent center (QC) cell divisions (Fig. S9). The QC is essential for maintenance and organization of the stem cell niche. Our CA-induced QC phenotype is reminiscent of the effects of the ETHYLENE RESPONSE FACTOR 115 (ERF115) overexpression line, which enhances the proliferation and disorganization of the QC (Heyman et al. 2013). Furthermore, our RNA-seq data reveal a significant increase in transcript levels of ERF114 and ERF115, particularly in the PR. We further confirmed the ERF115 transcripts level increased by RT-qPCR (Fig. S8a). We also saw a similar increase in PHYTOSULFOKINE PRECURSOR genes PSK2 and PSK5, downstream targets of the ERF115 (Fig. 6d). In contrast, we observed opposite effects on PLTs, PHB3, and RITF (Fig. 6d) which also are involved in root stem cell niche development within the ROS signaling pathway (Zhou et al. 2020). These results suggest that CA treatment may disrupt the QC through induced ERF115 and ERF114 expression.

Previous work has reported that ERF115 is induced by H2O2 (Kong et al. 2018), whereas CA promotes ERF115 expression while reducing H2O2. Therefore, to explore the relationship between H2O2, CA treatment, and ERF115 expression, we conducted experiments on the pERF115-GUS line using CA and the ROS scavengers KI and GSH (Fig. S8b). CA, KI, GSH, and AsA treatments all decrease H2O2 levels significantly in the PR (Fig. 4c) while modulating ERF115 expression in different ways. CA treatment significantly increases ERF115 expression in a concentration-dependent manner. At a concentration of 5 mm, CA induces ERF115 expression throughout the entire seedling, including the leaves and root system (Fig. 6e). Meanwhile, KI and GSH do not have a notable impact on ERF115 expression. However, AsA is a strong inducer of ERF115 expression (Fig. S8c). This indicates that the expression of ERF115 specifically responds to CA and AsA and not to other ROS scavengers that decrease H2O2. In addition, we found that H2O2 treatment induced the expression of ERF115 (Fig. S8b), especially in the root tip, which is consistent with previous observations by (Kong et al. 2018). To test whether the effect of CA treatment on ERF115 is a general feature of organic acids, we tested the effect of malic acid (MA) on ERF115 expression. We found that MA, another organic acid in the TCA cycle, does not alter the staining intensity of the pERF115-GUS lines (Fig. S8d). Overall, our results suggest that strong ERF115 induction is a relatively specific response to CA and AsA treatments.

To further understand the function of ERF115 under CA treatment, we performed exogenous CA treatments on 35S-ERF115 and erf114C erf115 lines (Lakehal et al. 2020). We found that PR growth in the ERF115 overexpression line was sensitive to CA treatment, whereas the erf114C erf115 mutant lines were not (ANOVA interaction, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 6f and g). In control treatments, erf114C erf115 has significantly shorter roots than wild type (WT), while 35S-ERF115 roots are not significantly different from WT. The lines behave similarly in plants treated for 6 d with 2 or 5 mm CA. However, after 14 d of high CA treatment (5 mm), erf114C erf115 lines are significantly longer than WT. In contrast, 35S-ERF115 roots are significantly shorter than WT roots. This indicates that the erf114C erf115 double mutant line is more resistant to high CA concentrations and that overexpressing ERF115 makes roots less resistant to CA (Fig. 6e and f).

Under control conditions, we also found that the erf114C erf115 lines exhibited slightly more abnormal QC divisions, while the 35S-ERF115 lines showed severe QC abnormalities compared with the wild type (Fig. S9). The QC area in both the 35S-ERF115 and in the erf114C erf115 lines were severely distorted under the 5 mm CA treatment compared with wild type. Specifically, 90% of Col-0 seedlings under 5 mm CA treatment exhibited an abnormal QC area, compared with only 15% in the control Col-0 group. Under 5 mm CA treatment, 100% of seedlings in both 35S-ERF115 lines and erf114C erf115 lines showed an abnormal QC area. However, under control conditions, 80% of 35S-ERF115 seedlings and 55% of erf114C erf115 seedlings exhibited an abnormal QC area (Fig. S9). Therefore, both the 35S-ERF115 and the erf114C erf115 lines have distorted QCs in control conditions and under 5 mm CA treatment, suggesting that the proper balance of ERF115 levels is important for QC development and that additional pathways play a role in CA modulation of QC divisions (Fig. S9). In summary, CA affects PR growth and QC divisions, partially through regulating the ERF115 expression.

Discussion

CA treatment affects root development and growth in a dose-dependent and tissue-specific manner. Here, we investigated the differential responses in primary and AR growth and morphology to better understand CA activity in plants, as well as the different genetic programs that regulate these roots. This approach revealed both general processes and root-type-specific pathways affected by this key metabolite. Because CA is a known chelator, we started by investigating the effects of CA on nutrient localization. We found that PR meristems, AR meristems, and the root–hypocotyl junction are all hotspots of nutrient enrichment in the root system, which may suggest that one role of ARs in normal conditions could be to improve nutrient uptake in plants. However, we did not find that CA treatment has a major effect on nutrient concentrations in root meristems.

Our HPLC-MS/MS results show that citrate does not accumulate under exogenous CA treatment, which raises a number of questions. For example, how is exogenous CA absorbed through the plant root? How does the plant maintain CA homeostasis under exogenous treatment? Does the root sense external CA? We found that CA treatment lowers the pH of the media directly surrounding the root, suggesting that CA is being exudated out at a rapid rate. Previous work has shown that CA can act as a ligand in various pathways, such as the strigolactone (SL) signaling pathway (Tal et al. 2022), highlighting its potential to act as a signaling ligand. An important topic for future research is to delineate CA–protein interactions to better understand its roles in root development and signaling regulation.

Our transcriptomic analysis revealed that CA influences multiple biological pathways, including ROS pathways, lignin biosynthesis, and stress responses. CA reduces H2O2 levels in PRs by upregulating Class III peroxidase transcript levels and activity. Many class III peroxidases use H2O2 as a substrate for lignin polymerization, and we found that CA treatment induces the expression of lignin synthesis genes that promotes lignin production in the PR as well as the peroxidases responsible for cross-linking it in the cell wall (Fig. 7). Furthermore, we found that altering the ratio of H2O2 levels at the hypocotyl–root junction is sufficient to induce AR and adventitious root formation. Similarly, we found that treatment with a lignin precursor is sufficient to increase lignin levels and increase AR formation. This suggests that the strong CA-driven induction of AR growth may result from its effects on both H2O2 levels and lignin.

Model of the proposed pathways by which CA treatment differentially affects primary and AR development. When PRs are treated with CA, there is a strong induction of ERF115, lignin biosynthesis genes, and PRXs (peroxidases), which leads to a reduction in H2O2 and root growth. However, ARs have a distinct response to CA, compared with PRs. In CA-treated ARs, the ERF115, lignin biosynthesis, and PRX response are suppressed compared with CA-treated PRs. As a result, H2O2 levels are higher. We found that increasing H2O2 in the hypocotyl is sufficient to increase AR formation. In addition, increased lignin synthesis also increases anchor growth while inhibiting primary growth.

A striking finding we present in this work is how exogenous CA treatment induces substantially different phenotypical and transcriptional responses in the PR compared with the AR, highlighting how a single signal can be interpreted in root-type-specific ways. While the PR has inhibited root hair elongation when exposed to CA, ARs remain largely unaffected in this regard. Similarly, while the PR has reduced levels of H2O2 levels under CA treatment, the AR conserves a persistent level of H2O2. While the biological context of why CA can function as a cue that biases development toward ARs, these root-type-specific differences raise the possibility that ARs are regulated by distinct physiological constraints and possess functions other root types do not.

We also found that CA induces ERF115 expression and controls PR stem cell and QC divisions through an H2O2 independent pathway. A summary of our results showing how CA differentially affects peroxidases, lignin synthesis, ERF115 expression, and primary and AR growth is shown in Fig. 7. Since other ERF family genes regulate CA levels in various plant species (Li et al. 2016; Li et al. 2020), it would be interesting to explore whether ERF115 can similarly regulate CA in Arabidopsis. Moreover, ERF115 acts as a repressor of adventitious root formation in the hypocotyl of Arabidopsis, mediating the crosstalk between jasmonate and cytokinin signaling pathways (Lakehal et al. 2020). The relationship between ARs and adventitious roots remains unclear. CA could serve as a valuable tool to explore this interaction.

In the future, it will be interesting to understand how communication between the hypocotyl and root affects AR development. These results also highlight an exciting open question in plant biology—how are different tissue-specific environmental responses conferred in genetically identical root types? Further investigations of the mechanisms that confer stress resilience in ARs have exciting potential to inform our understanding of tissue-specific responses, with prospective applications in biotechnology. Overall, this work also expands on our understanding of the diverse functions of CA in plants and reveals specific functions of primary metabolites in plants.

Materials and methods

Plant growth conditions

For CA treatments in Arabidopsis, compounds were added to sterile 1/2× MS plant growth media, and the pH of CA was adjusted to 5.7 using sodium citrate. Seeds were sterilized using 20% bleach and sowed on the treated plant growth media. Plants were incubated in a 16 h/8 h day/night cycle growth chamber at 22 ℃ for 5 to 14 d prior to phenotyping. For H2O2 treatment, 1 μL of 30% hydrogen peroxide solution was applied to different regions as shown in Fig. 3c. The seedlings were treated at 4 d old, and phenotypic observations were conducted 2 wk posttreatment.

Confocal microscopy

Fluorescence intensity of 35S-HyPer transgenic plants (Shi et al. 2022) (Gift from Prof. Mingyi Bai) was determined over regions of interest, at 530 nm after excitation at 488 nm or 405 nm. Emission was collected at 530 nm with a bandpass of 30 nm. Image analysis was performed with an ImageJ software. A fluorescence ratio was calculated as 488/405. For Arabidopsis, most of the roots were stained by propidium iodide.

ROS staining

H2O2 levels in the roots were assessed by using the 2',7'-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA) staining method. The roots from 7-d-old seedlings were incubated in Milli-Q water containing 50 µm H2DCFDA for 30 min under dark conditions. Subsequently, the roots were rinsed with sterilized water 3 times. Fluorescent signals were visualized under an Echo Revolution microscope (Echo). The excitation and emission wavelengths used for detection of the signals were set at 488 and 520 nm, respectively.

Synchrotron X-ray fluorescence imaging and X-ray absorption spectroscopy

The synchrotron X-ray fluorescence (XRF) imaging experiment was conducted at the Stanford Synchrotron Radiation Lightsource (SSRL), SLAC National Laboratory. Seven-day-old plants grown under control (no CA) and 5 mm CA treatment conditions were prepared. The whole seedlings were placed on weighing paper and left at room temperature to dry. Once dried, the plants were carefully affixed to tape or quartz slide to maintain their integrity. Samples were shipped to SSRL for analysis. At SSRL, samples on quartz slides were mounted onto sample cassettes compatible with the multisample holders available at each beamline end-station. The sample cassettes maintain sample positioning and can be transferred between the different multisample holders between the beamlines used in this experiment. Entire root sections were measured at the large format beamline 7-2, while smaller, high-resolution regions of interest were measured at the micro-imaging beamline 23. Beamline 7-2 is a wiggler side-station beamline, equipped with a water-cooled double crystal Si(111) monochromator for X-ray energy selection. The experimental hutch has a large sample mounting stage capable of accommodating up to 40 microscope slides for rapid imaging. Newport stages control sample positioning and movement during data collection. A 7-element Hitachi Vortex detector with Quantum Xspress3 software provides the detection system at this beamline. Data were collected at 9,700 eV, 35-micron beam spot size, and 25-micron step size (ie oversampled). The 35-micron beam spot size was focused using a capillary focusing optic. Beamline 2-3 is a bending magnet beamline, also equipped with a water-cooled double crystal Si(111) monochromator. The energy ranges of the monochromators at beamlines 7-2 and 2-3 are comparable. In contrast to beamline 7-2, the primary mode of sample mounting at beamline 2-3 is a multisample wheel, which is mounted onto a fully 360° rotational stage for sample changing. The in-hutch experimental station has a single element Hitachi Vortex detector, also with Quantum Detectors Xspress3 for the detection system. Data were collected at 9,700 eV, 5-micron beam spot size, and 5-micron step size. The 5-micron beam spot size was focused using Sigray axially symmetric lenses. All measurements at both beamlines were performed at room temperature in air under standard SPEAR3 ring condition (500 mE, 3 GeV).

Maps were processed using the Microanalysis Toolkit (Webb 2011). Briefly, maps were loaded into the software, and a row of 1 pixel was removed from all edges of the map to remove arbitrarily high counts in the first column of each map. A smoothing (blur) was applied to improve signal to noise ratio—this was done by applying a Gaussian distribution to an area of 5 × 5 pixels with a standard deviation of 0.75. Elemental channels can be cross-plotted using the correlation plot function. In the correlation plot, we examined the relationships between P, Mn, Fe, Cu, and Zn, assessing treatment-induced changes in their elemental distribution. Masks can be created around data within the correlation plot, and these data points can subsequently be masked on the XRF image. In this case, a mask was created around the higher concentration data points to separate the plant root data from the background. These data were subsequently exported from the correlation plot to excel to compare elemental abundances between the control and CA-treated samples. For comparison purposes, maps of the same element were scaled to the same total counts between the samples.

ICP-MS analysis

For elemental analysis, 7-d-old wild-type A. thaliana (Col-0) seedlings were harvested from both control and 5 mm CA-treated groups. Plant tissues were dried at 92 °C for 20 h until a constant dry weight was achieved. For each sample, ∼15 mg of dried tissue was used for subsequent analysis. The dried samples were digested in a mixture of 1 mL concentrated nitric acid (HNO3) and 0.5 mL hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and incubated overnight at 135 °C. After complete digestion, the samples were diluted to a final volume of 10 mL. Prior to ICP-MS measurement, the digestates were further diluted 5-fold or 100-fold, depending on elemental abundance in the samples. Elemental concentrations were determined by ICP-MS with internal standards (eg, Sc and Y) used for signal normalization

GUS staining

GUS activity assays were performed in the transgenic plants of pERF115-GUS using a previously described histochemical staining method (Wu et al. 2003). Briefly, the 7-d-old seedlings were incubated in a staining buffer (50 mm sodium phosphate, pH 7.0, 10 mm EDTA, 0.1% Triton X-100, 1 mg mL-1 of X-Gluc, 100 µg mL-1 of chloramphenicol, 1 mm potassium ferricyanide, 1 mm potassium ferrocyanide, and 20% methanol), vacuumed for 5 min, and then transferred to 37 ℃ overnight. The stained tissues were observed and photographed with a stereomicroscope (Nikon SMZ1270).

Peroxidase activity assay

Whole protein extract was prepared by first flash freezing and grinding 7-d-old seedlings and then extracting with 20 mm Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.5). After mixing and centrifuging, the pellet was discarded and the supernatant was immediately flash frozen and kept in −80 °C. In 96-well plates, 3 separate reaction mixtures were prepared based on their respective peroxidase substrates: 25 µm Amplex Red, 5 mm guaiacol, and 1 mm DAB (3,3′-Diaminobenzidine). All reaction wells are buffered with 20 mm sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) and contain 50 µm H2O2 and 10 µL protein extract. Immediately after adding the protein extract, the plate was mechanically agitated for 1 min and absorbance was monitored for 45 to 60 min. The measured absorbance of Amplex Red, DAB, and guaiacol peroxidase assays were 570, 452 and 470 nm, respectively.

Wiesner (phloroglucinol-HCl) staining

The Weisner staining protocol for lignin was adapted from the method described by (Liljegren 2010). For each sample, a fresh volume of 3% phloroglucinol in ethanol was mixed with 37 N HCl in a 2 to 1 ratio (phloroglucinol to HCl). Samples were stained for 5 min with gentle agitation before washing twice with absolute ethanol and imaged using a stereomicroscope (Nikon SMZ1270).

Total lignin quantification

Total lignin content was determined using acetyl bromide soluble lignin assay, adapted from Barnes and Anderson (2017). Fresh plant tissue was ground into a fine powder and dried. To isolate lignin and other alcohol-insoluble residues, 70 mg of dried ground tissue was rinsed vigorously in 70% ethanol for 10 min, washed with equal parts chloroform to methanol solution, and rinsed with acetone. After drying, samples were destarched in 90% DMSO with shaking overnight and washed once in 90% DMSO, 6 times in 70% ethanol, and once in acetone. Acetyl bromide soluble lignin was obtained by dissolving 5 mg of prepared sample in 25% acetyl bromide in glacial acetic acid at 70 °C for 1 h with gentle swirling every 10 min. After cooling on ice, an aliquot of the solution was diluted with equal parts 2 m NaOH and at a 1 to 39 parts in acetic acid. The absorbance at 280 nm is then measured in a spectrophotometer. To calculate lignin content, extinction coefficient of sample was considered to be 15.69 L/g.cm (Foster et al. 2010).

RNA-seq analysis

Root samples including PRs and ARs for RNA-seq were collected following a 5 mm CA treatment after 7 d, respectively. RNA was extracted by using QIAGEN RNeasy UCP Micro Kit. Five hundred nanograms of of RNA was used to generate RNA sequencing libraries using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA with Ribo-Zero Plant kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Samples were processed following manufacturer's instructions, modifying RNA shear time to 5 min. Resulting libraries were multiplexed and sequenced with 100 base pair (bp) paired-end reads (PE100) to a depth of approximately 50 million reads per sample on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Samples were demultiplexed using bcl2fastq Conversion Software (Illumina). RNA-seq data were analyzed by using GALAXY platform (https://usegalaxy.org/) (The Galaxy Community 2022). The brief workflow is TRIMMOMATIC—FASTQC—HISAT2— FEATURECOUNTS—LIMMA. We considered genes with a ∣logFC∣ >1.5 and a P-value <0.05 as differentially expressed. The reference genome was based on TAIR10 assembly. The analyzed RNA-seq data can be found in Table S1. GO enrichment and Venn diagram analysis were performed using TBtools (Chen et al. 2023).

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNAs were isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Complementary DNAs were made by reverse transcription according to the manufacturer's instructions (Bio-Rad, iScript gDNA Clear cDNA Synthesis Kit). RT-qPCRs were performed using gene-specific primers and SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) on a real-time PCR CFX96 (Bio-Rad). Data were collected using the CFX96 (Bio-Rad) sequence detection system following the manufacturer's instruction. The Arabidopsis AtEf1α gene was used as the internal control. At least 3 biological replicates and 3 technical repeats were conducted for each experiment. A list of these primers can be found in Table S2.

HPLC-MS/MS analysis of TCA metabolites

Each Arabidopsis sample analyzed using HPLC-MS/MS contained 0.1 to 0.15 g of whole roots and leaves. Tissue was collected 14 d after germination for Arabidopsis. Sterilized deionized water was used as an extraction solvent. Root samples were flash frozen using liquid nitrogen and ground into a powder, which was then dissolved in sterile deionized water. The aqueous solution was placed in a 4 ℃ refrigerator and shaken gently overnight, after which it was centrifuged at 12,000rpm for 1 min. The supernatant was filtered using a syringe with a 0.22-μm membrane filter into an Agilent HPLC vial. The filtered solution was used for final analysis. The concentration of TCA cycle intermediates in all the samples were determined by creating a standard curve using a set of known concentrations.

The parameters of the HPLC-MS/MS method are as follows: Analysis was performed on a Thermo Scientific Vanquish UHPLC system coupled with an Orbitrap-MS (Orbitrap Elite, Thermo Scientific) with a heated electrospray ionization source. Chromatographic separation was carried out on a Kinetex C18 column (150 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 mm) with a Kinetex C18 guard column maintained at 35 °C. The mobile phases were A (H2O/formic acid [FA], 1,000/1, v/v) and B (acetonitrile/FA, 1,000/1, v/v) with the gradient program: 0 to 6 min, 1% B; 6 to 7 min, 1% B to 90% B; 7 to 9 min, 90% B; 9 to 9.01 min, 90% B to 1% B; 9.01 to 12 min, 1% B. MS parameters included sheath gas flow rate of 50 arbitrary units, auxiliary gas flow rate of 20 arbitrary units, spray voltage of −3.5 kV, capillary temperature of 350 °C, S-lens RF (radio frequency) level of 30, and resolution of 30,000. We measured SRM transitions at m/z 133 → 115, 173 → 85, 145 → 101, 191 → 111, 117 → 73, 131 → 85, and 115 → 71.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analysis methods for individual experiments are described in the text and figures. For all experiments described, no data were excluded from the analyses. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Randomization and blinding were not used during experiments and outcome assessment. All statistics were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 10).
