Paul glaucoma implant extrusion following post-surgical scleritis in patients with uveitic glaucoma: A case series
Authors: Ibrahim AlObaida, Anas Alqurashi, Moustafa Magliyah, Mohammed Halawani, Khalid AlDihan, Konrad Schargel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis report describes two cases of plate exposure and severe extrusion of the Paul Glaucoma Implant (PGI) following post-surgical exacerbation of scleritis. Additionally, it outlines the management approach used to control ocular inflammation and perform explantation, including exchange to a Preserflo MicroShunt in one of the cases.ObservationsTwo females receiving systemic immunosuppression developed exacerbation of scleritis 7 and 5 months post PGI implantation. One patient demonstrated scleral melt and conjunctival thinning beneath the large PGI plate. Both patients received maximum doses of systemic corticosteroids and mycophenolate mofetil to control ocular inflammation. Owing to the high risk of infection and re-exposure, each patient underwent PGI explantation. In one case, a Preserflo MicroShunt was implanted during the same surgical session to minimize conjunctival manipulation and maintain long-term intraocular pressure control, which remained stable for more than a year.ConclusionsSurgically induced scleral necrosis occurs in patients following glaucoma drainage device surgery, even in patients already receiving systemic corticosteroids and steroid-sparing immunomodulators. In these cases, inflammation may progress silently beneath the glaucoma drainage device plate until scleral melt and conjunctiva damage become evident intraoperatively. Early recognition of scleritis exacerbation and prompt escalation of systemic immunosuppression are essential, and explantation may be required when structural integrity is compromised.Keywords: Paul glaucoma implant, Extrusion, Postoperative scleritis, Scleral necrosis, Glaucoma drainage implants, microshunt management strategies

Introduction

Surgically induced scleral necrosis (SISN), a rare and severe postoperative complication, is characterized by collagen degradation, local ischemia, and progression to scleral melt.1 It occurs most frequently following pterygium or cataract surgery and rarely occurs following glaucoma surgery. Reported cases include isolated events following trabeculectomy and glaucoma drainage device (GDD) implantation.1,2

The Paul Glaucoma Implant (PGI; Advanced Ophthalmic Innovations) is a modern non-valved GDD with a large endplate.3,4 Similar to other plate-based GDDs, conjunctival erosion can drive plate exposure, which requires urgent repair to prevent endophthalmitis.3,5,6 When SISN develops following GDD implantation and triggers device extrusion, management becomes challenging because ocular inflammation must be controlled before surgical intervention, typically with systemic corticosteroids and immunomodulators.1,7 Conversely, the Preserflo MicroShunt (Santen, Osaka, Japan) is a plate-free, minimally invasive bleb-forming surgery (MIBS) that requires less conjunctival dissection and has demonstrated long-term efficacy and safety in uveitic glaucoma.8, 9, 10

This report describes two cases of PGI extrusion associated with postoperative scleral inflammation, including one scleral melt beneath the PGI plate (Table 1). Both patients were receiving systemic corticosteroids and steroid-sparing immunomodulators for underlying systemic disease, and both ultimately required PGI explantation; one case subsequently received a Preserflo MicroShunt.

Case One

A 27-year-old female with refractory uveitic glaucoma and Vogt–Koyanagi–Harada syndrome (VKH) in both eyes presented for routine follow-up. The patient reported no pain, irritation, or trauma. The patient was functionally monocular, with best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of 20/25 in the left eye and hand motion in the right eye. Previous ocular surgeries included bilateral deep sclerectomy 5 years earlier and left trabeculectomy 3 years earlier. Seven months prior, the patient underwent right-eye PGI placement with an intraluminal 6-0 Prolene ripcord.

At 3 months postoperatively, high intraocular pressure (IOP) required ripcord removal, after which the patient developed hypotony and a shallow anterior chamber (AC). Surgical revision was performed with 7-0 Prolene suture ligation and AC reformation.

At presentation, the patient was using steroid eye-drops twice daily, brimonidine and brinzolamide thrice daily, timolol once daily, Bimatoprost/Timolol once daily, and 250 mg/day of acetazolamide in the right eye. Topical loteprednol was administered every other day in the left eye, Additionally, oral prednisolone (5 mg) and mycophenolate mofetil 1.5 g were taken twice daily, along with vitamin D and calcium supplements.

IOP was 21 and 30 mmHg in the right and left eyes, respectively (Goldmann). Slit-lamp examination of the right eye revealed a red conjunctival congestion and superotemporal PGI extrusion with visible choroid at the plate margins and overlying scleral thinning (Fig. 1A and B). No leak was detected. The cornea was edematous with inferior Descemet membrane detachment, and the AC was deep with a ligated tube positioned away from the cornea. Peripheral anterior synechiae partially obscured the iris. Left eye examination was unremarkable.

Slit-lamp findings of the right eye

(A) Corneal opacity with conjunctival congestion

(B) Localized area of conjunctival thinning with extrusion of the migrated Paul glaucoma implant plate, associated scleral inflammation, and engorged conjunctival vessels.

Owing to PGI extrusion, the exposed uveal tissue, and negative culture, PGI explantation with a corneal patch graft was planned. To optimize surgical conditions, acetazolamide was discontinued, and a 1-week preoperative regimen of oral 60 mg (weekly taper) and hourly topical steroids was initiated, consistent with institutional practice.11 A Preserflo MicroShunt was selected to provide IOP control with minimal conjunctival manipulation.

Surgical intervention

Under general anesthesia, examination revealed heavy conjunctival secretion and severe conjunctival damage. Scleral melt with uveal prolapse was observed beneath the PGI plate, with an additional smaller defect anteriorly at the tube entry site (Fig. 2A–C). The tube was ligated near the plate and transected distal to the ligature (Fig. 2B). Fixation sutures were cut, and the PGI plate was explanted. The area was irrigated with balanced salt solution and cefuroxime.

(B) Ligation of the PGI tube near the plate to separate the tube from the PGI plate (arrow). (C) Necrotic sclera with hidden uveal prolapse following plate suture release (arrow)

Two corneal patch grafts were fashioned into thick and thin grafts. A thicker graft was positioned posteriorly to cover the large uveal prolapse (Fig. 2D and E). The tube was extracted, and the conjunctiva was sutured except for a small opening to insert a thinner graft over the anterior defect (Fig. 2F). The conjunctiva was then fully closed with 9-0 Vicryl.

Attention was turned nasally for standard Preserflo MicroShunt implantation. Mitomycin-C 0.2 mg/mL was applied to the long sponge for 2 min and irrigated thoroughly. Intracameral cefuroxime (0.1 mL) and dexamethasone (0.3 mL) were administered.

Outcome

At 1 year postoperatively, there were no complications, and VKH remained controlled on oral prednisone (7.5 mg/day), mycophenolate mofetil (3 g/day), and vitamin D and calcium supplementation. The surgical site remained well covered, although a small stable 3 × 4 mm gap persisted anterior to the corneal patch without leakage. The Preserflo MicroShunt was stable nasally, with IOP of 21 and 12 mmHg in the right and left eyes, respectively, on anti-glaucoma medication at maximum dosage.

Case two

A 39-year-old female with uveitic glaucoma, high myopia, and horizontal nystagmus presented with a 2-day history of worsening foreign-body sensation in the right eye. The patient was functionally monocular, relying on the right eye with a BCVA of 20/250; the left eye had only light perception. Patient's ophthalmic history included bilateral lens aspiration for congenital cataracts (aphakia), cyclophotocoagulation, and pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) for retinal detachment repair in the left eye, complicated by SISN requiring scleral and dural patch grafts 2 years earlier.

Five months before presentation, the patient underwent PGI implantation with a 6-0 Prolene intraluminal ripcord in the right eye for uncontrolled IOP. The patient also reported minor blunt trauma to the right eye approximately 1 month prior. Other medical history included bilateral breast cancer treated with mastectomies.

At presentation, the patient was taking oral prednisolone (5 mg/day) and azathioprine (50 mg twice daily) for the left eye SISN. Treatment for the right eye included brimonidine once daily and intensive lubrication. The left eye was on Bimatoprost/Timolol at bedtime and brinzolamide/brimonidine thrice daily.

IOP was 10 and 31 mmHg in the right and left eyes, respectively. Slit-lamp examination of the right eye showed conjunctival congestion, swelling, tenderness, engorged scleral vessels, and a bleb with a visible ripcord. No leakage was detected (Fig. 3A and B). The tube was well positioned, and no signs of endophthalmitis were observed. The left eye was quiet.

The impression was postoperative exacerbation of scleritis in the right eye, with a stable bleb and negative culture. Systemic corticosteroid dosage was increased to 40 mg daily with a 5 mg weekly taper, with close monitoring and deferral of shunt revision until inflammation improved.

Over the next month, scleritis activity persisted, prompting a switch from azathioprine to mycophenolate mofetil (1 g twice daily). Preoperative management included 60 mg systemic prednisolone, tapered weekly and hourly topical steroids for 1 week pre-operatively.11

After 1 month, the PGI had extruded through the conjunctiva and previous Tutoplast graft, migrating anteriorly, exposing the temporal plate wing and ripcord. Without evidence of infection, and given the increased endophthalmitis risk in the only functional eye, the patient was admitted for urgent PGI explantation (Fig. 3. C–D).

Surgical intervention

Under local anesthesia, the PGI was identified with anterior migration, temporal wing exposure, and protrusion through the conjunctiva. Dissection was performed until the plate edges were completely freed and the implant was removed without intraoperative evidence of infection. Conjunctivalization of the underlying scleral surface was noted (Fig. 4).

Intraoperative findings in Case Two

Conjunctival dissection shows exposed conjunctivalization over the scleral bed and vascular congestion during Paul glaucoma implant explantation in the right eye.

Planned Preserflo MicroShunt implantation was deferred due to these findings. Conjunctival edges were trimmed and closed with continuous 9-0 Vicryl sutures. Subconjunctival dexamethasone and cefazolin were administered, followed by application of topical Maxitrol ointment.

Outcome

Two months after PGI explantation, the patient was asymptomatic and remained on full anti-glaucoma therapy, ofloxacin four times daily, prednisolone, and mycophenolate mofetil (2 g/day). IOP measured 15 mmHg in the right eye by Goldmann applanation. BCVA was 20/300. Examination showed conjunctival congestion; however, the surgical site was stable without leakage, infection, or AC inflammation (Fig. 3E and F).

Discussion

Plate exposure following GDD implantation is a rare but severe late complication and a major risk factor for endophthalmitis and vision loss.5,6 Proposed risk factors include ocular inflammation, multiple ocular procedures, and higher pre-operative medications, several of which were present in our cases.12,13 In addition, both patients had features associated with SISN, including female sex, which is associated with higher autoimmune disease prevalence; corticosteroid eye-drops, which impair wound healing; and multiple ocular surgeries, which compromise scleral integrity.1

The pathophysiology of SISN remains unclear and difficult to recognize because patients are typically asymptomatic or describe non-specific irritation resembling dry eye disease.1 In Case 1, SISN was detected early, and intraoperative examination revealed a larger-than-expected scleral defect with a secondary, smaller defect.

Previous reports have described SISN with scleral melt after vitreoretinal surgery, such as scleral buckle and PPV.14,15 Although rare, a recent study reported that SISN was less common in PPV compared to scleral buckle and that it was less likely to require surgical intervention.14 This is relevant to Case 2, whose left eye had previously undergone PPV for retinal detachment repair and subsequently developed SISN within weeks, requiring scleral and dural patch grafting.14,15

In contrast, SISN is extremely rare following glaucoma surgery, with one report following Ahmed glaucoma valve (AGV) implantation in a patient with rheumatoid arthritis.2 In the cases presented here, initial management focused on controlling inflammation. Case 2 had been receiving azathioprine for prior SISN in the fellow eye after PPV. Despite this, the patient developed SISN in the other eye and the inflammation remained uncontrolled, prompting transition to mycophenolate mofetil. Inflammation improved more effectively with mycophenolate mofetil than azathioprine over 2 months. However, determining whether improvement was due to mycophenolate mofetil or the removal of PGI was impossible.

Before the initial PGI implantation, both patients received systemic and topical corticosteroids according to institution guidelines described earlier and maintained the dosage of immunomodulator agents being administered; Case 1 received mycophenolate mofetil (2 g/day), and Case 2 received azathioprine (50 mg twice daily). Whether increasing mycophenolate mofetil to 3 g/day or transitioning earlier from azathioprine to mycophenolate mofetil could have prevented SISN is difficult to determine, given the multifactorial pathogenesis and rarity of the condition.

With advances in MIBS, traditional GDDs may not be feasible in eyes with marked conjunctival or scleral compromise, as it increases the risk of further exposure and infection. In such situations, the Preserflo MicroShunt may help maintain target IOP following explantation. Ranjan & Rao16 reported AGV implantation to control secondary refractory glaucoma in a 35-year-old female with necrotizing scleritis, followed by marked valve extrusion after 3.5 months.16

Although MMC has been implicated as a potential risk factor for scleral thinning and SISN, it may reduce fibrosis and improve outcomes in uveitic glaucoma and Preserflo MicroShunt surgery when inflammation is adequately controlled.1,10,11,17 In both cases, MMC was not used during the initial PGI implantation, yet both patients developed severe postoperative inflammatory complications requiring PGI explantation. In Case 1, Preserflo implantation with MMC was chosen because of rapidly progressive refractory glaucoma, marked IOP fluctuation, and a strong fibrotic response. To reduce risk, the device was implanted nasally away from the compromised superotemporal sclera, after escalation of systemic and topical corticosteroids, using low-dose MMC for a short exposure with extensive irrigation. The patient in Case 2 had severe scleral conjunctivalization and the risks outweighed the potential benefits. Therefore, we proposed that using MMC on high-risk inflammatory eyes should be determined on a case-by-case basis.

The present cases highlight two points: (1) Systemic immunosuppression does not eliminate the risk of SISN or GDD extrusion post-surgical scleritis, and (2) MIBS procedures may be viable for eyes at high risk of inflammation or conjunctival defects. However, this study is limited by the small case number, short-term follow-up, and multiple concurrent interventions that made determining the primary cause of post-surgical scleritis and GDD extrusion difficult.

Conclusion

In GDDs, SISN may progress silently beneath the plate, leading to scleral melt, conjunctival breakdown, and eventual plate or device extrusion. Initial management in both cases focused on controlling ocular inflammation with mycophenolate mofetil, which enabled safe explantation. One patient subsequently underwent Preserflo MicroShunt conversion, achieving stable IOP at 1-year. The other case had extensive conjunctival compromise that prevented further intervention.
