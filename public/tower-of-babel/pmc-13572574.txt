Cerebral oxygen extraction fraction as a physiological biomarker in vascular cognitive impairment: A study in older adults enriched for small vessel diseases
Authors: Jie Song, Wen Shi, Julia Suconic, Kaisha Hazel, George Pottanat, Ebony Jones, Tyler Deane, Archana Sharma, Cuimei Xu, Kumiko Oishi, Yifan Gou, Zhiyi Hu, Xin Li, Paul Rosenberg, Rita Kalyani, Abhay Moghekar, Sevil Yasar, Doris Lin, Marilyn Albert, Hanzhang Lu, Dengrong Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONVascular cognitive impairment and dementia (VCID) is a common cause of cognitive impairment, yet validated biomarkers, particularly those reflecting vascular function, remain lacking. Oxygen extraction fraction (OEF), which reflects the functional status of cerebral small vessels, is a potential physiological biomarker for VCID. We tested this hypothesis in older adults enriched for small vessel diseases, controlling for Alzheimer's disease (AD) co‐pathology.METHODSComprehensive cognitive, plasma, and imaging data were collected from 81 older adults with at least one vascular risk factor. OEF in major cerebral veins was quantified using magnetic resonance imaging. Associations between OEF and cognition, vascular, and plasma AD biomarkers were evaluated.RESULTSIn this VCID‐enriched cohort, poorer global cognition and executive function were associated with elevated OEF, but not with plasma phosphorylated tau 217. Increased OEF correlated with more vascular risk factors and greater white matter hyperintensity volume.DISCUSSIONOur findings suggest that elevated OEF in individuals with vascular risks is a physiological biomarker for VCID.

BACKGROUND

Vascular cognitive impairment and dementia (VCID) is a common cause of cognitive decline and frequently co‐occurs with Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathology. 1 Despite its high prevalence, VCID currently lacks validated biomarkers for diagnosis or prognosis, limiting opportunities for early intervention. 2 Current assessments of cerebrovascular pathology primarily rely on structural markers such as white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), cerebral microbleeds, or lacunar infarcts. However, these structural changes typically reflect late‐stage brain damage, which is often irreversible. Therefore, it is desirable to establish biomarkers based on microvascular function before overt anatomic abnormalities become visible.

Cerebral oxygen extraction fraction (OEF) represents the percentage of oxygen extracted by brain tissues from arterial blood. OEF provides insight into the balance between tissue metabolic demand and blood supply. 3 Under normal physiological conditions, OEF is ≈ 40%, reflecting a tight coupling between cerebral blood flow and metabolic needs. 4 In cerebrovascular diseases, however, the oxygen homeostasis is impaired due to vascular damage, leading to an increase in OEF. For example, in large‐vessel diseases such as carotid artery stenosis or occlusion, elevated OEF in the ipsilateral hemisphere has been linked to a higher risk for stroke. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Similarly, in patients with cerebral small‐vessel diseases (SVDs), several studies have reported increased OEF, due to insufficiency in blood and oxygen supply. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 These findings suggest that OEF may serve as a functional biomarker for VCID.

However, there have been few studies to demonstrate the impact of OEF on cognitive function. This is because the effects of VCID on cognition are subtle and often require years or decades to accumulate. Furthermore, VCID patients often have mixed pathologies and some of the other pathologies such as AD may have a more prominent effect on cognitive function. 13 In this study, we enrolled a special cohort of older adults enriched for VCID. Each participant must have at least one major vascular risk factor to be included. We measured cortical and subcortical OEF using a non‐invasive magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) technique that takes only 2 minutes. 14 , 15 We hypothesized that elevated OEF would be associated with poorer cognitive performance in this VCID‐enriched cohort. We further hypothesized that elevated OEF would be associated with a greater burden of vascular pathology. Demonstrating these relationships in a clinically representative, VCID‐enriched cohort represents a critical step toward validating OEF as a clinically actionable biomarker, which could support early diagnosis, improve patient stratification for clinical trials, and enable monitoring of intervention efficacy. To test these hypotheses, we examined the associations between OEF and cognitive performance. In addition, we investigated the relationships between OEF and established vascular health measures, including vascular risk factors and WMH, after accounting for AD co‐pathology.

METHODS

Participants and clinical assessment

The study protocol was approved by the Johns Hopkins University Institutional Review Board (IRB00112080). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. Older adults aged 59 to 88 years were recruited between April 2022 and March 2024 from the Johns Hopkins site of the Biomarkers for Vascular Contributions to Cognitive Impairment and Dementia (MarkVCID2) consortium (https://markvcid.partners.org/), which is a multi‐center study aimed at identifying biomarkers for VCID. The MarkVCID2 cohort was specifically enriched for individuals with vascular risk factors and neuroimaging evidence of SVD. 16 , 17

To be enrolled in the present study, an additional inclusion criterion was applied. A participant must have at least one of the following vascular risk factors based on self‐reported medical history 18 : (1) smoking (1 if ≥ 100 lifetime cigarettes, 0 otherwise), (2) hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg; 1 = recent, 0 = remote/absent), (3) diabetes (1 = yes, 0 = no), (4) hypercholesterolemia (1 = recent, 0 = remote/absent), and (5) body mass index (1 if > 30, 0 otherwise). A vascular risk score (VRS) was calculated by summing up the above five binary‐coded factors. Participants with neurologic conditions unrelated to SVD (e.g., history of major strokes, traumatic brain injury, multiple sclerosis, or intracranial mass lesions) were excluded from the study.

Each participant received a standard consensus diagnosis by the MarkVCID clinical team at Johns Hopkins University based on clinical summaries and neuropsychological assessments, following the recommendations of the National Institute on Aging and the Alzheimer's Association workgroup. 19 Diagnostic categories included: (1) cognitively normal, (2) subjective cognitive decline (SCD), 20 (3) mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and (4) dementia.

Cognitive assessment

All participants underwent a battery of neuropsychological tests covering four cognitive domains: (1) verbal episodic memory: Hopkins Verbal Learning Test (Total Recalls [trial 1–3] and Delayed Recalls [trial 4]), 21 Craft Story 21 Recall (Immediate Recall and Delayed Recall); 22 (2) executive function: Digit Span Backward, 22 Stroop Color‐Word Test, 23 Digit Symbol Test, 24 Trail Making Test Part B, 25 Category (animals) fluency tasks; 26 (3) attention: Digit Span Forward, 22 Trail Making Test Part A; (4) language: Multilingual Naming Test, 27 letter F and Category (animals) fluency tasks. 26 Z scores were calculated for each sub‐test and averaged within each domain to create domain‐specific cognitive scores. As an index of global cognitive performance, a composite cognitive score was computed by averaging the Z scores across all four domains. Neuropsychological testing was performed on the same day as the MRI for 80.2% of participants (65 out of 81). For the remaining participants, the median interval between neuropsychological testing and MRI was 22.5 days (interquartile range: 5.0–49.5 days).

Plasma collection and assays

Venous blood samples were collected during the study visit. Plasma concentrations (in pg/mL) of phosphorylated tau 217 (p‐tau217), amyloid beta 1–42 (Aβ42), amyloid beta 1–40 (Aβ40) were measured using Fujirebio Lumipulse G immunoassays. The p‐tau217 measure was used as the primary index of AD pathology. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 Several recent studies have shown that the plasma p‐tau217 has an AD diagnostic accuracy superior to that of other plasma‐based biomarkers, and showed an excellent consistency with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)‐based biomarkers and positron emission tomography (PET) imaging for detecting amyloid positivity. 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 P‐tau217 concentrations were log10‐transformed to approximate normal distribution and ensure variance homogeneity.

Apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 genotypes were determined from buffy coat by restriction endonuclease digestion of polymerase chain reaction (PCR)‐amplified genomic DNA (Taqman real‐time PCR assays). Genotypes were coded dichotomously as APOE ε4 non‐carriers (0) and carriers (1).

MRI experiments and processing

All MRI scans were performed on a 3T Prisma scanner (Siemens Healthineers), using a 32‐channel head coil for receiving and a body coil for transmission.

Cerebral OEF was measured using the accelerated T2‐relaxation‐under‐phase‐contrast (aTRUPC) MRI technique. 14 This technique does not require any exogenous contrast agents and has been validated against blood gas oximetry of direct cerebral venous blood samples in an animal model. 15 The imaging parameters were: 2D single slice in the mid‐sagittal plane, field of view (FOV) = 200 × 200 mm2, slice thickness = 10 mm, reconstructed in‐plane resolution = 0.8 × 0.8 mm2, three effective echo times (eTEs): 0, 40, and 80 ms, velocity encoding = 15 cm/s, generalized autocalibrating partial parallel acquisition acceleration factor = 2, 4 averages, and a total acquisition time of 2.1 minutes.

In addition, high‐resolution 3D T1‐weighted MRI and fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery imaging (FLAIR) MRI were acquired to evaluate intracranial volume (ICV), brain parenchymal volume (BPV), and white matter hyperintensity (WMH) volume. The T1‐weighted sequence used a magnetization‐prepared rapid gradient echo using a sagittal‐plane acquisition with a voxel size of 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm3. The FLAIR sequence used a sagittal‐plane acquisition and a voxel size of 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm3. The MRI protocol followed recommendations of the MarkVCID Consortium. 39 ICV and BPV were derived from the T1‐weighted images using FreeSurfer (version 7.4.1; Martinos Center for Biomedical Imaging). Fractional BPV (fBPV) was calculated as BPV/ICV × 100%. WMH volumes were calculated using a previously validated MarkVCID WMH biomarker kit based on the 3D FLAIR and T1 images. 40 , 41 Based on deep cerebral venous drainage territories defined in prior literature using the FreeSurfer atlas, 42 the WMH volume specifically within these territories was also determined. To adjust for head size, WMH volumes were normalized to the ICV of each subject, and these normalized WMH volumes were used in all subsequent statistical analyses.

aTRUPC MRI data were processed using a semi‐automatic algorithm 43 to obtain a voxel‐by‐voxel map of the transverse relaxation time (T2) of blood in major cerebral veins. Blood T2 was then converted to venous oxygenation (Yv) based on the hematocrit level of each subject. 44 , 45 For participants with hematocrit measurements (N = 66), subject‐specific hematocrit values were used; for others (N = 15), hematocrit was estimated using sex‐specific average values. OEF was then calculated as: OEF=(Ya−Yv)/Ya×100% where the arterial oxygenation (Ya) was assumed to be 98% for all subjects in this study. To assess the precision of OEF measurements, the width of the 95% confidence interval of the transverse relaxation rate (1/T2) of venous blood was quantified and referred to as ΔR2. A larger ΔR2 indicates lower precision in the OEF measurements.

For regional analyses, cortical OEF was measured from the superior sagittal sinus (SSS), through which the blood in the cortical tissue was primarily drained. 43 Subcortical OEF was measured from the combined regions of the straight sinus and the vein of Galen, which drain deep gray matter structures. Due to the large size of the SSS, which produces the most robust measurement, cortical OEF was the primary parameter of interest. Subcortical OEF was the secondary parameter of interest, because deep gray matter is thought to be particularly susceptible to SVD.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using in‐house MATLAB scripts (R2023b, MathWorks). The demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants were compared across diagnostic categories using analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test.

First, a linear regression model was used to examine the relationship between cognitive performance and OEF after accounting for AD co‐pathology. The global cognitive score was the dependent variable. OEF and AD pathological markers were the independent variables. Age, sex, and education were included as covariates. To investigate associations with specific cognitive domains, we repeated these analyses using domain‐specific Z scores as the dependent variable. In all analyses, outliers (defined as Z score ≤−2 or ≥ 2) were excluded to avoid results being driven by outliers. Additionally, to examine whether the relationship between OEF and cognition varied by disease stage, we tested adding diagnosis and an OEF × diagnosis interaction term to the models. Diagnosis was modeled either as an ordinal variable (0 = normal, 1 = SCD, 2 = MCI, 3 = dementia) or as a binary variable (0 = normal or SCD, 1 = MCI or dementia).

Next, linear regression was used to evaluate associations between OEF and vascular and AD pathologies. In this analysis, OEF was the dependent variable, VRS, WMH volume, and plasma AD pathological marker were the independent variables. Age and sex were covariates. Additionally, associations between OEF and APOE ε4 carrier status were examined.

To account for multiple comparisons, we applied Bonferroni correction for the number of cognitive domains tested. A two‐tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

We additionally included fBPV in the regression models examining the associations between OEF and cognitive performance (Table S3 in supporting information). After adjusting for fBPV, both elevated cortical and subcortical OEF remained significantly associated with poorer global cognitive performance and executive function, while fBPV was not significantly associated with either cognitive outcome.

Neither cortical nor subcortical OEF were associated with the diagnostic category. The diagnosis was primarily based on patient and informant questionnaires, which may be different from the neuropsychological test results.

Relationships between OEF and vascular and AD pathology

The results of the dependence of cortical OEF on vascular and AD pathology are shown in Table 4. For vascular pathology, we studied both the upstream factor of vascular risks and the irreversible brain damage, WMH. We found that higher VRSs were associated with a higher cortical OEF (β = 1.4 ± 0.6, p = 0.026; Figure 4A). When investigating the role of different vascular risks, it was found that obesity revealed the strongest influence on cortical OEF (β = 3.1 ± 1.4, p = 0.03). We also observed that higher WMH volume was associated with a higher cortical OEF (β = 2.7 ± 1.2, p = 0.029; Figure 4B). Interestingly, when including both VRS and WMH in the same model, OEF appears to be simultaneously related to both (Table 4). Further analysis revealed that VRS and WMH were not correlated themselves (r = 0.13, p = 0.26).

An additional observation was that cortical OEF was associated with AD pathological markers (Table 4, Figure 4C). Worse p‐tau217 corresponded to a lower cortical OEF (β = −6.0 ± 2.2, p = 0.009). Note that previous studies in non‐vascularly impaired participants have shown a similar relationship between OEF and AD. 46 , 47 , 48

Similar findings were noted when using subcortical OEF (Table S4 in supporting information). One observation was that subcortical OEF was not related to p‐tau217. In contrast, subcortical OEF remained significantly associated with both total WMHs (β = 2.7 ± 1.3, p = 0.033, Model 2 in Table S4) and WMHs within the deep cerebral venous drainage territories (β = 4.5 ± 1.9, p = 0.022, Model 4 in Table S4), with the latter association showing a slightly larger effect size.

In addition, no significant associations were found between both cortical and subcortical OEF and APOE ε4 carrier status (p > 0.3).

DISCUSSION

The main novel observation of this study was that, in patients with vascular risks, both higher cortical and subcortical OEF was found to be associated with poorer cognitive function. This finding provides a strong support for OEF to be further tested as a candidate biomarker in VCID. Further evidence of higher OEF as an indication of SVD includes the observation that OEF was elevated with higher vascular risks and with more severe WMHs.

SVD‐related VCID is present in > 80% of patients with cognitive impairment, according to several post mortem studies. 13 , 49 , 50 , 51 However, compared to AD, biomarkers for SVD are understudied. Several studies such as MarkVCID 52 , 53 , 54 and Diverse Vascular Contributions to Cognitive Impairment and Dementia (DIVERSE‐VCID) 55 aim to test and validate biomarkers that are suitable for diagnosis and prognosis of VCID, with potential applications in clinical trials. However, biomarkers that are being investigated in these studies are either focused on structural abnormalities with only indirect inference on the underlying vascular function, or requires a gas‐inhalation maneuver inside the MRI scanner. 39 The biomarker tested in the present work, OEF of the brain, provides a functional assessment of vascular health while requiring no special equipment. The OEF measurement only takes 2 minutes and can be performed on a standard MRI scanner. Therefore, the clinical impact of the proposed biomarker is potentially important for routine diagnosis, patient stratification for clinical trials, and monitoring of intervention efficacy.

OEF has been previously studied in the context of cognitive impairment and dementia. Interestingly, the earlier studies, 46 , 47 , 48 which primarily focused on AD‐related impairment, have suggested that cognitive decline was associated with a lower OEF. This is because one of the major hallmarks of AD pathology is reduced brain metabolism, often visible on fluorodeoxyglucose PET images, which results in a lower OEF as an injured brain does not require as much oxygen for its energy consumption. 56 , 57 This notion was confirmed by our observation of an inverse relationship between cortical OEF and plasma AD biomarkers. More recently, investigators have turned attention to the influence of VCID on OEF. Jiang et al. first reported that, among patients with cognitive impairment, those with higher vascular risks have a higher OEF. 46 Kang et al. further delineated that the watershed areas are among the most vulnerable to OEF elevations. 10 Yan et al. revealed that obesity in older individuals can also cause a higher OEF. 58 Lin et al. showed that older adults with high vascular risks have a more rapid increase in OEF. 48 The putative reason for these converging findings of an elevated OEF in VCID is “misery perfusion,” in which SVD causes diminished blood supply, thus forcing the brain tissue to draw on their “reserve” by extracting a higher fraction of oxygen from the incoming arterial blood.

However, few studies have shown that higher OEF secondary to SVD can predict cognitive decline. This is because OEF is under the competing influence of AD and VCID, and in most cohorts the effect of AD is the most dominant factor in terms of cognitive function. Therefore, more often, poorer cognition corresponds to a lower OEF. 46 , 47 , 59 The present study is one of the first to demonstrate that cognitive dysfunction is associated with both higher cortical and subcortical OEF. We were able to make this observation because of our special inclusion criteria that only enrolled patients with at least one vascular risk. Furthermore, the negative associations between OEF and cognition did not appear to vary across disease stages. In addition, within our cohort, cognitive performance was not associated with either the age‐related neurodegeneration biomarker (fBPV) or the plasma AD biomarker (p‐tau217). This suggests that, in this VCID‐enriched sample, cognitive function appears to be primarily driven by SVD pathology, and that OEF may be more sensitive than brain atrophy in detecting cognitive impairment. Therefore, the present study provides evidence that, among patients with vascular risks, OEF may be used as a biomarker to identify those with a high risk to develop VCID. This has the utility for selecting patients for VCID clinical trials.

Our results showed that cortical and subcortical OEF were related to global cognitive function. In domain‐specific analyses, we found a significant association between cortical and subcortical OEF and executive function Z score. In contrast, no correlation was observed between cortical and subcortical OEF and episodic memory. Because declines in executive function are common symptoms of VCID, 60 , 61 , 62 whereas impairment in episodic memory is characteristic of AD, 62 , 63 these findings further support that elevated OEF is associated with vascular burden.

We reproduced previous findings that higher OEF is associated with vascular pathologies. 10 , 11 , 12 , 46 , 48 , 58 Interestingly, we observed that cortical and subcortical OEF were simultaneously but independently associated with WMHs and VRSs, in particular obesity, while VRSs and WMHs themselves were not correlated. It is known that VRS represents an early, possibly the first, sign of SVD. With time that may span across decades, WMHs will develop. It has been postulated that there exists one or more biomarkers that appear between VRSs and brain structural damage. 64 Our findings suggest that OEF may serve as such a biomarker, which is altered by vascular risk and may appear before the irreversible brain damage. Therefore, studies of OEF may provide new insight into the pathological cascade of SVD development and progression, which may lead to stage‐specific biomarkers such as an ATN (amyloid/tau/neurodegeneration)–equivalent classification system for VCID.

In contrast to the positive association between OEF and vascular pathologies, we observed a negative association between cortical OEF and plasma p‐tau217 concentration, an established biomarker of AD pathology. This observation is consistent with a previous study reporting that lower OEF was associated with greater amyloid burden measured via CSF assays. 46 Mechanistically, this negative association may reflect reduced neuronal metabolic demand due to synaptic dysfunction and neurodegeneration in AD, as described in earlier studies. 46 , 47 , 48 Notably, the negative direction of this association does not contradict but rather complements the evidence linking elevated OEF to vascular pathology. Moreover, the opposing directions of the associations between OEF and vascular versus AD pathology suggest that OEF may eventually aid in distinguishing the relative contributions of vascular and AD pathology, although further studies in populations with mixed pathology are needed to validate its discriminative utility.

This study separately examined the roles of cortical and subcortical OEF in VCID. Cortical OEF can be measured in the SSS, which is one of the largest veins in the brain. Thus, its measurement is more reliable. Subcortical OEF, while not as reliable (with a larger ΔR2 compared to cortical OEF) due to the smaller caliber of the associated veins, has the potential of being more specifically related to SVD. Our results suggest that both cortical and subcortical OEF measurements were associated with cognitive function. Notably, however, only cortical OEF was related to AD pathology, while subcortical OEF showed no significant association with p‐tau217. Furthermore, the slope coefficients for this null association were approximately half the magnitude of those for the association between cortical OEF and p‐tau217 (Table S3). This indicates that the lack of a significant association between subcortical OEF and p‐tau217 is likely driven primarily by physiological differences between subcortical and cortical brain tissue, rather than by the lower precision of subcortical OEF measurements. In further support of its SVD specificity, subcortical OEF was associated with WMHs, particularly those within the deep venous drainage territory. Physiologically, the subcortical OEF value was derived from the vein of Galen and straight sinus, which collect venous outflow from both deep gray matter (e.g., basal ganglia, thalami, and amygdala) and deep white matter (via the deep medullary veins and subependymal veins). Thus, the association between subcortical OEF and WMH is consistent with the known vulnerability of both deep gray matter and white matter to hemodynamic impairment from SVD, which can manifest as tissue hypoxia and elevated OEF. 9 , 65 , 66 Therefore, with further technical development to improve the reliability of the measurement, subcortical OEF may be a desirable biomarker specific to VCID, but not AD, pathological processes.

Our findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, our sample size (N = 81) is modest. Second, the cross‐sectional design precludes the assessment of the prognosis value of high OEF. Longitudinal studies are needed to investigate how OEF evolves in relation to vascular burden and AD pathology over time, and to determine its potential as a predictive biomarker for cognitive decline. Third, we assumed a Ya of 98% for all participants when calculating OEF, consistent with previous OEF studies. 12 , 46 , 48 , 67 Although this assumption is generally valid in older adults with healthy pulmonary function, some individuals, particularly those with smoking‐related pulmonary diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, may exhibit lower Ya. 68 , 69 In these participants, using a fixed Ya value could introduce errors in OEF estimation. Future studies should consider measuring Ya simultaneously during the MRI scan with an MRI‐compatible pulse oximeter. Fourth, our study lacked cerebral perfusion measurements. Elevated OEF is generally regarded as a compensatory response to reduced oxygen delivery and has been associated with cerebral hypoperfusion in some SVD cohort studies. 11 , 12 However, without concurrent perfusion imaging, we cannot determine whether the observed association between OEF and cognition is driven by underlying hypoperfusion or by other physiological mechanisms. Future studies incorporating both OEF and cerebral blood flow measurements will be necessary to disentangle the interplay among oxygen extraction, cerebral perfusion, and cognitive impairment in populations with vascular risk factors. Finally, OEF was derived from major cerebral veins (e.g., the superior sagittal sinus, vein of Galen, and straight sinus), which drain large cortical and subcortical territories. While this approach captures both cortical and subcortical oxygenation patterns, it may not fully reflect small or focal abnormalities. Further technical developments to enable voxel‐wise OEF measurements are underway.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that, among older participants with vascular risks, elevated OEF may indicate SVD‐related VCID. These findings support the role of OEF as a vascular physiological biomarker of VCID.
