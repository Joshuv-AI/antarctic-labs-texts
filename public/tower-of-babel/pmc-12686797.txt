The domino effect: projecting the effects of US funding cuts to health aid in the global south
Authors: Rechel Shrisunder, Dwight Figueiredo
Published: 2025

The executive order issued on January 20, 2025, by the United States, announcing its withdrawal from the World Health Organization (WHO), will have profound implications for the Global South.1 As the main UN agency with mandate and capacity to coordinate international health responses since 1948, WHO has served as a cornerstone of international health security.2 This decision coincides with financial retrenchment, risking reversal of two decades of progress.

A modelling study projected TB cases and deaths in 26 high-burden TB countries dependent on external financial support. Increases in TB cases were estimated at 0.63 million (CI 0.45–0.81) (minimal impact), 1.66 million (CI 1.2–2.1) (moderate impact), and 10.67 million (CI 7.85–13.19) (worst-case). TB deaths are projected to increase by 99,800 (CI 65,200–130,000), 268,600 (CI 185,800–337,900), and 2,245,700 (CI 1,570,800–2,807,300), respectively.3

USAID funding possibly contributed to reduced mortality from HIV/AIDS by 65% (25.5 million deaths), malaria by 51% (8.0 million deaths), and neglected tropical diseases by 50% (8.9 million deaths). Current funding cuts would cause estimated 14,051,750 additional deaths by 2030, including 4,537,157 in children under 5 years.4

The Global Fund announced major cuts, citing a $6 billion shortfall in 2023–2025, mainly due to unmet U.S. pledges of $4 billion. These cuts affect over 200 grants in 100+ countries, especially sub-Saharan Africa, which receives 72% of Global Fund support.5 USAID/PEPFAR withdrawal from South Asia, sub-Saharan Africa6 and regions of Myanmar, India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh3 may cause drug shortages and weaken surveillance systems, fostering antimicrobial resistance. This threatens SDG 3.3 to end HIV, TB, and malaria epidemics by 2030.

Actions taken by the US Department of Government Efficiency to increase fiscal discipline and cut bureaucratic inefficiencies--without sound predictive & forecasting data--could have sweeping consequences disproportionately affecting the health systems of the Global South. The current situation underscores the importance of diversified, sustainable, and locally anchored funding mechanisms for the health sector. There is also an urgent need for regional solidarity and global accountability.
