Intraluminal aspiration for Ahmed glaucoma valve tube obstruction
Authors: Takuma Hayami, Masayo Kimura, Harumitsu Suzuki, Miho Nozaki, Tsutomu Yasukawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo describe a minimally invasive technique for managing intraluminal obstruction of an Ahmed glaucoma valve (AGV) tube using direct aspiration via the anterior chamber.ObservationsA 75-year-old man with AGV implantation for bilateral secondary glaucoma associated with chronic uveitis developed postoperative intraocular pressure (IOP) elevation in his left eye. Although incarceration at the tube tip was released with neodymium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet (Nd:YAG) laser treatment, IOP remained elevated. Anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) revealed tissue-like material within the tube lumen, confirming the diagnosis of intraluminal obstruction. A 30-gauge needle connected to a syringe was inserted into the tube lumen via the anterior chamber, and negative pressure was applied to aspirate and remove the obstructive tissue. IOP normalized promptly after the procedure, and postoperative AS-OCT confirmed disappearance of the occluding material with no recurrence during 1 year of follow-up.Conclusions and importanceIntraluminal aspiration via the anterior chamber may represent a minimally invasive therapeutic option for AGV tube obstruction that avoids conjunctival incision while preserving bleb function. AS-OCT may play a key role in identifying intraluminal obstruction and determining the indication for surgical intervention.Keywords: Ahmed glaucoma valve, Anterior segment optical coherence tomography, Intraluminal aspiration, Tube obstruction

Introduction

Glaucoma is a leading cause of blindness, and reduction of intraocular pressure (IOP) remains the only evidence-based treatment.1,2 The introduction and widespread use of glaucoma drainage devices (GDDs) have expanded surgical options for glaucoma. Among long-tube GDDs, the Ahmed Glaucoma Valve (AGV) is frequently selected in clinical practice, particularly because its valve mechanism reduces the risk of early postoperative hypotony compared with non-valved devices such as the Baerveldt Glaucoma Implant.3

Among the complications associated with GDDs, tube occlusion remains a potentially unavoidable event, with reported incidence rates ranging from 0.8% to 14%0.4, 5, 6, 7 Tube occlusion can lead to IOP elevation, necessitating prompt therapeutic intervention. Tube occlusion can occur either at the tube tip in the anterior chamber due to mechanical blockage, or within the tube lumen due to tissue incarceration. In the latter case, removing the obstructive material is particularly challenging in valved devices, as the valve mechanism prevents displacement of intraluminal contents toward the plate.8

Here, we report a minimally invasive technique for managing AGV intraluminal tube obstruction caused by vitreous incarceration, in which a 30-gauge needle was inserted into the tube via the anterior chamber to aspirate and remove the obstructive tissue.

Case presentation

A 75-year-old man with bilateral secondary glaucoma associated with chronic uveitis had previously undergone trabeculectomy in both eyes at our hospital. Both eyes were pseudophakic. In the right eye, AGV implantation had been performed 8 months after trabeculectomy because of bleb failure, after which IOP remained well controlled with a fixed combination of travoprost/timolol.

Six years after trabeculectomy, the patient was referred back to our hospital because of elevated IOP in the left eye despite treatment with travoprost/timolol, brinzolamide/brimonidine, ripasudil, and betamethasone. AGV implantation was performed in the left eye, resulting in good postoperative IOP control (5–9 mmHg without anti-glaucoma medications) for six weeks. However, at 22 weeks after AGV implantation, the IOP in the left eye increased to 30 mmHg despite treatment with latanoprost/timolol and ripasudil, and the patient was referred back to our hospital once again.

Comprehensive ophthalmic examination revealed a best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of 20/16 in both eyes. The anterior capsule or vitreous fiber was incarcerated at the tip of the AGV tube placed in the ciliary sulcus in the left eye (Fig. 1a). Although the incarceration was released with a neodymium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet (Nd:YAG) laser (Fig. 1b), IOP reduction was not achieved. Anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) using the RTVue XR Avanti (Optovue Inc., Fremont, CA, USA) with FullRange AC mode revealed a hyperreflective structure within the tube lumen (Fig. 1c), suggesting IOP elevation due to intraluminal obstructive tissue. Surgical removal of the tissue was considered necessary.

During surgery, no obstructive material was identified at the tube tip or within the visible portion of the tube lumen on direct visualization. An iris retractor was used to ensure adequate visualization of the surgical field due to poor mydriasis. Vitreous prolapse around the tube was identified and thoroughly excised using a vitreous cutter. The anterior chamber was then filled with a cohesive viscoelastic agent (Opegan Hi; Santen Pharmaceutical, Osaka, Japan) to maintain the anterior chamber depth. The AGV tube tip was stabilized using anterior capsule forceps. A 30-gauge needle connected to an empty 2.5-mL syringe was introduced via a clear corneal side-port incision on the opposite side of the tube insertion site and carefully inserted into the tube lumen without damaging the tube wall (Fig. 2a). The needle tip was advanced to approximately the level of the iris margin and was not inserted further. Negative pressure was applied by pulling and holding the plunger, and the needle was then withdrawn while maintaining the negative pressure, allowing direct aspiration and removal of the tissue within the tube. This maneuver enabled removal of an approximately 3-mm-long, thread-like piece of tissue (Fig. 2b). The anterior chamber was irrigated to remove the viscoelastic agent. After the surgery, the IOP remained stable without anti-glaucoma medications, measuring 8 mmHg on postoperative day 1, 11 mmHg at month 1, 12 mmHg at month 3, and 10 mmHg at month 12. No re-elevation of IOP was observed for 1 year after surgery. AS-OCT demonstrated no recurrence of intraluminal tissue incarceration at month 5 postoperatively, as confirmed using the CASIA2 (Tomey Corporation, Nagoya, Japan) with bleb mode (Fig. 1d and e).

Automated specular microscopy did not yield reliable endothelial cell counts before or after surgery, likely due to pre-existing corneal endothelial irregularity secondary to chronic uveitis. However, qualitative assessment of endothelial cell morphology did not reveal any apparent enlargement in cell size or increase in polymegethism, suggesting no clinically significant endothelial damage attributable to the procedure. No deterioration in BCVA was observed after surgery.

Discussion

We describe a minimally invasive technique for the management of intraluminal AGV obstruction, in which the obstructive material is aspirated through the anterior chamber by applying negative pressure directly within the tube lumen.

The etiology of AGV tube occlusion is diverse, with reported incidence rates ranging from 0.8% to 14%0.4, 5, 6, 7 While obstruction at the tube tip can often be managed with tissue plasminogen activator,9,10 Nd:YAG laser treatment,11,12 or surgical removal of visible occlusive tissue,13,14 diagnosis and management of intraluminal obstruction in valved devices are more technically challenging. AS-OCT has been reported to be useful in identifying intraluminal obstruction that is not visible on slit-lamp examination alone.15 In the present case, although Nd:YAG laser treatment released the visible incarceration at the tube tip, IOP did not decrease and no obstructive material was identified on subsequent slit-lamp examination. AS-OCT was therefore performed and revealed intraluminal obstructive tissue, prompting the decision to proceed with surgical intervention.

Once intraluminal obstruction is identified, several techniques to displace obstructive material have been reported. Hamanaka16 described a method using a 3-0 nylon suture to push the material through the tube lumen toward the plate. Coleman et al.7 reported that Balanced salt solution (BSS) irrigation from the anterior chamber toward the plate successfully resolved tube occlusion in five of six eyes; however, in the remaining eye, which was occluded by vitreous, the implant was exchanged without attempting irrigation. Bouris et al.6 similarly reported that BSS irrigation from the anterior chamber toward the plate resolved the tube occlusions in 35 of 46 eyes, although 11 eyes (24%), particularly those involving vitreous, required additional intervention such as YAG laser vitreolysis or anterior vitrectomy. Nakakura et al.15 reported a case in which the tube was externalized through a conjunctival incision to access the tube tip; although an attempt to pump the obstructive material from the tube tip toward the plate using a syringe was unsuccessful, the obstruction was ultimately resolved by mechanically extruding the material from the externalized tube. This method, however, is relatively invasive and carries a potential risk of damage to the filtering bleb and the tube itself.

Taken together, these reports suggest that distal displacement-based approaches, whether by pushing or flushing, are not uniformly reliable for intraluminal obstruction in valved devices such as the AGV, and that more invasive measures are often required when standard methods fail.

Considering these limitations, we performed a preoperative wet-lab simulation using an AGV FP7 model to evaluate potential approaches for removing the intraluminal obstruction. We first attempted the technique described by Hamanaka,16 in which a 3-0 nylon suture is inserted via the anterior chamber and advanced through the tube lumen toward the plate to mechanically dislodge the obstructive material, like "chimney sweeping". However, the suture could not be pushed past the valve mechanism. This finding is consistent with the structural properties of the AGV valve, which comprises silicone elastomer membranes in a Venturi-shaped chamber that physically impedes the passage of solid materials,8 and confirms that distal displacement-based approaches are fundamentally limited by the valve structure in valved devices such as the AGV. We therefore considered that direct aspiration of the obstructive material in a proximal direction would be a more reliable approach.

During the simulation, we also found that a 27-gauge blunt needle, commonly used to flush the AGV tube during implantation surgery, was difficult to insert smoothly into the tube lumen under intraocular conditions due to its tight fit with the tube. In contrast, a 30-gauge sharp needle could be inserted smoothly into the tube lumen and generated sufficient negative pressure for aspiration. Based on these findings, we selected a 30-gauge needle for the procedure.

Following the intervention, IOP promptly normalized, and postoperative AS-OCT confirmed the disappearance of the occluding material. No recurrence of tube obstruction was observed during 1 year of follow-up. It should be noted, however, that anterior vitrectomy was performed concurrently to remove vitreous prolapse from the anterior chamber and prevent re-obstruction of the tube. While it is unlikely that the vitrectomy directly removed the intraluminal obstructive tissue—as the tissue was subsequently retrieved by aspiration—it may have contributed to the postoperative IOP reduction by preventing recurrent tube obstruction and improving aqueous outflow. Therefore, the relative contribution of each procedure to the favorable postoperative outcome cannot be determined based on this single case alone.

This case has several limitations. First, the precise nature of the obstructive material remains unclear, as the harvested tissue was not submitted for histopathological examination. Given the history of chronic uveitis and the presence of vitreous prolapse into the anterior chamber, the obstructive material was most likely composed of vitreous fibers or inflammatory tissue. Second, AS-OCT was not performed prior to Nd:YAG laser treatment; therefore, it is possible that the laser fragmented and displaced the incarcerated tissue deeper into the tube lumen, contributing to the intraluminal obstruction. Additionally, preoperative AS-OCT evaluation was limited to the intraocular portion of the tube, and assessment of the extraocular portion beneath the preserved scleral flap should also be considered, as obstruction may extend beyond the visible intraocular segment. Performing AS-OCT before any intervention and evaluating both the intraocular and extraocular portions of the tube may help clarify the etiology of obstruction and guide subsequent treatment decisions. Third, this case involved concurrent anterior vitrectomy, which limits the ability to attribute the postoperative IOP reduction solely to the intraluminal aspiration technique.

Conclusion

Direct intraluminal aspiration via the anterior chamber is a promising minimally invasive approach for intraluminal AGV tube obstruction that warrants further evaluation in larger case series. This technique may be particularly useful in valved GDDs when the obstructive material is located deep within the tube lumen and cannot be grasped directly, or when conventional displacement-based approaches are limited by the valve mechanism. In addition, AS-OCT may be valuable for identifying the presence and location of intraluminal blockage in cases of unexplained IOP elevation after tube shunt surgery. Even when incarceration at the tube tip appears to be the primary cause of IOP elevation, intraluminal obstruction of both the intraocular and extraocular portions of the tube should also be considered and evaluated using AS-OCT.
