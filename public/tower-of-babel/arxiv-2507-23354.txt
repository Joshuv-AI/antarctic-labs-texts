Consistent N eff N_{\rm eff} fitting in big bang nucleosynthesis analysis Preprint: CTPU-PTC-25-29
Sougata Ganguly, Tae Hyun Jung, Seokhoon Yun

Abstract
The effective number of neutrino species, N eff N_{\rm eff} , serves as a key fitting parameter extensively employed in cosmological studies. In this work, we point out a fundamental inconsistency in the conventional treatment of N eff N_{\rm eff} in big bang nucleosynthesis (BBN), particularly regarding its applicability to new physics scenarios where Δ ​ N eff \Delta N_{\rm eff} , the deviation of N eff N_{\rm eff} from the standard BBN prediction, is negative. To ensure consistent interpretation, it is imperative to either restrict the allowed range of N eff N_{\rm eff} or systematically adjust neutrino-induced reaction rates based on physically motivated assumptions. As a concrete example, we consider a simple scenario in which a negative Δ ​ N eff \Delta N_{\rm eff} arises from entropy injection into the electromagnetic sector due to the decay of long-lived particles after neutrino decoupling. This process dilutes the neutrino density and suppresses the rate of neutrino-driven neutron-proton conversion. Under this assumption, we demonstrate that the resulting BBN constraints on N eff N_{\rm eff} deviate significantly from those obtained by the conventional, but unphysical, extrapolation of dark radiation scenarios into the Δ ​ N eff < 0 \Delta N_{\rm eff}<0 regime.

Obviously, the most important effective parameter is the baryon asymmetry parameter

Another widely-used parameter is the effective number of neutrino species

When

II
Assumptions for exploring
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
in BBN

To explore the effects of varying single parameter of
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
during BBN, we adopt a minimal set of assumptions that allow for a model-independent interpretation within two distinct regimes:
\Delta N_{\rm eff}>0
and
\Delta N_{\rm eff}<0
.

For
\Delta N_{\rm eff}>0
, we assume that the neutrino sector remains unaltered from the standard scenario, and the additional energy density arises solely from a dark radiation component which decouples from the Standard Model (SM) plasma well before neutrino decoupling and remains relativistic throughout the BBN epoch.
This assumption is typical in the literature.
Since it does not interact with the SM plasma, it does not affect either the neutrino or photon temperatures, and the standard neutrino-to-photon temperature ratio remains unchanged as follows

\displaystyle\frac{T_{\nu}(T_{\gamma})}{T_{\nu}^{\rm(0)}\!(T_{\gamma})}=1\quad\text{for $\Delta N_{\rm eff}>0$},

(5)

where
T_{\nu}(T_{\gamma})
denotes the modified neutrino temperature as a function of photon temperature
T_{\gamma}
, and
T_{\nu}^{\rm(0)}(T_{\gamma})
is the corresponding temperature in the standard scenario.
During the BBN era, both neutrinos and dark radiation redshift as
a^{-4}
, where
a
is the scale factor, so their energy density ratio remains constant.
This allows us to express the energy density of dark radiation as
\rho_{\rm DR}=(\Delta N_{\rm eff}/N_{\rm eff})\rho_{\nu}
, justifying the use of Eq. (
2
) for the modified Hubble rate.

For
\Delta N_{\rm eff}<0
, we assume that the deviation arises entirely from a suppressed neutrino temperature, i.e., a reduced
T_{\nu}/T_{\gamma}
ratio.
This leads to the relation

\displaystyle\frac{T_{\nu}(T_{\gamma})}{T_{\nu}^{\rm(0)}\!(T_{\gamma})}=\left(\frac{N_{\rm eff}}{N_{\rm eff}^{\rm(0)}}\right)^{\!\!1/4}\quad\text{for $\Delta N_{\rm eff}<0$}.

(6)

This is the simplest viable assumption, as any negative
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
necessarily implies a deviation in
T_{\nu}/T_{\gamma}
.
The resulting Hubble rate is again given by Eq. (
2
).

Eq. (
6
) can be realized in scenarios where a dark sector particle
\chi
decays electromagnetically after the neutrino decoupling (at
T_{\nu{\rm d}}\simeq 2\,{\rm MeV}
) but before the freeze-out of the neutron-to-proton ratio (at
T_{np}\simeq 0.8\,{\rm MeV}
).
If the decay occurs at
T_{\gamma}>T_{\nu{\rm d}}
, the injected entropy is shared with the neutrino sector, which preserves the standard scenario.
On the other hand, if the decay occurs at
T_{\gamma}<T_{np}
, the analysis becomes significantly more complicated because it starts to require the time dependence of
T_{\nu}/T_{\nu}^{\rm(0)}
and the impact of
\chi
on the early-time Hubble rate.

To avoid this complication, we focus on the case where the decay of
\chi
occurs in the interval
T_{np}<T_{\gamma}<T_{\nu{\rm d}}
, which enables a simplified analysis with
N_{\rm eff}
as the only additional parameter.
Even in this case, Eq. (
6
) must be interpreted as an approximation.
It is strictly invalid for
T_{\gamma}>T_{\nu{\rm d}}
, since neutrinos are still in equilibrium with photons before decoupling.
However, this approximation suffices for predicting the light-element abundances, as the neutron-to-proton ratio freezes out near
T_{np}
and is not sensitive to the earlier thermal history.

The approximation is also assumed to be flavor-universal.
Although small differences in neutrino temperature can exist between flavors, these ratios remain unchanged if the entropy injection occurs only in the visible sector. Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{T_{\nu_{e}}(T_{\gamma})}{T_{\nu_{e}}^{\rm(0)}\!(T_{\gamma})}=\frac{T_{\nu_{\mu}}(T_{\gamma})}{T_{\nu_{\mu}}^{\rm(0)}\!(T_{\gamma})}=\frac{T_{\nu_{\tau}}(T_{\gamma})}{T_{\nu_{\tau}}^{\rm(0)}\!(T_{\gamma})}\,.

(7)

Therefore, the ratio in Eq. (
6
) can be applied directly to electron neutrinos in the BBN calculation.

The assumption in Eq. (
6
) is adopted for simplicity but is also necessary to preserve the model-independent character of the analysis.
Exploring other scenarios would require introducing additional parameters beyond
N_{\rm eff}
.
For instance, if
\chi
decays around or after
T_{np}
, one must account for the lifetime, mass, and abundance of
\chi
, resulting in model-dependent predictions (see, e.g., Refs.
Ibe et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2020
;
Yeh et al. 2024
).
If
\chi
decays into hadrons
Reno and Seckel 1988
;
Kohri and Yokoyama 2000
;
Kohri 2001
;
Pospelov and Pradler 2010
, the neutron-to-proton ratio can be significantly altered by injected charged pions, kaons, and baryons, necessitating additional assumptions about decay branching ratios.

Similarly, constraints derived under the dark radiation assumption for
\Delta N_{\rm eff}>0
cannot be applied to models in which the excess energy is deposited into the neutrino sector (see, e.g., Refs.
Escudero et al. 2019
;
Sabti et al. 2020
;
Esseili and Kribs 2024
;
Chang et al. 2024
;
Deppisch et al. 2025
;
Kanzaki et al. 2007
).
In such cases, a more precise treatment of the neutrino momentum distribution is required, and
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
alone is not sufficient to characterize the scenario.
In fact, it is even possible to have a small
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
due to cancellations between competing dark sector effects, despite a significant shift in
T_{\nu}/T_{\gamma}

Hong et al. 2024
.

These limitations underscore that
N_{\rm eff}
may not serve as a universally reliable fitting parameter for BBN analyses.
Nevertheless, in the remainder of this work, we perform BBN fits using Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
), each of which is consistent with certain classes of new physics scenarios.
It is important to emphasize that conventional extrapolations of Eq. (
5
) into the
\Delta N_{\rm eff}<0
region do not correspond to any physically realizable model and thus yield unphysical results.
Likewise, extrapolating Eq. (
6
) into the
\Delta N_{\rm eff}>0
regime does not produce accurate predictions, as the momentum distribution of injected neutrinos becomes important in that case.

To avoid such inconsistencies, we separately constrain
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
using Eq. (
5
) for
\Delta N_{\rm eff}>0
and Eq. (
6
) for
\Delta N_{\rm eff}<0
.
Since the two are continuously connected at
\Delta N_{\rm eff}=0
, we combine the results into a single plot, as shown in Fig.
1
.

III
Impact on Neutron-Proton Freeze-Out

III.1
Weak interaction rates

We now examine how a nonzero shift in
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
modifies the neutron-proton freeze-out process by altering both the Hubble rate
H
and the neutrino temperature
T_{\nu}
.
The evolution of the neutron-to-baryon ratio, defined as
X_{n}\equiv n_{n}/n_{B}
, where
n_{n}
is the neutron number density, is governed by the Boltzmann equation

\displaystyle\frac{dX_{n}}{dt}\!=\!-\Gamma_{n\to p}(T_{\gamma},T_{\nu})X_{n}\!+\Gamma_{p\to n}(T_{\gamma},T_{\nu})X_{p}\!+\cdots,

(8)

where
X_{p}\simeq 1-X_{n}
denotes the proton-to-baryon ratio, and the ellipsis represents contributions from other nuclear reactions, which are negligible in the temperature range
T_{\gamma}>T_{np}
.
The rates
\Gamma_{n\to p}
and
\Gamma_{p\to n}
correspond to neutron-to-proton and proton-to-neutron conversions via weak interactions, respectively.

When neutrinos/anti-neutrinos and electrons/positrons follow equilibrium distributions, the individual reaction rates contributing to
\Gamma_{n\to p}
and
\Gamma_{p\to n}
can be written in analytic form
Kolb and Turner 1990
;
Weinberg 2008
;
Mukhanov 2004

\displaystyle\Gamma_{n\nu_{e}\to pe^{-}}=\frac{1+3g_{A}^{2}}{2\pi^{3}}G_{F}^{2}Q^{5}J_{1}^{\infty}(T_{\gamma},T_{\nu})\,,

(9)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ne^{+}\to p\bar{\nu}_{e}}=\frac{1+3g_{A}^{2}}{2\pi^{3}}G_{F}^{2}Q^{5}J_{-\infty}^{-m_{e}/Q}(T_{\gamma},T_{\nu})\,,

(10)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{p\bar{\nu}_{e}\to ne^{+}}=\frac{1+3g_{A}^{2}}{2\pi^{3}}G_{F}^{2}Q^{5}K_{-\infty}^{-m_{e}/Q}(T_{\gamma},T_{\nu})\,,

(11)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{pe^{-}\to n\nu_{e}}=\frac{1+3g_{A}^{2}}{2\pi^{3}}G_{F}^{2}Q^{5}K_{1}^{\infty}(T_{\gamma},T_{\nu})\,,

(12)

where
g_{A}\simeq 1.27
,
G_{F}
is the Fermi constant, and
Q\equiv m_{n}-m_{p}\simeq 1.293\,{\rm MeV}
is the neutron-proton mass difference.
The
J_{a}^{b}
and
K_{a}^{b}
functions are defined by

\displaystyle J_{a}^{b}(T_{\gamma},T_{\nu})=\!\!\int_{a}^{b}\!\!\sqrt{1-\frac{(m_{e}/Q)^{2}}{q^{2}}}\frac{q^{2}(q-1)^{2}dq}{(1+e^{-\frac{Q}{T_{\gamma}}q})(1+e^{\frac{Q}{T_{\nu}}(q-1)})}\,,

(13)

\displaystyle K_{a}^{b}(T_{\gamma},T_{\nu})=\!\!\int_{a}^{b}\!\!\sqrt{1-\frac{(m_{e}/Q)^{2}}{q^{2}}}\frac{q^{2}(q-1)^{2}dq}{(1+e^{\frac{Q}{T_{\gamma}}q})(1+e^{-\frac{Q}{T_{\nu}}(q-1)})}\,.

(14)

We also include the neutron decay rate
\Gamma_{n}=(878.4\,\sec)^{-1}

Navas et al. 2024
as part of the total
\Gamma_{n\to p}
.

Before freeze-out, when
\Gamma_{n\to p}\,,\Gamma_{p\to n}\gg H
, the neutron-to-baryon ratio follows its equilibrium value

\displaystyle X_{n,{\rm fo}}(T,T_{\nu})\simeq\frac{\Gamma_{p\to n}(T,T_{\nu})}{\Gamma_{n\to p}(T,T_{\nu})+\Gamma_{p\to n}(T,T_{\nu})}\,.

(15)

Note that
T_{\nu}
is a function of
T_{\gamma}
.
In the standard BBN (SBBN) scenario, the freeze-out temperature
T_{np}^{\rm(0)}
is close to or slightly higher than the electron mass,
m_{e}\simeq 0.511\,{\rm MeV}
, so the neutrino temperature is approximately equivalent to the photon temperature,
T_{\nu}^{\rm(0)}\simeq T_{\gamma}
.
This leads to the standard relations of
\Gamma^{(0)}_{p\to n}(T,T)\simeq\Gamma^{(0)}_{n\to p}(T,T)e^{-Q/T}
, which yield a freeze-out value of
X_{n}\approx 0.17
.

At the deuterium bottleneck temperature
T_{\text{D}}
, almost all neutrons become
{}^{4}\text{He}
.
Thus,
X_{n}
is directly related to the observable
Y_{\rm p}\simeq 2X_{n}|_{T=T_{\text{D}}}\simeq 2X_{n,{\rm fo}}|_{T=T_{np}}e^{-\Gamma_{n}(t_{\text{D}}-t_{np})}
, where the exponential factor comes from the fact that
X_{n}
slightly decreases from its freeze-out value due to the beta decay of the neutron.
This allows us to approximately consider

\displaystyle\frac{\delta Y_{\rm p}}{Y_{\rm p}^{\rm(0)}}\simeq\frac{\delta X_{n,{\rm fo}}\big(T_{np},\,T_{\nu}(T_{np})\big)}{X_{n,{\rm fo}}\big(T_{np}^{\rm(0)},\,T_{\nu}^{\rm(0)}\!(T_{np}^{\rm(0)})\big)},

(16)

where
Y_{\rm p}^{\rm(0)}
is the
Y_{\rm p}
predicted in the SBBN, and
\delta Y_{\rm p}\equiv Y_{\rm p}-Y_{\rm p}^{\rm(0)}
is the deviation from the SBBN for a nonzero
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
case.
For simplicity, we denote the right-hand-side of Eq. (
16
) as simply
\delta X_{n,{\rm fo}}/X_{n,{\rm fo}}^{(0)}
.

In the presence of additional dark radiation (i.e.,
\Delta N_{\rm eff}>0
with
T_{\nu}/T_{\nu}^{\rm(0)}=1
), the increased Hubble expansion rate reduces the efficiency of the weak interactions, as
\frac{d}{dt}\simeq-H\frac{d}{d\ln T}
.
Consequently, the freeze-out occurs earlier (
T_{np}<T_{np}^{\rm(0)}
), when
X_{n}
is higher, leading to a larger final abundance of

For the case of
\Delta N_{\rm eff}<0
, where entropy is injected into the visible sector, the

This alters
X_{n}^{\rm(eq)}

, since both

Combining these effects, we find that the freeze-out value of

decreases as

III.2
Analytic Estimates of
X_{n}
Sensitivity to
\Delta N_{\rm eff}

While exact numerical results are obtained separately using full calculations, we now analyze how small deviations in
N_{\rm eff}
affect the neutron-to-baryon ratio
X_{n}
, focusing on linear-order corrections.
This analysis is intended to capture the qualitative parametric dependence.

The leading-order modification to the Hubble rate can be expressed as

\displaystyle H\simeq H^{\rm(0)}\left(1+\frac{7}{86}\Delta N_{\rm eff}\right)\,.

(17)

On the other hand, the weak reaction rates depend on
T_{\nu}
, while we have different
T_{\nu}
for
\Delta N_{\rm eff}>0
and
\Delta N_{\rm eff}<0
.
So, let us treat each case separately below.

III.2.1
\Delta N_{\rm eff}<0

For negative
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
, the deviation is realized via a reduction in the neutrino temperature, as described by Eq. (
6
).
To linear order, this gives

\displaystyle\frac{\delta T_{\nu}}{T_{\nu}^{\rm(0)}}\simeq\frac{1}{12}\Delta N_{\rm eff}\,.

(18)

This in turn modifies the Hubble rate as

\displaystyle H\simeq H^{\rm(0)}\left(1+\frac{42}{43}\frac{\delta T_{\nu}}{T_{\nu}^{\rm(0)}}\right)\,.

(19)

Since the neutron-proton conversion rates are sensitive to the electron-neutrino temperature, we examine how small perturbations in
T_{\nu}
affect them.
At the temperatures of interest, the relevant rates approximately scale as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{n\nu_{e}\to pe^{-}}(T_{\gamma},T_{\nu})\propto\left(\frac{T_{\nu}}{Q}\right)^{5}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{T_{\nu}}{Q}\right)^{4}

(20)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{p\bar{\nu}_{e}\to ne^{+}}(T_{\gamma},T_{\nu})\propto\left(\frac{T_{\nu}}{Q}\right)^{4}\exp\left[-\frac{Q+m_{e}}{T_{\nu}}\right]

(21)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ne^{+}\to p\bar{\nu}_{e}}(T_{\gamma},T_{\nu})\propto\left(\frac{T_{\gamma}}{Q}\right)^{5}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{T_{\gamma}}{Q}\right)^{4}

(22)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{pe^{-}\to n\nu_{e}}(T_{\gamma},T_{\nu})\propto\left(\frac{T_{\gamma}}{Q}\right)^{5}\exp\left[-\frac{Q-m_{e}}{T_{\gamma}}\right]

(23)

Thus, to linear order in
\delta T_{\nu}/T_{\nu}^{\rm(0)}
, we find

\displaystyle\dfrac{\Gamma_{n\to p}(T_{\gamma},T_{\nu})}{\Gamma_{n\to p}(T_{\gamma},T_{\nu}^{\rm(0)})}

\displaystyle\simeq 1+\left(2+\dfrac{T_{\nu}^{(0)}}{Q+2T_{\nu}^{(0)}}\right)\dfrac{\delta T_{\nu}}{T_{\nu}^{(0)}}\,,

(24)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\Gamma_{p\to n}(T_{\gamma},T_{\nu})}{\Gamma_{p\to n}(T_{\gamma},T_{\nu}^{\rm(0)})}

\displaystyle\simeq 1+\left(2+\dfrac{Q+m_{e}}{2T_{\nu}^{(0)}}\right)\dfrac{\delta T_{\nu}}{T_{\nu}^{(0)}}\,.

(25)

Recall that
T_{\nu}
and
T_{\nu}^{\rm(0)}
are approximately linear functions of
T_{\gamma}
, while
\delta T_{\nu}/T_{\nu}^{\rm(0)}
is constant.

The
n/p
freeze-out temperature
T_{np}
is determined by the condition

\displaystyle\Big[\Gamma_{n\to p}+\Gamma_{p\to n}\Big]_{T=T_{np}}\simeq H(T_{np})\,.

(26)

We take the SBBN value
T_{np}^{{\rm(0)}}\simeq 0.82\,{\rm MeV}
, which gives the equilibrium abundance
X_{n,\rm fo}^{\rm(0)}=\frac{e^{-Q/T_{np}^{\rm(0)}}}{1+e^{-Q/T_{np}^{\rm(0)}}}\simeq 0.17
.
Around
T_{np}^{{\rm(0)}}
, we can also approximate
T_{\nu}^{(0)}\simeq T_{\gamma}
, and thus
\Gamma_{n\to p}(T_{np}^{\rm(0)},T_{np}^{\rm(0)})\simeq\frac{1}{1+e^{-Q/T_{np}^{{\rm(0)}}}}H(T_{np}^{\rm(0)})
and
\Gamma_{p\to n}(T_{np}^{\rm(0)},T_{np}^{\rm(0)})\simeq\frac{e^{-Q/T_{np}^{{\rm(0)}}}}{1+e^{-Q/T_{np}^{{\rm(0)}}}}H(T_{np}^{\rm(0)})
.
Linearizing in
\delta T_{\nu}
, we obtain the shift in the freeze-out temperature,
\delta T_{np}\equiv T_{np}-T_{np}^{(0)}
,

\displaystyle\dfrac{\delta T_{np}}{T_{np}^{(0)}}\simeq-0.5\dfrac{\delta T_{\nu}}{T_{\nu}^{(0)}}\simeq-4\times 10^{-2}\Delta N_{\rm eff}.

(27)

Consequently, the freeze-out value of the neutron fraction becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\delta X_{n,{\rm fo}}}{X_{n,{\rm fo}}^{(0)}}

\displaystyle\simeq 5\times 10^{-2}\frac{\delta T_{\nu}}{T_{\nu}^{\rm(0)}}\simeq 4\times 10^{-3}\Delta N_{\rm eff}\,.

(28)

The coefficient of
N_{\rm eff}
in Eq. (
28
) is an order of magnitude smaller than that in Eq. (
27
).
This implies that there exists a cancellation at
10\,\%
-tuning level among the different effects such as shifts of
T_{\nu}
,
H
, and
X_{n,{\rm fo}}
.
Since there is no symmetry that guarantees this cancellation, we consider it accidental.

III.2.2
\Delta N_{\rm eff}>0

In the case of positive
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
, corresponding to additional dark radiation degree, the neutron-proton conversion rates are unchanged from the SBBN case (i.e.,
T_{\nu}=T_{\nu}^{\rm(0)}
).
The change in
H
then leads to a shift in the freeze-out temperature as

\displaystyle\dfrac{\delta T_{np}}{T_{np}^{(0)}}\simeq 3\times 10^{-2}\Delta N_{\rm eff}

(29)

This results in a modified freeze-out abundance

\displaystyle\frac{\delta X_{n,{\rm fo}}}{X_{n,{\rm fo}}^{\rm(0)}}\simeq 4\times 10^{-2}\Delta N_{\rm eff}\,.

(30)

These results, Eqs. (
28
) and (
30
), demonstrate that the sensitivity of
X_{n}
to
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
is one order of magnitude weaker in the entropy injection case (
\Delta N_{\rm eff}<0
).
This suppression arises from an accidental cancellation between the competing effects of the reduced equilibrium abundance and shifts in
T_{np}
and
X_{n}^{\rm(eq)}
.
As a result, setting a robust lower bound on
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
using BBN becomes challenging under electromagnetic injection scenarios.

III.3
Numerical implementation with
PArthENoPE

For a quantitatively more precise study,
we perform numerical calculations using the publicly available
PArthENoPE
3.0 code
Pisanti et al. 2008
;
Consiglio et al. 2018
;
Gariazzo et al. 2022
, incorporating appropriate modifications to the weak interaction rates.
The default implementation of weak interaction rates in
PArthENoPE
includes higher-order corrections
Serpico et al. 2004
, which are beyond the scope of our present analysis.
Instead, we restrict ourselves to the leading-order expressions for the neutron-proton conversion rates, as defined in Eqs. (
9
)-(
12
).
To implement the effects of
\delta T_{\nu}
, we define correction factors as

\displaystyle x_{np}=\frac{\Gamma_{n\to p}}{\Gamma_{n\to p}^{(0)}}\,,\quad x_{pn}=\frac{\Gamma_{p\to n}}{\Gamma_{p\to n}^{(0)}}\,,

(31)

where
\Gamma_{n\to p}^{(0)}
and
\Gamma_{p\to n}^{(0)}
denote the standard leading-order rates.
We then rescale the weak interaction rates in the code by
x_{np}
and
x_{pn}
, respectively.
This approach consistently incorporates the effects of a modified neutrino temperature up to the leading order, which is sufficient for our purposes in this study.

In the
PArthENoPE
framework, we access the standard neutrino-to-photon temperature ratio
T_{\nu}^{\rm(0)}/T_{\gamma}
which is defined as

\displaystyle\frac{T_{\nu}^{\rm(0)}}{T_{\gamma}}\equiv\left(\frac{\hat{\rho}_{e\gamma B}(T_{\gamma})+\hat{p}_{e\gamma B}(T_{\gamma})}{\hat{\rho}_{e\gamma B}(T_{\nu{\rm d}})+\hat{p}_{e\gamma B}(T_{\nu{\rm d}})}\right)^{1/3}\,,

(32)

for photon temperature
T_{\gamma}<T_{\nu{\rm d}}\equiv 2.3\,{\rm MeV}
.
Then, we use Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
) to obtain
T_{\nu}
for a given
N_{\rm eff}
input, and calculate the correction factors
x_{np}
and
x_{pn}
at each time step.
Here,
\hat{\rho}_{e\gamma B}(T)\equiv\rho_{e\gamma B}/T^{4}
and
\hat{p}_{e\gamma B}(T)\equiv p_{e\gamma B}/T^{4}
, with
\rho_{e\gamma B}
and
p_{e\gamma B}
denoting the energy density and pressure of the electron-photon-baryon plasma, respectively.
For
T_{\gamma}>T_{\nu{\rm d}}
, the temperature ratio is fixed to unity.

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we have pointed out the difficulty of implementing a consistent treatment of
N_{\rm eff}
in BBN analysis.
The fundamental reason for this difficulty comes from the fact that neutrinos matter in BBN, while a negative
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
necessarily modifies the neutrino sector in a model-dependent way.
The fitting results obtained in the conventional method (where the neutrino temperature is unmodified even at
\Delta N_{\rm eff}<0
) cannot be used for any particle physics model.

Accepting some model dependence, we have also introduced a scheme to conduct
N_{\rm eff}
fitting for BBN, where we assume the dark radiation for
\Delta N_{\rm eff}>0
and early electromagnetic injection for
\Delta N_{\rm eff}<0
.
This allows, at least, a physically sensible interpretation of the BBN fitting result.
However, as shown analytically in Sec.
III.2
and also depicted explicitly in Figs.
1
and
2
,
Y_{\rm p}
loses its sensitivity on
N_{\rm eff}
at
\Delta N_{\rm eff}<0
, making the BBN fitting itself less useful for constraining
N_{\rm eff}
.

All these aspects indicate that
N_{\rm eff}
is not a good effective fitting parameter in the BBN analysis.
