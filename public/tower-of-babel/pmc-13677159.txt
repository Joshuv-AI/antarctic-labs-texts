‘It Makes Me Feel Confident’: A Community Based Participatory Study of Experiences of Employment for People With Intellectual Disabilities
Authors: Deanna Gallichan, Chloe Deacon, Karen Gresty, Simon Rundle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundA social enterprise brewery requested help from researchers to understand their employment opportunities for people with intellectual disabilities (‘trainees’). A community‐based participatory framework (CBPR) was used to develop the research.MethodDigital storytelling was selected to enable trainees to express their views about their work. Six participants created digital stories with support from researchers. Transcripts of the stories were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.FindingsThree main themes were developed: ‘Learning new skills’; ‘We do good work’; and ‘I belong at the brewery’, each with several subthemes. One overarching theme, ‘I feel more confident’ was developed to represent the ways in which different aspects of the work had developed their confidence.ConclusionWorking within the brewery seemed to have both immediate and far‐reaching benefits for participants. Further research would be needed to better understand the wider impact on the local community.

Introduction

Employment for People With Intellectual Disabilities

Figures suggest that only 5%–6% of adults with intellectual disabilities (also termed ‘learning disabilities’ in the UK), are in employment (Department for Health and Social Care 2025) despite consistent findings emphasising a positive link between employment and quality of life for this population. 1 Jahoda et al.'s (2008) review found that employment increased quality of life, well‐being and autonomy for people with intellectual disabilities. Subsequently, Jahoda et al.'s (2009) longitudinal study interviewed 35 people with intellectual disabilities as they started a new job, and again 9–12 months later. They found that work gave purpose, financial rewards, opportunities for relationships and increased self‐worth, but could also be a source of anxiety. If adaptations were not sufficient people worried about losing their jobs, and social relationships did not always leave people with a genuine sense of belonging. A review by Robertson et al. (2019) also concluded that employment was associated with better physical and mental health for this group, but the cross‐sectional nature of most studies made it difficult to infer causal links; those with better health may have been more likely to be employed. These studies suggest nuance is required to understand the potential benefits of work for people with intellectual disabilities.

Akkerman et al.'s (2018a, 2018b) study of job satisfaction in people with intellectual disabilities (n = 117) in the Netherlands attempted to understand these nuances with reference to self‐determination theory (Ryan and Deci 2000). They found that allowing workers to feel autonomous, effective and connected to others was more important to job satisfaction than their level of participation. They also found that conscientious workers tended to have their task demands increased, resulting in lower job satisfaction. Studies from other countries have also found that flexibility in job design, task matching and tailored support are crucial to the well‐being of people with intellectual disabilities in the workplace (see Flores et al. 2011; Meacham et al. 2017, 2021; Joyce et al. 2024).

Studies have also focused on different models of employment. Almalky's (2020) review differentiated between sheltered employment (i.e., exclusively for people with disabilities), supported employment (i.e., integrated with non‐disabled workers with support to develop skills), customised employment (i.e., supported within a competitive work setting) and competitive employment (i.e., equal wages to non‐disabled co‐workers). All forms had positive impacts on self‐esteem, self‐development, self‐confidence and independence, but benefits were more pronounced for supported or competitive employment. Randall et al. (2023) also reported similar findings. Almalky (2020) suggested that the integration opportunities in these models could enhance social skills and relationships. They also pointed out that sheltered employment could support people to learn skills that could lead to more integrated employment, and that there was a need to establish such developmental pathways.

Meltzer et al. (2018) reported similar findings in Australia comparing ‘open’ (i.e., mainstream, competitive) with ‘sheltered’ (i.e., segregated, production line type work) and social enterprise employment (i.e., organisations set up for community benefit employing marginalised groups). They found challenges to each type, with open employment often seen as less understanding. They suggested that social enterprises offered several benefits including community connection, supportive cultures and an emphasis on skill development, although there could be issues with waiting for paid positions, and there was not always sufficient focus on autonomy. Overall, it appears that the benefits from employment for people with intellectual disabilities relate strongly to the workplace set up.

Social Enterprise Brewery: The Community

The social enterprise brewery at the heart of this study was established in 2018 as an inclusive employment project for people with intellectual disabilities and autism. The majority of trainees have intellectual disabilities, although people with autism (without intellectual disabilities) were included in the research if they wished. The company brews, bottles, packs and sells beer, alongside a Tap Room service. In 2024, they provided 3300 h of volunteer work and over 700 h of work (paid and volunteering) for adults with intellectual disabilities and autism. At the time of the study there were five non‐disabled staff (two paid and three volunteers) who ran work sessions and twelve trainees (five in paid work and seven volunteers). The brewery also raises the profile of people with intellectual disabilities and autism within the local community.

The brewery combines elements of sheltered, supportive and competitive/open employment identified in the literature, within a social enterprise (Meltzer et al. 2018; Almalky 2020). Their developmental model matches tasks to people's abilities. People can start by volunteering with specific tasks (e.g., labelling and packing), sometimes as part of their support hours (i.e., perhaps closer to ‘sheltered employment’). Non‐disabled employees support trainees to develop their skills in aspects of brewing and customer service (supported employment) eventually leading to paid shifts which can include serving customers (i.e., closer to competitive employment).

As a social enterprise, the brewery wanted to capture the impact of their employment model. Previous attempts to do this had used questionnaire methodologies, but these were not sufficiently accessible and could not yield meaningful insights into trainees' lived experience. This limited the brewery's potential to demonstrate the broader value of its work, build social capital and develop future funding applications. This led the brewery board (represented by the third and fourth authors) to approach the researcher (first author and local resident) and request collaboration. This offered a unique opportunity to undertake real‐world research on the lived experiences of people with intellectual disabilities undertaking employment within a social enterprise that incorporated several employment models identified in the literature (e.g., Meltzer et al. 2018; Almalky 2020).

Community Based Participatory Research (CBPR)

Community Based Participatory Research (CBPR) is a framework where by research is developed in partnership with communities and grounded in the needs that they identify, rather than by academic researchers. Collins et al.'s. (2018) overview of CBPR within psychological research highlights its collaborative and flexible nature. Key characteristics of CBPR include: the importance of the relationships that communities develop with researchers; the central role of the community in developing the research questions; the flexibility required in research design to incorporate the community's needs and priorities; the role of community members in aspects of data collection or analysis; the importance of considering accessible and non‐academic formats for dissemination.

In this study, several elements aligned with a CBPR framework, most notably that the impetus for the research came from the brewery (i.e., the ‘community’), rather than the researcher, and that relationships and collaboration were crucial in developing the research questions, study design and execution.

This project brought together the community‐based, lived experience of adults with intellectual disabilities working at the brewery with academic and clinical expertise. The term ‘trainees’ will be used throughout this paper to represent this group as all roles at the brewery included a training element (i.e., including paid roles). The first author spent time with trainees and the brewery board (including the third and fourth authors) to develop the research question and design. These discussions identified that the research question needed to be sufficiently broad and accessible in order to explore trainees' experiences. The primary research question was ‘What is it like for people with intellectual disabilities to train and work at the brewery?’. A secondary aim was to explore the utility of the CPBR approach and data collection method with people with intellectual disabilities to inform future researchers.

Method

Design

The study was informed by a CBPR framework and funded by a scheme designed to build partnerships between community groups and university researchers.

After establishing the research question (see above), the first author visited the brewery over several weeks and joined social events held by trainees to develop the design. Discussions were held in small and large group and 1:1 formats to suit the needs of trainees. Time was taken to explain the concept of ‘research’ and working with the university. A qualitative design using digital storytelling as a data collection method was agreed. Digital stories are 3–5 min long and are a flexible method to allow people to tell their stories using multiple media sources such as voiceover, photographs, music, drawing and video clips. Digital storytelling developed as a way of empowering marginalised communities (Lambert 2013), and has been used as a data gathering method within participatory research, particularly with marginalised groups, such as Indigenous communities (Flicker and MacEntee 2020), women who have experienced relational abuse (Martin et al. 2019), people with intellectual disabilities (Harris et al. 2024) and a vulnerable marginalised community group (Voss et al., forthcoming). Fish and Syed (2021) provide a framework and guidelines for using digital storytelling within psychological research, arguing that this method offers greater potential to empower people to tell their unique stories compared to traditional interview methods.

Trainees were used to being photographed and videoed for local news items, and some trainees had produced artwork related to the brewery. As a group they were more enthused by this method of data collection and felt it was more accessible than traditional interviews. This method also had the potential to facilitate the creation of material that the brewery could use to communicate their impact more effectively to funders and stakeholders.

Ethics

Ethical approval was granted by the University of Plymouth Faculty of Health Ethics Committee. All study documents were drafted and discussed with trainees to ensure accessibility. Additional documents enabled participants to gain consent from people if they wished to include an image of them in their digital stories (e.g., colleagues). Digital stories could potentially identify participants, and this was explicitly discussed when establishing consent. Participants had experience of choosing whether to be included in local news items about the brewery, or on social media, and were therefore able to understand the implications of their digital story being identifiable. Participants were advised that they did not need to include personal information (e.g., such as pictures of themselves) in their stories. Different levels of information sharing were also offered as part of the consent process (see Table 1). These options were discussed thoroughly with participants using accessible information, and they were reminded of these when creating their digital story, and once the stories were complete, given options to change their mind. The Mental Capacity Act (2008) guided decisions about whether participants had capacity to consent. Potential participants were taken slowly through the accessible information materials and asked to explain each point in their own words. Breaks were given where needed. Any trainee who struggled to understand, retain, weigh up or communicate key information about the study was thanked for their time and their participation was not pursued.

Participants

A poster advertising the research was displayed in the brewery. Trainees who expressed an interest were visited at work by the second author who discussed participation using accessible documents. At the time of the study there were 12 trainees at the brewery. The research was discussed with eleven trainees, with seven expressing an interest. One of the trainees was found not to have capacity to consent to the research (see ‘ethics’ above). The remaining six had capacity to give informed consent to take part. A digital storytelling workshop was arranged and five participants attended. The sixth participant was unable to attend on the day but still wished to take part, so the second author supported them to make a digital story on a 1:1 basis at the brewery. Procedure

The digital story workshop was conducted in a local venue separate from the brewery. Each participant was supplied with a laptop borrowed from the university and a log in for basic video creation software. Participants were reminded about the purpose of the study and that they could pause or stop their participation at any time. Participants were supported as required by a combination of the researchers, brewery staff and their own support workers to develop a story about their experience at the brewery using the prompts ‘tell us about your work at the brewery’ and ‘how does working at the brewery make you feel?’ and ‘what are the good things and bad things about working at the brewery?’ These stories were transcribed live by a supporter who regularly checked to ensure that they had captured the intended meaning. The process of the workshop is illustrated in Figure 1.

Digital story workshop process.

Participants selected photos and other images throughout the digital storytelling workshop. For some participants, a specific element of their story prompted a search for a corresponding image. For others, a favoured image prompted them to relay a specific element of their story. Two participants used their own drawings, which were scanned in. Participants who could read generally preferred to read their story from a script when recording their voiceover. Participants who were less confident with reading or speaking aloud were reminded about the key elements of their story prior to recording the voiceover, and they then produced a spontaneous version. No correction was offered if these participants deviated from their original story; both versions were used for analysis. Voiceovers were played back to participants to ensure that they were happy with them before proceeding. Support was required for the video editing process, as this proved complicated for several trainees. This was conducted using person‐centred principles to ensure that edits reflected the wishes of participants. Time demands meant that final edits were completed after the workshop, and finalised in 1:1 sessions with participants. Once stories were finalised, participants were given a secure link to view and share it as they wished. Each story voiceover was transcribed anonymously by the second author, and these were saved securely alongside written versions.

Positionality

This study adopted a critical realist perspective (Bhaskar 1975) because this allowed acknowledgement of participants' realities of training and working at the brewery, but at the same time allowed interpretation of the ways in which their experience of work could be shaped by their cultural context and broader social structures. The researchers discussed their own assumptions and position in relation to the topic prior to conducting the analysis. The first author had nearly two decades of experience working as a clinical psychologist in services for people with intellectual disabilities, and lived in the community where the brewery was based. The second author had experience as a support worker, had undertaken undergraduate research with people with intellectual disabilities, and was also a community resident. Both agreed that their experiences had led them to believe that opportunities for valued and meaningful activity were important for the well‐being of people with intellectual disabilities, and that as local residents, the brewery was popular in the community. The potential for these beliefs to shape the research was actively discussed during analysis, and journaling was used to reflect on the coding process in relation to researchers' beliefs.

Analysis

There are numerous ways to analyse data from digital stories (Fish and Syed 2021). Reflexive thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke 2022) was chosen for its ability to be flexible to a range of data sources (i.e., including visual data in addition to transcripts), and because it is considered to fit within a critical realist epistemology. Transcripts included both the written version of the story and the voiceover as there were some differences between these for some participants (see ‘Procedure’). This ensured that all information given was captured. Digital stories were viewed several times during analysis to capture meaning of the chosen images alongside the voiceover.

Story transcripts and images were analysed in six stages (Braun and Clarke 2022): (i) familiarisation with the data set; (ii) coding; (iii) generating initial themes; (iv) developing and reviewing themes; (v) refining, defining and naming themes, (vi) writing up. Stages (i‐iii) were conducted by the first and third authors and facilitated by memos, reflective journaling and discussion. Coding was inductive (i.e., data rather than theory driven) and included both semantic (i.e., surface) and latent (i.e., implicit) meanings in the data. From stage iv, the analysis was developed in consultation with participants and non‐disabled brewery staff (including the third and fourth authors), in a ‘back and forth’ process with the data set to ensure that the themes captured the essence of the data and made sense to participants. A draft thematic map was presented and discussed with participants, alongside visuals to illustrate themes labels where possible. This led to further refinements (e.g., changing theme labels to ensure accessibility, clarifying subthemes). The overarching theme was developed at this stage, as it was repeatedly mentioned by stakeholders when discussing the data. Participants were invited to appear in a short video to disseminate the findings. Four participants took part, all of whom spontaneously spoke about the themes when discussing their brewery work (Burt 2025).

The ‘special interests’ of people with intellectual disabilities and/or autism can be idiosyncratic and not always valued by wider society. This data suggests that work can create an opportunity for people to develop specialist knowledge that has value for the wider community. This led to a discussion amongst the researchers about how all of us had interests that we were able to channel via our employment which afforded us social status as ‘experts’, and were not given the arguably more patronising term ‘special interests’. We reflected on the importance of work allowing people with intellectual disabilities the opportunities to become valued experts.

General Discussion

This study was informed by a CBPR framework, and explored the experiences of a small sample of people with intellectual disabilities (and autism) working in a social enterprise brewery. The findings support existing literature on the positive benefits of employment for this group (e.g., Jahoda et al. 2008, 2009; Robertson et al. 2019). Participants created stories about the skills they had learned, feeling proud of the work and as though they belonged at the brewery. Linking these themes was a consistent story of how working at the brewery had made them feel more confident.

The findings also link to those of Akkerman et al. (2018a) in relation to self‐determination theory (Ryan and Deci 2000), specifically the importance of feeling competent and autonomous. Garrels and Arvidsson (2019) reported that self‐determination can be lower in people with intellectual disabilities, and suggested that it can be enhanced when learning is ‘scaffolded’ to match the person's needs. This could be linked to the brewery model, which uniquely emphasised development from volunteering to paid work, appearing to shift flexibly between elements of sheltered, supportive, customised and competitive employment identified by Almalky (2020), within the benefits of a social enterprise as identified by Meltzer et al. (2018). This also supports Joyce et al. (2024) who found that diversity of roles, customised training and task matching were important in supporting the well‐being of people with intellectual disabilities at work. Although causal links are not possible from this study, it could be hypothesised that the brewery's developmental, flexible and person‐centred approach acted to ‘scaffold’ trainees towards greater self‐determination. This would need to be explored in more detail in future studies, utilising specific measures (e.g., as proposed by Verdugo et al. 2023) and a longitudinal design. With Robert's comments about not being paid in mind, it may also be helpful to understand how different skill levels between trainees are best managed within this model.

The sample in this study was predominantly male. Females are in the minority at the brewery and only one participated in the study. There is a gender gap in employment for people with intellectual disabilities, with females much less likely to be employed (Nuffield Trust 2023). The reasons for this are not clear but could reflect additional disadvantage for women with intellectual disabilities. Brewing can tend to be a stereotypically ‘male’ pursuit, so this specific workplace may have unintentionally limited female employment. More generally, the relative disadvantage of women with intellectual disabilities in accessing employment should be a focus of future research efforts with a broader population.

The study is limited in terms of size and breadth, but this needs to be offset with the advantages of partnering with a community and researching the topics that matter to them, which can strengthen local impact. It was possible to apply a CBPR framework and engage people with intellectual disabilities in the research process with accessible communication materials and repeated visits to build relationships and understanding. Participants have since visited the University to talk to students about employment, something that may not have been possible prior to this project. This suggests that co‐produced research is feasible with this group, and that the benefits can extend beyond the life of the project.

Digital storytelling proved to be flexible and accessible as a data collection method, allowing participants to combine their own words with chosen images and to create a final video that they could keep, and that the brewery was able to use (with permission). Close attention to person‐centred practise was vital to ensure that the final video edits conformed to the participants' wishes. The researchers would advocate for the use of this method in future research, but with some caution as considerable support was required for participants to operate the software. Researchers would therefore be well advised to consult with participants as to their existing skills to build in sufficient support, adaptations and time in their research plan.

The brewery in this study affords opportunities for the local community to engage with people with intellectual disabilities. The contact hypothesis (Allport 1954) suggests that exposure is key to reducing stigma, which can be high towards people with intellectual disabilities and autism (Pelleboer‐Gunnink et al. 2019). Meltzer et al. (2018) identified community connection as a benefit of social enterprise employment for people with intellectual disabilities, but this potential was not fully explored in this study. Future studies on community attitudes towards employing people with intellectual disabilities could enable the brewery to evidence their broader social impact.

To conclude, this small, community‐based study used a novel participatory method to find that people with intellectual disabilities could identify immediate and broader benefits of their training and employment within a social enterprise brewery. Combining different models of employment support within one setting and moving flexibly between them appears to have potential in terms of developing people's skills and confidence. We also demonstrated the potential of the CBPR framework and specific digital storytelling method for this group. Further research would be needed to better understand the impact of this flexible employment model on self‐determination and its broader community impact.
