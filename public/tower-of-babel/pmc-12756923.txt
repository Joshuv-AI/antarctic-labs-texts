Real-time ultrasound-guided subclavian vein cannulation: should it be the preferred method for central venous catheterization in critically ill?
Authors: Xinyue Zhang, Ruyuan Zhang
Published: 2025

We read with great interest the post hoc target trial emulation by Boulet, et al. [1], which utilized 3SITES data to compare the incidence of catheter-related infectious, thrombotic, and mechanical complications among internal jugular, femoral, and subclavian sites, assuming universal real-time ultrasound guidance. They showed that in a total of 989 catheters in the subclavian site, only 50 (5.1%) catheterizations were real-time ultrasound-guided. This means that in their original 3SITES trial [2], most of subclavian vein catheterizations were landmark-based, which was associated with a higher risk of immediate mechanical complications. In contrast, real-time ultrasound can overcome the drawbacks of the landmark-based subclavian vein access technique and improve its risk-benefit profile. As their current target trial emulation suggests, the incidence of major mechanical complications was markedly lower than that observed in the original trial. In an early study from Fragou et al. [3], real-time ultrasound-guided subclavian catheterization (n = 200) achieved a 100% success rate and decreased artery puncture rate to 0.5%, with no pneumothorax and hemothorax, in contrast with landmark-based method (n = 201) of 87.5% success rate and three complications rate of about 5%, respectively. Another recent randomized controlled trial (n = 449) demonstrated a 99.1% success rate with a 1.1% rate of artery puncture and no cases of pneumothorax [4].

Furthermore, leveraging local anatomical landmarks may improve the safety and efficacy of subclavian vein puncture. To address the limitation of the conventional long axis in-plane technique — which typically visualizes only a single vessel and places the pleura in view beneath the vein — we recently proposed the SAVES method (Seeing Artery and VEin Simultaneously in the long axis) for ultrasound-guided infraclavicular axillary/subclavian vein cannulation. Using SAVES, we achieved a 100% success rate (n = 142) without any cases of pneumothorax, hemothorax, or arterial puncture [5]. However, we acknowledge that this small, non-controlled retrospective study requires confirmation by a larger, controlled prospective study. As the number of punctures increases, the SAVES technique might be impossible to achieve a 100% success rate and without major mechanical complications. We agree with others that we should dismiss the misconception that “serious complications no longer arise when ultrasound is used” [6].

In their present article, it is unknown whether the long-axis or short-axis approach is used. While the long-axis view provides better visualization of the needle tip compared to the short-axis view, it has a longer learning curve. Nevertheless, the current study adds to growing evidence that real-time ultrasound guidance for subclavian vein catheterization is safer, finding it results in fewer catheter-related infections than jugular access and less asymptomatic thrombosis than femoral or jugular approaches. Then the question arises: Should real-time ultrasound-guided subclavian vein cannulation now be the preferred method for central venous catheterization in critically ill patients? The answer may still depend on a comprehensive evaluation of the indication for central venous catheter placement (e.g., hemodialysis), patient condition (e.g., chronic renal failure), vessel feature, operator proficiency, and resource availability (e.g., ultrasound).
