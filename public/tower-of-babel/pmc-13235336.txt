Voluntary wheel running in male mice mitigates select hyperglycemia-induced depressive-like behaviors and modulates BDNF and Tnf in a brain region-specific manner
Authors: Laura E Kusumo, Grace A Reese, Matthew J Folh, Jonathan P Duhon, Reece L Bonner, Grace M Read, Caroline G Fowler, Elisabeth G Vichaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetes is associated with an increased risk for depression. Emerging data suggests this effect is partly mediated by hyperglycemia-associated neuroinflammation and decreased BDNF. While exercise reduces depressive symptoms in other models of depression, limited research has investigated the efficacy of voluntary exercise for hyperglycemia-induced depression. Thus, we examined whether chronic voluntary exercise could rescue hyperglycemia-related affective dysfunction, reduce neuroinflammation, and increase BDNF levels in male C57BL/6J mice. Hyperglycemia was induced via administration of 50 mg/kg/day streptozotocin (STZ) over 5 consecutive days. Four weeks post-STZ, mice in the exercise group were provided running wheels in their home cage for approximately 7 weeks. As anticipated, STZ induced depressive-like behavior. Exercise attenuated behavioral deficits in the forced swim and splash test and increased BDNF expression within the brain. However, other depressive-like behaviors (i.e., open field activity, nest building, marble burying, and fear conditioning) and cerebellar Tnf expression failed to respond to exercise. This study provides novel evidence that a subset of hyperglycemia-induced depressive-like behaviors are responsive to voluntary exercise while others remain exercise-resistant. This emphasizes the potential benefit of exercise as a part of a multimodal treatment strategy for hyperglycemia-related depression.Keywords: Diabetes, Exercise, Streptozotocin, Neuroinflammation, Mice

Introduction

Depression is one of the most common and disabling mental health disorders in the world as it affects roughly 300 million adults and negatively affects personal and work life (World Health Organization, 2025a). A growing body of literature indicates that chronic inflammatory conditions are associated with the increasing prevalence of depression, although the mechanisms are still not fully understood. One of the most common chronic inflammatory diseases is diabetes mellitus (DM), a group of metabolic disorders, affecting more than 830 million people worldwide (World Health Organization, 2025b). Individuals with DM are two to three times more likely to experience depression than the general population (Ali et al., 2006; Gendelman et al., 2009). While the mechanisms underlying the increased prevalence and severity of depression among individuals with DM are not fully understood, inflammation is a likely contributor. A significant portion of depressed patients and a number of murine models of depression show elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) (Dantzer et al., 2008; Gilak-Dalasm et al., 2021; Osimo et al., 2019; Vogelzangs et al., 2012).

It has been established that exercise can alleviate depressive symptoms in clinical populations (Liu et al., 2025; Tian et al., 2025). One mechanism by which exercise may impact depression is through exerkines, signaling molecules released in response to exercise. Muscle-induced exerkines, including irisin and BDNF, can affect the brain; further, exercise can cause the brain itself to increase expression of exerkines, such as BDNF (Dadkhah et al., 2023; Garza et al., 2004). Research has shown that BDNF–a regulator of neurogenesis, synaptic plasticity, and neuroinflammation (Miranda et al., 2019; Wrann et al., 2013; Xu et al., 2017)–is reduced in serum/plasma (Karege et al., 2002; Kim et al., 2007) and brain tissue (Lee and Kim, 2010) of individuals with clinical depression. Rodent models of depression also report decreased BDNF levels within the brain (Qiao et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2016). While the effects of exercise on depression have been mainly investigated in the hippocampus and frontal cortex, emerging literature has explored the relationship between the cerebellum and the limbic system (Jung et al., 2022; Terburg et al., 2024). Given the cerebellum's role in motor functioning, it has also been shown to express high levels of BDNF and respond to exercise (Rauf et al., 2020; Taheri et al., 2020). Thus, we investigated the alterations in mouse frontal cortex, hippocampus, and cerebellum in response to chronic hyperglycemia and voluntary wheel running.

While exercise has been shown to benefit diabetic patients through increasing insulin sensitivity and reducing inflammation (Borghouts and Keizer, 2000; Narita et al., 2019), there is limited evidence exploring the direct relationship between exercise and affective dysfunction in mice that remain diabetic. Therefore, within this current study, we sought to examine the efficacy of exercise for depressive-like behavior in the streptozotocin (STZ) model of hyperglycemia, as it is relatively resistant to exercise-induced modulation of blood glucose levels (O'Neill et al., 2022) and produces a robust depressive phenotype (Baek et al., 2022; Kusumo et al., 2024; McCready et al., 2023; Sakurai et al., 2021). In addition to evaluating behavioral alterations, we also examined the impact of STZ and exercise on inflammatory markers and exerkines in the hippocampus, cerebellum, frontal cortex, and soleus muscle.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice from JAX® laboratories (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) were group-housed (4 mice/cage) on a 12 h light/dark cycle with food and water ad libitum. They were habituated to Baylor University's vivarium, transportation, and handling prior to experimental manipulations. STZ induction started when mice were 8 weeks old, designated experimental week 0. Pre-exercise (baseline) behavioral tests were run between experimental weeks 3-4. After completion of pre-exercise behavior, mice were single housed and provided low-profile wheels or a plastic hut, and post-exercise behavioral tests were run between experimental weeks 10-11. Behavioral testing was conducted during the light cycle phase (0700-1900 h). Experimental protocols were approved by the Baylor University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC; protocol #: 1550457).

Experimental design

We used a 2 (STZ vs. vehicle [VEH]) by 2 (exercise [EX] vs. sedentary [SED]) factorial design (n = 5-7 mice/group) (Fig. 1). Body weights and 2-h fasted blood glucose (BG) levels were monitored regularly. Four weeks post-STZ induction, mice were single-housed and provided continuous access to either a wireless low-profile running wheel (Med Associates, Fairfax, VT) (EX) or were single housed with a red or orange plastic hut for enrichment (SED) until study end (7 weeks) (Guo et al., 2020). Groups were determined by counterbalancing based on body weight and blood glucose. Wheel running was monitored using Wheel Manager software (Med Associates, Fairfax, VT). Running data during the wheel training period prior to STZ induction (before experimental week 0), two nights following cage changes during experimental weeks 6 and 8, and during behavioral testing (experimental weeks 10-11) was excluded due to high variability. We collected behavioral data prior to the introduction of running wheels (i.e., open field [OF], marble burying [MB], and nest building [NB]) to verify STZ-induced affective dysfunction prior to the start of exercise. A second behavioral set was conducted after 6 weeks of exercise (i.e., OF, MB, NB, splash test [ST], forced swim test [FST], and contextual fear conditioning test [FCT]) to evaluate the efficacy of voluntary wheel running. Tissue was collected at the end of experimental week 11 following the completion of behavior.

Experimental Timeline

Male C57BL/6J mice were handled 1 week prior to experimental start. At experimental week 0, mice were injected intraperitoneally with 50 mg/kg STZ for 5 consecutive days or equal volume of citrate buffer. From experimental week 3 to 4, repeatable behavioral tests were conducted to observe behavioral deficits. Once behavioral deficits were well-established, mice were single-housed and running wheels (EX) or a plastic hut (SED) were introduced in their home cage. Post-exercise behavioral tests were conducted starting at experimental week 10 with tissue collection occurring at the end of experimental week 11.

Hyperglycemia induction and monitoring

Hyperglycemia was induced by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of 50 mg/kg/day STZ for 5 consecutive days in citrate buffer (pH 4.5) vehicle (Furman, 2021). Body weights and BG levels were monitored consistently throughout the study to assess STZ-induced hyperglycemia. BG levels were taken at baseline prior to STZ induction, at 2 weeks post-STZ (14 days after the final STZ injection), and then repeatedly throughout the duration of the study. A 2 h fasting BG was taken using a glucometer (AUVON) and a blood sample from a tail vein nick. Mice with a BG level more than 250 mg/dL were considered diabetic. If an STZ treated mouse had a BG level less than 250 mg/dL at three or more time points and failed to display the anticipated weight loss in the first few weeks following STZ treatment, it was considered non-diabetic and excluded from the study. Based on this criterion, one STZ mouse was excluded from all analyses.

Behavior tests

Open Field (OF)

The open field (OF) task is used to measure locomotor activity (Nicolas et al., 2006). All mice were habituated to the testing environment for 30 min prior to the start of testing. Mice were placed in a plexiglass arena (30 cm × 30 cm x 40 cm) for 5 min. Total distance traveled, speed of the mouse when moving (total distance [cm]/total move time [sec]), and total time spent in the center arena were quantified by Noldus EthoVisionXT software Version 16 (Leesburg, VA) from overhead videos.

Splash Test (ST)

The ST, a measure of apathetic behavior (Cardinal et al., 2021; McCready et al., 2023; Pitzer et al., 2022), immediately followed OF. Mice were sprayed twice in quick succession on their dorsal side with a 10% sucrose solution. Behavior was recorded by an overhead camera for 5 min. Two experimenters blind to group assignment scored the videos for latency to groom and cumulative time grooming.

Marble Burying (MB)

The MB task is typically used to measure repetitive and/or anxiety-like behaviors. However, STZ-induced behavior has been associated with decreased marble burying (Gazzo et al., 2021; Kusumo et al., 2024; McCready et al., 2023; Zappa Villar et al., 2020). While not fully understood, this behavioral phenotype may be due to reduced exploratory interest or motivation. Mice were placed in a shoebox cage (30 cm × 19 cm x 13 cm) with fresh bedding for 30 min with marbles arranged into 4 columns and 5 rows (20 marbles total). At task end, a photo was taken and two experimenters blind to group assignment scored the number of marbles that were at least 2/3 buried.

Nest Building (NB)

NB is an easy-to-quantify daily living task to screen for changes in health and overall wellbeing (Deacon, 2006; Jirkof, 2014). Immediately following marble burying, mice were moved to a new shoebox cage (30 cm × 19 cm x 13 cm) with a nestlet square for 5 h. Two experimenters independently scored the quality of the nest based on an established protocol (Deacon, 2006). A score of 1 indicates the nestlet was untouched while a score of 5 indicates a near perfect nest.

Forced Swim Test (FST)

FST, developed to screen antidepressant efficacy of drugs (Porsolt et al., 1978), was run by placing mice in a clear cylindrical container with ∼3500 mL of water (24 °C ± 1 °C) and recorded for 6 min as previously described (Kusumo et al., 2024; McCready et al., 2023). Two experimenters blind to group assignment recorded the total time immobile during the last 5 min.

Fear Conditioning Test (FCT)

Fear conditioning is a test of associative learning (Fanselow and Poulos, 2005; Kim and Jung, 2006) to analyze emotional responses through freezing behavior (LeDoux, 2000). Previous studies have found that STZ increases freezing time (Gilley-Connor et al., 2026; Ikeda et al., 2015, 2021). On the training day, mice were placed in a chamber (16.5 × 18 mm) with shock grid floors (Coulbourn Instruments, LLC). Mice were able to explore the novel arena for 90 s before exposure to a 30 s tone followed by a mild foot shock (0.5 mA, 2 s duration). After the conclusion of the tone and foot shock, there is a 60 s interval before the next tone and shock pairing for a total of 3 tone/shock exposures (300 s total duration). Twenty-four hours later, on the testing day, mice were returned to the same chamber, and they were allowed to explore the arena for a total of 180 s with no tone or shock presentation. Trials were recorded and freezing behavior was quantified using Freezeframe software (Actimetrix).

Tissue collection

After behavioral testing, mice were euthanized by CO2, counterbalanced by group, and blood and tissue were rapidly collected. Blood was collected into EDTA-coated syringes followed by transcardial perfusion with phosphate buffered saline (PBS). The hippocampus, frontal cortex, cerebellum, and soleus muscle were dissected, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until tissue analyses.

Tissue analyses

Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR)

Gene expression changes related to exerkines (i.e., Bdnf, Ntrk2, and Fndc5) and inflammation (i.e., Il6, Il1b, Tnf) relevant to diabetes-associated depression were investigated in the hippocampus, cerebellum, prefrontal cortex, and soleus muscle. Inflammatory cytokines Il6, Il1b, and Tnf are commonly evaluated markers of neuroinflammation in response to diabetes-associated depressive-like behavior. We selected Bdnf as there is evidence from our lab and others that STZ modulates its expression (Gao et al., 2024; McCready et al., 2023; Patel et al., 2026); it is also a well-established exerkine and, hence, a strong candidate for mediating potential neuroprotective effects of exercise (Miranda et al., 2019; Stranahan et al., 2009; Van Praag et al., 1999). The Ntrk2 gene encodes the TrkB receptor, the receptor for mature BDNF. Finally, Fndc5, the precursor to irisin, is a well-established exerkine (Boström et al., 2012; Islam et al., 2021) and there is evidence that it can be neuroprotective and upregulate BDNF (Belviranlı and Okudan, 2018; Leger et al., 2024; Wrann et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2022). RNA was extracted with E.Z.N.A. RNA Isolation Kit II (Omega Bio-tek, Inc., Norcross, GA). cDNA was transcribed with the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applicated Biosystems, Grand Island, NY). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was conducted using TaqMan gene expression assays from Integrative DNA Technology (Coralville, IA) (Table 1). Reactions were performed in duplicate and relative gene expressions were calculated using the average of Gapdh and Rplp0 as housekeeping genes and the 2−DDCT method.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) were used to assess hippocampal total BDNF expression (Quantikine™ Total BDNF Immunoassay; R&D Systems; CAT #: DBNT00) as well as proBDNF and mBDNF levels in the cerebellum (Mature BDNF/proBDNF Combo, Human, Mouse, Rat, Rapid™ ELISA Assay; Biosensis; CAT #: BEK-2211/2217). Tissue was homogenized in a 2 mL Dounce homogenizer (DWK Life Sciences Kimble™ Kontes™) with 20 μL buffer per 10 mg tissue lysis buffer. The sample was centrifuged, and 2 μL of supernatant were used to evaluate BDNF levels per the manufacturer's instructions.

Statistical analyses

We used independent samples T-tests and analyses of variance (ANOVAs). Mixed effect ANOVAs with time as a within-subject variable and with treatment and exercise status as between subject factors were conducted for weight, blood glucose levels, and running. Independent samples t-tests were run to evaluate pre-exercise behavioral effects. Two-way ANOVAs were conducted to analyze endpoint post-exercise behavioral tasks and tissue analyses. Post hoc analyses were conducted to facilitate interpretation of interactions. All analyses were run using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29. Data is shown as mean±standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical outliers, defined as more than 3 standard deviations from the mean, were excluded.

Results

STZ affected body weight and blood glucose levels but did not alter wheel running

As anticipated, there was a time by STZ interaction on body weight (F (11, 242) = 39.977, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.645) (Fig. 2A) such that VEH treated mice gained weight throughout the study, while STZ mice failed to gain weight. This divergence occurred within the first experimental week. We also observed a time by EX interaction (F (11, 242) = 4.140, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.158) and a time by STZ by EX interaction (F (11, 242) = 3.721, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.145), such that non-diabetic mice that ran experienced mild weight loss after the start of running; however, this effect was not observed in STZ mice with exercise.

STZ, streptozotocin.

Fasting (2 h) blood glucose levels were taken at baseline and periodically throughout the study. There was a significant time by STZ (F (8, 176) = 27.051, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.551), time by EX (F (8, 176) = 2.326, p = 0.021, ηp2 = 0.096), and an STZ by EX interaction (F (1, 22) = 12.602, p = 0.002, ηp2 = 0.364) (Fig. 2B). These interactions indicate that STZ mice exhibited higher blood glucose levels compared to the VEH mice and that exercise onset corresponded to a reduction in blood glucose levels within the STZ mice, but not within the control mice. Post hoc analyses show that STZ/EX group had lower blood glucose levels compared to the STZ/SED group (p = 0.003) by experimental week 5. Despite the reduction in glucose levels, mice in the STZ/EX group remained above the diabetic threshold (>250 mg/dL) for the duration of the study.

Nightly wheel running was evaluated to determine if STZ mice were able to engage with the exercise intervention and if STZ-treated mice show reduced voluntary activity (i.e., fatigue-like behavior). There were no statistical differences observed in running between STZ or VEH mice (p = 0.778) (Fig. 2C), indicating that STZ mice still maintained normal running behavior and did not display evidence of fatigue. Therefore, we were confident that experimental mice continued to participate in the intervention throughout the study.

STZ induced behavioral deficits prior to exercise exposure

Prior to exercise intervention, STZ reduced the total distance traveled (t (24) = 2.135, p = 0.043, d = 0.840) (Fig. 3A) in the open field such that STZ mice traveled less compared to non-diabetic mice. As more anxious mice tend to stay toward the edges of an open arena, we evaluated time spent in the center as a proxy for anxiety-like behavior. We also calculated the speed of the mice in the open field arena. We did not observe differences in center duration (p = 0.863) (Fig. 3B) or speed (p = 0.367) (Fig. 3C).

In marble burying, there was an effect of STZ (t (24) = 5.012), p < 0.001, d = 1.855) (Fig. 3D) such that STZ mice buried fewer marbles. Further, mice scored poorer on nest building quality (t (24) = 4.245, p < 0.001, d = 1.564) (Fig. 3E), indicative of behavioral alterations in response to hyperglycemia. Based off the behavioral results prior to wheel running, we observed depressive-like behavior in response to STZ, and we introduced wheels at the end of experimental week 4.

Voluntary wheel running mitigated select STZ-induced behavioral deficits in the ST and FST

To evaluate effects of chronic voluntary wheel running on hyperglycemia-induced behavioral alterations, all mice underwent a secondary set of behavioral tasks. At experimental week 10, we re-assessed locomotor activity. In the open field arena, STZ-treated mice maintained reduced distance traveled (F (1, 22) = 8.651, p = 0.008, ηp2 = 0.282) (Fig. 4A) and displayed less center time (F (1, 21) = 9.963, p = 0.005, ηp2 = 0.322) (Fig. 4B). There was no effect of EX on these parameters. There was no effect of STZ (p = 0.996) or EX (p = 0.070) for speed (Fig. 4C).

Immediately following the final OFT, mice were subjected to the splash test (ST) in the same arena which is a common measure for apathetic-like behavior associated with affective dysfunction in murine models. There was a significant main effect of STZ (F (1, 21) = 5.157, p = 0.034, ηp2 = 0.197) with hyperglycemic mice showing a deficit in total grooming time which is indicative of depressive behavior. Interestingly, we also observed the therapeutic effect of EX (F (1, 21) = 8.512, p = 0.008, ηp2 = 0.288) on grooming behavior in response to the sucrose solution (Fig. 4D). Taken together, the ST showed that STZ reduced grooming behavior while EX was able to increase it.

Hyperglycemic mice continued to participate less in marble burying (F (1, 22) = 34.229, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.609) (Fig. 4E) and nest building (F (1, 22) = 49.475, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.693) (Fig. 4F) as reflected in experimental week 3 with no modulation by exercise.

In the forced swim test (FST), increased immobility time is often framed as learned helplessness or behavioral despair. It has been shown that immobility time can be reduced by antidepressants (Kryst et al., 2022). We observed a significant STZ by EX interaction (F (1, 22) = 8.007, p = 0.010, ηp2 = 0.267) (Fig. 4G) where wheel running decreased the total time immobile in STZ mice, rescuing the behavioral deficit observed in STZ mice that did not exercise.

Contextual fear conditioning was conducted to further assess memory for aversive experiences. There was a main effect of STZ in the FCT (F (1, 21) = 6.873, p = 0.016, ηp2 = 0.247) (Fig. 4H) such that STZ mice tended to freeze more when placed in the same environment that they experienced the shocks from the previous day, indicative of a stronger memory of the aversive experience. There was no modulation by exercise.

STZ and exercise modulated exerkines and inflammation differentially in the hippocampus and cerebellum

Potential STZ- and exercise-induced changes in gene expressions were evaluated within the hippocampus (Fig. 5A), cerebellum (Fig. 5B), and frontal cortex (Fig. S1.). As no significant changes were observed within the frontal cortex, our analyses focus on the hippocampus and cerebellum. Both STZ and exercise differentially impacted inflammatory markers in these regions. Upregulated Tnf levels were observed in response to exercise in the hippocampus (F (1, 22) = 12.090, p = 0.002, ηp2 = 0.355). Contrastingly, STZ increased Tnf (F (1, 21) = 6.590, p = 0.018, ηp2 = 0.239) expression in the cerebellum with no exercise effect.

Distinct patterns of exerkine gene expression changes were observed as well. Our analyses found that STZ downregulated hippocampal Bdnf (F (1, 22) = 4.384, p = 0.048, ηp2 = 0.166) while increasing the gene expression Ntrk2, which codes for the TrkB receptor (F (1, 22) = 6.564, p = 0.018, ηp2 = 0.230). Interestingly, STZ increased Bdnf expression in the cerebellum (F (1, 22) = 6.275, p = 0.020, ηp2 = 0.222) while exercise increased Bdnf in the hippocampus (F (1, 22) = 5.422, p = 0.029, ηp2 = 0.198) despite the STZ-induced downregulation. Exercise-induced hippocampal BDNF was confirmed by ELISA (F (1, 20) = 4.644, p = 0.044, ηp2 = 0.188) (Fig. 6A). There were no effects of Il6 or Fndc5 on the hippocampus or cerebellum.

Furthermore, we analyzed gene expression changes within the soleus muscle. We measured Fndc5, the precursor to irisin, and neuroinflammatory markers Il6 and Tnf, in the soleus (Lavi et al., 2022; Wrann et al., 2013) (Fig. 5C). Interestingly, STZ reduced Fndc5 (F (1, 20) = 6.096, p = 0.023, ηp2 = 0.243) (Fig. 5C), and there was no effect of exercise (p = 0.885). For Il6, there was a trend for an increase in response to exercise (p = 0.074), but it was not significant and with no observed STZ effect (p = 0.880). There was no STZ (p = 0.534) or exercise effect for Tnf (p = 0.394).

While we expected an exercise-related increase in hippocampal BDNF, increased levels of STZ-induced Bdnf in the cerebellum were not anticipated. Thus, to further investigate BDNF, we measured proBDNF and mature BDNF levels in cerebellar tissue by ELISA. There was a significant main effect of exercise (F (1, 16) = 15.206, p = 0.001, ηp2 = 0.487) such that exercise increased proBDNF levels (Fig. 6B). There was no STZ effect observed in proBDNF levels (p = 0.465). There was no STZ (p = 0.297) or EX (p = 0.629) effect observed in mature BDNF levels (Fig. 6C). For cerebellar total BDNF, there was a trend for exercise (p = 0.067) with no STZ effect (Fig. 6D).

Discussion

Chronic exercise has been shown to protect against and reduce behavioral and biological changes associated with mild to moderate depression. While exercise has been shown to mitigate brain and behavioral outcomes by enhancing glycemic control, it is less clear whether it can affect hyperglycemia-associated depression independently from disease modification. Therefore, we investigated whether voluntary wheel running could mitigate STZ-induced affective dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and exerkine production. Our findings confirm that STZ produced affective dysfunction in an array of behavioral tests and exercise attenuated a subset of behavioral outcomes (i.e., the splash and forced swim test). Furthermore, the impact of hyperglycemia on the brain showed region specificity, and BDNF increased in response to exercise. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate an attenuation of depressive-like behavior in STZ-induced hyperglycemic mice subject to voluntary exercise that maintained diabetic levels of blood glucose.

As previously reported, STZ induced hyperglycemia and depressive-like behaviors (Bijani et al., 2022; Delanogare et al., 2023; Gazzo et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2016). We selected this model as it has been shown to be resistant to the effect of exercise on blood glucose levels (O'Neill et al., 2022). While we observed a statistically significant effect of running on STZ-induced hyperglycemia, mice remained well within the diabetic range throughout the study. Behavioral deficits were observed within the open field, nest building, and marble burying tasks in response to STZ. These deficits were observed prior to the start of exercise and were not reversed by the intervention. We had considered that this set of deficits might represent fatigue, a neurovegetative symptom of depression; however, the lack of difference in nightly wheel running—a sensitive measure of fatigue-like behavior (Vichaya et al., 2023; Wolff et al., 2018)—suggests another factor may drive these behavioral deficits. There is evidence that links inflammation to motivational deficits in depressed individuals (Burrows et al., 2021; Treadway et al., 2025). The lack of participation in these movement-based tasks, while maintaining relatively normal running behavior, may reflect a motivational deficit. Further research examining the effects of STZ on motivated behavior and brain regions governing motived behavior is needed. Another STZ-induced behavior that was not reversed by exercise was enhanced fear conditioning, a measure of negative valence symptoms (Gilley-Connor et al., 2026).

Seven weeks of voluntary exercise was able to attenuate STZ-induced deficits in the ST and FST. This suggests that exercise may have greater efficacy for a subset of depressive-like symptoms. Depression is multimodal, with multiple symptom domains and affecting a variety of brain regions. Our behavioral observations align with this framework. The National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) developed the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) framework (National Institute of Mental Health, 2025) to conceptualize psychiatric disorders, including depression, as being linked to distinct neurobiological changes (Gjengedal et al., 2022; Insel et al., 2010). Prior clinical research also indicates that exercise may have domain-specific efficacy. Specifically, exercise has been reported to improve arousal fatigue (Nasstasia et al., 2019; Puetz et al., 2006), cognitive functioning (Olson et al., 2017; Quaney et al., 2009), and positive valence (e.g., anhedonia, energy, and motivation) (Toups et al., 2017). While future research is needed to clarify the research domains that are most responsive to exercise, future preclinical studies may be able to help pair these improvements to particular neural circuits. Additionally, it will be important to understand if these improvements are specific to hyperglycemia-induced depressive-like symptoms or if similar symptoms of depression are improved by exercise in stress-induced models, such as unpredictable chronic mild stress (Liu et al., 2013; Luo et al., 2020).

In the brain, we explored the effects of chronic exercise on gene and protein expression related to inflammation and exerkines within the hippocampus, cerebellum, and frontal cortex. Exercise had the greatest effect on the hippocampus, increasing the mRNA expression of Bdnf and Tnf. Our findings align with previous research showing that exercise upregulates hippocampal BDNF levels (Alomari et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2021; Liu and Nusslock, 2018; Wrann et al., 2013; Yau et al., 2018). While acute bouts of exercise can induce inflammation, regular exercise is generally understood to help maintain anti-inflammatory activity (Docherty et al., 2022; Luo et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023). Interestingly, similar to our observation, a chronic exercise protocol in male C57BL/6 mice on high fat diet increased hippocampal Tnf mRNA expression, while acute bouts (60 min) of forced treadmill running led to its downregulation (Fuller et al., 2024). This emphasizes the variability in murine models used to study diabetes or depression, as well as the influence of different exercise protocols. A potential caveat of our findings is that the observed hippocampal inflammatory effect may be due to temporary responses to an acute exercise session within the chronic exercise protocol, possibly related to the body's response to muscle adaptation and repair (Moldoveanu et al., 2001; Peake et al., 2017).

As exercise is expected to impact motor regions, such as the cerebellum, and given the emerging evidence that the cerebellum may have a role in regulating emotion (Zhang et al., 2024), we were interested in how STZ and wheel running may modulate expression of inflammatory and exerkine markers within the cerebellum. Interestingly, it showed greater susceptibility to alterations in gene expression related to STZ than the hippocampus and frontal cortex, with upregulations of Bdnf and Tnf. The Bdnf finding in the cerebellum is counter-intuitive, however, we have previously observed an STZ-induced increase in Bdnf in the hippocampus (McCready et al., 2023). This effect may be a compensatory mechanism. These effects of STZ were not significantly impacted by exercise. The finding that exercise attenuated particular depressive-like behaviors without preventing STZ-induced Tnf upregulation suggests that the effects of voluntary wheel running may not be through modulation of neuroinflammation.

To provide insight into the form of BDNF impacted by STZ and to determine if exercise may impact protein expression in the absence of mRNA changes, we ran ELISAs to look at proBDNF and mature BDNF. proBDNF is a precursor to the mature form. It binds to p75 neurotrophin receptor (p75NTR) and has been associated with cellular apoptosis, long-term depression (Kraemer et al., 2014; Woo et al., 2005), and depressive symptoms (Yang et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2013). Alternatively, mature BDNF has neuroprotective effects through binding to the tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB), activating pathways involved in neuronal growth and long-term potentiation (Miranda et al., 2019; Stranahan et al., 2009; Van Praag et al., 1999; Zhong et al., 2018). Reduced levels of mature BDNF have been shown in depression (Brunoni et al., 2008; Karege et al., 2002; Yoshida et al., 2012). In our analysis, we did not observe a difference in total, mature, or proBDNF in response to STZ. Therefore, further research will be needed to examine the STZ-induced increase in Bdnf expression. However, there was a significant increase in proBDNF in response to exercise. As skeletal muscles are associated with high levels of proBDNF, this effect may be a consequence of exercise-induced muscle-derived BDNF, which may explain the dissociation between protein and gene expression findings.

Unexpectedly, we did not observe any effects of STZ or exercise on gene expression within the frontal cortex. As we have previously demonstrated that STZ increases brain Il1b expression when measured at an earlier timepoint (McCready et al., 2023), our lack of effects in the frontal cortex was not anticipated. This may be a timing effect as different effects of STZ have been reported at different time points after hyperglycemia induction (Ahmed et al., 2019; Jodeiri Farshbaf et al., 2024; McCready et al., 2023). Further studies are necessary to characterize the time-dependent and brain-region specific inflammatory response to STZ-induced hyperglycemia. Additionally, we did not observe a significant effect of exercise on muscle Il6, Tnf, or Fndc5, the protein coding gene for irisin, a prominent exerkine (Boström et al., 2012; Park et al., 2022). This lack of effect may be related to the time of tissue collection as we collected tissue during the light cycle after the mice had completed their nightly running. An effect of STZ on Fndc5 was observed such that STZ mice had lower levels. This effect has been described by other labs (Duan et al., 2016; Jiang et al., 2021) and may indicate that chronic hyperglycemia disrupts muscle function.

It is important to note the few limitations of this study. This experiment was conducted exclusively in male mice. Female mice are more resistant to induction of hyperglycemia, and they may demonstrate a less severe behavioral profile even when hyperglycemia is induced (Kusumo et al., 2024). As depression is more prevalent in females in the clinical population and given that female mice run more than male mice (De Bono et al., 2006; Rosenfeld, 2017), it would be important to include female mice in future studies. Another limitation to the translation of these findings is species-associated interest in running. Rodents are intrinsically motivated to run for long distances (Meijer and Robbers, 2014; Novak et al., 2012), perhaps in support of their foraging needs. As such, mice can consistently run for 4to 20 km every night (De Bono et al., 2006; Konhilas et al., 2015) with this current cohort of mice running about 9 km per night for seven weeks. It is important for future studies to investigate the exercise duration and intensity required to achieve antidepressant effects. This will require the use of forced exercise protocols or limited wheel access.

Depression and diabetes rates continue to rise in the United States, and commonly prescribed antidepressants present challenges for both conditions, including treatment-resistance and some antidepressants elevating blood glucose. This highlights the need for alternative or complementary therapeutic interventions. Given its accessibility to much of the general population, we propose that physical activity may serve as a viable intervention for specific symptoms of diabetes-induced depression. Future research is needed to associate the neurobiological pathways impacted by exercise with specific domains of depressive-like symptoms. The strong hyperglycemia phenotype induced by STZ makes this model a good fit for these investigations.
