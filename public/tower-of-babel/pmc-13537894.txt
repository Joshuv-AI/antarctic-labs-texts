Changes in 24-Hour Ambulatory Blood Pressure After Bariatric and Metabolic Surgery in Patients With Morbid Obesity: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Dong-Wook Kim, Sungsoo Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to evaluate changes in office and ambulatory blood pressure following bariatric and metabolic surgery in patients with morbid obesity.Materials and MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed consecutive patients with morbid obesity who underwent bariatric and metabolic surgery between January 2020 and August 2023 at a single tertiary referral center. Office blood pressure and 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring were performed before surgery and repeated at 6 and 12 months postoperatively. The primary outcome was the change in mean 24-hour systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP).ResultsA total of 25 patients were included. The mean body mass index decreased from 39.2±6.9 kg/m2 preoperatively to 32.2±5.2 kg/m2 at 6 months and 28.8±4.2 kg/m2 at 12 months. Office SBP significantly decreased from 140.3±10.7 mmHg at baseline to 128.0±9.4 mmHg at 12 months (P<0.001), while office DBP decreased from 87.0±6.9 mmHg to 82.2±6.4 mmHg (P<0.001). Likewise, mean 24-hour SBP decreased from 132.7±10.7 to 126.5±8.8 mmHg at 12 months (P=0.030), and mean 24-hour DBP decreased from 85.0±6.7 to 79.5±6.5 mmHg (P=0.040).ConclusionBariatric and metabolic surgery was associated with significant reductions in both office and ambulatory blood pressure during the first postoperative year.Keywords: Bariatric surgery, Ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, Hypertension

INTRODUCTION

Obesity is closely associated with various metabolic disorders, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular disease. Among these, hypertension is one of the most common obesity-related comorbidities and an important risk factor for cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Therefore, appropriate blood pressure control is an essential component of obesity management [1,2].

Bariatric and metabolic surgery (BMS) is the most effective treatment for severe obesity and has been shown to achieve sustained weight loss and significant improvement in obesity-related comorbidities. Previous studies have demonstrated that approximately 40–60% of patients experience remission of hypertension or require fewer antihypertensive medications after surgery [3,4,5,6].

Most previous studies, however, have evaluated blood pressure using office blood pressure measurements. Because office blood pressure can be influenced by the white-coat effect and masked hypertension, it may not accurately reflect a patient's true blood pressure status. In contrast, 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) provides a more objective assessment of blood pressure during routine daily activities and sleep and has been shown to better predict cardiovascular outcomes than office blood pressure [7,8]. Despite these advantages, studies evaluating changes in ABPM after bariatric surgery remain limited.

Therefore, this study aimed to objectively evaluate changes in both office blood pressure and 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure following bariatric and metabolic surgery in patients with morbid obesity.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This retrospective cohort study included consecutive patients with morbid obesity who underwent BMS at Dankook University Hospital between January 2020 and August 2023. Hypertension was defined as the use of antihypertensive medication or a previous diagnosis of hypertension. Patients who underwent revisional bariatric surgery or had incomplete ABPM data during follow-up were excluded.

Patient demographics, comorbidities, surgical outcomes, and early postoperative complications were retrospectively reviewed. Office blood pressure was measured using an automated sphygmomanometer after at least 5 minutes of rest in the seated position, and the average of 2 consecutive measurements was recorded. 24-hour ABPM was performed using a validated non-invasive automated oscillometric device (TM-2430; A&D Medical, Tokyo, Japan) before surgery and repeated at 6 and 12 months after surgery. Blood pressure measurements were obtained every 30 minutes during the daytime (06:00–22:00) and every 60 minutes during the nighttime (22:00–06:00) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Mean 24-hour, daytime, and nighttime systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were recorded for analysis.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Dankook University Hospital (IRB number: 202205010). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study. Continuous variables were analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance. The reported P values represent the overall effect of time across baseline, 6 months, and 12 months. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS software package for Windows, version 31.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

1. Patient demographics and baseline characteristics

A total of 27 consecutive patients were initially enrolled. Two patients were excluded because they were lost to follow-up during the postoperative period. Consequently, the final analysis included 25 patients with complete baseline, 6-month, and 12-month ABPM data. The mean age was 37.5±11.4 years, and 21 patients (84.0%) were female. The mean preoperative body weight and body mass index (BMI) were 104.5±23.2 kg and 39.2±6.9 kg/m2, respectively. Fourteen patients (56.0%) were classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I, while 11 patients (44.0%) had an ASA score of II or higher. Twenty patients (80.0%) were taking one or more antihypertensive medications before surgery, and 7 patients (28.0%) had type 2 diabetes mellitus. 2. Surgical results

Among the 25 patients, 22 (88.0%) underwent laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy and 3 (12.0%) underwent laparoscopic Roux-en-Y gastric bypass. The mean operative time was 126.1±61.8 minutes, with a mean estimated blood loss of 45.7±14.0 mL. The average postoperative hospital stay was 4.3±2.0 days, and patients resumed a semi-fluid diet after 2.2±1.3 days. Postoperative morbidity occurred in 2 patients (8.0%), including one case of respiratory failure and one liver abscess. There were no cases of reoperation or readmission during the study period (Table 2).

3. Changes in office and ambulatory blood pressure following bariatric surgery

Significant improvements in BMI and blood pressure were observed during the first postoperative year. The mean BMI decreased from 39.2±6.9 kg/m2 at baseline to 32.2±5.2 kg/m2 at 6 months and 28.8±4.2 kg/m2 at 12 months after surgery (P<0.001).

Office blood pressure significantly decreased during follow-up. Mean office SBP decreased from 140.3±10.7 mmHg at baseline to 134.2±8.5 mmHg at 6 months and 128.0±9.4 mmHg at 12 months (P<0.001). Similarly, office DBP decreased from 87.0±6.9 mmHg to 83.0±7.2 mmHg and 82.2±6.4 mmHg, respectively (P<0.001).

ABPM also demonstrated significant reductions following bariatric surgery. The mean 24-hour SBP decreased from 132.7±10.7 mmHg at baseline to 128.5±9.2 mmHg at 6 months and 126.5±8.8 mmHg at 12 months (P=0.030), while the mean 24-hour DBP decreased from 85.0±6.7 mmHg to 82.0±6.5 mmHg and 79.5±6.5 mmHg, respectively (P=0.040). DISCUSSION

The present study demonstrated that BMS significantly reduced both office and ambulatory blood pressure during the first postoperative year in patients with morbid obesity. In addition to substantial weight loss, significant reductions were observed in office SBP and DBP as well as in 24-hour ambulatory SBP and DBP. These findings suggest that the antihypertensive effects of BMS are consistently observed not only in the outpatient setting but also during patients’ daily activities.

An interesting finding of this study was that the mean preoperative 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure was relatively well controlled (132.7/85.0 mmHg), despite most patients having a diagnosis of hypertension. Nevertheless, BMS resulted in a further reduction of 6.2 mmHg in SBP and 5.5 mmHg in DBP at 12 months. These findings suggest that BMS provides additional blood pressure benefits beyond conventional medical treatment.

Previous studies have consistently shown that BMS improves hypertension and reduces the need for antihypertensive medications [3,4,5,6]. The GATEWAY trial [3] demonstrated superior blood pressure control following Roux-en-Y gastric bypass compared with medical therapy alone, while the Swedish Obese Subjects study [5] reported sustained improvement in hypertension after BMS. However, most previous studies assessed blood pressure using office measurements or hypertension remission rates [9,10]. In contrast, the present study objectively evaluated blood pressure changes using 24-hour ABPM, providing additional evidence for the beneficial effects of BMS on blood pressure control.

ABPM is considered a more reliable method for evaluating blood pressure than office measurement because it minimizes the influence of the white-coat effect and masked hypertension and better reflects a patient’s usual blood pressure profile. Moreover, ambulatory blood pressure has been shown to predict cardiovascular events and mortality more accurately than office blood pressure [7,8]. Therefore, the significant reductions observed in ambulatory blood pressure in the present study may more accurately reflect improvements in patients’ usual blood pressure profile than office blood pressure measurements.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective study conducted at a single institution with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, the follow-up period was limited to 12 months, and the long-term durability of blood pressure reduction could not be evaluated. Third, changes in antihypertensive medication use during follow-up were not analyzed in detail and may have influenced blood pressure measurements. Finally, cardiovascular outcomes were not evaluated; therefore, whether the observed reductions in ambulatory blood pressure translate into improved long-term cardiovascular prognosis remains unclear.

In conclusion, BMS was associated with significant reductions in both office and ambulatory blood pressure during the first postoperative year. Because ambulatory blood pressure provides a more objective assessment of blood pressure than office measurement, ABPM may be a useful tool for evaluating the antihypertensive effects of BMS. Larger prospective studies with longer follow-up are warranted to confirm these findings.
