Global, Regional, and National Burden of Nonrheumatic Valvular Heart Disease and Its Attributable Risk Factors in 204 Countries and Territories, 1990 to 2019: Results From the Global Burden of Disease Study 2019
Authors: Le Li, Limin Liu, Zhicheng Hu, Likun Zhou, Zhuxin Zhang, Lingmin Wu, Ligang Ding, Zhao Hu, Lihui Zheng, Yan Yao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNonrheumatic valvular heart disease (NRVD) is increasingly becoming a significant public health concern, particularly among older people. In the context of population aging, a detailed report on the epidemiological trends of NRVD is crucial for policymakers to effectively allocate health care resources. This study aimed to explore these trends using data from the GBD (Global Burden of Disease) 2019 study.Methods and ResultsWe assessed the changing trends of the incidence, mortality, and disability‐adjusted life years rates for NRVD by calculating the estimated annual percentage change for each. Additionally, we examined the impact of a region's level of social development on NRVD epidemiological trends and identified key risk factors contributing to NRVD‐related deaths. Between 1990 and 2019, the global incidence of NRVD showed a gradual increase, with an estimated annual percentage change of 0.39 (95% CI, 0.30–0.48). The data revealed that women and older people are more likely to develop NRVD. High‐sociodemographic index countries exhibited higher age‐standardized incidence and mortality rates compared with low‐ and middle‐sociodemographic index countries. The most significant risk factor for NRVD‐related mortality was identified as high systolic blood pressure.ConclusionsOver the past 3 decades, the burden of NRVD has intensified, indicating a need for more health care resources to address this issue, which is closely associated with an aging population. Currently, high‐sociodemographic index countries report the highest incidences and deaths due to NRVD. However, low‐ and middle‐sociodemographic index countries should also implement measures to manage the rapidly increasing burden of NRVD.Keywords: Global Burden of Disease, nonrheumatic valvular heart disease, population aging, sociodemographic indexSubject Categories: Epidemiology

Valvular heart disease is recognized clinically as a prevalent cardiac pathology, characterized by dysfunctional heart valves and disrupted unidirectional blood flow during cardiac cycles. 1 Rheumatic heart disease (RHD) was the leading cause of valvular heart disease globally. However, over the past 4 decades, economic and medical advancement have substantially reduced RHD incidence. 2 With an aging population, the prevalence of nonrheumatic valvular heart disease (NRVD) will be significantly increased, particularly among older populations in developed nations. 3 By 2017, approximately 12.6 million cases of calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD) and 18.1 million cases of degenerative mitral valve disease were globally documented. 4 These conditions represent the most prevalent forms of NRVD. This era also saw approximately 102 700 CAVD‐related and 35 700 degenerative mitral valve disease‐related deaths worldwide. 4 Given these trends and their significant public health implications, a comprehensive review and analysis of NRVD's global influence is imperative.

NRVD's significance stems from its diverse cause, contrasting with RHD. Although RHD primarily results from untreated streptococcal infections, 5 NRVD develops from an intricate interplay of factors, including aging, genetic predispositions, and lifestyle influences. 6 , 7 Understanding these primary risk factors associated with NRVD is pivotal. It enables governmental bodies to implement strategies at the population level, fostering an environment that actively reduces exposure to known NRVD risk factors. Such factors include hypertension, smoking, diabetes, and others. 8

The GBD (Global Burden of Disease) 2019 study represents a comprehensive, long‐standing global initiative aimed at quantifying the health‐related burdens associated with >360 specific diseases. 9 The latest edition, GBD 2019, offers a crucial platform for assessing the worldwide impact of NRVD in relation to its risk factors. In a recent study, Chen et al undertook an analysis using the GBD data set to estimate the global incidence, mortality, and overall burden of both RHD and NRVD. 10 However, this analysis was restricted to data from 2017 and has not undergone subsequent updates in the context of a formal GBD study. Moreover, it fell short in providing a comprehensive overview of the major risk factors attributable to NRVD. The aim of this study was to elucidate the effects of geographical location, sociodemographic index (SDI), age, and sex on the worldwide trends in incident cases, fatalities, and disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) of NRVD based on data from the GBD from 1990 to 2019 in 204 countries and territories. Moreover, we tried to examine the influence of multiple risk factors on NRVD‐related mortality. Such detailed exploration is pivotal for tailoring effective public health strategies and health care resource allocation, thereby mitigating the disease's impact.

METHODS

Data Availability Statement

The data sets generated and analyzed during the current study are available at http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd‐results‐tool.

Overview of the GBD 2019 Study

The GBD study, a cornerstone in global health research, was designed to offer comprehensive and comparable estimates on mortality, disability, and their associated risk factors on a global scale. In its 2019 iteration, the GBD study provided estimations for 369 causes of death and disability, alongside 87 risk factors and their combinations, spanning globally, regionally, and across 204 countries and territories. The study meticulously presented all metrics, including mortality, incidence, and prevalence, along with their respective 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs). These UIs are instrumental in capturing not only sampling error in a singular statistical test but also the uncertainty inherent in various associated processes. The SDI, a composite measure of income per capita, educational attainment, and fertility rates, significantly illuminates the impact of sociodemographic development on disease burden variations related to risk factors. 11 Within this study, SDI values, ranging from 0 to 1, denote the extent of social advancement. These values are categorized into quintiles representing low‐, low‐middle‐, middle‐, high‐middle‐, and high‐SDI countries. The cutoff values used to determine quintiles for analysis were computed using country level estimates of the SDI, excluding countries with populations <1 million. 12 For our analysis, all data were sourced from the openly accessible database of the GBD 2019 study, available through the Global Health Data Exchange (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd‐results‐tool). Because this work is limited to the analysis of existing databases, no institutional review board approval or informed consent was required.

Attributable Burden

The GBD study incorporated the comparative risk assessment framework previously 13 to quantify the burden of several causes and impairments attributable to 87 environmental, occupational, metabolic, and behavioral risk factors. 14 Briefly, after assessing the casual evidence in each risk‐outcome pair, we selected 2 components to model the attributable burden of causes to risks, including deaths and DALYs.

Statistical Analysis

In the GBD 2019, nonfatal burden of NRVD including prevalence and incidence was estimated by the GBD group using the DisMod‐MR 2.1 modeling tool. Burden due to each severity of valve disease was estimated by multiplying the prevalence of each severity by the corresponding disability weight. DisMod‐MR is a Bayesian geospatial disease modeling software that uses data on various disease parameters, the epidemiological relationships between these parameters, and geospatial relationships to produce estimates of prevalence and incidence. 14 To estimate cause‐specific mortality for NRVD across different ages, sexes, locations, and years, the cause of death ensemble model was used, drawing upon the GBD cause of death database. Details of the modeling strategy for both fatal and nonfatal burden estimates are provided in Data S1. The annual figures for incidence, deaths, DALYs, and their age‐standardized rates (ASRs) were used to delineate the burden attributed to NRVD. ASRs were particularly instrumental in mitigating the distortions arising from shifts in age distribution and population size. The calculation of the ASR per 100 000 population uses the direct method, which aggregates the products of the age‐specific rates (a i ) for each age group (i) and the corresponding number of individuals or weights (w i ) within the same age subgroup of the standard population. This total is then divided by the sum of the standard population's weights, after which the quotient is multiplied by 100 000 to yield the ASR: ASR=∑i=1Aaiwi∑i=1Awi×100,000.

The DALYs metric was computed as the aggregate of years lived with disabiliies and years of life lost. Furthermore, the study incorporated the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC), calculated from ASRs, including age‐standardized incidence rate/age‐standardized death rate (ASIR/ASDR) and age‐standardized DALYs rate per 100 000 people. This was to assess the temporal trends in NRVD's burden. EAPC is a summary and widely used measure of the ASR trend over a specified interval. A regression line was fitted to the natural logarithm of the rates. In the equation y=α+βx+ε, y represents the ln‐transformed ASR, whereas x signifies the calendar year. The EAPC was calculated as 100×(10β−1), and its 95% CI can also be obtained from the linear regression model. 15 Details of calculations of EAPC and its 95% CI could be found in the Supplementary Methods. Figure S1 included the hypothesis testing of the linear correlation between ASR and year. In our study, we calculated the EAPC and 95% CI separately for each country and region, rather than using a mixed model for unified estimation. An EAPC value and its 95% CI >0 indicated an increasing trend in the corresponding ASR, and the opposite for a decreasing trend. To explore the relationship between changes in ASR and levels of social development, Spearman's correlation coefficient was calculated between EAPCs and SDI values. We used local polynomial regression to fit the correlation curves and calculated the corresponding 95% CIs by performing weighted averaging of the data around each data point. A P value of <0.05 is considered statistically significant.

Data Visualization

We used histograms to illustrate both the quantities and the evolving trends of NRVD's incidence cases, deaths, and DALYs spanning from 1990 to 2019. Geographic distribution and ASRs of NRVD's burden across 204 countries and territories were depicted through maps. Additionally, scatter plots and regression analyses were implemented to examine the relationship between ASRs and SDI values. Moreover, stacked area charts were used to illustrate the evolving impact of potential risk factors on NRVD death or DALYs over time. The extent of the impact is indicated by the magnitude of the area beneath the curve, with larger areas signifying greater influence. All statistical analyses were performed using the R program (version 4.3.1; R core team).

RESULTS

NRVD Incidence and Its Change Trend

In 2019, there was a significant rise in the global incidence of NRVD compared with 1990. The count of new cases surged from 808 030 (95% UI, 768 440–850 870) to 1 653 550 (95% UI, 1 556 530–1 755 010). Concurrently, the ASIR increased from 18.37 per 100 000 individuals (95% UI, 17.41–19.35) to 19.77 per 100 000 individuals (95% UI, 18.62–20.95), with an EAPC of 0.39 (95% CI, 0.30–0.48), as illustrated in Table 1 and Figure S2. Women were more prone to NRVD than men, with ASIR ratios of 1.35:1 in 1990 and 1.12:1 in 2019. In terms of SDI regions, there was a noticeable rise in NRVD cases across all 5 regions (Figure 1A). The region with the highest SDI witnessed the most significant NRVD burden, where incident cases climbed from 471.65 (95% UI, 447.37–497.71) in 1990 to 857.24 (95% UI, 804.11–914.45) in 2019. Region‐wise, the incidences augmented in all areas from 1990 to 2019, with the most pronounced increases in Andean Latin America and the slightest in Eastern Sub‐Saharan Africa (Table 1). Furthermore, we observed that the worldwide incidence of CAVD disease also rose from 1990 to 2019 in most regions (Figure S3). The United States, China, and Japan reported the highest numbers of new NRVD cases in 2019, whereas Tokelau, Niue, and Nauru had the fewest (Table S1 and Figure 2A). Italy, Slovenia, and Hungary exhibited the highest ASIR, whereas Niger, Guatemala, and Senegal had the lowest in 2019 (Table S1 and Figure 3A).

NRVD Deaths and Its Change Trend

The analysis reveals a significant increase in NRVD‐related deaths, rising from 77 930 cases (95% UI, 70 800–83 260) in 1990 to 1 653 550 cases (95% UI, 1 556 530–1 755 010) in 2019. Despite this, the ASDR decreased from 2.48 (95% UI, 2.21–2.64) in 1990 to 2.25 (95% UI, 1.89–2.47) in 2019. Notably, the death cases for women were consistently higher than that of men (1990: 44860 [95% UI, 39 650–49 050] versus 33 070 [95% UI, 30560–35 680]; 2019: 96 010 [95% UI, 78 370–108 440] versus 68 110 [95% UI, 61 050–73 330]), as shown in Table 2. Subgroup analysis based on SDI highlighted that those regions with high SDI witnessed the highest mortality (1990: 46 740 [95% UI, 42 170‐48 980]; 2019: 94 290 [95% UI, 77 330–104 390]), as illustrated in Figure 1B. Interestingly, only the high‐middle SDI region observed an increase in ASDR, with an EAPC of 0.43 (95% CI, 0.23 to 0.63). Geographically, South Asia reported the most deaths (1990: 5620 [95% UI, 3660–7480]; 2019: 14 330 [95% UI, 11 360–17 190]), as depicted in Figure 2B, yet experienced a relative decrease in ASDR (EAPC: −0.40 [95% CI, −0.49 to −0.31]). Central Asia experienced the most substantial increase in ASDR, with an EAPC of 3.31 (95% CI, 2.96 to 3.66), as shown in Figure 3B. Country‐specific analysis revealed that the United States, Germany, and Japan had the highest number of deaths (1990: 15454, 8074, and 5794, respectively; 2019: 28 720, 15 887, and 15 707, respectively) (Table S2).

NRVD DALYs and Its Change Trend

Globally, the DALYs experienced a marked increase, rising from 1 666 710 (95% UI, 1 490 490–1 849 030) in 1990 to 2 793 750 (95% UI, 2 518 740‐3 129 040) in 2019, as detailed in Table 3. However, the ASR of DALYs witnessed a relative decline during the same period, with an EAPC of −0.81 (95% CI, −0.87 to −0.74). A sex‐based analysis revealed that DALYs were consistently higher in women compared with men, with figures for 1990 being 873 010 (95% UI, 746 780–1 000 070) for women versus 793 700 (95% UI, 727 290–883 520) for men, and for 2019, 1 465 340 (95% UI, 1 274 140–1 675 170) for women compared with 1 328 410 (95% UI, 1 219 310–1 464 180) for men. When categorized by SDI, regions with high SDI exhibited the highest DALYs, showing a rise from 806 860 (95% UI, 748 410–870 520) in 1990 to 1 217 970 (95% UI, 1 065 440–1 359 140) in 2019, as illustrated in Figure 1C. Geographically, South Asia had the highest DALY both in 1990 (153 770 [95% UI, 96 670–212 840]) and 2019 (337 990 [95% UI, 258 620–418 860]) (Figure 2C). Central Asia, in particular, demonstrated the steepest increase in ASR of DALYs during this period (EAPC: 2.30, [95% CI, 2.02–2.57]) (Figure 3C). In terms of country‐specific data, the United States, Germany, and India had the highest DALYs in 1990, with respective values of 288 761, 122 774, and 120 953. By 2019, the leading countries were the United States, India, and Japan, with DALYs of 402 061, 262 582, and 194 960, respectively (Table S3).

Correlation Between SDI and NRVD's Incidence and Mortality

We explored the relationship between ASIR and ASDR in 1990 and their corresponding EAPC. When ASIR was <38, we found a significant positive correlation between EAPC and ASIR (Spearman coefficient: 0.44, P<0.01). However, when ASIR was ≥38, the correlation was negative (Spearman coefficient: −0.10, P<0.01). Our analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between EAPC and ASDR (Spearman coefficient: −0.21, P<0.01). Subsequently, we calculated Spearman's correlation coefficients to evaluate the association between the SDI in 2019 and the EAPC values of ASIR and ASDR across 204 countries. We observed a significantly positive correlation between the SDI and EAPCs of ASIR (Spearman coefficient: 0.71, P<0.01) and ASDR (Spearman coefficient: 0.12, P<0.01) (Figure 4). Furthermore, we examined the correlations between SDI and ASIR, ASDR, and ASR of DALYs in 12 global regions, as shown in Figure S4. Notably, the positive associations between ASIR and SDI, and between the EAPC of ASIR and SDI, were significant only when SDI was >0.6. The SDI groups of different locations are found in Table S4. DISCUSSION

This study offers extensive global and national assessments of the disease burden attributable to NRVD. The rising global burden of NRVD is primarily attributed to population growth and aging. In 2019, approximately 1.65 million NRVD cases and 164 120 NRVD‐related deaths were estimated worldwide. The study reveals a notably higher incidence of NRVD in high‐SDI countries like the United States, likely due to enhanced diagnostic and registry systems, coupled with aging populations. Additionally, elevated systolic blood pressure was identified as a critical risk factor for NRVD.

This study underscores the significant impact of an aging population on the escalating trend of NRVD burden. Age emerges as a paramount risk factor, with the likelihood of developing NRVD doubling with each passing decade. 6 , 16 The increase in NRVD prevalence across many countries is likely tied to enhanced life expectancy. Historically perceived as a degenerative condition resulting from time‐related leaflet deterioration and calcium accumulation, recent histopathological and clinical evidence suggests NRVD is an active, complex disease. It involves mechanisms like chronic systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, 17 , 18 exacerbated by aging. 19 For instance, the colocalization of apoB (apolipoprotein B) with oxidized low‐density lipoproteins in calcific aortic stenosis valves and the promotion of vascular cell calcification by oxidized lipids underscore these processes. 20 , 21 Additionally, the interconnected nature of fibro calcific remodeling and inflammation in aortic valve disease highlights the complexity of NRVD. 22 Given the adverse prognosis for older patients with NRVD and the global demographic shift toward an older population, it is imperative to address the rapid surge in NRVD cases.

This study identified sex differences in the disease burden of NRVD, with women exhibiting higher incidence, death cases, and DALYs than men. The underlying mechanisms remain uncertain, but relevant studies offer some insights. Vignac et al reported more significant aortic dilation in women, 23 and Martín‐Núñez et al found that women with diabetes are at higher risk of aortic valve calcification, linked to oxidative stress and metabolic dysfunction. 24 Given the poorer prognosis in women with aortic stenosis, 25 enhanced monitoring and early intervention are recommended.

This study reveals notable geographic disparities in NRVD epidemiology, with high‐SDI regions exhibiting the highest incidence and mortality rates. This difference may be attributed to the reduced presence of competing mortality factors, particularly in high‐SDI areas where other conditions leading to premature death are less prevalent. 4 High‐SDI areas, characterized by advanced health coverage, facilitate more frequent physical examinations and accessible medical services. This enhanced health care access aids in the early detection of valve diseases, including asymptomatic cases. 26 Additionally, increased life expectancy in older populations within high‐SDI regions contributes to a higher incidence of NRVD, an age‐related disease predominantly affecting the older population. 27

In addition to the epidemiological trends of NRVD, our study delved into its primary attributable risk factors using the GBD database. High systolic blood pressure emerged as the most significant risk factor for NRVD‐related deaths and DALYs. A genetic epidemiological study involving >500 000 individuals from the UK Biobank used Mendelian randomization to establish a causal link between systolic blood pressure and the risk of CAVD. This study found that a genetically predicted increase of 20 mm Hg in systolic blood pressure could amplify the risk of aortic stenosis by >3‐fold. 28 A diet high in sodium is a major risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, 29 linked to the impact of aldosterone on renal mineralocorticoid receptors. 30 , 31 In hyperlipidemic rabbit models, aldosterone receptors in aortic valve tissues were shown to be involved in early CAVD stages, with their selective blockade reducing disease processes. 32 Additionally, a high‐salt diet is strongly correlated with increased blood pressure, 33 offering another potential link between NRVD and sodium‐rich diets, which is another possible interpretation for the relationship between NRVD and a diet high in sodium. The GBD study has not only informed all relevant stakeholders but has also uncovered overlooked risk factors, such as low‐level lead exposure. This discovery has prompted further research in the field. Furthermore, we found that lead exposure was a potential risk factor for NRVD‐related deaths, likely due to its role in promoting atherosclerosis and hypertension. 34 , 35

In addition to incidence, death, and DALYs, the case‐fatality ratio, which represents the proportion of cases resulting in death, is crucial for understanding a disease's lethality. However, our study did not provide a direct calculation or modeling of the case‐fatality ratio. Although the GBD offers detailed estimates of incidence and mortality, it does not include a direct case‐fatality ratio. Estimating or modeling this ratio using a count‐based model with an offset term could provide deeper insights into the relationship between incidence and mortality. Without this, our interpretation of the mortality burden relative to the incidence of NRVD is constrained. Future studies should incorporate case‐fatality ratio modeling for a more comprehensive understanding of the disease burden.

One important aspect to consider in our study is the difference between raw and age‐adjusted trends across regions. Although the absolute number of deaths due to NRVD has increased globally, this rise is largely attributable to significant population growth and aging. Advancements in NRVD treatments and improved management of risk factors such as hypertension have contributed to better control of NRVD mortality rates. 36 This decline reflects improvements in NRVD prognosis due to better treatment and management. The same factors account for trends in DALYs, where ASRs have also shown a decrease. These findings highlight the importance of considering differential age distribution changes across regions when interpreting the results. The ASIR and annual change rate provide a more accurate picture of NRVD's impact, revealing that although more people are being diagnosed and dying from NRVD in absolute terms, the rates adjusted for age are actually improving. This suggests that, overall, advancements in medical treatments and risk factor management are having a positive effect on controlling the disease's impact.

Over the past 50 years, the management of NRVD has undergone significant transformation. Techniques like transcatheter aortic valve replacement and transcatheter mitral valve repair have shown promise in improving outcomes for severe aortic stenosis and degenerative mitral valve disease, respectively. 36 , 37 However, it is important to note that although these procedures hold potential, they are still relatively new and not widely accessible globally, particularly in low‐ and medium‐risk surgical patients, where surgical aortic valve replacement remains the preferred option. 38 The global gaps in access to cardiac surgical and interventional care also limit the impact of these procedures on reducing mortality on a global scale. 39 Future research should prioritize disease‐specific prevention strategies, early diagnosis, and efforts to reduce geographic disparities in disease burden.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the specific burdens of NRVD subtypes like aortic regurgitation and degenerative tricuspid valve disease could not be examined due to data gaps in the GBD study. Second, the lack of robust death registration systems in many countries necessitates the use of verbal autopsy studies for mortality estimates. Third, a significant limitation is the uncertainty inherent in the estimates, particularly when actual data are scarce, necessitating reliance on GBD's compensatory estimates. Fourth, nonlinearity was not tested in the trend, which could also play a significant role in the observed patterns. Last, our analysis could not account for certain risk factors, including metabolic factors, impacting the comprehensiveness of our estimations.

CONCLUSIONS

The global incidence and mortality rates of NRVD have been on a gradual incline. The disease disproportionately affects women and older people. A positive correlation exists between the incidence of NRVD and SDI values, indicating higher rates in developed regions compared with developing regions. Key risk factors for NRVD‐related mortality include high blood pressure, a diet high in sodium, and lead exposure. With the global trend of accelerated aging, both incidence and mortality rates of NRVD are projected to rise. Consequently, policymakers should judiciously allocate public health resources to mitigate the burgeoning burden of NRVD.
