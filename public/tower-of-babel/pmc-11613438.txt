Template and Solid-State-Assisted Assembly of an M9L6 Expanded Coordination Cage for Medium-Sized Molecule Encapsulation
Authors: Kenta Iizuka, Hiroki Takezawa, Makoto Fujita
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The M6L4 cage, self-assembling from six Pd(II) or Pt(II) 90-degree blocks and four triazine-cored triangular ligands, has an effective hydrophobic cavity of about 450 Å3 capable of encapsulating one or more small molecules. Here, from the same components, we successfully constructed an M9L6 cage with an internal volume expanded to 1540 Å3 via the self-assembly of an M8L6 precursor using pillar[5]arene as a template. This cage retains the high molecular recognition ability of the M6L4 cage while recognizing medium-sized guest molecules with molecular weights of up to ∼1600.

Size matters in host–guest chemistry. In the early days, crown ethers and macrocyclic hosts recognized metal ions and small molecules in their cavities.1 However, because of the size limitation (typically, <200 Å3 cavity volume), guest recognition by these synthetic hosts was restricted to mostly a single small molecule displaying no unique properties that might appear by multimolecular guest recognition. In the mid-90s, larger and three-dimensionally closed cavities were efficiently constructed through molecular self-assembly.2−4 The M6L4 cage (Figure 1) is one of the most potent self-assembled hosts, which provides an effective molecular recognition site with a cavity volume of approximately 450 Å3.3a The cavity surrounded by the four ligands possesses hydrophobic and electron-accepting properties and effectively accommodates multiple guest molecules to create conformationally fixed guest aggregates, which leads to new reactions and functions.5

Schematic representation of the concept: cavity expansion of M6L4 cage 1 into M9L6 cage 2.

Although numerous reports in recent years have described the self-assembly of cages with larger internal cavities,6,7 in most cases, the frameworks merely form closed structures without offering effective molecular recognition properties. Here, we succeeded in the expansion of the M6L4 cage into an M9L6 cage,8 without changing the M and L components,9 by utilizing the advantages of solid-state synthesis10,11 and the template effect12,13 of a medium-sized molecule. The internal volume of the expanded M9L6 cage is >1500 Å3, more than three times larger than that of the original M6L4 cage, and the cage was found to bind medium-sized molecules with molecular weights reaching ∼1600 while maintaining the high guest affinity of the M6L4 cage. This development is highly promising for advancing host–guest chemistry based on medium-sized molecule recognition, a previously unexplored area in host–guest chemistry due to the host’s size limitation. We demonstrate the molecular recognition of medium-sized compounds, including macrocyclic ones, that are of current pharmaceutical interest.

We observed the expansion of the M6L4 structure 1 into the M9L6 cage framework 2 through intermediary M8L6 cage 3 when we attempted to encapsulate a macrocyclic host pillar[5]arene14 in 1 during our efforts in further developing “host-in-host” chemistry.15 Stirring M6L4 cage 1 with 4 in aqueous solution did not result in guest inclusion (see the Supporting Information for details). To improve the efficiency of the inclusion, we attempted solid-state self-assembly of the cage in the presence of 4. When a powdered 8:6:1 mixture of (tmed)Pt(ONO2)2 (5, 1.76 g, 4.0 mmol; tmed = tetramethylethylenediamine; here, a Pt(II) block was employed to secure the kinetic stability of forming complexes), 2,4,6-tris(4-pyridyl)triazine (0.96 g, 3.1 mmol), and 4 (0.39 g, 0.52 mmol) was ground with a ball-milling machine (400 rpm) for 3 h, an orange solid material was obtained. The solid was well-soluble in water, whose D2O solution exhibited broad signals in 1H NMR that contained upfield-shifted signals of 4, suggesting the formation of an oligomeric host mixture that can accommodate 4 (Figure S2). After heating the resulting aqueous solution at 100 °C for 7 days, the 1H NMR spectrum of the solution showed converged sharp yet complex signals (Figure S3). Recrystallization from methanol gave a red crystalline powder of an inclusion complex in 45% yield (1.40 g, 0.23 mmol; Figure 3a). The self-assembly of cage components under standard solution conditions in the presence of template 4 yielded only a trace amount of the desired complex, mainly producing cage 1, along with an oligomeric mixture (see the Supporting Information).

The 1H NMR spectrum of the isolated inclusion complex in D2O showed a significant upfield shift and highly split signals from 4 (Figure 3a). Ten independent signals of the methoxy groups were exhibited from guest 4, indicative of the desymmetrized conformation from D5 to C1. The complex pattern of the cage signals also explains the desymmetrization. 1H-DOSY NMR suggested the formation of a complex larger than the M6L4 cage (Figure S6). To our surprise, ESI-MS measurement revealed the composition of the inclusion complex as M8L6·4 (3·4), not M9L6·4 (2·4), which was first aimed for (Figure S7).

The 3D structure of 3 was confirmed by single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis of the 3·4 complex (Figure 2b). The host framework 3 provides a box-shaped cavity to accommodate 4. Remarkably, two free pyridine moieties stacking with each other are observed in the cage framework. The included 4 adopts a highly distorted conformation in the cavity, desymmetrizing the whole structure of the inclusion complex into C1 symmetry. Each of the five electron-rich aromatic rings of 4 forms a π–π stacking structure with the electron-deficient ligand of the cage, which strongly suggests the stabilization of a strained conformation by the efficient donor–acceptor π–π interactions. The interactions are also evidenced by the red color of the crystal and the solution. It is noteworthy that the highly distorted conformation of 4 is quite rare,14 which resembles even the transition structure for the ring flipping in 4.14c,14d This observation indicates the powerful nature of cage 3 to distort an organic macrocycle, as previously demonstrated by calix[4]arene inclusion within the M6L4 cage.15

Cage 3 is stable enough in an aqueous solution after the removal of template 4 by washing with dichloromethane (Figure 3b). In the 1H NMR spectrum, two of the signals from the panel ligands were observed in a relatively upfield region, which reflected the stacked geometry of the noncoordinated pyridine rings. The empty cage 3 showed C2v symmetry in the spectrum, indicating the fast exchange of the positions of the stacked panel ligands. The empty structure of 3 after the template removal was also confirmed by single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis (Figure S14).16 The estimated volume of the cavity of 3 by the MoloVol program17 based on the crystal structure is 924 Å3, which is almost twice as large as that of the original M6L4 cage 1 (474 Å3).

1H NMR spectrum (300 K, 600 MHz, D2O) of (a) inclusion complex 3·4, (b) empty 3, and (c) 2 (*, residual solvents). The host symmetry changes from C1 (in 3·4) to C2v (in 3) to D3h (in 2).

To our delight, the quantitative conversion of empty M8L6 cage 3 into M9L6 cage 2 was achieved by the simple addition of an excess amount of (tmed)Pt(II) component 5 (see the Supporting Information for details). Thus, 5 (139 mg, 0.32 mmol, 10 equiv to cage 3) was added to an aqueous solution of cage 3 (2.1 mM, 15 mL), and the solution was stirred at 60 °C for 22 h. After solvent removal under reduced pressure, the crude product was recrystallized from 5 mL of methanol and washed with acetone thoroughly to give acetone solvate of 2 as an off-white powder (117 mg, 20.2 μmol, 65%). As shown clearly in 1H NMR, the free pyridyl moieties no longer existed, and cage 3 was converted into a more symmetric cage 2 (Figure 3c). Cage 2 was isolated by recrystallization from methanol followed by washing with acetone (65% isolated yield) and fully characterized by NMR and ESI-MS measurements (Figures S17–23). Although all six ligands in the M9L6 cage are equivalent, each ligand has C2v symmetry. Therefore, in the 1H NMR spectrum, three PyHα and PyHβ signals are observed in a 1:1:1 ratio. ESI-MS analysis after counteranion exchange with PF6– revealed a series of multivalent ions [M–n(PF6)]n+, where the M9L6 cage 2 sequentially loses its counterions (PF6–).

The expanded structure of M9L6 cage 2 was unambiguously determined by X-ray crystallographic analysis of the guest inclusion complex. After suspending an excess amount of 1,3,5-trimethoxybenzene (6)18 in the D2O solution of 2 and stirring for a while, excess 6 was removed, and the solution was analyzed by NMR (Figure S24), which revealed that the inclusion complex 2·(6)n (n = ∼10) was formed. The methoxy group of encapsulated 6 exhibited an upfield shift of approximately 1 ppm, which indicated shielding inside the cage. Slow evaporation of the solvent from an aqueous solution of 2·(6)n prepared in water in a similar manner yielded single crystals of 2·(6)n. X-ray diffraction analysis of these crystals revealed the structure of inclusion complex 2·(6)6 in which six guest molecules of 6 were tightly trapped within the cavity. During crystallization, the number of encapsulated guest molecules (n) was reduced to six. For another guest, calix[4]arene (7), we also succeeded in the crystallographic analysis of inclusion complex 2·(7)3 (Figure 4).

From these X-ray structures, it was found that the M9L6 host adopts a trigonal prismatic shape (D3h) with a calculated cavity volume of approximately 1540 Å3, which is more than three times larger than that of M6L4 cage 1. The total molecular weight of the included guests reaches approximately 1000 for 2·(6)6 and 1300 for 2·(7)3, indicating the capability of 2 to encapsulate medium-sized molecules with molecular weights exceeding 1000. Furthermore, it was shown that the cage can adapt and deform according to the guest molecules to tightly accommodate them, thereby demonstrating its versatility and adaptability as a host (Figure 4).

Accordingly, we examined the host–guest complexation of medium-sized molecules within M9L6 cage 2 (Figure 5). Upon addition of rifampicin (8), an antibiotic medium-sized pharmaceutical molecule with a molecular weight of 822.94, to an aqueous solution of cage 2, the solution turned orange, which indicated the formation of an inclusion complex via donor–acceptor interactions. The guest inclusion was studied by using 1H NMR spectroscopy (Figures 5b–e). After the addition of 0.7 equiv of 8 to the D2O solution of 2, the 1H NMR spectrum showed a notable upfield shift in the guest signals, which confirmed the formation of inclusion complex 2·8. Further addition of 8 led to the appearance of a new set of signals, and upon adding 2.0 equiv of 8, the signals became characteristic of a 1:2 host–guest complex, 2·(8)2. The 1H NMR spectrum of 2·(8)2 revealed significant splitting of the cage signals, along with two distinct sets of guest signals, suggesting the formation of an asymmetrical, noncovalent dimer and the positional fixing of the dimer (8)2 within the hydrophobic cavity. This confirms that cage 2 can accommodate molecules with a total molecular weight exceeding 1600.

(a) Structures of target medium-sized molecules: rifampicin (8) and daptomycin (9). 1H NMR spectra (300 K, 600 MHz, D2O) of (b) 8 and mixtures of 2 with (c) 0.7 equiv of 8 (exclusively forming 2·8), (d) 1.2 equiv of 8 [a mixture of 2·8 and 2·(8)2], and (e) 2.0 equiv of 8 [exclusively forming 2·(8)2].

As a larger guest, we examined the encapsulation of daptomycin (9), a macrocyclic peptide with a molecular weight of 1619.70. Upon mixing cage 2 with compound 9 in an aqueous solution, an inclusion complex formed immediately as evidenced by a significant upfield shift in the 1H NMR spectrum and a visible color change (Figures S31–34). 1H diffusion-ordered spectroscopy (DOSY) NMR spectra indicated identical diffusion coefficients for both the host and the guest (Figure S33). UV–vis titration experiments in an acetate buffer solution (pH = 5) showed the formation of a 1:2 host–guest complex with 9 with association constants of Ka1 = (4.8 ± 1.2) × 104 M–1 for the first complexation (1:1) and Ka2 = (2.6 ± 0.2) × 105 M–1 for the second (1:2), thereby indicating strong cooperativity in the bimolecular encapsulation (Figures S35 and S36). The hydrophobic regions of 9, including the decanoate, indole, and aniline moieties of the side chains, seem to associate to form a hydrophobic dimer within the cavity as suggested by the upfield shifts of these proton signals in the 1H NMR spectrum (assigned by 1H–13C heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC) NMR, Figure S34).

In conclusion, we successfully expanded the M6L4 octahedral cage into a larger M9L6 structure with more than three times the cavity volume by using a combination of templated self-assembly and solid-state mechanochemical complexation. This strategy provides a novel method for constructing large coordination complexes that cannot be achieved through conventional thermodynamic, equilibrated self-assembly in solution. The expanded M9L6 cage significantly extends the size limitations of guests to allow the encapsulation of medium-sized molecules with molecular weights ranging from ∼1600, which lie in the intermediate region between small molecules and large biomolecules, such as proteins and DNA/RNA. Medium-sized bioactive compounds, which have recently attracted attention as drug candidates, are also targets for our expanded cages, and we are currently exploring applications in life science research.

Historically, host–guest chemistry has expanded its scope with the enlargement of cavity sizes, targeting metal ions, small molecules, and small molecule aggregates, each time bringing about new chemistry. The development of the expanded cages, which possess superior molecular recognition functions distinguishing them from mere giant hollow frameworks, promises to create a new facet in host–guest chemistry for medium-sized compounds. We emphasize once again that size does matter in host–guest chemistry.
