On ℳ \mathscr{M} -arrangements of conics and lines with ordinary singularities
Marek Janasz, Piotr Pokora

Abstract
In this paper, we study combinatorial aspects of reduced plane curves known as ℳ \mathscr{M} -curves. This notation is a natural generalization of maximizing plane curves which are well-known in the theory of algebraic surfaces. We focus here on ℳ \mathscr{M} -arrangements of conics and lines with ordinary singularities of multiplicity less than five and we provide various numerical constraints on their existence, particularly in terms of their weak combinatorics. Moreover, we study in detail the scenario when our ℳ \mathscr{M} -arrangements consist of lines and just one conic.



1
Introduction

In the present paper, we study a relatively new class of plane curves known as
\mathscr{M}
-curves. These curves were recently introduced in
[
8
]
as a natural generalization of the classical known maximizing curves
[
13
]
. The theory of maximizing curves of even degree is well-established and there are many interesting constructions of such curves. Note that maximizing plane curves admit only
{\rm ADE}
-singularities and these curves are free with the maximal possible degree of the associated Jacobian ideal. These objects are relatively difficult to construct due to these restrictive conditions. Recently, Dimca and Pokora introduced the notion of maximizing curves of odd degree in
[
5
]
. However, these objects are extremely rare; we are only aware of a few examples of maximizing curves of odd degree up to
9
. One solution to this problem is to introduce a slightly larger class of reduced plane curves that have similar properties to maximizing curves. Hence, the concept of
\mathscr{M}
-curves emerges. Let us present their definition in a concise way (cf.
[
8
, Definition 4.1 and Definition 4.7]
).

Definition 1.1
.

Let
C=\{f=0\}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
be a reduced curve of degree
d\geq 5
that admits only
{\rm ADE}
-singularities and simple elliptic singularities, i.e., these are ordinary quadruple points and
J_{2,0}
-singularities. Denote by
J_{f}
the Jacobian ideal generated by the all partial derivatives of
f
. We say that
C
is an
\mathscr{M}
-curve if

{\rm deg}\,J_{f}=\begin{cases}3m^{2}-3m+3&\text{provided that }d=2m\geq 6\\
3m^{2}+1&\text{provided that }d=2m+1\geq 5\end{cases}\,\,\,.

It is worth noticing that the notion of
\mathscr{M}
-curves only makes sense if our curves have at least one simple elliptic singularity and
this is our fundamental assumption throughout the paper
. Our goal here is to study combinatorial properties of a certain class
\mathscr{M}
-curves, namely conic-line arrangements with only ordinary double, triple and quadruple points. The initial steps toward comprehending the role of
\mathscr{M}
-curves within the class of line arrangements were laid in a work
[
9
]
. For instance, if
\mathcal{L}
is a real
\mathscr{M}
-arrangement of lines (such an arrangement admits only double, triple and quadruple points), then
\mathcal{L}
is simplicial, see
[
9
]
. This non-trivial outcome, somewhat unexpected, demonstrates that
\mathscr{M}
-curves present a significant challenge. Moreover, the arrangements of conics and lines appear, somehow naturally, in constructions of algebraic surfaces possessing some extreme properties, see for instance
[
14
,
15
]
. Let us present a short outline of the present paper. In first part we recall all necessary preliminaries regarding free plane curves and
\mathscr{M}
-curves. Then we provide a combinatorial criterion on the existence of
\mathscr{M}
-arrangements of conics and lines with ordinary double, triple and quadruple points — see Theorem
3.1
. Then we focus on the
\mathscr{M}
-arrangements consisting of lines and exactly one conic. Although this setting seems quite restrictive, these arrangements play a fundamental role in the construction of many algebraic surfaces, as was demonstrated in
[
12
]
. The primary focus of this study is the combinatorial description of line arrangements obtained through the deletion of this unique conic from
\mathscr{M}
-arrangements. The following exposition will utilize the language of combinatorial Poincaré polynomials, which were recently introduced by the second author in
[
18
]
. Let us recall that if
\mathcal{CL}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
is an arrangement of
k
conics and
d
lines such that it admits only
n_{r}
singularities of multiplicity
r\geq 2
, then its combinatorial Poincaré polynomial has the form

\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{CL};t)=1+(2k+d-1)t+\bigg(\sum_{r\geq 2}(r-1)n_{r}-d+1\bigg)t^{2}.

It turns out that the polynomial
\mathfrak{P}
decodes the freeness of
\mathcal{CL}
provided that all ordinary singularities are quasi-homogeneous. Using the notion of combinatorial Poincaré polynomial we provide a complete description of the Poincaré polynomials of line arrangements obtained by the deletion of a unique conic from our
\mathscr{M}
-arrangements — see Theorem
4.3
. Moreover, we provide several examples explaining our results in practice. We conclude our paper by presenting a remark about the Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity of
S
-modules associated with
\mathscr{M}
-curves.

2
Preliminaries

In this paper we adopt the notation from
[
3
]
. Let
S=\mathbb{C}[x,y,z]
be the coordinate ring of
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
. For a homogeneous polynomial
f\in S
we denote by
J_{f}
the Jacobian ideal associated with
f
, i.e., the ideal of the form
J_{f}=\langle\partial_{x}\,f,\partial_{y}\,f,\partial_{z}\,f\rangle
. We define the Milnor algebra associated to
f
as
M(f)=S/J_{f}
.

We will need an important invariant that is defined in the language of the syzygies of
J_{f}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Consider the graded
S
-module of Jacobian syzygies of
f
, namely

AR(f)=\{(a,b,c)\in S^{3}:a\partial_{x}\,f+b\partial_{y}\,f+c\partial_{z}\,f=0\}.

The minimal degree of non-trivial Jacobian relations for
f
is defined

{\rm mdr}(f):=\min_{r\geq 0}\{AR(f)_{r}\neq 0\}.

Remark 2.2
.

If
C=\{f=0\}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
is a reduced curve then we write
{\rm mdr}(f)
or
{\rm mdr}(C)
interchangeably.

Let us now formally define the freeness property for reduced plane curves.

Definition 2.3
.

A reduced curve
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
of degree
d
is free if the Jacobian ideal
J_{f}
is saturated with respect to
\mathfrak{m}=\langle x,y,z\rangle
. Moreover, if
C
is free, then the pair
(d_{1},d_{2})=({\rm mdr}(f),d-1-{\rm mdr}(f))
is called the exponents of
C
, i.e., the degrees of generators of
{\rm AR}(f)
.

Remark 2.4
.

In the scenario where
C=\{f=0\}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
is free, generators of
{\rm AR}(f)
are not uniquely determined, contrary to the degree of these generators.

Remark 2.5
.

It is worth recalling here that
\mathscr{M}
-curves are free, see
[
8
]
for details.

In order to check whether a given plane curve
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
is free we can use the following criterion that comes from
[
7
]
. Let us recall that for a reduced curve
C=\{f=0\}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
one has

{\rm deg}\,J_{f}=\tau(C)=\sum_{p\in{\rm Sing}(C)}\tau_{p}

with
\tau_{p}
being the Tjurina number of a singular point
p\in C
.

Theorem 2.6

(du-Plessis – Wall)
.

Let
C=\{f=0\}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
be a reduced curve of degree
d
and let
r={\rm mdr}(f)
. Let us denote the total Tjurina number of
C
by
\tau(C)
.
Then the following two cases hold.

a)

If
r<d/2
, then
\tau(C)\leq\tau_{max}(d,r)=(d-1)(d-r-1)+r^{2}
and the equality holds if and only if the curve
C
is free.

b)

If
d/2\leq r\leq d-1
, then

\tau(C)\leq\tau_{max}(d,r)
,
where, in this case, we set

\tau_{max}(d,r)=(d-1)(d-r-1)+r^{2}-\binom{2r-d+2}{2}.

From now on we are working with conic-line arrangements
\mathcal{CL}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
that admits only ordinary quasi-homogeneous singularities. In particular, all ordinary singularities of multiplicity at most
4
are quasi-homogeneous
[
2
, Exercise 7.31]
.

Remark 2.7
.

Since we will only work with curves admitting ordinary quasi-homogeneous singularities, the Tjurina numbers of singular points are equal to the Milnor numbers
[
19
]
. Hence, we have the following chain of equalities:

\tau(C)=\sum_{p\in{\rm Sing}(C)}\tau_{p}=\sum_{p\in{\rm Sing}(C)}\mu_{p}=\sum_{r\geq 2}(r-1)^{2}n_{r},

where
n_{r}
denotes the number of
r
-fold intersection points, i.e., points in the plane where exactly
r
curves meet, and
\mu_{p}
denotes the Milnor number of a singular point
p\in C
.

Remark 2.8
.

In the light of the above remark, if
C=\{f=0\}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
is a reduced curve of degree
d
admitting only ordinary quasi-homogeneous singularities, then
C
is free if and only if
r={\rm mdr}(f)\leq(d-1)/2
and

(d-1)(d-r-1)+r^{2}=\sum_{r\geq 2}(r-1)^{2}n_{r}.

(1)

In this paper, we use the concept of weak combinatorics, which can be defined as data attached to a given conic-line arrangement, represented by a vector of the form:

C(d,k;n_{2},\ldots,n_{t}).

Here,
d
denotes the number of lines,
k
denotes the number of smooth conics,
n_{r}
denotes, as above, the number of
r
-fold ordinary intersections, and
t
is equal to the maximal multiplicity among singular points
p\in{\rm Sing}(\mathcal{CL})
.

3
Constraints on weak-combinatorics of
\mathscr{M}
-arrangements of conics and lines

We present our main combinatorial description of
\mathscr{M}
-arrangements of conics and lines with ordinary singularities.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{CL}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
be an arrangement of
k\geq 1
conics and
d\geq 3
lines with ordinary singularities of multiplicity at most
4
. Assume that
\mathcal{CL}
is an
\mathscr{M}
-arrangement.

(i)

If
d=2\ell
for some
\ell\geq 2
, then

n_{2}+2n_{3}+3n_{4}=(k+\ell)^{2}+\ell-k-3.

(ii)

If
d=2\ell+1
for some
\ell\geq 1
, then

n_{2}+2n_{3}+3n_{4}=(k+\ell)^{2}+2\ell-1.

Proof.

Firstly, let us consider the case
d=2\ell\geq 4
. Recall that by
[
8
, Theorem 4.3]
we have

n_{2}+2n_{3}+3n_{4}=\tau(\mathcal{CL})=3(k+\ell)^{2}-3(k+\ell)+3.

After applying Bézout’s Theorem to
\mathcal{CL}
, we get the following naïve combinatorial count:

4\cdot\binom{k}{2}+4k\ell+\binom{2\ell}{2}=n_{2}+3n_{3}+6n_{4},

(2)

Let us rewrite this count as follows:

4(k^{2}-k)+8k\ell+2\ell(2\ell-1)=2n_{2}+6n_{3}+12n_{4}=\tau(\mathcal{CL})+n_{2}+2n_{3}+3n_{4}.

We get

4(k^{2}-k)+8k\ell+2\ell(2\ell-1)-\tau(\mathcal{CL})=\\
4(k^{2}-k)+8k\ell+2\ell(2\ell-1)-3(k+\ell)^{2}+3(k+\ell)-3=(k+\ell)^{2}+\ell-k-3,

and hence

(k+\ell)^{2}+\ell-k-3=n_{2}+2n_{3}+3n_{4},

which completes the proof of the first case.

Now we pass to the second case where
d=2\ell+1\geq 3
. We recall that by
[
8
, Theorem 4.8]
one has

\tau(\mathcal{CL})=3(k+\ell)^{2}+1.

We use again Bézout’s Theorem applied to
\mathcal{CL}
obtaining the naïve combinatorial count:

4\cdot\binom{k}{2}+2k(2\ell+1)+\binom{2\ell+1}{2}=n_{2}+3n_{3}+6n_{4},

(3)

and we get

4k^{2}+8k\ell+4\ell^{2}+2\ell=\tau(\mathcal{CL})+n_{2}+2n_{3}+3n_{4}.

After some manipulations we finally arrive at

(k+\ell)^{2}+2\ell-1=n_{2}+2n_{3}+3n_{4},

which completes the proof.
∎

Example 3.2
.

Consider the following combinatorics
C(d,k;n_{2},n_{3},n_{4})=(7,1;5,4,3)
. Using Theorem
3.1

(ii)
with
\ell=3
, we see that

22=5+4\cdot 2+3\cdot 3=n_{2}+2n_{3}+3n_{4}\neq(k+\ell)^{2}+2\ell-1=4^{2}+6-1=21,

hence this combinatorics cannot lead to an
\mathscr{M}
-curve.

Example 3.3
.

Let us consider
\mathcal{CL}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
given by the following defining polynomial

f(x,y,z)=xy(x+y-z)(x^{2}-xz+5y^{2}-5yz)(x^{2}+2y^{2}-xz-2yz).

The arrangement
\mathcal{CL}
has weak combinatorics
C(3,2;1,0,3)
and it is known to be free with exponents
(d_{1},d_{2})=(2,4)
, see
[
20
, Example 2.1]
, and hence
\mathcal{CL}
is an
\mathscr{M}
-arrangement. Since
\ell=1
we can check that

10=n_{2}+2n_{3}+3n_{4}=(k+\ell)^{2}+2\ell-1=3^{2}+2-1=10,

which justifies Theorem
3.1

(ii)
.

Now we would like to study a special case, namely we consider
\mathscr{M}
-arrangements consisting of
d
lines and just one conic. Our result provides a complete set of constraints that allow us to find all weak-combinatorics of such arrangements.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{CL}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
be an
\mathscr{M}
-arrangement of
d\geq 3
lines and one conic with only ordinary singularities of multiplicity at most
4
.

(i)

If
d=2\ell\geq 4
, then
n_{2}+n_{3}=3\ell-6
and
n_{3}+3n_{4}=\ell^{2}+3
.

(ii)

If
d=2\ell+1\geq 3
, then
n_{2}+n_{3}=3\ell-2
and
n_{3}+3n_{4}=\ell^{2}+\ell+2
.

Proof.

In the case
(i)
our naïve combinatorial count (
2
) has the form

2\ell^{2}+3\ell=n_{2}+3n_{3}+6n_{4}.

From Theorem
3.1

(i)
, we get
n_{2}+2n_{3}+3n_{4}=\ell^{2}+3\ell-3
, and after combining it with the above combinatorial count we arrive at
n_{3}+3n_{4}=\ell^{2}+3
. Using again the naïve combinatorial count, we obtain

2\ell^{2}+3\ell=n_{2}+3n_{3}+6n_{4}=n_{2}+n_{3}+2(n_{3}+3n_{4})=n_{2}+n_{3}+2\ell^{2}+6,

and hence
n_{2}+n_{3}=3\ell-6
, which gives us the desired formula. We proceed analogously for the case
(ii)
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

The above result indeed allows us to find all weak-combinatorial types of
\mathscr{M}
-arrangements with just one conic. For instance, if
d=6
, then the following system of equations must be solved:

\begin{cases}n_{2}+n_{3}=3\\
n_{3}+3n_{4}=12\end{cases}.

There are exactly two solutions solutions in
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{3}
, namely
(n_{2},n_{3},n_{4})\in\{(3,0,4),(0,3,3)\}
.

Next, we focus on possible obstructions to the number of singular points of
\mathcal{CL}
situated on a smooth conic
C\in\mathcal{CL}
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{CL}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
be an
\mathscr{M}
-arrangement of
d\geq 3
lines and one conic
C
having only ordinary singularities of multiplicity at most
4
. Denote by
\mathcal{CL}^{\prime}=\mathcal{CL}\setminus\{C\}
and let
r=|\mathcal{CL}^{\prime}\cap C|
.

a)

Assume that
d=2\ell\geq 4
.

(\bullet)

If
r=2m
, then the only possible values for
m
are
m=\ell+2
or
m\leq\ell-1
.

(\bullet\bullet)

If
r=2m+1
, then
m\leq\ell-2
.

b)

Assume that
d=2\ell+1\geq 3
.

(\bullet)

If
r=2m
, then the only possible values for
m
are
m=\ell+2
or
m\leq\ell
.

(\bullet\bullet)

If
r=2m+1
, then
m\leq\ell-1
.

Proof.

We are going to use
[
11
, Theorem 1.4 (i)]
. Recall that if
\mathcal{CL}
is an
\mathscr{M}
-arrangement with
d\geq 3
lines and one conic
C
, then either

1.

d=2\ell
and
\mathcal{CL}
is free with the exponents
(\ell-1,\ell+2)
, or

2.

d=2\ell+1
and
\mathcal{CL}
is free with the exponents
(\ell,\ell+2)
.

With the above data in hand, we can apply the aforementioned result directly.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

It is worth emphasizing directly that the number of intersection points
r=|\mathcal{CL}^{\prime}\cap C|
plays a crucial role and we are going to explain it now using the so-called addition technique. Assume that
\mathcal{L}
is an
\mathscr{M}
-arrangement of
d\geq 3
lines that is not a pencil and let
C
be a conic such that
2d=|\mathcal{L}\cap C|
, the maximal possible number with according to Bézout’s Theorem. Let
\mathcal{CL}:=\mathcal{L}\cup C=\{f=0\}
and denote by
d_{1}\leq d_{2}
the first two smallest degrees of a minimal system of generators for the module of Jacobian syzygies
{\rm AR}(f)
. Now, according to
[
1
, Theorem 1.6]
, we have
d_{1}+d_{2}={\rm deg}(\mathcal{CL})+1
and hence
\mathcal{CL}
is
never
free.

4
Combinatorial Poincaré polynomials for
\mathscr{M}
-arrangements of conics and lines

In this section we would like to elaborate about combinatorial Poincaré polynomials for
\mathscr{M}
-arrangements of conics and lines with ordinary singularities. Let us recall the following definition.

Definition 4.1

(
[
18
, Definition 1.4]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{CL}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
be an arrangement of
k
conics and
d
lines such that it admits only ordinary intersection points. Then the combinatorial Poincaré polynomial of
\mathcal{CL}
is defined as

\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{CL};t)=1+(2k+d-1)t+\bigg(\sum_{r\geq 2}(r-1)n_{r}-d+1\bigg)t^{2}.

It was proved by the second author that if
\mathcal{CL}
is a free arrangement of
d
lines and
k
conics with only
ordinary quasi-homogeneous singularities
with the exponents
(d_{1},d_{2})
, then
\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{CL};t)
splits over the rationals, and we have

\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{CL};t)=(1+d_{1}t)(1+d_{2}t),

(4)

see
[
18
, Theorem 1.5]
.

Remark 4.2
.

Observe that if
k=0
, i.e., our arrangement consists of nothing but lines, then the combinatorial Poincaré polynomial coincides with the well-known Poincaré polynomial for line arrangements.

As was the case at the end of the previous section, the present section will concentrate on the case of
\mathscr{M}
-arrangements
\mathcal{CL}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
of
d
lines and one conic
C
with ordinary quasi-homogeneous singularities. The result below presents an explicit formula for the Poincaré polynomial of the line arrangement
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{CL}\setminus\{C\}
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{CL}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
be an
\mathscr{M}
-arrangement of
d\geq 3
lines and one conic with only ordinary singularities of multiplicity at most
4
. Consider the deletion arrangement
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{CL}\setminus\{C\}
and let
r=|C\cap\mathcal{L}|
– we assume that
r
is finite.

1.

If
d=2\ell\geq 4
, then
\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{L};t)=(\ell^{2}+\ell-2-r)t^{2}+(2\ell-1)t+1.

2.

If
d=2\ell+1\geq 3
, then
\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{L};t)=(\ell^{2}+2\ell-r)t^{2}+2\ell t+1.

Proof.

We will use a general result devoted to Poincaré polynomials
[
17
, Theorem 1.2]
, which can be formulated as follows. If
C_{1}\cup C_{2}
is a reduced plane curve admitting only quasi-homogeneous singularities and such that
|C_{1}\cap C_{2}|=r
is finite, then

\mathfrak{P}(C_{1}\cup C_{2};t)=\mathfrak{P}(C_{1};t)+\mathfrak{P}(C_{2};t)+t-1+(r-1)t^{2},

whereby, for a reduced plane curve
C
of degree
e
, its Poincaré polynomial is defined as

\mathfrak{P}(C;t):=1+(e-1)t+((e-1)^{2}-\tau(C))t^{2}.

Recall that if
C
is a smooth conic then
\mathfrak{P}(C;t)=1+t+t^{2}
, and hence we get

\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{CL};t)=\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{L};t)+2t+rt^{2}.

(5)

1.

Assume that
d=2\ell\geq 4
. Since
\mathcal{CL}
is an
\mathscr{M}
-arrangement we have the following identity:

\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{CL};t)=(1+d_{1}t)(1+d_{2}t)=(1+(\ell-1)\cdot t)(1+(\ell+2)\cdot t)=(\ell^{2}+\ell-2)t^{2}+(2\ell+1)t+1.

Plugging this into (
5
), we obtain

\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{L};t)=(\ell^{2}+\ell-2-r)t^{2}+(2\ell-1)t+1.

2.

Assume that
d=2\ell+1\geq 3
. We use again the fact that
\mathcal{CL}
is an
\mathscr{M}
-arrangement obtaining

\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{CL};t)=(1+d_{1}t)(1+d_{2}t)=(1+\ell\cdot t)(1+(\ell+2)\cdot t)=(\ell^{2}+2\ell)t^{2}+(2\ell+2)t+1,

hence

\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{L};t)=(\ell^{2}+2\ell-r)t^{2}+2\ell t+1.

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

By Proposition
3.6
, we know that if
\mathcal{CL}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
is an arrangement of
d=2\ell\geq 4
lines and one conic
C
such that
r=|C\cap\mathcal{L}|=2m
, where
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{CL}\setminus\{C\}
, then either
m=\ell+2
or
m\leq\ell-1
. Assume that
r=2\ell+4
, then by Theorem
4.3
we have

\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{L};t)=(\ell^{2}-\ell-6)t^{2}+(2\ell-1)t+1=(1+(\ell-3)t)(1+(\ell+2)t),

which suggests that
\mathcal{L}
might be a free arrangement with exponents
(\ell-3,\ell+2)
. On the other hand, if
\mathcal{L}
is an arrangement of
d=2\ell
lines with ordinary points of multiplicity at most
4
, then by
[
4
, Theorem 2.1]
we have

{\rm mdr}(\mathcal{L})\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot{\rm deg}(\mathcal{L})-2\geq\ell-2,

a contradiction.

The example below demonstrates that the bound
m\leq\ell-1
in Proposition
3.6

\textbf{a)}(\bullet)
is sharp, and the resulting arrangement
\mathcal{L}
in this case might be free.

Example 4.5

(
[
16
, p. 8]
)
.

Let us consider the arrangement
\mathcal{CL}_{1}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
given by the following defining polynomial

f(x,y,z)=xy(y+x-z)(y-x-z)(y+x+z)(y-x+z)(x^{2}+y^{2}-z^{2}).

We see that this arrangement has weak combinatorics
C(6,1;3,0,4)
.
Figure
1
presents a geometric realization of the mentioned arrangement.

Figure 1:
Arrangement
\mathcal{CL}_{1}
.

We see that
r=|C\cap\mathcal{L}|=4
. Using Theorem
4.3
, we get

\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{L};t)=(\ell^{2}+\ell-2-r)t^{2}+(2\ell-1)t+1=6t^{2}+5t+1=(1+2t)(1+3t),

which suggests that
\mathcal{L}
might be free. This is indeed the case and can be verified through a simple inspection.

Example 4.6

(
[
16
, p. 9]
)
.

Now we look at the odd case scenario where
d=2\ell+1
for some
\ell
. Consider the arrangement
\mathcal{CL}_{2}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
given by

f(x,y,z)=x(x-z)(x+z)(y-z)(y+z)(y-x)(y+x)(x^{2}+y^{2}-2z^{2}).

We see that this arrangement has weak combinatorics
C(7,1;5,2,4)
. Figure

5
A remark on the Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity of
\mathscr{M}
-curves

Here we would like to conclude our paper by providing precise values of the Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity of the
S
-modules
{\rm AR}(f)
and
M(f)
that are associate with
\mathscr{M}
-curves, and our observation should be viewed as a small contribution to a bigger picture presented in
[
6
]
. Recall that to any graded
S
-module
M
we can associated a coherent sheaf
\widetilde{M}
on
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
, the sheafification of
M
.

Definition 5.1
.

We say that a coherent sheaf
\widetilde{M}
is
m
-regular if

H^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}},\widetilde{M}(m-1))=H^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}},\widetilde{M}(m-2))=0.

The minimal
m
such that
\widetilde{M}
is
m
-regular is called the Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity of
\widetilde{M}
, and it is denoted by
{\rm reg}(\widetilde{M})
. We also set
{\rm reg}(M)={\rm reg}(\widetilde{M})
.

In order to present our main observation in this section, we need to recall some necessary notions, following the lines of
[
6
]
. Let
C=\{f=0\}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
be reduced plane curve. Consider the following submodule of Koszul-type relations
{\rm KR}(f)
, i.e., this is the submodule of
{\rm AR}(f)
generated by the following obvious relations of degree
d-1
, namely

(\partial_{y}f,-\partial_{x}f,0),\quad(\partial_{z}f,0,-\partial_{x}f),\quad(0,\partial_{z}f,-\partial_{y}f).

Define

ER(f)={\rm AR}(f)/{\rm KR}(f)

and

{\rm mdr}_{e}(f)={\rm min}\{r\in\mathbb{Z}:{\rm ER}(f)_{r}\neq 0\}.

If
{\rm mdr}(f)<d-1
then
{\rm mdr}_{e}(f)={\rm mdr}(f)
, and this situation occurs, for instance, if our curves are free.

Definition 5.2
.

For a reduced polynomial
f\in S_{d}
we define

1.

the coincidence threshold as

{\rm ct}(f)={\rm max}\{q:{\rm dim}\,M(f)_{k}={\rm dim}\,M(g)_{k}\text{ for all }k\leq q\},

where
g
is a homogeneous polynomial of
S
of the same degree
d
as
f
having the property that
C^{\prime}=\{g=0\}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
is a smooth curve.

2.

the stability threshold

{\rm st}(f)={\rm min}\{q:{\rm dim}\,M(f)_{k}=\tau(C)\text{ for all }k\geq q\}.

It turns our that if
C=\{f=0\}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
is a reduced singular curve, then

{\rm ct}(f)={\rm mdr_{e}}(f)+d-2.

Moreover, a nice relationship can be established using
{\rm ct}(f)
and
{\rm st}(f)
in the case where
C=\{f=0\}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
is free, namely in that scenario one has

{\rm ct}(f)+{\rm st}(f)=3(d-2).

(6)

Moreover, we have

{\rm st}(f)=3(d-2)-{\rm ct}(f)=2d-4-{\rm mdr}(f)=d-3+d_{2},

where
d_{2}
is the second exponent of the free curve
C
.
We will need the following result
[
6
, Theorem 2.6]
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
C=\{f=0\}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
be a reduced singular curve of degree
d
. Then the equality
{\rm reg}(M(f))={\rm st}(f)
holds if and only if
C
is free.

Finally, the following relation also holds
[
6
, p. 5]
:

{\rm reg}(M(f))={\rm reg}({\rm AR}(f))+d-3.

(7)

Now we are ready to formulate our main observation in this section.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
C=\{f=0\}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
be an
\mathscr{M}
-curve of degree
d
. Then

{\rm reg}(M(f))=\begin{cases}3m-2&\text{ if only }d=2m\geq 6,\text{ or}\\
3m-1&\text{ if only }d=2m+1\geq 5\end{cases}

and

{\rm reg}({\rm AR}(f))=m+1

regardless
d=2m\geq 6
or
d=2m+1\geq 5
.

Proof.

It is enough to observe that

{\rm reg}({\rm AR}(f))={\rm st}(f)-d+3=d_{2},

and the claim follows from the fact that if
C=\{f=0\}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
is an
\mathscr{M}
-curve of degree
d
, then
C=\{f=0\}
is free with exponents
(d_{1},d_{2})
, where

\displaystyle(d_{1},d_{2})=(m-2,m+1)

\displaystyle\text{ if only }d=2m\geq 6,\text{ or}

\displaystyle(d_{1},d_{2})=(m-1,m+1)

\displaystyle\text{ if only }d=2m+1\geq 5.

This gives us
{\rm reg}({\rm AR}(f))=m+1
, and then the values for
{\rm reg}(M(f))
follow.
∎
