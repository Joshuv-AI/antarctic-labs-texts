The prevalence of adverse drug reactions to antiretroviral therapy and associated factors among pediatric patients living with HIV in sub-Saharan African countries: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Bukola R Omotoso, Anthony A Olashore, Joseph B Sempa, Omotayo S Alaofin, Mukesh Dheda, Nomakhuwa E Tabane, Kadimo K Khutsafalo, Thabiso R Mofokeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe greatest burden of HIV infection is in sub-Saharan African countries. The burden of treatment failure among children living with HIV remains significantly high due to delayed diagnosis and antiretroviral therapy (ART) initiation, low ART adherence rate, virological non-suppression, and incidence of adverse drug reactions (ADRs) to ART, among other factors. Therefore, this systematic review aims to estimate the pooled prevalence of ADRs among HIV-positive pediatric patients on ART in sub-Saharan Africa, identify the types, and examine associated risk factors as documented in the literature.MethodsThis review is based on a systematic and extensive search of PubMed, Scopus, CINAHL, Web of Science, and EBSCOHost. Data were extracted from the included studies using the data extraction form designed by the team. A meta-analysis was done using R statistics, version 4.3.2.ResultsA total of nine articles were included. The pooled estimate of ADRs among children on ART was statistically significant 26% (95% CI 0.15, 0.36; P-value < 0.001) with high heterogeneity. The types of ADRs reported in the included studies are gastrointestinal, dermatological, central nervous system, hematological, liver pathology, metabolic disorder, cardiovascular, and hormonal imbalance symptoms. Some of the factors associated with the occurrence of ADRs include treatment duration, the combination of ART used, sex, age, malnutrition, and compliance. The pooled prevalence of mortality among children on ART was statistically significant, with high heterogeneity.ConclusionThe review highlighted the prevalence and factors associated with ADRs in HIV-positive children on ART in the SSA countries. As the SSA countries battle to achieve the UNAIDS 95-95-95 targets in this vulnerable population, preliminary insights from this review can help inform policies on integrating spontaneous reporting of ADRs and pharmacovigilance into the service delivery component of pediatric ART. This intervention will support patient retention in care, as ADRs are a significant cause of medication non-adherence.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12107-7.Keywords: Adverse drug reactions, Antiretroviral therapy, Antiretroviral drugs, Pediatric, Sub-Saharan Africa

Introduction

Despite the remarkable progress towards achieving the UNAIDS 95-95-95 (Joint United Nations Program on HIV/AIDS) targets in people living with HIV, the pediatric population seems to be lagging, especially in developing countries such as sub-Saharan Africa. Notably, sub-Saharan Africa bears the highest burden of pediatric HIV globally. Additionally, the challenges of treatment failure among children living with HIV remain significantly high due to delayed diagnosis and antiretroviral therapy (ART) initiation, low ART adherence rate, virological non-suppression, and incidence of adverse drug reactions (ADRs) to ART, among other factors [1]. The advent of antiretroviral drugs (ARVs) has brought hope to people living with HIV and has changed the face of HIV/AIDS disease among pediatric patients [2]. However, despite the effectiveness of ARVs, adverse drug reactions (ADRs) are a major challenge associated with ART [3]. ADRs associated with ART impose a significant problem on the healthcare system in Africa, with approximately 28.6% of reported cases [4]. Despite this, there is a significant lack of literature summarizing the evidence regarding the incidence of ADRs, their variability, and the factors that increase vulnerability among children living with HIV, especially in sub-Saharan Africa. The available review on ADRs mostly focuses on the adult population in Africa, indicating a prevalence of 28.6% [4]. Whilst only a few reviews have reported ADRs in children, they tend to focus on a single ARV [5] or the overall use of medications in children, including ARVs and other non-antiretroviral medications [6]. Furthermore, ADRs are a significant cause of nonadherence and regimen changes [3]. Even though many of the ADRs are preventable and manageable, ADRs contribute significantly to the mortality rate among people living with HIV/AIDS [2]. Recent studies have reported that ADRs are common among ART-naive children, though these are typically mild and temporary in nature [7, 8]. These include temporary gastrointestinal events such as nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea [7, 8].

Nonetheless, the more severe forms of ADRs reported in this population include renal toxicity and loss of bone mineral density [9, 10], hematological, neurological, dermatological, and hepatotoxic effects [8, 11]. The latter may be life-threatening and could result in death, hospital admission, significant disability or incapacity, and even a congenital anomaly or cancer [12]. Additionally, some ARVs precipitate psychiatric symptoms such as depression, anxiety, and insomnia, and may exacerbate pre-existing mental health conditions [13]. These neurological symptoms may affect medication adherence and treatment retention, although this is less well-documented in pediatric populations [14]. Zidovudine is linked to adverse effects such as neutropenia, macrocytic anemia, and lipoatrophy. In addition, Abacavir, Ritonavir, Efavirenz, and Rilpivirine are associated with significant cardiovascular complications, including myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, and prolongation of the cardiac QTc interval [15, 16]. Protease inhibitors, such as Ritonavir-boosted Lopinavir, Darunavir, and Atazanavir, are typically linked to gastrointestinal disturbances, including diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, and increased risk of hepatitis [16, 17].

Various factors contribute to the differing susceptibility of individuals to ADRs, including age, duration of treatment, the severity of HIV infection, pre-existing metabolic conditions, nutritional status, as well as race, and ethnicity [11, 18]. Research indicates that children of Black or Hispanic/Latino ethnicity may exhibit a heightened risk of experiencing some ADRs [11]. Evidence from the literature has shown variations in the general population regarding the severity of ADRs among different ethnic groups due to differences in their genetic makeup [2]. For instance, a specific gene (HLA-B*57:01 allele) has been associated with an increased risk of an abacavir-induced hypersensitivity reaction [5]. However, its occurrence and frequency differ by ethnic group, with lower prevalence in Black Africans compared to White people [19, 20]. Therefore, it may be inappropriate to generalize the rate and prevalence of ADRs globally. The variability, particularly regarding the influence of race and its relationship with genetic composition, complicates efforts to generalize the prevalence and factors associated with ADRs on a global scale, consequently presenting significant challenges in effectively addressing the issue. Thus, this systematic review aimed to estimate the prevalence of ADRs to ART among HIV-positive pediatric patients receiving ART in sub-Saharan Africa, identify the types of ADRs, and examine the associated risk factors as detailed in existing literature.

Methods

Protocol and registration

The protocol for this review was registered in PROSPERO (Registration ID CRD42024535907) (www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/). The title and the method of statistical analysis were modified based on the information available in the eligible studies.

Research question

What are the rates and associated risk factors of ADRs among pediatric patients on ART in sub-Saharan Africa? Authors use research questions to guide the search for related articles to identify concepts and research gaps to guide practice.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

The review includes studies written in the English language. All articles published or available from the earliest identifiable date through the databases till June 19, 2024, were included. The inclusion criteria were as follows: We included studies if they were (1) observational studies conducted in SSA countries using a cohort (prospective or retrospective) study design to describe ADRs to ART in pediatric patients. (2) Studies examining ADRs in children living with HIV < 19 years old who received standard first-line or second-line ART at any type of health facility that followed national treatment guidelines; and (3) studies addressing adults and children were considered, provided data for children are reported separately.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they were clinical trials, case-control, case reports, case series, reviews, qualitative studies, editorials, commentaries, or letters to the editor to avoid some level of methodological heterogeneity. Case-control studies were excluded due to susceptibility to selection bias. Clinical trial studies were excluded as the review focused on primary studies from real-life experience. Other excluded studies include those that do not report on ADRs, were not conducted in SSA countries, or were not published in English; the latter considerations being informed by potential bias arising from translation requirements and the predominance of indexed manuscripts in the English language. We also excluded grey literature owing to its lack of peer review, which raises concerns regarding its quality and reliability, as well as the difficulties associated with accessing it and its potential to introduce bias into systematic reviews.

Information sources and search strategy

This systematic review was conducted according to PRISMA guidelines (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) [21] (Fig. 1). This review was based on a systematic and extensive search of PubMed, Scopus, CINAHL, Web of Science, and EBSCOHost databases, as shown in the Appendix. A health sciences librarian conducted electronic searches of the included databases. We also extensively searched the reference lists of the extracted articles for any further relevant publications. Only Primary studies that address the review question were included. Clinical trial studies, Case studies, reviews, letters to the editors, conference abstracts, or studies containing incomplete or irrelevant data were excluded. The Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text search terms consist of: “antiretroviral,” “HIV treatment,” “paediatric,” “pediatric,” “child,” “children,” “adolescents,” “adverse drug reactions,” “adverse events,” “mortality,” “admission,” “hospitalization,” and “Africa.” (the full Boolean search string and date each database was searched are shown in the Appendix).

Selection process

All articles from the database searches were exported to a library on the EndNote reference manager (EndNote X21). Duplicates were identified and removed using the automated duplicate detection tool in EndNote X21, followed by manual review. The principal investigator (BRO) and second screener (OSA) did the initial title screening. The abstract and full articles of studies included at the title screening stage were guided by the eligibility criteria. The EndNote library was shared with a second screener (OSA) for abstract and full article screening. Any discrepancies in the screeners’ results of abstract screening were resolved through discussion until an agreement was reached. Another author (AAO) served as an arbitrator and independently resolved unresolved discrepancies in abstract and full article screening. Records that did not meet the eligibility criteria were excluded. Authors of studies where full articles were not readily available were contacted for access to such articles. The University of the Free State library service was contacted to access articles that were not available as full-text online.

Data collection process

The research team designed an Excel spreadsheet to extract data from articles included in the review. Two investigators (the primary investigator, BRO, and a co-author, OSA) remotely extracted data on primary outcomes of interest from the included studies. Any discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through discussion and consensus. When disagreement persisted, a third investigator (AAO) reviewed the entries and provided arbitration. AAO independently validated the extracted data and coding before conducting the data analysis. This process ensured the consistency and reliability of the extracted data.

Data items

Details on the characteristics of the included studies (names of the first author and year of publication, study sample, study design, facility type, age, gender, sample with ADRs, type of ART implicated, type of ADRs, causality assessment, mortality rate, and factors associated with ADRs) were extracted. ADRs, defined as unfavorable responses to ARVs, serve as the outcome variable. This variable and its associated factors, including mortality, were extracted as documented in the selected studies. No standardized definition was used across studies due to variability in reporting practices.

Risk of bias assessment

We assessed the risk of bias across studies using the MMAT tool and visualized potential publication bias using funnel plots, focusing primarily on selection and measurement bias. Full-text articles that meet the eligibility criteria undergo a critical appraisal process. The major aim of the appraisal is to assess the credibility of the articles, exclude articles that are not of high quality that may compromise the validity of the study, and identify the strengths and limitations of the studies to be included in the systematic review. Appraisal of selected studies also ensures that the risk of bias is identified to minimize the likelihood of incorporating biased or misleading findings. This study adopted the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT appraisal tool) for critical appraisal. The quality appraisal of the studies included was conducted using screening criteria for all studies (Table 1). Quantitative descriptive criteria of the MMAT-Version 2018 were used for the included studies [22]. The appraisal was conducted by three authors: BRO, OSA, and AAO. Studies were included if the response to the two screening questions was ‘YES.’ The appropriate categories of the studies to appraise were chosen based on the study type. Each category was rated according to how the criterion was met. ‘Yes’ was selected if the criterion was met, ‘No’ was selected if the criterion was not met, and ‘Can’t tell’ was selected if there was insufficient information in the paper to judge if the criterion was met or not. All discrepancies in the appraisal were resolved through meetings and discussions.

Effect measures and synthesis methods

Two reviewers (BRO and JBS) did statistical analysis and data synthesis. The meta-analysis utilized combined studies to estimate the prevalence of ADRs or ADR-associated reactions. Statistical analysis was conducted using R statistics, version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A random-effects meta-analysis was performed to accommodate differences both between and within studies. Relative weight, based on the variances within and between studies, was applied to each random-effects meta-analysis. Heterogeneity was evaluated using two approaches: a scale with thresholds at 25%, 50%, and 75% for low, intermediate, and high inconsistency, respectively, among the study findings, and a level of statistical significance, where p < 0.05 indicates variation in the true effects [23]. A non-significant p-value for heterogeneity suggests that observed variability may be due to chance, but low statistical power in small meta-analyses may also explain this. The results from the meta-analysis were summarized as an overall proportion and 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Results

Literature search and selection process

The search identified 689 studies that were exported to EndNote, where 345 duplicates were removed. A total of 344 articles were screened based on title and abstract, and 70 studies were screened in full text based on eligibility criteria. We also conducted a hand search of the reference lists of all included studies, where we identified 61 additional studies (these articles were screened separately and excluded based on the eligibility criteria). A total of 9 studies were included in this systematic review [2, 10, 14, 18, 24–28] (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

The majority of the studies were retrospective observational cohort studies (n = 7) [2, 10, 14, 18, 25, 26, 28], and only two were prospective cohort studies [24, 27]. The study period spanned from 2012 to 2019. Two studies were conducted in Uganda [14, 25], and two were conducted in Nigeria [18, 26]. Sample sizes of the included studies ranged from 80 to 382. Because the number of pediatric patients on ART is usually very small, sample size calculations were not feasible for the studies. A total of three studies (n = 3) were conducted in pediatric clinics [14, 24, 25], while three were conducted in teaching hospitals [10, 18, 26]. One of the remaining two studies was conducted in a general ART clinic [2], and the last was conducted in a research center [27]. All the studies included used patients’ medical records to identify ADRs (n = 9). In addition to the medical records, two other studies utilized self-reported side effects, clinical findings, and interviews to identify ADRs [24, 27]. Only two studies performed the causality assessment of the ADRs [10, 24] (Table 2). Palmar et al. were the only study that did not report the number of patients who reported ADRs [28]. Hence, this study was excluded from the analysis of the prevalence of ADRs but was included in the analysis of the mortality rate.

Prevalence of ADRs

Out of the nine included studies, one (n = 1) did not report the specific number of children who reported ADRs out of the total sample size [28]. Therefore, this study was excluded from the analysis of the prevalence of ADRs. The pooled estimate of ADRs among 1,529 children on ART was high, 26% (95% CI 0.15, 0.36) with high heterogeneity (I2 = 95.7%) (Fig. 3) [2, 10, 14, 18, 24–27]. Two articles were publications from the same country (Uganda), using patients from the same study center and authored by the same first author; however, the year of publication differed [14, 25]. Similarly, two articles were also published from the same country (Nigeria) by different groups of authors. However, in the latter, the study centers are located in different regions of Nigeria: specifically, the South-Western and North-Central regions [18, 26]. The overall age in these nine studies ranges from 2 months to 15 years (Table 2).

Types of ADRs reported in the included studies

One of the most reported ADRs is dermatological symptoms, such as skin rash, cutaneous reactions, mucosal abnormalities, pruritus, skin eruptions, nail discoloration, and hypersensitivity, which were also reported in six studies (n = 6) [2, 10, 14, 24–26] (Fig. 5). The pooled prevalence of dermatology symptoms among 1,326 children on ART was 42% (95% CI: 0.14–0.70; P-value = 0.003), also with high heterogeneity (I2 = 97.9%) (Fig. 5). This includes skin rash/Stevens-Johnson syndrome, systemic manifestation/pallor, cutaneous toxicity, nail pigmentation, hypersensitivity, and others (Table 2).

Following the dermatological symptoms were gastrointestinal symptoms (GIT), which included nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea, and anorexia. The pooled prevalence of GIT symptoms among 1,541 children on ART (n = 6 studies) [2, 10, 14, 24–26] (Fig. 4) was 40% (95%CI: 0.21–0.60; P-value < 0.001), also with high heterogeneity (I2 = 93.7%) (Fig. 4). The reported GIT symptoms include diarrhea, vomiting, nausea, abdominal pain, and others (Table 3).

Central nervous system (CNS) and nervous system symptoms (peripheral neuritis, anxiety, drowsiness, dizziness, headache, insomnia, difficulty staying asleep, abnormal dreaming) were reported in 4 studies [2, 10, 14, 26] (Fig. 6). The pooled prevalence of nervous system symptoms among 1,150 children on ART was 14% (95% CI: 0.00, 0.27; P-value = 0.046), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 92.1%) (Fig. 6). Nervous system symptoms include peripheral neuritis, amnesia, headache, dizziness, depression, and others (Table 3).

Hematology symptoms (anemia, serum creatinine, neutropenia) were reported in 5 studies (Fig. 7). The pooled prevalence of hematology symptoms among 1234 children on ART was 16% (95% CI: 0.06–0.26; P-value = 0.002) with high heterogeneity (I2 = 81.1%) [2, 10, 14, 25, 26] (Fig. 7). Hematology symptoms reported by the included studies include hematology parameters or laboratory findings such as anemia, hypoglycemia and high serum Creatinine levels (Table 3).

Liver pathology (Hepatitis, jaundice, liver functions elevated, transaminases elevated) was reported in 4 studies [2, 10, 14, 26] (Fig. 8). The pooled prevalence of liver pathology symptoms among 1150 children on ART was 7% (95% CI: 0.03–0.11; P-value = 0.001), with low heterogeneity (I2 = 40.5%) (Fig. 8). This includes elevated liver function, jaundice, and hepatitis (Table 3).

Metabolic disorders or complications were reported in two studies [10, 14] (Fig. 9). The pooled prevalence of metabolic disorders among 615 children on ART was 6% (95% CI: 0.02–0.10; P-value = 0.003), with zero heterogeneity (I2 = 0.00%) (Fig. 9). Metabolic disorders reported by included studies are lactic acidosis and lipodystrophy (Table 3).

Heart disease and hormonal imbalance were reported by only one study [14]. Similarly, mental health symptoms were only reported in one study [2], and chronic ADRs, such as bone and joint deformity, were reported in only one study [10]; thus, we could not estimate the pooled prevalence of these ADRs.

Types of antiretroviral drugs associated with ADRs in the included studies

The most implicated ARVs frequently associated with ADRs included the following: tenofovir, zidovudine, lamivudine, stavudine, emtricitabine, and didanosine from nucleoside reverse transcriptase (NRTIs), nevirapine, nelfinavir, and efavirenz from non-nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs) [2, 10, 18, 24, 26] (Table 2). These drugs were used in the guidelines adapted for the management of PLHIV (people living with HIV) in African settings during the study period.

Table 4 shows the list of ART regimens used in all included studies. Most of the included studies reported the overall ADRs in all the ART used in their cohort without reporting on specific ADRs associated with each of the ART combinations. Only a few of the included studies (n = 2) assessed the incidence of ADR in association with specific ART combinations. This makes it impossible to analyze the specific ART combinations associated with each ADR reported.

Factors associated with ADRs in the included studies

Regarding the factors associated with ADRs, most of the studies (n = 6) reviewed reported that treatment duration significantly affects the incidence of ADRs as most children reported the highest number of ADRs from the first two months of treatment initiation to 3 years after treatment initiation. Five studies (n = 5) reported that the combination of ART used determined the type and the incidence of ADRs. Four studies (n = 4) reported that sex and age have a significant impact on the occurrence of ADRs. Two studies (n = 2) identified malnutrition and compliance as significant factors influencing the incidence of ADRs. Only one study (n = 1) showed children of seronegative tutors (often mothers) being susceptible to a higher risk of ADRs [10] (Table 2).

Assessment of mortality

Four studies (n = 4) assessed the mortality rate in their cohort [14, 25, 27, 28]. The pooled prevalence of mortality among 2807 children on ART was 5% (95% CI: 0.02–0.08; P-value < 0.001), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 89.6%) (Fig. 10). The prevalence range for mortality of children on ART as reported in individual studies was between 0.8% to 8.3% (Table 2).

Discussion

Our findings revealed that there were few studies available on the incidence of ADRs in children in SSA countries, with nine studies from seven countries; of these, eight reported on the prevalence of ADR, while four reported on mortality. The pooled prevalence of ADRs in children on ART was 26% (95% CI: 0.15–0.36). This observed prevalence is comparable to the 26.5% reported among the adult population in a recent systematic review of ART-related ADR from Africa [4]. The findings from this review are higher compared to the overall estimated proportion of ADRs (2.9%) in children, irrespective of the medicine, in a global meta-analysis conducted in 2012 [6]. Although it remains notably high, the pooled prevalence in this review must be interpreted with caution due to its heterogeneity. Additionally, evidence from the literature, encompassing studies from both developed and developing countries, suggests that most existing reports on ADRs related to ART primarily focus on the adult population, with limited data concerning children and adolescents [29]. For instance, in Moirana et al., the pediatric population was underrepresented, as only two out of eighteen studies focused on pediatric patients [4]. Assessing the safety and efficacy of therapeutic drugs for pediatric patients has been challenging due to the notable limitations of their inclusion in clinical trial studies [30]. While some ADRs of the existing ART are already known, the potential long-term impact of some newly developed drugs is still unknown, especially in pediatric patients. Therefore, surveys on ADRs in pediatric patients are usually of utmost importance to increase the amount of safety data [31].

The prevalence rates from the included papers are diverse, ranging from as low as 14% in Mali [24] to 48% in Nigeria [18], and can be attributed to several factors. This includes differences in methodology (prospective vs. retrospective studies), types of ART combinations used in each setting, study period, definition of ADRs, definition of the outcome (ADR), and method of ADR detection. In a recent report from India, Ray and co-authors indicated that the high incidence of 45% in their cohort is attributable to active surveillance and intensive follow-up of children to detect ADRs [8]. Two retrospective studies from different regions demonstrated similar rates. Oshikoya et al. reported a rate of 48% in Nigeria [18], while Mutwa and colleagues, employing the same methodology, documented a rate of 42% [27]. Notably, only two of the nine studies from sub-Saharan African countries performed causality assessments for adverse reactions to antiretroviral drugs; both of these studies reported comparable incidence rates [10, 24]. Identification of ADRs based solely on patients’ self-reports or medical record reviews without causality assessment may be confounded with clinical conditions associated with HIV/AIDS symptoms, particularly in children presenting at advanced stages of infection. Occasionally, ADRs may be overlooked even by healthcare professionals within hospital settings [32]. Therefore, studies employing active surveillance and spontaneous reporting of ADRs are likely to yield more accurate estimations of ADR incidence.

The spectrum of ADRs reported in the meta-analyzed studies varies, depending on the ART regimen used. Dermatological and gastrointestinal ADRs were the most commonly reported in all the included studies. According to the report from Mali, nausea was the most common ADRs [24]. This corroborates reports from a study on Indian children where gastrointestinal symptoms were the most commonly reported ADRs, especially diarrhea, which were linked to LPV/ritonavir-based ART regimens [8]. Dermatological disorders such as skin rash were noted in six of the included studies (Fig. 5); some of these were from Ethiopia [2], Mali [24], and Nigeria [18]. This aligns with earlier authors from the United Kingdom who reported ADRs caused by nevirapine-based ART combinations, with severe rash being the most common ADR [33]. Also, in agreement with our findings, a study conducted among Indian children reported skin rash and gastrointestinal symptoms as the most common ADRs, especially diarrhea, which were associated with LPV/ritonavir-based ART regimens [8]. A few studies reported mild neurological ADR such as headache, dizziness, and mild elevation of liver enzymes, like transaminases. These variabilities were likely due to the study year, which also significantly influenced the differences, as pediatric ART guidelines are updated based on ADR reports while seeking well-tolerated regimens that consistently suppress viral replication in the long term in children. For instance, stavudine (D4T) was removed from the market after being associated with long-term toxicity, such as lipoatrophy and lactic acidosis [2]; this was replaced by an AZT-based regimen, thereby increasing the number of patients on the AZT combination.

Interestingly, in line with the literature [7, 8], it was observed that most reported ADRs were mostly mild and transient, which may explain the low mortality observed in this review. This is attributable to the fact that severe ADRs, such as worsened cardiac conditions, immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS), and metabolic disorders, were seldom reported by a limited number of studies [14, 27].

Unfortunately, we were unable to estimate the proportion of ADRs based on specific ART regimens due to the retrospective nature of the study and the reporting patterns observed in the included data studies. Moreover, most of the authors reported overall ADRs irrespective of the ART regimen. Nevertheless, the most implicated regimens for ADRs in studies from SSA countries include AZT + 3TC + NVP [2, 18], AZT + 3TC + NVP/EFV [24], 3TC + AZT + NFV, 3TC + D4T + NFV, and 3TC + DDI + NFV [10] (Table 3). It is important to note that most of these regimens are not currently recommended; this is due to the introduction of dolutegravir (DTG)-based ART for children. We observed that the combination of ART used in the study cohorts significantly contributed to the types of ADRs. In the included studies, the nevirapine-based regimen is commonly associated with dermatological and cutaneous ADRs such as skin rashes [18, 25, 34]. The zidovudine-based regimen is linked to hematological ADRs, including anemia [10, 25], as well as gastrointestinal ADRs like vomiting [34], while efavirenz-based therapy is associated with psychiatric symptoms such as depression and anxiety, which could worsen adherence and increase new infection.

The factors associated with the development of ADRs in pediatric patients on ART also vary based on baseline information, such as clinical presentation at the initiation of ART and the study duration. Noticeably, most ADRs occur during the first few months or years of ART initiation in all included studies. Depending on the follow-up period, the occurrence of ADR decreases, and pediatric patients become more stable as they continue on ART; thus, treatment duration plays a significant role in the incidence of ADRs. Only four of the included studies reported a higher susceptibility in females [18, 24, 26, 28]. Whilst we cannot explain the reasons behind this association, some authors opined that it might be due to increased clinical attention paid to girls due to previous reports of more ADRs in adult females than in males [28]. Physiological differences, including hormones, may also play a role in explaining drug metabolism differences, as reported in adults [35, 36]. Nonetheless, this might be speculative, and the relationship will need further investigation. Additionally, malnutrition with underlying severe immunodeficiency [18, 25] and consistent medication adherence have been suggested to be associated with elevated risks of ADRs with serious consequences. For instance, two studies, Allabi et al. [10] and Oumar et al. [24], reported high incidences of ADRs in children with good adherence. While the reasons behind these isolated reports are not fully understood, it has been established that inconsistencies in ART can lead to viral resistance to the first-line regimen, thereby further limiting the available treatment options for this demographic. Hence, this association should be further explored.

Only four included studies assessed the mortality rate in their cohort [14, 25, 27, 28]. Due to the retrospective design of these studies, detailed information about the cause and timing of death was not reported by most of the included studies. These studies only suggested the possible causes of mortality based on the recorded diagnosis at the time of death, which may not be directly attributable to ADR. These include tuberculosis, malnutrition, pneumonia, anemia, HIV-associated nephropathy, persistent diarrhea, lymphoma, and immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS). Only one of the included studies with a longitudinal design reported IRIS as a cause of death in one patient after one month of starting Nevirapine combination [27]. Although we could not estimate the common cause of mortality related to ADR from a single study, this review highlights that IRIS may be a factor to consider, particularly in cases of late ART initiation. This further emphasizes the importance of early detection of HIV infection and initiation of treatment. Nonetheless, the lack of research in the SSA that directly evaluates ADR-related mortality reveals a huge gap in the literature and emphasizes the need for more long-term studies to investigate this relationship.

Study limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the study was restricted to articles published in English, potentially excluding relevant research conducted in French- or Portuguese-speaking countries in the SSA region. Second, findings from this review are not necessarily representative of the entire SSA region due to the limited number of studies and, more importantly, high heterogeneity among the included studies caused by inconsistent ADR definitions and varying methodological designs; therefore, results should be interpreted with caution. The explanation provided for the sex difference is speculative due to the limited studies and a lack of rigorous statistical analysis; this finding should also be interpreted with caution and requires further investigation.

Conclusion

There remains a significant paucity of literature concerning the prevalence and factors associated with ADRs in HIV-pediatric population on ART in SSA, as the existing reports are limited and highly heterogeneous, primarily due to the absence of a standardized definition and their reliance predominantly on patient records. However, the prevalence of ADRs to ART in children is highly significant in the meta-analyzed studies and is comparable to what was reported in the adult population. The most frequently reported ADRs in SSA, consistent with trends in other developed countries, include disorders such as dermatological reactions and gastrointestinal disturbances, which are generally mild, short-lived, and amenable to treatment. Zidovudine- and nevirapine-based regimens are the primary drugs associated with ADRs, while malnutrition, female gender, and younger age are significant factors associated with the occurrence of ADRs. There is also a considerable gap in the literature regarding mortality attributable to ADRs among children in SSA, with only one study identifying and linking IRIS as a major cause, particularly in the context of advanced HIV infection due to late treatment initiation. Therefore, more insights into the prevalence of ADRs and associated ARV therapies are needed, as this may facilitate the development of well-tolerated treatment regimens and improve patient adherence, especially as SSA strives to attain the UNAIDS 95-95-95 targets. This will involve standardizing ADR definitions and strengthening data systems to enhance the quality and comparability of reporting across countries in the SSA region. Finally, facility-level ART program managers should ensure the early detection and reporting of ADRs, particularly during the initial months of ART initiation, as this may help reduce the occurrence of ADRs and support patient retention in care, given that ADRs are a significant cause of medication non-adherence.
