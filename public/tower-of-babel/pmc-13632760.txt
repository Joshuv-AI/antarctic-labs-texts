Harnessing interfacial kosmotropic effect for reversible quasi-solid-state zinc-iodine batteries
Authors: Yicai Pan, Lutong Shan, Jiapei Li, Yongqiang Yang, Kunlun Liu, Yongyu Liu, Dongyu Feng, Tian Zhang, Chuhao Luan, Pan Xue, Xiaodong Shi, Mingzhan Wang, Guo Hong, Wenjun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Simultaneously achieving fast kinetics and interfacial stability remains a formidable challenge for quasi-solid-state zinc-iodine batteries. Herein, we introduce the interfacial kosmotropic effect via a low-cost CaSO4·2H2O ionic matrix to actively reconstruct the interfacial chemistry. Through the precise regulation of the electrical double layer, the strong electrostatic fields from surface-bound kosmotropic ions organize limited water molecules into a low-activity, confined network, fundamentally reshaping the solvation structure. Benefiting from this optimized atomic-level architecture, the reaction pathways are fundamentally altered: on the cathode, it unlocks a single-step I−/I2 redox process that effectively severs the formation pathway of polyiodides; on the anode, it induces coordinatively unsaturated structures to accelerate desolvation and suppress parasitic reactions. Consequently, the system circumvents intrinsic kinetic limitations and facilitates a transition from electric-field-driven electromigration to regulated diffusion-dominated transport. This optimizes ion flux and enables stable Zn anode operation for >1000 h (99.7% avg. Coulombic efficiency). The Zn-I2 full cells deliver a high areal capacity of 0.91 mAh cm−2 with 93% capacity retention after 2500 cycles at 1.0 mA cm−2. Impressively, this strategy enables a minimalist iodine-free cathode design and successfully scales to pouch cells (stable for >600 cycles), offering a paradigm shift for developing safe, high-rate and durable quasi-solid-state batteries.

INTRODUCTION

Aqueous zinc-iodine (Zn-I2) batteries are rapidly emerging as a research hotspot in energy storage, owing to their intrinsic safety, low cost, straightforward reaction mechanism and high theoretical energy density (1). This system is based on two highly efficient and reversible redox couples, I−/I2 and Zn2+/Zn, which avoid complex transition pathways and consequently exhibits extremely low polarization and remarkable energy efficiency (2). However, its practical application is still confronted with severe challenges at both electrode interfaces. Iodide (I−), a classic chaotrope, can disrupt the water’s hydrogen-bond network. This disordered and more fluid solvent environment lowers the energetic penalty for dissolving the large, low-polarity iodine (I2) and polyiodide (I3−) species, thereby facilitating the formation of highly mobile polyiodides and exacerbating the shuttle effect (3–6). As a result, the cathode suffers from the consumption of active materials due to the formation of soluble polyiodides (7). On the anode side, two primary degradation mechanisms are at play: Non-uniform zinc deposition leads to dendrite growth and potential short circuiting, while the polyiodide species shuttling from the cathode causes continuous chemical corrosion and irreversible capacity loss (1, 8). Collectively, these interfacial side reactions lead to rapid capacity decay and low Coulombic efficiency (CE) for the full battery.

To fundamentally address these issues, electrolyte engineering has become the central focus of research, aiming to regulate the chaotic ion-solvent environment (9). While early attempts involving simple additives in aqueous electrolytes offered singular benefits, such as complexing polyiodides or constructing a solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) on the anode, they struggled to provide a comprehensive remedy for the intertwined issues plaguing both electrodes (7, 10–12). Subsequently, more advanced strategies such as high-concentration (HCEs), hydrogel and conventional quasi-solid/solid-state electrolytes were developed to counter these issues (13–15). While they achieve stabilization by either chemically reducing free water activity (as in HCEs) or physically blocking dendrites (as in solid-state and hydrogel electrolytes), they face an intrinsic trade-off between stability and kinetics. HCEs and solid-state electrolytes inherently suffer from sluggish ionic conductivity (15). In contrast, while hydrogel electrolytes maintain higher conductivity, they function merely as passive physical barriers rather than active solvation modulators; the water within their pores remains largely bulk-like and reactive, rendering them ineffective at fundamentally suppressing interfacial side reactions.

To fundamentally resolve the aforementioned trade-offs, we introduce the interfacial kosmotropic effect to actively and precisely regulate interfacial chemistry. Defined as the utilization of surface-bound high-charge-density ions (e.g. Ca2+, Al3+, Mg2+, SO42−, PO43−) to organize limited water molecules into ordered hydration shells (4), this effect lies at the core of our proposed solvation-regulating quasi-solid-state battery based on a low-cost CaSO4·2H2O ionic matrix (Fig. 1A). Unlike passive ionic conductors, this design redefines the electrolyte as an active architect of the interfacial environment. Within this water-deficient system, strong electrostatic fields from the ionic matrix construct a confined, low-activity water network within the electric double layer (EDL). This enables active regulation of the solvation structure (Fig. 1B), fundamentally distinguishing it from the chaotic bulk water environment of traditional aqueous systems and establishing the foundation for simultaneously enhanced stability and kinetics.

This structured interfacial environment fundamentally remodels the reaction pathways at both electrodes (Fig. 1C). For the anode, the CSO system promotes a unique desolvation pathway dominated by a coordination structure [Zn(SO4)(H2O)4] contact ion pairs. Compared to the sluggish kinetics in AQ systems, this significantly lowers the desolvation energy barrier, enabling uniform zinc deposition (Fig. 1C, left); For the cathode, the ordered water layer facilitates a concise, single-step charge transfer process: desolvated I− ions are directly converted into solid-phase I2 via in-situ transformation. By synergistically suppressing the dissolution of electrogenerated iodine (I2), this effectively severs the formation pathway of soluble polyiodides (I3−) inherent to the multi-step reactions in AQ systems (Fig. 1C, right). Consequently, this strategy delivers exceptional performance, including stable Zn anode operation (>1000 h, 99.7% CE) and ultralong full-cell cycling (>2500 cycles with 93% capacity retention). Notably, this design enables a minimalist, iodine-free cathode configuration that circumvents complex pre-loading steps, and has been successfully scaled up to pouch cells, demonstrating immense potential for practical applications. These metrics not only surpass most reported quasi-solid-state Zn-based systems but also rival the kinetics of state-of-the-art aqueous counterparts, demonstrating that the trade-off between safety and performance is no longer insurmountable.

RESULTS

Reshaped Zn2+ solvation structure and interfacial chemistry in the CSO electrolyte

We selected sparingly soluble calcium sulfate dihydrate (CaSO4·2H2O, CSO) as the ionic matrix to leverage its unique interfacial kosmotropic effect, owing to its exceptional electrochemical stability and strong interaction with aqueous electrolytes. This effect arises from the strong electrostatic field generated by surface-bound kosmotropic ions (i.e., Ca2+ and SO42−) at the solid-liquid interface, which establishes a highly ordered, structured water layer. A standard aqueous solution containing 1 M ZnSO4 and 0.8 M KI (hereafter denoted as the AQ electrolyte, and AQ-1 + 0.8 in all figures) served as the reference. The quasi-solid-state electrolyte was prepared by homogeneously mixing CSO powder with the AQ electrolyte at weight-to-volume ratio of 5 g mL−1 (fig. S1). The resulting mixture was pressed into a pellet at 2 MPa, hereafter referred to as the CSO electrolyte (CSO-1 + 0.8 in figures). Laser confocal microscopy (LCM) confirmed that the pressed pellet possesses a flat and compact surface, which is crucial for ensuring optimal interfacial contact (fig. S2). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images revealed a significant morphological evolution from pristine, micro-scale CaSO4·2H2O blocks to the final CSO matrix, which is uniformly decorated with a layer of nanoparticles (fig. S3). Energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) and x-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses confirm that these nanoparticles are in a crystallized ZnSO4·6H2O phase, while the underlying CSO matrix remains structurally stable (figs. S3 and S4). This structural homogeneity, arising from the nanoscale integration of the components, is critical to the electrolyte’s function. Furthermore, the uniform deposition of nanoparticles, rather than segregated crystals, evidences the strong affinity between the CSO matrix and the liquid phase. This affinity, combined with the robust structural integrity derived from the matrix’s internal hydrogen-bond network, provides the foundation for a stable, liquid-accommodating quasi-solid-state system.

Spectroscopic characterization and thermal analysis collectively confirm the confinement of water molecules within the CSO matrix. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) reveals a higher Tpeak and narrower dehydration range in the CSO electrolyte compared to AQ, signifying enhanced water-binding strength (fig. S5). Compared to the aqueous electrolyte, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) revealed a significant sharpening of the O − H stretching vibration peak around 3400 cm−1 in the CSO system, preliminarily indicating enhanced uniformity and constraint of the water environment (Fig. 2A and fig. S6). Raman spectrum around 3400 cm−1 provided further compelling evidence. Unlike the AQ system, which features a single broad band at 3450 cm−1, the CSO system exhibits two distinct sharp vibrational peaks at 3420 cm−1 and 3480 cm−1 (Fig. 2B). These sharp peaks represent distinct, highly uniform and vibrationally constrained molecular environments, signifying the formation of an ordered hydration structure induced by the ionic matrix (16, 17). A deeper analysis of the H−O−H bending vibration at 1640 cm−1 revealed a detectable high-wavenumber shift in the CSO system, further confirming increased hydrogen bond strength within the system (Fig. 2C) (18). Collectively, these results demonstrate that ion-dipole interactions have reconstructed the hydrogen-bond network, effectively transforming highly active free water into low-activity, uniformly bound water (19, 20). To elucidate the interfacial structure of water molecules, we employed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to explore the water structure constrained by the CSO dihydrate ionic matrix (Fig. 2D and fig. S7). In the simulation setup, electrolyte particles were added proportionally into the solvation box, and the CSO dihydrate crystal was treated as a whole with surface atoms allowed to relax, facilitating the observation of interfacial water dynamics. The highly hydrophilic crystal structure exposes many polar coordination sites (surface-bound Ca2+ and SO42−). As highlighted in the pink-circled regions of the image, most water molecules are directly adsorbed onto the matrix surface via the hydrogen bond network (indicated by dashed lines), visually confirming the existence and strength of our proposed interfacial effect. This strong interaction is further supported by binding energy calculations, as discussed below. These water molecules are electrostatically bound; although this binding strength is weaker than that of crystalline water, it is clearly distinguished from the free water state.

Furthermore, this low-water-activity environment inevitably promotes direct ion-ion interactions. In the FT-IR spectra (Fig. 2A), the v3 peak corresponding to the asymmetric stretching vibration of SO42− exhibits a significant redshift from 1085 cm−1 to 1073 cm−1. This redshift provides direct spectroscopic evidence for the formation of contact ion pairs (CIPs), as the direct coordination of Zn2+ to the SO42− anion breaks its tetrahedral symmetry, weakening the S−O covalent bonds and lowering the asymmetric stretching vibration frequency (21). This indicates that ion-induced ordering not only confines water but also fundamentally reshapes interionic coordination chemistry by weakening cation hydration. MD simulations coupled with x-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) were applied to further probe atomic−scale solvation change. In contrast to the conventional hexa-hydrated [Zn(H2O)6]2+ shell (coordination number ≈ 6) in the AQ electrolyte, MD simulations reveal that the primary coordination environment in the CSO system evolves into an anion-coordinated configuration, yielding an average total Zn–O coordination number of approximately 5.7 (Fig. 2E and figs. S8 to S10). To experimentally corroborate this reshaping of the solvation structure, wavelet transform (WT) and Fourier transform extended x-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analyses were performed. As shown in Fig. 2F, the WT contour plots visually illustrate a significant distortion of the Zn–O coordination shell in the CSO system. The observed reduction in intensity and the diffusion of the contour profile strongly suggest the disruption of the originally highly symmetric hydration shell. The fitting curve of the EXAFS spectrum, based on the anion-coordination model proposed by MD simulations, demonstrates excellent agreement with the measured data, providing solid experimental evidence for the alteration of the inner-sphere solvation structure. Further combining electrostatic potential (ESP) analysis with MD statistical calculations (Fig. 2G), we identified neutral [Zn(SO4)(H2O)4] contact ion pairs, featuring bidentate sulfate coordination, as the dominant species. In this configuration, the number of water ligands decreases to four as one SO42− anion enters the inner shell. Meanwhile, the coexistence of coordinatively unsaturated [Zn(SO4)(H2O)3] species accounts for the statistically unsaturated total coordination number of ∼5.7. The dominance of these neutral [Zn(SO4)(H2O)4] CIPs fundamentally shifts interfacial transport from field-driven migration to a diffusion-dominated mechanism (22), a transition further corroborated by the regulated nucleation kinetics discussed in the following section. This behavior originates from the electrostatic neutrality of CIPs. Unlike charged [Zn(H2O)6]2+ ions that encounter intense electrostatic resistance within the local electric field of the inner Helmholtz plane (IHP) (23, 24), these neutral CIPs minimize direct electrostatic interactions in the EDL, significantly reducing migration resistance (22). Coupled with pre-weakened Zn–O bonds, this mechanism facilitates a low-energy desolvation pathway (Fig. 2H). Conversely, the conventional AQ system suffers from non-uniform deposition and dendritic growth. This is attributed to the kinetic mismatch between the rapid migration of positively charged [Zn(H2O)6]2+ and the sluggish desolvation process, which triggers localized ion accumulation (the tip effect).

Beyond these kinetic advantages, the electrolyte also exhibits significantly improved electrochemical stability. Thermodynamically, the strong water confinement widens the electrochemical stability window (ESW) to 2.69 V (Fig. 2I). Furthermore, beyond stabilizing the solvent itself, unique matrix-ion interactions synergistically regulate bulk ion transport. This is directly evidenced by the significantly enhanced Zn2+ transference number (tZn2+ of 0.62 in CSO vs. 0.54 in AQ) (fig. S11). Notably, this enhancement is a relative effect, primarily attributed to the restricted mobility of the SO42− anions. The surface Ca2+ and SO42− sites on the CSO matrix effectively restrict the migration of these anionic charge carriers through matrix-mediated anion anchoring and electrostatic coordination effects (25, 26), thereby increasing the relative contribution of Zn2+ to the total ionic current. Furthermore, the interference from dissolved Ca2+ on the tZn2+ measurement is negligible, as the common-ion effect restricts the concentration of trace dissolved Ca2+ (∼49.3 μM, calculated in note S1) four orders of magnitude lower than that of Zn2+. Figure 2J visually summarizes this optimization of the interfacial electrical double layer structure: the CSO system establishes an anion-involved, highly ordered IHP. This ordered interface not only thermodynamically shields against parasitic water reactions but also kinetically induces uniform Zn2+ desolvation and deposition on a planar surface.

Mechanism of Zn anode stabilization by the CSO electrolyte

To dissect the interfacial evolution of the Zn anode itself, we conducted electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis on symmetric cells (27). The results unambiguously reveal that the CSO system establishes a physically dominated, highly reversible interface characterized by low, stable and periodic impedance (Fig. 3A), whereas the AQ system exhibits a chemically dominated, irreversibly passivated interface where degradation begins immediately upon plating (Fig. 3B). First, in the high-frequency region (10−6 ∼ 10−4 s), the interfacial properties diverge significantly. Although the ohmic resistance (τ1) is comparable, the AQ spectrum is dominated from the outset by a persistent, high-impedance passivation layer (τ2). Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) depth profiling corroborates this, confirming the AQ interface is rich in passivation species (e.g., ZnOH+, ZnOH− and ZnO−, indicative of Zn(OH)2/ZnO) (fig. S12). Second, in the mid-frequency region (10−4 ∼ 10−1 s), the contrast in core kinetics is even more profound. In the CSO system, the resistances for ion adsorption (τ3, Rads) and charge transfer (τ4, Rct) continuously decrease during the initial plating stage, indicating that the zinc deposition process creates a smoother, kinetically more favorable interface (12). Notably, these core kinetic resistances continue to decrease even during the subsequent stripping stage. Conversely, the passivation layer (τ2) in the AQ system physically obstructs the interfacial charge transfer process (τ4). Finally, in the low-frequency region (10−1 ∼ 100 s), the DRT analysis successfully decouples physical changes from chemical deterioration. In the CSO system, the rise in total impedance during stripping does not stem from kinetic degradation but is dominated by the constant phase element effect caused by surface roughening—a purely physical change (28–30). In the AQ system, however, the passivation layer also hinders ion diffusion (τ5), constituting an irreversible physical blockage driven by chemical byproducts.

To quantify the interfacial nucleation kinetics, nucleation overpotentials were evaluated across current densities from 0.2 to 4.0 mA cm−2 (Fig. 3D and fig. S13). As shown in Fig. 3C, the AQ system’s overpotential surges to 420 mV at 4.0 mA cm−2, while the CSO system consistently maintains much lower nucleation spikes of 190 mV at 4.0 mA cm−2 with highly reversible stripping/plating voltage profiles. This divergence originates from the electrostatic neutrality of CIPs; being less sensitive to the intense local electric field within the EDL, these species minimize direct electrostatic interactions with the electrode surface, thereby reducing migration resistance and ensuring a regulated ion flux for homogeneous in-situ nucleation (22). Conversely, the AQ system’s excessive overpotential (174 mV) triggers tip protrusions and electric field concentration, leading to uncontrolled dendritic surface expansion. To further quantify the desolvation kinetics, we performed temperature-dependent EIS analysis to calculate the activation energy (Ea) (Fig. 3E, fig. S14, and table S1). The CSO system delivers a significantly lower Ea of 27.95 kJ mol−1 compared to the 47.19 kJ mol−1 for the AQ system, proving that the pre-desolvated solvation structure fundamentally lowers the energy barrier for Zn2+ desolvation.

To evaluate interfacial transport stability under extreme polarization, a potentiostatic bias of −500 mV (exceeding the AQ nucleation threshold) was applied for chronoamperometry (CA) testing. As shown in Fig. 3F, the CSO system rapidly reaches a stable current plateau, confirming the transition from field-driven electromigration to a regulated diffusion-dominated regime facilitated by neutral CIPs (23). In sharp contrast, the continuous current increase in the AQ system signifies electric-field-driven dendrite initiation and uncontrolled surface area expansion (31). To verify the sustained stabilization mechanism of the CSO system, we tracked the interfacial evolution during long-term cycling. As depicted in fig. S15, the interfacial impedance of the CSO system shows only a slight increase after 200 cycles, while the AQ system’s impedance rises continuously and sharply, leading to a drastic degradation by the 100th cycle. The morphological evolution via laser confocal microscopy (LCM) confirms that AQ system suffers from severe dendritic growth with a massive height fluctuation of 135.3 μm, while Zn in the CSO system remains remarkably dense and flat after cycling with much smaller height fluctuation of only 14.8 μm (Fig. 3G). The detailed time-resolved evolution of the voltage profiles, impedance and morphology for the CSO system are provided in fig. S16. In addition to morphological stability, the chemical purity of the deposited zinc is maintained. Ex-situ XRD analysis at various cycle numbers (fig. S17) confirms the anode surface consistently exhibited only the diffraction peaks of metallic zinc, with no crystalline byproducts (e.g., zinc sulfate hydroxide) observed. This morphological robustness is rooted in the superior corrosion resistance of the CSO system, as evidenced by the significantly lower corrosion current and higher polarization resistance shown in the Tafel curves (Fig. 3H).

The fundamental difference in the initial interfacial chemistry translates directly into a divergence in the long-term cycling performance of the battery. This superior capability to suppress side reactions is first evidenced by Zn‖Cu half-cell tests measuring reversibility (Fig. 3I), where the CSO system achieves a high average CE of 99.7% over 1000 cycles, whereas the AQ system fails rapidly after ∼300 cycles. Subsequently, in long-term Zn‖Zn symmetric cell tests, the CSO system operates stably for over 1000 hours, while the AQ battery short-circuits after only about 50 hours due to dendrite growth (Fig. 3J). Furthermore, the CSO system also exhibits superior rate capability and stability at higher current densities (2.0 mA cm−2), as detailed in figs. S18 and S19. This enhanced performance is attributed to both a high tZn2+ that promotes uniform ion flux and a confined, low-activity water environment that minimizes parasitic side reactions. As with the transference number analysis, the contribution of the trace amount of dissolved Ca2+ cations to dendrite inhibition via the classical electrostatic shielding mechanism can be trivial, because of its concentration. Based on the common-ion effect, the concentration of Ca2+ in 1 M ZnSO4 (∼ 49.3 μM) is orders of magnitude lower than the typical threshold (10–100 mM) required for effective electrostatic shielding as established in literature (e.g., Ga3+, Ce3+ and Na+) (32–35). To further prove this point, we conducted additional electrochemical tests using aqueous electrolyte saturated with Ca2+ (AQ-Sat. Ca). The result (fig. S20) exhibits uncontrolled current climb in CA tests and severe voltage oscillations (0.6 V), indicating kinetic failure of the standard AQ system. Collectively, this evidence proves that by structuring interfacial water to regulate the ion-solvent environment, the CSO electrolyte constructs a kinetically reversible and chemically robust interface, fundamentally suppressing dendrite growth and side reactions, ensuring comprehensive performance advantages from initial deposition to long-term operation.

Mechanistic origins of single-step I−/I2 redox kinetics

The thermodynamic advantages of the CSO electrolyte are directly reflected in its electrochemical kinetics. The cyclic voltammetry (CV) and galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD) curves of the Zn-I2 full cell unambiguously reveal the fundamental differences in the electrochemical behavior of the iodine cathode (Fig. 4A). The CSO electrolyte enables a single-step, highly reversible redox process, characterized by minimal voltage hysteresis and a high CE of 99.6%. This is in stark contrast to the AQ system, which exhibits asymmetric, broad peaks suggesting a complex, multi-step reaction involving intermediates (e.g., I3−) and poor reversibility, with a CE of only 68.4%. To definitively identify the product of this single-step transformation, we utilized in-situ XRD to track the evolution of iodine species, confirming the high reversibility at the cathode interface (Fig. 4B): during charging in the CSO system, diffraction peaks for the (200) and (313) planes of I2 emerge at 24.8° and 50.5° and then completely vanish upon discharge. This impurity-free, periodic evolution of peaks provides direct evidence for the outstanding chemical and structural reversibility of the I−/I2 conversion. These findings are further corroborated by spectroscopic analysis. After charging and resting, the electrolyte soaking solution from the CSO cell is clear and colorless, and its UV-Vis spectrum is devoid of any characteristic triiodide (I3−) peaks, whereas the separator soaking solution from the AQ cell appears pale yellow and exhibits distinct I3− absorption peaks at 288 and 352 nm, visually demonstrating the perfect shuttle suppression of the CSO system (Fig. 4C and fig. S21). Correspondingly, ex-situ XPS analysis of the charged cathode (initially an iodine-free carbon host) shows a clear I2 signal for the CSO system, whereas the AQ system primarily shows an I3− signal (Fig. 4D), further confirming that polyiodide intermediates are either trapped within the cathode or incompletely converted.

To uncover the atomic-scale roots of these significantly enhanced and highly reversible kinetics, we integrated molecular dynamics (MD) simulations with first-principles calculations. I− ion is large (∼2.2 Å), has dispersed charge, and exerts a very weak binding force on water molecules; although I− is a chaotropic ion, it is still surrounded by a layer of loosely bound water molecules with a residence time on the picosecond scale. An incompletely solvated structure of I−, exhibiting a coordination number of only four, was found to be structurally plausible (36). MD simulations reveal a fundamental shift in the solvation configuration of I− ions in our system. In traditional bulk aqueous electrolytes, I− is encapsulated by a robust six-coordinate solvation shell to form a stable octahedral [I(H2O)6]− structure (37). MD simulations reveal that the water-sequestering CSO interface induces an incompletely solvated [I(H2O)3]− state (Fig. 4, E and F). This structural change suggests a higher-chemical-potential initial state for the subsequent redox reaction. First-principles calculations show that the adsorption of I− ions onto the CSO substrate is thermodynamically less favorable compared to water (Fig. 4G). This low affinity, coupled with the strong adsorption of water molecules onto the CSO substrate (Eads = −1.55 eV), offers direct theoretical proof of an interfacial kosmotropic effect. Specifically, these results affirm that the hydrophilic CSO surface is preferentially sheathed in a stable hydration layer that acts as an effective physical barrier, inhibiting the approach and direct adsorption of I− ions.

As shown in the DFT-calculated free energy profiles (Fig. 4H), the AQ system exhibits a high barrier of 1.97 eV, representing the cumulative kinetic penalty for the complete, stepwise desolvation of [I(H2O)6]−. In the CSO system, the thermodynamic elevation of the initial state lowers the activation barrier (38, 39), as the incompletely solvated [I(H2O)3]− species constitutes a more energetically favorable initial state for the reaction. The further reduction to a minimal 0.65 eV is primarily attributed to the spatial confinement of the transition state. Since the transition from spherical I− (radius ≈ 2.2 Å) to linear I3− (length ≈ 5.8 Å) entails substantial volumetric expansion (39), the quasi-lattice interfacial water layer in CSO imposes physical steric hindrance and solubility constraints. This environmental forcing inhibits the solvational stabilization of long-chain polyiodides and compels the reaction to bypass the high-energy I3− intermediate, channeling the charge transfer through a streamlined, single-step pathway with significantly accelerated kinetics. Ultimately, this low-energy pathway, originating from the distinct solvation structure, is the underlying reason for the sustained high diffusion coefficient observed in the CSO system. Fig. 4I schematically illustrates the stark contrast in electrode interfacial behaviors: While the AQ interface suffers from sluggish desolvation, the CSO interface facilitates the partial shedding of water molecules from I− ions. This allows for direct interaction with the electrode surface, effectively bypassing the high-energy desolvation penalty and enabling the observed barrier-free, rapid, single-step redox kinetics.

High-performance quasi-solid-state zinc-iodine batteries

To directly probe the dynamic interfacial evolution during cycling, we employed EIS analysis coupled with the DRT method. As shown in Fig. 5, A and B, the analysis reveals that the CSO system exhibits a highly reversible and kinetically stable interface, whereas the AQ system undergoes severe irreversible kinetic degradation. We systematically analyze the relaxation processes of the two systems sequentially from high to low frequency. First, in the high-frequency region (10−6 ∼ 10−4 s), the relaxation peaks (τ1/τ2) reflect the intrinsic impedance and the formation of the interfacial layer (27). In the CSO system, a low-resistance SEI peak (τ2) forms rapidly at the onset of cycling and remains stable (Fig. 5A and fig. S22). TOF-SIMS and XRD analyses confirm that this interface is a thin and dense composite layer composed of Ca(OH)2, CaSO4 and ZnO (figs. S23 and S24). In stark contrast, during the initial moments of charging in the AQ system, the generated iodine species rapidly trigger polyiodide shuttling, leading to the swift formation of a high-resistance, non-uniform physical passivation layer (τ1 and τ2) (Fig. 5B). Subsequent TOF-SIMS and XRD analyses show that this interface is covered by a thick and loose layer of byproducts, including Zn(OH)2 and basic zinc sulfate (6Zn(OH)2·ZnSO4·4H2O) (figs. S23 and S24). This disparity becomes even more pronounced in the mid-frequency region (10−4 ∼ 10−1 s), which corresponds to the charge transfer process (τ3, τ4), specifically involving the cathode-electrolyte interface and the electron transfer of redox species (40). In the CSO system, the two peaks representing charge transfer (τ3 and τ4) nearly merge into a single, lower-intensity peak. This provides direct evidence that the complex reaction has been simplified into a single, dominant and efficient pathway. Conversely, the DRT map of the AQ system presents a convoluted signal, representing multiple, competing redox pathways with different kinetics. It comprises at least two processes: a relatively faster process (manifesting as a peak shifting to the left on the DRT map), corresponding to the initial 2I− → I2 and I2 + I− → I3− reactions; and a slower, diffusion-limited conversion of higher-order polyiodides (e.g., I5−) (manifesting as a right-shifting or tailing peak) (41).

Finally, in the low-frequency region (10−1 ∼ 100 s) corresponding to the mass transfer process (τ5) (42, 43), the divergence between the two systems is equally striking. The τ5 peak of the CSO system exhibits an extremely low intensity, confirming that ion transport near the interface is exceedingly rapid and unhindered. The slightly higher intensity observed during discharge (compared to charge) is consistent with the minor impedance increase resulting from the I2 dissolution and conversion process at the cathode interface. Collectively, these results demonstrate the lower overall impedance and superior reversibility of the CSO system. Conversely, the AQ system displays a high-intensity τ5 peak, indicating that the ion transport channels are severely blocked by the passivation layer, which is attributed to the increased tortuosity and the trapping effect of polyiodides within the porous electrode (11). To quantify these kinetic advantages, we conducted kinetic analysis and galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) measurements. Kinetic analysis reveals that despite the phase-transition nature of the solid-state conversion, the CSO system maintains rapid kinetics with a high pseudocapacitive contribution (94.1% at 1.6 mV s−1), comparable to that of the aqueous counterpart. (Fig. 5C and fig. S25). Furthermore, GITT measurements offer decisive evidence regarding ion diffusion: the apparent diffusion coefficient (DGITT) of iodide species in the CSO system remains consistently high (on the order of 10−6 cm2 s−1) and highly reversible. In contrast, the diffusion coefficient in the AQ system plummets by nearly two orders of magnitude during the charging process (oxidation, I− → I2) (Fig. 5D).

The superior chemical stability of the CSO system is exemplified by its exceptional resistance to self-discharge (Fig. 5E and fig. S26). During a 48-hour rest period, the CSO system maintains an impressively stable open-circuit voltage (OCV) with a minimal decay rate (dV/dt) of only 3.75 mV h−1 with a high-capacity retention of 91.2%. In sharp contrast, the conventional AQ system (100 μL) suffers from catastrophic self-discharge, with an OCV decay rate of 6.88 mV h−1 and only 20.4% capacity retention (Fig. 5F and fig. S26). This failure is primarily rooted in the severe shuttle effect of unstable I3− intermediates, which redissolve into the bulk electrolyte and migrate to the anode, triggering futile redox cycles and parasitic consumption of active materials. To isolate the influence of total active species inventory from the intrinsic electrolyte properties, we evaluated a control group with normalized iodine content (AQ-1 + 0.8–64 μL, detailed derivation in note S2). As shown in fig. S26A, simply normalizing the electrolyte volume (64 μL) in the AQ system leads to a significant increase in voltage polarization, reflecting elevated charge-transfer resistance. Moreover, this control still yielded a significantly higher decay rate (5.42 mV h−1) and a recovered CE of only 60.2%, underscoring that the stability of CSO is an intrinsic chemical attribute rather than a consequence of active material dilution or reduced electrolyte volume. Instead, the SO42−-induced interfacial water restructuring acts as the indispensable active architect, anchoring polyiodides and ensuring high kinetic reversibility through a diffusion-limited conversion mechanism.

Beyond kinetic stability, the system demonstrates excellent rate capability and an ultralong cycle life. As shown in the rate capability test, the battery maintains a CE of over 95% even at a low current density of 0.3 mA cm−2 (Fig. 5G). At current densities of 0.5 and 1.0 mA cm−2, it achieves high areal capacity of 1.1 mAh cm−2 (at 800 cycles) and 0.91 mAh cm−2, respectively (Fig. 5H and fig. S27). In terms of long-term stability, it achieves an ultralong lifespan of over 2500 cycles at 1.0 mA cm−2 with 93% retention (calculated against the peak capacity) and 3500 cycles at 5.0 mA cm−2 (Fig. 5H and fig. S28). This outstanding durability is rooted in the formation of a highly stable SEI, as evidenced by the unchanging interfacial impedance over hundreds of cycles (fig. S29). In sharp contrast, the shuttling intermediates in the AQ system induce side reactions on the counter electrode, forming a passivation layer that hinders normal charge transfer and significantly increases the cell’s internal resistance (figs. S23 and S24). To validate practical viability, the technology was successfully scaled up from a coin-cell to a pouch-cell configuration (Fig. 5I inset and fig. S30). The pouch cell delivered a high areal capacity of 0.31 mAh cm−2 after 600 cycles at 5.0 mA cm−2, rivaling the performance of the coin cell (0.35 mAh cm−2) and demonstrating its immense application potential (Fig. 5I and fig. S28). Finally, when benchmarked against state-of-the-art aqueous Zn-based batteries, this work exhibits remarkable competitiveness across multiple key metrics, including areal capacity, capacity retention and cycle lifespan (Fig. 5J and table S2) (14, 26, 44–57). This stellar performance originates from our solvation-regulating quasi-solid-state design. This strategy not only ensures fundamental interfacial stability but also delivers tangible advantages, such as the use of low-cost industrial materials (CaSO4·2H2O) and a simplified manufacturing process, highlighting the scalability and advanced nature of the proposed system.

DISCUSSION

We have demonstrated that harnessing the interfacial kosmotropic effect is a powerful strategy for engineering interfacial solvation chemistry. In the CSO quasi-solid-state electrolyte, the electrostatic field from surface-bound Ca2+ and SO42− ions establishes a highly ordered water layer that decouples water activity from the bulk, thereby fundamentally altering reaction pathways at both electrodes. At the anode, an anion-coordinated solvation shell accelerates Zn2+ desolvation and suppresses parasitic hydrogen evolution, ensuring dendrite-free deposition. Concurrently, at the cathode, the ordered water network reshapes the iodine solvation structure, lowering the reaction energy barrier to just 0.65 eV. This unlocks a barrier-free, single-step redox mechanism that eliminates polyiodide shuttling. These molecular-level controls translate directly into exceptional full-cell performance, characterized by an ultralong cycle life exceeding 3500 cycles at 5.0 mA cm−2 with near-perfect Coulombic efficiency. The practical viability of this system is validated in pouch cells, proving that top-tier performance can be achieved alongside low cost and scalable manufacturing. By redefining electrolyte as an active architect of interfacial chemistry rather than a passive conductor, this work provides a new paradigm for stabilizing multivalent systems (e.g., Al3+, Mg2+ and Ca2+), paving the way for the next generation of safe, sustainable and commercially viable energy storage.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Electrolyte preparation

The aqueous electrolyte (AQ) was prepared by dissolving zinc sulfate heptahydrate (ZnSO4·7H2O, 1 M) and potassium iodide (KI, 0.8 M) in deionized water. This solution served as the baseline for comparison. Calcium sulfate dihydrate (CaSO4·2H2O) based quasi-solid-state electrolyte (CSO) powder was synthesized by mixing the as-prepared AQ electrolyte with CSO powder at a volume-to-weight ratio of 1 mL to 5 g. To ensure homogeneity, the resulting slurry was mechanically stirred for 0.5 h before obtaining the final powder.

Fabrication of iodine-free porous activated carbon (PAC) cathode

Typically, PAC, Ketjen black conductive carbon and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) binder were mixed at a weight ratio of 8:1:1 and ground for 30 min. The resulting powder mixture was then added to N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) (e.g., 8–10 mL for a 1 g total solid mass) in a sealed glass bottle. The suspension was stirred at room temperature for 6 h to form a homogeneous slurry. Subsequently, the slurry was uniformly coated onto a piece of carbon cloth using a blade and dried in an oven at 80°C for 12 h. The areal mass loading of the PAC was controlled at approximately 2 ∼ 3 mg cm−2. The fabrication of the pouch cell cathode followed the identical procedure as described above.

Assembly of zinc-iodine batteries

All coin cells were assembled in a CR2032-type configuration. The cells with AQ electrolyte were assembled by first placing the PAC cathode into the cathode case. A glass fiber separator (GF/D) was then placed on top, followed by the addition of 100 μL of the AQ electrolyte. Subsequently, the Zn anode, a spacer, and a spring were placed onto the wet separator, and the cell was sealed by crimping. The cells with CSO quasi-solid-state electrolyte were assembled by sequentially stacking the PAC cathode, approximately 400 mg of the as-prepared CSO electrolyte powder, and the Zn anode in a pressing mold. Given the liquid-to-solid ratio of 1 mL liquid to 5 g powder and an electrolyte density of ≈ 1.25 g mL−1, the liquid mass fraction is determined to be 20 wt.%, meaning each 0.4 g CSO pellet contains a lean-electrolyte volume of ≈ 64 μL (detailed derivation in note S2). This entire stack was first pressed at 2 MPa to form an integrated core with intimate interfacial contact. This integrated core was then placed into the coin cell cases and sealed by crimping to complete the assembly. Unless otherwise specified, these zinc-iodine batteries were operated within a voltage window of 0.9 ∼ 1.6 V vs. Zn2+/Zn. On the other hand, the Zn symmetrical cells and Zn-Cu half cells were also assembled to evaluate the anodic surface reaction, which were prepared using zinc foils (14 mm in diameter, 100 μm in thickness) or copper foils (14 mm in diameter 8 μm in thickness) as electrodes.

Characterizations

Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) and ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption spectra of the electrolyte were recorded at room temperature using Thermo Nicolet 6700 and Evolution 220 spectrometers, respectively. The crystallographic phases of the samples were identified by an x-ray powder diffractometer (Rigaku D/max 2500) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15405 nm). The morphology and corresponding elemental distribution were examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, TESCAN Mira3) equipped with an energy-dispersive x-ray spectrometer (EDS). The evolution of elemental valence states was analyzed by x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) on an ESCALAB 250 Xi spectrometer.

Electrochemical measurements

Galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD) measurements for all coin cells were performed on a NEWARE battery testing system. Cyclic voltammetry (CV), chronoamperometry (CA) and linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) were conducted on a CHI660E electrochemical workstation. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) ranging from 100 kHz to 10 mHz was measured on a BioLogic SP-150 instrument.

Computational methods

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using GROMACS 2025.1 (58). Water molecules were described using the OPC four-site water model (59). For bonded polyatomic species, molecular geometries were first optimized using ORCA 6.1.0 (60) at the PBE0-D4/def2-TZVPPD level of theory (61–63) with the RIJCOSX approximation and the def2/J auxiliary basis set (64). Electrostatic potentials were then calculated at the HF/6-31G* level and RESP atomic charges were fitted using Multiwfn (65, 66). The use of HF/6-31G* in this step follows the conventional RESP charge-derivation protocol for force-field construction rather than serving as the electronic-structure level for the desolvation Gibbs free-energy calculations. The resulting bonded and nonbonded parameters were converted into GROMACS-compatible topologies using Sobtop with a GAFF2-compatible scheme (67). Zn2+, Ca2+, K+ and I− were treated as single-site ions using ion parameters specifically developed for use with the OPC water model rather than generic small-molecule force-field parameters (68, 69).

Initial configurations were generated using Packmol (70). A cubic simulation box with dimensions of 5 × 5 × 5 nm3 was constructed for each electrolyte system. The AQ-1 + 0.8 system contained 40 Zn2+, 40 SO42−, 10 K+, 10 I− and 1100 H2O molecules, whereas the CSO-1 + 0.8 system contained 16 Zn2+, 225 Ca2+, 241 SO42−, 4 K+, 4 I− and 450 H2O molecules. After energy minimization, each system was equilibrated in the NVT ensemble at 298.15 K for 1 ns with a time step of 2 fs, followed by NPT equilibration at 298.15 K and 1 bar for 2 ns. A 30 ns production run was then performed in the NVT ensemble at 298.15 K with the same time step. The first 5 ns of the production trajectory were discarded, and the remaining trajectory was used for RDF, coordination-number and solvation-cluster analyses. Convergence of the RDFs and coordination numbers was examined by block averaging. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all three dimensions, and long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the particle-mesh Ewald method (71).

DFT calculations on the representative solvation clusters were performed using ORCA 6.1.0. The cluster geometries were fully optimized at the PBE0-D4/def2-TZVPPD level of theory with RIJCOSX acceleration and the def2/J auxiliary basis set. Harmonic vibrational frequency calculations were subsequently carried out at the same level of theory to confirm that the optimized structures corresponded to local minima without imaginary frequencies and to obtain zero-point-energy and thermal entropy corrections. The stepwise desolvation Gibbs free-energy profiles were calculated according to ΔGdesolv=ΔE+ΔZPE−TΔS, where ΔE is the electronic energy difference between adjacent desolvation states obtained from DFT calculations, ΔZPE is the zero-point-energy correction, T is the temperature and ΔS is the entropy change derived from the harmonic frequency calculations. All MD snapshots and representative solvation structures were visualized using VMD (72).
