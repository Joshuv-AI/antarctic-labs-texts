Sharp estimates for Jacobi heat kernels in double conic domains
Dawid Hanrahan

Abstract
. We study the even and odd Jacobi heat kernels defined in the context of the multidimensional double cone 𝕍 d + 1 \mathbb{V}^{d+1} and its surface 𝕍 0 d + 1 \mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0} , the multidimensional hyperboloid 𝕍 d + 1 ϱ \prescript{}{\varrho}{\mathbb{V}^{d+1}} and its surface 𝕍 0 d + 1 ϱ \prescript{}{\varrho}{\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0}} , and the multidimensional paraboloid 𝕍 ~ d + 1 \widetilde{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1} and its surface 𝕍 ~ 0 d + 1 \widetilde{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}_{0} . By integrating the framework of Jacobi polynomials on these domains, as analyzed by Xu, with contemporary methods developed by Nowak, Sjögren, and Szarek for obtaining sharp estimates of the spherical heat kernel, we establish genuinely sharp estimates for the even and odd Jacobi heat kernels on 𝕍 d + 1 \mathbb{V}^{d+1} and 𝕍 0 d + 1 \mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0} , and for the even Jacobi heat kernel on 𝕍 d + 1 ϱ \prescript{}{\varrho}{\mathbb{V}^{d+1}} and 𝕍 0 d + 1 ϱ \prescript{}{\varrho}{\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0}} . We also discuss the limitations encountered in extending these results to the remaining settings. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: Primary 35K08, 33C50. Key words: multidimensional double cone, Jacobi heat kernel.



1.
Introduction

Heat kernels are fundamental in both mathematics and physics. Despite extensive research on this topic, obtaining genuinely sharp estimates has only recently extended beyond classical contexts like the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{d+1}
, see
[
DM88
]
. Sharp estimates for the spherical heat kernel were achieved in
[
NSS18
]
, and later extended to Jacobi heat kernels for all compact rank-one symmetric spaces, including the classical domain
[-1,1]
, in
[
NSS21
]
using earlier techniques from
[
NS13
]
.

The goal of this paper is to establish genuinely sharp estimates for even and odd Jacobi heat kernels on various multidimensional conic and hyperbolic domains. We analyze the double cone
\mathbb{V}^{d+1}
and its surface
\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0}
along with the hyperboloid
\prescript{}{\varrho}{\mathbb{V}^{d+1}}
and its surface
\prescript{}{\varrho}{\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0}}
, originally introduced by Xu in
[
Xu21
]
, as well as the paraboloid
\widetilde{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
and its surface
\widetilde{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}_{0}
introduced by Xu in
[
Xu23
]
. The obtained results are stated in Theorems
1.6
and
1.12
and Corollaries
1.7
and
1.13
for double cones, in Corollaries
1.17
and
1.21
for the hyperbolic settings, and in Observations
1.23
and
1.25
for the parabolic settings. The approach presented in this paper builds upon the methods developed in
[
NSS21
]
. We define “genuinely sharp” as finding exact bounds for these kernels from both above and below, a level of accuracy previously attained only in a few specific settings
[
BM16
,
MSZ16
,
MS20
,
Se22
,
HK23
,
NSS24
]
.

Before presenting the results, we briefly discuss the general framework of heat kernels, referencing
[
DX14
, Section 3]
and
[
Xu21
, Sections 1 and 2]
.

Given a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with an appropriate weight
\varpi
, one can construct an orthogonal polynomial basis in
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,\varpi)
. For each degree
n
, the subspace
\mathcal{V}_{n}(\varpi)
spans the corresponding orthogonal polynomials. The orthogonal projection
{\rm proj}_{n}\colon\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,\varpi)\to\mathcal{V}_{n}(\varpi)
takes the form

{\rm proj}_{n}f(x)\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}f(y)P_{n}(\varpi;x,y)\,\text{{d}}\varpi(y),

where
P_{n}(\varpi;x,y)
is the reproducing kernel for
\mathcal{V}_{n}(\varpi)
. Our focus is on
(\Omega,\varpi)
satisfying two key properties.

(1)

There exists a linear second-order differential operator
\mathcal{D}
for which the orthogonal polynomials are its eigenfunctions, and the eigenvalues depend only on the degree
n
. This diffusion operator, defined on a suitable subspace of
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,\varpi)
, is characterized by orthogonal expansions.

(2)

Each
P_{n}
is given by a closed-form expression.

If these properties hold, one can define the associated heat kernel for
\tau\in(0,\infty)
as

h_{\tau}(\varpi;x,y)\coloneqq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\tau\lambda_{n}^{\mathcal{D}}}P_{n}(\varpi;x,y),

where
\lambda_{n}^{\mathcal{D}}
are eigenvalues of
\mathcal{D}
corresponding to
\mathcal{V}_{n}(\varpi)
. Intuitively,
h_{\tau}(\varpi;x,y)
captures the heat distribution between points
x
and
y
over time
\tau
when
\mathcal{D}
governs the diffusion process. Thus, it serves as a fundamental solution to initial value problems for the heat equation. When closed-form expressions for
P_{n}
are available, sharp estimates for
h_{\tau}(\varpi;x,y)
can be obtained for specific inputs.

Such scenarios, however, are uncommon. In
[
Xu21
]
, Xu examined the double cone
\mathbb{V}^{d+1}
and its surface
\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0}
, as well as the hyperboloid
\prescript{}{\varrho}{\mathbb{V}^{d+1}}
and its surface
\prescript{}{\varrho}{\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0}}
, for each
d\geq 2
and for even or odd orthogonal polynomials, separately. In most cases, Xu found weights
\varpi
that allowed both properties to hold and derived exact formulas for the resulting Jacobi heat kernels, named after their clear connection to classical Jacobi theory. On the other hand, for the multidimensional paraboloid
\widetilde{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
and its surface
\widetilde{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}_{0}
, weights found by Xu in
[
Xu23
]
do not allow both properties to hold (see Observations
1.23
and
1.25
).

The following sections summarize Xu’s contributions, focusing on essential aspects relevant to our results. Deriving the weight
\varpi
involved additional tools such as spherical harmonics and classical Jacobi polynomials, reflecting how multidimensional double cones and hyperboloids inherit features of both intervals and Euclidean balls. Xu began with simpler settings (intervals and balls) before constructing orthogonal polynomials or diffusion operators on
\mathbb{V}^{d+1}
,
\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0}
,
\prescript{}{\varrho}{\mathbb{V}^{d+1}}
,
\prescript{}{\varrho}{\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0}}
,
\widetilde{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
, and
\widetilde{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}_{0}
. Below we avoid restating all details, concentrating instead on key formulas critical to our analysis. For a comprehensive exposition, see
[
Xu21
]
and
[
Xu23
]
. Additionally you may check the monographs
[
DX13
,
DX14
]
.

1.1.
Jacobi Heat Kernel on the Surface of the Double Cone

The content of this section is derived from
[
Xu21
, Sections 3–5]
. Fix
d\in\{2,3,\ldots\}
and consider the domain

(1.1)

\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0}\coloneqq\big\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}:\lVert x\rVert^{2}=t^{2},\,0\leq\lvert t\rvert\leq 1\big\}.

We equip (
1.1
) with the weight function

w_{\beta,\gamma}(t)=\lvert t\rvert^{2\beta}(1-t^{2})^{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad\beta>-\frac{d}{2},\quad\gamma>-\frac{1}{2},

which is exactly the range in which
w_{\beta,\gamma}
is integrable with respect to the surface measure on
\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0}
(in
[
Xu21
]
the wider range
\beta>-\frac{d+1}{2}
is admitted, which suffices for the odd subspaces, cf.
[
Xu21
, Remark 4.1]
).

For each
n\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}
, the space
\mathcal{V}_{n}(w_{\beta,\gamma})
of orthogonal polynomials of degree
n
associated with
w_{\beta,\gamma}
is defined in terms of the so-called Jacobi polynomials on the surface of the double cone.

The space
\mathcal{V}_{n}(w_{\beta,\gamma})
can be represented as a direct sum

\mathcal{V}_{n}(w_{\beta,\gamma})=\mathcal{V}^{E}_{n}(w_{\beta,\gamma})\bigoplus\mathcal{V}^{O}_{n}(w_{\beta,\gamma}),

where
\mathcal{V}^{E}_{n}(w_{\beta,\gamma})
is the subspace consisting of polynomials that are even in the variable
t
and, similarly,
\mathcal{V}^{O}_{n}(w_{\beta,\gamma})
denotes the subspace consisting of polynomials that are odd in
t
.

Moreover, there exists a suitable second-order differential operator
\mathcal{D}^{E}_{\gamma}
acting on a subspace of
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0},c_{\beta=0,\gamma}w_{\beta=0,\gamma})
, where
c_{\beta=0,\gamma}
is the corresponding normalizing constant
1
1

1

That is,
c_{\beta=0,\gamma}
is the unique constant such that
c_{\beta=0,\gamma}w_{\beta=0,\gamma}
becomes a probability measure on
\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0}
.
, with all
\mathcal{V}^{E}_{n}(w_{\beta=0,\gamma})
being its eigenspaces. The associated eigenvalues are
-n(n+2\gamma+d-1)
.

We do not use the formula for
\mathcal{D}^{E}_{\gamma}
in subsequent sections, but for completeness we recall that

\mathcal{D}^{E}_{\gamma}\coloneqq(1-t^{2})\partial^{2}_{t}-(2\gamma+d)t\partial_{t}+\frac{d-1}{t}\partial_{t}+\frac{1}{t^{2}}\Delta^{(x)}_{0},

where
\Delta^{(x)}_{0}
denotes the Laplace–Beltrami operator acting in the direction of the spherical variable
x
, see
[
Xu21
, Theorem 4.2]
.

Analogically there exists a second-order differential operator
\mathcal{D}^{O}_{\gamma}
acting on a subspace of
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0},c_{\beta=-1,\gamma}w_{\beta=-1,\gamma})
, where again
c_{\beta=-1,\gamma}
is the corresponding normalizing constant, with all
\mathcal{V}^{O}_{n}(w_{\beta=-1,\gamma})
being its eigenspaces. The associated eigenvalues are
-n(n+2\gamma+d-3)
. Since the surface measure on
\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0}
carries the factor
\lvert t\rvert^{d-1}
, the weight
w_{\beta=-1,\gamma}
is integrable over
\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0}
(so that
c_{\beta=-1,\gamma}
is well defined) only if
d\geq 3
, in accordance with the range
\beta>-\frac{d}{2}
above; accordingly, whenever
\beta=-1
we assume
d\geq 3
.

Although we do not use the formula for
\mathcal{D}^{O}_{\gamma}
later on, we recall that

\mathcal{D}^{O}_{\gamma}\coloneqq(1-t^{2})\partial^{2}_{t}-(2\gamma+d-2)t\partial_{t}+\frac{d-3}{t}\bigg(\partial_{t}-\frac{1}{t}\bigg)+\frac{1}{t^{2}}\Delta^{(x)}_{0},

see
[
Xu21
, Theorem 4.2]
.

If
\beta=0
and
\gamma\geq 0
, then the reproducing kernel of
\mathcal{V}^{E}_{n}(w_{\beta=0,\gamma})
is given by the following formula (cf.
[
Xu21
, (5.7)]
)

(1.2)

P^{E}_{n}\big(w_{\beta=0,\gamma};(x,t),(y,s)\big)\coloneqq\int\limits_{-1}^{1}Z_{n}^{\gamma+\frac{d-1}{2}}\big(\xi(x,t,y,s;v)\big)\,\text{{d}}\Pi_{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}(v).

Here
\,\text{{d}}\Pi_{\eta}(z)\coloneqq c_{\eta}(1-z^{2})^{\eta-\frac{1}{2}}\,\text{{d}}z
for
z\in[-1,1]
if
\eta\in(-\frac{1}{2},\infty)
, where
c_{\eta}
is the normalizing constant, while
\,\text{{d}}\Pi_{-\frac{1}{2}}
is the mean of Dirac deltas
\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{-1}+\delta_{1})
. Furthermore,

\xi(x,t,y,s;v)\coloneqq\xi(v)\coloneqq I_{1}+vI_{2}

with
I_{1}\coloneqq\langle x,y\rangle
and
I_{2}\coloneqq\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\sqrt{1-t^{2}}
.

2

In
[
Xu21
, (5.7)]
(and in
[
Xu21

is printed as
\langle x,y\rangle{\rm sign}(st)
. This cannot be correct as stated: since
Z_{n}^{\lambda}(-z)=(-1)^{n}Z_{n}^{\lambda}(z)
and
\,\text{{d}}\Pi_{\eta}
is symmetric, the resulting expression is odd in

, whereas the reproducing kernel of a space of polynomials even in

is even in

. For

In (

), for
n\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
,
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
, and

, we use the special function

(1.3)

Z_{n}^{\lambda+\frac{1}{2}}(z)\coloneqq\frac{C_{n}^{\lambda+\frac{1}{2}}(1)C_{n}^{\lambda+\frac{1}{2}}(z)}{h^{\lambda+\frac{1}{2}}_{n}}=\frac{P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda}(1)P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda}(z)}{h_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda}},

where
C_{n}^{\lambda+\frac{1}{2}}
is the Gegenbauer polynomial of degree
n
, and
P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda}
is the classical Jacobi polynomial on the interval

, while
h^{\lambda+\frac{1}{2}}_{n}

are the squares of their norms in the space

[

, the function

is defined through the Gegenbauer polynomial, but here we will use the latter expression in (

) instead. Both expressions are equivalent since
P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda}
is a constant multiple of
C_{n}^{\lambda+\frac{1}{2}}
(see

The relationship between
P^{O}_{n}
and
P^{E}_{n}
is given by the following formula

(1.4)

P^{O}_{n}\big(w_{\beta,\gamma};(x,t),(y,s)\big)=\frac{\beta+\frac{d-1}{2}+\gamma+1}{\beta+\frac{d}{2}}stP^{E}_{n-1}\big(w_{\beta+1,\gamma};(x,t),(y,s)\big),

for
n\in\{1,2,\ldots\}
,
\beta>-\frac{d}{2}
and
\gamma>-\frac{1}{2}
, see
[
Xu21
, Theorem 5.1]
3
3

3

In
[
Xu21
, Theorem 5.1]
the author considered only
\beta>-\tfrac{1}{2}
, but the proof applies verbatim as long as the normalizing constants
c_{\beta,\gamma}
and
c_{\beta+1,\gamma}
are finite, i.e. for
\beta>-\tfrac{d}{2}
; for
\beta=-1
this means
d\geq 3
.
. Indeed, since
\lvert t\rvert^{2\beta}t^{2}=\lvert t\rvert^{2\beta+2}
, we have
\mathcal{V}^{O}_{n}(w_{\beta,\gamma})=t\,\mathcal{V}^{E}_{n-1}(w_{\beta+1,\gamma})

In this paper, we focus on finding sharp estimates for even and odd Jacobi heat kernels separately; however, it is sufficient to find the sharp estimates for the even kernels. The estimates for the odd kernels are consequences of (
1.4
), see Lemma

.

Definition 1.5
.

Let
\gamma\in(-\frac{1}{2},\infty)
. Then for each
\tau\in(0,\infty)
, the associated
even Jacobi heat kernel
on
\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0}
is given by

h^{E}_{\tau}\big(w_{\beta=0,\gamma};(x,t),(y,s)\big)\coloneqq\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\tau n(n+2\gamma+d-1)}P^{E}_{n}\big(w_{\beta=0,\gamma};(x,t),(y,s)\big),

and, if
d\geq 3
, the associated
odd Jacobi heat kernel
on
\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0}
is given by

h^{O}_{\tau}\big(w_{\beta=-1,\gamma};(x,t),(y,s)\big)\coloneqq\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\tau n(n+2\gamma+d-3)}P^{O}_{n}\big(w_{\beta=-1,\gamma};(x,t),(y,s)\big).

According to this, for the even Jacobi heat kernel on
\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0}
we shall show the following result.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\gamma\in[0,\infty)
. Then
h^{E}_{\tau}\big(w_{\beta=0,\gamma};(x,t),(y,s)\big)
, the even Jacobi heat kernel on
\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0}
, is comparable to

\tau^{-\frac{d}{2}}\big(\pi-\arccos(I_{1}+I_{2})+\tau\big)^{-\gamma-\frac{d}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(\tau+\frac{I_{2}}{\pi-\arccos(I_{1}+I_{2})}\bigg)^{-\gamma}\exp\bigg\{-\frac{\arccos^{2}(I_{1}+I_{2})}{4\tau}\bigg\}

for
\tau\in(0,1]
, and is comparable to 1 for
\tau\in(1,\infty)
, uniformly in
(x,t),(y,s)\in\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0}
. Here and below a quotient with vanishing numerator is understood as
0
; note that
I_{1}+I_{2}=-1
forces
I_{2}=0
, see Remark
4.2
.

A consequence of (
1.4
) and Theorem
1.6
is the following (see Lemma
2.8
and Section
3
).

Corollary 1.7
.

Let
\gamma\in[0,\infty)
and
d\geq 3
. Then
h^{O}_{\tau}\big(w_{\beta=-1,\gamma};(x,t),(y,s)\big)
, the odd Jacobi heat kernel on
\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0}
, is of the form
st\,k_{\tau}\big((x,t),(y,s)\big)
, where
k_{\tau}\big((x,t),(y,s)\big)
is positive and comparable to

\tau^{-\frac{d}{2}}\big(\pi-\arccos(I_{1}+I_{2})+\tau\big)^{-\gamma-\frac{d}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(\tau+\frac{I_{2}}{\pi-\arccos(I_{1}+I_{2})}\bigg)^{-\gamma}\exp\bigg\{-\frac{\arccos^{2}(I_{1}+I_{2})}{4\tau}\bigg\}

for
\tau\in(0,1]
, and to
\mathrm{e}^{-\tau(2\gamma+d-2)}
for
\tau\in(1,\infty)
, uniformly in
(x,t),(y,s)\in\mathbb{V}^{d+1}_{0}
. In fact,
k_{\tau}=c\,\mathrm{e}^{-\tau(2\gamma+d-2)}h^{E}_{\tau}(w_{\beta=0,\gamma};\cdot,\cdot)
with the constant

from Lemma
2.8
.

1.8.
Comments

Several remarks are in order.

(a)

The resulting estimates are genuinely sharp, representing one of the rare cases where this degree of accuracy has been achieved.

(b)

Multidimensional double cones and hyperboloids combine geometric features of both intervals and Euclidean balls, crucial for analyzing the corresponding Jacobi framework. For both settings, when
d=1
, the geometry simplifies considerably since the Euclidean component is absent, and thus this scenario is not covered in this work. For more information, in this case, we refer to
[
Xu21
, Section 2]
.

(c)

The parameters
\beta
related to the factors
t^{\beta}
in the weights are set to specific values, see Table
1
, as only then the subspaces generated by the Jacobi polynomials of degree
n
are eigenspaces of the underlying differential operators, see
[
Xu21
, Theorems 4.2 and 4.4]
. The parameter
\gamma
is restricted in accordance to the ranges in which useful, i.e. closed, formulas for the associated reproducing kernels hold, see
[
Xu21
, Theorems 5.1 and 5.5]
.

(d)

We focus mainly on
\tau\in(0,1]
; the uniform estimates for
\tau\in(1,\infty)
follow from the known large-time behaviour of the classical Jacobi heat kernel, see Section
3
.

(e)

The idea behind Theorems
1.6
and
1.12
(and thereby all corollaries) is to express the studied Jacobi heat kernels in terms of the heat kernel related to the classical Jacobi expansions on
[-1,1]
, see Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
. In principle, we follow the strategy proposed in
[
NSS21
]
.

2.
Technical preparation

In this section, we compile some supporting results that will be instrumental in proving the main theorems. We begin by providing an alternative representation of the Jacobi heat kernels, adapting the approach from
[
NSS21
]
. The core objective is to eliminate the oscillatory behavior of Jacobi polynomials, transforming them into a positive expression that is significantly more manageable for analysis.

Given
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
, we recall the formula for the heat kernel
G_{\tau}^{\lambda,\lambda}
associated with the classical Jacobi polynomials
P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda}
on
[-1,1]
, when the first argument equals
1
. For
\tau\in(0,\infty)
, we have

G_{\tau}^{\lambda,\lambda}(1,w)\coloneqq\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-\tau\lambda_{n}}\frac{P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda}(1)P_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda}(w)}{h_{n}^{\lambda,\lambda}}=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-\tau\lambda_{n}}Z_{n}^{\lambda+\frac{1}{2}}(w),

where
\lambda_{n}\coloneqq n(n+2\lambda+1)
and
Z_{n}^{\lambda}
is defined in (
1.3
).

The heat kernel from Definition
1.5
can be described in terms of
G_{\tau}^{\lambda,\lambda}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For each
\tau\in(0,\infty)
, the even Jacobi heat kernel from Definition
1.5
can be given by

\int\limits_{-1}^{1}G_{\tau}^{\gamma+\frac{d}{2}-1,\gamma+\frac{d}{2}-1}\big(1,\xi(x,t,y,s;v)\big)\,\text{{d}}\Pi_{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}(v).

Proof.

By expanding left hand side of the formula from Definition
1.5
we get

\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\tau n(n+2\gamma+d-1)}\int\limits_{-1}^{1}Z_{n}^{\gamma+\frac{d-1}{2}}\big(\xi(x,t,y,s;v)\big)\,\text{{d}}\Pi_{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}(v).

Note that
n(n+2\gamma+d-1)=n(n+2\lambda+1)
, where
\lambda=\gamma+\frac{d}{2}-1
. After the application of Fubini’s theorem the proof is completed.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

For each
\tau\in(0,\infty)
, the even Jacobi heat kernel from Definition
1.11
can be given by

\int\limits_{-1}^{1}\int\limits_{-1}^{1}G_{\tau}^{\gamma+\mu+\frac{d}{2}-\frac{1}{2},\gamma+\mu+\frac{d}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}\big(1,\xi(x,t,y,s;u,v)\big)\,\text{{d}}\Pi_{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}(u)\,\text{{d}}\Pi_{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}(v).

Proof.

By expanding left hand side of the formula from Definition
1.11
we get

\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\tau n(n+2\gamma+2\mu+d)}\int\limits_{-1}^{1}\int\limits_{-1}^{1}Z_{n}^{\gamma+\mu+\frac{d}{2}}\big(\xi(x,t,y,s;u,v)\big)\,\text{{d}}\Pi_{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}(u)\,\text{{d}}\Pi_{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}(v).

Observe that
n(n+2\gamma+2\mu+d)=n(n+2\lambda+1)
, where
\lambda=\gamma+\mu+\frac{d}{2}-\frac{1}{2}
. Fubini’s theorem concludes the proof.
∎

The rest of this section is devoted to recalling various estimates obtained in previous research in the context of the Jacobi heat kernel on
\mathbb{B}^{d}
. The first one describes the behavior of the function
G_{\tau}^{\lambda,\lambda}
(cf.
[
NSS21
, (7)]
).

Lemma 2.3
.

Fix
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
. Then

G_{\tau}^{\lambda,\lambda}(1,\cos\psi)\simeq\tau^{-\lambda-1}(\tau+\pi-\psi)^{-\lambda-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\bigg\{-\frac{\psi^{2}}{4\tau}\bigg\}

uniformly in
\tau\in(0,1]
and
\psi\in[0,\pi]
.

We shall use the following elementary estimate (cf.
[
NSS21
, (9)]
).

Lemma 2.4
.

Fix
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

(\tau+\pi-\eta)^{\kappa}\exp\bigg\{-\frac{\eta^{2}}{\tau}\bigg\}\lesssim(\tau+\pi-\theta)^{\kappa}\exp\bigg\{-\frac{\theta^{2}}{\tau}\bigg\}

uniformly in
\tau\in(0,\infty)
and
\theta,\eta\in[0,\pi]
with
\theta\leq\eta
.

Moreover the following integral estimate will be required in the proof (cf.
[
NSS21
, Lemma 2.1]
).

Lemma 2.5
.

Fix
\nu\in[-\frac{1}{2},\infty)
,
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
\Phi_{A,B}(w)\coloneqq\arccos(A+Bw)
. Then

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{1}\big(\pi-\Phi_{A,B}(w)+D\big)^{-\eta}\exp\bigg\{-\frac{\Phi_{A,B}^{2}(w)}{D}\bigg\}\,\text{{d}}\Pi_{\nu}(w)

\displaystyle\qquad\simeq D^{\nu+\frac{1}{2}}\big(\pi-\Phi_{A,B}(1)+D\big)^{-\eta}\bigg(B\big(\pi-\Phi_{A,B}(1)\big)^{-1}+D\bigg)^{-\nu-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\bigg\{-\frac{\Phi_{A,B}^{2}(1)}{D}\bigg\}.

uniformly in
B\in[0,1]
,
A\in[-1,1-B]
, and
D\in(0,\infty)
(with
B(\pi-\Phi_{A,B}(1))^{-1}=0
if
B=0
).

Remark 2.6
.

In fact, Lemma
2.5
can be generalized to

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{1}\big(\pi-\Phi_{A,B}(w)+cD\big)^{-\eta}\exp\bigg\{-\frac{\Phi_{A,B}^{2}(w)}{D}\bigg\}\,\text{{d}}\Pi_{\nu}(w)

\displaystyle\qquad\simeq D^{\nu+\frac{1}{2}}\big(\pi-\Phi_{A,B}(1)+D\big)^{-\eta}\bigg(B\big(\pi-\Phi_{A,B}(1)\big)^{-1}+D\bigg)^{-\nu-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\bigg\{-\frac{\Phi_{A,B}^{2}(1)}{D}\bigg\}.

for any fixed
c>0
. This observation is a straightforward consequence of the fact that for any fixed
c>0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
(x+y)^{\alpha}\simeq(x+cy)^{\alpha}
in the range
(x,y)\in[0,\infty)\times(0,\infty)
.

In the proof of Theorem
1.12
the integrand will contain an additional factor depending on the variable of integration. The following extension of Lemma
2.5
takes care of it.

Lemma 2.7
.

Fix
\nu\in[-\frac{1}{2},\infty)
,
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mu\in[0,\infty)
, and let
\Phi_{A,B}
be as in Lemma
2.5
. Then

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{1}\big(\pi-\Phi_{A,B}(w)+D\big)^{-\eta}\bigg(B^{\prime}\big(\pi-\Phi_{A,B}(w)\big)^{-1}+D\bigg)^{-\mu}\exp\bigg\{-\frac{\Phi_{A,B}^{2}(w)}{D}\bigg\}\,\text{{d}}\Pi_{\nu}(w)

\displaystyle\qquad\simeq\bigg(B^{\prime}\big(\pi-\Phi_{A,B}(1)\big)^{-1}+D\bigg)^{-\mu}D^{\nu+\frac{1}{2}}\big(\pi-\Phi_{A,B}(1)+D\big)^{-\eta}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\bigg(B\big(\pi-\Phi_{A,B}(1)\big)^{-1}+D\bigg)^{-\nu-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\bigg\{-\frac{\Phi_{A,B}^{2}(1)}{D}\bigg\}

uniformly in
B,B^{\prime}\in[0,1]
,
A\in[-1,1-B]
with
A>-1
if
B^{\prime}>0
, and
D\in(0,\infty)
(with the convention
B^{\prime}(\pi-\Phi_{A,B}(w))^{-1}=0
if
B^{\prime}=0
). Moreover, as in Remark
2.6
,
D
may be replaced by
cD
,
c>0
fixed, in the first two factors of the integrand.

Proof.

Write
\psi(w)\coloneqq\pi-\Phi_{A,B}(w)
,
\delta\coloneqq\psi(1)
,
g(w)\coloneqq\big(B^{\prime}\psi(w)^{-1}+D\big)^{-\mu}
, and let
F
be the integrand of Lemma
2.5
. Since
\psi
is nondecreasing and
\mu\geq 0
,
g
is nondecreasing, so
g\leq g(1)
and the upper bound follows from Lemma
2.5
. For the lower bound we may assume
B^{\prime}>0
, so that
A>-1
and
\delta>0
. We have
\cos\psi(w)=-(A+Bw)=\cos\delta+B(1-w)
and
B\leq 1+A+B=1-\cos\delta
, hence for
w\in[\frac{1}{2},1]

\cos\psi(w)\leq\cos\delta+\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\cos\delta)=\cos^{2}\tfrac{\delta}{2}\leq\cos\tfrac{\delta}{2},

so that
\psi(w)\geq\frac{\delta}{2}
and
g(w)\geq(2B^{\prime}\delta^{-1}+D)^{-\mu}\geq 2^{-\mu}g(1)
. By Lemma
2.4
(with
\tau=D
,
\kappa=-\eta
,
\theta=\Phi_{A,B}(w^{\prime})
, and
\eta
there replaced by
\Phi_{A,B}(w)
) we have
F(w)\lesssim F(w^{\prime})
whenever
w\leq w^{\prime}
. Averaging over
w^{\prime}\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
with respect to
\,\text{{d}}\Pi_{\nu}
(note that
\Pi_{\nu}([\frac{1}{2},1])>0
) gives
\int_{0}^{1/2}F\,\text{{d}}\Pi_{\nu}\lesssim\int_{1/2}^{1}F\,\text{{d}}\Pi_{\nu}
, hence
\int_{1/2}^{1}F\,\text{{d}}\Pi_{\nu}\gtrsim\int_{0}^{1}F\,\text{{d}}\Pi_{\nu}
. Therefore the left-hand side is bounded from below by
2^{-\mu}g(1)\int_{1/2}^{1}F\,\text{{d}}\Pi_{\nu}\gtrsim g(1)\int_{0}^{1}F\,\text{{d}}\Pi_{\nu}
, and Lemma
2.5
completes the proof. The last assertion follows as in Remark
2.6
.
∎

Finally, we express the odd Jacobi heat kernels in terms of the even ones.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\gamma,\mu\in(-\frac{1}{2},\infty)
and
\tau\in(0,\infty)
, and put
c_{d,\gamma}\coloneqq\frac{\gamma+\frac{d-1}{2}}{\frac{d-2}{2}}
and
c_{d,\gamma,\mu}\coloneqq\frac{\mu+\gamma+\frac{d}{2}}{\mu+\frac{d-1}{2}}
. If
d\geq 3
, then

h^{O}_{\tau}\big(w_{\beta=-1,\gamma};(x,t),(y,s)\big)=c_{d,\gamma}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\tau(2\gamma+d-2)}\,st\,h^{E}_{\tau}\big(w_{\beta=0,\gamma};(x,t),(y,s)\big).

Moreover, for every
d\geq 2
,

h^{O}_{\tau}\big(w_{\beta=-\frac{1}{2},\gamma,\mu};(x,t),(y,s)\big)=c_{d,\gamma,\mu}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\tau(2\gamma+2\mu+d-1)}\,st\,h^{E}_{\tau}\big(w_{\beta=\frac{1}{2},\gamma,\mu};(x,t),(y,s)\big).

Proof.

The first identity follows by inserting (
1.4
) with
\beta=-1
into Definition
1.5
and using
n(n+2\gamma+d-3)=(n-1)\big((n-1)+2\gamma+d-1\big)+(2\gamma+d-2)
. The second one follows in the same way from (
1.10
) with
\beta=-\frac{1}{2}
, Definition
1.11
, and
n(n+2\gamma+2\mu+d-2)=(n-1)\big((n-1)+2\gamma+2\mu+d\big)+(2\gamma+2\mu+d-1)
.
∎

Appendix

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\varrho\geq 0
and
\varrho\leq\lvert s\rvert,\lvert t\rvert\leq\sqrt{\varrho^{2}+1}
. Moreover, let
u,v\in[-1,1]
. We define

\displaystyle\xi_{\varrho}(x,t,y,s;u,v)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\langle x,y\rangle+u\sqrt{t^{2}-\varrho^{2}-\lVert x\rVert^{2}}\sqrt{s^{2}-\varrho^{2}-\lVert y\rVert^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+v\sqrt{1+\varrho^{2}-s^{2}}\sqrt{1+\varrho^{2}-t^{2}},

where
\lVert x\rVert^{2}\leq t^{2}-\varrho^{2}
and
\lVert y\rVert^{2}\leq s^{2}-\varrho^{2}
. Then we have
\lvert\xi_{\varrho}(x,t,y,s;u,v)\rvert\leq 1
.

Proof.

Observe that
0\leq t^{2}-\varrho^{2}\leq 1
and
0\leq s^{2}-\varrho^{2}\leq 1
. We define
\alpha\coloneqq\sqrt{t^{2}-\varrho^{2}}
and
\beta\coloneqq\sqrt{s^{2}-\varrho^{2}}
. We have

\displaystyle\langle x,y\rangle+u\sqrt{t^{2}-\varrho^{2}-\lVert x\rVert^{2}}\sqrt{s^{2}-\varrho^{2}-\lVert y\rVert^{2}}+v\sqrt{1+\varrho^{2}-s^{2}}\sqrt{1+\varrho^{2}-t^{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle x,y\rangle+u\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-\lVert x\rVert^{2}}\sqrt{\beta^{2}-\lVert y\rVert^{2}}+v\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}.

Consider two vectors,
\tilde{x}
and
\tilde{y}
, defined as follows

\tilde{x}\coloneqq\big(x,u\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-\lVert x\rVert^{2}},v\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\big),\qquad\tilde{y}\coloneqq\big(y,\sqrt{\beta^{2}-\lVert y\rVert^{2}},\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}\big).

Note that
\langle\tilde{x},\tilde{y}\rangle=\xi_{\varrho}(x,t,y,s;u,v)
. Moreover, a straightforward computation shows that
\lVert\tilde{x}\rVert^{2}\leq\lVert\tilde{y}\rVert^{2}=1
. The application of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

In Lemma
4.1
, we can distinguish several special cases:

(1)

the case
\varrho=0
corresponds to the double cone (its surface and the solid domain), whereas the case
\varrho>0
relates to the hyperbolic settings,

(2)

the special case
\lVert x\rVert^{2}=t^{2}-\varrho^{2}
,
\lVert y\rVert^{2}=s^{2}-\varrho^{2}
(where the term containing
u
vanishes) corresponds to the surfaces, whereas the general case relates to the solid domains,

(3)

if
\xi_{\varrho}(x,t,y,s;u,v)=-1
, then
\tilde{x}=-\tilde{y}
(equality in the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality), so comparing the last two coordinates,
v=1
forces
\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}=\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}=0
and
u=1
forces
\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-\lVert x\rVert^{2}}=\sqrt{\beta^{2}-\lVert y\rVert^{2}}=0
. In particular,
I_{1}+I_{2}=-1
implies
I_{2}=0
,
I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}=-1
implies
I_{2}=I_{3}=0
, and
I_{1}+I_{3}=-1
implies
I_{3}=0
(likewise for
I_{2}^{\varrho}
,
I_{3}^{\varrho}
).
