[¹⁸F]PSMA-1007 and [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT in patients with metastatic triple-negative breast cancer: a prospective head-to-head comparison and assessment of PSMA-targeted radioligand therapy applicability
Authors: Tatiana Nikolaevna Lazutina, Aleksandr Igorevich Khalimon, Soslan Rolanovich Urtaev, Malika Maratovna Khodzhibekova, Daria Yurevna Khodakova, Gulnara Faridovna Khamadeeva, Anastasia Igorevna Nikiforuk, Irina Valentinovna Pylova, Aleksei Victorovich Leontev, Andrey Dmitrievich Kaprin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is an aggressive molecular subtype of breast cancer with limited treatment approaches. Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA), widely applied as a target for molecular imaging and radioligand therapy of prostate cancer, demonstrates the highest expression in TNBC across all molecular subtypes of breast cancer, and represents a promising target for imaging and treatment of TNBC patients.MethodsThis prospective study included 20 patients with metastatic TNBC who underwent both [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007 and [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT. Qualitative and quantitative image analysis was performed to determine the uptake predominance of one of the tracers and the added value of the diagnostic CT, as well as to assess the potential applicability of PSMA-targeted radioligand therapy (PSMA-RLT).ResultsA total of 229 lesions were identified using both [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007 and [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT. Concordant and discordant lesions were observed for both tracers, with a greater number of discordant lesions identified by [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007. [¹⁸F]FDG demonstrated higher uptake in TNBC lesions. An exception was observed in lung lesions, where no significant difference was found between tracers. The application of diagnostic CT revealed additional TNBC lesions that were not identified by PET, for each tracer. None of the patients demonstrated a [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007 uptake level sufficient for potential PSMA-RLT application when a lesional SUV-based threshold was used.ConclusionDespite detectable [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007 uptake in TNBC lesions, [¹⁸F]FDG uptake was generally higher. Furthermore, the diagnostic CT can identify additional PET-negative lesions. Although PSMA expression is confirmed in TNBC, it may be insufficient to provide potential benefit from PSMA-RLT for this patient group.

Background

Approximately 2.3 million new cases of breast cancer (BC) are reported annually, making it a leading cause of cancer-related mortality in women [1]. Clinical outcomes and patient management depend on the status of key molecular targets, such as hormone receptors (HR) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), which are negative for triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). TNBC accounts for approximately 15–20% of all breast carcinomas, predominantly affects younger women, and is associated with aggressive clinical behavior, early recurrence, and poor prognosis [2, 3]. While endocrine and HER2-targeted therapy have substantially improved survival in HR-positive and HER2-positive disease respectively, clinically validated molecular targets for TNBC remain limited and are confined to specific subgroups, leaving chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors as the main treatment approaches [4, 5]. Nevertheless, treatment resistance and rapid progression are common, underscoring the urgent need for novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA), also known as glutamate carboxypeptidase II, is a type II transmembrane glycoprotein extensively applied in molecular imaging and radioligand therapy of prostate cancer [6]. In metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC), PSMA-targeted radioligand therapy (PSMA-RLT) has demonstrated significant improvements in patient survival and quality of life, leading to regulatory approval [7]. While PSMA expression in prostate cancer is predominantly determined in tumor cells, subsequent research has shown its presence in tumor-associated neovasculature across multiple solid malignancies, including BC [8]. This finding has expanded interest in PSMA-targeted imaging and therapy beyond prostate cancer. Immunohistochemistry-based studies have confirmed PSMA expression in both primary and metastatic lesions of invasive breast carcinomas. Wernicke et al. reported that PSMA expression in tumor-associated neovasculature was particularly high in HR-negative and high-grade tumors, with no significant differences between primary lesions and brain metastases [8]. Kasoha et al. further demonstrated PSMA expression not only in tumor-associated neovasculature but also in tumor cells, including distant metastases [9]. In one of the largest samples studied to date, Tolkach et al. identified neovascular PSMA expression in 60% of breast carcinomas, with the highest prevalence observed in high-grade tumors, HR-negative, HER2-positive and TNBC subtypes [10].

Early molecular imaging studies have demonstrated PSMA-radioligand uptake in TNBC lesions using 68Ga-labeled tracers [11]. Comparative studies with [18F]FDG PET/CT, a high-accuracy and standard-of-care method for TNBC imaging, have shown high lesion concordance while revealing distinct uptake patterns [12, 13]. More recently, direct head-to-head comparison of [18F]PSMA-1007 and [18F]FDG PET/CT has emphasized the need to define the diagnostic and theranostic role of PSMA expression in TNBC [14]. The potential applicability of PSMA-RLT in TNBC patients has been evaluated in several studies, which assessed PSMA positivity in TNBC lesions using the established PET inclusion criteria for mCRPC patients [13, 15]. Criteria based on the VISION trial and utilizing the tumor-to-reference tissue ratio were applied in previously published studies in TNBC patients [16, 17]. However, this approach is limited by the already known inter- and intrapatient variability of PSMA-targeted tracer uptake in normal organs [18–20]. Therefore, we decided to use an alternative lesional SUV-based approach, which is less strict than the TheraP criteria and requires a predominance of tumor lesions with SUVmax ≥ 10 without referencing normal organ uptake [21].

In our prospective study, we performed a comprehensive concordance analysis between the two imaging techniques, including the comparisons of volume-based PET metrics and hybrid imaging data, as well as an assessment of PSMA positivity using alternative lesional SUV-based approach, that were not evaluated in previous studies.

Materials and methods

All patients provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local ethical committee.

Patient characteristics

None of the patients had received any tumor-specific treatment within four weeks prior to PET/CT imaging. In all patients a metastatic disease was initially identified based on [18F]FDG PET/CT.

PET/CT acquisition

[18F]FDG and [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT were performed for all patients within a 2–13 day (median 4.5) interval using EARL-accredited 5-ring Discovery IQ PET/CT-system (GE HealthCare, USA) in accordance with European Association of Nuclear Medicine (EANM) and Society of Nuclear Medicine and Molecular Imaging (SNMMI) guidelines. Both tracers were administered using the MEDRAD Intego PET Infusion System (Bayer, Germany). The mean administered activity was 401 ± 80 MBq for [18F]FDG and 399 ± 46 MBq for [18F]PSMA-1007. PET acquisition was performed from vertex to mid-thigh with a 3 min per-bed position for both tracers, at 60 ± 0.3 and 90 ± 0.1 min after the administration of [18F]FDG and [18F]PSMA-1007, respectively. The acquired PET data were corrected for randoms, scatter, attenuation, decay, and dead time. Block sequential regularized expectation maximization (BSREM) algorithm with a β-value of 400 and a 256 × 256 matrix size, complied with EARL 18F standards 2, was utilized for PET image reconstruction for both tracers. For [18F]FDG PET/CT, the acquisition protocol included a diagnostic breath-hold contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) from vertex to mid-thigh at the venous phase, in addition to the standard non-contrast CT (NCCT) for attenuation correction and image fusion. CT slice thickness and increment were 1.25 mm for both NCCT and CECT. For the breath-hold chest CT (as part of the CECT), the slice thickness was 1.25 mm, and the increment was 1.0 mm.

Image analysis

Qualitative and quantitative image analysis was performed using AW Server 3.2 Ext. 4.8 and PET VCAR software (GE HealthCare, USA) by two physicians with over 10 years of experience, specialized in both nuclear medicine and radiology. The final conclusion for each lesion was based on a consensus between the two physicians.

Qualitative analysis included the determination of the number of tumor lesions, their localization, and a visual assessment of either tracer uptake predominance. Focal tracer uptake, as well as CT findings on NCCT or CECT, that were typical for malignancy, were considered PET- and CT-positive, respectively.

Each identified lesion demonstrated uptake of at least one tracer. The number of tumor lesions, as well as the character of lesions (concordant, discordant, or unique), were determined based on PET only, hybrid PET/NCCT, and PET/CECT data from both imaging techniques (Table 2).

Quantitative analysis included measuring the short-axis (for lymph nodes) and the long-axis (for other lesions) diameters of lesions identified by CT, as well as SUVmax, SUVpeak, functional tumor volume (FTV), and total lesion activity (TLA) for all lesions. Given that [18F]PSMA-1007 is a non-metabolic tracer, the terms FTV and TLA were introduced as analogs of metabolic tumor volume (MTV) and total lesion glycolysis (TLG), which are commonly used for [18F]FDG imaging. To obtain PET metrics values, a semi-automatic segmentation tool with threshold weight of 0.5 based on an estimated threshold method was utilized. SUV was normalized by lean body mass, calculated using the James formula. To minimize the impact of partial volume effect on PET metrics values, the threshold size was set at 13 mm based on NEMA IEC Body Phantom Set NU2-2018 annual acquisition data. “Target” lesions (greater than 13 mm) were analysed as an additional group. Per lesion, per patient, and region-based intertracer comparison of each PET metric, as well as per patient intertracer FTV Burden and TLA Burden (sum of FTV and TLA values of all lesions for each patient) comparison were performed. The number and intrapatient predominance of lesions with [18F]PSMA-1007 SUVmax ≥ 10 were assessed according to the TheraP-based PSMA-RLT inclusion criteria. For this analysis, SUV was additionally normalized using the more traditional but less accurate total body weight normalization method.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics Version 26 (IBM, USA). The normality of continuous data was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Descriptive statistics are provided as median and interquartile range for continuous data and as frequency (percentage) for discrete data. For continuous data, intertracer differences were evaluated using nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test. For discrete data, intertracer and interstudy differences were evaluated using the nonparametric McNemar test with Edwards’ correction. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Additionally, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis between [18F]FDG and [18F]PSMA-1007 uptake was performed for SUVmax, SUVpeak, FTV, and TLA.

[¹⁸F]PSMA-1007-positive lesions (n = 188) accounted for 91.7% of all [¹⁸F]FDG-positive ones (n = 205): 88.2% (n = 82) for lymph nodes lesions, 93.7% (n = 59) for bones, 96.3% for lungs (n = 26) and 100% for chest wall lesions (n = 20), 1 of 2 peritoneal foci. These were also interstudy concordant lesions, which accounted for 82.1% of all lesions based on PET alone (intertracer concordant lesions). Additional application of diagnostic CT increased the number of interstudy concordant lesions to 87.3% (n = 200) for PET/NCCT and 92.6% (n = 212) for PET/CECT. In both intertracer and interstudy comparisons, the total number of identified lesions was higher with [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007 (n = 212 and n = 221, respectively) than with [¹⁸F]FDG (n = 205 and n = 218, respectively) (Table 3). However, both differences were no statistically significant (p > 0.05). Region-based intertracer and interstudy statistical analysis was available for lymph nodes and bone lesions; intertracer statistical analysis also was available for lung lesions. In this analysis, no statistically significant differences were also found between [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007 and [¹⁸F]FDG, except in the intertracer comparison of bone lesions, where [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007 PET identified a significantly higher number of lesions compared to [¹⁸F]FDG PET (p = 0.03). Discordant lesions, which included the unique lesions, accounted for 17.9% and 8.3% (n = 41 and n = 19) of all lesions, respectively. These were identified predominantly by [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT (n = 24 and n = 11, compared to n = 17 and n = 8 for [¹⁸F]FDG) and were located in bones and brain for [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007, and in lymph nodes and bones for [¹⁸F]FDG. All four brain lesions were discordant [18F]PSMA-1007-positive and included three unique ones. The single discordant peritoneal lesion was [18F]FDG-positive, but not unique. However, the discordant character of this lesion was associated with its proximity to the liver, which is characterized by high physiological [18F]PSMA-1007 uptake. Unique lesions had no clinical impact on patient staging or management. [18F]FDG uptake for intertracer concordant lesions was predominantly higher in per lesion analysis regardless of region. In per patient analysis of intertracer concordant lesions [18F]FDG uptake was also predominantly higher, except for bone lesions, due to the significant variance in the number of bone lesions between patients.

Not all of the lesions identified by CT that corresponded to PET lesions were tumor-specific CT-positive. The mean diameter of lesions identified by CT (n = 169, 73.8% of all lesions) was 14.1 mm (range 3–84 mm), 14.5 mm (range 7–84 mm) for chest wall (n = 20, 100%), 19.7 mm (range 4–78 mm) for lungs (n = 30, 100%), 14.9 mm (4–57 mm) for bones (n = 19, 24.7%); 10.2 mm (range 3–28 mm) for lymph nodes (n = 92, 95.8%) (Table 3). Diameters of peritoneal (n = 2, 100%) and brain (n = 1, 25%) lesions were 9 mm, 10 mm, and 5 mm, respectively.

Per lesion and per patient intertracer comparison revealed significantly higher SUVmax, SUVpeak, TLA, and TLA Burden for [¹⁸F]FDG, and no significant difference in FTV and FTV Burden (Table 4). In the per lesion, region-based intertracer comparison, [¹⁸F]FDG SUVmax, SUVpeak were significantly higher for lymph nodes, bone and chest wall lesions (p < 0.05), while [¹⁸F]FDG TLA was significantly higher in lymph nodes and bone lesions (p < 0.05) (Table 5). No significant differences were found in FTV per each region group, in TLA for chest wall lesions, or in any metrics for lung lesions (p > 0.05). Additional analysis of target lesions revealed no further differences compared to the general group in per lesion, per patient, and region-based analyses (Tables 4 and 5).

Discussion

Over the past decade, interest in PSMA has increased significantly due to its favorable expression profile and ability to internalize via endosomal complexes, making it a highly promising target for molecular imaging and radioligand therapy, not only for prostate cancer but also for other malignancies. The development of novel treatment approaches, such as radioligand therapy, is crucial for managing patients with aggressive malignancies that have limited therapeutic options, including TNBC.

Results from a series of immunohistochemistry-based studies assessing PSMA expression across BC subtypes indicate that TNBC exhibits the highest expression levels [8, 9]. Consequently, TNBC represents the most promising BC subtype for PSMA imaging and PSMA-RLT.

To date, there are a limited number of studies focused on evaluating the potential of PSMA PET/CT in BC patients [11–14]. Their results, consistent with findings from immunohistochemistry-based studies, demonstrate a higher detection rate for TNBC.

S. Medina-Ornelas et al. assessed the [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 PET/CT detection rate compared to [18F]FDG PET/CT in 21 patients with primary metastatic BC across different molecular subtypes [12]. Patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative BC had a significantly lower number of PSMA-positive lesions than FDG-positive ones. In contrast, the number of PSMA- and FDG-positive lesions was similar in all of the five involved TNBC patients. Furthermore, no difference in the detection rate of bone metastases was observed between [¹⁸F]FDG and [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 PET/CT across all analysed molecular subtypes. In our study, both discordant and unique lesions, including bone lesions, were identified for both [18F]PSMA-1007 and [18F]FDG (Table 3).

N. Andryszak et al. performed the first prospective head-to-head comparison of [18F]PSMA-1007 and [18F]FDG PET/CT in 10 patients with metastatic TNBC [14]. The authors concluded that both tracers have a comparable detection rate for TNBC lesions. However, they noted that [18F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT identified a larger number of distant metastases with higher uptake compared to [18F]FDG. The authors also reported higher lesion detectability by [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT in regions where high physiological [¹⁸F]FDG uptake may potentially obscure focal tracer uptake, such as the brain and cranial bones. In our study with a larger sample size (20 patients), [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT detected slightly more lesions than [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT (but this difference was not statistically significant). However, [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007 uptake was significantly lower in per lesion, per patient, and region-based analyses (Tables 4 and 5). An exception was observed for lung lesions, which showed no significant intertracer difference. Furthermore, brain lesions in our study were also identified only by [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT. These lesions included three unique ones without any corresponding CECT findings. However, as brain MRI is part of the standard workup for TNBC patients, this particular advantage of PSMA PET is of limited clinical value. Unique discordant TNBC lesions were identified for each tracer, but this finding had no impact on staging or patient management. It should be noted that assessing the impact of PET/CT findings on treatment approach was beyond the scope of our study. Additionally, our results suggest a positive relationship between glycolysis and PSMA expression in TNBC lesions.

Previous studies were limited to intertracer SUV comparisons within TNBC lesions, which do not account for the total body tumor volume and activity. The latter volume-based metrics allow for a more detailed intertracer comparison and may be of interest in the context of PSMA-RLT. Despite significantly higher overall values of [18F]FDG TLA Burden, [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007 TLA Burden was predominant in four patients (Table 6). However, no specific features distinguishing these patients from the general group were identified. This may be associated with the small sample size.

The additional value of diagnostic CT as a part of hybrid imaging has not been evaluated in previous studies. The results of our study indicate that the number of interstudy concordant lesions increased with the additional application of diagnostic CT, which can identify additional PET-negative lesions.

N. Andryszak et al. suggest the potential applicability of PSMA-RLT in TNBC patients. However, their study did not evaluate whether [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007 uptake in tumor lesions met the current PSMA-positivity criteria established for mCRPC.

Currently, results from two studies that applied a PSMA expression score of ≥ 2 (lesional tracer uptake equal to or lower than the parotid gland and higher than the normal liver) as the PSMA-positivity criteria previously used in the VISION trial to determine the potential eligibility of PSMA-RLT for BC patients have been published [16, 17]. The authors conclude that some BC patients, including those with metastatic TNBC, exhibit PSMA-positivity and may potentially benefit from PSMA-RLT. However, several independent research groups have noted both high interpatient variability of PSMA-radioligand uptake in reference tissues (such as the liver, spleen, and salivary glands) and the influence of various factors (such as fasting, tumor burden, and the dose-related changes under PSMA-RLT) on uptake in normal organs [18–20, 22, 23]. Furthermore, tracer uptake in reference tissues (including the liver) varies across different PSMA-radioligands (for example, across widely utilized [¹⁸Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 and [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007) [18]. At the same time, results from several studies show no significant difference in the SUVs of tumor lesions between [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007 and [¹⁸Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 [24, 25]. The efficacy of PSMA-RLT is likely more influenced by the absolute tracer uptake in tumor lesions rather than their tumor-to-reference tissue ratio.

In our study, we decided to use PSMA-positivity criteria similar to those previously applied in the TheraP trial, as they are specifically aimed at assessing SUV in tumor lesions [21]. Provided that SUV harmonization is ensured, this approach appears to be more reliable. None of the patients in our sample had a predominance of lesions with SUVmax ≥ 10, even when we used SUV normalization to total body mass, which yields higher values than SUV normalized by lean body mass. This approach is less strict than the TheraP-based PET inclusion criteria, which require that all measurable lesions (diameter ≥ 10 mm) exceed this threshold, at least one lesion has SUVmax ≥ 20, and there are no FDG-avid/none-PSMA-avid discordant lesions.

The main limitation of our study is the single-center design and small sample size, which prevented subgroup interpatient analysis, as well as the lack of verification of all detected lesions, since it would be unnecessary from a clinical standpoint. Additionally, a correlation between [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007 PET metrics values and Ki-67 was not performed, as for some patients Ki-67 was only determined at the initial workup.

Conclusion

Standard-of-care [¹⁸F]FDG PET/CT remains the preferable molecular imaging modality for patients with metastatic TNBC compared to [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007 PET/CT. A positive relationship was observed between glycolysis and PSMA expression in TNBC lesions. The additional application of diagnostic CT to hybrid imaging increased the number of detected lesions for both [¹⁸F]PSMA-1007 and [¹⁸F]FDG scans. None of the patients met the lesional SUV-based PSMA-RLT inclusion criteria, which were based on the TheraP criteria but were less strict. Therefore, preliminary data provide no evidence to suggest that PSMA-RLT is applicable in all patients with metastatic TNBC. However, due to the limited sample size, further studies with subgroup analysis are required to define the subgroup of TNBC patients most suitable for PSMA-RLT.
