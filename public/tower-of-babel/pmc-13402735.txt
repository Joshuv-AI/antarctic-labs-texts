Paired analysis of meconium and neonatal hair reveals distinct biomarker profiles of prenatal exposure to trace metal elements
Authors: Mohammad Mouner Ahmad Alsayegh, Batool Adnan Sallom, Ekbal Ahmad Fadel, Flora Yahya Mayhoub
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prenatal exposure to trace metal elements remains a significant public health concern, particularly because these elements can cross the placental barrier and accumulate in fetal tissues. Meconium and neonatal hair are commonly used as non-invasive biomarkers of intrauterine exposure; however, their comparative behavior and degree of agreement remain insufficiently understood. This study aimed to quantify concentrations of lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), chromium (Cr), and nickel (Ni) in paired meconium and neonatal hair samples and to evaluate differences and associations between these two biological matrices. A cross-sectional study was conducted on 50 healthy full-term neonates in Latakia, Syria, during August 2025. Paired meconium and neonatal hair samples were collected within the first 24 h after birth. Following microwave-assisted acid digestion, trace metal concentrations were measured using graphite furnace atomic absorption spectrometry. Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Due to non-normality, paired comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and correlations were evaluated using Spearman rank correlation coefficients. All four trace metal elements were detected in both matrices. Meconium concentrations were consistently higher than those measured in neonatal hair, with statistically significant differences observed for all analyzed elements (p < 0.001). Correlation analysis showed weak and non-significant associations between meconium and neonatal hair concentrations for Pb, Cr, and Ni, while Cd showed a modest but non-significant correlation. These findings indicate limited agreement between the two biological matrices. Meconium exhibited higher trace metal concentrations than neonatal hair, suggesting a broader accumulation of prenatal exposure. However, the weak correlations between matrices indicate that they may capture different exposure characteristics rather than serving as interchangeable biomarkers. Introduction

Prenatal exposure to trace metal elements represents an important public health concern because several elements can cross the placental barrier and accumulate in fetal tissues. Depending on the level and duration of exposure, elevated concentrations of elements such as lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), chromium (Cr), and nickel (Ni) have been associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, including impaired fetal growth, neurodevelopmental disturbances, and increased risk of preterm birth1–4. These effects are thought to involve oxidative stress, endocrine disruption, and interference with essential biological pathways5,6.

Accurate assessment of intrauterine exposure remains a methodological challenge. Traditional biomonitoring approaches have relied on maternal blood, cord blood, or placental tissue; however, these matrices often reflect only recent or transient exposure and may not adequately capture cumulative fetal burden over the course of gestation7,8. As a result, increasing attention has been directed toward alternative biological matrices that integrate exposure over longer periods.

Meconium and neonatal hair have emerged as promising non-invasive matrices for evaluating prenatal exposure. Meconium begins to accumulate in the fetal gastrointestinal tract from the second trimester onward and is thought to reflect prolonged exposure through the ingestion of amniotic fluid and biliary excretion9,10. In contrast, neonatal hair incorporates trace metal elements during hair formation, primarily in late gestation, and may therefore represent a more restricted exposure window11,12. Despite their widespread use, the extent to which these two matrices provide comparable or complementary information remains insufficiently characterized.

Previous studies have reported detectable levels of trace metal elements in both meconium and neonatal hair, often suggesting that meconium may contain higher concentrations13–15. However, many of these investigations have been limited by small sample sizes, lack of paired analyses, or insufficient statistical evaluation of inter-matrix relationships. Furthermore, the degree of agreement between these biomarkers at the individual level has not been consistently examined, making it difficult to determine whether they can be used interchangeably or should be interpreted as reflecting distinct aspects of exposure16,17.

In regions with complex environmental conditions, such as areas characterized by mixed urban, agricultural, and industrial activities, local data on prenatal exposure remain scarce. In Syria, and particularly in Latakia City, there is a lack of recent biomonitoring studies assessing fetal exposure to environmental contaminants. Generating region-specific data is essential for understanding exposure patterns and informing future public health strategies.

Therefore, the present study aimed to quantify concentrations of Pb, Cd, Cr, and Ni in paired meconium and neonatal hair samples collected from full-term newborns in Latakia, Syria. In addition, the study sought to compare concentrations between the two matrices and to evaluate the degree of association between them at the individual level in order to better understand their potential roles as complementary biomarkers of prenatal exposure.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted during August 2025 in Latakia Governorate, Syria. The study aimed to assess prenatal exposure to selected trace metal elements through the analysis of paired biological samples collected at birth.

Study population and sampling

The study included 50 healthy full-term neonates delivered at public and private hospitals across Latakia Governorate. Participants were recruited consecutively during the study period according to case availability. All eligible mother–newborn pairs encountered during the sample collection period were invited to participate.

The sample size was determined by feasibility during the study period and by the availability of paired meconium and neonatal hair samples meeting the eligibility criteria.

Eligible participants were live-born neonates with gestational age ≥ 37 completed weeks and with paired meconium and neonatal hair samples collected within the first 24 h after birth. Neonates with congenital anomalies, preterm birth (< 37 weeks of gestation), or incomplete clinical information were excluded. Basic maternal and neonatal characteristics, including maternal age, smoking status, and mode of delivery, were obtained from medical records when available.

Sample collection

Paired biological samples were collected from each neonate within the first 24 h after birth. Meconium samples consisted of the first passed meconium and were collected using a sterile spoon, then transferred into labeled polypropylene tubes. Neonatal hair samples were obtained from the posterior scalp using sterile stainless-steel scissors. Approximately 20 mg of hair was collected whenever sufficient sample mass was available for analysis.

Immediately after collection, all samples were placed in labeled polypropylene tubes and stored frozen until laboratory analysis.

Sample preparation and digestion

Prior to digestion, neonatal hair samples were washed to reduce potential external contamination. Hair samples were briefly rinsed with acetone, followed by two to three rinses with deionized water, and then dried at room temperature before analysis. Meconium samples were thawed at room temperature and homogenized thoroughly to ensure uniform distribution of analytes.

For trace metal determination, approximately 0.5 g of homogenized meconium and 0.02 g (20 mg) of dried neonatal hair were accurately weighed using an analytical balance (± 0.0001 g). Each sample was transferred into clean digestion vessels, and 5 mL of concentrated nitric acid (HNO₃, 65%) and 2 mL of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂, 30%) were added.

Samples were digested using a microwave digestion system at 180–200 °C for 30–45 min until clear solutions were obtained. After cooling, the digested solutions were transferred into 50 mL volumetric flasks and diluted to volume with deionized water. The solutions were then filtered using metal-free filter paper prior to instrumental analysis. Final concentrations were expressed as µg/g dry weight.

Determination of trace metal elements

The concentrations of lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), chromium (Cr), and nickel (Ni) were determined after acid digestion using graphite furnace atomic absorption spectrometry (GF-AAS) with a Varian 220 atomic absorption spectrometer (Varian, Australia). Graphite furnace conditions were selected to provide adequate analytical sensitivity for trace metal determination in both meconium and neonatal hair samples.

Element-specific instrumental parameters were optimized according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Hollow cathode lamps were used for each element, and argon was employed as the carrier gas. Quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC)

Quality assurance and quality control procedures were applied throughout sample preparation and instrumental analysis. All reagents were of analytical grade, and deionized water was used for all dilutions. Glassware, polypropylene tubes, and digestion vessels were acid-cleaned before use to minimize potential contamination.

Procedural blanks were included in each digestion batch to monitor background contamination. Calibration curves were prepared using certified standard solutions with a five-point calibration approach. Good linearity was obtained for all analytes (R² > 0.995).

The limits of detection (LOD) and limits of quantification (LOQ) were established for each element. LOD and LOQ values were 0.02 and 0.06 µg/g for Pb, 0.005 and 0.015 µg/g for Cd, 0.01 and 0.03 µg/g for Cr, and 0.02 and 0.06 µg/g for Ni, respectively. No measurements were below the corresponding LOQ values.

Analytical accuracy was evaluated using spike recovery experiments performed on selected samples. Precision was assessed by duplicate analysis. Recovery values ranged from 90% to 110%, and relative standard deviations remained below 10% for all analyzed elements.

Statistical analysis

Data obtained from laboratory measurements were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Microsoft Excel. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Since all variables showed non-normal distributions (p < 0.05), non-parametric statistical methods were applied.

Trace metal concentrations were summarized using medians, first quartiles (Q1), third quartiles (Q3), and observed ranges (minimum–maximum). Paired comparisons between meconium and neonatal hair concentrations were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Associations between paired matrices were evaluated using Spearman rank correlation coefficients.

No measurements were below the corresponding limits of quantification (LOQ); therefore, no substitution or imputation procedures were required prior to statistical analysis.

In addition, descriptive exploratory analyses were performed to summarize median trace metal concentrations according to selected maternal and neonatal characteristics, including maternal age group, maternal smoking status, and mode of delivery. These subgroup analyses were descriptive only and were not subjected to inferential statistical testing.

A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Latakia University, Syria. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating mothers prior to sample collection. Participation was voluntary, and all collected information and biological samples were handled confidentially and used solely for research purposes.

Results

All analyses were conducted on paired samples. A total of 50 paired meconium and neonatal hair samples were analyzed for Pb, Cd, Cr, and Ni. All four trace metal elements were detected in both biological matrices. The distribution of all variables deviated from normality according to the Shapiro–Wilk test (p < 0.05), supporting the use of non-parametric statistical methods.

Descriptive statistics, including limits of detection (LOD), limits of quantification (LOQ), medians, interquartile ranges, and observed ranges, are summarized in Table 2. No measurements were below the corresponding LOQ values.

Across all four elements, meconium consistently exhibited higher concentrations than neonatal hair. For example, median Pb levels were approximately five-fold higher in meconium (8.20 µg/g) than in neonatal hair (1.60 µg/g). Similar patterns were observed for Cd, Cr, and Ni, with meconium showing both higher median values and wider ranges, indicating greater variability in trace metal concentrations.

Discussion

This study provides a comparative evaluation of trace element concentrations in meconium and neonatal hair as biomarkers of prenatal exposure. The findings revealed two key patterns:1 consistently higher concentrations of all analyzed metals (Pb, Cd, Cr, and Ni) in meconium compared to neonatal hair, and2 weak and non-significant correlations between the two matrices at the individual level. Together, these results highlight important differences in how these biological matrices reflect prenatal exposure.

The significantly higher concentrations observed in meconium across all metals suggest that meconium may capture a broader or more cumulative record of in utero exposure. Meconium begins to form during the second trimester and progressively accumulates substances ingested or absorbed by the fetus, including xenobiotics and environmental contaminants18,19. As such, it has been widely considered a matrix that integrates exposure over an extended gestational period. In contrast, neonatal hair growth occurs later in pregnancy and may reflect a narrower exposure window, potentially explaining the lower concentrations observed in hair samples20.

Despite these systematic differences in concentration levels, the absence of significant correlations between meconium and neonatal hair indicates limited agreement between the two matrices at the individual level. This finding suggests that the two matrices may not be interchangeable and could reflect different aspects of prenatal exposure. Rather than indicating inconsistency, this divergence likely reflects differences in biological incorporation pathways, growth dynamics, and temporal exposure windows21. For instance, meconium accumulation is influenced by fetal swallowing and biliary excretion, whereas hair incorporates metals through blood supply during follicular growth. These distinct mechanisms may result in differential metal deposition even within the same individual.

The lack of strong correlation has been reported in previous studies comparing different neonatal biomarkers, where variability in toxicokinetics, maternal–fetal transfer, and timing of exposure contribute to heterogeneous biomarker profiles22,23. Therefore, the present findings support the concept that a single biological matrix may not fully capture the complexity of prenatal exposure, and that combining multiple matrices could provide a broader indication of cumulative exposure of cumulative exposure assessment.

Descriptive subgroup analyses based on maternal characteristics (age, smoking status, and mode of delivery) did not reveal consistent patterns across metals. Although higher median concentrations were observed among smokers for several metals, these findings were not subjected to inferential statistical testing and should be interpreted with caution. Similar inconsistencies have been reported in studies where lifestyle factors interact with environmental exposures in complex and non-linear ways24.

Importantly, the results should be interpreted in light of several limitations. The study design was cross-sectional and based on a relatively limited sample size from a restricted geographic setting, which may limit generalizability. In addition, no maternal biomonitoring data or environmental measurements were available to directly link observed concentrations to specific exposure sources. The absence of clinical outcome data also precludes assessment of potential health effects associated with the measured metal levels. Furthermore, although standardized analytical procedures were applied, the study did not include external validation against other established biomarkers such as cord blood.

Despite these limitations, the study contributes to the growing body of evidence on neonatal biomonitoring by directly comparing two commonly used matrices within the same individuals using paired statistical analysis. The consistent differences observed between matrices, combined with the lack of strong correlation, underscore the importance of carefully selecting biomarkers based on the specific research question and exposure timeframe of interest.

Future research should aim to incorporate longitudinal designs, larger sample sizes, and integrated exposure assessment approaches, including maternal biomarkers and environmental monitoring. Such studies would help clarify the relationships between different matrices and improve the interpretation of neonatal biomonitoring data in environmental health research.

Conclusion

In conclusion, meconium and neonatal hair exhibited distinct profiles of trace metal element accumulation, with meconium showing consistently higher concentrations and limited correlation with neonatal hair at the individual level. These findings suggest that the two biological matrices reflect different dimensions of prenatal exposure and should not be used interchangeably. A multi-matrix approach may provide a broader understanding of fetal exposure to environmental contaminants.
