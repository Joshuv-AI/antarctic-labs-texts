Long-term electrochemical carbon capture from diverse CO2 sources with a recirculation mode
Authors: Yanjie Zhai, Shanhe Gong, Weisong Li, Qing Xia, Tingting Li, Jianyu Guan, Shao-Yuan Leu, Zhen-Yu Wu, Shu Ping Lau, Xiao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Electrochemical carbon capture offers a sustainable pathway for carbon management, yet current systems are hindered by low concentration of atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2), resulting in inefficiencies and limited stability. Here, we develop an electrochemical system employing a modular porous solid electrolyte (PSE) reactor for continuous, scalable carbon capture from diverse sources, including ambient air and flue gas, while regenerating high-purity CO2 ( >99%) without additional chemical input through a recirculation mode. By leveraging oxygen evolution and reduction reactions (OER/ORR) and employing sodium (bi)carbonate as a reversible cyclic mediator, the system achieves continuous CO2 capture from flue gas, delivering high Faradaic efficiencies for Na⁺ transport (~85%), and high carbon capture rates (3060 ml h−1 at 10 A, 100 cm2). Notably, direct air capture is demonstrated using ambient air, showing a long-term stability of over 2000 hours while maintaining high carbon removal efficiency (>93%) at 30 mA cm-2 and a large air treatment capacity (approximately 8820 kgair day-1 m-2), thus contributing to the goal of achieving net-zero emissions.

Introduction

Anthropogenic CO2 emission is a significant driver of global warming, making the development of efficient carbon-capture technologies critical to meeting global carbon targets1–4. Carbon-capture technologies are essential for implementing effective strategies to capture CO2 from both point sources, such as flue gas, and diffuse sources such as ambient air, to mitigate climate change2. Traditional carbon-capture methods primarily rely on scrubbing techniques, where low-concentration CO2 is absorbed by basic solutions such as monoethanolamine or hydroxides, forming carbon-containing compounds5. These compounds are subsequently thermally regenerated to release concentrated CO2 for subsequent usage. However, these processes face limitations: solvent degradation and substantial regeneration energy demands, oxidative and thermal instability leading to equipment corrosion6–9, and fugitive amine emissions10 that together challenge their long-term viability and scalability.

Electrochemical methods offer a green and sustainable alternative to traditional thermochemical carbon-capture techniques7,11–19. By utilizing renewable electricity as energy input, these methods can operate efficiently at room temperature, providing a decentralized solution for carbon-capture challenges. A typical electrochemical process involves the reduction of redox-active carriers to generate strong bases that form adducts with electrophilic CO2, later releasing concentrated CO2 upon oxidation and regenerating the sorbents (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2)20,21. Despite its potential, such a process often suffers from low capture rates and high sensitivity to O2 gas in most CO2 sources. Recent advancements in membrane technology have led to the development of integrated CO2 capture and release systems with resistance to O2 interference6,22–24. However, challenges persist, including low CO2 generation rates8,15, unwanted by-products such as H2 or Cl225,26 or the complexity of separating gases like O227–29, which requires energy-intensive downstream separations8,9,30–33. Furthermore, these systems suffer from design flaws, membrane failure, or catalyst degradation with limited operational stability of only tens to hundreds of hours, severely restricting scalability7,34. Direct air capture (DAC) within these systems is particularly constrained to low currents, resulting in impractical carbon-capture rates and limited treatment capacities.

Here, we report a modular carbon-capture design with a porous solid-electrolyte (PSE) reactor for directly capturing CO2 from various CO2 sources, from air (as low as 0.04 % CO2) to flue gas (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1). By employing oxygen evolution and reduction reactions (OER/ORR) redox electrocatalysis and a distinct recirculation mode by employing sodium (bi)carbonate as a cyclic mediator, the reactor captures low-concentration CO2 with high Faradaic efficiencies and capture rates, without consuming any additional chemicals. What is more, by employing a cation exchange membrane (CEM) and a proton exchange membrane (PEM) between the electrodes and the PSE layer, the system achieves efficient and scalable long-term stability with high carbon removal efficiency and air treatment capacity for DAC (ambient air in Hong Kong). Operating at ambient temperature, the system generates CO2 (>99%) without requiring heat integration, enabling a modular design with low energy consumption. Results

Reactor and process design for CO2 capture

Integrating CO2 capture and regeneration within a single PSE reactor is a promising strategy for continuous carbon capture, necessitating the creation of alkaline conditions for CO2 absorption and acidic conditions for subsequent release7. To achieve long-term operation, it is crucial to avoid electrochemically unstable components, such as the degradation of AEMs caused by unbuffered pH conditions, nucleophilic attack, continuous CO2 exposure combined with strong pressure difference between the middle and cathode chamber, or crossover of bicarbonate/carbonate species35,36. Here, we propose an integrated modular reactor that electrochemically generates an alkaline interface for efficient CO2 capture and maintains acidic conditions for CO2 generation, ensuring extended operational longevity. As shown in Fig. 1c, d, our design incorporates a PEM, a CEM, and an additional solid-electrolyte chamber between the cathode and anode, utilizing sodium (bi)carbonate as the reversible cyclic mediator for CO2 capture, thereby enhancing both the stability and efficiency of the process. The extra PSE layer, constructed from a styrene-divinylbenzene sulfonated copolymer (Supplementary Fig. 2) and functionalized with cation-conducting sulfonate groups, is porous, water-permeable, air-permeable, and Na+ conductive. This layer is crucial for nucleating CO2 bubbles, Na+ transportation, and minimizing ohmic loss (The essential role is illustrated in Supplementary Figs. 3–5, Supplementary Note 2). In our design, a sodium (bi)carbonate solution serves as a reversible cyclic mediator and is circulated between the middle chamber and the cathode chamber. At the cathodic catalyst/membrane interface, the ORR generates OH− ions that rapidly capture CO2 to form (bi)carbonate. Under an electric field, Na⁺ from the middle layer selectively crossed the CEM into the cathode chamber and combined with the as-formed (bi)carbonate to produce sodium (bi)carbonate solution. Notably, the sodium (bi)carbonate is recycled back into the middle chamber, without any consumption. Concurrently, H⁺ ions generated at the anode/PEM interface migrate into the middle chamber, replacing Na⁺ ions crossing into the cathode chamber. This introduction of H+ splits sodium (bi)carbonate in the middle chamber, releasing high-purity CO2.

Our design offers several advantages over the existing electrochemical methods. First, we can directly utilize ambient air, without further purification, to drive ORR and create an alkaline environment toward low-concentration carbon-capture, i.e., DAC. This approach circumvents the issue of oxygen poisoning that affects redox-active carriers. Second, the triple-phase boundary at the cathode facilitates rapid O2 diffusion to the catalyst/membrane interface, enabling favorable reaction kinetics of ORR to generate OH−. And the extensive gas-liquid contact space throughout the entire cathode chamber facilitates rapid CO2 diffusion, enabling the formation of sodium (bi)carbonate with favorable reaction kinetics and ion transport at high current densities and ensuring efficient CO2 capture. Third, our system realizes DAC using only renewable electricity, eliminating the need for additional chemical or water inputs, as O2 is recycled back to the cathode, and both Na⁺ and water are recycled from the middle chamber to the cathode to maintain system balance, making the process both sustainable and efficient. Fourth, the PEM-CEM configuration enhances system stability by allowing only Na⁺ and H⁺ ions to pass through the membranes, thereby preventing membrane degradation and extending operational lifespan. In addition, the PSE reactor offers a continuous and integrated carbon capture and generation process, simplifying on-site applications compared to traditional absorption/desorption cycles.

Electrochemical characterizations of the PSE reactor

We first evaluated the carbon-capture performance using several metrics: CO2-capture rate, CO2 generation rate, CO2 generation ratio (gCO2), (bi)carbonate generation rate, capture efficiency, Faradaic efficiency for Na⁺ transport (FENa+), energy consumption, and operational stability. The definitions of these metrics are detailed in Supplementary Note 3. Before demonstrating a practical recirculation system of electrochemical carbon capture, we first evaluated the electrochemical performance of the CO2 capture and generation processes separately. Here, CO2 capture refers to the absorption of CO2 gas at the cathode, which is, in principle, equivalent to (bi)carbonate generation. The CO2 generation rate denotes the CO2 gas produced in the middle chamber. Commercial Pt/C and IrO2 catalysts were employed for cathodic ORR and anodic OER, respectively37, with an active electrode area of 1 cm2 unless specified otherwise. The NaHCO3 solution continuously flowed through the PSE layer while supplying sufficient water at the cathode to remove crossing Na⁺ ions and reduce mass transport limitations. Notably, in principle, each Na⁺ transported into the cathode chamber is paired with HCO3− derived from carbon-containing solutions from the CO2-capture process. Consequently, the flux of Na⁺ crossing over to the cathode affects the overall efficiency of CO2 capture and thus is a critical metric for evaluating the performance of our reactor. The concentrations of Na+ were measured using ion chromatography (Supplementary Fig. 6). As shown in Fig. 2a, b, our PSE reactor exhibited an onset potential of approximately 0.9 V at 1 mA cm−2, and a FENa+ (~85%) was achieved across a wide range of current densities (10–500 mA cm−2) by flowing 0.5 M NaHCO3 in the middle chamber (Fig. 2c). During catalysis, H+ is produced in the anode and subsequently migrates into the middle chamber under an electric field. The migrated H+ acidifies the middle chamber sodium (bi)carbonate solution, leading to the generation of CO2. Consequently, the formation of a carbon-containing solution, CO32− or HCO3−, in the middle layer is important in influencing the efficiency of CO2 release. By employing 0.5 M NaHCO3 (0.5 M Na+), the system achieved a CO2 generation rate of 16.31 mmol h−1 cm−2 at 500 mA cm−2 (Fig. 2d). In contrast, Na2CO3 requires further acidification into NaHCO3 before CO2 can be generated, effectively doubling energy consumption. Using 0.25 M Na2CO3 solutions (0.5 M Na+), the system maintained FENa+ of ~85% and a CO2 generation rate of 8.23 mmol h−1 cm−2 CO2 gas at 500 mA cm−2. Notably, the presence of HCO3− in the middle chamber doubles the CO2 generation rate as compared to CO32− under identical electric current conditions. Of note, the (bi)carbonate here is directly supplied, but our goal is to develop a closed-loop system where (bi)carbonate is formed from CO2 capture at the cathode and then recycled to the middle chamber (see discussions in the “Electrochemical CO2 capture in a recirculation system” section). The different CO2 generation performances of CO32− and HCO3− motivate us to further investigate the formation of cathodic (bi)carbonate from low-concentration CO2.

Figure 2e–h illustrates the sodium (bi)carbonate formation process at the triple-phase interface of the cathode during the carbon-capture process. During catalysis, the OH⁻ ions were generated through the ORR, which then captured CO2 and combined with crossed Na⁺ to form sodium (bi)carbonate. The carbon-capture rate, in principle, equals the cathodic sodium (bi)carbonate generation rate. The capture efficiencies, i.e., the number of CO2 molecules captured per electron transferred, are used to evaluate the efficiency of CO2 capture by analyzing the bicarbonate ratio through titration in the direct flow mode (Supplementary Note 4). Supplementary Table 3 compares the capture efficiencies of different carbon-containing solutions, demonstrating that forming NaHCO3 (1.0 CO2 per e−) exhibits higher capture efficiency than forming CO32− (0.5 CO2 per e⁻), confirming that HCO3− formation is more efficient than CO32− for CO2 capture. Ideally, with an effective ORR catalyst and sufficient CO2 at the gas diffusion layer (GDL) interface, each one-electron transfer forms one HCO3− (1 CO2 per e−). In real conditions, capture efficiency is directly linked to the bicarbonate ratio and FENa+ (Fig. 2h), where inputted CO2 concentration plays a role in (bi)carbonate formation during the CO2-capture process. As shown in Fig. 2f, higher voltages and thus higher energy consumption are required for 5.6% CO2 + air than 5.6% CO2 + O2, due to the lower partial pressure of O2 in air. However, CO2-capture rates remain the same under the same current (Supplementary Note 5, Fig. 2f, Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). During this process, the main reaction that occurred was the ORR reaction in the triple-phase boundary (Supplementary Note 6, Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10). Under the condition of 5.6% CO2 + air, the sodium (bi)carbonate generation rate depends on the operation current, which is almost linearly with the operational current density, ranging from 10 to 500 mA cm−2. Moreover, the (bi)carbonate generation ratio is almost the same as the FENa+, which is above 78% across a wide range of cell currents, despite the different ratios of CO32− and HCO3−38. Interestingly, at current densities below 200 mA cm−2, the alkali solution was entirely NaHCO3, achieving an impressive capture efficiency of 0.82 CO2 per e⁻ (Fig. 2g, h, Supplementary Fig. 11). Sufficient CO2 was available at the catalyst-membrane interface to quickly react with the OH− ions generated and the Na⁺ ions crossing the membrane, forming NaHCO3. When the current density increased, a large quantity of OH⁻ was produced, rapidly depleting nearby CO2 molecules. With limited CO2 mass diffusion, part of the reaction stops at forming CO32−, shifting the product composition from pure HCO3− to a mixture of CO32− and HCO3−. For example, at 300 mA cm−2, 88.5% of the alkali solution is sodium (bi)carbonate, while at 500 mA cm−2, the proportion was 63.9% with a capture efficiency of 0.57 CO2 per e−. Despite the bicarbonate ratio and capture efficiency slightly decreasing at high current densities, our PSE reactor still achieved an impressive CO2-capture rate of 12.6 mmol h−1 cm−2 at 500 mA cm−2. The strong correlation between capture efficiency and bicarbonate ratio further supports the idea that maximizing HCO3− generation enhances the production rate of high-purity CO2.

To reveal an optimal operating window for the system, we conducted a systematic investigation by adjusting the water flow rate while controlling the air or flue gas feed at 500 sccm (Supplementary Note 7, Supplementary Figs. 12–15). At water flow rates of 0.5–2.0 ml min⁻1, both the bicarbonate generation rate and bicarbonate/carbonate ratio remained stable and almost the same with water flow rate at 1.0 ml min⁻1 (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). At lower water flow rates (0.1 ml min⁻1), we observed the precipitation of solid NaHCO3 (with a dissolution pH of 8.54 ± 0.26 at the cathode outlet), which obstructed mass transfer and hindered liquid/gas collection (Supplementary Fig. 14). At high flow rates (10 ml min⁻1) with current density over 300 mA cm−2, the effluent pH rose above 9.0, leading to major carbonate formation (Supplementary Fig. 15). This excessive water flow disrupted the gas-liquid mixing within the flow channels, hindered bicarbonate formation. Based on the above reaction mechanism, the primary limitation on capture rates at lower CO2 concentrations is the mass transport of CO2 from the inlet flow to an extensive gas-liquid contact space throughout the entire cathode chamber. For the concentration of 10.7%, 4.1%, and 1.6% CO2 in air, the maximum current density required to maintain over 80% bicarbonate generation ratio decreased from 300 mA cm−2 to 50 mA cm−2 and 25 mA cm−2, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 16–18). This indicates that as the CO2 concentration decreases, limited mass diffusion reduces the maximum current density required to keep high Faradaic efficiencies. To further evaluate the adaptability and performance of our PSE reactor in low CO2 environments, DAC experiments were conducted and analyzed in detail (see discussions in the “Directly air capture (DAC) from commercial air, and ambient air in HK” section).

Electrochemical CO2 capture in a recirculation system

To explore the capability of our reactor to capture and generate CO2 continuously under more practical operational conditions, we operated the PSE device by recirculating the carbon-containing solutions from cathodic CO2 contractors back into the middle PSE layer to act as mediators (Fig. 1a). During catalysis, OH⁻ generated by the ORR reacts with captured CO2 to form (bi)carbonate, which balances with Na+ at the cathode and is then recycled back into the PSE layer. Simultaneously, proton flux from the anode drives a neutralization of the (bi)carbonate, generating a high-purity CO2 stream (>99%) and completing the carbon generation process. No chemicals were consumed, nor were by-products generated during the process. Specifically, we recirculated 200 ml of NaHCO3 solution between the middle PSE layer and the cathode chamber, maintaining a flow rate of 0.9 ml min−1 (Supplementary Fig. 19).

Theoretically, Na⁺ concentrations should remain 0.5 M at both the cathode outlet and the middle layer inlet during recirculation. When electrolysis was performed by supplying 5.6% CO2, the reactor generated full HCO3− in the cathode at current densities ranging from 10 to 200 mA cm−2. The Na⁺ at the cathode, paired with the HCO3−, showed a linear trend, reaching 6.5 mmol h−1 cm−2 at 200 mA cm−2. The collected Na⁺ concentration at the middle layer outlet exhibited a linear decrease (Fig. 3a). During electrolysis, due to the generation of H⁺ at the anode, NaHCO3 in the middle chamber reacted with protons and Na+ crossing the CEM, generating high-purity CO2 through acidification. The CO2 generation rate, which is nearly equivalent to the H⁺ crossover rate, was determined by measuring the CO2 flow from the PSE layer using water-displacement and titration methods for both gas-phase and dissolved CO2 (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Figs. 20–22, and Supplementary Note 4). Gas chromatography confirmed that the recovered CO2 was of high purity up to 99% (excluding water vapor; Supplementary Fig. 20). In addition, the CO2-capture rate (bicarbonate generation rate) should theoretically pair with the Na⁺ crossover rate. As the current density increased, the CO2-capture rate exhibited the same trend as the Na⁺ crossover rate, increasing linearly and reaching 2.6 ml cm⁻2 min⁻1 at 200 mA cm⁻2, while maintaining a gCO2 above 80%. To further demonstrate the carbon balance between CO2 capture and CO2 generation, we used titration to test the HCO3− generation rates and measured the HCO3− concentrations at each inlet and outlet of the circulation system. Tests showed that the HCO3− concentration at the middle chamber outlet decreased, similar to the trend observed for Na⁺. Subsequently, the solution from the middle chamber was introduced into the cathode, where the OH⁻ generated by ORR at the triple-phase boundary captured CO2 molecules. The Na⁺ shuttled from the middle chamber and fixed the generated HCO3−, which merged into the solution existing in the middle chamber. At the same time, the HCO3− concentration in the cathode outlet solution, which is the inlet solution for the middle chamber, remained stable at 0.5 M. We calculated the bicarbonate generation rate by subtracting the HCO3− concentration of the cathode outlet solution from that of the cathode inlet solution. This rate, representing the CO2-capture rate, varied with current density and closely matched the amount of CO2 collected in the middle chamber. The amount of captured CO2 matched the generated quantity, demonstrating equilibrium between captured and generated carbon. The recirculation process within our PSE reactor demonstrates an effective CO2-capture system facilitated by NaHCO3. This system exhibits a high carbon-capture capacity while operating without consuming any chemicals or water throughout the process.

System optimization for low-concentration CO2 capture

While our PSE reactor demonstrates promising energy efficiency for carbon capture, its electrochemical performance has yet to be fully optimized. Potential improvements to enhance reactor efficiency include improving the ionic conductivity of the solid electrolyte, reducing the PSE layer thickness, raising the operating temperature, enhancing the ORR and OER catalysts, and selecting more conductive CEM and PEM. One key advantage of our carbon-capture device is its flexibility in utilizing different ORR catalysts. Replacing the expensive Pt/C catalyst with more abundant alternatives can substantially reduce material costs and mitigate CO poisoning effects often observed with Pt/C. The transition-metal single-atom catalyst (SAC) is a promising candidate, such as Fe or Co single-atomic sites coordinated in N-doped carbon39,40. These SACs exhibit ORR performance similar to commercial Pt/C, while providing high resistance to CO poisoning due to the weak interaction between CO and the transition-metal center41. We synthesized Co-SAC as a representative ORR catalyst to explore these potential benefits. The Co-SAC was synthesized using a hard-template method from our previous study, which exhibits high porosity and uniform distribution of atomical metal sites on the carbon matrix42 (“Methods” section, Inset of Fig. 3c and Supplementary Figs. 23–25). We then assessed the poisoning effects of CO impurities on CO2 capture. CO gas is commonly found in industrial flue gas due to incomplete combustion of hydrocarbons, which can affect the performance of Pt catalysts43,44. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 26, under a fixed current of 50 mA cm⁻2, the cell voltage increased immediately when CO was introduced into the cathode gas stream, with a subsequent rise of about 300 mV over three hours, indicating rapid Pt/C ORR activity degradation. Such poisoning effects, along with Pt scarcity and high cost, limit the practical application of Pt/C-based solid-electrolyte carbon-capture reactors. In contrast, the Co-SAC demonstrated high resistance to CO poisoning due to weak interactions between metal single-atomic sites and CO molecules44. No significant changes in cell voltage were observed when CO or other toxic gases (such as NO and SO2) were added to the input gas stream (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 27), demonstrating Co-SAC’s excellent stability for practical carbon-capture applications.

With inputting a CO2 concentration of 5.6%, the Co-SAC demonstrated comparable catalytic activity and (bi)carbonate generation performance compared to Pt/C (Supplementary Figs. 28 and 29), indicating that the noble-metal Pt/C catalyst can be feasibly replaced with earth-abundant materials. The bicarbonate generation rates of Co-SAC (Supplementary Fig. 30), derived from titration curves, showed a similar trend to those with Pt/C (Fig. 2g). Specifically, the sodium (bi)carbonate generation rate increased almost linearly with ORR current density, ranging from 10 to 500 mA cm−2. At CO2 concentrations of 5.6%, the solution was fully composed of NaHCO3 below 200 mA cm−2, while beyond 200 mA cm−2, a mixture of NaHCO3 and Na2CO3 was formed.

To evaluate the practical stability of our PSE reactor, we also conducted continuous electrolysis for carbon capture and generation in an environment containing 5.6% CO2 + O2, using a recirculation volume of 200 ml (Supplementary Fig. 19). As shown in Fig. 3d, the reactor operated continuously for over 1000 h at a fixed current density of 200 mA cm−2, without noticeable changes in cell voltage. Such stability demonstrates the robustness of our PSE system for prolonged operations under high current densities. We analyzed the middle chamber and catholyte solutions using acid titration and ion chromatography after each generation cycle to confirm the CO2 generation rate and Na⁺ crossover rates. The results showed minimal concentration changes in the recirculating NaHCO3 solution (Fig. 3d)45. We also evaluated the capture efficiency for carbon generation based on either the FENa+ or, more directly, on the collected CO2 gas volume (Fig. 3d). Our PSE system could continuously operate for 1000 h with CO2 generation efficiency exceeding 77.4%. The overall carbon-capture rate matched well with the carbon generation rate. Throughout the test, approximately 85–90% of Na⁺ from the middle layer was transferred to the cathode side, demonstrating consistent and stable operation. The measured gCO2 was slightly lower than the corresponding FENa+, likely due to minor gas leakage in the reactor system. Nevertheless, the close match between these values strongly supports our hypothesized CO2 generation mechanism, Na⁺ quantifications, and the overall stability of our system. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 31, the PEM-AEM system exhibited noticeable fluctuations in cell voltage and faradaic efficiency after 65 h using a cathode Pt/C catalyst, likely due to catalyst poisoning, leading to high voltage and membrane degradation.

Direct air capture (DAC) from commercial air and ambient air in HK

DAC technologies aim to extract carbon dioxide directly from ambient air, where the CO2 concentration is approximately 0.04%, significantly lower than other sources such as flue gases. This low concentration presents challenges such as low efficiency and high energy consumption for DAC processes. To improve the practical relevance of our study, we employed commercial air (air with 400 ppm CO2 (0.04%), Supplementary Table 4, see “Methods” section) and ambient air with a small amount of major pollution (air from Hung Hom, Kowloon, Hong Kong, Supplementary Tables 4 and 5, see “Methods” section) as the gas sources. Instead of using enriched gas mixtures like CO2 + O2 or CO2 + air mixtures, we introduced this air directly into our PSE reactor, designed explicitly for efficient CO2 capture. By utilizing actual air compositions, we aim to demonstrate the feasibility of our system under realistic operating conditions and identify optimal conditions that achieve both high carbon removal efficiencies and high CO2-capture rates. As shown in Fig. 4b, the required voltage was similar for capturing both commercial air and ambient air at the same current density, indicating the reliability of our system across different air sources. When CO2 concentration is reduced to about 0.04%, mass transport is critical in determining the overall carbon-capture performance. This limitation is inherent when dealing with low CO2 concentrations, as the mass transfer rate of CO2 molecules to reactive sites becomes a bottleneck. To evaluate the prototype under more stringent conditions relevant to practical applications, we also assessed the CO2 removal rate from air, which is critical for DAC. As shown in Fig. 4c, in a direct flow mode, by pumping air and an atomized water mixture, the (bi)carbonate generation rate, equal to the CO2-capture rate, increased almost linearly in low ORR current densities (1–30 mA cm−2) with a slight reduction at high current densities (30–50 mA cm−2). In our PSE reactor for capturing 0.04% CO2, the cathodic solution consisted entirely of NaHCO3 at current densities below 5 mA cm−2. At 10 mA cm−2, 51% of the solution was NaHCO3, and above 20 mA cm−2, Na2CO3 became predominant. Although the bicarbonate generation ratio remained above 80% at low current densities, the CO2 removal rate was lower. At 30 mA cm−2, the reactor achieved a 93% CO2 removal rate with bicarbonate efficiency over 82%, indicating highly efficient carbon capture even at low CO2 concentrations. When the current density increased to 40–50 mA cm−2, the removal rate exceeded 95%, but the (bi)carbonate generation ratio dropped below 70%. As expected, there is a trade-off between the removal rate and bicarbonate generation ratio; increasing the ORR current density reduced the bicarbonate generation ratio due to limited CO2 mass diffusion under DAC conditions. We selected 30 mA cm−2 for the stability test to achieve a high removal rate and bicarbonate generation ratio. The superior performance of Co-SAC underscores its potential as an effective catalyst for DAC applications, offering both efficiency and cost advantages over noble-metal catalysts. Most importantly, we operated the cell continuously for DAC using ambient air for over 2000 h at a fixed current density of 30 mA cm⁻2, with a high CO2 generation efficiency above 78.0% and cell voltage maintained at ~2.0 V (Fig. 4d). Additionally, no visible structural changes were observed in the Co-SAC catalyst before and after the stability test, indicating good structural stability of the catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 32). This long-term stability test demonstrates the durability of our PSE system in ambient air, making it viable for continuous DAC operations.

Techno-economic analysis (TEA)

Previous electrochemical CO2-capture studies often reported energy efficiency based on half-cell reaction performance, assuming ideal conditions such as the full conversion of alkaline solutions to bicarbonate during CO2 absorption27,46,47. These assumptions may underestimate energy consumption, particularly at relevant capture rates or under low CO2 concentrations. In contrast, our solid-electrolyte device provides a more practical assessment of energy consumption, as it was evaluated in a full-cell setup, encompassing both CO2 absorption and release without making such idealized assumptions (Fig. 5a). Our carbon-capture energy consumption starts at approximately 90 kJ per mol CO2 (0.9 V of onset voltage) and gradually increases with higher capture rates (Supplementary Fig. 29). We believe that there is considerable potential for improving energy efficiency, and several strategies are proposed for future enhancement. The cost of CO2 capture mainly depends on its concentration. Flue gas from fossil fuel combustion, such as coal or natural gas power plants, contains less than 15% CO2, while ambient air contains approximately 0.041% CO248. Capturing CO2 from power plants or steel mills with low concentrations can cost over $100 per ton. We performed a TEA based on the electrochemical performance of our PSE reactor for CO2 generation, adapting previously reported methodologies47,49,50. Several process assumptions were made based on current feedstock prices and electrolyzer performance, with sensitivity analysis considering potential future fluctuations. For the NaHCO3 input case, the total system cost was estimated at approximately US $55.68 tonCO2−1 (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Note 8). Key cost contributors include electrolyzer stack manufacturing ($10.73 per ton CO2), electricity ($24.62 per ton CO2, assuming $0.02 per kWh electricity price), input chemicals ($12.71 per ton CO2), and balance-of-plant (BoP) costs ($4.69 per ton CO2). Daily maintenance and stack replacement account for $2.93 per ton CO249,51. Our design can produce NaHCO3 as a reversible cyclic mediator for low CO2 concentrations (over 1%) in air, and Na2CO3 for DAC. Further cost reductions could be achieved by lowering electrolyzer costs and enhancing reaction performance. Reducing the cell voltage and increasing Na⁺ transport efficiency could decrease the cost of carbon capture by approximately $7 and $13 per ton of CO2, respectively (Fig. 5c). A detailed global sensitivity analysis shows that generation costs are susceptible to variations in electricity prices (ranging from $0.01 to $0.03 per kWh) and current densities (1–50 mA cm−2). Electricity prices are tied to operating costs, while current density affects electrolyzer size and capital cost for capturing 1 ton of CO2 (Supplementary Note 8). These two parameters dominate the operation cost and capital cost.

Developing strategies to avoid current density drops under specific voltage changes and monitoring electricity policy and price fluctuations will help ensure economic feasibility. For example, the generation cost for carbonate input is nearly double that of bicarbonate due to the doubled electrolyzer size and energy consumption. Still, the trends in sensitivity analysis are similar. Advances in renewable energy and electrolyzer technologies may help reduce generation costs in the near future. In addition, alternative redox couples such as HER/HOR or other organic and inorganic molecular redox systems could help lower overpotentials. Reduced ohmic drop could be achieved with a thinner middle layer and more conductive solid electrolytes. Adjusting parameters such as temperature for improved kinetics and thickness of the middle chamber for enhanced mass transport may also benefit different application scenarios (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. 36). These improvements could potentially reduce carbon-capture costs to around $33 per ton, which becomes economically sustainable compared with alternative conventional carbon-capture methods.

Discussion

Our PSE reactor establishes a versatile platform, which captures CO2 from diverse sources via ORR/OER redox electrocatalysis with a recirculation mode, generating a high-purity (>99%) CO2 stream. The reactor achieved carbon-capture rates of up to 10 A (3060 ml h−1, 100 cm2). Additionally, it exhibited a FENa+ of ~85%, indicating potential for practical applications. With improvements in cell components and operating temperature, we have achieved over 2000 h of continuous DAC operation. The system processed air at a rate corresponding to approximately 8820 kgair day−1 m−2. Future advancements in materials and device design are required, such as reducing the thickness of the solid-electrolyte layer, optimizing operating temperature and pressure, improving the couple redox catalysts, and refining reaction pathways. These improvements present opportunities to enhance energy efficiency and reduce the costs associated with carbon capture for sustainable, practical deployment.

Methods

Chemicals

Chemicals, including sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥98%), sodium carbonate (Na2CO3, ≥99.8%), and sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3, ≥99.8%), etc., were purchased from Aladdin. The Pt/C powder, Nafion-117 membrane, and Nafion-N2100TX membrane were obtained from Fuel Cell Store. The IrO2 electrode was purchased from Dioxide Materials. The deionized (DI) water (18.2 MΩ·cm) was used throughout all experiments.

Synthesis of Co-SACs

We synthesized Co-SAC by modifying our previously reported method7. Initially, 1.0 g of o-phenylenediamine, 0.44 g of CoCl2, and 2.0 g of SiO2 nanoparticles (7 nm, Aladdin) were mixed in 20 ml of 1.0 M HCl solution. This mixture was sonicated for 30 min and then stirred for 30 min. Subsequently, 15 ml of 1.0 M HCl solution containing 3.0 g of ammonium peroxydisulfate ((NH4)2S2O8) was added dropwise under vigorous stirring. The mixture was dried using a rotary evaporator after polymerizing in an ice bath for approximately one day. The resulting powder was annealed under an argon atmosphere at 800 °C for 2 h. Finally, the product underwent successive alkaline (2.0 M NaOH) and acidic (2.0 M H2SO4) leaching to remove the SiO2 nanoparticle templates and unstable cobalt species, respectively, yielding the Co-SAC.

Preparation of the electrode

40 mg of the as-synthesized catalyst with 4 ml of 2-propanol (Sigma-Aldrich) were mixed with 160 μl binder solution (5% Nafion 117) to create a catalyst ink. This ink was sonicated for about 30 min to ensure homogeneity and then spray-coated onto a 5 × 5 cm2 hydrophilic carbon cloth electrode (ELAT-Hydrophilic Plain Cloth, Fuel Cell Store) at 60 °C. The prepared electrode was hot-pressed onto the Nafion-N2100TX membrane at 135 °C for 2 min. The Pt/C electrode (Fuel Cell Store) was prepared following the same procedure. The IrO2 electrode from Dioxide Materials was used as the anode during the electrochemical experiments.

Assemble solid-state electrolyte reactor

The CO2-capture process was conducted using a porous solid-electrolyte (PSE) reactor, as depicted in Fig. 2b. This reactor features a proton exchange membrane (PEM) and a cation exchange membrane (CEM) configuration. The CEM adjacent to the cathode, composed of Pt/C on carbon cloth, is Nafion N2100TX. The PEM near the anode side, consisting of IrO2, is the Nafion-117 membrane. Both Nafion 117 and Nafion N2100TX membranes were near the porous solid electrolyte (Dowex 50WX8, hydrogen form, Sigma-Aldrich). Before use, the Nafion membranes were sequentially treated at 80 °C for one hour each in 3 wt% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), DI water, 1.0 M sulfuric acid (H2SO4), and again in DI water to remove potential contaminants and ensure complete protonation of the membranes. All the electrochemical measurements were operated at 25 °C. A BioLogic VSP-3e workstation was employed to record the electrochemical response. The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy measurements were conducted within a frequency range of 100,000 to 0.01 Hz. All cell resistance was measured by potentiostatic electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (PEIS), and all the cell voltages in our work were reported without any IR compensation (non-IR corrected) unless otherwise specified.

Electrochemical CO2 capture

Direct flow mode

For the standard tests, different amount of CO2 (Linde HKO Ltd., 99.9%), 500 standard cubic centimetres per minute (sccm) O2 (Linde HKO Ltd., 99.7%) or commercial air (Linde HKO Ltd., zero grade), were mixed by precisely adjusting the gas flow rates using digital mass flow controllers (Alicat), followed by a concentration calibration (10.7%, 5.6%, 4.1% and 1.2% CO2) measuring by a CO2 meter (CO2Meter) for all tests. Then the cathode side was supplied with mixed gas and DI water (continuously flowed with 0.9 ml min−1 of DI water) for the ORR. The water flow rate was controlled using a syringe pump, and the outlet flow rate was calibrated with a measuring cylinder. In the middle chamber, a styrene-divinylbenzene sulfonated copolymer (Dowex 50WX8, hydrogen form, Sigma-Aldrich) was used as the solid-electrolyte cation conductor. 0.25 M Na2CO3 (pH = 11.92 ± 0.24) or 0.5 M NaHCO3 (pH = 8.54 ± 0.26) solutions were introduced into the PSE layer, with their flow rates also regulated by a syringe pump. As the reaction progressed, the carbon-containing solution in the middle PSE layer gradually became more acidic, releasing high-concentration CO2 gas. Through a gas-liquid separator, high-concentration CO2 was collected using either a gas bag, and the gas volume was determined using the water-displacement method. As a demonstration, we collected pure CO2 gas using a gas-liquid separation device, filling an empty burette with gas and liquid from the middle chamber (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 21). The residual Na⁺ from the middle PSE layer and the concentration of NaHCO3 produced at the cathode were determined using ion chromatography (IC). The anode side was supplied with a 0.1 M H2SO4 solution at a flow rate of 1.8 ml min−1 for the oxygen evolution reaction (OER). For direct air-capture tests, we used 500 sccm commercial air (Linde HKO Ltd., zero grade) or ambient air (collected from Hung Hom, Hong Kong (22.3046° N, 114.1796° E)) as the input source. For a scaled 10 × 10 cm2 cell, the total gas flow increased to 50 l min−1 to ensure sufficient CO2 for CO2 capture, the concentration of Na2CO3 solution increased from 0.25 M to 0.50 M, and the solution of the middle PSE layer increased to 4.5 ml min−1 flow rate.

Recirculation mode

A closed-loop cycling system was developed, schematically illustrated in Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 19. Firstly, 200 ml of a 0.5 M NaHCO3 solution (pH = 8.54 ± 0.26) was introduced into the middle PSE layer at a 0.9 ml min−1 flow rate to initiate a reaction. As the reaction progressed, the carbon-containing solution in the middle PSE layer gradually became more acidic, releasing high-concentration CO2 gas. Through a gas-liquid separator, high-concentration CO2 was collected using either a gas bag, and the gas volume was determined using the water-displacement method. The remaining middle chamber solution was mixed with flue gas/air and then pumped back to the cathode, for the purpose of capturing CO2 to produce (bi)carbonate solution. After a gas-liquid separation process, the cathodic solution is pumped back into the middle chamber without any chemical or water consumption. This arrangement establishes a recirculation mode between the cathode and the middle chamber. 0.1 M H2SO4 was supplied at a flow rate of 1.8 ml min−1 using a syringe pump on the anode. For direct air-capture tests, 200 ml Na2CO3 solution (0.25 M) (pH = 11.92 ± 0.24) was introduced into the middle PSE layer at a 0.9 ml min−1 flow rate to initiate a reaction. In a practical stability test, a volume decrease of approximately 1–3% was observed in the catholyte every 100 h, as the gas-liquid separation process carried away some water vapor. It is thus necessary to replenish the reduced water volume to compensate for the evaporated water.

Material characterizations

Various advanced techniques were employed to analyze samples. A TESCAN MIRA field emission scanning electron microscope was utilized for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were obtained using a JEM-2100F transmission electron microscope. Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) images were carried out on a Titan Themis G2 instrument. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed on a PHI 5000 VersaProbe III with Al Kα radiation. All XPS spectra were calibrated by shifting the detected C 1s peak to 284.6 eV.
