Wired for life: Intracellular electron nanowires help methane-producing microbes grow
Authors: Sibel Ebru Yalcin, Nikhil S. Malvankar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Methane-producing microbes use iron-sulfur cofactors as a nanowire to split electron energy through specialized megacomplexes. Understanding this process could enable more efficient methane production.

We are all breathing now to get rid of the excess electrons left behind during metabolism. However, many bacteria and archaea (single-cell microorganisms) need to survive without such oxygen-like soluble, membrane-ingestible electron acceptors. A prime example is methanogenic archaea that use carbon dioxide (CO2) and hydrogen to produce an estimated 1 billion tons of methane. As methane is 30 times more potent in trapping heat than CO2, methanogens are major contributors to global climate change. Capturing these vast amounts of methane could offer an alternate renewable fuel source. Therefore, identifying the machinery that enables methanogens to grow using exceptionally low energy may provide key insights into how to optimize their efficiency for methane production.

To survive, methanogens need to perform thermodynamically unfavorable biological reactions. e.g., the first step of methanogenesis is reduction and condensation of CO2 to an organic carrier molecule, but available electron sources from the environment, such as hydrogen and formate, lack the energy necessary to drive this reaction. How do methanogens achieve this miraculous feat without breaking any physical laws? Recent studies provide one possible solution: electron bifurcation (EB), in which enzymes split the energy of an electron pair to perform uphill processes by lower-energy electrons coupled with a downhill process by higher-energy electrons (1) (Fig. 1). Thus, the overall process does not violate the laws of thermodynamics and minimizes the wasting of the released energy as heat and maximizes overall energy conservation. High-energy electrons derived from EB maximize the efficiency of metabolic machinery. EB is implicated in diverse enigmatic microbial metabolic processes such as CO2 reduction, nitrogen fixation, hydrogen and methane production (2), extracellular electron transfer (EET) (3), and direct interspecies electron transfer (DIET) (4). This long-range ET occurs extracellularly via micrometer-long chains of hemes in polymeric cytochrome “nanowires” at ultrafast (<100 fs) rates (5) and to relieve oxidative stress via stacked aromatic residues (6) to drive various globally important environmental processes and applications for bioenergy, bioelectronics, and bioremediation (7).

DOWN TO THE WIRE

In this issue of Science Advances (8), San Segundo-Acosta et al. report the discovery of another kind of nanowire made up of polyferredoxin MvhB that electronically connects electron-bifurcating heterodisulfide reductase (Hdr) and formylmethanofuran dehydrogenase (Fmd) catalyst (Fig. 1). Cryo–electron microscopy (cryo-EM) highlights the careful arrangement of iron-sulfur redox enzymes to maximize EB efficiency and limit short-circuit reactions that waste energy. By comparing the particle images using three-dimensional classification, the authors found different states of the complex due to a large rotation called the mobile arm (Mvh in Fig. 1) that lines up the iron-sulfur cofactors. First, an electron pair derived from formate via a formate dehydrogenase subunit or directly from hydrogen via an organic hydrogen carrier called F420 moves along a chain of iron-sulfur clusters in MvhB to reach the flavins. The flavin-based EB then splits the energy of this electron pair. The lower-energy electron is transferred to the Hdr radical acceptor, and the higher-energy electron is passed to an isolated iron-sulfur cluster that acts as part of another nanoscale wire when the Mvh-Hdr mobile arm swings back for electrons to pass through another set of clusters to reach the CO2 reduction site in Fmd.

This Mvh-Hdr-Fmd supercomplex by Class I methanogen Methanothermobacter marburgensis has distinct architecture and stoichiometry from that of Class II methanogen Methanospirillum hungatei’s formate dehydrogenase (Fdh)–Hdr–Fmd supercomplex. Discovery of these divergent complexes reconciles the structural discrepancy between Class I and II methanogenic supercomplexes while unifying the key role of iron-sulfur clusters in EB. Correlating complex stoichiometry to the growth environment suggests how diverse microbes adjust the stoichiometry for adaptation and acquire polyferredoxin FmdF by horizontal gene transfer from nonmethanogenic archaea. The authors further correlate the differences in supercomplex structure to the adaptation of methanogens to different environments, showing the importance of microbes adjusting the stoichiometry of the complexes for adaptation [see (8)].

These studies also highlight challenges to understanding the EB mechanism in complex and dynamical redox assemblies. EB enzymes couple electron transfer to large conformational changes. Therefore, it is critical to capture both pre- and postbifurcation conformational states (2). However, cryo-EM structures provide static images and require extensive sampling to capture conformational states and could be complemented with molecular dynamics and solution-based techniques that do not restrain protein motion, such as bulk [nuclear magnetic resonance and small-angle x-ray scattering (SAXS)] and nanoscale Raman and infrared spectroscopy to determine protein conformations (7). SAXS studies reveal how the EB enzyme coordinates conformational changes (9) when NADH activates the enzyme’s mechanics. The enzyme dynamics enable EB by leveraging the enzyme motions to complete the catalytic cycle. Visualizing such enzyme mechanics and conformations may reveal how EB occurs by directing electrons over large distances. Such dynamic information is critical to understanding how EB can help build microbial energy flux models and aid in the annotation of previously unknown genes.

CHALLENGES AND OPPORTUNITIES

In addition to challenges in understanding how conformational changes affect catalytic function, computational studies have suggested the role of inverted potentials at the bifurcating site that need to be experimentally tested (2). Another intriguing possibility is that the quantum effects of coherence (10) and spin polarization (11), which are predicted in microbial nanowires to account for measured ultrafast ET (5) and high electron conductivity (10), could also be responsible for achieving the correlated electron motion necessary for EB (2). Electron spin could control the path of bifurcating electrons because spin interaction between Fe-S clusters and flavosemiquinone species is proposed to increase the efficiency of flavin-based EB (1, 2). In addition to understanding the EB mechanism to increase metabolic efficiency, the next frontier is to translate these studies into novel protein designs that can transport charges at different potentials (2). These structural and mechanistic advances in understanding EB lay the perfect foundation for developing novel applications. The modular architecture of these enzymes suggests that metabolic engineering using synthetic biology can use such bifurcating units to enhance the efficiency of renewable energy produced by microbes during carbon fixation and hydrogen production. Recent studies have developed de novo designs inspired by EB enzymes to predict bioinspired structures capable of driving multiple, thermodynamically unfavorable ET reactions to achieve high efficiency.

EB VIA EXTRACELLULAR NANOWIRES?

So, why should we care about San Segundo-Acosta and colleagues’ findings? One reason is that these elegant structures and computations for short-range, intracellular EB may be relevant to many different microbes, not just the methanogens studied. It could extend to long-range, extracellular EB driving energy transfer and electron flow through diverse species and environments. This might mean that a variety of other microbial processes are also mediated by EB. A notable example is electron bifurcating flavins binding to outer-surface cytochromes (12), which can polymerize into nanowires (5, 7). These flavins are proposed to use EB to control the direction of electron flow to and from the microbe (3) by shifting the redox potential by ∼300 mV upon switching from electron-donating (anodic) to electron-accepting (cathodic) growth mode without large changes in gene expression or protein synthesis. EB is also proposed to uncouple interspecies electron and proton flux pathways during DIET (4). However, all these studies are limited to the cellular level, and the identity of proteins capable of EB is still unclear. As Segundo-Acosta et al. (8) have shown, leveraging revolutions in structural biology and data science is a perfect time for scientists to resolve long-standing puzzles and learn lessons in energy efficiency from these fascinating microbes.
