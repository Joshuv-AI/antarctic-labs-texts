Clearance of Early Complement Protein From CSF After Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage and Influence on Neurologic Outcome
Authors: Joseph R. Geraghty, Eitan A. Katz, Mitchell Butler, Fernando D. Testai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesAneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) triggers a robust inflammatory response, which has been associated with brain injury and poor outcome. The complement system is an integral part of the innate immune system activated after acute brain injury. The early complement proteins C1q and C3 have roles in glial-neuronal interactions and synaptic pruning. The objective of this study was to determine whether changes in CSF complement proteins over time after aSAH are associated with neurologic outcome.MethodsWe performed an exploratory retrospective case-control study investigating the association of CSF levels and clearance of C1q and C3 with outcome in patients with aSAH and compared with controls with normal CSF. Clearance was defined as the percent change of complement protein levels between 2 time points corresponding to peaks of early (<72 hours) and delayed (5–7 days) brain injury after aSAH. The primary endpoint was functional outcome, assessed by Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS; range 1–5 with higher scores denoting better outcome), at the time of discharge. Good functional outcome was defined as GOS 4–5. Complement protein clearance was adjusted by baseline differences, and receiver operating characteristic analysis was performed.ResultsEarly after aSAH, there was a robust increase in C1q (median 89.10 vs 6.42 ng/mL, p < 0.0001) and C3 (78.00 vs 8.72 µg/mL, p = 0.0001) in CSF of 20 patients with aSAH compared with 11 controls. Between early and late time points, both C1q (97.38 vs 47.85 ng/mL, p = 0.0326) and C3 (85.23 vs 23.10 µg/mL, p = 0.0004) decreased in those with good outcome. Percent clearance of C3 from CSF was larger in those with good vs poor neurologic outcome (median 74.85% vs 11.43% reduction over time, p = 0.0159). C3 clearance, particularly when adjusted by baseline Glasgow coma scale on admission, was a highly sensitive and specific marker of neurologic outcome after aSAH (area under curve = 0.990, 95% confidence interval 0.958–1.000, sensitivity 90.9%, specificity 100.0%).DiscussionIncreased CSF clearance of C3 within the first week after aSAH appears to be associated with improved neurologic outcome. Future strategies to promote clearance of inflammatory mediators such as early complement proteins from CSF may, therefore, improve outcomes after aSAH.

Introduction

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) triggers a robust inflammatory response that is most pronounced during the period of early brain injury (EBI), which includes the first 72 hours after aneurysm rupture. Early inflammation likely sets the stage for feared complications of aSAH, including cerebral vasospasm and delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI), which occur an average of 5–7 days after aSAH, although can occur up to 14 days after injury.1 Current methods to assess initial severity of brain injury have variable prognostic value and are not entirely objective.2-4 These include clinical assessments such as Hunt-Hess score and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) performed on admission and radiologic assessments such as the modified Fisher scale. Ongoing research has focused on identifying biomarkers that might predict outcome by more timely, objective, and cost-effective means.5 Biomarkers measured in the CSF are particularly useful in the setting of CNS injuries because of their ability to reflect intrathecal changes.6 Patients with high-grade aSAH typically have CSF diversion devices, which allow for routine CSF sampling. Thus, the presence of external ventricular drains (EVDs) provides a unique opportunity to measure, in real time, the concentrations of biochemical mediators that participate in signaling pathways associated with brain injury.

Crosstalk between the brain and immune systems after aneurysm rupture may be one potential mechanism contributing to secondary brain injury in aSAH. One component of the innate immune system that may contribute to neurotoxicity after acute brain injury is the complement system, which represents a large number of soluble proteins found throughout systemic circulation as well as in tissue, including the brain.7,8 Complement proteins are predominantly synthesized in the liver and, in smaller amounts, by other cell types including circulating monocytes and tissue macrophages. Within the CNS, complement proteins are produced by neurons and glia; however, microglia and astrocytes serve as the predominant source.9 Historically, the complement system has been associated with the recognition and elimination of pathogens, serving as a bridge between the innate and adaptive immune system.10 However, aberrant complement activation has also been observed in a wide range of noninfectious diseases.

Within the CNS, changes in the early classical complement cascade proteins C1q and C3 have been shown to play a role in microglial-mediated synaptic pruning during development and in neurologic and psychiatric diseases including temporal lobe epilepsy, Alzheimer disease, and schizophrenia.11-13 In traumatic brain injury models, early complement-mediated microglial phagocytosis of synapses contributes to chronic neuroinflammation and progressive cognitive decline.8,14 In aSAH, previous studies have largely focused on downstream complement proteins such as cleavage products of C3 and C5 and the membrane attack complex while others have focused on the alternative or lectin pathways of complement activation.15-19 Compared with C5, there is relatively less known about early, upstream complement proteins in SAH, especially those such as C1q and C3, which are implicated in activation of glial cells and synaptic pruning. We hypothesized that increased levels of C1q and C3 in CSF from patients with aSAH would be associated with poor neurologic outcome. Rather than focusing on a single time point after aSAH, we measured CSF concentrations of C1q and C3 at 2 time points corresponding to periods of early and delayed brain injury. In addition, we examined the association of time-dependent clearance of complement proteins from CSF with neurologic outcome.

Methods

Study Population

Patients were identified from the University of Illinois NeuroRepository, a biorepository containing patient specimens including CSF from 2010 to 2018. Within the biorepository, CSF collected from patients with aSAH prospectively was available, and patients eligible for this study were identified. Patients were eligible to participate in this study if they were at least 18 years of age, had nontraumatic SAH confirmed by CT, aneurysm visualized by either digital subtraction angiogram or noninvasive CT angiography, and the presence of an EVD, which allowed for CSF collection. CSF was collected as close as possible to the time of admission (no later than 72 hours from symptom onset to correspond with the time window of EBI) and then 5–7 days after symptom onset (to fall within the typical window for delayed brain injury).1 Only patients who had CSF collected at both time points were included. Exclusion criteria included nonaneurysmal SAH, pregnancy, previous neurologic disability (defined as preadmission modified Rankin scale ≥2), or history of stroke, brain tumor, intracranial surgery unrelated to SAH, traumatic brain injury, previously treated cerebral aneurysm, or other vascular malformation. We additionally recruited control participants to use as a reference. Control participants were nonpregnant persons 18 years of age or older who underwent lumbar puncture or lumbar puncture for evaluation of headache but had normal brain imaging and CSF analysis—including chemistry, differential cell count, and cultures.

Study Design

We conducted a retrospective case-control study based off of CSF collected prospectively as part of the University of Illinois NeuroRepository. The primary outcome was functional status at time of discharge, assessed via the Glasgow Outcome Score (GOS), a commonly used outcome measure in aSAH clinical research.20 The GOS ranges from 1 to 5, with higher values denoting increased independence. Good functional outcome was defined as GOS 4–5, and poor outcome was defined as GOS of 1–3, based on stratification used in previous studies.21,22 Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, and laboratory data were collected across all groups. For CSF, all measurements besides complement proteins were done within the first 72 hours and used for correlation analyses. Within the aSAH group, aneurysm location (anterior or posterior), aneurysm treatment (coiling vs clipping), initial modified Fisher scale, Hunt-Hess score, GCS at presentation, as well as complications during hospital stay such as angiographic vasospasm and DCI were recorded. Angiographic vasospasm was determined via cerebral angiography and the impression of a neurointerventionalist. DCI was defined as the development of new focal neurologic deficits, decreased level of consciousness (persistent drop in GCS by ≥2 points), or both, with or without the presence of angiographic vasospasm, which was not otherwise explained by routine medical evaluation.23 To quantify total blood volume for each patient, we also collected the initial noncontrast head CT for each patient.

CSF Collection and Storage

At each collection time point, approximately 10 mL of CSF was collected using sterile technique from the distal port of the EVD buretrol. After collection of CSF, samples were immediately placed on ice and centrifuged at 1,000× g for 5 minutes to remove any large debris. Supernatants were collected, aliquoted into cryovials, deidentified, and stored in a secure facility at −80°C until the time of analysis. All samples were analyzed for protein content and cell count, including red and white blood cells.

Inflammatory Biomarker Analysis

Measurement of C1q and C3 complement protein levels was performed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) using commercially available kits (C1q: Invitrogen BMS2099; C3: Abcam ab108823). Dilutions of each antibody were first optimized based on available literature, protocol instructions, and testing of several dilutions in an initial optimization.24 For C1q measurement, CSF from patients with aSAH was diluted 1:4 in assay buffer while that from healthy controls was undiluted and added directly to the ELISA well. For C3 measurement, CSF from patients with aSAH was diluted 1:5,000 while that from healthy controls was diluted 1:1,000 in assay buffer. Serum from a healthy control was used as positive control and diluted 1:50,000. All experimental samples were run in triplicate. Results were read using a standard plate reader (450 nm), and concentrations of C1q (ng/mL) and C3 (µg/mL) were calculated using a standard curve plotted based on known standard concentrations. Clearance of CSF complement protein was defined as the percent change between the early and late time points, with positive values reflecting increase in concentration and negative values reflecting decrease in concentration over time.

Blood Quantification From CT Scans

To quantitatively assess the total extent of hemorrhage, we analyzed the first available CT image in each patient. The open-source, deep learning model–based segmentation algorithm developed by Thanellas et al.25 validated in patients with SAH was used to identify hemorrhage automatically from noncontrast CT images. Images were preprocessed, and the segmentation algorithm was run according to the online instructions. The code for image preprocessing and quantification of hemorrhage volumes was written in Matlab (Version 9.5.0.1265761, The MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA) and adapted from previous studies in our laboratory.26 Volumetric quantification from segmentation algorithm outputs was done as previously described.26 Briefly, the segmentation algorithm produced a “binary mask” that reports at each voxel whether hemorrhage is present (voxel value = 1) or absent (voxel value = 0). Hemorrhage volumes were then obtained by summing over all voxels in the binary mask, multiplying this value by the image dimensions to convert to mm3, and dividing by 1,000 to obtain the final value in cm3 (mL). Final CT scan volume was then correlated with CSF concentrations of C1q and C3 measured within the first 72 hours.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics of controls and patients with aSAH with and without poor outcome were compared. Descriptive variables are presented as number of patients with percentages or median and interquartile range. Blood volume calculations are presented as mean and SD. Group sizes were determined based on available eligible patients during the study period; no formal a priori power calculation was performed for this exploratory study. Post-hoc power analysis was performed to estimate power. Fisher exact test or Pearson χ2 test was used to compare nominal variables. Mann-Whitney test was used to compare continuous variables. For comparisons of 3 or more groups, Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn multiple comparisons test was used to identify between-group differences. Two-way ANOVA with repeated measures was used to assess clearance of C1q and C3 over time and based on neurologic outcome. Complement levels were correlated with other CSF analyses via Spearman rank correlation coefficient. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to assess the ability of C3 clearance over time (expressed as percent change) to distinguish between good vs poor outcome and compared with commonly used clinical assessments. For any variable with baseline difference between patients with good and poor outcome (p < 0.05), a multivariable logistic regression model was developed to adjust the primary outcome of C3 clearance by these variables and included in ROC analysis. Youden index, which places equal value on sensitivity and specificity, was calculated to determine optimal test cutoffs. A p value of <0.05 after adjustment for multiplicity of testing was considered statistically significant. Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (Version 30, IBM SPSS, Chicago, IL) software was used to conduct the analysis. Figures were made using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.4.2, La Jolla, CA).

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. This study was approved by the local institutional review board (#2009-0694). Written informed consent was required from all patients or designated proxies.

Data Availability

Data not provided in the article because of space limitations may be shared (anonymized) at the request of any qualified investigator for purposes of replicating procedures and results.

Results

We screened 28 patients with aSAH. Of these, 20 had CSF collected within both prespecified time points and had no missing data. We then identified 11 controls, resulting in a total of 31 patients in the study. Baseline characteristics of patients are shown in Table. There was no difference in age or sex between control and patients with aSAH (p > 0.05). We next compared patients with aSAH stratified by functional outcome using the GOS. Among the 20 patients with aSAH, 9 (45.0%) had good outcome and 11 (55.0%) had poor outcome at time of discharge. Those with poor outcome were more likely to have lower GCS on admission compared with those with good outcome (median GCS 9 [6–14] vs 15 [14–15], p = 0.007). There were no differences in age, sex, aneurysm location, type of surgery, or modified Fisher scale between the 2 groups. Hunt-Hess score was higher in the poor outcome group (median 4 [3–5]) compared with the good outcome group (median 3 [2–3]); however, this was not statistically significant (p = 0.080). Sixteen out of 20 patients with aSAH (80.0%) developed angiographic vasospasm; however, there was no difference in those with good or poor outcomes (p > 0.999). Ten out of 20 patients with aSAH (50.0%) had evidence of DCI. There was a higher incidence of DCI in the poor outcome group (72.7%) compared with the good outcome group (22.2%); however, this did not meet the prespecified threshold for statistical significance (p = 0.070).

We next compared levels of C1q and C3 between controls and patients with aSAH over time. Compared with controls, CSF levels of C1q were significantly increased at both the early (median [IQR] 6.42 [3.72–17.85] vs 89.10 [43.26–117.50] ng/mL, p < 0.0001) and late (6.42 [3.72–17.85] vs 46.84 [25.40–67.46] ng/mL, p = 0.0016) time points (Figure 1A). Compared with controls, CSF levels of C3 were increased at the early time point (8.72 [6.37–15.33] vs 78.00 [23.08–166.70] µg/mL, p = 0.0001). There were increased C3 levels at the late time point (8.72 [6.37–15.33] vs 22.26 [12.99–32.87] µg/mL); however, this was not statistically significant (p = 0.0812, Figure 1B). Thus, while elevated C1q levels persist over time, C3 levels appear to be cleared out of the CSF in patients with aSAH.

After demonstrating that both C1q and C3 concentrations are elevated early in CSF from patients with aSAH compared with healthy controls, we then analyzed changes in C1q and C3 levels as a function of time across those with good vs poor outcome. Between the early and late time points, patients with good functional outcome had significant decreases in C1q (97.38 [45.06–125.70] vs 47.85 [25.79–65.05] ng/mL, p = 0.0326) and C3 (85.23 [73.98–168.39] vs 23.10 [13.25–37.13] µg/mL, p = 0.0004, Figure 2, A and B). When expressed as a percentage change over time between patients with good vs poor outcome, there was no difference in C1q (median −31.49% vs −4.79%, p = 0.1519, Figure 2C). However, there was a significant difference with C3, whereby those with good outcome had a median 74.85% (interquartile range: 59.52–84.41) reduction over time while those with poor outcome had a median 11.43% (interquartile range: 61.48% reduction to 36.78% increase, p = 0.0159, Figure 2D). This corresponds to a median 6.55-fold decrease in CSF C3 concentration overtime compared with those with poor outcome. Post-hoc power analysis based on observed differences in C3 clearance between good and poor outcome indicated that the sample size of 20 patients in the 2 groups provided an approximately 85–90% power to detect this difference using a two-sided Mann-Whitney U test at α = 0.05. This is likely because of the large effect size differences in C3 clearance between those with good vs poor neurologic outcome. Thus, in those with good neurologic outcome, CSF concentration of C3 is cleared while those with poor outcome are unable to clear C3 from the CSF, suggesting ongoing neuroinflammation.

As expected, patients with aSAH had elevated CSF protein, red blood cells, and white blood cells compared with healthy controls (all p < 0.001). Within participants with aSAH, there were no differences in CSF protein, red blood cells, or white blood cells between those with good or poor outcome (all p > 0.05). CSF red blood cells are likely an imperfect proxy for blood volume; therefore, we next analyzed blood volume directly by measuring hyperdense signal on head CT. Blood within the subarachnoid space was measured using previously described algorithms (Figure 4A). Average measured blood volume across all patients was 18.61 ± 17.64 mL. Blood volume on head CT did not correlate with early CSF levels of C1q (p = 0.9749, r = −0.0075, Figure 4B) or C3 (p = 0.6633, r = 0.1038, Figure 4C). Thus, it appears that elevated levels of complement proteins C1q and C3 in the CSF of patients with SAH does not directly reflect increased blood.

Given the observed difference in C3 clearance and its relation to functional outcome, we next assessed whether C3 clearance can be used to classify patients with good or poor outcome by ROC analysis. We compared C3 clearance with the Hunt-Hess score and admission GCS, 2 established clinical assessments of aSAH severity, which have prognostic value. GCS was chosen as it was significantly different among our patient population while Hunt-Hess was chosen because of its higher specificity for patients with aSAH, despite not reaching statistical significance in our study (Table). Given significant baseline difference in GCS in patients with good vs poor outcome, we also created a multivariable logistic regression model including both GCS and C3 clearance. Ordinal regression model was not performed given relatively low sample size within some of the GOS categories. In logistic regression, all 4 variables were able to classify patients with good vs poor outcome (all p < 0.05, Figure 5A). Baseline GCS (area under curve [AUC] = 0.848, p < 0.0001, 95% CI 0.667–1.000) and percent change in C3 (AUC = 0.818, p = 0.001, 95% CI 0.623–1.000) performed similarly and were superior to the Hunt-Hess score (AUC = 0.732, p = 0.045, 95% CI 0.505–0.960, Figure 5B). The model including both C3 clearance and GCS performed superiorly (AUC = 0.990, p < 0.0001, 95% CI 0.958–1.000).

With equal weight given to sensitivity and specificity, we next calculated Youden index to identify an ideal cutoff point that differentiated between patients with good vs poor outcome (Figure 5C). A Hunt-Hess score greater than 3 on admission was associated with 54.5% sensitivity and 88.9% specificity at classifying patients with poor outcome. Baseline GCS less than or equal to 13 was associated with 100.0% sensitivity and 72.7% specificity. A decrease in C3 between early and late time points less than 42.33% was associated with 63.6% sensitivity and 100.0% specificity at classifying those with poor neurologic outcome while those who cleared C3 by 42.33% or more were classified as good neurologic outcome. The model containing both GCS and C3 clearance maximized both sensitivity (90.9%) and specificity (100.0%). Thus, change in CSF C3 concentration over time, especially when adjusted by GCS, helps classify functional outcome at the time of discharge and offers added prognostic value in the management of aSAH as a highly specific and objective biomarker.

Discussion

The complement system is an evolutionarily conserved, noncellular component of the innate immune system involved in the recognition and clearance of both pathogens and cellular debris. In this study, we examined temporal trends in complement protein concentration at 2 critical time points after aSAH, the first associated with EBI and the second with DCI. These time points are thought to be when critical pathophysiologic responses to aSAH are occurring that drive morbidity and mortality. Compared with healthy controls, CSF from patients with aSAH shows high concentrations of C1q and C3 proteins early after aneurysm rupture. The levels of these proteins decrease over time; however, those with a good neurologic outcome had a larger decrease in CSF levels of C1q and C3. Compared with those with good functional outcome at time of discharge, patients with poor functional outcome had approximately 6 times less clearance of C3 in the CSF (Figure 2). The rate of C3 clearance was able to classify patients with good or poor outcome with 100% specificity. While the sensitivity of C3 clearance alone was modest, the highest sensitivity in our model was obtained when C3 clearance was adjusted by admission GCS. It is important that elevated complement levels did not simply reflect increased blood cells or volume in the subarachnoid space. Thus, our findings suggest that decreases in CSF C3 may serve as a novel and objective biomarker that helps distinguish between patients expected to have a good vs poor neurologic outcome at time of discharge from the hospital.

The role of the complement system in neurologic disease has received attention over the last decade. C1q, the initiating factor of the classical complement cascade, is produced by multiple cells within the CNS and serves as a “tag” for microglial-mediated synaptic pruning.9,27 C3 is produced mainly by reactive astrocytes but can also be expressed by microglia.28,29 Microglia are the only cells within the CNS known to express the complement C3 receptor CR3, although this can also be expressed on macrophages infiltrating the CNS from the periphery.27,30 In the context of aSAH and other forms of brain injury, C1q and C3 may also serve to tag cellular debris for subsequent phagocytosis by microglia or infiltrating immune cells. Activation of the C3/C3R pathway has been shown in preclinical models to influence microglial polarization, white matter injury, and neurologic outcome.31 Therefore, impaired C3 clearance observed in the CSF of patients with poor outcome after SAH may indicate failure of regulatory mechanisms to remove cellular debris and ongoing neuroinflammation. Excess CSF C3, which can interact with parenchymal glia and neurons via paravascular glymphatic routes between the subarachnoid space and brain tissue, may, therefore, amplify inflammation, synaptic changes, and neuronal loss.32,33 This hypothesis will require extensive future investigation in both patients with aSAH as well as preclinical models.

Previous studies of complement changes in aSAH have focused predominantly on downstream complement proteins, components of the alternative or lectin pathway of complement activation, or changes in serum levels rather than CSF.7 In aSAH, proteins within the lectin pathway, including ficolin-1 and mannose-binding lectin, are increased in patient CSF and associated with poor functional outcome.15,16,34 Also, studies focusing on more downstream complement mediators such as C3a, C4a, and C5 have shown elevations in both plasma and CSF concentrations and an association with outcome.17-19 Indeed, the recent “CompLement C5 Antibodies for decreasing brain injury after aneurysmal Subarachnoid Haemorrhage (CLASH)” trial was a first-in-human phase 2a study demonstrating relative safety, but the dosing regimen used did not effectively lower CSF concentration of C5a.35 This supported the need for a larger phase 2b study. In this study, we focused on upstream complement proteins involved in the classical complement cascade including C1q and C3. To date, the only study of C1q changes after aSAH in humans was done in serum, but did show an association between elevated C1q levels and higher incidence of DCI and poor prognosis.36 Several studies have looked at C3 signaling via C3R in preclinical models; however, there have been no studies looking at CSF levels in patients, including changes over time.31,37 Thus, our findings add to the existing literature showing that C1q and C3 concentrations are elevated in the CSF after aSAH and that clearance of complement proteins from CSF over time is associated with good neurologic outcome.

The improved outcome because of increased clearance of CSF C3 after aSAH observed in our study may suggest reduced parenchymal inflammation and lower risk of downstream neurologic deficits. Indeed, a recent study by Chen, et al. (2020) demonstrated a prominent role of recently rediscovered meningeal lymphatic vessels in the drainage of extravasated red blood cell products in a mouse model of SAH.38,39 Intact as well as degraded red blood cells were observed in both the meningeal lymphatics and the deep cervical lymph nodes after SAH.38 After ablation of meningeal lymphatics, decreased drainage of CSF worsened the severity of SAH, as indicated by increased proinflammatory microglia and impaired behavioral function in the open field and Y-maze tests.38 Our work suggests that a potential mediator of this impaired behavioral function may be related to excess complement proteins such as C3. Given their known role in glial-mediated synaptic alterations, increased early complement proteins after aSAH combined with impaired clearance may promote excess synaptic phagocytosis.

There are several limitations to our work. First, our study was done at a single institution on a limited number of patients. This may explain why some variables typically associated with outcome, such as Hunt-Hess score and incidence of DCI, were similar in patients with good and poor outcome.1,2 Although post-hoc analysis of C3 clearance between patients with good vs poor outcome showed sufficient power, the results provided here should be considered exploratory rather than confirmatory. Future work should investigate temporal changes in complement proteins in a larger, multi-institutional cohort to ensure generalizability. Second, not every patient with aSAH has an EVD placed, and therefore, our results may be biased toward those who generally have higher grade aSAH on initial presentation. Third, prior studies examining other complement proteins in CSF have shown some variability when CSF is collected from an EVD compared with lumbar puncture.35 Indeed, recent trials supporting the use of lumbar drains in patients with aSAH may expand the number of patients from whom CSF can be routinely collected and offer an opportunity for direct comparison between CSF obtained from an EVD and the lumbar spine, but further studies are needed to confirm whether our findings extend to samples obtained from lumbar drains.40 Fourth, the primary outcome of the study was GOS, which although being one of the most commonly used outcome measures in aSAH clinical studies, is not specific to aSAH and likely unable to detect subtle yet meaningful functional impairment in patients.20 Indeed, recent guidelines have called for development of more specific outcome measures, as few have been feasible for clinical implementation.41,42 Finally, our study only assessed neurologic outcome at the time of discharge from the hospital. While patients with aSAH are often admitted to the hospital for several weeks, we are unable to determine the effects of complement protein clearance on longer term outcomes. This should be assessed in future studies, and therefore, the results of this study should be considered exploratory.

In summary, patients with aSAH have robust inflammatory responses shortly after aneurysm rupture, including elevation of early complement proteins C1q and C3. Increased clearance of these inflammatory mediators from the CSF is associated with improved neurologic outcome. Future strategies to improve clearance of inflammatory mediators from CSF may, therefore, help improve outcome after aSAH.
