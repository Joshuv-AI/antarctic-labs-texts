Prevalence of High-Risk Human Papillomavirus Infection and Associated Risk Factors Among Women Attending the Gynecology Unit in Bahir Dar, Northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Alemayehu Abate, Melkamnesh Azage, Abaineh Munshea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPersistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV), particularly types 16 and 18, is the primary cause of cervical cancer The objective of this study was to determine the prevalence of HPV types 16 and 18 and identify associated risk factors among women attending the gynecology unit in Bahir Dar, northwest Ethiopia.MethodsAn institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted among women attending the gynecology unit in Bahir Dar, northwest Ethiopia. A total of 344 women were selected and screened for HPV DNA. Data were collected between January and December 2025, analyzed using SPSS version 26. Cervical samples were collected and tested for HPV genotypes using the Abbott RealTime PCR System. Bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed. Adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were used to determine the strength of association, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsThe prevalence of HPV infection was 38.4% (132/344). The prevalence of HPV-16 and HPV-18 was 18.3% and 6.7%, respectively, with a combined prevalence of 25.0%. Women aged 40–50 years (AOR ≈ 3.8) and >50 years (AOR ≈ 4.9) had higher odds of HPV infection compared with those aged 30–40 years. Divorced or widowed women were more likely to be infected than married women. Having two or more sexual partners, long-term use of hormonal contraceptives (>5 years), a history of sexually transmitted infections, and HIV-positive status (AOR = 2.37; 95% CI: 1.13–4.96) were significantly associated with HPV infection.ConclusionThe prevalence of HPV infection among women was high, with a significant portion attributed to HPV types 16 and 18. Significant predictors included older age, marital status, multiple sexual partners, long-term use of hormonal contraceptives, and HIV infection. Strengthening HPV vaccination, improving cervical cancer screening programs, and integrating HPV prevention with HIV and STI services are crucial to reducing the burden of cervical cancer in Ethiopia.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is a malignant tumor that develops from the cells of the uterine cervix. 1 The human papillomavirus (HPV) is responsible for over 99% of cervical cancer cases, and it is mainly preventable. 2 According to their carcinogenicity, HPV subtypes can be categorized as low-risk (such as 6, 11, 66, 73, and 82) and high-risk (such as 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, and 59). 3 Persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) genotypes 16 and 18 is responsible for about 70% of cervical malignancies. 4 There is considerable diversity in the distribution of the other HR-HPV genotypes (31, 33, 35, 45, 52, and 58) in addition to the infection with types 16 and 18, which accounts for up to 30% of the global cause of invasive cervical cancer. 5

In sub-Saharan African nations, the combined prevalence of HR-HPV is 32.3%. The genotype distribution of HR-HPV varied based on geographical location. 6 There is a distinct trend in the distribution among African nations. The most often found genotypes in sub-Saharan African nations were HPV-16, -18, -45, -35, and -33, whereas the most extensively distributed genotypes in the eastern part of Africa are HPV-16, -52, -18, -39, and -31. 7 The distribution of HR-HPV, namely HPV-16, -52, -18, -58, and -45, is nearly same in Ethiopia to that of other East African nations. 8

The World Health Organization’s (WHO) 90–70–90 aim for eradicating cervical cancer and expanding access to HPV vaccinations is a crucial component of this goal. 9 Currently, three preventive HPV vaccinations, targeted against high-risk HPV types are offered in numerous countries across the world to prevent HPV-related diseases. Three types of HPV vaccines are available: bivalent, quadrivalent, and nona-valent. 10 Several clinical, reproductive, and behavioral variables have been found to be significant predictors of HPV infection including having multiple sexual partners, initiating sexual encounters early, having a history of STIs, HIV infection, and increasing age. 11 These factors contribute to the persistence and transmission of HPV infection and subsequently increase the risk of cervical precancerous lesions and cervical cancer. 12 Despite the introduction of HPV vaccination in Ethiopia in 2018 and ongoing cervical cancer screening initiatives, there is little data on the prevalence and risk factors of high-risk HPV infection in Bahir Dar, Northwest Ethiopia. In particular, data from women attending gynecology units are scarce, although this population represents an important group for early detection and prevention of HPV infection and cervical cancer. Generating local epidemiological data is therefore crucial to guide public health interventions and strengthen cervical cancer prevention programs. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV-16/18) infection and identify associated risk factors among women attending the gynecology unit in Bahir Dar, Northwest Ethiopia.

Methods

Study Setting and Period

A cross-sectional study was conducted among women age above 30 years attending the gynecology unit of Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (FHCSH) in Bahir Dar, northwest Ethiopia. The study took place between January and December 2025.

Study Population

All women who visited gynecology unit in FHCSH during the study period constituted the source population.

Inclusion Criteria

All women who visited the gynecology unit of Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (FHCSH) during the study period constituted the source population. Women who attended the gynecology unit and provided informed consent to participate in the study were included as study participants.

Exclusion Criteria

Women who were critically ill and unable to participate in the interview process were excluded from the study. In addition, women who were unable to undergo cervical sampling due to medical or physical limitations were also excluded.

Sample Size Determination

The sample size was determined using the single population proportion formula. Considering into account the following assumptions: a 95% confidence level (Z1-α/2 = 1.96), a margin of error of 5% (d = 0.05), and a prevalence of 28.7%. 11

The required sample size, after calculating, was approximately 315 women. By adding a 10% non-response rate, the final sample size became: n-final = 315+32=347. Thus, the final sample size for this study was 347.

Sampling Procedure

A systematic random sampling technique was used to select study participants. According to gynecology unit of FHCSH registration data, over the previous three months approximately 840 women visited, resulting in an average of 280 visits each month. A systematic random sampling procedure with a skip interval of every 3rd was used to choose the 347 women (sampling interval k = N/n = 840/347 ≈ 3). Data collection continued on until the desired sample size of 347 was reached.

Data and Sample Collection Methods

Data were collected using pre-prepared structured questionnaire. The questionnaire was adapted from previous studies conducted on a similar topic5,7,10,11 and customized accordingly. Sociodemographic, reproductive and sexual behavior data and whole blood sample were collected by clinical nurses whereas cervical swabs were collected by senior gynecologist. Sociodemographic characteristics, clinical history, and behavioral factors were all covered in the questionnaire. The questionnaire was initially prepared in English and then translated into the local language, Amharic. It was then translated back to English to verify the consistency and accuracy of the data. The study was pretested on a sample of 5% (n = 15) of women attending gynecology unit of FHCSH who were not involved in the study in order to ensure uniformity.

Laboratory Investigations

Study participants were screened for HPV DNA and CD4 count was performed to investigate the immunity level. Abbott RealTime PCR System was used to detect human papillomavirus DNA in cervical swabs, based on a real-time multiplex PCR assay that allows simultaneous amplification, detection, and differentiation of target nucleic acids. HPV targets are amplified using a primer mix that includes two reverse primers and three forward primers that target a conserved L1 region. Fluorescent tagged probes are used to create signals for 14 HR HPV genotypes (HPV 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, and 68). A primer set that targets an endogenous human beta globin sequence is used to create Internal Control (IC) amplicons, which are then identified using an IC-specific probe. The endogenous human beta globin sequence is detected by the Abbott RealTime HR HPV assay as a sample validity verify for cell adequacy, sample extraction, and amplification efficiency. 13 CD4 count was analyzed using FACSPrestoTM near patient CD4 counter.BD FACSPresto technology is based on fluorescent photomicroscopy and light absorbance detection, on a multicolor platform. A single drop of capillary or venous blood was used to measure the percent and absolute CD4 T-lymphocytes using the FACSPresto, a portable CD4 counter with single disposable cartridges.

Data Quality Assurance

Data collectors and laboratory personnel received comprehensive training on the study protocol, research ethics, participant recruitment, sample collection procedures, safe specimen handling, HPV testing, participant counseling, and confidentiality requirements. Before the actual data collection, the questionnaire was pretested, and the principal investigator provided close supervision throughout the study. Daily monitoring was conducted to ensure adherence to the study protocol.

To maintain data quality, two experienced supervisors reviewed all completed questionnaires and laboratory records each day, assessing their completeness, internal consistency, accuracy, validity, and overall integrity. In addition, both internal and external laboratory quality control procedures were implemented. All laboratory reagents, consumables, and supplies were routinely checked for expiration dates and proper storage conditions before use to ensure the reliability and accuracy of laboratory results.

Data Processing and Analysis

Data were coded, entered into EpiData version 3.1 (EpiData Association, Odense, Denmark), and checked for completeness and consistency. After data cleaning, the dataset was exported to Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for statistical analysis.

Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, and cross-tabulations, were used to summarize participants’ sociodemographic, behavioral, and clinical characteristics. Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with any HPV infection. Variables with a p-value <0.25 in the bivariable analysis were considered candidates for the multivariable logistic regression model. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to identify independent predictors of HPV infection, and statistical significance was declared at a p-value <0.05. Variables with a p-value of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The reporting of this study conforms to the STROBE guidelines. 14

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Bahir Dar University (Protocol No. 12IRB/2024). Permission to conduct the study was also obtained from Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital prior to data collection.

All participants were informed about the purpose, procedures, potential benefits, and risks of the study, as well as their right to decline participation or withdraw at any stage without any consequences to their medical care. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before enrollment. Participant confidentiality and anonymity were maintained throughout the study by using unique identification codes instead of personal identifiers, and all study data were stored securely with access restricted to the research team.

Women who tested positive for human papillomavirus (HPV) were counseled and referred for appropriate clinical evaluation and management. Initial triage was performed using visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA) and/or cervical cytology. Women with abnormal triage results, including positive visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA) findings or abnormal cervical cytology, were referred for colposcopic evaluation. Where clinically indicated, cervical biopsy was performed to confirm the diagnosis and determine the grade of cervical intraepithelial lesions or the presence of invasive cervical cancer. Appropriate follow-up and treatment were provided in accordance with the Ethiopian national cervical cancer screening and management guidelines.

Socio-Demographic, Reproductive, and Clinical Characteristics of Study Participants

Of 347 planned participants, 344 were enrolled and analyzed, yielding a response rate of 99.1%.

Of study participants, 182 (53%) were aged 40–50 years, while 93 (27%) were above 50 years, and 69 (20%) were 30–40 years. Of the participants, 140 (41%) were married, 71 (21%) were divorced, 69 (20%) were widowed, and 64 (19%) were single. Of the participants, 104 (30%) had one lifetime sexual partner, while 240 (70%) reported having two or more lifetime partners. Of the participants, 143 (42%) had one current sexual partner and 201 (58%) had two or more. Factors Associated With High-Risk HPV Infection

Women between the ages of 40 and 50 had a 2.41-fold higher risk of HPV infection than women between the ages of 30 and 40 (AOR = 2.41, 95% CI: 1.12–5.20). Similarly, women aged above 50 years had 3.12 times higher odds of HPV infection compared with those aged 30–40 years (AOR = 3.12, 95% CI: 1.36–7.15). Divorced women had 3.46 times higher risk of HPV infection compared with married women (AOR = 3.46, 95% CI: 1.72–6.95, p < 0.001). Likewise, widowed women were 2.88 times more likely to have HPV infection than married women (AOR = 2.88, 95% CI: 1.42–5.86, p = 0.003). Women who reported having two or more current sexual partners had 2.14 times higher odds of HPV infection than women who had only one partner (AOR = 2.14, 95% CI: 1.29–3.56, p = 0.003). HIV-positive women are 2.37 times more likely than HIV-negative women to be infected by HPV. Discussion

Invasive cervical cancer and cervical intraepithelial lesions are linked to a chronic cervical infection with any of the high-risk HPV genotypes, including HPV 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, and 68. 15 Preventive measures against HPV-associated cancer can benefit greatly from epidemiological research on the distribution of HR-HPV and the assessment of HR-HPV prevalence in various geographic areas, particularly in an era where vaccines are widely available. 16 The objective of this study was to determine the proportion of HPV type 16 and type 18 and its role in the screening and vaccination in Bahir Dar, northwest Ethiopia.

In this study, the prevalence of HPV was 38.4% (132/344). This result was consistent with a systematic review and meta-analysis among sub-Saharan African women that indicated the pooled prevalence of HR HPV in sub-Saharan African nations was 34%, as determined by the analysis of 23 studies 17 and with a study done in Ghana that reported the general prevalence of HPV infection was 37.2%. 18 This finding was higher than a study conducted in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia that indicated the prevalence of HPV was 23.3% 19 and a study conducted in South Central Ethiopia that indicated the prevalence of HPV was 23.2%. 20

In the current study, the prevalence of HPV-16 and HPV-18 was 18.3% and 6.7%. The combined prevalence of both HPV type 16 and 18 was 25.0%. This finding was consistent with a study conducted in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia that reported the combined prevalence of type 16 and 18 was 24%, 21 and with another study done in Adama, Ethiopia that indicated the prevalence of HPV-16 and HPV-18 was 25%. 22

In this study, women aged 40–50 years and above 50 years had higher odds of HPV infection compared with those aged 30–40 years. This finding was similar with studies done in Ethiopia that reported the presence of oncogenic HPV infection was significantly correlated with women in the 55–65 age range (AOR = 7.91, 95% CI: 1.95-32.09). 23 The prevalence of HPV was independent of women in the 56–65 age range [adjusted odds ratio (AOR), 7.91; 95% confidence interval (CI), 1.95–32.09]. 24 This result was in line with a Japanese study that found that the rate of HPV infection rose with age and peaked in people over 60 (30.97%). 25 Longer cumulative exposure to HPV, reduced immune responses with aging, and the persistence or reactivation of latent HPV infections may all contribute to the higher risk in older age groups.

In our study, marital status was another significant factor in this study. Divorced and widowed women were more likely to be infected with HPV compared with married women. This finding was similar with a study done in Ghana that reported being single or divorced was highly associated with infection compared to being married, 26 and with a study done in South Africa that reported single women may have a higher risk of HPV infection than married women due to risky sexual behavior, such as not having a consistent sexual partner. 27 This may be related to changes in sexual partnerships, increased likelihood of new sexual relationships a decrease in social and medical support networks, all of which could raise the risk of contracting HPV.

In our study, the number of sexual partners was also significantly associated with HPV infection. Women who reported two or more current sexual partners had higher odds of HPV infection compared with those with one partner. This finding similar with other studies that reported an independent risk factor for HR-HPV infection was having more than two sexual partners.28,29 HPV is primarily transmitted through sexual contact, and having multiple sexual partners increases the probability of exposure to the virus.

In this study, long-term use of hormonal contraceptives for more than five years was also associated with HPV infection in this study. Women who used hormonal contraceptives for more than five years had higher odds of HPV infection compared with non-users. This finding was supported by Meta-Analysis study that reported Cervical cancer risk was increased by using hormonal contraception (aOR = 2.34; 95% CI = 1.83 to 4.66; p<0.001), 30 and by the result of a cohort study that reported long-term usage of oral contraceptives was significantly linked to a 16.8-fold higher risk of high-grade CIN (aOR 16.79, 95% CI 3.82 to 73.70, p <.001). 31 The possible explanation is that long-term usage of hormonal contraceptives may affect immunological responses and cervical epithelial alterations, which could promote HPV persistence and progression. This finding was also similar with studies that reported among current users of oral contraceptives the risk of invasive cervical cancer increased with increasing duration of use (relative risk for 5 or more years’ use, and compared with never users of oral contraceptives, the relative risks of cervical cancer increased with increasing duration of use: for durations of approximately less than 5 years, 5–9 years, and 10 or more years, respectively. .

In our study, a history of sexually transmitted infection (STI) was strongly associated with HPV infection. Women with a history of STI were more likely to have HPV infection compared with those without such a history. This result was in agreement with a study conducted in Ethiopia that revealed higher odds of HPV positivity (COR = 1.56; 95% CI: 0.59–4.11, p = 0.36) among participants with a history of STIs. 32 According to the study conducted in Brazil, individuals with other STIs had a higher prevalence of HPV infection, suggesting that a history of STIs may increase an individual’s susceptibility to HPV infection or the virus’s persistence. 33 This finding may be crucial for the creation of behavioral and medical interventions, such as STI screening and treatment programs. A number of variables, including a lack of knowledge and screening, lead to the spread of human papilloma virus infection. For women from developing countries like Ethiopia, HPV infection and cervical precancer continue to be the leading causes of cancer-related deaths.

In this study, HIV-positive women had 2.37 times higher odds of testing positive for HPV compared to their HIV-negative counterparts (AOR = 2.37, 95% CI: 1.13–4.96, p = 0.022).

This result was in agreement with a study conducted in Ethiopia that reported compared to women without HIV, women with HIV had a 3.53-fold increased risk of HPV infection. 34 Studies conducted in Shashemene, southern Ethiopia, 35 Nigeria, 36 Amazonas, 37 and Tanzania 29 also corroborated this result. This result emphasizes how women with HIV are more susceptible to HPV infection and its aftereffects.38,39

Limitations of the Study

This study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design of the study limits the ability to establish a causal relationship between the identified risk factors and HPV infection. Second, the study was conducted in a single health facility among women attending the gynecology unit in Bahir Dar, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the wider population of women in Ethiopia. Third, some behavioral and sexual history variables, such as the number of sexual partners and history of sexually transmitted infections, were based on self-reported information. This may introduce recall bias or social desirability bias, as participants might underreport sensitive behaviors.

Conclusion

The findings revealed that the prevalence of HPV infection among women attending the gynecology unit in Bahir Dar, northwest Ethiopia was high (38.4%). The prevalence of HPV type 16 and type 18, the two most oncogenic genotypes, was 18.3% and 6.7%, respectively, with a combined prevalence of 25.0%. Older age, marital status, multiple sexual partners, long-term use of hormonal contraceptives, history of sexually transmitted infections, and HIV-positive status. Women aged 40 years and above, divorced or widowed women were factors significantly associated with HPV infection The findings emphasized the need to strengthen HPV prevention and control strategies in Ethiopia, including expanding HPV vaccination programs, improving access to regular cervical cancer screening, and integrating HPV prevention with reproductive health, HIV, and STI services. The prevalence of HPV infection and its progression to cervical cancer among women could be significantly decreased by public health interventions that emphasize behavioral risk reduction, early identification, and vaccination.
