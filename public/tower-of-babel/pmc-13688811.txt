Pancreatic head solid-pseudopapillary neoplasm in a man treated by robot-assisted laparoscopic partial pancreatectomy: a case report
Authors: Rui Ma, Weikang Liu, Yinmo Yang, Xiaodong Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSolid-pseudopapillary neoplasm (SPN) of the pancreas is a rare tumor with low malignant potential, most commonly occurring in the pancreatic body or tail of young women. Atypical presentations, particularly in male patients and cases involving the pancreatic head, may pose diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. This report highlights surgical management considerations in a patient with inconclusive preoperative findings.Case presentationWe report the case of a 37-year-old man with a large lesion initially interpreted as a retroperitoneal mass closely associated with the pancreatic head. Preoperative imaging could not definitively establish the tumor origin. Following multidisciplinary team (MDT) evaluation, a diagnostic resection strategy was adopted. Robot-assisted laparoscopic exploration revealed that the tumor originated from the pancreatic head with an exophytic growth pattern. Local tumor excision combined with limited pancreatic resection was successfully performed while preserving the main pancreatic duct. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of SPN. The patient recovered uneventfully and remained clinically stable at follow-up.ConclusionsThis case illustrates the clinical decision-making process and surgical strategy selection in an atypical presentation of SPN with preoperative diagnostic uncertainty. The report underscores the potential role of robot-assisted surgery in facilitating precise dissection and parenchyma preservation in complex anatomical settings.

Introduction

Solid-pseudopapillary neoplasm (SPN) is a rare pancreatic tumor with low malignant potential and was first described by Frantz in 1959 [1]. The pathogenesis of SPN remains unknown, and it occurs in 0.17% to 2.7% of all pancreatic tumors [2]. In 1966, the World Health Organization classified SPN as an exocrine borderline pancreatic tumor, and subsequently reclassified it as a potentially malignant tumor in 2010 [3, 4]. SPN is most commonly detected in young women between the ages of 20 and 30 years and is predominantly located in the pancreatic body and tail, accounting for approximately 60% of reported cases [4]. SPN usually presents as a large, isolated, well-defined lesion that may be completely solid, completely cystic, or mixed [5]. Degenerative changes, including internal bleeding, necrosis, calcification, and cyst formation, are frequently observed [5, 6]. SPN is typically associated with a favorable prognosis, with a mortality rate of < 3% [7, 8]. However, approximately 5–15% of patients may exhibit locally aggressive behavior or develop metastasis. Complete surgical resection remains the primary treatment strategy for SPN [8]. Although SPN, pancreatic head involvement, and robot-assisted pancreatic surgery have each been previously reported, their coexistence in male patients, particularly in the setting of large tumors and preoperative diagnostic uncertainty, remains relatively uncommon. In this report, we present a case of a large SPN arising from the pancreatic head in a male patient, highlighting the diagnostic challenges, intraoperative decision-making, and the feasibility of a parenchyma-preserving strategy using robot-assisted laparoscopic surgery.

Case presentation

In this case, a 37-year-old man presented to our hospital after abdominal ultrasonography during a routine physical examination revealed a cystic–solid mass in the pancreatic head. The patient had no obvious symptoms other than occasional diarrhea and hypoglycemia. Preoperatively, the patient reported two episodes of suspected hypoglycemia, characterized by dizziness, fatigue, and sweating, which were relieved after oral intake of sweet food; blood glucose levels were not measured during these episodes. Routine blood glucose values were generally maintained at 5–6 mmol/L. The patient also experienced three episodes of diarrhea, all occurring after fatty meals, presenting as loose and unformed stools. He had high blood pressure, no prior abdominal surgery, no history of pancreatic disease, no previous oncological history, and no history of similar tumors in his family. Following admission, the patient underwent contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) examination. A well-defined cystic–solid mass measuring 8.1 × 6.9 × 12.0 cm was visualized in the right retroperitoneal region (Fig. 1), demonstrating well-defined margins and a close anatomical association with the pancreatic head and duodenum. This anatomical relationship caused anterior displacement of the compressed pancreatic head (Fig. 2). The tumor was closely adjacent to the inferior vena cava, growing along its right anterior wall. A clear separation plane was observed between the lesion and the vascular structure, with a smooth and intact vascular adventitia and no radiological evidence of vascular invasion. The mass was also found to be adjacent to the superior mesenteric vessels, including the superior mesenteric vein and superior mesenteric artery. Preserved loose connective tissue planes were present between the tumor and these vessels, and no imaging signs of vascular sheath infiltration were identified. No lymphadenopathy or distant metastasis was noted. Enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) revealed a cystic–solid lesion in the right retroperitoneal region with a slightly hypointense signal on T1WI, a mixed signal on FS-T2WI, and a hyperintense signal on DWI, showing internal heterogeneous signal characteristics (Fig. 3A–C). Preoperative imaging assessment, including contrast-enhanced CT and MRCP, demonstrated no evidence of main pancreatic duct invasion or compression. A preserved anatomical separation plane between the lesion and the main pancreatic duct was qualitatively identified on MRCP imaging (Fig. 3D), supporting the feasibility of a duct-preserving surgical strategy. No precise millimeter measurement of tumor-to-duct distance was obtained, as surgical planning was based on the determination of absent ductal involvement. The solid component exhibited marked contrast enhancement, whereas the cystic component remained non-enhancing on post-contrast images (Figs. 3A–D and 4). Although imaging suggested a lesion located in the right retroperitoneal region, its close anatomical relationship with the pancreatic head raised the possibility of pancreatic origin. Therefore, the lesion was interpreted radiologically as a mass located in the right retroperitoneal region, with differential diagnostic considerations including pancreatic-origin tumors. Blood tumor markers were normal, and routine laboratory examinations showed no significant abnormalities. Endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) was carefully considered but ultimately not performed. Additionally, for large resectable pancreatic or peripancreatic masses with clear surgical indications, several studies suggest that preoperative biopsy may be unnecessary when it does not alter clinical management and may introduce procedure-related risks or diagnostic ambiguity [9]. Although preoperative imaging suggested certain features consistent with a benign or low-grade malignant lesion, the multidisciplinary team (MDT) determined that tissue sampling would not alter the therapeutic strategy. For solid or mixed cystic–solid lesions located in the pancreatic head, surgical resection remains the standard and preferred management due to the potential risks of malignant transformation, obstructive complications, and persistent diagnostic uncertainty [10]. Furthermore, EUS-FNA carries procedure-related risks, including bleeding, pancreatitis, pancreatic fistula, and an extremely rare possibility of tumor seeding, while the limited tissue yield may result in incomplete pathological assessment or inaccurate grading in low-grade neoplasms [11]. The patient did not undergo preoperative biopsy or other invasive diagnostic procedures; therefore, the preoperative histological diagnosis was not clear. Following MDT, laparoscopic exploration and resection of the retroperitoneal mass were planned for this patient. A robot-assisted approach was selected to enhance surgical precision and facilitate meticulous dissection around critical vascular and pancreatic structures. To achieve greater surgical precision and reduce operative trauma, a robot-assisted approach was used. The da Vinci Xi Surgical System (Intuitive Surgical, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) was employed in this procedure. Trocar placement followed a classical fan-shaped configuration. A subumbilical longitudinal mini-incision was first created to establish pneumoperitoneum and insert the camera trocar. Subsequently, multiple robotic operating trocars were placed bilaterally below the costal margins, serving as the primary working channels for robotic arms and auxiliary instruments. During the operation, we first detached the gastrocolic ligament and hepatocolic ligament and lowered the hepatic flexure of the colon to reveal the duodenum and the head of the pancreas. Intraoperatively, the tumor extended cranially to the pancreatic uncinate process and caudally to the right renal lower pole, exhibiting well-defined planes with adjacent structures (Additional file 1). Further intraoperative exploration confirmed that the tumor originated from the pancreatic head, demonstrating an exophytic growth pattern and maintaining continuity with the pancreas via a rim of normal pancreatic parenchyma. Accordingly, given the absence of main pancreatic duct involvement and the presence of a preserved dissection plane, local tumor excision combined with limited pancreatic resection was performed. Intraoperatively, the main pancreatic duct was carefully identified and confirmed to be free of tumor involvement. Given the absence of ductal invasion and the intention to preserve pancreatic function, the main pancreatic duct was meticulously preserved. Dissection was performed along the anatomical plane between the tumor and the pancreatic duct using a combination of blunt and sharp techniques. The main pancreatic duct was skeletonized and clearly exposed, while small pancreatic ductal branches leading to the tumor were securely ligated to ensure ductal integrity. The tumor was removed en bloc. The pancreatic transection surface was subsequently closed with interrupted sutures. The total operative time was approximately 3 h, and the estimated intraoperative blood loss was 100 mL. A prophylactic intra-abdominal drain was placed near the pancreatic transection surface for postoperative monitoring. The specimen was retrieved through the subumbilical incision using a protective specimen retrieval bag and sent to the pathology department for analysis.

Microscopically, tumor cells exhibited pseudopapillary, solid nested, and trabecular growth patterns (Fig. 5), with round-to-oval nuclei and rare mitotic figures. Immunohistochemical examination showed that Cytokeratin, Vimentin, CD56, CD10, Synaptophysin, β-catenin, CyclinD1, and PgR were positive, while Insulinoma-associated 1, S-100, and E-cadherin were negative. 1% of cells were positive for Ki67. These results were consistent with the diagnosis of SPN. No vascular tumor emboli or perineural invasion were observed microscopically. Histopathological examination confirmed an R0 resection (no tumor cells were detected at the resection margins). The margin distance was not quantitatively measured; however, all margins were reported as microscopically negative. The patient recovered well after surgery. The patient was followed up for 3 months after surgery, during which no evidence of recurrence or disease progression was observed. Postoperative pancreatic fistula was assessed according to the International Study Group on Pancreatic Surgery criteria, and no pancreatic fistula was observed. Postoperative pancreatic function was evaluated through clinical assessment and laboratory monitoring. Endocrine function was assessed by serial measurements of blood glucose levels and hemoglobin A1c during follow-up, which demonstrated preserved glycemic control. No pancreatic enzyme supplementation or additional glycemic management was required, indicating preservation of both endocrine and exocrine function. The patient was discharged from the hospital on postoperative day 9. At the 3-month postoperative follow-up visit, the patient reported no specific discomfort. The surgical incision had healed well, no diarrhea was observed, and blood glucose levels remained within the normal range. Discussion

The preoperative histological diagnosis of SPN may be obtained by EUS-FNA, which can assist clinical decision-making in selected cases [1]. However, its application is context dependent. In the present case, preoperative tissue sampling was not performed because imaging demonstrated a resectable lesion and biopsy results were not expected to alter the therapeutic strategy. Consistent with its recognized low-grade biological behavior, the prognosis of SPN is generally favorable, with a 5-year survival rate of 95–98% after surgical resection [12]. Currently, surgical resection of the tumor is considered to be the main treatment for SPN, and complete resection of tumors with negative histological margins is the preferred treatment [13]. Depending on tumor location and its relationship with surrounding structures, various surgical procedures have been described for SPN, including pancreaticoduodenectomy, distal pancreatectomy, central pancreatectomy, enucleation, and other parenchyma-preserving resections [1]. Given the typically low-grade malignant potential of SPN, organ-preserving strategies may be considered when complete resection with negative margins is technically feasible and oncologically appropriate. Minimally invasive pancreatic surgery has progressively evolved with advances in laparoscopic and robotic techniques [13]. Compared with conventional laparoscopy, robot-assisted surgery may provide several technical advantages, including magnified three-dimensional visualization, improved instrument dexterity, and tremor filtration, which can facilitate meticulous dissection in anatomically complex regions [14, 15]. In the present case, these technical characteristics contributed to precise dissection around the pancreatic head and preservation of the main pancreatic duct. However, given the single-case nature of this report, broader conclusions regarding the comparative superiority of robotic surgery cannot be drawn.

SPN typically presents with characteristic features described as "young female-pancreatic body/tail", with male cases accounting for only 10% of all reported cases; approximately 60% of tumors occur in the pancreatic body and tail [16]. The diagnosis and treatment of this case revealed the unique challenges and coping strategies of atypical populations. Although SPN, pancreatic head involvement, and robot-assisted pancreatic surgery have each been previously described, this case illustrates a clinically distinctive combination of features. To our knowledge, reports describing large pancreatic head SPN in male patients managed with a parenchyma-preserving robotic approach under conditions of preoperative diagnostic uncertainty remain limited. Specifically, this case involved a large pancreatic head lesion in a male patient with inconclusive preoperative imaging regarding tumor origin, creating a scenario of diagnostic uncertainty. Furthermore, intraoperative assessment enabled a parenchyma-preserving strategy rather than pancreaticoduodenectomy. Therefore, the clinical value of this report lies not in the introduction of a novel surgical technology, but in illustrating surgical decision-making, intraoperative assessment, and function-preserving strategy selection in a diagnostically ambiguous and atypical presentation of SPN. This patient was a 37-year-old male with a tumor located on the head of the pancreas, which added to the complexity of diagnosis. Preoperative CT and MRI demonstrated a well-demarcated, heterogeneously enhancing mass; however, the precise organ of origin could not be definitively determined based on imaging findings alone. In the setting of an inconclusive preoperative diagnosis, the MDT formulated the operative strategy based on the composite interpretation of imaging characteristics and clinical findings. Although imaging studies did not establish a definitive diagnosis, the lesion demonstrated features commonly described in SPN, including a well-circumscribed morphology, apparent capsule integrity, and absence of main pancreatic duct dilatation [17]. Normal serum tumor markers were interpreted cautiously, as they do not exclude malignancy in pancreatic neoplasms and were not considered diagnostic but were integrated into the overall clinical assessment [18]. Given the diagnostic uncertainty, laparoscopic exploration was primarily intended to enable real-time intraoperative assessment of tumor morphology and its anatomical relationships with critical structures. The absence of invasive features was not assumed preoperatively but was instead evaluated intraoperatively through careful and gradual instrumental dissection. Identification of a preserved and safe dissection plane between the tumor, the main pancreatic duct, and adjacent vascular structures provided the technical and oncological basis for adopting a parenchyma-preserving approach. Specifically, the decision to perform limited pancreatic resection instead of pancreatoduodenectomy was based on the following intraoperative criteria: (1) absence of main pancreatic duct involvement; (2) presence of a clear and safe dissection plane between the tumor and adjacent major vessels; and (3) lack of macroscopic vascular invasion. These findings supported the feasibility of a parenchyma-preserving approach while maintaining oncological safety. Under these favorable anatomical conditions, limited pancreatic resection was considered a safe and function-preserving alternative to pancreaticoduodenectomy, allowing complete tumor removal while minimizing surgical trauma. Notably, the patient had no evidence of hormonal hypersecretion and demonstrated normal serum tumor markers. However, it is important to emphasize that neither inconclusive imaging findings nor normal tumor markers are sufficient to exclude malignancy in pancreatic masses. These features did not establish a definitive diagnosis but instead informed the differential diagnostic considerations, particularly by reducing the likelihood of functioning pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors while maintaining SPN as a potential diagnosis.

Following MDT, a diagnostic resection strategy was adopted, and a parenchyma-preserving approach consisting of local tumor excision with limited pancreatic resection was selected instead of pancreaticoduodenectomy. SPN was subsequently confirmed by pathology. This decision reflects a treatment rationale consistent with the recognized low-grade biological behavior of SPN, where parenchyma-preserving strategies may be considered when technically feasible and oncologically appropriate [19, 20]. Although histologically negative margins (R0) were achieved, the lack of quantitative assessment of the surgical margins constitutes a limitation of this study, and caution should be exercised when extrapolating oncological conclusions from this single-case report. Even when a preoperative diagnosis is inconclusive, it may be feasible to attempt functional preservation resection for low-grade tumors [7]. Consistent with the postoperative findings, the patient remained clinically stable at 3-month follow-up without evidence of procedure-related complications. However, the short follow-up period represents a limitation, and longer-term oncological outcomes cannot be determined from this single case. Large tumors located in the pancreatic head are often closely associated with major vascular structures, which may increase the technical complexity of surgical resection. In the present case, the robot-assisted laparoscopic approach facilitated precise dissection around the pancreatic head and adjacent vessels, contributing to preservation of the main pancreatic duct and surrounding pancreatic parenchyma [15]. However, given the single-case nature of this report, broader conclusions regarding the comparative advantages of robotic surgery cannot be drawn, and further validation in larger studies is warranted.

Several practical considerations arise from this case. Accurate preoperative evaluation of the relationship between the tumor and the main pancreatic duct remains essential for surgical planning, particularly when a parenchyma-preserving strategy is contemplated. In the present case, preoperative imaging and intraoperative findings consistently demonstrated absent ductal involvement, supporting limited pancreatic resection. In situations where tumors are closely associated with the main pancreatic duct, more technically demanding organ-preserving procedures may be considered in carefully selected patients. Adjunctive measures, such as intraoperative pancreatic duct stenting, may assist duct identification and protection during resection rather than serving as an alternative to definitive surgery. Finally, while robotic assistance may facilitate precise dissection in complex anatomical regions, broader conclusions regarding its comparative benefits require validation through larger clinical studies.

Conclusions

We report a rare case of a pancreatic SPN occurring in a male patient with a large lesion located in the pancreatic head. Preoperative imaging did not establish a definitive diagnosis, and surgical management was guided by intraoperative assessment. Complete tumor removal was achieved through a robot-assisted laparoscopic approach with parenchyma preservation. This case highlights the potential role of intraoperative evaluation in guiding surgical strategy in diagnostically challenging pancreatic tumors and illustrates the technical feasibility of robotic assistance in selected complex scenarios. However, broader conclusions regarding diagnostic algorithms or comparative therapeutic superiority cannot be drawn from a single case and require validation in larger studies.
