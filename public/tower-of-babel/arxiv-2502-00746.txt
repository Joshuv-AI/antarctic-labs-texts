The Hartman-Stampacchia Theorem and the Maximum Displacements of Nonvanishing Continuous Vector-Valued Functions
Nguyen Nang Thieu, Nguyen Dong Yen

Abstract
This paper aims at giving solutions to six interesting interconnected open questions suggested by Professor Biagio Ricceri. The questions focus on the behavior of nonvanishing continuous vector-valued functions in finite-dimensional normed spaces as well as in infinite-dimensional normed spaces. Using the celebrated Hartman-Stampacchia Theorem (1966) on the solution existence of variational inequalities, we establish sharp lower estimates for the maximum displacements of nonvanishing continuous vector-valued functions. Then, combining the obtained results with suitable tools from functional analysis and several novel geometrical constructions, we get the above-mentioned solutions.

Introduction

Through a series of seminar discussions during our visit to Catania University in October 2024, Professor Biagio Ricceri
[
8
]
formulated
six interesting interconnected open questions on the behavior of nonvanishing continuous vector-valued functions in finite-dimensional normed spaces as well as in infinite-dimensional normed spaces. Roughly speaking, these questions revolve around the maximum displacements and generalized maximum displacements of continuous vector-valued functions. The aim of the present paper is to obtain complete or partial solutions to the questions. It turns out that the Hartman-Stampacchia Theorem on the solution existence of variational inequalities (see
(
4

and
(
5

The obtained results enrich our knowledge on the behavior of nonvanishing continuous vector-valued functions in finite-dimensional normed spaces as well as in infinite-dimensional normed spaces.

Before formulating the notions of maximum displacements and generalized maximum displacements of continuous vector-valued functions, we need to explain some standard notations.

Throughout this paper, let
E
be a real normed space equipped with the norm
\|\cdot\|
and
K
be a nonempty convex subset of
E
. The dual space of
E
is denoted by
E^{*}
. The norm in
E^{*}
is denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{E^{*}}
. The boundary of a set
D\subset E
is abbreviated to
\partial D
, while the interior of
D
is denoted by
\mbox{\rm int}\,D
. The open ball (resp., closed ball) in
E
with center
x
and radius
r
is denoted by
B_{E}(x,r)
(resp.,
\bar{B}_{E}(x,r)
). The linear subspace generated by some vectors
x^{1},\dots,x^{m}
in
E
is abbreviated to
{\rm span}\{x^{1},\dots,x^{m}\}
. From now on, let
{\cal H}
be a real Hilbert space equipped with the scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and the norm
\|\cdot\|
.

Definition 1

(
Maximum displacement
) The maximum displacement of a continuous function
f:K\to E
is the quantity
\sup\limits_{x\in K}\|f(x)-x\|
.

If
x\in K
and
f(x)=x
, that is
\|f(x)-x\|=0
, then
x
is a fixed point of
f
. The function
f
needs not to have fixed points. The norm
\|f(x)-x\|
shows
how far
f
‘displaces’ a point
x\in K
away from its original position
. For this reason, the quantity
\sup\limits_{x\in K}\|f(x)-x\|
is called the maximum displacement of
f
. A counterpart of the latter is the number
\inf\limits_{x\in K}\|f(x)-x\|
, which signifies the deviation of
f
from having a fixed point, has been studied by Goebel in
[
2
]
under the name the
minimal displacement
.

Definition 2

(
Generalized maximum displacement
) The generalized maximum displacement of a pair of continuous functions
(\varphi,\psi)
, where
\varphi:K\to E^{*}
and
\psi:K\to E^{*}
with
K
being a nonempty convex subset of
E
, is the quantity
\sup\limits_{x\in K}\|\varphi(x)-\psi(x)\|_{E^{*}}
.

If
E
is a Hilbert space, then by the Riesz-Fréchet Representation Theorem
(
1
, Theorem 5.5)
we can identify the dual space
E^{*}
with
E
. In that case, setting
\varphi(x)=x
for all
x\in K
and using the notations of Definition
2
, we see at once that the generalized maximum displacement of the pair
(\varphi,\psi)
coincides with the maximum displacement of a continuous function
\varphi
introduced by Definition
1
. Thus, in the Hilbert space setting, Definition
2
is a natural extension of Definition
1
.

The above-mentioned six questions from
[
8
]
can be divided into three groups:

(a)

Questions 1, 3, and 4 are about maximum displacements.

(b)

Question 2 is about the approximate eigenvalues of continuous functions, in a sense.

(c)

Questions 5 and 6 are about generalized maximum displacements.

Question 1 asks to find a continuous function
f:\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
f(x)\neq 0
for all
x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)
and the maximum displacement of
f
, denoted by
d_{f}
, is strictly smaller than a real number
\gamma_{f}
(see formula (
3
) below).

In Section
2

2.2
that
d_{f}\geq\gamma_{f}
. This result not only solves the question in the negative, but also establishes a lower bound for the maximum displacement under consideration. Through concrete examples, we will show that the inequality
d_{f}\geq\gamma_{f}
may not hold if the nonvanishing property of
f
is violated. However, the inequality can still hold and, in some cases, it even holds as an equality, for many continuous functions
f:\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
f(u)=0
for some
u\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)
.

Section
3
extends Theorem
2.2
to the case where instead of the closed ball one considers an arbitrary nonempty compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
containing
0
as an interior point. In the same section, the problem of finding sharp estimates for the maximum displacements and the effect of changing the norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are studied in detail. The obtained theorems and corollaries are crucial for our subsequent investigations.

Thanks to the results of Sections
2
and
3
, in Section
4
we are able to derive complete solutions for the above Questions 2–5 and a partial solution for Question 6.

Some concluding remarks are given in Section
5
.

Despite the fact that the maximum displacement has been used in many papers in mechanics and physics, both notions described by Definitions

and

2
The First Question and Its Solution

The first question raised by Professor Biagio Ricceri
[
8
]
is stated as follows.

Question 1:

Whether there exists a continuous function
f:\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
f(x)\neq 0
for all
x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)
and the strict inequality

\sup_{x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)}\|f(x)-x\|<1+\inf_{x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)}\|f(x)\|

(1)

holds true?

Specializing Definition
1
to the case where
f
is a continuous function from
\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we see that the maximum displacement of
f
is the number

d_{f}:=\sup\limits_{x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)}\|f(x)-x\|.

(2)

This notation allows us to restate Question 1 as follows:
Is there any nonvanishing continuous function
f:\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that the number

\gamma_{f}:=1+\inf\limits_{x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)}\|f(x)\|

(3)

is strictly larger than the maximum displacement
d_{f}
?

To solve the above question, we will use a valuable observation of Professor Ricceri and the next fundamental theorem on the solution existence of finite-dimensional variational inequalities.

Theorem 2.1

(
The Hartman-Stampacchia Theorem
; See
(
4
, Lemma 3.1)
and
(
5
, Theorem 3.1)
)

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a nonempty compact convex set and let
f:K\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a continuous vector-valued function. Then, there exists a point
\bar{x}\in K
such that

\langle f(\bar{x}),y-\bar{x}\rangle\geq 0\quad\mbox{\rm for all}\ y\in K.

(4)

The problem of finding a point
\bar{x}\in K
satisfying condition (
4
) is called the
variational inequality
defined by the constraint set
K
and the operator
f
, which defines a vector field on
K
by assigning to every point
x\in K
the vector
f(x)
.

The following theorem answers Question 1 in the negative. Namely, there is no continuous vector-valued function with the desired properties.

Theorem 2.2

For any nonvanishing continuous function
f:\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one has

d_{f}\geq\gamma_{f},

(5)

where
d_{f}
and
\gamma_{f}
are defined respectively by (
2
) and (
3
).

Proof

Suppose to the contrary that there exists a nonvanishing continuous function
f:\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that (
5
) fails, i.e., the strict inequality (
1
) is valid.
Then, for any
x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)
one has

\|f(x)-x\|\leq\sup_{z\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)}\|f(z)-z\|<1+\inf_{z\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)}\|f(z)\|\leq 1+\|f(x)\|.

This yields

\|f(x)-x\|^{2}<(1+\|f(x)\|)^{2}\quad\mbox{\rm for any}\ x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)

or, equivalently,

-2\langle f(x),x\rangle+\|x\|^{2}<1+2\|f(x)\|\quad\mbox{\rm for any}\ x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1).

Therefore, we have for all
x\in\partial\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)
that

-\langle f(x),x\rangle<\|f(x)\|.

(6)

Thus, the fulfillment of the strict inequality (
6
) for every
x\in\partial\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)
is
a necessary condition
for having (
1
)
1
1

1

This important observation was shown to us by Professor Biagio Ricceri.
.

Now, applying Theorem
2.1
to the variational inequality defined by the nonempty compact convex constraint set
K:=\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)
and the operator
f
, we can find a vector
\bar{x}\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)
such that

\langle f(\bar{x}),y-\bar{x}\rangle\geq 0\quad\mbox{\rm for all}\ y\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1).

(7)

If
\bar{x}\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)={\rm int}\,K
, then from (
7
) we can easily deduce that
f(x)=0
, which contradicts the condition saying that
f
is nonvanishing on
K
. So, one must have
\bar{x}\in\partial\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)
. Combining this with (
7
), we get the representation
f(\bar{x})=-\lambda\bar{x}
for some
\lambda\geq 0
. The situation where
\lambda=0
is excluded, as
f
is nonvanishing on
K
. Hence,
f(\bar{x})=-\lambda\bar{x}
with
\lambda>0
. Therefore, we have

-\langle f(\bar{x}),\bar{x}\rangle=\lambda=\|f(\bar{x})\|,

which is a contradiction to (
6
).

We have thus shown that (
5
) holds and completed the proof.
\hfill\Box

Remark 1

The assumption on the nonvanishing property of
f
cannot be dropped in the formulation of Theorem
2.2
. To see that the inequality (
5
) may not hold if the assumption is violated, it suffices to choose
f(x)=x
for all
\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)
with
n\geq 1
and observe that
d_{f}=0
, while
\gamma_{f}=1
.

Remark 2

For many vanishing functions, the inequality (
5
) may hold and, in some cases, it even holds as an equality. As an example, let us choose
n=2
,

f_{\alpha}(x)=\big(x_{1}\cos\alpha-x_{2}\sin\alpha,\,x_{1}\sin\alpha+x_{2}\cos\alpha\big)

for all
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(0,1)
, where
\alpha\in[-\pi,\pi]
is a parameter. Then,
f_{\alpha}:\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(0,1)\to\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(0,1)
is the rotation around 0 of angle
\alpha
. Clearly,
\gamma_{f_{\alpha}}=1
for all
\alpha\in[-\pi,\pi]
.

For
\alpha=\pi/3
and
\alpha=-\pi/3
, it is easily verified that
d_{f_{\alpha}}=\gamma_{f_{\alpha}}
. So, with
f=f_{\alpha}
, (
5
) holds as an equality.

For every
\alpha\in[-\pi,\pi]\setminus[-\pi/3,\pi/3]
, one has
d_{f_{\alpha}}>\gamma_{f_{\alpha}}
. Thus, (
5
) holds as a strict inequality for
f=f_{\alpha}
.

For every
\alpha\in(-\pi/3,\pi/3)
, one has
d_{f_{\alpha}}<\gamma_{f_{\alpha}}
. This means that the inequality (
5
) fails to hold for
f=f_{\alpha}
.

The inequality (
5
) tells us that the number
\gamma_{f}
, which can be computed easily in many situations, is a lower estimate for
d_{f}
. Various extensions and the sharpness of the estimate (
5
) will be the subject of our considerations in the forthcoming section.

3
Lower Estimates for the Maximum Displacements

3.1
Lower estimates for functions defined on a class of convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}

As Theorem
2.1
can be applied to any nonempty compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it is of interest to extend Theorem
2.2
to the case where the closed ball is replaced by an arbitrary nonempty compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
containing
0
in its interior.

Theorem 3.1

Let
K
be a nonempty compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
0\in\mbox{\rm int}\,K
and let
f:K\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a nonvanishing and continuous function. Then, one has

\sup_{x\in K}\|f(x)-x\|\geq r+\inf_{x\in K}\|f(x)\|,

(8)

where

r:=\sup\,\{\rho>0\mid\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,\rho)\subset K\}.

(9)

Proof

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that (
8
) fails. Then,

\sup_{x\in K}\|f(x)-x\|<r+\inf_{x\in K}\|f(x)\|.

This implies that

\|f(x)-x\|<r+\|f(x)\|

for all
x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r)
. Squaring both sides of this inequality gives

\|f(x)\|^{2}-2\langle f(x),x\rangle+\|x\|^{2}<r^{2}+2r\|f(x)\|+\|f(x)\|^{2}

for all
x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r)
. Thus,

-\langle f(x),x\rangle<r\|f(x)\|\quad\mbox{\rm for any}\ x\in\partial\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r).

(10)

Applying Theorem
2.1
to the variational inequality associated with the function
f
and the compact convex set
\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r)
, we can find a vector
\bar{x}\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r)
such that

\langle f(\bar{x}),y-\bar{x}\rangle\geq 0\quad\mbox{\rm for all}\ y\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r).

Then, using the nonvanishing property of
f
and arguing similarly as in the proof of Theorem
2.2
, we can infer that
\bar{x}\in\partial\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r)
and
f(\bar{x})=-\lambda\bar{x}
for some
\lambda>0.
Therefore,

-\langle f(\bar{x}),\bar{x}\rangle=\lambda r^{2}=r\|f(\bar{x})\|.

This contradicts (
10
).

We have thus established the inequality (
8
), where the constant
r>0
is given by (
9
).

\hfill\Box

3.2
Finding sharp estimates

For any compact convex set
K
and continuous function
f
that satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
3.1
, the inequality

\sup_{x\in K}\|f(x)-x\|\geq\alpha+\inf_{x\in K}\|f(x)\|

(11)

holds for all
\alpha\leq r
, where
r
is defined in (
9
). This fact follows from (
8
). It is of interest to determine the largest possible value of
\alpha
for which (
11
) remains valid. The next proposition shows that for a given nonempty compact convex set
K
, the value
\alpha
is bounded above by
\sup\limits_{x\in K}\|x\|
.

Proposition 1

Let
K
be a nonempty compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
0\in\mbox{\rm int}\,K
. Suppose that there exists
\alpha>0
such that for any nonvanishing and continuous function
f:K\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the inequality (
11
) holds. Then, we have
\alpha\leq r_{1}
, where

r_{1}:=\sup\limits_{x\in K}\|x\|

(12)

Proof

Suppose to the contrary that
\alpha>r_{1}
. Fix any vector
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|x^{\prime}\|=\alpha
. Then, by (
12
) we have
x^{\prime}\notin K
. Define
f(x)=x-x^{\prime}
for all
x\in K
. Clearly,
f
is continuous on
K
and
f(x)\neq 0
for all
x\in K
. Hence, the compactness of
K
implies that
\inf\limits_{x\in K}\|f(x)\|=\min\limits_{x\in K}\|f(x)\|>0
. Therefore, we get

\sup_{x\in K}\|f(x)-x\|=\sup_{x\in K}\|x-x^{\prime}-x\|=\|x^{\prime}\|=\alpha<\alpha+\inf_{x\in K}\|f(x)\|.

This contradicts to (
11
). Thus, one has
\alpha\leq r_{1}
.

\hfill\Box

As a result of Theorem
3.1
and Proposition
1
, the largest possible value of
\alpha
must belong to the interval
[r,r_{1}]
, where
r
and
r_{1}
are defined respectively by (
9
) and (
12
).

Proposition 2

Let
K
be a nonempty compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
0\in\mbox{\rm int}\,K
. Let
\alpha\in[r,r_{1}]
be such that the inequality (
11
) holds for any nonvanishing and continuous function
f:K\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, one must have
\alpha=r
.

Proof

Suppose, to the contrary, that
\alpha>r
. Let
x^{\prime}\in\partial K
be such that
\min\limits_{x\in\partial K}\|x\|=\|x^{\prime}\|
. Then, from (
9
) it follows that
\|x^{\prime}\|=r
.

Take any
\varepsilon>0
and define
f(x)=x-(1+\varepsilon)x^{\prime}
for all
x\in K
. We see that
f
is continuous on
K
. The function
f
is nonvanishing on
K
. Indeed, if there is some
\bar{x}\in K
such that
f(\bar{x})=0
, then
\bar{x}=(1+\varepsilon)x^{\prime}
. This gives

x^{\prime}=\dfrac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\bar{x}=\dfrac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\bar{x}+\dfrac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}0.

Since
\varepsilon>0
, it follows that
x^{\prime}\in(0,\bar{x})\subset\mbox{\rm int}\,K
, which contradicts to the fact that
x^{\prime}\in\partial K
. Thus,
f(x)\neq 0
for all
x\in K
.

Since
f
is nonvanishing and continuous on
K
, the inequality (
11
) is fulfilled by our assumption on
\alpha
. We have

\sup_{x\in K}\|f(x)-x\|=\sup_{x\in K}\|x-(1+\varepsilon)x^{\prime}-x\|=(1+\varepsilon)\|x^{\prime}\|=(1+\varepsilon)r.

Combining this with (
11
) yields

(1+\varepsilon)r\geq\alpha+\inf_{x\in K}\|f(x)\|>\alpha.

Hence,
(1+\varepsilon)r>\alpha
for all
\varepsilon>0
. Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, we get
r\geq\alpha
, which leads to a contradiction.

\hfill\Box

Combining Propositions
1
and
2
with Theorem
3.1
gives us the next theorem.

Theorem 3.2

Let
K
be a nonempty compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
0\in\mbox{\rm int}\,K
. Then, the inequality (
11
) holds for any nonvanishing and continuous function
f:K\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if
\alpha\leq r
.

3.3
The effect of changing the norm

Until now, we have focused on the maximum displacements of nonvanishing continuous vector-valued functions when
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is equipped with the Euclidean norm. Since all norms in a finite-dimensional vector space are equivalent, one can wonder how the estimates like the one in (
8
) may change when considering a different norm instead of the Euclidean norm.

Let
\|\cdot\|_{*}
be an arbitrary norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, there exist positive constants
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
such that

\theta_{1}\|x\|_{*}\leq\|x\|\leq\theta_{2}\|x\|_{*}

(13)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By the homogeneous property of norms, the inequalities in (
13
) hold for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if they hold for all
x\in\partial\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)
. Thus, by setting

\theta^{*}_{1}=\min\limits_{x\in\partial\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)}\dfrac{1}{\|x\|_{*}}\quad\mbox{\rm and}\quad\theta^{*}_{2}=\max\limits_{x\in\partial\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)}\dfrac{1}{\|x\|_{*}},

(14)

we see that the inequalities in (
13
) holds for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if
\theta_{1}\leq\theta^{*}_{1}
and
\theta_{2}\geq\theta^{*}_{2}
.

The counterpart of estimate (
8
) in the norm
\|\cdot\|_{*}
can be expressed as follows.

Theorem 3.3

Let
K
be a nonempty compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
0\in\mbox{\rm int}\,K
and let
f:K\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a nonvanishing continuous function. Then, one has

\sup_{x\in K}\|f(x)-x\|_{*}\geq r_{*}+\dfrac{\theta^{*}_{1}}{\theta^{*}_{2}}\inf_{x\in K}\|f(x)\|_{*}

(15)

with
r_{*}:=\dfrac{r}{\theta^{*}_{2}}
, where
r
is defined in (
9
).

Proof

By (
14
) and the remark following it, we have

\|f(x)-x\|\leq\theta^{*}_{2}\|f(x)-x\|_{*}\quad\mbox{\rm for all}\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Thus,

\sup_{x\in K}\|f(x)-x\|\leq\theta^{*}_{2}\,\sup_{x\in K}\|f(x)-x\|_{*}.

Similarly, by (
14
) and the subsequent remark,

\theta^{*}_{1}\inf_{x\in K}\|f(x)\|_{*}\leq\inf_{x\in K}\|f(x)\|.

Therefore, by using (
8
) we obtain

\theta^{*}_{2}\sup_{x\in K}\|f(x)-x\|_{*}\geq\sup_{x\in K}\|f(x)-x\|\geq r+\inf_{x\in K}\|f(x)\|\geq r+\theta^{*}_{1}\inf_{x\in K}\|f(x)\|_{*}.

This implies that

\sup_{x\in K}\|f(x)-x\|_{*}\geq\dfrac{r}{\theta^{*}_{2}}+\dfrac{\theta^{*}_{1}}{\theta^{*}_{2}}\inf_{x\in K}\|f(x)\|_{*}.

Thus, (
15
) is valid.

\hfill\Box

From Theorem
3.2
and the definitions of
\theta^{*}_{1}
and
\theta^{*}_{2}
it follows that (
15
) is the best lower estimate for the maximum displacements of nonvanishing continuous vector-valued functions defined on
K
, if
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is equipped with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{*}
.

Corollary 1

Let
\|\cdot\|_{*}
be a norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
K
be a nonempty compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
0\in\mbox{\rm int}\,K
. Then, there exists a constant
\nu>0
depending only on the norm
\|\cdot\|_{*}
such that the inequality

\sup_{x\in K}\|f(x)-x\|_{*}\geq\nu\left(r+\inf_{x\in K}\|f(x)\|_{*}\right),

(16)

where
r
is defined by (
9
), holds for any nonvanishing continuous function
f:K\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof

Set
\nu:=\min\left\{\dfrac{1}{\theta^{*}_{2}},\dfrac{\theta^{*}_{1}}{\theta^{*}_{2}}\right\}
, where
\theta^{*}_{1}
and
\theta^{*}_{2}
are defined by (
14
). Given any nonvanishing continuous function
f:K\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we observe that

r.\dfrac{1}{\theta^{*}_{2}}+\dfrac{\theta^{*}_{1}}{\theta^{*}_{2}}\inf_{x\in K}\|f(x)\|_{*}\geq\left(\min\left\{\dfrac{1}{\theta^{*}_{2}},\dfrac{\theta^{*}_{1}}{\theta^{*}_{2}}\right\}\right)\left(r+\inf_{x\in K}\|f(x)\|_{*}\right).

So, applying Theorem
3.3
, we get (
16
) from (
15
).

\hfill\Box

Corollary 2

Suppose that
X
is an
n
-dimensional normed space equipped with a norm
\|\cdot\|_{*}
and
g:X\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a linear bijective mapping. Then, there exists a number
\nu>0
satisfying the following property: For any
r>0
, one can find a nonempty convex compact subset
K_{r}
of
X
with
0\in\mbox{\rm int}\,K_{r}
and with
\|x\|_{*}\leq r
for all
x\in K_{r}
, such that for any nonvanishing continuous function
f:K_{r}\to X
it holds

\sup_{x\in K_{r}}\|f(x)-x\|_{*}\geq\nu\left(r+\inf_{x\in K_{r}}\|f(x)\|_{*}\right).

(17)

Proof

Since
g:X\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a linear bijective mapping, by
(
9
, Theorem 1.21)
(see also
(
6
, Lemma 2.5)
) we can assert that
g
is a homeomorphism. Put
r_{2}=\dfrac{r\bar{\theta}}{\|g^{-1}\|_{*}}
, where
\|g^{-1}\|_{*}:=\sup\limits_{\|y\|=1}\|g^{-1}(y)\|_{*}
and
\bar{\theta}:=\min\limits_{x\in\partial\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)}\dfrac{1}{\|x\|_{*}}
. Let
K_{r}=g^{-1}\left(\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r_{2})\right)
. As
\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r_{2})
is a compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
K_{r}
is a nonempty compact convex set in
X
with
0\in\mbox{\rm int}\,K_{r}
. We have
\|y\|_{*}\leq r
for every
y\in K_{r}
. Indeed, for any
y\in K_{r}
, we can find
x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r_{2})
with
y=g^{-1}(x)
. Therefore,

\begin{array}[]{ll}\|y\|_{*}=\|g^{-1}(x)\|_{*}\leq\|g^{-1}\|_{*}\|x\|_{*}\leq\dfrac{\|g^{-1}\|_{*}\|x\|}{\bar{\theta}}\leq\dfrac{r_{2}\|g^{-1}\|_{*}}{\bar{\theta}}=r.\end{array}

Define a function
\|\cdot\|_{\#}:X\to\mathbb{R}
by setting
\|y\|_{\#}=\|g(y)\|
for all
y\in X.
As
g
is a linear bijective mapping,
\|\cdot\|_{\#}
is a norm on
X
. Clearly,
\|g^{-1}(x)\|_{\#}=\|x\|
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
f:K_{r}\to X
be a nonvanishing continuous function. Then, the mapping
g\circ f\circ g^{-1}:\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r_{2})\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is continuous. In addition, the nonvanishing property of
f
together with the linearity of
g
and
g^{-1}
imply that
(g\circ f\circ g^{-1})(x)\neq 0
for every
x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r_{2})
. Hence, applying Theorem
3.1
for the nonempty compact convex set
B_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r_{2})
and the function
g\circ f\circ g^{-1}
yields

\sup_{x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r_{2})}\|(g\circ f\circ g^{-1})(x)-x\|\geq r_{2}+\inf_{x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r_{2})}\|(g\circ f\circ g^{-1})(x)\|.

(18)

On the other hand, invoking the linearity of
g
once again, one has

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\sup\limits_{x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r_{2})}\|(g\circ f\circ g^{-1})(x)-x\|&=&\sup\limits_{x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r_{2})}\|g^{-1}\Big((g\circ f\circ g^{-1})(x)-x\Big)\|_{\#}\\
&=&\sup\limits_{x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r_{2})}\|(f\circ g^{-1})(x)-g^{-1}(x)\|_{\#}\\
&=&\sup\limits_{y\in K_{r}}\|f(y)-y\|_{\#}\end{array}

and

\inf_{x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r_{2})}\|(g\circ f\circ g^{-1})(x)\|=\inf_{x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,r_{2})}\|(f\circ g^{-1})(x)\|_{\#}=\inf_{y\in K_{r}}\|f(y)\|_{\#}.

Combining this with (
18
) gives

\sup_{y\in K_{r}}\|f(y)-y\|_{\#}\geq r_{2}+\inf_{y\in K_{r}}\|f(y)\|_{\#}.

(19)

Observe that
\|\cdot\|_{\#}
depends only on the function
g
. Then, using (
19
) and some arguments similar to those in the proofs of Theorem
3.3
and Corollary
1
with the Euclidean norm being replaced by
\|\cdot\|_{\#}
, we can find
\eta>0
depending only on the function
g
such that

\sup_{x\in K_{r}}\|f(x)-x\|_{*}\geq\eta\left(r_{2}+\inf_{x\in K_{r}}\|f(x)\|_{*}\right)=\eta\left(\dfrac{r\bar{\theta}}{\|g^{-1}\|_{*}}+\inf_{x\in K_{r}}\|f(x)\|_{*}\right).

Setting
\nu=\eta\min\left\{\dfrac{\bar{\theta}}{\|g^{-1}\|_{*},1}\right\}
, we obtain (
17
) from the last inequality.

\hfill\Box

4
Other Five Questions and Their Solutions

In the preceding two sections, we have solved the first question among the six ones proposed by Professor Biagio Ricceri
[
8
]
and provided several related results. In this section, we will study the remaining five questions. For each question, we will try either to give a complete solution or to provide a near-to-complete partial solution. In doing so, we will establish meaningful properties of nonvanishing continuous vector-valued functions.

4.1
Question 2

The next question reminds us of the concept of eigenvalue of linear operators. To be more precise, here we are concerned with approximate eigenvalues in a sense.

Question 2:

Can one find a continuous function
f:\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
f(x)\neq 0
for every
x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)
such that there is a
\mu>0
satisfying

\|f(x)-\mu x\|\leq\mu\quad\mbox{\rm for all}\ x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)?

(20)

Proposition 3

There is no function
f
fulfilling the required conditions.

Proof

Suppose on contrary that there is a nonvanishing continuous function
f:\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that there is a
\mu>0
satisfying (
20
). Then, applied to the variational inequality defined by the function
f
and the nonempty compact convex constraint set
\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)
, the Hartman-Stampacchia Theorem (see Theorem
2.1
) gives a vector
\bar{x}\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)
such that

\langle f(\bar{x}),y-\bar{x}\rangle\geq 0\quad\mbox{\rm for all}\ y\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1).

(21)

The case
\bar{x}\in{\rm int}\,\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)
is excluded, because if
\bar{x}\in{\rm int}\,\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)
then (
21
) yields
f(\bar{x})=0
. So,
\bar{x}\in\partial\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)
. Combining this with (
21
), we can assert that
f(\bar{x})=-\lambda\bar{x}
for some
\lambda\geq 0
. The situation
\lambda=0
cannot occur, because
f(x)\neq 0
for every
x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)
. Hence,
f(\bar{x})=-\lambda\bar{x}
with
\lambda>0
. But, the latter inequality is in conflict with (
20
) since we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mu\geq\|f(\bar{x})-\mu\bar{x}\|&=&\|\mu\bar{x}-f(\bar{x})\|\\
&=&\sup\limits_{u\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)}\,\langle\mu\bar{x}-f(\bar{x}),u\rangle\\
&\geq&\langle\mu\bar{x}-f(\bar{x}),\bar{x}\rangle\\
&=&\langle\mu\bar{x}+\lambda\bar{x},\bar{x}\rangle\\
&=&\langle(\mu+\lambda)\bar{x},\bar{x}\rangle\\
&=&\mu+\lambda.\end{array}

The proof is complete.

\hfill\Box

Interestingly, Proposition
3
can be proved via Theorem
2.2
. In fact, the following refined answer for Question 2 is valid.

Theorem 4.1

For any nonvanishing continuous function
f:\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any number
\mu>0
, one has

\sup_{x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)}\|f(x)-\mu x\|\geq\mu+\inf\limits_{x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)}\|f(x)\|.

(22)

Hence, there is no function
f
satisfying the conditions stated in Question 2.

Proof

Let
f:\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an arbitrary nonvanishing continuous function. Given any number
\mu>0
, we put
g(x)=\frac{1}{\mu}f(x)
for all
x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)
. Clearly, the function
g:\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is nonvanishing and continuous. Therefore, by Theorem
2.2
we have
d_{g}\geq\gamma_{g}
. Hence,
in agreement with the notations defined in (
2
) and (
3
), we get

\sup_{x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)}\|g(x)-x\|\geq 1+\inf_{x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)}\|g(x)\|.

Since
g(x)=\frac{1}{\mu}f(x)
for every
x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)
, multiplying both sides of the last inequality by
\mu
yields (
22
). The first assertion of the theorem has been proved.

For any nonvanishing continuous function
f:\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any number
\mu>0
, by the Weierstrass theorem (see, e.g.,
(
7
, Theorem 1.59)
) we find that the number
\alpha:=\inf\limits_{x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)}\|f(x)\|
is positive and there is a vector
\bar{x}
with

\sup_{x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)}\|f(x)-\mu x\|=\|f(\bar{x})-\mu\bar{x}\|.

So, from (
22
) it follows that

\|f(\bar{x})-\mu\bar{x}\|\geq\mu+\alpha>\mu.

This shows that the property (
20
) cannot hold.

\hfill\Box

As an illustration for Theorem
4.1
, let us consider a simple example.

Example 1

Setting
f(x)=x^{2}+1
for all
x\in[-1,1]
, we have a nonvanishing continuous function
f:\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{1}}(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}^{1}
. It is easily shown that
\sup\limits_{x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{1}}(0,1)}|f(x)-\mu x|=2+\mu
for every
\mu>0
, and the supremum is attained at
x=-1
. Since
\mu+\inf\limits_{x\in\bar{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(0,1)}\|f(x)\|=\mu+1
, the inequality (
22
) is strict for all
\mu>0
.

4.2
Question 3

It is well known (see, e.g.,
(
3
, Proposition 7.1)
) that the closed unit ball in an infinite-dimensional normed space is noncompact in the normed topology. Hence, the results from the previous sections cannot be fully generalized to infinite-dimensional normed spaces, making the next question both interesting and challenging.

Question 3:

Can we find a finite-dimensional linear subspace
F
of a Hilbert space
{\cal H}
and a continuous function
\varphi:{\cal H}\to{\cal H}
such that

(a)

\sup\limits_{x\in{\cal H}}\|\varphi(x)-x\|<+\infty
;

(b)

For each
x\in{\cal H}
, there exists
y\in F
such that
\langle\varphi(x),y\rangle\neq 0
?

To solve this question, we need the following auxiliary result, whose proof is based on Corollary
2
.

Lemma 1

Let
F
be a finite-dimensional linear subspace of a Hilbert space
{\cal H}
. Then, there is no continuous operator
\varphi:F\to F
such that

(i)

\sup\limits_{x\in F}\|\varphi(x)-x\|<+\infty
;

(ii)

For each
x\in F
, there exists
y\in F
such that
\langle\varphi(x),y\rangle\neq 0
.

Proof

On the contrary, suppose that there is a continuous operator
\varphi:F\to F
satisfying the conditions (i) and (ii). It follows from (ii) that
\varphi(x)\neq 0
for all
x\in F
. By Corollary
2
, there is a constant
\nu>0
such that, for any
r>0
, one can find a nonempty compact convex set
K_{r}
in
F
with
0\in\mbox{\rm int}\,K_{r}
,
\|x\|\leq r
for all
x\in K_{r}
, and

\sup_{x\in K_{r}}\|\varphi(x)-x\|\geq\nu\left(r+\inf_{x\in K_{r}}\|\varphi(x)\|\right).

Since
\sup\limits_{x\in F}\|\varphi(x)-x\|\geq\sup\limits_{x\in K_{r}}\|\varphi(x)-x\|
, this yields

\sup_{x\in F}\|\varphi(x)-x\|\geq\nu\left(r+\inf_{x\in K_{r}}\|\varphi(x)\|\right)\geq\nu r.

Letting
r\to\infty
, we get
\sup\limits_{x\in F}\|\varphi(x)-x\|=+\infty
, which contradicts the condition (i).

\hfill\Box

The next theorem gives an answer in the negative to Question 3.

Theorem 4.2

Suppose that
F\subset{\cal H}
is a finite-dimensional linear subspace. If
\varphi:{\cal H}\to{\cal H}
is such a continuous function that for each
x\in{\cal H}
there exists
y\in F
satisfying
\langle\varphi(x),y\rangle\neq 0
, then
\sup\limits_{x\in{\cal H}}\|\varphi(x)-x\|=+\infty
.

Proof

We have
{\cal H}=F\oplus F^{\perp}
, where
F^{\perp}
is the orthogonal complement of
F
. Define the continuous linear operators
P_{1}:{\cal H}\to F
and
P_{2}:{\cal H}\to F^{\perp}
as follows. For each
x\in{\cal H}
with
x=x_{1}+x_{2}
, where
x_{1}\in F
,
x_{2}\in F^{\perp}
, put
P_{1}(x)=x_{1}
and
P_{2}(x)=x_{2}
. For every
x\in F
, since
P_{1}\big(\varphi(x)\big)-x\in F
, we have

\displaystyle\|\varphi(x)-x\|

\displaystyle=\|P_{1}\big(\varphi(x)\big)+P_{2}\big(\varphi(x)\big)-x\|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\|P_{1}\big(\varphi(x)\big)-x\|^{2}+\|P_{2}\big(\varphi(x)\big)\|^{2}+2\langle P_{1}\big(\varphi(x)\big)-x,P_{2}\big(\varphi(x)\big)\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\|P_{1}\big(\varphi(x)\big)-x\|^{2}+\|P_{2}\big(\varphi(x)\big)\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\|P_{1}\big(\varphi(x)\big)-x\|.

Therefore,
\sup\limits_{x\in F}\|\varphi(x)-x\|\geq\sup\limits_{x\in F}\|P_{1}\big(\varphi(x)\big)-x\|.
This yields

\sup_{x\in{\cal H}}\|\varphi(x)-x\|\geq\sup\limits_{x\in F}\|\varphi(x)-x\|\geq\sup_{x\in F}\|P_{1}\big(\varphi(x)\big)-x\|.

(23)

By the assumption on
\varphi
, for each
x\in{\cal H}
, there exists
y\in F
such that
\langle\varphi(x),y\rangle\neq 0
. As

\langle\varphi(x),y\rangle=\langle P_{1}\big(\varphi(x)\big)+P_{2}\big(\varphi(x)\big),y\rangle=\langle P_{1}\big(\varphi(x)\big),y\rangle,

we get
\langle P_{1}\big(\varphi(x)\big),y\rangle\neq 0
. Hence, the continuous function
P_{1}\circ\big(\varphi{\big|}_{F}\big):F\to F
has the following property:
For each
x\in F
, there exists
y\in F
such that
\big\langle\left(P_{1}\circ\big(\varphi{\big|}_{F}\big)\right)(x),y\big\rangle\neq 0
.
So, thanks to Lemma
1
we know that

\sup_{x\in F}\|P_{1}\big(\varphi(x)\big)-x\|=\sup_{x\in F}\|\left(P_{1}\circ\big(\varphi{\big|}_{F}\big)\right)(x)-x\|=+\infty.

Combining this with (
23
) gives
\sup\limits_{x\in{\cal H}}\|\varphi(x)-x\|=+\infty
, and completes the proof.

\hfill\Box

Remark 3

The requirement (b) in Question 3 can be termed as a
nonvanishing property of
\varphi:{\cal H}\to{\cal H}
with respect to the linear subspace
F
. In connection with the concepts discussed in Sections
2
and
3
, the number
\sup\limits_{x\in{\cal H}}\|\varphi(x)-x\|
can be called the
maximum displacement
of
\varphi
. Then, the result in Theorem
4.2
reads as follows:
The maximum displacement of a continuous function
\varphi:{\cal H}\to{\cal H}
, which is nonvanishing w.r.t. a finite-dimensional linear subspace
F\subset{\cal H}
, is infinity.

Remark 4

The finite-dimensionality of the linear subspace
F
is an essential assumption of Theorem
4.2
. Indeed, if
{\cal H}
is infinite-dimensional, then the maximum displacement of a continuous function
\varphi:{\cal H}\to{\cal H}
, which is nonvanishing w.r.t.
{\cal H}
, can be finite (see Theorem
4.5
below).

4.3
Question 4

Apart from using the normed spaces setting, the next question is very different from Question 3 by its contents.

Question 4:

Let
E
be an infinite-dimensional real normed space. Can we find a continuous operator
\psi:E\to E
and finitely many
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}\in E^{*}
such that

(a1)

\sup\limits_{x\in E}\|\psi(x)-x\|<+\infty
;

(b1)

For each
x\in E
there exists
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
\varphi_{i}(\psi(x))\neq 0
?

To answer this question, let us first prove the following lemma.

Lemma 2

Let
E
be an infinite-dimensional real normed space and
L_{0}
be a finite-dimensional subspace of
E
. Then, there is no continuous operator
\psi_{0}:L_{0}\to L_{0}
such that

(i)

\sup\limits_{x\in L_{0}}\|\psi_{0}(x)-x\|<+\infty
;

(ii)

\psi_{0}(x)\neq 0
for all
x\in L_{0}
.

Proof

Suppose to the contrary that there exists a continuous operator
\psi_{0}:L_{0}\to L_{0}
such that (i) and (ii) hold. By Corollary
2
we can find a constant
\nu>0
such that for any
r>0
there is a nonempty compact convex set
K_{r}
in
L_{0}
with
0\in K_{r}
and
\|x\|\leq r
for all
x\in K_{r}
such that

\sup_{x\in K_{r}}\|\psi_{0}(x)-x\|\geq\nu\left(r+\inf_{x\in K_{r}}\|\psi_{0}(x)\|\right).

Thus, we get

\sup_{x\in L_{0}}\|\psi_{0}(x)-x\|\geq\sup_{x\in K_{r}}\|\psi_{0}(x)-x\|\geq\nu\left(r+\inf_{x\in K_{r}}\|\psi_{0}(x)\|\right)\geq\nu r.

Letting
r\to+\infty
, we deduce that
\sup\limits_{x\in L_{0}}\|\psi_{0}(x)-x\|=+\infty
, which contradicts (i).

\hfill\Box

The following theorem provides us with a solution to Question 4.

Theorem 4.3

Suppose that
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}
is a finite system of continuous linear functionals on an infinite-dimensional real normed space
E
. If
\psi:E\to E
is a continuous operator with
\sup\limits_{x\in E}\|\psi(x)-x\|<+\infty
, i.e.,
\psi
has a finite maximum displacement, then there must exist a vector
\bar{x}\in X
such that
\varphi_{i}(\psi(\bar{x}))=0
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. In other words, the conditions
(a1)
and
(b1)
in Question 4 cannot hold simultaneously.

Proof

Suppose to the contrary that there exist
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}\in E^{*}
and a continuous operator
\psi:E\to E
satisfying the conditions (a1) and (b1). Consider the linear mapping
A:E\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by

A(x)=(\varphi_{1}(x),\dots,\varphi_{n}(x))\quad\mbox{\rm for}\ x\in E.

Then,
M:=A(E)
is a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Put
m=\mbox{\rm dim}\,M
. It follows from (b1) that
m\geq 1
. So, there exist
v^{1},\dots,v^{m}\in M
such that
M=\mbox{\rm span}\;\{v^{1},\dots,v^{m}\}
and the vectors
v^{1},\dots,v^{m}
are linearly independent. Take
u^{1},\dots,u^{m}\in E
such that
A(u^{j})=v^{j}
for
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. Clearly,
u^{1},\dots,u^{m}
are linearly independent. Define the sets

\begin{array}[]{lll}L_{0}&=&\mbox{\rm span}\;\{u^{1},\dots,u^{m}\},\\
L^{\perp}&=&\big\{x\in E\mid\varphi_{i}(x)=0\ \mbox{\rm for all}\;i\in\{1,\dots,n\}\big\}.\end{array}

We see that
E=L_{0}\oplus L^{\perp}
. Indeed, take any
x\in E
. Then, there exists a unique vector
(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

A(x)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\xi_{j}v^{j}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\xi_{j}A(u^{j}).

For
x_{0}:=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\xi_{j}u^{j}
, one has
x_{0}\in L_{0}
and

A(x_{0})=A\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\xi_{j}u^{j}\right)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\xi_{j}A(u^{j})=A(x).

Therefore,
A(x-x_{0})=0
, which implies that
\varphi_{i}(x-x_{0})=0
for all
i\in\{i,\dots,n\}
. Thus,
x-x_{0}\in L^{\perp}
. Since
x\in E
is chosen arbitrarily,
E=L_{0}+L^{\perp}
. In addition, if
x\in(L_{0}\cap L^{\perp})
, then
A(x)=0
and there exist
(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{m})\in R^{m}
such that
x=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\xi_{j}u^{j}
. Hence,

0=A(x)=A\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\xi_{j}u^{j}\right)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\xi_{j}A(u^{j})=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\xi_{j}v^{j}.

Combining this with the linear independence of
\{v^{1},\dots,v^{m}\}
gives
\xi_{j}=0
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, which implies
x=0
. We have thus proved that
E=L_{0}\oplus L^{\perp}
.

We now show that the restriction
A{\big|}_{L_{0}}:L_{0}\to M
is a bijective mapping. Suppose that
A(x)=A(y)
for some
x,y\in L_{0}
. Then, there exist some vectors
(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{m}),(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

x=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\xi_{j}u^{j}\quad\mbox{\rm and}\quad y=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\mu_{j}u^{j}.

So, we have

\displaystyle 0=A(x)-A(y)

\displaystyle=A\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\xi_{j}u^{j}\right)-A\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\mu_{j}u^{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\xi_{j}A(u^{j})-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\mu_{j}A(u^{j})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}(\xi_{j}-\mu_{j})v^{j}.

This implies that
\xi_{j}=\mu_{j}
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. Or equivalently,
x=y
. Moreover, for any
w\in M
, there is
(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
w=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\omega_{j}v^{j}
. Thus,

w=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\omega_{j}A(u^{j})=A\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\omega_{j}u^{j}\right).

So,
w=A(x)
where
x=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\omega_{j}u^{j}\in L_{0}
. Therefore,
A{\big|}_{L_{0}}:L_{0}\to M
is a one-to-one mapping. Thus, by
(
9
, Theorem 1.21)
or
(
6
, Lemma 2.5)
, we can infer that
A{\big|}_{L_{0}}
is a homeomorphism.

Claim 2.

For any
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
, the set
S(\rho):=\{z\in L_{0}\mid\max\limits_{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\varphi_{i}(z)\leq\rho\}
is bounded.

Indeed, taking any
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
for any
z\in S(\rho)
, we have

\|A(z)\|=\left\|\big(\varphi_{1}(z),\dots,\varphi_{n}(z)\big)\right\|=\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}[\varphi_{i}(z)]^{2}}\leq\sqrt{n\rho^{2}}.

Since the linear mapping
A{\big|}_{L_{0}}
is a homeomorphism,
A{\big|}_{L_{0}}^{-1}
is a bounded linear operator, and hence,
S(\rho)
is bounded.

Let
\psi_{0}:E\to L_{0}
and
\psi_{1}:E\to L^{\perp}
be defined by setting
\psi_{0}(x)=x_{0}
and
\psi_{1}(x)=x_{1}
for every
x=x_{0}+x_{1}
with
x_{0}\in L_{0}
and
x_{1}\in L^{\perp}
. It follows from condition (a1) that there is
\beta\geq 0
such that
\sup\limits_{x\in E}\|\psi(x)-x\|=\beta
. Thus,
\|\psi(x)-x\|\leq\beta
for all
x\in E
. Put
\gamma=\max\limits_{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\|\varphi_{i}\|
. For any
x\in E
and for any
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, one has

\varphi_{i}\left(\psi(x)-x\right)\leq\|\varphi_{i}\|\|\psi(x)-x\|\leq\gamma\beta.

Furthermore, for every
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\varphi_{i}\left(\psi(x)-x\right)=\varphi_{i}\left(\psi_{0}(x)+\psi_{1}(x)-x\right)&=&\varphi_{i}\left(\psi_{0}(x)-x\right)+\varphi_{i}\left(\psi_{1}(x)\right)\\
&=&\varphi_{i}\left(\psi_{0}(x)-x\right).\end{array}

Thus,
\psi_{0}(x)-x\in S(\gamma\beta)
for all
x\in E
. Hence, by Claim 2, there exists
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\|\psi_{0}(x)-x\|\leq\sigma
for all
x\in E
. So,

\|\psi_{0}(x)\|\leq\sigma+\|x\|.

This gives
\|\psi_{0}(x)\|\leq\sigma+1
for all
x\in E
with
\|x\|\leq 1
. Note that the mapping
\psi_{0}{\big|}_{L_{0}}:L_{0}\to L_{0}
is linear and bounded, and hence, it is continuous. We also have

\sup\limits_{x\in E}\|\psi_{0}(x)-x\|\leq\sigma<+\infty.

(24)

On the other hand, noting that for every
x\in E
and for every
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\varphi_{i}\left(\psi(x)\right)=\varphi_{i}\left(\psi_{0}(x)+\psi_{1}(x)\right)=\varphi_{i}\left(\psi_{0}(x)\right)+\varphi_{i}\left(\psi_{1}(x)\right)=\varphi_{i}\left(\psi_{0}(x)\right),

we deduce from (b1) that for every
x\in E
, there exists
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
\varphi_{i}\left(\psi_{0}(x)\right)\neq 0
. It follows that
\psi_{0}(x)\neq 0
for all
x\in E
. Combining this with (
24
), we see that the continuous function
\psi_{0}{\big|}_{L_{0}}:L_{0}\to L_{0}
satisfies the conditions (i) and (ii) in Lemma
2
. We have arrived at a contradiction. So, there is no
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}\in E^{*}
and continuous operator
\psi:E\to E
such that the conditions (a1) and (b1) hold simultaneously.

\hfill\Box

4.4
Question 5

Unlike the above Question 4, where the continuous function
\psi
maps
E
to
E
, the next question asks about some properties of a continuous function
\psi
mapping
E
to its dual space
E^{*}
. These properties are stated in connection with a linear and continuous operator
\varphi
, that also maps
E
to
E^{*}
. In this context, the nonvanishing feature and the maximum displacement of continuous vector-valued functions, which have been studied intensively until now, are formulated in more sophisticated and quite new forms.

Question 5:

Let
E
be a normed space,
F\subset E
a linear subspace with
{\rm dim}(F)<\infty
,
\psi:E\rightarrow E^{*}
a continuous mapping, and
\varphi:E\rightarrow E^{*}
a linear and continuous operator such that

(a2)

For every
y\in E
there exists
x\in F
satisfying
\psi(y)(x)\neq 0
;

(b2)

For every
x\in F\setminus\{0\}
there exists
y\in E
satisfying
\varphi(y)(x)\neq 0
.

Then, does the equality

\sup\limits_{y\in E}\|\varphi(y)-\psi(y)\|_{E^{*}}=+\infty

(25)

hold true?

The next theorem gives an answer in the affirmative to Question 5.

Theorem 4.4

If
E
is a normed space,
F\subset E
is a linear subspace with
{\rm dim}(F)<\infty
,
\psi:E\rightarrow E^{*}
is a continuous mapping, and
\varphi:E\rightarrow E^{*}
is a linear and continuous operator such that both conditions
(a2)
and
(b2)
are satisfied, then the equality (
25
) is valid.

Proof.
If
E
is a trivial normed space, i.e.,
E=\{0\}
, then one has
F=\{0\}
and
E^{*}=\{0\}
. Hence, (a2) fails to hold. Thus, the assertion of the theorem is valid.

Consider the case
E
has nonzero vectors. Let
{\rm dim}(F)=m
. Condition (a2) implies that
m\geq 1
. Indeed, taking any
\bar{y}\in E
, we have
\psi(\bar{y})\in E^{*}
. By (a2), we can find
\bar{x}\in F
such that
\varphi(\bar{y})(\bar{x})\neq 0
. This forces
\bar{x}\neq 0
. Hence,
{\rm dim}(F)\geq 1
.

Clearly, the properties (a2) and (b2) are still valid if the norm in
F
is replaced by another equivalent norm. So, we can equip
F
with a scalar product and the corresponding Euclidean norm, denoted by
\|.\|_{2}
. Via the scalar product,
F^{*}
can be identified with
F
. Let
\theta
be a positive constant such that
\|x^{*}\|_{F^{*}}\geq\theta\|x^{*}\|_{2}
for all
x^{*}\in F^{*}
.

Claim 1.

The set
L:=\big\{\varphi(y){\big|}_{F}\mid y\in E\big\}
, where
\varphi(y){\big|}_{F}
denotes the restriction of
\varphi(y)
on
F
, coincides with
F^{*}
.

Indeed, by the linearity of the operator
\varphi:E\rightarrow E^{*}
,
L
is a linear subspace of the dual space
F^{*}
, which has been identified with
F
. Besides, for every
x^{*}\in F^{*}
and every
x\in F
, one has
x^{*}(x)=\langle x^{*},x\rangle
. If
L
is a proper linear subspace of
F
, then there exists
\bar{x}
in
F\setminus L
such that
\langle z,\bar{x}\rangle=0
for all
z\in L
. It follows that

0=\langle\varphi(y){\big|}_{F},\bar{x}\rangle=\varphi(y){\big|}_{F}(\bar{x})=\varphi(y)(\bar{x})

for all
y\in E
, contrary to (b2). We have thus proved that
L=F^{*}
.

Since the linear operator
\Phi:E\to L
, where
\Phi(y):=\varphi(y){\big|}_{F}
for all
y\in E
, is continuous and surjective, Claim 1 allows us to find a linear subspace
M
of
E
with
{\rm dim}\,M=m
such that
E={\rm ker}(\Phi)\oplus M
(see
(
9
, Definition 4.20)
for the definition of
direct sum
and
(
9
, Lemma 4.21)
for the existence of a
complement
of
{\rm ker}(\Phi)
in
E
). By the construction of
M
,
\Phi{\big|}_{M}:M\to L
is a linear homeomorphism. Hence, the inverse map
\Phi{\big|}_{M}^{-1}:L\to M
is a continuous linear operator.

We have

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rcl}\sup\limits_{y\in E}\|\varphi(y)-\psi(y)\|_{E^{*}}&=&\sup\limits_{y\in E}\left(\sup\limits_{v\in\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)}\big(\varphi(y)-\psi(y)\big)(v)\right)\\
&\geq&\sup\limits_{y\in E}\left(\sup\limits_{v\in\bar{B}_{F}(0,1)}\big(\varphi(y)-\psi(y)\big)(v)\right)\\
&=&\sup\limits_{y\in E}\left(\sup\limits_{v\in\bar{B}_{F}(0,1)}\big(\varphi(y){\big|}_{F}-\psi(y){\big|}_{F}\big)(v)\right)\\
&=&\sup\limits_{y\in E}\|\varphi(y){\big|}_{F}-\psi(y){\big|}_{F}\|_{F^{*}}\\
&\geq&\sup\limits_{y\in M}\|\varphi(y){\big|}_{F}-\psi(y){\big|}_{F}\|_{F^{*}}\\
&\geq&\theta\,\sup\limits_{z\in F}\|z-\psi\Big(\Phi{\big|}_{M}^{-1}(z)\Big){\big|}_{F}\|_{2},\end{array}

where the change of variable
z=\varphi(y){\big|}_{F}
, which yields
y=\Phi{\big|}_{M}^{-1}(z)
, has been done in the last transformation. The mapping
\psi\Big(\Phi{\big|}_{M}^{-1}(\cdot)\Big){\big|}_{F}:F\to F
, which is defined by setting
\psi\Big(\Phi{\big|}_{M}^{-1}(\cdot)\Big){\big|}_{F}(z)=\Big[\psi\Big(\Phi{\big|}_{M}^{-1}(z)\big)\Big]{\big|}_{F}
for all
z\in F
, is continuous by the continuity of
\psi
. In addition, for every
z\in F
, by using condition (a2) for
y:=\Phi{\big|}_{M}^{-1}(z)
we can find
x\in F
such that
\psi(y)(x)\neq 0
. Since the latter can be rewritten as
\langle\psi\Big(\Phi{\big|}_{M}^{-1}(z)\Big){\big|}_{F},x\rangle\neq 0
, we can apply Lemma
1
for
f:=\psi\Big(\Phi{\big|}_{M}^{-1}(\cdot)\Big){\big|}_{F}
to obtain

\sup\limits_{z\in F}\|z-\psi\Big(\Phi{\big|}_{M}^{-1}(z)\Big){\big|}_{F}\|_{2}=+\infty.

From this and (
4.4
) we can infer that the equality (
25
) is valid.

The proof is complete.
\hfill\Box

4.5
Question 6

It is of interest to know what happen if the condition
{\rm dim}(F)<\infty
in Question 5 and in Theorem
4.4
is violated. Probably, this is one of the strong motivations for the following question of Professor Ricceri
[
8
]
.

Question 6:

Let
E
be a normed space and
F\subset E
a linear subspace with
{\rm dim}(F)=\infty
. Does there exist a continuous mapping
\psi:E\rightarrow E^{*}
and a linear and continuous operator
\varphi:E\rightarrow E^{*}
satisfying the conditions

(a3)

For every
y\in E
there exists
x\in F
satisfying
\psi(y)(x)\neq 0
;

(b3)

For every
x\in F\setminus\{0\}
there exists
y\in E
satisfying
\varphi(y)(x)\neq 0

such that the inequality

\sup\limits_{y\in E}\|\varphi(y)-\psi(y)\|_{E^{*}}<+\infty

(33)

holds true?

To solve the above question in the affirmative in one special case, we need the next auxiliary result on nonvanishing continuous operators having finite maximum displacements. Note that the continuous function
\psi
in Question 6 maps
E
to the dual space
E^{*}
, while the function
\psi
in the forthcoming theorem maps
E
to
E
.

Theorem 4.5

Let
E
be an infinite-dimensional normed space. Then, there exists a nonvanishing continuous operator
\psi:E\to E
such that

\sup\limits_{y\in E}\|y-\psi(y)\|\leq 1.

(34)

Proof.
Given an infinite-dimensional normed space
E
, we can find a continuous mapping
f:\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)\to\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)
, which has no fixed point (see, e.g.,
(
3
, Remarks 8.7, p. 66)
). Define a map
\psi:E\to E
by the formula

\psi(x)=\begin{cases}x-f(x)&{\rm if}\ x\in\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)\\
x-f\left(\dfrac{x}{\|x\|}\right)&{\rm if}\ x\notin\bar{B}_{E}(0,1).\end{cases}

(35)

Claim 1
.
\psi
is nonvanishing.

Indeed, if
x\in\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)
, then
\psi(x)=x-f(x)\neq 0
because the mapping
f:\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)\to\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)
has no fixed point. If
x\notin\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)
, then
\|x\|>1
. Therefore, if
\psi(x)=x-f\left(\dfrac{x}{\|x\|}\right)=0
, then one has

1<\|x\|=\|f\left(\dfrac{x}{\|x\|}\right)\|\leq 1,

which is impossible.

Claim 2
.
\psi
is continuous.

Since the identity mapping is continuous, by (
35
) it is clear that the claim will be proved if we can show that the function

\widetilde{f}(x):=\begin{cases}f(x)&{\rm if}\ x\in\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)\\
f\left(\dfrac{x}{\|x\|}\right)&{\rm if}\ x\notin\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)\end{cases}

(36)

is continuous on
E
. Take any
\bar{x}\in E
and suppose that
V\subset E
is an open set containing
\widetilde{f}(\bar{x})
. There is an open neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}
such that

\widetilde{f}(x)\in V\quad{\rm for\ all}\ x\in U.

(37)

Indeed, if
\|\bar{x}\|<1
, then there exists an open set
U_{0}\subset B_{E}(0,1)
with
\bar{x}\in U_{0}
. As
\widetilde{f}(x)=f(x)
for all
x\in U_{0}
, by the continuity of
f
we can find an open set
U\subset U_{0}
such that
\bar{x}\in U
and
f(x)\in V
for all
x\in U
. Then, from (
36
) it follows that (
37
) holds with this
U
.

If
\|\bar{x}\|=1
, then by the continuity of
f:\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)\to\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)
we can find an open set
U_{1}
containing
\bar{x}
such that

f\big(U_{1}\cap\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)\big)\subset V\cap\bar{B}_{E}(0,1).
Hence, from (
36
) it follows that

\widetilde{f}\big(U_{1}\cap\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)\big)\subset V.

(38)

Clearly, the map
g:E\setminus\{0\}\to\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)
, where
g(x):=\dfrac{x}{\|x\|}
for every
x\neq 0
, is continuous. So, the map
f\circ g:E\setminus\{0\}\to\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)
is continuous. We observe for any
x\in E\setminus\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)
that

(f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x))=f\left(\dfrac{x}{\|x\|}\right)=\widetilde{f}(x).

Note that
(f\circ g)(\bar{x})=f(\bar{x})=\widetilde{f}(\bar{x})\in V
. So, by the continuity of the map
f\circ g:E\setminus\{0\}\to\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)
we can find an open set
U_{2}
containing
\bar{x}
such that
(f\circ g)\big(U_{2}\big)\subset V
. Therefore,

\widetilde{f}\Big(U_{2}\cap\big(E\setminus\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)\big)\Big)=(f\circ g)\Big(U_{2}\cap\big(E\setminus\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)\big)\Big)\subset V.

(39)

Setting
U=U_{1}\cap U_{2}
, we deduce from (
38
) and (
39
) that (
37
) is valid.

If
\|\bar{x}\|>1
, then by the continuity of
f\circ g
, where
g:E\setminus\{0\}\to\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)
is defined by setting
g(x)=\dfrac{x}{\|x\|}
for every
x\neq 0
, and the fact that

(f\circ g)(x)=\widetilde{f}(x)\quad{\rm for\ every}\ x\in E\setminus\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)

we can find an open neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}
with
U\subset E\setminus\bar{B}_{E}(0,1)
such that
(f\circ g)(U)\subset V
. This implies (
37
) and completes the proof of the continuity of
\widetilde{f}
.

Claim 3
.
The inequality (
34
) holds.

The desired inequality follows directly from (
35
).

Summing up all the above, we conclude that the operator
\psi:E\to E
constructed by (
35
) is nonvanishing, continuous, and it satisfies the inequality (
34
).
\hfill\Box

The next theorem gives an answer in the affirmative to Question 6 for one class of normed spaces. The question remains open if
E
is not a Hilbert space.

Theorem 4.6

For any infinite-dimensional Hilbert space
E
and for any a linear subspace
F\subset E
with
{\rm dim}(F)=\infty
, there exist a continuous mapping
\psi:E\rightarrow E^{*}
and a continuous linear operator
\varphi:E\rightarrow E^{*}
satisfying the conditions
(a3)
and
(b3)
in Question 6 such that the inequality (
33
) holds true.

Proof.
As
E
is a Hilbert space, we can identify
E^{*}
with
E
. Besides, for the given infinite-dimensional linear subspace
F\subset E
, we have
E=F\oplus F^{\perp}
, where
F^{\perp}
denotes the orthogonal complement of
F
in
E
. By
\pi
we denote the orthogonal projection from
E
onto
F
. Then, for every
x\in E
, it holds that
x_{1}:=\pi(x)
belongs to
F
,
x_{2}:=x-\pi(x)
belongs to
F^{\perp}
, and
x=x_{1}+x_{2}
. Since
{\rm dim}(F)=\infty
, according to
(
3
, Remarks 8.7, p. 66)
, we can find a continuous mapping
g:\bar{B}_{F}(0,1)\to\bar{B}_{F}(0,1)
having no fixed point. Define a map
\psi_{F}:F\to F
by the formula

\psi_{F}(u)=\begin{cases}u-g(u)&{\rm if}\ u\in\bar{B}_{F}(0,1)\\
u-g\left(\dfrac{u}{\|u\|}\right)&{\rm if}\ u\in F\setminus\bar{B}_{F}(0,1).\end{cases}

(40)

The arguments given in the proof of Theorem
4.5
show that
\psi_{F}:F\to F
is a nonvanishing continuous operator. Now, we can construct an operator
\psi:E\to E^{*}
, where
E^{*}=E
, by setting

\psi(x)=\big(x-\pi(x)\big)+\psi_{F}(\pi(x))

(41)

for all
x\in E
. Clearly,
\psi:E\to E
is a continuous operator.

For every
y\in E
, since
\psi_{F}(\pi(y))\in F\setminus\{0\}
and
{\rm dim}(F)=\infty
, there exists
x\in F
satisfying
\langle\psi_{F}(\pi(y)),x\rangle\neq 0
. From (
41
) it follows that

\langle\psi(y),x\rangle=\langle\big(y-\pi(y)\big)+\psi_{F}(\pi(y)),x\rangle=\langle\psi_{F}(\pi(y)),x\rangle,

where the second equality is valid because
y-\pi(y)\in F^{\perp}
. Hence, we get
\langle\psi(y),x\rangle\neq 0
. Thus, the condition (a3) is satisfied.

Setting
\varphi(x)=x
for all
x\in E
, we see at once that
\varphi:E\to E^{*}
, where
E^{*}=E
, is a linear and continuous operator. To verify the condition (b3), take any
x\in F\setminus\{0\}
and choose
y=x
. Then one has
\varphi(y)(x)=\langle y,x\rangle=\langle x,x\rangle\neq 0
.

Furthermore, for every
y\in E
, it holds that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\|\varphi(y)-\psi(y)\|_{E^{*}}=\|y-\psi(y)\|_{E}&=&\|y-\big[(y-\pi(y))+\psi_{F}(\pi(y))\big]\|_{E}\\
&=&\|\pi(y)-\psi_{F}(\pi(y))\|_{E}.\end{array}

(42)

In addition, by (
40
) we have

\|\pi(y)-\psi_{F}(\pi(y))\|_{E}=\begin{cases}\|g(\pi(y))\|&{\rm if}\ \pi(y)\in\bar{B}_{F}(0,1)\\
\|g\left(\dfrac{\pi(y)}{\|\pi(y)\|}\right)\|&{\rm if}\ \pi(y)\in F\setminus\bar{B}_{F}(0,1).\end{cases}

(43)

Combining (
42
) with (
43
) yields
\|\varphi(y)-\psi(y)\|_{E^{*}}\leq 1
. So, one gets

\sup\limits_{y\in E}\|\varphi(y)-\psi(y)\|_{E^{*}}\leq 1.

We have thus shown that the inequality (
33
) holds for the chosen operators
\psi
and
\varphi
.

\hfill\Box

5
Concluding Remarks

We have studied six open questions related to the maximum displacements and generalized maximum displacements of nonvanishing continuous vector-valued functions in finite-dimensional and infinite-dimensional normed spaces. Our main results are the following:

- Sharp lower estimates for the maximum displacements (Theorems
2.2
,
3.2
, and
3.3
);

- A refined solution to a question on a type of positive approximate eigenvalues of nonvanishing continuous vector-valued functions (Theorem
4.1
);

- Sufficient conditions for the infinite maximum displacements of nonvanishing continuous vector-valued functions on Hilbert spaces (Theorem
4.2
);

- Necessary conditions for nonvanishing continuous vector-valued functions on infinite-dimensional normed spaces to have finite maximum displacements (Theorem
4.3
);

- Sufficient conditions for a nonvanishing continuous vector-valued function on a normed space to possess infinite generalized maximum displacements of (Theorem
4.4
);

- The existence of nonvanishing continuous vector-valued functions on an arbitrary infinite-dimensional normed space with finite maximum displacements (Theorem
4.5
);

- The existence of a pair of continuous vector-valued functions on an arbitrary infinite-dimensional Hilbert space satisfying certain conditions, which has finite generalized maximum displacement (Theorem
4.6
).

These new results and the proofs show how the Hartman-Stampacchia Theorem on the solution existence of variational inequalities can be useful for the investigations of rather difficult questions in nonlinear functional analysis.
