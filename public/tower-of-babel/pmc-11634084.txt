Effect of a colorectal bundle in an entire healthcare region in Switzerland: results from a prospective cohort study (EvaCol study)
Authors: Benjamin Wiesler, Robert Rosenberg, Raffaele Galli, Jürg Metzger, Mathias Worni, Mark Henschel, Mark Hartel, Christian Nebiker, Carsten T. Viehl, Alexandra Müller, Lukas Eisner, Martina Pabst, Urs Zingg, Daniel Stimpfle, Beat P. Müller, Markus von Flüe, Ralph Peterli, Laura Werlen, Markus Zuber, Jörn-Markus Gass, Marco von Strauss und Torney
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Standardization has the potential to serve as a measure to mitigate complication rates. The objective was to assess the impact of standardization by implementing a colorectal bundle (CB), which comprises nine elements, on the complication rates in left-sided colorectal resections.Patients and methods:This prospective, multicentre, observational, cohort trial was conducted in Switzerland at nine participating hospitals. During the control period, each patient was treated in accordance with the local standard protocol at their respective hospital. In the CB period, all patients were treated in accordance with the CB. The primary endpoint was the Comprehensive Complication Index (CCI) at 30 days.Results:A total of 1141 patients were included (723 in the No CB group and 418 in the CB group). The median age was 66 years, and 50.6% were female. Median CCI before and after CB implementation was 0.0 (interquartile range [IQR]: 0.0–20.9). A hurdle model approach was used for the analysis. The CB was not associated with the presence or severity of complications. Older age (odds ratio [OR] 1.02, 95% CI: 1.00–1.03), surgery for malignancy (OR 1.34, 95% CI: 1.01–1.92), emergency surgery (OR 2.19, 95% CI: 1.31–3.41), elevated nutritional risk score (OR 1.13, 95% CI: 1.01–1.24), and BMI (OR 1.04, 95% CI: 1.00–1.06) were associated with higher odds of postoperative complications. In a supplementary per-protocol analysis, for each additional item of the CB fulfilled, the odds of anastomotic leakage (AL) were 24% lower (OR 0.76, 95% CI: 0.64–0.93).Conclusions:Dedicated teams can establish high-quality colorectal services in a network of hospitals with a joint standard. The study can serve as a model for other healthcare settings to conduct and implement quality improvement programs. The consistent implementation of the CB items can reduce the occurrence of AL.

Complication rates in colorectal surgery remain high with complication occurrence in up to 40%1,2. Anastomotic leakage (AL) is the most feared complication as it results in a higher postoperative morbidity and increased postoperative mortality3. Although risk factors for higher complication rates, such as immunosuppression, severe comorbidities, and institutional factors were identified, only minor improvements could be made in overall complication and AL rates in colorectal surgery beyond single-center series4–8.

For other areas of surgery, it has already been shown that standardization is an effective measure to reduce complication rates. For example, the implementation of the WHO Safer Surgery Checklist led to a decrease in overall perioperative morbidity and mortality in general surgery9,10. In the meantime, this is well established in general surgery11. The introduction of the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) guidelines led to a significant reduction in the length of hospital stay and lower rates of nonsurgical complications12,13. Despite the proven effectiveness of standardization, only a few attempts have been made to standardize the entire preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative treatment course in colorectal surgery.

However, for individual measures there is growing evidence that they can effectively reduce complications. These are, for instance, risk stratification of patients undergoing colorectal anastomosis, showering with an antiseptic agent before surgery, antibiotic decontamination, and mechanical bowel preparation, targeted nutritional support, intravenous antibiotic prophylaxis within 60 min prior to incision, optimization of perioperative glucose levels and securing intraoperative euthermia, using of clippers instead of shaving for hair removal, use of wound protectors and changing gloves after the contaminated part of the operation, as well as presence of an experienced surgeon during formation of the anastomosis5,14–26.

These measures have been integrated into a protocol called the colorectal bundle (CB), which standardises the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative process for left-sided colorectal resections. It offers a comprehensive evidence-based quality improvement program and was rolled out in nine participating hospitals covering the entire healthcare region of North West Switzerland. The objective of the present study was to evaluate the implementation of the CB and its impact on the occurrence of postoperative complications.

Patients and methods

Patients

The EvaCol study is an prospective, observational, cohort trial conducted within a network of nine hospitals in Switzerland covering a population of 1.5 million inhabitants and an entire healthcare region. The network encompasses both minor and intermediate-sized medical centers, in addition to a tertiary referral center. All patients who underwent left-sided colorectal resections in one of the contributing hospitals were included. Patients were excluded if they withdrew further use of data, if they were minors, or if they had undergone visceral surgery within the previous 30 days, for this would have introduced a bias to the outcomes. The last exclusion criteria only applied at the beginning of the study period. Minors were excluded because they were treated in other facilities and because adolescents cannot be directly compared to adults due to physiological differences. Screening for eligible patients was performed from referrals, outpatient clinic and theater lists at the participating hospitals. Written consent for the further utilization of clinical data was obtained from the patients during their outpatient consultations or during their hospitalization. The study period lasted from 1 October 2020 to 31 October 2022. During an initial control period of 12 months, patients were treated according to each hospital’s local standard. After a 1-month implementation period, all patients received treatment according to the CB. SDC, Table 1 (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D520) shows the treatment plan of the CB. The study protocol has already been published27. The manuscript preparation followed the ‘Strengthening the reporting of cohort studies in surgery’ (STROCSS 2019, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D521) guidelines28. The study was approved by the ethics committee on 09/07/2020 (Ethikkomission Nordwest- und Zentralschweiz, Project-ID: 2020-01494) and registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04550156). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection

The following data were collected: patient data (demographic data, previous surgery, American Society of Anesthesiologists [ASA] score, comorbidities, laboratory results, procedure indication, and preoperative cancer stage [cTNM]), preoperative data (compliance to the CB), intraoperative data (procedure urgency, operative approach, anastomosis formed, intraoperative complications, and duration of surgery) and postoperative data (comprehensive complication index, postoperative intensive care admission, hospital stay, anastomotic leakage rate, 30 day mortality, and cancer stage [pTNM])29,30. To quantify operator experience, the average number of procedures performed annually was recorded for each surgeon, and surgeons were classified as senior if they were either board-certified in visceral surgery by the Swiss Society of Surgery or classified as a colorectal surgeon by the European Board of Surgical Qualification (ESBQ). For follow-up, patients were interviewed 30 days after the operation using a standardized questionnaire. Data was collected from the individual subject files and included in a secuTrial database. In order to guarantee the accuracy and completeness of the data entered into the database, an independent reviewer of a certified clinical trial unit compared the extracted files with the source documentation. This process ensured that all data was available in the individual subject file and had been accurately transferred to the study database. Monitoring was conducted at a minimum of three distinct time points throughout the course of the study in all participating centers.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was the Comprehensive Complication Index (CCI) after 30 days30. To evaluate the effect of the CB implementation on complication rate, the CCI before and after implementation of the CB was compared. Secondary outcomes were surgical site infections (SSI) and anastomotic leakage rates. Further risk factors for AL and higher CCI in colorectal resection were identified. The confounders considered for the primary outcome were age, sex, ASA score, indication, urgency, comorbidities, nutritional risk score (NRS), use of anticoagulants or immunosuppressive medication, BMI, previous surgery, insurance status, and participation in ERAS. The list of confounders was modified for the secondary outcomes in accordance with the optimal fitting models.

Sample size

According to the previous publication of the CCI by Slankamenac et al. and previously published outcomes from the participating centers in the medical statistics of Swiss hospitals, an average CCI of 30 was assumed before the introduction of the CB30,31. For the subsequent power calculation, a difference of 10 points on the CCI was targeted with a power of 90%. The power calculation indicated that 149 patients per arm would have been required. However, due to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic and a more successful recruitment process than anticipated, a greater number of patients were ultimately included.

Statistical analysis

Frequencies and percentages are presented for categorical variables, while median and interquartile ranges are presented for (non-normally distributed) continuous variables. The initial intention was to assess the influence of the CB on the CCI through the utilization of a linear mixed-effects model. A residual analysis demonstrated that 61% of patients included in the study exhibited a CCI value of zero ant that the CCI was not normally distributed. In light of these considerations, we conducted the analysis in the form of a hurdle model comprising two steps32. The initial step encompassed the entire patient cohort and entailed the assessment of the probability of experiencing any complication. In the second step, only patients who had experienced complications were included and the probability for severe complications was ascertained.

The occurrence of complications was analyzed in the first step using a mixed-effects logistic regression model. The initiation of the CB and prespecified confounders were modeled as fixed effects, and study center was modeled as a random effect33. For this analysis and for the mixed-effects logistic regression models used in the secondary analyses, CI were calculated using basic bootstrapping with 1000 replications.

In the second step, a linear quantile mixed model based on the asymmetric Laplace distribution was used with the initiation of the CB and prespecified confounders modeled as fixed effects and study center as a random effect34.

Contrary to our initial expectations, the level of compliance with the CB during the control phase was already high, with a median compliance of six out of nine items. To assess the true impact of the CB on the primary and secondary endpoints, an additional ‘per protocol’ analysis was conducted. Therefore, the aforementioned analysis was repeated using the number of items of the CB that were fulfilled instead of the CB phases. In sensitivity analyses, multiple imputation by chained equations was used to impute all missing values except for the outcome CCI. Nomograms were calculated to predict the occurrence of complications, SSI and AL. The corresponding logistic regression models were obtained by bidirectional stepwise variable selection using the Akaike information criterion (AIC)35. A calibration of the nomogram model for CCI >0 was conducted. All analyses were performed in R version 4.3.2 (2023-10-31 ucrt) (RCoreTeam, 2023).

Theory

The present study postulated that the implementation of a standardised approach, facilitated by the introduction of the CB, will result in a reduction in complications, SSI and AL rates in left-sided colorectal resection procedures within the participating hospitals.

Results

Participants and descriptive data

During the study period, 1141 patients were enrolled (723 (63.4%) during the control period (No CB group) and 418 (36.6%) during the CB period (CB group)). The No CB group included more patients due to the longer duration of the control period compared to the CB period. Table 1 provides a summary of the nine items that comprise the colorectal bundle, along with the compliance rates observed for each individual item in the No CB and CB phases. Median compliance with the CB was six out of nine items in the No CB group and seven out of nine items in the CB group. No adverse events have been observed as a result of the introduction of the CB. An overview of the patient and procedural characteristics is presented in Table 2. Patients in the No CB group had a higher number of emergency procedures, fewer teaching procedures, and more end-to-end anastomosis (Table 2). The study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic. Two hundred and one patients (17.6%) underwent surgery during COVID-19-related capacity reductions. SDC, Table 2 (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D520) shows patient and procedural characteristics stratified by whether they underwent surgery during COVID-19-related capacity reduction. During the capacity reduction period, the proportion of patients who had open surgery and the rate of ostomy creation were higher than in the absence of capacity reduction.

Outcome data

The median CCI after 30 days overall in both the No CB group and in the CB group was 0.0 (Table 2). The CCI distribution in the No CB group and CB group is shown in Figure 1. Of all the patients, 7.6% in the No CB group and 9.6% in the CB group experienced SSI. AL occurred in 5.7% of patients in the No CB group and in 5.1% of patients in the CB group (Table 2). A total of 31 patients (2.7%) were lost to follow-up due to unavailability. During COVID-19-related capacity reduction, patients who underwent surgery had an AL rate of 9.1% compared to 4.7% without capacity reduction (SDC, Table 2, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D520). Additionally, SSI occurred in 10% of patients during capacity reduction, compared to 8.0% without reduced capacity.

Primary analysis

In the first step of the hurdle model, the CB was not associated with the occurrence of any complication (Table 3). Several confounders were associated with any complication, such as age (odds ratio [OR] 1.02, 95% CI: 1.0–1.03), operations due to malignancies (OR 1.34, 95% CI: 1.01–1.92), and emergency procedures (OR 2.19, 95% CI: 1.31–3.4), higher NRS (OR 1.13, 95% CI: 1.01–1.24), and higher BMI (OR 1.04, 95% CI: 1.00–1.06).

The CB was also not associated with the severity of complications in the second step of the hurdle model analyzing the patients who experienced at least one complication. In procedures for malignancies, the median CCI was 8.7 points higher than for procedures for diverticulitis (95% CI: 4.23–13.1) (Table 3). Finally, the median CCI was 15.3 points higher when immunosuppressive medication was used (95% CI: 6.1–24.6) (Table 3).

In an additional ‘per protocol’ analysis, there was no association between the CB compliance and complications or their severity (Table 3). Nomograms with risk factor contributions for complications, SSI and AL. To use the nomograms, locate the patient’s predictor values on the corresponding axes and draw a straight line upwards to obtain the associated points. Then, add the points for all predictors, locate the patient’s score on the total points axis, and draw a straight line downwards to obtain the corresponding probability.

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses of the primary endpoint

Sensitivity analyses with multiple imputed data are shown in SDC, Table 3 (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D520). The results of the sensitivity analyses did not differ substantially from the main results. The primary analysis identified two risk factors for complications: operations due to malignancy and the use of immunosuppressive medication. To evaluate the impact of the CB in the presence of these risk factors, the primary and per protocol analyses were repeated only on patients who were operated for a malignancy or received immunosuppressive drugs (SDC, Table 4, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D520). The colorectal bundle was not associated with the occurrence or the severity of complications in this subgroup analysis. The primary and per-protocol analyses were repeated solely for centers that had a CCI >0 prior to the introduction of the CB. Two centers were included in this exploratory subgroup analysis. Due to the limited number of centers included, the center was included as a fixed effect. Furthermore, the first step of the hurdle model could only be calculated due to the small number of patients. This additional analysis demonstrated that the application of the CB was not associated with the occurrence of complications (SDC, Table 5, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D520). In a subgroup analysis comprising solely nonemergency patients, the CB was associated with a 12% reduced probability of complications in the per-protocol analysis (OR 0.88, 95% CI: 0.78–0.99). However, no association was observed between the median CCI and the application of the CB (SDC, Table 6, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/D520).

Analysis of AL

In the intention-to-treat analysis, the CB was not associated with AL (Table 4). However, in the ‘per protocol’ analysis, each additional CB item complied with was associated with 24% lower odds of AL (OR 0.76, 95% CI: 0.64–0.93). This decrease is relative; with the addition of each subsequent item of the CB, the relative odds for AL are decreased by 24%. Furthermore, a malignant diagnosis and procedures for other indications than diverticulitis were associated with higher odds of AL (Table 4). Discussion

This multicenter, prospective, observational trial showed a low median CCI of 0.0 before the introduction of the CB. As the network mainly comprises of small to medium-sized centers, this finding emphasizes that high-quality colorectal surgery is feasible in dedicated teams at smaller centers with a joint standard before implementation of the CB. This can serve as a quality improvement model for other healthcare systems. The low CCI of 0.0 prior to the introduction of the CB rendered it almost impossible to demonstrate a favorable impact of the CB on complication rates. However, complications were associated with risk factors like older age, surgery for malignancy, emergency surgery, high NRS, high BMI, and use of immunosuppressants. To investigate the impact of the CB in a selected cohort of patients with an elevated risk of complications, the primary analysis was conducted on a subset of patients who had undergone surgery for malignancy or were on immunossuppressive medication. The application of the CB did not result in a reduction in the incidence of complications in this high-risk subgroup. Patients with malignancies and procedures for other indications than diverticulitis were found to have an elevated risk for AL. Based on the real-world data presented, identifying, and quantifying the individual impact of these risk factors can help to estimate the probability of a complicated course, and the occurrence of SSI or AL, as shown in Figure 2. An additional ‘per protocol’ analysis analyzing the association of the number of adhered items with the different outcomes was performed because compliance with the CB was already high during the control period (median compliance of six out of nine items). In this additional ‘per protocol’ analysis, higher compliance with the CB was associated with lower rates of AL.

Few single-center observational trials have evaluated the effect of bundles on SSI rates in colorectal surgery. An observational trial conducted at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center assessed the efficacy of a multidisciplinary patient care bundle, comprising 13 items, in reducing SSI36. A decrease in SSI rates was observed after the implementation of the bundle. The implementation of another SSI prevention bundle, consisting of 10 items, in Thailand resulted in a decrease in SSI rates, particularly when the compliance rate was above 70%37. The implementation of a nine-item colorectal bundle in the University Hospital of Lausanne (CHUV) had no impact on the rate of SSI compared with a historical cohort38. The current trial did not find an effect of the CB on the rates of complications or SSI. This was likely due to the a priori very low complication rate introduction in the hospital network and already high adherence to the CB prior to its introduction. The concept of standardization inevitably gives rise to a dichotomy between a tailored approach that is predicated on the individual factors of each case and standardization programs like the mentioned bundles that can be universally applied. There are measures that are beneficial for all patients who undergo colorectal resections, regardless of their indication and patient factors, as demonstrated in the EAGLE trial39. The CB was designed to include such factors. However, it should be noted that such programs cannot be implemented in exceptional cases.

It must be assumed that the efficacy of individual items may differ between colon and rectal resections. The finished MOBILE randomized controlled trial found no additional benefit of the combination of mechanical and antibiotic bowel preparation prior to colonic resection in a cohort of mainly MIS colorectal surgery patients40. However, the MOBILE 2 trial demonstrated a reduced rate of complications and SSI following mechanical and antibiotic bowel preparation in rectal resections41.

The risk prediction for complications in general and SSI and AL, in particular, are becoming more relevant given the changes in demographics and increasing co-morbidity of colorectal surgery patients in an ageing population. Tailoring indication for surgery, surgical approach, and mode of resection (e.g. with or without ostomy formation) to an individual patient risk profile would offer improved outcomes for this vulnerable patient group. While our study failed to show a general effect on initially very low overall complication rates, our data offers a precise tool for risk prediction. Only a few risk calculators have been developed so far. The RALAR score, for example, was developed based on a study cohort of 5398 patients from 24 Italian referral centers. The tool calculates the risk of AL based on several factors, like sex, BMI, and weight loss. The development of the RALAR score was limited due to the use of retrospective register-based data, a relatively long accrual period, and missing data. Additionally, the RALAR score has never been validated42. The PROCOLE prognostic index is another tool for predicting AL in patients with colorectal cancer. It was based on a systematic review with meta-analysis and consists of 27 parameters. The index was validated with retrospective cases. It achieved a sensitivity and specificity of 73%. However, the systematic review included also retrospective observational trials in order to maximize the number of cases. Limited data quality was accepted for this purpose. Furthermore, validation was only performed retrospectively43. The present study includes prospectively collected data from hospitals of varying sizes, representing an entire healthcare region with wide generalizability. High data quality and good generalizability can be assumed. Translating other scores to different clinical settings has proved challenging in the past43.

Another approach to predict adverse outcomes in colorectal surgery, is artificial intelligence and machine learning44. Merath et al. demonstrated the use of machine learning algorithms to accurately predict the probability of postoperative complications in colorectal surgery45. A predictive model for AL was developed at University Hospital Basel using machine learning techniques on a small population of patients. This model is not yet implemented in clinical practice, and a trial phase is currently underway44.

Strengths and limitations

The study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic. The cohort of patients who underwent surgery during COVID-related capacity reduction and those without reduction were comparable and thus included in the final analysis. However, the study outcome may be influenced by unknown effects of the COVID-19 pandemic. Randomization based on individual patients would have been biased by strong center effects and randomizing centers was not feasible given the limited number of included hospitals. Additionally, we would have excluded patients from centers randomized to standard care from the potential benefit of the bundle which was deemed to be unethical. That is why we decided to conduct a nonrandomized prospective cohort study by implementing a standardized CB across an entire hospital or unit.

The main limitation of the study is the incorrect assumption of a CCI of 30 prior to the implementation of the CB. This assumption was based on a study by Slankamenac et al., which reported a mean CCI of 30 in a cohort of 1200 patients from a Swiss University Hospital and medical statistics of Swiss Hospitals30,31. The low median CCI of 0.0 prior to the implementation of the CB made it very difficult to demonstrate the effect of the CB on complication rates. The inability of polymorbid and critically ill patients to consent to participate, coupled with the introduction of several quality improvement measures in the participating hospitals during the interim period, may have contributed to a lower-than-expected CCI within the study cohort. In a cohort with a higher median CCI, the CB may have had a beneficial impact. The subgroup analysis conducted at two centers with an CCI >0 did not reveal any beneficial effects of the CB. However, it should be noted that this analysis included only a limited number of patients and is therefore constrained by its exploratory nature. During data analysis, it was discovered that reporting the mean CCI values was not feasible due to the non-normal distribution of the data. As a result, the median CCI was calculated, and the hurdle model was selected for analysis. The CCI has been developed for use as an outcome measurement tool in surgical research, although its application has yet to be standardized46,47. Some studies only report on patients who experience complications and compare those groups rather than the entire cohort48. Others report mean CCI values instead of median scores. The approach of reporting the CCI with a two-step hurdle model approach offers a method to analyze the effects on overall complication rates as well as the severity of complications within those patients that experience them.

Despite this limitations, the study did demonstrate that standardization can be readily enhanced through the implementation of standardised protocols, such as the CB. The study population is substantial and representative, comprising a range of hospitals, from smaller community hospitals to tertiary referral centers. The participating hospitals represent the entirety of the healthcare region, which has a population of 1.5 million. The inclusion of a diverse range of hospital types across an entire healthcare region ensures that the results are highly generalizable. The study was prospective, with data monitored regularly by an external reviewer, resulting in the maintenance of high data quality and granularity. Furthermore, the generation of predictive models for the CCI, SSI, and AL enabled the construction of a user-friendly tool comprising commonly available variables that can be readily integrated into clinical practice to estimate the risk for these complications and overall complication load for a given patient.

Conclusions

Dedicated teams can establish high-quality colorectal services in a network of hospitals with a joint standard. The study provides a framework for future initiatives and can serve as a model for other healthcare settings to conduct and implement quality improvement studies. The introduction of the CB can reduce the occurrence of AL.
