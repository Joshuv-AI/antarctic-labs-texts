A comprehensive comparison of primary hyperventilation syndrome versus hyperventilation syndrome secondary to respiratory infections
Authors: Liuniu Xiao, Fen Guo, Xiao Ran, Shusheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe primary hyperventilation syndrome (HVS) is characterized by somatic/psychological symptoms, which may be related to abnormalities of ventilatory control, accompanied by sustained hypocapnia and respiratory alkalosis. The purpose of this study is to compare primary HVS with HVS secondary to respiratory infections.MethodA total of 167 patients diagnosed with HVS from January 1st, 2024, to May 31st, 2025, were included in this research. Participants were grouped according to positive or negative respiratory infection status. Demographic characteristics, laboratory examinations, and clinical outcomes were retrospectively collected and compared between the two groups.ResultsIn total, seventy-six HVS patients were secondary to respiratory infections, while the remaining 91 patients were primary. Primary HVS patients were mainly induced by psychological factors, since 85% felt tense or anxious before. There was a significantly higher Nijmegen Questionnaire score in patients with HVS secondary to respiratory infections. Besides, this group presented a significantly lower level of electrolytes, as well as a higher level of inflammatory condition on admission. The six-month recurrence rate also showed significantly higher for those in the secondary group.ConclusionHVS patients secondary to respiratory infections presented more severe hyperventilation syndromes and worse clinical outcomes. Appropriate managements specific to infection prevention, as well as respiratory training methods, could be provided to impede the recurrence of hyperventilation syndrome.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Hyperventilation syndrome, Respiratory infections, Nijmegen questionnaire, Prognosis

Introduction

Hyperventilation syndrome (HVS) is a complex disorder that can be conveniently defined physiologically as inappropriate breathing in excess of metabolic demand [1], leading to a set of respiratory and extra-respiratory syndromes [2]. These symptoms, varied and nonspecific, can be mostly caused by ventilatory hyperventilation-induced sympathetic nerve [3]. Based on different periods, there are two main clinical hyperventilation syndromes: acute episodic hyperventilation and chronic hyperventilation. The former often undergoes a sudden “panic attack” or respiratory stress, while the latter usually occurs in subjects accompanied by chronic psychological issues [1].

There is no golden standard for the HVS diagnosis, but it is surprisingly common [4]. The Nijmegen Questionnaire (NQ) score was introduced as a fast screening tool to detect HVS over 30 years ago. It incorporates a 16-item symptom checklist categorized into three factors, and has been validated by a general population that includes individuals with a clinical diagnosis of HVS [5]. It is widely used in different countries and translated into several languages, including Chinese, Korean, Greek, and so on. NQ score > 23 is defined as a classification for positive HVS; however, it is not diagnostic for a specific syndrome [5]. There are various methods used to diagnose HVS, including the arterial blood gas (ABG) test, hyperventilation provocation test (HVPT), and capnography. These measurements are costly or invasive procedures, which are not appropriate for HVS screening [6].

Respiratory disorders have been reported to be associated with HVS occurrence, such as asthma and long coronavirus disease 2019 (long COVID-19), etc [3, 7]. Dysfunctional respiratory syndromes could occur secondary to respiratory infections. Dysbalanced muscular efforts with increased thoracic muscular and decreased diaphragmatic movement could lead to insufficient ventilation; patients may present with inspiratory dyspnea accompanied by increased breathing rates [8]. In addition to respiratory disorders, psychosocial stress and other triggers also play an important role in the primary HVS. For example, HVS is a frequent satellite emotion presentation of generalized anxiety disorders, panic disorders, depression, phobias, and posttraumatic stress disorders [9]. Emotional stress could induce an excessive respiratory response to promote the development of HVS, which is likely rooted in the evolutionary “fight or flight” reaction [4].

Since HVS can be caused by different reasons, including organic or functional issues, clinical characteristics, laboratory examinations, and prognosis should be carefully compared. There have been few literature reports on this aspect, since a majority of symptoms of HVS can be improved through breathing regulation. However, patients secondary to respiratory infections often present a higher level of inflammatory reaction, and whether there is a correlation between respiratory infections and HVS symptoms remains unclear. This study aims to compare patients with HVS secondary to respiratory infections with those without, based on clinical characteristics and prognosis.

Materials and methods

Study design

Since HVS patients accounted for only 5%-8% of the total patient volume in our hospital’s emergency department, an odds ratio of 2 was set for the association between HVS and respiratory infections, with a significance level (α) of 0.05 (two-sided) and a statistical power (1‑β) of 80%. Based on these assumptions, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 150 participants (case group, n = 75; control group, n = 75).

This study included 167participants with obvious HVS symptoms, who were divided into two groups according to primary HVS or HVS secondary to respiratory infections. The diagnosis of respiratory infections relied primarily on clinical symptoms, supplemented by throat swab nucleic acid testing or serum antibody detection. For patients with elevated inflammatory markers, such as C-reactive protein (CRP) ≥ 10 mg/L, radiological examinations were performed, including chest computed tomography (CT) or radiograph. A total of 57 men and 110 women, aged 16 to 75 years, were retrospectively collected from January 1st, 2024, to May 31th, 2025. Clinical characteristics and laboratory examinations were detailed, classified, and compared between these two groups. Primary outcomes for this study included emergency observation time and related treatment costs. Admission basic vital signs, including respiratory rate (RR), heart rate (HR), mean arterial pressure (MAP), and blood oxygen saturation (SaO2), were also compared between. Breath-holding time (BHT), a non-specific indicator representing the severity of hyperventilation, was conducted and measured on admission [3]. In order to guarantee the standardization of BHT, all operators were required to perform based on breath holding protocol. Firstly, BHT patients, in a sitting position, were asked to inhale to tidal volume. At the end of this calm exhalation, they were instructed to pinch their nose and hold their breath until the first involuntary movement of the respiratory muscles occurred. This has been described as the physiological breaking point of breath-holding. The BHT measured at the first involuntary respiratory muscle movements (BHT-IRM) was reproducible and physiologically stable when measured against the urge to breathe. Secondly, operators should encourage the participants to hold the control pause until involuntary respiratory muscle movements, and both methods for assessing BHT were evaluated: breath holding continued until the first difficulty or first desire to breathe, and until the first involuntary motion of the respiratory muscles [10]. After three repetitions for the BTH-IRM measurement, the mean BTH-IRM was recorded in seconds [3]. During emergency observation, patients would receive systematic examination and symptomatic treatments, including fluid infusion, anti-infection therapies, electrolyte replacements, breathing retraining, etc. Secondary outcomes encompassed HVS recurrence in 6 months. The 6-month recurrence was selected because it represented a clinically meaningful period to evaluate medium-term treatment efficacy and disease stability in our hospital. Moreover, a recurrence of HVS within 6 months might suggest a significant impact on patients’ quality of life. As this was a retrospective study, written informed consent was not required.

Study population

All participants presented with a dominant high costal breathing pattern at rest, with at least five out of 11 hyperventilation syndromes, such as difficult inspiratory breathing (dyspnea), chest tightness, chest pain, palpitation, blurred vision, dizzy spells, bloated feeling of stomach, tingling fingers, stiff fingers or arms, limb numbness, and mouth tightness [11]. In addition to obvious symptoms, we retrained the diagnosis of HVS for all patients by an NQ score ≥ 23, with pH > 7.45 and partial pressure of arterial carbon dioxide (PaCO2) < 35 mmHg on ABG analysis.

HVS patients secondary to respiratory infections typically exhibited some of the following syndromes, including fever, cough, expectoration, nasal obstruction, runny nose, sneeze, pharyngalgia, and even pneumonia.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2. Qualitative variables were expressed as numbers (percentages) and analyzed by Chi-square (two-sided). Quantitative variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and checked by Student’s T-test (paired or unpaired). The threshold for statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Variables with P < 0.2 in univariate analysis were selected to be included in multivariate analysis.

Results

Demographic characteristics for HVS participants

From January 1st, 2024, to May 31st, 2025, a total of 167 consecutive outpatients underwent HVS, with more or less somatic or psychological syndromes. All participants presented with an average NQ score of 29, pH = 7.53, and PaCO2 = 24 mmHg, meeting the criteria for HVS diagnosis. Based on respiratory infections or not, there were seventy-six (45.51%) patients included into secondary group, and the remaining 91 (54.49%) assigned to the primary group. Participants in two groups presented a similar age and gender distribution. The main hyperventilation syndromes presenting in this study were dyspnea, chest tightness, palpitation, limb numbness, and mouth tightness. Besides, participants in infections group showed a significantly higher proportion of abdominal discomfort (39.47% vs. 16.48%, P < 0.001), with fewer finger tremors (36.84% vs. 53.85%, P = 0.028). A majority of patients with primary hyperventilation syndromes were often accompanied by psychological problems. There were significantly more participants feeling tension or anxiety before HVS in this group (84.62% vs. 18.42%, P < 0.001). All of the above, the NQ score, correlated with the severity of hyperventilation, showed an obvious increase in infections group (32.57 ± 0.367 vs. 25.97 ± 0.215, P < 0.001). Multivariate analysis indicated that feeling of tension or anxiety (P = 0.018) and NQ score (P < 0.001) were independent predictors in distinguishing between the primary and secondary forms of HVS. Admission basic vital signs for HVS participants

We compared the admission basic vital signs between the two groups, including RR, HR, MAP, and SaO2. HVS patients secondary to respiratory infections displayed a significantly higher RR (34.54 ± 0.404 vs. 25.26 ± 0.474, P < 0.001), as well as HR (105.0 ± 1.699 vs. 85.34 ± 1.355, P < 0.001). However, no significant differences were observed in SaO2 (97.78 ± 0.212 vs. 97.70 ± 0.388, P = 0.783) and MAP (92.47 ± 0.726 vs. 91.76 ± 0.650, P = 0.304) between the two groups, regardless of respiratory infections. Besides, BHT was significantly decreased in the respiratory infection group (8.276 ± 0.085 vs. 12.04 ± 0.215, P < 0.001). Detailed data are presented in Table 3.

Laboratory examinations for HVS participants

Laboratory examinations, including ABG analysis, blood routine test, electrolyte levels, and glucose on admission, were collected and compared between the two groups. ABG analysis showed comparable results, with similar high levels of pH, partial pressure of arterial oxygen (PaO2), and low levels of PaCO2, HCO3− (all P > 0.05). For electrolyte aspects, although the average levels of serum sodium, potassium, and calcium were within a normal range for all participants, the HVS patients secondary to respiratory infections group showed obviously lower levels compared to the primary group (all P < 0.05, Fig. 2). Lastly, there was no doubt that inflammatory indicators were significantly higher for respiratory infection patients, with higher levels of leukocyte (P < 0.001), neutrophil (P < 0.001), C-Reaction Protein (CRP) (P < 0.001), and lower levels of lymphocyte (P < 0.001), as shown in Table 4.

Clinical outcomes for HVS participants

Primary outcomes included emergency observation time and related treatment costs. Discussion

Dysfunctional breathing (DB) is a collective term that encompasses a range of breathing disorders characterized by chronic alterations in normal breathing patterns, which cannot be attributed to a specific medical diagnosis, leading to symptoms such as dyspnoea, chest pain, tightness, and so on, in the absence of underlying organic respiratory diseases [12–14]. A classification system for the common DB patterns includes the following five categories: (1) Hyperventilation syndrome, associated with symptoms that are both indicative of respiratory alkalosis with or without hypocapnia. (2) Periodic deep sighing, frequent sighing accompanied by an irregular periodic breathing pattern. (3) Thoracic dominant breathing manifests frequently with somatic disorders and is also classified as dysfunctional breathing, resulting in dyspnoea in the absence of identifiable pathology. (4) Forced abdominal expiration, inappropriate and excessive contraction of abdominal muscles during normal expiration. (5) Thoraco-abdominal asynchrony, a temporal mismatch between rib cage and abdominal contraction, resulting in reduced breathing efficiency [15]. The most common form of DB is HVS, with a prevalence of 6%–10% in the general population [12], which was first introduced in 1937 [11]. HVS is a condition triggered by psychological factors or abnormal regulation of the respiratory center, resulting in excessive ventilation, causing respiratory and extra-respiratory symptoms. It is primarily mediated by the sympathetic nervous system in the absence of any underlying organic lesion [16].

The diagnosis of HVS is a controversial topic; it was initially thought that symptoms resulted from hypocapnia caused by over-breathing, and reduced PaCO2 was regarded as a necessary diagnostic criterion [12, 17]. The NQ score is the most widely used screening tool for hyperventilation. This questionnaire contains a 16-item symptom list, grouped into three distinct factors, and has undergone rigorous validation processes in the general population with a clinical diagnosis of HVS [3]. The NQ score has been translated into different language versions following cross-cultural adaptation guidelines, such as Thai, Arabic, Greek, Farsi, Finnish, Norwegian, Chinese, etc. The validity and reliability of the questionnaire have been carefully checked to guarantee the consistency between different versions. A discriminable cut-off was chosen for HVS screening to obtain a reasonable sensitivity and specificity [6, 18]. Previous studies suggest that a total NQ score of 23 or above is positive for HVS diagnosis, but no validity tests have been performed since the absence of an accepted gold standard [3]. Acute hyperventilation is often associated with a feeling of fear or panic attacks, accompanied by a low arterial or end-tidal CO2 level [1]. Besides, hyperventilation provocation test (HVPT) and cardio-pulmonary exercise test (CPET) are considered classical methods to further confirm the HVS diagnosis [1]. However, these tests cannot be applied routinely in multiple hospitals, including ours. So we chose the NQ score and ABG analysis as indicators for HVS diagnosis.

The relationship between respiratory diseases and HVS remains ambiguous. Previous studies have reported HVS in asthmatic patients, which suggests that a lack of effective control or progressive deterioration of asthma symptoms is an independent predictor for HVS incidence [13]. Firstly, asthmatic patients with acute attacks underwent psychological oral breathing, accompanied by a significantly increased incidence of an elevated respiratory rate, resulting in an abnormal breathing pattern with low PaCO2 increasing. Secondly, the major asthma co-morbidities, such as anxiety disorders, especially panic attacks or specific phobias of blood, injections, or injuries, also promote the symptom of shortness of breath, which in turn increases the risk of hyperventilation [13]. Lastly, a number of asthmatic patients are often accompanied by several allergic diseases, such as food allergies, perennial allergic rhinitis, etc. When allergic symptoms flare up, individuals often experience discomfort in the larynx, which compels them to an increased oral breathing involuntarily, as a result, promoting the HVS incidence [2]. Another respiratory disorder that frequently gives rise to HVS is COVID-19. Recent studies have reported a high incidence of HVS after COVID-19. Jeremie Taverne et al. tracked a clinical evaluation for 147 outpatients after COVID-19 infection, and found that 7% complained of persistent dyspnea for three months, with a stereotypical symptomatology suggesting a diagnosis of HVS [19]. Klaus J. Wirth et al. also attempted to understand the cause of dyspnea in post-COVID symptom (PCS) patients with mild infection. The results showed that all PCS presented an occurrence of hyperventilation during the cardiopulmonary exercise test [20]. Edem Allado et al. tried to explore whether there was a relationship between HVS and history of acute severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection, and found an increased occurrence of HVS in cases of COVID-19 compared to COVID-19-free patients [7]. Furthermore, Léon Genecand et al. discovered dysfunctional breathing symptoms in patients with long COVID-19, and reported hyperventilation symptoms were diagnosed in 20.8% of patients, which brought an adverse functional impact on their quality of life [21]. However, there has been less previous evidence for the relationship between respiratory infections and HVS incidence. Besides, whether hyperventilation syndromes would be deteriorated with respiratory infections remains to be explored further. We therefore retrospectively collected related medical records and investigated the possible association.

Symptoms commonly described by our patients with HVS include dyspnea, chest tightness, chest pain, palpitation, blurred vision, dizzy spells, abdominal discomfort, finger tremors, stiff fingers or arms, limb numbness, and mouth tightness. Among all of the above symptoms, the top five were dyspnea, chest tightness, palpitation, limb numbness, and mouth tightness. Hyperventilation is frequently associated with increased minute ventilation that exceeds metabolic demands, which brings about a decrease in PaCO2, as well as an increase in PaO2, resulting in respiratory alkalosis [12] and obvious dyspnea, chest tightness, and palpitation. Furthermore, an acute rise in pH can deteriorate the internal environment disorders, leading to ionized calcium binding to albumin and causing hypocalcemia, and an intracellular shift of potassium, causing hypokalemia [22, 23], then limb numbness and mouth tightness appear in turn.

In our research, major symptoms of HVS were comparable between the two groups, while HVS participants secondary to respiratory infections presented with a significantly higher proportion of abdominal discomfort and a lower incidence of finger tremors. Firstly, all HVS participants presented similar levels of pH and HCO3−, representing comparable acid-base balance. Secondly, although PaO2 was increased and PaCO2 was decreased in all HVS patients, there was a similar homeostasis regardless of respiratory infections or not. Third, although the levels of electrolytes, such as sodium, potassium, and calcium, were significantly lower in infections group, they were all within a normal physiological range for both groups. So, symptoms (dyspnea, chest tightness, and palpitation) associated with respiratory alkalosis and electrolyte disturbance were consistently observed between the two groups. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies. Previous literature has indicated that hyperventilation primarily induces respiratory alkalosis through increasing minute ventilation, leading to decreased PaCO2 and elevated pH [24]. In infected states, such as urinary tract infections with suspected sepsis, blood gas changes might encompass metabolic components; however, in hyperventilation-dominant cases, respiratory components predominate [25]. The similarity in blood gas parameters is likely attributed to the overriding effect of hyperventilation on respiratory control mechanisms, which sustains the alkalotic state despite potential infectious stressors.

Besides, abdominal discomfort was significantly higher for patients with respiratory infections. The reason might be attributed to the infection-related concomitant symptoms. In this study, approximately 30% of infected patients were accompanied by fever, which could stimulate the sympathetic nerve system, thereby accelerating body metabolism and suppressing gastrointestinal motility. Concurrently, it could impair the function of digestive glands, leading to reduced secretion of key digestive agents, including gastric acid and pepsin [26]. Lastly, it was interesting to note that finger tremors were significantly higher in HVS participants without respiratory infections, which might be associated with their remarkable psychological and emotional issues. Specifically, 85% HVS patients without infections experienced unbearable tension or anxiety before the onset of disease. The core mechanism induced by tension or anxiety lies in neural and hormonal changes triggered by the body’s stress response, especially the “fight‑or‑flight” response [27]. Under this condition, sympathetic nerve activation, as well as an increase in adrenaline secretion, plays important roles in accelerating the neurotransmitter transmission, leading to involuntary and rapid contraction and relaxation of hand muscles, especially the small muscles that control finger movements precisely, manifesting as finger tremors.

Another important finding in our research was that the NQ score was an independent predictor to distinguish the primary and secondary forms of HVS, which suggests more severe hyperventilation syndromes in the infections group. This finding prompted an examination within the context of existing literature on HVS. While prior studies have extensively characterized HVS as a disorder often linked to anxiety and presenting with a wide array of non-specific symptoms, direct comparative analysis of NQ score—a potential marker of ventilatory drive or symptom burden—between patients with or without concurrent respiratory infections specifically has not been explicitly detailed in the existing literature [28]. Recent evidence suggests that respiratory infections might exacerbate dysfunctional breathing symptoms through various mechanisms [21], such as post-infectious inflammatory response, immune activation, and psychological stress. For one aspect, the respiratory infection itself can lead to symptoms, including shortness of breath, chest tightness, dyspnea, etc., which overlap highly with core items assessing HVS in NQ scores. Post-infectious inflammatory response of airways makes these physiological sensations more real, intense, and persistent, resulting in a higher NQ score. For another aspect, infections induce congestion and edema of airways with increased phlegm, bringing about narrowed airways and sensations of obstruction, and activating the immune system. To overcome breathing resistance and clear secretions, patients may unconsciously adopt oral breathing and frequent deep inhalations. These behaviors are also key scoring items in the NQ score and represent exacerbated hyperventilation. Besides, diagnostic uncertainty about the severity of infections can aggravate patients’ anxiety by increasing their fear and stress levels. Physiological anxiety also plays an important role in the development of hyperventilation, resulting in a higher NQ score.

The result of this study indicated that HVS patients with respiratory infections presented significantly lower levels of serum potassium, sodium, and calcium, although various electrolytes remained within normal physiological ranges, which suggests that the presence of infections may add further metabolic complexity to HVS patients. The observed reductions in electrolyte levels may be attributed to multiple factors. Firstly, respiratory infections can induce metabolic alterations and systemic inflammatory responses that disrupt homeostasis, exacerbating hyperventilation-associated respiratory alkalosis, driving intracellular potassium shifts, and reducing ionized calcium [20, 29]. Moreover, the presence of infections could amplify sympathetic nervous system activity, further driving electrolyte losses via gastrointestinal absorption dysfunction and renal excretion [30]. This is consistent with previous findings that airway inflammations in infectious patients could prolong respiratory symptoms, which might indirectly affect electrolyte balance [7].

Another important finding in our study was that BHT was significantly lower in the respiratory infection group. First of all, HVS patients with respiratory infections presented with infection-induced physiological stresses; there is no doubt that RR and HR were accordingly higher. Additionally, the heightened inflammatory responses and increased metabolic demands brought by infections can shorten the breath-hold breakpoint by enhancing the sensitivity of peripheral and central chemoreceptors to CO2 and reducing tolerance to hypoxia [31]. Furthermore, respiratory infections can partially impair diaphragmatic function, restricting oxygen utilization and further compromising the breath-hold capacity. This finding is in line with previous studies indicating that reduced BTH is correlated with elevated NQ scores, proving worse symptoms in HVS with respiratory infections [3].

When HVS patients came to our emergency department, respiratory training therapies were performed to control their uncomfortable symptoms [32, 33]. Two common breathing techniques used in the training therapies were Papworth and Buteyko methods [15]. For the Papworth method, patients were taught abdominal breathing, with an emphasis on controlled, slow nasal breathing. The Buteyko method involved nasal breathing and gradually increasing controlled breath-holding pauses in order to reduce hyperventilation over time. For HVS patients with respiratory infections, there was a significantly higher inflammatory response condition, such as a higher percentage of neutrophils and a lower percentage of lymphocytes, as well as elevated CRP levels. Except for controlling HVS symptoms, we must also effectively manage symptoms caused by the underlying infections to ensure comprehensive treatments for patients. This explains why the emergency observation time and treatment costs were significantly higher in this group.

It was interesting to note that there was a significantly higher 6-month HVS recurrence in HVS patients with respiratory infections, which was also a highlight for our study, as there have been no previous reports in the relevant literature. The disparity may be attributed to the synergistic effects of infection-induced airway inflammation and lung injury-associated hyperventilation [34]. This association is supported by preclinical models in which hyperventilation exacerbates inflammatory responses and perivascular edema [34]. Furthermore, seasonal variations, climatic conditions (such as cold, dry air), geographic locations, and air qualities are all environmental factors that are likely to contribute to an increased risk of respiratory illnesses by making individuals more susceptible to respiratory infections, as well as amplifying airway sensitivity [16]. Consequently, these external elements may indirectly drive the noted rise in hyperventilation episodes by fostering repeated or persistent respiratory challenges.

There were several limitations for the present study. First of all, the study adopted a retrospective single-center design, which may lead to selection bias and thus limit the generalizability of the conclusions. Secondly, the diagnosis of HVS relied on clinical symptoms, NQ scores, and ABG analysis. Due to limitations in our hospital, further identification tests, for example, cardiopulmonary exercise testing, hyperventilation provocation test, etc., could not be routinely performed [35]. Third, primary outcomes of this study, such as emergency observation time and treatment costs, might have limited clinical significance. However, since all participants in our research had mild symptoms, they did not require hospitalization, ventilatory support, or dynamic monitoring of functional status. As a result, more meaningful outcomes could not be further assessed in our study.

Conclusion

In conclusion, HVS patients secondary to respiratory infections present more severe hyperventilation syndromes, accompanied by a higher inflammatory condition and treatment costs. Appropriate managements specific to infection prevention, as well as respiratory training methods, could be provided to impede the recurrence of hyperventilation syndrome.
