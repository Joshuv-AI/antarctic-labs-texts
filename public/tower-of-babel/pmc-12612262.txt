Majusculamide-o, a simplified marine natural product analog, exhibits potent and specific cancer cell cytotoxicity
Authors: Niklas Alexander Wahl, Naiara Lebrón-Acosta, Michelle Pérez-Cuevas, Angel Hernandez-Mejias, Dmitry Leshchiner, Frederick A Valeriote, Eduardo J E Caro-Diaz, Sachi Horibata
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetastatic solid tumors (e.g., ovary, pancreas, liver, lung, and brain) contribute to a high mortality rate in cancer patients with few therapeutically effective anticancer drugs available for their treatment, highlighting the need to develop agents that target solid tumors. Natural products (NPs) derived from cyanobacteria possess potent anticancer activity, yet most are hard to synthesize and modify to improve therapeutic efficacy. Here, we have efficiently synthesized a simplified analog of the marine NP majusculamide D, majusculamide o (maj-o, 1), that has remarkable potency and selective cytotoxicity towards various metastatic cancer cells. We found that maj-o (1) treatment presents potent cytotoxicity in various cancer cell lines of the ovary (OVCAR3), pancreas (PANC1), brain (U251N), and lung (H125) in a dose-dependent manner, with the least cytotoxic effect towards non-metastatic or primary tumors of the liver (HEPG2) and ovary (OVCAR8). 1 significantly alters gene expression signatures with more treatment time and affects pathways associated with PI3K-Akt signaling and the Hippo pathway. Our finding suggests that maj-o has great potential to serve as a lead compound for the development of novel antineoplastics for solid tumors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23480-3.Keywords: Natural compounds, Cancers, Anti-cancer drugsSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Cancer, Drug discovery

Introduction

Cancer is the leading cause of death for those under 65 because of metastasis.1 Metastatic solid tumors that affect the ovary, pancreas, liver, lung, and brain have a dismal prognosis even when regionally constrained. The 5-year survival rates (in brackets) for individuals with these cancers are as follows: lung (25%), ovary (51%), brain (34%), liver (22%) and pancreas (13%).2 In most cases, there is a lack of effective drugs with only one or two choices, and these often offer an increase in overall survival of only a few months. As an example, for pancreatic cancer, the prime component for curative therapy is surgery, which is then combined with radiochemotherapy. Even with the most liberal selection of blood vessel-associated tumors and pre- or post-surgery/radio-chemotherapy, the results are dismal with a 5-year survival rate of ~ 27%, and ~ 70% of patients having disease recurrence.3,4 For metastatic disease, the median survival is less than 2 years.5 For over 30 years, 5-fluorouracil has been the mainstay of the chemotherapy component, with the more recent addition of gemcitabine as well as 5-fluorouracil combinations with folinic acid, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin. Viral vector-mediated and nanocarrier-type methods have recently emerged as a strategy to circumvent the lack of efficacy of nucleic acid-based therapies.6 Even so, these modern delivery approaches suffer from many disadvantages, including undesired side effects (e.g., trigger immune responses, inflammatory reactions) and complex production methods, all of which limit their clinical applicability.7 Newer, molecularly directed discovery programs, except those targeting lung cancer, have yet to deliver new drugs for these kinds of tumors.

Following the underwhelming results of combinatorial chemistry programs in both industry and academia,8,9 natural product (NP) drug discovery has experienced a renaissance.10 This has been fueled by new approaches to NP discovery, including tools in genomics, faster and more accurate dereplication using metabolomics, and more robust and concise strategies to produce NPs either by biosynthetic, semi-synthetic, or fully synthetic procedures.11 Specifically, marine NPs have achieved higher representation in our chemotherapeutic drugs, with seventeen approved agents sourced from the sea.12,13 Among these, twelve (71%) are indicated for antineoplastic treatment, and four (24%) included a compound derived from marine cyanobacteria, monomethylauristatin E (MMAE).14,15 Hence, drug discovery efforts from marine cyanobacteria continue to be productive in identifying preclinical anticancer agents. These organisms possess extraordinarily rich and varied secondary metabolomes quite distinct from terrestrial organisms and have proven to have potential as cancer drug leads that include the curacins, carmaphycins, apratoxins, and cryptophycins.16–21 Among these cyanobacterial compounds, lipopentapeptides, such as the microcolins and majusculamides (Fig. 1),22–26 stand out in terms of structures that remain underexplored regarding their development as lead compounds.

Structures of the marine-derived lipopentapeptides majusculamide D (maj-D) and the microcolins.

NPs derived from cyanobacteria, such as majusculamide D, possess potent and selective anticancer activity. However, most exhibit poor drug-likeness in terms of pharmacokinetics requiring modifications to improve therapeutic efficacy, a process that is often synthetically laborious. Despite these challenges, we previously reported the total synthesis of majusculamide D (maj-D) originally isolated from the cyanobacteria Lyngbya majuscula (re-isolated from moorena sp.), both establishing the absolute configuration of its 2,4-dimethyloctanamide side chain and promising selective cancer cell cytotoxicity (Fig. 2A).27 Zhao et al. have also reported similar results and have pursued structure–activity-relationship (SAR) studies.28 Their studies are consistent with SAR previously reported for the microcolins29,30 and demonstrated the therapeutic effects of maj-D and several analogs in vivo (Fig. 2B).31 To further the biological investigation of this class of lipopentapeptides, we have more efficiently synthesized a simplified analog of maj-D, majusculamide-o (maj-o, Fig. 2C). Based on initial results where maj-D was found to reduce tumor size in vivo, maj-o will likely prove to have a similar antitumor profile and have potential therapeutic relevance in discovering new effective analogs to treat solid tumors. Therefore, we have performed a comprehensive biological analysis to investigate its cancer cell cytotoxicity in OVCAR3 (metastatic ovarian cancer isolated from ascites),32 OVCAR8 (primary ovarian cancer),33 H125 (adenosquamous carcinoma of the lung),34 U251N (glioblastoma),35,36 HEPG2 (hepatoblastoma)37, and PANC-1 (pancreatic carcinoma of ductal cell origin).38.

Results

Synthesis of majusculamide o (maj-o)

Based on the synthetic approach described by Caro-Diaz, Valeriote and Gerwick,27 we sought to improve upon the overall synthetic scheme by increasing yields of peptide and amide coupling reactions while reducing lengthy column purifications whenever possible. For instance, DEBPT coupling reactions39,40 were originally employed to avoid α-epimerization, especially when activating Boc-N-Me amino acids41; however, this reagent can produce side products that require extensive chromatography for their removal. Furthermore, we experienced moderate to low yields and a lack of reproducibility when using DEPBT. Thus, we explored the use of a greener coupling reagent, COMU,42 to alleviate these concerns and began the synthesis of maj-o from commercially available Boc-N-Me-L-valine to synthesize dipeptide 3 using previously reported synthetic procedures27 (Scheme 1). Removal of the Boc protecting group using an excess of 4N HCl in dioxane, followed by in situ COMU activation of tyrosine derivative 4 in the presence of diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA) and the addition of the resulting dipeptide HCl salt, smoothly provided tripeptide 5 in 55% yield over 2 steps. Chemical characterization of tripeptide 5 after purification was indistinguishable by 1H-NMR from the material produced using DEBPT (Fig. S4). With tripeptide 5 in hand, we hypothesized that coupling octanoic acid to the N-terminus before the pyrrolyl proline fragment 7 would be advantageous for several reasons. First, this sequence would not have intermediates with Boc protecting groups in the final step, allowing for a strong acidic workup to remove coupling reagent side-products. Second, adding lipophilicity earlier in the sequence would improve solubility in a wider range of organic solvents, aiding in both reactions and purification. Lastly, reducing the complexity of the lipophilic side chain would now eliminate the need for any stereospecific transformations. Thus, we quantitatively removed the Boc protecting group of tripeptide 5 under strong acid conditions and performed a HATU coupling with commercially available octanoic acid. Following acidic work-up, we found lipotripeptide 6 to be pure enough to be carried forward without further purification and 6 was quantitatively debenzylated under standard Pd-catalyzed hydrogenation conditions. Subsequently, the resulting debenzylated material was coupled to pyrrolyl proline HCl salt 7 (prepared as previously reported by Caro-Diaz et. al.27), yielding maj-o in 52% over 4 steps. Overall, we have achieved the synthesis of maj-o in 9 steps (longest linear sequence, 18% overall yield) representing a doubling in overall yield compared to previous efforts while only requiring 3 silica column purifications. These improvements in terms of synthetic approach and reproducibility in yields provide a clear path to both the pre-clinical development of this class of lipopentapeptides and SAR studies that explore modifications to the tripeptide backbone of maj-D.

Efficient synthesis of maj-o (1). Employing COMU-based coupling reactions and octanoic acid replacement increases overall yield and reduces synthetic labor by limiting the number of purifications and eliminating stereospecific operations.

Majusculamide-o exerts a cytotoxic effect on cancer cells

To investigate the cytotoxic effect of maj-o on cancer cells, we treated a panel of cancer cell lines with maj-o. We used the following cell lines to represent the most clinically relevant model in terms of the lowest 5-year survival: OVCAR3 (ascites, metastatic),32 OVCAR8 (ovary, primary),33 H125 (adenosquamous carcinoma of the lung),34 U251N (glioblastoma), HEPG2 (hepatoblastoma),37 and PANC1 (pancreatic carcinoma of ductal cell origin).38 All cell lines could be effectively killed in a dose-dependent manner (Figs. 3A-G). The exact IC50-values of maj-o for OVCAR3, OVCAR8, H125, U251N, HEPG2, and PANC1 were 10.9 ± 3.2, 558.9 ± 146.1, 19.4 ± 6.6, 0.016 ± 0.015, 27.9 ± 10.0, and 9.2 ± 5.0 nM, respectively (Fig. 3H). Interestingly, OVCAR3 (metastatic tumor) shows much more vulnerability to maj-o than OVCAR8 (primary tumor), with an IC50 that is 50-fold lower. Furthermore, maj-o demonstrated to have an IC50 of only 0.016 nM towards the deadliest cancer, the glioblastoma cell line (Fig. 3D). On the other hand, the IC50 of maj-o was higher (28 nM) for the non-metastatic HEPG2 cells (Fig. 3E). These data show the cytotoxicity of maj-o in various cancer cells but with more potency towards metastatic cancer cells.

Maj-o treatment causes apoptosis of cancer cells.

Based on the observation that maj-o is more potently cytotoxic towards OVCAR3 cells compared to OVCAR8 cells, we performed flow cytometry to analyze whether OVCAR3 cell death is mediated through apoptosis. We quantified the level of apoptosis in OVCAR3 cells treated with maj-o concentrations of 0 (vehicle control), 5, 10, 20, and 30 nM for 24, 48, and 72 h. Even at low doses and short treatment durations, maj-o significantly induces apoptosis compared to the control, and this effect becomes more prominent with increasing concentrations. The percent apoptosis after 24 h of treatment is 2.51% ± 0.28, 4.80% ± 0.81, 4.44% ± 0.39, 5.20% ± 0.27, and 5.83% ± 0.35 for the vehicle control, 5 nM, 10 nM, 20 nM, and 30 nM, respectively (Fig. 4A). While the total cells undergoing apoptosis for the vehicle control (DMSO) remain unchanged after 48 and 72 h treatment, the percentage of apoptosis increases with maj-o treatment concentrations and time. At 48 h of maj-o treatment, the percent apoptosis is 3.46% ± 0.38, 13.72% ± 1.57, 13.07% ± 0.77, 13.95% ± 0.70, and 14.89% ± 0.66 for the vehicle control, 5 nM, 10 nM, 20 nM, and 30 nM, respectively (Fig. 4B). At 72 h of maj-o treatment, the percent apoptosis is 2.72% ± 0.24, 15.26% ± 1.27, 29.67% ± 3.62, 29.16% ± 1.44, and 33.59% ± 2.40 for the vehicle control, 5 nM, 10 nM, 20 nM, and 30 nM, respectively (Fig. 4C). Interestingly, at 48 h post-maj-o treatment, there are two subpopulations of apoptotic cells, which become more pronounced at 72 h. The two subpopulations of apoptotic cells differ based on the maj-o concentration in which the cells are treated. 5 and 10 nM treatments of maj-o favors one subpopulation of apoptotic cells, while 20 and 30 nM favors another subpopulation of apoptotic cells. This dose-dependent shift of subpopulations suggests a two-part mechanism of action where a high dose immediately kills cells by apoptosis regardless of exposure time, while a low dose of maj-o can only induce apoptosis after prolonged exposure. This hypothesis is supported by the shift in morphology of treated cells. Cells treated with maj-o appear contracted and fragmented (Figs. 3A-C, lower row), both typical signs of apoptosis.43 Altogether, our data strongly suggest that maj-o causes cell death via apoptosis.

Maj-o changes genes involved in PI3K-Akt, MAPK, and Hippo pathways, among others.

After confirming the cytotoxic effect and cellular mechanism of death of maj-o, we performed transcriptomic studies on maj-o-treated OVCAR3 cells for 0, 4, and 24 h (Fig. 5). Cells treated with maj-o for 0, 4, and 24 h have distinct differential gene expression patterns (Fig. 5A-B). After 4 h of treatment, 403 differentially expressed (DE) genes were found, with most showing expression changes of up to sixfold compared to the untreated cells (Fig. 5C). After 24 h of treatment, 1810 DE genes were found, again with most showing a sixfold expression change and some in the 20 to 25-fold range (Fig. 5D).

KEGG enrichment revealed that both 4 h (rank #2) and 24 h (rank #1) post-maj-o treatment enrich for the PI3K-Akt pathway (Fig. 6A-D). At the 4 h time point, the MAPK pathway was the most enriched pathway, which disappeared by 24 h treatment (Fig. 6E). A total of 19 and 22 genes were differentially expressed in the PI3K-Akt and MAPK pathways, respectively, post 4 h maj-o treatment (Fig. 6C-D). Previously, microcolin B and H, both closely structurally related to maj-o, have been reported as Hippo activators.24,30 We found that the Hippo pathway was enriched only in the 24 h but not in the 4 h maj-o treatment group (Fig. 6B and 6F). For 24 h post-maj-o treatment, a total of 60 and 31 genes were differentially expressed in the PI3K-Akt and Hippo pathways, respectively. The effect of maj-o on PI3K-Akt, MAPK, and Hippo pathways in other cancer cells.

To validate our RNA-seq data, we performed a qRT-PCR analysis on OVCAR3 cells treated with 0, 4, and 24 h of maj-o (Fig. 7A-C). Here, we randomly selected and successfully validated our RNA-seq datasets for genes that are upregulated (shown on left) and downregulated (shown on right). These are unique only to PI3K-Akt pathways at 4 and 24 h time points (ITGA2 and FGFR2; Fig. 7A), MAPK pathway at 4 h time point (JUN and FGF18; Fig. 7B), and Hippo pathways at 24 h time point (CCND1 and ID2; Fig. 7C).

(A-C) Expression change of genes involved in (A) PI3K-Akt pathways (ITGA2 and FGFR2), (B) MAPK pathway (JUN and FGF18), and Hippo pathways (CCND1 and ID2) at 0, 4, and 24 h post-maj-o treatment. * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.005, *** p < 0.001, ns as not significant as determined by one-way ANOVA. n = three independent experiments.

We next tested whether we observed the same expression pattern for another ovarian cancer cell line, OVCAR8, and another cancer cell line, PANC1. While ITGA2 was upregulated in both OVCAR8 and PANC1 cells at 24 h treatment, just like in the OVCAR3 cells, only PANC1 cells had upregulated ITGA2 at 4 h treatment, but not OVCAR8 cells. However, we did not observe downregulation of FGFR2 for both OVCAR8 and PANC1 cells at 4 h and 24 h time points (Fig. 7A). When we evaluated genes involved in MAPK pathways, we found JUN to be both upregulated in OVCAR8 and PANC1 cells; however, the FGF18 gene was not downregulated in both cell lines (Fig. 7B). Lastly, the upregulated Hippo pathway gene, CCND1, in maj-o treated OVCAR3 cells at 24 h time point showed no significant difference in OVCAR8 and PANC1 cells, while ID2 gene was upregulated in OVCAR8 cells but not significantly different in PANC1 cells pre- and post-maj-o treatment (Fig. 7C). While these results do not yet allow precise conclusions to be drawn about molecular processes, they show that maj-o has an impact on signaling cascades involved in tumorigenesis and the impact may be unique to each cancer type.

Discussion

The successful synthesis of maj-o (1) demonstrated that removal of the C40 and C42 methyl groups significantly reduces synthetic labor and complexity, increases the overall yield, and has a minor effect in terms of biological activity. For instance, the small reduction in potency of maj-o as compared to majusculamide D towards PANC-1 is consistent with the SAR model for the microcolins, where longer chain lengths of the lipophilic tail demonstrated higher potency.30 We hypothesize that the elimination of the two methyl groups at C40 and C42, respectively, reduces lipophilicity, therefore reducing potency. It is worth noting that based on observations reported by Zhao et. al. and our own synthetic studies, we believe that maj-o may still suffer from synthetic and metabolic liabilities that require further modifications for improved stability. For example, the acetoxy group still represents a minor pitfall since we experienced both deacetylation and elimination of this group, mostly under extended basic conditions (e.g., DIPEA) at temperatures above 4 °C. Furthermore, we foresee the C40 methyl group playing an important role in vivo to resist amidase metabolism and provide stability to the lipophilic tail which is essential for activity. Even so, maj-o demonstrated potent cytotoxic effects against various cancer cell lines at low nanomolar concentrations. Particularly, its effect on glioblastoma cells (U251N), the most aggressive cancer type in our panel, yielded a low picomolar IC50 value (16 pm), highlighting maj-o’s efficacy against highly malignant solid tumor cells. In addition, maj-o demonstrated stronger cytotoxicity (by approximately 50-fold) against OVCAR3, a high-grade serous ovarian cancer isolated from a metastatic tumor, compared to OVCAR8, a non-serous ovarian cancer isolated from a primary ovarian tumor. Our data suggests that maj-o may have a preference in cytotoxic killing against more aggressive metastatic cancer cells, which should be further investigated.

After demonstration of the cytotoxic effect of maj-o against cancer cells, we investigated the mechanism of induced cell death and found that maj-o promotes apoptosis in a concentration-dependent manner. Interestingly, even low concentrations were sufficient to initiate apoptosis, which further indicates the high potency of maj-o. Next, we evaluated the primary effect (4 h post-treatment) and secondary effect (24 h post-treatment) of maj-o treatment on gene expression and analyzed the affected pathways. We identified that MAPK and PI3K-Akt pathways were the most affected immediately after maj-o treatment. It is promising that maj-o affects these pathways, especially because they are characteristic features of many cancer types,47,48 including breast cancer, colorectal cancer, lung cancer, and others. Furthermore, given that both the MAPK and PI3K pathways can influence each other, the ability to target both is especially important. However, it is critical to note that we performed an unbiased approach and analyzed genes uniquely associated with the PI3K-Akt pathway at 4 and 24 h time points, and MAPK pathway at the 4 h time point, for sequencing validation purposes. Future studies will be crucial to determine whether maj-o targets other oncogenic drivers in these cancer types. We also tested the effect of maj-o in the Hippo pathway, as a previous study on microcolin B and H, a close relative of maj-D and maj-o, has been reported to directly target Hippo pathways. Our data showed enrichment of the Hippo pathway 24 h post-treatment, but not at 4 h post-treatment, suggesting that maj-o may affect the Hippo pathway as a secondary or delayed response. While this observation is preliminary and may reflect a cell type-specific effect, it provides a compelling rationale to further investigate maj-o as an anti-cancer agent, given that the Hippo pathway is often dysregulated in cancers.49 . One limitation of our sequencing study is that we used a higher concentration of maj-o because we had to shorten the treatment time to assess the primary and secondary effects of maj-o, with visible cytotoxic effect. Performing some analysis at a lower dose that is not therapeutically effective may provide insights into the holistic mechanism of maj-o. Furthermore, in vivo testing will provide us with more information on the therapeutic efficacy of the compound. Lastly, refinement of the maj-o structure through analog development will allow maximizing cytotoxic efficacy by dealing with potential limitations such as formulation, metabolism, toxicity, drug to target binding, and other factors.

In conclusion, maj-o has demonstrated to be a more accessible synthetic target and showed excellent potency and specificity against several solid tumor cancer cells. In addition, apoptosis was identified as the induced mechanism of cell death, and a general influence on signaling cascades was identified. Subsequent studies should be carried out investigating the mechanism of action and specific target of maj-o, and further synthetic studies that uncover its full SAR to afford a more drug-like lead.

Methods

Synthesis of maj-o

General methods: All reactions were carried out under nitrogen atmosphere. Solvents were dried as follows: THF, DMF, CH2Cl2 from an auto-column distillation system. All other solvents and reagents, including diisopropylamine (DIPEA), were purchased as their driest form from different sources and used directly. Flash column chromatography was performed using a CombiFlash EZ Prep system (RediSep® Silica Gold Columns) using mixtures of increasing polarity. Silica pads/plugs were run using fritted Buchner funnels and silica Gel (Mesh 60). 13C NMR and 1H NMR spectra were recorded on Bruker 500 MHz Avance III AV-500 NMR spectrometer and CDCl3 (Cambridge Isotopes) was treated with flame-dried K2CO3 (solid). For NMR data all chemical shifts (δ) are quoted in parts per million (ppm) referenced to the appropriate residual solvent peak (CHCl3, 7.26 and 77.16 ppm), with the abbreviations s, br s, d, t, q, and m denoting singlet, broad singlet, doublet, triplet, quartet and multiplet respectively. Coupling constants (J) are provided in Hertz (Hz) and analyzed by MestReNova software and confirmed by manual calculation. High resolution mass spectrometry and optical rotation measurements ([α]D) for maj-o (1) were recorded on a Waters UHPLC-Q-ToF (Xevo) and Jasco P-200 polarimeter, respectively.

Dipeptide 3: Benzyl Boc-N-methyl-L-valinate HCl salt was prepared from Boc-N-Me-L-valine (Sigma-Aldrich, catalog number 15538) using previously described experimental procedures27 to provide multigram quantities of Benzyl Boc-N-methyl-L-valinate HCl salt (98% yield over 2 steps, see Fig. S1). To a solution of O-acetyl-N-(tert-butoxycarbonyl)-L-threonine (1.07 g, 4.09 mmol), and HATU (4.09 g, 4.09 mmol) in CH2Cl2 (11.1 mL) at 0˚C, was added DIPEA (1.4 mL, 7.98 mmol), and stirred for 1 h at 0˚C. After the activation, benzyl Boc-N-methyl-L-valinate HCl salt (1.0 g, 3.89 mmol) was added, and the reaction was allowed to slowly reach room temperature and stirred overnight. The reaction mixture was then concentrated under reduced pressure, diluted with diethyl ether (150 mL), quenched with 2 M HCl (100 mL), and extracted with diethyl ether (3 × 75 mL). The combined organic layers were washed with 2N HCl (3 × 75 mL), brine, dried over MgSO4, and concentrated. The crude residue was purified via silica column chromatography (0–35% EtOAc in hexanes) to give benzyl N-(O-acetyl-N-(tert-butoxycarbonyl)-L-threonyl)-N-methyl-L-valinate 3 as a pale yellow oil (1.13 g, 2.43 mmol, 63%). 1H-NMR Spectroscopic data was in accordance with previously reported data27 (Fig. S2).

Tripeptide 5: To a solution of dipeptide 3 (1.13 g, 2.43 mmol) in THF (2.4 mL) was added 4N HCl in dioxane (12.2 mL, 48.6 mmol) at 0˚C. The reaction was allowed to reach room temperature and stirred for 3 h. The mixture was concentrated under reduced pressure to yield the corresponding HCl salt (978 mg, 2.44 mmol, quant.). The resulting salt product was combined with N-Boc-N-methyl-O-methyl-L-tyrosine (794 mg, 2.57 mmol), DIPEA (1.34 ml, 7.70 mmol) and DMF (7.33 mL), then cooled to 0˚C for 10 min. COMU (1.099 g, 2.57 mmol) was then added portion wise, and the reaction was stirred for 3 h at 0˚C and then partitioned between ethyl acetate (150 mL) and 1 N HCl (150 mL). The aqueous layer was extracted with ethyl acetate (3 × 100 mL) and the combined organic layers were washed with 1 M NaHCO3 (3 X 75 mL), H2O and brine, dried over MgSO4, filtered, and concentrated under vacuum. The resulting residue was purified via silica column chromatography (0–30% ethyl acetate in dichloromethane), providing benzyl Boc-N-methyl-O-methyl-L-tyrosinyl-N-(O-acetyl-N-(tert-butoxycarbonyl)-L-threonyl)-N-methyl-L-valinate 5 (918 mg, 1.40 mmol, 55%) as a white foam. 1H-NMR Spectroscopic data was in accordance with previously reported data27 (Fig. S3).

Majusculamide-o, 1: To a solution of tripeptide 5 (120 mg, 0.183 mmol) in THF (0.320 mL) was added HCl 4N in dioxane (1.830 mL, 7.32 mmol) at 0 °C. The reaction was stirred for 4 h at room temperature then concentrated under reduced pressure to afford the corresponding HCl salt (108 mg, 0.183 mmol, quantitative) as white semi-solid foam. In a separate reaction vessel, octanoic acid (28.9 mg, 0.201 mmol) and HATU (78 mg, 0.201 mmol) were combined in DMF (521 µl) at room temperature and DIPEA (35.0 µl, 0.201 mmol) was added dropwise. After 1 h stirring at room temperature, the mixture was transferred to a separate reaction vessel containing the tripeptide salt (108 mg, 0.182 mmol). Then, DIPEA (31.9 µl, 0.182 mmol) was added and the reaction was stirred overnight at rt. The reaction mixture was diluted with diethyl ether (15 mL) and quenched with 1N HCl (10 mL). The aqueous layer was extracted with diethyl ether (3 × 15 mL) and the combined organic layers were washed with 2N HCl (3 × 10 mL), water, brine, dried over MgSO4, and filtered. The organic layer was concentrated under reduced pressure to afford (benzyl N-(O-acetyl-N-((S)-3-(4-methoxyphenyl)-2-(N-methyloctanamido)propanoyl)-L-threonyl)-N-methyl-L-valinate 6 (110 mg, 0.161 mmol, 88% yield) as a pale yellow foam which was taken directly to the next step without further purification. A solution of benzyl N-(O-acetyl-N-((S)-3-(4-methoxyphenyl)-2-(N-methyloctanamido)propanoyl)-L-threonyl)-N-methyl-L-valinate (110 mg, 0.161 mmol) in 10% Pd/C (25.8 mg, 0.024 mmol) in MeOH (1.125 mL) was stirred under a hydrogen atmosphere for 1 h. The reaction mixture was quantitatively transferred and filtered using MeOH through a plug of Celite Filter Aid and concentrated to afford the debenzylated product, N-(O-acetyl-N-((S)-3-(4-methoxyphenyl)-2-(N-methyloctanamido)propanoyl)-L-threonyl)-N-methyl-L-valine (94.5 mg, 0.160 mmol, 99% yield), as a pale yellow foam. To a solution of the debenzylated product (94.5 mg, 0.160 mmol), pyrrolyl proline 7 (39.2 mg, 0.160 mmol), and DIPEA (84 µl, 0.479 mmol) in DMF (532 µl) was added COMU (70.5 mg, 0.160 mmol) at 0 °C. The reaction was allowed to reach rt and stirred for 2 h, then diluted with EtOAc (50 mL) and quenched with 1 M HCl (35 mL). The resulting residue was purified via silica column purification (0–35% EtOAc in DCM) to afford 1 (75 mg, 0.096 mmol, 60% yield), maj-o, as a white foamy solid. Cell culture

OVCAR3 and OVCAR8 are NCI-60 cell lines obtained from the National Cancer Institute Division of Cancer Treatment and Diagnosis Tumor Repository (NCI DCTD; Frederick, MD). Together with H125 (obtained from Dr. Frederick A Valeriote, Henry Ford Health, Detroit, MI), these three cell lines were grown in Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium 1640 (RPMI; Gibco, Cat# 11,875–093) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Cat# A52567-01) and 1% Pen-Strep (100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin; Gibco, Cat# 15,140–122) in a humidified 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. U251N, HEPG2, and PANC1 (also obtained through Dr. Valeriote) were grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Cat# 11,965–092) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% Pen-Strep, and 1% L-Glutamine (2 mM; Gibco, Cat# A2916801). All cell lines were tested for mycoplasma using a MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza, Cat# LT07-118).

Cell viability assays

All cell lines were plated in 96-well plates (Corning, Cat# 3598) with 5 × 103 cells per well and treated with a serial dilution of maj-o after 24 h. Maj-o was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 472301), and the DMSO concentration between the samples was normalized. Specific drug concentrations were optimized to generate full killing curves and are indicated in either the Fig. or Fig. legends. Cellular proliferation was measured using a CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega, Cat# G7573) at 72 h, and luminescence values were measured at an integration time of 20 ms on a Biotek Synergy Neo Plate Reader (Agilent).

Apoptosis flow cytometry analysis

OVCAR3 cells were plated in 24-well plates (Corning, Cat# 3526) with 5 × 104 cells per well and treated with 0, 5, 10, 20, and 30 nM maj-o after 24 h. Concentrations were chosen as sub-, 1x-, 2x-, and 3x-IC50 with 24, 48, and 72 h timepoints, and the DMSO concentration between samples was equalized. The optimal concentration of the dyes was determined by titration and calculating the SI (200 nM ApoTracker, 1:400 ZombieNIR). Cells and culture media were collected and stained with ApoTracker Green (BioLegend, Cat# 427402) and Zombie NIR (BioLegend, Cat# 423105) to assess apoptosis at 24, 48, and 72 h timepoints. Cells were stained in a 96-well rb plate (TFS, Cat# 163320), stabilized using 1% FBS in PBS, and fixed using 1% paraformaldehyde (PFA; EMS, Cat# 15710) in PBS. Samples were acquired on an Attune CytPix Flow Cytometer (ThermoFisher Scientific) located in the MSU Flow Cytometry Core Facility (supported by the Equipment Grants Program, award #2022–70410-38419, from the U.S. Department of Agriculture [USDA], National Institute of Food and Agriculture [NIFA]). Data was analyzed with FCS Express (DeNovo Software, version 7.24.0030), and FMOs were used as gating controls. The percentages of live (ApoTracker-/Zombie NIR-), early apoptotic (ApoTracker + /Zombie NIR-), and late apoptotic (ApoTracker + /Zombie NIR +) singlets were evaluated.

Whole RNA sequencing

OVCAR3 cells were plated in 6-well plates (avantor, Cat# 10,062–892) with 5 × 105 cells per well and treated with 638 nM (500 ng/mL) maj-o after 24 h. Durations of treatment were either 0 h, 4 h, or 24 h, and the DMSO concentration between samples was normalized. The RNA was isolated and purified using a RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat# 74,104). RNA amounts were quantified with a DS-11 Spectrophotometer (DeNovix). 1 μg of RNA per sample was shipped to the Van Andel Institute Genomics core (333 Bostwick Ave. NE, Grand Rapids, MI 49,503) for RNA analysis.

RNA analysis sequencing protocol

RNAseq data was aligned using STAR (v 2.7.2b) and quantified using RSEM (v 1.3.3). Differential expression (DE) was performed using the diffExp function in the OCTAD package with edgeR as the method for DE. The cutoff for significance was chosen as absolute value of log2FoldChange > 1 and padj < 0.05.

PCA/Heatmap

Raw counts of individual samples were used to generate normalized log2 counts with edgeR (v 4.6.2). The expression values per gene were standardized using ggplot2 (v 3.5.2). Only genes found in either list of DE genes were used subsequently. Arrangement of samples (columns) was ordered after treatment groups and plotted using pheatmap (v 1.0.13). PCA analysis was also conducted using ggplot2 (v 3.5.2).

Expression mapping and pathway enrichment protocol

Analyses were performed in RStudio (v 2025.05.0–496) based on the tidyverse (v 2.0.0) package. The overview (DE genes) was created using EnhancedVolcano (v 1.26.0) in combination with dplyr (v 1.1.4). Gene information was obtained through org.Hs.eg.db (v 3.21.0), the enrichment was done using clusterProfiler (v 4.16.0), DOSE (v 4.2.0), and enrichplot (v 1.28.1). Visualization of highlighted pathways was done using ggrepel (v 0.9.6) and ggplot2 (v 3.5.2).

qRT-PCR

OVCAR3, OVCAR8, and PANC1 cells were plated in 24-well plates (Corning, Cat# 3526) with 1.5 × 105 cells per well and treated with a dose corresponding to 7 × IC50, respectively. Durations of treatment were either 0 h, 4 h, or 24 h, and the DMSO concentration between samples was normalized. The RNA was isolated and purified using a RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat# 74,104). RNA amounts were quantified with a DS-11 Spectrophotometer (DeNovix). 1 μg of RNA per sample was converted into cDNA using a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Cat# 4,374,966) in combination with a MiniAmp Plus Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems). Subsequently, qRT-PCR was performed with 10 ng of cDNA and 250 nM of primers per sample, using the Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Cat# 4,367,659). Runs were performed with a Bio-Rad C1000 Thermal Cycler equipped with a CFX96 Optical PCR Module (both Bio-Rad).
