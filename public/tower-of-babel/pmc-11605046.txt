A Novel Approach to Peripheral Nerve Regeneration: Local FK-506 Delivery Using a Reservoir Flap Model
Authors: Jong Won Hong, Jung Hyun Lim, Eun Hye Kang, Young Seok Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePeripheral nerve injuries can lead to lasting functional impairments, impacting movement and quality of life. FK-506, a widely used immunosuppressant, has demonstrated potential in promoting nerve regeneration in addition to its immunosuppressive effects. This study investigates the use of a local reservoir flap to deliver FK-506 directly to the nerve injury site, aiming to enhance nerve regeneration while minimizing systemic immunosuppression.Materials and MethodsSciatic nerve injuries were surgically induced in 24 rats, which were divided into control, 0.5 mg/kg FK-506 (Exp 1), and 2.0 mg/kg FK-506 (Exp 2) groups. A superficial inferior epigastric artery flap served as a reservoir for FK-506, allowing direct delivery to the injury site. FK-506 was administered intermittently over a 4-week period. Outcomes included the Sciatic Functional Index (SFI), muscle recovery (width and weight), nerve morphology, expression of neurogenic markers such as GDNF, immune cell counts, and body weight.ResultsExp 1 (0.5 mg/kg) demonstrated significant improvements in SFI, GDNF expression, and muscle width compared to the control and high-dose groups. These findings suggest that FK-506 administration via a reservoir flap, particularly at a lower dose, supports effective nerve regeneration. Additionally, FK-506 treatment did not result in significant changes in immune cell profiles or body weight, indicating minimal systemic effects.ConclusionLocalized FK-506 administration via a reservoir flap effectively enhances peripheral nerve regeneration and minimizes systemic immunosuppression, making it a promising approach for clinical application in treating peripheral nerve injuries.Keywords: Peripheral nerve injury, nerve regeneration, immunosuppression

INTRODUCTION

Peripheral nerve injuries, unlike other tissue injuries, can leave sequelae even after recovery, significantly inconveniencing patients. Although central nervous system injuries are often considered more critical, peripheral nerve injuries also cause substantial discomfort.1 Recovery of sensory nerves is as essential as that of motor nerves. The loss of sensation eliminates the defense and warning systems against external trauma. In movement control, continuous, reciprocal information exchange is crucial, and without it, excessive movement and compensatory reliance on eyesight may occur.2

Numerous efforts have been made for peripheral nerve repair, such as drug, stem cell, and conduit therapies.1,3,4,5 Experimental results have been encouraging; however, few have translated successfully to clinical application. In contrast, FK-506, a representative immunosuppressant widely used clinically, has demonstrated nerve regeneration capacity alongside its immunosuppressive effects.6

Since the first hand transplant in 1998, vascularized composite tissue allotransplantation (VCA) has advanced to include face, larynx, and even uterus transplants. In hand and face transplants, functional recovery necessitates motor and sensory regeneration. Research indicates that the rate of nerve regeneration in hand and facial transplants is faster than that in nerve injuries, replantation, and facial reanimation surgeries, largely attributed to FK-506’s neuroregenerative potential.7,8,9 Despite being recognized since its development, FK-506’s nerve regeneration effects were initially of lesser clinical importance as its primary value was in immunosuppression.10 Thus, FK-506 plays a significant dual role in VCA, enhancing both immunosuppression and nerve regeneration.7,8

Much research has explored FK-506’s neuroregenerative properties, with particular interest in minimizing its immunosuppressive effects while leveraging its nerve regeneration potential4,9,11 FK-506 inhibits interleukin (IL)-2 production by suppressing T-helper cell proliferation via a calmodulin inhibition pathway.12 The signal pathway activated by FK-506 is mediated by FKBP, with FKBP12 associated with immunosuppression and FKBP52 with neuroregeneration.13 Studies have also examined direct local injections of FK-506 to the lesion site to reduce systemic immunosuppression. However, challenges include potential damage to surrounding tissues during needle injection, inaccurate delivery, and inconsistent retention at the nerve repair site.9 Recent studies have progressed towards controlled release methods using conduits, yet rapid clinical application remains challenging, and studies specifically assessing FK-506’s lack of impact on immune response are limited.14,15

In this study, we propose the use of a local flap as a reservoir to administer FK-506 injections directly to the nerve repair site. This approach aims to confine FK-506 to the target area, avoiding needle-related nerve damage and minimizing systemic effects. Additionally, by monitoring changes in body weight and immune cell impact, we aim to confirm the non-immunosuppressive impact of FK-506, thus providing a feasible method for clinical application.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animal & reservoir flap operation

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines and approval of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Yonsei University College of Medicine (Approval No. 2021-0219). The animal procedure involved inducing sciatic nerve injury and repair, subsequently covered by a superficial inferior epigastric artery flap. Male Sprague-Dawley rats, aged 5 weeks and weighing 250–300 g, were utilized. The superficial inferior epigastric artery flap was employed as the reservoir flap, with anesthesia. Following an inguinal incision, the superficial epigastric vessel branching from the femoral vessel was identified, and an adipofascial flap based on this pedicle was elevated. To ensure coverage of the sacral area, an approximately 4 cm axial flap was raised.

The sciatic nerve served as the model for nerve injury. An incision was made in the sacral region, followed by retraction of the gluteus maximus muscle to expose the sciatic nerve. After transection, nerve repair was performed with 10-0 nylon sutures using three stitches in total.

A subcutaneous tunnel was created from the inguinal to the sacral area. Through this tunnel, the elevated superficial inferior epigastric artery flap was transposed to cover the sciatic nerve repair site, positioned subcutaneously between the sacrum and gluteal region, and secured in place. This approach allowed for direct injection of FK-506 into the reservoir flap. Skin closure was completed with 5-0 nylon sutures (Supplementary Fig. 1, only online).

Experimental design

Sciatic nerve injury and repair were performed on one side only, leaving the other side intact as a normal control. The control group (Cont) underwent only sciatic nerve injury and repair with the addition of the reservoir flap. FK-506 (product name and manufacturer details) was administered in doses of 0.5 mg/kg for the experimental group 1 (Exp 1) and 2.0 mg/kg for the experimental group 2 (Exp 2) (Supplementary Table 1, only online). FK-506 was injected intermittently over a 4-week period, with injections given intraoperatively and on postoperative days 1, 3, 7, 10, 14, 21, and 28. This schedule involved four injections during the first week, two in the second week, one in the third week, and one in the fourth week. Observations continued over a total period of 12 weeks.

Body weight change & immune cell analysis

To assess whether the immunosuppressive effects of FK-506 had any adverse systemic impact, we monitored changes in body weight and immune cell populations. Blood sampling was performed using retro-orbital sinus/plexus sampling. Blood samples for immune cell analysis were collected weekly for 4 weeks, under inhalation anesthesia. Blood was drawn using heparinized 75 µL capillary tubes for microhematocrit (Marian Field Superior, Lauda-Königshofen, Germany). Following sampling, adequate compression was applied over the eyelid to prevent hematoma formation, taking care to avoid excessive pressure on the eye.

Immune cell analysis involved flow cytometry (FACS) to measure the levels of B cells, T cells, NK cells, helper T cells (Th), cytotoxic T cells (Tc), and regulatory T cells (Treg). Rat peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated as previously described. Anti-rat CD32 was added to the cell suspension for a 10-minute incubation at room temperature, followed by the addition of primary antibodies specific to each cell marker, incubated for 30 minutes at 4℃. Primary antibodies included CD3, CD4, CD8a, CD25, CD45Ra, and CD161a. Immune cell markers were identified as follows: B cell (CD45RA+/CD3−), T cell (CD3+/CD45RA−), NK cells (CD161a), Th (CD3+ CD4+), Tc (CD3+CD8+), and Treg (CD3+ CD25+ FoxP3+).16,17,18

The following antibodies were used: anti-rat CD3 (Cat No 550353, BD), CD32 (Cat No 550271, BD), CD4 (Cat No 561833, BD), CD8a (Cat No 561611, BD), CD25 (Cat No 554866, BD), CD45RA (Cat No 561624, BD), CD161a (Cat No 565413, BD), and FoxP3 (Cat No 17-4321-81, eBioscience). FACS preparation and analysis were conducted according to established protocols.18

Evaluation of nerve regeneration

To assess the extent of nerve recovery, we conducted walking tests and measured the weight, width, and circumference of the muscle. Sciatic nerve recovery was evaluated using the Sciatic Functional Index (SFI) obtained from the walking test. The walking test was performed every 2 weeks. To acclimate the rats to the walking chamber and foot-printing process, walking test training was conducted starting 1 week before the experiment. The SFI was determined by analyzing the footprints made by the rats during ambulation, comparing normal and injured limb impressions.

The walking test was conducted without anesthesia. Different colored inks were applied to each hind paw (right and left), and the rats were made to walk in a straight line. During analysis, three measurements were taken from the resulting footprints: the total spread (distance between the toes when fully spread), the intermediate toe length (distance between the 2nd and 4th toes), and the footprint length (Supplementary Fig. 2, only online).

For further assessment, we measured the weight, width, and circumference of the gluteus maximus muscle, innervated by the sciatic nerve. Muscle width was measured with care to avoid compression, and the circumference was measured at the thickest portion. Hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed for morphological analysis of the muscle tissue.

Nerve morphology evaluation

For the analysis of nerve morphology, we measured the nerve fiber count and thickness. Toluidine blue staining with semithin sections was used to facilitate this analysis. Toluidine blue staining was conducted using a standard protocol. First, low-magnification images (100×) were observed to assess the overall morphology. High-magnification images (1000×) were then taken at three randomly selected areas to evaluate nerve fiber count, density, myelin thickness, and the G-ratio. The thickness of the myelin sheath was calculated by subtracting the axon diameter from the myelinated fiber diameter.

Neurogenic factor analysis

To evaluate nerve regeneration, we assessed neurogenic factors, including neurofilament, S100, PGP9.5, and GDNF. Immunofluorescence staining (IF) was performed to detect neurofilament (Cat No. 556355, BD), S100 (Cat No. 610137, BD), and PGP9.5 (Cat No. 550565, BD). GDNF expression was analyzed using real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR).

For PCR analysis of GDNF expression, total RNA was extracted from nerve tissues, and cDNA was synthesized using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Catalog No. K1622; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Amplification was conducted with PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems™, Catalog No. A25741; Thermo Fisher Scientific) on the LightCycler® 96 System (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). GAPDH served as the reference gene, with the following primers: GAPDH forward (AGACAGCCGCATCTTCTTGT), GAPDH reverse (ATCCGTTCACACCGACCTTC), GDNF forward (GACTTGGGTTTGGGCTACGA), and GDNF reverse (TGGTAAACCAGGCTGTCGTC).

Statistics

One-way analysis of variance was used for data analysis, followed by post-hoc Bonferroni’s correction for multiple comparisons. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

The subjects were divided into three groups (Cont, Exp 1, and Exp 2) with eight rats in each group, totaling 24 rats. No infections or abnormal findings were observed during the 12-week experimental period. There were no significant differences in body weight among the three groups (Supplementary Fig. 3, only online). Immune cell analysis included total B cells, total T cells, and NK cells. For T cells, subtypes analyzed were Tc, Th, and Treg. No significant differences in immune cell counts were observed between groups (Fig. 1). Systemic effects of FK-506 related to immunosuppression were not evident.

Nerve regeneration in ExP 1 was improved compared to other groups. In the walking test, the SFI showed that the Exp 1 group demonstrated a faster recovery rate compared to the control and Exp 2 groups. Recovery was generally rapid, with statistically significant results observed throughout the study period (Fig. 2). Muscle analysis was performed to evaluate the extent of muscle recovery. In muscle width, Exp 1 showed a statistically significant difference. Although circumference and weight did not show statistical significance, Exp 1 exhibited a pattern of increased mean values (Fig. 3).

Nerve morphology showed less variation among the groups. Toluidine staining was performed for observation. Compared to normal nerves, injured nerves appeared thinner and irregular. For each nerve fiber, the myelinated fiber diameter, axon diameter, and myelin thickness were measured. Measurements were taken at 1000× magnification in three different areas, and the averages were calculated. As nerve regeneration progressed, these values generally increased. Although no statistically significant differences were observed across groups, the Exp 1 group demonstrated a trend of higher mean values compared to the other groups. The G-ratio in normal nerves ranged from 0.6 to 0.7, while it remained below 0.6 in all groups (Fig. 4).

Neurogenic factor in Exp 1 showed meaningful result in GDNF. In the neurogenic factor analysis using IF, Exp 1 showed a trend of improvement in neurofilament, S100, and PGP9.5, although no statistically significant differences were observed. In contrast, real-time PCR analysis of GDNF revealed a statistically significant increase in Exp 1 (Fig. 5). Taken together, these results suggest that FK-506 administered via a local reservoir flap effectively promotes nerve regeneration. DISCUSSION

Peripheral nerve injuries are often underestimated compared to central nervous system injuries. However, peripheral nerve injuries also cause considerable discomfort in affected patients. Motor nerve injuries result in functional impairments, while sensory nerve injuries lead to sensory loss or pain.19 In particular, sensory loss in the hand forces patients to rely on visual guidance for hand use, often leading to excessive force and compensatory movements.

The healing process in nerve injuries differs significantly from wound healing.6,20,21 Nerve injuries impact not only the injury site but also the proximal and distal regions. Restoration processes associated with Wallerian degeneration and nerve recovery are distinct from the mechanisms involved in wound healing. These nerve repair processes follow a different timeline than that observed in wound healing.20,21,22

Various drugs have been explored to promote peripheral nerve regeneration.9 In addition to pharmacological approaches, stem cell therapy has also been investigated.23 However, no single agent has yet achieved the status of a standard treatment. Among the drugs studied, FK-506—a commonly used immunosuppressant—has been reported to aid in nerve regeneration.7 Given the numerous practical obstacles in translating many laboratory findings to clinical settings, we aimed to investigate the potential of FK-506, which is already widely used in clinical practice, for its neuroregenerative effects.

Since the discovery of FK-506 in soil in the 1980s, the field of organ transplantation has seen remarkable advancements. FK-506, an immunosuppressant, primarily acts by inhibiting Th through its role as a calmodulin inhibitor, thereby suppressing IL-2 production.12 The neuroregenerative effects of FK-506 were recognized early on, yet for some time, interest in its application for nerve regeneration remained limited. Attention to FK-506’s neuroregenerative potential increased with the advent of hand and face transplantations. Vascularized composite tissue allotransplantation (VCA) is used when conventional reconstructive methods are inadequate, allowing reconstruction of homologous tissue from another species, with a primary goal of “functional” recovery. Functional recovery in VCA relies heavily on nerve regeneration, especially in hand and face transplantations, where the ability to move the hand and to open and close the eyes and mouth, as well as restore sensation, depends critically on the reconnection of both motor and sensory nerves for restoring movement and sensation.8,24 Indeed, in cases of hand or facial transplantation, the recovery of motor and sensory functions occurs at a faster rate than in replantation or autologous reconstruction for amputation or facial nerve injury, an effect attributed to FK-506’s role in promoting nerve regeneration.25

Many efforts have been made to enhance FK-506’s neuroregenerative capabilities. These approaches have ranged from conventional systemic injections to localized injections directly at the injury site. The primary goal has been to minimize the immunosuppressive effects while harnessing only the neuroregenerative benefits.26 Consequently, numerous studies have explored continuous release methods using conduits or three-dimensional techniques, including conduits designed for sustained release.14,15,27,28 Despite these advances, systemic injections remain a concern for immunocompetent patients due to the immunosuppressive effects. Additionally, local injections risk unintended spread to non-neural tissues, and the use of conduits can introduce foreign body reactions, making these methods less practical for routine use.

Our goal was to devise a method that could be implemented more readily in clinical practice. We developed a “reservoir flap” approach, applying a local flap over the nerve repair site to facilitate localized FK-506 injections. This method minimizes the risk of FK-506 leakage beyond the nerve and reduces the likelihood of direct damage to surrounding tissues. We hypothesized that an effective local flap would require vascularized tissue capable of maintaining viability around the nerve repair site. To achieve this, we designed an adipofascial flap based on the superficial epigastric artery, providing a long axial flap suitable for our experimental model. Given the potential influence of the adipofascial flap itself on outcomes, we included a control group with the adipofascial flap applied without FK-506.

In designing this study, we carefully deliberated the injection dosage, administration duration, and observation period to optimize nerve regeneration while minimizing immunosuppressive effects. The primary objective was to enhance nerve regeneration without compromising immunosuppression. Had nerve regeneration been our sole focus, a higher dosage and prolonged administration of FK-506 would have been employed.

The dosage and timing of FK-506 administration vary widely among researchers. Although FK-506 has been tested at various doses, studies have shown a dose-dependent effect up to a certain level, beyond which the efficacy plateaus.10,19,29,30 Additionally, some studies have reported positive effects even at low doses.31,32 In rat transplantation studies, FK-506 is typically administered at doses ranging from 0.5 mg/kg to 2.0 mg/kg. In our preliminary study, we observed that commonly used FK-506 doses led to weight loss and, in some cases, mortality. To establish a safer dosing strategy, we conducted a 1-month preliminary study administering FK-506 alone in various concentrations and intervals, confirming its association with weight loss and mortality at higher doses. Based on these findings, we selected 0.5 mg/kg as a low dose and 2.0 mg/kg as a high dose to differentiate between FK-506 administration levels.

The frequency and duration of FK-506 administration were determined based on the nerve regeneration timeline.5,6,21,22 We placed significant emphasis on the initial phase of axonal sprouting and new axon formation, which occurs over the course of 1 to several weeks. Specifically, we focused on the period up to 2 weeks post-injury, as this is when neurogenic factors are most abundantly released and Schwann cell proliferation begins during nerve regeneration.21,22,33 Consequently, we limited the administration period to 4 weeks to minimize the effects of immunosuppression. While the duration of FK-506 use has also varied, some reports indicate favorable outcomes with short-term administration.5,34,35,36,37

Our primary objective was to minimize systemic effects, and intermittent injections allowed for recovery periods between FK-506 doses to reduce immunosuppression impact. Since we could not entirely exclude the influence of the reservoir flap itself, a control group was included where only the reservoir flap was applied. The observation period was set to 12 weeks, a typical duration for assessing clinical recovery.

Our findings confirmed the effectiveness of FK-506 in promoting nerve recovery, with the low dose of 0.5 mg/kg demonstrating greater efficacy than the higher dose of 2.0 mg/kg. The most notable result was a statistically significant improvement in the SFI in the Exp 1 group, as measured by the walking test. Additionally, real-time PCR analysis revealed a significant increase in GDNF expression in Exp 1. Muscle analysis also showed a statistically significant increase in muscle width in the Exp 1 group. Although other outcomes did not achieve statistical significance, Exp 1 consistently exhibited an overall pattern of improvement, including positive trends in nerve fiber analysis and neurogenic factor levels. Notably, there were no significant changes in body weight or immune cell counts across any FK-506-treated groups, indicating minimal systemic effects. Further studies are needed to investigate the mechanisms underlying the greater efficacy observed at the lower dose and to explore additional factors that may contribute to these findings.

Although we did not present these findings due to a lack of statistical significance, there were no observed differences in the number or density of nerve fibers. This may be attributable to the relatively short 4-week duration of intermittent FK-506 injections. Neurofilament stabilizes axonal structure, S100 is an indicator of Schwann cell activation, and PGP9.5 reflects the presence and distribution of neuronal cells.3,38 These neurogenic factors were analyzed using IF due to limited tissue availability.

Our study has certain limitations. One limitation is the absence of electromyography (EMG) data, and another is the relatively small sample size of eight rats per group. Although we initially configured and set up EMG during the early stages of the experiment, it was challenging to establish consistent measurement conditions and obtain reliable results. While EMG is recognized as a definitive method for assessing nerve recovery in peripheral nerve injury and regeneration studies, many studies have validated their hypotheses using alternative methods. In particular, the SFI is considered a critical measure of nerve recovery outcomes, and we were able to present meaningful results using this metric. Future studies will aim to incorporate EMG with improved setup and consistency in nerve research.

In this study, we conducted additional experiments with a larger sample per group; however, there were cases of mortality during the early stages of the retro-orbital sinus/plexus sampling process. Following unsuccessful blood sampling attempts, repeated sampling efforts while the animals were awakening from anesthesia sometimes led to mortality due to pain or required euthanasia due to retrobulbar hematoma. As such, we have included a note on precautions for retro-orbital sinus/plexus sampling in the Methods section.

In future studies, increasing the dosage and duration of FK-506 administration, along with analyzing these factors via real-time PCR, may yield more definitive results.

FK-506 is not typically used for nerve injury patients. However, as FK-506 is a clinically approved drug, its pharmacokinetics and potential side effects are well-documented. If applied in the early stages of nerve regeneration, there is sufficient rationale for its immediate clinical use. Even if new immunosuppressants are developed in the future, FK-506 will likely remain important in areas of VCA, such as hand and facial transplantation, where nerve regeneration is crucial.

To date, no standard protocol for FK-506 in nerve regeneration has been established. In our study, the most favorable outcomes were observed with intermittent administration of 0.5 mg/kg over 4 weeks. Using a reservoir flap allowed us to precisely target the injection site. This method facilitates effective delivery not only laterally but also medially, enhancing the precision of administration to the injury site. In clinical settings, a local adipose flap could serve as an effective reservoir for targeted FK-506 delivery.
