Dual-function surface engineering for enhancing anode stability in alkaline seawater oxidation
Authors: Yuchun Ren, Denian Wang, Shengjun Sun, Zixiao Li, Yongchao Yao, Zhiyuan Yang, Hefeng Wang, Jiayun Song, Chaoxin Yang, Zhengwei Cai, Yanqin Lv, Guanwei Cui, Yongsong Luo, Imran Shakir, Fengming Luo, Min Wu, Qi Yu, Haitao Peng, Xuping Sun, Bo Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although seawater electrolysis holds great promise for green hydrogen production, the persistent challenges of chloride ions (Cl−)-induced chemical corrosion and localized acid etching under high potential severely hinder the lifespan of the anode. Herein, we propose that Os nanoparticles anchored on CoP nanowires supported by Ni foam (Os-CoP/NF) acts as a dual-protection anode with proton-buffering and Cl−-repelling capabilities to simultaneously inhibit corrosion during seawater oxidation. The self-released PO43− not only repels Cl− but also mitigates the gradual decrease in local pH over reaction time. Meanwhile, the dispersed Os sites can bind with Cl− to form Os–Cl species, which inhibit chlorine chemistry corrosion via the common-ion repulsive effect. The designed anode achieves stable operation for up to 4500-h ampere-level real seawater electrolysis. Notably, this protection strategy safeguards the active sites from corrosion both during operation and after the shutdown of system. The assembled membrane electrode also exhibits a lifespan of 500 h for both continuous electrolysis and intermittent electrolysis, demonstrating an electrode design concept with broad application potential.

Introduction

Rapid economic growth should not be achieved at the expense of exacerbating the energy crisis. A transformative upgrade of the energy system has become an urgent necessity1. Hydrogen (H2), as a promising clean fuel, offers the advantages of clean combustion and high calorific value. In addition, H2 also functions as a therapeutic agent with precise antioxidant activity, selectively scavenging harmful oxygen radicals, and playing a key role in the physiological and pathological regulation of various diseases2,3. Green H2 produced by water electrolysis, as a sustainable method, attracts significant research attention4–9. Current commercial water electrolyzers still rely on ultrapure water electrolytes to ensure long-term durability, and their large-scale implementation could intensify freshwater scarcity, particularly in water-stressed regions. Seawater, as an almost inexhaustible resource, naturally serves as an electrolyte due to its high salt content. Direct seawater electrolysis holds great promise for accelerating the transition to a new energy structure in coastal regions10–18. Although seawater-to-H2 production (STHP) is highly promising, its complex composition, particularly the abundance of chloride ions (Cl−), can accelerate electrode degradation, thereby imposing much higher technical requirements compared to conventional pure water electrolysis. Cl− will suffer from evolution reaction (CER) at 1.72 V versus reversible hydrogen electrode (vs. RHE), and its derivatives (hypochlorite, ClO−) can continuously and aggressively corrode the electrode. Even in highly alkaline seawater, where the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) potential is 1.23 V vs. RHE, selective electrolysis without CER remains challenging. In particular, the potential of the STHP system, driven by industrial-grade current density (j), inevitably exceeds that of CER, facilitating more robust Cl− adsorption. Therefore, preventing the occurrence of CER becomes increasingly challenging at the ampere-level j. In addition, the catalyst structure also suffers from corrosion caused by localized proton (H+) accumulation observed in both fresh/seawater electrolysis, which further accelerates the degradation of its operational lifespan, especially at industrial-level j19–23. Thus, the combined corrosive effects of chlorine chemistry and localized H+ can significantly accelerate catalyst deactivation, making the development of effective strategies to address this scientific challenge highly urgent.

Various strategies have been proposed to enhance resistance to chlorine chemistry-induced corrosion, including the construction of Cl− repulsion barriers, tuning of electronic structures, and tailoring of local reaction environments14,24–31. Constructing anion barriers is considered an effective and straightforward strategy, which can inhibit the diffusion and migration of Cl− toward the catalyst surface through electrostatic repulsion. However, trace amounts of Cl− can still cross the barrier and adsorb onto active sites, leading to chlorine evolution and ClO− formation. As a result, such a single anion barrier strategy cannot fully suppress chlorine chemistry-induced corrosion. To effectively extend the catalytic lifespan, implementing a dual protection strategy that enhances the selective adsorption of Cl−, in addition to constructing an anion barrier, is highly desirable, as it contributes to the comprehensive suppression of CER. It should be noted that during alkaline seawater oxidation, the degradation of catalytic activity arises not only from Cl−/ClO− corrosion but also from acid corrosion caused by local H+ accumulation. Especially at ampere-level j, the massive consumption of OH− is accompanied by the generation of H+, leading to a sharp drop in local pH, which can trigger metal ions leaching and ultimately result in structural degradation of the catalyst.

Herein, we present Os nanoparticles anchored on CoP nanowires supported by Ni foam (Os-CoP/NF) featuring a nanoscale structure that incorporates a dual protection strategy, achieving highly stable electrolysis in alkaline seawater. In situ and ex situ characterization techniques reveal that the dual protection originates from the synergistic roles of PO43− and Os–Cl species. During the alkaline seawater oxidation process, the self-released PO43− not only functions as an anionic barrier to inhibit Cl− adsorption but also serves as a buffering layer to maintain the local pH in a weakly alkaline environment and prevent metal ion leaching. Additionally, Os nanoclusters exhibit Cl−-selective adsorption capability, forming Os–Cl species during seawater oxidation while repelling free Cl− via the common-ion effect. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations further reveal that PO43− and Os–Cl species can prevent Cl− from absorbing on CoOOH. Such dual protection enables the anode operating at ampere-level j for 4500 h without any activity degradation and trace ClO− formation. The fabricated anion exchange membrane water electrolyzer (AEMWE) also possesses better stable performance than that of a commercial Pt/Ru-based electrolyzer.

Results

Material characterization

Os nanoclusters were anchored onto the CoP nanoarray through a combination of hydrothermal synthesis and subsequent phosphidation (Os-CoP/NF). X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of CoP powder reveals its characteristic of CoP (JCPDS No. 29-0497) (Supplementary Fig. S1). As for Os-CoP powder, the XRD pattern shows new peaks belonging to Os (JCPDS No. 06-0662), indicating the successful incorporation of Os nanoclusters. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observations (Supplementary Fig. S2) reveal that Os-CoP nanowires become noticeably rougher compared to CoP, attributed to the surface anchoring of Os nanoclusters. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image reveals an individual Os-CoP nanowire with numerous nanoparticles distributed along its edges, which show the Os nanoparticles anchored at the CoP nanowire (Supplementary Fig. S3a). As shown in the High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image (Supplementary Fig. S3b), lattice spacings of 0.216 and 0.175 nm are observed, which can be assigned to the Os (002) plane and the CoP (103) plane, respectively. Elemental mappings containing Co, P, and Os obtained from energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. S4) show their uniform distribution throughout the whole Os-CoP nanowires. To investigate the effect of Os incorporation on the electronic structure of CoP, the electronic localization function (ELF) was used for deep analysis. As shown in Fig. 1a, compared with CoP, the introduction of Os nanoparticles reduces the ELF value of Co, suggesting its Lewis acidity is enhanced, which enables Co sites to act as stronger electron acceptors and enhances their interactions with oxygen-containing intermediates (*OH, *O, *OOH) based on the concept of hard and soft acids and bases, thus strengthening OER kinetics. In addition, we performed differential charge density mapping, combined with Bader charge analysis, to further elucidate the electronic modulation induced by Os (Supplementary Fig. S5). The differential charge density mapping clearly reveals electron accumulation around the Os atoms (yellow regions) and electron depletion around the Co sites (blue regions), indicating electron transfer from Co to Os. Bader charge analysis quantitatively confirms this trend, indicating a charge transfer of about 0.66e from CoP to the Os clusters. Consistent with the ELF analysis, this electron redistribution renders the Co sites electron-deficient, thereby enhancing the affinity for oxygenated intermediates and promoting faster OER kinetics. Meanwhile, the electron-rich Os exhibits a stronger affinity toward Cl−, providing the electronic basis for the formation of protective Os–Cl species. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was further conducted. The Co 2p high-resolution XPS profile reveals characteristic peaks corresponding to Co 2p3/2 (781.4 eV), Co 2p1/2 (797.7 eV), and Co–P (777.8 eV) bonds32 (Fig. 1b). Relative to CoP, the Os-CoP sample displays stronger peaks intensity, while their binding positions remain essentially unchanged., indicating that Os nanoclusters slightly affect the valence state of Co, and similar results also occurs from high-resolution XPS spectrum in P 2p region (Supplementary Fig. S6). It arises from Os exhibiting a higher electronegativity (2.2) than Co (1.88), promoting electron flow from Co to Os and consequently raising the Co oxidation state in Os-CoP. In the Os 4f region, the high-resolution XPS profile exhibits two peaks located at 50.3 and 52.9 eV (Fig. 1c), confirming the presence of metallic Os species in Os-CoP33, while no Os signal has been detected in pure CoP. The coordination environments of Co in CoP and Os-CoP are deeply analyzed using X-ray absorption spectroscopy. The X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra (Fig. 1d) reveal that the Co K-edge positions of both Os-CoP and CoP lie between those of Co foil and CoO, indicating that the average oxidation state of Co in both materials falls between Co0 and Co2+. Note that the absorption edge of Os-CoP is slightly higher than that of CoP, attributed to strong electron absorption of Os, resulting in a higher average oxidation state of Co (Fig. 1e). A noticeable feature at approximately 1.85 Å appears in the Fourier-transformed extended X-ray absorption fine structure (FT-EXAFS) profiles (Fig. 1f), corresponding to the Co–P bond34,35. Upon Os incorporation, a slight shift of the Co–P peak position is observed. Catalytic activity improvements

Given that overpotential serves as a crucial indicator of catalytic activity, the catalysts were first systematically evaluated in alkaline seawater. Os-CoP/NF demonstrates the highest catalytic activity, delivering a j of 1 A cm–2 at an overpotential of only 386 mV (Supplementary Fig. S7a), which is substantially lower than that of CoP/NF (476 mV) and RuO2/NF (570 mV). While bare NF lacks sufficient intrinsic active sites, making it difficult to achieve high j (1000 mA cm–2). In addition, according to the uncorrected LSV data (Supplementary Fig. S7b), their overpotentials follow the trends as NF (474 mV@10 mA cm–2) > CoP/NF (351 mV@10 mA cm–2) > RuO2/NF (274 mV@10 mA cm–2) > Os-CoP/NF (236 mV@10 mA cm–2). Moreover, Os-CoP/NF shows competitive performance compared with recently reported catalysts (Supplementary Table S1). With a Tafel slope of 52.3 mV dec–1, Os-CoP/NF outperforms CoP/NF, RuO2/NF, and bare NF, reflecting accelerated reaction kinetics and enhanced electrolysis potential (Supplementary Fig. S8). The enhanced OER activity can be ascribed to its improved conductivity (Supplementary Fig. S9) and larger electrochemically active surface area (Supplementary Fig. S10). The intrinsic activity of CoP/NF and Os-CoP/NF was further evaluated by analyzing the correlation between peak current and scan rate obtained from cyclic voltammetry (CV) at multiple scan rates (Supplementary Fig. S11a–d). Active-site numbers were obtained following the equation in the “Methods” section, from which the turnover frequency (TOF) was subsequently derived. At 0.35 V, Os-CoP/NF exhibits a TOF value of 0.642 s–1, which is significantly higher than that of CoP/NF (0.025 s–1), underscoring its better intrinsic catalytic performance (Supplementary Fig. S11e). The improved intrinsic activity and higher j of Os-CoP/NF at a given potential can be attributed to the incorporation of Os nanoclusters, which modulate the electronic structure by tuning the electron density around the Co centers. Operando Bode plots (Supplementary Fig. S12) clearly reveal that Os-CoP/NF and CoP/NF exhibit distinct transition peak behaviors in the frequency range of 10–2–100 Hz. Os-CoP/NF shows the smallest phase angle, indicating that more charges are involved in Faradaic processes, contributing to its modified electronic structure. As the potential increases, Os-CoP/NF gains more electrons and adsorbs hydroxyl species to facilitate the reaction. The rapid-decrease phase angle of Os-CoP/NF suggests a faster transition from capacitive to Faradaic behavior compared with CoP/NF, indicating more efficient charge transfer and intermediate evolution on the electrode surface. The earlier appearance of its transition peak reflects a faster deprotonation process of intermediates during the OER process. 18O-labeled differential electrochemical mass spectrometry was employed to probe the reaction pathways of Os-CoP and CoP. Both catalysts were first labeled in alkaline seawater containing 40% 18O-enriched water, followed by chronoamperometric testing in regular alkaline seawater. No 16O18O signal was detected for either catalyst in Supplementary Fig. S13, indicating that both Os-CoP and CoP follow the adsorbate evolution mechanism (AEM). Methanol oxidation reaction (MOR) experiments were further conducted using methanol as a molecular probe to evaluate the OH– adsorption capability of the catalysts. As presented in Supplementary Fig. S14, Os-CoP/NF exhibits higher MOR activity compared to CoP/NF, suggesting its stronger OH– adsorption ability, which facilitates the AEM pathway. With a reduced bubble adhesion force (~0.021 mN) compared to CoP/NF (~0.037 mN), Os-CoP/NF enables quicker bubble detachment (Supplementary Fig. S15). This effect becomes especially important at high j, where rapid bubble detachment keeps active sites accessible to the electrolyte and thus mitigates performance decay.

Dual-protection-promoted stable seawater oxidation

The Os nanocluster-induced modulation of electronic structure and electron density distribution not only enhances the intrinsic catalytic activity, as evidenced by LSV and TOF results, but also contributes to improved durability. As illustrated in Fig. 2a, Os-CoP/NF achieves continuous 4500-h operation at ampere-level j (1, 1.5, and 2 A cm–2), outperforming other reported anodes (Supplementary Table S2). However, CoP/NF only operates stably at 1 A cm−2 for about 430 h (Supplementary Fig. S16). High-resolution XPS spectra in the Cl 2p region of post-OER CoP and Os-CoP are shown in Supplementary Fig. S17. Note that the more obvious peak belonging to the Cl species of CoP can be found compared with Os-CoP, which demonstrates that amounts of Cl– have been absorbed on the CoP surface. The XPS spectrum in the Cl 2p region of Os-CoP may be attributed to surface Os–Cl species. The active chlorine content was detected by N,N-diethyl-p-phenylenediamine in seawater electrolytes after continuous seawater oxidation and quantified through a UV-visible-near-infrared spectrophotometer (Supplementary Fig. S18). Notably, the solution corresponding to Os-CoP/NF after 4500-h electrolysis exhibits only 12.9% of the active chlorine content observed for CoP/NF (430 h) (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. S19), indicating that ample Cl– has been oxidized by CoP/NF during alkaline seawater oxidation. In addition, Os-CoP/NF, after electrolysis of each state, shows a lower Co leaching (0.11, 0.114, and 0.119 ppm) compared with that of CoP/NF (0.30 ppm) (Fig. 2c). As shown in the Os 4 f spectrum (Supplementary Fig. S20), there is no appreciable binding energy shift or change in peak shape compared to the fresh sample, indicating that the average oxidation state of Os remains essentially unchanged. TEM image (Supplementary Fig. S21) shows that Os-CoP maintains its nanowire morphology after a 500-h stability test, indicating its structural integrity. Figure 2d illustrates the role of Os–Cl and PO43– protective layers on Os-CoP under working conditions, showing that in situ generated PO43– further reinforces the catalyst’s anti-corrosion ability by serving dual roles: first, PO43– carries three negative charges, resulting in forming a strong electrostatic network and effectively excluding free Cl– from the surface of CoOOH (real active site). Second, the PO43–/HPO42– transformation provides an H+-buffering layer, which effectively weakens acid corrosion caused by H+ accumulation at high j. Moreover, Os nanoclusters tend to absorb Cl– and form robust Os–Cl bonds, following which inhibits bulk Cl– approaching by strong common-ion repulsion effects. Such dual protection enables Os-CoP/NF to achieve a higher anti-corrosion potential (Ecorr. = 1.31 V) than that of CoP/NF (Ecorr. = 1.26 V), thus ensuring its long-term durability in seawater oxidation (Supplementary Fig. S22). For large-scale H2 production from seawater, the overall energy consumption of AEMWE is fundamentally governed by the catalytic activity and long-term stability of the electrodes (Fig. 2e). As shown in Supplementary Fig. S23, the symmetric AEM electrolyzer assembled using Os-CoP/NF as a bifunctional electrode exhibits improved performance compared to the commercial Pt/Ru-based electrolyzer. At the same j, the Os-CoP/NF-based device delivers a lower energy consumption with 2.4 V@0.5 A cm–2 than Pt/Ru-based electrolyzer (3.0 V@0.5 A cm–2), further confirming the bifunctional catalytic activity of Os-CoP/NF for both HER (Supplementary Fig. S24) and OER. Identification of PO43− barrier

PO43– possesses a favorable Z (charge number) × r (radius) value, offering an effective electrostatic repulsion to suppress undesired chlorine-related reactions. To quantitatively evaluate the contribution of PO43– for Ecorr., Co(OH)F/NF was selected as the model electrode. Sodium phosphate solutions of varying concentrations were added to alkaline seawater, and the relationship between Ecorr. and PO43– concentration was systematically monitored. As shown in Fig. 3a, Co(OH)F/NF exhibits a relatively low Ecorr. in the absence of any protective layer. Upon the initial addition of sodium phosphate, the corrosion potential increases significantly due to the strong electrostatic repulsion of the PO43– (Supplementary Fig. S29). However, with further increases in PO43– concentration, Ecorr. shows only a slow upward trend, suggesting that the electrostatic repulsion effect of PO43– gradually reaches “saturation”, which indicates that the anti-corrosion ability provided by a single anionic layer is limited. Distinct bands at a range of 800–1200 cm–1 are clearly resolved for the potential-dependent Raman spectra recorded from 1.325 to 1.825 V vs. RHE (Fig. 3b). The appearance of these peaks is attributed to the surface oxidation of P elements into phosphate species during the OER process. It notes that the interconversion among PO43–, HPO42–, and H2PO4– serves as a buffering system to regulate the local pH19,36–38, preventing excessive acidification at the surface of the electrode under high j. The ~980 cm–1 peak is assigned to the symmetric stretching of PO43– (PO4), while the higher-wavenumber peaks correspond to the protonated species HPO42– (PO3 + POH) and H2PO4– (PO2 + P(OH)2)38. At low potentials (below ~1.525 V), Os-CoP/NF shows an earlier and stronger appearance of the PO4 band compared with CoP/NF, indicating a greater ability to accumulate non-protonated phosphate species on its surface. With increasing potential, the PO4 signal gradually weakens, accompanied by the emergence and growth of the 1000–1150 cm–¹ peaks (HPO42–/H2PO4–), which reflects the stepwise protonation of PO43–. When the potential reaches ~1.625 V, CoP/NF exhibits more pronounced protonation, as evidenced by the stronger PO2 band. Additionally, operando surface-enhanced IR analysis was conducted to provide additional evidence for the evolution of phosphate species (Fig. 3c). A peak at ~1005 cm–1 belonging to the presence of PO43– is observed under open-circuit conditions37, due to surface oxidation of the phosphide. Upon raising the applied potential, the peak intensity of PO43– grows progressively, following the formation of HPO42– (~980 and ~1100 cm–1) and H2PO4– (1200 cm–1) species due to the H+ accumulation37. Importantly, at lower potentials, Os-CoP/NF exhibits a stronger peak intensity of PO43– than CoP/NF, indicating that more PO43– is generated on the Os-CoP/NF surface during the initial stage. With increasing potential, the degree of transformation of phosphate species after protonation varies due to the different surface PO43– contents. Among them, the CoP/NF surface is dominated by H2PO4–, whereas the Os-CoP/NF surface primarily contains HPO42–. These observations collectively reveal the evolution of phosphate species on the catalyst surface. Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) was used to probe the concentrations and distributions of PO43– in selected regions of the electrodes after activation (Fig. 3d). Compared with CoP, the Os-CoP surface exhibits a higher PO43– concentration, demonstrating that Os promotes its formation. Stability tests conducted in alkaline freshwater reveal that Os-CoP/NF exhibits superior stability compared to Os-Co(OH)F/NF at 2 A cm–2 (Supplementary Fig. S30a). Under such conditions, it will accelerate the consumption of OH– and the generation of H+, leading to a drop in local pH and subsequent electrode corrosion. Fortunately, the presence of an H+-buffering layer formed by PO43– effectively suppresses local acidification, thereby enabling prolonged electrolysis with Os-CoP/NF19,39–41. Therefore, Os-CoP/NF processes a 70-h lifespan without any activity degradation and Co leaching (0.002 ppm), which is more stable than Os-Co(OH)F/NF (about 4 h and 0.071-ppm Co leaching) (Supplementary Fig. S30b). To more precisely investigate the H+ buffering behavior, Na2CO3 was employed as a pH probe to monitor the change of local pH on the catalyst with increasing potential42,43. Supplementary Fig. S31 presents the evolution of CO32– under different pH conditions. At pH = 13, a prominent characteristic peak was observed at approximately 1063 cm–1, corresponding to the CO32–. As the pH decreased, a weaker peak gradually appeared at 1016 cm–1, assigned to the HCO3–, indicating the progressive protonation of CO32– under more acidic conditions and reflecting a significant change in local pH. 0.05 M Na2CO3 was introduced into alkaline seawater, and Raman spectroscopy under reaction conditions was applied to track dynamic transformations of CO32–/HCO3– adsorbed on the surfaces of Os-Co(OH)F/NF and Os-CoP/NF catalysts. As shown in Fig. 3e, a weak peak emerges around 1016 cm−1 with increasing time (Os-Co(OH)F/NF), indicative of the protonation of surface-bound CO32– to form HCO3– 44,45. These observations suggest a continuous decline in local pH at the surface of Os-Co(OH)F/NF with increasing potential, reflecting a localized acidification phenomenon. In contrast, no characteristic peak corresponding to HCO3– can be observed from Os-CoP/NF. More importantly, the peak at around 1070 cm−1 exhibits only a slight attenuation with increasing potential, indicating that the catalyst surface maintained a relatively alkaline local environment. Additionally, the phosphate-sensitive pH characteristics were also utilized to monitor the interfacial acidity of Os-Co(OH)F/NF using in situ Raman spectroscopy. The Raman spectra show the gradual emergence of bands at 856, 937, and 990 cm−1 over time, which are assigned to P(OH), PO4, and PO3 species, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S32)38. This confirms that, in the absence of a buffering layer, significant accumulation of H+ occurs at the electrode surface, leading to severe protonation of the phosphate species. Based on the in situ Raman results, the trend illustrated in the schematic of Fig. 3f can be established: the presence of PO43– acts as an H+ buffering layer, effectively mitigating local acidification under high j. Selective Cl– adsorption on Os nanoclusters

In recent years, some studies have shown that Cl– in seawater not only does not corrode the catalyst structure but can also enhance its catalytic activity and stability30,46–48. Cl– can adsorb on noble metal active sites to form stable M–Cl species, which not only suppress the over-oxidation and dissolution of noble metals but also construct a protective layer with a common-ion repulsion effect. This layer effectively hinders further Cl– adsorption and promotes the generation of more *OH intermediates, thereby enhancing catalytic activity and stability. In addition, the Os–Cl coordination environment may similarly influence the local ion distribution within the electric double layer30. The presence of Os–Cl species generates a local common-ion environment that can electrostatically and chemically repel additional Cl– from approaching the electrode surface. Operando and ex situ analyses revealed the role of Os nanoclusters in stabilizing active sites during freshwater and seawater electrolysis. As evidenced by in situ Raman spectroscopy in alkaline freshwater (Fig. 4a), Os-CoP/NF requires a lower potential (~1.425 V vs. RHE) to form CoOOH under an applied electric field compared to CoP/NF (~1.525 vs. RHE), indicating that Os facilitates the generation of active species. This enhancement leads to a greater abundance of active phases at higher potentials, thereby promoting the conversion of OH– to O2. Notably, in alkaline seawater, the formation of Os–Cl bonds similarly promotes the generation of hydroxide species, suggesting that Cl– adsorption is beneficial for improving OER catalytic activity. These observations are consistent with previous reports46,47. As depicted in Fig. 4b, in situ Raman spectra evidenced the formation of Os–Cl (302 cm–1) bonds as the potential increased from OCP to 1.525 V vs. RHE49, demonstrating that free Cl– in the bulk phase preferentially adsorbed onto Os sites under the applied electric field. This adsorption behavior triggered a common-ion effect, which served to further repel Cl–. Supplementary Fig. S33 presents the XPS data in the Os 4 f region after stability tests, revealing the valence state evolution of Os in Os-CoP. Before seawater oxidation, the characteristic peaks of Os appear at 50.3 and 52.9 eV, corresponding to metallic Os0. Interestingly, even after 10-h or 100-h seawater oxidation, the peak positions show no significant shift, suggesting that Os remains unoxidized and in its metallic state. Based on the in situ Raman and ex situ XPS results, we speculate that the stability of the metallic Os state arises from its preferential adsorption of Cl– rather than OH–, which inhibits its oxidation13. Simultaneously, post-X-ray absorption spectra further confirm the existence of Os–Cl coordination. The Os L3-edge XANES spectrum (Supplementary Fig. S34) exhibits an edge position and white-line intensity lying between those of Os foil and K2OsCl6. This intermediate spectral feature indicates that Os in Os-CoP is not present exclusively in an Os–Os coordinated environment. Instead, the deviation from the metallic Os reference suggests the coexistence of Os–Cl coordination, consistent with partial coordination of Os atoms by chloride ligands in Os-CoP. Moreover, EXAFS fitting identifies coordination shells containing both Os–Os (bond distance = 2.7 Å) and Os–Cl (bond distance = 2.4 Å). These results provide direct experimental evidence for the coexistence of Os–Os and Os–Cl bonds (Fig. 4c). Density functional theory calculations indicate that OH– and Cl– exhibit markedly different adsorption preferences on various sites of the Os–CoOOH surface. As shown in Fig. 4d, Cl– preferentially adsorbs on the Os sites, exhibiting a significantly lower adsorption energy compared to OH–, indicating a strong Cl– affinity of Os. In contrast, OH– shows stronger adsorption on the Co sites, with a more negative adsorption energy than that of Cl–. This site-selective adsorption behavior suggests that the introduction of Os not only helps suppress the competitive adsorption of Cl– on the Co sites but also effectively diverts Cl– away from the key catalytic centers. As a result, this enhances the overall chloride corrosion resistance while preserving OER activity. Subsequently, we evaluated catalysts’ lifespans in alkaline freshwater with varying concentrations of NaCl (Supplementary Fig. S35). The results show that as the Cl– concentration increased, the lifespan of electrodes generally decreased (Fig. 4e). Theoretical investigation

To further explore the enhanced Cl–-repulsion mechanism, MD simulations were employed to investigate the distribution of Cl– over the active sites under working conditions. The relevant details of MD simulations are provided in Supplementary Information (Supplementary Table S3). Figure 5a illustrates the spatial and distance distribution characteristics of Cl– at different modified CoOOH (Blank, PO43–, Os–Cl, and PO43–/Os–Cl). The upper atomic model diagrams visually present the distribution profile of various ions in an aqueous environment (1 M KOH + 0.5 M NaCl) at an applied potential of 1.5 V vs. RHE, and the lower charts quantitatively describe the distance distribution of Cl– relative to the catalytic surface. It notes that a large number of Cl– accumulates on the CoOOH surface (within 0–5 Å) without any protective layer, indicating direct adsorption interactions between Cl– and CoOOH, forming an electrochemical corrosion layer. Upon introducing PO43–, the distribution of Cl– over CoOOH is markedly reduced, which demonstrates that PO43– exerts an electrostatic barrier for Cl– adsorption, which inhibits direct interaction between Cl– and CoOOH. As for the Os–Cl modified interface, compared with the blank interface, the enrichment of Cl– has been further reduced within 2.5 Å but enhanced between 5 and 10 Å. This indicates that Os sites possess strong adsorption for Cl–, promoting the formation of stable Os–Cl species, which helps suppress further migration of near-surface Cl– towards CoOOH, thereby mitigating electrode corrosion. When PO43– and Os–Cl coexist synergistically (PO43–/Os–Cl), the synergistic effect effectively prevents Cl– attack and maintains the lowest Cl– concentration within the near-surface region of CoOOH (~5 Å), thereby ensuring the long-term stability of the catalyst under ampere-level j. Compared with the results with an added electric field, MD simulations under electric field-free conditions exhibit similar trends in Cl– distribution. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S36a, MD simulations further modeled the distribution of the ions near the catalyst surface without any applied electric field to evaluate the role of the protective layer in a power-off situation. Even if the kinetics of Cl– migration are constrained without the driving force of an external electric field, the concentration of Cl– near the CoOOH surface (within 5 Å) is significantly higher than that of OH–, promoting the loss of active sites. Thanks to the formation of PO43– or Os–Cl species during alkaline seawater oxidation, the Cl– distribution near the CoOOH weakens significantly, attributed to the electrostatic force repulsion or common-ion effect of PO43– or Os–Cl species (Supplementary Fig. S36b, c). In the synergistic system containing both PO43– and Os–Cl under zero-potential conditions (Supplementary Fig. S36d), the concentration of Cl– near the surface has been reduced further, thus demonstrating the synergistic protective mechanism of the surface modifications (PO43– and Os–Cl). Therefore, such dual protection promotes anode exhibits excellent stability for both continuous and intermittent seawater electrolysis. Furthermore, to complement the MD analysis, DFT calculations were conducted to compare the reaction energy barriers of the chlorine oxidation reaction (ClOR) pathway on four models (Supplementary Fig. S37). Before performing the DFT theoretical calculation, we conducted relevant convergence tests to ensure accuracy (Supplementary Fig. S38). Among them, PO43–/Os–Cl@CoOOH, which integrates both Os selective adsorption and surface PO43– modification, exhibits a significantly higher ClOR energy barrier (4.24 eV) compared to CoOOH (3.77 eV), which lacks any protective strategy (Fig. 5b). This result suggests that the dual Cl–-repelling design, through common-ion effect and electrostatic repulsion, can synergistically inhibit Cl– adsorption and oxidation at the active sites. As a result, the thermodynamic feasibility of the ClOR pathway is greatly reduced, thereby enhancing the catalyst’s resistance to Cl– corrosion. It noted that local pH would change the pathway of Cl– evolution (acid/neutral medium) or oxidation (alkaline medium), the Gibbs free energy of CER (Supplementary Fig. S39) is much lower than ClOR for PO43–/Os–Cl@CoOOH, demonstrating the importance of maintaining a local alkaline environment. In addition, DFT calculations of the OER pathway revealed that PO43–/Os–Cl@CoOOH also exhibits the lowest reaction energy barrier (1.55 eV) for oxygen evolution among all models (Fig. 5c). It indicates that the co-modification with Os–Cl and PO43– not only suppresses the undesired ClOR side reaction but also optimizes the OER reaction pathway, resulting in improved OER activity. The dual Cl–-repelling design effectively improves anti-corrosion ability and activity, making it a promising strategy for stable seawater oxidation. To elucidate the interfacial origin of the enhanced seawater oxidation stability, we analyzed the interaction energies of essential OER intermediates and the associated Pourbaix diagrams for each catalyst. Supplementary Fig. S40a–d show that pristine CoOOH suffers from overly strong *O adsorption, whereas Os–Cl and PO43– respectively weaken *O binding and promote *OH formation. The dual-modified PO43–/Os–Cl surface achieves the most balanced *OH–*O–*OOH energetics, closely matching the ideal OER pathway (Supplementary Fig. S40d). Consistently, Pourbaix diagrams (Supplementary Fig. S40e–h) reveal that dual regulation suppresses *O over-coverage and prevents Cl⁻ insertion. Discussion

Our study demonstrates an anodic catalyst with a dual-protection mechanism that exhibits robust 4500-h alkaline seawater electrolysis at continuous and various ampere-level j. The key protective features include a spontaneously formed PO43– layer and the generation of Os–Cl species. The PO43–-rich anionic protection layer not only serves as a buffering barrier to prevent localized acid etching but also effectively blocks Cl– permeation, thereby mitigating chlorine chemistry-induced corrosion. Meanwhile, Os nanoclusters preferentially adsorb Cl– rather than OH– during seawater oxidation, through the common-ion effect, further enhancing Cl– repulsion and corrosion resistance. Benefiting from the synergistic effects of these protective mechanisms, the catalyst effectively maintains a locally weakly alkaline environment, suppresses Cl– adsorption, and inhibits the formation of ClO– species. This work not only provides us with a high-performance and durable anode material but also offers a universal methodology for the targeted design and synthesis of transition metal nanocatalysts for efficient saline water electrolysis.

Methods

Synthesis of Os-Co(OH)F/NF

NF was first cut into 2 × 4 cm2 and soaked in HCl (0.5 M) for 15 min. Then, NF was thoroughly washed multiple times with deionized water and ethanol. The processed NF was put into 30 mL solution (24 mL DI water + 4 mL ethyl alcohol) containing 2 mmol CoCl2·6H2O, 1 mmol NH4F, 0.1 mmol OsCl3, and 3 mmol CO(NH2)2. Then, the medium, together with NF, was transferred to an autoclave and reacted at 120 °C for 6 h. The sample is named Os-Co(OH)F/NF. The Co(OH)F/NF was obtained in the same way but without OsCl3.

Synthesis of Os-CoP/NF

In a tubular furnace, 0.5 g of NaH2PO2 was placed upstream and the as-prepared Os-Co(OH)F/NF downstream. The reaction proceeded at 350 °C for a duration of 2 h. After the temperature cooled down in an Ar atmosphere, Os-CoP/NF was obtained. It shows a loading of ~2.3 mg cm−2. CoP/NF was obtained in the same way and showed a loading of ~2.1 mg cm−2

Synthesis of commercial counterparts

5 mg of RuO2 or Pt/C was ultrasonically dispersed in a mixture of Nafion, ethanol, and deionized water in a volume ratio of 1:16.2:16.2 for 30 min to form a homogeneous ink (final concentration: 5 mg mL–1). A total of 300 μL of the prepared ink was deposited onto the cleaned 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm NF electrode, giving a loading of 1.5 mg.

Characterizations

The morphology of the samples was observed by SEM (Zeiss Gemini 300). Detailed microstructural characterization was carried out using TEM (Zeiss Libra 200FE), which provided morphological information, while HRTEM (FEI Tecnai G2 F20) was employed to resolve lattice fringes and atomic-scale features. LabX XRD-6100 (40 kV, 30 mA) and XPS (ESCALABMK II) were used to analyze the phase structure and chemical composition of the samples. Raman spectroscopy was recorded in 20 mL of 1 M KOH or 1 M KOH + seawater through the 532-nm-laser Lab RAM HR Evolution confocal microscope. Co leaching of Os-Co(OH)F/NF and Os-CoP/NF in alkaline freshwater/seawater during the reaction was determined by ICP-OES (iCAD7400). X-ray absorption spectroscopy was measured through the Ge (800) spherically bent crystal analyzers and the R250 mm Rowland circle. UV-vis-NIR spectroscopy was used to measure the absorbance of the samples.

Calculation methods

The computations employed spin-polarized DFT using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP). Exchange–correlation was described using the PBE form of the generalized gradient approximation, while the PAW approach was employed for electron–ion interactions. A 400 eV plane-wave cutoff was applied, and the geometry was optimized until the forces on each atom were under 0.02 eV Å−1. The CoP was modeled as a bilayer slab with a bottom layer fixed at its optimal block position in a (2 × 4) supercell. The (2 × 2 × 1) Monkhorst–Pack k-point sampling in the Brillouin zone was used for structural optimization and total-energy calculation of the CoP surface. CoOOH was modeled as a bilayer slab with a bottom layer fixed at its optimal block position in a (2 × 1) supercell. Structural optimization and total-energy evaluations of the CoOOH surface were performed using a (2 × 2 × 1) Monkhorst–Pack k-point mesh. To more accurately describe the strong correlation effects of the Co 3 d orbitals, we introduced a DFT + U correction on the Co atoms using the Hubbard U (U = 3.5) value. The VASPsol implicit solvation model (parameters: LSOL = .TRUE., EB_K = 78.4, LAMBDA_D_K = 3.0) was applied to all systems to account for the long-range electrostatic effects of bulk solvents. Data analysis and visualization were carried out with the help of the VASPKIT51 code and VESTA52. Considering the strongly acidic environment, the pH was set to zero in this study. Under standard conditions, the free energy of H+ and e− in an aqueous solution was set equal to 1/2 gaseous H2.

Molecular dynamics simulations

To understand ion behavior at the catalyst interface, MD simulations were employed. A model of the catalyst surface was constructed, upon which a mixed electrolyte solution comprising 1 M KOH and 0.5 M NaCl was introduced. The initial structural parameters are summarized in Supplementary Table S3. In the simulation setup, all catalyst atoms were held fixed, while only the solute and solvent particles were permitted to move freely. All simulations employed periodic boundary conditions in the x, y, and z dimensions. Water molecules were represented using the SPC/E model, with force field parameters for Na+, K+, Cl−, and OH− 53,54. The catalyst atoms were modeled with the Universal Force Field (UFF), and their charge distribution was evaluated via the charge equilibration (QEq) method. A conjugate-gradient–based energy minimization was applied, allowing up to 2000 optimization steps, employing a force convergence criterion of 10−6 kcal mol−1 Å−1 and an energy threshold set to 10−4 of the total system energy. Subsequently, a 500 ps equilibration was conducted under the NVT ensemble at 300 K, followed by a 1000 ps production run for trajectory collection and subsequent analysis. All systems were evaluated under a constant 1.5 V vs. RHE to simulate the electrode interface conditions. All molecular dynamics simulations were performed using the LAMMPS package.
