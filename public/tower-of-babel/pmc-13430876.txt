Menopause comfort scale: a scale development study
Authors: Gamze Goke Arslan, Nuran Nur Aypar Akbag, Yasemin Sanli, Sebnem Cinar Yucel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMenopause is a transitional period. A large proportion of women experience severe menopausal symptoms, which affect their quality of daily life and comfort. Improving the well-being and comfort of women who are affected physically, socio-culturally, psycho-spiritually, and environmentally during menopause is of extreme importance for nurses. The aim in conducting the present study was to develop the Menopause Comfort Scale to determine the comfort levels of menopausal women and to perform its validity and reliability study in Turkish.MethodsIn this descriptive, comparative, and methodological study, it was aimed to develop the Menopause Comfort Scale and to analyze its psychometric properties. In the sample, 423 women who met the inclusion criteria and volunteered to participate in the study were included. The Descriptive Information Form and the Menopause Comfort Scale were used to collect data.ResultsIn this study, the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) coefficient was calculated as 0.782. The Bartlett’s test of sphericity yielded the following result: χ² = 821.787 p = 0.000. As a result of the exploratory factor analysis, the 11 items were grouped under 4 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1.00. The overall scale explained 71.314% of the total (common) variance. The confirmatory factor analysis yielded the following: fit indices: (χ²/df = 1.34), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA): 0.04, Normed Fit Index (NFI): 0.93, Comparative Fit Index (CFI): 0.98 and Goodness of Fit Index (GFI): 0.96. The reliability coefficient of the scale was calculated as α = 0.814.ConclusionsIn conclusion, the Menopause Comfort Scale developed in this study is a valid and reliable measurement tool, and it can be administered to assess the comfort levels of menopausal women in psychological, environmental, physical, and social dimensions.

Background

Menopause is a life stage characterized by the loss of reproductive ability due to the decline of follicular activity in the ovaries, resulting in the permanent cessation of menstruation [1, 2]. A diagnosis of menopause requires at least 12 months of amenorrhea [2, 3]. In the United States of America, the age range for the start of menopause is 39–59 years, and the average age for entering menopause is 51 [4]. In Türkiye, the average age of menopause is approximately 47–49 years [2, 5]. The age of entering menopause not only varies from country to country, but is also affected by such factors as smoking, low body mass index, low income and education level, early menarche, and nulliparity [6].

Menopause is a transitional period consisting of the following stages: premenopausal, menopausal, and postmenopausal [7]. Menopausal symptoms usually begin in the perimenopausal period, 1–2 years before the last menstrual period. While these symptoms can last for an average of seven years in some individuals, vasomotor symptoms can persist for up to 15 years after the transition to menopause in 20% of women [8]. During this period, physiological symptoms such as hot flashes, night sweats, headaches, and palpitations, in addition to psychological symptoms such as difficulty concentrating, depression, forgetfulness, panic attacks, and irritability may occur due to decreased estrogen secretion [9, 10]. If the estrogen deficiency is moderate, not only complaints such as dryness and cracking of the skin, impaired skin moisture and loss of tone, sensitivity, brittle nails, hair loss, and loss of muscle, bone and joint strength, but also urogenital complaints such as frequent urination, vaginal dryness, and dysuria occur [11]. In long-term estrogen deficiency of menopause, women may develop cardiovascular diseases such as myocardial infarction, angina pectoris and peripheral vascular diseases, and osteoporosis. Moreover, they also experience psychological and social effects due to children’s leaving home, increased dependency, the feeling of losing the female role, retirement feelings, and loss of a spouse as they age [2, 6, 12]. It is reported that 80–90% of women experience some of these symptoms of menopause, and one-third of those who experience these symptoms report them as severe, affecting their quality of daily life and comfort [13]. Providing a suitable environment and enhancing comfort to improve the health and well-being of women who suffer from physical, sociocultural, psychospiritual, and environmental effects of menopause during the menopausal period is crucial for nurses [14].

The word “comfort” which originates from French word “conforter” stemming from Latin word “confortāre” is defined in dictionaries as ease that facilitates daily life [15]. Comfort theory, based on the act of relaxation, which forms the basis of nursing interventions, was developed by Kolcaba [15, 16]. In her comfort theory, Kolcaba defines comfort as solving physical, socio-cultural, psychospiritual, and environmental dimensions of problems by meeting needs of a patient at three levels: relief, ease, and transcendence [15].

While various instruments such as The Menopause Rating Scale and the Menopause-Specific Quality of Life Questionnaire are widely used to evaluate the menopausal period, these tools primarily focus on the frequency and severity of pathological symptoms or the functional limitations caused by them [17, 18]. However, menopause is not merely a collection of symptoms to be managed; it is a holistic life transition that affects a woman’s comfort across physical, psychospiritual, environmental, and sociocultural dimensions. Comfort, a desirable outcome for menopausal women, is also a key goal to be achieved in nursing care. According to Kolcaba’s Comfort Theory, when an individual’s comfort needs are met, their capacity to engage in health-seeking behaviors is enhanced [15]. In this context, a menopausal woman’s holistic sense of being ‘at ease’ may strengthen her capacity for transcendence in managing symptoms, thereby positively influencing her overall quality of life. Among the fundamental tasks of nurses are treating the woman holistically, identifying her comfort needs, and planning, implementing and evaluating appropriate nursing interventions to address her unmet needs. To support these clinical objectives, the Menopause Comfort Scale was developed as a patient-reported outcome measure. While the scale is self-administered by women to capture their subjective lived experiences directly, it provides an essential evidence-based foundation for nurses and other healthcare professionals to provide holistic support throughout the menopausal transition.

A review of national and international literature reveals that there is no specific instrument designed to measure ‘comfort’ based on holistic nursing frameworks. While existing scales generally view menopause as a sum of ‘problems or disease manifestations’ (symptoms), the Menopause Comfort Scale focuses on how well, peaceful, and empowered (comfort) a woman feels during this process. Consequently, rather than focusing on what is deficient or negative, this scale provides a more positive and supportive measurement criterion for nursing care, centering on how women can be supported and comforted.

Aim

The aim in conducting the present study was to develop the Menopause Comfort Scale (MCS) to determine the comfort levels of menopausal women and to perform its validity and reliability study in Turkish.

Research hypotheses

H1

The Menopause Comfort Scale is a valid scale for assessing the comfort levels of menopausal women.

H2

The Menopause Comfort Scale is a reliable scale for assessing the comfort levels of menopausal women.

Methods

Study design

In the present study, methodological and cross-sectional design was used to develop the MCS and to examine its psychometric properties.

Study population and sample

The recommended sample size for methodological research should be a minimum of five-fold or ideally ten-fold the number of the items in a scale [19, 20]. The MCS was developed by the researchers. Since the MCS has 17 items, it was considered that it would be appropriate to include 85 to 170 participants in the sample to examine its psychometric properties. However, to compensate withdrawals and/or losses during the study and to enhance the validity and reliability of the results, it was decided to include a greater number of women in the sample, and thus 423 who met the inclusion criteria and agreed to participate were included in the study. The study population consisted of all women aged 39–65, in the pre-/peri-/post-menopausal period and accessible through online social media platforms (WhatsApp, Twitter, Instagram, Facebook, Pinterest, and Snapchat) throughout Türkiye between January 2023 and January 2024. The Menopause Comfort Scale aims to assess the holistic comfort of women throughout the entire menopausal transition, rather than being limited to the post-menopausal phase. Therefore, the following clinical definitions were adopted to categorize the stages: Pre-menopause was defined as having regular menstrual cycles; Peri-menopause as having menstrual irregularities or changes in cycle length within the past 12 months; and post-menopause as the absence of menstruation for at least 12 consecutive months [3]. The inclusion of women from all stages of the menopausal transition was a deliberate methodological choice to ensure the scale’s sensitivity in identifying comfort needs from the earliest stages and to support its utility in early nursing interventions.

Inclusion criteria

Being in the age group of 39–65 years, being in the pre-/peri-/post-menopausal period, having at least a primary school education, being able to comprehend and read Turkish, and volunteering to participate in the study.

Women with a history of surgical menopause were excluded from the study.

Data collection tools

Descriptive information form

The form prepared by researchers based on the literature is administered to question the participating women’s socio-demographic characteristics (age, marital status, education level, and employment status), age at menarche, menopausal status and use of medications for menopausal symptoms [2, 6, 21].

Menopause Comfort Scale (MCS)

Prior to the preparation of the items of the MCS, the researchers thoroughly reviewed Kolcaba’s Comfort Theory [15]. Based on the concept of comfort, and analysis of menopause-related scales in the literature, a pool of 25 items was created [15, 21–23]. Each item was conceptualized across the four contexts of Kolcaba’s Comfort Theory: physical (focusing on physical conditions such as pain, sleep, and constipation), psychospiritual (focusing on faith, inner peace, and emotional balance), environmental (focusing on the suitability of the physical surroundings, such as ambient temperature and odor), and sociocultural (focusing on interpersonal relationships and support systems, such as loneliness and the social environment). To rate the responses given to the items, a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5 was used (1 = Strongly Disagree, 2 = Disagree, 3 = Undecided, 4 = Agree, 5 = Strongly Agree). In the scale, no items are reverse scored. To determine the appropriateness of the scale items in terms of language and content, the draft version of the scale was prepared after obtaining feedback from 10 nursing academics and faculty members who have studies on women’s health or Comfort Theory, and a linguist.

The content validity of the scale was evaluated using the Lawshe’s method [24]. According to the Lawshe’s method, experts rate the validity of the scale by giving one of the following responses for each item: “Necessary”, “Useful but unnecessary”, and “Unnecessary” [25]. Thus, to determine the content validity of the scale, the opinions of 10 experts were obtained. Lawshe prepared a table to determine the numerical value of the content validity ratio (CVR), called the “minimum acceptable content validity ratio value”. The result obtained after the calculation is compared with the criteria in the table according to the number of experts. If the number is greater than that in the table, it indicates that the item has an acceptable level for statistical significance (p < 0.05). If the CVR values are negative and lower than the value in the content validity table, it is interpreted as a lack of agreement, and these items are removed from the scale [25]. According to the Lawshe’s method, in scale development studies, the lowest CVR value for 10 experts should be 0.62 at a significance level of p˂0.05 [24]. After the items to be included in the scale are finalized, the Content Validity Index (CVI) is calculated. For the scale, the CVI is the average of the content validity ratios of all the items. If the CVI is greater than the CVR value determined according to the number of experts (CVI > 0.62), the overall content validity of the scale is interpreted as statistically significant [24, 25]. After the evaluations, the number of items in the draft scale was reduced from 25 to 17. Items that were conceptually overlapping, linguistically ambiguous, or did not directly reflect comfort specific to the menopausal transition were removed based on expert feedback. For instance, items assessing general health status rather than menopause-specific comfort, and items with low discriminant power regarding menopausal experiences, such as those related to ambient odor, were excluded to enhance the scale’s content validity and specificity. The 17-item version of the scale was again presented to the same experts to obtain their opinions. After the Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) were performed, 6 more items were removed; thus, the final form of the MCS included 11 items.

There are no reverse-scored items in the scale. The initial 25-item pool included both positively and negatively worded statements. However, during the content validity stage and the subsequent EFA, items describing positive comfort states were excluded due to low factor loadings and their weakening effect on internal consistency. This process resulted in a unidirectional scale focused on ‘discomfort-inducing’ factors. Although the absence of reverse-scored items may suggest a potential for acquiescence bias, this unidirectional structure emerged as a natural outcome of the validity and reliability analyses conducted to preserve the scale’s structural integrity and conceptual homogeneity. Consequently, higher total scores on the scale indicate decreased menopausal comfort (increased complaints).

Pilot application

To assess the clarity, readability, and interpretability of the items in the scale, it is recommended to conduct a pilot study with 30 participants meeting the inclusion criteria [26]. Accordingly, a pilot study was conducted with 30 women aged 39–65 years in the pre-/peri-/post-menopausal period. The women participating in the pilot study were not included in the study sample. Following the administration of the draft scale, brief face-to-face interviews were conducted with each participant to evaluate whether any items were perceived as ambiguous or culturally inappropriate. It took each respondent in the pilot study approximately 10–15 min to fill in the scale. Qualitative feedback from the pilot group indicated that the items were clear, understandable, and relevant to their experiences. Since no negative feedback or suggestions for modification were received regarding the wording or sentence structure, the draft version was finalized without further changes.

Data collection

Women invited to participate in the study via online social media platforms (WhatsApp, Twitter, Instagram, Facebook, Pinterest, and Snapchat) were provided with an informational text outlining the purpose of the study and inclusion and exclusion criteria before they were allowed to access the data collection forms. After the participant marked the “I Agree” option online, the data collection forms became accessible for them. Neither personal information nor email addresses were collected. All responses given to the data collection forms were stored anonymously. The first page of the online data collection forms included an informed consent form, accessible only after participants selected “I Agree.” The study data were collected based on the self-reports of the participants, ensuring that the findings reflect the women’s subjective experiences directly. It took each participant approximately 10 min to complete the data collection forms.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed using the SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) version 25, and AMOS (Analysis of Moment Structures) version 24.0. Descriptive statistics were presented as arithmetic mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage.

Content validity

For content validity, the CVR and CVI were calculated.

Construct validity

To assess the construct validity of the scale, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) were performed by randomly splitting the sample. EFA is important for scale development because with it, the underlying factor structure is identified and the minimum number of factors required to explain the relationships between items is determined [27, 28]. EFA was performed using the sample group from the first half (n = 210). The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test was performed to assess the adequacy of the sample size, and the Bartlett’s test of sphericity was performed to assess the suitability of the dataset for factor analysis. The Direct Oblimin rotation method was used for EFA [20, 27, 29]. The four factors identified by EFA were named based on Kolcaba’s Comfort Theory [15].

Before CFA was performed, a multicollinearity was performed using the AMOS program. The Variance Inflation Factor (VIF < 10) and tolerance values (> 0.2) were examined [27, 30, 31]. It was determined that the VIF value was less than 10 and the tolerance values were greater than 0.2, indicating no multicollinearity issues among the variables.

A significance level of 0.05 was accepted for all statistical tests. Chi-square (χ2), degrees of freedom (df), χ2/df ratio, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Normed Fit Index (NFI), and Goodness of Fit Index (GFI) were used to assess model fit.

In addition to all validity analyses performed, an independent samples t-test was conducted to evaluate the construct validity and consistency of the scale across different menopausal stages.

Reliability analyses

To assess the reliability of the scale, correlation coefficients, split-half reliability analysis (Spearman-Brown and Guttman split-half coefficients), internal consistency analysis, and item statistics were performed. To ensure that the items were equally distributed and that they represented the overall scale, odd-numbered items (I1, I3, I5, I7, I9, I11) formed the first half, and even-numbered items (I2, I4, I6, I8, I10) formed the second half. Internal consistency was assessed by calculating the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient separately for the overall scale and for the dimensions. Additionally, Hotelling’s T2 test was used to evaluate the response bias and to determine whether there was a significant difference between the item means. Hotelling’s T2 test is used in hypothesis testing to determine whether the mean vector of a multivariate normal distribution is equal to a constant value. A significant Hotelling’s T2 test result indicates that the means of the items in the scale are not equal, suggesting that participants differentiated between the items rather than giving uniform responses [32]. While this result provides evidence that the scale items were perceived differently by the participants, it was evaluated as a preliminary indicator of the absence of a systematic response bias.

Results

Results regarding the descriptive characteristics of the participating women

The mean age of the participating women was 47.92 ± 6.87 (Min-Max = 39–65) years. Of them, 85.3% (n = 361) were married, 29.8% (n = 126) were high school graduates, and 56% (n = 237) were not employed. Their mean age at menarche was 13.18 ± 1.40 (Min-Max = 9–16) years. Of them, 57.4% (n = 243) had not yet entered menopause, and 96.7% (n = 409) did not use any medication for menopause. In those who had entered menopause [53.9% (n = 97)], at least 3 years had passed since their last menstrual period.

Results regarding the validity of the scale

Content validity of the menopause comfort scale

The MCS item pool consists of 25 items. Eight items whose CVR values were between 0.20 and 0.40 were removed from the scale, and five items whose CVR values were between 0.60 and 0.80 were revised. The CVI value of the MCS was calculated as 0.70. The results of the content validity analysis demonstrated that the scale with 17 items was ready for exploratory factor analysis.

Exploratory factor analysis of the menopause comfort scale

Data obtained from 210 participants randomly selected from 423 participants in the sample were used for the Exploratory Factor Analysis. Before the EFA and CFA analyses were started, in determining the adequacy of the sample size, the KMO coefficient was calculated as 0.782 and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was determined as χ2 = 821.787 p = 0.000. These results indicated that the database had a sufficient sample size for exploratory factor analysis.

After the first EFA was performed, 6 items with factor loadings below 0.40, item-total correlations below 0.30, and differences smaller than 0.10 factor loadings, which were considered as overlapping factors, were removed from the scale. As a result of the revised EFA, it was observed that 11 items were grouped under 4 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1.00, which were labeled as “F1 = Psychological Comfort”, “F2 = Environmental Comfort”, “F3 = Physical Comfort”, and “F4 = Social Comfort”. The overall scale explained 71.314% of the total variance (Table 1).

Confirmatory factor analysis of the menopause comfort scale

Data obtained from 213 participants randomly selected from 423 participants in the sample were used for the Confirmatory Factor Analysis. The validity of the factors determined by EFA was verified using CFA. Various indices were examined to evaluate the fit of the MCS model. The confirmatory factor analysis yielded the following: fit indices: (χ²/df = 1.34), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA): 0.04, Normed Fit Index (NFI): 0.93, Comparative Fit Index (CFI): 0.98 and Goodness of Fit Index (GFI): 0.96. The reliability coefficient of the scale was calculated as α = 0.814. As a result of the CFA performed, the four dimensional model showed an acceptable fit (Table 2).

Results regarding the reliability of the scale

The reliability of the Menopause Comfort Scale (MCS) was analyzed using the entire sample group (n = 423). In the present study, the reliability coefficient was α = 0.814 for the overall MCS, 0.602 for the “Psychological Comfort”, 0.836 for the “Environmental Comfort “, 0.690 for the “Physical Comfort " and 0.754 for the “Social Comfort " dimensions.

In the split-half analysis, the Cronbach’s alpha value was calculated as 0.699 for the first half and as 0.607 for the second half. The correlation between the two halves was 0.755. The Spearman-Brown (Unequal) coefficient was calculated as 0.861 and the Guttman split-half coefficient was calculated as 0.856 (Table 3). Hotelling’s T2 test was conducted to determine whether the number of the responses given to the scale items by the participating women was equal to the number of the scale items. The results of the test were as follows: T2= 715.888, F = 70.062 and p = 0.000, indicating that the scale does not exhibit response bias.

Discussion

In the discussion section of the present study, the results of the validity and reliability analysis regarding the psychometric properties of the MCS were discussed. Content validity was determined through expert evaluations. To determine the content validity of the MCS, expert opinions were obtained from 10 nursing academics. The content validity of the MCS was evaluated using Lawshe’s method [24]. According to Lawshe’s method, it is recommended that in scale development studies, the lowest CVR value for 10 experts should be 0.62 at a significance level of p˂0.05 [24]. After the items to be included in the scale are finalized, CVI is calculated. If the CVI is greater than the CVR value determined according to the number of experts (CVI > 0.62), the content validity of the overall scale is interpreted as statistically significant [24, 25]. The CVI value of the MCS was calculated as 0.70. Since the CVI was above 0.62, it indicated that the MCS adequately measured the subject and that content validity was ensured.

One of the most frequently used methods to test the construct validity of a scale developed is factor analysis, a method aimed at revealing common factors by grouping the relevant variables. In scale development studies, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) must be performed before Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) is performed [33]. The results of the KMO and Bartlett’s test of sphericity should be considered to assess whether the dataset is suitable for EFA. According to the literature, to carry out the planned factor analysis, the KMO value must be > 0.60 and the Bartlett’s test of sphericity must be significant at p < 0.05 [28, 34]. For the MCS, the KMO value was above 0.60 and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (p < 0.05), indicating that the dataset was suitable for EFA. In the present study, the minimum sample size required for the 17-item scale was calculated as 170 people. The study was conducted with a sample of 423 women. Having a sample size above the threshold strengthens the representation of the population and the validity and reliability of the results.

In the present study, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was performed to determine under which factors the scale items clustered and how much of the variance was explained by these factors. According to the results obtained through EFA, a four-factor structure (psychological, environmental, physical, and social) emerged in the MCS, and the items accounted for 71.314% of the total variance. This four-factor structure demonstrates a full conceptual alignment with the four contexts of comfort defined in Kolcaba’s Comfort Theory: physical, psychospiritual, environmental, and sociocultural. The clustering of items under these specific factors empirically validates that the theoretical dimensions targeted during the initial development phase were accurately captured. In the relevant literature, it is suggested that the total variance explained in multidimensional scales should be at least 40% [35]; thus, these results demonstrate that the MCS has strong structural validity. Furthermore, these results are a logical indication of the multidimensional nature of comfort and they are consistent with those in the literature indicating that comfort scales exhibit multidimensional factor structures [36]. Dimensions identified in the development and cross-cultural validation studies of the Menopause Rating Scale (MRS), one of the widely used scales in the field of menopause, also had psychological, somatic, and urogenital dimensions [17]. The psychological comfort, physical comfort, and social comfort dimensions of the MCS are conceptually parallel with this structure; however, the environmental dimension of the MCS, defined separately, reveals the unique contribution of the comfort perspective.

CFA is used to assess to what extent the factors included in a scale represent the items and how well they explain the structure of the scale [37]. The main advantage of CFA is that it reveals the difference between theory and observation by showing how well the data fits the theory-based measurement model [27, 38]. In the present study, CFA was conducted to assess the compatibility of the results obtained through EFA with the theoretical structure. The results of CFA confirmed the structure obtained with EFA and supported the relationship between the items and the measured structure. Consistent with the literature, the model fit indices indicated that the results of CFA supported the structural validity of the MCS. In the MCS, the model fit indices were as follows: RMSEA < 0.08 (= 0.04); NFI > 0.90 (= 0.93); CFI > 0.90 (= 0.98) and GFI > 0.90 (= 0.96). The χ2/df ratio was less than 5 (= 1.34). All these indicate that the MCS has strong construct validity and that the model fits the data appropriately [39, 40].

Furthermore, the absence of a statistically significant difference in total scores between premenopausal and peri/postmenopausal women (t=-0.377; p = 0.706) confirms that the scale functions consistently throughout the entire menopausal transition. This finding demonstrates that the instrument is equally effective in capturing the ‘anticipatory comfort’ and psychological concerns of premenopausal women, as well as the lived physical and sociocultural experiences of those in the peri- and postmenopausal stages. Such consistency reinforces the scale’s construct validity and strengthens its applicability for holistic nursing assessment across all stages of the transition.

Since the responses given to the items in the MCS are rated on a five-point Likert-type scale, its reliability was assessed using the Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient determines the internal consistency of the scale items and ranges from 0 (low internal consistency) to 1 (high internal consistency). It is also recommended that the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient should be at least 0.60 [41, 42]. The reliability coefficient was calculated as 0.814 for the overall MCS, and as 0.602, 0.836, 0.690, and 0.754 for its “Psychological Comfort”, “Environmental Comfort”, “Physical Comfort”, and “Social Comfort” dimensions, respectively, demonstrating that the MCS is highly reliable and has strong internal consistency in measuring the intended construct. The differences between the Cronbach’s alpha values of the dimensions indicate that some dimensions, particularly the psychological and physical comfort dimensions, have different dynamics, which can be interpreted as an indication that both more concrete and more subjective aspects of comfort can coexist.

Another method used to test the reliability of a scale is split-half analysis. In split-half analysis, reliability is evaluated through Spearman-Brown, Guttman Split-Half Coefficient, and Inter-Half Correlation coefficients. In the literature, it is recommended that Spearman-Brown and Guttman Split-Half Coefficient values should be above 0.80 and inter-half correlation (split-half reliability) should be above 0.70 [33, 43]. In the present study, the Spearman-Brown (Unequal) coefficient was calculated as 0.861, the Guttman Split-Half Coefficient as 0.856, and the Inter-Half Correlation as 0.755, indicating that all the items in the MCS effectively measure the comfort levels of menopausal women and provide consistent, focused, and stable measurements.

To enhance reliability, potential response bias must also be taken into account [27, 33]. In the present study, Hotelling’s T2 test was performed to evaluate whether the number of the responses given by the participants was equal to the number of the scale items, and the results showed that the MCS did not have response bias. Response bias analysis is performed to find out whether participants responded to the scale items according to their true opinions or according to the researcher’s expectations or societal norms. Research should be free from response bias, and it is desired that participants give responses that reflect their true thoughts [32, 44]. The absence of response bias in the MCS indicates that the MCS is a highly reliable and valid scale. In the present study, item-total correlation analysis was also performed to evaluate reliability further. Item-total score correlation analysis is used to determine the level of correlation between an item score and the overall scale score [33]. The minimum value for the item-total score correlation coefficient should be 0.20. An item-total score correlation coefficient of 0.20 or higher indicates that the scale has better discriminatory power and reliability [45]. In the literature, it is also recommended that the Corrected Item-Total Correlation approach should be used instead of the item under consideration (uncorrected) for translation [27]. In the present study, the Corrected Item-Total Correlation values ranged from 0.361 to 0.538, which is above the recommended value of 0.20, demonstrating that each item consistently measured the intended construct, confirming the reliability of the MCS.

The findings of the present study support the conclusion that the MCS has a psychometrically valid and reliable structure for measuring the comfort levels of menopausal women. As a patient-reported outcome measure, the MCS serves not only as a clinical guide but also as a robust research instrument with broader applicability in various scientific and multidisciplinary contexts. Beyond being a measurement tool, the MCS can also be used as a valid and reliable instrument to assess the effects of care and education programs for menopausal women, to monitor the effect of clinical interventions on comfort, and to systematize a comfort-oriented approach in nursing practice. The validity and reliability analyses of the MCS conform to methodological standards. The development of the MCS was based on a theoretical approach that comfort is a multidimensional concept. The systematic definition of comfort, including physical comfort, psychospiritual comfort, social comfort, and environmental comfort dimensions, was established based on Kolcaba’s Comfort Theory, highlighting the importance of considering comfort as a whole, encompassing not only physical well-being but also the fulfillment of an individual’s environmental and psychosocial needs.

Conclusion

The MCS, developed in the present study, is a valid and reliable measurement tool that can be administered to assess the comfort levels of menopausal women in psychological, environmental, physical, and social dimensions. Content validity analyses, factor analyses, and reliability tests show that the MCS exhibits a statistically strong structure consistent with its theoretical basis. The MCS has the potential to contribute to holistic nursing care planning by enabling the assessment of the multidimensional changes experienced during menopause from a comfort perspective. In clinical practice, it can allow for the systematic evaluation of not only the physiological but also the psychosocial and environmental effects of the symptoms of menopause. Furthermore, it serves as a standard tool for measuring the effectiveness of educational programs, counseling services, and care interventions related to menopause. It is recommended that in future studies, the validity and reliability of the scale should be tested in different cultural groups, that test-retest reliability analyses should be performed, and that measurement invariance should be investigated. In conclusion, the MCS can be used as an assessment tool in research and clinical applications aimed at improving the quality of life of women in menopause.

Limitations

The present study has some methodological limitations. First, the sample consisted of women reached through online social media platforms across Türkiye, which may have resulted in the selection of the sample using a non-probability method and a higher representation of individuals with digital literacy in the study. Consequently, the results have limited generalizability only to the women surveyed and they cannot be generalized especially to women with limited internet access or low socioeconomic status. Since the psychometric properties of the MCS might vary in these underrepresented groups, future studies should deliberately target diverse populations-including rural and underserved areas- as a research priority to ensure the scale’s broader clinical applicability.

Second, since the present study utilized an online data collection method, test-retest reliability was not evaluated due to the methodological difficulties in re-contacting the same anonymous participants over a specific time interval. The absence of data on temporal stability is a significant limitation, especially considering the scale’s potential use in monitoring clinical comfort outcomes and evaluating nursing interventions over time. Without such data, it is difficult to determine whether fluctuations in scores reflect genuine clinical improvements or measurement errors. Therefore, conducting longitudinal studies to establish the test-retest reliability of the MCS should be regarded as a primary research priority to ensure its clinical applicability and reliability in practice.

Third, EFA and CFA were performed on different subsamples, but the cross-cultural consistency of the construct validity was not tested. Measurement invariance of the scale in different cultural contexts and different stages of menopause (pre-, peri-, post-) should be checked.

Another limitation of this study is the absence of criterion-related and convergent validity testing. Although the MCS demonstrates strong internal structural validity, it was not correlated with established measures of related constructs, such as the Menopause Rating Scale (MRS) or the Menopause-Specific Quality of Life Questionnaire (MENQOL). This decision was based on the study’s primary focus on defining comfort as a conceptually distinct phenomenon from symptom severity. This situation represents a limitation regarding the psychometric evidence of the newly developed scale, and it is recommended that these advanced validity analyses are conducted in future studies.

Finally, the scale was administered using a self-report method, which may lead to measurement errors resulting from social desirability bias or subjective perception differences. The menopausal experience is affected by cultural and individual meaning-making processes, a factor that can create variability in the perception of comfort.
