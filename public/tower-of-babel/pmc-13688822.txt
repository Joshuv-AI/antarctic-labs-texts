Malaria diagnosis practices in formal private drug retail shops in the Ho Municipality, Ghana: a cross-sectional survey
Authors: Shaibu Saaka, Victor Loglo, Vincent Uwumboriyhie Gmayinaam, Derrick Atandaa Aboyinga, Chrysantus Kubio, Mawuli Komla Kushitor, Evelyn Korkor Ansah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrivate drug retail outlets, including community pharmacies and over the counter medicine sellers (OTCMS), play a major role in malaria case management in Ghana. Appropriate diagnosis before treatment is essential for effective malaria control. Evidence on the availability and use of malaria diagnostic services in these outlets remains limited in many municipalities. This study assessed the current diagnostic practices and challenges of malaria diagnosis in formal private drug retail shops in the Ho Municipality, Ghana.MethodsA cross-sectional descriptive survey was conducted in selected formal private drug retail shops in the Ho Municipality, Volta Region, Ghana. Data were collected through phone interviews using structured questionnaires with the attendants and frontline staff of private drug retail shops. The study sample comprised 86 formal private drug retail shops, including both OTMS and Community Pharmacies. Information covered socio-demographic characteristics, availability and type of malaria diagnostic tests, training in malaria diagnosis, cost of testing, and respondents’ views on malaria diagnosis. Data were summarized using frequencies and percentages.ResultsOf the 86 facilities surveyed, 70 (81.4%) were OTCMS and 16 (18.6%) were pharmacies. Most respondents were aged 46 to 60 years (38.4%) and slightly more were male (51.2%). Malaria testing capacity was limited. Only 33 (38.4%) facilities had the diagnostic capacity for malaria, while 53 (61.6%) had no testing capability. All community pharmacies had malaria testing capacity compared with 24.2% of OTCMS. Testing before treatment was routinely practiced in only 11.6% of facilities, while 61.6% never tested before treatment. Rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) were the only diagnostic method available, and microscopy was not used in any shop. Among facilities that stocked RDTs, common types included HRP2 antigen tests and HRP2/pLDH combination kits. Most respondents (76.7%) had received training in malaria diagnosis, mainly organized by the Pharmacy Council. Malaria testing typically cost between USD 0.98 and USD 1.63 in most facilities offering the service. Concerns raised by respondents included perceived inconsistency between test results and clinical judgement, the relatively high cost of test kits, and the need for stronger public education on malaria diagnostic guidelines.ConclusionsMalaria diagnostic services remain limited in formal private drug retail shops in the Ho Municipality, particularly among OTCMS. Although most staff have received training, routine testing before treatment is uncommon and many facilities lack diagnostic capacity. Strengthening access to rapid diagnostic tests, improving adherence to test before treat guidelines, and subsidizing test kits could improve malaria case management in these shops.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12936-026-06068-8.

Introduction

Malaria is one of the most severe public health problems, with nearly half of the world's population at risk for infection. Globally in 2024, there were an estimated 282 million malaria cases and 610, 000 malaria deaths [1]. Africa bears the most significant malaria burden, with 94% of the malaria cases and 95% malaria of deaths in 2025 [1]. Ghana is recognized as one of the 15 highest-burden malaria countries globally, contributing approximately 5.3% of malaria cases in West Africa; in 2023, it accounted for about 2.5% of global malaria cases and 1.9% of malaria-related deaths worldwide [2].

The World Health Organization (WHO) currently recommends that all suspected malaria cases be confirmed using parasite-based diagnostic testing, either by microscopy or malaria rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs), and that only patients with confirmed malaria receive artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) [3]. This “test, treat and track (T3)” approach remains central to effective malaria case management and delaying the emergence of artemisinin resistance. The deployment of RDTs has substantially improved access to diagnostic services, particularly in high-burden, rural, and resource-limited settings where quality microscopy remains constrained [2, 4]. Studies continue to demonstrate that RDT-based diagnosis improves targeting of ACTs and reduces unnecessary antimalarial use when adhered to correctly [5].

Across sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), the private healthcare sector plays a critical role in the diagnosis and treatment of malaria. Approximately 35–40% of patients with febrile illness seek initial care in the formal private drug retail shops, including community pharmacies and OTCMs [5, 6]. The procurement and distribution of RDTs in the public sector have expanded markedly over the last decade. The number of RDTs distributed globally by national malaria programmes increased from fewer than 200 million in 2010 to over 400 million annually by 2023, driven largely by donor-supported scale-up in endemic countries [2, 4]. However, access to and routine use of RDTs in the formal private drug retail shops remains limited, despite the fact that a substantial proportion of febrile patients first seek care outside the public system [5, 6]. In many SSA countries, these shops continue to rely heavily on presumptive treatment of malaria, often without parasitological confirmation [7].

[8, 9, 10, 11]Earlier studies in Ghana and elsewhere showed that even when diagnostics were available, prescribers frequently did not request confirmatory testing, and patients with negative test results were still treated with antimalarial medicines [8, 9]. More recent evidence suggests that provider mistrust in RDT results, fear of missing malaria cases, perceived patient expectations, and concerns about repeat visits contribute to poor adherence to negative test results [5, 9].

Ghana’s National Malaria Elimination Programme (NMEP) has fully adopted WHO’s universal diagnostic testing policy and promotes the use of RDTs across all levels of care, including the formal private drug retail shops [10]. However, implementation gaps persist, particularly among formal private drug retail outlets. Community pharmacies and OTCMS often serve as the first point of contact for febrile patients because of lower costs, minimal waiting times, and patient preference for self-medication (Ashigbie et al., 2016). However, multiple studies continue to show low availability and utilization of RDTs in these settings. Evidence from middle-belt Ghana and urban Greater Accra indicates that only a minority of formal private drug shops stock RDTs, and even fewer use them routinely before dispensing antimalarial medicines (Kwarteng et al., 2019; Amankwa et al., 2019). [14]. It is therefore necessary to explore the extent to which this sector is performing regarding malaria diagnosis.

While formal private drug retail shops are an important source of malaria treatment in Ghana, little is known about their capacity to provide malaria diagnostic services, the extent to which malaria testing is performed before treatment, and the challenges affecting adherence to the national "test before treat" policy. More recent research from Ghana highlights ongoing challenges in formal private drug retail shops’ malaria case management, including inadequate diagnostic capacity, poor documentation, and weak integration into national surveillance systems [11]. These gaps emphasize the need for targeted interventions to improve RDT availability, provider training, supervision, and adherence to national malaria treatment guidelines within the private medicine retail sector. The Ho Municipality has its fair share of formal private health practitioners, as information from the Pharmacy Council reveals that there are more than 100 formal private drug retailers in the Municipality. Therefore, this study assessed malaria diagnostic practices, availability of diagnostic services, provider training, and perceived challenges among formal private drug retail shops in Ho Municipality, Ghana. Addressing this evidence gap is important for strengthening malaria case management within the formal private drug retail sector.

Material and methods

Study design

A facility-based cross-sectional descriptive survey was conducted among formal private drug retail shops in Ho Municipality, Ghana. This design was used because it allows for a snapshot view of data collected at a specific point in time, enabling the researchers to gather information efficiently from various health providers and understand the current practices and trends in private drug retail shops.

Study setting

The study was conducted in the Ho Municipality of the Volta region of Ghana. The Ho Municipality is one of the twenty-five (25) districts in the Volta Region. Its capital and administrative center is Ho. The city lies between Mount Adaklu and Mount Galenukui or Togo Atakora Range. The 2019 estimated annual population of Ho Municipality according to the Volta Region Statistical Service is 218,948 representing 8.4 per cent of the region's total population. Females constitute 50.8 per cent and males represent 49.2 per cent. About 62 per cent of the population resides in urban localities. The Municipality shares boundaries with Adaklu and Agotime-Ziope Districts to the South, Ho West District to the North and West and the Republic of Togo to the East. Its total land area is 2,361 square kilometres (912 sq. mi) thus representing 11.5 per cent of the region's total land area [12]. The town is home to three hospitals, including the Ho Teaching Hospital, inaugurated in the year 2019. The municipal has a total of 136 formal private drug retail shops made up of 18 community pharmacies and 118 OTCMS [13].

Study population

The study included shopping attendants in formal private sector drug retail facilities within the Ho municipality which sell antimalarials. Attendants in formal private drug retail shops and Pharmacies who consented to participate in the study were eligible for inclusion. Attendants who had worked for less than six months or facilities where no staff were available during data collection were excluded. Shops that were not in operation or had ceased operations during the data collection period. were excluded. Attendants who had worked for less than six months were excluded because they were unlikely to have acquired sufficient experience with routine malaria diagnostic practices and service delivery within their facilities.

The sampling frame consisted of 136 registered formal private drug retail shops, including 18 community pharmacies and 118 OTCMS. Substituting these values into the formula, the calculated sample size was 100.63, which was rounded up to 101 shops. The sample was proportionally allocated based on outlet category, resulting in a required sample of 13 community pharmacies and 88 OTCMs. During data collection, 86 shops were successfully surveyed, comprising 16 community pharmacies and 70 OTCMs. This represented an overall response rate of 85% and 80% for OTCMs.

All 16 community pharmacies were purposively included in the study due to their limited number within the sampling frame. For the OTCMs, a proportional sample of 88 was initially estimated from the 118 registered OTCMs using the calculated sample size allocation. However, during data collection, 70 OTCMs successfully participated in the study. The OTCMs were arranged alphabetically to develop the sampling frame, with each outlet assigned a unique identification number. Systematic random sampling was applied to select the required OTCMs. The sampling interval was calculated by dividing the total number of eligible OTCMs by the required sample size ((118/88 = 1.34)). A random starting point was selected using a random number table, after which every 1 st to 2nd outlet based on the calculated interval was selected systematically until the required sample was approached.

Data collection tool and procedure

The instrument was a structured tool which contained up to 21 questions made up of five sections: Section A dealt with background information; Section B dealt with the availability and use of malaria diagnostic tests; Section C dealt with the types of diagnostic methods; Section D the frequency and types of trainings and Section E dealt with the cost of malaria diagnosis (See Supplementary file S1 for details). Two research assistants were trained for four days before commencement of data collection. Phone calls were made to individual shops to schedule a time of the interview, after which the researchers went to the facility to administer the tool. The administration of the tool lasted approximately 15 min. Malaria experts reviewed the study tool. The tool was piloted in the three OTCMs to assess clarity, relevance, and flow. Findings from the pilot informed revisions to improve content quality and ensure suitability for data collection.

Data management and analysis

Data were entered into a Microsoft Excel database and exported to Stata version 17 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA) for analysis. Data cleaning and validation was done to ensure data quality before the analysis was done. Descriptive analysis was done using frequency and proportions and was presented in tables.

Discussions

This study assessed malaria diagnostic practices among formal private drug retail shops in the Ho Municipality and revealed important gaps in the availability and utilization of malaria diagnostic services, particularly among OTCMS. Although community pharmacies demonstrated relatively better access to RDTs and greater engagement in malaria testing, the overall uptake of diagnostic testing before treatment remained low. The findings further highlight concerns regarding inadequate training, inconsistent adherence to the WHO T3 strategy, stock-outs of test kits, and the economic factors influencing diagnostic practices.

The study found that formal private drug shops had very limited malaria diagnostic capacity. RDTs were rarely available or used in routine care, and many drug-seller attendants lacked formal training. Community pharmacies outperformed OTCMS in testing capacity and staff preparation. Similar Ghanaian studies report very low RDT uptake in private outlets (26% of OTCMS in one survey) [15]. A Ghana trial showed only 10.5% of OTC clients were tested at baseline, rising to 30.8% after subsidized RDTs and training [16]. Another systematic review found that RDT uptake in shops varies widely (8–100%) and improves with training, lower prices, and supervision [17].This gap means most fevers are treated presumptively, risking overtreatment and unchecked malaria transmission. This echoes WHO’s “test-before-treat” policy, which is not being met in the private retail sector. Without parasite-based diagnosis, many non-malarial fevers are likely to be treated with anti-malarial, which may undermine Ghana’s malaria control goals. The findings highlight the need to integrate private outlets into national malaria programs.

Among facilities that performed malaria testing, management following a negative test result varied. One-third of shops dispensed an antipyretic, while others referred clients or treated for malaria and another illness. Similar variability in the management of patients with negative malaria test results has been reported in other malaria-endemic settings, reflecting ongoing challenges in adhering to parasite-based case management guidelines particularly in resource-limited settings [8, 9]. For instance, in Uganda, healthcare workers or drug shop vendors will provide alternative treatments such as antibiotics or refer patients for further diagnosis to rule out other diseases [18]. A study by Ge et al. [22]revealed that, these practices sometimes vary significantly, with some providers overprescribing antimalarials despite negative test results, driven by presumptive treatment habits and the difficulty of diagnosing other infections in malaria-endemic areas [19].

Several studies affirm that the availability and usage of malaria diagnostic services, especially in the formal private retail drug shop, are often limited [20]. In Sub-Saharan Africa, many health facilities and pharmacies do not have adequate access to essential diagnostic tools like microscopy and rapid diagnostic tests [21]. The study further added that, this low availability hinders effective malaria diagnosis and contributes to mismanagement, including the over-prescription of anti-malarial drugs without proper diagnostic confirmation. The current study also added that, the low availability of malaria diagnostics in healthcare outlets, particularly in private or community health settings, has been linked to serious issues such as misdiagnosis and overtreatment with antimalarial drugs. This is consistent with other studies Africa, for instance, [22] revealed that, healthcare providers frequently bypass diagnostic tests like malaria RDTs due to factors like scepticism about their reliability and lack of proper training. This often leads to the presumptive treatment of febrile illnesses as malaria without confirmation, resulting in the inappropriate use of antimalarials and contributing to drug resistance.

Community Pharmacies had high availability and may have incentivize the adoption of diagnostic services as part of a comprehensive healthcare approach. The current study findings commend the integration of malaria diagnostic services in community pharmacies, highlighting key points about their availability, potential impact, and the need for incentives to increase adoption. On the contrary, a study in Rwanda showed that while many community pharmacies possess some level of knowledge and availability of malaria rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs), their use remains limited due to low demand from patients and regulatory restrictions on pharmacy-based testing [23]. The study further added that, Pharmacists are often more inclined to provide treatments such as ACTs without prior diagnostics, especially when there is no clear incentive to test first.

However, Community pharmacies sold RDTs more compared with OTCMS. for instance, a study in Nigeria found that while both pharmacies and OTCMS offered malaria diagnostic services, pharmacies were more inclined to stock RDTs and offer them to customers [24]. In Ghana, [25] found that community pharmacies not only sold more RDTs but also played a more prominent role in ensuring that customers adhered to RDT results, potentially reducing the unnecessary use of antimalarial drugs.

Results from the present study indicates that, the majority of attendants (76.7%) had training on the malaria diagnostic tests. Studies globally have highlighted the lack of formal training on malaria diagnostic tests among healthcare workers. A study conducted across five districts in Uganda revealed that, 52% of the healthcare workers lacked minimal exposure to formal malaria testing protocols such as the RDTs [26]. The study further revealed that, this lack of formal training directly impacts their ability to effectively administer and interpret RDTs, crucial for managing malaria cases. Findings of the current study is also consistent with a study conducted in India which focused on malaria elimination project. The study revealed that, over half of the workers had no formal education past the 12th grade, and although training improved their diagnostic skills significantly, continuous education and refresher courses were necessary to maintain competency [27].

Moreover, the current study highlights that approximately 8 out of 10 attendants received their training from the Pharmacy Council, with over half of them reporting that their training occurred more than 1–3 years ago. Findings from the current study also indicates that, is evident that the availability of malaria diagnostics in the private medicine retail sector is low. This is consistent with a systematic review conducted by [17] which showed that despite efforts to introduce RDTs, their availability remains limited in formal private drug retail shops across sub-Saharan Africa. The review further added that, in surveys conducted in six African countries, less than 20% of formal private drug retail shops had access to RDTs or microscopy for malaria diagnosis. Similarly, a study conducted among private drug outlets in Nigeria and Uganda also found that, the these shops play a significant role in providing antimalarials and continues to focus on medication sales without adequate emphasis on diagnostic testing [20].

Limitations

Self-reported information may be biased because respondents may have provided only socially desirable responses. To minimise this, the researcher tested the reliability of the tool after pre- testing.

Conclusion and recommendation

This study found that the availability and routine use of malaria RDTs remain limited in formal private drug retail shops in Ho Municipality, particularly among OTCMs. Although many providers had received previous training, gaps in RDT availability, periodic stock-outs, and inconsistent adherence to parasite-based diagnosis suggest that malaria case management in the private drug retail sector could be strengthened. Expanding access to quality-assured RDTs, strengthening provider training and supportive supervision, and promoting adherence to the national "test before treat" policy are essential to improve appropriate malaria diagnosis and treatment in this important first point of care. Also, the NMEP needs to engage private practitioners on the need to test before dispensing anti-malarias.
