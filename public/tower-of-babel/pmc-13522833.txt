In vitro activity of ceftaroline against clinical isolates of Gram-positive bacteria in paediatric patients: ATLAS programme 2018–2023
Authors: Pattarachai Kiratisin, Alona Kuraieva, Eric Utt, Susanna Esposito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study evaluated the antimicrobial activity of ceftaroline and comparator antimicrobials against clinical isolates of Gram-positive bacteria (GPB) from paediatric patients globally and in Africa/Middle East (AfME), Asia–Pacific (APAC), Europe and Latin America (LATAM) as part of the Antimicrobial Testing Leadership and Surveillance programme between 2018 and 2023.MethodsSusceptibility and minimum inhibitory concentrations of all organisms were determined using broth microdilution methodology for ceftaroline and comparators from Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) and European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) breakpoints.ResultsOverall and across regions, Staphylococcus aureus (CLSI/EUCAST, ≥99.0%) and methicillin-resistant S. aureus (CLSI, ≥95.6%) demonstrated high susceptibility to ceftaroline. High susceptibility to daptomycin, linezolid, teicoplanin, tigecycline (from the FDA), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole and vancomycin was demonstrated against these isolates for both breakpoints. Globally and across regions, for Streptococcus pneumoniae (CLSI/EUCAST, 93.3%–100%) and penicillin-intermediate S. pneumoniae (CLSI/EUCAST, 100%), high susceptibility to ceftaroline was observed. For penicillin-resistant S. pneumoniae, higher susceptibility to ceftaroline following the CLSI (96.5%–100%) than EUCAST (74.4%–88.7%) was observed except in LATAM. High susceptibility to linezolid, levofloxacin, tigecycline and vancomycin was demonstrated against these isolates across regions from both breakpoints, whereas, for ceftriaxone, higher susceptibility following the CLSI than EUCAST was observed. For β-haemolytic streptococci, high susceptibility to ceftaroline was observed (CLSI, 100%). High susceptibility to linezolid, penicillin and vancomycin was demonstrated against these isolates from both breakpoints.ConclusionsCeftaroline demonstrated potent activity against key GPB. This study significantly contributes to the limited current information on paediatric antimicrobial resistance patterns across AfME, APAC, Europe and LATAM.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as a significant public health concern, particularly affecting children.1,2 According to World Health Organization (WHO) data, infections caused by multi-drug-resistant bacteria have resulted in 700 000 deaths across various age groups including 200 000 of these deaths occurring in neonates.3 Gram-positive bacteria (GPB) such as Staphylococcus aureus including methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) and Streptococcus pneumoniae are the leading causes of hospital-acquired infections in neonatal and paediatric populations, resulting in significant morbidity and mortality from hospital-acquired pneumonia and bacteraemia.1,2 These bacteria are also associated with community-acquired infections such as respiratory and skin/soft-tissue infections.4 Pneumonia remains a leading cause of childhood mortality, with 501 910 deaths reported in children aged <5 years in 2021.5 Likewise, a substantial burden of bacterial skin diseases have been reported worldwide in 2021 with a global incidence of 337.7 (95% UI 322.6–353.6) million cases in individuals aged 0–19 years according to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 estimates.6 S. aureus bacteraemia in children is associated with mortality ranging from 2% to 15%.7

According to the GBD study (1990–2021), S. aureus was identified as the pathogen with the greatest increase in attributable deaths from 1990 to 2021. The GBD study also reported that MRSA had the highest increase in global attributable burden from 1990 to 2021 with AMR-attributable deaths doubling from 1990 [57 200 (34 100–80 300)] to 2021 [130 000 (113 000–146 000)].8 A recent meta-analysis estimated the global prevalence of MRSA colonization in children to be 5% [95% confidence intervals (CI), 4%–5%] of the paediatric population.9 The GBD 2021 study also identified S. pneumoniae, with 35 100 (24 000–46 200) attributable deaths, as one of the pathogens accounting for maximum AMR-attributable deaths in children (<5 years of age) in 2021.8 Notably, WHO, in the 2024 update of the Bacterial Priority Pathogens List categorized GPB such as MRSA, S. pneumoniae, and Group A and B streptococci (β-haemolytic streptococci) as priority pathogens for monitoring AMR patterns and development of new antimicrobials.10 Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A Streptococcus or GAS) is the primary cause of bacterial tonsillopharyngitis in children with a recent meta-analysis estimating a global burden of 288.6 million episodes of S. pyogenes sore throat in 2022 in children aged 5–14 years.11 A recent multicentre study across European hospitals reported an overall crude mortality rate of 2% and survival with disability rate of 12% in children with GAS infections.12 Streptococcus agalactiae (Group B Streptococcus or GBS) mainly causes invasive GBS (iGBS) infections in neonates.13 A recent meta-analysis estimated 231 800 (114 100–455 000) early-onset and 162 200 (70 200–394 400) late-onset cases and 91 900 (44 800–187 800) infant deaths due to iGBS globally in 2020.14 Overall, the increasing burden of infections attributable to drug-resistant GPB poses significant challenges due to limited treatment options available in paediatric patients, leading to increasing difficulties in managing resistant infections in these patients.1,2

Ceftaroline, the active form of pro-drug ceftaroline fosamil, is a fifth-generation broad-spectrum cephalosporin with bactericidal in vitro activity against drug-resistant GPB including MRSA and penicillin-resistant S. pneumoniae (PRSP) by irreversibly binding to penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) thereby inhibiting bacterial cell wall synthesis.15,16 The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency have approved ceftaroline for the treatment of adult and paediatric patients with complicated skin and soft-tissue infections/acute bacterial skin and skin structure infections and community-acquired pneumonia/community-acquired bacterial pneumonia.17,18

With the rise and growing burden of AMR and drug-resistant GPB infections, multiple surveillance studies have monitored the activity of ceftaroline and comparator antimicrobials against these pathogens including MRSA and PRSP.19,20 However, these studies have included isolates from both adult and paediatric patients without segregation of paediatric data. There are limited studies that have provided data on paediatric AMR patterns in drug-resistant gram-positive isolates for ceftaroline and comparator antimicrobials globally.21,22 Few surveillance studies in paediatric patients for assessing ceftaroline activity against GPB have been conducted in the USA,23–25 but limited data exist across Africa/Middle East (AfME), Asia–Pacific (APAC), Europe and Latin America (LATAM).26 This study evaluated the antimicrobial activity of ceftaroline and comparator antimicrobial agents against a large contemporary collection of clinical isolates of GPB from paediatric patients globally and across regions, in AfME, APAC, Europe and LATAM as part of the Antimicrobial Testing Leadership and Surveillance (ATLAS) programme between 2018 and 2023.

Materials and methods

Bacterial isolates

Non-duplicate clinical isolates of GPB (S. aureus, S. pneumoniae, S. pyogenes, S. agalactiae and Streptococcus dysgalactiae) were collected globally from paediatric patients (aged ≤17 years; single isolate per patient) between 2018 and 2023 following a pre-defined protocol. The participating centres collected a pre-defined number of isolates of selected bacterial species from infection sources [bloodstream, intra-abdominal, respiratory tract, urinary tract, skin/musculoskeletal and other infection sources including circulatory system (other than bloodstream), nervous system, reproductive system, the head, ears, eyes, nose and throat, and instruments] as part of the ATLAS programme, a global surveillance programme established to provide antimicrobial susceptibility data for different classes of antimicrobials against isolates collected globally.27 Region-wise data incorporated in this study are from AfME, APAC, Europe and LATAM. North America was excluded from this study as this region was not part of the global ATLAS programme funded by Pfizer. Isolates were shipped to a central laboratory, International Health Management Associates, Inc. (IHMA, Schaumburg, IL, USA) for species confirmation using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) (Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, MA, USA).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Susceptibility and minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) of all organisms were determined using broth microdilution methodology for ceftaroline and a panel of comparator antimicrobial agents, including ceftriaxone, clindamycin, daptomycin, erythromycin, gentamycin, levofloxacin, linezolid, teicoplanin, tigecycline, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole and vancomycin. Antimicrobial susceptibility was determined following the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI, Thirty-Fifth Edition)28 and European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST, version 14.0)29 breakpoints, wherever applicable, using custom 96-well broth microdilution panels prepared by IHMA (2018 to present). For S. pneumoniae and beta-haemolytic streptococci, CLSI recommends MIC testing in supplemented cation adjusted Mueller–Hinton broth with 2.5%–5% lysed horse blood (LHB). After inoculation, MIC panels were incubated at 35°C ± 2°C in ambient air for 20–24 hours. IHMA uses the CLSI method for all of the Streptococci MIC testing and prepares the panels in-house using dehydrated cation adjusted Mueller–Hinton broth supplemented with the LHB. All MICs were interpreted using both CLSI and EUCAST breakpoints as applicable. For tigecycline, due to absence of MIC interpretative criteria from the CLSI, MICs were interpreted using FDA-approved breakpoints.30 Methicillin resistance for each S. aureus isolate was determined using the oxacillin MIC method (MIC ≥4 mg/L, confirmed methicillin resistance).28 Accordingly, susceptibility and MIC data for methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA) and MRSA were obtained from the ATLAS database using oxacillin resistance following CLSI breakpoints. Owing to lack of oxacillin MIC following EUCAST breakpoints for methicillin resistance in S. aureus isolates, susceptibility and MIC data for MSSA and MRSA isolates were not available on the ATLAS database, hence they were not included in the study. Isolates of S. pneumoniae were classified as penicillin-intermediate (susceptible, increased exposure from EUCAST MIC definitions) or -resistant according to their penicillin MIC [CLSI (oral penicillin V): 0.12–1 mg/L or ≥2 mg/L; EUCAST (non-meningitis): >0.06–≤2 or >2 mg/L, respectively].28,29 For β-haemolytic streptococci, EUCAST has not published breakpoints for ceftaroline, specifying that susceptibility to ceftaroline can be inferred from testing benzyl penicillin.29

In this study, susceptibility was categorized as high (≥85.0%), moderate (≤84.5%–>60%), or low (≤60.0%–>40%) and very low (≤40.0%).

Results

A total of 10 327 isolates of GPB from 884 unique sites worldwide including 8933 isolates of GPB from 783 unique sites in 62 countries located in AfME, APAC, Europe and LATAM (excluding North America) were collected in 2018–2023 from hospitalized paediatric patients (Table S1, available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online).

S. aureus isolates demonstrated high susceptibility to ceftaroline (≥99.0%) worldwide and across regions included in this study following CLSI and EUCAST breakpoints (Table 1). Analysis of the MIC frequency distributions revealed that the MIC90 values for ceftaroline against S. aureus were 0.5 mg/L following both breakpoints. Globally, among S. aureus isolates, 0.7% (44/6416) and 0.1% (4/6416) were categorized, respectively, as susceptible dose-dependent (SDD; MIC 2–4 mg/L) and resistant (MIC ≥8 mg/L) to ceftaroline according to the CLSI. According to EUCAST, 0.7% (48/6416; MIC >1 mg/L for pneumonia) and 0.1% (7/6416; MIC >2 mg/L for indications other than pneumonia) were categorized as resistant to ceftaroline (Table S3). Globally, and across the regions, susceptibility was high for all comparator antimicrobials (≥85.7%) except levofloxacin (moderate susceptibility in APAC and AfME, 80.5% and 83.9%, respectively) and erythromycin (low to moderate susceptibility across regions, 51.0%–77.6%) following both breakpoints (Table 1).

For MSSA isolates, susceptibility to ceftaroline was 100% globally and across regions following CLSI (Table 1). Analysis of the MIC frequency distributions revealed that the MIC90 values for ceftaroline against MSSA were 0.25 mg/L following CLSI globally (Supplementary Table S3). Among comparator antimicrobials, susceptibility to daptomycin, linezolid, teicoplanin and vancomycin was 99.9%–100% globally and across regions following CLSI. High susceptibility was also observed for clindamycin, gentamicin, levofloxacin, tigecycline (available through FDA breakpoints) and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole globally and across regions (87.9%–100%) (Table 1).

For MRSA isolates, susceptibility to ceftaroline following CLSI, was high globally and across regions (95.6%–97.0%; Table 1). Analysis of the MIC frequency distributions revealed that the MIC90 values for ceftaroline, globally, against MRSA were 1 mg/L following CLSI. Globally, among MRSA isolates, 2.7% (44/1616) and 0.2% (4/1616) were categorized, respectively, as SDD (MIC 2–4 mg/L) and resistant (MIC ≥8 mg/L) to ceftaroline following CLSI (Table 2). High susceptibility to daptomycin, linezolid, teicoplanin, tigecycline (available through FDA breakpoints), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole and vancomycin was also observed globally and across regions following CLSI (91.0%–100%). Regional differences were observed in susceptibility to clindamycin (highest in AfME 91.4%; lowest in APAC 71.2%), gentamicin (highest in LATAM 89.5%; lowest in AfME 69.1%), erythromycin (highest in AfME 64.0%; lowest in APAC 33.0%) and levofloxacin (highest in LATAM 75.7%; lowest in APAC 61.5%) (Table 1).

S. pneumoniae isolates demonstrated high susceptibility to ceftaroline (93.3%–100%) worldwide and across regions following CLSI and EUCAST (Table 2). Among the comparator agents, globally and across the regions, linezolid, levofloxacin, tigecycline (according to FDA breakpoints) and vancomycin demonstrated high susceptibility (97.5%–100%) following both breakpoints. For ceftriaxone, globally and across regions, susceptibility was higher following CLSI compared with EUCAST; however, this difference was less pronounced in Europe. Regional differences were observed in susceptibility to clindamycin (highest in Europe ∼82%, lowest in APAC ∼35%) and erythromycin (highest in Europe 75.2%, lowest in APAC 23.5%) (Table 3).

All PSSP isolates were susceptible to ceftaroline, globally and across regions, following both breakpoints (Table 3). Globally and across regions, high susceptibility to ceftriaxone, levofloxacin, linezolid, tigecycline (according to FDA breakpoints) and vancomycin was observed for PSSP isolates (96.7%–100%). Regional differences were observed in susceptibility to clindamycin (highest in LATAM 96.5%, lowest in APAC ≥58.9%) and erythromycin (highest in Europe 88.1%, lowest in APAC 52.3%) (Table 3).

All PISP isolates were susceptible to ceftaroline, globally and across regions, following both breakpoints (Table 3). Globally and across regions, levofloxacin, linezolid, tigecycline (through FDA breakpoints) and vancomycin showed high susceptibility against PISP isolates (97.6%–100%). Susceptibility to ceftriaxone was higher following CLSI (99.0%–100%) than EUCAST (53.6%–80.5%). Susceptibility to clindamycin (32.4%–75.4%) and erythromycin remained the lowest among all antimicrobials tested (13.4%–53.3%) (Table 3).

For PRSP isolates, susceptibility to ceftaroline was higher following CLSI (96.5%–100%) than EUCAST (74.4%–88.7%) globally and across regions except in LATAM (100% according to both breakpoints) (Table 3). Levofloxacin, linezolid, tigecycline (according to FDA breakpoints) and vancomycin consistently showed high susceptibility (94.6%–100%). Ceftriaxone had higher susceptibility following CLSI (37.9%–64.7%) than EUCAST (0%–2.8%). Similar to PISP, susceptibility to clindamycin (9.4%–47.1%) and erythromycin (1.7%–23.7%) against PRSP remained the lowest among all antimicrobials (Table 3).

Discussion

With the increasing prevalence of drug-resistant GPB, ongoing monitoring of AMR trends is essential to inform treatment strategies for optimal therapeutic outcomes. This study assessed the antimicrobial activity of ceftaroline and comparator agents against clinical GPB from paediatric patients (≤17 years) collected between 2018 and 2023 globally, including AfME, APAC, Europe and LATAM.

In this study, ceftaroline demonstrated potent in vitro activity against S. aureus (≥99.0%, MIC90 0.5–1 mg/L), MSSA (100%, MIC90 0.25–0.5 mg/L) and MRSA (≥95.6%, MIC90 1 mg/L) isolates overall and across different regions. These results are in line with in vitro activity observed for ceftaroline against S. aureus (98.1%, MIC90 1 mg/L) and MRSA (95.9%, MIC90, 1 mg/L) isolates collected from paediatric patients (0–18 years) worldwide [from the ATLAS database (2004–2018)].21 Low MICs observed may be attributed to the high PBP binding affinity of ceftaroline.21,31 Similarly, in another global study from ATLAS (2017–2019), potent activity of ceftaroline against S. aureus (97.8%, MIC90 1 mg/L), including MSSA (100%, MIC90 0.25–0.5 mg/L) and MRSA (92.7%, MIC90, 1–2 mg/L) isolates in paediatric patients was observed,22 highlighting that consistent activity against S. aureus including MRSA isolates has been observed over the years. Similar high susceptibility to ceftaroline against S. aureus (≥99.8%), MSSA (100%) and MRSA (≥99.3%) was reported in paediatric patients in the USA [Assessing Worldwide Antimicrobial Resistance Evaluation (AWARE) study, January–December 201024 and January 2011–December 201225] and Kuwait (ATLAS 2012–2019).26 However, in a case report of a paediatric patient (aged 20 months) with invasive MRSA infection, high-level ceftaroline resistance (MIC90 32 mg/L) was reported without previous ceftaroline exposure.32 Another study in India (January–December 2021) reported high resistance in MRSA isolates against ceftaroline (CLSI33/EUCAST34, 30.0%/50.0%).35 This emergence of ceftaroline resistance, largely attributable to mecA and PBP mutations, undermines one of the few β-lactams active against MRSA.36 Taken together, these findings indicate that although ceftaroline remained consistently active against S. aureus (including MRSA) isolates globally, continued monitoring of AMR patterns, particularly the mecA and PBP mutations, in MRSA is essential to inform treatment decisions and strategies for infection control. As there are limited published antimicrobial surveillance data on paediatric patients from AfME, APAC, Europe and LATAM, the results from this study significantly contribute to the scarce surveillance data available for paediatric patients worldwide and across these regions.

Among comparators, in this study, high susceptibility (91.0%–100%) was observed to daptomycin, linezolid, tigecycline, teicoplanin, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole and vancomycin following CLSI and EUCAST in S. aureus and following CLSI in both MSSA and MRSA isolates. Previous ATLAS (2004–2018,21 2012–201926 and 2017–201922) and AWARE (January–December 201024) studies also reported high susceptibility (CLSI, 91.4%–100%; EUCAST, 97.4%–100%) to these antimicrobials. High susceptibility to clindamycin observed across regions for S. aureus MSSA, and MRSA (except moderate susceptibility in APAC and Europe) may belie the underlying capacity for resistance development mediated via the erm gene against these isolates and highlights the importance of conducting routine testing for inducible resistance.37

For S. pneumoniae, PSSP and PISP isolates, high susceptibility (93.3%–100%) to ceftaroline was observed according to CLSI and EUCAST. However, for PRSP, higher susceptibility to ceftaroline following CLSI (96.5%–100%) than EUCAST (74.4%–88.7%) was observed globally and across regions except LATAM (100% according to CLSI and EUCAST). Consistent with this study, two global ATLAS studies (2004–2018 and 2017–2019) demonstrated higher susceptibility for ceftaroline against PRSP following CLSI (99.3% and 99.0%, respectively) than EUCAST (86.6% and 87.0%, respectively).21,22 Of note, globally and across regions, although ceftaroline remained active against both PISP (CLSI/EUCAST, 100%/100%) and PRSP (CLSI/EUCAST, 96.5%–100%/74.4%–100%) isolates globally, susceptibility to ceftriaxone varied and was notably lower against PRSP (CLSI/EUCAST, 37.9%–64.7%/0%–2.8%) compared with PISP (CLSI/EUCAST, 99.0%–100%/53.6%–80.5%) isolates. This trend has been observed in previous global studies including an AWARE (2015–2016) study with isolates from both adult and paediatric patients (susceptibility according to EUCAST, PISP ceftaroline 99.5%–100%, ceftriaxone 52.8%–80.6%; PRSP ceftaroline 77.4%–100%, ceftriaxone 0%–6.7%)38 and an ATLAS (2017–2019) study in isolates from paediatric patients (susceptibility following CLSI/EUCAST: PISP ceftaroline 100%/100%, ceftriaxone 99.5%/66.9%; PRSP ceftaroline 99.0%/87.0%, ceftriaxone 54.6%/3.3%).22 This variation in susceptibility patterns of β-lactams (including ceftaroline and ceftriaxone) may be attributed to mutated PBPs and a strong binding affinity of ceftaroline to PBP2B leading to ceftaroline having more potent activity compared with other β-lactams.15,39 However, PBP mutations, which may lead to reduced ceftaroline susceptibility against PRSP isolates, necessitate continued surveillance.

Among other comparators, in this study, linezolid, levofloxacin, tigecycline and vancomycin demonstrated sustained activity against S. pneumoniae, PISP and PRSP isolates (94.6%–100%, MIC90 0.03–2 mg/L), similar to previous studies.21,22,26 This study demonstrated reduced susceptibility to clindamycin and erythromycin against these isolates globally and across regions. In line with this, previous ATLAS studies (2004–2018 and 2017–2019) reported low susceptibility to clindamycin (CLSI/EUCAST: S. pneumoniae ≤74.4%/≤74.7%; PISP 70.2%/61.5%; PRSP ≤35.8%/≤28.9%) and erythromycin (CLSI/EUCAST: S. pneumoniae, 60.3%/60.3%; PISP, 52.1%/42.2%; PRSP, 18.8%/13.2%) against S. pneumoaniae.21,22 Resistance to erythromycin (macrolide) is conferred via alteration of ribosomal target erm(B), a globally predominant genotype in resistant pneumococci, and mef genes encoding efflux pumps.40,41 Resistance to clindamycin (lincosamide) is mediated by erm(B) gene, through overlapping targets with macrolides.40,41 These findings highlight the need for routine testing and surveillance for inducible resistance in these isolates.

For β-haemolytic streptococci, all isolates were susceptible to ceftaroline, linezolid, penicillin and vancomycin. Previous ATLAS studies (2004–2018 and 2012–2019) have demonstrated similar results for these antimicrobials against S. pyogenes highlighting sustained activity for ceftaroline and other antimicrobials over the years.21,26 Another study in the USA (AWARE 2012–2014) in paediatric patients also reported that all β-haemolytic streptococcal isolates (100.0%) included were susceptible to ceftaroline.23 Notably, in this study, higher susceptibility to clindamycin and erythromycin was observed against S. pyogenes than S. agalactiae isolates globally and across regions, with regional variability observed in susceptibility patterns. These results are consistent with findings from a global ATLAS study (2015–2018) including isolates from both adult and/or paediatric patients reporting higher susceptibility to clindamycin and erythromycin against S. pyogenes (EUCAST, 89.2%–98.6% and 85.0%–93.8%, respectively) compared with S. agalactiae (EUCAST, 73.2%–84.7% and 64.2%–75.7%, respectively) across AfME, APAC, Europe and LATAM.42 Increasing resistance to both of these antimicrobials against S. agalactiae have also been reported in a study from Norway (N = 957 from secondary care hospitals) with significant increase in resistance observed from 2004–2006 to 2016–2018 against erythromycin (4% to 22%; P = 0.004) and clindamycin (4% to 20%; P = 0.013), attributed primarily to polyclonal expansion of resistant isolates.40 In this study, for S. dysgalactiae isolates, susceptibility to clindamycin and erythromycin remained the lowest among all antimicrobials. The study from Norway also reported a significant increase of clindamycin and erythromycin resistance to 12% by 2016–2018 (P = 0.012) from 2009 when no resistant S. dysgalactiae isolates were detected, attributed primarily to dissemination of MLSb-resistance gene via conjugative transfer.40 Overall, these findings revealed growing resistance to different antimicrobials against β-haemolytic streptococci highlighting the need for continued surveillance across regions for improved treatment outcomes.

This study has a few limitations. The participating centres or sites collected a pre-defined number of isolates for each species as part of ATLAS programme, therefore the data from this study cannot be interpreted as prevalent or used for epidemiological purposes. Limited isolate numbers observed for some species and regions in this study should be considered while interpreting the findings. Finally, lack of EUCAST-based oxacillin MIC for defining methicillin resistance in S. aureus isolates (following IHMA protocol) led to unavailability of susceptibility and MIC data for MSSA and MRSA isolates (following EUCAST criteria) on the ATLAS database, hence such data could not be incorporated in the study. Furthermore, solely using oxacillin MICs may have resulted in misclassification of some isolates.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that ceftaroline has potent in vitro activity against gram-positive isolates such as S. aureus including MSSA and MRSA, S. pneumoniae including PSSP, PISP and PRSP, and β-haemolytic streptococci including S. pyogenes, S. agalactiae and S. dysgalactiae isolates collected from paediatric patients globally and within geographically distinct regions. Among comparator agents, our study identified that daptomycin, linezolid, vancomycin, tigecycline, teicoplanin and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole had sustained activity against S. aureus, MSSA, and MRSA isolates; linezolid, levofloxacin, tigecycline and vancomycin demonstrated activity against S. pneumoniae, PSSP, PISP and PRSP isolates; and linezolid, penicillin and vancomycin were active against β-haemolytic streptococci isolates. The data reported in this study, collected as part of a comprehensive surveillance analysis, significantly enhance limited existing information on paediatric AMR patterns in clinical isolates of key GPB worldwide and across AfME, APAC, Europe and LATAM.
