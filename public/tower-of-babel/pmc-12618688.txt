Identification of potential natural analgesic compounds through molecular docking-virtual screening, molecular dynamics simulation, MM/GBSA, DFT, and ADMET computations
Authors: Samira Hossaini Alhashemi, Fateme Zare, Sara Sadeghian, Alireza Poustforoosh, Ali Dehshahri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to identify bioactive compounds from twelve medicinal plants known for their analgesic and anti-inflammatory properties using in silico approaches. The phytochemical components were subjected to cross-docking analyses against receptors implicated in pain and inflammation pathways. Based on binding energies, interaction profiles, and key amino acid residues within the receptor active sites, three compounds—apigenin, kaempferol, and quercetin—demonstrated the highest affinity for the cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) receptor. Notably, these compounds share similar structural scaffolds and exhibit analogous interactions with critical receptor residues. Furthermore, pharmacokinetic and toxicity assessments of the selected compounds indicated favorable oral bioavailability and an overall acceptable safety profile. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations revealed that these compounds possess relatively high softness. Molecular dynamics simulations of apigenin, kaempferol, quercetin, and the reference drug diclofenac complexed with COX-2 were performed over 100 ns. Analyses of root mean square deviation (RMSD), radius of gyration (Rg), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), and ligand-protein interactions confirmed the stability of these complexes. Finally, MM/GBSA calculations estimated the binding free energies, with diclofenac and apigenin exhibiting the most favorable values. This study highlights pharmacologically active compounds potentially contributing to the analgesic effects of these plants. Introduction

Pain and inflammation are prevalent global health issues that significantly impair quality of life and impose substantial socioeconomic burdens1. Although non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and opioids remain the mainstay therapies, their long-term administration is constrained by serious adverse effects, including gastrointestinal toxicity, cardiovascular complications, and potential for dependence2. These limitations have intensified the pursuit of safer alternatives, particularly from natural sources traditionally employed to manage pain and inflammatory conditions3. Medicinal plants such as Zingiber officinale4, Curcuma longa5, Lavandula angustifolia6,7, Boswellia thurifera8, and Elaeagnus angustifolia9–12 are extensively documented in both traditional medicine and recent clinical investigations for their analgesic and anti-inflammatory activities. Their bioactive phytochemicals—including flavonoids, terpenoids, and alkaloids—have been shown to modulate key pain-related signaling pathways, underscoring the potential of plant-derived compounds as a valuable source for the development of safer analgesic agents.

In recent years, computer-aided drug design and virtual screening have transformed drug discovery by expediting the identification of bioactive molecules while simultaneously reducing experimental costs and duration13–16. Techniques such as virtual screening, molecular docking, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and ADMET predictions17–19 are widely employed to prioritize compounds exhibiting favorable binding affinities, pharmacokinetic properties, and safety profiles prior to clinical evaluation20–22. These approaches are especially valuable for natural products, which often comprise structurally diverse metabolites with multiple biological activities. Recent studies have successfully utilized virtual screening to identify natural COX-2 inhibitors with enhanced selectivity compared to conventional NSAIDs, alongside phytochemicals targeting opioid receptors, interleukin-1 (IL-1), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNFα), nitric oxide, and histamine signaling pathways20,23–25. Collectively, these findings emphasize the critical role of computational strategies in the exploration of multitarget analgesic compounds derived from herbal sources.

Pain perception and inflammation are complex processes mediated by multiple receptor systems. Notably, nitric oxide (NO) functions as a neuromodulator and vasodilator, enhancing nociceptive signaling and contributing to central sensitization26, thereby amplifying pain perception. Histamine, released from mast cells during injury, induces vasodilation and increases vascular permeability while directly activating nociceptors, resulting in both inflammatory pain and hyperalgesia27. Cyclooxygenase isoenzymes (COX-1 and COX-2) catalyze prostaglandin synthesis; COX-2, in particular, is rapidly upregulated at sites of inflammation and serves as the primary molecular target of NSAIDs28. Key pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNFα) and interleukin-1 (IL-1), sensitize peripheral neurons, induce COX-2 expression, and sustain chronic inflammation29. Concurrently, opioid receptors (µ and κ) exert inhibitory effects by attenuating synaptic transmission of nociceptive signals within the central and peripheral nervous systems30,31. Collectively, these pathways represent complementary mechanisms underlying pain generation and modulation, underscoring the importance of targeting multiple receptor systems in the development of novel, multi-target analgesics.

The present study combines virtual screening, molecular docking, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, MM/GBSA binding Free energy calculations, density functional theory (DFT) analysis, and ADMET prediction to systematically evaluate 300 phytochemicals derived from twelve Iranian medicinal plants traditionally used for pain and inflammation. Unlike prior research that primarily focused on single receptors or limited computational validation20,23,24,32–35, this work employs a comprehensive multi-receptor strategy followed by rigorous computational assessment of compound stability, reactivity, pharmacokinetics, and toxicity. This study is novel in providing a comparative cross-docking analysis across multiple pain- and inflammation-related receptors, followed by advanced computational validations. The urgency of this research stems from the need to identify safe, non-opioid, plant-based analgesics as alternatives to conventional drugs. While the findings are limited by the predictive nature of in silico methods, they provide a rational foundation for subsequent in vitro and in vivo validation. In the future, structure optimization and experimental evaluation of these flavonoid scaffolds could accelerate the development of effective, multi-target natural analgesics with improved safety profiles.

Results and discussion

Virtual screening and molecular docking studies

Initially, twelve plants with well-documented pain-relieving properties were selected based on an extensive literature review, and their phytochemical components were analyzed. The chosen medicinal plants—Zingiber officinale, Eucalyptus camaldulensis, Lavandula angustifolia, Salvia rosmarinus, Curcuma longa, Mentha piperita, Capsicum annuum, Harpagophytum procumbens, Apium graveolens, Boswellia thurifera, and Elaeagnus angustifolia—were selected through an integrative approach combining traditional knowledge, pharmacological evidence from the literature, and phytochemical profiling. Ethnopharmacological records and Iranian traditional medicine texts have long recognized these species for their efficacy in alleviating pain, inflammation, and related conditions. For example, Z. officinale (ginger) and C. longa (turmeric) are widely acknowledged in Persian and Ayurvedic medicine for their anti-inflammatory effects, supported by clinical studies demonstrating cyclooxygenase (COX) inhibition and modulation of cytokine activity36,37. Likewise, B. thurifera (frankincense) and H. procumbens (devil’s claw) are established herbal remedies for musculoskeletal pain and arthritis, with boswellic acids and iridoid glycosides identified as principal bioactive constituents38,39. Aromatic herbs such as L. angustifolia (lavender) and S. rosmarinus (rosemary) contain flavonoids and terpenoids that exhibit both analgesic and neuroprotective properties40,41. Other species, including M. piperita (peppermint) and C. annuum (red pepper), have been traditionally utilized as topical or dietary agents for managing nociceptive and neuropathic pain, primarily via menthol- and capsaicin-mediated mechanisms42,43. Additionally, E. camaldulensis (eucalyptus) and A. graveolens (celery) possess essential oils and polyphenolic compounds reported to attenuate inflammatory responses44,45. Finally, E. angustifolia (Russian olive), a cornerstone of Persian traditional medicine, has demonstrated potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities in contemporary pharmacological studies46. Collectively, these plants represent a chemically diverse repertoire enriched in flavonoids, terpenoids, alkaloids, and phenolic acids—chemical classes repeatedly implicated in the modulation of pain and inflammation. The inclusion of these twelve species in the virtual screening campaign was thus predicated on the convergence of ethnomedicinal application, documented pharmacological activity, and the presence of bioactive scaffolds with known or predicted interactions at pain- and inflammation-related molecular targets. Multiple receptors were examined to discover the most effective natural pain-relieving compounds derived from these plants. According to the literature, the receptors of nitric oxide47, histidine48, COX-12, COX-249, TNFα50, opioids, and interleukin-151 play important roles in effectively treating pain. Therefore, PDB codes of 1m8e52 for nitric oxide, 1p8d53 for histidine, 5c1m20 for the µ-opioid receptor, 4djh54 for the κ–opioid receptor, 6 × 8255 for TNFα, 1bmq for IL-156, 1pxx23 for COX-2 and 3n8z57 for COX-1 were selected for in silico analysis.

These fourteen hits were further compared against each other and reference ligands (diclofenac and celecoxib) to select candidates with the highest predicted binding affinity, key active site interactions, and acceptable pharmacokinetic and toxicity profiles for advanced computational validation. Structures of top-ranked natural compounds from docking studies, grouped into five categories (I–V) based on scaffold similarity. Group I includes flavonoids (apigenin, quercetin, kaempferol, catechin) sharing a flavone scaffold with the highest affinity for the COX-2 receptor, suggesting strong anti-inflammatory and analgesic potential. Group II comprises phenolic and terpenoid-like compounds (6-gingerol, 6-shogaol, curcumin, capsaicin, eugenol, caffeic acid) exhibiting moderate binding across multiple pain-related receptors. Groups III (harpagoside), IV (boswellic acid, oleanolic acid), and V consist of structurally diverse compounds with varied docking affinities, some selectively targeting kappa-opioid or interleukin-1 receptors. This classification underscores the relationship between core scaffolds and receptor binding profiles identified in the virtual screening.

Notably, the internal ligand of the 1m8e receptor demonstrated a binding free energy of -11.3 kcal/mol, whereas all selected ligands exhibited lower affinities. Only harpagoside, boswellic acid, and celecoxib showed binding energies close to this value (-10.1 kcal/mol), suggesting that most studied compounds are unlikely to exert significant effects on the nitric oxide pathway.

The binding energy values of the selected compounds for the receptors 1p8d, 3n8z, 5c1m, and 6 × 82 were lower than those of their respective internal ligands (Table 2). Consequently, none of the studied compounds are predicted to have significant effects on the histidine, µ-opioid, COX-1, and TNFα receptors. For the 4djh receptor, the internal ligand exhibited a binding energy of -10.7 kcal/mol. Among the tested compounds, oleanolic acid and boswellic acid showed the closest binding energies at -10.4 and − 10.3 kcal/mol, respectively; these are the only compounds predicted to interact effectively with the κ-opioid receptor. Regarding the 1bmq receptor, the internal ligand’s binding energy was − 8.3 kcal/mol, while capsaicin displayed a slightly higher binding affinity of -8.8 kcal/mol. Both celecoxib and boswellic acid demonstrated binding energies comparable to the internal ligand, indicating effective interactions of capsaicin, celecoxib, and boswellic acid with the interleukin-1 receptor. For the 1pxx (COX-2 receptor), five compounds, including apigenin, kaempferol, quercetin, curcumin, and harpagoside, exhibited binding energies more favorable than that of diclofenac, which has a binding energy of -8.6 kcal/mol.

Although multiple receptor systems are involved in nociception and inflammation, cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) was selected as the primary focus of this study. Unlike the constitutively expressed COX-1, COX-2 is rapidly upregulated at sites of tissue injury and drives prostaglandin overproduction, establishing it as a central mediator of inflammatory pain. Clinically, most nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) exert their effects via COX-2 inhibition, underscoring their translational relevance. Furthermore, COX-2 benefits from a well-characterized binding pocket, extensive crystallographic data, and validated internal ligands, enabling rigorous molecular docking, molecular dynamics, and free energy calculations. Although other targets such as TNF-α, IL-1, nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and lipoxygenases (5-LOX) were included in the initial receptor panel, most tested phytochemicals exhibited comparatively weak binding affinities toward these proteins in the cross-docking experiments. In contrast, several compounds displayed favorable interactions with COX-2, consistent with its established role as a key regulator of pain and inflammation. Accordingly, COX-2 was prioritized for subsequent in-depth computational validation in this work.

For a more rigorous comparison, quantitative analysis showed that diclofenac formed one hydrogen bond with Ser530 (0.321 nm) and six π contacts, mainly with Met522 and Trp387. Celecoxib established two hydrogen bonds (Ser530 at 0.341 nm, Arg120 at 0.314 nm) and eight π interactions with Gly526, Val523, Leu352, and Leu531. Among phytochemicals, apigenin contributed one hydrogen bond with Ser530 (0.350 nm) and six π interactions, while kaempferol and quercetin each formed two hydrogen bonds (0.289–0.335 nm) with Ser530 and six π interactions involving Gly526, Val523, Ala527, Leu352, and Leu531. Curcumin showed no hydrogen bonds and five π contacts, whereas harpagoside formed three hydrogen bonds with non-key residues (His207, Thr212, Asn382) and four non-critical π interactions. Overall, apigenin, kaempferol, and quercetin consistently formed 1–2 hydrogen bonds with Ser530 and six π interactions with key pocket residues, closely resembling celecoxib. Diclofenac relied more on π stacking with fewer hydrogen bonds, while curcumin and harpagoside displayed weaker or less relevant interactions. These findings highlight apigenin, kaempferol, and quercetin as superior COX-2 binders.

Table 7 summarizes in silico toxicity predictions for the three selected compounds using ProTox 3.0. All compounds exhibited high LD50 values except quercetin, which showed a lower LD50 (159 mg/kg) and a moderately elevated Ames test probability (0.72), indicating moderate risk rather than definitive toxicity. Overall, the ADMET profile supports an acceptable safety margin for all compounds, in agreement with reported safety at pharmacological doses.

The predicted blood-brain barrier permeability and volume of distribution for the selected compounds were found to be within acceptable ranges. DFT analysis

The density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using Gaussian 09 with the B3LYP functional, coupled with the 6-311 G(d, p) basis set21. The structural geometries of the compounds were optimized at their ground state energies22. Subsequently, the energies of the highest occupied molecular orbitals (EHOMO), lowest unoccupied molecular orbitals (ELUMO), and the energy gap between HOMO and LUMO were determined for the selected compounds: apigenin, kaempferol, and quercetin. The HOMO and LUMO orbitals for all selected compounds are illustrated in Fig. 6. As shown in the Fig. 6, the HOMO and LUMO orbitals are distributed throughout the molecules. The energy values of the HOMO and LUMO orbitals are presented in Table 9, which indicates that quercetin possesses the highest energy values for both HOMO and LUMO. Understanding the values of HOMO and LUMO energies, as well as the energy gap between them, is crucial for predicting the behavior of molecules in chemical reactions59. According to the studies, a molecule with a larger energy gap is generally more stable, while a molecule with a smaller energy gap is more reactive in chemical reactions. The energy gaps for the compounds were calculated to be 4.45, 3.92, and 3.86 eV for apigenin, kaempferol, and quercetin, respectively. Based on the calculated energy gaps, the order of stability for the studied compounds is as follows: apigenin > kaempferol > quercetin.

The thermochemical parameters, including total energy (E_total), enthalpy (H), Gibbs free energy (G), and entropy (S), were calculated using the B3LYP/6-31G(d, p) level of theory. Additionally, global reactivity descriptors such as hardness (η), softness (σ), and electron affinity (A) for the studied molecules are presented in Table 9. According to Table 9, quercetin is thermodynamically more stable than the other compounds. As mentioned earlier, the stability or reactivity of molecules can be predicted using the HOMO-LUMO energy gap. Reactivity descriptors derived from HOMO and LUMO energies predict the chemical reactivity of molecules, the analysis of reactions, and the sites of reactions within the molecules60. Softness (σ) and hardness (η) are fundamental electronic descriptors derived from DFT that reflect a molecule’s chemical reactivity and stability. Hardness measures resistance to deformation or change in electron distribution; a harder molecule is less reactive and more kinetically stable. Softness, the inverse of hardness (σ = 1/η), indicates the ease with which a molecule’s electron cloud can be polarized, correlating with higher chemical reactivity and flexibility in interactions. In the analyzed compounds—apigenin, kaempferol, and quercetin—apigenin exhibited the highest hardness (2.22 eV) and lowest softness (0.45 eV⁻¹), suggesting greater kinetic stability and lower reactivity. Quercetin showed the lowest hardness (1.93 eV) and highest softness (0.52 eV⁻¹), indicating it is the most chemically reactive and electronically flexible among the three, with kaempferol occupying an intermediate position. Biologically, increased softness generally enhances the ability of a compound to interact adaptively with biological targets such as enzymes or receptors, facilitating stronger or more diverse binding interactions. Thus, quercetin’s higher softness may underpin its effective engagement with cyclooxygenase-2, supporting its analgesic and anti-inflammatory potential. Conversely, apigenin’s higher hardness suggests more rigid electronic structure, which may correspond to a different mode of interaction and potentially distinct biological stability. Therefore, softness and hardness provide valuable complementary insights into the electronic characteristics that influence the biological activity of these natural compounds.

MD simulation

Although molecular docking provides valuable initial insights into ligand-receptor interactions and binding affinities, limitations exist that must be acknowledged. Docking scoring functions often rely on simplified energy models that neglect dynamic protein conformational changes and explicit solvent molecules, potentially resulting in inaccurate binding energy predictions and ligand pose selections. To overcome these challenges, this study incorporated molecular dynamics simulations and MM/GBSA free energy calculations, offering more realistic assessments of ligand binding stability and affinity.

Molecular dynamics simulations of apigenin, kaempferol, quercetin, and diclofenac complexed with COX-2 (PDB ID: 1pxx) were performed over 100 ns to assess the stability and conformational behavior of the ligand–protein complexes. RMSD analysis (Fig. 8) revealed that the quercetin–COX-2 complex stabilized with the lowest average backbone deviation (~ 2.5 Å), indicating enhanced protein structural stability upon quercetin binding compared to diclofenac, which showed higher average RMSD fluctuations (~ 3.5 Å). Apigenin and kaempferol exhibited intermediate RMSD averages (~ 3.0 Å), with fewer and less pronounced deviations than diclofenac, suggesting comparatively stable complex formation61. To assess the intrinsic stability of the protein, we performed an MD simulation of the Apo form (ligand-free). The RMSD analysis (Fig. 8) shows that the Apo protein undergoes larger conformational fluctuations and exhibits lower overall stability compared with ligand-bound complexes. This indicates that ligand binding contributes to the stabilization of the overall protein structure.

The RMSD values of the protein throughout the MD simulation for ligand-protein complexes of apigenin, kaempferol, quercetin, and diclofenac with COX-2, which converged at about 2.5–4.5Å during the simulation. The Apo protein (protein without ligand) shows considerable instability compared to the other simulations.

Among these, hydrogen bonds involving Tyr385 and Ser530 are particularly pronounced and critical for ligand stabilization. Although the protein backbone RMSD and residue-wise RMSF remain low for all complexes (Figs. 8 and 11), the diclofenac and kaempferol complexes exhibit two transient increases in the radius of gyration (Rg) at approximately 13–14 ns and 68–78 ns (Fig. 12). This observation is not contradictory: RMSD and RMSF quantify global backbone deviation and average per-residue fluctuations, respectively, whereas Rg reflects the instantaneous mass distribution and overall compactness of the protein. Visual inspection of trajectory snapshots from these time windows reveals brief outward motion of a loop or lid region adjacent to the binding pocket, accompanied by a slight repositioning of the ligand toward the solvent. These localized and short-lived events increase Rg without causing significant backbone displacement or broad per-residue variability. Taken together, the data indicate that the complexes remain overall stable but undergo occasional local breathing events at the binding site that transiently reduce compactness. These breathing events likely reflect natural pocket flexibility important for ligand accommodation rather than destabilization of the complex. Quantitative analyses further highlight differences between flavonoid- and diclofenac-bound COX-2 (Supplementary Table S1). Quercetin exhibited the lowest protein RMSD (~ 2.5 Å) and maintained a compact structure with a mean radius of gyration of approximately 24.5 Å, indicating exceptional stability. Apigenin showed similar trends, with an RMSD of about 3.0 Å and an Rg of approximately 24.5 Å. In contrast, kaempferol displayed a slightly higher RMSD (~ 3.5 Å) and moderate fluctuations in Rg, while diclofenac exhibited the highest Rg (~ 26.0 Å) and RMSF values (> 4.0 Å in the active site), suggesting reduced structural stability (Figs. 8, 9, 10, 11 and 12). Flavonoid complexes maintained more persistent hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions with protein residues than diclofenac. Collectively, these results demonstrate that quercetin and apigenin, in particular, form highly stable complexes with COX-2, comparable to or exceeding the stability of the reference compound diclofenac. The two independent 100 ns simulations for each protein–ligand complex produced consistent RMSD and RMSF profiles (Supplementary Figures S1 and S2), demonstrating that the observed stability and interaction patterns are reproducible. No significant deviations between replicate trajectories were observed, supporting the reliability of the reported MD results.

Materials and methods

Virtual screening and molecular docking studies

First, twelve plants known for their pain-relieving and anti-inflammatory properties, including Zingiber officinale62–65, Eucalyptus camaldulensis66–69, Lavandula angustifolia70–75, Salvia rosmarinus74,76,77, Curcuma longa37,78,79, Mentha piperita74,80, Capsicum annuum L81,82. , Harpagophytum procumbens54, Apium graveolens55, Boswellia thurifera56, and Elaeagnus angustifolia57,58 were selected based on reliable scientific sources and traditional Iranian medicine.

Virtual screening was conducted on 300 phytochemicals derived from 12 medicinal plants traditionally used for their analgesic and anti-inflammatory properties. The compounds were initially obtained in SMILES format from Dr. Duke’s Phytochemical and Ethnobotanical Databases and converted to MOL/SDF format. All ligands were constructed and geometry-optimized using Chem3D 16.0 with the MM2 force field, and the minimized structures were subsequently converted into the docking-ready PDBQT format using Open Babel. Target proteins involved in pain and inflammation were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB), including nitric oxide synthase (1m8e), histamine receptor (1p8d), COX-1 (3n8z), COX-2 (1pxx), µ-opioid receptor (5c1m), κ-opioid receptor (4djh), TNF-α (6 × 82), and interleukin-1 (1bmq). Proteins were prepared by removing co-crystallized ligands, water molecules, and ions, followed by the addition of polar hydrogens and Gasteiger charges. Energy minimization of the proteins was performed using the AMBER force field. Molecular docking was conducted using AutoDock Vina. A cubic grid box of 30 × 30 × 30 Å3 was defined for each receptor, centered on the coordinates of the co-crystallized ligand. The exhaustiveness parameter was set to 8 (default), and nine binding modes were generated for each ligand to ensure adequate conformational sampling. Validation of the docking protocol was confirmed by re-docking native ligands into their respective receptors; RMSD values < 2.0 Å demonstrated the accuracy of the docking procedure. The AutoDock Vina scoring function, an empirical free-energy–based algorithm, was applied to estimate binding affinities (ΔG, kcal/mol), accounting for hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, torsional entropy, and van der Waals contributions. For the virtual screening step, docking scores of all phytochemicals were compared across the eight receptor targets. Ligands showing more favorable binding energies than their respective internal ligands or reference drugs (diclofenac and celecoxib) were prioritized as potential hits. The top-ranked compounds were selected for further computational analyses, including hydrogen bonding and π-interaction profiling, pharmacokinetic/toxicity prediction, density functional theory (DFT) calculations, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and MM/GBSA free energy analysis.

In silico prediction of activity spectra for substances (PASS)

The prediction of anti-inflammatory activity for the selected compounds was conducted using PASS Software (https://www.way2drug.com/passonline/index.php). This program computes the predicted activity spectrum of a compound, providing values for probable activity (Pa) and probable inactivity (Pi). The prediction of activity is based on a structure-activity relationship analysis of a training set that includes over 205,000 compounds exhibiting more than 3,750 types of biological activities83. The values of Pa and Pi range from 0.000 to 1.000. Only activities with Pa greater than Pi are considered feasible for a specific compound. If Pa exceeds 0.7, the likelihood of experimental pharmacological activity is high, while a Pa between 0.5 and 0.7 could be considered a moderately active compound. The Pa values less than 0.5corresponds to low active compounds84.

ADMET

In silico ADME, pharmacokinetic, and toxicological properties were computed for selected molecules using online servers. SwissADME (http://www.swissadme.ch/) was utilized to assess pharmacokinetic properties, AdmetSAR (https://lmmd.ecust.edu.cn/admetsar1/) was used to determine absorption and distribution, and ProTox 3.0 (https://tox.charite.de/protox3/) was used to assess toxicological properties. Distribution parameters assessed included blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration, plasma protein binding (PPB), and volume of distribution (L/Kg). Absorption parameters calculated included in vitro permeability in Caco-2 cells, human intestinal absorption (HIA), Caco-2 permeability (cm/sec), and P-glycoprotein (P-gp) inhibition. Toxicological parameters determined included LD50 (mg/kg), toxicity class, Ames test results, neurotoxicity, and cytotoxicity.

DFT analysis

The theoretical computations were performed at the Density Functional Theory (DFT) level using the Becke3-Lee-Yang-Parr (B3LYP) method with the 6-31G (d, p) basis set, utilizing the Gaussian 09 W program package. The calculated structural and vibrational spectroscopic properties were visualized using the GaussView 5.0 program package85. Molecular structures were optimized at the DFT level of theory with the B3LYP/6-311 + + G (d, p) basis set. The outputs were employed to calculate the Highest Occupied Molecular Orbital (HOMO) and Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Orbital (LUMO) energies, Electron Surface Potential (ESP), and thermochemical values using the GaussView 5.0 graphical interface86. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted to investigate the interactions between the highest-ranked compounds and the initial ligand concerning COX-2 (PDB ID: 1pxx) within a dynamic framework using the Desmond software. This simulation utilized a system derived from the docking calculations and was conducted within an orthorhombic box, employing the transferable intermolecular potential with a three-point (TIP3P) solvent model. The protein–ligand complex was placed in an orthorhombic simulation box with a buffer distance of 10 Å between the protein surface and the box edges in all directions, and periodic boundary conditions (PBC) were applied. System equilibration was established by introducing sodium and chloride ions at a concentration of 0.15 M, facilitated by the application of Schrödinger’s system setup, followed by energy minimization (using the default Desmond minimization protocol)88. Equilibration was performed in multiple stages under the NVT and NPT ensembles. The simulation was carried out for a duration of 100 nanoseconds using the default settings within the NPT ensemble framework and 2 2-fs integration time step. Throughout the simulation, the NPT ensemble maintained a consistent number of atoms, as well as stable pressure and temperature conditions. Temperature was maintained at 310.15 K (37 °C) using the Nose–Hoover thermostat, and pressure at 1.013 bar (1 atm) using the Martyna–Tobias–Klein barostat89. Post-simulation analyses included calculation of root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of the protein backbone, root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) of residues, radius of gyration (RoG), and ligand–protein interaction profiles, all performed using the Simulation Interaction Diagram (SID) module of Schrödinger Maestro. To further validate the stability of the complexes and improve sampling, each system was subjected to two independent 100 ns MD simulations initialized with different random seeds for velocity assignment. This replication approach is widely used in computational studies to assess the reproducibility of simulation outcomes without necessarily extending a single trajectory.

Conclusion

This study analyzed phytochemicals from twelve medicinal plants with documented analgesic and anti-inflammatory properties to identify novel natural anti-inflammatory agents. Eight protein targets implicated in pain and inflammation pathways were selected for cross-docking analyses using their respective PDB structures. Fourteen compounds exhibited favorable binding energies across these receptors, with five outperforming the internal ligand diclofenac on the COX-2 receptor (PDB ID: 1pxx). Key active site residues involved in ligand binding included Ser 530, Tyr 385, Val 349, Ala 527, Leu 352, Leu 531, Val 523, and Arg 120. Among these, apigenin, kaempferol, and quercetin were prioritized based on binding affinity, interactions with critical residues, and scaffold similarity. Although primarily identified in Lavandula angustifolia and Elaeagnus angustifolia, these flavonoids are widely distributed among the studied plants, reflecting a common phytochemical repertoire contributing to their analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects. Pharmacokinetic and toxicity assessments indicated favorable oral bioavailability and minimal toxicity for the selected compounds. Predicted anti-inflammatory activity was supported by in silico PASS analysis. Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations revealed that these compounds possess relatively high softness, correlating with enhanced chemical reactivity. Molecular dynamics simulations of apigenin, kaempferol, quercetin, and diclofenac complexed with COX-2 over 100 ns demonstrated stable ligand–protein interactions consistent with docking results. MM/GBSA free energy calculations further affirmed the strong binding affinity of apigenin and quercetin, closely approaching that of diclofenac.

While the selected compounds showed preferential binding to COX-2, their weaker and variable affinities for other receptors—such as COX-1, opioid, TNF-α, and IL-1—suggest a degree of selectivity beneficial for reducing side effects linked to COX-1 inhibition. Nonetheless, potential off-target interactions warrant cautious interpretation. The multi-target engagement may enhance therapeutic efficacy in complex pain pathways but raises concerns regarding target selectivity and safety. Therefore, comprehensive experimental validation, including broader receptor profiling, target selectivity assays, and in vitro/in vivo toxicity studies, is essential to fully characterize their drug-like properties. This work highlights COX-2 as a prioritized target and underscores the balance between efficacy and selectivity in developing natural analgesics from flavonoid scaffolds.
