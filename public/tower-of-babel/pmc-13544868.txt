Sexual Health in Men and Women with Wilson’s Disease: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Jalumuri SS Anjusha, Doniparthi V Seshagiri, Lakshminarayanapuram G Viswanathan, Shaik Ahmed, Sinu Ezhumalai, Binu V Sreekumaran Nair, Madhu Nagappa, Sanjib Sinha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and Objectives:Wilson’s disease (WD) is a systemic copper toxicity disorder, manifesting as hepatic and neurological symptoms. Sexual health in WD is not well studied. This study aimed to understand sexual health impairment and its related factors in men and women with WD.Methods:Men with WD (MeWD) and women with WD (WoWD) were classified as being in the disease-active phase (DAP) or disease maintenance phase (DMP). Disease-related disability was assessed using the Global Assessment Scale for Wilson’s Disease (GAS for WD). Sexual health was assessed with the Arizona Sexual Experience Scale (ASEX). Depression and anxiety were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scales, respectively. Healthy men (MeH) and healthy women (WoH) underwent similar assessments for comparison.Results:The study included 48 MeWD (11 DAP, 37 DMP), 39 WoWD (12 DAP, 27 DMP), 29 MeH, and 34 WoH. In MeWD, ASEX scores (median [Q1–Q3]) were 13 (10–16) in DAP, 13 (11–14) in DMP, and 14 (12–16) in MeH, with no significant differences. However, in WoWD, ASEX scores were more impaired in DAP 19.5 (15.25–24.75) than in DMP 15 (14–17) (P = 0.029) and WoH 14 (13–18) (P = 0.025). ASEX scores correlated with GAS for WD in both MeWD (P = 0.023) and WoWD (P < 0.001), but not with 24-hour urinary copper excretion at diagnosis.Conclusions:Men and women with WD experience impaired sexual health, which may be related to disease-associated disability. Longitudinal disease registries are required to understand the role of copper metabolism in sexual health impairment in WD.

Introduction

Wilson’s disease (WD) is a disorder of copper toxicity resulting from a genetic mutation that affects the mechanisms of copper transport. The hypercupric state is mainly responsible for organ damage, manifesting predominantly as liver and brain dysfunction.[1] Systemically elevated unbound copper levels can affect multiple systems beyond the liver and brain, including the kidneys, blood, bones, endocrine system, and reproductive system.[2] WD can adversely affect reproductive health, which may be related to excessive free copper load, disease-related disability, and medication effects. Maintaining optimal copper balance with lifelong decoppering therapy is essential for the restoration and maintenance of organ function. Symptoms of impaired reproductive health may be subtle, but they can have deleterious effects on sexual function and fertility outcomes. Similarly, symptoms of sexual dysfunction may be significant, but patients may not report them during routine clinical interviews with a physician.

Sexual function impairment in the general population can arise from multiple causes, including direct damage to the reproductive organs due to exposure to heavy metals, chemicals, and drugs, as well as systemic illnesses, metabolic syndrome, psychiatric disorders, and medication-related adverse effects.[3] WD is a systemic disorder of copper toxicity, and previous studies have demonstrated copper deposition in the hypothalamus, pituitary gland, and gonads, which can lead to hormonal dysregulation and impaired sexual function.[4] The diagnosis of WD is frequently delayed, suggesting that some patients may experience prolonged exposure to copper toxicity before treatment initiation.[5] While partial or complete recovery of organ function may be expected with early initiation of decoppering therapy, persistent organ dysfunction and progression to organ failure can occur in some patients despite treatment.[6]

Given the rarity of WD, available data on sexual health in affected individuals are limited. This study aims to provide a preliminary understanding of sexual function in patients with WD across different phases of illness, in relation to copper balance and disease status.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a cross-sectional observational study conducted in two cohorts: (1) men cohort: men with Wilson’s disease (MeWD) and healthy men (MeH); and (2) women cohort: women with Wilson’s disease (WoWD) and healthy women (WoH). The study was conducted between April 2023 and June 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee before commencement. Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants before enrollment.

Selection criteria

Patients with WD (MeWD and WoWD): Patients with WD in the 18–49-year age group were included. Medical records were reviewed to reconfirm the diagnosis of WD. Diagnostic evaluation included initial serum ceruloplasmin levels, 24-hour urinary copper excretion (UCE-24), confirmation of Kayser–Fleischer (K–F) rings by an ophthalmologist, brain imaging findings in patients with neurological presentation, and evaluation by a clinician experienced in the management of WD (minimum of five years’ experience). The diagnosis of WD was established using both the original[7] and modified[8] Leipzig criteria, with a score ≥4. Patients with WD who had major cognitive impairment or severe speech difficulties that precluded completion of questionnaires were excluded from the study.

Healthy controls (MeH and WoH): Healthy controls were recruited from patient caregivers visiting the study site and were randomly invited to participate during the study period. A structured clinical history and physical examination were performed to exclude individuals with major medical, neurological, psychiatric, or endocrine comorbidities.

Assessment of study participants

Patients with WD (MeWD and WoWD): All patients underwent a detailed clinical interview, neurological examination, and review of medical records. Sexual function was assessed using the Arizona Sexual Experience Scale (ASEX). Depressive and anxiety symptoms were evaluated using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scales, respectively. Disease severity and disability were assessed using the Global Assessment Scale for Wilson’s Disease (GAS-WD). Socioeconomic status (SES) was assessed using the Modified Kuppuswamy Scale.

Healthy controls (MeH and WoH): Participants were evaluated by a qualified physician and a neurology trainee to exclude major comorbid illness through history and formal clinical examination. Sexual function was assessed using the ASEX scale. Depressive and anxiety symptoms were assessed using the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scales.

Interpretation of assessment scales and other operational definitions

Sexual function was evaluated using the ASEX, which comprises five domains: sexual drive, sexual arousal, erectile function/lubrication, ability to reach orgasm, and satisfaction with orgasm. Each item is scored on a 6-point Likert scale (1–6), yielding a total score ranging from 5 to 30. Sexual dysfunction was defined as a total ASEX score ≥19, or a score ≥5 on any single item, or scores ≥4 on any three items.[9] The GAD-7 consists of seven questions assessing anxiety symptoms, each rated on a scale of 0 to 3 (ranging from “no difficulty” to “extremely difficult”), resulting in a minimum score of 0 and a maximum score of 21.[10] Similarly, the PHQ-9 has 9 questions to assess depressive symptoms, with scores ranging from 0 to 27.[11] The GAS for WD is a WD–specific disability assessment scale comprising Tier I (four sections assessing global disability, with 20 points) and Tier II (14 sections for neurological assessment, with 56 points.[12] SES classifies households based on education (score 1–7), occupation of the family head (score 1–10), and monthly family income (score 1–10), with income limits updated for the year 2022.[13]

Definitions of disease phases: Based on evidence that toxic copper levels normalize within approximately one year of adequate decoppering therapy,[14] patients with WD were classified into the following disease phases. Disease-active phase (DAP): from the onset or relapse of disease symptoms to completion of one year of adequate decoppering therapy. Disease-maintenance phase (DMP): one year after regular compliance with decoppering therapy, accompanied by clinical stabilization or partial recovery. Non-compliance with decoppering medications: stoppage of medications for more than three months was defined as non-compliance.

Statistical analysis

The data collected were entered into Microsoft Excel 365 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA) and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality assumptions for continuous variables. Mean ± standard deviation (SD) was used to summarize variables that were symmetrically distributed, whereas median (first quartile Q1, third quartile Q3) was used for skewed variables. The three groups were compared using either one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Kruskal–Wallis tests based on normality assumptions. Tukey honestly significant difference (HSD) test or Bonferroni post hoc test were carried out, respectively, when the ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests were significant. For comparison of variables between those with sexual dysfunction and those without, either an independent samples t-test or a Mann–Whitney test was used based on normality assumptions. Spearman’s rank correlation was used to assess the correlation between two continuous variables. The level of statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 for all tests.

Results

Men cohort

Demographic and disease characteristics

The study enrolled 48 MeWD. The median (Q1, Q3) age at disease onset was 14 (12–19) years. Neurological manifestations were present in 47 of 48 MeWD, whereas 15 of 48 had hepatic symptoms at initial presentation. The median delay between symptom onset and treatment initiation was 6 (3–12) months. Median urinary copper excretion at disease onset was 308 (189.25–619.0) µg/day.

At the time of sexual health assessment, MeWD had a mean age of 32.46 ± 7.82 years and a mean disease duration of 16.56 ± 8.53 years. During the disease course, 33 MeWD had noncompliance with decoppering therapy before the study participation, of whom 27 experienced clinical relapses characterized by recurrence or worsening of neurological symptoms. GAS-WD was 13 (6.5–22.75). UCE-24 at recruitment was 91 (38.0–159.0) µg/day. Liver function was normal. Five MeWD were on antipsychotic medications (three on quetiapine 25 mg, one on risperidone 4 mg, and one on chlorpromazine 200 mg), and two were on antidepressants (escitalopram 10 mg).

Sexual function in men with Wilson’s disease

Among MeWD, 11 were classified as being in the DAP and 37 in the DMP. Twenty-nine healthy men (MeH) served as controls. In MeWD, sexual function, as measured by ASEX, did not differ between DAP, DMP, and MeH; however, the DAP group had higher median PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores than the MeH group [Table 1a].

Women cohort

Demographic and disease characteristics

The study enrolled 39 WoWD. The median age at disease onset was 14 (11–20) years. Of the 39 WoWD, 36 had neurological symptoms, 10 had hepatic manifestations, and one was asymptomatic (detected through sibling screening). The median delay from symptom onset to initiation of decoppering treatment was 6 (3–12) months. The median UCE-24 at diagnosis was 197 (89–347) µg/day.

At the time of study participation for sexual health assessment, WoWD had a mean age of 29.9 ± 7.1 years, and the mean disease duration was 13.2 ± 8.0 years. During the disease course, 19 WoWD had noncompliance with decoppering medication, and 17 experienced symptomatic relapses at the time of study participation. Disease disability measured using the GAS for WD score was 11 (5–18). Liver function parameters were normal. The median UCE-24 at study participation was 84 (30.3–113) µg/day. Two participants were on antipsychotics (one on olanzapine 15 mg and another on quetiapine 25 mg), and none were on antidepressant medication.

Sexual function in women with Wilson’s disease

Among the 39 WoWD, 12 were in the DAP and 27 in the DMP. Sexual health measured by ASEX was impaired in DAP compared to both WoH and DMP. Anxiety and depression were also higher in DAP compared to WoH [Table 1b]. Sexual dysfunction was identified in 17 of 39 WoWD (43.58%). Women with sexual dysfunction had significantly higher disease disability scores measured by GAS for WD (P = 0.005) and higher depressive symptom scores (PHQ-9; P = 0.034) [Table 2b]. ASEX scores showed a positive correlation with disease disability (Spearman’s ρ = 0.598, 95% CI: 0.338–0.776; P < 0.001) and also with depression (ρ = 0.459, 95% CI: 0.164–0.692; P = 0.003) and anxiety (ρ = 0.412, 95% CI: 0.082–0.662; P = 0.009). No association was observed between ASEX scores and markers of copper toxicity [Table 3b].

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study assessing sexual health in men and women with Wilson’s disease, sexual function was evaluated using the ASEX scale and did not differ between MeWD and MeH. By contrast, WoWD in the DAP had greater sexual dysfunction compared with those in the DMP and WoH. In both MeWD and WoWD, sexual dysfunction was associated with disease-related disability, as measured by the GAS for WD, and with psychiatric symptom burden, but not with 24-hour urinary copper excretion. Copper toxicity is responsible for the neurological and hepatic dysfunction in WD. Dysability in any chronic illness can cause sexual impairment. Neurological and hepatic disability-associated comorbidities may contribute to sexual impairment in patients with WD.

An online survey from a neurology hospital in China reported sexual dysfunction in 33.9% of men and 63.7% of women with WD.[15] Similarly, the present study showed a higher proportion of sexual impairment in WoWD (17 of 39; 43.6%) than in men with WD (12 of 48; 25%). The rates of sexual dysfunction measured by ASEX in both men and women with WD in the Chinese cohort exceeded national averages. That study identified higher age, lower socioeconomic status, use of antipsychotic and sedative medications, rural background, and occupational factors as contributors to sexual dysfunction. However, it has limitations, including its online design and lack of assessment of psychiatric comorbidities and disease disability, both of which are important contributors to sexual dysfunction and are prevalent in patients with WD. The present study accounted for disease activity, disease disability, copper balance, and psychiatric comorbidities, providing additional insights into sexual dysfunction in WD.

In the present study conducted in a South Indian tertiary care referral center, sexual dysfunction was less frequent in MeWD (25%) compared to WoWD (43.58%), consistent with the Chinese cohort’s observation of greater vulnerability among women. An epidemiological survey conducted in rural South India, near the present study location, reported that 21.5% of men had sexual dysfunction as screened by the ASEX scale.[16] Similarly, a study conducted in an Indian metropolitan city reported 27.18% prevalence of sexual dysfunction measured by ASEX, with female gender and increasing age associated with sexual dysfunction.[17]

Sexual dysfunction in chronic diseases is influenced by multiple factors, including psychiatric comorbidities, medication effects, disease severity, age, and socioeconomic status. Disease-related disability may predispose patients to depression and anxiety, which in turn negatively affect sexual health.[18] In the present study, patients with and without sexual dysfunction did not differ significantly in terms of age, socioeconomic status, duration of illness, medication adherence, or copper toxicity measured by UCE-24 at baseline and at the time of study participation. However, patients with sexual dysfunction had higher anxiety (GAD-7), depression (PHQ-9), and disease disability (GAS for WD) scores.

Disease disability and its associated psychiatric comorbidities are likely the predominant contributors to sexual dysfunction in WD. Chronic illnesses, including neurological and hepatic disorders, along with depression and anxiety, are commonly associated with sexual dysfunction.[18] The etiological factors for sexual dysfunction in patients with WD may not differ from those in other chronic disease conditions. Early diagnosis and timely initiation of decoppering therapy may help minimize disability and reduce the risk of sexual dysfunction. In patients who have achieved stable copper balance yet continue to experience sexual dysfunction, management can follow standard clinical guidelines used for sexual dysfunction associated with chronic illnesses.

Copper toxicity has been shown in experimental and animal models to disrupt the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis, leading to testicular, ovarian, and hypothalamic dysfunction.[19] Studies have also demonstrated reversibility of reproductive hormonal dysfunction following chelation therapy in case reports and animal studies.[2021] Higher toxic levels of copper may be required to cause reproductive endocrine dysfunction than those required for liver and brain damage.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include separate analyses of men and women, accounting for biological differences in sexual health, and stratification of patients based on disease activity status.

However, the study has some limitations, including a modest sample size after stratification by disease phase, reflecting the rarity of WD. A few participants were on antipsychotics and antidepressants (at low doses), which may have had a confounding effect on sexual health. Emerging markers of copper toxicity, such as exchangeable copper and relative exchangeable copper, may provide better insights into copper toxicity–related reproductive health than UCE-24.

Conclusions

Sexual function in WD may be related to disease disability and psychiatric comorbidities resulting from brain and liver damage caused by copper toxicity. Markers of copper toxicity estimated by 24-hour urinary copper excretion showed no association with impaired sexual function. However, with the advent of newer biomarkers, such as exchangeable copper and relative exchangeable copper, which better reflect copper toxicity, further studies are warranted to evaluate their role in reproductive endocrine dysfunction, particularly in drug-naïve patients with reproductive hormonal analysis. Disease registries with larger sample sizes are required to support these findings.
