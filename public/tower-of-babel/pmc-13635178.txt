Disentangling cation–polyanion coupling reveals which anion motion dominates cation transport in solid electrolytes
Authors: Ke Li, Jitai Yang, Yu Zhai, Hui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polyanion dynamics, notably rotation, have long been linked to fast cation transport in solid electrolytes, yet their contribution to ionic conductivity remains unquantified, and even rotation’s role, disputed. Here, we quantify the contributions of polyanion translation, rotation, and vibration to conductivity in plastic crystals by combining a constraint algorithm and machine learning molecular dynamics. Contrary to prior belief that rotation primarily facilitates transport, we reveal that each motion mode can dominate: rotation when its frequency matches the cation hopping frequency, translation at higher rotation frequencies and vibration at lower rotation frequencies. We find that rotation and translation each can enhance conductivity by over two orders of magnitude by inducing cation disorder; rotation can further enhance cooperative cation transport; and polyanion rotation, translation, and vibration all modulate the migration barrier and prefactor. These findings demonstrate that polyanion dynamics are crucial to superionicity and offer design principles for superionic conductors.

Introduction

All-solid-state batteries, which offer high energy density and improved safety, have received considerable attention and extensive research in recent years1,2. One of their key components, the solid electrolytes (SEs), is crucial to the device’s overall performance, and much effort has been devoted to searching for SEs with high ionic conductivity. The rational design of advanced conductive materials largely hinges on a fundamental understanding of their nature at the atomic level3.

From a static perspective, thermally activated cation hops occur by overcoming the energy barrier defined by the rigid host lattice; specific anion packing4, polyanion mixing5,6, and site disorder7 can facilitate cation diffusion. In parallel, dynamic mechanisms of ion conduction have been actively investigated. From a phonon–ion coupling perspective, specific phonon modes have been shown to contribute directly to ion hopping, and their effects on ionic conductivity have been quantified by both theory and experiment8. From a local perspective, correlations between polyanion dynamics and cation hops have been identified in close spatiotemporal proximity9–12. However, in contrast to the phonon–ion coupling, their contribution to conductivity remains unquantified. Among polyanion dynamics, rotation has received the most attention. It is commonly observed in various inorganic superionic conductors, including sulfates13, phosphates14,15, hydrides16, closo-borates17–21, antiperovskites22,23, and thiophosphates9,24–26. Its role is most often described through the paddle-wheel effect13,27, a hypothesis proposed over thirty years ago and since refined, that polyanion rotation facilitates cation migration28. Over the years, some studies have supported its role in enhancing cation conduction9,10,12,23,26,29, while a comparable body of work has challenged its existence11,30–32.

Despite the ongoing debate, several inorganic plastic crystals have been reported in which fast superionic conduction is accompanied by polyanion rotation. In 2007, Matsuo et al.16 discovered that the ionic conductivity of lithium borohydride surged by three orders of magnitude to 10−3 S cm−1 near 390 K. This increase was accompanied by a structural transition from the orthorhombic to hexagonal phase and the onset of quasi-free rotation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{BH}}}}}_{4}^{-}$$\end{document}BH4−. Incorporating lithium halide or nanoconfining can stabilize the high-temperature phase and maintain the anion rotation near room temperature, resulting in fast Li+ conduction33,34. Despite extensive exploitation of the superionic phase, the mechanism behind the conductivity surge in host material, LiBH4, and the specific role of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{BH}}}}}_{4}^{-}$$\end{document}BH4− rotation in this surge remain unclear. In the dehydrogenation process of LiBH4, an intermediate, Li2B12H12, is formed and exhibits high conductivity in its superionic phase. The related electrolyte family spans an extremely wide chemical space, including closo-type compounds17–21 (e.g. M2BnHn (n = 10,12), MCBnHn (n = 9,11), M = Li, Na; Na2B12H12−xIx), nido-type compounds35 (e.g. NaB11H14, Na-7-CB10H13, Li-7-CB10H13, Na-7,8-C2B9H12, and Na-7,9-C2B9H12). These materials exhibit remarkable conductivity, reaching up to 70 mS cm−1 through anion mixing at ambient temperature36. Studies show that in this material class, anion orientational disorder is linked to fast cation transport12,37–39. Another newly developed sodium SE, Na3OBH4, shows conductivity comparable to that of its liquid counterpart. This antiperovskite-type ionic conductor features a dual-anion structure and a highly rotationally mobile \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{BH}}}}}_{4}^{-}$$\end{document}BH4−. The rotation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{BH}}}}}_{4}^{-}$$\end{document}BH4− is proposed to aid Na+-ion transport22,23,40.

Lithium thiophosphate represents another promising SE family and is derived from the Li2S–P2S5 system, with distinct units such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PS}}}}}_{4}^{3-}$$\end{document}PS43− and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{P}}}}}_{2}{{{{\rm{S}}}}}_{6}^{4-}$$\end{document}P2S64−, depending on composition41. One archetype, Li3PS4, transitions from the γ to β phase at intermediate temperature, along with an anomalous increase in conductivity and the onset of anion rotation26,42. The β further transitions to the α phase at higher temperature, which was also proposed to show exceptional conductivity43. Efforts to stabilize these two superionic phases at room temperature have included partial substitution26 and rapid heating of glass44. However, great controversy has arisen on the existence of the paddle-wheel effect in this type of compound10,11,26,30–32. Indeed, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PS}}}}}_{4}^{3-}$$\end{document}PS43− anion appears in many systems known for record-high conductivity 25,45–48, but the coupling between its dynamics and cation motion remains poorly understood. A novel sodium SE, Na11Sn2PS12, displays a three-dimensional chessboard framework of PS4 and SnS4 tetrahedra, achieving high room-temperature conductivity47. Unlike others that transition to a rotor phase at elevated temperatures, this material maintains its phase integrity and anion rotation, which was proposed to aid cation diffusion, down to very low temperatures9. Another recently found thiophosphate SE, Na4P2S6, exhibits high conductivity in γ phase24, where the orientational disorder of the prolate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{P}}}}}_{2}{{{{\rm{S}}}}}_{6}^{4-}$$\end{document}P2S64−, with low point-group symmetry compared to other rotors, and the flexibility of its dihedral angle have been reported.

Across these systems, the onset of polyanion rotation typically accompanies a phase transition, making it experimentally challenging to isolate the effects of rotation from structural changes. Moreover, this longstanding focus on rotation has overshadowed the potential contributions of polyanion translation and vibration, whose contributions to ionic conductivity have not been quantitatively addressed. Similarly, while both the migration barrier and the Arrhenius prefactor govern ionic conductivity, SE design has predominantly targeted the former, with the latter receiving comparatively less attention.

The Nernst–Einstein (NE) equation, which is an approximation to the Green–Kubo formula and converges faster, is often employed to estimate conductivity. However, given the high concentration of charge carriers in superionic conductors, ion–ion correlations can play an important role49–51 and lead to the failure of this approximation52. These correlations may themselves be coupled to anion dynamics, an aspect that has rarely been examined. Capturing them through the Green–Kubo formula, however, requires substantially longer simulations. Significantly longer timescales, even in the millisecond range, can be accessed by employing kinetic Monte Carlo coupled with local cluster expansion; this approach can establish a robust link between experimental and theoretical diffusion results5,53. Similarly, machine-learning interatomic potentials extend simulations to larger system sizes and longer timescales while retaining ab initio accuracy54–56.

In this study, we combine our recently developed constraint algorithm57 and machine learning molecular dynamics (MLMD) simulations to quantify the contributions of polyanion rotation, translation, and vibration to ionic conductivity across six plastic-crystal SEs from three material classes. For each material, we performed MLMD simulations for more than 100 ns at each temperature, in which specific polyanion motion modes were selectively constrained, and evaluated the ionic conductivity using the Einstein–Helfand (EH) relation. We quantify the contribution of the paddle-wheel effect to ionic conductivity and find it always operative. Yet we show that no single polyanion motion mode universally dominates cation transport. The paddle-wheel effect dominates conductivity only when the polyanion reorientation frequency, quantified using the Ivanov model, matches the cation hopping frequency; when reorientation is much faster or much slower than cation hopping, the paddle-wheel effect persists, but conductivity is instead dominated by polyanion translation or vibration, respectively. Beyond identifying the dominant motion modes, we find that anion dynamics modulate ionic conductivity through both the migration barrier, which has been the central focus in SE design, and the prefactor, which can also contribute comparably. Haven ratio analysis further shows cooperative cation transport across all systems. In the borohydrides, this cooperative motion is reinforced by anion rotation. In LiBH4 and Na2B10H10, anion dynamics are decisive for superionicity; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{BH}}}}}_{4}^{-}$$\end{document}BH4− translation and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{B}}}}}_{10}{{{{\rm{H}}}}}_{10}^{2-}$$\end{document}B10H102− rotation each contribute over two orders of magnitude to the conductivity by inducing disorder within the Li and Na sublattices. These findings provide a design principle for superionic conductors exploiting polyanion dynamics.

Across the six SEs, anion dynamics enhance the conductivity. The relative contributions of anion translation, rotation, and vibration vary in each SE, and one of the three dominates across all simulated temperatures. For Na2B10H10, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{B}}}}}_{10}{{{{\rm{H}}}}}_{10}^{2-}$$\end{document}B10H102− rotation governs Na+ transport (Fig. 2a). Under the rotational constraint, the conductivity drops by 3.1 orders of magnitude at 675 K; further constraining translation reduces the conductivity by at least 0.6 orders of magnitude. In the high-temperature polymorph of LiBH4, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{BH}}}}}_{4}^{-}$$\end{document}BH4− undergo rapid reorientation, which has been linked to fast Li+ diffusion58,59. The rotation does enhance the conductivity by an order of magnitude (Fig. 2b). Notably, no Li+ diffusion is observed under RTC at any simulated temperature. This reveals that translation, rather than rotation, governs Li+ transport. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{BH}}}}}_{4}^{-}$$\end{document}BH4− translation contributes more than two orders of magnitude to the conductivity at 575 K. In the thiophosphate α-Li3PS4, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{PS}}}}}_{4}^{3-}$$\end{document}PS43− vibration dominates Li+transport, contributing ~1.4 orders of magnitude at 650 K. Its effect on the conductivity is roughly twice that of either rotation or translation across all simulated temperatures. In the remaining three SEs, the most strongly affecting mode contributes less than one order of magnitude to the conductivity. For Na3OBH4, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{BH}}}}}_{4}^{-}$$\end{document}BH4− translation dominates Na+ transport (~0.6 orders of magnitude). In γ-Na4P2S6, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{P}}}}}_{2}{{{{\rm{S}}}}}_{6}^{4-}$$\end{document}P2S64− vibration dominates Na+ transport (~0.7 orders of magnitude). Na11Sn2PS12 differs from the other two thiophosphates; rotation, rather than vibration, dominates Na+ transport (~0.4 orders of magnitude).

In the borohydrides LiBH4 and Na2H10H10, cation mobility exhibits the strongest responses to anion dynamics among the six SEs studied. Notably, the rotational constraint reduces the conductivity in every one of the six SEs, demonstrating that the long-debated paddle-wheel effect is universally operative, even though its enhancement is not always the largest among the three motion modes. Across these six SEs, all three motion modes (rotation, translation, and vibration) emerge as dominant for cation transport. This reveals that, in plastic crystals, polyanion reorientation does not necessarily dominate ionic conductivity; no single polyanion motion mode universally dominates cation transport.

Anion rotation enhances cooperative transport in borohydrides

For every conductor, the NE conductivity differs substantially from the EH conductivity, but both yield the same dominant anion motion mode. The Haven ratio, defined as the ratio of NE conductivity to EH conductivity, quantifies the deviation of EH conductivity from NE conductivity and, more importantly, it characterizes how cations move cooperatively. Most of the Haven ratios shown in Fig. 3 are smaller than 1, indicating the presence of cation–cation correlation and the resulting cooperative transport. Discussion

Using a constraint algorithm and machine learning interatomic potentials, we quantify the contribution of each anion motion mode to the Einstein–Helfand conductivity across six representative plastic crystals. Although the paddle-wheel effect is universally operative in plastic crystals, no single anion motion mode universally dominates cation transport. Anion translation and vibration can also dominate, and even govern, cation transport. This demands a broader view of cation–anion coupling, beyond the long-standing focus on rotation as the most visually compelling mechanism of cation hopping. More broadly, quantifying the polyanion–cation dynamic coupling provides a complementary perspective to phonon–ion coupling on ion transport.

To understand the origin of these conductivity changes, we examine the activation barrier and the prefactor. Lowering the activation barrier has long been a central strategy in the design of SEs, and we find that anion dynamics can substantially modify this barrier. However, anion dynamics can also alter conductivity substantially without significantly changing the activation barrier, indicating that the prefactor, likely through the attempt frequency and migration entropy, is comparably important. These findings call for SE design strategies that target both the activation barrier and the prefactor. Beyond this single-ion picture, all six conductors exhibit Haven ratios below unity, indicating that cooperative cation motion is common across plastic-crystal SEs. In the borohydrides, anion rotation reinforces this cooperative motion, suggesting another way in which rotation can enhance ionic conductivity.

Finally, anion dynamics can induce dynamic cation disorder, which alone can enhance conductivity by over two orders of magnitude. This disorder takes two distinct forms: anharmonic broadening of cation positions around their lattice sites, and partial occupancy across multiple crystallographic sites. Both differ fundamentally from site disorder. This suggests that SE design should target the dynamic anion–cation couplings that activate such disorder, opening additional pathways for fast ion transport.

Methods

Machine learning interatomic potential

Geometry optimizations and single-point calculations were performed using the projector augmented-wave (PAW)64,65 method and the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE)65 functional in the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) 5.4.466,67. The VASP PAW potentials treat 1, 1, 3, 1, 6, 5, 6, and 4 valence electrons for Li, Na, B, H, O, P, S, and Sn, respectively. The electronic self-consistency convergence criterion was set to 10−6 eV. The convergence tests for k-point sampling and energy cutoff were carried out to ensure that forces are converged to less than 1 meV/Å. Specifically, the plane-wave cutoffs are 650 eV for Na2B10H10 and LiBH4, 600 eV for Na3OBH4, 500 eV for Li3PS4 and Na4P2S6, and 400 eV for Na11Sn2PS12. The Γ-centered k-point meshes are 2 × 2 × 2 for Na2B10H10, LiBH4, and Li3PS4; 3 × 3 × 3 for Na3OBH4 and Na4P2S6; and 2 × 2 × 1 for Na11Sn2PS12. Due to the relatively small number of atoms in the unit cells of LiBH4 and Na3OBH4, we begin with their 2 × 2 × 1 and 2 × 2 × 2 supercells, respectively. Structure manipulation was dealt with pymatgen68.

The DP-GEN scheme (version 0.12.1)69 was adopted for the deep potential (DP)56 model generation. During each iteration, four models are generated, and the σhigh and σlow are set to 0.26 and 0.11, respectively, to evaluate model deviation. A cut-off radii of 7 Å is selected for training, with 400,000 training steps. The exploration phase uses LAMMPS (version 2 Aug 2023)70, with MD steps increasing from 1500 to 10,000. Six temperatures are set, with the highest and lowest temperatures being, respectively, higher and lower than the temperature range used for production MD runs. After exploration, hundreds of candidate configurations are chosen for labeling. The exploration of each system is considered converged when the percentage of accurate configurations reaches 99.5%. After the models trained on unit cells (supercells for LiBH4 and Na3OBH4) converged, we expanded Na2B10H10, LiBH4, Na3OBH4, Li3PS4, and Na4P2S6 to 2 × 1 × 1, 3 × 3 × 2, 3 × 3 × 3, 2 × 2 × 2, and 2 × 2 × 2 supercells, respectively, repeating the process until the same convergence criteria were met. For Na11Sn2PS12, which already has a large number of atoms in its unit cell and is highly time-consuming, no expansion was performed. For Na3OBH4, we first carry out the DP-GEN iterations on the host supercell. After convergence, the MD exploration is performed on a supercell containing one Na vacancy, which converges without triggering any additional labeling. Consequently, the training set consists solely of host supercells. Finally, six model ensembles, each containing four models, were obtained.

To build the test set, configurations are sampled from MD trajectories spanning the temperature range of the production simulations, combining AIMD with longer DP-driven MD, so as to cover a broad and physically representative region of configuration space. All trajectories start from perturbed structures generated using DP-GEN, in which the cell matrix L is transformed into (I + P)L with each element of P drawn uniformly from [ −0.02, 0.02], and every atomic coordinate is independently displaced by a random value in [−0.2, 0.2] Å.

Taking Na3OBH4 as an example, its test set is built on the 3 × 3 × 3 supercell and combines an AIMD set with an MLIP-MD set. For the former, 200 perturbed structures were each run for 0.2 ps of AIMD (150–1000 K), and the final frame of each was retained, giving 200 DFT-labeled configurations. For the latter, 30 perturbed structures were each propagated for 100 ps with the current DP model over the same temperature range and sampled every 1 ps, yielding 3000 configurations; following Imbalzano et al.71, a global fingerprint was obtained for each by summing the local fingerprints of all atomic environments, reduced by principal component analysis, and 300 representative structures were selected by farthest-point sampling and labeled by DFT single-point calculations. The final test set thus comprises 200 AIMD and 300 MLIP-MD configurations.

Molecular dynamics simulation

MD simulations were performed with the trained DP models using a modified version of CP2K v2024.272. The selective constraints on rotor anion motion modes were implemented following the constraint algorithm developed in our previous work57. In RC and RTC simulations, we explicitly constrain each rotor anion’s 3 rotational degrees of freedom (DOF) and 3 translational+3 rotational DOF, respectively. In RTVC simulations, we constrain all DOF of each rotor anion. For Na3OBH4, six 20 ns simulations with different initial velocities were performed for each temperature and constraint using a 7 × 7 × 7 supercell containing 3087 atoms, in which 12 Na vacancies were introduced. For Na2B10H10, LiBH4, Li3PS4, Na4P2S6, and Na11Sn2PS12, three independent 50 ns simulations were performed in supercells containing 1056, 768, 864, 648, and 832 atoms, respectively. All systems were first equilibrated and then simulated in the canonical (NVT) ensemble using a Nosé–Hoover thermostat73,74 with a time constant of 100 fs and a chain length of 3. The equations of motion were integrated using the velocity Verlet algorithm. A time step of 0.5 fs was used for the hydrogen-containing materials and 2 fs for the thiophosphates. Defect calculations

Within the supercell approach, the formation energy of a defect D in charge state q is calculated by 14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta {E}_{D,q}=({E}_{D,q}-{E}_{H})+\mathop{\sum}_{i}{n}_{i}{\mu }_{i}+q{E}_{F}+{E}_{{{{\rm{corr}}}}}$$\end{document}ΔED,q=(ED,q−EH)+∑iniμi+qEF+Ecorr where ED,q and EH are the energies of the supercell containing defect D in charge state q and of the perfect supercell, resepectively. Here, ni is the number of atoms of species i removed (ni > 0) or added (ni < 0) from the pristine supercell, μi is the corresponding chemical potential, EF is the Fermi level, and Ecorr is the finite-size charge correction. The native defects considered in Na3OBH4 include vacancies, antisite defects, and interstitials. Candidate Na and H interstitial sites were identified using the Voronoi tessellation scheme implemented in pylada-defects84. For each defect, charge states of q = −3, −2, −1, 0, +1, +2, +3 were considered. Supercell total energies were computed using the GGA-PBE functional and the projector augmented-wave (PAW) method as implemented in VASP. The total energies of 243-atom Na3OBH4 supercells were calculated using a plane-wave energy cutoff of 520 eV and a Γ-centered 3 × 3 × 3 Monkhorst–Pack k-point mesh. The atomic positions in all supercells were relaxed until the force on each atom was less than 0.01 eV Å−1.

The elemental chemical potentials are written as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mu }_{i}={\mu }_{i}^{0}+\Delta {\mu }_{i}$$\end{document}μi=μi0+Δμi, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mu }_{i}^{0}$$\end{document}μi0 is the reference chemical potential of element i under standard conditions, and Δμi is the deviation from this reference. The values of Δμi are bounded by the phase diagram. When total-energy differences are taken between chemically and physically very different systems, the error cancellation is often incomplete85. Therefore, we used the fitted elemental chemical potentials \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mu }_{i}^{0}$$\end{document}μi0 reported by Liu et al., which were obtained by least-squares fitting to experimental formation enthalpies86. Ecorr includes: (i) the eFNV correction87 for charged defects, including both image-charge correction and potential alignment, (ii) Moss–Burstein type band filling due to shallow defects, (iii) the correction of the band edges for shallow acceptors and donors. The defect calculations were set up and analyzed using doped 3.2.088.

Most xc-functionals, in particular LDA and GGA functionals, severely underestimate the band gap, often by a factor of 2 or more89. The gold standard for quasiparticle band structure calculations of solids is the many-body GW method. In passing the partially self-consistent GW0, the deviation from experiments can fall below 0.2 eV90. The GW quasiparticle energies are calculated using a 6 × 6 × 6 Γ-centered k-point grid for primitive Na3OBH4, with the GW PAW potentials employed in the calculations. The DFT wavefunctions are used as the starting wavefunctions. The quasiparticle energies are iteratively updated until the difference in the band gap between two consecutive iterations is <0.05 eV. Finally, the GGA valence- and conduction-band edges are corrected separately using the corresponding GW quasiparticle energy shifts.
