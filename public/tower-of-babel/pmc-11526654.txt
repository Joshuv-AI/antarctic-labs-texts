Association between Neutrophil-to-high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol ratio and gallstones: insights from the national health and nutrition examination survey (2017–2020)
Authors: Dongchi Ma, Hengjun Ma, Yu Li, Lili Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInflammatory responses and lipid metabolism make vital impacts on the development of gallstones. This study investigated the relationship between gallstone disease (GSD) and the neutrophil-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHR) in American patients with gallstones.MethodsThe data analyzed were sourced from the 2017–2020 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and comprised of participants with complete data on GSD and NHR. The relationship between NHR and GSD was evaluated with weighted multivariable logistic regression analysis. Additionally, subset analyses, interaction tests, smoothed curve fitting, and threshold effect analyses were conducted.ResultsAmong the 7894 participants analyzed in this study, the prevalence of GSD was 10.98%, and the average NHR value was 3.41 ± 0.06. The fully adjusted multivariable logistic regression results demonstrated an obvious positive association between NHR and the likelihood of GSD (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.01, 1.16; P = 0.0197). Consistency of this association was confirmed through subset analyses and interaction tests across various subgroups, including those categorized by smoking status and asthma. Furthermore, smoothed curve fitting and threshold effect analyses revealed a nonlinear relationship with a threshold of 2.86.ConclusionsNHR shows a positive relationship to an increased likelihood of GSD among Americans. It can act as an easy and cost-effective tool for the early detection and management of individuals at risk for GSD.Keywords: High-density lipoprotein-cholesterol, Neutrophil-to-high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol ratio, Gallstones, Cross-sectional study, NHANES

Introduction

As a prevalent clinical condition worldwide, gallstone disease (GSD) ranks among the most common and costly gastrointestinal disorders [1]. There are three primary kinds of gallstones including cholesterol, bile pigment, and mixed, with cholesterol stones being the most frequent, particularly in Western countries [2]. Their formation is primarily due to the oversaturation of calcium salts and cholesterol in bile, leading to cholesterol deposits [3]. Gallstones are a known risk factor for gallbladder cancer [4] and are correlated with other conditions such as colorectal cancer [5], angiocardiopathies [6], and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [7]. Epidemiologic studies from 2000 to 2023 report a global prevalence of 6.1% for gallstones, with variations based on region, sex, and testing methods [8]. In the United States, the prevalence of gallstones ranges from 10 to 20%, having doubled over the past 30 years [9, 10]. With cholecystectomy costing about $6.5 billion annually, the substantial expense for prevention and treatment affects up to 15% of Americans, imposing a significant healthcare burden and underscoring the need for improved gallstone prevention strategies [11–13].

Gallstone formation is influenced by various factors, including sex [11], race [11], lifestyle [14], and diet [15]. Inflammation and lipid metabolism are also vital in the development of gallstones [16, 17]. The neutrophil-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHR), a novel comprehensive marker of inflammatory responses and lipid metabolism, has potential as a clinical indicator for cardiovascular disease [18], depression [19], acute biliary pancreatitis [20], hepatocellular carcinoma [21], and other conditions due to its ease of measurement, cost-effectiveness, and reliable standardization of hematological parameters. Prior research has indicated that inflammatory reactions can promote gallstone formation by altering protein and lipid metabolic pathways and increasing bile acid salt concentrations [22]. Neutrophils, as proinflammatory cells involved in pathogen resistance [23], have been less studied in relation to gallstones. Lipids contribute to the formation of keystones that lead to gallstone development, particularly when cholesterol levels are elevated [17]. High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), with its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, may act as a protective factor against gallstones and could be a strong predictor of gallstone occurrence [17, 24]. Several studies have shown that the NHR is more effective than markers such as monocyte-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (MHR) and neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio (NLR) in predicting certain diseases [25, 26], suggesting its strong predictive potential. However, the association between NHR and gallstones remains unexplored.

Therefore, this study examined the potential association between NHR and gallstone prevalence on the basis of data from the 2017–2020 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). It focused on the distinctive role of NHR in gallstone formation and explored the associations of inflammation and lipid metabolism with gallstones, offering new insights into gallstone biomarkers. Additionally, a better understanding of the NHR-gallstone relationship will assist healthcare professionals in developing targeted preventive and therapeutic strategies to enhance the care and management of GSD patients.

Methods

Data sources

The NHANES project studies nutritional and health trends among American individuals using a systematic multistage probability sampling method. Data are collected through face-to-face interviews, health check-ups, and laboratory tests and are published biennially. The Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) has approved the ethical aspects of the NHANES project, including obtaining written informed consent from all participants. Moreover, a detailed overview can be obtained at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm.

Study population

Data from the 2017–2020 NHANES cycles were combined for the present study, encompassing 15,560 participants completing demographic surveys, laboratory tests, and health status questionnaires. Individuals were excluded if they had missing data for neutrophil (n = 3409) or HDL (n = 1333) levels or if they had missing gallstone data (n = 2924). Following the final screening, 7894 participants were included in this study. Details are provided in Fig. 1.

Flowchart for screening subjects from the NHANES

Assessment of the NHR and gallstones

NHR, the exposure variable in this study, suggests the ratio of neutrophils (1000 cells/µL) to HDL-C (mg/dL) levels. Complete blood counts were obtained using a DxH 800 analyzer (Beckman Coulter) from participants’ blood samples, providing detailed hematocrit data. HDL-C levels were measured using an HDL-C-specific end-point reaction, with photometric monitoring at 600 nm to ensure accurate results. Participants were categorized into tertiles according to their NHR values: T1 (< 2.366), T2 (2.366–3.699), and T3 (> 3.699).

Gallstones were considered an outcome variable in the study. The presence of GSD was identified on the basis of the question: “Have you ever been told by a physician or other health professional that you have gallstones?”

Covariables

Given the potential influence of additional factors on gallstones, confounding variables were accounted for based on previous literature. Demographic factors included sex, age, race, length of schooling, and the household income–poverty ratio (PIR). Lifestyle factors considered were moderate activity, smoking status (defined as having smoked at least 100 cigarettes in a lifetime), and drinking status (defined as consuming any alcoholic drink at least once per month in the past year). Comorbidities assessed included diabetes, hypertension, asthma, and cancer.

Statistical analysis

To ensure national-level representativeness, appropriate weighting methods were applied to explain the complex sample design of the NHANES. Continuous data were depicted with medians (interquartile ranges) (normality tests suggested that continuous variables were all non-normally distributed), while categorical data were shown to be counts and percentages (%). To assess intersubgroup differences, the weighted Wilcoxon rank sum test and the weighted χ2 test were adopted for evaluating disparities between subgroups for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. The association between NHR and GSD was examined via weighted multifactor logistic regression (odds ratio[OR] and 95% confidence interval[CI]). Models applied included: Model 1, a covariable-unadjusted model; Model 2, adjusted for sex, age, and ethnicity; and Model 3, adjusted for education attainment, PIR, smoking status, drinking status, moderate activity, diabetes, hypertension, total cholesterol, and triglycerides, in addition to sex, age, and ethnicity. The study also included stratification by age, sex, ethnicity, education attainment, smoking status, drinking status, moderate activity, diabetes status, hypertension status, asthma status, and cancer status, with weighted multivariable regression subgroup analyses conducted. Multivariable regression was used to test for cross-subgroup heterogeneity in associations. Finally, the relationship between NHR and gallstones was analyzed using smoothed curve fitting (SCF) with a generalized additive model (GAM). Significant inflection points were identified through a recursive algorithm if nonlinear associations were detected, and the threshold effect of NHR on gallstone prevalence was assessed. Data analysis was performed with Empower Stats (http://www.empowerstats.com) and R 4.2.3 software, with statistical significance determined at p < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

Totally 7894 participants from the 2017–2020 NHANES with complete NHR data were analyzed, including 4070 females and 3824 males, all aged ≥ 20 years, with a median age of 48.00(33.00, 62.00) years and an overall prevalence of gallstones of 10.98%. We stratified participants’ clinical characteristics according to the presence of gallstones and found statistically significant differences between the two groups for gender, age, race, household PIR, smoking status, alcohol consumption status, moderate activity, diabetes status, hypertension status, cancer status and neutrophile count. (all P < 0.05). GSD patients were more frequently female, older, non-Hispanic white, diabetic, hypertensive, cancer patients, smokers, and nonalcohol drinkers. They exhibited significantly higher levels of neutrophils, while having lower PIRs (P < 0.05). Relevant details are presented in Table 1.

Clinical features of the participants according to the tertile of the NHR

The median NHR value was 3.03(2.13, 4.23), with participants divided into low (T1), medium (T2), and high (T3) tertiles of 2630, 2629, and 2635, respectively. The prevalence of gallstones in each tertile was 8.58%, 11.35%, and 12.82%, respectively. Clinical and biochemical characteristics were stratified by NHR tertile, as detailed in Table 2. The high-NHR tertile groups had higher proportions of males, smokers, and nondrinkers, and showed ethnogeographic differences (P < 0.05). Additionally, neutrophile count were significantly higher, while HDL-C levels levels were obviously lower (P < 0.05). Relative to those in the lowest NHR tertile, individuals in higher tertiles had a significantly greater proportion of diabetes and hypertension, as well as lower educational attainment and PIR (P < 0.05).

Relationships between the NHR and gallstones

Details of the subgroup analysis

The relationship between NHR and gallstone prevalence was evaluated through subgroup analysis and interaction tests, with the results detailed in Table 4. In addition, the positive association between NHR and gallstones was significantly influenced by asthma and smoking (P < 0.05). The association remained significant in females (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.04, 1.15, P = 0.0077), individuals having high school education or higher (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.03, 1.19, P = 0.0385), alcohol consumers (OR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.03, 1.25, P = 0.0340), nondiabetic individuals (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.04, 1.16, P = 0.0108), and noncancer patients (OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.02, 1.13, P = 0.0342). Conversely, stratification by age, race, and hypertension did not significantly alter the association between NHR and gallstones (P > 0.05).

Nonlinear relationships between the NHR and gallstones

In this work, the nonlinear association between the NHR and gallstones was assessed using SCF and threshold effect analysis. The findings from the fully adjusted model illustrated a nonlinear relationship between the NHR and gallstones (Fig. 2). The threshold effect analysis detected the critical inflection point as 2.86 (Table 5). Before reaching the inflection point, the NHR was shown to be significantly positively related to gallstones (OR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.14, 1.60; P = 0.0006). After the inflection point, the association became statistically insignificant (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.98, 1.09; P = 0.1826).

Discussion

In this work, the association between the NHR and the likelihood of developing gallstones was investigated, demonstrating a positive association that persisted even after accounting for multiple confounders. Further subgroup analyses clarified the interaction between asthma status and smoking status, indicating statistically obvious differences in the association between NHR and gallstones on the basis of the presence or absence of asthma and among various smoking status subgroups. Results from smoothed curve fitting suggested that an NHR value below 2.86 might be useful for assessing gallstone risk, providing relevant evidence for future clinical and basic research.

In recent years, a growing number of composite predictors based on hematological parameters have been utilized in clinical practice. Previous research has indicated that metrics including the MHR [20], systemic immune-inflammatory index (SII) [27], NLR [28], platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) [29], and triglyceride glucose index (TyG) [30] are employed to evaluate the potential risk of gallbladder disease, highlighting the significant role of inflammatory responses and lipid dysmetabolism in the progression of gallbladder disease.

NHR, an innovative composite indicator, is calculated from neutrophil counts in peripheral blood and HDL cholesterol concentrations. Unlike single indicators, NHR simultaneously reflects the relationship between two different pathways of lipid metabolism and inflammatory states, revealing the interaction between HDL-C and neutrophils [19]. However, the biological mechanisms underlying the relationship between NHR and GSD remain not fully understood. HDL-C plays a crucial role in human reverse cholesterol transport and offers various protective effects, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antithrombotic properties [31, 32]. Zhang et al. found that high levels of HDL-C serve as a protective factor against GSD, a finding supported by a prospective study in a European population [33]. HDL-C’s mechanism of action includes enhancing cholesterol solubility in bile by promoting hepatic bile acid synthesis and reducing cholesterol saturation, thereby preventing gallstone formation [34, 35]. Additionally, free cholesterol in HDL-C is preferentially secreted into bile as bile acids rather than cholesterol [17]. Neutrophils, as the most common white blood cells in human circulation [23], contribute to gallstone formation through the release of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) [36]. NETs are extracellular structures consisted of DNA, histones, and antimicrobial proteins trapping and killing pathogens [37]. According to research from the University of Erlangen, Germany, NETs facilitate the aggregation of calcium and cholesterol crystals during gallstone formation by acting as a binding matrix [36]. This aggregation leads to the formation of larger stones, with DNA encapsulating the crystals [36, 38]. In this study, conflicting results were observed for neutrophils between the unadjusted and fully adjusted models, indicating that their role in relation to GSD requires further investigation. In conclusion, neutrophils may be considered a risk indicator for GSD, while HDL-C levels could serve as a protective indicator, and using NHR to identify GSD shows promise.

Gallstone occurrence is caused by the interplay between environmental and genetic factors [39]. In this study, we revealed a positive association between NHR and gallstone risk among participants who smoked and had asthma. Consistent with these findings, a meta-analysis reported a significantly higher incidence of gallbladder disease among smokers [40]. A Mendelian randomization study demonstrated a positive dose-dependent relationship between smoking, addictive behavior, and increased risk of gallstones [41]. Smoking may contribute to higher cholesterol levels leading to dyslipidemia, which can cause cholesterol oversaturation and gallstone formation [42]. Additionally, nicotine might stimulate increased secretion from the mucous membrane of the gallbladder wall, promoting cholesterol precipitation from bile [43]. The mechanisms linking asthma to gallstone risk are not fully understood but may involve the following factors: (1) Asthma patients often have higher obesity rates. Elevated cholesterol levels in obese individuals can lead to cholesterol precipitation in bile, resulting in stone formation [44]. Obesity may also impair gallbladder contractility and cause insulin resistance, contributing to cholesterol deposition. (2) Long-term steroid use in asthma patients can alter bile acid and cholesterol metabolism, increasing gallstone risk [39]. (3) Chronic inflammation in asthmatic patients may impact systemic metabolism and immune responses, leading to changes in cholesterol metabolism and bile composition, which could indirectly raise the risk of gallstones [16].

Strengths and limitations

Several advantages of this study are outlined as follows: First, the use of NHANES as the data source ensures that the sample size is both large and representative. Second, confounders were thoroughly adjusted for in fully adjusted models to enhance the reliability of the findings. Additionally, subset analyses and interaction tests were performed, demonstrating the stability of the results. Lastly, considering that routine blood tests and biochemical analyses are both economical and convenient, and provide a broad range of informative parameters, NHRs exhibit significant potential for the prediction and management of GSD, warranting further in-depth research and analysis.

However, several shortcomings are present in this study. First, the identification of gallstones through personal interviews may introduce recall bias, recording errors, or interviewer bias. Some patients with gallstones who did not undergo physical examination and were asymptomatic might have been missed. Second, the database lacked information on specific types of gallstones, which somewhat limited the depth of the study. Third, while multiple covariables were adjusted for, the effects of other potential confounders could not be excluded. Finally, the used cross-sectional survey design precluded the determination of causal relationships between NHR and gallstones. Future large-scale prospective studies are necessary to investigate the relevant causal mechanisms.

Conclusion

This work demonstrates a strong association between an elevated NHR and gallstones in American adults. The positive association was maintained in the smoking and asthma subgroups, and further validation in other populations is necessary.
