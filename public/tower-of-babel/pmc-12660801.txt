District-level climate vulnerability assessment in Meghalaya using an IPCC AR5 framework
Authors: Vivek Lyngdoh, Marbakor Mary Lynrah, Evenstone Wahlang, Amica L Nongrang, Nivanaliza Wahlang, Albert Chiang, Joram Beda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate change poses growing risks to ecologically fragile and socio-economically vulnerable regions such as Meghalaya in the Indian Himalayan Region. This study aims to assess and compare district-level climate vulnerability in Meghalaya using an indicator-based framework aligned with the IPCC’s AR5 conceptualization. The primary objective is to identify key drivers of vulnerability and provide spatially explicit insights to inform adaptation planning. The analysis uses eight indicators representing both biophysical and socio-economic dimensions, slope gradient, forest cover, crop yield variability, population density, female literacy, infant mortality, Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI), and NREGS man-days, derived from official government sources for the period 2020–2024. Data were normalized according to their functional relationships with vulnerability, and unequal indicator weights were assigned through expert consultation. Composite Vulnerability Indices (CVI) were generated to rank districts, supported by GIS-based spatial analysis and attribution of indicator contributions. Results reveal that West Khasi Hills and East Garo Hills are the most vulnerable districts, primarily due to steep slopes, forest degradation, and high poverty levels. Conversely, East Jaintia Hills and South West Khasi Hills exhibit lower vulnerability owing to stronger adaptive capacities. The study concludes that enhancing forest conservation, livelihood diversification, and social protection mechanisms is critical for reducing vulnerability in high-risk districts. This framework offers a replicable approach for rapid vulnerability assessment in data-scarce mountainous regions.Keywords: Indicators, Sensitivity, Adaptive capacity, Adaptation, Susceptibility, DriversSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Climate change poses wide-ranging challenges across environmental and socio-economic sectors, with its adverse impacts being especially pronounced in developing countries, where limited social, technological and financial resources heighten vulnerability1,2. The Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) of the IPCC highlights that South Asia is among the most climate-vulnerable regions globally, facing increasing risks from floods, droughts, landslides, and heat extremes, which directly threaten food security, human health, and ecosystem services1. For instance, changes in monsoon dynamics and erratic rainfall patterns are projected to reduce agricultural productivity, while rising temperatures and humidity exacerbate vector-borne diseases such as malaria and dengue3. Similarly, fragile mountain ecosystems like the Himalaya face accelerated glacier retreat, altered hydrological regimes, and forest degradation, compounding risks for dependent communities4,5. These risks are amplified by socio-economic vulnerabilities in the Indian Himalayan Region, including poverty, livelihood dependence on natural resources, and limited access to infrastructure6. Given these risks, adaptation has become a central pillar of climate policy in the Global South. Adaptation strategies span multiple scales, ranging from community-based responses such as livelihood diversification, water harvesting, and agroforestry to institutional mechanisms including climate-resilient agriculture, early-warning systems, and the integration of vulnerability indices into state-level planning1,5,7. However, the effectiveness of adaptation is often constrained by governance gaps, data scarcity, and uneven access to resources, particularly in marginalized regions like the Indian Himalayan states5. Within this broader context, Meghalaya, situated in the Indian Himalayan Region, provides a critical case for examining district-level vulnerabilities and informing mountain-specific, multi-hazard adaptation strategies.

Meghalaya, located in the northeastern India within the Eastern Himalayan zone, is particularly vulnerable due to its steep terrain, erratic rainfall, and reliance on climate sensitive sectors such as agriculture and forestry. These vulnerabilities are exacerbated by uneven socio-economic development, poverty, and healthcare disparities, which collectively elevate the region’s sensitivity and limit its adaptive capacity. Vulnerability is widely recognized as a multidimensional concept encompassing biophysical exposure and socio-economic sensitivity. As defined in the IPCC, vulnerability refers to the “propensity or predisposition to be adversely affected”. This concept has guided global assessments and evolved significantly over the past two decades8.

Earlier studies focused on biophysical assessments primarily on natural hazards but contemporary literature embraces more integrated approaches that include socioeconomic, cultural, and institutional dimensions. Foundational work by Adger (2006) highlighted the role of adaptive capacity in shaping vulnerability9, while O’Brien et al. (2007) distinguished between outcome and contextual vulnerability the latter emphasizing underlying systemic fragilities rather than exposure to climate events10. This study aligns with the contextual vulnerability perspective by focusing on internal characteristics such as sensitivity and adaptive capacity, rather than external hazard exposure. The conceptual shift in vulnerability framing was formalized in the IPCC’s Fifth Assessment Report (AR5) which redefined vulnerability as an intrinsic property shaped by system-level sensitivity and adaptive capacity8. Exposure, previously treated as part of vulnerability in earlier reports, is now considered a separate component within the risk framework. In recent years, syntheses such as Understanding Human Vulnerability to Climate Change: A Global Perspective on Index Validation for Adaptation Planning (2022) and Taking Stock of Recent Progress in Livelihood Vulnerability Assessments to Climate Change in the Developing World (2024) have reinforced this evolution by advancing indicator-based methodologies. Both highlight the need to integrate socioeconomic, institutional, and governance dimensions into vulnerability analysis, while also addressing challenges of data scarcity and methodological consistency. These global insights provide the backdrop for regional applications11,12. Sharma et al. (2018), through the Indian Himalayas Climate Adaptation Programme (IHCAP), translated this conceptual model into an operational methodology for rapid assessments in data-scarce mountainous regions13. Their Tier 1 framework, utilizing secondary data and expert-derived indicator weights, serves as the analytical foundation for the present study.

Climate Vulnerability assessments have evolved significantly across South Asia, with India, Bhutan, Pakistan, and Afghanistan adopting varied approaches tailored to their unique geographic, socio-economic, and institutional context. These diverse approaches reflect both the complexity of the region and the growing recognition of localized, evidence-based planning in climate adaptation. Under the Himalaya Climate Change Adaptation Programme (HICAP), the International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development (ICIMOD), in collaboration with regional partners developed the Vulnerability and Adaptive Capacity Assessment (VACA) framework14. This household-level survey designed was implemented across three sub-basins within the Hindu Kush Himalaya (HKH) region namely the Upper Indus (Pakistan), Eastern Brahmaputra (India), and Koshi (Nepal) to capture insights into livelihood vulnerabilities linked to climate and environmental change. Building on this assessment, Gerlitz et.al. (2017) designed the Multidimensional Livelihood Vulnerability Index (MLVI) to assess climate-related livelihood vulnerabilities across 16 districts in the Hindu Kush region15. This index (MLVI), using primary data gathered by the application of the VACA survey represents three dimensions of vulnerability; exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity which is based on the IPCC Fourth Assessment Report. Further, Manglem & Deva (2020) critically reviews the divergent meanings and conceptualizations of vulnerability used in India16, comparing outcome vulnerability (focusing on impacts) and contextual vulnerability (considering underlying socio-economic factors)16. The study discusses how these conceptual choices influence the selection of methods (top-down versus bottom-up approaches) and the resultant vulnerability scores and rankings16. In line with this, several bottom-up vulnerability assessments have already been undertaken across the Himalaya, including studies on community livelihood vulnerability, urban informal settlers, and water-related vulnerabilities. While these provide valuable localized insights, they remain fragmented and often lack methodological consistency, which constrains their scalability and comparability across the wider Himalayan region. This knowledge gap, between rich but localized case studies and the need for systematic, scalable, and policy-relevant assessments, motivated the present study. The novelty of this work lies in operationalizing the IHCAP Tier 1 framework, which builds on the contextual vulnerability perspective while enabling district-level comparability across a broader mountainous region. By integrating theoretical understanding with a standardized, indicator-based methodology, the study advances vulnerability assessment from case-specific applications toward a consistent and policy-informative approach that supports climate adaptation planning in the Himalaya.

Across the broader Himalayan region, several frameworks have been developed to capture the complex interplay of climatic, ecological, and socio-economic factors influencing vulnerability. The Indian Himalayan Climate Adaptation Programme (IHCAP) has advanced a tiered indicator-based methodology to enable rapid appraisals in data-scarce mountain settings13, while ICIMOD’s transboundary initiatives have emphasized livelihood-centered and household-level assessments14. Studies from Nepal, Bhutan, and the Indian Himalayan Region highlight recurrent drivers such as terrain fragility, forest degradation, and socio-economic marginalization17,18, yet they also differ in scale and emphasis. Some, like the Multidimensional Livelihood Vulnerability Index, focus strongly on household sensitivity and adaptive capacity19, while others integrate exposure and hazard projections, creating methodological inconsistencies across the region. Meta-analyses of vulnerability assessments in the Himalaya point to fragmented approaches that often lack comparability and fail to translate into district-level policy actions18. These regional insights highlight the urgency of developing standardized yet context-sensitive methods that balance data constraints with policy relevance, especially for ecologically fragile states like Meghalaya.

At the national scale, efforts like the Council on Energy, Environment and Water’s (CEEW) Climate Vulnerability Index20 confirm the feasibility of district-level composite assessments based on sensitivity and adaptive capacity. Arora et al. (2023) critique India’s vulnerability frameworks for their limited translation into policy action, advocating for equity-integrated diagnostics21. Similarly, Gupta et al. (2024) emphasize the need for spatially explicit vulnerability assessments that capture ecological heterogeneity along altitudinal gradients in the Himalaya, underscoring the importance of linking spatial diagnostics with ecosystem-based adaptation planning22. Our study complements this perspective by extending vulnerability assessment to the district scale, integrating both ecological and socio-economic dimensions within a policy-relevant framework. Kumar & Mohanasundari (2025) employ a multidimensional approach combining statistical trend analysis using the Modified Mann–Kendall (MMK) test, a risk assessment framework based on the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6), and multiple linear regression23. The study integrates both quantitative climate data and household perceptions to assess vulnerability at the community level. The analysis shows an increasing trend in rainfall and temperature and certain indicators, such as housing quality and food security, are identified as significant contributors to overall vulnerability23.

The Indian Himalayan Region (IHR) is highly susceptible to climate change due to its fragile topography, sensitive ecosystems, and socio-economic reliance on climate-sensitive sectors8. Studies have highlighted the region’s vulnerability to multiple hazards, including floods, landslides, and glacial lake outburst floods, which are exacerbated by increasing human pressures and infrastructure development. The National Mission for Sustaining the Himalayan Ecosystem (NMSHE) provides a framework for assessing vulnerability as a function of exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity, offering a structured approach for mountain regions24. Within this regional Himalayan context, Meghalaya presents a compelling case for district climate vulnerability assessments. The State’s significant reliance on climate-sensitive sectors, such as agriculture and forestry, combined with its topographically fragile landscape and increasing exposure to extreme weather events, including heavy rainfall, landslides, and floods makes it particularly vulnerable to climate change impacts. A recent study on identifying climate vulnerability hotspots for the State of Meghalaya using high-resolution climate models suggested that the majority of the districts are highly vulnerable to climate change impacts25. It is also projected that there will be a rise in temperature by 1.5 °C to more than 3.8 °C under different projected future climate scenarios. At the same time, rainfall is expected to increase and is likely to become more erratic in nature25. Different projected scenarios14 further indicate that the monsoon precipitation intensity will be more but with a lesser span of rainy days. It is also expected that in future climate scenarios, the number of extreme climate events such as floods, heatwaves, storms, etc., is set to rise25. Furthermore, disparities in adaptive capacity across districts underscore the need for a detailed assessment to inform targeted policy interventions linked with quantitative results. These state-specific projections underscore the need for a district-level framework that can guide both adaptation planning and resource allocation, contributing to a mountain-specific, multi-hazard risk management approach applicable across the Himalayan region.

Meghalaya’s climate vulnerability is shaped by a unique interplay of socio-economic and biophysical factors, as outlined in regional and national studies13,15,21. Previous assessments have emphasized the need for localized, indicator-based frameworks that reflect contextual realities and support policy translation. Building on these insights, the present study not only identifies spatial patterns of vulnerability but also serves as a decision-support tool for adaptive governance. The composite vulnerability index enables targeted resource allocation to high-risk districts, supports sector-specific planning, and aligns with national programs such as Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA) and National Rural Livelihood Mission (NRLM). By adopting a Tier 1, IPCC AR5–aligned methodology, the study is replicable for other mountainous regions facing similar data constraints. Moreover, it facilitates interdepartmental convergence and strengthens the State’s case for accessing domestic and international climate finance1,20.

Despite the growing body of research on climate vulnerability in the Himalaya, important gaps remain in both scale and conceptual framing. Many assessments have focused on household- or community-level livelihood surveys, which, while valuable, often lack comparability across administrative units. Others have relied heavily on exposure-based indices, limiting their ability to capture the intrinsic socio-economic and institutional factors that shape resilience. At the same time, state- and national-level composite indices tend to obscure local heterogeneity, particularly in geographically complex regions such as Meghalaya. Few studies have applied a district-level, indicator-based approach that explicitly follows the IPCC AR5 framing, which defines vulnerability in terms of sensitivity and adaptive capacity independent of hazard exposure. This study addresses that gap by developing a replicable, district-scale framework tailored to the socio-ecological context of Meghalaya. By integrating both biophysical features (e.g., slope, forest cover, yield variability) and socio-economic dimensions (e.g., poverty, health, literacy, livelihood support), it advances a more context-specific and policy-relevant understanding of vulnerability in the Indian Himalayan Region.

Building on regional evidence and methodological guidance (13;21; 1), this study hypothesizes that districts in Meghalaya exhibiting steeper terrain, degraded forest cover, and pronounced socio-economic disparities (e.g., poverty, limited livelihood options, poor health access) are more vulnerable to climate impacts due to elevated sensitivity and reduced adaptive capacity. In light of these challenges, the study’s novelty is summed up as (i) is a first to apply a district-level assessment using the common framework for Meghalaya, which is based on the IPCC-AR5 (ii) will generate district-wise vulnerability profiles based on biophysical and socio-economic indicators, and (iii) identify key drivers of vulnerability at both the state and district levels.

Study area

Meghalaya is located in India’s northeastern region within the Eastern Himalayas (Zone II), spans 22,429 sq. km across 12 districts, including the bifurcated district of West Khasi Hills into Eastern West Khasi Hills District in 2021(however, this assessment considers the former 11 districts due to data constrains). Bordered by Assam and Bangladesh, the State features predominantly hilly terrain, with elevations ranging from 50 to 1966 m. The central and eastern part i.e. the Khasi and Jaintia Hills region respectively form a high plateau, while the western Garo Hills are lower in elevation. Influenced by the Southwest monsoon and Northeast winter winds, the state exhibits extreme rainfall variability from 4000 mm in most areas to over 8,000 mm in the southern slopes. The State’s population is largely rural, with a significant proportion dependent on climate sensitive sectors such as agriculture, forest-based livelihood and small-scale water resource management. Traditional institutions particularly the matrilineal system and village Dorbar play a central role in shaping resource access and adaptive capacity. Social cohesion, indigenous knowledge systems and local participation mechanism contribute to resilience, but also reveal inequities in exposure and sensitivity across groups. The present study focuses on the 11 administrative districts, offering a comparative analysis of their susceptibility to climate impacts.

By integrating spatial, socio-economic, and environmental indicators characterised by the diversity of the states, the assessment will generate actionable results to plan targeted adaptation action, raise public awareness on localised climate impacts and resilience pathways, and provide a robust scientific foundation for evidence-informed policy making and institutional response.

Methodology

Framework overview

This study employs a Tier 1, top-down, indicator-based approach to assess district-level climate vulnerability in Meghalaya. Tier 1 refers to rapid appraisal using secondary data and expert judgment, without the integration of participatory methods or climate hazard modelling. The approach is grounded in the manual Climate Vulnerability and Risk Assessment: Framework, Methods and Guidelines for the Himalayan Regions developed under Indian Himalayan Climate Adaptation Programme (IHCAP)13. This choice is a methodological strength in the context of Meghalaya, as it enables systematic district-level assessment under conditions of limited primary data and provides a replicable framework for other Himalayan regions facing similar constraints. This framework is closely aligned with the IPCC’s Fifth Assessment Report (AR5) guidelines, which conceptualize climate risk as the interaction of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability (Fig. 1). Importantly, AR5 treats vulnerability as an intrinsic property of a system reflecting its internal sensitivity and adaptive capacity while “exposure” is considered an external factor and is no longer viewed as a component of vulnerability. In line with this conceptual shift, the current assessment defines vulnerability solely in terms of sensitivity and adaptive capacity, independent of hazard and exposure. The study focusing on the near-term future (2030’s) and operates at the district level, reflecting the administrative structure commonly used in governance and planning across India. Capacity-building workshops with stakeholders and experts from leading Indian technical institutes were conducted to determine indicator selection and weighting, and the finalized approach was applied to district-level secondary data for Meghalaya.

Framework as per AR5, IPCC, 2014 (Risk arises from interaction of hazard, exposure and vulnerability. Vulnerability is endogenous characteristic of a system and is determined by its sensitivity and adaptive capacity).

Stepwise analytical process

Indicator selection

In this study, eight indicators were selected to assess district-level climate vulnerability in Meghalaya based on their data availability, reliability, relevance to sensitivity and adaptive capacity, and contextual significance within the State’s landscape and socio-economic systems. These indicators were validated through stakeholder workshops and an extensive literature review, ensuring their relevance and applicability to local conditions. The selected indicators capture Meghalaya’s unique biophysical terrain and socio-economic dynamics: elevations range from 60 m to 1,950 m, creating steep slopes and intense rainfall patterns that exacerbate landslides, erosion, and agricultural disruptions. Agriculture yield variability is particularly critical, supporting nearly 81% of the population. Forests, covering 76.33% of the state’s area, both mitigate climate impacts and remain vulnerable to changing climatic conditions. Socio-economic indicators further reflect disparities: the female literacy rate of 72.9% varies significantly across districts, with higher literacy enabling better adaptation to climate risks and lower literacy correlating with reduced resilience. Similarly, the infant mortality rate (IMR) of 29 per 1,000 live births highlights healthcare disparities, which are linked to economic constraints indicated by the Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) of 27.79%, reflecting limited access to education, healthcare, and basic amenities. Adaptive capacity is also assessed through average man-days under NREGS; low enrollment indicates limited livelihood stability, increasing economic vulnerability and reducing resilience. Together, these indicators provide a context-specific understanding of how biophysical features and socio-economic disparities interact across districts. Sensitivity and adaptive capacity indices

To enhance conceptual clarity and analytical structure, the selected indicators were first categorized into two major domains, namely, sensitivity and adaptive capacity, following the IPCC framework8; based on their functional relationship to climate vulnerability was determined by assessing whether an indicator increases susceptibility to climate impacts (positive interaction, contributing to Sensitivity) or enhances the system’s ability to respond and adapt (negative interaction, contributing to Adaptive Capacity). This classification is aligned with the IPCC AR5 framework, which defines vulnerability as an intrinsic property of a system shaped by its sensitivity to climatic stresses and its capacity to adapt. Sensitivity indicators (e.g., slope gradient, yield variability, poverty levels, infant mortality rate) are those whose higher values increase vulnerability. Adaptive capacity indicators (e.g., forest cover, female literacy, NREGS man-days) are those whose higher values mitigate vulnerability.

Normalization of indicators

Indicators selected for a vulnerability assessment have different units of measurement. To enable aggregation, indicators with varying units of measurement typically percentages and relative values across districts were normalised. Following the IPCC AR5 assessment two formulas were used based on each indicator’s functional relationship with vulnerability i.e. positive or negative13. Assigning weights to indicators

Climate vulnerability is shaped by multiple indicators, each exerting different levels of impact on sensitivity and adaptive capacity. To reflect their relative importance, weights were assigned to the selected indicators ensuring a balanced representation of their contribution to overall vulnerability within Meghalaya’s socio-ecological context.

Although, several methods exist for assigning unequal weights to the indicators such as statistical normalisation, principal component analysis, however this assessment relies on expert judgment and stakeholder consultation since these are simple practical methods which are widely used.

Following are the weights (WI) assigned to each of the eight indicators. While assigning weight it was ensured that the weight assigned to all the indicators add up to 100.

Yield variability (WI = 30)

Agriculture forms the backbone of Meghalaya’s economy, with nearly 81% of the population dependent on it, either directly or indirectly, for their livelihoods26. Expert consultations identified yield variability of major foodgrains (rice, wheat, and maize) as a key indicator representing the agricultural sector, given its strong influence on economic stability and food security. The sector’s high sensitivity to climatic fluctuations directly affects crop productivity and livelihood resilience, contributing to about 22% of the State’s GDP. Accordingly, yield variability was assigned the highest weightage to reflect both the economic significance and climate sensitivity of the agricultural sector.

Slope gradient (WI = 20)

The second highest weightage was assigned to percentage of area with slope greater than 30 degrees. The topography of the State and the hilly terrain adds to the sensitivity of the area to climate change impacts. The spatial distribution of the steep slopes coincides with areas receiving heavy rainfall, making the region vulnerable to soil erosion, landslides, sedimentation in low lands, causing damage to agricultural lands and infrastructures27.

Forest area (WI = 18)

Indicator with third highest weightage assigned was the percentage of area under forest. Although the State currently has a good percentage of area under forest, the inherent concerns continue to exist in the form of shifting agriculture, logging, mining and other human activities which have been responsible for fragmentation, destruction and degradation of the forests in the State. Further, the future projections based on a scientific study suggests that 70% of the forested grids would become extremely vulnerable in the long term under RCP 8.5 scenario25.

Multidimensional poverty index (WI = 17)

The indicator of households living below poverty line was assigned the fourth highest weightage. Meghalaya has around 12% of its population and over 2 lakh households living under the BPL28. The impacts of climate change are worst felt by the economically marginalized section of the society; thus, it was necessary to give higher weightage to this indicator.

Population density (WI = 1.0)

Meghalaya has a population density of only 132 persons/sq.km29; less than one-third of national average, hence considered less significant in contributing to vulnerability.

Female literacy rate (WI = 0.5)

Female literacy rate was the only indicator which captures the gender perspective and has a negative relationship with adaptive capacity. In Meghalaya, the female literacy rate is at 72.89% which is above the national average (65.46%)30.

Development of vulnerability index (VI)

Aggregation of different indicators with weights is necessary to obtain a composite aggregated vulnerability index. For this, the weights are multiplied with the normalised indicator value and then aggregated to obtain the overall vulnerability index value for each district.

The CVI for each district were computed as follows:

[VI = NV*W], [CVI = Sum (VIn)].

The resulting value VI ranges from 0 (least vulnerable) to 1 (most vulnerable). These dimensionless scores facilitate ranking and comparison.

Based on the VI, the districts are further categorized into 3 classes:

•High (0.48–0.58).

•Medium (0.38–0.47).

•Low (0.27–0.37).

Drivers’ attribution analysis

To identify the key factors contributing to overall vulnerability, a drivers’ attribution analysis was conducted to determine the relative influence of each indicator on the Composite Vulnerability Index (CVI). Indicators showing higher numerical contributions (≥ 0.06) within individual district profiles were identified as primary drivers of vulnerability. The statistical attribution analysis quantified the contribution of each indicator to the aggregate CVI. The mean normalized, weighted score of each indicator across all districts was expressed as a proportion of the mean CVI using the following equation:

Here, the driver contribution represents the proportional share of each indicator in the composite vulnerability. The numerator denotes the mean of normalized, weighted indicator values across all districts, while the denominator corresponds to the average CVI for all 11 districts. Indicators exhibiting higher percentage contributions were considered major drivers of vulnerability.

This decomposition-based approach has been widely applied in climate and livelihood vulnerability assessments to identify key component influences31–33. Similar methods have been used in environmental risk and sustainability indexing to enhance interpretability and guide policy prioritization34,35. Such attribution strengthens the interpretability of the CVI and supports targeted adaptation and policy interventions.

Data analysis

In this assessment, eight indicators were selected to evaluate district-level climate vulnerability in Meghalaya. These indicators were derived from secondary data extracted from official sources, including the NITI Aayog and the Ministry of Rural Development, chosen for their reliability, availability, and relevance to vulnerability assessment. The data were compiled and processed using Microsoft Excel and ArcGIS 10.8 for both statistical and spatial analyses, covering the period 2020–2024. The data cleaning protocol involved addressing anomalies such as zero values, which typically arise from non-detection or below-threshold occurrences, to minimize errors. Sensitivity and contribution analyses, combining statistical methods and expert judgment, were performed to identify the major drivers of vulnerability. Spatial overlays were generated to reveal geographic patterns and inform district-level prioritization. By intgrating statistical aggregation with geospatial visualization, the approach ensured methodological rigor and policy relevance.

Results

District level vulnerability profiles, rankings, and component scores

The Climate Vulnerability index (VI) offers a comparative measure of climate susceptibility across the districts of Meghalaya. The higher the value of VI of the particular area, the higher will be the vulnerability. For the State of Meghalaya, comparative rankings were computed for the eleven districts based on the eight selected indicators. As mentioned earlier the 12th district was carved out in 2021 and the district-specific data was not available for the assessment. As presented in Table 4 & Fig. 2, the analysis reveals that West Khasi Hills is the most vulnerable district with the highest VI score of 0.58 and ranks 1 among the 10 districts of Meghalaya followed by East Garo Hills (0.49) at rank 2, district of West Garo Hills (0.46) at rank 3 and East Khasi Hills (0.45) at rank 4. Whereas the district of East Jaintia Hills with a VI value of 0.27 was found to be relatively the least vulnerable district.

Discussion

The district-level climate vulnerability assessment conducted in this study reveals that West Khasi Hills, East Garo Hills and West Garo Hills are the most vulnerable districts in Meghalaya, primarily driven by reduced forest cover, steep slope gradients and high multidimensional poverty index. These findings align with broader regional assessments but also offer distinct insights due to the study’s focused Tier 1 methodology and indicator selection. A comparable study by Lynrah et al. (2025) conducted a block-level vulnerability assessment36 across 39 Community & Rural Development (C&RD) blocks in Meghalaya using the NMSHE common framework13. Their results similarly identified steep terrain, low forest resource availability and limited access to institutional credit as dominant drivers of vulnerability. However, the finer spatial resolution at the block level revealed additional micro-level disparities, such as inadequate Anganwadi center coverage and irrigation deficits, which were not captured at the district level.

The spatial variation in vulnerability across districts reflects the combined influence of steep topography, declining forest cover, agricultural instability, and socio-economic disparities. Steep slopes exacerbate soil erosion and landslides, as observed in other Himalayan states such as Uttarakhand and Himachal Pradesh, where terrain fragility amplifies livelihood insecurity18,37. Forest loss further heightens vulnerability by diminishing ecological stability and livelihood resources38. Likewise, fluctuations in agricultural yield mirror climate sensitivity patterns seen across mountain agroecosystems, where erratic rainfall and limited irrigation contribute to food insecurity39. High poverty levels and limited health access constrain adaptive capacity, while greater female literacy and employment under NREGS enhance resilience by improving institutional engagement and livelihood diversification40,41.

In contrast, the Multidimensional Livelihood Vulnerability Index (MLVI) developed by Gerlitz et al. (2017) for the Hindu Kush Himalayas employed primarily household survey data and the Alkire-Foster method to assess vulnerability across exposure, sensitivity and adaptive capacity dimensions15. While the MLVI offers granular insights into livelihood fragility, its inclusion of exposure as a vulnerability component diverges from the IPCC AR5-aligned framework adopted in the current study, which treats exposure as external to vulnerability. The methodology outlined by Sharma et al. (2018) under the Climate Vulnerability and Risk Assessment Manual for the Indian Himalayan Region provided the conceptual backbone for this study13. The methodology emphasized a tiered, indicator-based approach, with vulnerability defined solely by sensitivity and adaptive capacity. The current study’s use of expert-weighted indicators and normalization techniques reflects this guidance, ensuring methodological consistency with other Indian Himalayan Region assessments. The normalized and weighted indicators were aggregated using a linear additive method to derive the Composite Vulnerability Index (CVI), which facilitated systematic comparison of sensitivity and adaptive capacity across districts. Furthermore, the three-tiered analysis by Singh et al. (2025), which mapped vulnerability at district, block, and village levels, reinforces the importance of multi-scale assessments42. Their findings also placed West Khasi Hills and East Garo Hills in the high vulnerability category, corroborating the present study’s district-level rankings.

The interlinkages among these indicators demonstrate feedback mechanisms that reinforce vulnerability. For example, steep terrain and deforestation together accelerate erosion and agricultural decline, while reduced productivity perpetuates poverty and forest dependence, creating a socio-ecological feedback loop. Similarly, poverty and low literacy limit access to adaptation resources, weakening institutional capacity to address climate risks. These cross-sectoral interactions emphasize that vulnerability in Meghalaya is systemic rather than additive, resulting from the dynamic interplay of environmental and socio-economic processes.

Collectively, these comparative insights underscore the robustness of the current assessment while highlighting the value of integrating multiple spatial scales and methodological approaches. The convergence of findings across studies strengthens the case for targeted adaptation strategies in high-vulnerable districts and supports the development of replicable frameworks for other mountainous regions.

While global literature especially within the Himalayan region offers extensive analysis on climate vulnerability1,15,43, this study introduces a novel, context-specific framework tailored for Meghalaya’s unique socio-ecological terrain. By adopting a Tier 1, IPCC AR5-aligned approach that excludes exposure and emphasizes sensitivity and adaptive capacity13,44, the assessment brings conceptual clarity rarely observed in comparable studies. It further innovates by integrating regionally weighted indicators such as yield variability, slope gradient, and NREGS enrolment to reflect local drivers of vulnerability contrasting with frameworks like MLVI or CEEW that apply uniform weights or broader scales14,20 The inclusion of institutional indicators and GIS-based visualizations enables spatial targeting and district-wise planning that bridge scientific diagnostics with governance action21. Altogether, this study generates new knowledge by combining methodological rigor with policy-oriented insights, offering a replicable model for data-constrained mountainous regions beyond South Asia45. It not only explores vulnerability but reshapes how it can be understood and acted upon in localized adaptation strategies.

This study offers a replicable, regional calibrated framework that speaks to global researches and policy makers especially those working in fragile mountainous ecosystems. The IPCC-aligned framework used can be adapted to other contexts such as Eastern Africa or Southeast Asia where similar terrain socio-economic interlinkages exist. For regions with limited primary data availability, the Tier 1, secondary data driven approach provides a pathway to rapid vulnerability diagnostics. By highlighting socio-economic drivers like poverty and health care access, the study aligns with global calls for climate justice vulnerability-led adaptation, resonating with SDG frameworks. Given Meghalaya’s inclusion in the Indian Himalayan Region (IHR), the study strengthens opportunities for cross-border learning among Bhutan, Nepal facing shared vulnerabilities.

It is important to note that the Composite Vulnerability Index (CVI) derived in this study represents relative vulnerability, enabling comparison among districts rather than providing absolute measures of risk. The estimated index thus highlights spatial disparities in sensitivity and adaptive capacity across Meghalaya. Translating these insights into adaptation pathways, the findings suggest that districts such as West Khasi Hills and East Garo Hills require integrated strategies focusing on forest restoration, slope stabilization, and livelihood diversification. Moderately vulnerable districts like Ri-Bhoi and East Khasi Hills can enhance resilience through education, institutional strengthening, and improved social safety nets, while less vulnerable districts such as East Jaintia Hills can serve as models for scaling up best practices in community-based adaptation.

Limitations and future research

The study relies entirely on secondary data sources at the district level, while sufficient for Tier 1 methodology, this limits the granularity needed to capture intra-district disparities. The assessment is calibrated for the current climate conditions only (short term 2030’s). It doesn’t integrate future climate scenarios. For future research, this study suggests a need for localized vulnerability assessments with granular data to identify localized risks and resource gaps. Exploring long-term climatic impacts and integration of high resolutions climate projections and hazard modelling to stimulate dynamic risk under RCP’s and SSP’s.

While this study offers a data-driven district-level climate vulnerability assessment for Meghalaya, it could be strengthened by broadening the scope of contributing factors beyond biophysical and socio-economic indicators. Existing literature from the Himalayan region emphasizes that vulnerability is shaped not only by terrain and livelihood fragility but also by institutional support, access to climate information, and the role of indigenous knowledge systems45,46. In Meghalaya, where traditional governance mechanisms like the Dorbar Shnong and matrilineal systems shape resource access and resilience, these dimensions are highly relevant. For instance, household-level studies across the Himalaya reveal adaptation barriers such as limited extension services, poor institutional networking, and underutilized local coping strategies46. While the current analysis draws succinct conclusions through a Tier 1 vulnerability index, a deeper exploration of contextual drivers and enablers such as information facilities, government schemes, and community cohesion is needed to fill the knowledge gap. Positioning this study within broader Himalayan adaptation discourse would enhance its relevance, especially by aligning with equity-driven and community-based resilience frameworks21. Future iterations could consider hybrid approaches combining secondary data with targeted participatory insights to uncover micro-scale variations and refine policy linkages.

Conclusion

This study provides valuable insights into the climate vulnerability of Meghalaya by employing a comprehensive district-level assessment, integrating biophysical and socio-economic indicators to highlight the multifaceted challenges faced by the state. Indicators such as reduced forest cover, food grain yield variability, high rates of poverty (MPI), and healthcare disparities exemplified by infant mortality rates (IMR) underscore the interconnected nature of vulnerabilities across districts. The study’s findings reveal a strong need for targeted interventions to enhance resilience in high-vulnerable districts such as West Khasi Hills and East Garo Hills, which are particularly impacted by factors like steep slopes and deforestation.

The broader implications of this study emphasize the importance of integrating vulnerability assessments into climate adaptation planning and policy-making. Policymakers should prioritize district-specific strategies to strengthen adaptive capacities, such as improving healthcare and education infrastructure, promoting climate-resilient agricultural practices, and enhancing community participation in programs like NREGS. Investing in forest conservation and sustainable land-use practices is also critical to mitigate environmental risks. In practice, developing disaster-resilient infrastructure, ensuring equitable access to social safety nets, and conducting capacity-building programs for women and marginalized communities can significantly improve adaptive responses to climate impacts. Overall, the framework not only identifies vulnerability but enables a proactive roadmap for climate adaptation, one that is scientifically robust, socially inclusive and policy ready.
