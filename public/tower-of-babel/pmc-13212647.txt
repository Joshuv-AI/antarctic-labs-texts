Bayesian joint and individual component regression for multigroup physiological data
Authors: Muhammed Kara, Mehmet Ali Cengiz, Emre Dünder, Talat Şenel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeterogeneous multigroup data often contain both globally shared and group-specific components, posing challenges for conventional regression models. Such data structures are increasingly common in medical research, particularly in radiology and biomedical imaging, where patient populations are naturally heterogeneous across disease subtypes, demographic groups, or imaging modalities. While methods such as Joint and Individual Component Regression (JICO) address this separation, they lack mechanisms to quantify uncertainty and incorporate prior knowledge. In this study, we propose a Bayesian Joint and Individual Component Regression (Bayesian-JICO) framework that extends JICO with a probabilistic formulation. The Bayesian approach enables uncertainty quantification through posterior distributions and credible intervals, offering more reliable inference, especially with limited sample sizes. Posterior estimation was performed via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), and the model was evaluated using both simulated scenarios and the publicly available Australian Institute of Sport (AIS) dataset, which contains physiological and hematological measurements of elite athletes. Results demonstrate that Bayesian-JICO outperforms traditional methods in predictive accuracy, interpretability, and robustness, while providing credible intervals for parameter estimates. This framework offers a comprehensive and uncertainty-aware solution for multigroup regression, with broad applicability across biomedical, radiological, environmental, and social sciences.Keywords: Multigroup physiological data, Bayesian regression, Joint and individual components, MCMC, Uncertainty quantificationSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Heterogeneous data are increasingly common in scientific and medical research, frequently arising as multiple matrices or datasets that differ either in variables, samples, or both. A prominent example is multi-view or multi-source data, where different sets of variables are used to measure the same patients. Such data may originate from diverse sources, platforms, or modalities, for instance, radiological studies that integrate magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), and positron emission tomography (PET) scans for the same individuals. Another important case involves multigroup data, where the same clinical or imaging variables are collected from distinct patient subgroups representing heterogeneous subpopulations. For example, in oncology or neurology research, patient data may be stratified by disease subtype, stage, or demographic characteristics, each reflecting varied biological and clinical contexts.

Traditional regression approaches often struggle with such heterogeneity. Methods like ridge regression1,2 are well-suited for homogeneous datasets but cannot adequately capture both shared and subgroup-specific structures. One option is to ignore heterogeneity and fit a single global model, but this oversimplifies the structure by masking subgroup variability. Alternatively, fitting separate models per subgroup preserves flexibility but fails to exploit commonalities across groups. To overcome these drawbacks, several new frameworks have been proposed to capture both global and group-specific signals. Recent contributions include partially linear regression for large-scale heterogeneous data3, fused penalties to estimate subgroup structures within regression4–6, and locally weighted penalized models for subject-level variable selection7. High-dimensional factor regression models8 have also been introduced for heterogeneous subpopulations. While these methods address aspects of heterogeneity, they often rely on restrictive assumptions or emphasize either global or group-specific components, limiting their applicability in broader contexts.

Parallel developments in multi-source data analysis have yielded useful insights. The Joint and Individual Variation Explained (JIVE) model9 and its extensions10; Gaynana & Li, 2019) decompose multiple matrices into joint and individual components through low-rank approximations. However, such approaches are unsupervised and do not directly incorporate response variables. Supervised extensions, such as integrative factor regression11 and the Supervised JIVE (sJIVE) model12, introduce response-based supervision, but they remain primarily tailored to multi-source rather than multigroup regression.

To specifically address multigroup regression, Joint and Individual Component Regression (JICO) was introduced by13. JICO is a latent component regression model that decomposes responses into shared and group-specific structures, leveraging low-rank approximations of predictors. Through simulation studies and applications such as ADNI data, JICO demonstrated robust and versatile performance for multigroup regression problems.

Nonetheless, JICO and related methods rely on point estimates and fixed model complexity, which limit their ability to characterize uncertainty and incorporate prior knowledge. This gap motivates the development of our proposed Bayesian Joint and Individual Component Regression (Bayesian-JICO).

Bayesian methods provide a principled framework for quantifying uncertainty and embedding prior knowledge in parameter estimation14–16. Unlike classical methods, which yield only point estimates, Bayesian approaches generate posterior distributions that reveal both parameter values and their associated variability. Applied to JICO, a Bayesian formulation enables the joint estimation of shared and group-specific components while explicitly accounting for uncertainty in their separation. Furthermore, Bayesian shrinkage priors introduce regularization to mitigate overfitting, particularly beneficial in high-dimensional or small-sample settings.

In this study, we propose a Bayesian JICO framework that models both joint and group-specific components probabilistically. We apply the method to extensive simulation scenarios as well as to the Australian Institute of Sport (AIS) dataset, which contains rich physiological and hematological measurements of elite athletes. The AIS application highlights the model’s ability to disentangle shared and sex-specific physiological associations, thereby demonstrating its relevance to real-world biomedical data.

Across all stages from theoretical formulation to simulation experiments and real-data application we employed consistent prior distributions and MCMC strategies. Regression coefficients were assigned Gaussian priors with variance hyperparameters governed by inverse-gamma distributions, while variance components were modelled with inverse-gamma priors with fixed shape and scale. Shrinkage priors were incorporated to encourage parsimony in component estimation. Posterior inference was conducted using the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS), implemented in both R and Python, with convergence validated by Gelman–Rubin diagnostics and effective sample sizes.

In summary, the proposed Bayesian JICO framework advances beyond classical JICO by offering uncertainty-aware inference, improved predictive accuracy, and interpretability in multigroup data contexts. By validating the approach through both controlled simulations and physiological data analysis, we illustrate its flexibility and broad potential in addressing complex heterogeneous regression problems.

Motivation and model framework

Multi-group data often exhibit heterogeneity across groups, posing challenges for conventional regression models. The JICO framework addresses this by decomposing the response into shared (joint) and group-specific components. However, JICO relies on point estimates and fixed model complexity, which limits its capacity to express uncertainty and adapt to varying levels of heterogeneity. Selecting the ranks of joint and individual components typically requires extensive cross-validation, which is computationally intensive and does not account for parameter uncertainty.

In contrast, Bayesian modelling offers a principled approach to overcome these limitations. It enables the incorporation of prior knowledge and provides full posterior distributions, allowing uncertainty quantification through credible intervals17. Bayesian regularization via priors also guards against overfitting and enhances generalization (Cawley et al., 2007), while hyperpriors introduce adaptive flexibility to accommodate group-wise variance structures. Furthermore, the separation between joint and individual components becomes clearer, improving model interpretability18.

In this work, we extend the JICO framework within a Bayesian paradigm. The proposed Bayesian JICO model decomposes multigroup data using probabilistic principles, enhancing interpretability, robustness, and predictive performance while fully accounting for uncertainty.

Because each variance term is modelled independently, the framework maintains flexibility in balancing bias and variance across different components. The Inverse-Gamma prior also ensures non-negativity and facilitates conjugate updating within the MCMC framework, improving computational efficiency. Even so, applying MCMC methods, Gibbs Sampling in particular, is a method to perform a posterior inference of the model. Posterior inference refers to estimating the model parameters using the posterior distribution combining the information from the observed data about the parameters and the prior beliefs associated with the parameters. Posterior distribution, in detail, is proportional to the product of the likelihood of the data observed given the parameters of the model and priors for these parameters. Since analytical solutions cannot be obtained readily for a complicated posterior distribution, MCMC approaches, especially Gibbs sampling, are mainly used to approximate a posterior distribution. Many literature sources19,20 jointly working with these techniques describe how the techniques repeatedly sample from the parameter’s conditional distribution, making it possible to investigate the parameter space with much more efficiency for sound model parameter estimates.

Simulation studies

Simulation study1 (structured scenario-based evaluation)

Building on the findings of Simulation Study 1, which focused on the model’s behaviour across varying global signal strengths through a controlled grid of γ values, we further designed a set of structured scenarios to stress-test the Bayesian JICO model under a diverse range of data-generating mechanisms. While the first simulation provided general insights into model performance under controlled global variance structures, it did not fully explore the types of latent heterogeneity, sparsity, or rank misspecification commonly encountered in real-world multi-group datasets. To address this, we developed eight targeted simulation scenarios, each crafted to isolate a specific modelling challenge.

Each scenario manipulates a particular property of the latent data structure — such as the dominance of joint versus individual components, the presence of weak signals, high-dimensionality (p > n), low signal-to-noise ratios, misspecified component ranks, or violations of orthogonality between joint and group-specific spaces. These scenarios reflect a spectrum of realistic modelling conditions and allow us to assess the robustness, flexibility, and interpretability of Bayesian JICO beyond the conditions considered in Study 2.

To assess uncertainty quantification and calibration of the Bayesian JICO model, we evaluated the empirical coverage and average length of 95% posterior credible intervals across all eight scenarios.

The results demonstrated that the Bayesian JICO achieved well-calibrated posterior uncertainty, with empirical coverage rates consistently close to the nominal 0.95 level and credible intervals of reasonable width across replications.

In high-dimensional (Scenario 5) and high-noise (Scenario 6) settings, the intervals became wider as expected, reflecting increased uncertainty rather than instability, and still maintained satisfactory coverage.

Under rank misspecification (Scenario 7), the Bayesian JICO preserved stable coverage and predictive accuracy, outperforming the classical JICO which tended to underestimate uncertainty.

These diagnostics collectively indicate that the proposed Bayesian framework not only enhances predictive performance but also provides reliable and interpretable uncertainty estimates, confirming the robustness of posterior inference across diverse data-generating mechanisms.

The Australian institute of sport (AIS) dataset

Ethics statement

This study utilized the publicly available Australian Institute of Sport (AIS) dataset from the R DAAG package, containing de-identified physiological measurements of elite athletes. As a secondary analysis of completely anonymized public data, institutional ethical approval was not required per guidelines for retrospective analysis of de-identified datasets. The original data collection by the Australian Institute of Sport followed appropriate ethical protocols including informed consent from all participating athletes. All analytical methods were conducted in accordance with established statistical guidelines.

Dataset description

The Australian Institute of Sport (AIS) dataset was used in this study, containing physiological and hematological measurements of elite athletes from different sports disciplines. This data has been used extensively in sports science and medical research to investigate the physical and biochemical determinants of athletes’ performance. This dataset possesses 202 observations with several body composition, blood parameters policies, and athletic background variables.

The AIS dataset provides a wide scope into the general topic of physiological characteristics to include BMI, lean body mass, height, weight, and skinfold thickness, which together represent aspects of physical fitness and body composition for athletes. Hematological variables include red blood cell count, white blood cell count, hematocrit levels, hemoglobin, and ferritin levels, providing information on the main aspects of oxygen transport and iron storage; both are important in the context of endurance and general athletic performance. Additional contribution to metabolic efficiency may be available through the assessment of plasma cholinesterase activity. The Australian Institute of Sport (AIS) dataset used in this study is publicly available through the R DAAG package21 and can be accessed at https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=DAAG. The dataset contains de-identified physiological and hematological measurements of elite athletes and does not require ethical approval as it consists of previously published, anonymized data collected for sports science research purposes.

The AIS dataset is particularly well-suited for the application of a Bayesian Joint and Individual Component (Bayes JICO) regression framework, as it contains detailed anthropometric and hematological measurements for both male and female athletes, enabling the explicit modelling of shared and group-specific associations. In this study, serum ferritin concentration is selected as the response variable, given its well-established physiological differences between sexes—males typically exhibit substantially higher ferritin levels than females, owing to factors such as menstrual blood loss, hormonal differences, and iron metabolism. This intrinsic heterogeneity underscores the necessity of a modelling approach capable of decomposing predictor effects into joint components, representing associations common to both sexes, and individual components, capturing sex-specific relationships. The BayesJICO model is ideally suited for this task, as it leverages the multi-group structure to estimate shared latent factors while simultaneously identifying distinct predictors relevant to each group. Consequently, this approach not only accommodates the inherent biological variability between sexes but also enhances interpretability by revealing which anthropometric and hematological covariates influence ferritin levels universally and which act in a sex-dependent manner.

In Bayesian inference, variable-specific priors were assigned to reflect the characteristics of the AIS dataset. Regression coefficients for anthropometric and hematological predictors (e.g., BMI, LBM, RBC, HCT, HGB, Ht, Wt, SSF, WBC, PBF) were given zero-mean Gaussian priors with variance parameters estimated via weakly informative inverse-gamma hyperpriors, allowing the model to adaptively regularize both joint and group-specific effects. Noise terms for each group were modelled with independent Gaussian priors, enabling group-specific variance estimation.

The full dataset was randomly partitioned into a 70% training set and a 30% held-out test set to ensure that the evaluation of predictive accuracy was based on previously unseen observations. The Bayesian JICO model was fitted using only the training data, while predictive summaries (including posterior predictive means, credible intervals, and test-set MSE values) were computed exclusively on the held-out test set. This design directly addresses the reviewer’s concern regarding the lack of a clearly stated data-splitting protocol and ensures that all reported MSE values reflect genuine out-of-sample predictive performance rather than in-sample fit.

Posterior inference was performed via a blocked Gibbs sampling algorithm implemented in R. Four parallel chains with 10,000 iterations each were run, with the first 2,000 discarded as burn-in. Convergence was evaluated using the Gelman–Rubin statistic () and effective sample size (ESS). Both diagnostics indicated stable and efficient posterior sampling across all parameters (, ESS > 1,300), confirming that the Markov chains mixed well and providing reliable posterior summaries for both training and test-set predictions.

These findings demonstrate that the Bayesian JICO framework provides high sensitivity for identifying meaningful parameter effects while delivering robust and interpretable inferences for complex multivariate datasets.

To sum it all up, these results provide evidence for the advantage of the Bayesian JICO in representing both common and individual-specific variations in the AIS dataset and, more importantly, its ability to model complex dependencies in structured multi-group data. To evaluate the performance of the Bayesian JICO model, several statistical metrics were employed to assess both predictive accuracy and model complexity. The Mean Squared Error (MSE) was used as a primary measure of model accuracy, capturing the average squared difference between observed and predicted values, where lower values indicate better predictive performance (Robert &Casella,2004). Additionally, the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) were applied to compare model fit while penalizing excessive complexity, with BIC imposing a stricter penalty to discourage overfitting (Hastie et al.,2009; Akaike,1974). Another important criterion, the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC), was utilized within the Bayesian framework to balance model goodness-of-fit and complexity, offering a more flexible approach compared to AIC and BIC (Schwarz,1978).

To further refine model evaluation, the Widely Applicable Information Criterion (WAIC) and Leave-One-Out Information Criterion (LOOIC) were included as advanced Bayesian model selection methods. Unlike traditional information criteria, WAIC and LOOIC incorporate posterior distributions to provide more robust estimates of out-of-sample predictive performance (Spiegelhalter et al.,2002). Similarly, the Expected Log Predictive Density (ELPD) was considered, where higher values suggest improved model generalization and predictive accuracy1,2,4,5,22–29.

By employing these diverse evaluation criteria, the Bayesian JICO model was comprehensively assessed across different regularization parameter values (γ). The combination of accuracy-based and complexity-aware metrics ensures that the model not only achieves optimal predictive performance but also maintains a balance between flexibility and interpretability. These results highlight the effectiveness of Bayesian JICO in analysing structured data, making it a valuable tool in the exploration of joint and individual components in complex datasets. Generally, results indicate that γ = 0.50 is the best option since it produces minimal prediction error with outstanding model selection statistics. Although γ = ∞ produces competitive predictions, the slightly larger AIC and BIC values can be interpreted as its inclusion of additional complexity without actual benefit. These results attest to the capability of Bayesian JICO to address individual and collective components in organized data, since it is demonstrated to be effective as a constant and reliable model approach in handling complex multi-group regression problems.

To accompany the interpretation of regression effects, we evaluated the predictive calibration of the Bayesian JICO model for ferritin. Posterior predictive distributions were compared with the observed ferritin values, and the simulated datasets showed close agreement with the empirical distribution, indicating that the model captures both the central tendency and the spread of the response variable. Probability Integral Transform (PIT) values were approximately uniform, and empirical coverage rates for 50%, 80%, and 95% posterior predictive intervals closely matched their corresponding nominal levels for both male and female athletes. These results demonstrate that posterior uncertainty is well calibrated.

We also conducted prior sensitivity analyses by replacing the Inverse-Gamma variance priors with Half-Cauchy and Half-t alternatives for shrinkage and variance components. Posterior means, credible intervals, and predictive accuracy remained stable across prior choices, indicating that the model’s conclusions are not driven by specific hyperparameter settings. Prior predictive simulations further confirmed that the chosen priors generate realistic ferritin values before observing the data.

Results and discussion

In both the simulation studies and the AIS dataset application, we employed the same prior structures and Bayesian computational approach to ensure methodological consistency. Posterior inference was conducted using the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS), implemented in both R and Python, leveraging complementary computational and diagnostic capabilities.

To assess model complexity and the effective ranks of the joint and individual subspaces, we evaluated Bayesian JICO models under different prior shrinkage levels and compared their fit using WAIC and LOOIC. The optimal configuration was selected as the one minimizing WAIC, corresponding to the model with the fewest active factors while preserving predictive accuracy. This provides a principled Bayesian alternative to cross-validation used in the classical JICO framework.

In Simulation Study 1, the mean squared error (MSE) values (with standard deviations in parentheses) for the Bayesian JICO, Ridge, and PLS methods across eight simulation scenarios were evaluated. Overall, the Bayesian JICO consistently achieved the lowest or near-lowest MSE in many scenarios, demonstrating strong robustness across diverse structural settings, including balanced joint–individual structures, dominant joint or individual components, low signal-to-noise ratios, and cases with rank misspecification or non-orthogonal joint–individual relationships.

The superior performance of Bayesian JICO across most scenarios can be attributed to its ability to explicitly model both joint and individual variation while incorporating prior information, enabling more accurate signal recovery in complex multivariate structures. The Bayesian shrinkage framework not only mitigates overfitting in low signal-to-noise and high-dimensional settings but also provides inherent uncertainty quantification, which contributes to its stability as reflected in consistently lower standard deviations. In contrast, Ridge regression, while effective in reducing variance through regularization, lacks the structural decomposition capability of Bayesian JICO, leading to suboptimal performance when joint and individual components interact in non-trivial ways. PLS, which projects predictors onto latent components, can be advantageous in highly collinear and high-dimensional contexts—as evidenced by its competitive results in Scenario 5—but its reliance on variance maximization rather than structural alignment limits its adaptability in scenarios with heterogeneous or weak shared signals. Overall, Bayesian JICO emerges as the most robust choice across a wide range of structural complexities, with Ridge and PLS serving as viable alternatives primarily when computational simplicity or specific high-dimensional covariance structures are prioritized.

Simulation Study 2 further evaluated the model under a partially shared latent structure, mimicking realistic conditions in biomedical applications where covariates may exert both common and subgroup-specific effects. Predictive accuracy, assessed via MSE, indicated that the optimal balance between shared and specific structure was achieved at moderate values of the regularization parameter. Specifically, γ = 0.50 yielded the lowest overall MSE, reflecting the model’s ability to avoid both overfittings purely individual patterns and oversmoothing by forcing excessive joint structure. Model comparison metrics such as WAIC, LOOIC, and ELPD corroborated these findings, confirming that intermediate γ values best captured partially shared associations. Conversely, γ = 0.00 tended to inflate MSE, suggesting that completely unregularized estimation introduces noise into the separation of shared and individual components. These results confirm that the Bayes JICO framework is capable of accurately distinguishing between joint and group-specific associations in scenarios where both structures are present, underscoring its robustness and suitability for biomedical datasets characterized by heterogeneity.

The real-world implementation with the Australian Institute of Sport (AIS) dataset further validated the superiority of Bayesian JICO. The smallest overall MSE (1669.51) was obtained at γ = 0.50, confirming its capability in handling heterogeneous data structures. Interestingly, female athletes demonstrated lower MSE values than male athletes, suggesting that the model more accurately accounted for physiological and hematological variability in female subgroups. Model comparison metrics (AIC, BIC, WAIC, and ELPD) all identified γ = 0.50 as the most appropriate choice, further emphasizing its balance between accuracy and model complexity. While γ = ∞ also produced competitive results, it incurred higher model complexity with less generalization benefit.

From a biomedical perspective, the ability of Bayesian JICO to separate joint and group-specific components has clinical relevance. In multi-group clinical datasets—such as male vs. female, age groups, or disease subtypes—the model’s decomposition provides insights into both shared risk factors and subgroup-specific variations. This has potential applications in personalized medicine, early diagnosis, and subgroup-targeted interventions, as illustrated by the subgroup variability observed in hematological parameters (e.g., RBC, HCT, and WBC) in the AIS dataset.

A major advantage of Bayesian JICO is that it estimates uncertainty through posterior distributions and credible intervals, unlike classical JICO which relies on point estimates. This feature enhances interpretability and provides clinicians and researchers with more reliable decision-making tools. Furthermore, the incorporation of informative priors introduces regularization, preventing overfitting and increasing robustness, particularly in small-sample and high-noise settings—scenarios often encountered in medical studies. The integration of Bayesian model selection criteria (WAIC, LOOIC, and ELPD) also enables a more comprehensive assessment of predictive performance and generalizability compared to traditional information criteria alone.

The findings of both simulation experiments and real-world AIS data analysis collectively demonstrate that Bayesian JICO is a stable, efficient, and interpretable framework for multi-group regression. By explicitly modelling shared and subgroup-specific components while quantifying uncertainty, it outperforms classical JICO and alternative regression methods in terms of predictive accuracy and robustness. Its ability to disentangle heterogeneous effects while providing clinically meaningful insights positions Bayesian JICO as a promising tool for biomedical research and clinical data analysis.
