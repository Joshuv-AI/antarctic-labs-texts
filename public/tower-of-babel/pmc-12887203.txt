Metabolic Vulnerability Index and Atherosclerosis Incident: A Prospective Cohort Study From the UK Biobank
Authors: Xiangliang Liu, Xinqiao Chen, Wang Yang, Yuwei He, Yuting Liu, Yuguang Li, Naifei Chen, Jiuwei Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtherosclerosis underlies most cardiovascular disease, but traditional factors incompletely explain risk. This study examined whether a novel Metabolic Vulnerability Index (MVX), integrating inflammatory and metabolic biomarkers, predicts incident atherosclerosis independently of traditional and genetic risks.MethodsWe analyzed data from 171 600 participants in the UK Biobank without baseline atherosclerotic disease (median follow‐up, 13.6 years). MVX was derived from nuclear magnetic resonance metabolomics and comprised 2 components: the Inflammatory Vulnerability Index (including glycoprotein acetyls and small high‐density lipoprotein particle concentration) and the Metabolic Malnutrition Index (including branched‐chain amino acids and citrate). Associations with incident atherosclerosis (ascertained via hospitalizations and death) were analyzed using Cox models adjusted for demographics and clinical and lifestyle factors. Secondary analyses evaluated MVX components independently and examined potential effect modification by genetic risk using low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol polygenic risk scores.ResultsHigher MVX predicted significantly increased atherosclerosis risk (hazard ratio [HR] per SD: 1.22 [95% CI, 1.19–1.25]). The highest MVX quartile had 59% greater risk compared with the lowest (HR, 1.59 [95% CI, 1.49–1.69]). This association persisted after adjustment for traditional cardiovascular risk factors. The Inflammatory Vulnerability Index demonstrated robust positive associations with atherosclerosis (adjusted HR per SD, 1.18 [95% CI, 1.15–1.21]). MVX–atherosclerosis associations remained consistent across different genetic risk strata and demographic subgroups.ConclusionsThe MVX, particularly its inflammatory component, independently predicts atherosclerosis risk beyond traditional risk factors. This supports comprehensive metabolic profiling for refined risk assessment and underscores inflammation’s central role in atherosclerosis progression, highlighting potential novel intervention targets.Keywords: atherosclerosis, cardiovascular, glycoprotein acetyls, inflammation, metabolic vulnerability, metabolomicsSubject Categories: Atherosclerosis

Atherosclerosis, a progressive inflammatory arterial disease characterized by lipid‐rich plaque formation, remains the pathological foundation for most cardiovascular diseases and accounts for ≈17.9 million deaths annually worldwide. 1 , 2 Despite significant advances in prevention and treatment strategies, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease continues to be the leading cause of death globally, highlighting the critical need for improved risk prediction and early intervention approaches. 3

The pathophysiology of atherosclerosis involves complex interactions between lipid metabolism dysregulation, endothelial dysfunction, inflammatory processes, and immune system activation. 4 , 5 While traditional risk factors, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, and smoking, have been well established, they explain only ≈50% to 60% of atherosclerotic disease burden. 6 This substantial “risk gap” underscores the importance of identifying novel biomarkers and integrated assessment tools that may enhance risk stratification beyond conventional predictors.

Recent advances in high‐throughput metabolomics have enabled comprehensive profiling of small‐molecule metabolites, providing unprecedented insights into the metabolic underpinnings of cardiovascular disease. 7 , 8 Specific metabolomic signatures have been associated with atherosclerosis progression, suggesting their potential utility as biomarkers for risk assessment and therapeutic targets. 9 Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy offers a particularly robust platform for quantifying multiple metabolites simultaneously, allowing for detailed characterization of lipoprotein subclasses, amino acids, and other metabolic intermediates. 10

Emerging evidence suggests that inflammatory biomarkers, particularly glycoprotein acetyls (GlycAs), represent stable indicators of chronic low‐grade inflammation and may predict cardiovascular events with greater consistency than traditional markers such as CRP (C‐reactive protein). 11 , 12 Similarly, small high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) particle concentration has been linked to cardiovascular risk, with lower levels associated with increased atherosclerotic burden, emphasizing the importance of lipoprotein composition and functionality beyond simple lipid measurements. 13

In the metabolic domain, branched‐chain amino acids (BCAAs; leucine, isoleucine, and valine) have demonstrated associations with insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease. 14 , 15 Elevated circulating BCAAs may reflect impaired mitochondrial function and altered energy metabolism. Similarly, citrate, a key tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediate, serves as an indicator of energy metabolism status and has been implicated in atherosclerotic processes. 16

While individual metabolic and inflammatory biomarkers have shown promise, attempts to integrate multiple markers into comprehensive indices remain limited. Recently, the concept of a Metabolic Vulnerability Index (MVX) has been proposed, combining an Inflammatory Vulnerability Index (IVX) and a Metabolic Malnutrition Index (MMX) to provide a more holistic assessment of metabolic health. 17 This integrated approach may offer superior risk prediction compared with single biomarkers, but its effectiveness in predicting cardiovascular outcomes remains inadequately evaluated in large‐scale prospective studies.

Furthermore, the interplay between genetic predisposition and metabolic vulnerability in determining atherosclerosis susceptibility warrants investigation. Polygenic risk scores (PRSs) for low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) have emerged as powerful indicators of genetic susceptibility, 18 yet potential interactions between genetic risk and metabolic vulnerability metrics have not been systematically explored.

In this context, we aimed to investigate the association between the MVX and incident atherosclerosis in a large prospective cohort from the UK Biobank. By leveraging NMR metabolomics and longitudinal follow‐up data, we hypothesized that the MVX would associate with atherosclerosis risk and potentially complement traditional risk assessment approaches. Additionally, we explored potential interactions between the MVX and LDL‐C PRSs to determine the influence of metabolic vulnerability across different genetic backgrounds. These findings may enhance our understanding of atherosclerosis pathophysiology and contribute to the development of more precise risk prediction models.

Methods

Study Design and Population

The data sets generated and analyzed during the current study are available in the UK Biobank at https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/index.cgi. The UK Biobank recruited 502 186 adults from 2006 to 2010, using a multimodal protocol to systematically collect multidimensional health data combining standardized questionnaires, physical measurements, biochemical analyses, and integration with national electronic health records. 19 Following exclusions for inadequate MVX baseline parameters (n=228 185), missing covariate data (n=80 671), participants with baseline atherosclerosis or incomplete atherosclerosis follow‐up records (n=22 088), and the analytical population included 171 242 participants (Figure S1). This study operated under ethical oversight from the North West Multi‐Centre Research Ethics Committee, with documented consent procured from all enrolled individuals.

Calculation of the MVX

The MVX, IVX, and MMX were defined using validated sex‐specific equations derived from a previous prospective cohort study. 20 The IVX incorporated GlycA and small HDL particle number, whereas the MMX involved quantification of BCAAs (leucine, valine, and isoleucine) and citrate. To ensure robust measurement, biomarker concentrations underwent outlier correction via 1st to 99th percentile Winsorization before normalization to a 1 to 100 composite scale (Supplemental Method S1).

Biological specimen handling followed standardized UK Biobank protocols, with metabolomic characterization performed through high‐throughput NMR spectroscopy. 21 Fasting blood specimens were collected in EDTA‐containing tubes, cryopreserved at −80 °C until analysis and processed using randomization schemes.

Ascertainment of Outcomes

Atherosclerosis incidence during prospective follow‐up was ascertained through the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) code I70 designation (Atherosclerosis). Follow‐up duration spanned from baseline enrollment in the UK Biobank cohort until the first occurrence among 3 possible end point events: documentation of atherosclerosis through diagnostic coding, participant death, or study termination (September 2024).

Covariates

Analytic adjustments incorporated sociodemographic profiles (ethnicity, age, sex, educational background), lifestyle behaviors (alcohol and smoking status), laboratory parameters (logarithmically transformed CRP concentrations, serum albumin levels), and clinical indicators (diabetes diagnosis, overall health rating). The standard PRSs set for LDL‐C were derived from the UK Biobank’s PRS database, a framework systematically translating cumulative genetic contributions into susceptibility metrics. 22 Participants were classified into low or high genetic risk strata using PRS percentiles.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics: continuous measures with normal distribution expressed as mean±SD, while categorical parameters reported through frequency distributions. Between‐group differences in baseline characteristics were evaluated using Student’s t test for normally distributed continuous variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for nonnormally distributed continuous variables. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 or Fisher’s exact test. Multivariable‐adjusted Cox regression models estimated atherosclerosis risk profiles, assessing associations through hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs. The composite MVX metric was analyzed in dual configurations: as a z‐transformed continuum (per SD unit elevation) and as quartile‐stratified groups (quartiles 1–4), using the lowest quartile (quartile 1) as comparator. MVX was evaluated as both a continuous (per SD) and categorical (quartile‐based) variable to assess linear gradients and threshold effects, respectively. Covariate adjustment proceeded incrementally: Model 1 was the unadjusted crude model; model 2 incorporated demographic confounders (sex, age, ethnic background); model 3 expanded to include lifestyle factors (body mass index [BMI], smoking/alcohol consumption), clinical history (diabetes status, history of hypertension), and laboratory markers (CRP, serum albumin, LDL‐C levels) based on the adjustments made in model 2. Restricted cubic spline analysis was conducted to explore nonlinear exposure–response patterns. Effect modification analyses were conducted through the inclusion of 2‐way interaction terms. Sensitivity analyses excluded early‐diagnosis cases (<1 year follow‐up) or extreme biomarker outliers (exceeding 2.5th–97.5th percentiles) to evaluate robustness. All analyses adopted 2‐tailed α=0.05 significance thresholds, executed through R statistical software version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

The analytical cohort comprised 171 242 participants, with a median follow‐up duration of 13.6 years, during which 1177 incident atherosclerosis cases were documented. Comparative analysis (Table 1) revealed notable demographic and clinical disparities: individuals with atherosclerosis tended to be older, male sex, limited educational attainment, and smokers, along with hypertension history and lower overall health rating. Biochemical profiling showed significantly reduced serum albumin and small HDL concentrations, while elevated values were observed for BMI, CRP, citrate, GlycA, and BCAAs (isoleucine, leucine, and valine), along with specific metabolite measures (MVX and MMX quartiles).

Association Between the MVX and Incident Atherosclerosis

Multivariable‐adjusted analyses revealed distinct risk patterns across metabolic indices (Table 2). In fully adjusted models, MVX exhibited a dose–response relationship where the highest quartile (quartile 4) conferred 59% excess atherosclerosis risk (HR, 1.59 [95% CI, 1.32–1.92]; P<0.001) compared with quartile 1, with each SD increment associated with 22% elevated risk (HR, 1.22 [95% CI, 1.15–1.30]; P<0.001). Nonlinear regression modeling through restricted cubic splines quantitatively confirmed this relationship, demonstrating a monotonic dose–response relationship in which ascending MVX levels corresponded proportionally with atherosclerosis susceptibility (Figure 1A).

Similarly, IVX manifested significant positive associations after adjustment (model 3: quartile 4 HR, 1.72 [95% CI, 1.40–2.11]; P<0.001; per‐SD HR, 1.26 [95% CI, 1.17–1.35]; P<0.001). In contrast, MMX parameters showed no statistically meaningful connection with atherosclerotic development in adjusted models. The restricted cubic spline confirmed a similar increasing trend between IVX increment and atherosclerosis risk, whereas the MMX exhibited a nonliner U‐shaped curve (Figure 1B and 1C).

These results maintained robust upon sensitivity validation excluding cases within the first year of follow‐up or biomarker outliers (2.5th–97.5th percentiles). MVX exhibited sustained risk escalation, with quartile 4 showing elevated adjusted hazards (HR, 1.49 [95% CI, 1.14–1.97]; P=0.004; HR, 1.56 [95% CI, 1.29–1.90]; P<0.001, respectively; Table S1). The IVX exhibited analogous monotonic hazard escalation, while MMX revealed insignificant associations across all quartiles after adjustment.

Subgroup Analysis

Subgroup analyses, stratified by sex, ethnicity, smoking/alcohol status, education, diabetes, hypertension, BMI, CRP, LDL, and LDL PRS, demonstrated a robust and statistically significant association between higher MVX scores and incident atherosclerosis across subgroups, independent of sex, diabetes, hypertension history, BMI categories, CRP levels, LDL or LDL PRS levels (Figure 2). Significant hazard elevations persisted in White individuals (HR, 1.23 [95% CI, 1.15–1.31]; P<0.001), high school–educated participants (HR, 1.28 [95% CI, 1.19–1.37]; P<0.001), current alcohol drinkers (HR, 1.22 [95% C, 1.15–1.31]; P<0.001), smokers (HR, 1.25 [95% CI, 1.16–1.34]; P<0.001), with no significant effect modification. The IVX exhibited analogous risk patterns with nonsignificant subgroup interactions (White race: HR, 1.27 [95% CI, 1.18–1.36]; low education level: HR, 1.30 [95% CI, 1.19–1.41]; smokers: HR, 1.28 [95% CI, 1.18–1.39]; alcohol drinker: HR, 1.26 [95% CI, 1.17–1.36]; all P<0.001; Figure S2). Conversely, the MMX demonstrated no consistent associations (Figure S3).

Discussion

In this large‐scale prospective cohort study including 171 242 UK Biobank participants with a median follow‐up of 13.6 years, we found that the MVX demonstrated a robust association with incident atherosclerosis risk. Specifically, individuals in the highest MVX quartile exhibited a 59% increased risk of developing atherosclerosis compared with those in the lowest quartile, with each SD increment associated with a 22% elevated risk. This relationship persisted after comprehensive adjustment for traditional cardiovascular risk factors. Furthermore, IVX independently showed strong positive associations with atherosclerosis risk, while the MMX demonstrated no significant relationship after adjustment. These findings were consistent across various demographic and clinical subgroups, indicating the robustness of the MVX as a potential predictor of atherosclerotic disease.

Our results align with emerging evidence suggesting that integrated metabolic profiling may provide superior risk prediction compared with traditional biomarkers alone. Previous metabolomic studies have identified various circulating metabolites associated with cardiovascular outcomes, 23 , 24 but few have developed composite indices that capture both inflammatory and metabolic dimensions of disease risk. The significant association between MVX and atherosclerosis observed in our analysis supports the concept that metabolic vulnerability represents a multifaceted phenotype that encompasses interconnected pathophysiological processes.

The strong association between IVX and atherosclerosis risk is consistent with the established role of inflammation in atherosclerotic progression. 25 GlycA, a composite NMR signal of multiple acute‐phase glycoproteins, has emerged as a stable marker of systemic inflammation. 26 Unlike high‐sensitivity CRP, which exhibits substantial intraindividual variability, GlycA demonstrates greater temporal stability, potentially explaining its stronger predictive capacity. 27 Previous studies have shown associations between elevated GlycA levels and various cardiovascular outcomes, including coronary heart disease and stroke. 28 , 29

Similarly, small HDL particle concentration, the second component of the IVX, reflects lipoprotein quality rather than simple quantity. Small HDL particles possess distinct antiatherogenic properties, including enhanced cholesterol efflux capacity and antioxidant activity. 30 The inverse relationship between small HDL particles and atherosclerosis risk observed in our analysis is consistent with prior reports suggesting that HDL particle characteristics may be more relevant to cardioprotective effects than HDL‐C levels alone. 31 Recent evidence suggests that HDL subclass distribution may provide incremental prognostic information beyond traditional lipid parameters. 32

Interestingly, the MMX, comprising BCAAs and citrate, showed no significant association with atherosclerosis after adjustment for confounders. This finding contrasts with some previous reports linking elevated BCAAs with cardiovascular outcomes. 33 , 34 This discrepancy may arise from the indirect and nonspecific nature of BCAA effects, primarily mediated through their metabolic derivatives rather than direct vascular actions. BCAAs exert pathophysiological influence via branched‐chain keto acids and propionyl–coenzyme A, which impair cardiac glucose oxidation through pyruvate dehydrogenase inhibition. 35 The relationship between BCAAs and cardiometabolic risk may be mediated primarily through insulin resistance and diabetes, which were adjusted for in our models. Component heterogeneity further obscures associations, as individual BCAAs exert divergent effects: Isoleucine reduction improves insulin sensitivity via fibroblast growth factor 21–uncoupling protein 1 axis activation, whereas leucine may enhance mitochondrial biogenesis in early insulin resistance. 36 Additionally, BCAA actions exhibit disease‐stage dependence, transitioning from potentially beneficial metabolic modulation in early insulin resistance to detrimental effects in established diabetes due to impaired branched‐chain keto acid clearance. This mechanistic complexity, compounded by the opposing roles of BCAA components and dynamic concentration‐dependent effects, likely explains the null association observed in our model, and aligns with the U‐shaped relationship identified in our restricted cubic spline analysis.

The robust association between the MVX and atherosclerosis across diverse subgroups, including different genetic risk strata defined by LDL‐C PRSs, underscores the potential utility of metabolic profiling in personalized risk assessment. This finding suggests that metabolic vulnerability may represent a modifiable risk factor that operates independently of genetic predisposition. 37 Importantly, the consistency of MVX–atherosclerosis associations across BMI categories indicates that metabolic vulnerability captures dimensions of cardiometabolic risk beyond simple adiposity measures. 38

From a mechanistic perspective, the strong predictive capacity of the IVX relative to the MMX aligns with the contemporary understanding of atherosclerosis as fundamentally an inflammatory disease. 39 Chronic low‐grade inflammation promotes endothelial dysfunction, macrophage recruitment, and foam cell formation, which are key processes in atherosclerotic plaque development. 40 GlycA, reflecting systemic inflammatory burden, may serve as an integrated marker of these pathophysiological processes. 41 Simultaneously, altered lipoprotein metabolism, particularly dysfunctional HDL particles, compromises reverse cholesterol transport and enhances inflammatory signaling within the arterial wall. 42

Our findings have several clinical implications. First, they suggest that comprehensive metabolic profiling using NMR spectroscopy may enhance cardiovascular risk stratification beyond traditional risk factors. The ability of the MVX to predict atherosclerosis risk independently of conventional risk scores indicates its potential complementary role in clinical assessment. 43 Second, the inflammatory component’s predominant contribution to risk prediction highlights the importance of targeting inflammation in atherosclerosis prevention. 44 Emerging anti‐inflammatory therapies, such as colchicine and interleukin‐1β inhibitors, have shown promise in reducing cardiovascular events, aligning with our observations regarding inflammatory vulnerability. 45 Third, the relatively modest contribution of BCAAs to atherosclerosis risk suggests future intervention studies to assess whether modulating BCAA metabolism could influence atherosclerotic disease progression. 46 This approach would help clarify any potential role for dietary modifications in primary prevention.

Despite these strengths, several limitations warrant consideration. First, while the UK Biobank provides comprehensive phenotyping, participants are generally healthier than the general population, potentially limiting generalizability. 47 Furthermore, the cohort’s restricted racial and ethnic diversity may limit the extrapolation of our findings to non‐European populations. The use of complete case analysis for handling missing covariate data, while ensuring the internal validity of multivariable models, may reduce statistical power and introduce selection bias if data are not missing completely at random. Second, metabolomic profiles were assessed only at baseline, preventing evaluation of how longitudinal changes in metabolic vulnerability might influence disease progression. 48 Besides, although the MMX was predefined to include citrate and BCAAs, lactate was not incorporated in the formula. Future studies should examine whether integrating lactate or lactate/citrate ratios enhances the MMX’s predictive utility for atherosclerosis, particularly in cohorts with broader metabolic phenotypes. Third, this study specifically focused on atherosclerosis identified through the ICD‐10 I70 code, which by definition excludes cerebral, coronary, mesenteric, and pulmonary subtypes. 49 This inherently restricts the generalizability of results to atherosclerosis in other vascular territories. Future studies should investigate whether metabolic vulnerability exhibits vascular territory–specific effects. Fourth, while extensive covariate adjustment for numerous confounders including CRP and albumin, residual confounding from unmeasured factors, particularly potential inflammatory comorbidities and processes, cannot be entirely ruled out. 50 Subsequent investigations should incorporate detailed medical history of inflammatory diseases to mitigate this limitation. Fifth, the observational nature of our study precludes definitive causal inference, despite the prospective design and robust associations. 51

Future research should focus on validating the MVX in diverse populations, investigating its predictive capacity for specific atherosclerotic manifestations (coronary, cerebral, and peripheral), and evaluating whether interventions targeting metabolic vulnerability components can modify disease risk. 52 Longitudinal assessment of metabolomic profiles could provide insights into the temporal dynamics of metabolic vulnerability and identify critical windows for intervention. 53 Additionally, integration of metabolomic data with other omics platforms (genomics, proteomics, and transcriptomics) may further enhance risk prediction and elucidate underlying biological mechanisms. 54

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that MVX, particularly its inflammatory component, is independently associated with atherosclerosis risk in a large prospective cohort. These findings suggest that comprehensive metabolic profiling may enhance cardiovascular risk assessment beyond traditional approaches and highlight the central role of inflammation in atherosclerotic disease progression. Further research is warranted to determine whether targeting metabolic vulnerability can mitigate cardiovascular risk and improve clinical outcomes.
