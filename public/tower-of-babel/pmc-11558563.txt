Establishment of a Serum-Free Human iPSC-Derived Model of Peripheral Myelination
Authors: Aakash Patel, Marnie Williams, Kenneth Hawkins, Leandro Gallo, Marcella Grillo, Nesar Akanda, Xiufang Guo, Stephen Lambert, James J. Hickman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Myelination and the formation of nodes of Ranvier are essential for the rapid conduction of nerve impulses along axons in the peripheral nervous system (PNS). While many animal-based and serum-containing models of peripheral myelination have been developed, these have limited ability when it comes to studying genetic disorders affecting peripheral myelination. We report a fully induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived human model of peripheral myelination using Schwann cells (SCs) and motoneurons, cultured in a serum-free medium on patterned and nonpatterned surfaces. Results demonstrated iPSC-derived SC-expressed early growth response protein 2 (Egr2), a key transcription factor for myelination, and after ∼30 days in coculture, hallmark features of myelination, including myelin segment and node of Ranvier formation, were observed. Myelin segments were stained for the myelin basic protein, which surrounded neurofilament-stained motoneuron axons. Clusters of voltage-gated sodium channels flanked by paranodal protein contactin-associated protein 1, indicating node of Ranvier formation, were also observed. High-resolution confocal microscopy allowed for 3D reconstruction and measurement of myelin g-ratios of myelin segments, with an average g-ratio of 0.67, consistent with reported values in the literature, indicating mature myelin segment formation. This iPSC-based model of peripheral myelination provides a platform to investigate numerous PNS diseases, including Charcot–Marie Tooth disorder, Guillian–Barre syndrome, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, and antimyelin-associated glycoprotein peripheral neuropathy, with the potential for greater translatability to humans for improving the applicability for drug-screening programs.

Introduction

Schwann cells (SCs), the primary glial cells found in the peripheral nervous system (PNS), are derived from a type of neural crest cell referred to as Schwann cell precursors (SCPs). SCPs are found in embryonic nerves, and as the embryonic nerve matures, SCPs differentiate into immature Schwann cells, which further develop into myelinating or nonmyelinating Schwann cells directed by environmental cues.1 Schwann cells perform a myriad of functions, including facilitating neuromuscular junction formation, aiding pain mediation and nerve injury response, provide neurotrophic support and protection to axons, and myelin sheath and node of Ranvier formation to enhance the conduction velocity of propagating action potentials. Their multifaceted role makes them a crucial component in the proper functioning of the PNS.2−4 Myelination is a complex process that involves the alignment and wrapping of Schwann cells around neuronal axons in a multilamellar fashion, followed by the production of myelin, forming a myelin sheath. This occurs in a 1:1 relationship, where Schwann cells myelinate only a single axon, unlike myelination in the central nervous system, where one oligodendrocyte can myelinate multiple axons. Before myelination occurs, nonmyelinating Schwann cells surround bundles of multiple neuronal axons and “select” certain axons based on their diameter and surface expression of neuregulins (NRG), specifically neuregulin-1 type III, to extend out of the bundle for myelination.5 This process is known as radial sorting, and the group of axons encapsulated by the nonmyelinating Schwann cell is referred to as a Remak bundle.6

Once an axon has been selected for myelination, myelinating Schwann cells will migrate toward these axons, guided by the surface expression of NRG1 type III. As Schwann cells interact with the axons, axonal NRG1 starts a cascade of transcription factors, which leads to activation of a factor called early growth response 2, a hallmark indicator of myelinating Schwann cells.5 Initially, SCs will align along an axon and extend cell membrane processes to surround the axon and start producing myelin, a finding that was observed using an early in vitro model of myelination.7 As myelination progresses, the number of wrappings around the axon increases and forms what is called compact myelin. The myelin sheath is made up of ∼80% lipids and ∼20% soluble basic proteins, with myelin basic protein (MBP) constituting 30% percent of the myelin proteins. MBP plays a major role in the compaction of the myelin sheath and is highly expressed in compact myelin.8 The measure of compaction of the myelin sheath is referred to as the g-ratio and is calculated by dividing the diameter of axon by the diameter of the total fiber, meaning the axon with myelin sheath.9 The g-ratio can be used as an indicator of mature myelination, with 0.6-.7 being the widely accepted g-ratio range for mature myelination in the PNS.10−13 A higher g-ratio could be due to less compact myelin, either due to immature myelination or a product of demyelination due to some disease or disorder. While transmission electron microscopy (TEM) has been the traditional method for measuring the g-ratio, this method is restricted to ex vivo tissue slices or 3D in vitro samples, so it may not be applicable to all myelination models. Due to these constraints, alternate methods such as in vivo fluorescent imaging and magnetic resonance imaging to measure g-ratio have been established.14,15

As myelin sheaths form adjacent to each other on the same axon, a small gap remains between the two segments where clustering of voltage-gated sodium channels (VGSCs) is promoted, leading to the establishment of structures termed nodes of Ranvier.3,13,16−18 These structures consist of VGSC clusters flanked by the paranodal cell adhesion molecule contactin-associated protein 1 (Caspr), which is found at the ends of myelin segments and assists in the adhesion, and communication between the axon and SCs.19 The clustering of VGSCs between myelin sheaths prevents ionic flow in the areas insulated by myelin, causing the ionic current to flow out and depolarize the axonal membrane at the next node. This machinery causes active excitation of the axonal membrane to “jump” from node to node and is termed saltatory conduction, stemming from saltare, meaning “to jump” in Latin.17,20−22

The majority of reported in vitro peripheral myelination models have been animal-based models in a serum-containing medium. These models have allowed elucidation of many myelination-related processes such as the importance of proper basal lamina formation, the interactions of various proteins between both Schwann cells and axons, the importance of ascorbate for induction of myelination, and other transcription pathways that lead to myelination.7,23−27 In recent years, models of myelination in a serum-free medium formulation, as well as models utilizing human primary Schwann cells, have also been established, both of which proffer their own advantages. Serum contains a mix of factors that are undefined, some of which are inflammatory factors that could potentially affect the function and the phenotype of the model in a manner that is unknown. In contrast, models that are established in a defined, serum-free medium allow for control of factors in the medium, enabling investigation of the function of specific factors without relying on knockout models.28 Reported peripheral myelination models in serum-free media include models using rat sensory neurons and Schwann cells, rat motoneurons and Schwann cells, and human iPSC motoneurons with human primary SCs, all of which exhibit the hallmark features of myelination.17,20,29 More recently, models of peripheral myelination utilizing human primary Schwann cells in both sera-containing and serum-free media have also been developed.17,30 While these models are useful for studying Schwann cell myelination and the involved mechanisms and could potentially be used to study demyelination/remyelination, the applicability toward modeling genetic disorders is difficult. Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) are a rapidly growing tool in the field of organ-on-a-chip systems, making them an attractive route for creating disease models.31,32 iPSCs allow for differentiation of multiple cell types and can be induced to express specific genetic aberrations or mutations at the iPSC stage, resulting in differentiated cells that also contain the genetic mutation. These advances in iPSC technology have provided a crucial component in the development of fully human organ-on-a-chip models of various genetic diseases. The recent passing of the FDA Modernization Act 2.0, which explicitly calls for in vitro models as a way to expedite the preclinical and clinical stages of drug testing at a fraction of the cost, further cements the development of human organ-on-a-chip models as a burgeoning area of research.33

An iPSC to SC differentiation protocol developed by Kim et al. was utilized to differentiate SCs, which were then combined with iPSC-derived motoneurons in a serum-free medium, allowing us to establish the first reported fully iPSC-derived, human in vitro model of myelination in a serum-free medium.34 iPSC motoneurons and iPSC SCs were individually characterized via phase images, immunocytochemistry (ICC), and flow cytometry. Cocultures were grown on surfaces modified with the biomimetic, nonbiological substrate N-1[3 (trimethoxysilyl) propyl] diethylenetriamine (DETA), and on surfaces patterned using a combination of DETA and 2-[methoxypoly(ethyleneoxy)propyl]trimethoxysilane (PEG), utilizing established surface patterning methods.35,36 Both patterned and unpatterned surfaces were further coated with a mixture of entactin/collagen IV/laminin/fibronectin, previously used in a myelination model utilizing primary human Schwan cells.17 Myelination was confirmed via ICC, specifically the expression of MBP in S100b positive SCs aligned along a neurofilament positive motoneuron axon, and the node of Ranvier formation was demonstrated using a PAN-vgsc antibody and expression of paranodal cell adhesion molecule Caspr. The g-ratio of myelinated segments was measured by creating high resolution 3D reconstructions using z-stacks of myelin segments. The node of Ranvier imaging and myelin segments for g-ratio were collected from patterned surfaces, since they promoted linear growth of axons, making it easier to identify and isolate myelinated areas from individual axons. This platform is of interest in the organ-on-a-chip field, with applications for modeling genetic demyelinating disorders and testing of potential therapeutics for neurological disorders in a functional human model.

Methods and Materials

DETA Surface Modification, PEG-DETA Surface Patterning, and Extracellular Matrix (ECM) Coating

For unpatterned surfaces, 18 mm round glass coverslips (Thomas Scientific, Swedesboro, NJ, USA) were plasma-treated for 20 min at 750 mTorr using a plasma cleaner PDC-32G (Harrick Plasma) to remove surface contaminants and functionalize the surfaces with reactive hydroxyls. The DETA silane (N-1(3-(trimethoxylsilyl) propyl) diethylenetriamine (SIT8398.0; Gelest) monolayer was formed by the reaction of activated surfaces with a 0.1% (v/v) mixture of the organosilane in HPLC-grade toluene (T2904; Fisher, Suwanne, GA, USA). The DETA-coated coverslips were heated to 60 °C, then cooled to 45 °C, rinsed with toluene, reheated to approximately the same temperature, and then cured for at least 2 h at 110 °C.

Patterned surfaces were also prepared on 18 mm glass coverslips by plasma treatment for 20 min at 750 mTorr followed by a salinization reaction using 0.15% (v/v) PEG silane (3-[methoxy(polyethyleneoxy)6–9]propyltrimethoxysilane, 346170250; Fisher Scientific) and 0.05% (v/v) of 37% hydrochloric acid in HPLC-grade toluene (T2904; Fisher, Suwanne, GA, USA) for 30 min, resulting in protein or cell attachment to the coverslip to be entropically unfavorable. 50 μm line patterns were produced by ablating the PEG-coated surfaces using an ArF excimer laser (2 Ws, 45 s) through a photolithographic quartz mask. After PEG ablation, surfaces were backfilled with DETA using the same process described above, but cured in the oven for 1–2 h maximum. Surfaces were characterized by contact angle and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (K-Alpha, Thermo Fisher) as described previously.37 Patterned and unpatterned coverslips were then incubated at 37 °C for 1 h in an entactin/collagen IV/laminin (ECL) (08-110, Sigma-Aldrich)/Fibronectin (F1141, Sigma-Aldrich) solution to deposit the extracellular matrix proteins on the DETA-modified surfaces.

Human iPSC Culture and Expansion

Human iPSCs (line ND41865) were obtained from the Coriell Institute for Medical Research and denoted as passage 0 (P0). The cells were passaged up to P10 using NIH recommended protocols and cryopreserved. Cells from P6 to P10 were used for motoneuron (MN) and Schwann cell (SC) differentiation.

Human iPSC-Motoneuron Culture

iPSCs were passaged up to P10 using the NIH recommended protocols. Cells from P6 to P10 were used for motoneuron (MN) differentiation. MNs were differentiated from iPSCs by following previously published protocols with the modification of replacing Component C with 0.1 μM LDN193189 (Tocris #6053) and 6 μM SB431542 (Tocris #1614).38 Differentiated MNs were maintained in human motoneuron medium (hMN) as previously described.39 Vials of MNs were cryopreserved to be used in future experiments. Following the retrieval of MNs from cryostorage, the vial of cells was thawed in a water bath with gentle swirling. The cells were transferred to a 50 mL conical tube, and 9 mL of cold PBS was added to the cells dropwise. The cells were pelleted at 250 g for 5 min in a 4 °C centrifuge. The PBS was aspirated, and the MNs were suspended in 1 mL of hMN medium; a cell count was performed. The cells were plated on 18 mm round coverslips (Sigma CLS284518) at 200 cells/mm2 suspended in 1 mL of hMN medium for monoculture characterization. The cultures were maintained in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator. The following day, a full medium change was performed to remove nonadherent cells, and a 50% medium change was performed every other day for the duration of the experiments. Patch Clamp Electrophysiology

The single cell patch clamp was performed on differentiated motoneurons as described previously, using a Zeiss Axioscope 2FS Plus upright microscope.37,40 The largest motoneurons exhibiting a round or multipolar morphology were selected for patch recordings.41 A Sutter P97 pipet puller was used to make patch pipettes from borosilicate glass, with a resistance of 6–10 MΩ. The intracellular solution used in the patch pipet consisted of 140 mM K-gluconate, 2 mM MgCl2, 2 mM Na2ATP, 5 mM phosphocreatine, 2.4 mg phosphocreatine kinase, and 10 mM HEPES buffer, all at a final pH of 7.2. Voltage clamp and current clamp experiments were performed with a Multiclamp 700A (Axon, Union City, CA) amplifier, and signal filtration was performed at 3 kHz and digitized at 20 kHz using an Axon Digidata 1322A interface. pClamp 8 (Axon) software was used to calculate the membrane potential correction. Depolarization-evoked action potentials were recorded in current clamp mode, with pClamp 8 software used to record and analyze data.

Human iPSC-Schwann Cell Culture

SCs were differentiated following the protocol established by Kim et al.34 Differentiated SCs were maintained in Schwann cell medium (SCM; Sciencell no. 1701), and vials of SCs were cryopreserved for use in future experiments. Following retrieval of SCs from the cryostorage, the cell vials were thawed in a water bath with gentle swirling. The cells were transferred to a 50 mL conical tube, and 9 mL of cold PBS was added to the cells dropwise, and then, the cells were pelleted at 250 g for 5 min in a 4 °C centrifuge. The PBS was aspirated, the SCs were suspended in 1 mL of SCM, and a cell count was performed. The cells were plated on ECL-fibronectin-coated 18 mm round coverslips (Sigma CLS284518) at 200 cells/mm2 suspended in 1 mL of SCM medium for immunocytochemical characterization and in a T25 flask in 3 mL of SCM for flow cytometry characterization. The cultures were maintained in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator. The following day, a full medium change was performed to remove nonadherent cells. A 50% medium change was performed every other day for the duration of the experiments. The full differentiation timeline is shown in Supplemental Figure 1.

Flow Cytometry

iPSC SCs were plated in a T-25 flask in SCM, and after reaching confluency, the flask was switched to MM, and fed with half medium exchange every other day. Seven days after switching to MM, ascorbic acid was added at each medium change. At Day 14, the cells were lifted using Accutase (Sigma A6964). An equal volume of FACS buffer, consisting of PBS+1%BSA, was used to neutralize the accutase reaction, after which the cells were collected in 50 mL conical tubes (one for each flask) and centrifuged for 5 min at 300 g. The supernatant was aspirated, and the cell pellets were resuspended in 100 μL of FACS buffer. Next, 100 μL of PBS+0.2% saponin was added to the cell suspension to permeabilize the cells. Cells were then pelleted at 300 g for 5 min and resuspended in 100 μL of FACS buffer. 20 μL of Human FcR Block (Miltenyi Biotec) was then added to each tube on ice to block the Fc receptors for 20 min. After blocking, each tube of cells was split into 3 1.5 μL microcentrifuge tubes; 5 μL of Egr2-PE and 5 μL of Draq7 were added to one tube and 5 μL of unconjugated anti-S100b (Thermo Fisher PA5-78161) and 5 μL of Draq7 were added to the second tube, while the third tube was left unstained. The second tube was incubated on ice for 60 min after S100b addition, then pelleted at 300 g for 5 min, and resuspened in FACS buffer 3×, followed by the addition of AlexaFluor 488 at a concentration of 1:2000. All tubes were then left to incubate on ice for 60 min before being washed with 200 μL of FACS buffer, then spun down for 5 min at 300 g, and resuspended in 120 μL of FACS buffer. This washing was done two more times and then analyzed using a Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX LX Series Flow Cytometer with Plate Loader using the CytExpert version 2.0 Software. Prior to running flow analysis, the optical alignment and fluidics of the instrument were calibrated using CytoFlex Daily QC Fluorospheres. The unstained tube for each of the conditions was used to ensure proper gating of the stained population. Since Egr2 and S100b were performed in independent experiments, no compensation parameters were needed.

Immunocytochemistry, Confocal Microscopy, and Image Analysis

Cocultures were fixed between Days 30 and 35 with freshly prepared 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 15 min and then rinsed twice with PBS (pH 7.2, without Mg2+ and Ca2+). Samples for myelin sheath staining were permeabilized with a 0.2% Triton X-100/PBS solution, and samples for node of Ranvier staining were permeabilized with a 0.5% Triton X-100/PBS solution for 15 min at room temperature. Nonspecific binding sites were blocked with the permeabilization solution and a blocking buffer (5% Donkey serum plus 0.5% BSA in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature. Cocultures were incubated with primary antibodies in blocking buffer at 4 °C overnight. The following antibodies were purchased; Anti-MBP (Abcam ab62631), Anti-NF-H (Millipore as5539), Anti-S100b (Thermo Fisher 710363), Anti-PLP (Abcam ab28486), Anti-PAN (Millipore AB5210a), and Anti-Caspr1 (Novus Biologicals AF7548). The next day, the primary antibody/blocking buffer solution was removed, and the cells were rinsed 3× with PBS. Then, AlexaFluor 488, 568, and 647 nm secondary antibodies diluted 1:1000 in blocking buffer were added and incubated at room temperature for 2 h in the dark. The secondary antibody/blocking buffer solution was removed, and cells were rinsed 3× with PBS and then incubated for 5 min in a 1:1000 DAPI in PBS solution. After 5 min, the DAPI/PBS solution was removed and the cells were rinsed three times with PBS, before being mounted onto glass slides using VectaShield mounting medium (Invitrogen P36931). Coverslips stained for myelin sheath and node of Ranvier formation and g-ratio measurements were imaged and quantified using a Nikon Ti2 microscope and C2 confocal laser with the Nikon NIS Elements AR acquisition and analysis software. Images for iPSC MN and SC monocultures and axonal length and viability were collected using an Axioskop 2 mot plus upright spinning disk confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss), with the Axioskop 2 connected to an XCite 120 Fluorescence Illumination system (EXFO) beam, a multispectral scanning laser utilizing Volocity software (PerkinElmer).

Quantification of Axon Length, Motoneuron Survival, and G-Ratio

Axon length was quantified by analyzing confocal images from three separate cultures fixed at Day 3, using the NeuronJ plugin in ImageJ via neurofilament expression. MN survival was assessed by counting the total number of cells from three independent cultures that exhibited colocalization of DAPI, as well as neuronal marker neurofilament and MAP2. To measure the g-ratio and unmyelinated axon diameter, three separate cultures were characterized with neurofilament and MBP at Days 23–25 and Days 30–32. Z-stack images were captured with 0.3 μm slices using a 20× lens, 1.5× optical zoom multiplier, and 4.8× pixel zoom multiplier. Diameters of 10 different myelin segments on 10 different axons per time point were measured using the 3D length measurement feature of the Nikon NIS Elements AR software. For each myelin segment or unmyelinated axon, two diameter measurements from opposing ends of the same myelin segment or either end of the same axon were taken and averaged together to determine either the myelin segment g-ratio or unmyelinated axon diameter.

Statistical Analysis

Values are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) of a minimum of three independent experiments for the axon length and viability measurements. Data were evaluated with unpaired Student’s t test when analyzing the overall difference between two conditions, with and without SCs. Student’s t test analysis was run with a two-tail distribution and homo- or heteroscedastic variances. Values are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation for g-ratio measurements.

Results

Characterization of iPSC-Derived MN and SC Monocultures

Before establishing myelinating cocultures, motoneurons (MNs) and Schwann cells (SCs) were differentiated from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), and characterized for cell-specific features. SCs were differentiated using a protocol developed by Kim et al. and characterized for Schwann cell identity via phase images, ICC, and flow cytometry.34 Within 3 days of differentiation, SCs displayed characteristic SC-like morphology and initiated the extension of processes (Figure 1a).42 After 2 weeks in culture, SCs proliferated and exhibited a spindle-like morphology, with a characteristic swirling culture phenotype (Figure 1b). SCs exhibited the positive expression for markers myelin proteolipid protein (PLP), as seen in the work done by Kim et al., and S100b, a marker for a calcium-binding protein widely used to identify SCs (Figure 1c,d).34 The cells were further characterized via flow cytometry, which indicated that 92.63% of cells expressed S100b, suggesting an almost pure SC population (Figure 1e). Furthermore, SCs cultured in myelination medium treated with 50 μg/mL ascorbic acid showed that 88.31% of SCs were positive for myelination transcription factor Egr2, alluding to the myelination potential of iPSC-derived Schwann cells, which is ∼47% higher than what was seen in primary human SCs cultured in the same conditions (Figure 1e).17

Motoneurons were differentiated using a protocol previously established in our lab, and on Day 4, iPSC-MN cultures exhibited axonal outgrowth, which progressed to the formation of morphologically mature axons by Day 10 (Figure 2a,b).43 MNs were seen to express the MN marker ChAT and neuronal marker MAP2, with characteristic MN cell morphology via ICC, while 93.76% of cells expressed the MN marker ChAT via flow cytometry (Figure 2c–e). Single cell patch clamp electrophysiology was performed at 3 weeks in vitro on motoneuron batches utilized for both mono- and coculture experiments. Depolarization-evoked action potentials were recorded in current clamp mode to confirm that the differentiation process yielded functional, mature cells capable of firing spontaneous action potentials (AP) (Figure 2f). 16 MNs across 3 different batches of differentiation were patched, and 13/16 of these MNs showed robust spontaneous activity.

Motoneuron survivability was quantified by counting cells exhibiting colocalization of the nuclear marker DAPI and two neuronal markers, neurofilament and MAP2. Coculture conditions exhibited a significant increase in the average number of surviving MNs present 3 days post-plating compared to MN monoculture (Figure 5a). The axon length was measured using ImageJ, and a significant increase in average axon length was observed by MNs cocultured with SCs compared to MN monocultures (Figure 5b).

To confirm if the phase bright aligned areas seen in phase images (Figure 4c,f) were mature myelin segments, cocultures were stained with neurofilament to image the motoneuron axon, S100b for aligned Schwann cells, and MBP for myelin sheath formation. Myelination was seen at varying degrees throughout the culture, with some axons possessing sparse myelin segments along its length (Figure 6 top) and other axons with multiple, adjacent myelin segments (Figure 6 bottom). G-Ratio Measurement of iPSC-Derived Myelinating Cocultures

The g-ratio is a metric used to establish the degree of myelination by comparing the diameter of the total nerve fiber, meaning the axon and myelin sheath, to the diameter of the axon (). A higher g-ratio indicates a more compact myelin sheath, while a lower g-ratio means that the myelin sheath is less compact or loosely wrapped around the axon, which can directly translate to reduced conduction velocity of the nerve, with a higher g-ratio indicating an increased nerve conduction velocity. ICC and high resolution confocal microscopy were used to measure the g-ratio of 10 different myelin segments across 3 separate cultures between Days 30 and 32 and compared to values collected utilizing the same method at Days 23–25 (Table 1). This was performed on patterned surfaces to allow for easier identification of individual myelinated axons and to prevent crossing over of axons on top of one another. Neurofilament was used to stain the axon, and MBP was used to stain the myelin sheath. Discussion

In this study, we have described the development of the first fully iPSC-derived, serum-free human in vitro human model of peripheral myelination. We observed hallmark features of myelination, including node of Ranvier formation, SC alignment along motoneuron axons, and subsequent myelin sheath formation, with an average g-ratio of 0.67, indicative of mature myelination. ICC was used to visualize these features, with high resolution 3D confocal microscopy allowing for measurement of g-ratios, which is a new capability for the field. While both patterned and unpatterned surfaces were used, differences in efficiency were not investigated, as there seemed to be little difference between the two conditions in terms of myelination capability. The patterning mainly allowed for easier measurement of the G-ratio, and node of Ranvier imaging, as it directed axon growth in a single direction and limited crossing over of axons on top of one another. However, the ability to directionally pattern myelinated nerves acts as proof of concept for application to microelectrode arrays, where alignment of axons along electrodes could provide a method to measure the conduction velocity of myelinated versus unmyelinated axons. While many in vitro myelination models have been previously established and provided valuable insights into the process of myelination, they have mostly been composed of animal cells, with some of the later models utilizing human primary SCs.10,16,17,20,23,24,26,27,29,30,47−50 Our model utilizes iPSC-derived human Schwann cells and motoneurons, allowing for the modeling of genetic disorders that affect peripheral myelination, something that would not be possible using primary animal cells and would not be as accurate if using genetically modified primary human cells. This gives the advantage of a platform that can easily be used to study genetic disorders as they develop in a setting more translatable to human patients.

While the myelin sheath and node of Ranvier formation are some of the most discussed aspects of Schwann cell function, providing neurotrophic support is another crucial role of SCs. Immature SCs have been seen to secrete factors such as insulin-like growth factor 2, neurotrophins, and platelet-derived growth factors among many others, which have been known to support neuronal viability, and have led to the investigation of utilizing SCs for nerve repair, as well as investigation of lack of proper SC function contributing to neurodegenerative disease.1,44,51 By examining motoneuron survival and axon length, we saw a significant difference in both of these readouts in MN monocultures, vs when cocultured with SCs. This investigation was performed on Day 3 in order to allow for individual axonal measurements without the risk of axonal bundling to confirm that any support being proffered by SCs was separate from that due to myelination and since postthaw is when the highest loss of viability is usually observed when seeding neurons.

When characterizing the iPSC-derived SCs, it was found that ∼88% of the cells expressed the key myelination transcription factor Egr2 in response to dosing with ascorbic acid, compared to our previous work utilizing primary human SCs, where only ∼41% of cells expressed Egr2.17 This difference may be due to the maturation state of the SCs, as the primary SCs may already have a large portion of mature cells that are less likely to dedifferentiate to immature SCs and become myelinating SCs. In contrast, the iPSC-derived SCs may mostly be starting out at an immature SC phenotype, making them more likely to trend toward the myelinating phenotype in response to ascorbate. Alignment of SCs along motoneuron axons occurs before wrapping, and myelination can occur. In this iPSC-derived myelination coculture, alignment occurred sooner than what was observed in the previous human primary SC myelination model, which could be attributed to the higher number of Egr2-expressing SCs. By Day 15, multiple aligned SCs were observed via phase microscopy, and between Days 30 and 35, what appear to be myelin segments begin to form (Figure 4). Myelin sheath formation was then confirmed via immunostaining with neurofilament and MBP (Figure 6).

Node of Ranvier formation is another hallmark feature of myelination. These structures form in the small unmyelinated areas between myelin segments and are characterized by VGSC clusters flanked by proteins such as contactins and neurofascins, which create paranodal axo-glial junctions assisting in adherence of the myelin sheath to axons. We characterized the formation of these structures by staining for Nav-PAN and Caspr, which showed clustering of VGSCs between SCs, flanked by expression of the paranodal contactin-associated protein. These structures allow for the maintenance of the ionic current of motoneuron action potentials (APs) at nodes of Ranvier rather than diffusing along the entire length of the axon, by allowing for saltatory conduction over the insulated myelin segments, causing a significant increase in the conduction velocity of APs compared to an unmyelinated axon, a feature that is critical for proper PNS function. Diseases and neuropathies can impair the conductance of APs by causing loss of nodes of Ranvier, which can lead to impaired nerve function, making them a critical feature in any system that would be used for the investigation of PNS disorders. Since the model described here exhibits node of Ranvier formation, quantification, and morphological changes of this feature could be used to assess the effects of diseases that affect peripheral myelination as well as a readout for rescue in response to a therapeutic. Diseases like chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy have been suggested to involve an autoimmune response, which targets myelinated axons at the paranodes flanking these structures, which would make them a key investigation point when modeling this disease.52 This could also serve as a readout to potentially reveal mechanisms for diseases that have yet to be fully understood, by investigating which aspect of myelination is affected (initial wrapping, myelin sheath, nodes of Ranvier, etc.) or in what order they are affected, which could aid in the development of new or more efficient treatments.

The g-ratio is a useful indicator of the degree or maturity of myelination but has historically been difficult and labor-intensive to measure. It is defined as the ratio between the diameter of the axon and the diameter of the total nerve, which includes the axon and its myelin sheath. As myelination occurs, SCs extend membrane processes that wrap around the axon multiple times in a multilamellar fashion. This wrapping becomes more compact as myelin is produced, resulting in a g-ratio of myelinated axons between 0.6 and 0.7. In the PNS, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) is the most common method used to obtain this measurement, but depending on the platform, this method is not always easily applicable, as it requires microtoming thin sections (∼300 Å) in order to be performed. TEM is commonly conducted on ex vivo tissue slices, making it unsuitable for live cell imaging. Additionally, performing TEM on 2D culture models, like the one described in this report, is challenging and typically requires specialized facilities devoted to electron microscopy. Other methods to measure the g-ratio have been reported, such as immunohistochemistry, fluorescent spectral imaging, label-free spectroscopic analysis, coherent anti-Stokes Raman scattering imaging, magnetic resonance imaging, and light microscopy.12,14,53−55 Since TEM is not applicable for live imaging or imaging of 2D cultures, the exploration of alternate methods is valuable to the field. We used a fluorescence-based approach on fixed, patterned culture samples to determine the g-ratios of myelin segments. Low antibody concentrations, a pinhole size of 1.2 aU, and z-stacks of 0.3 μm per slice were used to create high resolution 3D reconstructions of myelin segments and measure diameters of axons and total nerve diameters. To determine the g-ratio of individual segments, two measurements were taken from opposite sides of the same segment and averaged. A total of 10 segments on 10 different axons across 3 separate cultures were analyzed in this manner at Days 30–32, as well as at an earlier time point, Days 23–25. Indicative of mature myelination, our results demonstrated an average g-ratio of 0.67 at Days 30–32 in our system, which falls within the range reported in literature acquired by TEM.13 In addition to measuring the g-ratio of myelinated segments, diameters of unmyelinated axons in the same cultures were also analyzed using the same technique. Interestingly, it was observed that unmyelinated axons in our culture had an average diameter of 1.46 ± 0.20 μm, while axons with myelin segments had an average diameter of 2.15 ± 0.49 μm, supporting the postulation that higher caliber axons are more likely to be myelinated compared to those with lower diameters.3,5 When comparing the g-ratio of myelin segments at Days 23–25 with segments at Days 30–32, an increase in average g-ratio from 0.41 to 0.67 was observed, suggesting maturation of the myelin segments over time via myelin sheath compaction and thickening of the axon (Table 1). While this analysis was done on fixed samples, it could potentially also be performed on live cultures with fluorescent labeling to measure changes in the same culture over time. Using this method, changes in the g-ratio caused by demyelinating diseases, nerve injury, or chemical injury, as well as response to potential therapeutics could be measured, furthering the value of this iPSC myelination model as a platform to model disease and test therapeutics.

Conclusions

As human-on-a-chip systems and iPSC technology continue to progress, so does the potential for modeling of different organs and diseases. Recently, the US Food and Drug Administration passed the FDA Modernization Act 2.0, in the hopes of reducing costs and expediting preclinical and clinical programs for the development of disease therapeutics. In this study, we describe the development of a fully human iPSC-derived coculture model of peripheral myelination in a serum-free, defined medium. Characterization of myelin segments and nodes of Ranvier formation provide evidence of hallmark features of myelination. Additionally, we measured g-ratios, which could serve as a potential method to look at demyelination and remyelination in disease versions of this platform. This marks the first time a fully iPSC-derived model of human myelination in a serum-free medium has been established, opening doors to creating models of genetic diseases affecting peripheral myelination, such as Charcot Marie Tooth, Guillan-Barre syndrome, and chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy as well as investigating mechanisms of myelination that are still left to be discovered. Furthermore, we established directional growth on line-patterned surfaces using chemical surface patterning, a technique that could enable patterning of microelectrode arrays to aid in the collection of functional data from myelinating cocultures, such as conduction velocity of action potentials, providing another highly useful readout for disease modeling and therapeutic investigation in a translatable, cost-effective platform.
