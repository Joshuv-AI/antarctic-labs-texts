Magnesium ferrite-based composites for water treatment: synthesis strategies, adsorption performance, modelling and regeneration
Authors: Duyen Thi Cam Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Magnesium ferrite (MgFe2O4)-based materials serve as effective agents for environmental remediation due to adjustable surface chemistry and inherent magnetic properties. This review evaluates synthesis protocols, structural characterization, and the adsorptive removal of diverse aqueous contaminants. Integrating MgFe2O4 into porous architectures, such as biochar, graphene oxide, zeolites, and metal–organic frameworks, expands the specific surface area up to 1300 m2 g−1. These composites exhibit high adsorption capacities across various categories of heavy metals, dyes, antibiotics, and other pollutants. Specific maximum uptake values reach 745.4 mg g−1 for Pb(ii), 1058.7 mg g−1 for Congo red dye, and 308.5 mg g−1 for doxycycline. The removal process operates via concurrent mechanisms, including electrostatic attraction, surface complexation, ion exchange, and π–π electron donor–acceptor interactions. Furthermore, the superparamagnetic nature of the composites allows rapid magnetic separation from aqueous phases. Reusability assessments confirm stable performance over multiple regeneration cycles using solvent or acid desorption. Consequently, these MgFe2O4-based composites offer efficient, sustainable, and recyclable options for industrial wastewater treatment.

Introduction

Industrial wastewater, agricultural runoff containing agrochemicals, and municipal wastewater continuously release complex and hazardous mixtures of contaminants into aquatic ecosystems. These discharges can include toxic heavy metals such as lead, cadmium, chromium, arsenic, and mercury, which persist in the environment and harm ecological health even at low concentrations.1,2 Persistent organic dyes, primarily originating from the textile and printing industries, represent another major pollutant group resistant to conventional degradation. The challenges of water contamination are further intensified by emerging contaminants.3–5 This category encompasses pharmaceuticals and personal care products entering aquatic systems post-consumption, various pesticides from modern agricultural practices, and excessive nutrients that trigger severe ecological issues such as eutrophication.6–8 The diverse chemical profiles of these pollutants necessitate robust and versatile water treatment methods.

Conventional wastewater treatment methods, including sedimentation, filtration, chemical coagulation, and aerobic or anaerobic biological digestion, provide a foundation for pollution control. However, these established routes face significant operational limitations, such as high energy consumption, incomplete removal of trace micropollutants, and the generation of large quantities of secondary toxic sludge requiring costly disposal.9–12 Advanced oxidation processes and membrane filtration technologies offer effective removal but entail high capital and operational expenditures.13–15 Consequently, adsorption technology has gained prominence as an advantageous alternative due to simple design, broad applicability, and high removal efficiency.16–18 The economic viability of adsorption processes depends on the regenerability of the adsorbent materials, minimizing solid waste generation and reducing long-term treatment costs.19–21

Spinel ferrites are of great interest as advanced adsorbent materials. Among these, magnesium ferrite (MgFe2O4) serves as a prominent candidate for environmental applications. Structurally, MgFe2O4 typically adopts an inverse spinel structure where O2− anions form a face-centered cubic lattice. In this configuration, approximately half of the trivalent iron ions (Fe3+) occupy the tetrahedral (A) sites, while the remaining Fe3+ and the divalent magnesium ions (Mg2+) are distributed among the octahedral (B) sites.22,23 This structure makes MgFe2O4 a soft magnetic n-type semiconductor. A critical benefit of the material is its tunable magnetic behavior; nanoparticles below a specific size threshold become superparamagnetic, characterized by zero coercivity and zero remanence.24,25 This allows the particles to freely disperse in water to maximize contact with pollutants and then be rapidly removed using an external magnet.25–27 In adsorption studies, MgFe2O4 is often compared to traditional magnetite (Fe3O4). While both materials allow for easy solid–liquid separation, they perform differently in practice.28 By contrast, Fe3O4 remains highly stable under harsh conditions.29,30 Furthermore, substituting iron atoms with lighter magnesium metal generates abundant defect active sites and surface hydroxyl groups, enhancing adsorption performance compared to pure Fe3O4.31 For instance, a MgFe2O4/γ-Fe2O3 architecture demonstrated enhanced de-chlorination performance compared to pristine Fe3O4.32 Crucially, MgFe2O4 is intrinsically non-toxic and biocompatible.28 Unlike alternative spinel ferrites containing manganese, zinc, cobalt, copper, or nickel, MgFe2O4 does not leach toxic heavy metals, making it a much safer choice for water treatment.33,34

Despite such advantages, pure MgFe2O4 nanoparticles have practical limitations. They tend to clump together due to magnetic interactions and have a relatively low specific surface area, typically between 30 m2 g−1 and 80 m2 g−1 when prepared using solvothermal or co-precipitation methods.30,35 To overcome these constraints and increase adsorption capacity, recent investigations have focused on developing composite structures. Combining MgFe2O4 with high-surface-area supports prevents particle aggregation and introduces new functional groups. Carbon-based materials are commonly used for this purpose; for instance, composites containing graphene oxide or reduced graphene oxide use their sheet-like structures to speed up the adsorption process.19,36,37 Similarly, biochar derived from agricultural waste provides a cost-effective and porous matrix. As a result, specific carbon composites, such as those derived from cactus, can achieve large surface areas of up to 692.32 m2 g−1.38

The structural modifications enable high pollutant uptake capacities.6,24 For example, copper-doped MgFe2O4 composite can adsorb 1039.10 mg g−1 of Congo red,8 while biogenic MgFe2O4 nanoparticles sequester 584.49 mg g−1 of malachite green.30 Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) provide high total surface areas and tunable pore sizes but often suffer from aqueous instability and separation difficulties.39–41 Pure MgFe2O4 ineffectively removed tetracycline (23%), but the integration with MIL-100(Fe) significantly improved removal efficiency (89%) of the composite.42 Combining magnetic spinels with zirconium-based materials creates composites with surface areas ranging from 136 m2 g−1 to 504 m2 g−1 while achieving 94% tetracycline removal.43 These composites maintain structural integrity during aqueous treatment and facilitate rapid post-treatment collection. Flexible fibers can uptake 283.13 mg g−1 of lead(ii),44 demonstrating that magnetic spinels are highly competitive with more complex framework structures. Two-dimensional transition metal carbides (MXenes) offer tunable interlayer spacing and abundant surface functional groups.45 Despite these structural benefits, some MXene composites remained have limited methylene blue capacities.39 Furthermore, unmodified two-dimensional carbides lack intrinsic magnetic recoverability, necessitating complex secondary functionalization steps.39 In contrast, MgFe2O4 nanoparticles can achieve high uptake capacities, e.g., up to 537.63 mg g−1 for methylene blue dye.24 Moreover, they inherently exhibit good magnetic properties, often offering saturation magnetizations up to 52.48 emu g−1 in biochar-based composites,46 and 5.10 emu g−1 in reduced graphene oxide-based networks.19 Regarding graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) hybrids, current literature primarily targets photocatalytic degradation and gas sensing applications rather than direct adsorption.23,47,48 Therefore, MgFe2O4 systems represent the feasible choice for high-performance and cost-effective environmental remediation solution.

This review summarizes recent literature on MgFe2O4-based adsorbents and analyzes their synthesis, structure, and properties. The primary objectives are to: (1) outline the main synthesis methods, from conventional co-precipitation and sol–gel methods to green synthesis using plant extracts, and evaluate their impact on crystallite size and morphology, (2) evaluate the physicochemical characterization of these materials, (3) assess adsorption performance across major pollutant classes and understand isotherm and kinetic models, (4) explain the mechanisms driving pollutant removal, including electrostatic interactions, surface complexation, ion exchange, and π–π stacking, and (5) examine the thermodynamic properties and reusability of these adsorbents. Ultimately, this review highlights the potential of MgFe2O4 composites as versatile, sustainable, and highly effective materials for water treatment.

Synthesis strategies for MgFe2O4-based adsorbents

The adsorption efficacy of MgFe2O4 materials in water remediation depends on physicochemical characteristics such as crystallite size, specific surface area (SBET), pore distribution, and magnetic saturation (Ms). The fabrication protocols employed during synthesis strictly define these properties. Current investigations focus on diverse synthetic routes for both pristine MgFe2O4 nanoparticles and composite architectures to achieve high pollutant uptake capacities, rapid kinetics, and structural stability. This section evaluates common and innovative synthesis strategies developed for these materials.

Chemical co-precipitation is a common method for synthesizing MgFe2O4 structures. The process involves the simultaneous precipitation of magnesium and iron cations as hydroxides in water, which is achieved by adding ammonia solution or strong alkaline agents such as sodium hydroxide or potassium hydroxide to the precursor mixture.46,51,59 Successful synthesis requires careful control of pH and temperature. Studies showed that hydroxides typically form at pH levels of 5, 9.2, or between 10 and 11, with heating at 60 °C, 70 °C, or 90 °C.46,49,59 Maintaining a 1 : 2 molar ratio of magnesium to iron is essential to form the correct spinel structure. Deviating from this ratio can produce unwanted iron oxide phases, such as maghemite.33 After the initial precipitation, the material is washed with deionized water to remove impurities and then calcined.49 The calcination step directly determines the final properties of the material. Increasing the calcination temperature from 300 °C to 800 °C increases crystallite size, causing grain diameters to grow from the nanoscale up to 100–200 nm.51,53 Higher temperatures also improve magnetic properties; for example, raising the temperature from 260 °C to 650 °C increases saturation magnetization from 2.2 emu g−1 to 6.7 emu g−1.50 Conversely, lower calcination temperatures (e.g., 80 °C) produce large agglomerates 3–10 µm but retain high specific surface areas (e.g., 179 m2 g−1), which favor physical adsorption over magnetic recoverability.51

To achieve better control over particle morphology, the sol–gel technique provides molecular-level dispersion and precise stoichiometric control.25,60Table 2 compares the advantages and disadvantages of these synthesis methods. While chemical co-precipitation is highly scalable and easy to adjust stoichiometrically, it often causes severe particle aggregation and requires high-temperature calcination.42,51,59 In contrast, the sol–gel method produces ultrafine, and uniform nanoparticles with minimal aggregation.21,24 A common sol–gel approach is the Pechini method, which uses α-hydroxycarboxylic acids (such as tartaric acid) to form metal chelates. Adding polyhydroxy alcohols then polymerizes the chelate, trapping the metal ions in a rigid matrix that prevents phase separation during heating.24 For example, using a 0.75 magnesium-to-iron molar ratio in the Pechini route followed by calcination at 700 °C produces pure phase spherical nanoparticles at approximately 36 nm with a saturation magnetization of 17.10 emu g−1.22 Another variation is the citrate–nitrate auto-combustion method, which combines metal nitrates with citric acid to trigger a rapid exothermic reaction. The fast release of gases during combustion creates a highly porous, foam-like structure with a large surface area. Processing the citrate–gel mixture at 300 °C produces 12 nm crystallites with a specific surface area of 113 m2 g−1.51 However, increasing the calcination temperature to 800 °C increases the crystallite size to 49.7 nm and drastically reduces the surface area to 10 m2 g−1. Despite generating high-quality microstructures, sol–gel methods are time-consuming and highly sensitive to calcination temperatures.61,62

Comparison of synthetic methods for MgFe2O4 nanoparticles

Hydrothermal and solvothermal methods avoid multi-step gelation by heating precursor fluids in sealed autoclaves under high pressure. This one-step method produces uniform, highly porous structures, although the need for specialized high-pressure equipment limits continuous industrial manufacturing.8,35 For example, using ethylene glycol with structure-directing agents such as sodium acetate and polyethylene glycol at 200 °C fabricates hierarchical microspheres ranging from 200 to 250 nm. These microspheres are composed of 10 nm subunits and have a surface area of 57 m2 g−1 with a saturation magnetization of 53.0 emu g−1.35Fig. 2 illustrates a hydrothermal synthesis pathway where urea is used to first form cubic magnesium–iron layered double hydroxides. These structures act as sacrificial templates. Calcining this intermediate template at 600 °C causes a structural transformation, converting it directly into cubic MgFe2O4 particles ranging from 200 to 500 nm. This process retains the cubic shape of the original template while creating a hollow interior, resulting in a final material with a surface area of 41.7 m2 g−1 and a saturation magnetization of 26.01 emu g−1.56

To reduce the high energy and chemical demands of traditional methods, green synthesis uses plant extracts as an environmentally friendly alternative. This approach eliminates hazardous chemicals, although the natural variability of plant compounds can make it difficult to standardize the process and ensure consistent material properties.30,63 In this method, plant-derived compounds act as capping and stabilizing agents during nanoparticle formation. For example, using gerbera floral waste extracts produced spherical MgFe2O4 particles with a surface area of 85.0 m2 g−1. These particles adsorbed malachite green and outperformed chemically synthesized equivalents.30 In another method, combustion synthesis harnesses rapid exothermic reactions to fabricate highly crystalline materials. Although the combustion process is energy efficient and rapid, this process poses safety risks and often leads to unpredictable particle aggregation.58 To improve material properties, combustion techniques often combine with ionic doping to modify the crystal structure. For instance, incorporating aluminum cations into the MgFe2O4 structure via gel-combustion increased both the total surface area (60–63.4 m2 g−1) and internal pore size (7.1–7.4 nm).compared to those (52.1 m2 g−1 and 6.9 nm) of pure MgFe2O4.58

Regardless of the synthesis method, operational conditions determine the properties and adsorption performance of MgFe2O4-based adsorbents. Specifically, calcination temperature controls the crystallinity and structure of the nanoparticles. Higher calcination temperatures generally improve saturation magnetization by increasing crystallite size and reducing magnetic disorder at the particle surface.33,49,50 However, excessive heating can collapse the porous structure and remove essential surface hydroxyl groups. For instance, MgFe2O4 prepared using glycine–nitrate combustion exhibited a manganese capture capacity of 1.56 mmol g−1 after 300 °C treatment.51 Calcining the material at 800 °C reduced this capacity to 0.38 mmol g−1 due to a significant loss of surface area. Consequently, identifying the optimal calcination temperature is essential.

Synthesis of MgFe2O4-based composites

MgFe2O4 nanoparticles are promising adsorbents for wastewater treatment due to their non-toxicity, biocompatibility, and efficient magnetic separation resulting from their superparamagnetic properties.19,46,50,64 However, a common limitation is their tendency to aggregate. This aggregation reduces their effective specific surface area and lowers overall adsorption performance.7,33,61 To address this issue, researchers have focused on developing MgFe2O4-based composites. Combining the magnetic nanoparticles with supporting materials improves structural stability and pollutant removal efficiency. Furthermore, the chosen synthesis method determines the structure and properties of the resulting composite.31 While pristine MgFe2O4 has good adsorptive and magnetic properties, its practical performance is significantly improved through composite formation and surface functionalization. These strategies increase the available surface area, introduce functional groups to improve pollutant binding, and enhance chemical stability. The main strategies for fabricating MgFe2O4-based composites.

Chemical co-precipitation methods

Chemical co-precipitation is a simple and scalable synthesis method that involves combining metal salt solutions, adding a precipitating agent, and then calcining the material.49–51,59 This method allows MgFe2O4 to be directly embedded into various support materials during its formation. For example, MgFe2O4/biochar composites can be prepared by co-precipitating iron and magnesium ions onto marine macroalgae waste, followed by pyrolysis. This process embedded the nanoparticles within the biochar, producing a material with a saturation magnetization of 52.48 emu g−1 that was highly effective for phosphate removal.46 Similarly, synthesizing MgFe2O4-doped biochar beads using a combined co-precipitation, sol–gel, and carbonization method overcomes the limitations of the individual components while improving the adsorption of antimony anions.28 In another application, magnetic polymer beads have been created by dispersing precipitated MgFe2O4 particles within an alginate matrix designed for strontium recovery.75

Advanced composites include MgFe2O4–biochar lanthanum alginate beads, which are prepared by co-precipitation onto a reed precursor followed by the in situ cross-linking of lanthanum alginate. This structure improves mass transfer and provides high phosphate adsorption capacity.31 Similarly, combining magnesium sulfate and ferric chloride with biochar and ammonia produces MgFe2O4/rice husk biochar nanocomposites. This method improved nanoparticle dispersion and increases the specific surface area to 151.32 m2 g−1.69 Metal–organic framework (MOF) composites, such as MIL-100(Fe)/MgFe2O4, could also be synthesized using co-precipitation followed by calcination at 900 °C, which yields a high specific surface area of 1325.73 m2 g−1.42 Despite the scalability and versatility of co-precipitation, particle aggregation remains a challenge if the support material does not stabilize the magnetic nanoparticles effectively.51 Without proper stabilization, the MgFe2O4 particles can clump into large agglomerates 3 to 10 µm in diameter. The final material morphology depends heavily on parameters such as metal ion concentration and pH. Additionally, the high calcination temperatures required for some of these methods limit the use of temperature-sensitive support materials.42,51

Sol–gel processing methods

Sol–gel techniques involve forming a colloidal solution from metal precursors, followed by gelation, controlled drying, and calcination. This method ensures a uniform distribution of components within the composite.25,52,67 For example, preparing a MgFe2O4/biochar composite via an in situ sol–gel pyrolysis process using banana pseudo-stem powder resulted in uniformly dispersed nanoparticles, which improved tetracycline degradation.36 Sol–gel pathways are also used for direct surface functionalization. Amine-functionalized materials can be synthesized and then immediately attached to ethanolamine without calcination; this approach prevents particle aggregation, minimizes particle size, and enables the chemisorption of heavy metals.61

Furthermore, sol–gel methods are commonly used to create graphene oxide nanocomposites. Combining the synthesized nanoparticles with graphene oxide sheets using ultrasonication produces composites with specific surface areas between 63.04 and 165.29 m2 g−1.66 A ternary composite of magnesium ferrite, titanium dioxide, and graphene oxide showed both high adsorption and effective photodegradation of methylene blue.67 Additionally, the resulting nanospheres can support the deposition of zirconium-based metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) via solvothermal methods, yielding specific surface areas up to 856 m2 g−1 for amine-functionalized ones.43,62 Coating the nanoparticles with polyethylene glycol (PEG) also improves particle dispersion, reducing large agglomerates to approximately 1 µm.22 Although these methods provide excellent structural integration, their multi-step nature requires significant processing time, and final material properties remain highly sensitive to the calcination temperature.24,43

Hydrothermal and solvothermal methods

Hydrothermal and solvothermal methods involve heating precursor solutions in sealed autoclaves under high pressure, allowing for controlled in situ crystallization and direct composite formation.21,33,35 These conditions offer precise control over the final morphology and the development of hierarchical structures. For example, using Opuntia stricta cactus biochar produced a MgFe2O4/C composite with a high specific surface area of 692.32 m2 g−1 and a methyl orange adsorption capacity of 158.12 mg g−1.38

Template-assisted hydrothermal processes can also create distinct material structures. For instance, using pine pollen as a biological template yields hierarchically porous MgFe2O4/γ-Fe2O3 magnetic microspheres that effectively remove both dyes and pharmaceuticals simultaneously.32 Another approach uses polystyrene microspheres that self-assemble into an ordered colloidal crystal matrix.29 Indeed, aqueous precursor solutions containing magnesium and iron cations were infiltrated into the voids of this sacrificial template (Fig. 4). Subsequent drying and high-temperature calcination removed the polystyrene template by combustion while simultaneously inducing metal oxide crystallization. This process yielded a three-dimensionally ordered macroporous MgO/MgFe2O4 structure. These composites possessed a specific surface area of 108 m2 g−1 and a saturation magnetization of 5.21 emu g−1, and their high internal porosity makes them effective for adsorbing large ions such as aqueous lanthanum and cerium.

Similarly, layered double hydroxides serve as sacrificial templates in urea-assisted hydrothermal processes to produce cubic magnetic MgFe2O4 that retains the shape of the template.56 Hydrothermal techniques can also repurpose industrial waste streams; for example, raw electroplating sludge has been used in a one-step synthesis of layered CuFe2O4/MgFe2O4 composites. The resulting material showed a high Congo red adsorption capacity of 1039.1 mg g−1.8 One-pot solvothermal reactions using ethylene glycol or polyethylene glycol can produce highly mesoporous MgFe2O4 and MgFe2O4–graphene oxide composites. These materials have significantly higher adsorption capacities than their unmodified counterparts.21,36 Solvothermal synthesis can also encapsulate the magnetic cores within porous MOFs. Growing networks such as MIL-100(Fe) or UiO-66(Zr) around pre-formed nanoparticles increased internal porosity and lowered the electronic bandgap energy to a range of 2.28 to 2.60 eV.43,72 Furthermore, incorporating microwave heating accelerated the synthesis, completing the reaction in just 30 min at 120 °C.62 However, the requirement for specialized high-pressure equipment and the batch nature of these reactions remain the primary limitations preventing large-scale, continuous industrial manufacturing.35

Combustion synthesis methods

Combustion synthesis is a rapid and energy-efficient method that uses exothermic reactions between metal nitrates and organic fuels to produce fine, uniform powders.58,64 Because these reactions are self-propagating, they reduce the need for external heating during fabrication. For example, gel-combustion has been used to synthesize MgAlxFe2−xO4 spinels with mesoporous structures and a specific surface area of 70.6 m2 g−1.58 Supporting materials can be integrated either during or immediately after the combustion phase. In one case, a magnetic biosorbent was prepared by incorporating combustion-derived nanoparticles into a chitosan–CaAl polymeric gel matrix via epichlorohydrin refluxing at 60 °C. This integration increased the fluoride adsorption capacity from 32.25 mg g−1 to 263.15 mg g−1.7 Similarly, MgFe2O4 was loaded onto prickly pear biochar by heating metal chlorides mixed with biomass at 400 to 600 °C under a continuous nitrogen flow.70 Although combustion synthesis is fast and economical, its highly exothermic nature poses operational safety risks. Furthermore, controlling the final morphology and particle shape of the composite often requires the use of additional templates or the addition of a post-treatment calcination step.58

Green synthesis methods

Green synthesis is an environmentally friendly approach that uses plant extracts as reducing, capping, and stabilizing agents to replace toxic chemicals.30 This method relies on inexpensive, renewable resources and improves the biocompatibility of the final product. For example, Tecoma stans floral extract has been used as both a solvent and a chelating agent to synthesize a MgFe2O4/ZnO nanocomposite.63 This material functions as both an adsorbent and a photocatalyst, showing higher removal efficiency and better reusability than unmodified nanoparticles. Although green synthesis is promising for sustainable materials engineering, the complex and variable chemical composition of natural extracts makes it difficult to precisely control synthesis parameters. This variability presents ongoing challenges for maintaining consistent material properties and product quality.30

Ex situ assembly and electrospinning methods

While in situ methods integrate components simultaneously, ex situ methods involve preparing individual components separately before physical assembly. This approach helps minimize nanoparticle aggregation through controlled forces, with ultrasonication being the most common dispersion technique. For example, combining MgFe2O4 with reduced graphene oxide involves electrostatic self-assembly, which couples oppositely charged nanomaterials during ultrasonication. In this process, rod-like magnetic nanoparticles deposit uniformly on the wrinkled edges of the reduced graphene oxide sheets, yielding a superparamagnetic composite with high stability and reusability over multiple cycles.19 A similar ultrasonication method anchors magnetic particles onto faujasite zeolite surfaces, producing a nanocomposite with a high specific surface area of 400 m2 g−1 that removes over 94% of aqueous cobalt and manganese ions.64 Furthermore, pH-dependent, ultrasonic-assisted assembly can be used to coat magnetic nanospheres with thin graphene oxide sheets via electrostatic interaction.57Ex situ methods are also used to graft chelating ligands onto carbon-based supports. For instance, preparing a hydroxyl-functionalized magnetic rice straw biochar composite involves the solvothermal treatment of sonicated biochar and metal oxide components; the resulting material retains over 82% of its capacity to adsorb lead and cadmium after five cycles.74

Surface chemical modification is essential for targeting specific heavy metal contaminants. For example, synthesizing amine-functionalized magnetic biochar involves a multi-step process that starts with the carboxylation of rice husk biochar using nitric acid to increase its surface reactivity (Fig. 5). Amine-functionalized MgFe2O4 nanoparticles are then covalently linked to the biochar using EDC–NHS coupling, which binds the amine groups of the ferrite to the carboxyl sites on the biochar. This modification creates a material optimized for the selective adsorption of cadmium and lead ions.60

Electrospinning is an effective method for fabricating flexible, self-supporting fibrous adsorbents. Dissolving metal nitrates and citric acid within polymer matrices produces highly hydrophilic magnetic fibers. Because of their high aspect ratio, these fibers resist aggregation in water, maximizing the exposure of active sites for lead adsorption. Although adjusting synthesis parameters allows researchers to tune the saturation magnetization for easier magnetic separation, precise formulation is critical. Inadequate chemical complexation can cause severe thermal embrittlement during heat treatment. Additionally, the scalability of this method is limited by the need for specialized equipment.44

An evaluation of the various synthesis strategies for MgFe2O4 composites reveals clear trade-offs among structural control, production scalability, and operational demands. Chemical co-precipitation offers a simple and scalable method for embedding magnetic cores directly into support materials, e.g., biochar or metal–organic frameworks.42,46 However, this approach often leads to severe nanoparticle aggregation and restricts the use of temperature-sensitive components due to the high calcination temperatures required.51 Conversely, sol–gel processing ensures a uniform, homogeneous distribution of components and allows for direct surface functionalization.24,61 Nonetheless, these multi-step procedures require long processing times and remain highly sensitive to thermal conditions. Similarly, hydrothermal and solvothermal reactions offer precise control over morphology, making it easier to integrate sacrificial templates to achieve high specific surface areas.29,38 Despite these structural advantages, the reliance on specialized high-pressure autoclaves limits continuous, large-scale industrial manufacturing.35

For rapid production, combustion synthesis minimizes external energy consumption through fast, self-sustaining exothermic reactions.58 However, these highly energetic reactions present safety hazards and often require additional templating or post-treatment to control particle morphology. Meanwhile, green synthesis methods using plant extracts provide sustainable and biocompatible options, though variations in the chemical composition of the extracts can compromise product consistency.30 Finally, ex situ assembly and electrospinning techniques minimize particle aggregation to produce flexible, self-supporting materials.19,44 However, achieving this precision requires specialized instrumentation, and improper formulation of complexing agents can make the resulting materials brittle.

Physicochemical characterization of synthesized adsorbents

Thorough characterization is essential to verify successful material synthesis and evaluate its quality. Similarly, Fig. 6C illustrates how XRD tracks phase evolution during pyrolysis in biochar composites, confirming that the magnetic phase remains stable within the carbon matrix.28,29

The magnetic properties of the materials are shown in the VSM hysteresis loops in Fig. 6B and D. The sigmoidal, S-shaped curves exhibit near-zero magnetic remanence and coercivity, indicating superparamagnetic behavior. This property is crucial for environmental adsorbents because it ensures good dispersion in water during treatment and allows rapid collection using an external magnetic field. Although encapsulating MgFe2O4 within non-magnetic phases (such as carbon, polymers, or MgO) reduces the saturation magnetization, the remaining magnetic susceptibility is still enough to allow rapid solid–liquid separation.26,34

In addition to phase identification, XRD data can be used to calculate average crystallite size, as higher calcination temperatures produce sharper diffraction peaks that indicate increased crystallinity.25,59 Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy is used to identify surface functional groups and chemical bonds. The spinel structure of MgFe2O4 typically exhibits two primary absorption bands between 400 and 450 cm−1 and 530–600 cm−1, which correspond to metal–oxygen stretching vibrations at octahedral and tetrahedral sites, respectively.24,60 Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) reveal surface morphology, particle shapes, and internal nanoscale features. While pure nanoparticles often display spherical or irregular shapes that aggregate due to high surface energy, composite materials show these magnetic particles distributed throughout the support matrix.24,36,38,53,63 Finally, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) confirms the elemental composition, while X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) clarifies surface chemical states and binding energy shifts caused by pollutant interactions, helping to identify specific adsorption mechanisms such as ion exchange.21,24,53

Brunauer–Emmett–Teller specific surface area (SBET) analysis is essential for evaluating adsorption performance. Studies show a positive correlation between larger surface areas and higher adsorption capacities.38,46,71 High surface areas provide more active sites, while well-developed pore networks facilitate pollutant diffusion into the internal matrix.6,35 Combining magnetic cores with porous materials such as MOFs or biochar yields composites with high surface areas, such as a MIL-100(Fe) nanocomposite with a surface area of up to 1325.73 m2 g−1.42 However, normalizing adsorption capacities by surface area provides a more accurate measure of active site density. It was revealed that materials used for cerium removal achieve capacities of up to 49.33 mg m−2,29 whereas the MIL-100(Fe) framework exhibits a tetracycline removal capacity of only 0.0248 mg m−2.42 These differences indicate that efficiency per unit area depends on the pollutant type, composite composition, and specific chemical interactions rather than total surface area alone.

Adsorption performance in wastewater treatment

Removal of heavy metals

Contamination of water resources by heavy metals presents public health risks due to the non-biodegradability and bioaccumulation of these elements. As summarized in Table 4, MgFe2O4-based adsorbents effectively immobilize both cationic species, such as Pb(ii), Cd(ii), Cu(ii), and Co(ii), and anionic metalloids, predominantly Cr(vi) and arsenic species (As(iii)/As(v)). Lead(ii) [Pb(ii)] represents a primary target for MgFe2O4 remediation systems. Unmodified nanoparticles can exhibit stable performance, achieving up to 99.9% removal efficiencies at pH 9.25 Integrating the magnetic phase into composite materials significantly enhances adsorption capacities. For instance, flexible electrospun magnetic fibers yielded a maximum adsorption capacity of 283.13 mg g−1.44 Furthermore, embedding the magnetic spinel within swine manure biochar yields a capacity of 242.72 mg g−1, where chemical precipitation into Pb5(PO4)3OH acts as the dominant immobilization pathway.26 Surface functionalization also increases adsorption performance. Apart from lead, these composites demonstrate high affinities for other toxic transition metals. For instance, the amine-functionalized biochar matrix yielded an adsorption capacity of 226.76 mg g−1 for cadmium(ii), while retaining 82% of its initial capacity after five continuous regeneration cycles.74 The same adsorbent also exhibits strong ionic adsorption competition characteristics when deployed directly in real smelting wastewater, maintaining 93.01% cadmium removal despite the presence of other competing ions.

For copper(ii) and cobalt(ii) adsorption, integrating the magnetic phase into porous supports significantly enhances performance. A nanocomposite combining MgFe2O4 with faujasite zeolite at a 3 : 1 weight ratio removed 94.5% of cobalt and 94.9% of manganese(ii) from 10 mg L−1 solutions, while retaining over 65% of its initial activity after three operational cycles.64 Unmodified, co-precipitated nanoparticles were also effective for cobalt adsorption, achieving a capacity of 67.41 mg g−1 through a pseudo-second-order chemisorption pathway.55 Remediating anionic metalloids such as chromium and arsenic requires precise pH control to ensure that the adsorbent surface maintains a positive charge for electrostatic attraction. For hexavalent chromium, pure nanoparticles showed optimal performance in highly acidic environments, achieving a 64.63% removal efficiency at pH 1 before declining as the solution became alkaline.25 Supporting the ferrite phase on rice husk biochar stabilizes this performance. For example, the resulting composite removed up to 96.87% of chromium ions at elevated temperatures, maintaining electrostatic attraction as long as the solution pH remains below the pHpzc of 4.1.69

Arsenic contamination presents distinct operational challenges due to varying oxidation states. MgFe2O4 fabricated via one-step solvothermal processing provides precise stoichiometric control, achieving maximum arsenic(iii) capture capacities of 103.94 mg g−1 and 45.52 mg g−1, respectively, when maintaining a strict 33 : 67 magnesium-to-iron atomic ratio.33 Non-stoichiometric ratios induce defective maghemite formation, which compromises overall adsorption efficiency. Furthermore, embedding the active magnetic phase within mesoporous ordered silica foam generates a stable interfacial environment capable of removing both arsenic valence states at a neutral pH of 7 through simultaneous complexation and electrostatic attraction.73 Although pure nanoparticles are functional, they are generally less effective than modified composites and surface-functionalized materials. Combining the magnetic core with porous biochar matrices expands the specific surface area. For instance, fabricating specialized MgFe2O4-doped biochar beads amplifies Sb(OH)6− adsorption capacity by a factor of 40 compared directly against unmodified biochar or isolated powdered spinel counterparts.28 Polymeric encapsulation delivers similar advantages; integrating pristine nanoparticles into a chitosan matrix raises fluoride adsorption from 32.25 mg g−1 to 181.81 mg g−1, while subsequent calcium–aluminum oxide modification increases the sequestration capacity to 263.15 mg g−1.7 Ultimately, leveraging these hierarchical supporting frameworks transforms moderate adsorption characteristics into effective environmental remediation technologies.

The removal mechanism for ionic dyes is predominantly electrostatic. Consequently, the relationship between solution pH and pHpzc represents the primary factor governing overall adsorption efficiency.24,30,36,56 Cationic dyes, such as methylene blue (MB) and rhodamine B (RhB), undergo effective removal under alkaline conditions where pH > pHpzc. Under these conditions, the deprotonated adsorbent surface exerts a strong attractive force on the cationic dye chromophores. For example, MgFe2O4 nanoparticles synthesized via the Pechini method exhibited a maximum MB removal capacity of 537.63 mg g−1 at pH 8.24 Similarly, a green-synthesized biogenic MgFe2O4 demonstrated an optimal malachite green (MG) removal capacity of 584.49 mg g−1 at pH 7.30

Conversely, removing anionic dyes such as methyl orange (MO) and Congo red (CR) requires acidic conditions (pH < pHpzc) to induce surface protonation. For example, a cubic MgFe2O4 derived from layered double hydroxides (LDHs) achieved a capacity of 535 mg g−1 for methyl orange at pH 3.5.56 The importance of surface charge tuning is further exemplified by the performance of MgAl0.4Fe1.6O4, which showed a maximum MO uptake capacity of 274.6 mg g−1 at pH 5.58

While pure ferrites perform adequately, composite materials deliver higher adsorption capacities due to complementary material interactions. For instance, a mixed metal oxide composite CuFe2O4/MgFe2O4 synthesized from electroplating sludge yielded an adsorption capacity of 1039.1 mg g−1 for Congo red. This performance was attributed to well-developed mesoporous and microporous structures, abundant active sites, and the combined effects of physical adsorption and chemisorption, involving surface complexation and hydrogen bonding between the CuFe2O4/MgFe2O4 matrix and Congo red molecules.8 Carbon-based composites also performed effectively; for example, a MgFe2O4/carbon composite derived from Opuntia stricta biochar achieved capacities of 158.12 mg g−1 for MO and 90.08 mg g−1 for RhB.38 Metal–organic framework composites, such as MgFe2O4/MIL-100(Fe), leverage the high porosity of the MOF shell to reach an adsorption capacity of 219.78 mg g−1 for RhB, with equilibrium attained in under 5 min.72

The adsorption behavior of antibiotics depends heavily on the presence of multiple ionizable groups, such as amine, hydroxyl, and sulfonamide functional groups. This molecular structure results in distinct chemical speciation (cationic, zwitterionic, or anionic forms) across the pH scale.6,42 Maximum adsorption typically occurs at the specific pH where electrostatic attraction between the antibiotic species and the adsorbent surface is optimized. For instance, tetracycline (TC) adsorption onto a MIL-100(Fe)/MgFe2O4 composite reaches its peak at a neutral pH of 7, matching the point where the zwitterionic form of tetracycline dominates.42 In contrast, sulfamethoxazole removal by a MgFe2O4-magnetic biochar (MBC300) operates with maximum efficiency at pH 3, whereas tetracycline removal on the same carbonaceous matrix is favored at pH 6.6 This divergence demonstrates the necessity of controlling the solution pH during aqueous treatment processes.

Developing composite materials is the primary focus of current literature regarding antibiotic remediation. For example, an eggshell membrane-templated MgFe2O4 material (MFO-0.5-700) achieved an adsorption capacity of 308.51 mg g−1 for doxycycline, higher than the capacities of unfunctionalized ones.53 Biochar-supported composites, such as MBC300, achieved capacities of 50.75 mg g−1 for SMX and 120.36 mg g−1 for TC.6 The integration of MgFe2O4 with MOFs offers high performance. Removal of other pollutants

Beyond heavy metals, dyes, and antibiotics, MgFe2O4-based materials are effective for removing agrochemicals and inorganic anions from aqueous solutions.7,19,24 For the organophosphate pesticide chlorpyrifos (CPF), a mesoporous MgFe2O4 (M-MGF, SBET = 170 m2 g−1) adsorbent synthesized using the solvothermal method (ethylene glycol as a solvent) achieved a Langmuir maximum adsorption capacity of up to 4201.68 mg g−1 at pH 7. This high uptake value demonstrates that well-developed mesoporosity is essential for capturing large organic pesticide molecules.21

The removal of fluoride (F−) and phosphate (PO43−) typically proceeds via ligand exchange mechanisms. For these inorganic anions, acidic pH conditions favor adsorption by protonating the functional groups on the adsorbent surface. Phosphate removal by lanthanum-doped MgFe2O4 beads was optimal within a pH range of 3–5, yielding an adsorption capacity of 53.82 mg g−1.31 This was explained by the fact that the embedded lanthanum ions enhanced affinity through the formation of stable lanthanum-phosphate complexes. Similarly, magnetic biochar composites (MFB-MCs) achieved a Sips adsorption capacity of 487.99 mg g−1 for phosphate, driven by the surface precipitation of magnesium phosphates.46 For fluoride removal, a MgO–MgFe2O4/GO composite reached an adsorption capacity of 34.03 mg g−1 at pH 6, mediated by the chemical affinity between fluoride ions and the magnesium oxide phase.71

Practical application in real wastewater matrices

Transitioning laboratory-scale adsorbents into practical environmental remediation requires evaluation against complex wastewater matrices to assess material resilience in the presence of competing ions. For example, mesoporous magnesium ferrite composites were applied to treat real agricultural runoff collected from sugarcane fields containing commercial insecticides. The material achieved a 91% chlorpyrifos removal efficiency over a 24-hour contact period while simultaneously decolorizing the treated effluent.21 Industrial metal-smelting operations generate effluents with complex matrices that test material durability under harsh conditions. Evaluation of a KEA-MgFe2O4–OH@RSBC framework using raw wastewater collected from a smelting facility in Zhejiang Province demonstrated low sensitivity to matrix interference. The magnetic composite removed 94.26% of lead, 93.01% of cadmium, and 69.65% of co-existing copper from the effluent. Dynamic flow-through column experiments validated the scalability of the process, maintaining stable adsorption efficiencies of 97.28% for lead and 97.01% for cadmium across a continuous 1080-min operation.74

In addition to industrial streams, assessments have focused on using these composites for low-concentration municipal nutrient recovery. Using synthetic sewage prepared to mimic municipal wastewater compositions, a lanthanum-doped composite, designated MgFe2O4-BM-La(b), processed approximately 1205 bed volumes and achieved a total phosphate capture value of 20.24 mgP L−1.31 The granular adsorbent maintained stable removal performance across three continuous regeneration cycles. Across these diverse matrices, rapid magnetic separation and reusability facilitate the translation of magnesium ferrite materials from laboratory-scale studies to industrial wastewater treatment applications.19,28,74

A common observation across the evaluated studies is the widespread adherence to the pseudo-second-order (PSO) kinetic model. This model consistently provides the highest coefficients of determination (R2) for a diverse array of pollutants, including heavy metals, cationic dyes, and complex antibiotic molecules. For instance, the adsorption of Pb(ii) onto hierarchical MgFe2O4 microspheres (R2 > 0.99),35 MB onto pristine MgFe2O4 nanoparticles (R2 = 0.9922),24 and doxycycline onto eggshell-membrane templated MgFe2O4 (R2 = 0.9998)53 is well described by the PSO equation. This agreement implies that chemisorption serves as the primary rate-limiting step, driven by valence forces through electron sharing or exchange between adsorbate molecules and active surface sites, such as hydroxyl or amine groups.21,24,25 Furthermore, calculated equilibrium adsorption capacities (qe) exhibit close agreement with experimentally determined measurements.25,36,67 In the case of As(iii) removal by MgFe2O4 (MF0.33), the experimental qe of 0.3752 mg g−1 was nearly identical to the calculated qe of 0.3846 mg g−1 (R2 = 0.999), validating the predictive accuracy of the applied model.33

While the PSO model is widely applicable, alternative models better describe specific systems where surface heterogeneity plays a significant role. The Elovich model, which assumes that the activation energy of adsorption increases with surface coverage, provides a strong fit for TC adsorption onto MgFe2O4–biochar (MBC300) (R2 = 0.9584),6 and for antimony (Sb(v)) removal by Fe/(MgFeO-BC) beads (R2 = 0.9754).28 These findings suggest that for complex composite surfaces, adsorption occurs on energetically heterogeneous sites rather than on a uniform monolayer. Additionally, mechanistic insights from intraparticle diffusion plots (qtvs. t0.5) typically reveal multilinear profiles. This multilinearity indicates that the adsorption process proceeds in stages: rapid surface diffusion followed by slower intraparticle diffusion into the mesopores and micropores. Consequently, while chemisorption drives the reaction kinetics, diffusive transport through the boundary layer and internal pore network also influences the overall rate.25,44

Adsorption isotherms

Adsorption isotherms quantify the equilibrium relationship between the adsorbate concentration in the fluid phase and its concentration on the solid phase at a constant temperature. These models are critical for determining the maximum adsorption capacity (qm) and defining the surface properties of the adsorbent.19,50,75 The analysis of equilibrium data for MgFe2O4-based systems primarily utilizes the Langmuir and Freundlich models, with the Sips and Temkin models applied in specific instances.

The Langmuir model often fits the adsorption of heavy metals and dyes, implying monolayer coverage on a surface composed of energetically equivalent sites with no lateral interaction between adsorbed molecules.24,25 High correlation coefficients for the Langmuir isotherm were reported for Pb(ii) on amine-functionalized biochar (MgFe2O4–NH2@sRHB) (R2 = 0.9856),60 and for MB on MgFe2O4 nanoparticles (R2 = 0.9999).24 In these systems, the adsorption isotherms plateau at high equilibrium concentrations, allowing for the reliable calculation of saturation capacities (qm), such as the 1058.66 mg g−1 capacity for CR on mixed-metal ferrites.8 Strong Langmuir fits therefore correlate mechanistically with pure or uniformly functionalized nanoparticles undergoing specific site binding, which is frequently driven by chemisorption.61

Conversely, the Freundlich empirical model describes multilayer accumulation occurring across highly heterogeneous surfaces characterized by an exponential distribution of localized binding energies.49,71 The practical applicability of this framework aligns with deploying multi-component composites that possess diverse surface chemistries, including varied carboxyl, hydroxyl, and graphitic functional groups.73 Current investigations document a dominant Freundlich behavior during the adsorption of complex organic pollutants and various antibiotic compounds. For instance, the adsorption of levofloxacin on pomelo peel biochar composites (BMF-700) (R2 = 0.99)34 and arsenic species on mesoporous silica-ferrite composites (MF0.33-MOSF) (R2 > 0.99)73 followed the Freundlich model. This trend indicates that adsorption occurs on sites with a wide distribution of binding energies, driven by the varied functional groups present on the composite surfaces. The Freundlich heterogeneity factor (1/n) typically lies between 0 and 1, confirming that these adsorption processes are favorable.25,28

Moreover, three-parameter models also provide great mathematical accuracy. The Sips model, a hybrid of Langmuir and Freundlich, best described the adsorption of In(iii) (R2 = 0.9877) and Ga(iii) (R2 = 0.9897) onto MgFe2O4.49,50 This model accounts for surface saturation at high concentrations while modeling heterogeneity at lower concentration ranges. Similarly, the Temkin model, which accounts for adsorbent–adsorbate interactions, describes tetracycline adsorption onto MOF composites (MIL-100(Fe)/MgFe2O4) (R2 = 0.984), implying a linear decrease in the heat of adsorption with surface coverage.42

Universally, the reported ΔG° values are negative across the tested temperature ranges, confirming that the adsorption of pollutants onto MgFe2O4-based materials is a spontaneous process. For example, ΔG° values for methylene blue adsorption on MgFe2O4/rGO ranged from −10.16 to −14.14 kJ mol−1 as temperature increased from 293 K to 308 K, indicating that the driving force for adsorption increases with temperature.19 Similar spontaneous behavior was observed for chlorpyrifos removal by mesoporous ferrites.21

The enthalpy change (ΔH°) distinguishes the nature of the adsorption process. Endothermic processes (ΔH° > 0), where adsorption capacity increases with temperature, are common. High positive enthalpy values were reported for MB on MgFe2O4/rGO (ΔH° = 44.32 kJ mol−1)19 and for Pb(ii) on amine-functionalized biochar (ΔH° = 35.24 kJ mol−1).60 The magnitude of these values, particularly those exceeding 40 kJ mol−1, often points towards chemisorption mechanisms involving strong coordinate bonding. Conversely, exothermic processes (ΔH° < 0) were observed for systems such as TC on MIL-100(Fe)/MgFe2O4 (ΔH° = −13.17 kJ mol−1)42 and MG on biogenic MgFe2O4 (ΔH° = −15.28 kJ mol−1).30 For these exothermic systems, lower temperatures favor pollutant removal, consistent with physisorption or weak chemical bonding.

The entropy change (ΔS°) provides insight into the disorder at the solid–liquid interface. Positive ΔS° values are predominant, as seen for Pb(ii) adsorption on flexible fibers (136.2 J mol−1 K−1),44 and doxycycline on ferrite (126.8 J mol−1 K−1).53 This increase in entropy is attributed to the displacement of ordered water molecules from the hydration shells of the metal ions and the adsorbent surface, leading to a net increase in system randomness. However, negative ΔS° values do occur, such as for chlorpyrifos adsorption (ΔS° = −28.89 J mol−1 K−1), suggesting an associative mechanism where the adsorption results in a more ordered state of the adsorbate on the surface.21

Regeneration and reusability

The practical use of adsorbents in industrial wastewater treatment depends on economic viability and operational sustainability. A critical factor governing these aspects is the capacity for efficient regeneration and the retention of high adsorption capacities over multiple reuse cycles25,38,39 Developing recyclable adsorbents reduces operational costs by minimizing the consumption of fresh materials and lowers the environmental burdens associated with disposing of spent solids.21,42 For MgFe2O4-based composites, the inherent magnetic susceptibility provides a distinct operational advantage. This magnetic property facilitates rapid and energy-efficient solid–liquid separation using external magnetic fields, avoiding the limitations of conventional filtration or centrifugation, particularly for nanoscale particulate systems.21,26 As summarized in Table 9, previous studies have evaluated the recyclability of these materials by testing different desorption strategies and the structural stability of the adsorbents over multiple cycles. Selecting an effective regeneration protocol depends on the chemical structure of the adsorbate and the specific binding mechanisms involved. Common regeneration agents include organic solvents, acidic solutions, and alkaline solutions.

For organic pollutants such as dyes and antibiotics, which are often bound through weaker physical forces (hydrogen bonding and π–π stacking) or hydrophobic interactions, solvent desorption is often sufficient for material restoration.34,42 Washing with mild organic solvents, such as ethanol or methanol, regenerates the adsorbents by solubilizing the organic adsorbate. For instance, ethanol washing effectively regenerated MgFe2O4/rGO nanocomposites saturated with MB,19 and biogenic MgFe2O4 loaded with MG.30 Similarly, methanol desorbed levofloxacin from a pomelo peel biochar composite (BMF-700), recovering the active surface sites.34

In contrast, the desorption of heavy metals often requires ion-exchange mechanisms or pH-induced surface charge reversal.25,65 Acidic eluents, particularly dilute hydrochloric acid (HCl), serve as the standard for removing cationic metals. The introduction of hydronium ions (H3O+) leads to protonated surface of the adsorbent that displaces the positively charged metal ions. This mechanism was demonstrated in an ion-imprinted polymer (MgFe2O4-starch-IIP), where 0.5 M HCl effectively desorbed Cu(ii) ions.65 Comparable acid-leaching protocols using 0.1 M HCl were effective for regenerating MgFe2O4 microspheres loaded with Pb(ii),35 and amine-functionalized biochar saturated with Cd(ii) and Pb(ii).60

Conversely, alkaline solutions such as sodium hydroxide (NaOH) are necessary for the desorption of anionic pollutants. High pH conditions induce the deprotonation of the adsorbent surface, creating a negatively charged interface that repels anionic species. This strategy effectively released chromate (Cr(vi)) from MgFe2O4/activated carbon composites,52 and fluoride ions from MgO–MgFe2O4/GO.71 Furthermore, specific studies dealing with amphoteric or multi-ion systems employ sequential treatments. For example, the regeneration of MgFe2O4 utilized for indium (In(iii)) and gallium (Ga(iii)) recovery involved a two-step wash with 1 M HCl followed by 0.1 M NaOH to achieve complete surface restoration.49,50

The reusability of MgFe2O4-based adsorbents is typically evaluated over three to five consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles (Table 9). These magnetic materials generally retain a high proportion of the initial adsorption capacity, although a gradual decline in performance is observed across successive runs. Pure MgFe2O4 nanoparticles demonstrate notable stability, retaining over 88% of the initial methylene blue removal efficiency after 5 cycles.24 Composite materials also display high durability. Lanthanum-doped biochar beads (MgFe2O4-BM-La(b)) retained approximately 75% of their initial phosphate capacity after the fifth cycle,31 while mesoporous MgFe2O4 (M-MGF) retained over 90% efficacy for chlorpyrifos removal.21

However, the reduction in adsorption capacity over repeated cycles remains an operational challenge. For example, the removal efficiency of CR by a mixed-metal oxide composite (CuFe2O4/MgFe2O4) decreased from 96.8% in the first cycle to 82.0% in the fifth.8 Similarly, the capacity of electrospun flexible fibers (MFOF-8) for Pb(ii) dropped from 283.13 mg g−1 to 241.23 mg g−1, representing a capacity retention of 85.2%.44 Several factors contribute to this attenuation. First, pollutants may undergo irreversible chemisorption within deep micropores, leading to pore blocking and a decreased available surface area.25,30 Second, chemical attack by strong acids or bases during regeneration can cause the irreversible alteration or loss of crucial surface functional groups.74 Finally, physical loss of adsorbent mass during magnetic separation and washing steps contributes to the observed efficiency decline.21

Despite these minor losses, MgFe2O4-based adsorbents possess sufficient chemical and mechanical stability for multi-cycle environmental applications. The majority of published studies reported efficiency retention rates higher than 80% after 5 cycles, validating the reusability of these magnetic materials. Future optimization of regeneration protocols should enhance the life cycle and economic appeal of these materials for water purification.

Limitations, future prospects and conclusions

Despite the verified advantages, critical technical gaps remain before industrial deployment is feasible. A primary challenge involves long-term structural and chemical stability under extreme environmental conditions. Highly acidic or saline industrial effluents can trigger the dissolution of the magnetic core over extended operational periods, undermining structural integrity under harsh conditions. While laboratory-scale synthesis is well documented, translating complex hybridization protocols into cost-effective, large-scale manufacturing remains a significant engineering bottleneck. The long-term environmental impact and potential nanotoxicity associated with the release of secondary nanoscale fragments during prolonged cyclic use require rigorous ecotoxicological assessment before widespread environmental release.

To overcome current limitations, future research should focus on advanced material engineering and predictive modeling. Developing multi-functional hybrid systems stands as a primary objective. These systems should combine adsorption with concurrent catalytic degradation pathways to enable complete mineralization rather than simple physical capture of targeted pollutants. Incorporating machine-learning-assisted optimization can dramatically accelerate adsorbent design. Using data-driven computational models to predict structural properties and binding affinities may help researchers optimize surface chemistry and pore networks specifically for multi-component wastewater systems.

MgFe2O4-based composites can serve as versatile platforms for treating wastewater treatment challenges. These magnetic composites effectively sequester a wide range of contaminants, including heavy metals such as lead(ii) and cadmium(ii), alongside persistent organic pollutants such as synthetic dyes and antibiotics. While pure nanoparticles provide a functional foundation, the incorporation with carbon-based networks or porous frameworks increases remediation performance. Furthermore, the inherent superparamagnetic properties of these materials ensure rapid recovery from treated effluents, avoiding traditional filtration limitations and minimizing secondary waste generation. Recyclability trials confirm that these adsorbents can retain 80% to 90% of their initial removal efficiency across many consecutive cycles. Thus, MgFe2O4-based composites can be potential adsorbents in practical treatment.
