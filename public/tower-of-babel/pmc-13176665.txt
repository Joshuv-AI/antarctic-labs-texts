Impact of Hormonal and Radiation Therapy on Bladder Outlet Obstruction in High‐Risk Localized Prostate Cancer: A Prospective Pressure‐Flow Study
Authors: Yuki Kyoda, Masakazu Hori, Mitsuhiro Nakanishi, Tetsuya Shindo, Kohei Hashimoto, Ko Kobayashi, Toshiaki Tanaka, Naoya Masumori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeThere are few reports investigating urodynamic changes after radiotherapy and hormonal therapy (HT) for prostate cancer. We prospectively evaluated differences in bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) using pressure‐flow studies (PFS) before and after HT and radiotherapy in patients with high‐risk localized or locally advanced prostate cancer.MethodsThis single‐center prospective observational study enrolled 20 consecutive men scheduled for HT followed by intensity‐modulated radiotherapy. PFS, uroflowmetry, symptom questionnaire surveys, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were performed at baseline, 6 months, and 24 months after initiation of HT. Radiotherapy was initiated after the 6‐month evaluation. The primary endpoint was the change in the bladder outlet obstruction index (BOOI), defined as the difference between baseline and 24 months after initiation of HT.ResultsAll patients completed the 24‐month protocol. Mean BOOI decreased from 44.3 ± 23.4 at baseline to 26.8 ± 25.0 at 6 months (p < 0.01) and remained significantly reduced at 33.2 ± 29.1 at 24 months (p < 0.05). Prostate volume was reduced significantly by 43% at 6 months (p < 0.01) and by 41% at 24 months (p < 0.01). MRI demonstrated resolution of malignant lesions by 6 months in all patients, with no recurrence at 24 months. Three patients (15%) exhibited a BOOI reduction > 30, and all three had periurethral tumors prior to treatment.ConclusionThis first prospective PFS‐based study demonstrates that combined HT and radiotherapy improve BOO, particularly during HT, likely through reduction of prostate and tumor volume. Patients with periurethral tumors may achieve greater improvement in BOO. These findings underscore the importance of considering treatment‐induced BOO changes in therapeutic decision‐making.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier: UMIN‐CTR; UMIN000040312

Introduction

Prostate cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer among men in almost two‐thirds of the world's countries [1]. Regarding definitive treatment options for localized prostate cancer, no significant differences in oncological outcomes have been reported between radical prostatectomy and radiotherapy [2]. Therefore, treatment planning should be based on a thorough discussion with the patient, including perioperative and postoperative complications. In practice, however, current guidelines provide no clear criteria for choosing between surgery and radiotherapy.

Because treatments for the prostate often cause substantial changes in voiding function, we consider it important to base treatment decisions in part on the extent and nature of these functional changes. In our previously reported retrospective study, we found that patients younger than 75 years, with a quality‐of‐life (QOL) score of less than 3 and with ≥ 50% positive biopsy cores were able to discontinue medications for lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) after receiving radiotherapy, suggesting an improvement in lower urinary tract function [3]. Among these findings, we focused on the observation that patients with ≥ 50% positive cores, indicating a larger intraprostatic tumor volume, might experience improvement in bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) after radiotherapy. Based on this observation, we hypothesized that radiotherapy may relieve BOO in patients with high‐risk localized or locally advanced prostate cancer, which are more likely to involve larger tumor volumes. Accordingly, we conducted a prospective study to investigate whether BOO improves after radiotherapy in such patients.

Pressure flow study (PFS) is a highly valuable tool that enables detailed evaluation of both storage and voiding functions, including the assessment of BOO [4]. However, its use is limited to specialized facilities because of its invasive nature, requiring catheter insertion, as well as the time‐consuming nature of the examination, which places a burden on both patients and examiners [5]. Consequently, only a few studies have reported PFS findings following radiotherapy for prostate cancer [6]. In the present study, we prospectively assessed the impact of radiotherapy on BOO using PFS in a single‐center setting.

Materials and Methods

Design

We conducted a single‐center, prospective observational study at a university hospital. The protocol was approved by the hospital's Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 312‐217) and conformed to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Further details are available on the university's website. All participants provided written informed consent after receiving a full explanation of the study protocol, with assurances of confidentiality. The study is registered with the UMIN Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN‐CTR; UMIN000040312).

Patients

Twenty consecutive men were prospectively enrolled from June 2020 through February 2022. Eligible patients were aged ≥ 50 years and scheduled to undergo radiotherapy combined with hormonal therapy (HT) for high‐risk localized or locally advanced prostate cancer. Risk classification followed the 2019 NCCN Guidelines in effect at study initiation [7]. High‐risk localized disease was defined as clinical stage T3a, Grade Group 4–5, or PSA > 20 ng/mL. Locally advanced disease corresponded to the “very high‐risk” category, defined as clinical stage T3b–T4, primary Gleason pattern 5, or > 4 biopsy cores with Grade Group 4–5. Key exclusion criteria were prior prostate, bladder, or urethral surgery and neurologic disease.

Hormonal Therapy Protocol

All patients received definitive radiotherapy plus HT for high‐risk localized or locally advanced prostate cancer. HT began at enrollment and continued for 24 months. Combined androgen blockade (CAB), surgical or medical castration plus a nonsteroidal antiandrogen, was administered during the first 6 months. Thereafter, the antiandrogen was discontinued and castration monotherapy was maintained through month 24. Management beyond 24 months was at the treating physician's discretion. Medical castration was achieved with a luteinizing hormone–releasing hormone (LHRH) agonist (leuprorelin or goserelin) or an LHRH antagonist (degarelix). Antiandrogens included bicalutamide and flutamide.

Radiotherapy Protocol

All patients received intensity‐modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) using 10 MV photon energy delivered by linear accelerators. Radiotherapy commenced approximately 6 months after the initiation of androgen deprivation therapy (ADT). Moderately hypofractionated or conventional fractionated radiotherapy was administered with a total dose of 70 Gy in 28 fractions (2.5 Gy per fraction) or 78 Gy in 39 fractions (2.0 Gy per fraction), respectively. Patients were treated 5 days per week over a period of 5.5 to 8 consecutive weeks. For patients requiring strict antithrombotic therapy, conventional fractionated radiotherapy was administered with a total dose of 72 Gy in 36 fractions.

Computed tomography (CT) simulation for radiotherapy planning was conducted with the patient in the supine position using a 2‐mm slice thickness, covering the entire pelvis to the penile urethra. All patients underwent CT scanning with an empty rectum and a moderately full bladder. The gross tumor volume (GTV) included the entire prostate, tumor‐infiltrated regions, and the proximal 2 cm of the seminal vesicles; in cases of seminal vesicle invasion, the entire seminal vesicle was included. The clinical target volume (CTV) was defined as the GTV plus a 3‐mm margin, excluding any overlap with organs at risk (OARs). The planning target volume (PTV) was generated by adding an 8‐mm margin to the CTV in all directions, except posteriorly where a 5‐mm margin was applied. OARs included the bladder, rectum, small intestine, large intestine (excluding the rectum), and femoral heads. All organs were contoured as solid structures.

The dose was prescribed to D50 of the PTV (the dose covering 50% of the PTV). No more than 2% of the PTV received over 105% of the prescribed dose, and at least D95 (the dose covering 95% of the PTV) was required to receive 95% of the prescribed dose. Daily image‐guided radiotherapy (IGRT) was performed with target matching to the prostate. Prophylactic irradiation of the pelvic lymphatic region was not performed.

Urological Evaluation

Before treatment, patients underwent symptom assessment using the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) and the Overactive Bladder Symptom Score (OABSS). The total prostate volume and transition zone volume for each patient were automatically measured with a deep learning–based artificial intelligence algorithm using the analysis workstation SYNAPSE VINCENT version 6.8 (FUJIFILM Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) and tumor volume was measured using the automatic segmentation function of the Tumor Tracking application in IntelliSpace Portal version 12.1 (Philips Medical Systems Nederland B.V.) on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) images. All measurements were performed by a single radiological technologist in a blinded manner.

Baseline free uroflowmetry (UFM) and PFS were also performed. PFS followed International Continence Society standards [8]. A 6‐Fr single‐lumen urethral catheter was placed for cystometry and saline infusion, and a second 6‐Fr catheter was inserted rectally to measure abdominal pressure. Studies were conducted with the patient seated, and saline was infused at 50 mL/min. Urological medications were adjusted according to individual symptoms. If LUTS persisted or newly developed after treatment, options included additional pharmacotherapy and, when BOO was identified as the cause, surgical intervention. Assessments (questionnaires, MRI, UFM, and PFS) were repeated 6 months after the initiation of HT, before the start of radiotherapy. At 12 months, questionnaires and UFM were obtained. At 24 months after the initiation of HT, all baseline assessments (questionnaires, MRI, UFM, and PFS) were repeated.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was the change in the BOO index (BOOI), defined as BOOI = PdetQmax − 2 × Qmax. Secondary endpoints were changes in the IPSS, OABSS, MRI‐derived measures, uroflowmetry (UFM) indices, and PFS parameters excluding BOOI. Based on the pretreatment PFS, patients were stratified using a BOOI cutoff of 40 into a BOO group and a No‐BOO group in subanalysis.

Statistical Analysis

Paired comparisons between baseline and post‐treatment values were performed using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. Between‐group comparisons of baseline characteristics were made with the Mann–Whitney U test or the chi‐square test, as appropriate. Two‐sided p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan).

During the 24 months after treatment initiation, no patient showed clear disease progression or required additional oncologic therapy. One patient experienced a PSA rise to 3.76 ng/mL. MRI showed no local recurrence, and diffusion‐weighted whole‐body imaging with background body signal suppression (DWIBS) revealed no distant metastases. In the absence of detectable metastasis, the patient was diagnosed with nonmetastatic castration‐resistant prostate cancer (nmCRPC) and started enzalutamide at 27 months from therapy initiation, which was beyond the observation period of this study. Although all patients had PI‐RADS 5 lesions on pretreatment MRI, these suspicious findings had disappeared by 6 months after treatment initiation and remained absent at the 24‐month evaluation.

There were no changes in LUTS medications between baseline and 6 months (i.e., before radiotherapy) in any patient. Medication changes occurred only after completion of radiotherapy. Comparing baseline with 24 months, 3 patients continued medication, 2 discontinued α‐blockers, 1 switched from an α‐blocker to a β3‐agonist, 1 initiated a β3‐agonist, and 13 required no medication. Temporary adjustments made during radiotherapy were excluded from this analysis.

Discussion

This study is a unique prospective investigation evaluating changes in BOO using PFS before and after combined HT and radiotherapy in patients with high‐risk localized or locally advanced prostate cancer. Overall, BOO improved significantly following combined HT and radiotherapy, with the improvement largely attributable to HT. However, among patients with a baseline BOOI ≥ 40, no significant improvement was observed and substantial interindividual variability was noted. To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has performed PFS both before and after HT. This is the first report to evaluate such changes using urodynamic assessment.

Two mechanisms may account for the observed improvement in BOO. First, treatment‐related prostate volume reduction likely contributed. A previous study showed that dutasteride‐induced prostate shrinkage was accompanied by a corresponding decrease in BOOI; specifically, a 28% reduction in prostate volume was associated with a 23% decrease in BOOI [9]. In the present cohort, a similar effect was observed: prostate volume decreased by approximately 41%, accompanied by a 25% reduction in BOOI, consistent with that report. Second, relief of cancer‐related obstruction is plausible. Prostate cancer lesions themselves may contribute to BOO, and treatment may alleviate obstruction by reducing the tumor burden. Notably, the three cases showing marked BOOI improvement had periurethral tumors abutting the urethra on pretreatment MRI (Figure 2). Our previous retrospective study likewise suggested that patients with a high proportion of positive biopsy cores, presumably reflecting larger tumor volume, experienced improved voiding function after treatment [3]. Additionally, several reports have described urinary retention due to local tumor progression that resolved after radiotherapy [10].

Taken together, these findings indicate that substantial improvement in BOO occurred before the initiation of radiotherapy, most likely as a result of HT‐induced reductions in prostate and tumor volume. Prior studies also suggest that HT can improve lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) via tumor debulking [11, 12]. In contrast, we observed no additional improvement in BOO after radiotherapy commenced, suggesting that radiotherapy per se may have little direct effect on relieving BOO once ADT‐related shrinkage has occurred. Nonetheless, we cannot exclude the possibility that radiotherapy alone could improve BOO in patients with relatively large tumor volumes if delivered prior to tumor reduction (i.e., without preceding ADT). In summary, when ADT and radiotherapy are considered as a single therapeutic strategy, the combined approach appears to contribute to BOO alleviation.

Chang et al. reported that, 2 years after external beam radiotherapy, LUTS had improved in 25% and worsened in 24% of patients [13]. However, the characteristics and risk factors underlying LUTS improvement versus deterioration after radiotherapy remain unclear. Improvement may reflect relief of BOO as discussed above, whereas worsening may partly derive from radiotherapy‐related adverse effects on the prostate and bladder. Radiotherapy induces inflammation and fibrosis in prostatic stromal tissue [14], and periurethral fibrosis can exacerbate BOO [15]. In our study, BOO tended to worsen after radiotherapy, although the change did not reach statistical significance, suggesting that any adverse impact was limited. Consistent with our findings, Do et al., in one of the few studies to perform PFS before and after radiotherapy, concluded that BOO remained essentially unchanged [6]. In clinical practice, radical prostatectomy is often considered advantageous in patients with concomitant BOO because it directly removes the obstructing prostate tissue. However, surgery carries a substantial risk of stress urinary incontinence and other functional complications. In contrast, radiation‐based therapy has traditionally not been regarded as effective for relieving BOO. The present findings suggest that combined hormonal therapy and radiotherapy can significantly reduce BOO, particularly during the hormonal therapy phase and especially in patients with periurethral tumors. These results indicate that radiation‐based therapy may not necessarily be inferior to surgery in terms of BOO relief in selected patients. Accordingly, our findings may provide additional information for shared decision‐making when selecting definitive treatment in patients with high‐risk prostate cancer and concomitant BOO.

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size (n = 20) was a major constraint. As a pilot study requiring three invasive PFS assessments per patient, enrollment was necessarily limited, which may have affected the robustness of the findings. Second, treatment comprised two modalities, hormonal therapy and radiotherapy. The majority of BOOI improvement occurred during the initial 6 months of hormonal therapy, and therefore the independent contribution of radiotherapy cannot be determined. However, in patients with high‐risk localized or locally advanced prostate cancer, combined androgen deprivation therapy and radiotherapy represents the current standard of care, and radiotherapy alone is generally not recommended. Thus, the present findings reflect real‐world outcomes under guideline‐concordant treatment conditions, which may be directly applicable to contemporary clinical practice. Third, despite overall relief of BOO, symptom scores (e.g., the total IPSS and QOL) did not improve. This may reflect the relatively mild baseline symptoms (mean baseline IPSS total score 10.3) and the lack of sustained BOOI improvement in the BOO subgroup, who likely had more severe symptoms. Fourth, in several cases with BOO following radiotherapy, it remains unclear whether the increase in the BOOI occurred during the early or late post‐radiation period. Because urodynamic assessments were not performed at intermediate time points (e.g., 12 and 18 months), the temporal pattern of BOO progression after radiotherapy could not be fully characterized. This lack of intermediate data represents an additional limitation of the present study. Finally, given the small sample size and single‐arm design, the present study should be considered hypothesis‐generating. Larger studies, ideally including comparative treatment cohorts, are warranted to further clarify the independent effects of each treatment modality and to refine patient selection. Future well‐designed, large‐scale randomized controlled trials are warranted to address these issues. In conclusion, combined hormonal therapy and radiotherapy were associated with a significant reduction in BOOI in patients with high‐risk localized or locally advanced prostate cancer, particularly during the hormonal therapy phase. Patients with tumors abutting the urethra appeared to derive greater improvement in BOO. Although the independent contribution of each treatment modality requires further investigation, these findings suggest that radiation‐based therapy combined with hormonal therapy may alleviate or stabilize bladder outlet obstruction while preserving urinary continence. This information may contribute to shared decision‐making when selecting definitive treatment in patients with concomitant BOO.
