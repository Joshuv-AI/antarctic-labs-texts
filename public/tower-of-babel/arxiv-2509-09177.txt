Clip Your Sequences Fairly: Enforcing Length Fairness for Sequence - Level RL
Hanyi Mao, Quanjia Xiao, Lei Pang, Haixiao Liu

Abstract
We propose FSPO (Fair Sequence Policy Optimization), a sequence-level reinforcement learning method for LLMs that enforces length-fair clipping on the importance-sampling (IS) weight. We study RL methods with sequence-level IS and identify a mismatch when PPO/GRPO-style clipping is transplanted to sequences: a fixed clip range systematically reweights short vs. long responses, distorting the optimization direction. FSPO introduces a simple remedy: we clip the sequence log-IS ratio with a band that scales as L \sqrt{L} . Theoretically, we formalize length fairness via a Length Reweighting Error (LRE) and prove that small LRE yields a cosine directional guarantee between the clipped and true updates. Empirically, FSPO flattens clip rates across length bins, stabilizes training, and outperforms all baselines across multiple evaluation datasets on Qwen3-8B-Base model.



1
Introduction

Recent progress on reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models (LLMs) has been catalyzed by GRPO
[
20
]
and the broader RLVR
[
25
]
paradigm, where rule-based, verifiable rewards are assigned to the entire response rather than token-wise signals. This framing proved effective for improving mathematical reasoning and other verifiable tasks. However, the optimization procedures used in current RLVR systems largely inherited token-level machinery from PPO-like methods
[
18
]
, including the use of token-level importance-sampling (IS) ratios and token-level clipping. Meanwhile, subsequent works began to emphasize that, once rewards are sequence-level, it is more faithful to operate with sequence-level IS so as to match the reward granularity
[
2
,
29
,
16
]
.

Despite the shift toward sequence-level IS, the theoretical distinctions and practical consequences of clipping in the sequence-level IS setting remain under-explored. Existing sequence-level IS methods
[
2
,
29
,
16
]
transplant the clipping mechanism from token-level methods directly: they exert a
fixed
clip range on the probability ratio of the whole sequence. We argue that directly using fixed clip ranges at sequence level is problematic: the dispersion of sequence-level log ratios scale with response length
L
.

This paper studies sequence-level clipping through the lens of
length fairness
. We formalize a simple criterion:
acceptance rates should be approximately constant across response lengths
. We show that fixed sequence-level clipping violates this criterion and can distort the training target. To address this, we propose
FSPO
(Fair Sequence Policy Optimization). FSPO keeps the IS semantics intact and restores length fairness by using a
\sqrt{L}
-scaled acceptance band on the sequence log-ratio, which equalizes acceptance across lengths.

To ground our analysis, we evaluate
FSPO
on sequence-level RL for mathematical reasoning. We compare against two sequence-level baselines: (i)
RLOO
with sequence-level IS and a fixed clip on the full-sequence ratio, and (ii)
GSPO
sequence-level clipping with ratio normalization. We report Avg@32 on
AMC23
,
MATH500
,
AIME24
, and
AIME25
, alongside diagnostic panels that measure acceptance-by-length to verify length fairness. Across two base model scales, we observe flatter acceptance across length bins, more stable training dynamics, and improved task scores; full results and ablations are presented in §
6
.

We introduce the backgrounds of RL of LLMs and RLVR in Appendix
A
, and provide a detailed and interesting justification for sequence-level IS weight in Appendix
B
.

2
Length Fairness and Length Reweighting Error (LRE)

Following the notation in Appendix
A
, we define the log importance ratio

S(y_{i}\mid x_{i})=\log s_{i}(\theta)=\log\left(P^{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{i}\mid x_{i})\right)-\log\left(P^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}(y_{i}\mid x_{i})\right)

Let
L=\mathrm{len}(y)
and fix a length-indexed band
b_{L}>0
.
Our acceptance event (not being clipped) is defined as

\mathcal{A}_{L}\;=\;\big\{y\bigm||S(y\mid s)|\leq b_{L}\big\}.

We define the acceptance ratio
q(L)\;=\;P^{\pi_{\theta}}(\mathcal{A}_{L}\mid L)
, and define

g_{L}^{\star}\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\,\nabla_{\theta}\log P_{\pi_{\theta}}(y\mid x)A(x,y)\mid L\,],\qquad g_{L}^{b}\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\,\nabla_{\theta}\log P_{\pi_{\theta}}(y\mid x)A(x,y)\mid\mathcal{A}_{L},\,L\,],

so that the true target and its clipped surrogate are

g^{\star}\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{L}[\,g_{L}^{\star}\,],\qquad g^{b}\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{L}[\,q(L)\,g_{L}^{b}\,].

To measure
length fairness
we use:

Definition 2.1

(Length Reweighting Error (LRE))
.

Let
\bar{q}=\mathbb{E}[q(L)]
. Define

\mathrm{LRE}=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\frac{q(L)}{\bar{q}}-1\right|\right]

Small LRE means acceptance rate is nearly constant across lengths.

Let
\kappa=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\|g_{L}^{\star}\|]}{\|g^{\star}\|}\geq 1
, which captures dispersion of per-length signal.

Now we provide two assumptions.

Assumption 2.1

(Bounded Stratification)
.

There exists
\eta\in[0,1)
such that for all
L
,

\|\,g_{L}^{b}-g_{L}^{\star}\,\|\ \leq\ \eta\,\|g_{L}^{\star}\|.

This assumption implies that clipping does not distort the target too much for every
L
(targets are not stratified severely by clipping).

Assumption 2.2

(Bounded co-variation)
.

The residual co-variation between
|q(L)-\bar{q}|
and
\|g_{L}^{\star}\|
is mild so that

\mathbb{E}\!\big[\,|q(L)-\bar{q}|\,\|g_{L}^{\star}\|\,\big]\ \leq\ \gamma\mathbb{E}\!\big[|q(L)-\bar{q}|\big]\ \mathbb{E}\!\big[\|g_{L}^{\star}\|\big].

This assumption is optional as discussed in Appendix
C
.

The following theorem motivates the practice to enforce length-fairness in clipping mechanism.

Theorem 2.1

(Directional guarantee under length fairness)
.

Under
2.1
,
2.2

\cos\angle\!\big(g^{b},g^{\star}\big)\ \geq\ \frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho},\qquad\rho\ \leq\ \kappa\Big(\eta+2\gamma(1+\eta)\,\mathrm{LRE}\Big).

It implies that smaller LRE provides a larger lower bound for the cosine similarity between the clipped gradient and true gradient. The proof of this Theorem and more discussion on its possible impact can be found in Appendix
C
.

3
Distribution of Sequence-Level Log Ratio

In this section we study the distribution of the sequence-level log importance–sampling (IS) ratio in order to provide evidence and practical guidance for designing procedures that achieve the
length fairness
criterion introduced earlier.

We view decoding under
\pi_{\theta}
with a limited context window
K
as a finite-state Markov chain on
V^{K}
, where
V
is the vocabulary; this reduction for autoregressive language models is discussed in detail by
[
28
]
. Under randomized sampling with nonzero temperature, the chain is irreducible and aperiodic. Therefore, the Markov CLT for additive functionals
[
6
,
12
]
applies to

S_{L}=\sum_{t\leq L}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid h_{t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{t}\mid h_{t})},

which yields the following theorem:

Theorem 3.1

(Gaussian of sequence-level log ratio)
.

The sequence log-IS ratio admits an asymptotically Gaussian law:

\frac{S_{L}-\mu_{L}}{\sqrt{L}}\ \Rightarrow\ \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),\qquad\mu_{L}=\Theta(L),\ \ \sigma^{2}>0.

Figure

1
(left) illustrates the empirical distribution of the sequence-level log IS ratio. The dataset includes all steps across the full training run. Consistent with the theorem, the empirical standard deviation of
S_{L}
grows approximately
\propto\sqrt{L}
. The observed estimator for
\sigma
is
\hat{\sigma}=0.0304
.

To further assess normality, we compute the standardized statistic

\hat{Z}\;=\;\frac{S_{L}-\hat{\mu}_{L}}{\sqrt{L}\,\hat{\sigma}},

where
\hat{\mu}_{L}
is computed within each length bin and
\hat{\sigma}
is estimated from all values of
(S_{L}-\hat{\mu}_{L})/\sqrt{L}
. The Q-Q plot is shown in
Figure

1
(right). The fitted line coincides with the ideal
y=x
reference, indicating an excellent Gaussian fit; the empirical distribution exhibits heavier tails, but within
\pm 2
standard deviations it is very close to normal.

\hat{\mu}_{L}
exhibits slightly larger fluctuations at larger lengths but remains small relative to
\hat{\sigma}
; thus we empirically observe
\hat{\mu}\approx 0
.

Theoretical clip-fraction patterns of RLOO, GSPO.

According to the previous analysis, we compute the theoretical clip fraction pattern for RLOO and GSPO. By
Theorem

3.1
, the sequence log–IS sum satisfies (approximately)

S_{L}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{L},\sigma^{2}L)
.
For a symmetric two–sided clip with a fixed clip-range
\forall L(b_{L}=\xi>0)
in log space,

\displaystyle\textbf{RLOO:}\quad q^{\mathrm{RLOO}}(L)

\displaystyle=\Pr\!\big(|S_{L}|>\xi\big)=2\,\Phi\!\left(-\,\frac{\xi-\mu_{L}}{\sigma\sqrt{L}}\right)\;\approx\;2\,\Phi\!\left(-\,\frac{\xi}{\sigma\sqrt{L}}\right),

(1)

\displaystyle\textbf{GSPO:}\quad q^{\mathrm{GSPO}}(L)

\displaystyle=\Pr\!\big(|S_{L}|>\xi L\big)=2\,\Phi\!\left(-\,\frac{\xi L-\mu_{L}}{\sigma\sqrt{L}}\right)\;\approx\;2\,\Phi\!\left(-\,\frac{\xi\sqrt{L}}{\sigma}\right),

(2)

where
\Phi(\cdot)
is the standard normal CDF, and the approximations use the
empirically small drift
\mu_{L}\approx 0
(see §
3
).

According to the above, both RLOO and GSPO induce clip probabilities that are biased by length.
To obtain a
constant
(length–independent) clip probability,
FSPO
sets

b_{L}=\mu_{L}+z\,\sigma\,\sqrt{L}
.
With the same calculation as in (
1
)–(
2
), we obtain

q^{\mathrm{FSPO}}(L)\;\approx\;2\,\Phi(-z),

which is independent of
L
and thus preserves the length fairness required by Theorem
2.1
.
Moreover, as shown in
Figure

1
(right), choosing the clipping hyperparameter
z<2
keeps us in a regime where the normal approximation is highly accurate and mitigates heavy-tail effects.

We plot the theoretical clip–probability curves in
Figure

2
, where we also display the empirically observed clip fractions for comparison, demonstrating close agreement with the theory.

4
Method: FSPO

For each prompt
x\sim\mathcal{D}
we sample
G
completions

\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim P^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}(\cdot\mid x)
and optimize the surrogate objective

\mathcal{J}_{\text{FSPO}}(\theta)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_{i}\}}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\min\!\Big\{\exp\!\big(S_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid x)\big)\,\widehat{A}_{i}\ ,\ \exp\!\big(\mathrm{clip}\big(S_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid x),-b_{L_{i}},\,b_{L_{i}}\big)\big)\,\widehat{A}_{i}\Big\}\right],

(3)

where
\widehat{A}_{i}
is an advantage estimate and
\mathrm{clip}(s,\ell,u)=\min\{\max\{s,\ell\},u\}
.
The log-IS ratio is

S_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid x)\;=\;\log\frac{P^{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{i}\mid x)}{P^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}(y_{i}\mid x)}\;=\;\sum_{t=1}^{L_{i}}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid h_{i,t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid h_{i,t})},

(4)

with
L_{i}=\mathrm{len}(y_{i})
and
h_{i,t}=(x,y_{i,<t})
the prefix at step
t
.
The (symmetric) clip band is

b_{L}\;=\;\underbrace{\hat{\mu}\,L}_{\text{drift}}\;+\;\underbrace{z\,\hat{\sigma}\,\sqrt{L}}_{\text{scale}},

(5)

so that clipping is performed in
log
-space by truncating
S_{\theta}
to
[-b_{L},b_{L}]
before exponentiation.

Drift term.

Following the discussion in
Section

3
, FSPO sets
\hat{\mu}=0
.
Empirically, this simple setting induces a flattened clipping fraction across length bins and leads to improved performance. Nevertheless, ne can relate
\mu_{L}
to the KL divergences between the two policies:

\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[S_{L}]\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{L}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid h_{t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{t}\mid h_{t})}\right]\;=\;\sum_{t=1}^{L}\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h_{t})\,\|\,\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid h_{t})\bigr).

(6)

A more refined treatment of the drift potentially leveraging this connection to the per-context KL divergence as in
Equation

6
) is left for future work.

Scale term.

In
b_{L}
, the parameter
z
controls the target clip fraction, while
\hat{\sigma}
can be tracked by a running estimator computed from recent batches.
In practice, we set
c\coloneqq z\hat{\sigma}
and treat
c
as a single tunable hyperparameter.
A natural extension is to use asymmetric scales
c_{\mathrm{upper}}
and
c_{\mathrm{lower}}
, allowing separate control of the upper and lower clip ranges as in
Yu et al. 2025
.
Current RL implementations commonly include
dual-clip

[
26
]
, which effectively clips the ratio at
(1+\epsilon_{\text{dual}})
when
A<0
; in our experiments, we also implement dual-clip in log-space with a
\sqrt{L}
-scaled range and tune
c_{\text{dual}}
.
For implementation details and guidance on hyperparameter tuning, see
Section

D.2
.

Compatibility with other components.

FSPO is a lightweight, plug-in modification that affects only the importance-ratio term in the policy loss.
In our implementation, to validate correctness and ease ablations, we modify only this term and keep the rest of the pipeline identical to the baseline (e.g., using the GRPO-style advantage).
FSPO is compatible with improvements to other components, such as alternative advantage estimators (e.g.,
\hat{A}^{\text{LOO}}

[
8
,
10
]
), data filtering
[
27
]
, and overlength penalties
[
27
]
, among others.

6.3
Effectiveness: learning dynamics and length stability

As shown in Figure
3
, both RLOO and FSPO learn quickly and
increase response length in early training. However, RLOO’s response length
later explodes to very large values, with much of the extra content being
filler. A plausible explanation is that longer sequences are more likely to be
clipped under RLOO; consequently, negative signals from long incorrect answers
are suppressed, and the model fails to learn to regulate length. Moreover, as
responses grow longer in later
stages, RLOO’s higher clip probability hampers learning and reward improvements become plateau, whereas FSPO exhibits steady gains.
On the other hand, GSPO learns more slowly at the beginning and struggles to increase
length, especially for the weaker 1.7B model. On the 8B model, FSPO attains the
best performance with moderate average length, suggesting more balanced
learning across lengths and more effective use of length.

Overlong rate.

To further assess FSPO’s effectiveness on downstream tasks, we report the

overlong rate
and the mean response length after excluding overlong
samples. The overlong rate is the proportion of samples that reach the
maximum response length and are truncated. FSPO exhibits a markedly lower
overlong rate, indicating stable control of response length. In contrast,
methods with incorrect sequence-level importance weights (GRPO and GSPO;
see Appendix
A
) show substantially higher overlong rates,
while their mean lengths after excluding overlong samples are slightly lower,
suggesting poor allocation of length: responses tend to be either too long or
too short, leading to suboptimal behavior.

Ablation: larger clip range

As shown in
Figure

, RLOO’s clip fraction is comparatively large,
partly because its clip range is set relatively small (Appendix

).
Note that in FSPO the

clip range for a sequence with

is
\exp(\sqrt{10000}\times 0.03)=20.09
, which is much larger
than the

used in RLOO. A natural hypothesis is that FSPO’s gains
stem from being more permissive on long sequences due to a larger clip range.
To disentangle this, we evaluate RLOO with a
fixed

(upper
=20
, lower
=0.95
). As shown in
Figure

, this variant
does not improve performance. The results are comparable to or even worse than
standard RLOO. This indicates that
length fairness
, rather than mere
leniency toward long responses, is the key factor behind FSPO’s effectiveness.

7
Conclusion

We justified sequence-level importance sampling (IS) for RLVR and revisited sequence-level clipping, showing that fixed clip ranges induce a length-reweighting pathology that biases acceptance across response lengths and distorts the effective objective. We formalized
length fairness
via the Length Reweighting Error (LRE) and established a cosine-direction guarantee linking small LRE to update-direction fidelity. Guided by an approximate Gaussian law for the sequence log-IS sum, we proposed
FSPO
: clipping in log-IS space with a
\sqrt{L}
-scaled band that preserves IS semantics while equalizing acceptance across lengths. Empirically, on math-reasoning benchmarks and two model scales, FSPO flattens acceptance-by-length and delivers consistent gains, with the largest improvements on the 8B model. A limitation is that our study centers on math-reasoning RLVR and adopts a single global scale parameter without extensive hyperparameter search. Looking ahead, we will extend evaluation to other RLVR settings (e.g., tool-use with automatic verifiers) and beyond, develop adaptive clip-range schedules, and combine FSPO with stronger advantage estimation to build more capable RL pipelines.

Appendix A
Preliminaries and Related Work

Setup.

Let
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
be a context (prompt) drawn from a data distribution
p(x)
,
and let
y_{i}=(y_{i,1},...y_{i,|y_{i}|})\in\mathcal{Y}
be a response (a token sequence).
Under a policy
\pi_{\theta}
(which is an LLM in our setting), the outcome distribution is

P^{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{i}\mid x_{i})\;=\;\prod_{t=1}^{|y_{i}|}\pi_{\theta}\big(y_{i,t}\mid h_{i,t}\big),

where
h_{i,t}=(x_{i},y_{i,<t})
is the previous state.

RLHF and PPO.

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
[
15
]
frames alignment as policy optimization against a reward model learned from human preference data. RLHF utilizes PPO
[
18
]
algorithm: it assigns reward signal to the last token and uses GAE
[
19
]
to compute per-token advantages
\hat{A}_{i,t}
. The typical PPO objective is

\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\;\frac{1}{|y_{i}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_{i}|}\min\!\Big(r_{i,t}(\theta)\,\hat{A}_{i,t},\;\mathrm{clip}\big(r_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon\big)\,\hat{A}_{i,t}\Big),

(7)

where

r_{i,t}(\theta)=\dfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid h_{i,t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid h_{i,t})}
is the token-level IS weight.

RLVR paradigm and GRPO.

For math, programming and other verifiable tasks, recent systems adopt the RLVR
[
25
]
paradigm: rule-based, sequence-level rewards that can be automatically checked. Representative methods include GRPO
[
20
]
, DAPO
[
27
]
, DrGRPO
[
10
]
, etc. A typical GRPO-style update draws a group of
G
completions
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}
for the same prompt
x
under
\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}
(note here we abuse the notation of index
i
as within-group index, while previous
i
is the index in the dataset), computes sequence rewards
R_{i}=G(x,y_{i})
, and uses the group mean as a baseline so that
\hat{A}_{i}=(R_{i}-\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}R_{j})/\sigma
, where
\sigma
is the standard deviation of rewards of this group. The advantage for each token is
\hat{A}_{i,t}=\hat{A}_{i}
for all
t=1,2,...,|y_{i}|
. The objective of GRPO is similar to equation
7
, thus inherits the token-level IS weight and clipping.

Sequence-level Importance Sampling

Recent works argue that LLM RL with sequence rewards should be performed with sequence-level importance sampling (IS). RLOO
[
2
]
models LLM as a one-step bandit and treats a whole response as an action. According to the implementation of TRL
RLOO_Trainer

[
5
]
, the objective of RLOO is

\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{RLOO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_{i}\}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\min\!\Big(s_{i}(\theta)\,\hat{A}^{\mathrm{LOO}}_{i},\;\mathrm{clip}\big(s_{i}(\theta),\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon\big)\,\hat{A}^{\mathrm{LOO}}_{i}\Big)\right],

(8)

where

s_{i}(\theta)=\frac{P^{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{i}|x_{i})}{P^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}(y_{i}|x_{i})}

(9)

is the sequence-level IS weight which matches the reward granularity, and
\hat{A}_{i}^{\text{LOO}}
is the leave-one-out unbiased advantage estimator in
[
8
]
. A recent work GSPO
[
29
]
pursue the same goal of sequence-level IS but normalize the IS ratio by length (e.g., using
s_{i}^{\mathrm{norm}}=\exp(\frac{1}{|y_{i}|}\log s_{i})
) before clipping:

\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GSPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\min\!\Big(s^{\mathrm{norm}}_{i}(\theta)\,\hat{A}_{i},\;\mathrm{clip}\big(s^{\mathrm{norm}}_{i}(\theta),\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon\big)\,\hat{A}_{i}\Big)\right].

(10)

The idea of normalization by length is out of a similar motivation of providing a same scale for different lengths
[
29
]
. However, we argue that this normalization does not reach the goal of balancing the clipping scale, and also undermines the correctness IS weights.

Appendix B
Why Sequence-level IS for RLVR

RLOO
[
2
]
models the entire generation as a single action (a bandit setting), but its context is RLHF and it does not yet discuss the inadequacy of token-level IS and the correctness of sequence-level IS for RLVR. GSPO
[
29
]
notes that, in RLVR, the granularity of importance sampling should match the granularity of the reward, but it does not provide a detailed theoretical justification.

Here we offer a detailed and illuminating discussion.
As proved by
[
7
,
17
]
and presented in
[
1
]
, the improvement of the objective between old and new parameters can be written as

\displaystyle J(\theta)-J(\theta_{old})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim P^{\pi_{\theta}}}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}A^{\pi_{\theta_{old}}}(s_{t},a_{t})\right]

(11)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{s_{t}\sim p_{\theta}(s_{t})}\!\left[\,\mathbb{E}_{a_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}|s_{t})}\!\big[\gamma^{t}A^{\pi_{\theta_{old}}}(s_{t},a_{t})\big]\right]

(12)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{s_{t}\sim p_{\theta}(s_{t})}\!\left[\,\mathbb{E}_{a_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a_{t}|s_{t})}\!\bigg[\frac{\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}|s_{t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a_{t}|s_{t})}\,\gamma^{t}A^{\pi_{\theta_{old}}}(s_{t},a_{t})\bigg]\right].

(13)

This is where token-level importance sampling is introduced, as we need to express expectations under
\pi_{\theta}
using samples sampled from
\theta_{old}
in practice. Note that the state distribution
s_{t}\!\sim p_{\theta}(s_{t})
is not corrected by IS:
p(s_{t})
factors into a product over all previous actions, so naively correcting it can lead to high variance and poor estimates. In fact, the trust region in TRPO and the clipping mechanism in PPO are designed precisely to mitigate the mismatch incurred when estimating
s_{t}\!\sim p_{\theta}(s_{t})
using samples from
s_{t}\!\sim p_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(s_{t})
, since one can show that
p_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(s_{t})
and
p_{\theta}(s_{t})
remain close when
\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}
and
\pi_{\theta}
are close.

However, this formulation becomes problematic in the RLVR setting, where all tokens in a sequence share a single advantage. From
Equation

11
to
Equation

12
, the expectations over
(s_{t+1},a_{t+1},s_{t+2},\ldots)
are marginalized out. That step requires the summand depends only on
(s_{t},a_{t})
and not on the
future
part of the trajectory. This condition fails in RLVR, in which
A(s_{t},a_{t})=A(\tau)
for all
t
in a sample sequence.

To make this concrete, consider an imagined batch with two samples
y_{a},y_{b}\sim P^{\pi_{\theta_{old}}}(y\mid x)
,

y_{a}=(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{t},y_{t+1}^{(a)},y_{t+2}^{(a)},\ldots),\qquad y_{b}=(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{t},y_{t+1}^{(b)},y_{t+2}^{(b)},\ldots),

which share the same tokens up to index
t
and diverge from
t{+}1
onward. Suppose
y_{a}
is correct and
y_{b}
is incorrect (e.g.,
A(y_{a})=0.5
,
A(y_{b})=-0.5
),
P^{\pi_{\theta_{old}}}(y_{a})=P^{\pi_{\theta_{old}}}(y_{b})
, and
P^{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{a})>P^{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{b})
. This setting implies that, under the current policy
\pi_{\theta}
, conditioned on the shared prefix
(y_{0},\ldots,y_{t})
,
y_{a}
is more likely to occur than
y_{b}
, i.e., the model is more likely to answer correctly than incorrectly given the prefix. Intuitively, the next update should increase the likelihood of the prefix
(y_{0},\ldots,y_{t})
. Sequence-level IS achieves this, because

\frac{P^{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{a})}{P^{\pi_{\theta_{old}}}(y_{a})}\;>\;\frac{P^{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{b})}{P^{\pi_{\theta_{old}}}(y_{b})},

and the shared-prefix gradients are accordingly weighted more for
y_{a}
than for
y_{b}
. By contrast, token-level IS assigns the same token-level ratios to the shared tokens
(y_{0},\ldots,y_{t})
for both
y_{a}
and
y_{b}
(and for any other sample with that prefix), so it cannot express this desirable preference. This illustrates the advantage of sequence-level IS for RLVR.

Interestingly, in the case of sequence-level IS weight, the problem of
p_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\neq p_{\theta}
as in PPO, TRPO does not hold, because the probability of the entire sequence is corrected. However, the clipping mechanism can be kept to solve the high-variance issue of sequence-level ratio and thus is still necessary.

Appendix C
Proof of
Theorem

2.1

Cosine lemma.

For nonzero
u,v
,
\cos\angle(u,v)\geq\frac{\|v\|-\|u-v\|}{\|v\|+\|u-v\|}
.

Proof

Note that
\cos\angle(g^{b},g^{*})=\cos\angle(g^{b},\bar{q}g^{*})
. To use cosine lemma, we want to bound
||g^{b}-\bar{q}g^{*}||
.

Recall
g^{b}=\mathbb{E}_{L}[q(L)g_{L}^{b}]
,
g^{\star}=\mathbb{E}_{L}[g_{L}^{\star}]
,
\bar{q}=\mathbb{E}[q(L)]
.
Decompose

g^{b}-\bar{q}\,g^{\star}=\mathbb{E}_{L}\big[(q(L)-\bar{q})\,g_{L}^{\star}\big]\;+\;\mathbb{E}_{L}\big[q(L)\,(g_{L}^{b}-g_{L}^{\star})\big].

We bound its norm as

\displaystyle\|g^{b}-\bar{q}g^{\star}\|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{L}\!\big[\,|q(L)-\bar{q}|\,\|g^{\star}_{L}\|\,\big]}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{cross-length reweighting}\end{subarray}}\;+\;\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{L}\!\big[\,\|\,q(L)\,(g^{(b)}_{L}-g^{\star}_{L})\|\,\big]}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{within-length stratification}\end{subarray}}

(by triangle inequality)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{q}\;\mathbb{E}_{L}\left[\left|\frac{q(L)}{\bar{q}}-1\right|||g^{*}_{L}||\right]+\eta\;\mathbb{E}_{L}\left[||(q(L)-\bar{q})g^{*}_{L}+\bar{q}\;g^{*}_{L}||\right]

(by
2.1
)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\eta)\bar{q}\;\mathbb{E}_{L}\left[\left|\frac{q(L)}{\bar{q}}-1\right|||g^{*}_{L}||\right]+\eta\bar{q}\;\mathbb{E}_{L}[||g^{*}_{L}||]

(by algebra)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{q}\;(2\gamma(1+\eta)\;\mathrm{LRE}+\eta)\;\mathbb{E}_{L}[||g^{*}_{L}||]

(by
2.2
and definition of LRE)

Recall that
\mathbb{E}_{L}[||g^{*}_{L}||]=\kappa||g^{*}||
and apply the cosine lemma with
u=g^{b}
,
v=\bar{q}g^{*}
, and
Theorem

2.1
is proved.

Weighted
-
LRE variant

If one wants to avoid any bounded co-variation concerns, define

\mathrm{LRE}_{w}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\frac{q(L)}{\bar{q}}-1\right|\frac{\|g_{L}^{\star}\|}{\mathbb{E}\|g_{L}^{\star}\|}\right].

Then the same argument yields the bound with
\mathrm{LRE}
replaced by
\mathrm{LRE}_{w}
.

More discussion: beyond length.

It is worth noting that the proof of
Theorem

2.1
does not fundamentally rely on the specific choice of
L
as the partitioning variable.
The argument only requires that the sample space can be partitioned into groups where (i) the signals across groups exhibit dispersion, and (ii) within-group stratification errors can be controlled.
Thus,
L
can be replaced by any other reasonable attribute that induces such a partition, for example bins of lengths (which justifies the binning process in our diagnostic plots), or other salient structural features of the data.
This perspective suggests a more general criterion for designing clipping mechanisms: it should not introduce systematic bias across groups of any reasonable partitioning attribute, unless such a bias is intentionally desired.

D.2
Algorithmic Hyperparameters and Tuning

Limitation

Due to compute constraints, we did not perform an exhaustive hyperparameter search. For settings similar to ours, we recommend fixing the base clipping scale
c
at
0.03
or higher. However, this value may not transfer across substantially different datasets or model sizes.

Interpreting
c
,
z
, and
\hat{\sigma}

In our formulation, the sequence log-IS ratio
S
is clipped by a band whose width is

\text{band}\;=\;z\cdot\hat{\sigma}\,

Setting
c=0.03
is equivalent to choosing
z{=}1
and
\hat{\sigma}{=}0.03
in our environment (cf. Sec.
3
). In our experiments we first obtain
\hat{\sigma}
from a short baseline run, which inevitably introduces one preliminary pass.

To avoid a dedicated pilot run while preserving stability, we recommend:

1.

Initialize with
z\in[1,\,1.5]
and a heuristic
\hat{\sigma}_{0}{=}0.03
for warmup.

2.

Maintain a running estimate
\hat{\sigma}_{t}
of the log-IS ratio std via an exponential moving average (EMA):
\hat{\sigma}_{t}\!\leftarrow\!(1-\alpha)\hat{\sigma}_{t-1}+\alpha\,\mathrm{std}_{\text{batch}}(S)
.

3.

Update the clip band as
z\cdot\hat{\sigma}_{t}
.
