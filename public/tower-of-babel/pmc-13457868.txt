Effect of slit-regulated 3D embroidered sensor structures on contact area-variation-based strain sensing performance in smart gloves
Authors: Moonjung Choi, Jeong-Whan Lee, Sumin Helen Koo, Joo Hyeon Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate sensing of finger joint angles is a key objective in wearable motion sensing systems for rehabilitation applications and requires high sensing reliability and accuracy. However, conventional textile-based strain sensors often exhibit low reliability, characterized by baseline drift and the double-peak phenomenon during repetitive deformation. These limitations are mainly attributed to the length-variation-based sensing mechanism, in which repeated deformation induces changes in the sensor’s length and shape, leading to signal instability. To address these limitations, this study adopts a contact area-variation-based sensing mechanism that minimizes noise arising from changes in the sensor’s length and shape during repetitive deformation. A glove-integrated embroidered strain sensor was developed, and three key structural design elements affecting sensing reliability were systematically investigated: slit presence, the number of effective conductive contact points, and embroidery stitch density. Nonparametric statistical analyses were applied, including Spearman’s rank correlation and the Mann–Whitney U test. The slit-structured sensor (S1) achieved 100% peak detection across all fingers, whereas the non-slit sensor (S0) showed inconsistent detection. Reproducibility improved from r = 0.818 to r = 0.972, signal magnitude increased up to fivefold (~ 15–80 mV), baseline drift was reduced to below 1%, and double-peak artifacts were suppressed. The system maintained integrity after 20,000 Martindale abrasion cycles. Among the investigated design elements, the slit structure was identified as the most dominant factor influencing sensing reliability. These results suggest that incorporating a slit structure provides a reliable sensing foundation and supports the feasibility of finger joint angle estimation in textile-based wearable systems. Introduction

Hand function plays a central role in daily living, communication, and independent functioning. However, it is often impaired by neurological conditions such as stroke1–3, cerebral palsy4,5, and Parkinson’s disease6, as well as by age-related motor decline7–9. Targeted hand rehabilitation has been shown to improve both motor10,11 and cognitive performance12,13, making it a priority in both therapeutic and preventive care. Conventional rehabilitation, however, is labor-intensive and constrained by therapist availability14–16, and often lacks quantitative feedback or real-time progress tracking17–19. These limitations highlight the need for wearable rehabilitation tools that can support individualized, scalable, and home-based training. In this context, smart gloves have emerged as a promising solution for continuous hand and finger motion monitoring and self-directed rehabilitation.

In neurorehabilitation and daily activity tracking, especially for aging populations, wearable systems must be comfortable, anatomically conforming, and capable of providing reliable motion data. However, angle estimation using textile-based wearable sensors remains technically challenging, primarily due to baseline drift20, double-peak phenomena21,22 and signal instability during repeated use23,24. This study specifically targets the stabilization of baseline signals as a prerequisite for reliable sensing.

Most textile-based strain sensors rely on resistance changes induced by length-variation strain sensing. While structurally simple and easy to integrate, these sensors often suffer from signal nonlinearity, hysteresis, and drift under repeated deformation25–27. These issues are particularly pronounced in highly curved or articulated regions such as fingers. Because finger joints are small and produce relatively limited elongation during motion, the effective strain applied to the sensor is minimal. This makes the signal more vulnerable to noise caused by textile distortion or surface interaction with skin, rather than true length variation28.

In many textile-based motion sensors, such limitations are reflected in reduced sensing reliability, as indicated by frequent baseline drift and the double-peak phenomenon during cyclic deformation. These phenomena serve as key indicators of low sensing reliability and are primarily attributed to the length-variation-based sensing mechanism, in which repeated deformation induces changes in the sensor’s length and shape, leading to unstable signal responses. To address this limitation, the present study adopts a contact area-variation-based sensing mechanism.

As an alternative, strain sensors based on contact area-variation have gained increasing attention. Rather than measuring strain through length extension, these sensors detect resistance changes that occur when conductive elements come into or out of contact in response to deformation29–32. This mechanism is less sensitive to substrate elasticity and mechanical lag, resulting in better signal stability, lower hysteresis, and improved reproducibility during cyclic loading33–35. In addition, the decoupling of strain from bulk textile elongation makes these sensors more suitable for anatomical regions where conventional strain measurement has shown limited reliability. A contact area-variation-based sensing mechanism is employed to minimize noise arising from changes in the sensor’s length and shape during repetitive deformation.

For fabrication, computerized embroidery presents a unique advantage for developing such sensors. Unlike coating or printing methods that may suffer from poor repeatability and durability36, embroidery allows precise control over stitch geometry, placement, and density, even on complex or flexible substrates37–39. It also enables seamless integration of sensing elements, conductive interconnection, and connector terminals in a single textile layer. These features are particularly important for glove-type systems, where finger-level precision, flexibility, and wearability are critical for practical use40–42.

However, developing textile strain sensors that function reliably on fingers remains technically challenging. Fingers exhibit complex three-dimensional motion and continuous surface deformation43,44, making it difficult to maintain consistent contact between sensor layers. Sensors placed on curved regions are especially prone to unstable signals, contact loss, or unintended activation during non-functional movements45. Prior studies have demonstrated the feasibility of embroidered sensors for joint or limb-level monitoring46–49, but their designs have not been optimized for the anatomical and mechanical characteristics of the fingers.

Studies on textile sensors for respiration and body surface deformation monitoring have demonstrated that incorporating three-dimensional (3D) structural elements can improve contact conformity and pressure responsiveness50–52. Although respiration and finger movement differ biomechanically, both involve cyclical surface deformation and require robust body–sensor interaction. These findings suggest that 3D structural principles could be adapted for finger-specific sensors.

To enable reliable finger motion sensing, this study devised a strain sensor that integrates (1) a contact area-variation-based sensing mechanism, (2) a three-dimensional wearable sensor structure, and (3) a scalable embroidery-based fabrication strategy tailored to the anatomical characteristics of the sensing region.

This study focuses on improving signal reliability in textile-based strain sensors by addressing baseline instability (baseline drift) and double-peak phenomena observed during repetitive motion. These limitations significantly degrade sensing accuracy and must be resolved prior to reliable resistance-based motion sensing.

Nevertheless, the structural origin of signal instability in textile-based finger sensors has not been systematically investigated. In particular, the relative influence of key structural parameters in three-dimensional embroidered sensor architectures remains unclear.

To address this, three structural variables were systematically investigated to evaluate their influence on sensor performance: the presence of a slit structure, the number of effective conductive contact points, and the embroidery stitch density. Sensor performance was evaluated in terms of signal reliability, reproducibility, baseline stability, and double-peak behavior under repetitive finger motion.

Accurate sensing of finger joint angles is the ultimate objective of wearable hand motion sensing systems, and achieving high sensing reliability is a fundamental prerequisite for accurate angle estimation. By identifying the dominant structural design factors governing sensing reliability, this study establishes a foundational sensor configuration that enables reliable signal acquisition for future finger joint angle estimation. This work therefore contributes a critical step toward addressing the challenge of accurate joint angle estimation in textile-based wearable sensors.

Results

Peak detection accuracy

Effect of the slit structure on peak detection accuracy

To evaluate the effect of slit structures on sensing reliability, the number of signal peaks detected by each sensor configuration was compared with the actual number of motion cycles (10 per finger) verified through frame-by-frame video analysis. For each sensor configuration, peak counts were obtained for each motion set and averaged across three repeated motion sets.

Sensors with a slit structure (S1) consistently detected all 10 motions across all five fingers, demonstrating full accuracy with recorded movements. In contrast, sensors without a slit structure (S0) showed inconsistent detection performance, particularly in the index and little fingers as summarized in Table 1; Fig. 1.

S1 refers to the sensor with a slit structure, whereas S0 denotes the sensor without a slit. Group comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test, as summarized in Table 1, to assess differences in peak detection accuracy.

For the thumb, S1 detected all 10 motions (M = 10.00), while S0 detected an average of 6.19 (SD = 3.58), yielding a significant difference (Z = − 5.918, p < 0.001). Similar trends were observed for the index (S1: M = 10.00; S0: M = 6.65, SD = 4.16; Z = − 4.605, p < 0.001), middle (S1: M = 10.00; S0: M = 6.32, SD = 4.55; Z = − 4.989, p < 0.001), and ring fingers (S1: M = 10.00; S0: M = 8.11, SD = 3.05; Z = − 4.224, p < 0.001). The most pronounced difference was found in the little finger, where S0 detected only 3.39 motions on average (SD = 4.39), compared to full detection in S1 (Z = − 5.963, p < 0.001).

These findings demonstrate that incorporation of the slit structure significantly improves peak detection accuracy across all fingers. The improvement was particularly evident in the little finger, which is more susceptible to mechanical instability. Overall, S0 sensors detected between 3.39 and 8.11 motions out of 10, confirming reduced accuracy compared to slit-integrated sensors.

Effects of contact points and stitch density on peak detection accuracy

To further examine the role of other structural parameters, sensor performance was compared across configurations with different numbers of effective conductive contact points (C0: 18 points; C1: 45 points) and embroidery stitch densities (WL: low density; WH: high density). For conductive contact points, significant differences were observed only in the index and little fingers. In the index finger, C1 showed higher accuracy than C0 (C0: M = 7.17, SD = 4.18; C1: M = 9.49, SD = 1.70; Z = − 2.519, p = 0.012). In contrast, the little finger exhibited higher accuracy in C0 than in C1 (C0: M = 8.23, SD = 3.53; C1: M = 5.17, SD = 4.96; Z = − 2.074, p = 0.038). No significant differences were detected in the thumb, middle, or ring fingers. In the comparison of embroidery stitch density, no significant differences were observed across any fingers.

These findings suggest that the influence of contact points and stitch density on peak detection accuracy is limited and finger-dependent, whereas the slit structure exerts a more consistent and robust effect across all fingers. This further supports the structural advantage of the slit-based configuration for stable signal detection.

Baseline stability

Morphological analysis on baseline drift

Baseline morphology across four motion–stop segments was examined to assess structural effects on long-term signal stability. Baseline drift refers to variations in the signal baseline during resting segments. In conventional length-variation-based textile motion sensors, repeated deformation often induces progressive increases in the sensor length due to the inherent mechanical properties of textile materials. This leads to a gradual upward shift of the baseline over successive motion cycles. Such baseline drift is widely recognized as a key indicator of insufficient signal stability and low sensing reliability.

Representative data are shown in Fig. 2. In the signal waveform, low peaks correspond to finger flexion, reflecting increased contact between conductive elements during motion. The representative configurations shown in Fig. 2 include S1-C1-WL (a) and S0-C1-WL (b), where S1 and S0 denote sensors with and without a slit structure, respectively, C1 indicates the maximum number of conductive contact points, and WL represents low embroidery stitch density. Sensors with a slit structure (S1) exhibited cleaner signals and more stable baselines during resting segments. In contrast, sensors without a slit (S0) showed increased noise and noticeable baseline fluctuations. In Fig. 2b, the circled regions highlight unstable baseline drift.

Quantitative comparison of baseline drift

Baseline drift was quantified by calculating the percentage change in mean voltage across four motion–stop segments within each trial. The average of these percentage changes was defined as the baseline drift rate (Fig. 3; Table 5). The S1 group demonstrated smaller absolute drift values than the S0 group across all fingers. Statistically significant reductions were observed in the index (Z = − 1.999, p < 0.05), middle (Z = − 3.523, p < 0.001), ring (Z = − 2.786, p < 0.01), and little fingers (Z = − 3.523, p < 0.001). The thumb showed a similar trend but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.051).

Occurrence of double peak

Key observations on double peak phenomenon

Signal morphology was examined through qualitative morphological analysis to complement the quantitative signal magnitude evaluation, focusing on double-peak occurrence and waveform consistency across repeated motion cycles. The double-peak phenomenon refers to the presence of multiple adjacent peaks within a single motion cycle. The occurrence of more than one peak within a single motion indicates that a single movement is erroneously detected as multiple events, often due to noise or signal instability. As the severity of double-peak artifacts increases, sensing reliability decreases. Furthermore, such distortions disrupt the proportional relationship between joint angle and signal response, thereby limiting the feasibility of accurate angle estimation. Sensors with a slit structure (S1) exhibited higher signal magnitudes, fewer double peaks, and more consistent waveform patterns across three repeated motion sets. In contrast, sensors without a slit (S0) showed lower signal magnitudes and more frequent double or multiple peaks (Fig. 4).

Quantitative comparison of double peak phenomenon

Signal magnitude, defined as the peak-to-peak voltage difference between flexion and extension phases identified by a custom peak detection algorithm, was used as a quantitative indicator of double-peak artifacts. For each motion set, signal magnitude was calculated across ten consecutive cycles and then averaged across the three motion sets to obtain a representative value for each condition. Higher signal magnitude corresponded to single, well-defined peaks, whereas lower values were associated with double-peak patterns. Signal magnitude was compared between sensors with a slit structure (S1) and without a slit structure (S0) to evaluate structural effects on signal quality.

Figure 6 shows that S1 sensors exhibited significantly higher signal magnitudes across all five fingers. Wear resistance of the embroidered sensor

To assess the extended mechanical durability of the sensor system under realistic use conditions, abrasion resistance tests were conducted on two textile configurations: (1) the base knit fabric used for both the slit structure and the glove platform, and (2) the embroidered sensor stitched onto the same base fabric using silver-plated conductive yarns.

The tests were performed in accordance with the KS K ISO 12947-2:2016 (Martindale method) protocol, using a load of 9 kPa and a mass of 595 ± 7 g. Each specimen was subjected to 20,000 rubs. The predefined failure criterion was the breakage of a conductive yarn, which would indicate a loss of electrical continuity.

Both samples completed the full abrasion cycle without any visible mechanical damage, yarn degradation, or detachment. In particular, no conductive yarn breakage was observed during or after testing. These results indicate that the embroidered sensing elements and the underlying textile substrate exhibit sufficient wear resistance to endure repeated mechanical stress. This confirms their suitability for integration into wearable systems that require both electrical performance and long-term mechanical durability.

Discussion

This study examined how three structural components, including the presence of a slit structure, the number of effective conductive contact points, and the conductive embroidery stitch density, affect the performance of contact area-variation-based embroidered strain sensors in glove-type wearable systems. Among these variables, the slit structure consistently exhibited the most significant and reliable impact on key sensing metrics, including reliability, reproducibility, baseline stability, and signal clarity. Sensors incorporating this structure produced repeatable responses across all fingers, demonstrated stable baselines during rest phases, and minimized artifacts such as double peaks and signal drift. Notably, the system achieved 100% peak detection accuracy, a baseline drift rate below 1%, and successfully passed 20,000 standardized Martindale abrasion cycles without conductive yarn failure. The folded three-dimensional geometry facilitated selective and intentional contact during finger flexion while reducing unintended activation during extension. This configuration enhanced signal selectivity and stability, particularly in anatomically constrained regions such as the little finger, where low motion independence and inter-finger dependency often lead to uneven strain distribution.

Beyond sensor-level performance, this work presents a fully integrated textile smart glove system designed around the proposed sensor architecture. Unlike conventional approaches that rely on fiber-level functionalization or treat sensors and garments as separate components, the proposed platform directly integrates embroidered sensors into a glove designed to optimize their performance. The contact area-variation sensing mechanism, combined with the slit-based three-dimensional structure and supplementary modules, enabled stable multi-finger sensing with reduced baseline drift and waveform distortion. The embroidered sensors responded promptly to finger motion without measurable signal delay, supporting real-time applications such as motion monitoring and gesture-based interaction.

Although textile-based sensors offer strong potential for wearable applications, their practical use has been limited by baseline drift and motion-induced signal artifacts during repeated use50. By systematically addressing these limitations through structural design optimization, the proposed approach improves the reliability and stability of textile-based sensing. These improvements support continuous and robust motion monitoring, with potential applications in rehabilitation and wearable human–machine interface systems. Such systems enable continuous motion monitoring and feedback outside controlled environments.

Collectively, this work advances textile-based sensor design by demonstrating that a structure-oriented, three-dimensional embroidered architecture can substantially improve signal stability and reproducibility in finger motion sensing. By integrating sensing elements and the wearable interface within a unified textile platform, the proposed system moves beyond material-level functionalization and establishes a mechanically robust and practically applicable framework for reliable motion monitoring.

Beyond controlled experimental validation, the proposed sensing platform holds potential for a range of real-world applications. In rehabilitation monitoring, its stable and drift-resistant signals may support reliable long-term tracking of patient progress in home-based settings. In addition, the textile-based and anatomically conformable design is suitable for continuous daily activity monitoring, while the improved signal clarity and reproducibility also support human–machine interface applications such as gesture recognition and wearable input systems. This structure-driven approach offers a scalable strategy for developing reliable textile sensors for complex anatomical regions beyond the fingers.

Slit embedded 3D structure of the strain sensor

The sensor detects resistance changes induced by contact between embroidered conductive regions during finger flexion and their separation during extension. However, the close proximity of these conductive surfaces can lead to unintended contact and false signal generation, particularly at rest.

To regulate this contact behavior, the proposed sensor incorporates a folded multilayer structure combined with a slit-embedded design. The folded structure aligns conductive regions to induce contact during finger flexion, while the slit introduces a controlled gap that prevents premature contact under no-motion conditions.

The fundamental sensing principle is illustrated in Fig. 10a, where signal generation is governed by changes in the effective contact area between conductive layers. Without the slit structure, unintended contact between conductive regions can occur even at rest, leading to signal distortion. To address this issue, a motion-responsive slit structure was introduced (Fig. 10b), which remains closed at rest and opens under flexion-induced deformation. The surrounding textile deforms under applied strain but does not structurally open, as the surrounding fabric does not contain any slit or opening features. This slit-mediated behavior increases contact area and decreases resistance during flexion, while decreasing contact and increasing resistance during extension, ensuring that signal generation corresponds only to intentional motion, thereby improving signal fidelity.

(a) Sensing principle based on changes in effective contact area between conductive regions. (b) Functional role of the slit mechanism during finger flexion and extension. (c) Slit configuration transition during motion: (A) closed during extension, (B) open during flexion.

As shown in Fig. 11, the slit structure consists of a laser-cut square pattern (1.5 cm × 2.6 cm, 1.2 mm spacing) embedded within the sensor region. This design allows the upper and lower embroidered layers to contact during flexion, reducing resistance, and separate during extension, increasing resistance. The slit additionally functions as a mechanical isolator, minimizing signal distortion from lateral forces and fabric deformation. Unlike conventional strain sensors that rely on tensile elongation, this design uses contact-area variability as the primary sensing mechanism. A folded structure aligns conductive patterns on both sides of the substrate, enabling targeted sensing in response to finger motion.

Design and fabrication of glove-type platform

Sensor placement through skin deformation analysis

A pilot study was conducted to determine the optimal placement of textile-based strain sensors on the hand by analyzing motion-induced skin deformation. Using Kinovea software and marker-based video tracking, strain on hand skin was quantified during finger flexion–extension in five participants. The lower region of the middle phalanx consistently exhibited the highest strain across all fingers, making it the most suitable site for sensor placement, as illustrated in Fig. 12a. This area, located just above the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint in the thumb and the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joints in the other fingers, showed greater deformation than directly over the joints. These findings support a placement strategy based on dynamic strain patterns rather than static anatomical landmarks. This approach enhances signal fidelity, reduces mechanical noise, and improves motion-tracking accuracy in glove-based wearable systems. Sensor placement in subsequent experiments followed this result.

Sensor integration

To develop a fully functional wearable system, embroidered strain sensors, interconnection lines, and connector modules were integrated into a smart glove platform. The embroidery process was designed to define the complete sensing architecture, including the strain sensor, interconnections, and connector terminals, all fabricated in a single sequence using a computerized multi-head embroidery machine. This integration aimed to ensure system-level signal stability and ergonomic wearability during dynamic hand motion.

Sensors were embroidered onto finger-specific textile segments using silver-plated conductive yarn. Based on the results from the skin deformation analysis described in Sect. 4.4.1 and shown in Fig. 12a, the embroidered sensors were positioned near the lower portion of the middle phalanx, just above the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint in the thumb and above the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joints in the other fingers. This placement ensured that joint flexion would mechanically open the slit, thereby inducing contact between the conductive regions, as implemented in the glove-type platform shown in Fig. 12b.

In the glove-type macro platform, conductive embroidery was used to create interconnection lines directly on the base fabric alongside sensor elements. A 1 mm stitch size and 2.5 mm line width with a zigzag pattern were applied to enhance durability and conductivity without inducing strain. The embroidered lines were positioned at controlled distances to prevent twisting, ensuring signal stability while conforming to the glove’s three-dimensional curvature.

Final assembly steps prioritized signal continuity and wearer comfort, ensuring that the system accommodated natural hand motion without mechanical interference.

Experimental design

Participants

This study controlled for hand size and finger length as potential intervening variables in evaluating the effects of sensor structure on sensing performance. Seven female participants (age range: 25–31 years; mean ± SD: 28.43 ± 2.19 years) were purposively selected based on anthropometric data for adult women, targeting individuals with hand and finger dimensions near the population average to minimize anatomical variability. The mean ± SD finger lengths were as follows: thumb, 6.89 ± 0.74 cm; index, 7.64 ± 0.27 cm; middle, 8.64 ± 0.52 cm; ring, 8.09 ± 0.41 cm; and little finger, 6.24 ± 0.27 cm. The mean total hand length was 17.83 ± 0.73 cm. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Yonsei University (IRB No. 7001988-202501-HR-2474-03) and were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

Experimental setup

For the experiments, a full-factorial design (2 × 2 × 2) was employed to evaluate eight sensor configurations, each representing a distinct combination of structural conditions. Three structural variables were manipulated: (1) the presence or absence of a slit structure; (2) the number of effective conductive contact points (hereafter referred to as contact points), indicating the total number of conductive regions that can make effective contact between the upper and lower embroidered layers (either 45 or 18 points, depending on the configuration); and (3) the conductive embroidery stitch density (0.4–0.8 mm), defined as the spacing between adjacent conductive stitches.

Research scope and controlled variables

This study was designed as an exploratory investigation to identify the dominant structural design factors governing sensing reliability in textile-based finger motion sensors. Accordingly, the scope of this study was intentionally limited to structural design variables in order to isolate their effects on sensing performance.

Three structural design factors were defined as independent variables: slit presence, the number of effective conductive contact points, and embroidery stitch density. Sensing reliability was defined as the dependent variable and was evaluated using multiple performance metrics, including peak detection accuracy, sensing reproducibility, baseline stability, and the occurrence of double-peak artifacts.

Human-related and environmental factors associated with wearable use were treated as controlled variables and excluded from the scope of this study. Specifically, variables such as hand morphology, wearing method, and sensor placement were standardized across participants, while environmental conditions such as temperature and humidity were kept constant throughout the experiments.

Sensing hardware

The system for measuring the change in the embroidered sensor integrated into the glove was implemented based on the STM32F411 Nucleo-64 (ARM Cortex-M4, @100 MHz, STMicroelectronics, U.S.A.). It supports 12-bit SAR ADC and can handle 8 channels of analog input signals. The ADC’s sampling rate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{s}$$\end{document}) was set to 500 Hz using the TIM2 timer module, and the data was stored in the data array using the DMA channel to reduce the CPU’s load. The sampling rate of 500 Hz was selected to satisfy the Nyquist criterion (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{s}$$\end{document} ≥ 2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{c}\:$$\end{document}= 200 Hz) and to provide sufficient temporal resolution for capturing transient dynamics and potential high-frequency artifacts originating from the embroidered sensor substrate, which may not be discernible at lower sampling rates. A Bessel low-pass filter (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{c}$$\end{document}= 100 Hz) was applied during data acquisition to reduce high-frequency noise while preserving the temporal accuracy of the acquired waveforms, owing to its maximally flat group delay characteristic that minimizes phase distortion. For morphological analysis, an additional 5 Hz low-pass filter was applied during post-processing to suppress residual noise while retaining the 1 Hz motion-relevant signal structure, thereby improving signal clarity in the figures. This configuration enabled real-time, stable acquisition of analog signals from the embroidered strain sensors. Each ADC channel had a resolution of ~ 0.8 mV/LSB (3.3 V / 4096 LSB), corresponding to the minimum detectable voltage change. A simplified equivalent circuit model of the sensing configuration is provided in the Supplementary Information (Fig. S1) for clarity.

Experimental protocol

The sensors were embedded into gloves and tested through repeated flexion–extension movements of all fingers. Each motion cycle involved flexion to approximately 90° and extension to 0°, with one cycle defined as a single motion unit. Each motion set comprised 10 consecutive cycles per finger, performed at a standardized speed of 1 Hz using a metronome to reflect typical finger movement frequency.

To ensure consistent joint angles across participants, a 3D-printed finger angle indicator was used to guide and verify the range of motion. Three motion sets were performed under the same conditions, as illustrated in Fig. 13.

Fig. 13Experimental protocol for repeated finger flexion–extension measurements.

Experimental protocol for repeated finger flexion–extension measurements.

The entire experimental protocol lasted 170 s, consisting of a 10-second initial delay, the first motion set (10 s), 60 s of rest, the second motion set (10 s), another 60 s of rest, the third motion set (10 s), and a final 10-second delay. Analog-to-digital converter (ADC) values were continuously recorded for signal analysis.

Data analysis

Signal data from eight embroidered strain sensor configurations were analyzed to evaluate how key structural variables influenced sensing performance. Because the number of participants was small, nonparametric statistical methods were primarily used for group comparisons.

For statistical analysis, the unit of analysis was defined as individual finger-level measurements for each participant under each sensor condition. For each condition, values were first averaged across motion cycles within each set and subsequently averaged across the three repeated motion sets to obtain a single representative value per finger per participant. Nonparametric statistical tests were then applied to these aggregated values.

Sensor performance focused on reliability was evaluated using four parameters: peak detection accuracy, sensing reproducibility, baseline drift rate, and signal magnitude. These metrics represent key aspects of sensing performance, including reliability, reproducibility, baseline stability, and signal quality.

Peak detection accuracy was calculated by comparing the number of signal peaks detected in each motion set with the reference number of motion cycles (10 cycles), verified using synchronized video recordings. These values were then averaged across three repeated motion sets. Sensing reproducibility was defined as the consistency of signal patterns across repeated motion sets and was quantified using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients. Correlation analysis was performed between signal responses obtained from repeated motion sets under identical conditions for each finger, using pairwise comparisons across the three sets. Baseline drift rate was defined as the average percentage change in mean voltage across resting segments within each trial. Signal magnitude was used as an indicator of double-peak behavior. It was calculated as the peak-to-peak voltage difference between flexion and extension phases within each motion cycle, as identified by a custom peak detection algorithm. Group comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 28.0), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

In addition to the quantitative analysis, morphological waveform analysis was conducted to visually examine baseline drift, double-peak occurrence, and waveform consistency across repeated motions. These visual observations provided additional insight into mechanical hysteresis and sensing reproducibility and supported the interpretation of the baseline stability and signal quality metrics.

This multi-criteria evaluation framework enabled a comprehensive assessment of sensor performance while considering the effects of the slit structure, the number of effective conductive contact points, and conductive embroidery stitch density. These combined findings support the practical applicability of the proposed sensor design in wearable systems.
