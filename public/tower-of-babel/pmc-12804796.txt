Targeting high-risk MYC-overexpressed osteosarcoma with an Aurora kinase inhibitor:--results from a pilot umbrella trial
Authors: Kai Tian, Yafei Jiang, Mengxiong Sun, Yu Lv, Jean Fan, Caixia Sun, Yujia Zhu, Yingying Yu, Xiaoyan Qiang, Peng Peng, Frank Wu, Weisong Zhao, Dongqing Zuo, Hongsheng Wang, Chongren Wang, Wei Sun, Jinzeng Wang, Zhengdong Cai, Yingqi Hua
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Osteosarcoma is a highly heterogeneous cancer, and molecular subtyping has the potential to increase diagnostic precision and facilitate targeted therapies. In our previous study, we classified osteosarcoma into four distinct subtypes. Here, we conducted a genomic subtype-guided pilot umbrella trial (ChiCTR2000036618, 2020/08/24) to evaluate the efficacy and safety of multiple precision therapies. Nineteen patients with refractory metastatic osteosarcoma were enrolled and stratified using whole-exome sequencing (WES) and immunohistochemistry (IHC). Patients were assigned to three arms: (A) PD-1 antibody plus gemcitabine and docetaxel; (B) PARP inhibitor combined with temozolomide; or (C) tinengotinib (TT-00420), a small-molecule aurora kinase inhibitor currently in clinical trials. The median progression-free survival and overall survival were 50 days (95% CI: 33–89) and 149 days (95% CI: 90–185). Tinengotinib has shown promising efficacy in our preclinical studies, suggesting its potential for clinical use, particularly in combination with immunotherapy. Additionally, we found that patients with MYC amplification presented increased tumor purity and ploidy, homologous recombination deficiency scores, and an immunosuppressive microenvironment. This study demonstrated the feasibility of using genomic molecular subtyping to guide the precise treatment of osteosarcoma. We also revealed that the abnormal genomic and transcriptomic profiles caused by MYC amplification could be suppressed by tinengotinib.

Introduction

Osteosarcoma, the most common primary malignant bone tumor, predominantly affects children and adolescents. It is highly aggressive and prone to metastasis1. Malignant bone tumors represent the third leading cause of cancer-related death among adolescents, and osteosarcoma has one of the poorest five-year survival rates among all pediatric cancers2,3. Over the past four decades, the five-year survival rate for osteosarcoma has remained stagnant at less than 70%, with metastatic cases showing an even poorer rate of less than 20%4. Numerous studies have investigated the roles of various molecular targets in osteosarcoma, such as VEGFR, mTOR, and PDGFR, and have evaluated the efficacy of single-agent versus combination chemotherapeutic regimens incorporating targeted therapies. However, these efforts have rarely resulted in significant clinical breakthroughs5–8.

Given the high degree of heterogeneity and genetic complexity in osteosarcoma, the current understanding of its biological mechanisms remains insufficient to drive successful clinical translation5,9,10. In our previous study, we delineated the genomic, epigenomic, and transcriptomic landscape of osteosarcoma, identifying four molecular subtypes with distinct features and clinical outcomes: (1) immune-activated (S-IA), (2) immune-suppressed (S-IS), (3) homologous recombination deficiency-dominant (S-HRD), and (4) MYC-driven (S-MD)11. Among these molecular subtypes, S-IA exhibited the most favorable prognosis and was less likely to progress to metastatic or treatment-refractory states, whereas S-MD was associated with the worst outcomes (five-year survival <40%) and demonstrated the highest propensity to develop metastatic or refractory disease. Recently, MYC amplification has also been widely validated as the most significant contributor to a poor prognosis in osteosarcoma patients11–13. However, the MYC signaling pathway is one of the most critical yet elusive oncogenic targets in human cancer, and no MYC inhibitor has yet been approved for clinical use14,15.

Tinengotinib (TT-00420) is a clinical-stage small-molecule kinase inhibitor that mainly targets Aurora A/B and has potent antitumor activity16–20. Aurora kinase promotes MYC protein stabilization through direct binding or phosphorylation21–23. Furthermore, inhibiting Aurora kinase leads to MYC protein degradation and selectively suppresses MYC overexpression in cancer cells while preserving physiological MYC expression in nonmalignant cells24,25. Therefore, tinengotinib is a potential therapeutic agent for tumors with MYC amplification/overexpression.

Clinically, tinengotinib has demonstrated favorable pharmacokinetics and tolerability17 and is currently undergoing global phase 3 clinical trials in the US, EU/UK, and Asia. Tinengotinib has been tested in multiple clinical trials for solid tumors and has received the “Orphan Drug Designation” from the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and “Fast Track Designation” from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of cholangiocarcinoma. It has also been included in the Breakthrough Therapy List by China’s National Medical Products Administration.

Here, we preclinically validated the efficacy of tinengotinib and demonstrated its potential for synergistic combination with immunotherapy in clinical applications (Fig. 1). Building on these findings, we conducted a pilot umbrella trial that integrated osteosarcoma molecular subtyping with genomic sequencing-guided targeted therapy for refractory metastatic osteosarcoma patients (Fig. 2). Results

Tinengotinib suppresses osteosarcoma progression in preclinical models

Tinengotinib is a novel inhibitor targeting multiple kinases, including Aurora kinase A and B. This compound has shown significant antitumor activity, and mechanistic studies have confirmed its ability to induce MYC degradation16,19. To investigate the effects of tinengotinib on osteosarcoma, we incubated the human osteosarcoma cell lines 143B, U2OS, MNNG/HOS, and HOS, as well as the murine osteosarcoma cell line LM-8, with or without tinengotinib at various concentrations for 48 h. Cell viability was subsequently assessed using the CCK8 assay. The results demonstrated that tinengotinib effectively inhibited osteosarcoma cell proliferation (Fig. 1a). Additionally, a colony formation assay revealed that tinengotinib treatment significantly reduced the number of colonies (Fig. 1b). These findings indicate that tinengotinib significantly suppresses osteosarcoma proliferation in vitro.

Given that tinengotinib targets both Aurora A and B, we compared its inhibitory effects with those of other Aurora A kinase inhibitors, ENMD-2076 and MLN8237, as well as the Aurora B kinase inhibitor barasertib, on osteosarcoma cell lines after 72 h of treatment using the CCK8 assay. The results demonstrated that tinengotinib exhibited superior inhibitory effects on the osteosarcoma cells (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1c). To further evaluate the in vitro anti-kinase activity of tinengotinib, we used western blotting to detect the autophosphorylation of Aurora A/B and the phosphorylation of downstream signaling histone H3 in MNNG cells treated with tinengotinib. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1a, tinengotinib effectively inhibited the Aurora pathway, which is consistent with findings from triple negative breast cancer (TNBC) studies19. Moreover, it induced significant degradation of the MYC protein in osteosarcoma cells, underscoring its potent therapeutic potential.

Next, to evaluate the antitumor efficacy of tinengotinib in vivo, we established an orthotopic osteosarcoma model by injecting human 143B osteosarcoma cells into the tibial medullary cavities of immunodeficient mice. Animals were randomized into two cohorts receiving either 15 mg/kg tinengotinib or a vehicle control via daily oral gavage. As demonstrated in Fig. 1d, tinengotinib treatment significantly reduced tumor weight compared with the vehicle control (P = 0.038). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis revealed that tinengotinib treatment promoted apoptosis and DNA damage, as evidenced by increased expression of cleaved caspase-3 (CC3) and phospho-histone H2A.X (γ-H2AX), but significantly suppressed proliferation and reduced Ki-67 expression in treated tumors (Fig. 1e). Most importantly, we validated the inhibitory effect in a MYC-overexpressed patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model, and the results showed that, compared with the vehicle, 15 mg/kg tinengotinib dramatically blunted tumor growth (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1f–g).

Tinengotinib synergizes with immuno-checkpoint blockade in osteosarcoma

Our previous studies revealed that MYC-driven osteosarcoma, which is associated with the poorest prognosis, is characterized by two key features: MYC overexpression and a weak immune response11. The MYC oncogenic pathway facilitates tumor immune evasion through microenvironmental remodeling14, with emerging evidence indicating direct transcriptional and posttranscriptional regulation of PD-L1 expression by MYC14,26–28.

Our experimental findings revealed that tinengotinib treatment upregulated PD-L1 expression in osteosarcoma cells (Supplementary Fig. S1b) while potently inhibiting the clonogenic survival of murine LM-8 osteosarcoma cells (C3H mouse-derived line) in vitro (Supplementary Fig. S1c). We used subcutaneous LM-8/C3H murine models to assess the antitumor efficacy of tinengotinib, anti-PD-1, and their combination. Compared with single-agent treatments, tinengotinib/anti-PD-1 combination therapy demonstrated superior antitumor efficacy, resulting in maximal tumor shrinkage (P = 0.034 vs tinengotinib; P < 0.001 vs anti-PD-1) (Fig. 1h–j). Immunohistochemical analysis showed that both tinengotinib monotherapy and PD1 combination therapy significantly reduced MYC expression in tumor tissues (Supplementary Fig. S1d), and the combined treatment increased the infiltration of CD4+ and CD8 + T lymphocytes in tumors (Supplementary Fig. S1e and S1f), indicating enhanced anti-tumor immune response.

These preclinical data suggest that tinengotinib exerts potent therapeutic activity against osteosarcoma through direct tumor growth inhibition while simultaneously priming the tumor immune microenvironment for enhanced PD-1 checkpoint blockade efficacy. The observed therapeutic synergy positions tinengotinib as a promising backbone therapy for precision medicine approaches targeting MYC-driven osteosarcoma, particularly when integrated with immune checkpoint inhibition strategies.

Characteristics of enrolled patients

Based on the molecular subtyping of osteosarcoma and preclinical studies of tinengotinib, we conducted a pilot umbrella clinical trial. A total of 22 patients with refractory metastatic osteosarcoma were screened from September 2021 to April 2023; 2 patients withdrew informed consent, 1 patient experienced rapid disease progression during screening, and 19 patients were ultimately included. Among the 19 patients, 5 were enrolled in Arm A (S-IS), 3 in Arm B (S-HRD), and 11 in Arm C (S-MD). S-IA cases were not included because these patients had a lower likelihood of progressing to metastatic, refractory status. The baseline patient demographics and disease characteristics are shown in Table 1. The median age of 19 enrolled patients was 17 years (range, 8–47 years), and 73.4% were male. Primary tumors are located in the pelvis (5.3%), upper limb (26.3%), and lower limb (68.4%). Thirteen (68.4%) patients had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status score of 1, while the remaining 6 (31.6%) had a score of 2. Patients with a severe tumor burden and chemotherapy resistance were included. At baseline, all patients had metastases, and 3 or more metastases were observed in 15.8% of the patients. These patients were heavily pretreated (median of 3 previous antitumor regimens (range, 1–4)), and most had received methotrexate (89.5%), doxorubicin (89.5%), cisplatin (95%), and ifosfamide (89.5%) before enrollment. Therapeutic efficacy in each arm

Patients were enrolled into one of the following arms based on their IHC and whole-exome sequencing (WES) results: (A) S-IS subtype, anti-PD-1 (tislelizumab) with gemcitabine and docetaxel; (B) S-HRD subtype, PARP inhibitor (pamiparib) with temozolomide; (C) tinengotinib (Fig. 2).

The therapeutic efficacy in each arm is illustrated in Fig. 3a. The enrolled patients underwent at least one postbaseline assessment. The median duration of follow-up at the time of cutoff (January 20th, 2025) was 147 days (interquartile range [IQR], 43–1265 days). The median overall survival (OS) was 149 (95% CI: 90–185) days (Fig. 3b). At the cutoff time, only 2 of the 19 patients were alive. Malignant progression of the tumor occurs quickly in most patients because of the severity and advanced stage of the disease. The median progression-free survival (PFS) was 50 (95% CI: 33–89) days; the estimated probability of PFS was 31.6% (95% CI: 8.36–54.8) at 12 weeks (Fig. 3c). Eleven patients enrolled in arm C were assigned to the tinengotinib treatment group, with a median OS of 100 (95% CI: 57–167) days (Fig. 3b). The median PFS was 57 (95% CI: 33–98) days; the estimated probability of PFS was 45.5% (95% CI: 11.2–not reached) at 12 weeks (Fig. 3c). Patient S003, who was treated with 5 mg of tinengotinib, had a favorable outcome. The first computed tomography (CT) evaluation revealed that the lung metastases had formed cavities (Fig. 4c). After one month of treatment with 8 mg of tinengotinib, two of the lung metastases in patient S010 had shrunk significantly and could be assessed as a PR, but unfortunately, another had progressed, resulting in an overall assessment of PD (Fig. 4d). These results indicate that tinengotinib has promising antitumor activity against lung metastases in certain osteosarcoma patients. The varied responses observed in lung metastases may stem from differences in MYC expression or driver status among tumor clones, or they might reflect unique challenges associated with imaging-based evaluations in osteosarcoma.

Genomic analysis of enrolled patients revealed significant heterogeneity

To characterize the genomic profiles of the study cohort comprehensively, we conducted an analysis of WES data from the patients. Due to sampling limitations, we were unable to obtain tumor tissues at the time of enrollment. MYC amplification drives immunosuppressive niche remodeling in osteosarcoma

Osteosarcoma multiomic molecular subtyping revealed that the MYC-driven subtype exhibits a “cold” phenotype. Immunologically, S-MD osteosarcomas show markedly low immune infiltration, reduced expression of immune checkpoint molecules (including PD-L1 and FOXP3), diminished T-cell receptor (TCR) diversity, and lymphocyte depletion11. These characteristics reflect a profoundly immunosuppressed tumor microenvironment, contributing to the aggressive clinical behavior observed in this subtype.

Owing to the pivotal role of MYC in shaping the tumor immune landscape, investigating the immunological differences between MYC-amplified and MYC-wildtype (MYC-WT) osteosarcomas is crucial. We further analyzed data from the Shanghai General Hospital Osteosarcoma (SGH-OS) cohort, using surgically resected tissues obtained after neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and divided the patients into two groups based on MYC status. The immunosuppressive landscape of MYC-amplified osteosarcomas was systematically characterized through multiplatform analyses. As shown in Fig. 6b, computational deconvolution of bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data via the ESTIMATE algorithm demonstrated significantly attenuated immune and stromal scores in MYC-amplified osteosarcomas. Immune cell profiling through CIBERSORT analysis (Fig. 6c) further revealed distinct microenvironmental alterations, with MYC-amplified osteosarcomas exhibiting reduced cytotoxic CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and neutrophil prevalence, coupled with increased proportions of M0 macrophages and resting memory CD4⁺ T cells. Complementing these findings, Fig. 6d illustrates diminished T-cell clonal diversity in MYC-amplified osteosarcomas, as evidenced by TRUST-based TCR repertoire analysis, which revealed significantly reduced TCR clone numbers. Collectively, these multimodal findings delineate an immune-depleted ecological niche in MYC-amplified osteosarcomas, emphasizing the need to develop microenvironment-reprogramming strategies for this immunologically quiescent malignancy subtype.

Transcriptomic pathway profiling revealed a distinct transcriptional landscape distinguishing MYC-amplified from MYC-WT osteosarcomas. Comprehensive pathway enrichment analysis via Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) with the Molecular Signature Database (MsigDB) Hallmark gene set demonstrated significant pathway dysregulation in MYC-amplified osteosarcomas. Striking downregulation was observed in key immunomodulatory pathways, including the interferon-α/γ response and the inflammatory, complement, and TNFα/NF-κB-mediated pathways (Fig. 6e). Conversely, MYC-amplified osteosarcomas exhibited marked activation of cell cycle regulatory mechanisms, particularly MYC-targeted V1/V2, E2F-mediated proliferation pathways, and G2/M checkpoint control (Fig. 6e).

Discussion

Here, we present a molecular subtyping-guided umbrella trial for refractory metastatic osteosarcoma, representing a significant yet preliminary therapeutic exploration. The integration of molecular subtyping with an umbrella trial design represents a transformative approach for addressing the profound heterogeneity of osteosarcoma. This study not only establishes the clinical feasibility of precision therapy for MYC-driven osteosarcoma, a subtype associated with a poor prognosis, but also reveals the immunosuppressive properties of MYC-amplified tumors. Our findings bridge preclinical validation, clinical exploration, and genomic profiling, offering a multidimensional roadmap for targeted intervention for refractory metastatic osteosarcoma.

MYC amplification, a defining feature of aggressive osteosarcoma, has historically posed therapeutic challenges because of the intrinsically disordered protein structure of MYC and the absence of enzymatic pockets or catalytic activity14,15. Tinengotinib overcomes this limitation by degrading the MYC protein and suppressing osteosarcoma progression in preclinical models. Unlike traditional kinase inhibitors that indirectly regulate MYC transcription, tinengotinib directly targets Aurora kinase to destabilize MYC oncoproteins. This approach aligns with contemporary strategies focusing on oncoprotein destabilization29,30, while its synergy with anti-PD-1 therapy in our experimental models highlights the dual suppression of tumor growth and immune escape. Notably, recent preclinical studies in TNBC models demonstrated superior tumor growth suppression with tinengotinib/PD-L1 inhibitor combination therapy compared with monotherapies19, suggesting the therapeutic potential of tinengotinib and immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) combination approaches for treating MYC amplified/overexpressed tumors. Recent advances in MYC-targeted therapies have further validated this direction. OMO-103, a leading MYC inhibitor, has completed a first-in-human phase I trial for advanced solid tumors and is now progressing to a phase II trial (NCT06650514) specifically for high-grade osteosarcoma31. The unique mechanism of action and preclinical efficacy of tinengotinib position it as a promising candidate within this evolving therapeutic landscape.

Our genomic profiling identified distinct immunological features in MYC-amplified osteosarcomas. These tumors showed higher tumor purity, increased aneuploidy, and elevated HRD scores - genomic markers of aggressive disease. Immunologically, they exhibited reduced TCR clonality and CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, along with increased M0 macrophages and resting memory CD4⁺ T cells. These findings suggest MYC amplification may promote an immunosuppressive microenvironment in osteosarcoma through altered immune cell composition. The observed patterns are consistent with known mechanisms of MYC-mediated immune evasion via cytokine/chemokine signaling, but provide new evidence specific to osteosarcoma biology32–34. Notably, dysregulated MYC upregulates the expression of nuclear aurora kinase A, which in turn induces PD-L1 expression and CD47 upregulation27,35. However, while enhancing PD-L1 expression, tinengotinib synergistically potentiated anti-PD-1 efficacy in our preclinical models. These findings suggest that the inhibitor may remodel the tumor immune landscape beyond direct PD-L1 modulation, potentially through concurrent MYC degradation and aurora kinase inhibition. Our findings provide mechanistic support for the combination of tinengotinib with immune checkpoint blockade in MYC-driven osteosarcoma.

Based on our preclinical evidence, a phase Ib/II trial of tinengotinib in combination with anti-PD-1 therapy could prioritize the following patient populations: (1) those with MYC-amplified or overexpressing osteosarcoma, confirmed by central FISH/IHC testing; (2) patients who have progressed after at least one line of standard therapy; and (3) cases demonstrating PD-L1 positivity (Combined Positive Score (CPS) ≥ 1) or the presence of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. Key endpoints would include safety and tolerability (primary for phase Ib) and objective response rate by RECIST 1.1, with 16-weeks progression-free survival as co-primary endpoints for phase II.

The therapeutic landscape for refractory osteosarcoma remains challenging, as evidenced by a systematic review showing only 21% of 58 clinical trials achieving positive endpoints, with most successes confined to multi-targeted kinase inhibitor studies36. In this context, our observed median PFS of 50 days (95% CI: 33–89) and OS of 149 days (95% CI: 90–185) demonstrate meaningful clinical activity, which are comparable to historical benchmarks in treatment-refractory osteosarcoma37–40. For appropriate historical comparison, we selected studies that met the following three criteria: (1) exclusive inclusion of recurrent or refractory populations, (2) similar pretreatment patient profiles, and (3) comparable radiographic assessment schedules. In patients with refractory osteosarcoma treated with the anti-PD-L1/TGF-β antibody TQB2858, the median PFS was 45 days37. In a phase II study of eribulin in patients with recurrent or refractory osteosarcoma, the median PFS was 38 days38. A phase I study of GD2 CAR-T cells in patients with recurrent or refractory osteosarcoma demonstrated a median progression-free survival of approximately 55 days39. In a phase I/II study of combination immunotherapy approach consisting of GD2BATs, interleukin-2 (IL-2), and GM-CSF in refractory/recurrent osteosarcoma, the median OS was 139 days40.

While the outcomes of this pilot umbrella trial appear modest, biomarker-stratified analysis revealed critical insights:

(1) Although the immune suppressed patients in arm A did not demonstrate a significant improvement in PFS compared to other treatment arms, it exhibited a notably prolonged OS. A subset of these patients also displayed favorable responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), underscoring the importance of molecular subtyping in guiding osteosarcoma therapeutic strategies. In contrast, the remaining two molecular subtypes patients showed poorer clinical outcomes, potentially attributable to their “cold tumor” phenotype characterized by sparse immune cell infiltration. Future therapeutic strategies for these subtypes may require combinatorial approaches to augment tumor immunogenicity and enhance immune cell infiltration, thereby overcoming therapeutic resistance.

(2) The S-HRD arm (Arm B) evaluation of pamiparib-temozolomide combination yielded unexpectedly limited clinical benefit (median PFS: 27 days; OS: 71 days) despite compelling preclinical efficacy in HRD-subtype PDX models11. Several factors may account for this translational gap: First, the heavily pretreated nature of this refractory population (median 3 prior therapies) likely contributed to both diminished treatment response and heightened toxicity profiles, as evidenced by the high-grade AE incidence (66.7% vomiting, 33.3% diarrhea). Second, growing evidence shows that HRD can be reversed, indicating that HRD detected at baseline may not remain stable throughout disease progression, thus limiting its predictive value for treatment response41. Meanwhile, PDX models tend to retain highly proliferative, HRD clones, which may overestimate sensitivity to DDR inhibitors and contribute to discrepancies between preclinical and clinical outcomes. These observations highlight critical considerations for future HRD-targeted trials: earlier-line intervention before genomic instability accumulation, incorporation of functional HRD assays beyond static genomic signatures, and optimized combination strategies to overcome compensatory DNA repair pathways. While the current results are disappointing, they provide essential clinical insights that were unavailable from preclinical models alone.

(3) MYC-amplified osteosarcomas in arm C showed delayed progression versus tumors of other molecular subtypes, although the statistical power was limited by cohort size (n = 19). Importantly, tinengotinib demonstrated superior tolerability to other Aurora kinase inhibitors, with notably lower rates of hypertension and cytopenia42–47, warranting prioritization in expanded trials.

This molecularly guided umbrella trial for osteosarcoma reveals both opportunities and challenges in precision oncology. Three limitations merit emphasis: (1) the small sample size limits the conclusions about subtype-specific efficacy, constraining statistical power and potentially introducing biases in these exploratory findings; (2) the absence of a control arm complicates the interpretation of survival benefits; and (3) the spatial resolution of the microenvironment remodeling via tinengotinib requires single-cell or spatial transcriptomic analyses. Future directions should establish standardized MYC activity biomarkers (e.g., target gene signatures) for patient selection and optimize combination strategies, particularly tinengotinib/anti-PD-1 regimens for MYC-driven tumors.

By integrating molecular subtyping, genomics, and clinical validation, this study provides a multidimensional chain of evidence from bench to bed side for the precision treatment of osteosarcoma. Our findings reinforce the universal relevance of molecular classification in solid tumors, particularly for malignancies marked by extreme heterogeneity and therapeutic resistance. The success of tinengotinib—a first-in-class multikinase inhibitor with MYC-destabilizing activity—in preclinical and early clinical settings underscores the feasibility of targeting historically “undruggable” oncoproteins such as MYC through innovative strategies such as protein degradation. This approach not only circumvents traditional limitations in direct MYC inhibition but also reveals opportunities to synergize with immune-modulating therapies, as evidenced by the dual antitumor and immunogenic effects of these compounds.

Furthermore, our umbrella trial design highlights both the promise and challenges of precision oncology in high-heterogeneity cancers. While molecular subtype-guided allocation enables personalized treatment matching, the observed variability in patient responses emphasizes the need for dynamic biomarker adaptation and combinatorial strategies. While these insights support broader adoption of mechanism-driven trial architectures in rare cancers, oversimplification of oncogenic complexity should be avoided. Ultimately, this work exemplifies the transformative potential of bench-to-bedside translation when grounded in robust molecular stratification. As the field advances, prioritizing noncanonical targeting modalities and adaptive trial platforms will be critical to overcoming the therapeutic stagnation that has long plagued osteosarcoma and similar intractable malignancies.

Methods

Cell lines

The human osteosarcoma cell lines 143B, MNNG/HOS, U2OS, HOS, and murine osteosarcoma cell line LM-8 were obtained from American Type Cell Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), and all the cell lines were cultured in DMEM (BasalMedia, L110KJ) supplemented with 10% FBS (ExCell, FSP500) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122). The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator.

Cell viability assay and colony formation

Osteosarcoma cell lines were cultured in 96-well plates at a density of 2000 cells/well overnight and then treated with 0.1% DMSO, tinengotinib, ENMD-2076 (MedChemExpress, HY-10987A), MLN8237 (MedChemExpress, HY-10971), or barasertib (MedChemExpress, HY-10127) at the indicated concentrations. After incubation for 72 h, the cells were incubated with CCK8 (Yeasen, 40203ES92) working solution for 2 h at 37°C. The resulting absorbance was measured at 450 nm. For the colony formation assay, the cells were plated in 6-well plates at 1000 cells/well and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Treatments with tinengotinib or 0.1% DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, 276855) were administered for 7 days. Colonies were fixed with methanol and stained with 0.5% crystal violet for quantification.

Western blot

Cells and tumor tissues were lysed with radioimmunoprecipitation (RIPA) buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Roche, 04693132001). The protein extracts were separated via SDS‒PAGE and then transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (PALL BioTrace, 66485) for western blotting. The bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence detection reagents. When detecting changes in AURORA pathway proteins, cells were stimulated with 100 nmol/L nocodazole (MedChemExpress, HY-13520) for 16 h, followed by treatment with tenogotinib for 24 h. The primary antibodies used included GAPDH (ABclonal, AC002), β-actin (ABclonal, AC004), c-MYC (ABclonal, A19032), PD-L1 (Abcam, ab213480), PD-L1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 13648), Phospho-Histone H3 (Cell Signaling Technology, 3377 s), Histone H3 (Abcam, ab1791-100), Aurora A (Cell Signaling Technology, 14475S), Aurora B (Cell Signaling Technology, 3094S), Phospho-Aurora A (Thr288)/Aurora B (Thr232)/Aurora C (Thr198) (Cell Signaling Technology, 2914S).

Mouse xenograft models

All the animal experimental studies were approved by the Animal Investigation Committee of the Shanghai General Hospital. Mice were maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle at a constant temperature of 22 °C and a relative humidity of 55%. All procedures were approved by the ethics committee of the Shanghai General Hospital Animal Care and Use Committee. The maximal tumor burden was not greater than that permitted by the ethics committee. Animals were anesthetized with intraperitoneal pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg) and euthanized by overdose of pentobarbital (150 mg/kg, i.p.).

For the 143B xenograft model, 143B tumor cells (1 × 106) were suspended in sterile PBS (20 μL) and injected into the medullary cavity of the tibia of each mouse (BALB/c-nu, female, 4 weeks). Seven days later, the mice were randomly divided into a control group (n = 5) and a treatment group (n = 5), and the mice were treated with 0.5% methylcellulose (MC) diluted in ddH2O or tinengotinib (15 mg/kg) orally daily. All the mice were evaluated at the indicated times to determine whether they had developed tumors. The mice were subsequently sacrificed, and the posterior limbs were excised for weighing and immunohistochemical analysis.

For the human osteosarcoma PDX model, the samples were cut into 2 mm × 2 mm blocks in tissue culture medium and then implanted subcutaneously into the abdominal wall of BALB/c-nu mice. The mice were randomized into vehicle or treatment group when the tumors reached approximately 50 mm3 and were then treated with 0.5% MC or tinengotinib (15 mg/kg) p.o. daily. The tumor volume was calculated every 3 days by the following standard formula: length × width2/2. After 43 days of treatment, all the mice were sacrificed, and the tumors were harvested and weighed.

For the LM-8 xenograft model, LM-8 tumor cells (1 × 106/20 μL) were injected into the abdominal wall of C3H mice (female, 4 weeks). The mice were randomized into 4 groups (n = 5) when the tumors reached approximately 100 mm3: (i) placebo: hamster IgG 10 mg/kg i.p. every 3 days for a total of five doses; (ii) tinengotinib: 8 mg/kg orally daily until the end of the study; (iii) anti-PD-1: anti-mouse PD-1 mAbJ43 (BioXCell) intraperitoneally at 10 mg/kg every 3 days for a total of five doses; or (iv) a combination of tinengotinib with anti-PD-1. After 28 days of treatment, all the mice were sacrificed, and the tumors were weighed and subjected to immunohistochemical analysis.

H&E staining and immunohistochemistry

Tumors from the mice were sectioned and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight before being embedded in paraffin. The 4-μm sections were deparaffinized for hematoxylin and eosin staining (H&E) staining and IHC according to standard protocols. The tumor specimens were immunostained for Ki-67 (Abcam, ab16667), CC3 (Cell Signaling Technology, 9661), γ-H2AX (Cell Signaling Technology, 9718), CD4 (Cell Signaling Technology, 25229), and CD8 (Cell Signaling Technology, 98941). Images were captured using a microscope (Leica Microsystems).

Patients and study design

We recruited patients with refractory metastatic osteosarcoma at Shanghai General Hospital from September 2021 to April 2023. The eligibility criteria for patients were as follows: (1) aged between 8 and 70 years with a histologically confirmed diagnosis of primary osteosarcoma; (2) locally advanced, recurrent, metastatic osteosarcoma that could not be treated with radical surgery; (3) refractory osteosarcoma (the existing treatment has failed), specific definition: failed to respond to standard chemotherapy drugs, including methotrexate, ifosfamide, doxorubicin, and cisplatin; (4) at least 1 measurable lesion according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 (RECIST v1.1)48; and (5) ECOG performance status of 0–2; (6) the main organ functions are basically normal; (7) the subjects volunteered to join the study and signed the informed consent form with good compliance and follow-up. The exclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (1) chemotherapy or immunotherapy three weeks before treatment; (2) history of clinically important or uncontrolled heart diseases; (3) ongoing adverse events ≥ grade 2 due to previous treatment; (4) requiring long-term use of glucocorticoids and immunosuppressants; (5) any other condition deemed by the investigator to significantly increase the risk of participation.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was approved by the Shanghai General Hospital Ethics Committee, number [2021]042. This study was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR) on August 24, 2020, with the registration number ChiCTR2000036618. Most patients underwent initial surgery at Shanghai General Hospital, where tumor tissues were subjected to routine WES analysis. Other patients who underwent surgery at other hospitals were enrolled on the basis of the immunohistochemical results of their tumor tissues.

This was an open-label, single-arm, exploratory umbrella clinical trial. The trial aimed to enroll patients with osteosarcoma whose first-line chemotherapy failed. The participants were molecularly subtyped on the basis of their genomic and expression profile characteristics, with initial stratification into three treatment groups corresponding to three subtypes. Patients who consented to participate in the umbrella trial were assigned to one of these three treatment arms on the basis of their IHC and WES results. MYC IHC serves as a complementary diagnostic tool, building on our prior findings that demonstrated its utility in distinguishing S-MD from other subtypes11.

Patients were divided into the following arms on the basis of their IHC and WES results: arm A, S-IS subtype, MYC IHC ( < 3 + ) and HRD score < 42; arm B, S-HRD subtype, MYC IHC ( < 3 + ) and HRD score > 42; and arm C, MYC-driven subtype (S-MD), MYC IHC (3 + ). The central pathological examination of tumor specimens was conducted by the Department of Pathology at Shanghai General Hospital. The MYC status was independently verified by two experienced pathologists using immunohistochemical analysis.

Study treatments

In the S-IS subtype (arm A), elevated immune-related signaling pathways are observed, but the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment results in decreased T-cell infiltration11. Guided by this rationale, arm A of this umbrella trial employed ICIs(tislelizumab) combined with gemcitabine and docetaxel to reverse T-cell dysfunction and amplify antitumor immunity. Tislelizumab was administered at a dose of 200 mg i.v. on day 1, gemcitabine was administered at a dose of 675 mg/m2 i.v. on days 1 and 7, and docetaxel was administered at a dose of 75 mg/m2. One treatment cycle lasted 21 days.

The S-HRD subtype (arm B) exhibits genomic instability marked by elevated HRD scores, a consequence of defective DNA double-strand break repair11. To leverage synthetic lethality in HRD-positive tumors, arm B combined the PARP inhibitor pamiparib with temozolomide, aiming to potentiate DNA damage response targeting. Pamiparib was administered at a dose of 40 mg orally twice daily. Temozolomide was administered at a dose of 75 mg/m2 orally once daily on days 1 to 7, starting on the second cycle. One treatment cycle lasted 21 days.

For the S-MD subtype (arm C), MYC-driven hyperproliferation mediated by overexpression poses therapeutic challenges because of the undruggable nature of MYC11. Arm C addressed this issue by administering tinengotinib, a novel multikinase inhibitor that targets Aurora kinase and MYC-associated pathways, thereby disrupting mitotic signaling and transcriptional regulation to curb malignant progression. Patients were divided into three cohorts according to body surface area (BSA): cohort A (age ≤ 18, BSA ≤ 1.5 m2), cohort B (age ≤ 18, BSA > 1.5 m2) and cohort C (age > 18). Tinengotinib was administered at a dose of 5 mg (cohort A), 8 mg (cohort B), or 10 mg (cohort C). One treatment cycle lasted 28 days. Tinengotinib (TT-00420) was provided by TransThera Sciences (Nanjing), Inc.

End points

Assessment of response was performed according to RECIST v1.1. Patients were treated until disease progression or intolerable drug side effects occurred. Follow-up evaluations were based on CT and physical examination every month according to RECIST 1.1. The primary endpoint was the 12-week progression-free survival rate. The secondary endpoints included PFS, OS, and safety outcomes. The starting point for calculating OS and PFS was the date of patient enrollment.

Safety

The safety evaluation population included all patients who received ≥ 1 dose of any of the investigational treatment components. Adverse events and serious adverse events were assessed in accordance with the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0 (CTCAE V5.0)49. Treatment-Emergent Adverse Events (TEAEs): Any adverse event that first occurred or worsened in severity after the first dose of the study treatment, regardless of suspected causality. Treatment-Related Adverse Events (TRAEs): A subset of TEAEs where a causal relationship with the study treatment was suspected, as assessed by the investigators.

WES and data analysis

WES library preparation and data analysis were performed according to our previous study11. Briefly, WES libraries were prepared and captured via an Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon V6 Kit (Agilent Technologies) and a KAPA Library Preparation Kit (Roche Diagnostics, Rotkreuz, Switzerland) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. An Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies) with a DNA chip was used to perform quality control. After quantification with a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), the WES libraries were run on a MGISEQ 2000 sequencer (MGI, Shenzhen, China) or an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system (San Diego, CA, USA).

After quality control, the sequencing reads were mapped to the UCSC hg19 reference genome with the Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (bwa mem, v0.7.17)50. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) duplicates were removed by using Picard (v2.18.11). The BAM files were then indexed by SAMtools (v1.9)51. Base quality score recalibration (GATK) was performed by employing the Base Recalibrator and ApplyBQSR tools from the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK, v4.0.7.0) according to GATK best practices52. Somatic mutations, including single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small insertions and deletions (INDELs), were identified using Mutect2 in GATK on processed exome data of matched tumor and white blood cell samples. Variants were annotated by ANNOVAR (v2019/04) on the Refseq gene model53. Copy number variations (CNVs) were detected by using CNVkit (v) with default parameters54.

Multiomic data of MYC-amplified and MYC-WT osteosarcomas were extracted from the SGH-OS cohort11. The tumor purity, ploidy, and HRD score were derived from WES data as reported in our previous study. Additionally, gene expression profiles as well as immune infiltration estimations, including ESTIMATE scores (v1.0.13)55, immune cell fractions estimated by CIBERSORT56, and TCR clone types inferred from TRUST (v3.0)57-based RNA-seq data, were also obtained. The genes differentially expressed between MYC-amplified and MYC-WT osteosarcomas were identified via the DESeq2 R package (v1.28.1)58. GSEA was performed by GSEA software (v4.0.3)59 using the Hallmark60 gene sets from the MsigDB, v7.1.

Statistical analysis

For the preclinical studies, all experiments were repeated three times, and the data are expressed as the means ± SDs. The statistical significance of differences was determined by two-way ANOVA. The primary efficacy analysis population included all eligible patients enrolled in the study. Safety was analyzed in all patients who received at least one dose of the study medication. The data cutoff for the analyses reported here was January 20, 2025. The Kaplan‒Meier method was used to estimate PFS and OS with 95% CIs. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (v8.02) and R packages (v4.0.2). Asterisks indicate significance levels (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001).
