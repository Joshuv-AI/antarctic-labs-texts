Influence of three antiseptic mouthwashes on microtensile bond strength of self-etch adhesive to dentin: Rinsing versus non-rinsing with water
Authors: Nichaya Songwathana, Salunya Tancharoen, Piyapanna Pumpaluk
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe purpose of this study was to examine the effect of several antiseptic mouthwashes on the dentin microtensile bond strength of self-etch adhesive, considering the influence of rinsing with water.MethodsForty flat dentin surfaces were produced by sectioning sound extracted third molars. They were randomly divided into four main groups: (1) artificial saliva (control); (2) 1 % hydrogen peroxide; (3) 0.2 % povidone-iodine; and (4) 0.12 % chlorhexidine. Each group was tested with and without rinsing with distilled water. Subsequently, a nanofilled resin composite was applied to the bonded dentin using the self-etch adhesive for producing resin-dentin sticks. The specimens were sectioned for the microtensile bond strength assessment. Failure modes were evaluated using a stereomicroscope. The dentin surfaces treated with three mouthwashes were analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer.ResultsThe rinsing control group exhibited significantly greater microtensile bond strength than the povidone-iodine and chlorhexidine groups. However, it did not differ from the hydrogen peroxide group. The non-rinsing groups displayed no significant differences in bond strength, except for povidone-iodine, which showed lower value than the remaining groups. In all the rinsing groups, noticeably higher microtensile bond strengths were observed when compared to the non-rinsing groups in all types of antiseptic mouthwashes.ConclusionAntiseptic mouthwashes affected the dentin microtensile bond strength of self-etch adhesive. Rinsing with water significantly enhanced the bond strength. Hydrogen peroxide exhibited the greatest microtensile bond strength in the rinsing group, while chlorhexidine and hydrogen peroxide performed effectively in the non-rinsing group.

Introduction

The application of antiseptic mouthwashes prior to dental treatment has gained critical importance in the wake of the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic. The American Dental Association and the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention have suggested the use of a 1 % hydrogen peroxide mouthwash and a 0.2 % povidone-iodine solution prior to dental procedures. Additionally, the New Zealand Dental Association advises a 30-s rinse with 1 % hydrogen peroxide, 0.2 % povidone-iodine, or 0.12 % chlorhexidine [1].

Hydrogen peroxide is recognized for its powerful oxidizing properties, which allow effective eradication of various bacteria and resistance to a range of viral infections. This compound has proven effective against COVID-19 due to the virus's sensitivity to oxidative agents [2]. Povidone-iodine is a widely used antimicrobial that neutralizes numerous microorganisms [3]. Chlorhexidine is also frequently recommended as an antimicrobial rinse; however, its effectiveness against coronaviruses may not be as robust as other mouthwash options [4]. Some research suggests that chlorhexidine may provide a safe alternative for reducing the risk of COVID-19 transmission, particularly for patients who are intolerant to iodine [5].

Recent research has focused on the adhesive strength between resin composites and enamel or dentin following exposure to antiseptic mouthwashes. A study discovered that the adhesive performance of etch-and-rinse universal adhesives on enamel surfaces was weakened by hydrogen peroxide and povidone-iodine [6]. Moreover, chlorhexidine has been proven to reduce the bond strength between orthodontic brackets and enamel [7,8]. However, some studies indicated that neither povidone-iodine nor chlorhexidine significantly impacted the bond strength between orthodontic composites and enamel [9]. Moreover, another study reported that the types of adhesives and exposure time of mouthwashes did not impact the dentin shear bond strength [10].

Despite these findings, limited studies exist examining the effects of hydrogen peroxide, povidone-iodine, and chlorhexidine on the dentin microtensile bond strength of self-etch systems following rinsing with water. Residual mouthwash may remain on the enamel and/or exposed dentin after a patient rinses their mouth, potentially affecting the performance of dental adhesives on dentin surfaces. This is particularly relevant for non-carious cervical lesions, class V cavities with wedge-shaped defects, or subgingival cavity margins, where thorough rinsing and grinding with a diamond may be challenging. Residual mouthwash in these areas could interfere with adhesive performance.

Consequently, this study aimed to investigate the microtensile bond strength (μTBS) of self-etch adhesive applied to dentin exposed to three antiseptic mouthwashes. The hypothesis was that antiseptic mouthwashes would not cause significant differences in the bond strength of the self-etch adhesive to dentin.

Ethics consideration

This research study was granted approval by the Institutional Review Board Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry/Faculty of Pharmacy, Mahidol University (COE.No.MU-DT/PY-IRB 2022/038.1708). Informed consent requirements were not applicable as the teeth examined in this investigation were sourced from unidentified patients who underwent routine dental treatments, and were not related to any research protocol.

Antiseptic preparation

Hydrogen peroxide (Solvay Peroxythai Limited, Thailand) was used to prepare 1 % hydrogen peroxide mouthwash (K-Lab mouthwash, Thailand). Artificial saliva, povidone-iodine, and chlorhexidine were prepared from a commercially available solution (Table 1).

Specimen preparation

Forty sound, extracted maxillary and mandibular third molars devoid of caries, fillings, or enamel cracks were collected from anonymous patients. After extraction, the teeth were immersed in 0.1 % thymol solution. Soft tissue remnants were removed using hand instruments.

A diamond saw (Isomet, Buehler, Lake Bluff, Illinois, USA) was employed to create a flat dentin surface by sectioning below the dentin-enamel junction. The dentin surfaces were polished using 600-grit silicon carbide paper for 60 s using a polishing machine (Struers, Rotopol-21, Cleveland, OH, USA).

Groups 3–8 were immersed in antiseptic mouthwashes for 30 s, while the control groups (Groups 1 and 2) were immersed in artificial saliva (M-Dent; Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand) for the same duration prior to the bonding process. Following immersion, specimens from Groups 2, 4, 6, and 8 were not rinsed, whereas Groups 1, 3, 5, and 7 were rinsed with water for 10 s using a triple syringe. Before applying the adhesive, all the groups were dried with mild air sprayed for 10 s using a triple syringe.

Bonding was performed by applying the self-etch adhesive (Clearfil™ SE Bond, Kuraray Noritake Dental Inc., Japan) to the dentin surface following the standard protocol. A composite core with a thickness of 3 mm was constructed by placing two 1.5 mm layers of nanofilled resin composite (Filtek™ Z350 XT, 3M ESPE, Seefeld, Germany) with a plastic instrument (Hu Friedy, USA). The specimens were polymerized using a light-emitting diode (Bluephase®, Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan Liechtenstein) for 40 s with an intensity of 1100 mW/cm2 at room temperature. All the specimens were stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h.

Microtensile bond strength assessment

Resin-dentin sticks (1 × 1 × 6 mm3) were prepared by vertically sectioning the specimens using a diamond saw (Isomet, Illinois, USA) [11,12]. Thirty resin-dentin sticks per group (n = 30) underwent testing using a universal testing machine (Instron-5500R, USA) at a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm/min until failure [13].

Failure mode analysis

The fractured specimens were attached to aluminium stubs and covered with palladium for 150 s. A stereomicroscope (SMZ-2T, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) was used to examine the bonded failure surface at a voltage of 15 kV and magnification of 40 × . X-ray diffraction analysis

Elemental analysis identified the structural changes or residual elements from the antiseptic mouthwashes on the dentin surface. Nine flat dentin specimens were created following the same method in this study. One specimen served as a control with no exposure to the antiseptic solution, while the other eight specimens were immersed in antiseptic mouthwashes. Each was immersed for 30 s, followed by rinsing or non-rinsing with water. The specimens were gently dried by air-blow using a triple syringe. The specimens were submitted to an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Bruker AXS Model D8 Discover, USA) using CuKα radiation at 40 KV and 40 mA. Data were collected over the 2θ range of 10ᵒ- 60ᵒ in the coupled step-scan mode with a 0.02 step size and a 1-s measurement per step.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS® version 28.0 (SPSS Inc., IBM Corporation, NY, USA). A two-way analysis of variance analyzed the differences within and between groups, as data were normalized and showed variance homogeneity. Pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Bonferroni test, with significance set at 0.05. Mean (MPa) and standard deviation (SD) were presented after analysis.

Discussion

Based on the outcomes of this study, the choice of antiseptic mouthwash and rinsing technique significantly influenced the dentin microtensile bond strength of the self-etch adhesive, resulting in notable differences within and between the rinsing and non-rinsing groups. Consequently, the null hypothesis was rejected. Kutuk et al. (2021) [10] and Shirani et al. (2022) [14] reported the contrast results that mouthwashes did not impact the shear bond strength to dentin. However, Shirani et al. [14] observed that mouthwashes influenced enamel adhesion when etch-and-rinse adhesives were used. The inconsistencies in the results may be attributed to the different dental adhesives and bond strength testing methods employed in their respective studies.

Considering 1 % hydrogen peroxide, microtensile bond strengths in both the rinsing and non-rinsing groups were comparable to the control group, showing no significant differences. This finding was aligned with the previous study suggesting that the shear bond strength of dentin used with self-etch or etch-and-rinse universal adhesives did not affect by 1.5 % hydrogen peroxide [10]. However, prolonged exposure to 0.75 % and 1.5 % hydrogen peroxide daily for 15–30 days (30 s) may reduce the dentin microtensile bond strength of resin composites using a universal adhesive [15]. Interestingly, daily applications of hydrogen peroxide for 1–7 days caused no significant differences in bond strength compared to the control.

The application of 0.2 % povidone-iodine resulted in an inferior microtensile bond strength when compared to that of the control group in both the rinsing and non-rinsing conditions. Da Silva et al. (2006) [16] similarly reported that 2 % iodine disinfectant influenced the microtensile bond strength to dentin. However, it did not impact the bond strength when acetone-based adhesive system was used. They reported that the presence of glycerol as a carrier in iodine disinfectants was not completely eliminated even after rinsing with water. Residual glycerol can diffuse through acetone-based adhesives; however, it causes phase separation when ethanol and/or water-based adhesives are used, interfering with monomer penetration into dentin. Therefore, the reduction in the bond strength of the self-etch adhesive in this study could be related to its composition, which could be ethanol/water-based. Furthermore, Limsiriwong et al. (2023) [17] examined the impact of mouthwashes on the repair efficacy of resin composite on resin-matrix ceramic materials. They discovered that resin-matrix ceramics subjected to 0.2 % povidone-iodine exhibited the weakest bond strength of the repair material. However, surface roughening using hydrofluoric acid or sandblast could improve the bond strength. Another recent study stated that all mouthwashes caused a significant reduction in surface hardness of bulk-fill and conventional resin composites leading to an increase in the roughness and color values [18]. Particularly, 0.2 % povidone-iodine resulted in the most degradation due to its acidic pH. They further found that 0.2 % povidone-iodine had the lowest pH (3.30 ± 0.04) among the tested mouthwashes and required the highest volume of sodium hydroxide for titration, indicating a high level of acidity resulting in surface alterations in the materials. In contrast, earlier studies indicated that 0.2 % povidone-iodine had no influence the shear bond strength to dentin [10].

The application of 0.12 % chlorhexidine to dentin with no rinsing with water exhibited lower microtensile bond strength than that of the rinsing group. Additionally, it had lower bond strength than that of the control group after rinsing with water. This suggests that residual chlorhexidine on the dentin surface may negatively impact the microtensile bond strength. A systematic review indicated that some studies reported lower bond strength in groups pre-treated with chlorhexidine than control groups, while others found that chlorhexidine enhanced dentin bond strength durability [19]. The review concluded that this variability could be due to the experimental methods, differences in adhesive systems, and types of chlorhexidine utilized. Additionally, ethanol-based chlorhexidine has been shown to impact the bonding efficacy of one-step self-adhesive, while water-based chlorhexidine tends to promote bond stability [20]. Furthermore, Shafiei et al. (2013) [21] found that the application of chlorhexidine compromised the initial bond strength of self-etch adhesives. Chlorhexidine cations may bind to the phosphate anions contain in self-etch adhesives or calcium of the hydroxyapatite, obstructing the phosphate groups of 10-MDP from forming calcium salts on the dentin surface. This interference can lead to a decreased bonding efficacy of the adhesive to dentin.

The control group, which involved the use of artificial saliva along with rinsing, achieved the greatest microtensile bond strength among all groups. Moreover, the rinsing control group presented significantly higher microtensile bond strength than the non-rinsing control group. Several studies have been focused on the impact of the presence of artificial saliva on the bond strength to dentin. They concluded that artificial saliva contamination following primer application could reduce bond strength and cause microleakage of resin composites [22,23]; however, Shimadzu et al. (2014) [23] found that the saliva contamination did not affect the microleakage when restoring with glass ionomer cement.

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of all the groups displayed a uniform pattern similar to that of dentin, with peaks consistently positioned. This observation suggests that exposure to antiseptic mouthwashes and artificial saliva for 30 s did not affect the crystallinity of dentin. Despite some antiseptic mouthwashes, such as hydrogen peroxide and povidone-iodine, having oxidizing characteristics, their concentrations were insufficient to compromise the integrity of dental structures.

Within the limitation of this study, the specific self-etch adhesive chosen for this study may not produce similar results with other dental adhesives, due to varying protocols for the treatment of dentin surfaces and various adhesive compositions. Therefore, future studies should aim to replicate the actual conditions found in the oral cavity by utilizing various types and concentrations of antiseptic mouthwashes, alongside different adhesive systems and restorative materials applied at varying intervals. Such an approach could provide clearer insights into how antiseptic mouthwashes impact the bond strength to dentin. Additionally, while XRD detector was employed to assess the entire area of the specimen, the changes in crystallinity may have been restricted to the surface or localized regions. Thus, further exploration is warranted to evaluate the surface properties of the specimens.

Conclusions

In this study, artificial saliva (control group) demonstrated the highest microtensile bond strength compared to the antiseptic mouthwash groups. Among the rinsing groups, hydrogen peroxide exhibited the strongest microtensile bond strength. In the non-rinsing groups, both chlorhexidine and hydrogen peroxide demonstrated comparable microtensile bond strength levels, while povidone-iodine yielded the lowest bond strength. Across all groups, rinsing significantly improved microtensile bond strength compared to non-rinsing conditions. Although antiseptic mouthwashes did not compromise the structural integrity of the dentin, they reduced the microtensile bond strength of self-etch adhesive. These findings underscore the importance of thorough rinsing after using antiseptic mouthwashes to enhance adhesive performance. Clinicians should exercise caution and ensure complete rinsing before restoring cavities with exposed dentin.
