The impact of laughter yoga as a NIC on health parameters in nurses and nursing students: a systematic review
Authors: Özüm Erkin, Elem Kocaçal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLaughter yoga is a laughter exercise that combines yoga and breathing exercises (Pranayama). Laughter yoga decreases stress and burnout levels, depression and anxiety, salivary cortisol levels. The aim of the research is to systematically examine the studies on the effect of laughter yoga on health parameters in nurses and nursing students.MethodsSystematic review of RCTs and quasi-experimental studies. The PRISMA protocol was used as the basis for the conduct of the study. Google Scholar, Medline/PubMed, Cochrane Library, Science Direct, CINAHL Complete and OVID databases were used for study search. Inclusion criteria was based on PICOS. The quality of the studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute-Meta analysis Statistical Assessment and Review Instrument (JBI-MAStARI). 10 studies were included in the systematic review after those excluded due to reasons not meeting the inclusion criteria.ResultsIt was determined that the sample size was 50 or more in 80% of the studies. It was determined that the maximum number of laughter yoga sessions was 24 and at least 5 sessions. It has been observed that stress levels evaluated with different measurement tools decrease with the effect of laughter yoga in four studies. In three studies it was found that the level of burnout decreased after laughter yoga. In two studies in which salivary cortisol levels were evaluated also showed that laughter yoga reduced cortisol levels.ConclusionsIt was concluded that stress and burnout levels, depression and anxiety, salivary cortisol levels decreased especially after laughter yoga. In addition, it was concluded that laughter yoga had positive effects on psychological well-being, life satisfaction, subjective happiness increase in and general health in the groups where laughter yoga was practiced.Keywords: Laughter, Laughter yoga, Complementary therapies, Health parameters

Background

Because most of an individual’s life is spent in work or educational environments, negative situations that occur in these environments can affect health in different ways. In particular, the hospital environment, where life and death occur, is considered one of the most stressful working environments in which complex problems can occur [1]. Tension in these environments reduces academic success and job satisfaction in nurses and nursing students and can lead to negative effects such as personality change, occupational stress, burnout, and fatigue [2].

A healthy environment is important for both patients and nurses. In the Theory of Human Caring she developed, Watson stated that nurses suffer and wear out because they always care for others without participating in the loving care they need for themselves, provide care, and try to be there for them [3]. With this statement, Watson expressed how heavy a responsibility nurses are under and strive to provide optimal benefit in their work with this responsibility. After a while, this situation causes some physical and psychosocial problems in nurses and nursing students. Some symptoms such as anxiety, insomnia, social dysfunction and depression can be observed indicating that the individual’s physical and mental health is deteriorating [4].

Dimensions of general health are closely related to the daily life of the individual and any health impairment. Deterioration in general health also negatively affects the mental and emotional health of individuals. Therefore, appropriate and necessary interventions are important in maintaining the individual’s well-being. Some of the strategies to improve an individual’s general health include humour, laughing, and laughter yoga [2].

Laughter is a natural behavioural process carried out by the brain and a psychophysiological response to stimuli that causes the formation of facial expressions, muscle contractions, and other neurophysiological processes. This psychophysiological response reduces anxiety and stress, increases self-esteem, and improves problem-solving and creative thinking abilities. It has also been observed that it has a positive effect on helpfulness, compassion and friendship in an individual’s interpersonal relationships [4]. Studies on laughter yoga, especially for nurses, have also stated that it has the effect of reducing nurses’ role conflicts and work stress [5].

Other benefits of laughter include reducing blood pressure, increasing muscle flexion, strengthening the immune system by increasing the levels of infection-fighting T cells and beta cells and disease-fighting gamma interferon proteins. In addition, laughter creates a feeling of well-being by triggering the release of endorphins, which are the body’s natural painkillers; It is also one of the most natural methods of burning calories [6]. Laughter yoga was designed by an Indian doctor named Madan Kataria in 1995 [7].

Laughter yoga is a laughter exercise that combines yoga and breathing exercises (Pranayama). Laughter yoga sessions last approximately 40 min and consist of four steps. These steps consist of deep breathing exercises (5 min), warm-up exercises (10 min), childlike games (10 min), and laughter exercises (15 min). Although the first three steps of every laughter yoga session are similar, laughter exercises in the fourth step may vary [8]. “Umbrella” is an example of a laughter yoga exercise. This exercise is performed in groups of four or six people. A person imaginatively holds an umbrella open; other members of the group gather around the umbrella and laugh constantly, trying to get out of the rain. Laughter is performed for approximately 15–30 s and by repeating the words “ho, ho, ha, ha, ha” twice by the exercise leader. The laughter is artificial at first, but quickly turns into natural laughter. This laughter, which usually starts involuntarily, then contagiously turns into a voluntary laughter circle with other people in the group [3, 9, 10]. The fact that laughter has such positive physiological and psychological effects on certain aspects of health has made significant progress in the field of nursing.

Two researchers proposed laughter yoga to the Iowa University Nursing Interventions Classification (NIC) Committee as a new NIC, which was recognised by the NIC authority in 2023. Laughter yoga was published in the 2023 NIC version [11]. Moreover, different aspects of laughter used in the clinical environment to prevent unnecessary interventions or as another classical therapeutic and complementary treatment option need investigation. Therefore, this study aimed to systematically examine the effects of laughter yoga on nurses and nursing students. We hope that discussing the study results will be effective in popularising the use of laughter yoga as an alternative therapy method among nurses and nursing students.

Methods

Study design

In this systematic review, randomized controlled and experimental studies using laughter yoga were discussed and the study results were presented as a whole. The PRISMA protocol [12] was used as the basis for the conduct of the study until reporting (Table 1).

Search and study selection

While searching for studies and selecting them, the approach described in a systematic review by Eriksen and Frandsen (2018) [13]. A systematic research was conducted using six databases as Google Scholar, Medline/PubMed, Cochrane Library, Science Direct, CINAHL Complete and OVID databases. Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) were used for keyword selection. For each of these databases a complex search strategy was developed consisting of a combination of keywords and text words in different spellings connected to ‘’Laughter, Laughter Yoga, Nursing, Nursing Students’’. “Laughter Yoga” [Title/Abstract]) OR (“Laughter” [Title/Abstract])) OR (“Yoga, Laughter” [Title/Abstract])) OR (“Laughter, Laughter Yoga” [Title/Abstract])) AND “(Nursing” [Title/Abstract])) OR (“Nursing Students” [Title/Abstract])) OR (“Nurse” [Title/Abstract])) OR (“Nurse, Nursing Students” [Title/Abstract])) OR (“Student, Nursing” [Title/Abstract])).

When title and abstract did not have sufficient information for screening purposes, a full-text copy was retrieved as well. After that, all full texts were retrieved and screened again independently by both reviewers. Additionally, bibliography lists of all retrieved articles were searched for relevant studies. This systematic review was conducted between March and May 2023.

The inclusion criteria were based on PICOS (Population, Intervention, Compare, Outcome, and Study Design) (Fig. 1).

Assessment of risk of bias and methodological quality

In order to reduce the risk of possible bias during the study, literature review, selection of articles, data extraction and evaluation of article quality were carried out independently by two researchers (ÖE, EK). The risk of bias in the included studies was analyzed with the Cochrane Collaboration’s Risk of Bias Tool [14]. The two reviewers independently evaluated the quality of the included studies using the Cochrane Collaboration’s Risk of Bias Tool (Table 2). Any disagreements were resolved through consensus adjudication. This tool assesses six domains: six domains were evaluated, taking into account selection bias (random sequence generation, allocation concealment), performance bias (blinding of participants and personnel), detection bias (blinding of outcome assessment), attrition bias (incomplete outcome data), reporting bias (selective reporting) and other bias (anything else, ideally prespecified). The risk of bias for each domain was classified as “high,” “unclear,” or “low.” Studies with a low risk of bias are considered unlikely to have significant alterations in their results. Conversely, an unclear risk level raises doubts about the reliability of the study outcomes, while a high risk of bias significantly undermines the results’ reliability.

Additional criteria concerning methodology were size of population, application of power analysis, adequacy of statistical tests (e.g. control of premises or multiple testing) and selective outcome reporting (report of all assessed outcomes with specification of statistical data as the p-value).

After removing the duplicates, the same members of the team independently screened titles and abstracts of the articles to exclude those that did not meet the eligibility criteria. The same team members discussed disagreements about study eligibility until consensus was reached. A total of 1046 studies from Google Scholar (n = 602), Medline/PubMed (n = 64), Cochrane Library (n = 52) Science Direct (n = 102), CINAHL Complete (n = 132), and OVID (n = 94) were accessed as a result of an electronic search with keywords. From these articles, 10 studies were included in the systematic review after excluding those that they did not meet the inclusion criteria, had inappropriate titles and abstracts, were duplicated, or had inappropriate content (Fig. 2).

PRISMA flowchart of the research

The researchers (ÖE, EK) independently assessed the quality of the studies. These independent assessments were then compared, and a consensus was reached in case of different views. The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute-Meta-analysis Statistical Assessment and Review Instrument (JBI-MAStARI) (Table 2), a tool developed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) to assess the quality of experimental and quasi-experimental research [15, 16].

Data analysis

The PRISMA model was used for data evaluation and analyses. The table used in the systematic evaluation of the studies included the author/year/country, purpose of the study, data collection tools, and PICOS model subheadings (Table 1).

Due to the small number of identified studies and the heterogeneity among them, a meta-analysis of the results was not feasible. The data extracted from the studies were qualitatively synthesized and presented in narrative form.

Ethical considerations

In the studies included in the systematic review, there was no risk of harm to the researchers and no funding support was received for the systematic review. PICOS was used for inclusion and exclusion criteria [13] and PRISMA models were used for evaluation and synthesis. The methodological quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute-Meta analysis Statistic Assessment and Review Instrument [15, 16], risk of bias was assessed using Cochrane Collaboration’s Risk of Bias Tool [14].

Results

Study characteristics

Of the studies, 80% were conducted in 2019 and following years. Studies were conducted in countries such as Iran [2], United States of America [15], California [3], Egypt [4], Korea [5], India [6], Turkey [8, 18–20].

60% of the studies were conducted using quasi-experimental methods [2–6, 17]. Quasi-experimental studies were conducted in a pre- and post-test format. It was determined that 40% of the studies were conducted as randomized controlled trials [8, 18–20]. Randomised controlled studies discussed the effect of laughter yoga on life satisfaction, anxiety, psychological well-being [19], stress, depression, loneliness [20], salivary cortisol levels [18] in nursing students, burnout, perceived stress, and life satisfaction in nurses [8].

It was determined that the sample size was 50 or more in 80% of the studies and nursing students constituted the sample in 70% of the studies [2, 5, 6, 17–20]. In 60% of the studies, laughter yoga sessions were conducted face to face [2–5, 17, 18]. It was determined that the maximum number of laughter yoga sessions was 24 [4] and at least 5 sessions [17]. In the studies, laughter yoga sessions (40%) were held twice a week for 4 weeks, for a total of 8 sessions [2, 5, 8, 18].

Effects on laughter yoga on health parameters

Among the included studies, the effects of laughter yoga on stress were the most examined (50%) [5, 6, 8, 17, 20]. There were also studies examining the effects of laughter yoga on burnout (30%) [3, 4, 8] and salivary cortisol levels (20%) in nurses and nursing students [5, 16]. Stress levels, evaluated using different measurement tools, decrease with laughter yoga [5, 6, 8, 17]. Studies examining the effect of laughter yoga on nurses’ burnout syndrome have found that the level of burnout decreased after laughter yoga [4, 5, 8]. Studies evaluating salivary cortisol levels have shown that laughter yoga reduces cortisol levels [5, 18].

It was concluded that nursing students’ physical health and sleep disorders improved, their anxiety and depression decreased, and their social functions increased [2]. Shattla et al. (2019) stated in their study that laughter yoga had a positive effect on the mental status and increased feelings of energy and happiness in nurses [4]. In addition, studies have shown that laughter yoga has a higher subjective happiness in the experimental group than the control group [5], decreases in psychological symptoms [18], positively affects life satisfaction levels and psychological well-being, and significantly reduces the anxiety and stress levels of nursing students [19].

Discussion

This study systematically examined the effects of laughter yoga in nurses and nursing students. Laughter yoga is a preferred nursing intervention and complementary therapy because it does not require special preparations, can be performed without the need for materials, is economical, can be performed anytime and anywhere, and is easily accessible and acceptable [11].

It is seen that almost all of the studies on laughter yoga on nurses and nursing students have increased in 2019 and the following years. This situation reveals the increasing interest in “laughter yoga”, which is a complementary therapy method and nursing intervention. While nearly half of the studies included in the research were conducted in Turkey, other studies conducted in different continents and cultures were also found. This indicates that yoga is widely used among nurses as an internationally accepted complementary therapy.

When the aims of the studies included in the systematic review were examined; studies examining the effect of laughter yoga on stress or burnout levels in nurses and nursing students were found. In addition, the effect of laughter yoga on different parameters such as cortisol level, subjective well-being, psychological symptoms, anxiety, life satisfaction and feeling of loneliness is also discussed [3–6, 8, 17–20].

When the samples of the studies included in this systematic review were examined, nearly one-fourth involved nursing students. More than half the studies (80%) included both experimental and control groups. The study designs were quasi-experimental and randomised controlled trials. The methodological quality of the studies was determined to be sufficient according to the JBI-MAStARI. The evidence levels of the studies can be considered high in terms of research design and methodological quality.

It was observed that laughter yoga was practiced face-to-face in more than half of the studies within the scope of the research. While it was determined that the studies conducted online were conducted since 2021; this situation was thought to be related to the COVID-19 pandemic. In the studies, it was determined that laughter yoga was frequently performed as 8 sessions / 4 weeks. As an intervention, it was seen that laughter yoga consists of deep breathing and warm-up exercises, childlike games, laughter yoga, and meditation stages. It is seen that the frequency of sessions and the flow of laughter yoga are in accordance with the standards developed by Dr. Madan Kataria [7, 21].

According to the World Health Organization, health is not merely the absence of disease or infirmity, but a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being [22]. In parallel, the human condition, which is closely related to health, is biopsychosocial and cultural, as stated by Maslow [23]. In this systematic review, more than half of the studies obtained outcomes related to mental health as a result of the laughter yoga initiative [6, 8, 17].

As a result of the laughter yoga intervention, stress, burnout, life satisfaction, anxiety among mental health parameters were addressed; while it is noteworthy that only sleep disturbance was examined as a physiologic parameter [2]. In half of the studies within the scope of the research, laughter yoga was found to have a positive effect on stress in nurses and nursing students [5, 6, 8, 17, 18]. In the literature, it is stated that laughter reduces stress hormones and stimulates circulation by buffering the effects of stress on the cardiovascular system, increases oxygenation, and has positive effects on blood pressure and heart rate [23].

It should also be emphasised that laughter reduces cortisol levels by affecting the amygdala, thalamic, hypothalamic, and subthalamic regions of the brain through sympathetic and parasympathetic responses in the central nervous system [24]. Similar to the literature, the effect of laughter yoga on salivary cortisol levels was examined in two studies within the scope of this systematic review [5, 18]. Studies conducted with nursing students have reported that salivary cortisol levels in the experimental group decreased after laughter yoga [5, 18]. This shows that laughter yoga is an effective nursing intervention for reducing stress both subjectively (self-report) and objectively (salivary cortisol levels).

Shattla et al. (2019) and Lynes et al. (2019) examined the effects of laughter yoga on nurses’ burnout [3, 4]. However, Shattla et al. (2019) [4] determined that laughter yoga had a positive effect on all aspects of burnout, Lynes et al. (2019) [3] found no change in the burnout status of people who received the intervention and those who did not. This may be explained by the difference in the frequency and duration of laughter yoga, as well as qualitative (such as personal characteristics, patient group cared for, and the unit of work) and quantitative differences in the sample.

Implications for nurse educators and Future Research

The findings of the current studies in the field of laughter yoga are significant; however, higher-quality studies are needed to confirm its definite effectiveness. The advantage of this method for nurses and nursing students is the understanding and positive attitude of them towards complementary approaches such as laughter yoga. Nurses and nursing students can be trainers of these interventions, and by learning them and using them personally, they can implement them for others.

Despite the current evidence, more studies are expected, mainly randomized controlled trials. On the other hand, qualitative research on nurses and nursing students who have engaged in laughter yoga provides us with a comprehensive understanding of the positive and negative experiences students encounter with this technique.

We have seen in the studies that most of the interventions included eight laughter yoga sessions; it seems that more investigations should be done to reach the best and most ideal number of sessions to get the best results. There are differences in the method of implementing this intervention; experts in this field should look for protocols to standardize the laughter yoga technique to reach a standard treatment. These standardizations will lead to greater convenience and validity in implementing secondary studies.

There is an excellent opportunity for nursing educators to learn this method, become professional in it, and teach it comprehensively among students. There are many resources for learning these methods, from articles to media such as educational websites, videos available on social networks, and courses held online by experts.

Limitations

The primary limitation of this study was the inability to access some relevant studies despite using keywords and commonly utilized databases that best described the core concepts of the subject area. Additionally, only studies published in English and those that were experimental or quasi-experimental were included. It is suggested that future studies will strengthen the results of such studies by conducting reviews without language restrictions. Another limitation was the lack of detailed information on the included randomized controlled trials (RCTs), which is crucial for evaluating study quality and potentially achieving higher scores. Furthermore, there may be unpublished reports that were not included in this review. Increasing the number of RCTs would significantly enhance our ability to make a conclusive assessment. Unfortunately, we were unable to perform a comprehensive review on spiritual and physiological health due to the limited number of available studies. The strength of an individual’s spirituality is one of the most important ways to self-improve and realize Maslow’s Basic Human Needs. While the use of integrative approaches such as laughter yoga increases the skills of clinicians, academic nurses and nurse candidates in coping with stress, problem solving and adapting to the clinic; it reduces making mistakes that may occur due to burnout, anxiety and depression, putting both the patient and themselves at risk. It is very important to include such approaches into the lives of nurses and nurse students for physical, psychological and emotional well-being. According to our knowledge, this is the first systematic review of the field of laughter yoga for nurses and nursing students. This study has thoroughly examined all the literature published in the mentioned databases and has critically assessed the discussed topic to propose a roadmap for future studies. The issue of laughter yoga will substantially influence nursing students’ and nurses physical, mental, and social well-being thus accentuating the significance of conducting studies with corresponding titles.

Conclusion and recommendations

Our findings show an increase in research on laughter yoga in nurses and nursing students since 2019, and that the studies are mostly quasi-experimental. The increase in the number of RCTs using laughter yoga as a nursing intervention in recent years has revealed that this intervention is both subjectively and objectively effective and evidence-based. In our study, it was observed that stress and burnout levels, depression and anxiety, salivary cortisol levels decreased especially after laughter yoga. In addition, it was concluded that laughter yoga had positive effects on psychological well-being, life satisfaction, subjective happiness increase in and general health in the groups where laughter yoga was practiced.

While the effect of laughter yoga on mental parameters was examined in almost all of the studies within the scope of our research, it is noteworthy that there are few studies on physiological. In addition, there is no study examining the effect of laughter yoga on the spiritual dimension. In this context, it is recommended to conduct studies examining the effects of laughter yoga on different health parameters like spiritual dimension as an intervention, taking into account that humans are biopsychosocial beings.

As a result of the meta-analysis studies to be conducted on the subject, it is thought that it will make a significant contribution to providing evidence-based information on the effect of laughter yoga and increasing the usefulness of the nursing intervention.

Laughter yoga has been registered by the NIC authority at the University of Iowa since 2023, and has been included in the latest version of the NIC. In this context, it is envisaged that laughter yoga as a NIC will be widely used in nursing research and education, in providing care to both sick and healthy individuals.

In conclusion, laughter yoga as a nursing intervention has been shown to be an inexpensive, non-invasive, easy, effective, and evidence-based practice that can be applied independently by nurses.
