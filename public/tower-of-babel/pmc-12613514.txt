Abnormal neurovascular coupling induces glymphatic dysfunction in a mouse model of familial hemiplegic migraine type 2
Authors: Chenyang Duan, Shouyi Wu, Yuanxiang Li, KaiBo Zhang, Cunxin Lin, Yunqian Yao, Tianxiao Wang, Yonggang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Migraine is a prevalent primary headache disorder that significantly impairs daily life. Recent imaging studies have suggested that chronic migraine may be associated with changes in the function of the glymphatic system. Here, we describe altered cortical physiology in a genetic mouse model of familial hemiplegic migraine type 2 (FHM2), with reduced expression of astrocytic Na+/K+-ATPases. We used cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) fluorescence tracing to demonstrate that impairment of the inflow and outflow functions of the glymphatic system was observed in FHM2 mice. Using two-photon recording, we observed exaggerated neurovascular coupling in the barrel cortex of FHM2 mice. This abnormal neurovascular coupling resulted in a decrease in AQP4 expression within the perivascular astrocyte endfoot. This alteration may be a significant mechanism contributing to impairment of the glymphatic system in FHM2 mice. In vitro, primary astrocyte cultures were established and it was found that astrocytes derived from FHM2 exhibited impaired K+ transport function, which may be associated with exaggerated neurovascular coupling. Overall, our study reveals that the glymphatic system is significantly impaired in FHM2 mice, with abnormal neurovascular coupling playing a key role in decreased AQP4 expression in astrocytes, this providing a new ways of approaching to migraine pathophysiology.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-025-02194-x.

Introduction

Migraine is a multifactorial neurovascular disorder that affects nearly 1 billion individuals worldwide and represents the leading cause of disability, particularly among women of reproductive age between 15 and 40 years [1, 2]. A rare monogenic subtype of this disorder is FHM2, which is an autosomal dominant form characterized by the occurrence of an aura accompanied by hemiparesis [3]. FHM2 is caused by loss-of-function mutations in the ATP1A2 gene, which encodes the α2 isoform of the Na+/K+ -ATPase that is predominantly expressed in astrocytes [4, 5]. Although earlier studies suggested that impaired glutamate clearance by astrocytes represented a central pathological mechanism in FHM2 mice, this alteration alone could not fully account for the pathophysiology of aura migraines in patients with FHM2[6–8].

Recent work has demonstrated the existence of the glymphatic system [9], a complex network of perivascular spaces (PVS) surrounding cerebral vessels that serves as a potential lymphatic-like circulation [10, 11]. The role of the glymphatic system in clearing metabolic waste products has been proposed to contribute to the pathophysiology of migraine [12–14]. The glymphatic system is particularly relevant to migraine for two major reasons. Firstly, migraine exhibits a complex relationship with sleep, both during attacks and in the interictal state, suggesting that a poor sleep phenotype may represent an intrinsic characteristic of the disorder [15–17]. Another mechanism linking Glymphatic dysfunction to headache involves the clearance of calcitonin gene–related peptide (CGRP) within the PVS. CGRP is a critical mediator of migraine whose central role has been supported by the demonstrated clinical efficacy of therapeutic antibodies targeting CGRP or its receptor [18, 19]. CGRP has been shown to be abundantly expressed in neurons located in the cerebral cortex, hippocampus, thalamus, and hypothalamus, where its levels rise markedly in multiple migraine animal models [20–22]. The excessive accumulation of CGRP, which has been shown to exacerbate migraine-like symptoms in mice, highlights the importance of its elimination from the central nervous system through the glymphatic pathway [23, 24]. Accordingly, impaired glymphatic clearance may be intimately linked to both the pathogenesis and the chronification of migraine.

In this study, FHM2 mice were employed to investigate glymphatic system impairment through the intracisternal administration of fluorescent tracers into the cisterna magna. The functional capacity of the intracranial glymphatic system was evaluated by quantifying CSF tracer clearance rates following this administration [25]. Two-photon microscopy was subsequently applied to compare stimulus-evoked functional hyperaemia in sensory cortical vasculature between wild-type (WT) and FHM2 mice in order to determine whether abnormalities of neurovascular coupling contribute to glymphatic dysfunction. In addition, primary astrocyte cultures derived from FHM2 mice demonstrated impaired K+ buffering capacity attributable to dysfunctional the total ATPase activity, suggesting that this molecular defect may underlie neurovascular uncoupling and impaired glymphatic transport.

Methods

Experimental animals

The genotyping of C57BL/6 mice that are heterozygous for the G301R knock-in mutation in the ATP1A2 gene has been described [26], and the model details are provided in Fig. S1. The experiments involved α+/G301R and WT mice. This is because the homozygous G301R mutation is lethal [27]. The mice, which were aged 3–4 months, were kept on a 12:12 light/dark cycle and had free access to food and water. The study focused on male mice due to prior findings of excessive vasodilation in neurovascular coupling in male FHM2 mice. All experimental protocols were approved by the Institute of Genetics and Developmental Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Approval No. 2025037), and every effort was undertaken to minimize animal suffering and to reduce the number of animals employed in the experiments.

Surgery and anesthesia

The experimental mice were anaesthetised with 1% pentobarbital sodium (Yuyanbio, administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 50 mg/kg) and secured in a stereotaxic frame (Digital Stereotaxic Instrument, Yuyanbio) [28]. Meloxicam (5 mg/kg, subcutaneously) and enrofloxacin (0.5 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, E114258, Aladdin) were administered once daily for three consecutive days following surgery to provide postoperative analgesia and infection prophylaxis. A heating pad maintained core body temperature at 37 °C during the procedure. For mice anaesthetised with isoflurane in oxygen, approximately 2.5% isoflurane (1349014, Sigma-Aldrich) was used during the induction phase, while the maintenance phase during surgery required approximately 1.5%. To ensure adequate anaesthesia for any potentially painful surgical procedures, the toe pinch reflex test was performed, and surgery commenced only after the mice had reached the appropriate level of anaesthesia. Throughout the surgical procedures, respiratory rate and muscle tone were closely monitored to maintain stable anaesthesia.

Cisterna magna CSF tracer injection

The cisterna magna injection procedure was performed as previously described, with minor modifications [29, 30]. Briefly, the mice were anaesthetised with 1% pentobarbital sodium. After making a small skin incision in the neck, the muscle layers were retracted and the cisterna magna was exposed. A solution of either 2% Dextran-FITC (3kDa) (Dextran-FITC, Xi’An Qiyue Bio, Xi’An, China) or 2% OVA-45 (OVA-45, Xi’An Qiyue Bio, Xi’An, China) dissolved in artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF, SL6630, Coolaber, BeiJing, China) was injected into the cisterna magna at a rate of 2 μL/min to a total volume of 10 μL, using a 25 µL microliter syringe (PN: 80,430, HAMILTON 700 series, Reno, U.S.A.). The injection system was connected to a constant-flow microinjection pump (KDS Legato 130, RWD Life Science, China). 30 minutes after initiating the injection, the animals underwent transcardial perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The brains were harvested and post-fixed in the same fixative solution for 24 hours.

Ex vivo imaging of fluorescent CSF tracers

Coronal brain sections (100 µm thick) were cut using a vibrating microtome (VT1200S, LEICA). This was performed between 1.2 and −1.8 mm from bregma, followed by fluorescence imaging using a 10× lens in a confocal microscope (TCS SP8, LEICA) to document tracer influx into the brain parenchyma. Researchers who were blinded to the experimental groups conducted a quantitative assessment of tracer distribution. ImageJ software (NIH) was then used to calculate both mean fluorescence intensity and mean fluorescent area (normalised to total section area). The mean fluorescence intensity of six coronal brain sections spanning from 1.2 mm to −1.8 mm from bregma was determined for each subject. Following established protocols, fluorescence coverage was determined by applying uniform thresholding. For each biological replicate, the mean values of both fluorescent coverage area and average fluorescence intensity were used. These values came from six sections. They were used as quantitative measures of cerebrospinal fluid tracer penetration.

Cortical glymphatic clearance assay

For anesthetized condition: Mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital and secured in a stereotaxic apparatus. After exposing the skull, 1 μL of Dextran–FITC (70 kDa, 2%) was infused into the cortex using a microinfusion pump (KDS Legato 130, RWD Life Science, China) at a rate of 0.2 μL/min. The injection needle was left in place for 5 min before slow withdrawal to prevent reflux. 2 hours after infusion, mice were sacrificed, and brain tissue and deep cervical lymph nodes (dcLNs) were collected. Brains were fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for 24 hours. Then the brain coronally sectioned at 100 μm, and imaged using a fluorescence microscope (6 sections per mouse, 600 μm apart). dcLNs were dehydrated in 30% sucrose (in PBS) for 48 h and sectioned at 40 μm with a LEICA CM1950 cryostat.

For Awake condition: To evaluate glymphatic clearance in the awake state, mice first underwent implantation of a guide cannula (Single/G1 0.5 mm-27 G/M3.0, RWD Life Science, China) targeting the barrel cortex (Bregma: AP: −1.7 mm, L:+3.5 mm, DV:1.0 mm from skull surface). Cannulas were secured to the skull with dental cement and Charisma resin (Heraeus Kulzer, Germany), and sealed with a dust cap to prevent blockage during recovery. After at least 7 days of recovery, 1 μL of Dextran–FITC (70 kDa, 2%) was infused through the cannula at 0.2 μL/min. The injection needle was left in place for 5 min to minimize tracer backflow. 2 hours later, brains were coronally sectioned at 100 μm and imaged, and dcLNs were prepared as 40 μm frozen sections for analysis.

Surgical procedures of a cranial window

The surgery to create a cranial window was performed as previously described, but with minor modifications [31, 32]. The mice were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium before the surgical procedure. The scalp was excised from lambda to the frontal suture, and the periosteum was carefully removed. A circular craniotomy with a diameter of 2.5 mm was created over the barrel cortex, positioned −1.7 mm posterior to bregma and 3.5 mm lateral to the midline, for chronic window implantation. In addition, a diameter of 3 mm was delineated on the parietal bone according to the size of the coverslip. A cranial window assembly, consisting of a circular glass coverslip with a diameter of 3 mm and a metal ring, was prepared and positioned over the exposed barrel cortex. The brain surface exposed through the craniotomy was perfused with PBS, and the coverslip–ring assembly was fixed to the skull with dental cement, thereby forming a sealed chamber in which PBS could be maintained as the imaging medium during subsequent in vivo imaging. Postoperatively, mice were administered anti-inflammatory and analgesic treatment and were allowed to recover for 7 days before imaging.

In vivo two-photon imaging

Before imaging, a 10 μL solution of aCSF containing 2% w/v 3-kDa FITC-dextran (Dextran-FITC, Xi’An Qiyue Bio, Xi’An, China) was injected retro-orbitally. During the imaging procedure, mice were maintained under 1.5% isoflurane anesthesia delivered in oxygen, and their respiratory rate and body temperature were continuously monitored to ensure physiological stability. The animals were then examined with a two-photon microscope (LSM 780 NLO, ZEISS) equipped with a specialized imaging system (ZEN3.9, ZEISS). Imaging parameters were set to an exposure time of 300 ms, a resolution of 1024 × 1024, and a depth of 16 bits, with image acquisition performed at a rate of one frame per second.

Lumen diameter and PVS width extraction

Mice anaesthetised with isoflurane in oxygen during the maintenance phase of surgery required approximately 1.5%. A cranial window was carefully created above the barrel cortex, after which the brain tissue was covered with 1.2% agarose gel (VA10252, GenStar, GuangZhou, China) and a 5 mm coverslip was placed on top. Then, a solution of 2% dextran-FITC (3kDa) dissolved in aCSF was injected into the cisterna magna at a rate of 2 μL/min to a total volume of 10 μL as described above. Before imaging, a retro-orbital injection of an intravascular tracer (0.2 ml of 2% TRITC-labelled 4kDa dextran solution; Xi’An QiYue Bio, Xi’An, China) was administered. During the imaging procedure, mice were maintained under 1.5% isoflurane anesthesia delivered in oxygen, and their respiratory rate and body temperature were continuously monitored to ensure physiological stability. Imaging was performed using a × 10 objective Nikon CFI microscope. The imaging parameters were set to a resolution of 1024 × 1024 and a depth of 16 bits, with acquisition at a rate of one frame per second[30, 31].

All data were managed using Begonia, a toolbox based on the MATLAB programming language. Line scans were recorded across penetrating small arteries and the PVS around them. The line scans were trimmed to position the vessel in the centre of the recording, and trials with poor signal quality were excluded. To enhance the signal quality, the X-T data were downsampled by averaging an integer number of samples. The aim was to achieve a sampling with 20 samples per micrometre.

Western blottin

Tissues were lysed thoroughly in RIPA buffer (R917927, Macklin, Shanghai, China) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (HY-K0010, MCE, Shanghai, China) to obtain total protein extracts. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA protein assay (B917925, Macklin, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein from each sample, after denaturation at 95 °C, were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes by the wet-transfer method. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk (232100, BD, New Jersey, USA) at room temperature for 1 h and subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodiesunder gentle agitation. After being washed three times with TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature and washed again. Protein bands were visualized with ECL reagent (WBKLS0500, Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) and captured using a Tanon 5200 imaging system. GAPDH (36 kDa, Rabbit, 1:3000, 5174, CST, Massachusetts, USA) was employed as the loading control. Quantitative analysis of the ATP1A2 band (105 kDa, Rabbit, 1:1000, 16836-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) was performed using ImageJ software based on grey-scale intensity measurements.

Immunofluorescence of mouse brain tissue

Frozen sections of mouse brain tissue were fixed sequentially in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 min, and incubated with blocking solution for 1 h. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. On the following day, fluorescent secondary antibodies were applied and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI for 15 min. Each step was followed by three washes in PBS for 10 min. Fluorescence images were acquired with a confocal microscope and analysed using ImageJ software. The primary antibodies applied in immunofluorescence experiments were anti-GFAP (#3670, CST, 1:400), anti-AQP4 (16473–1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China, 1:200), and anti-ATP1A2 (84999–4-RR, Proteintech, 1:200).

Isolation and sorting of astrocytes from dissociated cortical brain tissue

Four adult mice were used of the WT and FHM2 groups. The tissue was dissociated using the Papain (LK003178, Worthington, New Jersey, USA) and DNase (LK003172, Worthington, New Jersey, USA), and the freshly dissociated cells were filtered through a 20 µm Nitex filter to remove any residual tissue clumps. Myelin removal was performed using equilibrated density gradient centrifugation. Resuspend the cell pellet in 0.5% BSA (01–0251-00, Merck millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) in calcium- and magnesium-free PBS. Perform the myelin removal step subsequently. Collect the flow using Myelin Removal Beads II (130–096-733, Miltenyi, Cologne, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, for subsequent astrocyte isolation. After cell collection, stain with the anti-ACSA-2 kit (130–097-678, Miltenyi, Cologne, Germany) for 20 minutes on ice, in the dark. Incubate with DAPI, wash three times with PBS and finally sort by flow cytometry. Add the sorted cells directly to Trizol on ice, extract RNA using standard procedures and proceed with detection.

mRNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat. No. 74,004).RNA concentration was measured using NanoDrop OneC (Thermo Fisher), while quality was assessed with the High Sensitivity RNA Analysis Kit (Qiagen, Cat. No. Q33221). Only samples with RIN > 7 were retained for subsequent sequencing. Libraries were prepared using the Illumina Stranded mRNA Prep Ligation Kit (Cat. No. 20,040,534).quantified using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay (Catalog No. Q35853), then diluted to 10 nM/10 μL. After pooling and denaturing, the final concentration was adjusted to 1.4 pM. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina MiniSeq platform with 150 bp paired-end reads and 5% PhiX control. Libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit. After pooling, double-end sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform.

Primary astrocyte culture

Following the established protocol [33], primary astrocytes were isolated from the cortex of WT and FHM2 newborn mice (P1–P3). Cortical minisections were digested with 0.25% trypsin (C0201-500 ml, Beyotime) at 37 °C for 15 minutes, then mechanically homogenised to form a single-cell suspension. The cells were transferred to DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (PS), and cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO₂ with saturated humidity. After 6–10 days, the cells were shaken at 250 rpm for 4–6 hours to remove the microglia and oligodendrocytes. The purified astrocytes were then replated onto poly-L-lysine-coated culture dishes or coverslips for subsequent experiments.

Immunofluorescence of primary astrocytes

Primary astrocytes were isolated from mice and cultured into 24-well plates that were lined with sterile glass coverslips. After treatment, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 20 minutes, washed three times with PBS and permeabilised with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 minutes. Non-specific binding was then blocked by incubating the cells in PBS containing 5% BSA (Proteintech, Wuhan) at room temperature for 1 hour. Primary antibodies were then added according to the requirements of the experiment: GFAP (Rat, #3670, CST, 1:400), AQP4 (Mouse, 16,473–1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China, 1:200) and ATP1A2 (rabbit, 83,428–1-Ig, Proteintech, 1:200). The slides were then incubated overnight at 4C. The following day, after thorough washing with PBS, fluorescent secondary antibodies were added in the dark: Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG (RGAM002, Proteintech, 1:500) and Alexa Fluor 594 goat anti-rabbit IgG (GRAR004, Proteintech, 1:500). Incubate at room temperature for 1 hour, then wash with PBS. After this, counterstain the nuclei with 1 μg/mL DAPI for 10 minutes. Following another wash, images were acquired using a laser confocal microscope.

Total ATPase activity assay

Total ATPase activity was measured following the manufacturer’s instructions [34]. The assay is based on the enzymatic hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and inorganic phosphate (Pi), with activity quantified by detecting released Pi. Pi reacts with molybdate to form phosphomolybdate blue, which was measured spectrophotometrically at 660 nm using a microplate reader. A Pi standard (0.5 μmol/mL) was used for calibration.

PBFI assay

Intracellular K+ concentrations were determined using the PBFI assay [35]. Briefly, 1.0 μL of DMSO containing 5 mM PBFI-AM and 12.5% (w/v) PF-127 was added to 0.5 mL PBS containing 2 × 108 cells/mL. After incubation in a shaking incubator at 37 °C for 4 h, the cell suspension was diluted to 1 × 107 cells/mL. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of the cell suspension was mixed with 0.5 mL PBS and subjected to fluorescence measurement using a spectrophotometer (λ_ex = 340/380 nm, λ_em = 500 nm; Hitachi F-7000).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data are displayed as means ± SEM in all experiments except for the Western blot data that are displayed as means ± SD. Statistical details can be found in Results, figures, and figure legends. Statistical comparisons were made with two-tailed paired or unpaired t tests, oneway analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni post hoc test, one-way ANOVA with Tukey post hoc test, or two-way repeatedmeasures ANOVA test. All graphs and statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism, and figures were prepared using Adobe llustrator CS5. p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The influx of fluorescent CSF tracer is reduced in FHM2 mice

First, we performed intracisternal injections of CSF tracer (FITC-Dextran, 3kDa) in both WT and FHM2 mice. After allowing 30 minutes for tracer circulation in anesthetized animals, brains were extracted and sectioned coronally at 100 μm thickness using a vibratome (Fig. 1A-B). Quantitative analysis of tracer distribution was conducted by measuring fluorescence intensity in six sequential brain sections centered around the bregma (Fig. 1C-D). Our results demonstrated significantly impaired CSF influx in FHM2 mice compared to WT group. Subsequent analysis of tracer fluorescence intensity in six sequential brain sections revealed distance-dependent to bregma (Fig. 1E). Sections closer to the injection site (cisterna magna) showed minimal fluorescence intensity, whereas sections more distal to the injection site exhibited significantly greater fluorescence intensity (Fig. 1F). We investigated neurovascular coupling abnormalities in FHM2 mice using two-photon imaging techniques. Following craniotomy above the barrel cortex, cortical neurovascular coupling was assessed 1 week post-recovery through airpuff stimulation and periorbital stimulation. In the airpuff stimulation-induced experiment, FHM2 mice exhibited increased vascular dilation amplitude, altered vascular diameter during functional dilation, and a significantly higher area under the curve compared to controls. (Fig. 4A-D) Additionally, periorbital stimulation-induced functional hyperemia in cortical vessels, with FHM2 mice displaying significantly enhanced vasodilation amplitudes. These findings indicate that FHM2 mice exhibit exaggerated neurovascular coupling, leading to excessive vessel dilation during vascular congestion.(Fig. 4G-I)

Discussion

In this study, abnormal neurovascular coupling that induced glymphatic dysfunction in a mouse model of FHM2 was described. Data demonstrated that FHM2 mice exhibited significant abnormalities in neurovascular coupling. Two-photon imaging revealed that the amplitude and duration of stimulus-evoked vascular dilation were markedly increased in FHM2 mice compared with WT mice. These abnormal haemodynamic responses extended beyond the vessel wall into the surrounding PVS, suggesting that vascular dysfunction had disrupted the normal dynamics of PVS fluid exchange. Consequently, the function of the glymphatic system was markedly impaired in FHM2 mice. Subsequently, in vitro experiments using primary astrocyte cultures established from the cortices of WT and FHM2 mice were performed, which revealed that astrocytes derived from FHM2 mice exhibited impaired K+ transport function [36]. This dysfunction was likely associated with a decrease in ATPase activity resulting from ATP1A2 mutations, which may contribute to the excessive vasodilation observed during neurovascular coupling by altering extracellular K+ clearance and vascular smooth muscle excitability [26, 27]. Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated a significant reduction of AQP4 expression in cortical astrocytes of FHM2 mice. This finding was further confirmed in primary astrocyte cultures. AQP4 plays a key role in facilitating glymphatic system, its downregulation may represent a crucial molecular mechanism linking astrocytic dysfunction to glymphatic impairment. Together, these findings indicate that reduced AQP4 expression contributes to the disruption of glymphatic system function in FHM2 mice (Fig. 9).

The results of the present study provide evidence for a potential therapeutic target aimed at improving glymphatic system function in individuals with FHM2-associated mutations. Migraine with aura has been associated with reduced cerebral blood flow during the interictal period [37–39], and consistent with this, FHM2 mice displayed abnormal neurovascular coupling leading to impaired glymphatic system [36]. Our findings further demonstrated that abnormal neurovascular coupling caused exaggerated cerebral arterial vasodilation in vivo in FHM2 mice [27]. Previous investigations also showed that isolated middle cerebral arteries from FHM2 mice exhibited enhanced agonist-induced vasoconstriction associated with K+-dependent increased sensitivity of vascular smooth muscle cells to intracellular K+ [40].

Several limitations were recognized in this study. Pentobarbital anesthesia was used, which might have influenced the function of the intracranial glymphatic system. Two-photon imaging was performed only at day 7 after cranial window surgery, and long-term imaging data were not obtained. In addition, only the reduction of AQP4 expression in cortical astrocytes of FHM2 mice was evaluated, whereas alterations in perivascular AQP4 polarization were not characterized.

Research on glymphatic system function in migraine has been advanced by several animal studies. In 2017, sham-controlled mouse models of cortical spreading depression (CSD) revealed that waves of CSD triggered by cortical stimulation induced transient closure of the PVS, resulting in a reduction of glymphatic flow lasting approximately 40 minutes [41]. Moreover, repeated CSD episodes were associated with decreased clearance of fluorescent CSF tracers from the cortex to the PVS. Another study in a chronic migraine model, in which nitroglycerin (NTG) was administered intraperitoneally every other day for nine days, demonstrated reduced tracer signals in the brain parenchyma following cisterna magna injection compared to controls, indicating impaired glymphatic transport [23]. This NTG-induced migraine model also revealed decreased AQP4 expression and impaired perivascular AQP4 polarization, suggesting that aquaporin dysfunction contributes to glymphatic impairment during migraine progression [42, 43].

Astrocytic reactivity has been shown to impair glymphatic function and vascular remodeling [44]. Recent evidence further suggests that astrocyte activation diminishes vascular support and neurovascular coupling, processes that are essential for glymphatic function [45]. The glymphatic system is also regulated by AQP4 channels located at astrocytic endfeet surrounding PVS [46, 47]. Astrocytes are therefore critically involved in glymphatic activity through two main mechanisms: (1) chronic astrocytic injury and activation downregulate AQP4 expression at astrocytic endfeet, thereby reducing CSF flow within PVS [48, 49]; and (2) neurovascular coupling, which serves as the driving force of CSF movement in PVS, is compromised when astrocyte-mediated vascular responses are impaired, leading to reduced glymphatic clearance [50, 51].

This study demonstrated impaired glymphatic function in FHM2 mice and revealed that abnormal neurovascular coupling disrupted astrocytic endfeet, resulting in reduced AQP4 expression. These findings establish a mechanistic link between migraine symptoms with aura and dysfunction of the glymphatic system in this familial hemiplegic migraine model. Nevertheless, due to experimental limitations, further investigations are required to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying reduced AQP4 expression in astrocytes. Future studies will be needed to explore more comprehensively the relationship between glymphatic dysfunction and migraine pathophysiology.
