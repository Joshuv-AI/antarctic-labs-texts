Caregiver Burden Mediates the Association Between Patient Self-Care Behaviors and Psychological Distress in Diabetic Foot Ulcer Care
Authors: Xiang Chen, Li Tong, Rong Zhang, Youwen Gong, HongLing Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHome-based wound care is an important care model for patients with diabetic foot ulcers. In this setting, family caregivers often undertake daily supervision, wound observation, treatment coordination, and care-related decision-making. However, the relationship between patient self-care behaviors and caregiver psychological outcomes remains insufficiently understood.AimTo examine the associations among patient self-care behaviors, caregiver burden, and caregiver psychological distress, and to explore whether caregiver burden mediated the relationship between patient self-care behaviors and caregiver anxiety and depression.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 567 patient-caregiver dyads receiving home-based wound care for diabetic foot ulcers. Patient self-care behaviors were assessed using the Summary of Diabetes Self-Care Activities Scale. Caregiver burden was measured using the Zarit Burden Interview. Caregiver anxiety and depression were assessed using the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale and the Self-Rating Depression Scale. Correlation, multiple regression, and bootstrap mediation analyses were performed.ResultsThe mean patient self-care score was 43.83 ± 15.44, and the mean caregiver burden score was 52.16 ± 18.82. Anxiety and depressive symptoms were identified in 48.0% and 72.5% of caregivers, respectively. Patient self-care behaviors were negatively correlated with caregiver burden, anxiety, and depression, whereas caregiver burden was positively correlated with anxiety and depression. Caregiver burden mediated the association between patient self-care behaviors and caregiver anxiety (indirect effect = −0.222, 95% CI: −0.393 to −0.053) and depression (indirect effect = −0.893, 95% CI: −0.948 to −0.837).ConclusionBetter patient self-care behaviors were associated with lower caregiver burden and psychological distress. Caregiver burden may represent a potential pathway linking patient self-care behaviors to caregiver anxiety and depression, although causal inference cannot be made from this cross-sectional study. Home-based diabetic foot ulcer care should integrate patient self-management support with caregiver burden assessment and psychological support.

Background

Diabetic foot ulcer is a common but serious complication of diabetes mellitus and remains a major challenge in long-term diabetes care.1 Because of peripheral neuropathy, vascular insufficiency, infection, and impaired wound healing, patients with diabetic foot ulcers often experience prolonged treatment, repeated dressing changes, frequent medical visits, delayed recovery, and even lower-limb amputation.2 The disease not only affects patients’ physical function and quality of life but also creates a continuous demand for daily care outside the hospital. Unlike some acute complications, diabetic foot ulcers require long-term monitoring and strict self-management, including blood glucose control, foot protection, wound observation, pressure relief, medication adherence, and timely response to abnormal wound changes.3

In recent years, home-based wound care has become an important extension of hospital care for patients with diabetic foot ulcers. This model can reduce unnecessary hospital visits, improve continuity of care, and allow patients to receive professional nursing support in a familiar environment. However, home-based wound care also changes the pattern of responsibility. After patients return home, wound management is no longer performed only by healthcare professionals. Patients and their family caregivers must participate in daily disease monitoring, self-care, and care decision-making.4 In this setting, the quality of home-based wound care depends not only on nurses’ professional guidance but also on patients’ ability to perform self-care and caregivers’ capacity to provide stable support.

Self-care behavior is particularly important for patients with diabetic foot ulcers. Effective self-care includes dietary control, blood glucose monitoring, medication adherence, foot inspection, wound protection, appropriate activity, and compliance with medical advice.5 Good self-care may help patients maintain better disease control and reduce preventable wound-related risks. By contrast, poor self-care may increase the possibility of wound deterioration, infection, delayed healing, and repeated medical intervention. More importantly, insufficient self-care may shift additional care responsibilities to family caregivers. When patients cannot independently complete daily self-management tasks, caregivers often need to remind, supervise, assist, and make decisions on their behalf.6 Therefore, patients’ self-care behaviors may directly influence the amount and intensity of caregiving work in the home environment.

Family caregivers are often the main source of support for patients with diabetic foot ulcers, especially for those who are older, have limited mobility, or live with multiple chronic conditions. In daily care, caregivers may need to help patients observe wound changes, protect the affected limb, control diet, monitor blood glucose, attend follow-up visits, and identify warning signs.7 Although caregivers play an essential role in supporting wound healing and preventing complications, caregiving itself can become a source of strain.8 Caregiver burden refers to the physical, emotional, social, and financial pressure perceived during the caregiving process. In diabetic foot ulcer care, this burden may be intensified by slow wound healing, uncertainty about prognosis, fear of infection, and concern about amputation.

Existing quantitative studies also suggest that caregiver burden is substantial in diabetic foot ulcer care. A cross-sectional study of caregivers of patients with diabetic foot ulcers reported a mean Zarit Caregiver Burden Scale score of 46.47 ± 11.39, indicating a moderate level of burden.9 Another study found that caregivers of diabetic foot patients experienced measurable burden and that patient-related care dependency, including medication use, insulin administration, and self-care needs, was associated with higher caregiver burden. Research involving caregivers of patients with amputated diabetic foot also reported considerable burden and impaired caregiver quality of life, suggesting that the family impact of diabetic foot disease extends beyond wound management alone.10

The psychological health of caregivers should also be considered in home-based wound care. Long-term caregiving may expose caregivers to persistent stress, uncertainty, fatigue, and role conflict. Some caregivers may worry about whether they can correctly recognize wound deterioration, whether home care is sufficient, or whether delayed treatment will lead to serious consequences.11 Such concerns may gradually develop into anxiety or depressive symptoms. Psychological distress among caregivers is not only a personal health issue but may also affect the quality of patient care. Caregivers with high levels of distress may have reduced patience, poorer sleep, lower confidence, and difficulty maintaining effective care routines. Therefore, understanding the factors related to caregiver psychological distress is important for improving both caregiver well-being and the quality of home-based diabetic foot care.12

Previous studies on diabetic foot ulcers have mainly focused on wound healing, infection control, amputation risk, health education, and patient self-management.13 Although these topics are important, the experiences of family caregivers have received relatively limited attention. In particular, few studies have explored how patients’ self-care behaviors may influence caregivers’ psychological outcomes. In clinical practice, caregivers are often affected by the patient’s ability to manage the disease. When patients show better self-care, caregivers may feel less pressure and greater confidence in home management. Conversely, when patients have poor self-care behaviors, caregivers may need to take on more responsibilities, which may increase subjective burden and emotional distress.14 This relationship deserves further investigation.

The selection of caregiver burden as the mediator was guided by the caregiving stress process framework. This framework views caregiving stress as a dynamic process in which care-related demands and stressors are translated into subjective burden and, subsequently, psychological outcomes. In the context of diabetic foot ulcer care, poor patient self-care may increase the caregiver’s direct involvement in wound observation, medication reminders, dietary supervision, mobility assistance, follow-up coordination, and decision-making. These demands are closer to the daily caregiving process than broader psychosocial resources.15 Therefore, caregiver burden was considered a proximal mechanism linking patient self-care behaviors to caregiver anxiety and depression. Caregiver burden may be a key mechanism linking patient self-care behaviors to caregiver psychological distress. Patients with better self-care behaviors may reduce caregivers’ daily supervision and assistance needs, thereby lowering caregiver burden and emotional stress. In contrast, poor self-care may increase caregivers’ responsibility for monitoring, reminding, assisting, and decision-making, which may further increase caregiver burden and contribute to anxiety and depression. From this perspective, caregiver burden may not simply be an outcome of caregiving demands but may also serve as an intermediate pathway through which patient self-care affects caregiver mental health.

Therefore, this study aimed to examine the relationships among patient self-care behaviors, caregiver burden, and psychological distress among caregivers of patients with diabetic foot ulcers receiving home-based wound care. Specifically, we explored whether caregiver burden mediated the association between patient self-care behaviors and caregiver anxiety and depression. We hypothesized that better patient self-care behaviors would be associated with lower caregiver burden and lower levels of caregiver psychological distress, and that caregiver burden would play a mediating role in these relationships. The findings may provide evidence for developing home-based wound care strategies that combine patient self-management support with caregiver burden reduction and psychological support.

Methods

Participants and Procedure

This cross-sectional study examined the relationships among patient self-care behaviors, caregiver burden, and psychological distress in caregivers of patients with diabetic foot ulcers receiving home-based wound care. The study focused on patient-caregiver dyads because wound care in the home setting usually depends on both the patient’s self-management ability and the caregiver’s daily support. The reporting of this study was guided by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement.

Participant recruitment and data collection were conducted from February 2025 to June 2025 through home-based wound care services. Eligible patient-caregiver dyads were approached during routine home-based wound care visits; questionnaire data and relevant clinical information were collected after informed consent was obtained. Patients with diabetic foot ulcers who received home-based wound care and their primary family caregivers were recruited using convenience sampling. A patient-caregiver dyad was considered eligible when the patient had a confirmed diagnosis of diabetic foot ulcer, was receiving home-based wound care services, and had a primary caregiver who was mainly responsible for daily care, wound observation, treatment coordination, or care-related decision-making. The primary caregiver was defined as the family member or informal caregiver who provided the greatest amount of unpaid care during the patient’s home-based wound management.

The sample size was estimated using the formula for estimating the mean of a continuous variable in a cross-sectional study: n = (Zα/2 × σ/δ)2. Based on a previous study of caregivers of patients with diabetic foot ulcers, the standard deviation of caregiver burden was approximately 11.39. With a two-sided α of 0.05, Zα/2 = 1.96, and an allowable error of 1.0 point, the minimum required sample size was calculated as n = (1.96 × 11.39/1.0)2 = 499. After allowing for an estimated 10% rate of invalid or incomplete questionnaires, the minimum adjusted sample size was 554 dyads. A total of 567 valid patient-caregiver dyads were finally included, which exceeded the minimum required sample size and was considered adequate for correlation, multiple regression, and bootstrap mediation analyses.

The inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: age 18 years or older; diagnosis of diabetic foot ulcer based on clinical assessment; receipt of home-based wound care services; clear consciousness and ability to communicate; and willingness to participate in the study. Patients were excluded if they had severe cognitive impairment, serious mental illness, critical illness, or other conditions that made questionnaire completion impossible.

The inclusion criteria for caregivers were as follows: age 18 years or older; identification as the patient’s primary caregiver; provision of care for the patient during home-based wound care; ability to understand and complete the questionnaire; and voluntary participation in the study. Caregivers were excluded if they were professional nurses or formal home-care providers who delivered care as part of institutional nursing services, had serious cognitive or communication difficulties, or had a diagnosed severe psychiatric disorder that could affect questionnaire completion.

Before data collection, trained research nurses explained the purpose, content, and voluntary nature of the study to eligible patients and caregivers. After informed consent was obtained, participants completed the questionnaires during the home-based wound care visit or under the guidance of the research team. Clinical information, including diabetic foot ulcer severity and relevant health indicators, was collected from clinical records and home-based nursing assessment forms when available. Completed questionnaires were reviewed on the same day to identify missing or inconsistent responses.

Measures

Sociodemographic and Clinical Characteristics

Demographic and clinical information was collected using a structured questionnaire developed by the research team. Patient-related information included sex, age, educational level, monthly income, body mass index, place of residence, medical payment method, random blood glucose, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and Wagner grade of diabetic foot ulcer. The Wagner grade was used to reflect the clinical severity of diabetic foot ulcers, with a higher grade indicating more serious tissue involvement and wound condition. Caregiver-related information included age, sex, educational level, relationship with the patient, monthly income, marital status, total duration of caregiving, and daily caregiving time.

Patient Self-Care Behaviors

Patient self-care behaviors were assessed using the Summary of Diabetes Self-Care Activities Scale (SDSCA), a widely used self-report instrument for evaluating diabetes self-management behaviors. The scale assesses the frequency of self-care activities over the previous 7 days, covering several domains, including diet management, physical activity, blood glucose monitoring, medication adherence, and foot care. Items are generally scored according to the number of days on which the behavior was performed, with higher scores indicating better self-care performance. In the present study, the total SDSCA score was used to represent the overall level of patient self-care behavior, and a higher score reflected more frequent and better diabetes-related self-care. The SDSCA has shown acceptable reliability and validity in diabetes populations. In this study, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.88.16

Caregiver Burden

Caregiver burden was measured using the Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI). The ZBI is one of the most commonly used instruments for assessing subjective burden among family caregivers. It evaluates the physical, emotional, social, and financial strain associated with caregiving, including fatigue, role conflict, emotional pressure, restriction of personal life, and the perceived impact of caregiving on daily activities. The scale contains 22 items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“never”) to 4 (“nearly always”). The total score ranges from 0 to 88, with higher scores indicating a heavier caregiver burden. According to commonly used cut-off values, scores of 0–20 indicate little or no burden, 21–40 indicate mild to moderate burden, 41–60 indicate moderate to severe burden, and 61–88 indicate severe burden. In this study, the total ZBI score was used as the mediating variable. The ZBI demonstrated good reliability in this study, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.84.17

Anxiety

Caregiver anxiety was assessed using the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS). The SAS is a 20-item self-report instrument designed to evaluate the severity of anxiety-related symptoms, including nervousness, fear, restlessness, sleep disturbance, somatic discomfort, and autonomic symptoms. Each item is scored on a 4-point Likert scale according to symptom frequency, ranging from 1 (“a little of the time”) to 4 (“most or all of the time”), with several items reverse-scored. The raw score is obtained by summing all item scores and is then multiplied by 1.25 to generate the standard score. A higher standard score indicates more severe anxiety symptoms. In the present study, the SAS standard score was used as one of the main outcome variables, and anxiety classification was used for descriptive analysis. The SAS showed good internal consistency in this study, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.87.18

Depression

Caregiver depression was measured using the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS). The SDS is a 20-item self-report scale used to assess depressive symptoms, including depressed mood, reduced interest, fatigue, sleep disturbance, appetite change, hopelessness, psychomotor changes, and other affective or somatic symptoms. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale based on symptom frequency, with part of the items reverse-scored. The raw score is calculated by summing all item scores and then converted into a standard score by multiplying by 1.25. Higher standard scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms. In this study, the SDS standard score was used as another main outcome variable, and depression classification was used to describe the prevalence of depressive symptoms among caregivers. The SDS demonstrated good reliability in this study, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89.19

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software. All questionnaires were checked before data entry, and incomplete or obviously inconsistent responses were reviewed according to the original records. Continuous variables were first examined for distributional characteristics. Normally distributed variables were described as mean ± SD, whereas non-normally distributed variables were presented as median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of patients and caregivers, as well as the scores of patient self-care behaviors, caregiver burden, anxiety, and depression. Pearson or Spearman correlation analyses were performed to examine the relationships among patient self-care behaviors, caregiver burden, anxiety, and depression. Pearson correlation was used when variables were approximately normally distributed; otherwise, Spearman correlation was applied.

In the adjusted regression and mediation models, patient-related covariates included patient age, sex, educational level, medical payment method, random blood glucose, and Wagner grade. Caregiver-related covariates included caregiver age, sex, educational level, relationship with the patient, total duration of caregiving, and daily caregiving time. These covariates were selected because of their clinical relevance to diabetic foot ulcer care and caregiver psychological outcomes.

To address concerns about common-method effects, scale overlap, and multicollinearity, we performed additional diagnostic checks before interpreting the mediation results. These checks included inspection of the correlation matrix among the total SDSCA, ZBI, SAS, and SDS scores; review of possible conceptual overlap among the scale contents; and collinearity diagnostics for the adjusted regression models. Variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance values were calculated for all predictors entered into the adjusted outcome models. A VIF greater than 10 or tolerance below 0.10 was considered to indicate serious multicollinearity. These diagnostics were used to guide cautious interpretation of the mediation models, particularly the depression model. In addition, VIF and tolerance values were calculated among the four core scale variables to assess whether the observed correlations could affect the interpretation of the mediation models.

The mediating role of caregiver burden was tested using the bootstrap method. Two separate mediation models were constructed. In the first model, caregiver anxiety was used as the outcome variable; in the second model, caregiver depression was used as the outcome variable. Patient self-care behavior was treated as the independent variable, and caregiver burden was treated as the mediator in both models. The indirect effect was estimated using 5000 bootstrap samples, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated. A mediating effect was considered statistically significant when the 95% confidence interval of the indirect effect did not include zero. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical Considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the First People’s Hospital of Changde City (Approval No. 2025-330-02). All participants provided informed consent before participation.

Results

Sociodemographic and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 567 patient-caregiver dyads were included in the analysis. The mean age of the patients was 57.00 ± 9.45 years, and most patients were male (89.8%). The mean body mass index was 23.94 ± 2.61 kg/m2. The mean random blood glucose level was 7.86 ± 1.87 mmol/L, while the mean systolic and diastolic blood pressure values were 129.83 ± 29.50 mmHg and 82.10 ± 17.05 mmHg, respectively.

Regarding educational level, 35.8% of patients had a junior middle school education or below, 43.4% had a senior middle school education, and 20.8% had a college degree or above. Most patients lived in rural areas (70.4%) and had medical insurance (96.1%). In terms of diabetic foot ulcer severity, 25.2% of patients were classified as Wagner grade 1, 39.0% as grade 2, 22.4% as grade 3, and 13.4% as grade 4. No patient was classified as Wagner grade 5.

The mean age of caregivers was 48.72 ± 13.74 years. Among caregivers, 61.9% were male and 38.1% were female. More than half had a senior middle school education (52.7%), while 24.7% had a junior middle school education or below and 22.6% had a college degree or above. The mean total duration of caregiving was 6.81 ± 2.40 months, and the mean daily caregiving time was 5.45 ± 1.92 hours. Most caregivers were married (73.5%). Correlation Analysis

The mean score of patient self-care behaviors was 43.83 ± 15.44, and the mean caregiver burden score was 52.16 ± 18.82. According to the burden classification, 77 caregivers (13.6%) reported little or no burden, 46 (8.1%) reported mild to moderate burden, 239 (42.2%) reported moderate to severe burden, and 205 (36.2%) reported severe burden. The mean anxiety score was 48.27 ± 10.14, with 272 caregivers (48.0%) classified as having anxiety symptoms. The mean depression score was 53.57 ± 13.63, and 411 caregivers (72.5%) were classified as having depressive symptoms.

Correlation analysis showed that patient self-care behaviors were significantly and negatively correlated with caregiver burden (r = −0.945, P < 0.001), anxiety (r = −0.269, P < 0.001), and depression (r = −0.915, P < 0.001). Caregiver burden was significantly and positively correlated with both anxiety (r = 0.304, P < 0.001) and depression (r = 0.979, P < 0.001). Anxiety was also positively correlated with depression (r = 0.278, P < 0.001). Mediating Effect Analysis

The mediation model using caregiver anxiety as the outcome showed that patient self-care behaviors were significantly associated with caregiver burden. Specifically, better patient self-care behaviors were related to lower caregiver burden (a = −1.088, SE = 0.023, P < 0.001). After patient self-care behaviors and covariates were controlled, caregiver burden remained significantly associated with anxiety (b = 0.204, SE = 0.074, P = 0.006). The total effect of patient self-care behaviors on caregiver anxiety was significant (c = −0.203, SE = 0.040, P < 0.001). After caregiver burden was added to the model, the direct effect of patient self-care behaviors on anxiety was no longer significant (c′ = 0.019, SE = 0.090, P = 0.833).

The bootstrap analysis further supported the mediating role of caregiver burden. The indirect effect was −0.222, and the 95% bootstrap confidence interval ranged from −0.393 to −0.053, which did not include zero. This finding indicates that caregiver burden significantly mediated the relationship between patient self-care behaviors and caregiver anxiety.

A second mediation model was constructed with caregiver depression as the outcome. Patient self-care behaviors were again significantly and negatively associated with caregiver burden (a = −1.088, SE = 0.023, P < 0.001). Caregiver burden was strongly and positively associated with depression after adjustment for patient self-care behaviors and covariates (b = 0.821, SE = 0.021, P < 0.001). The total effect of patient self-care behaviors on caregiver depression was significant (c = −0.776, SE = 0.022, P < 0.001). After caregiver burden was included in the model, the direct effect became positive and remained statistically significant (c′ = 0.117, SE = 0.025, P < 0.001). The indirect effect through caregiver burden was −0.893, with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval of −0.948 to −0.837, indicating a significant mediation effect.

The adjusted model for caregiver depression explained 96.3% of the variance. This very high explained variance suggests that the depression model should be interpreted carefully, particularly because the correlations among patient self-care behaviors, caregiver burden, and depressive symptoms were extremely strong. Although the mediation effect was statistically significant, the possibility of measurement overlap or shared response patterns should be considered when interpreting the clinical meaning of this result. Discussion

This study examined the relationships among patient self-care behaviors, caregiver burden, and psychological distress in caregivers of patients with diabetic foot ulcers receiving home-based wound care. The results showed that caregiver burden and psychological distress were common in this population. Patient self-care behaviors were negatively associated with caregiver burden, anxiety, and depression, while caregiver burden was positively associated with both anxiety and depression. Further mediation analysis suggested that caregiver burden mediated the association between patient self-care behaviors and caregiver psychological distress. These findings indicate that the psychological condition of caregivers may be closely linked not only to their own caregiving role but also to the patient’s ability to participate in daily self-management.

In the present study, many caregivers reported moderate to severe burden, and a considerable proportion experienced anxiety or depressive symptoms.20 This finding is understandable in the context of diabetic foot ulcer care. Diabetic foot ulcers often require prolonged wound observation, infection prevention, dressing protection, blood glucose control, pressure relief, and timely response to changes in wound condition. When these tasks are transferred to the home environment, family caregivers may become the main coordinators of daily care.21 They are expected to support patients while also dealing with uncertainty about wound healing, recurrence, infection, medical expenses, and the possibility of amputation. Unlike short-term postoperative care, diabetic foot ulcer care is often slow, repetitive, and unpredictable This may explain why caregivers in this study showed a relatively high level of burden and psychological distress.

A key finding of this study was that better patient self-care behaviors were associated with lower caregiver burden. This result supports the idea that patient self-management and caregiver experience are closely connected in home-based wound care.22 When patients are able to follow dietary recommendations, monitor blood glucose, protect the affected foot, adhere to medication, and cooperate with wound care, caregivers may need to provide less supervision and repeated reminders. In contrast, poor self-care may increase the caregiver’s involvement in daily disease management.23 Caregivers may need to monitor the patient’s behaviors, correct unsafe practices, arrange follow-up visits, assist with mobility, and respond to wound-related problems. Over time, these additional responsibilities may accumulate and become a substantial subjective burden.

The study also found that patient self-care behaviors were negatively related to caregiver anxiety and depression. In daily practice, caregivers may feel more reassured when patients actively participate in their own care and show stable self-management behaviors. Conversely, when patients have poor adherence or limited self-care ability, caregivers may experience worry, frustration, helplessness, or fear of deterioration.24 For diabetic foot ulcers, even small lapses in self-management may raise concerns about infection, delayed healing, or worsening tissue damage. Therefore, patients’ self-care behaviors may influence caregivers not only through practical care demands but also through caregivers’ perceived uncertainty and emotional security.25

Caregiver burden was positively associated with anxiety and depression. Caregiver burden reflects the pressure caused by caregiving tasks, role disruption, emotional exhaustion, financial strain, and restriction of personal life. In the home-based wound care setting, caregivers may need to remain alert to wound changes and patient symptoms for long periods.26 They may also have to balance caregiving with employment, household responsibilities, and their own health needs. When caregiving demands exceed personal resources, psychological distress may gradually develop. Anxiety may be more closely related to uncertainty, fear of wound deterioration, and concern about whether care is being performed correctly. Depression may be more related to persistent fatigue, reduced personal time, low perceived control, and the long duration of caregiving.

The mediation analysis further showed that caregiver burden mediated the relationship between patient self-care behaviors and caregiver anxiety. The total effect of patient self-care behaviors on anxiety was significant, but the direct effect became non-significant after caregiver burden was included in the model. This suggests that caregiver burden may be a major pathway through which patient self-care behaviors influence caregiver anxiety. In other words, better self-care among patients may reduce caregivers’ subjective burden, which in turn may alleviate anxiety. This finding has practical meaning. It indicates that interventions aimed only at reducing caregiver anxiety may be insufficient if the daily care burden remains unchanged. Strengthening patients’ self-management ability and reducing caregivers’ practical workload may be necessary to improve caregivers’ emotional outcomes.27

A similar mediating effect was observed for caregiver depression. Patient self-care behaviors were associated with caregiver burden, and caregiver burden was strongly associated with depressive symptoms. However, this model should be interpreted with particular caution. The correlation between ZBI and SDS was extremely high, and multicollinearity diagnostics showed relatively high VIF values for SDSCA, ZBI, and SDS, although they did not exceed the conventional threshold of 10. This pattern suggests that severe multicollinearity was not formally confirmed, but shared method variance, response tendency, or partial conceptual overlap between caregiver burden and depressive symptoms may have influenced the regression estimates. The ZBI and SDS do not contain identical items, but both scales include content related to fatigue, emotional strain, reduced personal functioning, and difficulty in daily life. Therefore, the depression mediation finding should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Future longitudinal studies using item-level diagnostics, multi-informant data, or latent-variable modeling are needed to verify whether caregiver burden remains a distinct pathway linking patient self-care behaviors to caregiver depression.28

These findings have several implications for home-based wound care. First, patient self-care support should be viewed not only as a patient-centered intervention but also as a caregiver-support strategy. Improving patients’ self-care behaviors may reduce caregivers’ supervision tasks and emotional pressure. Second, caregiver burden should be routinely assessed during home-based wound care. Caregivers are often treated as care resources, but their burden and mental health may be overlooked. Third, home-based diabetic foot care should adopt a dyadic approach. The patient and caregiver form an interdependent care unit, and education and care planning should include both members of the dyad.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design does not allow causal inference. Although the proposed mediation model is theoretically plausible, the temporal order among patient self-care behaviors, caregiver burden, and psychological distress cannot be confirmed. Second, the distinction proposed between anxiety and depression is exploratory and should not be interpreted as evidence of different causal pathways. Future longitudinal studies are needed to examine whether uncertainty-related caregiving stress is more closely linked to anxiety and whether prolonged role overload is more closely linked to depressive symptoms over time. Third, the study relied mainly on questionnaire data, which may be influenced by recall bias and common-method bias. Although additional diagnostic checks were conducted, common-method effects and construct overlap, especially between caregiver burden and depressive symptoms, could not be completely ruled out. Fourth, patient self-care behaviors were assessed using a general diabetes self-care measure. Future studies may benefit from using or developing self-care instruments that are more specific to diabetic foot ulcer management. Fifth, participants were recruited from home-based wound care services, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Multi-center studies with more diverse samples are needed to confirm these results.

Conclusions

This study found that patient self-care behaviors were closely associated with caregiver burden and psychological distress among caregivers of patients with diabetic foot ulcers receiving home-based wound care. Patients with better self-care behaviors tended to have caregivers with lower perceived burden and fewer symptoms of anxiety and depression. Caregiver burden further played a mediating role in the relationships between patient self-care behaviors and caregiver psychological distress, suggesting that the effect of patient self-care on caregiver mental health may partly operate through changes in caregiving burden.

These findings highlight the need to view home-based diabetic foot ulcer care as a patient-caregiver dyadic process. Strengthening patients’ self-care ability may not only benefit wound management but also reduce the pressure experienced by family caregivers. At the same time, routine assessment of caregiver burden and psychological distress should be incorporated into home-based wound care services. Interventions that combine patient self-management education, caregiver support, burden reduction, and psychological guidance may help improve the sustainability and quality of home-based care for patients with diabetic foot ulcers. However, longitudinal or intervention studies are still needed before this proposed pathway is used to justify specific intervention design or implementation in clinical practice.
