Multi-sampling allows intra-tumoral heterogeneity querying and vulnerability profiling in glioblastoma
Authors: Diogo Moniz Garcia, Wan-Hsin Lin, Daniel P Wickland, Erik Jessen, Erik H Middlebrooks, Lauren E Haydu, Mieu Brooks, Ryan W Feathers, Lindsey Kinsella, Chris Sereduk, Steven S Rosenfeld, Nhan L Tran, Kaisorn L Chaichana, Panos Z Anastasiadis, Alfredo Quiñones-Hinojosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlioblastoma (GBM) remains a devastating cancer with limited treatment options, largely due to its heterogeneity. While supramaximal resection has recently provided survival benefits, therapeutic profiling of different tumor compartments, particularly its infiltrative edge remains largely unexplored.MethodsHere, we leveraged magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-guided multi-sampling, collecting 2 cores and 2 margins per case, to query GBM heterogeneity. Whole-exome and RNA-seq with drug testing in two patient-derived 3D models were used to reveal similarities and differences in genomic and transcriptomic makeups, cellular compositions, and drug responses across cores and margins. Bioinformatics interrogations further identified response biomarkers.ResultsMutation analysis showed that oncogenes exhibited a higher degree of spatial heterogeneity than tumor suppressor genes, regardless of MRI status. While the mesenchymal transcriptional subtype with extracellular matrix remodeling, stress response, and immune programs were preferentially enriched in enhancing cores, proneural tumors with neurological processes favored non-enhancing margins. Using a 15-drug GBM-targeted panel, ERK (ulixertinib) and PI3K pathway (paxalisib, CC-115) inhibitors showed preferential efficacy in enhancing cores and non-enhancing margins, respectively. The anti-apoptosis, pan-Bcl2 agent navitoclax and the epigenetic drug trotabresib represented the most effective, tumor-wide monotherapies. Importantly, drug combinations generally outperformed single agents across all regions.ConclusionsThis work demonstrates the regional heterogeneity of therapeutic vulnerabilities in GBM ex vivo, showing various drugs with tumor-wide or MRI-enhancement informed activity. These findings offer preclinical bases of numerous monotherapies and drug combinations for future clinical trial design.

Importance of the Study

We utilize 2 patient-derived 3D models to demonstrate, for the first time, the intra-tumoral heterogeneity of therapeutic vulnerability in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-defined areas of glioblastoma (GBM). We also extend the understanding of the molecular landscapes across contrast-enhancing cores and non-enhancing margins. MRI-defined tumor compartments inform transcriptomic programs but not DNA alterations. While the ERK inhibitor ulixertinib and PI3K pathway-targeting drugs paxalisib and CC-115 show spatially defined activities with moderate effects in enhancing cores and non-enhancing margins, respectively, the pan-Bcl2 agent navitoclax and bromodomain-targeting drug trotabresib exhibit strong, tumor-wide activities. Combination therapies outperform monotherapies and achieve high tumor-wide effects. This work builds upon the emerging supramaximal resection approach and not only presents vulnerability heterogeneity but also provides therapeutic profiles of the previously understudied GBM margins, providing a potential framework to advance clinical trial designs.

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most devastating adult brain cancer with limited therapeutic options and inevitable treatment resistance, largely due to tumor heterogeneity.1-3 In the operating room, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) traditionally guides surgical resection of the contrast-enhancing tumor (cET) core, leaving behind the infiltrative non-enhancing (nET) margin that includes active tumor cells and edematous tissue. Recent evidence shows that more aggressive supramaximal resections (SMR), which remove GBM beyond cET, significantly improve patient outcomes.4 This approach not only allows for sampling of underexplored nET but provides a novel avenue to holistically examine tumor heterogeneity and GBM therapeutic landscapes.

Multi-omics profiling of tumor samples collected across spatial regions and over time has advanced our understanding of GBM biology and its molecular and cellular heterogeneity.5-13 However, these advances have not yet translated into effective targeted therapies for routine patient care. Moreover, few studies have investigated responses to targeted therapy in both cET and nET. To address this gap, we applied an unbiased strategy using two distinct 3D culture models to evaluate GBM susceptibility to targeted agents. These models include well-established brain tumor-initiating cells (BTICs) and our recently developed microCancer (µCancer) platform. BTICs maintain genomic features of the original tumors and are widely used to study GBM proliferation, stem-like properties, migration and invasion, therapeutic response, and in vivo tumorigenesis.14-16 The short-term µCancer model preserves genomic and transcriptomic fidelity as well as tumor heterogeneity, and its rapid turnaround time makes it especially attractive for clinical decision-making (Lin and Kosari et al unpublished manuscript).17,18

Our ultimate goal is to inform clinical decision-making by defining personalized therapeutic vulnerabilities of individual tumors using patient-derived tumor models. To enable seamless translation of our approach into future clinical trials, we used cost-effective and clinically widely used methods, including whole exome sequencing (WES) and bulk RNA-seq, for molecular characterization. WES offers comprehensive coverage of coding regions compared to targeted panel-based sequencing and provides higher coverage depth with manageable data sizes compared to whole-genome sequencing (WGS). We also included bulk RNA-seq for gene expression analysis as single-cell strategies remain in need of standardization and validation prior to its clinical implementation.

Here, we employed MRI-guided multi-sampling not only for multi-omics profiling but also for functional drug testing in two independent patient-derived models. Six treatment-naïve patients with newly diagnosed isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH)-wildtype GBM were included in the study. To enable multiple applications for each sample, namely fresh tissues for 2 cell model development for a pre-defined drug testing panel with GBM-related agents and flash-frozen tissue for WES and RNA-seq, we prospectively aimed to collect an equal number of cET cores and nET margin samples (ie 2 and 2) from each tumor, enabling regional distinct interrogation of therapeutic heterogeneity and molecular characterization. WES with copy number inference revealed spatial heterogeneity of oncogene amplifications and tumor suppressor gene (TSG) deletions. Mutation-based characterization showed that oncogenes exhibited significantly greater spatial variability than TSGs. Transcriptomic analyses further uncovered divergent cellular states and pathway activations between GBM cores and margins. Functional drug testing identified MRI compartment-specific vulnerabilities and tumor-wide sensitivities, while combination regimens outperformed single agents. Together, this multi-scale, spatially integrated approach provides a blueprint for linking GBM heterogeneity to actionable vulnerabilities, with direct implications for therapeutic design and precision oncology.

Methods

Patient Enrollment and Sample Acquisition

A total of 6 patients with GBM sized >3cm in largest diameter were included to ensure an ample amount of tissue for our platform, each with 4 samples collected. T1- and T2-weighted brain MRI was preoperatively performed and reviewed by a neuro-radiologist to identify cET and nET regions, followed by neuronavigation-guided tumor sample collection. Collection occurred early in each case to minimize brain shift and ensure navigation accuracy. Categorization into cET and nET was made intraoperatively by the neurosurgeon. Each treatment-naïve tissue specimen was equally split, with 1) 2 portions for the generation of BTIC and µCancer, one each, and 2) 1 for sequencing following processing and snap-freezing by the institutional Biospecimen Accessioning and Processing Core (Figure 1A, Supplementary Figure S1A). Based on our experience, each model requires at least 100 mg, preferably 200 mg, of fresh tissue for each model generation. Therefore, in order to safeguard the normal surgical flow, including the collection of samples used for pathological diagnosis, we collected 2 nET samples and 2 cET samples from each patient. Samples for model generation were immediately transported from the operation room in 1xPBS-containing tubes on ice to the laboratories for tissue dissociation. Blood samples were collected for germline WES.

Imaging Analysis and 3D Spatial Mapping

Preoperative, postcontrast T2 FLAIR, and T1-weighted images were coregistered using an affine registration implemented in Statistical Parametric Mapping software (SPM12; Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging, University College London, London, UK). Regions-of-interest (ROIs) for enhancing and T2 hyperintense nonenhancing tissues were segmented from the T1-weighted and T2 FLAIR images, respectively, using a semi-automated threshold approach in ITK-SNAP (v4.0; www.itksnap.org). Sample locations tracked in the surgical navigation system were mapped to DICOM coordinates and annotated by a spherical ROI. Next, the binary ROIs and the corresponding skull-stripped brain model were transformed into a 3D geometry definition file using a custom script and imported into ParaView software (v5.12; ParaView, Kitware Inc., Clifton Park, NY, USA) for visualization.

Sequencing Pipeline and Bioinformatic Processing

Germline DNA and tumor DNA/RNA from snap-frozen tissues were extracted using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Cat# 69504) and AllPrep DNA/RNA Mini kit (Cat# 80204), respectively. Novogene performed variant calling and transcript quantification from WES and RNA-seq except 003-c2 and 007-m2, respectively, due to an inadequate amount (Supplementary Figure S1B).19

Tumor Purity

Tumor purity was estimated using PureCN.20 Because the somatic VCFs provided by Novogene were not compatible with PureCN, somatic variants between each tumor/normal pair were re-called using Mutect2 (GATK version 4.5.0.0).21 The inputs to Mutect2 were the tumor and normal bam files; gnomAD germline annotations from GATK (af-only-gnomad.hg38.vcf.gz); background statistics from a panel of normals of germline VCFs from all 6 samples used in this study; and a bed file containing the capture bait coordinates used during sequencing.

Copy Number Variation Calling from Exome Samples

Exome germline (normal) and somatic (tumor) had coverage quantification and copy number variant (CNV) detection performed by PatternCNV.22 The exon bed file for exome capture was sorted and merged for overlapping regions. Germline samples were used to create reference exon profiles for calling on somatic samples using default parameters. Adjusted coverage accounts for GC bias and sex chromosome number. Gene-level copy numbers were calculated by averaging the exon copy number values mapped to each respective gene as a function of the exon size. Copy number is reported as log2 CNV ratio as a function of the median coverage across a sample. Genes with multiple unique mappings to the human genome, specifically those mapping to both chrX and chrY, were excluded.

Transcriptional Subtyping

Two GBM transcriptional classifiers were used in this study. The TCGA classification was implemented using the R package ssGSEA.GBM.classification with 500 permutations. Simplicity scores were calculated as previously described.23 The pathway-based classification and corresponding simplicity scores were conducted using the web tool GBMclassifier.24,25

Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis

Gene network analysis was carried out on all samples except 007-m2 using the weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) package in R,26 which clusters genes into modules based on co-expression. In brief, a signed-hybrid network was constructed using the log2-normalized FPKM values with a soft-thresholding power of 16, a mergeCutHeight of 0.25, and a minimum module size of 30, resulting in a total of 45 modules. We correlated the module eigengene values (ie the first principal component of a module) with MRI enhancing status. Modules with P < .05 were considered significantly correlated with enhancing status.

Bulk RNA-Seq Deconvolution

RNA-seq deconvolution was performed to estimate cell-type proportions using CIBERSORTx27 in fractions mode with 100 permutations and default settings. A glioma signature matrix was used as a reference.7

Correlation between Treatment Response and Molecular Alterations

Hierarchical All-against-All association testing (HAllA)28 was used to assess the relationships between treatment response, gene expression, and copy-number variation. Features were first hierarchically clustered within each dataset based on similarity. Associations between all possible feature pairs were then computed using Spearman’s rank correlation, while accounting for similarity between features to limit false-positive associations. Treatment response defined by percent inhibition at Cmax CNA was represented by log2 CN ratio. Gene expression was represented by FPKM of all genes expressed in at least one sample, a more permissive filtering strategy to include more data to improve statistical power for the HAllA algorithm. The expression values or log2 CN ratios of cancer genes significantly associated with drug response at P <.005 were visualized by heatmaps generated using the ComplexHeatmap R package ComplexHeatmap.29

Generation of Patient-Derived GBM Tumor Models, Drug Testing, and Data Analysis

For µCancers, fresh tissue specimens were subjected to mechanical and enzymatic dissociation using the MACS tissue dissociation kit, human (130-095-929, Miltenyi Biotec), following manufacturer’s instructions. Dissociated cells were then resuspended in µCancer culture media containing DMEM/F12 (SH30023.01, HyClone) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated horse serum (16050-122, Gibco), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (MT30002CI, Corning), Y-27632 (10 µM, ALX-270-333, Enzo Life Sciences or S1049, Selleckchem), human EGF (10 ng/ml; AF-100-15, PeproTech), and insulin (5 µg/ml; I1882, Sigma). An equal number of cells (5x103) was seeded in 96-well Akura PLUS hanging drop plates (CS-03-001-00, InSphero) and cultured for 6 days to allow for µCancer establishment. Established µCancers were then transferred to 96-well clear round-bottom ultra-low attachment microplates (7007, Corning) for 8-concentration drug testing in the absence of Y-27632.

For BTICs, freshly resected tissue specimens were processed as previously.14 Briefly, cells were grown in 3D in anti-adhesion solution (07010, StemCell)-pretreated 75T flasks (156800, Thermo). Cells were maintained in the growth media containing L-glutamine and HEPES-containing DMEM/F12 media (25-503, GenClone or 11330-032, Invitrogen) supplemented with vitamin-A minus B27 (50x; 12587-010, Gibco) or Gem21 NeuroPlex (400-161, Gemini), antibiotic-antimycotic (100x; 15-240-062, Invitrogen), 20 ng/mL human EGF (AF-100-15, PeproTech), and 20 ng/ml basic FGF (100-18B, PeproTech). For drug testing, dissociated cells (5x103 in 100 µl per well) were cultured in 96-well clear round-bottom ultra-low attachment microplates (7007, Corning) for 4 days followed by targeted drug treatment across 8 concentrations for 6 days. Cell viability was assessed using the CellTiter-Glo luminescent assay (G7570, Promega).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis of drug efficacy was performed using GraphPad Prism software as indicated in the figure legends, and SAS 9.4 (Cary, NC) software was utilized for construction of generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLIMMX). To test the additional fixed effects of (a) Euclidean distance of the specimen from the tumor centroid, (b) patient sex, and (c) MRI status of the location of the specimen (ie enhancing or non-enhancing); each covariate was separately added to the first GLIMMX, however, none of the three resulting statistical models converged and are therefore not reported. To minimize inflation of Type I error due to multiple comparisons, we applied a Bonferroni correction to account for three comparisons. The threshold for statistical significance is set to P <.010 to be conservative.

Results

MRI-Guided Spatial Sampling Enables GBM Heterogeneity Characterization

An integrated platform was developed by combining intraoperative multi-regional sampling, WES and RNA-seq profiling, and functional interrogation using 2 patient-derived models (Figure 1A). Six treatment-naïve patients (4 males and 2 females) with newly diagnosed IDH-wildtype GBM were included (Supplementary Table S1). Two cET cores and 2 nET margins from distinct regions for each case were collected (Figure 1B, Supplementary Figure S1C, Supplementary Videos S1-S6), subjected to sequencing and model generation followed by drug testing. We exploited two complementary 3D culture systems to probe therapeutic vulnerabilities: BTICs, enriched in stem-like populations,14,31 and our newly developed µCancers, derived from bulk tumor tissue without selection or long-term expansion18 (Figure 1A). Whereas BTICs model cancer stem cells (CSCs), µCancers capture the broader tumor ecosystem, including differentiated GBM cells and non-neoplastic cell populations.18

Tumor-Wide and Region-Specific CNA Patterns in GBM

We first examined spatial distribution of copy-number alterations (CNAs), focusing on the GBM cytogenetic hallmark of chromosomes 7 gain (+7) and 10 loss (−10), which represent initial drivers of gliomagenesis.32,33 WES-inferred, tumor purity-corrected CNAs revealed the canonical +7/−10 signature in nearly all samples, except for the PT003 nET margin (Supplementary Figure S1D). Stratification of Log2 copy-number ratio (cnLog2R), values by cET and nET showed no significant association between +7/−10 amplitude and MRI enhancement. Tumor purity was statistically independent of enhancing status (Supplementary Figure S1E). Further, tumor enhancement and purity did not correlate with tumor distance from the centroid (Supplementary Figure S1F and G), consistent with irregular, infiltrative distribution of GBMs. These findings indicate that +7/−10 are clonal, tumor-wide events retained across both core and infiltrative margins, consistent with margins representing an extension of the tumor core.34

In contrast, focal CNAs of key GBM oncogenes and TSGs displayed marked spatial heterogeneity. EGFR amplification was observed in ∼50% cases, tumor-wide in some but restricted to individual regions in others, supporting parallel, region-specific evolutionary trajectories (Figure 1C, Supplementary Figure S1H). PDGFRA amplification showed similar diversity and exhibited a modest trend toward mutual exclusivity with EGFR (Supplementary Figure S1I). Additionally, heterogeneity was observed in MDMX amplification, which co-occurred with RTK amplification in most regions but was absent in some (Figure 1C). Notably, CDKN2A was deleted tumor-wide in most cases but retained in one nET region of PT008 despite universal CDKN2C loss. Together, these findings indicate that while clonal CNAs such as +7/−10 and CDKN2C loss defined tumor-wide events, amplification of EGFR, PDGFRA, MDMX, and CDKN2A deletion showed regional restriction. Importantly, these patterns were independent of MRI enhancement, highlighting that genomic heterogeneity extends beyond radiographic boundaries.

MRI-Independent Spatial Heterogeneity of SNVs and Indels

To complement CNA profiling, we assessed the spatial distribution of small variants, including single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertion-deletions (indels) across multi-region GBM samples. Approximately one-third of mutations were truncal and tumor-wide, while the remainder were shared or region-specific, with little alignment to MRI-defined compartments (Figure 1D, Supplementary Figure S2A-C).

EGFR exemplified this complexity. Although amplification was often clonal (Figure 1C, Supplementary Figure S1H), missense mutations showed distinct, regionally restricted patterns, with some tumors carrying multiple parallel variants on the same amplification background (Figure 1E, Supplementary Figure S2D). Additionally, PT004 uniquely harbored a tumor-wide PDGFRB gain-of-function mutation p. R561C that is rarely reported in GBM, suggesting an underrecognized role for PDGFRB signaling.

Moreover, mutations of TSGs such as PTEN, ATRX, LZTR1, and STAG1/2 were frequently clonal and shared across all regions (Figure 1E). These findings reveal a dichotomy of GBM evolution where neuro-oncology-related oncogene mutations diversify regionally to promote adaptive plasticity and show intra-tumoral heterogeneity, while TSGs remain largely clonal, providing a stable malignant framework (Figure 1F, P <.0001). This architecture underscores the limitations of radiographic compartmentalization and highlights the need for spatially resolved molecular profiling to guide therapeutic strategies.

Enhancing Status Reflects Spatial Transcriptional and Cellular Programs in GBM

Genomic alterations provide a blueprint to drive tumorigenesis, whereas transcriptional states more directly reflect cellular phenotypes and microenvironmental interaction. To uncover how transcriptional programs map onto radiographic compartments, we profiled RNA expression across multi-regional samples using 2 well-established transcriptional classifiers. With The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) classifier (proneural [PN], classical [CL], and mesenchymal [MES] subtypes),23 3 cases exhibited a uniform representative subtype across regions (PN in PT002 and PT007; CL in PT006), whereas the remaining tumors showed regional divergence (Figure 2A). Notably, MES states were preferentially enriched in cET core, while PN dominated infiltrative nET margins (Figure 2B). These transcriptional subtypes were independent of distance from tumor centroid (Supplementary Figure S2E), suggesting that enhancing status stratifies transcriptional identity.

Similar analysis was performed with the pathway-based classifier, which contains proliferative/progenitor (PPR), neuronal (NEU), mitochondrial (MTC), and glycolytic/plurimetabolic (GPM) functional subtypes.25 Enhancing cores were enriched for PPR and GPM subtypes, whereas nET margins were more enriched in MTC and NEU subtypes (Figure 2A-C), similar to other studies.8,35,36 Collectively, these results indicate that transcriptional states showed a better association with enhancement status than DNA alterations, suggesting that MRI-defined compartments reflect functional differences in tumor cell state.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)26 identified 3 modules (purple, blue, plum1) significantly enriched in cET and 2 (midnightblue, saddlebrown) in nET (Figure 2D and E, Supplementary Table S2). cET modules were enriched for extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, immune activation, and stress response (consistent with MES-like biology), whereas nET modules highlighted neuronal signaling, morphogenesis, and synaptic pathways, implicating tumor-neural circuit integration at the invasive edge (Figure 2F). Collectively, transcriptional heterogeneity, not genetic alterations, tracks with enhancement and reveal functional divergence between GBM core and margins. (Figure 2G).

To resolve how cellular ecosystems contribute to this divergence, we deconvoluted bulk RNA-seq data using CIBERSORTx27 with glioma-specific signature matrix.7 Twelve distinct cell states emerged, spanning neoplastic, stromal, glial, and immune compartments (Figure 3A). Differentiated-like neoplastic cells were enriched in cET, often linked to pseudopalisading necrosis,7 whereas oligodendrocytes localized to nET (Figure 3B and C, Supplementary Figure S3A), consistent with Ivy GBM atlas project (Ivy GAP) data.37 Stem-like populations showed high variability between tumors but no consistent enrichment by enhancement, suggesting coexistence of multiple glioma stem-like cell subtypes.

When stratified by transcriptional subtypes, proliferative stem-like states mapped to PN and PPR subtypes, while differentiated-like states were enriched in the MES/GPM tumors, which exhibited low stemness signatures (Figure 3D and E, Supplementary Figure S3B). These opposing PN-MES trends in cell state enrichment remained consistent across both core and margins, underscoring the distinct roles of PN and MES programs in shaping tumor cell properties (Figure 3F). Together, these data show that GBM cET cores and nET margins are distinguished not by genetic alterations, but by transcriptional and cellular ecosystems. Specifically, cores enriched for MES, GPM, and PPR states with ECM, stress, and immune programs, whereas margins enriched for PN, NEU, and MTC states with neuronal and morphogenetic processes (Figure 2G). This radiogenomic convergence highlights the clinical potential of MRI enhancement as a proxy for underlying transcriptional and cellular programs in GBM.

Spatially Distinct Therapeutic Vulnerabilities Revealed by Patient-Derived 3D Models

To functionally test how spatial heterogeneity affects therapeutic response, we established paired 3D models from cET and nET, including stem cell-enriched BTICs and bulk tumor-derived µCancers. Across 24 tumor regions from 6 patients, µCancers were generated with high efficiency, while BTICs were less frequent, in particular, derived from margins. Tumor purity showed no significant effect on the success of model establishment (Figure 4A and B, Supplementary Figure S4A). This marks the first use of matched core- and margin-derived 3D model systems for therapeutic interrogation.

Drug efficacy was determined as percent inhibition at Cmax, obtained from human clinical trials or FDA drug labels (Supplementary Table S3). Given that brain penetration pharmacokinetic data was not available for all tested drugs, this reference allows for drug efficacy assessment in a more physiologically relevant manner than an arbitrary concentration. We first tested the GBM standard-of-care treatment with radiation and chemotherapy TMZ. Because TMZ induces cytotoxicity through the progressive accumulation of DNA lesions over multiple cell cycles,30 the expandable BTIC model was used for these experiments. Despite the small sample size, our model recapitulated clinical observations showing that tumors with O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase promoter (MGMTp) methylation are more sensitive to TMZ (Supplementary Figure S4C-E).38-40 Further, heterogeneous responses to radiation were observed both within and across cases.

Next, a panel of 15 targeted agents were developed and tested in established BTIC and µCancers (Figure 4C, Supplementary Table S3). This drug panel was chosen to 1) target GBM core pathways identified by TCGA studies5,41; 2) the epigenetic modulator BRD4-involved YAP/TAZ signaling42-44 and telomere elongation,45,46 both of which are critical for gliomagenesis; and 3) the DNA damage repair pathway, which is important for tumorigenesis.47 Drug response across both models revealed generally consistent response patterns, with BTICs showing overall stronger effects (Figure 4C-E, Supplementary Figure S4B). This is likely due to lesser complexity of cancer cell populations in BTICs from selection during long-term expansion. Varying degrees of intra-tumoral heterogeneity were observed (Figure 4F and G, Supplementary Tables S4 and S5). In µCancers, PT002 and PT007 exhibited the highest (40.5 ± 27.6%, mean±SD), and lowest (7.8 ± 6.0%) degree of heterogeneity across tested agents, respectively (Figure 4F, middle [orange-scale heatmap], right). In BTICs, therapeutic heterogeneity was observed in three assessable cases (PT003, PT004, PT007) showing 7.8 ± 7.5%, 23.9 ± 7.67%, and 14.7 ± 13.2% of inhibition heterogeneity across 15 agents, respectively (Figure 4G, orange-scale heatmap).

Collectively, 3 drugs—trotabresib (BET inhibitor), navitoclax (pan-BCL2 inhibitor), and capivasertib (pan-AKT inhibitor)—consistently showed the greatest efficacy across tumors, whereas EGFR inhibitors were uniformly ineffective despite frequent EGFR alterations (Figures 1C and E and 4E, Supplementary Figure S1H), mirroring largely unsatisfactory clinical trials.48,49 Despite intra- and inter-tumoral heterogeneity, at least 2 moderately effective agents were identified for each region, and trotabresib and navitoclax demonstrated tumor-wide efficacy (Figure 4F and G, asterisks).

Importantly, therapeutic sensitivity varied by spatial compartments. Margin-derived µCancers were more sensitive to the PI3K-pathway inhibitors (paxalisib, CC-115) (Figure 5A). In contrast, core-derived models showed a greater sensitivity to ERK inhibition, aligning with ECM remodeling and stress-response programs in cET (Figures 2F-G and 5A). These data provide direct functional evidence that MRI-defined compartments harbor distinct drug vulnerabilities linked to their spatial biology.

To explore predictive biomarkers, we applied hierarchical All-against-All (HAllA) analysis28 to the vulnerability data from µCancers owing to a larger analysis power than BTICs. To enrich the biological relevance, we focused our analysis on OncoKB/COSMIC-defined cancer genes50,51 and presented those showing significant correlation (P <.005). Ripretinib sensitivity correlated with copy number gains at 4q12 (KIT, PDGFR, KDR), consistent with its pharmacologic targets (Figure 5B, Supplementary Figure S4F). Navitoclax response is associated positively with BCL2L1 copy number and negatively with BCL2L2, in line with its potency against BCL2L1-encoding BCL-xL52 (Figure 5B, Supplementary Figure S4F and G). The HAllA approach was then applied to transcriptomic profiles and identified differential gene expression correlated with µCancer response to navitoclax and trotabresib, the most tumor-wide effective agents (Figures 4E and F and 5C, Supplementary Figure S4H). The GO: BP term enrichment analysis of positively associated genes showed that trotabresib sensitivity was enriched in cases with histone H3-K4 mono/di-methylations (Figure 5C). This suggests on-target BRD4 inhibition, as transcriptionally active promoters/enhancers and super-enhancers are marked by H3K4 methylation and H3K27 acetylation and can be exploited by BRD4 to promote GBM tumorigenesis.53,54 Additionally, tumors with enriched RTK/Ras signaling responded better to navitoclax (Figure 5C), supporting the known functional interactions between Ras and Bcl-2 family proteins.55 Together, these analyses suggest that spatially distinct drug vulnerabilities and molecular correlates could inform patient stratification and guide therapeutic strategies in GBM.

Rational Combinations Enhance Tumor-Wide Therapeutic Response in GBM

Given the heterogeneity and adaptive plasticity of GBM, we tested rational drug combinations, alongside single agents, to co-target cooperative oncogenic pathways. We focused on the MEK and PI3K/mTOR pathways, which are major downstream effectors of RTK signaling commonly activated in GBM,5,41 using pimasertib and paxalisib, respectively. We also prioritized inclusion of epigenetic therapies in our panel (Figure 4C) because of their broad effects on gene expression, a key determinant of GBM tumorigenesis and heterogeneity.56-59 Thus, vorinostat (HDAC inhibitor) and trotabresib (BET inhibitor), both of which target transcriptional dysregulation,42,43,60,61 were evaluated together and individually in combination with paxalisib and/or pimasertib. We also combined vorinostat with navitoclax to promote cell death through distinct complementary mechanisms (Figure 4C). In our models, monotherapies produced only modest effects, reaching at best approximately 50% of inhibition in µCancers, and 50%-75% in BTICs (Figure 4E). Across both µCancer and BTIC models, combinations consistently outperformed monotherapies, expanding tumor-wide vulnerabilities and often achieving ≥65% inhibition (Figure 6A and B, Supplementary Tables S6 and S7).

We also constructed two multivariable generalized linear mixed models (GLIMMX) for fixed effects of drug and interaction term. The first GLIMMX (Figure 6C, Supplementary Table S8) effectively centered the baseline estimate near zero (-0.5%, 95% confidence interval [CI]: -30.9-29.8, P = .9644). BTIC models had significantly higher responses compared with µCancer (14.9%, 95%, CI: 11.0-18.8, P <.0001). Trotabresib, compared with the least effective agent, osimertinib, had 71.0% higher inhibition (P <.0001). Navitoclax (66.3%, P <.0001), and capivasertib (52.0%, P = .0013) also had significantly higher estimates of inhibition when compared with osimertinib (ie baseline, zero). Capmatinib, paxalisib, pimasertib, and vorinostat monotherapies did not statistically demonstrate any efficacy in the model (ie responses were not statistically different than zero). The significance of the interaction term indicates that when administered in combination, only moderate efficacy is achieved that is significantly better than zero: vorinostat (39.6%, P = .0013), paxalisib (39.1%, P = .0015), pimasertib (34.8%, P = .0051). In a second GLIMMX (Figure 6D, Supplementary Table S9) that aimed to compare all tested strategies against osimertinib, we effectively centered the baseline estimate near zero (-0.5%, 95%, CI: -21.8-20.8, P = .9524). All tested combinations achieved statistical significance when compared with osimertinib (baseline), with the greatest response estimated at 73.7% for the combination regimen vorinostat and navitoclax, (P <.0001). When accounting for case-based tumor-wide activity, the two most broadly effective combinations were noted to be trotabresib combined with vorinostat or pimasertib (Figure 6A and B, asterisks). These findings demonstrate that rational drug combinations targeting parallel signaling and epigenetic networks can overcome the limitations of monotherapy and enhance tumor-wide therapeutic responses in GBM.

Discussion

Glioblastoma continues to lack targeted treatment options, despite extensive understanding of its disease biology and heterogeneity. In contrast, the surgical field recently demonstrated that SMR extending beyond cET into infiltrative nET can improve survival.4 This observation highlights the clinical importance of tumor regions historically considered surgically/therapeutically inaccessible. Building on this opportunity, our study represents, to our knowledge, the first systematic study to interrogate GBM spatial heterogeneity across therapeutic as well as molecular and cellular layers, explicitly linking radiographic compartments with functional tumor biology and drug vulnerability (Figure 6E).

Our approach revealed several key insights. First, oncogene amplifications and TSG deletions exhibited both tumor-wide and heterogeneous distributions, consistent with prior reports.8,36 Second, neuro-oncology-related oncogene mutations, but not TSG, presented a high degree of intra-tumoral heterogeneity. Importantly, these genetic events were not associated with MRI-defined enhancing status. In contrast, transcriptional states appear to be related to enhancement status. cET cores exhibited a trend towards enrichment in MES, PPR, and GPM programs, together with ECM modeling, immune activation, and stress response signatures. Of note, while we and others demonstrated MES and GPM enrichment in cET cores,8,35,62 others have shown divergent findings,24,36,63 which reflect GBM’s known heterogeneity. nET margins, on the other hand, trended towards enrichment in PN, MTC, and NEU states, reflecting a distinct biology of infiltrative cells that engage neural circuits and favor metabolic adaptation over proliferative stress.35,64,65 These findings highlight that radiographic enhancement captures dynamic transcriptional and microenvironmental programs rather than static genomic lesions.

At the cellular level, deconvolution of regional transcriptomes showed that cETs harbored more differentiated-like tumor cells, which were reported at high levels in pseudopalisading cells surrounding GBM necrosis,7 whereas nETs were enriched for oligodendrocytic populations and neuronal programs. Stem-like states varied across tumors but showed no consistent spatial bias, suggesting coexistence of multiple glioma stem-like cell programs. When aligned with transcriptional subtypes, proliferative stem-like states predominated in PN/PPR tumors, while differentiated-like states enriched MES/GPM tumors, reflecting opposite PN-MES axes of stemness. These patterns reinforce that GBM cell states are dictated by transcriptional programs that map onto radiographic compartments.

Therapeutically, our patient-derived 3D models revealed that spatial heterogeneity is mirrored by distinct drug sensitivities. EGFR inhibitors were strikingly ineffective in both cores and margins, consistent with overall clinical trial failures48,49 and in contrast to early preclinical models.66,67 Additionally, ERK blockade (ulixertinib) preferentially suppressed cET cores, while PI3K pathway inhibitors (paxalisib, CC-115) were more effective in nET margins. These spatial biases may reflect stress-induced ERK signaling in hypoxic ECM-rich core versus PI3K activation in neuronally integrated margins. Importantly, several agents demonstrated broad tumor-wide efficacy, including navitoclax (pan-Bcl2), trotabresib (BET), and capivasertib (pan-AKT) suggesting that these are promising backbones for future therapeutic development strategies. Bioinformatics interrogation further suggested response biomarkers, including PDGFRA/KIT/KDR CNAs predicting ripretinib sensitivity, which require prospective validation in larger patient cohorts. An additional finding from this work is the demonstration that drug combinations consistently outperformed single agents. This observation is particularly relevant for GBM, where intra- and intertumoral heterogeneity undermines monotherapy approaches. Successful human translation may require strategies to identify optimal dosing and reduce toxicity via careful pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic studies, sequential therapy, and planned drug holidays. An alternative solution would lie in identifying (i) “pan-inhibitors” capable of clocking convergent pathways, or (ii) nodal vulnerabilities across multiple signaling networks to deliver clinical benefits through a single pharmacological intervention.

Our study advances current understanding of GBM, although several limitations and methodological constraints should be noted. Each specimen was intended to support DNA and RNA extraction from flash-frozen tissue as well as generation of 2 models from cryopreserved tissue; however, some samples were insufficient in quantity or quality for WES, RNA-seq, and full-panel drug testing. Tumor purity also varied across samples, with the lowest purity observed in 008-m1 (Supplementary Figure S1E). However, purity was accounted for in copy-number profiling and was unlikely to affect drug responses in BTICs, which were enriched for stem-like populations and displayed response profiles similar to those of μCancers (Figure 4D). To support data interpretation, tumor purity estimates derived from DNA profiling are provided in the figures. We included 2 complementary GBM models in this study. BTICs are a well-established and widely used model,14-16 but they require time to establish and may therefore be subject to culture-induced selection. At the same time, they are enriched in tumor-initiating cells that are critical for gliomagenesis and progression and provide a scalable platform for broader downstream applications. In contrast, μCancers offer faster turnaround (within 2 weeks) with less selection pressure and better preservation of tumor heterogeneity.18 Although this model is limited by resource constraints, it showed a higher success rate than BTICs in our study, as expected for a short-term culture system. While we did not observe an effect of tumor purity on model establishment, larger studies may help clarify this relationship. Further, we used established gene sets and signature matrix to identify transcriptional subtypes and cellular composition across cET and nET regions, while WGCNA was applied to identify data-driven, cohort-specific modules of co-expressed genes without reliance on prior predefined pathways. These approaches allow us to relate our findings to existing literature and provide orthogonal support for transcriptional subtyping. In contrast, HAllA was used to identify molecular features associated with drug response to targeted agents. As such, we focused on cancer genes showing significant association (P <.005) and may not capture the broader transcriptional programs. Together, these analyses provide complementary insights and highlight the need for larger cohorts to enable integrated modeling of different molecular features and therapeutic responses while minimizing overfitting.

Treatment options for GBM remain largely limited to chemoradiation with no approved targeted therapies. Substantial efforts have focused on improving responses to standard-of-care treatment and identifying patients who may benefit from targeted therapies. Several drug testing platforms have emerged, including commercially available assays.68-72 Unlike these platforms, which rely on a single tumor sample, our dual-model approach incorporates multi-regional sampling to capture therapeutic heterogeneity of GBM.73 As our study and others support the integration of image-guided heterogeneity into clinical trial design and patient stratification,8,9,36 treatment decision-making could benefit from identifying shared molecular alterations and drug sensitivities across regions. This concept is already being actively explored in other cancers characterized by substantial intratumoral heterogeneity.74-76 Comprehensive incorporation of molecular and therapeutic heterogeneity with surgical information, such as extent of resection (EOR) and the location of residual tumor, would maximize therapeutic benefit for patient care.

In summary, our work provides to our knowledge the first preclinical evidence that therapeutic vulnerabilities in GBM are spatially heterogeneous yet informed by radiographic enhancement for some. Through patient-derived models, we identified compartment-specific and tumor-wide agents, validated the superiority of drug combinations, and identified response biomarkers for clinical testing. This integrated approach combining drug testing and molecular profiling offers a blueprint for the development of spatially guided treatment strategies and clinical trials in GBM, with the goal of overcoming the heterogeneity that has long impeded progress in this lethal disease.
