Gene signatures and networks: linking COVID-19 to liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Ning Wang, Xue Wang, Xue Zhou, Guoyue Yang, Qing Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) outbreak has widespread impacts on the patients with pre-existing conditions. It is well-established that liver cirrhosis (LC) is a significant risk factor in the etiopathogenesis of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). This study was undertaken to investigate the potential gene signatures and the gene regulatory networks between COVID-19 and LC-HCC.MethodsRelevant gene signatures were identified through the shared differentially expressed genes (DEGs) based on COVID-19 and LC-HCC utilizing the bioinformatics analysis. Subsequently, the gene functional enrichment analysis (including Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO)) and protein-protein interaction (PPI) network were performed to identify the hub gene as the key biomarker. Finally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, along with biological function and gene expression regulatory network analyses were systematically performed.ResultsThe study successfully identified 78 common candidate gene signatures distinguishing COVID-19 from liver cirrhosis-hepatocellular carcinoma (LC-HCC). Subsequent KEGG and GO enrichment analyses revealed that these gene signatures were predominantly associated with the cell cycle. In constructing the PPI network, the core gene CDK1 was successfully identified based on the ten scoring methods. Furthermore, ROC analysis demonstrated that CDK1 possesses excellent predictive efficacy for both COVID-19 (AUC = 0.955) and LC-HCC (AUC = 0.946) cohorts. Moreover, it was observed that HCC patients with elevated CDK1 expression had poorer prognoses. Finally, the comprehensive gene regulatory networks were established.ConclusionThis study successfully identified the key biomarker and gene regulatory networks between COVID-19 and LC-HCC, thereby contributing to the prediction of clinical outcomes and the identification of novel therapeutic targets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11916-0.Keywords: COVID-19, Liver cirrhosis, Hepatocellular carcinoma, Differentially expressed genes, Biomarker

Introduction

Presently, it is widely assumed that SARS-CoV-2 infection is the main reason for the widespread of COVID-19, a global phenomenon characterized by its rapid evolution and widespread transmission, resulting in the affliction of numerous individuals who exhibit severe clinical manifestations [1, 2]. The COVID-19 pandemic has created the enormous medical pressure to healthcare systems and social economy globally [3, 4]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), more than 15 million COVID-19-related deaths have been reported worldwide. The patients infected with COVID-19 often show signs of respiratory like fever, coughing, and trouble breathing, but it may also be accompanied by gastrointestinal symptoms such as nausea and diarrhea [5, 6]. However, it has been observed that COVID-19 has an impact on various organs beyond the respiratory system [7].

Research findings have indicated that a range of 15–54% individuals with COVID-19 patients present with abnormal liver function [8–10]. Mechanisms of liver damage related to COVID-19 includes drug-induced injury, inflammatory response, hypoxic ischemia-reperfusion injury, and direct toxic effects of the virus [11]. Furthermore, the hepatocytes can be impacted by the SARS-CoV-2 virus, leading to direct liver damage and the initiation of immunopathological processes within the liver. Although hepatic injury is typically transient in most patients, severe cases of COVID-19 have been associated with permanent liver damage. Some studies have found that patients with pre-existing cirrhosis are more likely to present vulnerable to SARS-CoV-2 infection owing to their compromised immune system [12]. Consequently, cirrhosis patients with COVID-19 infection exhibit significantly worse prognosis.

It is well known that liver cirrhosis has been coined as a very strong hazard factor for the etiopathology of hepatocellular carcinoma. Among the various types of cancer, HCC ranks as the sixth most prevalent worldwide [13]. Moreover, the patients diagnosed with cancer have been found to have a heightened susceptibility to contracting SARS-CoV-2 and experiencing a more severe disease progression [14]. However, the influence of SARS-CoV-2 on the patients with cirrhosis and the trends of liver cancer remain unidentified. Taken together, we still need more researches to investigate the characteristics and mechanism of the development of LC and HCC with COVID-19 infection, to improve patients’ outcomes. This study was conducted for exploring the potential therapeutic targets for selecting more treatment options to ameliorate the prognosis.

Currently, researchers have also explored the pathogenesis of COVID-19 and sepsis through bioinformatics and systems biology approaches, offering novel strategies for future COVID-19-related research and treatment [15]. Additionally, blood transcriptome analysis was conducted to uncover a genetic association between COVID-19 and preeclampsia [16]. Machine learning and artificial neural networks were employed to investigate the immune microenvironment and molecular subtypes of hepatitis B-associated cirrhosis [17]. In the present study, our focus was on investigating the common gene signatures between COVID-19 and LC-HCC, to elucidate the underlying mechanism. The datasets GSE157103 for COVID-19 and GSE63898 for LC-HCC were employed to discover differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with COVID-19 and LC-HCC, respectively. Through intersecting COVID-19-DEGs and LC-HCC-DEGs, 78 shared DEGs were screened out. Next, the function of these candidate genes was evaluated using KEGG and GO enrichment analyses. Additionally, the hub gene was successfully identified according to the PPI network. And the predictive accuracy of hub gene in COVID-19 and LC-HCC groups was determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Finally, we completed a thorough analysis of gene regulatory networks to comprehensively explore the potential gene regulation model. This research might help to explore the novel therapeutic targets for improving the prognosis of the patients.

Materials and methods

Data collection

For identifying the shared gene signatures between COVID-19 and LC-HCC, two datasets GSE157103 and GSE63898, were obtained from GEO database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The GSE157103 dataset was processed using the GPL24676 platform (Illumina NovaSeq 6000), comprising 100 samples from COVID-19 patients and 26 control samples. The GSE63898 dataset utilized the GPL13667 platform (Affymetrix Human Genome U219 Array) and included 228 hepatocellular carcinoma samples and 168 liver cirrhosis samples. Both GSE157103 and GSE63898 datasets contain gene expression data that are highly relevant to the subject of our study. These datasets have undergone rigorous standardization, ensuring high data quality and reliability. When performing joint analysis of data from different datasets, we first extracted the common gene symbols shared across multiple datasets, then labeled each dataset as a distinct batch, and used the removeBatchEffect function to eliminate batch effects.

Identification of COVID-19 and LC-HCC related DEGs

To recognize the DEGs of COVID-19 and LC-HCC, the differential gene expression levels were assessed using the “Limma” package via R software [18]. The criteria for filtering differential mRNA expression were assigned as “adjusted P < 0.05 and Fold Change > 2”. A volcano plot is a commonly used bioinformatics visualization tool, primarily employed to display the magnitude of gene expression changes and the significance of these changes (adjusted p-value). The heatmaps are used to represent the expression levels of genes across the samples. Next, the volcano plot and heatmap were used to display the differentially expressed genes in the COVID-19 and LC-HCC groups. The shared DEGs in both COVID-19 and LC-HCC groups were identified utilizing the Venn diagram [19].

Function enrichment analysis

The function enrichment analysis of GO and KEGG for the shared DEGs between COVID-19 and LC-HCC groups was performed using the “ggplot2” and “Cluster Profiler” packages [20]. Moreover, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed to further investigate the significant signaling pathways based on KEGG and HALLMARK.

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis

The interactive proteins associated with gene signatures were investigated through protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis. By virtue of the STRING website (https://string-db.org/) [21], PPI network of the shared gene signatures was created, which was visualized using Cytoscape (Version: 3.8.0). Then, the hub gene was identified using ten scoring methods, which was exhibited via a Venn diagram [22].

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and gene expression analysis

ROC analysis was performed to judge the prediction efficiency of the hub gene according to the value of area under the curve (AUC) [23]. Furthermore, the association between hub gene and the clinicopathologic features of HCC patients were displayed utilizing Sankey diagram by the “ggalluvial” package [24].

Genetic alteration analysis and survival analysis

According to the somatic mutation data, the link between hub gene expression and genetic alterations in HCC cohorts was evaluated and visualized via the “maftools” package [25]. Moreover, the correlation between hub gene expression and patients’ clinical outcomes, including overall survival (OS), disease specific survival (DSS), and progression free interval (PFI) were carried out via the “survival” package [26].

Immune characteristics analysis

The correlation of the hub gene expression with the immune cells infiltration in HCC was explored via TIMER database [27]. Additionally, the correlation between the hub gene expression and the immune checkpoints was evaluated.

Regulatory networks analysis of gene signature, transcription factor (TF), and miRNA

The analysis of gene regulatory networks, such as TF-gene interaction network, gene-miRNA interaction network, and TF-miRNA coregulatory network were performed using the NetworkAnalyst 3.0 platform. In detail, we chose the ENCODE ChIP-seq database for analyzing the interactions between transcription factors and genes. We chose the miRTarBase v8.0 database for the gene-miRNA interaction network, which includes extensive experimentally confirmed data on miRNA-gene interactions. Ultimately, we established the TF-miRNA coregulatory network using the RegNetwork database.

Statistics analysis

All R packages utilized in this study were conducted via R v4.0.3 software (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, 2020). To control the false discovery rate (FDR) in our high-throughput data analysis, we analyzed the adjusts p-values to account for the large number of hypotheses tested, ensuring a balance between discovering significant results and minimizing false positives. The adjusted p-values were used to determine statistical significance, with a threshold of adjusted p < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

Identification of the common DEGs between COVID-19 and LC-HCC

The general research program was exhibited via a flow chart (Fig. 1). For the identification of the link between COVID-19 and LC-HCC, the gene expression of the COVID-19 cohorts in GSE157103 dataset and LC-HCC cohorts in GSE GSE63898 dataset were analyzed. The results revealed that there were 1364 DEGs (1259 up-regulation and 105 down-regulation) based on the COVID-19 cohorts (Fig. 2A) and 550 DEGs (144 up-regulation and 406 down-regulation) in LC-HCC cohorts (Fig. 2B). The specific information of DEGs can be obtained from Supplementary Data. Furthermore, the intersection analysis was performed via Venn, and 78 common DEGs were ultimately identified (Fig. 2C). The heatmap displayed the expression of DEGs in the cohorts (Fig. 2D and E). The specific information of the shared DEGs can be obtained from Supplementary Data. Moreover, we performed GO enrichment analysis of the 78 shared DEGs, and the Top5 pathways of BP, CC, and MF were displayed (Fig. 3A–C). The KEGG analysis of the shared DEGs mainly participated in cell cycle (Fig. 3D).

Establishment of PPI network and exploration of the hub gene between COVID-19 and LC-HCC

To identify the interaction of the proteins, the STRING database was applied to establish the PPI network of 78 shared DEGs (Figure S1). The top10 hub genes were screened using the 10 topological methods provided by the CytoHubba plug-in via Cytoscape software (Fig. 4A–J). The specific information of top10 hub genes can be obtained from Supplementary Data. After the intersection of top10 hub gene sets obtained from different topological methods, CDK1 was eventually identified as the core hub gene (Fig. 5). Furthermore, GSEA analysis was performed to explore the potential signaling pathways based CDK1 expression in COVID-19 and LC-HCC cohorts via KEGG and HALLMARK (Fig. 6A-D).

CDK1 expression level and diagnostic efficacy

We first investigated CDK1 expression, and the results revealed that CDK1 was upregulated in the patients infected with COVID-19, compared to the control group (Fig. 7A). Meanwhile, we also discovered that there was high CDK1 expression in HCC patients compared to the cirrhosis group (Fig. 7C). Furthermore, ROC analysis indicated that the AUC values of CDK1 were 0.955 and 0.946 in COVID-19 and LC-HCC cohorts, respectively, suggesting CDK1 had high diagnostic efficacy (Fig. 7B and D).

Clinical features of CDK1 expression in HCC

Additionally, we focused on exploring the clinical features of CDK1 expression in HCC. The association of CDK1 expression with different clinicopathological characteristics, including age, stage, and survival status in HCC cohorts, were displayed via Sankey diagram (Fig. 7E). The result of somatic mutation analysis suggested that HCC samples with high CDK1 expression appeared higher mutation (Fig. 7F). Furthermore, the results indicated that the HCC patients with high CDK1 expression exhibit a poor clinical prognosis based on Kaplan–Meier survival analysis of OS, DSS, and PFI (Fig. 7G-I). Consequently, CDK1 has the potential to be a prognosis-related biomarker in HCC patients. Moreover, the result of TIMER analysis revealed that CDK1 expression is positively correlated with the infiltration state of immune cells in HCC samples (Fig. 7J). Finally, we found that there was upregulation of immune checkpoint genes, including CD274, CTLA4, HAVCR2, LAG3, PDCD1, TIGIT, and SIGLEC15 in HCC patients with high CDK1 expression (Fig. 7K). Furthermore, we prepared a summary diagram showing the association of CDK1 with signaling pathways, immune interactions, and clinical outcomes (Figure S2).

Construction of regulatory networks of CDK1-associated transcription factor (TF) and miRNA

We further conducted the regulatory networks of CDK1-associated transcription factor (TF) and miRNA via NetworkAnalyst 3.0 platform. As shown in Fig. 8A, there displayed the regulatory network of CDK1-associated TF, of which CDK1 was regulated by 18 TF genes. Moreover, the regulatory network between CDK1 and miRNA was established, of which there were 52 CDK1-associated miRNAs (Fig. 8B). Finally, the CDK1-TF-miRNA coregulatory network was constructed, which indicated the complexity of regulatory associations between CDK1 and TF-miRNA (Fig. 8C).

Discussion

Numerous researches have shown that the spike protein from SARS-CoV-2 has the ability to target the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor, resulting in the onset of COVID-19 infection [28, 29]. And the patients with SARS-CoV-2 infection developed the extra-pulmonary organ damage, including cardiovascular, kidney, and liver injury [30–32]. Recent research indicates that liver injury associated with COVID-19 is a complex phenomenon involving drug-induced damage, systemic inflammation, hypoxia-ischemia-reperfusion injury, and potential direct harm from the SARS-CoV-2 virus. ACE2 receptor is believed to exert a vital role in the development of liver damage in COVID-19 [33]. In addition, individuals with cirrhosis face an elevated risk of severe diseases from COVID-19, with those having compensated cirrhosis being particularly susceptible to decompensation following the infection. In consequence, the cirrhosis patients with COVID-19 have been found to have higher mortality rates.

Some studies have found SARS-CoV-2 can be detected in the liver in view of its broad organ affinity. First, SARS-CoV-2 may directly cause dysfunction to the liver cell membrane leading to liver injury [34]. Second, the patients with chronic liver disease (liver fibrosis/cirrhosis) have the high expression of ACE2, aggravating the hepatotropism of SARS-CoV-2, leading to liver damage [35]. Liver fibrosis is characterized by the excessive accumulation and abnormal distribution of extracellular matrix in the liver, representing a pathological reparative process in response to chronic injury. This progression is a critical factor in the development of various chronic liver diseases, such as cirrhosis, liver cancer, and end-stage liver disease. Recent studies showed higher mortality was present in cirrhotic patients with COVID-19, compared to non-cirrhotic patients. In fact, there’s growing evidence that liver cirrhosis patients with immunocompromised status have high risk of COVID-19 infection, and exhibit high incidence of severe cases and mortality after infection [36–38]. Owing to COVID-19 infection, acute liver decompensation and liver failure have occurred in patients with cirrhosis, with a high mortality [39, 40]. The etiology may be caused by the combination of direct virus damage and cytokine storm, or it may be due to the inherent immune disorder and gut–liver axis dysfunction caused by COVID-19 infection.

However, the potential risk of developing liver cancer in cirrhotic patients with COVID-19 remains unclear. Moreover, the key regulatory targets and complicated gene expression regulatory networks between COVID-19 and LC-HCC are poorly defined. Therefore, it is in urgent need of exploring the molecular mechanisms between COVID-19 and LC-HCC. The primary objective of our research was to investigate the common gene signatures and gene regulatory networks between COVID-19 and LC-HCC. Initially, DEGs were determined in separate cohorts of COVID-19 and LC-HCC. By identifying the intersection of these DEGs, the shared DEGs between COVID-19 and LC-HCC were ultimately determined. Subsequently, the key hub gene CDK1 was identified based on the PPI network. According to the above results, CDK1 has shown promise as a critical molecular target between COVID-19 and LC-HCC. Next, we explored the expression level and the diagnostic efficacy of CDK1 in COVID-19 and LC-HCC cohorts. We found that CDK1 was high upregulated in COVID-19 and HCC samples, and has high diagnostic validity. Furthermore, we investigated the biological function of CDK1 in HCC cohorts. The HCC patients with high CDK1 expression presented poor clinical prognosis. Additionally, CDK1 expression was positively associated with the immune cell infiltrate and immune checkpoints in HCC cohorts. In summary, it can be found that CDK1 can be considered as an immune-based prognostic biomarker in HCC. Finally, we developed CDK1-associated gene regulatory networks encompassing CDK1-TF interaction, CDK1-miRNA interaction, and CDK1-TF-miRNA coregulatory networks, which could be helpful in uncovering the underlying mechanisms.

CDK1 is known to regulate cell cycle progression and may influence viral replication by modulating host cell machinery. CDK1 inhibitors have been identified as potential therapeutic targets for COVID-19 based on the drug-gene network [41]. Additionally, CDK1 may contribute to the hyperinflammatory response observed in severe COVID-19 cases by promoting the activation of pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, such as NF-κB or STAT3. CDK1 is a key regulator of cell cycle progression and is often overexpressed in cancer, including HCC. Its overexpression can drive uncontrolled cell proliferation and inhibit apoptosis, contributing to tumorigenesis [42]. We hypothesize that CDK1 may serve as a molecular bridge between COVID-19 and LC-HCC by promoting a pro-inflammatory and pro-proliferative microenvironment. For instance, COVID-19-induced inflammation and tissue damage may upregulate CDK1 expression, which in turn exacerbates liver injury and accelerates the progression of LC-HCC. Furthermore, CDK1 may enhance the susceptibility of liver cells to oncogenic transformation by impairing DNA repair mechanisms or promoting genomic instability. Research has found that SAMHD1 possesses a CDK1 target motif, which protects the assembly of COVID-19 viral particles. The tetramerization of SAMHD1 is an important pathophysiological mechanism underlying COVID-19-associated acute kidney injury and liver injury [43]. Several small-molecule inhibitors of CDK1 have been developed and tested in preclinical and clinical studies, particularly in the context of cancer. The small-molecule inhibitors targeting CDK and CDK-related proteins have demonstrated their significant roles in tumor chemotherapy and radiotherapy [44]. The small-molecule inhibitor of CDK1, RO-3306, significantly inhibits cell proliferation in GIST (gastrointestinal stromal tumor) with high CDK1 expression, providing a novel strategy and preclinical experimental data for overcoming imatinib resistance [45]. Consequently, combining CDK1 inhibitors with existing therapies, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors or tyrosine kinase inhibitors, may enhance therapeutic efficacy and overcome resistance mechanisms.

This study is subject to certain limitations. Notably, the sample size remains inadequate despite of the collection of numerous samples of COVID-19 and LC-HCC. To make these results more reliable, we expect that a larger sample size is required to verify these findings. Therefore, we will collect enough samples and validate the potential association. When performing joint analysis of data from different datasets, we first extracted the common gene symbols shared across multiple datasets, then labeled each dataset as a distinct batch, and used the removeBatchEffect function to eliminate batch effects. The heterogeneity of data from different experimental platforms and conditions may introduce variability. While we have employed robust statistical methods to integrate and harmonize the data. Furthermore, in vitro and in vivo experiments will be conducted to confirm the role of CDK1 in COVID-19 and LC-HCC. And the functional assays will also be performed to assess the biological function of CDK1 on cellular processes in HCC. The molecular mechanisms by which CDK1 may influence disease progression will be further explored. The investigations of gene signatures and the gene regulatory network contribute to identifying the key regulatory molecule and revealing the potential mechanism. Additionally, it was necessary to explore the potential significance of CDK1 in predicting clinical prognosis and identifying the novel therapeutic targets for HCC patients. It is hoped that future research will yield the necessary clinical evidence to substantiate these findings.

Conclusion

In summary, this research investigated the overlapping gene signatures between COVID-19 and LC-HCC, and identified CDK1 as the hub gene. Moreover, the exploration of the potential biological function and gene regulatory network of CDK1 were helpful in the development of novel therapeutic targets.
