Spectral properties of Lévy Fokker–Planck equations
Hardy Chan, Marco A. Fontelos, María del Mar González

Abstract
. Hermite polynomials, which are associated to a Gaussian weight and solve the Laplace equation with a drift term of linear growth, are classical in analysis and well-understood via ODE techniques. Our main contribution is to give explicit Euclidean formulae of the fractional analogue of Hermite polynomials, which appear as eigenfunctions of a Lévy Fokker–Planck equation. We will restrict, without loss of generality, to radially symmetric functions. A crucial tool in our analysis is the Mellin transform, which is essentially the Fourier transform in logarithmic variable and which turns weighted derivatives into multipliers. This allows to write the weighted space in the fractional case that replaces the usual L r 2 ​ ( ℝ n , e | x | 2 / 4 ) L_{r}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{|x|^{2}/4}) . After proving compactness, we obtain a exhaustive description of the spectrum of the Lévy Fokker–Planck equation and its dual, the fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck problem, which forms a basis thanks to the spectral theorem for self-adjoint operators. As a corollary, we obtain a full asymptotic expansion for solutions of the fractional heat equation.



1.
Introduction and statement of results

Given
s\in(0,1)
,
n\geq 2s
, we would like to describe the asymptotic behavior of the fractional heat equation

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\varphi_{t}+(-\Delta)^{s}\varphi=0,&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},t>0,\\
\varphi|_{t=0}=\varphi_{0},&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{cases}

in terms of convergence to its fundamental solution. In this paper we will always restrict to radially symmetric functions, that is, they depend only on the variable
r=|x|
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

In the classical case, that is, for
s=1
, it is well known that solutions of the heat equation converge to a Gaussian and there are several proofs of this fact (see the survey
[
49
]
, for example). The functional analytical approach is to study the heat equation in self-similar variables, which yields a Fokker–Plank equation. A precise description of the spectrum for this Fokker–Planck equation in
L^{2}
spaces with Gaussian weights, and its relation to an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, allows to obtain more precise asymptotics in terms of higher order moments.

In the fractional case, while one still obtains convergence to the fundamental solution in relative error (see, for instance,
[
50
]
and the references therein), the open question of finding the right analytic framework for refining this convergence has persisted for some time.

In this paper we give an answer to this problem, calculating in Theorem
1.1
the full spectrum of the Lévy Fokker–Planck operator

(1.2)

L_{s}u:=(-\Delta)^{s}u-\frac{1}{2s}x\cdot\nabla u-\frac{n}{2s}u,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

and of its dual, the fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck equation, in Theorem
1.3
. For this, we provide a suitable function space where this operator is self-adjoint. As a consequence, we immediately obtain asymptotics of all orders for (
1.1
).

Let us state our precise results. The fundamental solution for the fractional heat equation is given by

(1.3)

G_{s}(t,x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{n}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}e^{-t|\xi|^{2s}}\,d\xi.

It is well-known (e.g.
[
5
]
) that
G_{s}
is not of Gaussian type, but a radially decreasing function with polynomial tails:

G_{s}\left(t,x\right)=\frac{1}{t^{\frac{n}{2s}}}\mathcal{G}_{s}\left(\frac{|x|}{t^{\frac{1}{2s}}}\right)\asymp\dfrac{t}{(t^{\frac{1}{s}}+|x|^{2})^{\frac{n+2s}{2}}}.

Long-time behavior and asymptotic convergence to (a multiple) of the fundamental solution can be seen by writing equation (
1.1
) in self-similar variables

(1.4)

\tilde{x}=\frac{x}{(t+1)^{\frac{1}{2s}}},\quad\tilde{t}=\log(t+1),\quad\varphi(t,x)=\frac{1}{(1+t)^{\frac{n}{2}}}u(\tilde{t},\tilde{x}),

obtaining

u_{\tilde{t}}+(-\Delta_{\tilde{x}})^{s}u-\frac{1}{2s}\tilde{x}\cdot\nabla_{\tilde{x}}u-\frac{n}{2s}u=0,\quad\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,\tilde{t}>0,

which is the fractional version of the Fokker–Planck equation, usually termed Lévy Fokker–Planck. For simplicity, in most of our upcoming discussion, we will drop the tilde in the self-similar variables and just denote them by
(t,x)
. Thus, it is natural to consider the fractional Fokker–Planck operator
L_{s}
introduced in (
1.2
) which, for a radially symmetric function
u=u(r)
, is given by

(1.5)

L_{s}u=(-\Delta)^{s}u-\frac{1}{2s}r\partial_{r}u-\frac{n}{2s}u,\quad r>0.

This operator contains a fractional diffusion and a first order drift in competition (the zeroth order term does not play a significant role in our discussion, although it simplifies under Fourier transform).

Our first Theorem deals with the eigenvalue problem

(1.6)

L_{s}u=\nu u,\quad u=u(r),

which is a fractional version of the Hermite differential equation.

In the local case
s=1
, solutions to (
1.6
) in the space
L_{r}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{|x|^{2}/4})
of radial functions with an inverse Gaussian weight are given in terms of Hermite (
n=1
) or Laguerre (
n\geq 2
) polynomials
– we refer to Section
2.1

for more details. For general
s\in(0,1)
we introduce a suitable functional space
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}(s)
in which the operator
L_{s}
still enjoys good compactness properties and one can use the spectral theorem to produce a (countable) basis of eigenfunctions for (
1.6
). We characterize the full spectrum in Theorem
1.1
below.

The main idea behind
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}(s)
is that, changing the radial Fourier variable
\zeta
to its power

(1.7)

\vartheta=\zeta^{s}

brings the non-local operator
L_{s}
to the local
L_{1}
. More precisely, let
u^{\dagger}(\rho)
to be the inverse Fourier transform of
\widehat{u}(\zeta)
in the new variable
\vartheta
, that is,

u^{\dagger}(\rho)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\vartheta\to\rho}\left\{\widehat{u}(\vartheta^{1/s})\right\}(\rho).

Then, the crucial observation (see
Lemma

4.1
) is that the operation
u\mapsto u^{\dagger}
relates
L_{s}
to
L_{1}
by

(1.8)

L_{s}u=f\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad L_{1}u^{\dagger}=f^{\dagger}.

That is, we can use the classical spectral theorem for
u^{\dagger}
in
L_{r}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{|x|^{2}/4})
and then translate it back to
u
. As a consequence, it is natural to define the space
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}(s)
by the scalar product

\begin{split}\left\langle{u_{1},u_{2}}\right\rangle_{\dagger}&=\int_{0}^{\infty}u_{1}^{\dagger}(\rho)\overline{u_{2}^{\dagger}(\rho)}e^{\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}}\rho^{n-1}\,d\rho.\end{split}

Nevertheless, we have found that working with Mellin transform, rather than in Fourier variables, yields more information on the fractional case. For this, we first realize that this scalar product may be written as

(1.9)

\left\langle{u_{1},u_{2}}\right\rangle_{\dagger}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi_{s}^{-1}u_{1}(r)\,\overline{\Phi_{s}^{-1}u_{2}(r)}\,e^{\frac{r^{2s}}{4}}r^{n(2-s)-1}\,dr,

where
\Phi_{s}
is a “quasi”-isometry defined through a Mellin multiplier, and described precisely in Section
4.2
. The relevant observation is that the transformation (
1.7
) is encoded in
\Phi_{s}
. Indeed, we
will show in Proposition
4.6
that

\Phi_{s}^{-1}u(r)=\left(\tfrac{r^{2s}}{4}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n}{2s}}u^{\dagger}(r^{s}).

Even though the Mellin formulation may seem a bit obscure at this point, we will show in Section
4.3
that
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}(s)
can be interpreted as a fractional version of a Gevrey–Sobolev space. More significantly, this approach allows to prove Theorem
1.1
without relying on previous knowledge of the spectrum in the local case. Instead, we give an independent calculation of
all
solutions of the eigenvalue problem (
1.6
).

Finally, note that when
s\to 1
, we recover the usual
L_{r}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{|x|^{2}/4})
space with a Gaussian weight. From now on, unless there is a risk of confusion, we will simply write
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}:=\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}(s)
, dropping the dependence on
s
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
s\in(0,1)
,
n\geq 2
, the Hilbert space
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}
has a complete orthonormal basis

\{e_{k}^{(s)}(r)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of eigenfunctions of
L_{s}
, that is,

L_{s}e_{k}^{(s)}=\nu_{k}e_{k}^{(s)},\quad\nu_{k}=k,\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.

The eigenfunctions are given uniquely (up to a scalar multiple) by

(1.10)

e_{k}^{(s)}(r)=\Phi_{s}\left\{\big(\tfrac{r^{2s}}{4}\big)^{-\frac{n(1-s)}{2s}}e^{-\frac{r^{2s}}{4}}L_{k}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}\big(\tfrac{r^{2s}}{4}\big)\right\},

or, alternatively, in terms of special functions by (
5.11
).

Here
L_{k}^{(\alpha)}
is the generalized Laguerre polynomial of degree
k
with parameter
\alpha
, as defined in
Appendix

A
.
In the Fourier domain, setting
\zeta=|\xi|
to be the radial Fourier variable, one has the equivalent formula

(1.11)

\widehat{e_{k}^{(s)}}(\zeta)=e^{-\zeta^{2s}}\zeta^{2sk},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.

We remark that even if we have an explicit formula for the eigenfunctions (
5.11
), it is useful consider different points of view. Working on the Fourier side, we first realize that
the change of variable (
1.7
)
transforms the eigenfunction (
1.11
) into

\widehat{e_{k}^{(s)}}(\vartheta)=e^{-\vartheta^{2}}\vartheta^{2k},

which is the eigenfunction in the local case
s=1
. In particular, for
k=0
, this change brings the non-local heat kernel (
1.3
) to the Gaussian
G_{1}(t,r)=\frac{1}{(4\pi t)^{n/2}}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{4t}}
.

However, working with the transformation
\Phi_{s}
in Mellin variables brings forward the analogy with the local case. Indeed, for any
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
, any (reasonable) solution of the eigenvalue problem (
1.6
) is of the form

e^{(s)}_{\nu}=\Phi_{s}U_{\nu}

for some
U_{\nu}
(given precisely in Theorem
5.6
) with the asymptotic behavior as
r\to\infty

(1.12)

\left\{\begin{split}&U_{k}(r)\sim\,c_{s,k}e^{-\frac{r^{2s}}{4}}\left(\tfrac{r^{2s}}{4}\right)^{k+\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n}{2s}}\quad\text{ for }\quad\nu=k\in\mathbb{N},\\
&U_{\nu}(r)\asymp\left(\tfrac{r^{2s}}{4}\right)^{-\nu-\frac{n}{2s}}\quad\text{ for }\quad\nu\notin\mathbb{N}.\end{split}\right.

The distinctiveness of the case
\nu\in\mathbb{N}
is seen in the reduction of Kummer functions to Laguerre polynomials; see
Remark

5.9
. This motivates the choice of scalar product in (
1.9
), since only eigenfunctions for
\nu=k\in\mathbb{N}
have exponential decay.
Note also that this the most technical step in the proof of Theorem
1.1
and it uses complex analytic methods.

Our results include a statement regarding uniqueness. Note that, as in the classical case
s=1
, additional energy bounds are required in lower dimensions. This can be seen, in the local case, from the fact that solutions of an ODE can be characterized in terms of the asymptotic behavior both at
r\to 0
and
r\to\infty
(this is a classical result; see, for instance,
[
36
, Proposition 2.2]
). However, in the non-local setting this simple approach is not valid; we use again Mellin transform. Finally, remark that the uniqueness result
Proposition

5.22
could become pathological when
n=1
, where we need to impose
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
.

As a direct application of our arguments we can apply separation of variables to the fractional heat equation (
1.1
) in the space
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}
to produce a solution of the equation in terms of the eigenvalue expansion from Theorem
1.1
:

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
\varphi_{0}\in\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}
be a radially symmetric function. Then the Cauchy problem (
1.1
) has a unique (radially symmetric) solution in
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}
given by

\varphi(t,r)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}e^{-\nu_{k}t}e_{k}^{(s)}(r),\qquad a_{k}:=\langle\varphi_{0},e_{k}^{(s)}\rangle_{\dagger},\ \nu_{k}:=k.

The first consequence of this expansion is that, as
t\to\infty
, the solution
\varphi
behaves as the self-similar solution
e_{0}^{(s)}=G_{s}(1,\cdot)
since the first eigenvalue is
\nu_{0}=0
, that is,

\varphi(t,r)\sim\frac{1}{t^{\frac{n}{2s}}}e_{0}^{(s)}\left(\frac{r}{t^{\frac{1}{2s}}}\right),

and similar formulas hold for all higher moments.

The adjoint of
L_{s}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck operator, given by

L_{s}^{*}w:=(-\Delta)^{s}w+\frac{1}{2s}x\cdot\nabla w,

which in the radially symmetric case reduces to

L_{s}^{*}w:=(-\Delta)^{s}w+\frac{1}{2s}r\partial_{r}w.

Note that the
L^{*}_{s}
operator appears naturally when we consider solutions to the heat equation which blows up in finite time. Indeed, if we change variables

\tilde{x}=\frac{x}{(T-t)^{\frac{1}{2s}}},\quad\tilde{t}=-\log(T-t),\quad\varphi(t,x)=w(\tilde{t},\tilde{x}),

in equation (
1.1
) we obtain

w_{\tilde{t}}+(-\Delta_{\tilde{x}})^{s}w+\frac{1}{2s}\tilde{x}\cdot\nabla_{\tilde{x}}w=0.

The
L^{2}
-dual space of
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}
will be denoted by
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\ddagger}
and it will be characterized in Section
6.2
. It is defined by the scalar product

\begin{split}\left\langle{w_{1},w_{2}}\right\rangle_{\ddagger}&=\int_{0}^{\infty}(\Phi_{s}^{*})^{-1}w_{1}(r)\overline{(\Phi_{s}^{*})^{-1}w_{2}(r)}\,e^{-\frac{r^{2s}}{4}}r^{sn-1}\,dr\end{split}

for a “quasi”-isometry
\Phi_{s}^{*}
. The results for
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\ddagger}
are parallel to those for
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\ddagger}
and we refer the reader to Section
6
for the precise statements. Nevertheless, in the latter case one has a more user-friendly formula for the scalar product in
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\ddagger}
. Indeed, formally

\left\langle{u_{1},u_{2}}\right\rangle_{\ddagger}=\int_{0}^{\infty}G^{\ddagger}_{s}(r_{1},r_{2})w_{1}(r_{1})w_{2}(r_{2})\,dr_{1}dr_{2},

for a kernel given in (
6.9
).

Our second Theorem characterizes the dual basis to that of Theorem
1.1
:

Theorem 1.3
.

The Hilbert space
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\ddagger}
has a complete (orthonormal) basis

\{\omega_{k}^{(s)}(r)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of eigenfunctions of
L^{*}_{s}
, that is,

L^{*}_{s}\,\omega_{k}^{(s)}=\nu_{k}\omega_{k}^{(s)},\quad\nu_{k}=k\in\mathbb{N}.

Here

(1.13)

\omega_{k}^{(s)}(r)=\Phi^{*}_{s}\left\{\frac{2s}{\Gamma(k+\frac{n}{2})}L_{k}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}(\tfrac{r^{2s}}{4})\right\},\quad k\in\mathbb{N},

up to multiplicative constant.

Similarly to (
1.8
), we observe (in
Lemma

6.2
) that
w\mapsto w^{\ddagger}
relates
L_{s}^{*}
to
L_{1}^{*}
:

(1.14)

L_{s}^{*}w=g\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad L_{1}w^{\ddagger}=g^{\ddagger}.

In the classical case, it is known that, if
w
is a solution to the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck problem

L_{1}^{*}w=-\mathcal{G}_{1}^{-1}\divergence(\mathcal{G}_{1}\nabla w)=0,

then
u=w\mathcal{G}_{1}
is a solution to the Fokker–Planck equation

L_{1}u=-\divergence(\mathcal{G}_{1}\nabla(\mathcal{G}_{1}^{-1}u))=0

(recall that
\mathcal{G}_{1}
is a multiple of
e^{-|x|^{2}/4}
). For other values of
s
we have an analogous relation, which can be obtained by combining (
1.8
)–(
1.14
). Indeed, if
u^{\dagger}=w^{\ddagger}\mathcal{G}_{1}
, then

L_{s}^{*}w=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad L_{1}^{*}w^{\ddagger}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad L_{1}(w^{\ddagger}\mathcal{G}_{1})=L_{1}u^{\dagger}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad L_{s}u=0.

2.
Motivation

This Section is not necessary in the proofs and the expert reader may skip it. However, we decided to include a preliminary discussion in order to illustrate our approach in a simple setting. Our objective is to find a precise asymptotic expansion as
t\to+\infty
for the solution to the heat equation (
1.1
), and to relate it to an eigenfunction decomposition. Recall that we are only considering radially symmetric functions. The radial coordinate is denoted by
r=|x|
.

2.1.
A review of the local case
s=1

Calculations are very transparent in the case
n=1
,
s=1
, so we describe first the classical approach to the heat equation (see
[
49
]
for a nice presentation on the subject). The asymptotic behavior of a solution of the heat equation

(2.1)

\begin{cases}\varphi_{t}+(-\Delta)\varphi=0,\quad r>0,t>0,\\
\varphi|_{t=0}=\varphi_{0},\end{cases}

is given by the self-similar solution

(2.2)

G_{1}(t,r)=\frac{1}{t^{\frac{1}{2}}}\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\mathcal{G}_{1}\left(\frac{r}{t^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right),\quad\text{for}\quad\mathcal{G}_{1}(r)=e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{4}}.

Indeed, as
t\to+\infty
,

\varphi(r,t)\sim MG_{1}(r,t),

where
M
is the mass of the initial data
M=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{0}(r)\,dr
. More precise asymptotics can be obtained by expanding via higher order moments, and we quote here the following formula
[
11
, Theorem 4]
(based on the results in
[
15
]
), which gives a precise asymptotic expansion for
\varphi
,

\varphi(t,r)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial r^{k}}G_{1}(r,t),

where the constants
a_{k}
,
k=0,1,\ldots
depend on the initial data. Recalling (
2.2
), we can rewrite this expansion as

(2.3)

\varphi(t,r)=\frac{1}{(4\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{k}{2}}}a_{k}\frac{d^{k}}{dr^{k}}\mathcal{G}_{1}\Big(\frac{r}{t^{\frac{1}{2}}}\Big).

Another way to understand this formula is to use Rodrigues’ formula for the Hermite polynomials
\mathcal{H}_{k}(r)
, which is

(2.4)

e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{4}}\mathcal{H}_{k}(r)=(-1)^{k}2^{k}\frac{d^{k}}{dr^{k}}\mathcal{G}_{1}(r).

A quick review of Hermite and Laguerre polynomials is given in Appendix
A
.

We define the Hilbert space
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}(1):=L^{2}_{r}(\mathbb{R},e^{\frac{r^{2}}{4}})
as the set of even functions on
\mathbb{R}
with the scalar product

\langle u_{1},u_{2}\rangle_{\dagger}=\int_{0}^{\infty}u_{1}(r)\overline{u_{2}(r)}\,e^{\frac{r^{2}}{4}}\,dr

and the weighted
L^{2}
-norm

\|u\|^{2}_{\dagger}=\int_{0}^{\infty}|u(r)|^{2}\,e^{\frac{r^{2}}{4}}\,dr.

Note that if we are looking for even functions, we need to restrict to even values of
k
and thus we define

e_{k}(r):=e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{4}}\mathcal{H}_{2k}(r).

An important property of the set
\{e_{k}(r)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is that they form a complete set of eigenfunctions for Fokker–Planck operator

(2.5)

L_{1}u:=-u^{\prime\prime}-\frac{1}{2}ru^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}u=\nu u,\quad u=u(r),

with eigenvalues
\nu_{k}=k
,
k=0,1,2,\ldots
(see Appendix
A
, taking into account the shift of notation
\nu=\frac{\lambda}{2}
, and the fact that we are restricting to even functions).

Now, the key observation is that (
2.3
) is just a separation of variables formula for the solution of (
2.1
) in terms of the eigenfunctions of the Fokker–Planck operator
L_{1}
, that is,

(2.6)

u(t,r)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}e^{-kt}e_{k}(r),

where the
\alpha_{k}
depend on the initial data. We recover (
2.3
) by going back to the original variables (
1.4
), up to the usual shifts

t\leftrightarrow t+1
and
k\leftrightarrow 2k
.

As a side comment, recall that Hermite polynomials are special cases of Laguerre polynomials and Kummer’s functions (see (
A.1
) and (
A.2
)), more precisely,

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}_{2k}(r)&=H_{2k}\big(\tfrac{r}{2}\big)=(-1)^{k}2^{2k}k!L_{k}^{(-1/2)}\big(\tfrac{r^{2}}{4}\big)=(-1)^{k}2^{2k}\frac{\Gamma(k+1/2)}{\Gamma(1/2)}M\big({-k},\tfrac{1}{2};\tfrac{r^{2}}{4}\big).\end{split}

This relation will be useful to understand the higher dimensional generalization.

Next, one may also look at the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck version, which is a crucial ingredient in the study of convergence in relative error
\varphi/G_{1}
.
The
L^{2}
-adjoint of the operator
L_{1}
is given by

L_{1}^{*}w:=-w^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{2}rw^{\prime}=\mu w,\quad w=w(r),

defined on the dual space of
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}(1)
, which is denoted by
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\ddagger}(1):=L^{2}_{r}(\mathbb{R},e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{4}})
, with the scalar product

\langle w_{1},w_{2}\rangle_{\ddagger}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}w_{1}(r)\overline{w_{2}(r)}\,e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{4}}\,dr

and the norm

\|w\|^{2}_{\ddagger}=\int_{0}^{\infty}|w(r)|^{2}\,e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{4}}\,dr.

It is well known that the set
\{\mathcal{H}_{2k}(r)\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
forms a complete set of eigenfunctions for
L^{*}_{1}
in the space
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\ddagger}(1)
with eigenvalues
\mu_{k}=0,1,\ldots
and, in addition, they form an orthogonal basis. This last property can be checked directly using the properties of the Hermite polynomials.

From another point of view, let us look at the above calculations in Fourier variables.
Take Fourier transform of (
2.1
) in
\mathbb{R}
and
\zeta=|\xi|
, the radial Fourier variable. Then, for the problem

\begin{cases}\widehat{\varphi}_{t}+|\xi|^{2}\widehat{\varphi}=0,\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R},t>0,\\
\widehat{\varphi}|_{t=0}=\widehat{\varphi}_{0},\end{cases}

it is well known that the solution is given by

\widehat{\varphi}(t,\xi)=e^{-|\xi|^{2}t}\widehat{\varphi}_{0}(\xi)

so that

(2.7)

\begin{split}\varphi(t,x)&=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\xi x}e^{-|\xi|^{2}t}\widehat{\varphi}_{0}(\xi)\,d\xi=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}2\cos(x\zeta)e^{-\zeta^{2}t}\widehat{\varphi}_{0}(\zeta)\,d\zeta.\end{split}

On the Fourier side, since
\varphi_{0}
is an even function,

\widehat{\varphi}_{0}(\zeta)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}\zeta^{2k}e^{-\zeta^{2}}.

Formally substituting into (
2.7
) we obtain

\varphi(t,r)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos(\zeta r)e^{-\zeta^{2}(t+1)}\zeta^{2k}\,d\zeta.

The change of variable
\zeta\mapsto\zeta(t+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}
gives

(2.8)

\begin{split}\varphi(t,r)&=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}\frac{1}{(t+1)^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2k}{2}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos\Big(\zeta\frac{x}{(t+1)^{1/2}}\Big)e^{-\zeta^{2}}\zeta^{2k}\,d\zeta,\\
\end{split}

which is the Fourier version of the asymptotic expansion (
2.3
) for an even function
\varphi(t,\cdot)
.
To see this, note that an important property of Hermite polynomials is that the
Fourier transform of
e_{k}(r)
is
another polynomial of degree
2k
up to a complex multiplicative constant, more precisely,

\widehat{e}_{k}(\zeta):=\mathcal{F}(e_{k})(\zeta)=\zeta^{2k}e^{-\zeta^{2}},

which is just the Fourier representation of Rodrigues’ formula (
2.4
).
This implies that, for the Fourier counterpart of (
2.5
), given by

\zeta^{2}\widehat{u}(\zeta)+\frac{\zeta}{2}\partial_{\zeta}\widehat{u}(\zeta)=\nu\widehat{u}(\zeta),\quad\widehat{u}=\widehat{u}(\zeta),

the set
\{\widehat{e}_{k}(\zeta)\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
is an orthogonal, complete set of eigenfunctions with eigenvalues
\nu_{k}=0,1,2,....
.

2.2.
The local case
s=1
in higher dimensions
n>1

Let us go now to general
n
and consider the eigenvalue equation

(2.9)

L_{1}u:=-\Delta u-\frac{x}{2}\cdot\nabla u-\frac{n}{2}u=\nu u,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Our scheme is to take Fourier transform of the eigenvalue equation. Assume that
u
is radially symmetric, so that
\widehat{u}
only depends on the variable
|\xi|
. For simplicity, we will denote
\zeta=|\xi|

which, for radially symmetric functions leads to the Fourier transform
version of (
2.9
),

\zeta^{2}\widehat{u}(\zeta)+\frac{\zeta}{2}\partial_{\zeta}\widehat{u}(\zeta)=\nu\widehat{u}(\zeta),\quad\zeta>0,

which is independent of the dimension
n
. Hence, as above, eigenvalues are also
\nu_{k}=0,1,2,\dots
, with eigenfunctions

(2.10)

\widehat{e}_{k}(\zeta)=\zeta^{2k}e^{-\zeta^{2}}

again. Note, also, that we are only interested in the even powers.

Let us find an explicit expression for
e_{k}
, taking the inverse Fourier transform in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(which is the Hankel transform given in (
3.2
)), and use the formula (
A.4
):

(2.11)

e_{k}(r)=r^{\frac{2-n}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\zeta^{2k+\frac{n}{2}}e^{-\zeta^{2}}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\zeta r)\,d\zeta=2^{-n/2}k!L_{k}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}\big(\tfrac{r^{2}}{4}\big)e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{4}}.

One can also use the relation to Kummer’s function from (
A.2
) to write

e_{k}(r)=2^{-\frac{n}{2}}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{4}}\frac{\Gamma(k+\frac{n}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}M\big(-k,\tfrac{n}{2};\tfrac{r^{2}}{4}\big).

The crucial observation here is that the Laguerre polynomials
\{L_{k}^{(\alpha)}(r)\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
form an orthogonal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
with the appropriate weight. Indeed, recalling the product formula from (
A.3
), we have

2^{-(n-1)}\int_{0}^{\infty}L_{k}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}\big(\tfrac{r^{2}}{4}\big)L_{m}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}\big(\tfrac{r^{2}}{4}\big)e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{4}}r^{n-1}\,dr=\frac{\Gamma(k+\frac{n}{2})}{\Gamma(k+1)\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}\delta_{m,k}.

The natural space for the problem is thus
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}(1):=L^{2}_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{\frac{r^{2}}{4}})
with the scalar product

\langle u_{1},u_{2}\rangle_{\dagger}=\int_{0}^{\infty}u_{1}(r)u_{2}(r)e^{\frac{r^{2}}{4}}r^{n-1}\,dr.

Now (
2.7
) is replaced by the Hankel transform formula (
3.2
) in order to obtain the precise asymptotic expansion for the solution of the heat equation
\varphi
in terms of our eigenbasis
\{e_{k}(r)\}_{k}
,

\begin{split}\varphi(t,x)=&\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{n}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\xi\cdot x}e^{-|\xi|^{2}t}\widehat{\varphi}_{0}(\xi)\,d\xi=r^{\frac{2-n}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\zeta r)\zeta^{\frac{n-2}{2}}e^{-\zeta^{2}t}\widehat{\varphi}_{0}(\zeta)\zeta\,d\zeta\\
=&r^{\frac{2-n}{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\zeta r)\zeta^{\frac{n-2}{2}}e^{-\zeta^{2}(t+1)}\zeta^{2k}\zeta\,d\zeta.\end{split}

2.3.
The fractional setting

The approach described above is suited to be generalized to the fractional case, for any
s\in(0,1)
. Taking Fourier transform of the heat equation (
1.1
) we obtain

\begin{cases}\widehat{\varphi}_{t}+|\xi|^{2s}\widehat{\varphi}=0,\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R},\,t>0,\\
\widehat{\varphi}|_{t=0}=\widehat{\varphi}_{0}.\end{cases}

Similarly, for even functions, setting
\zeta=|\xi|
,

(2.12)

\begin{split}\varphi(t,x)&=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{n}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\xi\cdot x}e^{-|\xi|^{2s}t}\widehat{\varphi}_{0}(\xi)\,d\xi=r^{\frac{2-n}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\zeta r)\zeta^{\frac{n-2}{2}}e^{-\zeta^{2s}t}\widehat{\varphi}_{0}(\zeta)\zeta\,d\zeta\\
&=\frac{1}{s}\,r^{\frac{2-n}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\vartheta^{1/s}r)\vartheta^{\frac{n}{2s}}e^{-\vartheta^{2}t}\widehat{\varphi}_{0}(\vartheta^{1/s})\vartheta^{1/s-1}\,d\vartheta\end{split}

where, in the last step, we have made the change of variable
\vartheta=\zeta^{s}
. Let
\widehat{\varphi}_{0}(\vartheta)
be the Fourier transform of
\varphi_{0}
and denote by
\varphi_{0}^{\dagger}
the inverse Fourier transform of the function
\widehat{\varphi}_{0}(\vartheta^{1/s})
. If
\varphi_{0}^{\dagger}
belongs to the space
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}(1)
, then we can expand it in eigenbasis as

\varphi_{0}^{\dagger}(r)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}e_{k}(r),

as explained in Subsection
2.2
. In Fourier variables, taking into account (
2.10
),

\widehat{\varphi}_{0}(\zeta)=\widehat{\varphi}_{0}(\vartheta^{1/s})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}\vartheta^{2k}e^{-\vartheta^{2}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}\zeta^{2ks}e^{-\zeta^{2s}}.

Substituting in (
2.12
), we have that

\begin{split}\varphi(t,r)&=r^{\frac{2-n}{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\zeta r)\zeta^{\frac{n}{2}}e^{-\zeta^{2s}(t+1)}\zeta^{2ks}\,d\zeta\\
&=r^{\frac{2-n}{2}}\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}\frac{1}{(t+1)^{k+\frac{n+2}{4s}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}\Big(\zeta\frac{r}{(t+1)^{\frac{1}{2s}}}\Big)\zeta^{\frac{n}{2}}e^{-\zeta^{2s}}\zeta^{2ks}\,d\zeta,\end{split}

which is the non-local generalization of the asymptotic expansion (
2.3
) in Fourier variables. Remark that this formula reduces to (
2.8
) when
n=1
and
s=1
by taking into account that

\lim_{\alpha\to-1/2}J_{\alpha}(z)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi z}}\cos z.

The underlying idea in many the proofs in this paper is that the change of variable
\vartheta=\zeta^{s}
reduces a non-local problem to a local eigenvalue expansion. Thus, defining
e_{k}^{(s)}(\zeta)
by

\widehat{e}_{k}^{(s)}(\zeta)=\zeta^{2ks}e^{-\zeta^{2s}},\quad k=0,1,...

then the
\{e_{k}^{(s)}(r)\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
are eigenfunctions for the Lévy Fokker-Planck operator (
1.5
). Indeed, for any
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
the functions

(2.13)

\widehat{e}_{\nu}^{(s)}(\zeta)=\zeta^{2s\nu}e^{-\zeta^{2s}}

solve the eigenvalue equation (
1.5
) in Fourier variables, that is,

\zeta^{2s}\widehat{u}(\zeta)+\frac{1}{2s}\zeta\partial_{\zeta}\widehat{u}(\zeta)=\nu\widehat{u}(\zeta).

Unfortunately, this Fourier approach gives no information on which are admissible eigenfunctions or what is the right function space in the fractional case. The aim of our work is to show that the
\{e_{k}^{(s)}(r)\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
form an orthonormal basis for a Hilbert space that we denote by
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}(s)
. We will see that Mellin transform, rather than Fourier transform, gives us a precise answer to this question, which is the main ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

3.2.1.
Mellin vs. Fourier

The Mellin transform on
\re z=0
is exactly the Fourier transform in the Emden–Fowler variable
\varrho=-\log r
, for

(
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
). More precisely,

(3.3)

\mathcal{M}u(\lambda i)=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\lambda i}u(r)\,\frac{dr}{r}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\varrho\lambda i}u(e^{-\varrho})\,d\varrho=\sqrt{2\pi}\mathcal{F}_{1}\left\{u(e^{-\varrho})\right\}(\lambda),

where
\mathcal{F}_{1}
denotes the one-dimensional Fourier transform given in (
3.1
).

More generally, the Mellin transform of

on
\re z=\sigma
is the Fourier transform of the conjugation
r^{\sigma}u(r)
in the logarithmic variable: for
z=\sigma+\lambda i
(
\sigma,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
) and

,

(3.4)

\mathcal{M}u(\sigma+\lambda i)=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\sigma+\lambda i}u(r)\,\frac{dr}{r}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\varrho\lambda i}e^{-\sigma\varrho}u(e^{-\varrho})\,d\varrho=\sqrt{2\pi}\mathcal{F}_{1}\left\{e^{-\sigma\varrho}u(e^{-\varrho})\right\}(\lambda).

We are primarily interested in the case

, the Jacobian in polar coordinates.

By (

), the Mellin transform along each vertical line can be simply viewed as the Fourier transform up to a change of variable. Consequently, they enjoy similar elementary properties, convolution identities, inverse transforms and Plancherel theorems. Specifically, we emphasize that the differential operator
-r\partial_{r}=\partial_{\varrho}
Mellin-transforms to multiplication by

, demonstrating the suitability convenience of the Mellin transform in studying the Hermite differential equation.

Theorem 3.1

(Mellin inversion)
.

Suppose that

•

Suppose

3.2.4.
Multiplicative convolution identity

Similarly to Fourier transform, Mellin transform also satisfies some kind of convolution property.

Recall from (
3.3
) that Mellin and Fourier transforms are related by

\mathcal{M}u(\lambda i)=\sqrt{2\pi}\mathcal{F}_{1}\{u(e^{-\varrho})\}(\lambda).

Because of the logarithmic change of variable, the convolution identity for Mellin transform is valid for the multiplicative convolution instead. We denote it by the the operation
\star
that satisfies

(u_{1}\star u_{2})(e^{-\varrho})=[u_{1}(e^{-\cdot})\ast u_{2}(e^{-\cdot})](\varrho),

where
\ast
denotes the standard convolution in
\mathbb{R}
. In other words,
\star
is defined by

(u_{1}\star u_{2})(r)=\int_{0}^{\infty}u_{1}\left(\frac{r}{r^{\prime}}\right)u_{2}(r^{\prime})\dfrac{dr^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}}=(u_{2}\star u_{1})(r).

Then, by the convolution identity of the Fourier transform,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\left\{u_{1}\star u_{2}\right\}(\lambda i)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\pi}\mathcal{F}_{1}\left(u_{1}(e^{-\cdot})\ast u_{2}(e^{-\cdot})\right)(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\pi}\mathcal{F}_{1}\left(u_{1}(e^{-\cdot})\right)(\lambda)\sqrt{2\pi}\mathcal{F}_{1}\left(u_{2}(e^{-\cdot})\right)(\lambda)=\mathcal{M}u_{1}(\lambda i)\mathcal{M}u_{2}(\lambda i).

More generally, for any
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
, since
e^{-\sigma\varrho}(u_{1}\ast u_{2})(t)=[(e^{-\sigma\cdot}u_{1})\ast(e^{-\sigma\cdot}u_{2})](\varrho)
, there holds

\mathcal{M}\left\{u_{1}\star u_{2}\right\}(\sigma+\lambda i)=\mathcal{M}u_{1}(\sigma+\lambda i)\mathcal{M}u_{2}(\sigma+\lambda i).

By duality, given Mellin transforms
\tilde{u}_{1}(z)
,
\tilde{u}_{2}(z)
of admissible functions,

\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}^{-1}\left\{\widetilde{u}_{1}\widetilde{u}_{2}\right\}(r)=[\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}^{-1}\widetilde{u}_{1}\ast\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}^{-1}\widetilde{u}_{2}](r).

3.2.5.
Isometry and Plancherel identity

The Mellin transform yields an identification between

3.3.
Mellin–Sobolev function spaces

We work on the space of radially symmetric functions

on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For such functions we define the space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\beta}

In the particular case that

thanks to (
3.13

For
\beta=1
we obtain a variation of the usual Sobolev space
H^{1}_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with different scaling properties. Indeed,

\displaystyle-(\tfrac{n}{2}+\lambda i)\mathcal{M}u(\tfrac{n}{2}+\lambda i)

\displaystyle=-(\tfrac{n}{2}+\lambda i)\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\frac{n}{2}+\lambda i}u(r)\,\frac{dr}{r}=-\int_{0}^{\infty}(r^{\frac{n}{2}+\lambda i})^{\prime}u(r)\,dr

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\frac{n}{2}+\lambda i}u^{\prime}(r)\,dr=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\frac{n}{2}+\lambda i}(ru^{\prime}(r))\,\frac{dr}{r}=\mathcal{M}\left\{ru^{\prime}(r)\right\}(\tfrac{n}{2}+\lambda i),

so that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lvert{\tfrac{n}{2}+\lambda i}\right\rvert^{2}\left\lvert{\mathcal{M}u(\tfrac{n}{2}+\lambda i)}\right\rvert^{2}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lvert{\mathcal{M}\left\{ru^{\prime}(r)\right\}(\tfrac{n}{2}+\lambda i)}\right\rvert^{2}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(ru^{\prime}(r))^{2}r^{n-1}\,dr=\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x|^{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx.

We conclude that the norm in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}^{1}

\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|u|^{2}\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}r^{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

which is closely related, but not exactly equal, to the usual Sobolev norm of
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

Finally, we remark here that the

3.4.
The Mellin symbol of the fractional Laplacian

A key point in our discussion is to understand the fractional Laplacian in Mellin variables. We will show that Mellin transform converts it into a multiplication operator with a nicely behaved symbol:

Proposition 3.7
.

For
s\in(0,\frac{n}{2})
, we have

\mathcal{M}\left\{(-\Delta)^{s}u(r)\right\}(z)=\Theta_{s}(z)\mathcal{M}u(z-2s),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C},

where the Mellin symbol is given by

(3.15)

\Theta_{s}(z):=2^{2s}\dfrac{\Gamma\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n-z}{2}\right)}\dfrac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+2s-z}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{z-2s}{2}\right)},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}.

On any fixed vertical strip,

(3.16)

\Theta_{s}(z)\asymp|z|^{2s}\quad\text{as}\quad|z|\to\infty.

In addition, the fractional seminorm is written as

(3.17)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}|(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}u|^{2}r^{n-1}\,dr=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Theta_{s}(\tfrac{n}{2}+s+i\lambda)|\mathcal{M}u(\tfrac{n}{2}-s+i\lambda)|^{2}\,d\lambda.\end{split}

Before we give the proof of the Proposition, we need to introduce some special formulas. First we write the composition of Mellin transform with the Hankel transform in terms of a Mellin transform. For this purpose, we recall the following formula for the integral of Bessel functions
[
22

(3.18)

\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\mu}J_{\nu}(\zeta r)\,dr=2^{\mu}\zeta^{-\mu-1}\dfrac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\mu+\nu+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{-\mu+\nu+1}{2}\right)},

for
-{\rm Re\,}\nu-1<{\rm Re\,}\mu<\frac{1}{2}
,
\zeta>0
.

Lemma 3.8

For

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}\left\{\mathcal{F}u(\zeta)\right\}(z)=\mathcal{M}\left\{\mathcal{F}^{-1}u(r)\right\}(z)&=2^{z-\frac{n}{2}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n-z}{2}\right)}\mathcal{M}u(n-z).\end{split}

Proof.

In a recent revision of the paper, we have found that this is a result known from 1979

First, note that Hankel transform is self-inverse. Using (

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}\left\{\mathcal{F}u(\zeta)\right\}(z)&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\zeta^{z}\widehat{u}(\zeta)\,\frac{d\zeta}{\zeta}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\zeta^{z-1}\zeta^{-\frac{n-2}{2}}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\zeta r)\,d\zeta\right]r^{\frac{n}{2}}u(r)\,dr\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[2^{z-\frac{n}{2}}r^{-z+\frac{n-2}{2}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n-z}{2}\right)}\right]r^{\frac{n}{2}}u(r)\,dr=2^{z-\frac{n}{2}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n-z}{2}\right)}\mathcal{M}u(n-z),\end{split}

∎

4.
Functional analysis

Here we give the functional analysis framework for the eigenvalue problem (
1.6
), and the precise definition of the function space
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}
.

4.1.
The Fourier approach

Let us make more precise the change of variable (
1.7
). Let
\widehat{u}(\zeta)
be the Fourier/Hankel transform of
u
. Recalling that
\vartheta=\zeta^{s}
, we set

(4.1)

u^{\sharp}(\vartheta)=\widehat{u}(\vartheta^{1/s})=\vartheta^{\frac{2-n}{2s}}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\vartheta^{1/s}r)r^{\frac{n}{2}}u(r)\,dr.

Fix
u^{\dagger}=u^{\dagger}(\rho)
to be the inverse Fourier/Hankel transform of
u^{\sharp}
, that is,

u^{\dagger}(\rho)=\rho^{\frac{2-n}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\vartheta\rho)\vartheta^{\frac{n}{2}}u^{\sharp}(\vartheta)\,d\vartheta.

Using again equation (
3.2
) for the Fourier (Hankel) transform of
u
we can give an integral formula for
u^{\dagger}
. Indeed,

(4.2)

\begin{split}u^{\dagger}(\rho)&=\rho^{\frac{2-n}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\vartheta\rho)\vartheta^{\frac{n}{2}}\left(\vartheta^{\frac{2-n}{2s}}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\vartheta^{1/s}\rho_{1})u(\rho_{1})\,d\rho_{1}\right)\,d\vartheta\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}A_{s}(\rho,\rho_{1})u(\rho_{1})\,d\rho_{1},\end{split}

where we have defined the kernel

\displaystyle A_{s}(\rho,\rho_{1}):=\rho^{\frac{2-n}{2}}\rho_{1}^{\frac{n}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\vartheta\rho)J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\vartheta^{1/s}\rho_{1})\vartheta^{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n-2}{2s}}\,d\vartheta.

Note that the integral above is convergent as
\vartheta\to\infty
. Indeed, looking at the asymptotics of the Bessel function, the asymptotic behavior of the integrand (for fixed
\rho,\rho_{1}
) is given by

J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\vartheta\rho)J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\vartheta^{1/s}\rho_{1})\vartheta^{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n-2}{2s}}=\vartheta^{-\frac{n-1}{2s}(1-s)}\left(\cos\left(\vartheta\rho-\tfrac{n-1}{4}\pi\right)\cos\left(\vartheta^{1/s}\rho_{1}-\tfrac{n-1}{4}\pi\right)+O\left(\vartheta^{-1}\right)\right),

as
\vartheta\to\infty
, and the oscillating part is

\int_{1}^{\infty}\tau^{-\frac{n-1}{2}(1-s)}\cos\left(\tau^{s}\rho-\tfrac{n-1}{4}\pi\right)\cos\left(\tau\rho_{1}-\tfrac{n-1}{4}\pi\right)\cdot s\tau^{s-1}\,d\tau\\
=\frac{s}{2}\int_{1}^{\infty}\tau^{-\frac{n+1}{2}(1-s)}\,d\left(\frac{\cos(\tau^{s}\rho-\tau\rho_{1})}{s\tau^{s-1}\rho-\rho_{1}}+\frac{\cos(\tau^{s}\rho+\tau\rho_{1}-\frac{n-1}{2}\pi)}{s\tau^{s-1}\rho+\rho_{1}}\right),

which is integrable (after an integration by parts).

The crucial observation is encoded in the following Lemma (the function spaces are indicated below):

Lemma 4.1
.

If
u
is a solution to

(4.3)

L_{s}u=f,\quad u=u(r),\,r>0,

then
u^{\dagger}
is a solution to

(4.4)

L_{1}u^{\dagger}=f^{\dagger},\quad u^{\dagger}=u^{\dagger}(\rho),\,\rho>0.

Proof.

Taking Fourier transform of (
4.3
) yields

\zeta^{2s}\widehat{u}+\tfrac{1}{2s}\zeta\partial_{\zeta}\widehat{u}=\widehat{f},\quad\widehat{u}=\widehat{u}(\zeta),\,\zeta>0.

The change of variable
\vartheta=\zeta^{s}
reduces this equation to a local one

\vartheta^{2}u^{\sharp}+\tfrac{1}{2}\vartheta\partial_{\vartheta}u^{\sharp}=f^{\sharp},\quad u^{\sharp}=u^{\sharp}(\vartheta),\,\vartheta>0.

Now we Fourier back to arrive to

-\Delta u^{\dagger}-\tfrac{1}{2}\rho\partial_{\rho}u^{\dagger}-\tfrac{n}{2}u^{\dagger}=f^{\dagger},\quad u^{\dagger}=u^{\dagger}(\rho),\,\rho>0,

which is precisely (
4.4
).
∎

The relevant function spaces for equation (
4.4
) are
L^{2}({\rm exp}):=L_{r}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}})
and
H^{1}({\rm exp}):=H^{1}_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}})
. It is well known that we have compactness so that the spectral theorem can be applied

[
29
,
14
]
.

Now we translate those spaces to our setting. The space
L^{2}({\rm exp})
is equipped with the norm

\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\rm exp})}=\int_{0}^{\infty}|u(\rho)|^{2}e^{\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}}\rho^{n-1}\,d\rho.

In the light of (
4.9
) it is natural to define, for each
s\in(0,1)
, the Hilbert space
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}:=\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}(s)
with the norm induced by the scalar product

(4.5)

\begin{split}\left\langle{u_{1},u_{2}}\right\rangle_{\dagger}=\int_{0}^{\infty}u_{1}^{\dagger}(\rho)\overline{u_{2}^{\dagger}(\rho)}e^{\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}}\rho^{n-1}\,d\rho,\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\left\|{u}\right\|_{\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}}^{2}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\lvert{u^{\dagger}(\rho)}\right\rvert^{2}e^{\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}}\rho^{n-1}\,d\rho,\end{split}

and similarly the (energy) seminorm for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
by

\|u\|^{2}_{\dot{\mathds{H}}^{\ell}_{\dagger}}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}|\partial_{\rho}^{\ell}u^{\dagger}(\rho)|^{2}e^{\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}}\rho^{n-1}\,d\rho,

and the energy space
\mathds{H}^{\ell}_{\dagger}:p=\mathds{H}^{\ell}_{\dagger}(s)
by

\|u\|^{2}_{{\mathds{H}^{\ell}_{\dagger}}}=\|u\|^{2}_{{\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}}}+\|u\|^{2}_{\dot{\mathds{H}}^{\ell}_{\dagger}}.

Then, automatically, if
f\in\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}
, then
u\in\mathds{H}^{1}_{\dagger}
. Moreover, compactness for the operator
L_{s}
is also inherited from the local case.

Remark 4.2
.

We may observe that

\int_{0}^{\infty}|u^{\sharp}(\vartheta)|^{2}\vartheta^{n-1}\,d\vartheta=s\int_{0}^{\infty}|\widehat{u}(\zeta)|^{2}\zeta^{sn-1}\,d\zeta,

so that the transformation

can be interpreted as a differentiation of order

(i.e., fractional integration of order

Nevertheless, the Fourier approach gives little information on how the space

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{s}(n-z)^{-1}\mathcal{M}u(n-z)

\displaystyle=2^{\frac{n}{s}-n}2^{z}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{z}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{z}{2})}2^{-\frac{z}{s}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{z}{2s})}{\Gamma(\frac{z}{2s})}\mathcal{M}u(n-z)

\displaystyle=2^{\frac{n}{s}-n}2^{-\frac{z}{s}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{z}{2s})}{\Gamma(\frac{z}{2s})}\cdot\left(2^{z}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{z}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{z}{2})}\mathcal{M}\left\{\mathcal{F}^{-1}\widehat{u}\right\}(n-z)\right)

\displaystyle=2^{\frac{n}{s}-n}2^{-\frac{z}{s}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{z}{2s})}{\Gamma(\frac{z}{2s})}\cdot 2^{\frac{n}{2}}\mathcal{M}\left\{\widehat{u}(\zeta)\right\}(z)

\displaystyle=2^{\frac{n}{s}-n}2^{-\frac{z}{s}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{z}{2s})}{\Gamma(\frac{z}{2s})}\cdot 2^{\frac{n}{2}}\frac{1}{s}\mathcal{M}\left\{\widehat{u}(\zeta^{\frac{1}{s}})\right\}\left(\tfrac{z}{s}\right),

and recalling the definition of
u^{\sharp}
from (
4.1
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\left\{\Phi_{s}^{-1}u\right\}(n-z)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\frac{n}{s}-n}}{s}\cdot 2^{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{z}{s}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{z}{2s})}{\Gamma(\frac{z}{2s})}\mathcal{M}\left\{u^{\sharp}(\zeta)\right\}\left(\tfrac{z}{s}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\frac{n}{s}-n}}{s}\mathcal{M}\left\{(\mathcal{F}^{-1}u^{\sharp})(r)\right\}\left(n-\tfrac{z}{s}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\frac{n}{s}-n}}{s}\mathcal{M}\left\{u^{\dagger}(r)\right\}\left(n-\tfrac{z}{s}\right)

\displaystyle=2^{\frac{n}{s}-n}\mathcal{M}\left\{u^{\dagger}(r^{s})\right\}(ns-z)

\displaystyle=2^{\frac{n}{s}-n}\mathcal{M}\left\{r^{ns-n}u^{\dagger}(r^{s})\right\}(n-z).

Hence by Mellin inversion, taking into account that
\Real(n-z)
can be chosen in the interval
(n(1-s),n)
, we obtain the conclusion of the Lemma.
∎

In the light of (
4.9
), from the expression (
4.5
) it is natural to set the Hilbert space
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}:=\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}(s)
with the norm induced by the scalar product (up to multiplicative constant)

\begin{split}\left\langle{u_{1},u_{2}}\right\rangle_{\dagger}&:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi_{s}^{-1}u_{1}(r)\,\overline{\Phi_{s}^{-1}u_{2}(r)}\,e^{\frac{r^{2s}}{4}}r^{n(2-s)-1}\,dr=2^{\frac{n}{s}-n}\int_{0}^{\infty}u_{1}^{\dagger}(r^{s})\overline{u_{2}^{\dagger}(r^{s})}e^{\frac{r^{2s}}{4}}r^{sn-1}\,dr\\
&=2^{\frac{n}{s}-n}s^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}u_{1}^{\dagger}(\rho)\overline{u_{2}^{\dagger}(\rho)}e^{\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}}\rho^{n-1}\,d\rho.\end{split}

That is,

(4.12)

\begin{split}\|u\|^{2}_{\dagger}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}|\Phi_{s}^{-1}u(r)|^{2}\,e^{\frac{r^{2s}}{4}}r^{n(2-s)-1}\,dr=2^{\frac{n}{s}-n}s^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}|u^{\dagger}(\rho)|^{2}e^{\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}}\rho^{n-1}\,d\rho.\end{split}

Note that
\Phi^{-1}u
is well defined if, for instance,
u
is smooth with compact support outside the origin.
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}
is more precisely defined as the closure of such functions with respect to the
\|\cdot\|_{\dagger}
norm.

Lemma 4.7
.

If
u
belongs to
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}
, then
u
is holomorphic in a vertical strip of width at least
2s
.

Proof.

First assume that
u
is smooth of compact support outside the origin (so that
\mathcal{M}u(z)
is holomorphic on
\mathbb{C}
), and set
v:=\Phi^{-1}u
, that is
\mathcal{M}v(z)=\Lambda_{s}^{-1}(z)\mathcal{M}u(z)
. We have that
\mathcal{M}v(z)
is a meromorphic function with (possibly) simple poles given by those of
\Lambda_{s}^{-1}(z)
, given in (
4.8
).

For
z=\sigma+\lambda i
,

|\mathcal{M}v(z)|\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\sigma-1}|v(r)|\,dr\leq\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2\sigma-1+ns-2n}e^{-\frac{r^{2s}}{4}}\,dr\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}|v(r)|^{2}e^{\frac{r^{2s}}{4}}r^{n(2-s)-1}\,dr\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

By our definition of the space
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}
, we know that

\int_{0}^{\infty}|v(r)|^{2}\,e^{\frac{r^{2s}}{4}}r^{n(2-s)-1}\,dr<\infty,

so
\mathcal{M}v
is bounded in the half-plane
\{\Real z>n\frac{2-s}{2}\}
. In particular, it is analytic.

Now, writing
\mathcal{M}u(z)=\Lambda_{s}(z)\mathcal{M}v(z)
, and taking into account that the poles of
\Lambda_{s}(z)
are located at

z_{j}=-2j
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, and
z^{\prime}_{\ell}=n+2s\ell
,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, we deduce that
\mathcal{M}u(z)
is holomorphic on the strip

\{n\frac{2-s}{2}<\Real z<n\}
, which has width
\frac{n}{2}s
,
as desired.

Finally, for functions
u
that are limits of
u_{n}
with respect to the
\|\cdot\|_{\dagger}
norm, we realize that the above argument also works for each
u_{n}
and for differences
u_{n}-u
, thus showing that
\mathcal{M}u_{n}
is a sequence of holomorphic functions on the strip which converge uniformly. Thus the limit is also holomorphic (this is a classical result originally due to Weierstrass, see
[
30
]
).

∎

4.3.
A fractional version of Gevrey–Sobolev spaces

Now we would like to characterize
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}
in terms of known function spaces.

Gevrey classes were introduced in
[
21
]
as an intermediate space between
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
and real analytic functions. We could not find any canonical reference in the literature except for
[
40
]
. These spaces very often appear in the context of hyperbolic equations; for instance, a classical book is
[
24
, Page 281]
for the study of wave front sets in PDEs. They have been also recently used in the context of the Euler
[
25
]
and Navier–Stokes equations
[
2
]
. The precise notion is given below:

Definition 4.8
.

We say that
u
belongs to the
Gevrey–Sobolev
class
\mathcal{G}_{p}
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

(4.13)

\|u\|_{H^{m}}\leq C^{1+m}(1+m)^{pm},

for every
m=0,1,\ldots

A different —but very much related— definition is given by the following norm:

\|u\|_{\mathcal{G}_{p}}^{2}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{C^{m}}{m!}\int(1+|\xi|^{2})^{m/p}|\widehat{u}(\xi)|^{2}\,d\xi.

We will show first that, for
n=1
,
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}
is a fractional version of a Gevrey–Sobolev space.
For this, we remark that, formally,

\|u\|_{\dagger}^{2}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^{m}m!}\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^{2m}|u^{\dagger}(\rho^{s})|^{2}\rho^{n-1}\,d\rho.

In Fourier variables, restricting to
n=1
for simplicity,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^{2m}|u^{\dagger}(\rho^{s})|^{2}\,d\rho=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\frac{d^{m}}{d\vartheta^{m}}u^{\sharp}(\vartheta)\right|^{2}\,d\vartheta=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\frac{d^{m}}{d\vartheta^{m}}\widehat{u}(\vartheta^{1/s})\right|^{2}\,d\vartheta,

and (using the change
\vartheta^{1/s}=\zeta
)

\frac{d}{d\vartheta}\widehat{u}(\vartheta^{1/s})=\frac{1}{s}\zeta^{1-s}\frac{d}{d\zeta}\widehat{u}(\zeta),\quad\ldots\quad\frac{d^{m}}{d\vartheta^{m}}\widehat{u}(\vartheta^{1/s})=\frac{1}{s^{m}}\left(\zeta^{1-s}\frac{d}{d\zeta}\right)^{m}\widehat{u}(\zeta).

This yields

(4.14)

\begin{split}\|u\|_{\dagger}^{2}&=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^{m}m!s^{2m}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\left(\zeta^{1-s}\frac{d}{d\zeta}\right)^{m}\widehat{u}(\zeta)\right|^{2}\,s\zeta^{s-1}\,d\zeta\\
&=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^{m}m!s^{2m}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\mathcal{F}\left\{\big((-\Delta)^{\frac{1-s}{2}}r\big)^{m}u\right\}(\zeta)\right|^{2}\,s\zeta^{s-1}\,d\zeta\end{split}

which, inspired by (
4.13
), could be taken as our definition of a
fractional Gevrey–Sobolev
norm.

As a side remark, the
Zemanian
space
\mathcal{Z}_{\nu}
is defined as the set of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
functions
\omega(\zeta)
,
\zeta>0
, such that all the quantities

\gamma_{m,k}(\omega):=\sup_{\zeta>0}\left|\zeta^{m}\left(\frac{1}{\zeta}\frac{d}{d\zeta}\right)^{k}(\zeta^{-\nu-1/2}\omega(\zeta))\right|,\quad m,k\in\mathbb{N},

are finite (compare to our (
4.14
)). Zemanian spaces are the appropriate function spaces to define the distributional Hankel transform as an automorphism (see, for instance, the note
[
43
]
and the references therein).

We will also give the interpretation of the space
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}
in Mellin variables. For this, we take again a Taylor expansion of the exponential weight. The trick is that powers of
r
become translations in Mellin variables, instead of derivatives. Set

\sigma_{m}:=sm+\frac{n(2-s)}{2}.

Then the
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}
norm is written as

\begin{split}\|u\|^{2}_{\dagger}&=\int_{0}^{\infty}|\Phi_{s}^{-1}u(r)|^{2}\,e^{\frac{r^{2s}}{4}}r^{n(2-s)-1}\,dr=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^{m}m!}\int_{0}^{\infty}|\Phi_{s}^{-1}u(r)|^{2}\,r^{2sm+n(2-s)-1}\,dr\\
&=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^{m}m!}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{M}\Phi_{s}^{-1}u(\sigma_{m}+\lambda i)|^{2}\,d\lambda=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^{m}m!}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\Lambda_{s}^{-1}(\sigma_{m}+\lambda i)|^{2}|\mathcal{M}u(\sigma_{m}+\lambda i)|^{2}\,d\lambda\end{split}

where, for each
m
, we have used the isometry property of the Mellin transform (
3.14
).

Note that the integrand above may have poles (and also zeroes), which may require a higher order vanishing of
\mathcal{M}u
. These can be avoided, for instance, if
s
is irrational. However, to obtain a uniform bound for all
m
is more delicate. We will show in Section
4.4
that

(4.15)

\left\lvert{\Lambda_{s}^{-1}(\sigma_{m}+i\lambda)}\right\rvert\asymp\delta_{m}+|\lambda|^{p_{m}},

where
p_{m}
is given in (
4.16
) and
\delta_{m}
in (
4.17
). Looking at the asymptotic behavior as
m\to\infty
we conclude that

\|u\|^{2}_{\dagger}\asymp\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^{m}m!}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\delta_{m}+|\lambda|^{2m(1-s)})|\mathcal{M}u(sm+i\lambda)|^{2}\,d\lambda,

which again reminds us of a fractional Gevrey–Sobolev norm.

Likewise, one may give an interpretation for the
\mathds{H}_{\dagger}^{1}
seminorm (
4.1
).
For this, take the Taylor expansion of the exponential in the first step and then use the Mellin isometry property (
3.14
),

\begin{split}\|u\|^{2}_{\dot{\mathds{H}}^{1}_{\dagger}}&=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^{m}m!}\int_{0}^{\infty}|\partial_{\rho}u^{\dagger}|^{2}\rho^{2m+n-1}\,d\rho\\
&=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^{m}m!}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\mathcal{M}(\partial_{\rho}u^{\dagger})(m+\tfrac{n}{2}+\lambda i)\right|^{2}\,d\lambda.\end{split}

Now observe that

\mathcal{M}(\partial_{\rho}u^{\dagger})(z)=-(z-1)\mathcal{M}(u^{\dagger})(z-1),

so that

\begin{split}\|u\|^{2}_{\dot{\mathds{H}}^{1}_{\dagger}}&=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^{m}m!}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\{\lambda^{2}+(m+\tfrac{n}{2}-1)^{2}\right\}\left|\mathcal{M}\{u^{\dagger}(r)\}(m+\tfrac{n}{2}-1+\lambda i)\right|^{2}\,d\lambda\\
&=s\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^{m}m!}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\{\lambda^{2}+(m+\tfrac{n}{2}-1)^{2}\right\}\left|\mathcal{M}\{u^{\dagger}(r^{s})\}\left(s(m+\tfrac{n}{2}-1+\lambda i)\right)\right|^{2}\,d\lambda\\
&=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^{m}m!}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\{(\tfrac{\tilde{\lambda}}{s})^{2}+(m+\tfrac{n}{2}-1)^{2}\right\}\left|\mathcal{M}\{u^{\dagger}(r^{s})\}\left(s(m+\tfrac{n}{2}-1)+\tilde{\lambda}i\right)\right|^{2}\,d\tilde{\lambda}\end{split}

where we have used (
3.9
) for the second equality and the change
\tilde{\lambda}=s\lambda
for the third. Next, we drop tilde notation, use (
3.7
) to translate and take into account relation (
4.9
),

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}\{u^{\dagger}(r^{s})\}\left(s(m+\tfrac{n}{2}-1)+\lambda i\right)&=4^{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n}{2s}}\mathcal{M}\{(\tfrac{r^{2s}}{4})^{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n}{2s}}u^{\dagger}(r^{s})\}\left(\sigma_{m}-s+\lambda i\right)\\
&=4^{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n}{2s}}\mathcal{M}\{\Phi^{-1}_{s}u\}\left(\sigma_{m}-s+\lambda i\right)\\
&=4^{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n}{2s}}\Lambda_{s}^{-1}(\sigma_{m}-s+\lambda i)\mathcal{M}u(\sigma_{m}-s+\lambda i).\end{split}

We conclude that, up to multiplicative constant,

\begin{split}\|u\|^{2}_{\dot{\mathds{H}}^{1}_{\dagger}}&=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^{m}m!}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\{(\tfrac{\lambda}{s})^{2}+(m+\tfrac{n}{2}-1)^{2}\right\}\left|\Lambda_{s}^{-1}(\sigma_{m}-s+\lambda i)|^{2}\,|\mathcal{M}u(\sigma_{m}-s+\lambda i)\right|^{2}\,d\lambda,\end{split}

and then we can insert the estimate for
\Lambda_{s}^{-1}
analogous to (
4.15
).

We note here that
\|\cdot\|_{\dot{\mathds{H}}^{1}_{\dagger}}
is not our usual Sobolev
H^{s}
fractional seminorm. This is a consequence of our construction and is partly explained in Remark
4.2
.

4.4.
Technicalities

Here we obtain upper and lower bounds for the multiplier
\Lambda_{s}^{-1}(\sigma_{m}+i\lambda)
, understanding carefully the dependence of
m
as
m\to\infty
.

First, from (
4.7
)
we can check that, when
m\to\infty
, for
|\lambda|\gg 1
,

\left\lvert{\Lambda_{s}^{-1}(\sigma_{m}+i\lambda)}\right\rvert=2^{-(\frac{1}{s}-1)n}s^{-m}|\lambda|^{(1-s)(\frac{n}{2}-m)}(1+O(|\lambda|^{-1}))\asymp s^{-m}|\lambda|^{p_{m}},

where

(4.16)

p_{m}=n-\tfrac{n}{s}-\sigma_{m}+\tfrac{\sigma_{m}}{s}=(1-s)(m-\tfrac{n}{2}).

However, the expression of
\Lambda_{s}
may have poles/zeroes when
\lambda=0
. The following Lemma characterizes precisely its behavior at those points:

Lemma 4.9
.

For each
a\in\mathbb{R}
fixed, we have for
m\in\mathbb{N}
large,

(4.17)

\Lambda_{s}^{-1}(sm+a)\asymp\delta_{m}:=\frac{\sin(\frac{\pi(n-a)}{2s}-\frac{m\pi}{2})}{\sin(\frac{\pi(n-a)}{2}-\frac{sm\pi}{2})}\left(\frac{m}{es^{\frac{s}{1-s}}}\right)^{(\frac{1}{s}-1)(sm+a-n)}.

Proof.

Recall from (
4.6
) that

\Lambda_{s}^{-1}(sm+a)=2^{(\frac{1}{s}-1)(sm+a)}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n-a-sm}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{sm+a}{2}\right)}\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{n}{2}-\tfrac{n-a-sm}{2s})}{\Gamma(\tfrac{n-a-sm}{2s})}.

We use the relation
\Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi z)}
to have all the arguments inside the Gammas positive:

\Lambda_{s}^{-1}(sm+a)=2^{(\frac{1}{s}-1)(sm+a)}\frac{\sin(\pi(\tfrac{n-sm-a}{2s}))}{\sin(\pi(\tfrac{n-sm-a}{2}))}\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{n}{2}-\tfrac{n-a}{2s}+\tfrac{m}{2})\Gamma(1-\tfrac{n-a}{2s}+\tfrac{m}{2})}{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{a}{2}+\tfrac{sm}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(1-\tfrac{n-a}{2}+\tfrac{sm}{2}\right)}=:\delta_{m}^{(1)}\delta_{m}^{(2)}.

where we have defined

\delta_{m}^{(1)}:=\frac{\sin(\pi(\tfrac{n-sm-a}{2s}))}{\sin(\pi(\tfrac{n-sm-a}{2}))},\qquad\delta_{m}^{(2)}:=\Lambda_{s}^{-1}(sm+a)(\delta_{m}^{(1)})^{-1}.

Finally, expression (
B.1
) for the Gamma function immediately gives

\displaystyle\delta_{m}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=2^{(\frac{1}{s}-1)(sm+a)}\dfrac{(\frac{m}{2})^{\frac{m}{2}+\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n-a}{2s}-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\frac{m}{2}-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{n-a}{2s}}\cdot(\frac{m}{2})^{\frac{m}{2}+1-\frac{n-a}{2s}-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\frac{m}{2}-1+\frac{n-a}{2s}}}{(\frac{sm}{2})^{\frac{sm}{2}+\frac{a}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\frac{sm}{2}-\frac{a}{2}}\cdot(\frac{sm}{2})^{\frac{sm}{2}+1-\frac{n-a}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\frac{sm}{2}-1+\frac{n-a}{2}}}(1+O(m^{-1}))

\displaystyle=2^{(\frac{1}{s}-1)n}s^{-\frac{n}{2}+(n-sm-a)}e^{(\frac{1}{s}-1)(n-sm-a)}m^{(\frac{1}{s}-1)(sm+a-n)}(1+O(m^{-1}))

\displaystyle=2^{(\frac{1}{s}-1)n}s^{-\frac{n}{2}}\left(\frac{m}{es^{\frac{s}{1-s}}}\right)^{(\frac{1}{s}-1)(sm+a-n)}(1+O(m^{-1})).

This completes the estimate of
\delta_{m}
.
∎

We remark here that while
\delta_{m}^{(1)}
has a trivial lower bound independent of
m
, estimating its upper bound is more delicate since, as
m\to\infty
, there may be a sequence of
k=k_{m}
of even numbers such that

n-ms-a\to-k\in\mathbb{Z}
, even if
s
is irrational. We may try to give a sharper estimate for
\delta_{m}^{(1)}
, however this is a problem in the field of number theory which we do not attempt to consider here.

In any case, let us give here a flavour of the argument involved and consider the toy model of giving a lower found for
\sin(ms)
when
s\not\in\mathbb{Q}
. It may happen that
ms\to k\in\mathbb{Z}
, that is
\frac{k}{m}\to s\not\in\mathbb{Q}
. Thus we are looking at how to approximate an irrational number by rational numbers. If
\mu
is the
irrationality measure
of
s
, then fixed
\varepsilon>0
, for all sequences of
m
,
k
large enough,

\left|\frac{k}{m}-s\right|>\frac{1}{m^{\mu+\varepsilon}},

so that

(4.18)

\sin(\pi(ms))>\frac{1}{m^{\mu+\varepsilon-1}}.

For all
s
it holds that
\mu\geq 2
. Almost every irrational have
\mu=2
, and this includes all algebraic numbers of degree
>1
. However, there may be real numbers with
infinite
\mu
(these are called Liouville numbers), for which an estimate such as (
4.18
) does not hold. For some background on this topic, see
[
7
]
.

5.
The eigenvalue problem via Mellin transform

In this section we complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
. The idea is that the eigenvalue problem (
1.6
) can be explicitly solved in terms of Mellin variables, for functions in the space
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}(s)
. Although in the previous section we have strongly relied on the transformation (
1.7
) and the information available from the local case, now we will calculate the asymptotic behavior of all solutions
without
any previous knowledge.

5.1.
Explicit eigenfunctions under transformations

We take the Mellin transform of (
1.6
) to get

(5.1)

\mathcal{M}\left\{(-\Delta)^{s}u(r)\right\}(z)=\Big(\nu+\frac{n}{2s}\Big)\mathcal{M}\left\{u(r)\right\}(z)+\frac{1}{2s}\mathcal{M}\left\{ru^{\prime}(r)\right\}(z).

By
Proposition

3.7
and (
3.6
),

\Theta_{s}(z)\mathcal{M}u(z-2s)=(\nu+\tfrac{n-z}{2s})\mathcal{M}u(z).

Hence, recalling the symbol given in (
3.15
), equation (
5.1
) yields

(5.2)

2^{2s}\dfrac{\Gamma\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n-z}{2}\right)}\dfrac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+2s-z}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{z-2s}{2}\right)}\mathcal{M}u(z-2s)=\left(\nu+\tfrac{n-z}{2s}\right)\mathcal{M}u(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

A solution to this equation will be a (tentative) eigenfunction with eigenvalue
\nu
.

Lemma 5.1

(In Mellin variable)
.

For each
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
, the function

(5.3)

\widetilde{u}_{\nu}(z)=2^{z}\frac{\Gamma\left(\nu+\tfrac{n-z}{2s}\right)\Gamma\left(\tfrac{z}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{n-z}{2}\right)}.

is a solution of (
5.2
).

Remark 5.2
.

We will show in
Proposition

5.22
that this solution is unique in
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}
for
n\geq 2
, and for
n=1
provided that
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof of
Lemma

5.1
.

The essence is to observe the
2s
-periodicity of an auxiliary function. Rewriting (
5.2
) as

2^{-(z-2s)}2^{z}\dfrac{\Gamma\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n-z}{2}\right)}\dfrac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n-(z-2s)}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{z-2s}{2}\right)}\mathcal{M}u(z-2s)=\dfrac{\Gamma\left(\nu+\tfrac{n-(z-2s)}{2s}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\nu+\tfrac{n-z}{2s}\right)}\mathcal{M}u(z),

we realize that

v(z):=2^{-z}\dfrac{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{n-z}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{z}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\nu+\tfrac{n-z}{2s}\right)}\mathcal{M}u(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

satisfies

v(z-2s)=v(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

A solution of this equation is, obviously, the constant one, and this completes our claim.
∎

Now we would like to take inverse Mellin transform to define the eigenfunction
u_{\nu}(r)
in Euclidean variable. For this,
note that from (
B.2
) and (
B.3
) we know that
|\widetilde{u}_{\nu}(\sigma+i\lambda)|\leq C_{1}(\sigma)|\lambda|^{C_{2}(\sigma)}e^{-\frac{\pi}{4s}|\lambda|}
as
|\lambda|\to\infty
. Then one may define
u_{\nu}(r)
for each
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
by taking the inverse Mellin transform of (
5.3
). Uniqueness of the inverse is an issue only if
\nu\in\mathbb{N}
(since other values do not provide admissible eigenfunctions); this will be considered in Section
5.5
.

Next, let us prove an interpretation of formula (
5.3
) in terms of Fourier (Hankel) transform, and which brings us back to (
1.11
).

Proposition 5.3

(In Fourier variable)
.

The Hankel transform of the function
u_{\nu}(r)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for
\nu>-\frac{n-1}{4s}
, is given by

\widehat{u}_{\nu}(\zeta)=\mathcal{F}\left\{u_{\nu}(r)\right\}(\zeta)=2^{\frac{n}{2}+1}s\,e^{-\zeta^{2s}}\zeta^{2s\nu}.

Proof.

On the one hand, for
{\rm Re\,}(\nu+\tfrac{n-z}{2s})>0
, or
{\rm Re\,}z<n+2s\nu
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\left\{2^{\frac{n}{2}+1}s\,e^{-\zeta^{2s}}\zeta^{2s\nu}\right\}(n-z)

\displaystyle=2^{\frac{n}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\zeta^{2s}}\zeta^{2s\nu+n-z-2s}(2s\,\zeta^{2s-1}\,d\zeta)

5.2.
Fractional analogue of Hermite polynomials

We are interested in the inverse Mellin transform (particularly on the line

Lemma 5.4

For

Here

is the generalized Laguerre polynomial of degree

Proof.

The idea is that the rising factorial translates to derivatives of a monomial under Mellin transform. Keeping this in mind, using the so-called Rodrigues formula for Laguerre polynomials

L_{k}^{(\alpha)}(r)=\frac{1}{k!}r^{-\alpha}e^{r}\frac{d^{k}}{dr^{k}}(r^{\alpha+k}e^{-r}),

we compute, for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\left\{k!\,e^{-r}L_{k}^{(\alpha)}(r)\right\}(y)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}\left\{k!\,r^{\alpha}e^{-r}L_{k}^{(\alpha)}(r)\right\}(y-\alpha)=\mathcal{M}\left\{\dfrac{d^{k}}{dr^{k}}(r^{\alpha+k}e^{-r})\right\}(y-\alpha)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{y-\alpha-1}\dfrac{d^{k}}{dr^{k}}(r^{\alpha+k}e^{-r})\,dr=(-1)^{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{d^{k}}{dr^{k}}(r^{y-\alpha-1})r^{\alpha+k}e^{-r}\,dr

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}(y-\alpha-1)\cdots(y-\alpha-k)\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{y-1}e^{-r}\,dr

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\Gamma(k+\alpha+1-y)}{\Gamma(\alpha+1-y)}\Gamma(y),

for
\re y>0
. Choosing

,

\mathcal{M}\left\{k!\,e^{-r}L_{k}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}(r)\right\}(y)=\dfrac{\Gamma(k+\frac{n}{2}-y)}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-y)}\Gamma(y).

Now we make the change of variable

\tfrac{n-z}{2s}=\tfrac{n}{2}-y,

which implies

\mathcal{M}\left\{k!\,e^{-r}L_{k}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}(r)\right\}(\tfrac{n}{2}-\tfrac{n}{2s}+\tfrac{z}{2s})=\dfrac{\Gamma(k+\tfrac{n-z}{2s})}{\Gamma(\tfrac{n-z}{2s})}\Gamma(\tfrac{n}{2}-\tfrac{n}{2s}+\tfrac{z}{2s}),\quad\re(\tfrac{n-z}{2s})<\tfrac{n}{2}.

Recalling (
5.5
) with

we obtain

\mathcal{U}_{k}(z)=2^{\frac{z}{s}}\mathcal{M}\left\{k!\,e^{-r}L_{k}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}(r)\right\}(\tfrac{n}{2}-\tfrac{n}{2s}+\tfrac{z}{2s}).

Now, using (
3.8
)–(
3.9
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{k}(z)

\displaystyle=2^{\frac{z}{s}}\mathcal{M}\left\{2s\,k!\,e^{-r^{2s}}L_{k}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}(r^{2s})\right\}(ns-n+z)

\displaystyle=2^{\frac{z}{s}}\mathcal{M}\left\{2s\,k!\,r^{-n(1-s)}e^{-r^{2s}}L_{k}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}(r^{2s})\right\}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}\left\{2s\,k!\,2^{\frac{n(1-s)}{s}}r^{-n(1-s)}e^{-\frac{r^{2s}}{4}}L_{k}^{(\tfrac{n-2}{2})}(\tfrac{r^{2s}}{4})\right\}(z),

Proof of

This

and the (left) circular arc with radius

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}

\begin{split}I_{\sigma}(\rho)&=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}}f(z)\,dz\\

Proof of

By definition,

\displaystyle I_{\sigma,R,1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{z_{j}\text{ pole inside }\mathcal{C}}\Res\left(\rho^{-z}\dfrac{\Gamma\left(\nu+b-z\right)\Gamma(z)}{\Gamma\left(b-z\right)},z_{j}\right).

Since
\nu+\frac{n}{2s}
is not a non-positive integer, the integrand
f(z)
only admits simple poles, which come from
\Gamma(z)
at
z=-j\in(-\mathbb{N})\cap(-R,\sigma)
, and from
\Gamma(\nu+b-z)
at
\nu+b-z=-\ell\in-\mathbb{N}
with
z\in(-R,\sigma)
, i.e. at
z=\nu+b+\ell\in(\nu+b+\mathbb{N})\cap(-R,\sigma)
. (Note that the latter case can happen only when
\nu<-n
.) The residues are given, respectively, by

\displaystyle\Res\left(\rho^{-z}\dfrac{\Gamma(z)\Gamma(\nu+b-z)}{\Gamma(b-z)},-j\right)

\displaystyle=\rho^{j}\dfrac{\Gamma(\nu+b+j)}{\Gamma(b+j)}\Res(\Gamma,-j)=\rho^{j}\dfrac{\Gamma(\nu+b+j)}{\Gamma(b+j)}\dfrac{(-1)^{j}}{j!},

and

\displaystyle\Res

\displaystyle\left(\rho^{-z}\dfrac{\Gamma(z)\Gamma(\nu+b-z)}{\Gamma(b-z)},\nu+b+\ell\right)

\displaystyle=\rho^{-\nu-b-\ell}\dfrac{\Gamma(\nu+b+\ell)}{\Gamma(-\nu-\ell)}\Res\left(\Gamma(\nu+b-z),\nu+b+\ell\right)

\displaystyle=\rho^{-\nu-b-\ell}\dfrac{\Gamma(\nu+b+\ell)}{\Gamma(-\nu-\ell)}\cdot(-1)\Res\left(\Gamma,-\ell\right)

\displaystyle=\rho^{-\nu-b-\ell}\dfrac{\sin(\pi(\nu+\ell))}{\pi}\Gamma(\nu+1+\ell)\Gamma(\nu+b+\ell)\dfrac{(-1)^{\ell+1}}{\ell!}

\displaystyle=-\rho^{-\nu-b-\ell}\dfrac{\sin(\pi\nu)}{\pi}\Gamma(\nu+1+\ell)\Gamma(\nu+b+\ell)\dfrac{1}{\ell!}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle I_{\sigma,R,1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}\cap(-\sigma,R)}\rho^{j}\dfrac{\Gamma(\nu+b+j)}{\Gamma(b+j)}\dfrac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}\cap(-\nu-b-R,-\nu-b+\sigma)}\rho^{-\nu-b-\ell}\dfrac{\sin(\pi\nu)}{\pi}\Gamma(\nu+1+\ell)\Gamma(\nu+b+\ell)\dfrac{1}{\ell!},

as desired.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

5.8
.

From (
5.10
),
Lemma

5.10
and
Lemma

5.11
, it follows that

\displaystyle I_{\sigma,\nu,b}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0\vee\left\lceil{-\sigma}\right\rceil}^{\infty}\dfrac{\Gamma(\nu+b+j)}{\Gamma(b+j)}\dfrac{(-1)^{j}}{j!}\rho^{j}

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{\ell=0}^{\left\lfloor{-\nu-b+\sigma}\right\rfloor}\dfrac{\sin(\pi\nu)}{\pi}\dfrac{\Gamma(\nu+1+\ell)\Gamma(\nu+b+\ell)}{\ell!}\rho^{-\nu-b-\ell}.

It remains to write the summation in
j
into two: the first part from
0
to
\infty
, minus the second from
0
to
\left\lfloor{-\sigma}\right\rfloor
. Since by definition of the rising factorial
a^{(j)}=a(a+1)\cdots(a+j-1)
,
\Gamma(a+j)=\Gamma(a)a^{(j)}
, the summation in

can be written in terms of the Kummer (or confluent hypergeometric) function

M(a,b,z)={}_{1}F_{1}(a;b;z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{a^{(j)}}{b^{(j)}}\dfrac{z^{j}}{j!},

with
a=\nu+b
and

.
∎

5.4.
Eigenfunctions in Euclidean variable

When

\Psi

Theorem 5.13

(Explicit eigenfunctions)
.

Let
s\in(0,1]
. In the case

, we assume
\nu>-\frac{n}{2s}
and consider the Mellin inversion along the

are given explicitly for

in terms of special functions by

u_{\nu}(r)=\begin{cases}2\,{}_{1}\Psi_{1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}(\nu+\tfrac{n}{2s},\tfrac{1}{s})\\

-2s\left(\frac{2}{r}\right)^{2s\nu+n}{}_{1}\Psi_{1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}(s\nu+\tfrac{n}{2},s)\\

Notice that
u_{\nu}(r)
is analytic up to
r=0
exactly when
s\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
. Here
{}_{1}\Psi_{1}
denotes the Fox–Wright
\Psi
-function and
\Hyperg
denotes the Gauss hypergeometric function. The derivations will be carried out case by case. For the series representations, see
Lemma

5.14
,
Lemma

5.17
and
Lemma

5.21

Lemma 5.14

(Subcritical case)
.

Let
s\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1]
and
\nu>-\tfrac{n}{2s}
. The eigenfunctions

, obtained by the inverse Mellin transform of

on
\re z=\sigma

Remark 5.15
.

Observe that the series converges absolutely for all

While the condition

Remark 5.16
.

We note the following special cases:

•

When

•

When

Proof of
Lemma

5.14
.

We first scale away the factors using (

) and (

),

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}^{-1}\left\{\frac{\Gamma(\nu+\tfrac{n}{2s}-\frac{z}{s})\Gamma(z)}{\Gamma(\tfrac{n}{2}-z)}\right\}(\rho)

\displaystyle=:H_{1,2}^{1,1}\left(\rho\,\Big|\begin{array}[]{cc}(1-\nu-\tfrac{n}{2s},\tfrac{1}{s})&\\
(0,1)&(1-\tfrac{n}{2},1)\end{array}\right)

is, by definition, a special case of the Fox

17

(notice that

The decay of

.

Lemma 5.17

(Critical case)
.

Let
s=\frac{1}{2}
. Then

\displaystyle u_{\nu}(r)

\displaystyle=2^{-\frac{n-2}{2}}\dfrac{\Gamma(n+\nu)}{\Gamma(\tfrac{n}{2})}\Hyperg\big(\tfrac{n+\nu}{2},\tfrac{n+\nu+1}{2};\tfrac{n}{2};-r^{2}\big)

Here

which satisfies the differential equation

(1-z^{2})\frac{d^{2}u}{dz^{2}}-2z\frac{du}{dz}+\left(\nu(\nu+1)-\frac{\mu^{2}}{1-z^{2}}\right)u=0,

The inverse Hankel transform of

\displaystyle u_{\nu}(r)=r^{-\frac{n-2}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\zeta r)\zeta^{\frac{n}{2}+\nu}e^{-\zeta}\,d\zeta,

Remark 5.18
.

We list some useful properties of
P^{-\mu}_{\nu}(x)
, for
|x|<1
.

•

When
\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{N}
,

P^{-\mu}_{\nu}
and
P^{\mu}_{\nu}
are related by
P^{-\mu}_{\nu}(x)=(-1)^{\mu}\frac{(\nu-\mu)!}{(\nu+\mu)!}P^{\mu}_{\nu}(x)
. For general parameters
\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{R}
, a similar relation holds
[
22
, 8.737 1.]
.

•

For
\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{R}
,
P^{\mu}_{-\nu-1}(x)=P^{\mu}_{\nu}(x)
.

•

For
\mu+\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
,
P^{\mu}_{\nu}(-x)=(-1)^{\mu+\nu}P^{\mu}_{\nu}(x)
. In the general case
\mu+\nu\in\mathbb{R}
, see
[
22
, 8.737 2.]
.

•

When
\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{N}
, the Rodrigues formula reads

P^{\mu}_{\nu}(x)=\dfrac{(-1)^{\mu+\nu}}{2^{\nu}\nu!}(1-x^{2})^{\frac{\mu}{2}}\dfrac{d^{\mu+\nu}}{dx^{\mu+\nu}}(1-x^{2})^{\nu}.

In particular,
P^{\mu}_{\nu}(x)
is a closed algebraic expression involving powers of
x
and
\sqrt{1-x^{2}}
.

Lemma 5.19

(Eigenfunctions in even dimensions)
.

Let
n\geq 2
be even,
s=\frac{1}{2}
,
\nu=k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then:

•

u_{k}(r)
is a closed algebraic expression involving powers of
r
and
\sqrt{r^{2}+1}
.

•

The orthogonality condition holds for

even:

\int_{0}^{\infty}u_{k}(r)u_{l}(r)\big(\sqrt{r^{2}+1}\big)^{k+l+n-1}r^{n-1}\,dr=\frac{(k+1)!(n+k-1)!}{n+2k+1}\delta_{kl}.

Proof.

The first item follows from
Remark

5.18
. For the second, we use
[
22
, 7.122, 7.123]
to see that

\int_{-1}^{1}P^{\frac{n-2}{2}}_{\frac{n}{2}+k}(x)P^{\frac{n-2}{2}}_{\frac{n}{2}+l}(x)\,dx=\dfrac{2}{n+2k+1}\dfrac{(n+k-1)!}{(k+1)!}\delta_{kl}.

Reflecting the upper index,

\int_{-1}^{1}P^{-\frac{n-2}{2}}_{\frac{n}{2}+k}(x)P^{-\frac{n-2}{2}}_{\frac{n}{2}+l}(x)\,dx=\dfrac{2}{n+2k+1}\dfrac{(k+1)!}{(n+k-1)!}\delta_{kl}.

Writing the integral on
[0,1]
, we have

\big(1+(-1)^{k+l}\big)\int_{0}^{1}P^{-\frac{n-2}{2}}_{\frac{n}{2}+k}(x)P^{-\frac{n-2}{2}}_{\frac{n}{2}+l}(x)\,dx=\dfrac{2}{n+2k+1}\dfrac{(k+1)!}{(n+k-1)!}\delta_{kl}.

This is nontrivial for

even, and we change variable to
x=\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^{2}+1}}
which yields

\int_{0}^{\infty}P^{-\frac{n-2}{2}}_{\frac{n}{2}+k}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^{2}+1}}\Big)P^{-\frac{n-2}{2}}_{\frac{n}{2}+l}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^{2}+1}}\Big)\frac{r}{\big(\sqrt{r^{2}+1}\big)^{3}}\,dr=\dfrac{1}{n+2k+1}\dfrac{(k+1)!}{(n+k-1)!}\delta_{kl}.

Writing this in terms of
u_{k}
and

, the result follows.
∎

Lemma 5.20
.

As
s\to(\frac{1}{2})^{+}
,

\mathcal{M}_{2\sigma}^{-1}\left\{u_{\nu}(z)\right\}(r)\to C\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{\widehat{u}(\zeta)\right\}(r),\qquad\forall r\in[0,1),

for some explicit constant

.

Proof.

By legendre duplication formula, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2\sigma}^{-1}\left\{u_{\nu}(z)\right\}(r)

\displaystyle\to 2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\Gamma(\nu+n+2j)}{\Gamma(\tfrac{n}{2}+j)j!}\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{4}\right)^{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{\nu+n+2j}\pi^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{\nu+n}{2}+j)\Gamma(\frac{\nu+n+1}{2}+j)}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}+j)j!}\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{4}\right)^{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\nu+n}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{\nu+n}{2})\Gamma(\frac{\nu+n+1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}\Hyperg\big(\tfrac{n+\nu}{2},\tfrac{n+\nu+1}{2};\tfrac{n}{2};-r^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2\Gamma(n+\nu)}{\Gamma(\tfrac{n}{2})}\Hyperg\big(\tfrac{n+\nu}{2},\tfrac{n+\nu+1}{2};\tfrac{n}{2};-r^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=2^{-\frac{n-2}{2}}\dfrac{\Gamma(n+\nu)}{\Gamma(\tfrac{n}{2})}\Hyperg\big(\tfrac{n+\nu}{2},\tfrac{n+\nu+1}{2};\tfrac{n}{2};-r^{2}\big),

which is
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{\widehat{u}(\zeta)\right\}(r)
up to a constant.
∎

Lemma 5.21

(Supercritical case)
.

Let
s\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
,
\nu>-\frac{n}{2s}
. The eigenfunctions
u_{\nu}(r)
, obtained by the inverse Mellin transform of
\widetilde{u}_{\nu}(z)
on

\displaystyle=-2s\left(\frac{2}{r}\right)^{2s\nu+n}{}_{1}\Psi_{1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}(s\nu+\tfrac{n}{2},s)\\
(-s\nu,-s)\end{array};\,-\Big(\frac{2}{r}\Big)^{2s}\right)

\displaystyle=2s\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{j+1}\dfrac{\Gamma(s\nu+\frac{n}{2}+sj)}{\Gamma(-s\nu-sj)j!}\Big(\frac{2}{r}\Big)^{n+2s(\nu+j)}.

Proof.

As in the subcritical case
s\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
, we first scale away the factors using (
3.11
) and (
3.12
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2s\sigma}^{-1}\left\{\widetilde{u}_{\nu}(z)\right\}(r)

\displaystyle=2s\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}^{-1}\left\{\frac{\Gamma(\nu+\tfrac{n}{2s}-z)\Gamma(sz)}{\Gamma(\tfrac{n}{2}-sz)}\right\}\left(\frac{r^{2s}}{2^{2s}}\right).

Write
\rho=2^{-2s}r^{2s}
. Recall that by the inverse Mellin transform (
3.1
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}^{-1}\left\{\frac{\Gamma(\nu+\tfrac{n}{2s}-z)\Gamma(sz)}{\Gamma(\tfrac{n}{2}-sz)}\right\}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{\sigma-iR}^{\sigma+iR}\frac{\Gamma(\nu+\tfrac{n}{2s}-z)\Gamma(sz)}{\Gamma(\tfrac{n}{2}-sz)}\rho^{-z}\,dz.

Using Stirling’s and Euler’s reflection formulae, we can show that the integrand decays along the right semicircle like
R^{-(\frac{1}{2s}-1)R\cos\theta}e^{O(R)}
, for
\theta=\arg z\in(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\Gamma(s\nu+\frac{n}{2}+sj)}{\Gamma(-s\nu-sj)}\rho^{-\nu-\frac{n}{2s}-j}\Res\left(\Gamma(\nu+\tfrac{n}{2s}-z),\nu+\tfrac{n}{2s}+j\right)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}^{-1}\left\{\frac{\Gamma(\nu+\tfrac{n}{2s}-z)\Gamma(sz)}{\Gamma(\tfrac{n}{2}-sz)}\right\}(\rho)

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{\rho^{\nu+\frac{n}{2s}}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\Gamma(s\nu+\frac{n}{2}+sj)}{\Gamma(-s\nu-sj)j!}\Big({-}\frac{1}{\rho}\Big)^{j}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{\rho^{\nu+\frac{n}{2s}}}{}_{1}\Psi_{1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}(s\nu+\tfrac{n}{2},s)\\

\displaystyle u_{\nu}(r):=\mathcal{M}_{2s\sigma}^{-1}\left\{\widetilde{u}_{\nu}(z)\right\}(r)

\displaystyle=-2s\left(\frac{2}{r}\right)^{2s\nu+n}{}_{1}\Psi_{1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}(s\nu+\tfrac{n}{2},s)\\
(-s\nu,-s)\end{array};\,-\Big(\frac{2}{r}\Big)^{2s}\right)

\displaystyle=-2s\left(\frac{2}{r}\right)^{2s\nu+n}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\Gamma(s\nu+\frac{n}{2}+sj)}{\Gamma(-s\nu-sj)j!}\Big({-}\frac{2}{r}\Big)^{2sj},

as desired.

5.5.
Uniqueness for a non-local ODE

Here we prove the uniqueness claim for solutions of the eigenvalue equation (
1.6
) in the space
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}
(Remark
5.2
). Contrary to the local case
s=1
, where one may calculate the indicial roots of an ODE (see, for instance, the characterization in
[
36
, Proposition 2.2]
), in the non-local setting
s\in(0,1)
uniqueness is a non-trivial issue. Here we use a complex variable approach.

Proposition 5.22

(Uniqueness)
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
,
n\geq 2s
,
u\in\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}
be a radially symmetric solution to (
1.6
). Assume that
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
in case
n=1
. Then, up to a scalar multiple,

\mathcal{M}u(z)=2^{z}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{z}{2})\Gamma(\nu+\frac{n-z}{2s})}{\Gamma(\frac{n-z}{2})}.

Proof.

By
Lemma

4.1
,
u\in\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}
implies that
u\in\mathds{H}^{k}_{\dagger}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We have seen in the proof of Lemma
5.1
that, if set

\mathcal{M}u(z):=2^{z}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{z}{2})\Gamma(\nu+\frac{n-z}{2s})}{\Gamma(\frac{n-z}{2})}v(z),

then
v
is a
2s
-periodic function on some strip.
Using the multiplier notation, this is equivalent to

\mathcal{M}(\Phi_{s}^{-1}u)(z)=2^{\frac{z}{s}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n}{2s}+\frac{z}{2s})\Gamma(\nu+\frac{n-z}{2s})}{\Gamma(\frac{n-z}{2s})}v(z).

In order to handle the poles of
\Gamma(\frac{n-z}{2s})
, we may rewrite this as

(5.12)

\mathcal{M}(\Phi_{s}^{-1}u)(z)=2^{\frac{z}{s}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n}{2s}+\frac{z}{2s})\Gamma(\nu+\frac{n-z}{2s})}{\Gamma(\frac{n-z}{2s})\sin(\frac{n-z}{2s}2\pi)}\phi(\tfrac{n-z}{2s}).

for some
1
-periodic function
\phi
.

Note that, on the one hand, for
\tilde{z}\in\mathbb{C}
with
\re\tilde{z}=\sigma
,

(5.13)

\begin{split}\left\lvert{\mathcal{M}\left\{\partial_{\rho}^{k}u^{\dagger}(\rho)\right\}(\tilde{z})}\right\rvert&\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^{\sigma-1}\left\lvert{\partial_{\rho}^{k}u^{\dagger}(\rho)}\right\rvert\,d\rho\\
&\leq\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\lvert{\partial_{\rho}^{k}u^{\dagger}(\rho)}\right\rvert^{2}e^{\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}}\rho^{n-1}\,d\rho\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}}\rho^{1-n+2\sigma-2}\,d\rho\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty,\end{split}

as long as
u\in\mathds{H}_{\dagger}^{k}
and
\sigma>\frac{n}{2}
. On the other hand, transforming the derivatives

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}\left\{\partial_{\rho}^{k}u^{\dagger}(\rho)\right\}(\tilde{z})&=\mathcal{M}\left\{\rho\partial_{\rho}^{k}u^{\dagger}(\rho)\right\}(\tilde{z}-1)=-(\tilde{z}-1)\mathcal{M}\left\{\partial_{\rho}^{k-1}u^{\dagger}(\rho)\right\}(\tilde{z}-1)\\
&=-(\tilde{z}-1)\mathcal{M}\left\{\rho\partial_{\rho}^{k-1}u^{\dagger}(\rho)\right\}(\tilde{z}-2)=(\tilde{z}-1)(\tilde{z}-2)\mathcal{M}\left\{\partial_{\rho}^{k-2}u^{\dagger}(\rho)\right\}(\tilde{z}-2)\\
&=\cdots=(-1)^{k}(\tilde{z}-1)\cdots(\tilde{z}-k)\mathcal{M}\left\{u^{\dagger}(\rho)\right\}(\tilde{z}-k),\end{split}

then using (
4.9
) to relate with
\Phi_{s}^{-1}u
,

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}\left\{\partial_{\rho}^{k}u^{\dagger}(\rho)\right\}(\tilde{z})&=(-1)^{k}\frac{\Gamma(\tilde{z})}{\Gamma(\tilde{z}-k)}\mathcal{M}\left\{\left(\tfrac{\rho}{2}\right)^{\frac{n}{s}-n}\Phi_{s}^{-1}u(\rho^{\frac{1}{s}})\right\}(\tilde{z}-k)\\
&=2^{n-\frac{n}{s}}s(-1)^{k}\frac{\Gamma(\tilde{z})}{\Gamma(\tilde{z}-k)}\mathcal{M}\left\{r^{n-ns}\Phi_{s}^{-1}u(r)\right\}(s\tilde{z}-ks)\\
&=2^{n-\frac{n}{s}}s(-1)^{k}\frac{\Gamma(\tilde{z})}{\Gamma(\tilde{z}-k)}\mathcal{M}\left\{\Phi_{s}^{-1}(r)\right\}(s\tilde{z}-ks+n-ns),\end{split}

we can use (
5.12
) by setting
z=s\tilde{z}-ks+n-ns
. In the variable
w=\frac{n-z}{2s}=\frac{k+n-\tilde{z}}{2}
, we have

(5.14)

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}\left\{\partial_{\rho}^{k}u^{\dagger}(\rho)\right\}(k+n-2w)&=2^{n-2w}s(-1)^{k}\frac{\Gamma(k+n-2w)}{\Gamma(n-2w)}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n-2w}{2})\Gamma(\nu+w)}{\Gamma(w)\sin(2\pi w)}\phi(w)\\
&=2\sqrt{\pi}s(-1)^{k}\frac{\Gamma(k+n-2w)\Gamma(\nu+w)}{\Gamma(\frac{n+1}{2}-w)\Gamma(w)\sin(2\pi w)}\phi(w),\end{split}

where
\phi(w)
is
1
-periodic, after using Legendre duplication formula. Combining (
5.13
)–(
5.14
) yields

(5.15)

\begin{split}|\phi(w)|&\leq\frac{C(n,s,k)}{2\sigma-n}\left\|{u}\right\|_{\mathds{H}^{k}_{\dagger}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n+1}{2}-w)\Gamma(w)\sin(2\pi w)}{\Gamma(k+n-2w)\Gamma(\nu+w)}<\infty.\end{split}

whenever
\sigma=\re(k+n-2w)=\re\tilde{z}>\frac{n}{2}
, i.e.
\re w<\frac{n}{4}+\frac{k}{2}
. In fact,

|\phi(w)|\leq Ce^{\frac{5\pi}{2}|\lambda|}|\lambda|^{C}\quad\text{as}\quad|\lambda|:=|\im w|\to\infty,

in a band
\mathcal{B}:=\{\sigma_{*}-1<\re w<\sigma_{*}\}
for some
\sigma_{*}<\frac{n}{4}+\frac{k}{2}
.

Now we make the change of variable

\psi(\eta)=\phi(w),\qquad\text{ for }\qquad\eta=e^{2\pi iw},

which maps the band
\mathcal{B}
one-to-one into the complex plane. Moreover,
\psi(\eta)
is an holomorphic function on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
which satisfies

|\psi(\eta)|\leq\begin{cases}C|\eta|^{(\frac{5}{4})^{+}}&\text{as }|\eta|\to\infty,\\
C|\eta|^{-(\frac{5}{4})^{+}}&\text{as }|\eta|\to 0.\end{cases}

By a generalization of Liouville’s theorem, we conclude that

\psi(\eta)=a\eta+b\eta^{-1}+c

for some constants
a,b,c\in\mathbb{C}
. We go back to the variable
w
and write trigonometrically,

\phi(w)=a\sin(2\pi w)+b\cos(2\pi w)+c.

for some (renamed)
a,b,c\in\mathbb{C}
. Therefore, (
5.15
) gives

\left\lvert{a+\dfrac{b\cos(2\pi w)+c}{\sin(2\pi w)}}\right\rvert\dfrac{|\Gamma(k+n-2w)||\Gamma(\nu+w)|}{|\Gamma(\frac{n+1}{2}-w)||\Gamma(w)|}<\infty,\quad\text{ for }\re w<\tfrac{n}{4}+\tfrac{k}{2}.

Taking
w=-\frac{1}{2}
, we get
b=c
, so we arrive (using trigonometry and Euler reflection) to

\left\lvert{a\sin(\pi w)+b\cos(\pi w)}\right\rvert\frac{|\Gamma(k+n-2w)||\Gamma(\nu+w)||\Gamma(1-w)|}{|\Gamma(\frac{n+1}{2}-w)|}<\infty,\quad\text{ for }\re w<\tfrac{n}{4}+\tfrac{k}{2}.

If
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
, putting
w\in-\nu-\mathbb{N}
negative gives
b=0
, as desired. If
n\geq 2
, we fix
k=2
so that
\frac{n}{4}+\frac{k}{2}>1
, and putting
w=1
again forces
b=0
.
∎

Remark 5.23
.

If
\nu\notin\mathbb{Z}
and
n=1
, it can happen that
a=0
,
b\neq 0
,
\nu=\frac{1}{2}
, say.

6.
Eigenvalues for the adjoint problem

Now we consider the eigenvalue problem for the adjoint operator, this is

(6.1)

L_{s}^{*}w=(-\Delta)^{s}w+\frac{1}{2s}rw_{r}=\nu w,\quad w=w(r).

We will characterize the
L^{2}
-dual space of
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}
in Section
6.3
. As a consequence, having already a good description of the spectral properties for
L_{s}
in our function space, the properties of its adjoint
L_{s}^{*}
follow easily.

If, instead, one tries to use the arguments in Section (
5
) directly, one encounters the need of introducing homogeneous distributions (see, for instance,
[
31
]
and the references therein). Some of these difficulties are already present in the local case
s=1
, since eingenfunctions are polynomials

\omega_{k}^{(1)}(r)=L_{k}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}\big(\tfrac{r^{2}}{4}\big),

with eigenvalues
\nu_{k}=k
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, which do not have a well behaved Fourier/Mellin transform.

In the fractional case
s\in(0,1)
, we encounter the same obstacles, as we next explain. First, proceed as in the previous sections, taking Mellin transform of (
6.1
),

\Theta_{s}(\lambda)\,\mathcal{M}w(z-2s)=\left(\nu+\frac{z}{2s}\right)\mathcal{M}w(z).

Now we use the auxiliary function

v(z):=2^{-z}\dfrac{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{n-z}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\nu+\tfrac{z}{2s}+1\right)}{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{z}{2}\right)}\mathcal{M}w(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

which implies

v(z-2s)-v(z)=0.

Unfortunately, now we cannot conclude that
v\equiv 1
, since homogeneous distributions are involved. In any case, let us write, formally,

\widetilde{w}_{\nu}(z):=\mathcal{M}w(z)=2^{z}\dfrac{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{z}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{n-z}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\nu+\tfrac{z}{2s}+1\right)}v_{*}(z)

Again, we split

\widetilde{w}_{\nu}(z)=\Lambda^{*}_{s}(z)\mathcal{W}_{\nu}(z),

where we have defined the multiplier
\Lambda^{*}_{s}(\lambda)
by

(6.2)

\Lambda^{*}_{s}(z)=2^{z}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{z}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{z}{2})}2^{-\frac{z}{s}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{z}{2s})}{\Gamma(\frac{z}{2s})}.

and the function

(6.3)

\mathcal{W}_{\nu}(z):=2^{\frac{z}{s}}\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{z}{2s}\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{z}{2s}\big)}\frac{1}{\Gamma(\frac{z}{2s}+1+\nu)}v_{*}(z).

One may try to show the counterpart of Lemma
5.4
for the adjoint problem. For
\nu=k
,
k=0,1,\ldots
, up to multiplicative constant, the function in (
6.3
) is the Mellin transform of a polynomial

(6.4)

\mathcal{W}_{k}=\mathcal{M}\mathfrak{L}_{k},

where we have defined

(6.5)

\mathfrak{L}_{k}(r)=\frac{2s}{\Gamma(k+\frac{n}{2})}L_{k}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}(\tfrac{r^{2s}}{4}).

Unfortunately, proving (
6.4
) needs a serious immersion in distribution theory. Instead, in Section
6.3
we will use duality to calculate the eigenfunctions of (
6.1
).

6.1.
The multiplier

Similarly to the arguments in Section
4.2
, we define
\Phi^{*}_{s}
to be the functional with Mellin multiplier given by the multiplier
\Lambda^{*}_{s}
from (
6.2
). We will show that it has a simple formula using Fourier transform. Similarly to the above, we change variable
\vartheta=\zeta^{s}
and define

w^{\ddagger}(\rho)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{\vartheta^{\frac{n}{s}-n}{w^{\sharp}}(\vartheta)\right\}(\rho)=\rho^{\frac{2-n}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\zeta\rho)\zeta^{\frac{n}{2}}\zeta^{\frac{n}{s}-n}\widehat{w}(\zeta^{\frac{1}{s}})\,d\zeta,

where
w^{\sharp}(\vartheta)=\widehat{w}(\vartheta^{\frac{1}{s}})
as in (
4.1
).
From here we can understand
w\mapsto w^{\ddagger}
as an integral operator

(6.6)

\begin{split}w^{\ddagger}(\rho)&=\int_{0}^{\infty}A^{\ddagger}_{s}(\rho,\rho_{1})w(\rho_{1})\,d\rho_{1},\end{split}

where

(6.7)

A^{\ddagger}_{s}(\rho,\rho_{1})=\rho^{\frac{2-n}{2}}\rho_{1}^{\frac{n}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\zeta\rho)J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\zeta^{1/s}\rho_{1})\zeta^{-\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n-2}{2s}+\frac{n}{s}}\,d\zeta.

Although (
6.7
) does not converge, we provide a formal interpretation,
using the well known formula

\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{d}{d\rho}\right)^{m}[\rho^{\alpha}J_{\alpha}(\rho)]=\rho^{\alpha-m}J_{\alpha-m}(\rho),

for
m\in\mathbb{N}
, which leads in our case to

J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\zeta^{1/s}\rho_{1})=\frac{1}{\rho_{1}^{\frac{n-2}{2}}}\left(\frac{1}{\rho_{1}}\frac{d}{d\rho_{1}}\right)^{m}\left[\rho_{1}^{\frac{n-2}{2}+m}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}+m}(\zeta^{1/s}\rho_{1})\right]\frac{1}{\zeta^{m/s}},

so that the redefined kernel

A^{\ddagger}_{s}(\rho,\rho_{1}):=\rho^{\frac{2-n}{2}}\rho_{1}\left(\frac{1}{\rho_{1}}\frac{d}{d\rho_{1}}\right)^{m}\left[\rho_{1}^{\frac{n-2}{2}+m}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\zeta\rho)J_{\frac{n-2}{2}+m}(\zeta^{1/s}\rho_{1})\zeta^{-\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n-2}{2s}+\frac{n}{s}-\frac{m}{s}}\,d\zeta\right]

makes sense for
m\in\mathbb{N}
large enough.

The following Lemma states that
\Phi_{s}^{*}
also encodes the change of variable (
1.7
):

Lemma 6.1
.

It holds

(\Phi_{s}^{*})^{-1}w(r)=w^{\ddagger}(r^{s}).

Proof.

Using (
3.7
), (
3.9
) as well as (
3.19
) in the form

\mathcal{M}\left\{\mathcal{F}^{-1}u(r)\right\}(z)=2^{-\frac{n}{2}}2^{z}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{z}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{n-z}{2})}\mathcal{M}u(n-z),\quad\text{ for }\quad 0<\re z<\tfrac{n+1}{2},

and

\mathcal{M}\left\{\mathcal{F}^{-1}u(r)\right\}(\tfrac{z}{s})=2^{-\frac{n}{2}}2^{\frac{z}{s}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{z}{2s})}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{z}{2s})}\mathcal{M}u(n-\tfrac{z}{s}),\quad\text{ for }\quad 0<\re\tfrac{z}{s}<\tfrac{n+1}{2},

we compute

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\left\{(\Phi_{s}^{*})^{-1}w(r)\right\}(z)

\displaystyle=2^{\frac{z}{s}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{z}{2s})}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{z}{2s})}\cdot 2^{-z}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{z}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{z}{2})}\mathcal{M}w(z)

\displaystyle=2^{-\frac{n}{2}}2^{\frac{z}{s}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{z}{2s})}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{z}{2s})}\mathcal{M}\left\{\widehat{w}(\zeta)\right\}(n-z)

\displaystyle=2^{-\frac{n}{2}}2^{\frac{z}{s}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{z}{2s})}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{z}{2s})}\frac{1}{s}\mathcal{M}\left\{\widehat{w}(\zeta^{\frac{1}{s}})\right\}\left(\tfrac{n-z}{s}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\cdot 2^{-\frac{n}{2}}2^{\frac{z}{s}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{z}{2s})}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{z}{2s})}\mathcal{M}\left\{\zeta^{\frac{n}{s}-n}\widehat{w}(\zeta^{\frac{1}{s}})\right\}\left(n-\tfrac{z}{s}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\mathcal{M}\left\{\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{\zeta^{\frac{n}{s}-n}\widehat{w}(\zeta^{\frac{1}{s}})\right\}(r)\right\}\left(\tfrac{z}{s}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}\left\{\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{\zeta^{\frac{n}{s}-n}\widehat{w}(\zeta^{\frac{1}{s}})\right\}(r^{s})\right\}(z).

Since these equalities are valid on the strip
0<\re z<\tfrac{n+1}{2}s
,
Theorem

3.1
yields the result.
∎

As in
Lemma

4.1
, the transformation
w\mapsto w^{\ddagger}
relates
L_{s}^{*}
to
L_{1}^{*}
. Function spaces will be detailed below.

Lemma 6.2
.

If
w
solves
L_{s}^{*}w=g
, then
L_{1}^{*}w^{{\ddagger}}=g^{{\ddagger}}
.

Proof.

Similarly to
Lemma

4.1
, in the radial Fourier variable
\zeta=|\xi|
we have

\zeta^{2s}\widehat{w}(\zeta)-\frac{1}{2s}\left(n\widehat{w}(\zeta)+\zeta\partial_{\zeta}\widehat{w}(\zeta)\right)=\widehat{g}(\zeta),\qquad\zeta>0.

Putting
\vartheta=\zeta^{s}
, we have
\zeta\partial_{\zeta}=s\vartheta\partial_{\vartheta}
. Now
\widehat{w^{\ddagger}}(\vartheta)=\vartheta^{\frac{n}{s}-n}\widehat{w}(\vartheta^{\frac{1}{s}})
satisfies

(n+\vartheta\partial_{\vartheta})\widehat{w^{\ddagger}}(\vartheta)=\vartheta^{\frac{n}{s}-n}\left(\frac{n}{s}+\vartheta\partial_{\vartheta}\right)\widehat{w}(\vartheta^{\frac{1}{s}}),

and we conclude by multiplying both sides by
\vartheta^{\frac{n}{s}-n}
and taking the inverse Fourier transform.
∎

6.2.
The scalar product

We define the Hilbert space
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\ddagger}
by the scalar product

\begin{split}\left\langle{w_{1},w_{2}}\right\rangle_{\ddagger}&=\int_{0}^{\infty}(\Phi_{s}^{*})^{-1}w_{1}(r)\overline{(\Phi_{s}^{*})^{-1}w_{2}(r)}\,e^{-\frac{r^{2s}}{4}}r^{sn-1}\,dr.\end{split}

Thanks to Lemma
6.1
, we can rewrite it as follows:

\langle w_{1},w_{2}\rangle_{{\ddagger}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}w_{1}^{\ddagger}(r^{s})\overline{w_{1}^{\ddagger}(r^{s})}e^{-\frac{r^{2s}}{4}}r^{sn-1}\,dr.

We can also give a nice integral representation (although it needs to be interpreted distributionally (recall Section
6.1
):

Proposition 6.3
.

It holds

(6.8)

\begin{split}\langle w_{1},w_{2}\rangle_{\ddagger}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}G^{\ddagger}_{s}(r_{1},r_{2})w_{1}(r_{1})w_{2}(r_{2})\,dr_{1}\,dr_{2}\end{split}

for a kernel

(6.9)

\begin{split}G_{s}^{\ddagger}(r_{1},r_{2})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{K}_{s}(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})(\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2})^{\frac{n}{s}}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\zeta_{1}^{1/s}r_{1})r_{1}^{\frac{n}{2}}\zeta_{1}^{\frac{2-n}{2s}}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\zeta_{2}^{1/s}r_{2})r_{2}^{\frac{n}{2}}\zeta_{2}^{\frac{2-n}{2s}}\,d\zeta_{1}\,d\zeta_{2}.\end{split}

Here

(6.10)

\mathcal{K}_{s}(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})=\frac{1}{2}(\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2})^{-\frac{n}{2}}e^{-(\zeta_{1}^{2}+\zeta_{2}^{2})}I_{\frac{n-2}{2}}\left(2\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}\right).

Proof.

We first use formula (
6.6
) to write, up to a constant factor,

\begin{split}\langle w_{1},w_{2}\rangle_{\ddagger}&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}G^{\ddagger}_{s}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})w_{1}(\rho_{1})w_{2}(\rho_{2})\,d\rho_{1}\,d\rho_{2}\end{split}

for

G^{\ddagger}_{s}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2}):=\int_{0}^{\infty}A^{\ddagger}_{s}(\rho,\rho_{1})A^{\ddagger}_{s}(\rho,\rho_{2})e^{-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}}\rho^{n-1}\,d\rho,

We define

\mathcal{K}_{s}(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}):=(\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2})^{-\frac{n}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\zeta_{1}\rho)J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\zeta_{2}r)e^{-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}}\rho\,d\rho.

Then claim (
6.9
) follows directly from expression (
6.7
).

Now, to obtain a more explicit formula for
\mathcal{K}_{s}
we use
the following formula from
[
51
, Section 13.31]

(6.11)

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-p^{2}\rho^{2}}J_{\nu}(\zeta_{1}\rho)J_{\nu}(\zeta_{2}\rho)\rho\,d\rho=\frac{1}{2p^{2}}e^{-\frac{\zeta_{1}^{2}+\zeta_{2}^{2}}{4p^{2}}}I_{\nu}\left(\frac{\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}}{2p^{2}}\right),\quad\re(\nu)>-1,\,|\argg\,p|<\frac{\pi}{4},

which yields (
6.10
).
∎

Proposition 6.4
.

In the limit
s\to 1
,

G_{1}^{\ddagger}(r,r_{2})=\tfrac{1}{2}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{4}}r^{\frac{n}{2}-1}r_{2}^{\frac{n}{2}}\delta_{r=r_{2}}

as a function of
r
.
This shows, in particular, that one recovers the usual scalar product in
L^{2}_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n},e^{-|x|^{2}/4})
from the expression (
6.8
).

Proof.

Set
s=1
.
Let us integrate with respect to the variable
d\zeta_{1}
in formula (
6.9
). For this, note that

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\zeta_{1}^{2}}I_{\frac{n-2}{2}}\left(2\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}\right)J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\zeta_{1}r_{1})\zeta_{1}\,d\zeta_{1}=\tfrac{1}{2}e^{\zeta_{2}^{2}}e^{-\frac{r_{1}^{2}}{4}}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\zeta_{2}r_{1}).

The above formula follows from (
6.11
), just taking into account that
I_{\alpha}(z)=i^{\alpha}J_{\alpha}(iz)
. Substituting into
(
6.9
) we obtain

G^{\ddagger}_{s}(r_{1},r_{2})=\tfrac{1}{2}r_{1}^{\frac{n}{2}}r_{2}^{\frac{n}{2}}e^{-\frac{r_{1}^{2}}{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\zeta_{2}r_{2})J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(\zeta_{2}r_{1})\zeta_{2}\,d\zeta_{2}.

The result follows by the orthogonality property of the Bessel functions, which is

\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{\alpha}(\zeta r_{2})J_{\alpha}(\zeta r_{1})\zeta\,d\zeta=\tfrac{1}{r_{1}}\delta_{r_{1}=r_{2}}.

∎

We can give the following (not-sharp) estimate which tells us that
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\ddagger}
can be related to more standard Sobolev spaces:

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
h\in|\cdot|^{2}L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.
Then

\|w\|^{2}_{{\ddagger}}\leq C\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{s-1+n-ns}\frac{1}{h(z^{s})}\widehat{w}^{2}(z)\,dz.

A typical choice for
h
is
h(\zeta)=\zeta^{1+\varepsilon}(1+\zeta)^{-2\varepsilon}
, for some
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.

From (
6.6
) and the discussion in Proposition
6.3
we also have

\langle w_{1},w_{2}\rangle_{\ddagger}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{K}_{s}(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})\zeta_{1}^{\frac{n}{s}-n}\widehat{w}_{1}(\zeta_{1}^{\frac{1}{s}})\zeta_{2}^{\frac{n}{s}-n}\widehat{w}_{2}(\zeta_{2}^{\frac{1}{s}})\,d\zeta_{1}\,d\zeta_{2}.

Let us introduce the auxiliary function
h
in the statement of the Lemma and use Cauchy-Schwarz to estimate

(6.12)

\begin{split}\|w\|^{2}_{\ddagger}&\leq\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{K}_{s}^{2}(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})h(\zeta_{1})h(\zeta_{2})\,d\zeta_{1}\,d\zeta_{2}\right)^{1/2}\\
&\quad\cdot\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{h(\zeta_{1})h(\zeta_{2})}\left[\zeta_{1}^{\frac{n}{s}-n}\widehat{w}(\zeta_{1}^{\frac{1}{s}})\zeta_{2}^{\frac{n}{s}-n}\widehat{w}(\zeta_{2}^{\frac{1}{s}})\right]^{2}\,d\zeta_{1}\,d\zeta_{2}\right)^{1/2}.\end{split}

Taking into account that

I_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(z)\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{z}}e^{z}\quad\text{as}\quad z\to\infty,

while

I_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(z)\sim z^{\frac{n-2}{2}}\quad\text{as}\quad z\to 0,

using formula (
6.10
) we can show that
\mathcal{K}_{s}^{2}(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})\leq C(\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2})^{-2}
, thus

\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{K}_{s}^{2}(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2})h(\zeta_{1})h(\zeta_{2})\,d\zeta_{1}\,d\zeta_{2}\right)\leq C.

We conclude from (
6.12
) that

\|w\|_{{\ddagger}}^{2}\leq C\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{h^{2}(\zeta)}\left[\widehat{w}(\zeta^{\frac{1}{s}})\zeta^{\frac{n}{s}-n}\right]^{2}\,d\zeta\right).

Changing variable
z=\zeta^{\frac{1}{s}}
we arrive to the desired conclusion.
∎

6.3.
Duality

We define the duality pairing of
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\dagger}
and
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\ddagger}
by

(6.13)

(u,w)_{*}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi_{s}^{-1}u(r)(\Phi_{s}^{*})^{-1}w(r)\,dr.

We will show that this pairing is just the usual
L_{r}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
product (up to multiplicative constant):

Proposition 6.6
.

Suppose
u
and
w
are functions such that
\mathcal{M}u(z)
and
\mathcal{M}w(n-z)
are simultaneously well-defined in a strip around
\re z=\sigma
. Then

(u,w)_{*}=\frac{2^{\frac{n}{s}-n}}{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}uw\,dx.

Proof.

By the isometry property (
3.14
) of the Mellin transform,

\begin{split}(u,w)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}&=\int_{0}^{\infty}uwr^{n-1}\,dr\\
&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{M}u(\sigma+\lambda i)\mathcal{M}w(n-\sigma-\lambda i)\,d\lambda=-i\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}\mathcal{M}u(z)\mathcal{M}w(n-z)\,dz.\end{split}

We observe from (
4.6
) and (
6.2
) that, for any
z=\sigma+i\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, it holds that

\Lambda_{s}^{*}(n-z)\Lambda_{s}(z)=2^{n-\frac{n}{s}},

so that we can write

\begin{split}2^{-n+\frac{n}{s}}(u,w)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}&=-i\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}\Lambda_{s}(z)^{-1}\mathcal{M}u(z)\cdot\Lambda_{s}^{*}(n-z)^{-1}\mathcal{M}w(n-z)\,dz\\
&=-i\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}\mathcal{M}\left\{\Phi_{s}^{-1}u\right\}(z)\cdot\mathcal{M}\left\{(\Phi_{s}^{*})^{-1}w\right\}(n-z)\,dz\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi_{s}^{-1}u(r)(\Phi_{s}^{*})^{-1}w(r)\,dr,\end{split}

as desired.
∎

As a consequence, one obtains that the eigenfunctions for (
6.1
) are those dual to the eigenfunctions from Theorem
1.1
. In particular, this recover our formal guess (
6.4
).

Corollary 6.7
.

The eigenfunctions for (
6.1
) in the space
\mathds{L}^{2}_{\ddagger}
are given by

\omega_{k}^{(s)}(r)=\Phi^{*}_{s}\mathfrak{L}_{k}(r),\quad k\in\mathbb{N},

with eigenvalue
\nu_{k}=k
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, we have found two dual basis of eigenfunctions: for
j\neq k
,

(e_{j},\omega_{k})_{*}=0.

Proof.

Note that, using (
5.7
), (
6.5
), (
6.13
) and (
A.3
),

\begin{split}(e_{j},\omega_{k})_{*}&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}_{j}(r)\cdot\mathfrak{L}_{k}(r)\,dr\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}2s\,j!\left(\tfrac{r^{2s}}{4}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n}{2s}}e^{-\frac{r^{2s}}{4}}L_{j}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}\left(\tfrac{r^{2s}}{4}\right)\cdot\frac{2s}{\Gamma(k+\frac{n}{2})}\,L_{k}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}(\tfrac{r^{2s}}{4})\,r^{n-1}\,dr\\
&=\frac{2s\,j!}{\Gamma(k+\frac{n}{2})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n}{2s}}e^{-\rho}L_{j}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}(\rho)\,L_{k}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}(\rho)(4\rho)^{\frac{n}{2s}}\frac{d\rho}{\rho}\\
&=\frac{2s\,2^{\frac{n}{s}}}{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}\delta_{j,k}.\end{split}

∎

We finish with a quick calculation:

Lemma 6.8
.

Any two eigenfunctions
\omega_{k}
,
\omega_{j}
,
k\neq j
,
are orthogonal with respect to
\left\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\right\rangle_{{\ddagger}}

Proof.

Given
j,k
non-negative integers:

\begin{split}\langle\omega_{j},\omega_{k}\rangle_{{\ddagger}}&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathfrak{L}_{j}(r)\mathfrak{L}_{k}(r)\,e^{-\frac{r^{2s}}{4}}r^{sn-1}\,dr=\int_{0}^{\infty}L_{j}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}(\tfrac{r^{2s}}{4})L_{k}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}(\tfrac{r^{2s}}{4})\,e^{-\frac{r^{2s}}{4}}r^{sn-1}\,dr\\

by the orthogonality (

)
of the Laguerre polynomials

with respect to the weight

∎

Appendix A
Hermite and Laguerre polynomials

“Probabilist’s” Hermite polynomials are defined by

He_{k}(x)=(-1)^{k}e^{\frac{1}{2}x^{2}}\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}\left(e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}}\right).

They form an orthogonal basis of the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}})
. It is well known that they are the eigenfunctions of the equation

u^{\prime\prime}(x)-xu^{\prime}(x)=-\lambda_{k}u(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

with eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}:=k
,
k=0,1,2,\ldots
In contrast, “Physicist’s” Hermite polynomials are given by

H_{k}(x)=(-1)^{k}e^{x^{2}}\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}e^{-x^{2}}.

Here we will use a different normalization, given by

H{e_{k}}^{[\alpha]}(x)=\alpha^{\frac{k}{2}}He_{k}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)=\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{\frac{k}{2}}H_{k}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2\alpha}}\right).

For
\alpha=2
, these will be denoted by
\mathcal{H}_{k}(x)
, and they can be obtained by Rodrigues’ formula

\mathcal{H}_{k}(x)=(-1)^{k}2^{k}e^{\frac{1}{4}x^{2}}\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}\left(e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{4}}\right).

We remark that the
\mathcal{H}_{k}(x)
are an orthogonal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{4}})
.
We are interested in this particular normalization because the
\mathcal{H}(x)
are
the eigenfunctions of

u^{\prime\prime}(x)-\frac{1}{2}xu^{\prime}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\lambda u(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

with eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}:=k
,
k=0,1,2,\ldots
Note also that if we are interested in even eigenfunctions, functions, we need to restrict to the even integers.

Hermite polynomials are related to Laguerre polynomials, by the formulas

(A.1)

\begin{split}H_{2k}(x)&=(-1)^{k}2^{2k}k!L_{k}^{(-1/2)}(x^{2}),\\
H_{2k+1}(x)&=(-1)^{k}2^{2k+1}k!xL_{k}^{(1/2)}(x^{2}),\end{split}

where

L_{k}^{(\alpha)}
are the Laguerre polynomials, defined by

(A.2)

L_{k}^{(\alpha)}(x)=\frac{\Gamma(k+\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(k+1)\Gamma(\alpha+1)}M(-k,\alpha+1;x),

and
M
is Kummer’s (confluent hypergeometric) function. An important property of the Laguerre polynomials is that they satisfy the orthogonality relation

(A.3)

\int_{0}^{\infty}L_{k}^{(\alpha)}(x)L_{m}^{(\alpha)}(x)e^{-x}x^{\alpha}\,dx=\frac{\Gamma(k+\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(k+1)\Gamma(\alpha+1)}\delta_{m,k}.

In addition, they are a complete basis of the corresponding
L^{2}(0,\infty)
space.

We will also need the following integration formula from
[
22
, 6.643 4.]

\int_{0}^{\infty}\zeta^{k+\frac{n-2}{4}}e^{-\alpha\zeta}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(r\sqrt{\zeta})\,d\zeta=k!\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\frac{n-2}{2}}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{4\alpha}}\alpha^{-k-\frac{n-2}{2}-1}L_{k}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}\left(\frac{r^{2}}{4\alpha}\right),

valid for
k+\frac{n-2}{2}>-1
. In particular,

(A.4)

r^{-\frac{n-2}{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\zeta^{2k+\frac{n}{2}}e^{-\zeta^{2}}J_{\frac{n-2}{2}}(r\zeta)\,d\zeta=2^{-\frac{n}{2}}k!e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{4}}L_{k}^{(\frac{n-2}{2})}\left(\frac{r^{2}}{4}\right).

Appendix B
Asymptotics of the Gamma function

Using the asymptotics

\begin{split}\log\Gamma(z+\vartheta)&=\bigl(z+\vartheta-\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)\log(z+\vartheta)-(z+\vartheta)+\tfrac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)+O(|z|^{-1})\\
&=\big(z+\vartheta-\tfrac{1}{2}\big)\log z-z+\tfrac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)+O(|z|^{-1}),\end{split}

we find, as
\left|z\right|\rightarrow\infty
,

(B.1)

\Gamma(z+\vartheta)=\sqrt{2\pi}z^{z+\vartheta-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-z}e^{O(|z|^{-1})},

which is known as Stirling’s approximation for the Gamma function.

In particular, if
\vartheta
is real and
z=\frac{\lambda}{2}i
, then

\begin{split}\Gamma\left(\tfrac{\lambda}{2}i+\vartheta\right)&=\sqrt{2\pi}\left(\tfrac{\left|\lambda\right|}{2}e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}\sign\lambda}\right)^{i\frac{\lambda}{2}+\vartheta-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-i\frac{\lambda}{2}}e^{O(\left|\lambda\right|^{-1})}\\
&=\sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-\frac{\pi}{4}\left|\lambda\right|}\left(\tfrac{\left|\lambda\right|}{2}\right)^{\vartheta-\frac{1}{2}}e^{i\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\log\frac{|\lambda|}{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2}+(\vartheta-\tfrac{1}{2})\frac{\pi}{2}\sign\lambda\right)}\bigl(1+O(\left|\lambda\right|^{-1})\bigr),\end{split}

and

(B.2)

\begin{split}\left\lvert{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{\lambda}{2}i+\vartheta\right)}\right\rvert&=\sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-\frac{\pi}{4}\left|\lambda\right|}\left(\tfrac{\left|\lambda\right|}{2}\right)^{\vartheta-\frac{1}{2}}\bigl(1+O(\left|\lambda\right|^{-1})\bigr).\end{split}

Replacing
\lambda
by
\lambda/s
,

(B.3)

\begin{split}\left\lvert{\Gamma\left(\tfrac{\lambda}{2s}i+\vartheta\right)}\right\rvert&=\sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-\frac{\pi}{4s}\left|\lambda\right|}\left(\tfrac{\left|\lambda\right|}{2s}\right)^{\vartheta-\frac{1}{2}}\bigl(1+O(\left|\lambda\right|^{-1})\bigr).\end{split}
