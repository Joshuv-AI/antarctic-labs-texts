Leave-One-Out Neighborhood Smoothing for Graphons: Berry-Esseen Bounds, Confidence Intervals, and Honest Tuning
Behzad Aalipur, Rachel Kilby

Abstract
Estimating latent edge probabilities in network models often relies on aggregating information from structurally similar nodes. However, using the same adjacency matrix to both determine node similarities and estimate probabilities induces complex statistical dependencies. This coupling invalidates standard inferential tools, such as concentration inequalities and central limit theorems, leaving a critical gap in entrywise uncertainty quantification for networks. To resolve this, we introduce a leave-one-out (LOO) decoupling strategy. When predicting a target edge, the local neighborhood is constructed from a reduced adjacency matrix with the target row and column entirely removed. This structural modification-rather than the specific smoothing mechanism-is the core innovation: it restores exact conditional independence between the data-driven neighborhood selection and the observed edges being aggregated. Leveraging this restored independence, we derive variance-adaptive empirical Bernstein bounds and establish uniform Berry-Esseen normal approximations with explicit rates. These results enable finite-sample, variance-adaptive confidence intervals for the localized neighborhood mean and entrywise uncertainty quantification for P i ​ j P_{ij} on a high-probability interior (good) set up to an explicit bias term. We also establish a uniform Berry–Esseen approximation for the variance component (normal‑approximation intervals use a heuristic variance plug‑in in this version). Furthermore, the LOO architecture naturally admits an honest cross-validation scheme for tuning parameter selection, providing a complete and rigorous inferential framework while preserving strong mean-squared error performance.

Introduction

Graphon models have emerged as the canonical nonparametric framework for analyzing complex network data, providing a flexible representation of latent edge probabilities through a measurable kernel on the unit square
(
5
;
12
)
. Over the past decade, a rich literature has developed around estimating the underlying probability matrix
P
or the graphon
f
from a single observed adjacency matrix
A
. Early foundational work focused heavily on the Stochastic Block Model (SBM) and its variants, utilizing modularity maximization, maximum likelihood, and spectral clustering to recover block probabilities
(
4
;
1
;
15
)
. As the field moved toward continuous, nonparametric graphons, modern estimators-including universal singular value decomposition
(
7
)
, sorting-based smoothing
(
6
)
, and block-approximations
(
8
)
-have been proven to reliably achieve minimax-optimal rates for point estimation.

Among these, the neighborhood smoothing method introduced by
18
stands out. By adaptively aggregating structurally similar nodes based on empirical two-hop distances, neighborhood smoothing achieves optimal mean-squared error (MSE) without requiring the strict structural assumptions of block models or the selection of a global low-rank embedding.

However, a fundamental gap remains: the transition from optimal point estimation to rigorous statistical inference. Given an entrywise estimate
\widehat{P}_{ij}
, it is critically important for downstream scientific tasks to attach a valid measure of uncertainty to this prediction. Yet, existing inferential theory for networks has focused almost exclusively on low-dimensional or global functionals. Significant progress has been made in proving limit theorems for spectral embeddings
(
17
;
2
)
, asymptotic normality for community parameters in SBMs
(
3
)
, and global goodness-of-fit testing
(
10
)
. Conversely, entrywise uncertainty quantification-specifically, constructing valid confidence intervals and proving central limit theorems for individual edge probabilities under continuous graphons-has remained theoretically elusive. Recent attempts to bridge this gap have relied on computationally intensive resampling methods, such as network bootstrapping
(
11
;
9
)
, or generalized fiducial inference
(
16
)
, but exact distribution-free analytical bounds for neighborhood estimators are lacking.

The obstacle preventing entrywise inference is not merely technical, but structural. To achieve optimal MSE, neighborhood smoothing estimators
(
18
)
rely on a data-driven aggregation of similar nodes. This creates a severe “double-dipping” problem: the procedure reuses the adjacency matrix twice. The matrix
A
is first used to compute pairwise topological distances to select a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{i}
, and then the very same matrix
A
is used to average the edges within that neighborhood. Consequently, the random neighborhood weights depend directly on the edges being averaged. Even after conditioning on the latent probability matrix
P
, the summands in the estimator are strongly coupled. This intractable dependence invalidates the foundational assumptions of classical concentration inequalities and Berry-Esseen normal approximations.

In this paper, we demonstrate that this probabilistic bottleneck can be cleanly bypassed via a simple but highly principled structural modification:
leave-one-out (LOO) neighborhood smoothing
.

When estimating a single entry
P_{ij}
, we perform a topological surgery on the graph by constructing the neighborhood of node
i
using a reduced adjacency matrix
A^{(-j)}
in which the
j
-th row and column have been entirely deleted. All pairwise distances and nearest-neighbor selections are computed on this reduced matrix. Only after the neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}
is fixed do we average the original target edges
\{A_{kj}:k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}\}
.

This decoupling is the central theoretical lever of the paper. By strictly separating the data used for neighborhood selection from the data used for estimation, we restore exact conditional independence among the centered summands. This topological decoupling permits the application of classical empirical process tools to graphon neighborhood smoothing. Specifically, under standard piecewise Lipschitz assumptions on the graphon, we leverage this restored independence to establish three main contributions:

1.

Variance-Adaptive Concentration:
We derive tight, finite-sample concentration inequalities based on empirical Bernstein bounds that automatically adapt to the local variance of the edge probabilities.

2.

Asymptotic Normality:
We establish a uniform Berry–Esseen bound with explicit rates for the normalized variance component; normal‑approximation intervals in this version use a heuristic variance estimate and incur an additional variance‑estimation error term.

3.

Honest Cross-Validation:
The LOO structure naturally supports an unbiased cross-validation scheme for tuning the neighborhood size
h_{n}
.inequality. Here, honest cross-validation means that it does not reuse held-out edges in predictor construction, ensuring valid inference even after data-driven tuning.

Crucially, these inferential guarantees preserve the optimal rowwise
mean-squared error rate of classical neighborhood smoothing.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
formalizes the graphon model, the Zhang-Levina-Zhu (ZLZ),
18
empirical distance, and our leave-one-out neighborhood construction. Section
3.2
establishes the foundational conditional independence, proving the variance-adaptive concentration bounds, Berry-Esseen normal approximations, and the validity of the resulting confidence intervals. Section
4
details the honest cross-validation scheme for tuning parameter selection. Section
5
reports comprehensive simulation results illustrating the finite-sample coverage and MSE performance, and Section
6
concludes with a discussion of extensions to directed, weighted, and degree-corrected settings.

Model and Leave-One-Out Neighborhood Smoothing

We observe a single undirected simple graph on
n
vertices with adjacency
matrix
A\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
, no self‑loops (
A_{ii}=0
), and
independent upper‑triangular entries

A_{ij}\mid P_{ij}\ \sim\ \mathrm{Bernoulli}(P_{ij}),\qquad 1\leq i<j\leq n,

(2.1)

with
A_{ij}=A_{ji}
. The matrix
P=(P_{ij})
contains unknown edge
probabilities. We adopt the standard graphon parametrization below.

Assumption 2.1

(Graphon model and smoothness)
.

The edge probabilities satisfy
P_{ij}=f(\xi_{i},\xi_{j})
with

\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
.
The graphon
f:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]
is piecewise Lipschitz in each argument
with Lipschitz constant
L
on a fixed finite rectangular partition of

[0,1]^{2}
.

Assumption 2.2

(Non‑degeneracy)
.

There exists
\eta\in(0,1/2)
such that

\eta\leq f(u,v)\leq 1-\eta\qquad\text{for all }(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2}.

Assumption
2.2
places the model in the dense graphon
regime, where edge probabilities are uniformly bounded away from
zero and one. This excludes sparse scalings of the form

P_{ij}=\rho_{n}W(\xi_{i},\xi_{j})
with
\rho_{n}\to 0
.
Our Berry-Esseen and empirical Bernstein arguments rely critically
on variance bounded away from zero.
Extending the present theory to sparse or degree-corrected
regimes would require variance renormalization and additional
control of small-probability events, and is left for future work.

Remark 2.3
.

Assumption
2.2
requires that there exists
\eta\in(0,1/2)
such that

\eta\leq f(u,v)\leq 1-\eta
for all
(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2}
. This nondegeneracy
condition guarantees a uniformly nonvanishing conditional variance and is used to apply Berry-Esseen and variance‑adaptive concentration bounds. It excludes extremely sparse regimes where
P_{ij}\to 0
uniformly; extending our results to
such regimes requires variance re‑normalization and additional control of rare events, which we leave to future work. Empirically, moderate thinning of edges does not invalidate the procedure (see Section
5
).

Remark 2.4
.

The rectangular partition in Assumption
2.1
is fixed and does
not grow with
n
. All constants in our rates may depend on the Lipschitz
constant
L
and on the partition, but not on
n
.

Remark 2.5

(Dense regime convention)
.

For concreteness we work in the canonical (dense) graphon regime

P_{ij}=f(\xi_{i},\xi_{j})
with
f
fixed as in
Assumption
2.1
. In this regime the expected degree satisfies

\mathbb{E}\deg(i)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_{ij}\asymp n
, so local neighborhoods
contain many informative two‑hop paths and several concentration steps
simplify. All main results below are stated under
Assumptions

and
2.2
together with this dense

P_{ij}=\rho_{n}f(\xi_{i},\xi_{j})
are discussed briefly in Section
6
.

2.1
LOO neighborhoods and estimator

Consider the set of nodes
V=\{1,\dots,n\}
. Fix
j\in[n]
and let

A^{(-j)}
be the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
adjacency matrix obtained by deleting
row and column
j
from
A
. Define the two-hop matrices

M:=\frac{1}{n}A^{2},\qquad M^{(-j)}:=\frac{1}{n-1}\big(A^{(-j)}\big)^{2}.

For distinct
i,k\in V\setminus\{j\}
, define the leave-one-out (LOO)-distance

d^{(-j)}(i,k):=\frac{1}{n-1}\max_{\ell\in V\setminus\{i,k,j\}}\bigl|M^{(-j)}_{i\ell}-M^{(-j)}_{k\ell}\bigr|.

(2.2)

Let

d^{(-j)}_{i,(1)}\leq\cdots\leq d^{(-j)}_{i,(n-2)}

denote the order statistics of the set

\bigl\{d^{(-j)}(i,k):k\in V\setminus\{i,j\}\bigr\}.

Define the leave-one-out neighborhood of node
i
by

\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}=\left\{k\neq i,j:d_{ik}^{(-j)}\leq d_{i,(h_{n})}^{(-j)}\right\},

(2.3)

where
h_{n-1}
is the ZLZ bandwidth parameter with
n
replaced by
n-1
.
that is, the
h_{n}
nearest neighbors of
i
according to the
ZLZ two-hop distance computed on the reduced graph.

Given
\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}
, define the one‑sided LOO smoother and its
symmetrized version by

\tilde{P}_{ij}:=\frac{1}{h_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}A_{kj},\qquad\widehat{P}_{ij}:=\frac{\tilde{P}_{ij}+\tilde{P}_{ji}}{2}.

(2.4)

This construction preserves the original ZLZ neighborhood rule, with the sole modification that node
j
is removed prior to
distance computation.

The classical neighborhood smoother of
18
uses

\mathcal{N}_{i}
computed from the full adjacency matrix
A
to estimate the
entire
i
th row of
P
.
Define the distance function
d:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
by

d_{ZLZ}(i,j):=\begin{cases}0,&\text{for }i=j,\\
\max_{k\neq i,j}\Big|\big(A^{2}/n\big)_{ik}-\big(A^{2}/n\big)_{jk}\Big|,&\text{for }i\neq j.\end{cases}

(2.5)

This is the sup‑norm discrepancy between the
i
th and
j
th rows of the normalized two‑hop matrix
A^{2}/n
, measuring the similarity of their
two‑hop neighborhoods.
Therefore the ZLZ neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{i}
consists of the
h_{n}
nodes with the smallest ZLZ distance to
i
.
18
defined

\mathcal{N}_{i}=\{k\in V\setminus\{i\}:d_{ZLZ}(i,k)\leq q_{h}(\{D\}),\quad D=\{d_{ZLZ}(i,k):k\in V\setminus\{i\}\}\},

(2.6)

and
q_{h}(\{D\})
is the
h_{n}
-th order statistic of the set
\{d_{ZLZ}(i,k):k\in V\setminus\{i\}\}
. The ZLZ estimator is then defined by

\widetilde{P}^{\mathrm{ZLZ}}_{ij}:=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}_{i}|}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}A_{kj}.

Because
\mathcal{N}_{i}
depends on all columns of

A
, including column
j
, the weights used to average

\{A_{kj}:k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}\}
depend on the very summands being averaged. Hence
the centered terms
A_{kj}-P_{kj}
are not independent conditional on the
neighborhoods, preventing the direct application of variance‑adaptive
concentration or Berry-Esseen theory.

Remark 2.6
.

The symmetrized estimator
\widehat{P}_{ij}
respects the symmetry of the
simple graph model. All concentration and normal‑approximation results are
derived for the one‑sided quantities
\tilde{P}_{ij}
; symmetrization does
not affect the independence structure.

Proposition 2.7

(Conditional independence)
.

Conditional on
\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}
and
P
, the variables

\{A_{kj}-P_{kj}:k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}\}

are independent, mean-zero, and bounded in
[-1,1]
.

The leave-one-out construction does not alter the smoothing
mechanism itself; it alters only the dependency structure.
By removing the target column prior to neighborhood selection,
the summands in the neighborhood average become exactly
independent conditional on
P
.
This structural decoupling is the key step that allows
classical empirical process tools to be applied
without resampling or sample splitting.

Proof.

Condition on the latent positions
(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})
so that
P
is deterministic with

P_{kl}=f(\xi_{k},\xi_{\ell})
. Under the model (
2.1
), the edges

\{A_{k\ell}:1\leq k<\ell\leq n\}
are independent Bernoulli random variables with parameters

P_{k\ell}
.

By construction,
A^{(-j)}
is obtained from
A
by removing the
j
th row and column.
Hence
A^{(-j)}
is a measurable function of
\{A_{k\ell}:k\neq j,\ell\neq j\}
, and so is

\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}
. Therefore, conditional on
P
, the random set
\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}
is independent
of the collection
\{A_{kj}:k\neq j\}
.

For each
k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}
, we have
A_{kj}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(P_{kj})
and

A_{kj}-P_{kj}\in[-1,1]
with mean zero. Since the edges
\{A_{kj}:k\neq j\}
are independent conditional on
P
, and
\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}
is independent of these edges, it follows that the collection
\{A_{kj}-P_{kj}:k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}\}
is independent, mean-zero, and bounded in

[-1,1]
conditional on
(\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)},P)
.
∎

3
Consistency

Our objective is entrywise inference for the finite-sample edge
probability matrix
P=(P_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
,
where
P_{ij}=W(\xi_{i},\xi_{j})
.
All probability statements in Sections 3 and 4 are conditional on
the realized latent positions
(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})
,
equivalently on
P
.
Thus our results provide conditional finite-sample and asymptotic
inference for
P_{ij}
rather than for the graphon
W
itself.
In this section we quantify the deviation of the LOO estimator

\tilde{P}_{ij}
from the target
P_{ij}
. The analysis separates the
estimation error into a
variance
component, arising from the
randomness of observed edges given
P
, and a
bias
component,
arising from approximating
P_{ij}
by a local average over a
neighborhood of
i
. We begin with a decomposition that will be used throughout the section. For
i\neq j
write
For
i\neq j
, write

\Delta_{ij}:=\tilde{P}_{ij}-P_{ij}=U_{ij}^{(-j)}+B_{ij}^{(-j)},

(3.1)

where

\displaystyle U_{ij}^{(-j)}

\displaystyle:=\tilde{P}_{ij}-\frac{1}{h_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}P_{kj}=\frac{1}{h_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}(A_{kj}-P_{kj}),

(3.2)

\displaystyle B_{ij}^{(-j)}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{h_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}P_{kj}-P_{ij}.

(3.3)

By Proposition
2.7
, conditional on

\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}
and
P
the summands in
U_{ij}^{(-j)}
are independent,
mean zero and bounded, so
U_{ij}^{(-j)}
behaves like an average of
h_{n}

independent observations. The term
B_{ij}^{(-j)}
is a deterministic (given

P
and
\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}
) smoothness bias that can be controlled
using Assumption
2.1
and the neighborhood properties in
Section
2
. The following two subsections analyze these two components separately.

3.1
Neighborhood bias

The bias
B_{ij}
captures the approximation error from replacing

P_{ij}
by the local average of
P_{kj}
over the LOO neighborhood.
Under the piecewise Lipschitz graphon assumption and the ZLZ neighborhood
construction, neighbors in
\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}
are close in latent
position to
\xi_{i}
, which yields a small bias.

Lemma 3.1

(Uniform two-hop stability under deletion of one vertex)
.

Let
A\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
be the adjacency matrix of an undirected simple
graph with
A_{ii}=0
. For each
j\in[n]
, let
A^{(-j)}
be the

(n-1)\times(n-1)
adjacency matrix obtained by deleting the
j
th row and
column. Define the normalized two-hop matrices

M:=\frac{1}{n}A^{2},\qquad M^{(-j)}:=\frac{1}{n-1}\big(A^{(-j)}\big)^{2}.

To compare
M
and
M^{(-j)}
, embed
M^{(-j)}
into an
n\times n
matrix

M^{(-j)}_{\mathrm{embed}}
by

M^{(-j)}_{\mathrm{embed}}(i,\ell)=\begin{cases}M^{(-j)}_{i\ell},&i,\ell\neq j,\\
M_{i\ell},&\text{if }i=j\text{ or }\ell=j.\end{cases}

Thus
M^{(-j)}_{\mathrm{embed}}
and
M
have the same dimensions and agree
on the
j
th row and column.

Then, deterministically,

\sup_{1\leq j\leq n}\ \|M-M^{(-j)}_{\mathrm{embed}}\|_{\max}\;\leq\;\frac{2}{n},

where
\|B\|_{\max}:=\max_{1\leq i,\ell\leq n}|B_{i\ell}|
. In particular, for all

j\in[n]
and all
i,\ell\in[n]\setminus\{j\}
,

|M_{i\ell}-M^{(-j)}_{i\ell}|\leq\frac{2}{n}.

Proof.

Fix
j\in[n]
and
i,\ell\in[n]\setminus\{j\}
. Expanding the
(i,\ell)
entry gives

(A^{2})_{i\ell}=\sum_{m=1}^{n}A_{im}A_{m\ell},\qquad\big(A^{(-j)}\big)^{2}_{i\ell}=\sum_{m\neq j}A_{im}A_{m\ell},

hence

(A^{2})_{i\ell}-\big(A^{(-j)}\big)^{2}_{i\ell}=A_{ij}A_{j\ell},

so
\big|(A^{2})_{i\ell}-\big(A^{(-j)}\big)^{2}_{i\ell}\big|\leq 1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\big|M_{i\ell}-M^{(-j)}_{i\ell}\big|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{n}(A^{2})_{i\ell}-\frac{1}{n-1}\big(A^{(-j)}\big)^{2}_{i\ell}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{1}{n}(A^{2})_{i\ell}-\frac{1}{n}\big(A^{(-j)}\big)^{2}_{i\ell}\right|+\left|\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n-1}\right|\big(A^{(-j)}\big)^{2}_{i\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\big(A^{(-j)}\big)^{2}_{i\ell}.

Since
\big(A^{(-j)}\big)^{2}_{i\ell}=\sum_{m\neq j}A_{im}A_{m\ell}\leq n-1
, we get

\big|M_{i\ell}-M^{(-j)}_{i\ell}\big|\leq\frac{1}{n}+\frac{n-1}{n(n-1)}=\frac{2}{n}.

For
i=j
or
\ell=j
, we have
M^{(-j)}_{\mathrm{embed}}(i,\ell)=M_{i\ell}
by
definition, so the difference is zero. Hence

\|M-M^{(-j)}_{\mathrm{embed}}\|_{\max}\leq\frac{2}{n}

for each
j
, and taking the supremum over
j
preserves the bound.
∎

Lemma 3.2

(Stability of expected two-hop matrices under deletion)
.

Let
A
and
A^{(-j)}
be as in Lemma
3.1
, and

for a universal constant

Proof.

Fix
j\in[n]
. Let
M^{(-j)}_{\mathrm{embed}}
and
M^{(-j)\star}_{\mathrm{embed}}

be the

Taking the supremum over

).

Corollary 3.4

(Leave-one-out ZLZ row-wise risk and

Fix

\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{\ell\neq j}\big(\widetilde{P}_{i\ell}-P_{i\ell}\big)^{2}\;\lesssim\;\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{n}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

Identifying ZLZ’s notation via

J_{1}^{(-j)}(i,\ell):=\big(U^{(-j)}_{i\ell}\big)^{2},\qquad J_{2}^{(-j)}(i,\ell):=\big(B^{(-j)}_{i\ell}\big)^{2},

the ZLZ bounds (with
(n-1)
in place of
n
) give

\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{\ell\neq j}J_{1}^{(-j)}(i,\ell)\;\lesssim\;\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{n}\Bigr)^{1/2},\qquad\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{\ell\neq j}J_{2}^{(-j)}(i,\ell)\;\lesssim\;\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{n}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

Proof.

The decomposition

\widetilde{P}_{i\ell}-P_{i\ell}=\frac{1}{|N_{i}^{(-j)}|}\sum_{k\in N_{i}^{(-j)}}\bigl[(A_{k\ell}-P_{k\ell})+(P_{k\ell}-P_{i\ell})\bigr]

is identical to ZLZ equation (1), and the inequality

(\widetilde{P}_{i\ell}-P_{i\ell})^{2}\leq 2J_{1}^{(-j)}(i,\ell)+2J_{2}^{(-j)}(i,\ell)

follows exactly as in their argument, with the identifications

J_{1}^{(-j)}(i,\ell)=(U^{(-j)}_{i\ell})^{2}
and

J_{2}^{(-j)}(i,\ell)=(B^{(-j)}_{i\ell})^{2}
.
Next, ZLZ’s control of two-hop quantities

\frac{1}{n}(A^{2})_{ab}-\frac{1}{n}(P^{2})_{ab}

is established in equations (4)-(6) of their supplementary material.
Lemma
3.1
shows that the same bounds
hold when
A
is replaced by
A^{(-j)}
(and
n
by
n-1
). Therefore,
all subsequent ZLZ steps that rely on these concentration inputs remain
valid for the leave-one-out graph.

ZLZ bound the row-average of
J_{1}
using their decomposition (11) and
bounds (12), (14), and (15). The same argument applies verbatim to

A^{(-j)}
and
N_{i}^{(-j)}
, yielding

\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{\ell\neq j}J_{1}^{(-j)}(i,\ell)\;\lesssim\;\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{n}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

Similarly, ZLZ bound the row-average of
J_{2}
using Cauchy-Schwarz and
their “claim 2 of Lemma 2,” concluding in equation (16). Again,
Lemma
3.1
ensures the same concentration
inputs hold for
A^{(-j)}
, giving

\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{\ell\neq j}J_{2}^{(-j)}(i,\ell)\;\lesssim\;\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{n}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

Combining these bounds yields the stated row-wise risk bound for the
leave-one-out estimator.
∎

Assumption 3.5

(Piecewise bi-Lipschitz smoothness)
.

The graphon
W:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]
admits a finite vertical partition

0=b_{0}<b_{1}<\cdots<b_{K}=1
with blocks
I_{m}:=(b_{m},b_{m+1})
such that:

1.

(Within-block Lipschitz in the first argument.)
For all
m
and all
u,u^{\prime}\in I_{m}
,
x\in[0,1]
,

|W(u,x)-W(u^{\prime},x)|\leq L|u-u^{\prime}|.

2.

(Across-block
L^{2}
separation.)
There exist
\eta,\delta>0
such that for any
u\in I_{m}
and
u^{\prime}\in I_{m^{\prime}}

with
m\neq m^{\prime}
,

\lambda\big(\{x\in[0,1]:|W(u,x)-W(u^{\prime},x)|\geq\eta\}\big)\geq\delta.

3.

(Within-block
L^{2}
identifiability.)
For any
\varepsilon>0
with
b_{m}+\varepsilon<b_{m+1}-\varepsilon
, there
exists
\kappa(\varepsilon)>0
such that for all

u,u^{\prime}\in[b_{m}+\varepsilon,b_{m+1}-\varepsilon]
,

\int_{0}^{1}(W(u,x)-W(u^{\prime},x))^{2}\,dx\;\geq\;\kappa(\varepsilon)\,|u-u^{\prime}|^{2}.

Remark 3.6

(Scope of Assumption
3.5
.)
.

This assumption excludes graphons with flat regions or non-identifiable
row structure, and is tailored to settings where rowwise
L^{2}
proximity
reflects latent proximity within each block. In particular, it does not
cover graphons with a growing number of blocks or highly irregular
multiscale structure. The condition is standard in nonparametric
graphon estimation when entrywise inference is desired, but we
emphasize that it restricts the theory to graphons with fixed,
well-separated smooth components.

Remark 3.7

(Good-node and good-edge sets.)
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
satisfying the feasibility condition

b_{m}+\varepsilon<b_{m+1}-\varepsilon\qquad\text{for all }m=0,\dots,K-1,

so that each interior interval
[b_{m}+\varepsilon,b_{m+1}-\varepsilon]
is nonempty.
Define the block interior

I_{\varepsilon}:=\bigcup_{m=0}^{K-1}[b_{m}+\varepsilon,\;b_{m+1}-\varepsilon].

Let the random good-node set be

\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}:=\{i\in[n]:\ \xi_{i}\in I_{\varepsilon}\},

and the good-edge set

\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}:=\{(i,j):i\neq j,\ i\in\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon},\ j\in\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}\}.

Since
\xi_{i}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
, we have

|\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}|\sim\mathrm{Bin}\big(n,\lambda(I_{\varepsilon})\big)
.
In particular, for any
t>0
Hoeffding’s inequality gives

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|\frac{|\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}|}{n}-\lambda(I_{\varepsilon})\right|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp(-2nt^{2}).

Consequently, for fixed
\varepsilon>0
the fraction of good nodes satisfies

|\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}|/n=\lambda(I_{\varepsilon})+O_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2})
, and if

\lambda(I_{\varepsilon})=1-O(\varepsilon)
then
|\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}|=n(1-O(\varepsilon))

with exponentially high probability.

Lemma 3.8

(Rowwise
L^{2}
closeness implies latent closeness)
.

Assume Assumption
3.5
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
and define

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}=\bigcup_{m=0}^{K-1}[b_{m}+\varepsilon,b_{m+1}-\varepsilon].

Let
U_{i},U_{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
and set
P_{ab}=W(U_{a},U_{b})
.
Let
r_{n}=(\log n/n)^{1/4}
.
There exist constants
c_{0},c_{1}>0
depending only on
Assumption
3.5
and
\varepsilon
such that the following holds.
If

M_{n}\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{n}\Bigr)^{1/2}\leq c_{0},\qquad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(P_{i\ell}-P_{k\ell})^{2}\leq M_{n}\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{n}\Bigr)^{1/2},

then with probability at least
1-n^{-c}
(for some
c>0
) and for all sufficiently large
n
,

U_{i},U_{k}\text{ lie in the same block},\qquad|U_{i}-U_{k}|\leq c_{1}\sqrt{M_{n}}\,r_{n}.

Proof.

Let
f_{i}(x)=W(U_{i},x)
and
f_{k}(x)=W(U_{k},x)
, and define

D(U_{i},U_{k})=\int_{0}^{1}(f_{i}(x)-f_{k}(x))^{2}\,dx.

Concentration of the empirical row distance.

Conditional on
(U_{i},U_{k})
, the variables

Z_{\ell}:=(P_{i\ell}-P_{k\ell})^{2}=(W(U_{i},U_{\ell})-W(U_{k},U_{\ell}))^{2}

are i.i.d. in
\ell
, bounded in
[0,1]
, and satisfy

\mathbb{E}[Z_{\ell}\mid U_{i},U_{k}]=D(U_{i},U_{k})
.
Hoeffding’s inequality gives, for any
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}Z_{\ell}-D(U_{i},U_{k})\right|\geq t\ \middle|\ U_{i},U_{k}\right)\leq 2e^{-2nt^{2}}.

Taking
t=C(\log n/n)^{1/2}
yields that, with probability at least
1-n^{-c}
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(P_{i\ell}-P_{k\ell})^{2}\geq D(U_{i},U_{k})-C\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{n}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

(3.5)

If
U_{i},U_{k}
lie in different blocks, Assumption
3.5
(ii) gives

D(U_{i},U_{k})\geq\eta^{2}\delta
.
Using (
3.5
), for large
n
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(P_{i\ell}-P_{k\ell})^{2}\geq\frac{\eta^{2}\delta}{2}.

Choosing
c_{0}=\eta^{2}\delta/4
makes this incompatible with the assumed upper bound.
Thus
U_{i},U_{k}
must lie in the same block.

On the event of
U_{i},U_{k}
lie in the same block and belong to
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
,
so Assumption
3.5
(iii) implies

D(U_{i},U_{k})\geq\kappa(\varepsilon)|U_{i}-U_{k}|^{2}.

Combining this with (
3.5
) and the assumed upper bound gives

\kappa(\varepsilon)|U_{i}-U_{k}|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(P_{i\ell}-P_{k\ell})^{2}+C\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{n}\Bigr)^{1/2}\leq(M_{n}+C)\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{n}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

Therefore,

|U_{i}-U_{k}|\leq\sqrt{\frac{M_{n}+C}{\kappa(\varepsilon)}}\,\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{n}\Bigr)^{1/4}\leq c_{1}\sqrt{M_{n}}\,r_{n},

after adjusting constants.
∎

Corollary 3.9

(Bias bound on the good set)
.

\varepsilon>0
satisfying the feasibility condition above and suppose the neighborhood regularity condition (display (3.4) in the main text) holds with constant
C_{2}
.
Then there exists a constant

(depending on

and

.

For each

Lemma 3.11

(Bias bound for the LOO neighborhood smoother)
.

Assume Assumption
3.5
and let
r_{n}=(\log n/n)^{1/4}
.
Suppose that, with probability at least
1-n^{-c}
, for all
i\neq j
,

\frac{1}{|N_{i}^{(-j)}|}\sum_{k\in N_{i}^{(-j)}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(P_{i\ell}-P_{k\ell})^{2}\leq C_{2}\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{n}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

(3.6)

Then for all
i\neq j
with
U_{i}\in\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
,

|B^{(-j)}_{ij}|\leq Cr_{n}.

The canonical choice
h_{n}\asymp\sqrt{n\log n}

balances the stochastic fluctuation scale

h_{n}^{-1/2}
and the bias scale

r_{n}=(\log n/n)^{1/4}
.
Undersmoothing (
h_{n}=o(\sqrt{n\log n})
)
reduces bias at the cost of larger variance,
while oversmoothing increases bias.
Thus the canonical scaling is optimal for
first-order mean-squared error and yields
first-order coverage up to the explicit bias cushion.

Proof.

Fix
i\neq j
and write
f_{u}(x)=W(u,x)
.
Let
N=N_{i}^{(-j)}
and define

D(u,u^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{1}(f_{u}-f_{u^{\prime}})^{2}.

First we show that conditional on
(U_{i},U_{k})
, the variables
(P_{i\ell}-P_{k\ell})^{2}
are i.i.d. in
\ell
, bounded in
[0,1]
,
and have mean
D(U_{i},U_{k})
.
Hoeffding’s inequality and a union bound over
k\in N

(using
|N|\leq h_{n}\asymp\sqrt{n\log n}
) give that with probability

1-n^{-c}
,

\sup_{k\in N}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(P_{i\ell}-P_{k\ell})^{2}-D(U_{i},U_{k})\right|\leq C\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{n}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

(3.7)

Combining (
3.6
) and (
3.7
),

\frac{1}{|N|}\sum_{k\in N}D(U_{i},U_{k})\leq C^{\prime}\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{n}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

(3.8)

Now we control the average latent distance between
U_{i}
and its neighbors in
N
.
Let

\mathcal{C}_{i}=\{k\in N:U_{k}\text{ lies in a different block than }U_{i}\}.

Assumption
3.5
(ii) gives
D(U_{i},U_{k})\geq\eta^{2}\delta
for

k\in\mathcal{C}_{i}
. Using (

),

\frac{|\mathcal{C}_{i}|}{|N|}\eta^{2}\delta\leq C^{\prime}\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{n}\Bigr)^{1/2},\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\frac{|\mathcal{C}_{i}|}{|N|}\leq C^{\prime\prime}\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{n}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

Decompose

\frac{1}{|N|}\sum_{k\in N}|U_{i}-U_{k}|^{2}=\frac{1}{|N|}\sum_{k\in N\setminus\mathcal{C}_{i}}|U_{i}-U_{k}|^{2}+\frac{1}{|N|}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}|U_{i}-U_{k}|^{2}.

The second term is at most
|\mathcal{C}_{i}|/|N|
.
For
k\in N\setminus\mathcal{C}_{i}
(same block),
Assumption
3.5
(iii) gives

D(U_{i},U_{k})\geq\kappa(\varepsilon)|U_{i}-U_{k}|^{2}

\frac{1}{|N|}\sum_{k\in N\setminus\mathcal{C}_{i}}|U_{i}-U_{k}|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\kappa(\varepsilon)}\cdot\frac{1}{|N|}\sum_{k\in N}D(U_{i},U_{k}).

Using (
3.8
) and
(\dagger)
,

\frac{1}{|N|}\sum_{k\in N}|U_{i}-U_{k}|^{2}\leq C\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{n}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

(3.9)

Step 3: Bias bound.

Lipschitz continuity in the first argument gives

|P_{kj}-P_{ij}|\leq L|U_{k}-U_{i}|.

Thus

|B^{(-j)}_{ij}|=\left|\frac{1}{|N|}\sum_{k\in N}(P_{kj}-P_{ij})\right|\leq L\sqrt{\frac{1}{|N|}\sum_{k\in N}|U_{i}-U_{k}|^{2}}\leq Cr_{n},

using (
3.9
) and
r_{n}=(\log n/n)^{1/4}
.
∎

Lemma
3.11
establishes a bias bound for the leave-one-out neighborhood smoother, showing that under the piecewise bi-Lipschitz smoothness assumption, the bias term
\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\neq j:\\
(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}\end{subarray}}|B_{ij}^{(-j)}|
is of order
r_{n}=(\log n/n)^{1/4}
for nodes
i
and
j
whose latent positions lie in the interior of the same block. This result relies on the control of the average two-hop distance between node
i
and its neighbors in the leave-one-out neighborhood, as well as on the Lipschitz continuity of the graphon within blocks.

Remark 3.12
.

Under the canonical choice
h_{n}\asymp\sqrt{n\log n}
,
the stochastic fluctuation scale
h_{n}^{-1/2}
and the bias scale

r_{n}=(\log n/n)^{1/4}
are of the same order.
Thus the normal approximation is first-order accurate,
and bias cannot be ignored without undersmoothing.
Choosing a smaller bandwidth would reduce bias at the cost
of increased variance, while larger bandwidths inflate bias.
The canonical scaling balances these two effects.

3.2
Variance Bounds, Berry-Esseen Approximation, and Confidence Intervals

We introduce the conditional variance for
U_{ij}^{(-j)}
:

V_{ij}:=\mathrm{Var}\!\big(U_{ij}^{(-j)}\mid\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)},P\big)=\frac{1}{h_{n}^{2}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}P_{kj}(1-P_{kj}),

(3.10)

so that
V_{ij}\leq 1/(4h)
. While
V_{ij}
is analytically useful and measurable with respect to the

\sigma
-field generated by
(\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)},P)
, it depends on the unknown latent probabilities

\{P_{kj}\}
.

To obtain tight, variance-adaptive control of
U_{ij}^{(-j)}
using only observable quantities, we define the (Bessel-corrected) sample variance of the edges in the leave-one-out neighborhood:

\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{2}:=\frac{1}{h_{n}(h_{n}-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}\\
k<\ell\end{subarray}}(A_{kj}-A_{\ell j})^{2}=\frac{h_{n}}{h_{n}-1}\,\tilde{P}_{ij}(1-\tilde{P}_{ij}),

(3.11)

where
\tilde{P}_{ij}=\frac{1}{h_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}A_{kj}
is the empirical mean.

Instead of relying on classical Bernstein bounds that require the true variance
V_{ij}
,
we utilize the Empirical Bernstein inequality (Theorem 11 of
13
),
which directly incorporates the sample variance
\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{2}
.

For the normal-approximation intervals we estimate the conditional variance

V_{ij}=\frac{1}{h_{n}^{2}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}P_{kj}(1-P_{kj})

by the plug-in estimator

\widehat{V}_{ij}:=\frac{1}{h_{n}^{2}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}\widehat{P}_{kj}(1-\widehat{P}_{kj}),

which replaces the unknown probabilities
P_{kj}
by their smoothed estimates.

Lemma 3.13

(Empirical Bernstein inequality)
.

Assume
h_{n}\geq 2
. For any
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(|U_{ij}^{(-j)}|\geq\sqrt{\frac{2\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{2}\ln(2/\delta)}{h_{n}}}+\frac{7\ln(2/\delta)}{3(h_{n}-1)}\ \middle|\ \mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)},P\right)\leq\delta.

Sketch of proof.

Conditional on
(\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)},P)
, the variables

\{A_{kj}:k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}\}
are independent and bounded in
[0,1]
.
Moreover, by the leave-one-out construction,

\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}
depends only on
A^{(-j)}
and is independent of

\{A_{kj}:k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}\}
given
P
.

Their empirical mean is

\tilde{P}_{ij}=\frac{1}{h_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}A_{kj},

with conditional expectation

\frac{1}{h_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}P_{kj}.

Thus
U_{ij}^{(-j)}
is exactly the deviation of the empirical mean from its conditional expectation.

Applying Theorem 11 of
13
to the variables

X_{k}:=A_{kj}
and again to
1-X_{k}
yields the stated two-sided inequality.
∎

Lemma
3.13
provides a strictly finite-sample, variance-adaptive control of

U_{ij}^{(-j)}
directly in terms of the empirical variance
\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{2}
, thereby
bypassing the need for asymptotic plug-in arguments.

3.3
Berry-Esseen normal approximation

We now establish a normal approximation for the variance component
U_{ij}^{(-j)}
defined in (
3.2
). Recall

U_{ij}^{(-j)}=\frac{1}{h_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}(A_{kj}-P_{kj}),

and define the conditional variance

\sigma_{ij}^{2}:=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}\mathrm{Var}(A_{kj}\mid\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)},P)=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}P_{kj}(1-P_{kj}),

so that

V_{ij}=\mathrm{Var}(U_{ij}^{(-j)}\mid\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)},P)=\frac{1}{h_{n}^{2}}\sigma_{ij}^{2}.

Under Assumption
2.2
, there exists
\eta\in(0,1/2)
such that

\eta\leq P_{kj}\leq 1-\eta\qquad\text{for all }k,j.

Hence

\eta(1-\eta)\leq P_{kj}(1-P_{kj})\leq\frac{1}{4}.

Since
|\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}|=h_{n}
, it follows that

\eta(1-\eta)\,h_{n}\leq\sigma_{ij}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\,h_{n}.

(3.12)

In particular,

\sigma_{ij}\geq\sqrt{\eta(1-\eta)}\,h_{n}^{1/2},\qquad\sigma_{ij}^{-1}\leq C\,h_{n}^{-1/2}

(3.13)

for a constant
C>0
independent of
(i,j,n)
. Consequently,

V_{ij}=\frac{\sigma_{ij}^{2}}{h_{n}^{2}}\asymp h_{n}^{-1},

and the conditional variance of
U_{ij}^{(-j)}
does not degenerate.

Lemma 3.14

(Uniform plug-in control for
\widehat{V}_{ij}
)
.

Recall

V_{ij}=\mathrm{Var}\!\big(U_{ij}^{(-j)}\mid\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)},P\big)=\frac{1}{h_{n}^{2}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}P_{kj}(1-P_{kj}),\qquad\widehat{V}_{ij}=\frac{1}{h_{n}^{2}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}\widehat{P}_{kj}(1-\widehat{P}_{kj}).

Then for all
i\neq j
,

\big|\widehat{V}_{ij}-V_{ij}\big|\leq\frac{1}{h_{n}}\,\sup_{1\leq a,b\leq n}\big|\widehat{P}_{ab}-P_{ab}\big|.

Consequently, if one had uniform sup-norm consistency

\sup_{a,b}|\widehat{P}_{ab}-P_{ab}|=o_{p}(1),

then the plug-in variance estimator
\widehat{V}_{ij}
would inherit
ratio-consistency. However, we do
not
establish such a sup-norm
bound in this paper. Rowwise mean-squared error control does not imply
uniform entrywise consistency. Therefore we do not claim ratio-consistency
of
\widehat{V}_{ij}
, and normal intervals using
\widehat{V}_{ij}
incur
an additional, unquantified variance-estimation error.

Proof.

The map
\psi(x)=x(1-x)
is
1
-Lipschitz on
[0,1]
since

|\psi^{\prime}(x)|=|1-2x|\leq 1
. Hence for any
k
,

\big|\widehat{P}_{kj}(1-\widehat{P}_{kj})-P_{kj}(1-P_{kj})\big|\leq|\widehat{P}_{kj}-P_{kj}|\leq\sup_{a,b}|\widehat{P}_{ab}-P_{ab}|.

Summing over
k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}
and dividing by
h_{n}^{2}
gives

|\widehat{V}_{ij}-V_{ij}|\leq\frac{1}{h_{n}^{2}}\cdot h_{n}\cdot\sup_{a,b}|\widehat{P}_{ab}-P_{ab}|=\frac{1}{h_{n}}\sup_{a,b}|\widehat{P}_{ab}-P_{ab}|.

If
V_{ij}\asymp h_{n}^{-1}
uniformly (e.g., under Assumption
2.2
),
then dividing by
V_{ij}
yields the ratio consistency claim.
∎

Remark 3.15
.

Our theoretical results establish rowwise mean-squared error control
but do not prove uniform sup-norm consistency of
\widehat{P}
.
Accordingly, plug-in variance intervals based on
\widehat{V}_{ij}

should be interpreted as heuristic refinements rather than formally
guaranteed procedures.

To control the additional error induced by replacing
V_{ij}
by
\widehat{V}_{ij}
,
we use Lemma
3.14
, which reduces variance plug-in accuracy to the
uniform estimation error of
\widehat{P}
.

Theorem 3.16

(Uniform Berry-Esseen bound)
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
hold, and let
h_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Then there exists a universal constant
C>0
, independent of
i,j,n
, such that

\sup_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{U_{ij}^{(-j)}}{\sqrt{V_{ij}}}\leq x\ \middle|\ \mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)},P\right)-\Phi(x)\right|\leq Ch_{n}^{-1/2}.

(3.14)

Proof.

Fix
(i,j)
and condition on
(\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)},P)
. Define

X_{k}:=A_{kj}-P_{kj},\qquad k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}.

By Proposition
2.7
, the variables
\{X_{k}\}
are independent, mean zero and bounded in
[-1,1]
, but not necessarily identically distributed.

Let

S:=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}X_{k},\qquad\sigma_{ij}^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(S\mid\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)},P).

Define the third absolute moment sum

\rho_{3}:=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[|X_{k}|^{3}\mid\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)},P\right].

Since
|X_{k}|\leq 1
, we have

\rho_{3}\leq\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}[X_{k}^{2}\mid\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)},P]=\sigma_{ij}^{2}.

The Berry-Esseen inequality for independent, not necessarily identically distributed summands (see, e.g.,
14
) states that there exists a universal constant
C_{0}>0
such that

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{S}{\sigma_{ij}}\leq x\ \middle|\ \mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)},P\right)-\Phi(x)\right|\leq C_{0}\frac{\rho_{3}}{\sigma_{ij}^{3}}.

Using
\rho_{3}\leq\sigma_{ij}^{2}
yields

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{S}{\sigma_{ij}}\leq x\ \middle|\ \mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)},P\right)-\Phi(x)\right|\leq C_{0}\sigma_{ij}^{-1}.

By (
3.13
),
\sigma_{ij}^{-1}\leq Ch_{n}^{-1/2}
, where
C
is independent of
(i,j)
. Finally, since
U_{ij}^{(-j)}=h_{n}^{-1}S
and
V_{ij}=h_{n}^{-2}\sigma_{ij}^{2}
, we have the identity

\frac{U_{ij}^{(-j)}}{\sqrt{V_{ij}}}=\frac{S}{\sigma_{ij}},

and the uniform bound (
3.14
) follows.
∎

Corollary 3.17

(Uniform asymptotic normality)
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
3.16
,

\sup_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{U_{ij}^{(-j)}}{\sqrt{V_{ij}}}\leq x\right)-\Phi(x)\right|\longrightarrow 0\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Proof.

For fixed
(i,j)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, define

\Delta_{ij}(x):=\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{U_{ij}^{(-j)}}{\sqrt{V_{ij}}}\leq x\ \middle|\ \mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)},P\right)-\Phi(x).

By Theorem
3.16
,

\sup_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\Delta_{ij}(x)|\leq Ch_{n}^{-1/2}\qquad\text{a.s.}

Taking expectations and using Jensen’s inequality for
|\,\cdot\,|
,

\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{U_{ij}^{(-j)}}{\sqrt{V_{ij}}}\leq x\right)-\Phi(x)\right|=\left|\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{ij}(x)]\right|\leq\mathbb{E}[|\Delta_{ij}(x)|]\leq Ch_{n}^{-1/2}.

Taking the supremum over
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
(i,j)
yields

\sup_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{U_{ij}^{(-j)}}{\sqrt{V_{ij}}}\leq x\right)-\Phi(x)\right|\leq Ch_{n}^{-1/2},

and since
h_{n}\to\infty
, the right-hand side tends to
0
.
∎

h_{n}\asymp\sqrt{n\log n},

we have

h_{n}^{-1/2}=\left(\sqrt{n\log n}\right)^{-1/2}=(n\log n)^{-1/4}.

Therefore the Berry-Esseen remainder in
(
3.14
) satisfies

\sup_{i,j}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{U_{ij}^{(-j)}}{\sqrt{V_{ij}}}\leq x\right)-\Phi(x)\right|=O\!\big((n\log n)^{-1/4}\big).

Consequently, Corollary

Hence the stochastic fluctuation scale and the bias scale are of the same order,

We construct two families of confidence intervals for
P_{ij}
: (i)
finite-sample, variance-adaptive bands based on

together with a bias cushion from

3.4.1
Finite-sample Confidence Intervals via Empirical Bernstein Bounds

To construct finite-sample confidence intervals for the variance component
U_{ij}^{(-j)}
, we require a concentration inequality that depends only on observable quantities. Classical Bernstein inequalities involve the true variance
V_{ij}
, which is not directly accessible. In contrast, the Empirical Bernstein bound of
13
provides a deviation inequality that replaces the unknown variance with the sample variance, yielding a fully data-driven and nonasymptotic confidence interval.

Conditional on
(\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)},P)
, the variables
\{A_{kj}:k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}\}
are independent and bounded in
[0,1]
. Their empirical mean is the leave-one-out estimator

\tilde{P}_{ij}:=\frac{1}{h_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}A_{kj}.

(3.15)

The unbiased sample variance of the observed edges in the neighborhood is

\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{2}:=\frac{1}{h_{n}(h_{n}-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,\ell\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}\\
k<\ell\end{subarray}}(A_{kj}-A_{\ell j})^{2}.

(3.16)

Since the edges are binary, this expression simplifies to

\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{2}=\frac{h_{n}}{h_{n}-1}\,\tilde{P}_{ij}(1-\tilde{P}_{ij}).

(3.17)

We apply Theorem 11 of
13
to the independent bounded variables

X_{k}:=A_{kj}
,
k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}
, with sample size
h_{n}
. For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, a union bound over the upper and lower tails yields the conditional deviation inequality

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\big|U_{ij}^{(-j)}\big|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{2}\log(4/\alpha)}{h_{n}}}+\frac{7\log(4/\alpha)}{3(h_{n}-1)}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)},P\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

(3.18)

We therefore define the variance-adaptive half-width

w_{ij}^{(B)}(\alpha):=\sqrt{\frac{2\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{2}\log(4/\alpha)}{h_{n}}}+\frac{7\log(4/\alpha)}{3(h_{n}-1)}.

(3.19)

The resulting finite-sample confidence interval for the localized average is

CI_{ij}^{(B)}(\alpha):=\Big[\tilde{P}_{ij}-w_{ij}^{(B)}(\alpha),\;\tilde{P}_{ij}+w_{ij}^{(B)}(\alpha)\Big]\cap[0,1].

(3.20)

By construction, conditionally on
(\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)},P)
, this interval captures the neighborhood average

\frac{1}{h_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}P_{kj}

with probability at least
1-\alpha
for any finite
n
.

Theorem 3.18

(Finite-sample inference for
P_{ij}
on the good set)
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and
(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}
.
Let
CI^{(B)}_{ij}(\alpha)
be the empirical Bernstein interval defined in (
3.18
) (using the sample variance form).
On the event that the neighborhood regularity condition (3.4) holds (probability at least
1-n^{-c}
as in Corollary
3.9
), we have the bias cushion

\left|P_{ij}-\frac{1}{h_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}P_{kj}\right|\leq C(\varepsilon,C_{2})\,r_{n}.

Moreover, by the empirical Bernstein deviation (
13
) in (
3.18
)

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{1}{h_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}P_{kj}\in CI^{(B)}_{ij}(\alpha)\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

Hence, on the intersection of these two events,

P_{ij}\in CI^{(B)}_{ij}(\alpha)\oplus[-C(\varepsilon,C_{2})r_{n},\;C(\varepsilon,C_{2})r_{n}].

Consequently,

\mathbb{P}\!\big(P_{ij}\in CI^{(B)}_{ij}(\alpha)\oplus[-C(\varepsilon,C_{2})r_{n},\;C(\varepsilon,C_{2})r_{n}]\big)\geq 1-\alpha-n^{-c}.

Remark 3.19

(Target of the Empirical Bernstein interval)
.

It is important to note that
CI_{ij}^{(B)}(\alpha)
is centered at the empirical neighborhood mean
\tilde{P}_{ij}
and therefore provides finite-sample coverage for the localized average

\frac{1}{h_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}}P_{kj},

not for the individual probability
P_{ij}
. The distinction reflects the fact that the Empirical Bernstein inequality controls only the stochastic fluctuation component
U_{ij}^{(-j)}
and does not address the bias term
B_{ij}^{(-j)}
.

Remark 3.20

(Advantage of the Empirical Bound)
.

Unlike standard concentration inequalities that require substituting an estimated variance
\hat{V}_{ij}
for the true variance
V_{ij}
, a step that typically degrades finite-sample guarantees and forces reliance on Slutsky’s Theorem and asymptotic normality, the Empirical Bernstein interval completely avoids the cost of studentization. The interval
CI_{ij}^{(B)}(\alpha)
automatically adapts to the empirical variance of the neighborhood while strictly preserving its
1-\alpha
coverage probability for any finite sample size.

3.4.2
Normal‑approximation intervals

Treat
U_{ij}
as approximately normal and control the bias
B_{ij}

via Lemma
3.11
. For
\alpha\in(0,1)
let
z_{1-\alpha/2}

denote the
(1-\alpha/2)
-quantile of the standard normal and define

w_{ij}^{(N)}(\alpha):=z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat{V}_{ij}}+c_{\mathrm{bias}}\Big(\frac{\log n}{n}\Big)^{1/4},

(3.21)

where
c_{\mathrm{bias}}>0
is a bias‑cushion constant chosen large
enough to dominate the bias in Lemma
3.11
. The normal interval
is

\mathrm{CI}_{ij}^{(N)}(\alpha):=[\tilde{P}_{ij}-w_{ij}^{(N)}(\alpha),\ \tilde{P}_{ij}+w_{ij}^{(N)}(\alpha)]\cap[0,1].

(3.22)

By Theorem
3.16
, the uniform Berry-Esseen bound implies that

\sup_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}}\left|\mathbb{P}\!\big(P_{ij}\in\mathrm{CI}_{ij}^{(N)}(\alpha)\big)-(1-\alpha)\right|=O\!\big(h_{n}^{-1/2}\big).

Remark 3.21
.

The Berry-Esseen bound for the variance component holds uniformly
over all
(i,j)
. However, coverage statements for
P_{ij}
require
bias control and therefore hold uniformly only over
(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}
.

These intervals are asymptotically valid for the variance component

U_{ij}^{(-j)}
uniformly in
(i,j)
, and for
P_{ij}
uniformly
over
(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}
up to the bias cushion.
Under the canonical choice
h_{n}\asymp\sqrt{n\log n}
this becomes

O\!\big((n\log n)^{-1/4}\big)
.
The bias cushion in (
3.21
) ensures that the
bias term from Lemma
3.11
is asymptotically negligible at the same scale. Normal intervals using
\widehat{V}_{ij}
therefore incur an additional
variance-estimation error term, as discussed in
Lemma
3.14
.

Remark 3.22
.

The Empirical Bernstein bands (
3.20
) are finite-sample and
variance-adaptive but can be conservative. The normal bands
(
3.22
) are typically tighter but rely on asymptotic
approximation and an explicit bias cushion; the two constructions provide
complementary guarantees in practice.

4
Honest tuning via cross-validation

The leave-one-out (LOO) construction admits a simple and
honest
cross-validation (CV) scheme for selecting the neighborhood size
h_{n}
without invalidating subsequent inference. The key structural property is that, for indices
j
in a held-out set, the observed edge
A_{ij}
is independent of the corresponding LOO predictor
\tilde{P}_{ij}^{(-j)}(h_{n})
conditional on the LOO neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}(h_{n})
. Consequently, the squared prediction error on a validation set is an unbiased estimate of the prediction risk and admits standard concentration and normal approximation results, enabling valid CV-based tuning of
h_{n}
.

We use the term “honest” to denote that no held-out edge

A_{ij}
is used in constructing its predictor

\tilde{P}_{ij}^{(-j)}
.
This eliminates double use of data but does not
imply oracle optimality of the selected bandwidth.

4.1
Cross-validation procedure and notation

Fix a row index
i\in[n]
. Let
\mathcal{G}
be a finite grid of candidate neighborhood sizes,

\mathcal{G}\subset\{1,2,\dots,n-2\},

typically chosen around the theoretical scale
h_{n}\asymp\sqrt{n\log n}
.
For each
h_{n}\in\mathcal{G}
and each held-out column
j\neq i
, form the
reduced adjacency matrix
A^{(-j)}
by deleting the
j
-th row and column. This ensures that the neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}(h_{n})
is constructed without using any information from the held-out target column
j
. Compute the corresponding leave-one-out predictor

\tilde{P}_{ij}^{(-j)}(h_{n}):=\frac{1}{h_{n}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}(h_{n})}A_{kj},\qquad j\neq i.

Define the LOO cross-validation score

\widehat{R}_{i}(h)=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}(A_{ij}-\tilde{P}_{ij}^{(-j)}(h))^{2}.

Because the neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}(h)
is constructed from
A^{(-j)}
(which omits all edges incident to vertex
j
) and
i\notin\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}(h)
by construction, the held‑out edge
A_{ij}
is conditionally independent of the predictor
\tilde{P}_{ij}^{(-j)}(h)
given
P
. Consequently,
\widehat{R}_{i}(h)
is (conditionally) unbiased for the corresponding prediction risk. A full oracle inequality analysis of this tuning rule is not included in this version.

The corresponding prediction risk is the conditional
expectation of the same quantity. Because
A_{ij}
is conditionally independent of
\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}(h_{n})
given
P
, this evaluates to

R_{i}(h_{n}):=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\bigl(A_{ij}-\tilde{P}_{ij}^{(-j)}(h_{n})\bigr)^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{N}_{i}^{(-j)}(h_{n}),P\right].

(4.1)

We then select any minimizer of the empirical score over the grid,

\hat{h}_{n}\in\arg\min_{h_{n}\in\mathcal{G}}\widehat{R}_{i}(h_{n}).

6
Discussion and Extensions

This paper develops a principled framework for inference on individual edge
probabilities in graphon models based on a simple leave-one-out modification
of neighborhood smoothing. By constructing LOO neighborhoods that do not
depend on the column being predicted, we restore conditional independence of
the centered edge variables and thereby make classical concentration and
normal-approximation tools available. The resulting methodology delivers
entrywise confidence intervals and an honest cross-validation scheme for
data-driven tuning, while retaining the optimal row-wise mean-squared error
rate known for classical neighborhood smoothing.

The LOO construction is conceptually simple and quite general, which makes
it a promising building block for extensions beyond the basic undirected,
unweighted, static setting considered here. we outline several directions for future work.

1.

Directed and weighted graphons.

For directed networks, one could replace the
18
distance
by an asymmetric distance that incorporates both in and out neighborhood
information, and then apply the same LOO decoupling on the relevant
columns. For weighted networks, a similar strategy could be pursued using appropriate moment-based distances built from
A
and
A^{2}
, with boundedness or tail assumptions tailored to the edge-weight distribution.

2.

Extension to sparse regimes.

For models of the form
P_{ij}=\rho_{n}W(\xi_{i},\xi_{j})

with
\rho_{n}\to 0
, the effective variance of

U_{ij}^{(-j)}
shrinks with
\rho_{n}
.
Normal approximation would require renormalization by

\sqrt{h_{n}\rho_{n}}
, and empirical Bernstein bounds
must account for rare-event behavior.
While the leave-one-out decoupling remains valid,
the concentration and Berry-Esseen analysis
would require substantially different arguments.

3.

Degree correction and heterogeneous sparsity.

Many real networks exhibit highly heterogeneous degree distributions.
Extending the present analysis to degree corrected graphon or blockmodel formulations would require incorporating node-specific scale factors into both the distance and the bias analysis. The LOO idea remains applicable,
but the variance proxy
V_{ij}
and the bias bounds would need to be adapted to handle varying effective sample sizes across nodes.

4.

Adaptive and simultaneous confidence bands.

While we have focused on pointwise confidence intervals for individual
edge probabilities, the same machinery can be used to construct
simultaneous bands over subsets of rows or columns, for example by
combining the Berry-Esseen bounds with high-dimensional Gaussian
approximation results. It would also be natural to let the bias cushion
constant
c_{\mathrm{bias}}
be selected in a data-driven way, for
instance by cross-validation or stability criteria, in order to balance
coverage and tightness of the intervals.

5.

Applications to real networks.

Finally, it would be valuable to apply the proposed methodology to real
data sets, such as social, citation, or biological networks, in order to
assess its practical performance and interpretability. In particular, the
ability to attach uncertainty quantification to individual edge
probabilities or to row-wise summaries could provide new insights in
applied settings where only a single large network is observed.

Identifiability limitations.

The within-block
L^{2}
identifiability condition in
Assumption
3.5
is structurally stronger than
conditions used solely for MSE analysis. It is required here to
translate rowwise
L^{2}
closeness into entrywise bias control.
Extending the inference theory to settings with weaker or purely
combinatorial identifiability (e.g., high-
K
block models) remains
an open problem.

The main limitation of the current approach is the requirement of piecewise
Lipschitz smoothness for the underlying graphon. Extensions to rougher
kernels, highly discontinuous structures, or models with sharp community
boundaries would likely require modified distance metrics and a more
delicate bias analysis. Addressing these challenges, while retaining
tractable inference procedures, remains an important open problem.
