A QSPR study of coronary artery disease drugs using eccentricity-based indices
Authors: Naveed Iqbal, Shehnaz Akhter, Alhafez M Alraih, Zahid Iqbal, Adnan Aslam, Keneni Abera Tola
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuantitative structure-property relationship (QSPR) and Quantitative structure-activity relationship QSAR modeling are constructed on the principle, which describes that biological activity and physicochemical properties of a chemical compound can be deduced from its chemical structure. These relationships are commonly developed from graph invariants that can be computed from the molecular graphs of chemical compounds. Coronary artery disease occurs when the coronary arteries become narrowed or blocked, which can restrict the flow of oxygen-rich blood to the heart. Coronary artery disease is the leading cause of disability-adjusted life years lost and death worldwide. This study advances QSPR modeling by using eccentricity-based graphical invariants, specifically designed to enhance the predictive accuracy for physicochemical properties of drugs used to treat coronary artery disease, including atorvastatin, simvastatin, rosuvastatin, aspirin, clopidogrel, metoprolol, atenolol, enalapril, lisinopril, amlodipine, diltiazem, nitroglycerin, isosorbide dinitrate, ranolazine, gemfibrozil, and fenofibrate. We used the cubic, logarithmic, quadratic, and linear models to explore the structure-property relationship of drugs for coronary artery disease. We designed the models on the basis of the adjusted r-squared values, assuming the eccentricity-based invariants as independent variables, while the physico-chemical properties of sixteen drugs as dependent variables. The dependent variables include boiling point, enthalpy of vaporization, heavy atom count, molar volume, polarizability, complexity, molecular weight, and molar refractivity. The statistical analysis indicates that the most suitable structure-property models are nonlinear. The findings show that the eccentric Albertson index and the eccentric geometric arithmetic index attain superior predictive performance compared to other indices. The analysis shows that cubic regression is the optimal choice for predicting enthalpy of vaporization, molar refractivity, polarizability, and complexity. In contrast, quadratic regression is the best option for predicting molecular weight, while a linear model is most effective for assessing heavy atom count. Additionally, logarithmic regression is the most suitable choice for boiling point and molar volume. To validate the robustness of our regression models, we used them to assess the properties of five additional coronary artery disease drugs that were not part of the original dataset. The experimental values were compared with forecasted data, revealing a strong correlation between them. This demonstrates the reliability of our regression models in assessing these vital physicochemical parameters.Keywords: Coronary artery disease drugs, QSPR modeling, Physio-chemical properties, Eccentricity-based invariantsSubject terms: Analytical chemistry, Theoretical chemistry

Introduction

Cheminformatics is a modern field involving Mathematics, Statistics, Physics, Biochemistry, Chemistry, Biology, and other informational sciences that concentrate on treating, analyzing, gathering, and storing chemical data. Cheminformatics is an influential application of information technology to help chemists analyze new problems as well as in understanding, investigating, and organizing scientific data to design unknown processes and compounds1,2. Molecular and chemical graphs play a vital role in virtual screening, computational drug design, quantitative structure-activity (QSAR), quantitative structure-property (QSPR) relationship studies, and chemoinformatics. Chemoinformatics uses graphs for various applications, including database searching and retrieval, physicochemical property prediction, chemical structure illustration, and coding. QSAR study, virtual screening, and QSPR analysis are built on the structure-property principle, which expresses that physicochemical properties and biological activity of chemical compounds can be predicted based on their chemical structure. Such structure-property associations are commonly developed from graph topological indices, fingerprints computed from the molecular graph, and molecular descriptors summed from the chemical structure3–6.

Chemical graph theory investigates the chemical and physical properties, including molar refraction, melting points, freezing points, boiling points, the octanol-water partition coefficient, the motor octane number, the acentric factor, surface tension and density, molar volume, heat of formation and solubility of the chemical graphs with the aid of mathematical models which are developed by the graph-theoretic techniques of graph theory. Cheminformatics approaches are also used to predict properties relevant to drug discovery and optimization. For example, knowledge discovery can be employed to determine lead mixtures in pharmaceutical data matching. This modern technological era concentrates on advancing chemical sciences, including designing diagnostic kits for different biological approaches and infections, drug discovery, and bond formation in chemical compounds. All these developments require powerful tools to make these advancements possible as they progress. An emerging tool for analyzing these phenomena is a topological index, which gives the same numerical value to two isomorphic chemical networks.

Numerous topological invariants are employed in QSPR/QSAR studies3,5, supplying insights into the underlying structures. Different kinds of graph invariants used for this purpose include polynomial, distance, spectral, and degree-based invariants. Among the various classes, distance-based invariants are valuable as they are used to deduce the physicochemical, biological, and pharmacological properties including toxicity, similarity, connectivity, melting point, boiling point, entropy, chirality, surface tension, stability, density, critical temperature, molecular weight, molar refractivity, polarizability, molar volume and the enthalpy of vaporization7–9. Kirmani et al.10 examined the degree-based topological invariants of COVID-19 medications, detecting good relationships with their properties. Liu et al.11 computed the distance-based invariants for antiviral drugs. Havare12 employed the M-polynomial approach to set up QSPR models for cancer drugs. Adnan et al.13 associated the degree-based invariants with properties of anti-tuberculosis drugs. Shanmukha et al.14 viewed anticancer drugs, establishing connections between topological invariants and chemical characteristics. Zaman et al.15 developed QSPR models to forecast molecular properties utilizing a linear regression model. Ravi and Desikan16 operated reduced reverse degree-based invariants to develop QSPR models for hyaluronic acid-paclitaxel conjugates, showing strong predictive power. Gnanaraj et al.17 executed QSPR analysis on NSAID drugs, while Nasir et al.18 discussed blood cancer compounds. Further, QSPR modelling has been applied to study the drugs of infertility treatment drugs19, antitumor20, breast cancer21, anti-malarial22, Colorectal Cancer23, cardiovascular disease24, and HIV/AIDS25. These studies shows that QSPR analysis furnishes a novel technique for predictive modeling, enriching our understanding of how topological invariants can improve drug development and drug design. However, much of the QSPR research has concentrated on degree-based molecular descriptors, creating a gap in the exploration of eccentricity-based descriptors. This study handles this gap by presenting these indices and showing their strong correlation with drug properties, presenting a novel technique to predictive modeling and improving the accuracy of drug design beyond existing approaches. Our research combines machine learning regression strategies with topological invariants from hydrogen-depleted molecular graphs, concentrating on eccentricity-based descriptors. Our purpose is to improve the molecular interactions and enhance predictive modeling, eventually refining the drug design precision. This may aid in developing more useful generalised drugs for the under-considered disease.

Coronary artery disease is described by the formation of atherosclerosis in the coronary arteries, which can sometimes be asymptomatic. Coronary heart disease, also commonly known as ischemic heart disease, includes conditions such as silent myocardial ischemia, acute coronary syndrome, and stable angina. Deaths related to coronary heart disease are largely due to coronary artery disease. Acute coronary syndrome is generally symptomatic and possesses conditions such as myocardial infarction and unstable angina. Coronary artery disease is the leading cause of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) lost and death worldwide. This issue mainly affects low- and middle-income countries, resulting in about 129 million DALYs and 7 million deaths annually. In 2015, there were 8.9 million deaths and 164.0 million DALYs globally due to coronary artery disease. Survivors of myocardial infarction have a much higher risk of recurrence, and they have an annual mortality rate 5 to 6 times greater than that of people without coronary artery disease26. Coronary artery disease is characterized by an insufficient supply of oxygen and blood to the myocardium. The symptoms occur due to the blockage of the coronary arteries, leading to a mismatch in the demand and supply of oxygen. Coronary artery disease is commonly caused by the formation of plaques in the lumen of coronary arteries that restrict blood flow. Coronary artery disease is the major cause of death worldwide. Coronary artery disease was an unusual cause of death at the start of the 20th century. Casualties due to coronary artery disease increased in the mid-1960s and then decreased. However, this sickness is still the leading cause of death worldwide27. The chance of death from coronary artery disease grows significantly with age. The modifiable risk factors include systolic blood pressure, which plays a vital role, as it contributes to the increased threat of coronary artery disease linked with ageing28–30. Eccentricity-based topological invariants

Eccentricity-based topological invariants are essential for studying molecular structures, especially in network analysis and mathematical chemistry51 as they offer important information about molecular branching, stability, and general connectivity by encapsulating the extremal distances within a molecular framework52,53. Results and discussions

To determine the most suitable topological index for forecasting molecular properties, we perform regression analysis using the R language and SPSS, employing cubic, linear, logarithmic, and quadratic models. For each index, we evaluate the statistical significance of the models by examining metrics such as p-values, standard error, F-statistic, , and adjusted . We aim to specify the model that provides the most robust forecasting. The best-performing model is validated by comparing predicted versus actual values. Our selection criteria focus on achieving the highest adjusted values, the lowest standard error, and statistical effectiveness with . This approach offers a data-driven strategy that strikes a balance between clarity and accuracy.

Conclusion

Contributions

This study utilized the eccentricity-based edge partitioning approaches to compute the associated graph invariants of drugs used to treat coronary artery disease. In this examination, the cubic, quadratic, linear, and logarithmic models were developed, and their prediction performance was compared. The comparison showed that most of the physicochemical properties were best predicted using the cubic models. Quadratic regression analysis revealed the eccentric geometric arithmetic index as the top predictor of molecular weight, with the eccentric Albertson index a close second. The eccentric atom bond connectivity and eccentric sum connectivity indices also showed strong predictive ability in linear regression, ranking third and fourth, respectively. For the heavy atom count, linear regression analysis showed that the eccentric geometric arithmetic index was the best predictor, followed by the eccentric Albertson index. The eccentric atom bond connectivity index in linear regression, and eccentric first Zagreb index in quadratic regression, were third and fourth, respectively. For complexity, the eccentric atom bond connectivity index was the best predictor in cubic regression, followed by the eccentric sum connectivity index. The eccentric geometric arithmetic index and eccentric Albertson index were third and fourth, respectively. For the boiling point, logarithmic regression analysis showed that the eccentric geometric arithmetic index was the best forecaster, followed closely by the eccentric Albertson index in cubic regression. The eccentric first Zagreb index and eccentric inverse sum index in quadratic regression were third and fourth, respectively. For the enthalpy of vaporization, cubic regression analysis showed that the eccentric Albertson index was the top predictor, followed by the eccentric geometric arithmetic index in linear regression. The eccentric first Zagreb index and eccentric inverse sum index in cubic regression were third and fourth, respectively. For molar refractivity, and polarizability, cubic regression analysis showed that the eccentric Albertson index was the top predictor, followed by the eccentric geometric arithmetic index. The eccentric first Zagreb index and eccentric inverse sum index were third and fourth, respectively. For molar volume, logarithmic regression analysis showed that the eccentric Albertson index was the top predictor, followed closely by the eccentric geometric arithmetic index. The eccentric first Zagreb index and eccentric inverse sum index in cubic regression were third and fourth, respectively. These outcomes improve the development of drugs for coronary artery disease by helping the forecast of physicochemical properties, thereby helping more efficient optimization and identification of potential therapeutics.
