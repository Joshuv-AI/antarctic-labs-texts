Abstract
Neural network approaches have been demonstrated to work quite well to solve partial differential equations in practice. In this context approaches like physics-informed neural networks and the Deep Ritz method have become popular. In this paper, we propose a similar approach to solve an infinite-dimensional total variation minimization problem using neural networks. We illustrate that the resulting neural network problem does not have a solution in general. To circumvent this theoretic issue, we consider an auxiliary neural network problem, which indeed has a solution, and show that it converges in the sense of Γ \Gamma -convergence to the original problem. For computing a numerical solution we further propose a discrete version of the auxiliary neural network problem and again show its Γ \Gamma -convergence to the original infinite-dimensional problem. In particular, the Γ \Gamma -convergence proof suggests a particular discretization of the total variation. Moreover, we connect the discrete neural network problem to a finite difference discretization of the infinite-dimensional total variation minimization problem. Numerical experiments are presented supporting our theoretical findings.

Introduction

Total variation (TV) minimization is a well-established technique in image processing, as it is known to preserve discontinuities in the solution. In this context, one typically minimizes a functional consisting of a data term, which enforces the consistency between the observation and the solution, and the total variation, as a regularization term.
In this paper we consider the so-called
L^{1}
-
L^{2}
-TV model first introduced in
[
28
]
, which consists of a combined
L^{1}
/
L^{2}
data term. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d=1,2
, be an open, bounded and simple connected domain with Lipschitz boundary, then the
L^{1}
-
L^{2}
-TV model writes as

\inf_{u\in BV(\Omega)\cap L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\{E(u):=\alpha_{1}\|Tu-g\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\alpha_{2}\|Tu-g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lambda\operatorname{TV}(u)\right\},

(1)

where
T:L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
is a bounded and linear operator,
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\lambda\geq 0
are parameters,
g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
denotes the given data and
\operatorname{TV}(u)
is the total variation of
u
in
\Omega
.
We recall, that for
u\in L^{1}(\Omega)

\operatorname{TV}(u):=\sup\left\{\int_{\Omega}u\Div q\ \text{d}x\colon q\in C_{c}^{1}(\Omega)^{d},|q(x)|_{2}\leq 1\text{ for allmost all }x\in\Omega\right\},

(2)

where
C_{c}^{1}(\Omega)
denotes the space of continuously differentiable functions with compact support in
\Omega
and
|\cdot|_{2}
is the standard Euclidean vector norm (
\ell^{2}
-norm). We note that if
u\in W^{1,1}(\Omega)
, then
\operatorname{TV}(u)=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|_{2}\text{d}x

[
8
, Section 10.1]
. Further we define
BV(\Omega):=\{u\in L^{1}(\Omega)\colon\operatorname{TV}(u)<\infty\}
the space of functions with bounded (total) variation. The space
BV(\Omega)
equipped with the norm
\|u\|_{BV(\Omega)}:=\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\operatorname{TV}(u)
is a Banach space
[
24
]
.

It is easy to see that the
L^{1}
-
L^{2}
-TV model is a generalization or fusion of two other well-known models in image processing. More precisely, if we set
\alpha_{1}=0
in (
1
) then we obtain the
L^{2}
-TV model, which is well suited to the task of removing additive Gaussian noise in images, see e.g.
[
15
]
, while for
\alpha_{2}=0
we obtain the
L^{1}
-TV model, which has been introduced in
[
5
,
41
,
42
]
for eliminating impulse noise, as e.g. salt-and-pepper noise.
While these two special cases work well with either Gaussian noise or impulse noise only, it has been demonstrated in
[
28
,
35
,
36
]
that the
L^{1}
-
L^{2}
-TV model is superior to these two models in removing simultaneously Gaussian and impulse noise.
It is worth mentioning that several modifications of the
L^{1}
-
L^{2}
-TV model have been introduced in the literature, see e.g.
[
25
,
27
,
40
]
.

In order to numerically compute a solution of (
1
) a discretisation method is needed. Typically total variation minimization is discretized by a finite difference method. The reason for this might be that the image information is usually stored pixel-wise.
Moreover, the functional in (
1
) is non-smooth, which prevents the use of standard derivatives and complicates the process of finding its weak formulation, a requirement for applying the finite element method.
Nevertheless, attempts for using finite element methods for total variation minimization
[
10
]
and in particular for the
L^{1}
-
L^{2}
-TV model
[
3
,
4
,
27
]
have been made. We refer the reader to
[
16
]
for an overview of different finite difference and finite element methods for total variation minimization.

A recently becoming popular discretization technique is based on neural networks. Thereby the solution space is replaced by a space of neural networks rendering the problem finite dimensional.
This approach is known under the name physics-informed neural networks (PINNs)
[
32
,
44
]
, or in connection with optimization problems as the Deep Ritz Method
[
22
]
.
Both methods are originally introduced for solving partial differential equations, but their approach differs.
In PINNs the resulting optimization problem is constituted from the residual of the partial differential equation together with its respective boundary and initial values, while the Deep Ritz Method considers the energy functional of a partial differential equation.
For an overview about PINNs and neural network approaches in general we refer the reader to the review articles
[
19
,
45
]
and the references therein.
To the best of our knowledge, we are not aware of any work which considers problems of the type (
1
), i.e. total variation minimization over the space of bounded variation, in the context of neural network discretisation, as PINNs or the Deep Ritz Method.
Nevertheless, it is worth mentioning, that first attempts to apply PINNs to solve the total variation flow, see e.g.
[
26
]
, have been presented.
However, while minimizing the residual of the total variation flow is interesting in its own right, it leads to a different problem than solving (
1
).

In this paper we are interested in solving the
L^{1}
-
L^{2}
-TV model via a neural network approach. As with the Deep Ritz method, our approach is based on the following three basic steps:

1.

select a space of neural networks as the solution space;

2.

choose a quadrature rule for approximating the functional;

3.

optimize the resulting minimization problem via a suitable algorithm.

We choose to replace the solution space
BV(\Omega)\cap L^{2}(\Omega)
in (
1
) by a set of neural networks
\mathcal{H}^{M}
, whose activation functions are ReLUs (rectifier linear units) and
M\in\mathbb{N}
denotes the number of weights and biases of a neural network in
\mathcal{H}^{M}
. In the sequel we will call such neural networks ReLU neural networks (ReLU-NNs). While any other type of neural networks might be used in this approach too, we note that ReLU-NNs are theoretically quite well studied, which is useful for our theoretical analysis. The resulting optimization problem reads then

\inf_{u\in\mathcal{H}^{M}}\alpha_{1}\|Tu-g\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\alpha_{2}\|Tu-g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lambda\operatorname{TV}(u).

(3)

As the Deep Ritz Method and PINNs, this approach can be interpreted as unsupervised learning.
However, in a more classical view (
3
) is simply an optimization problem with solution space
\mathcal{H}^{M}
.
Unfortunately, it turns out that even if (
1
) has a solution in
BV(\Omega)\cap L^{2}(\Omega)
this solution is in general not in
\mathcal{H}^{M}
, see
Example

3.3
below. This has also been observed in
[
9
]
. The reason for this negative result is that the space
BV(\Omega)\cap L^{2}(\Omega)
contains functions with jumps and ReLU-NNs cannot represent such functions.
In fact ReLU-NNs can “only” represent continuous and piecewise affine functions
[
7
]
.
Despite this negative observation, we suggest an auxiliary neural network model, which is a constrained version of (
3
).
That is we add a constraint to (
3
) such that a solution in
\mathcal{H}^{M}
is indeed guaranteed.
We prove that this auxiliary model
\Gamma
-converges to the
L^{1}
-
L^{2}
-TV model, which fully justifies our approach. We are aware that
\Gamma
-convergence for a Deep Ritz Method is also studied in
[
21
]
, but in a completely different setting, i.e. for a different type of optimization problem, namely for variational problems arising from partial differential equations, and in Sobolev spaces and not in the space of bounded variation. Hence our analysis completely differs from the one in
[
21
]
. However, our
\Gamma
-convergence proof is inspired by the
\Gamma
-convergence analysis in
[
34
]
, where the
\Gamma
-convergence of a discrete total variation to the continuous total variation (
2
) is presented.

To numerically compute a solution of the auxiliary model, numerical quadrature to approximate the integrals are needed. Note that since
\mathcal{H}^{M}\subset W^{1,1}(\Omega)
, we have that
\operatorname{TV}(u)=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|_{2}\text{d}x
for any
u\in\mathcal{H}^{M}
. Therefore, the total variation of
u\in\mathcal{H}^{M}
is computed using the gradient of
u
, which must be carefully approximated in a numerical setting, i.e. when applying quadrature. It seems reasonable to approximate the gradient by a finite difference approximation. The reason for this is that replacing the integral by a numerical quadrature evaluates the gradient only at a finite number of points. However, this can be problematic for piecewise constant functions, because the gradient may be zero at the specific evaluation points chosen by the quadrature, even though the overall function might have significant variation. This leads to an inaccurate computation of the total variation.
A finite difference approximation of the gradient circumvents this shortcoming.
We would like to emphasize that despite the described discretizations we still optimize the resulting discrete auxiliary model over the space
\mathcal{H}^{M}
.
Further, we are able to show that this discrete auxiliary model again
\Gamma
-converges to (
1
).
In particular, the
\Gamma
-convergence proof suggests a particular discretization of the gradient and hence total variation which ensures that the discrete solutions approximate the continuous solution as the discretization becomes finer. The same particular discretization of the total variation has been already used in
[
34
]
to show
\Gamma
-convergence and later in
[
33
]
, but not in the context of neural networks.
Our proof is actually based on the
\Gamma
-convergence proof in
[
34
]
and adjusted to our neural network setting.
However, any convergence result of minimizers of the discrete auxiliary model to a solution of the continuous problem is missing in
[
34
]
, while we present such a result in our setting.
In
[
33
]
a convergence result of minimizers is presented, however with respect to the
L^{2}(\Omega)
topology and under certain regularity assumption on the data
g
, which we do not need. Hence our result is more general and even stated with respect to the
L^{1}(\Omega)
topology.

The discrete auxiliary model has interesting connections to existing models. In particular, it is connected to a respective finite difference discretization of (
1
). More precisely, if the respective finite difference discretization of (
1
) has a solution then it is equivalent to the finite difference approximation of the solution of the discrete auxiliary model.
Further, if
\alpha_{1}=0
then the discrete auxiliary model looks like the total variation regularized deep image prior (DIP-TV)
[
39
]
and if additionally also
\lambda=0
then it resembles the deep image prior (DIP)
[
46
]
. Note that in
[
39
]
an anisotropic discrete total variation is considered, while we consider an isotropic total variation, due to our definition of the continuous total variation. Additionally, we would like to remark that in image reconstruction one is usually interested in an isotropic approximation of the total variation
[
18
]
, which is why we focus only on the isotropic approximation of the total variation. However, our
\Gamma
-convergence proofs can be easily extended to cover also the anisotropic discrete total variation, although then converging to a different continuous total variation, namely to (
2
) with the
\ell^{2}
-norm
|\cdot|_{2}
replaced by the
\ell^{\infty}
-norm
|\cdot|_{\infty}
.
Hence, our analysis connects DIP and DIP-TV, when used with ReLU-NNs, to the continuous
L^{1}
-
L^{2}
-TV model.

For minimizing the resulting discrete objective functional we utilize a stochastic gradient descent method, more precisely Adam
[
31
]
. This allows us to compute a numerical solution without considering the dual formulation of (
3
) and its discrete approximations. Our implementation of the solution process is based on TensorFlow
[
1
]
.
However, this comes with a drawback, namely we observed that the square-root in the
\ell^{2}
-norm cannot be handled well.
To overcome this shortcoming we approximate
|\cdot|_{2}
and thus the discrete total variation. We suggest three different approximations, one of which is the commonly used Huber-regularization
[
30
]
. For all three presented options we prove that the regularized discrete auxiliary model
\Gamma
-converges to (
1
). In particular, utilizing
\Gamma
-convergence we justify all our proposed models approximating the
L^{1}
-
L^{2}
-TV model.

In order to compute the distance of a function, for example a solution of the auxiliary model, to a solution of (
1
) an error estimate might be necessary. Note that error estimates for problems of the type (
1
) have already been derived in
[
3
,
11
,
12
,
29
]
. However, all of these error estimates rely on a dual formulation of (
1
) and a respective approximate solution of the dual problem. As stated above, our solution process does not need a dual formulation of the problem and hence in our setting we do not have an approximate solution of the dual problem of (
1
). Consequently, the error estimates in
[
3
,
11
,
12
,
29
]
are not directly applicable here. Therefore, we present a new error estimate for the
L^{1}
-
L^{2}
-TV model which is solely based on (
1
).

.

Definitions and notations

We recall that the function
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
\sigma(x)=\max\{0,x\}
is called rectifier linear unit (ReLU) and commonly used in neural network based machine learning.

Definition 2.1

(ReLU-NN, depth)
.

A ReLU-NN is a dense feedforward neural network whose activation functions live only on the neurons of the hidden layers and are ReLUs. Thereby the depth of the ReLU-NN is the number of hidden layers plus one. That is a ReLU-NN with
L\in\mathbb{N}
hidden layers has depth
L+1
.

As we are interested to represent functions
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
with a ReLU-NN, we consider only ReLU-NNs whose input layer consists of
d\in\{1,2\}
neurons and whose output layer consists of 1 neuron. Thereby the number of neurons in the hidden layers might be arbitrary but fixed such that the number of weights and biases is
M\in\mathbb{N}
. We denote the set of ReLU-NNs with this fixed architecture by
\mathcal{H}^{M}
to emphasize the number
M
of weights and biases, which is in particularly descriptive in a convergence process where the number
M
might change.
The collection of all weights and biases of a neural network in
\mathcal{H}^{M}
is denoted by
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
.
For a neural network
u_{\theta}\in\mathcal{H}^{M}
we use the index
\theta
to emphasize that the neural network depends on the weights and biases
\theta
.

Definition 2.2

(continuous piecewise affine)
.

A function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is called
continuous piecewise affine
if there exists a finite partition of polyhedra that cover
\Omega
and
f
is affine on each polyhedron and continuous in
\Omega
.

Note that the continuity requirement in
Definition

2.2
automatically holds and could be dropped in the definition, since affine functions are continuous, and all polyhedra are closed and they cover
\Omega
. We anyway kept it to make the continuity explicitly clear. From
Definition

2.2
it immediately follows that any arbitrary continuous piecewise affine function is in the Sobolev space
W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
and hence also in
W^{1,p}(\Omega)
for
p\geq 1
.

For a Banach space
V
we denote its norm by
\|\cdot\|_{V}
and its continuous dual space by
V^{*}
. For the duality pairing between
V^{*}
and
V
we use
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{V^{*},V}
. If
V=L^{2}(\Omega)
, then we denote by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
the standard
L^{2}(\Omega)
inner product. For an operator
T
, we represent its adjoint by
T^{*}
.
By
|\cdot|_{r}
,
r\in[1,\infty]
, we denote the vector
\ell^{r}
-norm and
|\cdot|
is the absolute value of a value in
\mathbb{R}
. Hence, for
v\in L^{p}(\Omega)^{d}
, we define
\|v\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}:=\left(\int_{\Omega}|v|_{2}^{p}\text{d}x\right)^{1/p}
,
p\in\mathbb{N}
.
Given that our analysis is situated within the framework of
BV(\Omega)
-functions, it is useful for us to introduce an intermediate convergence in
BV(\Omega)
. It refers to a type of convergence that falls between strong and weak convergence.

Definition 2.3

(intermediate convergence, cf.
[
8
, Definition 10.1.3]
)
.

Given a sequence
(u_{n})_{n}\subset BV(\Omega)
. We say that
u_{n}
converges to
u\in BV(\Omega)
in the sense of
intermediate convergence
, if
u_{n}\to u
strongly in
L^{1}(\Omega)
, i.e.
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|u_{n}-u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}=0
, and
\operatorname{TV}(u_{n})\to\operatorname{TV}(u)
, i.e.
\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{TV}(u_{n})=\operatorname{TV}(u)
.

We recall some further frequently used definitions from functional analysis.

Definition 2.4

(coercive, lower semicontinuous, subdifferential)
.

Let
V
be a Banach space.

1.

A functional
F:V\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is
coercive
on
V
, if for
(v_{n})_{n}\subset V
with
\|v_{n}\|_{V}\to\infty
implies
F(v_{n})\to\infty
.

2.

A functional
F:V\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is called
(weakly) lower semicontinuous
if for all
u\in V
we have that
\liminf_{n\to\infty}F(v_{n})\geq F(u)
for any sequence
(v_{n})_{n}\subset V
(weakly) converging to
u
as
n\to\infty
.

3.

For a convex functional
F:V\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, we define the
subdifferential
of
F
at
v\in V
, as the set valued function
\partial F(v)=\emptyset
if
F(v)=\infty
, and otherwise as

\partial F(v)=\{v^{*}\in V^{*}\ :\ \langle v^{*},u-v\rangle_{V^{*},V}+F(v)\leq F(u)\ \ \forall u\in V\}.

3
Problem description

We are interested in solving the
L^{1}
-
L^{2}
-TV model numerically by a neural network approach. More precisely, we propose to minimize the functional in (
1
) over the set of ReLU-NNs which leads to the following optimization problem

\min_{u_{\theta}\in\mathcal{H}^{M}}\alpha_{1}\|Tu_{\theta}-g\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\alpha_{2}\|Tu_{\theta}-g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lambda\operatorname{TV}(u_{\theta}).

(4)

Solving (
4
) amounts to find
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, where
M\in\mathbb{N}
is the number of weights and biases, such that
u_{\theta}
minimizes (
4
). That is we could rewrite (
4
) as

\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\alpha_{1}\|Tu_{\theta}-g\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\alpha_{2}\|Tu_{\theta}-g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lambda\operatorname{TV}(u_{\theta}).

(5)

It is well-known that any
L^{1}
-function can be arbitrary exactly approximated by neural networks with non-polynomial activation functions
[
38
]
.
In the case of ReLU-NNs, we even have the following depth limited universal approximation result.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
7
, Theorem 2.3]
)
.

Let
u\in L^{1}(\Omega)
. Then for any arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
there is an
u_{\varepsilon}
which can be represented by a ReLU-NN with depth
at most
\lceil\log_{2}(d+1)\rceil+1
such that
\|u-u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}<\varepsilon
.

Note that
BV(\Omega)\subset L^{1}(\Omega)
and hence
Theorem

3.1
includes any
BV
-function. A similar result holds also for
BV
-functions with respect to the intermediate convergence.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
u\in BV(\Omega)
. Then for any arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
there is an
u_{\varepsilon}
which can be represented by a ReLU-NN with depth at most
\lceil\log_{2}(d+1)\rceil+1
such that
\|u-u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}}+|\operatorname{TV}(u)-\operatorname{TV}(u_{\epsilon})|<\varepsilon
.

Proof.

For
u\in BV(\Omega)
there exists a sequence
(v_{n})_{n}\subset C^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|u-v_{n}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}=0
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v_{n}|_{2}\text{d}x=\operatorname{TV}(u)

[
6
, Theorem 3.9]
. Hence for any
\epsilon>0
there is an
v_{\epsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that

\|u-v_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\left|\operatorname{TV}(u)-\operatorname{TV}(v_{\epsilon})\right|<\epsilon/2.

(6)

Further,
v_{\epsilon}
can be approximated by a continuous piecewise affine function
u_{\epsilon}
on a regular mesh of simplices (intervals for
d=1
, triangles for
d=2
) such that
u_{\epsilon}
coincides with
v_{\epsilon}
in every vertex of the mesh and using linear interpolation within each simplex. For sufficiently small mesh size we have

\|v_{\epsilon}-u_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla v_{\epsilon}-\nabla u_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\epsilon/2,

see e.g.
[
23
, Corollary 1.109]
. Hence, by using the triangle inequality and noting that
\operatorname{TV}(v)=\|\nabla v\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}
for all
v\in W^{1,1}(\Omega)\cup C^{\infty}(\Omega)
this yields

\|v_{\epsilon}-u_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\left|\operatorname{TV}(v_{\epsilon})-\operatorname{TV}(u_{\epsilon})\right|<\epsilon/2.

(7)

Combining (
6
) and (
7
) and using again the triangle inequality yields

\|u-u_{\epsilon}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\left|\operatorname{TV}(u)-\operatorname{TV}(u_{\epsilon})\right|<\epsilon.

That is
BV(\Omega)
is dense in the space of continuous piecewise affine functions with respect to the intermediate convergence. Since any continuous piecewise affine function from
\Omega
to
\mathbb{R}
can be represented by a ReLU-NN with depth of at most
\lceil\log_{2}(d+1)\rceil+1

[
7
, Theorem 2.1]
, the assertion follows.
∎

3.1
Existence of a minimizer

Unfortunately we cannot expect that (
4
), respectively (
5
), admits a minimizer as a simple one-dimensional example borrowed from
[
9
]
indicates:

Example 3.3

(cf.
[
9
, Example 1.1]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}^{M}
be the set of one hidden layer neural networks, where each one hidden layer neural network is defined as

u_{w,a,b}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\qquad u_{w,a,b}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\sigma(w_{i}\cdot x+b_{i}).

Here
m\in\mathbb{N}
is the number of neurons in the hidden layer,
w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the vector of weights from the input neuron to the hidden layer,
a\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the vector of weights from the hidden layer to the output neuron,
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a vector of biases, and
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the ReLU activation function, i.e.
\sigma(x)=\max\{0,x\}
.
Hence, we have that
M=3m
.
If we try to approximate the BV-function

g(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x\in[0,l_{u})\\
0&\text{if }x\in(-l_{\ell},0)\end{cases}

(8)

on
\Omega=(-l_{\ell},l_{u})\subset\mathbb{R}
by solving
\min_{u_{w,a,b}\in\mathcal{H}^{M}}\|u_{w,a,b}-g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
, i.e. we set
T=I
,
\alpha_{1}=0=\lambda
and
\alpha_{2}=1
in (
4
), then it turns out that this problem does not have a solution. In fact, any function in
\mathcal{H}^{M}
is continuous and since
g
is not continuous we obtain that
\|u_{w,a,b}-g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}>0
for all
u_{w,a,b}\in\mathcal{H}^{M}
. To see that this is not a contradiction to the universal approximation theorem, see
Theorems

3.1
and
3.2
, i.e. that any
BV
-function can be approximated by a ReLU-NN arbitrarily good, let us set
m=2
,
a_{1}=1
and
a_{2}=-1
. Further we choose
w_{1}=\frac{1}{h}=w_{2}
,
h>0
, and
b_{1}=1
,
b_{2}=0
. This yields

u_{w,a,b}(x)=\sigma(\frac{1}{h}x+1)-\sigma(\frac{1}{h}x)=\frac{\sigma(x+h)-\sigma(x)}{h}\xrightarrow{h\to 0}g(x),\qquad x\in(-l_{\ell},l_{u}),

and hence by the Dominated Convergence Theorem we get that

\inf_{u_{w,a,b}\in\mathcal{H}^{M}}\|u_{w,a,b}-g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=0.

Even if
\lambda>0
the issue described in
Example

3.3
preserves. Note that a solution of (
1
) with
T=I
,
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}>0
,
\lambda=1
and

as in (

\displaystyle u(x)=\begin{cases}c_{1}&\text{if }x\in[0,l_{u}),\\
c_{2}&\text{if }x\in(-l_{\ell},0),\end{cases}

where
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}
such that

and

depend on
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
. The analytic computation of the exact solution for this example can be founded in

. Hence, if

and

, then for any

a function in

cannot solve this problem, but only approximates a solution arbitrary close for sufficiently large

we learn that the failure of the existence of a solution of (

\min_{u_{\theta}\in\mathcal{H}_{c}^{M}}\alpha_{1}\|Tu_{\theta}-g\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\alpha_{2}\|Tu_{\theta}-g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lambda\operatorname{TV}(u_{\theta})

Theorem 3.4
.

The optimization problem in (
9
) possesses a solution
u_{\theta}\in\mathcal{H}^{M}
.

Proof.

The optimization problem (
9
) can be equivalently written as

\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\alpha_{1}\|Tu_{\theta}-g\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\alpha_{2}\|Tu_{\theta}-g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lambda\operatorname{TV}(u_{\theta}).

(10)

where
\Theta:=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\colon|\theta|_{\infty}\leq c\}
. Since
\Theta
is closed and bounded, it is compact (Heine-Borel Theorem).
Let us denote the functional in (
10
) by
J(\theta):=J_{c}(\theta)+J_{d}(\theta)
, where
J_{c}(\theta):=\alpha_{1}\|Tu_{\theta}-g\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\alpha_{2}\|Tu_{\theta}-g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
and
J_{d}(\theta):=\lambda\operatorname{TV}(u_{\theta})
. We show now that
J
is lower semi-continuous. Since the ReLU activation function
\sigma
is continuous, functions in
\mathcal{H}^{M}
are continuous with respect to
\theta
. Hence, for a sequence
(\theta_{n})_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{M}
with limit
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
for

we have
u_{\theta_{n}}\to u_{\theta}
as
\theta_{n}\to\theta
. Since strong convergence implies weak convergence we also have
u_{\theta_{n}}\rightharpoonup u_{\theta}
as
\theta_{n}\to\theta
. By the weak lower semi-continuity of the total variation we obtain
\operatorname{TV}(u_{\theta})\leq\liminf_{\theta_{n}\to\theta}\operatorname{TV}(u_{\theta_{n}})
and hence
J_{d}
is lower semi-continuous. Further, since
J_{c}
is continuous, we have that
J
is lower semi-continuous.

Since
\Theta
is compact and

is lower semi-continuous, the existence of a minimizer follows by the Extreme Value Theorem.
∎

We note that

3.2
\Gamma
-convergence

We will now analyze how minimizers of (

by utilizing
\Gamma

\Gamma
-convergence.

Definition 3.6

(
\Gamma
-convergence
[
13
, Definition 1.5]
)
.

Let
V
be a Banach space and
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}_{n}:V\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
. The sequence of functionals
(\mathcal{E}_{n})_{n}
is said to
\Gamma
-converge to
\mathcal{E}
if for all
u\in V
we have

1.

(
\liminf
-condition)
\mathcal{E}(u)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{n}(u_{n})
for every
(u_{n})_{n}\subset V
converging to
u
;

2.

(
\limsup
-condition)
\mathcal{E}(u)\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{n}(u_{n})
for at least one sequence
(u_{n})_{n}\subset V
converging to
u
.

For further reading on the concept of
\Gamma
-convergence we refer to
[
13
,
20
]
.

Theorem 3.7

(
\Gamma
-convergence)
.

We define
\mathcal{E}_{n},\mathcal{E}:L^{1}(\Omega)\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
as

\mathcal{E}_{n}(u):=\begin{cases}\alpha_{1}\|Tu-g\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\alpha_{2}\|Tu-g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lambda\operatorname{TV}(u)&\text{if }\ u\in\mathcal{H}_{c_{n}}^{M_{n}},\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(12)

and

\mathcal{E}(u):=\begin{cases}\alpha_{1}\|Tu-g\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\alpha_{2}\|Tu-g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lambda\operatorname{TV}(u)&\text{if }\ u\in BV(\Omega)\cap L^{2}(\Omega),\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(13)

Then there exist sequences
(c_{n})_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}
and
(M_{n})_{n}\subset\mathbb{N}
such that the sequence
(\mathcal{E}_{n})_{n}

\Gamma
-converges to
\mathcal{E}
(shortly
\Glim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{n}=\mathcal{E}
) with respect to the
L^{1}(\Omega)
topology.

Moreover, if
\mathcal{E}
is coercive on
BV(\Omega)
, then we have that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{u\in L^{1}(\Omega)}\mathcal{E}_{n}(u)=\min_{u\in L^{1}(\Omega)}\mathcal{E}(u)

(14)

and every limit of a subsequence of
(u_{n})_{n}
is a minimizer of
\mathcal{E}
, whereby
u_{n}
is a minimizer of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Note that for any
c\geq 0
and
M\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\mathcal{H}_{c}^{M}\subseteq W^{1,2}(\Omega)
.
We check the two conditions in
Definition

3.6
:

1.

By the lower semicontinuity of the total variation in the
L^{1}(\Omega)
topology
[
6
, Remark 3.5]
we have for any
(u_{n})_{n}\subset L^{1}(\Omega)
converging to
u\in L^{1}(\Omega)
that
\mathcal{E}(u)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}(u_{n})
and hence

\mathcal{E}(u)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{n}(u_{n}),

which proves condition
1
of
Definition

3.6
.

2.

To show condition
2
of
Definition

3.6
we follow the strategy in
[
34
, Section 2.7]
.

If
u\in L^{1}(\Omega)\setminus(BV(\Omega)\cap L^{2}(\Omega))
, then clearly
\mathcal{E}(u)=+\infty
. Hence, for any sequence
(u_{n})_{n}\subset L^{1}(\Omega)
which converges to
u
we have
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{n}(u_{n})\leq\mathcal{E}(u)
.

For
u\in BV(\Omega)\cap L^{2}(\Omega)
by
[
6
, Theorem 3.9]
there exists a sequence
(v_{n})_{n}\subset C^{\infty}(\Omega)
converging to
u
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v_{n}|_{2}\text{d}x=\operatorname{TV}(u).

(15)

Figure 1
:
Exemplary mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h_{n}}
(left) with nested finer mesh (right).

Let
(\mathcal{T}_{h_{n}})_{n}
be a sequence of nested regular meshes of simplices with mesh size
h_{n}
; an example for
d=2
(two dimensions) is shown in
Figure

1
. Then for all
v\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we can construct a continuous piecewise affine function
v_{h_{n}}
, whereby
v_{h_{n}}
coincides with
v
in every node of
\mathcal{T}_{h_{n}}
. Note that any continuous piecewise affine function can be represented by a ReLU-NN
[
7
, Theorem 2.1]
. Hence for any

there exists a
c_{n}>0
and an
M_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
v_{h_{n}}\in\mathcal{H}_{c_{n}}^{M_{n}}
. We choose
c_{n}
and
M_{n}
such that
c_{n}\geq c_{n-1}
and
M_{n}\geq M_{n-1}
by possibly adding new neurons to the existing hidden layers (i.e. not changing the depth of the neural networks) which yields the nested property
\mathcal{H}_{c_{n-1}}^{M_{n-1}}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{c_{n}}^{M_{n}}
. We define the operator
\Pi_{h_{n}}:C^{\infty}(\Omega)\to\mathcal{H}_{c_{n}}^{M_{n}}
with

. By
[

or
[

there are

such that

\|v-\Pi_{h_{n}}v\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C_{1}h_{n}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|_{2}\text{d}x

and

\|\nabla v-\nabla\Pi_{h_{n}}v\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C_{2}h_{n}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla^{2}v|_{2}\text{d}x.

(16)

Hence for all

we have

\displaystyle\Pi_{h_{n}}v\to v\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla\Pi_{h_{n}}v\to\nabla v

in
L^{1}(\Omega)
for
n\to\infty
. In fact, due to (
15
) for all

there is

such that for all

\left|\operatorname{TV}(u)-\|\nabla v_{n}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\right|\leq 2^{-k-1}.

Then, from (
16
) there exists an
\tilde{n}=\tilde{n}(n)
such that

\left|\|\nabla v_{n}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}-\|\nabla\Pi_{h_{\tilde{n}}}v_{n}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\right|\leq 2^{-k-1}.

The latter two inequalities yield by using the triangle inequality

\left|\operatorname{TV}(u)-\|\nabla\Pi_{h_{\tilde{n}}}v_{n}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\right|\leq 2^{-k}.

Since

we actually have
\tilde{n}=\tilde{n}(n_{k})=:\tilde{n}_{k}
and we can construct a sequence

\Pi_{h_{\tilde{n}_{k}}}v_{n_{k}}\in\mathcal{H}_{c_{\tilde{n}_{k}}}^{M_{\tilde{n}_{k}}}

such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\Pi_{h_{\tilde{n}_{k}}}v_{n_{k}}=u\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\nabla\Pi_{h_{\tilde{n}_{k}}}v_{n_{k}}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}=\operatorname{TV}(u).

Without loss of generality let
\tilde{n}_{k+1}>\tilde{n}_{k}

we obtain

\Pi_{h_{\tilde{n}_{k}}}v_{n_{k}}\in\mathcal{H}_{c_{n}}^{M_{n}}\quad\text{for all }n\geq\tilde{n}_{k}.

We construct a sequence

such that

\begin{split}&u_{n}=\Pi_{h_{\tilde{n}_{1}}}v_{n_{1}}\quad\text{for }n=1,\ldots,\tilde{n}_{1}-1,\text{ and }\\
&u_{n}=\Pi_{h_{\tilde{n}_{k}}}v_{n_{k}}\quad\text{for }n=\tilde{n}_{k},\ldots,\tilde{n}_{k+1}-1,k\geq 1.\end{split}

Then we have that
u_{n}\to u
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{TV}(u_{n})\to\operatorname{TV}(u)
, where we used that
\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|_{2}\text{d}x=\operatorname{TV}(v)
for all

[

and
\mathcal{H}_{c}^{M}\subset W^{1,1}(\Omega)
for all

and

.
This together with the continuity of

yields

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{n}(u_{n})=\mathcal{E}(u).

Hence
1
and
2
show the
\Gamma
-convergence of
(\mathcal{E}_{n})_{n}
to
\mathcal{E}
.

Since
\mathcal{E}
is coercive on

such that

. In particular we choose

,

, which ensures that any minimizer of
(\mathcal{E}_{n})_{n}
and
\mathcal{E}
is in

. In fact, for all

\mathcal{E}_{n}
is ensured by

3.3
Error estimate

We turn to (
1
) to derive an a-posteriori error estimate, which quantifies a possible error between a solution of (
1
) and any approximation. Differently to previous work, see
[
3
,
11
,
12
,
29
]
, our error estimate is solely based on the primal formulation of the total variation minimization problem.

Let
B:=2\alpha_{2}T^{*}T
and define
\|u\|_{B}^{2}:=\langle Bu,u\rangle
, then for a minimizer
u^{*}
of (
1
) and any arbitrary
v\in L^{2}(\Omega)
we have

\frac{1}{2}\|u^{*}-v\|_{B}^{2}\leq E(v)-E(u^{*}),

cf.
[
3
]
. We define
F:L^{2}(\Omega)\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
with
F(u):=\alpha_{2}\|Tu-g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
and
G:L^{2}(\Omega)\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
with
G(u):=G_{1}(u)+G_{\operatorname{TV}}(u):=\alpha_{1}\|Tu-g\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\lambda\operatorname{TV}(u)
. Then we obtain using the definition of subdifferential

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}\|T(u^{*}-v)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq F(v)-F(u^{*})+G(v)-G(u^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\langle\xi_{F},v-u^{*}\rangle+\langle\xi_{G},v-u^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|\xi_{F}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|v-u^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\xi_{G}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|v-u^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

where
\xi_{F}\in\partial F(v)
and
\xi_{G}\in\partial G(v)
. Since
F
is Fréchet differentiable we have
\xi_{F}=2\alpha_{2}T^{*}(Tv-g)
and obtain

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}\frac{\|T(u^{*}-v)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}{\|u^{*}-v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}\leq 2\alpha_{2}\|T^{*}(Tv-g)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\xi_{G}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

where
\xi_{G}
can be approximated by
\alpha_{1}T^{*}\frac{Tv-g}{|Tv-g|+\epsilon}+\lambda\nabla^{*}\frac{\nabla v}{|\nabla v|_{2}+\epsilon}

with
\epsilon>0
being a very small constant.

If the bilinear form
\langle Bu,u\rangle
is coercive, i.e. there exists a constant
\tilde{c}>0
such that
\langle Bu,u\rangle=\alpha_{2}\|Tu\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\geq\tilde{c}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
for any
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, we can further estimate as

\displaystyle\tilde{c}\|u^{*}-v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq 2\alpha_{2}\|T^{*}(Tv-g)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\xi_{G}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(17)

Note that the coercivity condition holds whenever
T
is invertible, which is, for example, the case for the identity operator
T=I
.

4
Numerical realization

In this section we propose a discretization of (
9
) and connect the resulting optimization problem to (
1
) again utilizing
\Gamma
-convergence.

4.1
Numerical integration

To realize a numerical implementation of (
9
) and (
11
) we need to approximate its integrals. Note that, since the functions in
\mathcal{H}^{M}
are continuous,
\operatorname{TV}(u_{\theta})=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{\theta}(x)|_{2}\text{d}x
for any
u_{\theta}\in\mathcal{H}^{M}
. Utilizing an appropriate quadrature (
9
) can be approximated as

\begin{split}\min_{u_{\theta}\in\mathcal{H}_{c}^{M}}\alpha_{1}\sum_{x\in\Omega^{h}}w_{1}^{x}|(Tu_{\theta})(x)-g(x)|&+\alpha_{2}\sum_{x\in\Omega^{h}}w_{2}^{x}|(Tu_{\theta})(x)-g(x)|^{2}\\
&+\lambda\sum_{x\in\Omega^{h}}w_{\operatorname{TV}}^{x}|(\nabla u_{\theta})(x)|_{2},\end{split}

(18)

where
\Omega^{h}\subset\Omega
is a finite set of points and
w_{q}^{x}
,
q\in\{1,2,\operatorname{TV}\}
, are suitable quadrature weights. The superscript
x
in
w_{q}^{x}
emphasize that the weights might depend on
x
. Further we define
N:=|\Omega^{h}|
the size of
\Omega^{h}
, or in other words, the number of points in
\Omega^{h}
.

In image processing, the data points are typically organized in a highly structured manner. These points represent the pixels of the image and are arranged on a regular, equidistant grid. This means that each pixel is uniformly spaced from its neighbors, both horizontally and vertically, forming a systematic grid pattern. Since we are interested in image processing tasks, in the sequel we assume that the data points can be arranged on a regular and equidistant grid.
This allows for standard discretizations techniques of the gradient operator, like forward and backward differences. In particular, discretizing the gradient operator as well as a blurring operator on unstructured grids might be tricky. Hence in the sequel we sometimes also write for
d=2
the number of data points
N=|\Omega^{h}|
as
N=N_{1}\times N_{2}
, where
N_{1}
is the number of points in the first dimension and
N_{2}
is the number of points in the second dimension.

Note that for
d=1
a none-equidistant discretisation does not usually rise any troubles. However, to keep the presentation simple and concise also for
d=1
we restrict to equidistant grids.

4.2
Numerical realization of the total variation

Considering the step function
g
given in (
8
), it is clear that
\nabla g(x)=0
for almost every
x\in(-l_{\ell},l_{u})
, except in 0, where its gradient cannot be computed. Taking a finite number of points
x\in\Omega^{h}\subset(-l_{\ell},l_{u})\setminus\{0\}
, means that
\sum_{x\in\Omega^{h}}w_{\operatorname{TV}}^{x}|\nabla g(x)|_{2}=0
, although the total variation of
g
in
(-l_{\ell},l_{u})
is 1, i.e.
\operatorname{TV}(g)=1

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{n},\mathcal{E}:L^{1}(\Omega)\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be defined as in (
24
) and (
13
), respectively, and
u_{n}
be a minimizer of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then there exist sequences
(c_{n})_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}
and
(M_{n})_{n}\subset\mathbb{N}
such that
(\mathcal{E}_{n})_{n}

\Gamma
-converges to
\mathcal{E}
with respect to the
L^{1}(\Omega)
topology. Moreover, if
\mathcal{E}
is coercive on
BV(\Omega)
, then we have that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{u\in L^{1}(\Omega)}\mathcal{E}_{n}(u)=\min_{u\in L^{1}(\Omega)}\mathcal{E}(u)

and every limit of a subsequence of
(u_{n})_{n}
is a minimizer of
\mathcal{E}
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the one of
Theorem

3.7
. We only recall the important steps and differences.

The
\liminf
-condition (
Definition

3.6
1
) follows directly from the lower semicontinuity of the functional
\mathcal{E}
.

The
\limsup
-condition (
Definition

3.6
2
) clearly holds for any
u\in L^{1}(\Omega)\setminus(BV(\Omega)\cap L^{2}(\Omega))
. For
u\in BV(\Omega)\cap L^{2}(\Omega)
there is a sequence
(v_{n})_{n}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
converging to
u
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v_{n}|_{2}\text{d}x=\operatorname{TV}(u)

[
8
, Theorem 10.1.2]
. Then for all
v\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
, we can construct a continuous piecewise affine function
v_{h_{n}}\in\mathcal{A}_{h_{n}}
, whereby
v_{h_{n}}
coincides with
v
in every node of
\mathcal{T}_{h_{n}}
.
Further we choose
c_{n}
and
M_{n}
such that
v_{h_{n}}\in\mathcal{H}_{c_{n}}^{M_{n}}
as well, i.e.
v_{h_{n}}\in\mathcal{A}_{h_{n}}\cap\mathcal{H}_{c_{n}}^{M_{n}}
. Defining the interpolation operator
\Pi_{h_{n}}:C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})\to\mathcal{A}_{h_{n}}\cap\mathcal{H}_{c_{n}}^{M_{n}}
with
v\mapsto v_{h_{n}}=\Pi_{h_{n}}v
and utilizing interpolation error estimates, see
[
17
, Theorem 3.1.5]
, we can construct analog to the proof of
Theorem

3.7
a sequence
(u_{k})_{k}
given as

u_{k}:=\Pi_{h_{k}}v_{k}\in\mathcal{A}_{h_{k}}\cap\mathcal{H}_{c_{k}}^{M_{k}}

such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}u_{k}=u\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{TV}(u_{k})=\operatorname{TV}(u).

Noting that
T
and
u\mapsto\alpha_{1}\|Tu-g\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\alpha_{2}\|Tu-g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
are continuous yields

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{k}(u_{k})=\mathcal{E}(u)

and hence the
\Gamma
-convergence assertion.

Since
\mathcal{E}
is coercive on
BV(\Omega)
it possesses a minimizer in
BV(\Omega)

[
35
, Theorem 3.3]
and the set
S:=\{u\in BV(\Omega)\colon\mathcal{E}(u)\leq a\}
is bounded in
BV(\Omega)
, where
a:=\mathcal{E}(0)
.
Since
0\in BV(\Omega)
and
0\in\mathcal{A}_{h_{n}}\cap\mathcal{H}_{c_{n}}^{M_{n}}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, any minimizer of
(\mathcal{E}_{n})_{n}
and
\mathcal{E}
is in
S
. Thereby
Theorem

3.4
ensures a minimizer of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
.
Since
\overline{S}
is compact in

[
2
, Theorem 2.5]
and such that
\inf_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\mathcal{E}_{n}=\inf_{\overline{S}}\mathcal{E}_{n}
, the
\Gamma
-convergence of
(\mathcal{E}_{n})_{n}
to
\mathcal{E}
and
[
13
, Theorem 1.21]
yield

\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{u\in L^{1}(\Omega)}\mathcal{E}_{n}(u)=\min_{u\in L^{1}(\Omega)}\mathcal{E}(u).

Moreover, since any subset of a compact set is relatively compact and the sequence of minimizers
(u_{n})_{n}\subseteq\overline{S}
, we have that every limit of a subsequence of
(u_{n})_{n}
is a minimizer of
\mathcal{E}

[
13
, Theorem 1.21]
.

Remark 4.2
.

Contrary to the proof of
Theorem

3.7
and
[
34
]
, in the proof of
Theorem

4.1
we do not use the nested property of the mesh. However, this can be easily achieved by requesting that
c_{n}\geq c_{n-1}
and
M_{n}\geq M_{n-1}
, whereby at most neurons to the existing hidden layers are added, for all
n>1
, as in the proof of
Theorem

3.7
.

The
\Gamma
-convergence result relies on a continuous and piecewise affine approximation of an arbitrarily smooth function. This approximation suggests particular discretisations for the gradient and total variation:

1.

In one-dimension (
d=1
), the gradient of a continuous piecewise affine function
u
in the interval
[x_{i},x_{i+1}]
is given by
\nabla u=\frac{u_{i+1}-u_{i}}{h_{n}}
. This suggests to use forward differences to approximate the gradient operator. Nevertheless, analogously we could have also set
\nabla u=\frac{u_{i}-u_{i+1}}{h_{n}}
suggesting backward differences. It is clear that this does not change the total variation of
u
, see (
21
), and hence the
\Gamma
-convergence proof keeps the same. In our numerical experiments for
d=1
we will use the forward difference approximation of the gradient.

2.

In two-dimensions (
d=2
), the gradient is given by (
22
), which means in a finite difference setting a combination of forward and backward differences is suggested. More precisely, the finite difference gradient
\nabla^{h}
is then

\nabla^{h}u_{i,j}=\nabla_{\text{FB}}^{h}u_{i,j}:=\left(\frac{u_{i+1,j}-u_{i,j}}{h_{n}},\frac{u_{i,j+1}-u_{i,j}}{h_{n}},\frac{u_{i,j}-u_{i-1,j}}{h_{n}},\frac{u_{i,j}-u_{i,j-1}}{h_{n}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}

(25)

and the discrete total variation as in (
23
). That is, for
v=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4})\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
we define
|v|_{r,1}
as

|v|_{r,1}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left|\left(v_{1},v_{2}\right)\right|_{r}+\left|\left(v_{3},v_{4}\right)\right|_{r}\right).

(26)

Then the discrete total variation of
u\in\mathbb{R}^{2^{n}\times 2^{n}}
is given as
h_{n}^{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2^{n}}|\nabla^{h}u_{i,j}|_{r,1}
. If
r=1
, then we have that
h_{n}^{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2^{n}}|\nabla^{h}u_{i,j}|_{1,1}=h_{n}^{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2^{n}}|\nabla^{h}u_{i,j}|_{1}
, while for
r\neq 1
in general we have
h_{n}^{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2^{n}}|\nabla^{h}u_{i,j}|_{r,1}\neq h_{n}^{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2^{n}}|\nabla^{h}u_{i,j}|_{r}
.

Note that if we set
c_{n}
and
M_{n}
in (
24
) such that
\mathcal{A}_{h_{n}}\cap\mathcal{H}_{c_{n}}^{M_{n}}=\mathcal{A}_{h_{n}}
, then
Theorem

4.1
shows the
\Gamma
-convergence of a finite difference approximation of
\mathcal{E}
to
\mathcal{E}
, as in
[
34
]
. We emphasize that showing the
\Gamma
-convergence of a finite difference approximation of
\mathcal{E}
to
\mathcal{E}
also seems to require a continuous approximation of a
C^{\infty}
-function. In fact, for piecewise constant approximations a
\Gamma
-convergence result cannot be shown for total variation minimization, as a counterexample presented in
[
10
]
illustrates.

4.3.1
Choice of discretisation

Based on the above result we will make the following choices on the total variation:

d=1

: We use a standard forward difference discretised gradient operator with incorporated boundary conditions and denote it by
\nabla^{h}_{\text{F}}
.
This leads to the one-dimensional discrete total variation

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\Omega^{h}}|\nabla^{h}_{\text{F}}u(x)|.

d=2

: Here we will consider two different discretised gradient operators. The standard forward difference discretized gradient operator denoted by
\nabla^{h}_{\text{F}}
and the above suggested forward-backward discretisation
\nabla^{h}_{\text{FB}}
. For both choices we incorporate in a standard way respective boundary conditions. The two-dimensional discrete total variations read then as

\operatorname{TV}_{2}^{h}(u):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\Omega^{h}}|\nabla^{h}_{\text{F}}u(x)|_{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{TV}_{2,1}^{h}(u):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\Omega^{h}}|\nabla^{h}_{\text{FB}}u(x)|_{2,1}

respectively.

We note that
\operatorname{TV}_{2}^{h}
is extensively used in image processing and is referred to as isotropic total variation in the literature, see e.g.
[
18
]
. That is the main reason why we additionally consider
\operatorname{TV}_{2}^{h}
in our numerical experiments, despite the missing
\Gamma
-convergence result.

4.4
Identifying and solving a numerical issue

The implementation of the neural network model is based on TensorFlow and in order to find a minimizer we utilize the optimization algorithm Adam
[
31
]
, which is a stochastic gradient descent method and already pre-implemented in TensorFlow.
It turns out that Adam in TensorFlow has difficulties in optimizing (
19
) with
\operatorname{TV}_{2}^{h}
and
\operatorname{TV}_{2,1}^{h}
for
d=2
, which seems due to the presence of the square-root. At least after some iterations the gradient becomes so steep that the algorithm returns an
NaN
and further computations seem impossible. In the rest of this section we set
d=2
and present and analyze options to circumvent this issue. In particular we will approximate the total variation considering the following options:

1.

Huber-regularization: This is a commonly used approximation technique for total variation minimization, see for example
[
14
,
27
,
43
]
. We define for
\gamma>0
the Huber-functions
\phi_{2,\gamma}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to[0,\infty)
and
\phi_{2,1,\gamma}:\mathbb{R}^{4}\to[0,\infty)
by

\phi_{2,\gamma}(u):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2\gamma}|u|_{2}^{2}&\text{if }|u|_{2}\leq\gamma,\\
|u|_{2}-\frac{\gamma}{2}&\text{if }|u|_{2}>\gamma,\end{cases}

(27)

for
u\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and

\phi_{2,1,\gamma}(w):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\phi_{2,\gamma}(u)+\phi_{2,\gamma}(v)\right)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\left(|u|_{2}+|v|_{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\gamma&\text{if }|u|_{2}>\gamma\text{ and }|v|_{2}>\gamma\\
\frac{1}{2}|u|_{2}-\frac{1}{4}\gamma+\frac{1}{4\gamma}|v|_{2}^{2}&\text{if }|u|_{2}>\gamma\text{ and }|v|_{2}\leq\gamma\\
\frac{1}{2}|v|_{2}-\frac{1}{4}\gamma+\frac{1}{4\gamma}|u|_{2}^{2}&\text{if }|u|_{2}\leq\gamma\text{ and }|v|_{2}>\gamma\\
\frac{1}{4\gamma}\left(|u|_{2}^{2}+|v|_{2}^{2}\right)&\text{if }|u|_{2}\leq\gamma\text{ and }|v|_{2}\leq\gamma\end{cases}

for
w=(u,v)
with
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Then in the case of
\operatorname{TV}_{2}^{h}
and
\operatorname{TV}_{2,1}^{h}
we replace
|\cdot|_{2}
by
\phi_{2,\gamma}(\cdot)
and
|\cdot|_{2,1}
by
\phi_{2,1,\gamma}(\cdot)
. Hence the Huber-regularized discrete total variation is then given by
h^{2}\sum_{x\in\Omega^{h}}\phi_{2,\gamma}(\nabla^{h}u_{\theta}(x))
or
h^{2}\sum_{x\in\Omega^{h}}\phi_{2,1,\gamma}(\nabla^{h}u_{\theta}(x))
respectively.

2.

\gamma
-lifting: For
\gamma>0
we replace
|\cdot|_{2}
by
|\cdot|_{2,\gamma}
and
|\cdot|_{2,1}
by
|\cdot|_{2,1,\gamma}
, whereby for
v=(v_{1},v_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
we define
|v|_{2,\gamma}:=\sqrt{v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}+\gamma}
and for
w=(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},w_{4})\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
we define

|w|_{2,1,\gamma}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{w_{1}^{2}+w_{2}^{2}+\gamma}+\sqrt{w_{3}^{2}+w_{4}^{2}+\gamma}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(|(w_{1},w_{2})|_{2,\gamma}+|(w_{3},w_{4})|_{2,\gamma}\right).

The choice
|\cdot|_{2,\gamma}
has been extensively studied in
[
2
]
.

3.

\max
-
\gamma
-lifting: Instead of
|\cdot|_{2}
and
|\cdot|_{2,1}
we use
\max\{|\cdot|_{2},\gamma\}
and
|\cdot|_{2,1,\max_{\gamma}}
respectively, where
\gamma>0
and

|w|_{2,1,\max_{\gamma}}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\max\{|(w_{1},w_{2})|_{2},\gamma\}+\max\{|(w_{3},w_{4})|_{2},\gamma\}\right)\qquad\text{for }w\in\mathbb{R}^{4}.

In
[
27
, Proposition 3.3]
it is shown that the Huber-regularization
\phi_{2,\gamma}
approximates the norm
|\cdot|_{2}
from below. Analogously one also shows that
\phi_{2,1,\gamma}
approximates the norm
|\cdot|_{2,1}
from below. On the contrary the
\gamma
-lifting and
\max
-
\gamma
-lifting approximate the corresponding norm from above, since
|v|_{2}\leq|v|_{2,\gamma}
for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
|w|_{2,1}\leq|w|_{2,1,\gamma}
for all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Further all three approximations converge for
\gamma\to 0
to the respective norm.

We recall that
\mathcal{H}^{M}\subset W^{1,1}(\Omega)
and hence for any
u\in\mathcal{H}^{M}
we have
\operatorname{TV}(u)=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|\text{d}x
.
We analyze now the

, where

and how their minimizers behave for
\gamma\to 0
.

Lemma 4.3

is indeed a minimizer of
E_{\text{FD}}
.
∎

A direct consequence of
Proposition

4.8
is that (
33
) does not have a solution if and only if (
19
) with
c,M=\infty
does not have a solution.

We emphasize that
Propositions

4.7
and
4.8
also holds if
|\cdot|_{\blacktriangleright}
is chosen to be any of the approximations presented in
Section

4.4
, i.e. Huber-regularization,
\gamma
-lifting and
\max
-
\gamma
-lifting, or any arbitrary discrete norm, e.g.
|\cdot|_{r}
,
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Actually, the proof of
Propositions

4.7
and
4.8
does not rely on the type of discretization but only on the fact that if
u_{\text{FD}}(x)=u_{\theta}(x)
for all
x\in\Omega^{h}
, then
E_{\theta}(u_{\theta})=E_{\text{FD}}(u_{\text{FD}})
.

Proposition

4.8
can be generalized in the following way.

Theorem 4.9
.

Given the set of points
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}=\Omega^{h}\subset\Omega
we define

\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}:=\{u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\colon\forall x,y\in\Omega^{h},x\neq y,u(x)\text{ and }u(y)\text{ are independent}\},

e.g.
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
could be the set of ReLU-NNs.
Then we have that

u_{\text{FD}}^{*}\in\argmin_{u_{FD}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mathcal{E}_{\text{FD}}(u_{\text{FD}})\Longleftrightarrow u_{\theta}^{*}\in\argmin_{u_{\theta}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}}\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(u_{\theta})

with
{u}_{\text{FD}}^{*}(x)=u^{*}_{\theta}(x)
for all
x\in\Omega^{h}
,
where
\mathcal{E}_{\text{FD}}(u_{\text{FD}}):=\sum_{x\in\Omega^{h}}\mathcal{G}(u_{\text{FD}}(x))
,
\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(u_{\theta}):=\sum_{x\in\Omega^{h}}\mathcal{G}(u_{\theta}(x))
and
\mathcal{G}:\mathbb{R}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof of
Propositions

4.7
and
4.8
. Nevertheless, we state it for completeness.

“
\Rightarrow
”: Let
u_{\text{FD}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
be any minimizer of
\mathcal{E}_{\text{FD}}
. By the definition of
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
there exists a
u_{\theta}^{*}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
such that
u_{\text{FD}}^{*}(x)=u_{\theta}^{*}(x)
for all
x\in\Omega^{h}
and hence
\mathcal{E}_{\text{FD}}(u_{\text{FD}}^{*})=\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(u_{\theta}^{*})
. Assume that there exists a
u_{\theta}\neq u_{\theta}^{*}
such that
\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(u_{\theta})<\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(u_{\theta}^{*})
. Then we can define
u_{\text{FD}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
u_{\text{FD}}(x)=u_{\theta}(x)
for all
x\in\Omega^{h}
. This yields
\mathcal{E}_{\text{FD}}(u_{\text{FD}})=\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(u_{\theta})<\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(u_{\theta}^{*})=\mathcal{E}_{\text{FD}}(u_{\text{FD}}^{*})
, which is a contradiction to the optimality of
u_{\text{FD}}^{*}
.

“
\Leftarrow
”: Let
u_{\theta}^{*}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
be a solution of
\mathcal{E}_{\theta}
. We define
u_{\text{FD}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that

u_{\text{FD}}^{*}(x)=u^{*}_{\theta}(x)
for all
x\in\Omega^{h}
. This implies that
\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(u^{*}_{\theta})=\mathcal{E}_{\text{FD}}(u_{\text{FD}}^{*})
. Assume that
u_{\text{FD}}^{*}
is not a minimizer of
\mathcal{E}_{\text{FD}}
, i.e. there is a
\tilde{u}_{\text{FD}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
\tilde{u}_{\text{FD}}\neq{u}_{\text{FD}}^{*}
such that
\mathcal{E}_{\text{FD}}(\tilde{u}_{\text{FD}})<\mathcal{E}_{\text{FD}}(u_{\text{FD}}^{*})
. We construct an interpolation
\tilde{u}_{\theta}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
of
\tilde{u}_{\text{FD}}
such that
\tilde{u}_{\text{FD}}(x)=\tilde{u}_{\theta}(x)
for all
x\in\Omega^{h}
. Consequently
\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(\tilde{u}_{\theta})=\mathcal{E}_{\text{FD}}(\tilde{u}_{\text{FD}})<\mathcal{E}_{\text{FD}}(u_{\text{FD}}^{*})=\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(u^{*}_{\theta})
, which is a contradiction to
u_{\theta}^{*}
being a solution of
\mathcal{E}_{\theta}
and hence
u_{\text{FD}}^{*}
is indeed a minimizer of
\mathcal{E}_{\text{FD}}
.
∎

Note that
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
in
Theorem

4.9
can be a piecewise polynomial space of any polynomial order and hence we could set
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}=\mathcal{H}^{M}
the set of ReLU-NNs as well.
Let us use
Theorem

4.9
to connect physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) and the Deep Ritz Method to a finite difference method by a simple example. To this end we only need to specify the function
\mathcal{G}
of
Theorem

4.9
. The equivalence of the respective solutions in the sense of
Theorem

4.9
follows directly from
Theorem

4.9
.

Example 4.10
.

Given the Poisson problem with Dirichlet boundary conditions

\displaystyle\Delta u

\displaystyle=g

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=g_{D}

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega.

1.

PINNs:

\mathcal{G}(u(x))=w^{x}|\Delta^{h}u(x)-g(x)|^{2}
, where
\Delta^{h}
is a finite difference discretization of the Laplace operator with incorporated boundary conditions and
w^{x}
some suitable (quadrature) weights.

2.

Deep Ritz Method:

\mathcal{G}(u(x))=w^{x}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla^{h}u(x)|^{2}-g(x)u(x)\right)
, where
\nabla^{h}
is a finite difference discretization of the gradient operator with incorporated boundary conditions and
w^{x}
some suitable (quadrature) weights.

Theorem

4.9
can also be related to supervised learning as the following examples illustrates.

Example 4.11

(Supervised learning)
.

Let
(x_{i},g(x_{i}))_{i=1}^{N}
be given training data, then
\mathcal{G}
mimics the empiric loss function. E.g. in the case of the square loss we have
\mathcal{G}(u(x_{i}))=\frac{1}{N}|u(x_{i})-g(x_{i})|^{2}

5
Numerical experiments

In the following we show numerical experiments supporting our theoretical findings and demonstrating that the model (
19
) can indeed be used to solve image processing tasks such as denoising, inpainting and deblurring as expected from total variation minimization. We recall that our implementation is done in Python using TensorFlow and can be found at
[
37
]
.
To obtain a solution, we utilize the optimization algorithm Adam, which is already implemented in TensorFlow.
Thereby in each iteration step we check whether the loss, i.e. the functional in (
19
) evaluated at the current iterate, is reduced, i.e. is smaller than the loss of all previous iterates, and only update the approximation
u_{\theta}
if this is the case. In this way we ensure that the current iterate is always the yet best approximation, i.e. the approximation which gives the smallest loss so far, leading to a monotonic decreasing sequence of energies.

In all our experiments we set
w_{q}^{x}=\frac{1}{N}
for all
x\in\Omega^{h}
and
q\in\{1,2,\operatorname{TV}\}
.
Although a comparison of different quadratures might be of interest, as this may have influence on the solution and solution process, it is beyond the scope of the present paper.

5.2
Applications

We consider three applications, namely denoising, inpainting and deblurring. In all the applications the image is scaled to the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
. In order to recover the original image from the corrupted observation we minimize (
19
). Thereby in all the experiments we choose a forward difference discretisation of the gradient operator with incorporated homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions. Further we choose
\lambda=1
and
\operatorname{TV}_{2}^{h}
with
\gamma
-lifting. That is the last term in (
19
), i.e.
\frac{\lambda}{N}\sum_{x\in\Omega^{h}}|\nabla^{h}u_{\theta}(x)|_{\blacktriangleright}
, is set to be
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\Omega^{h}}|\nabla_{\text{F}}^{h}u_{\theta}(x)|_{2,\gamma}
, where
\gamma=10^{-10}
.
The solution space
\mathcal{H}_{c}^{M}
consists of ReLU-NNs with 3 hidden layers, where each layer has 128 neurons, and we set
c=10000
. Adam with learning rate 0.001 is used to find an approximation and the training is always stopped after 300001 iterations. The parameters
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are selected differently depending on the application and specified at the respective places. However, they are not optimized in any way and only chosen such that the effect of the total variation can be observed. Next, we describe the image reconstruction tasks before we present and discuss the respective results.

Figure 9
:
Original disk-image of size
129\times 129
pixels (left) and inpainting mask (right).

Denoising

Image denoising is the task of removing noise from an image. We generate from the original image
u_{\text{orig}}
a noisy input
g=\mathcal{N}(u_{\text{orig}}+\eta)
, where
\eta
represents additive Gaussian noise with zero mean and standard deviation
\sigma
and
\mathcal{N}
represents salt-and-pepper noise with probability
s\in[0,1)
, i.e.

g(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ with probability }\frac{s}{2},\\
1&\text{ with probability }\frac{s}{2},\\
u_{\text{orig}}+\eta&\text{ with probability }1-s.\end{cases}

Hence for this application we set
T^{h}
being the identity operator in (
19
).

For
u_{\text{orig}}
being the disk-image of size
129\times 129
pixels, see
Figure

9

6
Conclusion and discussion

We have seen that the considered neural network approach changes the nature of the original problem. In particular, while the
L^{1}
-
L^{2}
-TV model is convex, its neural network version (
4
) is not convex anymore. Further we observe that even if the original problem has a solution the resulting neural network optimization problem does in general not, cf.
Example

3.3
. Hence, it seems that the neural network approach renders the problem more complicated to deal with. Note that the non-existence of a solution can be easily resolved, as we did by introducing (
9
). However, the non-convexity seems to be persistent and we are not aware how to circumvent this property. Nevertheless, this additional complexity seems to come with a benefit.
First, while optimizing on a rather coarse grid, we actually obtain a solution in a continuum, i.e. the solution is mesh-free and continuous, allowing us to depict the solution on an arbitrary fine grid and any domain.
Second, it seems that due to the nature of ReLU-NNs on a scale finer than the given data resolution, structures in the image are promoted which are attractive to humans. It is not clear to us why this is the case and further studies in this direction might be needed.

In order to guarantee the existence of a (neural network) solution we introduced the constrained problem (
9
). In particular (
9
) has a solution in
\mathcal{H}^{M}
as long as
c<\infty
.
However, the constraint
|\theta|_{\infty}\leq c
seems mainly theoretical. In particular, since our solution process relies on an iterative (stochastic) method,
numerically we do not expect to reach the analytic solution exactly anyway, but only approximate it.
In the case that the solution space is
\mathcal{H}^{M}
, i.e.
c=\infty
, we would obtain an approximation in the space of piecewise affine functions which is dense in the space
BV
.
Hence, solving the optimization problem over
\mathcal{H}^{M}
seems numerically sufficient, as the numerical solution in
\mathcal{H}^{M}
can arbitrarily closely approximate the exact
BV
solution, assuming that
M
is sufficiently large, and a stopping criterion, as the number of iterations, will always allow the numerical process to terminate with an approximation.

Although we used in our experiments the number of iterations as a stopping criterion, it does not seem ideal, as we do not know how much progress has been already made in the solution process. Also our derived error estimate seems not to be applicable in a stopping criterion, due to its oscillatory behavior. Finding a suitable stopping criterion for non-smooth optimization problems is in general a difficult task in numerical analysis. As such problems do not allow for usual derivatives, it is difficult to derive numerically usable optimality conditions.

We conclude by emphasizing that the presented neural network approach can be applied to any optimization problem of the form

\inf_{u\in V}F(u),

where
V
is a Banach space and
F:V\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, and any set of neural networks.
