Time-harmonic waves in Korteweg and nematic-Korteweg fluids Preprint: APS/123-QED
Patrick E. Farrell, Umberto Zerbinati

Abstract
We derive the Helmholtz–Korteweg equation, which models acoustic waves in Korteweg fluids. We further derive a nematic variant of the Helmholtz–Korteweg equation, which incorporates an additional orientational term in the stress tensor. Its dispersion relation coincides with that arising in Virga’s analysis of the Euler–Korteweg equations, which we extend to consider imaginary wave numbers and the effect of boundary conditions. In particular, our extensions allow us to analyze the effect of nematic orientation on the penetration depth of evanescent plane waves, and on the scattering of sound waves by obstacles. Furthermore, we make new, experimentally-verifiable predictions for the effect of boundary conditions for a modification of the Mullen–Lüthi–Stephen experiment, and for the scattering of acoustic waves in nematic-Korteweg fluids by a circular obstacle.

A Korteweg fluid is one that incorporates a term in the stress tensor that depends on density gradients
Korteweg 1901
. For example, an inviscid Korteweg fluid has stress tensor

\underline{\underline{\sigma}}^{(K)}=p\underline{\underline{I}}-u_{1}\rho\left(\nabla\rho\otimes\nabla\rho\right),

(1)

where
\underline{\underline{\sigma}}^{(K)}
is the stress tensor,
p
is the pressure, and
u_{1}>0
is a material constant. For a van der Waals fluid, the pressure can be expressed as

p=\rho c_{0}^{2}-\rho\nabla\cdot\left[\rho u_{1}\nabla\rho\right],

(2)

where
c_{0}>0
is the isotropic speed of sound. The Euler–Korteweg system is the combination of the usual Euler equations with the constitutive relation (
1
).

At first, Korteweg’s theory had little application, as remarked upon in Truesdell et al.
Truesdell et al. 2004
:

Korteweg, […] did not feel the second requirement of modern work, namely, to get different and new results as well.
His theory seems not to have been taken up by any later writer.

Later authors have found many applications and new results based on Korteweg’s theory.
While the Korteweg term is often neglected for rarefied gases and nearly incompressible fluids, the
Korteweg stress is important when we seek to model the behavior of fluids near liquid-vapor interfaces, where the
density gradient is large.
For example, Korteweg fluids have been used to model phenomena such as capillary waves and gas-saturated magma melts
Lauro 2008
;
Benzoni-Gavage 2013
.
Furthermore, Korteweg’s theory has been the starting point for the development of nematoacoustic models
Virga 2009
, which are used to describe the propagation of sound waves in nematic liquid crystals.
In fact, nematic liquid crystals can be regarded as Korteweg fluids on an acoustic length scale, where the stress tensor is augmented with a term related to the nematic director field
\vec{n}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}

\underline{\underline{\sigma}}^{(V)}=p\underline{\underline{I}}-u_{1}\rho\left(\nabla\rho\otimes\nabla\rho\right)+u_{2}\left(\nabla\rho\cdot\vec{n}\right)\nabla\rho\otimes\vec{n},

(3)

where
u_{2}>0
is a material constant related to the nematic liquid crystal and the pressure is now given by

p=\rho c_{0}^{2}-\rho\nabla\cdot\left[\rho\left(u_{1}\nabla\rho+u_{2}(\nabla\rho\cdot\vec{n})\vec{n}\right)\right].

(4)

Virga has given a full derivation of the stress tensor (
3
)
Virga 2009
.

A classical derivation of Korteweg’s model can be found in Toupin
Toupin 1962
;
Toupin 1964
, where continuum equations are derived from a classical principle of virtual work.
The Korteweg equations can also be derived from a non-standard variational principle
Fried and Gurtin 2006
, can be cast within the framework of hyperelasticity
Capriz 1989
, and derived from the kinetic theory of gases
Giovangigli 2020
.

The continuum thermodynamics of Korteweg fluids has been extensively studied by many authors, leading to the development of new concepts such as interstitial work, balance of self-equilibrated forces, and multipolarity
Dunn and Serrin 1985
;
Anderson et al. 1998
;
Mehrabadi et al. 2005
.

Korteweg fluids have also been extensively studied from a mathematical point of view.
For example, under certain assumptions, the Euler–Korteweg equations can be formulated as a Hamiltonian system
Benzoni-Gavage et al. 2005
;
Benzoni-Gavage 2010
.
Furthermore, the well-posedness of the Euler–Korteweg system has been studied in detail
Benzoni-Gavage et al. 2007
;
Murata and Shibata 2020
;
Tsuda 2016
.
Lastly, the Euler–Korteweg system has also been studied from a gradient flow perspective
Giesselmann et al. 2017
.

In this work we consider the propagation of time-harmonic pressure waves in Korteweg and particularly nematic-Korteweg fluids.
Acoustic waves in such fluids can exhibit anisotropic phenomena: the speed of sound depends on the orientation of the nematic director. Conversely, ultrasonic waves can change the nematic field, and thereby change the observed color of liquid crystals, under appropriate conditions. Moreover, the subject is somewhat controversial in the literature, with no consensus on the physical mechanisms behind these phenomena
Sonnet and Virga 2012
.

In this work we neglect effects on timescales longer than the acoustic timescale. The long-time asymptotic behavior of waves in Korteweg fluids has been studied by Benzoni-Gavage and coworkers
Benzoni-Gavage 2013
;
Benzoni-Gavage and Chiron 2018
.

I
Helmholtz–Korteweg equation

Following the well-known derivation of the wave equation from Euler’s equations, we begin considering the continuity equation and the balance law of linear momentum in the absence of external body forces, i.e.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{v})=0,\qquad\rho\left[\partial_{t}\vec{v}+(\underline{\underline{\nabla\vec{v}}})\vec{v}\right]=-(\nabla\cdot\underline{\underline{\sigma}})\;,

(5)

where
\vec{v}(\vec{x},t)
is the fluid velocity and
\underline{\underline{\sigma}}
is the Cauchy stress tensor.

We are interested in disturbances in the density field of the form
\rho(\vec{x},t)=\rho_{0}\left(1+s(\vec{x},t)\right)
, where
s(\vec{x},t)
is a time-harmonic condensation, i.e.

s(\vec{x},t)=\Re\left[S(\vec{x})e^{-i\omega t}\right],

(6)

with
\omega
being the frequency of the disturbances.
Furthermore, we will assume that the condensation is a small perturbation of the density field, i.e.
\lvert s(\vec{x},t)\rvert=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
, with
\varepsilon\ll 1
.
Lastly, we will assume that the velocity field is a small perturbation around the stationary regime, i.e.
\lVert\vec{v}(\vec{x},t)\rVert=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
.

Under these assumptions, we can rewrite (
5
) as

\displaystyle\rho_{0}\left[\partial_{t}s+\nabla\cdot\vec{v}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})\right]=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\vec{v}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})=-\rho^{-1}(\nabla\cdot\underline{\underline{\sigma}})\;.

Neglecting terms of order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
and observing that since
\lvert s(\vec{x},t)\rvert\ll 1
we have
\rho^{-1}\approx\rho_{0}^{-1}
, we end up with the linearised balance law, i.e.

\displaystyle\rho_{0}\left[\partial_{t}s+\nabla\cdot\vec{v}\right]=0,\qquad\partial_{t}\vec{v}=-\rho_{0}^{-1}(\nabla\cdot\underline{\underline{\sigma}})\;.

(7)

Taking the time derivative of the continuity equation and substituting for
\partial_{t}\vec{v}
yields a general wave equation, i.e.

\rho_{0}\partial_{t}^{2}s-\nabla\cdot\left(\nabla\cdot\underline{\underline{\sigma}}\right)=0.

(8)

Substituting the ansatz (
6
) in the general wave equation (
8
) yields

\Re\left[-\rho_{0}\omega^{2}S(\vec{x})e^{-i\omega t}\right]=-\Re\left[\nabla\cdot\left(\nabla\cdot\underline{\underline{\sigma}}\right)\right].

(9)

We first consider Korteweg fluids. Neglecting terms of higher order in the Korteweg constitutive relation (
1
) yields

\nabla\cdot\underline{\underline{\sigma}}\approx\nabla p=c_{0}^{2}\nabla\rho-\nabla\left[\rho\nabla\cdot(\rho u_{1}\nabla\rho)\right],

(10)

i.e. we consider a purely spherical response.
Using the time-harmonic ansatz (
6
), we obtain

\Re\left[\nabla\cdot\underline{\underline{\sigma}}\right]\approx\Re\left[c_{0}^{2}\rho_{0}\nabla S(\vec{x})e^{-i\omega t}-\rho_{0}^{3}u_{1}\nabla\left(\Delta S(\vec{x})e^{-i\omega t}\right)\right].

(11)

We can now substitute (
11
) in (
9
) and divide by
\rho_{0}e^{-\omega t}
. Assuming
S(\vec{x})
is analytic, we can extend the equation just derived to the whole complex plane and obtain the Helmholtz–Korteweg equation, i.e.

-\omega^{2}S(\vec{x})-c_{0}^{2}\Delta S(\vec{x})+\rho_{0}^{2}u_{1}\Delta^{2}S(\vec{x})=0.

(12)

We believe that this equation has not been considered previously in the literature.

We first comment on two limiting regimes of the Helmholtz–Korteweg equation: the nearly incompressible regime, with
u_{1}\rho_{0}^{2}\gg c_{0}^{2}
, and the nearly ideal regime, with
u_{1}\rho_{0}^{2}\ll c_{0}^{2}
.
As previously discussed, (
1
) can be derived within the hyperelasticity framework, in particular, the elastic energy density associated with (
1
) is given by

W(\rho,\nabla\rho)=c_{0}^{2}\rho+\frac{1}{2}u_{1}\lVert\nabla\rho\rVert^{2}.

(13)

This energy shows that as
u_{1}
increases, a larger penalty is imposed on the gradient of the density field, and in the limit this imposes a constraint of spatially-constant density.
Thus, for nearly incompressible fluids such as water, we expect a large value of
u_{1}
. However, the speed of sound
c_{0}
also increases with incompressibility. Thus, from (
12
), the limiting regime is determined by the density
\rho_{0}
.
With a sufficiently large density
\rho_{0}
and
\omega^{2}
comparable to
\rho_{0}^{2}u_{1}
, the Helmholtz–Korteweg equation (
12
) reduces to the time-harmonic Kirchhoff–Love equation, i.e.

\Delta^{2}S(\vec{x})-\mu S(\vec{x})=0,\qquad\mu=\frac{\omega^{2}}{c_{0}^{2}u_{1}\rho_{0}^{2}}\;.

(14)

The previous equation suggests that sufficiently dense and incompressible Korteweg fluids will behave as thin fluid shells at sufficiently high frequencies.
On the other hand, for compressible fluids we expect that both
u_{1}
and
c_{0}
will be smaller.
Hence, for a sufficiently rarefied Korteweg fluid we expect to retrieve the classical Helmholtz equation governing time-harmonic acoustic waves in compressible fluids, i.e.

\Delta S(\vec{x})+\mu S(\vec{x})=0,\qquad\mu=\frac{\omega^{2}}{c_{0}^{2}}\;.

(15)

We must also consider boundary conditions.
Since the Helmholtz–Korteweg equation is a fourth-order partial differential equation, we need to impose two boundary conditions.
Furthermore, for the Helmholtz–Korteweg equation to be consistent with the classical Helmholtz equation in the nearly ideal regime, we would like to consider boundary conditions that incorporate the ones imposed for the classical Helmholtz equation.
We will here consider three types of boundary conditions that might occur when the fluid is in contact with an obstacle along an interface
\Gamma
.

1.

Sound-soft boundary conditions, which correspond to imposing that the acoustic pressure vanishes along
\Gamma
.
From (
2
) we know that the acoustic pressure is given by

c_{0}^{2}\rho_{0}S(\vec{x})-\rho_{0}^{3}u_{1}\Delta S(\vec{x})=0,

(16)

thus the sound-soft boundary conditions correspond to imposing homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions on
S(\vec{x})
and on
\Delta S(\vec{x})
.

2.

Sound-hard boundary conditions, which correspond to imposing that the normal derivative of the fluid velocity
\partial_{\vec{\nu}}\vec{v}
vanishes along
\Gamma
.
Using (
7
) and assuming the fluid velocity is also time-harmonic, we get

i\omega\rho_{0}(\vec{n}\cdot\vec{\nu})=c_{0}^{2}\partial_{\vec{\nu}}S(\vec{x})-\rho_{0}^{2}u_{1}\partial_{\vec{\nu}}\Delta S(\vec{x}),

(17)

thus the sound-hard boundary conditions correspond to imposing homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions on
S(\vec{x})
and on
\Delta S(\vec{x})
.

3.

Impedance boundary conditions, which correspond to imposing that the normal derivative of the fluid velocity
\partial_{\vec{\nu}}\vec{v}
is proportional to the excess pressure along
\Gamma
. This boundary condition is equivalent to imposing Robin boundary conditions on
S(\vec{x})
and on
\Delta S(\vec{x})
.
The proportionality constant is usually denoted by
\zeta
and is called the impedance of the boundary.

II
Nematic Helmholtz–Korteweg equation

We now consider nematic Korteweg fluids, with stress tensor given by (
3
).
Virga has studied the propagation of sound waves in the most prominent such fluids, nematic liquid crystals
Virga 2009
.
Historically the interaction of acoustic waves with the nematic director field was first explained by means of the minimal entropy production principle
Dion and Jacob 1977
;
Dion 1979
, i.e. the acoustic anisotropy is assumed to be the result of calamitic molecules reorienting in order to minimize the propagation losses.
We here assume the aligning torque acting on the nematic director field is of elastic nature, rather than of a dissipative viscous one.
This idea was already proposed, and validated experimentally, by Mullen, Lüthi, and Stephen
Mullen et al. 1972
. These authors augment the elastic energy density with a term representing the interaction of the nematic director field with a planar acoustic wave with wave-vector
\vec{k}
, i.e.

W_{a}(\vec{k},\vec{n})=c_{1}\lvert\vec{k}\rvert^{2}+c_{2}\lvert\vec{k}\cdot\vec{n}\rvert^{2},

(18)

where
c_{1},c_{2}>0
are constitutive constants.
This approach was extended to the case of a general acoustic wave in Selinger et al.
Selinger et al. 2002
, where the elastic energy augmentation is assumed to couple the nematic director field with the gradient of the density field.
In this context Virga’s theory regards liquid crystals as Korteweg fluids and proposes the constitutive relation (
3
) for the Cauchy stress tensor, which in a hyperelastic framework can be derived from the elastic energy density

W(\rho,\nabla\rho,\vec{n})=c_{0}^{2}\rho+\frac{1}{2}u_{1}\lVert\nabla\rho\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{2}u_{2}(\nabla\rho\cdot\vec{n})^{2}.

(19)

The acoustic energy
W_{a}
is derived from
W
by assuming that the density
\rho
represents a planar wave.

Assuming the condensation
s(\vec{x},t)
is time-harmonic, as in (
6
), we can expand
\nabla\cdot\underline{\underline{\sigma}}
and drop any term of order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
to get

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\underline{\underline{\sigma}}\approx\Re\Big[-\rho_{0}c_{0}^{2}\nabla S(\vec{x})

\displaystyle+u_{1}\rho_{0}^{3}\nabla(\Delta S(\vec{x}))

(20)

\displaystyle+\rho_{0}^{3}u_{2}\nabla\left((\nabla S\cdot\vec{n})\vec{n}\right)\Big].

Substituting this expression in (
9
) and dividing by
\rho_{0}e^{-\omega t}
we get

\displaystyle-\omega^{2}S(\vec{x})-c_{0}^{2}\Delta S(\vec{x})

\displaystyle+\rho_{0}^{2}u_{1}\Delta^{2}S(\vec{x})

(21)

\displaystyle+\rho_{0}^{2}u_{2}\nabla\cdot\nabla\left[\nabla\cdot((\nabla S\cdot\vec{n})\vec{n})\right]=0.

We now focus our attention on the last term of the previous equation, which can be expanded, using (
76
), as

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\nabla\left[\nabla\cdot((\nabla S\cdot\vec{n})\vec{n})\right]=\nabla\cdot

\displaystyle\nabla\Big[\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}S}}\vec{n}\cdot\vec{n}

(22)

\displaystyle+\underline{\underline{\nabla\vec{n}}}\nabla S\cdot\vec{n}

\displaystyle+(\nabla S\cdot\vec{n})(\nabla\cdot\vec{n})\Big],

where
\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}S}}
is the Hessian matrix of
S
.
Substituting this expansion in (
21
) yields,

\displaystyle-\omega^{2}S(\vec{x})-c_{0}^{2}\Delta S(\vec{x})+\rho_{0}^{2}u_{1}\Delta^{2}

\displaystyle S(\vec{x})

(23)

\displaystyle+\rho_{0}^{2}u_{2}\nabla\cdot

\displaystyle\nabla\Big[\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}S}}\vec{n}\cdot\vec{n}

\displaystyle+\underline{\underline{\nabla\vec{n}}}\nabla S\cdot\vec{n}

\displaystyle+(\nabla S\cdot\vec{n})(\nabla\cdot\vec{n})\Big]=0.

A reasonable assumption to proceed, as done in
Virga 2009
, is that the nematic director field
\vec{n}
is regarded as undistorted at the acoustic length scale, so that we can assume
\underline{\underline{\nabla\vec{n}}}=0
.
Under this hypothesis, (
22
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\nabla\left[\nabla\cdot((\nabla S\cdot\vec{n})\vec{n})\right]=\nabla\cdot\nabla\left[{\vec{n}\cdot\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}S}}\vec{n}}\right].

(24)

This yields the nematic Helmholtz–Korteweg equation, i.e.

\displaystyle-\omega^{2}S(\vec{x})-c_{0}^{2}\Delta S(\vec{x})+

\displaystyle\rho_{0}^{2}u_{1}\Delta^{2}S(\vec{x})

(25)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\rho_{0}^{2}u_{2}\nabla\cdot\nabla\left[{\vec{n}\cdot\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}S}}\vec{n}}\right]=0.

We would like to comment on a secondary effect of the assumption that the nematic director field
\vec{n}
is undistorted at the acoustic length scale.
In particular, the assumption
\underline{\underline{\nabla\vec{n}}}=0
implies that any acoustic effect associated to the Oseen–Frank portion of the elastic energy density, i.e.

W_{OF}(\underline{\underline{\nabla\vec{n}}})=\frac{1}{2}K\left(\underline{\underline{\nabla\vec{n}}}\,:\underline{\underline{\nabla\vec{n}}}\right),

(26)

is neglected. (Here we have adopted the one-constant approximation for the Oseen–Frank elastic energy density, where
K>0
is the Frank elastic constant.)
This should come as no surprise, since the Oseen–Frank elastic energy density and the corresponding Leslie–Ericksen equations are usually derived under the assumption that the fluid under consideration is incompressible.
In
Farrell et al. 2023
an inviscid compressible variant of the Leslie–Ericksen equations has been derived from kinetic considerations.
In particular, the Euler equations are augmented with a compressible Ericksen tensor, i.e.

\underline{\underline{\sigma}}^{(E)}\coloneqq\lambda p\,\underline{\underline{\nabla\vec{n}}}^{T}\underline{\underline{\nabla\vec{n}}},

(27)

where
\lambda>0
is a constitutive parameter uniquely determined by the shape of the calamitic molecules constituting the liquid crystal.
Over a longer length scale than the acoustic one we will need also to take into account the effect of the augmentation term (
27
).

Lastly, we need to discuss boundary conditions for the nematic Helmholtz–Korteweg equation.
The boundary conditions for the nematic case differ slightly from the isotropic Helmholtz–Korteweg equation:

1.

Sound-soft boundary conditions will change since excess pressure is now defined, from (
4
), as

c_{0}^{2}\rho_{0}S(\vec{x})-\rho_{0}^{3}u_{1}\Delta S(\vec{x})-u_{2}\rho_{0}^{3}\left(\vec{n}\cdot\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}S}}\vec{n}\right)=0.

(28)

Sound-soft boundary conditions thus correspond to imposing homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions on
S(\vec{x})
and

\Delta S(\vec{x})=-\frac{u_{2}}{u_{1}}\left(\vec{n}\cdot\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}S}}\vec{n}\right).

(29)

2.

Sound-hard boundary conditions also change since the normal derivative of the fluid velocity
\partial_{\vec{\nu}}\vec{v}
now satisfies the equation

\displaystyle i\omega\rho_{0}(\vec{n}\cdot\vec{\nu})=c_{0}^{2}\partial_{\vec{\nu}}S(\vec{x})

\displaystyle-\rho_{0}^{2}u_{1}\partial_{\vec{\nu}}\Delta S(\vec{x})

(30)

\displaystyle-\rho_{0}^{2}u_{2}\partial_{\vec{\nu}}\left(\vec{n}\cdot\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}S}}\vec{n}\right).

(31)

Sound-hard boundary conditions thus correspond to imposing homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions on
S(\vec{x})
and

\partial_{\vec{\nu}}\,\Delta S(\vec{x})=-\frac{u_{2}}{u_{1}}\partial_{\vec{\nu}}\left(\vec{n}\cdot\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}S}}\vec{n}\right).

(32)

3.

Some computation shows that the impedance boundary conditions for the nematic Helmholtz–Korteweg equation are equivalent to imposing Robin boundary conditions on
S(\vec{x})
and

\displaystyle\partial_{\vec{\nu}}\,\Delta S(\vec{x})=i\zeta\Delta S(\vec{x})

\displaystyle+i\zeta\frac{u_{2}}{u_{1}}\left(\vec{n}\cdot\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}S}}\vec{n}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{u_{2}}{u_{1}}\partial_{\vec{\nu}}\left(\vec{n}\cdot\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}S}}\vec{n}\right).

(33)

III
Plane waves

We wish to build intuition about the Helmholtz–Korteweg and nematic Helmholtz–Korteweg equations. We also wish to verify their physical correctness, by checking that the dispersion relation they imply matches that derived by Virga
Virga 2009
;
Sonnet and Virga 2012
using the continuity equation and the balance law of linear momentum.
We therefore consider plane wave solutions, given by

S(\vec{x})=s_{0}e^{ik(\vec{x}\cdot\vec{d})},

(34)

where
s_{0}=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
,
\vec{d}
is the unit vector that prescribes the direction in which the wave propagates,
k
is the wave-number and the wave-vector
\vec{k}
is given by
\vec{k}\coloneqq k\vec{d}
.
Substituting (
34
) in (
12
) and dividing by
e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}
we obtain the dispersion relation

-\omega^{2}+c_{0}^{2}k^{2}+\rho_{0}^{2}u_{1}k^{4}=0.

(35)

We now introduce the nondimensional wave-vector defined as

\vec{\kappa}\coloneqq\frac{c_{0}}{\omega}\vec{k}.

(36)

Using the nondimensional wave-vector and the corresponding wave-number
\kappa
we can rewrite (
35
) as

-1+\kappa^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\tau_{1}^{2}\omega^{2}\kappa^{4}=0,\qquad\tau_{1}\coloneqq 4\frac{\rho_{0}\sqrt{u_{1}}}{c_{0}^{2}}.

(37)

Here
\tau_{1}
represents the characteristic time scale of Korteweg acoustic waves, i.e. the time scale over which the Korteweg nature of the fluid becomes relevant in the propagation of acoustic waves.
Clearly both (
35
) and (
37
) have solutions with both real and imaginary parts. In particular (
37
) has solutions

\kappa=\pm\frac{1}{\tau_{1}\omega}\left[-2\pm 2\sqrt{1+\tau_{1}^{2}\omega^{2}}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(38)

Since the wave-numbers
\kappa
can have both real and imaginary parts, we can have both propagating and evanescent waves, i.e. waves that oscillate in the direction of the real part of
\vec{\kappa}
and waves that decay exponentially in the direction of the imaginary part of
\vec{\kappa}
.

Following
Virga 2009
;
Sonnet and Virga 2012
, by analogy to the Helmholtz equation we assume that the wave-number
k
can be decomposed as

k=k^{(R)}+ik^{(I)},\qquad k^{(R)}=\frac{\omega}{c},

(39)

where
k^{(R)},k^{(I)}\in\mathbb{R}
and where
c>0
is the effective speed of sound, i.e. the actual (and possibly anisotropic) speed of sound with which the wave propagates, as opposed to the isotropic speed of sound
c_{0}
.
We then introduce a new dimensionless quantity
\kappa^{(I)}
, i.e.

\kappa^{(I)}\coloneqq\frac{c_{0}}{\omega}k^{(I)}.

(40)

Using (
40
) we can split (
37
) in real and imaginary parts. Considering first the imaginary part, we find two non-trivial solutions for
\kappa^{(I)}_{0}
, i.e.

\kappa^{(I)}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{2}{\tau_{1}^{2}\omega^{2}}-\left(\frac{c_{0}}{c}\right)^{2}}.

(41)

Substituting (
41
) in the real part of (
37
), we focus our attention on solutions that are either purely real or purely imaginary.

We first consider the purely real case, where
\kappa^{(I)}_{0}
vanishes, and
k=\omega/c
.
We express (
35
) using the characteristic time scale
\tau_{1}
to get

-\omega^{2}+c_{0}^{2}k^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\tau_{1}^{2}c_{0}^{4}k^{4}=0.

(42)

Substituting our expression for
k
and dividing by
\omega^{2}
yields

-1+\left(\frac{c_{0}}{c}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\tau_{1}^{2}\omega^{2}\left(\frac{c_{0}}{c}\right)^{4}=0.

(43)

Solving this equation for
c_{0}/c
we find only one real positive root, i.e.

\frac{c_{0}}{c}=\omega\tau_{1}\left[2\left(\sqrt{1+\omega^{2}\tau_{1}^{2}}-1\right)\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(44)

Thus, as
\omega\tau_{1}
increases, the speed of a propagating acoustic wave in a Korteweg fluid increases as well.
This matches
(
Virga 2009
, eq. (82))
.

We next consider the other case where
k
is purely imaginary, i.e.
k=ik^{(I)}
.
Inspired by the phenomenon of total internal reflection, we will assume

k^{(I)}=-\frac{\omega}{c}\sqrt{\alpha},\qquad\alpha\geq 0.

(45)

The real number
\alpha
has a physical interpretation in the context of total internal reflection, related to the angle of incidence, and the refractive indices of the two media involved.

Substituting (
45
) in (
35
) we find

-1-\left(\frac{c_{0}}{c}\right)^{2}\alpha+\frac{1}{4}\tau_{1}^{2}\omega^{2}\left(\frac{c_{0}}{c}\right)^{4}\alpha^{2}=0.

(46)

Solving for
\alpha
we find two solutions, i.e.

\alpha=4\left(\frac{c}{c_{0}}\right)^{2}\left[\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{1+\omega^{2}\tau_{1}^{2}}}{\omega^{2}\tau_{1}^{2}}\right],

(47)

The penetration depth
\delta
of the evanescent wave, i.e. the distance over which the amplitude of the wave decays by a factor of
1/e
, is the absolute value of the reciprocal of the imaginary part of the wave-number.
We begin substituting (
47
) in (
45
) and discarding the imaginary part of
\sqrt{\alpha}
to get

\delta=\frac{c_{0}}{2\omega}\left[\frac{-1+\sqrt{1+\tau_{1}^{2}\omega^{2}}}{\tau_{1}^{2}\omega^{2}}\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}

(48)

Asymptotically for
\omega\tau_{1}\gg 1
we can approximate (
48
) as

\delta\approx\frac{c_{0}}{2\omega}\sqrt{\tau_{1}\omega},

(49)

which suggests that the penetration depth of an evanescent wave is inversely proportional to the square root of
\omega
and directly proportional to the square root of
\tau_{1}
.

Lastly we would like to consider the nematic Helmholtz–Korteweg equation (
25
) and the corresponding plane wave solutions.
Substituting (
34
) in (
25
) we obtain

-\omega^{2}+c_{0}^{2}k^{2}+\rho_{0}^{2}u_{1}k^{4}+\rho_{0}^{2}u_{2}k^{4}\left(\vec{d}\cdot\vec{n}\right)^{2}=0,

(50)

where we have used the fact that
\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}S}}=k^{2}\underline{\underline{S(\vec{d}\otimes\vec{d})}}
.
This matches the dispersion relation derived by Virga
(
Virga 2009
, eq. (78))
, in the inviscid regime.
Collecting the fourth order terms in (
50
) and defining
\xi
as the angle between
\vec{d}
and
\vec{n}
we find

-\omega^{2}+c_{0}^{2}k^{2}+\left[\rho_{0}^{2}u_{1}+\rho_{0}^{2}u_{2}\cos^{2}(\xi)\right]k^{4}=0.

(51)

We now introduce the nematic Korteweg characteristic time scale
\tau_{2}
, defined as

\tau_{2}\coloneqq 4\frac{\rho_{0}\sqrt{u_{1}+u_{2}\cos^{2}(\xi)}}{c_{0}^{2}}.

(52)

Notice that due to the presence of the
\cos^{2}(\xi)
term in the definition of
\tau_{2}
, the characteristic time scale
\tau_{2}
is anisotropic.
Furthermore we can rewrite (
51
) as

-1+\kappa^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\tau_{2}^{2}\omega^{2}\kappa^{4}=0,

(53)

with roots

\kappa=\pm\frac{1}{\tau_{2}\omega}\left[-2\pm 2\sqrt{1+\tau_{2}^{2}\omega^{2}}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(54)

Proceeding in a manner analogous to the one used for the Helmholtz–Korteweg equation we find that for propagating waves the dispersion relation is given by

\frac{c_{0}}{c}=\omega\tau_{2}\left[2\left(\sqrt{1+\omega^{2}\tau_{2}^{2}}-1\right)\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(55)

which due to the anisotropic nature of
\tau_{2}
is also anisotropic. In particular, the speed of sound of a propagating acoustic wave in a nematic Korteweg fluid is greatest when the wave propagates along the nematic director field, as observed in the experiment of Mullen, Lüthi & Stephen
Mullen et al. 1972
.
The anisotropic nature of the speed of sound in nematic Korteweg fluids is observed in our numerical simulations, as can be seen in Figure

IV
Plane wave reflection

We consider
\mathbb{R}^{2}
where the upper half-plane is occupied by a Korteweg fluid, and the real axis is the interface between the fluid and an obstacle.
Let
S^{-}
denote an incoming plane wave of the form (
34
), i.e.

S^{-}(\vec{x})=s_{0}e^{ik\left(d_{1}x_{1}+d_{2}x_{2}\right)},\qquad-d_{1},d_{2}\leq 0.

(56)

Since the real axis is the interface between the fluid and the obstacle, by Snell’s reflection law we have a reflected wave of the form

S^{+}(\vec{x})=s_{0}Ae^{ik\left(d_{1}x_{1}-d_{2}x_{2}\right)},

(57)

where
A
is the amplitude.
Since
S^{+}
and
S^{-}
are solutions of (
12
) or (
25
), by the linearity of these equations
S\coloneqq S^{+}+S^{-}
is also a solution.
Furthermore, it is an easy exercise to show that the boundary conditions prescribed on
S
impose a constraint on the amplitude
A
of the reflected wave, i.e.

1.

For sound-soft boundary conditions along the real axis we have

s_{0}e^{ikd_{1}x_{1}}+s_{0}Ae^{ikd_{1}x_{1}}=0
, thus we need to impose
A=-1
.

2.

For sound-hard boundary conditions along the real axis we have
s_{0}ikd_{2}e^{ikd_{1}x_{1}}-s_{0}ikd_{2}Ae^{ikd_{1}x_{1}}=0
, thus we need to impose
A=1
.

3.

For impedance boundary conditions along the real axis we have

iA(d_{2}k-\zeta)e^{ikd_{1}x_{1}}=-i(d_{2}k+\zeta)e^{ikd_{1}x_{1}},

(58)

thus we need to impose
A=k\frac{d_{2}+\zeta k^{-1}}{d_{2}-\zeta k^{-1}}
.

First we focus our attention on the case where
k\in\mathbb{R}
.
Using our previous results, we know that the wave-number
k
of the Helmholtz–Korteweg equation is constant if the Korteweg fluid is homogeneous.
Therefore, we can conclude that the qualitative behaviour of the reflection of a plane wave in a Korteweg fluid is the same as in a simple fluid.
Quantitatively, while sound-soft and sound-hard boundary conditions impose the same constraint on the amplitude of the reflected wave in both Korteweg and simple fluids, impedance boundary conditions impose a different constraint on the amplitude of the reflected wave in Korteweg fluids since the wave-number
k
also depends on
u_{1}
, via
\tau_{1}
.

For the nematic Helmholtz–Korteweg equation the situation is more interesting. While sound-soft and sound-hard boundary conditions impose the same constraint on the amplitude of the reflected wave in both Korteweg and nematic Korteweg fluids, impedance boundary conditions have a qualitatively different effect in nematic Korteweg fluids.
From (
54
) we know that the wave-number
k
is smaller when the wave propagates orthogonal to the nematic director field.
Moreover, the amplitude of the reflected wave is smaller when the wave propagates orthogonal to the nematic director field.
Thus we can expect that the absorption of sound caused by the presence of impedance boundary conditions is smaller if the wave-vector is parallel to the nematic director field.

So far we have considered only the case where the wave-number
k
is real. We now turn our attention to the total internal reflection case, described by imaginary wave-numbers.
Assuming now that the lower half-plane is occupied by another Korteweg fluid, as a consequence of the propagation of
S^{+}
we will have a transmitted wave
S^{T}
in the lower half-plane.
The transmitted wave will have wave number
k^{T}=\frac{n_{T}}{n}k
, where
n_{T}
is the refractive index of the lower half-plane and
n
is the refractive index of the upper half-plane.
Using Snell’s law we can compute the direction
\vec{d}^{T}
of the transmitted wave, i.e.

d^{T}_{1}=\sin(\theta)\frac{n}{n_{T}},\qquad d^{T}_{2}=\sqrt{1-\sin^{2}(\theta)\frac{n^{2}}{n_{T}^{2}}},

(59)

where
\theta
is the angle of incidence of
S^{+}
.
When the angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle, i.e.

\theta>\theta_{c},\qquad\theta_{c}=\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{n_{T}}{n}\right),

(60)

the transmitted wave will undergo total internal reflection, i.e.
d_{2}^{T}
will be imaginary and the transmitted wave can be expressed as

S^{T}(\vec{x})=s_{0}e^{i\vec{k}^{T}\cdot\vec{x}},\qquad\vec{k}^{T}=\pm\begin{bmatrix}\sin(\theta)\\
i\sqrt{\alpha}\end{bmatrix}k,

(61)

where
\alpha
is the real number defined as

\frac{\alpha}{k^{2}}\coloneqq\left(\frac{n_{T}}{n}\right)^{2}-\sin^{2}(\theta).

(62)

With nematic fluids, since
n
and
n_{T}
depend on the nematic orientation, these observations suggest it may be possible to control whether reflection occurs with electromagnetic fields, as demonstrated in Figure
2
.

V
Scattering by a circular obstacle

We next consider the scattering of a plane wave by a circular sound soft obstacle, immersed in a nematic Korteweg fluid.
In particular, we will focus our attention on the physical regime associated with nematic liquid crystals where
u_{2}\ll u_{1}
, i.e.

\rho_{0}^{2}u_{1}\approx\ell^{2},\;\rho_{0}^{2}u_{2}\approx\gamma^{-1}\ell^{2},\;\;\text{ with }\gamma\gg 1\gg\ell.

(63)

We can express the sound soft scattering problem as a boundary value problem for the nematic Helmholtz–Korteweg equation (
25
), i.e.

\displaystyle-\omega^{2}S^{+}(\vec{x})

\displaystyle-c_{0}^{2}\Delta S^{+}(\vec{x})+\ell^{2}\Delta^{2}S^{+}(\vec{x})

\displaystyle+\gamma^{-1}\ell^{2}\nabla\!\cdot\!\nabla\left[{\vec{n}\cdot\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}S^{+}}}\vec{n}}\right]\!=\!0\;

\displaystyle\lvert\vec{x}\rvert>1,\;\;\,

\displaystyle S^{+}(\vec{x})

\displaystyle=S^{-}(\vec{x})\qquad

\displaystyle\lvert\vec{x}\rvert=1,\;\;\,

\displaystyle\Delta(S^{+}-S^{-})

\displaystyle+\gamma^{-1}\vec{n}\cdot\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}}}(S^{+}-S^{-})\vec{n}=0

\displaystyle\lvert\vec{x}\rvert=1,\;\;\,

\displaystyle\lvert\partial_{\lvert\vec{x}\rvert}S^{+}(\vec{x})

\displaystyle-ikS^{+}(\vec{x})\rvert=\mathcal{O}(\lvert\vec{x}\rvert^{-\frac{1}{2}})

\displaystyle\lvert\vec{x}\rvert\to\infty,

(64)

where
S^{+}
is the scattered wave and
S^{-}
is the incoming plane wave. The second and third equations in (
V
) are the sound-soft boundary conditions, while the last equation is the Sommerfeld radiation condition, and
k
is one of the real roots of (
50
).
We can consider (
V
) as a perturbation of the scattering problem for the Helmholtz equation.
It is important to notice that the problem is a singularly perturbed one, since the Helmholtz–Korteweg equation is a fourth order partial differential equation which reduces to a second order partial differential equation in the limit
\ell\to 0
, for which only one set of boundary conditions can be imposed.
For this reason we expect a boundary layer to form around the obstacle. To study the boundary layer we introduce the change of variables
\vec{\xi}=\ell\,\vec{x}
, and rewrite the first equation of (
V
) as

\displaystyle-\omega^{2}S^{+}(\vec{\xi})-\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{\ell^{2}}\Delta S^{+}(\vec{\xi})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\Delta^{2}S^{+}(\vec{\xi})

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma^{-1}}{\ell^{2}}\nabla\!\cdot\!\nabla\left[\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}S^{+}}}\vec{n}\cdot\vec{n}\right]\!=\!0.

(65)

As
\ell\to 0
we can consider only the dominant terms in (
V
) to derive a partial differential equation for the boundary layer
S_{\ell}
, i.e.

-c_{0}^{2}\Delta S^{+}_{\ell}(\vec{\xi})+\Delta^{2}S^{+}_{\ell}(\vec{\xi})+\gamma^{-1}\nabla\!\cdot\!\nabla\left[{\vec{n}\cdot\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}S^{+}_{\ell}}}\vec{n}}\right]=0.

(66)

Some solutions of this equation are given by solutions of

-c_{0}S^{+}_{\ell}(\vec{\xi})+\Delta S^{+}_{\ell}(\vec{\xi})+\gamma^{-1}{\vec{n}\cdot\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}S^{+}_{\ell}}}\vec{n}}=0,

(67)

and using the assumption that
\underline{\underline{\nabla\vec{n}}}\equiv 0
on the acoustic length scale and (
76
) this can be rewritten as

-c_{0}S^{+}_{\ell}(\vec{\xi})+\nabla\cdot\left[(I+\gamma^{-1}\vec{n}\otimes\vec{n})\nabla S^{+}_{\ell}\right]=0.

(68)

From this we see that the boundary layer
S^{+}_{\ell}
is governed by a reaction-diffusion equation with a transversally isotropic diffusion tensor
(I+\gamma^{-1}\vec{n}\otimes\vec{n})
.
Using the Vishik–Lyusternik method
Vishik and Lyusternik 1960
we can assume the solution of (
V
) has the form

S^{+}(\vec{x})=S^{+}_{0}(\vec{x})+S^{+}_{\ell}(\vec{\xi}),

(69)

where
S^{+}_{0}(\vec{x})
is the solution of the Helmholtz equation for
\lvert\vec{x}\rvert>1
.
Notice now that imposing the sound soft boundary conditions

\displaystyle\Delta S^{+}+\gamma^{-1}\vec{n}\cdot\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}}}S^{+}\vec{n}

\displaystyle=\Delta S^{+}_{0}+\gamma^{-1}\vec{n}\cdot\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}}}S^{+}_{0}\vec{n}

\displaystyle+\ell^{-2}\left(\Delta S^{+}_{\ell}+\gamma^{-1}\vec{n}\cdot\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}}}S^{+}_{\ell}\vec{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\Delta S^{+}_{0}+\gamma^{-1}\vec{n}\cdot\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}}}S^{+}_{0}\vec{n}+c_{0}\ell^{-2}S^{+}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\Delta S^{-}+\gamma^{-1}\vec{n}\cdot\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}}}S^{-}\vec{n},

(70)

therefore we can conclude that along the boundary of the circular obstacle the boundary layer
S^{+}_{\ell}
has value

S^{+}_{\ell}=\frac{\ell^{2}}{c_{0}}\left(\Delta S^{-}+\gamma^{-1}\vec{n}\cdot\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}}}S^{-}\vec{n}-\Delta S^{+}_{0}-\gamma^{-1}\vec{n}\cdot\underline{\underline{\mathcal{H}}}S^{+}_{0}\vec{n}\right).

(71)

Since we know the value of the boundary layer
S^{+}_{\ell}
on the perimeter of the circular obstacle we can rewrite the
S^{+}
as being an
\mathcal{O}(\ell^{2})
perturbation of the solution of the following Helmholtz scattering problem

\displaystyle-\omega^{2}S^{+}(\vec{x})-c_{0}^{2}\Delta S^{+}(\vec{x})

\displaystyle=0,\qquad

\displaystyle\lvert\vec{x}\rvert>1,

\displaystyle S^{+}(\vec{x})

\displaystyle=S^{-}(\vec{x})+\mathcal{O}(\ell^{2}),\qquad

\displaystyle\lvert\vec{x}\rvert=1,

\displaystyle\lvert\partial_{\lvert\vec{x}\rvert}S^{+}(\vec{x})-ikS^{+}(\vec{x})\rvert

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\lvert\vec{x}\rvert^{-\frac{1}{2}}),\qquad

\displaystyle\lvert\vec{x}\rvert\to\infty.

(72)

The solution of the previous equation can be expressed as a Mie series
Moiola 2024
, i.e.

S^{+}(r,\theta)=-\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{j}\frac{H_{j}^{(1)}(kr)}{H_{j}^{(1)}(kR)}e^{ij\theta},

(73)

where
(r,\theta)
are the polar coordinates of
\vec{x}
,
R
is the radius of the circular obstacle,
H_{j}^{(1)}
are the Hankel functions of the first kind,
k
is the wave number, and
a_{j}
are the coefficients obtained by expanding the incoming plane wave
S^{-}
in circular harmonics via the Jacobi–Anger formula
(
Moiola 2024
, eq. (22))
, i.e.

S^{-}(r,\theta)=e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{j}e^{ij\theta},\qquad a_{j}=i^{j}e^{-ij\psi}J_{j}(kr),

(74)

where
J_{j}
is the Bessel function of the first kind and
\psi
is the angle of incidence of the incoming plane wave.

Notice now that the wave number
k
appearing in (
74
) depends on the incoming plane wave and needs to satisfy the dispersion relation (
50
).
In particular, as discussed in Section
III
, the wave number
k
is smaller when the wave propagates orthogonal to the nematic director field. In regions where the incident plane wave is orthogonal to the nematic director field we can expect the amplitude of the reflected wave to be smaller, given the following asymptotic expansion of
S^{+}

(
Moiola 2024
, eq. (38))
:

S^{+}(r,\theta)=-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi kr}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{j}\frac{e^{i(kr-j\frac{\pi}{2})-\frac{\pi}{4}+j\theta}}{H_{j}^{(1)}(kR)},\qquad r\to\infty.

(75)

This is a striking difference between the behavior of a plane boundary, as discussed in Section
IV
, and the behavior of a circular obstacle. In fact while a change of the amplitude of the reflected wave is expected only for impedance boundary conditions in the case of a plane boundary, in the case of a circular obstacle a reduction of the amplitude of the reflected wave is expected also for sound-soft boundary conditions.

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we have considered the propagation of time-harmonic sound waves in a fluid governed by the Euler–Korteweg equation.
We derived two new equations describing these phenomena, which we name the Helmholtz–Korteweg and nematic Helmholtz–Korteweg equations.

Our analysis yielded new insights into the behavior of plane and evanescent waves and their dependence on both the nematic orientation and boundary conditions.
We also studied the reflection and transmission of plane waves at interfaces, considering different boundary conditions, and highlighted the unique behavior of waves in nematic-Korteweg fluids, including their anisotropy.
Numerical simulations confirmed that the speed of sound is highly dependent on the orientation of the nematic director field.
Finally, we explored the scattering of acoustic waves by circular obstacles, demonstrating that the nematic director field significantly alters the wave amplitude in a manner that is qualitatively distinct from the behavior at plane boundaries.

These results contribute to a deeper understanding of acoustic wave propagation in complex fluids, with potential applications in the design of devices with tunable acoustic properties.
