Potential role of predictive models in assessment of liver inflammation in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma: a two-center cohort study
Authors: Wei Xu, Bolun Li, Huai Gong, Jingdong Li, Zhanwei Yang, Yu Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatic inflammation in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains unclear. This study aimed to construct a clinically expedient predictive model to grade hepatic inflammation in HCC patients.MethodsThis is a two-center retrospective cohort study of HCC patients comprising Derivation cohort and External Validation cohort of 1201 and 505 patients, respectively. Variables of liver inflammation identified through uni- and multi-variate logistic regression analyses were incorporated into predictive nomograms and applied to Derivation cohort, subject to internal and external validation.ResultsLiver fibrosis severity score, portal hypertension severity, and model for end-stage liver disease-sodium independently predicted hepatic inflammation grade. Performance for distinguishing G1 and non-G1 (≥ G2) patients was good with C-index of 0.810 and 0.817 in Derivation and External Validation cohort, respectively. The nomogram performed poorly to predict grade G2, G3 and G2 + G3, but performed well to predict G4.ConclusionsOur nomogram exhibited good performance for scaling hepatic inflammation (G1 and G4) in HCC, and could be employed as adjunctive diagnostic tools to guide HCC management strategy.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Hepatic inflammation, Portal hypertension, MELD-Na

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) poses an increasing disease burden on global health [1]. Chronic inflammation is a major etiologic factor of HCC as it promotes hepatocellular death and mitosis, and predisposes to oncogenesis and metastasis [2, 3]. The severity of inflammation is a prognostic indicator for patients undergoing hepatectomy [4, 5]. Consequently, the biomarkers of hepatic inflammation may facilitate the discovery, clinical investigation, and implementation of immunosuppressive therapies to improve clinical outcomes of patients with or at risk for HCC.

Hepatic inflammation is analyzed by serologic, elastographic, and histopathologic methods. Liver biopsy has been the preferred diagnostic intervention to assess pathological grade in chronic hepatic diseases, but may be confounded by sampling errors, variable inter-examiner interpretations, and intraoperative complications [6]. Safe, accurate, and cost-effective non-invasive tests are needed such as serum markers and imaging modalities. Serum indices have been suggested as surrogate markers of hepatic fibrosis [7]. However, these tests have not been designed to grade inflammation. Whether such biomarkers can be used to measure parenchymal inflammation in patients with HCC remains unclear.

Radiological procedures such as liver stiffness measurement (LSM) obtained by ultrasonic (US) and magnetic resonance (MR) elastography have been considered as non-invasive techniques to quantify inflammation in chronic viral hepatitis [8–12]. However, the clinical utility of these tests is limited by their inaccessibility, an inability to accurately distinguish intermediate grades, and the lack of consensus thresholds.

Therefore, the discovery and clinical validation of biomarkers to grade hepatic inflammation in patients with HCC are of crucial importance. To our knowledge, few studies have addressed these issues. This study aimed to identify clinical markers to quantify hepatic inflammation and generate nomograms to differentiate parenchymal inflammation in HCC patients.

Patients and methods

Study population

This is a cross-sectional study of HCC patients undergoing initial hepatectomy. Cases were ascertained from a longitudinal prospective HCC database of two participating Chinese medical centers to develop nomograms to predict hepatic fibrosis in HCC patients [14]. Ethical compliance and approval, patient enrollment, and information security were described previously [14]. This study followed the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis checklist [13]. Exclusion criteria were (1) preoperative interventions such as local ablation, radiotherapy, transarterial chemoembolization, or targeted/immunotherapy; and (2) insufficient histological data regarding liver inflammation. A total of 1201 consecutive participants presenting to Hunan Provincial People’s Hospital between March 2015 and December 2021 were assigned to the Derivation cohort. External Validation cohort of 505 participants was assembled at Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College from January 2019 to January 2022.

Preoperative evaluation and surgery

Preoperative evaluation and surgical methods have been reported previously [4, 15]. Briefly, we performed retrospective assessment of clinicopathological data from a prospective longitudinal database. For repeated tests, the result from the time closest to surgery was selected. MRI and CT images were acquired from Picture Archiving and Communication Systems. Portal hypertension (PH) was assessed as described in our previous reports [4, 15]. Portal and hepatic vein tumor thromboses were scaled by following standard guidelines [16]. Biliary tract tumor thrombosis was categorized as described previously [17]. HCC staging was conducted according to the AJCC-TNM staging system (8th edition) [18]. Liver fibrosis severity score (LFSS) was calculated as described previously [14].

Pathological analysis

Surgical specimens were submitted for histopathologic evaluation as described previously [14]. Two experienced pathologists without access to clinical records examined each sample autonomously. Grading of inflammation (G0–G4) was restricted to non-neoplastic tissue sampled far from the tumor. G0–1 indicated an absence of inflammation, and G2–4 denoted mild, moderate, or severe inflammation, respectively [19]. Cases diagnosed as intermediate between categories were assigned to the higher group (e.g., G4 for G3–4 and G3 for G2–3). Discrepant results were reconciled through dialog.

Statistical analysis

Uni- and multi-variate ordinal logistic regression analyses were performed to identify variables associated with liver inflammation grades. Predictive models based on variables identified by multivariate analysis were established in the Derivation cohort and subjected to internal and external validation. Model performance was evaluated for discriminative and calibration abilities as previously reported in HCC patients [14]. Statistical analysis was performed as described previously [14].

Chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection was the most prevalent cause of liver disease, including 1034 patients (86.1%) in the Derivation cohort and 421 patients (83.4%) in the External Validation cohort. No G0 patients were identified (Table 1).

Performance of predictive models

Predictive nomograms were evaluated by summation of the three analyzed factors and designated in the X-axis as the probability of a G grade.

G1

The G1 predictive nomogram (Fig. 1a) exhibited good performance for differentiating G1 from non-G1 (≥ G2) patients (Fig. 1b, 1c). Area under receiver operating characteristic curves (AUROCs) to predict G1 in the Derivation and External Validation cohorts were 0.807 (95% CI 0.748–0.867) and 0.892 (95% CI, 0.793–0.990), respectively. AUROCs for G1 prediction from Derivation cohort data for internal validation were 0.805 (95% CI 0.701–0.910) for the development dataset (D-set) and 0.717 (95% CI 0.617–0.826) for the validation dataset (V-set), with a C-index of 0.810 (95% CI 0.745–0.869) (Fig. 1d). Consistency between prevalence and projection of G1 was good, as evidenced by GiViTI calibration and the Hosmer–Lemeshow (HL) test (χ2 = 10.972; p = 0.203), exhibiting good fit (Fig. 1e).

Internal validation of the External Validation cohort data yielded AUROCs for G1 prediction of 0.794 (95% CI 0.675–0.894) for the D-set and 0.718 (95% CI 0.608–0.820) for the V-set, with a C-index of 0.817 (95% CI 0.704–0.881) (Fig. 1f). GiViTI calibration and the H–L test confirmed robust nomogram performance (χ2 = 5.183; p = 0.738) (Fig. 1g).

G2

The G2 predictive nomogram (Fig. 2a) displayed fairly good differentiation of G2 from non-G2. The Derivation and External Validation cohorts displayed AUROCs of 0.730 (95% CI 0.681–0.778) and 0.764 (95% CI 0.701–0.828), respectively (Fig. 2b, c).

AUROCs for G2 prediction calculated by bootstrap analysis during internal validation of Derivation cohort data were 0.763 (95% CI 0.685–0.842) for the D-set and 0.735 (95% CI 0.655–0.815) for the V-set, respectively. The C-index was 0.610 (95% CI 0.558–0.662), disclosing poor differentiation of G2 from non-G2 (Fig. 2d). Consistency between G2 prevalence and projection was good, as indicated by GiViTI calibration and an HL Chi-squared test value of 9.473 (P = 0.304) (Fig. 2e). AUROCs obtained for the internal validation of G2 prediction in the External Validation cohort were 0.756 (95% CI 0.673–0.839) for the D-set and 0.768 (95% CI 0.693–0.844) for the V-set, respectively. However, consistency between G3 prevalence and projection was good, as evidenced by GiViTI calibration and an HL test value of 6.490 (P = 0.593) (Fig. 3e).

Discussion

To our knowledge, in this study we present the first large cohort investigation to determine predictive factors and construct nomograms to grade hepatic inflammation in patients with HCC.

HCC is a highly heterogeneous and immunogenic malignancy. HCC can secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby triggering inflammatory cascades [20]. Paraneoplastic crosstalk between HCC and chronic hepatitis of diverse etiologies may exacerbate local inflammation. Consequently, pathologic findings may reveal a superposition of two processes: a predisposing chronic hepatitis complicated by tumor-related inflammation [21]. Therefore, accurate grading of background inflammation may be more difficult in patients with HCC than in those without HCC. In this study, we examined varied tumor-related factors, but found that they had no impact on inflammation grade. An association between paraneoplastic factors and inflammation grade seems hypothetically plausible, but was not supported by our results; consequently, no tumor-related factors were incorporated into the nomograms.

Serum liver functional biomarkers have been recommended as surrogate markers for hepatocellular injury to facilitate treatment of chronic HBV infection [22]. However, elevations of these markers are nonspecific. None of the serum markers evaluated in our study could accurately diagnose G1 or G2. Furthermore, most indices performed poorly for the diagnosis of G3 or G4, with AUROCs below 0.7. The differentiation of adjacent G grades G2, G3, or G4 remains a perplexing challenge. As shown in Table 4, none of the indices were diagnostic for the G1 + G2, G2 + G3, or G3 + G4 aggregates. Consequently, no serum indices evaluated in this study, either alone or in combination, can be recommended as useful surrogates to grade liver inflammation in patients with HCC.

HBV infection was the primary HCC risk factor in our cohort. An unexpected finding was the dissociation of quantitative markers of HBV infection (e.g., HBsAg quantity and HBV-DNA) in the multivariate analysis. This finding suggests that sustained HBV suppression achieved through antiviral treatment does not necessarily reduce necroinflammation. Consequently, we propose that a low or undetectable HBV-DNA level is not a reliable index of reduced liver inflammation in HCC, in contrast to its value in monitoring the course of chronic viral hepatitis [23]. Management strategies for chronic liver diseases of other etiologies may not be applicable to HCC.

Neither MRI nor CT, even with multi-parametric imaging, can grade hepatic inflammation reliably [24]. Excluding patients with various extents of liver fibrosis, LSM seems effective for grading hepatic inflammation during chronic HBV infection [8]. However, this observation may be irrelevant in patients with HCC because of highly prevalent comorbid liver fibrosis. LSM values may be confounded by multiple factors such as device-related technical factors, obesity, inflammation, operator inexperience, discrepancies between biopsy sites and tumor locations, and sampling variation [25, 26]. Our two centers do not have access to LSM using MR or US elastography. Consequently, we are motivated to find indices to grade liver inflammation based on routine radiological modalities used in HCC management.

Liver fibrosis and inflammation are often concurrent [27, 28]. Therefore, we hypothesized that morphological features such as increased parenchymal stiffness and surface nodularity that are indicators of advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis may be useful markers of chronic inflammation. In this study, LFSS achieved poor AUC for G3 and fair for G4, and failed to discriminate intermediate grades G2 or G1 + G2. Diagnostic accuracies for G2 + G3 and G3 + G4 were reduced compared to G3 or G4, respectively, suggesting that combining intermediate grades does not improve diagnostic utility, and hepatic inflammation grades and fibrosis stages may be independent. These findings echo our previous report that inflammation grade and fibrosis stage were unrelated in HCC patients [14].

Our multi-parametric model exhibited good diagnostic accuracy for grades G1 and G4, but could not differentiate intermediate grades. Thus, we believe that the development of an accurate diagnostic modality to identify grades G2 and G3 may be of substantial clinical value. Because liver inflammation grades may overlap as observed in liver fibrosis stages, we classified G2 and G3 as an analytic category (91.3% of total), but found poor discriminative performance. Furthermore, the combining of G1 and G2, and G3 and G4 did not add diagnostic utility.

Our previous study identified MELD-Na as independent predictor of liver fibrosis stage in HCC patients [14]. In current study, MELD-Na was significant in multilogistical ordinal analysis, suggesting that liver inflammation grade can be partially reflected by fibrosis severity, although this association was limited to G4.

Indices of circulating immune effector cells may facilitate the grading of tumor-associated inflammatory responses. These include neutrophil-to-lymphocyte, platelet-to-lymphocyte and monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratios [29–32]. Neutrophils function as crucial pro-inflammatory mediators of tumor-microenvironment cross-talk [30]. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio may serve as a metric for monitoring local and systemic inflammation [30]. In current study, we examined several indices of circulating immune effector cells, but none were included in the final model. A possible explanation may be that we did not assay their subpopulations. Furthermore, we did not examine inflammatory infiltrates of HCCs. A proteomic study of inflamed perilesional tissue may facilitate the examination of cellular networks and provide a reference to study disordered communication related to HCC.

The strengths of this study include the interrogation of a real-world HCC dataset. The large cohorts and consecutive enrollment enabled sufficient representation of HCC clinical characteristics. Our study design permitted the association of particular factors with liver inflammation grades. The external validation of our nomogram and the concordant results of the two participating centers suggest the reproducibility of our findings even in populations with different in baseline characteristics. Therefore, we suggest that our findings can be generalized and integrated into clinical practice.

The histopathologic grading of inflammation in resected tissue may have strengthened the validity of our conclusions. The pitfalls of histological interpretation of percutaneous liver biopsy specimens were thus avoided. A final major strength was the deep analysis of our predictive model’s discrimination of inflammation grades.

The limitations of our study include observational retrospective design that might be predisposed to selection bias, potentially leading to disproportionate groupings of participants with particular inflammatory grades. However, selection bias may have been mitigated by the ample enrollment number and the consideration of validated predictors of inflammation. Second, all participants were Chinese, and a majority carried the HCC risk factor of chronic HBV infection. International collaborative studies conducted prospectively in large and ethnically diverse populations with HCC of other causes will be imperative to validate the broad clinical utility of our nomogram. Third, a quantitative assessment of circulating immune effector cell subpopulations should be explored as a surrogate marker for liver parenchymal inflammation.

Conclusions

We present novel predictive nomograms to facilitate the grading of hepatic inflammation (G1 and G4) in patients with HCC. These models exhibited good diagnostic performance, and can be implemented as adjunctive tests to guide clinical HCC management. However, the discrimination of intermediate liver inflammation grades remains a challenge that deserves further studies.
