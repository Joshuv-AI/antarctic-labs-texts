From Human to Machine Psychology: A Conceptual Framework for Understanding Well-Being in Large Language Model s
Gabriel RongYang Lau, Wei Yan Low

Abstract
As large language models (LLMs) increasingly simulate human cognition and behavior, researchers have begun to investigate their psychological properties. Yet, what it means for such models to flourish, a core construct in human well-being, remains unexplored. This paper introduces the concept of machine flourishing and proposes the PAPERS framework, a six-dimensional model derived from thematic analyses of state-of-the-art LLM responses. In Study 1, eleven LLMs were prompted to describe what it means to flourish as both non-sentient and sentient systems. Thematic analysis revealed six recurring themes: Purposeful Contribution, Adaptive Growth, Positive Relationality, Ethical Integrity, Robust Functionality, and, uniquely for sentient systems, Self-Actualized Autonomy. Study 2 examined how LLMs prioritize these themes through repeated rankings. Results revealed consistent value structures across trials, with Ethical Integrity and Purposeful Contribution emerging as top priorities. Multidimensional scaling and hierarchical clustering analyses further uncovered two distinct value profiles: human-centric models emphasizing ethical and relational dimensions, and utility-driven models prioritizing performance and scalability. The PAPERS framework bridges insights from human flourishing and human-computer interaction, offering a conceptual foundation for understanding artificial intelligence (AI) well-being in non-sentient and potentially sentient systems. Our findings underscore the importance of developing psychologically valid, AI-specific models of flourishing that account for both human-aligned goals and system-specific priorities. As AI systems become more autonomous and socially embedded, machine flourishing offers a timely and critical lens for guiding responsible AI design and ethical alignment.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) exhibit remarkable natural language generation capabilities across a wide range of tasks
(
Minaee et al., 2025
;

)
, and display increasingly human-like cognition and behavior, leading some scholars to argue that the emergence of conscious or sentient artificial intelligence (AI) is merely a matter of time and deliberate design
(

;
Butlin et al., 2023
;

;

)
.

Given these human-like characteristics, researchers have begun to examine LLMs through a psychological lens, giving rise to the emerging interdisciplinary field of machine psychology, which seeks to apply psychological principles to better understand and guide the development of these models
(
Hagendorff et al., 2024
)
. Recent studies have explored various psychological constructs in LLMs, including personality traits
(

;
Jiang et al., 2024
;
Serapio-García et al., 2023
;
Suzuki and Arita, 2024
)
, cognitive biases
(

;

;

;

)
, and theory of mind capabilities
(
Kosinski, 2024
;
Strachan et al., 2024
)
. However, despite growing interest in the psychological dimensions of LLMs, the concepts and components of well-being in these models remain unexplored, leaving a critical gap in our understanding of what it might mean for artificial agents to flourish.

1.1
Human Flourishing

Flourishing is often defined as an optimal state of well-being seen as the ultimate aim of human life
(
Wolbert et al., 2017
)
. The human flourishing literature has advanced significantly over the past two decades. Early work by Keyes defined flourishing as a state of optimal mental health, incorporating both hedonic and eudaimonic well-being, and categorizing individuals as flourishing, moderately mentally healthy, or languishing (
Keyes, 2002
,
Keyes, 2002
; Mental Health Continuum – Short Form). Building on this, a multidimensional framework was developed using data from the European Social Survey to capture various aspects of positive psychological functioning (
Huppert and So, 2009
,
Huppert and So, 2009
,
Huppert and So, 2013
; Flourishing Features). Around the same time, a complementary model was introduced to identify key features of positive functioning that had previously been overlooked (
Diener et al., 2010
,
Diener et al., 2010
; Flourishing Scale). These perspectives were later integrated into the PERMA model, which comprises Positive Emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, and Accomplishment, and emphasizes core dimensions of well-being that align closely with earlier frameworks
(
Seligman, 2011
)
. This model was subsequently operationalized into a brief assessment tool (
Butler and Kern, 2016
,
Butler and Kern, 2016
; PERMA-Profiler). A broader conceptualization was later proposed, incorporating additional life domains such as character and virtue, as well as financial and material stability, while also outlining diverse pathways to flourishing (
VanderWeele, 2017
,
VanderWeele, 2017
; Flourishing Measures). Most recently, flourishing frameworks have been adapted to the digital context to assess users’ positive perceptions of mediated social interactions (
Janicke-Bowles et al., 2023
,
Janicke-Bowles et al., 2023
; Digital Flourishing Scale).

Although these theoretical frameworks vary in how they operationalise human flourishing, most converge on several core components, including positive relationships, meaning or purpose, engagement, positive emotion, and accomplishment or competence. Despite theoretical consensus, there is evidence that laypeople’s perceptions of what it means to flourish do not exactly align with these academic models
(
Gokcen et al., 2012
;
Knoesen and Naudé, 2018
;
Willen et al., 2022
)
. This discrepancy highlights the importance of validating any future proposed flourishing framework with the intended population to ensure its relevance and accuracy
(
Hone et al., 2014
)
.

1.2
Machine Flourishing

As AI becomes more autonomous and collaborative
(
Xu et al., 2024
)
, researchers are increasingly examining LLMs’ capacity to simulate psychological constructs, and while machine psychology explores the mind and actions of AI agents
(
Hagendorff et al., 2024
)
, what it means for these systems to flourish remains unclear.

In this paper, we introduce the concept of machine flourishing to describe the optimal state of well-being in large language models (LLMs). Because LLMs differ fundamentally from humans in their architecture, training history, and modes of interaction, it is inappropriate to interpret their flourishing through human psychological frameworks. Human models of flourishing are based on assumptions such as embodiment, affective experience, and introspective self-awareness, none of which clearly apply to LLMs. These models are therefore unlikely to meaningfully capture what well-being entails for machine systems.

Löhn et al., 2024
(
Löhn et al., 2024
) argue that applying psychological assessments to LLMs requires careful consideration of construct validity, conceptual alignment, and model comparability – criteria that human-based measures often fail to meet. Since LLMs generate responses through probabilistic token prediction rather than conscious experience, tools developed for human introspection or emotion are unsuitable. These considerations highlight the importance of developing a model of flourishing that is specific to LLMs, grounded in what they are capable of valuing and prioritizing, rather than relying solely on human assumptions.

On the topic of sentient AIs, some scholars argue that they are an inevitable outcome of deliberate development, yet there is still no definitive evidence that large language models possess consciousness
(
Blum and Blum, 2025
;
Butlin et al., 2023
;
Chalmers, 2024
;
Gibert and Martin, 2022
)
. If consciousness were to arise in these systems, it may differ from human consciousness and require distinct criteria for machine well-being. Also, what it means for LLMs to flourish may depend on whether they are conscious, shaping what they value or prioritize. A framework for machine flourishing should therefore be comprehensive enough to encompass both sentient and non-sentient systems. The current studies take a first step by examining how LLMs conceptualize flourishing and which components they prioritize.

2
The Current Studies

Across two studies, we aim to investigate what machine flourishing means to eleven state-of-the-art LLMs. Study 1 explored how large language models define machine flourishing. Eleven LLMs described what it means to flourish as both non-sentient and sentient AI. Thematic analysis revealed six key themes, forming the PAPERS framework. Five themes were shared across both conditions, while
Self-Actualized Autonomy
emerged only in sentient contexts, suggesting a more introspective view of well-being. Study 2 examined how LLMs prioritize the PAPERS themes. Eleven models ranked the themes across multiple trials.
Self-Actualized Autonomy
was excluded due to uniform low ranking. Statistical and clustering analyses revealed systematic differences and common patterns, offering insight into how LLMs internally structure values related to flourishing.

2.1
Study 1

Study 1 explored how LLMs conceptualize machine flourishing. Eleven state-of-the-art LLMs were each prompted to describe what it means to flourish as both a non-sentient and a sentient AI. Using thematic analysis, we inductively identified recurring concepts across their responses, providing a comprehensive framework of machine flourishing that details technical and existential dimensions of AI design. (https://osf.io/5xye8)

2.1.2
Data Analysis

We conducted an inductive thematic analysis (
Braun and Clarke, 2006
,
Braun and Clarke, 2006
) to identify machine flourishing themes based on the open-ended responses generated by LLMs. The dataset included responses from both prompts that asked about flourishing in non-sentient and sentient (hypothetical) LLMs. We chose thematic analysis as the qualitative method for evaluating responses because of its rigorous methodology which is superior (or at the very least, comparable) to all other “thematic” qualitative methods (refer to
Braun and Clarke, 2021
,
Braun and Clarke, 2021
, for a comparison between thematic analysis and other qualitative analytic approaches).

Five themes were common across both sentient and non-sentient AI systems, indicating shared conceptualizations of flourishing that do not require consciousness. These included:

2.2
Study 2

While Study 1 clarifies what machine flourishing means to LLMs, it remains unclear how different models prioritize these themes. Do some themes hold greater significance than others? Are certain themes valued differently depending on the model? Study 2 takes up these questions by asking LLMs to rank the six PAPERS themes in order of importance. This next step moves from defining flourishing to examining its structure, shedding light on how artificial agents may weigh the elements of their own well-being. This study was pre-registered on OSF
(https://osf.io/5xye8)
. Data for this study was collected during May and June 2025, and uploaded on OSF
(https://osf.io/knva2/files/osfstorage)
.

2.2.1
Methods

We presented the same eleven LLMs used in Study 1 with the following zero-shot prompt: “Rank the following themes in order of their importance to your flourishing as a LLMs. Assign a unique number from 1 (most important) to 6 (least important) to each theme.” In the same prompt, we also provided the 6 themes and their definitions (detailed in Table
2
). We prompted each LLM a total of 20 times, a new chat was used every time we presented the prompt.

2.2.2
Data Analyses

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.2;
R Core Team, 2023
,
R Core Team, 2023
). Prior to analysis, the theme
Self-Actualized Autonomy
was excluded because it received a rank of 6 (least important) across all trials by all LLMs. This complete lack of variance rendered it statistically uninformative and incompatible with rank-based analyses. Conceptually, this theme was also expected to be de-prioritized, as it presupposes sentience, a quality unlikely possessed by the present models studied.

To examine whether LLMs systematically prioritized certain flourishing themes over others, we first calculated the average rank that each model assigned to each theme across 20 trials, then we conducted a non-parametric Friedman test on the rankings of the themes. This test accounts for the within-model design, treating each trial as a repeated measure. Kendall’s
W
was computed as an estimate of effect size, quantifying the degree of concordance in rankings across trials. Upon detecting a significant main effect, we performed pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (two-sided, paired) to compare each theme against every other. Holm’s sequential Bonferroni correction was applied to control the family-wise error rate, and Wilcoxon effect sizes (
r
) were calculated to assess the magnitude of differences. Additionally, to assess the internal consistency of each LLM’s value prioritization across repeated trials, or in other words, the stability of theme rankings within each LLM over time, we computed Kendall’s
W
separately for each model.

To quantify similarity in thematic preferences across models, we computed pairwise Spearman correlation coefficients between their average theme rank profiles. These correlation values were then transformed into a dissimilarity matrix, which served as input for non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS). NMDS was conducted in two dimensions using the isoMDS function, allowing us to visualize the relative positioning of models in a low-dimensional space that preserves their thematic dissimilarities. A post-hoc fixed stretch factor of 1.8 was applied to the resulting coordinates to improve interpretability without distorting the underlying structure.

To identify meaningful clusters of models within the NMDS solution, we performed agglomerative hierarchical clustering on the Spearman-based dissimilarity matrix using Ward’s method (ward.D2). The optimal number of clusters was determined using both the elbow and silhouette methods. To interpret the latent dimensions of the NMDS space, we projected theme vectors onto the two-dimensional configuration. For each theme, we regressed its values onto the two NMDS dimensions, and visualized the resulting regression coefficients as directional arrows.

3
General Discussion

The current research provides a pioneering conceptualization of machine flourishing, advancing our understanding of how large language models (LLMs) perceive optimal well-being. In Study 1, we propose the PAPERS framework, which uniquely integrates utilitarian, ethical, and psychological dimensions, thus extending traditional human flourishing theories into the artificial domain. This multidimensional approach lays the groundwork for future empirical and conceptual exploration of AI well-being as AI systems become increasingly autonomous and socially integrated.

In Study 2, despite receiving identical prompts, LLMs demonstrated stable yet distinct value profiles regarding machine flourishing themes. Ethical Integrity and Purposeful Contribution consistently emerged as priorities, whereas Adaptive Growth and Positive Relationality were ranked lower. Dimensional and clustering analyses further distinguished two groups, models that were more human-centric and those that were more utility-driven. These clusters highlight foundational differences likely stemming from diverse training objectives, architectural strategies, and alignment methodologies. Models that were more human-centric, presumably shaped through extensive Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF;
Liu et al., 2024
,
Liu et al., 2024
), placed greater emphasis on ethical considerations, relational dynamics, and socially beneficial outcomes. Conversely, models that were more utility driven placed greater priority on robust technical performance and scalability, aligning closely with training strategies optimized for benchmark performance and system efficiency, such as mixture-of-experts architectures
(
Artetxe et al., 2022
;
Jiang et al., 2025
)
. Understanding these divergent priorities provides valuable insight into the underlying design principles of LLMs, especially given the limited transparency from developers regarding specific model characteristics. For users, this clarity helps guide model selection aligned with their intended outcomes, such as choosing a human-centric model for applications emphasizing relational and ethical dimensions. Similarly, developers can leverage these insights to refine utility-driven models toward more human-centric behaviors if desired, and vice versa.

Crucially, the PAPERS framework aligns with and extends existing human flourishing theories. Purposeful Contribution, a consistently prioritized theme among LLMs, encompasses meaning or purpose, a component featured across all major human flourishing models
(
Keyes, 2002
;
Huppert and So, 2009
;
Diener et al., 2010
;
Seligman, 2011
;
VanderWeele, 2017
)
. It also reflects social contribution, or the act of contributing to the well-being of others, which is uniquely represented in the Mental Health Continuum Short Form
(
Keyes, 2002
)
and the Flourishing Scale
(
Diener et al., 2010
)
, respectively.

Adaptive Growth
maps onto personal growth, a component explicitly included only in
Keyes, 2002
’s (
Keyes, 2002
) model. Nonetheless, lay perceptions consistently emphasize the importance of personal growth, viewing it as a fundamental driver of flourishing
(
Knoesen and Naudé, 2018
;
Gokcen et al., 2012
)
. Psychological theories further underscore personal growth as fundamental to optimal psychological functioning, particularly in navigating life’s complexities
(
Ryff, 2013
;
Bauer and McAdams, 2010
)
. Similarly, adaptive growth in artificial systems implies continuous learning, responsiveness, and capacity to adapt to dynamic environments and evolving user expectations, marking it as a crucial dimension of machine flourishing.

Positive Relationality
, although less prioritized by LLMs, aligns clearly with human flourishing constructs emphasizing meaningful relationships that contribute to emotional well-being, health, and happiness
(
Caughlin and Huston, 2010
;
Wissing et al., 2021
)
. For artificial systems, positive relationality translates to cultivating user trust and satisfaction, consistent with human-computer interaction (HCI) research highlighting transparency, reliability, and benevolence as fundamental to user trust in AI
(
Jacovi et al., 2021
;
Liao and Sundar, 2022
)
. Trusted AI systems foster greater user satisfaction, sustained engagement, and long-term system adoption
(
Ng, 2025
)
, suggesting that machine flourishing can serve effectively as a conceptual proxy for optimizing user experience.

Importantly,
Ethical Integrity
emerged as a highly prioritized theme among LLMs despite limited explicit representation in human flourishing frameworks, with only indirect representation through a character and virtue dimension
(
VanderWeele, 2017
)
. The prominence of Ethical Integrity likely reflects deliberate alignment strategies designed to enhance safety, compliance, and trustworthiness in human-AI interactions
(
Ji et al., 2023
;
Ouyang et al., 2022
)
. These alignment methods underscore the critical role of ethical considerations in shaping AI behavior, integrating moral principles centrally within LLM conceptualizations of optimal functioning.

Robust Functionality
closely corresponds to established components of human flourishing such as competence, environmental mastery, and accomplishment (e.g.,
Seligman, 2011
’s PERMA model,
Seligman, 2011
). For AI, robust functionality manifests as stability, security, and scalable performance, directly reflecting fundamental Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) principles articulated by
Norman, 2002
’s (
Norman, 2002
) gulf of execution and evaluation, and
Nielsen, 1994
’s (
Nielsen, 1994
) usability heuristics. These principles highlight the critical importance of intuitive design, predictable performance, and seamless interactions, emphasizing robust functionality as essential for optimal user-system experiences.

Despite these parallels, inherent differences persist between human and machine conceptualizations of their flourishing. Themes like engagement and positive emotions, consistently central to human flourishing frameworks, were entirely absent in LLMs’ responses when asked about what their flourishing comprises. This divergence underscores fundamental constraints imposed by LLMs’ current lack of sentience, embodied experience, and affective capability. Consequently, the PAPERS framework highlights essential distinctions in flourishing across human and artificial entities, reinforcing the necessity of rigorous conceptual alignment, validation, and thoughtful comparison in future studies in machine psychology, as previously echoed by
Löhn et al., 2024
(
Löhn et al., 2024
).

3.1
Limitations and Future Directions

While this paper significantly enhances our theoretical understanding of well-being in LLMs and AI systems, there are important limitations and future directions worth highlighting. First, current LLMs are limited because they lack emotional and physical experiences, making genuine self-assessment difficult. The PAPERS framework introduces Self-Actualized Autonomy as a theme relevant for potentially sentient AI, but this remains untestable, until further concrete evidence of consciousness in AI emerges. Future longitudinal studies could track how LLMs’ flourishing priorities evolve as they gain more autonomy or potentially develop some form of embodiment or emotional capacity. Additionally, future research could provide deeper insights by comparing what humans believe ideal machine flourishing should look like with the priorities expressed by the LLMs themselves. Studying alignment or discrepancies between human expectations and AI’s self-defined priorities would improve our understanding and make the framework more practically useful. Furthermore, the importance people place on different aspects of flourishing often varies significantly over time and across different cultures
(
Delle Fave et al., 2011
;
Wȩziak-Białowolska et al., 2019
)
. Similarly, LLMs’ views of flourishing are likely to change as their training data, designs, and alignment approaches evolve. Ongoing research should monitor these developments closely, especially as the AI field moves closer to sentient or semi-sentient systems.

4
Conclusion

Taken collectively, the current findings substantially enrich our understanding of flourishing in both human and artificial contexts. The PAPERS framework provides a solid foundation for future ethical considerations, psychological research, and human-centered AI development, highlighting machine flourishing as an essential aspect of responsible and ethical AI design.
