Preparation and characterization of self-healing nanocomposite hydrogels based on chitosan/polyethylene glycol/bacterial nanocellulose for wound dressing applications
Authors: Shiva Soori, Niloofar Eslahi, Reza Jahanmardi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydrogels used for wound dressing applications often suffer from limited mechanical stability and insufficient self-repair capability, which restricts their long-term performance in dynamic physiological environments. This research aimed to prepare self-healing nanocomposite hydrogels based on chitosan, polyethylene glycol (PEG), and bacterial nanocellulose (BNC). For this purpose, PEG was first modified by an aldehyde agent, resulting in benzaldehyde-functionalized PEG (BM-PEG), to deliver bonding potential with the amine groups of chitosan. Nanocomposite hydrogels were then prepared by vortexing at different compositions with BNC as a reinforcing agent. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analysis confirmed the successful formation of chemical bonds between the components. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) micrographs revealed that incorporating BNC up to an optimal percentage increased the average pore size of the hydrogels and improved their compressive strength and storage modulus. Self-healing behavior was qualitatively evaluated through a cut-and-heal test, showing visible rebonding of the hydrogel networks. Moreover, the incorporation of BNC into the hydrogels increased the rate of self-healing, making the cutoff hardly detectable. The suitable biocompatibility and cell adhesion of the fabricated hydrogels were demonstrated through in vitro cell studies. Accordingly, nanocomposite hydrogels have been suggested as favorable candidates for wound dressing–related biomedical applications.

Introduction

Hydrogels are three-dimensional networks of polymers capable of retaining large amounts of water and body fluids while maintaining their structural integrity1. Depending on their constituents, they can demonstrate unique properties, including high water content, biocompatibility, and tunable mechanical characteristics2. Thus, hydrogels are widely investigated for biomedical applications, including drug delivery, tissue engineering, and wound dressings3. In wound care, particularly for burn injuries and chronic wounds, an ideal dressing should maintain a moist environment, conform to irregular wound geometries, protect the wound bed, and support cell attachment and tissue regeneration. However, conventional hydrogels often suffer from limited mechanical stability and deterioration under physiological conditions, which can restrict their long-term performance4.

In recent years, inspired by natural repair mechanisms, self-healing hydrogels have gained increasing attention because they can restore their structure after damage and extend their functional lifetime5–7. These materials typically rely on reversible interactions, such as dynamic covalent bonds or physical interactions, which enable network reconstruction after mechanical disruption. Hybrid hydrogels composed of natural and synthetic polymers are particularly attractive, as they combine biocompatibility with improved mechanical performance8,9. Among these, polyethylene glycol (PEG) is widely used because of its hydrophilicity, flexibility, and ability to form stable hydrated networks10. Recent studies have demonstrated that PEG-based hydrogels can exhibit self-healing behavior and support biological activity through dynamic crosslinking strategies. For example, Xie et al. reported PEG-based hydrogels with self-healing capability and enhanced L929 fibroblast cell migration11.

Chitosan, derived from the deacetylation of chitin, is another key component for biomedical hydrogels due to its inherent biocompatibility and bioactivity12. Its amino and hydroxyl groups enable crosslinking and contribute to hydrogel stability13,14. Chitosan-based hydrogels have been widely explored in wound-related and biomedical applications, where dynamic or stimuli-responsive bonding interactions can provide structural adaptability and controlled biological functionality15–18. For instance, Guo et al. developed chitosan-based hydrogels with dynamic interactions that exhibited self-healing behavior and promising wound healing performance19.

The combination of PEG and chitosan has been widely investigated to construct dynamic hydrogel networks. In particular, aldehyde-functionalized PEG can react with chitosan through Schiff-base reactions, forming reversible imine bonds that enable self-healing behavior20,21. These dynamic networks offer a balance between mechanical stability and adaptability, which is essential for biomedical applications. However, achieving sufficient mechanical strength while preserving self-healing capability remains a challenge.

To address this limitation, nanomaterials are often incorporated into hydrogel networks to enhance their performance. Cellulose nanomaterials are especially attractive due to their high surface area, mechanical strength, and biocompatibility22. Among them, bacterial nanocellulose (BNC) has gained particular attention because of its fibrous structure, biodegradability, and reinforcing ability23,24. BNC can improve network connectivity and mechanical stability while maintaining the dynamic nature of the hydrogel system, making it a promising component for advanced hydrogel design25.

Although dynamic PEG/chitosan hydrogels and nanocellulose-reinforced systems have been reported, the literature suggests that achieving a balanced combination of self-healing ability, mechanical robustness, cytocompatibility, and performance in wound dressing applications remains challenging26. Based on these considerations, this study aims to develop self-healing nanocomposite hydrogels based on chitosan, benzaldehyde-functionalized PEG, and BNC for wound dressing–related applications. The effects of BNC incorporation on gelation behavior, swelling, morphology, chemical structure, mechanical performance, self-healing behavior, cytocompatibility, and cell adhesion were systematically investigated. In particular, the role of BNC nanofibrillar reinforcement within a dynamic Schiff-base network is systematically evaluated, providing insight into structure–property relationships relevant to self-healing wound dressing materials.

Experimental

Materials

PEG (Mw = 6000 g/mol), formyl benzoic acid, 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP), N,N′-dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCC), tetrahydrofuran (THF), diethyl ether, and acetic acid were purchased from Merck (Germany). Chitosan (medium molecular weight, 190,000–310,000 Da, 75–85% deacetylated), potassium nitrate, and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) tablets were purchased from Sigma‒Aldrich (USA). An oxidized bacterial cellulose nanofiber (BNC) gel (2%) (formula: (C6H10O5)n, average diameter: 20–50 nm, purity ≥ 99%) was purchased from Nano Novin Polymer Co. (Iran).

Preparation of benzaldehyde-functionalized PEG

Two grams of PEG and 150 mL of THF were added to a three-necked flask equipped with a nitrogen flow and a condenser and immersed in an oil bath placed on a magnetic stirring heating plate. The mixture was stirred at 50 °C for one hour until the PEG was completely dissolved. The mixture was cooled to room temperature. Subsequently, 0.24 g of formyl benzoic acid and 0.049 g of DMAP were added to the mixture, and the mixture was stirred for an additional 2 h. Next, 0.41 g of DCC was added to the flask, and the stirring process was continued for 24 h. The entire reaction process was carried out under a nitrogen atmosphere. When the reaction was complete, a homogeneous, milky polymer solution formed. The obtained solution was concentrated via a Hei-VAP Advantage rotary evaporator (Heidolph, Germany) at 50 °C and 80 rpm, precipitated in diethyl ether, and dried under vacuum at 40 °C for 24 h27. The resulting benzaldehyde-modified PEG (BM-PEG) was synthesized as a telechelic PEG bearing benzaldehyde end groups, intended to provide aldehyde sites for dynamic Schiff-base crosslinking with chitosan.

Preparation of hydrogels

To prepare the hydrogels, solutions of BM-PEG and chitosan were first prepared. Accordingly, 2, 4, 6, and 8 wt% BM-PEG in deionized (DI) water solutions were prepared for 4 h at room temperature. Next, a 4 wt% chitosan solution was prepared by mixing chitosan in 0.1 M acetic acid for 4 h at room temperature. Once these solutions were obtained, they were mixed in an ice‒water bath (at 0 °C) and vortexed until gel formation occurred. To achieve better dispersion, all subsequent hydrogel formations in this study were performed in an ice–water bath, which slows down the rapid Schiff-base crosslinking reaction and provides sufficient time for more controlled mixing and more uniform distribution of BNC within the hydrogel network. To determine the optimal content of the materials in the hydrogel samples, different mixtures of chitosan and BM-PEG solutions at varying concentrations were prepared, as shown in Table 1.

Preparation of nanocomposite hydrogels containing BNC

After selecting the optimal sample, nanocomposite hydrogels containing varying contents of BNC (0, 0.25, 0.5, 1 and 2%) were prepared. To prepare hydrogel samples containing BNC, after dissolving BM-PEG in water, a predetermined volume of BNC was added to the solution. The mixtures were stirred at 700 rpm via a magnetic stirrer for 20 min. Similar to the fabrication method for hydrogels, the solutions were subsequently vortexed with a chitosan solution, resulting in nanocomposite hydrogels. Characterization of hydrogels

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed to study the chemical structure of the hydrogel samples. For this analysis, the samples were first freeze-dried for 48 h. The spectra were recorded via a Tensor 27 FTIR spectrometer from Bruker (Germany) in the wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm− 1.

X-ray diffraction

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed to evaluate the crystalline structure of the nanocomposite hydrogel samples. The samples were freeze-dried for 48 h before the test and analyzed via an EQUINOX 3000 X-ray diffractometer manufactured by Inel (France).

Rheometry test

Rheological studies were performed by using an RS6000 model rheometer (Anton Paar Ltd., Austria) at a temperature of 25 °C. The gap was set to 1 mm. The storage modulus (Gʹ) and loss modulus (Gʺ) were measured for the hydrogel discs with a diameter of 25 mm. The test was performed at a 1% strain angular frequency (ω), ranging from 0.1 to 100 rad/s.

Compression test

The mechanical properties of the prepared hydrogel samples were analyzed via compression mode via an ASTM150 Universal Tensile Testing Machine from Santam (Iran). The samples were cut into cylinders with a diameter of 5 mm and a thickness of 200 micrometres. Accordingly, the samples were first subjected to a compressive strain of up to 50%, and then the stress was reduced at a constant strain rate of 10% per minute.

Swelling ratio

To investigate the swelling behavior of the hydrogel samples, the samples were first dried. Each dried sample, weighing approximately 5 g, was subsequently placed in a separate PBS-filled container (pH 7.4) and incubated at 37 °C to simulate physiological conditions. At predetermined time intervals (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 24, 48, and 72 h), the samples were removed from the PBS solution, gently dried with filter paper to remove excess surface solution, and reweighed. Morphological study

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed to study the morphology of the hydrogel samples. Before this analysis, the samples were freeze-dried for 48 h, cross-sectioned, and then subjected to gold sputtering. Imaging was performed using a Philips XL30 apparatus (USA) operating at an accelerating voltage of 30 kV. The pore size of the hydrogels was determined via ImageJ software.

Self-healing test

First, a saturated potassium nitrate solution was prepared and placed in a Petri dish at the bottom of a desiccator. The desiccator was left for several hours to equilibrate at 25 °C and 98% humidity. Each sample was then cut in half with a surgical blade and placed side by side in the desiccator to evaluate its rebinding performance.

Cytotoxicity assessment

To investigate the biocompatibility of the prepared hydrogels, cell toxicity analysis was performed via the dimethylthiazolyl diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. Accordingly, L929 cells (NCBI C161) were obtained from the Pasteur Institute cell bank (Iran). After the cells were thawed, they were transferred to a flask containing Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The flask was then incubated at 37 °C, 90% humidity, and 5% carbon dioxide. Importantly, the culture medium was replaced every 3–4 days. Before testing, the samples were sterilized by washing with ethanol for 45 min and then subjected to UV radiation for another 45 min. The extraction process was carried out following ISO standard 10993-5. For each 3 cm² sample surface, 1 mL of culture medium was used. After 3 and 7 days, the medium was collected and added to the cells. A specific volume of DMEM was also used as a control.

In this study, to evaluate cell proliferation, 1 × 10⁴ cells were mixed with 100 µL of culture medium and added to each well of a 96-well cell culture plate. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to allow the cells to adhere to the plate. After confirming cell attachment, the culture medium was removed as much as possible, and 90 µL of extract from each sample, along with 10 µL of FBS, was added to each well. The cells were then incubated with these extracts for an additional 24 h. The culture medium was subsequently removed, and 100 µL of MTT at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL was added to each well, followed by a 4-hour incubation. After 4 h, the solution above the cells was discarded, and isopropanol was added to dissolve the formed purple crystals. To enhance the dissolution of the MTT precipitate, the plate was placed on a shaker for 15 min. The concentration of the dissolved substance in isopropanol was then measured via a BioTek ELx 808 ELISA (USA) at a wavelength of 570 nm. Wells with higher cell densities presented higher optical density (OD) values. For the MTT assay, untreated L929 fibroblasts cultured on standard tissue culture plastic were used as the negative control, while cells cultured in complete growth medium without hydrogel exposure were used as the positive control.

Cell adhesion test

To evaluate cell adhesion, each sterilized sample was placed in a 24-well plate. Then, 20,000 cells in a volume of 70 µL were added to each sample and incubated for 4–5 h to allow the cells to adhere. After cell attachment, a specific volume of culture medium containing 10% FBS was added to each well. After 48 h, the culture medium was removed, and the samples were washed with PBS for 30 s. A 3.5% glutaraldehyde solution (Merck) was subsequently used for cell fixation, and the plate was placed in the refrigerator for 2 h. The glutaraldehyde solution was then removed, and the samples were washed with PBS and ethanol at concentrations of 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 96%. Finally, cell adhesion to the samples was studied by SEM.

Morphology

Figure 2 shows SEM images of the freeze-dried CH-P8 and CH-P6 hydrogels. The cross-sections represent homogeneous and porous network structures with interconnected porosities. The CH-P6 and CH-P8 samples presented average pore diameters of 230 ± 32 μm and 261 ± 26 μm, respectively. This increase in pore size with increasing BM-PEG content could be related to the hydrophilic nature of the BM-PEG moiety. The hydrated state of BM-PEG and the exclusion volume of the PEG backbone could provide spatial hindrance during the formation of hydrogels. Hence, a greater pore size was formed with increasing BM-PEG content29. Notably, the increased pore size in CH-P8 led to increased water absorption according to the swelling results. The dimensions and morphology of the pores revealed that these hydrogels could be suitable for biomedical applications.

Self-healing

All samples containing different contents of BM-PEG were prepared for self-healing analysis. The hydrogels were cut via a surgical blade and then placed next to each other along the cut line to study their rebonding ability. Fig. 3 shows the self-healing performance of the hydrogels. All the samples exhibited good self-healing properties after approximately 30 min at room temperature. Over time, the strength of the bond at the cutoff increased to the extent that the sample could withstand gravitational forces, with no gaps observed at the healing line. This self-healing capability of chitosan and BM-PEG was attributed to the dynamic covalent Schiff base bonds formed within the hydrogels, which could repair the hydrogel network at the cutoff line. This self-healing behavior in hydrogels has also been reported by others30.

Fig. 3Self-healing performance of hydrogels containing different contents of BM-PEG.

Self-healing performance of hydrogels containing different contents of BM-PEG.

The visual assessment of the viscoelastic properties indicated that with increasing BM-PEG content, the hydrogels became stiffer and more brittle under tensile stress. Among the formulations, the CH-P6 sample showed a balanced viscoelastic profile, combining sufficient elasticity with structural integrity, whereas CH-P8 tended to fracture more readily. CH-P6 also showed more evident macroscopic rebonding behavior, uniform porosity in SEM images, and the shortest gelation time, suggesting strong network cohesion. Although CH-P8 displayed greater swelling and a larger pore size, excessive swelling can compromise dimensional stability and mechanical strength, which are undesirable for durable biomedical scaffolds. Therefore, considering gelation behavior, self-healing ability, porosity uniformity, and mechanical stability together, CH-P6 (6 wt% BM-PEG) was selected as the optimal formulation for further investigation.

FTIR

The FTIR spectra of all samples are shown in Fig. 4. In the chitosan spectrum, the peak at 3437 cm− 1 corresponded to the N-H and hydrogen bonded O-H stretch vibrations, the peak at 2923 cm− 1 was related to C-H stretching vibrations in -CH2OH and pyranose rings, and the peak observed at 1655 cm− 1 corresponded to the C = O stretching of amide band, related to the partial deacetylation31–33.

The benzaldehyde functionalization of PEG was evidenced by the appearance of a distinct aldehyde C = O stretching band at 1698 cm⁻¹ in the FTIR spectrum. Based on the reaction stoichiometry (PEG Mw = 6000 g mol⁻¹; 2.0 g corresponding to 0.333 mmol PEG chains and 0.667 mmol terminal –OH groups) and the use of excess formyl benzoic acid (0.24 g ≈ 1.60 mmol), BM-PEG is expected to be predominantly telechelic (di-functional). In similar DCC/DMAP-mediated esterifications used to prepare benzaldehyde-terminated PEG crosslinkers, end-group conversions in the ~ 80–90% range are commonly reported when confirmed by ¹H NMR; accordingly, and based on stoichiometric considerations, the effective aldehyde functionality in the present system is reasonably estimated to be ~ 1.6–1.8 aldehyde groups per PEG chain34.

In addition, the absorption band observed at ~ 1648 cm⁻¹ can be attributed to aromatic or conjugated vibrational modes associated with the benzaldehyde moiety, further supporting successful PEG modification30,35. This aldehyde functionality provides a sufficient density of dynamic Schiff-base sites to enable rapid sol–gel transition upon mixing with chitosan, consistent with the measured gelation times (Table 2). Increasing BM-PEG content increases the number of available aldehyde groups and thus the density of imine crosslinks, which aligns with the observed trend toward higher stiffness and reduced extensibility at higher BM-PEG contents.

As expected, when chitosan and BM-PEG were combined, the FTIR spectrum of the CH-P hydrogel showed a decrease in the intensity of the peaks corresponding to the amine group of chitosan and the aldehyde group of BM-PEG. Additionally, a new peak appeared in the range of 1628 cm−1, which corresponded to the imine group (C = N) in the final product. The observed peak at 1628 cm−1 in the CH-P sample indicated the formation of imine bonds (C = N) between chitosan and BM-PEG36,37. Moreover, the decrease in the intensity of the peak at 3437 cm−1 in chitosan and its shift to 3439 cm−1 in the hydrogel, along with the reduction in the intensity of the peak at 1698 cm−1 in the hydrogel compared to BM-PEG, further supported the formation of bonds between the aldehyde group of BM-PEG and the amine group of chitosan27. This confirmed the successful formation of the imine-linked hydrogel.

XRD analysis

To investigate the crystallinity changes upon hydrogel formation, the XRD patterns of PEG, BM-PEG, chitosan, and the CH-P hydrogel are shown in Fig. 5. It was observed that PEG exhibited two crystalline peaks at 2θ values of 19o and 24o29. The XRD pattern for the BM-PEG was quite similar to that of PEG; however, an additional peak was observed at a 2θ value of 7.5o. This could suggest the presence of an ordered domain or interchain packing introduced by aldehyde functionalization of in BM-PEG. Since functional groups like aldehyde do not produce diffraction peaks themselves, the observed peak was more likely associated with structural reorganization or aggregation of modified PEG chains38. Chitosan also exhibited a crystalline peak at a 2θ value of 20 o39.

As for the CH-P sample, two peaks can be observed at 2θ values of 19o and 23o. The peak observed at 2θ = 19 o for the CH-P sample was attributed to the overlap of the crystalline peaks from chitosan and BM-PEG in this region, while the peak at 2θ = 24o was solely attributable to BM-PEG. Additionally, in the CH-P hydrogel, the intensity of the peak at 2θ = 7.5o decreased, which was likely due to the disruption of the aforementioned order upon Schiff-base crosslinking with chitosan. This supported the integration and bonding of BM-PEG with chitosan in the hydrogel network, confirming successful imine bonding between the components39,40.

Morphology

SEM images of the nanocomposite hydrogel samples with different BNC contents are shown in Fig. 7. The average pore sizes were 343 ± 39 μm for the CH-P sample, 404 ± 60 μm for the CH-P-BNC0.25 sample, 488 ± 52 μm for the CH-P-BNC0.5 sample, 362 ± 48 μm for the CH-P-BNC1 sample, and 296 ± 56 μm for the CH-P-BNC2 sample. The incorporation of up to 0.5% BNC into the structure increased the average pore size. However, as the BNC content further increased, the pore size decreased. This decreasing trend in pore dimensions with increasing BNC content could be attributed to the entanglement of BNC nanofibers, which results in reduced pore dimensions, leading to a denser internal structure of the hydrogel. Furthermore, the micrographs suggest that hydrogels with higher BNC contents are denser, indicating that a lower BNC content could be more efficient for obtaining highly porous structures. Nonetheless, the resulting morphologies are considered favorable for biomedical applications43.

Self-healing

For this test, the hydrogels were cut using a surgical blade and then brought into contact along the cut interface to evaluate their rebonding behavior after 20 min, as shown in Fig. 8a. Visual observations indicate that the nanocomposite hydrogels exhibited faster and more effective rebonding compared with the control sample (CH-P). The enhanced self-healing behavior is associated with the presence of BNC, which reinforces the network structure and improves interfacial connectivity within the hydrogel matrix45. Owing to their high surface area, BNC nanofibers promote hydrogen bonding and physical interactions with surrounding polymer chains, contributing to improved mechanical integrity46. In addition, BNC facilitates dynamic Schiff-base interactions by providing a supportive framework for bond reformation at the cut interface, thereby promoting network reconstruction47.

Although the present study primarily relied on visual assessment, the complete disappearance of the cutoff (Fig. 8a) indicates visible network reformation. This qualitative observation is widely accepted as credible evidence of effective self-healing in dynamic Schiff-base hydrogels when the healed sample can withstand its own weight without rupture19,30,36,48. However, quantitative self-healing efficiency was not directly measured. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as qualitative evidence of dynamic network reformation rather than as a measured recovery ratio. Future work should include rheological recovery tests or compressive strength recovery measurements to more rigorously quantify healing efficiency.

The mechanisms suggested for self-healing are shown in Fig. 8b. The self-healing behavior of the hydrogel is primarily governed by dynamic Schiff-base (imine) bonds formed between the aldehyde groups of BM-PEG and the amine groups of chitosan, while BNC mainly serves as a reinforcing phase without directly participating in the self-healing chemistry. As shown previously, the hydrophilic nature of the hydroxyl groups in BNC could increase the swelling capacity of the hydrogels. This could provide a more efficient environment for Schiff base reactions, as these reactions often require some moisture or water49,50.

FTIR

The FTIR spectra of BNC, the hydrogel sample without BNC (CH-P), and those containing 0.5 wt% and 1.0 wt% BNC are shown in Fig. 9. For the BNC spectrum, the peak at 3520 cm−1corresponded to O–H stretching vibrations due to the large number of hydroxyl groups in cellulose as a polysaccharide51. The peak at 2893 cm−1was related to C–H stretching vibrations associated with the presence of CH2groups. The peaks at 1234 cm−1and 995 cm−1corresponded to C-O-C and CH2stretching, respectively52. In addition, the peak at 1431 cm−1 represented the CH2bending vibrations of BNC. The decrease in the intensity of this peak in the 0.5 and 1 wt% BNC samples may be due to a reduction in the degree of crystallinity of cellulose53. Additionally, the β-glycosidic bond in the cellulose ring for γ (COC) stretching was observed at 898 cm−1.The peak observed at 1728 cm−1was attributed to the C = O stretching vibration of the carboxyl groups introduced during the oxidation process of BNC54. The shoulder at 1634 cm−1corresponded to the bending vibration of water retained within the hydrated network55 With the incorporation of BNC into the structure, the intensity of the imine peak in the range of 1631 cm−1increased, suggesting increased crosslinking density or possible hydrogen bonding-induced shifts. While BNC itself is not reactive toward Schiff base formation under native conditions, its incorporation could promote closer packing or facilitate secondary interactions (such as hydrogen bonding) that increase the signal intensity in this region56–58. Furthermore, a gradual shift of the imine peak from 1628 cmto 1 in the pure hydrogel to 1631 cm−1and 1634 cm−1in the nanocomposites containing 0.5% and 1.0% BNC, respectively, was observed. This shift could be attributed to hydrogen bonding interactions between the imine groups and the hydroxyl or carboxyl functionalities in oxidized BNC, as well as increased crosslinking density and structural confinement, which could increase the vibrational frequency of the bond59. The absence of the peaks at 1577, 1646, and 1695 cm−1 in the BNC-containing samples compared with the control sample (CH-P) suggested that these peaks might overlap due to the interaction between the materials after mixing60. For all the samples, the broad band in the 3000–3550 cm−1range corresponded to O–H stretching vibrations61. The broadening and shift of this peak from 3446 cmto 1in the control sample to 3433 cm−1in the nanocomposite samples was likely due to the formation of hydrogen bonds between the OH groups in BNC and the polymers62,63.

XRD analysis

To investigate the effect of BNC incorporation on the crystallinity of the hydrogel, XRD analysis was performed, and the results are shown in Fig. 10. BNC had two crystalline peaks at 2θ values of 14.7o and 22.6o64,65. After BNC addition to the hydrogel, owing to the overlap of the BNC peak at 22.6o with the peak at 23.2o for the CH-P hydrogel, the intensity of this peak increased in the nanocomposites. For the sample containing 0.5 wt% BNC, the intensity of the peak at 7.7 o, related to the aldehyde group of BM-PEG, decreased, which could indicate bonding between this group and the functional groups in BNC66. However, when the BNC content was increased to 1.0 wt%, the intensity of this peak increased again, which could be due to the interference of BNC in the formation of the imine bonds.

Rheometry

The results of rheometry analysis using parallel plates to determine the viscoelastic properties of the samples are shown in Fig. 11a. The higher storage modulus (G’) than the loss modulus (G’’) in all the samples indicated the viscoelastic properties of all the hydrogels across the entire frequency range. This meant that the elastic properties of the hydrogels dominated their viscous properties67,68. Additionally, when 0.5 wt% BNC was incorporated into the hydrogel, the elastic modulus decreased slightly. The observed decrease in the storage modulus for the sample containing 0.5 wt% BNC, despite its shorter gelation time, resulted from dynamic rearrangements within the hydrogel network during the test. At lower nanoparticle contents (0.5 wt% compared with 1.0 wt%), the Schiff base linkages between BM-PEG and chitosan had greater mobility and remained susceptible to reversible bond exchange. This dynamic behavior could cause transient weakening in the network under stress, which led to a lower measured G′ value. In contrast, for the sample containing 1.0 wt% BNC, despite the longer gelation time, a higher G′ value was observed, which could result from a denser and more stabilized network, as confirmed by SEM micrographs, where smaller pore dimensions were observed. At this higher concentration, BNC could act as a physical crosslinking point or reinforce the network structure by promoting hydrogen bonding or entanglement. Such BNC–polymer interactions can also contribute to reinforcement by enabling load transfer to the nanofiber phase. In addition, reversible interfacial interactions under deformation can dissipate mechanical energy. Thus, the flexibility of the Schiff base bonds was reduced, and rearrangements under shear were limited69. Accordingly, the CH-P-BNC1 sample was considered optimal in terms of rheological properties.

To address the viscoelastic response at a constant deformation frequency, the frequency sweep data were examined at approximately 1 Hz. At this frequency, CH-P exhibited a storage modulus (G′) of ~ 2180 Pa and a loss modulus (G″) of ~ 11.5 Pa, CH-P-BNC0.5 showed G′ ~1774 Pa and G″ ~9.2 Pa, and CH-P-BNC1 showed G′ ~2384 Pa and G″ ~18.1 Pa. In all cases, G′ remained significantly higher than G″ (tan δ < 0.01), confirming a predominantly elastic and solid-like response at physiologically relevant deformation rates. In fact, rheological data are presented as representative results to illustrate the comparative mechanical behavior of the hydrogels.

Mechanical properties

To investigate the mechanical properties of the hydrogel samples, they were subjected to compressive strains of up to 50%, and the results are presented in Fig. 11b. The incorporation of BNC into the hydrogel structure improved the compression strength and modulus values. The hydrogels exhibited greater resistance as they underwent higher strains, and at equal strain levels, the hydrogel with a higher BNC content demonstrated greater resistance. This increase in the compressive strength of the hydrogels with the addition of BNC is partly attributed to the formation of denser networks, as previously observed in morphology studies, which increased the structural integrity of the hydrogel. In addition to morphological densification, BNC can reinforce hydrogels through load-transfer and stress-dissipation mechanisms. Due to its high stiffness and aspect ratio, BNC acts as a nano-reinforcing phase that bridges polymer chains and bears part of the applied load, enabling more efficient stress distribution throughout the network and reducing localized failure. Under compression, interfacial interactions between BNC and the polymer matrix (e.g., hydrogen bonding and physical entanglement) can reversibly break and reform, dissipating mechanical energy and delaying crack initiation and propagation. Similar reinforcement mechanisms have been reported for nanocellulose-reinforced hydrogels, including systems based on cellulose nanocrystals, in which the nanocellulose phase effectively redistributed stress and markedly enhanced modulus and strength compared to the unfilled matrix70. The obtained results are consistent with those of the rheometry analysis42,71. Compression data are presented as representative results to illustrate the comparative mechanical behavior of the hydrogels.

Cell viability

In this study, L929 fibroblasts were utilized to investigate the biocompatibility of the hydrogels via the MTT assay. As shown in Fig. 12a, for the hydrogel without bacterial nanocellulose (CH-P), the cell viability was approximately 98% on day 3 and decreased slightly to 92% by day 7, indicating a 6% reduction over time. Similarly, the hydrogel containing 0.5 wt% BNC (CH-P-BNC 0.5) had an initial cell viability of approximately 96% on day 3, which decreased to 91% by day 7, corresponding to a 5% reduction. Compared with the CH-P-BNC 0.5 hydrogel, the hydrogel containing 1.0 wt% BNC (CH-P-BNC 1.0) presented slightly greater cell viability (97% on day 3 and 93% on day 7). The observed increase in cell viability at higher BNC contents could be attributed to the improved adhesion and proliferation of fibroblasts on more hydrophilic and porous surfaces provided by the BNC-containing hydrogels. These findings confirm the excellent biocompatibility of all the fabricated hydrogels, supporting their potential for biomedical applications.

Cell adhesion

To investigate the potential of the prepared hydrogels for biomedical applications, in addition to their biocompatibility, the cell adhesion behavior was analyzed and micrographed via SEM, as shown in Fig. 12b. Accordingly, cellular adhesion to the hydrogel surfaces was evident across all the samples owing to the biocompatibility and porous structure of the hydrogels. The cells not only survived and maintained biological activity but also initiated division. Cellular adhesion generally involves initial attachment to the matrix surface, followed by aggregation to form cell colonies. These colonies may further develop into focal adhesions, promoting cell spreading under favorable surface conditions72. Key surface parameters such as roughness, stiffness, and hydrophilicity play critical roles in cellular adhesion to scaffolds73. Studies have shown that increased hydrophilicity enhances cellular adhesion and growth74. Hydrogels not only mimic the extracellular matrix (ECM), providing a microenvironment conducive to cellular survival but also facilitate the delivery of nutrients essential for cell viability75. An increase in the BNC content within the hydrogel increased the degree of cellular adhesion due to its hydrophilic nature, confirming the MTT results.

Overall, the combination of chitosan, benzaldehyde-functionalized PEG (BM-PEG), and bacterial nanocellulose (BNC) contributes synergistically to the mechanical properties, biocompatibility, and self-healing behavior of the hydrogels. Chitosan provides a biocompatible backbone with functional amines for dynamic Schiff-base formation with BM-PEG, enabling reversible covalent crosslinks that support intrinsic self-healing, while BM-PEG’s flexible chains facilitate network rearrangement under deformation. The addition of BNC reinforces the matrix through physical interactions such as hydrogen bonding and nanofiber entanglement, improving mechanical stability without suppressing network dynamics. Together, the complementary roles of dynamic chemical crosslinking and physical reinforcement yield a multifunctional hydrogel with balanced mechanical integrity, biological compatibility, and self-repair capability, consistent with recent advances in hybrid self-healing hydrogel design strategies.

Conclusions

Self-healing nanocomposite hydrogels based on benzaldehyde-functionalized PEG, chitosan, and bacterial nanocellulose (BNC) were successfully developed and systematically evaluated. The results demonstrate that dynamic Schiff-base crosslinking supports self-healing behavior through reversible imine bond formation, while incorporation of BNC effectively reinforces the hydrogel network. An optimal BNC content (1%) led to improved mechanical performance through combined network stabilization, load transfer, and stress-dissipation mechanisms while maintaining visible cut-and-heal behavior. All hydrogel formulations exhibited rapid gelation (below 2 min), with the CH-P-BNC0.5 sample showing the shortest gelation time (~ 60 s). Swelling analysis indicated that CH-P-BNC0.25 and CH-P-BNC0.5 samples exhibited higher swelling capacity, whereas higher BNC content resulted in a denser network with reduced swelling. Rheological study also revealed the viscoelastic properties of all the hydrogels across the entire frequency range. In vitro studies confirmed high cytocompatibility (cell viability > 85%) and effective L929 fibroblast adhesion, indicating their suitability for biomedical use. Overall, the synergistic integration of dynamic covalent crosslinking and nanofibrillar reinforcement enables a balanced combination of mechanical robustness, self-repair ability, and biocompatibility. These findings highlight the potential of the developed hydrogel system as a promising self-healing platform for wound-healing–oriented and related biomedical applications. Despite these promising results, the self-healing behavior was evaluated qualitatively, and quantitative recovery measurements such as rheological recovery or mechanical strength recovery were not performed. In addition, long-term stability under physiological conditions was not investigated. Therefore, future work should focus on quantitative evaluation of healing efficiency and extended performance studies.
