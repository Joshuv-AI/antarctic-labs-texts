Training frontline providers: a survey analysis of the advanced vestibular physical therapist certificate program’s impact on clinical practice
Authors: Regan G. Harrell, Chelsea J. Manetta, Susan L. Whitney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDizziness and vertigo affect millions annually, creating a $13.3 billion US economic burden. Physical therapists are key in treating vestibular disorders, but entry-level training varies significantly, necessitating specialized post-professional education. This study evaluates whether the Advanced Vestibular Physical Therapist (AVPT) Certificate Program effectively prepares PTs to meet patient needs.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was distributed to 230 graduates from four AVPT cohorts. The 18-question survey assessed program impact on clinical practice, professional development, and patient outcomes using 5-point Likert scales, plus demographic information and open-ended feedback.ResultsSixty-seven graduates responded (29% response rate). Nearly all (97%) agreed the program prepared them as frontline vestibular providers, while 92% reported improved patient outcomes. All participants (100%) agreed the program met continuing education needs, and 98% reported changed clinical practice. Average likelihood to recommend was 9.69/10. However, 30% reported employers did not value the certification, and 91% received no additional compensation.ConclusionsThe AVPT program successfully creates competent expert vestibular providers, improving clinical confidence, differential diagnosis skills, and patient outcomes while addressing critical gaps in entry-level education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09369-3.

Introduction

Dizziness and vertigo are debilitating symptoms that are common within the United States (US) [1, 2]. According to data from the National Ambulatory Medical Care Survey, there were 20.6 million ambulatory care visits for dizziness between 2013 and 2015 [3]. The economic impact vertigo and dizziness have on the US healthcare system is substantial with Ruthberg et al. reporting the annual total economic burden estimated to be $13.3 billion [4]. Dizziness can affect people across the life span and can stem from peripheral and central origins [5]. Prell et al. showed that across the life span, people experiencing dizziness sought care from emergency department physicians, otolaryngologists, neurologists, primary care physicians, chiropractors, physical therapists, alternative practitioners, orthopedists, psychiatrists, and cardiologists [6].

According to the study from Dunlap et al., 20.6 million adults sought ambulatory care for dizziness between 2013 and 2015 and only a small number of these patients were referred to physical therapy [3]. Common diagnoses that result in dizziness include benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV), unilateral hypofunction, and vestibular migraine [3, 7] while a large number of dizziness visits in ambulatory care settings go undiagnosed, leaving with a diagnosis of “dizziness” [3]. In a recent white paper from AMN Healthcare, the average wait time in 2025 for new patients to schedule an appointment is 31.5 days [8]. Given these prolonged wait times, physical therapists are uniquely positioned to help bridge the gap in delivery of care. Mughal et al. investigated the quality of otolaryngology education in medical trainees was lacking and found that there is a widespread demand for further postgraduate training [9]. In work by Smith et al., they found there is low otolaryngology trainee to patient ratio and that improving trainee opportunities would improve access to vestibular care [10]. Having physical therapists complete the initial evaluation for someone who presents with dizziness in an ambulatory care setting had greater than 80% agreement with an otolaryngologist in terms of diagnostic imaging requested and physician referral [11]. Wait times in a fall clinic were reduced by 25% with screening by a physical therapist for BPPV [12]. Receiving physical therapy after presenting for medical care for dizziness has been associated with fewer falls for the 12 months following a physician visit based on 805,454 patient visits [13]. These studies highlight the benefits of having trained physical therapists in ambulatory care centers to help manage wait times and the usage of health care resources.

In 2022, there were an estimated 233,980 full-time employed PTs in the US [14]. While PTs are trained as entry level clinicians with knowledge of the vestibular system, including anatomy, physiology, diagnostic testing, and treatment, the amount of training and content covered can vary greatly from program to program [15]. Barriers PTs face to successfully work in vestibular rehabilitation include inadequate support and training [16–22]. Within the physical therapy field, there are options for post-professional vestibular specific training ranging in delivery method (in-person or online), in time (one hour to 40 + hours), and in content. Given the lack of standardization of post-professional training, it can be difficult for those seeking further training to identify which course will best meet their needs. Regardless, there is a desire to have more specialized training within the vestibular rehabilitation space. In the United Kingdom, there has been a push for a recognized vestibular specialty within the physical therapy profession [23]. Thus far in the US, there are board certified neurologic clinical specialists, however this practice area covers professional roles, responsibilities, values, and competency in patient and client management of people with various neurologic conditions but does not have explicit vestibular training expectations [24]. The Vestibular Special Interest Group of the Academy of Neurologic Physical Therapy within the American Physical Therapy Association has a list of resources including continuing education courses and a bootcamp to help clinicians find mentorship and training [25]. In response to the need for vestibular medicine experts across healthcare, The Barany Society created a curriculum for vestibular medicine (VestMed) with the goal of standardizing vestibular care across the globe [26]. This curriculum lays out the framework for vestibular medicine providers across several disciplines but is not specific to PTs [26].

After seeing the need for specialty trained, competent vestibular physical therapists (physiotherapists), both in the US and abroad, the Advanced Vestibular Physical Therapist (AVPT) Certificate Program was created. The program was designed to meet the educational needs of PTs. This hybrid program delivers over 80 h of didactic and psychomotor training to participants through four content specific modules over the course of nine months. To our knowledge, at the time of its development, this is the first vestibular competency course that offers both hybrid and in-person learning opportunities. Three of the modules are completed virtually with internationally recognized faculty, and the fourth module takes place at the University of Pittsburgh in Pittsburgh, PA, USA and involves a three-day long laboratory schedule. The purpose of this survey was to assess whether the AVPT program is delivering on its objectives of creating expert, advanced vestibular rehabilitation providers.

Methods

The AVPT program had graduated four cohorts at the time of the survey. The first three cohorts graduated between 50 and 55 participants, with the fourth cohort increasing in size to 72. All participants admitted to the AVPT program must submit an application that is reviewed by the course faculty. All participants must have at least two years of vestibular rehabilitation experience prior to being accepted into the program.

Prior to beginning the program, each cohort completed a pre-course survey to gain a better understanding of the information the participants were interested in learning and what they were looking to gain from the course. A concept mapping approach using a hybrid coding method combining themes from the program’s mission along with comments from the pre-course surveys was used to identify themes and concepts in the pre-course survey (Table 1). The content of the modules for each AVPT cohort used the concepts from Table 1 along with the Basic and Expert concepts from VestMed [26].

The survey, which was disseminated to a total of 230 AVPT program graduates, was developed by the course administrators to assess the clinical utility of the program. Questions were derived from the mission of the program which states, “The AVPT program is a comprehensive didactic experience in vestibular physical therapy that prepares and certifies physical therapists to be frontline providers for patients experiencing balance, dizziness, and abnormal proprioception. The AVPT certification is delivered in a highly innovative format that allows participants from anywhere in the world to experience cutting-edge vestibular content with limited travel. The hybrid format includes self-paced online coursework, synchronous virtual meetings via Zoom, and four interactive weekend workshops.” Using the mission and the pre-course survey themes, an 18-question survey was created.

The survey included two sections: one section addressed personal reflection on the AVPT program, and the second consisted of demographic information. The questions reflecting on the course were scored on a 5-point Likert scale from strongly disagree to strongly agree. The first seven questions addressed the graduates’ perceptions on how the AVPT program changed their clinical practice. There were two questions regarding how their employer viewed the completion of the course, one open ended question asking graduates to provide any feedback they wanted to share, and one question asking the graduate to rank how likely they are to recommend the AVPT program on a scale from 1 to 10, with 1 being extremely unlikely and 10 being extremely likely. The last section of the survey collected demographic information about the graduates, including practice setting, years of experience when they completed the program, and percentage of vestibular caseload. The full survey can be found in Appendix A.

The survey was disseminated using Qualtrics software (Provo, UT). The University of Pittsburgh IRB deemed the survey not research by the DHHS and not a clinical investigation as defined by the FDA and the Declaration of Helsinki. STUDY25040052. Distribution of the survey occurred via email by the study coordinators to all graduates of the program, with a single reminder email sent one week prior to the survey closing date. Participants were provided the details of the survey and informed consent was acknowledged prior to completing the online survey.

The survey was open from April 2025 until June 2025. Descriptive statistics of the response rates were completed, and all statistical computations were completed with SPSS (Version 27).

Results

A total of four cohorts of program graduates completed the program for a total of 230 graduates. Sixty-seven completed the survey indicating a response rate of 29%. The completed surveys represented a well-distributed range of graduation years, with 14 (21.2%) respondents from 2022, 21 (31.8%) from 2023, 15 (22.7%) from 2024, and 16 (24.2%) from 2025.

Table 2 shows the demographic information of the respondents. While the response rate for the survey was 29%, the respondent sample was a good representation of our entire graduate population based on demographic data collected during the application process and throughout the program with no significant differences in age or years of practicing noted. Demographic data is collected on all participants in the program as part of the registration process. A majority of the respondents (60.6%) had 6 to 15 years of vestibular rehabilitation experience at the time of completing the AVPT course. While a variety of practice settings are represented within the program, most respondents work in an outpatient orthopedic or neuromuscular/vestibular setting.

Of the 67 respondents, 62 (92.5%) respondents agreed or strongly agreed that the APVT program improved their patient outcomes. When queried about whether the AVPT program met the respondents’ needs, 67/67 (100%) either agreed or strongly agreed. The participants were asked whether the AVPT program prepared the respondents to be frontline vestibular providers and 65/67 (97.0%) responded agree or strongly agree. 56/67 (83.6%) respondents either agreed or strongly agreed that the AVPT program fostered a sense of community among vestibular rehabilitation providers. For the final question regarding whether or not the AVPT program changed their clinical practice, 66/67 (98.5%) either agreed or strongly agreed.

There were two questions in the survey regarding the professional values of the program, “my employer finds values in my having completed the AVPT course” and “I have received extra compensation for having my AVPT credentials”. These responses were more varied, and these data are presented in Fig. 1.B. Four of the respondents stated that they strongly disagreed that their employer found value in them completing the course, with another nine respondents disagreeing. Eleven respondents (16.4%) neither agreed nor disagreed. For the question regarding compensation, 52 of the 67 (77.6%) respondents stated they either strongly disagreed or disagreed that they received increased compensation for the advanced training they received.

Comments from graduates related to overall impact of course on their practice

Program graduates identified the improvement of clinical skills as the most common response with comments about the program including “I’m better skilled in differential diagnosis” and “made me more confident in my clinical decision making.” The second broad theme that emerged was the ability to train and mentor others with responses including “was helpful, particularly with teaching and mentoring colleagues” and “I am doing in-services.” The final theme identified was improvement in overall clinical utility. One program graduate noted “I have seen that total visits required for my patients has also decreased.” The final question of the survey was asking the respondents how likely they are to recommend the program to a fellow physical therapist on a 1–10 scale. The average response was 9.69 (range 8.00–10.00).

Discussion

Overall, the AVPT certificate program has met the initial mission and participants’ goals in the first four cohorts of the program. While the response rate was 29%, there was a consensus that the AVPT program has helped improve clinicians’ abilities to be frontline providers and improve patient outcomes. Advanced vestibular educational programs are ideally suited to train physical therapists for roles in ambulatory care centers to manage wait times and decrease overall healthcare utilization, as noted by 97% of survey respondents agreeing with the statement that they felt the program prepared them to become frontline vestibular providers.

There are several vestibular conditions for which early diagnosis and management are essential. For conditions such as BPPV, which can be diagnosed and treated in the same visit, waiting for an appointment and potentially being referred for specialty visits and imaging costs the healthcare system up to $2000 per person and $2 billion annually [27]. For people with unilateral vestibular loss, starting vestibular rehabilitation within two weeks of symptoms has been shown to improve functional outcomes [1, 28–30]. Persistent postural perceptual dizziness (PPPD), the most common functional dizziness disorder, has been linked to a previous history of vestibular disorder, such as BPPV, vestibular migraine, and unilateral hypofunction. Early management of these conditions may reduce PPPD incidence, and timely treatment of PPPD may lessen the overall healthcare burden [31, 32]. Recognition and management of all these disorders is covered in the AVPT program in the virtual and in-person modules which strongly position PTs as frontline providers. Comments in the open-ended text field conveyed an improved sense of confidence in the graduates’ differential diagnosis skills, clinical reasoning skills, and improving problem solving for complex cases. Reducing wait times to see vestibular healthcare providers decreases the burden on the healthcare system and improves patient outcomes. This was supported by 92% of respondents who stated that the AVPT program had improved their patient outcomes.

While vestibular physical therapy is considered an entry level skill and included in the national licensing exam, physical therapy programs differ vastly in terms of time spent and content covered in entry level programs. In a study by Galgon et al., it was reported that on average entry level physical therapy programs spent 8.03 h on vestibular rehabilitation lectures and 6.61 h in lab [15]. In contrast, Stevens et al. reported that the average number of contact hours dedicated to foot and ankle content was 48.2 h [33]. Galgon’s study also reported that less than 80% of the surveyed programs provided education regarding Meniere’s disease, horizontal canal BPPV, cervicogenic dizziness, visual motion sensitivity, vestibular migraine, and persistent postural perceptual dizziness [15]. This lack of consistency in entry level education creates gaps in care that need to be addressed by post-professional education. All participants of the survey agreed that the AVPT program met their continuing education needs.

One of the other main missions of the AVPT program is to cultivate a community of practice of vestibular physical therapists around the world. As stated by Barab, “a community of practice is a persistent, sustaining, social network of individuals who share and develop an overlapping knowledge base, set of beliefs, values, history, and experiences, focused on common practice, and/or mutual experience” [34]. There are AVPT graduates from Australia, Belgium, Canada, Iceland, Ireland, India, Japan, Kenya, Mexico, Saudia Arabia, Thailand, and the United States. The program uses a digital web-based messaging system to create individual cohort communities and a larger graduate community to allow graduates to ask questions, get advice, and stay connected after completing the program. 83% of respondents agreed that the AVPT program created a sense of community. Comments on the open-ended question include “I most enjoyed meeting everyone”.

While the AVPT program is not unique in that it is a post-professional program, many residency and fellowship programs now exist within the United States. Work from Rodeghero et al. demonstrated that those who were fellowship trained had better patient outcomes in less visits compared to those who completed residency in those with musculoskeletal conditions [35]. The American Board of Residencies and Fellowship Education has accredited neurological residency programs across the country and has begun to accredit fellowships, but currently there is only one accredited neurologic fellowship, and its focus is Movement Disorders [36]. Since there are not accredited fellowships in vestibular rehabilitation, the AVPT program was designed to meet this need.

Feedback from graduates indicated that they noted improvements in patient outcomes, and one person commented that their overall number of visits per patient had reduced upon completion of the course. However, 30% of the respondents of the AVPT program identified that their employer did not always see the value of the program and 91% identified that they did not receive any additional compensation for completing this course. Although vestibular rehabilitation has been shown to reduce patient wait times, fall risk, and overall healthcare utilization, current health system structures, hiring practices, and reimbursement models often fail to fully acknowledge the benefits of vestibular certified providers and the need for this specialized care. Overcoming these systemic barriers through education and advocacy represents a key opportunity for growth, ensuring that patients can access highly trained specialists and benefit from their care.

This survey is not without limitations, first, as the data is self-reported by people who have completed the program and lacking in objective data of number of visits and patient outcomes, it is uncertain how clinical care has been enhanced by completing the program. Future iterations of this course could include some type of mechanism by which participants could track their own improvement in their patient outcomes. Second, since the survey was disseminated via an anonymous link, the possibility of a participant completing the survey more than once cannot be fully excluded. Third, as the survey was created and disseminated by the program’s creators and coordinators, there is a potential for coercion bias, as participants may have felt implicit pressure to respond in a particular way. To minimize this risk, participants were informed that participation was entirely voluntary and that their responses would have no impact on their relationship with the program or its faculty. Finally, the low response rate of 29% may introduce sample bias, as respondents are more likely to be highly engaged or to have had positive experiences with the program.

Conclusion

The AVPT certificate program is training frontline vestibular providers both in the United States and abroad. By increasing the number of frontline vestibular providers, the hope is to increase access to vestibular care globally and decrease wait times for intervention. The advanced, specialized vestibular training received by program graduates was shown to improve participant’s perceptions of overall knowledge on differential diagnosis and treatment techniques, as noted by program graduates’ responses, and may lead to improved care for individuals experiencing dizziness globally.
