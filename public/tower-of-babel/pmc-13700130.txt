Indirect moxibustion therapy improves insomnia and associated symptoms in patients with schizophrenia
Authors: Jing Cai, Mei Wu, Jianming Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the efficacy of low-dose dexzopiclone combined with Indirect moxibustion (IM), compared with sham Indirect moxibustion (SIM), on sleep quality (PSQI) and quality of life (SQLS) in patients with schizophrenia and comorbid insomnia.MethodsThis single-center randomized controlled trial (NCT06171308) obtained ethics approval from Shanghai Mental Health Center (SMHC-IRB-2023-08). A total of 120 participants were randomly assigned to the IM group (n = 60) or the SIM group (n = 60), receiving 8 weeks of standardized intervention.ResultsBaseline PSQI and SQLS scores were comparable between the two groups. After 4 and 8 weeks, the IM group demonstrated significantly lower PSQI and SQLS scores than the SIM group (PSQI at week 4: 10.10 ± 1.76 vs. 13.50 ± 1.65, week 8: 10.65 ± 1.54 vs. 12.77 ± 2.05, SQLS at week 4: 28.78 ± 8.84 vs. 35.56 ± 3.38, week 8: 30.36 ± 10.63 vs. 38.07 ± 3.42). The improvement in the IM group was significantly greater than that in the SIM group in terms of change from baseline (PSQI: −3.399 [95% CI: −4.078 to −2.719] at week 4, −2.115 [95% CI: −2.795 to −1.435] at week 8, SQLS: −6.801 [95% CI: −9.104 to −4.462] at week 4, −7.732 [95% CI: −10.071 to −5.393] at week 8), with all adjusted intergroup differences being statistically significant (P < 0.001).ConclusionsAdjunctive Indirect Moxibustion led to statistically significant and clinically meaningful improvements in sleep quality and quality of life compared to sham procedure in this patient population over an 8-week period. These preliminary findings suggest IM is a promising adjunctive therapy. Further multicenter, long-term trials with expanded outcome indicators are required to validate its generalizability and sustained efficacy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13063-026-09915-w.

Introduction

Schizophrenia affects approximately 1% of the global population [1]. Insomnia is a common but often overlooked comorbidity, present from prodromal to chronic stages [2–4]. Epidemiological evidence indicates that an estimated 25–50% of patients with schizophrenia experience clinically significant insomnia, characterized by persistent sleep difficulties and daytime functional impairment according to DSM-5 criteria [5–9]. Pathophysiologically, insomnia involves psychological hyperarousal, cognitive factors, and disrupted circadian and homeostatic regulation [10]. Clinically, it is categorized into short-term, chronic, and other specified subtypes. Importantly, sleep disturbance is no longer regarded as a mere secondary symptom of schizophrenia, but as an independent risk factor that exacerbates psychotic symptoms, impairs neurocognition, and deteriorates quality of life [11]. Therefore, effectively managing insomnia is essential to comprehensive schizophrenia care and reducing disease burden [12].

Despite its significance, managing insomnia in schizophrenia remains challenging and understudied. Quality of life—closely linked to prognosis [13]—is strongly influenced by sleep quality in both healthy and schizophrenia populations [14], suggesting that sleep improvement may yield broad benefits. Current pharmacotherapy relies mainly on benzodiazepine receptor agonists. Except for dexzopiclone, which is exempt from the 35-day use restriction specified by the US FDA guidelines, most hypnotic agents are only approved for short-term application. Clinicians often prescribe sedative antipsychotics to alleviate insomnia in clinically stable schizophrenia patients [15], whereas long-term hypnotic monotherapy raises risks of metabolic complications, drug reliance, and psychotic exacerbation for schizophrenia patients [16–18].

These concerns are particularly salient in the Chinese context. A recent real-world retrospective analysis of 33,569 valid prescriptions from 10 medical institutions in eastern China (2015–2021) found a consistent year-over-year increase in the use of benzodiazepines (BZDs) and Z-drugs (including zopiclone and dexzopiclone), with pronounced rises among elderly and female patients [19]. This rising prescription trend highlights an unmet clinical demand for low-risk auxiliary interventions for psychiatric patients with insomnia.

Given these limitations of conventional pharmacotherapy, moxibustion, a core Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) therapy, has emerged as a complementary intervention [20, 21]. It exerts therapeutic effects via thermal stimulation generated by burning mugwort, which regulates yin-yang balance and harmonizes qi and blood circulation [22, 23]. In TCM theory, insomnia results from visceral dysfunction, qi-blood imbalance, and yin-yang disruption, often triggered by emotional stress or chronic illness. Moxibustion delivers localized thermal stimulation to specific acupoints to correct the above pathological imbalances. Indirect moxibustion (IM), a classic moxibustion technique first documented in the Zhouhou Beiji Fang compiled by Ge Hong in the Jin Dynasty, adopts a medicinal buffer between moxa materials and the skin, enabling milder, more uniform, and safer thermal delivery [24]. This enhances safety and tolerability, especially in vulnerable psychiatric populations. Nevertheless, few studies have systematically assessed the efficacy of IM—particularly as an adjunct therapy for insomnia in schizophrenia.

To address this gap, we conducted a preliminary randomized controlled trial to evaluate the auxiliary efficacy of IM as an adjunct to low-dose dexzopiclone for improving sleep quality and quality of life in schizophrenia patients with comorbid insomnia. We hypothesized that IM would improve sleep quality—particularly in the subdimensions of sleep latency, efficiency, and daytime dysfunction, as measured by the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)—through its proposed mechanisms of regulating qi, calming the mind, and harmonizing yin and yang. We further anticipated that such sleep improvements would translate into broader psychosocial and quality-of-life benefits, assessed by the Schizophrenia Quality of Life Scale (SQLS). The primary study outcome was the between-group difference in PSQI score changes, while secondary outcomes included alterations in SQLS scores and treatment acceptability.

Materials and methods

Design

This single-center, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT06171308) was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Mental Health Center (SMHC-IRB-2023-08). All enrolled participants provided written informed consent prior to trial enrollment. Eligible participants were randomly assigned to the IM group (low-dose dexzopiclone + IM) or the sham indirect moxibustion (SIM) group (low-dose dexzopiclone + SIM) for an 8-week intervention. The detailed trial protocol has been previously published.

The formal Statistical Analysis Plan (SAP, version 1.0) was finalized and archived prior to participant screening, in accordance with CONSORT 2025 Item 3. This SAP prospectively predefined all confirmatory intergroup comparisons and classified intragroup longitudinal pairwise comparisons as post hoc exploratory analyses. Setting and participants

From November 2022 to November 2023, a total of 122 inpatients with schizophrenia and comorbid insomnia were screened at the Shanghai Mental Health Center. Two patients were excluded due to early hospital discharge before randomization. The remaining 120 eligible participants were randomly allocated to the IM (n = 60) or the SIM group (n = 60). All participants completed the 8-week intervention and assessments, with zero dropouts and missing data.

Diagnostic criteria included

Western Medical Diagnostic Criteria: (1) Diagnosis of schizophrenia based on the International Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th revision (ICD-10), with a Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) score > 60 [25]; (2) meeting the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for insomnia disorder, characterized by difficulty in sleep initiation and maintenance, early awakening, and persistent sleep disturbances occurring no less than three times per week for a minimum of 3 months [9].

TCM Syndrome Diagnostic Criteria: To ensure diagnostic consistency and reduce interpractitioner variability, the TCM diagnosis of heart-spleen deficiency type insomnia was independently determined by two licensed TCM physicians with over 5 years of clinical experience, in strict accordance with the national standard Criteria of Diagnosis and Therapeutic Effect of Diseases and Syndromes in TCM (ZY/T001.1–94) issued by the State Administration of Traditional Chinese Medicine [26]. Any diagnostic disagreements were resolved by a senior TCM expert.

Inclusion criteria

(1) Aged 20–60 years; (2) PSQI score > 7 with recurrent sleep difficulties (≥2 times per week) lasting for at least 1 month; (3) clinically stable disease status with consistent antipsychotic dosage for ≥1 month and no medication adjustment within 2 months prior to enrollment; (4) complete clinical laboratory data; (5) voluntary participation and signed informed consent.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Incomplete baseline data; (2) severe physical illness interfering with trial evaluation; (3) pregnant, breast-feeding, or having plans to conceive.

Withdraw criteria

(1) Occurrence of serious adverse events during the intervention; (2) voluntary withdrawal by participants; (3) disease deterioration requiring urgent clinical intervention; (4) poor adherence to trial medication and intervention protocols.

Randomization and blinding

This study adopted a randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled trial. An independent statistician generated a random sequence (1–120) using Excel, with 1–60 assigned to IM and 61–120 to SIM. Allocation concealment was achieved via sealed opaque envelopes. Participants and outcome assessors were fully blinded to group assignment. Given the operational characteristics of moxibustion intervention, therapists could not be blinded, but all operators followed unified standardized operating procedures and unified explanatory scripts to minimize performance bias.

Intervention

Participants in both the IM and SIM groups received standardized intervention sessions twice a week for 4 consecutive weeks, with 20 min per session and a total of 8 intervention sessions [27]. All interventions were performed by licensed professional acupuncturists with no less than 2 years of clinical experience, who received unified standardized operational training before the trial.

Intervention device and materials

Indirect moxibustion device: The CAJ-210 Indirect Moxibustion Instrument (Zhongshan Jiaxun Medical Device Co., Ltd.) was used for all sessions, equipped with independent programmable heating units to ensure precise and stable skin surface temperature control.

IM patch (Product Code: 2022112501, Zhongshan Jiaxun Medical Device Co., Ltd.): Disposable sterile patch with a 70 × 70 mm heat-resistant non-adhesive base and a central refined mugwort pancake (diameter: 30 mm, thickness: 5 mm, weight: 1.8 ± 0.1 g) (Fig. 2A).Fig. 2Indirect moxibustion (IM) patch

Concomitant treatment and medication control

All participants received fixed low-dose dexzopiclone (1 mg, Chengdu Kanghong Pharmaceutical Group Co., Ltd.) every night throughout the 8-week intervention period. All antipsychotic medications and concomitant clinical treatments were maintained at a stable dosage and regimen for at least 8 weeks before randomization and during the entire trial period to eliminate confounding factors. All concomitant treatments were accurately recorded at each follow-up visit.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome measure

The primary outcome measure employed in this study is the PSQI, a self-administered questionnaire specifically designed to quantify sleep quality and disturbances. It assesses sleep across seven dimensions: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. The PSQI has demonstrated strong concordance with sleep diary entries and polysomnography (PSG) findings in patients diagnosed with primary insomnia. Therefore, it is a recommended assessment instrument for individuals suspected of experiencing insomnia [28, 29]. Scores on the PSQI range from 0 to 21, with incrementally higher scores signifying progressively poorer sleep quality.

Secondary outcome measure

Quality of life was assessed using the Chinese version of the SQLS. The SQLS is a self-rated scale comprising 30 items organized into three subdimensions: psychosocial (encompassing 15 items), motivation and energy (7 items), and symptoms and side effects (8 items) [30]. All items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = never, 1 = rarely, 2 = sometimes, 3 = often, and 4 = always), with for reverse-scored items (items 12, 13, 15, and 20). Each subscale score ranges from 0 to 100, with lower scores reflecting better quality of life [31].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) following the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, with all 120 randomized participants remaining in their assigned groups. No missing data emerged during follow-up, so the predefined LOCF imputation procedure was not implemented.

Baseline demographic and scale comparisons were only used for descriptive reference. Statistical results from baseline tests were not adopted to draw equivalence in randomized cohorts, as random assignment inherently balances participant characteristics.

Longitudinal PSQI and SQLS data collected at baseline, week 4, and week 8 were analyzed via Linear mixed-effects models (LMM) with individual random intercepts, adjusting for age as a fixed covariate. Model estimation relied on REML, and type III sums of squares were applied to test all fixed effects. Tolerability and safety

No severe adverse events or abnormal laboratory indicators were observed in either group during the trial. Only mild transient dizziness occurred in individual participants, with 2 cases in the IM group and 3 cases in the SIM group. The incidence of adverse events was comparable between the two groups (P = 0.647). All mild adverse reactions resolved spontaneously without clinical intervention, confirming the good safety and tolerability of adjuvant IM therapy.

Discussions

Insomnia is a highly prevalent comorbidity in schizophrenia, with well-established detrimental effects on disease progression, neurocognitive function, and functional prognosis of patients [34, 35]. This study systematically evaluated the efficacy of IM as an adjunctive therapy for insomnia in this complex patient population. Based on LMM analyses, 8 weeks of IM combined with low-dose dexzopiclone led to statistically superior and clinically meaningful improvements in sleep and quality of life compared with sham control, with rapid onset and sustained therapeutic effects.

Overall, the IM group showed rapid and significant improvements as early as 4 week, which were maintained through 8 week, supporting IM as a valuable adjunct therapy for insomnia in schizophrenia.

Interpretation of statistical findings and efficacy

In line with our preregistered SAP, we adopted a stratified approach to multiple testing: all prespecified intergroup contrasts (IM vs. SIM at each time point) were adjusted using Bonferroni correction, whereas unplanned within-group comparisons were left unadjusted and treated as exploratory.

Unlike conventional repeated-measures ANOVA, the LMM eliminates the mandatory sphericity assumption, incorporates age as a fixed covariate to reduce demographic confounding, and accounts for individual random differences via random intercepts [36, 37]. The model outputs more precise estimated marginal means and reliable group-by-time interaction estimates, strengthening the credibility of our repeated-measure findings.

Notably, the SIM group also exhibited significant improvement, consistent with a substantial placebo effect commonly observed in trials of physical therapies involving device application and practitioner interaction [38]. This likely reflects positive expectations, therapeutic ritual, and other non-specific factors. However, the IM group demonstrated significantly greater improvement, supporting benefits beyond non-specific effects. This conclusion is reinforced by three key statistical findings: a significant group × time interaction for PSQI total score (F = 37.874, P < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.243), a net between-group difference of −2.115 points (95% CI [−2.795, −1.435]) at 8 weeks.

These benefits align with previous IM research in chronic insomnia and depression [39]. Within schizophrenia complementary therapy research, the 4-week sleep improvement magnitude of IM matches the 6-week outcomes of electroacupuncture and compares favorably with acupuncture and virtual reality interventions [40, 41]. IM demonstrated advantages over electroacupuncture and cognitive-behavioral therapy in key parameters like sleep latency and efficiency [42].

Overall, the IM group showed rapid and significant improvements as early as 4 week, which were maintained through 8 week, supporting IM as a valuable adjunct therapy for insomnia in schizophrenia.

Linking mechanisms to measured outcomes

The therapeutic effects of IM can be understood through integrated physiological and TCM frameworks. Physiologically, stimulation of acupoints PC6, SP6, and HT7 may modulate neuroendocrine stress responses, potentially through cortisol attenuation and melatonin regulation [43]. This provides a plausible basis for the specific PSQI improvements observed: reduced sleep latency may reflect a calmer pre-sleep state, while improved sleep continuity and daytime function suggest more consolidated sleep architecture.

From the TCM perspective, schizophrenia with insomnia is mainly attributed to phlegm-fire disturbing the heart and yin-yang imbalance. The selected acupoints—PC6 (regulating the pericardium and calming the spirit), SP6 (strengthening the spleen and resolving dampness), and HT7 (tranquilizing the mind)—directly address this pathogenesis. IM’s warm stimulation is theorized to unblock meridians, harmonize qi and blood, and restore visceral balance. The significant secondary improvements in SQLS scores, particularly in psychosocial and motivational subdimensions, likely reflect downstream effects of sleep restoration combined with IM’s holistic regulatory influence.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be considered. First, the two-arm trial design cannot completely distinguish the independent efficacy of IM from the synergistic effect of low-dose dexzopiclone. Future three-arm controlled trials (IM + placebo, SIM + dexzopiclone, SIM + placebo) are needed to clarify the independent therapeutic value of IM. Second, the 8-week duration and lack of post-treatment follow-up limit understanding of long-term sustainability. Third, all outcome indicators are subjective scale scores without objective polysomnographic verification. Finally, the single-center design may affect generalizability.

Clinical and policy implications

Combined with the large-scale real-world prescription data reported by Deng and Chen, the over-reliance on benzodiazepines and Z-drugs in Chinese psychiatric clinical practice brings prominent risks of dependence, metabolic disturbance, and cognitive decline [19]. As a non-pharmacological adjunct, IM provides a viable strategy to cut hypnotic exposure while improving sleep and holistic functional status.

Future research should adopt multicenter, large-sample, long-term follow-up designs, combine objective sleep monitoring indicators, and expand to other populations (e.g., insomnia comorbid with mood disorders). Further mechanistic studies focusing on neuroendocrine regulation and neurocognitive improvement are also warranted.

Conclusions

This preliminary 8-week randomized controlled trial confirms that adjunct Indirect Moxibustion (IM) plus low-dose dexzopiclone markedly ameliorates sleep disturbances and overall quality of life among schizophrenia patients with comorbid insomnia, with favorable safety and tolerability profiles. The therapeutic effect of IM emerges rapidly at week 4 and remains stable throughout the entire intervention cycle. Collectively, these outcomes indicate that IM serves as a safe, effective adjunct intervention for managing insomnia complicated by schizophrenia.

Future large-scale, multicenter trials with extended follow-up periods are warranted to validate these results across diverse clinical populations and treatment settings. A three-arm design comparing IM monotherapy, standard pharmacotherapy, and their combination would help elucidate the independent contribution of IM. Further research should also explore the efficacy of IM in other psychiatric conditions with comorbid insomnia and investigate its underlying mechanisms of action (such as changes in cortisol, melatonin, and neuroplasticity markers) through objective sleep measurements.
