A rare complication of pleural drainage: Communicating lung and subcutaneous abscesses
Authors: Søren Sperling, Laura Rishøj Møller, Lise Hommelgaard, Birgitte Siem Christensen, Klaus Leth Mortensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pleural drainage is a key element in the management of parapneumonic effusions. Although generally safe, rare complications may occur. We report a case of a 79-year-old male, admitted with a community-acquired pneumonia and a left-sided complex parapneumonic effusion requiring drainage. Initial treatment with antibiotics and drainage resulted in clinical improvement, and the patient was discharged. Seven weeks later, the patient was re-admitted with clinical deterioration. Imaging revealed a large lung abscess communication with a subcutaneous abscess through a pleurocutaneous fistula at the previous catheter insertion site. Despite transient complications, including hemoptysis during catheter flushing, the patient improved clinically. The subcutaneous abscess resolved without direct intervention, and follow-up imaging demonstrated gradual regression of the lung abscess. This case describes a rare but clinically significant complication of pleural drainage, resulting in fistula formation and dual abscess development. Furthermore, it illustrates that conservative management with targeted drainage and antibiotics may be effective even in complex presentations.

Introduction

Pleural effusions and lung abscesses are well-established complications to respiratory infections [1,2]. Depending on radiological findings and clinical presentation, these entities are typically treated with a combination of drainage and antibiotic therapy [3,4]. While infections related to pleural catheters is well-established with an incidence of 5%, formation of a fistula with dual abscesses is rare, and may result in prolonged therapy and, in some cases, require invasive management [5,6].

We describe a rare complication of pleural drainage resulting in fistula formation and communicating lung and subcutaneous abscesses.

Case presentation

First admission

In April 2025, a 79-year-old male with a history of asthma and ischemic heart disease was admitted with a 10-day history of dyspnea and productive cough. Imaging revealed a left-sided pleural effusion (Fig. 1A). Laboratory tests showed a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 202 mg/L, leukocytosis of 17.7 × 109/L, thrombocytosis of 532 × 109/L, and a pH of 7.45. Due to the need for supplement oxygen, drainage was performed using a 6 French pigtail catheter (Fig. 1B). Ultrasound revealed a thickened pleura, but without septations. A total of 1800 mL of clear fluid was drained over 2 days.Fig. 1(A) Thoracic X-ray showing a left-sided pleural effusion. Prior to admission, the patient had been treated with oral penicillin for 4 days by his general practitioner.

Pleural fluid analysis revealed a high leukocyte count (1676 × 106/L), a LDH of 544 (U/L), and a glucose of 8.1 mmol/L. The effusion was categorized as a simple parapneumonic effusion. No bacteria were isolated from the pleural fluid. Sputum culture revealed growth of pneumococci with intermediate susceptibility to penicillin.

The patient was treated with intravenous amoxicillin/clavulanic acid for 2 days, followed by oral amoxicillin/clavulanic acid for 3 days resulting in a drop in CRP from 202 to 80 mg/L. Due to an increase in CRP to 105 mg/L on day 6 and the development of fever, treatment was shifted to intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam for 3 days, which led to a further decline in CRP to 92 mg/L, and the patient became afebrile. The patient was subsequently discharged after 6 days of hospitalization with oral amoxicillin/clavulanic acid prescribed for additional 5 days.

Second admission

Seven weeks later, the patient was re-admitted in a markedly weakened condition. Regarding antimicrobial therapy, the patient was initially treated with IV piperacillin/tazobactam and oral metronidazole. After microbiological culture of the purulent fluid revealed growth of penicillin-resistant pneumococci, treatment was switched to intravenous ceftriaxone.

Following consultation with thoracic surgery, an 8 French pigtail catheter was placed in the lung abscess under ultrasound guidance, and purulent fluid was drained (Fig. 3). Five days after initiation of drainage, the patient developed severe hemoptysis during saline installation, indicating communication between the lung abscess and the lower airways. Bronchoscopy was performed for lung toilet, revealing no endobronchial lesions suggestive of malignant disease. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid was negative for bacterial growth and malignant cells. After 14 days of treatment, the catheter was removed. Prior to removal of the catheter, CT Imaging showed a marked reduction in the size of both abscesses, the patient reported significant clinical improvement, and inflammatory markers had normalized (CRP decreased from 144 to 6 mg/L; leucocytes from 24.0 × 109/L to 6.74 × 109/L). The patient was discharged with oral moxifloxacin 400 mg daily for additionally 4 weeks.

Outpatient follow-up

One week after discharge, the patient was seen in the outpatient clinic. Apart from reporting minor pleuritic chest pain, the patient was feeling well. Hemoptysis had decreased to a minimal amount, blood tests were within normal limits, and chest X-ray demonstrated slight regression of the lung abscess.

Four weeks after discharge, a follow-up bronchoscopy was performed and revealed no abnormal findings.

Nine weeks after diagnosis of the lung abscess CT scan showed regression in size from 62 × 50 mm × 80 m at discharge to 49 × 42 × 59 mm (Fig. 4A and B). Discussion

This case raises several important considerations regarding the management of a parapneumonic effusion. While parapneumonic effusion is a well-established complication of lower respiratory tract infection, it may also occur secondary to lung cancer or heart failure [7]. In this case, the effusion developed in relation to a lower respiratory infection, and drainage was performed due to dyspnea and the need for supplemental oxygen [2]. In patients who are respiratory stable, catheter insertion is sometimes unnecessary, and potential complications can be avoided [2,8].

During the first admission, Streptococcus pneumoniae was identified in sputum culture with intermediate susceptibility to penicillin. The clinical response to beta-lactam antibiotics varied during admission. This raises the possibility that continuation of amoxicillin/clavulanic acid after discharge may have been insufficient to control the infection. Microbiological analysis from the second admission demonstrated growth of penicillin-resistant S pneumoniae. As strain typing was not performed, we were unable to conclude whether the pneumococcal strain from the first admission had developed full resistance or a new strain was found during the second admission. Regardless of whether the isolates represented the same or different strains, the identification of penicillin-resistant S pneumoniae during the second admission is consistent with incomplete infection control following discharge.

At the second admission, a multidisciplinary consensus on treatment strategy was reached between radiologists, thoracic surgeons, and physicians from infectious diseases and respiratory medicine. As CT imaging indicated communication between the 2 abscesses, it was decided to place a single catheter in the lung abscess. By flushing the lung abscess and applying physical manipulation to the subcutaneous abscess, it was possible to evacuate all purulent material from both sites. Complete regression of the subcutaneous abscess was achieved without surgical intervention into the abscess cavity or fistula. The treating radiologist placed an 8 Fr catheter into the abscess. In cases where abscess or empyema is suspected, the use of a larger catheter (12-14 Fr) may be considered. Thus, it is possible that a larger catheter might have improved the drainage efficacy in this case.

As the patient developed hemoptysis during saline flushing, CT angiography could have been considered to exclude vascular injury or pseudoaneurysm. However, the hemoptysis resolved after cessation of flushing and remained minimal after the volume of saline was reduced. As no further episodes of clinically significant hemoptysis occurred, CT angiography was not performed. In retrospect, CT angiography could reasonably have been performed to exclude vascular injury or pseudoaneurysm.

Although a direct relationship cannot be established between drainage performed during the first admission and the formation of the fistula, several observations support this possibility. First, radiological imaging demonstrated that the fistula tract corresponded to the site of the previous pleural catheter. Second, infection control was probably incomplete at the time of discharge. Alternatively, the fistula may have resulted from progression of necrotizing pneumonia independent of the catheter tract. However, the close anatomical correspondence between the pleurocutaneous fistula and the previous catheter tract makes spread of the infection along the catheter tract a plausible mechanism for fistula formation.

Radiological follow-up demonstrated slow regression of the lung abscess. As blood tests normalized and the patient remained clinically well, antibiotic therapy was discontinued 4 weeks after discharge, despite incomplete radiological resolution. This supports the principle that antibiotic duration should be guided by a combination of clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and radiological response [2]. As 2 bronchoscopies were performed, an underlying malignant disease was considered unlikely. However, in cases with delayed radiological regression, the possibility of an underlying lung cancer should be considered, and transthoracic biopsy from the abscess wall may be warranted [9].

In summary, this case highlights the importance of recognizing a rare delayed complication following pleural drainage. It also illustrates that conservative management with targeted drainage and antibiotics can be effective, even in complex cases of lung and subcutaneous abscesses through a pleurocutaneous fistula.
