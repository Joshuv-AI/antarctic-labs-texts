Mitochondrial genome sequencing reveals mitochondrial single nucleotide polymorphisms and DNA haplotypes associated with egg-laying performance in chickens
Authors: Yu Yang, Cui Wang, Qingjiang Wu, Shengqiang Ye, Yongping Yang, Zheya Sheng, Yanping Feng, Yunguo Qian, Ping Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The relationship between mitochondrial single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) haplotypes and production performance in poultry has been widely reported. Here, the study aimed to investigate the association between mitochondrial SNPs or mtDNA haplotypes and egg-laying performance in Jingmen black-feathered green-shelled laying hens. Egg-laying performance data, including age at first egg (AE) and total egg number by 300 days of age (TE), were collected from 474 hens. Whole mitochondrial genome sequencing was performed using blood-derived DNA to identify mitochondrial SNPs and mtDNA haplotypes, and their associations with egg-laying performance were subsequently evaluated. A total of 101 SNPs were identified in this study. These SNPs were classified into 3 major linkage groups (A, B, and C) and 5 minor subgroups (D-H). Linkage groups A, F and H were significantly associated with TE in hens. The SNP site exerting the strongest effect on TE was 15944C/A/T; hens carrying the A genotype at this locus produced the highest number of eggs by 300 days of age (103.23 eggs), with a maximum SNP effect value of 15.96 eggs and the relative phenotypic difference of 15.46%. Haplotypes within the COX1, ND5 and d-loop region/MIT were significantly associated with TE. The haplotype exerting the strongest effect on total egg number by 300 days of age was identified across the d-loop region and comprised 14 SNPs. Hens carrying the L5 haplotype produced the highest number of eggs by 300 days of age (103.23 eggs), with a maximum SNP effect value of 16.15 eggs and the relative phenotypic difference of 15.65%. However, no mitochondrial SNPs or mtDNA haplotypes were significantly associated with AE. In conclusion, this study not only fills the knowledge gap regarding mitochondrial SNPs and mtDNA haplotypes in Jingmen black-feathered green-shelled laying hens, but also identifies SNPs and haplotypes associated with egg-laying performance, providing an important theoretical basis for the future selection of high-producing hens using mitochondrial genetic markers.

Introduction

Egg-laying performance is one of the most important economic traits in the poultry industry, as it directly determines production efficiency and profitability (Wang et al., 2024). Improving egg production traits, including age at first egg, total egg number and laying rate, has long been a central aim of layer breeding programs (Chen, et al., 2024). Green-Shelled laying hens are a representative local breed in China, valued for their distinctive eggshell color, favorable nutritional properties, and strong adaptability to local environments (Xie, et al., 2025). Noticeably, owing to distinctive consumer preferences in China, green-shelled eggs are more favored than white-shelled eggs. Therefore, improving the egg-laying performance of green-shelled laying hens remains of great importance for breeding programs. Jingmen black-feathered green-shelled laying hens are a high-quality dual-purpose chicken breed (meat and eggs) that has been developed through long-term natural selection in the Jingmen region of Hubei Province, China. This breed reaches sexual maturity at approximately 160 days of age, produces 150-180 eggs annually, and yields green-shelled eggs at a rate exceeding 95%. Moreover, eggs produced by this breed are nutritionally rich, containing abundant selenium, iodine, lecithin, cephalins, and various vitamins (Deng, 2005). In 2020, Jingmen black-feathered green-shelled laying hens were officially included in the National Catalogue of Livestock and Poultry Genetic Resources of China. However, compared with other commercial laying hen breeds or Chinese native dual-purpose chicken breeds (meat and eggs), the egg-laying performance of Jingmen black-feathered green-shelled laying hens remains suboptimal, which limits the further promotion and widespread application of this breed.

Mitochondria play a fundamental role in cellular energy metabolism, apoptosis, and oxidative homeostasis, all of which are critical for ovarian development, follicular maturation, and egg production (Kirillova, et al., 2021; Mani, et al., 2024). In poultry, mitochondrial dysfunction is commonly associated with a reduction in egg production (Zhan, et al., 2024). Accumulating evidence has shown that mitochondrial function is regulated by two genetic systems: the nuclear genome and the mitochondrial genome (Koch, 2016; Ludwig-Slomczynska and Rehm, 2022). Mitochondrial genome is maternally inherited, lacks recombination, and exhibits a relatively high mutation rate, making it a valuable genetic marker for population genetics and trait association studies (Boudali, et al., 2022). The mitochondrial genome of Chinese native chicken is a closed circular molecule (∼16,000 bp) that consists of 13 protein-coding genes, 2 ribosomal RNA (rRNA) coding genes, 22 transfer RNA (tRNA) coding genes, and 1 displacement loop (D-loop) (Jia, et al., 2022; Jin, et al., 2023, 2021). An increasing amount of evidence demonstrated a significant correlation between mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) haplotypes and key production traits in chickens, such as body weight, growth rate, and feed efficiency and other body measurement traits et al. (Li, et al., 2024; Tang, et al., 2022). Moreover, haplotypic and nucleotide diversity within the chicken mitochondrial genome is closely associated with breeding potential (Ran, et al., 2023). Different mtDNA haplotypes may influence chicken production performance by modulating cellular energy metabolism (Kong, et al., 2020). Regrettably, to date, no studies have investigated the relationship between mtDNA haplotypes and egg-laying performance in Jingmen black-feathered green-shelled laying hens.

Therefore, the present study aimed to systematically characterize mitochondrial genome variation in Jingmen black-feathered green-shelled laying hens using high-throughput mtDNA sequencing and identify single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and haplotypes associated with egg-laying traits. By integrating mtDNA SNP analysis, linkage disequilibrium assessment, haplotype construction, and association analysis with age at first egg and total egg number by 300 days of age, this study seeks to reveal mitochondrial genetic markers related to egg-laying performance. The findings are expected to provide new insights into the mitochondrial genetic basis of reproductive traits in laying hens and to offer valuable molecular information for the genetic improvement and conservation of green-shelled chicken resources.

Materials and methods

Animals and DNA extraction

Blood samples and egg-laying performance data of Jingmen black-feathered green-shelled laying hens were obtained from the breeding farm of Hubei Shendi Agricultural Science and Technology Co., Ltd (Hubei Province, China). Fig. 1 shows a Jingmen black-feathered green-shelled cock and hen. The study population comprised 1125 Jingmen black-feathered green-shelled laying hens. The flock represented a breeding population of a single Jingmen black-feathered green-shelled laying-hen line. Mating was conducted according to the principle of avoiding close inbreeding, with a male-to-female ratio of 1:10. Egg production was routinely recorded, and hens with relatively high egg production were preferentially retained for breeding. All hens were housed individually in cages within a modern standardized multi-tier housing system. Adult hens had an average body weight of approximately 1.4 kg. Egg production records, including age at first egg and total egg number by 300 days of age (from the onset of laying to 300 days of age, corresponding to approximately 43 weeks of age), were collected for all hens. All hens were maintained under the same standardized housing, lighting, temperature-management, and feeding conditions throughout the recording period, thereby minimizing variation attributable to these environmental factors. A total of 500 hens were randomly selected from the population for blood sampling. Blood samples were collected from live hens specifically for this study using anticoagulant tubes. The sampled birds were not specifically selected on the basis of pedigree or genomic relatedness, and individual relatedness was not further assessed.

Genomic DNA was extracted from whole blood using a blood DNA extraction kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Cat. no. D3494-04). DNA concentration (≥100 ng/μL) and purity (A260/A280 ratio: 1.8-2.0) were assessed using a Nano-100 spectrophotometer, and then DNA samples were diluted to a final concentration of 50 ng/μL. After quality assessment, blood-derived genomic DNA from 474 hens was qualified for mitochondrial whole-genome sequencing.

Mitochondrial whole genome sequencing

High-throughput sequencing of the mitochondrial genome: The complete mitochondrial genome was amplified by long-range PCR using the chicken mitochondrial reference genome sequence (GenBank No.: NC_053523) as the template for primer design. The primer sequences were GF, 5′-GTTGCGTCCTATCCTAGTCCTCTC-3′, and GR, 5′-GCAGGTGTAGTCCAGGCTTCACTTG-3′, generating an amplicon of 16788 bp. The amplified mitochondrial genomes were subjected to Fast NGS sequencing. Sequencing depth was assessed across the assembled mitochondrial genome, and SNPs, insertions and deletions were identified through depth-based variant filtering. Genome-wide mtDNA variation was subsequently summarized and analyzed. Fast NGS sequencing was conducted by Wuhan LingSi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

Long-range touchdown PCR was performed in a total volume of 50 μL containing 11.50 μL nuclease-free water, 25 μL 2 × PCR Buffer for KOD FX Neo, 10 μL 2 mmol/L dNTPs, 0.75 μL each of the GF and GR primers, 1 μL genomic DNA and 1 μL KOD FX Neo DNA polymerase (1 U/μL, KOD FX Neo, Cat. no. KFX-201). The amplification programme consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min, followed by 5 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s and extension at 74 °C for 17 min; 5 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s and extension at 72 °C for 17 min; 5 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s and extension at 70 °C for 17 min; and 20 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s and extension at 68 °C for 17 min. A final extension was performed at 72 °C for 10 min.

Sequencing of the mitochondrial genome PCR products: With reference to the chicken mitochondrial genome sequence (GenBank No.: NC_053523), a total of 20 pairs of mitochondrial primers covering the entire chicken mitochondrial genome were designed using Primer Premier 5, with each amplicon ranging from 800 to 1,000 bp in length. The chicken mitochondrial genome was amplified by PCR. PCR reactions were carried out in a total volume of 25 μL containing 12.5 μL MegaFi™ Pro Fidelity 2 × PCR Master Mix, 9.5 μL nuclease-free H₂O, 1 μL of each forward and reverse primer (10 μM), and 1 μL DNA template. The PCR cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 min; 35 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 15 s, annealing for 15 s, and extension at 72°C for 30 s; followed by a final extension at 72°C for 5 min and restoring at room temperature. PCR products were purified and sequenced by bidirectional Sanger sequencing. The sequencing method used is Sanger. Mitogenome sequences were assembled using Seqman software (DNASTAT Inc., Madison, WI, USA) and annotated using MITOS WebServer (http://mitos.bioinf.unileipzig.de) (Peng, et al., 2021). Genomic DNA quality was assessed using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Raw sequencing reads were filtered using fastp software (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp)) to remove low-quality reads and adapter contamination. Clean reads were used for mitochondrial genome assembly and sequence comparison. The assembled mitochondrial genome sequences were aligned against the chicken mitochondrial reference genome (GenBank No.: NC_053523) using BLAST and MAFFT software. SNPs and insertion/deletion variants were identified based on sequence alignment results and subsequently filtered according to sequencing depth and genotype quality. The following filtering criteria were applied: sequencing depth ≥30 ×, genotype quality (GQ) ≥30, and genotype call rate >98.39%.

MtDNA SNPs analysis

Using the published chicken mitochondrial genome sequence deposited in GenBank (GenBank No.: NC_053523) as the reference, SNPs information was identified based on sequencing chromatograms and sequence alignments. The mitochondrial sequences were translated according to the vertebrate mitochondrial genetic code, and SNPs loci were subsequently analyzed.

Association analysis between SNPs and egg-laying performance

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis was performed using the online SHEsis website (Chong, et al., 2018), and haplotypes were constructed with PHASE software (version 2.1).

Statistical analysis

Egg-laying performance traits are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Association was considered statistically significant when the P value < 0.05. Associations between mtDNA loci and egg-laying traits were analyzed using the general linear model (GLM) procedure implemented in SAS software (version 9.4M8; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Batch and genotype were included as fixed effects. The model was defined as follows:Y=μ+Batch+Genotype+ewhere Y represents age at first egg (AE), μ is the overall population mean, Batch represents the fixed effect of batch, Genotype represents the fixed effect of genotype, and e is the random residual error.

Notably, when analyzing the association between mtDNA loci and total egg number by 300 days of age (TE), age at first egg was included as a covariate in the GLM. The model was specified as:Y=μ+Batch+Genotype+e+AFE

Where Y represents total egg number by 300 days of age (TE), μ is the overall population mean, Batch represents the fixed effect of batch, Genotype represents the fixed effect of genotype, and e is the random residual error, AFE represents age at first egg fitted as a covariate.

For multiple testing correction, Bonferroni-adjusted significance thresholds were applied. In the SNP association analyses, the 13 SNPs were classified into 8 independent linkage groups based on the linkage disequilibrium structure; therefore, the significance threshold was set at (α = 0.05/8 = 0.00625). In the haplotype association analyses, the mitochondrial genome was divided into 7 functional regions, resulting in an adjusted significance threshold of (α = 0.05/7 = 0.00714). For the triallelic locus 15944 C/A/T and the haplotype analyses, Tukey’s honestly significant difference test (HSD) was used for post hoc pairwise comparisons when the overall analysis of variance (ANOVA) result was significant.

Results

Determination of SNPs in the mitochondrial genome

By sequencing the mitochondrial genomes of 474 Jingmen black-feathered green-shelled laying hens, a total of 101 SNPs were identified. Among these variants, 4 were INDELs (3.96%), which were predominantly located in the small rRNA (sRNA) region. Transitions (C/T or A/G) accounted for 93 SNPs (92.08%), while transversions (A/C, A/T, G/C, or G/T) comprised 3 SNPs (2.97%). 1 SNP (0.99%) was classified as a special site exhibiting both transition and transversion characteristics.

Regarding genomic distribution, 5 SNPs (4.95%) were located in tRNA regions, 9 SNPs (8.91%) were located in sRNA regions, 30 SNPs (29.70%) were located in the d-loop region, 55 SNPs (54.46%) were located in protein-coding sequences (CDS), and 2 SNPs (1.98%) were located in intergenic regions. Within the CDSs, SNPs were detected in all 12 mitochondrial protein-coding genes except ATP synthase F0 subunit 8 (ATP8), with the highest number observed in the Cytochrome b (Cytb) (10 SNPs). A total of 9 nonsynonymous mutations were identified in the coding regions, including 3 in NADH dehydrogenase subunit 2 (ND2), 1 in Cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (COX1), 1 in Cytochrome c oxidase LD analysis of SNP sites

Among the identified 101 SNPs, 19 exhibited a minor allele frequency (MAF) of less than 5%, and 6 SNPs (designated as N-type) showed only a single genotype within the population. Although the 6 loci in group N differed from the reference sequence, they were invariant within the study population. Therefore, these loci were excluded from the subsequent association analyses. Pronounced LD was observed among these SNPs, allowing them to be classified into three major linkage groups (A, B, and C) as well as additional minor groups (D-H).

The A linkage group comprised 2 subgroups, A1 and A2. Subgroup A1 consisted of 10 completely linked SNPs, whereas subgroup A2 included 6 completely linked SNPs. Incomplete linkage was observed between A1 and A2, with 6 discordant individuals. This discrepancy was attributed to a set of 11 completely linked low-frequency SNPs (designated S1; MAF < 5%).

The B linkage group was divided into 2 subgroups, B1 and B2. Subgroup B1 contained 25 completely linked SNPs, while B2 comprised a single SNP. Incomplete linkage between B1 and B2 resulted in 11 discordant individuals, which was jointly caused by 2 sets of low-frequency SNPs: a group of 3 completely linked SNPs (S2) and a group of 2 completely linked SNPs (S3).

The C linkage group included 4 subgroups (C1-C4). Subgroup C1 consisted of 20 completely linked SNPs, C2 contained 4 completely linked SNPs, and C3 and C4 each comprised a single SNP. All of C2, C3, and C4 showed incomplete linkage with C1. Specifically, 8 discordant individuals were observed between C2 and C1 due to S2; 11 discordant individuals between C3 and C1 were jointly caused by S2 and S3; and 16 discordant individuals between C4 and C1 were attributed to the combined effects of S2, S3, and an additional group of 3 completely linked low-frequency SNPs (S4).

The D linkage group consisted of 2 completely linked SNPs, whereas the G linkage group comprised 3 completely linked SNPs. Groups E, F, and H each contained a single SNP. Discussion

An increasing amount of evidence indicates that mtDNA polymorphisms are closely associated with economic traits in livestock. For example, mtDNA polymorphisms have been reported to be significantly associated with porcine backfat thickness (Liu, et al., 2023), porcine carcass loin thickness (Molinero, et al., 2025), porcine litter size (Liu, et al., 2020), and milk production traits in Holstein cattle (Brajkovic, et al., 2025). Additionally, mtDNA polymorphisms have also been reported to be significantly associated with production traits in poultry. For example, Li et al. reported a pronounced association between mtDNA haplotypes and body weight as well as morphological traits, such as chest depth, chest width, tibial length, tibial circumference, in Wuliangshan black-bone chickens (Li, et al., 2024). Tang et al. analyzed haplotypes in the mitochondrial d-loop region across different broiler breeds and identified several haplotypes associated with birth weight and feed conversion ratio (Xiujun, et al., 2023). Another study also identified a mitochondrial variant associated with higher body weight and greater egg specific gravity (Li, et al., 1998). Therefore, these findings suggest that mitochondrial DNA variations could induce phenotypic variation in chickens. Here, we explored mitochondrial genome polymorphisms in Jingmen black-feathered green-shelled laying hens by employing mitochondrial genome sequencing and analyzed their associations with egg-laying performance.

Through mitochondrial genome sequencing, we identified a total of 101 SNPs locus in the mitochondrial genome of Jingmen black-feathered green-shelled laying hens. These SNPs were predominantly distributed in CDS region (54.46%), followed by the d-loop region (29.70%). SNPs were detected in all 12 mitochondrial protein-coding genes except ATP8, with the highest number observed in the Cytb gene (10 SNPs). Linkage disequilibrium analysis revealed that these SNPs could be classified into 3 major linkage groups (A, B, and C) as well as 5 minor groups (D-H). Notably, only linkage groups A, F and H showed significant associations with total egg number by 300 days of age in Jingmen black-feathered green-shelled laying hens. And more notably, there was no linkage group that was significantly associated with age at first egg. Furthermore, haplotypes within the COX1, ND5 and d-loop region/MIT were significantly associated with total egg number by 300 days of age. However, these SNP haplotypes were not significantly associated with age at first egg. Further, 8 SNPs locus were identified in the COX1 gene, forming 4 major haplotypes, 7 SNPs locus were identified in the ND5 gene, forming 4 major haplotypes and 1 pooled Nx haplotype. Additionally, The d-loop region/MIT comprised 5 major haplotypes and 1 pooled Lx haplotype.

COX1 is a mitochondrial-encoded gene that encodes cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1, a core catalytic subunit of cytochrome c oxidase (Complex IV) (Dennerlein, et al., 2023; Witte, et al., 2023). COX1 mediates the transfer of electrons from cytochrome c to molecular oxygen, thereby ensuring proper operation of the electron transport chain and maintaining the proton gradient required for ATP synthesis. Dysfunction of COX1 would damage electron transfer efficiency and diminish the proton gradient, ultimately leading to decreased ATP production (Yang, et al., 2022). Notably, Shakeri et al. reported that downregulation of COX1 gene expression could lead to impaired mitochondrial function in broiler breast muscle cells, accompanied by a decrease in ATP synthesis (Shakeri, et al., 2023). In addition, other studies have reported that COX1 in mtDNA can also regulate the expression of nuclear genes. Zhu et al. found that variation in mitochondrial COX1 downregulated the expression of the nuclear gene dct-15 in Caenorhabditis elegans, thereby reducing lifespan, developmental rate, and locomotor capacity (Zhu, et al., 2022). ND5 encodes NADH dehydrogenase subunit 5, a component of mitochondrial respiratory chain Complex I (Zhang and Vik, 2021). Complex I is the entry enzyme of the electron transport chain and the largest complex within the oxidative phosphorylation system. As a core transmembrane subunit of Complex I, ND5 participates in electron transfer from NADH to ubiquinone and in proton translocation from the mitochondrial matrix to the intermembrane space. Dysfunction of ND5 can impair electron flow and proton translocation, thereby reducing ATP production (Giorgi, et al., 2025; Xuan, et al., 2024).

D-loop region is a non-coding control region of the mitochondrial genome. However, d-loop region plays a crucial role in the regulation of mtDNA replication and transcription (Clayton, 1982; Zhang, et al., 2022). Consequently, functional variations in the d-loop region may alter mtDNA replication or transcription, thereby indirectly affecting respiratory-chain assembly and cellular energy metabolism. Compared with protein-coding regions, the d-loop exhibits a higher mutation rate and greater sequence variability, making it one of the most polymorphic regions of the mitochondrial genome (Nicholls and Minczuk, 2014). Notably, accumulating evidence suggests that polymorphisms in the d-loop region may also have functional consequences by altering the efficiency of mitochondrial replication and transcription, thereby affecting cellular energy metabolism (Falkenberg, et al., 2024; Picard, et al., 2016). Notably, other results from the literature reported that mtDNA d-loop region haplotypes were significantly associated with production traits in livestock. Liu et al. identified 48 polymorphic sites in the mitochondrial DNA d-loop region, of which 30 variants were associated with porcine backfat thickness (Liu, et al., 2023). In addition, Liu et al. found 53 polymorphic sites in the mtDNA d-loop region, with 13 variants showing significant associations with oocyte number (Liu, et al., 2019). And more notably, Li et al. reported that mtDNA d-loop region haplotypes were significantly associated with production traits in chickens (Li, et al., 2024). Here, we identified 5 major haplotypes and 1 pooled Lx haplotype in the mitochondrial d-loop region. These haplotypes were significantly associated with the total number of eggs produced by 300 days of age. In conclusion, we speculated that variation in the mitochondrial d-loop region can regulate egg-laying performance by controlling mitochondrial biogenesis.

Egg production is a highly energy-dependent physiological process in poultry (Hu, et al., 2025; Li, et al., 2025). The initiation and maintenance of laying require a sustained and efficient supply of ATP to support follicular development, yolk precursor synthesis and transport, ovulation, and oviductal secretion. A previous study found that, in hens at the peak laying stage (35 weeks of age), atretic large white follicles exhibited significantly reduced OXPHOS and disrupted mitochondrial dynamics compared with normal large white follicles (Hu, et al., 2025). Additionally, compared with hens at 35 weeks of age, those at 70 weeks exhibited significantly reduced OXPHOS activity and disrupted mitochondrial dynamics in their large white follicles. These findings suggested that impaired OXPHOS and mitochondrial dynamic imbalance may promote large white follicle atresia, thereby compromising egg-laying performance in hens. Therefore, we speculated that the differences in egg-laying performance among haplotypes may be attributable to variation in their capacity to regulate ATP production. Here, we found that hens carrying the O2, N4 and L5 haplotypes produced more eggs by 300 days of age. We speculated that these hens may have a greater capacity for efficient ATP production, thereby better meeting the high energy demands of egg formation and supporting the production of more eggs within the same period. However, this hypothesis requires further experimental validation.

Moreover, the lower laying efficiency observed in hens carrying certain haplotypes may also be associated with dysfunction of mitochondria. As poultry age, ovarian function gradually declines, as evidenced by increased autophagy and apoptosis in ovarian and follicular tissues, together with reduced steroid hormone synthesis, ultimately leading to impaired egg-laying performance (Zhan, et al., 2024). This age-related deterioration is closely associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and may be further exacerbated by a decline in antioxidant capacity. Similarly, another study also revealed that, with advancing age, the expression of genes involved in mitochondrial function, oxidative stress regulation and mitophagy was significantly downregulated in the small white follicles of laying hens, which hinted the mitochondrial dysfunction (Poudel, et al., 2026). Contrarily, reducing nuclear and mitochondrial DNA damage in laying hens could improve their productive performance, such as laying rate, eggshell quality and egg weight (Makarenko, et al., 2019). Notably, mitochondrial dysfunction and certain unfavorable mtDNA haplotypes may promote oxidative stress by impairing OXPHOS and increasing electron leakage from the respiratory chain (Esposito, et al., 1999). Moreover, oxidative stress would induce the degradation of mitochondrial DNA, which could exacerbate the mitochondrial dysfunction (Shokolenko, et al., 2009). Excessive oxidative stress may promote granulosa cell death and follicular atresia, thereby reducing the pool of follicles available for ovulation and ultimately impairing egg-laying performance (Kang, et al., 2018; Zhu, et al., 2021). Therefore, we speculate that certain haplotypes associated with lower egg production may confer mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby increasing oxidative stress and apoptosis in ovarian and follicular tissues. This may reduce the number of follicles available for ovulation while promoting follicular atresia, ultimately resulting in decreased egg production.

Surprisingly, we found no significant association between mitochondrial SNPs and age at first egg in Jingmen black-feathered green-shelled laying hens. We speculated that this may be attributable to the strict management practices used on the farm to control the onset of laying. In commercial production, hens are managed to reach sexual maturity within a relatively narrow age range, thereby synchronizing the peak laying period and maximizing overall egg production efficiency. Moreover, hens that began laying unusually early and deviated from the typical age at first egg of the flock may have been culled during the stringent selection process.

Although we identified mitochondrial genome SNPs and haplotypes associated with egg-laying performance in Jingmen black-feathered green-shelled laying hens through mitochondrial genome sequencing, several limitations remain. Firstly, the potential mechanisms underlying the differences in egg-laying performance among different haplotypes were not investigated. Further studies are needed to determine the mechanisms through which different mitochondrial haplotypes influence egg-laying performance in chickens. Secondly, the population analyzed consisted of a single indigenous chicken breed maintained under specific management conditions. Therefore, the generalizability of these findings to other chicken breeds or production systems remains to be determined and should be validated in independent populations. Thirdly, egg production is a polygenic trait largely determined by nuclear genomic variation, and the contribution of mito-nuclear interactions cannot be excluded because nuclear genomic information was not analyzed in the present study. Therefore, these mitochondrial variants should be interpreted as potential markers associated with laying performance rather than causal genetic determinants. Future studies integrating mitochondrial and nuclear genomic data, combined with functional validation of mitochondrial metabolism, are needed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying these associations.

Conclusion

This study identified mitochondrial genome polymorphisms and haplotypes associated with egg-laying performance in Jingmen black-feathered green-shelled laying hens. Key haplotypes within the COX1, ND5, and d-loop regions were significantly associated with total egg number by 300 days of age. However, the observed effect sizes represent phenotypic differences among mitochondrial haplotype groups and should not be interpreted as the proportion of genetic variance explained by mtDNA variants. Overall, our results provided valuable insights into the mitochondrial genetic basis of reproductive traits and highlight mtDNA haplotypes as potential maternal markers for genetic improvement in indigenous laying hens.
