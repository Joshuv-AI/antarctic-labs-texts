Heart failure types and predictors of 30-day rehospitalisation among patients aged ≥ 60 years with acute heart failure: a multicentre prospective study in Vietnam
Authors: Huan Thanh Nguyen, Tam Huu Thai, Hau Thi Mai Pham
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe burden of heart failure (HF) is increasing due to global population ageing. However, there is limited information on the prevalence and adverse outcomes of different HF types among older patients with acute HF. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence of HF types and identify predictors of 30-day rehospitalisation among patients aged ≥ 60 years with acute HF.MethodsThis multicentre prospective study included patients aged ≥ 60 years with acute HF. Patients were followed for 30 days post-discharge. Predictors of rehospitalisation for HF were analysed using Cox proportional hazards regression.ResultsOf the 277 patients (median age, 76 years [interquartile range, 67–82.5]; male, 46.2%), the prevalence of HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF), and HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) was 49.5%, 20.5%, and 30.0%, respectively. During the 30-day follow-up, rehospitalisation for HF occurred in 42 patients (30.7%) in the HFrEF group, 12 patients (21.1%) in the HFmrEF group, and 12 patients (14.5%) in the HFpEF group (P = 0.02). Significant predictors of rehospitalisation for HF included pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) ≥ 40 mmHg (hazard ratio [HR], 2.37; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.39–4.03; P = 0.002) and guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) (HR, 0.50; 95% CI, 0.25–0.97; P = 0.040).ConclusionsAmong patients aged ≥ 60 years with acute HF, HFrEF was the most prevalent type and had the highest incidence of 30-day rehospitalisation. Elevated PASP was associated with an increased risk of rehospitalisation, while GDMT was linked to a reduced risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05478-x.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is defined as a clinical syndrome resulting from structural and/or functional abnormalities of the heart [1]. Despite significant advances in management over recent decades, HF remains a critical endpoint of cardiovascular disease and a leading cause of global mortality and morbidity [2]. The prevalence and incidence of HF notably increase with age [2, 3]. In 2012, the prevalence of HF among adults aged 65 to 70 years was 4.3%, and it is projected to rise to 8.5% by 2030 [3].

In older adults, HF often presents with distinct clinical characteristics and may respond inadequately to standard management strategies [4]. These patients face a higher risk of adverse cardiovascular events and poorer prognoses compared with younger individuals [5]. This disparity is attributed to progressive physiological ageing, declining organ function, complex comorbidities, and geriatric syndromes, which contribute to increased vulnerability and diminished reserve capacity in older adults [6]. Beyond these issues, acute HF is a leading cause of hospitalisation in older adults and is associated with increased mortality, reduced quality of life, and a substantial economic burden [7]. Despite these challenges, few studies have assessed the characteristics of HF and post-discharge outcomes among older patients hospitalised for acute HF.

Although HF has been extensively studied, older adults are often underrepresented in major clinical trials [8]. Consequently, the management of HF in this population is largely informed by subgroup analyses, which may lead to misinterpretations regarding the benefits of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) [9]. Furthermore, since the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines categorise HF into HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF), and HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) [1], there is limited clinical information on these HF types specifically within the ageing population. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of HF types and investigate the incidence and predictors of 30-day post-discharge rehospitalisation for HF among patients aged ≥ 60 years hospitalised for acute HF.

Materials and methods

Study design, participants, and data collection

This multicentre prospective study was conducted among patients aged ≥ 60 years who were admitted for acute HF at three hospitals in Vietnam: The University Medical Center of Ho Chi Minh City, Thong Nhat Hospital, and Vinh Long Province General Hospital, from June 2023 to May 2024. Eligible participants were assessed before hospital discharge by the investigators to collect sociodemographic and geriatric characteristics, medical history, and HF-specific details through interviews and electronic medical records. Exclusion criteria included active malignancy, acute HF secondary to acute coronary syndromes, and the presence of moderate to severe mitral and/or aortic valve disease (except for mitral regurgitation secondary to left ventricular dilatation). Clinical trial registration: not applicable.

Baseline characteristics

Sociodemographic characteristics

Age was classified into two groups: 60–74 years and ≥ 75 years. Sex was categorised as male or female. Patients were classified according to their place of residence as living in urban or rural areas. Marital status was recorded as married, single, or widowed/divorced. Educational attainment was stratified as pre–senior high school, senior high school, or tertiary education. According to the World Health Organization guidelines for the Asia–Pacific region, body mass index (BMI) was classified as underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–22.9 kg/m2), overweight (23.0–24.9 kg/m2), or obese (≥ 25 kg/m2).

Geriatric characteristics

Functional status was evaluated using the Katz Index of Activities of Daily Living (ADL) and the Lawton Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) scales. Patients were coded as having limitations in ADL or IADL if they self-reported being unable to complete one or more tasks in each scale. The 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale was used to assess depressive symptoms; a total score of ≥ 6 indicated depression. Nutritional status was assessed using the Mini Nutritional Assessment–Short Form, with malnutrition defined as a total score of ≤ 7. Frailty was screened using the Edmonton Frail Scale, which includes nine domains; a total score of ≥ 8 was considered indicative of frailty.

Characteristics of heart failure

The reason for admission (dyspnoea, chest pain, or leg swelling), the form of acute HF (decompensated or de novo), and the New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification before the index hospitalisation were recorded. According to the ESC guidelines for HF, HF was categorised into three types based on left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF): HFrEF (LVEF ≤ 40%), HFmrEF (LVEF 41–49%), and HFpEF (LVEF ≥ 50%) [1]. The LVEF measured after haemodynamic stabilisation and in the absence of intravenous inotropes during the current admission was used to determine HF type.

Measurements of the left ventricular internal diameter in diastole (LVIDd), left atrial diameter (LAd), pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) on parasternal long-axis view, and biplane Simpson’s LVEF were performed in accordance with established guidelines [10]. A dilated left ventricle was defined as LVIDd > 55 mm, and a dilated left atrium was defined as LAd > 40 mm. Concentric left ventricular hypertrophy was identified based on an increased left ventricular mass index and relative wall thickness > 0.42 [10]. Moderate-to-severe functional mitral regurgitation secondary to left ventricular dysfunction was observed, with normal mitral valve leaflets and chordae.

Several measures were implemented to minimise variability in echocardiographic assessments. Specifically, all examinations were performed by cardiologists with over 10 years of echocardiography experience at each centre; all sites used the same echocardiography system (EPIQ 7 C, Philips Healthcare); and PASP was measured and calculated in accordance with guideline-recommended protocols using tricuspid regurgitation velocity and inferior vena cava dimensions [11].

Treatment of heart failure

In accordance with the ESC guidelines [1, 12], quadruple therapy was defined as the concurrent use of four classes of medication: an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEI), an angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) or angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI), a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA), a beta-blocker, and a sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor (SGLT2i). The use of quadruple therapy before discharge and at 30 days post-discharge in patients with HFrEF, along with SGLT2i in those with HFmrEF or HFpEF, was considered GDMT.

Follow-up, study endpoints, and handling of censoring

A follow-up visit was scheduled within 10 days after discharge. Two follow-up phone calls were made on days 20 and 30 post-discharge to patients or their family members to assess survival status and rehospitalisation. The time to the first occurrence of either death or rehospitalisation was treated as censored data. For patients without events, loss to follow-up, or withdrawal from the study, the censoring date was defined as the date of the last telephone follow-up. Hospital electronic medical records were used to confirm rehospitalisation events. Rehospitalisation for HF was defined as an admission due to worsening HF signs or symptoms requiring intravenous treatment.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated for the first objective using the single-population proportion formula: n = Z21− α/2*[p*(1-p)/d2], where n is the required minimum sample size, Z1− α/2 = 1.96 (for α = 0.05 and a 95% confidence interval), and d is the margin of error, assumed to be 0.06. Because the prevalence of HF types according to the ESC guidelines among Vietnamese patients aged ≥ 60 years with acute HF is unknown, p was set at 0.5 to yield the maximum possible value of p(1 − p) = 0.25. Therefore, the study required a minimum of 267 participants.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (%). Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations for normally distributed data, or as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs; 25th–75th percentiles) for non-normally distributed data. The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare medians among the three groups. Time-to-event data were analysed using Kaplan–Meier estimates and Cox proportional hazards regression models. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and two-sided P values were derived from the Cox models. Variables with P < 0.10 in the univariable analysis were entered into the multivariable regression model to identify independent predictors of 30-day rehospitalisation. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

From June 2023 to May 2024, a total of 335 patients aged ≥ 60 years were hospitalised for acute HF across the participating hospitals. We excluded 45 patients with acute coronary syndromes, nine patients with severe degenerative valvular regurgitation or stenosis, three patients with active malignancy, and one patient who did not provide informed consent. The remaining 277 patients aged ≥ 60 years included in the analysis had a median age of 76 years (IQR, 67–82.5; range, 60–95). Supplementary Figure S1 presents the flow diagram of patient enrolment and follow-up process. The overall prevalence of HFrEF, HFmrEF, and HFpEF was 49.5% (n = 137), 20.6% (n = 57), and 29.9% (n = 83), respectively.

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of patients according to HF type. There were no significant differences in age, sex, or geriatric characteristics among the three HF groups. Notably, patients with HFpEF were more likely to live in urban areas and to be obese compared with those with HFrEF or HFmrEF. Regarding medical history, dyslipidaemia, hypertension, and chronic coronary syndromes were the most prevalent conditions in the study population. Chronic coronary syndromes were reported more frequently among patients with HFrEF than among those with HFmrEF or HFpEF.

Table 3 presents the treatment and 30-day post-discharge adverse outcomes by HF type. At discharge, patients with HFrEF were more frequently prescribed ARNI, MRA, and furosemide compared with those with HFmrEF or HFpEF. The proportions of patients prescribed ACEI, ARB, beta-blockers, and SGLT2i were similar across the three HF types. Among patients with HFrEF, there was an increase in the prescription of renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system inhibitors (ARNI, ACEI, or ARB), MRA, and SGLT2i, but a decrease in beta-blocker prescriptions before discharge compared with pre-admission. Conversely, patients with HFmrEF or HFpEF demonstrated an increase in SGLT2i prescriptions before discharge compared with pre-admission. During the 30-day follow-up, no patients were lost to follow-up. Rehospitalisation for HF occurred more frequently in the HFrEF group compared with the HFmrEF and HFpEF groups. Discussion

This is the first study to report the prevalence of HF types, as well as the incidence and predictors of short-term rehospitalisation for HF, among patients aged ≥ 60 years with acute HF in Vietnam. Our findings indicate that approximately half of the patients had HFrEF, with notable differences among the three HF groups. Elevated PASP and the use of GDMT were identified as key predictors of 30-day rehospitalisation for HF.

Prevalence of heart failure types

HF represents a major global cardiovascular burden, with around one in four individuals at risk of developing HF during their lifetime, and advancing age being a principal risk factor for its onset [13]. In older adults, increased susceptibility to HF is attributed to the accumulation of senescent cardiomyocytes, impaired mitochondrial energy metabolism, apoptosis, geriatric syndromes, and multiple comorbidities [14]. Consequently, older adults are more likely to develop HF than younger individuals, resulting in a higher prevalence of HF in this population compared with the general population [13]. However, following the 2021 update of the ESC guidelines defining HF types according to left ventricular systolic function [1], there remains limited evidence describing the distribution of HF types specifically among older populations.

In population-based studies from Denmark, Spain, and China, HFrEF was identified as the most common type. However, these studies also reported a gradual shift towards an increasing prevalence of HFmrEF and HFpEF, with HFpEF being particularly common among older adults [15–17]. Among patients hospitalised for acute HF, our findings are consistent with reports from the United States, Sweden, and India, which identified HFrEF as the predominant type [18–21]. In contrast, a recent study from Japan demonstrated a predominance of HFpEF among patients admitted with a primary diagnosis of HF [22]. Furthermore, the Registro Nacional de Insuficiencia Cardiaca (RICA) study in Spain, which included individuals aged ≥ 70 years, found that HFpEF was the most prevalent type (65%), followed by HFrEF and HFmrEF [23].

Our study demonstrated a similar rising trend in the distribution of HF types, with HFrEF being more common than HFpEF and HFmrEF. Several factors may contribute to the discrepancy between our findings and those of the RICA study, likely arising from differences in the age profiles and comorbidity patterns of the study populations. First, the burden of HFpEF increases with advancing age [24]. While our study included patients aged ≥ 60 years, the RICA study focused on those aged ≥ 70 years, of whom 64.7% were aged ≥ 80 years [23]. Second, our study population had a higher proportion of chronic coronary syndromes compared with the RICA study (76.2% vs. 27%). Ischaemic heart disease is a predominant cause of HFrEF, as myocardial dysfunction results from multifactorial pathophysiological processes such as myocardial cell death and fibrosis [25]. Consistent with this, previous evidence indicates that patients with HFrEF are more likely to have coronary artery disease than those with HFpEF [26]. Collectively, these observations suggest that the prevalence of HF types among patients hospitalised for acute HF may vary according to population characteristics, including age and comorbidities.

Differences between heart failure types

Clinically, HF is categorised into three types based on LVEF, each exhibiting distinct pathophysiology, characteristics, management strategies, and clinical outcomes [1]. Previous research has identified older age, female sex, obesity, and comorbidities such as atrial fibrillation and hypertension as key characteristics of HFpEF, whereas ischaemic heart disease and mitral regurgitation are more strongly associated with HFrEF [27–29]. HFmrEF is considered an intermediate type, sharing greater similarities with HFrEF, particularly in relation to the prevalence of ischaemic heart disease [30].

Our study corroborates these findings and provides two additional insights into differences among the three HF types in patients aged ≥ 60 years hospitalised for acute HF. First, we observed a significantly higher proportion of urban residents in the HFpEF group. This disparity may be attributed to variations in biological and behavioural characteristics, as well as sociocultural environments, between urban and rural populations [31]. Given that physical inactivity and higher BMI are associated with an increased risk of HFpEF [32], our findings align with previous studies showing that older adults living in rural areas of Vietnam tend to be more physically active through agricultural work and have significantly lower BMI than their urban counterparts [33, 34]. However, further research is warranted to clarify the influence of living environment and lifestyle factors on the risk of developing different HF types.

Second, our study observed a trend towards an increased prevalence of depression across the HFpEF, HFmrEF, and HFrEF groups. Depressive symptoms frequently co-occur with HF and are associated with adverse outcomes in older patients [35]. Both conditions share overlapping pathophysiological mechanisms, including dysregulated inflammation and heightened sympathetic nervous system activity [36]. However, previous studies examining the prevalence of depression among different HF types have produced inconsistent findings [37–39]. Gastelurrutia et al.. and Vu et al., who studied outpatient clinic populations, reported a higher prevalence of depression in patients with HFpEF compared with those with HFrEF [37, 38]. In contrast, Xu et al.., focusing on hospitalised patients as in our study, found a greater prevalence of depression among those with HFrEF than HFpEF [39]. These discrepancies may reflect differences in study settings (inpatients versus outpatients). Furthermore, the NYHA functional class has been shown to correlate positively with depressive symptoms [40]. In both our study and that of Xu et al. [39]., patients with HFrEF had the highest proportion of NYHA class III/IV, whereas Gastelurrutia et al.. reported a higher prevalence of advanced NYHA class among those with HFpEF [37]. Collectively, our findings and prior evidence suggest that the severity of HF may influence the burden of depression across different HF types.

Predictors of 30-day rehospitalisation for heart failure

Thirty-day post-discharge rehospitalisation following an acute HF event remains a major concern in clinical practice, with reported rates ranging from 10.9% to 24.8% [41–44]. Such events may result from decompensated HF due to incomplete stabilisation before discharge or suboptimal management during the pre-discharge and early post-discharge periods [45, 46]. Previous studies have demonstrated that intensive treatment strategies following acute HF, combined with comprehensive post-discharge care programmes, can substantially reduce the risks of death and readmission [44, 47]. Moreover, identifying factors associated with rehospitalisation is essential to improve risk prediction and guide optimal treatment strategies.

Our study demonstrated that pre-discharge and early post-discharge prescriptions of quadruple medical therapy for HFrEF and SGLT2 inhibitors for HFmrEF or HFpEF were associated with a greater reduction in 30-day rehospitalisation compared with patients who did not receive these treatments. However, information on drug dosages was not included in the final analysis owing to heterogeneity in medication types and dosing across therapeutic classes. For patients with HFrEF, current guidelines recommend the use of ARNI, ACEI or ARB, beta-blockers, MRA, and SGLT2i as the main components of GDMT [1]. In addition, SGLT2i is recommended for patients with HFmrEF or HFpEF to lower the risk of HF hospitalisation or cardiovascular death [12]. Importantly, initiating these medications after achieving haemodynamic stabilisation during acute HF has been shown to confer significant clinical benefits [48]. Therefore, recent HF guidelines advocate initiating GDMT prior to discharge for patients hospitalised with HF [12]. Nevertheless, the management of older adults with HF remains particularly challenging due to multimorbidity, cognitive impairment, and frailty [6], which may limit the feasibility of in-hospital GDMT optimisation. Our findings suggest that early initiation of GDMT before hospital discharge in patients aged ≥ 60 years with acute HF may be associated with a reduction in short-term adverse outcomes. However, this association should be interpreted with caution because of the limited number of events, and it requires confirmation in larger studies with greater statistical power.

We also observed a higher risk of rehospitalisation for HF among patients aged ≥ 60 years with elevated PASP. This finding is consistent with previous evidence indicating that increased PASP, as estimated by echocardiography, serves as a predictor of hospitalisation for HF [49]. This association may be explained by the fact that PASP partially reflects the degree of congestion in HF [50]. In the ESC-EORP-HFA Heart Failure Long-Term Registry, residual congestion at discharge was associated with an increased one-year risk of hospitalisation among patients recovering from acute HF [51]. Consequently, the management of pulmonary congestion has become a key therapeutic target in HF, and remote PASP monitoring has been shown to guide treatment and reduce rehospitalisations [52]. Given that PASP tends to rise with advancing age [53], our findings suggest that patients aged ≥ 60 years with acute HF and pre-discharge PASP values ≥ 40 mmHg should be regarded as particularly vulnerable and at heightened risk of rehospitalisation.

Our study also found that HF type was not an independent predictor of 30-day rehospitalisation. Differences between our findings and those of previous studies, such as the work by Pagnesi et al. [54]., may partly reflect variations in study settings, patient populations, and methodological approaches. Specifically, our study exclusively included patients aged ≥ 60 years hospitalised with acute HF, whereas Pagnesi et al.. examined a mixed cohort of outpatients and inpatients with differing baseline characteristics and comorbidity profiles. Moreover, the relatively small sample size and limited number of rehospitalisation events in our study may have reduced statistical power to detect differences among HF types. Collectively, these factors may account for the absence of a significant association between HF type and short-term rehospitalisation observed in our study.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, due to variations in laboratory procedures across study sites, data on blood biochemical tests were not included in the analysis. Second, the study did not account for medication dosages, achievement of target doses for HF therapies, or the rationale for specific drug selections. Third, although rehospitalisation events were confirmed using hospital electronic medical records, they were not adjudicated by an independent clinical events committee. Fourth, despite data collection from three tertiary hospitals, the overall sample size was modest, which may limit the external validity of the findings. Fifth, as this study was conducted in Vietnam, caution should be exercised when extrapolating the results to other populations, where differences in ethnicity, obesity prevalence, lifestyle factors, and healthcare systems may influence HF characteristics and outcomes. Finally, the short follow-up period limited our ability to assess longer-term cardiovascular outcomes, including the potential impact of geriatric syndromes such as frailty. Based on these limitations, the primary intention of this study was descriptive rather than to draw definitive causal inferences. Future studies with larger sample sizes and extended follow-up are warranted to better characterise prognosis and treatment effects in this population.

Conclusions

Among patients aged ≥ 60 years hospitalised for acute HF in Vietnam, HFrEF was the most prevalent HF type. The risk of 30-day rehospitalisation for HF was significantly higher in patients with a PASP ≥ 40 mmHg, whereas it was lower in those who received GDMT prior to discharge.
