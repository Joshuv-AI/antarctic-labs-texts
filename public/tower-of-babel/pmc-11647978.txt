Medication Use and Treatment Indications in Huntington's Disease; Analyses from a Large Cohort
Authors: Stephanie Feleus, Lara E.M. Skotnicki, Raymund A.C. Roos, Susanne T. de Bot
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuntington's Disease is a rare neurodegenerative disorder in which appropriate medication management is essential. While many medications are prescribed based on expert knowledge, overviews of actual medication use in HD are sparse.ObjectivesWe provide a detailed overview of medication use and associated indications across HD disease stages, considering sex and regional differences.MethodsData from the largest observational HD study, ENROLL‐HD, were used. We created HD‐related medication and indication classes to identify medication trends in manifest, premanifest and control subjects. We studied medication use in adult, childhood‐ and adolescent‐onset HD, incorporating disease stage (including phenoconverters), sex and regional differences.ResultsIn 8546 manifest HD patients, 84.6% used medication (any type), with the average number of medications per user rising from 2.5 in premanifest HD to 5.2 in end stage disease. Antipsychotics (29.2%), SSRIs (27.5%) and painkillers (21.8%) were most often used. Medication use varied with disease progression. Several differences were observed between the sexes, and notably between Europe and Northern America as well. Medication use increased after phenoconversion (from 64.8% to 70.6%, P < 0.05), with the largest difference in antipsychotic use (4.4%–7.8%, P < 0.05). Medication patterns were different in childhood‐onset HD, with no use of painkillers, less use of anti‐chorea and antidepressant drugs, and more for aggression and irritability.ConclusionsMedication use in HD increases with disease progression, with varying types of medications prescribed based on disease stage, sex, and region of living. Recognizing these medication trends is vital for further personalized HD management.

Huntington's Disease (HD) is a rare, hereditary, neurodegenerative disorder, caused by a trinucleotide CAG‐repeat expansion ≥36 in the Huntingtin gene. 1 The disease is clinically characterized by a triad of symptoms, including motor abnormalities, neuropsychiatric disturbances and cognitive decline. 2 Although chorea is the hallmark HD motor symptom, dystonia and postural abnormalities are often reported as well. Psychiatric symptoms vary widely among patients. Depressive thoughts, anxiety, agitation and aggression are common, but apathy, perseverations, hallucinations and delusions accompany HD as well, making HD a complex multisymptomatic disorder. 3 , 4

HD is divided in a premanifest stage when disease characteristics are not present and a manifest stage after disease onset. Disease onset is inversely correlated with CAG‐repeat expansion length. 5 The majority of patients have ≥40 CAG repeats and they will develop disease characteristics during mid‐life. 6 , 7 Individuals who have ≥60 CAG repeats are likely to develop juvenile HD, affecting ~4% of all cases. 8 , 9 Symptoms may arise already during childhood (<10 years), associated with CAG repeat lengths ≥80 or during adolescence (10–20 years). 10

Currently, HD cannot be cured. Therefore, disease symptoms and signs are managed symptomatically. 11 , 12 Due to the varying nature of HD symptomatology, a wide variety of medications are prescribed. Tetrabenazine, and its deratives deutetrabenazine and valbenazine, are the sole medications that have shown to be effective in a randomized controlled trial design. 13 , 14 , 15 Other agents are prescribed based on their effectiveness in non‐HD patients for similar symptoms and/or based on expert knowledge. 12 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19

Although several reviews on recommended pharmacological treatment of HD symptoms are available, 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 overviews of actual medication use in HD are sparse, performed on small samples or focused on specific medication types or symptoms. 24 , 25 , 26 We aim to provide a complete overview of prescribed medications in HD, stratified for disease stage, sex and region of living. To cover all aspects of HD, we also studied childhood‐ and adolescent‐onset HD, and those patients who recently converted from premanifest to manifest stage. By better recognizing HD medication patterns, clinicians could improve quality of care. Moreover, for further personalized medicine a clear overview of current medication use in HD is essential.

Methods

Data used in this study were generously provided by the participants in the Enroll‐HD study and made available by CHDI Foundation, Inc. Enroll‐HD is a global clinical research platform designed to facilitate clinical research in HD. 27 Core datasets are collected annually from all research participants as part of this multi‐center longitudinal observational study. Data are monitored for quality and accuracy using a risk‐based monitoring approach. All sites are required to obtain and maintain local ethical approval. Study site personnel are trained and certified annually to minimize intra‐rater and inter‐rater variability. This study used the fifth periodic dataset (PDS5), extracted on October 31, 2020. All subjects have given written informed consent.

We created a cross‐sectional dataset with actively participating subjects at the time of data extraction. Subjects without an HD expanded gene served as controls. HD gene expansion carriers (HDGECs) were divided in premanifest and manifest subjects as assessed by the local Enroll‐HD investigator. Manifest subjects were divided in adult onset, childhood onset (HD onset <10 years) and adolescent onset (HD onset 10–20 years). Adult‐onset manifest subjects were classified in disease stages based on the Unified Huntington's Disease Rating Scale—Total Functioning Capacity (UHDRS‐TFC). 28 UHDRS‐TFC scores range from 0 to 13. Lower scores indicate a decline in functionality: UHDRS‐TFC score 11–13 (stage 1), 7–10 (stage 2), 3–6 (stage 3), 1–2 (stage 4) and 0 (stage 5).

Phenoconverters were identified from the complete longitudinal PDS5 dataset, spanning 8 years, as premanifest subjects at baseline who had transferred at any time point to manifest stage based on the diagnostic confidence level (DCL). The DCL ranges from 0 to 4 and indicates the certainty of the clinical diagnosis. 29 To have an unequivocal judgment, we regarded a DCL = 4 (the investigator is ≥99% certain that motor abnormalities are signs of HD) as HD manifest and a score ≤3 as premanifest. Prescriptions were filtered to reflect only active medications taken at the visit before phenoconversion and at the first visit after phenoconversion.

By using the World Health Organization's Anatomical Therapeutical Chemical classification system 30 and clinical expertise, we have created the following medication classes to focus on HD related medication: anti‐dementia medications, antipsychotics, benzodiazepines, cannabinoids, dopamine agonists, painkillers, serotonin‐norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), tetrabenazines, tetracyclic antidepressants and urinary antispasmodics. Combination medication products were classified according to their main active ingredient. The full list of clustered medications per medication class is included in supplementary material A.

In the Enroll‐HD database, the indication per medication is reported “as mentioned by the participant.” Indications were recoded and independently verified by two HD experts to tailor HD symptomatology. Disagreements were resolved by discussion. Herewith, a list of categorized clinical symptoms and signs of HD was generated including aggression and irritability, anxiety, apathy, autonomic dysfunctioning, chorea, cognitive impairment, depression, dystonia, hallucinations and psychotic disorders, impulsivity, myoclonus, obsessive—compulsive symptoms and perseverations, pain, rigidity, sleep disorder, spasms, stress, substance abuse, swallowing disorders and tics. The full list of clustered clinical symptoms and signs of HD is included in supplementary material B.

Duplicates were removed based on medication‐indication combinations to prevent outcome inflation. Statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software version 4.1.2 31 and the dplyr v1.1.2, 32 tidyverse v2.0.0, 33 forcats v1.0.0, 34 epiDisplay v3.5.0.2, 35 stringr v1.4.0, 36 ggplot2 v3.4.4 37 and ggpattern v1.0.1 38 packages. Group differences between subjects were analyzed using independent sample t‐test or chi‐squared test, according to parametricity of the variables. Significance was defined as a P‐value <0.05. Descriptive analyses were performed, stratified on disease stage, sex and region of living. Polypharmacy was defined as the concurrent use of five to nine medications, including non‐HD medications. 39 Excessive polypharmacy was defined as concurrent use of 10 or more different medications, including non‐HD medications. 39

Results

The Enroll‐HD PDS5 dataset contained data from 17,182 actively participating subjects, of which almost 50% classified as adult‐onset manifest HD. Polypharmacy was highly prevalent (23.0% in adult manifest HD) and increased rapidly over disease stages (P < 0.001). Demographic data for all groups are shown in Table 1.

Medications and Indications Across Disease Stages

In adult‐onset manifest HD, the top 10 of most often reported medications consisted of olanzapine (9.6%), sertraline (9.3%), tetrabenazine (8.7%), acetylsalicylic acid (6.9%), citalopram (6.8%), mirtazapine (6.8%), risperidone (6.0%), paracetamol (5.9%), levothyroxine (5.9%) and clonazepam (5.6%). The corresponding medication groups were among the most prevalent (Fig. 1). In general, with increasing disease stage the number of medication users increased. A notable exception were SNRIs and SSRIs that showed peak prevalent use mid disease stage. While subjects in the last disease stage used on average the most medications per subject, the HD‐related medication types seemed to be clustered in antipsychotics, benzodiazepines and painkillers.

HD‐related medication use. Numbers above bars represent the percentage of unique subjects per group. HD, Huntington's disease; SNRIs, serotonin‐norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors; SSRIs, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs); TCAs, tetracyclic antidepressants.

Premanifest subjects showed similarities with control subjects. In both groups, levothyroxine and painkillers were most frequently used. In premanifest subjects, SSRIs such as sertraline (n = 155) and citalopram (n = 140) were more common (12.8% vs. 8.9%, P < 0.001). Although premotor manifest, 0.2% of premanifest subjects were using tetrabenazines. Painkillers were the sole HD‐related medication group that were more frequently used by control subjects than premanifest subjects (19.0% vs. 15.1%, P < 0.001).

HD‐related indications are shown in Fig. 2. Chorea and depression treatment were very frequent but declined in later disease stages. Medication use for autonomic dysfunctioning increased enormously from HD stage 3 onwards. A similar trend, although less pronounced was seen in aggression and irritability treatment. Noticeably, pain treatment was high in all groups. General pain, headache and musculoskeletal pain were most often reported painkiller indications in both pre‐ and manifest subjects.

Phenoconversion: Premanifest to Manifest

In the complete longitudinal Enroll‐HD PDS5 dataset, 562 phenoconverters were identified. Average age at phenoconversion was 47.9 years (SD 12.7 years). Before phenoconversion, 364 subjects (64.8%) were using medication versus 397 subjects (70.6%, P < 0.05) after phenoconversion. Both before and after phenoconversion the average number of medications (any type) used per subject was 2.9. The only statistically significant difference in HD‐related medication use was found in antipsychotic use (4.4% vs. 7.8%, P < 0.05).

Regarding the HD‐related indications, pharmacological treatment of chorea more than doubled after phenoconversion (2.3% vs. 5.0%, P < 0.05). When focusing solely on those subjects with antipsychotic use, depression (28.0%) and chorea (24.0%) were equally reported as indication before phenoconversion (data not shown). However, after phenoconversion, the main indication for antipsychotics became chorea (31.8%). Overall, the most reported HD‐related indication before and after phenoconversion was depression (23.8% vs. 25.4%, not statistically significant). Other HD‐related indications showed either a small change or no change at all. In supplementary material C, differences in HD‐related medication use and HD‐related indications pre‐ and post phenoconversion are visualized.

Sex Differences in Adults

Female adult‐onset HDGECs reported higher medication use (any sort) than males (78.9% vs. 72.7%, P < 0.001, data not shown). The sex difference was most obvious in the early to middle disease stages (stage 1 – stage 3) and in the various antidepressants and painkillers. Noticeably, males were overrepresented in antipsychotic use (all disease stages) and in tetrabenazines use in all disease stages except stage 2. However, when looking at indications, chorea was treated equally in manifest females (26.5%) and males (26.6%). In males, antipsychotics were more often used to treat aggression and irritability than in females (16.0% vs. 13.1%, P < 0.05). The occurrence of other HD‐related indications corresponded to the frequency of prescribed medications related to these indications (data not shown). The sex specific distribution and corresponding percentages of medication users of HD related medications are shown in supplementary material D.

Regional Differences in Adults

Due to limited subjects in Australasia and Latin America, regional differences were analyzed by comparing Europe to Northern America. In almost all subgroups, Northern American subjects used more medications (Table 2). When focusing on adult‐onset manifest subjects, differences between the two continents were clearly distinguishable. In Europe antipsychotics (33.5%) were prescribed most often, versus SSRIs (38.1%, P < 0.001) in Northern America. Antipsychotics and tetracyclic antidepressants were the only HD related medication groups prescribed more often in Europe than in Northern America. Distribution across disease stages was comparable between Europe and Northern America. Supplementary material E contains continental data stratified per medication class and disease group.

The preferential treatment within a medication group differed between continents. Olanzapine was the preferred antipsychotic in Europe. Northern America preferred risperidone. Clonazepam was the most often prescribed benzodiazepine in Northern America, while lorazepam was preferred in Europe. Trazadone was preferred in Northern America, while in Europe venlafaxine was the preferential SNRI. Both continents preferred sertraline, followed by citalopram as SSRI treatment in HD. Focusing on indications in adult‐onset manifest subjects, 36.9% of European subjects were pharmacologically treated for depression versus 62.3% in Northern America (P < 0.001). Chorea was pharmacologically treated in 23.0% of European subjects vs. 35.4% in Northern America (P < 0.001). In Europe, respectively 20.7% and 15.9% were treated for pain and sleep disorder, against 35.7% and 24.6% in Northern America (both P < 0.001). In Northern America, higher treatment rates for cognitive impairment were reported compared to Europe (5.2% vs. 1.3%, P < 0.001). For other HD related indications, the continental differences were smaller (data not shown). When looking at the preferential medications per HD‐related indication, some differences arose (Table 3). In Northern America valproate was first choice treatment for aggression and irritability, compared to olanzapine in Europe. Bupropion is the 2nd most prescribed medication for depression in Northern America, but at the 18th place in Europe (data not shown). Of note, deutetrabenazine (n = 233 in Northern America, preferential for indication chorea) is not available in Europe. Childhood‐ and Adolescent‐Onset Huntington's Disease

We included 22 subjects with childhood‐onset HD and 201 subjects with adolescent‐onset HD (Table 1), the majority of whom were in the early to middle disease stages. Of note, subjects’ current age does not necessarily reflect age within HD onset ranges. Although group sizes were too small for statistical analyses, several points are noteworthy. No painkiller use was reported in childhood‐onset HD. Painkiller use in adolescent‐onset HD was comparable to adults in disease stage 1 (P > 0.05). Tetrabenazines seemed to be prescribed less in childhood‐onset HD (4.5%) than in adult‐onset HD (11.4%) or adolescent‐onset HD (12.9%). Accordingly, from an indication perspective, the frequency of chorea treatment in childhood‐onset HD (20.0%) was much lower than in the later onset groups. In childhood‐onset HD, no preferential medication for the treatment of chorea was found. Reported medications included tetrabenazines and antipsychotics but also amantadine and levodopa. In contrast to adult‐onset HD, antipsychotics in childhood‐onset HD were more often prescribed to treat aggression, irritability, hallucinations and psychotic disorders than chorea.

Treated symptomatology in childhood‐onset HD differed most from adult‐onset HD. The occurrence of aggression and irritability treatment was much higher (31.8%), compared to adolescent‐onset HD (8.5%) or any of the adult‐onset HD disease stages (adult data displayed in Fig. 2). Depression treatment occurred less than half as much in childhood‐onset HD (13.6%) as in adolescent‐onset HD (34.8%) or in any of the adult‐onset HD stages (Fig. 2). Childhood‐onset HD seemed to be less often treated for anxiety (9.1%) and sleep disorder symptoms (9.1%) compared to adolescent‐onset HD (respectively 18.9% and 16.4%). Myoclonus was more often reported as treatment indication in childhood‐onset HD (4.5%), than in adolescent‐onset HD (0.5%) or adult‐onset HD (highest prevalence rate 1.8% in disease stage 5, data not shown). An overview of HD‐related medication use and HD‐related indications in childhood‐ and adolescent‐onset HD is displayed in supplementary material F.

Discussion

This is the first extensive overview of all medication use and treatment indications in HD. We presented information across disease stages, including childhood‐onset HD and adolescent‐onset HD, and stratified on sex and region of living. In general, as expected based on the neurodegenerative nature of HD, higher rates of medication use are associated with increasing disease stage. Polypharmacy is associated with an increased risk for drug–drug interactions and adverse events, such as falls, hospitalization and increased risk of death. 40 Polypharmacy (23.0%) is prevalent in manifest HD and comparable to Multiple Sclerosis (28.0%) 41 but rates are lower than in Parkinson's Disease (40%). 42 The lower polypharmacy rate in HD may be skewed by a younger mean age and fewer subjects in later disease stage, since medication use increases with age and increasing disease stage. 40 Likewise, age differences could explain the slightly higher medication use in controls, who were 10 years older on average, compared to premanifest HD.

Antipsychotics and antidepressants were most frequently used, in line with common symptomatology in HD. 3 , 4 We are the first to report increased medication use for autonomic dysfunctioning from HD stage 3 onwards. This is in line with findings that autonomic symptoms are most prominent in advanced HD stage. 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 Chorea treatment steeply increased up to disease stage 3 and declined afterwards, corresponding to known reduced chorea in advanced stages. 47 We reported that commonly prescribed antidepressants, such as SSRIs and SNRIs, increase in early to middle stage HD and decrease in later disease stage. By visualizing the prevalence of depressive symptom treatment as well, we verified that treatment of depressive symptoms indeed declines in later stages, and that, besides SSRIs and SNRIs, no other pharmacological compounds are increasingly prescribed for depression. Our results are consistent with previous studies on an increasing‐decreasing pattern of depression, 48 , 49 although it remains unclear whether HD patients in more advanced stages are truly less affected by depressive symptoms or whether they have difficulty recognizing and reporting them. 3 , 50

Painkillers were frequently used, up to 37.3% (Fig. 1), but pain as an indication did not increase as much across disease stages (Fig. 2: max 25.5%), suggesting that drugs categorized as painkillers are often used for other indications. Our findings partially align with limited prior research. While one study reported up to 15% analgesic use in HD gene carriers, with pain interference rates reaching 42%, 51 smaller studies indicated around 49% analgesic use in premanifest HD. 52 , 53 Our study was conducted using a different approach, focusing solely at medication or indication without combining the two. Our approach aimed to encompass diverse drugs with analgesic effects and a wide range of painful indications, minimizing the impact of single‐indication per medication as entered in the Enroll‐HD database. Consideration of time frames is crucial when reporting prevalence rates, as painkillers are often used briefly. Unfortunately, variations in reporting intervals of analgesic use in previous research hinder direct comparisons. Differences with previous studies could further be explained by variation in recoding and grouping variables. Many combination preparations with analgesics (eg, codeine) are included in the PDS5, that we labeled as painkillers and others did not. Our approach does not allow direct comparison with previous studies, but emphasizes the importance of considering multiple indications for a single drug. To improve research transparency, we provide the full recoding list in supplementary material A.

There is a lack of any support for the pharmacological treatment of cognitive symptoms in HD. 12 However, up to 3.2% in our dataset received drug treatment for cognitive impairment, mainly in Northern America. Donepezil, memantine and rivastigmine were most often prescribed. This may be based on the limited evidence for these drugs in other neurodegenerative diseases. 54 , 55 , 56 , 57 Despite the fact that apathy and swallowing disorders are well known in HD, 58 , 59 medication use for these indications is low. Probably because little effective pharmacological treatments are available. 12

We reported significantly higher rates of SSRI use in premanifest HD compared to controls. SSRIs are among others prescribed to treat depression, anxiety and irritability. 12 Our findings support previous research that neuropsychiatric symptoms may arise long before motor onset. 59 , 60 Although in a minority (0.2%), tetrabenazines are used in premanifest HD, which seems paradoxical, as this medication is mostly prescribed to treat chorea. Either prodromal motor changes or disease stage misclassification could have played a role here. As expected, antipsychotics as antichorea treatment significantly increased after phenoconversion. Tetrabenazine use increased post‐phenoconversion, but the change was not significant, possibly due to a preference for antipsychotics in this stage to address both chorea and behavioral symptoms simultaneously.

We are the first to report that antipsychotics are more often used to treat aggression and irritability in HD males than in females. In general however, HD females report more medication use than men, especially painkillers and antidepressants. Previous research, also in the general population, has shown that pain and depression are more prevalent in women, although its etiology is not yet completely understood. 61 , 62

Preferential pharmacological HD treatments vary between continents. We verified that the preference for antipsychotics and tetracyclic antidepressants in Europe was not confounded by differences in disease stage distribution. Remaining explanations include (sociocultural) prescribing habits, personal preferences of the clinician and/or regional availability of medications. Overall rates of pharmacological HD treatment are higher in Northern America than in Europe, which was not confounded by differences in disease stage distribution. This is in line with previous research that prescription drug rates are, in general, higher in the United States of America (66%) 63 than in Europe (49%). 64 Of note, within Europe wide variations between countries exist. 63 Non‐pharmacological treatments may explain some of the treatment differences, but were not part of this study.

Using symptom treatment as an indicator suggests that in childhood‐onset HD, chorea, depression, and pain are less common, while aggression and irritability are more prevalent. Our findings align with previous studies, showing lower rates of chorea and pain and higher rates of aggression. 64 , 65 The reported lower medication use for anxiety and sleep disorders in childhood‐onset HD is inconsistent with the prevalence of these symptoms in literature. 66 However, undertreatment of symptoms due to poor recognition in the juvenile HD group should be considered, as well as non‐pharmacological alternative treatments. Literature cases support the use of tetrabenazine, 64 , 66 tiapride, 64 , 67 pimozide and haloperidol 68 for chorea management in juvenile HD, but a clear preferred evidence‐based pharmacological management is lacking.

Strengths & Limitations

This study has several strengths. We have grouped medications and indications in a clinically meaningful way to provide a comprehensive article for use in HD practice. A major advantage is the large sample that even includes rare subtypes of childhood‐ and adolescent‐onset HD. Male and female stratified analyses raise awareness of gender‐specific medication patterns in HD. Inclusion of patients from all around the globe in various disease stages increases generalizability.

While our study provides invaluable insights, it is not without limitations. The Enroll‐HD database restricts entries to a single indication per medication, overlooking instances where one medication addresses multiple symptoms in HD (eg, antipsychotics may improve both chorea and behavioral symptoms at the same time). 12 Late‐stage patients are underrepresented due to loss‐to‐follow‐up, limiting the study's scope. Due to de‐anonymization reasons, only continental data was available, prohibiting a comparison on country level. The annual data collection of Enroll HD impedes a complete overview of medication use. Occasionally used medications (eg, season specific medication, short‐term treatments) could easily be missed. Therefore, numbers for freely available (over‐the‐counter) medications are expected to be higher. Enroll‐HD data is based on patient reporting, giving room to non‐specific entries (eg, self‐medication) and dietary supplements. There is no objective pharmacy verification or check on compliance. Longitudinal studies tracking medication responses over time would offer a deeper understanding of treatment effectivity and tolerability. The ongoing HD‐MED study 69 , 70 is promising in providing detailed longitudinal HD medication data. As we described medication use and associated indications cross‐sectionally, we cannot draw conclusions about treatment effects. Medication interferences, especially that of antidopaminergic medication, 71 , 72 on HD symptom progression should be further investigated and considered in clinical practice.

Concluding Remarks

Medication use and polypharmacy are prevalent across HD stages. Antipsychotics and antidepressants are most frequently used. The dynamic nature of HD is highlighted by varying types of medications, prescribed based on disease progression, sex, and region. Recognizing these medication trends is vital for further personalized HD management.
