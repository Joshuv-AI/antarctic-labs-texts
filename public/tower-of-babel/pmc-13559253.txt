Postoperative infection risk factors in lung cancer patients after bronchoalveolar lavage
Authors: Shumin He, Jiaye Chen, Youzu Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung cancer is a malignant tumor with high incidence and mortality worldwide. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) is an important tool for its diagnosis and evaluation. However, as an invasive procedure, it may carry a risk of secondary infection. This study aimed to investigate the incidence and independent risk factors of secondary pulmonary infection following BAL in patients with lung cancer.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted, enrolling 1,027 patients with lung cancer who underwent BAL at Taizhou Hospital of Zhejiang Province, China, from January 2022 to March 2025. Patients were divided into an infection group and a non-infection group based on the occurrence of postoperative pulmonary infection. Univariate analysis was used to compare clinical data, imaging characteristics, and BAL procedural parameters between the two groups. Variables with P<0.05 in the univariate analysis and clinically relevant factors were incorporated into a multivariate Poisson regression model to identify independent risk factors for postoperative infection.ResultsIn this study, the incidence of secondary pulmonary infection after BAL in lung cancer patients was 2.63% (23/875). Univariate analysis showed that the proportion of patients with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) score ≥3 (21.7% vs. 4.5%, P=0.004), total lavage volume [150 (135, 200) vs. 100 (100, 120) mL, P<0.001], and the proportion of upper lobe lavage (73.9% vs. 47.8%, P=0.02) were significantly higher in the infection group compared with the non-infection group. Multivariate Poisson regression analysis revealed that PS score ≥3 [relative risk (RR) 4.24, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.62–11.10, P=0.003], total lavage volume (per 10 mL increase, RR 1.34, 95% CI: 1.23–1.46, P<0.001), and the presence of necrosis/cavitation within the tumor (RR 2.60, 95% CI: 1.04–6.53, P=0.042) were independent risk factors for post-BAL infection.ConclusionsThe incidence of secondary pulmonary infection following BAL in patients with lung cancer is 2.63%. A PS score ≥3, intratumoral necrosis/cavitation, and a higher total lavage volume are independent risk factors for postoperative pulmonary infection, with total lavage volume showing a clear dose-response relationship with infection risk. For patients with these high-risk factors, clinicians should carefully evaluate the lavage volume and strengthen peri-procedural management.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Among 875 patients with primary lung cancer undergoing diagnostic bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), 23 (2.63%) developed post-BAL pulmonary infection. Poor performance status, greater total lavage volume, and tumor necrosis or cavitation were independently associated with infection. A significant dose-response relationship was observed between lavage volume and infection risk.

What is known and what is new?

• BAL is an important diagnostic procedure in lung cancer, but evidence regarding risk factors for post-BAL pulmonary infection remains limited. This study systematically evaluated procedural, patient-related, and tumor-related factors and identified a dose-response association between lavage volume and post-BAL infection risk.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Post-BAL infection risk should be considered in relation to both procedural factors and patient or tumor characteristics. The observed associations do not establish specific clinical thresholds, but support individualized procedural planning and further validation in large, multicenter prospective studies.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains a critical global public health challenge with an escalating disease burden. According to GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates from the International Agency for Research on Cancer, approximately 2.5 million new lung cancer cases and 1.8 million related deaths occurred worldwide, representing 18.7% of total cancer mortality (1,2). As a high-incidence region for lung cancer, China ranks this malignancy as the most prevalent cancer among adult males and the second most prevalent among adult females. The associated medical expenditures account for 22.6% of national cancer-related healthcare costs, thereby imposing substantial socioeconomic pressures (3-7). These epidemiological realities underscore the imperative to advance precision diagnostic technologies and refine complication prevention protocols for improved clinical outcomes. Breakthroughs such as targeted therapy and immunotherapy have significantly improved patient prognosis, but they require adequate tumor tissue or cell specimens for molecular pathological testing. However, approximately 30% of patients with advanced lung cancer cannot obtain qualified tissue samples through conventional biopsy due to unfavorable lesion locations, poor cardiopulmonary function, or bleeding risks.

Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) is a key diagnostic technique for pulmonary diseases. By obtaining bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) via fiberoptic bronchoscopy, it provides irreplaceable clinical value in molecular subtyping of lung cancer, analysis of the tumor microenvironment, and differentiation of concomitant infections (8,9). With its precise lesion localization capability, BAL overcomes the sampling limitations of conventional biopsy, enabling multidimensional detection of distal airway cytology, microbiome, and tumor biomarkers (9-11). It not only collects exfoliated tumor cells for routine pathology but also detects driver gene mutations, assesses tumor mutational burden, and characterizes immune microenvironment features through liquid biopsy technology, serving as an important complement or even an alternative for molecular subtyping of lung cancer. In recent years, with the development of liquid biopsy and single-cell sequencing, the value of BAL is being redefined and enhanced.

In the clinical diagnosis and treatment of lung cancer, secondary pulmonary infection is a common complication. It generally refers to respiratory tract infections that occur due to weakened immune function caused by the primary disease or related treatments. As an invasive diagnostic procedure, BAL itself may increase the risk of such infections. Studies have shown that during BAL, the infusion and aspiration of lavage fluid can cause mechanical injury to the airway epithelium and induce a transient imbalance in the local immune microenvironment, thereby creating favorable conditions for pathogen colonization (12). According to statistics, the incidence of infection after BAL in the general population is approximately 1–5%, while in lung cancer patients, the risk increases to 3–10% due to their frequent immunosuppression, structural airway abnormalities, and repeated invasive procedures (13). For lung cancer patients with widespread immune dysregulation, secondary pulmonary infection may lead to interruption of antitumor therapy and rapid deterioration of the disease, thereby severely undermining the diagnostic value of BAL. Currently, although BAL plays an increasingly important role in precision molecular diagnosis and treatment of lung cancer, the risk of BAL-associated infection in this high-risk population has not yet received sufficient attention, and specific risk assessment tools or preventive strategies tailored to this group are lacking. Most previous studies on post-bronchoscopy infections have focused on the overall population of patients undergoing bronchoscopy or on conventional biopsy procedures, and their risk models often include common factors such as advanced age, smoking history, and central tumors (14). BAL is primarily based on fluid lavage and recovery, and its impact on the airway microenvironment and microbiota may differ from the direct tissue injury caused by biopsy. Therefore, whether these conclusions can be directly extrapolated to BAL procedures remains questionable. Moreover, most studies have not analyzed lung cancer patients, especially those with advanced disease and specific immune microenvironment characteristics, as an independent risk subgroup. They have also rarely systematically examined the potential effects of operational parameters such as lavage fluid volume and recovery rate, as well as tumor imaging features (e.g., necrosis, cavitation), on infectious outcomes. Although some studies have explored the value of BAL in the etiological diagnosis of pulmonary infections (15,16), they have not provided targeted guidance on the risk factors for infection after BAL in lung cancer patients. There remains a lack of systematic research on the independent risk factors for secondary infection after BAL and the specific risks associated with this patient population.

Based on the current situation, this study aimed to determine the incidence of secondary pulmonary infection after BAL in patients with lung cancer through a retrospective cohort analysis, systematically evaluate its independent risk factors, and provide a basis for constructing targeted infection risk assessment strategies and formulating evidence-based intervention measures. The goal is to reduce the incidence of postoperative infection and related mortality, and ultimately improve the overall prognosis of lung cancer patients. Moreover, this study provides important baseline data and directions for future research in related fields, which holds significant clinical implications. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1468/rc).

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective study included 1,027 patients who were pathologically diagnosed with primary malignant lung tumors and underwent BAL at Taizhou Hospital of Zhejiang Province, China, between January 2022 and March 2025. The study cohort comprised both newly diagnosed patients and those with post-treatment recurrence, aiming to reflect the real-world application of this technique throughout the continuum of lung cancer management. Most enrolled patients underwent examination due to imaging findings suggestive of high-risk pulmonary lesions or the presence of high-risk backgrounds for lung cancer. The primary purpose of BAL was to obtain exfoliated tumor cells and biomarkers from BALF, thereby assisting histopathological diagnosis and molecular subtyping, particularly in patients for whom sufficient tissue samples are difficult to obtain via conventional biopsy. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Clinical Trial Ethics Committee of Taizhou Hospital of Zhejiang Province (approval No. YJ260503190673). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Definition of the primary outcome

The primary outcome of this study was secondary pulmonary infection following BAL. To accurately identify newly developed post-procedural infections and improve diagnostic specificity, a stringent definition was applied.

Secondary pulmonary infection was defined as occurring within 14 days after BAL and required both of the following criteria to be met:

Mandatory radiological evidence: compared with the pre-procedural baseline chest CT, postoperative imaging had to demonstrate newly developed or clearly worsened pulmonary abnormalities, including consolidation, ground-glass opacities, or infiltrative shadows.

Mandatory radiological evidence: compared with the pre-procedural baseline chest CT, postoperative imaging had to demonstrate newly developed or clearly worsened pulmonary abnormalities, including consolidation, ground-glass opacities, or infiltrative shadows.

Additional clinical or laboratory evidence (at least one of the following): (i) Clinical symptoms: newly developed or significantly worsened respiratory infection-related symptoms including fever >37.4 ℃, aggravated cough, purulent sputum; (ii) Significant elevation of inflammatory markers: WBC: postoperative absolute value ≥10×109/L with an increase >2.0×109/L compared with baseline; or C-reactive protein (CRP) postoperative absolute value ≥10 mg/L with an increase >10 mg/L compared with baseline; or procalcitonin (PCT) postoperative absolute value ≥0.25 ng/mL.

This composite definition was intended to distinguish pulmonary infection from isolated post-procedural fever or inflammatory responses.

In this study, secondary pulmonary infection specifically referred to infections localized to the lungs. Patients who developed fever or elevated inflammatory markers within 14 days after BAL but had clear evidence of infection confined to other sites (e.g., uncomplicated urinary tract infection or catheter-related bloodstream infection), without new infiltrative changes on chest imaging, were not classified into the infection group.

The observation window for postoperative infection was defined as within 14 days after BAL. This time frame was determined based on commonly used standards in previous studies on bronchoscopy-related complications (14,18), taking into account the typical incubation period of post-procedural infections as well as the possibility of delayed onset, while allowing reasonable differentiation between procedure-related events and subsequent community-acquired or hospital-acquired infections.

Finally, it should be noted that, due to the retrospective nature of this study, systematic respiratory microbiological testing before BAL was not routinely performed. Although post-BAL respiratory microbiological results were available for some patients, pre-BAL microbiological results were unavailable for most patients. Therefore, microbiological evidence was not considered a mandatory criterion for the diagnosis of secondary pulmonary infection.

Study cohort and grouping

Based on the above exclusion criteria and outcome definitions, a final study cohort of 875 patients with lung cancer was established (Figure 1). According to the occurrence of post-BAL infection, patients were categorized into the infection group (n=23) and the non-infection group (n=852).

Procedure protocol

All BAL procedures in this study were performed in accordance with the standardized protocol outlined in the Chinese Expert Consensus on Cytomorphological Examination of Bronchoalveolar Lavage Fluid (2020) (19). Key parameters were as follows:

Operator and localization

All procedures were performed by experienced interventional pulmonologists, each of whom had performed at least 200 BAL procedures. Pre-procedural chest CT was routinely used for localization, and the target segment or subsegment was selected based on lesion distribution.

Lavage parameters

After wedging the bronchoscope into the target bronchus, sterile normal saline at 37 ℃ was instilled in 3–5 aliquots, with 20–60 mL per aliquot. The total lavage volume was determined by the bronchoscopist based on the target segment and the quality of the recovered fluid, within a prespecified range of 100–250 mL. All patients completed the planned BAL procedure, and no procedure was terminated prematurely because of patient intolerance. Each aliquot was immediately aspirated using a negative pressure of 50–100 mmHg.

Quality control and recording

Total instilled and recovered volumes were recorded, and the recovery rate was calculated. A recovery rate ≥40% for lavage in the right middle lobe or left lingular segment, and ≥30% for the lower lobes or other lobes, with no grossly visible bloody turbidity in the recovered fluid, was considered indicative of a technically adequate procedure.

The following data were retrospectively collected from the hospital electronic medical record system and compared between the infection group and the non-infection group.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1). All hypothesis tests were two-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. (I) For continuous variables, the Shapiro-Wilk test was first used to assess normality (significance level α=0.10). Normally distributed variables were described as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed variables were described as median with interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. (II) For categorical variables, data were presented as counts (percentages) [n (%)]. Based on the expected frequencies, the Pearson chi-square test was used when all expected cell counts were ≥5; otherwise, Fisher’s exact test (for 2×2 tables) was applied.

Given the low incidence of the outcome event (2.63%), a Poisson regression model was employed for multivariate analysis to directly and robustly estimate the association between exposure factors and infection outcomes, calculating the relative risk (RR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) for each risk factor. This method is suitable for non-recurrent, low-incidence binary outcomes and provides reliable effect estimates even when the variance assumptions of the Poisson model are not fully satisfied. Variable selection for the multivariate model followed two principles: (I) variables with P<0.05 in univariate analysis; (II) potential confounding factors with strong clinical biological plausibility (e.g., age, sex) even if not reaching statistical significance, as reported in the literature. Because the infected group had limited sample size, the number of independent variables included in the model was strictly controlled to avoid overfitting. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to assess multicollinearity among independent variables, with VIF <5 indicating no severe multicollinearity.

Results

The median age was comparable between the two groups (66 vs. 67 years, P=0.08), and the proportion of males was also similar (82.6% vs. 85.7%, P=0.76).

There were no significant differences between the infection and non-infection groups in smoking history (P=0.69), serum ALB levels [37.9 (33.9, 42.5) vs. 38.0 (34.5, 41.7) g/L, P=0.71], or BMI [22.8 (20.3, 23.7) vs. 22.5 (20.3, 24.7) kg/m2, P=0.67]. The overall prevalence of comorbidities was not significantly different between the two groups (30.4% vs. 25.4%, P=0.76). Respiratory comorbidities [including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and bronchiectasis] were present in 13.0% (3/23) of the infection group and 16.2% (138/852) of the non-infection group, including COPD (2 vs. 50 cases), bronchiectasis (1 vs. 30 cases), and interstitial lung disease (0 vs. 22 cases), with no significant difference between groups (P>0.99). The prevalence of diabetes was also comparable (17.4% vs. 10.4%, P=0.30).

All patients were clinically or pathologically diagnosed with primary malignant lung tumors. The main histological types included adenocarcinoma (n=212, 24.2%), squamous cell carcinoma (n=382, 43.7%), and small cell lung cancer (n=151, 17.3%). The cohort included 65 cases of recurrent lung cancer. Tumor-related characteristics, including histological type (P=0.07), maximum tumor diameter (P=0.54), tumor stage (P=0.79), and recurrence status (1 case in the infection group vs. 92 cases in the non-infection group; no statistical test performed due to the small number of events), showed no significant differences between the two groups.

According to the ECOG PS scale, scores of 0–2 indicate that patients are generally capable of self-care, whereas scores ≥3 indicate poor functional status. In this study, 5 patients (21.7%) in the infection group had a PS score ≥3, which was significantly higher than that in the non-infection group (38 patients, 4.5%), with a statistically significant difference (P=0.004).

In terms of imaging features, there were no significant differences between the two groups in the distribution of central-type tumors, endobronchial lesions, or bronchial obstruction (P=0.12, 0.27, and 0.66, respectively). Notably, the proportion of tumor necrosis or cavitation was higher in the infection group (30.4%, 7/23) than in the non-infection group (16.2%, 138/852), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P=0.09).

BAL procedure parameters

All patients underwent BAL. The median total lavage volume was significantly higher in the infection group than in the non-infection group [150 (135, 200) vs. 100 (100, 120) mL, P<0.001]. In addition, a higher proportion of patients in the infection group underwent upper lobe lavage (73.9% vs. 47.8%, P=0.02).

The recovered lavage volume also differed significantly between the two groups (P<0.01), whereas there were no statistically significant differences in recovery rate or concomitant interventional procedures (all P>0.05; Table 2).

Analysis of clinical characteristics of the infection group

Among the 23 patients in the infection group, 13 (56.5%) developed fever, with a median duration of 3.0 days (IQR: 2.0–4.0 days). Blood cultures were obtained in 5 patients (21.7%), of whom 2 (40.0%) were positive, yielding Staphylococcus hominis and Staphylococcus epidermidis (1 case each). Sputum cultures were performed in 14 patients (60.9%), with 5 (35.7%) positive results, including Klebsiella pneumoniae (n=2), Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (n=1), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (n=1), and Staphylococcus aureus (n=1). All patients received antibiotic therapy. The most commonly used antibiotic was cefoperazone-sulbactam (47.8%, 11/23), followed by meropenem (21.7%, 5/23) and piperacillin-tazobactam (17.4%, 4/23). Combination therapy was used in 39.1% of patients (9/23), and antibiotic escalation occurred in 26.1% (6/23). The median duration of antibiotic therapy was 7 days (IQR: 5–8 days), and the median antibiotic cost was 1,380 CNY (IQR: 734–2,277 CNY). A total of 73.9% of patients (17/23) experienced interruption or delay in anticancer therapy, with a median delay duration of 7 days (IQR: 6–30 days; range: 3–60 days).

Multivariable analysis

A Poisson regression model was used to evaluate the independent associations between potential risk factors and post-BAL infection. Based on the results of univariate analysis (P<0.05) and clinical relevance, the initially selected variables included age, sex, lavage volume, PS score, tumor necrosis/cavitation, upper lobe lavage, and histological type. Given the small number of outcome events (n=23), to minimize the risk of model overfitting, key variables (lavage volume, upper lobe lavage, PS score ≥3, and tumor necrosis/cavitation) were included in the primary model. The remaining variables were subsequently added one by one in sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the core effect estimates.

As shown in Table 3, PS score ≥3 (RR 4.24, 95% CI: 1.62–11.10, P=0.003), total lavage volume (per 10 mL increase, RR 1.34, 95% CI: 1.23–1.46, P<0.001), and tumor necrosis/cavitation (RR 2.60, 95% CI: 1.04–6.53, P=0.042) were independent risk factors for post-BAL infection, all of which reached statistical significance. Upper lobe lavage was not independently associated with infection in the multivariable model (RR 2.33, 95% CI: 0.91–5.96, P=0.08).

To assess model fit, multicollinearity diagnostics were performed. The VIF is commonly used to evaluate collinearity, with VIF <5 generally considered to indicate no significant multicollinearity. In this study, all VIF values ranged from 1.03 to 1.15, well below the threshold of 5. In addition, given the use of a Poisson regression model, potential overdispersion was also assessed to exclude an increased risk of false-positive results. The dispersion parameter of the model was 0.97 (P>0.99), indicating no evidence of overdispersion. These findings support the appropriateness of the Poisson regression model and the robustness of the results.

To verify the robustness of the above findings, sensitivity analyses were further performed. First, the dose-response relationship was assessed by changing the scale of lavage volume. When lavage volume was re-entered into the model per 50 mL increment, the RR was 4.32 (95% CI: 4.45–7.45), which was highly consistent with the expected mathematical value derived from the per 10 mL effect (1.345 ≈ 4.32), as shown in Figure 2. Second, to evaluate the stability of the effect estimates of the core variables, potential confounders such as age, sex, and histological type were sequentially added to the primary model. The results showed that the RR estimates for the core variables (PS score, total lavage volume, upper lobe lavage, and tumor necrosis/cavitation) remained essentially unchanged, with variation within 10%, indicating the robustness and reliability of the model findings.

Comparison of clinical outcomes

To evaluate the impact of post-BAL infection on clinical outcomes, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed. Patients in the infection and non-infection groups were matched in a 1:2 ratio using nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper width of 0.2, based on baseline characteristics including age, sex, PS score, tumor stage, and comorbidities. After matching, 18 patients in the infection group and 35 patients in the non-infection group were successfully matched, and baseline characteristics were well balanced between the two groups (all standardized mean difference <0.2).

The comparison of hospital costs and length of stay after matching is shown in Table 4. The median hospitalization cost in the infection group was 11,144 CNY (IQR: 8,316–15,455), significantly higher than that in the non-infection group (6,469 CNY, IQR: 4,362–8,412; W=75, P<0.001). The median length of hospital stay was also significantly longer in the infection group (8 days, IQR: 5.5–12.5) compared with the non-infection group (4 days, IQR: 2–5.5; W=108, P<0.001). The median differences were 4,675 CNY and 4 days, respectively, indicating that post-BAL infection was associated with a substantial increase in healthcare resource utilization.

Discussion

BAL is an important diagnostic tool in lung cancer (20,21), providing valuable biological information to support clinical decision-making (22,23), and its safety has been confirmed in multiple studies (24). However, given the potential risk of infectious complications following BAL, this study focused on post-procedural infection in patients with lung cancer, aiming to identify its associated risk factors. In this retrospective study, we analyzed 875 patients with lung cancer who underwent diagnostic BAL. The results showed that 2.63% (23/875) of patients developed secondary pulmonary infection after BAL. PS score ≥3, total lavage volume, and the presence of tumor necrosis/cavitation were identified as independent risk factors for post-BAL infection. Notably, a significant dose-response relationship was observed between lavage volume and infection risk, with the RR increasing by 34% for every 10-mL increase in lavage volume. The principal novel finding of this study was the dose-response relationship between lavage volume and post-BAL pulmonary infection risk.

The findings of this study are generally consistent with those reported in previous literature. The incidence of infectious complications following BAL in the general population ranges from 1% to 5% (25,26). In lung cancer-specific analyses, Chellapandian et al. reported an infection rate of 3.0% (27), which is highly consistent with our result (2.63%). However, a higher incidence (4.1–6.3%) was reported by Takiguchi et al. (25), whose study identified central-type tumors were identified as a risk factor for infection. In contrast, central-type tumors were not an independent risk factor after multivariable adjustment in our study. It should be noted that patients who had received chemotherapy or immune checkpoint inhibitors within 1 month prior to BAL, as well as those with active immunodeficiency, were excluded in this study. Therefore, the study population was skewed toward a relatively low-risk group with no recent anticancer therapy and comparatively preserved immune function. This may partly explain why the observed infection rate in our cohort (2.63%) was at the lower end of the range reported in the literature. Caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to a broader real-world population of lung cancer patients, particularly those with impaired immune function following immunotherapy or chemotherapy.

The PS score is a key indicator for assessing the functional status and physiological reserve of cancer patients and plays an important role in clinical decision-making for lung cancer. According to the ECOG PS scale, scores of 0–2 generally indicate that patients can tolerate active anticancer therapy, whereas a score of ≥3 reflects a marked decline in functional status, with patients being largely bedridden or confined to bed/chair. This is often considered a relative contraindication or a threshold requiring cautious evaluation before initiating active treatment.

In this study, PS score was dichotomized (0–2 vs. ≥3) for analysis based on this clinical cutoff. The results showed that PS score ≥3 was an independent risk factor for post-BAL infection (RR 4.24, 95% CI: 1.62–11.10, P=0.003). The proportion of patients with PS score ≥3 was significantly higher in the infection group than in the non-infection group (21.7% vs. 4.5%, P=0.004), suggesting that poor functional status is associated with a substantially increased risk of post-BAL infection in patients with lung cancer. From a mechanistic perspective, patients with poor PS are often in a state of immune exhaustion (28,29), accompanied by malnutrition and impaired mucosal repair capacity (30). These factors may synergistically exacerbate the transient disruption of airway defense functions caused by the lavage procedure, thereby increasing susceptibility to opportunistic pathogen colonization (31-33). This finding is consistent with the results reported by Kreitmann et al. regarding the association between PS and respiratory complications (34). It is noteworthy that the 95% CI for PS score ≥3 was relatively wide (1.62–11.10), which may be attributed to the limited number of infection events (n=23). Further validation in larger prospective studies is therefore warranted.

In this study, total lavage volume was confirmed as an independent risk factor for post-BAL infection. The median lavage volume in the infection group was 150 mL, significantly higher than the 100 mL in the non-infection group (P<0.001). Multivariate analysis showed that for every 10 mL increase in lavage volume, the infection risk increased by 34% (RR 1.34, 95% CI: 1.23–1.46, P<0.001). Taking the baseline infection rate of 2.63% in our cohort as reference, each 10 mL increase in lavage volume corresponded to an absolute infection risk increase of approximately 0.89 percentage points. The lavage volume range (100–250 mL) recommended by current BAL guidelines is primarily based on a balance between diagnostic specimen acquisition and patient tolerance, and its target population is the general population. However, our results suggest that for lung cancer patients, even when the lavage volume is kept within this "safe range", the infection risk continues to rise as the lavage volume increases. This finding indicates that lung cancer patients, as a special population, may have a lower tolerance threshold for lavage procedures, and thus greater caution is needed in clinical practice. To verify the reliability of this finding, we performed a sensitivity analysis to test the linearity of the lavage volume effect. Re-entering lavage volume in 50 mL increments into the model gave an RR of 4.32 (95% CI: 2.85–6.53), which was highly consistent with the theoretical value derived from the 10 mL effect (1.345 ≈ 4.32), confirming the existence and robustness of the dose-response relationship. Regarding the mechanisms by which increased lavage volume raises infection risk, we propose the following explanations. First, large-volume fluid instillation dilutes immune-active substances on the alveolar surface, including secretory IgA, complement, cytokines, and alveolar macrophages, causing a transient decline in local immune defense. Second, mechanical flushing may temporarily disrupt the airway epithelial mucociliary barrier (35), weakening the physical clearance of pathogens. In addition, excess lavage fluid that is not completely recovered may stagnate in narrowed airways, potentially serving as a culture medium for bacterial colonization and proliferation, further increasing infection risk. Currently, studies specifically addressing the impact of lavage volume on post-BAL infection in lung cancer patients are limited. Existing guidelines provide only a recommended lavage volume range, without offering an optimal threshold for specific populations. Future research should focus on optimizing BAL procedural parameters in lung cancer patients, exploring the combined effects of lavage volume, number of lavages, lavage site, and other factors on infection risk, to provide a basis for more targeted clinical practice guidelines.

In addition to lavage volume, other factors related to the bronchoscopic lavage procedure also deserve attention. The proportion of patients receiving upper lobe lavage was significantly higher in the infection group than in the non-infection group (73.9% vs. 47.8%, P=0.02), but after adjusting for confounding factors in the multivariate model, the RR for upper lobe lavage was 2.33 (95% CI: 0.91–5.96, P=0.08), which did not reach statistical significance. This suggests that the association between upper lobe lavage and infection might be influenced by confounding factors such as lavage volume, and the multivariate analysis indicates that upper lobe lavage itself is not an independent risk factor. Although the recovered lavage volume differed between the two groups, the recovery rate did not [infection group, 40% (35–40%) vs. non-infection group, 40% (35–45%); P=0.31]. Given that the absolute recovered volume is influenced by the instilled lavage volume, this finding should be interpreted in the context of the higher lavage volume in the infection group rather than as evidence of a difference in lavage recovery efficiency. In clinical practice, BAL is often combined with other interventional procedures. The proportion of combined procedures was 73.9% in the infection group and 87.1% in the non-infection group. According to relevant guidelines and hospital operational standards, we included major combined procedures such as EBUS-TBNA (8 cases in the infection group vs. 288 cases in the non-infection group), TBLB (16 vs. 671), and mass resection (2 vs. 47) in univariate analysis, and none of the differences were statistically significant. Notably, the proportion of BAL alone was relatively higher in the infection group, which might be related to special lesion locations or poorer tolerance in these patients, factors that themselves could be associated with infection risk. However, due to the limited sample size, definitive conclusions cannot be drawn.

In previous studies on post-bronchoscopy infection, imaging features have often been listed as risk factors for infection. Shimoda et al. (18) conducted a retrospective analysis of 327 lung cancer patients and found that the presence of intratumoral necrosis/cavitation was an important risk factor for post-bronchoscopy infection. Based on this, the present study included intratumoral necrosis/cavitation as a candidate variable to explore its independent role in post-BAL infection. Univariate analysis showed that the proportion of necrosis/cavitation was higher in the infection group than in the non-infection group [30.4% (7/23) vs. 16.2% (138/852)], showing a certain trend but not reaching statistical significance (P=0.09). In multivariate analysis, after adjusting for PS score, lavage volume, and upper lobe lavage, the RR for necrosis/cavitation was 2.60 (95% CI: 1.04–6.53, P=0.042), making it an independent risk factor for post-BAL infection. This change suggests that in univariate analysis, the effect of necrosis/cavitation was masked by factors such as poor PS and the need for larger lavage volumes; after controlling for these confounders, its independent effect emerged. Necrosis and cavitation are common pathological features during tumor progression, and their formation is often accompanied by a significant increase in tumor burden and extensive destruction of tissue structure (36). Large-scale apoptosis and tissue disintegration within tumor necrotic foci release various pro-inflammatory factors and cellular contents (37,38), such as high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) (39) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) (40,41), which can activate local immune responses but, under chronic stimulation, lead to exhaustion of immune cells, thereby weakening the host’s defense against pathogens and creating conditions for bacterial proliferation, thus increasing infection risk. At the same time, due to vascular destruction and insufficient blood supply, necrotic areas develop a hypoperfused and hypoxic microenvironment (42,43), which not only suppresses immune cell activity and antibiotic penetration but may also promote the growth of drug-resistant strains, making infections more difficult to control. The above mechanisms provide a biological basis for necrosis/cavitation as an independent risk factor. As a clinically available imaging feature, necrosis/cavitation is an important clue for identifying high-risk patients. For patients with this feature, the risk of post-BAL infection should be kept in mind. Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to further explore the relationship between necrosis/cavitation and specific pathogen infections, the severity of infection, and its potential role in different subgroups, so as to provide a basis for developing individualized infection prevention strategies.

After identifying core risk factors such as PS score, lavage volume, and intratumoral necrosis/cavitation, we also analyzed other potentially relevant variables. Demographic characteristics including age, sex, smoking history, and comorbidities (e.g., COPD, diabetes) showed no significant association with infection risk in this study. The differences between our findings and previous risk models highlight the need for a BAL-specific risk assessment system. For example, the bronchoscopy risk score proposed by Takiguchi et al. (age ≥70 years, smoking, central tumor) (25) was not significantly associated with infection in our study, possibly reflecting differences in risk profiles between BAL and conventional bronchoscopic procedures: BAL involves less direct injury to the central tumor area and relies more on lavage fluid flushing of the distal airways. Similarly, the risk factors proposed by Souma et al., such as bronchial stenosis (26), are mainly applicable to endobronchial ultrasound-guided sheath-guided transbronchial biopsy (EBUS-GS-TBB), whereas the infectious mechanism of BAL is more focused on lavage-mediated disruption of the mucosal barrier.

Microbiological findings should be interpreted cautiously. Sputum cultures yielded Klebsiella pneumoniae (n=2), Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (n=1), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (n=1), and Staphylococcus aureus (n=1). Blood cultures were positive in only 2 of 5 patients tested, both yielding coagulase-negative staphylococci, suggesting that bacteremia was not a prominent feature in this cohort, although contamination could not be excluded. Broad-spectrum antibiotics were commonly used, with cefoperazone-sulbactam most frequently prescribed (47.8%), followed by meropenem (21.7%) and piperacillin-tazobactam; combination therapy was administered in 39.1% of patients.

Among patients who developed infection after BAL, 73.9% experienced delay or interruption of anticancer treatment, with a median delay of 7 days. For lung cancer patients, especially those in the perioperative period or during immunotherapy cycles, a 7-day treatment interruption may affect tumor control. Although this study did not directly assess survival outcomes, the association between treatment delay and increased risk of tumor progression is supported by the literature, and this time loss should be taken seriously in clinical management.

Regarding the analysis of clinical outcomes, the infection group showed a 4,675 CNY increase in hospitalization cost (approximately 72%) and a 4-day prolongation of hospital stay compared with the control group, both differences being statistically significant. This result is consistent with previous economic analyses of infectious complications after bronchoscopic procedures (44). The median hospital stay of 8 days in the infection group closely matched the median antibiotic course of 7 days, suggesting that the extended hospitalization was mainly driven by anti-infective treatment. The analysis demonstrates that secondary pulmonary infection after BAL in lung cancer patients not only significantly increases medical costs but also prolongs hospital stay and compromises the continuity of anticancer therapy.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, single-center design may have introduced selection bias and residual confounding. The indications for BAL, lavage volume, and lavage site were determined according to clinical judgment rather than a prespecified uniform protocol. Therefore, the observed association between lavage volume and post-BAL pulmonary infection may have been influenced by unmeasured patient- or procedure-related factors. Second, the retrospective design may have resulted in misclassification of post-BAL pulmonary infection. Our predefined outcome required both newly developed or clearly worsened pulmonary abnormalities on follow-up imaging compared with the pre-procedural baseline and additional clinical or laboratory evidence. Nevertheless, noninfectious conditions, including tumor progression and obstructive pneumonia, may produce overlapping clinical, laboratory, and radiographic findings (45-47). Moreover, systematic respiratory microbiological testing before BAL was not routinely performed, and pre-BAL microbiological data were unavailable for most patients. Therefore, the clinical significance and temporal relationship of post-BAL culture results could not be definitively established, and pre-existing colonization or previously undetected infection could not be excluded, highlighting the complexity of interpreting microorganisms detected in respiratory samples in patients with lung cancer (48-50). Third, the number of infection events was small (n=23), limiting statistical precision. Although we used modified Poisson regression with robust variance estimation and performed sensitivity analyses, the resulting estimates, particularly for PS score ≥3 and tumor necrosis/cavitation, had wide CIs. The model was not externally validated, and the findings therefore require confirmation in larger, multicenter cohorts. Fourth, patients who had received chemotherapy or immune checkpoint inhibitors within 1 month before BAL or had active immunodeficiency were excluded. Accordingly, the findings may not be generalizable to patients receiving recent anticancer therapy or those with severe immune dysfunction. Finally, the observational nature of this study precludes causal inference and does not establish a specific lavage-volume threshold for clinical practice.

Future prospective multicenter studies with standardized BAL indications and operational protocols are still needed to systematically collect clinical, imaging, immunological, and microbiological data, and to validate the current findings using standardized lavage procedures. Further research can focus on the following directions: optimizing lavage volume thresholds based on immune status stratification; using metagenomics to characterize the pathogen spectrum in BALF; exploring targeted antibiotic prophylaxis or immunomodulatory interventions; and evaluating the cost-effectiveness of risk-stratified BAL protocols.

Conclusions

This study found that the occurrence of respiratory tract infection after BAL in patients with lung cancer is associated with PS (PS score ≥3), total lavage volume, and intratumoral necrosis/cavitation. Among these, each 10 mL increase in total lavage volume was associated with a 34% increase in infection risk, demonstrating a dose-response relationship. Secondary pulmonary infection after BAL significantly increased hospitalization costs and length of stay, and caused delay or interruption of anticancer therapy in most affected patients. These findings suggest that integrating patient status, tumor imaging characteristics, and procedural parameters may provide a reference for preliminary risk assessment of post-BAL infection. Although the conclusions of this study are hypothesis-generating in nature and require validation through multicenter prospective studies, the work itself provides preliminary evidence for identifying high-risk patient populations and developing more individualized BAL strategies, potentially contributing to clinical decision-making that balances diagnostic benefits and infection risk management in the future.
