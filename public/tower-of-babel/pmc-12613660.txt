An examination of the role of PTSD and childhood trauma on treatment outcomes for individuals with borderline personality disorder in dialectical behavior therapy
Authors: Katherine E Christensen, Shelley McMain, Alexander Chapman, Janice R Kuo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBorderline personality disorder (BPD) is a complex and severe mental health condition often accompanied by several co-occurring clinical problems. Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is one of the most common co-occurring disorders, with prevalence rates of PTSD reported to be between 25% and 58% among persons with BPD. The primary aim of the current study was to evaluate the association of a PTSD diagnosis and different forms of childhood trauma with changes in BPD severity, PTSD severity, and frequency of suicidal behaviors among suicidal individuals with BPD undergoing 12-months of standard dialectical behavior therapy (DBT).MethodsThis was a secondary data analysis that included individuals with BPD (N = 120) in the 12-month DBT condition. Participants completed measures of childhood abuse, PTSD severity, BPD severity, and frequency of suicidal behaviors at baseline as well as at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months. Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) were used to examine both study hypotheses.ResultsResults suggested that the presence of PTSD did not predict reduced rates of reduction in BPD severity, PTSD severity, or frequency of suicidal behaviors. Results also revealed that higher severity of childhood sexual and emotional abuse was associated with quicker reductions in BPD severity, whereas higher severity of childhood physical abuse was associated with slower reductions. Similarly, higher severity of emotional abuse was associated with quicker reductions in PTSD severity, while higher severity of physical abuse was associated with slower reductions in this outcome.ConclusionsStandard DBT appears to be comparably effective for both individuals with BPD without PTSD and BPD + PTSD. The addition of a PTSD diagnosis did not significantly predict slower recovery among those with BPD + PTSD. In addition, different forms of childhood trauma significantly affect the trajectories of individual treatment. Our findings suggest that DBT interventions may be particularly beneficial for individuals with higher levels of emotional abuse.Trial registrationNCT02387736. Background

Co-Occurring borderline personality disorder and posttraumatic stress disorder

Borderline personality disorder (BPD) is a complex, severe, and persistent mental health condition [1]. An estimated 75–95% of individuals with BPD have engaged in self-directed violence (i.e., suicide attempts [SA], non-suicidal self-injury [NSSI]), often necessitating hospitalization and medical interventions [1, 2]. Furthermore, approximately 91% of individuals with BPD have at least one co-occurring mental health condition [3]. Among these, posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is among the most common and debilitating co-occurring disorders [4]. PTSD is estimated to present among 25–58% of individuals with BPD [4, 5]. Those with both diagnoses (BPD + PTSD) exhibit poorer quality of life, higher rates of co-occurring disorders, and higher prevalence of repeated childhood trauma [6] when compared to PTSD or BPD alone.

Notably, individuals with BPD + PTSD have an even higher risk of engaging in suicidal behaviors than those with either BPD or PTSD alone [7]. Findings suggest that those with BPD + PTSD have a greater likelihood of lifetime suicide attempts (SA) [6] as well as a higher rate of SA when compared to individuals with BPD without PTSD (e.g., 79.5% vs. 66.8%) [8]. Other studies have similarly found that individuals with BPD + PTSD have an increased risk of suicide, higher levels of impulsiveness, and a greater number of hospitalizations than those with either BPD or PTSD alone [9].

PTSD as predictor of outcomes in DBT

Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) [10] is the most empirically supported and widely practiced intervention for individuals with BPD [11]. A handful of studies have examined whether a co-occurring PTSD diagnosis is associated with outcomes in DBT and other empirically supported treatments for BPD, including generalized psychiatric management (GPM) [12] and mentalization-based therapy (MBT) [13]. Some data suggest that, over the course of treatment (DBT and GPM) and post-treatment follow-up, the frequency of suicidal behaviors and severity of BPD among those with BPD + PTSD are comparable to those with BPD without PTSD [14]. Other findings, however, have suggested that co-occurring PTSD is associated with significantly less reduction in NSSI during DBT [4]. Barnicot and Crawford found that, among those with BPD + PTSD undergoing either MBT or DBT, heightened severity and chronicity of PTSD at baseline predicted higher frequency of self-harm (i.e., NSSI, SA), less likelihood of abstaining from self-harm during treatment, and greater self-reported BPD severity [7]. Furthermore, Harned and colleagues found that suicidal individuals with BPD who were receiving 12-months of DBT showed lower rates of remission of PTSD (i.e., 34.8%), compared with mood (i.e., 67.6%), eating (i.e., 63.6%), and/or substance use disorders (i.e., 87.5%) [5]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the presence of PTSD may be associated with weaker treatment response, increased emotion dysregulation, and a slower trajectory of change in suicidal behaviors and severity of BPD; however, given the inconsistencies among previous findings, it remains unclear how the presence of PTSD impacts DBT outcomes, as measured by symptom severity and self-harming behaviors.

Childhood trauma in BPD

In addition to the above-noted gaps, very few studies have examined the association between different types of childhood trauma and treatment outcomes among persons with BPD. Childhood trauma could include physical, sexual, and emotional abuse, neglect, and other adverse childhood experiences. Findings suggest that between 81 and 91% of individuals with BPD report some form of childhood trauma, including physical (44–59%), sexual (18–27%), or emotional abuse (66–73%) [15, 16]. Importantly, studies of childhood trauma can be limited by recall bias and/or difficulty accurately assessing different trauma experiences; as well, historically, some stressors that may not qualify as Criterion A events for PTSD (e.g., emotional abuse), have not been as consistently researched.

Several studies suggest that the presence of co-occurring BPD + PTSD is associated with heightened prevalence and severity of childhood trauma, complicating the clinical picture of this group and potentially leading to difficulty improving in treatment. Indeed, individuals with BPD + PTSD experience a higher total number of traumatic childhood events than those with either BPD or PTSD alone [6, 17, 18]. In a study comparing rates of childhood physical, sexual, and emotional abuse between individuals with BPD only, PTSD only, or BPD + PTSD, those with BPD + PTSD experienced the highest rates of childhood trauma compared to either disorder alone (i.e., 50.8%, 35.4%, and 81.5% for childhood physical, sexual, and emotional abuse, respectively) [9]. Further, those with BPD + PTSD are exposed to childhood trauma at significantly younger ages than those with PTSD only [19], and experience more types of childhood trauma (e.g., estimates of 3.32 abuse types) than those with either BPD (e.g., estimates of 2.02 abuse types) or PTSD alone (e.g., estimates of 2.96 abuse types) [9]. Notably, extant data also suggest that childhood sexual abuse, more so than other forms of abuse, may uniquely account for greater clinical impairment among individuals with BPD + PTSD. For instance, among those with BPD + PTSD, childhood sexual abuse is associated with more severe clinical presentations, poorer prognoses, lower remission rates of PTSD, lower health-related quality of life, and higher rates of suicidality and NSSI than other forms of childhood trauma [20–22]. As well, ten-year data from the McLean Study of Adult Development (MSAD) showed that, among those with BPD + PTSD, both the presence and severity of childhood sexual abuse predicted time-to-remission from PTSD such that those reporting childhood sexual abuse were only half as likely to experience remission from PTSD and, for each 5-point increase in childhood sexual abuse severity, the likelihood of remission from PTSD decreased by 21% over the course of 10 years [22]. This suggests that those with childhood sexual abuse may experience heightened levels of emotion dysregulation, which could account for their differential treatment responses.

Taken together, these findings highlight the central role of childhood abuse in shaping both BPD and PTSD as well as their frequent co-occurrence. While childhood neglect is undoubtedly harmful, its associations with BPD, PTSD, and NSSI + SA tend to be less direct and more diffuse [23, 24]. This pattern underscores the importance of prioritizing abuse variables in studies of cooccurring BPD + PTSD, while also acknowledging that neglect remains an important dimension for future research.

Childhood trauma as a predictor of outcomes in DBT

Although extant literature suggest that the impact of childhood trauma may explain variability in treatment responses and remission rates among this group, very few studies have begun to examine how different forms of childhood trauma might impact the course of treatment for individuals with BPD with or without co-occurring PTSD. Previous research has identified that different types and severities of childhood trauma predicts differential treatment outcomes among individuals with BPD undergoing schema therapy, transference-focused psychotherapy (TFP), MBT, and/or DBT [25–27]. For example, Keefe and colleagues found that DBT was a better fit than GPM for individuals with higher severity of emotional abuse, more dependent personality traits, higher social maladjustment, and higher severity of depression [27]. However, despite DBT’s established efficacy for BPD [28, 29] as well as emerging evidence suggesting various treatment moderators, there is a significant dearth of research that has examined the moderating role of specific forms of childhood trauma in DBT outcomes, despite its significant association with BPD.

Notably, Euler and colleagues recently examined whether childhood sexual abuse, emotional abuse and neglect, and physical abuse and neglect predicted outcomes in a 4-week, shortened version of DBT (i.e., intensive DBT [I-DBT]) [30]. I-DBT is an intensive outpatient program which follows the DBT framework and includes several hours of individual and group therapy per week [30]. In this study, physical neglect was associated with smaller reductions in depression severity while emotional neglect was associated with greater reductions in depression severity. Euler and colleagues hypothesized that once those with histories of emotional neglect received attention and care during their treatment course, their depression improved, while those with histories of physical neglect may not have been as sensitive to emotional support. Finally, higher global severity of childhood trauma was associated with greater decreases in impulsivity [30]. Therefore, there is some indication that certain forms of childhood trauma may affect severities of depression and impulsivity in DBT; however, findings are limited and mixed with respect to how and which types of childhood trauma might account for these findings, especially within a population of individuals with co-occurring BPD and PTSD.

Current study

For the present study, we aimed to examine the associations between a co-occurring PTSD diagnosis and different forms of childhood trauma with changes in BPD severity, PTSD severity, and frequency of suicidal behaviors (i.e., NSSI + SA) among self-harming individuals with BPD undergoing 12-months of standard DBT. Based on extant research on co-occurring BPD and PTSD evidence that childhood abuse is more directly associated with PTSD severity, BPD severity and NSSI + SA, whereas neglect tends to exert broader and less specific effects [23, 24], we opted to examine childhood sexual, physical, and emotional abuse, rather physical and emotional neglect in our present analyses.

Given research suggesting that emotion dysregulation among those with PTSD and childhood sexual trauma is a potential mechanism to explain differential treatment outcomes, we hypothesized that, when compared to those with BPD without PTSD, the presence of PTSD would be associated with a reduced rate of reduction in BPD severity, PTSD severity, and the frequency of NSSI + SA throughout the course of DBT (Hypothesis 1). We also hypothesized that a history of childhood sexual abuse would reduce the rate of reduction in BPD severity, PTSD severity, and the frequency of NSSI + SA among individuals with BPD, when controlling for other forms of abuse (Hypothesis 2). Indeed, evaluating the moderating effects that childhood sexual abuse has on BPD severity, PTSD severity, and frequency of NSSI + SA among individuals with BPD + PTSD may better inform for whom DBT treatment may be best and least suited and direct future intervention efforts for this population.

Method

This study was a secondary data analysis from a multisite, single-blind, two-arm randomized clinical trial that compared the clinical efficacy and cost-effectiveness of 6-month standard DBT to 12-month standard DBT [31, 32]. Given that the primary hypotheses of the current study were to examine the unique impact that different forms of childhood trauma have on standard DBT among those with BPD, only data from the 12-month condition were used.

Participants

In the parent trial, 240 participants were recruited from two sites: The Centre for Addiction and Mental Health (CAMH; Toronto, Ontario) and Simon Fraser University (SFU; Burnaby, British Columbia). See McMain et al. [31] for full details of study protocol. Participants were recruited from clinic waitlists, via advertisements, and by personal referrals. If eligible, participants were subsequently randomized to one of two treatment conditions. For the proposed study, only data from participants randomized to the 12-month DBT condition (N = 120) were included. Forty-two (35%) of the 120 individuals in our sample were considered treatment dropouts (i.e., missed four consecutive therapy sessions).

The age of the sample ranged from 18 to 56 years old (M = 27.26; SD = 8.67), and 79.22% of participants (n = 95) identified as female, 14.20% of participants (n = 17) identified as male, and 6.70% of participants (n = 8) identified as other. Given that GEE utilizes the highest number of participants in any timepoint that were assessed, Hypothesis 1 included n = 118 and Hypothesis 2 included n = 105. Inclusion criteria

Individuals were deemed eligible for the study if they met the following criteria: (1) between 18 and 60 years of age; (2) met Diagnostic and Statistical Manual-IV (DSM-IV) criteria for BPD; (3) displayed recent suicidal behaviors (i.e., two episodes of NSSI or SA in the past 5 years, with at least one episode in the past 8 weeks); (4) spoke English proficiently; and (5) had health insurance in the past year in British Columbia or Ontario.

Exclusion criteria

Individuals were excluded from the study for the following reasons: (1) met DSM-IV criteria for a psychotic disorder, bipolar I disorder, or dementia; (2) demonstrated an IQ 70; (3) had received 8+ weeks of DBT within the last year; or (4) had plans to move out of Canada during the study trial.

Procedure

Intervention

DBT consisted of the comprehensive treatment model, including the following four components: (1) weekly, 60-minute individual therapy sessions aimed at increasing motivation to stay in treatment; (2) weekly, 2-hour DBT skills training groups, which included opportunities to learn skills related to mindfulness, interpersonal effectiveness, emotion regulation, and distress tolerance; (3) as-needed phone coaching on a 24/7-basis to support skill implementation; and (4) weekly therapist consultation meetings to enhance protocol adherence and provide therapist support. Individuals who missed four or more consecutive sessions were considered treatment dropouts.

Therapists

Therapists had either a master’s or doctoral degree and had at least 2 years of experience treating patients with BPD and receiving supervision in DBT. Adherence to DBT treatment was measured via the University of Washington DBT Adherence Coding Scale [33] by 12 independent raters. A random selection of 336 individual sessions and 62 group sessions indicated that overall adherence was good (DBT-12 individual therapy M[SD] = 4.15[0.21]). Scores ≥ 4 are considered “adherent” to DBT on the University of Washington DBT Adherence Coding Scale.

Measures

In the larger parent trials, participants were assessed at the following intervals throughout the course of the study: baseline, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months into treatment; and 15, 18, 21, and 24 months after treatment. Given that the primary aim of this study was to examine the impact of PTSD and childhood trauma over the course of DBT, outcomes were examined throughout the treatment phase only (i.e., baseline, 3, 6, 9, and 12-months).

Assessment of inclusion and exclusion criteria

The International Personality Disorder Examination – BPD Module (IPDE-BPD) [34] was used to assess BPD diagnosis. The Structured Clinical Interview for the DSM-IV, Axis I (SCID-I) [35] was administered to evaluate for the presence of what were previously known as “Axis I” disorders. The Structured Clinical Interview for the DSM-IV, Axis II (SCID-II) [36] was administered to evaluate for the presence of what were previously known as “Axis II” disorders. The Wechsler Test of Adult Reading (WTAR) [37] was used to assess participants’ cognitive functioning at the time of the study.

Predictor variables

PTSD Diagnosis. Participants were classified as BPD + PTSD if they met criteria for a PTSD diagnosis on the SCID-I, which comprehensively assesses for the presence of current and lifetime PTSD symptoms, including the presence of a Criterion A traumatic event (i.e., exposure to an event which actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence).

Childhood Abuse Types. To assess individuals’ experience with and severity of childhood trauma, the Child Trauma Questionnaire – Short Form (CTQ-SF) [38] was administered. For the current study, the Physical Abuse, Sexual Abuse, and Emotional Abuse subscales were included in the statistical analyses given their stronger reliability than the Neglect subscales [38]. The CTQ-SF has demonstrated good criterion validity and high measurement invariance across 4 diverse samples [38] as well as a good convergent validity with the Childhood Experiences Questionnaire-Revised (CEQ-R) [39] among a clinical sample of Danish individuals [39]. The CTQ-SF was administered at the 3-month time point given that it was designed to measure historical childhood trauma as well as to minimize participant fatigue at baseline.

Outcome measures

Total NSSI + SA. To measure the total frequency of NSSI + SA throughout the course of treatment, the Suicide Attempt and Self-Injury Interview (SASII) [40] was administered to participants every 3 months. The SASII demonstrates high interrater reliability with intraclass correlations (ICCs) ranging from 0.87 to 0.98. Finally, convergent validity has been established by comparing data from the SASII to a variety of alternative data collection methods (i.e., therapist notes, participant diary cards, and medical records) [40]. The internal consistency of the SASII for the current study’s sample was = 0.77.

BPD Severity. The Borderline Symptom Checklist- 23 (BSL-23) [41] was administered every 3 months to monitor the severity of BPD over the course of treatment. Bohus and colleagues also found that the BSL-23 had moderate to high convergent validity with the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI; r = 0.83–0.87) and the global severity index of the Revised Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90-R; r= 0.48–0.89) [42–44]. The internal consistency of the BSL-23 for the current study’s sample was = 0.92.

PTSD Severity. The PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) [45, 46] was administered every 3 months to monitor the severity of PTSD over the course of treatment. The PCL-5 demonstrated convergent validity with the PTSD Checklist (PCL; r= 85) [47], the Posttraumatic Stress Diagnostic Scale (PDS; r= 0.85) [48], and the Detailed Assessment of Posttraumatic Stress (DAPS; r= 0.84) [49]. Furthermore, the PCL-5 demonstrated discriminant validity with the Depression (r = 0.60), Mania (r = 0.31), and Antisocial Personality Features (r = 0.39) subscales of the Personality Assessment Inventory (PAI) [46, 50]. The internal consistency of the PCL-5 for the current study’s sample was = 0.92.

Data analytic plan

Data was analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences 28 (SPSS 28) [51] with an intent-to-treat (ITT) approach. Given the dependencies in participant data over time, Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) [52] was used to examine both study hypotheses, i.e., the moderating impact of (1) co-occurring PTSD and (2) different forms of childhood trauma on standard DBT outcomes among a sample of individuals with BPD. GEE is a semi-parametric derivation of generalized linear modeling (GLM) that indicates how the average of an outcome variable changes with covariates while accommodating for repeated measures of the same participant over time. It is based on the quasi-likelihood theory and does not make assumptions about the distributions of participant responses over time. Thus, GEE can accommodate skewed and non-normal data distributions. Further, GEE retains participants with missing data by estimating the working correlation and using the all available pairs method (i.e., all non-missing pairs of data are used to estimate the working correlation parameters) for maximum statistical power. The data in the current analysis met the aforementioned assumptions of GEE.

Furthermore, the quasi-likelihood under the independence model criterion (QIC) [53] was used to select the preferred correlation structure and best fitting model in each GEE analyses. Unstructured, autoregressive, and exchangeable covariance structures were considered for each GEE analysis. Covariance structures that evidenced the lowest QIC value were retained and interpreted. Of note, GEE only requires one timepoint per participant to estimate the rest of their scores across time (i.e., the n used in the analyses is the highest number of participants at any timepoint that were assessed). For the full sample, the highest number of participant scores was n = 118.

To test Hypothesis 1, Diagnostic Status (i.e., BPD + PTSD vs. BPD without PTSD) and Time in treatment were entered as predictors. Two-way interactions between Diagnostic Status (BPD + PTSD or BPD without PTSD)Time were entered to test the moderating effects of a diagnosis of BPD + PTSD on BSL-23 total score, PCL-5 total score, and total NSSI + SA frequency throughout pre- to post-treatment. To test Hypothesis 2, Mean Physical Abuse, Sexual Abuse and Emotional Abuse CTQ scores, and Time in treatment were entered as predictors. Two-way interactions between Physical AbuseTime, Sexual AbuseTime, and Emotional AbuseTime were entered to test the moderating effects of each form of childhood abuse on total BSL-23 score, PCL-5 score, and total NSSI + SA frequency throughout pre- to post-treatment. In this study, Time was considered a categorical variable.

Discussion

Individuals with BPD + PTSD have poorer treatment outcomes when compared to those with other co-occurring disorders with BPD [7]. Childhood trauma, commonly associated with both BPD [15] and PTSD [54], may also explain variability in clinical outcomes among this uniquely complex population. This study was designed to further enhance scientific and clinical understanding of the influence of PTSD and childhood trauma on DBT outcomes by clarifying the specific impact a PTSD diagnosis and different forms of childhood trauma have on changes in BPD severity, PTSD severity, and frequency of NSSI + SA among a sample of individuals with BPD undergoing 12 months of standard DBT.

In contrast to our hypotheses, results revealed that there were no significant differences in changes in our clinical outcomes (i.e., BPD severity, PTSD severity, NSSI + SA) between individuals with BPD + PTSD and those with BPD without PTSD. As well, in contrast to our hypotheses, severity of childhood sexual and emotional abuse predicted quicker rates of reduction in BPD and PTSD severity, while the severity of childhood physical abuse predicted slower rates of reduction in BPD and PTSD severity.

The effect of PTSD on change in clinical outcomes

[18],Our finding that the presence of PTSD did not predict reduced rates of reduction in our outcomes suggests that there may not be a meaningful difference between the benefit achieved by those with BPD without PTSD and those with BPD + PTSD undergoing standard DBT. Although both groups exhibited reductions in our outcomes, there was not a significant difference in the rates of reduction between the two groups. To date, much of the literature focuses on the adverse effect that co-occurring PTSD has on treatment outcomes. Indeed, consistent with the current study’s hypothesis, various studies suggest that individuals with co-occurring BPD and PTSD demonstrate impaired outcomes when compared to their BPD-only counterparts [4, 18, 55]. While our hypothesis is largely consistent with much of the literature to date, the lack of support for our hypothesis could possibly be due to differences in sample characteristics, assessment tools, and/or methodological approaches across multiple studies. Indeed, while the current study included individuals with recent and chronic NSSI + SA as well as who fully met DSM-IV diagnostic criteria for BPD [30], many previous studies have smaller sample sizes [4, 5, 18, 56], limited to no requirements for active NSSI + SA [4, 56], and outdated and/or less rigorous diagnostic methods [9]. Thus, it is possible that these studies may not specifically capture the clinical presentations of individuals with co-occurring BPD and PTSD as conceptualized in the current study.

However, consistent with current findings, other research has suggested that the presence of PTSD does not have an adverse effect on certain psychodiagnostic outcomes among those with BPD in DBT, GPM, MBT, or residential treatment with a combination of DBT, TFP, and MBT [7, 14, 57]. It is possible that these studies align with present findings due to largely consistent methodological approaches among assessment measures and outcome data as well as similar sample characteristics.

Furthermore, our findings are derived from a clinical sample of individuals with recent and chronic suicidal behaviors. It is possible that among individuals with BPD with recent NSSI + SA, the additional “load” of co-occurring PTSD did not further increase the already elevated clinical severity of the sample. While there is evidence that more persistent and severe types of PTSD predict poorer outcomes on BPD severity, substance use, and self-harm frequency [7, 17, 18], the evaluation of specific subgroups of PTSD was outside the scope of the current study. Thus, it is possible that the additional diagnosis of PTSD did not have a large impact on the treatment outcomes examined in the current study.

The effect of childhood trauma on change in clinical outcomes

Interestingly, findings from our second aim were also inconsistent with our hypotheses, which predicted that childhood sexual abuse would predict a reduced the rate of reduction in our outcomes, when controlling for other forms of abuse. Rather, higher levels of childhood sexual and emotional abuse significantly predicted quicker rates of reduction in BPD severity, while higher levels of childhood physical abuse predicted a slower rate of reduction in BPD severity over the course of DBT. Consistently, higher levels of childhood emotional abuse similarly predicted a quicker rate of reduction in PTSD severity, while higher levels of childhood physical abuse predicted a reduced rate of reduction. It is possible that, since DBT focuses on the invalidating rearing environment, sexual and emotional abuse were more directly addressed than physical abuse.

Notably, while different forms of childhood trauma tend to co-occur, our findings suggest that different types of abuse predict distinctive treatment outcomes. Interestingly, childhood physical and sexual abuse – both consisting of acts of physical violence toward a child – predicted different trajectories of change; it is possible that these differential outcomes are due to differences in how childhood physical and sexual abuse are measured on the CTQ-SF [38]. For example, each of the Physical Abuse items on the CTQ-SF inquires about physical violence perpetrated by a family member (e.g., I got hit so hard by someone in my family that it left me with bruises or marks), while all Sexual Abuse items inquire about sexual violence perpetrated by anyone (e.g., Someone tried to touch me in a sexual way or tried to make me touch them). Thus, it is possible that those with caregiver-perpetrated abuse (i.e., those with childhood physical abuse in our sample) may have a more complex relationship with their trauma, and therefore, experience slower rates of improvement in DBT.

For example, research has found that children with caregiver-perpetrated abuse tend to experience abuse from an earlier age and for a longer duration of time than their counterparts with non-caregiver-perpetrated abuse [58]. Adding to the sheer complexity of this relationship, caregivers who abuse their children may attribute different meanings to their abusive behavior [59], thereby implicating multiple forms of abuse when directing physical violence toward their own children. Therefore, in our sample, individuals with childhood physical abuse may have had attenuated treatment outcomes given the unique implications of caregiver-perpetrated childhood abuse.

In contrast, childhood sexual abuse predicted an accelerated rate of reduction in BPD severity, but not PTSD severity, over time. It is possible that, within our sample, incidences of childhood sexual abuse are not as strongly linked to one’s family members given the wording of Sexual Abuse items on the CTQ-SF [38]. Perhaps without the unique complications that arise from caregiver-perpetrated abuse, those with higher levels of sexual abuse may uniquely benefit from DBT (i.e., exhibit faster rates of improvement in BPD severity). However, given that childhood sexual abuse has been highly associated both with BPD [20, 60] and PTSD [54, 61], it was somewhat surprising that childhood sexual abuse did not also influence changes in PTSD severity overtime. Perhaps these findings are due to differences in other clinical features in this sample that may uniquely impact the trajectory of change in BPD severity versus PTSD severity. In addition, review of the main effects in Table 6 indicated that, in our sample, higher baseline childhood sexual abuse was associated with higher BPD severity at baseline. Thus, it is possible that the accelerated rate of improvement in BPD severity was due to a ceiling effect. Indeed, future research is needed to clarify and expand upon the factors that affect the complex relationship between childhood sexual abuse, BPD, and PTSD.

Finally, childhood emotional abuse significantly predicted accelerated rates of improvement in both BPD and PTSD severities overtime. Among individuals with BPD and PTSD, childhood emotional abuse is a highly common and disruptive experience [15, 16], which is implicated in the development of both BPD [10] and PTSD [62]. While somewhat surprising, these findings are consistent with Linehan’s [10] biosocial theory of BPD, which states that BPD is an outcome of the combination of persistent invalidating experiences in childhood and an individual’s biological predisposition to develop emotion dysregulation. Indeed, emotional abuse could be considered a more centralfeature within this invalidating rearing environment than other forms of abuse. These findings are also consistent with the threat/deprivation model [63], which posits that exposure to threat (i.e., emotional abuse) is associated with increased emotional reactivity, thereby leading to higher levels of emotion dysregulation. As such, those with emotional abuse may have an accelerated treatment response in DBT due to potential ceiling effects of heightened levels of threat in one’s environment. Consistently, and as noted above for childhood sexual abuse, our GEE models also suggested that higher levels of emotional abuse were related to higher baseline BPD and PTSD severity levels. This too may explain the increased improvement of symptoms over time as those with higher emotional abuse perhaps had more “room” for improvement. Therefore, within this model, it is possible that DBT is particularly effective for individuals with a history of emotional abuse.

Limitations and future directions

This study was among the first to examine the effects that childhood sexual, physical, and emotional abuse have on comprehensive DBT outcomes among a sample of individuals with BPD. Utilizing a large sample size as well as repeated assessments across 5 timepoints, our findings offer a robust look into the ways that different forms of childhood trauma can impact important clinical outcomes, specifically, BPD severity, PTSD severity, and total frequency of suicidal behaviors. Although the present study addressed many of the limitations found in the methodology of prior studies, it has several limitations to note. First, one methodological limitation of the current study is that its sample is limited in generalizability. This sample was comprised of a suicidal, predominantly female group of individuals with BPD; therefore, replication of the present analyses with a larger, more representative BPD sample in terms of severity and self-reported gender is recommended.

Second, our analyses neglected to account for other important factors that could have influenced our findings. Since gender identity has been associated with clinically meaningful differences in BPD pathology between males and females [64], accounting for gender as a moderator when replicating data analyses is warranted. Relatedly, our study only controlled for the effect that co-occurring PTSD had on DBT outcomes among a BPD sample; however, participants within our study had several other co-occurring diagnoses which were unaccounted for. Indeed, recent literature has identified differential clinical profiles of co-occurring diagnoses among BPD individuals with substance use disorders [65], eating disorders [66], and bipolar disorders [66]. Thus, future research should also delineate the potential role of other co-occurring diagnoses when examining the effect that co-occurring PTSD has on DBT outcomes. Relatedly, this study did not examine follow-up periods. As such, it is unclear if treatment effects may have still been observed during follow-up phases. Future research should include follow-up periods to assess the continued impact of treatment on outcome data.

Finally, while childhood trauma encompasses abuse, neglect, and exploitation, the current study focused specifically on abuse (emotional, physical, and sexual). This decision was guided by prior research demonstrating that CTQ abuse subscales show stronger psychometric reliability and clearer associations with clinical outcomes compared to neglect subscales [38, 67]. Recent work also highlights that abuse dimensions, in particular, robustly predict trauma-related psychopathology [68]. In addition, the present sample was only sufficiently powered to examine abuse variables, limiting our ability to test the unique effects of neglect. As such, our findings may not capture the full range of childhood trauma experiences that could influence DBT outcomes. Future studies with larger samples should expand these analyses to include neglect, which will help to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how different forms of childhood trauma shape treatment response and allow for more tailored clinical interventions.

Clinical implications and conclusion

The findings from the current study offer several clinical implications. First, standard DBT appears to be comparably effective for both individuals with BPD without PTSD and BPD + PTSD. The addition of a PTSD diagnosis did not significantly predict slower recovery among those with BPD + PTSD. Thus, while adapted DBT treatments such as DBT-PTSD [69–71] or DBT-PE [72] have accrued support in recent years, our study suggests that perhaps standard DBT may remain a viable treatment option for individuals with BPD + PTSD. Indeed, standard DBT could be an appropriate alternative for those with BPD + PTSD who do not have access to disorder-specific treatments for BPD + PTSD. Nevertheless, findings from this study could also be used to inform adapted treatments such as DBT-PTSD [69–71] or DBT-PE [72]; suggesting that DBT-based interventions, with or without adaptations, are viable treatments for individuals with BPD + PTSD and/or those with emotional abuse or sexual abuse histories.

Second, this study highlights the importance of comprehensively assessing for the presence of different types of childhood abuse, particularly when evaluating for trauma. Although PTSD Criterion A events are often assessed when individuals initially present to treatment, other significant forms of childhood trauma (e.g., emotional abuse and neglect, physical neglect) may be largely overlooked. Findings from our study suggest that, rather than a PTSD diagnosis, it may be that different forms of childhood trauma specifically significantly affect the trajectories of individual treatment. Of note, our findings suggest that DBT interventions may be particularly beneficial for individuals with higher levels of emotional abuse. Taken together, understanding which forms of childhood trauma lead to better or worse outcomes among clinical populations may allow future interventions to become more individually tailored and more strategically target the factors that significantly affect the trajectory of recovery over time.
