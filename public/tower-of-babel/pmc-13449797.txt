Evaluation of subchronic oral dose toxicity of Antrodia camphorata mycelium powder in rodents as a novel food source
Authors: Won-Yong Shim, Eun-Sung Park, Sung-Il Jang, Shingo Horii, Wu-Che Wen, Sun-Don Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntrodia camphorata (A. camphorata), also known as “Niuchangchih” in Taiwan, is a medicinal fungus traditionally used for its hepatoprotective, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties. However, the safety and potential toxic effects of its mycelium form have not yet been fully established. This study aimed to evaluate the subchronic oral toxicity and determine the no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) of A. camphorata mycelium powder in Sprague Dawley (SD) rats under Good Laboratory Practice (GLP)-compliant conditions.SD rats (10/sex/group) were administered A. camphorata mycelium powder orally at doses of 0, 500, 1000, and 2000 mg/kg/day as a 10 ml/kg/day suspension in water for 13 weeks. A 4-week recovery study was conducted at 0 and 2000 mg/kg/day with 5 rats/sex/group.Dose-dependent increases in liver and adrenal weights were noted, accompanied by hepatocellular and adrenal cortical hypertrophy, which were fully reversed in males and showed a tendency toward recovery in females after the recovery period. Repeated oral administration of A. camphorata mycelium powder for 13 weeks produced no adverse systemic toxicity in rats. The NOAEL was determined to be 2000 mg/kg/day in both sexes, supporting its safety for further development as a functional food or medicinal product. These findings provide important safety data supporting its further application in health supplements.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-026-05425-z.Keywords: Antrodia camphorata, Nonclinical study, Subchronic toxicity, Food safety

Introduction

Antrodia camphorata (A. camphorata), also referred to as “Niuchangchih” in Taiwan, is a distinctive medicinal mushroom endemic to Taiwan. It has been traditionally utilized for treating various health conditions due to its broad spectrum of pharmacological activities, including immunomodulatory and anticancer effects [1–3]. Notably, the fruiting body of A. camphorata has historically been used in Taiwan to alleviate symptoms associated with alcohol consumption [1, 3]. Additionally, it serves as a traditional remedy and dietary supplement for treating numerous ailments such as cancer, diarrhea, liver injury, inflammation, abdominal pain, hypertension, pruritus, and motion sickness [2, 4]. However, A. camphorata exhibits a very slow growth rate and is scarcely noticeable in the wild until the host tree collapses [1]. Moreover, inducing the growth and formation of the A. camphorata fruiting body even under controlled laboratory conditions proves to be challenging [2]. As a result, various cultivation methods have been developed to meet the increasing market demand [2, 5], including mycelium powder forms, such as the test article used in our study.

Antroquinonol, a tetrahydro ubiquinone derivative predominantly found in the A. camphorata mycelium, has been identified as its major bioactive compound [6]. Recent studies have demonstrated its broad pharmacological properties, including antioxidant and hepatoprotective activities [7, 8]. Our previous studies have highlighted the therapeutic potential of A. camphorata mycelium powder in treating liver injuries, particularly those induced by alcohol consumption [9]. The observed benefits include enhanced antioxidative enzyme activity, reduced levels of liver damage enzymes, and decreased lipid peroxidation and accumulation. These promising results suggest potential hepatoprotective effects of A. camphorata mycelium powder.

Given the promising therapeutic potential of A. camphorata, it is crucial to evaluate its safety profile through a comprehensive subchronic toxicity study. This study investigated the potential toxic effects of A. camphorata mycelium powder during repeated administration. Understanding the safety and toxicological characteristics of this compound is essential to support its further development as a potential therapeutic application.

Materials and methods

Preparation and analysis of Antrodia camphorata mycelium powder

The preparation of A. camphorata mycelium powder was supported by Golden Biotechnology Corp. (Deposition; Tamsui, New Taipei, Taiwan) [10]. The test article, designated as A. camphorata mycelium powder (Strain identification: BCRC39106), was provided as a light brown powder and stored at room temperature (15–25 °C) throughout the study.

The homogeneity and stability of A. camphorata mycelium powder in the vehicle were verified in an analytical method validation study (CORESTEMCHEMON Study No. 23-NV-0609). The concentration and homogeneity of the test article formulation were determined using a validated high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) method conducted in compliance with OECD Principles of Good Laboratory Practice and according to ICH Q2(R2) guideline [11]. Chromatographic analysis was performed using an HPLC system (Waters, USA) equipped with a quaternary pump, autosampler, column oven, and UV detector. Separation was achieved on a reverse-phase C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.6 μm particle size) maintained at 30 °C. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (deionized water) and solvent B (acetonitrile). A gradient elution program was applied as follows: 0–3 min, 80% B; 3–3.5 min, 100% B; 3.5–5 min, 80% B; followed by re-equilibration to initial conditions. The flow rate was set at 0.4 mL/min, and the injection volume was 2 µL. Detection was carried out at a wavelength of 265 nm. Antroquinonol, a patented proprietary compound developed and supplied by Golden Biotechnology Corp., was selected as a representative standard marker based on its abundance and stability in the test article. The standard marker was quantified using an external standard calibration curve prepared over a concentration range of 0.5–10 µg/mL, showing good linearity (R² ≥ 0.999). The retention time of antroquinonol under the described conditions was approximately 2.0–2.5 min (Fig. 1). The results confirmed that the test article was stable for up to 5 h at room temperature and for 7 days when protected from light and stored under refrigerated conditions. Accordingly, dose formulations were prepared at least once weekly and stored refrigerated and protected from light until administration.

The main component was identified as antroquinonol, a characteristic bioactive compound of A. camphorata. Prior to dosing, the test article underwent comprehensive quality evaluation, including appearance, moisture content, total heavy metal, and quantification of antroquinonol by HPLC. Microbiological testing was also performed in accordance with safety standards [12], including total aerobic microbial count, total yeast and mold count, and the presence of Escherichia coli and Salmonella spp. The detailed results of these tests are presented in Table 1.

For formulation, the powder was first crushed and finely ground using a mortar and pestle, then weighed separately for each dose level. The weighed powder was subsequently mixed with the vehicle, water for injection (WFI; E2Z8B21, 48XBF95), supplied by DAI HAN PHARM. CO., LTD. (Seoul, Republic of Korea). The vehicle was selected based on the sponsor’s request.

The homogeneity and stability of A. camphorata mycelium powder in the vehicle were verified in an analytical method validation study (CORESTEMCHEMON Study No. 23-NV-0609). The results confirmed that the test article was stable for up to 5 h at room temperature and for 7 days when protected from light and stored under refrigerated conditions. Accordingly, dose formulations were prepared at least once weekly and stored refrigerated and protected from light until administration.

Experimental animals and maintenance

Specific Pathogen-Free (SPF) Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats, six weeks of age, were procured from Samtako Bio Korea Co., Ltd. (Osan, Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea). Selection of this strain was based on its widespread use in preclinical toxicity studies and the availability of extensive historical control data. Upon arrival, animals underwent an eight-day quarantine and acclimatization period, during which general health status was monitored.

Animals were housed in a controlled environment (temperature: 22 ± 3 °C; relative humidity: 55 ± 15%; ventilation rate: 10–20 air changes per hour; light/dark cycle: 12 h; illumination: 150–300 lx). Environmental conditions were continuously recorded, with no deviations observed that could influence animal welfare or data interpretation. All animals had ad libitum access to irradiated pellet diet (Teklad certified global 18% protein rodent diet, Inotiv, USA) and UV-sterilized, ultrafiltered drinking water, both confirmed to comply with safety requirements.

All procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of CORESTEMCHEMON Inc. (Approval number: CHEM-2023-0761) and conducted in accordance with OECD guidelines and Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) regulations for nonclinical laboratory studies, as stipulated by the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) of the Republic of Korea [13–15]. All experimental procedures were also performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations for the care and use of laboratory animals.

A. camphorata treatment and repeated dose oral toxicity study

A total of 80 Specific Pathogen-Free (SPF) Sprague–Dawley rats (10 males and 10 females per group) were randomly allocated to four treatment groups: one vehicle control group and three groups receiving A. camphorata mycelium powder at 500, 1000, or 2000 mg/kg/day. An additional 10 rats per group (5 males and 5 females per group) were assigned to the vehicle control and the high-dose (2000 mg/kg/day) groups as recovery animals, enabling assessment of potential delayed toxicity and reversibility of findings. The test article was administered via oral gavage once daily, seven days per week, for 13 weeks. The oral route was selected as it reflects the intended clinical mode of administration. Individual dosing volumes were calculated weekly based on the most recent body weight.

Clinical observations and functional assessments

All animals were monitored at least twice daily throughout both dosing and recovery periods for mortality and general condition. Clinical signs, including their onset, duration, and severity, were recorded on a daily basis. Any moribund or deceased animal was immediately weighed and subjected to a full necropsy. Body weights were measured on the first day of dosing (Day 1) and subsequently at weekly intervals. Fasting body weights were also recorded on the scheduled necropsy day. Food and water intake were quantified weekly during the 13-week dosing phase and throughout the 4-week recovery phase by measuring the difference between the supplied and remaining amounts.

Ophthalmological evaluations were conducted before dosing initiation and during the final study week for all animals in the control, high-dose, and recovery groups. Following pharmacological mydriasis, the anterior segment, optical media, and fundus were examined using a fundus camera. Functional observational assessments were performed on all surviving animals during the 11th week of treatment, evaluating sensory reflexes, neuromotor coordination, and spontaneous locomotor activity. In addition, estrous cyclicity in all females was assessed by daily examination of vaginal smears for five consecutive days prior to scheduled necropsy to evaluate potential effects on reproductive function.

Clinical pathology evaluations

Urine samples were collected from animals housed in individual metabolic cages. General urinalysis was performed using test strips (Multistix 10SG, SIEMENS, USA) analyzed by an automatic reader (CliniTek 100, SIEMENS, USA), assessing parameters such as glucose (GLU), bilirubin (BIL), ketone body (KET), specific gravity (SG), pH, protein (PRO), urobilinogen (URO), nitrite (NIT), occult blood (BLO), leucocyte (LEU), clarity and urine color. Sediment analysis was conducted microscopically following centrifugation (1500 rpm, 5 min, Combi-514R, Hanil, Republic of Korea) and Sternheimer-Malbin staining.

For hematological tests, blood samples were collected from the posterior vena cava under deep isoflurane anesthesia after overnight fasting. General hematological parameters assessed included red blood cell (RBC), hemoglobin concentration (HGB), hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), red cell distribution width (RDW), hemoglobin distribution width (HDW), reticulocyte (RET), platelet (PLT), mean platelet volume (MPV), white blood cell (WBC), neutrophil (NEU), lymphocyte (LYM), monocyte (MONO), eosinophil (EOS), basophil (BASO), and large unstained cells (LUC) measured using a Coulter counter (ADVIA 2120, SIMENS, USA).

Coagulation testing involved plasma prepared from centrifuged (3000 rpm, 10 min, Combi-514R, Hanil, Republic of Korea) blood samples collected into anticoagulant-containing tubes at a blood-to-additive ratio of 9:1. Activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) and prothrombin time (PT) were determined using a coagulation analyzer (ADVIA 2120, SIEMENS, USA).

For clinical biochemistry, blood samples were collected into 5 mL Vacutainer tubes (SST™ II Advance, BD, UK) containing a clot activator. Following clotting at room temperature for 15–20 min, samples were centrifuged for 10 min to obtain serum. Biochemical parameters measured included aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), creatine phosphokinase (CPK), total bilirubin (TBIL), glucose (GLU), total cholesterol (TCHO), triglycerides (TG), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), albumin/globulin ratio (A/G), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (CRE), inorganic phosphorus (IP), calcium ion (Ca2+), sodium ion (Na+), potassium ion (K⁺), chloride ion (Cl⁻), urea (URE), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL), analyzed using a clinical chemistry analyzer (AU680, Beckman Coulter, USA).

For thyroid hormone analysis, separated serum samples were analyzed by ELISA using commercial kits for T3 (CSB-E05085r, Cusabio, USA), T4 (CSB-E05082r, Cusabio, USA), and TSH (LS-F5124, LS Bio, USA).

Organ weights and histopathological evaluations

Following completion of blood collection for clinical pathology, all animals were sacrificed by transection of the posterior vena cava and abdominal aorta under deep isoflurane anesthesia. After an overnight fast, animals underwent a full necropsy. The external body surface and internal organs were examined macroscopically, and any visible abnormalities were documented. Absolute weights of major organs—including the brain, pituitary gland, thymus, lungs, heart, liver, spleen, kidneys, adrenal glands, prostate, seminal vesicles with coagulating glands, testes, epididymides, uterus, ovaries, and the thyroid with attached parathyroid glands—were recorded. Relative organ weights were then calculated based on the corresponding terminal body weights.

Tissues from all animals were fixed in appropriate preservative solutions for histological preparation. Comprehensive microscopic evaluation was conducted on all tissues from the control and high-dose groups, as well as on grossly abnormal or target organs (liver and adrenal glands) from the low- and mid-dose groups. Paraffin-embedded sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and examined microscopically. Diagnostic terminology followed the International Harmonization of Nomenclature and Diagnostic Criteria (INHAND) recommendations [16, 17]. All histopathological data were compiled and analyzed using the Provantis® data acquisition system (Instem, UK).

Statistical analysis

Statistical evaluations were performed using the Provantis® software package (Instem, UK). Quantitative parameters, including body weight, feed and water intake, hematological indices, clinical biochemistry results, organ weights, functional observation scores, and serum thyroid hormone concentrations, were analyzed by analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Dunnett’s post hoc multiple comparison test. For pairwise comparisons between the control and high-dose groups, Student’s t-test was applied. Histopathological incidence data were assessed using either the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on the expected frequency distribution. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Clinical observations and functional assessments

In the high-dose group (2000 mg/kg/day), three females died during the study—two on Day 42 and one on Day 56. All three exhibited dyspnea shortly after dosing. Histopathological evaluation of pulmonary tissues revealed inflammatory cell infiltration and accumulation of alveolar macrophages, particularly within the alveolar spaces (Supplement Figure S1).

During routine clinical monitoring, salivation was recorded in all treated male groups from Day 44 through Day 90. In females, salivation occurred between Day 44 and Day 76 in the low-dose group, and from Day 44 to Day 90 in both the mid- and high-dose groups. A marked elevation in water intake was noted in high-dose males from Day 8 through Day 91 (Fig. 2), which corresponded with a statistically significant increase in urine output confirmed by urinalysis (Table 2). Water consumption in males returned to baseline values during the recovery phase.

No treatment-related alterations were identified in body weight, feed consumption, functional observation parameters, or estrous cycle patterns. Ophthalmologic assessments revealed no abnormalities in either sex across all dose levels. Changes in body weight over time are shown in Fig. 2, and no statistically significant differences in weight gain were observed throughout the experimental period.

Clinical pathology evaluations

Urine volume in males displayed a statistically significant dose-dependent increase in the main study groups (Table 2), which normalized during the recovery phase.

There were no test article-related changes observed in hematology, clinical biochemistry, or thyroid hormone analysis. Although some parameters showed statistically significant differences, these variations were minimal in magnitude and lacked a consistent dose–response pattern. No findings suggestive of an effect on the thyroid-liver axis were identified [18].

Organ weights and histopathological evaluations

Gross examination at necropsy revealed adrenal gland enlargement in both sexes, with changes correlating positively with dose level and accompanied by microscopic evidence of cortical hypertrophy. In males, absolute liver weight was significantly higher in the high-dose group, and relative liver weight was elevated in both the mid- and high-dose groups. In females, both absolute and relative adrenal weights were significantly greater in the mid- and high-dose groups. Additionally, liver weights—absolute and relative—were significantly increased in these same groups (Fig. 3). None of these alterations persisted into the recovery period.

Discussion

A. camphorata, an endemic medicinal mushroom cultivated in Taiwan, is known for its diverse pharmacological activities including immunomodulatory and hepatoprotective effects [1–3, 9]. Despite its increasing therapeutic use, the safety and potential toxic effects of A. camphorata have not been extensively studied to date underscoring the need for comprehensive toxicological evaluation. In this study, a 13-week repeated oral toxicity study was conducted under GLP conditions following OECD TG 408 to evaluate the toxicological profile of A. camphorata mycelium powder. This study was designed to provide critical data to support the safety of application of A. camphorata by systematically investigating its effects in rats.

During the study, three female animals in the high-dose group (2000 mg/kg/day) were found dead. All animals exhibited signs of dyspnea shortly after administration and death occurred within 12 h. Although these fatalities were considered most likely attributable to gavage-related reflux, this interpretation is supported by previously published findings. It is well documented that reflux following oral gavage can result in severe respiratory effects and mortality, even in the absence of systemic toxicity [19, 20]. In repeated-dose toxicity studies, unscheduled deaths preceded by clinical signs such as dyspnea and respiratory distress have been reported to arise from gastroesophageal reflux and subsequent aspiration of the test article formulation, rather than from technical dosing errors or intrinsic systemic toxicity [20]. Importantly, these studies demonstrated that comparable systemic exposure via alternative routes (e.g., intravenous administration) did not produce similar adverse effects, further supporting a non-systemic mechanism. Additionally, reflux-mediated retrograde aspiration into the upper respiratory tract has been shown to induce nasal and respiratory lesions, with associated clinical signs and mortality, without clear evidence of direct test article-related toxicity [21]. Such findings highlight that gavage-related reflux is a recognized confounding factor in toxicology studies and may lead to respiratory compromise and death independent of systemic toxic effects [22]. Due to the physicochemical properties, forced administration of the test article may be associated with gastric reflux [19], which is further discussed in relation to the observed salivation, increased water consumption, and subsequent increase in urine output in the following sections. Therefore, similar physiological responses such as gastric reflux, increased water intake, and increased urine volume may also occur in humans when the test article is administered in viscous suspension form. Taken together, these observations support the conclusion that the deaths observed in this study are more consistent with procedure-related reflux/aspiration events rather than systemic toxicity.

Salivation was specifically observed intermittently and primarily in close temporal proximity to dosing, without persistence or progression over time. Such findings are commonly reported in oral gavage studies [23, 24] and are often attributed to the taste, viscosity, or mild irritative properties of the administered formulation, which can stimulate oropharyngeal receptors and induce a reflex salivary response. Importantly, no other clinical signs indicative of autonomic dysfunction—such as lacrimation, altered pupil size, abnormal respiration, or changes in heart rate—were observed in any treated animals [25]. Furthermore, there were no correlating findings in body weight, food consumption, functional observational battery assessments, or gross pathological examinations that would suggest systemic involvement. Based on the transient nature, lack of dose-dependent severity, and absence of supporting toxicological findings, the salivation noted in treated animals is therefore considered more consistent with a localized, reflex-mediated response to oropharyngeal or gastrointestinal stimulation rather than a manifestation of systemic autonomic nervous system involvement.

Increase of water consumption in the male high-dose group was accompanied by an increase in urine volume, suggesting a test article-related effect. However, no concurrent increases in renal function parameters such as BUN or creatinine were observed (Supplement Table S6), and no histopathological degenerative changes were detected in urinary system organs. Therefore, these changes were considered non-adverse effect. The increase in water intake and urine output is presumed to reflect a physiological response to maintain homeostasis under exposure to a high concentration of the test article [26].

Upon necropsy, macroscopic enlargement of the adrenal glands was observed in both sexes, demonstrating a dose-dependent relationship. This finding was supported by increased adrenal weights, as well as histopathological evidence of diffuse cortical hypertrophy, particularly in females. Additionally, a dose-related increase in liver weights was noted in both sexes, although histopathological evidence of hepatocellular hypertrophy was restricted to females. The hepatocellular hypertrophy observed was minimal, not accompanied by inflammatory or degenerative changes, and was not associated with significant elevations in liver enzyme levels such as ALT, AST or ALP which are important biomarkers for drug induced liver injury (DILI) in nonclinical and clinical studies. According to established toxicological principles, liver hypertrophy without accompanying functional or degenerative alterations is generally regarded as an adaptive, non-adverse response to metabolic stimulation [27, 28]. Importantly, the mortality observed in female animals is considered unrelated to the hepatocellular hypertrophy, as there was no supporting evidence of liver dysfunction or pathological progression that would indicate a causal relationship between the hepatic findings and death. With regard to the apparent sex-specific response, including the occurrence of hepatocellular hypertrophy and mortality predominantly in females, this difference suggests that the test article may exert a greater pharmacological or metabolic impact in female animals. Such sex-related differences are well recognized in toxicology and may be attributed to variations in hepatic enzyme expression, hormone-regulated metabolic pathways, or systemic exposure (e.g., toxicokinetics) between males and females. In addition, A. camphorata mycelium powder has previously demonstrated hepatoprotective properties, including reductions in liver damage enzymes in an efficacy study [9], supporting the notion that the hepatic finding in this study is non-adverse. Similarly, the diffuse cortical hypertrophy observed in the adrenal glands was considered a non-adverse effect. This interpretation was based on the minimal severity of the lesion and the absence of accompanying degenerative changes such as inflammation, necrosis, or cellular atypia. In addition, adrenal cortical hypertrophy is not generally regarded as a preneoplastic lesion [29]. The observed sex-specific pattern, with a higher incidence in females, may be associated with greater sensitivity of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (HPA) to stress in females compared to males [30, 31]. Furthermore, these findings showed a tendency toward recovery following the recovery period, supporting their non-adverse nature.

Antroquinonol is a tetrahydro-ubiquinone derivative found predominantly in the mycelium of A. camphorata and is one of several bioactive compounds, including terpenoids, benzenoids, lignans, benzoquinone derivatives, succinic and maleic acid derivatives, polysaccharides, sterols, nucleotides, and fatty acids [6, 7, 32–34]. It acts as an anti-cancer, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and hepatoprotective agent [6, 7, 32–40]. Antroquinonol is also being investigated for its potential benefits in treating conditions such as hyperlipidemia, non-small cell lung cancer, and atopic dermatitis [33, 34, 37]. Recently, it was reported that antioxidant and hepatoprotective activities were demonstrated in an alcohol-induced liver damage model, primarily through activation of the Nrf2 pathway [9, 39]. Notably, in the 13-week repeated-dose toxicity study, the observed tendency for decreases in certain parameters associated with DILI suggests that although active antroquinonol was present at a concentration of 13.1 mg/g, it was still at a sufficient concentration to exert a pharmacological effect on liver-related markers [9]. These findings suggest potential hepatoprotective effects of A. camphorata mycelium powder, consistent with previous efficacy and toxicity studies.

In the present 13-week repeated-dose study in rats, several test article–related findings were observed, including mortality in a limited number of animals, increases in organ weights, and certain histopathological changes. However, the deaths were not considered to be attributable to systemic toxicity; rather, they were judged to be associated with the intrinsic physicochemical characteristics of the test article (e.g., administration-related or material-specific effects). Although some organ weight changes and histopathological findings showed partial correlation, these observations were not accompanied by inflammatory responses or degenerative lesions indicative of adverse toxicity. Moreover, the observed hypertrophy in certain organs demonstrated a tendency toward reversibility during the recovery period. Therefore, these findings are considered adaptive and non-adverse in nature, rather than toxicologically significant effects. Taken together, the statistically significant changes observed during the dosing period alone are not sufficient to be interpreted as adverse effects. Accordingly, it is scientifically justified to designate the NOAEL as 2000 mg/kg/day, the highest dose tested, in this study.

Several limitations of the study should be acknowledged. As this investigation was performed in rodents, direct extrapolation to humans remains limited. Oral gavage represents a bolus dosing method that differs fundamentally from voluntary oral intake in humans, where swallowing behavior, posture, and ingestion rate are physiologically regulated. Therefore, certain observations in this study, including isolated mortality events or transient physiological responses, may be influenced by procedure-related factors and/or the physicochemical characteristics of the test article under bolus dosing conditions, rather than reflecting intrinsic systemic toxicity. Accordingly, such findings should be interpreted with caution and are not considered directly predictive of clinical risk in humans. Evaluation at higher concentrations may be needed to fully define the safety margin. In addition, the physicochemical characteristics of the test article, which may have contributed to physiological responses, warrant further study to optimize formulations or dosing methods. Although adrenal cortical hypertrophy was considered an adaptive and non-adverse effect based on minimal severity, lack of histopathological degenerative changes, and partial reversibility, the absence of functional adrenal biomarkers such as ACTH, corticosterone, and aldosterone limited a more comprehensive mechanistic evaluation of potential hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis involvement. Nevertheless, the present findings provide important nonclinical evidence supporting the safety of A. camphorata mycelium powder and contribute valuable data for its further development and potential clinical application.

Conclusion

Antrodia camphorata mycelium powder administered orally to rats for 13 weeks did not cause any adverse effects at doses up to 2000 mg/kg/day. The study established the NOAEL at 2000 mg/kg/day for both sexes. These findings contribute to the foundational safety data necessary for the future application of A. camphorata in therapeutic or functional product development.
