Optimization over convex polyhedra via Hadamard parametrizations
Tianyun Tang, Kim-Chuan Toh

Abstract
In this paper, we study linearly constrained optimization problems (LCP). After applying Hadamard parametrization, the feasible set of the parametrized problem (LCPH) becomes an algebraic variety, with conducive geometric properties which we explore in depth. We derive explicit formulas for the tangent cones and second-order tangent sets associated with the parametrized polyhedra. Based on these formulas, we develop a procedure to recover the Lagrangian multipliers associated with the constraints to verify the optimality conditions of the given primal variable without requiring additional constraint qualifications. Moreover, we develop a systematic way to stratify the variety into a disjoint union of finitely many Riemannian manifolds. This leads us to develop a hybrid algorithm combining Riemannian optimization and projected gradient to solve (LCP) with convergence guarantees. Numerical experiments are conducted to verify the effectiveness of our method compared with various state-of-the-art algorithms.



keywords:
convex polyhedra, Hadamard parametrization, algebraic variety, Riemannian optimization

Mathematics subject classification: 90C26, 90C30, 90C46

Introduction

1.1
Optimization over convex polyhedra

In this paper, we aim at exploring a new approach for solving the following optimization problem over a convex polyhedron:

\min\left\{\phi(x):\ Ax=b,\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\right\},

(LCP)

where
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
A=[A_{1};A_{2};\ldots;A_{m}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

We denote the feasible set of (
LCP
) as
\mathcal{C}_{A,b}
and make the following basic assumption.

Assumption 1
.

\phi
(not necessarily convex) is continuously differentiable;
A
has full row rank;

\mathcal{C}_{A,b}\neq\emptyset.

Problem (
LCP
) covers many types of optimization problems including linear programming (LP) and convex quadratic programming (CQP)
[
34
]
. Naturally, it has wide range of applications such as portfolio optimization
[
44
]
and machine learning
[
47
]
.

Numerous algorithms have been developed to tackle (
LCP
), as evidenced by various studies
[
16
,
46
,
22
,
14
]
. For specific types of objective functions, such as linear and convex quadratic functions, well-established techniques like simplex methods
[
12
]
and interior point methods
[
55
]
have been comprehensively implemented in robust commercial solvers such as Gurobi
[
20
]
and Mosek
[
37
]
. In addition to these classical approaches, contemporary algorithms that leverage the dual augmented Lagrangian method
and semi-smooth Newton method
[
33
,
31
,
32
]
have been shown to achieve asymptotic super-linear convergence. These methods have proven to be more efficient than commercial solvers for certain large scale structured problems.
For specialized cases of convex polyhedra, including Birkhoff polytopes and transportation matrices, entropy-regularized methods
[
60
,
11
]
can offer the advantage of low per-iteration computational complexity to achieve a reasonable level of accuracy. While the aforementioned algorithms directly address (
LCP
) by exploiting the properties of convexity and linearity inherent to convex polyhedra, this paper focuses on
Hadamard parametrization for solving (
LCP
) by transforming it into a nonlinear and nonconvex problem even when the original problem is convex.
This approach will be further elaborated in the subsequent subsection.

1.2
Hadamard parametrization and its limitations

In (
LCP
), the challenge of non-smoothness arises primarily from the nonnegativity constraint
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}.
One effective strategy for circumventing this constraint is through Hadamard parametrization that involves transforming
x
into a squared variable
y\circ y,
which leads us to the following modified problem with only equality constraints:

\min\left\{f(y):=\phi(y\circ y):\ {A(y\circ y)}=b,\ y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}.

(LCPH)

This transformation intrinsically preserves the non-negativity constraint on
x
. We define the feasible set of (
LCPH
) as
\mathcal{P}_{A,b}:=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ {A(y\circ y)}=b\right\},
and referring to it as a parametrized polyhedron. Note that both (
LCPH
) and (
LCP
) share the same global optimal value. Although
\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
doesn’t involve inequality constraints, we must mention that it could still be non-smooth because it might contain singular points, whose tangent cone is not a linear space, i.e.,
\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
might not be a smooth manifold.

The utilization of squared variables in optimization can be traced back to the 1960s
[
39
]
, with recent applications in Lasso-type problems
[
24
,
25
]
. More recently, this idea was further explored by Ding and Wright in
[
15
]
for general optimization problems, whose inequality constraints
c(x)\leq 0
are converted into equality constraints of the form
c(x)+v\circ v=0
by
introducing squared slack variables. It is worth noting that when it comes to the
problem (
LCP
), their conversion simply add the constraint
x=v\circ v
to the problem, which is slightly different from our case where the variable
x
is directly replaced by
y\circ y
.
At the moment, it is unclear whether the two approaches could show
similar practical behaviour.

Despite its simplicity and intuitive appeal, the usage of squared variables
has been subjected to considerable criticism for its inherent non-convexity and stringent regularity requirements
[
2
,
17
]
. The former issue pertains to the case when
\phi
is convex: whence
the problem (
LCP
) is a convex optimization problem but the new problem (
LCPH
) is in general non-convex. Recent works on landscape analysis have observed that under the linearly independent constraint qualification (LICQ) assumption
[
40
]
, the smooth parametrizations of various optimization problems
[
30
,
8
,
10
]
have benign non-convexity. More generally, Levin et al., in their comprehensive study
[
29
]
, have identified conditions where a second-order stationary point in a smooth-parametrized problem also serves as a first-order stationary point of the original problem, which will be a global minimizer if the original problem is convex. Apart from the benign non-convexity, the LICQ assumption also implies that the feasible set of the parametrized problem is a smooth manifold, thus allowing us to use Riemannian optimization method
[
1
,
6
]
to solve it with guaranteed convergence.

While the non-convexity could be benign under the LICQ assumption, the regularity issue is perceived as more serious because of two issues. The first issue is about the optimality conditions of (
LCPH
). The optimality condition of (
LCP
), derived from Farka’s lemma
[
34
]
, does not require any regularity conditions. For a given
x\in\mathcal{C}_{A,b},
checking the first order optimality condition is equivalent to an LP feasibility problem, which can be done in polynomial time. In contrast, the derivation of the optimality conditions of (
LCPH
) is based on the implicit function theorem, which relies on the LICQ property. Note that the statement of the traditional KKT conditions for a given primal-dual solution
(x,\lambda)
of (
LCPH
)
[
40
, chapter 12]

though doesn’t require the LICQ property but it
requires the knowledge of the multiplier.
However, when it comes to landscape analysis
[
29
]
, we are only given the primal variable
y\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
. In this case, as far as we know, how to recover the Lagrangian multiplier
\lambda
to verify the optimality conditions of
y
hasn’t been studied yet.

The second issue is about algorithms to solve (
LCPH
). Because
\mathcal{C}_{A,b}
is a convex polyhedra, traditional algorithms like barrier method
[
40
, chapter 17]
and projected gradient method
[
3
]
can be applied to solve it without LICQ condition. However, when it comes to (
LCPH
), the commonly used algorithms like sequential quadratic programming
[
40
, chapter 18]
and Riemannian optimization method
[
30
,
57
]
rely on the LICQ property, which may not always hold for real-world problems.

In this paper, we aim to address the above two issues by conducting a comprehensive analysis of the (
LCPH
) problem. The details of our contributions will be elaborated
in the following subsections.

1.3
Optimality conditions

If the LICQ property holds at some
y\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b},
then
\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
is a smooth manifold in a neighbourhood of
y.
In this case, we can easily recover the Lagrangian multiplier
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
by computing the projection of
\nabla f(y)
onto the tangent space
{\rm T}_{y}\mathcal{P}_{A,b},
which is equivalent to solving a positive definite linear system. After obtaining
\lambda,
we can move on to verify the first order and second order KKT conditions, which is equivalent to that the Riemannian gradient is zero and the Riemannian Hessian is positive semidefinite
[
6
, chapter 4-6]
. The violation of each of the conditions will produce a direction to decrease the objective function value.

However, when the LICQ property doesn’t hold at
y\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b},
its tangent cone may not be a linear space and the above approach doesn’t work. In order to verify the optimality of a given singular point
y\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
, in Section
2
, we will derive explicit formulas for both the tangent cone and the second-order tangent set of
y.
With this information, we develop a systematic way to recover the Lagrangian multiplier and verify the first and second-order necessary conditions for (
LCPH
). One corollary of our result is that the “
2\Rightarrow 1
” criterion in
[
29
]
holds for (
LCP
) and (
LCPH
). Here, instead of using the traditional KKT conditions
[
40
, chapter 12]
, we choose the more geometric optimality conditions from
[
5
, chapter 3]
, which are properties of the primal variable
y\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
itself and the conditions are always necessary. Thus, it is more suitable for landscape analysis and can be used to design local search algorithms. However, when it comes to the traditional KKT conditions, they are in general not necessary without the LICQ property. In this case, we can say nothing about the optimality of the primal-dual pair
(y,\lambda),
even if they satisfy the traditional KKT conditions.

1.4
Riemannian optimization on algebraic variety

Riemannian optimization is a widely-used technique for solving smooth parametrized problems. However, directly applying the Riemannian optimization approach to (
LCPH
) poses challenges, as the feasible set
\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
is an algebraic variety that may contain singular points. Although manifold optimization is well-understood and supported by a variety of solvers
[
7
,
56
]
, optimization over algebraic varieties remains an emerging area of study
[
27
,
28
,
41
]
.

When extending Riemannian optimization method to algebraic variety, a pivotal insight is that every real algebraic variety can be decomposed into finite smooth submanifolds
[
54
]
. With such decomposition, we can apply Riemannian optimization method on one of the manifolds and switch to another one when the iterations approach the boundary. This idea has been used in the determinantal variety
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}_{\leq r}:=\left\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}:\ {\rm rank}(X)\leq r\right\}

[
18
,
26
]
and some special low-rank SDP problems, whose singular points are exactly rank-1 solutions
[
50
,
51
]
.

In Section
4
, we will adopt the above idea to solve (
LCPH
). In order to apply Riemannian optimization, we will first stratify
\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
according to the maximal linearly independent set of active constraints. Given the singularity of
\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
, these submanifolds may not be closed, thus potentially causing Riemannian optimization methods to fail at convergence when the iterates approach a submanifold
boundary – a phenomenon termed
apocalypses

[
28
]
. To
overcome this difficulty, we design a globally convergent hybrid method, which combines Riemannian optimization with the projected gradient method. The projected gradient method allows the iteration point to jump from one submanifold to another
while decreasing the function value. On the other hand,
the Riemannian gradient descent can efficiently decrease the function value on the selected submanifold by exploiting its underlying sparsity structure and smoothness. It is worth mentioning that recent works
[
43
,
42
]
show that applying projected gradient directly to (LCPH) don’t suffer from the
apocalypses
issue. However, it could be complicated to computed the metric projection onto the non-convex set
\mathcal{P}_{A,b}.
On the contrary, when using Riemannian optimization method, we can apply some iterative methods to compute the retraction for general nonlinear constraints as long as LICQ holds. We will present this in detail in Section
4
.

2
Optimality conditions

In this section, we will derive the optimality conditions of (
LCPH
) as well as its landscape analysis. Before we derive the optimality conditions, we first study the geometric properties of the algebraic variety
\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
in the following subsection.

2.1
Geometric properties of
\mathcal{P}_{A,b}

Definition 1
.

For any
y\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b},
let
[M;N]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
be a full rank matrix such that the rows of
M
is a basis of
{\rm Range}[A{D}_{y}]
and the rows of
N
is a basis of its orthogonal complement in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
(hence

MN^{\top}=0
).
It is easy to see that
MA{D}_{y}
has full row rank,
NA{D}_{y}=0
and
Nb=0.
Let
{\rm rank}(M)={\rm rank}(A{D}_{y})=r.
We call
(M,N,r)
a regular representation of
y
in
\mathcal{P}_{A,b}.
Note that by definition, for any
h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
A{D}_{y}h=M^{\top}z
for an unique
z\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
.

Note also that the regular representation for a given
y\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
is not unique because the basis vectors of a linear space can be non-unique. Also,
y\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
is a regular point if and only if
r=m,
in which case
N
is a null matrix. The regular representation of
y\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
is only related to
{\rm supp}(y)
because
{{\rm Range}[A{D}_{y}]={\rm Range}[A{D}_{z}]}
if
{\rm supp}(y)={\rm supp}(z).
The following two propositions provide the analytic formulas of the tangent cone and second-order tangent set of
\mathcal{P}_{A,b}.
We put their proofs in appendix.

Proposition 2
.

For any
y\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
with regular representation
(M,N,r),
the inner and contingent tangent cones of
y
are given as follows:

\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}_{A,b}}^{i}(y)=\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}_{A,b}}(y)=\widehat{\mathcal{T}}(y):=\left\{h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ MA\left(y\circ h\right)=0,\ NA(h\circ h)=0\right\}.

(5)

Proposition 3
.

Consider
y\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
with regular representation
(M,N,r)
and
h\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}_{A,b}}(x).

Let
[V;W]\in\mathbb{R}^{(m-r)\times(m-r)}
be a full rank matrix such that the rows of
V
is a basis for
{{\rm Range}[NA{D}_{h}]}
and
VW^{\top}=0.
Then the inner and outer second-order tangent sets of
(y,h)
are given as follows:

\mathcal{T}^{i,2}_{\mathcal{P}_{A,b}}(y,h)=\mathcal{T}^{2}_{\mathcal{P}_{A,b}}(y,h)=\widehat{\mathcal{T}^{2}}(y,h):=\big\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ MA\left(y\circ w+h\circ h\right)=0,\\
VNA(h\circ w)=0,\ WNA\left(w\circ w\right)=0\big\}.

(6)

Remark 1
.

Proposition
3
implies that
\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
is second-order directionally differentiable at any point
y\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
along any direction
h\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}_{A,b}}(y)

[
5
, definition 3.32]
. Note that the set
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}(y)
in Proposition
2

is independent of the representation
(M,N,r)
. That is,
if
(M^{\prime},N^{\prime},r)
is another representation of
y\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
, then
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}(y)=\widehat{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}(y):=\{h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:M^{\prime}A(y\circ h)=0,N^{\prime}A(h\circ h)=0\}.
A similar remark also holds
for the set
\widehat{\mathcal{T}^{2}}(y,h)
in Proposition
3
.

2.2
Optimality conditions of (
LCP
) and (
LCPH
)

With the formula of tangent cones and second-order tangent sets of
\mathcal{P}_{A,b},
we are now able to derive the optimality conditions. Since the feasible set of (
LCP
) is a convex polyhedral set, the linear constraint qualification (LCQ) holds and we don’t need other regularity assumptions. The first-order necessary conditions of (
LCP
) are as follows.

Definition 2
.

[first-order necessary conditions]

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a first-order stationary point of (
LCP
) if there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

(1)\ Ax=b,\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\ (2)\ \nabla\phi(x)-A^{\top}\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\ (3)\ \left(\nabla\phi(x)-A^{\top}\lambda\right)\circ x=0.

(7)

We refer to the above three conditions as primal feasibility, dual feasibility and complementarity, respectively.

We do not consider second-order necessary condition of (
LCP
) because verifying this property is in general NP-hard
[
38
]
. Also, for many applications of (
LCP
),
\phi
is a convex function so that a first-order stationary point is already a global minimizer.

Now, we move on to study the optimality conditions of (
LCPH
). As mentioned in Subsection
1.3
, instead of using the traditional KKT conditions
[
40
, chapter 12]
, we use the following more geometric optimality conditions
[
5
, chapter 3]
.

Definition 3
.

[first-order necessary conditions]

y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a first-order stationary point of (
LCPH
) if
y\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
and
\nabla f(y)^{\top}h\geq 0,
for any
h\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}_{A,b}}(y).

Definition 4
.

[second-order necessary conditions]

y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a second-order stationary point of (
LCPH
) if
y
is a first-order stationary point, and
\nabla f(y)^{\top}w+h^{\top}\nabla^{2}f(y)h\geq 0
for any
h\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}_{A,b}}(y)
satisfying
\nabla f(y)^{\top}h=0
and any
w\in\mathcal{T}^{2}_{\mathcal{P}_{A,b}}(y,h).

Although the above two definitions are purely geometric, from the formulas in Proposition
2
and
3
, we can obtain the algebraic characterization of stationary points. We state the results in the following two lemmas, which offer reformulations of the above two optimality conditions to make them easily verifiable.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose
y\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
has regular representation
(M,N,r),

x=y\circ y.
Then
y
is a first-order stationary point of (
LCPH
) if and only if there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
such that

\left(\nabla\phi(x)-A^{\top}M^{\top}\lambda\right)\circ y=0,

(8)

which is equivalent to
\nabla\phi(x)\circ y\in{\rm Range}[{D}_{y}A^{\top}],
i.e.,
{\rm Proj}_{{\rm Ker}[A{D}_{y}]}\left(\nabla\phi(x)\circ y\right)=0.

Proof.

Necessity.
Suppose
y\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
is a first-order stationary point. For any
h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
MA(y\circ h)=0,
we have that
MA(y\circ(h\circ\mathds{1}_{y}))=0.
Because
NA{D}_{y}=0,
we have that
NA\left((h\circ\mathds{1}_{y})\circ(h\circ\mathds{1}_{y})\right)=0.
Thus,
h\circ\mathds{1}_{y}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}_{A,b}}(y).
From Definition
3
, we have that
\left\langle\nabla\phi(x)\circ y,h\circ\mathds{1}_{y}\right\rangle\geq 0,
which implies
\left\langle\nabla\phi(x)\circ y,h\right\rangle\geq 0.
Since this inequality holds for any
h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
MA(y\circ h)=0,
we have that
\nabla\phi(x)\circ y\in{\rm Range}[{D}_{y}A^{\top}M^{\top}]={\rm Range}[{D}_{y}A^{\top}].
Thus, there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
such that (
8
) holds.

Sufficiency.
Suppose (
8
) holds. Then for any
h\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}_{A,b}}(y),
we have that

\left\langle\nabla\phi(x)\circ y,h\right\rangle=\left\langle\left(A^{\top}M^{\top}\lambda\right)\circ y,h\right\rangle=\left\langle\lambda,MA(y\circ h)\right\rangle=0,

where the last equality follows from the fact that
MA(y\circ h)=0

for
h\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}_{A,b}}(y)
(see Proposition
2
).

Lemma 2
.

Suppose
y\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
has regular representation

Since our manifold decomposition is abstract, we will use two examples to illustrate it.

Simplex.

Perhaps the simplest example is the simplex

Proposition 7
.

For any
Y\in\mathcal{BH}_{n},
it is smooth if and only if
\mathcal{BG}(Y)
is connected.

Proposition
7
tells us that determining the singularity of a given point
Y\in\mathcal{BH}_{n}
only requires checking the connectedness of its underlying sparsity pattern. Note that this can be done in
\mathcal{O}(n+|{\rm supp}(Y)|)
time by Depth-First Search or Breadth-First Search. This is much cheaper than applying the LDL decomposition to the matrix
A{\rm Diag}(x)A^{\top},
which has
the complexity of
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{3}\right).
The following proposition constructs the manifold decomposition of
\mathcal{BH}_{n}
explicitly. We also put its proof in the appendix.

Proposition 8
.

Consider
Y\in\mathcal{BH}_{n}
such that
\mathcal{BG}(Y)
has connected components
\sqcup_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{H}_{i}
for any
i\in[k]
such that the vertex set
\mathcal{V}\left(\mathcal{H}_{i}\right)=\left(\mathcal{I}_{i},\mathcal{J}_{i}\right)
and
[n]=\sqcup_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{I}_{i}=\sqcup_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{J}_{i}.
Then for any
Z\in\mathcal{BH}_{n},

Z\in\mathcal{M}(Y)
if and only if
\mathcal{BG}(Z)
has the same connected components as
\mathcal{BG}(Y).

For examples like the simplex and Birkhoff polytope, we are able to construct the manifold decomposition explicitly due to their special structures. However, this analysis is difficult to be extended to a general convex polyhedron. In the next section, we will introduce a globally convergent Riemannian optimization method for solving the general problem (
LCP
). Specifically, details like the regular representation
(M,N,r)
, the maximal support
\mathcal{I}
of
\mathcal{M}(y)
as in (
24
), and the total number of submanifolds are not explicitly required for our algorithm’s operations.

4
Algorithm

In this section, we will consider finding a first-order stationary point of (
LCP
) by solving (
LCPH
) as an auxiliary problem. Our strategy is to apply manifold decomposition to
\mathcal{P}_{A,b},
mentioned in the previous section and employ Riemannian optimization techniques on the submanifolds. Detailed algorithmic design and convergence analysis will be presented in the subsequent subsections. Throughout this section, we let
(M,N,r)
be a regular representation of
y
and
x=y\circ y.

4.1
Riemannian gradient descent (RGD)

Suppose we have some iterate
y\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b},
which is also on the submanifold
\mathcal{M}(y)
whose explicit formula might be unknown. When applying Riemannian gradient descent, we need to compute the Riemannian gradient and retraction. Here we introduce some basic material on Riemannian optimization. For more detailed information about Riemannian optimization, please refer to the textbooks
[
6
,
1
]
.

Tangent space.
Consider
y\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
with regular representation
(M,N,r),

x=y\circ y
and
\mathcal{I}:=\cup_{z\in\mathcal{M}(y)}{\rm supp}(z)
to be the maximal support. Because of the LICQ property, the tangent space of
\mathcal{M}(y)
at
y
is given as follows:

{\rm T}_{y}\mathcal{M}(y)=\left\{h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ MA(y\circ h)=0,\ {\rm supp}(h)\subset\mathcal{I}\right\}.

(29)

The collection of all the tangent spaces
\cup_{z\in\mathcal{M}(y)}\{(z,h)\,:\,h\in{\rm T}_{z}\mathcal{M}(y)\}
is known as the tangent bundle and is denoted as
{\rm T}\mathcal{M}(y).

With the manifold decomposition structure of
\mathcal{P}_{A,b},
we will discuss our Riemannian optimization approach in the following subsections.

Riemannian gradient.
Because
{\rm supp}\left(2\nabla\phi(x)\circ y\right)\subset{\rm supp}(y)\subset\mathcal{I}
and
{\rm Ker}[MA{D}_{y}]={\rm Ker}[A{D}_{y}],
we get the following formula:

\displaystyle{\rm grad}_{\mathcal{M}(y)}f(y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{\rm Proj}_{{\rm T}_{y}\mathcal{M}(y)}\left(2\nabla\phi(x)\circ y\right)={\rm Proj}_{{\rm Ker}[A{D}_{y}]}\left(2\nabla\phi(x)\circ y\right)

(30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left(\nabla\phi(x)\circ y-{D}_{y}A^{\top}\lambda\right),

where
\lambda
is any solution of the linear system (
18
) with one particular choice to be (
19
). From (
30
) and Lemma
1
, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 9
.

For any
y\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b},
it is a first-order stationary point if and only if
{\rm grad}f(y)=0,
which is further equivalent to the complementarity with the multiplier
\lambda
coming from the linear system (
18
).

Retraction.
Apart from the Riemannian gradient provided in (
30
), we also need a retraction mapping to ensure that the next iterate is also on the current manifold (see 3.6 of
[
6
]
). A retraction
{{\rm Retr}:}{{\rm T}\mathcal{M}(y)}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}(y)
is a smooth mapping such that for any
z\in\mathcal{M}(y)
and
h\in{\rm T}_{z}\mathcal{M}(y)
, it satisfies

•

{\rm Retr}(z,0)=z
,
{\rm D}{\rm Retr}(z,0)[h]=h
for any
h\in{\rm T}_{z}\mathcal{M}(y).

Notably, a retraction mapping is not unique. For specific manifolds such as the unit sphere and Stiefel manifold
[
6
, chapter 7]
, there exist retraction mappings with analytical formulas. For a general manifold defined from equality constraints with LICQ property, we can use the Newton retraction proposed by Zhang in
[
61
]
to compute the retraction iteratively. In detail, the Newton retraction for
{\rm Retr}(y,-t\cdot{\rm grad}f(y))
is the Gauss-Newton method applied to solve the nonlinear system
MA(z\circ z)=Mb
with the initial point set to be

y-t\,{\rm grad}f(y).
Suppose
z_{k}
is the
k
th iteration of the Gauss-Newton method, then

z_{k+1}
is updated as follows:

2MA{\rm Diag}(z_{k}\circ z_{k})A^{\top}M^{\top}\lambda_{k}=Mb-MA(z_{k}\circ z_{k}),\ z_{k+1}=z_{k}+{\rm Diag}(z_{k})A^{\top}M^{\top}\lambda_{k}.

(31)

If
{\rm supp}(z_{k})\subset{\rm supp}(y),
we also have that
{\rm supp}(z_{k+1})\subset{\rm supp}(y).
From (
30
), we have that
{\rm supp}\left(y+t\cdot{\rm grad}f(y)\right)\subset{\rm supp}(y).
Therefore, the Gauss-Newton method preserves the zero entries of
y.

Because
{\rm supp}(z_{k})\subset{\rm supp}(y),
we have that
NA{\rm Diag}(z_{k})=0
and
Nb=0.
Similar to the (
18
), by using the LDL decomposition,
A{\rm Diag}(z_{k}\circ z_{k})A^{\top}=L_{k}{\rm Diag}(d_{k})L_{k}^{\top},
the Newton retraction iteration can be equivalently written as

2L_{k}{\rm Diag}(d_{k})L_{k}\lambda_{k}=b-A(z_{k}\circ z_{k}),\ z_{k+1}=z_{k}+{\rm Diag}(z_{k})A^{\top}\lambda_{k},

(32)

where one particular choice of
\lambda_{k}
is
L_{k}^{-\top}{\rm Diag}(d_{k})^{+}L_{k}^{-1}\left(b-A(z_{k}\circ z_{k})\right)/2.

Remark 5
.

Equations (
30
) and (
32
) indicate that we can calculate the projection and retraction mappings without the knowledge of the maximal support
\mathcal{I}
or the regular representation
(M,N,r)
. This representation is concealed within the LDL decomposition of
A{\rm Diag}(z_{k}\circ z_{k})A^{\top}
. Importantly, when the stepsize is sufficiently small, the preservation of nonzero entries ensures that the new point remains on the current submanifold.

It is crucial to note that Newton retraction is conceptually the limit point of the Gauss-Newton method. Attaining this limit point exactly in practice is generally not feasible due to the presence of a nonzero residual. However, given Gauss-Newton method’s local quadratic convergence and the proximity of points on the tangent space to the manifold before retraction, the residual of the Newton retraction can typically reach the machine precision in just a few iterations. This means that we can practically consider the iterate as the limit point. In our tests, even setting the residual tolerance of the retraction to values like
10^{-8}
, which is far from the machine precision, did not pose any numerical issues for our algorithm.

While we acknowledge there is a slight discrepancy between the theory developed and practical implementation due to our use of the (inexact) Newton retraction, we believe it might be overly cautious to limit ourselves to straightforward manifolds such as spheres or orthogonal matrices with explicit retractions. Exploring the convergence behavior of Riemannian optimization methods with inexact retraction mappings is a promising avenue for future research.

With the Riemannian gradient and retraction mapping, we state and prove the following proposition on the sufficient decrease of function value.

Proposition 10
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
be a given constant.
For any
y\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
such that
{\rm grad}f(y)\neq 0,
there exists
\beta_{y,1},\ c_{y,1}>0
such that for any
z\in\mathcal{M}(y)\cap B_{\beta_{y,1}}(y)
,

{\rm RGD:}\ f\left({\rm Retr}\left(z,-t{\rm grad}f(z)\right)\right)\leq f(z)-t\cdot\delta\|{\rm grad}f(z)\|^{2},\ \forall t\in[0,c_{y,1}].

(33)

Remark 6
.

In Proposition
10
,
c_{y,1}
might be unknown. In practice, we can use the following Armijo linesearch with backtracking rate of
0.5

[
40
, section 3]
to adaptively find a step size:

\min\left\{k\in\mathbb{N}:\ f\left({\rm Retr}\left(y,-(1/2)^{k}{\rm grad}f(y)\right)\right)\leq f(y)-(1/2)^{k}\cdot\delta\|{\rm grad}f(y)\|^{2}\right\}.

(34)

Proposition
10
ensures that the stepsize
(1/2)^{k}\geq\min\{1,c_{y,1}/2\}.

Proposition
9
and Proposition
10
show that we can use RGD to decrease the function value if the complementarity is violated. However, the first-order stationarity of (
LCPH
) is not enough to ensure
x=y\circ y
to be a first-order stationary point of (
LCP
) because we have to further ensure the dual feasibility. A simple example is that for a convex function
\phi
, a first-order stationary point of (
LCP
) is also a global minimizer, but a first-order stationary point of (
LCPH
) can be a global maximizer.
Besides, a sufficient decrease is not assured when
\|{\rm grad}f(y)\|
is large because
\beta_{y,1}
and
c_{y,1}
may not have a uniform lower bound.
The aforementioned inconsistency is caused by the possible unboundedness of
f(y)
and
\mathcal{M}(y)
’s curvature, with the latter issue being non-negligible due to the potential openness of the submanifold
\mathcal{M}(y)
, as also seen in fixed-rank manifolds
[
6

. In the next subsection, we will introduce the projected gradient method to tackle these issues.

4.2
Projected gradient descent (PGD)

As mentioned in the previous subsection, RGD may suffer from small stepsize issue because of the singularity of
\mathcal{P}_{A,b}.
One simple way to overcome this difficulty is to combine RGD with the projected gradient method
[
3
]
, which updates the iteration as follows:

x_{k+1}:={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{C}_{A,b}}(x_{k}-t_{k}\nabla\phi(x_{k})).

(35)

This amounts to solving the following convex projection problem:

\min\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|x-\left(x_{k}-t_{k}\nabla\phi(x_{k})\right)\|^{2}\;:\ Ax=b,\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\right\},

(36)

whose dual problem is given by

\min_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\Big\{\frac{1}{2}\|{\rm Proj}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}\big(x_{k}-t_{k}\nabla\phi(x_{k})+A^{\top}\lambda\big)\|^{2}-\left\langle\lambda,b\right\rangle\Big\}.

(37)

The latter can be solved by a (regularized) semi-smooth Newton method (see algorithm SSNCG1 of
[
32
]
). We need the following proposition, which originates from
[
3
]
.

Proposition 11
.

(Lemma 2.1 and Lemma 2.2 of
[
3
]
)
For any
t>0
and
x\in\mathcal{C}_{A,b},
define
g_{t}(x):={\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{C}_{A,b}}\left(x-t\nabla\phi(x)\right)-x
and
h_{t}(x)=\|g_{t}(x)\|/t.
We have that (1)
\left\langle\nabla\phi(x),g_{t}(x)\right\rangle\leq-\|g_{t}(x)\|^{2}/t
; (2)
g_{t}(x)=0
if and only if
x
is a first-order stationary point of (
LCP
); (3)
h_{t}(x)
is monotonically nonincreasing with respect to
t.

With Proposition
11
, we have the following result on the sufficient decrease property of the projected gradient method.

Proposition 12
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
be a given constant. For any
x=y\circ y\in\mathcal{C}_{A,b}
that is not a stationary point of (
LCP
), there exists
\beta_{y,2},c_{y,2}>0
such that for any
u\in\mathcal{C}_{A,b}\cap B_{\beta_{y,2}}(x)
,

{\rm PGD:}\ \phi\left({\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{C}_{A,b}}\left(u-t\nabla\phi(u)\right)\right)\leq\phi(u)-t\cdot\delta h_{t}(u)^{2}\quad\forall\;t\in(0,c_{y,2}].

(38)

Remark 7
.

In Proposition
12
,
c_{y,2}
is unknown in practice. Similar to the RGD method, we can perform the following Armijo linesearch to the PGD method:

\min\left\{k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}:\ \phi\left({\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{C}_{A,b}}\left(x-(1/2)^{k}\nabla\phi(x)\right)\right)\leq\phi(x)-(1/2)^{k}\cdot\delta h_{(1/2)^{k}}(x)^{2}\right\}.

(39)

Proposition
12
ensures that the step size
(1/2)^{k}\geq\min\{1,c_{y,2}/2\}.

Although PGD has a nice sufficient decrease property, solving problem (
37
) might be expensive especially when (
LCP
) is primal degenerate. In the next subsection, we will design a hybrid method that uses the RGD whenever the iterate is not close to a singular
point of
\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
and employs the PGD otherwise. As the set of singular points of
\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
is “sparse”
in
\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
, we expect that the more expensive PGD step will be used infrequently.

4.3
A hybrid method with convergence guarantee

In this subsection, we will design our algorithm, which is a combination of the RGD and PGD methods. The main idea is to use the former to decrease the function value when the iteration
is on the smooth part of
\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
and switch to the projected gradient method when it becomes “nearly singular”. We define the near-singularity as follows.

Definition 7
.

Consider
y\in\mathcal{M}(y)
with regular representation
(M,N,r)
and
x=y\circ y.
Let
L{\rm Diag}(d)L^{\top}
be the LDL decomposition of
A{\rm Diag}(x)A^{\top}.
Suppose

d_{i_{1}}\geq d_{i_{2}}\geq\cdots\geq d_{i_{r}}>0=d_{i_{r+1}}=\cdots=d_{i_{n}}.
For any

is
\sigma-

d_{i_{r}}<\sigma.
Otherwise, we say that

is
\sigma-
regular, and define
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}(y):=\left\{z\in\mathcal{M}(y):\ z{\rm\ is\ }\mbox{$\sigma-$regular}\right\}.

From Lemma
3
, we know that the vector
d
is unique in the LDL decomposition of
A{\rm Diag}(x)A^{\top}.
Thus, the above definition is only a property about
y
and it does not depend on the manifold decomposition of
\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
.
Now with Definition
7
, we can state our algorithm as follows.

Algorithm 1:

Choose
y_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{A,b},
and
\delta,\epsilon,\sigma\in(0,1).
Set
x_{0}=y_{0}\circ y_{0}
and
k=0.

Step 1.

(a) If
y_{k}
is
\sigma-
regular and
\|{\rm grad}f(y_{k})\|>\epsilon
, then
y^{+}\leftarrow{\rm Retr}_{y_{k}}\left(y_{k},-t_{k}{\rm grad}f(y_{k})\right)
satisfies (

coming from the linesearch procedure (

x^{+}\leftarrow y^{+}\circ y^{+}.
Goto Step 2.

(b) If

is
\sigma-

\|{\rm grad}f(y_{k})\|\leq\epsilon

x^{+}\leftarrow{\rm Proj}_{\mathcal{C}_{A,b}}(x_{k}-t_{k}\nabla\phi(x_{k}))

coming from
the linesearch procedure (

Set

In Algorithm 1, we apply RGD when the current iteration is
\sigma-
regular and the Riemannian gradient norm is larger than some threshold

These two conditions are important for the good behaviour of RGD. If any one of the two conditions is not satisfied, we use the PGD method.
In practice, we choose

and

to be

to avoid using the PGD method
too frequently. Another point to note is that the RGD step keeps the iteration on the current submanifold. However, the PGD step may switch the iteration point to another submanifold.
Thus the PGD step allows us to adaptively identify a submanifold of
\mathcal{P}_{A,b}
while guaranteeing the decrease in the function value. Because Gauss-Newton method only has local convergence guarantee, we will first randomly generate a point in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and project it onto the convex polyhedron
\mathcal{C}_{A,b}
to get
x_{0}.
Such step can be computed by the semi-smooth Newton method or the code
PPROJ

to get the initial point

In Algorithm 1, we intentionally use a simple algorithmic framework to facilitate our convergence analysis. In order to achieve good practical performance, it is important to use some numerical techniques to accelerate our algorithm. For example, when choosing the stepsize, compared with the naive Armijo linesearch mentioned in Remark
6
and Remark
7
, it would be better to use the Barzilai-Borwein stepsize together with a non-monotone linesearch
[
3
]
.

Also, in order to terminate our algorithm, we will check the KKT conditions of the current iteration and terminate our algorithm when the following KKT residuals are smaller than some tolerance:

\displaystyle{\rm Rp}:=\max\left\{\frac{\|Ax-b\|}{1+\|b\|},\frac{\|{\rm Proj}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{-}}(x)\|}{1+\|x\|}\right\},\ {\rm Rd}:=\frac{\|{\rm Proj}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{-}}\left(\nabla\phi(x)-A^{\top}\lambda\right)\|}{1+\|\nabla\phi(x)\|},

\displaystyle{\rm Rc}:=\frac{|\left\langle\nabla\phi(x)-A^{\top}\lambda,x\right\rangle|}{1+\|\nabla\phi(x)\|}.

(40)

To check the above KKT conditions, we have to compute the Lagrangian multiplier
\lambda.
One choice is the method discussed in Subsection
2.3
. However, through our numerical experiments, we find that it is more convenient to use the multiplier computed from the projected gradient step as the Lagrangian multiplier. In our implementation, we
compute the multiplier to check the KKT residuals based on the following two cases:

Case 1.

If the current iteration is smooth, the unique Lagrangian multiplier
\lambda
will be obtained from the linear system of the projection mapping (
17
).

Case 2.

If the current iteration is singular, the Lagrangian multiplier
\lambda
will be obtained from the optimal solution of the projected gradient subproblem (
37
).

The above Case 2 is essentially using projected gradient method to recover the dual variable. Since projected gradient method is not used in every iteration, we will periodically switch to projected gradient, say once every 20 iterations for degenerate problems, to check KKT conditions so that our algorithm can find a solution of required accuracy in time.

Remark 8
.

In this section, we have stated our hybrid method together with its convergence analysis. Actually, from our convergence analysis, our algorithm still converges if we use the PGD step in Step 1(a) of Algorithm 1. Our tests have shown that the RGD step always preserves the support of the iterate, that is, some entries will approach zero without reaching it exactly. On the other hand, the PGD step can reduce these values to exactly zeros, thereby modifying the iterate’s support. To enhance the vector’s sparsity early on, we switch to the PGD step occasionally, perhaps every ten steps even in Step 1(a). This technique is particularly useful for problems where most of the entries at the optimal solution are zeros.

One might naturally question whether our algorithm offers any benefit over purely using
projected gradient method. Although it’s not yet proven theoretically, our numerical tests indicate that the Gauss-Newton method employed in the retraction mapping
converges faster than the semi-smooth Newton method
for solving (
37
), especially for degenerate problems. One potential reason is that the point on the tangent space before retraction is usually very close to the manifold especially when the algorithm is about to converge and the stepsize becomes small. In this case, the initial point is inside the quadratically convergent region and the residual is already very small. So after one or two iterations, the Gauss-Newton method’s residual will reach machine precision. On the contrary, for the projected gradient method, the semismooth Newton method employed to solve the subproblem (
37
) generally does not enjoy the quadratic convergence property in the early iterations, and worse still, there is no quadratic convergence when the subproblem
is degenerate.

Despite the advantages of RGD, we do not claim that Algorithm 1 will consistently outperforms the projected gradient method.
Our goal is to present it as a viable alternative, or as an acceleration, to the well established projected gradient method. Ideally, our algorithm should exhibit superior performance in some interesting applications. In the following section, we will conduct numerical experiments
to compare the performance our approach with the projected gradient method.

Numerical experiments

In this section, we conduct numerical experiments to verify the effectiveness of Algorithm 1. As mentioned in the previous section, our algorithm is closely related to the projected gradient method so we will compare Algorithm 1 with it.
Instead of implementing the projected gradient method ourselves, we compare with the well-developed solver

When solving the linear systems in the projection and retraction steps, we will first use the preconditioned conjugate gradient (PCG) method with diagonal preconditioing and only switch to using the LDL decomposition of the coefficient matrix as the preconditioner when the PGG method cannot reach the tolerance of

within say, 20 iterations. We will continue to use the old LDL decomposition as the preconditioner for solving the subsequent linear systems until the PCG method
triggers another need to recompute the LDL decomposition.
This strategy has been explored in our previous feasible method for solving general low-rank SDP problems

. All the experiments are run using Matlab R2021b on a Workstation with a Intel(R) Xeon(R) CPU E5-2680 v3 @ 2.50GHz Processor and 128GB RAM.

Convex quadratic programming

Consider the following standard form convex quadratic programming problem (CQP)

\min\left\{x^{\top}Qx/2+c^{\top}x:\ Ax=b,\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\right\},

(43)

where
Q\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+},

A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},

and

[

[\kappa,1]

-Qx^{(1)}

. The matrix

and primal feasible point

have entries drawn randomly from the uniform distribution in
[0,1].
The vector
b
is set as
Ax^{(2)}.
The
Matlab

rng(’default’); l=log(kappa); u=0; egv = exp(unifrnd(l,u,n,1)); S=randn(n,n); S = S+S’; [P,S] = eig(S); Q = (P.*egv’)*P’; x1 = randn(n,1); c = -Q*xs; A = rand(m,n); x2 = rand(n,1); b=A*x2;

5.2
Gromov-Wasserstein learning

In this paper, we are particularly interested in computing the following Gromov-Wasserstein distance (GWD)

{\rm GW}\left(A,B,\mu,\nu\right):=\min\Big\{\frac{\mu^{\top}(A\circ A)\mu+\nu^{\top}(B\circ B)\nu}{2}-\left\langle X,AXB\right\rangle:\ X\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)\Big\},

(44)

where
A\in\mathbb{S}^{m},

B\in\mathbb{S}^{m}
are symmetric distance matrices,
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+},

\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
are given probability vectors and the set
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is defined as follows:

\Pi(\mu,\nu):=\left\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}_{+}\,:\ X{\bf 1}_{n}=\mu,\ X^{\top}{\bf 1}_{m}=\nu\right\}.

(45)

Problem (
44
) was first proposed by Mémoli in
[
36
]
and has wide range of applications including graph partition and alignment
[
59
]
, molecule analysis
[
52
]
and generative modeling
[
9
]
.

In our experiments, we consider the Gromov-Wasserstein distance in graph matching, where matrices
A
and
B
are adjacent matrices of some simple undirected graphs. We generate the graphs that imitate K-NN (K-nearest neighbors) graphs with certain randomness following the procedure mentioned in subsection 5.1 of
[
59
]
. For completeness, we restate the data generation procedure. We generate the source graph
G(\mathcal{V}_{s},\mathcal{E}_{s})
and target graph
G(\mathcal{V}_{t},\mathcal{E}_{t})
as follows. For every
v\in\mathcal{V}_{s},
we randomly select
K\sim{\rm Poisson}\left(0.1\times|\mathcal{V}_{s}|\right)
neighbors from
\mathcal{V}_{s}\setminus v,
each of which has
w\sim{\rm Poisson}(10)
interactions to
v.
The target graph
G\left(\mathcal{V}_{t},\mathcal{E}_{t}\right)
is constructed by adding
q\%
noise nodes and generating
q\%
noisy edges via the simulation method. We choose
\mu={\bf 1}_{m}/m

and
\nu={\bf 1}_{n}/n.

Apart from (GWD), our algorithm can also compute the following more complicated Gromov-Wasserstein berycenter (GWBC) problem:

\min\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}{\rm GW}\left(C,C_{i},\mu,\nu_{i}\right):\ C\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}\right\},

(46)

where
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}=1,

C_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n_{i}}
,

i\in[k]
are given distance matrices,

\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
,

\nu_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}_{+}
,
i\in[k]
, are given probability vectors.
Problem (
46
) was proposed by Peyré et al. in
[
45
]
, where it was applied in shape interpolation and quantum chemistry. In problem (
44
), the variable
C
is the barycenter of the data matrices
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}.
Notably, computing
{\rm GW}\left(C,C_{i},\mu,\nu_{i}\right)
involves another optimization problem with variables
X_{i}\in\Pi\left(\mu,\nu_{i}\right)
for
i\in[k]
. After combining the outer and inner problems, we get the following non-convex optimization problem over a convex polyhedron whose objective function is a cubic polynomial:

\min\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\left(\frac{\mu^{\top}(C\circ C)\mu+\nu_{i}^{\top}(C_{i}\circ C_{i})\nu_{i}}{2}-\left\langle X_{i},CX_{i}C_{i}\right\rangle\right)\,:\,\begin{array}[]{l}C\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},\\
X_{i}\in\Pi\left(\mu,\nu_{i}\right)\;\forall\;i\in[k]\end{array}\right\}.

(47)

Problem (
44
) is very challenging to solve and currently the state-of-the-art approach is
based on the block-coordinate minimization method, with its subproblems solved by a Sinkhorn-type method
[
58
]
. Observe that when
X_{i}
’s are fixed, the variable
C
has the closed form solution

C=\frac{1}{\mu\mu^{\top}}\circ\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}X_{i}C_{i}X_{i}^{\top},
where

\frac{1}{\mu\mu^{\top}}
denotes the elementwise reciprocal of
\mu\mu^{\top}
.
Thus we can substitute this formula into (
47
) to get the following simplified problem
after some manipulations:

\min\Bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{i}\nu_{i}^{\top}(C_{i}\circ C_{i})\nu_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\mu}\right)^{\top}\left(\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}X_{i}C_{i}X_{i}^{\top}\Big)\circ\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}X_{i}C_{i}X_{i}^{\top}\Big)\right)\frac{1}{\mu}:\\
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+},\;X_{i}\in\Pi\left(\mu,\nu_{i}\right),\ i\in[k]\Bigg\}.

(48)

In the above,
\frac{1}{\mu}
denotes the elementwise reciprocal of
\mu
.
Problem (
48
) is an optimization problem over a convex polyhedral set, so we can directly use our Riemannian based optimization method to find a stationary point of it.
Note that for (
48
), the total dimension of the variables
is
m+m\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}
, which can be quite large even just for
m=n_{i}=1000
for all
i\in[k]
.

One application of Gromov-Wasserstein barycenter is multi-graph matching problem, where we match a source graph to many target graphs simultaneously. This problem has been tested in
[
58
]
using protein-protein interaction (PPI) network

.
of yeast with its noisy version
[

6
Conclusion

In this work, we study the method of Hadamard parametrization for optimization problems
over a convex polyhedron. In order to study the optimality conditions without the
LICQ condition, we derive explicit formulas of the tangent cone and second-order tangent set at a given point in the parametrized polyhedron, which is an algebraic variety. In order to extend Riemannian optimization method to solve the new problem on an algebraic variety, we propose a manifold decomposition method that decomposes the parametrized polyhedron into a finite disjoint union of Riemannian manifolds. We also design a globally convergent hybrid algorithm that adaptively chooses a submanifold and applies Riemannian gradient descent on it. Our hybrid algorithm uses a projected gradient method to jump from one submanifold to another when the Riemannian gradient descent method stalls or when a nearly singular iterate is encountered. Numerical experiments are conducted to demonstrate the efficacy
of our algorithm as compared to other state-of-the-art algorithms.
Our algorithm is a first-order method that only uses the function value and gradient information of the objective function. In the future, we will study how to incorporate second-order information in our algorithmic framework. Also, currently, we have not established any convergence rate for our algorithm due to the non-smooth nature of the algebraic variety. It would be interesting to study the convergence rate of optimization algorithms on an algebraic variety.
