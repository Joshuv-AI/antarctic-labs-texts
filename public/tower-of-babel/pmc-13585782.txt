The Potential of Complementary and Alternative Therapies in Improving Diarrhea-Predominant Irritable Bowel Syndrome: Strategies for Long-Term Efficacy
Authors: Yuqing Deng, Xinyi Ao, Xin Liang, Weian Hao, Yali Liu, Long Zhao, Zhi Li, Shanshan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diarrhea-predominant irritable bowel syndrome (IBS-D) is a common disorder of gut–brain interaction. Current therapeutic approaches frequently fail to provide sustained symptom relief. In recent years, complementary and alternative therapies—particularly traditional Chinese medicine, acupuncture, and dietary interventions—have attracted increasing attention due to their low side-effect profiles and are gradually becoming important adjunct strategies in the management of IBS-D. This review systematically summarizes the research advances of the past decade across these three therapeutic categories for IBS-D with a focus on their clinical efficacy and underlying mechanisms. The findings suggest that traditional Chinese medicine, acupuncture, and dietary interventions can provide sustained relief from core symptoms of IBS-D, including diarrhea and abdominal pain. Their therapeutic effects are mediated through multiple mechanisms, including modulation of the gut microbiota and its metabolites, restoration of intestinal mucosal barrier integrity, attenuation of visceral hypersensitivity, and regulation of the gut–brain axis. These therapies exhibit multi-target and systemic effects with a low incidence of adverse events, highlighting their promising clinical potential. They offer feasible strategies for achieving long-term remission and facilitating personalized management of IBS-D.

Introduction

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a disorder of gut–brain interaction characterized primarily by chronic and recurrent abdominal pain associated with altered stool form or bowel habits [12]. In recent years, its prevalence has been rising globally [3]. Epidemiological studies indicate that the global prevalence of IBS ranges from 4.1 to 11.1% [45], while in China, it ranges from 1.4 to 11.5% [6]. To standardize diagnosis and classification, the Rome IV diagnostic criteria categorize IBS into four subtypes: diarrhea-predominant IBS (IBS-D), constipation-predominant IBS (IBS-C), mixed-type IBS (IBS-M), and unsubtyped IBS (IBS-U). Among these, IBS-D is the most common subtype [78], and its prevention and treatment have become an important public health concern worldwide.

IBS-D is characterized by a prolonged course, recurrent episodes, and resistance to cure [9], underpinned by a complex and multifactorial pathophysiology. Recent studies have shown that dysfunction of the gut–brain axis, bile acid metabolism disorders, visceral hypersensitivity, intestinal barrier impairment, and gut microbiota dysbiosis play pivotal roles in the onset and progression of IBS-D [10]. These mechanisms are not only interrelated but also form multiple positive feedback loops among neural regulation, gut microbiota, and the mucosal barrier, which mutually reinforce one another and ultimately contribute to the chronicity and recurrence of IBS-D symptoms [11].

At present, the treatment of IBS-D is increasingly shifting toward achieving long-term symptom relief and improving quality of life. Commonly used pharmacological therapies—including but not limited to opioid receptor agonists, non-absorbable antibiotics, serotonin 5-HT receptor antagonists, bile acid sequestrants, and antidiarrheal agents [12]—often fall short of expected therapeutic goals. Complementary and alternative medicine (CAM), particularly traditional Chinese medicine, acupuncture, and dietary interventions, has attracted growing attention for its natural and mild properties, fewer side effects, multi-target regulation, and emphasis on holistic health and individualized care. These approaches not only enhance patients’ self-management but also synergistically alleviate IBS-D symptoms on multiple levels, demonstrating unique therapeutic advantages [13]. As a result, CAM is increasingly recognized as a valuable complement to conventional treatments, with rising interest in its potential for long-term symptom management and chronic disease control in IBS-D.

According to the U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH), complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) refers to medical practices outside conventional Western medicine, including therapies used alongside or in place of standard treatments. Common CAM approaches include herbal medicine, acupuncture, yoga, meditation, dietary interventions, and mindfulness [14]. Compared to conventional symptom-targeted treatments, CAM emphasizes holistic regulation and chronic disease management, making it particularly suitable for IBS, a recurrent condition influenced by psychological and lifestyle factors [1516].

Notably, The Expert Consensus on Diagnosis and Treatment of Irritable Bowel Syndrome with Traditional Chinese Medicine(2024) [17] issued by the Spleen and Stomach Diseases Branch of the Chinese Association of Traditional Chinese Medicine, further clarifies the TCM approach to managing IBS-D. It classifies IBS-D under the category of “xie xie” (diarrhea) and attributes its fundamental pathogenesis to liver qi stagnation, spleen–stomach disharmony, and dysfunction in intestinal transmission and transformation. The main syndromic patterns include liver depression with spleen deficiency, spleen deficiency with excessive dampness, and damp-heat in the large intestine. The consensus highlights “the insufficient rate of long-term symptom remission” as a major challenge in current IBS treatment and recommends three CAM modalities—Chinese herbal medicine, acupuncture, and dietary intervention—as part of the therapeutic strategy. Guided by this consensus, the present review focuses specifically on these three core interventions, excluding other CAM approaches such as meditation, yoga, or massage, to emphasize treatment pathways that are both widely practiced and supported by relatively strong clinical evidence.

Research Method

The literature search was conducted using a combination of English keywords, including “Irritable Bowel Syndrome with Diarrhea” OR “IBS-D” OR “Diarrhea-predominant Irritable Bowel Syndrome,” traditional Chinese medicine formulas such as “Tongxie Yaofang,” “Shenling Baizhu San,” “Fuzi Lizhong Wan,” “Sishen Wan,” and “Gegen Qinlian Decoction,” as well as interventions like “Acupuncture,” “Electroacupuncture,” “Moxibustion,” “TEA,” and “Transcutaneous Electrical Acupuncture.” Additionally, dietary terms including “Dietary therapy,” “Dietary intervention,” “Low FODMAP diet,” “gluten-free diet,” “Mediterranean diet,” and “nutritional therapy” were included. Chinese Herb

Tongxie Yaofang

The Expert Consensus on Diagnosis and Treatment of Irritable Bowel Syndrome with Traditional Chinese Medicine (2024) classifies IBS-D under the traditional category of “Xiexie” (diarrhea) and identifies four primary syndrome patterns: liver depression and spleen deficiency, spleen deficiency with excessive dampness, spleen-kidney yang deficiency, and damp-heat in the large intestine. Corresponding core prescriptions recommended for each pattern include Tongxie Yaofang(TXYF), Shenling Baizhu San, Fuzi Lizhong Wan combined with Sishen Wan, and Gegen Qinlian Decoction, respectively [17]. This review conducted a comprehensive search of clinical studies in databases including PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase, and found that among traditional Chinese medicine formulas, TXYF currently has the most substantial clinical evidence support. Therefore, this paper focuses on TXYF as a starting point to explore its potential value in the long-term management of IBS-D, aiming to provide a theoretical foundation and practical reference for mechanistic research and individualized treatment of IBS-D with Chinese herbal formulas.

In recent years, numerous clinical studies have systematically evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of TXYF, demonstrating its effectiveness not only in alleviating core symptoms such as diarrhea and abdominal pain but also in maintaining long-term symptom relief. Mechanistic studies further reveal that TXYF exerts multiple pharmacological effects, including modulation of gut microbiota composition and bile acid metabolism, inhibition of abnormal neural activation, restoration of the intestinal mucosal barrier, and regulation of local inflammatory responses. Through these multi-target and multi-pathway interventions, TXYF holds promise for sustained symptom relief and long-term management of IBS-D, making it one of the most representative strategies among complementary and alternative therapies.

Mechanistic Insights from Basic Research

TXYF can reshape the gut microbiota composition and indirectly regulate bile acid dysmetabolism. Studies have shown that TXYF significantly increases the abundance of Bacteroides species expressing bile salt hydrolase (BSH), thereby promoting the deconjugation of primary bile acids. This, in turn, upregulates Fgf15 expression in the small intestine, which exerts negative feedback to suppress hepatic bile acid synthesis, reduces activation of TGR5 receptors and the excitability of CGRP-expressing neurons, ultimately alleviating diarrhea and neural hypersensitivity [18]. In addition, TXYF can modulate the abundance of Akkermansia and Clostridium sensu stricto 1, significantly reducing colonic 5-HT levels and thereby alleviating visceral hypersensitivity. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) experiments further confirmed that microbiota remodeling is a fundamental mechanism underlying the therapeutic effects of traditional Chinese medicine in IBS-D intervention [19].

In terms of regulating abnormal neural activation, animal studies have shown that TXYF can downregulate the expression of NGF/TrkA and its downstream pathway TRPV1, thereby inhibiting aberrant neural activation and exerting analgesic effects. Additionally, TXYF significantly suppresses neurotransmitters such as 5-HT and SP, reduces excessive downstream neuronal activation, and inhibits the expression of glial activation markers such as S100β and GFAP, indirectly mitigating the over-amplification of synaptic signaling. Furthermore, TXYF has been shown to restore disordered synaptic arrangement and dense vesicle distribution in the intestinal tract of IBS rats, suggesting its potential role in ultrastructural repair [20–22].

IBS-D is often associated with decreased expression of tight junction proteins such as ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1, leading to epithelial barrier dysfunction. Treatment with TXYF has been shown to significantly upregulate the expression of these proteins, thereby enhancing the integrity of the intestinal mucosal barrier [23–25]. Moreover, since barrier disruption can trigger or exacerbate local inflammatory responses, TXYF also improves the inflammatory microenvironment and maintains barrier stability by inhibiting the NF-κB and Notch pathways, thereby downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-1β [23].

In summary, TXYF exerts multi-targeted and synergistic effects on key pathological processes of IBS-D, including bile acid dysregulation, neurotransmitter imbalance, mucosal barrier damage, and gut microbiota dysbiosis. This multidimensional intervention holds promise for the long-term alleviation of IBS-D symptoms. Encouragingly, an increasing body of clinical evidence in recent years has demonstrated the potential of TXYF in relieving core symptoms such as diarrhea and abdominal pain, improving patients’ quality of life, and maintaining sustained therapeutic efficacy, thereby providing solid evidence to support its role as a complementary treatment for IBS-D Fig. 2).Fig. 2Mechanisms of Chinese herbs in IBS-D treatment: Chinese herb alleviates IBS-D symptoms via multiple pathways, including modulation of gut microbiota and bile acid metabolism, suppression of neural hyperactivation, repair of mucosal barrier integrity, and regulation of intestinal inflammation. Clinical Trials of TXYF for IBS-D

A clinical study conducted by Professor Shunyong He’s team enrolled 60 patients with IBS-D who received individualized TXYF treatment, compared against the commonly used drug pinaverium bromide. After 3 weeks of treatment, the total efficacy rate of TXYF reached 92.5%. Additionally, TXYF significantly improved the intestinal microecological environment, including increased abundances of Bifidobacteria and Escherichia coli and an improved B/E ratio, suggesting that its therapeutic effects may be mediated through modulation of gut microbiota homeostasis [26]. Building upon the preliminary evidence of TXYF’s clinical efficacy, subsequent research has shifted focus toward its long-term effectiveness. A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase II clinical trial led by Professor Shibing Liang’s team extended both the intervention and follow-up periods. IBS-D patients with an IBS-SSS score ≥ 75 received TXYF granules for 8 weeks, followed by a 12-week observation period. The results demonstrated that TXYF not only significantly alleviated core symptoms such as abdominal pain—with a total efficacy rate of 79.2%—but also showed a progressively improving trend during follow-up, with no apparent decline in effect, suggesting a sustained therapeutic benefit over time [27]. Building on these findings, Professor Yaoliang Lai’s team further introduced the concept of a “post-treatment therapeutic effect (PTTE)” period to systematically evaluate the long-term efficacy of TXYF. In their study, patients underwent a 4-week intervention followed by an extended 57-week follow-up. The results showed that the therapeutic benefits of TXYF persisted for 17 to 25 weeks post-treatment, after which symptoms stabilized, indicating a sustained and plateauing effect [28].

In summary, research on TXYF has gradually expanded from short-term efficacy evaluations to medium- and long-term follow-up studies, providing preliminary evidence of its beneficial effects in prolonging remission and reducing recurrence in IBS-D patients. Mechanistic studies and clinical data complement each other, systematically demonstrating TXYF’s multi-target therapeutic advantages and offering a solid theoretical and evidence-based foundation for its application in individualized treatment and long-term management of IBS-D.

Peppermint Oil

Peppermint oil is a volatile oil extracted from the fresh stems and leaves of the peppermint plant through steam distillation, followed by freezing and partial dementholization [29]. In recent years, peppermint oil has attracted increasing attention as a natural intestinal antispasmodic and has been applied in the treatment of diarrhea-predominant irritable bowel syndrome (IBS-D), particularly demonstrating The American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) recommends its use as an antispasmodic in the AGA Clinical Practice Guideline on the Pharmacological Management of Irritable Bowel Syndrome With Diarrhea [30]. Although The Expert Consensus on Diagnosis and Treatment of Irritable Bowel Syndrome with Traditional Chinese Medicine (2024) has not yet included peppermint oil as a recommended intervention, considering its recognition in international guidelines and its widespread clinical application, this review incorporates peppermint oil to provide a more comprehensive overview of current research progress in complementary and alternative therapies for IBS-D.

Findings from Clinical Trials

A multicenter, open-label, phase III single-arm study conducted by Japanese researchers provided strong evidence for the efficacy of peppermint oil [31]. The study enrolled 69 patients who met the Rome III criteria for IBS and administered a 4-week treatment using ZO-Y60, a novel peppermint oil delivery system. The results showed that both patient global assessment (PtGA) and physician global assessment (PGA) improvement rates exceeded 70% at weeks 2 and 4, reaching 85.1% by week 4, suggesting that peppermint oil demonstrates favorable overall efficacy in East Asian populations. Another randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial further validated the efficacy of a novel small intestinal–targeted peppermint oil (PO) formulation in patients with IBS-D [31]. Seventy-two patients meeting Rome III criteria received a 4-week treatment, and results indicated that the peppermint oil group showed significantly greater improvement in overall IBS symptom scores and gastrointestinal discomfort relief compared to the placebo group. Additionally, adverse events were mild, supporting the formulation’s good safety profile and rapid onset of action [32].

With the progression of research, some trials have presented differing perspectives on the efficacy of peppermint oil. A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study conducted at an academic center in the United States enrolled patients with moderate to severe IBS for a 6-week treatment period. Although both the peppermint oil group and the placebo group showed improvements in IBS-SSS scores, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups. Additionally, all secondary outcome measures showed no intergroup differences, indicating that the advantages of peppermint oil were not demonstrated in this population and study context [33].

Moreover, some studies have explored optimizing the efficacy of peppermint oil by targeting its site of release. A multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial evaluated the effects of small intestine–release and colon–release peppermint oil formulations in patients with IBS, enrolling 190 individuals who met the Rome IV criteria. After 8 weeks of treatment, there was no significant difference in overall symptom relief between either formulation and the placebo group. Only the small intestine–release group showed a mild advantage in reducing abdominal pain, discomfort, and severity scores. Although adverse events were generally mild, the incidence of side effects in both peppermint oil groups was slightly higher than in the placebo group, suggesting that their safety should still be carefully evaluated [34].

Current research on the use of peppermint oil for treating IBS, particularly IBS-D, yields inconsistent results. Some studies—especially those conducted in Asian populations or utilizing specific sustained release formulations—have reported favorable clinical outcomes, whereas high-quality trials have failed to demonstrate significant differences compared to placebo [3132]. This variability in efficacy may be attributed to factors such as study design, site of formulation release, population characteristics, and IBS subtypes. Future research should focus on large-scale, multicenter, and standardized clinical trials to clarify the true efficacy and appropriate indications of peppermint oil in IBS-D management, thereby facilitating its standardized application in functional gastrointestinal disorders [3334].

Acupuncture

As a commonly used non-pharmacological treatment for IBS-D, acupuncture has been widely applied in clinical practice both in China and abroad due to its high safety, minimal side effects, and holistic regulatory effects. Numerous studies have demonstrated that acupuncture can effectively alleviate core symptoms such as abdominal pain and diarrhea, improve bowel satisfaction, and enhance quality of life, making it one of the representative interventions within the CAM framework.

Basic Research on Acupuncture Mechanisms

The abnormal activation of sensory neurons in the enteric nervous system (ENS) is considered a central mechanism in the development of visceral hypersensitivity [35]. Multiple animal studies have shown that in the IBS model rats, nociceptive ion channel receptors such as P2X3 and P2X4 are significantly upregulated in the dorsal root ganglia (DRG), enteric plexus, and spinal dorsal horn. These changes are accompanied by decreased resting membrane potentials and increased firing frequencies of neurons, indicating a hyperexcitable neuronal state. Electroacupuncture stimulation at acupoints such as ST25 and ST37 can downregulate the expression of these receptors, significantly inhibit neuronal excitability, and alleviate visceral pain responses [3637]. In addition, electroacupuncture can regulate the synthesis and release of neurotransmitters such as tryptophan hydroxylase (TPH), neuropeptide Y (NPY), and somatostatin (SS), thereby restoring the balance of neurotransmission between the central and peripheral components of the brain–gut axis and serving as a bridge from peripheral analgesia to central modulation [38]. Notably, electroacupuncture also modulates synaptic function between DRG and spinal neurons, intervening in the neural sensitization associated with IBS [39].

At the level of central nervous system regulation, acupuncture has been shown to remodel both functional and structural brain networks. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies have demonstrated that acupuncture significantly alters the connectivity patterns of several brain networks in patients with IBS-D, including the auditory network, ventral attention network, and the default mode network (DMN)–dorsal attention network (DAN). These changes are closely associated with reductions in IBS-SSS and anxiety scores, suggesting that acupuncture may alleviate symptoms by modulating central pathways of the gut–brain axis [4041]. Structural studies on white matter have also revealed that acupuncture promotes neuroplastic remodeling in regions such as the right thalamic radiation and cingulate cortex, and these structural changes are correlated with symptom relief and improved quality of life, providing neuroimaging evidence for acupuncture-induced brain remodeling [42]. These findings are further supported by animal studies. In IBS model rats, the hypothalamic expression of corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and its receptors CRHR1/2 is significantly upregulated, indicating a hyperactive hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and heightened sensitivity to stress. Electroacupuncture can significantly reduce CRH system activity, modulate HPA axis function, and attenuate visceral hypersensitivity [43–45]. Moreover, electroacupuncture has been found to suppress the expression of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) in astrocytes within the medial thalamus (MT) and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), thereby interrupting the sustained activation of central pain pathways [46].

In terms of mucosal barrier repair, electroacupuncture significantly improves intestinal permeability by inhibiting the MCs/Tryptase/PAR-2/MLCK signaling axis [24]. Barrier disruption can trigger or exacerbate local inflammatory responses. Electroacupuncture not only upregulates the expression of tight junction proteins and enhances epithelial barrier integrity, but also suppresses the expression of inflammatory markers such as IL-6 and LPS [2547]. Moreover, it modulates the signaling of S-nitrosoglutathione (GSNO) derived from enteric glial cells (EGCs), enhances the structural stability of EGCs, upregulates NLRP6, and promotes the synthesis of MUC2 and E-cadherin, thereby further strengthening intestinal barrier function [48].

In terms of gut microbiota modulation, studies on animal models have shown that in the IBS-D state, there is a reduced relative abundance of Lactobacillus and Clostridium cluster XIVa, along with an enrichment of potentially pathogenic bacteria such as Bacteroides, Prevotella, and Clostridium cluster XI. Moxibustion treatment can reverse this dysbiosis, enhance microbial alpha diversity, and regulate the composition of bacterial genera, thereby alleviating visceral hypersensitivity and diarrhea symptoms [49].

With the ongoing advancement of mechanistic research, acupuncture has been increasingly recognized for its multifaceted effects in regulating gut–brain axis function, alleviating neural sensitization, improving mucosal barrier integrity, and modulating the gut microbiota. Clinical Efficacy of Acupuncture in IBS-D

Professor Jun Zhao’s team conducted a multicenter, randomized, sham-controlled study involving 170 patients with refractory IBS. The results showed that the acupuncture group experienced a mean reduction of 140 points in IBS Symptom Severity Score (IBS-SSS) over 4 weeks, significantly greater than the 64.4-point reduction in the sham group, with sustained effects observed during follow-up [50]. Similarly, Lixia Pei’s team carried out a large-scale multicenter randomized controlled trial with 519 participants, comparing acupuncture to PEG4000/pinaverium bromide. The acupuncture group showed a significantly greater reduction in IBS-SSS scores (between-group difference of 28.78, p < 0.001), with therapeutic effects lasting over 12 weeks, supporting its short- and medium-term efficacy [51]. In contrast, Ling-Yu Qi’s study did not find statistically significant differences between acupuncture and sham groups; however, trend-level improvements in overall response rates suggest that clinical outcomes may be influenced by factors such as sample size and stimulation protocol [52].

Moreover, both moxibustion and transcutaneous electrical acustimulation (TEA) have demonstrated promising therapeutic potential. Two randomized controlled trials conducted by Zhaoqin Wang’s team confirmed that mild moxibustion significantly outperformed sham moxibustion in alleviating IBS-D symptoms, with a response rate reaching 81.58%, and sustained efficacy observed up to 12 weeks [53]. In addition, Pingping Hu’s team showed that TEA effectively improved abdominal pain and quality of life, with a significantly lower dropout rate in the intervention group, suggesting good patient compliance and stable treatment outcomes [54].

Current clinical research is gradually shifting from a focus on short-term symptom relief to long-term evaluations of sustained efficacy, relapse prevention, and quality of life improvement. Acupuncture has demonstrated promising results in these areas, and the consistency between its mechanisms of action and clinical outcomes underscores its potential in the long-term management of IBS-D, further reinforcing its value as a key complementary therapy.

Overall, as a major component of complementary and alternative medicine (CAM), acupuncture has evolved from being a symptomatic short-term intervention to a viable strategy for long-term management of IBS-D. With continued advances in mechanistic studies and accumulating clinical evidence, acupuncture is showing increasing potential to offer IBS-D patients a more systematic and durable treatment approach.

Dietary Intervention

More than 80% of patients with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) report food-related symptoms [11]. FODMAP-containing foods are poorly absorbed in the small intestine and rapidly fermented by colonic bacteria, leading to gas production, bloating, abdominal pain, and altered bowel habits [55]. As a result, dietary intervention has become a key component in the management of diarrhea-predominant IBS (IBS-D). In line with this, the low FODMAP diet (LFD) has been incorporated into several international IBS guidelines and expert consensus statements as an important evidence-based dietary intervention. For example, the American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) recommends a limited trial of a low FODMAP diet to improve global IBS symptoms, while the American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) states that the low FODMAP diet is currently the most evidence-based dietary intervention for IBS. Similarly, the British Society of Gastroenterology (BSG) recommends the low FODMAP diet as a structured dietary strategy, generally delivered under the guidance of trained dietitians, for patients with persistent IBS symptoms [115657]. Among these strategies, the low FODMAP diet (LFD) has gained widespread acceptance in clinical practice due to robust and reliable evidence [58]. Numerous clinical studies have demonstrated that LFD can effectively improve symptoms in 50–80% of IBS patients and significantly enhance their quality of life [59].

Experimental Evidence of Dietary Interventions

Dietary intervention plays a central role in the treatment of IBS-D Fig. 4), with one of its key mechanisms being the modulation of gut microbiota. The intestinal microbiota and its metabolites are critically involved in the pathogenesis of IBS-D. Studies have shown that a high-FODMAP (HFM) diet significantly increases the abundance of Gram-negative bacteria and fecal lipopolysaccharide (LPS) levels, leading to disruption of the intestinal barrier, local inflammation, and visceral hypersensitivity (VH)—effects that can be reversed by antibiotics, a low-FODMAP diet (LFD), or LPS receptor antagonists (LPS-RS) [60]. In terms of microbial metabolites, multiple studies have demonstrated that LFD reduces levels of total short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), acetate, and butyrate, indicating suppressed carbohydrate fermentation. Meanwhile, levels of isobutyrate and isovalerate—metabolites associated with protein fermentation—are relatively increased. Beyond modulating gut microbiota, a low-FODMAP diet (LFD) may also exert therapeutic effects by improving intestinal barrier function. Studies have shown that LFD intervention significantly reduces serum zonulin levels, indirectly suggesting its potential to restore tight junction integrity [47]. Moreover, LFD can regulate the composition of volatile organic compounds (VOCs), thereby suppressing inflammation-associated metabolic pathways, which may contribute to maintaining immune homeostasis and alleviating mucosal inflammation [64]. In terms of bile acid and tryptophan metabolism, LFD has been shown to reduce circulating bile acid levels while increasing the production of indole-3-propionic acid (IPA) and other microbial-derived metabolites. Although the correlation between these metabolic changes and clinical symptom improvement remains limited, these findings indicate that LFD possesses potential for metabolic regulation [65].

The aforementioned mechanistic studies provide a solid theoretical foundation for the application of LFD in IBS-D, while accumulating clinical evidence continues to support its therapeutic efficacy and potential for long-term management.

Clinical Outcomes of Dietary Interventions in IBS-D

Some studies have compared the low FODMAP diet (LFD) with commonly used first-line pharmacological treatments to further evaluate its effectiveness in relieving IBS symptoms in real-world settings. A randomized clinical trial compared the efficacy of LFD and the antispasmodic drug otilonium bromide (OB) in IBS patients. The results showed that LFD had a higher effective rate in improving IBS symptoms after 8 weeks of intervention (71 vs. 61%, p = 0.03), particularly among patients who met the Rome IV criteria (77 vs. 62%, p = 0.004), with the therapeutic benefit maintained during a 24-week follow-up period [66]. In addition, the CARIB trial conducted in Sweden [65] demonstrated that dietary interventions—including LFD and low-carbohydrate diets—had significantly better overall efficacy compared to pharmacological treatment, further supporting the value of dietary therapy in IBS management [67].

However, there are still certain limitations to the clinical implementation of the low FODMAP diet (LFD), including poor long-term tolerance, low dietary adherence, increased food costs, and a lack of responsiveness in certain patient populations. Moreover, strict dietary restrictions may lead to inadequate intake of micronutrients [68]. In light of these concerns, some studies have proposed a simplified version of the LFD, which initially restricts only the major triggering FODMAP components—such as fructans and galacto-oligosaccharides—then gradually expands restrictions based on the patient’s symptom response [69]. A double-blind, randomized controlled trial compared the efficacy of a 4-week simplified FODMAP restriction phase (eliminating only fructans and galacto-oligosaccharides) with the full LFD. The results showed no statistically significant difference in the primary endpoint—response rate defined as a ≥ 30% reduction in abdominal pain intensity (62.5 vs. 56.3%, p = 0.71). Improvements in IBS symptom severity score (reductions of 50 and 100 points) and other secondary endpoints were also comparable between groups. In addition, there were no significant differences in energy, nutrient, dietary fiber, or gluten intake, suggesting that the simplified LFD offers similar therapeutic efficacy while potentially improving feasibility and adherence [58].

It is worth noting that some patients experience symptom recurrence after discontinuing the low FODMAP diet (LFD) and reintroducing high-FODMAP foods. Studies have shown that specific FODMAP components—such as fructans, galacto-oligosaccharides, and mannitol—are common triggers. The intake of these foods can lead to rapid symptom exacerbation and negatively impact patients’ quality of life [7071]. This also underscores the importance of personalized dietary management. It is recommended that, during long-term interventions, FODMAP-containing foods be gradually and cautiously reintroduced, with symptom monitoring used to assess individual tolerance. This approach helps minimize the risk of symptom relapse while avoiding gut microbiota imbalance and nutritional deficiencies that may result from overly restrictive diets.

These findings collectively support dietary modification as a symptom-targeted short-term intervention strategy; however, its long-term application faces challenges such as nutritional risks and poor adherence. This has prompted research to explore more universally applicable strategies. For instance, a randomized non-inferiority trial showed that a starch- and sucrose-reduced diet (SSRD) was comparable to a low-FODMAP diet (LFD) in alleviating IBS symptoms (4-week response rates: 79.2 vs. 78.2%), and also demonstrated additional benefits in weight control (p = 0.006) and sugar craving reduction (p = 0.05) [72]. The exploration of the Mediterranean diet offers another promising approach: it significantly improved gastrointestinal symptoms (83 vs. 37%, p < 0.001) and depression (52 vs. 20%, p = 0.015), without the need for strict FODMAP restriction, highlighting its superior clinical feasibility [73].

These explorations suggest that future dietary interventions should not only ensure therapeutic efficacy but also take into account metabolic regulation, psychological well-being, and the patient’s practical ability to adhere. The goal is to gradually develop a more precise and sustainable nutritional management model.

Gut Microbiota as Predictive Markers of Dietary Response

Moreover, the above studies not only provide microbiota-based evidence supporting the biological mechanisms of dietary interventions but also highlight the potential of gut microbiota as predictive biomarkers. Baseline microbial composition and functional characteristics have been shown to predict treatment responses in patients with IBS-D. One study identified two microbiota subtypes in IBS patients: IBS-P (pathogenic type) and IBS-H (healthy type). The IBS-P group exhibited increased Firmicutes and decreased Bacteroidetes, with enrichment of genes involved in carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism. Following intervention, their microbiota composition shifted toward the healthy type, resulting in better clinical outcomes [74]. In addition, the variability in response to the low FODMAP diet (LFD) among IBS patients can be predicted by microbial markers. According to 16S rRNA V3–V4 region sequencing, responders had a baseline enrichment of Prevotella 9 and Veillonella, whereas non-responders showed increased abundance of carbohydrate-fermenting genera such as Barnesiella, Paraprevotella, Bifidobacterium, and Ruminococcus 1. After LFD intervention, Butyricicoccus and Veillonella were further enriched in responders, suggesting that these genera may play functional roles in mediating dietary efficacy. Notably, a linear discriminant analysis (LDA) model constructed using five key genera was able to distinguish responders from non-responders with high accuracy (96.87%), underscoring the potential of fecal microbiota as a predictive biomarker for LFD response [75]. Another study conducted a systematic analysis using both data-driven and hypothesis-driven approaches, integrating datasets from previously published studies to evaluate microbiota and metabolic pathway changes in IBS patients before and after LFD intervention. The results demonstrated that treatment response could be predicted based on pre-intervention fecal microbiota composition. Methanogenesis and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) metabolic pathways were significantly enriched in responders. Specific genera, such as Ruminococcus, Ruminococcaceae UCG-002, and Anaerostipes, were identified as consistent predictors of LFD efficacy [63].

In summary, the application of the low FODMAP diet in the treatment of IBS-D has progressively evolved from mechanistic exploration to clinical validation. By modulating gut microbiota composition, metabolic profiles, intestinal barrier function, and inflammatory responses, the diet establishes a multidimensional intervention pathway. It not only provides short-term relief of hallmark symptoms but also shows promise in prolonging remission and reducing the risk of relapse. Although long-term adherence remains challenged by compliance and nutritional balance concerns, the development of simplified protocols and individualized management strategies continues to broaden their applicability and sustainability. Discussion

Despite the growing diversity of treatment strategies for diarrhea-predominant irritable bowel syndrome (IBS-D) in recent years—including pharmacological agents, dietary interventions, psychological therapies, and microbiota modulation—achieving long-term remission and controlling relapse remain major challenges [87]. Currently, “maintenance of remission and reduction of relapse” is considered a core goal in IBS management. However, most clinical trials to date have limited follow-up durations of only 8 to 12 weeks and primarily focus on short-term symptom relief, lacking systematic evaluations of treatment efficacy ≥ 6 months post-intervention.

Against this backdrop, complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) therapies—characterized by multi-target and holistic regulatory effects—have demonstrated promising efficacy in alleviating diarrhea, abdominal pain, and improving quality of life [88]. A growing body of basic and clinical evidence supports the role of CAM in reducing visceral hypersensitivity, restoring intestinal mucosal barrier integrity, and modulating gut microbiota. However, it should be noted that current clinical evidence regarding certain CAM interventions, particularly Tongxie Yaofang and acupuncture, has been mainly derived from studies conducted in Chinese or East Asian populations, while evidence from other countries and continents remains limited. This geographic and ethnic concentration may restrict the generalizability of these findings to broader IBS-D populations. Also, In addition to Tongxie Yaofang, several other Traditional Chinese Medicine products have also been investigated in IBS and IBS-D. For example, JCM-16021 has been evaluated in IBS-D through clinical and mechanistic studies [89], and Xiang-Sha-Liu-Jun-Zi Tang has also been assessed in patients with IBS in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial [90]. Moreover, other CAM therapies, such as Iberogast and Kampo, have been reported in some international guidelines and clinical studies for IBS management [9192]. However, these interventions were not included as recommended therapies in the Expert Consensus on Diagnosis and Treatment of Irritable Bowel Syndrome with Traditional Chinese Medicine (2024). Therefore, they were not discussed as major therapeutic categories in this review, which primarily focused on consensus-based TCM-related interventions for IBS-D.

Nevertheless, current CAM-related studies are largely confined to short-term clinical observations, and there is a paucity of large-scale, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with long-term follow-up that use “sustained remission” as a primary outcome. Additionally, limitations in study design, including small sample sizes, simplistic outcome measures, and insufficient mechanistic exploration, have hindered the establishment of robust evidence for CAM in long-term IBS-D management. Therefore, future research should focus on the critical scientific question of whether CAM interventions can effectively prolong remission and delay relapse. This requires the development of standardized clinical trial frameworks, integrated with investigations of brain–gut axis modulation and biomarker identification, to provide a solid theoretical and evidence-based foundation for the sustained application of CAM in IBS-D management.

Meanwhile, the clinical management of IBS-D continues to lack universally recognized and reproducible diagnostic biomarkers, posing a major bottleneck to the advancement of individualized therapy [10]. Nonetheless, emerging research has preliminarily identified a range of metabolic and microbiota-associated markers closely linked to IBS-D. For instance, zonulin, small intestinal permeability (s-IP), levels of n-3 PUFAs, and the n-6/n-3 or AA/EPA ratio reflect the status of gut barrier integrity and inflammation. Urinary metabolites such as L-serine and 5-hydroxy-L-tryptophan indicate alterations in neurochemical and energy metabolism pathways. Fecal metabolites including propionate and cyclohexanecarboxylate esters have shown potential in predicting therapeutic responses. Furthermore, compounds such as indole-3-propionic acid (IPA), phenolic metabolites, bile acids, and various lipid-related metabolites are considered key indicators of microbiota–metabolite axis modulation following dietary interventions [63647593]. Collectively, these findings lay a theoretical foundation for developing molecular subtyping and treatment response prediction models in IBS-D. Future efforts should prioritize multi-omics integration and the establishment of standardized, reproducible biomarker detection platforms to facilitate their translation into clinical practice.

With ongoing research advancements, the treatment paradigm for diarrhea-predominant irritable bowel syndrome (IBS-D) is gradually shifting from standardized protocols toward individualized therapeutic approaches. Studies have indicated that not all IBS-D patients respond equally to low FODMAP diets; those who benefit the most often belong to specific subgroups characterized by distinct gut microbial sensitivities [74]. Further investigations have revealed marked interindividual differences among IBS-D patients in terms of microbial diversity, the abundance of key bacterial genera, and metabolic activity. This heterogeneity in the gut microenvironment not only influences clinical manifestations but also significantly affects treatment outcomes [75]. As a result, the traditional symptom-based therapeutic model exhibits inherent limitations in addressing the substantial individual variability observed in IBS. There is an urgent need to adopt multidimensional, data-driven precision strategies that tailor interventions based on microbial, metabolic, and clinical profiles.

The concept of personalized treatment emphasizes incorporating multiple factors—such as patients’ symptom profiles, gut microbiota characteristics, dietary sensitivities, and psychological comorbidities—into intervention decision-making, to achieve more precise symptom control and long-term remission. However, this process requires the integration of multidimensional data and involves complex implementation pathways, which present challenges in terms of feasibility and scalability in clinical practice. Against this backdrop, the introduction of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies offers a promising breakthrough for precision management of IBS, facilitating data-driven decision-making and personalized care strategies.

Current studies have explored the preliminary applications of artificial intelligence (AI) in the management of IBS-D, such as intelligent estimation of FODMAP content in foods and disease recognition based on fecal imaging, demonstrating the feasibility of AI in assisting diagnosis and recommending personalized intervention strategies [9495]. However, most of these efforts remain at the stages of algorithm development and proof-of-concept, without large-scale clinical implementation. Future research should focus on the deep integration of AI with multi-omics data. By leveraging multimodal modeling technologies to identify potential molecular subtypes and key biomarkers of IBS, AI has the potential not only to improve diagnostic precision but also to provide robust data-driven support and theoretical foundations for personalized treatment and precision management of IBS-D.

Conclusion

Diarrhea-predominant irritable bowel syndrome (IBS-D) is a chronic, relapsing functional gastrointestinal disorder, and its long-term management remains a major clinical challenge. Current pharmacological treatments offer only limited efficacy in relieving symptoms and improving quality of life, often hampered by poor patient compliance and adverse effects. Therefore, there is an urgent need for safer and more effective therapeutic strategies.

According to the latest Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) consensus, complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) shows certain advantages in IBS-D management. This review aims to explore the evidence-based foundation underlying CAM interventions, systematically summarizing recent advances in the clinical application of Chinese herbal medicine, acupuncture, and dietary interventions as outlined in the consensus.

Findings indicate that these CAM therapies not only demonstrate clear efficacy in alleviating key symptoms such as diarrhea and abdominal pain, but also show promising potential in prolonging symptom remission and improving overall patient quality of life. At the mechanistic level, these three approaches exert synergistic effects by targeting key pathological processes of IBS-D, including modulation of gut microbiota and their metabolites, restoration of the intestinal mucosal barrier, attenuation of visceral hypersensitivity, and rebalancing of gut–brain axis function. These mechanisms align well with the multifactorial and dynamic pathophysiology of IBS-D.

Notably, as both basic and clinical research on CAM continues to deepen, emerging research hotspots include the evaluation of long-term efficacy, the development of individualized treatment strategies, and the identification of predictive biomarkers. Future studies should prioritize large-scale, multicenter, and standardized clinical trials to validate the therapeutic consistency and mechanistic reliability of CAM interventions and to facilitate their integration into standardized IBS-D treatment frameworks. Ultimately, CAM may serve as an indispensable component of personalized therapy and long-term disease management for IBS-D.
