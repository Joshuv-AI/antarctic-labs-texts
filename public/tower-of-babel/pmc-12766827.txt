Understanding safety measure practices and improvement strategies among health professionals in resource-limited health care settings in the Awi Zone, Amhara, Ethiopia: a qualitative study
Authors: Agumas Fentahun Ayalew, Amare Mebrat Delie, Lovel Fornah, Mulugeta Shegaze Shimbre, Wei Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe aim of this study was to explore current safety practices and improvement strategies for safety measures.DesignA qualitative descriptive method was utilised from January to February 2025, employing both convenience and purposive sampling techniques. A total of 51 direct observations, 8 focus group discussions and 19 key informant interviews were conducted as part of the data collection.SettingThis study was conducted in health institutions, Woreda health offices and Zonal health offices within the Awi Zone of Ethiopia.ParticipantsComprising frontline health professionals, case team leaders, facility administrators and health officials at both the woreda and zonal levels.ResultsThe data analysis identified two principal themes: ‘safety measures practice’ and ‘improvement strategies’. Safety practices varied from low to moderate. Five areas were found to have ways to improve safety measures: infrastructure and supply chain management, training and capacity-building, community engagement, policy strengthening and enforcement and human resources management with behavioural change interventions.ConclusionThe implementation of safety measures was inconsistent. To enhance safety measures, addressing individual behaviours and issues that are structural, cultural and systemic is essential. Encouraging participation, fostering teamwork and implementing regular monitoring can help make safety practices a routine part of the organisation. This would make the workplace safer, improve healthcare and enhance the overall effectiveness of the healthcare system. This study provides valuable insights that can inform the development of effective implementation strategies in other resource-limited settings.

Introduction

Healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) are major patient safety concerns that disproportionately impact resource-limited environments worldwide.1 With death rates up to three to five times higher in resource-limited settings owing to systematic gaps in infection prevention and control (IPC), HAIs affect 7–15% of hospitalised patients in high-income nations worldwide compared with 10–25% in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Driven mainly by surgical site infections (SSIs), bloodstream infections and catheter-associated urinary tract infections (UTIs), the HAI frequency varies from 12.4% to 25.6% in sub-Saharan Africa.2 In Ethiopia, this trend is also evident. Studies have shown a 16.96% prevalence of HAIs, with SSIs being the most common, accounting for 39.66% of all cases.3 Another study reported that 23.3% of intensive care unit patients developed healthcare-associated UTIs.4

In many LMICs, adherence to IPC guidelines remains low. In Africa, for example, hand hygiene compliance averages approximately 30.4%, mainly due to inadequate training, limited awareness and a shortage of hand hygiene supplies. In contrast, high-income countries report compliance rates between 40% and 60%, whereas in resource-limited settings, compliance rates range from only 8% to 30%.1 Research conducted in Ethiopia has shown that hand hygiene compliance among healthcare workers varies between 14.9% and 38%.5 6 Similarly, the use of personal protective equipment (PPE) is suboptimal, with only approximately 32% of healthcare professionals demonstrating high levels of compliance.7 Work overload, lack of resources and inadequate training are three obstacles to appropriate hand hygiene and PPE usage.7 8 Interventions to improve hand hygiene and PPE utilisation should focus on addressing resource constraints, providing regular training and promoting positive attitudes towards infection prevention practices.7 8

Most previous studies on this topic concentrated exclusively on quantitative data from government health institutions; specific areas of Ethiopia might hide significant issues related to IPC, gaps, compliance patterns and suggestions for development strategies. This paper addresses this gap by examining the particular context of private and government healthcare institutions in underserved environments with limited resources in the Awi Zone, Amhara regional state, Ethiopia. This research aims to determine the compliance rate regarding safety measure implementation and provide context-specific improvement solutions for poor compliance or problems, using the views of front-line healthcare workers through qualitative methodologies. In line with Ethiopia’s health sector reform plan and the WHO’s worldwide IPC policy,1 the results provide practical insights to improve IPC practices in resource-constrained healthcare settings.

Methods and materials

Study settings, design and period

The study involved 17 different health institutions in the Awi Zone, which included five private clinics, four public health centres, three general hospitals (two public and one private), four Woreda health offices and one zonal health office. These facilities represent the diverse structure of the Ethiopian three-tier health system, which is organised into primary, secondary and tertiary levels: primary-level healthcare, which includes health posts and health centres serving rural and urban populations; secondary-level healthcare, which is provided by district-level or Woreda-level general hospitals; and tertiary care, which is provided by referral and specialised hospitals. Woreda health offices oversee the administration and coordination of health services at the district level, whereas the zonal health office coordinates activities across the zone.9

Therefore, the study was conducted at primary-level health centres, secondary-level hospitals and private clinics, which include diverse health professionals (HPs) who provide healthcare services. Additionally, we include woreda- and zonal-level health offices, which are responsible for managing and coordinating both government and private health facilities within the current Ethiopian health system, as key informants. This research location was chosen because of its limited resources and earlier evaluations, which revealed that gaps in occupational health and safety procedures among HPs in the study area were assessed in an unpublished study and are currently under review, as were gaps in the quality of work life of HPs.10 Thus, the Awi Zone is essential for exploring improvement strategies for safety and safeguards. Injibara, the capital of the Awi Zone in Amhara, lies 445 kilometers northwest of Addis Ababa, Ethiopia’s capital. This qualitative descriptive research was conducted from January to April 2025. The present investigation utilised a qualitative descriptive study design alongside an inductive thematic analysis. It is an emergent approach where themes arise directly from the data and do not entail the imposition of prior theory or ideas. This operational mode is particularly suitable for exploratory qualitative research because it facilitates patterns and meaning to emerge from what the participants say and eschews following established theory.

Study participants’ eligibility criteria and sampling techniques

The target population for this study included all HPs providing direct and indirect patient care in private and public health institutions, as well as Woreda and Zonal health office officers in the Awi Zone, Ethiopia. Hence, this study included all frontline healthcare providers and case team leaders in private and public health institutions in the study area. Health institution managers and woreda/zonal health authorities were also studied. The source population comprised HPs employed in the selected health institution during the study period. The study population consisted of those who met the inclusion criteria and consented to participate in the study. Inclusion requires 2 years of work experience and a willingness to participate in research, regardless of department. If HPs were on annual, sick or maternity leave during data collection or had not worked in health institutions for other reasons, they were excluded. We also excluded non-permanent healthcare workers: our hybrid sampling strategy combined convenience and purposive sampling. First, we used convenience sampling to choose accessible and cooperative health facilities. Second, within the selected facilities, criterion-based purposive sampling was employed to select departments (professional roles) and recruit HPs who met the inclusion criteria, including at least 2 years of work experience and willingness to participate. This approach ensured that the study was both practical and realistic while also allowing the inclusion of participants who had adequate experience and meaningful insights into safety practices.

Purposeful sampling was used to select specific departments and professional roles most relevant to the study’s objectives. First, the list of included health institutions was determined on the basis of their type, representation and convenience: five private medium clinics, four government health centres, three hospitals (two government and one private), four Woreda health offices and one zonal health office were included. The final eligible study participants were subsequently identified, and the data collectors visited the health facilities and explained the study’s aims. Frontline HPs, such as nurses, public health workers, pharmacists, midwives, laboratory technicians and medical doctors, were invited to observe and participate in focus group discussions (FGDs). At the same time, case team leaders, medical doctors, health institution managers, hygiene and sanitation officers and regulatory officers of the woreda and zonal health offices were invited to participate in key informant interviews (KIIs).

Data collection tools, methods and procedures

An open-ended, semistructured interview guide and observation checklists facilitated uniformity in the interviews and provided a more comprehensive exploration of specific issues or themes. We developed the tool after reviewing different relevant literature on occupational safety practices. The tool was contextually adapted on the basis of preliminary discussions with local health office representatives and occupational health and safety experts to ensure cultural and professional relevance. The tool contains four main parts. Part 1 contains the sociodemographics of the participants; part 2 focuses on observational data collection for healthcare professionals’ safety practices; parts 3 and 4 focus on front-line HPs’ FGDs and KIIs, respectively; and both focus on improvement strategies for safety practices (online supplemental file 1).

Non-participator direct observations (DOs), FGDs and KIIs gathered the data. Two proficient and trained Master of Public Health in Nutrition and Master of Public Health in Health Economics data collection (SB, male and SM, female) and one Master of Public Health in Biostatistics as a supervisor (AMD, male) collected the data. The data collectors and researchers had no prior relationship with the study participants. The participants were informed about the researcher’s personal goals and reasons for doing the research prior to the data collection. Each FGD had six or more participants to ensure substantive dialogue and diverse perspectives. We conducted FGDs with homogeneous participant groups from comparable health institutions (private vs government) and departments (eg, labs, pharmacy, nursing and midwifery) to facilitate open debate and ensure participation across all HPs. The concept of information saturation reflects the number of FGDs and KIIs conducted, as well as the final sample size or number of HPs included in the study. While the number of direct observation participants was determined by the FGD participants, the primary objective of including DOs was to ensure and integrate FGD data with data collectors’ DOs, which can decrease socially desirable biases during FGDs and KIIs. Information saturation was determined when no new themes, ideas or insights emerged from the interviews and FGDs, and subsequent data confirmed previously identified patterns. This point was reached after we included a total of 70 participants across DOs, comprising 51 HPs, 8 FGDs (with the same 51 HPs who were also participants in the DOs) and 19 KIIs from diverse health facilities. To achieve saturation, additional FGDs and three KIIs were conducted in addition to the intended FGDs. Data related to healthcare professionals’ safety practices, such as adherence to PPE, hand hygiene, sharps disposal, waste management and environmental sanitation, were collected with the documentation of DOs. Each focus group discussion lasted 60–90 min to address critical issues while maintaining member engagement. KIIs took place with the respondents selected on the basis of professional positions, expertise and participation in hygiene, sanitation, regulatory and policy implementation activities. Audio recording also featured FGDs and KIIs with note-taking prior to interviews for the purposes of observation data collection and during interviews.

Trustworthiness of the study

To guarantee trustworthiness, all the members of the research team underwent 3 days of rigorous training on the research aims, data collection tools and ethics. In an effort to increase clarity and usage, the data collection process was pilot tested with a minimal number of participants at a comparable site but not within the study site. We ensured the trustworthiness of our study by applying four key strategies: credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability. First, credibility was strengthened by dedicating sufficient time to data collection, drawing information from three different sources and conducting member checks to share preliminary findings with a small group of participants to confirm their accuracy and authenticity. Next, transferability was enhanced by providing a detailed description of the study context, participants and data collection procedures, enabling readers to judge how applicable the findings might be to other settings. Third, dependability or overall methods were maintained by maintaining a transparent audit trail of methodological decisions, interview guides and coding frameworks. Peer debriefing by the research team ensured the consistency of the data interpretation. Finally, confirmability was ensured by reflexivity, whereby researchers had reflective notes and analytic memos during the study to consider possible biases and ensure that interpretations were based on participants’ views and not researcher assumptions.11

Data processing and analysis

Safely storing and coding FGDs, KIIs and observational recordings ensured their secrecy and anonymity. FGDs and KII records were transcribed, translated from Amharic to English and imported into open-code software. Initial impressions, familiarity, important results, insights into the data and memoranda and footnotes were achieved by regularly reading the transcripts. To code all the emergent ideas and insights, we used two independent researchers and both open coding (facilitating the identification and categorisation of emerging concepts directly from the data) and in vivo coding (allowing the inclusion of participants’ own expressions to preserve the authenticity and contextual meaning of their narratives) techniques. The combination of both coding methods was used to enhance the interpretive rigour and ensure that the findings were firmly grounded in the participants’ perspectives. Continued merging or grouping of similar codes into larger categories led to themes and subthemes for results, conversations and reports. A descriptive qualitative study design with inductive reasoning was used to develop themes and categories directly from the data without imposing pre-existing theory or frameworks. We then draw or link our data-driven findings via abductive reasoning to existing frameworks (normalisation process theory) in the discussion. It is a form of theoretical triangulation or retrospective abductive reasoning, strengthening the interpretation and relevance of the findings within a broader theoretical and practical context. Triangulating FGDs, KIIs and direct observation data achieved interactive comprehension. Quotes, extracts and brief descriptions convey important results and topics.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public did not participate in the design, methods, reporting or dissemination of this study. Healthcare workers, as participants in interviews and FGDs, offered contextual insights that informed the interpretation of outcomes. Future dissemination efforts will involve sharing summary results with collaborating health institutions to increase awareness and facilitate improvement.

Results

The following results outline improvement strategies for enhancing compliance with safety measures among healthcare professionals. These strategies were developed in response to the gaps identified during observational assessments of safety measure practices; the challenges and barriers reflected through participants’ responses during FGDs and KIIs in the healthcare setting; and the barriers and challenges reported by HPs in various studies. The improvement strategies for safety measure practices were comprehensively explored through combining and integrating data from observations, FGDs and KIIs and triangulating them with theory. Additionally, it provides hints about the outcomes of improving safety measures.

Sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants

51 HPs participated in the DOs and 8 FGDs, while 19 HPs with various health sector leadership and administrative roles were interviewed as key informants. The majority (60.8%) held a diploma, and 39.2% held a Bachelor of Science degree. A total of 37.3% had fewer than 5 years of experience, 35.3% had 5–10 years and 21.6% had 11–15 years. Finally, 47.1% worked in private health institutions, and 52.9% worked in government health institutions (table 1).

The Conceptual Framework

This study was supported by a conceptual framework. The conceptual framework developed in this work is shown in figure 1, which shows the links among the practice of safety measures, improvement strategies for safety measures and their outcomes. The framework offered a clear and structured way to examine participants’ safety practices and their approaches to improvement. It also guided the data analysis process by helping organise and interpret emerging themes, ensuring that the findings captured both individual behaviours and broader organisational factors. By grounding the results within an established conceptual understanding of workplace safety and quality improvement, this approach strengthens the study’s theoretical contribution.

Conceptual framework and thematic mapping indicating the themes, subthemes, categories of compliance rates, improvement strategies, safety measures and outcomes.

Within this framework, two main themes were identified: safety measure practices and strategies for improving safety measures. The first theme, safety compliance, focuses on how effectively healthcare professionals adhere to established safety guidelines. Low-safety practices and moderate-safety practices were two subthemes, which included inadequate hand hygiene (36.37%), poor use of PPE (45.43%) and weak waste disposal and environmental hygiene (33.33%). Moderate safety practices were observed in the handling and disposal of sharps (67.36%).

The second theme, improvement strategies, addresses these gaps and includes four subthemes. (1) Infrastructure and supply chain management ensure consistent PPE availability and facility renovation. (2) Training and capacity-building initiatives include organised training and courses and refresher training and orientations. (3) Community engagement involves community health education and changing attitudes. (4) Policy strengthening, enforcement and integration emphasise local adaptive rules, monitoring and multidisciplinary cooperation. (5) Human resource management and behavioural change strategies seek to lower workload, apply incentives, impose penalties and encourage behaviour modification by peer influence (Table 2).

Safety measures among HPs

To compute the overall practice with safety measures, we used the following formula: Compliance rate (%) = Total number of compliant responsesTotal possible responsesx100, where total number of compliant responses=sum of all compliance scores across all participants, and total possible responses=number of participants×number of questions.

This study revealed low practices across most basic components of safety measures in government and private health facilities. The participants from government health institutions demonstrated better practices (48.90%) than those from private facilities did (39.11%), but both require improvement. Moderate practices among the government and private HPs were obtained for the handling and disposal of sharps (67.12% in the government and 67.65% in private facilities). However, fewer practices were noted for using PPE, hand hygiene practices, waste disposal and environmental hygiene, with private HPs reporting less compliance across most components (table 3).

Strategies to improve safety measures (standard precautions)

The study suggested five main techniques or subthemes to improve compliance with the safety measures used to manage safety in healthcare settings. These strategies were developed first in response to gaps identified via observational assessments of safety measure practices (theme 1), including inadequate hand hygiene, poor use of PPE and weak waste disposal and environmental hygiene. Second, the participants’ reflections during the FGDs and KIIs suggested insufficient resources and infrastructure limitations, inadequate training, gaps in community and HPs’ knowledge and attitudes toward safety measures, behavioural resistance, insufficient support and staff shortages as major challenges. Furthermore, the suggested challenges and barriers, such as low compliance rates, excessive workload, insufficient training, limited resources and infrastructure, behavioural resistance and weak policy enforcement, are comparable with these findings and are supported by the literature158 (online supplemental file 2).

Infrastructure and supply chain management

Sustainable compliance was underlined as dependent on infrastructure enhancements and changes in resource management. They were identified as critical to ensuring the consistent implementation of safety measures. Through government-private partnerships, participants underlined the requirement of consistent PPE supply chains, as stockouts were a significant obstacle in private clinics. “We need bulk procurement to avoid shortages”-FGD-private pharmacy 6. Furthermore, facility renovation was deemed necessary to assist infection control methods, including water tanks, better ventilation and the replacement of mud-walled buildings. Woreda’s officer said, “Appropriate hygiene practices depend on improving water supply infrastructure in health institutions” (KII-woreda officer 9). Another Woreda officer described his practical experience: “better organizational building and maintaining healthcare infrastructure, adhering to high sanitation and safety standards,” KII-Woreda officer 7. Making facilities better by giving them enough space, water and sanitation infrastructure makes it easier to put safety measures in place. This strategy strengthens the foundation for all other safety measures by addressing material and structural limitations.

Training and capacity-building initiatives

Training and capacity development have become top concerns for almost all study participants. Regular organised training sessions, courses and refresher programmes are very important for helping HPs learn more, improve their skills and follow safety rules. It helps individuals learn about standard safety measures, how to use PPE correctly and how to eliminate trash properly. Capacity-building programmes also give staff a chance to talk about safety problems, share experiences and adopt best practices. The participants suggested organised, department-specific training courses using training-of-trainers models to cascade information effectively. “Mandatory IPC training for all staff, not just nurses” –FGD-private pharmacy 5. “Refreshers were advised to stay up with changing guidelines and procedures every three to six months”; FGD-Private nurse 5.

Zonal officers also recommend repeated training and refreshers as mandatory to change HPs’ attitudes and improve their knowledge and practices. They elaborated this:

Health professionals must receive regular, structured training on standard precautions; furthermore, mandatory refresher courses should be implemented to reinforce best practices…”KII-Zonal Officer 15

Moreover, it is crucial to strengthen training courses and offer healthcare professionals ongoing professional growth. Conducting repeated orientations helps to guarantee that everyday medical procedures, including IPC strategies. - KII-Zonal Officer 16

Community engagement

Community engagement through training and health education helps change both patients’ and staff’s feelings toward safety measures. It has become clear that a culture of safety depends critically on public knowledge and community participation. When people in a community understand safety practices, they are less likely to resist them and more likely to work together during procedures. HPs highlighted the need to address social and behavioural issues to increase patient/client participation and understanding through health education and awareness of mask usage, hand cleanliness and overcoming cultural opposition.

Community engagement programs should be strengthened to increase patient awareness about standard precautions… FGD-Private laboratory 2

… One important step is changing attitudes through health education for HPs and the community because health education and morning sessions at health institutions are insufficient. KII-Government Health Center head 10

Policy strengthening, enforcement and integration

Policies that are locally adaptable and continuously monitored can help change the culture of safety measure practices, and other relevant aspects can be integrated into daily tasks, providing a structured approach to ensure compliance. The participants said, “Without strict follow-up, the use of infection prevention practices declines.” FGD-private midwifery 4. The participants demanded localised modifications of internationally copied guidelines and standards to balance resource limitations. “Policies require change and local adaptation” FGD-private nurse 2. The participants understood the role of enforcement and institutional support as “strict monitoring and supervision should be implemented to ensure adherence to guidelines,” KII-woreda officer 7.

Private clinics need more control, support, monitoring, and evaluation systems strictly since some materials used are also substandard. KII-Private clinic head 14

Following policies and guidelines strictly, integration and interdisciplinary cooperation should also be considered to simplify efforts at infection prevention. It provides staff with a structured framework for behaviour and holds them accountable. They also encourage compliance and integrate safety practices into everyday work. This systematic enforcement ensures that adherence is more consistent, reduces the likelihood of occupational exposure and errors and standardises procedures. Cross-departmental teamwork was proposed to maximise limited resources. Private pharmacy professionals reported that “a multidisciplinary approach is needed where pharmacists, physicians, nurses, and laboratory professionals collaborate to prevent infection in healthcare settings”-FGD-private pharmacy 4. Zonal health officers agreed on this issue, as infection prevention tasks or activities were not a focus area for most stakeholders and should be integrated with other programmes. “…budget allocation for infection control should be integrated into financial planning to ensure sustainability”-KII-Zonal officer 15.

…Collaboration between local health offices and other higher administrative bodies is necessary. -KII-woreda officer 7

Strengthening collaboration between private clinics and government health offices is necessary.-FGD-Private laboratory 2

Human resource management and behavioural change strategies

Essential human resource strategies, including appointing infection prevention officers and lowering demanding workloads, were mentioned as advantageous mechanisms of compliance with safety measures. Staff who felt overburdened claimed to have taken shortcuts on safety procedures, implying that staffing changes would considerably increase compliance. The government health center head (KIIGHCH1) described this condition as “employing appropriate staff is crucial”.

Reducing the burden by employing more employees or using improved task management techniques will help us to follow safeguards more successfully-FGD-Government Pharmacy 5

… revising the human resources manual 15 and employing environmental health professionals and trained sanitary workers would help ensure adherence to infection control measures- KII-Woreda regulatory officer 9.

Behavioural modification techniques such as peer modelling, experience sharing and positive reinforcement were advised to help overcome reluctance. The proposed changes to workplace standards included public acceptance of compliant employees, and incentives were recommended. In addition, penalties and corrective actions are used for non-compliance.

…. might be raised by supporting peer learning and experience-sharing within the healthcare community as well as performance-based incentives. -KII-woreda officer 9

Good practice recognition and incentives help inspire compliance-FGD-government pharmacy 2.

…. corrective measures, including incentives and penalties, are needed depending on the degree of compliance.-KII-woreda officer 6

This suggests that improving staffing, reducing workload and utilising behavioural incentives can enhance safety measures at work. Positive reinforcement, sanctions and behaviour modification can help with resistant or negative employees. Increased motivation and accountability lead to long-term behaviour improvements. This improves safety compliance. Behavioural therapies and organisational assistance minimise healthcare exhaustion (burnout), neglect and health and related risks. In general, on the basis of most participants’ thoughts, implementing improvement strategies with best practices results in high compliance with safety measures. This leads to a safer work environment, increases the quality of life and occupational safety of HPs, reduces infection rates, saves costs and improves patient trust, as supported by a previous study.12 13

Discussion

This qualitative descriptive study explored low compliance with safety measures and identified contextually grounded strategies to increase compliance among HPs in resource-limited Ethiopian healthcare settings. While a few previous studies in Ethiopia focused mainly on quantifying the level of adherence or identifying associated factors of safety practices, this study contributes to the literature by examining the lived experiences of frontline HPs and suggesting practical strategies using normalisation process theory (NPT). According to the NPT, the integration of complex practices such as safety measures involves four domains: coherence (sense-making), cognitive participation (engagement), collective action (enactment) and reflexive monitoring (appraisal).14 15 The data analysis revealed uneven compliance across key IPC practices; hand hygiene compliance was only 36.47%, whereas waste management compliance was 33.33%. These figures, although comparable to rates reported in other LMICs, such as Tanzania16 and Ghana,17 point to notable gaps in coordination and collective effort. Many HPs have struggled to integrate IPC into routine practice, largely due to poor infrastructure, weak or poorly enforced policies and unclear role responsibilities. For example, the lack of functional handwashing stations and essential PPE reduced both the perceived feasibility and value of IPC, undermining its coherence. Even when healthcare workers understood the importance of IPC, the absence of necessary materials eroded their motivation. PPE use was generally low and varied notably between private and public institutions: 38.19% in the private sector compared with 51.85% in government facilities. Private health workers attributed this gap to their exclusion from government supply chains, a form of systemic marginalisation that prevents lessons from the private sector from being incorporated into national IPC policy reviews. This pattern mirrors broader inequities within health systems in Nigeria4 and other LMICs,5 where vertical programmes often overlook the interdependence of diverse healthcare providers. Many professionals also described IPC protocols as burdensome rather than meaningful, suggesting a breakdown in cognitive participation. Without strong leadership and institutional support, healthcare workers often lack the encouragement and resources to embed IPC into daily routines, which is consistent with findings from previous studies.18

Following the observational and contextual understanding of safety measure practices among HPs in healthcare settings, this study provides five major improvement strategies as a subtheme for enhancing safety measure practices. Accordingly, the proposed strategies were aligned and linked directly and/or indirectly with the suggested gaps, challenges and barriers, ensuring that the proposed improvement strategies for safety measure practices were evidence-based and contextually relevant. Infrastructure and supply chain management: Participants emphasised chronic challenges such as inadequate water supply, PPE shortages and unreliable procurement systems. They proposed ensuring a consistent supply of materials, dedicated government budget allocations and public–private partnerships to stabilise access. These recommendations relate directly to collective action, as reliable material support is critical for embedding IPC in daily routines. Addressing these strategies through consistent PPE availability and facility renovation can mitigate resource shortages and infrastructure limitations, enabling staff to implement safety measures effectively. Similar studies have highlighted the importance of improved water and sanitation infrastructure in minimising contamination risk.19 Others have endorsed public–private collaboration to strengthen procurement and supply chains.20 Training and capacity building: Participants advocated for regular, department-specific IPC training and refresher courses, orientations, peer learning and experience-sharing sessions. Such initiatives enhance cognitive participation by fostering shared understanding and commitment among health workers. This can directly address knowledge and information gaps by increasing awareness and enhancing performance in safety measure practices. HPs who receive regular training are better equipped to follow protocols, reducing occupational risk exposure and HAIs. Evidence from previous research supports these approaches,7921 22 underscoring the value of continuous professional development and workplace learning in improving IPC performance. These findings are consistent with WASH programmes promoting the practical feasibility of IPC interventions.23 Community engagement: Participants also proposed involving religious and community leaders to promote IPC, recognising that culturally grounded approaches can improve coherence and social acceptance. Public awareness campaigns and patient education were seen as vital for encouraging adherence, particularly in overcoming behavioural gaps through improving cultural resistance towards safety measures and gaps such as the use of masks and gloves. Framing IPC as a community norm rather than a purely medical directive aligns with the participatory health promotion strategies recommended in the global health literature.24 25 Policy and governance: Participants stressed the need to adapt national IPC guidelines to local realities and to strengthen accountability through regular audits, the integration of IPC into licensing systems and better policy enforcement. The inclusion of the private sector in national IPC planning was seen as crucial for ensuring inclusivity and system-wide learning. Regular monitoring and evaluation facilitate reflexive monitoring, allowing institutions to identify and address gaps systematically. Therefore, this strategy specifically aligns with addressing policy and enforcement gaps, ensuring that safety practices are followed consistently. These findings echo prior evidence supporting policy integration and multisector collaboration.22 26 Encouraging collaboration between public and private facilities and across departments can improve communication, adherence and overall IPC outcomes. This recommendation is consistent with the strengthening framework of the WHO’s health systems23 and highlights the importance of collective action in sustaining IPC practices.27 Human resources and behaviour change: Participants emphasised addressing staffing shortages, hiring IPC specialists (including environmental HPs) and maintaining adequate workforce levels. These measures strengthen collective action through improved workload management and cognitive participation by increasing motivation. The proposed behavioural strategies included performance-based rewards, penalties for non-compliance and opportunities for peer learning and feedback. This improvement strategy was used to manage attitudes and behavioural resistance barriers and sustain adherence to safety measures. Previous studies suggest that behavioural interventions such as public health communication, peer monitoring, goal setting and performance feedback are effective in promoting IPC compliance.2831

Overall, both participant insights and the supporting literature reveal a strong understanding of IPC implementation as a socially embedded process. Institutional culture, infrastructure and leadership all shape how safety measures are adopted and maintained. Sustainable IPC requires more than written policies to be integrated into the local culture, supported by leadership and reinforced through equitable systems. This study offers comprehensive, interconnected strategies to strengthen IPC practices critical not only for improving compliance but also for creating safer workplaces, enhancing professional well-being, reducing HAIs, lowering costs and fostering patient trust.12 13 32

Conclusions

This study provides new insights into the inconsistent application of IPC measures within Ethiopian healthcare settings. By applying the NPT framework, the findings emphasise that successful IPC integration depends on promoting coherence, engagement, collective effort and continuous evaluation. We recommend that healthcare leaders and policymakers adopt inclusive, evidence-based strategies, particularly those that enhance infrastructure, support behavioural change through peer learning and integrate private facilities into national IPC systems to achieve lasting improvements in IPC.
