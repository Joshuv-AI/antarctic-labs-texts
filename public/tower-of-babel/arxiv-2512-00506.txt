Expected Cost Analysis of Online Facility Assignment on Regular Polygons
Md Rawha Siddiqi Riad, Md Manzurul Hasan

Abstract
This paper analyzes the online facility assignment problem in a geometric setting where facilities with unit capacity are positioned at the vertices of a regular n n -gon. Customers arrive sequentially at uniformly random positions along the edges. They must be assigned immediately to the nearest available facility, with ties broken by coin toss. The sequential nature and unknown future arrivals require a probabilistic analysis of the expected assignment cost. Our main contribution is a recursive characterization of the expected cost: for any occupancy state S S , the expected remaining cost V ⁡ ( S ) V(S) equals the average over all edge positions of the immediate assignment cost plus the expected future cost after assignment. We prove that this integral equation can calculate a solution and provide the expected value for small n n ( n = 3 , 4 , 5 n=3,4,5 ). For larger values of n n and expected cost, we develop efficient numerical methods, including a discretized dynamic programming approach and Monte Carlo simulation. The work establishes a fundamental probabilistic approach for online assignment in polygonal environments.



1
Introduction

The online facility assignment problem has become increasingly relevant in different areas such as logistics, computer networks, and distributed systems, where requests must be served immediately upon arrival without knowledge of future demand.

1.1
Problem Motivation

The online facility assignment problem in geometric settings captures essential features of real-world systems where service providers with limited capacity must serve customers appearing at random locations. Examples include ride-sharing platforms matching drivers to riders, delivery services allocating packages to vehicles, and sensor networks routing data to processing stations.

1.2
Problem Definition

We consider a regular
n
-gon with facilities at each vertex, each with unit capacity. Customers arrive sequentially according to our assignment system: each customer selects an edge uniformly at random from the
n
edges, then a position
t\in[0,1]
uniformly along that edge. Upon arrival, the customer must be assigned to the nearest available facility using the shortest path along the polygon edges. When multiple facilities are equally distant, the customer chooses uniformly at random (Coin Toss). The process terminates after exactly
n
customers, when all facilities are occupied.

The objective is to minimize the
expected total assignment cost
, defined as the sum of distances travelled by all customers using a greedy approach. The online nature creates a fundamental scenario that each assignment must be made without future knowledge, yet it must account for how it affects future assignment possibilities.

1.3
Contributions

This paper makes the following key contributions:

i.

Theoretical Foundation
: We derive a recursive integral equation representing the expected cost
V(S)
for any occupancy state
S\subseteq V
:

V(S)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{e\in E}\int_{0}^{1}\left[C_{S,e}(t)+\sum_{m\notin S}^{n}P_{m|S,e}(t)V(S\cup\{m\})\right]dt

where
C_{S,e}(t)
is the immediate cost and
P_{m|S,e}(t)
are transition probabilities.

ii.

Algorithmic Solutions
: We develop efficient computational methods including exact dynamic programming for moderate
n
and Monte Carlo simulation for larger instances.

iii.

Computational Characterization
: Through experiments for
n=3
to
12
, we provide expected costs and analyze scaling behaviour with polygon size.

Our work provides the first exact probabilistic characterization of online facility assignment in polygonal environments, establishing a foundation for analyzing more complex geometric assignment problems.

2
Related Work

The problem of assigning customers to facilities in an online setting sits at the intersection of online algorithms, geometric optimization, and stochastic analysis. The classical Online Facility Location (OFL) problem, where the algorithm must decide whether to open a facility or connect to an existing one, was formalized by Meyerson
[
11
]
. He provided a randomized algorithm with a logarithmic competitive ratio, a result that has been refined and generalized for various metric spaces
[
4
]
. However, our problem differs from OFL in that facility locations are fixed and capacities are strict. This variant, formally known as the
Online Facility Assignment
(OFA) problem, has been studied by Ahmed et al.
[
1
]
, who proved that a greedy strategy yields a linear competitive ratio on line and graph geometries. Recently, Malik et al.
[
8
]
extended this to regular polygon geometries, focusing on worst-case bounds.

Our work complements these competitive analyses by addressing the average-case scenario, which shares theoretical roots with the Online Bipartite Matching problem. The seminal work by Karp et al.
[
7
]
established that for adversarial arrivals, the optimal competitive ratio is
1-1/e
. However, when the assumption of adversarial arrivals is relaxed to
random order arrivals
(ROA), algorithms often perform significantly better. Goel and Mehta
[
5
]
demonstrated that the competitive ratio approaches 1 as the size of the instance grows under random permutations. For weighted variants involving distances, Kalyanasundaram and Pruhs
[
6
]
showed that simple greedy algorithms can fail in adversarial settings but perform well stochastically. A comprehensive survey of these online matching techniques and their applications in resource allocation is provided by Mehta
[
9
]
.

In the specific context of geometric probability, the expected cost of matching random points has a deep theoretical concept. The result discussed by Ajtai, Komlós, and Tusnády
[
2
]
says that for
n
random points in a unit square, the optimal matching cost scales as
\Theta(\sqrt{n\log n})
. However, their work provides asymptotic bounds for large
n
and does not offer methods for calculating exact costs for finite
n
. Furthermore, as the state space in these problems grows exponentially, the determination of exact analytical solutions becomes computationally intractable. To address this, Monte Carlo methods
[
10
]
are frequently employed to approximate high-dimensional integrals and expectations in stochastic systems. Caflisch
[
3
]
highlights that for problems where standard numerical integration fails due to dimensionality, Monte Carlo convergence rates remain independent of dimension. Our work synthesizes these approaches: we rely on the recursive structure for exactness in small instances and leverage Monte Carlo integration to estimate the expected costs for larger polygon sizes.

3.1
Online Assignment Process

The assignment process proceeds sequentially as follows:

i.

A customer arrives at random edge
e\in E
and position
t\in[0,1]

ii.

The customer is assigned to the nearest available facility using the edge based distance.

iii.

If multiple facilities are equally distant, the customer chooses uniformly at random among them (Coin Toss).

iv.

The set of occupied facilities is updated to include the chosen facility.

v.

The process repeats until all
n
facilities are occupied.

The objective is to minimize the expected total distance travelled by all customers, where the expectation is taken over the random arrival positions and any tie-breaking randomization.

4
Theoretical Analysis

To analyze the behaviour of the online assignment process, it is essential to express the expected total cost in a recursive form. Therefore, in the following subsection, we derive the expected cost recurrence that links each occupancy state to its future evolution.

4.1
Expected Cost Recurrence

The foundation of our analysis is a recursive equation that characterizes the expected total assignment cost from any given state of facility occupancy. Let
V(S)
represent the
expected total cost
starting from state
S\subseteq V
(where
S
is the set of occupied facilities) until all facilities become occupied

Theorem 4.1

For any state
S\subset V
with
|S|<n
, the expected total cost satisfies the recurrence:

V(S)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{e\in E}\int_{0}^{1}\left[C_{S,e}(t)+\sum_{m=1}^{n}P_{m|S,e}(t)\cdot V(S\cup\{m\})\right]dt

with the boundary condition
V(V)=0
. Here:

i.

C_{S,e}(t)=\min\limits_{m\notin S}d(e,t,m)
is the immediate cost—the distance to the nearest available facility when a customer arrives at position
t
on edge
e
.

ii.

P_{m|S,e}(t)
is the probability that facility
m
is chosen given the current state
S
, arrival edge
e
, and position
t
.

iii.

The sum
\sum_{m=1}^{n}P_{m|S,e}(t)V(S\cup\{m\})
represents the expected future cost after the current assignment.

Proof

We derive this recurrence through careful conditioning on the random arrival process:

Step 1: Total Expectation
. Since customers arrive uniformly over edges and positions:

V(S)=\mathbb{E}[\text{Total Cost}\mid S]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{e\in E}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}[\text{Total Cost}\mid S,e,t]dt

The factor
\frac{1}{n}
is for uniform edge selection, and the integral averages over all possible positions along each edge.

Step 2: Cost Decomposition
. For a fixed arrival at
(e,t)
, the total cost decomposes into immediate and future components:

\mathbb{E}[\text{Total Cost}\mid S,e,t]=\underbrace{C_{S,e}(t)}_{\text{immediate cost}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\text{Future Cost}\mid S,e,t]}_{\text{remaining cost}}

The immediate cost
C_{S,e}(t)
is deterministic given
(S,e,t)
, as it’s simply the minimum distance to any available facility.

Step 3: Future Cost Expectation
. The future cost depends on which facility gets occupied:

\mathbb{E}[\text{Future Cost}\mid S,e,t]=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(\text{choose }m\mid S,e,t)\cdot\mathbb{E}[\text{Total Cost}\mid S\cup\{m\}]

By definition,

\mathbb{P}(\text{choose }m\mid S,e,t)=P_{m|S,e}(t)
and
\mathbb{E}[\text{Total Cost}\mid S\cup\{m\}]=V(S\cup\{m\})
.

Step 4: Boundary Condition
. When all facilities are occupied (
S=V
), no further costs are incurred, so
V(V)=0
.
Combining these steps yields the stated recurrence.

∎

4.2
Monte Carlo Interpretation of the Recurrence

Although Theorem
4.1
expresses the expected cost as an exact integral, our
implementation evaluates this recurrence using a Monte Carlo estimator.
Instead of computing

V(S)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{e\in E}\int_{0}^{1}\big[C_{S,e}(t)+\sum_{m\notin S}P_{m\mid S,e}(t)\,V(S\cup\{m\})\big]\;dt

Analytically, the program approximates the expectation

V(S)=\mathbb{E}\big[C_{S,E}(T)+V(S^{+})\big],

where
E
is a uniformly random edge and
T\sim\mathrm{Uniform}[0,1]

is a random position on that edge.

For each of the
K
Monte Carlo samples, the algorithm:

i.

selects a random edge
E
uniformly from
\{0,\dots,n-1\}
,

ii.

selects a random position
T\in[0,1]
,

iii.

computes the immediate cost
C_{S,E}(T)
,

iv.

determines the nearest available facility using the greedy rule
(coin-toss tie breaking),

v.

obtains the next state
S^{+}=S\cup\{m\}
,

vi.

forms the sample value

Y=C_{S,E}(T)+V(S^{+}),

where
V(S^{+})
has already been computed by dynamic programming.

The estimator for
V(S)
is the sample average

\widehat{V}(S)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}Y_{k},

which is an unbiased Monte Carlo approximation of the recurrence in
Theorem
4.1
. The DP ordering guarantees that all future-state values
V(S^{+})

are known before evaluating
V(S)
. Thus, the code implements the theoretical
recurrence exactly, with the integral replaced by numerical sampling.

4.3
Structural Properties

Recurrence serves several important mathematical properties that provide intuition about the problem structure.

Lemma 1
(Probability Normalization)

For all states
S
, edges
e
, and positions
t
, the transition probabilities satisfy:

\sum_{m=1}^{n}P_{m|S,e}(t)=1

Proof

This follows directly from the assignment rules. For any arrival
(e,t)
, the customer must choose exactly one facility. If there’s a unique nearest facility,
P_{m|S,e}(t)=1
for that facility and
0
for others. If there are
k
equally distant facilities, each has probability
\frac{1}{k}
, and these probabilities sum to
1
.

∎

4.4
Special Case Analysis

To build intuition, we examine important special cases that reveal the recurrence’s structure.

Corollary 1
(Both Endpoints Available)

When a customer arrives on edge
e=(i,j)
and both endpoints are available (
i,j\notin S
), the recurrence simplifies to:

\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{1}\left[\min(t,1-t)+\frac{V(S\cup\{i\})+V(S\cup\{j\})}{2}\right]dt

for
t\neq\frac{1}{2}
, with appropriate tie-handling at the midpoint.

Proof

When both endpoints are available and
t<\frac{1}{2}
, facility
i
is closer and is chosen with probability 1. When
t>\frac{1}{2}
, facility
j
is chosen. At
t=\frac{1}{2}
, both are equally distant and chosen with probability
\frac{1}{2}
each. The immediate cost is
\min(t,1-t)
, and the future cost averages the two possible next states.

∎

Corollary 2
(One Endpoint Available)

If only one endpoint
i
of edge
e=(i,j)
is available (
i\notin S,j\in S
), the contribution becomes:

\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{1}\left[t+V(S\cup\{i\})\right]dt

since the customer must choose facility
i
regardless of position, with immediate cost
t
.

Here is the detailed complexity analysis for the Monte Carlo Dynamic Programming algorithm.

Time Complexity

The time complexity is determined by the number of states in the DP, the number of Monte Carlo samples per state, and the work done per sample.

i.

Number of States (
S
):
The state is represented by a bitmask of length
n
. The total number of unique states is
2^{n}
.

ii.

Monte Carlo Samples (
K
):
For every single state, the algorithm runs a simulation loop
K
times.

iii.

Work Per Sample (
W
):
Inside the simulation loop, the algorithm must iterate through all free facilities to calculate distances. In the worst case (at the start), there are
n
free facilities. Finding the minimum distance and identifying ties requires scanning the list of free facilities once. Therefore, the work per sample is linear with respect to
n
:
W=\mathcal{O}(n)

The total time complexity is calculated as:

\text{Time}=\text{States}\times\text{Samples}\times\text{Work per Sample}

\text{Time}=\mathcal{O}(2^{n}\cdot K\cdot n)

Space Complexity

The space complexity is dominated by the storage required for the Dynamic Programming table.

i.

DP Table (
V
):
We store one floating-point value for every possible state mask.

\text{Space}_{table}=\mathcal{O}(2^{n})

ii.

Auxiliary Storage:
The grouping of masks by population count also requires storage proportional to the number of states. Vectors for ties and free facilities take
\mathcal{O}(n)
space, which is negligible compared to
2^{n}
.

6
Conclusion

This paper has established a complete theoretical and computational framework for the online facility assignment problem on regular polygons, deriving an exact recursive characterization of expected costs and developing efficient dynamic programming and Monte Carlo solutions that handle the edge-based distance metric and unit capacity constraints. Our analysis reveals fundamental structural properties, including cost monotonicity, and provides the expected total costs for various polygon sizes, offering both practical algorithms for moderate instances and foundational insights. Also identifies some open problems, including non-regular polygons, non-uniform arrivals, higher capacities, and adversarial analysis.
