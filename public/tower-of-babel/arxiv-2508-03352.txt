A Note on Zoll Manifolds with Entire Grauert Tubes
Kyobeom Song

Abstract
We compute several algebraic indices of the algebraization of the tangent bundle of a Zoll manifold that admits an entire Grauert tube. As an application, we prove that any Zoll manifold of type ℍ ​ ℙ 2 \mathbb{HP}^{2} with an entire Grauert tube is isometric to the canonical ℍ ​ ℙ 2 \mathbb{HP}^{2} .



1
Introduction

A Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
is called a
Zoll manifold of period
\ell
, or simply a
C_{\ell}
-manifold, if it satisfies

(i)

every geodesic
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to M
suffices
\gamma(t+\ell)=\gamma(t)
, and

(ii)

\gamma
is injective on
(0,l)
.

A quick example is the round sphere
S^{n}
. Additional examples arise as compact rank-one symmetric spaces (CROSS)
\mathbb{RP}^{n},~\mathbb{CP}^{n},~\mathbb{HP}^{n},~\text{and}~\mathbb{OP}^{2}
, with their canonical metrics.

By the Bott–Samelson theorem, any Zoll manifold has the same cohomology ring as exactly one of these five CROSSes, which we call the
type
of the manifold. However, this does not imply isometry: Darboux initiated explicit investigations of
C_{l}
-manifold in 1894, and in 1903, Zoll produced the first exotic Zoll metric on
S^{2}
, which made the name. For an extensive historical reference, we recommend
[
2
]
.

In this paper, we particularly study Zoll manifolds whose tangent bundle admits an
entire Grauert tube
: the tangent bundle
TM
carries a complex structure
J
such that the function
u(v)=\|v\|
is a plurisubharmonic exhaustion solving the homogeneous complex Monge–Ampère equation

\bigl(i\partial\bar{\partial}u\bigr)^{n}=0,

i.e.
(TM,J,u)
becomes a Stein manifold with center
M
.

Main Question.

Are the CROSSes the unique Zoll manifolds admitting entire Grauert tubes?

This question is important in three distinct contexts:

1.

Extension of the classical Zoll metric classification problem.

The most intriguing feature of a Zoll metric lies in its tangent bundle
\mathrm{TM}
. As is well known, the tangent bundle of a Riemannian manifold carries a natural symplectic structure given by the Poincaré
2
–form. Moreover, the geodesic flow induced by a Zoll metric preserves this form, so that finding Zoll metrics is equivalent to determining which tangent bundles admit a symplectic manifold structure for which the geodesic flow generates an
S^{1}
–equivariant action. In this setting, the main question asks whether strengthening the symplectic condition to require a Kähler structure excludes any exotic examples beyond the CROSS.

2.

Strengthening classification results for Stein manifolds by their center.

In
[
12
]
, Patrizio and Wong proved that among Stein manifolds, those whose center is a CROSS are unique up to biholomorphism for each CROSS. This stands as one of the cornerstone classification results for Stein manifolds. The main question is its natural generalization: if one replaces the requirement “center is a CROSS” by “center is a Zoll,” does the same uniqueness still hold?

3.

Exploring examples of good complexifications.

Following Totaro
[
15
]
, a
good complexification
of a closed smooth manifold
M
is a smooth affine algebraic variety
U
over
\mathbb{R}
such that

U(\mathbb{R})\;\cong\;M\quad\text{(diffeomorphism)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad U(\mathbb{R})\hookrightarrow U(\mathbb{C})\;\text{ is a homotopy equivalence.}

A long‐standing conjecture asserts that a closed real manifold is nonnegatively curved
iff
it admits a good complexification. Manifolds with good complexifications are already known to satisfy strong topological restrictions: their Euler characteristic is non-negative (Kulkarni
[
5
]
) and, more strongly, all odd Betti numbers vanish (Totaro
[
15
]
). If the conjecture were true, such conditions would immediately settle classical problems concerning the cohomology of nonnegatively curved manifolds, e.g., the Hopf conjecture.

At present, every
known
nonnegatively curved manifold does admit a good complexification, yet producing new examples of good complexification remains notably difficult. In this context, the Main Problem acquires its significance: Burns and Leung
[
3
]
observed that entire Grauert tubes of Zoll manifolds are themselves a good complexification. Thus, should the Main Problem turn out to have a negative answer, it would be an exotic new example of a manifold with a good complexification.

To date, the Main Question is known to hold for type
S^{n}
and
\mathbb{RP}^{n}

[
3
]
, and
\mathbb{CP}^{n}

[
10
]
, but remains completely open for
\mathbb{HP}^{n}
and
\mathbb{OP}^{2}
. In fact, the difficulty in these two cases is expected to be substantially greater for the following reasons. The framework that has succeeded in the known cases can be summarized in three steps:

1.

Use the Zoll structure on
M
to algebraize the tangent bundle
\mathrm{TM}
into a projective manifold
X
.

2.

Analyze the indices of
X
to show that
X
is Fano, and then apply Fano characterization theorems to conclude that
X
is biholomorphic to the corresponding model case.

3.

Exploit the well‐known geometry of the model case
X\cong\overline{Q}^{n}
or
\mathbb{CP}^{n}\times\mathbb{CP}^{n}
to prove that the totally real submanifold
M\subset X
is isometric to the model.

For the first step, the remarkable work of Burns and Leung
[
3
]
applies to Zoll manifolds of any type. Nevertheless, the second step—determining the biholomorphic type of the
projective algebraization
X
of
TM
—faces serious obstacles when the Zoll
manifold is of type
\mathbb{HP}^{n}
or
\mathbb{OP}^{2}
.
According to Patrizio–Wong
[
12
]
, the algebraizations arising in each canonical model are summarized in Table
1
.

Table 1:
Algebraizations of
TM
for the five CROSS types

Canonical

In the first three cases (
S^{n}
,
\mathbb{RP}^{n}
, and
\mathbb{CP}^{n}
)
the algebraizations either have high index or Picard number
2
; hence powerful characterization theorems are available and play a decisive role in resolving the corresponding instances of the Main Problem.
By contrast, for
Gr(2,2n+2)
and
E_{6}/P_{1}
, virtually no general
characterization theorems exist, and the few available results rely on strong
hypotheses concerning the VMRT or the existence of dominating curve families.
Even in the third step, the geometry of
Gr(2,2n+2)
and
E_{6}/P_{1}
is more difficult than the previous three cases, making these two types considerably harder than the others.

In this paper, as a preliminary study toward resolving the Main Question for the still‐open types
\mathbb{HP}^{n}
and
\mathbb{OP}^{2}
, we first record several observations on the various algebraic indices that the projective algebraization
X
must satisfy. Using these observations, we then settle the Main Question in the case of
\mathbb{HP}^{2}
:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(M,g)
be a Zoll manifold of type
\mathbb{HP}^{2}
admitting an entire Grauert tube. Then
(M,g)
is, up to a constant rescaling, isometric to the quaternionic projective plane
\mathbb{HP}^{2}
equipped with its canonical metric.

2
Structure of the Tangent Bundle

Let
(M,g)
be a Zoll manifold with period
2\pi
whose tangent bundle admits an
entire Grauert tube
structure.
In this section we describe several geometric structures on
TM
and introduce its projective algebraization
X
.

2.1
The Riemann foliation

Fix a point
p\in M
and a unit tangent vector
v\in T_{p}M
.
Let
\gamma\colon\mathbb{R}\to M
be the geodesic with
\gamma(0)=p
and
\gamma^{\prime}(0)=v
.
Define a map

\varphi_{\gamma}\colon\mathbb{C}\;\longrightarrow\;TM,\qquad\varphi_{\gamma}(\sigma+i\tau)=\tau\,\gamma^{\prime}(\sigma).

If
\gamma
and
\beta
are geodesics having distinct images, then

\operatorname{im}\varphi_{\gamma}\cap\operatorname{im}\varphi_{\beta}=\varnothing
inside
TM\setminus M
.
Hence the collection
\{\operatorname{im}\varphi_{\gamma}\}_{\gamma}
forms a foliation of
TM\setminus M
, called the
Riemann foliation
. Under the Zoll condition of period
2\pi
, note that each leaf
\varphi
can in fact be viewed as an immersion of the cylinder
\phi:\mathbb{C}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\;\to\;TM
.

2.2
(Entire) Grauert tubes

For
0<r\leq\infty
set

T^{r}M\;:=\;\bigl\{\,v\in TM\mid\|v\|<r\bigr\}.

If there exists a complex structure
J
on
T^{r}M
such that every map

\varphi_{\gamma}:\mathbb{C}\to TM
introduced earlier is holomorphic with respect to
J
when
\varphi_{\gamma}(\sigma+i\tau)\in T^{r}M
,
then
J
is called the
adapted complex structure
and

T^{r}M
is called a
Grauert tube
.
The case
r=\infty
is referred to as an
entire Grauert tube
.

Let

u:TM\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\qquad u(v)=\|v\|,

be the fiberwise norm function.
Lempert and Szőke
[
6
,
14
]
proved that
T^{r}M
is a Grauert tube

iff
the triple
\bigl(T^{r}M,M,u^{2}\bigr)
forms a

Monge–Ampère model
, that is:

(1)

T^{r}M
is a Stein manifold with

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}T^{r}M=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}M
and center

M=\{u=0\}
;

(2)

u^{2}
is a non-negative, strictly plurisubharmonic exhaustion of

T^{r}M
;

(3)

on
T^{r}M\setminus M
the function
u
satisfies the
homogeneous complex Monge–Ampère equation

\bigl(i\,\partial\bar{\partial}u\bigr)^{n}=0.

On any Grauert tube, the rank condition

\operatorname{rank}\partial\bar{\partial}u=n-1
gives rise to a
one-dimensional foliation on
T^{r}M\setminus M
, namely the
Monge–Ampère foliation
. Lempert and Szőke showed that this foliation coincides with the Riemann foliation on the Grauert tube. For comprehensive accounts, see

[
1
,
4
,
6
,
14
]
.

Remark 2.1

(Harmonicity of the norm function)
.

Although
u
is not harmonic on all of
TM
, along each leaf we have

\bigl(u\circ\varphi_{\gamma}\bigr)(\sigma+i\tau)=|\tau|,

which is harmonic on
\bigl(\mathbb{C}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\bigr)\setminus\bigl(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\bigr)
.

2.3
The space of oriented geodesics

Because
(M,g)
is Zoll, each leaf
\operatorname{im}\varphi_{\gamma}
is biholomorphic to the infinite cylinder.
Adding the two limit points

\infty_{\gamma}:=\lim_{\tau\to+\infty}\varphi_{\gamma}(\sigma+i\tau),\qquad 0_{\gamma}:=\lim_{\tau\to-\infty}\varphi_{\gamma}(\sigma+i\tau),

compactifies the leaf to a Riemann sphere.
The point
\infty_{\gamma}
corresponds to the oriented geodesic
\gamma
, while
0_{\gamma}
corresponds to the reversed geodesic
-\gamma
.
Define the
space of oriented geodesics

D\;=\;\{\infty_{\gamma},\,0_{\gamma}\mid\gamma\text{ is an oriented geodesic of }(M,g)\}.

Since
D
is the free quotient of the unit tangent bundle
UM
by the
S^{1}
–action generated by the geodesic flow,
it inherits a smooth manifold structure.

2.4
Projective algebraization
X
of
TM

By compactifying each leaf with the two limit points introduced in §
2.3
, we define

L_{\gamma}\;:=\;\varphi_{\gamma}\;\sqcup\;\{\infty_{\gamma},0_{\gamma}\},\qquad X\;:=\;TM\;\sqcup\;D.

Throughout we view
X
as the
projective algebraization
of
TM
. The name ”Projective algebraization” will be explained very soon.

2.5
An antiholomorphic involution
N_{-1}

Define a map

N_{-1}\colon X\longrightarrow X,\qquad N_{-1}(v)\;=\;-v\;\;(v\in TM),\quad N_{-1}(\infty_{\gamma})=0_{\gamma},\quad N_{-1}(0_{\gamma})=\infty_{\gamma}.

Restricted to each compactified leaf
L_{\gamma}\cong\mathbb{CP}^{1}
, the map
N_{-1}
is the complex conjugation, hence an antiholomorphic involution.

Remark 2.2
.

The involution
N_{-1}
preserves virtually all structures introduced so far:
its fixed‑point set is the zero section
M\subset X
, and it stabilizes every leaf as well as the divisor
D
.

2.6
Parallel vector fields

Let
\gamma
be a geodesic on
M
and
\{\gamma_{t}\}
a smooth one‑parameter family with

V:=\partial_{t}\gamma_{t}\!\mid_{t=0}

a Jacobi field along
\gamma
.
Setting

\zeta\;:=\;\partial_{t}L_{\gamma_{t}}\!\mid_{t=0},

we obtain a section
\zeta
of
TL_{\gamma}
, called the
parallel vector field
induced by
V
.
If
V
is parallel, then

V-iJV

is holomorphic; conversely, one can reconstruct the complex structure
J
of the entire Grauert tube from such vector fields - for detailed references, we recommend
[
6
,
14
]
.

A non‑trivial parallel vector field never vanishes on
X\setminus M
, including
\infty_{\gamma}
and
0_{\gamma}
. This will be crucial for estimating the normal bundle of each leaf
L_{\gamma}\cong\mathbb{CP}^{1}
.

2.7
Results of Burns–Leung

Let
(M,g)
be a Zoll manifold of period
\ell
whose tangent bundle is an entire Grauert tube.
By
[
3
]
and
[
9
]
, the structures above satisfy:

Proposition 2.3

(Burns–Leung)
.

(i)

The complex structure on
TM
extends smoothly to
X
, making
X
a compact complex manifold with
D
a codimension‑
1
submanifold.

(ii)

A Kähler potential

\rho\;=\;\log\!\bigl(1+\cosh(4\pi u/\ell)\bigr)

defines a Kähler metric on
TM
that extends to
X
.

(iii)

The line bundle
\mathcal{O}_{X}(D)
is positive; hence
D
is ample and
X
is a projective manifold.

(iv)

The map
N_{-1}
is an antiholomorphic involution of
X
.

3
Observations on
X

Characterizing the variety
X
constructed above forms the second step in the three-stage framework outlined in the Introduction for resolving the Main Problem, and is the program’s key component. For the two unsolved types,
\mathbb{HP}^{n}
and
\mathbb{OP}^{2}
, we first record several algebraic properties of
X
.

3.1
Cohomology of
X

One structural feature of
X
is that it admits a decomposition

X=TM\cup\bigl(X\!\setminus\!M\bigr),

to which the Mayer–Vietoris sequence applies. Topologically

TM\simeq M,\qquad X\!\setminus\!M\simeq D,\qquad TM\cap\bigl(X\!\setminus\!M\bigr)\simeq UM,

where
UM
is the unit tangent bundle of
M
.

Type convention.

We say that
M
is of
type
\mathbb{HP}^{n}
(respectively,
\mathbb{OP}^{2}
)
if it has the same cohomology ring as
\mathbb{HP}^{n}
(respectively,
\mathbb{OP}^{2}
), namely

H^{*}(\mathbb{HP}^{n})=\mathbb{Z}[z]/\!\langle z^{\,n+1}\rangle,\qquad H^{*}(\mathbb{OP}^{2})=\mathbb{Z}[z]/\!\langle z^{\,3}\rangle.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
(X,UM,D)=(X_{4,n},UM_{4,n},D_{4,n})
when
M
is of type
\mathbb{HP}^{n}
(so
a=4
),
and
(X,UM,D)=(X_{8,2},UM_{8,2},D_{8,2})
when
M
is of type
\mathbb{OP}^{2}
(so
a=8,\,n=2
).
Then:

(1)

For the unit tangent bundle
UM

H^{k}\!\bigl(UM_{a,n}\bigr)\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z},&a\mid k,\;0\leq k\leq an-1,\\
\mathbb{Z},&a\mid(k+1),\;an+1\leq k\leq 2an-1,\\
\mathbb{Z}_{\,n+1},&k=an,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2)

For the divisor
D

H^{k}\!\bigl(D_{a,n}\bigr)\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}^{\lfloor k/a\rfloor+1},&2\mid k,\;0\leq k\leq an-2,\\[2.0pt]
\mathbb{Z}^{\lfloor(2an-2-k)/a\rfloor+1},&2\mid k,\;an\leq k\leq 2an-2,\\[2.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(3)

For the projective algebraization
X

H^{k}\!\bigl(X_{a,n}\bigr)\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}^{\lfloor k/a\rfloor+1},&2\mid k,\;0\leq k\leq an,\\[2.0pt]
\mathbb{Z}^{\lfloor(2an-k)/a\rfloor+1},&2\mid k,\;an+2\leq k\leq 2an,\\[2.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In particular, for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,

H^{k}\!\bigl(X_{4,n}\bigr)\cong H^{k}\!\bigl(\mathrm{Gr}(2,2n+2)\bigr),\qquad H^{k}\!\bigl(X_{8,2}\bigr)\cong H^{k}\!\bigl(E_{6}/P_{1}\bigr).

Proof.

During this proof, we abbreviate

UM_{a,n}=UM,\qquad D_{a,n}=D,\qquad X_{a,n}=X.

Step 1. Two sphere fibrations for
UM
.

The unit tangent bundle admits the two sphere fibrations

S^{an-1}\;\hookrightarrow\;UM\xrightarrow{\;\tau\;}M,\qquad S^{a-1}\;\hookrightarrow\;UM\xrightarrow{\;\pi\;}D.

The homotopy long exact sequence for the first fibration gives

\pi_{1}\!\bigl(S^{an-1}\bigr)=0\;\longrightarrow\;\pi_{1}(UM)\;\longrightarrow\;\pi_{1}(M)=0,

so
\pi_{1}(UM)=0
.
Applying the same argument to the second fibration yields

\pi_{1}(UM)=0\;\longrightarrow\;\pi_{1}(D)\;\longrightarrow\;\pi_{0}\!\bigl(S^{a-1}\bigr)=0,

hence
\pi_{1}(D)=0
.
Because both sphere bundles have simply connected base and total space,
the Gysin exact sequence is available in each case.

Step 2. Cohomology of
UM
.

For the fibration
\tau\colon UM\to M
the Gysin sequence reads

H^{k-an}(M)\;\longrightarrow\;H^{k}(M)\;\longrightarrow\;H^{k}(UM)\;\longrightarrow\;H^{k-an+1}(M)\;\longrightarrow\;H^{k+1}(M).

•

For
0\leq k\leq an-2
, from the first four terms, we obtain
H^{k}(UM)\cong H^{k}(M)
.

•

For
an+1\leq k\leq 2an-1
, from the last four terms, we have
H^{k}(UM)\cong H^{k-an+1}(M)
.

For the critical degrees
k=an-1,\,an
,

H^{an-1}(M)\;\longrightarrow\;H^{an-1}(UM)\;\longrightarrow\;H^{0}(M)\xrightarrow{\;\cup\,\chi\;}H^{an}(M)\;\longrightarrow\;H^{an}(UM)\;\longrightarrow\;H^{1}(M),

where
\chi
is the Euler class of
\tau
.
Because
H^{an-1}(M)=H^{1}(M)=0
,

H^{an-1}(UM)
and
H^{an}(UM)
are the kernel and cokernel, respectively,
of the cup‑product map

H^{0}(M)\xrightarrow{\cup\,\chi}H^{an}(M)
.
Since
M
is of type
\mathbb{HP}^{n}
(respectively,
\mathbb{OP}^{2}
),
this map is multiplication by
n+1
.
Therefore

H^{an-1}(UM)=0,\qquad H^{an}(UM)\cong\mathbb{Z}_{\,n+1}.

Step 3. Cohomology of
D
.

Apply the Gysin sequence to the sphere bundle

\pi\colon UM\to D
:

\displaystyle H^{k+1}(D)\;\longrightarrow\;H^{k+1}(UM)\;\longrightarrow\;H^{k}(D)\;\longrightarrow\;H^{k+2}(D)\;\longrightarrow\;H^{k+2}(UM)\;\longrightarrow\;H^{k+1}(D).

We argue by induction on
k
using the Gysin sequence.

•

Odd degrees, first range.

An induction from
k=-1
to
k=an-1
that involves only the
third, fourth, and fifth
terms of the sequence shows
H^{k}(D)=0
.

•

Odd degrees, second range.

A similar induction from
k=2an-1
to
k=an+1
based on the
second, third, and fourth
terms again yields
H^{k}(D)=0
.

•

Even degrees.

For even
k
we perform two inductions:

•

Starting from the
H^{0}(D)=\mathbb{Z}
and using the
last five
terms,
we have
H^{k+2}(D)\cong H^{k}(D)\oplus H^{k+2}(UM)
for
k\leq an+a-2
.

•

Again, starting from the
H^{2an}(D)=0
and using the
first five
terms,
we have
H^{k}(D)\cong H^{k+2}(D)\oplus H^{k+1}(UM)
for
k\geq an+a
.

Combining these inductions over the odd and even degrees gives precisely the cohomology groups claimed in Proposition
3.1
.

Step 4. Cohomology of
X
.

Consider the Mayer–Vietoris sequence for the open cover

X=(TM)\cup(X\setminus M)
:

H^{k-1}(UM)\xrightarrow{\;\delta\;}H^{k}(X)\longrightarrow H^{k}(M)\oplus H^{k}(D)\xrightarrow{\;j\;}H^{k}(UM)\xrightarrow{\;\delta\;}H^{k+1}(X).

(i) Degrees
0\leq k\leq an
.

The map
j
is surjective because

\tau^{*}\colon H^{k}(M)\to H^{k}(UM)
is surjective.
Hence
\delta=0
and
H^{k}(X)
is the kernel of the map
j
.

(ii) Degrees
an+2\leq k\leq 2an
.

Using the shifted Mayer–Vietoris sequence

H^{k}(M)\oplus H^{k-1}(D)\xrightarrow{\;j\;}H^{k-1}(UM)\longrightarrow H^{k}(X)\longrightarrow H^{k}(M)\oplus H^{k}(D)\longrightarrow H^{k}(UM),

we distinguish two cases.

Odd
k
.

Here
H^{k-1}(UM)=0
and
H^{k}(M)\oplus H^{k}(D)=0
,
so
H^{k}(X)=0
.

Even
k
.

Now
H^{k-1}(D)=0
,
H^{k}(M)=0
(since
k\geq an+2
),
and
H^{k}(UM)=0
,
therefore

H^{k}(X)\cong H^{k-1}(UM)\oplus H^{k}(D)
.

(iii) Degree
k=an+1
.

Because
j
is surjective and

H^{an+1}(M)\oplus H^{an+1}(D)=0
,
we have
H^{an+1}(X)=0
.

Combining (i)–(iii) with the computations of
H^{*}(UM)
and
H^{*}(D)

established earlier completes the proof of Proposition
3.1
.
∎

3.2
X
is Fano

For a Zoll manifold of type
\mathbb{HP}^{n}
or
\mathbb{OP}^{2}
we already know that

H^{2}(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}
.
By comparing cocycle representatives and using Morse–index data
one can show that
X
is a Fano variety of Picard number
1
.
This opens the way to applying the rich theory of Fano varieties
to our main problem. We state the result precisely and then prove it.

Theorem 3.2
.

For
X_{a,n}
as in Proposition
3.1
we have

-K_{X_{a,n}}\;=\;\mathcal{O}_{X_{a,n}}\!\bigl(\tfrac{a(n+1)}{2}\,D\bigr).

In particular,
X_{a,n}
is a Fano variety with Picard number
1
.

Proof.

Throughout the proof write
X=X_{a,n}
.
Because
H^{2}(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}
, let
\alpha\in H^{2}(X;\mathbb{Z})

be a positive generator. We begin by showing
\alpha=[D]
.

Step 1. Identifying the generator of
H^{2}(X)
.

From Proposition
3.1
we have the fragment

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{0}(UM)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{2}(D)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{2}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\chi}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cup\,\chi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i^{*}}

where
i\colon D\hookrightarrow X
is inclusion,
and
\chi=e(UM)=e(N_{D/X})
is the Euler class of the sphere bundle

\pi\colon UM\to D
.
Because
UM
is (up to isomorphism) the normal bundle of
D
in
X
,
the Thom isomorphism implies

i^{*}\alpha=\chi=e(N_{D/X})=c_{1}\!\bigl(\mathcal{O}_{X}(D)\!\mid_{D}\bigr)=i^{*}[D].

The map
i^{*}\colon H^{2}(X)\to H^{2}(D)
is an isomorphism,
so
\alpha=[D]
.
Thus for some integer
r

-K_{X}=\mathcal{O}_{X}(rD).

Step 2. Determining
r
.

Fix a closed geodesic
\gamma
on
M
and let

L:=L_{\gamma}\cong\mathbb{CP}^{1}\subset X
be its compactified leaf.
By the adjunction formula

K_{L}=K_{X}\!\mid_{L}+\det N_{L/X}.

Because
L\cong\mathbb{CP}^{1}
,

K_{L}\cong\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{CP}^{1}}(-2)
.
Since
L
meets
D
transversally in exactly two points,

K_{X}\!\mid_{L}=\mathcal{O}_{L}(-rD)=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{CP}^{1}}(-2r).

Lemma 3.3
.

For every leaf
L_{\gamma}
we have

\det N_{L_{\gamma}/X}\;\cong\;\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{CP}^{1}}\!\bigl(an+a-2\bigr).

Proof.

By the Bott–Samelson theorem
the Morse index of any closed geodesic on
M_{a,n}
is
a-1
. Therefore, there exist
an-1
normal Jacobi fields

V_{1},\dots,V_{an-1}

along
\gamma
satisfying:

(a)

Each
V_{k}
vanishes at the initial point of
\gamma
.

(b)

During one full period of
\gamma
there are exactly
a-1

conjugate points (counted with multiplicities);
at each such point a number of the
V_{k}
equal to the
multiplicity vanish, while the remaining fields remain linearly
independent.

(c)

At all other points the collection
\{V_{k}\}
is linearly
independent.

Extend every
V_{k}
to a parallel vector field
\zeta_{k}
(cf. §
2.6
) on the compactified leaf
L_{\gamma}
.
Recall that a parallel vector field can vanish only along the real line

M\subset L_{\gamma}
.
Hence the wedge

\zeta_{1}^{1,0}\wedge\cdots\wedge\zeta_{an-1}^{1,0}

determines a holomorphic section of

\det N_{L_{\gamma}/X}

whose total vanishing order is

(an-1)+(a-1)=an+a-2
.
This proves the claim.

∎

Combining the adjunction formula with Lemma
3.3
,

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{CP}^{1}}(-2)\;=\;\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{CP}^{1}}(-2r)+\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{CP}^{1}}(an+a-2),

so
-2=-2r+(an+a-2)
and therefore

r=\tfrac{a(n+1)}{2}
.

Step 3. Fano property.

Since
r>0
, the anticanonical line bundle
-K_{X}
is ample, making

X
a Fano variety. Because
H^{2}(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}
,
the Picard number is
\rho(X)=1
.
∎

3.3
Degree of
X

If a Zoll manifold is
homeomorphic
(not just a type) to
\mathbb{HP}^{n}
or
\mathbb{OP}^{2}
, then it is known to have the volume of the corresponding model. More precisely, Weinstein
[
16
]
proved that for any Zoll manifold
M
of period
2\pi
, there is an
integer

i(M)
which satisfies

i(M)\;:=\;\frac{\operatorname{Vol}(M)}{\operatorname{Vol}(S^{n})}\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\,\langle\chi^{\,n-1},[D]\rangle.

Here, we call
i(M)
as
Weinstein number
. Reznikov
[
13
]
showed that
i(M)=i\!\bigl(M_{\mathrm{Model}}\bigr)
when
M
is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{HP}^{n}
or
\mathbb{OP}^{2}
.
Because
D
is very ample, this relation forces the projective degree of
X
to equal that of the model variety
X_{\mathrm{Model}}
(namely
\,\mathrm{Gr}(2,2n+2)
or
E_{6}/P_{1}
):

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
M
be a Zoll manifold with entire Grauert tube and period
2\pi
. If it is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{HP}^{n}
or
\mathbb{OP}^{2}
, then we have

\deg X\;=\;\deg\bigl(X_{\mathrm{Model}}\bigr).

Proof.

Using Poincaré duality and the theorems above, we obtain:

\displaystyle\deg X

\displaystyle=D^{n}\;=\;\int_{X}c_{1}(D)^{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{X}c_{1}(D)^{n-1}\wedge\operatorname{PD}(D)\;=\;\int_{D}c_{1}(D)^{\,n-1}

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle\chi^{\,n-1},[D]\bigr\rangle\;=\;2\,i(M)\;=\;2\,i\!\bigl(M_{\mathrm{Model}}\bigr)\;=\;\deg\bigl(X_{\mathrm{Model}}\bigr).

∎

Remark 3.5
.

Note from the proof that
\deg X=2\,i(M)
.
Hence, with the stronger assumption, i.e.
M\cong\mathbb{HP}^{n}
or
\mathbb{OP}^{2}
,

\deg X_{4,n}\;=\;2\,i\!\bigl(\mathbb{HP}^{n}\bigr)=\frac{2}{\,2n+1\,}\binom{4n-1}{2n-1},\qquad\deg X_{8,2}\;=\;2\,i\!\bigl(\mathbb{OP}^{2}\bigr)=78.

4
Main Result for the type
\mathbb{HP}^{2}

Using the structural data obtained in the preceding sections, we can now resolve the
Main Problem
when the Zoll manifold
M
is of type
\mathbb{HP}^{2}
.
Recall that for
M
of this type the projective algebraization
X
is a Picard‑number‑one Fano variety of
\dim X_{4,2}=8,~\operatorname{index}(X_{4,2})=6~(\text{coindex }=3)
. By Mukai’s classification of coindex‑
3
Fano manifolds
[
11
]
, it should be one of the sextic double cover of
\mathbb{CP}^{8}
, quartic double cover of
Q^{8}
, or Grassmannian
Gr(2,6)
. Because of the cohomology group of
X
, we obtain:

Lemma 4.1
.

There is an isomorphism of varieties

X_{4,2}\;\cong\;\mathrm{Gr}(2,6).

Proof of Theorem 1.1.

Let
i^{\prime}\colon X\!\to\!\mathrm{Gr}(2,6)
be the isomorphism from Lemma
4.1
.
Then
i^{\prime}(D)
is a smooth very ample divisor on
\mathrm{Gr}(2,6)
.
Because the Plücker embedding is projectively normal,
i^{\prime}(D)
is the hyperplane section cut out by a single linear form on
\mathbb{CP}^{14}
.
Linear forms on the Plücker space correspond to skew‑symmetric
2
‑forms on
\mathbb{C}^{6}
, whose ranks can only be
2,4,
or
6
.
Within each fixed rank the conjugacy action of
\mathrm{PGL}(6)
is transitive, and the only rank yielding a nonsingular hyperplane section is rank
6
.
Hence there exists
A\!\in\!\mathrm{PGL}(6)
such that

i:=A\circ i^{\prime}\colon X\longrightarrow\mathrm{Gr}(2,6)

maps
D
to the standard rank‑
6
divisor

D_{\mathrm{std}}\;=\;\Bigl\{\,[\zeta]\in\mathrm{Gr}(2,6)\mid\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}\zeta_{2i-1,\,2i}=0\Bigr\}.

Next, according to Leung, conjugacy classes of anti‑holomorphic involutions on
\mathrm{Gr}(2,6)
are classified by their fixed‑point sets (real forms)
[
7
, Th. 1.1]
.
For
\mathrm{Gr}(2,6)
the only possibilities are the real Grassmannian and
\mathbb{HP}^{2}

[
8
, Th. 3.4]
.
Define
\tau:=i\circ N_{-1}\circ i^{-1}
, then
\tau
is an anti‑holomorphic involution preserving
D_{\mathrm{std}}
and whose fixed locus is
i(M)
, which has the same cohomology as
\mathbb{HP}^{2}
.
Consequently, for
B\in\mathrm{PGL}(6)
and the canonical quatornionic involution
J
,
\tau=B\circ J\circ B^{-1}
.
Because
\tau
fixes
D_{\mathrm{std}}
,
B\in\mathrm{PGSp}(6)
.
Setting
I:=B^{-1}\!\circ i
, we obtain an isomorphism

I\colon X\xrightarrow{\;\sim\;}\mathrm{Gr}(2,6)

such that

I(D)=D_{\mathrm{std}},\qquad I\circ N_{-1}\circ I^{-1}=J,\qquad I(M)=\mathbb{HP}^{2}.

In particular,
M
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{HP}^{2}
.

Let
u\colon X\setminus D\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be the vector–norm function coming from the Zoll metric on
M
, and let

u_{0}
be the canonical norm on

\mathrm{Gr}(2,6)\setminus D_{\mathrm{std}}\cong T\mathbb{HP}^{2}
,
pulled back to
X\setminus D
via the isomorphism

I\colon X\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathrm{Gr}(2,6)
.
We show that
u=u_{0}
, which will upgrade the diffeomorphism

I\!\mid_{M}\colon M\to\mathbb{HP}^{2}
to an isometry.

Explicit formula for
u_{0}
.

According to Patrizio and Wong
[
12
]
, in Plücker coordinates
\zeta=(\zeta_{a,b})_{1\leq a<b\leq 6}
,

N(\zeta):=\frac{\sum_{a<b}\!\bigl|\zeta_{a,b}\bigr|^{2}}{\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{3}\zeta_{2i-1,\,2i}\Bigr|^{2}},\qquad u_{0}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}\cosh^{-1}\!\bigl(2N(\zeta)-1\bigr)

Step 1: Asymptotics of
u
on a leaf.

Fix a compactified leaf
L_{\gamma}\cong\mathbb{CP}^{1}
and put the
cylindrical coordinate
z=e^{\sigma+i\tau}
on

L_{\gamma}\setminus\{0_{\gamma},\infty_{\gamma}\}\cong\mathbb{C}^{\times}
,
so
z=0
corresponds to the point
0_{\gamma}=L_{\gamma}\cap D
and

z=\infty
to
\infty_{\gamma}=L_{\gamma}\cap D
.
Along the leaf we have

u\bigl(e^{\sigma+i\tau}\bigr)=|\tau|\;\Longrightarrow\;u(z)=-\log|z|+o(1)\quad\text{as }z\to 0,\;\infty,

and
u\!\mid_{L_{\gamma}}
is harmonic on
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
.

Step 2: Asymptotics of
u_{0}
on the same leaf.

Because
u_{0}
is a plurisubharmonic exhaustion of
X\setminus D
,
its restriction to
L_{\gamma}
is subharmonic, vanishing on

M\cap L_{\gamma}
and diverging at the two intersection points with
D
.
Using the explicit formula above and the fact that

L_{\gamma}
meets
D
transversely, one finds likewise

u_{0}(z)=-\log|z|+o(1)\quad\text{as }z\to 0,\;\infty.

Step 3: A subharmonic difference.

Set
f:=u_{0}-u
.
Then
f
is subharmonic on
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, bounded,
and satisfies
f(e^{i\theta})=0
for every
\theta

(because
|z|=1
corresponds to
\tau=0
).

Let
M(r):=\max_{|z|=r}f(z)
.
Hadamard’s three‑circles theorem gives, for

0<r_{1}<r<r_{2}
,

M(r)\leq\frac{\log r_{2}-\log r}{\log r_{2}-\log r_{1}}\,M(r_{1})+\frac{\log r-\log r_{1}}{\log r_{2}-\log r_{1}}\,M(r_{2}).

Sending
(r_{1},r_{2})\to(0,1)
shows
M(r)\leq 0
for
0<r<1
,
whereas
(r_{1},r_{2})\to(1,\infty)
gives
M(r)\leq 0
for
r>1
.
Since
f(e^{i\theta})=0
, the maximum principle forces
f\equiv 0
on

\mathbb{C}^{\times}
; hence
u_{0}=u
on the entire leaf.

Step 4: Global equality and isometry.

As the leaves of the Riemann foliation cover
X\setminus D
,
we conclude
u_{0}=u
everywhere.
Equality of the norm functions implies the equality of the underlying
Riemannian metrics, so the real form
I(M)
is
isometric
to
\mathbb{HP}^{2}
.
