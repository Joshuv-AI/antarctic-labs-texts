The impact of Internet usage on cognitive impairment among Chinese older people: a social participation perspective
Authors: Yuanping Pan, Zhiyong Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInformation and communication technologies (ICTs) play a significant role in maintaining cognitive function among older people. However, there is limited research on the association between Internet usage and cognitive function. This study aimed to investigate the association between diverse Internet usage patterns and cognitive function, as well as the underlying mechanisms, in Chinese older people.MethodsWe analysed data from 8,349 respondents enrolled in the 2020 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Ordinary Least Square (OLS) regression was performed to examine the effects of diverse Internet usage purposes and access devices on cognitive function among older people. The propensity score matching (PSM) was employed to estimate the average treatment effect of Internet usage on cognitive performance and to validate the robustness of the findings.ResultsInternet usage was significantly associated with higher cognitive scores among older people in China (β = 1.74, 95% CI 1.51–1.97). Specifically, watching news (β = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.28–0.64) and playing games (β = 0.11, 95% CI: 0.01–0.23) were positively associated with cognitive scores. Accessing the internet via desktop computers (β = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.06–1.21) and mobile phones (β = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.05–0.59) was also associated with better cognitive scores compared with non-users. Social participation partially mediated the association between Internet usage and cognitive function. The positive effects of Internet usage on cognitive function were more pronounced among urban residents, females, and those aged 71 to 80.ConclusionsInternet usage plays a crucial role in maintaining cognitive function in older people. Targeted efforts to improve Internet access and usage, as well as enhance social participation, may significantly promote cognitive health among older people.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06846-0.Keywords: Internet usage, Cognitive function, Social participation, Older people, CHARLS

Introduction

As life expectancy among older people continues to increase, maintaining cognitive health in this population has become increasingly urgent [1]. Globally, these age-related demographic changes have contributed to a rising prevalence of cognitive impairment [2]. Cognitive impairment frequently leads to disability and a loss of self-care abilities in older people [3, 4]. Preserving cognitive function is a critical component of successful aging and can substantially reduce healthcare expenditures for both individuals and society [5].

With the growing popularity of the Internet, a rising number of older people are beginning to use it. In Europe, the proportion of individuals aged 65–74 who used the Internet increased from 27% in 2010 to 49% in 2016 [6]. In China, individuals aged 60 and above accounted for 14.3% of all Internet users in 2023.The widespread adoption of the Internet among older people presents an opportunity to support better cognitive functioning [7]. Cognition is a higher-order brain function influenced by multiple factors, including age, genetics, and the living environment [8]. Prior studies have identified several age-related changes in the brain, such as alterations in gray and white matter, neurochemical shifts, reduced blood flow, and synaptic degeneration [6]. Consequently, the speed at which the cognitive system processes information tends to decline with age, leading to a general downward trend in cognitive function for most individuals [9]. Emerging evidence suggests that Internet usage may contribute to maintaining cognitive health in older people by providing cognitive stimulation through diverse online activities [10]. Engagement in brain-stimulating games and structured cognitive training exercises may attenuate cognitive decline [6]. According to the cognitive reserve hypothesis, increased experiential resources are associated with enhanced cognitive function [11]. Therefore, Internet usage may contribute to building cognitive reserve, thereby improving cognitive performance.

Social participation refers to individuals’ involvement in activities that facilitate interactions within social settings and shared spaces, and is recognized as a key component of healthy aging [12]. Positive social engagement can alleviate loneliness and depression in older people, which in turn may slow the decline in cognitive function [13]. The use of Information and Communication Technologies (ICTs) can overcome temporal and spatial barriers, enhance communication with family and friends, and foster social engagement [14]. A randomized controlled trial study found that community-dwelling older people who used a mobile application significantly increased their frequency of participation in social activities [15]. The association between social engagement and cognitive functioning has been widely examined [16]. Evidence from low- and middle-income countries demonstrated that social participation significantly reduces the risk of cognitive impairment among individuals aged 50 and above [17]. Additionally, a systematic review found that social participation improves cognitive functioning in middle-aged and older populations [18]. However, few studies have explored the role of social participation in the association between Internet usage and cognitive impairment.

Internet usage encompasses a variety of activities, primarily categorized into recreational, social, and instrumental uses [19]. These different types of Internet usage may have distinct effects on cognitive function. Empirical evidence suggests that using smartphones to watching news is significantly associated with better cognitive performance among middle-aged and older people [20]. Smartphones have contributed to digital inclusion, but they do not a complete substitute for traditional computers with physical keyboards and larger displays [21]. Another study reported that individuals who use computers have a lower risk of developing dementia compared with non-users [22]. However, limited research has examined how these diverse forms of Internet usage specifically affect cognitive function in older people [20]. Understanding the association between different types of Internet access devices and cognitive function can help inform more targeted strategies in network infrastructure development aimed at promoting cognitive health.

Given the significant digital divide in China, which is characterized by disparities across urban and rural areas, genders, and age groups, this study takes these inequalities into consideration. We further explore the association between Internet usage and cognitive functioning among older people with diverse demographic characteristics. In particular, urban areas generally demonstrate higher levels of informatisation compared with rural regions [23]. Urban residents are typically more educated, leading to better digital literacy and technological proficiency, enabling them to use digital devices more effectively to access information. Therefore, conducting stratified analyses based on urban versus rural residency can offer deeper insights into how Internet usage impacts the cognitive function of older people.

Therefore, this study investigated the impact of different Internet usage patterns among Chinese older people on their cognitive functions. The mediating role of social participation in the association between Internet usage and cognitive functions was examined. To account for potential confounding by sociodemographic factors, we performed a stratified analysis by place of residence, gender, and age groups to examine this association.

Methods

We used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative survey of middle-aged and older people aged 45 years and above in China. The CHARLS baseline survey covered 150 district units, 450 village units, and approximately 10,000 households, employing a multistage sampling approach to assess the health, social, and economic conditions of community residents. All data were collected by trained CHARLS interviewers through face-to-face, computer-assisted personal interviews. The purpose of the survey is to evaluate the social, economic, and health status of the middle-aged and older population in China. Details regarding the study design and sampling methodology have been reported previously [24]. To ensure methodological consistency in the measurement of cognitive function, we used data from the 2020 wave of CHARLS, which is the most recent dataset available and can be accessed through the official website (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/). In accordance with World Health Organization criteria, older people were defined as individuals aged 60 years and above [25]. We excluded participants under 60 years of age and those with missing values for any study variables. The final analytical sample comprised 8,349 observations (Fig. 1). All study participants provided informed consent before they participated in CHARLS. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University, with Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval number IRB00001052-11015. All study procedures were conducted in compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Dependent variable

Two key components of cognitive function were measured: episodic memory and mental intactness [26]. Cognitive functioning was assessed using the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS), word recall tests, and graphic drawing tests [27]. Episodic memory was evaluated through the word recall tests. In this assessment, interviewers read a list of 10 Chinese nouns, and respondents were asked to repeat the words in any order immediately (immediate recall) and again after a four-minute delay (delayed recall). The episodic memory score was calculated as the average of the immediate and delayed recall scores, with a possible range from 0 to 10.

Measures of mental status included orientation, computation, and drawing. The orientation dimension consisted of questions regarding the year, month, date, day of the week, and season, with one point awarded for each correct response, yielding a maximum score of 5. For the computation task, participants were instructed to subtract 7 from 100 five times consecutively, earning one point for each correct answer, leading to a total of five points. In the drawing task, interviewers showed participants a figure consisting of two overlapping pentagram stars to evaluate their drawing abilities; a correct drawing yielded one point. The total cognitive score was calculated by summing the episodic memory and mental status scores, with a range from 0 to 21. Higher scores indicate better cognitive function.

Independent variable

In the survey, respondents were asked whether they had accessed the Internet in the past month. The answers included activities such as chatting, watching news, viewing videos, playing games, managing finances, and other online activities. Participants were also asked about the devices used for Internet access, which included desktop computers, laptops, tablet computers, mobile phones, and other devices. These responses were treated as a dichotomous variable, coded as “yes = 1” and “no = 0.”

Mediating variable

Social participation was measured by asking respondents whether they had engaged in any of the following activities during the past month: (1) visiting or socializing with friends; (2) playing mahjong, chess, cards, or participating in community room activities; (3) providing assistance to non-cohabiting relatives, friends, or neighbors; (4) participating in activities such as dancing, fitness, or practicing kung fu; (5) joining clubs and organizations; (6) engaging in volunteering or charitable activities; and (7) attending school or training courses. One point was assigned for each activity participated in, yielding a maximum score of seven points. Higher scores indicated a greater level of social participation.

Control variables

Referring to previous research, we controlled for the following variables: gender (male/female), age (60–70/71–80/>80), marital status (single/partnered), and educational attainment (primary school and below/middle school/high school and above). Additional control variables included place of residence (rural/city), regional classification (eastern/central/western), health insurance coverage (yes/no), smoking status (yes/no), alcohol consumption (yes/no), depression status (yes/no), number of chronic diseases (none/1/2/≥3), and activity of daily living (ADL) limitations (yes/no) [20].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations (SD), and categorical variables were reported as percentages. Differences in baseline characteristics between Internet users and non-users were assessed using t-tests for continuous variables and Pearson chi-square tests for categorical variables. Prior to conducting Ordinary Least Square (OLS) regression, multicollinearity diagnostics were performed, indicating that all variables had variance inflation factors (VIFs) below 10. We then employed OLS regression to examine the association between Internet usage and cognitive function among older people. To address potential endogeneity arising from self-selection and unobserved confounders, Propensity Score Matching (PSM) was applied to estimate the average treatment effect of Internet usage on cognitive performance. Furthermore, to explore how specific purposes of Internet usage and types of access devices relate to cognitive function, these factors were included as key independent variables in the regression model, with adjustments made for all covariates. Mediation analyses using the Bootstrap method (5,000 resamples) were conducted to assess whether social participation mediates the relationship between Internet usage and cognitive function. An indirect effect was considered statistically significant if the corresponding confidence interval did not include zero. To further explore potential reverse mediation, we also examined a model in which Internet usage served as a mediator in the association between social participation and cognitive function. Lastly, to better understand the differential effects of Internet usage across subgroups of older people, stratified analyses were performed by urban-rural residence, geographic region, gender, and age group. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA version 14.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas, USA).

Results

Table 1 summarizes the sociodemographic and health-related characteristics of the participants, stratified by Internet usage. The majority of participants were aged 60–70 years (70.80%), female (49.83%), married or partnered (79.63%), and living in rural areas (65.00%). Most participants had attained primary school education or below (71.49%) and were covered by health insurance (95.23%). Compared with non-users, Internet users were significantly more likely to be younger (aged 60–70), male, partnered, and urban residents (all P < 0.001). Higher education levels were also more common among Internet users, with 28.47% having completed high school or above, compared with only 5.24% among non-users (P < 0.001). In addition, Internet users had better functional status (81.55% vs. 70.83% without ADL limitations; P < 0.001), were less likely to report depressive symptoms (29.06% vs. 44.88%; P < 0.001), and had significantly higher cognitive function scores (mean = 13.08 vs. 9.34; P < 0.001).

Table 7 presents the results of subgroup analyses examining the association between Internet usage and cognitive function across different residential locations, gender, and age groups. Internet usage was significantly associated with higher cognitive function among both rural (β = 1.71, P < 0.001) and urban (β = 1.79, P < 0.001) older people, with a slightly stronger association observed in urban areas. Discussion

With the increasing prevalence of Internet usage among older people, it is essential to investigate its impact on their cognitive health and the underlying mechanisms. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to examine the association between Internet usage and cognitive function among Chinese older people, and to explore the mediating role of social participation through mediation analysis. Our findings indicate that Internet usage is positively associated with better cognitive function in this population.

Our findings indicate that Chinese older people who use the Internet exhibit significantly better cognitive function than those who do not, consistent with previous studies [28]. This association may be attributed to the cognitive stimulation provided by Internet usage, which has been linked to increased cortical thickness in the frontal temporal lobe-related cortex [29]. Older people who use the Internet to watch news or play games tend to have higher cognitive scores. This is consistent with research [20]. In contrast, no statistically significant associations were observed between Internet usage for chatting, watching videos, or financial management and cognitive function. This may be due to the varying levels of cognitive engagement required by different online activities. For instance, watching news or playing games may involve more active cognitive processing than chatting or passively watching videos. Additionally, the limited sample size for certain activities, such as the fact that only 3.87% of older people reported using the Internet for financial management, may have attenuated potential associations.

Internet usage may create a cognitively enriching environment that supports cognitive function in later life [30]. According to the cognitive reserve hypothesis, engaging in mentally stimulating activities can enhance cognitive function by promoting neurotransmitter release and mitigating cognitive decline [31, 32]. The acquisition of new information through Internet usage may strengthen cognitive reserve, enabling the brain to recruit more efficient cognitive networks to resist dementia [33]. Given the positive association between Internet usage and cognitive function, these findings carry important implications for both individual well-being and public health. The rapid expansion of Internet access among older people may yield modest yet widespread cognitive benefits across this population [7].

In our study, older people who used desktop computers and mobile phones had better cognitive function. Individuals who access the Internet solely through mobile phones tend to have lower digital skills and engage in a narrower range of online activities compared to those who also use desktop computers [21]. With the advancement of ICTs, many Internet devices have become increasingly multifunctional and complex, posing usability challenges for older people [34]. Therefore, in order to make it more convenient for older people to use the Internet to obtain cognitive benefits, it is necessary to design Internet devices that are more in line with the needs of older users [35]. In addition, the digital literacy of older people should be improved, and they should be encouraged to try a variety of Internet devices to obtain more online resources and improve their cognitive function.

Social participation partially mediates the association between Internet usage and cognitive function. Internet usage is associated with higher levels of social participation, which in turn enhances cognitive functioning in older people [36, 37]. Consistent with previous findings, active social participation facilitated by Internet usage has been linked to improved cognitive function [8]. Through online social networking, older people can expand their social networks, strengthen interpersonal connections, and participate more easily in various social activities [38]. In addition, the development of portable mobile online social networks further increases the opportunities for socializing and reduces social isolation, thereby protecting cognitive function of older people [39, 40]. Overall, communication and interaction with others foster social connectedness, expand social networks, and ultimately reduce the risk of cognitive decline.

Previous research has often employed social participation as a mediating variable to demonstrate the positive effects of Internet usage [41, 42]. However, few studies have examined the reverse relationship by treating Internet usage as a mediator when investigating the effects of social participation. To enhance the robustness of our findings, we conducted a reverse mediation effect analysis, treating Internet usage as the mediating variable between social participation and cognitive impairment. The results indicate that the mediating effect of this path was also significant (Table S1). These findings suggest that, on the one hand, the Internet as a tool can help older people maintain and expand their social participation, thereby promoting cognitive function. On the other hand, older people who actively engage in social activities may be more inclined to use the Internet to maintain social connections, and Internet usage itself may directly stimulate cognitive functioning.

Older people living in urban areas appear to benefit more from Internet usage than their rural counterparts [43]. Those in urban areas may be more familiar with the workings of the Internet and thus use it more easily [44]. Consequently, targeted measures aimed at enhancing the convenience of Internet usage and boosting digital literacy could significantly promote cognitive health through Internet engagement. From a gender perspective, Internet usage may be especially beneficial for cognitive function in older women. A possible explanation is that older women are more likely to derive emotional value from online activities such as communication and shopping, thereby enhancing cognitive outcomes. Internet usage was significantly associated with higher cognitive scores among individuals aged 60–70 and 71–80, but not among those aged 80 and above. This may be due to more frequent Internet usage and greater exposure to online information among the younger age groups, resulting in more pronounced cognitive benefits. These findings could help policymakers take targeted measures to utilize the Internet to maintain cognitive function among older people.

Despite the contributions discussed above, this study has several limitations. First, given the cross-sectional nature of our study design, we cannot establish causal relationships among Internet usage, social participation, and cognitive function. Longitudinal studies are needed to verify the temporal sequence and potential causal pathways among these variables. Second, the participants in this study were all from China, the results of this study should be generalized to other countries or regions with caution. In this paper, we performed additional robustness checks to guarantee the reliability of the results. Nonetheless, there may be other omitted factors that could have contributed to the variation in results. Third, since the internet usage data for this study were obtained from participants’ self-reports, recall bias may have occurred. Finally, due to data limitations, we were unable to analyse the duration of Internet usage, which may also influence cognitive outcomes.

Conclusions

In summary, this study provides novel evidence on the association between Internet usage and cognitive functioning among older people in China, emphasizing the mediating role of social participation in this association. Internet usage frequently encompasses various cognitively stimulating activities, which can be instrumental in mitigating cognitive decline among older people. Therefore, leveraging Internet usage emerges as a crucial strategy for cognitive intervention. Based on these findings, proactive interventions must be implemented promptly to enhance cognitive function in older people.
