Heterogeneity and stability of patterns of post-traumatic stress symptoms and sleep quality: evidence from one year longitudinal study
Authors: Suhui Cheng, Jingye Zhan, Huiliang Gan, Zhilei Shang, Yanpu Jia, Wenjie Yan, Weizhi Liu, Geyu Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aimed to explore the heterogeneity of psychological states among civilians during the COVID-19 pandemic and the patterns of change over time. The focus was on Post-Traumatic Stress Symptoms (PTSS) and sleep quality.MethodsThe PCL-5 and a simplified Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index were utilized to measure PTSS and sleep quality. Initial data collection in February 2021 yielded 542 responses, with a follow-up 11 months later capturing 329 valid responses. Latent Profile Analysis identified psychological response heterogeneity, while Latent Transition Analysis and variance analysis explored subgroup changes. Chi-square and regression analyses examined the influence of gender on classification and transitions.ResultsThree stable subgroups of PTSS and sleep quality were identified: Healthy (T1 10.94%, T2 12.77%), At Risk (T1 58.97%, T2 42.55%), and PTSD (T1 30.09%, T2 44.68%). Variance analysis showed significant differences in PTSS and sleep quality among the three subgroups at T2. LTA indicated the probabilities of subgroup members remaining in their original group were 0.69, 0.84, and 0.67, respectively, with women more likely to be classified in the PTSD group and to transition to it.DiscussionThe study identified three distinct psychological patterns of sleep and PTSS, which were found to be stable and persistent. However, the analysis of changes revealed that individuals are more susceptible to shifting towards a moderate risk state. Female gender was identified as a risk factor for the more vulnerable psychological patterns. By providing a more nuanced classification of the comorbidity of PTSS and sleep disorders, this study lays a theoretical foundation for personalized treatment and intervention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03553-8.Keywords: Post-traumatic stress symptoms, Sleep quality, Latent profile analysis, Latent transition analysis, Longitudinal study

Introduction

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a psychological condition that may develop in individuals after experiencing a traumatic event [42, 43]. Among the varied symptoms of PTSD, sleep problems are also present, including nightmares and an inability to sleep soundly. Sleep issues themselves are also an independent psychological problem, influenced by many factors such as stress, emotional state, and lifestyle habits [21]. Research has shown that there is a complex interplay between sleep problems and PTSD [15]. Sleep disturbances and PTSD often co-occur, and their relationship is bidirectional and reciprocal. Sleep problems are not merely a consequence of PTSD,nightmares and REM fragmentation can lead to emotional dysregulation, and excessive sympathetic nervous system arousal can impede effective emotional memory consolidation [2, 22, 27].

Public health events, acting as traumatic stressors, have been linked to a significant increase in psychological distress. Over the past two decades, the prevalence of PTSS following such events has been estimated at 17% [42, 43]. The COVID-19 pandemic, as a public health event of unprecedented scale, has further contributed to a diverse array of psychological issues, including depression, anxiety, sleep disorders, and PTSS [7]. Due to the ongoing nature of the pandemic and the inconsistency in the time of individual infections, the temporal logic of the sequence of PTSD and sleep problems has become difficult to grasp. Under these circumstances, adopting the concept of comorbidity [21, 26, 34], that is, focusing on the symptoms themselves rather than their order, helps us to understand these issues more deeply.

Traditional psychological state assessments often rely on a single score standard, which is simple but the results are often rough and cannot reveal the heterogeneity within the group [35]. Currently, some studies are considering sleep disorders and PTSD together, using Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) to explore their comorbid patterns [17, 38], providing us with a new perspective for understanding the heterogeneity of psychological patterns of sleep and PTSS. In two studies that examined the heterogeneity of sleep problems and PTSS as a combined construct, three distinct types were identified, characterized by relatively high, moderate, and low levels of PTSS and sleep problems. The high-level group tested positive for PTSS, while the remaining two groups tested negative [17, 32]. The exploration of heterogeneity suggests the existence of a significant intermediate state, which may offer a better explanatory perspective for long-term dynamic changes over time. Heterogeneity research is crucial for PTSD studies because individuals' responses to a uniform traumatic event are heterogeneous, as are the psychological state changes following the same traumatic event. Therefore, in this study, we will also use Latent Transition Analysis (LTA)to explore changes in heterogeneity. In addition, LTA as a dynamic analysis method, can help us identify the transition patterns of different subgroups over time [8, 30]. However, research in this area is still insufficient, Only one study has conducted latent transition analysis on PTSD symptoms during the pandemic, tracking transitions across three time points. It not only identified two distinct subgroups with high and low PTSS but also determined that being female, fear of infection, and financial concerns were strong predictors of pattern transitions. Currently, no studies have examined the heterogeneity and long-term changes in sleep and PTSS patterns during the pandemic. Therefore, this study has three objectives. First, we aim to identify the heterogeneous characteristics of PTSS and sleep patterns at two time points during the pandemic using LPA, expecting to find three distinct subgroups. Second, we aim to determine the developmental process of this heterogeneity, expecting it to be stable over time, and will use LTA and variance analysis for a comprehensive assessment [6, 23].Third, we aim to identify demographic factors that influence transitions, and we expect gender to be a risk factor.

Through the methods of LPA, LTA, and variance analysis, it reveals the stability and transition of different psychological patterns and also discusses whether demographic characteristics will have an impact, providing a theoretical basis and practical guidance for personalized psychological intervention and treatment.

Methods

Participants

This study regards the public health event COVID-19 as a source of trauma and is part of the national trauma recovery program [19, 39]. Data collection was conducted in February 2020 through the Questionnaire Star platform (https://www.wjx.cn) platform. The inclusion criteria for participants were as follows: aged over eighteen, with normal cognitive function, no comorbid mental disorders, and required to have truamatic experienced events related to COVID-19.Participants were followed up 11 months later, with both data collections being voluntary and financially compensated. The survey excluded questionnaires with response times that were too short (2 min), indicating potentially careless answering, as well as those with excessively long durations (30 min), which might have resulted from skipping questions.542 valid questionnaires were obtained at Time 1 (T1). At Time 2 (T2), 392 responses were collected, with 329 meeting the criteria for validity, yielding an effective retention rate of 83.9%. Data analysis was restricted to the 329 participants who were involved in both T1 and T2.

Measures

Pittsburgh sleep quality index scale (PSQI)

This scale is extensively utilized for evaluating sleep quality. The full scale comprises 24 items. For the convenience of the study and considering previous literature that has shown a single item of sleep quality to have comparable validity to full sleep scale in reflecting sleep conditions [10], this research utilizes a brief four-item measure to assess sleep quality [40]. These items include subjective sleep quality (0 = very good, 1 = good, 2 = poor, 3 = very poor), sleep onset latency (0 = none, 1 = less than once a week, 2 = once or twice a week, 3 = more than three times a week), sleep fragmentation (0 = none, 1 = less than once a week, 2 = once or twice a week, 3 = more than three times a week), and sleep duration (0 = seven hours or more, 1 = six to seven hours, 2 = five to six hours, 3 = less than five hours).In this study, the Cronbach's alpha reliability of the scale was 0.760.

PTSD checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5)

The PCL-5, a comprehensive 20-item scale, quantifies the severity of an individual's experience with PTSD symptoms over the preceding month, as defined by the DSM-5. In this study, the traumatic event in focus is COVID-19. Rated on a scale from 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely), scores are aggregated for an overall severity measure. The PCL-5 delineates four subscales corresponding to DSM-5's PTSD symptom clusters: intrusions (items 1 through 5), avoidance (items 6 and 7), negative alterations in cognitions and mood (NACM; items 8 through 14), and alterations in arousal and reactivity (AR; items 15 through 20). In this study, the Chinese version of PCL- was used for testing, which has been confirmed to have good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach α coefficient of 0.91 [14].

Demographic questionnaire

The self-administered demographic questionnaire included items on gender, age, occupation, and level of education.

Statistical analysis

In this study, data analysis was performed using R Studio 5.0 and SPSS 26.0. The analysis was conducted in three steps: First, LPA was conducted on the data from both Time 1 (T1) and Time 2 (T2) using the tidyLPA package [33]. Second, chi-square tests were conducted in SPSS to examine the influence of demographic variables on profile classification at T1. Then, t-tests were used to assess the pre-post differences in PCL-5 and PSQI scores within the T1 profiles. Subsequently, a one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was performed with T1 profiles as the independent variable and PCL-5 and PSQI scores at T2 as the dependent variables. LTA was conducted using the Lmest package [4], which employs the Latent Markov model to estimate the initial probabilities of states as well as the transition probabilities between time points.

Results

Latent profile analyze of sleep quality and PTSS at T1

Profile characteristics at T1: A 3-profile model

We conducted separate LPA for the two timepoints (T1 and T2) independently. Considering both model fit indices and theoretical interpretability, we selected the class-invariant parameterization (CIP) model with equal variances and covariances fixed to 0 [37].

Both T1 and T2 analyses employed this CIP model with three profiles, ensuring consistency across time points while maintaining good model fit. This approach facilitated comparison of profile characteristics over time, with fit indices for alternative models reported in supplementary materials.

Smaller values of the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Sample-Size Adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (SABIC), and Integrated Completed Likelihood (ICL) in the fit indices represent a better LPA model fit. The Entropy index represents the accuracy of classification, with values above 80% indicating a high level of classification accuracy [36].

The following results first present the T1 analysis.

The fit results of Sleep Quality and PTSS at T1 time point across 1–6 profiles for the Equal variances and covariances fixed to 0 model are shown in Table 1. Calculating the PCL-5 scores within each profile revealed that when the number of profiles was three, the average values of the profiles were 34.86, 17.61, and 6.10, respectively, with the cutoff value of the PCL-5 scale being 33. The PSQI scores for the three profile are 6.86, 4.50, and 2.19, respectively. Taking into account the model's practicality and simplicity, we ultimately determined three profiles, which were named Health, Risk, and PTSD. Prediction of T2 sleep quality and PTSS from T1 latent profiles

In general, over time, there was a significant increase in the severity of PTSD symptoms (MT1 = 12.70, MT2 = 15.55, t = −4.506, p < 0.001) and a decline in sleep quality (MT1 = 3.40, MT2 = 4.42, t = −5.988, p < 0.001).

Further analysis by profile revealed divergent trends of change across profiles (Fig. 2). For PTSD symptoms, the PTSD group showed a significant decrease in PCL-5 scores (MT1 = 34.86, MT2 = 23.83,t = 4.332, p < 0.01). The Risk group showed an increase, but it was not significant (MT1 = 17.61, MT2 = 19.76,t = −1.853, p > 0.05). The Health group exhibited a significant increase in PCL-5 scores, yet they remained at a relatively low level (MT1 = 6.10, MT2 = 11.85,t = −5.746, p < 0.01).

PCL-5 score variations among three groups over two time points

The changes in sleep quality showed a consistent trend (Fig. 3). Within the PTSD group, there was a substantial reduction in PSQI scores (MT1 = 6.86, MT2 = 5.17, t = 2.644, p < 0.05). The Risk group's scores edged upwards, though the change did not reach statistical significance (MT1 = 4.50, MT2 = 5.07, t = −1.711, p > 0.05). Conversely, while the Health group exhibited a pronounced rise in PSQI scores, this increase still positioned their scores in the lower range.(MT1 = 2.19, MT2 = 3.94, t = −9.830, p < 0.01).

PSQI score variations among three groups over two time points

To examine the symptom differences among the three profiles at T2, ANOVA was conducted with profile as the independent variable and PCL-5 and PSQI scores as the dependent variables. For PTSD symptoms, a significant main effect of profile was observed, F(2, 326) = 29.434, p < 0.01.Post-hoc comparisons indicated that the Health (M = 11.85) had significantly lower scores compared to both the PTSD (M = 23.83) and the Risk (M = 19.76), with no significant difference between the PTSD and Risk.

Similarly, for sleep quality, a significant main effect of profile was found, F(2, 326) = 8.743, p < 0.01. Post-hoc comparisons revealed that the Health (M = 3.94) had significantly lower scores than the PTSD (M = 5.17) and the Risk (M = 5.07), with no significant difference between the PTSD and Risk.

Latent transition analysis

This study aims to preserve the distinct features of profiles at both time points while also conducting an analysis of individual profile transitions over time. To address this discrepancy, we utilized the LTA, which integrates the data from both time points to ensure the consistency of state estimation, and calculates the probabilities of individuals belonging to specific states at each time point.

Discussion

Findings

This study employed LPA to delve into the heterogeneity of PTSS and sleep quality among individuals at two time points. The research revealed that during the pandemic, individuals' psychological states could be categorized into three types, and this classification demonstrated consistency across time. Whether it was two months after the onset of the pandemic or a year later, individuals' psychological states could be classified into three categories: health, at risk, and Post- PTSD. The PTSD group's scale scores exceeded the cutoff value, and below the cutoff value, we further identified two distinct psychological states: one is the Health group with very mild PTSS symptoms and good sleep quality in the face of the pandemic; the other is the Risk group in a transitional state. This heterogeneity not only maintains consistency across time but also shows persistence. Our study provides insights into the PTSD symptoms of a general adult population, which are relatively milder compared to those of adolescents in severely affected Wuhan areas and individuals who have experienced bereavement [13, 41] Similarly, it is also lower than other samples exposed to drinking and war [28, 32].

By tracking the three groups from the beginning of the pandemic, we found that even a year after the pandemic, there were still significant differences between the three groups, further confirming the persistence of heterogeneity. The latent transition analysis based on the overall psychological state at two time points, through cross-validation, further confirmed the stable existence of heterogeneity. The psychological state of the group still exhibited three different response patterns: healthy, at risk, and PTSD, and the probability of stability at the two time points ranged from 0.67 to 0.83.

This study utilized latent transition analysis and initially discovered that the three distinct psychological states within the population exhibit temporal stability. Notably, when comparing the profile results from two time points, we observed significant differences in sleep issues between the Risk and Health groups, which are below the critical threshold. This suggests that sleep problems may be an external manifestation of poor psychological states. We observed a trend of both the Health and PTSD groups shifting towards the Risk group. The emergence of new sleep issues in the Health group, as well as the persistent sleep problems in the recovering PTSD group, coupled with the strong stability demonstrated by the Risk group, all underscore the profound impact of the pandemic on people's sleep patterns [18].

In the tracking of the three groups at the onset of the pandemic, under the dual perspectives of more precise state identification by LPA and the examination of symptom change differences by variance analysis, it was found that the three groups not only differ in symptoms but also have their own unique patterns of change. The PTSD group showed symptom improvement, and according to the results of two LPAs, some even improved to the Risk group or the Health group. A tracking study of patients diagnosed with mental disorders before the pandemic found that their PTSS also eased during the pandemic [11]. This could indicate a case of early affliction leading to early recovery, or it may be related to the resilience gained from early traumatic experiences [24].

The state of the Health group showed a slight deterioration, indicating that even those who initially demonstrated greater resilience could be affected by the pandemic. More than half of the individuals in the Health group exhibited strong psychological resilience, remaining in the Health group in both LPA results. As the pandemic continued over time, isolation became more common, increasing the risk of depression and PTSS [20, 39]. Research into the developmental trajectory of overall psychological states found that less loneliness was a significant characteristic that distinguished the resilient group from others [35]. However, some individuals experienced a decline and even transitioned to the PTSD group after eleven months. This delayed phenomenon may be related to exhaustion. A study found that people who adopted more proactive coping strategies early on could feel exhausted due to the persistent presence of stressors, leading to more psychological issues [16]. An LPA study on the Big Five personality traits found that as long as neuroticism remains low, individuals with higher levels of other traits are more likely to cope proactively [3].

It is worth noting that the overall symptom differences within the Risk group before and after are not significant, but the reactions of individuals within the group are not uniform. Half of the individuals remain in an unchanged state, which may indicate emotional numbness. 28.71% of people may have adopted proactive strategies that led to their transition into the Health group, while the remaining 21.78% may have transitioned into the PTSD group due to a lack of early intervention and prolonged exposure to stressors. We suggest that future research could incorporate other variables, such as emotion regulation strategies, to conduct more in-depth empirical studies and uncover the underlying causes of the changes.

This study also explored the potential factors contributing to heterogeneity and found that gender plays a role, with women being more likely to be categorized into the PTSD group and also more likely to transition into the PTSD group. Studies have found that women's susceptibility and gender difference exhibit cross-cultural consistency [12, 25]. In similar latent transition analysis studies examining PTSD symptoms, females have also been found to be more likely to transition into groups with higher levels of PTSD symptoms (PTSS) [8, 35]. In addition, poor economic status, being single or having a poor relationship with a partner, and low education level were also found to be risk factors that make psychological states vulnerable to impact during a pandemic [1, 9, 12, 31]. However, in our study, relevant demographic information was not collected. The age and education level, which were collected, did not influence individual subgroup classification. We speculate that the COVID-19 quarantine policies may have different impacts on different occupations, which cannot be reflected in age and education level. Therefore, we suggest that future studies consider factors such as economic status, occupation, and social relationships.

Limitations and implications

This study has achieved certain results in exploring the PTSS and sleep quality of populations during the pandemic, but it also has some limitations. Firstly, due to considerations of research convenience, we only analyzed some dimensions of sleep quality, failing to cover all relevant information. For instance, using a complete PSQI scale might reveal the association between the use of hypnotic medication and the risk of depression [5].Currently, sleep measurement relies not only on subjective scales but can also be enhanced through objective recordings, such as actigraphy or polysomnography, to improve the accuracy of the assessment [29]. Secondly, the study focused on PTSS and sleep quality without incorporating other psychological scales to deeply analyze the factors affecting these variables.

Nevertheless, this study provides a new perspective in methodology. Although our data points are fewer than those in studies using the latent growth model, we fully utilized the data from two time points, not only identifying the latent states at each time point but also exploring the changes over time through latent transition analysis. This offers new analytical methods for future research.

The study reveals the heterogeneity of psychological responses in the population, finding that the three different states of the groups have different clinical significance. This provides a multifaceted view for clinical intervention, such as using network analysis to determine the core symptoms of the PTSD group, or exploring how to prevent the transition of the Risk group to the PTSD group through intervention measures. In addition, analyzing the protective factors behind the Health group from the perspective of personality traits is also a direction for future research.

Finally, through latent transition analysis, this study highlights the special role of sleep in the long-term pandemic. Sleep problems are not only closely related to PTSS but may also exert an independent influence. Future research can use network analysis or cross-lagged models to delve into the complex relationship between sleep and PTSS.

Conculsion

This study found that two months and one year after the onset of the pandemic, people's response patterns in Post-Traumatic Stress Symptoms (PTSS) and sleep quality can be categorized into three states: the Healthy group, the Risk group, and the PTSD group. The state in which individuals find themselves remains relatively stable over time. Women are more likely to be identified as part of the PTSD group and to transition into the PTSD group.
