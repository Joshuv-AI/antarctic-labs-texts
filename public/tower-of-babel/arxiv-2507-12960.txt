Accelerated free energy estimation in ab initio path integral Monte Carlo simulations
Pontus Svensson, Fotios Kalkavouras, Uwe Hernandez Acosta, Zhandos A. Moldabekov, Panagiotis Tolias, Jan Vorberger, Tobias Dornheim

Abstract
We present a methodology for accelerating the estimation of the free energy from path integral Monte Carlo simulations by considering an intermediate artificial reference system where interactions are inexpensive to evaluate numerically. Using the spherically averaged Ewald interaction as this intermediate reference system for the uniform electron gas, the interaction contribution for the free energy was evaluated up to 18 times faster than the Ewald-only method. Furthermore, a ξ \xi -extrapolation technique was tested and applied to alleviate the fermion sign problem and to resolve the sign for large particle numbers. Combining these two techniques enabled the evaluation of the free energy for a system of 1000 electrons, where both finite-size and statistical errors are below chemical accuracy. The general procedure can be applied to systems relevant for planetary and inertial confinement fusion modeling with low to moderate levels of quantum degeneracy.

The description of thermal systems of interacting fermions is a cornerstone of our understanding for a wide range of quantum systems, including ultra-cold atoms
Giorgini et al. 2008
;
Ceperley 1992
, quantum dots
Reimann and Manninen 2002
;
Dornheim and Yan 2022
, and dense plasmas
Lorenzen et al. 2014
. In particular, dense quantum plasmas are abundant in astrophysics, where they are found in gas giants
Guillot 1999
;
Fortney and Nettelmann 2010
;
Helled et al. 2020
such as Jupiter
French et al. 2012
, Saturn
Preising et al. 2023
and some classes of exoplanets
Guillot 1999
, and stars
Kippenhahn et al. 2012
most notable in later stages of stellar evolution in the form of red giants
Fortov 2009
, white dwarfs
Chandrasekhar 1931
;
Chabrier et al. 2000
and the atmospheres of neutron stars
Gudmundsson et al. 1983
;
Haensel et al. 2007
. However, high-density plasmas are also central in human-made applications such as inertial confinement fusion (ICF)
Nuckolls et al. 1972
;
Betti and Hurricane 2016
;
Hurricane et al. 2023
and the synthesis of novel materials
Miao et al. 2020
. In recent groundbreaking experiments, ICF implosions have exceeded the Lawson criteria and achieved capsule gain
Abu-Shawareb et al. 2022
, a key step towards achieving energy production through the ICF concept.

A formidable regime of dense plasmas to model theoretically is warm dense matter (WDM), which is characterised by a complex interplay between interactions, quantum degeneracy, and thermal excitations
Lorenzen et al. 2014
;
Bonitz et al. 2020
;
Vorberger et al. 2025
. All the previously mentioned effects must be taken into account as both
r_{s}
– the ratio between the Wigner-Seitz radius and the Bohr radius – and
\theta
– the ratio of the thermal excitation energy and the electronic Fermi energy – are of order unity, which characterises the strength of interactions and quantum degeneracy, respectively. Therefore, there remain uncertainties in the fundamental properties of WDM, such as the equation of state (EOS) and transport properties, which limit predictive modeling of, for example, the Jovian interior
Wahl et al. 2017
;
Helled et al. 2020
and ICF implosions
Hu et al. 2015
.

The most widely used description for WDM systems is a hybrid method (DFT-MD)
Bonitz et al. 2024
, where electrons are described using density functional theory (DFT)
Hohenberg and Kohn 1964
;
Mermin 1965
, while ions are treated by molecular dynamics (MD)
Rapaport 2004
. Formally, DFT is exact given the correct exchange-correlation functional
Hohenberg and Kohn 1964
, but this functional remains unknown and practical calculations resort to approximate descriptions, often based on the properties of the uniform electron gas (UEG)
Perdew and Zunger 1981
;
Karasiev et al. 2014
;
Dornheim et al. 2018
. Path integral Monte Carlo (PIMC)
Ceperley 1995
;
Vorberger et al. 2025
;
Böhme et al. 2022
provides a suitable benchmark at finite temperature, since it is exact within the statistical error. However, for fermionic systems, PIMC is limited by the fermion sign problem (FSP) in the number of particles and the level of quantum degeneracy it can model
Dornheim 2019
. The FSP arises because all fermionic observables are ratios where the denominator is the average sign
S
, which decreases exponentially with particle number and the inverse temperature
Dornheim 2019
;
Troyer and Wiese 2005
. This vanishing sign causes computations of large or cold systems to be dominated by statistical errors
Hatano 1994
.

To address the FSP,
Xiong and Xiong 2022
have suggested a
\xi
-extrapolation method
Xiong and Xiong 2022
where an additional
\xi
parameter that smoothly interpolates from the bosonic (
\xi=1
) to the fermionic (
\xi=-1
) limit was introduced. By introducing an empirical model for the
\xi
-dependence, calculations can be carried out in the FSP-free parameter regime and extrapolated to the fermionic results, circumventing the exponential computational cost with respect to the particle number
Dornheim et al. 2023
;
Dornheim et al. 2024
. This extrapolation method has been successfully applied to moderately degenerate systems (
\theta\geq 1.0
) for the computation of energy
Xiong and Xiong 2023
;
Dornheim et al. 2023
;
Morresi and Garberoglio 2025
, static structure
Dornheim et al. 2023
;
Dornheim et al. 2024
;
Dornheim et al. 2024
;
Dornheim et al. 2025
, imaginary time correlation function
Dornheim et al. 2023
;
Dornheim et al. 2025
, density response
Dornheim et al. 2024
;
Dornheim et al. 2024
, and the average sign itself
Dornheim et al. 2025
.

The (Helmholtz) free energy is central for our understanding of thermal systems, for example it is directly related to the exchange-correlation functional in DFT
Mermin 1965
;
Gupta and Rajagopal 1982
;
Smith et al. 2018
where finite-temperature corrections are key at intermediate temperatures
Sjostrom and Daligault 2014
;
Karasiev et al. 2016
;
Ramakrishna et al. 2020
;
Bonitz et al. 2024
, but the free energy is also commonly used to investigate the stability of different phases
Alfe et al. 1999
;
Wilson and Militzer 2011
;
Wu et al. 2021
. As the free energy is a thermodynamic potential, a free energy parametrisation automatically yields a self-consistent EOS where all thermodynamic properties are obtained through differentiation. So far, first-principles tabulations of the EOS have focused on energy and pressure
Militzer et al. 2021
, but semi-empirical constructions commonly model the free energy
More et al. 1988
;
Lyon and Johnson 1995
. By accelerating first-principle computations of the free energy, we are moving closer to reliable and internally consistent equation of state tables in the WDM regime.

In this letter, we present computations for the free energy of the spin-unpolarised UEG from PIMC with unprecedentedly large system sizes and low statistical errors. Using a combination of robust extrapolation techniques and the introduction of an intermediate reference system where interactions are computationally cheap, we are able to model
N=1000
electrons. The efficiency of this scheme allows us to evaluate the free energy to well within chemical accuracy (i.e.,
1\,\text{kcal/mol}\approx 1.6\,\text{mHa}

Pople 1999
). In the main text, we focus on the condition
r_{s}=3.23
and
\theta=1.0
characteristic of the electronic conditions possible to achieve in hydrogen jet experiments
Zastrau et al. 2021
;
Fletcher et al. 2022
;
Hamann et al. 2023
, but the methodology is general and applicable for either bosons and not too degenerate Fermi systems. The complete analysis for the UEG at
r_{s}=10
is given in the supporting information.

The partition function or the free energy is not a thermodynamical average
per se
, but relates to a volume in phase space
Frenkel and Smit 2002
. Therefore, the free energy is not readily available from an MC or MD simulation, and the thermodynamic integration (TI)
Zwanzig 1954
;
Frenkel and Smit 2002
method or the adiabatic connection (AC) formula
Pribram-Jones et al. 2016
has traditionally been used for its computation. Both methods require multiple computations, e.g., with an interaction that can be smoothly turned from that of a reference system – commonly the ideal system – to the target system. Moreover, the application of the AC method to inhomogeneous systems, such as the electronic problem in the external potential of a fixed ion configuration, poses an additional obstacle. Recently,
Dornheim et al. 2025
introduced the extended ensemble technique in which the free energy differences between systems 1 and 2 can be directly computed
Dornheim et al. 2025
. The extended partition function in question is

Z_{\text{ext}}=cZ_{1}+Z_{2},

(1)

where
Z_{i}
is the partition function of system
i
, and
c
is an arbitrary coefficient that is chosen to optimise the ergodicity
Dornheim et al. 2025
. In the extended ensemble, the difference in free energy per particle
f_{i}
between the two systems is directly related to the thermal averages in the extended ensemble
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\text{ext}}
via

f_{1}-f_{2}=-\frac{k_{\text{B}}T}{N}\log\left(\frac{c^{-1}\langle\hat{\delta_{1}}\rangle_{\text{ext}}}{\langle\hat{\delta_{2}}\rangle_{\text{ext}}}\right),

(2)

where
\hat{\delta}_{i}
is one in system
i
and zero otherwise,
k_{\text{B}}T
is the temperature in energy units, and
N
is the number of particles.

The Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\eta}=\hat{K}+\eta\hat{V}
where
\hat{K}
is the kinetic energy operator and
\hat{V}
is the Ewald summation
Ewald 1921
;
Dornheim et al. 2018
, interpolates between the ideal (
\eta=0
) and interacting systems (
\eta=1
). By considering
\eta=0
and
\eta=1
for the two systems in Equation (
2
) along with exact results for noninteracting systems
Zhou and Dai 2018
;
Barghathi et al. 2020
, the free energy for bosons can be computed
Dornheim et al. 2025
;
Dornheim et al. 2025
in what we will refer to as the
\eta
-ensemble. However, for a large number of particles, it was found practically difficult to ergodically explore the entire extended ensemble due to the presence of configurations that are strongly suppressed for interacting systems in the ideal case
Dornheim et al. 2025
. Therefore, multiple intermediate
\eta
-steps are introduced, a prevailing strategy in free energy calculations with substantially different configurational spaces
Frenkel and Smit 2002
. Structurally, the
\eta
-ensemble becomes reminiscent of the TI with the in-between steps. However, in the
\eta
-ensemble,
\eta
-values with a finite difference are considered, whereas in TI a continuous function of the coupling constant is integrated.

The
\eta
-ensemble is performed in the bosonic sector and is therefore FSP free, but a large number of intermediate
\eta
-steps will result in a prohibitive computational cost for accurate free energy calculations for large
N
. The majority of the computational cost in each MC step comes from the evaluation of the Ewald summation. To avoid this problem, we evaluate the
\eta
-ensemble using a nonphysical interaction or artificial interaction,
\hat{V}\rightarrow\hat{V}_{\text{art}}
, which is computationally cheap, and any error is corrected for in a second step henceforth referred to as the
a
-ensemble. The
a
-ensemble concerns the Hamiltonian

\hat{H}_{a}=\hat{K}+a\hat{V}+(1-a)\hat{V}_{\text{art}},

(3)

were
a=0
and
a=1
is used for the two systems in Equation (
2
). If the physical interaction
\hat{V}
and the artificial one
\hat{V}_{\text{art}}
are sufficiently similar, no intermediate
a
steps are required, as no substantial energy penalty is incurred when altering
a
. This procedure accelerates the computation as the majority of the data collection is performed with a fast artificial interaction, but it does not constitute any approximation as it can simply be viewed as establishing a transitional reference system, as is common practice when performing TI
Alfe et al. 1999
;
Caillol and Gilles 2000
;
Plummer et al. 2025
.

The artificial interaction in question is in principle a free choice, but it should be both efficient and close to the physical one to avoid unnecessary computations in the
a
-ensemble. Working with the Coulomb interaction, a variety of cut-off based approximations have been developed, which all could be used as the artificial interaction; see review by
Fukuda and Nakamura 2012

Fukuda and Nakamura 2012
and references therein. In this work, we have used the spherically averaged Ewald potential by
Yakub and Ronchi 2003
(YR)
Yakub and Ronchi 2003
;
Yakub and Ronchi 2005
that has been successfully applied in MD
Yakub et al. 2007
, MC
Demyanov and Levashov 2022
;
Demyanov et al. 2024
and PIMC
Filinov et al. 2020
;
Dornheim et al. 2025
, and recently has attracted new theoretical interest
Demyanov and Levashov 2022
. By construction, the YR interaction yields energies similar to the Ewald summation, and its simple algebraic structure makes it cheap to evaluate allowing for classical MC simulation with up to
10^{6}
particles
Demyanov and Levashov 2022
. The new
a
-ensemble with the YR potential as the artificial interaction has been implemented in the ISHTAR code
Dornheim et al. 2024
, which employs the canonical adaptation
Mezzacapo and Boninsegni 2007
;
Dornheim et al. 2021
of the worm algorithm
Boninsegni et al. 2006
;
Boninsegni et al. 2006
. All reported computations have been performed using the primitive factorisation
Ceperley 1995
.

To summarise, we have introduced and exemplified the use of an accelerated method for free energy estimation based on
ab initio
PIMC. The method accelerates the computation in two primary ways. First, an intermediate “artificial” reference system is introduced in which interactions are numerically evaluated more efficiently. The majority of interaction effects can be captured in this artificial system, and any remaining error can be corrected by the introduced
a
-ensemble which in our work only required a single computation with the numerically more costly physical interaction. In this work, the use of the artificial interaction reduced the computation effort by a factor of up to 18 for the interaction contribution. Second, a
\xi
-extrapolation methodology is employed to resolve the sign for larger system sizes that are otherwise prevented by the fermionic sign problem. This extrapolation was shown to be accurate to
0.3\%
over two orders of magnitude in
\xi
for
\theta=1
. The generality of the procedure was demonstrated by considering two different density conditions.

Accelerating the calculation of free energies paves the way for scaling up computations to remove the final systematic error – the finite size effects – at warm dense matter conditions. The presented method can be combined with other acceleration techniques to consider even large systems, e.g., employing GPUs
Thorpe et al. 2025
, hierarchical energy evaluation
Müller et al. 2023
, and contraction schemes
John et al. 2016
. High-precision free energy estimates for the UEG open for the possibility to explore a potential spin phase transition at finite temperature, which have been intensely studied in the ground state
Ceperley and Alder 1980
;
Loos and Gill 2016
. Future work might also explore the long-wavelength physics with the presented method via the density stiffness theorem
Giuliani and Vignale 2008
;
Dornheim et al. 2023
, which relates the static linear and non-linear density response to free energy differences. In this regard, the simulation of large systems is crucial to study the optical limit of
\bm{k}\rightarrow 0
, where the minimum wavenumber
|\bm{k}|=2\pi/L
is determined by the box length
L
. Lastly, the present study focuses on the UEG, but it is straightforward to apply our methodology to light elements such as hydrogen and beryllium
Dornheim et al. 2025
to inform planetary and inertial confinement fusion modeling.
Moreover, our approach can easily be applied to the simulation of inhomogeneous systems such as electrons in a fixed ionic configuration, which might be of great value for the benchmarking of DFT and potentially even for the data-driven construction of novel exchange correlation functionals
Kasim and Vinko 2021
.

Extended ensemble and corrections via the
a
-ensemble

The extended ensemble method
Dornheim et al. 2025
is used to calculate the contribution of interactions to the free energy (in the bosonic sector) in two steps. First, the free-energy difference between the interacting and ideal bosonic systems is calculated using the
\eta
-ensemble. In particular, for a large number of particles the acceptance probability is reduced for the move which alters
\eta
as the likelihood of finding two electrons in close proximity increases in the ideal system, configurations which are strongly suppressed in the interacting one
Dornheim et al. 2025
. Therefore, to maintain an ergodic exploration of the extended ensemble, the
\eta
-ensemble is subdivided into
N_{\eta}
steps and the free energy difference is computed for a set of
\eta
-values, i.e.
\left\{\eta_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{N_{\eta}+1}
, where
\eta_{1}=1
,
\eta_{N_{\eta}+1}=0
and
\eta_{i+1}<\eta_{i}
. To accelerate this process, these computations are carried out with an artificial interaction which mimics our target interaction – the Ewald summation in this case – but is numerically less expensive to evaluate. Here, the spherically averaged Ewald interaction by Yakub and Ronchi (YR)
Yakub and Ronchi 2003
;
Yakub and Ronchi 2005
was used. The details of the moves in the
\eta
-ensemble were given by
Dornheim et al. 2024

Dornheim et al. 2024
. Second, the
a
-ensembel is used to correct for the use of the artificial interaction in the
\eta
-ensemble by considering the Hamiltonian in Equation (3) of the main text. In principle, the
a
-ensemble can be subdivided into
N_{a}
computations with a set of
a
-values
\left\{a_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{N_{a}+1}
, where
a_{1}=1
,
a_{N_{\eta}+1}=0
and
a_{i+1}<a_{i}
. However, if the artificial interaction is sufficiently similar to the physical one, a single step
N_{a}=1
is sufficient to maintain ergodicity, as has been the case in this work. This is how the method accelerates the computations, as a reduced number of calculations with the more expensive Ewald interactions is needed.

The moves to switch
a
-values are constructed analogously to the moves in the
\eta
-ensemble with the Metropolis-Hastings
Metropolis et al. 1953
;
Hastings 1970
acceptance probabilities

A(a_{i}\to a_{i+1})=\textnormal{min}\left\{1,\frac{1}{c_{a_{i}}}\textnormal{exp}\left(\epsilon\left\{(a_{i}-a_{i+1})V(\bm{X})+(a_{i+1}-a_{i})V_{\textnormal{art}}(\bm{X})\right\}\right)\right\},

(S1a)

and

\displaystyle A(a_{i+1}\to a_{i})=\textnormal{min}\left\{1,{c_{a_{i}}}\vphantom{\frac{1}{c_{a_{i}}}}\textnormal{exp}\left(\epsilon\left\{(a_{i+1}-a_{i})V(\bm{X})+(a_{i}-a_{i+1})V_{\textnormal{art}}(\bm{X})\right\}\right)\right\},

(S1b)

where
\bm{X}
is the path configuration,
c_{a_{i}}
corresponds to
c
in Equation (1),
\epsilon=\beta/P
, and
P
is the number of factorisations of the density matrix. The move only modifies the
a
-value, and not the path configurations
\bm{X}
. Furthermore, the move is applied in both the diagonal and off-diagonal sectors of the worm algorithm
Boninsegni et al. 2006
;
Boninsegni et al. 2006
.

The resulting free energy computed from a series of PIMC calculations is
f_{\text{Ew}}^{(\text{F})}=f_{id}^{(\text{B})}+\Delta f_{\eta,\text{Ew}}^{(\text{B})}+\Delta f_{S,\text{Ew}}
or using the acceleration method
f_{\text{Ew}}^{(\text{F})}=f_{id}^{(\text{B})}+\Delta f_{\eta,\text{art}}^{(\text{B})}+\Delta f_{a,\text{art-Ew}}^{(\text{B})}+\Delta f_{S,\text{Ew}}

\displaystyle\Delta f_{\eta,\text{Ew}}^{(\text{B})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta N}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\eta}}\log\left[\frac{r_{\eta_{i},\eta_{i+1}}^{\text{Ew}}}{c_{\eta_{i}}}\right],

(S2a)

\displaystyle\Delta f_{\eta,\text{art}}^{(\text{B})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta N}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\eta}}\log\left[\frac{r_{\eta_{i},\eta_{i+1}}^{\text{art}}}{c_{\eta_{i}}}\right],

(S2b)

\displaystyle\Delta f_{a,\text{art-Ew}}^{(\text{B})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta N}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{a}}\log\left[\frac{r_{a_{i},a_{i+1}}^{\text{art-Ew}}}{c_{a_{i}}}\right],

(S2c)

and

\displaystyle\Delta f_{S,\text{Ew}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta N}\log\left[S_{\text{Ew}}\right],

(S2d)

and see Figure 1 in the main text for the origin of each term. In the above,
c_{\eta_{i}}
corresponds to
c
in Equation (1) in the main text. The ratios of samples in each subsystem in the extended ensemble is
r_{\eta_{i},\eta_{i+1}}^{\text{Ew}}
,
r_{\eta_{i},\eta_{i+1}}^{\text{art}}
and
r_{a_{i},a_{i+1}}^{\text{art-Ew}}
in both the
\eta

a

\eta
-ensemble was benchmarked by
Dornheim et al. 2024

Dornheim et al. 2024

\text{Computational cost}(N,P)=C_{0}P+C_{1}^{\text{Int.}}PN,

(S3)

\eta
-ensemble is achieved as
C_{1}^{\text{Int.}}
for the YR interaction is substantially smaller than that for the Ewald interaction.

Finite size corrections (FSC)

The finite-size corrections (FSC) applied follow the methodology given in the supplementary material of
Groth et al. 2017

Groth et al. 2017
. The finite-size error for the exchange correlation free energy at
r_{s}
and
\theta
is given by

\Delta f_{xc}(r_{s},\theta)=\frac{1}{r_{s}^{2}}\int_{0}^{r_{s}}d\bar{r}_{s}\;\bar{r}_{s}\Delta v(\bar{r}_{s},\theta),

(S4)

where
\Delta v(r_{s},\theta;N)
is the finite size error on the interaction energy. The major contribution to
\Delta v
is the discretisation error of the interaction integral imposed by the box and not the errors on the structure factor
S(\bm{k})
itself
Dornheim et al. 2016
. Therefore, the finite-size correction is approximated by

\Delta v(r_{s},\theta)\approx\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}}\tilde{v}_{\bm{k}}\left(\bar{S}(\bm{k})-1\right)-\left(\frac{1}{2L^{3}}\sum_{\bm{G}\neq 0}\tilde{v}_{\bm{G}}\left(\bar{S}(\bm{G})-1\right)+\frac{\xi_{M}}{2}\right),

(S5)

where
\tilde{v}_{\bm{k}}=4\pi/\bm{k}^{2}
is the Fourier transformed Coulomb interaction,
L
is the side length of the box,
\bm{G}=2\pi\bm{n}/L
where
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
, and
\xi_{M}
is the Madelung constant. As an approximation, the static structure factor is taken from a dielectric theory, commonly the random phase approximation (RPA)
\bar{S}(\bm{k})=S_{\text{RPA}}(\bm{k})
. Malone implemented this procedure in
uegpy
\bibnote
See
https://github.com/fdmalone/uegpy
which has been successful in removing most finite-size errors. However, for large numbers of particles this implementation suffers from some stability issues. Therefore, the procedure has been reimplemented with the classical STLS scheme
Singwi et al. 1968
as the underlying dielectric theory, that is,
\bar{S}(\bm{k})=S_{\text{STLS}}(\bm{k})
.
