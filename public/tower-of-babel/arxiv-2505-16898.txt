Active Quantum Reservoir Engineering: Using a Qubit to Manipulate its Environment
Marcelo Janovitch, Matteo Brunelli, Patrick P. Potts

Abstract
Quantum reservoir engineering leverages dissipative processes to achieve desired behavior, with applications ranging from entanglement generation to quantum error correction. Therein, a structured environment acts as an entropy sink for the system and no time-dependent control over the system is required. We develop a theoretical framework for active reservoir engineering, where time-dependent control over a quantum system is used to manipulate its environment. In this case, the system may act as an entropy sink for the environment. Our framwork captures the dynamical interplay between system and environment, and provides an intuitive picture of how finite-size effects and system-environment correlations allow for manipulating the environment by repeated initialization of the quantum system. We illustrate our results with two examples: a superconducting qubit coupled to an environment of two-level systems and a semiconducting quantum dot coupled to nuclear spins. In both scenarios, we find qualitative agreement with previous experimental results, illustrating how active control can unlock new functionalities in open quantum systems.

I
Introduction

Quantum systems inevitably couple to their surroundings. This is the virtue that allows them to be observed and the chagrin that ruins their unique quantum properties. To minimize unwanted noise and decoherence, they are typically kept as isolated from their environment as possible.
In recent decades, dissipative processes have been recognized not only as a source of decoherence but also as a resource. This insight underpins quantum reservoir engineering
Poyatos et al. 1996
;
Verstraete et al. 2009
;
Harrington et al. 2022
, where controlled dissipation enables desired functionality, such as non-reciprocity
Metelmann and Clerk 2015
, topology
Bardyn et al. 2013
and autonomous quantum error correction
Leghtas et al. 2015
;
Lescanne et al. 2020
;
Gertler et al. 2021
;
Xu et al. 2023
, or stabilizes resourceful state such as entangled
Kraus et al. 2008
;
Diehl et al. 2008
;
Barreiro et al. 2011
;
Lin et al. 2013
;
Leghtas et al. 2013
;
Zapletal et al. 2022
or squeezed states
Kronwald et al. 2013
;
Wollman et al. 2015
;
Dassonneville et al. 2021
. A key ingredient in reservoir engineering is a low-entropy environment, typically the vacuum state of the electromagnetic field.

In many scenarios of interest a low-entropy environment is not available because the relevant energy scales are comparable to temperature, rendering the standard approach to reservoir engineering inapplicable. This is typically the case in solid state qubits; the collective magnetization of the material hosting the qubit can act as bath, composed, e.g., of nuclear spins for spin qubits
Urbaszek et al. 2013
or two-level defects for superconducting qubits
Klimov et al. 2018
;
Spiecker et al. 2023
. In this case, time-dependent control over the quantum system opens the door to a novel,
active
approach to reservoir engineering, where the environment is manipulated through the system. Once the environment state is tailored, it can add functionality to the quantum system, acting as an engineered dissipative channel.
For instance, it has been shown that repeated resetting of a superconducting qubit to the ground state allows for cooling of the environment
Spiecker et al. 2023
. Furthermore, spin qubits in semiconductors have been extensively used to manipulate the magnetization of their surrounding nuclei, also known as dynamic nuclear polarization
Urbaszek et al. 2013
; this led to orders of magnitude improvement in coherence time
Stepanenko et al. 2006
;
Lai et al. 2006
;
Latta et al. 2009
;
Gangloff et al. 2019
;
Jackson et al. 2022
;
Nguyen et al. 2023
. Compared to conventional (or passive) reservoir engineering, where the environment acts as an entropy sink and no time-dependent control over the system is required, the roles of system and environment are exchanged in active reservoir engineering: the system typically acts as an entropy sink for the environment.
Despite its potential, a theoretical framework for active reservoir engineering remains largely undevelopped.

In this work, we fill this gap by deriving and analyzing a theoretical framework for active reservoir engineering. This framework allows for an intuitive understanding of the joint system-bath dynamics and provides results that are in qualitative agreement with existing experiments. It offers insight into the role of finite-size effects and classical correlations in open quantum systems, paving the way for enhanced quantum control via active reservoir engineering. While we focus on specific platforms, our results apply to different central spin systems, including NV-centers
King et al. 2010
;
Khandelwal et al. 2023
and can be generalized to other platforms.

II
Overview

We focus on the two scenarios sketched in Fig
1
, a superconducting qubit coupled to a bath of two-level systems, motivated by the experiment in Ref.
Spiecker et al. 2023
, and a semiconducting quantum dot coupled to nuclear spins, motivated by Refs.
Jackson et al. 2022
;
Nguyen et al. 2023
;
Hogg et al. 2024
. In both cases, we consider a protocol where repeated initialization of the system (henceforth called qubit) allows for manipulating the magnetization,
m
, of the environment. For a given magnetization, the qubit will precess around an effective magnetic field
\vec{B}_{m}
, which depends on
m
. In addition, the qubit may exchange energy with the environment. This occurs through
incoherent
flip-flop processes, where the qubit and an environmental spin (or two-level system) flip together. Importantly, these processes are also dependent on the effective field
\vec{B}_{m}
: The Bloch vector of the qubit flips from being parallel to antiparallel with the magnetic field, or vice versa. If the qubit starts out (anti-)parallel to
\vec{B}_{m}
, then the flip-flop process increases (decreases) the magnetization
m
by one, c.f. Fig.
1
(a). This provides an intuitive understanding of different strategies for active reservoir engineering: if the magnetization should decrease, the qubit should be prepared in a state that points in the direction opposite to
\vec{B}_{m}
. To increase the magnetization, a state parallel to
\vec{B}_{m}
should be prepared. In general, the reservoir has a probability,
P_{m}
, of being found in a given magnetization, which is manipulated through the qubit.

II.1
Superconducting qubit

For the superconducting qubit, we consider an environment of two-level systems (TLSs) that have approximately the same splitting as the qubit itself, such that energy exchanges between system and environment are almost resonant. In this scenario, the effective field
\vec{B}_{m}
is particularly simple — it always points in the positive
z
-direction, see Fig.
1
(b). The magnetization of the environment can thus be decreased by initializing the system in the spin-down state
\ket{\downarrow_z}
. Repeatedly initializing this state results in the environment being cooled, as it moves to lower energy states, see Fig.
1
. This protocol was employed in the experiment of Ref.
Spiecker et al. 2023
. Conversely, repeated initialization in the spin-up state
\ket{\uparrow_z}
, results in population inversion (increased magnetization) as also demonstrated experimentally in Ref.
Spiecker et al. 2023
.

Refined control of the state of the environment is possible by exploiting correlations between the system and the environment. As a simple example, consider the state
\ket{\downarrow_z}
for
m>0
and
\ket{\uparrow_z}
for
m<0
. In this case, the magnetization is decreased for positive
m
and increased for negative
m
, resulting in a concentration of population in the
m=0
state, as illustrated in Fig.
1
(b). While this correlated state may be difficult to prepare, it illustrates the potential of exploiting correlations to manipulate the state of the environment.

The superconducting qubit scenario, together with the protocols introduced here, is discussed in detail in Sec.
IV
.

II.2
Quantum dot spin qubit

In the quantum dot spin qubit scenario, there is a large mismatch between the energy splitting of the qubit and the nuclear spins that constitute the environment
Coish et al. 2008
;
Jackson et al. 2022
;
Nguyen et al. 2023
. To enable energy transfer between the system and the environment, a Rabi drive with strength
\Omega
is applied close to resonance with the nuclei frequencies (Hartmann-Hahn resonance). This has two important consequences. First, the energy exchange between the system and the environment can be switched off by removing the Rabi drive. Second, the direction of the field
\vec{B}_{m}=(\Delta-m|A_{\rm c}|)\hat{e}_{z}+\Omega\hat{e}_{x}
depends on the magnetization of the environment, where
\Delta
is the detuning of the drive and
A_{\rm c}
is a coupling strength. For large positive (negative) magnetization, the field points down (up) in the
z
-direction. In between it rotates, pointing in the
x
-direction at
m|A_{\rm c}|=\Delta
. This
m
-dependence of the field direction implies that if we repeatedly prepare the state
\ket{\uparrow_z}
, the magnetization will decrease (increase) for large positive (negative)
m
, resulting in a concentration of population around
m=0
. This protocol has been implemented experimentally in Refs.
Nguyen et al. 2023
;
Hogg et al. 2024
, increasing the decoherence time of the spin qubit by one order of magnitude.

As for the superconducting qubit, further control of the bath is enabled by exploiting correlations. In the quantum dot spin qubit, a way to create such correlations is to switch off the Rabi drive and use the free evolution in the
m
-dependent field in a Ramsey protocol. In this manner, a state can be prepared with a Bloch vector that rotates in the
xz
-plane as a function of
m
, see Fig.
1
. This state will result in periodic increase and decrease of the magnetization as a function of
m
, creating peaks in its distribution by repeated preparation. A distribution with such qualitative features was observed experimentally in Ref.
Jackson et al. 2022
. Furthermore, as the locations of the peaks depend on the duration of the Ramsey protocol, all peaks except the one at
m=0
can be suppressed by changing this duration from preparation to preparation. This protocol was implemented in Refs.
Jackson et al. 2022
;
Nguyen et al. 2023
;
Hogg et al. 2024
, increasing the coherence time by two orders of magnitude.

The quantum dot spin qubit scenario and the protocols discussed here are presented in more detail in Sec.
V
.

III
Master equation for active reservoir engineering

We consider central spin models with an anisotropic hyperfine contact interaction,

H=\vec{B}_{0}\cdot\vec{S}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\omega_{k}I_{z}^{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\vec{S}\bm{A}\vec{I}_{k},

\vec{S}=(S_{x},S_{y},S_{z})
are spin-
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}

\vec{I}_{k}=(I^{k}_{x},I^{k}_{y},I^{k}_{z})
are spin-
s
operators describing the environment,
\omega_{k}
the frequencies of each bath constituent, and
\bm{A}
is a
3\times 3
coupling matrix. For simplicity, we consider that each spin in the environment couples identically to the system. Inhomogeneous couplings are considered in Apps.
A
,
B
. Equation (
1
) is beyond the integrable Gaudin magnets, mainly due to the anisotropy
Gaudin, M. 1976
;
Garajeu 2002

Schliemann et al. 2003

Tsyplyatyev and Loss 2011

Barnes et al. 2012

He et al. 2022

A master equation for active reservoir engineering (MARE) can be derived from Eq. (

;
Fischer and Breuer 2007
;
Breuer et al. 2006
;
Breuer 2007
;
Barnes et al. 2012

Riera-Campeny et al. 2021

Riera-Campeny et al. 2022

Riera-Campeny et al. 2022

A

J_{z}=\sum_{k}I^{k}_{z}
. The joint state of the qubit and the magnetization is denoted

The system density matrix can be obtained via
\rho_{S}(t)=\sum_{m}\rho_{m}(t)
and the probability distribution of the magnetization through
\tr\rho_{m}=P_{m}
. The MARE reads

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{m}=-i\quantity[\vec{B}_m\cdot\vec{S},
\rho_{m}]

(2)

\displaystyle+\Gamma_{m}\quantity[\dyad{\downarrow_m}{\uparrow_{m-1}}\frac{\rho_{m-1}}{V_{m-1}}
\dyad{\uparrow_{m-1}}{\downarrow_m} -\frac{1}{2}\qty{\dyad{\downarrow_m}{\downarrow_m}, \frac{\rho_{m}}{V_{m}}}\nonumber]

\displaystyle+\Gamma_{m+1}\quantity[
\dyad{\uparrow_m}{\downarrow_{m+1}}\frac{\rho_{m+1}}{V_{m+1}}
\dyad{\downarrow_{m+1}}{\uparrow_{m}} -\frac{1}{2}\qty{\dyad{\uparrow_m}{\uparrow_m} ,

\ket{\uparrow_m}

Our MARE has the general form shown in Ref.
Breuer 2007
to describe non-Markovian dynamics of
\rho_{S}

The first line in Eq. (
2

\vec{B}_{m}
. The second line corresponds to the jumps that increase the magnetization by flipping the system spin down (along the axis defined by
\vec{B}_{m}
), and the third line describes the jumps that flip the system spin up, decreasing the magnetization. Since a spin flip in the system is always accompanied by a change of magnetization by one, the total magnetization

M=m+\frac{1}{2}(\outerproduct{\uparrow_m}{\uparrow_m}-\outerproduct{\downarrow_m}{\downarrow_m}),

(3)

is a conserved quantity. This implies that each time the system is prepared in an initial state, the magnetization of the environment can change at most by one. When using a single qubit for active reservoir engineering, an iterative protocol is thus crucial to obtain a substantial manipulation of the environment, as depicted in Fig.
1

Riera-Campeny et al. 2021

can be leveraged to solve the MARE analytically.
Akin to a Lindblad master equation in the secular approximation, the diagonal and the off-diagonal elements of
\partial_{t}\rho_{m}
in the basis
\{\ket{\sigma_m}\},\penalty\ \sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow
decouple, and the populations evolve as,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p(\downarrow,m)

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{m}\quantity[\frac{p(\uparrow,m-1)}{V_{m-1}} - \frac{p(\downarrow, m)}{V_m}],

(4)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p(\uparrow,m)

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{m+1}\quantity[\frac{p(\downarrow,m+1)}{V_{m+1}} - \frac{p(\uparrow, m)}{V_m}],

(5)

where

The energy exchange between the system and the reservoir can thus be described by a classical rate equation for the joint probability,

To describe the scenarios illustrated in Fig.

III.1
Thermodynamics of active reservoir engineering

The process of removing entropy of the reservoir through the qubit is constrained by the laws of thermodynamics.
At step
2
in Fig.
1
(a), an initially pure state evolves together with the bath through the MARE; during this process the entropy of the system bath compound cannot increase, as we explain below.

More precisely, we model the system-bath entropy using the
observational entropy

Šafránek et al. 2019
;
Strasberg and Winter 2021
;
Riera-Campeny et al. 2021
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\text{obs}}=-\sum_{\sigma,m}p(\sigma,m)\ln p(\sigma,m)+\sum_{m}P_{m}\ln V_{m}.

(7)

The first term in Eq. (
7
) corresponds to the Shannon entropy of the joint probability
p(\sigma,m)
. It contains contributions of the marginal entropies, such as the bath Shannon entropy, and its mutual information
Riera-Campeny et al. 2021
. The Shannon entropy of the bath,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{B}=-\sum_{m}P_{m}\ln P_{m}

(8)

is central to engineering
P_{m}
—it quantifies the uncertainty about the magnetization,
m
. The Shannon entropy of the remaning marginal, can be related to the von Neumann entropy of the system (see App.
B.5
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\rho_{S})=-\tr\rho_{S}\ln\rho_{S}.

(9)

The second term in Eq. (
7
) is the average of the Boltzmann entropy,
S^{\text{Boltz}}_{m}=\ln V_{m}

Šafránek et al. 2019
.

With the conserved quantity we can establish the second law of thermodynamics,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{S}_{\text{obs}}\geq 0;

(10)

see App.
B.4
for a derivation.
The purpose of quantum active reservoir engineering is to use the qubit, initially pure, to decrease the Shannon entropy of the bath. As the qubit entropy rises, the second law limits how much the Shannon entropy of the bath can decrease through Eq. (
10
).

At step 1 of Fig.
1
(a) the qubit is reset, bringing the von Neumann entropy of the system to zero. Only at this stage can the entropy of the system-bath compound,
\mathcal{S}_{\text{obs}}
,
decrease
, as in a Szilard engine
Szilard 1929
;
Leff and Rex 1990
;
Spiecker et al. 2023
.

In summary, the thermodynamic principles of quantum active reservoir engineering are the following. (i) The redistribution of observational entropy during system-bath interactions, in which the qubit’s purity is consumed to lower the Shannon entropy of the bath. (ii) The resetting of the qubit, whereby the observational entropy is reduced bit-by-bit as the process is repeated.

III.2
Assumptions

The MARE relies on a number of assumptions that we summarize here, see Apps.
A
and
B
for more details.

First, we treat the part of the interaction that changes the magnetization perturbatively, i.e., the terms in Eq. (
1
) proportional to
J_{y}
and
J_{x}
. In contrast to previous approaches
Riera-Campeny et al. 2021
;
Riera-Campeny et al. 2022
, we treat the part of the interaction proportional to
J_{z}
non-perturbatively. This is particularly relevant to capture the dynamics over many cycles, when the magnetization gets substantially modified.

Second, we require the environmental decoherence time to be short compared to the energy exchange between system and bath (i.e. compared to the rates,
\Gamma_{m}
). In our model, this arises due to the spread in the frequencies
\omega_{k}
. However, we stress that any other decoherence mechanism would establish a finite bath decoherence time. This assumption justifies a classical description of the environment. We note that the MARE cannot capture any effect that requires quantum coherence in the environment, such as nuclear dark states
Appel et al. 2025
;
Cai et al. 2025
or the use of the enviroment as a quantum register
Denning et al. 2019
;
Appel et al. 2025
.

Third, the MARE relies on a secular approximation which assumes that the splitting of
\vec{B}_{m}\cdot\vec{S}
is much larger than the rates
\Gamma_{m}
for all values of
m
.

III.3
Quantum Fokker-Planck Master equation

In the limit of a large reservoir, the MARE can be expanded into a quantum Fokker-Planck master equation (App.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho(m)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(m)\rho(m)

(11)

\displaystyle-\partial_{m}\mathcal{A}(m)\rho(m)+\partial_{m}^{2}\mathcal{B}(m)\rho(m),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(m)\rho(m)

\displaystyle=-i[\vec{B}(m)\cdot\vec{S},\rho(m)]

(12)

\displaystyle+\Upsilon(m)\mathcal{D}[\outerproduct{\downarrow\!(m)}{\uparrow\!(m)}]\rho(m)

\displaystyle+\Upsilon(m)\mathcal{D}\quantity[\dyad{\uparrow\!(m)}{\downarrow\!(m)}]\rho(m),

where

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{m}_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{M}\frac{N}{N+1},

(18)

\displaystyle\langle S_{z}\rangle_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle M\rangle}{N+1}.

(19)

The long-time

For the second moment, the equation of motion is,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\expectationvalue{m^2}=-2\kappa\expectationvalue{m^2}+2\kappa N\expectationvalue{S_z m}+\kappa\frac{N}{2},

(20)

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle m^{2}\rangle\!\rangle_{0}-\expectationvalue{S_z}_{0}^{2}+2\expectationvalue{\!\expval{S_z m}\!}_{0}+\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}}),

where we used
\expectationvalue{M^2}=\expectationvalue{m^2}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}+2\expectationvalue{S_z m}

\langle\!\langle AB\rangle\!\rangle=\langle AB\rangle-\langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle,\penalty\ \varsigma=\sqrt{\langle\!\langle m^{2}\rangle\!\rangle}

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{m}_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\mu_{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}}),\hskip 14.22636pt\langle S_{z}\rangle_{\infty}=\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}}),

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle m^{2}\rangle\!\rangle_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle m^{2}\rangle\!\rangle_{\beta}-\frac{1}{4}+\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}}).

(23)

We now consider the following cyclic protocol, as sketched in Fig.

\expectationvalue{S_z}_{0}=-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}

The qubit thermalizes with the bath at each cycle and can be regarded as a thermometer; for the first cycles, its entropy reaches

Using correlations for engineering
P_{m}

In this section, we investigate how a correlated state allows to narrow the bath distribution. We first consider an ideal correlated state, before turning to a class of states inspired by recent experiments on spin qubits. V
Quantum-dot spin qubit

We use the MARE to understand the working principles behind a series of recent experiments in which a spin qubit is used to manipulate its nuclear environment
Jackson et al. 2022
;
Nguyen et al. 2023
;
Hogg et al. 2024
. These systems exhibit a wealth of interesting features at different time scales, due to the prominent role of strong system-bath interactions and system-bath correlations. Despite the complexity of these systems, we construct results based on analytic solution of Eq. (
2
).
Beyond reproducing the qualitative aspects of these experiments, the MARE provides a landscape of possibilities in engineering out-of-equilibrium nuclear states.

V.1
System and model

In a semiconducting quantum-dot, the Zeeman-split ground state of an electron or hole can be used to implement a spin qubit. The Zeeman field also affects the nuclear spins of the semiconductor substrate which lie in the range of the spin qubit’s wave-function
Coish and Loss 2004
and couple to the spin qubit via hyperfine interactions. The
nuclei have sharp frequencies, associated to the chemical
elements in the host semiconductor
Cywiński et al. 2009
. For instance, in GaAs quantum
dots there are two species, each with
s=\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}

Nguyen et al. 2023

Hogg et al. 2024

\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}
and
\nicefrac{{9}}{{2}}

N\approx 10^{4}-10^{5}
nuclei, and in Si the dominant isotope has spin
-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}

Cai et al. 2025
with
N\approx 10^{3}-10^{4}
nuclei.

The interaction between the qubit and the nuclei can have many different terms

Coish and Baugh 2009

With the goal of narrowing the bath distribution in mind, we now discuss how to use correlated states.

VI
Conclusions and outlook

We have developed a theoretical framework for active reservoir engineering. Our framework provides an intuitive understanding for how repeated preparation of a controlled quantum system can be used to manipulate its environment and drive it towards a target state. It recovers the qualitative behavior observed experimentally in different platforms, including superconducting qubits coupled to an environment of TLSs
Spiecker et al. 2023
, as well as spin qubits in quantum dots coupled to nuclear spins
Jackson et al. 2022
;
Nguyen et al. 2023
;
Hogg et al. 2024
. Our framework shows that the dynamics of the system and the reservoir is determined by the effective field felt by the system and the Bloch vector of the prepared state. Knowing the effective field as a function of the environment magnetization thus allows for developing effective strategies for active reservoir engineering.

Our framework enables the development of novel strategies for active reservoir engineering. Furthermore, it may provide insight into the Hamiltonian underlying an experiment. A promising avenue in this direction is to use the periodic narrowing of the nuclear spin distribution to map out the direction of the effective field as a function of magnetization. To make quantitative predictions, the approximations underlying our framework have to be carefully examined for the experiment of interest. In particular, long coherence times in the environment may prevent a classical description for the magnetization. In this case, the MARE provides a relevant benchmark to determine if the observed behavior of the reservoir is truly nonclassical and cannot be captured by our approach. In addition, the repeated initialization of the system may require to investigate time-scales shorter than the coherence time of the reservoir. This could be achieved by combining our framework with the coarse-graining approach for Markovian master equations
Schaller and Brandes 2008
. Another promising avenue is provided by modifying the projector operators allowing for coherent features in the reservoir
Breuer 2007
to describe dark states
Appel et al. 2025
;
Cai et al. 2025
, and including further non-perturbative contributions
Barnes et al. 2011
;
Tsyplyatyev and Loss 2011
;
Wu and Yu 2012
. The MARE describes a quantum system interacting with a reservoir that decoheres with respect to a given basis; it could be applied to contexts other than active reservoir engineering, such as the emergence of classical outcomes in a detector—treated as a reservoir—coupled to a quantum system
Allahverdyan et al. 2013
.

Appendix A
General Theory

In this Section we provide a derivation of the MARE for a general quantum systems. We discuss important properties and assumptions and provide a general quantum Fokker-Planck master equation via jump-length expansion.

A.1
Microscopic derivation of the generalized MARE

The MARE is a generalization of the results of Ref.
Riera-Campeny et al. 2021
;
Riera-Campeny et al. 2022
, in which we include the part of the system-bath interaction that does not lead to jumps in the bath nonperturbatively.

Projectors and rotating frame.—
We consider a system and a bath with Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle H=H_{S}+H_{B}+H_{SB}.

(47)

We introduce the correlated projector
superoperator
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\rho=\sum_{m}\rho_{m}\otimes\frac{\Pi_{m}}{V_{m}},

(48)

and the projectors into subspaces with a fixed
m
,
\Pi_{m}
. Our only assumptions about
m
are that it is the eigenvalue of an operator acting on the bath,
M_{B}
such that
[M_{B},H_{B}]=0
, with spectral decomposition,

\displaystyle M_{B}=\sum_{m}m\Pi_{m}.

(49)

The operators
\Pi_{m}
are orthogonal projection operators,
\Pi_{m}\Pi_{m^{\prime}}=\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}\Pi_{m}
, but not necessarily rank-1; their rank is denoted
V_{m}=\tr\Pi_{m}
and called
volume factor
. In the applications we are interested here,
m
denotes the magnetizatiom of the bath. When
m
corresponds to a coarse-grained energy, then the state
\Pi_{m}/V_{m}
denotes the microcanonical ensemble
Riera-Campeny et al. 2021
.

We now decompose the
H_{SB}
term in the Hamiltonian in two parts,

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=H_{S}+H_{B}+\delta H+V,

(50)

\displaystyle\delta H

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\expectationvalue{H_{SB}}_{m}\Pi_{m}

(51)

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=H_{SB}-\delta H,

(52)

and we used the microcanonical average
\expectationvalue{\circ}_{m}=\tr_{B}\{\circ\Pi_{m}/V_{m}\}
.

We will treat
V
perturbatively (not to be confused with the volume factors,
V_{m}
). Part of the interaction,
\delta H
, is treated non-perturbatively by working in an adequate rotating frame. To this end, we consider the rotation,

\displaystyle R(t)=e^{it(H_{S}+H_{B}+\delta H)}=e^{it(H_{S}+\delta H)}e^{itH_{B}},

(53)

where we note that, in general
[H_{S},\delta H]\neq 0
and
e^{it(H_{S}+\delta H)}=\sum_{m}R_{m}(t)\Pi_{m}
, with

\displaystyle R_{m}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{itH_{m}},

(54)

\displaystyle H_{m}

\displaystyle=H_{S}+\expectationvalue{H_{SB}}_{m}.

(55)

The rotation now writes,
R(t)=\sum_{m}R_{m}(t)\Pi_{m}e^{itH_{B}}
. If the system has only two levels, we can always write
H_{m}=\vec{B}_{m}\cdot\vec{S}
where
S_{x,y,z}
are spin operators—we will not make this assumption during the derivation below.

With this transformation, we have
H\to\tilde{V}=RVR^{\dagger}
. Henceforth, we work with the superoperator,
\mathcal{V}\circ=-i[\tilde{V},\penalty\ \circ]
, which fulfils,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\mathcal{V}\mathcal{P}=0.

(56)

This leads to the standard Nakajima-Zwanzig equation,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}\tilde{\rho}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\differential t\mathcal{P}\mathcal{V}(t)\mathcal{G}(t,t^{\prime})\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{V}(t^{\prime})\tilde{\rho}(t^{\prime}),

(57)

where
\mathcal{G}(t,t^{\prime})=\mathcal{T}\exp{i\int_{t'}^t\dd s \mathcal{Q}\mathcal{V}(s)}
is the memory kernel.

Weak coupling.—
Using that
V
is a perturbation, the expansion of the Nakajima-Zwanzig equation up to first non-zero order gives,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}_{m}=

(58)

\displaystyle-\sum_{m^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{t}\differential t^{\prime}\tr_{B}\Bigg\{\Pi_{m}\quantity[\tilde{V}(t),\qty[\tilde{V}(t'), \tilde{\rho}_{m'}(t')\frac{\Pi_{m'}}{V_{m'}}]]\Bigg\},

where we introduced the correlated projector,
\mathcal{P}
, explicitly.

It is always possible to write the interaction in the form

\displaystyle V=\sum_{\alpha}\mathsf{S}_{\alpha}\otimes\textsf{B}_{\alpha},

(59)

where
\textsf{S}_{\alpha}
acts on the system and
\textsf{B}_{\alpha}
on the bath. We then obtain,

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(t)=\sum_{\alpha,m,m^{\prime}}\tilde{\textsf{S}}_{mm^{\prime}}^{\alpha}\otimes\Pi_{m}\tilde{{\textsf{B}}}_{\alpha}\Pi_{m^{\prime}},

(60)

where we introduced
\tilde{{\textsf{B}}}_{\alpha}(t)=e^{itH_{B}}{\textsf{B}}_{\alpha}e^{-itH_{B}},\penalty\ \tilde{\textsf{S}}^{\alpha}_{mm^{\prime}}=R_{m}(t)\textsf{S}_{\alpha}R^{\dagger}_{m^{\prime}}(t)
. Note that, although
R(t)
in general does not preserve the original product structure of the interaction, mixing operators from system and bath, the product can be restored at the cost of introducing the sum over
m,m^{\prime}
. This is the immediate consequence of treating
\expectationvalue{H_{SB}}_{m}
exactly, and, as will become clear in the final form of the master equation, imprints a dependence on
m
in the basis of the jump operators. Substituting Eq. (
60
) in Eq. (
58
) we obtain,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}_{m}=\sum_{m^{\prime}\alpha\beta}\int_{0}^{t}\differential t^{\prime}C_{mm^{\prime}}^{\alpha\beta}(t^{\prime}-t)

(61)

\displaystyle\quantity[\tilde{\textsf{S}}_{mm'}^\alpha(t) \frac{\tilde{\rho}_{m'}(t')}{V_{m'}} \tilde{\textsf{S}}_{m'm}^{\beta}(t') - \tilde{\textsf{S}}_{mm'}^\alpha(t) \tilde{\textsf{S}}_{m'm}^{\beta}(t') \frac{\tilde{\rho}_{m}(t')}{V_{m}}]+\text{h.c.}

where we used the property,

\displaystyle\Pi_{m}\tilde{V}\Pi_{m^{\prime}}=\sum_{\alpha}\tilde{\textsf{S}}_{mm^{\prime}}\Pi_{m}\tilde{{\textsf{B}}}_{\alpha}\Pi_{m^{\prime}},

(62)

and introduced the microcanonical correlation functions,

\displaystyle C_{mm^{\prime}}^{\alpha\beta}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\tr_{B}\quantity{ \Pi_{m} \tilde{\bop}_\alpha(t) \Pi_{m'} \tilde{\bop}_\beta(t')},

(63)

\displaystyle=\tr_{B}\quantity{ \Pi_{m} \tilde{\bop}_\alpha(t-t') \Pi_{m'} \bop_\beta(0)}.

The dependence on
t-t^{\prime}
instead of
t,t^{\prime}
is a consequence of
[\Pi_{m},H_{B}]=0
. The correlation functions have the property,

\displaystyle C_{mm^{\prime}}^{\alpha\beta}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=C_{m^{\prime}m}^{\beta\alpha}[-(t-t^{\prime})].

(64)

The next steps follow closely the microscopic derivation of a Lindblad master equation. We will perform the Markov and secular approximations.

Markov approximation.—
We change variables to
\tau=t-t^{\prime}
, and set,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{m}\approx\sum_{m^{\prime}\alpha\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential\tau C_{mm^{\prime}}^{\alpha\beta}(\tau)\times

(65)

\displaystyle\quantity[\tilde{\textsf{S}}_{mm'}^\alpha(t) \frac{\tilde{\rho}_{m'}(t)}{V_{m'}} \tilde{\textsf{S}}_{m'm}^{\beta}(t-\tau) - \tilde{\textsf{S}}_{mm'}^\alpha(t) \tilde{\textsf{S}}_{m'm}^{\beta}(t-\tau) \frac{\tilde{\rho}_{m}(t)}{V_{m}}]

\displaystyle+\text{h.c.}

The integrals can be evaluated by introducing the Fourier modes of the operators

\displaystyle\tilde{\textsf{S}}^{\alpha}_{mm^{\prime}}(t)=\sum_{\omega}e^{-i\omega t}\textsf{L}_{mm^{\prime}}^{\alpha}(\omega).

(66)

As we now show, the frequencies
\omega
are related to the eigenfrequencies of
H_{m}
. Introducing the notation,
H_{m}\ket{\lambda_m}=\xi_{\lambda_{m}}\ket{\lambda_m}
we write the rotation matrices,

\displaystyle R_{m}=\sum_{\lambda}e^{it\xi_{\lambda_{m}}}\outerproduct{\lambda_m}{\lambda_m}

(67)

with which,

\displaystyle\tilde{\textsf{S}}^{\alpha}_{mm^{\prime}}(t)=\sum_{\lambda,\nu}e^{it\quantity(\xi_{\lambda_m} -\xi_{\nu_{m'}})}F^{\alpha\lambda\nu}_{mm^{\prime}}\outerproduct{\lambda_m}{\nu_{m'}},

(68)

where
F^{\alpha\lambda\nu}_{mm^{\prime}}=\bra{\lambda_{m}}\textsf{S}_{\alpha}\ket{\nu_{m'}}
are complex scalars satisfying,

\displaystyle F_{mm^{\prime}}^{\alpha\lambda\nu}=(F_{m^{\prime}m}^{\alpha\nu\lambda})^{*}.

(69)

In general, the eigenvectors from different
m
’s are not orthogonal, i.e.,
\bra{\nu_{m'}}\ket{\lambda_m}\neq\delta_{\lambda,\nu},
for
m\neq m^{\prime}
.

Equation (
68
) leads to the eigenfrequencies
\omega=\omega(\lambda_{m},\nu_{m^{\prime}})=-\xi_{\lambda_{m}}+\xi_{\nu_{m^{\prime}}}
, and the corresponding eigenoperators,
L^{\alpha}_{mm^{\prime}}(\omega)=F^{\alpha}_{\lambda_{m},\nu_{m^{\prime}}}\outerproduct{\lambda_m}{\nu_{m'}}
. Here we consider the case of nondegenerate frequencies, i.e., if
\omega(\lambda_{m},\nu_{m^{\prime}})=\omega(\lambda^{\prime}_{m},\nu^{\prime}_{m^{\prime}})
, then
\lambda_{m}=\lambda_{m}^{\prime}
and
\nu_{m^{\prime}}=\nu_{m^{\prime}}^{\prime}
. To simplify the notation, we carry on the calculation with the summation over
\omega
, keeping in mind the explicit dependence on the eigenstates and eigenvalues of
H_{m}
and
H_{m^{\prime}}
.
Using the Fourier decomposition we have,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{m}=\sum_{m^{\prime}\alpha\beta}\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}e^{it(\omega^{\prime}+\omega)}W_{mm^{\prime}}^{\alpha\beta}(\omega)

\displaystyle\quantity[\textsf{L}_{mm'}^\alpha(\omega) \frac{\tilde{\rho}_{m'}}{V_{m'}}\textsf{L}_{m'm}^{\beta}(\omega') - \textsf{L}_{mm'}^\alpha(\omega) \textsf{L}_{m'm}^{\beta}(\omega') \frac{\tilde{\rho}_{m}}{V_{m}}]+\text{h.c.},

(70)

where we introduced,

\displaystyle W^{\alpha\beta}_{mm^{\prime}}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential\tau e^{-i\tau\omega}C^{\alpha\beta}_{mm^{\prime}}(\tau).

(71)

Secular approximation.—
We retain only terms with
\omega^{\prime}=-\omega
. However, note that, since
V
is Hermitian,

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\textsf{S}_{\alpha}\otimes{\textsf{B}}_{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha}\textsf{S}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\otimes{\textsf{B}}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}.

(72)

That is, either the terms in the sum are Hermitian or the sum contains their Hermitian conjugate. For simplicity, let us assume henceforth that at least the system operators are Hermitian,
\textsf{S}_{\alpha}=\textsf{S}_{\alpha}^{\dagger},\penalty\ \forall\,\alpha
. This implies that,

\displaystyle\textsf{L}_{m^{\prime},m}^{\alpha}(-\omega)=[\textsf{L}_{m,m^{\prime}}^{\alpha}(\omega)]^{\dagger}=(F^{\alpha\lambda\nu}_{mm^{\prime}})^{*}\outerproduct{\nu_{m'}}{\lambda_m}.

(73)

Rotating back to the original frame, the master equation writes,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{m}=-i[H_{m}+H^{\text{LS}}_{m},\rho_{m}]+\sum_{m^{\prime}}\sum_{\lambda\nu}\Gamma_{mm^{\prime}}^{\lambda\nu}

(74)

\displaystyle\times\quantity[\dyad{\lambda_m}{\nu_{m'}} \frac{\rho_{m'}}{V_{m'}}\dyad{\nu_{m'}}{\lambda_{m}} -\frac{1}{2}\qty{\dyad{\lambda_m}{\lambda_m}, \frac{\rho_{m}}{V_{m}}}],

\displaystyle H^{\text{LS}}_{m}=-\sum_{m^{\prime}}\sum_{\lambda\nu}\zeta^{\lambda\nu}_{mm^{\prime}}\outerproduct{\lambda_{m}}{\lambda_{m}},

(75)

where

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\beta}W^{\alpha\beta}_{mm^{\prime}}F^{\alpha\lambda\nu}_{mm^{\prime}}\quantity(F^{\beta\lambda\nu}_{mm'})^{*}=:\Gamma_{mm^{\prime}}^{\lambda\nu}/2+i\zeta_{mm^{\prime}}^{\lambda\nu}.

(76)

From the properties in Eq. (
64
,
69
) the rates are symmetric tensors,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{mm^{\prime}}^{\lambda\nu}=\Gamma_{m^{\prime}m}^{\nu\lambda},

(77)

which is understood as generalized detailed balance condition.

In general, the above MARE does not dispose of a conserved quantity as Eq. (
2
).

It has been shown in Ref.
Breuer 2007
that under very general conditions the generator of non-Markovian evolution can be written as Eq. (
74
), but previous microscopic derivations
Riera-Campeny et al. 2021
;
Riera-Campeny et al. 2022
obtained a simpler structure. We have obtained the generator in its general form explicitly, i.e., with
m
-dependent jump operators.

A.2
Trace-preservation

The results from Ref.
Breuer 2007
assure that Eq. (
74
) generates a completely-positive and trace-preserving evolution (CPTP) of
\rho_{S}
.
Yet, it is instructive to show trace-preservation. Introducing,

\displaystyle q(m,\lambda,\nu)

\displaystyle=\bra{\lambda_m}\rho_{m}\ket{\nu_m}=[q(m,\nu,\lambda)]^{*},\penalty\ \lambda\neq\nu,

(78)

\displaystyle p(m,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\bra{\lambda_m}\rho_{m}\ket{\lambda_m},

(79)

we can decouple coherences from populations in Eq. (
74
),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}q(m,\lambda,\nu)=-\Bigg[i\quantity(\bar{\xi}_{\lambda_m} - \bar{\xi}_{\nu_m})

(80)

\displaystyle+\sum_{m^{\prime}\eta}\frac{\Gamma_{mm^{\prime}}^{\lambda\eta}+\Gamma_{mm^{\prime}}^{\nu\eta}}{2V_{m}}\Bigg]q(m,\lambda,\nu)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p(m,\lambda)=\sum_{m^{\prime}}\sum_{\nu}\Gamma_{mm^{\prime}}^{\lambda\nu}\quantity[\frac{p(m', \nu)}{V_{m'}} - \frac{p(m,\lambda)}{V_m}],

(81)

with
(H_{m}+H^{\rm LS}_{m})\ket{\lambda_m}=\bar{\xi}_{\lambda_{m}}\ket{\lambda_m}
.
Trace preservation of the system density matrix is equivalent to
\sum_{\lambda m}\partial_{t}P(m,\lambda)=0
. Performing the sum, we conclude that this is the case due to the detailed balance condition, Eq. (
77
).

A.3
Quantum Fokker-Planck Master Equation

If
N
is large enough, the discrete variable
m\in I(\mathds{Q})\subset\mathds{Q}
can be rescaled and approximated by a continuous interval,
I(\mathds{R})\approx I(\mathds{Q})/N
. On this wise, we seek an expansion on the jump length
\Delta x=x^{\prime}-x,\penalty\ x,\penalty\ x^{\prime}\in I(\mathds{R})
—this will lead to a quantum Fokker-Planck master equation.

Equation (
81
) is our starting point. Extending it to the continuum,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P(x,\lambda)=\sum_{\nu}\int_{I(\mathds{R})}\differential(\Delta x)\times

(82)

\displaystyle\quantity[\Upsilon^{\lambda\nu}(x-\Delta x|x)P(x-\Delta x,\nu) - \Upsilon^{\lambda\nu}(x|x-\Delta x)P(x,\lambda)],

where we introduced variables
\Delta x=x^{\prime}-x,\penalty\ x,\penalty\ x^{\prime}\in I(\mathds{R})
,
P(x,\lambda)=p(Nx,\lambda)
, and
the rates

\Upsilon^{\lambda\nu}(x^{\prime}|x)=\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}\delta(x-m/N)\delta(x^{\prime}-m^{\prime}/N)\frac{\Gamma^{\lambda\nu}_{m^{\prime}m}}{V_{m^{\prime}}},

(83)

which we will treat as smooth functions of
x
and
x^{\prime}
.

We focus on expanding Eq. (
82
); it is a (classical) master equation, and used to derive a Fokker-Planck master equation by the standard expansion method from Ref.
Kampen 2007
. The expansion is perfomed on the assumption that the length of the jumps are small. In the cases considered below, only the transitions
m\to m\pm 1
occur which correspond to
\Delta x=1/N
. If the expansion is justified,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P(x,\lambda)=-\sum_{\eta}\partial_{x}\quantity[c_1^{\lambda\eta}(x) P(x, \lambda)]+\partial_{x}^{2}\quantity[c_2^{\lambda\eta}(x)P(x, \lambda)]

(84)

where we introduced the first and second Kramers-Moyal coefficients (drift and diffusion),

\displaystyle c_{1}^{\lambda\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\differential(\Delta x)\Delta x\Upsilon^{\lambda\nu}(x+\Delta x|x),

(85)

\displaystyle c_{2}^{\lambda\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\differential(\Delta x)(\Delta x)^{2}\Upsilon^{\lambda\nu}(x+\Delta x|x).

(86)

The equation for the coherent sector is extended more simply, since it contains no jumps,
m\to m^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Q(x,\lambda,\nu)=

(87)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\quantity{2i\qty(\bar{\xi}_{\lambda(x)} - \bar{\xi}_{\nu(x)}) +\sum_\eta\qty[ c_0^{\lambda\eta}(x)+c_0^{\nu\eta}(x)]}Q(x,\lambda,\nu),

where
Q(x,\lambda,\nu)=q(Nx,\lambda,\nu)
and
c_{0}(x,\lambda)
is the zeroth-order Kramers-Moyal coefficient,

\displaystyle c_{0}^{\lambda\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\differential(\Delta x)\Upsilon^{\lambda\eta}(x+\Delta x|x).

(88)

Notably, the above coefficient also appears in the expansion of classical master equations, but the contributions from the zeroth order jump-terms cancel the ones from the zeroth order no-jump terms. Since only the no-jump terms contribute to the coherent sector, the coefficients
c_{0}^{\lambda\nu}
survive.

With this observation, we unify the decoupled equations in the form a quantum Fokker-Planck master equation,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(x)\rho(x)-\partial_{x}\mathcal{A}(x)\rho(x)+\partial_{x}^{2}\mathcal{B}(x)\rho(x),

(89)

where we introduced the
x
-dependendent Liouville superoperator,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(x)\rho(x)

\displaystyle=-i\quantity[H(x) + H_\text{LS}(x),~\rho(x)]

(90)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\lambda\nu}c_{0}^{\lambda\nu}(x)\mathcal{D}\quantity[\dyad{\lambda(x)}{\nu(x)}]\rho(x),

where
H(x)=H_{m=xN}
,
H_{\rm LS}(x)=H^{\rm LS}_{m=xN}
and
H(x)\ket{\lambda(x)}=\lambda(x)\ket{\lambda(x)}
. Further, we introduced

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}[L]\circ=L\circ L^{\dagger}-(1/2)\{L^{\dagger}L,\circ\},

(91)

and the drift and diffusion superoperators,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\nu}c^{\alpha\nu}_{1}(x)\mathcal{J}\quantity[\dyad{\lambda(x)}{\nu(x)}],

(92)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\nu}c^{\alpha\nu}_{2}(x)\mathcal{J}\quantity[\dyad{\lambda(x)}{\nu(x)}],

(93)

where we introduced the jump superoperator,
\mathcal{J}[L]\circ=L\circ L^{\dagger}
. As a final step, one can use
m=Nx
to obtain a continuous description for
m
, as presented in Eq. (
11
).

Inhomogeneous Anisotropic Central Spin Model

We now particularlize the microscopic derivation to a Hamiltonian that is more general than Eq. (

B.1

We consider a bath with

To compute the volume factor of a fixed total magnetization, we use a probabilistic approach: we consider the infinite temperature state, where each micro-state is equally likely. We then consider the probability,

\displaystyle\varsigma^{2}_{s}=\text{var}{(S_{z})}=\frac{1}{2s+1}\sum_{j=-s}^{s}j^{2}=\frac{s(s+1)}{3}.

B.3

We follow Ref.
Riera-Campeny et al. 2021

B.4
Second law of thermodynamics

The existence of a conserved quantity also allows us to generalize the second law of thermodynamics from Ref.
Riera-Campeny et al. 2021
to the MARE. We consider the observational entropy written in terms of Eqs. (
5

4

\displaystyle S_{\text{obs}}=\sum_{\sigma,m}p(\sigma,m)[-\ln p(\sigma,m)+\ln V_{m}].

(161)

We prove that,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}S_{\text{obs}}\geq 0.

(162)

The crucial step is to show that the above is minus the time-derivative of the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the generic probability vector
\vec{p}
and the corresponding steady-state vector,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}D_{\text{KL}}(\vec{p}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \vec{p}_{\text{ss}})=\partial_{t}\sum_{\sigma m}p(\sigma,m)\ln\frac{p(\sigma,m)}{p_{\text{ss}}(\sigma,m)}.

(163)

It sufficies to concentrate on,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sum_{\sigma,m}p(\sigma,m)\ln p_{\text{ss}}(\sigma,m)

(164)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\sum_{m}p(\uparrow,m)\ln p_{\text{ss}}(\uparrow,m)+p(\downarrow,m)\ln p_{\text{ss}}(\downarrow,m)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\sum_{m}\quantity[P(M = m+\nicefrac{1}{2}) - p(\downarrow, m+1)]\ln p_{\text{ss}}(\uparrow,m)

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ +p(\downarrow,m)\ln p_{\text{ss}}(\downarrow,m)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\sum_{m}p(\downarrow,m)\ln p_{\text{ss}}(\downarrow,m)-p(\downarrow,m+1)\ln p_{\text{ss}}(\uparrow,m),

where we used that
P(M)=\sum_{\sigma,m}\delta_{M,m+\sigma}p(\sigma,m),\penalty\ \partial_{t}P(M)=0,\penalty\ \partial_{t}p_{\text{ss}}(\sigma,m)=0
. Proceeding, we use Eq. (
160

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sum_{\sigma,m}p(\sigma,m)\ln p_{\text{ss}}(\sigma,m)

(165)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\sum_{m}p(\downarrow,m)\ln p_{\text{ss}}(\downarrow,m)

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\sum_{m}p(\downarrow,m+1)\ln p_{\text{ss}}(\downarrow,m+1)

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\sum_{m}p(\downarrow,m+1)\ln\frac{V_{m+1}}{V_{m}}.

The first two terms cancel each other upon change of indices and we are left with,

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\sum_{\sigma,m}p(\sigma,m)\ln p_{\text{ss}}(\sigma,m)=

(166)

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\sum_{m}\quantity[p(\downarrow, m+1) \ln V_{m+1} - p(\downarrow, m+1) \ln V_{m}]

\displaystyle=-\partial_{t}\sum_{m}\quantity[p(\downarrow, m) \ln V_{m} + p(\uparrow, m) \ln V_{m}]

\displaystyle=-\partial_{t}\sum_{\sigma,m}p(\sigma,m)\ln V_{m},

where we used again the definition of

and the conservation law,
\partial_{t}P(M)=0

With the result above, we have that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}D_{\text{KL}}(\vec{p}|\vec{p}_{\text{ss}})=-\partial_{t}S_{\text{obs}}

(167)

To complete the proof, we note that the dynamics of
\vec{p}
is Markovian, Eq. (

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}D_{\text{KL}}(\vec{p}|\vec{p}_{\text{ss}})\geq 0,

(168)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\text{rel}}=\mathcal{S}(\mathscr{D}\rho_{S})-\mathcal{S}(\rho_{S}).

(173)

We can then write the observational entropy as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\text{obs}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}_{B}(P)+\mathcal{S}(\rho_{S})+\mathcal{C}_{\text{rel}}

(174)

\displaystyle-D_{\text{KL}}[\vec{p}|P\penalty\ p_{S}]+\sum_{m}P_{m}\ln V_{m}

Finally, we argue that

Note that

\displaystyle D_{\text{KL}}(\rho_{S}|\mathds{1})
