Integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analysis reveals molecular mechanisms of melatonin-regulated hair follicle growth in cashmere goats and comparative insights across goat breeds
Authors: Luyao Xie, Chun Li, Jindong Tian, Shaoyin Fu, Rui Su, Cong Feng, Ming Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCashmere goats are prized for their high-quality fibers, which are soft, fine, strong, and exhibit remarkable elasticity and luster. Cashmere growth occurs in secondary hair follicles and follows a distinct, periodic cycle. This study seeks to offer a more comprehensive understanding of the regulatory mechanisms driving cashmere growth, as well as the impact of exogenous melatonin, by integrating transcriptomic and proteomic data.ResultsRNA-Seq data from skin samples of Inner Mongolia cashmere goats were analyzed, with the growth period divided into three stages. Differential genes and pathways involved in cashmere growth, including Wnt, BMP, Hippo, MAPK, TNF and JAK-STAT signaling pathways, were identified. Comparative transcriptomic and proteomic analyses between the control and melatonin groups revealed melatonin-sensitive key genes (MSKGs) such as DLX3, KRT35, KRT85, and KRTAP11-1. Enrichment analysis highlighted that melatonin influences signaling pathways such as Wnt, Hedgehog, ECM, FGF, and keratin-related genes, potentially regulating secondary shedding and hair follicle regeneration. The study also identified conserved genes and pathways related to cashmere growth in different cashmere goat breeds, including Wnt, Hippo, and JAK-STAT pathways.ConclusionsThis study provides valuable insights into the regulatory mechanisms of the hair follicle growth phase in cashmere goats, revealing how exogenous melatonin influences cashmere growth. It also highlights potential targets, such as ATP6V0A4, DLX3, and SMOC2, for future research in improving cashmere yield and quality. These findings lay the foundation for developing strategies to control cashmere growth and shedding across different goat breeds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12263-9.Keywords: Cashmere goat, Secondary hair follicle, Hair follicle growth cycle, Melatonin, Transcriptome, Proteome, Signaling pathway

Background

Cashmere goats are an essential genetic resource in China, valued for both their high-quality cashmere and meat production. Through long-term natural selection and artificial breeding, they have evolved into one of the world’s finest-fiber and highest-yielding cashmere breeds. Among them, the Inner Mongolia cashmere goat is particularly renowned for its long, white, impurity-free fibers with high luster and net cashmere rate, while the Shanbei White cashmere goat, developed through crossbreeding, exhibits high per-unit cashmere yield and superior fiber fineness. Beyond China, Italy plays a key role in the global cashmere industry as the world’s largest importer of raw cashmere. Although Italy’s domestic cashmere production is limited to small flocks, Italian cashmere goats follow a seasonal growth cycle similar to that of their Asian counterparts, underscoring the global significance of cashmere production [1].

The superior fiber quality and yield of cashmere goats are closely linked to their hair follicle growth cycle, which varies among breeds but follows a general seasonal pattern. The secondary hair follicles of Inner Mongolia, Shanbei White, and Italian cashmere goats follow typical growth cycles, each with its unique timeline. In Inner Mongolia cashmere goats, the anagen phase extends from April to November, during which hair follicles divide and elongate into the dermis, resulting in gradual cashmere growth. The catagen phase, from December to January, is marked by aging follicular cells, contraction of the dermal papillae, and a reduction in secondary follicles. The telogen phase, from February to March, sees the hair follicles enter a dormant state [2–4]. For Shanbei White cashmere goats, the anagen phase is from July to February, the catagen phase from March to May, and the telogen phase from May to July. The hair follicle cycle of Italian cashmere goats is similar to that of Asian cashmere goats. Morphological and transcriptomic analyses highlight Keratin 4 as a key marker of early anagen, aiding in the identification of optimal cashmere harvesting periods [1].

These cyclical growth of hair follicles is regulated by multiple factors, including hormones, signaling pathways, and key genes. With the rapid advancement of high-throughput sequencing technology, differential expression analysis and enrichment analysis have identified key regulatory elements involved in hair follicle modulation. Critical hormones and growth factors, such as melatonin [5], prolactin [6], and fibroblast growth factors (FGFs) [7, 8], contribute significantly to hair follicle regulation. Additionally, essential signaling pathways, including Wnt [9], MAPK [10], PI3K-Akt [8], Hedgehog [11], and NF-κB [9], play major roles in hair follicle development. Among the key genes identified, LHX2, FGF9, and WNT2 have been linked to cashmere traits, further underscoring the genetic complexity of hair follicle regulation [12]. Similarly, keratin genes (KRTs), keratin-associated genes (KAPs) [3] and bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) [13, 14] have also been proven to participate in hair follicle growth.

Among these regulatory factors, melatonin has been recognized as a key mediator between photoperiod and hair follicle development. Melatonin is a hormone secreted by the pineal gland, also known as pineal hormone. Its primary function is to convey light-dark cycle information, adjusting secretion based on the length of night to influence various bodily functions and respond to changes in photoperiod, such as seasonal rhythms [15]. The seasonal rhythm of cashmere growth is closely tied to the photoperiod. Under natural conditions, light exposure influences hormone levels in cashmere goats, regulating the activity of secondary hair follicles and affecting cashmere growth [16]. Studies have shown that melatonin directly regulates hair follicle growth by influencing follicle development and seasonal cashmere shedding [5, 17]. At the molecular level, studies have demonstrated that melatonin influences hair follicle cycling at the molecular level by regulating gene expression. Melatonin affects gene expression related to the hair follicle cycle by modulating immune-related transcription factors (e.g., JUNB, VDR, Trib3) and activating key signaling pathways such as Wnt, Hedgehog, ECM-receptor interaction, and NF-κB, which are crucial for follicle transition and secondary shedding [18]. RNA-sequencing analysis of melatonin-treated Inner Mongolian cashmere goats identified seasonal and monthly differentially expressed genes (SCGs and DEGs), revealing that melatonin particularly affects gene expression during the anagen phase. This process involves the activation of key signaling pathways such as Wnt, Hedgehog, ECM-receptor interaction, and NF-κB, which are critical for hair follicle regeneration and seasonal shedding [2].

Proteomics provides a dynamic perspective on gene regulation by measuring protein expression levels, offering deeper insights into gene expression control mechanisms. Recent studies have leveraged proteomic approaches to explore protein-level dynamics, uncovering novel regulatory pathways and expanding our understanding of hair follicle cycling. Gao et al. [19] studied the hair follicles of cashmere goat embryos and found that key genes and signaling pathways controlling hair follicle development exhibit dynamic changes at the protein and metabolic levels. Comprehensive analysis indicated that the oxytocin signaling pathway plays a sustained role in hair follicle development by activating MAPK and calcium ion signaling pathways. Zhao et al. [20] used proteomics to explore differences in protein abundance between fine and coarse cashmere in Tibetan cashmere goats, revealing potential candidate genes and related signaling pathways. Nai et al. [21] used SWATH mass spectrometry to identify differentially regulated proteins in Inner Mongolia cashmere goats, revealing their involvement in metabolism and fatty acid pathways. Protein interaction analysis highlighted keratins as key regulators of secondary hair follicle development, emphasizing their role in fiber growth. Han et al. [22] performed a proteomic analysis of secondary hair follicles during the telogen-to-anagen transition, identifying key signaling pathways and highlighted ADAM17, SFRP1, and PPP1CA as core regulators of follicle renewal. Additionally, integrating proteomics with transcriptomics has further advanced the understanding of hair follicle development. A combined analysis in Qinghai Tibetan sheep revealed concordant expression patterns at both protein and transcript levels, highlighting key proteins involved in metabolic processes and cell adhesion during primary hair follicle formation. This study underscored the interplay between transcriptional regulation and post-translational modifications in follicle initiation [23]. However, while this research examined primary hair follicle development in sheep embryos, similar studies on secondary hair follicles in cashmere goats remain scarce. In particular, the impact of melatonin on the cashmere goat hair follicle cycle has yet to be thoroughly investigated using a proteo-transcriptomic approach.

To investigate the regulatory mechanisms of hair follicle anagen phases, with a particular focus on the molecular effects of exogenous melatonin on cashmere shedding and regeneration, we divided the anagen period of Inner Mongolia cashmere goats into three stages and identified differential genes and signaling pathways involved in each stage of cashmere growth. By integrating transcriptomic and proteomic results and comparing the control group with the melatonin-treated group, we identified critical genes related to the cashmere anagen period in Inner Mongolia cashmere goats. Furthermore, RNA-Seq technology was employed to analyze differentially expressed genes in various breeds of cashmere goats, and these results were compared with gene clusters from Inner Mongolia cashmere goats to identify conserved genes and signaling pathways involved in cashmere anagen across different breeds.

Methods

Skin sample collection

Skin samples were collected from six healthy Inner Mongolia Hanshan White Cashmere Goats provided by the Hanshan White Cashmere Goat Breeding Farm. The goats were randomly divided into two groups: the control group (n = 3) and the melatonin group (n = 3), with the latter receiving subcutaneous melatonin implants (2 mg/kg body weight) at the ear base every two months. Prior to sampling, the goats were anesthetized using 1% pentobarbital sodium, and the right middle flank area was prepared by shearing the hair and disinfecting the skin with alcohol. Approximately 1 cm² of skin tissue was excised using sterile forceps and scalpel blades, with each subsequent sample collected adjacent to the previous site to minimize tissue damage. Yunnan Bai Yao powder (Chinese medicine: Z53020798) was applied immediately to control bleeding. The samples were collected monthly from April to September, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent RNA extraction and sequencing. The transcriptome sequencing data of Italian cashmere goats and Shanbei cashmere goats are obtained from NCBI SRA with accession PRJNA556407 [1] and PRJNA477237 [24], respectively.

RNA extraction, library construction and sequencing

Total RNA from 36 skin samples was extracted using TRIzol™ reagent (Invitrogen, USA) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA purity was determined with a NanoPhotometer® spectrophotometer (Implen, USA). Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) was depleted from 3 µg of total RNA using the Epicenter Ribo-zero™ rRNA removal kit (Epicentre, USA). RNA libraries were constructed with the NEBNext® Ultra™ Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) according to the provided protocol. The libraries were then sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform, utilizing strand-specific sequencing.

Quality control, alignment and expression quantification

FastQC (v0.12.0, https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) was used to filter out low-quality bases and adapters from the raw reads generated by RNA-seq. The quality of clean reads (Q20 and Q30) was tested. High-quality clean reads were retained for subsequent analysis. STAR [25] (v2.7.11b) was used to build a genome index and align clean reads with the goat genome (NCBI assembly ARS1.2). RSEM [26] (v1.3.3) was used to quantify the results and remove genes with no expression or low expression to obtain the expression matrix required for subsequent analysis.

Differential expression analysis and gene cluster detection

The likelihood ratio test (LRT) of DESeq2 [27] (v1.46.0, https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/DESeq2.html) was used to identify differentially expressed genes in the Inner Mongolia cashmere goat control group, the Italian cashmere goat during the growth period regression period, and the Shanbei cashmere goat during the growth period rest period using the “~time” model. The LRT of the “~condition” model was used to detect differentially expressed genes between the control group and the melatonin group. The expression matrix was normalized using the sizeFactor estimated by DESeq2 to obtain the normalized expression matrix. The weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) [28] (v1.73, https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/WGCNA/index.html) method was used to analyze the differentially expressed genes detected in the Inner Mongolia cashmere goat control group. The Pearson correlation between the gene cluster and the trait data (age in months) was calculated.

Pathway enrichment

AnnotationHub (v3.14.0, https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/AnnotationHub.html) was used to retrieve annotation information from the Ensembl database to generate OrgDb annotation files. The differential genes were annotated using Gene Ontology (GO) [29] and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [30] analysis. Annotations are all implemented through the clusterProfiler [31] (v4.14.6, https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/clusterProfiler.html). Functional items with adjusted P-value < 0.05 in GO and KEGG results are significantly enriched functions of differential genes.

Protein extraction, digestion and quantification process

Sample proteins were extracted by adding steel balls, lysate (8 M urea/50 mM Tris-HCl) and Roche inhibitor, processing on ice, homogenizing with a tissue grinder and centrifuging to collect the supernatant. Protein quantification was performed using the Bradford method, in which the sample protein concentration was calculated by measuring the OD595 value and protein purity was analyzed by SDS-PAGE. Then, 150 µg of protein was extracted from each sample, 3 µg of trypsin was added, and the peptides were digested by incubation at 37 °C for 14–16 h. The peptides were separated by nano-liquid chromatography (Thermo EASY-nLC™ 1200 system) with gradient elution on a C18 column and detected by an Orbitrap Exploris™ 480 mass spectrometer in DIA mode with a scanning range of 350–1500 m/z and a resolution of 120,000 m/z in HCD fragmentation mode.

Protein identification and quantification

The DIA-NN software [32] (v1.8) was used to search and identify proteins and conduct quantitative analysis on the DIA mass spectrometry data. The main parameters of DIA-NN were as follows: the protein identification mode was DirectDIA (no actual DDA data was required to construct a spectrum library, and a theoretical virtual spectrum library was constructed directly from the protein sequence library of the corresponding species using information analysis methods), the enzyme cutting parameter was set to Trypsin/P, the maximum number of missed cuts was set to 2, the fixed modification was Carbamidomethyl (C), and the variable modifications were Oxidation (M) and Acetyl (Protein N-term). The protein identification confidence strategy was Target-decoy, and the protein identification threshold was spectral false positive (PSM FDR) < 0.01 and protein FDR < 0.01.

Proteomic statistical analysis

The differential protein analysis between the control group and the melatonin group was completed by the metaX [33] package. The two conditions of P-value < 0.05 and fold change >1.2 obtained by T test are simultaneously satisfied to screen out significantly different proteins. The pathway enrichment method is the same as the transcriptome pathway enrichment method. Functional items with P-value < 0.05 are significantly enriched functions of differential proteins.

Result

Differential expression and stage-specific expression during the anagen period of hair follicles in inner Mongolia cashmere goats

In Inner Mongolia cashmere goats, 36 samples (control group C and melatonin group M) were collected monthly from April to September, with 3 biological replicates per group each month (Fig. 1A). The data yielded 1.34 billion clean reads. RNA-seq analysis identified 18,032 expressed genes, with 4,921 differentially expressed genes in the C group (fold change ≥ 1, P-value ≤ 0.05) (Additional file 1).

Previous studies have dynamically analyzed the hair follicle growth cycle based on transcriptome data, dividing it into the anagen, catagen, and telogen phases, with the anagen phase exhibiting distinct stage-specific expression patterns [2]. We conducted a WGCNA analysis on transcriptome data from the anagen phase of cashmere goats, identifying 4,921 differentially expressed genes. A co-expression network was constructed, and hierarchical clustering based on the topological overlap matrix (TOM) was applied to detect five distinct co-expression modules, each assigned a unique color by the WGCNA algorithm. Among them, MEturquoise and MEblue emerged as the key modules due to their significant correlations with temporal traits. Clustering analysis revealed a high degree of similarity among genes within the same module, while MEturquoise and MEblue exhibited relatively independent co-expression relationships. Additionally, MEblue and MEyellow exhibited partially similar temporal correlation patterns, particularly during the early and late months, in contrast to the inverse trends observed in MEturquoise. Correlation analysis demonstrated that MEturquoise was negatively associated with traits from April to May but positively associated from August to September, whereas MEblue displayed the opposite trend (Fig. 1B). These findings suggest that MEturquoise (CC1) and MEblue (CC2) represent key gene clusters potentially regulating the anagen phase.

Gene expression trends (Fig. 1C) and referencing previously reported phenotypic data on fiber length changes [2] suggest that the anagen phase (April–September) can be subdivided into three distinct stages: April and May as Stage 1 (S1), June and July as Stage 2 (S2), and August and September as Stage 3 (S3). CC1 gene expression gradually increased from S1 to S3, with a significant upregulation starting in August, while CC2 gene expression showed an opposite downward trend, with a significant downregulation beginning in August. Based on these expression patterns and functional enrichment analysis, we inferred the roles of CC1 and CC2 in the anagen phase. CC1 is associated with the upregulation during the anagen phase, promoting cashmere growth, while CC2 is related to the slower cashmere growth and hair follicle degeneration.

CC1 contains a total of 1,572 genes (Additional file 2), which are positively correlated with the progression of the anagen phase. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted on CC1. GO enrichment analysis (Fig. 1D) (Additional file 3) of the CC1 revealed that its genes are primarily involved in biological processes such as skin development, hair follicle development, hair cycle, hair cycle process, and hair follicle morphogenesis, suggesting a crucial role in promoting hair follicle development and cashmere fiber growth. In addition, CC1 is enriched in several classical regulatory pathways, most notably the Wnt signaling pathway, which has been extensively validated to be closely associated with hair follicle growth. Wnt is considered a major regulator in hair follicle morphogenesis, with the Wnt/β-catenin canonical pathway playing a critical role in initiating hair follicle morphogenesis, maintaining and inducing the regeneration of dermal papilla cells, and promoting hair shaft growth. Andl et al. [34] found that Wnt signaling is essential for initiating hair follicle development. Key transcription factors within the CC1 module—such as DLX3, HOXC13, and LEF1 (Additional file 2) — confirm some downstream regulatory mechanisms of hair follicle development and the hair growth cycle. DLX3 and LEF1 are downstream regulators of Wnt signaling, with DLX3 being a crucial transcriptional regulator of hair formation and regeneration [35], while LEF1 is a transcription factor involved in regulating hair follicle development and hair shaft differentiation. Both have the function of promoting the maturation of secondary hair follicles [36]. Moreover, CC1 is enriched in GO terms related to response to BMP, indicating its potential role in modulating the anagen phase of hair follicles via the BMP signaling pathway. It has been shown that BMP signaling primarily regulates the differentiation of the hair shaft in hair follicles [13]. Through signaling between dermal papilla cells and follicular epithelial cells, BMP influences the expression of critical transcription factors (e.g., FOXN1 and HOXC13), thereby affecting keratin synthesis and hair shaft structure formation. BMP-responsive genes in CC1 may help sustain hair follicles in an active growth phase by regulating these differentiation processes, thus accelerating cashmere fiber growth. Furthermore, CC1 contains keratin genes (KRTs) (Additional file 2) such as KRT25, KRT26, KRT27, KRT35, KRT36, KRT39, KRT74, and KRT84, along with keratin-associated proteins like KRTAP11-1 and KRTAP3-1, which are implicated in hair follicle development [2, 37]. Additionally, KEGG pathway analysis (Additional file 4) revealed enrichment of the Hippo signaling pathway in CC1. This highly conserved pathway in mammals is involved in regulating diverse biological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, and organ size [38]. Previous studies have also implicated Hippo signaling in promoting hair follicle growth.

CC2 contains a total of 1,538 genes that are negatively correlated with the progression of the anagen phase and may be associated with slowing the growth rate of cashmere. GO (Fig. 1D) (Additional file 3) and KEGG (Additional file 4) enrichment analyses show that this module is significantly enriched in pathways such as organ growth, cell adhesion, the ERK1/2 cascade, response to cAMP, extracellular matrix (ECM) organization, MAPK, JAK-STAT, Fc epsilon RI, TNF, and Toll-like receptor signaling pathways. Among them, the ERK1/2 and MAPK signaling pathways play key roles in regulating hair follicle stem cell states and transitioning between hair cycle phases. ERK1/2 signaling has been shown to mediate VEGF-induced proliferation in outer root sheath cells [39]. The MAPK pathway is also crucial for maintaining the quiescent state of hair follicle stem cells and regulating the transition between hair growth phases [40]. Furthermore, the JAK-STAT pathway, involving JAK3 and STAT5B, is dynamically regulated throughout the hair growth cycle and plays a role in maintaining hair follicle quiescence. Inhibiting JAK-STAT signaling can promote hair growth by stimulating the activation or proliferation of hair follicle stem cells [41]. The TNF signaling pathway plays a certain role in hair regeneration. Studies have found that micro-injury-induced TNF-α surge can stimulate hair regeneration in mice [42]. Macrophages in the wound promote the transformation of hair follicles around the wound from the telogen phase to the anagen phase and hair follicle regeneration through TNF signal transduction [43]. The TNF ligand superfamily (TNFSF9, TNFSF14) and TNF receptor superfamily (TNFRSF1B) are highly expressed in the CC2 and gradually decrease thereafter (Additional file 5). In addition, the endogenous Wnt inhibitor Dickkopf1 (DKK1) was found in CC2 (Additional file 2). DKK promotes the degeneration of hair follicles by inhibiting Wnt/β-catenin signal transduction and inducing apoptosis of follicular keratinocytes [44].

Effects of exogenous melatonin on the anagen phase of cashmere growth in inner Mongolia cashmere goats

To reveal the differential gene expression patterns during the anagen phases of Inner Mongolia cashmere goats under the influence of exogenous melatonin, we used DESeq2 to identify differentially expressed genes between the C group and the M group, with criteria of fold change ≥ 1 and adjusted P-value ≤ 0.05. Pairwise comparisons identified 531 differential genes in the S1 phase, 190 in the S2 phase, and 154 in the S3 phase (Fig. 2A) (Additional file 6).

To explore the differential protein expression patterns under the influence of exogenous melatonin during the anagen phase of Inner Mongolia cashmere goats, we employed label-free DIA quantitative proteomics technology to analyze 36 samples, investigating the protein composition, expression differences, and corresponding biological functions. After protein identification and standardized quantification, 3,900 reliable proteins were obtained. Significant differential proteins between the C group and the M group were screened using univariate analysis, with T-test fold change > 1.2 and P-value < 0.05. Pairwise comparisons identified 80 differential proteins in the S1 phase, 280 in the S2 phase, and 915 in the S3 phase (Fig. 2B) (Additional file 7).

Transcriptome functional enrichment analysis

Transcriptome GO and KEGG results (Additional file 8 and 9) show that the S1 stage is related to skin epidermal development, hair cycle, hair follicle morphogenesis and development, with the relevant signaling pathway being the Wnt signaling pathway. The expression of Wnt-related genes (Wnt11, LEF1, FZD10, HOXC13, DLX3, FOXN1) was upregulated. Previous studies have established that the Wnt signaling pathway plays a crucial role in the activation of hair follicle cell division and the promotion of hair follicle development [45]. Potter et al. [46] confirmed that HOXC13 can regulate the expression of FOXN1, and knocking down these two genes can lead to abnormal hair growth. Moreover, several other genes, including KRT27, KRT35, and KRTAP11-1, were also upregulated in the S1 stage (Additional file 6). Chai et al. [18] showed that the expression of these genes, related to wool and cashmere growth, might be influenced by melatonin and linked to interaction networks involving immune-related genes, thus confirming the association of these KRT and KRTAP genes with cashmere growth. Taken together, the upregulation of Wnt-related genes (Wnt11, LEF1, FZD10, HOXC13, DLX3, FOXN1) and other key genes such as KRT27, KRT35, and KRTAP11-1 (Additional file 6) indicates that these factors are likely the driving forces behind the rapid growth observed during the S1 stage.

In the S2 stage, the KEGG results (Additional file 9) indicated that upregulated genes were enriched in the calcium signaling pathway (CASQ2, CHRNA7, EDNRB, FGF5). Research on the FGF5 gene suggests that it inhibits hair growth by suppressing the activation of dermal papilla cells and induces aging [47]. ECM receptor interaction-related genes (COL1A1, COL1A2, COL6A3, FREM1, FREM2, SV2C, THBS3) were downregulated in the S2 stage. ECM regulates cell proliferation, migration, and differentiation by interacting with cell surface molecules, and plays an important role in the regulation of biological processes and the formation of cell functions. Studies have confirmed that ECM is a crucial matrix necessary for hair follicle cells growth. Xu et al. [48] demonstrated that enhanced interaction between ECM and cells may play a vital role in the hair growth process. Zhu et al. [49] found that the rapid growth of hair follicles in the growth phase of cashmere goats requires high expression of ECM and cell surface proteins. In addition, chemokines, key factors of the ECM pathway, and others promote the secondary shedding of cashmere and subsequent growth after shedding [2]. Therefore, the upregulation of FGF5, which is associated with the inhibition of hair growth, combined with the downregulation of ECM-receptor interaction-related genes in the S2 stage, suggests that these changes may be responsible for the slowing of cashmere growth during this phase.

Based on the KEGG and GO analysis results, a downregulation trend was observed in the S3 phase for several key genes involved in signaling pathways related to Wnt-related genes (NOTUM, SFRP2, WNT6), Hedgehog-related genes (SHH, PTCH2), and FGF-related genes (FGF2, FGF21). NOTUM is a lipase whose main function is to remove the carboxyl group of Wnt protein to inhibit its signaling activity [50], thereby playing a role in negatively regulating the Wnt signaling pathway. Its downregulated expression might positively impact Wnt signaling activity and be associated with hair follicle development following secondary cashmere shedding. In contrast, SFRP2 is involved in the transduction of Wnt signaling and was generally considered to be an antagonist of Wnt protein in the past because it binds to Wnt protein and prevents the activation of the signal [51]. However, subsequent studies have found that SFRP2 can actually enhance the transduction of Wnt signaling [52]. In the S3 stage, the downregulation of SFRP2 expression may be related to the shedding of cashmere, which may lead to a slowdown in the growth rate of cashmere and promote the occurrence of secondary shedding. The Hedgehog signaling pathway is involved in regulating epidermal development, hair follicle development, and the maintenance of hair follicle stem cells, and it interacts with signal transduction pathways such as Wnt, BMP, and Notch [53]. It has been found that excessive activation of the Hedgehog signaling pathway can significantly accelerate hair growth in mice and induce hair follicle proliferation [11]. The FGF family has multiple biological functions. In the process of hair growth, FGF2 and FGF21 play an important role. Among them, FGF2 is involved in regulating the proliferation of hair follicle cells. Previous studies have shown that adenosine can stimulate the growth of mouse dermal papillae and prolong the growth period through FGF2 and FGF7 [54]. FGF21 participates in hair follicle development and can promote the development of secondary hair follicles [55]. In summary, the downregulation of Wnt-related genes (NOTUM, SFRP2, WNT6), Hedgehog-related genes (SHH, PTCH2), and FGF-related genes (FGF2, FGF21) during the S3 stage appears to play a crucial role in inhibiting hair follicle proliferation, which could lead to the onset of secondary cashmere shedding.

Proteomic functional enrichment analysis

The proteomic KEGG results (Additional file 10) showed that in the S3 stage, the differential proteins were mainly related to human diseases, such as Staphylococcus aureus infection, Vibrio cholerae infection, neurodegenerative diseases (including Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease), etc. The up-regulated proteins related to Staphylococcus aureus infection suggest the involvement of the KRT family, highlighting the importance of KRT in hair follicle development [19]. Some differential proteins were enriched in synthesis and metabolism processes, such as arginine and proline metabolism up-regulated in the S1 stage, and amino acid biosynthesis, cysteine and methionine metabolism up-regulated in the S2 stage.

The proteomic GO results (Fig. 2D) (Additional file 11) showed that in the S1 stage, the up-regulated differential proteins were mainly enriched in intermediate filaments and intermediate filament cytoskeleton, with up-regulated KRT (such as KRT32, KRT35, KRT40) and KRTAP (such as KRTAP11-1, KRTAP13-3) genes related to hair follicle development and growth. In the S2 stage, some up-regulated proteins overlapped with the intermediate filaments and intermediate filament cytoskeleton from the S1 stage, and the genes related to KRT (KRT17, KRT32, KRT35, KRT39, KRT71, KRT72, KRT74, KRT75, KRT84) and KRTAP (KRTAP11-1, KRTAP13-3, KRTAP3-1) involved in the development and growth of hair follicles were up-regulated. The up-regulated proteins were significantly enriched in epidermal and skin development, hair cycle, hair follicle development and morphogenesis (DLX3, DSG4, KRT17, KRT71, KRT84, TP63) and keratin fibers, keratinization, etc. In the S2 and S3 stages, collagen-related proteins (such as COL1A1, COL1A2, COL2A1, DPT, FMOD, LOX, LUM) exhibited a down-regulation trend. Research suggests that COL1A1 and COL1A2 are key genes for cashmere hair regeneration, encoding the main components of ECM and participating in focal adhesion and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways [56]. Moreover, FGF21, a down-regulated protein gene in the S3 stage, aligns with the results of transcriptome enrichment analysis. Therefore, the up-regulated proteins in the S1 and S2 stages (such as KRT32, KRT35, KRTAP11-1, KRTAP13-3, and DLX3) may promote the development and growth of hair follicles, while the down-regulated collagen-related proteins (such as COL1A1, COL1A2, and COL2A1) and FGF21 in the S3 stage may be associated with the inhibition of hair follicle growth and secondary cashmere shedding.

Key genes in cashmere anagen phase under the influence of exogenous melatonin

To identify the melatonin-sensitive key genes (MSKGs) in the cashmere anagen phase of Inner Mongolia cashmere goats, we first screened out the co-expressed genes (CEGs) that appeared in both transcriptomic and proteomic analyses. By filtering based on stages and regulatory patterns (Fig. 3A), we obtained CEGs that were co-expressed in the transcriptome and proteome at different stages and under different regulatory conditions. Then, these genes were integrated and deduplicated to obtain CEGs in the transcriptome and proteome, of which there were 23 commonly up-regulated genes (T_P_UP) and 20 commonly down-regulated genes (T_P_DOWN) (Fig. 3B). Finally, we identified the overlapping genes between T_P_UP and CC1 differential genes, as well as between T_P_DOWN and CC2 differential genes, which led to the discovery of MSKGs related to the cashmere anagen phase in Inner Mongolia cashmere goats. Among the up-regulated MSKGs are ATP6V0A4, CRNN, DLX3, FABP7, KAP8, KRT32, KRT35, KRT40, KRTAP11-1, LCN2, LOC100861181, LOC102176457, LOC102181374, LOC102181431, LOC102184693, RNF39 and SMOC2. The only down-regulated MSKG identified is PCOLCE2.

Conservative genes related to hair growth in different cashmere goat breeds and their analysis

Ten Italian cashmere goat samples yielded 681 million clean reads after quality control. Five samples each belonged to the anagen and catagen phases. RNA-seq analysis identified 3,839 differentially expressed genes (fold change ≥ 1, P-value ≤ 0.05) (Additional file 12). Six Shanbei white cashmere goat samples produced 558 million clean reads after quality control. Three samples each were in the anagen and telogen phases. RNA-seq identified 5,365 differentially expressed genes (fold change ≥ 1, P-value ≤ 0.05) (Additional file 13).

To identify conserved genes (CGs) related to cashmere growth in different breeds of cashmere goats, we compared the upregulated genes in Italian cashmere goats (Italian_U) with the common expression genes between Inner Mongolian cashmere goats CC1, and the downregulated genes in Italian cashmere goats (Italian_D) with the common expression genes between Inner Mongolian cashmere goats CC2. We also compared the upregulated genes in Shanbei cashmere goats (Shanbei_U) with CC1 and the downregulated genes in Shanbei cashmere goats (Shanbei_D) with CC2 (Fig. 4A). Subsequently, we performed enrichment analysis on these CGs. KEGG (Fig. 4B) results revealed significant enrichment pathways in the common downregulated and upregulated CGs in Italian and Shanbei cashmere goats. Italian cashmere goats jointly down-regulated CGs were enriched in the JAK-STAT pathway, which is dynamically regulated during the hair cycle to maintain the quiescent state of hair follicles. Its activity is inhibited in the early growth phase, increases in the catagen and telogen phases, and reaches its peak in the middle of telogen [41, 57]. Shanbei cashmere goats jointly up-regulated CGs were enriched in the Wnt signaling pathway and Hippo signaling pathway, both of which have been proven to be closely related to hair growth. Studies have shown that genes in the Hippo signaling pathway overlap with genes in the Wnt and TGF-β signaling pathways [58]. Liu et al. [59] found through transcriptome analysis that the key pathways of Hippo, TGF-β, and MAPK are related to cashmere growth. GO (Fig. 4C) (Additional file 14) results showed that the common up-regulated CGs in Italian cashmere goats were primarily enriched in hair follicle morphogenesis and BMP signaling pathways. Previous studies have indicated [14] that BMP plays a key role in regulating hair follicle differentiation and growth, affecting the control of epidermal cell differentiation and apoptosis during development, and plays an important role in initiating the anagen phase of hair follicles after birth. One study found that BMP regulates hair follicle cycle through inhibition of Wnt/β-catenin activity [60], while another study pointed out that BMP-mediated hair follicle stem cell activation can be balanced through paracrine TGF-β signaling [61].

Therefore, the up-regulation of genes related to negative regulation of the BMP signaling pathway (CHRD, DLX1, FZD1, GPR155, SMAD6, SMAD7) and hair follicle morphogenesis-related genes (DLX3, KRT25, KRT27) may be associated with hair follicle development and cashmere growth. The common down-regulated CGs in Italian cashmere goats were mainly enriched in the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, consistent with the KEGG results. In addition, they were enriched in organ growth and collagen metabolism processes. Thus, down-regulation of JAK-STAT signaling pathway-related genes (CSF1R, CSF2RA, IL6R, IL6ST, IL7R, TNFRSF18) and collagen-related genes (ADAMTS14, DDR2, MMP2, MMP25) may be the reason for the inhibition of cashmere goat hair follicle growth and development. Furthermore, to explore whether exogenous melatonin affects cashmere growth in other breeds, we compared the differential genes in Italian and Shanbei cashmere goats with the MSKGs. By screening for common up-regulated or down-regulated genes, we ultimately found that only the up-regulated genes in Italian cashmere goats, ATP6V0A4, DLX3, and SMOC2, showed common expression.

Discussion

In this study, we focused on skin samples from Inner Mongolian cashmere goats in both the control and melatonin groups, covering the period from April to September, which corresponds to the growth phase of the hair follicle cycle. Many studies have used RNA-Seq technology to explore a series of regulatory factors and signaling pathways involved in the cyclical growth of hair follicles. For example, Geng et al. [62] used RNA-Seq to analyze samples from three stages and identified key genes as well as signal pathways that may be involved in hair follicle development at different stages. Li et al. [2] conducted transcriptome sequencing analysis on cashmere goats at different stages throughout the year, identified key genes involved in the regulation of hair growth in each period, and divided the hair follicle growth cycle into three stages, of which the growth period is from April to October.

Based on WGCNA analysis, two key gene clusters, CC1 and CC2 were identified. Combined with expression and functional enrichment results, it was inferred that CC1 is positively correlated with the progression of the anagen phase and may be involved in promoting hair follicle growth and cashmere development, while CC2 is negatively correlated with the progression of the anagen phase, possibly involved in the later stages of hair follicle regression and the slowing down of cashmere growth. Additionally, based on the gene expression trends of CC1 and CC2 in Fig. 1C, the period from April to September was divided into three stages: S1 (April to May), S2 (June to July) and S3 (August to September). The expression of CC1 genes gradually increased from S1 to S3, with a significant up-regulation starting in August, while CC2 genes showed an opposite trend, with a significant down-regulation starting in August. CC1 was mainly enriched in the Wnt, BMP and Hippo signaling pathways. CC2 was mainly enriched in pathways such as Toll-like receptor, TNF, JAK-STAT, Fc epsilon RI, cAMP, and ECM-receptor interaction, etc.

Moreover, by comparing samples from the control and melatonin-treated groups during the growth phase in Inner Mongolian cashmere goats, stage-specific differential genes and proteins were identified. Integrated transcriptomic and proteomic enrichment analysis results revealed that Wnt-related genes (Wnt11, LEF1, FZD10, HOXC13, DLX3, FOXN1), KRT-related genes (KRT27, KRT32, KRT35, KRT40) and KRTAP-related genes (KRTAP11-1, KRTAP13-3, KRTAP3-1) might be the initiators of the rapid growth phase in S1; up-regulation of the FGF5 gene and down-regulation of ECM receptor interaction-related genes in S2 might be associated with the slowing down of cashmere growth; and down-regulation of Wnt-related genes (NOTUM, SFRP2, WNT6), Hedgehog-related genes (SHH, PTCH2), FGF-related genes (FGF2, FGF21) and collagen fiber tissue (COL1A1, COL1A2, COL2A1, DPT, FMOD, LOX, LUM) in S3 might be related to inhibiting hair follicle proliferation and promoting secondary cashmere shedding. Based on the above differential genes and proteins, this study identified 43 CEGs (Fig. 3B) by comparing the data of the three stages (Fig. 3B). After further screening these genes for co-expression with the key gene clusters (CC1 and CC2), MSKGs related to the cashmere anagen phase in Inner Mongolian cashmere goats were confirmed, including the up-regulated genes ATP6V0A4, CRNN, DLX3, FABP7, KAP8, KRT32, KRT35, KRT40, KRTAP11-1, LCN2, LOC100861181, LOC102176457, LOC102181374, LOC102181431, LOC102184693, RNF39, SMOC2, and the only down-regulated gene PCOLCE2.

Existing studies have demonstrated that the genes DLX3, KAP7.1 (LOC100861181), KAP8, KAP13.1 (LOC102181431), KRT32, KRT35, KRT40, KRT85 (LOC102184693) and KRTAP11-1 are primarily expressed in skin tissue and are associated with cashmere growth (Fig. 3B). DLX3 is a transcription activator involved in hair follicle differentiation and cycle, positively regulates the expression of skin genes, is located in the regulatory cascade downstream of Wnt signaling, and acts as an upstream regulator of other transcription factors (HOXC13) that regulate hair follicle differentiation. Studies have found that DLX3 can promote hair follicle maturation through the dermal papilla and inner and outer root sheaths, and DLX3 deficiency can lead to hair follicle defects [36], further verifying its importance in hair follicle development and cashmere growth. KRT is a family of fibrous structural proteins that constitute the main proteins of hair and the outer layer of skin. Numerous studies have shown that KRT, KRTAP, and KAP genes encode the majority of cashmere and hair proteins and are involved in hair follicle development and cashmere formation and growth. KAP7.1, KAP8.2, KAP13.1, KRT32, KRT35, and KRT85 are primarily involved in regulating hair fiber morphology and diameter. Research has found that KAP7.1 and KAP8.2 are differentially expressed in hair follicles, participate in hair formation and are essential for the regulation of cashmere fiber diameter [63]. Increased levels of KAP13.1 protein may lead to a reduction in wool fiber diameter [64]. KRT32, KRT35, and KRT85 are expressed in the cuticle and fiber cortex [65]. Correlation analysis and protein interaction network analysis have indicated that KRT family members such as KRT32 are key factors contributing to differences in hair fiber diameter [66]. Yu et al. [67] identified the spatial expression pattern of KRT35 during the hair follicle growth phase, suggesting that KRT35 and KRT85 play roles in determining fiber morphology. KRT40 and KRTAP11-1 are primarily involved in hair follicle development, with KRTAP11-1 potentially playing a key role in the assembly of keratin bundles in the hair cortex. Liang et al. [68] studied the expression of KRTAP11-1 in different tissues during the hair follicle development stage, discovering that KRTAP11-1 mRNA expression levels initially increase and then decrease during the catagen and telogen phases, which promoted the development of hair follicles. Furthermore, studies have shown that the KRTAP11-1 gene is highly expressed in a tissue-specific manner in the wool hair follicles of Tibetan sheep, with in situ hybridization experiments revealing its symmetrical expression throughout the wool cortex [69]. A search of the STRING database [70] for these key genes in goats found that KRT35 protein interacts with DLX3, KRT85, and KRTAP11-1 proteins, further confirming the importance of these genes in hair follicle development and cashmere growth. This pattern encompasses the key differential genes and differential protein-coding genes related to cashmere growth during the anagen phase from April to September. The regulatory patterns of melatonin can be roughly divided into three categories: (1) In S1 and early S2, cashmere enters a rapid growth period after the first shedding. (2) In late S2 and early S3, cashmere growth is inhibited, which may be a prelude to the second shedding in the later period. (3) In late S3, cashmere sheds for the second time and induces hair follicle regeneration and secondary development. The rapid growth of hair follicles may be related to the increase in the expression level of keratin-related genes (such as KAP8, KRT32, KRT35, KRT40 and KRTAP11-1) and may also be related to the high expression of WNT11, FZD10, LEF1, DLX3 in the Wnt signaling pathway and the downstream transcription factors HOXC13 and FOXN1 regulated by them. When the growth rate of cashmere slows down and secondary shedding occurs, it may be because the expression level of FGF5 is up-regulated during the S2 stage, the expression of ECM-related genes such as COL1A1 and COL1A2 is low, and the down-regulation of FGF2 expression and Wnt signaling pathway-related genes such as WNT6, SHH, PTCH2, and SFRP2 in the early S3 stage. After the second shedding of cashmere, the reduced expression levels of the FGF21 and NOTUM during the S3 stage may be involved in promoting the secondary development and regeneration of the hair follicles.

In addition to studying stage-specific gene expression in the hair follicle anagen phase of Inner Mongolian cashmere goats and identifying key genes involved in hair follicle development and hair growth under the influence of exogenous melatonin, this study also helps to determine the CGs and signaling pathways related to cashmere hair growth in different breeds of cashmere goats. We screened gene expression in Italian cashmere goats and Shanbei cashmere goats, comparing it with the expression in Inner Mongolian cashmere goats. The results showed that the genes expressed by Inner Mongolia cashmere goats and Italian cashmere goats were more similar than those of Shanbei cashmere goats. Subsequently, an enrichment analysis was conducted on these CGs. The genes commonly up-regulated in Italian and Inner Mongolian cashmere goats mainly involved the negative regulation of the BMP signaling pathway (CHRD, DLX1, FZD1, GPR155, SMAD6, SMAD7) and hair follicle morphogenesis (DLX3, KRT25, KRT27). In contrast, the genes commonly upregulated in Shanbei and Inner Mongolian cashmere goats were mainly related to the Wnt signaling pathway and the Hippo signaling pathway. These results suggest that these genes may play significant roles in hair development in cashmere goats. The genes down-regulated in Italian and Inner Mongolian cashmere goats were mainly enriched in the JAK-STAT pathway (CSF1R, CSF2RA, IL6R, IL6ST, IL7R, TNFRSF18) and collagen biosynthesis processes (ADAMTS14, DDR2, MMP2, MMP25), with the down-regulation of these genes potentially contributing to the inhibition of hair follicle growth and development in these cashmere goat breeds. Furthermore, this study also compared the differential genes in Italian and Shanbei cashmere goats with the MSKGs related to the cashmere anagen phase in Inner Mongolian cashmere goats. The results revealed that only the ATP6V0A4, DLX3, and SMOC2 genes, which are up-regulated in Italian cashmere goats, showed co-expression, indicating that exogenous melatonin might also have an effect on cashmere growth in Italian cashmere goats, with genes like DLX3 potentially serving as important targets for future research.

However, despite providing important clues to the understanding of cashmere growth, this study also has some limitations. Notably, the key genes identified have not yet undergone experimental validation, which limits a deeper understanding of their functions and mechanisms of action. Therefore, future research can focus on experimentally validating the functions of these key genes, such as determining their exact role in hair growth through gene silencing or over-expression experiments. In addition, transgenic animal models or cell culture systems can be employed to further explore the molecular mechanisms of these genes in cashmere growth. Through these experimental studies, we can have a more comprehensive understanding of the regulatory mechanism of cashmere goat hair growth and provide more targeted technical support for the development of the cashmere goat hair industry.

Conclusions

In summary, this study systematically analyzed RNA-Seq data from skin samples of Inner Mongolia cashmere goats during the anagen phase and categorized this phase into three stages based on its molecular and phenotypic characteristics. Key differentially expressed genes and pathways involved in cashmere growth were identified, including Wnt, BMP, Hippo, MAPK, TNF and JAK-STAT signaling pathways. Through the analysis of MSKGs (e.g., DLX3, KRT35, KRT85, and KRTAP11-1) during the anagen phase of Inner Mongolia cashmere goats, along with KEGG and GO analyses of transcriptomic and proteomic data, we hypothesize a regulatory pattern of exogenous melatonin on the anagen phase of hair follicles. Conserved genes and pathways related to cashmere growth, including Wnt, Hippo, and JAK-STAT, were identified across different goat breeds. Comparative transcriptomic analysis further suggest that melatonin may also influence cashmere growth in Italian cashmere goats, with ATP6V0A4, DLX3, and SMOC2 emerging as potential research targets. These findings provide valuable insights into the molecular regulation of hair follicle growth, laying a foundation for optimizing cashmere yield and quality.
