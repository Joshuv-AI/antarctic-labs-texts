Pesticide exposure and the risk of arterial hypertension and obesity in farm workers: a systematic review of literature
Authors: Johannes Ulrich, Lena Jäggi, Daniel Rüfli, Samuel Fuhrimann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFarm workers experience some of the highest chronic pesticide exposures worldwide, yet potential links with key cardiovascular risk factors remain underexplored. This systematic review focuses specifically on associations between occupational pesticide exposure and hypertension or obesity in farm-exposed adult populations, highlighting two major, modifiable cardiovascular risk factors in a high-risk group.MethodsMEDLINE was systematically searched for observational studies of hypertension and/or obesity among adults with farm-related pesticide exposure. Using the PECOS framework, two reviewers independently screened records and extracted data on study design, population, exposure assessment, outcomes, and main findings.ResultsOf 355 records, 14 studies met inclusion criteria (10 cross-sectional, 2 case–control, 2 cohort). Most assessed general pesticide use (n = 9), with fewer examining specific pesticide groups, chemical classes, or active ingredients (each n = 4). Obesity was positively associated with organophosphates (n = 3), atrazine (n = 1), and general pesticide exposure (n = 5), while two studies reported inverse associations; no other active ingredient showed consistent effects. General pesticide exposure was associated with hypertension in three studies, but overall evidence for blood pressure was heterogeneous.ConclusionsOccupational exposure to organophosphates, atrazine, and general pesticide use may increase obesity risk among farm workers, suggesting a potential contribution to cardiovascular disease, whereas associations with hypertension remain uncertain. Interpretation is constrained by limited, heterogeneous data and by a predominant focus on compounds now restricted or banned in many high-income settings. Future longitudinal studies should apply standardized, quantitative exposure assessment and evaluate currently used pesticides and mixtures in diverse agricultural contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05967-7.Keywords: Pesticides, Farm Workers, Obesity, Hypertension, Organophosphates, Atrazine, Cardiovascular Risk

Background

According to the World Health Organization, cardiovascular disease (CVD) accounted for 17.9 million deaths in 2019, making it the leading cause of mortality globally [1]. Age-standardized CVD mortality rates vary widely by region, ranging from 73.6 to 432.3 deaths per 100,000 population [2], underscoring its substantial contribution to the global burden of disease. While age-standardized mortality has declined in many areas due to advancements in medical care, a rise in crude CVD mortality is projected in the coming years [3]. This trend is expected to place an increasing economic burden on healthcare systems, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [4]. Notably, arterial hypertension and obesity have emerged as major global risk factors for CVD, especially among young adults, and their prevalence is anticipated to grow further by 2050 [3, 5].

CVD encompasses four main entities: coronary artery disease (also referred to as coronary heart disease), cerebrovascular disease, peripheral artery disease, and aortic atherosclerosis. Approximately half of the global incidence of CVD and one-fifth of CVD-related mortality are attributable to five modifiable risk factors: obesity, hypertension, elevated low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, tobacco smoking, and diabetes mellitus [6].

Pesticides can adversely affect human health through mechanisms such as endocrine disruption, neurotoxicity, and oxidative stress, as has been well documented in studies on cancer, neurological disorders, as well as reproductive and respiratory outcomes [7–10]. Building on this body of evidence, emerging research has suggested that pesticide exposure may also play a role in the development of CVD, with several systematic reviews and meta-analyses reporting associations, particularly for organochlorines [11–13]. Specifically, a growing number of studies have explored the relationship between pesticide exposure and diabetes [14–20], largely motivated by the hypothesis that certain pesticides act as endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDC) that interfere with glucose and lipid metabolism, thereby increasing diabetes risk. Since diabetes is a well-established risk factor for CVD, these findings raise the possibility that pesticides may also contribute to cardiovascular outcomes.

Several plausible biological mechanisms support this potential link. Endocrine-disrupting effects may promote metabolic disturbances leading to obesity, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. Besides, pesticide-induced inflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction may accelerate atherosclerosis and vascular damage. Taken together, these pathways provide strong grounds to hypothesize that pesticide exposure contributes to CVD both directly and indirectly through its influence on modifiable risk factors [21, 22].

There has therefore been growing interest in examining obesity and hypertension as intermediary outcomes to better understand the potential indirect effects of pesticide exposure on CVD. Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have reported associations with obesity [23–25] and hypertension. However, the available evidence is limited and inconsistent. This inconsistency may be attributable to conflicting results between human and animal studies, the small size of many studies and the heterogeneity of investigated substances and administration routes [25]. When multiple pesticides are evaluated simultaneously, it often remains unclear whether observed effects can be attributed to a single substance or to combined effects resulting from exposure to mixtures. Furthermore, particularly in human research, the available evidence is largely derived from observational studies, which may limit causal inference. Moreover, most findings pertain to organochlorines such as the DDT metabolite p, p’-DDE, often in the context of prenatal exposure, whereas evidence on other pesticide classes—including pyrethroids, dichlorophenols, and organophosphates—is sparse.

These gaps are particularly notable in adult populations with ongoing, high-level exposures. Farmers and farmworkers represent such a group: they are routinely exposed to pesticides during mixing, application, and equipment cleaning [26], and they also carry a higher burden of CVD and its risk factors [27–29]. Reasons for this association may vary considerably across geographic regions and between high-income and middle-/low-income countries. Part of the increased risk may be related to low socioeconomic status, a well-established cardiovascular risk factor, as well as to behavioral risk factors such as smoking [30, 31]. In addition, structural factors, including limited access to healthcare and inadequate follow-up in an industry that often relies on seasonal or migrant workers, may play a role [32]. Finally, occupation-specific exposures, such as pesticide exposure, may also contribute to the development of cardiovascular risk factors.

Despite this, no comprehensive investigation has specifically assessed the relationship between occupational pesticide exposure and obesity or hypertension in farmworkers. Given that both risk factors can be readily diagnosed with simple anthropometric and blood pressure measurements, they represent practical markers for surveillance and prevention in occupational health. This review thus aimed to provide a comprehensive assessment of the existing literature with three specific objectives: first, to evaluate the association between occupational pesticide exposure and the cardiovascular risk factors of obesity and hypertension in farmworkers; second, to examine whether evidence differs by pesticide class and type, active ingredient, and study design; and third, to identify key methodological gaps and research needs to guide future studies and inform public health and occupational safety policies.

Methods

A systematic literature search was conducted on October 28, 2024, to identify relevant studies examining the association between pesticide exposure and cardiovascular risk factors among farm-exposed populations in the PubMed/MEDLINE database via OVID (see Supplementary Materials for the full search strategy). The review was structured using the PECOS framework (Population, Exposure, Comparator, Outcome, Study design), as detailed in Table 1. No restrictions were applied regarding publication date or language. The chemical-family terms (Table 1) were included as examples of frequently studied pesticide classes to improve sensitivity; this list was not intended to be exhaustive. The overall search combined general pesticide terms with class/family-specific terms and was adapted from a previously published strategy used by our group [33], tailored to the current review question.

PECOS framework

The inclusion criteria for this systematic review were: (a) original peer-reviewed articles published as full texts; (b) all epidemiological study designs, including meta-analyses; (c) studies reporting pre- and/or postnatal pesticide exposure; (d) study populations consisting of adults ( = > 18 years) who are farm workers or individuals living on farms; and (e) outcomes of arterial blood pressure and/or BMI. For clarity, we use the term hypertension to refer to categorical outcomes defined by study-specific diagnostic criteria (e.g., blood pressure cut-offs and/or antihypertensive medication use), and blood pressure to refer to continuous systolic/diastolic measurements; where studies used alternative terms (e.g., ‘arterial hypertension’ or ‘high blood pressure’), we report these as described but apply the above nomenclature consistently in the synthesis.We included studies assessing pesticide exposure at any life stage (prenatal, postnatal/childhood, or adulthood), because the relevant exposure window and latency for adult cardiometabolic outcomes are uncertain. Outcomes were required to be measured in adulthood.

The exclusion criteria were: (a) studies focusing on children (17 years and younger) or populations not related to farming; (b) studies investigating environmental contaminants other than pesticides; (c) studies addressing pregnancy-induced hypertension or preeclampsia; (d) studies that did not report specific measurements of arterial blood pressure or BMI; (e) studies focusing on acute pesticide poisoning, which may cause transient blood pressure abnormalities; (f) case reports, case series, and review articles; (g) experimental studies using model organisms; and (h) laboratory-based studies.

Study selection followed PRISMA guidelines [34]. All retrieved records were imported into Rayyan (https://rayyan.ai), an online systematic review management tool, where automated deduplication was performed. Two independent reviewers screened titles, abstracts, and full texts for eligibility. Data from included studies was extracted using a standardized form, capturing key information including study design, participant characteristics, methods of pesticide exposure assessment, and measured outcomes.

Results

Study selection and flow diagram

A total of 356 potentially relevant records were identified through the database search. After removing one duplicate, 355 records were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 26 articles were selected for full-text review. One full-text article, published in Russian, was not retrievable. Following full-text screening, 14 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final review. A detailed summary of the study selection process is provided in Fig. 1.

Sample sizes ranged from 70 to 106,111 participants. Study populations included farmers, farm residents/family members, pesticide applicators, and female greenhouse workers [37]. Two studies compared conventional and organic farming [39, 40]. The type of agricultural activity or specific crops cultivated was often not clearly specified.

While most studies included both male and female participants, two studies focused exclusively on male subjects [42] or predominantly male subjects (97% male) [47], and one study was limited to female participants [37]. Notably, all but one study [44] reported the number of male and female participants; however, none presented outcome data (BMI or hypertension) stratified by sex.

Pesticide exposure

Pesticide exposure assessment differed notably across studies (Table 2). We categorized exposure reporting into four levels: (i) general pesticide use/contact, (ii) pesticide group (e.g., insecticides, herbicides, fungicides), (iii) chemical class (e.g., organophosphates, pyrethroids), and (iv) active ingredient (e.g., atrazine, glyphosate). In this review, “general pesticide use/contact” refers to overall pesticide exposure without specification of group, class, or active ingredient, typically based on self-reported pesticide use/contact or work-related pesticide activities.

Most studies (n = 9) assessed exposure as general pesticide use/contact, without further specification [35–38, 40, 42, 44, 46]. Four studies reported exposure at the pesticide-group level [39, 41, 45, 47], four at the chemical-class level [36, 41, 43, 46], and four at the active-ingredient level [41, 45, 47, 48]. While most studies assessed only one exposure level (n = 9), some assessed two (n = 3) or three (n = 2) levels; none assessed all four exposure levels (Fig. 2A, B). Among classes, organophosphates were most frequently examined (n = 4), followed by carbamates (n = 2) and pyrethroids (n = 2). Glyphosate was the most frequently assessed compound (n = 3), while mancozeb, paraquat, malathion, and 2,4-D were each reported in two studies; all other compounds appeared only once (Fig. 2C, D).

Self-reported exposure was the predominant approach, generally captured by interviewer-administered questionnaires, structured interviews, or app-based questionnaires, and in several studies included duration/frequency metrics or cumulative indices [39, 40, 45–47]. Two studies used biomarker-based exposure assessment: one quantified pesticide residues in blood and measured acetylcholinesterase activity [35], and another analyzed urinary pesticide metabolites/biomarkers [43]. In one of the Indian studies [35], exposure was assessed using blood biomarkers, including quantification of multiple pesticide residues by LC–MS/MS and measurement of acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity; the authors reported detection of pesticide residues spanning insecticides, fungicides, and herbicides, with several analytes present at higher concentrations in the exposed/case group compared with controls. In the Korean case–control study [43], exposure was assessed using spot urine samples with quantitative analysis by GC/MS; biomarkers included urinary dialkyl phosphate metabolites of organophosphates (dimethylphosphate [DMP], dimethylthiophosphate [DMTP], diethylphosphate [DEP], and diethylthiophosphate [DETP]) and urinary pyrethroid metabolites (cis-DCCA, trans-DCCA, DBCA, and 3-phenoxybenzoic acid [3-PBA]). Both biomarker-based studies relied on single time-point sampling (spot urine in [43] and single blood sampling [35]), which may not reflect longer-term exposure patterns. One additional study quantified a specific active ingredient biomarker (malathion blood concentration measured by thin-layer chromatography) in the context of a farmer population, but did not clearly parameterize broader pesticide exposure beyond this analyte [48]. For one study, exposure characterization was insufficiently described to fully reproduce the exposure construct [36].

Health outcomes

Across studies, outcomes included BMI/overweight/obesity and/or blood pressure/hypertension (Table 2). Six studies evaluated adiposity outcomes without assessing blood pressure [37, 39, 41, 45–47], seven assessed both adiposity and blood pressure outcomes [35, 36, 38, 40, 43, 44, 48], and one study focused on hypertension only [42] (Fig. 2D). Definitions varied: obesity/overweight was usually based on BMI thresholds, sometimes with waist circumference [40, 45], while some studies used self-reported height/weight or self-reported medical diagnosis [38, 41, 47] and in a small number of studies the measurement method was not clearly reported [35, 43]. Hypertension definitions also differed, with only a few studies provided detailed protocols for blood pressure measurement: three studies reported measuring blood pressure after a rest period of 5 to 30 min [37, 44, 48], and only one study conducted two measurements and used the average value [42]. Another study relied on self-reported diagnoses of hypertension but partially validated these through random checks against hospital records [38]. Two studies applied explicit cut-offs (e.g., ≥ 140/90 mmHg) [40, 42], but others analysed continuous SBP/DBP without cut-offs [37, 39, 44, 48]. For adiposity outcomes, several studies used measured anthropometry, whereas others relied on self-reported height/weight or diagnosis [38, 41, 47]; in a small number of studies, the measurement approach was not reported [35, 43].

Adjustment for confounding factors varied across studies. Where adjustment was performed, the most common covariates included age and sex, often alongside smoking and alcohol consumption, and indicators of socioeconomic status (education/income); several studies additionally adjusted for physical activity and/or diet-related variables [40, 42, 45, 46]. In approximately half of the studies, either no multivariable adjustment was performed or the adjustment strategy was not reported [35–38, 44, 48].

Overall, the direction and strength of associations varied by outcome, exposure definition, and study design. For adiposity outcomes (BMI/overweight/obesity), seven of 13 studies reported at least one positive association between pesticide exposure and higher BMI/overweight/obesity [39–41, 44–48], two studies reported an inverse association (lower BMI in exposed groups) [37, 39], and four studies reported no statistically significant association [35, 36, 38, 48]. Positive associations were most consistently reported for organophosphate-related exposure metrics (e.g., long-term use or biomarker-based indicators) [43, 45] and for atrazine in the cohort setting [47], although effect sizes and definitions differed across studies. For blood pressure/hypertension outcomes (n = 8), four studies reported evidence consistent with higher blood pressure or higher hypertension prevalence among exposed groups [39, 40, 42, 48], while four studies reported no clear association [35–37, 44]. Several estimates were close to conventional significance thresholds (e.g., aPR 1.95, 95%CI 0.96–3.47 in [42]; aRR 1.54, 95%CI 0.98–2.42 in [40]), underscoring the importance of considering effect size and precision in addition to statistical significance.

Discussion

This systematic review investigated the association between occupational pesticide exposure and two major modifiable cardiovascular risk factors, adiposity (BMI/overweight/obesity) and blood pressure/hypertension, among adult farmworkers. Across 14 studies, evidence was limited and heterogeneous. For adiposity outcomes, several studies reported higher BMI, overweight, or obesity among exposed groups, whereas others reported no association or lower BMI. Evidence for blood pressure/hypertension was based on fewer studies and was less consistent. These mixed findings likely reflect differences in exposure definitions and measurement, variability in outcome definitions and ascertainment (including measured versus self-reported outcomes and varying diagnostic thresholds), and inconsistent control for confounding. The predominance of cross-sectional designs and uneven geographic coverage, with no evidence from Europe, further constrain comparability and causal interpretation. In addition, exposure windows varied widely, precluding identification of a single critical period and underscoring the importance of considering exposure timing and study design when interpreting results.

Grouping pesticides by intended use (e.g., insecticides, herbicides, fungicides) is relevant for occupational practice and prevention, but these categories are toxicologically heterogeneous and provide limited mechanistic insight. By contrast, class- or ingredient-specific assessments are more informative biologically but were less common. Organophosphates were assessed in four studies, while carbamates and pyrethroids were assessed in two studies each (Table 2). At the active-ingredient level, evidence was sparse. Atrazine (an herbicide) was examined in two studies, with one cohort study reporting a positive association between atrazine exposure metrics and BMI change, whereas other pesticide groups/ingredients did not show a consistent pattern within that cohort. Overall, the current literature does not support firm conclusions regarding specific compounds, but it highlights candidate exposures (notably organophosphate-related metrics and atrazine) that merit more rigorous evaluation.

Internal validity

The internal validity of the available evidence is strongly influenced by how pesticide exposure was measured. Most included studies relied on self-reported exposure histories, which are vulnerable to recall error and exposure misclassification, especially when exposure is captured as a coarse yes/no variable or when pesticide-related tasks are not differentiated. This is especially relevant in agricultural settings where pesticide contact can occur through multiple routes beyond application, including mixing and storage, equipment cleaning, re-entry work in treated fields or greenhouses, laundering contaminated clothing, and take-home exposure. Pesticide-related tasks may also vary substantially between individuals and change across seasons and over a working life, further increasing exposure misclassification when broad proxies are used [49]. Such misclassification can attenuate associations and may differ by sex if exposure definitions align primarily with application tasks, while women may have relatively greater exposure through re-entry or household-adjacent activities.

Where chemical classes were examined, several studies reported positive associations between organophosphate-related exposure measures and BMI. This pattern is biologically plausible given that some organophosphates have endocrine-disrupting properties. Endocrine-disrupting chemicals have been implicated in the global obesity epidemic and support the “environmental obesogens” hypothesis, whereby such exposures may influence metabolic and hormonal pathways involved in adipocyte differentiation and the regulation of appetite and satiety [50, 51]. However, heterogeneous exposure metrics and predominantly cross-sectional designs limit causal inference and hinder comparisons across studies.

Among active ingredients, atrazine emerged as an exposure of interest. In one cohort study, associations with BMI change were apparent only when atrazine exposure was evaluated, whereas other pesticide groups or ingredients did not show a clear pattern [47]. Atrazine is an herbicide with endocrine-disrupting properties and environmental persistence [52]. It has been banned in the European Union since 2003, but remains in use in several countries, including the United States and China [53, 54]. Experimental evidence has linked atrazine exposure to adverse endocrine effects, including altered gonadotropin and testosterone signaling, reinforcing its classification as an endocrine-disrupting chemical [55]. While these mechanistic data support plausibility, the epidemiological evidence in occupational settings remains limited and requires confirmation in well-designed longitudinal studies with improved exposure characterization.

A small number of studies used biomarker-based measures, which can improve exposure specificity and reduce reliance on recall. At the same time, many biomarkers, particularly spot urine metabolites, reflect recent exposure, and single measurements may not represent longer-term exposure patterns relevant to outcomes such as obesity and hypertension. Biomarker approaches are therefore most informative when paired with repeated sampling, task-based exposure histories, or cumulative indices. In cross-sectional settings, additional interpretive challenges may arise, including difficulties aligning short-term biomarkers with long-term outcome development, and potential reverse causality concerns for certain chemical types. Future studies should align exposure assessment strategy with hypothesized latency and timing of weight gain and cardiometabolic changes, ideally in prospective designs. Outcome assessment also differed substantially across studies. For hypertension, some studies relied on limited measurement protocols (e.g., single visits and variable rest periods), which may introduce measurement error and inflate or obscure associations through phenomena such as the white coat effect. For adiposity outcomes, measurement approaches ranged from direct anthropometry to self-reported height/weight or diagnosis in some settings. Greater standardization of measurement protocols and clearer reporting of diagnostic thresholds would improve comparability across studies.

Finally, inconsistent adjustment for confounding limits interpretability. Many studies did not adjust for core determinants of obesity and blood pressure such as age, sex, socioeconomic factors, diet, physical activity, smoking, and alcohol use. Without consistent control over these variables, it remains difficult to separate pesticide-related effects from correlated lifestyle and occupational factors. Although most studies included both sexes, none reported sex-stratified pesticide–outcome associations. This is a critical gap because exposure patterns and biological responses may differ by sex. In agricultural settings, women may experience proportionally greater indirect exposure through re-entry work, equipment cleaning, laundering contaminated clothing, or take-home pathways—routes that may be underestimated when exposure definitions focus primarily on pesticide application. Biological responses may also differ by sex, including endocrine mechanisms relevant to adiposity and blood pressure regulation and potentially sex-specific protective factors for cardiovascular outcomes. Robust confounder adjustment and sex-stratified analyses would strengthen causal inference and support more targeted occupational health interventions.

External validity and implications

The evidence base is geographically skewed, with most studies from Asia and relatively few from the Americas; no European studies met inclusion criteria. Differences in pesticide regulation, application practices, crops, and occupational protections limit generalizability across regions. Nonetheless, the findings underscore the need for large, harmonized longitudinal studies in diverse agricultural settings, combining standardized outcome measurement with improved exposure characterization (task-based histories, repeated biomarker sampling where appropriate, and class/active-ingredient resolution when feasible). Such studies would better support targeted occupational safety strategies and public health interventions aimed at reducing cardiovascular risk in agricultural worker populations.

Prior systematic reviews have often focused on broader populations, different exposure windows (including prenatal exposure), or other pesticide groupings (e.g., persistent organochlorines) and cardiovascular endpoints. By contrast, the present review targets adult agricultural populations and focuses on the intermediate cardiometabolic risk factors of adiposity and blood pressure/hypertension. Cardiovascular disease incidence and mortality in farming cohorts are important complementary outcomes, but they fall outside the predefined scope of this review and represent priorities for future syntheses.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review to specifically examine the association between occupational pesticide exposure and the development of obesity and hypertension among farm workers. One strength of our review was the use of double screening. Previous reviews have mainly focused on the general population, prenatal exposure, or organochlorine pesticides, making this review a novel contribution to the field. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we did not conduct a sensitivity analysis or formally assess the risk of bias of the included studies. Second, the considerable heterogeneity in exposure assessment, outcome definitions, and study designs also precluded a meta-analysis. Third, although our search strategy combined broad pesticide terminology with selected class/family-specific terms, the exposure terms were not intended to be exhaustive at the level of individual compounds or biomarkers; accordingly, we cannot exclude the possibility that studies using only unlisted class/family terminology or compound-/biomarker-specific terminology were missed. Finally, our search was restricted to a single database (MEDLINE), which may have resulted in the omission of relevant studies indexed elsewhere. Nevertheless, MEDLINE provides broad coverage of health-related research, and the use of broad pesticide and outcome terms was intended to capture the core epidemiological literature relevant to our research question.

Conclusions

Based on the studies included in this review, the current evidence remains inconclusive regarding associations between occupational pesticide exposure and adiposity and blood pressure outcomes among farm workers. In addition to methodological heterogeneity across exposure assessment, outcome definitions, and analytic approaches, the evidence base is likely affected by occupational selection processes, including healthy worker bias, whereby individuals who develop chronic health conditions may leave agricultural work or shift away from higher-exposure tasks. These factors may lead to underestimation of associations in predominantly cross-sectional studies. Future research should prioritize well-designed longitudinal studies with standardized outcome measurement, improved exposure characterization (including task-based histories and repeated measurements where feasible), comprehensive adjustment for confounders, and analyses that address selection and time-varying confounding, alongside sex-stratified analyses and evaluation of currently used pesticide classes and active ingredients.
