Dissemination of KPC-2-producing carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae ST656 in Eastern China
Authors: Zhixuan Chen, Xinru Yuan, Weihua Han, Li Shen, Junhong Shi, Jinjin Yang, Jiana Fu, Can Yang, Haojin Gao, Yu Huang, Jianbo Lv, Fangyou Yu, Bingjie Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP), particularly strains producing Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemase (KPC), pose a severe global health challenge due to limited therapeutic options and high mortality. While ST11 is the predominant KPC-producing lineage in China, the clinical and epidemiological significance of ST656 remains largely unexplored. We conducted a genomic and epidemiological investigation of twenty-five KPC-2-producing K. pneumoniae ST656 isolates collected from patients with severe pulmonary comorbidities in a tertiary hospital in Eastern China between July 2020 and October 2021. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing, whole-genome sequencing (WGS), phylogenetic analysis, plasmid profiling and conjugation assays were performed to determine resistance mechanisms, clonal relationships and plasmid transferability. Clinical information showed all 25 isolates originated from distinct patients in a tertiary hospital in Eastern China, predominantly older male post-lung transplantation cases with severe pulmonary disease, from intensive care units and thoracic surgery wards. All isolates displayed highly similar multidrug-resistant profiles, being resistant to most β-lactams, levofloxacin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, with limited susceptibility to amikacin and tigecycline. Genomic characterization of representative strain CRE132 showed that blaKPC−2 was located on a conjugative IncFII plasmid, and that this plasmid could transfer carbapenem resistance to Escherichia coli in conjugation assays. ST656 isolates in our study exhibited modest increase in virulence-associated traits, growth rates and certain adaptability compared with ST11 isolates. This study represents the first genomic and epidemiological investigation demonstrating nosocomial clonal dissemination of KPC-2–producing K. pneumoniae ST656 in China. These findings highlight the importance of continued genomic surveillance and improved understanding of this emerging lineage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04620-1.

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae, a Gram-negative opportunistic pathogen, is associated with diverse infections in both community and hospital settings [1]. Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE), particularly CRKP, have emerged as a pressing global health concern due to increasing prevalence and multidrug resistance [2]. The emergence of CRKP strains resistant to all β-lactam antibiotics, including last-resort carbapenems, exemplifies the growing crisis of antimicrobial resistance worldwide [3]. The rapid dissemination of CRKP is facilitated by carbapenemase-encoding plasmids, which spread through horizontal gene transfer (HGT) and enhance their persistence in hospital environments [4].

The most clinically significant carbapenemases in Enterobacterales include Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemases (KPC), metallo-β-lactamases (NDM, IMP, and VIM variants) and oxacillinase (OXA)−48-like enzymes [5]. KPC, a class-A serine β-lactamase [6–8], was first identified in the United States in 1996 [7]. It has since become a prevalent carbapenem resistance mechanism in Enterobacterales, with widespread dissemination reported in numerous regions, including India, Mediterranean countries and Europe [9–12]. In China, KPC-2 is most prevalent, whereas both KPC-2 and KPC-3 circulate widely in Europe and the Americas [13]. KPC-2-producing K. pneumoniae was first reported in China in 2007 [14] and has subsequently disseminated extensively throughout China and multiple other regions [15]. ST11 KPC-2-producing K. pneumoniae represents the predominant CRKP lineage in China [16] and the resistance phenotype is primarily mediated by blaKPC−2 [16, 17]. The blaKPC−2 gene is plasmid-borne, enabling both vertical transmission within clonal lineages and horizontal dissemination across diverse strains [18].

The ST258 lineage and its derivative ST512 represent the predominant CRKP strains in the Americas and southern Europe, while demonstrating relatively low prevalence in other global regions [19–21]. ST11, a genetically diverse clade and the recombinant ancestor of ST258, accounts for approximately 12% of CRKP isolates in Europe and exhibits significantly broader geographical distribution than ST258 or ST512 [22–24]. Furthermore, ST11 represents the predominant CRKP lineage in China [16, 25].

Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) analysis classifies K. pneumoniae ST656 as an evolutionarily distinct clone, differentiated by its unique allelic profile at seven housekeeping genes. A study of 1,361 K. pneumoniae isolates from two Vietnamese intensive care units identified 154 (12%) as ST656. In contrast to globally disseminated sequence types, local expansion of ST656 was prominent [26]. The first NDM-5 and MCR-8.2 co-harboring ST656 K. pneumoniae clinical isolate was isolated from the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid from a lung transplant patient in Beijing, China, which exhibited chromosomal gene-mediated resistance to colistin and tigecycline [27]. One ST656 extensively drug-resistant K. pneumoniae strain was isolated in Hangzhou, China, carrying mcr-1.1, mcr-8.2, crrB mutations and a tigecycline resistance gene [28]. However, rare cases of carbapenem-resistant ST656 have been reported. A strain of ST656 CRKP co-harboring the blaKPC−2, blaSHV−2, and blaTEM−1 β-lactamase genes was isolated in Wenzhou, China and a Tn1721-ISKpn6-blaKPC−2-ISKpn8-ΔTn3-IS26 configuration was observed in conjugative plasmids [29]. Overall, reports of blaKPC−2-carrying ST656 remain scarce, and comparative data on its virulence and fitness are limited.

In this study, we characterized a hospital-based dissemination of ST656 CRKP carrying blaKPC−2. Utilizing whole-genome sequencing, we delineated the molecular resistance determinants and transmission dynamics to inform antimicrobial therapy and infection-control strategies aimed at limiting the spread of resistant pathogens. Furthermore, we compared the characteristics of plasmids, virulence and fitness between ST656 and ST11 clones.

Materials and methods

Bacterial isolation and clinical information

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital (K22-125Y). From July 2020 to October 2021, twenty-five distinct CRKP isolates were collected from a tertiary hospital in Shanghai, China. All clinical isolates originated exclusively from patients aged > 51 years with severe pulmonary comorbidities. Among all 25 isolates, 19 (76%) were obtained from sputum specimens, 5 (20%) from pleural fluid, and 1 (4%) from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. All patients were recruited from the thoracic surgery department, with 12 cases (48%) admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) and 13 cases (52%) hospitalized in Ward 5 of the thoracic surgery division. Primary diagnoses included severe pulmonary infection, interstitial lung disease, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Notably, 17 cases (68%) were post-lung transplantation recipients, while 15 cases (60%) had severe pulmonary infection. All isolates exhibited carbapenem-resistant phenotypes as determined by VITEK-2 Compact system (bioMérieux, France), with Escherichia coli (E. coli) ATCC 25922 serving as the quality control organism for antimicrobial susceptibility testing.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using N335 susceptibility cards and the Vitek-2 system (bioMérieux, France) to determine minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for the following agents: β-lactams (amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, piperacillin/tazobactam, cefuroxime, cefuroxime axetil, cefoxitin, ceftazidime, ceftriaxone, cefoperazone/sulbactam, cefepime, ertapenem, imipenem), amikacin, levofloxacin, and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole. Results were interpreted according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines (CLSI 2022 M100-ED31). Intermediate results were reported separately and not grouped with resistance. Tigecycline MICs were established via standard broth microdilution methodology in Mueller-Hinton broth, with clinical breakpoints applied per U.S. FDA recommendations. The breakpoints of tigecycline were interpreted by the U.S. FDA (S ≤ 2, I = 4, R ≥ 8 µg/mL) [30]. All antimicrobial susceptibility tests were performed in duplicate to ensure reproducibility. E. coli ATCC 25922 was employed as the quality control strain throughout all antimicrobial susceptibility testing procedures to validate system performance and result reliability.

Whole-genome sequencing and annotation

Genomic DNA was extracted using a commercial DNA extraction kit (Qiagen, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Whole-genome sequencing was performed using short-read sequencing technology. For short-read sequencing, paired-end libraries (2 × 150 bp) were prepared and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw sequencing data were processed by removing adapter sequences using AdapterRemoval [31] and performing quality filtering with SOAPec [32]. High-quality reads were de novo assembled into contigs and scaffolds using SPAdes v3.12 [33] and A5-miseq v20160825 [34]. In addition, to obtain a high-quality reference assembly, long-read sequencing of the representative isolate CRE132 was conducted using the PacBio platform. HiFi reads were assembled with Hifiasm v0.14.2-r315 (https://github.com/chhylp123/hifiasm), yielding a highly contiguous genome suitable for plasmid characterization and subsequent SNP-based phylogenetic analysis. Capsular serotyping and virulence factor identification were conducted using Kleborate v2.0.0 (https://github.com/katholt/Kleborate). Antibiotic resistance genes were identified using ResFinder 4.7.2 (https://genepi.food.dtu.dk/resfinder) with default parameters. 120,000 K. pneumoniae strains were downloaded from the NCBI database (accessed in July 2025). Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) was performed using MLST (v.2.19.0, https://github.com/tseemann/mlst) with the K. pneumoniae 7-locus MLST scheme, and 78 genomes were assigned as ST656. A phylogenetic tree was constructed by comparing the 25 ST656 K. pneumoniae strains in this study with the global ST656 K. pneumoniae strains. All ST656 genomes (n = 103) were aligned to the PacBio-assembled CRE132 reference genome for SNP calling. The SNPs were analyzed with Snippy v4.6.0 under default parameters [35]. Recombination was identified and masked using Gubbins (v3.4.1) [36]with default parameters, and the resulting recombination-free core SNP alignment was used for downstream phylogenetic analysis. The SNP phylogenetic tree was constructed using iq-tree v3.0.1 under default settings [37]. The phylogenetic tree was then visualized using chiplot v2.6.1 [38].

Plasmid analysis included conjugation potential prediction using oriTfinder (http://bioinfo-mml.sjtu.edu.cn/oriTfinder/) and visualization with Proksee v1.2.0 (https://proksee.ca/). The genetic environment surrounding blaKPC−2 in plasmid p[CRE132]−2 was compared with plasmid p33221_KPC-2 and pKY399972.1_KPC-2 using Easyfig v2.2.3. Plasmid similarity was assessed using BLASTn (NCBI BLAST v2.17.0) with default parameters, and percent query coverage and nucleotide identity were extracted from the alignment output.

Capsule quantification

To quantitatively evaluate the mucoviscosity of K. pneumoniae strains CRE99, CRE124, and CRE132, uronic acid was extracted and quantified as described previously [39]. Briefly, overnight cultures grown in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth were diluted 1:100 in fresh medium and incubated at 37 °C for 16 h. Capsular material was extracted with 1% Zwittergent 3–12 at 50 °C, clarified by centrifugation, and precipitated from the supernatant with ethanol. The pellet was dried and re-suspended in 100 µl of sterile water, to which 1.2 mL of tetraborate solution (12.5 mM sodium tetraborate in sulfuric acid) was added. The mixture was incubated at 100 °C for 5 min, then immediately transferred to ice and maintained for 10 min. Chromogenic development was achieved by adding 10 µL of 3-hydroxybiphenyl reagent, with absorbance measured at 520 nm after 5 min incubation at room temperature. Uronic acid content was calculated from a standard calibration curve. Data represent mean ± SD of three independent experiments.

Semi-quantification of bacterial viscosity

The assay was conducted to evaluate the relative mucoviscosity of bacterial cultures as an indirect measure of capsular polysaccharide production. The reference strain K. pneumoniae HS11286 (ST11) was included as a comparator control. Single colonies were inoculated into LB broth and cultured for 18 h. OD600 measurements were normalized to bacterial cell density based on pre-assay OD600 values. 1 mL of bacterial culture was centrifuged at 21,000 g for 15 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of sterile phosphate buffered saline (PBS). After centrifugation again at 1,000 g for 5 min, 200 µL of the supernatant was aspirated, and the OD value at 600 nm was measured by a microplate reader. The experiment was repeated three times.

Biofilm semi-quantitative assay

Overnight cultures of K. pneumoniae were diluted 1:100 in fresh trypticase soy broth (TSB) containing 0.5% glucose and incubated in 96-well plates (200 µL/well) at 37 °C for 24 h. Afterwards, supernatants were aspirated and wells were gently rinsed three times with phosphate-buffered saline. Adherent cells were fixed with absolute methanol for 15 min and stained with 1% crystal violet for 8 min. After rinsing and drying, 33% glacial acetic acid was used to release the biofilms into solution and OD 600 was measured using a microplate reader. Blank wells containing uninoculated medium were used to subtract background absorbance, and OD values were normalized by bacterial density. All samples were tested in triplicate and repeated three times.

Plasmid conjugation

To assess the transferability of resistance plasmids from K. pneumoniae, a conjugation assay was performed. Due to pan-resistance in K. pneumoniae ST656, we constructed E. coli DH5α-Apr as a conjugation recipient, and K. pneumoniae ST656 was susceptible to apramycin. A non-mobilizable apramycin-resistance plasmid was introduced into E. coli DH5α by heat-shock transformation to generate the DH5α-Apr strain. Transformants were selected on LB agar supplemented with apramycin (100 µg/mL). Both donor and recipient cultures were grown to logarithmic phase, mixed at a 1:1 ratio, pelleted by centrifugation (4,000 × g, 5 min), and resuspended in 100 µL of normal saline (NS). The mixture was incubated for 18 h at 37 °C and plated onto TSB agar supplemented with apramycin (100 µg/mL) and meropenem (2 µg/mL) The above transconjugants were confirmed by PCR amplification and AST. Bacterial identification was performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). Conjugation frequency was calculated as the number of transconjugants per donor cell. Successful plasmid transfer was confirmed via PCR amplification of blaKPC−2 and APR. Additionally, the MICs of transconjugants were determined using the VITEK-2 Compact system to evaluate phenotypic resistance profiles. All tests were repeated in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism v9.0 (GraphPad Software). The results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. The significance of differences was determined using an unpaired t-test. Statistical significance was considered at P < 0.05, and the results were denoted as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001, and ns, P > 0.05.

Results

The clinical features of ST656 Klebsiella pneumoniae

A total of 135 clinical CRKP isolates were collected from a tertiary hospital in Eastern China between October 2019 and October 2021. WGS was performed on all isolates to determine their sequence types (STs). The isolates were classified into 12 distinct STs, with ST11 being the most prevalent (48.1%, 65/135), followed by ST15 (25.9%, 35/135) and ST656 (18.5%, 25/135). The remaining sequence types included ST37 (1.5%, 2/135), ST1229 (0.74%, 1/135), ST1326 (0.74%, 1/135), ST1401 (0.74%, 1/135), ST580 (0.74%, 1/135), ST636 (0.74%, 1/135), ST65 (0.74%, 1/135), ST722 (0.74%, 1/135), and ST967 (0.74%, 1/135). On July 16, 2020, the first ST656 CRKP strain was identified, representing a previously unreported sequence type in this healthcare setting. From then on, another 24 ST656 strains were detected between ICU and Ward 5 of the thoracic surgery division in the following year. The relatively low prevalence of ST656 prompted detailed epidemiological investigation of this sequence type. The median age of the 25 patients was 64 years (range, 52–74 years) with a male predominance (96%). These 25 strains were isolated from three types of specimens, including sputum (76%, n = 19), pleural fluid (20%, n = 5) and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (4%, n = 1). The first blaKPC−2-positive isolate was detected on July 16, 2020, from the sputum of a patient admitted to the ICU primarily diagnosed with post-lung transplantation status, severe lung infection and interstitial lung disease. Following the initial detection, a subsequent 12-month surveillance period (October 2020-October 2021) revealed 24 additional ST656 isolates distributed between ICU (n = 11, 45.8%) and Thoracic Surgery Ward 5 (n = 13, 54.2%). Major diagnoses included post-lung transplantation (71%, n = 17), severe pulmonary infection (62.5%, n = 15) and interstitial lung disease (44%, n = 11) (Table 1).

Antimicrobial susceptibility characteristics of ST656 Klebsiella pneumoniae

Susceptibility testing showed that all isolates had almost identical antimicrobial susceptibility profiles (Table S1). All strains demonstrated resistance to β-lactam antibiotics, including amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, piperacillin/tazobactam, cefuroxime, cefuroxime axetil, cefoxitin, ceftazidime, ceftriaxone, cefoperazone/sulbactam, ertapenem, imipenem, meropenem and levofloxacin, while two isolates were susceptible to cefepime. Additionally, all isolates demonstrated consistent resistance to levofloxacin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. Twenty-four isolates(96%) showed resistance to amikacin and twenty-one isolates (84%) showed susceptibility against tigecycline.

Characteristics of resistance genes

To further investigate the antibiotic resistance genotypes, WGS was performed. The results (Fig. 1) revealed that all 25 isolates carried the blaKPC−2 gene, which encodes carbapenem-hydrolyzing activity and accounts for their uniform resistance to β-lactam antibiotics. In addition, every strain harbored blaTEM−1D and blaTEM−1B, both associated with ampicillin resistance. Two isolates also carried blaSHV−1 and blaSHV−187, which conferred resistance to penicillin. Notably, one isolate possessed the blaL1 gene, encoding a metallo-β-lactamase that confers resistance to nearly all β-lactams, including carbapenems. As blaL1 is usually associated with Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, this unexpected finding may reflect a rare horizontal gene transfer event, contamination, or an assembly artifact, and thus warrants further verification. The isolates collectively carried 10 distinct aminoglycoside resistance genes, including members of the aac, aadA, ant, aph, and rmtB families, which mediate resistance to drugs such as amikacin. In terms of fluoroquinolone resistance, all isolates carried oqxA61, while most (24/25) also harbored oqxB19 and qnrB6, which together confer resistance to ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin. Additionally, mph(A), which is linked to macrolide resistance, was present in all isolates. The resistance determinants identified by WGS are consistent with the multidrug-resistant phenotype observed across the collection. Porin gene analysis showed that ompK35 and ompK37 were present in all 25 isolates, while ompK36 was detected in 24 isolates. According to ResFinder, we identified several amino-acid substitutions in ompK36 and ompK37. Phylogenetic relatedness

To investigate the genetic relatedness of the twenty-five KPC-2-producing ST656 K. pneumoniae isolates, a phylogenetic tree was first constructed based on whole-genome SNP analysis. The results showed that these isolates were genetically closely related, indicating possible clonal transmission (Figure S1). Clonal transmission was inferred when the pairwise core-genome SNP distance between isolates was ≤ 20 SNPs, which is a commonly accepted threshold for recent transmission events among K. pneumoniae strains [40]. However, genomic relatedness alone cannot confirm transmission in the absence of detailed epidemiological data, and therefore these findings should be interpreted as indicative evidence of clonal spread. To further place these isolates in a broader genomic context, we mapped the twenty-five ST656 genomes to the reference genome CRE132, which was sequenced using the PacBio platform. MLST analysis identified 78 genomes of ST656 from 120,000 downloaded K. pneumoniae genomes. Together with our twenty-five isolates, a total of 103 K. pneumoniae strains were included to construct a global phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2). The analysis divided these strains into four distinct clusters. All twenty-five isolates from this study, together with five genetically close global isolates, were grouped within Cluster I, forming a tight and recently expanded clade in China. In contrast, Clusters II–IV comprised more diverse isolates collected across Asia and Western countries between 2009 and 2024, representing older or geographically dispersed ST656 lineages. It is worth noting that all 25 ST656 isolates in our study carried KPC-2, among which 11 out of the 78 global ST656 strains (14%) contained KPC-2, isolated from China and Vietnam. By analyzing the isolation locations and times of these 103 strains of ST656 K. pneumoniae, we found that they were mainly isolated from Asian countries, such as China and Vietnam, and the isolation dates were mainly between 2019 and 2021.Fig. 2Phylogenetic tree of K. pneumoniae ST656 isolates. Genome and molecular characteristics of CRE132 plasmids

CRE132 was selected for further sequencing using the PacBio platform. The genome consists of a circular chromosome of 5,248,636 bp and four plasmids: p[CRE132]−1 (288,920 bp), p[CRE132]−2 (98,498 bp), p[CRE132]−3 (34,648 bp), p[CRE132]−4 (5,750 bp). Prokka annotation identified protein-coding genes and RNA genes on the chromosome. Whole-genome sequencing analysis identified two resistance plasmids in this isolate (Table 2), with particular attention given to blaKPC−2, a critical determinant of carbapenem resistance. The plasmid p[CRE132]−2, which carries the carbapenem resistance gene blaKPC−2, belongs to the IncFII(p14) incompatibility group. The IncFII-type plasmid carrying blaKPC−2 in CRE132 showed high sequence homology to the pKPHS2 plasmid backbone commonly observed in ST11 isolates, suggesting possible horizontal gene transfer events. A schematic representation of the genetic environment of the blaKPC−2 genes on the p[CRE132]−2 is shown in Fig. 3A. The analysis further revealed that plasmid p[CRE132]−1 carries multiple resistance determinants—including the extended-spectrum β-lactamase genes blaSHV−2 and blaTEM−1B—contributing to the strain’s multidrug-resistant phenotype.

Genetic environment of blakpc-2 gene

The resistance plasmid p[CRE132]−2 was a 98,498 bp IncFII plasmid that carried the blaKPC−2 gene. BLASTn comparison showed that p[CRE132]−2 shared 82% query coverage with 99.67% identity to plasmid p33221 and 74% query coverage with 99.46% identity to plasmid pKY399972.1, indicating that these plasmids possess highly conserved backbone regions despite variability in accessory elements. In all three plasmids, blaKPC−2 was located within a conserved mobile genetic element structure composed of Tn3-TnpR-ISKpn27-blaKPC−2-ISKpn6 (Fig. 3B), belonging to a characteristic Non-Tn4401 Element-KPC type 1 (NTEKPC-1) structure. Compared to the classic Tn4401 transposon, NTEKPC-1 is usually linked to ISKpn27 upstream of blaKPC. This insertion likely facilitates conjugative transfer of the plasmid, given that IS elements promote horizontal gene transfer via recombination and mobilization of adjacent genetic material [41]. Plasmid profiles of CRKP isolates and plasmid conjugation

A plasmid analysis (Fig. 1) was performed on twenty-five CRKP isolates and it revealed that all isolates contained four plasmid replicons including IncFIA(HI1), IncFIB(K), IncFII(p14) and IncR, which were commonly found in IncF plasmid. Based on variations in plasmid replicon types, blaKPC is primarily located on IncF-type plasmids [42], which is consistent with our observations. We constructed E. coli DH5α-Apr as a conjugation recipient to assess the transferability of resistant plasmids from strain CRE132. The results of the conjugation experiment showed that blaKPC−2 could be successfully transferred to the recipient E. coli DH5α-Apr at a frequency of 5.2 × 10− 6 cells per recipient cell. The transconjugant acquired most of the antimicrobial resistance of the donor strain CRE132, except for levofloxacin, which is likely mediated by chromosomal mutations or non-transferable PMQR genes. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing confirmed the acquisition of carbapenem and fluoroquinolone resistance phenotypes in the transconjugants (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we identified and characterized the first documented nosocomial dissemination of KPC-2-producing ST656 CRKP in a teaching hospital in Eastern China between July 2020 and October 2021. Although sporadic ST656 isolates carrying blaKPC−2 have previously been reported from individual clinical cases, no study has described a hospital-based clonal cluster involving this lineage. Here, we combined detailed epidemiological tracing with whole-genome sequencing, plasmid characterization and conjugation assays to provide robust evidence for plasmid-mediated intra-hospital dissemination. We identified 25 isolates of ST656 from patients with severe pulmonary infection in ICU and thoracic surgery wards. The high proportion of isolates from sputum (76%) and the median age of the patients (64 years) underlines the clinical importance of K. pneumoniae in pulmonary infections particularly in populations who have undergone lung transplantation.

ST656 is an uncommon clone with limited reports globally and in China [44]. Phylogenetic analysis revealed substantial genetic divergence between the twenty-five K. pneumoniae strains investigated in our study and seventy-eight globally sourced ST656 strains from the NCBI database, indicating that the cluster represents a local event rather than part of a broader international lineage. Although carbapenem-resistant ST656 strains have been detected in Asia, the lineage has received little attention, with only sporadic cases described. Our study suggests the possibility that this clone may be underrecognized in clinical settings, although additional epidemiological data are required to confirm its circulation. In contrast to ST11 - the dominant epidemic clone in China [45], ST656 has been reported only as sporadic cases internationally and here we report a new cluster of KPC-2-producing ST656 CRKP.

All twenty-five CRKP strains exhibited uniform resistance to carbapenems, extended-spectrum cephalosporins, and fluoroquinolones. A conserved set of seven resistance genes—blaKPC−2, blaTEM−1B, aac(3)-IId, mph(A), qnrB6, sul1, and tet(D)—was identified in all isolates through whole-genome analysis, with each strain possessing additional resistance genes. The KPC family of β-lactamases represents the most common mechanism of carbapenem resistance in K. pneumoniae [46, 47]. The KPC-2 variant dominates globally and is responsible for conferring broad-spectrum resistance to all β-lactam agents [47, 48]. In our study, one notable finding is that all twenty-five ST656 CRKP isolates harbored KPC-2 gene whereas only 14% (11/78) of globally collected ST656 strains were KPC-2 positive. This suggests that acquisition of blaKPC−2 may have contributed to the emergence of this cluster under antibiotic selection pressure.

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that ST656 was first reported in 2009 in China and the initial isolate did not harbor blaKPC−2. The first ST656 carrying blaKPC−2 emerged in 2015, suggesting that acquisition of this plasmid-borne carbapenemase gene occurred during its evolutionary spread. The presence of blaKPC−2 likely accelerated the dissemination of ST656. To date, KPC-2-producing ST656 strains have mainly been reported in Asian countries, particularly China and Vietnam, while being rarely observed in Europe and North America. Our study suggests that the ST656 clone might be overlooked and spreading silently in clinical settings. Importantly, the potential nosocomial spread of such uncommon clones should be carefully monitored, as their continued circulation may resemble early expansion patterns seen in epidemic lineages such as ST11. Although genomic analysis supports clonal dissemination of ST656 isolates, the available epidemiological information was limited. The isolates were collected from patients who overlapped in time and ward location, suggesting potential person-to-person or device-associated transmission. However, environmental sampling was not performed, and no clear index case or staff-associated link could be identified. Therefore, while the genomic similarity strongly supports recent clonal dissemination within the hospital, the precise transmission route remains undetermined. Future outbreak investigations integrating detailed patient movement data and environmental screening will be required to confirm the transmission pathways of ST656. In addition to genomic evidence, the clinical context may also have influenced the emergence of this ST656 cluster. Both intensive care units and thoracic surgery wards are environments where carbapenems and other broad-spectrum antibiotics are frequently used, which may create selection pressures that favor the persistence of KPC-producing K. pneumoniae.

The presence of blaKPC on conjugative plasmids facilitates its rapid dissemination, contributing to the challenge in controlling infections caused by KPC-producing K. pneumoniae once they emerge. In the genome of CRE132, blaKPC−2 was located on a 98,498 bp IncF plasmid with high conjugation and transfer capabilities. China has been identified as the country with the highest prevalence of IncF plasmids [49], and a close correlation was shown between ST11 KPC-producing K. pneumoniae and IncFII-like plasmids [50]. We further analyzed the genetic environment of the resistance genes. Our comparative genomic analysis revealed that plasmid p[CRE132]−2, an IncFII plasmid carrying blaKPC−2, shared high similarity with previously reported plasmids such as p33221 and pKY399972.1, both of which also harbored blaKPC−2. The resistance gene was embedded within a conserved NTEKPC-1 structure (Tn3-TnpR-ISKpn27-blaKPC−2-ISKpn6), a genetic arrangement distinct from the classical Tn4401 element. The presence of ISKpn27 upstream of blaKPC−2 was likely to enhance plasmid mobility, facilitating horizontal gene transfer. Importantly, the close resemblance between p[CRE132]−2 and plasmids isolated from different bacterial hosts, including Enterobacter cloacae, highlighted the role of interspecies plasmid dissemination in accelerating the spread of carbapenem resistance. Such dissemination is further promoted by the intrinsic features of IncF plasmids, including efficient transfer among Enterobacterales and stable maintenance under antibiotic pressure, along with their frequent linkage to multidrug-resistance genes [51].

Conjugation experiments further demonstrated that the resistance genes from the CRE132 isolate could be successfully transferred to recipient strains, confirming the occurrence of horizontal gene transfer via plasmids, which accelerates the spread process between bacteria. Therefore, routine admission screening for carbapenemase genes in clinical isolates is recommended to effectively curb the dissemination of these antimicrobial resistance determinants.

In comparison with ST11, the predominant epidemic clone in China and one of the most extensively studied CRKP lineages worldwide, ST656 exhibits several features that may pose a comparable or even greater clinical threat. ST11 is strongly associated with large-scale nosocomial outbreaks, high-level multidrug resistance, and the stable carriage of blaKPC−2, which have made it the dominant carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae clone in China and beyond. Although ST656 remains relatively rare, our study demonstrates that all 25 isolates harbored blaKPC−2, whereas only a minority of global ST656 strains carried this gene. This suggests that once ST656 acquires blaKPC−2, it may display an enhanced capacity for local dissemination like ST11. Our assays showed slightly higher capsule production, viscosity, and biofilm formation, as well as a faster growth rate in ST656 compared with the reference ST11 strain HS11286. These variations may reflect enhanced environmental adaptability or colonization potential. The slightly faster growth rate observed in ST656 isolates may facilitate colonization of hospital surfaces and medical devices, enabling them to outcompete other strains under antibiotic pressure. This growth advantage may support persistence and silent transmission in healthcare settings. However, the actual pathogenic potential of ST656 remains uncertain in the absence of in vivo or clinical outcome data. Further investigations using animal infection models or epidemiological surveillance are required to clarify the clinical significance of these observations.

Although our study provided evidence about the emergence of the ST656 KPC-2 producing CRKP in China with clinical implications, this study has still some limitations. The analyzed ST656 CRKP isolates exhibited a concerning multidrug-resistant phenotype, demonstrating high-level resistance to a broad spectrum of clinically relevant antibiotics. However, analysis about co-location of blaKPC−2 and multiple AMR genes on the same plasmid is insufficient and the conjugation experiment was verified solely for blaKPC−2. Moreover, plasmid stability assays were not conducted, leaving the long-term persistence of the IncFII plasmid in ST656 remains unclear. While phylogenetic analysis indicated the strain’s potential geographical origin, the absence of detailed epidemiological investigations including environmental sampling and patient-to-patient transmission mapping limits our ability to confirm direct transmission.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides the first documentation of nosocomial dissemination of the rare clone ST656 CRKP harboring blaKPC−2 in China. All twenty-five isolates carried the blaKPC−2 gene, indicating that plasmid-mediated transmission may have contributed to accelerating carbapenem resistance among K. pneumoniae strains. The presence of an IncF plasmid highlights a potential route for spread within the hospital. These observations highlight the importance of recognizing emerging, underreported CRKP clones and the need for ongoing genomic surveillance and strengthened infection-control practices to prevent further unnoticed dissemination in healthcare settings.
