Association between the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet and risk of incident heart failure and heart failure-related death: a UK Biobank cohort study
Authors: Lee P Liao, Patroys De Paz, Simone Marschner, Gary C H Gan, Liza Thomas, Luigi Fontana, Sarah Zaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence supporting dietary interventions for heart failure (HF) prevention remains limited. The Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet has been proposed as a cardioprotective dietary pattern, but population-level evidence linking DASH adherence to HF risk is sparse.AimTo examine the association between adherence to the DASH diet—characterised by high intake of vegetables, fruits and whole grains, and lower consumption of sodium and red and processed meat—with combined risk of HF incidence and death due to HF, as well as its risk factors, namely hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).MethodsA prospective cohort analysis was conducted using UK Biobank participants free of HF at baseline (2006–2010), with follow-up to 2024. Dietary intake was assessed using ≥2 instances of 24-hour recall. DASH scores were derived and categorised into quintiles. Fine-Gray competing risks Cox models estimated adjusted HRs (aHRs) and 95% CIs for a primary composite outcome of incident HF or HF-related death, adjusting for sociodemographic factors, deprivation, obesity, smoking, hypertension and T2DM and accounting for all-cause mortality. Secondary outcomes were incident hypertension and T2DM.ResultsAmong 124 777 participants (mean age 56.1±7.8 years; 56% female; 7% with diabetes; 36% with hypertension), followed for a mean of 12 years, 2931 HF events and 441 HF-related deaths occurred. Compared with the lowest DASH adherence quintile, the highest quintile had a lower risk of HF after adjustment for sociodemographic factors (aHR 0.79, 95% CI 0.70 to 0.89, p<0.001) and comorbidities (aHR 0.89, 95% CI 0.79 to 1.00, p<0.05). Greater DASH adherence was associated with reduced risk of hypertension (aHR 0.91, 95% CI 0.86 to 0.96, p<0.001) and T2DM (aHR 0.82, 95% CI 0.75 to 0.90, p<0.001).ConclusionsGreater adherence to the DASH diet was associated with lower combined risk of HF and death due to HF, as well as hypertension and T2DM, supporting the role of a DASH diet in HF prevention.Keywords: HEART FAILURE, Hypertension, Diabetes Mellitus

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) affects over 1 million people in the UK, with 200 000 new diagnoses each year.1 With lifetime healthcare costs related to HF exceeding US$100 billion, and its prevalence continuing to rise each year,2 there is a clear need for effective prevention strategies. Growing interest is now directed towards upstream population-level interventions that target modifiable risk factors earlier in the disease trajectory, even as pharmacological therapies remain central to management.

Dietary modification represents a potentially powerful yet underused strategy for HF prevention, although large-scale clinical trials remain limited. Current guidelines frequently emphasise sodium restriction, reflecting its role in fluid balance and blood pressure control.3 However, evidence supporting routine sodium restriction in HF prevention remains inconsistent.4

The Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet has been investigated in the context of HF, although studies to date have shown mixed results. The diet is defined by a low sodium intake (<2.5 g/day), high intake of fruits, vegetables and grains and reduced intake of red and processed meats.5 It is well recognised for its antioxidant and antihypertensive effects6 providing biological plausibility for its potential role in HF prevention. This is reinforced by the understanding that oxidative stress, hypertension, inflammation and metabolic dysfunction are well-established elements of the causal pathway leading to HF.

In a UK population, high adherence to the DASH diet has been shown to lower cardiovascular disease mortality.7 With respect to HF, adherence to the DASH diet was shown to enhance quality of life among patients.8 Large cohort studies conducted in Sweden (n=76 222),9 and the USA (n=30 239),10 also demonstrate an inverse association between DASH adherence and HF risk, although this relationship appears to vary by age and sex. Nevertheless, other investigations have reported minimal or no dietary benefit.11 12

To date, no large prospective study has examined the association between the DASH diet and incident HF in a UK population. Using data from the UK Biobank, a large prospective cohort with extensive patient information, this study examines the relationship between higher adherence to the DASH diet and the composite outcome of incident HF and death due to HF, as well as its key risk factors, namely hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).

Method

Study design and population

The UK Biobank is a large, multicentre prospective cohort comprising over 500 000 adults aged 37–73 years, with participant data linked to hospital admissions and mortality records. Recruitment occurred between 2006 and 2010 across 22 assessment centres. At baseline, participants completed a touch-screen questionnaire capturing self-reported measures, followed by a verbal interview to obtain medical history, socio-demographic information, lifestyle and behavioural factors. A standardised physical examination was conducted, including anthropometric assessments and the collection of blood and urine samples.

Dietary assessment

Dietary intake was assessed between 2009 and 2012 using Oxford WebQ, a validated 24-hour recall assessment that has been shown to be reproducible when using at least two dietary assessments.13 Oxford WebQ was first administered between April 2009 and September 2010 and on four other occasions: February to April 2011 (cycle 1); June to September 2011 (cycle 2), October to December 2011 (cycle 3) and April to June 2012 (cycle 4). Food and beverage items were categorised according to the framework established by the UK National Diet and Nutrition Survey.14 If participants completed at least two dietary assessments, they were included in the study. Participants with implausibly low or high energy intakes were excluded if their total energy intake exceeded the sex-specific 99th percentile of the observed distribution (>13 300 kJ (women), >16 000 kJ (men)).

DASH diet

The DASH diet score is based on eight dietary components: fruits (four to five servings/day), vegetables (four to five servings/day), nuts and legumes (four to five servings/week), low-fat dairy (two to four servings/day), whole grains (six to eight servings/day), sodium (<2.5 g/day), sweetened beverages (five or fewer/week) and red and processed meat (two or fewer servings/day). Foods included in each category and conversions from servings to g/day are shown in online supplemental material 1. The DASH diet score, proposed by Fung,15 was used for the current study, where intake of each component was ranked into quintiles and assigned scores from 1 (lowest adherence) to 5 (highest adherence), with reverse scoring applied where appropriate. Component scores were summed to generate a total DASH score, which was subsequently categorised into quintiles.

Missing dietary data were defined at the level of the derived g/day variables. A participant’s data for a particular DASH component were counted as missing only if all contributing food items for that component were unreported in the WebQs they completed, meaning no grams-per-day value could be derived.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome was a composite outcome of incident HF or HF-related death and was collected until September 2024 (most recent UK Biobank data update). Evidence of HF or HF-related death was drawn from the death register, primary care, hospital admissions, self-report information with International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Edition (ICD-10) of I50.0 (congestive HF), I50.1 (left ventricular failure) and I50.9 (HF unspecified) (UK Biobank Data-field ID: 131354). These validated ICD-10 I50.x codes (I50.0, I50.1, I50.9) represent the most specific and consistently recorded HF diagnoses within UK Biobank. Death registry included all deaths that occurred before 31 August 2024 (England and Wales) and 30 November 2024 (Scotland). Baseline was defined by the date of the patient’s latest dietary assessment, and those who had HF at that date were excluded. The time to event was then calculated from baseline until incident HF or HF-related death. Similarly, data for the secondary outcomes of hypertension or T2DM were obtained from the death register, primary care, hospital admissions and self-report information. Time to each outcome was calculated separately from the date of the last diet assessment at baseline to the date of diagnosis or death.

Baseline characteristics and assessment of covariates

Covariates were selected a priori based on established associations with HF16 and included sex, age, ethnicity (white, black, Asian, mixed, unknown), educational qualification, history of hypertension and T2DM, smoking status (people who smoke or people who previously smoked vs people who have never smoked), alcohol status (daily drinkers vs all others), body mass index (BMI) (>30 vs ≤30), physical activity (metabolic equivalent of task min/week, reflecting the accumulated weekly energy expenditure from reported activities) and Townsend Deprivation Index (an index of socioeconomic deprivation derived from unemployment, lack of car ownership, lack of home ownership and household overcrowding, categorised into quintiles (1–5), with higher values reflecting greater deprivation). Detailed variable definitions and a description of how these were measured can be found in online supplemental material 2.

Statistical analysis

To estimate the association between adherence to the DASH diet and risk of HF/HF-related death, an adjusted Fine-Gray competing risks Cox model was used, accounting for all-cause death. A step modelling approach was used, with model 1 adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, smoking and the Townsend Deprivation Index to control for key demographic and socioeconomic confounders known to influence HF risk, while model 2 was additionally adjusted for BMI, history of hypertension and history of T2DM, as these factors may lie on the causal pathway and mediate part of the association between DASH adherence and HF risk.16 An interaction analysis was performed by including a sex×DASH quintile interaction term in the Fine-Gray model to assess whether associations differed between males and females and a global Wald test was used to evaluate the joint significance of the interaction terms.

For the secondary outcome, the association of DASH diet with (i) hypertension and (ii) T2DM, an adjusted Fine-Gray competing risks Cox model accounting for all-cause death with the same models mentioned above was used. Hypertension was adjusted for with baseline T2DM, while T2DM was adjusted for with baseline hypertension.

Linear trends of adherence to the DASH diet were assessed by adding DASH score quintiles as an ordinal continuous variable to the Cox model. Statistical significance was determined by p<0.05. All analyses were conducted using R V.4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) within the RStudio environment (V.2025.06.13).

Results

Baseline characteristics

The UK Biobank recruited 501 936 participants between 2006 and 2010. The current study included 124 777 participants, excluding participants who did not complete at least two dietary 24-hour recalls (n=375 272), had a diagnosis of HF at baseline (n=624) and/or reported implausible energy intakes (n=1263) (figure 1).

Flow chart showing eligibility criteria for this study. HF, heart failure.

Participants had a mean age of 56.13±7.82 years and mean BMI of 26.70±4.57 kg/m2. The median DASH score was 24 (IQR 21, 27) (maximum score of 40). Those in quintile 5, which corresponded to high adherence to the DASH diet, had favourable characteristics compared with those in lower quintiles (table 1). All characteristics were statistically significant with p<0.01. These characteristics included a reduced incidence of obesity (BMI ≥ 30) (15% in quintile 5 vs 25% in quintile 1) and smoking (40% in quintile 5 vs 45% in quintile 1) and lower levels of daily alcohol consumption (21% in quintile 5 vs 25% in quintile 1). Demographic variables differed across DASH quintile, a higher proportion of individuals with a university degree (51% in quintile 5 vs 42% in quintile 1) and more females were in the category of highest adherence (64%) compared with males (35%). The highest adherence to DASH had lower cases of history of hypertension (33% in quintile 5 vs 38% in quintile 1) and history of T2DM (5.93% in quintile 5 vs 8.95% in quintile 1).

Primary outcome: adherence to the DASH diet and HF risk

At a mean follow-up time of 12 years, 2931 (2.35%) participants developed incident HF and 441 (0.35%) participants died from HF. Of these, 1050 (36%) were female and 1881 (64%) were male. After adjustment for age, sex, smoking, ethnicity and socioeconomic status (model 1), participants with the greatest adherence to the DASH diet at baseline had a lower risk of incident HF (adjusted HR (aHR) 0.79, 95% CI 0.70 to 0.89, p<0.001) compared with those with lower adherence (figure 2, model 1). There was a similar trend when the model was further adjusted for comorbidities (model 2)—obesity, hypertension and T2DM (aHR 0.89, 95% CI 0.79 to 1.00, p<0.05) (figure 2, model 2). Both models showed a significant positive linear trend test across quintiles (model 1, p<0.001; model 2, p<0.05). The interaction analysis demonstrated no evidence of interaction by sex (global p for interaction=1.00).

Secondary outcome: risk of hypertension and T2DM

Over the follow-up period, 12 868 participants (10.31%) developed hypertension and 5161 participants developed T2DM (4.14%). There were no deaths where hypertension or T2DM was the primary cause. Greatest adherence to the DASH diet was associated with a decreased risk of hypertension (aHR 0.91, 95% CI 0.86 to 0.96, p<0.001) and T2DM (aHR 0.82, 95% CI 0.75 to 0.90, p<0.001) compared with low adherence (figure 3).

Discussion

In this large prospective cohort of UK adults, it was found that greater adherence to the DASH diet was associated with a 21% lower risk of combined incident HF and death due to HF (when adjusted for age, sex, smoking and sociodemographic factors) as well as a 9% and 18% lower risk of its associated risk factors of hypertension and T2DM, respectively. We found no difference in the association between high DASH diet adherence and lower HF risk between males and females. These findings were apparent even though participants in the highest adherence quintile consumed far less sodium, fruits, vegetables and grains than recommended by the DASH diet. Collectively, the findings support the role of a DASH dietary pattern in the prevention of HF.

The observed association between DASH adherence and HF is consistent with prior large cohort studies. In this study, after further adjustment for comorbidities likely to lie on the causal pathway to HF, a significant 11% reduction in HF risk persisted. These findings are consistent with other large cohort studies, including a Swedish study (n=76 122) that reported a 15%–17% reduction in HF risk,9 and the REasons for Geographic And Racial Differences in Stroke (REGARDS)10 study (n=30 239), which observed a 27% lower HF risk among participants in the highest adherence group. The larger effect size observed in REGARDS may reflect the underlying HF risk associated with ethnic disposition (40% black participants compared with the UK Biobank cohort of 97% white) and the older baseline age of participants (mean age 64 years vs 56 years in the current study) given the strong age-related increase in HF risk.16 Together these studies, alongside our present findings, provide converging evidence across diverse populations that adherence to a DASH diet lowers HF risk.

A previous study by Ma et al,17 provided important evidence by examining the frequency of adding salt to foods as a behavioural proxy for long-term sodium preference and evaluating its association with HF and HF-related mortality. Unlike Ma et al, our analysis centres on HF as the primary outcome and includes both HF incidence and HF-related deaths. We also evaluated DASH adherence as a comprehensive dietary pattern rather than focusing on sodium-related behaviour, recognising that previous studies have demonstrated that the cardiometabolic benefits of DASH extend beyond salt reduction to include its high-fibre and antioxidant compound content.18 19 Methodologically, Ma et al used tertiles of salt-adding behaviour, whereas we applied quintiles of DASH adherence, allowing finer resolution of dose-response relationships and demonstrating a clear graded trend in which higher adherence was associated with lower HF risk. Notably, the magnitude of risk reduction observed in our highest DASH adherence quintile (11%) was directionally consistent with the reduction reported by Ma et al in their lowest salt-adding tertile (18%), despite the different exposures examined. In addition, our models incorporated hypertension, a key modifiable risk factor for HF, as a covariate, enabling us to assess whether the association between DASH adherence and HF risk may operate through this pathway. Together, these differences mean that our study offers complementary, HF-specific insights that extend the evidence provided by Ma et al.

Currently in the UK, the ‘Eatwell Guide’ (EWG) promotes a healthy balanced diet and has been shown to improve cardiovascular health.20 The diet is similar to the DASH diet in limitation of processed meats and sugar consumption, although is less prescriptive, with no restrictions on sodium intake and smaller servings than the DASH diet, for fruits and vegetables (minimum five servings for EWG and eight to 10 servings for the DASH diet). This may partly explain the suboptimal dietary intakes observed in the current cohort in regard to individual food components. In Australia, the Handbook of Non-Drug Interventions project launched by the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners21 identifies the DASH diet as superior to others for overall health and wellness. This may account for the lower prevalence of HF in the Australian population (0.5%–0.6%),22 compared with the UK population in this study and further supports the integration of the DASH diet into guideline development.

Importantly, the association between DASH adherence and risk of combined incident HF and death due to HF remained significant after adjustment for hypertension and T2DM. This suggests that the DASH diet may confer protection via additional pathways whether through broader effects on cardiometabolic risk factors or mechanisms not accounted for in our models. Consistent with this, higher DASH adherence was associated with a 9% lower risk of hypertension and an 18% lower risk of T2DM in this cohort. The decreased risk of hypertension with the DASH diet has been shown in previous randomised controlled trials (RCTs).23 Francisco et al24 reported a 26% risk reduction of developing hypertension in a Brazilian population (n=5632) with only an approximately 3-year follow-up for high versus low adherence. A cohort study in China (n=2751) also found a 15% lower risk of hypertension in the highest adherence quintile.25 Thus, across various ethnicities and geographical regions, a decreased risk of hypertension has been reported.

With respect to T2DM, only a limited number of RCTs have examined its association with the DASH diet, and these have reported varying magnitudes of risk reduction. Liese et al26 found that at 5-year follow-up there was a 64% reduction in odds of developing T2DM, while a meta-analysis of observational studies found a mean difference of 18% reduction in risk with high adherence to the DASH diet.27 Nonetheless, available evidence demonstrates beneficial effects of the DASH diet on glycaemic regulation and insulin sensitivity,28 suggesting its appropriateness for diabetes management.

Several biological mechanisms may explain the observed associations. The DASH diet is rich in antioxidants, potassium, magnesium and fibre, which reduce oxidative stress, inflammation and endothelial dysfunction.6 Given that these processes are central to HF pathophysiology and are potentiated by T2DM and hypertension, targeting these shared mechanisms can interrupt and retard progression to HF development. Additionally, previous studies have demonstrated reductions in cardiac biomarkers such as cardiac troponin-1, a diagnostic marker of cardiac injury, as well as N-terminal proB-type natriuretic peptide.29 Interestingly, following adjustments for all covariates in our study, including hypertension, T2DM and obesity, the effect size of the DASH diet was lower supporting the likelihood that attenuation of these risk factors interrupts the shared pathways that drive progression to HF.

Despite these benefits, adherence to DASH dietary targets in this cohort was modest notably lower than reported in other cohorts.9 10 We postulated that this was driven by the substantially low intake of fruits, vegetables and wholegrains and nearly twofold increase in sodium observed in the cohort, even among participants in the highest adherence to the DASH diet. Soberingly, this reflects the poor dietary quality of the UK population, with high intake of ultra-processed food, rich in sodium, free sugars and unhealthy fats and has been independently associated with cardiometabolic disease and cardiovascular mortality.30 In fact, a past study with UK Biobank participants showed that >46% of daily energy intake comprised ultra-processed foods.31

From a policy perspective, these findings support consideration of closer alignment between HF prevention guidelines and DASH-style dietary recommendations in the UK. Public health strategies that prioritise reductions in ultra-processed food consumption may facilitate greater adherence to DASH principles and enhance its cardioprotective effects at a population level. While current UK EWG promotes a balanced dietary pattern, it is less prescriptive than DASH, particularly with respect to sodium intake and recommended fruit and vegetable consumption. Strengthening dietary guidance in these areas may represent a pragmatic opportunity to improve cardiovascular health and reduce the future burden of HF.

Limitations

As an observational cohort study, causal inference cannot be established, and residual confounding from unmeasured factors remains possible despite extensive adjustment. Dietary intake was self-reported and assessed at baseline, and changes in diet over time could not be captured (where diet is a time-varying covariate), introducing potential misclassification. We also cannot determine whether lifelong or transient adherence to DASH is required for benefit. It must also be noted that the UK Biobank cohort is healthier and less socioeconomically and ethnically diverse than the general population, which may limit generalisability.

The current study reported 3414 HF cases (including those who died of HF) over 12 years (2.7% of the population) and was defined using linked administrative data sources, death registries and self-report, which maximises capture, but small numbers of HF diagnoses may be misclassified. Although the competing risk of non-HF-related death was accounted for, detailed HF phenotyping, including measures such as left ventricular ejection fraction, was unavailable for most participants. Cardiac imaging of participants is currently underway and future analyses incorporating these data may allow further exploration of HF subtypes.

The analytic sample was restricted to participants who completed two or more Oxford WebQs to improve the reliability of dietary estimates. However, episodically consumed foods, such as red/processed meat and nuts/legumes,32 showed higher missingness, even among individuals completing multiple WebQs. This may reflect true episodic intake patterns rather than questionnaire non-completion. Since these foods are not eaten daily, their intake is less likely to be captured by 24-hour recalls, which may lead to underestimation of these components in the DASH score.

Furthermore, HF diagnoses in UK Biobank are not differentiated by ejection-fraction subtype, limiting our ability to examine whether associations varied between HF with preserved ejection fraction and HF with reduced ejection fraction.

Conclusions

Greater adherence to the DASH diet was associated with a significantly lower risk of developing HF and death due to HF. These associations persisted even after accounting for major cardiometabolic comorbidities, suggesting that the DASH dietary pattern may be associated with HF risk through additional pathways beyond its effects on hypertension and T2DM. Despite generally low adherence to recommended DASH targets, meaningful risk reductions were observed, highlighting the potential public health impact of improving dietary quality. These findings support stronger incorporation of the DASH dietary pattern into HF prevention guidelines.
