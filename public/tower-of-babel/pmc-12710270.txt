Hemoglobin variability predicts mortality in acute kidney injury patients undergoing continuous renal replacement therapy: a multi-database cohort study
Authors: Yumin Huang, Ran Jiang, Kang Liu, Jinghang Li, Rongrong Sheng, Wei Xu, Huijuan Mao, Buyun Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundShort-term hemoglobin variability (Hb-ARV) during continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) may reflect blood volume instability in acute kidney injury (AKI), yet its mortality implications remain unclear.MethodsThis multi-database study analyzed adult AKI patients receiving CRRT >24 h from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC)-IV, eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU), Amsterdam University Medical Center (AmsterdamUMC) database, and Jiangsu Province Hospital (JSPH) CRRT database. Hb-ARV, quantifying hemoglobin fluctuations during CRRT, was assessed via Cox regression and restricted cubic splines for associations with in-hospital and 1-year mortality.ResultsThe MIMIC-IV, eICU, AmsterdamUMC, and JSPH cohorts included 1,321, 437, 631, and 488 patients, respectively, with median (IQR) Hb-ARVs of 5.1 (3.9–6.6) g/L, 5.8 (4.1–8.2) g/L, 5.0 (3.9–6.8) g/L, and 6.3 (5.1–8.0) g/L. In-hospital mortality rates were 48.2%, 41.2%, 43.7%, and 44.9%. Restricted cubic splines revealed J-shaped/linear mortality trends with Hb-ARV. Compared to the second quartile of Hb-ARV, the highest quartile was significantly associated with increased in-hospital mortality in MIMIC-IV (adjusted HR 1.32, 95% CI: 1.05–1.66), AmsterdamUMC (adjusted HR 1.74, 95% CI: 1.23–2.46), and JSPH (adjusted HR 1.96, 95% CI: 1.31–2.93) cohorts. In the eICU cohort, where a linear trend was observed, each 1 g/L increase in Hb-ARV was associated with an adjusted HR of 1.07 (95% CI: 1.03–1.11) for in-hospital mortality. Highest quartile of Hb-ARV was also associated with increased 1-year mortality in MIMIC-IV and AmsterdamUMC cohorts.ConclusionsExcessive Hb-ARV during CRRT predict higher short- and long-term mortality in patients with AKI.Keywords: Acute kidney injury, continuous renal replacement therapy, average real variability, hemoglobin, mortality

Background

Acute kidney injury (AKI) affects approximately 50%–60% of patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) [1] and independently associated with adverse outcomes, including increased in-hospital mortality [2,3]. Renal replacement therapy, particularly continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), is essential for helping to manage fluid overload, correct electrolyte disturbances, and remove uremic toxins in patients with severe AKI [4]. Despite these therapeutic benefits, the short-term mortality among CRRT recipients remains high, at approximately 50% [2,5]. Besides the timing of initiation [6,7], dose [8–10], anticoagulation [11,12], modality [5,13], fluid balance [14,15], and net ultrafiltration rate [16], dynamic fluctuation of fluid status and hemodynamic instability have also been identified to impact the prognosis of critically ill patients with AKI receiving CRRT [17,18].

Hemoglobin, a complex iron-containing protein found in red blood cells (RBC), is sensitive to plasma volume status. During ultrafiltration in CRRT, hemoglobin levels can increase when plasma volume decreases, as hemoglobin cannot transfer through the hemodialysis membranes. Therefore, fluctuations in hemoglobin levels—referred to as hemoglobin variability [19,20]—may indirectly reflect dynamic changes in plasma volume, blood loss, erythropoietic disturbances, and inflammation during CRRT [19–25]. In different methods to measure hemoglobin variability, the standard deviation (Hb-SD) and coefficient of variation (Hb-CV) of hemoglobin reflect shifting from the mean, whereas the average real variability of hemoglobin (Hb-ARV) reflects the average absolute difference between consecutive hemoglobin values over time, making it more sensitive to acute instability in hemoglobin levels. Short-term Hb-ARV may act as a practical indicator for changes in plasma volume and blood consumption in patients undergoing CRRT. However, its association with mortality among patients with AKI undergoing CRRT has been rarely investigated.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the relationship between Hb-ARV during CRRT and patient outcomes across four cohorts from North America, Europe, and China. We hypothesized that excessive Hb-ARV during CRRT was associated with short- and long-term mortality in this patient population.

Methods

Study design and databases

This international, multi-database, retrospective cohort study based on four databases: the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC)-IV version 3.0, eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU, version 2.0), Amsterdam University Medical Center (AmsterdamUMC) database version 1.0.2, and Jiangsu Province Hospital (JSPH) CRRT database. The MIMIC-IV 3.0 database is a publicly accessible ICU database that records demographic data, vital signs, medications, laboratory tests, and other relevant information for 364,627 adult patients admitted to the ICU at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, MA, USA, between 2008 and 2022 [26,27]. The eICU database contains data for 139,367 patients across 200,859 ICU admissions from 208 hospitals in the U.S. between 2014 and 2015 [28]. The AmsterdamUMC database, the first freely accessible European ICU database, includes data on 23,106 ICU and high-dependency unit admissions from 2003 to 2016 [29]. The JSPH CRRT database includes data on 787 patients with AKI who underwent CRRT at a large tertiary teaching hospital in Eastern China from October 2019 to October 2023.

Participant selection

Eligible patients from the MIMIC-IV 3.0, eICU, AmsterdamUMC, and JSPH databases were considered for inclusion. We included adult patients with AKI who received CRRT for > 24 h within 14 days of ICU admission. Participants were excluded if they died within 48 h of admission, had uremia, were undergoing intermittent dialysis or plasma exchange, had counts of hemoglobin < 3 during CRRT, or had missing baseline data, including fluid balance during CRRT, exposure, or outcomes (Protocol in Supplement 1).

Data collection

Data were extracted from the four databases using the structured query language in PostgreSQL [27]. The collected data included demographic information (age, sex, race), body mass index (BMI), admission type, ICU type, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, chronic heart failure, cancer, and Charlson Comorbidity Index score), disease severity on admission (Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score, OASIS) [30], Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score29 before CRRT, laboratory tests, vital signs, CRRT data (including CRRT mode, anticoagulation, fluid balance, norepinephrine equivalent, and time interval from ICU admission to CRRT), hemoglobin information during CRRT, and clinical outcomes.

Definitions

We used CRRT to find patients with renal failure, then exclude patients with chronic renal failure to finally identify patients with AKI. AKI was defined according to Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes AKI guideline [31]. As receiving RRT is a criterion of stage 3 AKI, all patients were regarded as AKI after uremia were excluded. Sepsis was defined as patients with suspected infection with associated organ dysfunction (SOFA ≥ 2) [32,33]. Norepinephrine equivalents were used to standardize the dose of vasoactive drugs [34]. Baseline serum creatinine was defined as the lowest creatinine value between 1 year prior to admission and discharge if creatinine was normal or had a diagnosis of chronic kidney disease (CKD); otherwise, it was estimated using serum creatinine calculated from the simplified modification of diet in renal disease equation set to 75 mL/min per 1.73 m2 in the MIMIC-IV and eICU databases [35]. As the CKD diagnosis was unavailable in Amsterdam and JSPH, the baseline serum creatinine levels in these two cohorts were estimated, with the lowest creatinine value between 1 year before admission and discharge. The estimated glomerular filtration rate was determined using the CKD-EPI 2009 creatinine formula [36]. CKD was defined as a baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or having diagnostic codes of CKD in ICD-9 or ICD-10 codes.

Exposure and outcomes

The exposure was the Hb-ARV, which was calculated using the formula [37]: Hb−ARV=1n−1×∑k=1n−1|Hb k+1−Hbk|, where ‘n’ represents the number of hemoglobin measurements, and hemoglobin comes from the results of complete blood count. The primary and secondary endpoints were in-hospital mortality and 1-year mortality, respectively. In all four cohorts, in-hospital mortality was definitively recorded for all patients. In addition, patients in the MIMIC-IV and AmsterdamUMC databases had confirmed 1-year mortality data.

Statistical methods

The baseline characteristics and outcomes were complete in all four cohorts, except for BMI, which had missing rates of 16.5%, 3.2%, 0%, and 5.6% in the MIMIC-IV, eICU, AmsterdamUMC, and JSPH databases, respectively. Given its established clinical relevance and relatively low missingness, BMI was imputed using multiple imputation with chained equations (five imputations). In contrast, patients with missing fluid balance data were excluded from the analysis because fluid balance was essential for the analysis and unsuitable for imputation. Serum albumin was excluded because of excessive missing data (missing in > 60% of patients), which would compromise the validity of imputation.

Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) where applicable. Continuous variables were presented mean ± standard deviation or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), depending on the normality test results. Data were analyzed across the four cohorts.

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression models were used to assess the relationship between Hb-ARV levels and outcomes. Restricted cubic splines using the median as the reference value and four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles were used to examine potential non-linear relationships between Hb-ARV levels and in-hospital mortality. The exposure was divided into quartiles, with the lowest mortality-risk group as the reference. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to estimate the cumulative incidence of all-cause mortality across quartiles, with differences assessed using the log-rank test. In the multivariate model, we adjusted for potential confounders based on clinical relevance and our prior literature [38,39], with consideration for information of hemoglobin. The complete list of variables included demographic information (age, sex), BMI, baseline creatinine, Charlson Comorbidity Index score (missing in the AmsterdamUMC database), admission type (surgery or not), invasive ventilation and sepsis at admission, OASIS score on admission (missing in the JSPH database), SOFA score before CRRT, fluid balance before CRRT, time interval from ICU admission to CRRT, norepinephrine equivalent and fluid balance during CRRT, and information of hemoglobin (including time-weighted average hemoglobin, change of hemoglobin, average counts of hemoglobin per day).

To assess the robustness of the results, several sensitivity analyses were performed. First, propensity score-weighting analyses using twang package were employed to recalculate hazard ratio (HR) to strengthen causal inference. Second, subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate interaction effects. Third, the relationship between in-hospital mortality and hematocrit/RBC counts was analyzed. Fourth, the impact of exposure frequency was addressed by performing sensitivity analyses using time-standardized hemoglobin variability (Hb-ARV/counts of hemoglobin per day). Lastly, serum albumin was additionally adjusted to examine potential confounding by nutritional status in spline models within subsets of patients with albumin data.

Four mediation models were used to evaluate whether Hb-ARV mediated the association between BMI, fluid balance, net ultrafiltration, RBC transfusion, and mortality [40,41]. The models described: (1) the average causal mediation effect (ACME), which quantified the portion of the exposure-mortality effect explained by the mediator; and (2) the average direct effect, quantifying the effect of exposure on mortality after accounting for the mediator. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis method was used to determine the potential threshold, and sensitivity or specificity for a special cutoff of Hb-ARV value. Statistical analysis was conducted using R software version 4.3.2. A 2-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval and informed consent

The JSPH Institutional Review Board approved this study (2023-SR-877). As a tertiary teaching hospital, all patients admitted to our institution sign a written informed consent form upon admission, agreeing that their anonymized clinical data may be used for scientific research. This consent is part of the standard admission procedure. For this retrospective study, the requirement for additional informed consent was waived by the ethics committee due to the use of de-identified data. Access to the MIMIC-IV, eICU, and AmsterdamUMC databases was granted to Dr. Wu (Record ID 32559175) under their respective data use agreements. The study complied with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational cohort studies.

Results

Baseline data

According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, the study extracted 1,321 patients in the MIMIC-IV database, 437 patients in the eICU database, 631 patients in the AmsterdamUMC database, and 488 patients in the JSPH database, with a total of 2,877 patients analyzed (Figure 1).

Exposure and outcome

The median (IQR) Hb-ARV levels were 5.1 (3.9, 6.6) g/L, 5.8 (4.1, 8.2) g/L, 5.0 (3.9, 6.8) g/L, and 6.3 (5.1, 8.0) in the MIMIC-IV, eICU, AmsterdamUMC, and JSPH cohorts, respectively. The Hb-ARV of patients on CRRT was significantly lower than that of patients with hemoglobin counts ≥ 3 in the MIMIC-IV, eICU, AmsterdamUMC databases (Table S3). The correlations between Hb-ARV and various baseline variables were plotted using the Pearson function (Figure 2). In the four cohorts, Hb-ARV was consistently related to BMI (r −0.099 to −0.123), average fluid balance per day during CRRT (r 0.102 to 0.348), and average RBC transfusion per day during CRRT (r 0.144 to 0.334). No significant, consistent correlations were observed for the other variables.

Correlations between Hb-ARV and baseline characteristics.Only Spearman’s correlation coefficients with a p-value <0.05 are shown; p-values were not adjusted, as this was an exploratory analysis. The body mass index, average changes in fluid balance per day, and red blood cell transfusion during CRRT per day were all correlated with Hb-ARV in the four cohorts.

The in-hospital mortality of the study population was 48.2% (637 patients) in the MIMIC-IV cohort, 41.2% (180 patients) in the eICU cohort, 43.7% (276 patients) in the AmsterdamUMC cohort, and 44.9% (219 patients) in the JSPH cohort. The 1-year mortality was 62.4% (824 patients) in the MIMIC-IV cohort and 54.5% (344 patients) in the AmsterdamUMC cohort.

Relationship between exposures and outcomes

Restricted cubic spline curves show a J-shaped curve (Figure 3A, 3C, 3D) or linearity (Figure 3B) relationship between Hb-ARV and in-hospital mortality among the four cohorts. As a nonlinear relationship was present, quartile grouping of Hb-ARV was performed to analyze the relationship between each group and in-hospital mortality. In the MIMIC-IV, AmsterdamUMC, and JSPH cohorts, the Kaplan–Meier curves revealed that cumulative 60-day (Fig. S1, all p < 0.001) survival probabilities were lowest for patients in quartile 4, with the highest Hb-ARV. Hb-ARV also showed a J-shaped curve with 1-year mortality in the MIMIC-IV and AmsterdamUMC cohorts (Figure 4), with the highest in-hospital mortality observed in the Hb-ARV quartile 4 (Table 3, all p < 0.05). Notably, Hb-SD and Hb-CV were not consistently associated with crude in-hospital and 1-year mortalities in four cohorts (Fig. S2-S3), while Hb-ARV consistently showed a distinct J-shaped relationship (Fig. S4).

In the multivariable regression models, each 1 g/L increase in Hb-ARV was significantly associated with an increased risk of in-hospital mortality in all four cohorts (adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) 1.04, 95% CI: 1.01–1.08 in MIMIC-IV; aHR 1.07, 95% CI: 1.03–1.11 in eICU; aHR 1.10, 95% CI: 1.06–1.14 in AmsterdamUMC; and aHR 1.10, 95% CI: 1.05–1.15 in JSPH). The second outcome was 1-year mortality, which was highest in the fourth quartile of Hb-ARV in both the MIMIC-IV and AmsterdamUMC cohorts (Fig. S5). Compared to the second quartile of Hb-ARV, the fourth quartile of Hb-ARV was associated with a significantly increased risk of 1-year mortality across the MIMIC-IV (aHR 1.28; 95% CI: 1.04–1.56; p = 0.02) and AmsterdamUMC (aHR 1.56; 95% CI: 1.14–2.12; p = 0.005, Table 3) cohorts.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

The subgroup analyses furtherly showed no significant interaction between groups with elderly or not, sex, surgical status, illness severity, or CRRT duration. A greater impact of Hb-ARV might exist in those who received more RBC transfusions in the MIMIC-IV, Amsterdam, and JSPH cohorts (Fig. S6–S7).

The results were valid when sensitivity analysis using hematocrit and RBC counts, both showing a J-shaped or linearity restricted cubic spline curve (Fig. S8–S9, Table S4–S5). Accounting for the impact of detecting frequency to the exposure, the sensitivity analyses using time-standardized hemoglobin variability (Hb-ARV/counts of hemoglobin per days) as an exposure also showed a similar result (Fig. S10). To strengthen causal inference, propensity score weighting analyses using twang package showed similar results after weighting on a comprehensive set of baseline characteristics (Tables 2 and 3). Furthermore, to examine potential confounding by nutritional status, we adjusted for serum albumin in spline models within subsets of patients with albumin data (MIMIC-IV, eICU, and AmsterdamUMC). The J-shaped association persisted, supporting the robustness of the findings (Fig. S11).

Mediation and ROC analyses

Four multivariate mediation models were constructed to investigate the potential mediation role of Hb-ARV in the relationship between fluid balance change per day, RBC infusion per day during CRRT, BMI, and net ultrafiltration rate (NUF) and in-hospital mortality (Table S6). The results revealed that Hb-ARV was a statistically significant but weak mediator of daily fluid balance changes to in-hospital mortality across the MIMIC-IV (ACME: 2.88 × 10−6, 95% CI: 5.72 × 10−8 to 0.00, p = 0.04, Proportion mediated = 5.80%), and JSPH cohorts (ACME: 5.85 × 10−6, 95% CI: 8.03 × 10−7 to 0.00, p = 0.02, Proportion mediated = 6.20%); a weak mediator of daily blood transfusions to in-hospital mortality within both the MIMIC-IV (ACME: 2.92 × 10−5, 95% CI: 5.35 × 10−6 to 0.00, p = 0.02, Proportion mediated = 28.9%) and JSPH cohorts (ACME: 7.44 × 10−5, 95% CI: 6.39 × 10−6 to 0.00, p = 0.04, Proportion mediated = 6.67%); and a weak mediator of BMI to in-hospital mortality in the eICU (ACME: −8.01 × 10−4, 95% CI: −1.98 × 10−3 to 0.00, p = 0.04, Proportion mediated = −7.05%) and JSPH cohorts (ACME: −1.52 × 10−3, 95% CI: −3.10 × 10−3 to 0.00, p = 0.02, Proportion mediated = 1.32%).

ROC analyses using fixed thresholds informed by RCS curves showed modest discriminative ability across cohorts, with area under curves (AUC) ranging from 0.53 to 0.60. Using a cutoff of 8 g/L in MIMIC-IV and eICU yielded specificities around 75–89% but low sensitivities (15–39%), while a higher threshold of 12 g/L in the JSPH cohort showed high specificity (93.7%) and very low sensitivity (11.0%) (Table S7).

Discussion

Main findings

In the analysis of four global cohorts, excessive Hb-ARV was found to be associated with increased in-hospital and 1-year mortality in critically ill patients with AKI undergoing CRRT. These findings were consistent across all subgroups. Sensitivity analyses confirmed a similar association between hematocrit variability and RBC count variability and increased in-hospital mortality, supporting the reliability and consistency of our results.

Current knowledge of Hb-ARV levels during CRRT

To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has examined the relationship between hemoglobin variability during CRRT and survival outcomes in critically ill patients with AKI. In patients receiving chronic hemodialysis, excessive variability in monthly measured hemoglobin is associated with an increased risk of mortality [42–47] and may contribute to a higher incidence of cardiovascular events [48,49], cerebrovascular events [50], and infection risk [51,52]. However, the association between hemoglobin variability in hemodialysis and mortality risk in patients undergoing hemodialysis is likely confounded by concurrent disease severity [53]. In contrast, short-term Hb-ARV may reflect fluctuations in plasma volume during CRRT [19,20], as well as potential bleeding [23] and loss of blood cells [24,25]. Therefore, Hb-ARV levels may be significantly associated with the prognosis of critically ill patients receiving CRRT. Our study is the first to demonstrate a significant association between excessive Hb-ARV levels and increased in-hospital and 1-year mortality in critically ill patients with AKI undergoing CRRT.

The mechanisms underlying the association between excessive Hb-ARV levels and increased mortality remain unclear. Elevated Hb-ARV values may indicate rapid increases or significant decreases in hemoglobin levels within a short period. Rapid increases in hemoglobin could be related to aggressive net ultrafiltration, leading to hypovolemia and hypotension [16], which are strongly linked to increased mortality [54,55]. Conversely, significant decreases in hemoglobin may suggest fluid resuscitation or significant blood loss. Fluid resuscitation is often associated with shock or severe hypovolemia, whereas blood loss could occur because of hemorrhage or clotting in the CRRT circuit, both of which are indicators of poor prognosis [56]. Hemoglobin fluctuations may also be influenced by erythropoiesis disturbances and inflammatory responses, both of which are common in patients undergoing CRRT and associated with adverse outcomes [52]. Our mediation analyses suggested that RBC transfusion, fluid balance, and BMI might minimally affect in-hospital mortality via excessive Hb-ARV. These findings support the idea that Hb-ARV could serve as a weak modifiable marker for fluid balance, transfusion, and nutrition, all of which could impact outcomes in patients with AKI undergoing CRRT.

Interestingly, Hb-ARV, rather than Hb-SD or Hb-CV, was consistently associated with mortality in four cohorts. Hb-ARV reflects variation relative to the previous hemoglobin measurement, whereas Hb-SD and Hb-CV reflect variation relative to the mean. Thus, Hb-ARV levels may be more sensitive to changes and better correlate with patient outcomes. Additionally, a J-shaped relationship was observed between Hb-ARV levels and mortality risk, indicating that minimal Hb-ARV variation was not linked to improved prognosis. In particular, low Hb-ARV may reflect persistent, uncorrected anemia—possibly due to under-transfusion—or may characterize patients with early mortality, who did not survive long enough to exhibit hemoglobin fluctuations. Supporting this, in the MIMIC-IV cohort, the first octile group for Hb-ARV had lower SOFA scores, less use of ventilation and vasopressors, higher baseline creatinine, lower initial hemoglobin, and fewer hemoglobin measurements. Despite similar crude mortality with the second octile group, adjusted analysis showed higher mortality risk of the first octile group, which may help explain the J-shaped association.

Clinical implications

Our findings generate the hypothesis that strategies aimed at reducing Hb-ARV may help mitigate adverse outcomes in patients undergoing CRRT. First, avoiding rapid ultrafiltration is crucial, as it can lead to hemodynamic instability and elevated Hb-ARV [16]. Recent studies suggested that continuous monitoring of hematocrit and relative blood volume during CRRT may optimize ultrafiltration management and help prevent intravascular volume depletion [19,20]. Further validation is required to determine whether this approach can reduce mortality risk, although a pilot trial indicating that ultrafiltration profiles guided by online monitoring systems did not significantly impact the incidence of intradialytic hypotension in ICU settings [57]. Second, future studies should investigate whether regional citrate anticoagulation can reduce hemoglobin consumption and variability, particularly in patients at high risk of bleeding [58]. Third, it is worth exploring whether the use of biocompatible dialyzers could minimize hemoglobin loss and reduce Hb-ARV [25]. Fourth, adequate nutrition is vital for patients undergoing CRRT, as protein-energy wasting may impair RBC production and lead to anemia [59]. Finally, timely and effective treatment of the underlying cause of AKI is essential to reduce inflammation and promote recovery.

Strengths and limitations

This study identified Hb-ARV as a potential modifiable factor to impact both in-hospital and 1-year mortality in CRRT for patients with severe AKI. A key strength of our study was the replication of analyses across four distinct cohorts from different countries, yielding consistent results. Additionally, since Hb-ARV can be derived from widely available routine clinical hemoglobin measurements, the integration of Hb-ARV into patient care can be feasible during daily clinical practice.

However, this study has several limitations. First, residual confounding may persist despite multiple adjustments. For example, serum albumin, a key marker of nutritional and inflammatory status, was missing across cohorts and could not be included. Furthermore, Hb-ARV may act as a surrogate for unmeasured illness severity or clinical instability, rather than as an independent causal factor. Second, although Hb-ARV was correlated with clinical factors such as fluid balance, transfusion volume, and BMI, its independent contribution to mortality remains uncertain. Moreover, while the mediation effects through transfusion and fluid balance were statistically significant, their small effect sizes suggest limited clinical impact. Third, baseline serum creatinine definitions varied due to inconsistent CKD data, possibly introducing measurement bias. We also excluded patients with < 3 hemoglobin measurements—many of whom died early—which may have introduced survivorship and selection bias. While each 1 g/L increase in Hb-ARV was associated with a 4–10% increase in mortality, this effect size, though statistically significant, may have limited prognostic value at the individual level. Finally, as an observational study, causality cannot be inferred. Current evidence on interventions specifically targeting Hb-ARV in CRRT patients is limited. Therefore, prospective trials are needed to clarify the mechanistic role of Hb-ARV and assess whether modifying it can improve clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

Excessive Hb-ARV during CRRT—reflecting fluctuations in plasma volume and blood loss—is associated with an increased risk of in-hospital and 1-year mortality in critically ill patients with AKI. Our consistent findings in four cohorts generate the hypothesis that strategies aimed at reducing excessive Hb-ARV, such as optimizing volume status, minimizing blood loss, and improving bleeding control, may mitigate adverse outcomes in patients undergoing CRRT. Prospective studies are warranted to validate this hypothesis.
