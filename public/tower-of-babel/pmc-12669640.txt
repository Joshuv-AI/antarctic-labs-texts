Visual motion perception and driving hazard visibility at night-time
Authors: Catherine Kennon, Joanne M Wood, Philippe F Lacherez, Alex A Black, Damian Cuda, Emily Henry, Allison M McKendrick
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMotion perception is an important visual process involved in driving, and has been associated with daytime driving performance, however, few studies have investigated the association of motion perception with night driving. This study examined the relationship between motion perception and performance on a computer-based off-road Night-time Hazard Visibility Test (NHVT) in 70 licensed drivers (M = 53.4 ± 23.9 years; 18–84 years), with and without eye disease. Motion perception was tested binocularly on four motion tasks: minimum displacement thresholds (Dmin), minimum contrast and minimum velocity of a drifting Gabor, and motion surround suppression based on minimum duration of a high-contrast Gabor using two Gabor sizes. Participants also completed chart-based measures of visual acuity and contrast sensitivity under photopic and mesopic conditions and hazard response times measured using the NHVT. In bivariate linear regression models, poorer motion perception thresholds for all tests were associated with delayed NHVT response times (p ≤ 0.006). In multivariate models, only Dmin remained independently associated with NHVT response times, when adjusted for chart-based tests (p ≤ 0.016). Our results provide new insight into aspects of motion perception important for night-time hazard detection, with inter-individual variability in ability to detect small motion displacements being important for rapid identification of moving hazards in night driving scenes.Keywords: Motion perception, Night driving, Night hazard visibility test, Hazard perception response times, Minimum displacement thresholds, Drifting gabor gratings, Motion surround suppressionSubject terms: Psychology, Motion detection

Introduction

Perceiving and interpreting motion in the visual world is an important and complex visual process involved in everyday activities, including driving. Examples of driving tasks that are likely to depend on motion perception include detecting the motion of other road users including vehicles, pedestrians and cyclists, judging the speed of one’s own motion and other road users to estimate time-to-contact, judging distances from oncoming moving vehicles to enable accurate gap selection, and judging the direction of heading of one’s own motion to guide steering and control1–6. The visual system also needs to detect small motion displacement cues that are salient within the visual scene and spatially localised (for example, detecting a cyclist or pedestrian that is moving relative to the background), and be able to globally integrate large complex motion fields present within the whole driving scene, enabling timely detection of moving hazards such as other vehicles, pedestrians and cyclists7–10.

Several studies have demonstrated an association between laboratory-based measures of impaired motion perception and reductions in daytime driving performance measured using off-road computer-based measures of hazard perception presented in videos of driving scenes using the Hazard Perception Test (HPT), as well as on closed and open roads, and in driving simulators, as described below.

Impaired minimum displacement thresholds (Dmin), assessed foveally using random-dot kinematograms, were significantly associated with delayed response times to road hazards assessed using the daytime HPT in drivers of different ages2,11,12, with eye disease8,13, and for peripherally presented Dmin targets12. Impairments in minimum drift rates assessed foveally using Gabor patches were also associated with delayed HPT response times in drivers of different ages2,11. However, a more recent study failed to find an association between minimum contrast thresholds for a drifting Gabor (presented either centrally or peripherally) and HPT response times12.

In closed-road studies, impaired Dmin has also been shown to be associated with poorer overall driving performance scores that included hazards hit, sign recognition, and lane-crossing times in older drivers of different ages, with and without eye disease13,14. Similarly, impaired Dmin has been shown to be associated with overall driving safety scores in on-road studies of older drivers15–17, and in those with eye disease9. Impaired minimum drift rate was also associated with worse standardised composite driving scores assessed on-road during a daytime driving assessment in older drivers with and without eye disease18, and with increased at-fault prospective and near-miss crashes recorded during naturalistic driving19. Driving simulator studies have also demonstrated that impaired minimum drift rates measured peripherally were associated with poorer driving performance in older drivers for rater scores of performance, simulator crashes, minimum distance of approach to hazards and lane deviation times20,21. Associations between reduced psychophysical thresholds to motion-in-depth and emergency braking, and dynamic visual acuity and hazard detection have also been demonstrated in driving simulator studies of young drivers with normal vision5.

While the focus of the studies reported above has been on daytime driving, night driving is a particular challenge due to low light levels that reduce the visual information in the driving environment, resulting in fewer anticipatory cues that enable detection of hazards. These challenges are reflected in the higher fatality rates at night compared to the day22,23, particularly for crashes involving vulnerable road users, such as pedestrians and cyclists24.

Light levels on night-time roads are within the mesopic zone, with abrupt changes in light levels due to oncoming headlights and other light sources within the driving environment25–27, which are likely to influence the perception of moving stimuli within the driving environment. Both rod and cone photoreceptors contribute to visual function under mesopic light levels28, with complex interactions between the two photoreceptor systems through shared neural pathways29, creating potential conflicts for visual processing, given that rod responses are slower than cones30,31. These changes are reflected through studies demonstrating that many characteristics of motion perception are degraded under mesopic light levels, including speed perception32–35, global motion coherence36–38, and biological motion36–41. The ability of the visual motion system to adapt to abrupt changes in luminance that are typical of the night-time driving environment is also reduced42.

There is also evidence from two closed-road studies, that higher psychophysical thresholds for motion on a Dmin task are associated with impaired pedestrian recognition and overall night driving performance7,10. Impaired Dmin was significantly associated with shorter night-time pedestrian recognition distances10, and poorer overall night-time driving performance7.

In this study we aimed to further explore the association between motion perception and night driving. A range of motion perception tests were selected which have been shown to be associated with indices of daytime driving. Performance on a newly developed off-road computer-based test (night-time hazard visibility test (NHVT)43, was used as an index of night driving. The NHVT is similar to the daytime HPT but includes presentation of real-world video clips of road hazards at night-time (e.g., other vehicles, cyclists, pedestrians). The night-time driving scenes include hazards with different levels of anticipatory cues (i.e., semantic or contextual cues regarding the upcoming presence of a hazardous event, e.g., an intersection), and contain hazards typically encountered on roads at night-time, many of which are visually challenging (e.g., low contrast pedestrians positioned within visually complex scenes or in the presence of glare). In some driving scenes, the hazards have limited anticipatory cues, with recognition of hazards relying on visual detection, which is representative of night-time driving. This differs to daytime HPT tests which were designed to discriminate between novice and experienced drivers, and include anticipatory cues regarding the onset of a hazard within the scene44. The daytime HPT has been shown to predict driving performance and crash risk in both younger and older drivers17,45–48. Studies have also demonstrated that the NHVT is sensitive to simulated vision impairments, including refractive and cataract blur, and is significantly associated with night-time pedestrian recognition distances in closed road studies, providing preliminary support of its validity as a measure of night-time visibility response times43,49.

The aim of this study was thus to assess the relative strength of the association between a range of motion perception tests and a measure of night-time hazard perception, in order to determine whether previously identified associations between motion perception and hazard perception are relevant to the night-time driving environment. We explored these associations in a community-dwelling sample of participants across a range of ages, with and without eye disease, who are representative of the broader driving population.

There were also significant bivariate associations between NHVT response times and photopic visual acuity (VA) (β = 0.517, 95% CI = 0.31, 0.724, p < 0.001) and photopic contrast sensitivity (CS) (β = -0.458, 95% CI = -0.242, -0.673, p < 0.001), with reduced visual function associated with slower NHVT response times. A multivariate model of photopic VA and CS predicted 30.3% of the variance in NHVT response times (F(2,67) = 14.56, p < 0.001). Similarly, there were also significant bivariate associations between slower NHVT response times and reduced mesopic VA (β = 0.560, 95% CI = 0.359, 0.760, p < 0.001) and reduced mesopic CS (β = -0.566, 95% CI = -0.366, -0.765, p < 0.001). A multivariate model of mesopic VA and CS predicted 35.0% of the variance in NHVT response times (F(2,67) = 18.07, p < 0.001).

In the multivariate models, the association between the motion tests and overall NHVT response times were attenuated after adjusting for the photopic and mesopic chart measures (Table 2, Models 2 and 3). Only Dmin remained independently associated with NHVT response times when adjusted for the chart tests (Model 2: β = 0.413, 95% CI = 0.121, 0.704, p = 0.006; Model 3: β = 0.362, 95% CI = 0.068, 0.655, p = 0.016).

As the Dmin test was the only motion test independently associated with overall NHVT response times, an additional analysis assessed its association with response times for the different NHVT hazard types (pedestrians, cycles/scooters, cars). In the bivariate models (Table 3, Model 1), Dmin was associated with delayed NHVT response times for all hazard types, particularly for response times to pedestrians (β = 0.638, 95% CI = 0.451, 0.824, p < 0.001). Discussion

In this study, reduced performance on a variety of motion perception tasks was shown to be significantly associated with delays in the detection of driving-related hazards in videos of night-time road scenes on the NHVT, in a cohort of drivers with a range of ages, visual function and motion perception ability. Previous studies have demonstrated that motion perception ability is associated with hazard perception in videos of daytime road scenes8,11,12. Our study significantly extends that previous work, demonstrating that hazard perception in night-time road scenes can also be partially predicted by psychophysical thresholds to motion.

Motion perception is complex and can be assessed in the laboratory and clinically using a wide variety of stimuli. In this study we chose to use relatively primitive stimuli (gratings, random-dot kinematograms) and to measure threshold performance for aspects of motion perception considered to be building blocks for more complex motion percepts. While the visual requirements for on-road driving utilise other complex cues, our findings imply that the response time to identify a hazard partially depends on threshold visual abilities. This may be particularly the case in night-time road scenes where objects are often low-contrast, such as slow moving pedestrians wearing dark-coloured, non-reflective clothing. Indeed, our data demonstrates that Dmin thresholds were independently associated with delayed responses for detection of pedestrian hazards. Nevertheless, because we did not include laboratory measures of more complex aspects of visual motion processing, our experiment does not address whether there are other laboratory measures that might reveal even stronger relationships with the NHVT. One possible example is optic flow parsing, which enables observers to distinguish between self-motion and the motion of objects within the visual scene50. Previous research demonstrates that optic flow parsing ability is largely preserved with healthy aging51. While it is clear that severe failures of optic flow parsing create profound perceptual difficulties52, additional research is required to reveal whether smaller differences in flow parsing ability within otherwise healthy observers are associated with hazard detection ability in night-driving scenes.

While performance on all the included motion perception tests was associated with NHVT scores, only Dmin thresholds remained independently associated after correcting for age, or performance on the chart based tests (VA and CS). Dmin was the only motion task that was itself mesopic, however, this seems unlikely to be the key explanatory factor given that Dmin also predicts daytime HPT performance8,11,12. The motion velocity task that involves drifting Gabors arguably tests similar visual attributes, because at threshold the minimum drift rate represents the smallest shift in a grating that can be detected. Consistent with this view, Lee, Black et al.53 showed that impaired minimum motion velocity perception was also associated with poorer daytime HPT performance in age-adjusted analysis in a sample including older adults with and without glaucomatous field loss. The same relationship was not found here, which may relate to differences in populations tested, and measurement noise, but might also have been influenced by the fact that the motion velocity task was photopic, whereas the Dmin task was mesopic. The finding that visual abilities tested using Dmin and clinical chart-based tests only explained around one third of the variance in NHVT response times is expected, given that perception of hazards is influenced by other factors including driver experience, cognitive abilities and reaction times54.

Even though many of our videos included pedestrians as the hazards, we did not include a biological motion task in the motion test battery. Previous research shows that the threshold for detecting biological motion in super-imposed noise was not associated with pedestrian hazard perception times within videos of daytime road scenes, yet Dmin thresholds were12. A possible explanation for this previous observation is that the extraction of biological motion signals from noise is poor in peripheral vision55, hence the initial detection of pedestrians in the video scenes is more likely to rely on simpler motion or object detection cues, with the specific combined biomotion information becoming relevant once detected and foveated. This does not mean that biomotion cues are not important for road safety. Indeed, using retroreflective strips to accentuate biomotion cues can markedly improve night-time pedestrian visibility56–61. Exaggerating biomotion cues in this fashion may partially compensate for other deficits in motion perception and improve road safety.

In this study, we included a measure of surround suppression of motion. Surround suppression has been shown to promote figure-ground segmentation of moving objects62, and also to guide attention to more peripherally eccentric salient scene locations63. When assessed with central fixation, surround suppression of motion weakens in older adults64, and has been shown to be causally associated with a reduction in the stimulus duration necessary to perform rapid figure-ground segmentation of moving objects62. In our sample of adults of varying ages, we found marked inter-individual differences in surround suppression indices, however, these did not independently explain NHVT scores when included in a model with VA and CS (see Table 2). Previous demonstrations of the link between surround suppression and motion segmentation used textured motion-defined form stimuli so that the only cue to detect the relevant object was motion62. This is clearly not the case in driving scenes. Night driving scenes largely lose the benefit of colour cues, however, other object cues such as contrast, occlusion, and differences in relative motion between objects and self-motion are still present. Our results indicate that the ability to detect nighttime hazards is partially explained by threshold salience of objects (visual acuity, contrast sensitivity and threshold motion displacements). Future research is needed to determine whether other measures, such as optic flow parsing ability, are additionally informative in predicting nighttime driving hazard detection.

Our study population included a small sample of 16 drivers with eye disease with clinically assessed visual function that met the standards for an Australian drivers’ license. Including those with eye disease in studies of visual processing requirements for driving is important because they form a significant and growing number of older road users. In this study, we were interested in the relationship between motion perception abilities and the NHVT, regardless of the underlying cause of deficits in motion perception. Whether certain eye or visual pathway disorders cause damage to motion perception is outside the scope of this study and specific studies in the future will explore this.

While the NHVT is designed to infer night driving ability, it is important to recognise that the NHVT is an off-road test comprised of videos of night driving scenes presented on a computer monitor. Natural driving scenes have a wider visual angle, however, previous research shows that computer-based driving (HPT studies) have proven validity for predicting crash risk and driving performance17,45–48. Of specific relevance, response times on the NHVT have been shown to be significantly associated with night-time pedestrian recognition distances in a closed-road study49. That association, together with our finding here that Dmin thresholds are related to NHVT response times for pedestrian hazards implies that Dmin thresholds can predict aspects of the ability to detect pedestrians at night while driving.

In conclusion, our results provide new insight into the aspects of motion perception that are important for the detection of hazards in night-time road scenes. While there are many complex motion cues present in driving scenes, inter-individual variability in the threshold ability to detect small motion displacements appears to be important for the rapid identification of moving hazards at night.

Methods

Participants

Participants included seventy licensed drivers with a mean age of 53.4 ± 23.9 years (range 18–84 years), 37 male (53%) and 35.2 ± 23.5 years driving experience, recruited from QUT’s Optometry Clinic, students and staff. Sixteen participants had a diagnosis of mild to moderate eye disease: comprising 6 central vision conditions, 4 with glaucoma, 4 with cataracts and 2 with other conditions. The study followed the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Queensland University of Technology Human Research Ethics Committee. Participants signed an informed consent form before commencing the study which gave a full explanation of the study, the procedures involved, and possible consequences.

Procedures

Testing was undertaken in a single session. Participants wore their habitual correction used for driving (if any) with appropriate working distance correction for tests conducted at 1 m. Participants completed visual function tests under photopic (130 cd/m2) and mesopic (0.3 cd/m2) conditions, followed by the NHVT and a series of computer-based motion perception tasks under ambient mesopic lighting conditions (0.3 cd/m2). All participants underwent an assessment of visual function which included an ocular health screening (slit lamp biomicroscopy and ophthalmoscopy) to establish the presence of eye diseases.

Visual acuity

Distance high contrast VA was measured binocularly under photopic and mesopic conditions using the Early Treatment for Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) chart (Good-Lite Co., Elgin, IL) at a working distance of 4 m using the letter-by-letter scoring method65.

Contrast sensitivity

Letter CS was measured binocularly under photopic and mesopic conditions with the Pelli-Robson Contrast Sensitivity at a working distance of 1 m, with + 1.00DS working distance correction, and scored letter-by-letter (0.05 log units per each letter correct). Guessing was encouraged, and any confusions between the letters “O” and “C” were recorded as correct responses66.

Motion perception tests

Participants completed a series of computer-based motion perception tests conducted binocularly under mesopic room lighting conditions (0.3 cd/m2). All motion tests were written in Python using the module PsychoPy version 2021.2.367, and displayed on a calibrated 54 cm BenQ monitor with a refresh rate of 120 Hz, a spatial resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels and a pixel size of 0.28 mm. All tests used two interleaved psychometric staircases which included three-down, one-up with four reversals, with thresholds for each staircase calculated as the mean of the final two reversals for each staircase, where the final threshold was the average of the two means. Crosshair markers were presented prior to the beginning of each trial and removed during presentation of the stimulus as appropriate. Participants verbally reported the direction of motion of the stimuli, which was recorded on the keyboard by the experimenter. The four motion perception tasks are outlined below:

Minimum motion displacement

This task determines the smallest displacement of dots required to perceive the direction of motion. This was assessed using square random-dot kinematograms, comprised of 1 × 1 pixel white dots (luminance 23.5 cd/m2) on a black background (luminance 0.3 cd/m2) with a dot density of 43% (average monitor luminance 0.7 cd/m2). The stimulus was a central field of pixels (3.9° diameter) presented within a larger region of pixels (4.9° diameter). Each stimulus was presented for 667 ms in a square-wave temporal envelope. The stimulus moved in one of four directions; up, down, left, or right, with participants required to indicate the direction of motion. Testing was conducted at a 3 m working distance.

Minimum contrast of a drifting gabor

This task determines the lowest level of contrast required to determine the direction of motion of a drifting Gabor. This was assessed using a vertically orientated Gabor patch (σ = 1.35°), of spatial frequency 3 c/° and drift rate of 2 c/° presented on a uniform grey background (average monitor luminance 81.8 cd/m2). Each stimulus was presented for 250 ms in a square-wave temporal envelope. The stimulus moved in one of two directions (left or right), with participants required to indicate direction of motion55. Testing was conducted at a 1 m working distance.

Minimum velocity of a drifting gabor

This task determined the slowest speed required to determine the direction of motion of a drifting Gabor. This was assessed using a vertically orientated Gabor patch (σ = 2°), of spatial frequency 3 c/° and 50% Michelson contrast (average monitor luminance 81.4 cd/m2). Each stimulus was presented for 1 s, and the stimulus moved in one of two directions, (left or right), with participants required to indicate the direction of motion11. Testing was conducted at a 3 m working distance.

Motion surround suppression

This task measured the difference in stimulus duration required to determine the direction of drift of a small and large vertically orientated Gabor patch, to determine a surround suppression index39,62,64. This was assessed using high-contrast Gabor patches (75% Michelson contrast), with a spatial frequency of 1 c/°, drift rate of 2°/s (mean luminance 87.3 cd/m2). Stimuli were presented in a square-wave temporal window and duration thresholds measured for two different sized patches (2.1° and 15°) which moved in one of two directions, (left or right), with participants required to indicate the direction of motion. The suppression index was calculated as the difference between the thresholds, where a positive suppression index signified spatial surround suppression, which is associated with improved ability to segment moving objects from wide-field moving backgrounds62. Testing was conducted at a 1 m working distance.

Night-time hazard visibility test

Our research team has recently developed and evaluated a night-time hazard visibility test (NHVT) to assess night-time hazard visibility for night-time driving scenes43. The test consists of 26 videos of real traffic situations which were captured using a dashcam (QVIA AR790, field of view: 107 degrees horizontally; 55 degrees vertically), internally mounted in the vehicle to capture the driving environment in front of the vehicle. The videos included potential night-time traffic hazards, filmed from the driver’s perspective and contain 32 hazards, 19 (59%) were pedestrians, 6 (19%) were cars, 5 (16%) were cycles/scooter, with the remaining 2 (6%) involving multiple hazards that occurred simultaneously. Figure 2 provides some examples of the traffic hazards included in the NHVT videos. Traffic hazards were defined as any road users or objects within the driving scene that have the potential to become a hazard, requiring the driver to take evasive action, such as slowing down, braking, or changing course to avoid a crash.

Examples of hazards from the Night Hazard Visibility Test videos, with red dotted lines highlighting the hazards.

Videos were presented on a computer monitor, where the mean luminance across all the driving scenes was 3.0 cd/m2, ranging from 0.3–6.2 cd/m2, reflecting various road lighting environments, from dark suburban streets to well-lit city streets. The monitor screen resolution was 1920 × 1080 pixels, and dimensions of 51 cm × 28.5 cm (38–40 degrees × 24–26 degrees) at a working distance of 60 to 65 cm, with a working distance correction (+ 1.50 DS). The luminance range of these driving scenes may not fully represent the true range of light levels on night-time roads and glare intensities from oncoming headlights, and the visual angle presented will be smaller than that experienced during normal driving. However, this off-road approach provides a range of night driving scenarios in a safe and controlled environment and is supported by studies that validate the use of daytime computer-based HPT tests to predict crash risk and driving performance45–48.

Participants were adapted to the mesopic light levels of the ambient room lighting (0.3 cd/m2) and instructed to view the videos and indicate the presence of any traffic hazards by clicking the computer mouse on the road user involved, for example, other vehicles, pedestrians or cyclists. A traffic hazard was defined as “any road user or object within the driving scene that has the potential to become a hazard and would require you as a driver to take evasive action such as slow down, brake, or change course to avoid a crash”.

The outcome measure was the hazard response time, defined as the time between when the hazard first appeared and the first time that the participant correctly clicked the mouse on a defined area of interest (AOI) within the scene. These AOIs represented areas of the screen at a given time point when there was an active hazard present and were defined using the Tobii Studios software (Tobii Technology, Danderyd, Sweden). As the time at which hazards appeared varied across the videos, response times for each hazard were converted to z-scores, using the mean and standard deviation (SD) of all responses to each hazard to standardize responses. The average response time was calculated across all hazards, being the mean (and SD) of the first correct click on a hazard relative to when the hazard first became visible; this was used to convert the z scores to a NHVT response time to aid in interpretation of the findings.

Analysis

Analysis was performed using SPSS version 30 (IBM, Chicago, IL). Descriptive statistics are reported as mean and standard deviations (SD). Initially, the association between each motion perception test and overall NHVT response times was explored using bivariate linear regression models, presented as the β-coefficients and 95% CIs (Model 1). To examine the independent association of each motion perception test and overall NHVT response times, multivariate linear regression models were conducted adjusting for photopic measures of VA and CS (Model 2), or mesopic measures of VA and CS (Model 3). For motion tests that were independently associated with overall NHVT response times, an additional analysis was run to assess their association with the NHVT response times for the different hazard types (pedestrians, cycles/scooters and cars). For each model we visually evaluated the assumptions of normality, linearity and homoscedasticity and all assumptions were met. Multicollinearity in the multivariate models was assessed by quantifying the variance inflation factor (VIF), with high multicollinearity defined as a VIF of more than 10.
