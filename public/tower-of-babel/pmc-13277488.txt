Comparative efficacy of agomelatine and escitalopram in people with epilepsy and comorbid major depressive disorder: A double-blind randomized controlled trial
Authors: Mariana Fernandes, Claudio Liguori, Francesca Avvento, Diego Lombardi, Natalia Manfredi, Luisa Mari, Francesca Izzi, Alessandro Castelli, Andrea Pagano, Giovanni Di Mauro, Anna Teresa Giallonardo, Emanuele Cerulli Irelli, Carlo Di Bonaventura, Andrea Romigi, Nicola Biagio Mercuri, Fabio Placidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Agomelatine and escitalopram improved depressive symptoms in people with epilepsy.•Seizure frequency, sleep and daytime alertness were unaffected by antidepressant treatment.•Escitalopram showed more consistent benefits on mood and quality of life.

Introduction

Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder affecting approximately 50 million people worldwide [1], [2] and is characterized by recurrent unprovoked seizures and often coexists with psychiatric comorbidities. Among its comorbidities, major depressive disorder (MDD) is the most prevalent [3], with reported prevalence rates of 22.9–23.1% in people with epilepsy [4]. Despite the high prevalence of MDD in people with epilepsy (PwE), it frequently remains underdiagnosed and undertreated [5], [6], [7], [8], leading to a significant decline in quality of life [9].

The relationship between epilepsy and depression is bidirectional and highly complex [10], [11], encompassing both psychological and common neurobiological mechanisms. The psychosocial burden of epilepsy, including stigma, lifestyle constraints, and chronic stress, may contribute to the onset of depressive symptoms. Conversely, alterations in common neurobiological pathways, namely serotonergic, glutamatergic, and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis dysfunction, along with structural and functional brain changes, may predispose individuals to both epilepsy and depression, suggesting that MDD may precede or follow the onset of epilepsy [12].

The misconception that antidepressants universally lower the seizure threshold has been a significant barrier to their use in PwE [3]. However, evidence from previous studies suggests that many modern antidepressants, particularly selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), do not significantly increase seizure risk and may even exert neuroprotective effects [13], [14]. Among these, escitalopram, a well-established SSRI in this patient population, has shown strong efficacy in managing MDD, also in PwE, due to its lower potential for interactions with antiseizure medications (ASMs), making it a preferred first-line treatment choice [15]. Agomelatine was considered another promising option for treating MDD, as it represents an antidepressant with a novel mechanism of action [16]. Unlike SSRIs, agomelatine's mechanism of action targets melatonin receptors as an agonist (MT1/MT2) and serotonin 5-HT2C receptors as an antagonist, suggesting potential benefits for seizure modulation and sleep regulation [17], [18], [19], [20], [21]. While escitalopram has been widely used in clinical practice, the comparative effects of agomelatine and escitalopram in PwE remain unclear. This study aims to evaluate and compare the efficacy and safety of agomelatine and escitalopram in treating MDD in PwE, with additional assessments of seizure frequency, sleep quality, and cognitive performance.

Methods

Study participants and design

This multicenter, phase IV, double-blind, randomized, parallel-group controlled trial was conducted across 3 specialized epilepsy centers in Italy to compare the effects of agomelatine and escitalopram on depressive symptoms, quality of life, and seizure frequency in PwE and comorbid MDD. Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older with a confirmed diagnosis of focal or generalized epilepsy and comorbid MDD, according to DSM-5 criteria (i.e., the presence of at least five depressive symptoms during the same 2-week period, including either depressed mood or loss of interest or pleasure) [22], and showing a Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) score greater than 13. They were required to be on stable ASM therapy for at least 6 months before enrollment. Exclusion criteria included a history of psychiatric disorders other than MDD, previous antidepressant use, participation in other clinical trials, or an inability to comply with the study protocol. Patients were also excluded if they were using central nervous system active medications beyond ASMs, had a history of psychogenic nonepileptic seizures or status epilepticus in the previous 12 months, or had clinically significant laboratory abnormalities that could interfere with the participation − such as liver enzyme levels (transaminases) more than 3 times the upper limit of normal (ULN), hemoglobin < 10 g/dL, or leukocyte count < 3500/mm. Further exclusion criteria included hepatic injury risk factors (e.g., obesity, diabetes, substantial alcohol intake, or use of hepatotoxic medications), pregnancy or inadequate contraception in women of childbearing potential, and a QTc interval > 450 ms on baseline electrocardiogram, and any pre-existing liver disease.

Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either agomelatine or escitalopram using a computer-generated randomization sequence. To maintain blinding, identical-appearing capsules were used to ensure that neither participants nor investigators were aware of treatment allocations. The study design involved five visits over a 24-week treatment period. Following the screening visit (V0), eligible participants were randomized and received their assigned treatment at baseline (V1). Study visits occurred at baseline (V1), and following four weeks (V2), twelve weeks (V3), and twenty-four weeks (V4) after starting the experimental treatment. At each visit, depressive symptoms, quality of life, sleep quality, daytime sleepiness, and cognitive function were evaluated, and seizure frequency during the past month was recorded. Agomelatine was administered orally at 25 mg once daily in the evening, while escitalopram was administered orally at 10 mg once daily in the evening. Patients maintained stable doses throughout the study period without modifications.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University Hospital of Rome Tor Vergata (R.S. 191/17–––192/17) and was registered in the European Union Clinical Trials Register under EudraCT number 2017-000990-35. All patients provided written informed consent.

Measures

Self-reported questionnaires

The Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) [23], [24] was administered individually to each patient to evaluate depressive symptoms. This self-report measure consists of 21 items, each offering four response options, with higher total scores reflecting greater symptom severity. The total score classification includes the following ranges: 0–13, indicating minimal or no depressive symptoms; 14–19, representing mild depression; 20–28, reflecting moderate depression; and 29–63, indicating severe depression. While these cut-off values were established for the general population, studies have confirmed their validity in assessing depression severity in PwE [25].

The Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HDRS) [26], [27] is a clinician-administered scale comprising 21 items, each addressing a specific depressive symptom. The assessment is conducted through a structured interview performed by a trained professional. The scale evaluates various aspects of depression, including mood, feelings of guilt, suicidal ideation, sleep disturbances, appetite changes, difficulties with concentration, psychomotor agitation, and somatic complaints. Each symptom is rated on severity, typically on a scale of 0 to 4, with higher scores reflecting more severe symptoms. The total score is obtained by summing individual item scores, providing an overall measure of the patient's depressive state.

The Quality of Life in Epilepsy Inventory-31 (QOLIE-31) [28], [29] is a self-reported questionnaire comprising 31 items that assess various aspects of quality of life in individuals with epilepsy. The questionnaire is designed to capture patients’ personal experiences and perceptions of their health status. Responses are recorded on a Likert scale, reflecting the frequency or severity of specific symptoms and difficulties. The scores from different domains are combined to generate an overall quality of life index, where higher scores indicate better quality of life, and lower scores suggest greater impairment.

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [30], [31] was used to assess sleep quality and disturbances over a 1-month period. The questionnaire consists of 19 items that contribute to 7 components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each component is scored on a scale from 0 to 3, with the global PSQI score ranging from 0 to 21. A global score greater than 5 is indicative of poor sleep quality.

The Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) [32], [33] was used to evaluate excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS). Participants rated their likelihood of dozing off or falling asleep in 8 different daily situations using a 4-point scale (0–3). The total ESS score ranges from 0 to 24, with scores of 10 or above suggesting the presence of EDS.

Neuropsychological battery

The Mental Deterioration Battery (MDB) [34] is a neuropsychological assessment designed to evaluate functional efficiency across various cognitive domains, including verbal and visuospatial abilities, memory, constructive praxis, language, and logical-deductive reasoning. The battery consists of 7 tests, divided into 2 sections based on whether they involve verbal or visuospatial processing.

The verbal section includes the Immediate and Delayed Recall of Rey's 15 Words, Phonological Verbal Fluency, and the Sentence Construction test. Specifically, the Immediate and Delayed Recall of Rey's 15 Words (RAVLT-I and RAVLT-D, specifically) assesses learning and recall abilities using a list of 15 words representing concrete objects with varying usage frequencies. Immediate recall is tested across 5 trials, while delayed recall is assessed after a 15-minute interval. The Phonological Verbal Fluency test evaluates the accessibility and efficiency of the lexical-semantic memory store. Participants are required to generate as many words as possible beginning with the letters F, A, and S, with a time limit of 1 minute per letter. The Sentence Construction test examines syntactic organization by assessing the ability to form meaningful sentences. Participants must construct a coherent sentence using 2 given words in 2 trials and 3 given words in 3 trials, for a total of 5 trials.

The visuospatial section comprises the Raven's Coloured Progressive Matrices, Immediate Visual Memory, Freehand Drawing Reproduction, and Programmed Drawing Reproduction. Specifically, the Raven's Coloured Progressive Matrices (CPM) assess logical-deductive intelligence within the visuospatial domain. Participants must identify the correct stimulus figure from 6 alternatives that complete the pattern presented on each of the 36 test plates. The Immediate Visual Memory test evaluates visual memory retention by requiring participants to recognize a previously presented stimulus figure among 4 alternatives after a 3-second delay. The Freehand Drawing Reproduction test assesses constructive praxis by instructing participants to replicate three geometric models (a star, a cube, and a house) through freehand drawing. The Programmed Drawing Reproduction test further examines constructive praxis by requiring participants to reconstruct the same 3 models from the freehand drawing task using predefined structural elements such as dots, lines, and angles, which must be connected by segments. Each model is reproduced multiple times, with the star drawn twice and the cube and house drawn 3 times.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0. Descriptive statistics were computed for baseline characteristics, including counts and percentages, as well as measures of central tendency such as means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges, depending on data distribution. Data were analyzed using non-parametric tests. First, the Mann-Whitney U test was used for comparisons between the agomelatine and escitalopram groups at the final time point (V4). Second, within-group changes from baseline (V1) to V4 were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed‑rank test for both the escitalopram and agomelatine groups. Finally, the Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the delta scores (V4-V1) between the two groups to assess treatment effects. Change scores (V4 − V1) were calculated for each outcome variable to facilitate between-group comparisons of treatment effects. To account for multiple comparisons, p-values were additionally adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure within each treatment arm and outcome domain. Statistical significance after correction was set at an FDR-adjusted p-value < 0.05.

Results

Sample Description

A total of 305 patients were assessed for eligibility during the pre-screening phase, which was primarily conducted during outpatient visits. Of these, 241 were excluded based on the study's inclusion and exclusion criteria, resulting in 64 participants undergoing screening. This sample consisted of 33 women (51.6%) and 31 men (48.4%), with a mean age of 55.39 years (SD = 16.07). Sixty-four patients were screened, but 13 were subsequently excluded due to screening failure because they did not meet the depression severity criterion, having a BDI-II score below the inclusion cut-off. Thus, 51 participants were randomized and initiated treatment: 25 patients were allocated to the agomelatine group and 26 patients to the escitalopram group. Of those assigned to agomelatine, 5 patients (20%) discontinued the study: 2 due to adverse events, 2 by personal choice, and 1 who reconsidered participation. In the escitalopram group, the dropout rate was higher, with 13 patients (50%) failing to complete the study: 1 due to adverse events, 8 by personal choice, and 4 who later reconsidered and chose to withdraw. Drug tolerability and safety

During the study, 4 moderate adverse events were reported, all of which resulted in the immediate termination of the study treatment. The overall incidence of adverse events was low (7.8%). There was 1 adverse event in the escitalopram group (insomnia), and 3 in the agomelatine group (swelling, diarrhea and erythema). All adverse events were considered potentially drug-related and resolved after the treatment discontinuation. No serious adverse events were reported.

Sleep outcomes and quality of life

Regarding sleep measures, no significant differences were observed between the agomelatine and escitalopram groups in sleep quality at the final time point (V4; p = 0.832; Table 2). Similarly, between-group comparisons of the changes from baseline to V4 also failed to show any significant differences. In the within-group analyses (Table 3), sleep quality improved slightly in both groups, but did not reach statistical significance, with PSQI scores changing from 8.00 [IQR 7.0–11.0] to 7.5 [IQR 4.5–10.0] in the agomelatine group; and from 9.00 [IQR 6.5–11.0] to 7.50 [IQR 5.0–13.0] in the escitalopram group. Similarly, for daytime sleepiness (ESS), no significant between-group difference was observed at follow-up (p = 0.578), nor for the delta (V4-V1) between the groups, as assessed with the Mann-Whitney U test. Within-group changes also failed to reach significance, with ESS scores remaining essentially unchanged in the agomelatine group, from 4.0 [IQR 3.0–8.0] at baseline to 5.0 [IQR 3.0–7.5] at follow-up, and showing a non-significant decrease in the escitalopram group, from 7.0 [IQR 2.0–8.0] to 5.0 [IQR 3.0–7.0].

No significant differences were observed between the agomelatine and escitalopram groups in quality of life at the final time point (V4; p = 0.429, Table 2). Similarly, between-group comparisons of change scores in QOLIE-31 did not reach statistical significance. Within-group changes from baseline (V1) to V4 showed that the QOLIE-31 scores remained stable in the agomelatine group, from 62.0 [IQR 48.0–69.0] at baseline to 61.0 [IQR 50.0–69.5] at follow-up, whereas the escitalopram group exhibited an improvement from 52.0 [IQR 46.5–62.0] to 61.0 [IQR 55.0–75.0], which remained significant after FDR correction. Discussion

Potential interactions between antidepressants and ASMs pose specific challenges in treating MDD in epilepsy, particularly regarding seizure worsening [3], [35]. Effective and well-tolerated treatments could help PwE improve their mental health and global quality of life. This study assessed the impact of agomelatine and escitalopram on depressive symptoms, quality of life, cognitive performance, sleep, and seizure frequency in PwE and comorbid MDD.

The main finding of this study is that both treatments reduced depressive symptoms; however, there was no significant difference between the two treatment groups in depressive symptoms reduction, as measured by the BDI-II and HDRS, indicating comparable efficacy in mood improvement. Specifically, the most robust changes after multiple-comparison correction were observed for HDRS in the agomelatine group and for both BDI-II and HDRS in the escitalopram group. These findings align with previous studies showing that, without significantly increasing the seizure frequency, SSRIs, including escitalopram, can efficiently treat MDD in PwE [15], [36]. Agomelatine acts differently because of its specific mechanism of action, comprising 5-HT2C antagonism and melatonin receptor agonism. Although its possible antiseizure effect via melatoninergic pathways has been speculated [37], this study found no significant effect on seizure frequency, in contrast to earlier animal studies suggesting that agomelatine has both antidepressant and possible antiseizure effects [17], [20]. Moreover, previous literature postulated that SSRI, such as fluoxetine, can increase epileptogenesis and magnify disease severity in a rat model of acquired epilepsy [38]. It is important to note, however, that none of the antidepressant treatments in this study resulted in worsening of seizures, supporting that escitalopram, as an SSRI, and agomelatine are generally safe in PwE.

Both antidepressants showed no significant impact on sleep quality and daytime sleepiness. This finding differs from previous studies indicating that agomelatine enhances sleep architecture and reduces sleep disturbances [18], [39], [40], [41], [42], even when compared to escitalopram [21]. These results may reflect the multifactorial nature of sleep problems in epilepsy, which may not always respond consistently to antidepressant therapies. Additionally, specific features of our study population may explain these differences. In terms of quality of life as assessed by QOLIE-31, no significant differences were found between the two groups. However, the escitalopram group showed a significant within-group improvement over time, which remained significant after correction for multiple comparisons, while the QOLIE-31 scores remained stable in the agomelatine group. This finding supports previous studies that linked SSRIs, particularly escitalopram, to enhancing general well-being in PwE [36], [43].

Regarding cognitive function, no significant differences were observed between agomelatine and escitalopram across the neuropsychological battery. In the within-group analyses, a significant improvement after adjustment for multiple comparisons was observed only for immediate verbal memory (RAVLT-I) in the agomelatine group. Conversely, the cognitive changes found in the escitalopram group for changes in immediate verbal and visual memory did not remain significant after FDR correction. These findings only partially align with previous studies, indicating that SSRIs, namely escitalopram, may improve executive function and memory performance in people with depression [44]. Additionally, the cognitive improvement observed with agomelatine provides only limited support for prior evidence of broader pro-cognitive benefits in depression [45], [46] and other neurological conditions [47]. Overall, these data suggest that the cognitive effects of antidepressant treatment in PwE require further investigation in larger studies to better clarify their extent and clinical relevance, especially considering the heterogeneity of the sample, which included both focal and generalized epilepsy. Since these epilepsy types may be associated with distinct cognitive profiles and patterns of impairment, such heterogeneity may have contributed to variability in neuropsychological performance. However, stratified analyses by epilepsy type were not possible due to the limited sample size.

With a low occurrence of AEs (insomnia, erythema, and swelling, as well as diarrhoea), both treatments were mostly well-tolerated. Agomelatine’s most common side effects were swelling, diarrhoea, and erythema, while insomnia was reported as an AE related to escitalopram treatment. These results match current safety profiles for both drugs [21], [36], [48], [49]. Notably, neither treatment increased the frequency of seizures, supporting previous studies showing the relative safety of these antidepressants in epilepsy [13], [14].

This study has certain limitations that need to be addressed. First, despite an a priori sample size estimation, the actual number of participants enrolled may have limited the statistical power to identify variations between the two treatments. The high dropout rate, particularly in the escitalopram group, probably reflects the demanding study design and the inability to modify pharmacological treatment, which makes it difficult to fully understand treatment adherence and long-term effects. Furthermore, it is possible that the 24-week study period was insufficient to record any potential delayed effects on cognitive function and seizure frequency. Larger, longer-term studies using objective neurophysiological measurements should be included in future research.

Despite its limitations, this study offers valuable insights for clinical practice into the management of MDD in PwE. Both agomelatine and escitalopram were associated with improvement in depressive symptoms, without evidence of seizure worsening or adverse effects on sleep quality and daytime alertness, supporting their potential usefulness in this population. Cognitive benefits were more limited after correction for multiple testing, with evidence of improvement restricted to immediate verbal memory in the agomelatine group. These findings may help physicians in selecting antidepressants according to individual patient needs while maintaining alertness and sleep quality. Overall, this study emphasizes the need to consider epilepsy and depression as linked diseases that warrant a combined approach to improve overall outcomes.

Although no significant differences were found between treatment groups, the overall findings suggest that both escitalopram and agomelatine may represent safe and possibly effective treatments for MDD in PwE, without worsening seizures. Escitalopram showed a more consistent pattern of benefit across mood and quality-of-life measures, whereas agomelatine appeared to have a more selective and limited impact on specific domains. However, the unexpectedly high dropout rate in the escitalopram group raises questions about adherence and tolerability, which should be further explored. To better understand the long-term effects of these treatments on epilepsy and to determine whether agomelatine's distinct pharmacological profile may offer specific benefits in epilepsy, additional studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up times are needed.
