Advanced heat-assisted flexible forming of perforated AZ31B magnesium alloy: a hybrid experimental–numerical study for high-precision biocompatible cranial implant fabrication
Authors: Sudarshan Choudhary, Rahul Gurpude, Amrut Mulay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Single Point Incremental Forming (SPIF) is widely recognised as a flexible manufacturing process for producing complex sheet metal components, particularly in small-batch and customised applications. Magnesium alloy known as a lightweight operational material used mainly in biomedical sector. Current research investigates the formability and surface quality aspects of perforated AZ31B magnesium alloy extended to cranial preparations which has limited formability at room temperature. To overcome this limitation, present work introduces warm heating setup technology aimed to enhance formability of the AZ31B Mg alloy. The study was performed at 400 °C so that the material could deformed till the desired depth of the implant. Identifying a 12 mm tool diameter and a 3.5 mm hole diameter as optimal parameters proves crucial as it yields uniform higher formability and lower surface roughness. Moreover, considering optimal parameters the study finds that a cranial implant, made by zig-zag tool path, 300 mm/min feed rate, and a 0.2 mm step depth, shows both enhanced forming precision and a decrease in surface roughness. Additionally, research investigates changes in microstructure and hardness studies, providing valuable insights into material properties and performance. A finite element model was constructed using the commercial FE-package ABAQUS/Standard. The study investigates von Mises stresses, Plastic equivalent strain, and forming depth across different input parameters.

Introduction

Incremental Sheet Forming (ISF), particularly Single Point Incremental Forming (SPIF), is a flexible and cost-effective manufacturing process used to produce complex sheet metal components without requiring dedicated dies. With the increasing demand for customized products, there is a growing need for faster and more adaptable manufacturing techniques, especially in biomedical fields such as cranial implant fabrication [1]. Unlike conventional methods such as stretch forming, which require expensive and shape-specific dies, SPIF gradually deforms the sheet using a simple forming tool that follows a predefined path. This progressive and controlled deformation allows precise shaping of patient-specific implant geometries[2, 3]. The high flexibility of SPIF makes it especially suitable for prototyping and small-batch production, offering a practical and efficient solution for customized cranial implants while reducing tooling cost and manufacturing time [4, 5]. In the era of thermally-assisted ISF, limited progress has been made. Xu et al. [6] demonstrated that Electrically-Assisted Double-Sided Incremental Forming (EA-DSIF) enhances the geometric accuracy and surface finish of AZ31B sheets, attributing improvements to reduced flow stress and dynamic recrystallization. Similarly, Zhang et al. [7] developed an oil-bath warm ISF setup, identifying forming temperature as the dominant factor influencing formability, followed by step depth and tool diameter. Liao et al. [8] integrated ultrasonic vibration (UV) with warm SPIF, reporting that UV aids in stress distribution and increases plastic strain uniformity, thereby improving formability and energy efficiency. Expanding on hybrid methods, Song et al. [9] proposed a synchronized electric pulse and ultrasonic vibration ISF process, which yields superior surface quality compared to using electric pulses alone. Furthermore, Li & Wang [10] introduced an equivalent heating tool method for friction stir incremental forming, significantly reducing computational time in simulations while maintaining temperature trend accuracy. Simultaneously, research on forming at elevated temperatures has provided deeper insights. Mohanraj et al. [11] experimentally investigated warm ISF on AZ31 and Al 6061, concluding that 300 °C offered optimal formability with minimal geometrical deviation for asymmetric parts. Lee & Yang [12] utilized infrared heaters and heated tools, successfully forming complex pyramidal and cylindrical cups with wall angles up to 72° with AZ31, a substantial increase from the 20° achievable at room temperature. For more severe deformations, Su et al. [13] studied the forming limit under isothermal local loading, finding that higher temperatures increase the forming limit angle but reduce extreme thinning, with 250 °C as a critical threshold. Regarding perforated sheet forming a key aspect for modulation in implants recent studies have begun to explore its feasibility. Bouzidi et al. [14] conducted a foundational feasibility study on SPIF for perforated sheets, noting that required axial force is significantly greater than for non-perforated sheets compared to perforated sheet, but study of warm forming conditions is limited. The interaction between hole geometry and formability remains an open area of research. In the specific domain of biomedical implant fabrication, the translation of ISF to patient-specific geometries has advanced. Palumbo et al. [15] presented a structured CAD/CAM methodology for designing and manufacturing titanium cranial prostheses via sheet metal forming, achieving high geometrical accuracy. For facial implants, Araújo et al. [16] and more recently, Liu et al. [17] have utilized ISF with circle grid analysis and FEM to predict failure and optimize the forming of complex facial transplants. The work of Bouilia et al. [18] demonstrated that complex biomedical components could be fabricated using SPIF, a principle that recent studies continue to validate and refine for new materials and conditions. Campanella et al. [19] developed a numerical model for predicting forming limits in hot incremental forming of AZ31B, incorporating temperature-dependent material behavior. Furthermore, advancements in damage modeling, such as the modified Johnson-Cook parameters used by Li et al. [20] for high-temperature flow behavior, have improved the accuracy of simulations predicting fracture in warm forming processes.

Although several studies have attempted to improve magnesium alloy formability using elevated temperatures. Despite the increasing use of lightweight magnesium alloys in biomedical applications, their adoption in Single Point Incremental Forming (SPIF) for complex implant geometries remains limited due to poor room-temperature formability and the absence of reliable and uniform heating strategies. Most previous studies have focused either on improving forming limits using external heating methods or on optimizing individual process parameters, but have not considered the combined effects of warm forming, perforated sheet geometry, and toolpath strategies within a unified SPIF framework. Furthermore, the complete process chain from heating system design and process optimization to implant fabrication and material behaviour analysis has not been comprehensively addressed, particularly for cranial implant applications.

The present work addresses this gap by introducing a heat-assisted SPIF approach with a simple and effective localized heating arrangement designed to provide uniform and controlled heating of perforated AZ31B magnesium alloy sheets [19, 21]. This enables stable warm forming at 400 °C while preserving the flexibility and advantages of the SPIF process. Special emphasis is placed on cranial implant manufacturing, where lightweight properties, biocompatibility, surface integrity, and geometric accuracy are essential. Unlike earlier research, this study systematically investigates the combined influence of tool diameter, hole diameter, process parameters, and toolpath strategies on formability and surface quality. In addition, the study examines the resulting microstructural evolution and hardness changes to provide a deeper understanding of material behaviour under warm forming conditions relevant to biomedical performance. A finite element model is also developed to complement the experimental work by analysing stress distribution, equivalent plastic strain, and forming depth. By integrating heating system development, experimental characterization, numerical simulation, and final implant fabrication, this research establishes a practical and validated forming strategy for producing high-precision magnesium alloy cranial implants.

Experimental methodology

Experimental study aims to optimize process parameters using a systematic approach including material selection, its characterization, simulation, parameters selection and process optimization. Further investigation extends to determining optimal parameter combinations for fabrication of cranial implant from perforated AZ31B sheet.

Material selection and mechanical characterization

AZ31B magnesium alloy sheets of size 110 mm × 110 mm and thickness 1 mm were selected due to their proven suitability for incremental forming applications, adequate room- and warm-forming capability. AZ31B magnesium alloy sheets were selected due to their proven biocompatibility and biodegradability, which allow safe interaction with biological tissues without releasing harmful by-products. Additionally, their ability to gradually degrade in the human body makes them suitable for temporary biomedical applications such as cranial implants. The alloy offers a high specific strength-to-weight ratio making it attractive for lightweight structural components in automotive, aerospace, and biomedical sectors. Microstructural and micro-hardness study

The microstructural characterization of AZ31B magnesium alloy was conducted to examine grain morphology and grain boundary evolution before and after the forming process. Samples were extracted from both the as-received material and the deformed regions of the formed components. Prior to microscopic examination, all specimens underwent standard metallographic preparation. This involved sequential grinding and polishing using diamond suspensions under aqueous conditions until a scratch-free surface was achieved. To reveal the grain structure, the polished samples were etched for 5 s using a freshly prepared solution consisting of 2 g picric acid, 35 ml ethanol, 5 ml acetic acid, and 5 ml distilled water. Optical microscopy was then employed to examine the etched surfaces. Micrographs were captured for both the base material and the formed zones to facilitate comparative analysis of microstructural changes induced by forming and thermal exposure.

Vickers microhardness testing was employed to evaluate the mechanical response of AZ31B magnesium alloy before and after the forming process. The tests were performed using a METCO Economet VH-1MDX microhardness tester. Prior to testing, all specimens were metallographically polished to ensure a smooth and uniform surface suitable for indentation. For each sample, hardness measurements were taken at three different locations along the formed surface. The average of these readings was used to represent the hardness value of each condition. Both base material and optimally formed samples were tested under 100 g loading conditions for 10 s for direct comparison between the samples.

Components geometrical accuracy

To evaluate the geometric accuracy of the formed components, a non-contact three-dimensional scanning technique was employed using a Picza 3D scanner (Make: Roland; Model: PIX-30). The scanner as shown in Fig. 4c generates dense point-cloud data representing the surface geometry of the formed parts. The acquired point clouds were processed to extract the Z-axis profile of the formed components, which was then compared with the corresponding CAD model. The primary focus was placed on depth accuracy along the Z-axis, as it directly reflects forming precision and symmetry. The measured depth values from the scanned data were matched against the nominal depth of the CAD model to quantify dimensional deviations.

Surface roughness investigation

The surface roughness is an important indicator of component quality because it directly influences surface cleanliness, tissue interaction, and overall functional performance of formed parts. In this study, surface roughness is quantified using the arithmetical mean roughness (Ra), which represents the average deviation of the actual surface profile from an ideal mean line. Since incremental forming involves continuous tool–sheet contact, changes in process parameters can significantly influence surface texture through friction, localized deformation, and toolpath motion. Surface roughness measurements were carried out using a surface roughness tester (Make: Mitutoyo, Model: SJ-310), as shown in Fig. 5a. The instrument evaluates Ra by scanning the surface profile over a cutoff length (Lc) of 0.8 mm. To ensure reliable and representative results, three measurements were taken for each sample, and the average value was reported. These measurements were recorded at three different locations along the inner surface of the formed component, where tool–sheet interaction is most pronounced and surface variations are more likely to occur. A cranial implant with resulting surface textures under different forming conditions is presented in Fig. 5b. Finite element analysis for AZ31B sheet metal

The Finite Element Method (FEM) serves as the mathematical tool for this numerical study using Abaqus/Explicit version 2024. The blank was meshed with square-shaped S4R elements, each measuring 1 mm. Given that the h/L ratio where h is thickness and L is the length was less than 0.3 in this study, shell elements were utilized [22]. In the simulation, the sheet is created using the extrude feature and is considered a 3D deformable homogeneous solid. The blank had dimensions of 110 mm × 110 mm x 1 mm which can be observed as in Fig. 6a. The tool implemented in the simulation is hemispherical shaped with a diameter of 8 mm, 10 mm and 12 mm and is modeled as a 3D rigid analytical body.Fig. 6a Part assembly b Boundary conditions applied to assembled part

a Part assembly b Boundary conditions applied to assembled part

The tooltip and sheet surface are configured with surface-to-surface contact and a friction coefficient of 0.1 at their interface [23]. The sheet edges are fixed allowing no displacement in x, y, and z direction as shown in Fig. 6b. The tool motion is helical, based on coordinates generated in UG-NX software. These coordinates x, y, and z with respect to time t are exported to Abaqus for simulation. Mesh size plays a critical role in the precision of simulation outcomes, finer meshes yield more accurate results and improved failure predictions, though they increase computation time. Thus, selecting an optimal mesh size is crucial. For FEA, the sheets were meshed using C3D8I solid ABAQUS elements, which are eight-node trilinear displacement elements with reduced integration. Microstructural evolution and Vicker’s microhardness

The optical micrographs of the as-received AZ31B magnesium alloy, shown in Fig. 14a, b, reveal a homogeneous microstructure characterized by uniformly distributed equiaxed grains. In contrast, the microstructure of the formed samples, presented in Fig. 15a, b, exhibits noticeable grain elongation within the deformation zone. When the material was subjected to a forming temperature of 400 °C, significant grain growth occurred, with grains enlarging to some extent across the deformed region. Under uniform heating conditions, grain coarsening was found to increase proportionally with temperature, leading to a relatively uniform grain size distribution. Simultaneously, grain refinement and an increase in grain coarsening were observed with rising temperature, which can be attributed to enhanced nucleation rates at elevated temperatures. The grains within the deformed region were comparatively larger, more uniformly distributed, and revealed improved formability characteristics. Furthermore, thermal activation of slip systems in magnesium alloys facilitates increased dislocation mobility during deformation. This promotes the formation of dislocation density within the forming zone. As dynamic recrystallization and grain boundary progress, dislocations are progressively absorbed at newly formed grain boundaries, leading to a reduction in dislocation density within the grains. These findings are in strong agreement with Li et al. The Vickers microhardness measurements revealed relatively minor spatial variations across individual samples, indicating consistent material behavior. The as-received AZ31B magnesium alloy exhibited an average hardness of 64.66 N/mm², which aligns well with reported values for solution-treated AZ31B alloys. Forming under optimal conditions of Run 8, a substantial increase in hardness was observed, with the average hardness rising to 81 N/mm². The observed hardness improvement is attributed to the combined effects of microstructural evolution and material homogenization during forming. This microstructural evolution contributes directly to the observed increase in hardness. Specifically, grain boundary strengthening arising from grain size modification, residual stresses induced during deformation, and strain hardening all contribute to the increased hardness. In the present study, three different toolpath strategies like follow part, follow periphery, and zig-zag were investigated to evaluate their influence on material deformation behavior and part accuracy. The follow part toolpath combines circular and zig-zag movements and allows the tool to travel across the entire geometry of the component in a continuous manner. The follow periphery strategy involves circular contouring along the outer boundary of the part with incremental displacement in the Z-direction. In contrast, the zig-zag toolpath begins at a fixed depth and traverses the full geometry along back-and-forth contour lines before descending to the subsequent depth. Compared to the zig-zag strategy, both follow part and follow periphery toolpaths tend to deform the entire contour in a single pass, which can lead to sudden material stretching and springback. This behavior often results in dimensional inaccuracy and reduced geometric control. The zig-zag toolpath, by forming the material progressively in a layer-by-layer manner, distributes deformation more uniformly and reduces abrupt strain accumulation. As a result, the zig-zag strategy provides better control over material flow and shape accuracy, making it the most suitable toolpath for achieving stable forming conditions in the present work.

Surface quality of cranial implant

Based on the experimental data from Table 7, the achieved surface roughness is predominantly governed by the interaction between step depth (Δz) and toolpath strategy. The optimal surface finish, a minimum Ra of 1.14 µm, was recorded in Run 6, utilizing a follow-periphery toolpath with a feed rate (f) of 300 mm/min and a minimal step depth (Δz) of 0.2 mm. This parameter set minimizes cutting forces and promotes stable, continuous tool engagement, thereby reducing tool deflection and vibrational artifacts that degrade surface topography. Conversely, the poorest finish, a maximum Ra of 2.95 µm, occurred in Run 3 with a follow-part toolpath at the same 300 mm/min feed rate but a significantly larger step depth of 0.6 mm. This aggressive combination increases the uncut chip area, leading to elevated cutting forces, greater system vibration, and intermittent engagement particularly in corners which collectively amplify the peak-to-valley height of the machined surface. The results confirm that a shallow depth of cut paired with a stable toolpath strategy is critical for minimizing surface roughness.

Conclusions

The present study establishes a comprehensive warm Single Point Incremental Forming (SPIF) framework for perforated AZ31B magnesium alloy, specifically targeted for high-precision cranial implant fabrication, which has not been systematically demonstrated in earlier literature. Unlike previous studies that focused only on forming limits or heating methods independently, this work integrates localized heating design, perforation-based formability enhancement, process parameter optimization, numerical simulation, and final implant fabrication, thereby developing a complete and practical process chain for biomedical magnesium alloy forming.
