Decreased Fc fucosylation aligns with enhanced RSV-specific antibody functionality in adults compared to young children
Authors: Anke J. Lakerveld, Wenjun Wang, Rutger M. Schepp, Jan Nouta, Steinar Gijze, Anne T. Gelderloos, Nynke Y. Rots, Robert S. van Binnendijk, Gestur Vidarsson, Manfred Wuhrer, Puck B. van Kasteren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a major cause of severe respiratory tract infections in infants and older adults. While RSV vaccines have recently been approved for older adults and pregnant women, and newborns can be protected through monoclonal antibody immunization, vaccine development for infants remains challenging. Understanding immunological differences between target groups is essential for improving vaccination strategies, but no clear correlates of protection have been identified yet. Antibody Fc-mediated effector functions may play an important role. Here, we aim to delineate RSV-specific antibody features underlying differences in antibody functionality between children and adults.MethodsIn an observational cohort of 24-month old children and adults (n = 46 per group), we assessed RSV-specific IgG/IgA levels, IgG subclasses, avidity, Fc glycosylation, neutralization, and antibody-dependent NK cell activation, cellular phagocytosis, and complement deposition using a systems serology approach including (multiplex) bead-based immunoassays, mass-spectrometry, and in vitro cellular assays.ResultsHere we show that, compared to children, antibodies from adults display enhanced functionality. Furthermore, RSV-specific antibodies display differences in avidity and glycosylation patterns between the two groups, which might relate to differences in the number of previous exposures. Importantly, our data strongly suggest that IgG Fc afucosylation drives the enhanced capacity of RSV-specific antibodies to activate NK cells in adults.ConclusionsOur data provide a detailed overview of similarities and differences between RSV-specific antibodies in children and adults. This information will support the ongoing quest for correlates of protection and the design of future vaccination strategies in different target populations.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a highly infectious RNA virus causing seasonal epidemics of respiratory disease. Whereas most RSV infections result in mild symptoms, this virus is a major cause of severe lower respiratory tract infections in infants and older adults1,2. Despite many years of RSV vaccine development, immunity to this virus is still poorly understood, and correlates of protection have yet to be defined. High (mucosal) antibody levels might protect against RSV infection and disease through neutralization, especially in older adults3,4, but literature remains inconclusive5–7. Even in the presence of (neutralizing) antibodies, RSV reinfection is common8,9. Traditionally, the humoral immune response is quantified solely by measuring antibody levels and neutralization titers. However, antibodies also mediate other important effector functions via binding of their Fc tail to Fc receptors (FcRs). These Fc-mediated effector functions have been suggested to play an important role in protection against severe RSV10,11 and other viral respiratory diseases12–16.

Well-known Fc-mediated effector functions are antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP), antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), and antibody-dependent complement deposition (ADCD). Antibody-opsonized virus particles or virus-infected cells can be recognized by immune cells via their Fc receptors, resulting in phagocytosis (ADCP) or killing of the infected cell (ADCC). Natural killer (NK) cells are the main performers of ADCC, and assays often measure antibody-dependent NK cell activation (ADNKA) as a proxy for ADCC. Antibody opsonization can also result in complement activation and deposition on the cell surface (ADCD), leading to killing via the formation of membrane attack complexes or enhanced phagocytosis by attracted immune cells.

RSV vaccine development has progressed cautiously after the disastrous failure of a formalin-inactivated RSV vaccine in young children in the 1960s, leading to enhanced disease and even two deaths among RSV-naive children17–19. Recent RSV vaccines largely focus on the induction of neutralizing antibodies targeting the viral fusion protein F, which exists in a prefusion (pre-F) and postfusion (post-F) conformation. Specifically antibodies targeting the prefusion conformation mediate neutralization20. In 2023, the first RSV vaccines have received marketing approval from the FDA and EMA for use in older adults and pregnant women, to protect their newborns from birth21. Both are subunit vaccines that contain stabilized versions of the RSV pre-F protein. In addition, an mRNA-based RSV vaccine was approved for use in older adults22. Various vaccines are still in development, including several that aim to protect infants and children23. For infants with a high risk of developing severe disease, prophylaxis through the administration of an RSV F-specific monoclonal antibody (palivizumab) has been used in the clinic since 199824. However, its use has remained limited due to a short half-life, requiring monthly administrations with associated high costs. A novel RSV pre-F-specific monoclonal antibody with a longer half-life (nirsevimab) was recently approved for marketing and is currently being implemented into the national immunization programs of several countries25.

For the optimal design and implementation of future vaccination strategies, a better understanding of the immunological background of different target populations is necessary. Previously, we have shown that children and adults differ with respect to their RSV-specific antibody functionality26. We observed that, irrespective of antibody levels, RSV post-F-specific antibodies of adults were better at inducing ADNKA than those of children. The factors underlying this difference remained to be uncovered, but it is known that antibody functionality is highly regulated through epitope specificity, isotype and subclass composition, avidity, and glycosylation of the Fc tail (reviewed in ref. 27). For example, IgG3 is more potent in inducing Fc-mediated effector functions than IgG1, which in turn is more potent than IgG2 and IgG428.

Antibody Fc glycosylation influences Fc-mediated effector functions by affecting binding to FcRs and complement. The Fc tail contains a conserved N-linked glycosylation site consisting of a constant core structure that can be modified by the addition of fucose, bisecting N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc, known as bisection), galactose, and sialic acid29. Afucosylation, i.e., the absence of a fucose moiety, is known to strongly enhance ADCC and ADCP through facilitating binding to FcγRIIIa and FcγRIIIb, of which the former is the only FcγR found on NK cells30,31. ADCD on the other hand, is significantly enhanced for galactosylated antibodies through enhanced IgG-hexamerization potential and stronger interaction with complement protein C1q31,32. Overall, differences in antibody Fc glycosylation are associated with sex, age, and hormonal changes, while various autoimmune, infectious, cardiometabolic, and malignant diseases may show vastly skewed IgG Fc glycosylation profiles33,34.

Here, we provide an in-depth analysis of RSV antigen-specific antibodies in children (24 months) and adults (25–45 years). We assessed pre-F, post-F, and nucleocapsid (N)-specific IgG and IgA levels, IgG subclasses, IgG avidity, and Fc glycosylation in a cross-sectional cohort (n = 46 per group). In addition, we quantified virus neutralization and the Fc-mediated effector functions ADCP, ADCD, and ADNKA. Together, these data provide a comprehensive overview of the RSV-specific antibody landscape in young children and adults, supporting future research into correlates of protection and enhancing our understanding of RSV immunity in different populations.

Methods

Study design

To compare antibody effector functions between age groups, serum samples were selected from 24-month-old children (n = 46) and adults (25–45 years; n = 46) from the cross-sectional OKIDOKI study36. Samples had been collected during the winter of 2015/2016 (Oct–Feb). Exclusion criteria were known or suspected immunodeficiency, craniofacial or chromosomal abnormalities, coagulation disorders, or use of anticoagulant medication. Sample selection for the purpose of this study was based on availability of material, maintaining equal sex ratios, and equal distribution over the sampling period. Infants that had received palivizumab were excluded. Cells and viruses

THP-1 monocytic cells (ATCC TIB-202) were cultured in ATCC-modified Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)−1640 medium (30-2001; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 2 mM L-glutamine, 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 4500 mg/L glucose, and 1500 mg/L sodium bicarbonate, supplemented with 10% heat inactivated fetal bovine serum (hiFBS; HyClone, Fisher Scientific), 1% penicillin/streptomycin/glutamine (PSG; cat#10378016, Gibco), and 0.05 mM 2-mercapto-ethanol. Cells were cultured at concentrations between 0.2-0.8×106 cells/mL to maintain phagocytic activity of the cells. Vero cells (ATCC CCL-81) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 5% hiFBS and 1% PSG. All cells were cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

For NK cell isolation, peripheral blood from healthy adult volunteers was collected in heparin tubes and the peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) fraction was obtained by density gradient centrifugation (Lymphoprep; Alere Technologies AS). NK cells were purified from PBMCs by negative selection using a CD56+ NK cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec). Isolated NK cells were cultured in RPMI supplemented with 10% hiFBS, 1% PSG, and 5 ng/mL recombinant human IL-15 (Biolegend). Before use, NK cells were rested overnight at a concentration of 1 × 106 NK cells/mL at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Recombinant RSV/A/NLD/25147-X/1998 (here referred to as RSV-X; GenBank FJ944820.1) encoding green fluorescent protein (GFP) at genomic position 5 was propagated in Vero cells. The virus stock was purified between layers of 10% and 50% sucrose by ultracentrifugation. The 50% tissue culture infective dose (TCID50) per mL was determined on Vero cells using the Spearman and Karber method37.

RSV-specific multiplex immunoassay

To quantify the concentration of RSV-specific IgG and IgA, we performed a multiplex immunoassay following the previously described method38. Briefly, diluted serum samples were incubated with RSV post-fusion F-, pre-fusion F-, or nucleoprotein (N)-coupled beads. Captured antibodies were detected using a secondary R-phycoerythrin-labelled F(ab’)₂ goat anti-human IgG, Fcγ fragment specific (1:400; RRID: AB_2337681; cat#109-116-170; Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories) or R-Phycoerythrin-labeled F(ab’)₂ Goat Anti-Human IgA (1:800; cat#2052-09; SouthernBiotech). Participants were considered RSV seronegative if they had both a pre-F IgG concentration <1 AU/mL and a pre-F IgA concentration <0.2 AU/mL39.

For IgG subclass quantification, we used mouse monoclonal antibodies specific to human IgG1 (clone HP6069; 1:1000; RRID: AB_2534049; cat#A-10630, lot 2341658), IgG2 (clone HP6002; 1:1000; RRID: AB_2532258; cat#05-3500, lot UD286798), IgG3 Hinge region-biotin (clone HP6047; 1:1000; RRID: AB_2539711; cat#MH1532, lot 2395188), and IgG4 (clone HP6025; 1:1000; RRID: AB_2534053; cat#A-10651, lot 2752588) (all from Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Detection was achieved using R-phycoerythrin-labeled F(ab’)₂ goat anti-mouse IgG, Fcγ fragment-specific antibodies (1:400; AB_2338626; cat#115-116-071; Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories). The IgG3-specific monoclonal antibody was biotin-labeled, and detection required the use of streptavidin-phycoerythrin (SA-PE, Invitrogen).

Measurements were performed using a Flexmap 3D (Luminex Corp.) in combination with xPONENT 4.3 software (DiaSorin/Luminex). Serum IgG and IgA antibody concentrations were quantified as arbitrary units (AU)/mL by interpolation from a five-parameter logistic curve of an in-house reference serum, using Bio-Plex Manager software version 6.2 (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, California, United States). IgG subclass levels were expressed in median fluorescent intensity (MFI) after subtraction of the PBS background signal. If resulting values were ≤ 0, they were replaced with the lowest positive measured value divided by 2. The cut-offs for subclass seropositivity were set to the mean + 2 standard deviations of MFI values obtained from 35 RSV seronegative samples from different infant cohorts.

The IgG avidity index was determined using a similar bead-based multiplex immunoassay set-up, adapted from previously described ELISA-based methods for avidity determination40. Following the incubation of serum samples with antigen-conjugated beads, all samples were incubated for 10 min at room temperature in the presence of 3.0 M ammonium thiocyanate (NH4SCN) or PBS followed by a washing step. The avidity index was expressed as the percentage of residual IgG (MFI) signal in NH₄SCN-treated samples relative to the PBS control, which was set at 100%. As literature has suggested that exposure to NH4SCN might influence antigen integrity41, thus potentially affecting our read-out, we performed a control experiment in which we pre-incubated antigen-coated beads with NH4SCN before incubating with serum (in the absence of NH4SCN). The results suggested that NH4SCN at the concentration used affected the structural integrity of pre-F and N antigen, but not of post-F (Fig. S1). Therefore, we have only included the avidity data for post-F-specific antibodies.

Data from the multiplex immunoassays can be found in Supplementary Table S2.

Virus neutralization (VN) assay

The virus neutralization assay was performed as described previously with a few adaptations42. Vero cells were seeded in 96-wells plates two days prior to the neutralization assay. Two-fold serial dilutions were prepared using heat-inactivated serum samples starting at 1:10 in DMEM supplemented with 2% hiFBS and 1% PSG. Serum dilutions and a negative control without serum were mixed with an equal volume of RSV-X-GFP diluted in DMEM to achieve approximately 500 spots per well and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Serum-virus mixtures were added to the Vero cell monolayers in duplicate (50 μL/well) and incubated for 2 h. Supernatant was removed and cells were overlayed with 1% methyl cellulose in DMEM with 2% hiFBS and 1% PSG. After two days incubation at 37 °C, fluorescent foci were detected and counted with a fluorescence EliSpot reader (AID Autoimmun Diagnostika GmbH). Fifty percent focus reduction neutralization titers (FRNT50) were calculated by nonlinear regression analysis with PRISM (Graphpad), normalized to control wells incubated with RSV without serum. Data can be found in Supplementary Table S3.

Antibody-dependent NK cell activation (ADNKA) assay

Sterile ELISA plates (Immulon, Thermo Scientific) were coated with 0.5 μg/ml recombinant post-F (strain A2, 11049-V08B, Sino Biological) or pre-F in 100 μl PBS, or coated without antigen as negative control, and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, wells were blocked with 5% BSA in PBS. After washing with PBS, 50 μl heat-inactivated serum (diluted 500x in PBS) was added to the wells and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. After incubation, 25000 primary isolated NK cells in RPMI (10% hiFBS, 1% PSG) were added per well, in combination with brefeldin A (1:1000; BD Biosciences) and anti-human CD107a-PerCP/Cy5.5 (clone H4A3; 1:50; RRID: AB_1227509; cat# 328615; Biolegend). After incubation for 4 h at 37 °C, cells were washed with FACS buffer (PBS supplemented with 2 mM EDTA and 0.5% BSA) and stained for flow cytometric analysis. Extracellular staining was performed with anti-human CD3-FITC (clone UCHT1; 1:50; RRID: AB_314059; cat# 300405; Biolegend), anti-human CD56-PE (clone HCD56; 1:50; RRID: AB_604093; cat# 318305; Biolegend) and fixable viability staining-eFluor780 (cat#65-0865-14; 1:5000; Thermo Fisher Scientific). After fixing and permeabilization (fixation/permeabilization kit BD Biosciences), cells were stained intracellularly with anti-human IFN-γ-PE/Cy7 (clone B27; 1:100; RRID: AB_2123322; cat# 506517; Biolegend). Cells were resuspended in FACS buffer and acquired on a FACS LSRFortessa X20 (BD Biosciences). Data was analyzed with FlowJo software. NK cells were gated as CD3-CD56+ cells. The gating strategy is depicted in Fig. S2a. As CD107a and IFN-γ read-outs yielded similar results, only the former are shown. For each serum sample, results are expressed as geometric mean percentage of CD107a+ NK cells from four different NK cell donors. Data can be found in Supplementary Table S3.

Antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) assay

A bead-based antibody-dependent phagocytosis assay was adapted from Ackerman et al.43. Recombinant trimeric post-F (Flys-GCN4) produced in eukaryotic cells was biotinylated using a Sulfo-NHS-SS-Biotinylation kit according to manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Biotinylated post-F was coupled to 1.0 μm red fluorescent NeutrAvidin beads (Invitrogen) in a ratio of 1 μg protein to 1 μL beads and incubated overnight on a shaker at 4 °C in the dark. Subsequently, beads were washed and blocked with 2% BSA for one hour at room temperature. The antigen-coupled beads were then resuspended in PBS with 0.1% BSA to a concentration of 2.5 × 107 beads/mL. Using a volume of 20 μl, 0.5 × 106 beads were added per well of a 96-wells plate. Heat-inactivated serum, 1250x diluted in PBS, was added 1:1 (v:v) to the antigen-coupled beads to a final dilution of 2500x and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Antigen-coupled beads incubated with pooled serum with a very low post-F IgG concentration were used as negative control. After washing the beads twice with PBS supplemented with 0.1% BSA, 25000 THP-1 cells in 100 μl RPMI (10% hiFBS and 1% PSG) were added per well. Cells were incubated with the serum-bead complexes for 1 h at 37 °C. After washing with FACS buffer, cells were stained for flow cytometric analysis with fixable viability staining-eFluor780 (cat#65-0865-14; 1:5000; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were fixed in 1% formaldehyde and acquired on a FACS LSRFortessa X20 (BD Bioscience). Data was analyzed with FlowJo software. The gating strategy is depicted in Fig. S2b. Data are reported as phagocytic scores (integrated mean fluorescence intensity, iMFI), calculated as the percentage of bead-positive cells times the MFI of bead-positive cells divided by 1000. Data for each individual is averaged from technical duplicates. Data can be found in Supplementary Table S3.

Antibody-dependent complement deposition (ADCD) assay

A bead-based antibody-dependent complement deposition assay was adapted from Fischinger et al.44. Biotinylated post-F, as described for the ADCP assay, was coupled to red fluorescent NeutrAvidin beads and beads were brought to a concentration of 2.5 × 107 beads/mL as described above. 0.5 × 106 beads were added per well to a 96-wells plate. Heat-inactivated serum was added 1:1 (v:v) to the beads to a final dilution of 100x and incubated for 2 hours at 37 °C. BSA-blocked beads without antigen incubated with positive control serum were used as negative control. After washing with PBS with 0.1% BSA, 100 μL guinea pig complement (CL4051, Cedarlane Laboratories) diluted 1:25 in HBSS was added to the beads. After 15 minutes incubation at 37 °C, beads were washed with cold FACS buffer and stained with anti-guinea pig complement C3-FITC (1:100; cat# 0855385; MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana) for 30 min. Samples were fixed in 1% formaldehyde and acquired on a FACS LSRFortessa X20 (BD Bioscience). Geometric mean fluorescence intensity (gMFI) of single beads was acquired with FlowJo software. The gating strategy is depicted in Fig. S2c. Data for each individual is averaged from technical duplicates. Data can be found in Supplementary Table S3.

Fc glycosylation analysis of RSV-specific antibodies

Using the GLYcoLISA approach34, RSV-specific antibodies were sequentially captured using post-F-, pre-F-, and N-coated plates. Next to these affinity captured steps, the bulk IgG was enriched using a protein G (Thermo Fisher) coated plate. Briefly, 96-well Maxisorp NUNC-Immuno plates (Thermo Fisher) were separately coated with a concentration of 1 μg/mL antigens and 5 μg/mL protein G at 4 °C overnight. After washing with 0.05% Tween-20 in PBS, 10-fold diluted serum was first added to the post-F-coated plate and incubated for 1 hour while shaking at room temperature. Subsequently, the serum was transferred sequentially to the pre-F-coated, N-coated, and lastly to the protein G-coated plates following the same procedure (Fig. 1). The serum-incubated plates were then washed three times with PBS with 0.05% Tween, two times with PBS, and two times with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate. Antigen-bound antibodies were eluted from the plate using 100 mM formic acid spiked with the SILuMAB IgG1 protein standard (Sigma-Aldrich)45. Eluates were dried by vacuum centrifugation. Tryptic cleavage of IgG and LC-MS (glyco-)peptide measurements were carried out following the GLYcoLISA protocol34. In brief, (glyco-)peptides were separated on a NanoEase M/Z peptide BEH C18, 1.7 μm particles 130 Å pores, 75 μm × 100 mm (Waters) at a flow rate of 600 nL/min using a binary gradient of 0.02% aqueous TFA and 95% acetonitrile. MS signals of (glyco-)peptides were quantified by integrating MS peak areas after generating sum spectra of the retention times of interest. Raw LC-MS data files were converted to mzXML files using ProteoWizard MSConvert (version 3.0.23037). The mzXML files were processed in LaCyTools version 2.1.0 (build 20230525), using calibration signal-to-noise (S/N) cut-offs of 9 and 27 for antigen-specific and total IgG, respectively. GlycoDash (version 1.6.1, https://github.com/Center-for-Proteomics-and-Metabolomics/glycodash) was used to perform spectral and analyte curation, followed by total area normalization and calculation of glycosylation traits. GlycoDash processing was performed separately for post-F, pre-F, N, and total IgG data. An analyte was included in the final data analysis when it fulfilled three quality criteria (S/N > 9, isotopic pattern quality <0.2, absolute mass error <20 ppm) in at least 80% of adult and/or child sample spectra. For a list of quantified glycopeptide analytes, see Supplementary Table S4–9. Data obtained from positive and negative control samples to show assay specificity can be found in Fig. S3.

Statistical analysis and visualization

GraphPad Prism version 9.3 was used to determine FRNT50 scores. Statistical analysis and graph design were performed with RStudio version 4.4.0. Residuals (observed value minus predicted value) were calculated from a linear regression model for the antibody functions and post-F IgG levels. Samples with post-F IgG levels <100 AU/mL were excluded from this analysis because they were outside the linear range of the functional assays and ADCD, VN, and post-F levels were log10-transformed. To assess differences between two groups, the two-sided nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. Multiple group comparisons of antigen-specific antibody levels and glycosylation patterns (i) between age groups for each antigen and (ii) between antigens within each age group were performed using the nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test with Bonferroni’s correction for multiple testing. Correlations were assessed using the nonparametric Spearman method. The correlation matrix was generated using the rcorr function in the Hmisc package and visualized using the corrplot package. Missing data were excluded pairwise.

Ethics statement

Participants for the main analysis were included from an observational surveillance study with the primary aim of assessing pneumococcal carriage in children and their parents (“OKIDOKI”), performed in the Netherlands (trialsearch.who.int: NTR5405). This study was approved by the Medical Ethical Committee (METC) Noord-Holland. The current analysis is in line with the secondary aims of the study. RSV seronegative controls to determine IgG subclass cut-off values were selected from historical infant observational cohorts described previously26,35.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) for NK cell isolation for the ADNKA assay were obtained at the National Institute for Public Health and the Environment (RIVM, the Netherlands) from healthy adult volunteers. Blood samples were processed anonymously, and the research goal, primary cell isolation, required no review by an accredited Medical Research Ethics Committee (MREC), as determined by the Dutch Central Committee on Research involving human subjects (CCMO).

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their parents. This study was conducted according to good clinical practice, which includes the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Adults have significantly higher median levels of RSV pre-F-specific IgG and IgA than children

To obtain an in-depth overview of differences in RSV-specific antibody characteristics between the two populations, we analyzed serum samples (n = 46 per group) from children (24 months) and adults (25-45 years). Using a bead-based multiplex immunoassay (Luminex), we first assessed post-F-, pre-F-, and N-specific IgG and IgA binding levels. Adults displayed a slightly higher median level of pre-F-specific IgG, although several individual children had levels that exceeded the highest level observed in adults (Fig. 2a). No significant differences were found between children and adults in median post-F and N-specific IgG levels (Fig. 2a). Furthermore, median IgA levels were significantly increased in adults compared to children for all antigens (Fig. 2b). Based on previously determined cut-off concentrations of 1 AU/mL pre-F IgG and 0.2 AU/mL pre-F IgA39, two children and none of the adults were found to be RSV seronegative (open circles). Adult RSV antigen-specific antibodies display enhanced functionality compared to those of children

After gaining a global overview of antibody levels per group, we next assessed several antibody functions, including virus neutralization and Fc-mediated effector functionalities. Considering the high and comparable levels of post-F-specific IgG between children and adults, we opted to assess post-F-specific Fc-mediated effector functions (ADNKA, ADCP, and ADCD). Additionally, as post-F has several shared epitopes with pre-F, we expect that antibodies targeting either of these antigens will exhibit similar Fc-mediated effector functions. Overall, serum antibodies of adults showed higher absolute functionalities than those of children (Fig. 5, left panels). As absolute antibody functionality correlates strongly with antibody levels, we next plotted the antibody functionality against serum IgG levels to better reflect the influence of antibody concentrations (Fig. 5, right panels). In line with our previous results26, there was a clear distinction between adults and children in the capacity of their antibodies to induce ADNKA, with RSV-specific antibodies of adults showing stronger activation of NK cells, even when corrected for antibody levels (Fig. 5a). Additionally, a similar pattern was observed for ADCP and ADCD, although the separation between the groups appeared to be less distinct than for ADNKA, especially for ADCD (Fig. 5b, c). Neutralization titers were measured for a subset of each group (n = 20 per group) with comparable pre-F IgG levels, as these are most important for virus neutralization20. Again, antibodies of adults showed a higher neutralization capacity than those of children, even after correction for antibody levels (Fig. 5d). Other important antibody characteristics that might affect Fc-mediated effector functions are avidity—the polyclonal binding strength between antibody and antigen—and glycosylation of the Fc tail46,47. Increased capacity for RSV-specific ADNKA associates strongly with decreased Fc fucosylation

We then asked whether avidity and/or Fc glycosylation patterns might provide an explanation for the observed differences in antibody functionality. To assess these possible associations, we overlayed the correlation plots with a color gradient representing these antibody characteristics. If indeed an association would exist between avidity/glycosylation and functionality, one would expect to observe a distinct pattern in the color gradient with an upper-left to lower-right orientation. A trend towards such a pattern can be seen for the avidity index and ADCP, but it is much less pronounced for ADNKA and ADCD (Fig. S5, left panels). Using the residuals obtained from a linear regression between antibody functionality and levels reveals only a moderate correlation between avidity and relative functionality (Fig. S5, right panels). Importantly, when plotting adults and children separately, no clear patterns in the color gradient are seen (Fig. S6). This suggests that the observed relationship between avidity and ADCP might primarily reflect other differences between the two groups rather than a direct effect of avidity on functionality. Other antibody characteristics may thus be more important, for example, glycosylation of the Fc tail.

Indeed, Fig. 8a shows a very clear pattern in the color gradient representing fucosylation status, with higher fucosylation levels (darker colors) being associated with antibodies displaying a relatively decreased capacity for ADNKA and ADCP. This effect is especially evident for ADNKA, and is also seen when looking at adults and children separately (Fig. S7). Notably, the three children exhibiting exceptionally high ADNKA levels clearly stand out with lower fucosylated antibodies, indicated as the lighter data points (Fig. S7b). Correlation analysis of Fc fucosylation status and the residuals obtained from linear regression between ADNKA and antibody levels reveals a very strong correlation (r = 0.81, p < 0.0001, Spearman) with lower fucosylated samples consistently showing higher relative ADNKA scores than samples with higher levels of fucosylation (Fig. 8b). For ADCP the effect was less strong, which might be explained by the use of the monocytic THP-1 cell line expressing no or little FcγRIII48. Bisection of the Fc-glycan also seems to slightly associate with increased Fc-functionality (Fig. S8a). None of the other glycosylation traits showed clear patterns for ADNKA and ADCP (Fig. S8b–d), and no associations were observed for ADCD (Fig. S9). Discussion

The field of RSV vaccine development is evolving rapidly. The first vaccines and immunizations have recently received approval, and others are in (late-stage) development. These vaccines target a variety of populations: pregnant women, infants, children, and older adults23. With no clear correlate of protection for RSV defined to date, it is crucial to better understand differences in the antibody landscape between different populations. In this study, we performed an extensive RSV antigen-specific antibody analysis in 24-month-old children and adults. We measured pre-F-, post-F-, and N-specific IgG and IgA binding levels, IgG subclasses, IgG avidity, IgG Fc glycosylation, neutralization titers, and the post-F-specific Fc-mediated effector functions ADNKA, ADCP, and ADCD. We show that, on average, almost all measured antibody features are increased in adults, except for IgG3 seropositivity and Fc fucosylation, which are decreased. The increased Fc-mediated effector functions in adults, especially ADNKA, appear to be mostly explained by decreased Fc fucosylation.

Our data are in line with our previous study, which showed an increased capacity of adult antibodies to induce ADNKA independent of antibody concentrations26. In the previous study population, we did not observe a clear difference between adults and children in terms of ADCP and ADCD capacity, although a trend was visible for ADCP. The current study included a larger number of participants, and the difference in ADCP and ADCD between children and adults became more apparent. One of the limitations of this study is that these Fc-mediated effector function assays have a limited dynamic range. Especially the ADCD assay has a narrow range, with many of the 24-month-old children falling below the limit of quantification. Differences in the lower ranges are therefore more difficult to distinguish.

A recent study by our group did not reveal any differences in Fc-mediated effector functions between children and adults upon a primary infection with SARS-CoV-249. The observed differences between 24-month-old children and adults in RSV-specific functionality may be explained by repeated exposures to RSV, resulting in increased antibody maturation. RSV reinfections are common, and adults are infected with RSV multiple times throughout their life8,9,50. The 24-month-old children have likely experienced only a first, or in some cases second, infection with RSV. Most children in our cohort are deemed seropositive based on a previously determined cut-off value combining both pre-F-specific IgG and IgA levels39.

B cells undergo class switch recombination and somatic hypermutation, resulting in different antibody isotypes and subclasses with increasingly higher affinities. For RSV, it has been shown that primary RSV infections lead to the induction of both IgG1 and IgG3, while reinfections primarily lead to the production of IgG1 and IgG251–53. Recurrent RSV infections, therefore, result mostly in an increase in IgG1, IgG2, and in a decrease of IgG3 as an individual ages54. Correspondingly, we mainly detected IgG1 and some IgG2 in the serum of both children and adults. As IgG3 has a shorter half-life28, these antibodies may have already waned in most children, resulting in a low percentage that is seropositive for IgG3. The observed lack of IgG4 production upon RSV infection is also in line with literature55. Additionally, due to antibody maturation upon reinfections, the observed increase in avidity in adults was expected, and it has also been shown in literature that F-specific IgG avidity increases with age54.

We found that afucosylation is associated with the observed increase in Fc-mediated effector functions, especially ADNKA, in adults compared to children. Most human antibodies are fucosylated, but enveloped viruses and membrane-bound antigens have been shown to induce the production of afucosylated antibodies56. While the presence of these highly potent antibodies has been suggested to be protective in malaria and HIV infection, they have also been linked to excessive immune activation and clinical pathology upon Dengue and SARS-CoV-2 infection56–58. A particularly strong wave of afucosylated antibodies is apparent in the first week after seroconversion in immune-naive individuals experiencing COVID-19, which is most pronounced in severe cases. However, it is also likely that these afucosylated antibodies serve a protective role against secondary infections with SARS-CoV-2, especially since a recent study showed that afucosylated IgG in convalescent plasma therapies was the only parameter associated with protection59. In addition, afucosylation of the RSV F-specific monoclonal antibody palivizumab resulted in reduced viral titers in the lungs of cotton rats60. While mostly disappearing after the initial immunization in COVID-19, afucosylation partly reappears after immune boosting61. The level of fucosylation of total IgG1 has previously been shown to decrease during childhood and adolescence, in line with our observations for bulk IgG1. This decrease in adulthood is likely due to the multiple exposures to RSV and other enveloped viruses56. Additional research is required to further elucidate the factors that determine RSV-specific antibody fucosylation, particularly considering its apparent variability in this population. Furthermore, we observe an increased RSV-specific galactosylation compared to bulk IgG, which has previously been associated with recent or active immunization56,62.

The design of our study did not allow to investigate antibody features as correlates of protection. Fc afucosylation and ADNKA have been associated with protection in an adenovirus-vectored RSV vaccination trial in adults10, but further investigation remains necessary, especially in children. With regard to avidity, previous research has shown that RSV-infected hospitalized infants below 3 months of age had lower avidity antibodies than healthy controls6. However, when looking at disease severity based on the need for oxygen, no difference was found in avidity. In RSV-infected children between 2 and 5 years old with a lower respiratory tract infection, lower avidity antibodies were found compared to the antibodies of children with an upper respiratory tract infection, suggesting some role in protection against severe disease63. These studies did not investigate Fc-mediated effector functions, but the protective effect of increased avidity may be linked to enhanced Fc-mediated effector functions and requires additional research. Ultimately, protection against severe disease is likely determined by a complex balance between different antibody features. Whereas a (very) high antibody titer may protect against disease via neutralization alone, as for example shown by the efficacy of nirsevimab64, Fc-mediated effector functions have also been shown to correlate with protection in vivo and thus might contribute to protection10,11,60. Therefore, the development of the antibody profile towards less fucosylated IgG may be important for protection against severe disease, but additional research into correlates of protection in young infants is necessary for a more complete understanding. Furthermore, such endeavors should take into account the potential differences between local and systemic immune responses, as these might protect via different mechanisms7.

Notably, our study was focused on the RSV F-protein, which is the target of most current RSV vaccines. Nevertheless, the RSV attachment protein G is also an important protein that is abundantly present on the envelope of the RSV virion. Upon natural infection in children, higher levels of F-specific antibodies are measured compared to G-specific antibodies65. Limited research efforts have investigated the role of G-specific antibody Fc-mediated effector functions. One study showed that non-hospitalized RSV-infected infants (<2 years) exhibit higher G-specific IgG1 levels and increased G-specific ADCP, compared to hospitalized RSV-infected infants, who show elevated G-specific antibody-dependent neutrophilic phagocytosis (ADNP)66. The authors also reported differences between G- and F-specific antibodies in children, highlighting the importance of including both antigens in future research.

With this study, we provide information on the functionality and features of RSV-specific antibodies in infants and adults. In vivo, however, immune protection through effector functions relies on the complex interplay of these antibodies with the immune cells they engage with. As these immune cells themselves may also vary in functionality and features between age groups67, a complete understanding of Fc-mediated effector functions can only be obtained when both are studied in combination. Therefore, future research should ideally make use of both serum and immune cells of each participant, but the limited availability of pediatric primary immune cells forms a considerable barrier for such studies.

Conclusions

In summary, there are clear differences in RSV-specific antibody features between 24-month-old children and adults. These differences appear to be mostly explained by differential glycosylation patterns, likely related to the fact that adults have been exposed to RSV more often than 24-month-old children. For the optimal design of future vaccination strategies, it will be important to keep in mind that the RSV-specific humoral signature in children differs from that in adults. Our data suggests that especially low Fc fucosylation drives the Fc-mediated antibody responses that are implicated in protection during RSV infection10,11. Additional research is necessary to elucidate the precise role of these antibody features in protection from disease in specific risk groups.
