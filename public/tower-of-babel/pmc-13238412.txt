Using ePortfolios to Assess the Influence of Community Engagement on Undergraduates’ Socially Responsible Leadership Development
Authors: Francesca Lo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis article describes findings from a mixed‐methods study analyzing undergraduate student ePortfolios to examine how community‐engaged learning supports socially responsible leadership development. Results highlight ePortfolios as an effective tool for assessing and facilitating student learning. The author advocates for integration of leadership education with community engagement efforts to foster personal transformation and social change.

Introduction

While research has shown how community‐engaged learning plays an influential role in the development of socially responsible leadership (Astin and Sax 1998; Astin et al. 2000; Dugan 2006), the body of research remains small, and there is a need to further examine their connections and ensure students are engaging with communities in ethical and equitable ways. ePortfolios offer a promising practice to both facilitate and assess student learning, which can then be utilized to improve program design to more effectively promote deep learning, self‐awareness, and a sustained commitment to positive social change.

Using a mixed‐methods approach, this study analyzes ePortfolio reflections from a Leadership Certificate program and Minor in Leadership at a large public university. It explores which leadership competencies students develop through community‐engaged learning (CEL) experiences and how they describe their growth and approach to community engagement. By centering reflection and identity exploration, ePortfolios provide a unique lens into students’ evolving leadership development.

This paper contributes to scholarship by demonstrating the transformative potential of structured ePortfolio processes and advocating for integration of leadership education with community engagement efforts to cultivate leaders who are not only skilled but also critically conscious and ethically grounded.

Literature Review

Community Engagement and Student Leadership Development

A small but growing body of research links community engagement and students’ leadership development (Astin and Sax 1998; Astin et al. 2000; Dugan 2006). More recently, analyses from Multi‐Institutional Study of Leadership (MSL) data confirm strong relationships between community engagement and leadership development. Dugan and Komives (2010) found community service participation positively influenced the development of the eight theoretically grounded measures of socially responsible leadership, except consciousness of self and change.

In addition, Dugan et al. (2013) examination of cumulative findings from MSL research highlighted community service as a high‐impact practice for building leadership capacity across gender, race, and other demographic groups. Similarly, Soria et al. (2013) reported positive relationships between socially responsible leadership development and participation in community service in different contexts, specifically students who engaged in service on their own and with a student organization. Despite these findings, the body of scholarship remains limited and largely quantitative. More qualitative research is needed to deepen understanding and refine best practices for designing community engaged learning experiences that effectively develop students’ socially responsible leadership capacity.

Moreover, these experiences must be intentionally structured to help students consider issues of power, privilege, and systemic oppression so they engage with communities in ethical and equitable ways (Dugan et al. 2013; Messmore and Davis 2020; Wagner and Pigza 2016). Wagner and Pigza (2016) proposed a set of values grounded in critical perspectives to inform intentionally designed leadership and CEL experiences, including: awareness of context, reciprocal participation, critical examination of power and privilege, reflective practice, sustained engagement, and commitment to change and justice. Messmore and Davis (2020) described strategies that center social justice, critical race theory, and trauma informed practices to mitigate harm towards communities and marginalized students.

Further qualitative research that examines how students enact these principles can enhance program design. Given this need for qualitative approaches to explore links between community engagement and leadership development, ePortfolios offer a particularly promising tool for both facilitating and assessing such learning.

ePortfolios to Facilitate and Assess Student Learning

ePortfolios are an increasingly utilized higher education high impact practice to foster deep learning, reflection, and ownership of the learning process (Amaya et al. 2013; Hornor 2021; Rhodes 2018; Watson et al. 2016). A growing body of research highlights how ePortfolios enhance student learning through documenting experiences, promoting reflective writing, facilitating integrative learning to create new knowledge, honing professional identity and self‐efficacy, and providing opportunities for lifelong learning and career development (Richards‐Schuster et al. 2014; Peet 2015; Schiele et al. 2017; Weber et al. 2023). In particular, studies have found the value of using ePortfolios in tandem with other high‐impact practices, such as internships and community‐based learning, as students are able to make connections between various educational experiences (Association of American Colleges and Universities 2022).

Beyond supporting learning, ePortfolios provide higher education institutions with an innovative way to assess student development and program impacts. ePortfolios can examine measures that are challenging to assess using traditional quantitative tools, such as socially responsible leadership, because they allow for nuanced and in‐depth articulation of experiences and learning (Richards‐Schuster et al. 2014; Weber et al. 2023). While ePortfolio assessment of student learning is increasing, more research is needed on how ePortfolio data can improve program design (Crowel and Calamidas 2016; Weber et al. 2023).

Studies using ePortfolios to assess student learning from community engagement experiences have shown promising results. O'Laughlin and Serra (2016) found ePortfolios enhance student reflection on social justice issues, community strengths, and partnership building, while Richards‐Schuster et al. (2014) demonstrated their value in assessing civic engagement learning outcomes and program impact of a social justice minor. In a case study analysis of an institution‐wide e‐Leadership Portfolio, Hornor (2021) used AAC & U VALUE rubrics to benchmark student learning across high‐impact practices, including service‐learning, while Weber et al. (2023) highlighted how ePortfolios helped students in a summer community‐based internship program articulate growth and future goals while informing design improvements. These findings underscore the potential of ePortfolios to illuminate how community engagement shapes socially responsible leadership development.

Conceptual Framework

The social change model of leadership development defines socially responsible leadership as “a purposeful, collaborative, values‐based process that results in positive social change” (Dugan and Komives 2010, p. 526). The capacity to engage in socially responsible leadership is developed across seven values that operate at the individual, group, and societal level resulting in change for the common good.

The student leadership competencies framework (Seemiller 2013) was derived from analyzing standards, models, leadership theories, and learning outcomes of accredited academic programs in higher education. They encompass the knowledge, values, abilities, and behaviors that help an individual contribute to or successfully engage in a role or task. The 60 competencies are arranged in eight categories that include: learning and reasoning, self‐awareness and development, interpersonal interaction, group dynamics, civic responsibility, communication, strategic planning, and personal behavior.

Since the ePortfolio data in this study utilize the student leadership competencies as a framework for students to reflect on their leadership development, this study aligns the student leadership competencies framework to the social change model of leadership to better understand students’ socially responsible leadership development. Method

The ePortfolio data used in this study provides an opportunity for both quantitative and qualitative assessment as students identified specific Student leadership competencies they developed and provided in‐depth written reflections on their learning. Using a mixed‐methods, explanatory sequential design (Creswell and Plano Clark 2018), this study first utilized a quantitative frequency analysis of student leadership competencies to identify commonly expressed learning outcomes. This was followed by a phenomenological inquiry (Patton 2015), including analysis of students’ ePortfolio content, to provide a deeper understanding of how students describe their development of leadership competencies and approach to community engagement.

Context

Recognizing reflection as an essential yet often overlooked practice in leadership education, a cocurricular Leadership Certificate program was launched in 2013 at a large public research university in the Pacific Northwest. The program supports students’ reflection on their leadership development and learning through mentoring, a structured 2‐credit course, and the creation of a leadership ePortfolio. Adapted from the Integrative Knowledge Portfolio Process (Peet, M.R. 2010), the course guides students to connect their leadership development to key undergraduate learning experiences using the student leadership competencies framework (Seemiller 2013). ePortfolio components included: Personal introduction: core values, leadership identities, motivations, and future goals.Leadership philosophy: evolving understanding of leadership.Key learning reflections: at least three curricular and co‐curricular experiences, addressing 21 competencies across eight categories, with prompts on demonstration, learning, application, and future integration. Each experience includes an artifact as evidence.

Data Collection

The target data source for this IRB‐approved study was the ePortfolios completed by 162 Leadership Certificate students and 35 Minor in Leadership students during the 2023–24 and 2024–25 academic years. Sixty‐four students granted permission for review. A screening process identified 54 portfolios with CEL experiences that met three criteria: fits the institution's definition of community engagement: the collaboration between the University and our extended communities, including Tribal Nations and Indigenous communities for the equitable and mutually beneficial co‐creation and exchange of knowledge, insights, creativity, resources, and capacities to address critical social issues and/or envision and contribute to the future well‐being of our communities and planet;CEL experience takes place off‐campus; andif a student receives pay for the experience, it is framed as an explicit learning experience like an internship (versus a job that does not emphasize student learning goals).

Of the 54 students, 50 are Leadership Certificate students, and six are Minor in Leadership students (with 2 duplicates). 45 identified as female, six identified as male, two identified as transgender, and one identified as non‐binary. Thirty‐six percent identified as Asian/Asian American, nine percent identified as Black/African/African American, 11% identified as Latino, eight percent identified as Middle Eastern, six percent identified as Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander, and 30% identified as White. Thirty‐eight percent were first‐generation college students, 24% were Equal Opportunity Program students, 20% identified as LGBTQIA+, 12% were transfer students, and two percent were international students. Forty‐eight percent were majoring in arts and sciences, seven percent in built environments, 12% in business, six percent in education, 34% in computing and engineering, one percent in environment, six percent in informatics, three percent in nursing, 12% in public health, and one percent in social work.

The 54 ePortfolios included reflections on 247 total key learning experiences, 92 of which fit the definitional criteria of a CEL experience. These CEL experiences came in a variety of contexts including courses, capstones, student organizations, internships, research, and study abroad opportunities. The remaining 155 experiences were considered non‐CEL experiences for the purposes of this study.

Quantitative Data Analysis

To determine which leadership competencies students developed through CEL experiences, a frequency analysis was conducted on the 1329 total Student leadership competencies identified in students’ ePortfolio reflections across 247 total key learning experiences. Frequencies were calculated for all 60 individual competencies as well as for the eight overarching Student Leadership Competency categories. Percentages of the total number of competencies were also computed within CEL (92 experiences) and non‐CEL experiences (155 experiences) to enable proportional comparison across the two groups.

To examine connections to socially responsible leadership, competencies that correspond to the values of the social change model of leadership (SCM) were grouped and compared. These SCM‐aligned competencies were then rank‐ordered within the full set of 60 competencies for both CEL and non‐CEL experiences to assess differences in their relative prominence. All competency counts and calculations were performed manually using a spreadsheet and reviewed multiple times to ensure accuracy.

Qualitative Data Analysis

To explore how students described their development of leadership competencies and approach to community engagement, a phenomenological analysis of ePortfolio content (Patton 2015) was used since ePortfolio data capture students’ personal descriptions of their lived experience, and a primary goal of this study is to identify the shared essence of CEL experiences across individual students. Reflections from 92 CEL experiences and leadership philosophy statements were coded and organized into themes using the constant comparative method (Patton 2015).

To strengthen the trustworthiness of the analysis, an audit trail was maintained to document coding decisions and theme development, codes and themes were reviewed multiple times to ensure consistency, and representative excerpts were used to ground interpretations in students’ own words. This approach identified recurring patterns and relationships to explain students’ development of leadership competencies and approach to community engagement.

Limitations

Of the 197 students who completed the program, 54 ePortfolios (approximately 27%) were included in this study. Because students were contacted during the summer after graduation, the response rate may have been reduced, and those who granted permission may represent a more engaged or responsive subset of the overall pool. As with all qualitative analyses of student artifacts, the depth and quality of ePortfolio reflections varied and were influenced by the structure of the assignment prompts, which may have guided students toward certain competencies or values. Finally, the study draws on a single data source and relies on researcher interpretation, which limits the extent to which the findings can be generalized.

A chi‐squared (χ 2) test of independence was conducted to examine whether civic responsibility competencies (diversity, others’ circumstances, inclusion, social justice, social responsibility, and service), are identified more frequently in CEL experiences compared to non‐CEL experiences. The observed data showed 73 CEL experiences and 51 non‐CEL experiences, with expected counts under the null hypothesis of no association being 51.7 and 72.3, respectively. The calculated chi‐squared statistic is 16.6 with 1 degree of freedom, corresponding to a p‐value far below 0.01, demonstrating a statistically significant association between CEL experiences and identifying civic responsibility competencies. ePortfolio Content Qualitative Analysis

The recurring themes that emerged from an in‐depth analysis of students’ ePortfolio content provided insight into how students’ CEL experiences influenced their leadership development and how students approached community engagement. The recurring themes included: deepening consciousness of self, developing capacity for facilitating change, and ethical community engagement. The themes inform best practices for designing community engagement experiences that foster deep learning, self‐awareness, and a sustained commitment to positive social change.

Theme 1: Deepening Consciousness of Self

Student reflections demonstrated awareness of their beliefs, values, attitudes, and emotions that motivate them to take action. For some students, CEL experiences helped them discover their personal values. For example, a Human Centered Design and Engineering student reflected on an inclusive design internship experience: I had previously been interested in accessible and inclusive practices, but I don't think that it really became a personal value until I got to do it myself. Accessibility and inclusivity are now two things that I feel are core to who I am as a designer.

While CEL experiences helped some students discover personal values, for other students, these experiences helped reinforce and deepen their values. For example, one Political Science student who participated in several community‐based research projects reflected, My experiences…have strengthened my commitment to my core values of valuable collaboration within communities, compassion for equitable solutions, and the courage to advocate for underrepresented populations through critical data and policy analysis. These principles remain central to both my personal values and professional pursuits within the federal and international spaces of humanitarian and legal services, that I hope to someday be a part of.

Theme 2: Deepening Capacity for Creating Change

Student reflections demonstrate that community engagement experiences support students in the learning process of creating change both with developing practical strategies to enact change along with developing their sense of self‐efficacy to create change. Students highlighted a range of strategies to facilitate change including: Individual impact: “My experience volunteering with [overdose prevention program] taught me the importance of creating change locally in your own community. The best way we could improve harm reduction efforts and education was by training individuals in the community and giving them the skills, resources, and confidence to continue the mission…By volunteering with [program], I am helping to create change one individual at a time.” Addressing systemic barriers: “My role allowed me to directly contribute to breaking down the barriers that prevent many first‐generation, low‐income students from accessing higher education. By providing them with guidance and exposure to university life, I felt I was part of something bigger than myself—an effort to create systemic change within the educational system.” Coalition‐building: “Through collaboration with [program] and other grassroots groups, I have gained a deeper understanding of coalition‐building and the power of sustained, community‐driven advocacy in the fight for migrant justice.” Research: “I have also found a new value in being a researcher, which impacts how I engage in social change. While I knew the importance of evidence‐based practice, I've since learned first‐hand the cyclical relationship between research and practice, and how they are inextricably linked. I am no longer just a disruptor doing only direct‐action work…I am also a weaver and builder, doing lasting work, identifying connectivity between group and concepts, developing ideas for practice, and supporting collective movements and visions.” Storytelling: “The knowledge I gained through the course empowered me to act and raise awareness within our community of the lived experiences of detained immigrants. By amplifying the stories and voices of those affected, I recognized the power we hold to initiate change, starting with educating ourselves and others.” Art: “I learned from an amazing group of artists about theatre as a medium for social change. Art can be more than a hobby. It is an expression to the world that unites people—something that I will always work towards.” Advocacy and direct service: “Through my work, I had the chance to witness how small, everyday actions can create lasting change. Whether it was helping someone do their laundry or simply offering a smiling face, I saw how small efforts can snowball into more significant shifts in someone's life… I also recognized the broader societal need for systemic change to address issues like homelessness, and I developed a more nuanced understanding of how advocacy and direct service work together to create a more just world.”

Theme 3: Ethical Community Engagement

Student reflections on CEL experiences along with their reflection on the evolution of their leadership philosophy demonstrate aspects of critical approaches to community engagement as proposed by Wagner and Pigza's (2016) set of values. Awareness of context: A student who worked in a hunger relief agency reflected on their increased awareness of both self and societal context: “Volunteering at [a community market] deepened my awareness of the structural and societal challenges tied to food insecurity. What I initially saw as a logistical issue quickly revealed itself to be part of a much larger picture involving poverty, healthcare access, education, and systemic inequality. It also pushed me to educate myself further and reflect on the privilege I carry…This shift toward social awareness wasn't just an external change—it was an internal one, reminding me that leadership starts with understanding the world as it really is.” Reciprocal participation: Student often reflected on their learning from the communities they worked with. For example, an Environmental Studies student who participated in an international ecological restoration project reflected on his learning about shared power: “Additionally, working alongside Indigenous Ecuadorians provided valuable insights into traditional ecological knowledge. The collaborative nature of our work underscored the importance of shared responsibility in environmental restoration…Beyond the technical knowledge, the experience also deepened my appreciation for community‐driven initiatives, as I witnessed how local knowledge and collaboration are integral to effective ecological restoration.” Critical examination of power and privilege: Student reflections captured explorations of both their marginalized and privileged identities and how these influence their values and commitment to positive change. For example, a Physiology student reflected on how her personal identities have shaped her leadership goals: “As a Zimbabwean, I have witnessed firsthand what the lack of adequate healthcare looks like in underserved populations, and as a Black woman, I have seen how marginalized communities, particularly Black individuals, are often overlooked and underserved in healthcare systems. These experiences have deeply shaped my aspirations and commitment to addressing these inequities…My goal is to bridge the gap in healthcare access for marginalized populations, ensuring that everyone, regardless of their background, has the opportunity to receive quality care.”

Discussion and Implications

Results from this study supported existing research that demonstrates the positive, influential role of CEL on students’ socially responsible leadership development. Analyzing the frequency of cited leadership competencies in students’ ePortfolios offers a new research approach to assess the influence of community engagement on students’ leadership development. Results showed a statistically significant association between CEL experiences and identifying civic responsibility competencies, suggesting the power of CEL in developing students’ sense of social responsibility.

Deepening Consciousness of Self

While some MSL survey data analyses do not show a positive, influential role on the consciousness of self and change measures of socially responsible leadership (Dugan and Komives 2010), this study suggests CEL experiences strengthen self‐awareness of beliefs, values, attitudes, and motivations as seen in the higher frequency of associated leadership competencies—personal values, personal contributions, and self‐understanding—in CEL experiences. Dugan and Komives (2010) noted how typical service experiences lack quality and depth necessary to influence students’ sense of self.

However, this study reveals how structured ePortfolio processes support students’ evolving sense of self and facilitate reflection on CEL experiences to discover, reinforce, and deepen their values and commitment to their communities. Building on prior research (O'Laughline and Serra 2016; Richards‐Schuster et al. 2014; Hornor 2021; Weber et al. 2023), this study suggests that reflective scaffolding through ePortfolios may activate dimensions of SCM (consciousness of self, change) that survey‐based studies sometimes fail to detect.

Capacity for Creating Change

In addition, this study suggested CEL experiences support students in the learning process of creating change, the ultimate goal of the creative process of leadership—to make a better world and a better society for self and others (HERI, 1996). This is evident as the creating change competency landed in the top five most frequently cited competencies for CEL experiences and in the bottom five most frequently cited competencies for non‐CEL experiences. Moreover, student reflections illustrated their increasing capacity to facilitate change whether it be through individual impact, addressing systemic barriers, coalition‐building, research, storytelling, art, or advocacy and direct service.

Furthermore, Dugan et al. (2013) highlighted leadership self‐efficacy as a key predictor of gains in leadership capacity but community service was not found to be one of the significant contributors for self‐efficacy. While self‐efficacy is not one of the explicit student leadership competencies (Seemiller, 2014), students’ ePortfolio reflections highlighting their increasing capacity to facilitate change demonstrate their internal belief in the likelihood that they will be successful when engaging in leadership.

Ethical Community Engagement

A critical aspect of socially responsible leadership is engaging with communities in ethical and equitable ways. This study illustrates how students ethically and equitably engaged with communities as they reflected on their CEL experiences. Reflections demonstrated how students are embodying values grounded in critical perspectives (Wagner and Pigza 2016) as they deepen their consciousness of self and learn to work in tandem with communities towards social change.

Students also articulated how their CEL experiences deepened their understanding of systemic and structural barriers that drive societal inequities. While these reflections on power, privilege, and systemic oppression were extensive throughout the ePortfolios in this study, it is interesting to note that the leadership competency of systems thinking was in the bottom five of cited competencies for CEL experiences. Students who reflected on the systems thinking competency often drew connections with their coursework where they were able to witness how complex social systems play out in a real‐world setting.

In addition, it is important to acknowledge that development was not uniform across students or competencies. Some students engaged deeply with issues of identity, power, and justice, while others engaged at a more surface level, suggesting variation in how CEL fosters critical reflection. Similarly, reflections across ePortfolios revealed variation in critical community engagement. Some narratives emphasized service or personal growth without examining positionality, reciprocity, or the systemic conditions shaping community issues, while others primarily described activities rather than analyzing how identity or power influenced their engagement. These counterexamples illustrate that CEL is not uniformly transformative; reflective depth depended on design features such as prompt specificity, mentorship, duration of engagement, and opportunities for partner‐informed feedback.

Integration of Community Engagement and Leadership Education

In their study exploring the relationship between students’ socially responsible leadership and community service engagement in different contexts, Soria et al. (2013) recommend institutions look at community service as one vital aspect of leadership development work. Findings from this study not only reaffirm the influential role of community engagement on students’ socially responsible leadership development, but it also makes a case for institutions to further integrate their CEL efforts with their student leadership development efforts. This integration of efforts align and center the values of ethical service and socially responsible leadership to signal that the kind of leadership an institution aims to cultivate is grounded in values associated with creating positive social change in and with communities.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the research base that links community engagement and socially responsible leadership development by bringing in a mixed methods approach utilizing student reflections in a structured ePortfolio program. Study results highlight the influential role of CEL, paired with reflective practice, in shaping students’ consciousness of self, capacity for creating change, and ethical engagement with communities. As institutions seek to prepare students for leadership in a complex and inequitable world, integrating CEL with leadership development efforts is a powerful catalyst for personal transformation and social change.
