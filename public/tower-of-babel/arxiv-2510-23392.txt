Sturm-Liouville problems on graphs with Robin boundary conditions
Yuri Latushkin, Vyacheslav Pivovarchik, Alesia Supranovych

Abstract
. We study characteristic functions and describe asymptotics of the eigenvalues for the spectral Sturm-Liouville problem on graphs equipped with Robin-Kirhhoff boundary conditions. Also, we show how to recover the coefficients in the Robin conditions for the quantum graphs provided the shape of the graphs and some Robin eigenvalues are known.

Primary 34B24; Secondary 47E05

1.
Introduction.

A differential operator is a synthesis of three fairly independent objects:
(1) the set of support of functions on which the differential operator is acting (say, a finite interval, a graph of certain shape, a multidimensional domain, or a manifold), (2) the differential operation itself (determined, say, by the potential in the Sturm-Liouville problems), and (3) the domain of the operator acting in a suitably chosen function space (which is usually determined by boundary conditions). In this paper we will deal with Sturm-Lioville problems on graphs, and in this context, respectively, one anticipates three fairly independent
inverse problems
to be considered: Given the spectrum (or any specific spectral data) of the Sturm-Lioville operator, find: (1) the shape of the graph, (2) the potential, (3) the parameters in the boundary conditions. In recent years problems (1) and (2) have been addressed in a vast amount of sources and attracted attention of many. However, problem (3) to the best of our knowledge was considered only by a very few authors, and it is the main objective of this paper to attract attention to this topic.

We begin with a brief account of the history of Sturm-Liouville inverse problems. For a
finite interval
it starts with the celebrated Ambarzumians Theorem
[
2
]
, continues at Borg’s paper
[
8
]
, and culminates in the classical work on the solution of the inverse problem given by Levitan and Gasymov
[
27
]
and Marchenko
[
28
]
.
The inverse Sturm-Liuville problems on
metric graphs
were first considered by Gerasimenko
[
20
]
and Exner and Seba
[
18
]
who constructed the potential of the Sturm-Liouville equation using given spectral data. This marks the beginning of research on problem (2) from the above mentioned list of inverse problems on graphs. The analysis of the inverse problems of type (2) was further continued in
[
1
,
11
,
14
,
17
,
21
,
32
,
33
,
34
,
36
,
37

.
The work on the inverse problems of type (1) began by Gutkin and Smilansky in
[
22
]
and by von Below in
[
5
]
.
Gutkin and Smilansky showed that if the lengths of the edges are non-commensurate then the spectrum of the quantum graph (that is, the second order derivative operator on the graph with standard boundary conditions) uniquely determines the shape of the graph. Von Below considered the opposite case and showed that there exist co-spectral graphs, i.e., non-isomorphic graphs with the same spectrum of a Sturm-Liouville problem.
Many interesting results on classes of cospectral graphs can be found in
[
4
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
19
]
. In
[
25
]
(see also
[
7
]
) a “geometric” Ambarzumian Theorem was proved stating that the unperturbed spectrum uniquely determines the shape of a
P_{2}
graph. In
[
15
]
this result was generalized to the case of simple connected equilateral graphs with the number of vertices not exceeding five and to the case of trees with the number of vertices not exceeding eight. An example of co-spectral trees with nine vertices was given in
[
31
]
.

The inverse problems of type (3) on graphs appear to be significantly less popular. In general, the problem could be formulated as follows: Suppose that the shape of a graph is known, that the potentials on the edges are known (in particular, they may be known to be identically zero), and we are given spectral data and need to find unknown constants in the boundary and matching conditions. We are aware of only very few papers on this topic:
[

]
and
[

In the current paper we consider a general Robin problem on compact graphs with

vertices obtained by replacing the standard Kirchhoff conditions
\sum_{j}y^{\prime}_{j}(\ell)=\sum_{k}y_{k}^{\prime}(0)
at a vertex

For trees, we study asymptotics of the eigenvalues of the respective Sturm-Liouville problems and show how to recover the constants

The Robin problems and their characteristic functions

Let
G

We consider the Sturm-Liouville equations on the edges,

-y_{j}^{\prime\prime}+q_{j}(x)y_{j}=\lambda y_{j},\,x\in e_{j}=[0,\ell],\,j=1,2,\ldots,g,

(2.1)

where

Given real numbers

Robin spectral problem
on the graph

v_{i}

If

for each edge

e_{k}

y_{j}^{\prime}(\ell)+b_{i}y_{j}(\ell)=0\,\text{ (respectively, $-y_{k}^{\prime}(0)+b_{i}y_{k}(0)=0$)}.

(2.2)

At each interior vertex
v_{i}

y_{j_{1}}(\ell)=\dots=y_{j_{d_{\textrm{in}}}}(\ell)=y_{k_{1}}(0)=\dots=y_{k_{d_{\textrm{out}}}}(0)

(2.3)

for the incoming to
v_{i}

(2.4)

Given the Robin problem (
2.10
)–(

) at all vertices

For any given

In order to define the characteristic functions of the Robin problem and of the Dirichlet-standard auxiliary problems

Substituting this into the vertex conditions at each vertex,

We denote by

.

We call

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
G
be an equilateral compact connected simple graph. Then

\begin{split}&\phi(\lambda,b_{1},b_{2},...,b_{p})=\phi(\lambda,0,0,...,0)+\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{p}b_{i}\phi_{i}(\lambda)\\
&\quad+\sum\nolimits_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}\leq p}b_{i_{1}}b_{i_{2}}\phi_{i_{1}i_{2}}(\lambda)+\ldots+\big(\prod\nolimits_{i=1}^{p}b_{i}\big)\phi_{12...p}(\lambda).\end{split}

(2.6)

Proof.

The matrix
\Phi(\lambda,b_{1},0,0,...,0)
differs from
\Phi(\lambda,0,0,0,...,0)
in the row corresponding to the Kirchhoff condition at the vertex
v_{1}
.
Indeed, let us denote by
e_{j_{1}},\ldots,e_{j_{d_{\textrm{in}}(v_{1})}}
the incoming to and by
e_{k_{1}},\ldots,e_{k_{d_{\textrm{out}}(v_{1})}}
the outgoing from the vertex
v_{1}
edges. For definiteness, we assume that each of
d_{\textrm{in}}(v_{1})
and
d_{\textrm{out}}(v_{1})
is at least one.
The Kirchhoff condition at
v_{1}
for the Robin problem then gives

\begin{split}&\sum\nolimits_{m=1}^{d_{\textrm{in}}(v_{1})}\big(\alpha_{j_{m}}s^{\prime}_{j_{m}}(\lambda,\ell)+\beta_{j_{m}}c^{\prime}_{j_{m}}(\lambda,\ell)\big)\\
&\quad+b_{1}\big(\alpha_{j_{1}}s_{j_{1}}(\lambda,\ell)+\beta_{j_{1}}c_{j_{1}}(\lambda,\ell)\big)-\sum\nolimits_{n=1}^{d_{\textrm{out}}(v_{1})}\alpha_{k_{n}}=0.\end{split}

On the other hand, setting
r=1
,
i_{1}=1
, the Dirichlet condition at
v_{1}
for the Dirichlet-standard auxiliary
\{1\}
-problem gives
\alpha_{j_{1}}s_{j_{1}}(\lambda,\ell)+\beta_{j_{1}}c_{j_{1}}(\lambda,\ell)=0
(recall that for the latter problem the Dirichlet condition is imposed for all edges incident to
v_{1}
and thus the continuity conditions at
v_{1}
automatically holds).
Then the row of the matrix
\Phi(\lambda,b_{1},0,\ldots,0)
corresponding to the Kirchhoff condition at
v_{1}
is of the form

\begin{split}&\big\{\ldots 0,s^{\prime}_{j_{1}}(\lambda,\ell)+b_{1}s_{j_{1}}(\lambda,\ell),c^{\prime}_{j_{1}}(\lambda,\ell)+b_{1}c_{j_{1}}(\lambda,\ell),0\ldots 0,\\
&\qquad\qquad s^{\prime}_{j_{2}}(\lambda,\ell),c^{\prime}_{j_{2}}(\lambda,\ell),0\ldots 0,s^{\prime}_{j_{d_{\textrm{in}}(v_{1})}}(\lambda,\ell),c^{\prime}_{d(v_{1})}(\lambda,\ell),\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad 0\ldots 0,-1,0,\ldots,-1,0,\ldots 0\ldots 0\big\}\\
&=\big\{\ldots 0,s^{\prime}_{j_{1}}(\lambda,\ell),c^{\prime}_{j_{1}}(\lambda,\ell),0\ldots 0,\\
&\qquad\qquad s^{\prime}_{j_{2}}(\lambda,\ell),c^{\prime}_{j_{2}}(\lambda,\ell),0\ldots 0,s^{\prime}_{j_{d_{\textrm{in}}(v_{1})}}(\lambda,\ell),c^{\prime}_{d(v_{1})}(\lambda,\ell),\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad 0\ldots 0,-1,0,\ldots,-1,0,\ldots 0\ldots 0\big\}\\
&+b_{1}\big\{\ldots 0,s_{j_{1}}(\lambda,\ell),c_{j_{1}}(\lambda,\ell),0,0,...,0\big\},\end{split}

where the curly brackets in the last line give the row of the matrix
\Phi_{1}(\lambda)
for the Dirichlet-standard auxiliary
\{1\}
-problem that corresponds to the Dirichlet condition at
v_{1}
for the edge
e_{j_{1}}
.
Thus,

\begin{split}\det\big(\Phi(\lambda,b_{1},0,0...,0)\big)&=\det\big(\Phi(\lambda,0,0,...,0)\big)+b_{1}\det\big(\Phi_{1}(\lambda)\big),\text{
that is,}\\
\phi(\lambda,b_{1},0,...,0)&=\phi(\lambda,0,0,...,0)+b_{1}\phi_{1}(\lambda).\end{split}

Repeating this procedure inductively we finally arrive at (
2.6

\Phi(\lambda,b_{1},0,\ldots,0)
and
\Phi_{1}(\lambda)
can be written as
\Phi^{(v_{1})}(\lambda,b_{1},0,\ldots,0)=\Phi^{(v_{1})}(\lambda,0,\ldots,0)+b_{1}\Phi^{(v_{1})}_{1}(\lambda)
where
\Phi^{(v_{1})}
is the row of the matrix
\Phi
corresponding to the vertex
v_{1}
. A similar argument yileds

\Phi^{(v_{2})}(\lambda,b_{1},b_{2},0,\ldots,0)=\Phi^{(v_{2})}(\lambda,b_{1},0,\ldots,0)+b_{2}\Phi^{(v_{2})}_{2}(\lambda,b_{1})
making the induction possible.
∎

In this section,

Theorem 3.1
.

Let

be an equilateral compact tree and assume that the potential is identically zero.
The spectrum

of the Robin boundary value problem (

)–(
2.4

with the following asymptotics,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\lambda_{k}^{(1)}}

\displaystyle=(\pi k)/\ell+o(1),\text{ as $k\to+\infty$},

(3.2)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\lambda_{k}^{(\pm l)}}

\displaystyle=\big(\pm\arccos z_{l}+2\pi k\big)/\ell+o(1)\text{ for $l=2,...,p-1$}.

(3.3)

Proof.

It is convenient to view

as a new complex variable and introduce the functions

\begin{split}f(\sqrt{\lambda})&=-\sqrt{\lambda}\sin(\sqrt{\lambda}\ell)\widetilde{\psi}\big(\cos(\sqrt{\lambda}\ell)\big)\,\text{ and }\\

&\hskip 85.35826pt+b_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot b_{p}\phi_{12...p}(\lambda)\end{split}

(3.4)

so that

and

, cf. (

). In particular, the eigenvalues
\lambda

\sqrt{\lambda}
’s of

. The functions

and

are even entire functions. The set of zeros of the function

is the union of the following

\begin{split}&\big\{\pm\pi k/\ell\big\}_{k=0}^{\infty},\,\big\{\pm(\arccos z_{l}+2\pi k)/\ell\big\}_{k=0}^{\infty},\\
&\big\{\pm(-\arccos z_{l}+2\pi k)/\ell\big\}_{k=1}^{\infty},\,l=2,\ldots,p-1,\end{split}

, and

To establish the asymptotic relations (
3.2
)–(
3.3

centered at the

\sqrt{\lambda}\mapsto f(\sqrt{\lambda})+g(\sqrt{\lambda})=\phi(\lambda,b_{1},\ldots,b_{p})

To begin, we consider the first sequence in (
3.5

+

\pi k/\ell

\begin{split}|\phi_{i}(\lambda)|&=|\psi_{i}(\cos(\sqrt{\lambda}\ell))|=|\psi_{i}((-1)^{k}+o(r))|\leq c,\\

To continue, we consider the second sequence in (

We will need the notion of

Definition 4.1
.

An entire function
\omega:\sqrt{\lambda}\mapsto\omega(\sqrt{\lambda})
of exponential type
\sigma>0
is said to be of
sine type
provided the following holds:
(1) all zeros of
\omega
lie in a horizontal strip
|\operatorname{Im}\sqrt{\lambda}|<h
,
(2) for a certain fixed value
\operatorname{Im}\sqrt{\lambda}=h_{1}
and positive constants
M_{1},M_{2}
one has

M_{1}\leq|\omega(\operatorname{Re}\sqrt{\lambda}+ih_{1})|\leq M_{2}
for all
\operatorname{Re}\sqrt{\lambda}\in(-\infty,\infty)
,
and (3) the types of
F
in the upper and in the lower half-planes are equal.

A product of sine type functions is again a sine type function.
The functions
\sqrt{\lambda}\mapsto\sin\sqrt{\lambda}\ell
,
\cos\sqrt{\lambda}\ell
and (nonconstant) polynomials of
\cos\sqrt{\lambda}\ell
with real coefficients, see Lemma
4.3
below, are of sine type while the functions
\sqrt{\lambda}\mapsto\sqrt{\lambda}\sin\sqrt{\lambda}\ell
and
\sin(\sqrt{\lambda}\ell)/\sqrt{\lambda}
are not of sine type due to the factors
\sqrt{\lambda}
and
1/\sqrt{\lambda}
; the function
\sqrt{\lambda}\mapsto\textrm{const}
is not a sine type either.
Any sine-type function can be presented in the form

\omega(\sqrt{\lambda})=c\lim\nolimits_{n\to+\infty}\prod\nolimits_{k=-n}^{n}\big(1-{\sqrt{\lambda}}/{\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}}\big)

(4.3)

where
c\neq 0
is a constant and
\sqrt{\lambda}_{k}
are zeros of
\omega
; here, if
\sqrt{\lambda}_{k}=0
then the corresponding factor
\big(1-\sqrt{\lambda}/{\sqrt{\lambda}_{k}}\big)
should be replaced by
\sqrt{\lambda}
.
We recall the following equivalent reformulation of Definition
4.1
that goes back to
[
26
]
, see also
[
29
, Proposition 11.2.19]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

An entire function
\omega:\sqrt{\lambda}\mapsto\omega(\sqrt{\lambda})
of exponential type
\sigma>0
is of sine type if and only if there exist positive constants
m
,
M
and
h
such that

m\leq|\omega(\sqrt{\lambda})|e^{-\sigma|\operatorname{Im}\sqrt{\lambda}|}\leq M

for all
\sqrt{\lambda}
satisfying
|\operatorname{Im}\sqrt{\lambda}|>h
.

We stress that in the next lemma the polynomial
P_{n}
is not constant.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
P_{n}(z)=a_{n}z^{n}+a_{n-1}z^{n-1}+...+a_{0}
be a polynomial of degree
n\geq 1
with real coefficients. Then
\sqrt{\lambda}\mapsto P_{n}(\cos\sqrt{\lambda}\ell)
is a sine type function.

Proof.

Using Lemma
4.2
it is easy to see that the function
\sqrt{\lambda}\mapsto\cos\sqrt{\lambda}\ell
is
a function of exponential type
\ell
and is a sine type function;
the same is true for
\sqrt{\lambda}\mapsto(\cos\sqrt{\lambda}\ell-c)
where
c
is a constant. The product

P_{n}(\cos\sqrt{\lambda}\ell)=a_{n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(\cos\sqrt{\lambda}\ell-z_{i})
, where
z_{i}
are the zeros of
P_{n}
, is therefore a function of exponential type
n\ell
and is a sine type function.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
be entire functions of
\sqrt{\lambda}
of equal exponential types
\sigma>0
. Also, we assume that
\omega_{1}

are of sine type. Furthermore, let us assume that

\omega_{2}

\omega_{2}(0)=\omega^{\prime}(0)=\ldots=\omega^{(n-1)}(0)=0
for some

\omega:\sqrt{\lambda}\mapsto\omega_{1}(\sqrt{\lambda})+(\sqrt{\lambda})^{-n}\omega_{2}(\sqrt{\lambda})

Then the function

Proof.

Using Lemma
4.2
we find positive constants

Choose

\begin{split}\big|\omega_{1}(\sqrt{\lambda})&+(\sqrt{\lambda})^{-n}\omega_{2}(\sqrt{\lambda})\big|e^{-\sigma|\operatorname{Im}\sqrt{\lambda}|}\leq M_{1}+|\sqrt{\lambda}|^{-n}M_{2}\leq M_{1}+M_{2}/h^{n},\\
\big|\omega_{1}(\sqrt{\lambda})&+(\sqrt{\lambda})^{-n}\omega_{2}(\sqrt{\lambda})\big|e^{-\sigma|\operatorname{Im}\sqrt{\lambda}|}\geq m_{1}-|\sqrt{\lambda}|^{-n}M_{2}\geq m_{1}-M_{2}/h^{n},\end{split}

as required in Lemma
4.2
to check that
\omega
is of sine type.
∎
