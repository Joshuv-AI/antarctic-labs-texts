Integrative Transcriptomic and Machine Learning Analysis Identifies JAG1 and NR2F2 in Keloid
Authors: Lingyi Yang, Wenshen Mo, Wendi Cao, Lin Mi, Xiaowei Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo identify key genes associated with keloid through integrative analysis of multiple transcriptomic datasets, and to explore their potential regulatory roles in disease development with experimental validation.MethodsTranscriptomic data of keloid were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Batch effects were corrected using SVA and ComBat. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the limma package. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed to detect key modules, and intersected with DEGs to obtain candidate genes. Multiple machine learning algorithms were applied to identify key feature genes, which were further validated in independent external datasets. In parallel, wound tissues from a silicone-splinted murine excisional wound model (which recapitulates mechanical tension of human skin injury) and matched normal skin tissues were collected for histological assessment using haematoxylin–eosin (H&E) and Masson staining, and gene expression levels were validated by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT–PCR).ResultsA total of 82 DEGs were identified. WGCNA revealed the MEpink module as the key module, and intersection analysis yielded 61 candidate genes, which were primarily enriched in inflammation- and fibrosis-related pathways. Protein–protein interaction network analysis indicated that JAG1 and NR2F2 occupied central positions within the network. Machine learning identified six key feature genes, among which NR2F2 and IL15 showed relatively strong discriminatory performance. Histological analysis demonstrated increased collagen deposition and disorganized tissue architecture in keloid samples. qRT–PCR confirmed that JAG1 and NR2F2 were upregulated in keloid tissues, consistent with transcriptomic findings.ConclusionJAG1 and NR2F2 participate in inflammation- and fibrosis-related signaling cascades to drive keloid pathogenesis, and possess promising value for the molecular subtyping of keloid lesions. Collectively, our results lay a solid foundation for future mechanistic research on the molecular regulatory networks governing keloid development.

Introduction

Keloid is a benign fibroproliferative skin disorder resulting from aberrant wound healing, characterized by persistent fibroblast activation, excessive collagen deposition, and growth beyond the boundaries of the original injury.1–3 Unlike normal scars or hypertrophic scars, keloids exhibit invasive growth, rarely regress spontaneously, and are associated with a high rate of postoperative recurrence. These features often lead to considerable cosmetic and functional impairment and complicate clinical management.4–6 At present, first-line clinical regimens mainly include intralesional glucocorticoid injection, surgical excision, radiotherapy, laser intervention, and multimodal combination therapy; nevertheless, high recurrence rates remain common for monotherapy. Park et al7 conducted a clinical study demonstrating that Nd:YAG laser combined with triamcinolone acetonide injection significantly reduced postoperative recurrence rates and achieved superior therapeutic efficacy compared with corticosteroid injection alone. However, the study also highlighted that the molecular mechanisms underlying the synergistic effects of this combination therapy remain unclear, with insufficient basic experimental evidence to explain its biological basis. Guan et al8 reported that punch excision combined with radiotherapy achieved favorable clinical outcomes in patients with trunk keloids, with no recurrence observed during the 18-month follow-up period. Nevertheless, radiotherapy carries potential risks, including pigmentation abnormalities and local tissue damage, which limit its applicability in certain patient populations. Overall, current therapeutic strategies for keloids remain primarily symptom-oriented, and effective targeted therapies based on disease-specific molecular characteristics are still lacking.

The pathogenesis of keloid is considered multifactorial, involving genetic predisposition, mechanical tension, chronic inflammation, and alterations in the local microenvironment.9,10 Among these, dysregulated fibroblast proliferation and differentiation are regarded as central pathological features. Several signalling pathways, including transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), Notch, and NF-κB, have been implicated in these processes.6,11 In recent years, transcriptomic sequencing and bioinformatics-based studies have further elucidated the molecular characteristics and potential regulatory networks associated with keloid formation. Wang et al12 identified JAG1 as one of four immune-related genes associated with keloids through transcriptomic analysis and further confirmed its increased expression in keloid tissues by immunohistochemistry. These findings suggest that JAG1 may play a potential role in keloid pathogenesis. However, the specific biological functions of JAG1 and the molecular mechanisms through which it contributes to keloid development remain incompletely understood and require further experimental investigation.

Aramaki-Hattori et al13 demonstrated increased nuclear localization of YAP/TAZ in keloid fibroblasts compared with fibroblasts from unaffected skin, suggesting that YAP/TAZ-mediated mechanotransduction may contribute to keloid formation. These findings highlight the potential importance of mechanical signaling in keloid pathogenesis. However, the dynamic molecular alterations and regulatory mechanisms during scar formation and progression remain incompletely understood and warrant further investigation using appropriate trauma-induced in vivo models.

Deng et al14 applied single-cell RNA sequencing to characterize fibroblast heterogeneity in keloids and identified distinct fibroblast subpopulations, with mesenchymal fibroblasts being significantly enriched in keloid tissues and potentially contributing to excessive collagen deposition. These findings provide important insights into the cellular heterogeneity and fibrotic mechanisms of keloids. Nevertheless, longitudinal studies, multi-cohort integration, and further functional validation are required to elucidate the dynamic cellular and molecular changes during keloid formation and progression.

Collectively, these studies have substantially advanced our understanding of keloid pathogenesis and molecular characteristics. However, due to the complex interactions among genetic susceptibility, mechanical signals, metabolic reprogramming, and cellular heterogeneity, current findings remain insufficient to fully explain the marked biological heterogeneity and individual variability of keloids. Therefore, integrating multi-cohort transcriptomic datasets with machine learning models and external validation strategies is warranted to identify robust molecular signatures and systematically characterize the key regulatory networks underlying keloid development.

With advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies, transcriptome-based bioinformatic approaches have become a key strategy for elucidating disease-associated molecular mechanisms.15–17 Nevertheless, conventional analytical methods often rely on single-dimensional data and may fail to capture coordinated gene regulation. In addition, the high dimensionality and complexity of transcriptomic data increase the risk of model instability and overfitting when using individual analytical frameworks.

The introduction of machine learning has provided new opportunities for biomarker discovery in complex diseases. By integrating multiple algorithms, it is possible to identify key features with strong discriminatory power from high-dimensional data, thereby improving model robustness and generalizability.18 In addition, combining machine learning with systems biology approaches such as weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) enables the identification of disease-associated gene modules at the network level, offering a more comprehensive view of underlying molecular mechanisms.19,20

In this context, the present study integrates multiple transcriptomic datasets to systematically identify keloid-associated genes through differential expression analysis, WGCNA, and machine learning approaches. Functional enrichment were further performed to explore their potential roles in keloid pathogenesis. To support these findings experimentally, a C57 mouse wound model was established, and histopathological features were assessed using haematoxylin–eosin staining. Expression levels of key genes were validated by quantitative real-time PCR. Together, this study aims to characterise the regulatory network of keloid across molecular and tissue levels, providing a framework for further investigation of its underlying mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets.

Materials and Methods

Transcriptomic datasets of keloid were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database and integrated for analysis. After data normalisation and batch effect correction, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was then performed to detect disease-associated modules, which were intersected with DEGs to obtain candidate genes.

Subsequently, twelve feature selection algorithms were integrated to construct 120 machine learning model combinations for key feature identification and model development. Model performance was systematically evaluated in terms of robustness and predictive capacity. Data Sources and Preprocessing

Transcriptomic datasets related to keloid were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). GSE7890 (19 fibroblast samples: 10 keloid-derived and nine normal scar-derived samples) and GSE158395 (13 tissue samples: seven keloid tissues and six normal skin tissues) were used as the training cohort, while GSE190626 (six tissue samples: three keloid tissues and three normal skin tissues) and GSE246562 (12 fibroblast samples: six normal dermal fibroblast-derived samples and six keloid dermal fibroblast-derived samples) served as external validation cohorts.

To minimise inter-platform variability and batch effects, surrogate variable analysis (SVA) combined with the ComBat algorithm was applied for data correction. Principal component analysis (PCA) was subsequently performed to assess the effectiveness of batch correction and to ensure the stability and comparability of the integrated datasets.

Identification of Differentially Expressed Genes and Construction of Gene Co-Expression Networks

Differential expression analysis was performed on the training datasets using the limma package. Genes with |log2 FC| > 0.585 and false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P value < 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs)21,22 and results were visualised using ggplot2.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was then conducted to construct gene co-expression networks. After removing low-variance genes (standard deviation ≤ 0.5), an appropriate soft-thresholding power was selected to achieve a scale-free topology (R2 ≥ 0.8). The minimum module size was set to 60 genes, and modules with a correlation coefficient > 0.75 were merged. This approach enabled the identification of modules significantly associated with keloid.23

Functional and Pathway Enrichment Analysis

The intersection of differentially expressed genes and genes from key WGCNA modules was subjected to functional enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler package. Gene Ontology (GO) annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis were performed, with P < 0.05 set as the threshold for significance. Results were visualised using multiple graphical approaches.

The intersecting genes were then imported into the STRING database (species: Homo sapiens), with a confidence score threshold of 0.4, to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network. The resulting network was further analysed and visualised using Cytoscape (version 3.10.4), and the top 20 hub genes were identified based on network topology for subsequent analysis.

Construction of Machine Learning Models and Identification of Key Genes

Based on the training transcriptomic data, twelve machine learning algorithms, including LASSO, XGBoost, and random forest, were integrated to generate 120 classification model combinations. Ten-fold cross-validation was performed during model training to evaluate model stability and reduce the risk of overfitting. The training dataset was randomly divided into ten subsets, with nine subsets used for model training and the remaining subset used for internal validation. This process was repeated ten times, and the average performance was calculated. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, and F1 score, and the optimal model was selected accordingly. Feature genes were then ranked based on their contribution within the selected model, and the most informative genes were identified.

The robustness of the model was further assessed using independent external validation datasets. Classification performance and generalisability were evaluated by comparing AUC values across both training and validation cohorts.

Establishment and Analysis of the Murine Silicone-Splinted Excisional Wound Model

Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, 30–35 g) were obtained from Jiangsu Qinglongshan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). After one week of acclimatisation, a full-thickness dorsal skin wound model was established under sterile conditions. Briefly, mice were anaesthetised, the dorsal hair was removed, and a circular full-thickness skin wound (~1 cm in diameter) was created.24

Mice in the control group did not undergo modelling procedures; normal skin from the corresponding anatomical site was collected as control tissue. Animals were randomly assigned to either the control group or the wound model group, with n = 3 mice per group. All mice were sacrificed for tissue collection at day 14 after wounding. The three biological replicates for qRT-PCR correspond to three individual mice. Wound-healing progress was monitored and recorded on days 1, 2, 7, and 14 post-injury (Figure 2).Figure 2Experimental design of the murine full-thickness dorsal skin wound model. Wound healing progression and tissue collection were performed at days 1, 2, 7 and 14 post-injury.Timeline of wound healing in a mouse model over 14 days.

Experimental design of the murine full-thickness dorsal skin wound model. Wound healing progression and tissue collection were performed at days 1, 2, 7 and 14 post-injury.

Upon completion of all experimental procedures, mice were humanely euthanized. Mice were anesthetized with 20% urethane (1.5 g/kg, i.p.) followed by immediate cervical dislocation to ensure thorough and irreversible euthanasia. Death was confirmed by the permanent disappearance of spontaneous respiratory movement and complete cessation of heartbeat. Dorsal wound tissues and corresponding control skin samples were collected promptly. Although the murine silicone-splinted excisional wound model enables investigation of mechanical-driven wound repair and fibrotic remodelling, it cannot fully recapitulate the pathological features of human pathological scars and keloids. Therefore, human keloid transcriptomic datasets from the GEO database were further integrated in this study to improve clinical relevance and identify conserved molecular signatures associated with keloid formation. A portion of each tissue sample was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for hematoxylin–eosin and Masson’s trichrome staining, while the remainder was snap‑frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for qRT–PCR analysis.

All animal care and experimental procedures were performed in strict accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of China and the ARRIVE guidelines. The animal experiment protocol was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Qinghai University (Ethics Approval No.: P-SL-2023145). All operations were conducted to minimize animal pain, distress and suffering throughout the experiment.

Haematoxylin–Eosin Staining

Tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at approximately 4 μm. Sections were deparaffinised, rehydrated, and stained with haematoxylin–eosin (H&E) to assess histological morphology.

Masson’s Trichrome Staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were subjected to Masson’s trichrome staining to evaluate collagen deposition.

Statistical Analysis

Bioinformatic analyses were conducted using R software. qRT–PCR data were derived from three biological replicates and are presented as relative expression levels normalised to β-actin using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Student’s t-test was used for two-group comparisons of qRT-PCR gene expression data. P < 0.05 was defined as the threshold for statistical significance.

Identification of Key Genes and Functional Enrichment Analysis

Based on the optimal model, six feature genes were identified: JAG1, IL15, NR2F2, TNFAIP2, TSHZ1, and STMN2. Comparative analysis of their expression levels (Figure 8B) showed significant differences between groups (P < 0.001). JAG1, NR2F2, and STMN2 were upregulated in keloid samples, whereas IL15, TNFAIP2, and TSHZ1 showed higher expression in control samples.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis (Figure 8C) indicated that these genes exhibited strong discriminatory performance, with AUC values exceeding 0.85. Among them, NR2F2 (AUC = 0.937) and IL15 (AUC = 0.929) showed the highest performance.

Experimental Validation

Histological Assessment of Fibrotic Features by H&E and Masson Staining

H&E and Masson staining demonstrated characteristic fibrotic features in keloid tissues, including increased collagen deposition, disorganised fibre arrangement, and altered tissue architecture. To further assess fibrotic features during wound repair, we performed serial macroscopic observation of wound healing in a murine dorsal full-thickness skin wound model.

H&E staining (Figure 9A) showed progressive fibroblast proliferation and inflammatory cell infiltration within the wound area, accompanied by gradual thickening and disorganisation of tissue structure. Masson’s trichrome staining (Figure 9B) further confirmed these changes, revealing extensive collagen deposition in the dermis, with densely packed and interwoven collagen fibres. Discussion

In this study, we integrated multiple publicly available transcriptomic datasets to systematically identify 82 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and the key co-expression module MEpink. Further integration of differential expression analysis and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) identified 61 candidate genes, which were mainly enriched in inflammatory responses, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, and classical fibrotic pathways, including TGF-β and PI3K-Akt signaling. To validate the reliability of the bioinformatics findings, a full-thickness skin defect wound model was established on the dorsal skin of mice. Histological evaluation showed increased fibroblast proliferation and structural disruption during wound repair, as demonstrated by hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining, while Masson staining revealed extensive deposition and remodeling of collagen fibers. These histopathological alterations confirmed the development of fibrotic phenotypes during wound healing at the morphological level and were highly consistent with the enrichment results indicating fibroblast activation and excessive ECM accumulation.

KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated that the candidate genes were significantly enriched in inflammation- and fibrosis-related signaling pathways, including TNF, TGF-β, PI3K-Akt, and JAK-STAT signaling. These pathways play important roles in regulating inflammatory responses, fibroblast activation, and ECM deposition,25 suggesting that keloid formation may not be driven by a single molecular event but rather by the persistent dysregulation of multiple pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic signaling pathways. Furthermore, PPI network analysis revealed several potential regulatory nodes, indicating the presence of a complex molecular interaction network involved in keloid development.

Through further screening using multiple machine learning algorithms, six key candidate genes were identified. These genes exhibited significant differential expression between keloid and normal skin samples and demonstrated certain discriminatory capability in the training cohort. Among them, JAG1 and NR2F2 not only showed high connectivity within the PPI network but also contributed substantially to the performance of the machine learning models, suggesting that they may play important roles in the molecular regulatory network of keloid and possess potential diagnostic value.

JAG1 (Jagged canonical Notch ligand 1) is an important ligand of the Notch signaling pathway and plays critical roles in cell fate determination, proliferation, differentiation, and tissue remodeling.26 Recent studies have shown that aberrant activation of Notch signaling promotes fibroblast activation, myofibroblast differentiation, and collagen and ECM deposition, all of which are essential pathological processes in keloid formation.27–29 Therefore, the elevated expression and high network centrality of JAG1 observed in keloid tissues in this study suggest that JAG1 may contribute to maintaining the activated fibroblast phenotype and promoting the formation of a persistent fibrotic microenvironment. In addition, the interaction between Notch signaling and classical fibrotic pathways such as TGF-β signaling suggests that JAG1 may serve as an important regulatory node linking multiple pro-fibrotic signals during keloid development. However, although the altered expression of JAG1 was experimentally validated in the present study, its direct functional role and downstream molecular mechanisms in keloid pathogenesis remain to be elucidated.

NR2F2 (nuclear receptor subfamily 2 group F member 2, also known as COUP-TFII) is a nuclear receptor transcription factor involved in angiogenesis, inflammatory regulation, mesenchymal cell maintenance, and tissue homeostasis.30–32 Although the role of NR2F2 in keloid formation remains largely unexplored, previous studies have demonstrated that NR2F2 participates in the regulation of cellular plasticity, tissue remodeling, and ECM-associated processes.33–35 In the present study, NR2F2 was significantly upregulated in keloid tissues and showed a high contribution to the machine learning model, suggesting that it may participate in maintaining the abnormal transcriptional state associated with keloid formation. Moreover, the reported regulatory relationship between NR2F2 and non-coding RNAs, particularly the NR2F2/miR-21/Smad7 axis, suggests that NR2F2 may serve as a regulatory node linking transcriptional regulation with post-transcriptional mechanisms involved in fibrotic processes.36,37

Notably, the simultaneous identification and experimental validation of JAG1 and NR2F2 in the present study suggest that these genes may represent important components of the broader regulatory network involved in keloid pathogenesis rather than merely independent biomarkers. Previous studies have implicated JAG1-mediated Notch signaling in fibroblast activation and fibrotic remodeling in keloids, whereas NR2F2 has been associated with myofibroblast activation and extracellular matrix regulation in other fibrotic disorders. Together with our experimental findings, these observations suggest that JAG1- and NR2F2-associated regulatory processes may converge on fibroblast dysfunction and ECM remodeling, thereby contributing to keloid development. However, although their expression patterns were experimentally validated in the present study, whether JAG1 and NR2F2 are functionally interconnected and the molecular mechanisms through which they contribute to fibrotic remodeling remain unclear. Further targeted functional and mechanistic studies are therefore required to clarify their regulatory relationship.

Furthermore, this study revealed that IL15, TNFAIP2, and TSHZ1 were more highly expressed in normal skin tissues, whereas JAG1, NR2F2, and STMN2 were significantly upregulated in keloid tissues, suggesting that different genes may exert distinct regulatory effects during disease development. This complex expression pattern further reflects the dynamic imbalance of the molecular regulatory network in keloid, characterized by activation of pro-fibrotic and pro-inflammatory programs accompanied by dysregulation of certain homeostatic regulatory pathways.38–40

Based on the findings of this study, JAG1 and NR2F2 were identified as key candidate genes exhibiting both high network centrality and strong discriminatory capability, suggesting their potential value as molecular biomarkers for keloid. Furthermore, the inflammatory and fibrotic regulatory networks associated with JAG1 and NR2F2 provide new directions for further elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying keloid development and exploring potential therapeutic targets. However, as this study was primarily based on transcriptomic analysis and computational modeling, the clinical applicability, biological functions, and regulatory interactions of JAG1 and NR2F2 require further validation in independent clinical cohorts and functional experiments.

Several limitations should be considered. First, although the murine silicone-splinted excisional wound model enables investigation of mechanical-driven wound repair and fibrotic remodelling, it cannot fully recapitulate the pathological features of human pathological scars and keloids. Mouse skin wounds do not develop true keloid-like lesions that overgrow beyond the original wound margin. For this reason, we integrated human keloid transcriptomic data from the GEO database to compensate for this limitation and screen keloid-relevant molecular signatures. Accordingly, observations obtained from mouse tissues require further validation using human-derived experimental systems. In addition, histological and molecular detection were only performed on day-14 samples; multi-time-point tissue sampling was not carried out, so we cannot evaluate the dynamic temporal changes of histology and gene expression during wound repair. Second, the integration of transcriptomic datasets from different platforms introduces potential residual heterogeneity and bias despite batch correction. Differences in sample sources, experimental platforms, and biological backgrounds may contribute to dataset-specific variations that cannot be completely eliminated. Third, although external validation cohorts were included, the sample sizes for experimental validation remain relatively modest, and larger multi-center cohorts are required to further confirm the robustness and generalizability of the proposed model. In addition, the analysis is mainly based on transcriptomic data, with limited functional validation at the protein and pathway levels. Finally, the regulatory relationship between JAG1 and NR2F2 remains unclear, and whether they participate in a shared regulatory axis requires further mechanistic investigation.

Conclusion

Taken together, these findings position JAG1 and NR2F2 within a network of genes associated with keloid, linked to inflammation–fibrosis coupling. While their expression patterns and network properties suggest potential relevance as molecular indicators, their functional roles and translational value remain to be established. This study provides a framework for dissecting the regulatory architecture of keloid and for guiding future mechanistic and therapeutic investigations.
