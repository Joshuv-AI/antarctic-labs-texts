Potential Benefit of Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter 2-Inhibitors in Cisplatin-Associated Nephrotoxicity Among Patients With Cancer Having Diabetes Mellitus
Authors: Juan J Cintrón-García, Maulinkumar Patel, Clark R Andersen, Valda Page, Biruh Workeneh, Sreedhar Mandayam, Reem Al-Dallal, Ala Abudayyeh, Omar Mamlouk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCisplatin is often nephrotoxic, frequently causing treatment interruptions or discontinuation. Mitigation includes dose adjustments, hydration, and supplemental magnesium. Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) may protect the kidneys by reducing tubular cells’ cisplatin uptake. We examined the potential kidney protective effect of SGLT2i in patients with cancer having diabetes mellitus receiving cisplatin.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective study of patients who received i.v. cisplatin from July 2017 to January 2024. Data extracted included comorbidities, variables associated with acute kidney injury (AKI; defined as ≥ 50% serum creatinine increase within 4 weeks), hyponatremia, hypomagnesemia, and anemia. Patients were divided according to SGLT2i exposure at cisplatin initiation and matched 1:1 using inverse propensity scores based on baseline characteristics. Descriptive statistics summarized baseline and outcome variables; parametric or nonparametric tests assessed group differences. Logistic regression and analysis of variance were used for further outcome analyses.ResultsA total of 288 patients (n = 144 per group) were included. Median cisplatin dose per infusion was 40 mg/m2 with a median of 5 infusions, and median follow-up was 2 months. Incidence of AKI was lower in the SGLT2i group compared with controls (13.9% vs. 28.5%; odds ratio [OR]: 0.42; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.23–0.76). The SGLT2i group also showed higher mean estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) at 3 months postcisplatin therapy, lower rate of hypomagnesemia, hyponatremia, and anemia than the control. Difference in hyponatremia was not statistically significant.ConclusionOur findings suggest that continuation of SGLT2i may offer kidney-protective effects against AKI in patients with diabetes mellitus undergoing cisplatin chemotherapy. A well-designed randomized clinical trial is needed to validate this potential benefit.Keywords: cisplatin, nephrotoxicity, SGLT2i

The platinum-based agent cisplatin remains a cornerstone of modern chemotherapy owing to its effectiveness against a variety of cancer types. One of cisplatin’s prominent dose-limiting toxicities is nephrotoxicity. The speculated mechanism for this toxicity is related to drug accumulation in the proximal tubular cells, interference with various signaling pathways, inflammation, and oxidative stress, leading to apoptosis and necrosis. Cisplatin’s nephrotoxicity can result in salt wasting, magnesium wasting, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, and Fanconi syndrome.1 Patients with a cumulative cisplatin dose of more than 300 mg/m2 are at risk for AKI; however, a single cisplatin dose of more than 60 mg/m2 may also increase this risk.1 Other factors that may contribute to cisplatin-induced AKI risk include old age, Black race, female sex, comorbid conditions (e.g., hypertension, heart disease, and diabetes mellitus), concomitant nephrotoxic medications, and low serum levels of albumin or magnesium.2, 3, 4, 5 The incidence of AKI is approximately 30%, but it can vary from 5.2% to 35%, depending on the criteria used for AKI definition, patient age and comorbidities, and cisplatin dose.6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11

The pathophysiology of cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity is multifactorial. Proposed mechanisms include tubular necrosis, oxidative stress, increased inflammatory response, and kidney vascular injury.12,13 Proposed strategies to help mitigate cisplatin’s nephrotoxicity include intravenous hydration, diuresis with mannitol, supplemental magnesium, avoiding concurrent use of nephrotoxic agents, and lowering the cisplatin dose.14,15 Amifostine has been linked to a reduced risk of cisplatin-related nephrotoxicity; however, its clinical utility remains unclear because of its side effects, cost, and the availability of alternative strategies, such as using other platinum-based drugs or lowering the cisplatin dose.16, 17, 18 Other interventions, including organic cation transporter 2 (OCT2) receptor substrates, antioxidants, and anti-inflammatory agents, have shown potential in preclinical studies; however, the results of these studies have not been replicated in clinical trials.6 Despite these mitigation strategies, cisplatin-induced AKI remains a significant challenge that often leads to treatment interruption or discontinuation.

SGLT2is, which limit the reabsorption of filtered glucose and sodium in the proximal tubules, may protect the kidneys from cisplatin-induced toxicity. SGLT2is were initially approved by the US Food and Drug Administration in 2013 as an adjunct therapy to improve glycemic control in patients with diabetes mellitus.19 Since then, SGLT2is have demonstrated a broad range of clinical benefits, such as reducing the risk of major cardiovascular events (including in patients with cancer), slowing the progression of chronic kidney disease, and improving blood pressure control.19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25 Evidence supporting the use of SGLT2is to mitigate cisplatin-related nephrotoxicity has been confined to animal studies.

Multiple preclinical studies suggest that SGLT2is can reduce the injury of organs susceptible to cisplatin toxicity by disrupting certain mechanisms, including the inhibition of inflammatory pathways and the activation of antioxidative stress pathways.26,27 Furthermore, proposed mechanisms specific to nephroprotection involve the reduction of cellular uptake, as well as the inhibition of the mitochondrial pathway of apoptosis.28,29 One study reported attenuation of cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity in canagliflozin-treated mice with improvement in creatinine and blood urea nitrogen, as well as inhibition of cisplatin-induced proximal tubular apoptosis in canagliflozin treated mice.28 Notable histologic differences were also reported by Satyam et al.,29 with tubular-interstitial inflammation, brush border loss, epithelial denudation, and cystic dilation in rats treated with cisplatin, whereas rats treated with dapagliflozin (SGLT2i) only presented with moderate interstitial inflammation.

To date, few studies have investigated the potential nephroprotective role of SGLT2is in patients with cancer receiving cisplatin30,31 Therefore, we conducted a retrospective cohort study to investigate this potential role in patients receiving cisplatin for cancer and already taking SGLT2i as a part of their diabetes mellitus management.

Methods

This study was approved by the institutional review board at The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center, and its procedures were in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. We followed the STROBE reporting guidelines.

Study Design and Data Source

We conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study reviewing the medical records of all patients with diabetes mellitus and either solid or hematological malignancies who received i.v. cisplatin therapy at our institution between July 2017 and January 2024.

Study Population

We included all patients who received at least 1 dose of cisplatin, had a documented diagnosis of diabetes mellitus, were on antidiabetic medications during cisplatin therapy, and had serum creatinine measurements taken after each dose of cisplatin. Patients were excluded if diabetes mellitus was diagnosed after exposure to cisplatin, if they were not on diabetic medications during cisplatin therapy, if there was not a documented exposure to SGLT2is, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, or dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors at any time during the cancer therapy (pre-, peri-, and post-cisplatin therapy), or if follow-up labs after cisplatin therapy were unavailable (Figure 1).

Study flow diagram of patient selection. The flowchart illustrates the step-wise identification of the study population prior to propensity score matching. Starting with 7652 adult patients treated with cisplatin, exclusions were made for patients without diabetes (n = 7044) and specific clinical factors (n = 91). The final pre-matching cohorts consisted of 144 patients receiving SGLT2i during cisplatin therapy and 373 patients in the GLP-1 and/or DPP-4i comparison group.

We collected data pertaining to the use of SGLT2is for diabetes mellitus management and divided patients into 2 groups based on their SGLT2i exposure at the time of cisplatin initiation. The patients in the active therapy group (n = 144) were already receiving SGLT2i when they started cisplatin therapy and continued receiving SGLT2is for various periods during cisplatin therapy (± GLP1 receptor agonist or DPP-4 inhibitors). In contrast, the control cohort (n = 373) comprised of 237 patients receiving DPP-4 inhibitors ± GLP-1 receptor agonists concurrently with cisplatin therapy. The remaining 136 patients were nonconcurrent users who had documented exposure to DPP-4 inhibitors ± GLP-1 receptor agonists (n = 109) or SGLT2i (n = 27) during the pre- or post-cisplatin periods only (Figure 1). The reasons for discontinuation of SGLT2i before starting cisplatin therapy in the latter group were not documented; however, it is not related to the kidney function, as these patients did not have an AKI diagnosis during SGLT2i therapy.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the incidence of AKI, defined as an increase in serum creatinine level of at least 50% from baseline during the 4 weeks after each cisplatin infusion.32 We considered only the initial AKI episode after the start of cisplatin therapy. AKI recovery at 3 months post-AKI was categorized as complete if the serum creatinine level decreased to less than 0.35 mg/dl above the baseline value; partial if it remained more than 0.35 mg/dl above the baseline value; and no recovery if the serum creatinine level did not decrease from its peak value or if the patient remained on kidney replacement therapy.

Secondary outcomes included the incidence of hyponatremia (any serum sodium level value < 135 mEq/l), hypomagnesemia (any serum level value < 1.6 mg/dl), and anemia (any hemoglobin value < 12.0 g/dl in women and < 13 g/dl in men), observed from the initiation of cisplatin therapy and through 4 weeks after the last dose. Additionally, we assessed the kidney function (creatinine-based eGFR) at 3 months after the last cisplatin infusion (± 4 weeks) and monitored SGLT2i-specific side effects, including genitourinary infections and diabetic ketoacidosis.

Data Collection and Covariates

We collected patients’ demographic characteristics (age, sex, race, and body surface area), comorbidities (hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, congestive heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and liver failure), types of cancer for which cisplatin was given, and baseline laboratory values (serum creatinine level and eGFR on the day of first dose of cisplatin, and the most recent hemoglobin A1C value within 1 year before the first cisplatin infusion).33 We also collected covariates of AKI during cisplatin therapy, including the total cumulative cisplatin dose, i.v. magnesium, and/or mannitol at the time of each cisplatin infusion, as well as the use of medications such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers, antimicrobial agents, chemo-immunotherapy, and diabetes mellitus medications (insulin, metformin, and sulfonylurea) concurrently with cisplatin.

Statistical Analysis

Data from 144 patients under active therapy were matched to a pool of corresponding data from 373 patients in nonactive therapy control as a 1-to-1 inverse-propensity score match. Matching was performed over the baseline variables including cancer type, race (non-White vs White), sex, statuses of hypertension, congestive heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, liver, chronic kidney disease, concurrent chemotherapy, mannitol or magnesium supplementation at the day of cisplatin infusion, insulin, as well as numeric age, baseline creatinine, eGFR at baseline, body surface area, median dose of cisplatin, and total cisplatin dose (log base-2 transformed to reduce skewness). Following matching, baseline variables were summarized by group using mean, SD, median, and range or frequency with percentage, as well as the standardized mean difference. Outcome variables were additionally modeled by logistic regression or analysis of variance with relation to the group, adjusting for baseline GLP-1 status to compensate for remaining postmatch unbalance of that variable. We used complete case data for matched variables as the source for the matching, so following the match, there was no missing data for those variables. Matching used a simple inverse propensity match based upon a logistic regression model. Scores were not stabilized, nor were weights trimmed. Aside from the matched data, the key result of a match is the reported balance between the matched variables. Analyses of matched data did not use covariate adjustment.

Statistical analyses assumed a 95% level of statistical confidence. Statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.5.0, Posit Software, Boston, MA). Matching was performed using the “Match” function of the “Matching” package, matching without replacement but otherwise using default parameters.34

Results

After propensity score matching, 288 patients were included in the analysis. The matched cohort had a mean age of 62.9 years (SD: 9.79), with the majority identifying as White (72.9%) and male (71.5%). Baseline characteristics of the study population, both before and after propensity score matching, are presented in Table 1. For all patients, the median cisplatin dose per infusion was 40 mg/m2 (range: 10–120 mg/m2), and the median number of infusions was 5 (range: 1–30 infusions). The median follow-up duration was 2 months (range: 2–17 months). In the SGLT2i group, 87.5% of patients received SGLT2i throughout their cisplatin therapy, and in 12.5% patients, SGLT2i were discontinued at a variable time after the first dose of cisplatin. Within the SGLT2i cohort (n = 144), the specific medications were empagliflozin (n = 89), dapagliflozin (n = 37), and canagliflozin or ertugliflozin (n = 19). Primary Outcome: AKI

Among matched patients with cancer having diabetes mellitus receiving cisplatin therapy, those treated with SGLT2is experienced lower rates of AKI (Table 2). The incidence of AKI was 13.9% compared with 28.5% in the control group. This corresponds to a reduced odds of AKI (OR: 0.42, 95% CI: 0.23–0.76; P = 0.004). Among patients who developed AKI, both AKI severity and kidney recovery at 3 months differed between the 2 groups (Figure 2). The control group consistently had higher AKI rate across all stages compared with SGLT2i group: 28.5% versus 14.0% for stage ≥ 1; 12.5% versus 8.0% for stage ≥ 2; and 3.5% versus 1.0% for stage 3. At 3 months, 65% of patients receiving SGLT2i who developed AKI achieved complete kidney recovery, whereas in the control group, 48.8% achieved complete recovery.

Secondary Outcomes

At 3 months following cisplatin therapy, mean eGFR was higher in the SGLT2i group compared with controls (84.4 ± 25.7 vs. 78.3 ± 25.1 ml/min per 1.73 m2), with a mean difference of 6.08 ml/min per 1.73 m2 (95% CI: 0.23–11.93; P = 0.043). Patients receiving SGLT2is experienced a significantly lower incidence of hypomagnesemia (23.2% vs. 57.3%; OR: 0.23, 95% CI: 0.14–0.38; P < 0.001) and anemia (83.3% vs. 95.1%; OR: 0.21, 95% CI: 0.09–0.52; P < 0.001) compared with the control group. Although the incidence of hyponatremia was lower in the SGLT2i cohort (60.4% vs. 67.4%), it did not reach statistical significance (OR: 0.73, 95% CI: 0.45–1.18).

Regarding SGLT2i-associated side effects, no significant differences were observed between the 2 groups in relation to the incidence of genitourinary infections. Although 2 patients in the SGLT2i group developed diabetic ketoacidosis (there were none in the control group), no cases of necrotizing fasciitis were reported in the entire study cohort.

Discussion

This retrospective single-center study evaluated the association between SGLT2i use and the incidence of AKI in patients with diabetes mellitus undergoing cisplatin chemotherapy. We observed a significantly lower odds of AKI in SGLT2i cohort with 58% reduction in AKI incidence than the control group (OR: 0.42, 95% CI: 0.23–0.76).

Beyond the lower incidence of AKI, we observed secondary benefits that deserve attention. The eGFR at 3 months following the final cisplatin dose was higher in patients who received SGLT2i therapy than in the control group. This observation may be because of the lower incidence of AKI and higher rate of complete kidney recovery from AKI in the SGLT2i group. Collectively, these findings suggest that SGLT2is are associated with better kidney outcomes in this population.

Despite previous evidence of the nephroprotective effects of SGLT2is in animal studies, there is a paucity of clinical data evaluating those benefits in humans.30,31 Our study departs from the currently available data by demonstrating kidney-protective effects. For instance, although the AKI rates in our control group (28.5%) and SGLT2i group (13.9%) are similar to those reported by Idowu et al.30 (14.1%) and Ishigami et al.31 (29.9%), respectively, we observed a statistically significantly lower incidence of AKI in the SGLT2i group compared with the control group.30,31 This possibly stems from differences in the methodology. The analysis by Ishigami et al.31 was conducted in a small, unmatched sample (n = 33 patients in SGLT2i cohort; n = 134 in non-SGLT2i group), in which the SGLT2i cohort received higher cisplatin doses than the control group (135 mg [95–203] vs. 124 mg [60–200]). In addition, they limited their evaluation to patients receiving high-dose cisplatin (> 50 mg/m2) and did not clearly define the timing of SGLT2i exposure. In their study the OR for AKI associated with SGLT2i use was 1.27 (95% CI: 0.52–3.11).31 In contrast, our study included all cisplatin doses, including high doses (40 mg [20–120]), used a propensity-matched design, and ensured that all the patients in the active group were receiving SGLT2i at the time of cisplatin initiation. Furthermore, 87.5% of our treatment cohort maintained therapy throughout the treatment course, whereas Idowu et al.30 included patients who initiated SGLT2i as late as 3 months after cisplatin initiation. Similar to our study, Idowu et al.30 used 1:1 propensity score matching to equally divide 300 patients into 2 groups based on SGLT2i use; however, the resulting hazard ratio for AKI was 0.83 (95% CI: 0.42–1.61).

Established evidence indicates that hypomagnesemia can exacerbate cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity by increasing oxidative stress and promoting tubular cell apoptosis.5 At the same time, magnesium supplementation has been consistently associated with a decreased incidence of cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity.4,35 Therefore, SGLT2i may reduce cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity by restoring serum magnesium levels and reducing urinary magnesium excretion.36,37 Notably, in the present study, patients receiving SGLT2is had a lower incidence of hypomagnesemia, despite receiving i.v. magnesium supplementation at doses and frequencies comparable to those of the control group during cisplatin therapy.

In addition to the kidney outcomes, we evaluated other clinical parameters influenced by SGLT2i therapy, specifically anemia and hyponatremia. SGLT2is have also been reported to decrease the incidence of anemia, which is attributed to increased erythropoietin levels and improved iron metabolism by decreasing hepcidin.38, 39, 40 Similarly, a lower incidence of hyponatremia has also been reported in patients with diabetes mellitus on SGLT2i compared with other antidiabetic therapies (DPP-4 or GLP-1). This was largely attributed to increased distal sodium reuptake and glucosuria associated with osmotic diuresis.41, 42, 43 In our cohort, the incidence of both anemia and hyponatremia were low in the SGLT2i group; however, this difference was statistically significant only for anemia (Table 2).

Furthermore, cisplatin and SGLT2i have been associated with distinct metabolic complications, including new onset diabetes mellitus/hyperglycemia (cisplatin) and diabetic ketoacidosis (SGLT2i), respectively.42 We observed 2 cases of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) among the 144 patients receiving SGLT2is during cisplatin therapy, and none in the control group. The 1.4% incidence rate is notably higher than the estimated 0.2% DKA incidence reported in the general diabetic population, indicating that concurrent administration of these therapies may warrant close monitoring.44

Beyond cisplatin nephrotoxicity prevention and diabetes mellitus management, SGLT2i may also have implications for cancer treatment. Although we did not evaluate tumor response in this study, promising data suggest that SGLT2 has a role in tumor response and that SGLT2i have an antitumor effect. SGLT2 is overexpressed by many types of tumor cells and is believed to be involved in tumorigenesis, tumor progression, and metastasis. Accordingly, large-scale studies have shown that SGLT2i can inhibit tumor growth and improve prognosis through multiple pathways that are independent of the effects of SGLT2i on blood glucose. These pathways include the reduction of tissue proinflammatory markers (e.g., transforming growth factor beta 1 and interleukin-1 alpha) and the upregulation of cell autophagy and apoptosis through the increase of tissue total nitrate and nitrite and the activity of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2, beclin-1, paraoxonase 1, c-Jun N-terminal kinase, and caspase-3.45,46

A primary strength of our study is the novelty of its findings within the onco-nephrology field. Although the protective effects of SGLT2i have been documented in preclinical models, this study provides real-world evidence in a human population. Furthermore, our comprehensive assessment of secondary end points—including 3-month eGFR, kidney recovery, electrolyte stability, and anemia—provides a potential broader clinical utility of SGLT2i as a supportive therapy during cisplatin treatment.

Among the study limitations, we first identify that the relatively small number of patients may have limited the statistical power and the generalizability of the study’s findings. Second, the retrospective design may have introduced information bias regarding SGLT2i exposures. Third, the retrospective nature of data resulted in the lack of clear documentation regarding the clinical rationale for prescribing SGLT2i over other medications and the specific reasons for discontinuation of the medication. Notably, the data related to proteinuria were unavailable. Fourth, the absence of an active comparator new-user design could have introduced potential for prevalent user bias despite our efforts to control confounders. Furthermore, the sample size precluded a specific drug versus drug sensitivity analysis (e.g., SGLT-2i vs. GLP-1 or DPP4i), as the resulting subpopulations (n < 40 per group) would lack sufficient power to provide precise estimates and broad CIs. Lastly, the study did not evaluate the difference in mortality rates between the active and control groups.

Conclusion

SGLT2i use was associated with improved kidney outcomes in patients with cancer receiving cisplatin. We find our observations promising and worth exploring for the continuation of SGLT2i in patients with cancer having diabetes mellitus who are receiving cisplatin. Nonetheless, there is currently insufficient evidence to recommend initiating SGLT2i in patients with or without diabetes mellitus solely for potential kidney protection. A well-designed randomized clinical trial with a larger sample size is needed to evaluate this potential indication.
