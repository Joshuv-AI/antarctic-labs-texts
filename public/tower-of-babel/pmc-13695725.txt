Single‐Nucleus Transcriptomics Identifies Microglial Interferon Regulatory Factor 5 as a Regulator of Neuroinflammation During Heart Failure Progression After Myocardial Infarction in Rats
Authors: Wanxin Tang, Yang Yu, Jing‐Yu Bing, Lei Tong, Robert M. Weiss, Shun‐Guang Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe paraventricular nucleus (PVN) of the hypothalamus is a key autonomic and cardiovascular regulatory center that contributes to neuroinflammation‐driven sympathetic excitation in heart failure. To define the cellular and transcriptional mechanisms underlying inflammatory signaling during heart failure progression, we performed single‐nucleus RNA sequencing of the PVN in rats 2 weeks after myocardial infarction (MI).MethodsPVN tissues were collected 2 weeks post MI for single‐nucleus RNA sequencing analysis. Sequencing data were processed through alignment, dimensionality reduction, clustering, and marker‐gene identification to define cell populations and gene expression profiles. Gene Set Variation Analysis and transcriptional regulatory network analyses were performed to identify altered signaling pathways and key transcription factors.ResultsA total of 16 341 nuclei were classified into 5 major cell types: neurons, oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, oligodendrocyte progenitor cells, and microglia. Functional analyses identified microglia as the primary mediators of inflammatory responses in the PVN. Gene Set Variation Analysis revealed substantial pathway alterations across cell types, with microglia exhibiting marked activation of immune‐related and cytokine‐producing pathways in MI rats. Moreover, IRF5 (interferon regulatory factor 5) was identified as a master transcriptional regulator associated with inflammatory activation and was significantly upregulated in PVN microglia after MI. Increased IRF5 expression in PVN microglia was confirmed by immunofluorescence.ConclusionsSingle‐nucleus RNA sequencing identified distinct cell‐specific gene signatures, regulatory networks, and signaling pathways in the PVN during heart failure, with microglial IRF5 emerging as a central regulator of immune activation and inflammatory processes. Activated IRF5 promotes microglial activation and neuroinflammation, thereby enhancing PVN neuronal activity and driving sympathetic and neurohumoral dysregulation in rats with MI. Targeting IRF5 and its downstream pathways may therefore provide new insights into the central inflammatory mechanisms contributing to cardiac dysfunction during heart failure progression.

Heart failure (HF) is a complex condition characterized by the inability of the heart to pump blood effectively, leading to inadequate perfusion of tissues and organs. Myocardial infarction (MI)‐induced HF triggers a sustained inflammatory response that extends beyond the heart and profoundly impacts the brain. 1 , 2 This inflammation engages the heart–brain axis, a bidirectional communication network through which cardiac injury influences neural control mechanisms. Circulating inflammatory mediators generated after MI can access the brain, including the hypothalamus and brainstem, and activate resident immune cells such as microglia and astrocytes, promoting neuroinflammation that enhances sympathetic outflow and neuroendocrine release. 3 The chronic sympathetic and neurohumoral activation drive maladaptive cardiac remodeling, including myocyte hypertrophy, fibrosis, and impaired contractility, further straining the already compromised heart. Thus, neuroinflammation not only represents a central response to peripheral injury but also serves as a critical driver of the pathological feedback loop that links cardiac dysfunction with excessive sympathetic and neurohumoral activation in HF progression. 4

The paraventricular nucleus (PVN) of hypothalamus, a key cardiovascular and autonomic brain center, plays a crucial role in regulating sympathetic activity, neurohormonal secretion, and hemodynamic responses, all of which profoundly affect cardiovascular function. 5 , 6 , 7 The excitatory milieu within the PVN, advanced by inflammatory mediators, oxidative stress, and elevated levels of neurotransmitters such as norepinephrine, arginine vasopressin, and corticotropin‐releasing hormone, contributes to increased sympathetic outflow, activated renin‐angiotensin system (RAS), elevated stress hormone levels, and impaired vagal tone. Understanding the dynamic signaling pathways within the brain, particularly in the PVN, could provide new avenues for targeted interventions aimed at mitigating the adverse effects of sympathetic and neurohumoral dysregulation to improve cardiac outcomes.

Immune activation and inflammation play a key role in the pathogenesis and progression of chronic HF. Increased expression of inflammatory mediators—including cytokines such as TNF‐α (tumor necrosis factor‐α), IL (interleukin)‐1, IL‐6, and chemokines such as stromal cell‐derived factor 1 and MCP‐1/CCL2 (monocyte chemoattractant protein‐1), is notably observed in both the circulation and the brain following MI. 8 In particular, these inflammatory processes within the PVN exacerbate cardiac dysfunction by promoting neurohumoral activation, sympathetic outflow, impaired baroreflex function, and arrhythmogenesis. However, the cellular and molecular mechanisms driving brain inflammation in the setting of HF remain largely unexplored. Following MI, the injured heart communicates with the brain through both neural and humoral pathways, modulating transcriptomic programs and cellular signaling within these central regions. Therefore, cell‐specific transcriptional profiling of central autonomic nuclei may provide critical insights into these processes.

Recent advances in single‐nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA‐seq) have provided a powerful tool to investigate transcriptomic regulation at cellular resolution, enabling the identification and characterization of distinct cell populations, regulatory pathways, and genetic phenotypes. 9 , 10 In the brain, snRNA‐seq allows the analysis of gene expression dynamics and transcriptional changes, offering insights into the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying neuronal circuitry, synaptic plasticity, and related processes in both health and disease. 11 In particular, studies using snRNA‐seq have revealed distinct neuronal subtypes in the PVN 12 , 13 along with cell‐specific expression patterns involving neurotransmitter receptors, neurotransmitters and ion channels. 14

In this study, we performed snRNA‐seq analysis of the PVN in a MI rat model induced by left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD) ligation to delineate gene expression alterations and elucidate molecular mechanisms driving sympathetic and neurohumoral activation. Particularly, we focused on gene regulatory networks and signaling pathways associated with immune responses and inflammation. Specifically, the analysis identified IRF5 (interferon regulatory factor 5), a member of IRF family, in PVN microglia as a previously unrecognized core transcription factor contributing to neuroinflammatory processes in the development of MI‐induced HF.

METHODS

The authors declare that all data supporting the findings of this study are available within the article and its Supplemental Material. Additional information regarding the detailed experimental methods and analyses is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Animals

This study was conducted strictly in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The experimental protocols regarding the animal use were approved by the University of Iowa Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and all possible measures were taken to minimize both the number of animals used and any potential suffering they might experience. Experiments were carried out on male adult Sprague–Dawley rats (250–300) purchased from Envigo/Harlan (Indianapolis, IN) and housed in a temperature‐ (23 °C±2 °C) and light‐controlled (12:12‐hour light–dark cycle) animal care facility on the University of Iowa Healthcare Campus. The animals had ad libitum access to standard rat chow and tap water.

Induction of MI and Echocardiographic Assessment

Rats were anesthetized (ketamine 90 mg/kg and xylazine 10 mg/kg IP) and underwent surgery under aseptic conditions to ligate the left coronary artery to induce MI, or an identical surgical procedure without ligating the left coronary artery to produce control (SHAM) rats, as previously described. 6 , 15 Within 24 hours after coronary artery ligation or sham surgery, rats were tranquilized with ketamine (60 mg/kg, IP) and left ventricular (LV) function was evaluated by 2‐dimensional echocardiography, as previously described. 15 , 16 The ischemic zone (IZ) as a percentage of LV circumference (% IZ), LV ejection fraction, and LV end‐diastolic volume were calculated from the echocardiographic data. Compared with SHAM rats, MI rats had reduced LV ejection fraction and increased LV end‐diastolic volume and LV end‐systolic volume (Table 1).

Acquisition of PVN Tissue

Under anesthetized conditions, rats were decapitated, and the brains were immediately removed and frozen in liquid nitrogen 2 weeks after MI. The frozen brain was subsequently cut into 300‐μm coronal sections and bilateral PVN were obtained using a punch device (inner diameter 1.5 mm, Stoelting, Wood Dale, IL). The PVN tissues from 3 SHAM or 3 MI rats were pooled together to form a single sample. Subsequently, the pooled PVN samples for SHAM and MI were subjected to single‐nucleus isolation.

Single‐Cell Nucleus Isolation and Counting

The cell nuclei in PVN were isolated using the 10× Genomics Chromium Nuclei Isolation Kit with RNase Inhibitor (cat no. PN‐1000494) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, the frozen tissue samples (20–30 mg) were transferred to prechilled sample dissociation tubes. Lysis Buffer (200 μL) was added, and the tissue was dissociated with a pestle. Then, an additional 300 μL of lysis buffer was added, and the mixture was incubated on ice for 10 minutes. The dissociated tissue was pipetted onto an assembled nuclei isolation column and collection tube, followed by centrifugation at 16 000 g, 4 °C for 20 seconds. The column was discarded, and the collection tube was vortexed for 10 seconds to resuspend the nuclei. After centrifugation at 500 g, 4 °C for 3 minutes, the supernatant was removed, and the pellet was resuspended with debris removal buffer (500 μL) and then centrifuged at 700 g, 4 °C for 10 minutes. After removal of the supernatant, the nuclei were resuspended in 1 mL of wash and resuspension buffer and centrifuged at 500 g, 4 °C for 5 minutes, and this step was repeated once. The nuclei pellet was then resuspended in 50 to 500 μL of wash and resuspension buffer, vortexed for 3 seconds, and the final nuclei concentration was determined using the LUNA‐FL TM Automated Fluorescence Cell Counter. The nuclei suspension was adjusted to the appropriate concentration for library construction.

Library Construction and Sequencing

snRNA‐seq libraries were prepared using chromium single cell 3′ reagent v2 kits (10× Genomics, Cat# PN‐120267), following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, a suspension containing ∼17 000 single‐cell nuclei was loaded onto each channel of the chromium single cell controller instrument (10× Genomics) to generate the gel bead emulsion (GEM). The goal was to capture nuclei from 10 000 cells per sample. Upon dissolution of the Single Cell 3′ Gel Bead in a GEM, primers containing (1) an Illumina R1 sequence (read 1 sequencing primer), (2) a 16‐nt 10× barcode, (3) a 10‐nt unique molecular identifier, and (4) a poly‐dT primer sequence, were mixed with cell lysate and master mix. After incubation with GEMs, barcoded, full‐length cDNA from polyadenylated mRNA was produced. Then, the GEMs were broken, and the pooled fractions were recovered. Silane magnetic beads were used to remove leftover biochemical reagents and primers from the post‐GEM reaction mixture. Full‐length, barcoded cDNA was then amplified by T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio‐Rad). Sufficient mass of barcoded cDNA was generated for library construction. The enzymatic fragmentation and size selection were used on barcoded cDNA before library construction. Sample index and read primer sequences were added during library construction via end repair, A‐tailing, adaptor ligation, and polymerase chain reaction. The final libraries contained primers used in Illumina bridge amplification. The resulting libraries were quality controlled and then sequenced by the NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina).

Data Processing for Sequencing

Cell Ranger software (v.7.0.1, 10× Genomics) was used for sequence alignment and gene quantification. In brief, Illumina sequencing files in .fastq format were aligned to the rat genomic sequence (introns and exons) using Rnor_6.0 annotation. Nuclei were identified above background, and gene expression was quantified by the Cell Ranger software, yielding filtered genes‐by‐cells expression matrices suitable for downstream data analysis. 17

Cell Clustering and Identification

The unique molecular identifier count matrix was analyzed using the R package Seurat (v. 4.2.0). Quality control was conducted based on the following excluding criteria: (1) cells with gene counts <500 or >3000; (2) cells with RNA molecule counts <800; (3) cells with mitochondrial gene expression rates >10%; and (4) cells with ribosomal gene expression rates >5%. After applying these quality control criteria, the remaining cells were included in subsequent analyses. The filtered unique molecular identifier matrix underwent dimensionality reduction, clustering, and visualization processing.

Data were proceeded in the following steps: first, the normalize‐data function was used to balance the sequencing depth; next, the find‐variable‐features function was applied to identify highly variable genes; and subsequently, the scale‐data function was used for data normalization. Principal component analysis was then performed to identify the top 10 principal components in the unique molecular identifier matrix for dimensionality reduction. Following this, t‐distributed stochastic neighbor embedding and uniform manifold approximation and projection algorithms were employed for visualization and expression similarity analysis after dimensionality reduction. The find‐neighbors function was used to determine neighborhood affiliations for clustering, and the find‐clusters function computed cell clustering at different resolutions. In this study, a resolution parameter of 0.3 was employed for cell identification. At this resolution, the Find‐All‐Markers function was used with min.pct=0.3 and logFC.threshold=0.25 as cutoff values to calculate differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between clusters. DEGs with a P value <0.05 (adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method) were considered as marker genes for individual clusters. Cell identification was conducted by comparing these DEGs with known cell markers with the PanglaoDB database and documents. 18 , 19 A list of cell type markers used was provided in Table S1.

Single‐Cell Gene Set Variation Analysis

Single‐cell gene set variation analysis (GSVA) is a nonparametric, unsupervised method for estimating the variation of gene set enrichment through the expression matrix, allowing for the evaluation of pathway enrichment for each cell. The GSVA package (v. 1.44.5) was employed to analyze the single‐nuclei data. 20 Initially, the data were normalized using the normalize‐data function in the Seurat package. Then, the GSVA function was employed with default parameters to compute GSVA scores for each cell. To meet the specific requirements of our study, we customized the reference gene sets by filtering terms from the REACTOME and Gene Ontology Biological Process databases. The criteria for filtering gene sets included keywords related to inflammation, sympathetic nervous system, RAS, and signaling pathways. Terms associated with cell division, tumors, development, viruses, and infections were excluded from these customized gene sets. Within each cluster, differences in pathway scores between the MI and the SHAM were compared using the limma package, with a significance cutoff of P<0.05. A list of these customized gene sets was provided in Table S2.

Gene Regulatory Network Analysis

The pySCENIC package (v. 0.12.1) in a Python environment with default parameters was used to compute and construct the gene regulatory network. 21 In brief, the process involved initial calculation of the coexpression between transcription factors and genes and a subsequent transcription factor motif analysis based on the mm9 RcisTarget database containing motifs with genome‐wide rankings. Then, the AUCell method was employed to score the activity levels of each regulon in the individual cells, with scores ranging from 0 to 1. The differences in regulon scores between the MI and the SHAM were evaluated using the limma package in R with the default configuration. P<0.05 was usually considered statistically significant. The regulatory network was visualized with Cytoscape (v. 3.7.1). A list of the constructed regulons is provided in Table S3.

Enrichment Analysis

Enrichment analysis of marker genes in each cluster and transcription factor regulating genes was conducted using the Gene Ontology enricher function in the R package clusterProfiler (v. 4.4.4). 22 The P value cutoff was set at 0.05 for the analysis. The enrichment results were visualized using the ggplot2 R package (v. 3.4.0), with a display of the top 10 enriched terms in general. Because pathway enrichment analyses involved testing a large number of hypotheses, both unadjusted P values and Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate‐adjusted P values were calculated and reported in Table S4. Given the exploratory nature of this study and the relatively small sample size with modest signal‐to‐noise characteristics, nominal P values were used to identify candidate pathways, and adjusted values are provided to inform the stringency of statistical significance. This approach was intended to balance sensitivity and false discovery control, enabling the identification of biologically relevant signals for subsequent validation.

Validation of IRF5 Expression by Immunofluorescent Staining

Rats were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with a saline flush, followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde. The brains were quickly removed and postfixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, then cryoprotected by immersion in 30% sucrose for 48 hours at 4 °C. Subsequently, the brains were frozen in optimal cutting temperature compound on dry ice and sectioned coronally at a thickness of 20 μm, ensuring inclusion of the PVN. The sections were incubated with primary antibodies, including rabbit anti‐rat IRF5 (1:300, PA5‐19504, Thermo Scientific), along with mouse anti‐rat CD11B (1:100, MCA275R, Bio‐Rad), mouse anti‐rat NeuN (neuronal nuclei; 1:200, MAB377, Millipore), or mouse anti‐rat GFAP (glial fibrillary acidic protein; 1:400, 3670S, Cell Signaling), for 48 hours at 4 °C. Subsequently, the sections were probed with secondary antibodies: Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti‐rabbit IgG (1:200, #A‐11008; Invitrogen) and Alexa Fluor 568 goat anti‐mouse IgG (1:200, #A‐11031; Invitrogen) for 1 hour at room temperature. Immunofluorescent staining was visualized using a confocal microscope. For each animal, a minimum of 4 PVN sections were randomly selected. Colocalized expression of IRF5 with CD11b or NeuN in the PVN was quantified by 2 operators blinded to the experimental conditions using ImageJ software. The number of positive cells in the 4 PVN sections of each animal was averaged to obtain an individual value for data analysis.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R4.2.2 and GraphPad Prism 11. The correlation analysis between Regulon scores and GSVA scores was conducted using the Pearson correlation method, with a P value <0.05 considered statistically significant. For assessing specific single‐cell gene differential expression between MI and SHAM, the normalized data excluding zero values were subjected t tests, with a P value <0.05 considered statistically significant. A 2‐tailed unpaired Student's t test was used for comparations between MI and SHAM in functional validation experiments and a permutation test was used for comparations between MI and SHAM in immunofluorescent analysis. P<0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Characterization of Cell Types in the PVN

snRNA‐seq was performed as outlined in Figure 1A, beginning with the isolation of individual nuclei from the PVN, followed by library preparation and sequencing. Processing of the sequencing data using the Cell Ranger software suite resulted in the identification of 16 341 high‐quality cells, comprising 8444 from the SHAM group and 7897 from the MI group, after exclusion of low‐quality nuclei. Unsupervised clustering analysis subsequently resolved these cells into 10 distinct clusters.

Based on identified DEGs known as cluster‐specific marker genes, we classified the nuclei into 7 major cell types (Figure 1B): neurons (n=10 145), oligodendrocytes (n=1787), astrocytes (n=1333), interneurons (n=1381), oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (n=759), microglia (n=531), and endothelial cells (n=405). The relative proportions of these cell types are shown in Figure 1C, with neurons representing the largest fraction (62.1%) and endothelial cells the smallest (2.5%). Cell type classification was determined using three well‐established DEGs uniquely associated with each cluster (Figure 1D).

Furthermore, we identified several genes with cell type–restricted expression that have not previously been reported in these populations, suggesting their potential as novel markers. These representative genes include: Il1rapl2 in neurons (Figure 1E), encoding the IL1RAPL2 (IL‐1 receptor accessory protein like 2) 23 , 24 ; Prex2 in astrocytes (Figure 1F), a guanine nucleotide exchange factor for Rac1 that mediates downstream effector activation 25 ; Grm3 in oligodendrocytes (Figure 1G), encoding a metabotropic glutamate receptor 26 ; Col5a3 in oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (Figure 1H), which encodes an α‐chain of fibrillar collagens 27 ; and Inpp5d in microglia (Figure 1I), a phosphatase involved in inositol phosphate signaling, immune regulation, and inflammatory responses. 28 , 29

Cell‐Specific Gene Ontology Pathway Enrichment Analysis in the PVN

Gene Ontology pathway enrichment analysis was performed to characterize the biological functions of individual cell types within the PVN. In neurons, the enriched pathways were predominantly associated with the regulation of transsynaptic signaling, glutamate receptor activity, receptor‐mediated signaling, and ligand‐gated ion channel signaling (Figure 2A). These pathways underscore the principal role of PVN neurons in excitatory neurotransmission and neuronal activity, processes that may contribute to autonomic activation and neuroendocrine release. In contrast, astrocyte‐enriched pathways were primarily related to the regulation of ErbB4, inositol lipid, VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor), PI3K/Akt (phosphoinositide 3‐kinase/protein kinase B), ERK1/2 (extracellular signal‐regulated kinases 1/2), and GPCR (adenylate cyclase–mediated G protein‐coupled receptor) signaling (Figure 2B), suggesting that astrocytes in the PVN are closely involved in the synthesis and regulation of growth factors and second messenger systems.

In oligodendrocytes, enriched pathways included stress‐activated protein kinase, PI3K/Akt, VEGF, insulin receptor, inositol lipid, and MAPK (mitogen‐activated protein kinase)‐mediated signaling (Figure 2C). These pathways indicate that, in addition to their well‐established role in myelination and axon ensheathment, oligodendrocytes contribute to broader processes such as cellular metabolism, growth, and protein synthesis within the PVN. The snRNA‐seq analysis also identified oligodendrocyte progenitor cells, an understudied population in the PVN. Enriched pathways in oligodendrocyte progenitor cells were primarily associated with transforming growth factor‐β receptor signaling, netrin‐1 signaling, and transsynaptic signaling (Figure 2D), suggesting potential roles in neuronal differentiation, axon guidance, and modulation of neurotransmission.

Consistent with their well‐established role as the brain's resident immune cells, snRNA‐seq analysis revealed that microglia in the PVN were enriched for pathways involving IL‐3/IL‐5/GM‐CSF (granulocyte‐macrophage colony‐stimulating factor), Fc receptor, interleukin, and T‐cell receptor signaling, all of which are integral to inflammation, immune responses, and cytokine production (Figure 2E). In addition, microglia exhibited enrichment of pathways related to Flt3, neurotrophic tyrosine receptor, and stress‐activated protein kinase signaling, indicating broader functions in supporting neuronal homeostasis, neurotrophic signaling, and brain development.

Differential Inflammatory Pathways in the PVN Between MI and SHAM

To investigate potential alterations in inflammatory signaling during HF progression, GSVA scoring and differential pathway analyses were performed to compare MI and SHAM groups. The top 5 upregulated pathways for each cell types are presented in Figure 2F.

In neurons, MI was associated with significant upregulation of IL‐27 signaling and downregulation of pathways negatively regulating IL‐8 production, indicating robust activation of proinflammatory processes within the PVN. Additionally, protein kinase C signaling was markedly upregulated, further implicating enhanced neuronal excitability and inflammatory signaling in MI. Astrocytes displayed upregulation of angiotensin‐activated signaling pathways, consistent with previous reports implicating activation of the brain RAS in MI. Notably, astrocytes are a known source of angiotensinogen, the precursor of angiotensin II, supporting their potential role in sustaining local renin‐angiotensin activity. In oligodendrocytes, IFN‐α (interferon‐alpha)–related immune signaling was among the most significantly upregulated pathways, suggesting their involvement in neuroimmune interactions in the context of HF.

Microglia exhibited a strong immune activation profile, with 4 of the top 5 upregulated pathways associated with cytokine production and immune responses, including IL‐3/IL‐5 and GM‐CSF signaling, PI3K signaling, TLR2 (toll‐like receptor 2) signaling, and Shc1‐mediated epidermal growth factor receptor signaling. The only downregulated pathway among the top 5 was associated with CBL (casitas B‐lineage lymphoma)‐mediated E3 ubiquitin ligase activity. These findings highlight microglia as key mediators of neuroinflammation in the PVN during HF progression.

Collectively, these results demonstrate cell type–specific inflammatory responses in the PVN in MI, with neurons and microglia exhibiting pronounced proinflammatory activation and astrocytes and oligodendrocytes contributing to complementary neuroimmune mechanisms. These findings underscore the pivotal role of neuroinflammation within the PVN in the development of HF.

Cell‐Specific Regulons in the PVN

A regulon, defined as a set of genes regulated by a common transcription factor, provides critical insights into cell type–specific regulatory networks. Transcriptional regulation within the PVN was assessed using regulon scores generated through single‐cell regulatory network inference and clustering analysis. A heatmap illustrates several major regulons identified across distinct cell populations (Figure 3A). For example, Mef2b(+) and Nr2e1(+) activity was enriched in astrocytes, Olig1(+) and Nkx6‐2(+) in oligodendrocytes, Luzp2(+) in neurons, and Tal1(+) and Maf(+) in microglia. These cell‐specific regulon profiles likely contribute to the unique functional properties of each cell type in adapting to the pathophysiological environment following MI. Violin plots of some of these regulons further demonstrate their marked variability in transcription factor activity across these populations (Figure 3B through 3F).

To further elucidate the role of Tal1(+) in microglia, we performed enrichment analysis of Tal1(+) regulon target genes specifically expressed in this cell type (Figure 3G). The Tal1(+) regulon was significantly associated with pathways regulating inflammatory responses, including immune activation, TLR signaling, and IL‐mediated signaling. These findings underscore a central role for Tal1‐driven transcriptional activity in microglia‐mediated neuroinflammation within the PVN in MI, particularly associated with HF progression. Moreover, Tal1(+) target genes were enriched in pathways positively regulating the ERK1/2 cascade, consistent with our previous work implicating ERK1/2 signaling in sympathetic activation and enhanced brain RAS in HF. 30 , 31

Identification of Differential Regulons in PVN Microglia in MI

Figure 4A depicts the intersections of differential regulons identified across various cell types in MI. Although many regulons were shared among ≥2 cell types, each population also retained a distinct set of regulons, underscoring the cell type–specific regulatory programs engaged in response to MI. Within PVN microglia, we identified 7 differential regulons in MI, comprising 5 upregulated and 2 downregulated regulons (Figure 4B through 4H). Notably, the activity scores of Rest(+), Otud4(+), Zfp958(+), Irf5(+), and Wrnip1(+) were significantly elevated in MI compared with SHAM, suggesting activation of transcriptional networks involved in immune and stress responses. In contrast, the activity scores of Nr2f2(+) and Foxn2(+) were markedly reduced in MI, indicating suppression of regulatory pathways potentially linked to homeostatic or anti‐inflammatory functions.

Association of Irf5(+) With Inflammatory Signaling Pathways in PVN in MI

To elucidate the functional relevance of the differential regulons identified in PVN microglia of MI, we performed a correlation analysis between these regulons and the most significantly activated pathways in microglia (Figure 5A). All 5 upregulated regulons in MI rats exhibited positive associations with key inflammatory pathways, whereas the 2 downregulated regulons showed negative correlations. Among the upregulated regulons, Irf5(+) demonstrated the strongest association, with correlation coefficients >0.4 across multiple inflammation‐related pathways. These pathways included positive regulation of IL‐1/IL‐1β production, ERK/MAPK signaling targets, IL‐3/IL‐5 and GM‐CSF signaling, TLR2 signaling, and signaling mediated by Cbl—collectively underscoring their involvement in microglial inflammatory responses in the context of HF.

Scatterplot analysis (Figure 5B) revealed a robust positive correlation between IRF5 activity and the pathway governing IL‐1 production (r=0.46, P=1.13×10−28), highlighting its potential role in driving proinflammatory cytokine synthesis. Similarly, IRF5 activity strongly correlated with IL‐3/IL‐5 and GM‐CSF signaling pathways (r=0.48, P=1.42×10−31; Figure 5C). IL‐3 and IL‐5 are known to promote the activation and proliferation of immune cells, including macrophages, whereas GM‐CSF, produced by infiltrating immune cells at sites of inflammation, augments neutrophil and macrophage function. 32 These findings suggest that IRF5 acts as a critical transcriptional regulator linking microglial activation to inflammatory amplification within the PVN in the HF progression.

Comprehensive analysis of the Irf5(+) regulon revealed 223 target genes that are cisregulated by the core transcription factor IRF5. The relative distance of each gene from IRF5 within the network reflects the predicted strength of its regulatory association. Transcription factor binding motifs represent specific genomic sequences recognized by transcription factors. Although consensus transcription factor binding motifs sequences exhibit variability at certain nucleotide positions, they retain highly conserved bases at critical sites essential for transcription factor binding. Figure 6B illustrates the consensus DNA binding motifs for IRF5, displaying motif logos for the forward strand (above the dashed line) and reverse strand (below the dashed line). These motifs are predominantly located within ~1 kb upstream or downstream of IRF5‐regulated genes. Binding of IRF5 to these motifs facilitates transcriptional activation of its target genes.

The activity score of a regulon reflects its transcriptional potential to regulate associated gene expression. Notably, IRF5 activity was predominantly enriched in PVN microglia, with only sparse activity detected in other cell types (Figure 6C). This finding indicates that the transcriptional program governed by IRF5 is primarily microglia‐specific within the PVN in MI. Furthermore, expression of IRF5 itself was significantly upregulated in PVN microglia under MI conditions (Figure 6D), whereas its expression in neurons and astrocytes remained largely unchanged. These results collectively highlight the central role of IRF5‐mediated transcriptional regulation of inflammatory responses in PVN microglial activation in MI.

To validate the cell‐specific expression of the transcription factor IRF5 within the PVN, we performed immunofluorescent staining in both MI and SHAM animals. Confocal microscopy images revealed robust IRF5 protein expression in PVN microglia (Figure 7A), whereas no detectable IRF5 signal was observed in the astrocytes in either MI or SHAM rats (Figure 7B). Additionally, IRF5 was also localized in PVN neurons in both MI and SHAM rats (Figure 7C).

Quantitative analysis revealed that IRF5 protein expression was significantly upregulated in PVN microglia of MI rats compared with SHAM controls (Figure 7D), corroborating the elevated IRF5 transcript levels identified by snRNA‐seq analysis in microglia cluster. In contrast, IRF5 protein expression in PVN neurons remained unchanged under MI conditions (Figure 7E). IRF5 protein was not expressed in PVN astrocytes under SHAM or MI conditions. Although IRF5 is also expressed in other brain regions, such as the cerebral cortex (Figure 8A), its fluorescence intensity was lower in the cortex compared with the PVN. To account for regional size and anatomical differences of the cortex, IRF5 expression was quantified at the cellular level by assessing IRF5‐positive cells colocalized with CD11b+ microglia within defined imaging fields under identical acquisition settings rather than comparing whole‐region fluorescence intensity. Quantitative analysis of IRF5‐positive cells colocalized with microglia in the cortex revealed no significant difference between MI and SHAM rats (Figure 8B). These findings indicate that IRF5 upregulation following MI is region specific and particularly pronounced in the PVN, rather than reflecting a generalized increase across the brain. This region‐selective upregulation provides strong evidence that IRF5 in PVN microglia regulates downstream transcriptional programs that drive microglial activation and neuroinflammation in the context of HF.

DISCUSSION

The PVN is a critical integrative hub mediating neuroinflammation‐driven sympathetic excitation and neurohumoral dysregulation in HF. 34 In this study, we used snRNA‐seq analysis to comprehensively profile PVN cell populations, classify major cell types, and identify novel cell type–specific genes and enriched functional pathways in a rat MI model 2 weeks after LAD ligation. This time point represents a pivotal stage of MI‐driven deterioration in cardiac function, pulmonary congestion, and progression of myocardial remodeling, 35 , 36 , 37 providing an optimal window to capture robust transcriptomic changes in genes and signaling pathways associated with neuroinflammation during HF progression. Accordingly, the identified differentially expressed genes and signaling pathways reflect compensatory roles in neurotransmission, immune regulation, homeostatic maintenance, and growth factor production in the context of HF. Importantly, we delineated cell type–specific transcriptional alterations in response to MI, including differential pathway activation and the predominance of unique regulons across PVN cell types. Our analysis revealed that the most prominent changes in the PVN were associated with immune and inflammatory signaling, with microglia exhibiting particularly robust transcriptional remodeling. Specifically, we identified IRF5 as a key transcription factor selectively upregulated in PVN microglia following MI, suggesting a pivotal role in orchestrating inflammatory cytokine production and immune‐related signaling cascades that promote neuroinflammation‐driven sympathetic outflow and neurohumoral activation during HF progression. These findings underscore the complex, cell‐specific molecular interactions underlying neuroinflammation within the PVN and highlight microglial activation as a potential mechanistic link between central inflammation and peripheral cardiac dysfunction. Collectively, our results provide novel insight into heart–brain axis regulation of cardiac function in HF and establish a foundation for future studies targeting neuroimmune pathways to mitigate disease progression.

To ensure accuracy and reliability of the data set, stringent quality control measures were applied to retain only high‐quality PVN nuclei, excluding cells with low transcript counts, abnormally high gene expression, or elevated mitochondrial and ribosomal gene signatures. This filtering minimized technical artifacts and provided a robust foundation for downstream clustering and pathway analyses. In this work, snRNA‐seq analysis identified seven major cell types within the rat PVN, with neurons constituting 62.1% of all nuclei and microglia accounting for 3.2% (8.4% of nonneuronal cells), consistent with previous reports in rats. 38 In contrast, single‐cell studies of the mouse PVN have reported lower neuronal (38%) and microglial (2%) proportions. 12 , 13 likely reflecting species‐specific differences, variations in overall neuronal versus nonneuronal composition, and differences in sampled PVN subregions. Importantly, our analysis uncovered numerous novel genes with cell type–specific transcripts in the PVN. For instance, Snhg11, Ryr2, and Nrxn3 were highly enriched in neurons; Itih3, Slc4a4, and Gfap in astrocytes; Mog and Mag in oligodendrocytes; and Itgam and Cx3cr1 in microglia. Although many of these genes are implicated in immune processes in peripheral disorders, 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 their roles within the brain remain poorly understood. Additionally, we identified several previously unreported genes that may serve as novel markers for discrete brain cell populations, including Inpp5d for microglia, Prex2 for astrocytes, and Grm3 for oligodendrocytes. Collectively, these findings provide valuable resources for mapping PVN cellular diversity and lay the groundwork for future studies on cell type–specific mechanisms underlying neuroinflammation and brain–heart interactions.

Enrichment analysis represents a fundamental approach in bioinformatics, enabling rapid identification of functional patterns within differentially expressed genes. By examining enriched pathways across distinct cell types in the PVN, we gain critical insights into their specialized roles under both physiological and pathological conditions. Please note that the pathway‐level findings should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating insights rather than definitive statistical evidence, given the potential for false positives inherent to multiple testing. In this study, Neurons exhibited enrichment in transsynaptic signaling, ion channel activity, and glutamate receptor pathways, highlighting their central roles in neurotransmission, neuronal excitability, and neurohumoral regulation. In addition, strong enrichment in Ras, adenylate cyclase–mediated G protein‐coupled receptor, calcium, and second messenger signaling pathways suggests that neuronal populations may engage diverse intracellular cascades that differentially shape PVN output. Oligodendrocytes, classically recognized for axonal myelination, displayed enrichment in stress‐activated protein kinase and PI3K/Akt signaling, implicating broader contributions to cellular metabolism, protein synthesis, and adaptive responses to stress. Astrocytes showed enrichment in pathways related to growth factor synthesis and second messenger signaling, consistent with their roles in neurotrophic support, extracellular homeostasis, and modulation of neuronal function. Oligodendrocyte progenitor cells exhibited enrichment in pathways linked to neuronal differentiation, neurotransmission regulation, and inflammatory modulation, indicating that their role extends well beyond oligodendrogenesis and myelination.

Microglia, the resident immune cells of central nervous system (CNS), demonstrated robust enrichment in immune response and cytokine production pathways, underscoring their central role in neuroinflammation and immune‐mediated signaling within the PVN. This finding is particularly relevant in the context of disease, where microglial activation is a major driver of neuroinflammatory responses. 44 , 45 , 46 Taken together, these pathway‐specific enrichments reveal the molecular heterogeneity of PVN cell types and provide a framework for understanding how distinct cellular populations contribute to PVN function. Importantly, they highlight potential mechanisms by which altered cellular signaling may drive pathological states such as MI‐induced HF, offering new directions for mechanistic investigation and therapeutic targeting.

Indeed, the intricate transcriptional networks regulating inflammatory responses highlight the critical contribution of microglia within the PVN to neuroinflammation in HF progression. In this study, GSVA of inflammation‐related pathways revealed that multiple PVN cell types engage in signaling cascades linked to immune activation and cytokine production in MI‐induced HF. Strikingly, IL‐27 signaling was activated in neurons, suggesting a potential role in post‐MI cardiac remodeling, consistent with reports of increased IL‐27 levels in circulation and cardiac tissue following MI. 47 , 48 Similarly, enrichment of the regulation of Ifna signaling pathway in oligodendrocytes points to an adaptive contribution of these cells in sustaining central inflammatory responses during HF progression. Most notably, microglia displayed the strongest activation of proinflammatory pathways, including IL‐1/IL‐3, GM‐CSF, IL‐5, TLR2, and Shc1 signaling, underscoring their central role in orchestrating and propagating neuroinflammation within the PVN. 32 Collectively, these findings demonstrate that, although microglia are the dominant drivers of CNS inflammation in MI‐induced HF, neurons and glial subtypes also contribute through distinct transcriptional programs, thereby revealing a multifaceted cellular network that integrates immune signaling with neurohumoral dysregulation in HF progression.

Regulons, defined as sets of genes coordinately regulated by a transcription factor, serve as valuable markers for delineating cell clusters, identifying activated transcriptional programs, and mapping signaling pathways. 49 Single‐cell regulatory network inference and clustering analysis integrates scRNA‐seq data with transcription factor regulatory information, thereby enabling the reconstruction of gene regulatory networks and elucidation of gene expression dynamics across diverse cell types. 50 These analyses provide critical insights into how transcriptional regulators orchestrate cellular processes and shape responses to pathological stimuli or homeostatic perturbations. 51 In the present study, we identified a unique regulon, Tal1(+), that was selectively enriched in microglia. The gene networks regulated by Tal1(+) were predominantly linked to immune activation and inflammatory signaling, further implicating Tal1 as a key driver of microglial proinflammatory programs. Notably, Tal1(+) regulons also mapped to the positive regulation of ERK/MAPK signaling, suggesting that Tal1 may function upstream to potentiate ERK‐driven responses. Given that MAPK signaling in the PVN is known to contribute to sympathetic hyperactivity, endoplasmic reticulum stress, RAS activation, and neuroinflammation in MI‐induced HF, it is plausible that Tal1 serves as a transcriptional node coupling microglial inflammatory gene expression with MAPK pathway activation. Such a mechanism would amplify both local neuroinflammation and downstream neurohumoral dysregulation, thereby exacerbating HF progression. 30 , 31 , 52

Activated regulons capture distinct transcriptional programs and exhibit cell type–specific regulatory responses under disease conditions. Although many regulons are shared across different cell types, others are uniquely restricted to specific populations, reflecting specialized roles in pathophysiology. In this study, we identified 7 unique regulons in microglia that underwent significant alterations in MI rats 2 weeks after LAD ligation. These regulons highlight the transcriptional reprogramming of microglia in response to cardiac injury and suggest that microglia adopt disease‐specific regulatory states. Among them, correlation analysis revealed IRF5 as the most prominent regulon, encompassing the core transcription factor IRF5 and >200 downstream target genes enriched in immune and inflammatory pathways.

IRF5 is known for orchestrating antiviral responses, stress‐induced apoptosis, and lipopolysaccharide‐mediated neuroinflammation. 53 , 54 , 55 Mechanistically, IRF5 promotes the production of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6, which can disrupt synaptic function and induce neuronal injury. 56 Importantly, IRF5 expression in the CNS is largely restricted to microglia, where it operates downstream of the TLR/MyD88 signaling axis. 57 This positioning suggests that IRF5 acts as a central transcriptional node driving microglial activation and neuroinflammation in the setting of MI‐induced HF.

The regulatory network connecting the IRF5 regulon to its downstream target genes, as inferred from our snRNA‐seq data set using pySCENIC analysis, 33 represents a previously unreported finding. Notably, many of the IRF5‐regulated genes identified here have established roles in neuroinflammatory processes associated with neurodegenerative diseases. For instance, CSF1R‐mediated inflammatory signaling has been strongly implicated in the pathogenesis of Parkinson's disease, and the adaptor protein Blnk has been associated with CNS inflammatory pathways, 58 Furthermore, Arhgap15, a gene selectively expressed in microglia, has been linked to seizure‐related neuroinflammation, 59 Together, these findings suggest that IRF5 coordinates a broad transcriptional profile that potentiates immune activation within the CNS. To our knowledge, this study is the first to implicate IRF5 as a key transcriptional regulator in PVN microglia under MI‐induced HF conditions. Consistent with the transcriptomic findings, immunofluorescent staining confirmed that IRF5 is predominantly expressed in microglia, but not astrocytes, and is markedly upregulated in PVN microglia, but not in the cortex, following MI. However, we acknowledge that variation in the selection of cortical regions or other brain nuclei may influence broader regional comparisons, representing a limitation, as multiple brain regions were not systematically examined. Although IRF5 is also detected in neurons, its expression in neuronal populations did not significantly differ between MI and SHAM rats, suggesting that the MI‐associated regulation of IRF5 is primarily within the microglia. Nonetheless, whether neuronal IRF5 contributes to neuroinflammatory signaling or other aspects of PVN dysfunction remains an open question requiring further investigation.

It is important to note that the brain is not directly injured by MI, and snRNA‐seq is inherently constrained by technical limitations, including expression dropouts in which certain transcripts are not consistently detected across individual cells. 48 , 49 Moreover, because central neural regulation during HF progression largely reflects a compensatory response rather than a primary site of injury, transcriptional changes in the PVN may be modest and not fully captured within the 2‐week window following MI. As a result, some molecular signals driving neuroimmune interactions may remain below the detection threshold at the single‐cell levels. Nevertheless, a potential mechanistic link between myocardial injury and PVN IRF5 activation likely involves convergent neural and humoral pathways that promote microglial activation and IRF5‐dependent transcriptional reprogramming, thereby amplifying neuroinflammation and contributing to sympathetic and neurohumoral dysregulation during HF progression.

CONCLUSIONS

The identification of the IRF5 regulon in PVN microglia, along with its downstream gene network, provides compelling evidence for a transcriptional profile underlying neuroinflammatory signaling in MI‐induced HF progression. This network includes genes with established roles in microglial activation, immune signaling, and cytokine production, suggesting that IRF5 serves as a key player integrating peripheral injury cues with central inflammatory responses. Beyond mapping transcriptional activity, the present findings establish a reference atlas of microglial regulatory pathways during HF progression, offering novel insights into the cellular and molecular mechanisms of neuroinflammation. Importantly, these results highlight IRF5 and its downstream targets as potential therapeutic entry points for mitigating neuroimmune activation and sympathetic overdrive in the setting of HF.
