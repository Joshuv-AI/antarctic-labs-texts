School-Based Health Centers Addressing Health Equity for LGBTQ + Patients (SBHCs HELP): protocol for a stepped-wedge trial to implement innovations promoting structural competency
Authors: Cathleen E Willging, Daniel Shattuck, Marisa Sklar, Rachel A Sebastian, Robert L Stout, Mary M Ramos
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSchool-based health centers (SBHCs) provide vital behavioral, sexual, and reproductive healthcare services to school-aged youth across the United States. Adolescents who are sexual and gender diverse (SGD) are far more likely to suffer from adverse health outcomes than their cisgender and heterosexual peers. Emerging structural competency frameworks call for cultivating capacities in SBHCs to modify organizational service delivery environments, including provider and staff knowledge and behaviors, to influence SGD adolescent well-being. Nationally recognized guidelines for nurturing structural competency include (1) adopting, disseminating, and adhering to SGD supportive policies and procedures; (2) creating welcoming physical environments; (3) systematically documenting and using sexual orientation and gender identity information in clinical care; (4) training all employees in best practices for interacting with SGD patients; and (5) developing the clinical workforce to deliver high-quality services to SGD patients. This community-engaged study will test the effectiveness of the Dynamic Adaptation Process (DAP) in implementing these guidelines in SBHCs in culturally and geographically diverse areas of New Mexico.MethodsWe will conduct mixed-method readiness assessments to identify inner- and outer-context determinants affecting the implementation of structurally competent changes in SBHCs; employ a stepped-wedge trial to examine how the DAP-enabled implementation impacts adoption and changes in SBHC, student (patient), and implementation outcomes; and investigate inner- and outer-context determinants, bridging factors, and associated mediators and moderators influencing implementation processes and outcomes related to guideline adoption and SGD student care (e.g., reduced barriers, greater satisfaction and engagement).DiscussionThis study addresses the long-term goal of high-quality care and decreased health disparities for SGD youth. As investments in SBHCs rise nationwide, opportunities to enhance services for SGD youth will also grow. This study will demonstrate the usefulness of a multifaceted implementation strategy, the DAP, in helping SBHCs build structural competency to serve a sizeable population of students affected by stigmatization, discrimination, and other social forces that create inequities in health. Accordingly, we will advance a model featuring a set of implementation strategies to reduce knowledge and practice gaps, create welcoming environments, and improve the quality of care for SGD youth.Trial registrationISRCTN13844475; 20 September 2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11785-4.Keywords: Equity, Disparities, Dynamic Adaptation Process, Implementation, Sexual and gender diverse, School-based health centers, Stigma, Structural competency

Background

In the United States (U.S.), sexual and gender diverse (SGD) youth—young people identifying as lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, or of other sexualities and genders (LGBTQ+)—experience many behavioral, sexual, and reproductive health disparities [1]. They have suicidal behavior levels three to four times that of cisgender and heterosexual youth and much higher rates of depression, non-suicidal self-harm, and overall poorer mental health [2–4]. They are also at higher risk of alcohol [3–10] and other substance use [11–14], which contributes to poorer health as adults [15, 16]. Further, SGD youth are more likely to have an earlier age for their first sexual encounter, more lifetime sexual partners, no condom use at last sexual encounter, and unintended pregnancy [13, 17, 18]. Additionally, they are at increased risk of sexual violence and physical dating violence, compromising sexual and reproductive health [18–21]. Despite some changes in social acceptance and visibility of SGD people, these disparities are not appreciably diminishing for many SGD youth [22], and may be intensifying due to political initiatives criminalizing gender-affirming care and removing discussion of SGD issues in schools [1].

Structural factors—stigma, discrimination, social, political and economic marginalization, and rurality—influence health and healthcare for SGD people. In healthcare settings specifically, experiences or expectations of bias and discrimination stem from stigma and provider unpreparedness to support SGD youth, making patients less likely to get care [23]. Fears of disclosing one’s SGD identity, being judged, or being misunderstood may also hinder the use of care [24]. These fears may be well-grounded, with research showing that actual or perceived discriminatory attitudes among primary care providers lead to worse treatment or health outcomes for SGD people [25]. Exclusionary environments shaped by institutional norms and values add to disparities for SGD youth [17, 26–29]. Finally, rurality can affect their well-being. Studies of SGD adults associate living in rural areas with increased obesity [30], depression [31], and tobacco use [32]. Rurality factors affecting health include distance to care [33] and provider shortages [34], in addition to perceived increased anti-SGD stigma and inadequate legal protections [35]. The growing literature on rural SGD youth reveals higher rates of victimization, discrimination, feeling unsafe, depression, and suicidality compared to urban dwellers [36, 37].

In a systematic review, most SGD youth experienced stigma in healthcare settings and few pediatricians felt equipped to care for them [38]. In pediatric contexts, there is a chasm in knowledge about SGD patient care, including pre-exposure and post-exposure prophylaxis, puberty blockage, hormonal or surgical transitioning, electronic health record (EHR) logistics, and referral sources for specialty care and social support [39]. Prominent professional sources (e.g., the American Academy of Pediatrics) disseminate SGD guidelines for pediatric settings [40–42]. Yet, training in SGD care concerns (e.g., bullying, confidentiality, coming out, family rejection, gender identity development, puberty, sexual and reproductive health) is wanting [28, 39]. Sexual orientation and gender identity data are required to tailor prevention and treatment efforts, improve provider-patient rapport, and reduce disparities [43]. In SBHCs, such data can aid health promotion and SGD-affirming care and render an invisible population visible [44]. However, clinics are often slow to adopt such innovations due to challenges in developing and implementing routine systems to gather, store, and access data, including via EHRs [45–47].

SBHCs are a critical safety net in the healthcare system for youth, especially in rural areas. On school campuses, they offer comprehensive and developmentally appropriate services in the place where youth spend most of their time. Many provide an integrated model of primary and behavioral healthcare by an interdisciplinary team that collaborates with school staff to address the service needs of students and improve health and academic outcomes [48]. SBHCs care for youth who might otherwise lack access due to financial and non-financial barriers [49]. They often provide free or low-cost care to students regardless of insurance status, and many assist students and families with obtaining medical insurance (e.g., Medicaid, State Children’s Health Insurance Program). Moreover, most SBHCs are administered by federally qualified health centers and can be found in areas with high poverty rates [50]. Their location helps eliminate barriers caused by geography (e.g., living in a designated health professional shortage area, transportation). Access to confidential services is essential to addressing the unique barriers to care youth face [51]. Outside of SBHCs, few youth routinely receive comprehensive health screening, preventive counseling, and private visits with providers [52]. SBHCs fill this void through access to confidential services and private time with providers, delivering behavioral, sexual, and reproductive healthcare without involving potentially unaccepting parents or guardians [53]. Although studies of SGD student care in SBHCs are scarce, available findings suggest that SBHCs are well-positioned to improve services for this structurally vulnerable adolescent population [54].

Theoretical foundation

This study is foregrounded in the theoretical concept of structural competency, which holds that health equity can be achieved by addressing upstream factors (e.g., stigma, discrimination, inadequate and fragmented treatment, and other social determinants of health) affecting SGD student health [55–58]. The absence of structural competency in SBHCs may inhibit students from disclosing their SGD status in clinical encounters, contribute to subpar care and patient dissatisfaction, and hinder SBHC service utilization and patient retention due to student fears and experiences of being judged, gossiped about, or bullied in the larger school community [38]. Through strategic efforts to nurture structural competency drawing from implementation science, SBHCs can improve the care received by SGD youth and, thus, their health. Such efforts entail increasing awareness and ability within SBHCs to modify organizational service delivery environments (e.g., provider and staff knowledge and behaviors) to tackle barriers to SGD inclusivity and well-being. Challenges to cultivating such competency in any healthcare setting include a lack of implementation leadership, insufficient clinical preparedness, poor understanding of what equitable treatment looks like, and time and resource constraints that diminish motivation and overall capacity for organizational change efforts [59, 60]. Leveraging structural competency and dismantling institutional barriers are critical to overcoming knowledge and practice gaps influencing service delivery environments and clinical interactions to address SGD student vulnerabilities, thereby improving health outcomes [28, 61].

Study objectives

This four-year community-engaged implementation science study seeks to optimize the experiences of SGD students receiving SBHC care by implementing five changes to service delivery environments likely to promote structural competency. Based on nationally recognized recommendations or guidelines, the changes include (1) adopting, disseminating, and adhering to SGD supportive policies and procedures; (2) creating welcoming physical environments for SGD patients; (3) systematically documenting and using sexual orientation and gender identity information in clinical care; (4) ongoing training for all employees in best practices for interacting with SGD patients; and (5) developing the clinical workforce to encourage the delivery of high-quality services to SGD patients [62]. Our interdisciplinary research team will use a multifaceted implementation strategy—the Dynamic Adaptation Process (DAP) [63, 64]—to introduce and facilitate the adoption of the guidelines in a statewide SBHC network in the largely rural, economically challenged, and culturally rich state of New Mexico. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the DAP is informed by the Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, Sustainment (EPIS) Framework [65, 66]. It comprises four key components: initial assessment, partner engagement and training, problem-solving, and outcomes feedback [63, 64]. In keeping with the EPIS Framework, we will consider inner-context (e.g., organizational climate, provider/staff attitudes), outer-context (e.g., policies, funding, inter-agency environment), bridging (e.g., community partnerships), and innovation (e.g., fit) factors affecting implementation using quantitative (surveys and organizational metrics) and qualitative (ethnography and interviews) data sources. Our recent work using the DAP to implement six SGD-inclusive practices in New Mexico high schools to improve student health equity resulted in statistically significant adoption rates (p < .000), underscoring the model’s effectiveness as an implementation strategy (Willging CE, Shattuck D, Ramos M, Richard B, Dickson E, Aarons G: Leveraging the Dynamic Adaptation Process to address LGBTQ + health equity in New Mexico high schools, Under Rev).

Exploration and preparation phases

During this pre-implementation period, the research team will conduct baseline readiness assessments to implement the guidelines in participating SHBCs. For this study, readiness can be understood in terms of motivation (beliefs about and support for the guidelines, which contribute to desires to adopt them), general capacity (referring to aspects of organizational functioning, including culture, climate, staff capacity, and leadership), and innovation-specific capacity (or human, technical, and fiscal conditions such as knowledge, skills, and other innovation-related abilities that can enable successful implementation), as described below [67].

After the assessments, the SBHCs convene implementation resource teams (IRTs) of SBHC personnel, school community members (e.g., students, parents, teachers), and research team members (e.g., coaches). The research team will provide the IRTs with feedback reports summarizing key findings from the assessments of their respective SBHCs. These reports will include contextual details for nuanced implementation planning through the DAP, including the strengths and weaknesses in SBHCs that may influence the implementation and uptake of each guideline. To further prepare for implementation, the IRTs will have access to modular training webinars on the DAP, LGBTQ + Health Disparities, LGBTQ + Inclusive Healthcare Practices, Structural Competency, and Implementation Leadership. Adaptation will be integrated into the training for IRTs, attending to (a) why/what to adapt concerning the guidelines, (b) what not to adapt, (c) when to seek advice on adaptation, and (d) how to use IRTs for implementation support. In addition, the IRTs will be furnished with a toolkit of resources adapted from our previous work to prioritize guidelines to carry out, weighing each on feasibility and impact dimensions [62]. The IRT members will receive continuing education units to complete webinars and can avail themselves of free training opportunities identified in the toolkit.

Implementation and sustainment phases

As the IRTs enter the Implementation phase, they will meet with coaches with specialized expertise in SGD healthcare and inclusive practices, adolescent health, and implementation planning. The coaches will begin holding monthly to bimonthly visits with the IRTs to help them hone their knowledge and skills, create action plans, and meet implementation goals for the five guidelines. Training will continue during the one-year implementation period, expanding reach to include other SBHC personnel and school community members. At the same time, the research team will repeat the readiness assessments with all schools and collect data to track progress and impacts.

The coaches will draw upon the assessment data to assist the IRTs in enacting and monitoring outcomes of their action plans, helping them think through successes, challenges, necessary adaptations to the guidelines, resources to support efforts (e.g., community collaborations), and next steps. Adjustments to action plans may also occur, such as adding materials and training in response to unique SBHC-level barriers (e.g., limited discrimination policies, negative staff attitudes). Coaching support will be withdrawn upon the conclusion of the implementation period. However, SBHCs are still expected to maintain efforts to support guideline uptake and utilization as they enter the Sustainment phase.

Methods/design

Community academic partnership and youth advisory council

This study features a Community-Academic Partnership (CAP) and a Youth Advisory Council (YAC). The 10-person CAP includes officials from the state departments of health and education and SBHC and SGD advocacy organizations. It will facilitate SBHC buy-in, professional staff development, and linkages to community resources (e.g., free technical assistance on new practices). We will also establish an 8-person YAC for high school students who use SBHC services. The research team will update both bodies at quarterly meetings (virtual and in-person) on study progress and barriers. Both groups will help tailor technical assistance materials and measures and offer feedback on data collection, analysis, interpretation, and dissemination. The research team will deliver age-appropriate training to the YAC to maximize its contributions.

Study context

New Mexico ranks 44th in median household income [68], with the fourth-highest poverty rate in the U.S. (17.6%) [69]. Hispanic/Latinx and Native American people represent 60% of residents [70]. About 7.5% of adults [71] and 16.3% of high schoolers [72] identify as sexual minorities; 0.67% of adults [73] and 5.8% of high schoolers [72] identify as gender minorities. The state defines SGDs as a protected class of citizens and bans insurance exclusions for transgender people to promote gender-affirming care [74]. Still, access barriers (e.g., rurality, stigma) and cultural competence deficits contribute to health and healthcare disparities for SGD people [75–78]. There are 45 SBHCs in the state-funded network serving high schools that receive medical oversight and direction from a sponsoring agency (e.g., federally qualified health center, healthcare facility). Over 90% of the SBHCs are in health professional shortage areas for primary care, behavioral health, or both, including in otherwise underserved rural communities [79].

Study design

For this stepped-wedge implementation trial, we will use a sequential mixed-methods design [80] to support and study the effectiveness of the guidelines described in Table 1. We will recruit 24 SBHCs serving school communities that vary in student population size, ranging from a couple hundred students to a couple thousand, and geographic location (e.g., urban, rural). We will use stratified or balanced randomization that accounts for these characteristics to assign each SBHC into three cohorts of eight sites. The cohorts will be successively activated to use the DAP to implement the guidelines over 12 months. Engagement with the DAP will, therefore, commence at differing start dates, with each SBHC taking part in three conditions: (a) standard practice, (b) DAP-enabled implementation of guidelines, and (c) sustainment of the guidelines. Repeated measurements of key outcomes will occur as the SBHC moves through the conditions. By the trial’s end, all SBHCs will have received the intervention (DAP-enabled implementation), although the time they enter the intervention condition will be determined randomly. Trial design

Our trial design allows for modeling the intervention’s effect over time within participants and between participant intervention effects. Assessment of outcomes will occur at five time points. Data for Cohort 1 will include one assessment during standard practice, one during DAP-enabled implementation of practice domains, and three during the sustainment period. For Cohort 2, there will be two assessments during standard practice, one during the DAP-enabled implementation of structurally competent guidelines and two during the sustainment period. For Cohort 3, data collection includes three assessments during standard practice, one during the DAP-enabled implementation of guidelines, and one during the sustainment period.

From this design, there are two main comparisons for evaluating the effect of the DAP: the within-SBHC effects (i.e., across phases) and the between-SBHC effects (i.e., across SBHCs). It is critical to note that baseline measurements (before engagement in DAP-enabled implementation and at the same point in calendar time) will provide statistical control of factors that systematically differ across SBHCs (e.g., readiness for change). We hypothesize that there will be greater improvements with DAP-enabled implementation relative to standard practice over time for all outcomes of interest.

Through the trial, we will assess the extent to which SBHCs build structural competency through the successful implementation of the guidelines and examine SBHC (motivation, general capacity, and innovation-specific capacity), student (perceptions of SBHC setting and barriers to, satisfaction with, and engagement in care), and implementation (adoption, acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility) outcomes. The Pacific Institute for Research and Evaluation has granted ethics approval for this trial, covering informed consent and assent procedures.

Sample

After recruiting the 24 SBHCs, we will invite their staff to participate in DAP processes and data collection activities consisting of surveys, observations, and interviews. Most SBHCs in New Mexico have between three and five personnel, including a medical provider, a behavioral health provider, and a care coordinator or other staff (e.g., medical assistants). We anticipate the participation of 96 SBHC personnel per school year (36 medical providers, 24 behavioral health providers, and 36 clinic coordinators and other staff). We will also recruit students who use services at the participating SBHCs to complete patient surveys after clinical visits (described below). We anticipate the completion of 20 patient surveys per SBHC during each assessment period for a total of 2400 surveys.

Quantitative data collection and analysis

At each of the five assessment time points, quantitative data will be collected from all participating SBHCs using the LGBTQ + Practices Checklist to measure our primary implementation outcome and the SBHC Staff Survey and the Student Patient Survey to measure secondary outcomes.

Primary implementation outcome and measurement

Our primary outcome is the adoption of guidelines. We will determine adoption and fidelity to the guidelines using the LGBTQ + Practices Checklist, which rates each guideline’s core elements as either “present,” “absent,” “not applicable,” or “unknown.” While SBHCs are in the standard practice and sustainment periods, the SBHC’s point of contact will be asked to complete the checklist either on their own or with the assistance of a researcher. When SBHCs are in their implementation period, the IRT will complete the checklist with their coach. We will calculate the total adoption rate for each SBHC as the proportion of checklist items across guidelines that are present divided by the total checklist items. (See Additional File 2 for LGBTQ + Practices Checklist.) Preliminary psychometric testing of the checklist items demonstrated acceptable test-retest reliability for most items and a high level of internal consistency for items in most domains. In small group interviews, participants of the checklist test-retest procedure found it to be easy to complete, comprehensive, and informative as a tool to assess the extent to which their own SBHCs were providing supportive and affirming care for LGBTQ + students.

Secondary outcomes and measurement

We will invite SBHC personnel across cohorts to participate in a self-administered online SBHC Staff Survey, including before the site’s movement into the DAP-enabled implementation condition and after DAP-engagement. This 20-minute survey will examine key issues affecting the readiness of SBHCs and their personnel to implement guidelines for structurally competent care for SGD patients, the extent to which readiness may change over time, and other potential determinants of change (e.g., SBHC needs and strengths, implementation barriers and facilitators). SBHC personnel will receive a $25 gift card each time they complete the survey.

The SBHC Staff Survey will collect demographic information and examine secondary outcomes at the level of SBHC personnel, wherein readiness is determined by staff motivation to adopt an innovation, general organizational capacities, and innovation-specific capacities [67]. We will explore motivation using the Attitudes toward Lesbians and Gay Men Scale (α = 0.80) [81], Bisexualities: Indiana Attitudes Scale-Abridged (α = 0.91) [82], Attitudes toward Transgender Individuals Scale (α = 0.95) [83], and the Evidence-Based Practice Attitude Scale (α = 0.79) [84]. To understand general capacity, we will use five subscales of the Organizational Readiness to Change Assessment Context module (α = 0.86) on staff organizational culture, formal leadership and teambuilding, evaluation of goal setting and tracking and communicating performance, readiness for change (e.g., attitudes of opinion leaders for practice change), and resources (e.g., availability of funds, staff time, facilities, and equipment) to support changes broadly [85]. To assess innovation-specific capacity, we will apply the Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual, and Transgender Development of Clinical Skills Scale (α = 0.86) [86] and the Implementation Climate Scale (α = 0.91) [87]. To assess implementation outcomes, we will examine attitudes relevant to specific guidelines via the Acceptability of Intervention Measure (α = 0.85), Intervention Appropriateness Measure (α = 0.91), and Feasibility of Intervention Measure (α = 0.89) [88].

The anonymous, 5-minute Student Patient Survey will examine perceptions of SBHC settings, experiences with SBHC providers, barriers to care, satisfaction with care, and engagement in care among service users [89]. The surveys will be collected in all SBHCs over a one-month administration period, beginning at baseline and repeated in later semesters. Students who receive care at the SBHC during the survey administration period will be asked to participate. The survey will be self-administered on tablets in both English and Spanish. Students will only be eligible to complete the survey once during each administration period. Students will receive a $5 gift card each time they complete the survey.

The Student Patient Survey will measure the extent to which patients receive youth-centered care at SBHCs that is supportive and inclusive of diverse gender and sexual identities. The survey will include questions drawn from the Youth Experience of Care Scale, the Youth Health Engagement Scale, and Youth SBHC Involvement Scale [89, 90]. These tools will gather data on the quality of student-provider interaction and the provision of developmentally appropriate and youth-friendly care, including how often providers at the SBHC listen carefully to patients, explain things in a way they understand, show respect for what they have to say, and spend enough time with them. The Student Patient Survey will include additional items to measure care specifically supporting SGD youth.

Quantitative analysis

We will import data from the LGBTQ + Practices Checklist, SBHC Staff Survey, and Student Patient Survey into the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) for descriptive analyses aggregated at the SBHC level, comparing data across SBHCs to determine areas of relative strength and weakness regarding readiness to implement guidelines [91]. The surveys should prove instrumental in identifying individual and organizational factors that will support or inhibit the adoption of guidelines for developing structural competence when caring for SGD patients.

We will use hierarchical linear modeling for repeated measures for primary study analyses [92]. The effect of the DAP-enabled implementation strategy on adoption will be tested using standard strategies for discontinuous change [93]. At the level of repeated measurements (level 1) that are nested within SBHCs (level 2), the basic model will include a linear polynomial across all occasions, a dummy-coded indicator for standard practice, a dummy-coded indicator for measurements in the DAP-enabled implementation of structurally competent guidelines, and a dummy-coded indicator for measurements in the sustainment of the guidelines. Analyses will test for differences in baseline levels of adoption, change over time in adoption (within and between phases), and change in adoption when transitioning from active engagement with the DAP-enabled implementations strategy to the Sustainment phase. For this analysis, strong support for this strategy will be provided if there are no baseline differences and there is greater improvement in adoption with DAP-enabled implementation relative to standard practice. Planned comparisons will be specified to obtain statistical significance tests, and statistical controls will be incorporated at the corresponding levels of measurement, guided by preliminary analyses and including key participant demographics and SBHC characteristics.

In addition to the primary outcome of adoption, secondary outcomes of interest include SBHC staff and provider, student, and other implementation outcomes, as described above. Secondary outcomes will be structured with repeated measurements (level 1) nested within SBHCs (level 2). The basic model formulation for secondary outcomes of interest is similar to that of the primary outcome. DAP-enabled implementation will be compared to standard practice with models testing for differences in initial outcomes and change over time when transitioning from standard practice to DAP-enabled implementation.

Power analysis

To ensure an appropriate sample size, we used algorithms developed by Hemming and Taljaardo (2016) [94] to estimate the number of clusters and participants to achieve 80% power to detect intervention effects under a range of assumptions about effect size, intraclass correlation coefficient (ICCs), and cross-time correlations. The number of SBHC clusters and steps in crossing over SBHCs from pre- to post-intervention is constrained by logistical considerations. To be conservative, we examined effect sizes for this study ranging from d = 0.8 to 2.0, ICCs from 0.02 to 0.10 based upon reviews of educational research, and cross-time correlations (r; before vs. after intervention) from 0.4 to 0.7. For d = 0.1 and r = .7, the number of clusters to detect an effect on the primary outcome at 80% power was projected to be 22, and for all higher combinations of the power parameters with 24 SBHCs is greater than 80%. For secondary outcomes tested at alpha = 0.0125, power is > 80% for d = 1.0 and r = .7, and for all larger effect sizes.

Qualitative data collection and analysis

To further examine implementation processes and complement quantitative findings, we will use baseline assessment data gathered through Rapid Assessment Procedure-Informed Ethnography (RAPICE) [95], ongoing ethnographic observations [96], and small group interviews with IRTs [97]. We will also employ the Stages of Implementation Completion (SIC) to assess the proportion and duration of important implementation progress milestones completed by each site [98].

Rapid assessment procedure-informed clinical ethnography

Upon transition of an SBHC into the DAP-enabled implementation condition, we will use RAPICE to complement and contextualize the results of the staff and patient surveys [95]. This ethnographic approach minimizes data collection burdens for participants while enabling the systematic gathering of rich in situ data on implementation environments, perceptions and behaviors of SBHC personnel, and patient experience. This approach involves: (1) forming a multidisciplinary research team that includes members with clinical and administrative expertise and ethnographic and mixed-methods training; (2) multiple data collection methods (i.e., ethnographic observation, informal and unstructured interviews, fieldnotes, and surveys [above]); (3) iterative data collection and analysis in real-time to facilitate continuous adjustment of research questions and methods; and (4) rapid triangulation.

RAPICE will deepen our understanding of current readiness and implementation levels and factors likely to affect future uptake. We will conduct 3–4 observation periods of 3–4 h within the SBHCs assigned to a cohort upon its activation. We will use in-the-moment observations as an elicitation device for conversations comprising the unstructured interviews. We will compile detailed notes and digitally record and transcribe the proceedings when circumstances allow. Topics to broach include past and present experiences related to implementing elements of the guidelines captured in the LGBTQ + Practices Checklist; perceptions of SBHC safety and inclusiveness; school and community contexts for SGD people; and perceived need for and ability to implement supportive interventions for SGD patients. The interviews will allow for targeted inquiry of inner- and outer-context determinants and bridging factors to address using the DAP to enable implementation [65, 66]. For each day of observation, our ethnographers will transcribe fieldnotes immediately after returning from the field and complete a summary matrix. Halfway through the RAPICE period, they will debrief with other RAPICE team members, including a pediatrician and a nurse with experience working in SBHCs and the implementation coaches. The purpose of these discussions is to share and gather input into the initial observations and interviews and facilitate cross-observer triangulation of collected data. This feedback will further guide data collection for the remaining RAPICE period. (See Additional File 3 for RAPICE Protocol Guide and Summary Template.)

Ethnographic observation

Coaches will conduct ongoing participant observation, a classic ethnographic method that requires researchers to be embedded and participate in the contexts that are the focus of inquiry [96]. Participant observation will complement RAPICE by allowing for continued qualitative data collection that minimizes participant burden. The intimate and detailed knowledge produced through the coaches’ engagement with IRTs and the larger SBHCs will enhance the research team’s ability to gather real-time data on implementation processes, facilitators, and barriers that quantitative methods or the focused ethnographic encounters of RAPICE may miss. Coaches will complete fieldnotes and logs to capture activities and take part in periodic debriefs with the multidisciplinary RAPICE team. In this way, qualitative data can be collected, interpreted, and used on an ongoing and iterative basis to understand the on-the-ground reality of implementation and influence the implementation process itself.

Small group interviews

We will conduct small group interviews with IRTs upon completing the Implementation phase to document their experiences with guideline implementation and overall progress and satisfaction with the DAP [97]. The objective is to better understand local implementation milieus and the DAP’s usefulness in enabling IRTs to build capacity, spearhead and implement adaptations, and monitor guideline implementation over time. The small group interviews will consist of four IRT members and occur on-site when IRTs regularly meet. Members will complete demographic forms (recording age, gender identity, sexual orientation, race/ethnicity, education, employment, socioeconomic status, etc.) and a 10-minute survey on individual perceptions and experiences related to the IRTs and the DAP. An interview will be re-categorized as a focus group if five or more members can participate. The small group interview guides will consist of ten to twelve open-ended questions lasting 90 to 120 minutes. During the interviews, IRT members will be prompted to collectively reflect on participant satisfaction, implementation of guidelines (e.g., contextual relevance, feasibility, strategies to overcome barriers, and processes for forging and maintaining local linkages with health, behavioral health, SGD resources for referral purposes), and perceived outcomes on the SBHCs, students, and implementation. Structured as a collective discussion [97, 99, 100], the interviews will clarify the inner- and outer-contextual factors influencing the IRTs’ ability to adhere to their original action plans, reasons for possible divergence from these plans (e.g., community resistance), and subsequent adaptations (e.g., community education).

Qualitative analysis

We will analyze the observation and interview data using the rigorous and accelerated data reduction (RADaR) technique to identify pattern formation and emerging themes [101]. This technique uses a matrix approach for team-based data reduction, coding, and interpretation. In RADaR, the data are prepared and transferred to data tables, and then through a series of iterative steps, the RAPICE team codes and reduces the data to refined clusters based on categories. At each step, the team meets and discusses their observations and interpretations of each code and cluster until agreement is met. These steps repeat until the categories are clustered again into clearly observable themes; at this point, the team meets to identify exemplar quotes and counterexamples and draft the preliminary findings [102]. We will continue interviewing until we observe pattern formation with themes, i.e., saturation, after which we will consider the data collection complete [103]. The CAP and YAB will review these preliminary findings to lend insights into the credibility and trustworthiness of the qualitative findings [99, 103]. The suggested modifications will be considered new data and integrated into the analysis cycle.

Stages of implementation completion

We will study implementation progress for each guideline via the SIC and the LGBTQ + Practices Checklist. This eight-stage measure will assess progress for pre-implementation (Exploration/Preparation), Implementation, and Sustainment phases using activity completion dates and duration of activities [98]. The research team completes the SIC by coding data from interviews, surveys, action plans, and coaching logs concerning markers or milestones. We will customize the SIC to capture key stages of implementation progress for each guideline, using the LGBTQ + Practices Checklist to determine when fidelity or competency is reached. These stages include (a) engagement; (2) consideration of feasibility; (3) readiness planning; (4) staff trained; (5) fidelity monitoring process in place; (6) implementation begins; (7) ongoing implementation; coaching, and feedback; and (8) competency. We will derive three scores from the SIC for each guideline: (1) number of stages completed, with the score being the last stage in which at least one activity is performed; (2) stage duration, i.e., time between date of completion of the first and last activity in a stage; and (3) proportion of activities completed in each stage, calculated as the number of activities completed divided by the number of possible activities. SIC scores will facilitate an understanding of which implementation milestones are predictive of the successful adoption of the guidelines.

Mixed-methods analysis and triangulation

We will use joint display analysis to integrate and analyze qualitative and quantitative findings [104, 105], visually presenting both sets of results within potentially related domains. For instance, we may display results from quantitative measures of innovation-specific capacities alongside qualitative findings on connections to/use of community resources, the presence of champions, or the role of stigma in SBHCs. Using this approach, we will place the results of each dataset side-by-side to examine: (1) convergence (do results provide the same answer to the same question, e.g., do findings from RAPICE and the LGBTQ+ Practices Checklist concur on baseline implementation levels for guidelines? ); (2) expansion (are unanticipated findings of one dataset explained by another, e.g., can survey data suggesting a disempowering implementation climate be explained by interview data? ); and (3) complementarity (does embedding results of the qualitative analysis in the quantitative dataset help contextualize results, e.g., will it explain variability represented by confidence intervals or variance estimates in statistical analyses on “organizational readiness” and “attitudes?”) [80]. Hypothesized mediating and/or moderating effects unveiled via qualitative inquiry (including proposed causal mechanisms and conditional influencers of effects) will be explored in quantitative analysis [106, 107]. Our inclusive process will value the input of key partners (IRT, CAB, and YAC members) to assess consistencies and discrepancies between qualitative and quantitative data and analyses to determine if we are capturing constructs most relevant to promoting structural competency via DAP-enabled implementation of the guidelines per the EPIS.

Discussion

As with most implementation science research, practical and operational issues may exist. Per the multilevel EPIS Framework, potential challenges exist at the individual, SBHC, and societal levels [65, 66]. Providers may resist practice change or harbor negative attitudes about guideline implementation. Their characteristics, i.e., job tenure, can also impact implementation. SGD students may avoid SBHCs due to parental notification concerns. SBHCs may also resist new practices due to time or financial constraints. The level of tangible organizational support for implementation improves effectiveness, while culture, climate, and leadership can influence staff willingness to take on new practices [108]. At the societal level, being SGD still carries a stigma that can lead to an increased need for structurally competent services [75, 109]. Stigma may also influence both the individual and institutional will to address disparities affecting SGD students. The community-engaged approach for this research and support from key partners will be incredibly helpful in enrolling SBHCs, retaining the participation of multiple SBHC programs, and overcoming such challenges.

With over 2500 SBHCs in virtually every state and with most based in secondary schools, SBHCs are vital to the U.S. adolescent healthcare landscape [50]. As investments in SBHCs rise nationwide, opportunities to enhance services for SGD youth will grow [110]. This study will demonstrate the utility of a multifaceted implementation strategy, the DAP, in enabling SBHCs to build structural competency to serve a sizeable population of students affected by stigmatization, discrimination, and other social forces that create health and healthcare disparities. Accordingly, we will advance a model and a set of implementation strategies to help build structural competency in SBHCs to reduce knowledge and practice gaps, create welcoming environments, and improve the quality of care for SGD youth.
