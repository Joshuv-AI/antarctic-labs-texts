Enhancing the accuracy of a multivariable prediction model to identify medical patients suitable for same day emergency care services
Authors: Catherine Atkin, Suzy Gallier, James Hodson, L Li, Felicity Evison, Vinay Reddy-Kolanu, Elizabeth Sapey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo assess the performance of the Glasgow admission prediction score (GAPS) and ambulatory score (AmbS) for identifying emergency department (ED) attendances suitable for medical same day emergency care (SDEC) services and to derive and validate a novel tool for this purpose, the SDEC Triage Tool (SDEC-T).MethodsA retrospective diagnostic study using routine healthcare data from three hospitals in a diverse urban setting (Birmingham, UK). All unplanned ED attendances by adults requiring internal medicine assessment were included. The primary outcome was suitability for SDEC, defined as discharged alive with a length of stay under 12 hours (LOS<12). The SDEC-T was derived using multivariable analysis, informed by stakeholder workshops.Results152 877 attendances were included (median age: 58 years; 54.3% female; 68.4% White ethnicity); LOS <12 was achieved in 45.0% (n=68 752). The GAPS and AmbS had moderate predictive accuracy, with areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROCs) of 0.741 (95% CI 0.738 to 0.744) and 0.733 (95% CI 0.730 to 0.736), respectively. The SDEC-T comprised elements of the GAPS, AmbS, National Early Warning Score 2 (NEWS2) and presenting complaint and achieved an AUROC of 0.850 (95% CI 0.845 to 0.854) on internal validation. Stakeholders considered the tool acceptable and suitable for deployment across settings.DiscussionThe SDEC-T demonstrated improved discrimination using routinely available variables, balancing predictive performance with clinical practicality. Its design supports implementation across hospitals with varying digital maturity.ConclusionsIn a diverse patient cohort, the SDEC-T outperformed existing tools for identifying patients suitable for medical SDEC services.

Introduction

Demand on emergency departments (EDs) is rising, resulting in overcrowding, long delays and poor patient outcomes.1 2 Most patients requiring referral and assessment have a medical issue requiring review by general and acute internal medical teams.3 Annual benchmarking audits suggest that performance against key quality indicators is deteriorating, including assessment by a clinical decision maker within 4 hours of arrival.4 5 To address this, healthcare organisations have developed new pathways to streamline and expedite clinical decision making, including same day emergency care (SDEC) services, which provide selected patients with specialist assessment and management within trolley or chair spaces, without admission but with the facility to return on subsequent days for further review.6 Under the UK’s National Health Service (NHS) Long Term Plan7 (2019), NHS England required every acute hospital with a type 1 ED (consultant-led 24 hour service) to provide SDEC services.7 8 There is an expectation that a third of all medical attendances can be managed without overnight admission and that this could be increased from a fifth in 2018 through the increased provision of SDEC.7 Since some patients assessed within SDEC will be found to require admission, this necessitates SDEC being used for more than 40% of unplanned medical attendances.6

Key to meeting these targets is the correct identification of patients suitable for SDEC as soon as possible after presentation. Bringing patients into SDEC who then require admission interrupts patient flow and can place patients at risk of harm if their care needs exceed those which can be provided by SDEC. Conversely, bringing patients into SDEC who could be discharged more rapidly from EDs creates delays in care without discernible benefit.9 Identifying SDEC-suitable patients can be achieved using risk stratification tools, with the Glasgow admission prediction score (GAPS) and the ambulatory score (AmbS) being two such tools that have been deployed in the UK.10–13 These tools were designed for use early within the assessment pathway to predict the likelihood of same-day discharge (AmbS) or admission to hospital (GAPS). Despite the AmbS being supported by the Royal College of Physicians,14 uptake of this and other selection tools has been low, as shown in recent benchmarking audits, which also described considerable variability in SDEC size, patient flows, discharges and staffing levels.4 15 A potential reason for the low use of the AmbS and GAPS nationally is their inconsistent performance when used in different settings.10 16–18 A recent study found that implementing the AmbS or GAPS to select patients potentially suitable for SDEC would have resulted in >45% of these patients subsequently requiring an inpatient admission.19

Using new tools to improve healthcare is a key component of the NHS Long Term plan,7 but adoption of innovation is variable.20 Stakeholder-driven innovation has been shown to increase adoption in healthcare settings.21 Consequently, developing tools that can accurately identify patients suitable for SDEC in different settings would be of great benefit; working with relevant stakeholders during the development process would increase the likelihood of widespread adoption. This study had four objectives: to establish the requirements of healthcare professionals, policymakers and service users for a SDEC selection tool; to evaluate the performance of two existing tools (the AmbS and GAPS); to develop and validate a novel tool, the SDEC Triage Tool (SDEC-T), for identifying patients suitable for medical SDEC; and to gather stakeholder feedback on the utility and performance of SDEC-T.

Methods

Ethics

The retrospective study was approved by the East Derby Research Ethics Committee (reference 20/EM/0158) and Health Research Authority (reference number 279353) and was conducted in accordance with the TRIPOD guidelines.22

Public and patient involvement

Two virtual workshops were organised, with attendance open to healthcare professionals, policymakers involved in designing SDEC services and the public. The first was held prior to obtaining ethical approval, which enabled co-development of the research questions and outcome measures. A second evaluation workshop was then organised with the same members after completion of the analysis, to present the novel tool and discuss its potential utility across different care settings. For the workshops, patients and members of the public were recruited from the PIONEER Acute Care Patient and Public Advisory Group, a national group made up of individuals who have lived experience of acute care services, who are recruited through open applications. Healthcare professionals were recruited using the Society for Acute Medicine, with advertisements via the Society for Acute Medicine newsletter, and via social media. The make-up of the group is reported in online supplemental table 1.

Setting

The data study included three hospitals within the University Hospitals Birmingham NHS Foundation Trust, which serve a diverse population of 2.2 million people in the West Midlands area, namely Birmingham Heartlands Hospital (BHH), Queen Elizabeth Hospital Birmingham (QEHB) and Good Hope Hospital (GHH).

Study population

All attendances to the three EDs by adults (aged 16+years) between 1 April 2023 and 31 March 2024 (inclusive) were retrospectively identified. Patients who were referred directly to an Acute Medical Unit (AMU) without first attending the ED (e.g. from the NHS 111 service or General Practitioners (GPs) were also included in the study. Attendances that did not require internal medical team assessment were then excluded, as these would not be considered for SDEC, specifically patients who:

Were referred from the ED to a surgical specialty, psychiatry, stroke medicine, trauma or the intensive care unit.

Were referred from the ED to a surgical specialty, psychiatry, stroke medicine, trauma or the intensive care unit.

Presented with suspected venous thromboembolism, as optimum management for these is via a well-established community pathway.

Attended AMU to receive a pre-planned administration of intravenous (IV) antibiotics.

Were only referred to a fracture clinic, knee or shoulder clinic or for physiotherapy.

Were discharged from the ED without an onwards referral.

Further details of the exclusion criteria are summarised in online supplemental figure 1.

Validation of the GAPS and AmbS tools was initially performed for the overall population. Prior to the derivation of the novel tool, the cohort was randomly divided into a derivation set (80%), which was used to produce the tool, and a validation set (20%) for internal validation.

Data extraction

Data were extracted from the electronic health records (EHR) system, including age, sex, self-reported ethnicity, the source of the ED referral and the mode of arrival. Any previous inpatient stays at the three study hospitals within either 30 days or 1 year were identified. The primary presenting complaint at triage was extracted and grouped into broader categories for analysis; this was not recorded in patients directly attending the AMU. The triage category (standard-, urgent- or immediate-level care) was additionally recorded and mapped to the Manchester Triage Score to calculate the GAPS; patients directly attending the AMU were treated as being triaged to standard-level care.23

The Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS), National Early Warning Score (NEWS) and NEWS2 were calculated using the first sets of observations recorded within 6 hours of arrival, with any IV prescriptions during this period noted. The times of arrival at the ED and discharge from hospital (to the nearest minute) were used to calculate the length of stay (LOS).

Primary outcome measure

Since the suitability for treatment in SDEC could not be readily quantified using retrospectively collected data, the LOS was used as a surrogate outcome. Attendances were deemed suitable for SDEC if patients were discharged alive with a LOS of<12 hours (LOS<12), as these could potentially have been treated and discharged on the same day. This is consistent with the outcome used when deriving the AmbS.12

Calculation of existing risk stratification tools

For the AmbS, it was not possible to identify whether patients had ‘access to personal transport/can take public transport’. However, all three hospitals can provide transport to patients; hence, all patients were deemed to meet this criterion. For the component regarding the ‘anticipation’ of IV treatment, it was assumed that such treatments administered within 6 hours of arrival would likely have been anticipated. Finally, for the components of both scores relating to previous inpatient admissions, only inpatient stays at the study hospitals could be identified from local EHR systems; hence, any admissions to other hospitals were not considered. Derivation of a novel tool

Analysis of the novel tool considered all components of the AmbS and GAPS (except for access to transportation), as well as the primary presenting complaint. However, the MEWS/NEWS were replaced with NEWS2, as this is now the acuity score mandated for use in NHS hospitals.24

Statistical methods

Attendances for which NEWS/MEWS scores were and were not recorded were compared using Mann-Whitney U-tests for continuous/ordinal factors or χ2 tests for nominal factors. The predictive accuracies of the GAPS and AmbS for the prediction of LOS <12 were assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and quantified using the area under the ROC curve (AUROC). For the derivation set, separate univariable binary logistic regression models were produced to assess the associations between each factor and LOS<12. A multivariable analysis was then performed using backwards-stepwise variable selection (removal at p>0.1) to identify independent predictors of LOS<12. The resulting model was then converted into a predictive score (SDEC-T). For nominal variables, reference categories were changed such that effects were in a positive direction, where possible. The natural logs of the odds ratios (LnORs) of each factor were then multiplied by a constant, to give values of a reasonable magnitude, and rounded to the nearest integer, to assign a point score to each component. Optimal threshold values for the SDEC-T, GAPS and AmbS were defined as those that maximised the Youden’s J statistic. The three tools were then applied to the validation set, with the predictive accuracy quantified by the AUROC, as well as measures of classification accuracy, using the previously defined thresholds.

Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS v29 (IBM Corp. Armonk, NY), with p <0.05 deemed to be indicative of statistical significance. Clustering by hospital was not considered when deriving the SDEC-T; however, validation was additionally performed for each hospital separately, to identify any variability in performance. Cases with missing data were excluded from univariable analyses of the affected factors; multivariable analyses used a complete-cases approach. Continuous variables were not found to be normally distributed and are summarised using medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs).

Results

Scoping workshop

Among the 31 workshop attendees, there was consensus that a tool to identify patients suitable for SDEC would be helpful to improve care and standardise practice. Most centres represented did not have any set criteria for selecting patients for SDEC, with the remainder using the NEWS2, Clinical Frailty Scale and one using AmbS. Specific concerns raised by attendees included perceived issues with incorporating frailty due to a noted variability in the ability to deliver SDEC for patients with frailty over time and issues with incorporating transportation that may be affected by urban versus rural location. For a novel tool, it was agreed that the following features were important:

Only incorporating data that were readily available and simple to measure on admission so that the tool could be used as soon as possible after presentation.

Only incorporating data that were readily available and simple to measure on admission so that the tool could be used as soon as possible after presentation.

The presenting complaint would be a useful addition and is currently taken into consideration in many centres when selecting patients for SDEC.

The presenting complaint would be a useful addition and is currently taken into consideration in many centres when selecting patients for SDEC.

The tool should be simple to calculate and to implement into electronic systems.

Performance of GAPS and AmbS

The MEWS and NEWS were unavailable for 11.3% (n=17 341) of the cohort due to no observations being recorded within 6 hours of arrival, and these attendances were excluded from the analysis. These excluded attendances were in patients who were significantly younger, less likely to be triaged as requiring urgent/immediate-level care and had a considerably shorter length of stay than the remainder of the cohort (online supplemental table 3). The AmbS was additionally incalculable in patients with missing data for sex (n=12) or acute confusion (n=5).

Both the GAPS and AmbS were significantly predictive of LOS<12, with AUROCs of 0.741 (95% CI: 0.738 to 0.744) and 0.733 (95% CI: 0.730 to 0.736), respectively. Subgroup analyses by hospital found the GAPS to have notably poorer performance for GHH (AUROC: 0.680 vs 0.759 for QEHB), while performance of the AmbS was more consistent across hospitals (online supplemental figure 2).

For the factors common to both tools, the SDEC-T was broadly consistent with the GAPS and AmbS, with two exceptions. First, acute confusion was not found to be a significant independent predictor of LOS<12 due to the low prevalence and correlation with the NEWS2; hence, it was not included in the SDEC-T. Second, referral from a GP was independently associated with a greater likelihood of LOS<12, which was the converse of the GAPS. The SDEC-T additionally incorporated the primary presenting complaint, with the likelihood of LOS<12 being lowest for psychological symptoms, and highest for chest pain.

SDEC-T validation

Prior to validating the SDEC-T, the optimal thresholds for the three tools were identified using the admissions from the derivation set, namely SDEC-T≥9, GAPS≤21, and AmbS≥6.5. For n=27 078 (88.6%) admissions from the validation set for which all three scores were calculable, the SDEC-T was strongly predictive of LOS<12, with an AUROC of 0.850 (95% CI 0.845 to 0.854), which was similar across the three hospitals (range: 0.845–0.858, (online supplemental figure 4)), and outperformed the GAPS (AUROC: 0.737, 95% CI 0.731 to 0.743) and AmbS (AUROC: 0.734, 95% CI: 0.728 to 0.740; online supplemental figure 5); this persisted across subgroups defined by age and the time of admission (online supplemental table 4). Using a threshold of SDEC-T ≥9 would result in 49.9% of attendances being deemed potentially suitable for SDEC and yield 84.6% sensitivity and 72.4% specificity (table 5).

Evaluation workshop

The SDEC-T was discussed by 27 attendees, reviewing performance and potential deployment. There was agreement that the inclusion of the presenting complaint was advantageous, as was the removal of the availability of transport, as all centres could offer transport if needed. The proposed cut-off score was thought to be reasonable. Overall, the workshop agreed that SDEC-T could be deployed in different settings and that performance was appropriate for use.

Discussion

This paper presents the development and validation of a novel tool for identifying patients suitable for treatment in SDEC, with input from healthcare professionals, policymakers and patient groups. Provision of SDEC services in the NHS has only become routine practice within the last 10 years, and has only recently become a key strategy within Urgent and Emergency Care.6 25 A recent scoping review examining the evidence base for adult medical SDECs in NHS hospitals only identified 18 studies, which were generally of low quality.26 Reports by the Society for Acute Medicine have described significant variation in the size, staffing models and approaches to patient selection of SDECs nationally.27 Unsurprisingly, there is also significant variability in the performance of SDECs, including both the proportion of medical patients triaged to these services and those discharged from SDEC.4

To our knowledge, this is the first study to propose a tool based on a regionally diverse population specifically intended for use in medical SDECs. The SDEC-T returned an AUROC of 0.850 on internal validation, which was superior to the GAPS and AmbS (AUROC: 0.737 and 0.734). In contrast to the GAPS, the SDEC-T had similar performance across the three hospitals despite considerable variability in patient demographics, giving greater confidence in the potential generalisability of the SDEC-T. The SDEC-T is highly usable in practice as it is based on a simple lookup table, which could either be completed manually or incorporated into an electronic system. The components of the tool were deliberately limited to variables routinely recorded at ED triage, selected to balance predictive performance with practicality and generalisability; therefore, data unavailability should be negligible in practice. However, three components of the SDEC-T, namely the anticipation of IV treatment, triage category and primary presenting complaint, are subject to a degree of subjectivity which may impact the tool’s performance. Future research should externally validate the applicability, utility and generalisability of the SDEC-T in prospective studies and in other hospital settings. The use of richer data and more complex models, including AI approaches, may improve performance but must be balanced against regulatory, governance and implementation requirements. Further work should consider SDEC operational capacity in response to increased patient volumes indicated by the study’s findings. A total of 45.0% of attendances met the surrogate outcome of LOS<12, with the suggested threshold for SDEC-T deeming 49.9% of attendances as potentially suitable for SDEC. The practical implications of accommodating such a volume need further investigation to ensure the operational viability of SDEC services.

Strengths and limitations

This study has key strengths, including the large sample size of attendances from three hospitals serving diverse populations. The primary limitation was the missing data for MEWS/NEWS/NEWS2, which resulted in 11.3% of cases being excluded. This introduced selection bias since patients included in the analysis tended to be older and more likely to require urgent/immediate care than the population without an acuity score. However, since those without an acuity score tended to be discharged directly from ED, it is likely that these patients would not be screened for SDEC as they did not require internal medicine assessment. second, data were unavailable for some of the components of the GAPS and AmbS, meaning that assumptions needed to be made when assigning points (eg, assuming the prescription of an IV drug within 6 hours of arrival could have been anticipated), which may have resulted in the predictive accuracy of these components being overestimated.

Additional components could be considered for incorporation but were not evaluated in this analysis due to lack of recording and availability early in the attendance process. For example, frailty scores were not included as a validated frailty score was not recorded in a standardised method within this EHR data, and indeed documentation currently varies nationally.28 Patients with frailty may be more likely to have had an inpatient admission in the previous 12 months, which was associated with reduced likelihood of LOS<12 hours. Given policy emphasis on increasing provision of frailty-focussed SDEC services,29 30 the relationship between frailty and SDEC suitability across the wider healthcare system may be complex and requires consideration in further research. Finally, due to the retrospective study design, LOS<12 was used as a surrogate of suitability for SDEC. However, this will have been imperfect as some patients with longer hospital stays may have been suitable for SDEC but been subject to delays, while some with shorter stays may still have been better served by being treated in the ED. As operational definitions and targets evolve nationally,6 the target length of stay may change, which may affect score performance; implementation in clinical practice requires consideration of monitoring requirements.

Conclusion

The SDEC-T has demonstrated greater accuracy than existing tools for identifying patients suitable for SDEC in a diverse UK cohort and may optimise healthcare resource allocation and improve patient outcomes in emergency settings.
