Background

Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) is a rare, multisystem antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis characterized by eosinophilic inflammation and small-to-medium vessel involvement. The disease may affect multiple organ systems, including the lungs, heart, kidneys, peripheral nervous system, skin, and gastrointestinal tract. Ocular manifestations have been described in ANCA-associated vasculitis, particularly in granulomatosis with polyangiitis, in which orbital inflammatory disease is relatively common. Orbital manifestations of EGPA may include dacryoadenitis, orbital inflammation, myositis, and episcleritis. However, lacrimal gland involvement and dacryoadenitis remain uncommon manifestations of EGPA.1–3 The 2022 ACR/EULAR classification criteria provide a standardized framework for EGPA classification, and the differential diagnosis of lacrimal gland enlargement includes IgG4-related disease, idiopathic orbital inflammation, sarcoidosis, lymphoproliferative disorders, and granulomatosis with polyangiitis.4 We report a rare case of unilateral dacryoadenitis presenting as mechanical ptosis in a 68-year-old man with EGPA.

Case Presentation

A 68-year-old man presented with a 3-week history of left upper eyelid edema and mechanical ptosis. The patient had initially been treated for a hordeolum without clinical improvement. His medical history was significant for hypertension treated with enalapril, childhood asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis, and mononeuritis, as well as prior occupational exposure to galvanoplasty for 38 years. Best-corrected visual acuity was 20/20 in both eyes. External examination revealed diffuse left upper eyelid edema with mechanical ptosis, an enlarged and hyperemic lacrimal gland associated with periocular inflammation, and conjunctival injection (Figures 1 and 2). Intraocular pressure was 22 mmHg bilaterally. Fundus examination revealed a cup-to-disc ratio of 0.7 in both eyes. Orbital computed tomography (CT) demonstrated enlargement of the left lacrimal gland with associated orbital inflammatory changes (Figure 3). The patient subsequently underwent an incisional biopsy of the lesion (Figure 4). Histopathologic examination demonstrated active small-vessel vasculitis with a mixed inflammatory infiltrate composed of mature lymphocytes, plasma cells, neutrophils, and eosinophils involving the lacrimal gland and surrounding soft tissues, without evidence of malignancy. Peripheral eosinophilia was not identified at the time of evaluation; however, eosinophilic infiltration was evident on histopathologic examination.

Clinical photograph of the left eye demonstrating mild superior-temporal conjunctival chemosis associated with orbital inflammatory involvement.

Adjacent osseous structures were preserved.

Incisional biopsy performed through a superior transpalpebral orbitotomy (A). Intraoperatively, the orbital portion of the lacrimal gland appeared enlarged, whitish, indurated, and friable, with a fish-flesh appearance suggestive of lymphomatous infiltration. The excised biopsy specimen is shown (B). Due to the size of the lesion and the risk of injury to adjacent structures, complete excision was not attempted.

Serologic evaluation demonstrated positive p-ANCA and negative c-ANCA antibodies. ANA testing was negative. Peripheral eosinophilia was not identified at the time of evaluation. The erythrocyte sedimentation rate was within normal limits, whereas high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) was elevated. Chest radiography showed no significant pulmonary abnormalities. The diagnosis was established following multidisciplinary evaluation by the Ophthalmology and Rheumatology services. Induction therapy consisted of six cycles of intravenous cyclophosphamide (1 g) combined with intravenous methylprednisolone (250 mg), administered every 21 days, resulting in marked clinical improvement (Figure 5). At the 4-month follow-up, the patient showed sustained resolution of eyelid edema and mechanical ptosis, with no clinical evidence of recurrent orbital inflammation.

Discussion

Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) is a rare ANCA-associated small-vessel vasculitis characterized by eosinophilic inflammation and multisystem involvement. Ocular manifestations are uncommon and are reported less frequently than in granulomatosis with polyangiitis. Reported ophthalmic manifestations include orbital inflammation, dacryoadenitis, episcleritis, retinal vasculitis, ischemic optic neuropathy, and orbital pseudotumor-like disease. Ocular and orbital involvement appears to occur more frequently in ANCA-positive patients, suggesting a more prominent vasculitic phenotype associated with orbital inflammatory disease.5 In the present case, positive p-ANCA serology together with histopathologic evidence of eosinophilic-rich small-vessel vasculitis strongly supported the diagnosis. Systemic corticosteroids and immunosuppressive therapy remain the cornerstone of treatment in severe orbital involvement associated with EGPA.5,6 Our patient demonstrated marked clinical improvement following induction therapy with cyclophosphamide and methylprednisolone.

The differential diagnosis of unilateral lacrimal gland enlargement includes IgG4-related disease, sarcoidosis, idiopathic orbital inflammation, lymphoproliferative disorders, and granulomatosis with polyangiitis. In our patient, the initial clinical and radiologic findings raised concern for orbital lymphoma because of the infiltrative appearance of the lacrimal gland lesion and associated orbital involvement. However, the combination of childhood asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis, mononeuritis, positive p-ANCA serology, and histopathologic evidence of eosinophilic-rich small-vessel vasculitis favored EGPA over these alternative diagnoses. These findings emphasize the importance of integrating systemic history, serologic evaluation, and tissue biopsy when assessing patients with atypical orbital inflammatory disease.

Danesh et al described two patients with recurrent bilateral dacryoadenitis associated with systemic eosinophilic disease who were considered to have a disorder within the spectrum of eosinophilic asthma or EGPA. Unlike our patient, both cases had marked peripheral eosinophilia but lacked histopathologic evidence of vasculitis or granulomatous inflammation, precluding a definitive diagnosis of EGPA. In contrast, our patient presented with unilateral mechanical ptosis secondary to dacryoadenitis and biopsy-confirmed eosinophilic-rich small-vessel vasculitis, together with childhood asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis, mononeuritis, and positive p-ANCA serology, providing stronger evidence for EGPA. These findings suggest that isolated unilateral dacryoadenitis may represent an early ophthalmic manifestation of EGPA and highlight the importance of biopsy in establishing the diagnosis when clinical and imaging findings are nonspecific.7

Orbital involvement in EGPA is clinically significant because ophthalmologists may be the first physicians to suspect the diagnosis. Early recognition is essential, as untreated ANCA-associated vasculitis may result in substantial systemic morbidity.8–12 In our patient, multidisciplinary collaboration between ophthalmologists, rheumatologists, internists, and pathologists facilitated prompt diagnosis and initiation of immunosuppressive therapy, resulting in complete resolution of the orbital inflammatory findings after induction treatment.

Although orbital imaging was useful for identifying lacrimal gland enlargement and defining the extent of orbital involvement, the radiologic findings were nonspecific and initially suggested a lymphoproliferative process. Similarly, positive p-ANCA serology supported an ANCA-associated vasculitic process but was not sufficient by itself to establish the diagnosis. Histopathologic examination demonstrating eosinophilic-rich small-vessel vasculitis, interpreted together with the patient’s systemic manifestations and multidisciplinary assessment, provided the strongest evidence supporting EGPA. Although biologic agents such as mepolizumab are increasingly used as steroid-sparing therapies in EGPA, their high cost and limited availability in Mexico may restrict routine use, making cyclophosphamide an important therapeutic option for severe disease in many clinical settings.

This report has some limitations that should be acknowledged. Peripheral eosinophilia was not identified at presentation despite eosinophilic infiltration demonstrated on lacrimal gland histopathology. Specific anti-myeloperoxidase (MPO) and anti-proteinase 3 (PR3) antibody testing was not available at our institution; therefore, ANCA characterization was limited to indirect p-ANCA and c-ANCA testing. In addition, follow-up was limited to four months, precluding assessment of long-term remission or disease recurrence. Although the 2022 ACR/EULAR classification criteria are intended for disease classification rather than diagnosis, the patient’s clinical history (childhood asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis, and mononeuritis), positive p-ANCA serology, and histopathologic findings collectively provided strong support for the diagnosis within the overall clinical context.

Conclusions and Importance

Orbital involvement in eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis is an uncommon manifestation that may mimic neoplastic or other inflammatory orbital diseases. This case highlights unilateral dacryoadenitis with mechanical ptosis as a rare ophthalmic presentation of EGPA. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion when orbital inflammation is accompanied by suggestive systemic features, including asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis, or peripheral neuropathy. Histopathologic evaluation, serologic testing, and multidisciplinary assessment were instrumental in establishing the diagnosis in this patient and guiding appropriate management. Early recognition and timely immunosuppressive therapy may improve both ocular and systemic outcomes.
