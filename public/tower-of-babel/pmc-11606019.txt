Removal of an embedded duodenal foreign body in a Schizophrenic adolescent with a guidewire-formed lasso
Authors: Yingqi Yang, Hongkai Yu, Pengpeng Cai, Chengyan Xiang, Xiaomeng Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundForeign bodies (FBs) in the gastrointestinal tract are a common occurrence in pediatric patients, often requiring medical intervention for removal. Conventional extraction methods can be challenging, particularly when the FB is unusually large, long, abnormally positioned, or tightly embedded, presenting a gap in safe, non-surgical management techniques.Case presentationIn this report, we describe the innovative use of a lasso structure formed by a guidewire and biopsy forceps to successfully remove a tightly embedded toothbrush from the duodenum of a pediatric patient with schizophrenia, avoiding invasive surgical interventions, and the patient recovered well post-procedure without complications.ConclusionsThe success of this approach offers a promising alternative for clinicians managing similar cases, potentially reducing the need for surgical intervention and contributing to safer and more efficient treatment options for the removal of FBs in pediatric gastroenterology.Keywords: Foreign body, Gastrointestinal tract, Pediatric

Background

Gastrointestinal foreign bodies (FBs) are common in pediatric patients [1]. Children’s reduced self-protection abilities, lack of guardian awareness, and mental illness increase the risk of FB ingestion [2]. FBs may become lodged at different locations in the gastrointestinal tract, with upper gastrointestinal tract foreign body impaction being the most common [1, 2]. In severe cases, FBs may cause ulcers or perforation of the gastrointestinal tract. Endoscopic removal is the primary method for extracting FBs from the upper gastrointestinal tract [3], boasting a success rate of 94.7% [4] and minimal complications [5]. Common instruments include rat-tooth forceps, alligator forceps, snares, and baskets [1]. However, retrieving FBs is challenging when they are large, elongated, abnormally positioned, or embedded. In such cases, failure of traditional approaches may lead to invasive surgical interventions, presenting a gap in safe, non-surgical management techniques. Addressing this gap is crucial for reducing the risks of surgery and improving prognosis, especially in vulnerable pediatric populations.

In this case, we successfully removed a toothbrush from the duodenum of a 16-year-old pediatric patient with schizophrenia. The toothbrush had caused severe ulcers, with a high risk of perforation and significant pain. A lasso structure, formed by a guidewire and biopsy forceps, was innovatively used to successfully remove the toothbrush endoscopically.

Case presentation

A 16-year-old male teenager suffering from schizophrenia was admitted to the emergency center with severe abdominal pain after swallowing a toothbrush 2 days before. Abdominal CT showed a strip-shaped low-density shadow about 1.2*19 cm locating in the descending duodenum (Fig. 1). Urgent upper gastro-intestinal endoscopy showed that the toothbrush was tightly embedded between the duodenal bulb and the junction of the descending and horizontal parts. Deep ulcer caused by the toothbrush almost led to perforation of the wall of duodenum (Fig. 2). Initial attempts using conventional tools, such as forceps and snares, proved unsuccessful. The thick toothbrush handle prevents the forceps from securing a tight grip. Additionally, the two ends of the toothbrush were both firmly embedded in the duodenal mucosa, leaving no space between the toothbrush and mucosa. Consequently, the snare was unable to encircle the toothbrush. After multiple failures, a zebra guidewire was inserted through the biopsy channel into the duodenum, the tip of which passing by one side of the toothbrush handle. Then a biopsy forcep was inserted and the tip of the zebra guidewire was grasped by the biopsy forceps from the other side of the toothbrush. The guidewire was pulled slowly through the biopsy channel, forming a lasso around the toothbrush (Figs. 3 - 4). Finally, the toothbrush was successfully removed by slowly tightening and pulling the lasso. The toothbrush was 19 cm long (Fig. 5). The pediatric patient experienced minimal trauma, low economic costs, and had a good postoperative recovery with no perforation or infection complications, and discharged next day.

Discussion and conclusions

Removing FBs from the pediatric gastrointestinal tract poses a significant challenge for pediatricians [6]. In this case, a lasso formed by a zebra guidewire was innovatively used to successfully extract a toothbrush from the patient’s duodenum, avoiding surgery. This approach offers new insights into safe techniques for removing FBs that are too large or too long, abnormally positioned, or embedded from the gastrointestinal tract of pediatric patients.

The narrow anatomy and fragile mucosa of the pediatric gastrointestinal tract make it challenging to use traditional tools for FB removal. This case demonstrates the effectiveness of the lasso structure we employed for removing upper gastrointestinal FBs in pediatric patients, particularly when the FBs are large, long, abnormally positioned, or embedded. Notably, the lasso was formed using a guidewire and biopsy forceps, both commonly available instruments in pediatric gastroenterology. The simplicity and accessibility of the materials used to create the lasso, along with its proven effectiveness, give this method significant clinical applicability.

Notably, the patient in this case was a 16-year-old child suffering from schizophrenia. Schizophrenia patients are at higher risk of accidental FB ingestion due to positive symptoms such as hallucinations, delusions and poor judgment [7, 8]. Furthermore, schizophrenia patients often show emotional indifference, lack of motivation, and impaired self-awareness and social function, which may result in unnoticed and untreated FB ingestion, raising the risk of complications like ulcers and perforations. The pediatric patient with schizophrenia swallowed a toothbrush and was not identified until two days later. If the ingestion had been detected promptly, the FB would not have progressed to the duodenum, thereby lessening the challenge for the endoscopist in its removal.

Therefore, it is crucial for pediatricians, especially pediatric gastroenterologists, to prevent FB ingestion in pediatric patients with mental illness. The first step in prevention is identifying pediatric patients at risk of FB ingestion. For these pediatric patients, any ingestible objects, especially small items, should be promptly removed from the ward and placed out of reach [9]. Pediatricians should also provide timely pharmacological and psychological interventions, including cognitive behavioral and supportive therapies, to effectively prevent future FB ingestion in pediatric patients with mental illness [10]. In conclusion, pediatricians must prioritize preventing FB ingestion in pediatric patients with mental illness and implement timely interventions to reduce the incidence of such events.

In conclusion, the lasso structure created using a guidewire and biopsy forceps is a simple, safe, and reproducible technique for removing large, thick-diameter impacted FBs from the gastrointestinal tract of pediatric patients when conventional methods fail, offering significant clinical applicability.
