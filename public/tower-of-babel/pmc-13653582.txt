Indium Clusters With 6s 2 Lone‐Pair Effect as Excitonic Electron Reservoirs for Direct 2e− Photocatalytic H2O2 Photosynthesis
Authors: Shijie Xie, Teng Shao, Yuye Jiao, Yurou Song, Huijie Cheng, Yaru Shao, Tianyi Ma, Jungang Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe intrinsic activity–selectivity trade‐off in photocatalytic 2e− oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) for H2O2 photosynthesis originates from the kinetic dilemma between weak O2 adsorption at single‐atom sites and unfavorable O–O cleavage at multimetal sites. Herein, we demonstrate that indium (In) clusters with a unique 6s 2 lone‐pair effect can break this long‐standing single‐atom/cluster trade‐off by acting as excitonic electron reservoirs on carbon nitride. InSAC‐DCN delivers a remarkable H2O2 generation rate of 1930.8 µmol∙g−1∙h−1 with selectivity exceeding 90%, far surpassing both pristine carbon nitride and In single‐atom counterparts. Combined fs‐TAS and in situ characterizations reveal that In clusters enable ultrafast exciton dissociation and instantaneously deliver electrons to the geometric Yeager‐type side‐on O2 adsorption site, thereby favoring direct 2e− H2O2 formation. DFT calculations elucidate lowered energy barriers for ∗OOH formation, suppressed O–O dissociation, and accelerated water dissociation. Meanwhile, the synergistic coupling between water oxidation and ORR establishes a self‐sustained proton‐feedback loop for enhanced overall efficiency. This work highlights that exciton engineering coupled with p‐block metal clusters enables efficient and selective 2e− ORR beyond conventional single‐atom strategies to overcome the activity–selectivity trade‐off, establishing a distinct material‐design paradigm for high‐performance photocatalysts toward H2O2 photosynthesis.Keywords: electron reservoir, hydrogen peroxide, indium cluster, oxygen reduction, photocatalysis

Introduction

As a carbon‐free energy carrier and ecofriendly oxidant that produces only water (H2O) and oxygen (O2) as byproducts, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is highly attractive for addressing current energy crises and environmental problems [1, 2, 3, 4]. Photocatalytic H2O2 synthesis via the two‐electron oxygen reduction reaction (2e− ORR) from H2O and O2 over semiconductors has emerged as a transformative approach [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. However, the practical implementation of 2e− ORR‐based photocatalysis remains hindered by insufficient electron supply for efficient O2 activation, and poor control over O2 adsorption configuration and O–O bond integrity, which give rise to a long‐standing activity–selectivity trade‐off [6, 10, 11, 12].

The primary challenge in photocatalytic H2O2 synthesis is to design active sites that enable selective 2e− ORR [13], maintain O–O bond integrity [14], optimize O2 adsorption configuration [15, 16], and accelerate ∗OOH hydrogenation [17]. The ORR proceeds via O2 adsorption/activation to ∗O2 −, followed by the rate‐determining step ∗O2 − + H+ → ∗OOH [15], in which sufficient protons from water oxidation reaction (WOR) are essential to drive this critical step [18]. Oxygen reduction and WOR are inherently coupled, with O2 functioning simultaneously as both a 4e− WOR product and a 2e− ORR reactant [19]. Thus, rapid consumption of in situ generated O2 by ORR can kinetically boost WOR via a proton‐feedback loop, creating a self‐sustaining cycle for H2O2 synthesis [20].

Another critical challenge is preserving O–O bond integrity, as uncontrolled cleavage leads to the undesired 4e− ORR. Single‐atom catalysts (SACs) favor end‐on O2 adsorption, which suppresses O–O bond cleavage but suffers from limited O2 adsorption capacity and slow electron transfer [21]. Conversely, conventional transition metal clusters promote Yeager‐type side‐on O2 adsorption [22], where excessive d‐orbital electron back‐donation into the antibonding π orbital of O2 unavoidably triggers the unfavored O–O cleavage toward 4e− water production. Simultaneously delivering sufficient electrons without excessive activation of the O–O bond remains a longstanding bottleneck [23]. However, conventional d‐block transition‐metal dopants (e.g., Fe, Co, Ni, and Cu) typically act as electron donors or exhibit weak electron‐withdrawing behavior when anchored on carbon nitride [24], yielding insufficient electron density at active sites, thereby failing to sustain selective 2e− ORR. In comparison, indium (In, p‐block main‐group element) possesses a unique 6s 2 inert electron‐pair effect, which can fundamentally alter the electronic interaction with the carbon nitride matrix [25, 26]. This unique electronic configuration prevents In from acting as a trivial electron donor; instead, such structural perturbation drives an intensive orbital hybridization between the unoccupied 5s/5p orbitals of In and the lone‐pair electrons in N 2p orbitals, successfully inducing an unconventional reversed charge transfer from nitrogen lone pairs in carbon nitride to In sites. Furthermore, the large atomic radius of In induces lattice distortion, which further extracts electrons from N atoms [27, 28]. More importantly, In exhibits moderate s/p orbital overlap with O2 molecules [29], avoiding excessive O–O bond cleavage that usually occurs on transition‐metal sites with d orbitals [30]. Such unique electronic and structural characteristics enable In clusters with high electron density to function as excitonic electron reservoirs [31], which dramatically accelerate direct 2e− O2 activation, favorable side‐on adsorption, and exceptional O–O bond preservation, thereby breaking the long‐standing activity–selectivity trade‐off for efficient photocatalytic H2O2 synthesis.

Herein, this study presents a cluster‐mediated engineering strategy by anchoring indium single‐atom (InSA‐DCN) and indium cluster (InSAC‐DCN) catalysts on graphitic carbon nitride (DCN) to break the activity–selectivity trade‐off for non‐sacrificial photocatalytic H2O2 synthesis (Scheme 1). Benefiting from an unconventional reversed charge transfer enabled by the 6s 2 inert pair effect of indium, the sub‐nanometer In clusters structurally evolve into efficient excitonic electron reservoirs. InSAC‐DCN exhibits a significantly enhanced photocatalytic H2O2 generation rate of 1930.8 µmol∙g−1∙h−1, representing a 28.9‐ and 2.8‐fold increase over DCN and InSA‐DCN, respectively. Experimental and theoretical analyses reveal that O2 adsorption on isolated In atomic sites adopts an end‐on configuration, which promotes the formation of In‐peroxide (In‐OOH) and leads to a two‐step 1e– pathway for H2O2 production. Conversely, Yeager‐type side‐on O2 adsorption on InSAC‐DCN enhances O2 adsorption capacity, while transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy indicates novel electron transfer pathways and charge trap sites, enhancing electron availability and accelerating the direct 2e– O2 reduction mechanism in the InSAC‐DCN system. DFT calculations validate that this optimized orbital alignment fundamentally lowers the thermodynamic barrier for ∗OOH formation, suppresses O–O dissociation, and accelerates water dissociation. Furthermore, the evolution of O2 from WOR and its participation in the 2e− ORR process were confirmed, which in turn promotes the 4e− WOR. This work elucidates the unique role of In clusters in regulating charge dynamics, O2 adsorption configuration, and 2e− ORR selectivity, offering a promising route to overcome the activity–selectivity trade‐off in photocatalytic H2O2 synthesis.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Structural Characterization

DCN, InSA‐DCN, and InSAC‐DCN were synthesized via modified literature protocols [32]. Specifically, dicyandiamide was used as the precursor for DCN synthesis, while ground dicyandiamide and an indium metal salt served as precursors for InSA‐DCN, both via a standard stirring and calcination procedure. Subsequent calcination of InSA‐DCN using the molten salt method yielded InSAC‐DCN (Figure 1a). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Figure 1b) showed that bare DCN exhibits a thick stacked lamellar nanosheet structure. After anchoring In single atoms (InSA) and In clusters (InSAC), the stacked structure was significantly thinned (Figure 1c–d), attributed to In doping‐induced lattice distortion and the suppression of layer stacking. High‐angle annular dark‐field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF‐STEM) images (Figure 1e–f) show uniformly distributed bright In spots in both catalysts, confirming high dispersion of In species. Notably, InSA‐DCN contains isolated In atoms, while InSAC‐DCN features both isolated In atoms and sub‐nanometer In clusters (diameter ∼0.6–0.9 nm, green dashed circle in Figures 1f and S1a), and each cluster is estimated to contain 4–6 In atoms. The 3D elemental mapping (inset of Figure 1f) further confirms uniform In dispersion. Energy‐dispersive x‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) mappings (Figures 1g and S1b) confirm uniform distribution of C, N, and In in both InSA‐DCN and InSAC‐DCN. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP‐OES) measurements determined the In mass contents of InSA‐DCN and InSAC‐DCN to be 0.13 and 0.32 wt%, respectively.

The XRD patterns of the prepared DCN, InSA‐DCN, and InSAC‐DCN are shown in Figure 1h. Following the introduction of In species, the characteristic diffraction peak of DCN weakened and slightly shifted to higher angles. However, no diffraction signals assigned to crystalline indium nanoparticles or large clusters were detected. This indicates the absence of metallic particles within the system and suggests a reduction in thickness coupled with structural distortion in DCN [33]. Fourier transform infrared (FT‐IR) spectroscopy (Figure 1i) showed no obvious difference in the wavenumber ranges of 700−900, 1000−1800, and 3000−3400 cm−1, indicating that the skeleton of DCN hardly changed before and after incorporation of In species [34].

Electronic Structure and Coordination Environment

To investigate the interaction between In species and the DCN skeleton, in situ high‐resolution x‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were conducted under dark and visible‐light irradiation, with additional In 3d characterization after introducing gaseous O2 (Figure 2a−c). The C 1s spectrum (Figure 2a) reveals no obvious binding energy (BE) peak shift across all samples under varied test conditions, precluding direct electronic coupling between C and In [35]. In sharp contrast, a highly synchronized and responsive charge redistribution was revealed within the N and In atom spatial domains. In the dark state, the N 1s spectra of both InSA‐DCN and InSAC‐DCN experience distinct positive BE shifts relative to pristine DCN (Figure 2b), while the In 3d 5/2 peaks of InSAC‐DCN concurrently shift to lower BE values compared to InSA‐DCN (Figure 2c). This spontaneous ground‐state charge redistribution acts as a spectroscopic fingerprint confirming unconventional reverse charge transfer from nitrogen lone pairs to anchored In sites. This phenomenon is driven by the intrinsic structural perturbation and the 6s 2 inert electron‐pair effect of the p‐block Indium [32, 36], where strong orbital hybridization takes place between the vacant In 5s/5p orbitals and the N 2p states [27]. Under visible illumination, the In 3d 5/2 peaks of InSA‐DCN and InSAC‐DCN shift negatively by 0.1 and 0.25 eV, respectively. Meanwhile, their N 1s binding energies shift positively in a synchronized manner, while bare DCN displays a minor negative shift under identical test conditions. This coupled spectroscopic response unambiguously validates a continuous photogenerated electron migration accumulating at In species. Crucially, when gaseous O2 is introduced into the illuminated system (Light + O2), the In 3d 5/2 BE conversely shifts back toward higher BE regions (Figure 2c). This reversible electronic shift directly evidences a key charge transfer process where the accumulated photogenerated electrons on the electron‐rich In clusters are instantaneously discharged into the antibonding π* orbitals of the trapped O2 molecules. This dynamic electron evacuation from In to O2 perfectly bridges the charge‐transfer causality, providing a robust electronic prelude that corroborates the geometric Yeager‐type side‐on O2 adsorption mode validated by in situ FT‐IR.

(d) Normalized In K‐edge XANES spectra, (e) FT‐EXAFS spectra, and (f) corresponding EXAFS fitting curves in R space for In foil, In2O3, InSA‐DCN, and InSAC‐DCN. (g) In K‐edge WT‐EXAFS contour plots.

The oxidation states and coordination environment of In were further determined by In K‐edge x‐ray absorption near‐edge structure (XANES) spectroscopy. The XANES profiles show that the absorption edges of InSA‐DCN and InSAC‐DCN (Figure 2d) lie between those of In foil and In2O3, indicating that In exists as positively charged In δ + (0< δ < 3), with InSAC‐DCN exhibiting a more negative oxidation state [31]. Fourier transform extended x‐ray absorption fine structure (FT‐EXAFS) spectra (Figure 2e) show that InSA‐DCN exhibits only one peak at 1.65 Å corresponding to In−N coordination. In comparison, InSAC‐DCN exhibits an additional peak at 3.18 Å assigned to In−In coordination [37], verifying the coexistence of In single atoms and In clusters in InSAC‐DCN. EXAFS fitting results (Table S1, Figures S2 and 2f) reveal that the average coordination number of In–N shells was calculated to be 4 for all In species, and each In atom in the clusters coordinates with about 2.3 In atoms in the second shell. This relative lower coordination number signifies the coexistence of isolated In single atoms and sub‐nanometer In clusters. Wavelet‐transformed EXAFS (WT‐EXAFS) (Figure 2g) provides direct evidence for the presence of In single atoms in InSA‐DCN and the coexistence of In single atoms and sub‐nanometer clusters in InSAC‐DCN, respectively.

Photocatalytic H2O2 Production Performance

Photocatalytic H2O2 production was evaluated in pure water (pH = 7, no sacrificial agent) under visible light irradiation (λ > 400 nm, 100 mW∙cm−2) with continuous O2 bubbling. As shown in Figure 3a,b, InSA‐DCN and InSAC‐DCN exhibit considerably enhanced H2O2 production compared with DCN. Over a 4‐hour continuous illumination period, the total H2O2 concentration reaches only 53.5 µM for DCN, while InSA‐DCN generates 550 µM, and InSAC‐DCN further increases to 1544.6 µM. The corresponding H2O2 generation rate of InSAC‐DCN is 1930.8 µmol∙g−1∙h−1, which is 2.8 times that of InSA‐DCN (687.5 µmol∙g−1∙h−1) and 28.9 times that of DCN (66.9 µmol∙g−1∙h−1). These findings suggest that the incorporation and modification of In species are critical factors in the observed catalytic activity enhancement. In addition, as illustrated in Figure 3c, a comparative analysis of photocatalytic H2O2 generation under varied atmospheric conditions (N2, air, and O2) was conducted. H2O2 production over InSAC‐DCN is significantly reduced in an air‐saturated solution (instead of O2) by a factor of 6 and completely inhibited when O2 is replaced by N2. These results indicate that O2 acts as an essential reactant and H2O2 production proceeds via oxygen reduction rather than the hole oxidation of H2O. Figure 3d presents the catalytic performance across varying pH values. InSAC‐DCN shows a photocatalytic rate of 824.8 µmol∙g−1∙h−1 at pH = 3, maintains a high rate of 1933.2 µmol∙g−1∙h−1 in pure water (pH 7), and retains approximately one‐quarter of its maximum efficiency when the pH reaches 13. It is noteworthy that the performance degradation of InSAC‐DCN was attributed to the disruption of In cluster sites under acidic conditions. However, its catalytic activity surpasses that of InSA‐DCN, indicating the critical role of In clusters in enhancing performance. A series of control experiments were performed to identify key factors for catalytic performance (Figure 3e). The findings indicate that the photocatalytic reaction cannot be activated in the absence of light irradiation or the catalyst. Modifying the preparation sequence to introduce In species after molten salt treatment led to a significant reduction in photocatalytic performance, likely due to the incompatibility of this process with In species anchoring. After acid pretreatment, the activity declines by approximately 60% to a level close to InSA‐DCN, confirming that In clusters are the dominant active sites. The addition of isopropanol as an electron donor improves the H2O2 yield by 2.58 times, further verifying the electron‐deficient nature of the ORR process. In addition, InSAC‐DCN retains more than 95% of its initial activity after six consecutive cycles (Figure 3f), showing excellent stability. Characterizations of the post‐reaction InSAC‐DCN sample reveal no significant changes in composition, structure, or morphology (Figure S3). Furthermore, the optimal In loading (0.32 wt%) and precursor are optimized to enhance H2O2 synthesis (Figure S4). InSAC‐DCN exhibits exceptional activity, prominent stability, and a wide pH adaptability range, highlighting the crucial role of In clusters in boosting photocatalytic H2O2 production.

Band Structure and Charge Carrier Dynamics

UV‐Vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV‐Vis DRS, Figure 4a) shows that the introduction of InSA and InSAC species markedly enhances light absorption and modestly narrows the bandgap (2.73 eV for DCN, 2.66 eV for InSA‐DCN, and 2.57 eV for InSAC‐DCN, respectively, Figure 4b). Mott–Schottky measurements (Figure S5) reveal that the conduction band minimum slightly shifts from −1.34 eV (vs. NHE) for DCN to −1.22 eV for InSA‐DCN and −1.15 eV for InSAC‐DCN, while barely affecting the valence band maximum (ca. 1.4 eV vs. NHE). Such band positions ensure thermodynamic feasibility for H2O2 generation via O2 reduction (+0.68 V vs. NHE), rather than water oxidation (1.76 V vs. NHE). Furthermore, the intense band centered at approximately 350−400 nm is attributed to π−π* transitions, a characteristic of conjugated heterocyclic aromatic ring systems [38]. The enhanced absorption in this region for InSA‐DCN and InSAC‐DCN is attributed to decreased film thickness. Moreover, the enhanced absorption around 425 nm in InSAC‐DCN originates from symmetry‐broken n−π* transitions induced by framework distortion, which promotes charge separation [39, 40]. Therefore, the data indicate that the heptazine units of DCN undergo structural distortion after In incorporation. On the other hand, more pronounced light absorption is associated with an increased number of n→π* transitions, which provide more electrons for the reaction [40]. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements (Figure 4c) show that InSAC‐DCN possesses the smallest Nyquist arc, indicating a significantly reduced charge‐transfer resistance and accelerated interfacial charge migration [41]. The facilitated charge migration is further verified by the enhanced photocurrent density (Figure S6).

Following photon absorption, the excited photocatalysts typically relax to their ground states through radiative emission (photoluminescence, PL) or non‐radiative recombination via charge trapping, where a fraction of trapped charge carriers engage in surface‐mediated catalytic reactions [42]. As shown in Figure 4d, the PL intensity of InSA‐DCN and InSAC‐DCN is significantly quenched, indicating that radiative recombination is largely retarded. Consistently, the PL onset wavelength underwent a gradual red shift, aligning well with the narrowed bandgap observed (Figure 4b). Gaussian deconvolution of PL spectra (Figure 4e, Figure S7) reveals three major emission peaks, providing clear insights into the nature of the excited states [43]. Specifically, the P3 emission peak is substantially suppressed in InSA‐DCN and InSAC‐DCN, arising from the formation of new electron‐transfer channels between the π* conduction band and the In species. Moreover, the remarkably short excited‐state lifetime of InSAC‐DCN (3.24 ns, Figure 4f, Table S3) is attributed to the rapid exciton dissociation and efficient charge injection from the DCN matrix into the In cluster sites. This accelerated interfacial electron transfer effectively bypasses radiative recombination channels, facilitating more photogenerated electrons to participate in the subsequent ORR process [44]. Collectively, the pronounced n→π* transitions and the suppressed π*–n recombination synergistically accelerate charge separation and transfer. Consequently, this facilitates a greater number of electrons to participate in the catalytic reactions, ultimately leading to a significantly enhanced H2O2 production performance.

Electron Transfer Dynamics Revealed by fs‐TAS

Femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy (fs‐TAS) was employed to visualize photogenerated electron dynamics and verify the electron‐reservoir role of In clusters. As shown in Figure 5a–f, a negative signal was exclusively observed in the 450–600 nm range for DCN, which was primarily ascribed to the ground‐state bleaching (GSB) processes [45, 46]. The prominent absorption bands at ∼550–700 nm are characteristic of electron transition in DCN, and the reduction in intensity arises from efficient electron‐hole recombination within the DCN material. In contrast, InSA‐DCN and InSAC‐DCN exhibit positive excited‐state absorption (ESA) signals across 550–700 nm. Following the modification of InSA, a novel absorption feature emerges at ∼600 nm (Figure 5b,e). Subsequent incorporation of InSAC not only retains this peak but also introduces an additional absorption band at ∼670 nm (Figure 5c,f). This signal fingerprints the formation of an interfacial charge‐transfer complex (CTC) induced by the strong hybridization between In 5s/5p and N 2p orbitals. The strongest positive ESA absorption difference (ΔA) of InSAC‐DCN indicates accelerated charge excitation and separation processes. These observations demonstrate that InSA modification modulates the charge recombination dynamics of pristine DCN, while the subsequent addition of InSAC further facilitates the formation of an alternative electron transfer pathway.

Furthermore, the TA decay curves of DCN can be effectively fitted using a double‐exponential function, whereas those of InSA‐DCN and InSAC‐DCN require a triple‐exponential function (Figure 5g–j, Table S4). The ultrafast component τ1 (< 1 ps) is characteristic of ultrafast exciton dissociation and instantaneous electron injection from the framework into the metal sites [47]. More pivotally, the intermediate lifetime τ2 provides a critical kinetic differentiation between the single‐atom and cluster configurations. For InSA‐DCN, τ2 is limited to a mere 0.92 ps, indicating that electrons trapped at solitary In sites undergo immediate interfacial dissipation or rapid back‐transfer due to the structural capacity limitations of isolated atoms. In sharp contrast, InSAC‐DCN delivers a significant 11‐fold increase in τ2 to 10.48 ps. This substantial expansion in electron retention time provides concrete quantum‐dynamic evidence for the successful realization of the “excitonic electron reservoir” on the sub‐nanometer In clusters. Benefiting from multi‐atom coordination, the cluster functions as a highly efficient capacitive buffer that dynamically populates and anchors the transient electronic flux within its shallow‐trap domain, preventing premature non‐radiative annihilation. Furthermore, the long‐lived component τ3 extends into the nanosecond regime, representing the charge‐preservation phase harvested for interfacial catalysis. Although the average carrier lifetime (τave) of InSAC‐DCN (1035.41 ps) shows a modest increment over InSA‐DCN (762.4 ps), the macroscopic H2O2 production rate increased by a factor of 2.8. This kinetic uncoupling demonstrates that the macroscopic catalytic activity enhancement is governed by the synergistic effect of prolonged electron lifetimes (τ2 and τ3). To exclude the interference of molten salt‐induced surface recombination centers, we further performed fs‐TAS characterizations on molten‐salt‐treated pure DCN without indium species, which verifies the shallow‐trap capacitive buffer effect originating from In clusters (Figure S8). The temporally and spatially concentrated electrons within the In cluster reservoir provide a continuous charge supply to the adsorbed O2 molecules. This perfectly matches the kinetic requirements of a direct, synchronized 2e– reduction pathway, thereby optimizing the macroscopic performance of H2O2 photosynthesis.

ORR Pathway and H2O2 Selectivity

Two mechanistic pathways are involved in H2O2 synthesis: the direct 2e– reduction pathway and the superoxide‐mediated sequential two‐step 1e– pathway [11]. To elucidate the H2O2 formation mechanism, the contribution of each pathway was systematically evaluated [48]. In situ electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy was conducted using 5,5‐dimethyl‐1‐pyrroline N‐oxide (DMPO) as a spin‐trapping agent to identify transient radical intermediates. As depicted in Figure 6a, six typical characteristic signals of DMPO‐∙O2 – were detected under illumination in the presence of O2 and methanol, indicating that the photocatalytic mechanism proceeds via a sequential two‐step single‐electron route (O2 + e– + H+ → ∗OOH; ∗OOH + e– + H+ → H2O2) [49]. The pronounced EPR signal of InSA‐DCN further substantiates that more photogenerated electrons participate in the photocatalytic synthesis of H2O2. The stronger DMPO‐∙O2– signal for InSA‐DCN indicates a more dominant superoxide‐mediated pathway, consistent with its sequential 1e− mechanism. Conversely, the relatively weaker ∙O2 − signals, together with the superior H2O2 generation rate, suggest rapid O2 reduction mainly via a direct 2e– ORR pathway for H2O2 production in InSAC‐DCN [50]. Meanwhile, as previously reported, superoxide dismutase (SOD) facilitates the dismutation of ∙O2 −, thereby enhancing H2O2 generation [51]. Therefore, SOD was introduced to elucidate the role of ∙O2 − intermediates [52]. The H2O2 production rates of DCN and InSA‐DCN increased with increasing SOD concentrations. However, InSAC‐DCN exhibited a negligible enhancement (Figure 6b), which confirms that ∙O2 − is less involved in InSAC‐DCN. Furthermore, upon adding benzoquinone (BQ) as an ∙O2 − trapping agent [49], the H2O2 yields of DCN and InSA‐DCN decreased significantly, whereas InSAC‐DCN maintained approximately 50% of its initial H2O2 generation capacity (Figure 6c). These results indicate that H2O2 formation in DCN and InSA‐DCN predominantly follows a sequential two‐step 1e– pathway, while InSAC‐DCN proceeds via a mixed mechanism where the direct 2e– ORR pathway predominantly governs the overall kinetics [50].

Rotating ring‐disk electrode (RRDE) voltammetry was performed to quantitatively determine the ORR pathway selectivity and the number of transferred electrons (n) [53]. The selectivity of H2O2 production was monitored in an O2‐saturated electrolyte by measuring both the reduction current of O2 at the disk part of RRDE (I d) and the oxidation current of the produced H2O2 on the Pt/C ring part (I r) [54]. As shown in Figure 6d, both disk current and ring current progressively increase as the applied potential decreases. Meanwhile, the disk current of InSAC‐DCN and InSA‐DCN is greater than that of DCN, indicative of more effective electron transfer for oxygen reduction [2]. Additionally, the ring current of InSAC‐DCN was greater than that of InSA‐DCN and DCN, indicating that InSAC‐DCN generates a high yield of H2O2. Moreover, Figure 6e summarizes the H2O2 selectivity and transferred electron numbers (n) of DCN, InSA‐DCN and InSAC‐DCN recorded over a potential window of 0.1–0.5 V versus RHE. At 0.1 V versus RHE, InSAC‐DCN delivers a much higher H2O2 selectivity of ∼90%, far exceeding those of InSA‐DCN (∼78%) and bare DCN (∼56%) (upper panel) [44]. Correspondingly, the calculated transferred‐electron number n of InSAC‐DCN at 0.1 V versus RHE is 2.18, which is much closer to the ideal two‐electron ORR theoretical value, while InSA‐DCN and DCN display larger n values of 2.44 and 2.89, respectively (lower panel). Throughout the whole tested potential range of 0.1–0.5 V versus RHE, InSAC‐DCN consistently maintains superior H2O2 selectivity and smaller electron transfer numbers compared with the other two catalysts. Collectively, these results confirm that InSAC‐DCN possesses greatly boosted catalytic performance toward the direct two‐electron ORR pathway and achieves a maximum H2O2 selectivity up to 90%.

O2 temperature‐programmed desorption (O2‐TPD, Figure S9) shows that InSAC‐DCN exhibits a larger O2 desorption signal and a higher desorption temperature, demonstrating superior O2 adsorption capacity and binding strength. In addition, InSAC‐DCN demonstrates the highest apparent formation constant (k f), which is 17.49 times greater than that of DCN, while its decomposition constant (k d) remains comparatively low. This kinetic behavior suggests that the incorporation of In clusters significantly enhances H2O2 generation efficiency with minimal impact on H2O2 decomposition (Figures 6f and S10), securing the high selectivity of H2O2 generation. Furthermore, the introduction of In species endowed InSAC‐DCN with enhanced hydrophilicity (Figure S11) [55].

Concurrently, to ascertain the oxidation half‐reaction and verify whether the ∙OH radicals arise from water oxidation rather than H2O2 decomposition, the generation of ∙OH was also probed by EPR spectroscopy. Notably, InSAC‐DCN exhibits the strongest ∙OH signal among all samples (Figure 6g) [56], confirming its superior water oxidation activity and efficient proton (H+) supply capability. Strikingly, the ∙OH signal of InSAC‐DCN is significantly weakened under N2 purging relative to that under O2‐saturated conditions. Since ·OH is mainly generated via water oxidation by photogenerated holes, the enhanced ·OH signal in the presence of O2 indicates that the water oxidation process is effectively promoted by the ORR process. Meanwhile, only negligible •OH signals were observed in the O2‐free H2O2 system, revealing that H2O2 photodecomposition barely contributes to the strong DMPO‐•OH signal in aerobic photocatalysis. This observation directly verifies that the ORR provides a positive feedback to accelerate the WOR, forming a closed synergistic loop between ORR and WOR.

Furthermore, to elucidate the overall photocatalytic H2O2 generation mechanism, the half‐redox reactions were investigated independently. The H2O2 production rate of InSAC‐DCN in an aqueous 2‐propanol solution (serving as an electron donor, 10% v/v) saturated with O2 (Figure 6h, lower panel) was about 3 times higher than that when water was used as the electron donor. Meanwhile, when an electron acceptor (Figure 6h, upper panel) was employed, O2 evolution was observed and H2O2 formation was inhibited under identical conditions, confirming the oxidative role of photogenerated holes in the system (2H2O + 4h+ → 4H+ + O2). Moreover, H2 18O isotope experiments were also conducted under identical conditions to those of H2 16O (Figure 6i), a distinct 18O2 (m/z = 36) signal was detected in the evolved gas, illustrating that the oxidation products (18O2 and H+) generated from 4e− WOR could be involved in the 2e− ORR to produce H2 18O2 [18]. Thus, the overall photocatalytic process involves photogenerated electrons at the reduction sites reducing O2 to H2O2, while photogenerated holes at the oxidation sites oxidize H2O to O2, with the generated O2 actively engaging in the in situ photocatalytic synthesis of H2O2.

In Situ FT‐IR and DFT Mechanistic Insights

In situ FTIR spectrometry was employed to monitor the evolution of intermediates in the 2e– ORR process, which involves two proton‐coupled electron transfer processes, with ∗OOH serving as the key reactive intermediate (Figure 7a−c). The peak located at 1685 cm−1 and the emerging vibrational band at ∼955 cm−1 corresponded to the O2 consumption and O–O stretching mode of the conventional end‐on oxygen (∗O2) adsorption mode, confirming the reduction of molecular oxygen during photocatalytic reduction. The peaks at 1630 and 1090 cm−1 are attributed to the O–H bending mode and O–O stretching vibrations of surface‐adsorbed superoxide (∗OOH), respectively [11, 12]. Meanwhile, the peak range of 1260−1435 cm−1 is attributable to surface‐adsorbed hydroperoxide (∗H2O2) [54, 57], confirming the formation of H2O2. Additionally, prominent absorption peaks around 3600 cm−1 are attributed to the O–H stretching vibrations of surface‐bound ∗H2O2 (Figure S12) [49]. Notably, InSAC‐DCN exhibits an exclusive IR peak at 880 cm−1 that is assigned to the O–O stretching vibration of Yeager‐type side‐on bridged O2 adsorption. Benefiting from light‐triggered electron accumulation on In clusters verified by the negative BE shift of In 3d in in situ XPS (Figure 2c), side‐on adsorption enables stronger orbital overlap between In clusters and O2 molecules than end‐on O2 on single In sites, which promotes more efficient electron back‐donation into the O2 π* antibonding orbital. Accordingly, the O–O bond is notably activated with an elongated bond length and reduced bond order, reflected by a notable red shift of the O–O vibrational frequency from 955 cm−1 (end‐on O2) to 880 cm−1 (side‐on O2, Figure 7a). Upon visible‐light illumination, the peak intensity of adsorbed ∗O2 and ∗OOH species increases rapidly over InSAC‐DCN, indicating accelerated O2 adsorption and rapid protonation to form ∗OOH. In contrast, DCN and InSA‐DCN show much weaker and slower evolution of these oxygen intermediates. The enhanced accumulation of ∗OOH and the suppressed excessive O–O bond cleavage further confirm that the In‐cluster‐induced side‐on O2 adsorption configuration favors selective 2e− ORR toward H2O2 rather than the unselective 4e− ORR pathway [54].

DFT calculations were performed to elucidate the atomic‐level origin of the enhanced photocatalytic performance induced by In incorporation into DCN (Figure 7d). Detailed structural models and corresponding reaction pathways employed in the theoretical calculations are presented in Figures S13 and S14. From DCN to InSA‐DCN and further to InSAC‐DCN, the Gibbs free energies (ΔG) for the ∗O2 adsorption step show a successive decrease, indicating that the incorporation of In atoms, especially In clusters, significantly strengthens O2 adsorption, which is in accordance with the in situ FT‐IR results in Figure 7a. This provides a more thermodynamically favorable starting point for the ORR. Notably, ORR activity was correlated with the binding strength of the ∗OOH intermediate, which is the rate‐determining species for 2e− ORR toward H2O2 synthesis. InSAC‐DCN exhibits a substantially lower ∗OOH binding free energy (−0.62 eV) compared to InSA‐DCN (−0.30 eV) and DCN (−0.08 eV), demonstrating that In clusters effectively reduce the energy barrier for ∗OOH formation and thus accelerate ORR kinetics. Furthermore, the free energy of ∗H2O2 gradually decreases from DCN to InSA‐DCN and to InSAC‐DCN, revealing that In clusters can better stabilize the ∗H2O2 intermediate and thermodynamically favor the selective generation of H2O2. More importantly, on InSAC‐DCN, the ΔG(∗HOOH) is significantly lower than ΔG(∗O), which indicates that the reaction pathway tends to proceed toward H2O2 formation rather than O–O bond cleavage, thermodynamically hindering the competing 4e− ORR pathway while kinetically accelerating the 2e− route. In addition, the Volmer step (ΔG(H2O)) was also evaluated via DFT calculations (Figures 7e and S15). The ΔG(H2O) value for DCN is as high as 2.8 eV, whereas those for InSA‐DCN and InSAC‐DCN decrease to 1.78 and 1.3 eV, respectively. This significant reduction in the energy barrier of the Volmer step facilitates water dissociation to supply sufficient protons to promote the hydrogenation process of ORR [20]. Therefore, the In cluster sites enhance O2 adsorption and activation, reduce the energy barrier for ∗OOH formation, stabilize the ∗H2O2 intermediate to promote selectivity, and accelerate water dissociation to facilitate proton supply. These synergistic effects collectively suppress the 4e− ORR pathway and boost the 2e− ORR activity, accounting for the superior photocatalytic activity and H2O2 selectivity of InSAC‐DCN.

Charge density difference (CDD) analysis (Figure 7f−i) reveals that In clusters induce far more pronounced interlayer charge polarization and asymmetric electrostatic potential than single In atoms [58]. The extended charge accumulation/depletion regions generate robust local dipoles that drive efficient exciton dissociation and directional charge migration, which is the key to the excitonic electron reservoir behavior. This charge reconstruction directly underpins the unique density of states (DOS, Figure 7j−k). Unlike the localized states in InSA‐DCN, InSAC‐DCN exhibits high‐density delocalized states near the Fermi level, arising from intensive In–N orbital hybridization. These states enable robust π‐backdonation to O2, stabilize Yeager‐type side‐on adsorption, and ensure sufficient electron supply without over‐activating the O–O bond. The synergy between charge polarization (CDD) and Fermi‐level‐enhanced DOS makes In clusters highly efficient excitonic electron reservoirs, which break the single‐atom/cluster trade‐off and deliver outstanding activity and selectivity for 2e− ORR.

Conclusion

In summary, this work presents a distinct p‐block cluster‐mediated excitonic electron‐reservoir strategy to resolve the long‐standing activity–selectivity trade‐off and the single‐atom/cluster performance boundary in photocatalytic 2e− oxygen reduction for H2O2 production. By precisely anchoring indium clusters (InSAC‐DCN) on carbon nitride, we demonstrate that In clusters break the intrinsic kinetic and coordination limitations of conventional SACs and deliver exceptional photocatalytic performance with a H2O2 production rate of 1930.8 µmol∙g−1∙h−1 and selectivity above 90% under non‐sacrificial conditions. Comprehensive characterizations and DFT calculations confirm that In clusters serve as electron reservoirs and charge‐accumulation sites, which effectively facilitate exciton separation and sustain sufficient electron supply for O2 activation. Crucially, in situ FTIR results identify that In clusters modulate O2 adsorption from an end‐on (InSA) to a side‐on mode, significantly enhancing O2 adsorption capacity while preserving O–O bond integrity under sufficient electron and proton fluxes. This structural optimization lowers the energy barrier for ∗OOH formation and kinetically suppresses the competing 4e− ORR pathway, thereby achieving high H2O2 selectivity. Notably, in situ generated O2 from water oxidation is continuously consumed by ORR, forming a synergistic WOR–ORR cascade that further boosts overall reaction kinetics via a proton‐feedback loop. This study unravels the critical role of p‐block metal clusters in regulating charge dynamics and O2 adsorption for selective 2e− ORR, and provides a universal material design paradigm for high‐efficiency solar‐to‐chemical conversion. The electron reservoir concept mediated by inert electron‐pair metals offers a new direction for developing advanced photocatalysts toward sustainable H2O2 photosynthesis and other selective oxygen‐involving catalytic reactions.
