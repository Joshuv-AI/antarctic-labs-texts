Mean-Field Dynamics of the Bose–Hubbard Model in High Dimension
Shahnaz Farhat, Denis Périce, Sören Petrat

Abstract
This paper is a first step toward rigorously establishing the validity of mean-field approximations for the dynamics of quantum systems in a large dimensional limit. Our result is a derivation of the large d d mean-field limit of the Bose–Hubbard model on a square lattice. The Bose–Hubbard model effectively describes bosons on a lattice with on-site interactions and nearest-neighbour hopping, serving as a foundational framework for understanding strong particle interactions and the superfluid to Mott insulator phase transition. We prove a trace norm estimate between the one-lattice-site reduced density of the Schrödinger dynamics and the mean-field dynamics in the limit of large dimension. Here, the mean-field approximation is in the hopping amplitude and not in the interaction, leading to a very rich and non-trivial mean-field equation. This mean-field equation does not only describe the condensate, as is the case when the mean-field description comes from a large particle number limit averaging out the interaction, but it allows for a phase transition since it contains the full non-trivial interaction. Our work is a rigorous justification of a simple case of the highly successful dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT) for bosons, which somewhat surprisingly yields many qualitatively correct results in three dimensions.



1
Introduction

One of the big aspirations of mathematical physics is to advance our rigorous understanding of phase transitions. Within this research area, much recent attention has been paid to the phenomenon of Bose–Einstein–Condensation (BEC), a phase of matter of cold Bose gases that was predicted in 1924 by Bose
[
6
]
and Einstein
[
16
,
17
]
. Since then, BEC has been studied extensively by theoretical physicists, and at least since the 1980s also by mathematical physicists with more rigorous methods. After the first experimental realizations in the labs of Cornell/Wieman
[
2
]
and Ketterle
[
13
]
in 1995, the study of BEC has received a new wave of attention throughout experimental, theoretical, and mathematical physics. The motivation of our work comes from different perspectives.

1.

Lots of recent effort has been put into understanding BEC at zero temperature, e.g., in the Gross–Pitaevskii limit (see, e.g.,
[
45
]
for a review) and the thermodynamic limit (see, e.g,
[
48
,
22
,
23
]
for a derivation of the Lee–Huang–Yang formula). This yields insight into the behavior of the condensate and its excitations, e.g., a rigorous proof of Bogoliubov theory in the Gross–Pitaevskii regime
[
5
]
, but it is very far from understanding the (thermodynamic) phase transition to BEC.

2.

A very useful and simple method for studying many-body systems is the mean-field approximation. For bosonic cold atoms, the interaction potential is scaled down with the inverse of the particle number
N

[
18
]
(or density
[
15
]
), thus considering weak interaction. Then the inverse particle number can be regarded as a small parameter, and the interaction can be effectively replaced by its mean-field. In this mean-field limit, many physical effects can be rigorously established regarding the dynamics and the low-energy properties (see, e.g.,
[
7
,
8
]
for a list of references). In particular, one can prove the validity of Bogoliubov theory (see, e.g.,
[
32
,
33
]
), and perturbative expansions beyond Bogoliubov
[
7
,
8
]
. However, these types of mean-field models do not describe phase transitions.

3.

For Bose gases in the continuum, one would ultimately like to prove a thermodynamic phase transition. However, Bose gases on the lattice offer a different possibility for a phase transition, namely a quantum phase transition between a BEC and a localized state usually called a Mott insulator; see, e.g.,
[
49
]
for a review. There are only a few mathematically rigorous works on this topic, for example
[
1
]
, where the quantum transition to BEC is proved at half filling, and
[
20
]
, where the authors establish
d
-dependent bounds on the critical line delimiting the Mott insulator phase.

Our work addresses these points in the following way.
1
: We study a limit that may describe a phase transition.
2
: Our limit is a mean-field limit, not for large particle number but for large dimension. We hope and indeed show that some of the methods of large
N
mean-field limits are still relevant for this case. Since in our model the averaging is done over the hopping terms, and the interaction is treated non-perturbatively, our mean-field model is strongly interacting.
3
: Our microscopic model is the Bose–Hubbard model, which is a lattice model that has been successfully used to describe the BEC-Mott transition.

Our main result is convergence of the reduced one-lattice-site density matrix of the many-body Schrödinger dynamics to the mean-field dynamics, with an error bound that goes to zero as the dimension
d
goes to infinity. Other parameters such as the density and the coupling remain fixed. Thus, we rigorously justify the validity of the mean-field approximation for a quantum system in large dimension. We choose the Bose–Hubbard model to illustrate this statement both for its remarkable usefulness in physics and for the technical simplifications it offers as a lattice model. The Bose–Hubbard model is a popular model used to describe bosons on a lattice with on-site interactions, allowing hopping between nearest-neighbor lattice sites. It is well known for capturing strong interactions between particles
[
4
]
and providing one of the simplest descriptions of the Mott transition to date; see
[
21
]
and later
[
30
]
, see also
[
27
,
26
]
.

A common technique to study models such as Bose–Hubbard is Dynamical Mean-field Theory (DMFT). This theory is well known for its description of the Mott insulator/superfluid phase transition
[
29
,
21
]
. It is usually formulated via Green’s function, using a self-consistency condition to reduce the problem on the full lattice to an effective single-site problem. DMFT is typically justified in the physics literature by stating that mean-field theories become exact in the limit of infinite dimensions
[
39
]
; see also
[
36
]
for fermions. A remarkable fact is that DMFT tends to provide qualitatively good results in three dimensions already
[
24
]
. In the literature, the effective equation we are deriving here is often called the mean-field model of Fisher et al.
[
11
]
, referring to
[
21
]
. A more involved mean-field type equation is obtained in
[
11
]
by scaling different parts of the hopping term in different ways. Compared to this scaling and the fermionic case, our equation can hence be considered as a simple case of DMFT.

Let us also mention the result
[
21
]
, where the authors consider the Bose–Hubbard model on a complete graph (the hopping term is of equal strength between all vertices). In comparison, our model has only nearest-neighbor hopping. Rigorous justifications for the effective thermodynamic behavior of the Bose–Hubbard model on a complete graph were obtained in
[
10
]
. As mentioned above, in the mathematical literature, mean-field limits are typically considered as many-particle limits for the Bose–Hubbard model
[
42
]
or, more generally, for continuous models where the Hartree equation is obtained as effective dynamics (see, e.g.,
[
3
,
25
]
for reviews). This approach requires dividing the interaction term by the number of particles to ensure that the kinetic energy and the interaction energy are of the same order.

Our goal is to provide a rigorous justification, in the
d\to\infty
limit, that DMFT is a good approximation of the Schrödinger equation in the context of the Bose–Hubbard model. Here the dimension
d
is the mean-field parameter that plays the usual role of the particle number
N
as we average over the
d
directions instead of the
N
particles. It is interesting to note that, in the large
d
limit, the roles of the kinetic energy and the interaction between particles are reversed compared to the mean-field limit
N\to\infty
. The terms we aim to average in our regime are the hopping terms between nearest-neighbor sites which come from the kinetic energy. Since we only consider on-site interactions, the interaction between particles acts as a one-site operator and therefore does not contribute to correlations between two different lattice sites. For our setting, the basic idea behind the mean-field approximation is that the coordination number of the lattice (the number of nearest neighbors) increases with the dimension. This means that we have a mean-field picture locally around every site, which allows us to control the correlations between sites. Note that our main estimates are for the reduced one-lattice-site density matrix, and not for the one-particle reduced density matrix that is usually used to describe convergence in the large
N
limit.

2
The Model and Main Results

2.1
Model

We consider the
d
-dimensional square lattice with periodic boundary conditions
\Lambda\coloneq\left(\mathbb{Z}/\penalty L\mathbb{Z}\right)^{d}
of volume
|\Lambda|\coloneq L^{d}
, with
L\in\mathbb{N},L\geq 2
. We write
x\sim y
if
x,y\in\Lambda
are nearest neighbors. The one-site Hilbert space is
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})
and its canonical Hilbert basis is denoted by
(\left|n\right>)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. We define the standard creation and annihilation operators
a^{*}
,
a
satisfying the CCR
[a,a^{*}]=1
,
[a,a]=0=[a^{*},a^{*}]
; explicitly,

\displaystyle a\left|0\right>\coloneq 0,a\left|n\right>\coloneq\sqrt{n}\left|n-1\right>\forall n\in\mathbb{N}^{*},

\displaystyle a^{*}\left|n\right>\coloneq\sqrt{n+1}\left|n+1\right>\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

The number operator is given as
\mathcal{N}\coloneq a^{*}a
. To simplify our notation in some later proofs, we introduce an order on
\Lambda
such that
\forall x\in\Lambda

\displaystyle\#\left\{y\in\Lambda|y>x\text{ and }x\sim y\right\}=\#\left\{y\in\Lambda|x>y\text{ and }x\sim y\right\}=d.

For example, the lexicographic order does the job. The Fock space is

\mathcal{F}\coloneq\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})^{\otimes\left|\Lambda\right|}\cong\mathcal{F}_{+}\left(L^{2}(\Lambda,\mathbb{C})\right)\coloneq\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{N}}L^{2}(\Lambda,\mathbb{C})^{\otimes_{+}k},

(1)

where
\otimes_{+}
denotes the symmetric tensor product. We emphasize that the Fock space is not symmetric with respect to lattice sites, as in general two lattice sites cannot be exchanged without changes to the lattice structure. It is, however, symmetric with respect to particle exchanges, as one can see from the isomorphism in (
1
).

Given a one-site operator
A
and
x\in\Lambda
, we define

\displaystyle A_{x}\coloneq\mathds{1}^{\otimes\left|\left\{y\in\Lambda,y<x\right\}\right|}\otimes A\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes\left|\left\{y\in\Lambda,x<y\right\}\right|}.

In the following, we define
\langle x,y\rangle
to mean that
x,y\in\Lambda,x\sim y
and
x<y
. The kinetic energy is given by the negative second quantized discrete Laplacian

\displaystyle-\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Gamma(\Delta_{d})\coloneq

\displaystyle\sum_{\langle x,y\rangle}(a^{*}_{x}-a^{*}_{y})(a_{x}-a_{y})=-\sum_{\langle x,y\rangle}\left(a^{*}_{x}a_{y}+a^{*}_{y}a_{x}\right)+2d\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\mathcal{N}_{x}.

Furthermore, we denote by
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{F}}
the number operator on Fock space, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{F}}\coloneq d\Gamma(1):=\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\mathcal{N}_{x}.

Given hopping amplitude
J\in\mathbb{R}
, chemical potential
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, and coupling constant
U\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the Bose–Hubbard Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{d}\coloneq

\displaystyle-\frac{J}{2d}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Gamma(\Delta_{d})-\mu\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{F}}+\frac{U}{2}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\mathcal{N}_{x}(\mathcal{N}_{x}-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{J}{2d}\sum_{\langle x,y\rangle}\left(a^{*}_{x}a_{y}+a^{*}_{y}a_{x}\right)+(J-\mu)\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\mathcal{N}_{x}+\frac{U}{2}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\mathcal{N}_{x}(\mathcal{N}_{x}-1).

(2)

Here, we have scaled down the hopping term with the inverse of the dimension
d
.
The time-dependent Schrödinger equation for
\Psi_{d}\in\mathcal{F}
is

i\partial_{t}\Psi_{d}(t)=H_{d}\Psi_{d}(t).

(3)

The mean-field approximation amounts to a lattice site product state ansatz
\Psi_{d}\approx\prod_{x\in\Lambda}\varphi_{x}
where
\varphi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})
is a one-lattice-site wave function. Such a state
\prod_{x\in\Lambda}\varphi_{x}
is sometimes called Gutzwiller product state
[
28
,
44
]
. Our main results state that if
\Psi_{d}(0)\approx\prod_{x\in\Lambda}\varphi_{x}(0)
, then also
\Psi_{d}(t)\approx\prod_{x\in\Lambda}\varphi_{x}(t)
for all times
t>0
, where
\approx
is meant in an appropriate reduced sense. We can guess the right mean-field equation by rewriting the Hamiltonian (
2
) for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}H_{d}&=-J\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\left(\alpha a^{*}_{x}+\overline{\alpha}a_{x}-|\alpha|^{2}\right)+(J-\mu)\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\mathcal{N}_{x}+\frac{U}{2}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\mathcal{N}_{x}(\mathcal{N}_{x}-1)\\
&\quad-\frac{J}{2d}\sum_{\langle x,y\rangle}\Big((a_{x}^{*}-\overline{\alpha})(a_{y}-\alpha)+(a^{*}_{y}-\overline{\alpha})(a_{x}-\alpha)\Big),\end{split}

(4)

neglecting all terms that are quadratic in
a-\alpha
(i.e., the last line in (
4
)), and choosing
\alpha
as the averaged annihilation operator. Then the corresponding mean-field equation is

i\partial_{t}\varphi(t)=h^{\varphi(t)}\varphi(t)

(5)

with nonlinear mean-field operator

h^{\varphi}\coloneq-J\big(\alpha_{\varphi}a^{*}+\overline{\alpha_{\varphi}}a-\left|\alpha_{\varphi}\right|^{2}\big)+(J-\mu)\mathcal{N}+\frac{U}{2}\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{N}-1),

(6)

and with order parameter
\alpha_{\varphi}\coloneqq\langle\varphi,a\varphi\rangle
. The role of
\alpha_{\varphi}
as an order parameter in the Mott-insulator/superfluid phase transition is developed in Remark
7
below. For a discussion of the corresponding mean-field energy functional and convergence of the ground state energies we refer to
[
19
]
.

The well-posedness of (
5
) is discussed in Section
3
; see in particular Theorem
8
. We would like to emphasize the richness of this mean-field dynamics (for
U\neq 0
) as it may exhibit a phase transition. For example, it is possible to find an initial state in the Mott insulator phase, i.e., with
\varphi(0)\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\alpha_{\varphi}(0)=0
, but
\alpha_{\varphi}^{\prime}(0)>0
, meaning that the state ends up in the superfluid phase (
\alpha_{\varphi}(t)\neq 0
) for a neighborhood of
t>0
.

The approximation
\Psi_{d}\approx\prod_{x\in\Lambda}\varphi_{x}
is not expected to hold in
\mathcal{F}
, but in the sense of reduced lattice site density matrices. Given
\Psi_{d}
, the corresponding density matrix is
\gamma_{d}\coloneq\left|\Psi_{d}\right>\left<\Psi_{d}\right|\in\mathcal{L}^{1}\left(\mathcal{F}\right)
and satisfies the von Neumann equation

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\gamma_{d}(t)=\left[H_{d},\gamma_{d}(t)\right].

(7)

For
\gamma_{d}\in\mathcal{L}^{1}\left(\mathcal{F}\right)
a positive trace one operator, we define its first reduced one-lattice-site density matrix as

\displaystyle\gOne\coloneq\frac{1}{\left|\Lambda\right|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\mathrm{Tr}_{\Lambda\backslash{\left\{x\right\}}}\left(\gamma_{d}\right),

where the partial trace is defined as follows. For all
\Gamma\subseteq\Lambda
, and for all
\left(A^{(x)}\right)_{x\in\Lambda}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C}))
, we set

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{\Gamma}\left(\bigotimes_{x\in\Lambda}A^{(x)}\right)\coloneq\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bigotimes_{x\in\Gamma}A^{(x)}\right)\bigotimes_{x\in\Lambda\backslash\Gamma}A^{(x)}.

The operator
\gOne:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})\to\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})
should not be confused with the reduced one-particle density matrix
\gamma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{particle}}:L^{2}(\Lambda)\to L^{2}(\Lambda)
defined via its integral kernel
\gamma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{particle}}(x,y)=\langle\Psi_{d},a^{*}_{y}a_{x}\Psi_{d}\rangle
with
x,y\in\Lambda
. Given
\varphi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})
, let us also introduce the corresponding orthogonal projections

p=p_{\varphi}:=|\varphi\rangle\langle\varphi|,\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \penalty\ q=q_{\varphi}:=1-p_{\varphi}.

(8)

In our main results (Theorems
1
and
2
below), we prove convergence of
\gOne(t)
to
p_{\varphi}(t)
.

2.2
Main Results

Our main results are estimates for the trace-norm difference of
\gOne(t)
and
p_{\varphi}(t)
, which we denote by
\big\|\gOne(t)-p_{\varphi}(t)\big\|_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}
. We prove two similar estimates. Theorem
1
proves an error bound that holds for any value of the parameters
J,\mu,U
of the Bose–Hubbard model (
2
). The convergence rate is slightly worse than
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
. However, we need to assume stronger conditions on the initial data, and the bound contains a double exponential growth in time. On the other hand, Theorem
2
holds only for repulsive interaction, i.e.,
U>0
. However, it holds for a larger class of initial data, and the error bound only grows exponentially in time. Our first main result is the following.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\gamma_{d}
be the solution to (
7
) with initial data
\gamma_{d}(0)\in\mathcal{L}^{1}\left(\mathcal{F}\right)
such that
\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma_{d}(0)\right)=1
, and let
\varphi
be the solution to (
5

\varphi(0)\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}}=1
. Let

be defined as in (
8

We assume that there exist

a,c>0
such that

\displaystyle\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\mathrm{Tr}\left(p_{\varphi}(0)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{N}=n}\right)\leq ce^{-\frac{n}{a}}\penalty\ \text{ and }\penalty\ \mathrm{Tr}\left(\gOne(0)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{N}=n}\right)\leq ce^{-\frac{n}{a}}.

(9)

Then for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\gOne(t)-p_{\varphi}(t)\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\left(\left\lVert\gOne(0)-p_{\varphi}(0)\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}+\frac{C_{2}e^{C_{1}t}+\mathrm{Tr}\left(p_{\varphi}(0)\mathcal{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{d\left(C_{4}+2\left(\sqrt{2(a+e)}e^{\frac{C_{1}}{2}t}+1\right)\sqrt{\ln(d+1)}\right)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\quad e^{JC_{3}\left(C_{4}+2\left(\sqrt{2(a+e)}e^{\frac{C_{1}}{2}t}+1\right)\sqrt{\ln(d+1)}\right)t},

(10)

with the following constants independent of

and
t

\displaystyle C_{1}\coloneq 2eJ\max\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left(p_{\varphi}(0)\mathcal{N}\right),1\right),

\displaystyle C_{2}\coloneq 4\left(c(1+a)+e^{-1}\right)\left(2+4(a+e)\right),

\displaystyle C_{3}\coloneq\left(\mathrm{Tr}\left(p_{\varphi}(0)\mathcal{N}\right)+1\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle C_{4}\coloneq 4\mathrm{Tr}\left(p_{\varphi}(0)\mathcal{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+2.

Note that the
d

\displaystyle\left(d\sqrt{\ln(d+1)}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{C\sqrt{\ln(d+1)}t}=e^{C\sqrt{\ln(d+1)}t-\frac{1}{2}\ln(d)-\frac{1}{4}\ln(\ln(d+1))}\limit\displaylimits_{d\to\infty}0

for any
C,t>0
. Our second main result is as follows.

.

Let
\gamma_{d}
be the solution to (

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gOne(\mathcal{N}+1)^{2k}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{N}\geq M}\right)+\mathrm{Tr}\left(p_{\varphi}(\mathcal{N}+1)^{2k}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{N}\geq M}\right)\limit\displaylimits_{M\to\infty}0.

and any choice of
M\gg 1
such that
M^{k}\left\lVert\gOne-p_{\varphi}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}\ll 1
as
d\to\infty
is sufficient to prove that

\displaystyle\left|\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gOne\mathcal{O}\right)-\mathrm{Tr}\left(p_{\varphi}\mathcal{O}\right)\right|\limit\displaylimits_{d\to\infty}0

In particular this applies to the order parameter since
\left\lVert(\mathcal{N}+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}a\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}}\leq 1
.
In line with the above arguments, we can similarly establish that the reduced one-particle density matrix
\gamma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{particle}}
, defined via its integral kernel
\gamma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{particle}}(x,y)=\langle\Psi_{d},a^{*}_{y}a_{x}\Psi_{d}\rangle
with
x,y\in\Lambda
,
converges in Hilbert-Schmidt norm. Specifically, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Lambda|^{2}}\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda}\Big|\langle\Psi_{d}(t),a_{y}^{*}a_{x}\Psi_{d}(t)\rangle-|\alpha_{\varphi(t)}|^{2}\Big|^{2}\rightarrow 0\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{as}\penalty\ d\to\infty.

To prove this, we can insert first the identities
p_{x}+q_{x}=1
and
p_{y}+q_{y}=1
inside
\langle\Psi_{d}(t),a_{y}^{*}a_{x}\Psi_{d}(t)\rangle
and then all the resulting terms can be bounded by
{\rm Tr}(\gOne(q+q\mathcal{N}^{2}q))
which converges to zero as
d\rightarrow\infty
. The convergence of the latter quantity follows from Propositions
23
and
26
.
Moreover, we can also establish that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d|\Lambda|}\sum_{<x,y>}\langle\Psi_{d}(t),a_{x}^{*}a_{y}\Psi_{d}(t)\rangle\to|\alpha_{\varphi(t)}|^{2}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{as}\penalty\ d\to\infty,

which implies convergence of the kinetic energy.

Remark 5
.

Our theorems imply factorization of higher order reduced lattice-site density matrices. For example, let us denote by

\displaystyle\eta_{d}^{(2)}\coloneq\frac{1}{\left|\Lambda\right|^{2}}\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda}\mathrm{Tr}_{\Lambda\backslash\left\{x,y\right\}}\left(\gamma_{d}\right)

the general reduced two-lattice-site density matrix. With a standard argument where we insert
(p_{\varphi}+q_{\varphi})\otimes(p_{\varphi}+q_{\varphi})
on both sides of

we can obtain with a similar argument as in Lemma

\displaystyle\left\lVert\eta_{d}^{(2)}-p_{\varphi}^{\otimes 2}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}\lesssim\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma_{d}^{(1)}q_{\varphi}\right)}\limit\displaylimits_{d\to\infty}0.

Remark 6
.

We think that our work does not fall into the setting for well-know many-body mean-field limit derivations because of two reasons:

•

hopping occurs only between nearest neighbours,

•

the Fock space does not have lattice-site symmetry.

For instance,
[
35
]
is written in a general setting that would include
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})
as particle Hilbert space,
a^{*}\otimes a
as interaction and
(J-\mu)\mathcal{N}+\frac{U}{2}\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{N}-1)
as external potential, but then the model would be

-\frac{J}{\left|\Lambda\right|}\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda}\left(a^{*}_{x}a_{y}+a^{*}_{y}a_{x}\right)+(J-\mu)\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\mathcal{N}_{x}+\frac{U}{2}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\mathcal{N}_{x}(\mathcal{N}_{x}-1),

(15)

i.e., a model with constant hopping amplitude between any two sites, with the Hamiltonian acting on symmetric products of the one-lattice-site Hilbert space. In this case, indeed, the large-d limit would be an equivalent setting to the standard many-body mean-field. We believe that (
15
) can also be treated with our method of proof, but this is not as physically relevant because it would break the local characteristic of the Bose–Hubbard model. Note that this local aspect makes our limit a bit similar to the large volume mean-field limit, see, e.g.,
[
15
,
14
,
41
]
, where the interaction is averaged over a small proportion of the total system volume as well.

Both theorems are proven using a Gronwall estimate for
\text{Tr}(\gOne q_{\varphi})
. This quantity heuristically counts the average number of lattice sites that do not follow the product state ansatz. It is inspired by the corresponding quantity for the weak coupling limit introduced by Pickl
[
43
]
. The main technical challenge is then caused by the unboundedness of the creation and annihilation operators in the hopping term, which is a bit analogous to the technical problems that arise when considering the weak-coupling limit with singular interactions; see, e.g.,
[
31
]
. More concretely, we need to bound
\text{Tr}(\gOne q_{\varphi}(\mathcal{N}+1)q_{\varphi})
in terms of
\text{Tr}(\gOne q_{\varphi})
or terms that go to zero as
d\to\infty
. We do this in two different ways, leading to the two main theorems.

For Theorem
1
, we use a moment method which localizes in the particle number
\mathcal{N}
. This idea has been used before in the context of bosonic dynamics
[
37
]
and spectrum
[
33
]

[
34
]
under the name
localization in Fock space
. For this, we first separate

\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gOne q_{\varphi}(\mathcal{N}+1)q_{\varphi}\right)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gOne q_{\varphi}(\mathcal{N}+1)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{N}<M}q_{\varphi}\right)+\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gOne q_{\varphi}(\mathcal{N}+1)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{N}\geq M}q_{\varphi}\right).

Then the first term can simply be bounded by
M
, whereas we use moment estimates to bound the second term by
e^{MC(t)}d^{-1}
. Then it turns out to be possible to optimize in
M
to close the Gronwall argument. The introduction of this cutoff parameter is inspired by
[
40
]
, where a Landau level cutoff is introduced, and the remainder term is controlled through moments of the kinetic energy operator in a similar manner as what we do here with the number of particles operator.

For Theorem
2
, we proceed using an energy estimate inspired by
[
32
]
(which deals with proving Bogoliubov theory for the dynamics of the weakly interacting Bose gas). The idea is to write the Hamiltonian (
2
) as in (
4
), i.e., as

H_{d}=\sum_{x\in\Lambda}h^{\varphi(t)}_{x}+\widetilde{H}(t).

Here,
\widetilde{H}(t)
describes the excitations around our product state ansatz. A similar splitting was used in
[
32
]
, where, after a unitary transformation,
\widetilde{H}(t)
converges to a Bogoliubov Hamiltonian. The energy of the excitations should now be defined as

E^{\mathrm{exc}}(t):=\Big\langle\Psi_{d},\left(\widetilde{H}(t)+\sum_{x\in\Lambda}q_{x}h^{\varphi(t)}_{x}q_{x}\right)\Psi_{d}\Big\rangle,

(16)

where the first term corresponds to the kinetic energy, and the second term to the mean-field energy of the excitations (including the interaction energy). The energy
E^{\mathrm{exc}}(t)
is not conserved, as excitations from the product state can be created and annihilated. However, it can be bounded using a Gronwall argument. The crucial point here is that the interaction term enters only as

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\Lambda}q_{x}\mathcal{N}_{x}(\mathcal{N}_{x}-1)q_{x},

and hence the Gronwall argument does not produce higher powers than
q_{x}\mathcal{N}_{x}^{2}q_{x}
. This ultimately allows us to control terms that involve
q_{x}\mathcal{N}_{x}^{2}q_{x}
or
q_{x}\mathcal{N}_{x}q_{x}
, in particular
\text{Tr}(\gOne q_{\varphi}(\mathcal{N}+1)q_{\varphi})
.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We first prove global well-posedness of the mean-field equation (

We expect that these methods can be applied to a larger class of lattice models. For example one may consider a nearest neighbour interaction term and average out this interaction as it is done in this paper for the hopping term. If
V
denotes the nearest neighbours’ coupling, this would lead to the approximation

\displaystyle\dfrac{V}{2d}\sum_{\braket{x,y}}\left(\mathcal{N}_{x}\mathcal{N}_{y}+\mathcal{N}_{y}\mathcal{N}_{x}\right)\eqvl\displaylimits_{d\to\infty}V\braket{\varphi,\mathcal{N}\varphi}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\mathcal{N}_{x}.

A natural generalization of our results would be to consider a general graph
\mathcal{G}_{z}
with a large (and constant) coordination number

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{G}_{z}\right|\gg z\,.

The hopping term should then be replaced by

and the resulting estimates hold after replacing

Remark 7

(Overview of the phase transition)
.

This paper focuses on the dynamics, but the phase transition in the mean-field model provided by (
6
) is better understood from an energy perspective. Given a minimizer of the energy functional

\displaystyle\varphi\mapsto\braket{\varphi,h^{\varphi}\varphi}=-J\left|\alpha_{\varphi}^{2}\right|+\braket{\varphi,((J-\mu)\mathcal{N}+\frac{U}{2}\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{N}-1))\varphi}

(17)

over
\varphi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}}=1
and
\mathcal{N}\varphi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})
, we claim that
\alpha_{\varphi}=0
indicates a Mott insulator state, whereas
\alpha_{\varphi}\neq 0
indicates a superfluid state.

Indeed, if
\alpha_{\varphi}=0
, then the ground state of (
6
) is
\left|n_{0}\right>
with

\displaystyle n_{0}\coloneq\argmin\displaylimits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left((J-\mu)n+\frac{U}{2}n(n-1)\right)=\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\mu-J}{U}\right\rceil,

where
\left\lfloor\right\rceil
is the nearest integer function. Here, the number of particles at every site is constant and fixed, which is representative of an insulator state. Notice, nevertheless, that a degeneracy occurs for half-integer values of
\frac{\mu-J}{U}
.

A very different behavior occurs when
J\gg\mu,U
. We assume for simplicity that
\mu=U=0
. Then the energy is minimal (and equal to
0
) if and only if the following Cauchy–Schwarz inequality is sharp:

\displaystyle\left|\alpha_{\varphi}\right|^{2}\leq\braket{\varphi,\mathcal{N}\varphi}.

This situation is obtained for the family of coherent states

\displaystyle\varphi_{\alpha}\coloneq e^{-\frac{\left|\alpha\right|^{2}}{2}+\alpha a^{*}}\left|0\right>,\alpha\in\mathbb{C},

satisfying
\braket{\varphi_{\alpha},a\varphi_{\alpha}}=\alpha
and
\braket{\varphi_{\alpha},\mathcal{N}\varphi_{\alpha}}=\left|\alpha\right|^{2}
. The macroscopic state is then

\displaystyle\varphi_{\alpha}^{\otimes\left|\Lambda\right|}=e^{-\frac{\left|\alpha\right|^{2}\Lambda}{2}+\alpha\sum_{x\in\Lambda}a_{x}^{*}}\left|\Omega\right>=e^{-\frac{\left|\alpha\right|^{2}\Lambda}{2}+\alpha\sqrt{\left|\Lambda\right|}a^{*}(u)}\left|\Omega\right>

with
\Omega=\left|0\right>^{\otimes\left|\Lambda\right|}\in\mathcal{F}
being the vacuum state,
u:x\in\Lambda\mapsto\left|\Lambda\right|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\in L^{2}(\Lambda)
being a
1
-particle state with constant density over the lattice, and

\displaystyle a^{*}(u)\coloneq\sum_{x\in\Lambda}u(x)a_{x}^{*}.

Hence,
\varphi_{\alpha}^{\otimes\left|\Lambda\right|}
is a Bose–Einstein condensate of
\left|\alpha\right|^{2}\left|\Lambda\right|
particles. We refer to
[
21
]
for more details about the phase-transition, and to
[
19
]
for a derivation of the mean-field energy functional (
17
) starting from the Bose–Hubbard model.

3
Global Well-posedness of the Mean-Field Dynamics

The goal of this section is to prove well-posedness of the mean-field dynamics. The mean-field dynamics can be written as

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}\varphi=h^{\varphi}\varphi=A\varphi+F(\varphi),\\
\varphi(0)=\varphi_{0},\end{cases}

(18)

where the linear operator
A
and the nonlinear operator
F
are defined as

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=(J-\mu)\mathcal{N}+\frac{U}{2}\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{N}-1),

(19)

\displaystyle F(\varphi)

\displaystyle:=-J\left(\alpha_{\varphi}a^{*}+\overline{\alpha_{\varphi}}a-|\alpha_{\varphi}|^{2}\right)\varphi.

(20)

The Duhamel version of the mean-field dynamics (
18
) is

\varphi(t)=\tilde{\varphi}_{0}(t)-i\int_{0}^{t}e^{-i(t-s)A}F(\varphi(s))\,ds,

(21)

with
\tilde{\varphi}_{0}(t):=e^{-itA}\varphi_{0}
.
When examining the semi-linear equation (
18
) or (
21
) above, we cannot directly apply fixed-point arguments to study global well-posedness because both
A
and
F
are unbounded operators, and the nonlinear operator
F
is not Lipschitz continuous. Therefore, a different approach is required. Our strategy is to approximate the nonlinear term so that it becomes Lipschitz continuous. This allows us to establish the existence of a unique solution to the approximated problem by standard methods. Then, we show that the obtained solution converges to the solution of the untruncated mean-field equation. To this end, we have the following result. Denote by
W^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C}))
the Sobolev space endowed with the norm

\|\varphi\|_{W^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C}))}:=\|\varphi\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C}))}+\|\partial_{t}\varphi\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C}))}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\varphi(t)\|_{2}\ dt+\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\partial_{t}\varphi(t)\|_{2}\ dt.

Theorem 8

(Global existence of the solution to the mean-field dynamics)
.

Assume
\varphi_{0}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{N}^{2})
. There exists a solution
\varphi(\cdot)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{N}^{2}))\cap W^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C}))
to the mean-field dynamic (
18
) for almost all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

The proof of Theorem
8
is postponed to the end of this section. In Paragraph
3.1
, we first introduce the approximate model of (
18
). Next, in Paragraph
3.2
, we establish several conservation laws for the approximate dynamics. In the following Paragraph
3.3
, we prove the global well-posedness of the approximate dynamics. Finally in Paragraph
3.4
, we show that the solution of the Duhamel formula for the mean-field dynamics
21
can be obtained as the limit of these approximate solutions and we conclude by completing the proof of Theorem
8
.

3.1
Approximating the Mean-field Dynamics

First, we truncate the mean-field dynamics by introducing a cutoff on the number operator
\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{N}\leq M}
. We then prove uniform estimates in
M
, which yield well-posedness at the end of the section. Similar cutoff (or localization) techniques have appeared before and were used to control large excitation sectors in rigorous derivations and analysis of many-body quantum dynamics, see e.g.
[
9
,
38
]
. Let
M>0
and consider the truncated creation and annihilation operators

\displaystyle a_{M}:=a\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{N}\leq M},\qquad a^{*}_{M}:=\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{N}\leq M}a^{*}

(22)

and

\displaystyle\alpha_{M}:=\langle\varphi_{M},a_{M}\varphi_{M}\rangle,

(23)

where
\varphi_{M}
is the solution to the approximated problem

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}\varphi_{M}=h_{M}^{\alpha_{M}}\varphi_{M}=A\varphi_{M}+F_{M}(\varphi_{M}),\\
\varphi_{M}(0)=\varphi_{0}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{N}^{2}),\end{cases}

(24)

where we have introduced the approximated nonlinear operator
F_{M}
as

\displaystyle F_{M}(\varphi_{M})

\displaystyle:=-J\left(\alpha_{M}a^{*}_{M}+\overline{\alpha_{M}}a_{M}-|\alpha_{M}|^{2}\right)\varphi_{M}.

(25)

The solution to (
24
) solves the weak form of the preceding nonlinear equation (
24
) which is usually known as Duhamel formula

\begin{cases}\varphi_{M}(t)=\tilde{\varphi}_{0}(t)-i\int_{0}^{t}e^{-i(t-s)A}F_{M}(\varphi_{M}(s))\,ds,\\
\tilde{\varphi}_{0}(t):=e^{-itA}\varphi_{0},\qquad\varphi_{0}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{N}^{2}).\end{cases}

(26)

Remark 9
.

Note the following:

1.

For the weak formulation (
26
) of the approximated nonlinear equation (
24
), the existence of a unique local solution can be established using fixed-point arguments for a broader class of initial data, specifically
\varphi_{0}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})
, which leads to the existence of a unique local solution
\varphi_{M}\in C([0,T],\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C}))
. This follows from the fact that
F_{M}
is a nonlinear operator satisfying

. However, to extend the solution to global times, we rely on the conservation laws, which require the initial data
\varphi_{0}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{N}^{2})
, as
A
remains an unbounded operator.

2.

Since

, to ensure the equivalence between (
24
) and (
26
), it is enough to restrict our analysis to initial data
\varphi_{0}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{N}^{2})
. For further details, see
[
12

.

3.

One could in addition truncate the unbounded linear term
A
. On the one hand, this approach ensures equivalence between (
24
) and (
26
) for all initial data
\varphi_{0}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})
. On the other hand, it allows us to obtain a unique global strong solution

. However, ensuring convergence to the solution of the mean-field dynamics becomes more complicated.

Properties of the Approximate Solution

In this subsection, we state some conservation laws for the approximated problem.

.

Assume that
\varphi_{M}
is a solution to (

(i)

\|\varphi_{M}(t)\|_{\ell^{2}}=\|\varphi_{0}\|_{\ell^{2}}=1.

(ii)

\langle\varphi_{M}(t),\mathcal{N}\varphi_{M}(t)\rangle=\langle\varphi_{0},\mathcal{N}\varphi_{0}\rangle.

(iii)

\langle\varphi_{M}(t),h_{M}^{\alpha_{M}}\varphi_{M}(t)\rangle=\langle\varphi_{0},h_{M}^{\alpha_{M}}\varphi_{0}\rangle.

Statement (i) is true by definition of the truncation. For (ii), note that

This gives

For (iii), we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\langle\varphi_{M},h_{M}^{\alpha_{M}}\varphi_{M}\rangle

\displaystyle=i\langle\varphi_{M},[h_{M}^{\alpha_{M}},h_{M}^{\alpha_{M}}]\varphi_{M}\rangle+\langle\varphi_{M},\partial_{t}h_{M}^{\alpha_{M}}\varphi_{M}\rangle

\displaystyle=-J\left\langle\varphi_{M},\left(\partial_{t}\alpha_{M}a_{M}^{*}+\partial_{t}\overline{\alpha_{M}}a_{M}-\partial_{t}\alpha_{M}\overline{\alpha_{M}}-\partial_{t}\overline{\alpha_{M}}{\alpha_{M}}\right)\varphi_{M}\right\rangle=0.

\displaystyle=-iJ\alpha_{M}\langle\varphi_{M},[a^{*}_{M},\mathcal{N}^{k}]\varphi_{M}\rangle-iJ\overline{\alpha_{M}}\langle\varphi_{M},[a_{M},\mathcal{N}^{k}]\varphi_{M}\rangle

\displaystyle=-iJ\alpha_{M}\langle\varphi_{M},\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{N}\leq M}[a^{*},\mathcal{N}^{k}]\varphi_{M}\rangle-iJ\overline{\alpha_{M}}\langle\varphi_{M},[a,\mathcal{N}^{k}]\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{N}\leq M}\varphi_{M}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq 2k|J||\alpha_{M}||\langle\varphi_{M},\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{N}\leq M}a^{*}\mathcal{N}^{k-1}\varphi_{M}\rangle|

Iterating this
(2k-2)
times leads to

\langle\varphi_{M},\mathcal{N}^{k}\varphi_{M}\rangle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{2k-2}\left(C|J|k\|\mathcal{N}^{1/2}\varphi_{0}\|_{\ell^{2}}\right)^{j}\left\langle\varphi_{0},(\mathcal{N}+j)^{k-\frac{j}{2}}\varphi_{0}\right\rangle\frac{t^{j}}{j!}.

(29)

3.3

\displaystyle|\alpha_{M,i}(s)|\leq\sqrt{M}\|\varphi_{i}(s)\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}\leq\sqrt{M}c^{2},

(31)

\displaystyle|\alpha_{M,1}(s)-\alpha_{M,2}(s)|\leq 2\sqrt{M}c\|\varphi_{1}(s)-\varphi_{2}(s)\|_{\ell^{2}},

(32)

\displaystyle\left||\alpha_{M,1}(s)|^{2}-|\alpha_{M,2}(s)|^{2}\right|\leq 4Mc^{3}\|\varphi_{1}(s)-\varphi_{2}(s)\|_{\ell^{2}}.

(33)

We have then for all
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\|\Gamma_{M}(\varphi_{1})(t)-\Gamma_{M}(\varphi_{2})(t)\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-i(t-s)A}\left(F_{M}(\varphi_{1}(s))-F_{M}(\varphi_{2}(s))\right)\,ds\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq|J|\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(\left\|\overline{\alpha_{M,1}(s)}a_{M}\varphi_{1}(s)-\overline{\alpha_{M,2}(s)}a_{M}\varphi_{2}(s)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\quad\ \ +\left\|\alpha_{M,1}(s)a_{M}^{*}\varphi_{1}(s)-\alpha_{M,2}(s)a_{M}^{*}\varphi_{2}(s)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\quad\ \ +\left\||{\alpha_{M,1}(s)}|^{2}\varphi_{1}(s)-|{\alpha_{M,2}(s)}|^{2}\varphi_{2}(s)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\bigg)ds.

We begin by considering the first term. Using (
30
) with
k=0
, along with (
31
) and (
32
), we get

\displaystyle\left\|\overline{\alpha_{M,1}(s)}a_{M}\varphi_{1}(s)-\overline{\alpha_{M,2}(s)}a_{M}\varphi_{2}(s)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq|\alpha_{M,1}(s)-\alpha_{M,2}(s)|\|a_{M}\varphi_{1}(s)\|_{\ell^{2}}+|\alpha_{M,2}(s)|\|a_{M}(\varphi_{1}(s)-\varphi_{2}(s))\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq 3{M}c^{2}\|\varphi_{1}(s)-\varphi_{2}(s)\|_{\ell^{2}}.

Similarly, for the second term, applying (
30
) with
k=0
, along with (
31
) and (
32
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|{\alpha_{M,1}(s)}a_{M}^{*}\varphi_{1}(s)-{\alpha_{M,2}(s)}a_{M}^{*}\varphi_{2}(s)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq 3{M}c^{2}\|\varphi_{1}(s)-\varphi_{2}(s)\|_{\ell^{2}}.

Finally, for the last term, by using (
30
) with
k=0
, along with (
31
) and (
33
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\||{\alpha_{M,1}(s)}|^{2}\varphi_{1}(s)-|{\alpha_{M,2}(s)}|^{2}\varphi_{2}(s)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left||{\alpha_{M,1}(s)}|^{2}-|{\alpha_{M,2}(s)}|^{2}\right|\|\varphi_{1}(s)\|_{\ell^{2}}+|{\alpha_{M,2}(s)}|^{2}\|\varphi_{1}(s)-\varphi_{2}(s)\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq 5{M}c^{4}\|\varphi_{1}(s)-\varphi_{2}(s)\|_{\ell^{2}}.

To summarize, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\Gamma_{M}(\varphi_{1})(t)-\Gamma_{M}(\varphi_{2})(t)\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq MT|J|(6c^{2}+5c^{4})|||\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}|||.

(34)

More generally, for any
k\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{N}^{k}\left(\Gamma_{M}(\varphi_{1})(t)-\Gamma_{M}(\varphi_{2})(t)\right)\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq MT|J|(6{M}^{k}c^{2}+5c^{4})\sup_{[0,T]}\|\varphi_{1}(s)-\varphi_{2}(s)\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{N}^{k})}.

(35)

Specifically, for the other component of the norm, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{N}^{2}\left(\Gamma_{M}(\varphi_{1})(t)-\Gamma_{M}(\varphi_{2})(t)\right)\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq MT|J|(6{M}^{2}c^{2}+5c^{4})|||\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}|||.

(36)

By combining the two estimates (
34
) and (
36
) above, we obtain

\displaystyle|||\Gamma_{M}(\varphi_{1})-\Gamma_{M}(\varphi_{2})|||\leq MT|J|(6c^{2}+6c^{2}{M}^{2}+10c^{4})|||\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}|||.

Considering the semilinear equation of the form (
24
), and noting that our nonlinearity is Lipschitz continuous (or can be made a contraction by choosing
T
sufficiently small), we can approach the problem in two ways. First, we can apply the local well-posedness results from
[
12
]
, specifically
[
12
, Lemma 4.3.2 and Proposition 4.3.3]
, to obtain a unique local solution. Then, we can extend this solution globally using
[
12
, Theorem 4.3.4]
by employing conservation laws, including the norm and the moment bounds (i)-(v) in Lemma
10
. On the other hand, we can establish global well-posedness by directly applying the Banach fixed point arguments. To this end, we consider the closed ball on the Banach space
X
defined by

B_{X}(\tilde{\varphi}_{0},R]:=\{\varphi\in X;\quad|||\varphi-\tilde{\varphi}_{0}|||\leq R\}.

Then, we check that for
T>0
small enough and for
R>0
large enough, the map
\Gamma_{M}
satisfies the condition of the Banach fixed point theorem, namely

•

\Gamma_{M}
maps
B_{X}(\tilde{\varphi}_{0},R]
into itself,

•

\Gamma_{M}:(X,|||\cdot|||)\to(X,|||\cdot|||)
is a contraction map,

guaranteeing the existence of a fixed point (
\varphi_{M}=\Gamma_{M}(\varphi_{M})\in X
). The solution can then be extended globally using the same conservation laws employed for the first approach.

Remark 12
.

In the above theorem, we can also apply the fixed point theorem in the Banach space
X=C([0,T],\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C}))
, which guarantees the existence of a unique local solution
\varphi_{M}\in C([0,T],\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C}))
. Subsequently, we can globalize the solution (which is equivalent to obtaining an estimate of the norm
\|\varphi_{M}(t)\|_{\ell^{2}}
on
[0,T]
) for the set of initial data
\varphi_{0}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{N}^{2})
, ensuring that

\|\varphi_{M}(t)\|_{\ell^{2}}=\|\varphi_{0}\|_{\ell^{2}},

where the above equality is proved in Lemma
10
. The above estimate implies that

\lim_{t\uparrow T}\|\varphi_{M}(t)\|_{\ell^{2}}=\|\varphi_{0}\|_{\ell^{2}}<\infty,

which guarantees that the solution does not blow up in finite time and thus
T=\infty
.

∎

3.4
Convergence

Assume that
\varphi_{0}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{N}^{k})
, then by (i) and (v) from Lemma
10
, we have that
(\varphi_{M})_{M\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\mathcal{N}^{k}\varphi_{M})_{M\in\mathbb{N}}
are bounded sequences in the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})
. Then there exist a convergent subsequence still denoted by
(\varphi_{M})_{M\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

•

\varphi_{M}
converges weakly to
\varphi
and the limit is unique,

•

\mathcal{N}^{k}\varphi_{M}
converges weakly to
\mathcal{N}^{k}\varphi
for all
k\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

As a consequence of this convergence, we have

\displaystyle\|\varphi\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\liminf_{M\rightarrow\infty}\|\varphi_{M}\|_{\ell^{2}},

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{N}^{k}\varphi\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\liminf_{M\rightarrow\infty}\|\mathcal{N}^{k}\varphi_{M}\|_{\ell^{2}}.

This in fact implies strong convergence.

Lemma 13
.

Assume that
\varphi_{0}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{N}^{k})
and let
(\varphi_{M})_{M}
be a sequence of solutions to (
24
) with
\|\varphi_{0}\|_{\ell^{2}}=1
and
\varphi
its associated weak limit. Then we have for all
k\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{N}^{k}(\varphi_{M}(t)-\varphi(t))\|_{\ell^{2}}\underset{M\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

(37)

Proof.

This follows from the
\text{weak}^{*}
convergence in the Banach space
\mathcal{L}^{1}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C}))=(\mathcal{K}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})))^{*}
, where
\mathcal{L}^{1}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C}))
and
\mathcal{K}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C}))
denote the space of trace-class and compact operators, respectively. Let
p_{\varphi_{M}}=|\varphi_{M}\rangle\langle\varphi_{M}|
be the projection onto the state
\varphi_{M}
, so that in particular
p_{\varphi_{M}}^{2}=p_{\varphi_{M}}
. We also have

\text{Tr}(p_{\varphi_{M}})=\|\varphi_{M}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}=1,\qquad\text{Tr}(\mathcal{N}^{k}p_{\varphi_{M}})=\langle\varphi_{M},\mathcal{N}^{k}\varphi_{M}\rangle<C^{\prime},

for some
C^{\prime}>0
, depending on
k
,
t
,
J
, and
\varphi_{0}
, but independent of
M
, as given in part (v) of Lemma
10
. This ensures the existence of a subsequence, still denoted by
(p_{\varphi_{M}})_{M}
, such that

p_{\varphi_{M}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\nu\quad\text{as }M\to\infty\quad\text{ weakly * in }\mathcal{L}^{1}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})),

\mathcal{N}^{k}p_{\varphi_{M}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\mathcal{N}^{k}\nu\quad\text{as }M\to\infty\quad\text{weakly * in }\mathcal{L}^{1}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})).

For any compact operator
B\in\mathcal{K}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C}))
, this implies

\text{Tr}(p_{\varphi_{M}}B)\to\text{Tr}(\nu B)\quad\text{and}\quad\text{Tr}(\mathcal{N}^{k}p_{\varphi_{M}}B)\to\text{Tr}(\mathcal{N}^{k}\nu B)\quad\text{as }M\to\infty.

For
k\geq 0
, this leads to

\displaystyle\text{Tr}(\mathcal{N}^{k}p_{\varphi_{M}})=\text{Tr}((\mathcal{N}+1)^{-1}(\mathcal{N}+1)\mathcal{N}^{k}p_{\varphi_{M}})\to\text{Tr}((\mathcal{N}+1)^{-1}(\mathcal{N}+1)\mathcal{N}^{k}\nu)=\text{Tr}(\mathcal{N}^{k}\nu).

(38)

Specifically, for
k=0
we have

\text{Tr}(p_{\varphi_{M}})=\|\varphi_{M}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}\to\text{Tr}(\nu),

which implies
\text{Tr}(\nu)=1
.
Now, using results from
[
46
]
, we obtain strong convergence for all
k\geq 0
,

\|\mathcal{N}^{k}p_{\varphi_{M}}-\mathcal{N}^{k}\nu\|_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}={\rm Tr}\left(\left|\mathcal{N}^{k}p_{\varphi_{M}}-\mathcal{N}^{k}\nu\ \right|\right)\to 0\quad\text{as }M\to\infty.

Since both
p_{\varphi_{M}}
and
\nu
are bounded in norm, we conclude that
p_{\varphi_{M}}^{2}=p_{\varphi_{M}}
converges strongly to
\nu=\nu^{2}
. Therefore,
\nu
is a projection, and
\nu=P_{\chi}=|\chi\rangle\langle\chi|
. Additionally, we have

\text{Tr}(p_{\varphi_{M}}\nu)=|\langle\varphi_{M},\chi\rangle|^{2}\to\text{Tr}(\nu^{2})=\text{Tr}(\nu)=1.

On the other hand, we also know

\langle\varphi_{M},\chi\rangle\to\langle\varphi,\chi\rangle,

which implies
|\langle\varphi,\chi\rangle|^{2}=1
, leading to
P_{\varphi}=P_{\chi}
. Therefore, by exploiting (
38
), we have

\mathcal{N}^{k}\varphi_{M}\rightharpoonup\mathcal{N}^{k}\varphi\quad\text{and}\quad\|\mathcal{N}^{k}\varphi_{M}\|_{\ell^{2}}\to\|\mathcal{N}^{k}\varphi\|_{\ell^{2}}.

Since
\ell^{2}
is a Hilbert space, these results imply strong convergence

\|\mathcal{N}^{k}(\varphi_{M}-\varphi)\|_{\ell^{2}}\to 0\quad\text{as }M\to\infty.

∎

Lemma 14

Assume that

\displaystyle\|\varphi\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\liminf_{M\rightarrow\infty}\|\varphi_{M}\|_{\ell^{2}}=\|\varphi_{0}\|_{\ell^{2}}=1,

(40)

\displaystyle|\alpha_{\varphi}|=|\langle\varphi,a\varphi\rangle|\leq\|\varphi\|_{\ell^{2}}\|a\varphi\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\|\mathcal{N}^{1/2}\varphi_{0}\|_{\ell^{2}}.

(41)

Moreover, we also have

\displaystyle|\alpha_{M}-\alpha_{\varphi}|

\displaystyle=|\langle\varphi_{M},a_{M}\varphi_{M}\rangle-\langle\varphi,a\varphi\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq|\langle\varphi_{M},(a_{M}-a)\varphi_{M}\rangle|+|\langle\varphi_{M},a(\varphi_{M}-\varphi)\rangle|+|\langle(\varphi_{M}-\varphi),a\varphi\rangle|

where we have introduced
{\mathcal{E}}_{1}(M)
as

{\mathcal{E}}_{1}(M):=\|a\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{N}>M}\varphi_{M}\|_{\ell^{2}}.

(42)

Then we estimate

\displaystyle\bigg\|\varphi(t)-\tilde{\varphi}_{0}(t)-i\int_{0}^{t}e^{-i(t-s)A}F(\varphi(s))\,ds\bigg\|_{\ell^{2}}

(43)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left\|\varphi_{M}(t)-\varphi(t)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

(44)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left\|\varphi_{M}(t)-\tilde{\varphi}_{0}(t)-i\int_{0}^{t}e^{-i(t-s)A}F_{M}(\varphi_{M}(s))\,ds\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

(45)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-i(t-s)A}F_{M}(\varphi_{M}(s))\,ds-\int_{0}^{t}e^{-i(t-s)A}F(\varphi(s))\,ds\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

(46)

The first term (

) converges to zero by Lemma
13
. The second term (
45
) is zero because
\varphi_{M}
is a solution to the approximated problem (
26
).
It remains to estimate the difference between the nonlinear parts,

\displaystyle\eqref{estnonlinearterm}\leq|J|\int_{0}^{t}

\displaystyle\Big(\left\|\overline{\alpha_{M}(s)}a_{M}\varphi_{M}(s)-\overline{\alpha_{\varphi}(s)}a\varphi(s)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

(47a)

\displaystyle+\left\|\alpha_{M}(s)a_{M}^{*}\varphi_{M}(s)-\alpha_{\varphi}(s)a^{*}\varphi(s)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}

(47b)

\displaystyle+\left\||\alpha_{M}(s)|^{2}\varphi_{M}(s)-|\alpha_{\varphi}(s)|^{2}\varphi(s)\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\Big)ds.

(47c)

For (

), we find

(
47a
)

\displaystyle\leq|\alpha_{M}-\alpha|\|a_{M}\varphi_{M}\|_{\ell^{2}}+|\alpha|\|(a_{M}-a)\varphi_{M}\|_{\ell^{2}}+|\alpha|\|a(\varphi_{M}-\varphi)\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{N}^{1/2}\varphi_{0}\|_{\ell^{2}}\left(2\|(\mathcal{N}+1)^{1/2}\varphi_{0}\|_{\ell^{2}}\|\varphi_{M}-\varphi\|_{\ell^{2}}+\|\mathcal{N}^{1/2}(\varphi_{M}-\varphi)\|_{\ell^{2}}+2{\mathcal{E}}_{1}(M)\right),

from (

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{1}(M)

\displaystyle=\|a\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{N}>M}\varphi_{M}\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{N}+1>M}(\mathcal{N}+1)^{-1/2}\|_{\mathcal{L}}\|(\mathcal{N}+1)^{1/2}a\varphi_{M}\|_{\ell^{2}}

(

8
.

, there exists a solution
\varphi(\cdot)
to the Duhamel formulation of the mean-field dynamics (

). Let

4
Preliminaries

4.1
Reduced Densities Matrices

Given a density matrix
\gamma_{d}\in\mathcal{L}^{1}\left(\mathcal{F}\right)
we define a two-lattice-site reduced density matrix

\displaystyle\gamma_{d}^{\left(2\right)}\coloneq\frac{1}{d\left|\Lambda\right|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\braket{x,y}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}_{\Lambda\backslash{\left\{x,y\right\}}}\left(\gamma_{d}\right)=\frac{1}{2d\left|\Lambda\right|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\Lambda\\
x\sim y\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}_{\Lambda\backslash{\left\{x,y\right\}}}\left(\gamma_{d}\right).

Note that this two-lattice-site reduced density matrix is symmetrized over all interacting pairs of sites and not over all pairs of sites. The normalization factor
2d\left|\Lambda\right|
is indeed the number of interacting pairs of sites.

Note that
\gTwo
is symmetric under the exchange of its two variables, i.e.,

\displaystyle\forall A,B\in\mathcal{L}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})),\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gTwo A\otimes B\right)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gTwo B\otimes A\right),

and reduces to
\gOne
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{1}\left(\gTwo\right)=\mathrm{Tr}_{2}\left(\gTwo\right)=\gOne.

Moreover, if
C\in\mathcal{L}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C}))
and
D\in\mathcal{L}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})^{\otimes 2})
, then it follows directly from its definition that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\left|\Lambda\right|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma_{d}C_{x}\right)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gOne C\right),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2d\left|\Lambda\right|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\Lambda\\
x\sim y\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma_{d}D_{x,y}\right)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gTwo D\right).

Furthermore, the following standard results hold. If
A\in\mathcal{L}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C}))
is self adjoint such that
A\geq 0
or
\gOne A\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C}))
, then

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{x\in\Lambda}A_{x},\gamma_{d}\right]^{(1)}=\left[A,\gOne\right].

(49)

If
B\in\mathcal{L}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})^{\otimes 2})
is self adjoint such that
B\geq 0
or
\gTwo B\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})^{\otimes 2})
, then

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2d}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\Lambda\\
x\sim y\end{subarray}}B_{x,y},\gamma_{d}\right]^{(1)}=\mathrm{Tr}_{1}\left(\left[B,\gTwo\right]\right)+\mathrm{Tr}_{2}\left(\left[B,\gTwo\right]\right).

(50)

4.2
Energy Bounds

With the definitions of one and two-lattice-site density matrices we can rewrite the energy per lattice site as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma_{d}H_{d}\right)}{\left|\Lambda\right|}=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gOne\left(\left(J-\mu\right)\mathcal{N}+\frac{U}{2}\mathcal{N}\left(\mathcal{N}-1\right)\right)\right)-J\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gTwo a^{*}\otimes a\right).

(51)

Note that the mean-field energy can be written as

\displaystyle\braket{\varphi,h^{\varphi}\varphi}=J\left(\braket{\varphi,\mathcal{N}\varphi}-\left|\alpha_{\varphi}\right|^{2}\right)-\mu\braket{\varphi,\mathcal{N}\varphi}+\frac{U}{2}\braket{\varphi,\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{N}-1)\varphi}.

(52)

The following bounds allow us to control the Bose–Hubbard energy and the mean-field energy in terms of moments of the number operator.

Lemma 15
.

Let
\gamma_{d}\in\mathcal{L}^{1}\left(\mathcal{F}\right)
and
\varphi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})
. Then there exists
C>0
such that, for
U=0
,

\displaystyle\left|\braket{\varphi,h^{\varphi}\varphi}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\braket{\varphi,\mathcal{N}\varphi},

(53)

\displaystyle\frac{\left|\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma_{d}H_{d}\right)\right|}{\left|\Lambda\right|}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gOne\mathcal{N}\right)\right),

(54)

and, for
U\neq 0
,

\displaystyle\left|\braket{\varphi,h^{\varphi}\varphi}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\braket{\varphi,\mathcal{N}^{2}\varphi}\right),

(55)

\displaystyle\frac{\left|\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma_{d}H_{d}\right)\right|}{\left|\Lambda\right|}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gOne\mathcal{N}^{2}\right)\right).

(56)

Proof.

Recalling (
41
) and (
52
) immediately yields (
53
) and (
55
). In order to obtain (
54
) and (
56
), we estimate the two-site term in (
51
) with Cauchy–Schwarz to obtain

\displaystyle\left|\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gTwo a^{*}\otimes a\right)\right|\leq\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gOne\mathcal{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gOne\left(\mathcal{N}+1\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gOne\mathcal{N}\right)+1.

∎

4.3
Conservation Laws

For both the Bose–Hubbard model (
2
) and the mean-field model (
6

4.4
Gronwall Estimate

Both Theorems
1
and
2
are proven via a Gronwall estimate for the quantity
\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gOne q\right)
. This is directly related to the trace norm difference of reduced density matrices, analogous to the case of the weak coupling limit
[
31
]
, as the following Lemma shows.

Lemma 18
.

Let
p
be a rank one projection and
\gamma
a positive trace
1
operator on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})
and
q\coloneq 1-p
. Then

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma q\right)\leq\left\lVert\gamma-p\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}\leq 2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma q\right)}.

(69)

Proof.

In order to get the upper bound in (
69
), we first notice that since
\gamma\leq 1
and
\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma\right)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(p\right)=1
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert p\gamma p-p\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\left(1-\gamma\right)p\right)=1-\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma p\right)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma(1-p)\right)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma q\right),

so

\displaystyle\left\lVert\gamma-p\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert(p+q)\gamma(p+q)-p\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma q\right)+2\left\lVert q\gamma p\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma q\right)+2\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma q\right)}\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma p\right)}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma q\right)}\left(\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma q\right)}+\sqrt{1-\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma q\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma q\right)},

where we used
\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{1-x}\leq\sqrt{2}
for
0\leq x\leq 1
.
The lower bound follows directly from

\mathrm{Tr}\left(\gamma q\right)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\left(p-\gamma\right)p\right)\leq\left\lVert\gamma-p\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}.

∎

Proposition 25
.

The term

where

\displaystyle\tilde{C}(t)

\displaystyle=C(J,\mu,U)\left(1+\langle\varphi(0),\mathcal{N}\varphi(0)\rangle^{2}\right)

(116)

\displaystyle\Big(1+\sum_{j=0}^{6}\left(8J{\langle\varphi(0),\mathcal{N}\varphi(0)\rangle}^{1/2}\right)^{j}\left\langle\varphi(0),(\mathcal{N}+j)^{4-\frac{j}{2}}\varphi(0)\right\rangle\frac{t^{j}}{j!}\Big),

with
C(J,\mu,U)>0
depending polynomially on the model parameters
J
,
\mu
and

.

Proof.

We need to estimate each term in
\mathcal{R}
. We start by explaining in detail how to estimate one of the terms in (
105
). By Cauchy–Schwarz and Hölder’s inequality we have

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{J}{d|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\sum_{\underset{y\sim x}{y\in\Lambda}}\langle\Psi_{d},p_{x}h^{\varphi}_{x}p_{y}a^{*}_{x}a_{y}q_{x}q_{y}\Psi_{d}\rangle\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{J}{d|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\big\|a^{*}_{x}q_{x}\Psi_{d}\big\|\Bigg\|h^{\varphi}_{x}p_{x}\Bigg(\sum_{\underset{y\sim x}{y\in\Lambda}}q_{y}a^{*}_{y}p_{y}\Bigg)\Psi_{d}\Bigg\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2J}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\sqrt{\langle\varphi(0),(\mathcal{N}+1)\varphi(0)\rangle}\sqrt{\langle\varphi,(h^{\varphi})^{2}\varphi\rangle}\big\|a^{*}_{x}q_{x}\Psi_{d}\big\|\Bigg(\frac{1}{2d}+\frac{1}{(2d)^{2}}\sum_{\underset{z\sim x}{z\in\Lambda}}\sum_{\underset{y\not=z,y\sim x}{y\in\Lambda}}\|q_{z}\Psi_{d}\|\|q_{y}\Psi_{d}\|\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2J}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\sqrt{\langle\varphi(0),(\mathcal{N}+1)\varphi(0)\rangle}\sqrt{\langle\varphi,(h^{\varphi})^{2}\varphi\rangle}\big\|a^{*}_{x}q_{x}\Psi_{d}\big\|\Bigg(\frac{1}{2d}+\frac{1}{4d}\sum_{\underset{y\not=z,y\sim x}{y\in\Lambda}}\|q_{y}\Psi_{d}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4d}\sum_{\underset{z\sim x}{z\in\Lambda}}\|q_{z}\Psi_{d}\|^{2}\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq CJ\sqrt{\langle\varphi(0),(\mathcal{N}+1)\varphi(0)\rangle}\sqrt{\langle\varphi,(h^{\varphi})^{2}\varphi\rangle}\Bigg(\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x}\|a^{*}_{x}q_{x}\Psi_{d}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{d}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{d}\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\underbrace{\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\sum_{\underset{y\sim x}{y\in\Lambda}}\|q_{y}\Psi_{d}\|^{2}}_{=2d\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\|q_{x}\Psi_{d}\|^{2}}+\frac{1}{d}\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\underbrace{\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\sum_{\underset{z\sim x}{z\in\Lambda}}\|q_{z}\Psi_{d}\|^{2}}_{=2d\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\|q_{x}\Psi_{d}\|^{2}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq CJ\sqrt{\langle\varphi(0),(\mathcal{N}+1)\varphi(0)\rangle}\sqrt{\langle\varphi,(h^{\varphi})^{2}\varphi\rangle}\left(\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d},(q_{x}\mathcal{N}_{x}q_{x}+q_{x})\Psi_{d}\rangle+\frac{1}{d}\right).

The other terms of (
105
) can be estimated analogously, so we arrive at

\displaystyle\left|\eqref{sym}\right|\leq CJ\left(1+\langle\varphi(0),(\mathcal{N})\varphi(0)\rangle\right)\sqrt{\langle\varphi,(h^{\varphi})^{2}\varphi\rangle}\left(\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d},(q_{x}\mathcal{N}_{x}q_{x}+q_{x})\Psi_{d}\rangle+\frac{1}{d}\right).

In order to bound (
106
), we use Cauchy–Schwarz to find

\displaystyle\left|\frac{J}{d}\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\sum_{\underset{x\sim y}{y\in\Lambda}}\langle\Psi_{d},q_{y}p_{x}h^{\varphi}_{x}a^{*}_{x}a_{y}q_{x}p_{y}\Psi_{d}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{J}{d}\frac{1}{|\Lambda\big|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\sum_{\underset{x\sim y}{y\in\Lambda}}\langle h^{\varphi}_{x}p_{x}a^{*}_{y}q_{y}\Psi_{d},a^{*}_{x}q_{x}p_{y}\Psi_{d}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{J}{d}\frac{1}{|\Lambda\big|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\sum_{\underset{x\sim y}{y\in\Lambda}}\sqrt{\langle\varphi,(h^{\varphi})^{2}\varphi\rangle}\left(\|q_{x}\Psi_{d}\|^{2}+\|\mathcal{N}_{x}^{1/2}q_{x}\Psi_{d}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\|q_{y}\Psi_{d}\|^{2}+\|\mathcal{N}_{y}^{1/2}q_{y}\Psi_{d}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq CJ\sqrt{\langle\varphi,(h^{\varphi})^{2}\varphi\rangle}\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d},(q_{x}\mathcal{N}_{x}q_{x}+q_{x})\Psi_{d}\rangle.

Estimating the other terms of (
106
) in an analogous way, we get

\displaystyle\left|\eqref{est2}\right|\leq CJ\left(1+\sqrt{\langle\varphi(0),\mathcal{N}\varphi(0)\rangle}\right)\sqrt{\langle\varphi,(h^{\varphi})^{2}\varphi\rangle}\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d},(q_{x}\mathcal{N}_{x}q_{x}+q_{x})\Psi_{d}\rangle.

To bound (
107
), we use Cauchy–Schwarz to estimate

\displaystyle\left|\frac{J}{d}\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\sum_{\underset{x\sim y}{y\in\Lambda}}\langle\Psi_{d},q_{y}h^{\varphi}_{x}p_{x}a^{*}_{x}a_{y}q_{x}q_{y}\Psi_{d}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{J}{d}\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\sum_{\underset{x\sim y}{y\in\Lambda}}\langle a^{*}_{y}q_{y}\Psi_{d},h^{\varphi}_{x}p_{x}a^{*}_{x}q_{x}q_{y}\Psi_{d}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{J}{d}\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\sum_{\underset{x\sim y}{y\in\Lambda}}\|a^{*}_{y}q_{y}\Psi_{d}\|\Big(\langle\Psi_{d},q_{x}a_{x}\underbrace{p_{x}(h^{\varphi}_{x})^{2}p_{x}}_{=\langle\varphi,(h^{\varphi})^{2}\varphi\rangle p_{x}}a^{*}_{x}q_{x}\Psi_{d}\rangle\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{J}{d}\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\sum_{\underset{x\sim y}{y\in\Lambda}}\sqrt{\langle\varphi,(h^{\varphi})^{2}\varphi\rangle}\Big(\langle\Psi_{d},q_{y}(\mathcal{N}_{y}+1)q_{y}\Psi_{d}\rangle\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\langle\Psi_{d},q_{x}(\mathcal{N}_{x}+1)q_{x}\Psi_{d}\rangle\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq CJ\sqrt{\langle\varphi,(h^{\varphi})^{2}\varphi\rangle}\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d},(q_{x}\mathcal{N}_{x}q_{x}+q_{x})\Psi_{d}\rangle,

and similarly

\displaystyle\left|\frac{J}{d}\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\sum_{\underset{x\sim y}{y\in\Lambda}}\langle\Psi_{d},q_{y}p_{x}h^{\varphi}_{y}p_{y}a^{*}_{x}a_{y}q_{x}q_{y}\Psi_{d}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{J}{d}\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\sum_{\underset{x\sim y}{y\in\Lambda}}\sqrt{\langle\varphi(0),\mathcal{N}\varphi(0)\rangle}\|q_{y}\Psi_{d}\|\left(\left\langle\Psi_{d},q_{x}q_{y}a^{*}_{y}\underbrace{p_{y}(h^{\varphi}_{y})^{2}p_{y}}_{=\langle\varphi,(h^{\varphi})^{2}\varphi\rangle p_{y}}a_{y}q_{y}\Psi_{d}\right\rangle\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{J}{d}\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\sum_{\underset{x\sim y}{y\in\Lambda}}\sqrt{\langle\varphi,(h^{\varphi})^{2}\varphi\rangle}\sqrt{\langle\varphi(0),\mathcal{N}\varphi(0)\rangle}\|q_{y}\Psi_{d}\|\left(\left\langle\Psi_{d},q_{x}q_{y}a^{*}_{y}p_{y}a_{y}q_{y}\Psi_{d}\right\rangle\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq CJ\sqrt{\langle\varphi(0),\mathcal{N}\varphi(0)\rangle}\sqrt{\langle\varphi,(h^{\varphi})^{2}\varphi\rangle}\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d},(q_{x}\mathcal{N}_{x}q_{x}+q_{x})\Psi_{d}\rangle.

For (

\displaystyle\left|\frac{J}{d}\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{<x,y>}\langle\Psi_{d},q_{x}q_{y}a^{*}_{x}a_{y}p_{x}h^{\varphi}_{x}q_{x}q_{y}\Psi_{d}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{J}{d}\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{<x,y>}\langle h^{\varphi}_{x}p_{x}a_{x}q_{x}q_{y}\Psi_{d},a_{y}q_{x}q_{y}\Psi_{d}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq CJ\sqrt{\langle\varphi,(h^{\varphi})^{2}\varphi\rangle}\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d},q_{x}\mathcal{N}_{x}q_{x}\Psi_{d}\rangle.

Estimating the other terms in an analogous way, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\eqref{est2}+\eqref{est3}+\eqref{est4}\right|\leq CJ\left(1+\langle\varphi(0),\mathcal{N}\varphi(0)\rangle\right)\sqrt{\langle\varphi,(h^{\varphi})^{2}\varphi\rangle}\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d},(q_{x}\mathcal{N}_{x}q_{x}+q_{x})\Psi_{d}\rangle.

Using

\displaystyle|\dot{\alpha_{\varphi}}|\leq C\left(|\mu|\sqrt{\langle\varphi(0),\mathcal{N}\varphi(0)\rangle}+U\langle\varphi,(\mathcal{N}+1)^{3/2}\varphi\rangle\right)

\displaystyle|\dot{\overline{{\alpha_{\varphi}}}}{\alpha_{\varphi}}+\overline{\alpha_{\varphi}}\dot{\alpha}_{\varphi}|\leq CU\sqrt{\langle\varphi(0),\mathcal{N}\varphi(0)\rangle}\langle\varphi,(\mathcal{N}+1)^{3/2}\varphi\rangle,

we furthermore find

\displaystyle|\eqref{est5}|\leq

\displaystyle CJ\left(|\mu+U|\sqrt{\langle\varphi(0),\mathcal{N}\varphi(0)\rangle}+U\langle\varphi,\mathcal{N}^{3/2}\varphi\rangle+U\sqrt{\langle\varphi(0),\mathcal{N}\varphi(0)\rangle}\sqrt{\langle\varphi,(\mathcal{N}+1)^{3/2}\varphi\rangle}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d},\big(q_{x}\mathcal{N}_{x}q_{x}+q_{x}\big)\Psi_{d}\rangle,

Combining all estimates, we arrive at the bound

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{R}\right|\leq C(J,\mu,U)\left(1+\langle\varphi(0),\mathcal{N}\varphi(0)\rangle\right)\sqrt{\langle\varphi,(h^{\varphi})^{2}\varphi\rangle}\left(\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d},(q_{x}\mathcal{N}_{x}q_{x}+q_{x})\Psi_{d}\rangle+\frac{1}{d}\right),

,
\mu
and

.
We also have by Cauchy–Schwarz

\displaystyle\langle\varphi,(h^{\varphi})^{2}\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\varphi,\left(-J\big({\alpha_{\varphi}}a^{*}+\overline{\alpha_{\varphi}}a-|{\alpha_{\varphi}}|^{2}\big)+(J-\mu)\mathcal{N}+\frac{U}{2}\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{N}-1)\right)^{2}\varphi\right\rangle

(117)

\displaystyle\leq C(J,\mu,U)\left(1+\langle\varphi(0),\mathcal{N}\varphi(0)\rangle^{2}\right)\left(1+\langle\varphi,\mathcal{N}^{4}\varphi\rangle\right).

\displaystyle\sqrt{\langle\varphi,(h^{\varphi})^{2}\varphi\rangle}

\displaystyle\leq C(J,\mu,U)\left(1+\langle\varphi(0),\mathcal{N}\varphi(0)\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\Bigg(1+\sum_{j=0}^{6}\frac{\left(8J{\langle\varphi(0),\mathcal{N}\varphi(0)\rangle}^{1/2}t\right)^{j}}{j!}\left\langle\varphi(0),(\mathcal{N}+j)^{4-\frac{j}{2}}\varphi(0)\right\rangle\Bigg).

With this proposition we can now prove a Gronwall estimate for

Proposition 26
.

For
f
as defined in (
87
), we have for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

f(t)\leq e^{\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{C}(s)ds}f(0)+\frac{1}{d}\int_{0}^{t}\left(1+\tilde{C}(s)\right)e^{\int_{s}^{t}\tilde{C}(r)dr}ds,

(119)

with

\displaystyle\tilde{C}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{C(J,\mu,U)}{U}\left(1+\frac{1}{U}+\langle\varphi(0),\mathcal{N}\varphi(0)\rangle^{2}\right)

(120)

\displaystyle\Bigg(1+\sum_{j=0}^{6}\left(8J{\langle\varphi(0),\mathcal{N}\varphi(0)\rangle}^{1/2}\right)^{j}\left\langle\varphi(0),(\mathcal{N}+j)^{4-\frac{j}{2}}\varphi(0)\right\rangle\frac{t^{j}}{j!}\Bigg),

where
C(J,\mu,U)>0
is a polynomial in
J
,
\mu
and
U
.

Proof.

Using
\dot{H}_{d}=0=\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\dot{h}^{\varphi}_{x}+\dot{\tilde{H}}
, the time derivative of
f
can be computed as

\displaystyle\dot{f}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d},[H_{d},H_{d}+q_{x}h^{\varphi}_{x}q_{x}-h^{\varphi}_{x}+cq_{x}]\Psi_{d}\rangle+\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d},(q_{x}\dot{h}^{\varphi}_{x}q_{x}-\dot{h}^{\varphi}_{x})\Psi_{d}\rangle

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d},[h^{\varphi}_{x},q_{x}h^{\varphi}_{x}q_{x}+cq_{x}]\Psi_{d}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d},[\tilde{H},q_{x}h^{\varphi}_{x}q_{x}-h^{\varphi}_{x}+cq_{x}]\Psi_{d}\rangle+\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d},(q_{x}\dot{h}^{\varphi}_{x}q_{x}+\dot{\tilde{H}})\Psi_{d}\rangle.

Using Proposition
24
we get

\displaystyle\dot{f}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d},[\tilde{H},cq_{x}]\Psi_{d}\rangle+\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d},q_{x}\dot{h}^{\varphi}_{x}q_{x}\Psi_{d}\rangle+\mathcal{R}.

For the first two terms of this expression, we find

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d},q_{x}\dot{h}^{\varphi}_{x}q_{x}\Psi_{d}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\left\langle\Psi_{d},q_{x}\left(-J\dot{\alpha}_{\varphi}a^{*}_{x}-J\dot{\overline{\alpha_{\varphi}}}a_{x}+2JU\mathrm{Im}(\langle\varphi,\mathcal{N}a\varphi\rangle\overline{\alpha_{\varphi}})\right)q_{x}\Psi_{d}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq C(J,\mu,U)\left(1+\langle\varphi(0),\mathcal{N}\varphi(0)\rangle\right)\left(1+\sqrt{\langle\varphi,\mathcal{N}^{2}\varphi\rangle}\right)\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\left\langle\Psi_{d},\big(q_{x}\mathcal{N}_{x}^{2}q_{x}+q_{x}\big)\Psi_{d}\right\rangle,

and

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d},[\tilde{H},cq_{x}]\Psi_{d}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq C(J,\mu,U)\left(1+\frac{1}{U}+\langle\varphi(0),\mathcal{N}\varphi(0)\rangle^{2}\right)\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\left\langle\Psi_{d},(q_{x}\mathcal{N}_{x}^{2}q_{x}+q_{x})\Psi_{d}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{d}.

\mathcal{R}
from Proposition

and

up to an error

from Proposition

imply

\left|\frac{d}{dt}f(t)\right|\leq\tilde{C}(t)\left(f(t)+\frac{1}{d}\right)+\frac{1}{d},

depends on the initial data and on the other parameters of our model as defined in (

).
With Gronwall’s lemma we arrive at (

6.3
Conclusion of the Proof

We combine the above results to prove our second main result.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

We use the equivalence of
f(t)
and
g(t)
up to an error
d^{-1}
from Proposition
23
, and the Gronwall estimate for
f(t)
from Proposition
26
to find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d}(t),q_{x}(t)\Psi_{d}(t)\rangle

(121)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d}(t),\big(q_{x}(t)\mathcal{N}_{x}^{2}q_{x}(t)+q_{x}(t)\big)\Psi_{d}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{U}\left(f(t)+\frac{1}{d}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{U}e^{\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{C}(s)ds}f(0)+\frac{1}{d}\left(\frac{4}{U}+\frac{4}{U}\int_{0}^{t}\left(1+\tilde{C}(s)\right)e^{\int_{s}^{t}\tilde{C}(r)dr}ds\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{U}\left(1+J^{2}+U+\left(J-\mu-\frac{U}{2}\right)^{2}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{U}+\langle\varphi(0),\mathcal{N}\varphi(0)\rangle^{2}\right)e^{\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{C}(s)ds}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\left\langle\Psi_{d}(0),(q_{x}(0)\mathcal{N}_{x}^{2}q_{x}(0)+q_{x}(0))\Psi_{d}(0)\right\rangle+\frac{1}{d}\frac{4}{U}\left(1+e^{\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{C}(s)ds}+\int_{0}^{t}(1+\tilde{C}(s))e^{\int_{s}^{t}\tilde{C}(r)dr}ds\right),

\tilde{C}(t)
is defined in (

Now note that since

, we get that
\tilde{C}(t)

\tilde{C}(t)\leq C(J,\mu,U)\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{6}t^{j}\right)

,
\mu

121

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\langle\Psi_{d}(t),q_{x}(t)\Psi_{d}(t)\rangle

(122)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d}\frac{1}{U}+C(J,\mu,U)e^{C(J,\mu,U)\sum_{1}^{7}|t|^{j}}\left(1+\frac{1}{U^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\left\langle\Psi_{d}(0),\left(q_{x}(0)\mathcal{N}_{x}^{2}q_{x}(0)+q_{x}(0)\right)\Psi_{d}(0)\right\rangle+\frac{1}{d}\right),

and the Theorem follows from using (
69
