Water-Free Cyclosporine 0.1% in Dry Eye Disease: Formulation Rationale, Clinical Evidence, and Unresolved Questions
Authors: Eleftherios Chatzimichail, Mohamed Elalfy, Theo Empeslidis, Georgios D Panos, Zisis Gatzioufas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDry eye disease (DED) is a chronic, multifactorial ocular surface disorder driven by interacting mechanisms including inflammation, tear-film instability, hyperosmolar stress, and neurosensory dysfunction. Although topical cyclosporine is an established anti-inflammatory treatment for DED, conventional formulations may be limited by poor aqueous solubility, variable ocular penetration, delayed onset of effect, and instillation discomfort. Water-free cyclosporine ophthalmic solution 0.1% was developed to address these challenges by dissolving cyclosporine in a preservative-free semifluorinated alkane vehicle, perfluorobutylpentane (F4H5), with ethanol as a formulation aid, avoiding oils, surfactants, and aqueous excipients. This review summarizes the pharmacologic rationale, preclinical evidence, clinical trial programme, safety profile, and current therapeutic role of water-free cyclosporine 0.1% in DED. Across available studies, including Phase 2, ESSENCE-1, ESSENCE-2, and a Phase 3 trial in China, treatment consistently improved objective ocular surface signs, particularly total corneal fluorescein staining, with separation from vehicle observed as early as 2 weeks in pivotal trials. In pooled ESSENCE-1 and ESSENCE-2 analyses including 1162 patients, more than 50% of treated patients achieved a clinically relevant ≥3-grade improvement in total corneal fluorescein staining after 2 weeks. A 1-year open-label extension further suggested sustained efficacy and acceptable long-term safety. In contrast, superiority over vehicle for symptom endpoints at early primary time points has been less consistent, likely reflecting symptom–sign discordance in DED, short controlled follow-up, population heterogeneity, and the lubricating properties of the vehicle. Overall, water-free cyclosporine 0.1% represents a credible addition to anti-inflammatory therapy for moderate-to-severe DED, especially when rapid improvement in corneal staining and good drop tolerability are priorities. However, the evidence base remains largely vehicle-controlled and industry-linked, with limited head-to-head comparison against other immunomodulators.

Introduction

Dry eye disease is defined by the Tear Film and Ocular Surface Society Dry Eye Workshop II (TFOS DEWS II) as a multifactorial disease of the ocular surface characterized by a loss of homeostasis of the tear film and accompanied by ocular symptoms, in which tear-film instability, hyperosmolarity, ocular surface inflammation and damage, and neurosensory abnormalities play etiologic roles.1,2 This framework is clinically important because it places inflammation at the center of disease perpetuation rather than treating dryness as a purely lubricative problem.

In routine practice, topical cyclosporine is commonly introduced when artificial tears, environmental measures, and lid-directed management fail to control disease adequately. Its appeal lies in mechanism: cyclosporine inhibits calcineurin-dependent T-cell activation, reduces inflammatory cytokine signaling, and may support recovery of conjunctival goblet cell density and epithelial surface integrity.2,3 Yet the therapeutic potential of cyclosporine has always been constrained by formulation science. The molecule is highly lipophilic and poorly water soluble, complicating ocular delivery and historically requiring emulsions or other carrier systems that may themselves contribute to burning, stinging, blurred vision, and poor treatment persistence.

Conventional topical cyclosporine preparations established proof of concept for ocular surface immunomodulation, but their delivery platforms are imperfect. Oil-in-water emulsions, nanomicellar formulations, and cationic emulsions each attempt to overcome poor solubility, but often at the cost of formulation complexity and variable tolerability. For many patients, the challenge is not simply whether cyclosporine works, but whether it can be delivered comfortably and consistently enough for its anti-inflammatory effects to emerge.

Water-free cyclosporine 0.1%, initially developed as CyclASol and subsequently marketed in different jurisdictions under different trade names, was designed to overcome some of these limitations by delivering cyclosporine in a clear, preservative-free, water-free vehicle based on perfluorobutylpentane (F4H5).4–6

This review adds to the existing literature by providing a focused critical appraisal of the water-free cyclosporine 0.1% development programme, integrating formulation rationale, preclinical and clinical evidence, long-term safety data, and regulatory context. In particular, it examines whether the formulation rationale translates into clinically meaningful benefit, how the consistent effects on objective signs should be interpreted alongside more variable symptom outcomes, which questions remain regarding patient selection and disease phenotype, and how this agent should be positioned relative to existing topical immunomodulators.

Methods of Literature Research

This narrative review was based on a structured literature search designed to identify preclinical, clinical, and regulatory evidence relevant to water-free cyclosporine ophthalmic solution 0.1% for dry eye disease. PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, and Google Scholar were searched from database inception to 01/04/2026. Search terms included combinations of “dry eye disease”, “cyclosporine”, “ciclosporin”, “water-free cyclosporine”, “cyclosporine ophthalmic solution 0.1%”, “semifluorinated alkane”, “perfluorobutylpentane”, “F4H5”, “NOV03”, “CyclASol”, “corneal fluorescein staining”, “ESSENCE-1”, and “ESSENCE-2”. Reference lists of relevant articles were also screened manually.

Eligible sources included peer-reviewed preclinical studies, phase 2 and phase 3 clinical trials, pooled analyses, extension studies, regulatory documents, and relevant reviews addressing cyclosporine formulation, ocular pharmacology, efficacy, safety, tolerability, or therapeutic positioning in dry eye disease. Exclusion criteria were non-ophthalmic cyclosporine studies, publications not addressing dry eye disease or ocular surface disease, duplicate reports, conference abstracts without sufficient methodological detail, and articles for which the full text was unavailable. Publications in English were considered.

A narrative rather than systematic review format was chosen because the available evidence spans heterogeneous source types, including formulation science, preclinical pharmacology, vehicle-controlled clinical trials, extension data, regulatory material, and broader contextual literature on dry eye disease. This format allowed integrated critical appraisal of mechanistic rationale, clinical efficacy, symptom–sign discordance, safety, and unresolved therapeutic questions.

Historical Overview of Cyclosporine Use in Dry Eye Disease

The therapeutic application of cyclosporine in DED represents a major milestone in the management of ocular surface inflammation. In 2003, the US Food and Drug Administration approved Restasis, a 0.05% cyclosporine ophthalmic emulsion, marking the first immunomodulatory therapy specifically approved for chronic dry eye and shifting management away from purely symptomatic lubrication toward targeted anti-inflammatory treatment.3 More than a decade later, Cequa, a 0.09% nanomicellar cyclosporine solution, expanded the formulation landscape by aiming to improve penetration and bioavailability.7,8 In Europe, Ikervis, a 0.1% cationic cyclosporine emulsion, was approved for severe keratitis associated with DED and further illustrated how vehicle design could influence ocular residence time and tissue exposure.9,10

These milestones highlight two linked themes in cyclosporine therapy: first, the centrality of inflammation as a therapeutic target in DED; and second, the importance of drug delivery technology in determining both efficacy and tolerability. Water-free cyclosporine 0.1% should therefore be viewed as part of this broader evolution in formulation science rather than as an isolated innovation. Pathophysiology of Dry Eye Disease and Mechanistic Rationale for Cyclosporine

DED is characterized by loss of tear-film homeostasis driven by tear-film instability, hyperosmolarity, ocular surface inflammation, epithelial damage, and neurosensory abnormalities.1,2 Central to this process is disruption of the lacrimal functional unit, which includes the lacrimal glands, ocular surface, meibomian glands, and their neural connections. Impairment of this unit leads to reduced tear secretion, altered tear composition, and amplification of inflammatory signaling cascades.2

Conjunctival goblet cells play a pivotal role in preserving tear-film stability. Neural reflexes stimulate blinking, lacrimal tear secretion, and mucin release from goblet cells. Mucins form a hydrated muco-aqueous layer that anchors the tear film to the epithelial surface and helps maintain optical quality and epithelial integrity.11 Reduction in goblet cell density and dysregulation of mucin expression are established features of DED, contributing to tear-film instability and progressive surface damage.12 Inflammatory cytokines further exacerbate goblet cell loss and epithelial apoptosis, perpetuating the disease cycle.2

Cyclosporine targets this inflammatory cascade at a fundamental immunologic level. As a calcineurin inhibitor, it binds cyclophilin and inhibits calcineurin phosphatase activity, preventing activation of nuclear factor of activated T cells and thereby reducing transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-2.3 Beyond T-cell modulation, cyclosporine decreases inflammatory mediator expression on the ocular surface and inhibits apoptosis of conjunctival goblet cells, supporting restoration of mucin production and tear-film stability.2

Because cyclosporine is highly lipophilic, its clinical performance depends not only on immunomodulatory potency but also on ocular surface residence time, partitioning within the tear-film lipid layer, and effective delivery to inflamed ocular surface tissues.

A major pharmaceutical challenge in delivering cyclosporine is its high lipophilicity and poor aqueous solubility. Traditional formulations require emulsions, surfactants, or nanocarriers to facilitate ocular delivery. In contrast, cyclosporine readily dissolves in perfluorobutylpentane (F4H5), a semifluorinated alkane that allows a clear, preservative-free solution without oils or surfactants.5,6 This water-free approach not only improves physicochemical stability but may also enhance corneal penetration while minimizing formulation-related ocular surface irritation.

Several topical cyclosporine formulations have been developed to address the challenges of delivering a highly lipophilic immunomodulator to the ocular surface, including emulsion-based, nanomicellar, and water-free vehicles. Why Water-Free Reformulation Matters

Traditionally, ophthalmic drops are formulated in aqueous vehicles to maximize biocompatibility. Paradoxically, making such formulations physiologically compatible often requires buffers, tonicity modifiers, surfactants, and preservatives, each of which can contribute to ocular surface toxicity or discomfort. This is particularly relevant in DED, where the ocular surface is already vulnerable.

Non-aqueous vehicles have therefore attracted increasing attention in ophthalmic drug delivery. Their appeal lies not only in improved solubilization of hydrophobic drugs, but also in enhanced stability, potential tissue penetration, and reduced dependence on excipients that may irritate the ocular surface.5,13 Because they do not rely on water, they avoid the need for pH and osmolarity adjustment and may integrate more naturally with the superficial lipid layer of the tear film. Such integration may stabilize the tear film, reduce friction, and prolong ocular surface residence time. In addition, the absence of water may confer physicochemical stability advantages, including avoidance of hydrolytic degradation and reduced susceptibility to microbial growth, thereby supporting a preservative-free formulation.

These features are especially relevant for a molecule like cyclosporine, whose clinical utility is limited as much by formulation constraints as by pharmacology. A delivery system that improves comfort, residence time, and ocular penetration without requiring oils, preservatives, or surfactants could improve both biologic performance and adherence. That premise underpins interest in water-free cyclosporine.

However, non-aqueous vehicles may also have potential limitations, including patient perception of an oily or unfamiliar drop sensation, greater manufacturing complexity, specific storage considerations, and broader environmental questions related to the production and disposal of semifluorinated alkane-based formulations.

Preclinical Evidence

Experimental data support the plausibility of the formulation concept. In a desiccating stress mouse model, cyclosporine dissolved in F4H5 reduced dry-eye signs with a faster therapeutic response than a commercially available cyclosporine emulsion and showed activity comparable to dexamethasone on key inflammatory outcomes.14 Although animal models cannot answer the symptom question that dominates human trials, they do suggest that the carrier does not blunt the pharmacologic effect of cyclosporine and may accelerate ocular surface recovery.

In a later preclinical characterization study, Agarwal et al compared an F4H5-based cyclosporine formulation with Ikervis in ex vivo and in vivo models.6 Corneal penetration ex vivo was substantially higher with the F4H5/ethanol formulation, and in vivo concentrations in cornea, conjunctiva, and lacrimal gland were similar or greater despite a markedly smaller administered drop volume. These observations are important because dosing efficiency, not nominal concentration alone, likely determines whether a topical immunomodulator reaches therapeutic tissue levels at the ocular surface.

Taken together, the preclinical literature supports two conclusions. First, water-free delivery is pharmacologically credible for a highly lipophilic molecule such as cyclosporine. Second, some of the proposed clinical advantages—earlier onset and more efficient anterior segment delivery—have a biologically plausible basis. What preclinical work cannot establish, however, is whether these formulation-level gains are sufficient to overcome the symptom-sign dissociation that complicates all DED trials.

In the phase 2 dose-ranging study, water-free cyclosporine 0.05% and 0.1% were compared with vehicle over 16 weeks, with an open-label Restasis arm included for contextual comparison rather than masked superiority testing.15 Both active concentrations showed reductions in corneal and conjunctival staining relative to vehicle, with onset detectable by day 14. The study also suggested improvement in OSDI, but the phase 2 design and open-label comparator limit the strength of any head-to-head inference against older cyclosporine products.

ESSENCE-1 provided the first pivotal demonstration of efficacy for the 0.1% formulation.16 At week 4, the active arm achieved a significantly greater reduction in total corneal fluorescein staining than vehicle. This difference was already apparent by week 2 and persisted through the study. The second hierarchically tested primary endpoint, change in OSDI, did not reach significance at the primary time point, although eye dryness scores improved in favor of treatment.

ESSENCE-2 served as the confirmatory phase 3 trial and again demonstrated benefit for objective signs.17 Total and central corneal staining improved after 2 weeks and remained significantly better than vehicle at day 29. A clinically meaningful improvement of at least 3 grades in total corneal fluorescein staining was achieved by a higher proportion of treated participants than vehicle-treated participants. Dryness scores improved in both groups, but the between-group difference was not significant at the primary time point.

The phase 3 Chinese study by Peng et al extended the evidence base to a separate population and replicated the same overall pattern.18 At day 29, total corneal fluorescein staining improved more with active treatment than with vehicle, whereas the difference in dryness score was again not statistically significant. The internal consistency of these results across geographies strengthens the conclusion that the formulation improves ocular surface staining, but does not fully settle the symptom question.etation of this apparent discrepancy. In the ESSENCE-2 open-label extension, patients received water-free cyclosporine 0.1% for 52 weeks, and improvements in prespecified efficacy end points were sustained or deepened over time.19 Corneal staining improved early and then plateaued, tear production continued to increase, and symptom scores reached their lowest levels at 1 year. These findings suggest that short controlled trials may capture the onset of tissue repair more reliably than the full symptom trajectory of a chronic inflammatory surface disease.

The 2025 pooled analysis of ESSENCE-1 and ESSENCE-2 reinforced the robustness of the sign outcome.20 Across more than 1100 patients, total corneal fluorescein staining favored active treatment at both day 15 and day 29, with benefit observed across age, sex, and baseline severity subgroups. More than half of treated patients achieved at least a 3-grade staining improvement by day 15, and most patients rated the drops as comfortable on instillation. These pooled data make the case for an early ocular surface effect more persuasive than any single trial alone.

Safety and Tolerability

Across the clinical development program, the tolerability profile of water-free cyclosporine 0.1% has been broadly favorable and internally consistent.16–20 The adverse events most often linked to treatment have been transient instillation-site pain or irritation, blurred vision, eye pain, and temporary reductions in visual acuity, usually graded as mild. In the ESSENCE-2 open-label extension, ocular treatment-emergent adverse events occurred in a minority of participants over 1 year, and the most common event was instillation-site pain, with all cases reported as mild.19

The current European product information also identifies instillation-site reactions as the most common adverse effect, with blurred vision, irritation, eye pain, erythema, reduced visual acuity, and pruritus reported less frequently.4 Systemic exposure appears minimal after topical administration, supporting a primarily local ocular safety profile. Nevertheless, available long-term safety data remain limited to approximately 12 months, which is relevant for a chronic disease that may require prolonged or repeated treatment. Importantly, no clinically relevant signal for systemic immunosuppression has emerged, and blood concentrations have been reported below quantifiable limits in pharmacokinetic studies.4,5

In DED, tolerability is not a cosmetic secondary issue but a determinant of persistence with therapy. The pooled pivotal analysis reported comfortable instillation in the large majority of patients.20 This does not prove superiority over all older formulations in real-world adherence, but it supports the view that the water-free delivery platform may mitigate one of the main barriers to chronic cyclosporine use.

Current Place in Therapy

The available evidence supports positioning water-free cyclosporine 0.1% as an anti-inflammatory option for patients with moderate-to-severe DED whose disease remains uncontrolled despite tear substitutes and supportive measures. Its strongest differentiating feature is not unequivocal superiority on symptoms at 4 weeks, but the consistency and early onset of improvement in corneal staining, coupled with acceptable tolerability.4,16–20 This profile may be especially relevant in patients with clinically evident keratitis, marked central staining, or situations in which ocular surface integrity and visual function are the main immediate concerns.

At the same time, the current literature does not justify overstatement. There are no robust masked active-comparator trials against other modern immunomodulatory agents, and the exploratory phase 2 comparison with open-label Restasis should not be interpreted as definitive comparative effectiveness evidence.15 Nor is it clear whether the formulation performs differently across major DED phenotypes such as Sjögren-related aqueous-deficient disease, evaporative disease linked to meibomian gland dysfunction, or mixed inflammatory phenotypes.

Symptom response in DED is shaped by more than surface staining. Neuropathic ocular pain, psychological comorbidity, environmental triggers, and treatment burden all contribute to symptom expression and to the familiar mismatch between signs and symptoms. A formulation can therefore be biologically effective without producing dramatic between-group differences in patient-reported outcomes over 4 weeks. This is not unique to cyclosporine, but it must temper interpretation.

From a practical standpoint, water-free cyclosporine 0.1% may be positioned as part of a stepwise, phenotype-guided approach to DED management rather than as a universal first-line therapy. Initial management should generally include patient education, environmental and behavioral modification, lid hygiene where appropriate, and regular use of lubricants or tear substitutes. In patients with persistent inflammatory disease, particularly those with moderate-to-severe DED, clinically meaningful corneal staining, or inadequate response to lubricants alone, escalation to a topical immunomodulator is reasonable. Water-free cyclosporine 0.1% may be especially relevant when ocular surface inflammation coexists with prominent staining or when tolerability of conventional emulsion-based cyclosporine formulations is a concern.

Choice and sequencing of anti-inflammatory therapy should be individualized. A short course of topical corticosteroid may be considered when rapid control of an inflammatory flare is required, while recognizing the need for monitoring and avoidance of prolonged unsupervised use. Lifitegrast may be considered in patients in whom symptoms predominate or when a different anti-inflammatory mechanism is desired. Adjunctive interventions should be selected according to the dominant disease phenotype: punctal occlusion may be considered in aqueous-deficient DED after ocular surface inflammation is controlled, whereas thermal pulsation, meibomian gland–directed therapy, or intense pulsed light may be more appropriate when evaporative disease or meibomian gland dysfunction is prominent. Thus, water-free cyclosporine 0.1% is best viewed as one component of an individualized treatment pathway, with its strongest rationale in patients requiring sustained topical immunomodulation and improved formulation tolerability.

Limitations and Unresolved Questions

Several limitations should be acknowledged candidly. First, the controlled evidence base is dominated by vehicle-controlled trials, whereas the key decision in clinical practice is often between one active therapy and another. Second, many studies involve manufacturer participation, which does not invalidate their findings but increases the need for independent replication and comparative work. Third, the primary controlled time points in pivotal studies were relatively early. This favors detection of changes in surface staining, but may underestimate the time needed for symptom trajectories to separate in a chronic inflammatory disease.

Fourth, the vehicle is not inert. The spreading and friction-reducing properties of perfluorobutylpentane may themselves improve the ocular surface, which raises the threshold for demonstrating superiority on symptoms and may partly explain the modest absolute between-group differences observed in some endpoints.4,5,17,18 Fifth, there remains little evidence to guide phenotype-based use, sequencing with corticosteroids or lifitegrast, or the utility of biomarkers such as matrix metalloproteinase-9 or HLA-DR in identifying likely responders.2,21–23

The role of water-free cyclosporine in specific settings also warrants further study. These include post-refractive surgery dry eye, severe keratitis, and advanced ocular surface disease, where inflammation is clinically important but where the balance between rapid symptom relief and long-term immunomodulation may differ.24–26 Combination strategies with agents targeting complementary inflammatory pathways, including lifitegrast, are also of interest but remain insufficiently studied.21,22

Finally, any review focused on one formulation risks becoming narrower than the disease it addresses. To remain clinically persuasive, interpretation must stay anchored to broader DED trial design issues—especially symptom-sign discordance, vehicle effects, and the need for clinically interpretable responder endpoints—rather than implying that formulation science alone solves the therapeutic problem.

Conclusion

Water-free cyclosporine 0.1% represents a meaningful formulation advance in topical immunomodulatory therapy for DED. The preclinical rationale is coherent, and the clinical programme consistently shows early and clinically relevant improvement in objective ocular surface damage, particularly corneal fluorescein staining. Safety and tolerability appear favorable, with predominantly mild instillation-related reactions.

The principal unresolved issue is not whether the drug affects the ocular surface—it clearly does—but how that effect should be weighed against less consistent early symptom separation, active-vehicle effects, and the absence of head-to-head comparative trials. In practice, water-free cyclosporine 0.1% may be considered for patients with moderate-to-severe DED with prominent corneal staining or poor tolerance of conventional cyclosporine emulsions, while recognizing that early symptomatic benefit may be modest. Future studies should move beyond proof of concept and ask sharper clinical questions: which phenotypes benefit most, how this formulation compares with alternative anti-inflammatory strategies, and whether earlier control of surface inflammation improves long-term symptoms, visual quality, and adherence.
