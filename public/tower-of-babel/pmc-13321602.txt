Global and Indian perspectives on trimester-specific reference intervals for thyroid hormones in pregnant women: a systematic review
Authors: Periyasamy Kuppusamy, Jaiganesh Chinnasamy, Khushi Pankaj Jain, Kasthuri Kannayiram, Sasidharan Sivakumar, Nilesh Chandra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Establishment of trimester-specific reference intervals (RIs) for thyroid hormones is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and misinterpretation in pregnant women. This systematic review comprehensively analysed gestational age-specific reference intervals in different populations. The electronic databases such as Medline, Embase, Scopus, and Google Scholar were searched to identify relevant articles. The reference population, consisting of healthy pregnant women in the first, second, and third trimesters of pregnancy, meeting eligibility, such as the absence of thyroid disease, a negative thyroid peroxidase (TPO) antibody test, and sufficient urinary iodine concentration (UIC), was selected for the present study. Of the 1,160 studies identified in the databases, 48 were eligible for the systematic review. Data on trimester-specific RIs, established from a 95% confidence interval (CI) between the 2.5th percentile (lower limit) and the 97.5th percentile (upper limit) of thyroid hormones, were extracted. In most studies, the upper limit of TSH in healthy pregnant women during the first (85.4%), second (94.1%), and third trimesters (72%) was determined to be 2.15–4.51 mIU/L, 2.73–4.84 mIU/L, and 2.90–4.91 mIU/L, respectively. Furthermore, the lower and upper limits of FT4 during the first (80.5% and 87.8%), second (83.3% and 90%), and third trimesters (82.1% and 96.4%) have been reported as being between 10.17 and 14.16 pmol/L and 15.22–23.94 pmol/L, 8.31–11.71 pmol/L and 15.47–20.59 pmol/L, and 7.39–9.78 pmol/L and 12.03–19.82 pmol/L, respectively. The study results showed that reference value for thyroid hormones vary within the same population and also between different populations, particularly for TSH. Given this high variability, even within the Indian population, it is imperative to establish population-specific reference values.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13044-026-00298-3.

Introduction

Pregnancy significantly influences thyroid physiology and functions, primarily through pregnancy-specific maternal factors, including increased hepatic production of thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG), the thyroid-stimulating effect of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) that acts as a weak agonist to the TSH receptor, elevated iodine requirements, impaired autoimmune regulation, and placental deiodination of iodothyronine [1]. Metabolic changes in the maternal thyroid environment during pregnancy result in elevated levels of thyroid hormones, and women with these changes, particularly early in pregnancy, typically have low TSH levels and elevated levels of total T3 and T4 [2]. The fetus is highly dependent on maternal thyroid hormones, and adequate levels are essential for proper growth and development [3, 4]. Although, fetal hormone production begins in the second trimester, maternal thyroid function remains critical, particularly in early pregnancy.

As pregnancy progresses, maternal thyroid hormone levels typically return to normal during the second and third trimesters [5, 6]. Thyroid disorders during pregnancy can have adverse effects on both the mother and fetus, particularly during the critical early stages of fetal brain and skeletal development [2]. Mild iodine deficiency can lead to thyroid problems during pregnancy such as subclinical or overt hypothyroidism, or isolated hypothyroxinemia [7, 8], which may increase the risk of complications like restricted fetal growth, low birth weight, miscarriage, stillbirth, and preterm birth [9–12].

Accurate diagnosis of thyroid disorders in pregnant women remains a challenge. To ensure effective care and prevent misdiagnosis or misinterpretation of thyroid hormone levels, it is essential to establish trimester-specific and population-specific RIs. Therefore, RIs allow for accurate interpretation of test results, facilitating early detection and management of thyroid dysfunction during pregnancy [13]. Inappropriate RIs may lead to delayed treatment, which may adversely affect pregnancy outcome. Data from various studies indicate substantial variation in thyroid hormone RIs across ethnic populations, due to differences in iodine intake and the influence of genetic and environmental factors [14, 15]. Thus, the development of trimester- and population-specific RIs has become a major focus of interest for clinical chemistry researchers and laboratory professionals.

Recent literature suggests that TSH concentrations vary by ethnicity throughout pregnancy, unlike FT4 levels [16–18]. Furthermore, the TSH threshold for initiating intervention is controversial, and initiation of medications at TSH levels slightly above the reference range has been observed in recent years [12]. Also, literature has demonstrated that South Asian women have higher TSH levels than East Asian and European women, and more cases of subclinical hypothyroidism are reported [16].

Despite this recognized need for precise diagnostic thresholds, existing systematic reviews of thyroid hormone reference values in pregnancy exhibit substantial heterogeneity and largely overlook critical methodological determinants such as specific population characteristics (e.g., singleton versus multiple pregnancies, precise gestational timepoints), iodine status, and thyroid antibody status [19, 20]. Furthermore, these reviews often fail to report the clinically standard 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles necessary for deriving accurate, population-specific RIs, opting instead to present the 5th and 95th percentiles [21]. This methodological oversight is diagnostically problematic; failing to capture the central 95% of the healthy population decreases diagnostic specificity and artificially inflates the false-positive rate for thyroid dysfunction, potentially leading to unnecessary clinical interventions.

Consequently, there remains a significant lack of systematic data evaluating the diagnostic implications of employing precise, pregnancy-specific reference intervals. In the current context, a comprehensive study is needed to analyze population-specific reference intervals for thyroid hormones in pregnant women in each trimester of pregnancy. To address this gap, the present systematic review evaluates these trimester-specific ranges, utilizing the strict 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles, to provide a more accurate framework for clinical decision-making.

Materials and methods

This systematic review followed a protocol prospectively registered with PROSPERO [CRD42024584636] and was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guideline.

Search strategy and eligibility criteria

A comprehensive search strategy was conducted in electronic databases such as MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, and Scopus, until August, 2024. Additional searches were conducted using the Google Scholar database to identify missing studies. No publication date restrictions were applied, and the search was limited to studies published in English. The search strategy used for retrieving articles from the above-mentioned databases are given in the Supplementary Table 1. We included studies that reported RIs for thyroid profile, including TSH, free T4 and T3, and total T4 and T3 levels in healthy pregnant women.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study complies with the American Thyroid Association (ATA) and International Federation of Clinical Chemistry (IFCC) guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of thyroid disorders. Inclusion criteria: women of childbearing age, singleton pregnancy by natural conception, normal iodine intake and adequate IUC (150–250 µg/L), negative anti-TPO antibodies (< 35 IU/ml), and gestational age-specific RIs calculated from a 95% confidence interval between the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles [22]. Exclusion criteria: multiple pregnancies, known thyroid disease, diffuse goiter, or under thyroid medications. Studies that reported mean and standard deviation or calculated RIs based on the 5th and 95th percentiles were also excluded. Additional exclusion criteria were diabetes mellitus before or during pregnancy, hypertension, obesity (more than 30 kg/m² at the initial visit of the cohort), and conception by in vitro fertilization. Furthermore, studies that evaluated the performance of methods or compared the results of instruments were not included in the present study. Case reports or case series or RIs established in animal models were also excluded.

Screening of articles

The search strategy used in the study is presented in Fig. 1. A total of 1,160 database search results were included in the Rayyan online tool (https://new.rayyan.ai). After removing 290 duplicates, 870 articles were reviewed at the title and abstract levels by two independent researchers (PK and JC). Of these articles, 120 were individually reviewed in full text by two authors (PK and JC). Disagreements were resolved in consultation with the third author (SS). Forty-eight publications were included in the systematic review after removing studies with insufficient data [11, 13, 15, 17, 23–66]. The degree of agreement between the two authors (PK and JC), calculated using Cohen’s kappa coefficient, was 0.87 for the title and abstract, and 0.93 for the full text.

Data extraction and presentation

Data were extracted using a standardized extraction form. This form includes: author and year, study site, study type, sample size, study group, gestational age, inclusion and exclusion criteria, analytical methods, results, and conclusions. The general characteristics and outcome measures of the included studies are shown in the Supplemental File 1. All data were entered into MS Excel and presented graphically. The data are presented according to 2.5th and 97.5th percentile of thyroid hormones with the corresponding gestational age. Studies reporting analyte measurements in different units were converted to a common unit for comparison purposes. Since, there is no validated tool for meta-analysis of reference intervals, the study unable to find the effect size and heterogeneity among the studies.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of studies with potential risk of bias in its design, conduct, and analysis was assessed by two independent authors (PK and JC) using the revised Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal [67]. Disagreements between authors were resolved by the third reviewer (SS). The JBI methodological checklist includes 8 questions for cross-sectional studies and 11 questions for cohort studies, categorized as “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” and “Not applicable.” Studies with a score between 0 and 3 (indicating “yes”) had a “high” risk of bias, those with a score between 4 and 6 (indicating “moderate” risk of bias), and those with a score of 7 or higher (indicating “yes”) had a “low” risk of bias.

Results

Background of the study characteristics

The literature search, selection and assessment of the studies’ eligibility are presented in Fig. 1. A total of 48 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in this review (Supplemental File 1). The exclusion criteria reported by the studies for inclusion of their study population were as follows: (i) known thyroid disease, diffuse goiter, thyroid nodule detected by ultrasound or under thyroid treatment(s) (87.5%), (ii) women conceived by in vitro fertilization (16.7%), (iii) having multiple fetuses (64.6%). Approximately 50% of studies applied additional exclusion criteria such as pregnant women with significant risk factors, including obesity (> 30 kg/m2), smoking, hypertension, autoimmune diseases, pre-pregnancy diabetes, recurrent miscarriage, hyperemesis gravidarum, pregnancy complications such as gestational diabetes, hypertension, preeclampsia, preterm delivery, and small for gestational age.

The included studies had sample sizes ranging from 69 to 41,634 in the first trimester, 49 to 7,907 in the second trimester, and 19 to 41,634 in the third trimester. Maternal age ranged from 15 to 48 years. Studies reported the week of gestation in each trimester are as follows: (i) a total of 48 studies reported data for the first trimester of pregnancy as less than 14 weeks, except a study by Dorizzi et al. reported with 14 to 16 weeks of gestation [53]; (ii) a total of 34 studies reported data for the second trimester including a gestational age between 13 and 28 weeks; (iii) 32 studies reported data for the third trimester of pregnancy, with gestational weeks ranging from 28 to 41.8 weeks, and two studies reported data from 24/25 weeks of pregnancy [45, 59]. Additionally, 56.3% of studies recorded details of pregnancy outcomes in their study populations.

Eighteen studies (37.5%) used a fasting morning blood sample for hormone testing. Three studies (6.3%) determined thyroid hormones RIs using a radioimmunoassay (RIA) method, while the others used a chemiluminescence or electrochemiluminescence immunoassay method, which are commonly used in clinical settings. Of the 48 included studies, 27 studies measured UIC, and the remaining 21 studies recorded iodine sufficiency status in their populations. The included studies were conducted in 25 different countries, including China (14), India (4), Korea (3), Spain (3), Czech Republic (2), Iran (2), Italy (2), and one each from Australia, Brazil, Bulgaria, Canada, Chile, Denmark, Finland, Greece, Japan, Mexico, Netherlands, Pakistan, Philippines, Poland, Rio de Janeiro, Sri Lanka, Switzerland, United Arab Emirates (UAE), and the United States.

Discussion

This systematic review comprehensively assessed trimester-specific RIs for thyroid hormones in pregnancy. The study assessed the variability, trends, and influencing factors of thyroid hormone levels in healthy pregnant women, according to ATA recommendations, specifically including those without known thyroid disease, with sufficient iodine status, and negative anti-TPO antibodies, and RIs reported using the 2.5th to 97.5th percentile [14]. A major concern highlighted by this review is the lack of methodological uniformity across studies. Previously, Osinga et al. [21] found that only 11% of studies followed the recommendations of international thyroid and endocrine societies, while the present study showed that 51% applied additional exclusion criteria (e.g., TPO Ab positivity, pregnancy complications, obesity, smoking, and comorbidities), with the observed reduction in reference limits being modest at approximately 5%. This highlights the heterogeneity in RIs for thyroid hormones across the populations.

Literature evidence suggest that several maternal factors significantly influence thyroid function during pregnancy, one of them being maternal age. In non-pregnant women, advanced age is generally associated with elevated TSH levels [68, 69]. However, population-based data from China indicate that while total T3 and T4 levels showed age-related differences, however, TSH levels in early pregnancy may remain unchanged [70]. Our review included studies of women of advanced maternal age, including those over 45 years [36, 46], which may partly explain the heterogeneity observed in thyroid hormone reference ranges. Meanwhile, body mass index (BMI) also plays a critical role in fluctuation of thyroid hormone levels. Overweight or obese women are at increased risk of thyroid dysfunction. Mannisto et al. [44] found that a higher BMI was associated with lower FT4 levels and higher TSH and FT3 levels. These findings highlight the need to consider both age and BMI when interpreting thyroid function during pregnancy and when establishing population-specific RIs.

Thyroid autoimmunity remains a critical factor in the assessment of thyroid hormones in pregnant women. The presence of anti-TPO antibodies in subclinical hypothyroidism is associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes including pregnancy loss, preterm birth, intrauterine growth restriction, and impaired neurodevelopment in children [10]. This highlights the importance of screening for anti-TPO antibodies in pregnant women with elevated TSH levels. Furthermore, maternal thyroid function is strongly influenced by factors such as ethnicity, iodine intake, and biological variations [16–18, 71]. For example, some studies have overlooked potential differences in thyroid function between nulligravida and multigravida women, an area that warrants future research.

Evidence shows that thyroid reference intervals directly influence clinical decision-making, particularly regarding the levothyroxine (LT4) therapy. Unrecognized or misclassified thyroid dysfunction in early pregnancy is strongly associated with adverse maternal and fetal events, most notably preterm delivery and miscarriage, alongside preeclampsia, placental abruption, and neonatal complications [72]. Specifically, observational studies consistently link TSH levels > 4.0–4.5 mIU/L with increased miscarriage and preterm delivery risk, whereas mild elevations (2.5–4.0 mIU/L) show variable associations [73]. Therefore, establishing accurate, trimester-specific TSH and FT4 reference ranges is essential. These intervals provide the diagnostic thresholds necessary to guide targeted LT4 therapy particularly in women with thyroid autoimmunity thereby minimizing the risks of both under- and overtreatment and optimizing maternal-fetal outcomes.

Our analysis confirms a consistent trend of gestational variation in thyroid hormone levels. Several studies have demonstrated that free triiodothyronine, free thyroxine, and hCG levels progressively decrease as pregnancy progresses, while TSH levels tend to increase [56, 74]. Furthermore, Zha et al. [33] observed a strong inverse relationship between TSH and hCG levels, highlighting the dynamic physiological adaptations of the thyroid axis during gestation.

First trimester

Thyroid hormone levels during the first trimester of pregnancy show considerable variability, attributable to physiological factors such as diurnal variation in TSH levels and possible methodological interference with hCG, known for the structural similarity of the two molecules that causes the elevation of hCG to suppress TSH synthesis [44]. Among the parameters of thyroid function, TSH is considered the most reliable marker. However, first-trimester TSH levels vary considerably among populations. However, regardless of the methods or instruments used to measure thyroid hormones, four-fifths of the studies (85.4%) set the upper limit of the TSH reference range between 2.15 and 4.51 mIU/L. Some studies have documented significantly higher values, for example, 6.65 mIU/L in Indian women [48] and 8.3 mIU/L in Arab women from the UAE [13]. These values ​​exceed the non-pregnant ranges, almost twice those of non-pregnant women from the UAE (4.32 mIU/L) and highlight the potential for misclassification if non-pregnant RIs are used. Similarly, FT4 values ​​ranged from 7.72 to 26.49 pmol/L, with 80.5% of studies placing the lower limit between 10.17 and 14.16 pmol/L and the upper limit (87.8%) between 15.22 and 23.94 pmol/L. Notably, high upper limits were reported in studies conducted in Brazil, China, Japan, the United Arab Emirates, and the United States. FT3 ranges were also wide (1.86–8.28 pmol/L), with most studies reporting upper limits between 3.56 and 6.87 pmol/L, and one study conducted in Pakistan showed a lower limit of 1.86 pmol/L [59]. Similarly, 90% of the included studies showed an upper limit of FT3 ranging from 3.56 to 6.87 pmol/L, and studies conducted in China and Greece showed a high upper limit with 7.23 pmol/L and 8.28 pmol/L, respectively [33, 46].

Second trimester

TSH RIs ranged from 0.01 to 6.66 mIU/L in the second trimester of pregnancy. Most studies (94.1%) reported upper limits between 2.73 and 4.84 mIU/L, except for Indian and UAE studies which reported higher values [13, 48]. FT4 levels ranged from 7.1 to 22.91 pmol/L, with 83.3% of studies reporting the lower limit between 8.31 and 11.71 pmol/L, and 90% of studies reporting the upper limit between 15.47 and 20.59 pmol/L. A Chinese study determined the lowest 2.5th percentile of FT4 levels to be 7.1 pmol/L [34], while a study on Indian women showed a high upper limit of FT4 at 22.91 pmol/L [47]. FT3 reference limits for pregnant women in the second trimester ranged from 1.96 to 8.14 pmol/L, among which two studies conducted in Finland and Greece showed a high upper limit of 7.25 and 8.14 pmol/L, respectively [44, 46], with all other studies showing an upper limit of 4.62 to 6.37 pmol/L.

Third trimester

Khalid et al. [10] demonstrated that the RIs for TSH and free thyroid hormones (T3 and T4) in the third trimester had narrow ranges. However, our study showed that the ranges were 0.11 to 6.9 mIU/L. Among these, a high upper limit of 6.9 mIU/L was observed in a study of women from the United Arab Emirates [13], and nearly 72% of the included studies showed an upper limit between 2.90 and 4.91 mIU/L, and a high upper limit (5.02 to 5.95 mIU/L) was observed in six studies of the Chinese population [30, 31, 33, 34, 36, 39], and two studies of the Korean population (5.57 mIU/L) [15, 57]. Similarly, FT4 levels ranged from 6.16 to 22.27 pmol/L, with most studies (96.4%) showing upper limits between 12.03 and 19.82 pmol/L. FT3 values ​​were narrower (2.01 to 5.68 pmol/L), except in a Philippine study that reported a 97.5th percentile of 3.7 pmol/L [60].

Overall, this study highlights a trend of increasing TSH levels and decreasing FT4 levels as pregnancy progresses. The main causes of FT4 fluctuations from the first to the third trimester are mainly alterations in thyroid binding protein (TBG) levels and increased thyroid stimulation by hCG in early pregnancy [75]. Other factors, such as iodine status, which varies between populations and assessment methods, have a major impact on thyroid hormone levels. We also agree with the Osinga group’s recommendation: the large disparity between studies highlights the difficulty of providing a universal recommendation. Future recommendations will instead focus on improving local diagnostic assessment rather than defining universally applicable reference values ​[76].

Indian studies and regional variations

Our synthesis of Indian studies revealed significant intra-population variability. For example, Sekhri et al. [48] and Goel et al. [49] reported high upper limits of TSH in the first and second trimesters (up to 6.66 mIU/L), and these are 1.5 times higher than the RIs of non-pregnant Indian women (4.29 mIU/L). Whereas, other Indian studies have aligned more closely with international limits [47, 50]. FT4 and FT3 also showed substantial inter-study differences within Indian cohorts, and equivalent to the RIs of non-pregnant ranges, suggesting the need for better harmonization in a larger sample size.

In India, pregnancy-specific RIs for thyroid hormones are not universally established, and Indian laboratories generally follow international recommendations, which are not necessarily reflected regionally due to the influence of iodine nutritional status, ethnic differences, and other socioeconomic factors. Additionally, some studies have attempted to establish reference intervals for thyroid hormones in Indian women. However, these studies have not systematically evaluated the establishment of trimester-specific reference intervals.

Key points regarding establishment of thyroid hormone reference intervals in Indian pregnant women:

Iodine status

Iodine deficiency can influence thyroid hormone levels, particularly in regions with low iodine intake. Therefore, sequential assessment of thyroid hormones and UIC in each trimester of pregnancy would have been ideal for establishing RIs for Indian pregnant women.

Regional Variations

Reference values ​​may vary across regions in India, depending on regional differences in iodine nutrition and geographical location. Therefore, studies should focus on determining region-specific RIs ​​for thyroid hormones in the Indian population.

Age-related factors

TSH levels can vary with age. Literature shows that these levels generally increase with age [77].

Method Differences

Reference values ​​may vary depending on the testing method used, and there are slight differences between the methods and instruments used.

Perspective on international guidelines for disease-specific reference limits

Thyroid function undergoes significant physiological changes during pregnancy, and the use of non-pregnant TSH reference ranges may potentially lead to misclassification of thyroid status in pregnant women. Thyroid dysfunction is assessed using TSH and free T4 with trimester-specific ranges, and both subclinical hypothyroidism and overt hyperthyroidism require trimester-appropriate interpretation. International guidelines including the ATA, 2017, European Thyroid Association, European Society of Endocrinology, National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), and global consensus recommendations advocate pregnancy-specific TSH reference limits [8, 14, 78, 79]. These generally recommend upper TSH limits of 4.0 mIU/L in the first trimester and 3.0 mIU/L in the second and third trimesters.

Our findings align with these international recommendations. Most studies report first-trimester upper TSH limits between 2.15 and 4.51 mIU/L across populations. TSH exceeding 4.0 mIU/L warrant evaluation of TPO antibody status to assess autoimmune thyroid disease risk. TSH gradually rises as hCG declines in the second trimester and stabilizes in the third [14]. In our study, second and third-trimester upper TSH limits were slightly higher than guideline cut-offs, ranging 2.73–4.84 mIU/L and 2.90–4.91 mIU/L, respectively. In contrast, the Indian Thyroid Society (ITS) and FOGSI-2019 guideline recommends an upper TSH limit of 2.5 mIU/L in the first trimester and 3.0 mIU/L in later trimesters [80], emphasizing the need for population-specific reference ranges. Population-specific trimester reference ranges are preferred, as evidence shows no increased adverse events or overtreatment with mildly elevated TSH [81]. Treatment is generally recommended for TSH > 4.0 mIU/L or selectively for TSH 2.5–4.0 mIU/L in women with thyroid autoimmunity, reflecting an individualized, evidence-based approach.

Future directions

Prospective, population-based cohorts with long-term follow-up are essential to validate RIs in pregnancy. There is no comparison of thyroid hormone levels between nulliparous and multiparous women, which requires further research.

Analytical considerations

The distinction between overt and subclinical thyroid disease is determined by FT4 measurement. FT4 quantification by immunoassay could be affected by endogenous and exogenous factors [75]. Therefore, a comparative study of FT4 RIs by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) and chemiluminescence or electrochemiluminescence should be performed to verify accuracy. The RIA method is currently less and less used and known with methodological differences. Harmonization of assay platforms is essential to facilitate international comparisons.

Controlling for confounding factors

Future studies should account for maternal age, BMI, smoking, and comorbidities with an adequate sample size to establish RIs [63]. Women with conditions such as hyperemesis gravidarum or transient gestational thyrotoxicosis (e.g., TSH < 0.01 mIU/L, elevated FT4) should be excluded from calculating RIs [82]. Various medications can affect TSH concentration, although this is usually due to physiological changes rather than methodological interference. Therefore, when establishing RIs during pregnancy, a detailed survey of women receiving medications is considered [75].

Outcome-linked validation

Establishing RIs based on populations with known health outcomes, rather than retrospective diagnostic data, will improve clinical relevance and avoid misclassification of subclinical thyroid disease [83].

Limitations

Ethnicity is not a single biological determinant of the cumulative effects on thyroid function, and more likely driven by factors such as dietary patterns (iodine intake), BMI, smoking, environmental exposures and socioeconomic conditions [2]. However, due to substantial ethnic diversity in the country, detailed data were not collected to assess potential differences in reference intervals. Iodine is crucial for determining thyroid function, and nearly half of the studies did not assess UIC, however, these studies reported that their populations had adequate iodine intake. Thyroglobulin antibody status was not assessed, which could have increased the robustness of the results. Six studies had small sample sizes (< 120), ranging from 59 to 115, which limits statistical power [11, 24, 28, 48, 50, 55]. Our study did not specifically examine the impact of methodological assay variations on reference ranges, which represents a limitation considering the existing literature on this topic. These uncertainties may have influenced the study outcomes and should be considered when interpreting the results.

Conclusion

As the pregnancy progresses, the reference intervals for TSH increase each trimester, while FT4 reference values decrease. However, there is considerable heterogeneity in establishing population specific-RIs for thyroid hormones in pregnant women for each trimester. Furthermore, this review highlights significant variation in thyroid hormones RIs within the Indian population. There is no study established with the population specific and regional specific reference values for pregnant women in India. Given the variations in thyroid hormone RIs according to gestational age, iodine status, and ethnic differences, prospective longitudinal cohort studies are warranted to establish RIs for the Indian population.
