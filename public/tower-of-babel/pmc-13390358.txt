Measuring patriotic emotional granularity: task-based validation, brief scale development, and cross-method evidence
Authors: Lu Fan, Yidan Lu, Xinyi Zhu, Jiajia Jin, Shuhui Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResearch on patriotic emotion has predominantly relied on global self-report measures, leaving unclear whether individuals differ in how specifically they distinguish among multiple patriotism-related feelings. The present research introduces Patriotic Emotional Granularity (Patriotic-EG) and examines its measurement using both a task-based paradigm and a brief self-report scale.MethodsIn Study 1, we developed a Patriotic Photo Emotion Differentiation task to assess task-derived Patriotic-EG. In Study 2, we developed and validated a brief self-report scale of Patriotic-EG using item analysis and confirmatory factor analysis. A bridging subsample was used to examine the association between the brief scale and the task-derived Patriotic-EG index.ResultsIn Study 1, the task-derived Patriotic-EG index showed theoretically consistent associations with patriotic affect, national identity, and perceived social support, supporting its initial placement within a civic-affective nomological network. In Study 2, item analysis and confirmatory factor analysis supported a brief 8-item scale with a one-factor structure, good internal consistency, and acceptable factorial validity. The scale was positively associated with patriotic affect and national identity, showed only weak associations with social desirability, and demonstrated incremental validity in predicting relevant civic-affective outcomes. In the bridging subsample, the brief scale was positively correlated with the task-derived Patriotic-EG index, providing initial cross-method evidence.ConclusionOverall, the findings suggest that Patriotic-EG is a measurable construct capturing individual differences in the differentiation of patriotic emotional experience. Patriotic-EG can be assessed using complementary task-based and self-report approaches, with meaningful but incomplete convergence between the two methods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04856-0.Keywords: Patriotic emotional granularity, Emotional granularity, Patriotic affect, Multitrait-multimethod, Scale development, National identity

Introduction

Patriotic emotion and the need for a granular perspective

Patriotic emotion is often understood as an affective bond toward one’s nation, reflected in feelings such as belonging, pride, responsibility, honor, and dignity, and shaped by historical narratives, cultural symbols, and collective experiences [17, 24]. In higher education, such feelings are frequently discussed in relation to civic learning, identity formation, and ideological education. Empirical research in this area has relied predominantly on global self-report indicators of patriotic intensity or broad attitudinal endorsement, leaving unclear whether individuals differ in how specifically they distinguish among multiple patriotism-related emotional states when responding to nation-relevant stimuli. This limitation matters because civic-affective development may involve not only patriotic intensity, but also the differentiation and structure of patriotic emotional experience.

Emotional granularity and patriotic emotional experience

Emotional granularity refers to individual differences in the extent to which people differentiate and label emotional experiences with specificity [2, 3]. Related work has also used the term emotion differentiation to describe the precision with which people distinguish among affectively similar states [12, 19]. Greater granularity has been linked to adaptive emotional functioning, flexible regulation, and better adjustment [3, 8, 11]. Although most prior research has focused on general affect or negative emotion differentiation in everyday or clinical contexts, recent PED-task work suggests that both positive and negative emotional granularity are associated with cognitive reappraisal, social anxiety, and interpersonal functioning in adolescents [15].

Evidence remains limited for granularity in higher-order, socially embedded domains such as civic- and nation-related emotion. This is important because patriotic feeling is rarely a single undifferentiated state, but may involve partially overlapping responses such as pride, honor, gratitude, duty, responsibility, belonging, and even concern or shame. Extending granularity research to this domain may therefore broaden the conceptual scope of emotional granularity and offer a more nuanced framework for civic-affective experience in higher education.

Emotional granularity may also be relevant to patriotic and nation-related processing. Using a dot-probe paradigm, Fan et al., [9] found that positive emotional granularity shaped attentional allocation to China-related versus non-China-related images, with higher granularity associated with more flexible, stage-specific responses to patriotic cues. However, that work focused on attentional bias rather than the direct measurement of differentiated patriotic emotional experience, leaving open the question of how Patriotic-EG can be operationalized and assessed [9].

Building on this literature, we define Patriotic Emotional Granularity (Patriotic-EG) as individual differences in the extent to which people distinguish among multiple patriotism-related affective states elicited by nation-relevant cues. Patriotic-EG concerns the specificity and differentiation of patriotic experience rather than its overall intensity. Thus, two individuals may report similarly strong patriotic feeling but differ in whether that feeling is experienced as a relatively undifferentiated positive state or as a nuanced pattern involving pride, honor, belonging, responsibility, or mission.

Measuring Patriotic-EG: task-based and self-report approaches

A key methodological challenge is how to assess this construct without reducing it to another global self-report measure. Emotional granularity has commonly been measured through repeated emotion ratings, including experience-sampling designs and laboratory-based differentiation paradigms such as photo emotion differentiation tasks [3, 7]. Because these methods derive indices from repeated responses under standardized or semi-standardized conditions, the present research first develops a Patriotic Photo Emotion Differentiation task (Patriotic PED-task) to obtain a task-derived indicator of Patriotic-EG. This task-based approach is valuable because it captures differentiation in response to nation-relevant stimuli rather than relying solely on generalized judgments about oneself.

Task-based indicators, however, are less suitable for large-scale or routine assessment because they require time, standardized administration, and computational scoring. A brief self-report scale could therefore complement, rather than replace, task-based assessment in larger survey studies and repeated educational measurement. More generally, multi-method assessment is central to construct validation because different methods may capture overlapping but non-identical aspects of the same construct [4, 6]. Accordingly, we examined whether task-based and self-report indicators of Patriotic-EG show meaningful cross-method convergence.

Nomological network of Patriotic-EG

To locate Patriotic-EG within a nomological network, we focused on three constructs relevant to civic-affective development. Patriotic affect served as the focal criterion because it reflects the overall intensity of patriotic emotional experience. National identity reflects identification with the national group and commitment to its values, symbols, and historical meanings [10, 30, 32]; because it is broader and more identity-based than patriotic affect [16], it should be related to Patriotic-EG but less strongly. Perceived social support reflects emotional, informational, and instrumental support from family, peers, and educational environments [23], which may facilitate civic reflection, emotional expression, and identity development in educational contexts.

The present research and hypotheses

The present research comprised two sequential studies. Study 1 developed the Patriotic PED-task and examined whether the task-derived Patriotic-EG index showed interpretable individual differences and theoretically coherent associations with patriotic affect, national identity, and perceived social support. Study 2 developed a brief self-report scale and evaluated its reliability, factor structure, relations with adjacent constructs, distinction from social desirability, and cross-method convergence with the task-based index.

Specifically, we examined whether Patriotic-EG can be operationalized under standardized stimulus conditions; whether a brief self-report scale shows acceptable psychometric properties; whether the scale is distinguishable from patriotic affect, national identity, and social desirability; and whether task-based and self-report indicators show initial cross-method convergence. In Study 1, we expected task-derived Patriotic-EG to be positively associated with patriotic affect, national identity, and perceived social support. The structural model linking these variables was treated as an exploratory summary of nomological relations; because the design was cross-sectional, indirect paths were interpreted descriptively rather than causally.

In Study 2, we expected the brief scale to show acceptable internal consistency and a coherent one-factor structure; positive but non-redundant associations with patriotic affect and national identity; weak overlap with social desirability; incremental validity beyond social desirability; and positive cross-method convergence with task-derived Patriotic-EG.

The partially overlapping validation variables reflected the distinct goals of the two studies: Study 1 emphasized broader nomological placement, whereas Study 2 emphasized psychometric validation, social-desirability distinction, and cross-method convergence. Patriotic affect and national identity were included in both studies to maintain conceptual continuity.

Methods

Overview

The present research comprised two sequential studies. Study 1 focused on the task-based operationalization and initial validation of Patriotic Emotional Granularity (Patriotic-EG) using a Patriotic Photo Emotion Differentiation task (Patriotic PED-task). Study 2 focused on the development and validation of a brief self-report scale of Patriotic-EG and its cross-method association with the task-based indicator. Both studies were conducted with university student samples in China and were approved by the relevant institutional ethics committee. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation.

Study 1: Task-based initial validation of Patriotic-EG

Participants and procedure

Participants were undergraduate students recruited from three universities in Zhejiang Province, China, using cluster sampling. A total of 790 students initially participated. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wenzhou University, and all participants provided informed consent. Data collection consisted of two sequential components. First, participants completed a computer-based Patriotic Photo Emotion Differentiation task (Patriotic PED-task) in a laboratory setting. Immediately afterward, they completed an online questionnaire survey assessing patriotic affect, national identity, and perceived social support. This procedure followed the original design of the task-based validation study.

Data screening and final analytic sample

Patriotic-EG was derived from the PED-task using an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) approach. Because ICC values can become negative when agreement is very low, negative ICCs were treated as not interpretable within the present scoring framework and were therefore excluded. From the initial sample of 790 participants, 75 were excluded due to negative ICC values, 83 due to invalid or poor-quality responses, and 98 due to missing data on key measures. The final analytic sample for Study 1 consisted of 534 participants (124 males, 410 females).

Measures

Patriotic Photo Emotion Differentiation Task (Patriotic PED-task)

The Patriotic PED-task was adapted from photo-based emotion differentiation paradigms [7] and used 10 patriotism-related affect terms identified as frequently used among Chinese college students [17]: struggling, dedication, belonging, identity, honor, mission, responsibility, justice, pride, and dignity. These terms were selected because they represent culturally salient and education-relevant components of patriotic emotional experience among Chinese university students.

Theoretically, the selected terms cover several interrelated but distinguishable aspects of patriotic emotion. Belonging and identity reflect affective attachment to the national group; pride, honor, and dignity capture self-evaluative and collective-evaluative patriotic feelings; responsibility, mission, dedication, and struggling reflect obligation-oriented and action-related patriotic emotions; and justice reflects the moral-normative dimension of patriotic experience. Thus, the term set was intended to capture the heterogeneous structure of patriotic emotion rather than a single undifferentiated positive feeling. Empirically, these terms were grounded in prior work showing that they are frequently used by Chinese college students when describing patriotic affective experience [17]. Accordingly, the present study used them as a standardized stimulus-bound rating vocabulary to examine whether individuals differ in how specifically they differentiate multiple patriotism-related feelings.

Stimulus selection

Candidate images were identified via Baidu Image Search to represent each of the 10 affect terms. Images were screened and standardized for experimental presentation (1040 × 650 pixels; JPG format), with minor adjustments such as cropping and size normalization. An independent pilot sample (n = 32, not included in the main study) rated each candidate image on 7-point scales of arousal, typicality, and perceived patriotic affect strength. For each affect term, one image with high arousal and typicality and the highest patriotic-affect rating was selected, resulting in a final set of 10 images used in the main task. Because the images were retrieved online and may be subject to copyright restrictions, the original stimuli are not reproduced in the article or supplementary materials; instead, pilot summaries and brief, non-identifying textual descriptions are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Task procedure

Participants completed 10 trials, with one image presented per trial. In each trial, an image was shown and followed by the list of the 10 patriotic affect terms. Participants rated the intensity of each affect term elicited by the image on a 7-point scale ranging from 1 (none) to 7 (overwhelmingly intense). The task flow is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Task-derived Patriotic Emotional Granularity (Patriotic-EG)

Patriotic-EG was computed from participants’ PED-task rating profiles using ICC(3, k), a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement and average measures.

Shrout and Fleiss [27], higher ICC values indicate greater similarity of ratings across affect terms within trials, and thus lower emotional differentiation. To facilitate interpretation such that higher scores indicate higher granularity, ICC values were standardized across participants and reverse-transformed as: Patriotic-EG = 1 − z(ICC),

where z(ICC) denotes the sample-based z-score of ICC. Larger Patriotic-EG values therefore indicate greater differentiation of patriotism-related feelings.

Patriotic affect

Patriotic affect was assessed using the patriotic affect subscale of the China College Students’ Moral and Emotional Questionnaire [17]. The subscale includes six items rated on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = completely disagree, 6 = completely agree). Item scores were summed, with higher scores indicating stronger patriotic affect. Internal consistency in Study 1 was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.797).

National identity

National identity was measured using the National Identity Scale adapted from Li and Liu [16], comprising five items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very inconsistent, 5 = very consistent). Item scores were summed, with higher scores indicating stronger national identity. Internal consistency in Study 1 was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.972).

Perceived social support

Perceived social support was assessed using the Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS; [33]), a 10-item measure covering objective support, subjective support, and support utilization. Scoring followed the SSRS manual rules [34]. Internal consistency in Study 1 was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.729).

Data analysis

Analyses proceeded in four steps. First, we computed the task-based Patriotic-EG index and examined descriptive statistics and Pearson correlations among Patriotic-EG, patriotic affect, national identity, and perceived social support. Second, we evaluated the measurement model of the questionnaire constructs using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) in Mplus 8.0. Third, we estimated an integrative structural model in Mplus 8.0 and obtained bootstrap confidence intervals based on 5,000 resamples for indirect associations. Given the cross-sectional design, indirect paths were interpreted as descriptive summaries of covariation within a nomological network rather than as evidence of causal mediation. Finally, auxiliary analyses, including descriptive statistics and demographic comparisons, were conducted in SPSS 25.0.

Study 2: Brief scale development and cross-method bridging

Participants and design

Study 2 was designed to develop a brief self-report scale of Patriotic-EG and to evaluate its psychometric properties, external correlates, distinction from social desirability, and cross-method association with the task-based index. Because self-reports of emotionally and politically charged constructs can be susceptible to socially desirable responding [21], and because social desirability has been shown to affect responses to nativism-related attitudes [14], we included social desirability as a validity-relevant control variable. Recent work also suggests that self-reported emotional abilities may show limited convergence with performance-based measures [31], making cross-method validation particularly important.

A total of 381 undergraduate students were initially recruited for the questionnaire phase of Study 2. After data screening and exclusion of incomplete or invalid cases, 301 participants were retained for the main psychometric validation analyses. The final analytic sample consisted of 149 males and 152 females, with a mean age of 20.20 years (SD = 1.49).

Among the Study 2 participants, 109 also had available task-based Patriotic-EG data and were therefore included in the bridge analysis. The bridge subsample comprised 48 males and 61 females, with a mean age of 19.08 years (SD = 0.89, range = 18–24). Thus, the bridge sample constituted a subsample of the broader Study 2 sample rather than an independent sample.

Procedure

Participants completed an online questionnaire including demographic items, the candidate Patriotic-EG self-report items, the patriotic affect scale, the national identity scale, and a short social desirability scale. For a subsample of participants (n = 109), task-based Patriotic-EG data were also available, allowing examination of the association between the brief self-report measure and the task-based indicator. Because the primary aim of Study 2 was scale development and initial validation, all analyses involving item structure, reliability, and external correlates were conducted in the final psychometric analytic sample (n = 301), whereas the bridge analysis was conducted in the subsample with both self-report and task-based data.

Measures

Candidate brief Patriotic-EG Scale

A 10-item candidate self-report scale was initially developed to assess the extent to which individuals can distinguish among different patriotic emotional experiences in nation-relevant contexts. Items were designed to reflect differentiation of patriotic emotional states rather than global patriotic intensity. All items were rated on Likert-type response scales, with higher scores indicating greater perceived differentiation of patriotic emotional experience. This approach follows the logic of recent scale development in emotion differentiation research, where brief self-report measures have been developed to complement more time-intensive task-based assessments [13].

Based on item analysis and confirmatory factor analysis, two items (Q7 and Q9) were removed, resulting in a final 8-item brief scale consisting of Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4, Q5, Q6, Q8, and Q10. Internal consistency of the final scale in the present sample was good (Cronbach’s α = 0.852). More detailed psychometric indices, including McDonald’s ω, composite reliability, average variance extracted, and CFA model fit, are reported in the Results section.

Patriotic affect

Patriotic affect was assessed using the same six-item patriotic affect subscale described in Study 1 [17]. The scale was rated on a 6-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating stronger patriotic affect. Internal consistency in the present Study 2 sample was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.742).

National identity

National identity was measured using the same five-item scale described in Study 1 [16]. Higher scores indicate stronger national identity. In the present Study 2 sample, however, the original 5-item scale showed low internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.405), indicating that the scale did not function as a highly homogeneous measure in this sample. Item-level inspection suggested that one item, referring to feeling ashamed of existing national problems, was weakly aligned with the remaining items. Conceptually, this item may capture a more reflective or critical form of national attachment rather than simple national identification. That is, feeling concerned or ashamed about national problems may coexist with strong national attachment, rather than necessarily indicating low identification.

To maintain consistency with Study 1 and with the original scale, the main analyses first retained the original 5-item score. However, given the low reliability of the original version in Study 2, we also conducted and reported sensitivity analyses using a reduced 4-item version excluding this theoretically distinctive item. Results based on both the original 5-item version and the reduced 4-item version are reported in the main Results section.

Social desirability

Social desirability was assessed using a short-form social desirability scale consisting of eight items [18]. Higher scores indicate greater socially desirable responding. Internal consistency in the present Study 2 sample was modest but acceptable for a brief screening measure (Cronbach’s α = 0.641).

Task-based Patriotic-EG (bridge subsample)

In the bridge subsample, task-based Patriotic-EG was indexed using the PED-task scoring procedure described in Study 1. For the bridge analysis, the relevant variable was the task-derived Patriotic-EG score, which was correlated with the brief self-report scale score.

Results

Study 1: Task-based initial validation of Patriotic-EG

Method-bias assessment for the questionnaire component

Because Study 1 combined a task-derived behavioral index (Patriotic-EG from the Patriotic PED-task) with self-report questionnaires, concerns about common method bias primarily applied to the questionnaire measures. Several procedural steps were used to reduce shared-method threats, including separating the laboratory task and the subsequent online questionnaire by mode and setting, protecting anonymity, and emphasizing honest responding. As a supplementary diagnostic, Harman’s single-factor test (unrotated exploratory factor analysis) extracted four factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, and the first factor accounted for 17.54% of the total variance, below the commonly used 40% heuristic benchmark. The four extracted factors jointly explained 62.30% of the variance. Taken together, these results do not suggest that a single common method factor dominated the covariance among the questionnaire variables.

Overall, this correlation pattern was consistent with the proposed nomological network. The smaller associations with national identity and perceived social support were also directionally consistent with theory, suggesting that task-derived Patriotic-EG was embedded in a broader civic-affective network without collapsing into these adjacent constructs [1].

Integrative structural summary of task-based Patriotic-EG and adjacent constructs

To summarize the relation between task-derived Patriotic-EG and adjacent self-report variables, we estimated an integrative structural model linking Patriotic-EG, social support, national identity, and patriotic affect (Fig. 2). Prior to structural estimation, a four-factor confirmatory factor analysis was conducted to evaluate the distinctiveness of the modeled constructs. The four-factor measurement model showed excellent fit, χ²/df = 1.32, CFI = 0.998, TLI = 0.997, RMSEA = 0.000, 90% CI [0.000, 0.021], and SRMR = 0.012, supporting the discriminant validity of the questionnaire-based constructs in the model.

Within the structural model, the total association between Patriotic-EG and patriotic affect was statistically reliable, B = 0.493, 95% bootstrap CI [0.162, 0.823]. This association included a significant direct component, B = 0.365, 95% bootstrap CI [0.049, 0.681], and a significant total indirect component, B = 0.128, 95% bootstrap CI [0.029, 0.253]. Because the design was cross-sectional, these indirect paths are interpreted here as descriptive summaries of covariation rather than as evidence of causal mediation. Even so, the overall pattern was directionally consistent with the proposed nomological network (Model of Study 1): higher task-derived Patriotic-EG tended to co-occur with stronger patriotic affect and with theoretically relevant correlates in the surrounding civic-affective system.

Interim summary

Taken together, Study 1 provides initial support for Patriotic-EG as a task-measurable individual-difference construct. The Patriotic PED-task yielded interpretable variation in Patriotic-EG, and the resulting task-derived index showed theoretically coherent associations with patriotic affect, national identity, and perceived social support. These findings support the task-based operationalization of Patriotic-EG and establish a first layer of validity evidence. At the same time, a task-based paradigm is relatively time-consuming and less convenient for broader survey use. Study 2 therefore sought to develop a brief self-report scale of Patriotic-EG and to examine whether a more economical measure could retain acceptable psychometric properties while also showing meaningful convergence with the task-based index.

Study 2: Brief scale development and cross-method bridging

Item analysis and final scale formation

Study 2 began with a 10-item candidate self-report scale intended to assess Patriotic Emotional Granularity in a brief and scalable format. Initial item analysis indicated that the 10-item version showed acceptable internal consistency. Examination of corrected item-total correlations and “alpha if deleted” statistics suggested that Q7 performed relatively weakly compared with the other items. Because item deletion decisions were not based on internal consistency alone, alternative shortened versions were then compared using confirmatory factor analysis.

When candidate 8-item solutions were compared, the version excluding Q7 and Q9 showed the best overall balance of internal consistency and structural fit. By contrast, the alternative solution excluding Q7 and Q8 yielded poorer model fit and included a weaker remaining loading on Q9. On this basis, Q7 and Q9 were removed, resulting in a final 8-item brief Patriotic-EG scale consisting of Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4, Q5, Q6, Q8, and Q10. The final 8-item scale showed good internal consistency in the psychometric analytic sample (n = 301). Cronbach’s α was 0.852, McDonald’s ω was 0.845, and composite reliability (CR) was 0.852, indicating satisfactory internal reliability. Because the original 5-item national identity scale showed low internal consistency in Study 2, sensitivity analyses were conducted using a reduced 4-item version excluding the item referring to feeling ashamed of existing national problems. The results were consistent with, and somewhat stronger than, those based on the original 5-item version. Using the original 5-item national identity score, the brief Patriotic-EG scale was positively associated with national identity, r = .173, p = .003. Using the reduced 4-item version, the association became stronger, r = .279, 95% CI [0.171, 0.380], p < .001. Thus, the positive association between the brief Patriotic-EG scale and national identity was not driven solely by the theoretically distinctive item. Instead, the association appeared more robust when national identity was indexed by items reflecting more direct national identification.

Overall, this pattern of correlations suggests that the brief Patriotic-EG scale was related to theoretically adjacent patriotic constructs in expected directions, but was not strongly overlapping with social desirability.

Associations with patriotic affect and national identity controlling for social desirability

To determine whether the observed associations between the brief Patriotic-EG scale and adjacent constructs were primarily driven by socially desirable responding, partial correlations were computed controlling for social desirability. The relation between the brief Patriotic-EG scale and patriotic affect remained significant after controlling for social desirability, pr = 0.483, p < .001. Likewise, the partial correlation between the brief scale and national identity remained significant, pr = 0.173, p = .003. When the reduced 4-item national identity score was used, the association remained significant and was somewhat stronger, pr = 0.271, p < .001.

These results indicate that the associations of the brief scale with patriotic affect and national identity were not reducible to shared variance with social desirability. In particular, the partial correlation with patriotic affect remained almost unchanged relative to the zero-order correlation, suggesting that the link between Patriotic-EG and patriotic affect was robust rather than an artifact of socially desirable responding.

Incremental validity beyond social desirability

We next examined whether the brief Patriotic-EG scale explained unique variance in patriotic affect and national identity beyond social desirability.

For patriotic affect, a baseline model including social desirability alone was first estimated, followed by a second model adding the brief Patriotic-EG score. Adding the brief scale significantly improved model fit, ΔF(1, 298) = 90.67, p < .001. In the final model, the brief Patriotic-EG scale showed a significant positive effect, B = 0.531, SE = 0.056, t = 9.522, p < .001, whereas social desirability also remained significant but weaker, B = 0.122, SE = 0.049, t = 2.484, p = .014. The final model accounted for 25.5% of the variance in patriotic affect (R² = 0.255, adjusted R² = 0.250). This pattern indicates that the brief scale contributed substantial unique explanatory power beyond social desirability.

For national identity, a parallel hierarchical regression was conducted. Again, adding the brief Patriotic-EG scale significantly improved model fit, ΔF(1, 298) = 9.25, p = .003. In the final model, the brief scale was a significant positive predictor of national identity, B = 0.143, SE = 0.047, t = 3.041, p = .003, whereas social desirability was not significant, B = − 0.007, SE = 0.042, t = − 0.166, p = .868. Although the variance explained was modest (R² = 0.030, adjusted R² = 0.024), the result indicates that the brief scale still contributed unique variance to national identity beyond social desirability. Given the low internal consistency of the original 5-item national identity scale in Study 2, we further repeated the analysis using the reduced 4-item version. The overall pattern remained positive and became stronger. Adding the brief Patriotic-EG scale significantly improved prediction of the reduced national identity score, ΔF(1, 298) = 23.68, p < .001. These results suggest that the incremental association between Patriotic-EG and national identity was not dependent on the problematic item and was more evident when national identity was indexed by the more homogeneous 4-item score.

Taken together, these analyses support the incremental validity of the brief Patriotic-EG scale. Its contribution was stronger for patriotic affect than for national identity, which is theoretically consistent with the idea that Patriotic-EG is more closely linked to patriotic emotional experience than to broader identity-based orientation.

Cross-method bridging between the brief scale and the task-based index

To examine initial cross-method convergence, the final 8-item brief Patriotic-EG scale was correlated with the task-derived Patriotic-EG index in the bridge subsample (n = 109). The two indicators showed a significant positive Pearson correlation, r = .277, 95% CI [0.094, 0.442], p = .004. This result indicates that the brief self-report scale was not disconnected from task-based performance, but instead captured variance that overlapped meaningfully with the standardized behavioral differentiation index.

A supplementary Spearman correlation was smaller and did not reach significance, ρ = 0.159, p = .099, suggesting that the cross-method association was modest and more apparent at the linear-score level than at the rank-order level. Overall, this pattern is consistent with the interpretation that the self-report and task-based measures were related but not interchangeable. The brief scale may reflect a more generalized self-perception of one’s tendency to differentiate patriotic emotional experience, whereas the PED-task captures online differentiation under standardized stimulus conditions. Thus, the bridge analysis provides initial support for cross-method convergence while also preserving room for method-specific variance.

Discussion

The present research introduced Patriotic Emotional Granularity (Patriotic-EG) as a construct indexing individual differences in the differentiation of patriotism-related emotional states and examined its measurement through two complementary approaches. Three main findings emerged. First, Study 1 showed that Patriotic-EG can be operationalized using the Patriotic Photo Emotion Differentiation task (Patriotic PED-task). The task-derived index was positively associated with patriotic affect and showed smaller, theoretically consistent associations with national identity and perceived social support, supporting its placement within a civic-affective nomological network. Second, Study 2 supported a brief 8-item self-report scale with good internal consistency, an acceptable one-factor structure, theoretically coherent associations with patriotic affect and national identity, and weak overlap with social desirability. Third, the bridge analysis showed a positive association between the brief scale and the task-derived index, suggesting meaningful but method-specific convergence.

Patriotic-EG as a distinct aspect of patriotic emotion

Conceptually, the key contribution is to distinguish Patriotic-EG from adjacent patriotic constructs. Existing work has primarily focused on patriotic affect, patriotic attitudes, or national identity [20], whereas the present findings highlight an additional dimension: how individuals differentiate among multiple patriotic emotional states. Thus, Patriotic-EG adds a structural dimension to the study of patriotic emotion. This view is consistent with work conceptualizing emotional granularity as a regulation-relevant ability rather than a fixed trait [28].

Patriotic affect reflects emotional intensity, whereas Patriotic-EG reflects the specificity of patriotic experience, consistent with broader distinctions between affective intensity and differentiated emotional representation [2, 12]. Similarly, national identity reflects broader cognitive-evaluative identification, whereas Patriotic-EG concerns the organization of patriotic emotional experience [20]. The moderate association with patriotic affect and weaker association with national identity in Study 2 support this distinction without suggesting redundancy.

Extending emotional granularity to a higher-order civic-affective domain

These findings extend emotional granularity research into a higher-order, socially embedded civic-affective domain. Whereas prior work has focused mainly on general affective life or negative emotion differentiation [8, 19], patriotic emotion involves culturally and symbolically grounded responses. The present findings suggest that granularity is meaningful in this domain, complementing evidence that emotional granularity is associated with adaptive functioning and national-symbol processing [9, 15].

Across both studies, Patriotic-EG was not merely a restatement of generic patriotic feeling: the task-derived index showed theoretically coherent associations in Study 1, and the brief scale retained associations with patriotic affect and national identity after controlling for social desirability in Study 2. These findings support the application of emotional granularity to patriotic emotion as a socially and educationally relevant domain [25].

Methodological contribution: task-based and self-report approaches are complementary

Methodologically, Study 1 showed that Patriotic-EG can be measured through a standardized task-based differentiation paradigm, moving assessment beyond global retrospective endorsement toward stimulus-bound emotion ratings. Study 2 addressed the complementary need for a concise self-report measure, with the final 8-item scale showing acceptable psychometric properties and a positive association with the task-based indicator. The bridge correlation was significant but modest (r = .277, p = .004), indicating meaningful but limited cross-method convergence. The non-significant Spearman correlation further suggests limited rank-order consistency. The brief scale may reflect generalized self-perceptions of differentiation ability, whereas the PED-task captures online differentiation under standardized stimuli. Thus, the two measures should be viewed as complementary rather than interchangeable, consistent with multitrait–multimethod reasoning [4, 6]. The partially overlapping validation variables reflected the distinct purposes of the two studies: Study 1 emphasized nomological placement, whereas Study 2 emphasized psychometric validation, social-desirability distinction, and cross-method convergence. This staged strategy is consistent with evidence that self-report and performance-based emotional granularity may capture related but distinct processes [29].

Evidence against simple social desirability or construct redundancy

Because patriotic content can be vulnerable to socially desirable responding [14, 21], we tested whether the brief scale merely reflected norm-consistent response tendencies. The evidence did not support this interpretation: the scale showed only a weak, non-significant association with social desirability, retained its associations with patriotic affect and national identity after controlling for social desirability, and explained unique variance in both outcomes. These results strengthen the interpretation that the brief scale captures differentiated patriotic emotional experience rather than patriotic affect intensity, national identity, or social desirability. Its stronger unique prediction of patriotic affect than of national identity is theoretically reasonable because Patriotic-EG is closer to emotional experience than to broad identity orientation [12].

Implications for civic-affective and educational research

The findings suggest that patriotic emotion should not be treated only as global intensity, endorsement, or identity attachment. Patriotic-EG highlights the structure and specificity of civic-affective experience, providing a fine-grained way to study how individuals organize multiple patriotism-related feelings. It also extends emotional granularity theory to culturally symbolic and identity-related forms of emotion.

Educationally, this framework implies that civic-affective development may involve not only stronger patriotic feeling, but also more differentiated and context-sensitive ways of experiencing patriotic content. Future work could examine how classroom climate, reflective civic learning, interventions, value internalization, and identity development relate to such differentiation.

Higher Patriotic-EG should not be interpreted as direct evidence of better civic literacy, constructive patriotism, political tolerance, or lower extreme nationalism, because these outcomes were not measured. Future studies should directly test whether differentiated patriotic emotional experience is associated with more reflective and less exclusionary civic-affective development. The two measures also provide a flexible research toolkit: the brief scale is feasible for larger samples, whereas the Patriotic PED-task remains useful when standardized stimulus-bound differentiation is of interest. However, the evidence does not yet support high-stakes individual assessment; both measures are better positioned as research tools or exploratory educational instruments.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the final brief scale showed good internal consistency and acceptable factor structure, its AVE did not reach the conventional 0.50 threshold. Given the acceptable composite reliability and factor loadings, the scale provides usable initial evidence, but future work should improve item homogeneity. Second, both studies were cross-sectional, so the structural and regression results should be interpreted as association patterns rather than causal mechanisms. Longitudinal, experimental, or intervention-based designs are needed to examine whether Patriotic-EG changes over time or can be shaped by educational experience. Third, the samples were university students with limited demographic diversity. Future studies should test generalizability across regions, age groups, and educational levels, and evaluate test-retest reliability, measurement invariance, and subgroup fairness [26]. Fourth, one national identity item in Study 2, referring to feeling ashamed of existing national problems, showed weaker coherence with the remaining items. Sensitivity analyses excluding this item yielded a similar and somewhat stronger pattern, suggesting that it may reflect a more reflective or critical form of national attachment. Future work should refine item wording and content coverage and use Item Response Theory (IRT) methods to identify items that function differently across groups [5]. Fifth, the bridge analysis remains limited by its smaller sample and modest convergence between self-report and task-based indicators. Future work should strengthen cross-method validation using larger bridge samples, repeated assessments, robustness checks, and additional methods such as text-based scenarios, experience sampling, or behavioral indicators beyond PED-style tasks [22]. Finally, the educational relevance of Patriotic-EG remains preliminary because the present studies did not directly assess civic literacy, constructive versus blind patriotism, political tolerance, or outgroup attitudes. Future research should include these outcomes to determine whether differentiated patriotic emotional experience is associated with more reflective and less exclusionary civic-affective development.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present research provides initial evidence that Patriotic Emotional Granularity is a meaningful and measurable construct. Across two studies, Patriotic-EG was operationalized through a task-based differentiation paradigm and a brief self-report scale, with both approaches showing theoretically coherent validity evidence and modest cross-method convergence. These findings support a multi-method foundation for studying patriotic emotion in terms of emotional specificity and structure rather than global intensity alone.
