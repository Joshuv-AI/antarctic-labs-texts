Association of alzheimer’s disease with prior allergic rhinitis
Authors: Shih-Han Hung, Shiu-Dong Chung, Herng-Ching Lin, Chin-Shyan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is a growing public health concern, with neuroinflammation implicated in its pathogenesis. Allergic rhinitis (AR), a prevalent chronic inflammatory disorder, may contribute to systemic inflammation and potentially influence AD risk. This study sought to critically assess the association between a history of AR and subsequent AD development in a large, representative Taiwanese cohort. Leveraging Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database (LHID2010), this nationwide case-control study identified 4,681 individuals aged ≥ 65 years with a first-time AD diagnosis (cases) and 14,043 propensity-score-matched controls. A rigorous definition of prior AR required at least two clinical diagnoses, including one by an otolaryngology specialist. Multivariable logistic regression was used to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), adjusting for potential confounders. The prevalence of prior AR was significantly higher in AD patients than in controls (25.29% vs. 21.01%, p < 0.001). Following meticulous adjustment for demographic variables, socioeconomic status, geographic factors, and medical comorbidities (including hyperlipidemia, diabetes, coronary heart disease, hearing loss, and hypertension), prior AR was robustly associated with elevated odds of AD (adjusted OR = 1.279, 95% CI = 1.182 ~ 1.384). This association remained significant for both males (adjusted OR = 1.196, 95% CI = 1.053 ~ 1.358) and females (adjusted OR = 1.339, 95% CI = 1.210 ~ 1.482). This study suggests a significant association between prior AR and an increased odds of developing AD in an elderly Taiwanese population. Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the most common form of dementia, presents a significant and escalating public health challenge globally, with its prevalence projected to increase dramatically as populations age. This neurodegenerative disorder not only affects the individuals diagnosed with it but also impacts their families, caregivers, and healthcare systems1,2. This neurodegenerative disorder is characterized by progressive cognitive decline, including memory impairment, and leads to a substantial loss of independence in activities of daily living3,4. While advanced age is the most prominent risk factor, a complex interplay of genetic and environmental factors, including lifestyle and comorbid health conditions, contributes to AD pathogenesis5,6.

Growing evidence points towards neuroinflammation as a critical component in the pathophysiology of AD7,8. Chronic inflammatory processes within the brain, involving the activation of microglia and astrocytes and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, are thought to contribute to neuronal dysfunction, damage, and the exacerbation of hallmark AD pathologies such as amyloid-beta plaques and neurofibrillary tangles9,10. This has led to increased interest in how systemic inflammation, potentially triggered by conditions outside the central nervous system, might influence brain health and AD risk11.

Allergic rhinitis (AR) is a common chronic inflammatory condition of the nasal airways, mediated by an IgE-dependent hypersensitivity response to allergens12,13. It is characterized by symptoms such as nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, and sneezing, and is associated with the release of various inflammatory mediators that can have systemic effects14. Given that AR is a state of chronic peripheral inflammation, there is a plausible biological basis for hypothesizing its potential contribution to neuroinflammation and, consequently, to the risk of developing AD15,16. Studies have suggested that systemic inflammation can compromise the integrity of the blood-brain barrier and promote the entry of inflammatory mediators into the brain, thereby potentially initiating or accelerating neurodegenerative processes17,18.

Previous epidemiological investigations into the association between allergic diseases and AD have yielded varied results, highlighting the need for further research. Some studies have suggested a positive association between atopic disorders, including AR, and an increased risk of dementia or AD19,20. Conversely, other research has presented conflicting findings or explored the complexity of this relationship, suggesting that the nature of the association might be influenced by factors such as the specific type of allergic disease, its severity, duration, or even genetic predispositions21,22.

While previous studies have explored the link between allergic diseases and dementia, much of the evidence has been derived from Western cohorts. This study aims to fill this gap by utilizing a large Taiwanese cohort with a rigorous diagnostic definition of AR, thereby providing a large-scale epidemiological replication in an Asian population. By utilizing a highly representative Taiwanese database, this observational study validates prior findings in a different ethnic and geographic context, thereby contributing incremental but essential real-world evidence to the global epidemiology of AD.

Methods

Database

This investigation drew upon data from Taiwan’s Longitudinal Health Insurance Database 2010 (LHID2010), a component of the National Health Insurance (NHI) program. Initiated in 1995, the NHI provides universal, single-payer health coverage to virtually all Taiwanese citizens, encompassing a wide array of medical services with minimal out-of-pocket expenses. The LHID2010 contains comprehensive medical claims and registration data for 2,000,000 individuals, randomly sampled from the 2010 NHI Beneficiary Registry. Its national representativeness regarding gender, age, and insurance premium strata is well-documented by Taiwan’s National Health Research Institutes, making it a validated resource for numerous epidemiological and clinical studies.

This study received ethical sanction from the Institutional Review Board of National Taiwan University (approval no. 202312EM054) and was conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the utilization of de-identified, secondary administrative data and retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for patient informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board of National Taiwan University.

Identification of study patients

We meticulously identified all individuals aged 65 years or older who received an initial diagnosis of AD between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2019. AD diagnoses were coded using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM: 331.0, 290.40–290.43) or the Tenth Revision (ICD-10-CM: G30) during ambulatory care encounters. To bolster diagnostic validity, case inclusion necessitated a minimum of two distinct ambulatory care claims documenting AD. The date of the first qualifying AD diagnosis was designated as the index date for each case. Exclusion criteria were applied to individuals with a pre-index date history of major psychosis or substance use disorders (ICD-9-CM: 291–299, 303–305; ICD-10-CM: F10–F19), stroke (ICD-9-CM: 430–438; ICD-10-CM: I60–I69), or traumatic brain injury (ICD-9-CM: 801–804, 850–854; ICD-10-CM: S06). In order to ensure the diagnostic specificity of the AD cohort under investigation, and to minimize the potential overlap with vascular dementia, it was necessary to strictly exclude individuals with a history of stroke prior to the index date. Stroke constitutes a primary risk factor for vascular dementia, and its exclusion serves to refine the study’s focus on the neuroinflammatory links specific to AD. This rigorous selection process yielded a final cohort of 4,681 AD patients.

As the LHID2010 is a representative random sample specifically designed to reflect the national population distribution, additional survey weighting was not applied. Instead, we employed propensity score matching to balance the distributions of age, sex, and socioeconomic strata (monthly income) between the cases and controls. A control group was selected from the remaining LHID2010 beneficiaries aged ≥ 65 years who were free of any AD diagnosis history. Propensity score matching was employed to ensure comparability between cases and controls. These scores were derived from a logistic regression model incorporating age, sex, monthly income strata (NT$0–15,840, NT$15,841–25,000, ≥NT$25,001; US$1 ≈ NT$28 in 2021), geographic region of residence (Northern, Central, Southern, Eastern Taiwan), residential urbanization level (scaled 1 [most urbanized] to 5 [least urbanized]), and prevalent comorbidities including coronary heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, hearing loss, and hyperlipidemia. Hearing loss was identified based on ICD-9-CM codes (389.xx) or ICD-10-CM codes (H90, H91) from the ambulatory care claims. In the statistical models, hearing loss was treated as a categorical variable (presence vs. absence) and was included as a covariate in both the propensity score matching and multivariable logistic regression analyses to control for its potential confounding effect on cognitive decline. Specifically, we performed 1:3 propensity score matching using a greedy matching algorithm with a caliper of 0.2 times the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. This process was intended to balance the baseline characteristics between the study groups. For controls, the index year was a matched year involving an ambulatory care visit, corresponding to the case’s index year. This procedure resulted in 14,043 matched controls. Following the matching process, unconditional logistic regression was employed to estimate the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). This approach treats the matched cohorts as balanced independent groups to ensure model stability in a large-scale database context. Logistic regression models using clustered (robust) standard errors were also estimated to account for within-pair correlation and obtain valid statistical inference. In addition, we conducted conditional logistic regression as a sensitivity analysis, which explicitly accounts for the matched pairs. Results from both approaches were compared to assess the robustness of the findings.

Identification of risk factor exposure

The primary exposure of interest was a history of AR preceding the index date. AR diagnoses were identified using ICD-9-CM code 477.9 or ICD-10-CM code J30.9. To enhance the specificity of AR ascertainment, individuals were classified as exposed if they had accumulated at least two AR diagnoses prior to the index date, with the crucial stipulation that at least one of these diagnoses was rendered by a board-certified otolaryngologist. While administrative data do not provide detailed information at the level of individual symptoms, clinical diagnosis by specialists in Taiwan is generally performed in accordance with the ARIA (Allergic Rhinitis and its Impact on Asthma) guidelines, which classify the disease based on the persistence and severity of symptoms such as rhinorrhea and nasal congestion. As the LHID2010 is based on administrative claims data, direct measurements of peripheral inflammatory markers—such as serum IgE, eosinophil counts, or specific Th2 cytokines (e.g., IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13)—were unavailable for analysis. Consequently, the presence of AR was used as a clinical proxy for chronic Th2-mediated systemic inflammation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical evaluations were executed using SAS software (version 9.4, SAS Institute, Cary, NC). To assess the quality of propensity score matching, standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated for all baseline covariates, with an SMD < 0.1 indicating a negligible imbalance between groups. The core association between prior AR and AD was quantified using multivariable logistic regression, yielding ORs and corresponding 95% CIs. These models incorporated adjustments for sociodemographic characteristics and medical comorbidities. For the outcome analysis, a ‘doubly robust’ approach was employed by including matching variables as covariates in a standard logistic regression to adjust for any residual confounding. It was decided that other atopic conditions, such as asthma and atopic dermatitis, should not be included in the primary multivariable model. This was due to the potential for multicollinearity, given the frequent overlap of these conditions as part of the ‘atopic march’. Conversely, the independent influence of these factors was assessed through a dedicated sensitivity analysis. Furthermore, the primary analysis tested a single, pre-specified hypothesis concerning the association between AR and AD; consequently, adjustments for multiple comparisons were not applied to the primary results. The standardized mean differences for baseline characteristics in Table 1 were primarily provided for the purpose of assessing the balance of covariates between the matched cohorts, as opposed to for hypothesis testing. However, the potential for inflated Type I error due to multiple testing should be acknowledged, and these results should be interpreted with appropriate caution. In the multivariable logistic regression models, age was incorporated as a continuous covariate. To ensure the robustness of the model, multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF); no significant multicollinearity was observed, as all VIF values remained below 5. The linearity assumption for continuous variables was verified using the Box-Tidwell test. Finally, model goodness-of-fit was confirmed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, with a p-value > 0.05 indicating an adequate fit between the model and the observed data. Statistical significance was determined by a two-sided p-value ≤ 0.05.

Results

Table 1 provides a detailed comparison of the sociodemographic profiles for AD cases and their matched controls. All values of the standardized mean difference that are less than 0.1 are generally interpreted as indicating that the propensity-score matching procedure has successfully balanced the groups. This has resulted in the revelation of no statistically significant inter-group differences in mean age, sex distribution, geographic region, or level of residential urbanization. Furthermore, the prevalence rates of critical comorbidities, including hyperlipidemia, diabetes, coronary heart disease, hearing loss, and hypertension, were comparable between the case and control cohorts post-matching.

A key finding, detailed in Table 2, was the significantly higher prevalence of antecedent AR among individuals diagnosed with AD (25.29%) compared to the control group (21.01%; p < 0.001). Subsequent multivariable logistic regression analyses (Table 2) corroborated this observation. In the unadjusted model, a history of AR conferred a 1.273-fold increase in the odds of AD (95% CI = 1.178 ~ 1.376). This association retained statistical significance and magnitude after comprehensive adjustment for sex, monthly income, age, urbanization level, geographic location, coronary heart disease, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, hearing loss, and hypertension, with individuals having prior AR exhibiting 1.279 times the odds of developing AD (adjusted OR = 1.182, 95% CI = 1.226 ~ 1.384).

Analyses stratified by sex further illuminated this association (Table 2). Among male participants, a history of AR was linked to a statistically significant increase in AD odds (adjusted OR = 1.196, 95% CI = 1.053 ~ 1.358). A similar pattern of elevated AD odds was evident among female participants with prior AR, following adjustment for the covariates (adjusted OR = 1.339, 95% CI = 1.210 ~ 1.482). Furthermore, in the stratified analysis restricted to patients without comorbid asthma or atopic dermatitis, the association between AR and AD remained robust. This confirms that the observed link is independent of other major atopic phenotypes.

Finally, we estimated logistic regression models with clustered (robust) standard errors to address within-pair correlation and the potential underestimation of variance. The results showed that prior allergic rhinitis was significantly associated with an increased risk of Alzheimer’s disease (Table 3). The estimated odds ratios were the same as those from the primary analysis, with slightly larger standard errors, resulting in comparable or marginally attenuated statistical significance.

Discussion

This large-scale, population-based case-control study conducted in Taiwan, utilizing the extensive LHID2010 database, presents compelling findings regarding the relationship between AR and diagnosis of AD among the elderly Taiwanese population. The study reveals a statistically significant association, indicating that individuals with a history of AR are 1.279 times more likely to be diagnosed with AD compared to those without AR. This substantial increase in odds, after meticulous adjustments for various demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical factors, underscores the importance of considering AR as a condition associated with increased odds of AD. In contrast to the findings of preceding studies, which examined all-cause dementia, the present investigation concentrated exclusively on AD, due to the distinct neuroinflammatory pathogenesis that characterizes this condition. The hypothesis was formulated that chronic peripheral inflammation from AR specifically influences the neurodegenerative processes associated with amyloid-beta and tau pathology, which are hallmark features of AD. The objective of this study was to provide a more targeted analysis of the chronic inflammatory link proposed in our hypothesis. To this end, AD was isolated from other forms of dementia, such as vascular dementia.

Moreover, the association between AR and AD persisted even after conducting sex-stratified analyses. Specifically, the adjusted ORs were 1.196 for males and 1.339 for females, further highlighting the consistent link between AR history and diagnosis of AD across different genders. These results emphasize the need for further research to elucidate the underlying mechanisms that might explain this correlation, potentially paving the way for targeted interventions or preventive strategies for individuals with AR to reduce their risk of developing AD.

Our findings contribute to a growing body of evidence suggesting a link between allergic diseases and neurodegenerative disorders. Several epidemiological studies have explored this connection, though with some variability in their conclusions. For instance, a recent Mendelian randomization study suggested a potential causal association between AR and an increased risk of AD16. Similarly, a nationwide cohort study in Korea reported that individuals with AR had a higher risk of developing all-cause dementia and AD20. Other studies investigating broader categories of atopic diseases have also indicated positive associations with dementia risk19. However, the literature is not entirely uniform, with some studies showing mixed or null associations, potentially due to differences in study design, population characteristics, diagnostic criteria for AR and AD, and the extent of confounder control21,22. The consistency of our findings with several large-scale studies strengthens the hypothesis that AR may be a relevant antecedent to AD.

While previous studies have linked broader atopic diseases to dementia19,20, our emphasis on AR-specific pathways, including local nasal inflammation and its potential systemic sequelae, underscores the notion of AR as a standalone contributor. In the context of AR, the localized Th2-driven allergic response in the nasal mucosa has the potential to result in the systemic release of pro-inflammatory mediators. These circulating cytokines have the potential to compromise blood-brain barrier integrity and activate microglia, thereby facilitating neuroinflammatory cascades associated with the pathogenesis of AD. Despite the absence of clinical markers of inflammation intensity in the available dataset, the robust association observed in the AR-only subgroup suggests that AR contributes independently to this systemic inflammatory burden. This specificity may be partially attributed to the olfactory pathway, where chronic nasal inflammation could directly impact the olfactory bulb—one of the earliest brain regions affected by AD pathology. Such findings distinguish AR from other atopic phenotypes like atopic dermatitis, emphasizing its unique role in the chronic inflammatory link to cognitive decline.

The biological mechanisms underlying the association between AR and AD are complex and involve multiple factors. One key factor is chronic inflammation, which plays a central role in this relationship. In AR, there is a persistent inflammatory response mediated by T-helper 2 (Th2) cells in the nasal mucosa. These Th2 cells release various pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and other mediators that contribute to the inflammatory process23. This localized inflammation, which refers to the body’s response to injury or infection in a specific area, has the potential to extend beyond its initial site and enter the systemic circulation. This means that the inflammatory molecules produced at the local level can spread throughout the body, leading to a phenomenon known as low-grade systemic inflammation15. Chronic systemic inflammation is a well-recognized risk factor for AD, partly due to its ability to disrupt the blood-brain barrier (BBB). A compromised BBB permits the entry of inflammatory mediators into the brain, thereby initiating neuroinflammatory cascades that con-tribute to neurodegeneration7.

Moreover, chronic inflammation can activate microglia and astrocytes, the brain’s resident immune cells. These cells become overactive in response to the inflammatory signals, leading to sustained inflammation within the brain tissue. This persistent neuroinflammation contributes to neuronal damage, impairing the function of brain cells and accelerating the progression of AD. Furthermore, chronic systemic inflammation is implicated in the development of the pathological hallmarks of AD, such as beta-amyloid plaques and tau tangles. These abnormal protein aggregates are known to accumulate in the brains of patients with AD, disrupting neuronal communication and causing cognitive decline11.

Sarlus et al. demonstrated in an animal model that peripheral allergic inflammation could influence the brain’s inflammatory status and enhance tau phosphorylation, a key feature of pathology of AD15. Furthermore, mast cells, pivotal in allergic reactions, are increasingly implicated in neuroinflammation and neurodegenerative diseases24,25. Upon activation, mast cells release many mediators, including histamine, proteases, cytokines, and chemokines, which can increase BBB permeability and directly modulate neural cell function, potentially contributing to the neuroinflammatory cascade seen in AD26,27.

The olfactory pathway also presents a plausible route linking AR to AD. Chronic inflammation in the nasal passages, characteristic of AR, could lead to olfactory dysfunction28. Olfactory dysfunction is a recognized early symptom of AD, and the olfactory bulb is one of the first brain regions affected by pathology of AD29,30. It has been hypothesized that pathogens or inflammatory mediators could gain access to the brain via the olfactory nerve, or that chronic inflammation in the olfactory system could trigger or exacerbate neuro-degenerative processes in connected brain regions31,32. Although the extent to which this contributes to neurodegenerative processes remains unclear, this hypothesis warrants further investigation. We emphasize that the olfactory pathway represents a supplementary, not exclusive, mechanism and acknowledge that its role should be interpreted with caution until more definitive mechanistic studies are available.

To enhance clarity, it is important to distinguish between evidence drawn from human clinical studies and that derived from preclinical animal models. Our primary findings, based on a large, nationally representative cohort in Taiwan, demonstrate a robust association between AR and elevated odds of AD, independent of common comorbidities. This observation aligns with recent human studies. For example, a nationwide longitudinal cohort study in Korea found that individuals with AR had a significantly higher risk of developing all-cause dementia and AD20. Moreover, recent Mendelian randomization analyses provide genetic evidence suggesting a potential causal link between allergic diseases and AD risk16. These findings suggest that allergic inflammation may precede and contribute to the development of neurodegenerative conditions.

In contrast, mechanistic insights into the biological plausibility of this association are primarily drawn from animal and in vitro studies. For instance, Sarlus et al. demonstrated in a murine model that peripheral allergic inflammation could enhance tau phosphorylation in the brain, a key pathological hallmark of AD15. Additional preclinical research has shown that mast cell activation can increase blood-brain barrier permeability and promote neuroinflammation via the release of cytokines and histamine24–26. While these findings offer a compelling mechanistic framework, it is critical to interpret them as complementary to, rather than interchangeable with, human clinical data.

Furthermore, biomarker-based investigations in human cohorts have begun to bridge this translational gap. Studies have linked elevated systemic inflammatory markers—such as C-reactive protein and interleukin-6—with increased risk for cognitive decline and AD. Although not specific to AR, these findings underscore the potential role of chronic peripheral inflammation in modulating neurodegenerative processes. Future prospective studies incorporating AR-specific biomarkers, including eosinophil counts, serum IgE, or nasal cytokine profiles, may further clarify the mechanistic pathways connecting allergic inflammation to cognitive decline.

Distinguishing between these sources of evidence not only strengthens the credibility of our conclusions but also highlights critical directions for future interdisciplinary research. Regarding the biological mechanism, it is important to distinguish between local nasal inflammation and systemic Th2-mediated responses. Although AR is characterized by local symptoms such as rhinorrhea and nasal congestion, the allergic cascade involves the systemic circulation of Th2-derived pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-4 and IL-13. These circulating mediators can increase blood-brain barrier permeability and foster a neuroinflammatory environment conducive to amyloid-beta accumulation. Furthermore, to strengthen the specificity of the AR-AD association, we assessed potential co-exposures to other atopic diseases. In a stratified analysis excluding individuals with comorbid asthma or atopic dermatitis, the association between AR and AD remained robust. This suggests that the chronic allergic inflammation inherent to AR serves as an independent contributor to AD risk, rather than being a secondary effect of a broader atopic profile.

In order to interpret the association between AD and AR pathologies, it is essential to distinguish between two potential pathophysiological pathways: the sensory deprivation hypothesis and the chronic systemic inflammation hypothesis. The sensory deprivation hypothesis is a theoretical model postulating that the cessation of sensory inputs, including but not limited to hearing, vision, and olfaction, results in diminished neural stimulation and expedited cognitive deterioration. Within the domain of AR, it is well-documented that chronic nasal inflammation frequently gives rise to olfactory dysfunction, a phenomenon that has been acknowledged as an early clinical indicator of AD. Moreover, the extant literature suggests a synergistic relationship between multi-sensory impairment, including vision loss, and an increased risk of developing dementia. Nevertheless, the present findings provide stronger support for the chronic systemic inflammation hypothesis. While sensory deprivation is a significant contributing factor, our analysis rigorously adjusted for hearing loss, a robust proxy for sensory-related cognitive impact. With regard to vision loss, although it is a recognized confounder, its recorded prevalence in administrative databases such as the LHID2010 is often notably low due to under-reporting of non-surgical visual impairments in the elderly. The finding that a significant association between AR and AD remains after adjustment for major comorbidities and hearing loss suggests that systemic inflammation continues to be an independent driver. Specifically, the Th2-mediated release of pro-inflammatory cytokines into the systemic circulation has the potential to compromise the blood-brain barrier and trigger neuroinflammatory cascades. This process is distinct from the neural input reduction that is observed in sensory loss. It is recommended that future research utilize granular clinical data on sensory acuity and inflammatory biomarkers in order to further delineate the interplay between these two pathways.

Furthermore, our study specifically focused on AR rather than non-allergic or vasomotor rhinitis to isolate the impact of IgE-mediated, Th2-driven chronic inflammation on neurodegeneration. While both conditions can cause nasal obstruction and potential olfactory-related sensory deprivation, the robust association found specifically for AR underscores the importance of the systemic inflammatory load in the pathogenesis of AD.

In terms of the causal pathway, olfactory dysfunction (anosmia or hyposmia) may serve as a critical mediator between AR and AD. Chronic nasal inflammation in AR patients can lead to structural and functional damage to the olfactory neuroepithelium. Given that olfactory loss is a well-established prodromal marker of Alzheimer’s pathology, our findings suggest that AR might facilitate neurodegeneration through this direct sensory-neural route. In our statistical model, olfactory dysfunction was treated as a mediator on the causal path rather than a confounder; therefore, it was not adjusted for to avoid over-adjustment bias.

Emerging evidence also suggests that dietary patterns, particularly their inflammatory potential, may influence the development of both allergic and neurodegenerative diseases. The Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII), a validated measure of diet-associated inflammation, has been linked to an increased risk of allergic rhinitis and other atopic conditions36. Similarly, pro-inflammatory diets have been associated with a higher risk of Alzheimer’s disease, potentially through chronic systemic inflammation and immune dysregulation37. Studies from Asian populations further indicate that inflammatory dietary patterns are associated with atopic dermatitis, an early manifestation of the atopic march characterized by heightened allergic sensitization38. Given that allergic rhinitis shares common immunological pathways with other atopic diseases, these findings support the hypothesis that systemic inflammation may represent a shared mechanistic link between allergic conditions and Alzheimer’s disease.

It is important to note that the observational nature of the administrative dataset precludes any definitive conclusions regarding causality or biological mechanisms. While there is a hypothesis that chronic systemic inflammation or olfactory dysfunction might serve as potential pathways linking AR to AD, these remain purely speculative hypotheses. The present data set does not lend support to these mechanistic interpretations. The observed statistical association emphasizes the necessity for subsequent translational and prospective clinical studies, which should be equipped with neuroimaging and inflammatory biomarkers, in order to rigorously test these hypotheses.

Our study has several strengths. Using a large, nationally representative database (LHID2010) enhances our findings’ statistical power and generalizability to the Taiwan-ese elderly population. The case-control design with propensity-score matching for several demographic and comorbidity variables helps to mitigate potential confounding. Furthermore, we applied stringent diagnostic criteria, requiring at least two diagnoses of AD for cases and at least two diagnoses of AR, with one con-firmed by an otolaryngologist, which likely increased diagnostic accuracy. To address the potential confounding effects of coexisting atopic conditions, we conducted a stratified analysis restricted to patients with AR who had no comorbid diagnoses of asthma, atopic dermatitis, or eczema. This AR-only subgroup analysis revealed that the association between AR and AD remained statistically significant, indicating that the observed relationship was not solely driven by the presence of other allergic disorders (data not shown). These findings suggest that AR alone, independent of other common atopic conditions, may contribute to systemic inflammation that influences neurodegenerative processes. By isolating the effect of AR, this analysis enhances the specificity of our results and further supports the hypothesis that chronic allergic inflammation could be an independent risk factor for AD.

However, it is important to acknowledge several limitations in our study. Firstly, like all observational studies that rely on administrative databases, the LHID2010 does not contain information on lifestyle factors such as smoking, BMI, physical activity and alcohol consumption, genetic markers like ApoE genotype, dietary patterns, or specific educational level, cognitive reserve, and healthcare-seeking behavior, which may result in residual confounding. With regard to the direction of bias, it is conceivable that individuals with higher socioeconomic status or greater healthcare access are more likely to be diagnosed with both conditions, which could result in an overestimation of the observed association. In an attempt to control for this, a matching process was performed based on urbanization level, and multiple comorbidities were adjusted for, which may serve as indirect proxies for healthcare utilization. However, the presence of unmeasured confounding remains a possibility. Additionally, a healthcare utilization bias may exist, where patients who frequently visit specialists for AR have a higher chance of their cognitive decline being detected. While we adjusted for multiple chronic comorbidities and used propensity score matching to balance healthcare-seeking proxies, some residual selection bias may remain. Furthermore, high-fat intake has been shown to influence allergic sensitization and systemic inflammatory status33. We also lacked granular details on AR severity, comorbid nasal polyposis, and specific pharmacological treatments, such as intranasal corticosteroids or monoclonal antibodies, which could modulate the systemic inflammatory response. Future research incorporating treatment history and clinical biomarkers is needed to clarify whether effective anti-inflammatory management of AR can mitigate the risk of AD.

Secondly, while our sensitivity analysis restricted to patients without comorbid asthma or atopic dermatitis confirmed the independent association of AR with AD, we did not construct separate statistical models to test the specific associations of asthma or atopic dermatitis with AD. The AR-only analysis was designed exclusively to isolate the effect of AR without comorbid atopy. Consequently, this study does not test whether other atopic conditions have similar, weaker, or stronger associations with cognitive decline. The relative contributions of distinct atopic phenotypes to neurodegeneration remain to be thoroughly explored in future studies using multi-exposure models.

Thirdly, it is worth noting that although our study found a link between AR and the development of AD, the gradual onset of AD suggests that some diagnoses of AR may have occurred during the preclinical stages of the disease. Furthermore, a primary limitation of the case-control methodology is its retrospective nature, which can demonstrate an association but cannot definitively establish causality. Nevertheless, a plausible temporal sequence between AR and AD is suggested by the stark disparity in their respective ages of onset. Previous studies indicate that the mean onset age for AR was 24.3 years, in contrast to 77.1 years for AD34. This observation aligns with the established understanding that AR typically manifests in younger populations, whereas AD is predominantly a condition of late adulthood. This substantial interval suggests that the chronic inflammatory burden from AR may exert its influence over several decades, rather than being a phenomenon limited to a specific late-life age bracket. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis incorporating a 3-year lag period has been conducted, with all AR diagnoses occurring within the three years prior to the AD index date excluded. The results of this analysis remained consistent with our primary findings, suggesting that the association is not primarily driven by reverse causation during the prodromal phase of AD.

Fourthly, the identification of AD was based on ICD codes from administrative claims without direct validation through neuroimaging, cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers, or standardized neuropsychological testing. Despite the necessity for a minimum of two diagnoses to enhance validity, the risk of misclassification between AD and other forms of dementia, such as vascular or mixed dementia, cannot be fully eliminated. However, by excluding patients with a history of stroke, the inclusion of vascular-related cognitive impairment was minimized. Moreover, earlier validation studies have shown that clinical diagnoses in specific disease conditions in the national database demonstrate acceptable accuracy35. Consequently, our findings should therefore be interpreted as an association between AR and the clinical syndrome of AD, acknowledging the possibility of residual diagnostic overlap with other neurodegenerative conditions. In addition, the definition of AR required a diagnosis by an otolaryngology specialist. While this rigorous criterion ensures high diagnostic specificity, it may introduce a selection bias toward patients with more severe symptoms or higher healthcare engagement. Consequently, individuals with milder AR who managed their symptoms with over-the-counter medications or primary care visits may have been misclassified into the control group. This potential misclassification of exposure generally leads to an underestimation of the odds ratios (bias towards the null). Therefore, our findings may represent a conservative estimate of the association, primarily reflecting the impact of more clinically significant or persistent allergic inflammation on AD risk.

Lastly, while we used propensity score matching to balance covariates, our primary analysis utilized standard logistic regression rather than conditional models. Although this approach is widely accepted in large-scale epidemiological studies using administrative data, it might theoretically lead to an underestimation of standard errors. However, given the robust nature of our findings and the large sample size, this is unlikely to affect the overall conclusions of the study.

In conclusion, this study provides further evidence of an association between AR and AD in an elderly Taiwanese cohort. The findings accentuate the potential role of chronic peripheral inflammation in neurodegeneration. Future prospective studies are imperative to confirm this association, delineate the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms with greater clarity, and investigate whether effective management of AR is associated with a reduction in the odds of AD. Additionally, further research should prioritize collecting more granular data on characteristics of AR, AD biomarkers, and genetic factors to enhance our understanding of this intricate interplay.
