Herpes simplex virus encephalitis following acute brain injury: illustrative cases
Authors: Allison K. Shay, Nicholas M. Cordero, Nam Dinh Phuong Nguyen, Jueria Rahman, Jack Zhang, Zachary L. Hickman, Gregory Kapinos, Laura Salgado-Lopez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDHerpes simplex virus (HSV) is the most common cause of sporadic viral encephalitis in adults, yet HSV encephalitis (HSVE) occurrence in the setting of traumatic brain injury (TBI) and intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) is rarely documented. Brain injury induces systemic immunosuppression that may predispose to viral reactivation. Neurological deterioration in these patients is often attributed to the primary injury, which may delay recognition of superimposed viral encephalitis.OBSERVATIONSThe authors present 3 cases of HSVE following neurological injury: a 68-year-old woman with a spontaneous thalamic ICH, a 36-year-old man with TBI requiring craniotomy, and an 80-year-old man with nonaneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage. Acyclovir treatment was initiated for all based on clinical suspicion and pending BioFire FilmArray Meningitis/Encephalitis Panel (MEP) results, but 2 patients died due to irreversible neurological damage and significant comorbidities.LESSONSHSVE should be considered in patients with unexplained or disproportionate neurological deterioration after acute brain injury, even when the primary injury provides an apparent explanation for encephalopathy. New temporal, insular, or orbitofrontal abnormalities on MRI; refractory seizures; persistent fever; or clinical decline despite stable structural imaging should prompt early MEP testing and empiric acyclovir therapy while results are pending. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE26525

Herpes simplex virus (HSV) is the most common cause of sporadic viral encephalitis in immunocompetent adults, with an incidence of 2–4 cases per 1 million persons annually.1,2 While HSV-1 classically causes orolabial disease and HSV-2 predominantly causes genital infection, both viruses establish lifelong latency in sensory ganglia with the potential for reactivation throughout the host’s lifetime.3 Despite the availability of acyclovir, HSV encephalitis (HSVE) remains associated with significant morbidity and mortality, with case fatality rates of 15%–28% and neurological sequelae in up to 50% of survivors.4,5 Poor outcomes are strongly associated with delaying acyclovir treatment more than 2 days from hospital admission, and each additional day of delay increases the 6-month mortality risk.6,7

HSVE following neurosurgical intervention is rarely documented and is most commonly reported after epilepsy surgery, tumor resection, spinal surgery, and craniocervical decompression.8,9 Proposed mechanisms include direct manipulation of neural structures harboring latent virus and perioperative immunosuppression from corticosteroid administration. Reactivation in the ganglia may then lead to axonal spread along the trigeminal or olfactory pathways or hematogenous dissemination. Similarly, brain injury induces systemic immunosuppression, predisposing the patient to HSV reactivation. Traumatic brain injury (TBI) activates the sympathetic nervous system, resulting in a catecholamine surge that upregulates PD-1 expression on T lymphocytes and impairs CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell function.10,11 This immunosuppression is characterized by lymphopenia, reduced interferon-γ production, and aberrant monocyte polarization—alterations that compromise antiviral immunity.12,13 In a prospective cohort study of 344 patients with severe brain injury, HSV reactivation, detected in tracheal aspirates, occurred in 39% of HSV-seropositive patients and was independently associated with poor neurological recovery at 6 months.14 However, prior studies have primarily characterized respiratory HSV shedding in critically ill patients rather than CSF-confirmed HSVE, a qualitatively distinct and more severe clinical entity. Whether brain injury– and critical illness–associated immunosuppression is associated with clinically apparent HSVE remains poorly characterized.

Illustrative Cases

We present a case series of 3 adults (2 men, 1 woman) who developed HSVE following spontaneous thalamic intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), traumatic subdural hematoma, and nonaneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), highlighting the diagnostic challenges and the critical importance of early recognition and treatment in this patient population. In all 3 cases, empiric acyclovir therapy was initiated when clinical or radiographic findings raised concern for concurrent HSVE. CSF testing was performed promptly using the BioFire FilmArray Meningitis/Encephalitis Panel (MEP; bioMérieux), a multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assay. Case 1: Thalamic Intraparenchymal Hemorrhage With Intraventricular Hemorrhage

A 68-year-old woman with a medical history of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus, and obstructive sleep apnea was transferred from an outside hospital to our institution for management of a spontaneous basal ganglia intraparenchymal hemorrhage with intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH). According to the patient’s son and the transferring medical team, the patient was in her usual state of health until the evening prior to admission, when she became lethargic and began vomiting. Her initial Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was 10 (E4V2M4) and her ICH score was 2. Noncontrast CT (NCCT) imaging of the head performed at the outside facility revealed an acute left thalamic hematoma with extension into the ventricular system (Fig. 1). The patient was started on levetiracetam for seizure prophylaxis and ceftriaxone for a suspected urinary tract infection. On arrival at our institution, the patient was intubated and an IRRAflow external ventricular drain (EVD) was placed in the emergency department with initiation of CSF exchange with lactated Ringer’s solution. She was admitted to the intensive care unit for close neurological monitoring and intracranial pressure (ICP) management. Intrathecal tissue plasminogen activator was administered over a 3-day course, with serial CT imaging demonstrating progressive clearance of the IVH. Continuous EEG monitoring revealed left frontotemporal seizures, and the patient was started on levetiracetam and phenobarbital with subsequent seizure control. Neurological examinations initially remained stable with preserved brainstem reflexes. An EVD clamp trial was attempted on HD 7, and reattempted on HD 9, but both attempts were aborted due to elevated ICP. Serial NCCT of the head remained stable. On HD 10, the patient acutely deteriorated, becoming less responsive with bilateral nonreactive pupils, requiring emergency hyperosmolar therapy with 23.4% hypertonic saline. Repeat NCCT of the head revealed new extensive infarcts. An attempted MRI study was aborted due to hemodynamic instability.

Over the subsequent days, the patient exhibited progressive neurological decline. MRI of the brain without contrast obtained on HD 13 demonstrated diffuse cerebral edema with T2/FLAIR hyperintensities involving the temporal lobes, insular cortex, and frontal regions, raising a strong suspicion for HSVE. Based on these imaging findings, treatment with empiric intravenous acyclovir was initiated prior to confirmatory testing at 10 mg/kg using ideal body weight, every 12 hours with renal dose adjustment and concurrent intravenous hydration, and continued until HD 22. CSF obtained via the EVD demonstrated lymphocytic pleocytosis (24 cells/µL, 94% lymphocytes), elevated protein (111 mg/dL), and normal glucose. The MEP returned positive for HSV-1, confirming the diagnosis. Despite appropriate antiviral therapy and ongoing ICP-directed management, the patient developed refractory elevated intracranial hypertension. Her clinical course was further complicated by endocrine dysfunction consistent with central diabetes insipidus, requiring vasopressin and hormonal replacement.

The patient’s neurological examination continued to decline, ultimately progressing to coma with loss of brainstem reflexes. EEG demonstrated severely suppressed activity, consistent with a poor prognosis. On HD 22, the patient was declared brain dead.

Case 2: Traumatic Subdural Hematoma

A 36-year-old man with no significant past medical history presented with progressive headache and aphasia following a fall with a head strike while intoxicated 2 days prior to admission. He was neurologically impaired with a GCS score of 13 (E4/V4/M5), progressive global aphasia, and hypophonia, but without focal motor deficits. NCCT of the head demonstrated a left holohemispheric acute subdural hematoma measuring 1 cm in maximal thickness with significant mass effect and 8 mm of left-to-right midline shift (Fig. 2A). He underwent emergency left-sided trauma craniotomy for hematoma evacuation without intraoperative complications. Perioperative dexamethasone was administered. On HD 8, however, routine surveillance CT revealed reaccumulation of extra-axial fluid and blood products with worsening midline shift and impending uncal herniation, prompting urgent return to the operating room for removal of the craniotomy bone flap and evacuation of the collection (Fig. 2C). The patient’s postoperative course was subsequently complicated by worsening leukocytosis exceeding 20 × 103/µL, new-onset high-grade fevers, and hypotension requiring vasopressor support beginning early on HD 13. Despite broad-spectrum empiric antibiotics and a negative infectious workup, including blood and urine cultures, the patient remained febrile. Repeat neuroimaging on HD 15 demonstrated a left frontal hygroma and a new left insular hypodensity remote from areas of traumatic contusion (Fig. 2D). Lumbar puncture performed on HD 16 revealed a lymphocytic pleocytosis with normal glucose and mildly elevated protein. Acyclovir treatment was empirically started at 10 mg/kg using ideal body weight, every 8 hours with concurrent intravenous hydration for 14 days. The same-day MEP was positive for HSV-1, consistent with HSVE. The patient had subsequent clinical improvement, including gradual resolution of aphasia and recovery of consciousness. Electroencephalography demonstrated diffuse cerebral dysfunction without epileptiform activity. Serum HSV-1 immunoglobulin G (IgG) positivity supported viral reactivation rather than a new primary infection as the etiology of the patient’s HSVE. The patient completed a 14-day course of antiviral therapy with continued neurological improvement and was ultimately discharged home on HD 60. At follow-up, he demonstrated near-complete language recovery and functional independence, undergoing elective cranioplasty 5 months later without evidence of recurrent infection or HSV reactivation.

Case 3: Acute Cerebral Hypoxia With SAH

An 80-year-old man with a past medical history of diabetes mellitus type 2 was found unresponsive in his apartment with foaming at the mouth and was brought to the emergency department with severe hypoxia, tachypnea, and altered mental status. He was intubated and admitted to the medical intensive care unit. His initial presentation was dominated by acute hypoxemic respiratory failure, septic shock secondary to aspiration pneumonia with gram-positive cocci bacteremia, and a left sylvian fissure SAH with mesial temporal lobe edema (Fig. 3A). CT angiography of the head was negative for intracranial aneurysm or other vascular lesions. Leukocytosis ranged from approximately 11.88 to 15.41 × 103/µL during the first several hospital days (HDs) and improved within 48 hours of antibiotic therapy. He received stress-dose hydrocortisone 50 mg every 6 hours for 7 days. On HD 1, the patient developed convulsive activity and was subsequently found on continuous electroencephalography to be in super-refractory status epilepticus (SRSE). FIG. 3.Case 3. Lumbar puncture was performed on HD 3 with an elevated opening pressure of 27 cm H2O, glucose 120 mg/dL, protein 71 mg/dL, white blood cell count (WBC) 59, lymphocytes 90%, red blood cell count (RBC) 1050, and MEP positive for HSV-1. Acyclovir treatment was initiated empirically pending the PCR results, at 10 mg/kg using ideal body weight, every 8 hours with renal dose adjustment and concurrent intravenous hydration, and was continued until HD 22. Repeat MRI on HD 7 demonstrated persistent edema involving the mesial left temporal lobe, insular cortex, and anterior temporal region with minimal interval extension (Fig. 3B). These results, along with the positive HSV-1 PCR results, confirmed the diagnosis of HSVE, which contributed to the patient’s altered mental status and refractory seizure activity. Despite treatment with acyclovir and multiple antiseizure medications, including midazolam infusion, phenobarbital, levetiracetam, and phenytoin, the patient showed minimal neurological recovery, with only eye opening to pain and no command following. EEG demonstrated diffuse slowing with periodic discharges but no ongoing seizures. Given the patient’s poor neurological prognosis, goals-of-care discussions were held with the family on HDs 18 and 19, and care was transitioned to comfort measures only. The patient was compassionately extubated on HD 21 and subsequently died on HD 22.

Discussion

Observations

Across cases, patients were managed with active neurocritical care strategies including continuous EEG monitoring, escalation of antiseizure therapy, tiered ICP management including CSF diversion and hyperosmolar therapy, and serial imaging, highlighting that HSVE developed despite aggressive intensive care unit management. The presence of seizures, as observed in cases 1 and 3, highlights a potential diagnostic confounder, as seizure-related encephalopathy may obscure recognition of superimposed infectious processes. In all 3 cases, the initial presentation provided an alternative explanation for the patients’ encephalopathy, potentially masking the presence of a superimposed viral encephalitis.

In case 1, the early neurological decline was initially attributed to thalamic hemorrhage with intraventricular extension; however, stable serial neuroimaging and a clinical examination disproportionate to the primary injury accompanied by electrographic seizures and refractory intracranial hypertension prompted further investigation. In case 2, unexplained fevers and neurological deterioration following craniotomy prompted consideration of postoperative bacterial infection before viral encephalitis was suspected. This diagnostic uncertainty is consistent with prior literature demonstrating that confounding factors such as severe underlying disease contribute to delays in acyclovir administration.4 The classic clinical features of HSVE—fever, headache, altered mental status, and focal neurological deficits—overlap substantially with findings expected in patients with TBI or ICH.4,15 Furthermore, the absence of fever, as observed in case 1, does not exclude HSVE; up to 20% of patients with HSVE are afebrile at presentation.4 Neurological deterioration disproportionate to or temporally inconsistent with the initial brain injury became a critical finding in case 2 as well, wherein a new insular hypodensity remote from the area of traumatic contusion suggested a separate pathological process.

Neuroimaging Findings

MRI was instrumental in the diagnosis and treatment of HSVE in all 3 cases. The characteristic pattern of T2/FLAIR hyperintensity involving the temporal lobes, insular cortex, and orbitofrontal regions, especially if asymmetric, is suggestive of HSVE and should prompt immediate CSF analysis and empiric treatment with acyclovir.15,16 In a study of 251 cases of temporal lobe encephalitis, HSV was the most commonly identified infectious organism, and lesions confined to the temporal lobe, insula, and cingulate cortex were associated with higher odds of HSV compared to other causes.16 In this series, MRI findings preceded CSF confirmation in multiple cases and directly prompted early initiation of acyclovir treatment, underscoring the importance of imaging-driven clinical decision-making in suspected HSVE.

Impact of Diagnosis Confirmation and Treatment Timing on Outcomes

CSF analysis in our cases demonstrated the expected findings of viral encephalitis: lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, and normal glucose. The MEP enabled rapid confirmation of HSV in all cases, facilitating timely diagnosis once the clinical suspicion was raised. Patient 2, in whom acyclovir treatment was initiated on HD 16, achieved near-complete neurological recovery with functional independence. In contrast, case 1 progressed to brain death despite initiation of antiviral therapy once HSVE was suspected radiographically on HD 13. In case 3, despite early acyclovir initiation on HD 3, the patient died on HD 22—likely reflecting the cumulative burden of SRSE, septic shock, and advanced age.

These observations align with the established literature demonstrating that delayed acyclovir therapy is independently associated with poor outcomes in HSVE.7 A multicenter study found that a delay of greater than 2 days between hospital admission and initiation of acyclovir treatment was independently associated with poor outcome at 6 months.7 A recent population-based cohort study confirmed that each additional day of delay in acyclovir administration after admission increases the 6-month mortality risk (adjusted relative risk 1.06 per day), and a delay of greater than 6 hours after lumbar puncture was associated with an 85% increased risk of death.6 In a retrospective study of cases from 1995 to 2013, coma, restricted diffusion on MRI, acyclovir delay beyond the 1st HD, and older age were the primary predictors of poor outcome.16

Brain Injury–Induced Immunosuppression

TBI and ICH induce systemic immunosuppression through activation of the sympathetic nervous system and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis. Our cases extend these observations by demonstrating that HSV reactivation in the setting of brain injury can manifest as clinically significant encephalitis, not merely asymptomatic viremia. The presence of HSV-1 IgG positivity in case 2 supports viral reactivation rather than a primary infection, consistent with the understanding that most adult sporadic HSVE cases represent reactivation of latent virus. The stress of acute brain injury, surgical intervention, and critical illness may have provided the immunological trigger for reactivation in our patients.

Approximately 55 cases of HSVE following elective neurosurgical procedures have been reported, including epilepsy surgery, tumor resection, and spinal surgery.8 Proposed mechanisms include direct manipulation of neural structures harboring latent virus and perioperative corticosteroid administration. Our cases differ in that the inciting events were acute traumatic or spontaneous brain injuries, rather than elective procedures. This suggests that the immunosuppressive effects of the brain injury itself, independent of surgical manipulation or exogenous steroids, may be sufficient to trigger HSV reactivation.

Lessons

These cases illustrate the complex and multifactorial nature of neurocritical care in patients with acute brain injury of varying etiologies and the potential for HSV reactivation in this population. They also emphasize the need for a high clinical suspicion for viral encephalitis in patients with unexplained neurological decline, especially in the context of brain injury. Early antiviral therapy, such as acyclovir, and appropriate hormonal management are essential to optimizing outcomes, although in some cases, such as cases 1 and 3, the resulting damage may be irreversible. Case 3 should be interpreted cautiously since the patient’s septic shock, stress-dose corticosteroid exposure, aspiration pneumonia, and suspected hypoxic injury preceded HSVE confirmation and may have contributed to immunosuppression concurrent with the patient’s brain injury. In this context, reliance solely on the expected trajectory of the primary neurological injury may delay recognition of secondary processes such as HSVE, reinforcing the need for early consideration of viral encephalitis in brain-injured patients with an atypical or disproportionate neurological decline.

This report is limited by its small sample size, retrospective design, and absence of a comparison group, which preclude estimates of incidence or causality. Because all patients were critically ill with complex neurological injuries and systemic complications, the relative contribution of HSVE versus the primary brain injury to clinical outcomes cannot be fully determined.

HSVE after acute brain injury may be underrecognized because its clinical features overlap with common neurocritical care complications, including seizures, fever, altered mental status, and radiographic edema. Early PCR testing and empiric acyclovir therapy should be pursued when clinical or radiographic findings suggest HSVE, as delayed recognition may contribute to irreversible neurological injury despite aggressive supportive care.
