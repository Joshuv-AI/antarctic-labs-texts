Shallow periodic patterns on silicon nanostructures for engineered thermal conductivity reduction
Authors: Francesca Lucchesi, Carlotta Ragazzo Capello, Antonella Masci, Matteo Del Vecchio, Elisabetta Dimaggio, Giovanni Pennelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSilicon nanostructures have been intensively studied to prove the correlation between thermal conductivity reduction and surface roughness, with the aim to develop sustainable and high efficient thermoelectric devices. In this work, we shed light on the mechanism responsible for the pronounced reduction of the thermal conductivity. We report on the thermal characterization of suspended silicon nanostructures whose surfaces were modified with a shallow periodic grating etched on their top surfaces. A clear correlation between the grating periodicity and the resulting thermal conductivity is observed. Although the etched pattern is only 20 nm deep compared to the total nanostructure thickness of 190 nm, and thus leaving the overall cross-section almost unchanged, a remarkable reduction in thermal conductivity is measured, reaching values up to five times lower than that of unetched nanowires. This reduction lies well below the limit expected from purely diffusive phonon transport: the shallow pattern only marginally affects the boundary mean free path through surface scattering, even when accounting for fully thermalized boundary scattering (Casimir limit) at the modified surface.Keywords: Silicon nanowires, Thermal conductivity, Phonon conduction, Phonon interferenceSubject terms: Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Physics

Introduction

In recent decades, significant research efforts have been devoted to the investigation of the properties of thermal transport at the nanoscale, primarily driven by phonons1–4. This growing interest extends beyond the fundamental understanding of phonon dynamics and scattering mechanisms, encompassing the potential for advanced green energy technologies, particularly in enhancing the efficiency of thermoelectric materials through direct thermal-to-electrical energy conversion5,6. Moreover, the control of phonon propagation has been proposed as an effective strategy for the management of the heat flow and to enable regulated thermal transport. Based on these studies, devices such as thermal diodes7–10, thermal transistors, and thermal switches11 are being developed, offering a potential alternative to traditional electronic components.

High efficient thermal-to-electrical energy conversion can be achieved using materials that exhibit high electrical conductivity combined with ultra-low thermal conductivity. Nanostructuring provides a powerful strategy to suppress phonon transport by enhancing phonon scattering at the boundaries, such as the walls of nanowires, while preserving good electrical conductivity. This is possible because charge carriers (electrons and holes) typically have much shorter mean free paths compared to phonons. Consequently, nanostructuring enables the use, for thermoelectric applications, of materials like silicon which is abundant in the Earth’s crust, technologically mature, and biocompatible, despite its high bulk thermal conductivity (148 W/(m K))12. Extensive experimental studies have been conducted on the thermal conductivity of silicon nanowires, revealing values significantly below the Casimir limit13,14. A further significant reduction in thermal conductivity has been observed in nanowires exhibiting substantial variations in cross-sectional width15–17. This behavior is attributed to enhanced phonon scattering occurring during propagation from narrower to wider sections. Several studies demonstrated a clear correlation between the reduction in thermal conductivity and the surface roughness of the nanostructures, both in nanowires18–22 and in thin silicon nanomembranes23. These findings highlight the crucial influence of surface morphology on phonon transport at the nanoscale. In many studies, surface roughness has been introduced via Metal-Assisted Chemical Etching (MACE)24,25. Although this method produces random and uncontrolled surface features, often affecting only a small fraction of the nanowire cross-section, it has been shown to significantly reduce thermal conductivity, even when the roughness is limited to just a few nanometers18,26 with a reduced impact on the cross-section of the nanostructure. For example, in a previous work, our group measured a very reduced thermal conductivity of 1.8 W/(m K)22 on forests of silicon nanowires with a monocrystalline core, but very rough surfaces.

The target of this work is to deepen the understanding of the mechanism responsible for this pronounced reduction of the thermal conductivity. We demonstrate that shallow periodic surface structuring, implemented via Focused Ion Beam (FIB) patterning with an etch depth of 20 nm on wires 190 nm thick, can significantly suppress phonon propagation. Unlike stochastic surface roughening, this deterministic approach provides a high level of spatial control, allowing phonon-scattering sites to be positioned with precision, so that a clear relationship between surface modification and phonon suppression can be established. Such capability marks an important step toward defining practical design principles for phonon-engineered nanostructures.

We fabricated wide (more than 650 nm) and thin suspended silicon nanostructures, using a top-down approach, and measured both their electrical and thermal conductivities. The smooth nanowires (without FIB carving) demonstrated thermal conductivity values consistent with those previously reported in similar structures27, as well as an electrical resistance in line with the expected doping concentration resulting from the implemented diffusion process. In contrast, nanowires carved with a shallow periodic pattern showed a significant thermal conductivity reduction. Depending on the pattern geometry, values as low as 20% of those measured in smooth nanowires can be achieved. The electrical resistance resulted comparable with that of smooth nanowires, after annealing to recover ion implantation damages.

Fabrication and measurements

Fabrication

Suspended silicon nanostructures (nanowires) (see the SEM images of Figure 1) are fabricated in the top silicon layer of a Silicon-On-Insulator (SOI) wafer 220 nm thick (buried oxide 1 m, silicon handler 700 m), p type doped with a resistivity of 10 cm. A 50 nm thick SiO layer is grown on the top silicon layer by thermal oxidation, and then trenches are defined by e-beam lithography through standard PMMA resist, development and buffered oxide etching (BHF). After resist removal in acetone, trenches are transferred to the silicon layer through wet anisotropic etching (KOH) using the top oxide layer as a mask. The design of the trenches allows the definition both of the nanowires (width and length) and of large silicon isles where metal contacts and pads for external connections will be fabricated at the end of the processing. As a low resistance is required for the electrical measurements (see below), the nanostructures are then doped by thermal diffusion at 900 C for 10’, followed by a drive-in step at 1150 C for 5’ (see the Methods section).

The final resistivity resulted of about 0.003 cm, which corresponds to a doping concentration of about cm. On the same chip, of about 1X1 cm, up to 30 nanodevices are simultaneously fabricated in the same process run, repeated for more than 20 fabrication runs. A periodic pattern of shallow trenches has been engraved by Focused Ion Beam sculpturing, on the top of few nanodevices. The beam dose/acceleration has been accurately calibrated by AFM imaging, as reported in Methods, to obtain trenches 20 nm deep. This depth is about one-tenth of the nanowire thickness that results of 190 nm (30 nm of silicon are consumed in the oxidation and drive-in processes): hence, they are shallow, involving only a small portion of the nanowire cross section. As the FIB exposure (at 30 kV of accelerating voltage) causes lattice damages that degrade the electrical properties28,29, a thermal annealing process at 900 C for 1 minute30 has been performed on some chip to compare the electrical and thermal conductivities with and without annealing-induced recovery of the crystal lattice. After the annealing step, contact/pads for external connections are defined by e-beam lithography, thermal evaporation of 50 nm of gold on 15 nm of chrome (for adhesion and diffusion barrier) and lift-off. The suspension of the nanostructures has been achieved by underetching the buried oxide in BHF. The etching time has been long enough to suspend the nanostructures, but to leave most of the oxide under the contact areas that remained anchored to the substrate. Panel (d) of Fig. 3 shows a SEM image of a device that is 5.2 m long and features a carved periodic pattern of 400 nm pitch and same depth of 20 nm. The thermal conductivity is 44.7 ± 1.8 W/(m K), which is comparable to that of smooth (unpatterned) nanowires. This suggests that, at this scale, the periodic pattern no longer significantly affects thermal transport. This observation further supports the hypothesis that the shallow periodic pattern effectively influences phonon propagation only when the feature spacing is of the order of, or below, the phonon mean free path, which is approximately 200 nm at room temperature. Consequently, scattering centers spaced much farther apart than 200 nm are expected to have a negligible effect on phonon transport.

The experimental results demonstrate that a strong reduction of the thermal conductivity in silicon nanostructures can be achieved carving a shallow (20 nm depth on a thickness of 190 nm) periodic pattern on their surface. A comparable result has been previously achieved by Maire and co34. but on polycristalline silicon thin film, where scattering of the grain borders gives an important contribution to the thermal conductivity. In our case, we used monocristalline silicon nanostructures and a very shallow etching. The fabricated nanostructures (nanowires) have the same thickness (190 nm) and width (650 nm), and different pitches. Non-carved nanowires exhibited a measured thermal conductivity of about 45 W/(m K). This value is consistent with the expected reduction in phonon transport, compared to bulk silicon, caused by surface scattering at the nanostructure walls. In fact, we applied a purely diffusive model based on thermalized boundary scattering and on bulk silicon phonon dispersion (acoustic phonon spectrum), which gives the Casimir limit in these nanostructures. This model gives a thermal conductivity of 43 W/(m K) for unpatterned nanostructures, which is comparable to the measured value. Please refer to the Methods section for a short description of the model and of the used formulas. The same diffusive model (see the Methods section) applied to the patterned nanostructures of 100 nm, gives a thermal conductivity of 42 W/(m K), which is only slightly lower than that of the not-carved nanostructures. Instead, the experimental value results of 8.3 W/(m K), which is significantly reduced with respect to non-engraved nanostructures. A possible interpretation of this experimental behavior could be based on the phonon interference effect35,36. At room temperature, phonons with wavelength in the range 1–100 nm give the 80% of contribution to the silicon thermal conductivity37,38. Therefore, it is reasonable to expect that a grating with a period of 100 nm produces interference on most of the phonons responsible to the heat conduction; interference phenomena are less important for larger pitch, for which ordinary scattering events (phonon-phonon, umklapp, impurity scattering) prevail, and are completely lost for the 400 nm pitch. However, other phenomena can determine a strong reduction of the thermal conductivity. It has been demonstrated38 that phonon dispersion, that we considered equal to that of bulk silicon in our simple model, is modified in thin nanomembranes. Moreover, the phonon dispersion is affected by the configuration and roughness of the surface, in particular when the features on the surface are comparable with the mean free path of phonons. This modification of phonon dispersion, and in particular of the acoustic phonon spectrum, can give a phonon group velocity, density of state and phonon relaxation rates different from that of bulk silicon39, bringing to phonon confinement. Both phonon interference and modification of the phonon dispersion are lost when the period is increased to 400 nm, much larger than the phonon mean free path, for which the ordinary scattering events prevail.

Conclusions

We investigated the thermal conductivity of silicon nanowires whose surfaces were modified with a shallow periodic pattern via FIB etching. The etching, only 20 nm deep on a total thickness of 190 nm, does not significantly change the nanowire cross-section. Nevertheless, the periodic pattern dramatically reduces the thermal conductivity by a factor of about five (well below the Casimir limit), when the pattern periodicity is 100 nm.

Such a pronounced reduction cannot be explained by a simple diffusive phonon transport model, based on the bulk silicon phonon dispersion and scattering parameters. This effect diminishes for periods of 200 nm and practically disappears for periods larger than 400 nm.

The main outcomes of the article can be resumed as:

shallow etches on surface of silicon nanostructures bring to a strong reduction of the thermal conductivity;

shallow etches on surface of silicon nanostructures bring to a strong reduction of the thermal conductivity;

the reduction strongly depends on the geometry (on the periodicity) of the etching, and it is stronger for a periodicity of 100 nm;

the reduction strongly depends on the geometry (on the periodicity) of the etching, and it is stronger for a periodicity of 100 nm;

a simple diffusive model for phonon, that considers the phonon dispersion of bulk silicon (sound speed), well explains the thermal conductivity of smooth nanowires, but fails to explain the reduction of the thermal conductivity with shallow engraving.

Further theoretical work, considering phonon interference, and/or the effect that the periodic shallow etch has on the phonon dispersion, in needed to explain the experimental results.

However, these results indicate that a precise, controlled pattern can be designed for the reduction of the thermal conductivity of nanowires, hence it is a powerful test bench for the investigation of phonon transport at the nanoscale.

Methods

Fabrication

The starting substrate is a Silicon On Insulator wafer with a top silicon layer 220 nm thick, buried oxide layer 1 m on a silicon handler 700 m thick. Initially, the top silicon layer is doped p type with a resistivity of 10 cm. A silicon dioxide layer 50 nm thick is grown by thermal oxidation at 1150 °C for 10’ in a Rapid Thermal Annealer/Oxidation machine (RTA JPELEC100). Standard PMMA resist is then spun on top (350 K molecular weight, 3% in anisole) and e-beam lithography is used to define trenches that delimit the nanostructures, the leads for electrical measurements and large area pads for external connections. E-beam lithography has been performed with a SEM JEOL 6500 F equipped with a home-made patter generator. BHF etching is then used to transfer the trenches to the top oxide layer, and then to the top silicon layer through KOH etching (35% in water, IPA saturated, at 43 °C for 5’) after resist strip in acetone. At this point, the chips need to be heavily p doped, to allow the electrical measurements. Diffusion by solid doping source is used, with a standard two step procedure (predeposition and drive-in). For the predeposition, the top silicon dioxide layer is removed by BHF etching, leaving exposed the silicon layer. The chips are then positioned in contact with the solid source, and moved in a tubular furnace, heated at 900 °C for 10 minutes in nitrogen flux. After the predeposition, the drive-in is performed in the Rapid Thermal Annealer at 1150 °C for 5’ in oxygen flux, to prevent the out diffusion of the doping. The drive-in process leave a top silicon layer uniformly doped, because the dopant is confined between the buried oxide and the top oxide layer grown during the drive-in. The final thickness of the top silicon layer resulted 190 nm (part of it is “consumed” during the two oxidations). At this point, the top oxide layer is removed by BHF and Focused Ion Beam (FIB) is used to fabricate the periodic structure on the silicon nanostructures. FIB has been performed with a ZEISS CROSSBEAM X550, provided also of SEM column used for the images of the device. The FIB etching parameter are: beam current 10 pA, dose 10, beam step 5 nm X 5 nm, etching with 10 cycles. These parameters have been accurately calibrated to achieve an etch depth of 20 nm. The measurement of the etch depth has been done through no-contact AFM imaging (see Fig. 1, AFM Park XE100). However, the measurement of very narrow holes as those shown in Fig. 1 cannot be performed with a good reliability, even if a depth around 20 nm can be supposed. Hence, FIB parameters have been calibrated on large-area structures (squares of several m), as that shown in the AFM image of Figure 4. The etch depth resulted quite repeatable: 20 nm ± 2 nm. To be noted that the FIB etching, whic is performed with very low current, leaves a smooth surface. The FIB parameters, calibrated on these large-area structures, have then be used to engrave the very narrow silicon structures. The depth of the narrow features on the nanowires is not necessarily the same as that of the larger-area structures, partly due to possible re-deposition of sputtered material during fabrication. It is likely that the actual depth of the periodic patterns in top of the nanowires is even smaller. However, the process has proven to be highly repeatable across multiple fabrication runs.

Measurement and data reduction

The thermal conductivity has been measured by 3 measurements, through a self-heating, self-measuring technique as illustrated in the main text: the voltage amplitude of the third harmonic distortion is measured by means of a SRS630 lock-in amplifier, and reported as a function of the biasing current. The analytic relationship between and the amplitude of the sinusoidal biasing current I is approximate, valid when the width W of the nanowire is uniform and it is very small with respect to its length L. In the present case, the nanostructures are thin (190 nm), but quite large (650 nm), compared with the length m. Moreover, as reported in the text, the cross-section of the engraved nanowires is not constant: of course, is a material property and must be evaluated at the net of the geometrical parameters, and in particular cross-section variations must be taken into account. For these reasons, we performed a Finite Element Method (FE) analysis to reduce the experimental results and to find a precise relationship between and , given the current amplitude I. Specifically, we considered a 3D model with the width and length as measured by SEM imaging. For the shallow trenches, we considered the width and pitch measured by SEM imaging, and a depth of 20 nm (that measured on large-area etches performed with the same FIB parameters, see previous paragraph).
