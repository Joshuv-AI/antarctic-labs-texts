Optimal solutions employing an algebraic Variational Multiscale approach Part II: Application to Navier-Stokes
Suyash Shrestha, Marc Gerritsma, Gonzalo Rubio, Steven Hulshoff, Esteban Ferrer

Abstract
This work presents a nonlinear extension of the high-order discretisation framework based on the Variational Multiscale (VMS) method previously introduced for steady linear problems. Building on the concept of an optimal projector defined via the symmetric part of the governing operator, we generalise the formulation to treat the 2D incompressible Navier–Stokes equations. The approach maintains a clear separation between resolved and unresolved scales, with the fine-scale contribution approximated through the approximate Fine-Scale Greens’ function of the associated symmetric operator. This enables a consistent variational treatment of nonlinearity while preserving high-order accuracy. We show that the method yields numerical solutions that closely approximate the optimal projection of the continuous/highly resolved solution and inherits desirable conservation properties. Numerical results confirm the framework’s robustness, accuracy, and its potential for application to a broad class of nonlinear multiscale problems.

Introduction

This work continues the development initiated in
[
1
]
, where we proposed a discretisation framework grounded in the Variational Multiscale (VMS) paradigm

In the present work, we extend this framework to more general unsteady and nonlinear problems, specifically the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations. This extension builds on the abstract formulation from Part I
[
1
]
, incorporating nonlinear residuals into the coarse-scale–fine-scale interaction. The goal remains consistent with Part I: to compute a numerical solution that corresponds to an optimal projection of the analytical solution, where optimality is defined with respect to the symmetric part of the underlying operator. The extension to nonlinear settings necessitates a careful treatment of the coarse–fine scale interaction, especially as the unresolved scales now appear nonlinearly in the governing equations. We demonstrate how the previously developed framework can be systematically generalised to such cases while preserving its variational character and high-order accuracy. This paper specifically employs the Mimetic Spectral Element Method (MSEM)

6

In particular, the extension is straightforward for discretisations that respect the discrete de Rham complex, as the underlying structural properties are naturally preserved. For methods that do not satisfy the de Rham sequence, some reformulations may be required, including the verification of stability properties such as the
inf-sup
condition and appropriate reconstruction of compatible function spaces.

In parallel, significant advances have been made in the formulation of VMS methods for the Navier–Stokes equations, particularly in the context of stabilisation and turbulence modelling. For example,

Despite these extensive developments in both conservative discretisation and multiscale modelling, these strategies have largely evolved independently. To the best of our knowledge, no existing approach unifies a conservative high-order formulation, such as the MEEVC scheme, with a VMS strategy that aims to recover an a priori chosen
projection
of the exact solution. In this work, we bridge this gap by formulating a VMS framework compatible with the MEEVC scheme, enabling the computation of projected solutions that not only respect the structure of the governing equations but also preserve key physical invariants at the discrete level. This synthesis offers a promising direction for developing structure-preserving, high-fidelity solvers for incompressible flows. The VMS philosophy we employ in this work shares some likeness with
[
26
]
, where scale separation is dictated through the bilinear forms of the associated weak form. However, we choose to work solely with symmetric bilinear forms and employ the explicit Fine-Scale Greens’ function to treat the unresolved scales.

We take a moment to clarify the intended meaning of the term “optimal projector” as used in this work. The notion of optimality here is specific to the projector we construct, which is chosen based on its alignment with the structure of the governing PDE. This should not be interpreted as a claim of universal superiority over all other possible projectors or solutions. Rather, it reflects a deliberate design choice guided by the goals and formulation of the present study. While alternative projectors may be better suited in different contexts or under different norms, our aim is not to exhaustively compare all such options. Instead, we focus on demonstrating how the proposed framework can recover solutions that are optimal with respect to the chosen projection.

Background and theoretical preliminaries

2.1
The abstract problem

Consider a generic elliptic problem defined as follows. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a contractible open bounded domain with a Lipschitz continuous boundary
\partial\Omega
. Let
V
be a Hilbert space in
\Omega
endowed with a norm
||\cdot||_{V}
and an inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{V}
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a linear isomorphism between
V
and its dual space
V^{*}
,
\mathcal{L}:V\to V^{*}
. We state the generic problem as: given
\underline{f}\in V^{*}
, find
\underline{u}\in V
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\underline{u}

\displaystyle=\underline{f},\quad\text{in }\Omega.

(1)

We may variationally characterise the solution to such an elliptic problem as the critical (saddle) point of the energy functional
\mathcal{I}
associated with
\mathcal{L}

[
27
,
28
,
29
]
. This ultimately leads to a weak formulation of the original problem. The weak formulation can be discretised using the Galerkin approach with the restriction of the test and trial spaces to a
suitably
chosen set of finite-dimensional spaces
[
27
]
. Provided that we choose these spaces suitably, we can show that the corresponding discrete solution is in fact a projection of the continuous (strong) solution onto the finite-dimensional space where the projector is defined using the energy functional
\mathcal{I}
. Hence, the discrete solution is the stationary point of
an
error norm embedded in the structure of
\mathcal{I}
. We demonstrated this concept in
[
1
]
for a simple Poisson problem, and we shall demonstrate it for the Stokes flow problem in this paper in
Section

3
.

While the problem in (
1
) possesses the advantageous aforementioned structure when
\mathcal{L}
is elliptic, it no longer has this structure for a generic non-elliptic
\mathcal{L}
. To be more concrete, we mean that for a generic, possibly non-linear
\mathcal{L}
, we can no longer characterise the solution as the critical (saddle) point of the energy functional and a Galerkin discretisation of the mixed formulation
does not
yield a solution that is the projection of the continuous solution, i.e. the Galerkin solution is sub-optimal in the norm embedded in the structure of
\mathcal{I}
associated with the underlying elliptic problem. This loss of optimality for the Galerkin scheme for generic
\mathcal{L}
s does not necessarily affect the stability of the discretisation. What it does imply is that the solutions on coarse spaces may share a weaker likeness to the analytical (or highly resolved) solutions, attributed to a larger error in the optimal norm.

2.2
Review of Part I

In order to ensure that the Galerkin formulation does yield a solution equal, or at least very close to, the optimal projection of the continuous solution, we propose to supplement the scheme with VMS analysis. In Part I
[
1
]
, we introduced an algebraic VMS framework tailored for linear advection-diffusion problems. The methodology relies on a refined-mesh
1
1

1

“refined-mesh” as in two meshes varying in refinement
formulation using a hierarchical polynomial basis that naturally induces a scale separation within the finite-element space. The resolved (coarse) scales are approximated using a coarse mesh, while the unresolved (fine) scales are explicitly computed through a consistent approximation of the Fine-Scale Greens’ function
[
4
]
on a
p
-refined second mesh. We note that we only require the Fine-Scale Greens’ function of the
symmetric
part of the differential operator. This theoretical formulation was verified through a series of one and two-dimensional benchmark linear advection-diffusion problems in
[
1
]
. Employing this approach yielded a coarse-scale solution that closely matched the projection of the analytical solution onto the coarse mesh, while simultaneously yielding an approximation of the unresolved scales. The goal of the present work is to demonstrate the generalisation of the abstract algebraic VMS framework towards the Navier-Stokes equations.

Projector definition

Proposition 1

The discrete solution minimises the error of the Hodge Laplacian (

\begin{array}[]{cccccl}\left(\varepsilon^{h},{\omega^{\prime}}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&-\left(\nabla\times\varepsilon^{h},{\underline{u}^{\prime}}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&&=&0,&\forall\varepsilon^{h}\in\bar{U},\forall(\omega^{\prime},\underline{u}^{\prime})\in U^{\prime}\times V^{\prime}\\[6.45831pt]

(19)

4
Optimal projection: Navier-Stokes

Having defined our
optimal
projector for the Stokes problem, we now consider the full Navier-Stokes equations.

4.1
Continuous mixed formulation

Much like the Stokes flow problem, we express the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations in a mixed formulation by defining the vorticity
\omega
and further rewriting the convective term in the Lamb (Rotational) form. The continuous problem in a domain
\Omega
over a time period
\mathcal{T}=(0,T]
reads

\displaystyle{\omega}-\nabla\times\underline{u}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{in }\Omega\times\mathcal{T}

(20)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\underline{u}}{\partial t}+\omega\times\underline{u}+\frac{1}{Re}\nabla\times{\omega}+\nabla P

\displaystyle=\underline{0},\quad\text{in }\Omega\times\mathcal{T}

(21)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\underline{u}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{in }\Omega\times\mathcal{T}

(22)

\displaystyle\underline{u}\cdot\hat{n}=u_{\perp},\quad\text{on}\>\Gamma_{n}\times\mathcal{T},

\displaystyle\quad\quad\omega=\hat{\omega},\quad\text{on}\>\Gamma_{\omega}\times\mathcal{T}

(23)

\displaystyle P=\hat{P},\quad\text{on}\>\Gamma_{P}\times\mathcal{T},

\displaystyle\quad\quad{\underline{u}}\times\hat{n}=u_{\parallel},\quad\text{on}\>\Gamma_{t}\times\mathcal{T}

(24)

\displaystyle\text{with }\Gamma_{n}\cup\Gamma_{P}=\Gamma_{\omega}\cup\Gamma_{t}

\displaystyle=\partial\Omega,\quad\text{and}\>\Gamma_{n}\cap\Gamma_{P}=\Gamma_{\omega}\cap\Gamma_{t}=\varnothing,

(25)

where
P:=\mathord{\text{$\mkern-2.0mu\raisebox{-6.45831pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\mathchar 22$}\mkern-7.0mup$}}+\frac{1}{2}\underline{u}^{2}
is the total or Bernoulli pressure. The system is supplemented with an initial condition for the velocity and vorticity.

At the continuous level, the formulation exhibits strong conservation and dissipation properties. Mass conservation is enforced by the divergence-free condition. Kinetic energy
\mathcal{K}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\underline{u},\underline{u}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
and enstrophy
\mathcal{E}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\omega,\omega\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
evolve in time through viscous dissipation: the kinetic energy decays proportionally to the enstrophy, and the enstrophy in turn dissipates via palinstrophy
\mathscr{P}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\times\omega,\nabla\times\omega\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
. Furthermore, kinetic energy and enstrophy are exactly conserved in the inviscid limit (
Re\rightarrow\infty
). These relations emerge naturally from the structure of the equations and are captured through balance laws derived under suitable assumptions (e.g., periodic domains or vanishing boundary fluxes). Moreover, the total vorticity
\mathcal{W}:=\left(1,\omega\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
is also conserved, a result directly linked to the circulation theorem. The four conserved quantities are listed as follows (see
[
17
]
for further details)

\nabla\cdot\underline{u}=0,\quad\quad\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{K}}{\partial t}+\frac{2}{Re}\mathcal{E}=0,\quad\quad\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{E}}{\partial t}+\frac{2}{Re}\mathscr{P}=0,\quad\quad\mathcal{W}=\int_{\partial\Omega}u_{||}\>\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{t}.

The essence of the MEEVC scheme is to conserve these symmetries at the discrete level. We propose to do the same while simultaneously supplementing the scheme with VMS analysis.

Remark 4.1
.

While the MEEVC scheme
[
17
]
is designed to conserve enstrophy at the discrete level, this property depends critically on the accuracy of numerical integration. In particular, the nonlinear convective term and all other bilinear forms must be integrated exactly to ensure enstrophy conservation. In practice, this requires either exact integration or the use of sufficiently high-order quadrature rules. If standard quadrature is used (i.e., of the same polynomial order as the basis functions), the conservation of enstrophy is no longer guaranteed. In this work, we employ Gauss–Lobatto quadrature rules of equal order to the basis functions, which generally results in under-integration. As a consequence, our implementation (both basic Galerkin and VMS) does not strictly conserve enstrophy.

In line with the MEEVC scheme, we spatially define the three physical quantities in the aforementioned Sobolev spaces as previously shown for Stokes flow.
However, we first perform a temporal discretisation to reduce the time-dependent problem to an effective steady problem that can be solved every time step. We employ the simple second-order accurate symplectic time integrator, namely the Crank-Nicolson scheme

\displaystyle\frac{\underline{{u}}_{n+1}-\underline{{u}}_{n}}{\Delta t}=-{\omega}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\times{\underline{u}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{1}{Re}\nabla\times{\omega}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}-\nabla{P}_{n+\frac{1}{2}},\quad\quad\text{where }[\cdot]_{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{2}([\cdot]_{n+1}+[\cdot]_{n}).

(26)

We henceforth directly work with the time-discretised equations, with the effective steady problem to be solved every time step given as

\displaystyle{\omega}_{n+1}-\nabla\times{\underline{u}}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=0

(27)

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{2Re}\nabla\times{\omega}_{n+1}-\frac{1}{\Delta t}{\underline{u}}_{n+1}+\nabla{P}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2Re}\nabla\times{\omega}_{n}-\frac{1}{\Delta t}{\underline{u}}_{n}+{\omega}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\times{\underline{u}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}

(28)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot{\underline{u}}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=0.

(29)

We derive an infinite-dimensional weak form of the problem as follows

\begin{gathered}\begin{array}[]{cccccl}\left(\varepsilon,{\omega}_{n+1}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&-\left(\nabla\times\varepsilon,{\underline{u}}_{n+1}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&&=&\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\varepsilon u_{||}\>\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{t},&\forall\omega\in H(\text{curl},\Omega)\\[5.81247pt]

&\left(\eta,\nabla\cdot{\underline{u}}_{n+1}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&&=&0,&\forall\eta\in L^{2}(\Omega)\end{array}\\

The system undoubtedly shares similarities with the system in (
11

. Consequently, we note that the basic Galerkin discretisation of this system will
not
yield a desirable projection of the exact solution. Hence, we employ VMS analysis wherein we leverage the parts that do fit in the aforementioned variational formulation and formulate an approach that does yield a close approximation of the projected solution.

While the system for the resolved scales in (
34
) is finite-dimensional, it depends continuously on the fine-scale solution, which is strictly governed by the infinite-dimensional system in (
35
). In the present framework, the discretisation procedure involves solving the resolved-scale problem on a coarse mesh and the fine-scale problem on a finer mesh. We construct these two meshes in a hierarchical manner, with the finer mesh being
p
-refined such that the coarse space is exactly contained in the fine space. This strategy effectively removes the barrier posed by the infinite-dimensional nature of the fine-scale problem, at the cost of only approximating the fine scales within a richer but still finite-dimensional subspace. If the fine-scale component is neglected, i.e. if the fine scales are assumed to vanish, then the formulation reduces to the classical Galerkin method on the coarse mesh. However, this simplification fails to capture the influence of unresolved-scale interactions, which can significantly affect accuracy, particularly in convection-dominated regimes.

There are several strategies available for solving this coupled nonlinear system. In this work, we adopt a Fine-Scale Greens’ function formulation for the unresolved scales, based on the symmetric part of the governing operator. This involves formally inverting the left-hand side of (
35
) to construct an approximate Fine-Scale Greens’ operator associated with the Stokes-like substructure embedded within the VMS formulation of the Navier–Stokes equations. At each time level, we employ Picard iterations, with a convergence tolerance of
1\times 10^{-12}
in the
L^{2}
norm, to jointly solve (
34
) and (
35
), thereby updating both the resolved and unresolved components of the solution. This approach avoids the need for a second nonlinear solve on the fine mesh while still preserving the nonlinear coupling between scales through the iteration process, ensuring fidelity to the full multiscale system. The nonlinear iterations it takes to solve the problem is dependent on the time step size (
\Delta t
). For the tests considered in this work, the solution converged in
\mathcal{O}(10^{1})
iterations.

Remark 4.2
.

In this work, we make a deliberate choice to project the pressure separately using the
L^{2}

L^{2}(\Omega)
. Therefore, we disregard the value of
\bar{P}

\bar{P}:=\mathcal{P}_{L^{2}}\widetilde{P}
and

.

There remains potential for future work to explore alternative treatments of the pressure term. One possible direction is to employ the pressure Poisson equation to reconstruct the pressure field. However, in this study, we restrict our attention to the simpler and more direct approach described above, where the pressure is projected separately using the
L^{2}
projector.

5
Numerical tests

In this section, we present numerical experiments to verify the proposed framework. Two test cases are considered for the illustration of the framework. The first is the two-dimensional Taylor–Green vortex problem, which admits a smooth analytical solution and is used here to verify the order of convergence of the method. 6
Summary

This work presented an algebraic VMS formulation tailored for high-order discretisations of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations, designed to enhance coarse-scale accuracy while preserving key conservation properties. Building on a Galerkin foundation, the method introduces a scale separation via an orthogonal projector based on the underlying symmetric problem. Employing this projector in combination with the explicit construction of the Fine-Scale Greens’ function of the symmetric operator enables a consistent treatment of unresolved scales without introducing artificial dissipation.

To evaluate the method, we first performed convergence tests using the Taylor-Green vortex test case. These showed the expected convergence rates, including exponential convergence of the VMS solution to the exact projection, as well as exponential convergence of the unresolved scales to their exact counterparts. We then considered solutions of the inviscid vortex roll-up problem, which is a challenging benchmark known for its sharp gradients and dynamic multiscale features. A highly-resolved solution obtained with the base Galerkin scheme served as a reference. Through projection onto a coarse mesh, we established a benchmark representing the best-possible resolved-scale behaviour in the chosen norm. Comparisons against coarse-mesh simulations reveal that the proposed VMS approach closely reproduces this projection, outperforming the coarse Galerkin solution, which exhibited spurious modes and poor resemblance to the reference.

Importantly, both the Galerkin and VMS schemes were shown to satisfy the same conservation properties under the MEEVC framework, with the VMS conservation guaranteed by the orthogonality between resolved and unresolved scales. These results affirm the potential of the proposed approach to deliver accurate, stable, and conservative approximations in under-resolved settings, laying a solid foundation for future extensions to more complex problems.

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\left({\bar{\mathord{\text{$\mkern-2.0mu\raisebox{-6.45831pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\mathchar 22$}\mkern-7.0mup$}}}},{\bar{\mathord{\text{$\mkern-2.0mu\raisebox{-6.45831pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\mathchar 22$}\mkern-7.0mup$}}}}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}{\lVert\underline{\varphi}^{h}\rVert_{H(\mathrm{div})}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lx@sectionsign\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{lem:poincare}}}{{\geq}}\frac{1}{C_{P_{div}}}\frac{\lVert{\bar{\mathord{\text{$\mkern-2.0mu\raisebox{-6.45831pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\mathchar 22$}\mkern-7.0mup$}}}}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}}{\lVert{\bar{\mathord{\text{$\mkern-2.0mu\raisebox{-6.45831pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\mathchar 22$}\mkern-7.0mup$}}}}\rVert_{L^{2}}}\geq\frac{1}{C_{P_{div}}}\lVert{\bar{\mathord{\text{$\mkern-2.0mu\raisebox{-6.45831pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\mathchar 22$}\mkern-7.0mup$}}}}\rVert_{L^{2}},

where taking the infimum over
\bar{\mathord{\text{$\mkern-2.0mu\raisebox{-6.45831pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\mathchar 22$}\mkern-7.0mup$}}}
yields the
inf-sup
constant
\frac{1}{C_{P_{div}}}
, thereby satisfying the
inf-sup
condition. If, on the other hand, the pressure is not prescribed on any part of the boundary (i.e.,
\Gamma_{p}=\varnothing
), and only the normal velocity is prescribed along
\partial\Omega
, then the functions in
\bar{V}
vanish on the entire boundary. In this case, the divergence operator maps onto a subspace of
L^{2}(\Omega)
with
\mathrm{Range}(\nabla\cdot)^{\perp}=\mathbb{R}
, corresponding to the space of constant functions. As a result, the
inf-sup
condition is only satisfied with the pressure in
L^{2}(\Omega)\backslash\mathbb{R}
, with the previously stated
inf-sup
constant
\frac{1}{C_{P_{{div}}}}
. The singular constant pressure mode is a well-known physical singular mode for incompressible flows and is not a numerical artifact.

Appendix C
Conservation properties

The conservation properties of the proposed VMS approach are linked to the orthogonality conditions between the resolved and unresolved scales. We recall the orthogonality conditions imposed by our chosen optimal projector for the Navier-Stokes equations as follows

\begin{array}[]{ccllll}\left(\varepsilon^{h},{\omega}^{\prime}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&-\left(\nabla\times\varepsilon^{h},{\underline{u}}^{\prime}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&&=&0,&\forall\varepsilon^{h}\in\bar{U},\forall(\omega^{\prime},\underline{u}^{\prime})\in U^{\prime}\times V^{\prime}\\[5.81247pt]
\displaystyle\frac{-1}{2Re}\left(\underline{v}^{h},\nabla\times{\omega^{\prime}}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta t}\left(\underline{v}^{h},\underline{{u}}^{\prime}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&+\left(\nabla\cdot\underline{v}^{h},{P}^{\prime}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&=&\underline{0},&\forall\underline{v}^{h}\in\bar{V},\forall(\omega^{\prime},\underline{u}^{\prime},P^{\prime})\in U^{\prime}\times V^{\prime}\times W^{\prime}\\[11.62495pt]
&\left(\eta^{h},\nabla\cdot{\underline{u}^{\prime}}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&&=&0,&\forall\eta^{h}\in\bar{W},\forall\underline{u}^{\prime}\in V^{\prime}\end{array}

(55)

which holds for any arbitrary time level
n
. Conservation of mass is trivially satisfied as we explicitly enforce
\nabla\cdot\bar{\underline{u}}
and
\nabla\cdot\bar{\underline{u}}
to zero through the means of Lagrange multipliers
\bar{P}
and
P^{\prime}
respectively.

Considering the conservation of total vorticity, we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\left(1,\bar{\omega}_{n+1}+\omega^{\prime}_{n+1}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

(56)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\left(1,\bar{\omega}_{n+1}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\cancel{\left(1,{\omega}^{\prime}_{n+1}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}

(57)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\left(1,\bar{\omega}_{n+1}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\int_{\partial\Omega}u_{\parallel}\>\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{t}.

(58)

The term
\left(1,{\omega}^{\prime}_{n+1}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
vanishes as dictated by the first equation of (
55
) with a particular choice of
\varepsilon^{h}=1
.

To show the conservation of energy, we start by noting the following two relations for the kinetic energy
\mathcal{K}
and enstrophy

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta t}\left(\underline{u}_{n+\frac{1}{2}},\underline{u}_{n+1}-\underline{u}_{n}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\frac{1}{2\Delta t}\left(\left(\underline{u}_{n+1},\underline{u}_{n+1}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\left(\underline{u}_{n},\underline{u}_{n}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)=\frac{1}{\Delta t}(\mathcal{K}_{n+1}-\mathcal{K}_{n})

(59)

\displaystyle\left(\underline{u}_{n+\frac{1}{2}},\nabla\times\omega_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\left(\omega_{n+\frac{1}{2}},\omega_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=2\mathcal{E}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}.

(60)

What we want to show is that the total kinetic energy is dissipated with the enstrophy, and that energy is exactly conserved in the inviscid limit. Specificity, we need to show

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{\Delta t}((\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{n+1}+{\mathcal{K}}^{\prime}_{n+1})-(\bar{\mathcal{K}}_{n}+{\mathcal{K}}^{\prime}_{n}))+\frac{2}{Re}(\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{n+\frac{1}{2}})=\\
\frac{1}{\Delta t}\left(\underline{\bar{u}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}},\underline{u}^{\prime}_{n}-\underline{u}^{\prime}_{n+1}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\frac{1}{Re}\left(\underline{\bar{u}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}},\nabla\times\omega^{\prime}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\\

) and (

) where we define resolved and unresolved kinetic energy and enstrophy
