Spectral square roots of the multivector
Adolfas Dargys ∗ , Artūras Acus ∗∗

Abstract
. The problem of multivector (MV) multiple square roots in real geometric Clifford algebras 𝐶𝑙 p , q \mathit{Cl}_{p,q} with symbolic coefficients is considered. The method to find multiple MV square roots that is based on R. Bott’s periodicity table and matrix eigensystem in 𝐶𝑙 p , q \mathit{Cl}_{p,q} is proposed. The method can be applied to MV having both numerical and symbolic coefficients. In addition, method allows to determine the domain of the existence of thus obtained spectral square roots. A number of examples is presented for multivectors in low, p + q ≤ 3 p+q\leq 3 , and higher dimensional Clifford algebras, including 4D (anti)-Euclidean space and relativistic 𝐶𝑙 1 , 3 \mathit{Cl}_{1,3} and 𝐶𝑙 3 , 1 \mathit{Cl}_{3,1} algebras. Tables of the required basis vectors for conversion of MV to Bott’s matrix representation have been found from respective algebra idempotents using ideal theory and presented for real Clifford algebras in Appendix.

The respective matrix representations (reps) of CAs have been found by R. Bott

2.
Multivector matrix reps

The Clifford algebra
\mathit{Cl}_{p,q}
is an associative vectorial and noncommutative
algebra having
2^{n}
basis elements, where

n=p+q
is the dimension of the vector space. Geometrically it is
defined by orthogonal unit basis vectors
\mathbf{e}_{i}
, where

i=1,\dots,n
.
s=p-q
is the signature of
algebra, where squares of
p
and
q
vectors are, respectively,
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{2}=1
and

\mathbf{e}_{i}^{2}=-1
. If
q=0
the vector space is Euclidean
and if
p=0
it is anti-Euclidean. Otherwise the space has a mixed
signature. Apart of vectors the space accommodates oriented planes
called bivectors
\mathbf{e}_{ij}=\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j}
, oriented volumes called
trivectors
\mathbf{e}_{ijk}=\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{j}\mathbf{e}_{k}
, and oriented supervolumes called
quadvectors, pentavector etc. The number of subscripts indicates
the grade of basis element, so that the scalar is a grade-0
element, the vector is a grade-1 element, etc. The largest elementary
(super)volume belongs to blade
\mathbf{e}_{ijk\dotsm n}
, which commonly is called
the pseudoscalar and indicated by
I
. Thus, for example in 3D algebras
(
\mathit{Cl}_{3,0}
,
\mathit{Cl}_{2,1}
,
\mathit{Cl}_{1,2}
and
\mathit{Cl}_{0,3}
) the MV consists of
the following elements (basis blades)

\{1,\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2},\mathbf{e}_{3},\mathbf{e}_{12},\mathbf{e}_{13},\mathbf{e}_{23},\mathbf{e}_{123}\equiv I\}
.
The first element represents the scalar.

The MV
\mathsf{A}
is a sum of different blades multiplied by real
coefficients
a_{J}
. In 3D algebras the general MV expanded in
coordinates (basis elements) reads,

(1)

\begin{split}\mathsf{A}=&a_{0}+a_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+a_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}+a_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}+a_{12}\mathbf{e}_{12}+a_{23}\mathbf{e}_{23}+a_{31}\mathbf{e}_{31}+a_{123}I=\\
&a_{0}+\mathbf{a}+\mathcal{B}+a_{123}I,\end{split}

where
a_{0}=\langle\mathsf{A}\rangle_{0}
is the scalar,

\mathbf{a}\equiv\langle\mathsf{A}\rangle_{1}=a_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+a_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}+a_{3}\mathbf{e}_{3}
is the vector and

\mathcal{B}\equiv\langle\mathsf{A}\rangle_{2}=a_{12}\mathbf{e}_{12}+a_{23}\mathbf{e}_{23}+a_{31}\mathbf{e}_{31}
represents bivector
part. Thus, to single out
a sum of scalar and pseudoscalar from MV given by Eq. (
1
), it is enough to
apply grade-0 and grade-3 selectors,

\langle\mathsf{A}\rangle_{0}+\langle\mathsf{A}\rangle_{3}=a_{0}+a_{123}I

(see Table

3

3.
Diagonalization method

3.1.
Square root algorithm

The algorithm is based on the Bott’s table reps and the
equivalence between MV geometric product and matrix product of
reps. Also refer to Table
1
. The
diagonalization method consists of the following steps.

1.

Select the MV
\mathsf{A}
that belongs to a particular
\mathit{Cl}_{p,q}
.

2.

Convert the MV
\mathsf{A}
to
matrix rep
\hat{\mathsf{A}}
with the help of corresponding table in
Sec.
10
. Elementary reps that belong to
bivectors, trivectors etc are the products of respective
vector reps from the selected table. Multiply the
basis elementary reps by respective real coefficients that belong to MV.

3.

Find eigenvalues and eigenvectors, i.e. eigensystem, of the constructed
rep matrix. Since the coefficients of
\mathsf{A}
are real numbers,
any program to find eigensystem is suitable for this purpose. If the initial
MV has symbolic coefficients then respective symbolic program, for example, in

Mathematica
or
Maple
package, should be used.

4.

Using the eigenvalues, construct a diagonal spectral matrix, and then
extract square roots from diagonal entries. Select one of the plus/minus sign combinations before the roots.

5.

Then, construct the transformation matrix from the eigenvectors
that will allow you to perform transformation of the diagonal matrix back to the
initial representation
\hat{\mathsf{B}}
, i.e. to a new matrix
\hat{\mathsf{B}}
.
In the matrix
\hat{\mathsf{B}}
, there is hidden the MV square root
for which sign combinations you have selected on the diagonal matrix are already taken into account.

6.

With formula (
3
), convert
\hat{\mathsf{B}}
to square root MV

\mathsf{B}
and check that all coefficients are real. Only pairs of complex conjugate coefficients, which reduce to real numbers,
are allowed as explained by Fig.
1
. The square of
the roots must yield the initial MV,
\mathsf{B}^{2}=\mathsf{A}
.

7.

Repeat items
1-6
with all possible
plus/minus sign combinations for remaining roots.

8.

The described procedure also
may be used to find square roots of
-1
that is needed for Clifford-Fourier transform
[
4
]
. Then, in addition, the
following conditions must be satisfied, where
n=p+q
,

\begin{split}&\langle\mathsf{A}^{2}\rangle_{0}=-1,\qquad\qquad\quad\ \text{root condition},\\
&\langle\mathsf{A}^{2}\rangle_{k}=0,\ 1\leq k\leq n,\quad\text{constraints.}\\
\end{split}

The described procedure may be applied to find roots of MVs
with symbolic as well as numerical coefficients.

3.2.
Some practical issues

Here some points relevant to square root method are discussed shortly.

1.
Maximal number of roots
. The maximal number of spectral
roots is equal to all possible plus/minus sign combinations on
the diagonal of eigenvalue matrix. This property follows from Bott’s table
(also, refer to the tables in Sec.
10
, where Bott’s
matrix symbol is written next to algebra name). Bott’s matrices may be real, complex
or quaternionic. The maximal number of square roots is
n_{t}=2^{t}
.
where
t
is the dimension of the Bott’s matrix rep.
For example,
t=1
for algebras
\mathit{Cl}_{0,1}
(complex numbers) and

\mathit{Cl}_{0,2}
(quaternions);
t=2
for algebras
\mathit{Cl}_{2,0}
,
\mathit{Cl}_{3,0}
and

\mathit{Cl}_{1,3}
(quaternions);
t=4
for algebras
\mathit{Cl}_{3,1}
,
\mathit{Cl}_{2,2}
and
\mathit{Cl}_{2,1}
with reps
\mathbb{R}(4)
and
{}^{2}\mathbb{R}(2)
,
respectively (Sec.
10
).
Appearance of repeated roots, complex coefficients or no-root solutions may
diminish the number of possible square roots.

2.
Quaternionic reps
. The problems may arise with
quaternionic reps. The simplest way to circumvent the
problem is to replace the quaternionic entries by
2\times 2

complex matrix blocks that represent Hamilton imaginaries
\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}
.
Since maximal number of the roots must
remain the same as it is in quaternionic rep, therefore the number of
plus/minus sign combinations must be smaller then that given by formula
n_{t}=2^{t}
. For
example, if initial quaternion matrix is
1\times 1
, we have only
two plus/minus roots. If quaternions are replaced by complex

2\times 2
matrices then the root signs on diagonals must remain
the same, i.e.
\{++\}
and
\{--\}
. Thus, in this case the combinations
\{+-\}

and
\{-+\}
are forbidden, otherwise spurious roots will appear
in the solution. For concrete examples refer to
Subsec.
4.2
and Subsec.
5.4
.

3.
Pairs of plus/minus roots
. In general, the spectral square root
algorithm gives pairs of roots
having opposite plus/minus signs, simply because in the diagonal
root matrix there always appear combinations with opposite signs,
for example
(+,-,+,-)
and
(-,+,-,+)
in the case of
4\times 4
matrix rep. Thus, the MV roots always consist of root pairs
having plus/minus signs, and it is enough to restrict the calculations
to
n_{t}/2
sign combinations.

b)

3.3.
Eigensystem and related problems

Apart from Bott’s table, the spectral root algorithm also
requires to know the diagonal eigenvalue matrix
\mathsf{A}_{d}
and respective eigenvector matrix
\hat{\mathsf{T}}

constructed from MV rep
\hat{\mathsf{A}}
. They allow
to get all combinations of roots from spectrum (the eigenvalues) and to
transform scalar roots with different sign combinations on the diagonal back to initial matrix representation,
i.e. to get square root matrix
\hat{\mathsf{B}}
.
After conversion of
\hat{\mathsf{B}}
to MV
\mathsf{B}
one gets one of the possible MV square roots.
The transformation matrix
\hat{\mathsf{T}}
that is made up of eigenvectors
can be constructed from matrix
\hat{\mathsf{A}}
eigenvectors.
In
Mathematica
, the eigenvalue list and eigenvector matrix
are generated automatically by command
Eigensystem[ ]
.
If rep
\hat{\mathsf{A}}
that represents MV is real and symmetric then
all eigenvalues are real too.
If the matrix is Hermitian then all eigenvalues are real
but the transformation matrix
\hat{\mathsf{T}}
is complex. For large MVs
\mathsf{A}
, usually both the
diagonal eigenvalue matrix and
\hat{\mathsf{T}}
are complex.
As mentioned, the eigenvectors allow to construct the
transformation matrix to bring the diagonal signed root matrix back to the initial representation, i.e.
to matrix
\hat{\mathsf{B}}
. The transformation exists if the matrix
\hat{\mathsf{A}}
has distinct eigenvalues.
This happens if the characteristic polynomial of
m\times m
matrix
\hat{\mathsf{A}}
has
m
different roots.

Let’s consider a concrete example, when the matrix
\hat{\mathsf{A}}
is of dimension
4\times 4
.
We assume that
\hat{\mathsf{A}}
has four eigenvalues
\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}\}
which
may be real, imaginary or complex. First,
construct a diagonal
4\times 4
matrix
\hat{\mathsf{A}}_{d}
with eigenvalues
a_{i}
on the diagonal.
Then, all possible square roots of
\hat{\mathsf{A}}_{d}
are given by all possible combinations of plus/minus signs of roots on the diagonal,

(6)

\hat{\mathsf{A}}_{d}^{1/2}=\begin{bmatrix}\pm\sqrt{a_{1}}&0&0&0\\
0&\pm\sqrt{a_{2}}&0&0\\
0&0&\pm\sqrt{a_{3}}&0\\
0&0&0&\pm\sqrt{a_{4}}\\
\end{bmatrix},

where
a_{i}
are some functions of MV coefficients. All in all, there are
16

combinations of plus/minus signs in matrix (
6
)
and, respectively, we expect 16 different root matrices. For example, for sign combinations,

\{+\sqrt{a_{1}},+\sqrt{a_{2}},+\sqrt{a_{3}},+\sqrt{a_{4}}\}
and

\{-\sqrt{a_{1}},-\sqrt{a_{2}},-\sqrt{a_{3}},-\sqrt{a_{4}}\}
, we have the
first pair of plus/minus roots. The next two combinations,

\{-\sqrt{a_{1}},\sqrt{a_{2}},\sqrt{a_{3}},\sqrt{a_{4}}\}
and

\{\sqrt{a_{1}},-\sqrt{a_{2}},-\sqrt{a_{3}},-\sqrt{a_{4}}\}
, gives the second pair, etc. Thus, in general there are eight plus/minus root
pairs. If one of the eigenvalues is zero then we shall have
smaller number of roots. For example, if the third root on the diagonal
then the signed second pair is,
\pm\{\sqrt{a_{1}},\sqrt{a_{2}},0,\sqrt{a_{4}}\}
,

\pm\{-\sqrt{a_{1}},\sqrt{a_{2}},0,\sqrt{a_{4}}\}
. In case, all eigenvalues
are zeroes, we assume that spectral roots are absent.
Thus, in the example we expect 8 eight plus/minus pairs having different
combinations and accordingly a double number of MV roots.

According to Bott’s 8-periodicity table
[
7
]
, some of CAs are represented by
quaternion matrices, i.e., the entries of reps are quaternions.
The standard computer programs usually are suitable for work with real and
complex matrices only, hence problems may arise with quaternionic eigens. In
such a case it is convenient to replace the elementary Hamilton
quaternions
\mathbf{i}
,
\mathbf{j}
and
\mathbf{k}
, which satisfy

\mathbf{i}^{2}=\mathbf{j}^{2}=\mathbf{k}^{2}=-1
, by following
2\times 2
complex matrices,
respectively,

(7)

\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}=\Bigl[\begin{matrix}\mathrm{i}&0\\
0&\mathrm{i}\end{matrix}\Bigr],\quad\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}=\Bigl[\begin{matrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{matrix}\Bigl],\quad\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{3}=\Bigl[\begin{matrix}0&\mathrm{i}\\
\mathrm{i}&0\end{matrix}\Bigl],

and perform all computations by complex and doubled matrix reps.
For example, if MV has been represented by
2\times 2

quaternionic matrix then
t=2
and
one gets
n_{t}=2^{2}=4
, i.e., one expects not more than 4 different
quaternionic MV roots in order to be an agreement with
Bott’s table. Thus, to be in agreement with quaternionic representation
and get only true roots, the diagonal complex
4\times 4
matrix
should be restricted, in particular, they must have not more than four sign
combinations:
\pm\{\sqrt{a_{1}},\sqrt{a_{2}},\sqrt{a_{3}},\sqrt{a_{4}}\}
,

\pm\{\sqrt{a_{1}},\sqrt{a_{2}},-\sqrt{a_{3}},-\sqrt{a_{4}}\}
, which now are
in agreement with quaternionic sign pairs,
\pm\{Q_{1},Q_{2}\}
and

\pm\{Q_{1},-Q_{2}\}
, where
Q_{i}
is a general quaternion. Thus, in
this case the
4\times 4
matrix will give no more than four roots.

Finally, to have the roots in a MV form, we must perform inverse
transformation,
\hat{\mathsf{T}}\hat{\mathsf{B}}\hat{\mathsf{T}}^{-1}
, back to
primary matrix representation and convert the obtained matrix to
MV form using the formula (
3
). After
transformation and conversion, the number of MV square roots
may remain the same or become smaller. The latter case may happen
when the transformation matrix
\hat{\mathsf{T}}
is complex
and may bring in complex term(s) into the final root MV
\mathsf{B}
.

3.3.1.
The square root of idempotent

The MV is
idempotent if its the square is equal to MV itself
[
7
]
.
When
\mathbf{e}_{1}^{2}=1
, in low dimensional algebras a typical
idempotent is
\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\mathbf{e}_{1})
. In the first item
of tables in Sec.
10
, the reader will find the sets of
idempotents of real CAs employed to construct the matrix reps
of basis vectors. The rep of the
idempotent usually is a diagonal matrix with 1’s and 0’s on the diagonal, for example,
for MV in
\mathit{Cl}_{2,0}
,
\mathit{Cl}_{1,1}
and
\mathit{Cl}_{3,0}
algebras one of the idempotents is

[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}]
. In

\mathit{Cl}_{3,1}
, an example of idempotent may be

\tfrac{1}{4}(1\pm\mathbf{e}_{1})(1\pm\mathbf{e}_{24})
that represents diagonal

4\times 4
matrix with a single
1
on the diagonal
and the remaining
0
’s. The proposed
diagonalization method fails if it is applied to such type of
matrices. However, there is no need to calculate the square root
of the idempotent since, due to above mentioned property (the square of the
idempotent is equal to initial MV), it should be evident that the
square root of the idempotent must be equal to plus/minus
idempotent itself.

\mathsf{B}=b_{0}+\mathbf{e}_{1}b_{1}=\sqrt{\mathsf{A}}

The latter formula has no singularities.

4.1.2.
\mathit{Cl}_{1,0}

{{}^{2}\mathbb{R}(1)}
(hyperbolic numbers)

In
\mathit{Cl}_{1,0}

\mathbf{e}_{1}^{2}=1

According to first table in Sec.

[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{smallmatrix}]

\mathsf{A}=a_{0}+a_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}

is diagonal in matrix representation
{{}^{2}\mathbb{R}(1)}

(10)

\hat{\mathsf{A}}=a_{0}\left[\begin{vmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{vmatrix}\right]+a_{1}\left[\begin{vmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{vmatrix}\right]=\left[\begin{vmatrix}a_{0}+a_{1}&0\\
0&a_{0}-a_{1}\end{vmatrix}\right].

Thus, one can write the root outright,
\hat{\mathsf{B}}=\sqrt{\hat{\mathsf{A}}}=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}\pm\sqrt{a_{0}+a_{1}}&0\\
0&\pm\sqrt{a_{0}-a_{1}}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
,
from which follows that in contrast to complex numbers the hyperbolic
numbers may have up to four sign combinations:
\{++\}
,
\{--\}
,
\{+-\}
,

\{-+\}
and, respectively, four
roots. Since
\hat{\mathsf{A}}
is diagonal the transformation matrix constructed from
eigenvectors is very simple

\hat{\mathsf{T}}=\hat{\mathsf{T}}^{-1}=\big[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\big]
.
Transformation of
(++)
and
(+-)
root matrices back to initial representation gives
matrices

(11)

\left[\begin{vmatrix}\sqrt{a_{0}+a_{1}}&0\\
0&\sqrt{a_{0}-a_{1}}\end{vmatrix}\right],\quad\left[\begin{vmatrix}-\sqrt{a_{0}-a_{1}}&0\\
0&\sqrt{a_{0}+a_{1}}\end{vmatrix}\right].

For combinations
(--)
and
(-+)
, the signs of the matrices are opposite.
After the conversion to MV form we find two pairs of real roots,

(12)

\mathsf{B}=\sqrt{\mathsf{A}}=\begin{cases}\pm\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl((\sqrt{a_{0}-a_{1}}+\sqrt{a_{0}+a_{1}})-\mathbf{e}_{1}(\sqrt{a_{0}-a_{1}}-\sqrt{a_{0}+a_{1}}\,)\bigr),\\
\pm\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl((\sqrt{a_{0}-a_{1}}-\sqrt{a_{0}+a_{1}})-\mathbf{e}_{1}(\sqrt{a_{0}-a_{1}}+\sqrt{a_{0}+a_{1}}\,)\bigr).\end{cases}

If
a_{0}>a_{1}
there are four roots.
If
a_{0}=a_{1}=1

12

\mathsf{B}=\pm\sqrt{a_{0}}(1+\mathbf{e}_{1})/\sqrt{2}
. If
a_{0}<a_{1}
the root coefficients are complex

When
a_{0}>a_{1}
the roots (
12

expressed by hyperbolic functions, in a form similar to trigonometric ones in
Eq. (
8

(13)

\sqrt{\mathsf{A}}=\pm(a_{0}^{2}-a_{1}^{2})^{1/4}\lparen\cosh\frac{\varphi_{h}}{2}+\mathbf{e}_{1}\sinh\frac{\varphi_{h}}{2}\rparen,\quad\varphi_{h}=\textrm{Artanh}(a_{0},a_{1}),

where
\textrm{Artanh}(a_{0},a_{1})

If we draw horizontal and vertical axes in plane, then the
angle
\varphi_{h}
will lie between the horizontal axis
and vector that connects coordinate center and point
(a_{0},a_{1})
on the
hyperbola (refer to Fig.
2

\varphi_{h}=0

asymptotes (dashed lines). If

and
d_{2}
are distances from a point
on the hyperbola to left and right focus on the horizontal axis then the difference

|d_{1}-d_{2}|

\varphi_{h}
and plays the role of hyperbola radius.

4.2.1.
\mathit{Cl}_{0,2}

\mathbb{H}(1)

10

From this follows that the quaternion has two plus/minus roots

\mathbf{k}

\mathbf{i}\to\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}\mathrm{i}&0\\

-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr],\ \mathbf{k}=\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}\to\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{12}=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&\mathrm{i}\\

\hat{\mathsf{A}}=\left[\begin{matrix}a_{0}+\mathrm{i}a_{1}&a_{2}+\mathrm{i}a_{12}\\

\sqrt{\hat{\mathsf{A}}}=\hat{\mathsf{T}}\left[\begin{matrix}\pm\sqrt{a_{0}-m}&0\\

1&1\\
\end{matrix}\right],

\{++\}

(17)

(\sqrt{\mathsf{A}}\,)_{1,2}=\pm\tfrac{1}{2}\Bigl(\sqrt{a_{0}+m}+\sqrt{a_{0}-m}+\tfrac{\langle\mathsf{A}\rangle_{1}+\langle\mathsf{A}\rangle_{2}}{\sqrt{m^{2}}}\bigl(\sqrt{a_{0}+m}-\sqrt{a_{0}-m}\bigr)\Bigr).

Since

\langle\mathsf{A}\rangle_{1}

If angle

Thus, we have found that the quaternionic numbers have two (not

\sqrt{\hat{\mathsf{A}}}
in

1\times 1

contrast to Eq. (

Eq. (

(19)

\big(\sqrt{\mathsf{A}}\big)_{1,2}=\pm\big(|m|+\langle\mathsf{A}\rangle_{1}+\langle\mathsf{A}\rangle_{2}\big)/(2|m|)^{1/2}\,.

\mathit{Cl}_{1,1}

Quadratic MV equations

A simple quadratic MV equation is

(101)

\mathsf{X}^{2}+\mathsf{A}\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{X}\mathsf{A}+\mathsf{B}=0.

Noting that
\mathsf{X}^{2}+\mathsf{A}\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{X}\mathsf{A}=(\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{A})^{2}-\mathsf{A}^{2}
it is
an easy task to write down MV solution,

(102)

\mathsf{X}=-\mathsf{A}+\sqrt{\mathsf{A}^{2}-\mathsf{B}}\,.

Since, as we have seen, the MV roots require pairs with plus/minus signs, it is enough to
take into account only different pairs that follow from coefficients of MV
(\mathsf{A}^{2}-\mathsf{B})
.

The time-dependent differential matrix Riccati equation

(103)

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{X}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\mathsf{B(t)}+\mathsf{C(t)}\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{X}\mathsf{D(t)}+\mathsf{X}\mathsf{A(t)}\mathsf{X},

plays a prominent role in optimal filter design, control and
system theory
[
3
,
2
]
. In
Eq. (
103
),
\mathsf{X}
,
\mathsf{A}
,
\mathsf{B}
,
\mathsf{C}
are
MVs, where
\mathsf{X}
represents unknown. For stationary case all MVs are
constant and Eq. (
103
) reduces to nonlinear Riccati equation

(104)

\mathsf{X}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{C}\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{X}\mathsf{D}=\mathsf{B},

If
\mathsf{D}=\mathsf{C}
and
\mathsf{C}
is the center of algebra, i.e.,

\mathsf{C}
commutes with all multivectors, then resulting Clifford-Riccati equation

(105)

\mathsf{X}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{C}\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{X}\mathsf{C}=\mathsf{B}

can be solved. For example, in
\mathit{Cl}_{3,0}

and
\mathit{Cl}_{4,1}
algebras the center is a sum of scalar and
pseudoscalar,
\mathsf{C}=\eta+\xi I
,
\eta,\xi\in\mathbb{R}
. Then,
solution of Eq. (
105
) is

(106)

\mathsf{X}=\Big(-\mathsf{C}\pm\sqrt{\mathsf{BA}+\mathsf{C}^{2}}\,\Big)\mathsf{A}^{-1}.

If MVs are the scalars,
\mathsf{A}=a
,
\mathsf{B}=c
and
\mathsf{C}=b/2
,
then the MV equation (
106
) simplifies to

x_{1,2}=(-b\pm\sqrt{b^{2}+4ac})/2a
, which is the solution of the
well-known scalar quadratic equation. The fundamental difference between scalar
x_{1,2}
and
multivector
\mathsf{X}
solutions is that the former belongs
to commutative set while the latter belongs to
non-commutative one and concrete algebra. Thus, to solve the equation (
106
) completely, one
must know how to extract the square root from
\mathsf{BA}+\mathsf{C}^{2}
.
The algorithm for inverse MV

\mathsf{A}^{-1}
can be found in
[
29
]
.
Finally, let us illustrate the solution (
106
) for

\mathit{Cl}_{3,0}
when all known MVs in Riccati-Clifford

105
) consist of sum of scalar and pseudoscalar
(
\mathbf{e}_{123}\equiv I

\mathsf{A}=1+2I
,
\mathsf{B}=2+3I
,
\mathsf{C}=3+4I
. Then,
I^{2}=-1

and
\mathsf{A}^{-1}=(1-2I)/5
. For plus sign in Eq. (
106

finds that the solution is scalar-pseudoscalar too,

\mathsf{X}=\frac{53\sqrt{2}+4\sqrt{541}-22\sqrt{11+\sqrt{1082}}+\big(-51\sqrt{2}+2\sqrt{541}+4\sqrt{11+\sqrt{1082}}\big)I}{10\sqrt{11+\sqrt{1082}}}.

Insertion of
\mathsf{X}

satisfies the Riccati-Clifford equation.

9.
Conclusions

In the paper, spectral method to compute square roots of multivector (MV) in real
Clifford algebras
\mathit{Cl}_{p,q}
is proposed and investigated in detail. The method uses
isomorphism between MVs and matrices, the Bott’s periodicity table and matrix eigensystem.
The method can be applied to simple as well as general
MVs that belong to real Clifford algebra, in symbolic and numerical forms.
A number of examples are presented that illustrate how to use the algoritm in practice
as well as how to determine a domain of the existence of MV square roots.
The algorithm was also applied to real spinors of
\mathit{Cl}_{3,1}
and

\mathit{Cl}_{1,3}
algebras which are important in relativistic quantum and
spacetime theories
[
22
]
. Usually, both algebras are considered equivalent
in constructing relativity theory. However, we
have found that the equivalence breaks down if MV square root
appears. This is because a spinor in the real
\mathit{Cl}_{3,1}
algebra
has only two plus/minus roots, while in the
real
\mathit{Cl}_{1,3}
algebra there may be up to 12 square roots. We believe that presented in the paper
MV root equations may be useful in
analysis of nonlinear MV algebraic and differential equations. Also, they may replace matrices at present
used in robotics, control and other computer systems.

At present, the most popular and helpful methods to find MV square roots are
numerical ones
[
8
,
9
,
20
,
21
]
.
The first endeavour to calculate square roots in symbolic form can be found in
[
9
,
21
]
.
Finally, it should be emphasized that the proposed algorithm is based on MV spectrum and,
thus, the maximal number of roots is limited by dimension of Bott’s irreducible
matrix representation of multivector. As far as we know,
a general algorithm that predicts all possible multiple roots, including discrete and continuous ones,
was proposed for 3D geometric algebras in
[
20
]
.
