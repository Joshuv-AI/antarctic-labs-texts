Funding for Suicide Prevention and Crisis Services in US State Budgets
Authors: Jonathan Purtle, Michal Weiss, Ryan K. McBain, Jonathan H. Cantor, Jennifer L. Pomeranz, Sachini Bandara, Fernanda Montoya, Elizabeth A. Stuart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWhat were the magnitudes, trends, and correlates of funding explicitly allocated for suicide prevention and crisis services in US state budgets between fiscal years 2021 and 2026?FindingsThis repeated cross-sectional state-year panel study of 306 enacted state budgets found that per capita funding for suicide prevention and crisis services increased significantly over time (eg, state mean, $1.05 in 2021 vs $6.55 per capita in 2025), was highest in Western and Northeast states, and was more than twice as large in states that had enacted a 988 telecom fee than in states that had not.MeaningThese findings suggest that funding for suicide prevention and crisis services has increased over time, but the magnitude of amount and increase varies among states with and without 988 telecom fees.

Introduction

In 2024, an estimated 48 821 people in the US died by suicide, about 1 in 20 adults had serious thoughts of suicide, a mean of 13 857 people older than 12 years received emergency department care for a suspected suicide attempt every month, and about 1 in 10 adults reported having experienced a mental health crisis.1,2,3,4 Although many studies have characterized the financial costs of suicide and mental health crises,5,6 few have documented financial investments in suicide prevention and mental health crisis service infrastructure. Prior work has characterized federal funding for crisis services,7,8,9,10,11 but state-level total investments in suicide prevention and crisis services have not been documented.

This knowledge gap is important because states have significant autonomy in deciding whether and how to fund suicide prevention and mental health crisis service infrastructure.8 For example, the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline was launched nationally in July 2022 but states are largely responsible for funding 988 call centers and system capacity.12,13,14 As of July 2026, 12 states had enacted 988 telecom fee legislation to finance 988 operations and other crisis services in their states.15 These telecom fees are flat fees on phone bills (eg, 30¢ per cell phone line per month) regardless of 988 use, for which all revenue is dedicated to 988 and crisis services. Many other states have opted to fund 988 via general budget appropriations, rather than a dedicated fee.15,16 Prior work has documented the funding amounts generated by these fees,8,16,17 but associations between telecom fee adoption and funding for suicide prevention and crisis services have not been explored. States also vary in their suicide prevention infrastructure. For example, as of January 2025, 21 states had enacted a law that created a dedicated suicide prevention office or coordinator and 11 had a law mandating the creation of a suicide prevention taskforce.18 Quantifying state-level financial investments in suicide prevention and crisis service infrastructure is important to contextualize the results of behavioral health policy evaluations—such as those related to the effects of 98819,20—and measure factors that could mediate or moderate policy effects.21

The current study aimed to quantify funding explicitly allocated for suicide prevention and mental health crisis services in enacted state budgets, and assess differences in the magnitude of these budget allocations over time, across US Census regions, among states with varying suicide rates, and among states that had vs had not enacted 988 telecom fee legislation in the prior year.

Methods

A repeated cross-sectional state-year panel study was conducted in which all enacted state budgets for fiscal years 2021 to 2026 were analyzed. The study was carried out in accordance with recommendations for legal mapping studies22,23 and is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline. The study was not considered human participants research as per the Common Rule (45 CFR 46) because the study did not involve human participants.

Data Sources

The National Association of State Budget Officers (NASBO) website and supplemental searches of state websites were used to identify the enacted budget of every state and the District of Columbia for each fiscal year from 2021 through 2026.24 Corresponding documents were obtained for states that detail budget line items outside of budget bills (eg, “Enacted Appropriations by Line Item” documents for Illinois, “Legislatively Adopted Budget Detailed Analysis” for Oregon). This search resulted in a dataset with 306 state-year budget observations. Data were not missing for any budget years. Supplemental budgets were excluded. Capital budgets were included, but nonbudget bills and appropriations that were not listed in enacted state budgets were not included.

The full text of each enacted budget or corresponding budget document was obtained and systematically reviewed for the following terms: suicide, 988, 9-8-8, and crisis. Then, each funding line item explicitly and potentially allocated for suicide prevention and/or mental health crisis services was identified by a doctoral trainee (M.W.) and copied and pasted into an Excel sheet (Excel, version 17.0; Microsoft Corp). Next, the doctoral trainee and a doctoral-level policy researcher with expertise in legal mapping (J.P.) collaboratively reviewed each individual funding line item. The inclusion criterion was that funding was explicitly dedicated to programs, services, or system infrastructure for suicide prevention or crisis services, the latter determined by assessing alignment with components of the US Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration’s National Guidelines for a Behavioral Health Coordinated System of Crisis Care (eg, crisis hotlines, mobile crisis teams, crisis stabilization centers, and related infrastructure).25

Decisions about ambiguous line items were made through discussions between the 2 coders in consultation with a public health lawyer (J.L.P.) as needed. State websites were also reviewed to obtain additional information about named programs to inform determinations about whether they met inclusion criteria. Examples of funding include, but are not limited to: crisis call centers staffing, implementation of suicide prevention programs and messaging campaigns, capital projects such as construction of crisis stabilization facilities, supports for survivors of suicide loss, and investments in crisis system needs assessment, evaluation, and technology infrastructure. eTable 1 in Supplement 1 lists all 1150 budget items included in the final analytic dataset.

Funding for crisis interventions that were not explicitly related to mental health services or prevention (eg, 911, families experiencing financial crisis, rape crisis) were excluded. Funding for mental health services in general (eg, appropriations to behavioral health agencies) was excluded if there was no explicit mention of suicide prevention and crisis services. Similarly, funding items were excluded if the specific amount of funding for suicide prevention and crisis services was not specified (eg, a budget item that dedicated one funding amount for suicide and opioid overdose prevention but did not specify the proportion of the funds dedicated to each of the 2 issues). However, instances where budgets included terms such as “funding up to” an amount for suicide prevention and crisis services were included at the maximum amount because it reflected the highest possible investment in the suicide prevention and crisis service item. Budget reappropriations were excluded because they were accounted for in the prior budget year. Given all of the exclusion criteria, the funding amounts used in the analyses are likely underestimates of state-level investments in suicide prevention and crisis services.

Variables

Line item funding values were summed to calculate the total of suicide prevention and crisis service funding for every state each year. When states with biennial budgets did not differentiate between first-year and second-year funding, the biennial amounts were annualized.

The primary dependent variable was state per capita annual funding explicitly for suicide prevention and crisis services. This was calculated by dividing each state’s total annual suicide prevention and crisis service funding amount by the state’s estimated population size for the calendar year when the fiscal year began (eg, 2025 population data for a 2026 fiscal year that started in July 2025). US Census Bureau data were used for state population estimates. The secondary dependent variable was the percentage of each state’s total annual budget that was explicitly allocated for suicide prevention and crisis services, using NASBO to identify total annual budget information. This variable was conceptualized as an indicator of the extent to which suicide prevention and crisis service funding was a priority, which might not be captured by the per capita measure because states vary in their overall per capita spending.

Independent variables were year, state US Census region, state age-adjusted suicide death rate quartile rank, and state 988 telecom fee enactment status. State suicide rate data were obtained from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s WONDER website, and every state was assigned to the quartile rank of its age-adjusted suicide death rate for each calendar year from 2019 through 2024. Quartile rank was used for ease of interpretation. The calendar year state quartile ranks were then linked with states with a 1-year lag prior to the start of the fiscal year (eg, 2024 suicide data used for a 2026 fiscal year that started in July 2025). This variable was conceptualized as a measure of relative state-level need for suicide prevention and crisis service funding.

State 988 telecom fee enactment status was a dichotomous variable indicating whether each state had enacted 988 telecom fee legislation in the calendar year prior to the start of the fiscal year (eg, 2024 telecom fee status used for a 2026 fiscal year that started in July 2025). Data on 988 telecom fee enactment status were obtained from the National Alliance on Mental Illness.15 The 1-year lag period was used to account for the time needed for fee revenue to be collected before it is allocated.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were conducted using R, version 4.5.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing). Total funding, state-level mean and median per capita funding, and state-level mean and median percentage budget allocation variables were calculated for the entire study period and stratified by independent variables. Linear regression models with state-clustered standard errors assessed the significance of differences in state-level per capita and percentage budget funding means by year, US Census region, suicide death rate quartile, and 988 telecom fee status, as well as the significance of a temporal trend. State-clustered standard errors were used to account for correlation induced by repeated observations within states. Generalized estimating equation models were used in sensitivity analyses. All P values were from 2-sided tests and results were deemed statistically significant at P < .05. Because there is wide variation in how states structure their budget documents and specify line item funding, comparisons between individual states were not made.

Results

In 306 enacted state budgets for fiscal years 2021 to 2026, a total of $7 245 066 907 was explicitly allocated for suicide prevention and crisis services. During this period, the mean (SD) per capita budget amount allocated for suicide prevention and crisis services was $3.63 ($8.23) (median, $0.87 [IQR, $0.05-$4.31]) and the mean (SD) annual percentage of the total state budget allocated was 0.054% (0.111%) (median, 0.014% [IQR, 0.001%-0.066%]). In the 306 state-year observations, $0 was explicitly allocated for suicide prevention and crisis services in 66 (21.6%) of the state-years.

Annual mean state per capita funding for suicide prevention and crisis services increased significantly during the study period (B [SE]: $0.96 [$0.31]; 95% CI, $0.34-$1.57; P = .003) (Figure 1), as did annual percentage budget allocations (B [SE]: 0.010 [0.004] percentage points; 95% CI, 0.004-0.021 percentage points; P = .005) (Figure 2). For example, annual mean (SD) state per capita funding increased from a low of $1.05 ($2.04) in 2021 to a high of $6.55 ($12.76) in 2025 (Table), while mean (SD) state percentage budget allocations increased from 0.022% (0.046%) to 0.079% (0.130%) during this period. Median values showed a similar pattern.

Allocations Explicitly for Suicide Prevention and Crisis Services in US State Budgets (Fiscal Years 2021-2026)

For the combined period between 2021 and 2026, the mean (SD) state-level per capita allocations for suicide prevention and crisis services were highest in states in the Western US Census region ($7.19 [$14.52]) and lowest in states in the Southern ($2.12 [$3.56]) and Midwestern ($2.07 [$3.12]) regions, as were the mean percentage budget allocations for suicide prevention and crisis services, although these differences were not statistically significant after adjusting for state-level clustering (Table).

No significant association was observed between a state’s age-adjusted suicide death rate quartile for the calendar year prior to the start of the fiscal year and its mean state-level per capita or percentage budget allocation for suicide prevention and crisis services. (Table).

The mean (SD) state-level per capita allocation for suicide prevention and crisis services was more than twice as large in states that had enacted a 988 telecom fee in the calendar year prior to the start of the fiscal year than in states that had not yet enacted a 988 telecom fee ($7.73 [$5.32] vs $3.13 [$8.38]; P = .003) (Table). The mean (SD) state-level percentage budget allocation was also significantly higher in states that had enacted a 988 telecom fee than in those that had not (0.089% [0.061%] vs 0.050% [0.115%]; P = .04). Generalized estimating equation models yielded results similar to the linear regression models with state-clustered standard errors that were used in the primary analyses (eTable 2 and eTable 3 in Supplement 2).

Discussion

State budget funding explicitly allocated for suicide prevention and crisis services has increased sizably between fiscal years 2021 and 2026. This finding likely reflects increased federal investments in crisis service infrastructure7,8,9,10,11,26 as well as growing policymaker and public concern about suicide and mental health crises. For example, prior research documented increases in the number of bills passed in state legislatures that mentioned suicide and the volume of social media posts by state legislators that mention suicide.27,28 Our study can help contextualize the results of evaluations of suicide prevention and crisis service policy initiatives. For example, prior studies have documented regional and between-state variation in the availability of crisis services26,29,30,31 and the extent to which the launch of 988 may have prevented suicide deaths.19 The study’s datasets can potentially be used to help explain such variations and the degree to which funding items and/or amounts mediate or moderate policy effects.21

Per capita investments in suicide prevention and crisis service funding have generally been largest among states in the Western US Census region. This regional difference in funding might be influenced by regional differences in political partisanship. Populations in Western states generally lean more Democratic than Republican,32 and prior research among US adults33,34 and state legislators28,35 has shown that attitudes toward 988 and crisis hotlines are more favorable among Democrats than Republicans.

Although the observed increases in funding indicate opportunities to strengthen population-based suicide prevention and crisis service infrastructure, the magnitude of funding could be interpreted as relatively small. For example, the highest annual state-level mean funding amount observed was $6.55 per capita, in 2025; this is less than 5% of what the Department of Veterans Affairs allocated for suicide prevention among veterans in 2024 (about $177 per veteran).36 Although the suicide death rate among veterans is more than double that of nonveterans,37 the magnitude of the difference in funding may provide a rationale to further increase funding for suicide prevention and crisis services in state budgets to better align with the epidemiology of suicide.

The finding that states that enacted 988 telecom fee legislation allocated significantly more funding per capita for suicide prevention and crisis services, relative to states that did not enact 988 telecom fee legislation, suggests that such fees are a promising strategy to enhance crisis service infrastructure.16 This finding is important, as prior research with 988 administrators and call center leaders has repeatedly found that insufficient funding is a major impediment to maximizing the impact of 988.38,39,40,41 Telecom fees for 988 could be a sustainable and critical strategy to earmark funding for suicide prevention and crisis services as states contend with recent fiscal challenges resulting from federal policy changes (eg, new administrative costs related to implementation of the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program and Medicaid work requirements).42

Limitations

This study has some limitations. It was limited to state funding explicitly allocated for suicide prevention and crisis services in state budgets. City and county funding was not included, nor was state funding for mental health services in general or Medicaid funding for crisis services, which varies across states.8,9,43 As such, the study likely underestimates the true extent of state-level investments in suicide prevention and crisis services and the magnitude of these underestimates may vary across states.

States also vary in terms of the specificity of spending categories within their budgets, which limits the ability to make comparisons of funding between individual states or characterize funding for specific suicide prevention programs and crisis services. The study focused on funds budgeted for suicide prevention and crisis services, as opposed to expenditures. Expenditure amounts are often less than budgeted amounts and future research could focus on expenditures for suicide prevention and crisis services. Finally, state suicide rates were lagged prior to fiscal years and thus the study is not capable of exploring associations between funding for suicide prevention and crisis services and subsequent changes in suicide death rates. This represents an area for future work.

Conclusions

This repeated cross-sectional state-year panel study of 306 enacted state budgets for fiscal years 2021 to 2026 found that funding explicitly dedicated for suicide prevention and crisis services increased significantly, according to both per capita and percentage budget allocation measures. State enactment of a 988 telecom fee was significantly associated with higher levels of dedicated funding, suggesting that such fees could be a promising legislative approach to finance the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline and other suicide prevention and crisis service initiatives.
