The mediating role of perceived social support on loneliness and depression in community-dwelling Nepalese older adults
Authors: Sital Gautam, Anju Poudel, Roshani Agrawal Khatry, Rachana Mishra
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLoneliness has been identified as a significant independent predictor of depression among older adults. Social support has been reported to play a protective role against both loneliness and depression in older adults. However, few studies to date have investigated the three constructs concurrently and the mediating role of Perceived Social Support (PSS) on the relationship between loneliness and depression in this age group. The current study aimed to assess the relationship between loneliness and depression among community-dwelling Nepalese older adults based on the mediating role of PSS.MethodsThis descriptive cross-sectional research study included 367 community-dwelling older adults from a randomly selected ward in Kathmandu, Nepal. The University of California, Los Angeles Loneliness Scale, Geriatric Depression Scale, and Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support were used. Correlation analysis, regression analysis, and the PROCESS 4.1 macro developed by Hayes with a bootstrapping procedure were used to test the mediating effect of the PSS on the relationship between loneliness and depression.ResultsLoneliness was negatively associated with the PSS (β = -0.405; 95% CI: −0.473, -0.337; P < 0.001), and the PSS was positively associated with depression (β = 0.057; 95% CI: 0.014, 0.099; P = 0.009). The direct effect of loneliness on depression was positive and significant (β = 0.151; 95% CI: 0.117, 0.184; P < 0.001), whereas the indirect effect of loneliness on depression through the PSS was negative (path c; β = -0.023, 95% CI: -0.041, -0.005). The results indicated that the PSS partially mediated the relationship between loneliness and depression.ConclusionThese findings suggest that interventions focusing on reducing loneliness and fostering social support may be able to lessen depressive symptoms in older adults. By addressing these factors, healthcare providers and carers can promote the mental health of older adults.

Background

Older adults, people aged 60 years and above, often encounter multiple age-related changes, namely, relationship losses, functional declines, medical illnesses, loss of social roles, and reduced social networks. These changes can increase older adults’ susceptibility to loneliness [1, 2]. Loneliness refers to a subjective experience of a lack of desired quantity and quality of personal, social, or community relationships [3, 4]. Loneliness can be social—a lack of adequate social networks and emotional—the absence of intimate relationships that provide a sense of attachment [4, 5]. Internationally, the estimated prevalence of loneliness among older adults ranges from 19.5 to 60% [6–8]. This finding is concerning since loneliness is strongly linked to depression among older adults. According to the WHO [9], depression is a mental disorder characterized by sadness, loss of interest or pleasure, low self-worth or guilt, sleep or appetite disturbances, fatigue, and poor concentration.

A growing body of evidence suggests a significant association between loneliness and depression in older adults [10, 11]. Loneliness has also been identified as an independent risk factor for depression in this age group, controlling for other covariates [12, 13]. Furthermore, longitudinal studies have confirmed that loneliness is a major predictor of depression in older adults [14, 15]. A twelve-year longitudinal study (2004–2017) conducted with 4,211 community-dwelling older adults in the UK revealed that higher baseline loneliness scores were significantly associated with higher depression symptom severity scores among older adults. This study also revealed that loneliness is responsible for 18% of depression among older adults [15].

In eliminating loneliness and reducing depression in older adults, it is vital that gerontologists understand the underlying mechanism between these two complex constructs. Hawkley and Cacioppo’s [16] loneliness model explains the potential mechanism for the development of depression in lonely older adults. According to Hawkley and Cacioppo [16], loneliness triggers the feeling of social threat or danger, which produces cognitive biases such as retaining more negative social information and expecting more negative social interactions. These negative cognitive schemas drive lonely people to actively distance themselves from other people in society, leading to feelings of hostility, pessimism, stress, low self-esteem, and anxiety [16]. The model suggests that Perceived Social Support (PSS) could be an important factor impacting loneliness and subsequent depression among older adults. PSS refers to how people view friends, family, and other people as resources that can offer them financial, emotional, and general support when they need it [17, 18]. Previous studies have provided strong evidence of the independent relationship between social support and loneliness and depression in older adults. Social support can be emotional, informational, appraisal, or instrumental [19, 20]. Evidence suggests that older adults with more social support report less loneliness, irrespective of the different indicators of social support [21, 22]. Similarly, studies have shown that an increase in social support leads to a decrease in the level of depression among older adults [23, 24]. Social support has been reported to play a protective role against both loneliness and depression in older adults. While social support can independently influence both loneliness and depression, the current study intends to add to the existing knowledge by exploring how social support mediates the relationship between loneliness and depression.

Despite the growing interest in this area, few studies to date have investigated the three constructs concurrently and the mediating role of PSS in the relationship between loneliness and depression in this age group [25, 26]. Existing studies have recruited older adults from different sociocultural contexts than those in Nepal. The context of older adults impacts their social network type, which can influence PSS and loneliness and, in turn, mental health [27, 28]. There is a need to embrace the unique voice of older adults from different religious, social, cultural, and ethnic backgrounds. It is especially significant in a country such as Nepal, where the prevalence of loneliness (68.7%) and depression (60.6%) among older adults is increasing [10, 29, 30]. In addition, both loneliness and depression have been reported to predict negative health outcomes among older adults [11, 31]. Therefore, this study aimed to answer two research questions: What is the relationship between PSS, loneliness, and depression among older adults? How does PSS mediate the relationship between loneliness and depression among older adults?

We hypothesized that the PSS would play a mediating role between loneliness and depression. Consistent with the vast majority of previous research in this area and theoretical frameworks, we considered loneliness as an independent variable, PSS as a mediator variable, and depression as a dependent variable [25, 26, 32]. We proposed that the role of PSS as a mediator helps explain the process by which loneliness may lead to depression, specifically how the presence or absence of PSS may affect the degree to which loneliness contributes to depressive symptoms. When individuals experience loneliness but have adequate social support, the negative emotional impact of loneliness may be diminished, thereby reducing the risk of developing depression. Conversely, when social support is lacking, loneliness is more likely to lead to depression. The protective role of PSS highlights its potential to not only reduce depressive symptoms directly but also mitigate the negative impact of loneliness, which is often a precursor to depression in older adults. By examining this mediation, we can better understand how to intervene in the loneliness-depression relationship. This can inform health care as well as social workers in designing effective preventive measures to combat loneliness and depression among older adults.

Methods

Aim

To assess the relationship between loneliness and depression among older adults based on the mediating role of perceived social support.

Study design and participants

This descriptive cross-sectional study was carried out over a period of two and a half years [Jan 2021-July 2023] in a randomly selected ward of Kathmandu, the capital and largest city of Nepal. The sample size was calculated using the formula n = z2pq/d2. where z = the value of the normal variate, p = proportion of the population with the characteristic of interest derived from the prevalence rate of the dependent variable (depression) supported by past studies, q = 1-p, and d = allowable error. With a z of 1.96 at the 95% CI, a p of 0.606 [25], a q of 0.394, and a d of 5% at the 95% CI, the estimated sample size was (1.96)2 × 0.606 × 0.394/(0.05)2 = 366.89 = 367. The inclusion criteria were older adults aged 60 years and older and not living with cognitive impairment or having an acute episode of ill health. Adults aged 60 years and over are referred to as older adults as per the Senior Citizen Act of Nepal. Hence, this age group was chosen. Similarly, the possible existence of cognitive impairment in older adults were determined by asking their family members before data collection.

Multistage (3-step) random sampling was employed to obtain a sample of older adults in Kathmandu. One ward was randomly selected from thirty-two wards in Kathmandu. Then, the total number of toles in the selected ward was listed. From that list, six toles (approx. 65–80 households in each tole) were selected randomly. All households in the selected toles were listed. The desired sample size, i.e., 367 older adults from the randomly selected households, was included in the study. Eight older adults refused to participate in the study due to time constraints. When the randomly selected household had no older adults who met the inclusion criteria, another household was selected randomly, and the process continued until the desired sample size, i.e., 367, was achieved. Similarly, in the case of multiple eligible participants in one household, only one (senior most) older adult was included in the study.

The Ethical Review Board (ERB) of the Nepal Health Research Council (NHRC) provided ethical approval for the study (Reference number: 30/2021P). Written approval was obtained from the selected ward. The participants received information sheets with study details and provided written informed consent before data collection. Participants were assured that participation in the study was voluntary, that they could withdraw at any time, and that non participation would not impact them in any way. Privacy was maintained by collecting data separately in a quiet place within the premises of their houses. Data access was limited to the research team.

Measures

Sociodemographic information

It consisted of 13 items related to sociodemographic and health information, such as age, sex, religion, marital status, children, family size, educational level, employment status, living arrangement, presence of health problems, self-efficacy, income, and social network size.

University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) loneliness scale- version 3.

It is a 20-item scale in which each item can be answered on a 4-point Likert scale, where 1 indicates ‘never,’ 2 denotes ‘rarely,’ 3 signifies ‘sometimes,’ and 4 specifies ‘often.’ The total score was obtained from the sum of the scores of the twenty items (20 to 80). The reverse score was used for nine items. Higher scores indicated increased intensity of loneliness [33].

Geriatric depression scale (GDS)

It is a 30-item scale in which each item can be answered as ‘yes’ or ‘no.’ The total score was obtained from the sum of the scores of the thirty items (0 to 30). Scores of 0–9 indicated ‘normal,’ scores of 10–19 indicated ‘mild depression,’ and scores of 20–30 indicated ‘severe depression’ [34].

The multidimensional scale of perceived social support (MSPSS)

It is a 12-item scale that measures the PSS scores of family, friends, and significant others. Each item could be answered on a 7-point Likert scale, where 1 indicates ‘very strongly disagree’ and 7 denotes ‘very strongly agree.’ The total scores ranged from 12 to 84. A higher score indicated greater PSS [35].

Validity and reliability

The UCLA Loneliness Scale, GDS, and MSPSS are widely used tools worldwide, including Nepal. The Nepali versions of the tools are available and validated in different studies. The reported Cronbach’s alpha values for the UCLA Loneliness Scale, GDS, and MSPSS tools in Nepal are 0.813, 0.89, and 0.89, respectively, indicating good internal consistency [10, 36, 37]. Pretesting of the instrument was conducted among 10% of the total sample, 36 community-dwelling older adults who were not part of the final study. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the UCLA Loneliness Scale, GDS, and MSPSS tools were 0.80, 0.86, and 0.84, respectively.

Data analysis

The sociodemographic and health-related information of the participants was analyzed using descriptive statistics. Means and standard deviations are used to present continuous variables, and categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Bivariate correlation analysis was conducted to assess the correlation between the study variables. After controlling for age, sex, religion, marital status, children, family size, educational level, employment status, living arrangement, presence of health problems, self-efficacy, income, and social network size, a mediation model was constructed. The Process 4.1 macro proposed by Hayes (Model 4) was used to test the mediating effect of PSS on the relationship between loneliness and depression. To estimate the size and significance of the mediation effect, Hayes’ PROCESS macro model 4 (37) with a bootstrapping procedure was used. To test the significance of the indirect effect, a five thousand 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (CIs) were used. An indirect effect was considered significant at the 0.05 level if the 95% CI did not include zero. The statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS V27 and AMOS 17.0 programs.

Most of them were Hindu by religion, and 36% had secondary-level education. More than half (56.1%) were unemployed (Table 1). Most of them (90.7%) had one or more types of health problems (Table 2).

Mediation analysis

Loneliness was negatively associated with the PSS (β = -0.405; 95% CI: −0.473, -0.337; P < 0.001), and the PSS was positively associated with depression (β = 0.057; 95% CI: 0.014, 0.099; P = 0.009). The direct effect of loneliness on depression was positive and significant (β = 0.151; 95% CI: 0.117, 0.184; P < 0.001), whereas the indirect effect of loneliness on depression through the PSS was negative (path c; β = -0.023, 95% CI: -0.041, -0.005). The indirect effect is considered statistically significant because the confidence interval does not include zero. Overall, the model suggests that, on average, for each one-unit increase in loneliness, depression is expected to decrease by approximately 0.023 units through the pathway of PSS (Fig. 1). As hypothesized, the PSS partially mediated the relationship between loneliness and depression.

Discussion

The current study aimed to assess the relationship between loneliness and depression among community-dwelling Nepalese older adults based on the mediating role of PSS. An important finding of the study is that loneliness was positively correlated with depression among older adults. This finding is similar to those reported by the vast majority of previous studies [10, 11]. This study also found that the direct effect of loneliness on depression was positive and significant. Existing theoretical models suggest that loneliness can have negative social, cognitive, and biological effects that can eventually result in depression [11, 16]. There is evidence linking loneliness to negative social interaction perceptions, negative cognitive schemas, anticipation of social threat or rejection, elevated stress levels, decreased self-esteem, and biological impacts on inflammation and the stress response [11, 38]. These findings, in turn, may represent a plausible mechanism explaining the correlation between loneliness and depression. Indeed, a number of population-based studies have also established that one of the major contributing factors to depression in older adults is loneliness [12, 15]. Therefore, assessing and addressing loneliness in older adults is vital. The changing sociocultural context of Nepal also suggests that loneliness be assessed and addressed in older adults. Nepal is witnessing a major sociocultural transition along with a demographic shift. The traditional filial piety, the obligation to take care of older adults, is fading in the younger generation. Conventional extended families are being replaced by nuclear families. The role of women, the primary caregivers of older adults in the past, has shifted. In addition, youth migration is increasing [39]. These factors can lead to loneliness and eventually depression among Nepalese older adults.

Another significant finding of the study was that loneliness was negatively correlated with PSS and that PSS was positively correlated with depression. Similar to the findings of the current study, previous studies have also concluded that social support is linked to lower levels of loneliness in older adults [21, 40]. However, an unexpected finding was that older adults with more PSS reported greater levels of depression. The reason for this unexpected finding might be the use of a quantitative approach that is highly focused on the extrinsic nature of social support, which might not fully represent Nepalese older adults’ cultural perceptions or expectations of social support. In addition, the PSS might have been enough to reduce older adults’ sense of loneliness but depression. Indeed, it is vital to comprehensively explore the concept of PSS in the context of older Nepalese adults.

Most importantly, the results of the mediation analysis indicated that the PSS partially mediated the relationship between loneliness and depression. The mediating role of PSS between loneliness and depression in older adults has also been reported previously [41, 42]. These findings suggest that interventions that target social support and loneliness reduction may be able to lessen depressive symptoms in this population. Indeed, a number of studies have shown the effectiveness of group reminiscence therapies, psychological support groups, family-based therapies, video conversations, phone calls, and community services in maintaining and broadening the social sphere, connectivity, and engagement in older adults, in turn reducing their loneliness and depression [41–43].

Despite numerous strengths of this study, the cross-sectional design of this study means that the results should be interpreted with caution. Similarly, the coexistence of these two variables may account for significant correlations rather than a cause-and-effect relationship. A longitudinal study would provide more reliable results. In this study, loneliness, the PSS, and depression were the only three variables. For a comprehensive understanding, future research could examine the relationships between additional variables. The use of quantitative approach might not have fully represented Nepalese older adults’ cultural perceptions or expectations of social support, which could be the focus of the future research.

Conclusion

The study revealed that the PSS partially mediates the relationship between loneliness and depression. The results imply that treatments focusing on reducing loneliness and fostering social support may be able to lessen depressive symptoms in older adults. By addressing these factors, healthcare providers and carers can promote the mental health of older adults. Care for older adults should transcend physical limitations and take social factors into consideration. To lessen depression in older adults, regular monitoring for loneliness and needs-based care is vital.
