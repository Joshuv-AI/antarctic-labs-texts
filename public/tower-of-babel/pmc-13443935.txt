Interpretable sentiment-aware transformer-based model for individual log anomaly detection in distributed systems using word-level explanations
Authors: Andrés H. Catalán, Rodrigo A. Carrasco, Gonzalo A. Ruz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Logs are a primary source of information for monitoring the health, security, and performance of large-scale distributed systems such as cloud services and data centers. However, the volume and variability of log messages make manual inspection impractical and challenge traditional anomaly detection methods. In this work, we propose an interpretable, sentiment-aware transformer model for detecting anomalies in individual log entries. Our approach builds on the Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers with In-Task Pre-Training and Fine-Tuning (BERT-ITPT-FiT) architecture, which leverages the natural-language content of logs while remaining parser-free and robust to format changes. To make the model’s decisions transparent, we combine it with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and introduce a mapping from subword attributions to word-level importance scores, enabling operators to see which terms contribute most to a normal or anomalous classification. We evaluate the method on multiple publicly available system log datasets, reporting results separately for in-domain (training and testing on the same system) and out-of-domain (testing on previously unseen systems) scenarios. The proposed model achieves F1-scores up to 99.96% in-domain and 96.97% out-of-domain, indicating strong generalization across systems, and maintains an average processing throughput of approximately 3,750 log messages per second, supporting large-scale deployment. Beyond raw performance, our results show that scalability, semantic robustness, and interpretability are critical for building reliable anomaly detection tools for modern distributed infrastructures.

Introduction

Highly complex, distributed systems generate massive datasets, logs, and other data essential for effective operational monitoring and efficient maintenance. Unlike time-series data, logs are semi-structured texts produced by logging statements embedded within software source code. Over the past decade, software logs have become crucial for ensuring the reliability of numerous systems, as they often represent the sole source of data capturing software runtime information1. These logs are essential for gaining insights into system performance; however, interpreting the vast amount of data poses a significant challenge for expert operators. As these systems become more sophisticated, the diversity and volume of logs increase, rendering manual examination impractical. Therefore, automated methods for detecting log anomalies are crucial in preventing system failures, which can result in substantial losses in revenue, time, and performance2.

Anomaly detection, as a key component of log mining, involves identifying unusual or atypical patterns in a system that deviate from expected behavior in log data. These anomalies typically signal potential errors, defects, or failures within software systems3. In general, two widely used approaches in the literature for detecting anomalies in logs are keyword-based (individual anomalies) and sequence-based anomalies2,4. This study focuses on the former, as analyzing logs individually enables direct identification of critical events without relying on surrounding context, making it particularly suitable for real-time anomaly detection in large-scale distributed systems.

Log data is generated continuously in real-world scenarios and must be analyzed in real time. This dynamic nature, characterized by evolving system utilization, changing log templates, and the emergence of new events, highlights the practicality of individual log analysis. On the other hand, sequential anomaly detection assumes that events occur in a fixed order, an assumption that can fail in real-world environments where log data is frequently lost or incomplete, further disrupting the event flow5,6. When we use an individual approach, the natural language-based textual information in each log event becomes crucial for analysis. Building on this, our method leverages sentiment analysis to evaluate the subjectivity of each log event, linking textual polarity (e.g., positive or negative sentiment) to system behavior. For example, as shown in Fig. 1, if a log event contains negative words like “critical”, “failed”, or “damage”, it is likely indicative of abnormal behavior or an anomaly; otherwise, it should reflect normal operation7. In this case, since there is no neutral or uncertain opinion in the logs and negative messages can be clearly distinguished from others, indicating normal operation, the task naturally becomes a binary classification problem. In this context, sentiment analysis should not be understood as the analysis of human opinions or emotions, but rather as the identification of linguistic polarity in technical log messages, where negatively oriented expressions are associated with anomalous or degraded system states.Fig. 1Illustration of log messages linking negativity to anomalies and positivity to normality.

However, our study identified several key elements that must be considered when using sentiment analysis in event logs. First, semantics are fundamental, as other contextual words often accompany negative words. Therefore, sometimes, the mere presence of a word associated with a specific component or element of a system in an event log can indicate an anomaly. Second, preprocessing has a significant impact on classification performance. Using overly aggressive cleaning tools can lead to the loss of crucial textual information, which, if not considered, can result in misclassification. Third, combining simple vectorization techniques with machine learning classifiers appears to be sufficient for achieving good classification results in a single system (or dataset). Nonetheless, this changes when the model attempts to classify logs from different systems (data it has never seen before). Therefore, the sophistication of word embedding with pre-trained models and the choice of classification technique are both crucial when building an architecture that can achieve a good level of generalization. Fourth, although the previous statement is true, the generalization capability will always depend on the diversity of the training data relative to the various systems being analyzed. In other words, it will significantly depend on the textual similarity of the event logs from one system to another. Ultimately, classifying whether an event log is anomalous appears to be insufficient for system operators. It is also essential to understand the severity of an event log compared to others. Therefore, explainability and opening the “black box”is crucial when using classifiers, especially those based on neural networks. In event logs, some information may be more critical than others, making it essential to differentiate between them.

Our work builds on the state-of-the-art findings from two recent studies: Pylogsentiment7 and TransSentLog2. Both studies utilize deep learning models for detecting individual log anomalies through sentiment analysis. We chose these works for three main reasons. First, the use of deep learning for log-based anomaly detection has recently gained traction, driven by its proven ability to surpass traditional machine learning methods in fields like image and speech recognition8. These models excel at capturing complex patterns and semantic relationships within unstructured data, making them particularly well-suited for analyzing raw logs, where textual information often contains subtle contextual cues critical for detecting anomalies5. Second, they represent recent advancements in the field and have been acknowledged in contemporary research for various purposes. Unlike many state-of-the-art approaches that focus on sequential log anomaly detection, modeling temporal dependencies across multiple log lines, PyLogSentiment and TransSentLog are explicitly designed for individual log anomaly classification. This distinction is crucial, as our work also targets the analysis of log messages at the line level. Consequently, these two models offer the most appropriate and relevant baselines for a fair and task-aligned comparison. For instance, Pylogsentiment has been recently discussed in9–15, while TransSentLog has been highlighted in16–19 within recent studies. Third, these works address challenges similar to those tackled by our approach and test their models on publicly available datasets, enabling direct comparisons against established performance benchmarks. However, although these two contributions represent significant advances in individual log anomaly detection, they also exhibit certain limitations that our method aims to address. On the one hand, TransSentLog makes an important advancement by not relying on Nerlogparser20, unlike Pylogsentiment. Nonetheless, its text cleaning relies on extracting words “as is”, which can lead to the loss of acronyms that hold significant meaning in technical data like logs.

Additionally, although TransSentLog significantly improves preprocessing speed compared to its predecessor, its extraction philosophy suggests that its speed can be further enhanced. Second, the architecture of TransSentLog relies on Global Vectors (GloVe)21, a widely used word embedding technique that effectively processes text in logs and achieves promising results. However, other embedding methods, particularly pre-trained models, have demonstrated superior performance in sentiment analysis tasks22. This fact is significant when the model needs to be exposed to data it has never seen before (from different systems). Third, although TransSentLog utilizes Integrated Gradients23 for interpreting predictions, explainability techniques remain scarce in the literature, limiting efforts to understand model behavior and compare interpretations across various approaches5. This fact highlights the need for our work to address this gap by exploring and advancing explainability methods in log anomaly detection using deep learning.

To address the previously mentioned limitations, this study proposes an approach for detecting individual log anomalies based on sentiment analysis characterized by the following key elements. Like its predecessor, TransSentLog, it does not require a log parser during the preprocessing phase, thereby improving computational performance and reducing textual loss by introducing a character extraction philosophy instead of word extraction. A word embedding obtained from a pre-trained Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) model24 is also employed, enhancing the classifier’s ability to understand textual context and improving its generalization capabilities. Finally, a custom neural network is connected to the output of the pre-trained model, performing fine-tuning for the sentiment analysis task and enabling the interpretation of the network through SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values25. The use of this network allows for greater flexibility in adapting the architecture to the specific characteristics of log anomaly detection tasks, ensuring better alignment with domain-specific requirements. Moreover, this design choice facilitates the integration of interpretability methods, such as SHAP, which traditionally operate at the attribute level. We developed a novel approach that translates token-level outputs into word-level importance scores, addressing the challenge of directly applying this method to textual data processed by BERT-based architectures. To overcome the limitations of subword tokenization, our method reconstructs semantically coherent words from subword tokens and aggregates their corresponding SHAP values. This strategy bridges the gap between tokenized representations and meaningful, interpretable insights, enabling a more faithful understanding of how specific words influence model predictions. We consider this step essential for robust and transparent individual log anomaly detection.

In this sense, the conceptual novelty of this work does not lie merely in replacing a previous classifier with BERT or in adding an explainability layer. Rather, it lies in combining parser-free full-text log processing, contextualized modeling for cross-system generalization, and a post-hoc reconstruction procedure that maps sub-token SHAP attributions into semantically coherent word-level explanations, thereby making anomaly detection in individual logs both robust and operationally interpretable.

Related work

Anomaly detection in log data is critical in managing complex systems, as it involves identifying patterns that deviate from expected behavior. Such anomalies often indicate potential faults, failures, or security breaches that could compromise the normal operation of software systems3,4. Generally, most of the literature has focused on the classification of anomalies in log sequences, relying on diverse neural network-based and sequential representation learning methodologies6,26–41; however, log anomaly detection based on individual log events and sentiment analysis has received relatively little attention. Developed initially to categorize opinions in social media and product reviews by classifying text based on polarity42–48, this approach, when applied to log data, facilitates the identification of textual patterns that suggest abnormal system behavior, thereby proving to be a valuable tool for detecting anomalies2.

Several studies have explored various methodologies to enhance detection accuracy and interpretability in the field of log anomaly detection, utilizing sentiment analysis. Studiawan & Payne49 introduced a novel approach that combines sentiment and aspect terms within log events using two attention mechanisms (context attention and content attention) alongside a GRU network. This architecture was designed to classify sentiments within log events, offering a detailed analysis of the textual patterns that may signal abnormal behavior. While innovative in its focus on attention mechanisms, their method is highly dependent on the quality of the attention modules and the effectiveness of the GRU network in capturing long-range dependencies in text, which may be challenging when dealing with vast datasets. In a similar manner, Zhang et al.15 developed SentiLog, a system that extracts logging statements from various source codes of parallel file systems. This approach aimed to create a more generalized model by utilizing a wide range of log sources. The use of sentiment analysis in this context allowed for the detection of anomalies across different file systems. However, the practicality of this approach is limited by the need to access and extract logging statements from source code. This task may not always be feasible in real-world scenarios. In a related study, Studiawan & Payne7 developed Pylogsentiment, which integrates sentiment analysis with individual log events for anomaly detection. Their approach begins with a log parser, Nerlogparser20, to extract structured information from log events. The message component, which conveys sentiment information, is then transformed into numerical vectors using GloVe word embeddings, enhancing the representation of the log events. Pylogsentiment utilizes a GRU network to classify events as normal or abnormal; however, the log parser can significantly impact its efficiency. Lastly, Pham & Lee2 introduced TransSentLog, which stands out by applying a Transformer architecture coupled with sentiment analysis for log anomaly detection. This method uses individual log events, focusing on the interpretability of the model’s decisions through Integrated Gradients23. The Transformer’s ability to capture context and relationships within the log text offers a powerful tool for anomaly detection, particularly in identifying nuanced patterns that simpler models might overlook. However, this architecture relies on GloVe word embeddings, which, while effective at handling log text and producing good results, have been outperformed by other word embedding techniques in sentiment analysis tasks.

As discussed in previous studies on log anomaly detection using sentiment analysis2,7,15,49, various word embedding techniques such as GloVe and transformer-based models have been explored. In our approach, we adopt BERT due to its bidirectional encoding and its ability to capture deep contextual semantics from both directions, which is particularly valuable for nuanced log interpretation. This feature is particularly beneficial for textual classification tasks where the meaning of tokens often depends on the surrounding context24. Nevertheless, it is essential to note that, in the case of log data, messages are typically short and repetitive, which can reduce the apparent need for deep semantic modeling. Indeed, our experiments confirm that even simple TF-IDF representations, when paired with robust machine learning models, can yield strong performance, even in out-of-domain scenarios.

Despite this, we argue that the semantic richness offered by a BERT-based model makes it particularly well-suited for our context, where both classification accuracy and model explainability are essential. This trade-off is especially relevant considering our explainability framework relies on SHAP values, which directly benefit from the contextualized embeddings produced by a pretrained model like BERT. The interpretability of the model is thus strengthened by its ability to encode nuanced token relationships, enabling token-level attributions that are faithful to semantic meaning. While distilled or specialized variants such as DistilBERT50, RoBERTa51, XLNet52, and CodeBERT53 offer advantages in terms of efficiency, training robustness, or domain adaptation, these models are derived from or inspired by BERT and typically achieve such gains through architectural simplifications or targeted modifications. For instance, DistilBERT improves inference time by reducing the number of layers, while RoBERTa removes the next-sentence prediction objective and employs dynamic masking. XLNet introduces permutation-based pretraining to enhance autoregressive modeling, and CodeBERT incorporates source code representations. However, these adaptations may alter key properties relevant for interpretability, particularly in token-level attribution scenarios. By employing the original BERT-base model, we retain the full contextual modeling capacity upon which SHAP’s explanation fidelity depends, ensuring consistent behavior and alignment with the theoretical foundations of our interpretability framework. Moreover, as shown in our experiments, our proposed approach allows this otherwise computationally demanding model to operate efficiently, making it suitable for real-world deployment in operational environments.

Regarding Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI), which focuses on developing methods that produce interpretable, intuitive, and human-understandable explanations of algorithmic decisions to foster trust and accountability in high-stakes domains54,55, we adopt SHAP values to enhance explainability in our model. Specifically, token-level attributions are computed and subsequently aggregated into semantically coherent word-level explanations, delivering post-hoc insights into model predictions while supporting transparency in complex transformer-based architectures. This method is particularly suitable in our context due to its model-agnostic formulation, theoretical grounding in cooperative game theory, and ability to produce consistent local explanations across diverse model architectures25. Compared to alternative techniques such as Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME)56, which approximates local behavior using linear models and suffers from stability issues, SHAP offers more reliable, theoretically principled attributions. Integrated Gradients is another popular method for neural networks, particularly effective when gradients are accessible; however, it is not model-agnostic and may struggle to handle discrete input spaces, such as tokenized text23. Similarly, attention weights57 are often used as a proxy for feature importance in Transformer-based models; however, they reflect internal model mechanisms rather than actual causal influence, thereby limiting their interpretability.

There has been limited exploration of integrating SHAP with Transformer-based architectures. For example, Kokalj et al.58 proposed TransSHAP, a pre-hoc wrapper that applies SHAP over whole-word masks before BERT re-tokenization. While this avoids sub-token fragmentation, it incurs costly perturbations and forfeits exact SHAP additivity, limiting compatibility with gradient-based explainers. In parallel, Nie & Wu59 introduces a dual-channel prediction-interpretation pipeline with SHAP for character-based Chinese clinical narratives, where per-character aggregation remains semantically faithful, yet this does not transfer to languages whose semantics are primarily expressed at the multi-character word level. Beyond such wrappers, Rizinski et al.60 leverage SHAP on fine-tuned Transformer models to extract words that push the model’s decision and distill an explainable lexicon (XLex), later merged with the Loughran-McDonald dictionary (XLex+LM) to trade model capacity for speed and rule-based interpretability. Their objective is lexicon induction and downstream lexicon-based sentiment analysis, not faithful attribution under subword tokenization; accordingly, they neither formalize reconstruction from sub-tokens nor ensure SHAP’s additivity at the word level. By contrast, this work introduces a post-hoc procedure that (i) reconstructs semantically coherent words from sub-token SHAP attributions, (ii) preserves SHAP additivity via exact aggregation over a word’s constituent sub-tokens, and (iii) remains agnostic to the SHAP variant, supporting both gradient- and perturbation-based explainers. We further operationalize a word-level SHAP dictionary for inference (DSHAP), including an out-of-vocabulary back-off via cosine similarity in the Transformer embedding space, enabling faithful, human-readable explanations in production settings.

To the best of our knowledge, prior work has not introduced a post-hoc procedure that (i) reconstructs semantically meaningful words from sub-token SHAP attributions, (ii) preserves SHAP’s additivity at the word level by exactly aggregating all constituent sub-tokens, and (iii) remains explainer-agnostic, supporting both gradient- and perturbation-based variants. We fill this gap with a reconstruction step that maps sub-token attributions back to full-word units, yielding faithful and human-readable explanations, crucial for high-stakes settings such as anomaly detection in operational log data.

Methodology

This architecture comprises two distinct stages: training and inference phases, as shown in Fig. 2. The general concept is that during the training phase, the model is trained on preprocessed, vectorized logs to develop generalization capability, enabling it to classify new logs as either abnormal or normal. Additionally, SHAP values are computed at this stage to quantify each word’s contribution to anomalous predictions, yielding a dictionary of word-level importance scores. In the inference phase, incoming log entries are matched against this dictionary to assign interpretability scores. If a word is not found in the dictionary, a cosine similarity-based approximation is used to infer its relevance based on the most similar known terms.Fig. 2Proposed architecture for detecting anomalous log messages.

Data partitioning and oversampling strategy

After generating BERT embeddings, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{E}$$\end{document}, the data is prepared for model training by dividing it into training, validation, and test sets. This division is followed by addressing class imbalance through an oversampling strategy. Such preparation ensures the model is trained and evaluated on a dataset that accurately reflects the problem space. The vectors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{E}$$\end{document} and their corresponding labels \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{y}$$\end{document} are partitioned using stratified sampling. This technique maintains the class distribution across all subsets, ensuring that each set accurately represents the original dataset’s class proportions. The dataset is divided into three distinct sets: the training set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\textbf{X}_{\textrm{tr}}, \textbf{y}_{\textrm{tr}})$$\end{document}, the validation set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\textbf{X}_{\textrm{val}}, \textbf{y}_{\textrm{val}})$$\end{document}, and the test set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\textbf{X}_{\textrm{te}}, \textbf{y}_{\textrm{te}})$$\end{document}. Each subset contains a representative sample of the overall class distribution.

Class imbalance, a prevalent issue in many datasets, occurs when the minority class is significantly underrepresented compared to the majority class. This is particularly common in anomaly detection tasks, such as analyzing log data, where anomalous events typically constitute a smaller number of samples than normal events. This imbalance can lead to biased model predictions, as the model may become more proficient at predicting the majority class while failing to accurately identify the minority class. To mitigate this, an oversampling strategy is applied exclusively to the training set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\textbf{X}_{\textrm{tr}}, \textbf{y}_{\textrm{tr}})$$\end{document}, after the data splitting step. The minority class \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {C}_0$$\end{document} and the majority class \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {C}_1$$\end{document} are identified, and the minority class is oversampled by random duplication with replacement until both classes are equally represented. A fixed random seed is used to ensure reproducibility. This process results in a balanced training dataset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{\textrm{bal}} = \mathscr {C}_0 \cup \mathscr {C}_1$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{\textrm{bal}}$$\end{document} represents the newly balanced dataset. The balanced training set is subsequently split into features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{X}_{\textrm{tr,b}}$$\end{document} and labels \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{y}_{\textrm{tr,b}}$$\end{document}, which are then utilized for model training.

Oversampling was considered as a hyperparameter and applied only when cross-validation on the training folds indicated consistent improvements in minority-class performance (e.g., macro-F1 or AUPRC) without degrading model calibration. This approach aligns with prior work showing that class imbalance can substantially affect classifier stability and minority-class sensitivity61–63. In practice, oversampling was beneficial for datasets with pronounced class imbalance, such as Casper, Zookeeper, Hadoop, and Windows, whereas it provided no measurable improvement for datasets where the two classes were comparatively balanced (e.g., Honey5). The resampling ratio was selected through a grid search that always included the “no oversampling” baseline, ensuring that oversampling was used only when it yielded a clear and validated benefit. Validation and test sets were left untouched and preserve their original class priors. All reported performance metrics are computed on the original (unbalanced) test set to accurately reflect real-world operating conditions.

In this approach, we did not employ resampling techniques such as Tomek Links64, Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE)65, or Adaptive Synthetic (ADASYN)66. While these methods have been occasionally applied in text classification tasks using embeddings, they were initially designed for continuous tabular data and rely on linear interpolation or distance metrics between feature vectors. In high-dimensional embedding spaces, such interpolations may yield vectors that do not correspond to any plausible or semantically coherent sentence, potentially introducing noise into the learning process. Consequently, we opted for random oversampling of minority instances, which preserves the original embedding space and avoids generating artificial points with ambiguous semantics. While this duplication can increase the risk of overfitting, several regularization strategies have been implemented to mitigate these effects, including dropout, weight decay, early stopping, and learning rate scheduling. Additionally, model performance was monitored on a held-out validation set to ensure generalizability. Notably, our architecture achieved strong results without the need for more complex resampling techniques, highlighting the effectiveness of this more straightforward yet robust strategy for addressing class imbalance in embedded textual data.

Neural network architecture and fine-tuning process

This study employs a custom neural network model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {N}$$\end{document} that leverages BERT for vectorization to enhance sentiment analysis performance through fine-tuning; the configuration details are shown in Algorithm 3. Unlike the internal feed-forward sublayers of the Transformer encoder, the neural network described in this section corresponds to the downstream classifier built on top of BERT. The connection between the pre-trained model and this classifier is established through the [CLS] token, which provides the aggregate sequence representation. This output, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{X}_{\textrm{tr,b}}$$\end{document}, represents the balanced set of semantic vectors used as input features for the training data. This architecture seamlessly integrates the contextualized representations generated by the pre-trained model into the training process. The first layer is a fully connected layer \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{FC}_1$$\end{document} that projects the input into a hidden dimension space \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_h$$\end{document}, utilizing learnable parameters for transformation. The output of this layer is given by:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{z}_1 = \textbf{X}_{\textrm{tr,b}} \cdot \textbf{W}_1 + \textbf{b}_1 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Subsequently, batch normalization \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{BN}_1$$\end{document} is applied to ensure stable learning by normalizing activations, as described by Ioffe & Szegedy67. Word-level attribution from subword SHAP values

To explain the predictions of our fine-tuned neural network model, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {N}$$\end{document}, for log anomaly detection, we adopt the SHAP framework, which assigns a contribution value to each input token based on cooperative game theory. Specifically, SHAP seeks to compute the value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _t$$\end{document} assigned to each token \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t \in \{1, \dots , M\}$$\end{document}, defined as:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \phi _t = \sum _{S \subseteq \{1, \dots , M\} \setminus \{t\}} \frac{|S|!(M - |S| - 1)!}{M!} \left[ f(S \cup \{t\}) - f(S) \right] , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where f(S) denotes the model prediction restricted to a subset of tokens S, and the combinatorial term ensures fair marginal contribution over all permutations.

Since the exact computation of SHAP values is intractable for high-dimensional data, we utilize DeepExplainer25,74, an approximation designed explicitly for deep neural networks. This method integrates the logic of DeepLIFT75 and Integrated Gradients23, enabling efficient estimation of SHAP values via gradient backpropagation and layer-wise relevance propagation. In our case, the neural network uses ReLU activations, which are piecewise linear and compatible with the assumptions of DeepExplainer. This property enables SHAP values to be propagated precisely through the network under piecewise linear activations and a single baseline input, utilizing a combination of additive contribution rules and chain derivatives. This satisfies, or closely approximates, the local accuracy and consistency principles required by the SHAP framework.

Model update strategy for new logs

In real-world operational settings, log data is continuously generated as systems run, evolve, and encounter unexpected behaviors. Over time, new patterns may emerge due to infrastructure updates, software reconfigurations, component replacements, or the introduction of novel failure modes. These changes can cause a distributional shift between the training data and incoming logs, potentially degrading model performance if not addressed77. To ensure that our anomaly detection model remains effective and robust in such environments, we outline a practical strategy for processing and interpreting new logs. This strategy supports inference, interpretability, and incremental adaptation without requiring complete retraining from scratch, thereby aligning with the real-time demands of production monitoring systems.

Importantly, this procedure benefits from the use of a pre-trained language model, such as BERT, which enables strong generalization to unseen log expressions through its deep semantic understanding. This architectural choice not only improves classification accuracy but also facilitates the integration of new data under the same representation framework, making model updates more stable and consistent over time. Moreover, BERT’s ability to model contextual dependencies is critical for interpretability. When applying SHAP, importance scores are assigned at the token level; however, the underlying embeddings reflect nuanced semantic relationships. This allows the model to identify words or phrases that may not appear inherently negative in isolation, but whose meaning shifts in context, a phenomenon that simpler methods may overlook. Consequently, BERT not only enhances generalization during inference but also supports richer and more faithful explanations during model interpretation.

Step 1: Preprocessing and vectorization

New log entries are first preprocessed using the same pipeline described in Sections Log preprocessing and Log pre-trained vectorization, which includes log cleaning, tokenization, and embedding using the fine-tuned BERT model. The cleaning step ensures that irrelevant artifacts such as timestamps, IP addresses, or repeated template markers are removed, preserving only the informative content of each log. The cleaned logs are then tokenized into subword units, maintaining consistency with the vocabulary used during training. Finally, the token sequences are passed through the BERT encoder, which has been fine-tuned on the training logs, to produce contextual embeddings. By applying this exact preprocessing sequence, we ensure that new logs are represented in the same feature space as the training data, preserving compatibility with the trained classifier and SHAP interpretation framework.

Step 2: Model inference

The embedded log samples are then passed to the trained classifier \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {N}$$\end{document}, which produces anomaly predictions. Since the model operates on fixed-size contextual embeddings produced by BERT, it can process previously unseen log messages without requiring structural adjustments. This property enables seamless out-of-domain generalization, provided that the encoder sufficiently captures the semantic structure of the new logs. In practice, this means that as long as the new logs share syntactic or contextual similarity with the training data, the model can detect anomalous patterns even in messages that were not part of the original training corpus. This makes the approach particularly suitable for real-time environments, where retraining is not always feasible but generalization is essential.

Step 3: SHAP-based interpretation

While SHAP values provide reliable token-level attributions for log inputs, these attributions are typically computed with respect to a specific training set and its vocabulary. Consequently, when new logs introduce previously unseen tokens, the original SHAP explainer cannot directly assign scores without re-evaluating the model over the new data. To avoid full recomputation while preserving explainability, we adopt a dynamic dictionary mechanism. This dictionary, denoted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{\textrm{SHAP}}$$\end{document}, maps each known token w to its aggregated SHAP contribution score, which is computed during training. At inference time, for any token \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w^*$$\end{document} in a new log, we distinguish two cases. If \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w^* \in \mathscr {D}_{\textrm{SHAP}}$$\end{document}, its precomputed score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi (w^*)$$\end{document} is retrieved and directly assigned. Otherwise, if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w^* \notin \mathscr {D}_{\textrm{SHAP}}$$\end{document}, the cosine similarity between its BERT embedding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{e}_{w^*}$$\end{document} and all known embeddings \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{\textbf{e}_w \mid w \in \mathscr {D}_{\textrm{SHAP}} \}$$\end{document} is computed. The most similar word \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{w}$$\end{document} is identified, and the score is estimated as:26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \phi (w^*) \approx \phi (\hat{w}) \cdot \cos \big (\textbf{e}_{w^*}, \textbf{e}_{\hat{w}}\big ) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This approach enables the attribution of SHAP-like importance scores to previously unseen tokens by leveraging semantic proximity in embedding space, thereby extending the model’s interpretability in a controlled and scalable manner. However, although the semantic-similarity back-off mechanism provides a practical way to assign approximate word-level importance scores to unseen tokens during inference, its fidelity relative to a full SHAP recomputation was not quantitatively evaluated in the present study. Therefore, this component should be understood as an operational approximation designed to support scalable deployment, while its approximation accuracy remains an important direction for future work.

Step 4: Optional drift detection

While our pipeline is designed to be robust to new log entries, thanks to the use of a pre-trained BERT model capable of capturing deep semantic structures and generalizing to previously unseen expressions, as corroborated by our out-of-domain experiments, distributional shifts may still occur as systems evolve78. To monitor such shifts, statistical drift detection techniques can be applied, based on the comparison of embedding distributions over time.

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{X}_{\textrm{ref}}$$\end{document} denote a set of reference log embeddings obtained from the training set, and let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{X}_{\textrm{new}}$$\end{document} represent embeddings for recently collected logs. Since the original BERT-based representations are high-dimensional (d=768), one possible approach is to reduce dimensionality using Principal Component Analysis (PCA)79, projecting both sets into a lower-dimensional subspace (typically k = 10) that preserves at least 95% of the variance. This yields \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{Z}_{\textrm{ref}} = \textrm{PCA}_k(\textbf{X}_{\textrm{ref}})$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{Z}_{\textrm{new}} = \textrm{PCA}_k(\textbf{X}_{\textrm{new}})$$\end{document}, which can then be used for monitoring purposes. To detect potential distributional shifts, the probability distributions P and Q of the reduced embeddings can be estimated using kernel density estimation (KDE)80, and their divergence can be quantified. A widely used metric for this purpose is the Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD)81, defined as:27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{JSD}(P \parallel Q) = \frac{1}{2} \textrm{KL}(P \parallel M) + \frac{1}{2} \textrm{KL}(Q \parallel M), \quad \text {where } M = \frac{1}{2}(P + Q) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{KL}(\cdot \parallel \cdot )$$\end{document} denotes the Kullback-Leibler divergence82. The JSD is symmetric and bounded between 0 and łog 2, making it a suitable candidate for measuring distributional changes over time. Alternatively, the Wasserstein Distance83 may also be employed to assess how much one distribution must be transformed to resemble another:28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} W(P, Q) = \inf _{\gamma \in \Gamma (P, Q)} \mathbb {E}_{(x, y) \sim \gamma } [\Vert x - y\Vert _2] \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where Γ (P, Q) denotes the set of all joint probability distributions γ (x, y) whose marginals are P and Q, respectively. In this formulation, x ∼ P and y ∼ Q are random variables representing samples from the reference and new log embedding distributions. The expectation is computed with respect to the optimal transport plan γ, which minimizes the average cost of moving probability mass from P to Q under the Euclidean distance.

These distance metrics could be computed periodically over sliding windows of incoming log data. If the JSD or Wasserstein distance exceeds a given threshold, either empirically established or calibrated from baseline fluctuations, this may signal a distributional drift. Such drift could potentially compromise both classification accuracy and the semantic validity of SHAP-based explanations. Crucially, because all log entries are embedded into a shared vector space by the pre-trained BERT encoder, this type of distributional monitoring remains semantically coherent without requiring manual feature engineering.

Step 5: Periodic model refresh

When significant drift is detected or performance begins to degrade on recent logs, we recommend a periodic model refresh. This involves fine-tuning the existing architecture on a curated set of newly labeled logs. The training procedure should follow the original setup, including class balancing if needed. Importantly, this retraining step should also include an update of the SHAP dictionary \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{\textrm{SHAP}}$$\end{document}. While the dynamic assignment mechanism described in Equation 26 enables approximate scoring for previously unseen tokens, performance improves when actual SHAP scores are available for new high-frequency words. We propose defining a refresh trigger based on a combination of factors, such as the appearance of new tokens not present in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {D}_{\textrm{SHAP}}$$\end{document} that exceed a given frequency threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _f$$\end{document} (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _f = 50$$\end{document} occurrences), a marked decrease in the average cosine similarity between new tokens and their closest embeddings in the existing dictionary, or the detection of statistical drift using the divergence metrics described in Step 4. Once such a trigger is activated, the updated training set should be used to recompute SHAP values, thereby regenerating the dictionary and restoring precise attribution capabilities. This dual update process ensures that the model maintains both high predictive accuracy and robust explainability as the operational environment evolves.

Evaluation metrics

We primarily use the F1-Score and Precision and Recall to evaluate the proposed architecture, employing “metrics”provided by the Scikit-learn library. These metrics comprehensively evaluate the model’s performance in binary classification tasks. Precision assesses the proportion of true positive (TP) results among all instances predicted as positive, indicating the model’s effectiveness in identifying actual anomalies, where false positives are expressed as FP. Recall, also known as sensitivity or true positive rate, evaluates the proportion of authentic positive results in all actual positive instances, highlighting the model’s ability to detect anomalies where false negatives are represented as FN. Unlike previous approaches, our algorithm does not depend on a log parser. Instead, it introduces a character-level extraction strategy that offers two key advantages over traditional word-based methods. First, as shown in Table 3, this approach significantly improves computational efficiency when compared to the state-of-the-art architectures that inspired our work. Second, by operating at the character level, it ensures the accurate capture of all relevant terms, including acronyms, while effectively filtering out symbols or numbers unrelated to the textual content. Moreover, we verified the robustness of this extraction method across a diverse set of systems and formats encompassed by our datasets. For the computational comparisons, we focused on the Casper, Honey5, and Windows datasets, as they were the only ones for which execution time information was publicly available.

We compare the effect of word embeddings and other machine and deep learning models with the proposed architecture, conducting experiments with various combinations of state-of-the-art models and vectorization methods. It is observed that TF-IDF consistently outperforms across all models on in-domain data. This can be attributed to the fact that, when dealing with out-of-domain data, GloVe embeddings combined with neural networks excel due to their ability to capture global semantic relationships and contextual dependencies. GloVe allows the model to infer the meaning of new word combinations. Finally, we conducted a final experiment to assess the influence of word embeddings under a single model architecture, aiming to more clearly visualize how vectorization impacts anomaly classification in logs and evaluate it under a common standard. To this end, we first compared traditional static embeddings, such as TF-IDF, Word2vec, and GloVe, using our custom neural network, which was previously employed for fine-tuning BERT. Subsequently, we evaluated pre-trained contextual embeddings, including DistilBERT, RoBERTa, and XLNet, under the same architecture to enable a fair comparison.

As shown in Fig. 12, BERT outperforms all other word embeddings, particularly in the out-of-domain setting. While the in-domain performance appears nearly identical across all embeddings, with TF-IDF, GloVe, and BERT all achieving an F1-Score of 99.96, and Word2vec closely following with 99.80, this similarity can be attributed to the repetitive and formulaic nature of log messages, which allows even simple representations to excel when the test data resembles the training distribution. However, the differences become much more pronounced in the out-of-domain scenario. BERT maintains a high F1-score of 96.97, whereas the performance of TF-IDF, GloVe, and especially Word2vec drops considerably, achieving only 86.68. This observation underscores the benefit of BERT’s pre-training on large corpora and its subword tokenization mechanism, which enables better generalization to unseen log formats and terminology. The ability to decompose rare or unknown tokens into meaningful subunits allows BERT to extract semantic cues that static embeddings like Word2vec or GloVe, trained on a fixed vocabulary, cannot recover from. Moreover, the strong in-domain results observed for the traditional embeddings suggest that, in scenarios where log messages are highly repetitive, these methods can effectively memorize and reproduce patterns seen during training. TF-IDF, in particular, shows high performance due to the lexical overlap between training and test logs within the same domain. Nonetheless, this memorization-based behavior fails to extend to unfamiliar contexts, making these models vulnerable in cross-domain deployments. Importantly, even in in-domain scenarios, the choice of BERT remains justified. Real-world log systems are dynamic and may introduce new or previously unseen tokens over time, even within the same domain. BERT’s subword modeling and contextual embeddings enable it to handle such variations more effectively than static methods. Additionally, as will be demonstrated in subsequent sections, BERT facilitates a much richer explainability process, for example, through SHAP values-where its semantic depth enables the generation of interpretable and robust attributions at the word level, further supporting transparent anomaly detection. This experiment highlights that embedding choice is not merely a technical preference but a core design decision that significantly impacts model robustness, interpretability, and deployment reliability.

On the other hand, as shown in Fig. 13, BERT consistently outperforms other pretrained models such as DistilBERT, RoBERTa, and XLNet, achieving the highest overall F1-Score of 98.47. While performance differences in in-domain datasets are minimal, with all models exceeding 99.90, the disparities become more pronounced in the untrained out-of-domain setting, where BERT reaches 96.81 compared to 94.89 for DistilBERT, 92.84 for RoBERTa, and 91.03 for XLNet. The architectural characteristics of each model can explain these differences. DistilBERT, as a compressed version of BERT, offers faster inference but sacrifices contextual capacity by reducing the number of layers and parameters, which impacts its generalization in complex scenarios such as log anomaly detection across unseen domains. RoBERTa improves upon BERT by introducing dynamic masking and removing the next sentence prediction objective; however, this may limit its ability to capture sequential coherence, even in individual log entries. XLNet, while autoregressive and permutation-based, may underperform in capturing the bidirectional contextual dependencies that BERT natively models. These dependencies are particularly important when used in conjunction with token-level explanation frameworks such as SHAP.

Subsequently, we applied a SHAP values-based algorithm to provide an explainability framework for our model. We aim to analyze the average contribution of specific words to our classes of interest, namely the negative class representing anomalies and the positive (normal) class. By quantifying the importance of each word, we can identify which terms are most strongly associated with the presence of anomalies as well as those associated with normal behavior. To achieve this, we first calculated SHAP values for each word within individual logs across both classes. These values represent the local impact of each word on the model’s prediction for that specific instance. Subsequently, we aggregated these SHAP values across all instances in both classes and averaged them across five independent runs to derive a robust global contribution score for each word. This aggregation provides a global perspective, allowing us to determine which words consistently influence the model’s predictions towards either the negative or positive class.

This multi-run averaging process, whose stability will be demonstrated in the following section through Spearman correlation107 and Jaccard similarity108 analyses, ensures the reliability and generalizability of the obtained rankings. The resulting importance scores were normalized and ranked, as illustrated in Fig. 14. This Fig. highlights the words most strongly correlated with anomalous and normal behavior, providing valuable insights into the linguistic patterns our model consistently associates with the presence or absence of anomalies. A key advantage of using BERT is its deep semantic understanding during vectorization, which allows it to capture not only words that explicitly indicate negative instances but also those that are semantically related to negativity. This level of semantic understanding is crucial because it enables the model to recognize words that may not be obviously negative on their own but are contextually connected to negative outcomes, a feature that simpler methods might overlook. In contrast, we also analyzed the coefficients from an SVM model using TF-IDF vectorization. This approach tends to emphasize isolated words that explicitly reflect negativity, particularly adjectives in this case, due to the way TF-IDF assigns weights. However, this method cannot capture other words that might be contextually related to negativity or anomalies. Examples of such words include “disabled”, “unregistered”, and “failed”, as detailed in Fig. 15.

Finally, we applied SHAP values to three preprocessed logs to analyze the contribution of individual words to the model’s anomaly predictions, as illustrated in Fig. 16. SHAP assigns a local importance score to each token, allowing for a transparent understanding of how specific terms impact classification outcomes. Leveraging BERT’s contextual embeddings, the model captures semantic subtleties that extend beyond the isolated meaning of words, enabling it to identify tokens that, although not inherently negative, may be indicative of anomalous behavior in specific system contexts. Notably, tokens such as “Bluetooth”, “query”, and “session” received high SHAP scores in certain logs. These terms, although benign in general usage, may appear in system logs under unusual circumstances. For instance, “Bluetooth” may signal unexpected driver activity or unauthorized device interactions, potentially indicating misconfiguration or security risks. The word “query” is often associated with database or system-level lookups; its repeated presence may indicate performance bottlenecks or abnormal request patterns. Similarly, “session” could be linked to frequent authentication events or repeated session terminations, possibly indicating system instability or unauthorized access attempts.

These interpretations illustrate how SHAP can translate token-level attribution scores into log-level explanations that are more readily interpretable by humans. Rather than treating explanations as abstract token weights, this approach provides a principled way to quantify the contribution of words to anomaly predictions, thereby enabling the comparison of logs in terms of their negative or anomaly-related evidence. In this sense, the explainability layer is not only useful for inspecting individual predictions, but also constitutes a necessary intermediate step toward higher-level prioritization frameworks, where quantified log-level evidence can later be aggregated and associated with system components to support transparent operational decision-making.

Conclusions and future work

This study proposes a novel sentiment-aware supervised learning framework for detecting individual log anomalies. Our contributions include: (i) a lightweight log-cleaning technique based on character-level extraction that preserves semantic integrity while improving processing efficiency, (ii) a customized neural architecture that integrates BERT embeddings with regularization strategies to enhance generalization, (iii) strong classification performance on both in-domain and out-of-domain datasets, demonstrating robust transfer capabilities, and (iv) a SHAP-based interpretability method that reconstructs meaningful word-level attributions from subword tokens, addressing a key challenge in transformer-based explainability.

While transformer-based models and SHAP explanations are often perceived as computationally demanding, our empirical results demonstrate that the proposed architecture achieves high throughput during inference, with efficient processing times across large-scale datasets. In this operational stage, the semantic-similarity back-off mechanism provides a practical way to assign approximate word-level importance scores to previously unseen tokens without requiring a full SHAP recomputation. Although this strategy supports scalable deployment, its fidelity relative to exact SHAP evaluation was not quantitatively assessed in the present study and therefore remains an important direction for future work. At the same time, the runtime analysis of full SHAP computation confirms that the explainability stage remains practically feasible as an offline component, even when applied to extensive log corpora. Finally, stability analyses based on Spearman correlation and Jaccard similarity further validate the robustness and reproducibility of the resulting interpretability outputs across multiple runs.

Future work will focus on further reducing inference latency and refining embedding strategies to enhance overall model efficiency. Beyond performance, the proposed explainability framework contributes an interpretable evidence layer that can help connect data-driven anomaly detection with higher-level decision-support mechanisms. While the present study does not formally evaluate operator-level benefits through user studies, the resulting word-level attributions constitute a necessary step toward transparent prioritization and downstream operational reasoning. Building on these results, we plan to deploy and validate our approach in real-world operational systems to assess its practical impact, including its usefulness for real-time or near-real-time decision support, as well as its robustness in production environments.

Finally, we acknowledge a potential limitation of sentiment-based individual log analysis. While it offers robustness in scenarios where log sequences are incomplete or unordered, it may overlook anomalies expressed in sentiment-neutral or context-dependent language.
