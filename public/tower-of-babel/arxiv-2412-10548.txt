Compact Probe Request Fingerprinting with Asymmetric Pairwise Boosting
Giovanni Baccichet, Fabio Palmese, Alessandro E. C. Redondi, Matteo Cesana

Abstract
Probe Requests are Wi-Fi management frames periodically sent by devices during network discovery. Tracking Probe Requests over time offers insights into movement patterns, traffic flows, and behavior trends, which are keys in applications such as urban planning, human mobility analysis, and retail analytics. To protect user privacy, techniques such as MAC address randomization are employed, periodically altering device MAC addresses to limit tracking. However, research has shown that these privacy measures can be circumvented. By analyzing the Information Elements (IE) within the Probe Request body, it is possible to fingerprint devices and track users over time. This paper presents a machine learning-based approach for fingerprinting Wi-Fi Probe Requests in a compact fashion. We utilize Asymmetric Pairwise Boosting to learn discriminating filters which are then used to process specific bit sequences in Probe Request frames, and quantize the results into a compact binary format. Extensive evaluation on public datasets demonstrates a two-order-of-magnitude storage reduction compared to existing methods while maintaining robust fingerprinting performance.



I
Introduction

Wi-Fi devices periodically transmit unencrypted frames, known as
Probe Request (PR)
, to search for available networks in their proximity. The collection of such frames enable entities such as urban planners, transportation authorities, and commercial enterprises to monitor and analyze urban flows, occupancy estimation, and extract useful metrics for resource planning
[
1
]
.

Extracting information from collected
PR
frames is however very challenging nowadays, due to two main reasons:

1.

Storage Footprint
: The massive proliferation of Wi-Fi devices has led to an exponential growth in
PRs
volume, overwhelming traditional storage and management capabilities. This growth in data presents significant challenges in terms of cost and complexity.

2.

MAC Address Randomization
: To avoid unauthorized eavesdroppers from tracking a device, one of the most popular countermeasures implemented nowadays is
Media Access Control (MAC)
Address randomization, where a device generates randomized addresses used in a pseudo-random pattern during the
PR
emission process, hindering tracking.

Methods to counteract
MAC
address randomization have been a focal point of recent research efforts
[
2
,
3
]
: such methods attempt to extract fingerprints from
Information Elementss (IEs)
contained in the payload of
PR
frames, that can be later used to track a device without relying on its MAC address. However, little attention has been given to the problem of handling or compressing large volumes of
PRs
efficiently. Compression techniques tailored to
PRs
allow to significantly reduce the storage footprint of these requests without losing critical information. This not only helps in lowering storage costs but can also enhance data processing efficiency, enabling quicker and less consuming analysis. Ultimately, the ability to compress
PRs
ensures that the benefits of monitoring and analyzing Wi-Fi signals can be sustained as the number of connected devices continues to rise.

Our goal within this study is to design a compact yet discriminative
PR
fingerprint. In order to do this, we rely and adapt a
Machine Learning (ML)
approach named
Asymmetric Pairwise Boosting (APB)
, initially used successfully for audio and video fingerprints
[
4
]
.

APB
learns (i) discriminative portions of
PR
as well as (ii) thresholds to quantize each portions to a 1-bit element: the concatenation of such bits forms a compact fingerprint which can be efficiently stored and matched for fast
PR
processing.

In details, the main contributions of this work can be summarized as follows:

•

We introduce a
PR
binary fingerprint based on the
APB
algorithm. To ensure reproducible research, our implementation is made publicly available

. Moreover, the data used for training the proposed fingerprinting technique come from open datasets, detailed in

.

•

•

II
Related Work

The study of
PR
frames in Wi-Fi networks has become a well-investigated research area due to the significant privacy and security implications associated with these frames.
PRs
, used to discover available networks in the transmission range of the devices, contain
MAC
addresses that can be potentially used to track devices. To address these privacy concerns, manufacturers and
Operating System (OS)
developers have implemented several countermeasures. These include randomizing the source address, minimizing metadata in
PR
frames (e.g., obscuring the
Preferred Network List (PNL)
or eliminating non-essential fields), and periodically rotating frame content between probe bursts. This developments have sparked active research into
PRs
fingerprinting methodologies for applications such as network optimization, security monitoring, and user analytics.
Early work by Vanhoef et al.
[
7
]
explored the use of temporal and spatial patterns in
PR
frames. They identified that, despite the MAC randomization, devices often exhibit unique patterns in terms of SSID lists, inter-packet timing, and sequence numbers, which can be leveraged to link multiple
PR
to the same device. Similarly, Matte et al.
[
8
]
proposed a novel attack method that leveraged timing observations of
PR
frames. They developed an
Inter-Frame Arrival Time (IFAT)
signature to distinguish devices based on the rate at which they sent these requests. Recent research by Pintor et al.
[
9
]
provided a comprehensive analysis of
IE
fingerprinting using
Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN)
clustering, yielding promising results in a controlled laboratory setting. However, it highlighted a significant limitation: the misclassification of devices that share the same device model due to nearly identical IE fields.

In 2024, Baccichet et al.
[
6
]
addressed the challenge of identifying devices with the same model and
OS
version by utilizing multi-channel behavior patterns.
While all these works focus mainly on the accuracy of
PR
fingerprinting, i.e., the capability to link two
PR
to the same device, none of them tackles the problem of compressing such fingerprints.

Methodology

Algorithm 1

Asymmetric Pairwise Boosting

Let
y_{n}\in\{-1,+1\}
denote the label describing the ground truth relationship between the pair
\langle x_{a(n)},x_{b(n)}\rangle
, where
n=1,\ldots,N
. The label
y_{n}
takes the value
+1
if the two
PRs
are matching (originating from the same device) and
-1
if the frames are from different devices.

Moreover, let
\mathcal{H}=\{h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{B}\}
define a set of bitmask filters. For each
PR
, we obtain a vector of
B
scalar intermediate representations
\{b_{1}(x_{n}),b_{2}(x_{n}),\ldots,b_{B}(x_{n})\}
, where each element
b_{i}(x_{n})
is the result obtained by filtering the
PR

x_{n}
with the bitmask filter
h_{i}
.

The output of the
APB
algorithm consists of a set of
M<B
selected filters
F_{i}
and corresponding thresholds
t_{j}
, such that,

F_{i}(x_{n})=\begin{cases}+1&\text{if }b_{i(m)}(x_{n})>t_{j(m)},\\
-1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(1)

Where
i(m)
with
m=1,...,M
, denotes the index of the filter selected during the
m
-th iteration, and
j(m)
the index of the corresponding threshold in the set
\mathcal{T}=\{t_{1},t_{2},...,t_{T}\}
.

In the Algorithm
1
intermediate representations are computed as follows:

h_{i,p}(x_{a(n)},x_{b(n)})\triangleq\xi((b_{i}(x_{a(n)})-t_{p})(b_{i}(x_{b(n)})-t_{p}))

(2)

Where
\xi
is defined as:

\xi((b(x_{a})-t)(b(x_{b})-t))=\begin{cases}+1&\text{if }b(x_{a,b})>t,\\
-1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(3)

Each step of the boosting algorithm selects the filter and the threshold that provide the best possible separation between matching and non-matching
PRs
. This involves choosing the weak classifier that minimizes the weighted error function over the set of all training
PRs
. The assignment of a weight to each pair of
PRs
is intended to emphasize the importance of misclassified pairs. Specifically, the more accurately the previously selected weak classifiers can classify a pair
\langle x_{a(n)},x_{b(n)}\rangle
, the lower the corresponding weighting factor
w_{n}
becomes.

III-A

Candidate bitmask filters

The set of candidate filters contains filters of four different types (A,B,C and D). Each filter has length
L
, preceded by a prefix of
P
zeros and followed by a suffix of
S
zeros, such that
P+L+S=1784
bits and matches the size of a
PR
. Type A filters are characterized by the first half of non-zero bits being
+1
, and the second half being
-1
. Type B filters are the complement of Type A, with the first half being
-1
and the second half
+1
. In Type C filters, all non-zero bits are
-1
, while in Type D, all non-zero bits are
+1
. Our objective was to replicate Haar-like features, generally used in image processing, using bitmasks. Similar to Haar-like features, our filters can be defined as the difference of the sum of the bits within specific areas of a
PR
covered by the bitmask. These areas can be located at any position within the
PR
and have a length
L\in\{4,8,16\}
. We used a total of 2,492 different filters, varying the position of the
L
filtering bits throughout the complete length of a
PR
. These filters were designed to select entire bytes, portions of fields, or overlapping portions of data across different fields of a
PR
.

III-B

Fingerprint Matching

The
APB
algorithm assigns a weight, namely
confidence
,
c_{m}
with
m=1,...,M
to each of the selected weak classifiers. Such weights are subsequently exploited to compute a weighted Hamming distance between a pair of test
PRs
,
x_{n_{1}}
and
x_{n_{2}}
, according to

H(x_{n_{1}},x_{n_{2}})=\sum_{m=1}^{M}c_{m}\xi_{m}(x_{n_{1}},x_{n_{2}})

(4)

Where the
\xi
function is described in (
3
) and assumes value
+1
when both filtered
PRs
fall on the same side of the threshold corresponding to the selected filter,
-1
otherwise.

Then
H(x_{n_{1}},x_{n_{2}})
is compared to a threshold
\tau
to determine if
\langle x_{n_{1}},x_{n_{2}}\rangle
is a matching pair. The value of
\tau
is selected based on the desired trade-off between
True Positive Rate (TPR)
and
False Positive Rate (FPR)
.

We note that ignoring these weights by setting
c_{m}=1
does not significantly impact the discriminative power. At the same time, this change allows to use the simple Hamming distance for matching two fingerprints, which can be efficiently performed in hardware.

For that reason, we can simplify the prediction process: two
PRs
having fingerprints
F(x_{1})
and
F(x_{2})
are predicted as matching (
+1
) or non-matching (
-1
) according to the following equation:

\hat{y}=\begin{cases}+1&\text{if }\sum F(x_{1})\oplus F(x_{2})<\tau,\\
-1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(5)

Where
\hat{y}
is the prediction (matching / non-matching pair) and
\tau\in[0,M]
. Figure
1
illustrates the matching process for one bit of the
fingerprint.

PRs
fall on the same side of the threshold.

IV-A

Data Pre-Processing

We pre-process the binary
PRs
by considering only the
IEs
which have been demonstrated to be relevant by the state of the art, particularly following the insights from
[
9
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
11
]
, and
[
12
]
. The most relevant fields for the defined use case are

,
Extended Capabilities
, and

. The first two fields are used by the transmitting device to advertise its technical capabilities, while the latter carries additional information not specified by the standard.

Finally, we concatenated all the columns to generate the raw binary data to be processed by our algorithm using specific filters. After the pre-processing step, the size of each
PR
is equal to 223 bytes (1784 bits).

V-A

Training Data

Since the algorithm we use considers pairs of
PRs
and classifies them as matching or non-matching, we require both a training set and a test set containing pairs of
PRs
, where each pair is either matching or non-matching. To achieve this, we performed the following:

•

Matching Pairs Set
: leveraging the labeled dataset, we identified 1000 unique pairs of
PRs
from the same devices to serve as matching examples.

•

Non-Matching Pairs Set
: We created non-matching pairs by selecting
PRs
from different devices. This ensured that the pairs represented distinct devices, facilitating the algorithm’s ability to differentiate between them.

•

Balancing Dataset
: We ensured that both matching and non-matching pairs were evenly represented to avoid bias and improve the algorithm’s generalization capability.

•

Dataset Partitioning
: The dataset was divided into training and test subsets (60-40) maintaining the balance of matching and non-matching pairs in each subset. The training dataset is used as input to the
APB
algorithm, while the test set is used to compute to evaluate performance and possibly tune matching hyperparameters.

Further research could explore optimizing the trade-offs between memory usage, identification accuracy, and other relevant performance metrics to enhance the applicability of our technique across different use cases.

VI
Conclusion

PR
fingerprinting enables the defeat of MAC randomization, facilitating applications such as device counting, occupancy estimation, and urban flow monitoring. This paper proposes a compact yet discriminative
PR
fingerprint, whose structure is learned through a ML technique called
APB
. By leveraging a dataset of
PR
frames from various devices (including some of identical types), the proposed methodology identifies the discriminative parts of a
PR
and compresses them into a single bit using specifically learned thresholds.

The resulting binary fingerprint, whose size is fully customizable by the user, significantly reduces memory requirements for processing
