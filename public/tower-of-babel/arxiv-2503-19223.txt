Abstract

Digital image spoofing has emerged as a significant security threat in biometric authentication systems, particularly those relying on facial recognition. This study evaluates the performance of three vision-based models, MobileNetV2, ResNET50, and Vision Transformer (ViT), for spoof detection in image classification, utilizing a dataset of 150,986 images divided into training (140,002), testing (10,984), and validation (39,574) sets. Spoof detection is critical for enhancing the security of image recognition systems, and this research compares the models’ effectiveness through accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score metrics. Results reveal that MobileNetV2 outperforms other architectures on the test dataset, achieving an accuracy of 91.59%, precision of 91.72%, recall of 91.59%, and F1 score of 91.58%, compared to ViT’s 86.54%, 88.28%, 86.54%, and 86.39%, respectively. On the validation dataset, MobileNetV2, and Vision Transformer (ViT) excel, with MobileNetV2 slightly ahead at 97.17% accuracy versus ViT’s 96.36%. MobileNetV2 demonstrates faster convergence during training and superior generalization to unseen data, despite both models showing signs of overfitting. These findings highlight MobileNetV2’s balanced performance and robustness, making it the preferred choice for spoof detection applications where reliability on new data is essential. The study underscores the importance of model selection in security-sensitive contexts and suggests MobileNetV2 as a practical solution for real-world deployment.

2.2
Vision based ML Analysis

2.2.1
ML models for Image Classification Analysis

In the context of our spoof detection system, the selection between MobileNetV2 and Vision Transformer (ViT) is contingent upon their distinct architectural designs and how these align with the operational demands of identifying tampered images. MobileNetV2, a convolutional neural network (CNN), is engineered for computational efficiency, making it particularly suitable for real-time applications or deployment on resource-constrained devices. Its architecture leverages depthwise separable convolutions to capture local features such as inconsistencies in texture or lighting, that are often indicative of spoofing, while maintaining a low computational footprint. This efficiency is critical for systems requiring rapid processing, such as those deployed on edge devices or in environments with limited computational resources. The ability of MobileNetV2 to focus on localized patterns can be particularly advantageous in detecting subtle cues of tampering, such as irregularities in edges or shading, which are common in spoofed images used to deceive facial recognition systems. Moreover, its relatively lightweight design ensures that it can operate effectively without imposing significant latency, a key consideration for real-time spoof detection.
In contrast, ViT, a transformer-based model, processes images as sequences of patches and employs self-attention mechanisms to model global dependencies across the entire image. This architectural approach enables ViT to potentially identify more complex, holistic patterns of spoofing that may not be apparent through local feature analysis alone. For instance, it could detect inconsistencies that span larger regions of an image, such as unnatural distortions or contextual anomalies, which might be missed by a purely convolutional approach. However, this global processing capability comes at the cost of increased computational complexity, which may limit its feasibility in real-time systems or on devices with constrained processing power. Additionally, ViT typically requires large datasets to achieve optimal performance, as transformers are inherently data-intensive. In scenarios where our spoof detection system has access to extensive training data, ViT could leverage its capacity to model intricate relationships, potentially enhancing detection accuracy. Yet, if the dataset is limited, its performance might lag behind that of MobileNetV2, which benefits from the inductive biases inherent in convolutional operations.
Another critical aspect for our spoof detection system is the interpretability of model decisions, which is paramount in security-sensitive applications. MobileNetV2, as a CNN, often lends itself to visualization techniques such as Grad-CAM, which can highlight regions of an image that influence the model’s classification. This capability allows researchers and practitioners to understand why an image was flagged as spoofed, providing insights into the system’s reliability and aiding in its refinement. Conversely, ViT remains more opaque due to the complexity of its self-attention mechanisms, making it challenging to interpret its decision-making processes. This lack of transparency could be a drawback in contexts where explainability is required, such as in validating the system for deployment in high-stakes environments.
Given these considerations, MobileNetV2 emerges as a more practical choice for our spoof detection system, particularly in scenarios that prioritize efficiency, real-time processing, and interpretability. Its lightweight architecture and ability to capture local tampering cues make it well-suited for deployment on edge devices or in systems where computational resources are limited. Nonetheless, if computational resources are abundant and the dataset is sufficiently large, ViT could offer enhanced detection capabilities by leveraging its global feature modeling. Ultimately, the decision hinges on the specific operational constraints and performance requirements of our system, balancing the trade-offs between computational efficiency, detection accuracy, and the need for interpretable outcomes. Results and Discussion

This study investigated the efficacy of three vision-based models, Vision Transformer Large Patch 16 (ViTL16), ResNET50, and MobileNetV2, for spoof detection using a dataset of 150,986 images, aiming to differentiate between real and fake images.

Starting with training loss, MobileNETv2 begins at approximately 0.075 and experiences a sharp decline to around 0.02 by the 10th epoch, subsequently approaching 0 by the 60th epoch. This rapid initial drop suggests that MobileNETv2 adapts quickly to the training data, likely benefiting from efficient feature extraction or optimization techniques inherent to its architecture. In contrast, ViT16 starts with a slightly higher training loss of about 0.08, decreasing more gradually to 0.02 by the 20th epoch before leveling off near 0 by the end of the training period. While both models ultimately achieve near-zero training loss, MobileNETv2’s faster convergence in the early stages highlights a difference in how quickly each model learns from the training set.
On the test loss front, both models exhibit notable instability, with neither showing clear signs of convergence. For MobileNETv2, test loss fluctuates significantly between 0.4 and 0.8, while ViT16’s test loss varies between 0.3 and 0.6. This high variability indicates that both models struggle to generalize effectively to unseen data, potentially pointing to issues like overfitting or a mismatch between the training and test data distributions. Despite their success in minimizing training loss, the lack of stability in test loss suggests that the models may not be robust enough for reliable performance outside the training environment, posing challenges for real-world applications where generalization is critical.
When examining training accuracy, both models demonstrate strong performance on the training set. MobileNETv2’s accuracy rises swiftly from approximately 97% to 99% by the 10th epoch, eventually reaching between 99.5% and 100% by the 60th epoch. ViT16 follows a similar trajectory but at a slower pace, climbing from 97% to 99% by the 20th epoch and leveling off near 99.5% by the end. This near-perfect training accuracy for both models underscores their ability to fit the training data effectively, with MobileNETv2 showing a slight edge in the speed of its initial improvement. However, this high training accuracy contrasts sharply with their performance on the test set, hinting at potential overfitting.
Test accuracy further highlights the generalization challenges faced by both models. MobileNETv2’s test accuracy oscillates between 88% and 92%, while ViT16’s ranges from 86% to 92%, with both settling around 90% by the 60th epoch. Unlike the steady gains seen in training accuracy, test accuracy for both models lacks a clear upward trend, remaining inconsistent throughout the training process. This instability reinforces concerns about their ability to perform reliably on new data, suggesting that while they excel at memorizing the training set, they struggle to adapt to variations encountered in the test set.
In summary, the training dynamics of MobileNETv2 and ViT16 reveal strong learning capabilities on the training data, with MobileNETv2 converging faster in terms of loss and accuracy in the early epochs. However, their test performance marked by fluctuating loss and unstable accuracy indicates significant limitations in generalization. These findings suggest that while both models are highly effective at fitting the training set, additional strategies, such as improved regularization or data augmentation, may be necessary to enhance their robustness and ensure better performance on unseen data in practical scenarios.

The ViT model’s performance on the test set yields an overall accuracy of 86.54%. It demonstrates exceptional sensitivity in detecting real images, correctly classifying 97.20% of them as real, as reflected in its TP rate. However, its ability to identify fake images is notably weaker, with a TN rate of 75.89%, meaning that 24.11% of fake images are misclassified as real (FP). This high FP rate represents a significant vulnerability, as it suggests that nearly a quarter of spoof attempts could be erroneously accepted, posing a substantial security risk. Conversely, the FN rate is relatively low at 2.80%, indicating that only a small fraction of real images are incorrectly labeled as fake. On the validation set, ViT’s performance improves considerably, achieving an accuracy of 96.36%. The TP rate rises slightly to 97.46%, while the TN rate increases dramatically to 95.27%, reducing the FP rate to 4.73% and the FN rate to 2.54%. This enhanced performance suggests that the validation data may have been more representative or that the model was better tuned for this set, resulting in a more balanced classification capability.
In contrast, the MobileNETv2 model exhibits superior performance on the test set, with an overall accuracy of 91.59%. It correctly identifies 88.82% of real images (TP) and 94.36% of fake images (TN), demonstrating a strong ability to detect spoof attempts. The FP rate is significantly lower than ViT’s at 5.64%, indicating a reduced likelihood of accepting fake images as real, which is a critical advantage in spoof detection. However, the FN rate is higher at 11.18%, suggesting that a notable proportion of real images are misclassified as fake. On the validation set, MobileNETv2 achieves an impressive accuracy of 97.17%, with a near-perfect TP rate of 99.00% and a TN rate of 95.32%. The FP rate remains low at 4.68%, and the FN rate drops substantially to 1.00%, reflecting excellent generalization and a highly balanced performance across both classes.
A comparative evaluation of the two models reveals distinct differences in their classification capabilities. MobileNETv2 consistently outperforms ViT in overall accuracy, achieving 91.59% on the test set and 97.17% on the validation set, compared to ViT’s 86.54% and 96.36%, respectively. In terms of real image detection, ViT excels on the test set with a TP rate of 97.20% against MobileNETv2’s 88.82%, but MobileNETv2 surpasses it on the validation set with 99.00% versus 97.46%. For fake image detection, MobileNETv2 demonstrates a clear advantage, with TN rates of 94.36% (test) and 95.32% (validation) compared to ViT’s 75.89% (test) and 95.27% (validation), particularly excelling on the test set. Error patterns further distinguish the models: ViT’s FP rate of 24.11% on the test set starkly contrasts with MobileNETv2’s 5.64%, highlighting ViT’s greater propensity to misclassify fake images as real. Meanwhile, MobileNETv2’s higher FN rate of 11.18% on the test set, compared to ViT’s 2.80%, indicates a tendency to conservatively label images as fake, an error less critical than false positives in security contexts. On the validation set, both models achieve similar FP rates (4.73% for ViT vs. 4.68% for MobileNETv2), but MobileNETv2’s FN rate of 1.00% outperforms ViT’s 2.54%.
The implications of these findings are significant for deploying these models in practical spoof detection systems. ViT’s proficiency in real image detection is notable, but its high FP rate on the test set undermines its reliability, potentially allowing a substantial number of spoof attempts to evade detection. This weakness may result from overfitting or inadequate representation of fake images in the training data. MobileNETv2, however, offers a more robust and balanced performance, particularly with its low FP rate on the test set, which enhances security by minimizing the acceptance of fake images. Although its elevated FN rate on the test set suggests a need for refinement to reduce the misclassifications of real images, this issue is less severe than the risk posed by false positives. Additionally, MobileNETv2’s lightweight architecture provides practical advantages for deployment in resource-constrained environments, unlike ViT, which may demand greater computational resources. The superior performance of both models on the validation set relative to the test set could indicate effective tuning or easier validation data, but the test set results, being more indicative of generalization to unseen data, underscore MobileNETv2’s greater reliability.
To further improve these models, several strategies merit consideration. Expanding the dataset, particularly with additional fake images, could enhance ViT’s spoof detection capabilities. An ensemble approach combining the strengths of ViT and MobileNETv2 might yield a more resilient system, leveraging ViT’s real image detection prowess and MobileNETv2’s fake image rejection efficiency. Moreover, adjusting class weights or employing targeted data augmentation could mitigate the imbalance in error rates, particularly MobileNETv2’s FN rate on the test set. In conclusion, while both models exhibit commendable performance, MobileNETv2 stands out as the more effective choice for spoof detection, owing to its higher accuracy and superior fake image detection, especially on the test set. Its lower FP rate is paramount for ensuring security, positioning it as the recommended model for real-world applications, with further optimization needed to minimize false negatives. Conclusion

Based on the comprehensive evaluation of the performance metrics for the ViTL16 and MobileNetV2 models across both test and validation datasets, MobileNetV2 emerges as the superior choice for spoof detection. On the test dataset, MobileNetV2 achieved an accuracy of 91.59%, precision of 91.72%, recall of 91.59%, and an F1 score of 91.58%. These closely aligned values reflect a well-balanced model that effectively minimizes both false positives and false negatives, demonstrating robust generalization to unseen data. In contrast, ViTL16 recorded lower test performance, with an accuracy of 86.54%, precision of 88.28%, recall of 86.54%, and an F1 score of 86.39%, indicating potential overfitting or a reduced ability to handle new samples effectively. On the validation dataset, both models performed exceptionally well, with MobileNetV2 achieving an accuracy of 97.17%, precision of 97.24%, recall of 97.16%, and an F1 score of 97.17%, slightly outperforming ViTL16’s accuracy of 96.36%, precision of 96.38%, recall of 96.37%, and F1 score of 96.36%. While these high validation scores suggest that both models excel on data similar to the training set, the notable drop in ViTL16’s performance on the test dataset highlights a key disparity in generalization capabilities. The discrepancy between test and validation results underscores the critical need to assess models on diverse datasets to ensure real-world applicability. MobileNetV2’s consistent outperformance, particularly on the test dataset, positions it as the more reliable option for spoof detection tasks where robustness to unseen data is paramount. Looking ahead, future research could explore strategies such as data augmentation or regularization to enhance ViTL16’s generalization, potentially closing the performance gap with MobileNetV2. For now, MobileNetV2 stands out as the preferred model due to its balanced and effective performance in practical scenarios.
