Morse Index Theorem for Sturm-Liouville Operators on the Real Line
Ran Yang, Qin Xing

Abstract
The classical Morse index theorem establishes a fundamental connection between the Morse index-the number of negative eigenvalues that characterize key spectral properties of linear self-adjoint differential operators-and the count of corresponding conjugate points. In this paper, we extend these foundational results to the Sturm-Liouville operator on ℝ \mathbb{R} . In particular, for autonomous Lagrangian systems, we employ a geometric argument to derive a lower bound for the Morse index. As concrete applications, we establish a criterion for detecting instability in traveling waves within gradient reaction-diffusion systems. AMS Subject Classification: 37B30, 53D12,58J30,34B24,35K57. Keywords: Morse index, Maslov index, Spectral flow, Sturm-Liouville theory, Reaction-diffusion equation, Spectral unstable.

Introduction and description of the problem

We consider the following
n
-dimensional Sturm-Liouville operator:

(1.1)

\displaystyle Lw:=-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(P(t)\dot{w}(t)+Q(t)w(t))+Q^{\top}(t)\dot{w}(t)+R(t)w(t)\text{ with }\dom(L)=W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Here, the coefficient matrices satisfy

P(t)\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{Sym}(n,\mathbb{R}))
,
Q(t)\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{Mat}(n,\mathbb{R}))
, and
R(t)\in C(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{Sym}(n,\mathbb{R}))
, where
\mathrm{Mat}(n,\mathbb{R})
is the set of all
n\times n
matrices and
\mathrm{Sym}(n,\mathbb{R})
is the set of all
n\times n
symmetric matrices.

We impose the following assumptions:

(L1)

The matrices
P(t)
,
Q(t)
, and
R(t)
converge to their respective limits
P_{\pm}
,
Q_{\pm}
, and
R_{\pm}
as

. Furthermore, there exist positive constants

,
C_{2}
, and
C_{3}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|P(t)\right\|\geqslant C_{1},\quad\left\|Q(t)\right\|\leq C_{2},\quad\text{and}\quad\left\|R(t)\right\|\leq C_{3}\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}.

(L2)

The block matrices

\begin{pmatrix}P_{-}&Q_{-}\\
Q_{-}^{\top}&R_{-}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\begin{pmatrix}P_{+}&Q_{+}\\
Q_{+}^{\top}&R_{+}\end{pmatrix}

are both positive definite.

The starting point of our study is the classical Sturm-Liouville theory, which asserts that the Morse index of one-dimensional Sturm-Liouville operator equals the number of zeros of the eigenfunction for the first non-negative eigenvalue
[
21
, Theorem 2.3.3]
.

However, when
n>1
, the results of Sturm-Liouville theorem no longer apply, and determining the Morse index becomes significantly more challenging.

In
[
3
]
, Arnold observed that, for a system of differential equations, a useful generalization of the notion of ‘oscillation’ (or number of zeros) is given by the Maslov index
[
2
,
22
,
8
]
, a topological invariant counting signed intersections of Lagrangian planes in a symplectic vector space. Increasing attention has been devoted to the Maslov index and its applications to study the stablity of standing pulses, solitary waves in reaction-diffusion equation
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
20
]
and the linear stability elliptic relative equilibria in
N
-body problem
[
17
,
5
]
. The use of the Maslov index as a tool for determining the Morse index(i.e.,unstable eigenvalue) was pioneered in the works of Jones
[
20
]
and Bose & Jones
[
7
]
. Further related studies can be found in the literature, including
[
12
,
15
,
14
,
19
]
.

Our primary objective is to determine the number of negative eigenvalues of
L
, i.e., its Morse index. To achieve this, we establish a connection between the Morse index and the Maslov index. Specifically, we demonstrate that the Morse index can be expressed in terms of the Maslov index with positive direction at every crossing, i.e., the crossing form is positive definite at every crossing. Although the Maslov index is conceptually less elementary than the Morse index, it offers a computational advantage, as it can be determined numerically in a relatively straightforward manner
[
9
,
11
,
17
]
.

In the standard symplectic space
\left(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\omega\right)
, we denote by
\Lambda({n})
the Lagrangian Grassmannian, which is the collection of all Lagrangian subspaces of
\left(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\omega\right)
.

By applying a symplectic change of coordinates, the equation
Lu=0
can be rewritten as the Hamiltonian system:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\dot{z}=JB(t)z,\quad\text{where}\quad B(t):=\begin{pmatrix}P^{-1}(t)&-P^{-1}(t)Q(t)\\
-Q^{\top}(t)P^{-1}(t)&Q^{T}(t)P^{-1}(t)Q(t)-R(t)\end{pmatrix}.

Given condition (L1), the limit matrices
\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}B(t):=B(+\infty)
and
\lim\limits_{t\to-\infty}B(t):=B(-\infty)
exist and are both well-defined.

Proposition 1.1
.

[
19

L
is Fredholm if and only if both
JB(+\infty)
and
JB(-\infty)
are hyperbolic, meaning that their spectra lie off the imaginary axis.

Let
\gamma_{\tau}
denote the fundamental matrix solution of the linear Hamiltonian system:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{\gamma}_{\tau}(t)=JB(t)\gamma_{\tau}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\\
\gamma_{\tau}(\tau)=I,\end{cases}

which satisfies the semigroup property:
\gamma_{\sigma}(\xi)\gamma_{\tau}(\sigma)=\gamma_{\tau}(\xi)
for all
\xi,\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
.

For
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the stable and unstable subspaces as follows:

(1.3)

\displaystyle E^{s}(\tau):=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}\mid\lim_{t\to+\infty}\gamma_{\tau}(t)v=0\right\},\quad E^{u}(\tau):=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}\mid\lim_{t\to-\infty}\gamma_{\tau}(t)v=0\right\}.

It is well-known (Cfr.
[
18
]
and references therein) that the path
\tau\mapsto E^{s}(\tau)
and
\tau\mapsto E^{u}(\tau)
are both Lagrangian and to each ordered pair of Lagrangian paths we can assign an integer known in literature as Maslov index of the pair and denoted by
\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{CLM}}}
(Subsection

E^{s/u}(\tau)=\gamma_{\sigma}(\tau)E^{s/u}(\sigma)
for any
\sigma,\tau\in\mathbb{R}
.

We define the asymptotic stable subspaces at infinity as:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} E^{s}(+\infty)&:=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}\mid\lim_{t\to+\infty}\exp(tJB(+\infty))v=0\right\}=V^{-}(JB(+\infty)),\\
E^{u}(-\infty)&:=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}\mid\lim_{t\to-\infty}\exp(tJB(-\infty))v=0\right\}=V^{+}(JB(-\infty)).\end{aligned}

Under the condition (L2), by Lemma
2.1
and
2.5
, we have that

(1.4)

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\to+\infty}E^{s}(\tau)=V^{-}(JB(+\infty))\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{\tau\to-\infty}E^{u}(\tau)=V^{+}(JB(-\infty)),

where the convergence is meant in the gap (norm) topology of the Lagrangian Grassmannian.

In
[
19
]
, under condition (L2), the authors establish a fundamental relationship between the Morse index
\mathrm{m^{-}}(L)
and the Maslov index
\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{CLM}}}(E^{s}(\tau),E^{u}(-\tau);\tau\in[0,+\infty))
, given by

\mathrm{m^{-}}(L)=-\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{CLM}}}(E^{s}(\tau),E^{u}(-\tau);\tau\in[0,+\infty)).

For n > 1, computing the Maslov index becomes challenging without additional knowledge of the n-1 solutions. To address this,
[
6
, Theorem 4.1]
considers the second-order linear operator

H=-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}+A(t),\quad\text{with }\dom H=W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Under condition (L1), the authors employ the Evans function and Rouché’s theorem to establish the result

\mathrm{m^{-}}(H)=\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}\dim(E^{u}(\tau)\cap\Lambda_{D}),

where

\Lambda_{D}=\{(u,0)^{\top}|u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}

is the horizontal (Dirichlet) Lagrangian subspace.

Inspired by this result, we adopt a different methodology to extend these results to general Sturm-Liouville operators.

Theorem 1.2
.

Under the assumptions (L1) and (L2), there exists
T_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}
such that, for all
T>T_{\infty}
, we have:

\mathrm{m^{-}}(L)=\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{CLM}}}(\Lambda_{D},E^{u}(\tau);\tau\in(-\infty,T])=\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}\dim(E^{u}(\tau)\cap\Lambda_{D}).

Remark 1.3
.

Let
L:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a smooth autonomous Lagrangian function satisfying the following Legendre convexity condition:

L
is
C^{2}
-convex, meaning that the quadratic form

\displaystyle\left\|D_{vv}^{2}L(q,v)\right\|\geqslant\ell_{0}I>0,\quad\forall(q,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}.

In what follows, we denote by
u^{-},u^{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
two rest points of the Lagrangian vector field
\nabla L
. That is, they satisfy

\nabla L\left(u^{\pm},0\right)=0.

A heteroclinic orbit
u^{*}
asymptotic to
u^{\pm}
is a
C^{2}
-solution of the following boundary value problem:

(1.5)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\partial_{v}L(t,u(t),\dot{u}(t))=\partial_{q}L(t,u(t),\dot{u}(t)),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\\
\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow-\infty}u(t)=u^{-},\quad\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow+\infty}u(t)=u^{+}.\end{cases}

By linearizing (

) along

, we obtain the following Sturm-Liouville operator:

Lw:=-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(P(t)\dot{w}(t)+Q(t)w(t))+Q^{\top}(t)\dot{w}(t)+R(t)w(t),\quad\text{with }\dom(L)=W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{n}),

where

P(t):=\partial_{vv}L(u(t),\dot{u}(t)),\quad Q(t):=\partial_{uv}L(u(t),\dot{u}(t)),\quad R(t):=\partial_{uu}L(u(t),\dot{u}(t)).

Since (
1.5
) is autonomous, it follows that
\dot{u}^{*}(t)\in\ker(L)
. Moreover, the vector

(P(\tau)\ddot{u}^{*}(\tau)+Q(\tau)\dot{u}^{*}(\tau),\dot{u}^{*}(\tau))^{\top}\in E^{u}(\tau).

By applying Theorem
1.2
, we conclude that the number of critical points of

(i.e., points where
\dot{u}^{*}(t)=0
) serves as a lower bound for the Morse index of the operator
L
.

3
Instability of Traveling Solutions in Gradient Reaction–Diffusion Systems

Consider the reaction-diffusion system:

(3.1)

\displaystyle u_{t}=u_{xx}+\nabla F(u),\quad u\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
x,t\in\mathbb{R}
denote space and time, respectively,
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\nabla F
represents the gradient of the function
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. A traveling wave solution
u^{*}
, which depends on the moving frame
\xi=x-ct
, satisfies equation (
3.1
) along with the asymptotic condition:

\displaystyle u^{*}(\xi)\to u_{\pm}\text{ as }\xi\rightarrow\pm\infty.

Here,
u_{\pm}
denote the constant equilibria of (
3.1
), satisfying
\nabla F\left(u_{\pm}\right)=0
.

Rewriting in the moving frame
\xi=x-ct
, a traveling front solution
u^{*}
of (
3.1
) can be regarded as a homoclinic solution
w^{*}(\xi)=u^{*}(x,t)
of the following equation:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}w_{\xi\xi}+cw_{\xi}+\nabla F(w)=0,\\
\lim\limits_{\xi\rightarrow-\infty}w(\xi)=u_{-}\text{ and }\lim\limits_{\xi\rightarrow+\infty}w(\xi)=u_{+}.\end{cases}

The stability analysis is closely related to the spectral properties of the operator:

L:=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\xi^{2}}+c\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}+B(\xi),

which arises from linearizing (
3.2
) along
w^{*}
, where
B(\xi)=\nabla^{2}F\left(w^{*}\right)
. The matrices
B({\pm\infty}):=\lim\limits_{\xi\rightarrow\pm\infty}B(\xi)
are well-defined, and there exists a constant
C>0
such that:

\langle B(\xi)v,v\rangle\leqslant C|v|^{2},\quad\forall(\xi,v)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Since a wave solution of (
3.1
) possesses translation invariance, it is termed nondegenerate if zero is a simple eigenvalue of
L
.

Definition 3.1
.

A nondegenerate wave solution of (
3.1
) is spectrally stable if all nonzero eigenvalues of
L
are negative.

In this paper, we consider the following assumption:

(H)

The matrices
B(\pm\infty)
are both negative definite.

Due to the presence of the
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}
term,
L
does not exhibit a Hamiltonian structure. This issue can be circumvented by considering the transformed operator:

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}:=-e^{\frac{c\xi}{2}}Le^{-\frac{c\xi}{2}}=-\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\xi^{2}}+\frac{c^{2}}{4}I-B(\xi),

as discussed in
[
16
,
13
]
.

Under assumption (H), conditions (L1) and (L2) hold for
\mathbb{L}
. By applying a symplectic change of coordinates, the equation
\mathbb{L}u=0
can be rewritten as the Hamiltonian system:

(3.3)

\displaystyle\dot{y}=JA(t)y,

where

A(t)=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&B-\frac{c^{2}}{4}I\end{pmatrix}.

Denoting
E^{u}(\tau)
as the unstable space of (
3.3
), Theorem
1.2
gives:

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{m^{-}}(\mathbb{L})=\sum_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}\dim\left(\Lambda_{D}\cap E^{u}(\tau)\right).

In
[
23
]
, the author considers the eigenvalue problem of the operator:

\widehat{L}:=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\xi^{2}}+c\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}+QB(\xi),

where
Q=\begin{pmatrix}I_{r}&0\\
0&-I_{n-r}\end{pmatrix}
, and establishes the following result:

Proposition 3.2
.

[
23
, Corollary 2.5.]

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\mathbb{C}^{-}
and
\phi\in W^{2,2}\left(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)
, we have:

\phi\in\ker(\widehat{L}-\lambda I)\quad\text{if and only if}\quad e^{\frac{c\xi}{2}}\phi\in\ker(\widehat{\mathbb{L}}+\lambda I),

where
\widehat{\mathbb{L}}:=-\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\xi^{2}}+\frac{c^{2}}{4}I-QB
.

Setting
Q:=I
and following a similar argument as in Proposition
3.2
, we obtain:

Lemma 3.3
.

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\mathbb{C}^{-}
and
\phi\in W^{2,2}\left(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{n}\right)
, we have:

\phi\in\ker(L-\lambda I)\quad\text{if and only if}\quad e^{\frac{c\xi}{2}}\phi\in\ker(\mathbb{L}+\lambda I).

By the translation invariance property of (
3.1
), it follows that
\dot{w}^{*}\in\ker{L}
. Consequently,
e^{\frac{c\xi}{2}}\dot{w}^{*}\in\ker{\mathbb{L}}
, which implies

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}e^{\frac{c\tau}{2}}\ddot{w}^{*}(\tau)+\frac{c}{2}e^{\frac{c\tau}{2}}\dot{w}^{*}(\tau)\\
e^{\frac{c\tau}{2}}\dot{w}^{*}(\tau)\end{pmatrix}\in E^{u}(\tau).

Theorem 3.4
.

If there exists
\xi_{0}
such that
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}w^{*}|_{\xi=\xi_{0}}=0
, then
w^{*}
is spectrally unstable.

Proof.

From the given assumption, together with (
3.5
) and (
3.4
), we conclude

\mathrm{m^{-}}(\mathbb{L})\geqslant\dim(\Lambda\cap E^{u}(\tau_{0}))\geqslant 1.

Combined with Proposition
3.2
, this implies that at least one positive eigenvalue exists for
L
, completing the proof.
∎

Maslov, Hörmander, Triple Index and Spetral Flow

This final section is dedicated to recalling fundamental definitions, key results, and essential properties of the Maslov index and related invariants used throughout our analysis. Primary references include
[
22
,
18
,
24
]
and their cited works.

4.1
The Cappell-Lee-Miller Index

Consider the standard symplectic space
(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\omega)
. Let
\Lambda({n})
denote the Lagrangian Grassmannian of
(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\omega)
. For
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a<b
, define
\mathscr{P}([a,b];\mathbb{R}^{2n})
as the space of continuous Lagrangian pairs
L:[a,b]\to\Lambda({n})\times\Lambda({n})
with compact-open topology. Following
[
8
]
, we recall the Maslov index for Lagrangian pairs, denoted by
\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{CLM}}}
. Intuitively, for
L=(L_{1},L_{2})\in\mathscr{P}([a,b];\mathbb{R}^{2n})
, this index enumerates (with signs and multiplicities) instances
t\in[a,b]
where
L_{1}(t)\cap L_{2}(t)\neq\{0\}
.

Definition 4.1
.

The
\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{CLM}}}
-index is the unique integer-valued function

\displaystyle\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{CLM}}}:\mathscr{P}([a,b];\mathbb{R}^{2n})\ni L\mapsto\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{CLM}}}(L(t);t\in[a,b])\in\mathbb{Z}

satisfying Properties I-VI in
[
8
, Section 1]
.

An effective approach to compute the Maslov index employs the crossing form introduced in
[
22
]
. Let
\Lambda:[0,1]\to\Lambda({n})
be a smooth curve with
\Lambda(0)=\Lambda_{0}
, and
W
a fixed Lagrangian complement of
\Lambda(t)
. For
v\in\Lambda_{0}
and small
t
, define
w(t)\in W
via
v+w(t)\in\Lambda(t)
. The quadratic form
Q(v)=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\omega(v,w(t))
is independent of
W

[
22
]
. A crossing occurs at
t
where
\Lambda(t)
intersects
V\in\Lambda({n})
nontrivially. The crossing form at such
t
is defined as

(4.1)

\displaystyle\Gamma(\Lambda(t),V;t)=\left.Q\right|_{\Lambda(t)\cap V}.

A crossing is regular if its form is nondegenerate. For quadratic form
Q
, let
\sign(Q)=\mathrm{m^{+}}(Q)-\mathrm{m^{-}}(Q)
denote its signature. From
[
25
]
, if
\Lambda(t)
has only regular crossings with
V
, then

(4.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{CLM}}}(V,\Lambda(t);t\in[a,b])=&\mathrm{m^{+}}(\Gamma(\Lambda(a),V;a))\\
&+\sum_{a<t<b}\sign\Gamma(\Lambda(t),V;t)-\mathrm{m^{-}}(\Gamma(\Lambda(b),V;b)).\end{split}

For the sake of the reader, we list a couple of properties of the
\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{CLM}}}
-index that we shall use throughout the paper.

•

(Reversal)
Let
L:=\left(L_{1},L_{2}\right)\in\mathscr{P}\left([a,b];\mathbb{R}^{2n}\right)
. Denoting by
\widehat{L}\in

\mathscr{P}\left([-b,-a];\mathbb{R}^{2n}\right)
the path traveled in the opposite direction, and by setting
\widehat{L}:=\left(L_{1}(-s),L_{2}(-s)\right)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{CLM}}}(\widehat{L};[-b,-a])=-\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{CLM}}}(L;[a,b])

•

(Stratum homotopy relative to the ends)
Given a continuous map

L:[a,b]\ni s\rightarrow L(s)\in\mathscr{P}\left([a,b];\mathbb{R}^{2n}\right)
where
L(s)(t):=\left(L_{1}(s,t),L_{2}(s,t)\right)

such that
\dim L_{1}(s,a)\cap L_{2}(s,a)
and
\dim L_{1}(s,b)\cap L_{2}(s,b)
are both constant, and then,

\displaystyle\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{CLM}}}(L(0);[a,b])=\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{CLM}}}(L(1);[a,b])

4.2
Triple Index and Hörmander Index

We summarize key concepts about the triple and Hörmander indices, following
[
24
]
. For isotropic subspaces
\alpha,\beta,\delta
in
(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\omega)
, define the quadratic form

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathscr{Q}\coloneqq\mathscr{Q}(\alpha,\beta;\delta):\alpha\cap(\beta+\delta)\to\mathbb{R},\quad\mathscr{Q}(x_{1},x_{2})=\omega(y_{1},z_{2})

where
x_{j}=y_{j}+z_{j}\in\alpha\cap(\beta+\delta)
with
y_{j}\in\beta
,
z_{j}\in\delta
. For Lagrangian subspaces
\alpha,\beta,\delta
,
[
24
, Lemma 3.3]
gives

\displaystyle\ker\mathscr{Q}(\alpha,\beta;\delta)=\alpha\cap\beta+\alpha\cap\delta.

Definition 4.2
.

For Lagrangians
\alpha,\beta,\kappa
in
(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\omega)
, the triple index is

(4.4)

\displaystyle\iota(\alpha,\beta,\kappa)=\mathrm{m^{-}}(\mathscr{Q}(\alpha,\delta;\beta))+\mathrm{m^{-}}(\mathscr{Q}(\beta,\delta;\kappa))-\mathrm{m^{-}}(\mathscr{Q}(\alpha,\delta;\kappa))

where
\delta
satisfies
\delta\cap\alpha=\delta\cap\beta=\delta\cap\kappa=\{0\}
.

By
[
24
, Lemma 3.13]
, this index also satisfies

(4.5)

\displaystyle\iota(\alpha,\beta,\kappa)=\mathrm{m^{+}}(\mathscr{Q}(\alpha,\beta;\kappa))+\dim(\alpha\cap\kappa)-\dim(\alpha\cap\beta\cap\kappa).

The Hörmander index measures the difference between Maslov indices relative to different Lagrangians. For paths
\Lambda,V\in\mathscr{C}^{0}([0,1],\Lambda({n}))
with endpoints
\Lambda(0)=\Lambda_{0}
,
\Lambda(1)=\Lambda_{1}
,
V(0)=V_{0}
,
V(1)=V_{1}
:

Definition 4.3
.

The Hörmander index is

(4.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}s(\Lambda_{0},\Lambda_{1};V_{0},V_{1})&=\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{CLM}}}(V_{1},\Lambda(t);t\in[0,1])-\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{CLM}}}(V_{0},\Lambda(t);t\in[0,1])\\
&=\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{CLM}}}(V(t),\Lambda_{1};t\in[0,1])-\mu^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{CLM}}}(V(t),\Lambda_{0};t\in[0,1]).\end{split}

Remark 4.4
.

Homotopy invariance ensures Definition
4.3
is well-posed (cf.
[
22
]
).

For four Lagrangians
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}
,
[
24
, Theorem 1.1]
establishes:

(4.7)

\displaystyle s(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2};\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2})=\iota(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\kappa_{2})-\iota(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\kappa_{1})=\iota(\lambda_{1},\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2})-\iota(\lambda_{2},\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}).

4.3
Spectral Flow

Introduced by Atiyah-Patodi-Singer
[
4
]
, spectral flow measures eigenvalue crossings. Let
E
be a real separable Hilbert space, and
\mathscr{CF}^{sa}(E)
denote closed self-adjoint Fredholm operators with gap topology. For continuous
A:[0,1]\to\mathscr{CF}^{sa}(E)
, the spectral flow
\text{sf}(A_{t};t\in[0,1])
counts signed eigenvalue crossings through
-\epsilon
(
\epsilon>0
small).

For each
A_{t}
, consider the orthogonal decomposition

\displaystyle E=E_{-}(A_{t})\oplus E_{0}(A_{t})\oplus E_{+}(A_{t}).

Let
P_{t}
be the orthogonal projector onto
E_{0}(A_{t})
. At crossing
t_{0}
where
E_{0}(A_{t_{0}})\neq\{0\}
, define the crossing form

(4.8)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cr}[A_{t_{0}}]\coloneqq P_{t_{0}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}P_{t_{0}}:E_{0}(A_{t_{0}})\to E_{0}(A_{t_{0}}).

A crossing is regular if
\operatorname{Cr}[A_{t_{0}}]
is nondegenerate. Define

\displaystyle\sgn(\operatorname{Cr}[A_{t_{0}}])\coloneqq\dim E_{+}(\operatorname{Cr}[A_{t_{0}}])-\dim E_{-}(\operatorname{Cr}[A_{t_{0}}]).

Assuming regular crossings, the spectral flow becomes

(4.9)

\displaystyle\text{sf}(A_{t};t\in[0,1])=\sum_{t_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{*}}\sgn(\operatorname{Cr}[A_{t_{0}}])-\dim E_{-}(\operatorname{Cr}[A_{0}])+\dim E_{+}(\operatorname{Cr}[A_{1}])

where
\mathcal{S}_{*}=\mathcal{S}\cap(a,b)
contains crossings in
(a,b)
.

For the sake of the reader we list some properties of the spectral flow that we shall frequently use in the paper.

•

(Stratum homotopy relative to the ends)
Given a continuous map

\displaystyle\bar{A}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathscr{C}^{0}\left([a,b];\mathscr{C}\mathscr{F}^{sa}(E)\right)\text{ where }\bar{A}(s)(t):=\bar{A}^{s}(t)

such that
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}\bar{A}^{s}(a)
and
\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}\bar{A}^{s}(b)
are both independent on
s
, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{sf}\left(\bar{A}_{t}^{0};t\in[a,b]\right)=\operatorname{sf}\left(\bar{A}_{t}^{1};t\in[a,b]\right)

•

(Path additivity)
If
A^{1},A^{2}\in\mathscr{C}^{0}\left([a,b];\mathscr{C}\mathscr{F}^{sa}(E)\right)
are such that
A^{1}(b)=A^{2}(a)
, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{sf}\left(A_{t}^{1}*A_{t}^{2};t\in[a,b]\right)=\operatorname{sf}\left(A_{t}^{1};t\in[a,b]\right)+\operatorname{sf}\left(A_{t}^{2};t\in[a,b]\right)

where
*
denotes the usual catenation between the two paths.

•

(Nullity)
If
A\in\mathscr{C}^{0}([a,b];\operatorname{GL}(E))
, then
\operatorname{sf}\left(A_{t};t\in[a,b]\right)=0
.
