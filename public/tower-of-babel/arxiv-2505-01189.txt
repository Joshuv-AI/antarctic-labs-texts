Principal Non-singularity of Fourier Matrices on ℤ p × ℤ q \mathbb{Z}_{p}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q} and ℤ 2 k × ℤ q \mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q}
Weiqi Zhou

Abstract
Let F n F_{n} be the n × n n\times n Fourier matrix (on cyclic groups ℤ n \mathbb{Z}_{n} ), a reknowned theorem of Chebotarëv asserts that all minors in F n F_{n} for prime n n are non-zero. In this short note it is shown that (i) all principal minors in the Kronecker product F p ⊗ F q F_{p}\otimes F_{q} are non-vanishing (principal non-singularity) for distinct odd primes p , q p,q if q q is large enough and generates the multiplicative group ℤ p ∗ \mathbb{Z}_{p}^{*} ; (ii) the Fourier matrix on ℤ 2 k × ℤ q \mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q} is principally non-singular upon permutation (in particular, for k = 1 k=1 the identity permutation suffices) for odd prime q q and k = 1 , 2 , 3 k=1,2,3 . The proof is just an exposition of existing techniques re-organized in a unified way. The result will have implications in combining Riesz bases of exponentials. Keywords : Chebotarev’s Theorem, Uncertainty Principle, Principle Minor, Fourier Matrices, Kronecker Product. 2020 MSC : 42A99, 15A15

Introduction

Let
A
be a square matrix whose rows and columns are indexed by the same set, and
\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}
subsets of the index set. Denote by
A[\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}]
the submatrix obtained by taking row indices from
\mathcal{I}
and column indices from
\mathcal{J}
(If
\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}
are singletons then it is the corresponding entry, i.e.,
A[i,j]
means the
i,j
-th entry). A
minor
is the determinant of a submatrix satisfying
|\mathcal{I}|=|\mathcal{J}|
. If in addition
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{J}
, then the submatrix is called
principal
and its determinant is called a
principal minor
. We shall say that a matrix is
principally non-singular
if all its principal minors are non-zero, and
principally non-singular upon permutation
if there is a column permutation so that all principal minors in the permuted matrix are non-zero.

Let
Z_{n}=\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
be the additive cyclic group of order
n
, and set
\zeta_{n}=e^{2\pi i/n}
. The
Fourier matrix
on the finite Abelian group
\mathbb{Z}_{n_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathbb{Z}_{n_{d}}
is the matrix indexed by
\mathbb{Z}_{n_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathbb{Z}_{n_{d}}
with its
j,k
-th entry given by
\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}\zeta_{n_{\ell}}^{j_{\ell}k_{\ell}}
where both
j=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{d})
and
k=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d})
are in
\mathbb{Z}_{n_{1}}\times\ldots\times\mathbb{Z}_{n_{d}}
. It is typical to further attach the factor
n^{-1/2}
with
n=n_{1}\ldots n_{d}
to make the matrix unitary, but in this article it is more convenient not to include this scaling factor. When
d=1
, it is then the usual
n\times n
DFT matrix scaled by
\sqrt{n}
and we denote it by
F_{n}
. Therefore
F_{n}
can also be viewed as an
n\times n
Vandermonde matrix on
n
-th roots of unity.

A celebrated theorem of Chebotarëv (see e.g.,
[
16
]
) states that if
p
is a prime number, then all minors in
F_{p}
are non-zero. This theorem is also equivalent to the enhanced (compared to the usual uncertainty principal as in
[
6
,
15
]
) uncertainty principle (see
[
17
]
) that
|\supp(\vec{x})|+|\supp(F_{p}\vec{x})|\geq p+1
, where
\supp(\vec{x})=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}:x_{k}\neq 0\}
is the
support
of
\vec{x}
. For composite
n
this is in general not true, for example if
n=4
and
\mathcal{I}=\{0,2\}
, then all entries in the principal submatrix
F_{4}[\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}]
are identically
1
.

There have been efforts on understanding whether permuted Fourier matrices can be principally non-singular, this is primarily motivated by classical questions in sampling theory such as when can we combine Riesz exponential bases on disjoint sets to form a new Riesz exponential basis on the union of these sets, or conversely how can we split a Riesz exponential basis on a set to get Riesz exponential bases on each individual component in a partition of the original set. A
Riesz basis
is the image of an orthonormal basis under a bounded (away from both
\infty
and
0
) linear operator. In the absence of an orthogonal exponential basis (e.g., triangles and disks admit no orthogonal Fourier bases
[
9
]
), a Riesz exponential basis would be a good choice for analyzing and reconstructing functions. In the introductory part of e.g.,
[
12
,
14
]
there are rich examples showing the complexity of this problem.

Denote the multiplicative group modulo
n
by
\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{*}
. In simpler settings such as when the underlying set is a finite collection of intervals with rational end points, then the possibility of combing or splitting Riesz exponential bases will rely critically on the principal non-singularity of
F_{n}
upon permutations. The conjecture that
F_{n}
is principally non-singular upon permutation (in particular it is conjectured that the identity permutation shall suffice if
n
is square free) is thereof posed in the recent work
[
2
,
3
]
. This is supported by numerical experiments up to
n=30
and proved for
n=2p
and
n=qr
in
[
13
]
for odd primes
p,q,r
with
q
large enough and generating
\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{*}
. It is also known (see e.g.,
[
1
]
) that as
n
grows the smallest non-zero principal minor in
n^{-1/2}F_{n}
(scaled to be unitary) decay exponentially (e.g., the minimum principal minor is about
10^{-11}
in for
n=30
), which makes them difficult to detect numercially. Principally non-singularity of a Fourier matrix on some finite Abelian group
G
can also be interpreted as a form of uncertainty principle that vectors on
G
and their Fourier transforms can not have disjoint supports.

Let
A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
and
B\in\mathbb{C}^{m^{\prime}\times n^{\prime}}
, the Kronecker product
A\otimes B
is an
mm^{\prime}\times nn^{\prime}
matrix defined as

A\otimes B=\begin{pmatrix}A[0,0]B&\ldots&A[0,n-1]B\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
A[m-1,0]B&\ldots&A[m-1,n-1]B\end{pmatrix}.

(1)

Since any
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{mn}
can be uniquely write as
k=q_{k}n+r_{k}
for some
q_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
and some
r_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, from (
1
) it is clear that
F_{m}\otimes F_{n}
is the Fourier matrix on
\mathbb{Z}_{m}\times\mathbb{Z}_{n}
with the index correspondence being that the
j,k
-th entry in
F_{m}\otimes F_{n}
corresponds to the
(q_{j},q_{k}),(r_{j},r_{k})
-th entry in the Fourier matrix on
\mathbb{Z}_{m}\times\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. This also shows that
F_{m}\otimes F_{n}
only differs from
F_{n}\otimes F_{m}
by a simultaneous permutation.

The purpose of this short note is to show in Corollary
1
and Corollary
2
respectively that
F_{p}\otimes F_{q}
is principally non-singular for distinct odd primes
p,q
if
q
is large enough and generates
Z_{p}^{*}
, and that
\underbrace{F_{2}\otimes\ldots\otimes F_{2}}_{k\text{ terms}}\otimes F_{q}
is principally non-singular upon permutation (in particular, for
k=1

q
and
k=1,2,3
. The proof is essentially the same as in
[
8
,
13
]
with the method summarized and unified in Theorem
1
.

Main Results

The following lemma is originally stated in
[
8
, Lemma 2]
for polynomials on finite fields of prime orders, it actually holds in many cases (e.g., it is true in
\mathbb{C}[x]
) since all we need for it to hold is just a polynomial ring with a well defined notion of formal derivatives that satisfies the product rule, allows factorization into linear factors, and is non-vanishing, hence we state it for polynomials on integral domains for most generality (see e.g.,
[
11
, Chapter III-6, Theorem 6.7, Lemma 6.9, Theorem 6.10]
) and for completeness also include the proof (despite that the proof is exactly the same).

Lemma 1
.

[
8
, Lemma 2]

Let
D
be an integral domain of characteristic
q
, and
p(x)\in D[x]
a polynomial. Suppose that
a\neq 0
is a root of
p(x)
with multiplicity
m
, and
h
is the number of non-zero coefficients of
p(x)
, then
m<h
if one of the following two conditions holds:

•

q>0
and the order of
p(x)
is less than
q
;

•

q=0
.

Proof.

We may without loss of generality assume that the constant term of
p(x)
is not
0
, otherwise we may simply divide the lowest term out. The proof is then by induction on the order of polynomials. For linear polynomials the statement trivially holds. Suppose it holds for all polynomials of order up to
k-1
, and consider now an order
k
polynomial
p(x)
. Since
D
is an integral domain, not only can we write
p(x)=(x-a)^{m}r(x)
for some polynomial
r(x)
with
r(a)\neq 0
, but also we can apply the product rule to compute its formal derivative to be
p^{\prime}(x)=(x-a)^{m-1}\left(mr(x)+(x-a)r^{\prime}(x)\right)
, and verify that the multiplicity of
a
as roots of
p^{\prime}(x)
is
m-1
. Indeed, the term
m(x-a)^{m-1}r(x)
is non-vanishing since either the characteristic is
0
or
m<q
(
D
being an integral domain implies
m\leq k
, while by assumption we will have
k<q
if
q>0
). Since
p(x)
has a non-zero constant term, the number of non-zero coefficient of
p^{\prime}(x)
is precisely
h-1
, thus by the induction assumption we get
m-1<h-1
, which leads to
m<h
as desired.
∎

Theorem 1
.

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
, and suppose that
F
is a matrix in which every entry is an integer power of
\zeta_{m}
, then
F\otimes F_{n}
is principally non-singular if both conditions below are satisfied:

(i)

The ideal
\langle 1-\zeta_{n}\rangle
is prime in
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{mn}]
, and the characteristic of
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{mn}]/\langle 1-\zeta_{n}\rangle
is either
0
or at least
n
;

(ii)

f(F)
is principally non-singular in
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{mn}]/\langle 1-\zeta_{n}\rangle
, where
f
is the canonical epimorphism from
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{mn}]
to
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{mn}]/\langle 1-\zeta_{n}\rangle
.

Proof.

Let
G
be the index set of rows and columns in
F
, we will use
G\times\mathbb{Z}_{n}
to index entries in
F\otimes F_{n}
. The purpose is to show that
(F\otimes F_{n})[\mathcal{K},\mathcal{K}]
is invertible for any given index subset
\mathcal{K}\subseteq G\times\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. First we shall align some notations:

Set

\mathcal{I}=\{i:\left((i,i^{\prime}),(j,j^{\prime})\right)\in\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{K}\}.

Intuitively
i\in\mathcal{I}
means that
(F\otimes F_{n})[\mathcal{K},\mathcal{K}]
contains some rows from the block

\begin{pmatrix}F[i,0]F_{n},&\ldots,&F[i,|G|-1]F_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

For each fixed
i\in G
, further set

\mathcal{I}_{i}=\{i^{\prime}:\left((i,i^{\prime}),(j,j^{\prime})\right)\in\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{K}\},

so that
\mathcal{I}_{i}
contains row indices from the above block that are active in
(F\otimes F_{n})[\mathcal{K},\mathcal{K}]
.

Similarly we will use

\mathcal{J}=\{j:\left((i,i^{\prime}),(j,j^{\prime})\right)\in\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{K}\},\quad\mathcal{J}_{j}=\{j^{\prime}:\left((i,i^{\prime}),(j,j^{\prime})\right)\in\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{K}\},

respectively for active vertical blocks and active column indices in corresponding vertical blocks. Since
(F\otimes F_{n})[\mathcal{K},\mathcal{K}]
is a principal submatrix we certainly have
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{J}
, and
\mathcal{I}_{i}=\mathcal{J}_{j}
if
i=j
.

We may view
(F\otimes F_{n})[\mathcal{K},\mathcal{K}]
as a linear operator on
\left(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{mn})\right)^{|\mathcal{K}|}
, thus to show it is invertible, it suffices to verify that
(F\otimes F_{n})[\mathcal{K},\mathcal{K}]\vec{z}=0
holds only if
\vec{z}=0
for
\vec{z}\in\left(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{mn})\right)^{|\mathcal{K}|}
. Clearly by a scaling we can take
\vec{z}\in\left(\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{mn}]\right)^{|\mathcal{K}|}
instead. Moreover, if
\vec{z}
is not identically zero, then we may without loss of generality assume that there is at least one entry in it that is not divisible by
1-\zeta_{n}
, otherwise we may simply factor
1-\zeta_{n}
out from
\vec{z}
.

For each
j\in\mathcal{J}
, let
\vec{z}^{(j)}\in\left(\mathbb{Z}(\zeta_{mn})\right)^{|\mathcal{J}_{j}|}
be the subvector of
\vec{z}
obtained by keeping only entries whose indices are in
\mathcal{J}_{j}
, then

(F\otimes F_{n})[\mathcal{K},\mathcal{K}]\vec{z}=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}F[i,j]F_{n}\vec{z}^{(j)}=0,\forall i\in\mathcal{I}.

Since
F_{n}
is a Vandermonde matrix, for each
j\in\mathcal{J}
we may further define the polynomial
S_{j}(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{mn}][x]
to be

S_{j}(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{J}_{j}}z^{(j)}_{k}x^{k},

where
z^{(j)}_{k}
is the entry indexed by
k
in
z^{(j)}
, then since
\mathcal{J}_{j}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{n}
by construction, each
S_{j}(x)
is a polynomial of order at most
n-1
, and we get

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}F[i,j]F_{n}\vec{z}^{(j)}=0,\forall i\in\mathcal{I}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}F[i,j]S_{j}(\zeta_{n}^{\ell}),\forall i\in\mathcal{I},\forall\ell\in\mathcal{I}_{i}.

Now for each
i\in\mathcal{I}
, define the polynomial
T_{i}(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{mn}][x]
to be

T_{i}(x)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}F[i,j]S_{j}(x),

and let
\vec{T}(x)
and
\vec{S}(x)
be vectors formed by
T_{i}(x)
and
S_{j}(x)
respectively, then (recall that
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{J}
) we obtain

\vec{T}(x)=F[\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}]\vec{S}(x)=F[\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}]\vec{S}(x),

and each
T_{i}(x)
vanishes on
\zeta_{n}^{\ell}
for all
\ell\in\mathcal{I}_{i}
.

Set
D=\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{mn}]/\langle 1-\zeta_{n}\rangle
, then
(i)
implies that
D
is an integral domain. Apply the canonical epimorphism
f:\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{mn}]\to D
to coefficients of each
T_{i}(x)
, we obtain a new polynomial
\tilde{T}_{i}(x)\in D[x]
. Since
\zeta_{n}=-(1-\zeta_{n})+1
we see that
f(\zeta_{n})=1
, therefore
f(\zeta_{n}^{k})=f(\zeta_{n})^{k}=1
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. Now
T_{i}(x)
vanishing on
\zeta_{n}^{\ell}
for all
\ell\in\mathcal{I}_{i}
implies that it is divisible
\prod_{\ell\in\mathcal{I}_{i}}(x-\zeta_{n}^{\ell})
, consequently
\tilde{T}_{i}(x)
is divisible by
(x-1)^{|\mathcal{I}_{i}|}
.

Let
\tilde{S}_{j}(x),\tilde{S}(x),\tilde{T}(x),\tilde{F}
be the images of
S_{j}(x),\vec{S}(x),\vec{T}(x),F
under
f
respectively. By
(ii)
we also have that

\tilde{S}(x)=(\tilde{F}[\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}])^{-1}\tilde{T}(x).

For each
i\in\mathcal{I}
and each
j\in\mathcal{J}
, denote the entry
(\tilde{F}[\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}])^{-1}[j,i]
(this is well defined since
\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{I}
) by
\tilde{c}_{j,i}
, then we have

\tilde{S}_{j}(x)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\tilde{c}_{j,i}\tilde{T}_{i}(x).

Now let
i_{0},i_{1},\ldots i_{|\mathcal{I}|-1}
be an enumeration of the set
\mathcal{I}
with the property that

|\mathcal{I}_{i_{0}}|\leq|\mathcal{I}_{i_{1}}|\leq\ldots\leq|\mathcal{I}_{i_{|\mathcal{I}|-1}}|,

and set
j_{0}=i_{0},j_{1}=i_{1},\ldots,j_{|\mathcal{J}|-1}=i_{|\mathcal{I}|-1}
(This is well defined since
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{J}
). Then the polynomial
\tilde{S}_{j_{0}}(x)
has
1
as a root of multiplicity
|I_{i_{0}}|
, but by construction it has at most
|J_{j_{0}}|=|I_{i_{0}}|
non-zero coefficients, therefore by Lemma
1
this indicates that
\tilde{S}_{j_{0}}(x)
is identically
0
. Repeat the procejure on
\mathcal{I}\setminus\{i_{0}\}
, and
\mathcal{J}\setminus\{j_{0}\}
, we assert that
\tilde{S}_{j_{1}}(x)
is identically
0
, and so forth
\tilde{S}_{j_{2}}(x),\ldots\tilde{S}_{j_{|\mathcal{J}|-1}}(x)
are all identically
0
. This means
\tilde{S}(x)=0
, and thus every coefficient (i.e.,
z^{(j)}_{k}
for every
j\in\mathcal{J}
and every
k\in\mathcal{J}_{j}
) in
S(x)
is divisible by
1-\zeta_{n}
, which contradicts their choices if they are not identically zero. Therefore
\vec{z}
has to be
0
, which shows that
F\otimes F_{n}
is principally non-singular.
∎

Let
\varphi
be the Euler Totient function, and
\mathbb{F}_{p^{k}}
the finite field of
p^{k}
elements. It is well known (see e.g.,
[
4
, Corollary 2.1.25]
) that
\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{*}
is cyclic if and only if
m\in\{1,2,4,p^{k},2p^{k}:p\text{ odd prime},k\in\mathbb{N}\}
.

Lemma 2
.

[
13
, Lemma 3]

Let
q
be an odd prime and
m\in\mathbb{N}
with
\gcd(m,q)=1
, then the ideal
\langle 1-\zeta_{q}\rangle
is prime in
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{mq}]
if and only if
q
generates
\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{*}
(hence
\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{*}
is cyclic). Moreover, in such case

\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{mq}]/\langle 1-\zeta_{q}\rangle\cong\mathbb{F}_{q^{\varphi(m)}}.

Lemma 3
.

[
13
, Lemma 4]

Let
p,q
be distinct odd primes, then the imgae of
\zeta_{p}
under the canonical epimorphism from
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{pq}]
to
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{pq}]/\langle 1-\zeta^{q}\rangle
is still a primitive
p
-th root of unity.

Lemma 4
.

[
19
, Theorem A]

For every odd prime
p
, there is a constant
\Gamma_{p}
such that if
q>\Gamma_{p}
is another odd prime that generates
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{*}
, then all minors of
F_{p}
(interpreted as the Vandermonde matrix on
p
-th roots of unity in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{p-1}}
) is non-vanishing in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{p-1}}
.

Remark: Roots of unity behaves differently in finite fields compared to in extensions of
\mathbb{Q}
, the usual cyclotomic polynomial may decompose in finite fields. For example one can check that
2
is an
11
-th primitive root of unity in
\mathbb{F}_{23}
, but
2^{4}+2^{2}+2^{1}+2^{0}=23=0\pmod{23}
. This is the reason why
F_{p}
may have vanishing minors in finite fields (see
[
19
, Example 4.1, Example 4.3]
). Now since
q^{p-1}=1\pmod{p}
, in the setting of Lemma
4
, the
p
-th cyclotomic polynomial will actually decompose into linear factors in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{p-1}}
(this can be derived by e.g., applying
[
18
, Theorem 59]
). The proof of
[
19
, Theorem A]
, as well as methods in e.g.,
[
5
]
, use the formula of generalized Vandermonde determinant in
[
7
]
and checks the sum of coefficients of the Schur function in the formula. Since The structure of either a Fourier matrix or a principal submatrix is not fully reflected in this formula, and there is no direct example for vanishing principal minors, we may ask that, is it possible to remove the condition
q>\Gamma_{p}
in Lemma
4
if we only ask for principal non-singularity?

Corollary 1
.

Let
p,q
be distinct odd primes, if
q
generates
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{*}
and
q>\Gamma_{p}
(whose value is defined in Lemma
4
), then
F_{p}\otimes F_{q}
is principally non-singular.

Proof.

The statement is proved by applying Theorem
1
on
F=F_{p}
and
n=q
. Indeed, when we apply the canonical epimorphism
f:\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{pq}]\to\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{pq}]/\langle 1-\zeta_{q}\rangle
, we see that
(i)
is satisfied by Lemma
2
, while
(ii)
is satisfied by combining Lemma
3
(which indicates that
f(\zeta_{p})
remains a primitive
p
-th root of unity) and Lemma
4
(which shows that
f(F_{p})
is principally non-singular). Therefore by Theorem
1
,
F_{p}\otimes F_{q}
is principally non-singular.
∎

Lemma 5
.

[
8
, Lemma 1]

If
q
is a prime, then the ideal
\langle 1-\zeta_{q}\rangle
is prime in
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{q}]
and
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{q}]/\langle 1-\zeta_{q}\rangle\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Corollary 2
.

Let
F_{2}^{\otimes k}=\underbrace{F_{2}\otimes\ldots\otimes F_{2}}_{k\text{ terms}}
, if
q
is an odd prime and
k\in\{1,2,3\}
, then
F_{2}^{\otimes k}\otimes F_{q}
is principally non-singular upon permutation. In particular, for
k=1
the identity permutation suffices.

Proof.

We will prove the statement by applying Theorem
1
with
F=F_{2}^{\otimes k}P
and
n=q
for some appropriate permutation matrix
P
. Notice also that in this setting we have
m=2
.

Since
q
is odd we get
\zeta_{2q}=-\zeta_{q}^{2}
, which indicates that
\mathbb{Z}(\zeta_{2q})=\mathbb{Z}(\zeta_{q})
. Thus applying Lemma
5
we see that
(i)
holds for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Every entry in
F_{2}^{\otimes k}
is either
1
or
-1
, which is still
\pm 1
under the canonical epimorphism from
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{q}]
to
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{q}]/\langle 1-\zeta_{q}\rangle
. A principal minor will only vanish if it is divisible by
q
. Therefore if there is a permutation
P
such that every principal minor in
F_{2}^{\otimes k}P
is a power of
2
, then
(ii)
will be true for all
q
.

If
k=1
, then this trivially holds with
P
being the identity permutation
I
. For
k=2,3
the corresponding permutations and the principal non-singularities after permutations are produced and verified by an exhaustive search using the Python program in the appendix. There are
8
such permutations for
k=2
and
64
such permutations for
k=3
.

Now as both conditions are satisfied, we can conclude by Theorem
1
that
F\otimes F_{q}
is principally non-singular. Then using the identity (see e.g.,
[
10
, Lemma 4.2.10]
) that
(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D)=(AC)\otimes(BD)
when the multiplications
AC,BD
are well defined, and denote by
I
the identity permutation on
\mathbb{C}^{q\times q}
we get

F\otimes F_{q}=(F_{2}^{\otimes k}P)\otimes(F_{q}I)=(F_{2}^{\otimes k}\otimes F_{q})(P\otimes I).

Since
P\otimes I
is again a permutation matrix, this indicates that
F_{2}^{\otimes k}\otimes F_{q}
is principally non-singular upon permutation.
∎

It is worth mentioning that
F_{2}^{\otimes k}
is a real Hadamard matrix of Sylvester type, and at the same time a representation of Walsh basis. Thus its principal non-singularity upon permutation can be used for constructing Walsh Riesz basis on subcollections of dyadic intervals. It is also tempting to ask whether the principal non-singularity upon permutation in all finite fields is a property also possessed by other real Hadamard matrices.

Appendix

The following Python 3 code is used to verify that
F_{2}\otimes F_{2}
and
F_{2}\otimes F_{2}\otimes F_{2}
are principally non-singular upon permutation in finite fields. It merely generates all possible column permutations and for each permutation looks through all principal minors. The largest principal submatrices are those
7\times 7
submatrices in
F_{2}\otimes F_{2}\otimes F_{2}
, their determinants will not exceed
7!=5040<8192=2^{13}
in absolute value (since every entry is either
1
or
-1
), thus for each principal minor we only need to check whether its absolute value is in the set
\{2^{k}:k=0,1,\ldots,13\}
.

import scipy.linalg

import itertools,numpy,math

bound=3

bPowers={1}

x=1

while x<=math.factorial((1<<bound)-1):

x*=2

bPowers.add(x)

F2=((1,1),(1,-1))

F=[[1,1],[1,-1]]

n=2

for k in range(2,bound+1):

F=numpy.kron(F,F2)

n*=2

res=[]

for perm in itertools.permutations(range(n)):

isSingular=False

for m in range(2,n):

for comb in itertools.combinations(range(n),m):

I,J=numpy.ix_(list(comb),[perm[x] for x in comb])

d=round(abs(scipy.linalg.det(F[I,J])))

if d not in bPowers:

isSingular=True

break

if isSingular:

break

if not isSingular:

res.append(perm)

print(f"There are {len(res)} valid permutations for k={k}:")

*map(print,res),

print("Job Completed.")
