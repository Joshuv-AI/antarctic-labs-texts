Emotional resilience and affective forecasting bias in junior high school students: the moderating role of negative coping
Authors: Xinze Liu, Yanqing Su, Lin Zhang, Shengqiang Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndividuals' affective forecasts often systematically differ from their actual affective experiences, a phenomenon formally termed affective forecasting bias. This bias may compound academic stress and undermine long-term psychological adjustment, necessitating heightened attention.MethodsIn Study 1, we examined 248 ninth-grade students in a real examination scenario in 2025 to validate the existence of affective forecasting bias. In Study 2, participants from Study 1 were selected based on the top and bottom 27% of emotional resilience scores, while their negative coping styles were simultaneously measured to investigate the impact of emotional resilience on affective forecasting bias and the moderating role of negative coping styles.ResultsJunior high school students demonstrated significant affective forecasting bias. Emotional resilience significantly influenced affective forecasting bias, with individuals exhibiting higher emotional resilience showing smaller affective forecasting bias. Negative coping styles negatively moderated the effect of emotional resilience on affective forecasting bias.ConclusionsJunior high school students exhibit affective forecasting bias, which is influenced by emotional resilience, and this influence is moderated by negative coping styles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04543-0.

Introduction

Emotions play a pivotal role in human life, being not only integral to mental health but also exerting significant influence on decision-making and cognitive processes. Human psychological functioning extends beyond immediate stimuli or situational contexts, suggesting that decision-making is shaped not merely by transient emotional states but also by anticipatory evaluations of potential emotional consequences [41]. Human affective forecasting frequently demonstrates systematic inaccuracies, manifesting discernible discrepancies between anticipated and actual affective experiences [15], a phenomenon termed affective forecasting bias. This cognitive bias may engender detrimental decision-making, compromise psychological well-being, undermine public policy efficacy, and disrupt interpersonal dynamics. Given these ramifications, the psychological research community has prioritized investigating the mechanisms underlying this bias, with the ultimate aim of developing targeted interventions to mitigate its adverse societal and individual impacts.

Affective forecasting bias has demonstrated cross-situational prevalence [3, 5, 10, 13, 25]. Research across diverse experimental contexts has revealed the stability of this bias among participants of varying demographic groups. Adolescence is a critical developmental period characterized by rapid psychological and emotional development [33], often accompanied by heightened vulnerability to emotional disorders such as depression and anxiety. Adolescent mental health remains a critical societal concern. Notably, adolescents also exhibit affective forecasting bias. For instance, Geng et al. [14] conducted a study with junior high school participants, requiring them to forecast affective responses to hypothetical outcomes (victory or defeat against top performers) in academic competitions. The results revealed that participants systematically overestimated their affective experiences.

Academic performance constitutes a central developmental task during adolescence and serves as a primary context for affective experiences [35]. In China, ninth-grade students face the critical transition to upper secondary education, a stage marked by significantly intensified academic pressure. The general increase in schoolwork pressure among adolescents may have contributed to the rise in emotional problems during this developmental period [1]. Students experience higher levels of stress and anxiety when facing examinations, with fear of failure being a primary source of pressure [32]. While academic outcomes are not the sole metric for evaluating adolescents, the quality and fluctuations of grades are intensely scrutinized by both adolescents themselves and their social circles, creating emotionally charged scenarios characterized by high self-relevance. Building on this, Sun et al. [35] empirically validated the existence of academic affective forecasting bias in junior high school students through real-world examinations and laboratory-simulated scenarios. Their work further highlights the critical role of prospective academic emotions in educational decision-making and learning engagement.

Adolescence represents a pivotal period for psychological and emotional development. The persistence of affective forecasting bias may exert detrimental effects on adolescent growth, development, and mental health. As examinations constitute a frequent stressor for adolescents, with outcomes directly impacting emotional states, this study employs real-world examination settings with junior high school participants to further validate the presence of affective forecasting bias in authentic academic contexts (Hypothesis 1).

Current research has extensively explored the mechanisms underlying affective forecasting bias. From an attentional perspective, individuals often exhibit forecasting biases due to partial processing of event information during affective forecasting. Representative theories include the isolation effect [10] and focalism (Levine & Cohen, 2017) [26, 30]. Whereas the isolation effect refers to individuals' systematic neglect of common attributes across decision alternatives during option evaluation, the focalism arises from the tendency to overweight focal events while overlooking other contextual factors that may influence emotional outcomes. From a conscious awareness perspective, affective forecasting bias may arise from individuals' misestimation of their own traits, abilities, or behavioral responses, as demonstrated by phenomena such as the region-β paradox [16], pleasure paradox [15, 42], and immune neglect [13, 23, 25], all of which stem from individuals' neglect of their own affective regulatory mechanisms. Recent studies further suggest that personality factors, including the Big Five personality traits [22], emotional intelligence [21], and trait anxiety [39], significantly influence the direction and magnitude of forecasting biases [34].

Resilience, defined as a personality trait reflecting stress adaptation capacity [8], emphasizes recovery competence and positive coping styles [45]. Evidence indicates that affective forecasting might serve as a resilience source in the presence of difficulties, positively biased positive affective forecasting (i.e., overestimating positive emotional states) was associated with higher resilience [7], revealing an intrinsic connection between resilience and affective forecasting bias. Notably, resilience shows positive associations with trait mindfulness [46]. Sun et al. [35] revealed that adolescents with elevated trait mindfulness exhibit reduced forecasting biases, implying potential resilience effects through mindfulness pathways.

However, existing resilience research inadequately addresses normative adolescents' emotional competence in negative contexts, limiting developmental implications. Moreover, emotional factors remain understudied in resilience research [48]. Therefore, Zhang Min introduced Davidson et al.'s conceptualization of emotional resilience into China, defining it as an individual's ability to generate positive emotions and rapidly recover from negative affective experiences [9]. Zhang and Lu [48] developed the Adolescent Emotional Resilience Questionnaire (AERQ) to measure this construct and subsequently conducted a series of empirical studies using this instrument [49–51]. As an essential resilience component emphasizing emotional regulation [48], emotional resilience demonstrates stronger relevance to adolescent development and affective forecasting research. From a construct perspective, emotional resilience focuses on the capacity for emotional recovery, directly corresponding to the durability bias in affective forecasting [41]—that is, the miscalculation of the duration of emotional reactions. From a mechanism perspective, immune neglect refers to individuals overlooking the existence of a psychological immune system that can mitigate negative emotions, thus tending to overestimate the duration of negative affect [17]. Emotional resilience precisely aligns with this automatic process of emotional recovery. Furthermore, while emotion regulation involves conscious, strategic choices [18], emotional resilience emphasizes automatic emotional recovery processes, which are more likely to lead to systematic discrepancies between forecasts and experiences. Furthermore, a significant positive correlation has been observed between emotional resilience and mindfulness [38], suggesting mindfulness may exert its regulatory effects on emotional processes through the mediating pathway of emotional resilience. Building on resilience findings, we hypothesize that higher emotional resilience will predict smaller affective forecasting biases (Hypothesis 2). Meanwhile, the trait-congruent effect also supports this hypothesis [20], where individuals' stable personality traits predispose them to preferentially process information consistent with their characteristics. Emotional resilience, as a personality trait, reflects weaker positive emotional capacity in low-resilience individuals, rendering them more susceptible to negative emotional stimuli. Additionally, lower resilience implies poorer emotional recovery ability [50], meaning these individuals maintain negative emotional states longer and demonstrate heightened attention and memory biases toward negative information [51]. From an attentional perspective, the focalism constitutes one of the key mechanisms underlying affective forecasting bias. This phenomenon refers to individuals' excessive focus on the target event while neglecting other contextual factors in daily life that may influence emotional states. Zhang et al.'s research revealed that low-resilience participants exhibited stronger attentional biases toward to negative-valenced emotional stimuli, exacerbating focalism and consequently larger forecasting bias. Regarding memory mechanisms, the learning-from-experience hypothesis posits that accurate recall of past affective experiences is crucial for adaptive learning [43]. Memory biases, identified as a critical cognitive contributor to forecasting bias [34], were more pronounced in low-resilience individuals who showed enhanced memory retention for negative information.

Coping styles refer to the cognitive and behavioral patterns individuals adopt when confronted with frustration and stress, also termed as coping styles or mechanisms [47]. This construct is typically categorized into problem-focused coping and emotion-focused coping dimensions [12], The Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire (SCSQ; [44]) operationalizes coping styles through two dimensions: positive coping (e.g., problem-solving, seeking social support) and negative coping (e.g., avoidance, wishful thinking).

Negative coping encompasses avoidant and emotion-venting styles when managing stressful events [19]. The negative valence effect in affective forecasting [6] suggests greater forecasting bias for negative versus positive events. Positive coping styles have been shown to exhibit a negative correlation with negative emotional states such as anxiety and depression [4]. These styles may attenuate the psychological impact of adverse events, thereby reducing affective forecasting bias. Consequently, negative coping styles are more suitable for investigating the mechanisms underlying affective forecasting bias.

Resilience demonstrates negative associations with negative coping [36], indicating that individuals with lower resilience tend to adopt more negative coping styles. When individuals predominantly adopt negative coping styles, even with high levels of emotional resilience and relatively strong emotion regulation abilities, the influence of these coping mechanisms persists, ultimately leading to greater affective forecasting bias. Conversely, when individuals employ lower levels of negative coping, combined with heightened emotional resilience and robust emotion regulation capabilities that facilitate proactive problem-solving, the magnitude of affective forecasting bias is significantly reduced. Therefore, negative coping styles may weaken the protective effect of emotional resilience on affective forecasting bias. According to the immune neglect perspective, when individuals face negative events, effective coping strategies can paradoxically lead to greater affective forecasting bias. Individuals who employ emotion-processing strategies exhibit higher levels of affective forecasting bias compared to those who employ emotion-avoidance strategies [23]. This occurs because effective coping strategies (the psychological immune system help individuals recover emotionally from negative events more effectively,however, individuals tend to overlook this adaptive capacity when making forecasts, leading them to overestimate the impact of negative events and consequently exhibit greater affective forecasting bias. Based on this theoretical framework, we propose Hypothesis 3: Negative coping will moderate the relationship between emotional resilience and affective forecasting bias.

Present investigation

The present investigation examined these hypotheses through two studies with junior high school students. Study 1 validated the existence of affective forecasting bias in junior high school students using a real-world examination context. Building upon this paradigm, Study 2 investigated the impact of emotional resilience on affective forecasting bias, while exploring the moderating role of negative coping styles in the relationship between emotional resilience and affective forecasting bias.

Study 1: ecological validation of affective forecasting bias

In Study 1, we conducted a field experiment within examination contexts eliciting high emotional involvement among junior high school students to test Hypothesis 1. Furthermore, given the controversial findings in previous research regarding whether emotional valence influences affective forecasting bias, the present study investigated this issue. It identified the relationship between emotional valence and affective forecasting bias and offered a preliminary explanation for the controversial nature of this conclusion.

Method

Measures

Affective forecasting assessment

Participants were asked to predict their grade ranking in the upcoming final examination. They then rated on a 9-point scale (1 = not at all happy/upset, 9 = extremely happy/upset) how happy they would feel if the predicted ranking was achieved (affective forecasting for success) and how upset they would feel if it was not achieved (affective forecasting for failure). This scale adopted a positive scoring format.

Affective experience assessment

Participants were instructed to recall their pre-exam predicted ranking after receiving actual results. They first reported whether their actual ranking matched the prediction. For those who achieved their predicted ranking, they rated their actual happiness level using the same 9-point scale (affective experience of success). For those who failed to meet the prediction, they rated their actual upset level using the identical scale (affective experience of failure). This scale used positive scoring.

Procedures

The experimental procedure was as follows:Participant recruitment: Six classes were randomly selected from the ninth grade of a middle school, using cluster sampling with class as the unit. Data screening: To ensure the quality of the questionnaire responses, attention-check items (e.g., "Please select 4 for this item") were included. These items were not scored and were used solely to identify inattentive respondents. Based on participants' responses, 29 participants who failed the attention-check items were excluded. In addition, due to participant attrition in the measurement of affective forecasting bias (which involved both pre-test and post-test), 7 pre-test questionnaires with missing post-test data and 6 post-test questionnaires with missing pre-test data were also excluded. Grouping criteria: One week before the final exam, participants completed the affective forecasting questionnaire, in which they predicted their grade ranking in the upcoming exam, as well as their anticipated happiness if the prediction was achieved and their anticipated distress if it was not. On the day the grade rankings were announced, participants completed an affective experience questionnaire, in which they recalled their initially predicted ranking and reported whether their actual ranking met that prediction. Participants who achieved their predicted ranking were assigned to the positive group and rated their actual happiness; those who did not were assigned to the negative group and rated their actual distress.

Participants and design

Participants

Power analysis using G*Power 3.1 [11], revealed minimum sample sizes of 27 for one-sample t-test and 128 for independent-samples t-test(α = 0.05, two-tailed; power = 0.80, d = 0.5). We specifically recruited ninth-grade students from a public junior high school in Jinan through cluster sampling (6 classes), as this cohort approaching high school entrance examinations may demonstrate heightened academic investment. The final analytical sample comprised 248 participants (123 male, 125 female) with a mean age of 14.48 years (SD = 0.58).

Design

The study employed a single-factor between-subjects experimental design. The independent variable was emotional valence with two levels (positive vs. negative). Participants were divided into positive and negative groups based on whether their actual post-exam ranking achieved their predicted grade ranking. The dependent variable was affective forecasting bias, calculated as the absolute value of the discrepancy between affective forecasting values (pre-exam predictions) and affective experience values (post-exam reports).

Results and discussion

Testing affective forecasting bias (Hypothesis 1)

One-sample t-tests comparing affective forecasting bias scores with zero (indicating no forecasting bias) revealed significant deviations in both emotional conditions: positive group, t(137) = 10.56, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 1.80, 95% CI = [1.32, 1.93]; negative group, t(109) = 11.83, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 2.27, 95% CI = [1.71, 2.40]. These findings demonstrate that junior high school students exhibited systematic affective forecasting biases under both positive and negative emotional contexts, thereby confirming Hypothesis 1.

Valence impacts affective forecasting bias

First, a manipulation check for emotional valence was conducted by performing an independent samples t-test on the predicted rankings of the positive and negative groups. The results showed no significant difference in predicted rankings between the positive group (M = 270.61, SD = 260.47) and the negative group(M = 265.38, SD = 277.46), t(246) = −0.15, p = 0.88, Cohen's d = −0.02, 95% CI = [−72.73, 62.28], indicating that the two groups were comparable in terms of prediction difficulty. An independent-samples t-test revealed no significant difference in affective forecasting bias between the positive valence group and negative valence group, t(246) = 1.86, p = 0.06, Cohen's d = −0.24, 95% CI = [−0.03, 0.89], suggesting that emotional valence did not significantly affect participants' forecasting biases. A further independent samples t-test was conducted on the signed bias of the positive and negative groups. The results revealed a significant difference between the two groups, t(246) = −3.10, p = < 0.01, Cohen's d = −0.40, 95% CI = [−1.62, −0.36]. In conjunction with the descriptive statistics (see Table 1), the mean signed bias for the positive group was positive, indicating that participants significantly overestimated their level of happiness when making affective forecasts. In contrast, the mean signed bias for the negative group was negative, indicating that participants significantly underestimated how upset they would feel. These findings demonstrate a significant difference in the direction of affective forecasting bias between the two groups. Study 2: effects of emotional resilience moderated by negative coping

Study 1 demonstrated the existence of affective forecasting bias and explored the influence of emotional valence. Building on this, Study 2 introduced the personality variable of emotional resilience, which was operationalized using the extreme grouping method, to examine its effect on affective forecasting bias and its interaction with emotional valence. Additionally, Study 2 analyzed the moderating effect of negative coping styles on the relationship between emotional resilience and affective forecasting bias, thereby testing Hypotheses 2 and 3.

Method

Measures

Measurement of emotional resilience

The AERQ [48] was employed to assess emotional resilience. This 11-item scale consists of two dimensions: positive emotional competence (6 items,e.g., "When in a bad mood, I can think of something happy") and emotional recovery competence (5 items; e.g., " Criticism from others makes me feel upset for a long time"), with responses rated on a 6-point scale ranging from 1 ("completely disagree") to 6 ("completely agree"). In this scale, items 1, 3, 4, 8, and 9 (positive emotional competence dimension) are positively scored, while items 2, 5, 6, 7, 10, and 11 (emotional recovery competence dimension) are reverse-scored. Originally validated for adolescents aged 11–20 years, the measure demonstrated good reliability in the current sample, showing a Cronbach’s α of 0.85 for the total scale, with subscale α coefficients of 0.82 (positive emotional competence) and 0.83 (emotional recovery competence).

Measurement of negative coping

The SCSQ [44], specifically validated for Chinese student populations, was administered in this study. This instrument comprises two dimensions: positive coping and negative coping styles. Our investigation focused on the 8-item negative coping subscale, utilizing a 4-point response format ranging from 0 ("not adopted") to 3 ("frequently adopted"). Sample items from the negative coping subscale include "Try to forget the whole thing" and "Rely on others to solve problems." No items in this scale are reverse-scored. Psychometric evaluation demonstrated satisfactory reliability, with Cronbach's α coefficients of 0.79 for the full scale and 0.76 for the negative coping subscale.

Measurement of affective forecasting bias

For the assessment of affective forecasting values, based on Study 1, two additional items were added. In addition to requiring participants to predict their grade ranking, anticipated happiness level upon achieving the ranking, and anticipated upset level if failing to achieve it, participants were further asked to predict the likelihood of both achieving and not achieving this ranking. These predictions were rated on an 11-point scale ranging from −5 (very low) to + 5 (very high). The assessment of affective experience values was consistent with the methodology employed in Study 1.

The grouping criteria for emotional valence were identical to those used in Study 1.

Participants and design

Participants

Power analysis using G*Power 3.1 indicated minimum sample sizes of 128 for ANOVA (α = 0.05, two-tailed; power = 0.80; effect size f = 0.25) and 77 for multiple linear regression (effect size f2 = 0.15). From the original pool in Study 1, we selected participants scoring in the upper and lower 27th percentiles on the AERQ. This study focuses specifically on the impact of different levels of emotional resilience on affective forecasting bias, with no need for further differentiation among members within the high and low groups. Therefore, the extreme-group approach was adopted. This method offers the clearest differentiation. When intra-group score variations are not required for analysis, discarding the less discriminative middle-range data can actually avoid interference from uninformative data, thereby maximizing the discriminative value of the core data. When the item variable is continuously scored, the 27% cutoff represents the optimal choice [24]. This screening yielded 131 junior high school students (Mage = 14.52 years, SD = 0.60; 65 males; 66 females) comprising two groups: high-resilience (n = 67) and low-resilience (n = 64).

Design

The study adopted a 2 (emotional resilience: high vs. low) × 2 (emotional valence: positive vs. negative) between-subjects factorial design to investigate the effects of emotional resilience on affective forecasting bias. Negative coping styles were further incorporated as a moderator variable to examine its regulatory role in the relationship between emotional resilience and forecasting bias.

Results and discussion

Emotional resilience and affective forecasting bias (Hypothesis 2)

Based on participants' scores on the AERQ, the 27th and 73rd percentile cut-off values were calculated as 35 and 44.77, respectively. Participants with scores below 35 were assigned to the low-resilience group, while those with scores above 44.77 were assigned to the high-resilience group. Subsequent analyses were conducted on the grouped data. The results of the ANOVA (with descriptive statistics shown in Table 2) using emotional resilience and emotional valence as independent variables revealed significant effects on affective forecasting bias. The main effect of emotional resilience was significant, F (1, 127) = 4.81, p < 0.05, η2 = 0.04, with the high-resilience group demonstrating significantly lower forecasting bias (M = 1.62, SD = 0.23) than the low-resilience group (M = 2.33, SD = 0.23). No significant main effect emerged for emotional valence, F (1, 127) = 0.04, p = 0.85, η2 = 0.000, indicating comparable bias levels between positive and negative valence conditions. The interaction effect was non-significant, F (1, 127) = 0.30, p = 0.58, η2 = 0.002. These findings support Hypothesis 2. A further ANOVA was conducted with signed bias as the dependent variable (see Table 3). The main effect of emotional resilience was not significant, F (1, 127) = 1.74, p = 0.19, η2 = 0.01, indicating that although the level of emotional resilience influenced affective forecasting bias, it did not show a clear tendency toward overestimation or underestimation. The main effect of emotional valence was significant, F (1, 127) = 4.50, p < 0.05, η2 = 0.03. In conjunction with the descriptive statistics, the mean signed bias for the positive group was positive (M = 0.97, SD = 0.31), whereas the mean signed bias for the negative group was negative(M = −0.003, SD = 0.34), indicating a significant difference in the direction of affective forecasting bias between the two groups. This finding is consistent with the results of Study 1. Emotional resilience and likelihood

An independent samples t-test was conducted with emotional resilience as the independent variable, and the likelihood of achieving or failing to achieve grade ranking as dependent variables. The results revealed a statistically significant difference between the high emotional resilience group and the low emotional resilience group in their predicted likelihood of achieving the desired ranking, t(129) = −3.69, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = −0.65, 95% CI = [−2.06, −0.62]. The high emotional resilience group reported significantly higher predicted likelihood of success (M = 2.09, SD = 2.09) compared to the low emotional resilience group (M = 0.75, SD = 2.06), indicating that individuals with higher emotional resilience exhibited more optimistic estimates of exam outcomes. In contrast, no significant difference was observed between the two groups in their predicted likelihood of failing to achieve the ranking, t(120) = 1.92, p = 0.06, Cohen's d = 0.35, 95% CI = [−0.02, 1.54]. Although nonsignificant, the high emotional resilience group showed marginally lower estimates of failure likelihood (M = −0.81, SD = 2.61) compared to the low emotional resilience group (M = −0.05, SD = 1.86), suggesting a tendency for individuals with lower emotional resilience to display more pessimistic evaluations of potential negative outcomes.

The moderating effect of negative coping (Hypothesis 3)

A stronger negative correlation was observed between emotional resilience and negative coping styles (M = 17.56, SD = 4.96), r = −0.38, p < 0.01. However, no significant association emerged between negative coping styles and affective forecasting bias, r = −0.06, p = 0.48. General discussion

Our research selected ninth-grade students as participants and utilized real exam scenarios as experimental contexts to investigate junior high school students' affective forecasting biases. The results of Study 1 supported Hypothesis 1, confirming that middle school students exhibit affective forecasting biases across both positive and negative emotional states, consistent with previous findings. The study by Sun et al. [35] posits that emotional valence influences affective forecasting bias, with greater forecasting bias observed under failure feedback conditions compared to success feedback conditions. This suggests individuals exhibit heightened forecasting bias in negative emotional contexts. When Hoerger and Quirk [22] investigated the relationship between affective forecasting and the Big Five, they found no significant difference in the effect sizes between positive and negative affect. This indicates that conclusions regarding whether emotional valence influences affective forecasting biases remain contradictory. This study analyzed the influence of emotional valence using absolute affective forecasting bias and signed affective forecasting bias as dependent variables. The results revealed that emotional valence had no significant effect on absolute bias but did have a significant effect on signed bias. Specifically, while emotional valence did not significantly influence the accuracy of affective forecasts, the direction of forecasting bias (overestimation vs. underestimation) varied as a function of emotional valence. A preliminary analysis of this seemingly contradictory finding is provided.

The results of Study 2 supported Hypothesis 2, indicating that emotional resilience influences affective forecasting bias. Participants with higher emotional resilience exhibited smaller forecasting biases compared to those with lower resilience. This result can be explained by the trait-congruent effect: individuals with low emotional resilience are more prone to attentional bias and mnemonic bias toward negative emotional information, making them more susceptible to focalism and memory bias, thereby leading to greater affective forecasting bias. Simultaneously, the Cognitive Appraisal Theory of Stress offers a robust framework for explaining the relationship between emotional resilience and affective forecasting bias. According to this theory [27], individuals engage in a cognitive appraisal process when confronted with stressful events, which comprises two distinct stages: primary appraisal and secondary appraisal. Primary appraisal focuses on evaluating the significance and potential impact of the event on one's well-being, whereas secondary appraisal involves an assessment of one's own coping abilities and available resources. Emotional resilience may reduce affective forecasting bias by influencing both of these appraisal stages. First, during the primary appraisal stage, individuals with high emotional resilience are more likely to appraise a potential stressor as a challenge rather than a threat, thereby mitigating the intensity bias. Second, during the secondary appraisal stage, emotionally resilient individuals are better able to accurately assess their coping resources and adaptive capacities, recognizing that they possess effective strategies to manage stress. This accurate self-assessment helps to reduce the duration bias. In contrast, individuals with low emotional resilience are more susceptible to cognitive biases during the appraisal process, such as threat focus and resource underestimation. These biases lead them to systematically overestimate the emotional consequences of stressful events, resulting in larger affective forecasting bias. In conclusion, by optimizing the stages of the cognitive appraisal process, emotional resilience serves as a significant protective factor for the accuracy of affective forecasts. Notably, Study 2 revealed non-significant valence-resilience interactions, suggesting resilience's influence operates independently of emotional valence. Participants with higher emotional resilience demonstrated reduced affective forecasting bias across both positive and negative contexts. Additionally, resilience modulated likelihood estimations: high-resilience participants predicted greater likelihood of achieving target rankings, reflecting optimistic future expectations. Although no significant between-group difference emerged in predicting unfavorable outcomes, low-resilience individuals displayed a trend toward pessimistic estimations, reinforcing trait-congruence effects.

The results of Study 2 also supported Hypothesis 3, indicating that negative coping styles attenuates the protective effect of emotional resilience on affective forecasting bias. After controlling for demographic variables such as gender and age, emotional resilience negatively predicted affective forecasting bias, consistent with Hypothesis 2. Negative coping styles negatively predicted affective forecasting bias, a finding that can be explained by immune neglect: individuals underestimate the emotional recovery function of their own psychological immune system (e.g., effective coping strategies), such that when facing negative events, those with stronger coping abilities paradoxically exhibit greater affective forecasting bias due to their failure to anticipate this adaptive advantage. Combining cognitive neural mechanisms, the prefrontal cortex–amygdala region contains a broad distributed network that includes not only the amygdala and ventromedial prefrontal cortex but also the lateral prefrontal cortex and dorsomedial prefrontal cortex [28]. Among these, the lateral prefrontal cortex and dorsomedial prefrontal cortex regulate negative emotions through cognitive reappraisal. There is a close association between the decline in individuals' executive function and the brain structure and function related to emotion regulation. The prefrontal cortex is a critical brain region responsible for executive function and emotion regulation, and its volume positively predicts emotional resilience. Negative coping styles impair individuals' executive function [2], thereby affecting their emotion regulation capacity. This impairment makes it difficult for individuals to effectively reevaluate negative events or inhibit excessive attention to negative emotional cues. Thus, negative coping styles may weaken the protective function of emotional resilience.

Constraints on generality

This study expands the theoretical framework regarding personality determinants of affective forecasting bias, employing authentic examination scenarios to enhance ecological validity. The research involved third-year junior high school students from six classes. The exclusive selection of final-year junior high students was based on their heightened emotional engagement with examination contexts due to proximity to high school entrance exams, compared to students in the initial two grades. While gender distribution demonstrated relative balance and inter-class variations in educational environments and academic performance enhanced sample diversity, limitations exist in cultural heterogeneity. The Chinese-language measurement instruments, though appropriate for studying Chinese students, may constrain generalizability to other cultural contexts. Future investigations should incorporate cross-cultural samples and diverse age cohorts to validate these findings.

Study contributions

Our research holds both theoretical and practical significance. Theoretically, first, it further corroborates the existence of affective forecasting bias, providing empirical evidence for the universality of this phenomenon. Second, it reveals the impact of emotional resilience on affective forecasting bias, enriching theoretical frameworks concerning the mechanisms underlying affective forecasting bias by introducing a novel trait dimension in personality. Furthermore, the investigation of the negative moderating role of negative coping styles unveils the complexity of the relationship between emotional resilience and affective forecasting bias. Practically, this research offers pratical implications. Given that affective forecasting bias can influence adolescents' mental health and decision-making processes, numerous scholars have explored interventions targeting such biases. Current research on interventions has primarily focused on addressing two underlying mechanisms — focalism and immune neglect. Approaches such as defocusing exercise [34] and adaptation training [37] have been employed for targeted interventions. By elucidating the influence of emotional resilience and the moderating effects of negative coping styles, this study offers novel perspectives for future interventions. These findings may ultimately contribute to enhancing adolescents' psychological well-being and facilitating more rational decision-making processes.

Limitations and future directions

Our research has several limitations. First, while we identified the moderating role of negative coping styles, other potential moderators or mediating variables between emotional resilience and affective forecasting bias may exist. Future research should explore additional moderators influencing this relationship and investigate the mediating factors in the mechanisms through which emotional resilience affects affective forecasting bias, thereby better elucidating their complex interplay. Second, although we examined emotional resilience's influence on affective forecasting bias, no intervention targeting emotional resilience was implemented. Future studies could develop effective interventions to enhance emotional resilience, potentially reducing the adverse effects of forecasting bias. Third, our measurement of affective forecasting bias relied solely on self-report scales administered before and after participants' final exams. Previous research has investigated physiological indicators during affective forecasting, revealing distinctive neural activation patterns in specific brain regions associated with this process [29, 31, 52]. Future work should incorporate more objective neurophysiological measures to deepen understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying forecasting bias. Finally, in analyzing the impact of emotional resilience on affective forecasting bias and the moderating effect of negative coping styles, this study employed an extreme group approach for the core variable of emotional resilience. While this method enhanced inter-group differentiation, it did not utilize all of the original data, which may potentially reduce the generalizability of the research findings. Future studies should consider using larger samples while retaining data from participants with moderate levels of emotional resilience in the analysis, in order to improve the external validity of the conclusions.

Conclusion

This study examined junior high school students in authentic examination contexts, investigating the impact of emotional resilience on affective forecasting bias and the moderating role of negative coping styles in this relationship. The findings revealed three key insights: First, middle school students demonstrated systematic affective forecasting biases. Second, emotional resilience significantly influenced these biases, with individuals exhibiting higher resilience showing attenuated forecasting bias. Third, negative coping styles negatively moderated the negative predictive effect of emotional resilience on affective forecasting bias, with this effect diminishing as individuals increasingly adopted negative coping styles.
