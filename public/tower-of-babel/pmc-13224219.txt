Subarachnoid cisterns as surgical corridors: Integrating microsurgical anatomy and neuroimaging for intracranial navigation
Authors: Noé Pérez-Carrillo, Matias Baldoncini, Alvaro Contreras Salazar, Alvaro Campero, Valeria Alicia Forlizzi, Julio Cesar Pérez Cruz, Francisco de Jesús García-Mendoza, Alberto Manuel Angeles-Castellanos, Alejandro Méndez-Viveros, Feres Chaddad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: The subarachnoid space comprises a complex system of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)-filled compartments known as cisterns, which contain critical neurovascular structures and serve as essential anatomical corridors in neurosurgery. Despite their importance, current literature often lacks integrated descriptions correlating microsurgical anatomy with modern neuroimaging, limiting their applicability in surgical planning and intraoperative navigation.Methods: A comprehensive narrative review of the supratentorial, tentorial, and infratentorial subarachnoid cisterns was performed, integrating detailed microsurgical anatomical descriptions with radiological correlation using computed tomography cisternography and high-resolution magnetic resonance imaging sequences, including T2 and fast-imaging employing steady-state acquisition. Emphasis was placed on clinically relevant cisterns and their relationships with adjacent neurovascular structures.Results: Subarachnoid cisterns were systematically categorized according to their anatomical location and surgical relevance. Each cistern demonstrates distinct boundaries, contents, and patterns of communication, forming a continuous network that facilitates CSF dynamics and provides natural pathways for surgical access. Correlation with neuroimaging allows precise identification of these compartments and enhances understanding of their role in pathological conditions such as subarachnoid hemorrhage, tumors, and hydrocephalus. Key cisterns, including the Sylvian, carotid, interpeduncular, ambient, quadrigeminal, and cerebellopontine cisterns, were highlighted for their relevance in common neurosurgical approaches.Conclusion: A thorough understanding of subarachnoid cistern anatomy, combined with accurate interpretation of neuroimaging, is essential for safe and effective neurosurgical practice. The integration of anatomical and radiological perspectives enhances surgical planning, facilitates identification of operative corridors, and may improve outcomes in intracranial procedures.

INTRODUCTION

The major intracranial vessels and nerves travel through compartments filled with liquid limited by trabeculae and membranes in the subarachnoid space (SAS), between the arachnoid membrane and the pial surface, called subarachnoid cisterns. Knowledge of the limits and contents of these compartments has made micro neurosurgery possible and is of utmost importance to neurosurgeons for planning and execution of intracranial approaches.[52] Furthermore, knowledge of the distribution of SAS is helpful to understand key aspects of the physiopathology of traumatic brain injury (TBI), subarachnoid hemorrhage, and neuroinfectious diseases, among others.[31]

Detailed anatomical observations of the SAS and kinetics of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) have been made since 1875, after Key and Retzius published their work regarding mechanisms of CSF absorption, which many years later led to the development of imaging modalities to study the relationship between CSF and its reservoirs.[29]

The first work about radiological evaluation of the ventricular system and subarachnoid cisterns was published by Walter Edward Dandy in 1918 and 1919, in which he described normal findings and alterations due to gross space occupying pathologies; however, this technique was considered dangerous, even by Dandy itself, and many neurosurgeons of the time, including his former professor Harvey Cushing, positioned against this diagnostic method.[21,23] Nowadays, radiologic visualization of subarachnoid cisterns has improved with the emergence of computed tomography (CT) cisternography, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) modalities, and nuclear medicine techniques, allowing neurosurgeons to better diagnose and plan the surgical approach of a wide range of pathologies (e.g., tumoral, traumatic, and vascular).[12,16,26,30,32]

The first detailed description of subarachnoid cisterns was published by Yasargil in 1976 after performing over 1500 surgeries with the use of a microscope. He emphasized the relevant and practical aspects of micro neurosurgery and detailed description of their limits and contents.[52]

In the present work, we contrast the visualization of subarachnoid cisterns between anatomic models and imaging modalities including CT cisternography and T2 and fast imaging employing steady-state acquisition (FIESTA) MRI sequences and review the functions and clinical significance of subarachnoid cisterns.

Many subarachnoid membranes and cisterns have been described; however, some of them lack surgical relevance and are inconsistent among dissections. We will focus on those that are more relevant to surgical practice.

To our knowledge, no previous publications have been made regarding direct comparisons of anatomical dissection and imaging findings. We believe that it will be of great utility for young and former neurosurgeons to further improve their capacity for abstraction when evaluating imaging studies in diagnosing and searching for surgical corridors along the subarachnoid cisterns.

ANATOMY OF SUBARACHNOID CISTERNS

SAS is filled with fenestrated mesenchymal columns of collagen-reinforced tissue, remnants of the common precursor that form leptomeninges, which extend from the arachnoid to the pia mater called trabeculae. These columns provide support for neural and vascular structures and tend to be thicker around the transitions between, and within, subarachnoid cisterns, delimiting them like true inner membranes [Table 1].[31] The subarachnoid trabeculae can bridge between the arterial, venous, and brain elements in various conformations.[9] Must be remembered that named subarachnoid membranes may be destroyed during dissection and disposition may be variable among subjects.

SUPRATENTORIAL CISTERNS

Sylvian cistern (Cistern of cerebral vallecula)

The Sylvian fissure is formed by the anatomical relationship between the frontoparietal operculum, which extends from the anterior perforated substance to the supramarginal gyrus, and can be divided into an anterior (stem or sphenoidal) and posterior (insulo-opercular) compartments, the latter further divided into lateral and medial parts [Figure 1].[3,10] It is continuous medially and caudally with the crural and the carotid cistern.[49]

Right pterional approach. The superficial Sylvian vein receiving frontal and temporal cortical veins can be seen covering the Sylvian fissure after removal of the outer subarachnoid membrane.

Named arachnoid membranes and imaginary lines divide the Sylvian cistern in three compartments, which are from superficial to deep: superficial opercular compartment, delimited laterally by the outer arachnoid membrane and medially by the lateral Sylvian membrane, it contains the superficial Sylvian veins and the most proximal part of the cortical segment (M4) and most distal part of the opercular (M3) segment of the middle cerebral artery (MCA); deep opercular compartment, delimited laterally by the lateral Sylvian membrane and medially by the operculo-cisternal plane, an imaginary line passing through the deepest point of opposition between the frontoparietal and temporal opercula, it contains the medial Sylvian membrane, the opercular segment (M3) of the MCA that lies on the intermediate Sylvian membrane, and veins that connect the deep and superficial Sylvian venous systems; cisternal compartment, medial to the operculo-cisternal plane, it contains the insular (M2) and the distal part of the sphenoidal (M1) segment of the MCA [Figures 2 and 3].[22,43] Other structures that can be found along the Sylvian cistern are a segment of the internal carotid artery from the anterior choroidal artery to the terminal division, the origin of the basal veins of Rosenthal, and the lenticulostriate arteries from M1.[22,49]

Carotid cistern

The carotid cisterns are located between the lateral edge of the optic chiasm medially and the anterior part of the uncus. The limits of the carotid cistern are lateral wall, formed by the medial surface of the temporal lobe and the outer arachnoid membrane; the medial wall, formed by the medial carotid membrane; superior wall, formed by the anterior perforated substance; and inferior wall, formed by the outer arachnoid membrane facing the dura over the posterior clinoid process and the cavernous sinus.[22,43]

The proximal Sylvian membrane, which travels between the lateral orbital gyrus and the uncus, marks the limit between the Sylvian and carotid cisterns; the medial carotid membrane, which attaches to the superior aspect of the oculomotor nerve and corresponds to the opticocarotid membrane, separates the carotid and chiasmatic cisterns; and the lateral lamina terminalis membrane, attached to the lateral part of the rectus gyrus and the optic nerve, separates the chiasmatic and carotid cisterns.[18]

Frequently, no membrane separates the carotid, interpeduncular, and crural cisterns, thus creating a confluent area through which CSF can communicate easily through the posterior part of the carotid cistern.[22]

The carotid cistern contains the internal carotid artery in its clinoid, ophthalmic and communicating segments, the origins of the anterior choroid artery, the posterior communicating artery, the anterior cerebral artery (ACA), and the MCA, and perforating branches directed to the chiasmatic cistern to enter the anterior perforated substance, optic tract, and uncus [Figures 4 and 5].[22,52]

Chiasmatic cistern, cistern of lamina terminalis, and olfactory cistern

The chiasmatic cistern is an unpaired cistern that contains the optic nerves and chiasm, pituitary stalk, superior hypophyseal, ophthalmic, and infundibular arteries.[52,49] The limits are the limbus sphenoidale anteriorly, the lamina terminals cistern superiorly, the carotid cistern laterally (separated by the medial carotid membrane), and the interpeduncular cistern posteriorly located behind the pituitary stalk (separated by the diencephalic leaf of the Liliequist’s membrane).[18,49,50] The chiasmatic cistern commonly has extensions through the diaphragm sellae and optic foramen [Figures 6 and 7].[49]

The cistern of lamina terminalis is an unpaired cistern located above the optic chiasm just in front of the lamina terminalis.[22] The lateral lamina terminalis membrane, attached to the lateral part of the rectus gyrus and the optic chiasm, marks the limited between the lamina terminalis and the carotid cisterns; while the medial lamina terminalis membrane, attached to the medial parts of the rectus gyrus and the optic chiasm, marks the limit between the lamina terminalis and the pericallosal cisterns.[18]

The cistern of lamina terminalis contains the anterior communicating artery (ACoA) complex, the venous system of the lamina terminalis, and the arterial branches that arise near the ACoA: the recurrent artery of Heubner, orbitofrontal artery, and frontopolar artery [Figure 6].[6,52]

The olfactory cisterns are situated in the olfactory sulcus which is delimited laterally by the posterior orbital gyri and medially by the rectus gyri. The olfactory membrane forms the posteroinferior limit of the olfactory cistern as it blends into the lateral edge of the lateral lamina terminalis membrane.[15,17] The olfactory cisterns contain the olfactory bulb, tracts and nerves, olfactory veins, orbital veins, orbitofrontal artery, and olfactory artery [Figure 8].[49]

Pericallosal cistern

The pericallosal cistern lies deep in the interhemispheric fissure between the outer surface of the corpus callosum and the gyri cinguli and extends from the rostrum to the splenium. This cistern continues rostrally with the arachnoid over the interhemispheric fissure and the lamina terminalis cistern and caudally with the arachnoid that embraces the exit of the venae magna to the straight sinus.[49]

The pericallosal cistern can be divided into three compartments: inferior, positioned above the lamina terminalis cistern, which contains the A2 segment of the ACA after ascending through the medial lamina terminalis membrane; anterior, which lays just in front of the genu of the callosum and contains the A3 segment; and superior, lying above the body of the callosum, it contains the A4 and A5 segments.[22] The pericallosal cistern also contains the anterior cerebral veins following the path of the arteries [Figure 9].

Oculomotor cistern

A sleeve of arachnoid accompanies the cranial nerves as they exit the brain; this sleeve is especially prominent around the olfactory, optic, oculomotor, trochlear, trigeminal, and facial–vestibulocochlear nerves.[42,43] Among the mentioned, the most mentioned and better described is the oculomotor cistern. The sleeve of arachnoid that accompanies the oculomotor nerve is formed by the diencephalic, mesencephalic, medial carotid, lateral pontomesencephalic, and anterior pontine membranes, and the outer arachnoid membrane covering the posterior clinoid process commonly attaches to form a sleeve around the nerve.[22]

The oculomotor cistern has a pyramidal shape and begins with the oculomotor porus, an opening in the oculomotor triangle that forms the posterior part of the roof of the cavernous sinus and communicates with the basal cisterns.[34,49] This cistern contains the trigonal segment of the oculomotor nerve [Figure 10].[34]

Cistern of transverse fissure

The cistern of transverse fissure, previously known as the cistern of velum interpositum, is on the roof of the third ventricle, between the superior and inferior choroid tela, extends from the splenium of the corpus callosum to the foramen of Monro, and has no connection with the ventricular system.[47,54] Medial posterior choroidal arteries, branches of the pericallosal artery, and internal cerebral vein are endorsed by dense trabeculae [Figure 11].[47]

CISTERNS AT THE LEVEL OF TENTORIUM

Interpeduncular cistern and Liliequist’s membrane

The incisural space is divided into thirds, and each third contains a subarachnoid cistern: anterior third interpeduncular cistern; middle third ambient cistern; and posterior third quadrigeminal cistern.[46]

The interpeduncular cistern is situated between the cerebral peduncles and the diencephalic and mesencephalic membranes at the confluence of the supra and infratentorial parts of the SAS.[22] The Liliequist membrane and the mammillary bodies are the anterosuperior limit, while the top basilar membrane that separates the prepontine from the interpeduncular cistern forms the inferior limit.[49]

Liliequist membrane consists of two main leaves, mesencephalic and diencephalic. The diencephalic leave can be attached anterior, posterior, or at the level of the mammillary bodies; meanwhile, the mesencephalic leave is attached at the zone surrounding the basilar artery and can be further divided by the oculomotor nerve into a medial and a lateral part. A third and a fourth leaves have been described, but they are rare and their existence as independent arachnoid membranes is controversial.[50]

The interpeduncular cistern communicates laterally with the Sylvian cistern below the anterior perforated substance, the oculomotor, crural, and ambient cistern and anteriorly with the chiasmatic cistern below the optic chiasm.[34,49] This cistern is considered the point of transition between supra and infratentorial spaces; thus, it provides access to the midline and infratentorial levels.[18,46]

The contents of the interpeduncular cistern are the bifurcation of the basilar artery, peduncular segments of the posterior cerebral arteries, including the thalamo-perforating arteries, peduncular segments of the superior cerebellar arteries, perforating branches of the posterior cerebral arteries, one meningeal branch, and the artery to the third cranial nerve, posterior communicating arteries that connect with the midpoint of the peduncular segments of the posterior cerebral arteries, the basal vein of Rosenthal, the interpeduncular and posterior communicating vein, and the third cranial nerve in its interpeduncular segment [Figure 12].[5,52]

Crural and ambient cistern

The crural and ambient cisterns are named together as the lateral mesencephalic cistern.[46]

The crural cistern (term not yet accepted in the official anatomical terminology) is situated between the cerebral peduncle and the posterior part of the uncus. The borders are the carotid cistern anteriorly, the interpeduncular cistern anteromedially, the ambient cistern posteriorly, and the cerebellopontine cistern inferiorly. The crural, carotid, and interpeduncular cisterns communicate through the confluent area.[22] The limit between the crural and ambient cisterns is the lateral pontomesencephalic membrane, also referred to as the crural membrane, which attaches to the lateral surface of the oculomotor nerve.[18,46]

The crural cistern is divided into superior and inferior compartments by the intracrural membrane that travels anteromedially from the uncus to the cerebral peduncle.[18,46,50] The anterior choroid artery courses above the intracrural membrane, while the posterior communicating artery courses below it.[18]

The crural cistern contains the P2a segment of the posterior cerebral artery (PCA), peduncular vein, peduncular perforating branches, a segment of the anterior choroidal, medial posterior choroidal, short and long circumflex, thalamogeniculate, and inferior temporal arteries.[5,52,22]

The ambient cistern extends above and below the tentorium.[47] This cistern is situated between the midbrain and the temporal lobe and extends from the posterior edge of the uncus anteriorly to the lateral edge of the collicular plate posteriorly.[22] The ambient cistern is divided into superior and posterior compartments by the superior cerebellar membrane which goes from the midbrain to the oculomotor cistern. The neighboring spaces of the ambient cistern are the oculomotor, crural, and interpeduncular cisterns anteriorly and the quadrigeminal cistern limited by the cerebellar precentral membrane posteriorly.[47]

The ambient cistern contains the P2p and lateral posterior choroidal arteries and a segment of the medial posterior choroidal, short and long circumflex, thalamogeniculate, inferior temporal, superior cerebellar, and parieto-occipital arteries, the basal vein of Rosenthal, and the trochlear nerve [Figure 13].[22,52]

Quadrigeminal cistern

The quadrigeminal cistern is located below the splenium of the callosum around the pineal region. Its limits are the dorsal mesencephalon anteriorly, the arachnoid from the dorsum callosum posteriorly, the pulvinar laterally, and the pericallosal cistern superiorly.[47]

The quadrigeminal cistern contains the P4 segment of the PCA and the distal superior cerebellar artery (SCA), the medial posterior choroidal artery, the trochlear nerve, the vein of Galen, internal cerebral veins, basal veins of Rosenthal, internal occipital vein, occipitotemporal veins, precentral cerebellar vein, tectal veins, pineal veins, superior cerebellar vein, superior vermian veins, and posterior pericallosal veins [Figure 13].[5,19]

INFRATENTORIAL CISTERNS

Posterior cerebellomedullary (Cisterna magna)

The posterior cerebellomedullary is located at the midline dorsal to the medulla and cerebellar vermis and extends from the upper edge of the atlas to the pyramid of the cerebellar vermis. The lateral limits are the lateral cerebellomedullary cisterns. The posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) membranes divide this cistern into three compartments: one medial and two lateral.[48]

The contents of the posterior cerebellomedullary cistern are the cortical segment of the PICA, the inferior vermian vein, the medial posterior medullary vein, and the vein of the cerebellomedullary fissure [Figure 14].[19,48]

Recently, the posterior enlargement of the SAS extending from the foramen magnum to the level of C2 has been described as an independent compartment named as the “occipito-atlantal cistern.” This space along with the posterior cerebellomedullary cistern has been described to act as a buffer zone for CSF dynamics and regulate the flow of CSF from the cranial cavity to the spinal SAS. Influence of head movements through myodural bridges from the suboccipital muscles may act as a pump for CSF circulation in this region.[25]

Superior cerebellar cistern, cerebellopontine cistern, prepontine cistern (basilar cistern), premedullary cistern, and lateral cerebellomedullary cistern

These cisterns wrap the brainstem in a septate way with their limits given by fenestrated subarachnoid membranes: antero-inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) membranes, which follow the course of the AICA and separates the cerebellopontine, prepontine, and lateral cerebellomedullary cisterns; basilar membranes, traveling parallel to the basilar artery and separates the prepontine cistern form the laterally placed cerebellopontine cisterns; anterior pontine membranes dividing the prepontine cistern from the cerebellopontine cisterns, and the top basilar membrane dividing the prepontine and interpeduncular cisterns.[19,48]

The superior cerebellar cistern covers the superior vermis and is bounded laterally by the cerebellar hemispheres. It contains the terminal branches of the superior cerebellar arteries and the superior cerebellar and vermian veins.[1]

The prepontine cistern contains the basilar artery and the homonymous veins. The premedullary cistern contains the anterior medullary segment of the vertebral artery (VA), the origin of the anterior spinal arteries, and the XII nerve.[19]

The cerebellopontine angle includes the cerebellopontine and lateral cerebellomedullary cistern and is delimited by the petrous bone and its dura anteriorly, the pons and cerebellar peduncle superiorly, the cerebellum posteriorly, and the olive inferiorly.[41]

The lateral cerebellomedullary cistern contains the anterior medullar, lateral medullar, tonsilomedullar and telovelotonsillar segments of PICA, lateral medullary segment of the VA, IX, X, and XI nerves, and the Bochdalek’s corpuscles, a protruding part of the choroid plexus of the fourth ventricle.[19]

The cerebellopontine cistern is the main cistern of the cerebellopontine angle. It contains the V, VI, VII, and VIII nerves, the rostral and caudal trunks of bifurcation of the AICA, and the cerebellomesencephalic and cortical segments of the SCA are located within the cerebellopontine cistern [Figure 15].[19,48]

FUNCTIONS AND CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE OF SUBARACHNOID CISTERNS

Knowledge about the covering of the brain has been known since the era of Galen and Vesalius, but the first one to use the term “arachnoid” was Blasiud in 1666. After that, many anatomists such as Raymundi Vieussens, Frederik Ruysch, Antonio Pacchioni, Domenico Cotugno, Marie François Xavier Bichat, Magendie, and Luschka contributed to the understanding of meningeal anatomy and CSF dynamics, but detailed descriptions of the SAS were not available until the publication of Axel Key and Gustaf Retizius in 1875 where they first used the term “cistern.”[21]

Pia-arachnoid complex (PAC), which encloses the pia mater, arachnoid, and the SAS, must be seen as a dynamic environment that responds to insults providing mechanical and metabolic support to neural structures. The pia mater adheres firmly to the cortex and deepens the sulci giving the SAS an irregular geometry that produces a complex solid-fluid relationship between the brain and the CSF.[31] The density of CSF (1.007 g/cm3) is similar to brain’s (1.040 g/cm3), making the buoyant forces of the fluid reduce the brain’s weight from 1400 g to 46 g approximately; if this floating effect would not exist, the brain parenchyma would compress the neurovascular structures contained in the SAS.[36]

Classical hypothesis dictates that the CSF is actively secreted by the choroid plexuses to flow in a unidirectional way from the ventricles to end up in the SAS after passing through the foramens of Magendie and Luschka and then reabsorbed by the arachnoid granulations. Definition of hydrocephalus and treatment strategies is ingrained in this classical hypothesis; however, novel evidence has suggested different mechanisms and is toward to redefine the understanding of CFS dynamics.[44] Experiments in animal models and human cadavers have been developed to better understand the mechanical properties of the PAC.[7,14,40]

Doubts about the unidirectional flow of CSF emerged after measurements of pressions in the intracranial and intraspinal spaces, demonstrating no gradient between them at least in the upright position.[44] Furthermore, there are reported cases in the literature about obstruction of the outlet of the third ventricle without enlargement of the ventricular system, as is the case of a 70-year-old woman that years after the trigger event, did not develop ventriculomegaly even though MRI with time-spatial labeling inversion pulse technique kept demonstrating lack of pulsation of CSF in the cerebral aqueduct.[52]

The increasing volume of CSF with aging and the subsequent enlargement of ventricles can end up connecting them with the SAS by alternative CSF pathways formed by the over-slimming of thin structures like the choroidal fissure in the inferior choroidal point or the roof of the third ventricle involving the cistern of transverse fissure and quadrigeminal cistern.[53] This observation may lead to novel treatments for hydrocephalus involving the perimesencephalic cisterns.

Subarachnoid trabeculae have a variety of forms and their architecture varies among their interfaces with nerves and vascular structures found in the SAS. They even possess capillaries and nerve endings that have been described to have a role in monitoring of the CSF pression and cerebral vasospasm.[1,31,39] CSF dynamics in the SAS are influenced by the SAT, a feature that is best appreciated in pathological situations such as hydrocephalus and subarachnoid hemorrhage.[31]

SAT also acts as mechanical pillars in the SAS damping and constraining the movement of brain relative to the skull when an external force is applied, and, because of their close proximity and interactions with blood vessel and nerve structures, they may have a role in mechanosensation and other signaling paths after TBI, and subsequent changes in their architecture due to trauma could influence future responses to new insults.[24]

SURGICAL APPROACHES TO SUBARACHNOID CISTERNS

Subarachnoid cisterns act as natural corridors, and deep knowledge of neuroanatomy is essential to safely navigate throughout them. Anatomic variants must be considered when planning the surgical approach for the wide range of pathologies that can affect the SAS.

SAM may not be a good reference while navigating through the SAS, and it can even preclude an obstacle during surgery, but some of them are specific surgical targets such as the Liliequist membrane during a third ventriculostomy. To successfully achieve drainage of the CSF when performing an endoscopic ventriculostomy, the Liliequist membrane must be open to ensure adequate drainage into the SAS.[51]

Radiological evaluation of the subarachnoid cisterns may be limited in normal subjects or when the disease itself does not enlarge the SAS sufficiently to obtain an enhanced visualization of cisterns.[22,35] Even endoscopic evaluation could be precluded by the lack of expertise or adequate equipment.[15] We believe that the correlation between anatomy and imaging is a great tool to overcome those pitfalls.

Many surgical approaches have been described to access the different cisterns that surround the brain; the interested reader may consider reading specialized literature on the topic. Here, we will provide a general look at the main strategies to access the previously described cisterns according to their tentorial disposition.

Supratentorial level

The pterional approach (PA) is a workhorse of neurosurgery to access the anterior and middle cranial fossa and the most anterior and superior portions of the posterior cranial fossa.[27] The PA is related to the subfrontal, transsylvian, pretemporal, and anterior subtemporal corridors that lead to the supratentorial and the uppermost and median infratentorial cisterns, insula, basal ganglia, sellar and parasellar areas, hypothalamic area, the third ventricle, and even medial structures such as the mesial temporal lobe.[38]

A variation of the PA is the frontotemporal-orbitozygomatic which adds removal of the orbitozygomatic bar, orbital rim, and zygomatic arch in its one, two, and three pieces variants to broaden access to the skull base and lessen the need for brain retraction.[28] The ongoing trend toward smaller craniotomies has led to modifications of the original approach described by Yasargil, giving birth to the micro/mini pterional approach.[33]

The cisterns surrounding the ACoA complex can also be reached through a bifrontal approach, where a corridor from the anterior cranial fossa to the lamina terminalis is created.[20] The pericallosal cistern can be reached through an interhemispheric approach, which is also helpful to treat pathologies affecting the lateral and third ventricles adding the transchoroidal, transforniceal, and interforniceal approaches and their variations sectioning the corpus callosum or the cingulum.[4]

Tentorial and infratentorial level

The perimesencephalic cisterns can be reached laterally through a pterional and frontotemporal-orbitozygomatic approach using a subtemporal and anterior temporal approach.[38] They can also be reached by infratentorial supracerebellar, occipital interhemispheric, and transcortical transchoroidal approaches.[15] Care should be taken when aiming at a transcortical approach to avoid transection of key white matter tracts that could end up in visual, memory, language disturbances, etc.[13]

The quadrigeminal cistern is one of the most difficult areas to access because of the complexity of its vascular relationships, having the basal veins, internal cerebral veins, vein of Galen, and some of their tributaries together in an enclosed space by the tentorium, splenium of the corpus callosum, the occipital lobe, and mesencephalic structures. Pathologies involving this zone have been treated successfully using the supracerebellar infratentorial, occipital transhemispheric, and parietal interhemispheric, sometimes aided by endoscopy such as in infratentorial cisterns.[2,8,11,37]

Access to infratentorial cisterns can be achieved through a wide variety of approaches including the retrosigmoid, presigmoid, far lateral (with its transcondylar, supracondylar, or paracondylar extensions), and midline suboccipital approaches. The approach depends on the involved cisterns and technical and functional aspects. For example, it would be a good option to perform a translabyrinthine approach to diminish the risk of damaging cranial nerves in the cerebellopontine angle and the need for cerebral retraction in a patient with no serviceable hearing; on the contrary, a retrolabyrinthine approach would be the choice if serviceable hearing is present to preserve the neurotologic structures.[17,45]

CONCLUSION

Deep knowledge of the SAS and its contents is mandatory and key to success when performing intracranial surgery. Imaging studies such as CT cisternography and T2 MRI sequence are widely available and are a great aid when planning the surgical approach; however, they may be subject to pitfalls such as anatomic variations and lack of significant enlargement of SAS that allows visualization of the structures. Other imaging techniques such as time-spatial labeling inversion pulse, FIESTA, contrasted phases, endoscopy, and nuclear medicine studies may add more certainty of anatomy, but they are not available in all centers.

New insights into CFS dynamics and SAT function may help to better understand the physiopathology of neurosurgical diseases, especially hydrocephalus and TBI.
