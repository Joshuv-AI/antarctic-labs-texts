Safety and Tolerability of Different Total Neoadjuvant Therapies in Patients With Locally Advanced Rectal Cancer
Authors: Shohei Udagawa, Hiroki Osumi, Senzo Taguchi, Tatsuki Noguchi, Takashi Sakamoto, Shimpei Matsui, Toshiki Mukai, Tomohiro Yamaguchi, Shota Fukuoka, Koichiro Yoshino, Keitaro Shimozaki, Takeru Wakatsuki, Mariko Ogura, Akira Ooki, Keisho Chin, Kensei Yamaguchi, Takashi Akiyoshi, Eiji Shinozaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTotal neoadjuvant therapy (TNT) is the standard treatment for locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC). Few studies have focused on the toxicity of TNT. Moreover, the differences in safety and tolerability between radiotherapy‐first and chemotherapy‐first strategies remain unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the differences in treatment‐related toxicities between radiotherapy‐first and chemotherapy‐first strategies for TNT in patients with LARC.MethodsPatients with LARC who were treated with TNT at a single cancer center between June 2020 and July 2023 were retrospectively included. TNT consisted of induction chemotherapy followed by long‐course chemoradiotherapy (INCT‐LC‐CRT) or long‐course chemoradiotherapy/short‐course radiotherapy followed by consolidation chemotherapy (LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT). Adverse events (AEs) and treatment exposure were compared between the groups.ResultsOverall, 93 patients were included. Patients in the INCT‐LC‐CRT and LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT groups were 28 (30.1%) and 45 (48.4%) / 20 (21.5%), respectively. Chemotherapy‐related grade 3 or 4 AEs were significantly more frequent in the LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT group than in the INCT‐LC‐CRT group (50.8% vs. 17.9%, p < 0.01) but were not significantly different between the LC‐CRT‐CNCT and SC‐RT‐CNCT groups (53.3% vs. 40.0%, p = 0.42). The proportion of patients who received a full dose of chemotherapy was higher in the INCT‐LC‐CRT group than in the LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT group (53.6% vs. 24.6%; p < 0.01). One (3.6%) and six (9.2%) patients discontinued chemotherapy owing to AEs in the INCT‐LC‐CRT and LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT groups, respectively.ConclusionsGrade 3 or 4 AEs during chemotherapy were more frequent and required dose modification in the LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT group than in the INCT‐LC‐CRT group.Keywords: consolidation chemotherapy, induction chemotherapy, locally advanced rectal cancer, total neoadjuvant therapy

Introduction

Approximately 730,000 new cases of rectal cancer were diagnosed, and 340,000 deaths were reported worldwide in 2020 [1]. One of the standard treatments for locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) is total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT) combined with preoperative radiotherapy, chemoradiotherapy (CRT), and chemotherapy [2, 3]. Neoadjuvant CRT improves local control; however, it does not reduce the incidence of distant metastases or improve overall survival (OS), considering that improving pathologic complete response rates remains a key objective [4]. Thus, TNT was developed to further improve local control and OS. While TNT has been adopted for LARC in Europe and the United States, the standard strategy in Japan is surgery followed by adjuvant chemotherapy with lateral lymph node dissection, as recommended in the Japanese guideline [5]. TNT provides an opportunity to avoid surgery and adopt a nonoperative management (NOM) approach. Recently, several large‐scale randomized controlled trials and meta‐analyses have reported that TNT provides additional benefits to neoadjuvant radiotherapy, including disease‐free survival, high pathological response rates, and potential improvement in OS in patients with LARC [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. However, the optimal treatment sequence for TNT, including the sequence of radiotherapy and chemotherapy, has not been clearly defined. Induction chemotherapy (INCT) is defined as chemotherapy before chemoradiotherapy, while consolidation chemotherapy (CNCT) is defined as chemotherapy after radiotherapy or CRT, both of which are one of the TNT strategies. CNCT after CRT results in a higher rate of NOM than does INCT before CRT [7].

A sequential treatment strategy comprising radiotherapy and chemotherapy is expected to increase treatment‐related toxicities. Although treatment efficacy is a critical consideration, safety remains an equally important factor when evaluating therapeutic strategies. Acute toxicities associated with pelvic radiotherapy such as diarrhea and hematologic suppression have been well documented in prior CRT studies, and these adverse events overlap with the toxicity profiles of systemic chemotherapy [14]. Consistent with these overlapping toxicities, previous TNT studies have reported that approximately 30%–40% of patients experience grade 3 or higher adverse events (AEs), predominantly hematologic and gastrointestinal toxicities [6, 15]. However, comparing the treatment‐related toxicities of chemotherapy‐first and radiotherapy‐first strategies in previously reported data is challenging because of variations in chemotherapy regimens, duration of administration, and radiation doses [16, 17]. Evaluating the differences in treatment‐related toxicity between chemotherapy‐first and radiotherapy‐first strategies will contribute to the optimization of TNTs for LARC. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the differences in treatment‐related toxicity between chemotherapy‐first and radiotherapy‐first strategies for TNT in patients with LARC.

Methods

Study Design

We conducted a retrospective observational study at the Cancer Institute Hospital of the Japanese Foundation for Cancer Research (JFCR) in Japan. The inclusion criteria were (1) diagnosis of LARC, (2) histologically confirmed rectal adenocarcinoma, and (3) treatment with TNT between June 2020 and July 2023 at JFCR. The exclusion criteria are (1) the presence of distant metastases before the start of TNT, and (2) patients receiving chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or CRT at another hospital. Clinical stage was diagnosed according to the Union for International Cancer Control TNM classification (8th edition). All data were obtained through medical record review. The data cut‐off date for follow‐up was April 2025. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and the requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design.

Treatment Procedure

Two treatment groups were established: CNCT and INCT long‐course CRT (INCT‐LC‐CRT). The CNCT group was further divided into two groups: short‐course radiotherapy (25 Gy in 5 Fr) followed by consolidation chemotherapy (SC‐RT‐CNCT) and LC‐CRT (50 Gy in 25 Fr, 50.4 Gy in 28 Fr, or 54 Gy in 27 Fr with capecitabine) followed by CNCT (LC‐CRT‐CNCT). The INCT‐LC‐CRT group received CRT of 50 Gy in 25 Fr with capecitabine during radiotherapy after INCT. INCT and CNCT consisted of six cycles of CAPOX (capecitabine 1000 mg/m2 orally twice a day on Days 1–14, oxaliplatin 130 mg/m2 intravenously on Day 1, and a chemotherapy‐free interval between Days 15 and 21). The addition of bevacizumab (7.5 mg/kg intravenously on Day 1) is allowed in INCT. Concurrent chemoradiotherapy consisted of capecitabine (825 mg/m2) administered orally twice daily. Volumetric modulated arc therapy was used for radiotherapy. Gross tumor volumes (GTVs), including primary tumor (GTV primary) and metastatic lymph nodes (GTV node), were determined using computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging. Clinical target volumes (CTVs) were defined as: a 15 mm cranial margin, 5–15 mm caudal margin, and 5 mm anterior–posterior and lateral margins around GTV primary (CTV primary); 5 mm margins around GTV node (CTV node), and inclusion of mesorectal, presacral, and internal iliac lymph nodes for prophylactic coverage (CTV subclinical). The superior border of CTVs was set at the sacral promontory, with extension to the L4–L5 intervertebral space in selected patients with a high risk of lymph node metastasis, as determined by the physician. Planning target volumes (PTVs) were defined as 5–6 mm margins around CTV primary (PTV primary), 5 mm margins around CTV node (PTV node), and 4–5 mm margins around CTV subclinical (PTV subclinical). Supportive care, including prophylactic antiemetics, was provided clinically. Tumor restaging after TNT was assessed using computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy. Surgery was recommended after TNT; however, NOM was an acceptable alternative when the tumor was assessed as having a clinical complete response (cCR), defined as a flat scar with no nodularity and normal wall distensibility, or a near‐complete response (near CR), defined as minimal irregularities with no nodularity and decreased wall distensibility, and if the patient desired NOM. Patients with signs of tumor regrowth on follow‐up endoscopy or imaging tests are recommended for surgery. Histological assessment of the primary tumor response was performed in accordance with the Japanese Classification of Colorectal, Appendiceal, and Anal Carcinoma [18]. Relative dose intensity (RDI) of oxaliplatin was calculated for each patient as the ratio of the actual cumulative dose delivered to the planned cumulative dose, expressed as a percentage. Planned doses were defined according to standard dosing schedules for each regimen. Delays between cycles were not incorporated into the calculation, consistent with cumulative dose–based RDI definitions commonly used in retrospective studies. Capecitabine RDI could not be calculated because remaining‐pill information was unavailable.

Statistical Analyses

Each AE was evaluated according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (ver. 5.0) and was defined as the time from the start of each treatment to the start of subsequent treatment (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, CRT, or surgery). The most severe grades of hematological, non‐hematological, and total AEs were identified. Disease‐free survival (DFS) was defined as the interval between the start date of the treatment and the first occurrence of unresectable locoregional failure, distant metastasis, or death from any cause. OS was defined as the interval from the start of treatment to death from any cause. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher's exact test. DFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and the results were compared using the log‐rank test. A Cox proportional hazards model was used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Logistic regression analysis was conducted to analyze the factors related to the objective variables. All p‐values were obtained using two‐tailed tests. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using EZR version 1.41 statistical software (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), which is a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [19]. Figures were created using R (version 4.5.1; The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and R Studio (Posit, PBC, Boston, MA, USA).

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 93 patients with LARC who were treated with TNT were assessed. Of these, 28 patients underwent INCT‐LC‐CRT and 65 underwent CNCT. Among the 65 patients in the CNCT group, 20 underwent SC‐RT and 45 underwent LC‐CRT before CNCT. The median age was 57 years (range 28–81 years), and 63 patients (67.7%) were male. Tumor depths were cT2 in 10 patients (10.8%), cT3 in 65 patients (69.9%), and cT4 in 18 patients (19.3%). Lymph node metastasis was cN0 in 23 (24.7%), cN1 in 41 (44.1%), and cN2 in 29 (31.2%) patients, respectively. The clinical stage was cStage I in eight patients (8.6%), cStage II in 15 (16.1%), and cStage III in 70 (75.3%). Patients treated with INCT‐LC‐CRT were at a more advanced stage, including the N2 stage, than those treated with LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT. The characteristics of the 93 patients are summarized in Table 1. In the INCT‐LC‐CRT group, median time between last administration of capecitabine and chemoradiotherapy was 3.7 weeks (IQR: 3.1–4.1) and median time between completion of chemoradiotherapy and endoscopic assessment was 6.1 weeks (IQR: 5.1–7.6), and median time between completion of chemoradiotherapy and surgery was 9.6 weeks (IQR: 7.3–12.0) in patients who underwent surgery. In the CNCT group, median time between completion of radiotherapy or CRT and start of chemotherapy was 4.3 weeks (IQR: 3.7–5.9), median time between last administration of capecitabine and endoscopic assessment was 3.0 weeks (IQR: 1.4–4.3), and median time between last administration of capecitabine and surgery was 6.4 weeks (IQR: 4.4–8.7) in patients who underwent surgery. In the INCT‐LC‐CRT group, cCR and near CR were observed in three patients (10.7%) and one patient (3.6%), respectively. In the CNCT group, cCR and near CR were observed in one patient (2.2%) and 13 patients (28.9%), respectively, in the LC‐CRT‐CNCT group. cCR and near CR were observed in one patient (5.0%) and 3 patients (15.0%), respectively, in the SC‐RT‐CNCT group (Table S1). The baseline characteristics were comparable between patients with clinical CR/near CR and those without CR (Table S2). In the INCT‐LC‐CRT group, two patients underwent NOM and 24 patients underwent primary tumor resection, including one patient who also underwent lung metastasectomy. In the LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT group, 14 patients underwent NOM and 45 patients underwent primary tumor resection, including two patients who also underwent lung or liver metastasectomy. No perioperative deaths occurred. Among the 69 patients who underwent primary tumor resection and were evaluated for histological response, histological grades 1a, 1b, 2, and 3 were observed in 10 (14.5%), 17 (24.6%), 33 (47.8%), and 9 (13.0%) patients, respectively (Table S1). Three‐year DFS rate for the LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT and INCT‐LC‐CRT group was 79.8% (95% CI: 67.8–87.8) and 67.9% (95% CI: 47.3–81.8), respectively (HR 1.55, 95% CI 0.66–3.64, p = 0.31) (Figure S1a). Three‐year OS rate for the LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT and INCT‐LC‐CRT group was 93.2% (95% CI: 82.7–97.4) and 95.8% (95% CI: 73.9–99.4), respectively (HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.06–4.59, p = 0.54) (Figure S1b).

Toxicity During Chemotherapy

AEs during chemotherapy are shown in Figure 1 and Table S3. In the INCT‐LC‐CRT group, grade 3–4 and 1–2 AEs during chemotherapy were 5 (17.9%) and 22 (78.6%), respectively. Grades 3–4 hematological and non‐hematological AEs were three and two, respectively. In the LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT group, grade 3–4 and 1–2 AEs during chemotherapy were 32 (50.8%) and 30 (46.1%), respectively. Grades 3–4 hematological and non‐hematological AEs were observed in 20 (30.8%) and 18 (27.7%) patients, respectively. Grades 3–4 AEs during chemotherapy were significantly more frequent in the LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT group than in the INCT‐LC‐CRT group (50.8% vs. 17.9%, p < 0.01). When the patients were categorized into hematological and non‐hematological AEs, grade 3–4 AEs during chemotherapy were more frequent in the LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT group than in the INCT‐LC‐CRT group (hematological AEs, 30.8% vs. 10.7%; p = 0.06; non‐hematological AEs, 27.7% vs. 7.1%; p = 0.02). Neutropenia was the most common grade 3–4 AEs during chemotherapy in both INCT‐LC‐CRT group (7.4%) and LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT group (27.7%), with a statistically significant difference between the groups (p = 0.03). The timing of the first grade ≥ 3 neutropenia during CNCT was also evaluated. Among the 18 affected patients in the LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT group, 12 (66.7%) developed neutropenia within the first three cycles, whereas six patients (33.3%) experienced their first event during later cycles (cycles 4–6). Gastrointestinal toxicity and hand‐foot syndrome occurred more often in the LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT group. In the CNCT group, there were 24 (53.3%) and 8 (40.0%) grade 3–4 AEs during chemotherapy in the LC‐CRT and SC‐RT groups, respectively, with no significant differences between the two groups (p = 0.42) (Table S4). One patient in the LC‐CRT‐CNCT group died suddenly from an unknown cause during chemotherapy. Logistic regression analysis showed that grade 3–4 AEs were associated with LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT (OR: 7.19, 95% CI: 2.08–24.80, p < 0.01) (Table S5). No patients in the INCT‐LC‐CRT group received G‐CSF prophylaxis, whereas five patients in the LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT group received G‐CSF support. Febrile neutropenia did not occur in either group. No red blood cell transfusions were required. AE‐related emergency hospitalization occurred in one patient in the LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT group and in none of the patients in the INCT‐LC‐CRT group.

Toxicity During Chemoradiotherapy

AEs during CRT and RT are shown in Figure 2 and Table S6. In the INCT‐LC‐CRT group, the incidences of grade 3–4 and grade were one (3.6%) and 21 (75.0%), respectively. Grade 3–4 AEs during CRT were observed in five (11.1%) and 33 (73.3%) patients in the LC‐CRT‐CNCT group, respectively. Grade 1–2 and 3 AEs during radiotherapy were seven (35.0%) and three (15.0%) patients in the SC‐RT‐CNCT group. The most common AEs during CRT were rectal pain in the INCT‐LC‐CRT and LC‐CRT‐CNCT groups, and diarrhea in the SC‐RT‐CNCT group. No significant differences were observed in the AE types or frequencies between the INCT‐LC‐CRT and LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT groups. No AEs leading to the discontinuation of radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy were observed in either group. The median interval between radiotherapy and the start of CNCT was 5.0 weeks (IQR, 4.0–6.0) after LC‐CRT and 3.6 weeks (IQR, 3.4–3.9) after SC‐RT.

Treatment Exposure and Compliance in TNT

Treatment exposure and compliance with TNT are shown in Table 2 and Figure 3. All the patients received a full dose of radiation. Except for one patient who was infected with the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus‐2, all patients completed radiation therapy without delaying their scheduled plan for more than 2 days. The completion rate of six cycles of CAPOX was not significantly different between the INCT‐LC‐CRT and LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT groups (92.9% vs. 84.6%; p = 0.37). However, the proportion of patients who received the full dose of CAPOX was higher in the INCT‐LC‐CRT group than in the LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT group (53.6% vs. 24.6%; p < 0.01). The proportion of patients who did not receive the full dose of CAPOX did not differ significantly between the LC‐CRT‐CNCT and SC‐RT‐CNCT groups (77.8% vs. 70.0%, p = 0.54). The median RDI of oxaliplatin was 100% (IQR, 83.2–100) in the INCT‐LC‐CRT group, 84.6% (IQR, 80.8–100) in the LC‐CRT‐CNCT group, and 90.4% (IQR, 82.7–100) in the SC‐RT‐CNCT group. Chemotherapy discontinuation incidence due to AEs in the INCT‐LC‐CRT and LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT groups was one (3.6%) and six (9.2%), respectively.

Discussion

In the present study, grade 3–4 AEs during chemotherapy, particularly neutropenia, were significantly more common in the LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT group than in the INCT‐LC‐CRT group. In addition, during chemotherapy, the LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT group required significantly more dose modifications than the INCT‐LC‐CRT group. Furthermore, LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT was associated with grade 3–4 AEs during chemotherapy in logistic regression analysis.

Most recent studies have compared TNT with preoperative CRT or have been conducted as single‐arm trials [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 20, 21, 22]. Only a few studies have compared chemotherapy‐first and radiotherapy‐first strategies using the same chemotherapy regimens and radiotherapy schedules and have comprehensively examined AEs. The CAO/ARK/AIO‐12 trial, a randomized phase II study, compared INCT using three cycles of folinic acid, fluorouracil, and oxaliplatin (FOLFOX) before CRT with CNCT using three cycles of FOLFOX after CRT. Although grade 3–4 AEs during chemotherapy occurred in 33 (21%) and 25 (23%) patients in the INCT and CNCT groups, respectively, hematologic toxicities of all grades, except neutropenia, for which no data were reported, tended to be more frequent in the CNCT group. In addition, treatment compliance with FOLFOX was lower in the CNCT group than in the INCT group [6]. These results are consistent with those of the present study; however, as only three cycles of FOLFOX were administered, only grade 1–2 toxicities were observed. The OPRA trial, a randomized phase II study, also compared INCT before CRT with CNCT after CRT [7]. The chemotherapy regimens consisted of eight cycles of FOLFOX or five cycles of CAPOX. In this study, the proportion of patients with grade 3–5 AEs was similar between the INCT‐CRT and CRT‐CNCT groups. However, the study did not report data on grade 3 neutropenia and only provided data on grade 4 events, which may explain the discrepancy with the present study. In addition, grade ≥ 3 AEs were observed more frequently in older patients and women than in younger patients and males [15]. In the present study, the patients treated with INCT‐LC‐CRT were at a more advanced stage than those treated with LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT alone. Based on this report and the imbalance in patient characteristics, logistic regression analysis was performed using sex, age, T stage, N stage, and treatment strategy (INCT or CNCT) as explanatory variables, with grade 3–4 AEs as the dependent variable. One possible reason is that radiation‐induced bone marrow suppression can be prolonged, particularly when the pelvic bones, which contain a substantial proportion of the body's active bone marrow, are irradiated [23, 24]. Therefore, chemotherapy following radiation therapy for pelvic tumors should be administered with caution.

While intensified chemotherapy is a widely adopted strategy to improve clinical outcomes, careful consideration of treatment‐related toxicity is essential when attempting to escalate the therapeutic intensity. A recent pooled analysis of the CAO/ARO/AIO‐12 and OPRA trials demonstrated that oncologic outcomes did not differ according to TNT strategy, and compliance to TNT as well as rates of grade ≥ 3 AEs were similar between the two trials. These findings suggest that differences in chemotherapy timing or the total amount of chemotherapy administered may have only a limited impact on overall treatment tolerance [25]. In contrast, adherence to intensified chemotherapy regimens appears to be an important determinant of treatment effectiveness. A post hoc analysis of the CAO/ARO/AIO‐04 trial showed that reduced dose intensity was associated with inferior oncologic outcomes [26]. Consistent with this, the OPRA trial, reported lower completion rates among patients an incomplete clinical response, suggesting that the ability to tolerate treatment may influence long‐term outcomes [27]. Although various treatment strategies for TNT have been evaluated in previous studies, the optimal TNT treatment strategy remains unclear, and the development of more efficacious treatment strategies is necessary. Most current chemotherapies for INCT and CNCT involve FOLFOX or CAPOX regimens [6, 7, 9, 10, 11, 20, 21, 22]. Thus, FOLFOXIRI represents a potential strategy for intensified chemotherapy that may prolong OS [8]. However, higher percentages of grade 3 or 4 and febrile neutropenia have been reported in patients with metastatic colorectal cancer treated with FOLFOXIRI [28, 29, 30]. In the INCT setting, the TRUST study, a phase II study that adopted six cycles of FOLFOXIRI plus bevacizumab followed by CRT, reported grade 3–4 neutropenia in 20 (41.7%) patients and febrile neutropenia in two (4%) [31]. Although FOLFOXIRI as an INCT has shown improved survival outcomes and higher pCR rates than CAPOX, it has also been associated with an increased incidence of grade 3–4 neutropenia [32]. An increased risk of neutropenia has been reported in phase II studies of SC‐RT followed by consolidation chemotherapy with FOLFOXIRI [33]. These findings suggest that chemotherapy intensification strategies, such as FOLFOXIRI, should be used with caution in the consolidation setting and may be more appropriate when used as induction chemotherapy.

In the CNCT group, the incidence and types of AEs during chemotherapy were similar between the LC‐CRT and SC‐RT groups. To date, no prospective trial has evaluated the differences in AEs during CNCT between SC‐RT and LC‐CRT using the same regimen or number of cycles. Both the CAO/ARO/AIO‐12 and Polish II trials adopted three cycles of FOLFOX following LC‐CRT and SC‐RT, respectively [6, 11]. Grade 3–4 AEs occurred in 22% and 23% of the patients in the CAO/ARO/AIO‐12 and Polish II trials, respectively. The EMSEMBLE‐1 trial, a single‐arm phase II study that adopted SC‐RT followed by six cycles of CAPOX, reported a 23.3% incidence rate of grade 3–4 neutropenia and an 83.3% CAPOX completion rate, comparable to those in the LC‐CRT‐CNCT group in the present study [20]. Currently, there is no clear difference in the occurrence of AEs between SC‐RT and LC‐CRT during CNCT. A clinical trial comparing SC‐RT with LC‐CRT, followed by CNCT, is currently ongoing (NCT 04246684).

This study has some limitations. First, this was a retrospective analysis conducted at a single Japanese cancer center with a relatively small sample size (n = 93). Given the limited number of patients in each treatment subgroup, comparisons—such as between LC‐CRT‐CNCT and SC‐RT‐CNCT—may have been underpowered, and non‐significant findings could reflect a type II error rather than true equivalence. In addition, the treatment strategy in Japan differs from Western practice, which may limit the generalizability of our results. Because treatment allocation was not randomized, unmeasured confounders and potential physician selection bias may also have influenced which patients received each TNT sequence. Second, the logistic regression analysis may be subject to overfitting, and because multiple comparisons were performed without propensity score–based balancing, the risk of type I error cannot be excluded. Third, there are difficulties in data collection regarding distinguishing AEs between chemotherapy and radiotherapy, and follow‐up during CRT varied among physicians. However, the evaluation of non‐hematologic grade 3 or higher AEs was minimally affected, because additional medical examinations are conducted based on the severity of symptoms. Fourth, bevacizumab was administered only in the INCT‐LC‐CRT group (22/28 patients). Recent studies have shown that adding bevacizumab to chemotherapy is feasible [22, 31], its use may have contributed to the higher rate of neutropenia observed in this cohort [34]. Because only six patients did not receive bevacizumab, a meaningful sensitivity analysis excluding them was not feasible. Therefore, bevacizumab exposure remains a potential source of hematologic confounding, although the higher overall toxicity observed in the CNCT cohorts despite more frequent bevacizumab use in the INCT cohort suggests that bevacizumab alone is unlikely to account for the observed differences. Fifth, detailed measures of treatment intensity could not be assessed because the relative dose intensity of capecitabine could not be calculated, as remaining‐pill information was not available. Finally, although our study identified differences in treatment‐related toxicity between sequences, we were unable to assess their impact on patient‐reported quality of life. Hematologic toxicities may have less effect on daily functioning than gastrointestinal toxicities such as diarrhea, which our analysis could not capture. Taken together, these limitations indicate that the present findings should be interpreted with caution, and future prospective studies are needed to refine patient selection criteria and determine how best to balance oncologic efficacy and treatment tolerability.

In conclusion, grades 3–4 AEs during chemotherapy were more frequent and required dose modification in the LC‐CRT‐CNCT and SC‐RT‐CNCT groups than in the INCT‐LC‐CRT group. The safety and tolerability of the chemotherapy component of TNT may differ between the INCT‐LC‐CRT and LC‐CRT‐CNCT/SC‐RT‐CNCT groups.
