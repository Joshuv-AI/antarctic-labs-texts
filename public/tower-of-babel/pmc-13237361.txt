Clinical Severity and Outcome Measures in Seborrheic Dermatitis: A Systematic Review
Authors: Sophia A Mense, Theresa Hopkins, Carmen Li, Jiaqi Chen, Sawyeh Maher, Meredith Polaskey, Christy H Chang, Raj Chovatiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSeborrheic dermatitis (SD) is a common chronic inflammatory dermatosis associated with substantial symptom burden and impaired quality of life. Despite increasing interest in development of advanced therapies for SD, standardization of outcome measures appears limited. We sought to understand how disease severity, symptoms, and quality of life are measured in seborrheic dermatitis clinical studies.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review of clinical studies (both interventional and observational) assessing SD severity and treatment response. Eligible studies were identified through comprehensive database searches (Embase, PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, CINAHL Complete) and screened according to predefined inclusion criteria. Outcome measures were categorized by framework (clinician, patient, or combined), assessed domain(s), instrument type, and temporal, geographic, and study design characteristics.ResultsA total of 215 studies were included in the final analysis. Most studies relied primarily on clinician-reported outcomes (ClinROs), with fewer than half incorporating any patient-reported outcomes (PROs). Investigator-defined ClinROs predominated, while formally named, SD-specific indices, such as the Seborrheic Dermatitis Area and Severity Index, were infrequently used. Core clinical signs, including erythema and scaling, were consistently assessed across multiple study decades, whereas subjective symptoms and quality-of-life domains were measured less consistently. Although the use of combined ClinRO + PRO frameworks increased over time, clinician-centered assessments remained dominant across study designs and regions.ConclusionOutcome measurement in seborrheic dermatitis research is heterogeneous and remains predominantly clinician-centered, with limited validation and inconsistent use of validated ClinRO or PRO instruments. Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Seborrheic dermatitis (SD) is associated with meaningful symptom burden and quality-of-life impairment, yet outcome measurement practices in SD clinical research remain variable and incompletely standardized.This systematic review examined how SD severity, symptoms, and quality of life are measured in clinical studies and evaluated how outcome assessment approaches have changed over time.What was learned from the study?Most SD studies relied primarily on clinician-reported outcomes, with fewer than half incorporating patient-reported outcomes and limited use of validated seborrheic dermatitis-specific severity instruments.Although combined clinician- and patient-reported frameworks increased modestly over time, outcome measurement approaches remained heterogeneous across studies.Variability in outcome selection and inconsistent use of validated instruments may limit cross-trial comparability and underestimate patient-experienced disease burden in SD.

Introduction

Seborrheic dermatitis (SD) is a common, chronic inflammatory dermatosis characterized by erythema, scaling, and pruritus in sebaceous-rich areas of the scalp, face, and trunk. Affecting approximately 4% of the global population, SD is associated with substantial symptom burden and impaired quality of life [1, 2]. Although increasing recognition of disease burden has been accompanied by a growing number of clinical trials and emerging therapies, outcome measurement in SD research remains highly heterogeneous. Wide variation exists in the use of clinician-reported outcomes (ClinROs), patient-reported outcomes (PROs), global assessments, and study-specific severity measures, limiting cross-study comparability and evidence synthesis.

Across dermatology, methodological guidance emphasizes the importance of distinguishing observable clinical signs, best assessed using ClinROs, from subjective symptoms and quality-of-life (QoL) impacts, which require PRO instruments for valid measurement [3–6]. Similar challenges have been observed in other inflammatory skin diseases, including atopic dermatitis and psoriasis, where early heterogeneity in outcome measures prompted the development of standardized frameworks and clearer separation between clinician- and patient-reported assessments [7, 8]. Validated dermatology-specific PRO instruments, such as Skindex, the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI), and Scalpdex, have improved patient-centered assessment, while recent consensus initiatives have called for clearer outcome taxonomies, standardized definitions, and improved reporting to reduce heterogeneity and enhance evidence synthesis [9–13].

In SD specifically, multiple clinician-rated severity indices have been proposed, including Investigator Global Assessment (IGA) scales, the Seborrheic Dermatitis Area and Severity Index (SDASI), Seborrheic Dermatitis Severity Index (SASI), Severity Index (SI), SEborrheic Dermatitis Area and Severity Index (SEDASI), Adherent Scalp Flaking Score (ASFS), and related scoring systems [14–18]. However, these instruments vary substantially in domain composition, scoring structure, and psychometric validation, and none have achieved widespread adoption. Prior reviews have noted that inconsistent instrument design and limited validation restrict interpretability and comparability across studies [15, 16].

Despite growing emphasis on structured ClinRO and PRO frameworks, no prior study has comprehensively evaluated how SD severity and treatment response are measured across the clinical literature. Existing reviews have focused on individual instruments but have not examined outcome measurement practices across time, geographic regions, or study designs, nor assessed the balance between validated instruments and investigator-defined or insufficiently described measures [3, 15, 16]. An additional concern is the frequent clinician rating of inherently subjective symptoms, particularly pruritus, which introduces potential construct misalignment and measurement bias [6, 19, 20].

To address these gaps, we conducted a systematic evaluation of outcome measures used in seborrheic dermatitis clinical studies. Our objectives were to characterize the use of ClinROs and PROs; identify domains and instruments used to assess SD severity, symptoms, and QoL; quantify clinician-rated assessment of subjective symptom domains; evaluate the use of validated versus investigator-defined measures; and examine temporal, regional, and study design–related trends. By mapping the outcome measurement landscape in SD, this study aims to inform future methodological standardization and improve the reliability and patient-centeredness of SD severity assessment.

Methods

Study Design and Search Strategy

The primary objective of this study was to characterize outcome measurement frameworks used in clinical studies of SD, including the relative use of ClinROs and PROs, the clinical domains assessed (e.g., erythema, scaling, pruritus, QoL), and the specific instruments or scale types employed (e.g., seborrheic dermatitis–specific indices, investigator-defined scales, dermatology-specific QoL instruments). Secondary objectives included quantifying the prevalence of clinician-rated assessments of inherently subjective symptoms (e.g., pruritus), identifying and describing nonstandard or poorly described severity and improvement measures, and examining temporal, geographic, and study design-based trends in outcome measurement practices across the SD literature.

To address these objectives, we conducted a structured, systematic search of electronic databases to identify clinical studies evaluating SD severity or treatment response. Searches were performed in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and CINAHL Complete from inception through 15 September 2024, using controlled vocabulary terms and keywords for seborrheic dermatitis (e.g., “seborrheic dermatitis,” “dandruff”) combined with terms related to outcome measurement (e.g., “severity,” “clinical score,” “scale,” “global assessment,” “improvement,” “quality of life”). The full search strategies for each database are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

No restrictions were placed on publication date; searches were limited to English-language studies conducted in human participants. In addition, ClinicalTrials.gov was searched to identify unpublished or ongoing studies reporting SD outcome measures. Reference lists of included studies were manually screened to identify additional eligible records. The search strategy was developed a priori on the basis of a prespecified protocol (PROSPERO CRD42024567000). Results were collated and deduplicated in EndNote (Microsoft) and uploaded to Rayyan (Qatar Computing Research Institute) for screening [21].

Study Selection and Bias Assessment

A total of 11,943 records were retrieved. Two authors (C.L. and J.C.) independently screened all titles and abstracts utilizing Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) methodology [22]. Studies were eligible if they: (1) involved human participants with a clinical diagnosis of seborrheic dermatitis; (2) reported at least one measurable SD-related severity, symptom, or treatment-response outcome; and (3) were original clinical investigations, including randomized controlled trials (RCTs), nonrandomized clinical trials, prospective and retrospective observational studies, cross-sectional studies, and case series. Studies were excluded if they were reviews, qualitative studies, editorials, commentaries, or non-English-language publications without available English translation. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. Full texts of potential studies were retrieved and independently reviewed by two authors (C.L. and J.C.) for final inclusion. Discussion and consensus were again utilized to resolve discrepancies.

Risk of bias and study quality was assessed for all included studies using the appropriate Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools, selected according to study design (randomized clinical trial, cross-sectional study, cohort study, or case series) [23]. Risk-of-bias judgments were recorded as low, unclear, or high risk for each domain. Overall study quality was determined by the number of deficiencies across domains, with results summarized narratively.

Data Extraction and Analysis

Data were extracted using a structured form based on a prespecified data dictionary by three authors (S.A.M., T.H., and S.M.). For each study, bibliographic and contextual variables including title, first author, year of publication, country, study design, and clinical setting were recorded. Each outcome was mapped to a standardized outcome classification on the basis of rater (ClinRO versus PRO), construct (global severity [including global improvement or response-based construct], composite severity, or single-domain severity), and degree of standardization. Named or established instruments (e.g., SD-specific indices, dermatology-specific QoL instruments, visual analog scales (VAS), or numeric rating scales (NRS) symptom scales) were explicitly identified and classified as formal instruments, whereas study-specific, investigator-defined measures without published validation were categorized as nonstandard clinician- or patient-reported outcomes, as appropriate. The resulting data contained both outcome-level and study-level records. For study-level analyses, outcomes within the same study were aggregated to generate a single analytic record capturing instrument categories used, overall outcome framework (ClinRO only, PRO only, or combined ClinRO and PRO), and domains assessed.

Clinician-Reported Outcomes versus Patient-Reported Outcomes

Outcome measures were classified according to the rater completing the assessment, consistent with contemporary guidance on clinical outcome assessments and PRO reporting [3, 24, 25]. ClinROs were defined as measures scored exclusively by investigators or clinicians, regardless of the specific domain assessed [14, 16]. PROs were defined as instruments completed directly by patients, including symptom severity ratings, patient global assessments, multisymptom PRO composites, and dermatology-specific QoL instruments [9, 10, 12, 13, 24]. When rater attribution was explicitly stated, outcomes were classified accordingly. When rater attribution was not explicitly stated but could be reasonably inferred from the description of data collection (e.g., assessments performed by clinicians during study visits), measures were classified as ClinROs. Measures explicitly described as patient-completed were classified as PROs. Outcomes for which rater attribution could not be determined or reasonably inferred were flagged as unclear.

Instrument Category Classification

Each outcome was assigned to an instrument category on the basis of its name, scoring structure, construct, and degree of standardization. Formal SD-specific severity indices (e.g., SDASI) and related indices (e.g., SASI, SEDASI, ASFS, SI) were classified according to their published definitions, acknowledging the limited validation and heterogeneous application of SD-specific severity tools [14–17]. Additional named SD-specific or composite severity instruments reported in a smaller number of studies included the Scalp Seborrheic Dermatitis Severity Index (SSSD), Total Symptom Score (TSS), and Total Dermatitis Severity Score (TDSS), which were classified according to author-described definitions. Global clinician-reported assessments included IGA as well as the Investigator’s Static Global Assessment (ISGA), Physician’s Global Assessment (PGA), and Facial Investigator Global Assessment (F-IGA), all of which were grouped as named global ClinROs [26–28]. Patient-reported symptom measures included the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) and Numeric Rating Scale (NRS), while validated dermatology-specific QoL instruments included the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI), Scalpdex, and Skindex, which were grouped as established QoL measures given their documented reliability and validity in dermatology populations [9, 10, 12, 13, 24, 29, 30]. Outcomes based on study-specific, investigator-defined scales without published validation were categorized as investigator-defined ClinROs and further subclassified as global severity, global improvement, composite severity, or single-domain severity measures based on their reported structure and anchoring. Patient-completed study-specific symptom or multisymptom instruments without established validation were classified as nonstandard PROs [4].

Mixed Frameworks and Hybrid Instruments

At the study level, the overall outcome framework was classified as ClinRO only, PRO only, or combined ClinRO and PRO. When studies used instruments that incorporated both clinician-rated and patient-rated components, these components were disaggregated and coded separately according to rater, rather than treated as a distinct hybrid category, consistent with established outcome taxonomy recommendations in dermatology [3, 6].

Clinician-Rated Subjective Symptom Domains

Subjective symptoms such as pruritus, burning, and stinging are conventionally assessed using PRO instruments. To identify studies in which these inherently subjective domains were assessed exclusively by clinicians, we created a binary clinician-rated subjective symptom indicator. An outcome was coded as clinician-rated subjective when (1) the domain was inherently subjective and (2) the study explicitly attributed scoring to the investigator (e.g., “pruritus scored 0–3 by the physician”), with no corresponding patient-completed instrument (e.g., itch VAS or NRS). In accordance with rater-based outcome classification frameworks, these measures were retained as ClinROs but flagged as potential sources of construct misalignment between the domain assessed and the rater performing the assessment [6, 19, 20].

Study-Level Aggregation

For study-level analyses, outcome-level records were aggregated such that each study contributed a single analytic record while allowing for multiple outcome instruments per study. Study design, clinical setting, geographic region, and publication year were retained as reported in the original articles and used to derive standardized design, setting, and region categories. Clinical domains, including erythema, scaling, pruritus, and quality of life, were coded as present at the study level if they were assessed by any outcome measure within a given study. Binary indicators were created to capture the presence of any ClinRO, any PRO, and any clinician-rated subjective symptom domain within each study. Based on these indicators, each study’s overall outcome framework was classified as ClinRO only, PRO only, or combined ClinRO + PRO. Additional binary indicators captured the use of specific instrument categories, including any SDASI, any DLQI, any VAS or NRS, and any investigator-defined composite, as well as the presence of at least one unclear or nonstandard outcome measure. For temporal analyses, publication year was grouped into predefined decades (before 1980, 1980s, 1990s, 2000s, 2010s, and 2020s).

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were descriptive and performed in R (version 4.4.2). Study characteristics (design, setting, region, and decade of publication) were summarized using frequency and proportion counts. Stratified summaries by decade, geographic region, and study design were generated to examine temporal and regional patterns in measurement practices. No meta-analyses or pooled effect estimates were performed because of heterogeneity in outcome definitions, study populations, and interventions. Study quality and risk of bias were descriptively summarized using frequency counts of the number of JBI critical appraisal tools criteria met, respective to the study design.

Ethical Approval

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Results

Study Characteristics

A total of 7220 deduplicated records were initially screened, with 215 published clinical studies included in the final analysis (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table 2) [31–266]. Publication volume increased substantially over time: pre-1980 (n = 4 [1.9%]), 1980s (14 [6.5%]), 1990s (22 [10.2%]), 2000s (72 [33.5%]), 2010s (60 [27.9%]), and 2020s (43 [20.0%]) (Table 1). Study designs were predominantly randomized controlled trials (128 [59.5%]) and nonrandomized clinical trials (63 [29.3%]), with smaller proportions of observational cross-sectional studies (14 [6.5%]), observational case–control or cohort studies (7 [3.3%]), and case series (3 [1.4%]). Most studies were conducted in single-center dermatology outpatient clinics (160 [74.4%]), followed by multicenter studies (42 [19.5%]). Pediatric dermatology clinics (8 [3.7%]) and hospital or inpatient settings (5 [2.3%]) were infrequently represented. Geographically, most studies were conducted in Europe (83 [38.6%]), followed by the Middle East (48 [22.3%]), Asia (41 [19.1%]), and North America (29 [13.5%]); fewer studies were multinational (11 [5.1%]) or conducted in South America (2 [0.9%]) or Oceania (1 [0.5%]).

Treatment Categories Represented Across Included Studies

Included studies evaluated a broad range of SD interventions. Most treatment studies examined topical therapies, particularly topical antifungals, topical corticosteroids, and topical calcineurin inhibitors. Medicated shampoos and keratolytic formulations (e.g., selenium sulfide-, zinc pyrithione-, coal tar-, and salicylic acid-based products) were also frequently represented, especially in scalp-focused studies. Less commonly represented interventions included systemic antifungals, oral isotretinoin, complementary or herbal therapies, and procedural approaches such as phototherapy, photodynamic therapy, and cryotherapy.

ClinRO and PRO Use

ClinROs were used in the majority of studies (Table 2). Overall, ClinRO-only frameworks were employed in 126 (58.6%), while combined ClinRO and PRO frameworks were used in 88 (40.9%). PRO-only frameworks were rare, occurring only in one study (0.5%). Across decades, ClinRO-only frameworks were used in 3 (75.0%) studies published before 1980, 9 (64.3%) in the 1980s, 13 (59.1%) in the 1990s, 48 (66.7%) in the 2000s, 30 (50.0%) in the 2010s, and 23 (53.5%) in the 2020s. Combined ClinRO + PRO frameworks were used in 5 (35.7%) of studies in the 1980s, 9 (40.9%) in the 1990s, 24 (33.3%) in the 2000s, 30 (50.0%) in the 2010s, and 20 (46.5%) in the 2020s. PRO-only frameworks were observed only in one study published before 1980 (25.0%).

Use of Investigator-Defined versus Formal Clinician-Reported Outcome Measures

Clinician-reported outcomes were used in the majority of included studies, most commonly in the form of investigator-defined severity measures (Table 2). Overall, investigator-defined ClinROs were used in 175 studies (81.4%), whereas formal clinician-reported instruments were used in 85 studies (39.5%) (see Supplementary Table 3 for a listing of commonly used clinician-oriented instruments). At the study level, investigator-defined ClinROs were used exclusively in 129 studies (60.0%), formal ClinROs were used exclusively in 39 studies (18.1%), and both investigator-defined and formal ClinROs were used in 46 studies (21.4%); 1 study (0.5%) did not report a clinician-reported severity measure. Use of investigator-defined ClinROs varied over time. Investigator-defined clinician-reported measures were used in 3 studies published before 1980 (75.0%), all studies published in the 1980s (14 [100.0%]) and 1990s (22 [100.0%]), and most studies in the 2000s (68 [94.4%]). Although reliance on investigator-defined ClinROs declined in later decades, they remained common in the 2010s (44 [73.3%]) and 2020s (24 [55.8%]). Across study designs, investigator-defined ClinROs were most frequently reported in randomized controlled trials (109 [85.2%]), nonrandomized clinical trials (57 [90.5%]). Lower use was observed in observational cross-sectional studies (3 [21.4%]) and observational case–control or cohort studies (3 [42.9%]).

Types of Investigator-Defined Clinician-Reported Outcome Measures

Among studies using investigator-defined ClinROs, single-domain clinician-rated measures were nearly ubiquitous, reported in 156 studies (89.1%). These most commonly assessed individual signs or symptoms such as erythema, scaling, or pruritus. Composite investigator-defined ClinROs, incorporating multiple clinical domains within a single score, were reported in 132 studies (75.4%). In contrast, investigator-defined global clinician assessments, such as overall disease severity or global improvement ratings, were less frequently reported (n = 89 [50.9%]). These subtype categories were not mutually exclusive.

Named Severity Indices

Formally-named, SD-specific severity indices were infrequently used across the included literature. The most commonly reported index was SDASI, which appeared in 20 studies (9.3%). Other named indices were used in small proportions of studies, including the SASI (n = 6 [2.8%]), SEDASI (6 [2.8%]), ASFS (7 [3.3%]), SI (7 [3.3%]), and SSSD (6 [2.8%]). Additional named instruments, such as the TSS (2 [0.9%]) and TDSS (2 [0.9%]), were rarely reported. Global clinician-reported assessments were reported in a minority of studies, most commonly IGA (24 [11.2%]), with fewer studies using ISGA (5 [2.3%]), PGA (4 [1.9%]), or F-IGA (2 [0.9%]).

Patient-Reported Symptom Measures

Patient-reported symptom measures were used in a minority of studies (Table 2; see Supplementary Table 3 for a listing of commonly used patient-oriented instruments). Overall, investigator-defined patient-reported symptom measures were reported in 85 studies (39.5%), whereas formal patient-reported symptom instruments were less common, including VAS in 23 studies (10.7%) and NRS in 9 studies (4.2%). At the study level, most studies did not include any patient-reported symptom instrument (130 [60.5%]). When patient-reported symptom measures were included, they were more frequently investigator-defined than formal, validated instruments.

Clinician-Rated Subjective Symptom Domains

Clinician rating of inherently subjective symptom domains was common. Overall, 73 studies (34.0%) included at least one clinician-rated subjective symptom, with pruritus most frequently scored by investigators without a corresponding patient-completed instrument. This practice occurred across study designs, including randomized controlled trials (49 [38.3%]), nonrandomized clinical trials (23 [36.5%]), and observational case–control or cohort studies (1 [14.3%]); no case series or observational cross-sectional studies included clinician-rated subjective symptom domains. Regionally, clinician-rated subjective symptom domains were observed across all major geographic regions. Among regions with larger study counts, clinician-rated subjective symptoms were most frequently reported in studies conducted in Europe (31 [37.3%]), Asia (14 [34.1%]), North America (9 [31.0%]), and the Middle East (14 [29.2%]). Clinician-rated subjective symptoms were also reported in multinational studies (5 [45.5%]). No clinician-rated subjective symptom domains were observed in studies conducted in Oceania or South America. Over time, the frequency of clinician-rated subjective symptom assessment declined, from studies published before 1980 (1 [25.0%]) to the 1980s (7 [50.0%]), 1990s (11 [50.0%]), 2000s (37 [51.4%]), 2010s (11 [18.3%]), and 2020s (6 [14.0%]). Despite this decline, clinician-rated assessment of subjective symptoms remains present in contemporary SD research.

Quality-of-Life Assessment

QoL outcomes were infrequently incorporated into seborrheic dermatitis studies (Table 2). Overall, 23 studies (10.7%) assessed QoL, including validated dermatology-specific QoL instruments (n = 20 [9.3%]) and unspecified QoL measures (3 [1.4%]). Among validated instruments, the DLQI was most used (17 [7.9%]), followed by Scalpdex (3 [1.4%]) and Skindex (1 [0.5%]). Use of validated QoL instruments increased over time, with no studies published before 2000 reporting a formal QoL measure, followed by the 2000s (1 [1.4%]), 2010s (10 [16.7%]), and 2020s (9 [20.9%]).

Domains Assessed Across Studies

Across the included studies, assessment of SD severity focused predominantly on a limited set of clinical domains. The most frequently assessed domains were scaling (198 [92.1%]), erythema (181 [84.2%]), and pruritus (147 [68.4%]). Lesion extent was assessed in 60 studies (27.9%), whereas sensory irritation was reported in 38 studies (17.7%). Other clinical features were evaluated infrequently, including seborrhea (16 [7.4%]), inflammatory lesions (16 [7.4%]), crusting (14 [6.5%]), induration or edema (11 [5.1%]), dryness (8 [3.7%]), inflammation (7 [3.3%]), hair loss (4 [1.9%]), and folliculitis (2 [0.9%]) (Table 3).

Patterns of domain assessment varied across decades. Scaling and erythema remained consistently the most frequently assessed domains across all time periods, while pruritus remained commonly reported, including the 2000s (53 [73.6%]) and 2020s (26 [60.5%]) (Fig. 2).

Risk of Bias and Study Quality

The appraisal of 215 included studies through the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist revealed overall low risk of bias (139 studies) with 56 studies noted to be medium risk and 20 studies high risk [23]. Study quality, also assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist, was overall high quality, although 34 studies exhibited deficiencies in 1 domain, 18 studies in 2 domains, and 77 studies in 3 or more domains (primarily among RCTs owing to their larger sample). Ambiguities were less prevalent, with unclear ratings in several domains across 31 studies. Nearly all studies used appropriate statistical analyses (RCTs: 119/128; case series: 2/3; case–control: 6/7; cross-sectional: 13/14). The validity and reliability of outcome measures were strong across designs (RCTs: 124/128 reliable; cross-sectional: 14/14 valid).

Discussion

In this systematic review of 215 clinical studies, we observed that outcome measurement in SD appears to remain predominantly clinician-centered, highly heterogeneous, and infrequently anchored in validated instruments. Most trials and observational studies relied on ClinROs, with fewer than half incorporating any PROs. Although the use of combined ClinRO + PRO frameworks increased over time, ClinRO-dominant designs continued to predominate, suggesting that the broader shift toward patient-centered outcome measurement in dermatology has only partially reached SD clinical research.

The distribution of study designs and settings underscores the central role of SD severity measurement in interventional research. Nearly 60% of included studies were RCTs, and approximately one-third were nonrandomized clinical trials, with most conducted in outpatient dermatology settings. Treatment-related literature was mainly focused on topical therapies for scalp and facial disease, particularly antifungals, corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors, and shampoos, whereas systemic and procedural approaches were less commonly represented. Outcome measures used in these contexts are therefore likely to influence how treatment response is conceptualized in clinical trials, practice guidelines, and regulatory decision-making. However, most studies relied on investigator-defined clinician-reported severity measures rather than named or established instruments, with substantial variation in the domains assessed and scoring approaches. Over time, the proportion of ClinRO-only studies declined overall, with a corresponding increase in studies using combined ClinRO + PRO frameworks; however, overall adoption of PROs remained modest. For a disease in which itch, burning, and perception of visual involvement drive much of the patient burden, reliance on ClinROs alone risks underrepresenting these domains.

Outcome heterogeneity also has important implications for trial comparability and interpretability in SD research. When studies assess SD severity using different constructs, domain combinations, and investigator-defined scoring systems, results become difficult to compare across interventions and cannot be reliably synthesized across trials [7]. This limits evidence pooling and contributes to research waste, particularly in a field where multiple therapies are being evaluated using overlapping but inconsistently defined end points. Importantly, the frequency with which an outcome measure was used should not be interpreted as evidence of superior validity, reliability, or overall suitability. Greater standardization of outcome selection and reporting would improve cross-trial comparability and strengthen the clinical relevance of future SD evidence.

This imbalance is particularly concerning given evidence demonstrating discordance between clinician and patient perceptions of disease severity and impact. National survey data show that clinicians systematically rate SD severity and impact as lower than patients rate themselves [56]. In addition, when patients were asked about desired qualities in prescription topical treatments for seborrheic dermatitis, reduction of itch was identified as the highest priority, with 71% of patients citing it as a key treatment goal [57]. Because itch is inherently a patient-reported symptom that cannot be reliably inferred from clinician observation, ClinRO-dominant assessments may fail to capture the outcomes that matter most to patients. Furthermore, only 25% of patients reported being “very satisfied” with the effectiveness of their prescription treatments, suggesting substantial unmet needs that are underrecognized when outcomes are evaluated primarily through clinician-centered measures [57]. Together, these findings highlight the importance of broader PRO integration to improve the accuracy and relevance, and patient-centeredness of outcome assessment in future SD research and clinical practice.

Nearly one-third of studies assigned subjective symptoms, most commonly pruritus, to clinician rating without a corresponding patient-reported instrument, a practice observed across study designs, geographic regions, and decades. Although such measures are appropriately classified as ClinROs based on rater attribution, clinician rating of subjective symptoms may introduce measurement bias. This is especially relevant in composite scores combining observable signs, such as erythema and scaling, with clinician-rated symptoms, which may limit comparability with studies that use validated PRO instruments such as itch NRS or VAS.

Domain-level mapping further demonstrated that SD severity assessment primarily prioritizes visible clinical signs. Scaling and erythema were assessed in most studies (92.1% and 84.2%, respectively), and pruritus was reported in approximately two-thirds of studies (68.4%). In contrast, lesional extent (27.9%), sensory irritation (17.7%), seborrhea (7.4%), inflammatory lesions (7.4%), and secondary features such as crusting, hair loss, or folliculitis were captured inconsistently. QoL domains were evaluated in only a small proportion of studies (10.7%), suggesting limited assessment of functional and psychosocial disease impact, despite the availability of validated dermatology-specific instruments such as the DLQI, Scalpdex, and Skindex with established reliability and responsiveness in SD and related dermatoses [10, 11, 13, 58–60].

The pattern of instrument use is consistent with the sign-focused conceptualization of SD severity. Despite the availability of named SD-specific indices, formal severity instruments were used relatively infrequently. The SDASI, which has the strongest published validation evidence among SD-specific instruments, appeared in fewer than 10% of studies [14]. Other named indices, including the SASI, SEDASI, ASFS, and related instruments, were each used in small proportions of studies and have more limited validation in SD populations.

These findings suggest several important implications for future SD research. Greater consistency in integrating ClinROs with PRO and QoL measures may improve the completeness and interpretability of SD severity assessment. In particular, approaches that pair structured ClinROs for observable signs with validated PRO instruments may better reflect the multidimensional burden of SD. In addition, the limited uptake of formal SD-specific severity indices highlights ongoing challenges in outcome standardization, suggesting that future work may benefit from a specific focus on feasibility, validation, and broader adoption of reproducible severity instruments.

Other inflammatory skin diseases may offer a roadmap for outcome standardization in seborrheic dermatitis. In atopic dermatitis, the Harmonising Outcome Measures for Eczema (HOME) initiative was established to address research waste arising from inconsistent outcome reporting and has since developed a consensus-based core outcome set through international consensus meetings and Delphi processes, incorporating domains for clinical signs, symptoms, quality of life, and long-term disease control [7]. Similar harmonization efforts in psoriasis have led to adoption of standardized core domains, reducing outcome heterogeneity and improving comparability across studies [8]. Comparable collaborative efforts to develop a core outcome set for SD could similarly promote consistent, interpretable, and patient-relevant outcome assessment in future SD research.

This study has several limitations. Inclusion was restricted to published, English-language clinical studies; therefore, outcome measurement practices in non-English publications or unpublished studies may differ. Classification of outcomes as ClinROs versus PROs, as well as assignment of domains within composite measures, depended on the clarity of reporting. Misclassification is possible when studies did not explicitly specify who completed an instrument or fully describe its structure. Study quality was assessed using JBI critical appraisal tools, and the common reliance on investigator-defined measures with incomplete reporting may limit reproducibility and interpretability across studies. In addition, we did not formally evaluate psychometric properties such as responsiveness, minimal clinically important differences, or inter-rater reliability, and therefore cannot determine the relative performance of individual instruments beyond their frequency of use and reporting characteristics.

A key strength of this study is the application of PRISMA methodology to provide the most comprehensive characterization of SD outcome measurement across clinical studies to date. By cataloging instruments, assessed domains, rater types, and reporting clarity across 215 interventional and observational studies spanning multiple decades and geographic regions, this systematic review and synthesis delineates a measurement landscape characterized by predominant use of clinician-reported outcomes, heterogeneous and often investigator-defined severity measures, modest integration of patient-reported outcomes, frequent clinician rating of subjective symptoms, and limited use of validated QoL instruments.

Conclusions

SD clinical research shows substantial variability and frequent reliance on investigator-defined ClinROs, with limited use of PROs and validated severity instruments. Greater standardization of outcome measures, including integration of validated ClinRO indices, patient-reported symptom scores, and QoL assessments, may improve comparability, interpretability, and patient-centeredness in future SD clinical research.
