The Mediating Role of Professional Identity in the Relationship Between Chinese Intern Nursing Students' Attitudes Towards Nurses and Academic Resilience: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Chunxia Wang, Jinxiu Chen, Yun Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo examine the association between Chinese intern nursing students' attitudes towards nurses and academic resilience, and to explore professional identity's statistical mediating role.DesignA cross‐sectional study.MethodsThe study was conducted on the intern nursing students in a tertiary hospital in Hubei Province, China from February 2025 to March 2025, using the convenience sampling method. Data were collected using the General Information Questionnaire, Nursing Students' Attitudes towards Nurses during Clinical Practice, Professional Identity Scale for Nursing Students and Nursing Student Academic Resilience Inventory. Data were analysed using non‐parametric tests and Spearman correlation analysis. Mediation analysis was conducted using the SPSS PROCESS v4.0 macro.ResultsA positive correlation was found between intern nursing students' attitudes towards nurses and their professional identity, as well as with academic resilience. Additionally, there was a positive correlation between professional identity and academic resilience. Mediation analysis showed that professional identity of intern nursing students statistically mediated the association between attitudes towards nurses and academic resilience, with the indirect association accounting for 60.19% of the total association.ConclusionProfessional identity statistically mediates the association between intern nursing students' attitudes towards nurses and academic resilience. These findings suggest that fostering positive attitudes towards nurses and enhancing professional identity are concurrently associated with higher academic resilience, and the causal direction of this relationship warrants further investigation with longitudinal studies.Implications for the ProfessionNursing educators and clinical nurses could consider focusing on facilitating positive interactions that shape students' attitudes while implementing programs such as reflective practice and career counselling to cultivate professional identity, thereby strengthening academic resilience, reducing attrition and improving workforce stability.ImpactThese findings can inform nursing educators, clinical preceptors and policymakers in designing targeted interventions to enhance intern nursing students' academic resilience and reduce attrition.Reporting MethodThis study adhered to the STROBE reporting guideline.Patient or Public ContributionNo patients or members of the public were involved in this study.Keywords: academic resilience, attitude, intern nursing students, mediating role, professional identity

Introduction

Nursing students experience significantly higher levels of academic stress compared to students in other disciplines (Bartlett et al. 2016). Primary stressors encompass academic workload (Quinn and Peters 2017), life adaptation challenges (Shudifat and Al‐Husban 2015), clinical practice demands and career‐related apprehensions (Tan et al. 2024). This stress intensifies during highly dynamic and demanding clinical internships (Liang et al. 2019; Zheng et al. 2022). Unlike non‐intern nursing students who mainly receive theoretical education, intern nursing students undergo a critical role transition from students to practicing clinical caregiver throughout clinical practice (Zhao et al. 2024). Previous study has shown that intern nursing students frequently report feeling overwhelmed by the weight of responsibility, fear of making mistakes and emotional exhaustion from witnessing patient suffering (Pasay‐An et al. 2025). These experiences constitute additional stressors unique to this group. Empirical research shows that approximately 9% of nursing students drop out annually (Krautscheid et al. 2022), with graduation rates ranging from 80% to 87% (Smith‐Wacholz et al. 2019). Moreover, up to 51.4% of nursing students consider changing their major, and those who do tend to exhibit low academic resilience (Hwang 2025). This attrition jeopardizes nursing workforce stability and impedes clinical nursing quality enhancement, underscoring the urgency of improving clinical adaptability and professional commitment among nursing interns.

Academic resilience (AR) refers to an individual's capacity to positively adapt, effectively cope with and ultimately achieve academic success when facing academic pressure and challenges (Martin and Marsh 2006; Tan et al. 2024). As a protective factor for intern nursing students, AR helps them regulate academic stress, reduce frustration and dropout risk (Hwang and Shin 2018), focus on their strengths and enhance resilience in dealing with clinical challenges (Martin and Marsh 2006); higher AR correlates with greater adaptability, professional maturity and academic achievement (Shen et al. 2024). However, most current research indicates that the AR of nursing students typically ranges from low to moderate levels and fluctuates with experiential and affective states (Abou Hashish et al. 2024; Setlogelo and Nyoni 2024), highlighting the importance of exploring its enhancement mechanisms.

Nursing students' attitudes towards nurses (NSATN) refer to their overall perceptions, emotional orientations and behavioural tendencies towards nurses during clinical practice (Kaplan et al. 2024). Positive attitudes may help foster supportive relationships, enhance motivation and confidence and facilitate clinical skill acquisition and professional identity formation, whereas negative attitudes may hinder learning engagement and limit the benefits of clinical practice (Aragaw et al. 2019; Kaplan et al. 2024; Mattila et al. 2010; Özsaban and Bayram 2020). A previous study has emphasized that the supportive behaviours of clinical nurses play a significant role in enhancing the academic resilience of nursing students and have advocated for the adoption of multiple strategies to help nursing students better cope with the stress and challenges in clinical practice (Ye et al. 2024).

Professional identity (PI) reflects an individual's acceptance of occupational roles and affirmative appraisal of their profession's purpose, social value and associated attributes (Shi et al. 2025). Nursing students with high PI tend to adopt positive coping strategies and achieve higher self‐efficacy (Li et al. 2023), effectively alleviating learning burnout and academic stress (Li 2024). Thus, PI plays a crucial role in fostering academic resilience.

While positive nurse–student interactions have been shown to promote students' motivation and learning, and PI has been recognized as a protective factor against academic stress, whether PI acts as a pathway through which nursing students' attitudes towards nurses influence AR remains unclear. Conservation of Resources (COR) theory holds that individuals gain more abundant coping resources through resource accumulation, thereby enhancing their composure and sense of efficacy in task handling (Chen et al. 2024; Hobfoll 2011). Building on this framework, we propose that PI may function as a key node for internal resource consolidation. Situational cognitive resources (positive attitudes of intern nursing students towards nurses) may contribute to the formation of enduring psychological resources (PI). This consolidation of intrapersonal resources may, in turn, support students in coping with academic and clinical adversity and be associated with higher AR. However, this theoretical pathway has not been tested. Addressing this gap is essential, as testing PI's mediating role would reveal the mechanism linking attitudes to AR and provide a theoretical foundation for targeted interventions to enhance AR, filling a key gap in current nursing education and clinical practice research.

Therefore, this study aims to describe the levels of academic resilience among intern nursing students, examine the association between attitudes towards nurses and academic resilience among Chinese intern nursing students and explore the statistical mediating role of professional identity. We hypothesize the following:

Intern nursing students' attitudes towards nurses are positively associated with both professional identity and academic resilience.

Intern nursing students' attitudes towards nurses are positively associated with both professional identity and academic resilience.

Professional identity is positively associated with academic resilience.

Professional identity statistically mediates the association between intern nursing students' attitudes towards nurses and academic resilience.

Method

Design

This was a cross‐sectional study.

Settings and Participants

Participants were selected through convenience sampling from the intern nursing students at a tertiary hospital in Hubei Province from February 2025 to March 2025. A tertiary hospital represents the highest level of hospital in China, with comprehensive capabilities in clinical care, education and research. The hospital in this study serves as a major clinical internship base for multiple nursing schools. The inclusion criteria were: ① Full‐time intern nursing students; ② Voluntary participation in the study with informed consent. Exclusion criteria: ① On leave during the survey period; ② Undergoing psychological treatment during the survey period or having experienced psychological trauma within the past 3 months—to prevent confounding effects on the research variables and to protect vulnerable individuals from potential psychological discomfort that may be caused by the survey content (Vyshka et al. 2023); ③ Internship duration less than 1 month. Sample size was determined via a formal a priori power analysis conducted using G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al. 2009). This power analysis was based on the multiple regression rather than specifically powering the indirect effect. With a medium effect size (f 2 = 0.15), an alpha level of 0.05, a statistical power of 0.80 (Cohen 1988) and 10 independent variables, the calculated minimum sample size was 118 participants. We recruited a total of 222 participants, which exceeded the estimated requirement.

Instrument

General Information Questionnaire

This questionnaire was self‐developed by the researchers to collect demographic data of the intern nursing students. It includes 8 items: gender, education level, residential status, whether nursing is the first major choice, teaching type during internship, total completed internship duration, frequency of experiencing job burnout during the internship and future career plans after graduation.

Nursing Students' Attitudes Towards Nurses During Clinical Practice (NSATN)

This scale was developed by Kaplan et al. (2024). It was selected because it specifically captures intern nursing students' interaction experiences with clinical nurses, which aligns with our research's focus on clinical practice contexts. Our research team translated the original scale into Chinese following Brislin's translation principles. The final Chinese version of NSATN comprised 25 items across two dimensions: Cooperation and Understanding as well as Communication and Professional Support. The Cronbach's α of the scale is 0.971. A 5‐point Likert scale was used, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with a total score ranging from 25 to 125. The higher the score, the more positive the attitude of the nursing students towards nurses during clinical practice. The original NSATN has a reported Cronbach's α of 0.946. In this study, the Chinese version of the NSATN demonstrated a Cronbach's α of 0.984.

Professional Identity Scale for Nursing Students (PISNS)

This scale was developed by Li et al. (2024). It is specifically designed to measure the professional identity level of nursing students and has been validated and demonstrated good applicability to Chinese nursing students. It consists of three dimensions: Professional Value, Professional Cognition and Professional Status, with a total of 28 items. A 5‐point Likert scale was used, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with a total score ranging from 28 to 140. The higher the score, the higher the professional identity level of the nursing students. The Cronbach's α of this scale is 0.971 and in this study, the Cronbach's α of this scale was 0.967.

Nursing Student Academic Resilience Inventory (NSARI)

This inventory was developed by Ali‐Abadi et al. (2021) and was translated into Chinese by Yu et al. (2024). It is developed specifically to measure academic resilience in nursing students, with its Chinese version showing strong reliability and validity. It consists of six dimensions: Optimism, Communication, Self‐esteem/evaluation, Self‐awareness, Trustworthiness and Self‐regulation, with a total of 24 items. It used a 5‐point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (strongly disagree) to 4 (completely agree), with a total score ranging from 0 to 96. The higher the score, the higher the academic resilience of the intern nursing students during the clinical practice. The Cronbach's α of the Chinese version of the scale is 0.939, and in this study, it was 0.978.

Data Collection

This study employed an electronic questionnaire distributed via the Questionnaire Star platform, supplemented by on‐site paper‐based surveys in the hospital. Although the mixed‐mode approach helped improve the response rate, it may introduce response bias. The questionnaire's cover page explicitly stated the research purpose and completion requirements. Following hospital ethics approval, intern nursing students voluntarily completed the survey anonymously. Researchers implemented single‐response restrictions and mandatory question settings, incorporating attention‐check items for quality control. A total of 236 questionnaires were distributed. Electronic questionnaire data were automatically exported from the Questionnaire Star platform into the database. Paper‐based questionnaire data were independently entered and cross‐checked by two researchers, with discrepancies resolved by a third researcher. Invalid responses (including identical answers throughout and completion in less than 2 min) were excluded. Ultimately, 222 valid questionnaires were collected, yielding an effective recovery rate of 94.07%.

Data Analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non‐normally distributed continuous variables were described using median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were evaluated using frequency and percentage. As the academic resilience scores were not normally distributed, differences in academic resilience scores across demographic characteristics were analysed using the Kruskal–Wallis test and Mann–Whitney U test. For variables with three or more groups that showed significant overall differences in the Kruskal–Wallis test, we performed Dunn's post hoc test with Bonferroni correction for multiple pairwise comparisons. Spearman correlation analysis was used to analyse the correlations among NSATN, PI and AR.

Covariates for the mediation model were selected based on conceptual considerations and prior empirical evidence rather than solely on univariate significance. Educational level was included because prior research shows that postgraduate students have been shown to be more resilient than undergraduates, as their successful undergraduate experience fosters more mature coping skills and psychological growth (Chow et al. 2018). Controlling for education level eliminates the confounding effect of academic stage maturity on core associations. Burnout frequency was adjusted for because academic resilience and burnout are inversely related; students with higher resilience are better able to withstand academic pressures (Romano et al. 2021). Previous studies have shown that students with career change intentions exhibit lower academic resilience (Hwang 2025). Controlling for career plans removes the interference of occupational intention differences on the observed indirect association. Teaching type was excluded from the primary model due to extremely sparse subgroup sizes, which would have compromised the stability of regression estimates. The mediating role of PI in the relationship between NSATN and AR was verified using the SPSS PROCESS v4.0 macro (Model 4) (Hayes and Scharkow 2013), with 5000 bootstrap samples and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The regression model underlying PROCESS assumes normality of residuals; therefore, we tested the residuals of the model predicting AR from NSATN and PI. The Shapiro–Wilk test indicated a significant deviation from normality (W = 0.959, p < 0.001). To address this violation, we used HC3 standard errors in the PROCESS macro. All reported path coefficients, p values and CIs for the direct and total effects are based on HC3 robust standard errors. An effect was considered significant if the 95% CI did not include zero. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

Chinese intern nursing students reported medium‐to‐high levels of AR and NSATN are positively associated with AR. The core finding is that PI statistically mediates the association between NSATN and AR among intern nursing students during their internship under this cross‐sectional design. Given these findings, fostering positive attitudes towards nurses and strengthening PI may be promising directions to support intern nursing students' AR, though the causal effects of such approaches require further verification.

Analysis of Status and Influencing Factors of AR of Intern Nursing Students During Internship

The mean score of AR of intern nursing students during internship in this study was 71.38 ± 13.94 (possible range: 0–96), which was at a medium‐to‐high level, higher than the research reported by Shahidi Delshad et al. (2023). One possible explanation for this difference may relate to the timing of data collection. Our study was conducted in the later stage of the internship when intern nursing students had accumulated experience in coping with academic pressure. Resilience can promote learning and growth in adverse situations (VanMeter and Cicchetti 2020; Zhou et al. 2022). Nursing work itself requires facing high‐intensity pressure, and intern nursing students may gradually enhance their resilience levels in coping with academic and professional challenges through long‐term exposure to a stressful environment. In addition, intern nursing students need to rotate through different departments, that is, they need to adapt to dynamic working scenarios, and this state may prompt them to form stronger adaptation and coping abilities, which is conducive to improving the level of AR.

Our findings revealed that educational level, teaching type, frequency of experiencing job burnout and future career plans after graduation were significantly associated with AR among intern nursing students. Differences related to educational level may stem from variations in curricular demands. In addition to educational level, teaching type also contributes to variations in intern nursing students' AR. We observed that students in the ‘a single nurse instructing only one student’ group had higher median AR scores than those in the ‘a single nurse instructing multiple students’ group. A one‐to‐one teaching model may allow students to receive sufficient guidance and support, which is beneficial for improving academic self‐efficacy (Ma et al. 2023), thereby alleviating burnout and academic pressure (Bulfone et al. 2022). However, given the extremely imbalanced sample sizes between these subgroups, this finding should be interpreted with caution. It remains preliminary and requires further validation in larger, more balanced samples before any definitive conclusions can be drawn. As for job burnout, students who often experienced burnout had significantly lower AR scores than those who experienced burnout sometimes or never. Job burnout could impair cognitive performance and physical health (Chalak et al. 2023). Since good physical and mental health is a strong predictor of AR among nursing students (Bushehri et al. 2026), frequent burnout may weaken AR by depleting these essential health resources. Furthermore, students who planned to work in nursing had significantly higher AR scores than those who were undecided and those who planned to pursue postgraduate studies in nursing. Some nursing undergraduates choose to pursue postgraduate studies because they perceive the intense work pressure and psychological burden of clinical practice, using postgraduate study as a way to escape clinical work and delay employment (Wang et al. 2024). Such avoidance‐oriented motivation may reflect an existing high level of stress. Given that elevated stress is known to negatively affect resilience (Abdelrahman et al. 2025), these students may be vulnerable to diminished AR. Similarly, students without clear career plans also show lower AR. Research has found a positive correlation between resilience and career commitment (Lin et al. 2025), suggesting that students with firm career plans may have higher levels of resilience than those who are undecided.

Based on these findings, several strategies warrant consideration for clinical nursing education. Nursing managers could consider optimizing clinical teaching models. Periodic job burnout screenings could be implemented during clinical practice, with psychological counselling provided for students experiencing high‐frequency burnout. Career awareness programs and employment guidance could also be organized to improve students' understanding of the nursing profession and assist them in developing concrete career plans (Wei et al. 2021).

Correlations Among NSATN, PI and AR

Our study found that positive attitudes of intern nursing students towards nurses were associated with higher levels of PI, which is consistent with Arreciado's view (Arreciado Marañón and Isla Pera 2015). Positive teaching interactions, good workplace culture, teamwork and professional role modelling promote learning. Positive attitudes of intern nursing students towards nurses contribute to clinical learning, professional development and shaping professional identity (Aragaw et al. 2019; Kaplan et al. 2024; Tuomikoski et al. 2020).

Additionally, positive attitudes towards nurses were associated with higher levels of AR. This finding aligns with Abou Hashish's view, who noted that clinical instructors serve as key contributors to the development of nursing students' AR (Abou Hashish et al. 2024). A possible explanation is that positive interactions with nurses may reduce clinical stress and provide immediate emotional support, thereby enhancing students' capacity to bounce back from academic challenges. According to Self‐Determination Theory (SDT), the satisfaction of three basic psychological needs, including autonomy (being the origin of one's behaviour), competence (feeling effective) and relatedness (feeling connected with others), fosters intrinsic motivation (Deci et al. 1994; Deci and Ryan 2008; Morsink et al. 2022). We speculate that within our research context, positive attitudes towards nurses may enhance AR by supporting students' autonomy (e.g., inviting students to propose observations or ask questions about patient care (Almarwani et al. 2024)), competence (e.g., delegating tasks appropriately (Hartnett 2015)) and relatedness needs (e.g., establishing supportive nurse‐student relationships (Mattila et al. 2010)). The fulfilment of these needs can stimulate intrinsic learning motivation, thereby enhancing problem‐solving abilities and adaptive coping strategies and ultimately improving AR (Shengyao et al. 2024).

Our study also found that PI was positively associated with AR among intern nursing students. Zou pointed out that professional identity prompts nursing students to actively engage in learning, set goals and develop resilience in the face of academic setbacks (Hunsu et al. 2023; Zou et al. 2024). Accordingly, nursing curricula could reinforce PI education. Clinical managers could consider enhancing the clinical environment, offering targeted practice opportunities for low PI students and optimizing communication between nurses and intern nursing students (Zeng et al. 2022).

The Mediating Role of PI

Our cross‐sectional findings indicate that PI serves as a statistical mediator in the association between NSATN and AR, with the indirect association accounting for 60.19% of the total association. NSATN reflects intern nursing students' attitudes and perceptions towards nurses, which operate as an internal cognitive resource. A positive attitude of intern nursing students towards nurses tends to promote the formation of PI (Kaplan et al. 2024; Tuomikoski et al. 2020). From a COR perspective (Hobfoll 2011), this process may represent a transformation from situational cognitive resources to a more stable, enduring psychological resource: professional identity. This resource consolidation process is crucial because PI, being more enduring than situational attitudes (Johnson et al. 2012; Liu 2009), can continue to support intern nursing students across different clinical settings and over time. Thus, PI may serve as a reservoir of resources that intern nursing students can draw upon when facing academic adversity. Acting as an intrinsic motivator for individual career development (Sun et al. 2016), PI makes students with high levels of PI more likely to view academic challenges as meaningful components of their profession rather than as threats. This positive cognition in turn fosters coping behaviours, such as seeking help, adjusting study strategies and persisting in the face of setbacks, which ultimately support their AR.

It should be noted that the above mechanistic interpretation is theoretically derived and based on cross‐sectional associational evidence; the temporal sequence and causal direction of this pathway require further verification in longitudinal study designs.

Nursing Practice Implications

The findings of this study offer several practical implications for nursing education, clinical practice and workforce development.

For nursing education policy, policymakers may consider integrating professional identity development into clinical internship guidelines. At the clinical practice level, initiatives that highlight the professional value of nursing and foster a positive clinical learning climate, such as sharing sessions of exemplary nursing practice and nurse role‐modelling activities, may help students form positive attitudes towards the nursing profession and nurses, which may in turn support the development of professional identity. Regular experience‐sharing sessions focused on PI development may also help students internalize psychological resources. In addition, enhancing peer and clinical staff support, alongside regular discussions of clinical dilemmas, may help students build adaptive coping capacities. At the educational level, integrating reflective learning and mindfulness strategies into the nursing curriculum may be beneficial (Walsh et al. 2020). Furthermore, clinical mentorship programs could emphasize nurses' roles as key facilitators of students' professional development and promote respectful, constructive supervisory feedback to support students' professional growth. These implications underscore the need for multi‐level interventions to cultivate PI and AR among intern nursing students, ultimately contributing to workforce retention and professional sustainability.

However, given the cross‐sectional nature of our data, these implications should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating rather than confirmed effective interventions. Future longitudinal or intervention studies are needed to test whether these strategies can effectively improve AR among intern nursing students.

Conclusion

This study found that PI serves as a statistical mediator in the cross‐sectional association between intern nursing students' attitudes towards nurses and academic resilience. Chinese intern nursing students' attitudes towards nurses are positively associated with academic resilience. These findings highlight the importance of fostering both positive attitudes towards nurses and professional identity development to promote academic resilience in clinical internship settings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The single‐center design and relatively small sample size limit the generalizability of the findings; caution is advised when applying these results to other populations or different clinical contexts. Additionally, all data were collected through self‐reported questionnaires, which may introduce potential bias, such as social desirability bias or recall bias. The cross‐sectional design precludes causal inferences and cannot capture dynamic changes in variables over time. The subgroup sample sizes for teaching type were severely imbalanced, which rendered inferential comparisons involving this variable statistically underpowered and potentially non‐representative. Therefore, we excluded teaching type from the primary adjusted mediation model to avoid unstable estimates. The related unadjusted findings reported in Table 1 should be regarded as exploratory rather than conclusive.

Future multi‐center, longitudinal studies with larger samples are needed to validate these findings and explore causal relationships across diverse settings. It is worth noting that while our study focused on the mediating role of PI, other possible mediators such as self‐efficacy and clinical belongingness were not examined. Future research could explore these alternative pathways to gain a more comprehensive understanding of how NSATN shapes AR and to identify more targeted intervention points.
