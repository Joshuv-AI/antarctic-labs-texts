Determinants of surgical management outcomes among adult patients with intestinal obstruction at Mzuzu central hospital, Malawi
Authors: Dickson Mwenitete, Chank Mwalweni, Violet Moyo, Frank Banda, Sope Msuku, Patrick Mapulanga, Judith Mkwaila, Rodwell Gundo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIntestinal obstruction causes significant morbidity and mortality worldwide, particularly in low-resource settings. Understanding factors influencing surgical outcomes is crucial for improving patient care. This study aimed to assess the determinants of surgical management outcomes among adult patients with intestinal obstruction at Mzuzu Central Hospital, Malawi.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study included 262 adult patients diagnosed with intestinal obstruction between July 2021 and June 2024. Data were collected through patient chart reviews, focusing on demographic characteristics, care- and disease-related factors, and outcomes of surgical management (death and discharge). Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 22. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise patient characteristics, and multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify predictors of surgical outcomes, with statistical significance set at p ≤ 0.05.ResultsThe mean age of the patients was 46.6 ± 19.2 years (range, 15–94 years). The overall mortality rate was 39(14.9%), whereas 223(85.1%) were successfully discharged, and 30(16.2%) developed wound infections. The average duration of illness before admission was 5.0 ± 7.5 days, and the average hospital stay was 10.6 ± 9.3 days. Most patients, 138(52.7%), presented with small bowel obstruction, predominantly due to adhesions, 63(24.0%) and viable small intestinal volvulus, 24(9.3%). Among patients with large bowel obstruction, the leading causes were viable sigmoid volvulus, 39(14.1%) and gangrenous sigmoid volvulus, 28(10.7%). In the bivariate analysis, variables such as age, ASA score, malaria test results, haemoglobin levels, preoperative sepsis, and abdominal distension had p-values < 0.25 and were therefore included in the multivariable logistic regression model. After adjustment, only ASA score ≥ III [AOR = 5.05, 95% CI: 1.86–13.60; p < 0.001] and abdominal distension [AOR = 2.36, 95% CI: 0.99–5.61; p = 0.050] remained independently associated with unfavourable surgical outcomes.ConclusionAn ASA score ≥ III and clinical manifestation of abdominal distension are key determinants of unfavourable surgical management outcomes in patients with intestinal obstruction at Mzuzu Central Hospital. Strengthening perioperative assessment and timely surgical intervention are recommended to enhance the patient outcomes.Keywords: Determinants, Adults, Bowel obstruction, Surgical management, Outcomes, Mzuzu central hospital

Introduction

Intestinal obstruction is an emergency surgical disease characterised by blockage of the intestinal tract, which impedes the normal passage of digestive contents [1]. This condition can occur in either the small or large intestine and may be classified as partial or complete, depending on the severity of the blockage. The causes of intestinal obstruction are diverse, ranging from post-surgical adhesions and tumours to hernias and inflammatory conditions such as Crohn’s disease [2]. Blockage leads to failure of distal progress of intestinal contents, increased intraluminal pressure, and compromised bowel circulation which, if left untreated, can result in ischaemia, ischaemic necrosis, and perforation [1].

Intestinal obstruction is a common and significant health problem worldwide, accounting for a substantial proportion of emergency surgical admissions in our hospital. In a global report by the World Health Organization, approximately 3.2 million cases of bowel obstruction occurred in 2015, resulting in 264,000 deaths [3]. In most African countries, it accounts for a significant proportion of morbidity and mortality, which varies by region [3]. Small intestinal obstructions are more prevalent than large intestinal obstructions, with adhesions from previous surgeries being the leading cause globally, accounting for approximately 60%–75% of cases [4]. Mortality rates associated with intestinal obstruction range from 2% to 30%, influenced by factors such as the patient’s age, comorbidities, and the presence of complications like ischaemia or sepsis [3, 5, 6].

In high-income regions, such as North America and Europe, intestinal obstruction is frequently managed in well-resourced healthcare settings, where advanced imaging techniques and surgical interventions are readily available. In these regions, postoperative adhesions remain the leading cause of intestinal obstruction, particularly after colorectal surgeries [7]. In contrast, in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), the burden of intestinal obstruction is exacerbated by limited access to healthcare services, delayed diagnosis, and the prevalence of different etiological factors, such as infectious diseases, including tuberculosis, which can cause strictures leading to obstruction [6].

Various treatment options are available for intestinal obstruction, depending on the cause and severity of the blockage. Some cases necessitate surgical intervention, whereas others can be managed conservatively only. Conservative management includes a low-fibre diet, enemas, tube deflation, decompression, and the use of self-expanding metal stents. Surgical approaches may involve minimally invasive laparoscopic surgery or more complex open surgical procedures to address obstruction [8].

Intestinal obstruction is a significant cause of surgical admissions and is associated with considerable mortality. Various factors influence the outcomes of intestinal obstruction, including patient characteristics, care-related factors, and disease-specific variables. A study from Ethiopia identified age, preoperative sepsis, American Society of Anaesthesiologists (ASA) scores of III or higher, and the use of intraoperative vasopressors as key factors associated with mortality in patients undergoing emergency laparotomy [9]. Additionally, the absence of fever, shorter duration of illness before surgery, and viable intraoperative intestinal conditions were linked to favourable surgical outcomes in intestinal obstruction cases [8].

However, limited research has been conducted on the surgical management of intestinal obstruction in Malawi. Notably, Yohann et al. [10] assessed the epidemiology, outcomes, and preoperative capacity of acute care surgery and found that mortality rates decreased when patients received the necessary surgical care. Another study found a significant relationship between age and aetiology. As the age of patients increased, they were more likely to have functional causes of intestinal obstruction, including peritonitis, ileus, perforation, Hirschsprung’s disease, or appendicitis, compared to mechanical causes such as faecal impaction, hernia, adhesions, gastric outlet obstruction, or intussusception [11]. Curiously, Shah [11] investigated paediatric intestinal obstruction, while Yohann et al. [10] concentrated largely on the epidemiological aspect with little emphasis on outcomes. Another study reported that Sigmoid Volvulus (SV) is a common cause of acute bowel obstruction in Malawi [12]. However, this study did not consider the factors associated with these findings.

Overall, there is limited literature in Malawi addressing the determinants of surgical management outcomes for intestinal obstruction. Moreover, no study has investigated this issue at Mzuzu Central Hospital. Therefore, this study aimed to address this gap by examining the outcomes of surgical management and their determinants among adult patients with intestinal obstruction at the Mzuzu Central Hospital.

Methods

Study design

A retrospective, cross-sectional study of patients diagnosed with and treated for intestinal obstruction was conducted over a three-month period from March to May 2025 using existing medical records.

Study setting

This study was conducted at the Mzuzu Central Hospital, a public tertiary referral facility in Mzuzu City, northern Malawi. The hospital has a capacity of 410 beds and serves as the main referral center for six health districts, covering an estimated population of 2,289,780 [13, 14]. In addition to referrals from public district hospitals, it also receives patients from private and mission hospitals. Some patients bypass the referral system altogether and present directly to the hospital as walk-in cases.

The Surgery Department is a core clinical specialty department at Mzuzu Central Hospital. It provides both emergency and elective non-traumatic general surgical operations, paediatric surgeries, trauma and burn care, urological services, and endoscopic procedures. The department also operates a 24-hour emergency service, conducts ward consultations, and holds biweekly specialised surgical clinics. There are two main adult admission wards, namely, the male and female surgical wards, with a combined bed capacity of 68. The department is staffed by two full-time surgeons, two medical officers, and five clinical officers. In addition, three part-time general surgeons contribute to partnerships with Kamuzu University of Health Sciences, the University of Cincinnati, and the Chinese medical team. In terms of nursing services, the department includes one chief nursing officer, two principal nursing officers, one senior nursing officer, nine nursing officers, thirty-four nurse midwife technicians, 11 hospital attendants, and two ward clerks (Mzuzu Central Hospital Strategic Plan 2023–2026). The department also serves as a clinical training site for various health institutions.

The surgical department is complemented by radiology and laboratory services that offer advanced imaging modalities and a comprehensive range of diagnostic investigations. Nonetheless, recurrent equipment malfunctions in radiology and persistent reagent shortages in biochemistry undermine the consistency, reliability, and overall effectiveness of these essential services at the facility.

Study population

The study population consisted of all cases of intestinal obstruction at Mzuzu Central Hospital from 1 July 2021 to 30 June 2024.

Inclusion criteria

This study included patients admitted to the surgical ward at Mzuzu Central Hospital with a diagnosis of intestinal obstruction. Data were retrospectively extracted from their case files, and patients may have undergone either laparotomy or non-operative (conservative) management during admission.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with incomplete clinical information necessary for analysis were excluded, specifically those with missing data on the underlying cause of intestinal obstruction, type of management (surgical or non-surgical), and outcomes such as discharge or death.

Sample size determination

A census of all 328 adult patients diagnosed with intestinal obstruction at Mzuzu Central Hospital during the study period was conducted. After applying the inclusion criteria, 262 patient records were included in the study.

Sampling technique

The study used purposive total population sampling by systematically including all eligible adult patient case files diagnosed with intestinal obstruction during the study period.

Data collection tool

Evidence from existing national guidelines in Malawi was used to develop a data extraction sheet, supplemented where necessary by World Health Organization guidelines [15] and relevant literature on the surgical management of intestinal obstruction [8, 9]. A multidisciplinary team comprising a surgeon, clinical officer, anaesthetist, radiographer, and four nursing officers developed the data extraction tool. The tool was pre-tested on 5% of the sample size using the case files of patients diagnosed with intestinal obstruction outside the study period (July 2021 to June 2024) at Mzuzu Central Hospital. The pre-test allowed the investigators to refine variables that were difficult to extract data from. For example, the original variable ‘surgeon’s experience’ had no available data. After it was revised to ‘surgeon’s title,’ the data became accessible. Data were extracted by a trained data extraction team through a systematic review of each eligible case file guided by the finalised data extraction sheet.

Data collection

Following the development and pre-testing of the data extraction sheet, data collection was conducted by a multidisciplinary team comprising one surgeon, an anaesthetist, one radiographer, one clinical officer, and four nursing officers. Prior to data collection, the team underwent two days of training focused on standardised procedures for data extraction using the developed tool. The trained team systematically reviewed the case notes of patients diagnosed with intestinal obstruction between 1 July 2021 and 30 June 2024 at Mzuzu Central Hospital. Relevant information was extracted and recorded using a finalised data collection sheet.

Study variables

The dependent (outcome) variable in this study was the surgical outcome of intestinal obstruction (death or discharge). The independent (explanatory) variables included sociodemographic characteristics such as age, sex, education level, and economic status; care-related factors such as duration of illness, vital signs, laboratory investigations, imaging modalities, antibiotic administration, and other preoperative management practices; type of intervention; surgeon’s title; time from admission to operation; use of the WHO surgical checklist; type of intraoperative procedure; intraoperative blood transfusion; duration of surgery; and disease-related factors including the type and causes of bowel obstruction, comorbidities, presence of preoperative sepsis, and clinical presentation. These independent variables were assessed to determine whether they were associated with surgical outcomes of intestinal obstruction management.

Operational definitions

Comorbidity: The presence of one or more additional diseases or medical conditions occurring alongside a primary condition in a patient [16].

Surgical management: Operative and non-operative treatments provided to patients with bowel obstruction.

Surgical management outcome: The result or consequence of surgical treatment, often measured in terms of recovery, complication rates, mortality, and morbidity [8].

Normal Vital Signs: Standard measurements that reflect the basic functions of the body, typically including: Temperature: 35.5–37.5 °C; Pulse (Heart Rate): 60–100 beats/min; Respiratory Rate: 12–20 breaths/min; Systolic Blood Pressure: approximately 100–139 mmHg; Diastolic Blood Pressure: approximately 60–89 mmHg; Oxygen Saturation: 94–100% on room air [17, 18].

Sepsis is defined as a systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) in the presence of a suspected or confirmed source of infection, characterised by two or more of the following clinical signs: pulse rate greater than 90 beats per minute, respiratory rate greater than 20 breaths per minute, body temperature less than 35.5 °C or greater than 37.5 °C (or a history of fever), and white blood cell count less than 4,000/mm³ or greater than 12,000/mm³.

WHO surgical safety checklist: A tool developed by the World Health Organization to improve the safety of surgical procedures. It is a simple checklist used by surgical teams to confirm critical safety steps before, during, and after surgery to reduce complications and mortality [15].

ASA grade: A system used to assess and communicate a patient’s preoperative physical fitness and risk before surgery [19, 20]. The ASA grades are as follows: ASA I, a normal healthy patient; ASA II, a patient with mild systemic disease; ASA III, a patient with severe systemic disease; ASA IV, a patient with severe systemic disease that is a constant threat to life; ASA V, a moribund patient who is not expected to survive without the operation; and ASA VI, a declared brain-dead patient whose organs are being removed for donor purposes.

Clavien-Dindo classification: A widely used system to grade the severity of surgical complications based on the required intervention [21]: Grade I: Any deviation from the normal postoperative course without the need for pharmacological treatment or surgical, endoscopic, or radiological interventions; Grade II: Complications requiring pharmacological treatment (e.g. antibiotics, blood transfusion); Grade III: Complications requiring surgical, endoscopic, or radiological intervention: IIIa: Without general anaesthesia and IIIb: Under general anaesthesia; Grade IV: Life-threatening complications requiring intensive care management: IVa: Single organ dysfunction; IVb: Multi-organ dysfunction; and Grade V: Death of the patient.

Quality control

The research team provided comprehensive training to the abstractors before the data collection commenced. All information was accurately recorded using a standardised data collection tool. The principal investigator, along with other team members, supervised the data collectors and performed daily reviews to ensure the completeness and accuracy of the collected data.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were analysed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 22. Frequencies, percentages, and descriptive summaries were used to describe the variables. Bivariate logistic regression was performed to estimate the unadjusted (crude) odds ratios (CORs) for each predictor. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals were reported for variables with p-values ≤ 0.05.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the College of Medicine Research and Ethics Committee (COMREC) under protocol number P.11/24–1244. In addition, an institutional support letter was obtained from the Director General of Mzuzu Central Hospital. The study involved a retrospective review of medical case files and did not include direct interactions with patients. Therefore, individual informed consent was not obtained from the participants. Instead, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee following the national research ethics guidelines, which permit the use of anonymised medical records for research purposes under approved protocols. To ensure confidentiality and protect participants’ privacy, all identifying information, including names, was removed from the case files before data collection. Data clerks were instructed to photocopy only eligible case files after thoroughly concealing any personal information.

Factors associated with surgical management outcomes among adult patients with intestinal obstruction (July 2021 – June 2024)

Bivariate and multivariate logistic regressions were performed to assess the association between independent variables and surgical management outcomes. All variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were included in the multivariate model after checking for multicollinearity to identify independent associations. The multivariate analysis revealed that an ASA score of ≥ III and the presence of abdominal distension were significantly associated with unfavourable surgical outcomes. Patients with ASA scores ≥ III had five times higher odds of experiencing an unfavourable outcome compared to those with ASA scores of I or II [AOR = 5.05; 95% CI: 1.86–13.60; p < 0.001]. Additionally, patients who presented with abdominal distension had more than twice the odds of an unfavourable outcome compared to their counterparts [AOR = 2.36; 95% CI: 0.99–5.61; p = 0.050]. Discussion

This study examined the determinants of surgical management outcomes in adult patients with intestinal obstruction at Mzuzu Central Hospital in Malawi. A total of 262 cases managed between July 2021 and June 2024 were analysed. These findings provide critical insights into the clinical and systemic factors influencing outcomes in a resource-limited tertiary care setting. An ASA score ≥ III and the presence of abdominal distension were significantly associated with unfavourable outcomes, emphasizing the prognostic value of preoperative clinical status and intra-abdominal disease severity in guiding surgical decision-making.

The demographic characteristics of our study population including age, sex, residence, and occupation closely align with findings from other studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa [22–24]. The mean age of participants was 46.6 ± 19.2 years, and 67.9% were male, consistent with reports from Ethiopia, Malawi, and Zambia, where middle-aged men constituted many patients with intestinal obstruction [22, 23, 25]. This trend is often attributed to gender-related occupational risks and delayed health-seeking behaviour among men. The predominance of rural patients (78.6%), 59.5% of whom were farmers, also mirrors findings from Malawi, Zambia, and Ethiopia, highlighting the influence of socioeconomic and occupational factors on surgical outcomes [22, 24]. Farming involves significant physical exertion, predisposing individuals to hernias and bowel obstructions. Moreover, a high-fibre diet typical of rural communities has been associated with sigmoid volvulus [26], which was likewise the most common cause of large bowel obstruction in this study, accounting for 14.1% of cases.

Small bowel obstruction (SBO) was more prevalent than large bowel obstruction (LBO), comprising 56% of the cases. Adhesions, often resulting from prior abdominal surgeries, were the leading cause of SBO (24.0%), consistent with findings from other low- and middle-income countries [27–29]. Nevertheless, these results are inconsistent with reports from other African countries, particularly Ethiopia, where small bowel volvulus predominates as the main cause of SBO [30]. This highlights the need for meticulous surgical techniques and postoperative care to reduce adhesion formation. Sigmoid volvulus, the main cause of LBO, remains a significant public health issue in Malawi and sub-Saharan Africa due to factors such as chronic constipation, high-fibre diets, low socioeconomic status, and delayed presentation [10, 12, 25, 29]. Public health efforts emphasising dietary education and early symptom recognition are critical for preventing the progression to full obstruction.

Surgical findings revealed that resection and anastomosis were the most frequently performed intraoperative procedures (45.4%), followed by adhesiolysis and band release (34.1%). Similar trends have been reported in other low- and middle-income countries, such as Ethiopia, where resection and anastomosis accounted for 70.4% of cases [8], and India, where resection and anastomosis, and adhesiolysis were observed in 44% and 38% of cases, respectively [31]. In contrast, evidence from high-income settings advocates laparoscopy as the preferred approach for managing adhesive small bowel obstruction [32]. However, its limited adoption in resource-constrained environments may be attributed not only to shortages of equipment and trained personnel but also to late presentation, higher rates of complicated obstruction, and financial constraints [33–35].

Systemic barriers to timely surgical care were evident in the patients’ presentation patterns. On average, patients presented after 5.0 ± 7.5 days of symptoms, with 59.5% referred from other health facilities, highlighting the limited surgical capacity at the primary and secondary care levels [23, 36]. Although this study did not find a statistically significant association between the duration of illness and surgical outcomes, delayed presentation is widely recognised as a contributor to bowel ischaemia, perforation, and sepsis, all of which increase surgical risk and mortality [37, 38].

The postoperative mortality rate was 13.5%, substantially higher than the rates reported in Malawi’s district hospitals (0.53% to 0.9%) [10, 23] and Ethiopia (8.1% to 11.25%) [8, 9], but comparable to figures from India (14%) and the United Kingdom (14.3%) [5, 39]. However, it remains lower than the rates reported at Kamuzu Central Hospital in Malawi (18.7%) and hospitals in Cameroon (19.8%) [36, 40]. These differences likely reflect variations in case severity, resource availability, and the timeliness of surgical intervention, as central hospitals generally receive more severe and referred cases than district hospitals.

A high ASA score was a strong predictor of unfavourable surgical outcomes. Patients with ASA scores ≥ III were five times more likely to experience unfavourable outcomes than those with lower scores. This corroborates evidence from Malawi, Zambia, Kenya, and Nigeria linking higher ASA classification to increased postoperative infections and mortality [23, 41, 42]. Higher ASA scores often reflect comorbidities such as diabetes and hypertension, which impair physiological resilience, reduce the capacity to tolerate surgical stress, and increase perioperative risks [43, 44]. Therefore, the consistent use of the ASA classification is vital for risk stratification and clinical decision-making [45–47].

Similarly, abdominal distension doubled the mortality risk. This clinical sign often indicates advanced intra-abdominal pathology, such as volvulus or peritonitis, and is linked to approximately 18% of surgical deaths in Malawi [45]. Its inclusion in early risk assessments may facilitate the timely identification of high-risk patients [12, 40].

The diagnostic and monitoring capacities were limited. Full blood counts were performed in 85.9% of patients, with 29.4% showing elevated white cell counts suggestive of infection. However, liver function tests (14.9%) and urea/electrolytes (20.2%) were underutilised, limiting the assessment of fluid status, renal and hepatic function, and early sepsis detection [8, 9]. Vital sign monitoring was inconsistent; blood pressure was recorded in 85.4% of cases, but temperature and oxygen saturation were documented in only 25.2% and 13.7% of cases, respectively. These deficiencies mirror the common challenges in LMICs, where resource and staffing constraints impede routine clinical monitoring [10, 18].

Imaging primarily involves abdominal X-rays (36.3%) and ultrasounds (40.1%), which are affordable and accessible modalities with limited diagnostic accuracy for complex cases [48, 49]. Only 9.9% of patients underwent CT scans, despite their superior accuracy in defining the cause and severity of obstruction [50, 51]. The lack of access to CT likely contributed to diagnostic uncertainty, delayed surgical planning, and poorer outcomes, underscoring the need to equip central hospitals with advanced imaging capabilities.

Postoperative complications were common. Wound infection occurred in 16.2%, re-laparotomy in 13.0%, and anastomotic leak in 10.8% of the patients. While wound infection rates were lower than those reported in some Indian studies (13.5%–38%) [52–54], they exceeded the Ethiopian rate (3.7%) [9]. The relaparotomy rate aligns with other resource-limited settings [55] but surpasses rates typical of high-income countries (< 10%) [56]. The anastomotic leak rate was comparable to that of Egypt and Ethiopia (8%–24%) [55–58] but higher than the < 5% reported in high-income settings [59], reflecting differences in perioperative care quality, nutrition, and infection control. These findings highlight the need to strengthen the surgical systems, perioperative protocols, and postoperative monitoring.

The underutilisation of the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist is concerning, as it was applied in only 31.4% of surgeries compared to 57.5%–67.6% reported in other LMICs [60, 61]. Given its proven efficacy in reducing perioperative morbidity and mortality [61, 62], improving checklist adherence represents a low-cost, high-impact strategy for enhancing surgical outcomes [8, 60].

Although sepsis was not statistically associated with outcomes in this study, 18% of patients presented with preoperative sepsis, a known risk factor for mortality and prolonged hospitalisation [8, 9]. This underscores the need for robust infection control, timely antibiotic administration and enhanced clinical surveillance.

The current study reports an average length of hospital stay (LOS) of 10.6 ± 9.3 days for surgical patients. Similar findings were reported in a study conducted in Ethiopia, where the length of stay of patients ranged between 2% and 14% [9, 63]. However, this contrasts with a study from Singapore, which reported a significantly longer average LOS of 24.6 days [64]. The difference may be explained by variations in healthcare systems, where high patient volume and limited resources in our setting could lead to quicker patient turnover and earlier discharge.

Study limitations

The retrospective design of this study relied on existing case records, limiting our ability to verify or clarify incomplete data and potentially introducing information bias. Additionally, the absence of data on patients’ HIV status, an important factor given its high prevalence in Malawi and potential impact on surgical outcomes, represents a significant limitation of this study. Furthermore, the study did not ascertain the patients’ history of prior abdominal surgery, which could influence the aetiology and management of intestinal obstruction. Despite these limitations, our findings provide valuable insights into surgical outcomes in this setting. Future research using prospective cohort designs with comprehensive data collection is recommended to address these gaps and strengthen the evidence.

Conclusion

This study identified abdominal distension and ASA classification of ≥ III as key determinants of unfavourable outcomes in patients with intestinal obstruction. Although full blood counts were adequately performed, essential laboratory tests, such as liver function tests and urea and electrolytes, were underutilised, limiting comprehensive patient assessment. Furthermore, the low utilisation of computed tomography scans, which offer superior diagnostic accuracy, likely contributed to the diagnostic challenges and delayed management. Addressing these gaps through enhanced diagnostic capacity, thorough preoperative evaluation, and timely intervention is essential for improving surgical outcomes in resource-limited settings.
