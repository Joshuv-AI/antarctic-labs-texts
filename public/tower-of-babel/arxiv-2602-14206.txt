Kernel estimation of Chatterjee’s dependence coefficient
Mona Azadkia, Holger Dette

Abstract
. 6 introduced a copula-based measure of dependence, which implies independence if it vanishes and is equal to 1 1 if one variable is a measurable function of the other. For continuous distributions, the dependence measure also appears as stochastic limit of Chatterjee’s rank correlation ( 4 ) . They proved asymptotic normality of a corresponding kernel estimator with a parametric rate of convergence. In recent work 12 revealed empirically and theoretically that under independence the asymptotic variance degenerates. In this note, we derive the correct asymptotic distribution of the kernel estimator under the null hypothesis of independence. We show that after a suitable centering and rescaling at a rate larger than n \sqrt{n} (where n n is the sample size), the estimator is asymptotically normal. The analysis relies on a refined central limit theorem for double-indexed linear permutation statistics and accounts for boundary effects that are asymptotically non-negligible. As a consequence, we obtain a valid basis for independence testing without relying on permutations and argue that tests based on the kernel estimator detect local alternatives converging to the null at a faster rate than those detectable by Chatterjee’s rank correlation.



Keywords and Phrases: Dependence measure; Independence testing; Copulae.

1.
Introduction

A fundamental problem in mathematical statistics is the quantification of the
dependence between two real-valued random variables
X
and
Y
based on a sample
of independent and identically distributed observations
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
.
Classical dependence measures such as Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlation,
Kendall’s
\tau
and Gini’s
\gamma
take values in
[0,1]
or
[-1,1]
and vanish
under independence; moreover, they are highly sensitive to linear and monotone
relationships. However, it is well known that these measures may fail to detect
non-monotone forms of dependence, even in situations where
Y=f(X)
for some
deterministic function
f
. In addition, a vanishing classical dependence measure
typically only implies independence under restrictive structural assumptions, such
as joint normality.

Motivated by these limitations, numerous alternative dependence measures have been
proposed in the literature mainly for testing the hypothesis of
independence (see
3
;
11
;
5
for some early references and

13
;
7
;
2
;
14
for
more recent
approaches relying on distance- and kernel-based methods). As
pointed out by
4
, these measures are less suited for measuring the strength of the dependence.

In recent years there has been considerable interest in dependence measures
\mu=\mu(X,Y)
for two (real-valued) random variables
X
and
Y
satisfying
the following
axioms:

(1.1)

0\leq\mu(X,Y)\leq 1
,

(1.2)

\mu(X,Y)=0
if and only if
X
and
Y
are independent,

(1.3)

\mu(X,Y)=1
if and only if
Y=f(X)
for some measurable function

f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

An early work in this direction is the paper by
6
, who defined, for distributions with continuous marginals, the association measure

(1.1)

\displaystyle r=r(X,Y)=6\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\big(\partial_{1}C(u,v)\big)^{2}dudv-2

which
12
called the
Dette-Siburg-Stoimenov correlation coefficient
. Here
C
denotes the copula of the vector
(X,Y)
and
\partial_{1}C
its partial derivative with respect to the first coordinate. In the same paper the authors proposed a kernel-type estimator (see Section
2
for details) for which they proved asymptotic normality with a
\sqrt{n}
rate, where
n
denotes the sample size.
4
considered the measure

(1.2)

\displaystyle\xi=\xi(X,Y)=\frac{\int\text{Var}(\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}(Y\geq y)\mid X])dP_{Y}(t)}{\int\text{Var}(\mathbbm{1}(Y\geq y))dP_{Y}(y)},

meanwhile known as
Chatterjee’s dependence coefficient
. It turns out that for continuous distributions the two measures (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) actually coincide. An estimator of (
1.2
) known as
Chatterjee’s rank correlation
converges weakly to a normal distribution with a
\sqrt{n}
-rate as well (see
4
,
10
and
9
).
12
investigated the power properties of tests for independence based on both estimators by asymptotic theory and by means of a simulation study. In particular they pointed out a sign error in the asymptotic analysis
of the kernel type estimator by
6
, which shows that their estimator scaled with
\sqrt{n}
converges in fact to a constant if
X
and
Y
are independent. These observations indicate that under independence the estimator converges weakly to a non-degenerate limiting distribution with a rate larger than
\sqrt{n}
.
In this note we confirm this conjecture, explicitly determine this rate and prove that under appropriate scaling the kernel type estimator of
6
converges weakly to a normal distribution. Our sophisticated analysis actually relies on an appropriate centering term appearing in a central limit theorem for double-indexed linear permutation statistics.

From a statistical point of view our results indicate that for independence testing the kernel type estimator of
6
is more efficient than Chatterjee’s rank correlation with respect to local alternatives, which confirms the empirical finding of
12
. More, specifically, while a test based on Chatterjee’s correlation can detect local alternatives converging to the null at a rate
n^{-1/4}
, the test based on the kernel-type estimator of
6
is sensitive with respect to local alternatives converging to the null at a rate
(n/h_{1})^{-1/4}
, where
h_{1}
denotes a bandwidth converging to
0
at a rate slower than
n^{-1/2}
.

2.
A kernel type estimator and its asymptotic distribution

For a sample
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
of independent identically distributed
random variables with distribution function
F
and copula
C
,
6

proposed to estimate the partial derivative

\tau(u,v)={\partial}_{1}C(u,v)=\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\ C(u,v)
of the copula by the statistic

(2.1)

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{n}(u,v)=\frac{1}{nh_{1}}\sum^{n}_{i=1}K\Bigl(\frac{u-\hat{F}_{n1}(X_{i})}{h_{1}}\Bigr)\bar{K}\Bigl(\frac{v-\hat{F}_{n2}(Y_{i})}{h_{2}}\Bigr).

Here
K
denotes a symmetric kernel supported on the interval
[-1,1]
, with corresponding cumulative distribution function

\bar{K}(x)=\int^{x}_{-\infty}K(t)\ dt,

h_{1},h_{2}
are bandwidths converging to
0
with increasing sample
size
and
\hat{F}_{n1}

and
\hat{F}_{n2}
denote the empirical distribution functions of
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
and

Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
, respectively. It can be shown that (under appropriate assumptions)
\hat{\tau}_{n}
is a consistent estimator of
\partial_{1}C
and therefore
6
defined the estimator of the dependence measure (
1.1
) by

(2.2)

\displaystyle\hat{r}_{n}=6\hat{\tau}^{2}_{n}-2,

where

(2.3)

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}^{2}_{n}=\int^{1}_{0}\int^{1}_{0}\hat{\tau}_{n}^{2}(u,v)\ dudv,

is the estimator
of the integral

\tau^{2}=\int^{1}_{0}\int^{1}_{0}(\partial_{1}\ C(u,v))^{2}_{2}\ dudv.
Note that under independence, that is
C(u,v)=uv
, we have
\tau^{2}=1/3
, and therefore the coefficient
r
in (
1.1
) vanishes.
In their main statement
6
proved
that an appropriately standardized version of this estimator is asymptotically normal, that is

(2.4)

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\ (\hat{r}_{n}-r)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,144\sigma^{2}),

with an explicit expression for
\sigma^{2}
. As pointed out by
12
, this formula contains
an error due to a wrong sign. Additionally there is a further minor error, which was not identified in the last-named reference, and we give the correct formula for
\sigma^{2}
in Section
4.4
of the appendix. As pointed out by
12
, an important consequence of the completely corrected formula is that the asymptotic variance in the case of independence, where
C(u,v)=uv
vanishes. In other words, in the case of independence the limiting (normal) distribution in (
2.4
) is degenerate. This raises the question, if it possible to derive a weak convergence result in this case with a different scaling and a non-degenerate limiting distribution. In the following result we give an affirmative answer to this question.

Theorem 2.1
.

For continuous and independent
X
and
Y
, symmetric kernel
K
supported on the interval
[-1,1]
and bandwidths
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
such that

nh_{1}^{2}\rightarrow\infty,\quad nh_{1}^{4}\rightarrow 0,\quad nh_{2}\rightarrow\infty,

we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{n}{h_{1}}}\big(\hat{r}_{n}-b_{n}\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,144\sigma_{0}^{2}),

where

\displaystyle b_{n}:=

\displaystyle\frac{6}{n^{2}h_{1}^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\int_{0}^{1}K\Big(\frac{u-i/n}{h_{1}}\Big)K\Big(\frac{u-j/n}{h_{1}}\Big)du

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~\times\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\int_{0}^{1}\bar{K}\Big(\frac{v-i/n}{h_{2}}\Big)\bar{K}\Big(\frac{v-j/n}{h_{2}}\Big)dv

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~+\frac{6}{n^{2}h_{1}^{2}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}K^{2}\Big(\frac{u-i/n}{h_{1}}\Big)du\Big)\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}\bar{K}^{2}\Big(\frac{v-i/n}{h_{2}}\Big)dv\Big)-2,

and

(2.5)

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}^{2}:=\frac{2}{45}\int_{0}^{1}\Big(1-\int_{-t}^{1}K(v)\bar{K}(v+t)dv\Big)^{2}dt.

Remark 2.2

(Bias)
.

Note that, one may choose
h_{2}
sufficiently small so that

\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\int_{0}^{1}\bar{K}\Big(\frac{v-i/n}{h_{2}}\Big)\bar{K}\Big(\frac{v-j/n}{h_{2}}\Big)dv

can be replaced by its limit
(n-2)/3n
. Hence the expression of
b_{n}
may be replaced by the simpler surrogate

\displaystyle\tilde{b}_{n}:=\frac{2(n-2)}{n^{3}h_{1}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq i}\int K\Big(\frac{u-i/n}{h_{1}}\Big)K\Big(\frac{u-j/n}{h_{1}}\Big)du-2,

since the diagonal terms are of smaller order.
Nevertheless, we retain the original formulation, as this approximation would sacrifice precision, especially for small sample sizes
n
.

Additionally, note that the expression of
b_{n}
cannot be further simplified by replacing the integral

{1\over h_{1}}\int_{0}^{1}K\Big(\frac{u-i/n}{h_{1}}\Big)K\Big(\frac{u-j/n}{h_{1}}\Big)du

by
(K*K)\left((i-j)/(nh_{1})\right)
, where
K*K
denotes the convolution of
K
with itself. This is because boundary effects contribute to the bias at the same order as the interior terms.

Remark 2.3

(Local alternatives and power comparison)
.

The asymptotic distribution in Theorem 2.1 has important implications for the power of tests for independence based on
\hat{r}_{n}
. Under the null hypothesis of independence,
\hat{r}_{n}
admits a central limit theorem with centering
b_{n}
and scaling
\sqrt{n/h_{1}}
, which is strictly faster than the usual
\sqrt{n}
rate. This accelerated rate implies that departures from independence of smaller order can be detected.

To make this precise, consider a sequence of local alternatives under which the population coefficient satisfies

\xi_{n}:=\xi(X,Y)\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

A test based on
\hat{r}_{n}
rejects for large values if

(2.6)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{n}{h_{1}}}(\hat{r}_{n}-b_{n})>12\sigma_{0}u_{1-\alpha},

where
u_{1-\alpha}
denotes the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the standard normal distribution.
Therefore, nontrivial power is achieved whenever

\sqrt{\frac{n}{h_{1}}}\xi_{n}\to c>0,

and the detection boundary for
\hat{r}_{n}
is of order

\xi_{n}\asymp(n/h_{1})^{-1/2},

or, equivalently, as argued in
1
for smooth parametric alternatives such as Gaussian correlations,

\rho_{n}\asymp(n/h_{1})^{-1/4}.

By contrast, a test based on Chatterjee’s rank correlation estimator satisfies a classical
\sqrt{n}
-CLT under independence, and it is known that its optimal detection boundary is
\rho_{n}\asymp n^{-1/4}
.
Since
h_{1}\to 0
, we have
(n/h_{1})^{-1/4}\ll n^{-1/4}
, showing that tests based on
\hat{r}_{n}
are sensitive to alternatives converging to the null at a strictly faster rate.

This comparison is in line with the empirical findings reported in
12
and we also illustrate it by a small simulation study in the following section.
To rigorously establish this detection threshold, it is necessary to analyze the asymptotic behaviour of the statistic under shrinking alternatives, which is not possible by a CLT for double-indexed linear permutation statistics as used in Section
4
for the proof of Theorem
2.1
. In fact such a result could be obtained by a more refined analysis of the arguments in
6
studying the boundary effects of the kernel estimator
\hat{r}_{n}
.

4.
Proofs

To prepare for the proof of Theorem
2.1
, recall that
\hat{r}_{n}=6\hat{\tau}_{n}^{2}-2
. Therefore, it suffices to establish the asymptotic normality of
\hat{\tau}_{n}^{2}
, which is the statement of the following result.

Theorem 4.1
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.1
we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{n}{h_{1}}}(\hat{\tau}^{2}_{n}-\hat{b}_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,4\sigma_{0}^{2}),

where

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{n}:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}h_{1}^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\int_{0}^{1}K\Big(\frac{u-i/n}{h_{1}}\Big)K\Big(\frac{u-j/n}{h_{1}}\Big)du

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~\times\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\int_{0}^{1}\bar{K}\Big(\frac{v-i/n}{h_{2}}\Big)\bar{K}\Big(\frac{v-j/n}{h_{2}}\Big)dv

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~+\frac{1}{n^{2}h_{1}^{2}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}K^{2}\Big(\frac{u-i/n}{h_{1}}\Big)du\Big)\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}\bar{K}^{2}\Big(\frac{v-i/n}{h_{2}}\Big)dv\Big),

and
\sigma_{0}^{2}
defined in (
2.5
).

4.1.
Proof of Theorem
2.1

Since
\hat{r}_{n}=6\hat{\tau}_{n}^{2}-2
, Theorem
4.1
implies

\sqrt{\frac{n}{h_{1}}}\bigl(\hat{r}_{n}-b_{n}\bigr)=6\sqrt{\frac{n}{h_{1}}}(\hat{\tau}_{n}^{2}-\hat{b}_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,144\sigma_{0}^{2}).

This establishes the claimed asymptotic normality of
\hat{r}_{n}
.

4.2.
Proof of Theorem
4.1

Define two random permutations
\phi
and
\lambda
on
\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
\phi(i):=n\hat{F}_{n1}(X_{i})
and
\lambda(i):=n\hat{F}_{n2}(Y_{i})
. If
X
and
Y
are independent,
\phi
and
\lambda
are independent as well and consequently
\pi:=\lambda\phi^{-1}
is a uniformly distributed random permutation on
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. For notational convenience, write
\pi_{i}=\pi(i)
.
Note that

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}^{2}_{n}

\displaystyle=\int\int\Big(\frac{1}{nh_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K\Big(\frac{u-\phi(i)/n}{h_{1}}\Big)\bar{K}\Big(\frac{v-\lambda(i)/n}{h_{2}}\Big)\Big)^{2}dudv

\displaystyle=\int\int\Big(\frac{1}{nh_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K\Big(\frac{u-i/n}{h_{1}}\Big)\bar{K}\Big(\frac{v-\pi(i)/n}{h_{2}}\Big)\Big)^{2}dudv

By expanding the integrand we have

(4.1)

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}^{2}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}h_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}B_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}},

where

\displaystyle B_{ij}:=\int_{0}^{1}\bar{K}\Big(\frac{v-i/n}{h_{2}}\Big)\bar{K}\Big(\frac{v-j/n}{h_{2}}\Big)dv,\qquad a_{ij}:=\frac{1}{h_{1}}\int K\Big(\frac{u-i/n}{h_{1}}\Big)K\Big(\frac{u-j/n}{h_{1}}\Big)du.

By dividing the indices in (
4.1
) into
i\neq j
and
i=j
we have

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}^{2}_{n}

\displaystyle=S_{n}+b_{n0},

where

\displaystyle S_{n}:=\frac{1}{n^{2}h_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq i}^{n}a_{ij}B_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}},\qquad b_{n0}:=\frac{1}{n^{2}h_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii}B_{\pi_{i}\pi_{i}}.

Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[b_{n0}]=\frac{1}{n^{2}h_{1}^{2}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}K^{2}\Big(\frac{u-i/n}{h_{1}}\Big)du\Big)\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}\bar{K}^{2}\Big(\frac{v-i/n}{h_{2}}\Big)dv\Big),

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(b_{n0})=O(\frac{1}{n^{3}h_{1}^{2}}).

Therefore it is enough to study asymptotic behaviour of
S_{n}
.

Defining

\displaystyle b_{ij}:=B_{ij}-\bar{b}_{i}-\bar{b}_{j}+\bar{b},\qquad\bar{b}_{i}:=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{k\neq i}B_{ik},\qquad\bar{b}:=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}B_{ij},

we obtain the representation

\displaystyle S_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}h_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}b_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}+\frac{1}{n^{2}h_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}\bar{b}_{\pi_{i}}+\frac{1}{n^{2}h_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}\bar{b}_{\pi_{j}}-\frac{\bar{b}}{n^{2}h_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}

(4.2)

\displaystyle=D_{n}+2T_{n}-C_{n}.

where

\displaystyle T_{n}:=\frac{1}{n^{2}h_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{b}_{\pi_{i}}\Big(\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}\Big),\qquad D_{n}:=\frac{1}{n^{2}h_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}b_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}},\qquad C_{n}:=\frac{\bar{b}}{n^{2}h_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}.

Note that
C_{n}
is a deterministic constant and it is easy to see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\bar{b}_{\pi_{i}}]}{n^{2}h_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}\Big)=\frac{1}{n^{2}h_{1}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\bar{b}_{\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}\Big)=\frac{\bar{b}}{n^{2}h_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}\Big)=C_{n}.

The following lemmas describe the asymptotic behaviour of
D_{n}
and
T_{n}
and will be proved in Section
4.3
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For continuous and independent
X
and
Y
we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{n}{h_{1}}}\left(T_{n}-\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]\right)\rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2}),

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]=\frac{1}{n^{2}h_{1}^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\int_{0}^{1}K\Big(\frac{u-i/n}{h_{1}}\Big)K\Big(\frac{u-j/n}{h_{1}}\Big)du\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\int_{0}^{1}\bar{K}\Big(\frac{v-i/n}{h_{2}}\Big)\bar{K}\Big(\frac{v-j/n}{h_{2}}\Big)dv,

and
\sigma_{0}^{2}
defined in (
2.5
).

Lemma 4.3
.

For continuous and independent
X
and
Y
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D_{n}]=0,\qquad\mathrm{Var}(D_{n})=O\Big(\frac{1}{n^{2}h_{1}}\Big).

Using Lemma
4.2
and Lemma
4.3
we can complete the proof of Theorem
4.1
. More specifically, we have

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S_{n}]=2\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]-C_{n}=\mathbb{E}[T_{n}],

and

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{n}{h_{1}}}(S_{n}-\mathbb{E}[S_{n}])=\sqrt{\frac{n}{h_{1}}}D_{n}+2\sqrt{\frac{n}{h_{1}}}(T_{n}-\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]).

Since
\mathrm{Var}(D_{n})=O(1/n^{2}h_{1})
, and
nh_{1}^{2}\to\infty
, we have
\sqrt{n/h_{1}}D_{n}\rightarrow 0
, which gives us

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{n}{h_{1}}}(S_{n}-\mathbb{E}[S_{n}])\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,4\sigma_{0}^{2}).

and the assertion follows from (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) together with Lemma

4.3.1.
Proof of Lemma
4.2

Under independence of
X
and
Y
we have

\displaystyle T_{n}:=\frac{1}{n^{2}h_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{b}_{\pi_{i}}L_{i},

where
L_{i}:=\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}
. Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bar{b}_{\pi_{i}}]=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq i}B_{ij}.

Thus we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]=\frac{1}{n^{2}h_{1}^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\int_{0}^{1}K\Big(\frac{u-i/n}{h_{1}}\Big)K\Big(\frac{u-j/n}{h_{1}}\Big)du\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\int_{0}^{1}\bar{K}\Big(\frac{v-i/n}{h_{2}}\Big)\bar{K}\Big(\frac{v-j/n}{h_{2}}\Big)dv,

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})=\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\bar{b}_{\pi_{i}})}{n^{4}(n-1)h_{1}^{2}}\Big(n\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}^{2}-\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\Big)^{2}\Big).

Note that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\bar{b}_{\pi_{i}})=\frac{1}{n(n-1)^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\sum_{j\neq i}B_{ij}\Big)^{2}-\Big(\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}B_{ij}\Big)^{2}.

Note that for fixed
i
we have

\displaystyle\bar{b}_{i}=\frac{1-(i/n)^{2}}{2}+O\left(h_{2}\right)+O(1/n),

which gives us

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\bar{b}_{\pi_{i}})=\frac{1}{45}+O(h_{2})+O(\frac{1}{n}).

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})=\Big(\frac{1+O(h_{2})+O(1/n)}{45n^{4}(n-1)h_{1}^{2}}\Big)\Big(n\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}^{2}-\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\Big)^{2}\Big).

Also note that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}=n^{2}h_{1}+O(nh_{1}),\qquad n\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}^{2}-\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}\Big)^{2}=\Theta(n^{4}h_{1}^{3}),

which gives us

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})=O\Big(\frac{h_{1}}{n}\Big).

Let
\bar{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}/n
. Note that there exists constants
c,c^{\prime},C,C^{\prime}>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}(\bar{b}_{i}-\bar{b})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\bar{b}_{i}-\bar{b})^{2}}\leq\frac{cn^{2}}{Cn^{3}},\qquad\frac{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}(L_{i}-\bar{L})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(L_{i}-\bar{L})^{2}}\leq\frac{c^{\prime}(nh_{1})^{2}}{C^{\prime}(nh_{1})^{3}}=O\Big(\frac{1}{nh_{1}}\Big).

Therefore

\displaystyle n\frac{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}(w_{i}-\bar{w})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(w_{i}-\bar{w})^{2}}\frac{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}(L_{i}-\bar{L})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(L_{i}-\bar{L})^{2}}=O\Big(\frac{1}{nh_{1}}\Big),

which together with Theorem 4 in
8
gives us

\displaystyle\frac{T_{n}-\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

To finish the proof we need to find
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})/h_{1}
. Note that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{n}{h_{1}}\mathrm{Var}(T_{n})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(L_{i}-\bar{L})^{2}}{45n^{3}h_{1}^{3}}.

For fixed
i
, by change of variables we have

\displaystyle a_{ij}=\int_{v:i/n+h_{1}v\in[0,1]}K(v)K\Big(v+\frac{i-j}{nh_{1}}\Big)dv,

which gives

\displaystyle L_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}=\int_{v:i/n+h_{1}v\in[0,1]}K(v)\Big\{\sum_{j\neq i}K\Big(v+\frac{i-j}{nh_{1}}\Big)\Big\}dv.

Note that for fixed
i
,
\{v:i/n+h_{1}v\in[0,1]\}=[-\frac{i}{nh_{1}},\frac{1-i/n}{h_{1}}]
. Also

\displaystyle\sup_{i\leq n}\sup_{|v|\leq 1}\Big|{\sum_{j\neq i}K\Big(v+\frac{i-j}{nh_{1}}\Big)-n\int_{0}^{1}K\Big(v+\frac{i/n-x}{h_{1}}\Big)dx}\Big|=o(nh_{1}).

Since
K
is supported on
[-1,1]
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}K\Big(v+\frac{i/n-x}{h_{1}}\Big)dx=h_{1}\int_{(i/n-1)/h_{1}}^{i/nh_{1}}K(v+w)dw=h_{1}\int_{\max\{(i/n-1)/h_{1},-1-v\}}^{\min\{i/nh_{1},1-v\}}K(v+w)dw.

For
i\leq nh_{1}
we have

\displaystyle h_{1}\int_{\left(i/n-1\right)/h_{1}}^{i/nh_{1}}K(v+w)dw\rightarrow h_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{i/nh_{1}}K(v+w)dw=h_{1}\int_{-1}^{v+i/nh_{1}}K(z)dz=h_{1}\bar{K}\Big(v+\frac{i}{nh_{1}}\Big),

which gives us

\displaystyle L_{i}=\int_{(i/n-1)/h_{1}}^{i/nh_{1}}K(v)\sum_{j\neq i}K(v+\frac{i-j}{nh_{1}})dv=\int_{-i/nh_{1}}^{1}K(v)\Big(nh_{1}\bar{K}(v+\frac{i}{nh_{1}})+o\left(nh_{1}\right)\Big)dv.

Therefore for
i=nh_{1}t
with
t\in[0,1]
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{L_{i}}{nh_{1}}=\int_{-t}^{1}K(v)\bar{K}(v+t)dv.

For
i\in[nh_{1},n(1-h_{1})]
we have
L_{i}/nh_{1}\to 1
. Additionally by symmetry the same is true for
i/n\geq 1-h_{1}
. Taking the average over all
L_{i}
yields

\displaystyle\bar{L}=nh_{1}+O(nh_{1}^{2})+o(nh_{1}).

Let
I_{L}:=\{i:i\leq nh_{1}\}
and
I_{R}:=\{i:i\geq n(1-h_{1})\}
and
I_{0}:=\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus(I_{R}\cup I_{L})
. For interior
i\in I_{0}
we have
L_{i}-\bar{L}=O(nh_{1}^{2})+o(nh_{1})
. Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I_{0}}\left(L_{i}-\bar{L}\right)^{2}=O\left(n^{3}h_{1}^{4}\right).

For
i\in I_{L}
, we have
L_{i}=nh_{1}\int_{-i/nh_{1}}^{1}K(v)\bar{K}(v+\frac{i}{nh_{1}})dv+o(nh_{1})
. Therefore

\displaystyle L_{i}-\bar{L}=nh_{1}\Big(\int_{-i/nh_{1}}^{1}K(v)\bar{K}\Big(v+\frac{i}{nh_{1}}\Big)dv-1\Big)+o(nh_{1}).

Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I_{L}}\left(L_{i}-\bar{L}\right)^{2}=n^{2}h_{1}^{2}\sum_{i\in I_{L}}\Big(1-\int_{-i/nh_{1}}^{1}K(v)\bar{K}\Big(v+\frac{i}{nh_{1}}\Big)dv\Big)^{2}+o\left(n^{3}h_{1}^{3}\right).

Now
|I_{L}|\leq nh_{1}
, therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I_{L}}\left(L_{i}-\bar{L}\right)^{2}=n^{3}h_{1}^{3}\int_{0}^{1}\Big(1-\int_{-t}^{1}K(v)\bar{K}(v+t)dv\Big)^{2}dt+o(n^{3}h_{1}^{3}).

By symmetry ot follws

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I_{R}}\left(L_{i}-\bar{L}\right)^{2}=n^{3}h_{1}^{3}\int_{0}^{1}\Big(1-\int_{-t}^{1}K(v)\bar{K}(v+t)dv\Big)^{2}dt+o(n^{3}h_{1}^{3}),

and combining these results gives

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(L_{i}-\bar{L})^{2}=2\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor nh_{1}\rfloor}(nh_{1})^{2}\bar{K}^{2}\Big(-\frac{i}{nh_{1}}\Big)\sim 2n^{3}h_{1}^{3}\int\bar{K}^{2}(-t)dt+o(n^{3}h_{1}^{3}).

Therefore

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(L_{i}-\bar{L}\right)^{2}}{45n^{3}h_{1}^{3}}=\frac{2}{45}\int_{0}^{1}\Big(1-\int_{-t}^{1}K(v)\bar{K}(v+t)dv\Big)^{2}dt.

Hence we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{n}{h_{1}}}(T_{n}-\mathbb{E}[T_{n}])\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2}).

Proof of (
4.4
)

Let
S=n^{2}h_{1}D_{n}=\sum_{i\neq j}a_{ij}b_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}
. Since
\mathbb{E}[S]=0
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(S)=\sum_{i\neq j}\sum_{k\neq\ell}a_{ij}a_{k\ell}\mathbb{E}[b_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}b_{\pi_{k}\pi_{\ell}}].

Note that
\mathbb{E}[b_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}b_{\pi_{k}\pi_{\ell}}]
depends only on the overlap pattern of
(i,j)
and
(k,\ell)
.

(1)

Identical or reversed pairs

|\{i,j,k,\ell\}|=2
.
In this case we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[b_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}b_{\pi_{k}\pi_{\ell}}]=\mathbb{E}[b_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}^{2}]=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}b_{ij}^{2}=\mu_{2}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\neq j,\,k\neq\ell\\
|\{i,j,k,\ell\}|=2\end{subarray}}a_{ij}a_{k\ell}\mathbb{E}(b_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}b_{\pi_{k}\pi_{\ell}})=2\mu_{2}\sum_{i\neq j}a_{ij}^{2}=2\mu_{2}S_{1}.

(2)

One shared index

|\{i,j,k,\ell\}|=3
.
In this scenario we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[b_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}b_{\pi_{i}\pi_{\ell}}]=\frac{1}{n(n-1)(n-2)}\sum_{r\neq s\neq t}b_{rs}b_{rt}.

Additionally we have
\sum_{s\neq r}b_{rs}=0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{s\neq r}b_{rs}b_{rt}=\Big(\sum_{s\neq r}b_{rs}\Big)^{2}-\sum_{s\neq r}b_{rs}^{2}=-\sum_{s\neq r}b_{rs}^{2}.

Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[b_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}b_{\pi_{i}\pi_{\ell}}]=-\frac{\mu_{2}}{n-2}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\neq j,\,k\neq\ell\\
|\{i,j,k,\ell\}|=3\end{subarray}}a_{ij}a_{k\ell}\mathbb{E}(b_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}b_{\pi_{k}\pi_{\ell}})=-\frac{\mu_{2}}{n-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\neq j,\,k\neq\ell\\
|\{i,j,k,\ell\}|=3\end{subarray}}a_{ij}a_{k\ell}=\frac{4\mu_{2}}{n-2}(S_{2}-S_{1}).

(3)

All distinct

|\{i,j,k,\ell\}|=4
.
In this case, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[b_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}b_{\pi_{k}\pi_{\ell}}]=\frac{1}{n(n-1)(n-2)(n-3)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\neq j,\,k\neq\ell\\
|\{i,j,k,\ell\}|=4\end{subarray}}b_{ij}b_{k\ell}

Using
\sum_{i\neq j}b_{ij}=0
, therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\neq j,\,k\neq\ell\\
|\{i,j,k,\ell\}|=4\end{subarray}}b_{ij}b_{k\ell}=2\sum_{i\neq j}b_{ij}^{2},

therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[b_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}b_{\pi_{k}\pi_{\ell}}]=\frac{2\mu_{2}}{(n-2)(n-3)}.

This gives us

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\neq j,\,k\neq\ell\\
|\{i,j,k,\ell\}|=4\end{subarray}}a_{ij}a_{k\ell}\mathbb{E}(b_{\pi_{i}\pi_{j}}b_{\pi_{k}\pi_{\ell}})=\frac{2\mu_{2}}{(n-2)(n-3)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\neq j,\,k\neq\ell\\
|\{i,j,k,\ell\}|=4\end{subarray}}a_{ij}a_{k\ell}=\frac{2\mu_{2}(S_{3}^{2}+2S_{1}-4S_{2})}{(n-2)(n-3)}.

Putting these together gives us the desired result.

4.4.
Corrected variance

In this section we provide the correct formula for the asymptotic variance in the central limit theorem (
2.4
). It was shown in
12
that a revised version of Equations (24)-(26) in
6
is

\displaystyle n^{1/2}\left(\hat{r}_{n}-\xi\right)=\frac{12}{n^{1/2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(Z_{i}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]\right)+o_{p}(1)

where
Z_{i}=Z_{i1}-Z_{i2}-Z_{i3}
with

\displaystyle Z_{i1}=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbbm{1}\{F_{2}(Y_{i})\leq v\}\tau(F_{1}(X_{i}),v)dv,

\displaystyle Z_{i2}=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\mathbbm{1}\{F_{1}(X_{i})\leq u\}\tau(u,v)\partial_{1}\tau(u,v)dudv,

\displaystyle Z_{i3}=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\mathbbm{1}\{F_{2}(Y_{i})\leq v\}\tau(u,v)\partial_{2}\tau(u,v)dudv,

Then note that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Z_{i1})

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{3}}\tau(s,v\wedge w)\tau(s,v)\tau(s,w)dsdvdw-\big(\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\tau^{2}(u,v)dudv\Big)^{2},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Z_{i2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\Big[\int_{[0,1]^{3}}\tau^{2}(u,v)\tau^{2}(u,w)dvdwdu-\Big(\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\tau(u,v)^{2}dudv\Big)^{2}\Big],

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Z_{i3})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\Big[\int_{[0,1]^{3}}\tau^{2}(u,v)\tau^{2}(w,v)dudvdw-\Big(\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\tau(u,v)^{2}dudv\Big)^{2}\Big],

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{Cov}(Z_{i1},Z_{i2})

\displaystyle=-\int_{[0,1]^{3}}\tau^{2}(u,v)\tau^{2}(u,w)dudvdw+\Big(\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\tau(u,v)^{2}dudv\Big)^{2},

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{Cov}(Z_{i1},Z_{i3})

\displaystyle=-\int_{[0,1]^{4}}\tau^{2}(u,y_{1})\tau(x_{1},w)\partial_{2}\tau(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbbm{1}\{y_{1}\leq w\}dx_{1}dy_{1}dudw+

(4.5)

\displaystyle\qquad\Big(\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\tau(u,v)^{2}dudv\Big)^{2},

(4.6)

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{Cov}(Z_{i2},Z_{i3})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\int_{[0,1]^{4}}\tau^{2}(u,v^{\prime})\tau^{2}(u^{\prime},v)\partial_{2}\tau(u,v)du^{\prime}dv^{\prime}dudv-\Big(\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\tau(u,v)^{2}dudv\Big)^{2}\Big].

Putting these together we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Z_{i1}-Z_{i2}-Z_{i3})

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{3}}\tau(s,v\wedge w)\tau(s,v)\tau(s,w)dsdvdw

\displaystyle+\frac{5}{4}\int_{[0,1]^{3}}\tau^{2}(u,v)\tau^{2}(u,w)dudvdw

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\int_{[0,1]^{3}}\tau^{2}(u,v)\tau^{2}(w,v)dudvdw

\displaystyle+\int_{[0,1]^{4}}\tau^{2}(u,y_{1})\tau(x_{1},w)\partial_{2}\tau(x_{1},y_{1})\mathbbm{1}\{y_{1}\leq w\}dx_{1}dy_{1}dudw

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{[0,1]^{4}}\tau^{2}(u,v^{\prime})\tau^{2}(u^{\prime},v)\partial_{2}\tau(u,v)du^{\prime}dv^{\prime}dudv

\displaystyle-4\Big(\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\tau^{2}(u,v)dudv\Big)^{2}.

Note that the variance
\sigma^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(Z_{i1}-Z_{i2}-Z_{i3})
derived above differs from the corresponding expression in
6
. This discrepancy arises because the quantities corresponding to (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) were not computed correctly in
6
. In addition, a sign error in that paper, detected by
12
, led to the use of
\mathrm{Var}(Z_{i1}+Z_{i2}+Z_{i3})
in place of the correct
\mathrm{Var}(Z_{i1}-Z_{i2}-Z_{i3})
.
Moreover, a simple calculation shows that in the case of independence, that is
C(u,v)=uv,

\tau(u,v)=v
,
\sigma^{2}=0
