Independent mortality predictors in older COVID-19 patients with delayed hospitalization: A retrospective study from Eastern China, 2023
Authors: Qiaoli Liu, Lifu Zhao, Mei Li, Chunwei Shi, Piao Hu, Haifeng Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Delayed hospitalization of older patients with COVID-19 results in the loss of the early antiviral treatment window, as well as the progression to critical illness involving shock, hypoxemia, and corticosteroid therapy. Nevertheless, the hierarchy of independent mortality predictors in this specific context remains to be fully established. The study aimed to identify and rank these predictors to inform clinical management. This retrospective observational study was conducted at the First People’s Hospital of Xiaoshan District, China (January–February 2023). The present study included hospitalized patients aged ≥ 60 years with first-time severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 infection and delayed hospitalization (>5 days from symptom onset). The data were predominantly collected electronically. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression were performed to identify independent risk factors for mortality. A total of 300 older hospitalized COVID-19 patients (median age 82 years; 57.3% male) were included. Overall mortality was 17.3% (52/300). In multivariable logistic regression, independent factors associated with mortality included older age (OR: 1.11, 95% CI: 1.03–1.19), elevated cardiac troponin I (OR: 1.01, 95% CI: 1.0–1.02), presence of shock (OR: 26.72, 95% CI: 7.27–98.18), prolonged corticosteroid use (OR: 8.19, 95% CI: 2.39–28.05), and requirement of oxygen therapy for hypoxia (OR: 2.98, 95% CI: 1.70–5.23). Shock was the strongest independent predictor of mortality in older COVID-19 patients with delayed hospitalization, outweighing prolonged corticosteroid use and hypoxemia. These findings emphasize the critical importance of early shock recognition in this vulnerable population, while also highlighting the need for judicious corticosteroid use. The identified risk hierarchy may serve as a framework for clinical decision-making in resource-constrained settings.

1. Introduction

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2) has caused the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic and led to significant worldwide morbidity and mortality in the past few years.[1] In China, protective quarantine measures were implemented, and as of December 23, 2022, a total of 5241 cumulative deaths and 397,195 confirmed cases had been reported.[2] However, the first wave of COVID-19 did not sweep across the country until December 26, 2022, when China abolished the social distancing and isolation strategies and adopted a “living with COVID-19” strategy.[3] This policy shift involved the gradual lifting of previous large-scale containment measures and a reorientation of focus from infection prevention to the prevention of severe disease and mortality.

This strategic change preceded the first nationwide wave of mass SARS-CoV-2 infections driven by the Omicron variant. Our study period (January–February 2023) immediately followed this shift, providing a unique opportunity to investigate clinical outcomes in a large cohort of older inpatients infected during this initial wave. The rapid and widespread community transmission placed unprecedented strain on healthcare systems, making real-world evaluation of risk factors and treatment patterns particularly relevant.

Older adults are more susceptible to SARS-CoV-2,[4,5] and bear a disproportionately high burden of severe disease.[5–9] In China, between December 8, 2022, and January 12, 2023, there were 59,938 in-hospital deaths associated with COVID-19. Notably, patients aged 65 years or older accounted for 90.1% of these deaths.[10]

Oral antivirals, such as Nirmatrelvir/ritonavir and Molnupiravir,[11–13] are cornerstone therapies but require initiation within 5 days of symptom onset. In a massive wave, delayed hospitalization is common, often causing patients to miss this critical window. Although factors like advanced age and comorbidities contribute to mortality,[14–16] severe COVID-19 ultimately culminates in life-threatening physiological derangements. Specifically, shock and profound hypoxemia are recognized as the predominant immediate causes of death, representing the final common pathways in this population.[6,17,18] Hypoxemia and even acute respiratory distress syndrome, driven by cytokine storm in a subset of COVID-19 patients, have positioned corticosteroids—known for their anti-inflammatory effects – as a key treatment.[19,20]

However, the role of immunomodulatory therapy, particularly corticosteroids, remains nuanced. Although administration of corticosteroid therapy was previously reported to improve the prognosis of severe COVID-19 pneumonia,[21] its use has also been associated with increased 28-day mortality in certain contexts,[22] which has led to continuous discussion about its overall usefulness in practical settings, particularly at high doses or for extended periods.

The general risk factors associated with COVID-19 mortality have been extensively documented. However, systematic analyses focusing on older adults with delayed hospitalization are lacking. In particular, the hierarchical significance of key predictors has not been well characterized in this vulnerable population. Against the background of the “living with COVID-19” strategy and the subsequent Omicron wave, we therefore conducted a retrospective study to identify key mortality predictors in older COVID-19 patients with delayed hospitalization. We hypothesized that shock, hypoxemia, and prolonged corticosteroid use would all be independently associated with mortality, with shock exhibiting the strongest association. Elucidating the hierarchical significance of these risk factors may enable clinicians to better prioritize monitoring and interventions for this high-risk group.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and setting

This was a retrospective observational study conducted at the First People’s Hospital of Xiaoshan District, a district-level tertiary hospital serving approximately 2.11 million residents in Xiaoshan District, Zhejiang Province, China. During the study period (January–February 2023), the hospital managed approximately 400 confirmed COVID-19 cases, of which 362 were screened for this study.

The study period was selected because it immediately followed the major shift in China’s national COVID-19 management policy in December 2022, which led to the first widespread, community-level Omicron variant outbreak. This period represents a distinct and homogeneous phase of the pandemic, allowing for the recruitment of a large cohort of older inpatients during the initial wave of infections in the context of the “living with COVID-19” strategy.

2.2. Study population

Diagnosis was confirmed by positive PCR assay performed by the hospital’s clinical laboratory. The inclusion criteria were: aged 60 years or older; hospitalized with a first diagnosis of SARS-CoV-2 infection; and having a time from symptom onset to admission of >5 days.

The exclusion criteria were: pregnancy; refusal to participate in the study; and previous COVID-19 infection. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and all participants provided informed consent.

A consecutive sampling method was used. The final sample size of 300 represents all eligible patients who met the criteria during the study period, without a priori power calculation. Participant selection details are shown in Figure 1.

Flowchart showing participant selection for the study.

2.3. Definition

Delayed hospitalization was defined as admission more than 5 days after symptom onset. Shock was defined as the presence of clinical symptoms suggestive of hypoperfusion, accompanied by any of the following hemodynamic criteria: systolic blood pressure < 90 mm Hg, a ≥30% decrease from baseline, or a pulse pressure < 20 mm Hg. Mortality was defined as either in-hospital death or death within 2 weeks after discharge among patients who were discharged at their own or their legal guardian’s request due to critical illness with poor prognosis. Post-discharge survival information was collected during telephone interviews conducted to obtain informed consent.

2.4. Data collection

Data were primarily collected from electronic medical records. We collected demographic data, including age and sex, as well as information on comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, chronic lung disease, chronic kidney disease, and cerebrovascular disease. Laboratory values were obtained within 24 hours following admission, encompassing white blood cell (WBC) count, lymphocyte (L) count, hemoglobin (Hb), platelet (PLT) count, C-reactive protein (CRP), D-dimer, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine (SCR), creatine kinase-myocardial isoenzyme, and cardiac troponin I (cTnI). Additionally, we gathered data on the interval from symptom onset to hospitalization, the time from symptom onset to the initiation of oral antiviral treatment, the presence of shock and hypoxia, the duration of corticosteroid therapy >7 days (methylprednisolone, ≥40 mg/day), and patient outcomes (survivor or non-survivor). For critically ill patients discharged at their own or their family’s request, verbal informed consent was obtained via telephone from their legal representatives, and their vital status within 2 weeks after discharge was retrospectively collected during the telephone interview conducted to obtain informed consent.

Routine clinical care followed the national COVID-19 diagnosis and treatment guidelines (Tentative 10th Version) in effect at the time.[23] Specifically, awake prone positioning was routinely applied in non-intubated patients; high-flow nasal cannula was prioritized as first-line noninvasive support; invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) was initiated only when hypoxemia persisted despite optimized noninvasive support. For severe/critical cases with progressive deterioration, a short-term course of corticosteroids (methylprednisolone ≥ 40 mg/day, typically ≤10 days) was considered. Prolonged (>7 days) or high-dose therapy was discouraged. Oral antivirals (nirmatrelvir/ritonavir, molnupiravir) were used based on drug availability and physician discretion.

Vaccination status was not systematically recorded in the medical records and therefore was not assessed as an eligibility criterion; this limitation is acknowledged in the Discussion.

2.5. Variables

The dependent variable was mortality (alive/dead). Independent variables considered included demographics (age, sex), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, chronic kidney disease, chronic lung disease, cerebrovascular disease), laboratory parameters (WBC count, L count, CRP, D-dimer, BUN, serum creatinine, cardiac troponin I), in-hospital complications (shock, bacterial infection, hypoxemia), treatments (oral antivirals, oxygen therapy type, prolonged corticosteroid use [>7 days]), and timing variables (time from symptom onset to hospitalization or antiviral treatment).

Variables were selected based on clinical plausibility and prior literature. The core independent variables retained in the final multivariable model (age, shock, hypoxemia, prolonged corticosteroid use, and cardiac troponin I) were those that showed a significant association in univariable analysis (P < .10) and remained independently predictive after multivariable adjustment.

2.6. Statistical analysis

All variables were assessed for parametric or non-parametric distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (P25–P75), while categorical variables were presented as (n, %). The dependent variable was mortality, and independent variables are detailed in the Variables section. To compare baseline characteristics between survivors and non-survivors (Table 1), the Mann–Whitney U test, Chi-squared test, or Fisher exact test were employed as appropriate. A P-value of <.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance. To identify risk factors associated with mortality (Table 2), both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted. Variables with a P-value of <.10 in the univariate analysis were included in the initial multivariable model, followed by backward elimination based on the likelihood ratio test, with a retention criterion of P < .05. Multicollinearity among independent variables in the final model was assessed using variance inflation factors, with a variance inflation factors <3 indicating no significant collinearity. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk).

3. Results

3.1. Participant selection

Among 362 screened COVID-19 patients, 12 were excluded for age < 60 years and 16 for admission < 5 days from symptom onset. Of 334 with delayed hospitalization, 3 reinfection cases and 30 consent refusals yielded 301 enrollees. One transfer-related withdrawal left 300 patients in the final analytic sample (Fig. 1). Of the 334 eligible patients, 30 (9.0%) declined consent and were excluded. The high participation rate (90.1%) suggests that the final cohort is likely representative of the target population.

3.2. Participant characteristics

A total of 300 older patients hospitalized with COVID-19 were included. The median age was 82 years, and 172 (57.33%) were male. Hypertension was the most frequent comorbidity (69.67%), followed by diabetes mellitus (30.67%), coronary artery disease (27.67%), chronic lung disease (27.67%), cerebrovascular disease (21.33%), and chronic kidney disease (8.3%).

Laboratory results showed median levels of CRP at 52.28 mg/L, D-dimer at 1.125 mg/L, BUN at 7.57 mmol/L, SCR at 78.35 μmol/L, and cTnI at 26 ng/L. The median time from symptom onset to hospitalization and antiviral treatment was 7 and 9 days, respectively. Oral antivirals were given to 192 patients (64%). Bacterial infections and shock were noted in 259 and 47 patients, respectively. Conventional oxygen therapy was used by 170 patients (56.67%), with 40 (13.33%) receiving non-IMV and 21 (7%) undergoing IMV. Prolonged corticosteroid use was recorded in 65 patients (21.67%).

In our study of 300 older SARS-CoV-2 patients, 248 survived (82.67%) and 52 died (17.33%). Non-survivors were older and had higher rates of coronary and cerebrovascular diseases. They also exhibited more pronounced laboratory abnormalities: higher WBC count, CRP, D‑dimer, BUN, SCR, cTnI, and lower Ls (all P < .01). They required more ventilation support, experienced more shock and bacterial infections, and received longer corticosteroid treatments (all P < .001). Oral antiviral use was also higher among non-survivors (80.77% vs 60.48%, P = .006).

There were no statistically significant differences observed between the groups with respect to sex, hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, chronic lung disease, Hb levels, PLT count, ALT, or the duration from symptom onset to the initiation of antiviral treatment and hospitalization (P > .05).

3.3. Univariate analysis

In univariate logistic regression (Table 2), patients who died were older (OR: 1.05, 95% CI: 1.01–1.10) and more likely to have coronary artery disease (OR: 2.03, 95% CI: 1.09–3.79), chronic kidney disease (OR: 2.47, 95% CI: 1.00–6.08), and cerebrovascular disease (OR: 2.90, 95% CI: 1.52–5.53). They also had elevated CRP (OR: 1.01, 95% CI: 1.01–1.02), D-dimer (OR: 1.11, 95% CI: 1.06–1.17), BUN (OR: 1.11, 95% CI: 1.07–1.16), SCR (OR: 1.00, 95% CI: 1.00–1.01), cTnI (OR: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.02), and lower L count (OR: 0.11, 95% CI: 0.03–0.35). A shorter time from symptom onset to oral antiviral administration was protective (OR: 0.46, 95% CI: 0.29–0.76). Regarding in-hospital factors, mortality was associated with bacterial infection (OR: 9.81, 95% CI: 1.32–73.04), shock (OR: 90, 95% CI: 35.03–231.21), hypoxia (OR: 4.98, 95% CI: 3.43–7.23), prolonged corticosteroid use (OR: 8.03, 95% CI: 4.07–15.84), and oral antiviral use (OR: 0.46, 95% CI: 0.29–0.76).

Patients who died did not differ significantly from survivors by sex, time from symptom onset to hospitalization, WBC count, Hb, PLT count, ALT, creatine kinase-myocardial isoenzyme, hypertension, diabetes, or chronic lung disease.

3.4. Multivariate analysis

After multivariable adjustment (Table 2), non-survivors were older (OR: 1.11, 95% CI: 1.03–1.19) and more likely to have elevated cTnI (OR: 1.01, 95% CI: 1.00–1.02), present with shock (OR: 26.72, 95% CI: 7.27–98.18), have received prolonged corticosteroid therapy (OR: 8.19, 95% CI: 2.39–28.05), and require oxygen therapy for hypoxia (OR: 2.98, 95% CI: 1.70–5.23). In the multivariable model, coronary artery disease, D-dimer, and oral antiviral use were not independently associated with mortality.

4. Discussion

This retrospective study aimed to identify and prioritize risk factors to guide clinicians in monitoring and intervention strategies for older adults with delayed hospitalization. Key findings include a mortality rate of 17.33% in this study. Shock was the strongest predictor of mortality (OR: 26.72), followed by prolonged corticosteroid therapy (OR: 8.19) and hypoxia (OR: 2.98). Older age (OR: 1.11 per year increase) and elevated cTnI (OR: 1.01 per ng/L increase) were also independently associated with mortality, albeit with smaller odds ratios. These results provide a hierarchy of risks to inform clinical priorities.

The 17.33% mortality rate in our older adult cohort with delayed hospitalization is important. This rate is lower than the 21.89% reported in an early Wuhan study, where high mortality may be attributable to severe acute illness, as reflected by a 33.3% mechanical ventilation rate.[6] Our cohort, infected during the less virulent Omicron-dominant wave, had a 20% mechanical ventilation rate but a higher prevalence of chronic conditions (hypertension: 69.7% vs 19.4%; diabetes: 30.7% vs 10.9%). This suggests that, unlike the early pandemic where acute respiratory failure was the main cause of death, current mortality in frail, multimorbid older adults may be due to pneumonia-induced decompensation of preexisting multimorbidity. This hypothesis needs further investigation. According to the Chinese Diagnosis and Treatment Protocol for COVID-19 (Tentative 10th Version for our study; earlier versions for the Wuhan study),[23] IMV is indicated only after stepwise noninvasive support, starting with oxygen therapy and then high-flow nasal cannula or noninvasive ventilation if hypoxemia persists. Both studies adhered to this principle. In our practice, we avoided invasive ventilation when feasible, and the significant difference in oxygen therapy between groups (Table 1, P < .001) reflects baseline severity, not a protocol change. Therefore, the lower mortality in our cohort cannot be attributed to different ventilation rates, but rather to the lower virulence of Omicron and differences in comorbidity profiles.

Shock was identified as the strongest predictor of mortality (OR: 26.72), reflecting the critical physiological state of systemic hypoperfusion, cellular oxygen debt, and multiorgan dysfunction.[24] This odds ratio is substantially higher than the 2.39 reported for septic shock in a previous ICU study.[25] The difference may be attributable to 2 factors. First, our study included both septic and cardiogenic shock, whereas the previous study only included septic shock. Second, the ICU study cohort had a high illness severity, with 77.5% requiring noninvasive ventilation and 5.6% receiving extracorporeal membrane oxygenation.[25] Such uniformly high severity may have reduced shock’s discriminatory power as a mortality predictor, as the overall critical illness overshadowed any additional risk conferred by shock. In contrast, our diverse group of older adults with delayed hospitalization encompasses a broader spectrum of clinical severity, making shock a more prominent predictor. To our knowledge, this is the first study to quantify shock’s association with mortality (OR: 26.72) in this specific population.

In addition to acute circulatory failure, certain therapeutic interventions also significantly influenced outcomes. Prolonged corticosteroid therapy (OR: 8.19) emerged as a potentially modifiable iatrogenic factor contributing to mortality in older adults with delayed hospitalization. The RECOVERY trial, which compared higher-dose corticosteroid regimens (dexamethasone 20 mg daily for 5 days, followed by 10 mg daily) to usual care (including dexamethasone 6 mg daily), reported a significantly increased 28-day mortality with higher doses (19% vs 12%; rate ratio 1.59, 95% CI: 1.20–2.10).[26] Although direct comparison of odds ratios and risk ratios is not straightforward, our observed OR of 8.19 is markedly higher, likely due to the frailty and multimorbidity of our older population and the higher proportion requiring mechanical ventilation, which may increase susceptibility to the adverse effects of prolonged corticosteroids. Notably, compared to the usual care group, a randomized controlled trial (RCT) evaluating dexamethasone 6 mg demonstrated that corticosteroids can reduce mortality in carefully selected hypoxic COVID-19 patients – those requiring IMV (29.3% vs 41.4%; rate ratio, 0.64; 95% CI: 0.51–0.81) or oxygen without IMV (23.3% vs 26.2%; rate ratio, 0.82; 95% CI: 0.72–0.94).[27] Several factors may explain the apparent discrepancy between these beneficial effects and our finding of harm. First, the mean age of patients in our study was substantially higher (80.26 ± 8.65 years) compared to that of the RCT population (66.1 ± 15.7 years); older adults are particularly susceptible to the adverse effects of prolonged corticosteroid therapy. Second, our study documented real-world prescribing patterns, with patients receiving methylprednisolone at doses of ≥40 mg/day (up to 80 mg/day in critically ill patients) and, in some cases, for durations exceeding 10 days – a substantially more intensive and prolonged regimen than the fixed short-course dexamethasone 6 mg evaluated in the RCT. The higher cumulative dose and prolonged duration of corticosteroid exposure in our cohort may amplify the risks of secondary infections and metabolic complications,[28,29] potentially outweighing any acute anti-inflammatory benefits. To our knowledge, this is the first study to quantify the association between high-dose (methylprednisolone ≥ 40 mg/day), prolonged-duration corticosteroid therapy and mortality risk specifically in frail older adults with delayed hospitalization.

Beyond iatrogenic factors, the severity of respiratory failure itself was a major determinant of survival. This study found that hypoxia was independently associated with mortality (OR: 2.98, 95% CI: 1.70–5.23), which aligns with the well-established paradigm that severe hypoxemia is a major driver of death in COVID-19 pneumonia,[30] often progressing to acute respiratory distress syndrome.[31] An approximately 3‑fold increase in odds reflects the substantial mortality risk associated with clinically significant hypoxemia that requires supplemental oxygen. Our results underscore the importance of early recognition of hypoxia and prompt escalation of respiratory support, including high-flow nasal cannula or noninvasive ventilation, to prevent further deterioration.[32] Moreover, the independent effect of hypoxia after adjusting for shock highlights its distinct pathophysiological role, separate from circulatory failure.

While acute respiratory and circulatory failures are paramount, baseline patient characteristics also played a role. In line with numerous studies, older age was independently associated with mortality in our cohort (OR: 1.11 per year increase).[33] This finding underscores the well-known vulnerability of the elderly population to severe COVID-19 outcomes, likely due to immunosenescence, a higher comorbidity burden, and reduced physiological reserve.[34,35] Even within this already older cohort (median 82 years), each additional year of age conferred an 11% increase in the odds of death, consistent with recent findings in critically ill elderly patients,[36] highlighting the need for age-stratified risk assessment and intensive monitoring.[37]

Similarly, subclinical myocardial injury also independently predicted death. Elevated cTnI was independently associated with mortality.[38,39] While the per-unit OR: (1.01 per ng/L increase) appears modest in this study, the wide range of cTnI values in our patients means that clinically elevated levels can translate into a substantially increased risk. This finding supports the growing evidence that myocardial injury, even when mild, is a marker of poor prognosis in COVID-19,[40] possibly reflecting direct viral damage, systemic inflammation, microvascular thrombosis, or underlying coronary artery disease.[41,42] Accordingly, admission cTnI measurement may aid in early risk stratification and guide clinical decision-making for this high-risk population.[43]

This study has notable strengths. First, to our knowledge, this is among the first studies to systematically examine the hierarchy of risk factors for mortality in older adults with delayed hospitalization. Second, it provides real-world evidence that complements clinical trial findings, as such patients are often excluded from trials yet are commonly encountered in clinical practice. Third, the high participation rate (90.1%) among eligible patients suggests that our cohort is likely representative of the target population of older COVID-19 patients with delayed hospitalization. Fourth, the study was performed at a national Class A Tertiary General Hospital with standardized data management systems and recognized expertise in COVID-19 care, which strengthens the reliability and generalizability of our results.

However, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, vaccination status was unavailable, which may confound risk estimates. However, the study was conducted in early 2023 when most older adults had been vaccinated and Omicron predominated, partially attenuating this bias. Future studies should collect vaccination data. Second, we only extracted data on methylprednisolone ≥ 40 mg/day for ≥7 days, precluding dose‑duration stratified analysis and possibly overestimating the association with mortality. Consecutive sampling and explicit criteria were used to mitigate selection bias. Prospective studies with complete dose‑duration data are needed. Third, factors causing delayed hospitalization were not documented, limiting differentiation between patient‑ and system‑level causes. Restricting the cohort to a homogeneous post‑policy window (January–February 2023) reduced external variability. Further research should specifically examine determinants of delayed hospitalization.

5. Conclusions

In this study involving 300 older adults experiencing delayed hospitalization, the observed mortality rate was 17.33%. Independent risk factors for mortality included shock (OR: 26.72), prolonged corticosteroid therapy (OR: 8.19), hypoxia (OR: 2.98), advanced age, and elevated cTnI. These findings highlight that shock is the most significant predictor of mortality in this vulnerable cohort, while extended high-dose corticosteroid therapy is linked to a markedly increased risk. Clinicians should prioritize early shock detection and be cautious with prolonged corticosteroid use in this group. Future research should aim to create and validate comprehensive risk prediction models.
