On the Area of immersed Minimal annuli in a slab
Elham Matinpour

Abstract
. In this note, we organize minimal annuli in a slab based on the winding number of the circles that foliate them and study the area of minimal annuli with given winding number. Specifically, we deduce some results regarding the convexity of the length function of corresponding level curves, and apply them to estimate the area of the annuli by comparing to the area of waist of covers of catenoids.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification.
Primary 53A10; Secondary 53A05

Key words and phrases.
minimal annuli, catenoid, winding number, area estimates, minimal surfaces, differential geometry

1.
Introduction

The first nontrivial minimal surface discovered is an annulus in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the catenoid. A result of the work of Schoen
[
7
]
features the catenoid as the unique minimal surface with finite total curvature and two embedded ends, and Lopez and Ros
[
4
]
show that the catenoid is the unique complete planar domain of finite total curvature embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. There are more examples of minimal annuli embedded in a slab such as a neck of a Riemann example. Rotationally symmetric compact pieces of the catenoid are characterized in a slab in several ways. For instance, Pyo showed that the catenoid in a slab is the only minimal annulus meeting the boundary of the slab in a constant angle
[
pyo2009minimal
]
. Also it was proved by Bernstein and Breiner that all embedded minimal annuli in a slab have area bigger than or equal to the minimum area of the catenoids in the same slab
[
1
]
. That minimum is attained by the catenoidal waist along the boundary of which the rays from the center of the slab are tangent to the waist. This waist is marginally stable because it is stable but any subset of the catenoid properly containing the waist is unstable, we call them the maximally symmetric marginally stable piece of catenoid in that slab. Bernstein and Breiner used Osserman-Schiffer’s theorem [Theorem 1.,
[
5
]
] on the convexity of the length of the circles foliating the minimal annulus. They also conjectured that the maximally symmetric marginally stable pieces of the catenoids in a slab achieve the least area among all smooth minimal surfaces spanning the same slab.

Later, Choe and Daniel
[
2
]
proved their conjecture under the additional assumption that the intersections of the minimal surface in the slab with horizontal planes have the same orientation. In the same work, they also proposed some related questions. Specifically, they consider an immersed minimal annulus with figure-eight level curves, denoted by
\Sigma
, within a horizontal slab, denoted by
\Omega
. They inquire whether the maximally symmetric and marginally stable waist of the catenoid in
\Omega
still attains the minimum area when compared to
\Sigma
, [Problem 2.
[
2
]
]. We give an affirmative answer to this question for annuli with vertical flux in Theorem
4.3
.

Fix two parallel planes
P_{\pm}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
P_{+}\neq P_{-}
and denote by
\Omega

, so that

so that

. Here,

is an open annular domain.

Recall that for a given pair of connected simple closed curves
\sigma_{+}\subset P_{+}
and
\sigma_{-}\subset P_{-}
, a necessary condition for the existence of a minimal surface in
\Omega
spanning
\sigma_{\pm}
is the existence of a waist of a catenoid in

so that the length of its boundary in

is less than or equal to the length of

[
1

and [Theorem 4,
[

]
]. And, for a given embedded annulus

Let

where

.

By the fact that coordinate functions on

(1) If

We identify
\mathcal{A}(\Omega,-k)

\ell(h_{-})
.

F_{3}

. For

. We are concerned with the following question:

Is still true that

Area(\Sigma)\geq Area(C_{k})

with equality if and only if
\Sigma

The idea of checking their winding numbers comes from the work of Osserman-Schiffer. In the proof of their convexity result, [Theorem 1.,
[

]

The first case is the set of all minimal annuli with even winding number, zero is included, and the second case is the set of all minimal annuli with odd winding number.

Furthermore, the essence of this note is based on the observation that self-intersections of the level curves of an annulus may allow for deformations that yield new minimal annuli with smaller areas. Specifically, fix the slab
\Omega
to be horizontal, and choose an appropriate scale to compare and evaluate the minimal annuli in
\Omega

Preliminaries

Background on the space

Theorem 2.1
.

The least area element in
\mathcal{A}(\Omega,1)
is
C_{\Omega}
, the maximally symmetric marginally stable piece of the catenoid spanning

Proof.

This result directly follows from [Theorem 1.1,

∎

Theorem 2.2
.

The least area element in
\mathcal{A}^{*}(\Omega,k)
is a
k
-fold cover of catenoid, for

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{F}\in\mathcal{A}^{*}(\Omega,k)
. By the definition of

\mathbb{G}\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega,1)
so that
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{G}\circ P_{k}

is the maximally symmetric marginally stable piece of the catenoid in

represents the least area element in the set

.
∎

2.2.
Geometric background

Let
\Sigma
be an annulus parameterized in
\mathcal{A}_{V}(\Omega)
, and denote its minimal conformal parameterization by
\mathbb{X}(z)=(X_{1}(z),X_{2}(z),X_{3}(z)):A_{R_{0},R_{1}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Here, the functions
X_{k}
are harmonic in
z
, and the functions
\phi_{k}:=\frac{\partial X_{k}}{\partial x}-i\frac{\partial X_{k}}{\partial y}
are analytic in
z:=x+iy
, for
k=1,2,3
. The element of arc length
ds
on the surface
\Sigma
is given by

ds^{2}=\lambda^{2}|dz|^{2},\ \ \text{where}\ \lambda=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3}|\phi_{i}|^{2}}.

And,

L(r)=|\mathbb{X}(\{|z|=r\})|,

is the length of the image of the circle
\{|z|=r\}\subset A_{R_{0},R_{1}}
. Let
\psi_{i}=z\phi_{i}
, and define
\mu=r\lambda=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3}|\psi_{i}|^{2}}
, then we may write

L(r)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mu(re^{i\theta})d\theta

Recall that, for
\mathbb{X}\in\mathcal{A}_{V}(\Omega)
one has

L^{\prime\prime}(t)\geq L(t),\ \text{for}\ t=\log r

For a general minimal annulus
\mathbb{X}(A_{R_{0},R_{1}})\in\mathcal{A}_{V}(\Omega)
, the Weierstrass representation of
\mathbb{X}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
can be expressed as

\mathbb{X}(p)=Re\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Big(\frac{1}{2}(g^{-1}-g),\frac{i}{2}(g^{-1}+g),1\Big)dh

where
g
is the extended Gauss map, a meromorphic function defined on its Riemann surface,
A_{R_{0},R_{1}}
, and
dh
is a holomorphic 1-form on
A_{R_{0},R_{1}}
. Note that,

\mathbb{X}(p)=Re\int\mathbb{\Phi}dz

where
\mathbb{X}(z)=(X_{1}(z),X_{2}(z),X_{3}(z)),\ \mathbb{\Phi}=(\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3})
, and
\phi_{i}=\frac{\partial X_{i}}{\partial x}-i\frac{\partial X_{i}}{\partial y}
, for
z=x+iy
.

\mathbb{X}
is well defined on its domain if and only if no component of
\mathbb{\Phi}
has a real period. That is

\text{Period}_{\gamma}(\mathbb{\Phi})(=\text{P}(\gamma)):=Re\oint_{\gamma}\mathbb{\Phi}dz=0

for all closed curves
\gamma
on
A_{R_{0},R_{1}}
: this is the Period Problem. A good reference on Weiestrass representation is
[
3
]
.
We express

(2.1)

\phi_{1}=\frac{1}{2}f(1-g^{2}),\ \phi_{2}=\frac{i}{2}f(1+g^{2}),\ \phi_{3}=fg

where
dh=fgdz
,
f
is analytic in
A_{R_{0},R_{1}}
, the Gauss map
g
is meromorphic in
A_{R_{0},R_{1}}
,
g
has poles precisely at the zeros of
f
, and each zero of
f
has an order equal to twice the order of the pole of
g
at that point. Then

(2.2)

\lambda=\frac{1}{2}|f|(1+|g|^{2})=\frac{1}{2}|\phi_{3}|(\frac{1}{|g|}+|g|)

and

(2.3)

\mu=\frac{1}{2}|\psi_{3}|(\frac{1}{|g|}+|g|)

Recall that
\psi_{i}=z\phi_{i}
, for
i=1,2,3
. We can compute

(2.4)

\begin{split}\psi_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\psi_{3}(\frac{1}{g}-g),\ \ \ \psi_{2}=\frac{i}{2}\psi_{3}(\frac{1}{g}+g)\\
\frac{\psi_{3}}{g}=\psi_{1}-i\psi_{2},\ \ \ \psi_{3}g=-\psi_{1}-i\psi_{2}.\end{split}

Lemma 2.2
.

For any annulus
\mathbb{X}:A_{R_{0},R_{1}}\rightarrow\Omega
in
\mathcal{A}_{V}(\Omega)
, up to homothety, we have
dh=\frac{F_{3}}{2\pi}\frac{dz}{z}
.

This is proved in
[
1
]
, see Corollaries 2.2 and 2.4., their proof is based on the fact that the embedded annuli in
\mathcal{A}_{V}(\Omega)
are transverse to horizontal planes, Lemma 2.1
[
1
]
. However, the transversally result holds for our immersed surfaces
\mathbb{X}\in\mathcal{A}_{V}(\Omega)
since the height function is harmonic and constant on boundary components, so it cannot have any critical point on the interior.

Remark 2.3
.

For the above reparameterization of the surface
\mathbb{X}
, the complexified height differential is
dh=c\frac{dz}{z}
. That gives the map
\psi_{3}=z\phi_{3}=\frac{zdh}{dz}=c
is constant.

Osserman-Schiffer
[
5
]
observed that the length functions

and

can be derived from the two functions
\frac{\psi_{3}}{g}
and

, enabling the derivation of certain relationships between

and

(2.5)

\frac{d^{2}L}{dt^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\big(r^{2}\Delta|\frac{\psi_{3}}{g}|+r^{2}\Delta|\psi_{3}g|\big)d\theta

and

(2.6)

L=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\big(|\frac{\psi_{3}}{g}|+|\psi_{3}g|\big)d\theta.

Furthermore, they assumed that the functions
\frac{\psi_{3}}{g}
and
\psi_{3}g
satisfy:

(2.7)

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\psi_{3}}{g}(re^{i\theta})d\theta=0,\ \ \int_{0}^{2\pi}\psi_{3}g(re^{i\theta})d\theta=0.

In essence, solving the two integral equations above is equivalent to addressing the period problem for the parameterization of
\mathbb{X}
, as long as the flux is vertical. Consequently, the representation
\mathbb{X}
associated with the surface
\Sigma=\mathbb{X}(A_{R_{0},R_{1}})
is considered well-defined on
A_{R_{0},R_{1}}
if it satisfies these two integral equations. We summarize the above:

Proposition 2.3
.

The provided Weierstrass representation data for
\mathbb{X}
satisfies the period problem,
P(\gamma)=0
for any closed cycle on
A_{R_{0},R_{1}}
, and (
2.7
) holds.

Setting
F_{-}(z)=\frac{\psi_{3}}{g}
or

, the functions

are analytic on
A_{R_{0},R_{1}}
and satisfy:

(2.8)

\int_{0}^{2\pi}F_{\pm}(re^{i\theta})d\theta=0

Assuming that
F_{\pm}
are non-zero on
A_{R_{0},R_{1}}
, we have two possibilities:

(2.9)

\text{Case 1.}\ F_{\pm}(z)=G_{\pm}^{2}(z),\ \ \ \text{or}\ \ \ \text{Case 2.}\ F_{\pm}(z)=zG_{\pm}^{2}(z),

where
G_{\pm}(z)
are analytic in
A_{R_{0},R_{1}}
and have there the Laurent expansions:

G_{\pm}(z)=\underset{n=-\infty}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}a_{n}z^{n}

Note that,

(2.10)

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\text{arg}F_{\pm}(re^{i\theta})d\theta=2\pi k_{\pm},

where

denote the winding number of the circles that foliate the annular domain of

.

Lemma 3.1
.

For surfaces in
\mathcal{A}_{V}(\Omega,2k)
, the relationship
L^{\prime\prime}(t)>2L(t)
holds, where
k=0,1,2,\cdots
.

Proof.

Let

, for

. In this case,

. Using (

, denoted as

a_{0}^{2}+2\underset{n=1}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}a_{n}a_{-n}=0.

Compute

\begin{split}|a_{0}|^{2}&=2\Big|\underset{n=1}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}a_{n}r^{n}a_{-n}r^{-n}\Big|\leq\underset{n=1}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}(|a_{n}|^{2}r^{2n}+|a_{-n}|^{2}r^{-2n})\\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|G_{\pm}(re^{i\theta})|^{2}d\theta-|a_{0}|^{2}\end{split}

Hence,

(3.1)

4\pi|a_{0}|^{2}\leq\int_{0}^{2\pi}|G_{\pm}|^{2}d\theta

By substituting this into inequality (
2.11
), and considering
|F_{\pm}|=|G_{\pm}|^{2}

(3.2)

\int_{0}^{2\pi}r^{2}\Delta|F_{\pm}|d\theta\geq 2\int_{0}^{2\pi}|F_{\pm}|d\theta

Equality holds, for any
r\in(R_{0},R_{1})

4\pi|a_{0}|^{2}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}|G_{\pm}|^{2}d\theta,

where
\int_{0}^{2\pi}|G_{\pm}|^{2}d\theta=2\pi\underset{n=-\infty}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}|a_{n}|^{2}r^{2n}
. Hence,
\underset{n=-\infty}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}|a_{n}|^{2}r^{2n}=2|a_{0}|^{2}
, for

. Note that,
\underset{n<0}{\sum}|a_{n}|^{2}z^{2n}

\underset{n\geq 0}{\sum}|a_{n}|^{2}z^{2n}

consider the following function:

f(z)=\begin{cases}&\underset{n\geq 0}{\sum}|a_{n}|^{2}z^{2n}\hskip 45.52458pt\text{on}\ D_{R_{1}}\\
&2|a_{0}|^{2}-\underset{n<0}{\sum}|a_{n}|^{2}z^{2n}\ \ \text{on}\ \mathbb{C}\backslash D_{R_{0}}\end{cases}

is entire and bounded, thus it is constant. It follows that both

\ell_{\Sigma}(h)>\ell_{C,\epsilon}(h)
for
h\in[h_{-}+\epsilon,h_{+})
. Letting
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
implies
\ell_{\Sigma}(h)>\ell_{C}(h)
for
h\in(h_{-},h_{+})
. An identical argument applies when
h_{0}=h_{+}
.

Let
h_{*}\in(h_{-},h_{+})
and define the function
A_{\Sigma,h_{*}}
on
[h_{*},h_{+})
by

A_{\Sigma,h_{*}}(h)=\mathcal{H}^{2}(\Sigma\cap\{h_{*}\leq x_{3}<h\})

and the function
A_{C,h_{*}}
similarly.

The co-area formula implies that

\frac{d}{dh}A_{\Sigma,h_{*}}(h)=\int_{\{x_{3}=h\}}\frac{1}{|\nabla_{\Sigma}x_{3}|}

By the Cauchy- Schwarz inequality:

\frac{d}{dh}A_{\Sigma,h_{*}}(h)\geq\frac{\ell_{\Sigma}^{2}(h)}{\int_{\Sigma_{h}}|\nabla_{\Sigma}x_{3}|}=\frac{\ell_{\Sigma}^{2}(h)}{F_{3}}

One has equality if and only if
\frac{1}{|\nabla_{\Sigma}x_{3}|}
and
|\nabla_{\Sigma}x_{3}|
are linearly dependent, so are both constant. This is the case on the catenoid
C
, so using the above estimate for length:

\frac{d}{dh}A_{\Sigma,h_{*}}(h)>\frac{\ell_{C}^{2}(h)}{F_{3}}=\frac{d}{dh}A_{C,h_{*}}(h)

By integrating, we find that
\frac{d}{dh}A_{\Sigma,h_{*}}(h)>\frac{d}{dh}A_{C,h_{*}}(h)
for
h\in[h_{*},h_{+})
. Letting
h_{*}\rightarrow h_{-}
proves the theorem for the catenoid waist
C
described above. Additionally, Theorem 1.1 in
[
1
]
establishes the result for the maximally symmetric marginally stable waist of the catenoid spanning
\Omega
, denoted as
C_{\Omega}
.
∎
