Neuregulin 4‐dependent MAPK amplification mediates urate‐induced β‐cell dysfunction
Authors: Yunyang Wang, Kun Zhang, Zhiyuan Li, Shuhui Hu, Yiru Zhang, Yulei Zhao, Xiaohan Liu, Jie Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and ObjectivesClinical research indicates an association between hyperuricemia (HU) and diabetes; however, the precise impact of soluble serum urate on glucose metabolism is still unclear. Using a novel spontaneous HU mouse model, this study seeks to characterize glucose metabolism and elucidate the associated molecular mechanisms.MethodsWe employed targeted disruption of the Uricase (Uox) gene and established an original mouse model exhibiting a robust spontaneous HU phenotype. To seek the candidate gene(s) in the proapoptosis process in pancreatic β cells, we analyzed the microarray data of the isolated islets from Uox knockout (KO) mice and wild‐type counterparts. Apoptosis levels and expression of associated proteins in MIN6 cells transfected with candidate siRNA were quantified by flow cytometry and Western blotting, respectively.ResultsMale Uox‐KO mice exhibited glucose intolerance without concomitant insulin resistance. TUNEL staining showed an elevated apoptosis level in pancreatic tissues of male Uox‐KO mice. Microarray analysis identified neuregulin‐4 (Nrg4) as a potential mediator of hyperuricemia (HU)‐induced β‐cell apoptosis. In vitro studies using the mouse β‐cell line MIN6 demonstrated that urate upregulates Nrg4 expression and triggers apoptosis, with Nrg4 being essential for urate‐induced cell death. Mechanistically, urate promotes pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis by elevating Nrg4, which subsequently amplifies urate‐induced mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) activation.ConclusionOur findings collectively establish that HU disrupts glucose homeostasis and induces pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis via Nrg4 upregulation and subsequent amplification of the MAPK signaling cascade.

INTRODUCTION

Hyperuricemia (HU), characterized by elevated serum urate (SU) concentrations (≥7.0 mg/dL or 416.5 μmol/L in both sexes) 1 , is experiencing a concerning rise in prevalence within China, reaching an estimated 17.4% (95% CI: 15.8–19.1%) in the general population, with notably higher rates among males (22.7% [95% CI: 20.2–25.4%]) 2 . Numerous epidemiological and clinical studies have linked HU to the development of glucose intolerance or diabetes mellitus 3 , 4 , 5 , however, no causal relationship has been concluded even in mendelian randomization studies 6 . Impaired glucose tolerance (IGT), an early marker of prediabetes 7 , correlates with increased cardiovascular mortality 8 . Notably, community‐based studies reveal SU levels as a strong, independent predictor of IGT, even after accounting for other metabolic risk factors 9 . Type 2 diabetes mellitus pathogenesis involves multiple factors, including insulin resistance linked to obesity, impaired insulin secretion, and β‐cell mass loss primarily through apoptosis. Nevertheless, the precise role and mechanisms by which SU disrupts glucose homeostasis remain poorly defined, partly due to the scarcity of suitable animal models for exploring urate‐glucose metabolism interactions.

To address this gap, we developed a spontaneous HU mouse model featuring Uricase (Uox) gene deficiency, characterized by stable, chronically elevated SU levels 10 . This model provides a valuable platform for investigating glucose metabolism alterations. While studies have implicated pathways like insulin IRS2/PI3K/AKT signaling 11 and IRS/PI3K/Akt signaling 12 in HU‐associated insulin resistance, pathways specifically mediating HU‐induced β‐cell apoptosis are less explored. Therefore, the current study aimed to: (1) elucidate the relationship between HU and glucose intolerance, and (2) delineate the specific apoptotic pathway involved, utilizing our spontaneous HU mouse model.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Uricase (Uox) knockout mice and their wild‐type (C57BL/6J background) littermates were generated as previously detailed 10 . This study was approved by the Animal Research Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University. Mice were maintained under specific pathogen‐free (SPF) conditions at 22°C under a 12‐h light/dark cycle, provided ad libitum access to standard rodent chow and sterile water.

Blood biochemistry and insulin measurements

Biochemical indicators including SU, total cholesterol, triglycerides, and high‐ and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol levels were quantified immediately from serum collected from anesthetized, spontaneously breathing mice 13 using an automatic biochemical analyzer (Toshiba). Fasting and random glucose levels were monitored by tail bleeding daily with a glucometer (ACCU‐CHEK® Inform, Roche Pharmaceuticals). Fasting plasma insulin was measured using an Ultra‐Sensitive Mouse Insulin ELISA Kit (catalog no. 90080; Crystal Chem Inc.). Diabetes was defined as random blood glucose ≥16.7 mmol/L.

Glucose tolerance test and insulin tolerance test

Following an 8‐h fast, mice received an intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of D‐glucose (2 g/kg body weight) for GTT. Blood glucose was measured at 0, 15, 30, 60, and 120 min postinjection using glucometers. After a 4‐h fast, mice were administered insulin (Humulin R; Eli Lilly, 1 unit/kg, i.p.) for ITT. Blood glucose was monitored at the same time points as GTT.

Glucose‐induced insulin release in vivo

After a 6‐h fast, mice received glucose (2 g/kg, i.p.). Blood samples were collected from the tail vein at 0, 15, 30, and 60 min postinjection. Plasma insulin concentration was determined using the Ultra‐Sensitive Mouse Insulin ELISA Kit.

Pathology analysis

Pancreata were harvested from euthanized mice, weighed, and fixed in ice‐cold 10% formalin for 30 min, followed by paraffin embedding and sectioning (5 μm). Serial sections underwent hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC) using anti‐insulin rabbit polyclonal antibodies (Abcam, ab181547; 1:200). Islets apoptosis were analyzed by the terminal deoxynucleotidyl TUNEL technique according to the manufacturer's instructions (catalog no. 11684795910; Roche). β‐cell fractional area was determined by quantifying the percentage of insulin‐positive pancreas area as a total of the full pancreas area for each section, followed by averaging of six sections per mouse. Images were analyzed using the Pannoramic Digital Slide Scanner.

Islets isolation

Pancreatic islets were isolated using a modified enzymatic digestion protocol 14 , 15 . Briefly, mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation. Under sterile conditions and stereomicroscopic guidance, the abdomen was cleaned with 75% ethanol. A laparotomy exposed the abdominal cavity. The common bile duct near the duodenum was ligated, followed by retrograde cannulation and slow perfusion with 3 mL ice‐cold collagenase IV solution (Sigma‐Aldrich, #C5138S; 0.5 mg/mL in Hanks' balanced salt solution) to distend the pancreas. The excised pancreas was digested at 37°C for 11 min. Islets were purified by gentle vortexing in Histopaque‐1,077 (Sigma‐Aldrich, #10771).

Microarrays

RNA isolation and quantification

Total RNA was extracted from isolated islets using an RNeasy micro kit (QIAGEN, #74004) following the manufacturer's protocol. RNA integrity was verified using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2,100; only samples with 260/280 nm ratios between 1.8 and 2.1 and 28S/18S ratios between 1.5 and 2.0 were processed.

Library preparation

PolyA+ RNA was enriched from total RNA (100–1,000 ng), reverse‐transcribed into double‐stranded cDNA, and labeled using the Low Input Quick Amp Labelling Kit (One‐Color, Agilent, #5190‐2305). Labeled cRNA (600 ng) was hybridized to Agilent SurePrint G3 Mouse Gene Expression 8×60K Microarrays using the Gene Expression Hybridization Kit (Agilent, #5188–5242).

Data analysis

Feature Extraction software 10.7 (Agilent) was used for data extraction. Normalized array values (log2‐transformed) with fold changes ≥2.0 or ≤0.5 and P < 0.05 were considered significantly altered. Raw data underwent quantile normalization. Differential expression analysis was performed using Limma and agilp packages in R (v3.4.1).

Association analysis in genome‐wide association study (GWAS)

LocusZoom picture of regional association to SU levels in Europeans from the Kottgen and colleagues' study 16 , drawn by LocusZoom (http://locuszoom.sph.umich.edu/). The x‐axis and y‐axis correspond to the physical position on chromosome 15 and the strength of association to SU levels in the −log10 scale of the P values, respectively. The color of the markers reflects the linkage disequilibrium (LD, measured in r 2) for each dot.

Cell culture and transfection

The mouse pancreatic β‐cell line MIN6 (Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences) was cultured in DMEM containing 5 mM glucose, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 50 μmol/L β‐mercaptoethanol (Invitrogen), at 37°C under 5% CO2. Cell viability following soluble urate exposure (0, 5, 10, 15 mg/dL for 24 or 48 h) was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit‐8 (Beyotime, #C0038) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, MIN6 cells (2 × 105/well) were seeded in 96‐well plates and treated with urate.

siRNA targeting mouse Nrg4 (Sigma‐Aldrich) or scrambled control siRNA was transfected into MIN6 cells (2 × 105/well) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (3 μL/well, Life Technologies). Mouse Nrg4 cDNA was PCR‐amplified and cloned into the XhoI and BamHI sites of pcDNA3.1 (kindly provided by Biomedical Engineering Laboratory, Peking University). Plasmid transfections were performed using Lipofectamine LTX (3 μL/well, Life Technologies). Transfection efficiency was confirmed 24 h post‐transfection by qRT‐PCR.

Flow cytometry

MIN6 cells' apoptosis was analyzed using the FITC Annexin‐V Apoptosis Detection Kit (catalog no. 14085; Abcam) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, single‐cell suspensions (2 × 105/well) were washed twice with staining buffer, and then stained with 5 μL Annexin‐V‐FITC and 5 μL propidium iodide (PI) for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. A total of 10,000 events were evaluated per group (n = 3), and cellular debris was omitted from the analysis. Cell fluorescence was determined by flow cytometry using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and the results were analyzed using FlowJo (version 10.4.1) software.

RNA extraction and qRT‐PCR analysis

Mouse islets were pulverized in liquid nitrogen, and total RNA was isolated using trizol reagent (Roche Pharmaceuticals). cDNA was prepared by reverse transcription (catalog no. RR037A; Takara‐Bio), and real‐time PCR assays were carried out with a LC480 Light Cycler (Roche) under the following conditions: 94°C for 2 min and 40 cycles of 94°C for 5 s, 60°C for 15 s and 72°C for 31 s. The threshold cycle (Ct) was determined and used to calculate ΔCT values. The ΔΔCt (2−ΔCt) was used to calculate relative mRNA expression, with each measurement performed in triplicate. Primer sequences are shown in Table S1.

Western blot assay

MIN6 cells were lysed in M‐PER mammalian protein extraction reagent (Thermo Scientific). About 50 μg proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane. After membranes were blocked with 5% milk for 30 min, they were probed with various primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated goat secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, and visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence kit (Thermo Scientific). Primary antibodies were included as anti‐total p38 MAPK (catalog no. 9212; dilution at 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology), anti‐phospho‐p38 MAPK (catalog no. 9216; dilution at 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology), anti‐total ERK1/2 (catalog no. 9102; dilution at 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology), anti‐phospho‐ERK1/2 (catalog no. 9106; dilution at 1:2000, Cell Signaling Technology), anti‐mTOR (catalog no. 2972; dilution at 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology), and anti‐β‐actin (catalog no. ab8226; dilution at 1:2000, Abcam). Each measurement was performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (v10.0). Comparisons between two groups employed Student's t‐test. Multiple group comparisons utilized one‐way anova followed by Dunnett's post hoc test vs the control group. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

RESULTS

Urate impairs glucose tolerance via comprising β‐cell function in the absence of insulin intolerance or dyslipidemia

Our established Uox‐KO mouse model faithfully recapitulates human HU 10 . To minimize interference from gender factors, all experiments used male mice. Assessment of glucose homeostasis revealed no significant difference in body weight between age‐matched Uox‐KO and WT mice (Figure 1a). SU levels were markedly and stably elevated in Uox‐KO mice from 6 to 14 weeks of age compared with WT controls (Figure 1b). HU did not alter fasting or random blood glucose levels during this period (Figure 1c,d). However, Uox‐KO mice exhibited significant glucose intolerance, evidenced by higher blood glucose concentrations at 15 and 30 min during GTT and a significantly increased area under the curve (AUC) (Figure 1e). Insulin tolerance tests (ITT) confirmed the absence of insulin resistance in Uox‐KO mice (Figure 1f).

(e) After 6 h of starvation, mice were subjected to glucose tolerance test (GTT) with 2 g/kg glucose (males, 10‐week‐old, n = 13, 15 for Uox‐KO mice and WT mice separately). (f) After 4 h fasting, 1 units/kg insulin was administrated intraperitoneally to mice for insulin tolerance test (ITT) (males, 10‐week‐old, n = 8 in each group). To determine if the observed glucose intolerance stemmed from β‐cell dysfunction, in vivo glucose‐stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) was assessed. Following glucose injection (2 g/kg, i.p.), Uox‐KO mice displayed significantly lower plasma insulin levels at 15 and 30 min compared with WT mice, recovering only at 60 min (Figure 1g), indicating impaired insulin release. Collectively, these data suggest that HU‐induced glucose intolerance primarily results from compromised pancreatic β‐cell function rather than insulin resistance.

Analysis of lipid profiles revealed no differences in total cholesterol, HDL‐cholesterol, or LDL‐cholesterol between genotypes. However, serum triglycerides (TG) were significantly lower in Uox‐KO mice (Figure 1h–k).

Nrg4 is identified as a urate‐responsive gene in β cells

We next explored whether β‐cell survival contributes to HU‐induced hyperglycemia. H&E staining revealed no overt histological lesions in pancreatic islets of Uox‐KO mice (Figure 2a). To determine whether cell death contributes to reduced β‐cell mass in Uox‐KO mice, we measured islet apoptosis by TUNEL staining. The number of TUNEL‐positive β cells was higher in Uox‐KO male mice than in WT controls (Figure 2b). To probe molecular mechanisms, islets were isolated from non‐fasting male mice and subjected to microarray analysis and Western blot (Figure 2c). We found the mTOR protein levels were higher in Uox‐KO mice islets than in WT mice (Figure 2d).

Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated distinct gene expression profiles between Uox‐KO and WT islets (Figure 3a). Microarray identified 167 significantly downregulated genes (fold change ≤0.5) and 29 significantly upregulated genes (fold change ≥2.0) (P < 0.05) (Figure 3b), highlighting specific molecular changes in HU‐exposed islets. Among the top 10 differentially expressed genes ranked by fold change, Nrg4 exhibited significant upregulation (fold change = 8.99, P < 0.05; Figure 3c). Regional association plots from Köttgen et al.'s GWAS for SU levels showed strong associations and significant SNPs within the NRG4 locus (Figure 3d). Based on this human genetic association and subsequent validation, Nrg4 was selected as a candidate gene mediating urate‐induced β‐cell apoptosis. qRT‐PCR confirmed significant upregulation of Nrg4 mRNA in islets from Uox‐KO mice and in MIN6 cells treated with urate (Figure 3e), consistent with microarray findings.

Urate induces apoptosis and reduces insulin secretion in pancreatic β cells

MIN6 cells treated with urate (5, 10, 15 mg/dL) for 24 or 48 h showed concentration‐ and time‐dependent effects. Cell viability remained largely unaffected at 5 and 10 mg/dL for 24 h, but a significant 15% reduction occurred at 10 mg/dL after 48 h (Figure 4a). Under this condition (10 mg/dL urate, 48 h), insulin secretion was significantly reduced compared with controls (Figure 4b). Flow cytometry analysis confirmed urate‐induced (10 mg/dL urate, 48 h) early apoptosis in MIN6 cells (Figure 4c). Western blotting revealed increased levels of the proapoptotic marker cleaved Caspase‐3, decreased levels of the anti‐apoptotic protein Bcl‐2, and elevated Nrg4 protein expression in urate‐treated cells (10 mg/dL urate, 48 h; Figure 4d).

Nrg4 potentiates urate‐induced pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis via MAPK pathway amplification

Given Nrg4 induction by urate in β cells, we investigated its role in cell survival. Efficient knockdown (siNrg4) and overexpression (Nrg4‐OE) of Nrg4 in MIN6 cells were confirmed by qRT‐PCR; urate treatment increased Nrg4 expression in all subgroups (Figure 5a). Urate consistently suppressed insulin secretion regardless of Nrg4 modulation (Figure 5b). While urate alone significantly induced apoptosis (P < 0.001), Nrg4 overexpression dramatically amplified this effect, increasing the annexin‐V+/PI− (early apoptotic) population by 67.5% compared with urate‐treated control cells (Figure 5c).

To test the underlying mechanisms of urate‐induced apoptosis, we examined the effect of Nrg4 on the activation of two documented apoptosis‐mediated pathways: ERK1/2 MAP kinase (MAPK) and p38 MAPK 17 . While urate alone activated the ERK1/2 pathway, Nrg4 overexpression amplified the MAPK with the stimulation of urate. Nrg4‐OE markedly enhanced urate‐induced phosphorylation (activation) of both ERK1/2 and p38 MAPK (Figure 6a). These data demonstrate that: (1) Urate decreases insulin secretion primarily by inducing apoptosis via the ERK1/2 MAPK pathway; (2) Nrg4 exerts a potent proapoptotic effect specifically in the presence of urate, significantly amplifying MAPK pathway activation (Figure 6b).

DISCUSSION

The causal role of HU in diabetes pathogenesis, particularly concerning urate's link to glucose intolerance, remains debated. In vivo evidence suggests urate‐lowering therapy improves glucose tolerance and insulin resistance in diabetic db/db mice 18 , and numerous studies report positive associations between SU and IGT 9 , 19 , 20 . For instance, a large Chinese cohort study found SU levels independently predicted IGT risk, with individuals in the highest SU quartile having over twice the adjusted odds of IGT 9 .

A significant barrier to mechanistic exploration has been the lack of physiologically relevant HU animal models, as functional Uox in rodents prevents sustained HU. Our Uox‐KO mouse model overcomes this limitation. The key finding here is that sustained HU alone is sufficient to induce glucose intolerance, aligning with population studies linking elevated urate to prediabetes 21 , 22 . We observed significantly elevated blood glucose during the early phase of GTT in Uox‐KO mice. Crucially, the concomitant reduction in glucose‐stimulated insulin secretion in vivo, coupled with normal insulin sensitivity, implicates compromised β‐cell function as the primary driver of glucose intolerance in this model. Importantly, neither fasting glucose nor fasting insulin was altered in Uox‐KO mice, even with aging 23 .

To identify candidate genes accounting for urate toxicity to β cells, we identified the top 10 regulated genes from microarray data. The GWAS from Köttgen and colleagues displays a strong association between urate level and the NRG4 gene 16 , displaying multiple significant SNPs located within the NRG4 in LocusZoom picture. This human genetic link, combined with our validation of Nrg4 upregulation in vivo and in vitro, led us to focus on Nrg4 as a key player in urate‐induced β‐cell apoptosis.

Reports on circulating Nrg4 levels in diabetes are conflicting. Some studies observed elevated levels in prediabetic and diabetic patients 24 , 25 , suggesting it as a risk factor, while others found decreased levels in newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes mellitus, implying a protective role 26 . Using a mouse‐derived β‐cell line in this study, we demonstrate that urate directly induces Nrg4 expression and that forced Nrg4 overexpression dramatically enhances urate‐induced apoptosis, establishing Nrg4 as an essential mediator of urate's cytotoxic effect on β cells. Consistently, we found Ucn3 and Slc30a8, particularly important in β cells as maturation markers 27 , 28 , were listed among the top 10 differentially expressed genes downregulated in microarray analysis.

Nrg4 is originally detected in the adult pancreas and muscle and is considered the essential factor for tissue development 29 . It specifically activates epidermal growth factor receptor (ErbB4), promoting cell proliferation, inhibiting apoptosis, and improving cell energy metabolism in biological process 30 . However, in pathological conditions, Nrg4 executes its proapoptotic function in inflammatory macrophages 31 and breast cancer cells 32 . Consistently with our study, Nrg4 plays its proapoptotic role in urate‐treated cells which are considered to be in the situation of pathology with inflammatory preference.

The underlying mechanism of urate‐induced dysfunction of pancreatic β cells has not been well elucidated. Previous studies demonstrated β‐cell toxicity of soluble urate via NF‐κb‐inducible nitric oxide synthase‐nitric oxide (NF‐κb‐iNOS‐NO) signal axis, NADPH oxidase‐dependent mechanism, and activation of mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway 33 . Urate can also promote apoptosis in other tissues or cell lines such as human kidney proximal tubular cells and mature adipocytes via p38 MAPK and extracellular‐signal‐regulated kinase1/2 (ERK1/2) pathways 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 . Given MAPK pathway mediates a number of biological processes, including apoptosis 39 , we speculate MAPK signal pathway is involved in the Nrg4‐mediated urate‐induced cell death. In line with our hypothesis, we find that the enhanced Nrg4 stimulated by urate accelerates islets β cells apoptosis through downstream ERK1/2 and p38 MAPK activation indeed (Figure 6), which demonstrates a novel signal of cellular apoptosis in glucose dysmetabolism promotion. The mechanism underpinning the proapoptotic effect of Nrg4 includes signaling through both the ERK1/2 and p38 MAPK pathways with the stimulation of urate.

Limitations of this study also need to be pointed out. To meet the animal ethic, the mice were euthanized with CO2 followed by cervical dislocation prior to islet isolation. CO2, as an additional stress on the islets may magnify gene expression differences between KO and WT islets. We cautiously select the Nrg4 gene which is on the Top 2 list to minimize this limitation. Single β‐cell suspension isolated from extracted islets in vivo is an ideal protocol for apoptosis analysis by flow cytometry. Limited to the techniques in our lab, we performed the mechanism‐related experiments in mouse pancreatic β‐cell lines with soluble urate pretreatment, which mimics the status of HU in vivo well. Transcript genome sequencing is an efficient way to trace the regulated genes in pathophysiological process. Potentially, other significant upregulated and downregulated genes found by microarray in Uox‐KO islets need further investigation, which could help to delineate a full picture of the effects of urate on glycol metabolic disorders.
