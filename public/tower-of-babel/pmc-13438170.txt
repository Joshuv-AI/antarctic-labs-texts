Intricacies in Serial Electrocardiograms in a Patient With Takotsubo Syndrome Mimicking Apical Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy
Authors: John E Madias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSome patients with takotsubo syndrome reveal transiently features of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy at imaging, particularly of the apical variety; this “pseudohypertrophy” is due to myocardial edema, and can be diagnosed by the absence of electrocardiogram evidence of left ventricular hypertrophy, and particularly a transient reduction of the amplitude of QRS complexes.Keywords: “pseudohypertrophy” and takotsubo syndrome, apical hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, ECG and takotsubo syndrome, ECG in apical hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy vs. takotsubo syndrome, takotsubo syndrome

The interesting case report by Vögeli et al. [1], provides the opportunity to delve in some of the intricacies of the associated electrocardiogram (ECG) changes of patients with takotsubo syndrome (TTS) with an occasional present or absent (i.e., like in the present case) but suspected or mimicking apical hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (aHCM). The presented patient had suffered a classic apical morphological pattern of stress‐triggered TTS, and her initial ECG revealed the hallmarks of TTS, characterized by diffuse profoundly inverted T‐waves and a prolonged QTc with the 2D transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) showing a left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction (LVEF) of 35% with apical akinesis; at follow up TTE at 4 weeks the patient revealed normalization of the LV function with a LVEF of 70% and marked concentric apical hypertrophy with morphological features consistent with an “ace of spades” appearance, suggestive of aHCM [1], which did not make sense considering that the patient's initial TTE did not disclose this appearance. A follow‐up cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (cMRI) at 3 months revealed a normal LV function with a LVEF of 57%, no myocardial hypertrophy, and no myocardial edema (ME) at T2 mapping, while the T1 mapping showed an increased extracellular volume of 52% [1], which I will attribute it to still persisting ME, a common feature of TTS. Thus, it was determined that the reported patient represented a case of TTS with the features of complicated “pseudohypertrophy”, originally described in 2013 [2], and followed by relevant commentaries and case reports [3, 4, 5, 6], discussing this association. The ECG at the 4‐week follow‐up revealed normalization of the QTc prolongation, and increase in the amplitude of the QRS complexes (or alleviation of the attenuation QRS complexes) (Table 1), noted in the admission ECG, a feature associated with TTS, and attributed to ME [7]. Something else that could be helpful in the early exclusion of aHCM in cases similar with the presented patient, is that this condition is associated with ECG features of LV hypertrophy with sometimes enormous R‐waves in leads V3 and V4 and giant inverted T‐waves [8]. It will be of interest to confirm whether a possibly available previous ECG of the patient revealed ECG QRS amplitudes similar to the one recorded at the 4‐week follow‐up ECG, rather than the admission ECG. In conclusion, the presented case [1] was of a patient with apical TTS with subacute morphological features, mimicking aHCM, a form of “pseudohypertophy” [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]; the attenuated ECG QRS amplitudes [7] may be used to exclude masquerading aHCM, in patients presenting with TTS and complicating “pseudohypertophy” due to ME.
