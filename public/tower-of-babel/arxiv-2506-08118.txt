Operator theoretic measure of causality in linear dynamical systems
Ankit Srivastava, Louis Cattafesta, Scott Dawson

Abstract
This paper presents an operator-theoretic framework (Linear Operator Causality Analysis, LOCA ) for analyzing causality in linear dynamical systems, focusing on fluid mechanics. We demonstrate that the matrix exponential of the linearized differential equations fundamentally encodes the causal relationships between system modes at any future time. We further develop a global measure of causality that quantifies the presence and extent of global causality across all time horizons. This approach provides a physics-based alternative to statistical and information-theoretic causality measures such as Granger causality and Transfer Entropy. Unlike these data-driven techniques, which infer causality from time-series data, LOCA leverages the linearized governing equations, yielding a more rigorous and interpretable measure of causal interactions in deterministic systems. We establish that, under appropriate conditions, Granger causality and Transfer Entropy effectively measure the squared magnitude of the matrix exponential. Moreover, our causal framework reveals fundamental connections to key system properties such as controllability, observability, and graph-theoretic transitive closure. To complement this operator-based approach, we introduce a data-driven methodology akin to Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) that estimates causal connections directly from time series data by approximating the matrix exponential. LOCA also mitigates common issues in data-driven causality analyses, such as misleading inferences due to correlated variables or state truncation. To validate our methodology, we apply it to linearized Couette flow, demonstrating how our framework captures both direct and indirect causal interactions among flow structures. Through this example, we highlight the advantages of our approach, including its robustness to correlation-induced biases and its ability to identify causally significant modes. Ultimately, this framework provides a powerful tool for analyzing causality in fluid flows, offering new insights into modal interactions and the propagation of perturbations in dynamical systems.

Introduction

Fluid dynamics is characterized by complex interactions across a wide range of spatial and temporal scales. The intricate coupling between different modes and scales leads to instabilities, flow separation, and turbulence, which are challenging to predict and control. Understanding the causal relationships governing these interactions is essential for advancing fluid dynamics research and its applications.

The concept of causality has been a subject of intense scrutiny across multiple disciplines in science and engineering. The counterfactual approach, developed in philosophy
(
Lewis 1973
)
and statistics
(
Rubin 1974
)
, posits that an event A causes event B if B would not have occurred had A not happened. In contrast, probabilistic frameworks in epidemiology
(
Suppes 1973
)
and computer science
(
Pearl 2014
;
Pearl et al. 2000
)
define causality in terms of probability distributions, where A causes B if the probability of B given A is greater than the probability of B alone. The field of economics has contributed the notion of Granger causality
(
Granger 1969
)
, which asserts that A causes B if past values of A improve predictions of B beyond what past values of B alone can predict, a concept particularly useful for time-series data. Social sciences have employed structural equation modeling
(
Bollen 2014
)
, a statistical technique that combines factor analysis and multiple regression to test complex causal hypotheses. Epidemiology has utilized the Bradford Hill criteria
(
Hill 1965
)
to evaluate causal evidence in observational studies. At the same time, biology and social sciences have emphasized mechanism-based causality
(
Machamer et al. 2000
)
, focusing on identifying and understanding the underlying processes that connect cause and effect. This interest has also led to the development of a range of causality quantification metrics. Some examples are ATE (Average Treatment Effect)
(
Imbens & Rubin 2015
)
, Granger causality
F
-test
(
Granger 1969
)
and the related PDC (Partial Directed Coherence)
(
Baccalá & Sameshima 2001
)
, the Causal Impact method
(
Brodersen et al. 2015
)
, estimators based upon the Rubin Causal Model
(
Imbens & Rubin 2015
)
, metrics coming from Information theory such as Directed Information (DI)
(
Massey et al. 1990
)
and Transfer Entropy (TE)
(
Schreiber 2000
)
, and those coming from Ecology and climate science such as Cross Convergent Mapping (CCM)
(
Sugihara et al. 2012
)
to detect causality in complex, nonlinear systems. In addition, there are dedicated methods for causality analysis in deterministic systems in physics and signal processing which employ the Hilbert transform and the associated Kramers-Kronig relations
(
de Kronig 1926
;
Kramers 1927
;
Srivastava 2020
)
.

Identifying and quantifying causal relationships in fluid-dynamic systems represent a crucial frontier in understanding complex flow phenomena. Early attempts to identify causality in fluid systems relied primarily on statistical and information-theoretic methods. Whereas statistical and information-theoretic methods infer causality from observed data, our novel framework quantifies causal influence directly from the linearized governing equations. This operator-theoretic formulation, a physics-based alternative, not only complements or guides data-driven analyses but also opens up new avenues for understanding complex fluid systems.
The pioneering work of
Favre et al. 1957
introduced space-time correlations to fluid dynamics, providing a framework for identifying potential cause-effect relationships through time delays between signals. Cross-correlation analysis identifies time delays between signals to suggest potential cause-and-effect relationships. While conceptually simple and computationally efficient, this approach only captures linear relationships and cannot distinguish between direct causal links and common-cause relationships.
Bendat & Piersol 2011
applied the coherence function, which measures the frequency-dependent correlation between signals, to fluid dynamics problems. This technique proved valuable for analyzing aeroacoustic phenomena and identifying frequency bands where different flow variables exhibit potential causal relationships.

The past fifteen years have witnessed advancements in applying causality concepts to fluid dynamics, with methods increasingly borrowed from statistics, information theory, and dynamical systems theory.
Tissot et al. 2014
used Granger causality to study the dynamics and the energy redistribution between scales and components in wall-bounded turbulent flows.
Liang & Lozano-Durán 2016
demonstrated, through rigorous causality analysis, that the streak-roll regeneration cycle in turbulent channel flows comprises two mutually causal subcycles interconnected by wall-normal and spanwise velocity interactions.
Lozano-Durán et al. 2019
showed that energy-containing eddies in wall turbulence exhibit self-similar causal interactions across buffer and logarithmic layers regardless of eddy size.
Bae et al. 2019
used transfer entropy to show that the self-sustaining process in the logarithmic layer of wall-bounded turbulence is predominantly unidirectional rather than cyclic, with log-layer motions primarily maintained by energy extraction from the mean shear that governs the system’s dynamics and time-scales.
Lozano-Durán et al. 2020b
demonstrated through cause-and-effect analysis based on interventions in direct numerical simulations that transient growth, particularly the Orr/push-over mechanism induced by spanwise variations of the base flow, is sufficient for sustaining realistic wall turbulence even when exponential instabilities, neutral modes, and parametric instabilities of the mean flow are suppressed.
Lozano-Durán & Arranz 2021
proposed an information-theoretic approach, leveraging Shannon entropy, to quantify causality with applications to turbulence.
Martínez-Sánchez et al. 2022
applied conditional transfer entropy to identify causal relationships among reduced-order model modes, revealing vortex-breaker modes as the primary drivers of large-scale structures in flow around a wall-mounted square cylinder.
Massaro et al. 2023
demonstrated the effectiveness of transfer entropy in capturing directional causality in turbulent dynamical systems, highlighting its sensitivity to Markovian order.
Lopez-Doriga et al. 2024
applied linear and nonlinear Granger causality analyses to turbulent duct flows, revealing that linear interactions dominate causality within symmetry groups.

In contrast, nonlinear effects govern interactions between symmetric and antisymmetric coherent structures.
Martínez-Sánchez et al. 2024
introduced SURD, a method that decomposes causality into synergistic, unique, and redundant components, providing a more robust measure of causal inference in complex nonlinear systems. In other contexts, linear operator-based methods help capture structures and dynamics of even highly turbulent flows
(
Schoppa & Hussain 2002
;
Del Alamo & Jimenez 2006
;
McKeon & Sharma 2010
;
Abreu et al. 2020
)
, and various statistical and information-theoretic approaches to causality have been applied to the linearized Navier-Stokes equations
(
Cerbus & Goldburg 2013
;
San Liang & Lozano-Durán 2016
;
Lozano-Durán et al. 2020a
;
Lozano-Durán & Arranz 2022
;
Martínez-Sánchez et al. 2022
;
Lopez-Doriga et al. 2024
)
.

While these methods provide valuable insights, they may not fully capture the indirect causal pathways inherent in high-dimensional fluid systems. To address this gap, we propose using properties of the underlying governing equations and linearizations to deduce causal interactions from a physics-based, rather than data-driven, perspective. Data-driven causality analyses typically seek causal relationships between predefined spatial functions. By contrast, we will show how operator-driven analysis can enable the identification of optimally causal basis functions. In particular, we will highlight how existing methods, while useful, can be limited in their ability to capture the full complexity of fluid systems, thereby underscoring the need for our proposed approach.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we formulate three definitions of causality under our LOCA framework - immediate causality, delayed causality, and global causality. Section 3 explores the relationships between our causality framework and other established methods, including controllability, observability, and data-driven methods like Granger causality and Transfer Entropy. Section 4 develops a data-driven approach for inferring causal relationships directly from time-series data. Section 5 demonstrates the practical application of our methodology to linearized planar (Couette) flow, highlighting its advantages over traditional causality metrics. Finally, Section 6 summarizes our findings and discusses potential directions for future research.

2
Definitions and quantification of causality

Here, we provide an unambiguous notion for quantifying causality, which is particularly useful for situations where the underlying governing equations are known. Suppose that the system under consideration has a state vector
{\bm{u}}
, with dynamics governed by

\dot{\bm{u}}={\bm{f}}({\bm{u}}),

(1)

where
{\bm{f}}
is a (nonlinear) function governing the dynamics of the system. Evolving this system from an initial condition
{\bm{u}}^{0}={\bm{u}}(t=0)
over a time horizon
\tau
, we can define the finite-time propagation operator

{\bm{u}}^{\tau}={\bm{g}}^{\tau}({\bm{u}}^{0}).

(2)

Suppose we wish to analyze the causal mechanisms present in this system. In that case, we may analyze the effect of a specific component
u_{j}
upon another state
u_{i}
over a specified time horizon. Mathematically, if we denote the perturbation in state
u_{j}
at a certain time
t_{0}
as
\partial u_{j}^{0}
, then we are interested in quantifying the change
\partial u_{i}^{t_{0}+\tau}
where
\tau>0
. In other words, we assume that the causal influence that a perturbation of state
j
has on the value of state
i
at a time horizon of
\tau
time units in the future is proportional to

G^{\tau}_{j\to i}=\frac{\partial u_{i}^{\tau}}{\partial u_{j}^{0}}=\frac{\partial g^{\tau}_{i}({\bm{u}}^{0})}{\partial u_{j}^{0}}.

(3)

This work will focus on linearized dynamics about some base state
\overline{{\bm{u}}}
. In this case, we instead consider the linearized equivalent to equation
1

\dot{{\bm{u}}}^{\prime}=\left.\frac{\partial{\bm{f}}}{\partial{\bm{u}}^{\prime}}\right|_{{\bm{u}}=\overline{{\bm{u}}}}{\bm{u}}^{\prime}\vcentcolon={\mathsfbi{A}}{\bm{u}}^{\prime},

(4)

where
{\bm{u}}^{\prime}={\bm{u}}-\overline{{\bm{u}}}
is the deviation of
{\bm{u}}
from the base state. Henceforth, we will omit the
\cdot^{\prime}
notation and assume that the system’s state is relative to this base state.
In this case, equation
2
becomes

{\bm{u}}^{\tau}=e^{{\mathsfbi{A}}\tau}{\bm{u}}^{0},

(5)

and the causality definition given in equation
3
simplifies to

G^{\tau}_{j\to i}=\left[e^{{\mathsfbi{A}}\tau}\right]_{ij}.

(6)

While our defined notion of causality is proportional to
G^{\tau}_{j\to i}
, we will ultimately modify this definition to account for the statistics of the state variables, motivated by the notions of causality introduced in section
3.1
. In particular, if we assume that the dynamics given in equation
5
also have an error term (which could be due to unmodeled nonlinear dynamics)

{\bm{u}}^{\tau}=e^{{\mathsfbi{A}}\tau}{\bm{u}}^{0}+{\bm{\epsilon}},

(7)

then we define a modified
F
-statistic associated with the strength of the causality from state
u_{j}
to state
u_{i}
by

F_{j\to i}=\frac{\left[e^{{\mathsfbi{A}}\tau}\right]^{2}_{ij}\sigma_{j}^{2}}{\sigma_{\epsilon,i}^{2}},

(8)

where
\sigma_{j}^{2}=var(u_{j})
and
\sigma_{\epsilon,i}^{2}=var(\epsilon_{i})
are the variances of state variable
j
and the error in the equation for variable
i
, respectively.

The state dimension is typically very large in discretized fluid flow models. Therefore, we are often interested in assessing causality not between state components directly but rather between the coefficients of a set of spatial basis functions, which only span a subspace of the original space of state variables.
For example, if we had a set of orthonormal spatial basis functions (e.g. POD modes) assembled as columns of a matrix
{\mathsfbi{U}}
with corresponding temporal coefficients
{\bm{c}}={\mathsfbi{U}}^{*}{\bm{x}}
, then the equivalent to equation
7
would be

{\bm{c}}^{\tau}=e^{\tilde{\mathsfbi{A}}\tau}{\bm{c}}^{0},

(9)

where the equivalent linearized continuous and discrete time operators in POD coefficient coordinates are given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathsfbi{A}}

\displaystyle={\mathsfbi{U}}^{*}{\mathsfbi{A}}{\mathsfbi{U}},

(10)

\displaystyle e^{\tilde{\mathsfbi{A}}\tau}

\displaystyle={\mathsfbi{U}}^{*}e^{{\mathsfbi{A}}\tau}{\mathsfbi{U}}.

(11)

A reduced-order model for these dynamics can be obtained by selecting a subset of these basis functions (such as the leading
r
POD modes), which, when assembled as columns of a matrix
{\mathsfbi{U}}_{r}
, yield the transformations

\displaystyle{\bm{c}}

\displaystyle={\mathsfbi{U}}_{r}^{*}{\bm{x}},

(12)

\displaystyle\tilde{{\mathsfbi{A}}}

\displaystyle={\mathsfbi{U}}_{r}^{*}{\mathsfbi{A}}{\mathsfbi{U}}_{r}.

(13)

Immediate causality
: Our notion of causality is connected to the classical idea of counterfactual causality
(
Lewis 1973
)
where causality is understood by insisting that if we deem that
c_{j}
causes
c_{i}
, then any change in
c_{j}
at some time
t=t_{0}
should change the trajectory of
c_{i}
for
t>t_{0}
. As far as immediate causality is concerned, this means that if
\dot{c}_{i}(t_{0})\neq 0
when
{c}_{j}(t_{0})
is perturbed, then the future trajectory of
c_{n}(t)
will also diverge. Since this local relationship is mediated by
\mathsfbi{A}
through
\dot{\mathsfbi{c}}=\mathsfbi{A}\mathsfbi{c}
, immediate causality from mode
m
to mode
n
is ensured if
A_{nm}\neq 0
and in this sense, immediate causality is the same as the direct coupling encoded in the dynamical matrix
\mathsfbi{A}
. For example, in the following system:

\mathsfbi{A}=\begin{bmatrix}-1&1&-1\\
0&-2&2\\
0&0&-0.02+\mathrm{i}\\
\end{bmatrix}

(14)

there are immediate causal connections in the directions
2\rightarrow 1
,
3\rightarrow 1
, and
3\rightarrow 2
but no immediate causal connections in the reverse directions.

Delayed causality
: Going beyond immediate causality, over larger time horizons, counterfactual causality is embedded in the partial derivative
G^{\tau}_{j\to i}
- we call this delayed causality. Mathematically, if we denote the perturbation in mode
c_{j}
at a particular time
t_{0}
as
\partial c_{j}^{t_{0}}
, then we are interested in quantifying the change
\partial c_{i}^{t_{0}+\tau}
where
\tau>0
. Since the systems under consideration are linear and time-invariant, we can instead talk about the quantities
\partial c_{i}^{\tau},\partial c_{j}^{0}
, and we are interested in quantifying the time delayed partial derivative
G^{\tau}_{j\to i}=\partial c_{i}^{\tau}/\partial c_{j}^{0}
. If
\partial c_{i}^{\tau}/\partial c_{j}^{0}=0
, then we can say that there is no causal influence of the mode
c_{j}
on the state of mode
c_{i}
at the future time
\tau
. This time-delayed partial derivative can be explicitly calculated for the linear systems under consideration and is given by the matrix exponential
\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}t}
. Since
\partial c_{i}^{\tau}/\partial c_{j}^{0}=[\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}\tau}]_{ij}
, the matrix
[\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}t}]
encapsulates the essential causal relations among the modes at any future time.

Global causality
: One can ask a further question - is there any causal influence of one variable over another? Since the matrix exponential is time-delay dependent, the answer to this question cannot be provided by it. Discussions of causality based on a fixed time lag can be quite misleading. Consider, for example, the matrix
\mathsfbi{A}
defined as:

\mathsfbi{A}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(15)

This matrix is nilpotent (specifically,
\mathsfbi{A}^{3}=0
), ensuring convergence of the matrix exponential series. Since
\mathsfbi{A}
is nilpotent, we can compute
\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}t}
by truncating the series expansion at
k=2
terms:

\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}t}=\mathsfbi{I}+\mathsfbi{A}t+\frac{(\mathsfbi{A}t)^{2}}{2!}.

(16)

Substituting the powers of
\mathsfbi{A}
, we obtain:

[\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}t}]_{31}=\frac{t^{2}}{2},

(17)

showing a quadratic dependence on
t
. This implies that a causality metric based on
[\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}t}]_{31}
, computed for a time lag
t\ll 1
, will show no causal relation from mode 1 to 3. However, a strong causal relation exists between modes 1 and 3 for
t
on the order of 1
\mathrm{s}
and dominates for
t\gg 1
.

For the following case:

\mathsfbi{A}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
1&0&0\\
-1&1&0\\
\end{bmatrix},

(18)

it can be shown that:

[\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}t}]_{31}=\frac{t^{2}}{2}-t=\frac{1}{2}t(t-2).

(19)

In this situation,
\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}t}
exhibits no causality if the time lag is very small or close to 2. In the next section, we show that a metric to measure global causality is
\mathsfbi{M}=(\alpha\mathsfbi{I}-|\mathsfbi{A}|)^{-1}
where
\alpha
is some constant greater than the spectral radius of
|\mathsfbi{A}|
.

Causally disconnected modes
: We can also determine the conditions under which some modes of a dynamical system are forever causally disconnected from other modes. We are interested in finding those scenarios where some off-diagonal elements of
\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}t},\mathsfbi{M}
are 0 — this would indicate the presence of causally disconnected modes in the system. One such scenario occurs when the matrix
\mathsfbi{A}
is in block diagonal form. In this case,
\mathsfbi{A}^{k}
is also block diagonal for each
k
from which it follows that
\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}t}
is block diagonal for all
t
. In this scenario,
|\mathsfbi{A}|
is also block diagonal, and so is
\alpha\mathsfbi{I}-|\mathsfbi{A}|
and it is well known that the inverse of a block diagonal matrix is also block diagonal. Thus,
\mathsfbi{M}
is block diagonal as well.

Connections with other methods

We now discuss connections between the analysis and definitions provided in §
2
. We start by discussing other notions of causality (typically applied in a data-driven manner) in §
3.1
before discussing connections with notions from graph theory in §
3.2
and observability and controllability of linear systems in §
3.3
.

3.1
Connection with Granger causality and transfer entropy

Here, we first discuss the connection between our operator-theoretic causality framework and the widely used statistical approach of Granger causality. We will show that under appropriate conditions, the Granger causality
F
-statistic scales with the squared magnitude of the matrix exponential element that quantifies the causal influence from one state to another.

We focus on pairwise conditional Granger causality from state
c_{j}
to state
c_{i}
in an
m
-dimensional discrete-time system with a single time lag. Let the system state at time steps
t_{n}=n\,\Delta t
be

{\bm{c}}=\left[c_{1}(t_{n}),\dots,c_{m}(t_{n})\right]^{T}
. Here, we use
{\bm{c}}
to denote the variables under consideration, given that in practice, for high-dimensional fluid flows, one must typically use a low-dimensional representation of the full system state, e.g., mapping the full state to POD coordinates via equation
12
.

We will focus on pairwise conditional Granger analysis with a single time lag, which quantifies the causality from one variable (here taken to be
c_{j}
) to another (
c_{i}
), assuming knowledge of all other state variables.
The analysis
proceeds by identifying from data
unrestricted
and
restricted
models.
The
unrestricted
model for
c_{i}(t_{n})
includes past values of
c_{j}
:

c_{i}(t_{n})=\sum_{p=1}^{m}A_{ik}\,c_{k}(t_{n-1})+\epsilon^{u}_{i}(t_{n}),

(20)

where
A_{ik}
are coefficients representing the influence of
c_{k}
at the previous time step on the current value of
c_{i}
, and
\epsilon^{u}_{i}(t_{n})
is the residual. The
restricted
model omits
c_{j}
:

c_{i}(t_{n})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{m}A_{ik}\,c_{k}(t_{n-1})+\epsilon^{r}_{i,j}(t_{n}).

(21)

If excluding
c_{j}
significantly degrades the prediction of
c_{i}
, we conclude
c_{j}

Granger-causes

c_{i}
. Note that in the restricted model (unlike the unrestricted case), the residual
\epsilon^{r}_{i,j}
will generally depend on
j
.
To evaluate the extent of causality, the approach taken by Granger causality analysis is to compare the Residual Sum of Squares (RSS) for the restricted (RSS
r
) and unrestricted (RSS
u
):

\mathrm{RSS}^{r}=\sum_{n}\bigl[\epsilon^{r}_{i,j}(t_{n})\bigr]^{2},\quad\mathrm{RSS}^{u}=\sum_{n}\bigl[\epsilon^{u}_{i}(t_{n})\bigr]^{2}.

(22)

through the
F
-statistic:

F=\frac{\mathrm{RSS}^{r}-\mathrm{RSS}^{u}}{\mathrm{RSS}^{u}\,/\,\bigl(N-(m+1)\bigr)},

(23)

where the numerator reflects the single parameter (
p=1
) introduced by
c_{j}
,
N
is the total number of data points, and the denominator’s
(m+1)
represents the total parameter count in the unrestricted model. If
F
exceeds a critical threshold, we infer Granger causality from
c_{j}
to
c_{i}
.

Now, we connect this statistical approach to our dynamical systems framework. Suppose that in continuous time, the system is locally governed by
\dot{\mathsfbi{c}}(t)=\mathsfbi{A}\,\mathsfbi{c}(t)
, so over a time step
\Delta t
,

\mathsfbi{c}(t_{n})=\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}\,\Delta t}\,\mathsfbi{c}(t_{n-1}).

(24)

In this case, the regression coefficients
A_{ik}
correspond to elements of the matrix exponential
[\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}\Delta t}]_{ik}
. Then the contribution of
c_{j}
to
c_{i}
over one step is given by
[\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}\Delta t}]_{ij}\,c_{j}(t_{n-1})
. When
c_{j}
is
excluded
from the model, this omitted term appears in the restricted-model residual:

\epsilon^{r}_{i,j}(t_{n})=[\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}\Delta t}]_{ij}\,c_{j}(t_{n-1})+\epsilon^{u}_{i}(t_{n}).

(25)

In equation
25
, we make the assumption that the retained terms in the restricted model take the same values as the equivalent terms in the unrestricted model.
Hence,

\sum_{n}\bigl[\epsilon^{r}_{i,j}(t_{n})\bigr]^{2}=\sum_{n}\Bigl([\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}\Delta t}]_{ij}\,c_{j}(t_{n-1})+\epsilon^{u}_{i}(t_{n})\Bigr)^{2}.

(26)

Expanding,

\begin{split}\sum_{n}\bigl[\epsilon^{r}_{i,j}(t_{n})\bigr]^{2}&=([\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}\Delta t}]_{ij})^{2}\sum_{n}\bigl(c_{j}(t_{n-1})\bigr)^{2}\\
&\quad\;+\;2\,[\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}\Delta t}]_{ij}\sum_{n}c_{j}(t_{n-1})\,\epsilon^{u}_{i}(t_{n})\;+\;\sum_{n}\bigl[\epsilon^{u}_{i}(t_{n})\bigr]^{2}.\end{split}

(27)

Thus, the difference between restricted and unrestricted RSS is

\begin{split}\mathrm{RSS}^{r}-\mathrm{RSS}^{u}&=([\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}\Delta t}]_{ij})^{2}\sum_{n}\bigl(c_{j}(t_{n-1})\bigr)^{2}\\
&\quad\;+\;2\,[\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}\Delta t}]_{ij}\sum_{n}c_{j}(t_{n-1})\,\epsilon^{u}_{i}(t_{n}).\end{split}

(28)

If
c_{j}
is uncorrelated with
\epsilon^{u}_{i}(t_{n})
, the cross-term vanishes on average, leaving

\mathrm{RSS}^{r}-\mathrm{RSS}^{u}\approx([\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}\Delta t}]_{ij})^{2}\sum_{n}\bigl(c_{j}(t_{n-1})\bigr)^{2}.

(29)

Under the assumptions of stationarity and large
N
,
\sum_{n}(c_{j}(t_{n-1}))^{2}\approx N\,\sigma_{c_{j}}^{2}
. We also expect
\sum_{n}[\epsilon^{u}_{i}(t_{n})]^{2}\approx N\,\sigma_{\epsilon,i}^{2}
, where (as in equation
8
)
\sigma_{\epsilon,i}
is the variance of the error in the equation for the
i
-th state. Thus,

\begin{split}F&\approx\frac{([\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}\Delta t}]_{ij})^{2}\,N\,\sigma_{c_{j}}^{2}/1}{N\,\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}/(N-(m+1))}\\
&\;\;\approx\;\;\frac{([\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}\Delta t}]_{ij})^{2}\,\sigma_{c_{j}}^{2}(N-(m+1))}{\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}},\end{split}

(30)

showing that the Granger causality
F
-statistic scales with
\bigl([\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}\Delta t}]_{ij}\bigr)^{2}
. Since the primary focus of this work is to perform causality analysis directly using the linearized dynamics rather than data, we can exclude the term proportional to the number of timesteps of data
(N-(m+1))
to obtain an expression equivalent to equation
8
. It is this quantity that will be considered henceforth when quantifying causality.

We can thus conclude that subject to the assumptions of this analysis (most notably that the restricted and unrestricted models share the same coefficients except for those involving the excluded variable), our notion of causality defined in equation
8
agrees with Granger analysis.
Since transfer entropy and Granger causality are equivalent for linear Gaussian processes
(
Barnett & Bossomaier 2012
)
, an extension of this result is also that transfer entropy, under appropriate restrictions, is also equivalent, with the causality dependent on
\bigl([\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}\Delta t}]_{ij}\bigr)^{2}
. Note that in transfer entropy, causality is typically quantified using a log-likelihood quantity rather than an
F
-statistic, where the equivalent to equation
8
can be shown to be

F^{(L)}_{j\to i}=\log\left(\frac{1+[\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}\Delta t}]^{2}_{ij}\sigma_{c_{j}}^{2}}{\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}}\right).

(31)

In some works, this quantity is also used within the Granger framework
(
Barnett & Seth 2014
;
Barnett & Seth 2015
)
.

3.2
Connection with Graph Theory and global causality metrics

We note that even if
A_{mn}=0
, indicating no direct coupling from mode
n
to mode
m
at any given instance, indirect coupling can arise through interactions with intermediate modes. This indirect influence becomes evident over longer time horizons due to the cumulative effect of the system’s interconnectedness. This becomes explicit in the power series expansion of the matrix exponential:

\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}t}=\mathsfbi{I}+\mathsfbi{A}t+\frac{1}{2}\mathsfbi{A}^{2}t^{2}+\frac{1}{6}\mathsfbi{A}^{3}t^{3}+\dots

(32)

Each term
\mathsfbi{A}^{k}t^{k}/k!
in this series represents the cumulative influence of interactions over time. Importantly, higher-order terms
\mathsfbi{A}^{k}
for
k\geq 2
describe the indirect coupling pathways through which modes can influence one another via intermediate modes. If
A_{ij}=0
, there is no direct influence of mode
j
on mode
i
. However, consider the case where mode
j
influences mode
k
(
A_{kj}\neq 0
), and mode
k
influences mode
i
(
A_{ik}\neq 0
). This creates an indirect pathway from mode
j
to mode
i
, which is captured by the second-order term
\mathsfbi{A}^{2}
. Specifically:

(\mathsfbi{A}^{2})_{ij}=\sum_{l}A_{il}A_{lj}

(33)

This summation includes all possible intermediate modes
l
through which mode
j
can indirectly influence mode
i
and includes the non-zero term
A_{ik}A_{kj}
. Higher-order terms
\mathsfbi{A}^{3}
,
\mathsfbi{A}^{4}
, etc., describe indirect interactions through longer chains of intermediate modes, progressively accounting for more complex indirect pathways. For smaller time horizons
t\ll 1
, the higher order powers of
\mathsfbi{A}
may be ignored, in which case, there is no causal connection between modes
i,j
. As
t
increases, these higher-order terms can no longer be ignored. Over longer time horizons, the contributions of higher-order terms in the matrix exponential become more significant, especially in systems with strong interactions between modes. Thus, even when there is no direct coupling (
A_{ij}=0
), indirect effects will become observable through the cumulative influence of intermediate pathways.

This picture can be viewed from the perspective of graph theory where the nodes are the coefficients
c_{i}
and the
\mathsfbi{A}
matrix is related to the adjacency matrix. To compare analogous concepts in graph theory, we will denote the adjacency matrix with the same symbol
\mathsfbi{A}
. In graph theory, an
adjacency matrix

\mathsfbi{A}
is a square matrix used to represent a finite graph
(
West 2001
)
. The elements of the matrix
\mathsfbi{A}
, denoted
A_{ij}
, describe the connections between nodes (or vertices) of the graph. Specifically,
A_{ij}=1
if there is a direct edge from node
i
to node
j
, and
A_{ij}=0
otherwise. The adjacency matrix is symmetric for an undirected graph, meaning
A_{ij}=A_{ji}
for all
i,j
. In the case of directed graphs, the matrix is generally asymmetric, with
A_{ij}=1
only if there is a directed edge from
i
to
j
.

The concept of
transitive closure
(first introduced in relational mathematics and set theory
(
Tarski 1941
)
) of a graph is closely related to the adjacency matrix. The transitive closure of a graph captures not only the direct connections between nodes but also the indirect connections that can be reached through a sequence of edges. In terms of the adjacency matrix, the transitive closure is the matrix that indicates whether a path exists between any two nodes, regardless of how many intermediate nodes are traversed. Formally, the transitive closure can be represented as the reachability matrix, which is obtained by considering powers of the adjacency matrix, such that if there is a path of any length from node
i
to node
j
, the corresponding element in the reachability matrix will be non-zero. This concept is crucial for understanding how information or influence can propagate through a network over multiple steps, as in many physical and social systems.

The transitive closure of a graph can also be described using the Boolean Neumann series
(
Warshall 1962
)
:

\mathsfbi{T}=\mathsfbi{I}\lor\mathsfbi{A}\lor\mathsfbi{A}^{2}\lor\mathsfbi{A}^{3}\lor\cdots=\bigvee_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathsfbi{A}^{k}.

(34)

Moreover, we can use this as a template for defining a metric corresponding to causality over time. The idea is that the successive powers of
\mathsfbi{A}
encode hierarchies of indirect causality. A sum such as
\mathsfbi{T}=\sum_{k}\mathsfbi{A}^{k}=(\mathsfbi{I}-\mathsfbi{A})^{-1}
(assuming convergence) is a candidate for global causality. However, it suffers from the possibility of negatives canceling out the positives. In other words, if
T_{ij}=0
, one cannot definitively conclude that
A^{k}_{ij}=0
for all
k
and rule out a causal connection. A better candidate is
\mathsfbi{S}=\sum_{k}|\mathsfbi{A}^{k}|
where
|.|
is understood as elementwise absolute value. It is clear that if
S_{ij}=0
, then
A^{k}_{ij}=0
for all
k
, and there must not exist any causal connection from
j
to
i
. There does not always exist a simple expression for
\mathsfbi{S}
. However, we can use the submultiplicative property of matrices to show that
\mathsfbi{S}\leq(\mathsfbi{I}-|\mathsfbi{A}|)^{-1}
where the inequality is understood elementwise. Equality holds if
\mathsfbi{A}
is strictly non-negative. Thus, we can compute

\mathsfbi{M}=(\mathsfbi{I}-|\mathsfbi{A}|)^{-1}

(35)

as the global causality metric. A zero value at any location in this matrix implies that the corresponding element of
\mathsfbi{S}
is zero thus definitively ruling out causality. It can be shown that if the spectral radius
\rho(|\mathsfbi{A}|)>1
, then the series which was used to form the matrix
\mathsfbi{M}
does not converge. To overcome this issue, we will consider rescaling the time. If
\mathsfbi{A}
is associated with a continuous time system of linear differential equations, we consider a new timescale
\tau=t/a
, for
a>0
with
a\neq 1
. We then have

\frac{d\mathsfbi{c}}{ad\tau}=\mathsfbi{A}\mathsfbi{c},

(36)

and so

\frac{d\mathsfbi{c}}{d\tau}=a\mathsfbi{Ac}.

(37)

Since our determination of causality should be independent of the timescale chosen, we can equivalently consider
\mathsfbi{A}_{2}=a\mathsfbi{A}
, with the associated matrix

\mathsfbi{M}_{2}=(\mathsfbi{I}-a|\mathsfbi{A}|)^{-1}.

We can thus equivalently consider any of the family of operators

\mathsfbi{M}(\alpha)=(\alpha\mathsfbi{I}-|\mathsfbi{A}|)^{-1},

As long as
\alpha>\rho(|\mathsfbi{A}|)
, the series for
\mathsfbi{M}
converges, and it serves as an appropriate measure of global causality.

3.3
Connection with observability and controllability

The current system, augmented with a control input and an output, is:

\dot{\mathsfbi{c}}=\mathsfbi{A}\mathsfbi{c}+\mathsfbi{B}\mathsfbi{u},

(38)

\mathsfbi{y}=\mathsfbi{C}\mathsfbi{c}.

(39)

The
controllability matrix
is defined as

\mathcal{C}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathsfbi{B}&\mathsfbi{A}\mathsfbi{B}&\mathsfbi{A}^{2}\mathsfbi{B}&\cdots&\mathsfbi{A}^{n-1}\mathsfbi{B}\end{bmatrix},

(40)

and the
observability matrix
is given by

\mathcal{O}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathsfbi{C}\\[11.38109pt]
\mathsfbi{C}\mathsfbi{A}\\[5.69054pt]
\mathsfbi{C}\mathsfbi{A}^{2}\\[5.69054pt]
\vdots\\[5.69054pt]
\mathsfbi{C}\mathsfbi{A}^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}.

(41)

Suppose that
M_{ij}=0
for some indices
i\neq j
. As discussed earlier, this implies:

\left[\mathsfbi{A}^{k}\right]_{ij}=0,\quad\forall\,k\geq 0.

(42)

Assume further that the control input is applied only to the
j
th state; that is, only
B_{j}\neq 0
. Then, the
i
th component of each term in the controllability matrix is given by

\left[\mathsfbi{A}^{k}\mathsfbi{B}\right]_{i}=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\left[\mathsfbi{A}^{k}\right]_{il}B_{l}=\left[\mathsfbi{A}^{k}\right]_{ij}B_{j}=0\quad\forall\,k.

(43)

This means that the entire
i
th row of
\mathcal{C}
is zero, implying that
\mathcal{C}
is rank deficient and the system is not fully controllable. Thus, lack of global causality for a mode
j
results in the loss of controllability in the system if the control input is only applied to the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
mode. Similarly, assume that the measurement is taken solely from the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
state so that

\mathsfbi{C}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\cdots&C_{i}&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}.

(44)

Then, for every
k
we have

\mathsfbi{C}\mathsfbi{A}^{k}=C_{i}\left[\mathsfbi{A}^{k}\right]_{ij}=0,

(45)

which shows that the contribution from the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
state to the output is never observed. Consequently, the observability matrix
\mathcal{O}
loses rank, and the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
state is unobservable. This connection between causality and controllability/observability also extends to the Gramians, which are defined by:

\mathsfbi{W}_{o}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}^{*}\tau}\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}\tau}\mathrm{d}t,

(46)

\mathsfbi{W}_{c}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}\tau}\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}^{*}\tau}\mathrm{d}t,

(47)

It is well known that if the controllability and observability matrices are not full rank, then the corresponding Gramians are singular. Therefore, there is a direct connection from the lack of global causality (
M_{ij}=0
) to the singularity of the Gramians. Consequently, the metric
\mathsfbi{M}
can serve as an alternative to detecting controllability and observability, and the elements of
\mathsfbi{M}
could be useful for guiding appropriate locations for sensor and actuator placement.

These Gramians can also be used to find reduced-order models that optimally retain observability and controllability of the system via balanced truncation
(
Moore 1981
)
. In particular, here state truncation is performed after first transforming the coordinates such that
\mathsfbi{W}_{o}
and
\mathsfbi{W}_{c}
are diagonal and identical, with ordered diagonal entries corresponding to the Hankel singular values of the system. For high-dimensional systems, this can be implemented without needing to explicitly form the associated operators using forward and adjoint simulations through balanced proper orthogonal decomposition
(
Rowley 2005
;
Willcox & Peraire 2002
)
. We refer the reader to these references for further details regarding these methods.

4
Causal inference from data

Since causality is embedded in the system matrix
\mathsfbi{A}
, it is interesting to see if the elements of this matrix can be ascertained from data. This can be framed as an optimization problem, and there are likely multiple ways of achieving this objective, each with its advantages and disadvantages. Here, we propose one method of doing so, which is based on the Dynamic Mode Decomposition
(
Schmid & Ecole 2008
)
technique. Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) is a data-driven technique used to approximate the underlying dynamics of a complex system from time-series data. In the context of a linear dynamical system, DMD allows us to estimate the system matrix, which can then be used to infer causal relationships between the components. To estimate the system matrix
\mathsfbi{A}
, we generate data by simulating the system for multiple randomly chosen initial conditions
\mathsfbi{c}_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, where the initial conditions are drawn from a complex-valued distribution. The system is then evolved forward, yielding a time series for each component of
\mathsfbi{c}(t)
. The numerical integration provides a discrete set of time points,
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{m}
, at which the state vector is recorded, forming a dataset.

Let
\mathsfbi{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
denote the data matrix, where each column of
\mathsfbi{X}
represents the state vector at a specific time step. We define two data matrices,

\mathsfbi{X}_{1}=[\mathsfbi{c}(t_{1}),\mathsfbi{c}(t_{2}),\ldots,\mathsfbi{c}(t_{m-1})],\quad\mathsfbi{X}_{2}=[\mathsfbi{c}(t_{2}),\mathsfbi{c}(t_{3}),\ldots,\mathsfbi{c}(t_{m})],

(48)

which captures the system’s state at consecutive time steps. The goal of DMD here is to find an approximate linear map
\mathsfbi{A}_{\mathrm{DMD}}
such that

\mathsfbi{X}_{2}\approx\mathsfbi{A}_{\mathrm{DMD}}\mathsfbi{X}_{1}.

(49)

To solve for
\mathsfbi{A}_{\mathrm{DMD}}
, we use the least-squares solution given by

\mathsfbi{A}_{\mathrm{DMD}}=\mathsfbi{X}_{2}\mathsfbi{X}_{1}^{\dagger},

(50)

where
\mathsfbi{X}_{1}^{\dagger}
denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of
\mathsfbi{X}_{1}
. This approach yields the matrix
\mathsfbi{A}_{\mathrm{DMD}}
that best approximates the linear mapping from
\mathsfbi{X}_{1}
to
\mathsfbi{X}_{2}
in a least-squares sense. This estimated matrix
\mathsfbi{A}_{\mathrm{DMD}}
gives us a relation between
\mathsfbi{c}(t_{m+1})
and
\mathsfbi{c}(t_{m})
.
Accordingly,
\mathsfbi{A}_{\mathrm{est}}=(\mathsfbi{A}_{\mathrm{DMD}}-\mathsfbi{I})/\Delta t
provides a first-order estimate of the continuous-time system matrix
\mathsfbi{A}
associated with the underlying dynamics.
We can use the elements of
\mathsfbi{A}_{\mathrm{est}}
to now perform causality analysis by computing
\mathsfbi{M}=(\mathsfbi{I}-|\mathsfbi{A}_{\mathrm{est}}|)^{-1}
.

from

To illustrate the process, we consider specific systems governed by a different
\mathsfbi{A}
matrix. We then generate data
\mathsfbi{c}^{(i)}(t)
based on random initial conditions
\mathsfbi{c}^{(i)}_{0}
for
i=1,2,3...N
. In the following examples, we generally take
N\approx 500
different initial conditions, and for each initial condition, we simulate for 100 seconds (
\Delta t=0.1\,\mathrm{s}
). This data, in totality, is then used to calculate
\mathsfbi{A}_{\mathrm{DMD}}
and then
\mathsfbi{A}_{\mathrm{est}},\mathsfbi{M}
. Causality matrices are then presented based upon normalized values of
|\mathsfbi{M}|
. The matrices considered are:

(51)

These results for two different stable
\mathsfbi{A}
matrices are presented in Fig. (

a) corresponds to an upper triangular
\mathsfbi{A}^{(1)}
matrix with eigenvalues
-1,-2,-3
(see Eq.

). The DMD estimate results in a matrix with eigenvalues

, giving a normed error of
0.0018
in the estimate of the eigenvalues. The
\mathsfbi{M}

. Fig. (

\mathsfbi{A}^{(2)}

) and it is interesting to note that even though
A^{(2)}_{21}=A^{(2)}_{32}=0
,
M^{(2)}_{21},M^{(2)}_{32}\neq 0
indicating indirect channels of global causality. Although not shown here, we have tested our framework for larger systems, where it emerges as a highly data-efficient technique for gauging causality.

5
Application: Linearized Couette flow

We now test the performance and behaviour of the proposed causality analysis methodology on a fluid flow example. We consider incompressible flow between two parallel plates of infinite extent, with the flow driven by relative plate motion.
We assume the plates are in the
x-z
plane, at
y=\pm=1
, with plate motions
U(y=\pm 1)=\pm 1
. The laminar solution gives the classic linear Couette velocity profile in the
x
-direction, given by

U(y)=y.

(52)

While we focus exclusively on this profile, we find that other channel flow velocity profiles (e.g. planar Poiseuille flow) produce comparable results.
The equations of motion linearized about this base velocity profile can be expressed in terms of the wall-normal velocity (
v
) and vorticity (
\eta
). Homogeneity in the streamwise (
x
) and spanwise (
z
) directions allow for the use of Fourier transforms in these directions, such that we can independently consider Fourier modes of the form

v(x,y,z,t)=\hat{v}(y,t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k_{x}x+\mathrm{i}k_{z}z}

(53)

\eta(x,y,z,t)=\hat{\eta}(y,t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k_{x}x+\mathrm{i}k_{z}z},

(54)

where
k_{x}
and
k_{z}
are the streamwise and spanwise wavenumbers. The linearized Navier–Stokes equations can be expressed as

\mathsfbi{E}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{v}\\
\hat{\eta}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}L_{vv}&0\\
L_{v\eta}&L_{\eta\eta}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{v}\\
\hat{\eta}\end{pmatrix}=\mathsfbi{L}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{v}\\
\hat{\eta}\end{pmatrix},

(55)

where the constituent operators are given by

\mathsfbi{E}=\begin{pmatrix}\Delta&0\\
0&\mathsfbi{I}\end{pmatrix},

(56)

L_{vv}=\mathrm{i}k_{x}U\Delta-\mathrm{i}k_{x}U_{yy}-\frac{1}{\Rey}\Delta^{2},

(57)

L_{\eta\eta}=\mathrm{i}k_{x}U-\frac{1}{\Rey}\Delta,

(58)

L_{\eta v}=\mathrm{i}k_{z}U_{y}.

(59)

Here
\Rey
is the Reynolds number (based on channel half-height and maximum baseflow velocity), and
\Delta=D_{yy}-k_{x}^{2}-k_{z}^{2}
is the Laplace operator, with
D_{yy}
denoting the second derivative in the
y
direction.
U_{y}
and
U_{yy}
denote the first and second derivatives of the baseflow in
y
(though for the Couette flow case considered here
U_{yy}=0)
. The corresponding discrete-time system of equations can be expressed as

\begin{pmatrix}\hat{v}\\
\hat{\eta}\end{pmatrix}_{k+1}=\mathsfbi{A}_{d}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{v}\\
\hat{\eta}\end{pmatrix}_{k},

(60)

where the discrete-time dynamics
\mathsfbi{A}_{d}
corresponding to a timestep (
\Delta t
) are given by

\mathsfbi{A}_{d}=\exp\left[(\Delta t)\mathsfbi{E}^{-1}\mathsfbi{L}\right].

(61)

The no-slip boundary condition for the velocity perturbations, along with the continuity equation, results in Dirichlet boundary conditions for wall-normal vorticity fluctuations (
\hat{\eta}(y=\pm 1)=0
), and clamped boundary conditions for wall-normal velocity fluctuations (
\hat{v}(y=\pm 1)=D_{y}\hat{v}(y=\pm 1)=0
).
As an aside, note that the imposition of no-slip boundary conditions makes
\mathsfbi{E}
invertible, making equation
61
well-posed.
We chose this velocity-vorticity formulation primarily because it clearly shows the distinct underlying physical mechanisms associated with the linearized dynamics. These equations are discretized with a Chebyshev pseudospectral collocation method.
We analyze causality by considering the equivalent discrete-time operator for various time horizons. We set
\Rey=2000
, and
k_{z}=k_{z}=1
.

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we have developed an operator-theoretic framework for analyzing causality in linear dynamical systems, particularly those arising in fluid mechanics. Our approach leverages the fundamental insight that for systems in the canonical form
\dot{\mathsfbi{c}}=\mathsfbi{A}\mathsfbi{c}
, the matrix exponential
\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}t}
encodes causal connections between modes at any future time
t
. In contrast, the matrix
\mathsfbi{M}=(\alpha\mathsfbi{I}-|\mathsfbi{A}|)^{-1}
provides a measure of global causality integrated over all time. This framework offers a physics-based alternative to statistical and information-theoretic approaches, such as Granger causality and Transfer Entropy, which do not inherently utilize the governing equations available in many deterministic systems.

We have established that causality in linear systems can be characterized at three distinct levels: (1) immediate causality, determined by non-zero off-diagonal elements of
\mathsfbi{A}
; (2) delayed causality, captured by the matrix exponential
\mathrm{e}^{\mathsfbi{A}t}
at various time horizons; and (3) global causality, represented by the matrix
\mathsfbi{M}
. Our analysis reveals that even when immediate causality is absent (i.e., when
A_{ij}=0
), delayed or global causality can emerge through indirect pathways involving intermediate modes, a phenomenon elegantly captured by the power series expansion of the matrix exponential.

The operator-theoretic causality framework demonstrates deep connections with several fundamental concepts in dynamical systems theory. We have shown that the absence of global causality between specific modes (
M_{ij}=0
) directly implies limitations in both controllability and observability when control inputs or measurements are restricted to those modes. This relationship extends to the controllability and observability Gramians, offering potential guidance for optimal sensor and actuator placement in practical applications.

We have demonstrated that under appropriate conditions, both Granger causality and Transfer Entropy effectively measure the squared magnitude of the matrix exponential. However, our analysis of linearized Couette flow reveals important distinctions between these statistical approaches and our operator-based method, particularly in systems with correlated variables. When applied to uncorrelated POD modes, all methods converge to similar results, confirming our established theoretical relationship. Notably, our analysis reveals that the most causally significant interactions can be highly sensitive to the time horizon considered and may involve lower-energy modes that significantly impact the dynamics of higher-energy structures. This finding underscores the importance of considering the full range of modes when analyzing causal mechanisms, as truncated representations may omit crucial causal pathways. For practical applications where the system matrix is unknown, we have developed a data-driven approach based on Dynamic Mode Decomposition to estimate causal connections directly from time series data. This method effectively bridges the gap between purely theoretical analysis and purely data-driven approaches, allowing for robust causal inference in complex systems with minimal data requirements.

Overall, our operator-theoretic framework for causality provides a powerful analytical tool for understanding the complex modal interactions in fluid flows and other linear dynamical systems. By leveraging the underlying physics rather than relying solely on statistical correlations, this approach offers deeper insight into the causal mechanisms driving system behavior, with significant implications for predicting, controlling, and designing fluid systems.
