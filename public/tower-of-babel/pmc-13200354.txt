Hypertension in middle childhood
Authors: Mirjam Močnik, Zala Hrovat, Sonja Golob Jančič, Martina Filipič, Mladen Crnobrnja, Nataša Marčun Varda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHypertension is increasingly recognized among children. The etiology and presentation depend crucially on the child’s age. In infancy, secondary causes clearly predominate; however, this is less the case as the child grows. Our study aimed to evaluate children with hypertension aged 6 to 11 years, where secondary causes are not as frequent as in younger age, and primary/obesity-related hypertension is not as pronounced as in adolescence.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed data from 784 children with consistently elevated blood pressure confirmed by ambulatory blood pressure monitoring. Among these, we analyzed the frequency of hypertension presence and etiology in children aged 6 to 11 years, along with anthropometric measures, target organ damage, and laboratory results.Results22.2% of children with hypertension belonged to the group of children aged from 6 to 11 years. A total of 167 children were analyzed retrospectively. In 18%, the cause was at least possibly secondary, most commonly of renal etiology, where a straightforward association can not be confirmed or excluded. In primary hypertension, excess weight was frequent, with 76.7% overweight, which significantly affected clinical presentation. Liver steatosis and left ventricular hypertrophy were more common in children with obesity-related primary hypertension (p < 0.001 for both variables).ConclusionsThe occurrence of hypertension between 6 and 11 years is mostly due to essential hypertension, and overweight is the most critical risk factor. Target organ damage is significantly associated with being overweight rather than having hypertension, which exacerbates cardiovascular risk in middle childhood. Some comorbidities associated with obesity are less pronounced in middle childhood than in adolescence, providing an early opportunity to implement preventive measures. In middle childhood, cases of secondary hypertension are rare but not uncommon. Especially, children with kidney disease are at increased risk for the development of hypertension and should be screened.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06817-3.Keywords: Hypertension, Middle childhood, Etiology, Presentation

Introduction

The prevalence of high blood pressure (BP) and hypertension in childhood has been increasing in recent decades, mainly in line with the rise of obesity, which is a significant risk factor for the development of high BP in childhood [1, 2]. Diagnosis can be challenging, as (ab)normal BP values ​​vary with age, sex, and height. Furthermore, there are different guidelines and recommendations for diagnosing arterial hypertension, including the recommendations from the European Society of Hypertension in 2016 [3], the American Academy of Pediatrics in 2017 [4], and the Canadian Hypertension Guidelines in 2020 [5]. According to all three guidelines, a diagnosis should be made only after three measurements taken at different time periods. Recently proposed Consensus Panel’s agreement proposes only two visits to confirm the diagnosis and recommends home blood pressure monitoring. In children up to the age of 16, the definition is based on tables by sex, age, and height. Above that, the cut-off is 130/85 mmHg [6]. The epidemiological data are therefore based on different definitions used. The prevalence of sustained hypertension (3.89%) differs significantly from that of occasional hypertension (11.85%) in the pediatric population, indicating important variations in the global picture and highlighting the importance of measurement on multiple occasions [7]. More importantly, an increasing number of recent studies and recommendations support the use of 24-hour blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) to support the diagnosis, especially in excluding so-called white-coat hypertension [6, 8–10].

Correct and timely diagnosis is essential, as an increasing number of studies have shown that high BP in childhood is associated with cardiovascular disease in adulthood [11]. The prevalence of hypertension in childhood is estimated at 2–5% [12] and is divided into primary, without apparent cause, and secondary with a clearly defined cause (renal, endocrinological, neurological diseases, pharmacological agents, vascular diseases) being the dominant form in early childhood, i.e., up to 6 years of age [13, 14]. On the contrary, in primary hypertension several different risk factors can be accountable for, and are divided into non-modifiable (sex, family history, ethnicity) and modifiable factors (obesity, sodium intake, obstructive sleep apnea, physical activity, the protective role of breastfeeding, pre-, peri- and neonatal factors, smoking exposure, traumatic experiences, and psychological stress) [14, 15]. With the increase in obesity in recent decades, it has become clear that the prevalence of elevated BP is also increasing, especially in adolescents, where regular BP measurement is recommended for screening purposes in cases of obesity [16, 17].

The group of children in the middle childhood, defined as being between 6 and 11 years of age [18], is less clearly defined in terms of hypertension etiology and presentation, as they are usually not considered a separate group but rather part of a broader group of pediatric patients across a wider age range [19]. In this context, research has been focused on the impact of obesity on BP throughout middle and late childhood, and less on the etiology and clinical picture of high BP in middle childhood. The aim of our study was therefore a retrospective analysis of children with hypertension in middle childhood as a separate age group, unlike in similar studies, their proportion in pediatric hypertension, and possible causes in this age group with clinical presentation.

Methods

Selection of participants

We have retrospectively extracted the data of the children who have been referred to or managed in our department between 2008 and 2023 with an underlying diagnosis of primary hypertension (ICD-10 code I10), and were aged between 6 and 11, as presented schematically in Fig. 1. In this time period, 1598 pediatric patients were referred to or managed in our institution due to hypertension under observation, and 1437 of them had ABPM at least once. We reviewed all ABPM and recorded the age at the first abnormal ABPM, which was considered in the analysis, to exclude white-coat hypertension. Of the 784 referred pediatric patients, 174 had at least one abnormal ABPM during this time period and were of the appropriate age for inclusion in our study. Next, we retrospectively collected clinical data from children in middle childhood. Some data are missing, and 7 children with substantial missing information were excluded.

Patient selection process; ABPM – ambulatory blood pressure measurement

Ambulatory blood pressure measurement

ABPM was performed over 24 h with automated measurements at 20-minute intervals during the day and 30-minute intervals during the night using standard equipment (Spacelab Healthcare®) and appropriately sized cuffs. Hypertension was confirmed in children with elevated office BP on different occasions according to the European Society of Hypertension. Next, ABPM was performed, and abnormal values above the 95th percentile of systolic/diastolic BP were identified using reference values based on height [20]. The ABPM results included average 24-hour systolic/diastolic pressures, as well as average daily and nocturnal values. At least 70% of readings had to be valid. The absence of a nocturnal drop was not considered diagnostic. The wake-sleep cycle was not formally assessed; however, with unreliable results, the ABPM was usually repeated.

Retrospective information collection

167 middle-aged children were included in the analysis, and data on sex, secondary cause, anthropometric measurements, overweight estimation according to the World Health Organization Growth Standards [21] with body mass index (BMI) calculation and percentile determination, and ABPM results with average reported values were included. Anthropometric measurements were performed in the hospital environment using validated scales and included height and weight. The evaluation included an abdominal ultrasound to assess liver steatosis, echocardiography to detect left ventricular hypertrophy as evaluated by a pediatric cardiologist, an ophthalmologic evaluation to detect hypertensive retinopathy, and laboratory results, as shown in Table 1, aiming to identify significant associated conditions and laboratory markers. In our cardiology department, the Z-score system is used for left ventricular hypertrophy. The thickness of the left ventricular walls (septum and posterior wall in diastole) with scores over + 2 is usually diagnostic for left ventricular hypertrophy, along with abnormal indexed left ventricular mass (LVMI). When results are borderline, relative wall thickness is used. In interpreting all results, other factors are being observed, such as chamber size and left ventricular function, as well as the type of hypertrophy (concentric in hypertensive patients) [22].

Selected laboratory results, included in the analysis

Additionally, we included estimation of glomerular filtration rate using CKiD U25 Creatinine-Cystatin C equation, vitamin D, homocysteine, uric acid, carotid intima media thickness (cIMT) measurement, and pulse wave velocity (PWV) measurement results (SphygmoCor®, measured radial-carotid), which were not done routinely during the whole time period, merely in the second part of the time period included, but we believe are important in the latest evaluation of a child with hypertension. This is also true for some other tests in Table 1, which explains the missing data.

Statistical analysis

SPSS Statistics (IBM, version 22 and 26) was used for statistical analyses. Descriptive statistics were used to evaluate the cohort characteristics, and normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests. Categorical variables are presented as numbers and relative percentages, while continuous variables are presented according to the result of normality tests, including mean and median, as well as standard deviation/interquartile range. The chi-square test was used to assess the differences in frequencies between selected patient groups. To determine differences between patients with primary and secondary causes, independent-samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney tests were used, depending on the normality of the data. For BMI, Z-scores were calculated, and Bonferroni adjustments were applied to the multivariate analysis when possible; in some cases this was not possible due to missing data. P-values, lower than 0.05, were considered statistically significant.

Statement of ethics

The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (protocol code UKC-MB-KME-28/24).

Results

Among all children with persistently elevated BP confirmed by ABPM, 22.2% were aged 6 to 11. Of 167 analyzed mid-aged children, 78 were boys and 89 were girls, with a median age of 9 years (interquartile range 3 years). 137 (82%) children clearly lacked a secondary cause and were classified as having primary hypertension. Another 30 children had either another clear secondary cause or a kidney disease as a possible secondary cause, as demonstrated in Table 2. In kidney disease, hypertension was observed during follow-up. In other secondary causes, hypertension was the initial presentation that led to diagnosis.

We classified children into underweight (BMI below the 5th percentile for age), normal weight, and overweight (BMI above the 85th percentile for age), as shown in Fig. 2, with the vast majority being overweight. Classification of children with hypertension in middle childhood according to weight status

The frequency of overweight was statistically higher in girls (69/89, 77.5%) than in boys (51/78, 65.4%), with a Chi-square (p < 0.001).

Laboratory results at the point of diagnosis are presented as continuous variables in Table 3. Children with primary hypertension had significantly higher weight and BMI, lower daily diastolic pressure extended to average and nocturnal values after Bonferroni correction, urea, creatinine, and homocysteine, compared to children with secondary hypertension. Also, after Bonferroni adjustment, creatinine still differed significantly between groups; meanwhile, the difference in urea was not confirmed.

Given the importance of overweight prevalence in the group with a primary cause, comparisons by weight status were also performed, as shown in Appendix 1. As expected due to the presence of obesity, there are statistically significant anthropometric differences, though children with overweight were a bit older, barely reaching statistical significance. Other differences included higher systolic pressure, barely significantly higher left cIMT, but more convincingly higher basal insulin, uric acid, liver enzymes, triglycerides, LDL, proteinuria, homocysteine, and lower HDL.

Finally, we have also examined the presence or absence of liver steatosis, left ventricular hypertrophy, and hypertensive retinopathy, as shown in Fig. 3. Among these, liver steatosis was most frequently present (15% of all patients; 2 patients with secondary cause and 23 patients with primary hypertension), followed by left ventricular hypertrophy (9.6% of all patients; 1 patient with secondary cause and 15 patients with primary hypertension). Liver steatosis and left ventricular hypertrophy were therefore more common in children with primary hypertension, as confirmed by the Chi-square test, when stratified by etiology (p < 0.001 for both variables). Additionally, they were more common in overweight children when stratified by weight percentile (p < 0.001 for both variables). Both children with hypertensive retinopathy had primary hypertension and were overweight.

Discussion

Our study aimed to characterize and evaluate hypertension in children aged 6 to 11 years, a distinct group. Younger children frequently have secondary hypertension; meanwhile, adolescents exhibit primary hypertension or obesity-associated hypertension more often. Specifically, a recent study identified older age (> 12.5 years), obesity, and serum uric acid as significant factors associated with primary hypertension [23]. Our study differs from others in analyzing the specific age group, less commonly evaluated specifically, and not strongly associated with obesity and the primary form of hypertension, but not strongly associated with secondary causes. Although superficially a steady period in a child’s life, it is marked by several profound changes, possibly affecting the development of hypertension [18]. In our study, hypertension remained significantly associated with secondary causes, especially renal etiologies. In cases of primary hypertension, overweight has been the most critical risk factor.

The estimated prevalence of hypertension in general in children varies between 2% and 5% and is rising globally, primarily due to increasing obesity [12, 24]. The predominant type in early childhood is secondary hypertension [13]. As in other studies across pediatric groups [23–25], renal etiology was the most common cause of secondary hypertension in our cohort. Therefore, in the search for a secondary cause, a kidney ultrasound shows the highest diagnostic yield [25]. Secondary hypertension was more common in children aged below 5 years than in pre-teen [26], although in our study, using age-comparable patients with 18% of demonstrable secondary causes showed a lower prevalence in this age group. Indeed, a recent systematic review of studies conducted in primary care and school settings reported a lower prevalence of secondary hypertension (3.7%) than in referral clinics (20.1%) [27]. This suggests that many cases of primary hypertension are not referred to the tertiary hypertension unit, resulting in a higher prevalence of secondary causes among those evaluated, which may also be the case in our local environment due to follow-up of patients with kidney disease. Necessary to mention that, in children with kidney disease, it is not a straightforward conclusion that hypertension is solely the result of kidney disease; therefore, the possible, indirect causality has been emphasized. However, in children with congenital anomalies of the kidneys and urinary tract (CAKUT), which were the most prevalent in our cohort of children with kidney disease with hypertension, the risk of developing hypertension increases significantly after the age of 5, which is in accordance with our higher prevalence of possible secondary causes, attributable to kidney disease in our patients [28].

In addition, recently conducted studies demonstrate a higher prevalence of hypertension in preadolescent school-going children associated mainly with obesity in this age group [29], emphasizing the increasing prevalence of hypertension due to obesity in this age group rather than due to secondary causes. Indeed, in cases of primary hypertension, the most pronounced characteristic among diagnostic measures in our study was overweight, which was higher than the general prevalence of overweight of 15–20% among 7–10 year-olds and up to 22% among 11–14 year-olds in our country [30]. In patients with a secondary cause, such as CAKUT with associated obesity, it is difficult to distinguish to what extent obesity contributes to the development of hypertension in this age group. Even in as young as 6–8 years, overweight and obesity present a vital compounding factor in hypertension development, having nearly twice the risk of developing hypertension compared to normal-weight peers [31]. Similar to other studies, in children with overweight, the increase in systolic BP predominated with statistically significantly higher values. It is important to note that in some studies, isolated systolic hypertension in young people was associated with white-coat hypertension [32], which was excluded in our cohort with ABPM. Obesity, with its metabolic changes, was frequently associated with isolated systolic hypertension [32], as confirmed in our study, in which differences in BP were not observed by cause of hypertension; meanwhile, systolic BP was elevated in the overweight group.

Kidney function markers, namely urea, creatinine, and estimated glomerular filtration rate, but not cystatin C, were slightly elevated in children with a secondary cause, probably attributed to the predominant renal secondary etiology in this age group. Interestingly, creatinine levels were not increased in the overweight group, as might be expected; however, they were observed more frequently in children after age 10 and thus were not characteristic of middle childhood [33, 34]. Overweight did expectably affect liver enzymes, associated with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) encompassing a wide range of conditions from simple fat accumulation to severe forms of liver disease [35, 36], which was also demonstrated with a higher frequency of liver steatosis within ultrasound examination among children with overweight or primary hypertension, where overweight was more frequently present. However, comparing to other studies of MASLD prevalence describing 7–14% of all children having MASLD [37] and scoring from 30 to 50% in obese adolescents [36, 38], in our cohort MASLD was present to a lower extent, confirming less pronounced effects of obesity in this age group, however, not insignificant as expected to rise further [37].

The results of lipid examination are primarily associated with overweight status, with triglycerides and LDL, as expected, being significantly higher, corresponding to lower HDL levels, confirming higher rates of combined dyslipidemia [39] in our overweight cohort. Similarly, uric acid was associated with obesity, as observed in previous studies, but did not differ by cause, unlike the recent study that, after excluding overweight and obesity, still identified uric acid as a significant factor for primary hypertension in children [23].

Homocysteine was the only laboratory parameter that differed significantly by both cause and weight status. It was higher in patients with secondary causes and in overweight patients. Homocysteine is an intermediate in methionine metabolism, considered a risk factor for several pathological disorders. In pediatrics, it is mainly associated with cardiovascular disease, obesity, and renal disease, with emerging evidence of elevated levels in autism spectrum and attention deficit disorder [40]. As both hypertension and renal disease are associated with atherosclerotic process, our results are in accordance with their prognostic value in children at cardiovascular risk, confirming their role as a potential marker for atherosclerosis in children with particular chronic diseases [41, 42].

Otherwise, we observed no differences in thyroid function, vitamin D, and parathyroid hormone (to exclude secondary hyperparathyroidism) in the investigated hypertensive children. This is particularly interesting given the increasing studies among obese children indicating thyroid dysfunction even before the development of anthropometric obesity characteristics and hyperinsulinemia [43, 44] and vitamin D deficiency [45], both apparently not dominating the effects of excess weight present in our cohort. This may be due to the younger age in our cohort, again presenting a window of opportunity to reduce the endocrinologic consequences of being overweight.

In the search for kidney damage, microalbuminuria has been most commonly assessed in adults, reflecting the notion that it likely reflects glomerular injury and increases the risk of cardiovascular disease [46]. Microalbuminuria was rarely present in our cohort; many results were not acceptable for inclusion because they were below the detection limit. Consequently, the number of included results is limited. Despite the challenge of 24-hour collection, these results yielded higher diagnostic power and revealed greater protein levels among overweight individuals, although overall within normal limits. These results, therefore, do not contribute to the general lack of consensus on microalbuminuria detection in primary hypertension in children, although research suggests a higher incidence of left ventricular hypertrophy when microalbuminuria is present [46].

The presence of hypertensive retinopathy was low, as confirmed by similar studies, despite the fact that those undergoing retinal examination were likely at higher risk [47]. The prevalence of hypertensive retinopathy increases with hypertension severity [48].

The prevalence of left ventricular hypertrophy ranges from 8 to 40% of hypertensive children [49], making the prevalence in our cohort at the lower range. However, an extensive study of children aged 6–11 years found that even in this age group, the prevalence of left ventricular hypertrophy is the highest in the group of children with both elevated BP and BMI, reaching 24.5%, and that the risk was increased to 9.45 times compared to children with both normal weight and BP [50]. Interestingly, the risk of left ventricular hypertrophy with only elevated BP in children was only 0.89, and with only obesity was 5.69, confirming that the correlation with left ventricular hypertrophy is generally stronger for the degree of obesity [50, 51] than for blood pressure [52]. The latter is also seen in our cohort, where most of the children with left ventricular hypertrophy were overweight and had primary hypertension.

PWV and cIMT were assessed to determine subclinical atherosclerosis [53], revealing no differences between children with primary or secondary hypertension. There was a statistically higher value of left cIMT in overweight children, barely reaching significance, and no differences in PWV among overweight children. The latter would be somewhat expected; however, it is not in accordance with some previous studies, which describe increased PWV in overweight children [54]. In those studies, the analyzed children were over 11, suggesting that patient age is probably even more important and that interventions in this age group are more effective.

This study has several limitations. Most of the children in our study were prepubertal; however, pubertal status was not rigorously assessed, which may have affected insulin values. In addition, our data were collected retrospectively and contained substantial missing information. In addition, our comparisons could be extended further between sex, normal-weight, and overweight children with or without a secondary cause. Still, given the small number of children with a secondary cause, we did not want to divide or further reduce the groups.

In addition, our study includes the perspective from one center, where many cases with suspicion of primary hypertension with obesity are referred to a tertiary center for further work-up. The referral with a diagnosis of primary hypertension may introduce selection bias that was tackled with a uniform approach to exclude secondary causes in all referred patients. Additionally, this was the reason we included only children with abnormal ABPM to exclude patients with white-coat hypertension, as recently recommended, since many children with obesity display abnormal office BP (and then receive referral, especially with obesity presence) with normal ABPM, which was a quite frequent finding in children with obesity [9, 10]. Need to emphasize, according to studies, the majority of patients with white-coat hypertension progress to an abnormal ABPM phenotype [55], underlining the need to follow up of children with abnormal office BP by continuing ABPM.

Conclusions

Children in middle childhood are significantly affected by primary hypertension, that was most commonly associated with being overweight, with an essential impact on comorbidities, which might be less pronounced in this age group, providing a window of opportunity to implement preventive measures. In middle childhood, children with kidney disease are at increased risk for the development of hypertension and should be screened.
