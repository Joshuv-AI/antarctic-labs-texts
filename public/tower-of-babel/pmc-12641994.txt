Impact of intrapartum cervical tear on subsequent pregnancy outcomes
Authors: Elchanan Dreyfuss, Ofer Fischer, Liron Seidman, Or Bercovich, Yarin Mash, Eran Hadar, Asaf Romano, Ohad Houri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the impact of intrapartum cervical tears on outcomes in subsequent pregnancies, with specific consideration of tear dimensions and anatomical location.DesignA retrospective cohort study of all women with intrapartum cervical tears. Primary outcomes were recurrent cervical tears and subsequent preterm birth.ResultsOn our cohort, 238 of 101,239 (0.23%) women who underwent vaginal delivery between August 2008 and May 2023, were diagnosed with intrapartum cervical tears. Among them, 116 (70%) were diagnosed with an anterior tear, 44 (26%) had a posterior tear, and 6 (4%) in both locations. Moreover, 56 (60%) had a tear of up to 2 cm, and 42 (40%) had a tear larger than 2 cm. Out of the women with cervical tears, 141 (59%) had a subsequent delivery. There was no significant difference in the rate of subsequent preterm birth (14% vs. 10%, OR 1.47 CI 95% 0.7–2.8, p = 0.26). Of the 141 who had a subsequent delivery, 107 (76%) delivered vaginally. A recurrent cervical tear had occurred only in 6 vaginal deliveries (5.4%), significantly higher compared to the overall incidence in our center (5.4% vs. 0.23%, OR 18.8 CI 95% 8.2–43.1, p < 0.01). Four out of the 6 (67%) cases were in women with a cervical cerclage. The location or the size of the cervical tear did not affect the likelihood of a recurrent tear or subsequent preterm birth.ConclusionsIntrapartum cervical tears are a significant risk factor for recurrent tears in subsequent pregnancies, yet with very low absolute incidence regardless of tear’s location and size. Most recurrences occurred in women with cervical cerclage.Keywords: Cervical tear, Risk factors, Preterm labor

Introduction

Preterm birth (PTB) stands as a significant contributor to neonatal morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Cervical insufficiency is one of the recognized pathways leading to spontaneous PTB, with cervical trauma identified as a potential risk factor. Possible etiologies of such trauma include cervical conization, loop electrosurgical excision, mechanical dilation during pregnancy termination, and obstetric cervical lacerations [3, 4].

While asymptomatic, small cervical tears (< 0.5 cm) have been observed in vaginal deliveries [5], clinically significant cervical lacerations are less frequent, complicating 0.2–4.8% of such deliveries. Substantial tears may result in serious maternal morbidity, including postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) and the need for blood transfusion [6–8]. Moreover, associations between cervical procedures and an elevated risk of cervical insufficiency and PTB have been documented [9–14]. However, there is scarce and conflicting information regarding the impact of cervical tears during labor on future obstetrical outcomes. Some suggest that intrapartum cervical lacerations may not significantly affect the course and outcome of subsequent pregnancies [7, 15], while others demonstrated a notable impact on various obstetrical outcomes, including recurrent cervical lacerations and subsequent PTB [16, 17]. Importantly, there remains a lack of information regarding the potential influence of the size and location of cervical tears.

The primary objective of our study was to investigate the implications of intrapartum cervical tears on future pregnancies. Specifically, recurrent cervical tears and PTB, while accounting for both the size and location of the cervical tear.

Methods

A retrospective cohort study was conducted in a tertiary university medical center between August 2008 and May 2023.

Study population

The cohort included all women who had a vaginal delivery (spontaneous or assisted) following 22 gestational weeks (GW) or with a neonatal birthweight of at least 500 g. We reviewed all women who were diagnosed with intrapartum cervical tear and included only those with clinically significant cervical tears, defined as those associated with abnormal vaginal bleeding as determined by the attending physician, or those requiring cervical suturing. We excluded women who delivered a non-viable fetus, nonvertex presentation, or by cesarean delivery.

As the study aimed to evaluate recurrence and outcomes in subsequent pregnancies, we compared outcomes within the same women to minimize confounding by baseline maternal factors such as cervical tissue characteristics or other unmeasured variables.

Data collection

Data were retrieved from the hospital’s computerized database. Women with cervical tears were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, (ICD-10-CM Diagnostic Code 069.2). We retrieved data regarding demographic characteristics, medical and obstetrical history (including dilatation and curettage, cervical cerclage, hysteroscopic intervention - diagnostic and operative), pregnancy course, delivery data, and immediate maternal and neonatal outcomes. In addition, by reviewing each case, we collected data about the location and size of the cervical tear, which was documented in the patient’s record. The location of the tear was classified as anterior or posterior. Tears located primarily at the lateral aspects (3 and 9 o’clock) were classified as anterior tears. In addition, Tears were further classified based on their size, namely up to or larger than 2 cm. PPH was defined as an estimated blood loss of 500 ml. During the study period, all cervical cerclages were performed using the McDonald approach.

Next, we collected data on subsequent pregnancies including the presence of cervical cerclage, cervical sonographic shortening (second-trimester cervical length less than 25 mm) or clinical cervical dynamics defined as dilation > 1 cm; and pregnancy outcomes - abortion or delivery, gestational age at delivery, mode of delivery, PPH, cervical cerclage and recurrent cervical tears.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the incidence of recurrent cervical tear in the subsequent delivery. Secondary outcomes were the incidence of preterm delivery (< 37 gestational weeks), the need for cervical cerclage in subsequent pregnancies, and PPH incidence in the subsequent pregnancies, all stratified by the tear size or location.

Ethics

The study was approved by the local Institutional Review Board (0132–22-RMC). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were assessed for normal distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test, histograms, and Q-Q plots. Normally distributed continuous variables were described as means with standard deviations (SD), while skewed variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs).

Comparisons between continuous variables were performed using either the student t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, depending on the normality of the data and the sample size. Categorical variables were compared using either the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, based on the sample size and expected cell frequencies. A univariate analysis was conducted to examine the crude association between each variable with the primary outcome. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.0 (R Core Team, 2023).

Results

Regarding obstetrical outcomes, 107 (45%) women were diagnosed with PPH, and 40 (16%) required blood product transfusions. Discussion

We sought to determine the impact of cervical tears on subsequent pregnancy outcomes, mainly recurrent tears and PTB. We have found a low impact of intrapartum cervical tears on recurrent cervical tears, and no impact on PTB, regardless of the size and location of the primary tear. When recurrent cervical tears occur, most cases are among women with cervical cerclage.

In our cohort, the rate of primary, clinically significant, cervical tears was 0.23%. This aligns with previous literature reporting a low but notable incidence of cervical tears during vaginal delivery. Melamed et al. [7] demonstrated a similar incidence of cervical tears (0.2%) in a comparable series. Parikh et al. [6] also showed a similar prevalence (0.2%) in a smaller series of 16,000 vaginal deliveries. Landy et al. [8] examined 71,000 women and reported a higher incidence of cervical tears of 1.1%. To the best of our knowledge, these are among the largest series published regarding the incidence of cervical tears during vaginal delivery.

Only a limited number of studies have investigated the incidence of recurrent cervical tears, yielding conflicting results [7, 15–17]. In our cohort, women with a history of cervical tears had an overall low risk of recurrence; however, this risk was statistically significantly higher compared to women without prior tears. Among the 141 women in our study who underwent a subsequent delivery following a prior cervical tear, 107 delivered vaginally, and only 6 (5.4%) of them experienced a recurrent cervical tear. This rate is comparable to the findings of Hemou et al. [17], who reported a 4.9% recurrence rate (21 of 429 women).

Conversely, other studies have reported lower recurrence rates. Melamed et al. [7] analyzed 42 women with primary cervical tears and found no cases of recurrence, while Wong et al. [15] reported only one recurrent tear (1.8%) among 55 cases. However, the low recurrence rates observed in these studies may be influenced by their relatively small sample sizes of women with subsequent pregnancies.

Notably, in our cohort, among the subgroup of women who experienced recurrent cervical tears, 4 out of 6 (67%) had undergone cervical cerclage during gestation. Cervical cerclage has been associated with an estimated risk of cervical laceration ranging from 0.2% to 1.2%, as reported in a recent meta-analysis [18, 19]. Although there are no data specifically addressing the risk of recurrent cervical tears in women with repeated cerclage, this observation raises the possibility of a cumulative or intrinsic vulnerability. Foreign body reaction around the cervical stitch and the subsequent formation of scar tissue may render the cervix more susceptible to trauma during labor and delivery. The current findings may suggest a potential independent risk of cervical laceration associated with prior cerclage.

Regarding the risk of subsequent PTB following cervical tear, we found that while the risk does increase slightly from 10% to 14%, it is not statistically significant. In a study of 129 women with cervical tears, Zafran et al. [20] also found no increased risk for PTB. This is supported by similar findings presented by Wong et al. [15] and Melamed et al. [7], with approximately 10% PTB rate. It should be noted, however, that Hamou et al. [17] presented a statistically significant increased 1.8 risk for PTB, but this represents an absolute risk of 4%, lower than the risk for PTB found in our study. The lack of association between cervical tears and an increased risk of PTB might be attributed to the rarity of cervical tears overall. Although our findings indicate a statistically significant increase in the risk of recurrent cervical tears in subsequent deliveries, the absolute risk remains low. Therefore, the clinical impact of recurrent cervical tears on PTB risk appears to be minimal.

As far as we know, our research is the first to examine whether the risk of recurrent cervical tear or subsequent PTB is influenced by the size or location of the tear in the cervix. The hypothesis is that following cervical trauma such as a tear during labor, scar tissue forms in the cervical region, and its tensile strength is lower compared to healthy cervical tissue, thus increasing the risk of PTB. The assumption was that the more significant and larger the tear, the greater the tissue damage to the cervix, leading to more severe obstetric implications. However, our results have shown that the size or the location of the tear does not directly impact these risks. This may suggest that there are other variables related to cervical trauma and the risk of premature birth, such as inflammatory conditions or genetic predisposition.

Our study has several limitations, including its retrospective design and the use of data from a single tertiary center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Although the odds ratio for subsequent PTB was 1.47, this association did not reach statistical significance, might be due to the limited sample size and low event rate. Additionally, certain variables such as lifestyle behaviors and genetic predisposition were not available, and others—such as maternal age and BMI—were not adjusted for in the analysis. Tear size and location data were not available for all patients. The definition of ‘a significant cervical tear’ based on the attending physician’s subjective assessment of excessive vaginal bleeding. Further prospective studies with larger sample sizes and comprehensive data collection are warranted to validate our findings and elucidate the complex interplay between cervical trauma and obstetrical outcomes.

Conclusion

Intrapartum cervical tears are associated with an increased risk of recurrence in subsequent pregnancies; however, the overall incidence remains low. The presence of a cervical cerclage appears to contribute significantly to this risk. Importantly, no association was found between cervical tears and an increased risk of preterm birth (PTB), regardless of the tear’s location or size.
