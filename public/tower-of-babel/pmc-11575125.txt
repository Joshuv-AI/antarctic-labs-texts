Influence of nitrogen treatments on some nutrient concentration and bioactive compounds of broccoli
Authors: Ercan Ekbic, Gulhan Kose
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNitrogen fertilization in broccoli is very important for plant growth and productivity. However, it is known that the nitrogen source of the nitrogen fertilizer is also effective on quality parameters. In this study we investigated the effects of different nitrogen sources (ammonium sulphate and calcium nitrate) on bioactive compounds and leaf nutrients of broccoli cultivars (Monet, Italian and Karadede).ResultsThe highest total phenolic content was 0.24 mg g− 1 fw in cultivar Monet. Nitrate-based fertilizer treatments provided higher total phenolic (0.28 mg g− 1 fw) and flavonoid (0.56 mg g1 fw) contents and FRAP (0.24 mmol g− 1 fw) values compared to ammonium-based fertilizer treatments. On the other hand, DPPH (0.77 mmol g− 1 fw) values were higher in the ammonium nitrogen treatments. Among the cultivars, Monet had the highest total phenolics, flavonoids and FRAP values. When leaf nutrient concentrations were analyzed, ammonium sulfate application resulted in higher sulfur (0.86%), zinc (20.72 mg kg− 1) and manganese (28.80 mg kg− 1) concentrations compared to calcium nitrate. Calcium nitrate treatment provided the highest calcium (2.09%) and iron (37.50 mg kg− 1) concentration in Monet cultivar. Phosphorus (0.31–0.35%) and magnesium (0.073–0.084%) concentrations differed among cultivars.ConclusionsIn conclusion, the choice of nitrogen source and cultivar in broccoli cultivation plays an important role in the bioactive compound profile and nutrient composition of the broccoli crop. The results provide useful information for the improvement of broccoli quality.Keywords: Antioxidant activity, Nitrogen fertilization, Nutrient, Total phenolic, Total flavonoids

Background

Broccoli (Brassica oleracea var. italica) is a cruciferous vegetable of great importance due to its high nutritional value and potential health benefits [1, 2]. It is a rich source of antioxidants, especially vitamins C and E, and bioactive compounds such as flavonoids and glucosinolates [3, 4]. In broccoli cultivation, several agronomic practices must be carefully followed to ensure good plant growth, satisfactory yield, and high quality. Among these practices, fertilization plays a vital role that directly affects plant nutrition and development. In particular, nitrogen (N) fertilization is very important for broccoli cultivation because N is an essential macronutrient for plant growth and development, contributing to the synthesis of proteins, chlorophyll, and other vital biomolecules [5, 6]. Nitrogen fertilizers have two main sources: ammonium (NH4) and nitrate (NO3). These forms are readily taken up and metabolized by plants, but their effects on plant physiology, biochemistry, and nutrient uptake can be quite different. Ammonium sulfate (NH4)2SO4) is a widely used fertilizer that provides nitrogen in the form of ammonium and sulfur, an essential macronutrient for plant growth [7]. Sulfur plays a key role in enzymes involved in nitrogen metabolism, and its presence can affect plant growth, yield, and quality [8]. On the other hand, calcium nitrate Ca(NO3)2, a nitrate-based fertilizer, also provides calcium, which is essential for plant growth, cell wall formation and membrane integrity [9, 10]. The effects of different N sources on plant growth, yield, and quality have been studied in many plant species, including several vegetable species [11–14]. Particularly in the case of broccoli and other Brassica species, some researchers have investigated the effect of N fertilization on bioactive compounds and nutrient composition. For example, Fabek et al. [15] highlighted that increasing nitrogen levels increased glucosinolate concentration in broccoli, but the highest values were obtained in a specific dose range (120–240 kg ha− 1). On the other hand, Rivera-Martin et al. [16] reported that nitrogen application increased the content of bioactive components in broccoli, especially when combined with Zn. It was reported that different nitrogen sources applied to chikoris changed the chemical content and values of bioactive compounds in the plant [17]. In particular, it was reported that a ratio of 75% nitrate and 25% ammonium maximized the total content of phenolic compounds (flavonoids and phenolic acids). Different forms of nitrogen applied in crop production affect the amount of nutrients taken up by plant roots, depending on several factors. In a study investigating the effect of different ammonium and nitrate ratios on plant nutrient uptake, it was reported that when the nitrate ratio was higher than the ammonium ratio (25:75, NH4:NO3), Ca, Mg, and Mn were higher in the leaves of pecan plants [18].

Regarding the effects of different nitrogen sources, limited research has been conducted specifically on broccoli. Zhao-fan et al. [19] investigated the effects of different nitrogen sources (urea-N, nitrate-N, ammonium-N, nitrate-ammonium mixture) and their ratios on yield, quality, and nutrient (N, P, K) uptake of broccoli. It was reported that instead of a single nitrogen source, a mixture of different sources in appropriate ratios increased yield and nutrient uptake in broccoli. This suggests that the specific effects of ammonium- and nitrate-based fertilizers on the accumulation of bioactive compounds and nutrient profiles in Brassicaceae need to be further investigated.

In this respect, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of ammonium sulphate and calcium nitrate fertilizers on total phenolics, flavonoids, antioxidant activity and some macro- and micronutrient concentrations of three different broccoli cultivars.

Materials and methods

Experimental conditions

The experiment was conducted in Ordu province located in the Eastern Black Sea region of northern Türkiye. Ordu is located between 41°08’ − 40°20’ north latitude and 36°40’ − 38°06’ east longitude (Fig. 1). According to the climate data of Ordu province between 1959 and 2022, the average annual temperature is 14.5 °C and the total annual precipitation is 1049.1 mm. January and February are the months with the lowest average temperatures of 4 °C and the highest temperature (27.4 °C) was observed in August. While the maximum precipitation was observed in October (131.2 mm), the minimum precipitation was observed in May (56.1 mm) [20]. The field soil used in the experiment had a heavy texture (clay 48%, loam 30% and silt 22%) and a pH of 6.51. Potassium level (10.4%) and organic matter (3.36%) were sufficient, phosphorus level (1.18%) was very low.

Methods

Experimental design and treatments

The experiment was conducted using factorial experimental design in completely randomized plot arrangement with 3 replications and included 18 plants in each replication. ‘Italian’, ‘Karadede’ and ‘Monet’ broccoli cultivars were used as plant material in the study. ‘Karadede’ was provided from Argeto Vegetable Seeds Breeding Production and Marketing Ltd, ‘Italian’ from Torun Agricultural Seed Production Industry Trade Ltd and the cultivar ‘Monet’ from SIM Arzuman Agricultural Products Food Industry Trade Ltd. For seed sowing, a 2:1 mixture of peat (Klasmann, pH:6.0) and perlite was prepared and filled into 70-cell (7 × 10) viols. Seed sowing was performed in late July. Two to three seeds were sown in each cell of the viol and placed in an unheated plastic greenhouse. After emergence, the seedlings were thinned to one plant per cell and horticultural treatments such as irrigation and weed control were regularly applied until transplanting. No pests or diseases were encountered during the seedling period. Broccoli seedlings that had reached transplanting size 30 days after seed sowing were planted in plots with a spacing of 70 cm x 40 cm between and within rows. Fertilization was applied following the standard doses (20:20:22 kg da− 1 N: P:K respectively) for broccoli cultivation. In the experiment, ammonium sulphate (NH4)2SO4 (21% N) (Gubretas) and calcium nitrate Ca(NO3)2 (15% N) (AS Kimya) fertilizers were used as nitrogen sources, while triplesuper phosphate (TSP = 40% P) (Gubretas) and potassium sulphate (K2SO4 = 50% K) (Gubretas) commercial fertilizers were used as phosphorus sources. The full dose of P was applied once during transplanting, while N and K fertilizers were applied in two equal doses: the first half during transplanting and the second as top dressing, which was conducted 30 days after transplanting. Fertilizers were applied manually along the plant rows, followed by hoeing to incorporate them into the soil. Drip irrigation was used for watering the plants. Hoeing was also performed during the top fertilization to enhance soil aeration, and weed control was maintained throughout the growing season. Harvesting was carried out between October 31 and December 10, when the heads were marketable size and firm.

Phenolics, flavonoids and antioxidant activity

The main heads in each plot were harvested, mixed homogeneously and mashed in a blender for further analysis. From the mash, 5 g samples were taken and put into tubes. Then, 15 mL of methanol (Tekkim TK.120320) was added, and the samples were kept at + 4ºC for 2 days.

The total phenolic compounds in the samples were determined by the Folin-Ciocalteu method [21] with some modifications. For this, 300 µL fresh extract was mixed with 4.3 mL distilled water. Next, 100 µL of Folin-Ciocalteu (Merck 109001) and 300 µL 2% sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) (Merck 106392) were added, and the mixture was incubated for 2 h at room temperature. The absorbance values were read in a UV-vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation/ UVmini-1240) at 760 nm. The results were calculated in terms of gallic acid and expressed as fresh weight (mg GAE g-1 fw).

The total flavonoid content of the samples was determined using the method of Zhishen et al. [22]. A 300 µL extract was mixed with 4.0 mL methanol, then 100 µL 10% aluminum nitrate Al(NO3)3 and 100 µL 0.1 M ammonium acetate (NH4CH3CO2) were added to the mixture. The prepared samples were incubated at room temperature in dark conditions for 40 min, and their absorbance values were read at 415 nm using a UV-vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation/ UVmini-1240). The results were expressed as mg quercetin equivalents (QE) per g of fresh sample (mg QE g-1 fw).

Blois [23]’s approach for measuring antioxidant activity using the DPPH assay was modified. To conduct DPPH analysis, a 0.26 mM DPPH (1.1-diphenyl-2-picryl-hydrazyl) (Merck 300267) solution was prepared. Next, 20 µL of extract was mixed with 2980 µL of ethyl alcohol and 1 mL of DPPH solution. The mixture was vortexed and kept in the dark for 30 min. After incubation, the absorbance values were measured at 517 nm using a UV-vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation/ UVmini-1240). The absorbance values were expressed as µmol Trolox equivalent per gram of fresh weight (mmol TE g-1fw) using the Trolox (10–100 µmol L-1) standard slope chart.

FRAP (Ferric reducing antioxidant power) analysis was performed according to the method of Benzie and Strain [24] with modifications. Briefly, 0.2 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.7) was prepared and 1.15 mL of this buffer was combined with 100 µL of extract and 1.25 mL of 1% potassium ferricyanide K3Fe(CN)6. The reaction mixture was incubated at 50 °C for 20 min and then allowed to cool to room temperature. Then 1.25 mL of 10% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and 0.25 mL of 0.1% ferric chloride (FeCl3) were added and the solution was vortexed for 1 min. The absorbance was measured at 700 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation/ UVmini-1240). The results were expressed as millimoles (mmol) of Trolox equivalents (TE) per gram of fresh weight (fw) (mmol TE g-1 fw).

Mineral nutrient concentration in broccoli leaves

The leaf samples were oven-dried at 65oC until a constant weight was achieved and ground into powder before being incinerated using the wet combustion method. In this method, 0.2 g of the ground sample was taken and digested in a closed microwave digestion system (Marsexpress Cem Corp., Matthews, NC, USA) with 5 mL of concentrated nitric acid (HNO3) (Merck 100456) and 2 mL of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) (Merck 107209). Following digestion, the sample volumes were adjusted to 20 mLwith deionized water. The sulfur (S, %), calcium (Ca, %), potassium (K, %), phosphorus (P, %), magnesium (Mg, %), iron (Fe, mg kg-1), zinc (Zn, mg kg-1) and manganese (Mn, mg kg-1) element concentrations of the prepared samples were analyzed using ICP-OES (Vista-Pro Axial, Agilent Tech., Mulgrave, Australia). SRM 1547 peach leaf sample was used as the standard to confirm the accuracy of the analyses.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using Generalized linear model (GLM), considering a two-factor completely randomized design, using JMP v.10.0 software. Statistical analysis was performed, and means were compared by least significant difference post-hock test at the 5% probability level [25].

Results

Phenolics, flavonoids and antioxidant activity

The effect of different N sources on total phenolic and flavonoid contents of three broccoli cultivars was found to be statistically significant in terms of cultivar, nitrogen applications and the interaction between cultivar and N source (CxN) (Table 1). The highest total phenolic content among the cultivars was obtained from ‘Monet’ cultivar with 0.24 mg g− 1 fw value. Among the nitrogen fertilizers with different sources, Ca(NO3)2 applications showed higher values than (NH4)2SO4. The highest levels of total phenolics (0.28 mg g− 1 fw) and total flavonoids (0.56 mg g− 1 fw) were obtained from the ‘Monet’ cultivar and Ca(NO3)2 treated plots. The total flavonoid values were higher in NO3-sourced nitrogen treatments (0.45 mg g− 1 fw) than in NH4-sourced nitrogen treatments (0.36 mg g− 1 fw). The ‘Monet’ cultivar had the highest total flavonoid value (0.46 mg g− 1 fw). The effect of fertilizer with different N forms on FRAP content in broccoli was found to be significant, while the differences among cultivars and the interaction (CxN) effect were statistically insignificant (Table 2). NO3 applications increased the FRAP content of broccoli compared to NH4 application. The FRAP content was 0.24 mmol g− 1 fw in NO3 treated plots and 0.19 mmol g− 1 fw in plots treated with NH4-containing N fertilizer. When examining the effect of different N source applications on the DPPH value in broccoli, it was observed that the differences between the cultivars and the interaction effect were not significant, but the N sources were significant. Table 2 shows that NH4-containing nitrogen fertilization resulted in a statistically significant higher DPPH value (0.77 mmol g− 1 fw) compared to NO3-containing fertilization (0.75 mmol g− 1 fw).

Leaf nutrient concentrations

Table 3 presents the effects of (NH4)2SO4 and Ca(NO3)2 applications on leaf macronutrients. The statistical analysis showed that the effect of fertilizers with different N source on S concentration in leaves was not significant. The S concentration of broccoli leaves ranged from 0.60 to 0.86%. Furthermore, the application of (NH4)2SO4 resulted in higher leaf S value compared to the application of Ca(NO3)2. Significant differences were found in leaf Ca concentration among cultivars and NxC interactions. The ‘Monet’ cultivar had the highest leaf Ca value at 2.09% when treated with calcium nitrate, while the ‘Italian’ cultivar had the lowest at 1.39% with the same treatment. There was not significant effect of cultivar and N treatment on foliar K concentration. Regarding leaf P concentration, neither N source caused statistically significant differences in the examined cultivars, and the P concentration of the leaves ranged from 0.31 to 0.35%. The difference in leaf Mg concentration between the cultivars was found to be significant. In the study, the ‘Monet’ (0.084%) and ‘Karadede’ (0.081%) cultivars were in the same statistical group and exhibited higher Mg levels than the ‘Italian’ (0.073%) cultivar. The leaf Mg values ranged from 0.071 to 0.089%.

Table 4 presents the effects of fertilizer applications with different nitrogen sources on leaf Fe, Zn, and Mn concentrations. Results showed a significant interaction between cultivars and nitrogen sources on leaf Fe concentration. The highest leaf Fe concentration was obtained from Ca(NO3)2 application to the ‘Monet’ cultivar, with a value of 71.98 mg kg− 1, while the lowest leaf Fe concentration was obtained from Ca(NO3)2 application to the ‘Italian’ cultivar, with a value of 37.50 mg kg− 1. Although cultivars did not result in significant differences in leaf Zn concentrations, the use of different nitrogen sources and the interaction of these two factors were found to be significant. Generally, leaf Zn concentrations were found to be between 13.66 and 23.68 mg kg− 1. Based on the average results of the treatments, a higher leaf Zn value was obtained from the application of (NH4)2SO4 (20.72 mg kg− 1) than from the application of Ca(NO3)2 (16.37 mg kg− 1). Based on the interaction, the ‘Karadede’ and ‘Italian’ cultivars showed higher leaf Zn values with (NH4)2SO4 application. The statistical analysis revealed that the leaf Mn concentration was significant with different N sources, cultivar and the interaction between these two factors (NxC). Especially, the application of (NH4)2SO4 resulted in a higher leaf Mn concentration with a value of 28.80 mg kg− 1 compared to calcium nitrate application (22.49 mg kg− 1). The highest leaf Mn concentration among cultivars was obtained from ‘Monet’ cultivar with a value of 28.92 mg kg− 1 and the lowest Mn concentration was obtained from ‘Karadede’ cultivar with a value of 21.64 mg kg− 1. The Ca(NO3)2 application to the ‘Monet’ cultivar resulted in the highest leaf Mn concentration with a value of 31.77 mg kg− 1. However, the same application of fertilizer caused the lowest leaf Mn concentration (13.12 mg kg− 1) in ‘Karadede’ cultivar.

Discussion

This study pointed out that fertilizers with different nitrogen sources, (NH4)SO4 and CaNO3, have a significant effect on the bioactive compound and nutrient concentration in broccoli. The results demonstrated that the utilization of NO3 nitrogen fertilizer was more effective in increasing the overall total phenolic, total flavonoid, and FRAP contents of broccoli, while NH4 nitrogen fertilizer was more effective in increasing the DPPH value. The higher DPPH content under NH4 treatment compared to NO3 treatment (Table 2) is a situation associated with plant growth and development [26]. In this study, the variations observed in the parameters of total phenolic content, total flavonoid content and FRAP content under different N forms could be related to the concentration effect due to growth inhibition in NO3 treatment or the dilution effect caused by NH4 treatment promoting plant growth [26]. The increase in plant growth under the (NH4)2SO4 form of N compared to the Ca(NO3)2 form could be a result of the synergistic effect of N and S in broccoli plants [27]. It was found that green and purple basil plants treated with NO3 showed greater growth increases in root and leaf biomass compared to the control, and had lower total phenolic compounds and, in the case of purple basil, lower total flavonoid content compared to plants treated with NH4 [28]. Similarly, amaranth plants treated with NO3 showed greater height than those treated with NH4, but had lower total flavonoid and phenolic content [29]. Lee and Paek [14] conducted a study to determine the optimal ammonium and nitrate ratio for the production of bioactive compounds in adventitious roots of the Eleutherococcus koreanum plant. They reported that a high ammonium ratio had negative effects and that the optimum NH4:NO3 ratio was 5:25 mM. The researchers emphasized that the pH value of the medium decreased with the increase of NH4 ratio, which led to decreased biomass production and increased free oxygen radicals, causing stress in adventitious roots. Aksu [30] observed that the total phenolic and antioxidant content in plants increased with increasing nitrogen dose. Nunez-Ramirez et al. [13] found no significant difference between medium and high nitrogen doses, and bioactive components were not affected by nitrogen doses. In fact, total phenolic and flavonoid contents in plants vary widely depending on the form of nitrogen, the nutrient element combined with that form in the fertilizer, the dose of fertilizer, and the plant species and varieties used. The changes in plant sulfur concentrations in relation to nitrogen (N), cultivar (C) and N x C interaction were not found to be statistically significant (Table 3). The analysis of sulfur concentrations in broccoli leaves revealed that the sulfur values ranged from 0.60 to 0.86% for both NH4 and NO3 nitrogen fertilizations. These values fall within the ideal range of 0.30–0.75% [31]. Furthermore, the application of (NH4)2SO4 resulted in a higher leaf sulfur concentration compared to the application of Ca(NO3)2. Sulfur is an essential macronutrient that stimulates several physiological processes fundamental to plant growth. It plays an important role in amino acid synthesis, particularly in the production of cysteine and methionine [32], which are precursors of essential proteins and enzymes. These compounds contribute to the formation of chlorophyll, potentially increasing photosynthetic efficiency. Sulfur is also essential for the synthesis of glucosinolates [33] and other secondary metabolites that can increase plant resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses, including disease resistance and improved tolerance to environmental stresses. In addition, sulfur is an important element of nitrogen metabolism in plants, contributing to improved nitrogen assimilation and, ultimately leading to increased yield [27]. Therefore, the higher sulfur concentration in the leaves was attributed to the increased sulfur element from (NH4)2SO4 fertilizer. It was observed that the Ca and P concentrations of the cultivars were between the limits reported by Havlin [31] for the related elements (1.2–2.5% for Ca; 0.3–0.7% for P). Calcium in plants also plays a critical role in strengthening cell walls and plant tissues and in enzymatic reactions, thereby increasing the plant’s resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses [34]. Nitrogen fertilization increases organic acid content, which facilitates calcium uptake by the plant. Ca(NO3)2 fertilizer provides essential nitrogen and Ca for plants. The preference of plants for N in the form of NO3 has led to a noticeable increase in Ca concentration, particularly in the ‘Monet’ cultivar, through the application of Ca(NO3)2 fertilizer in our study. It was found that this application further improved the nutritional value of broccoli, a crop favored for its Ca richness. On the other hand, except for the K concentration of the ‘Karadere’ cultivar, the K and Mg concentrations of the cultivars were found to be below the limits reported for the related elements (2–4% for K; 0.23–0.4% for Mg). Since excess N is known to have an antagonistic effect on K uptake [35], the reduced K levels observed may be due to this effect. The low Mg levels of the plants grown in the experimental area indicate either a low level of Mg in the soil or that the plants were unable to take up Mg. In fact, the Ca in the Ca(NO3)2 fertilizer may have adversely affected the uptake of Mg, which may be present in low levels in the soil [36]. However, it is suggested that the ammonium ions in the ammonium sulfate source have been replaced by Mg in the soil, resulting in the plant benefiting slightly more from Mg. The highest iron concentration was obtained by applying nitrogen in the form of nitrate to the ‘Monet’ cultivar, and this value was found to be within the optimal range of Fe concentration. Zinc, which is involved in numerous physiological processes such as protein synthesis, antioxidant defense mechanisms, auxin and carbohydrate metabolism in plants, plays an important role in the development of vegetative organs. Manganese, which serves as a cofactor for various enzymes, also plays an important role in various biochemical and physiological processes in plants, similar to Zn [37].The concentrations of Fe, Zn and Mn in the cultivars were found to be below and/or between the sufficiency ranges (50–150 for Fe; 20–80 for Zn; 25–150 mg kg for Mn) given by Havlin [31]. The concentrations of Zn and Mn in leaves were found to be higher under N application in the form of NH4 than in the form of NO3, which was considered as a reflection of the acidic nature of the corresponding NH4 fertilizer.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the application of different nitrogen fertilizers can influence the nutrient concentration, the total phenolic, total flavonoid and FRAP contents of broccoli. NO3-based fertilizer showed a tendency to increase phenolic compounds and antioxidant properties, while NH4-based fertilizer, especially (NH4)2SO4, promoted sulfur uptake, which is essential for the growth and quality of sulfur-loving species like broccoli. The observed improvements in nutrient concentration underline the importance of sulfur availability in broccoli cultivation. In the ‘Monet’ cultivar, a significant increase in leaf Ca concentration was achieved by nitrogen fertilization in the nitrate form. In addition, ammonium-based nitrogen fertilizer gave more favorable results for Zn and Mn concentrations. The proper selection and application of nitrogen forms in fertilizer is critical to improving the quality and nutrient concentration of broccoli. Future research can extend these findings by investigating the interactions of nitrogen fertilizer with other nutrients and under different environmental conditions, thus contributing to optimized fertilization strategies.
