Development of mRNA vaccines against herpes zoster
Authors: Dongdong Li, Jun Luo, Junwei Wang, Lining Wang, Mengyu Zhang, Jian Shen, Xiaoyu Chu, Weian Mao, Yan Zhang, Hongwu Xin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHerpes zoster (HZ), caused by varicella-zoster virus (VZV), remains a substantial public health burden, particularly in aging populations, due to the risk of severe complications like postherpetic neuralgia (PHN). While existing vaccines (eg. Shingrix®) have advanced prevention efficiencies, they present notable limitations, such as high reactogenicity and complex manufacturing. mRNA vaccines represent a transformative alternative with rapid manufacturing, precise antigen design, and favorable safety. This review synthesizes the current landscape of mRNA vaccine development for HZ, focusing on the rational design of antigen constructs, innovations in lipid nanoparticle delivery systems, insights from preclinical immunogenicity studies, and the progress of leading candidates into clinical trials. In summary, this review highlights the potential of mRNA vaccines to address the limitations of current options, potentially offering a new paradigm for safer, highly efficacious, and scalable HZ prevention. Continued clinical validation is crucial to realize this potential and alleviate the global disease burden.

Introduction

Varicella-zoster virus (VZV), also designated as human herpesvirus 3 (HHV-3), is a double-stranded DNA virus belonging to the Alphaherpesvirinae subfamily, which also includes Herpes simplex virus types 1 and 2. The spherical virion measures approximately 80–120 nm in diameter. Its architecture is organized into four concentric layers, including viral genome, nucleocapsid, tegument, and envelope.1 VZV genome (125 kb) consists of two long and short unique segments, each flanked by terminal repeat sequences.2 The genome contains at least 71 open reading frames (ORFs) encoding 67 genes. The outermost layer is a host-derived lipid envelope studded with viral glycoproteins, including glycoprotein B (gB), glycoprotein E (gE), glycoprotein H (gH), among others.3 These glycoproteins form spike-like structures that mediate viral attachment and host cell membrane fusion.3 Immediately beneath the envelope lies the tegument, a proteinaceous matrix containing regulatory proteins, such as VP16 and IE62.2 These tegument proteins play a critical role in hijacking host transcription and translation machinery to promote viral replication shortly after infection.2

VZV infection is primarily via respiratory droplet or contact transmission. Following initial replication in the mucosal epithelial cells of the upper respiratory tract, the virus disseminates to tonsillar and local lymphoid tissues.4 Infected immune cells subsequently enable the virus to spread hematogenously, delivering it to the skin. There, the virus primarily replicates within epithelial cells and fibroblasts, leading to the characteristic widespread vesicular rash of chickenpox.4,5 Concurrently, VZV establishes lifelong latency in the nervous system through at least two pathways. The first is retrograde axonal transport, whereby the virus travels from infectious cutaneous sites along sensory neuron to neuronal nuclei in dorsal root or cranial nerve.1,6 The other pathway is via cell-associated viremia, in which T cells may directly carry the virus to ganglion regions.1,6 Within ganglia, the viral genome enters a silent latent state without active replication.1,6 Although the precise entry mechanism remains incompletely defined, VZV is thought to enter cells via direct fusion with the plasma membrane or endocytosis, mediated by interactions between viral envelope glycoproteins (e.g., gB, gC, gD, and the gH/gL complex) and cellular receptors such as mannose-6-phosphate receptor and myelin-associated glycoprotein.1 After uncoating in the cytoplasm, the viral capsid releases the genomic DNA into the nucleus, where immediate-early protein IE62 forms complexes with host factors like Sp1 to broadly activate early and late viral gene expression, thereby initiating the replication cycle.1 Viral serine/threonine kinases encoded by ORF47 and ORF66 further fine-tune progression through phosphorylation of viral proteins, including IE62. Following viral DNA replication in the nucleus, newly assembled nucleocapsids acquire a primary envelope by budding through the inner nuclear membrane.1,6 This envelope is subsequently lost upon fusion with the outer nuclear membrane, releasing naked nucleocapsids into the cytoplasm.1,6 These capsids then obtain their final envelope containing viral glycoproteins and tegument proteins from trans-Golgi network-derived vesicles, culminating in the formation of mature virions.1,6 This highly efficient process enables detection of progeny virus as early as 9 hours post-infection, with abundant viral particles reaching the cell surface within 12 hours.3

VZV demonstrates marked neurotropism, with its mechanisms of latency and reactivation being central to pathogenesis. During primary infection, VZV invades sensory nerve endings and undergoes retrograde axonal transport to the neuronal cell bodies in dorsal root ganglia or cranial nerve ganglia.4,5 Within the neurons, the virus establishes latency whereby the genome persists as a circular episome and most lytic gene transcription is significantly suppressed.1,7 Reactivation of the latent virus can occur when host cellular immunity declines due to factors like aging, stress, or immunosuppressive therapy. Although the precise signaling pathways are not fully elucidated, they may involve neuronal stress and alterations in the epigenetic landscape.1,7 The reactivation process is initiated by the renewed expression of immediate-early genes, particularly IE62, which triggers the complete lytic gene expression cascade.1,7 Newly assembled virions are then transported anterogradely along the sensory nerve axons to the skin areas they innervate, where extensive replication in epidermal cells leads to local inflammation and cytopathic effects, clinically manifesting as herpes zoster (HZ).3

HZ, resulting from the reactivation of VZV, has a lifetime risk of approximately one in three in the general population, with an annual incidence of 3–5 per 1,000 person-years.8 Incidence increases sharply with age, exceeding 8–10 cases per 1,000 person-years in adults aged ≥60 years.8 The most debilitating complication is postherpetic neuralgia (PHN), affecting 10–30% of all HZ patients, with risk escalating to 30–50% in those ≥70 years old.9,10 Other complications include ophthalmic involvement (10–25%) and Ramsay Hunt syndrome.10,11 The management of PHN constitutes the primary disease burden, often requiring long-term, multi-drug regimens (e.g., gabapentinoids, tricyclic antidepressants). Prophylaxis with the recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV) is highly effective. In pivotal trials, RZV demonstrated an efficacy of >90% against HZ and PHN in adults ≥50 years, with sustained protection exceeding 85% for at least 7–10 years.12,13 In contrast, the older live-attenuated vaccine showed lower and waning efficacy, particularly in the elderly. Although highly protective, the relatively high reactogenicity of RZV may impact vaccine series completion and broad public acceptance.12,13 This underscores a need for next-generation platforms with improved tolerability profiles. In this context, mRNA vaccine technology presents a promising frontier. By encoding viral antigens without the need for a potent exogenous adjuvant, mRNA platforms offer the potential to elicit robust and durable cellular and humoral immunity, while potentially mitigating the reactogenicity associated with strong adjuvant systems.

This review systematically synthesizes recent preclinical and clinical advancements in mRNA-based vaccines for HZ, with data meticulously gathered from scientific literature, trial registries (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov), and regulatory bodies (e.g., China’s Center for Drug Evaluation, CDE). By providing a comprehensive analysis of the evolving landscape of VZV vaccines, this study aims to establish a robust scientific foundation and offer strategic insights to guide future research and development in this critical area of vaccinology.

Approved HZ vaccines

Zoster vaccine live (ZVL)

The current landscape of ZVLs includes several products with varying efficacy, safety profiles, and age-specific approvals. Zostavax, developed by Merck & Co., was the first ZVL vaccine, receiving FDA approval in 2006. Zostavax is a high-potency live-attenuated Oka/Merck-strain VZV vaccine administered as a single subcutaneous dose. Zostavax delivers replicating-competent attenuated VZV antigens to boost preexisting VZV-specific memory lymphocyte pools, augmenting both VZV-specific CMI and antibody titers.14–16 Enhanced VZV-specific T-cell immunity maintains immune surveillance against ganglionic latent virus, constraining viral reactivation and reducing the risk of herpes zoster and post-herpetic neuralgia. However, vaccine-elicited CMI wanes progressively over time, and immunogenicity is substantially blunted in the oldest-old population, resulting in reduced clinical protection in adults ≥80 years old. Being a replication-competent live vaccine, Zostavax is contraindicated in severely immunocompromised subjects due to theoretical risk of disseminated VZV disease.17–21 It was indicated for immunocompetent adults aged 60 years and older, demonstrating approximately 50% efficacy in preventing HZ and 70% efficacy against PHN.17,19 The vaccine works by boosting VZV-specific cell-mediated immunity (CMI) and antibody levels. However, the protective efficacy wanes over time and declines significantly with age.17–21 It is highest at 63.9% in adults aged 60–69, decreases to about 37.6% in those aged 70 and older, and drops to 17.5% in individuals ≥80 years, where the protective trend lacks statistical significance.17 In terms of safety, 63.6% of recipients have local injection site reactions within 5 days, most frequently pain, erythema, and swelling.17 Due to declining efficacy over time and the availability of a more effective vaccine, Zostavax is no longer marketed in major markets including the US and EU.22,23

More recent live attenuated vaccines include SkyZoster (SK Bioscience) and Canvar (Changchun BCHT Biotechnology). SkyZoster was first approved by the Korean MFDS in September 2017 and later received authorization from Malaysia’s NPRA in January 2023. It is indicated for adults aged 50 and older and elicits a CMI response comparable to Zostavax.24 Clinical data involving 651 adults aged ≥50 reported an overall adverse event rate of 11.67%, with vaccine-related reactions at 5.68%.25 The vast majority (99.17%) of events were mild, and no serious AEs were reported.25 Common local reactions included injection site pain, erythema, and itching.25 Canvar is a single-dose ZVL approved in June 2023. It is licensed for adults aged 40 and older, addressing an important gap in the 40–50 age group. The vaccine shows overall protective efficacy of 57.62% in those aged ≥40, 58.72% in those ≥50, and 55.64% in those ≥60 (https://www.bchtpharm.com/product_1/12.html). Canvar also exhibits a good safety profile, with local injection site reactions (e.g., redness, swelling, pain) and systemic symptoms (e.g., fever and fatigue) being mostly mild and occurring at rates below 10% (https://www.bchtpharm.com/product_1/12.html).

RZV

The RZV, known as Shingrix® and developed by GlaxoSmithKline (GSK), is a subunit vaccine for the prevention of HZ and its complications, notably PHN. Its antigenic component is a recombinant form of VZV glycoprotein E (gE). To enhance the immune response, the antigen is combined with the AS01B adjuvant system23 (https://www.fda.gov/media/108597/download). This adjuvant is a liposome-based formulation containing 50 µg of 3-O-desacyl-4’-monophosphoryl lipid A (MPL) and 50 µg of QS-21, which is extracted from a South American plant Quillaja saponaria23(https://www.fda.gov/media/108597/download). The vaccination regimen consists of two intramuscular doses (0.5 mL each) administered 2 to 6 months apart. The pivotal Phase 3 clinical trials, ZOE-50 (NCT01165177) and ZOE-70 (NCT01165229), were randomized, placebo-controlled studies conducted across 18 countries.12,13 The ZOE-50 trial enrolled participants from August 2, 2010, to July 21, 2011, and a mean follow-up of 3.2 years.12 The ZOE-70 trial was conducted concurrently at the same sites, with a mean follow-up of 3.7 years.13 These trials were designed to evaluate the efficacy, immunogenicity, and safety of RZV in immunocompetent adults. The vaccine demonstrated exceptionally high and sustained efficacy across all age groups. In the ZOE-50 trial, the overall vaccine efficacy against HZ was 97.2% (95% CI, 93.7 to 99.0). The efficacy was consistently high and showed no significant decline with advancing age: 96.6% (95% CI, 89.6 to 99.3) for ages 50–59, 97.4% (95% CI, 90.1 to 99.7) for ages 60–69, and 97.9% (95% CI, 87.9 to 100.0) for participants 70 years and older.12 The ZOE-70 trial confirmed high efficacy in the older population, with an overall efficacy of 89.8% (95% CI, 84.2 to 93.7).13 The protection efficacy was 90.0% for ages 70–79 and 89.1% for those aged 80 years and older. A pooled analysis of participants aged 70 and older from both trials showed a protection efficacy of 91.3% (95% CI, 86.8 to 94.5). Crucially, the vaccine was also highly effective in preventing PHN. In the pooled population of participants aged 50 and older, the protection efficacy against PHN was 91.2% (95% CI, 75.9 to 97.7). For the specific subgroup of participants aged 70 and older, the protection efficacy was 88.8% (95% CI, 68.7 to 97.1).12,13 Another study evaluated the long-term immunogenicity and safety of gE/AS01B vaccine. The results demonstrated that Shingrix® induced robust and persistent immune responses in older adults for up to 6 years post-vaccination. The gE-specific CD4+ T-cell frequencies remained 3.8 times higher than pre-vaccination levels at year 6, despite a 25% decrease from the peak observed at month 36. Similarly, the humoral immune response showed remarkable durability, with anti-gE antibody concentrations maintaining a 7.3-fold increase over baseline levels at the 6 years mark, having decreased by only 20% since month 36. The immune responses plateaued after month 48 for cellular immunity and month 24 for humoral immunity, with comparable persistence observed across different age groups (60–69 years and ≥70 years). The vaccine maintained a favorable safety profile throughout the extended follow-up period, with no vaccine-related serious AEs reported between months 36 and 72, supporting its potential for providing long-term protection against herpes zoster.26 Other trials also demonstrated the high protection efficacy of AS01B-adjuvanted gE protein vaccine.27,28 On 25 October 2017, ACIP recommended Shingrix® for use in immunocompetent adults aged ≥50 years.23 Furthermore, Shingrix® also demonstrated a high protective efficacy against HZ in immunocompromised patients, such as hematologic malignancies and HIV, with a favorable safety profile.29–32

The Shingrix® vaccine, while highly effective, presents several significant drawbacks. Firstly, although raw Quillaja saponaria plant material is not intrinsically scarce and crude saponin extracts are widely used in beverage formulations, QS-21 (fraction-21 saponin) relies on complex, low‑yield chromatographic fractionation and purification from bulk crude extracts. Coupled with high market demand for the shingles vaccine, this cumbersome purification workflow constitutes the primary bottleneck for large‑scale QS‑21 supply and contributes to high production costs and a premium price for the vaccine. This supply constraint, combined with the intricate manufacturing of the antigen component, has historically led to global supply shortages and limited production capacity.33 Secondly, the potent adjuvant also causes a high rate of transient but pronounced local and systemic reactogenicity, including injection-site pain, redness, swelling, and systemic symptoms like myalgia, fatigue, and fever, which can deter some individuals from completing the two-dose regimen. Based on the ZOE-50 and ZOE-70 clinical trials, RZV demonstrated a higher frequency of solicited adverse reactions compared to placebo, though without an increase in severe medical conditions.12,13 A key finding was the incidence of Grade 3 reactions (severe enough to prevent normal activity), which were significantly more common in RZV recipients. In the ZOE-50 trial (adults ≥50 years), 17.0% of vaccine recipients reported Grade 3 solicited symptoms vs. 3.2% with placebo. Specifically, Grade 3 injection-site reactions (e.g., pain) occurred in 9.5% of the RZV group, and Grade 3 systemic reactions (e.g., myalgia, fatigue) occurred in 11.4%.12 A similar pattern was observed in the ZOE-70 trial (adults ≥70 years), with lower but still elevated rates.13 Crucially, the incidence of serious AEs, potential immune-mediated diseases, and deaths was similar between the RZV and placebo groups in both studies. This indicates that while the vaccine is more reactogenic, leading to transient but pronounced local and systemic symptoms, it was not associated with an increased risk of serious safety concerns in the studied populations.12,13

Clinical development of other RZV vaccine candidates

LZ901, developed by Green Bamboo Biotech, features a unique tetrameric molecular structure that provides double Fc regions for antigen-presenting cell binding compared to natural VZV antigens, thereby actively presenting VZV antigens to immune cells. Each 0.5 mL dose contains 100 μg tetrameric recombinant VZV gE-Fc fusion protein expressed in CHO cells, adsorbed onto aluminum hydroxide (traditional aluminum-salt adjuvant). Distinct from AS01B (liposome-formulated MPL plus QS-21 saponin that intrinsically favors strong Th1-biased responses), aluminum hydroxide is a classical depot-forming adjuvant historically known to preferentially promote Th2-type humoral immunity rather than potent cellular-mediated immunity. In May 2023, the Phase II clinical trial of LZ901 was completed in China, showing statistically and clinically significant results: the high-dose group exhibited significantly higher antibody geometric mean concentration (GMC), geometric mean titer (GMT), and seroconversion rates compared to the low-dose group, and both dose groups were superior to the placebo group. The AEs were primarily mild to moderate, with Grade I, II, and III AEs occurring at rates of approximately 23.74%, 6.02%, and 1.00%, respectively, and no Grade IV or serious AEs were observed. In a head-to-head clinical trial, LZ901 induced superior cellular immunogenicity and exhibited a better safety profile than Shingrix® in adults ≥50 years.34 Following these results, the group initiated a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase III clinical trial in China in September 2023. Additionally, the group received IND approval for LZ901 from the FDA in July 2022 (https://www.luzhubiotech.com/techAndProduct/productInResearch). LYB004, developed by China’s Jiangsu Recbio Technology, is an RZV in which VZV gE is displayed at high density on a universal virus-like nanoparticle (U-VLP™) scaffold via Covalink® conjugation, formulated with Panobio’s proprietary A01 adjuvant, a MF59-like squalene-based oil-in-water emulsion used at either a high (A01B) or low (A01C) dose level. It has completed a Phase I clinical trial in Australia (NCT06335849) and received approval from CDE in July 2024 (CXSL2400466 and CXSL2400467). LWSD603, developed by Jiangsu Lwisdd Biotech, employs a truncated gE antigen similar to Shingrix® and incorporates a novel, first-in-class composite adjuvant system. The adjuvant combines MF59, a squalene-based oil-in-water emulsion, with CpG1018, a TLR9-agonist CpG oligodeoxynucleotide. Its IND application has been approved by China’s CDE (CXSL2500498), marking the initiation of clinical development. Jiangsu Zhonghui Yuantong BioTech has developed a novel RZV utilizing a proprietary truncated VZV gE protein antigen and a self-developed dual-adjuvant system. The vaccine received IND approval in China in August 2024. Following the initiation of its Phase I clinical study in February 2025, which confirmed a favorable interim safety profile, the program was advanced into Phase II clinical studies in July 2025 (http://www.abbbio.com/Products_details/5.html). GNW002 is an RZV vaccine independently developed by Genovabio. It employs a self-developed dual-adjuvant system combining an aluminum salt adjuvant with a CpG oligodeoxynucleotide (TLR9 agonist), formulated with a gE-Fc dimer fusion protein. In preclinical animal studies, GNW002 demonstrated safety, cellular immunity, and humoral immunity levels comparable to Shingrix®. This RZV (CHO Changchun cell) has obtained IND approvals in both China and the US and is currently in Phase I clinical trials (https://www.genevax.com.cn/newsinfo/6818959.html). BCHT Biotechnology has screened out an adjuvant system named BK-02 for the RZV through a series of Preclinical Studies. BK-02 is a novel self-developed composite adjuvant system that combines BK-02C, a TLR9 agonist (CpG2006), with BK-02 M, a squalene-based oil-in-water emulsion (MF59). The vaccine received an implicit clinical trial approval from CDE on June 4, 2025 (CXSL2500213). Maxvax has developed an RZV adjuvanted with its proprietary MA105 adjuvant. MA105 is a proprietary composite adjuvant system comprising QS-21 and Poly I:C (a TLR3/MDA-5 agonist) co-delivered in a mixed cationic/neutral liposome vehicle of DOTAP, DOPC and cholesterol. The vaccine’s Phase III clinical trial successfully met its primary efficacy endpoint, demonstrating strong preventive effects and a favorable safety profile in adults over 40 (CTR20241986). Based on these positive results, the company has now submitted a New Drug Application for market approval. REC610 is developed by Recbio and formulated with its proprietary adjuvant BFA01. BFA01 is a liposome-based composite adjuvant system developed by Recbio, and its formulation is essentially identical to GSK’s AS01B, each 0.5 mL dose containing 50 μg of QS-21 and 50 μg of MPL carried in DOPC and cholesterol liposomes. The two immunostimulants act together within the draining lymph node to elicit potent gE-specific CD4+ T-cell and antibody responses. What distinguishes BFA01 is not its composition but its independence: Recbio created the adjuvant in-house, secured its own supply of critical raw materials such as QS-21, and built commercial-scale manufacturing capacity, so the vaccine no longer depends on any single foreign supplier. Phase I clinical trial demonstrated REC610 has a favorable safety, tolerability, and similar immunogenicity with Shingrix® (CXSL2300518). REC610 entered Phase III trials in China in October 2024 (CXSS2500145). Grand Theravac Life Science Nanjing Co. Ltd. developed an RZV utilizing the company’s proprietary novel adjuvant TVA01. It has demonstrated favorable safety and immunogenicity in a Phase I clinical trial conducted in the Philippines. Subsequently, a Phase I clinical trial in China has been initiated in December 2024 (CXSL2400311 and CXSL2400310). CRV-101 (generic name amezosvatein), developed by Curevo Vaccine, is an RZV composed of VZV glycoprotein E (gE, 100 µg/dose) plus the SLA-SE adjuvant. SLA-SE stands for second-generation lipid adjuvant formulated in a squalene-based oil-in-water emulsion, where a chemically synthesized TLR4 agonist SLA was dissolved in ~3.6% squalene stable emulsion. It was tested in a Phase 1 dose-escalation trial from 2019 (healthy 18–49 years, two IM doses 2 months apart) and a Phase 2 head-to-head vs. Shingrix® in 876 adults ≥50 years (NCT05304351, started February 2022, two IM doses 2 months apart). At 100 µg gE +15 µg SLA-SE, anti-gE antibodies at Day 84 were non-inferior to Shingrix® (Vaccine response rate: 100% vs. 97.9%) and stayed non-inferior at Day 421, while Grade 2/3 reactogenicity was far lower (7.3% vs. 33.3%) with zero Grade 3 local reactions. Z-1018 is Dynavax’s recombinant shingles vaccine made of 100 µg VZV glycoprotein E (gE) adjuvanted with CpG 1018 plus aluminum salt. It is being studied in a randomized, observer-blinded Phase 1/2 trial (NCT06569823) in adults 50–69 (Part 1) vs. Shingrix, with the selected 100 µg gE + CpG 1018 + alum at 0/8 weeks now advancing into Part 2 in adults ≥70. vs. Shingrix, the chosen arm showed comparable immunogenicity (100% vs. 96.9% anti-gE IgG, 89.7% vs. 93.5% CD4+ T-cell, 89.7% vs. 90.3% composite) with far lower Grade 2/3 reactogenicity (12.5% vs. 52.6% local, 27.5% vs. 63.2% systemic).

Collectively, these developed recombinant zoster vaccine candidates adopt diverse adjuvant strategies that deviate from the AS01B paradigm. AS01B is a liposome-based composite adjuvant built on the synergistic combination of the TLR4 agonist MPL and saponin QS-21, which drives robust Th1-biased cellular‑ and humoral‑immune responses, but faces manufacturing bottlenecks linked to QS-21 production and is associated with notable local and systemic reactogenicity. Domestic candidates explore multiple alternative routes: classical aluminum-salt depot adjuvants; squalene-based oil-in-water emulsions (MF59-like) alone or combined with TLR9-activating CpG oligodeoxynucleotides; liposomal formulations incorporating alternative pattern-recognition-receptor agonists such as Poly I:C; and one candidate (REC610, BFA01 adjuvant) that recapitulates the MPL/QS-21 liposome composition of AS01B with independent domestic raw-material supply. These alternative adjuvant systems aim to achieve balanced Th1/Th2 immunity while mitigating supply-chain limitations and potentially reducing reactogenicity. The choice of adjuvant is expected to profoundly shape the magnitude, polarization, and durability of antigen-specific humoral and T-cell immunity, and clinical head-to-head comparisons vs. Shingrix® will be critical to validate their real-world performance.

mRNA vaccines

Nucleic acid vaccines include DNA and mRNA platforms. The principles involve introducing antigen-encoded genetic material into host cells, which then expressed the protein to elicit adaptive immune response.35,36 The most important feature of nucleic acid vaccines is that they only induce an immune response against the expressed protein, which minimizes off-target side effects.35,36 While DNA vaccines offer advantages such as high stability and relatively simple production, their clinical application in humans has been heavily limited by poor immunogenicity. Unlike mRNA, DNA must traverse two membrane barriers (the plasma membrane and the nuclear envelope) to reach the nucleus for transcription; this dual-barrier trafficking restricts nuclear import and results in low antigen-expression levels. Of note, advanced delivery strategies such as in vivo electroporation can substantially improve the in-vivo delivery and clinical performance of DNA vaccines.37,38 Nevertheless, the need for a specialized companion electroporation device limits broad deployment for general prophylactic vaccination, confining such DNA-electroporation platforms primarily to targeted therapeutic indications including cancer immunotherapy39–41 and HPV-driven malignancies such as recurrent respiratory papillomatosis.42 Given these practical constraints, researchers have shifted their focus toward mRNA vaccines.43

The concept of mRNA-based therapeutics emerged over three decades ago. The first report of in vitro transcribed (IVT) mRNA in animals was published at 1990, when reporter gene mRNA was injected into mice and produced detectable protein.44 The subsequent study in 1992 demonstrated that vasopressin-encoding mRNA could induce a physiological response in rats.45 Despite these promising early results, the technology did not attract significant investment due to major challenges including mRNA instability, high innate immunogenicity, and inefficient in vivo delivery. The field was ultimately revolutionized by two critical breakthroughs: nucleoside modifications to reduce immune recognition and enhance stability, and the development of lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) as a highly efficient delivery system.46–49 These advancements enabled robust protein expression in vivo and paved the way for clinical application of mRNA vaccines, which was decisively demonstrated during the COVID-19 pandemic. Compared with conventional vaccines, mRNA platform has several significant advantages, including strong immunogenicity and a rapid flexible design and manufacturing process.50,51 Unlike DNA vaccines, mRNA is not associated with any risk of integration, increasing both potential safety and expression efficiency. Furthermore, the cell-free production avoids the use of cell culture or any toxic chemicals, minimizing risks of contamination and adverse reactions. Owing to these advantages, mRNA technology has gained immense attention and is now being developed for a wide range of applications beyond infectious diseases, including cancer immunotherapy, protein replacement therapy, in vivo gene editing, and in vivo CAR T cell therapy.52–54

Based on their molecular design and capacity for replication, mRNA vaccines can be categorized into non-replicating mRNA (nrRNA) and self-amplifying mRNA (saRNA).55 The nrRNA platform consists of a simplified structure featuring the antigen-coding sequence flanked by 5 ‘and 3’ untranslated regions (UTRs), a 5 ‘cap, and a 3’ poly(A) tail, all optimized for efficient translation.56 Since nrRNA has no replication elements in the sequence, its protein expression is transient and directly proportional to the delivered dose, often necessitating higher amounts of RNA to achieve sufficient immunogenicity.57,58 In contrast, saRNA vaccines are engineered from positive-sense single-stranded RNA virus genome (e.g., alphaviruses) and possess a more complex structure.49 They retain the functional genes that encode viral RNA replication machinery, while structural genes were replaced by the antigen of interest.59 This design provides a significant advantage over nrRNA. Upon delivery, the replicase drives intracellular amplification of the RNA, which further induced a relatively higher antigen expression and immune response, even at minimal doses.60 As saRNA is considerably longer and complex due to the incorporation of replication elements, it poses potential challenges for both the manufacturing and delivery carrier design.55,61,62

Lipid nanoparticles for mRNA delivery

The biological function of IVT mRNA, whether for protein replacement or eliciting an immune response, hinges on efficient delivery to target cells and subsequent expression in the cytoplasm. A significant challenge in this process is the inherent instability of naked mRNA. It is rapidly degraded by extracellular RNases. Moreover, naked mRNA usually exhibits poor cellular uptake due to electrostatic repulsion with negatively charged cell membrane. To address these limitations, advanced delivery systems have been developed to protect the nucleic acid and facilitate its intracellular transport. A number of delivery vehicles, such as liposomes, LNPs, polymer-based nanoparticles, and protein-based vehicles, have been explored for this purpose.63,64 Typically, these nanocarrier-mRNA complexes are internalized by antigen-presenting cells at secondary immune organs (lymph nodes, spleen, etc.), with subsequent endosomal escape enabling the release of mRNA into the cytosol for translation.63,65 Among these platforms, LNPs have emerged as the leading clinical vehicle for mRNA. Notably, all approved COVID-19 mRNA vaccines, such as Moderna’s mRNA-1273 and BioNTech/Pfizer’s BNT162b2, rely on LNP technology. The appeal of LNPs lies in their favorable characteristics, including a relatively simple manufacturing process, high biocompatibility, and a strong capacity for mRNA encapsulation.66 The efficacy of LNPs is attributed to a well-defined, four-component structure, where each lipid plays a distinct yet synergistic role. The ionizable lipid is critical for encapsulating mRNA during formation and interacting with the cell membrane, and facilitating the endosomal escape.67,68 A structural phospholipid, such as 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC), contributes to the nanoparticle’s stability and can modulate membrane properties to enhance fusion and improve in vivo distribution.69,70 Cholesterol is incorporated to reinforce the lipid bilayer’s stability and prevent premature leakage of encapsulated mRNA.71,72 Finally, a lipid-anchored polyethylene glycol (PEG) layer is essential for conferring colloidal stability, reducing nonspecific protein adsorption and prolonging circulation time.73 Clinical experience from authorized COVID-19 and RSV mRNA vaccines offers important real-world context for evaluating expected reactogenicity profiles in the herpes zoster target population, which is predominantly adults aged ≥50 years.74–76 For COVID-19 mRNA vaccines (BNT162b2, mRNA-1273), solicited adverse events are dominated by transient local injection-site pain and systemic symptoms including fatigue, myalgia, headache, and chills; the frequency of grade 3 reactogenicity generally declines with advancing age, though older adults still experience measurable LNP-driven innate immune activation.75,76 The recently approved mRNA RSV vaccine mRESVIA (mRNA-1345) for adults ≥60 years similarly demonstrates mostly mild-to-moderate local and systemic reactogenicity, with low rates of grade 3 events and few serious vaccine-related adverse events reported in large-scale trials, despite robust LNP-mediated innate stimulation.77–79 Across these approved mRNA platforms, reactogenicity is largely driven by the combination of lipid-nanoparticle-triggered innate sensing and mRNA-mediated pattern-recognition-receptor activation, rather than by the encoded antigen itself. These clinical observations carry direct implications for HZ mRNA vaccine development: adults ≥50 to 85 years represent an immunosenescent population where innate immune responsiveness to LNPs may differ from younger cohorts, and tolerability will be a critical practical barrier to two-dose uptake, analogous to the tolerability concerns limiting full adherence to Shingrix®. It is notable that Moderna’s discontinued HZ candidate mRNA-1468 showed comparable reactogenicity to Shingrix® in early-phase data, illustrating that favorable pre-clinical safety for mRNA-LNP constructs does not automatically translate to improved reactogenicity in human older-adult populations (https://feeds.issuerdirect.com/news-release.html?newsid=5150137996547682&symbol=MRNA and https://feeds.issuerdirect.com/news-release.html?newsid=5369469295110030&symbol=MRNA). Therefore, dose-finding, LNP formulation optimization, and careful monitoring of local and systemic solicited adverse events in Phase I/II trials are essential priorities for next-generation HZ mRNA vaccine candidates.

Application of RNA technology to VZV

The ORF region of VZV mRNA vaccine expressed gE protein, which is the most abundantly expressed protein on the surface of viral particles or infected cells. Moreover, gE protein is also the main antigen target for VZV vaccine, containing the major antigenic epitopes recognized by both antibodies and T lymphocytes.80,81 From 2020 to 2025, significant progress has been made in the application of mRNA technology for vaccine development. Currently, there are three mRNA vaccines that have been approved for use, including BNT162b2 (Pfizer-BioNTech) and mRNA-1273 (Moderna and the NIH) for COVID-19 and mRESVIA (Moderna) for respiratory syncytial virus (RSV). Facilitated by advances in the mRNA vaccine technology, the development of mRNA VZV vaccines has also achieved remarkable progress.

Preclinical studies

Preclinical studies of HZ mRNA vaccines.

Side-by-side comparison with protein subunit vaccine

Monslow et al. first reported preclinical evaluation of an mRNA-LNP vaccine platform targeting VZV gE protein.82 In non-human primates (NHP), VZV gE mRNA-LNP vaccine generated humoral and cellular immune responses comparable to the adjuvanted protein subunit vaccine (Shingrix®) and markedly superior to the live attenuated vaccine (ZOSTAVAX®). The mRNA platform showed favorable dose–response characteristics, with a two-dose regimen of 50 μg proving as effective as a single 100–200 μg dose. Although the cellular immune response at two weeks after the second dose of the mRNA vaccine (2 × 50 μg) was significantly higher than that of Shingrix® (2 × 50 μg), their levels became comparable by day 224. Other studies also report a significantly stronger short-term Th1-biased immune responses of VZV mRNA vaccines shortly after the second immunization when compared with Shingrix®.83–86 Bhattacharya et al. reported that, at 16 weeks post-boost, the protein vaccine maintained a significantly higher levels of antigen-specific bone marrow-derived memory B cells and effector memory CD4+ T cells compared to SM102 LNPs.87 These findings suggest that although mRNA vaccines elicit stronger short-term immune responses due to their Th1-biased immunity, their long-term immune memory and protection may be inferior to that of protein-based vaccines. Therefore, the immune characteristics of mRNA vaccines, particularly their long-term protective efficacy, require further investigation.

A major issue with Shingrix® is their high reactogenicity. Based on the comparative safety data, mRNA-LNP vaccines against VZV demonstrated a favorable and potentially superior safety profile compared to Shingrix®.83,87,88 The study by Li et al. found that, while both vaccine types induced a transient body weight loss in mice, a key difference was observed at the injection site. Histological evaluation revealed that Shingrix® produced a notable inflammatory cell infiltration, whereas VZV mRNA vaccine showed no observable inflammation, similar to the PBS control.83 Both vaccines showed no signs of systemic organ damage. The study by Bhattacharya et al. provided further insight, showing that their mRNA-LNP candidates induced significantly lower levels of key pro-inflammatory cytokines (MCP-1, CXCL-1, CXCL-10) in serum compared to the Shingrix® control.87 Huang et al. reported that Shingrix® induced transient body weight loss in mice and upregulated genes associated with platelet activation and pain responses in macaques, while mRNA vaccine ZOSAL showed no such adverse effects.85 Together, these findings indicate that mRNA-LNP vaccines are associated with reduced local reactogenicity and a weaker systemic inflammatory response.

Sequence optimization

Moderna’s patent US20230374079A1 evaluated several VZV gE antigen variants derived from the Oka strain for immunogenicity. The designs included the full-length gE (GE-full_AEAADA), a full-length mutant with a Y582G substitution in the endocytosis motif, and C-terminally truncated versions (gE-del-562 and gE-del-574) affecting the trans-Golgi targeting motif. A key construct, gE-del-574 & Y569A, combined the truncation with a Y569A point mutation to optimize trafficking and expression.89 Immunization results demonstrated that the gE-del-574 & Y569A variant elicited the most potent immune response, achieving the highest geometric mean titers (GMT) at doses of 10 μg and 2 μg. Based on this superior efficacy, Moderna selected the mRNA encoding the gE-del-574 & Y569A antigen as its lead VZV vaccine candidate.82 The conventional approach to mRNA sequence optimization, which primarily relies on codon optimization, critically fails to improve mRNA structural stability due to the prohibitively large search space, leaving a vast region of highly stable and efficient designs unexplored. To address this, Zhang et al. developed LinearDesign, a principled algorithm that efficiently screens mRNA sequences with optimal stability and codon usage by adapting lattice parsing from computational linguistics. This software was successfully applied to design a vaccine for the VZV, where the optimized gE antigen sequences demonstrated superior chemical stability, enhanced protein expression, and significantly increased antibody titers in mice compared to the codon-optimization benchmark.90

Building upon these foundational works, subsequent research has engaged in a more systematic comparative analysis of gE antigen design. These later studies have comprehensively evaluated a panel of gE sequences, including full-length vs. variously truncated versions, as well as wild-type sequences against those with targeted mutations. Bhattacharya et al. performed the head-to-head comparison of mRNA-LNP vaccine candidates targeting gE protein of VZV for protection against HZ.87 The candidates were designed encode different versions of the gE antigen: the full-length wild-type protein (gE-Full), a truncated form with a modified C-terminal domain (gE-T), and a soluble extracellular domain similar to the antigen in Shingrix® (gE-S), each formulated with either a novel NOF-based LNP or a comparator SM102-based LNP. The study demonstrates that all three NOF LNP-formulated candidates elicited potent humoral immunity, inducing gE-specific IgG antibody titers that were comparable to Shingrix® and superior to the SM102 LNP formulations. For cellular immunity, gE-FL and gE-T candidates delivered with NOF LNPs generated significantly higher frequency of polyfunctional CD4+ T cells and effector memory CD4+ T cells than the Shingrix® control, while the gE-S construct was notably less effective.

Cao et al. conducted a comparative study to evaluate the immunogenicity of mRNA vaccines encoding different structural variants of VZV gE.91 The researchers designed three LNP-encapsulated mRNA constructs: the extracellular domain of gE (gE-E), the full-length gE protein (gE, 623 aa), and a C-terminal double mutation (gE-M) with specific substitutions (Y569A and S593A/S595A/T596A/T598A) known to influence intracellular trafficking. Their findings demonstrated that gE-M construct consistently exhibited superior performance among all candidates, eliciting the highest gE-specific IgG antibody titers and enhanced T-cell responses, including increased frequencies of IFN-γ-producing splenocytes and elevated levels of antigen-experienced central memory CD4+ T cells.91 Furthermore, Liu et al. retained the ORF of gE-M, and incorporated three different non-coding regions (UTRs and polyA tail) from ZIKV mRNA vaccine (Z), Pfizer/BioNTech BNT162b2 mRNA vaccine (P), and Moderna’s mRNA-1273 vaccine (M). Comparison experiments suggested that gE-M-M induced the strongest humoral immune response and CMI among three candidates.84

Jiang et al. designed various mRNA constructs encoding different forms of gE antigen, including full-length gE (CVG206), truncated versions (CVG199: 1–593 aa; CVG200: 1–573 aa with Y569A mutation; CVG201: 1–546 aa lacking transmembrane domain; CVG202: 1–162 aa enriched with B/T cell epitopes), and fusion proteins with functional tags (CVG203: CVG200 + ubiquitin tag; CVG204: CVG200 + LAMP1 tag; CVG205: full-length gE + IE62 protein). In vitro expression analysis in HEK293T cells revealed that CVG199, CVG200, and CVG206 exhibited high surface gE expression levels, approximately fivefold or higher than CVG202, CVG203, and CVG205, which showed negligible expression and were excluded from further studies. Immunogenicity evaluation in naive mouse models demonstrated that CVG199, CVG200, CVG201, CVG204, and CVG206 induced significant humoral immune responses with robust gE-specific IgG antibody production. However, cellular immune responses varied considerably, with CVG206 (full-length gE) eliciting the strongest CD4+ and CD8+ T cell activation, as measured by IFN-γ and IL-2 secretion via ELISpot and intracellular cytokine staining (ICS). Immunogenicity distribution analysis indicated that the C-terminal region (approximately 546–623 aa) contributed over 60% of the total cellular immunity, explaining the superior performance of full-length constructs. Among all candidates, CVG206 consistently showed the highest immunogenicity, durable protection in long-term studies, acceptable safety profiles in nesting behavior tests, and superior thermal stability in lyophilized form, establishing it as the optimal vaccine candidate.88

Huang et al. systematically engineered four optimized gE-encoding mRNA vaccine candidates (ZOSAL-B, ZOSAL-BQ, ZOSAL-BM, and ZOSAL-BMQ) through strategies including signal peptide replacement, C-terminal truncation with a Y569A mutation for membrane enrichment, and the incorporation of an Exin21 mRNA-stabilizing motif.92 Immunogenicity studies in mice revealed that the ZOSAL-BMQ, which combined all these modifications, induced the highest anti-gE IgG titers, memory B cell frequencies, and antigen-specific T cell activation. Furthermore, mechanistic studies suggested that the enhanced immunogenicity of ZOSAL-BMQ was associated with cell membrane location, improved in-cell mRNA stability, and slightly increased activation of dendritic cells early after vaccination.92 Cheng et al. reported that removing N- or O-glycosylation sites from gE markedly reduced immunogenicity, underscoring the importance of native glycosylation for optimal antigenicity.93 Munoz-Moreno et al. reported a highly stable lyophilized mRNA vaccine for HZ by systematically designing and evaluating a panel of 10 different codon-optimized constructs targeting the VZV gE. The sequence alignment and engineering focused on modifying key domains of the wild-type gE protein to alter its subcellular localization, including mutations in the cytoplasmic tail (e.g., Y569A to disrupt trans-Golgi network targeting and Y582A to inhibit endocytosis), truncations of varying lengths, and the creation of secreted versions by fully or partially removing the transmembrane domain. A key finding was that the gE_WT Co2 candidate induced a stronger cellular immune response than gE_ms5 Co1 (1–581 aa), even though the latter showed greater cell surface localization. This suggests that robust T-cell activation, particularly the potent CD4+ and CD8+ responses observed with gE_WT Co2, may not be solely dependent on high surface expression and could be influenced by other factors such as intracellular processing and presentation pathways. In alignment with previous research, the secreted gE_ms6 variant (amino acids 1–538) demonstrated that the expressed gE protein was predominantly secreted extracellularly. Correspondingly, this construct elicited the lower levels of both antibody and cellular immune responses than gE_WT Co2 and gE_ms6 variant.94

Currently approved mRNA vaccines universally incorporate pseudouridine modifications to enhance mRNA stability and translational efficiency. However, this chemical modification is associated with drawbacks, including an increased propensity for ribosomal frameshifting, potentially reduced immunogenicity, and higher production costs. To address these limitations, Zhang et al. utilized an AI-driven codon optimization strategy to design an unmodified mRNA sequence (Vac07) encoding the gE antigen. This approach aimed to maintain high stability and potent protein expression while circumventing the issues linked to nucleoside modification. In mouse models, LNP-encapsulated Vac07 elicited robust immune responses, which was comparable or even superior to the licensed Shingrix® vaccine.95 However, a critical limitation is the lack of a thorough safety evaluation for this unmodified mRNA platform.

Using an AI-assisted screening pipeline (a model called RDTransformer), Dong et al. developed a series of mRNA-LNP vaccine candidates encoding different variants of the Varicella Zoster Virus (VZV) glycoprotein E (gE) antigen. The AI model was pre-trained on a large dataset of non-coding RNA sequences and then fine-tuned to predict which of the empirically designed gE variants would exhibit high protein expression and strong IgG antibody reactivity. Through this AI-assisted screening, nine promising candidate sequences were identified and synthesized for experimental evaluation: five full-length gE variants (VZV-gEmB, VZV-gEmD, VZV-gEmH, VZV-gEmK, VZV-gEmL) with mutations in the C-terminal domain, and four truncated variants (VZV-gEmN, VZV-gEmP, VZV-gEmA, VZV-gEmQ) with specific amino acid deletions in the C-terminal domain. All nine candidates elicited strong immune responses in mice, significantly surpassing the licensed vaccine Shingrix®. Notably, VZV-gEmD was identified as the optimal sequence. It demonstrated superior, long-lasting immune response in both mouse and non-human primate models.96

Collectively, these reports supported that optimal immunogenicity is generally achieved with cell-surface membrane-bound gE rather than secreted soluble gE forms. Trafficking-modulating mutations (e.g., Y569A disrupting trans-Golgi network targeting, combined with cytoplasmic-tail motif mutations) frequently promote antigen enrichment at the plasma membrane, which correlates with enhanced gE-specific antibody titers and polyfunctional CD4+/CD8+ T cell responses. By contrast, secreted gE variants that lack the transmembrane domain typically generate diminished cellular immunity, despite sometimes inducing measurable humoral responses. Nevertheless, strong T-cell activation cannot be attributed solely to magnitude of cell-surface antigen display. Certain constructs with moderate surface expression still elicited potent T-cell responses, indicating that intracellular antigen processing and MHC-I-dependent presentation also make critical contributions to overall immunogenicity. Accordingly, ideal mRNA gE antigen design should aim to balance improved membrane trafficking with preservation of efficient intracellular antigen-processing pathways.

Optimization of LNP components, administration routes, and heterologous immunization strategies

Cheng et al. developed a synergistic lipid nanoparticle (Syn-LNP) for an mRNA-based shingles vaccine by incorporating two ionizable lipids, cKK-E12 and L319, at an optimal molar ratio of 6:4. This novel Syn-LNP demonstrated superior mRNA delivery efficiency both in vitro and in vivo compared to LNPs formulated with a single ionizable lipid or the FDA-approved MC3-LNP, attributed to its enhanced endosomal escape capability. The vaccine candidate, mgE@Syn-LNP, encapsulating mRNA encoding the full-length gE, elicited robust and durable humoral and cellular immune responses in both C57BL/6 and BALB/c mice. The immune potency was comparable to the adjuvanted RZV (Shingrix®) and significantly higher than the live-attenuated vaccine. Furthermore, mgE@Syn-LNP provided complete protection in guinea pigs against a live viral challenge, preventing skin lesions and reducing viral load by over 2000-fold, while showing no significant toxicity.93 Wang et al. developed novel ionizable sterol lipids (ISLs) to create three-component lipid nanoparticles (ISL-3C-LNPs) for VZV mRNA vaccine delivery. The unique structure of the ISLs integrates a central ionizable amine headgroup, a covalently linked cholesterol moiety, and a branched aliphatic tail from ALC-0315, eliminating the need for free cholesterol. The lead formulation, CS22021-based ISL-3C-LNP, demonstrated remarkable localized biodistribution with mRNA expression restricted to the injection site after intramuscular administration, showing significantly reduced off-target effects in liver and spleen compared to conventional 4C-LNPs. This targeted delivery profile is attributed to the absence of free cholesterol, which alters protein corona formation and subsequent biological interactions. Notably, the platform elicited enhanced gE-specific CD8+ and CD4+ T cell activation compared to traditional 4C-LNPs, while maintained a superior safety profile.97 Similarly, Wang et al. developed a series of sterol-conjugated ionizable lipids (using cholesterol, ergosterol, or sitosterol). Screening revealed that lipids with branched tails (e.g., LNP1, 2, 5, 6, 7) achieved significantly higher mRNA transfection efficiency in mice compared to linear-tailed variants. The optimized LNP formulation, containing 40–50% ionizable lipid, demonstrated excellent stability, maintaining structural integrity and mRNA encapsulation at 4°C and 25°C. As a VZV gE mRNA vaccine, the lead candidate LNP1 elicited a superior immune response, inducing stronger T-cell activity and comparable antibody levels vs. commercial ALC-0315 LNPs. Crucially, LNP1 exhibited a greatly improved safety profile with minimal weight loss, reduced inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6), and no organ toxicity, highlighting its advantage as a safer and efficacious mRNA delivery platform.98 In another study, a novel ionizable lipid FS01 was developed, which integrated a squaramide headgroup and an ortho-butylphenyl tail.99 The experiments demonstrated that FS01-LNPs could enhance the mRNA delivery through unique π–π stacking interactions with nucleobases. In mouse models, FS01-LNPs demonstrated superior or comparable efficacy to FDA-approved LNPs (Dlin-MC3-DMA, SM-102, ALC-0315), eliciting robust immune responses in VZV and HBV prophylactic vaccine studies. Crucially, FS01-LNPs induced a well-balanced innate immune activation with significantly reduced inflammation and hepatotoxicity, highlighting an improved safety profile that positions FS01 as a promising candidate for mRNA therapeutics.98 Zhang et al. developed a library of 21 novel ionizable lipids, featuring a hydroxyethylamine headgroup and diverse hydrophobic tails. The design incorporated three distinct head groups and eight different tail structures. Three lead candidates, including YK-305, YK-310, and YK-319, were identified. They demonstrated excellent mRNA encapsulation, low cytotoxicity, and superior muscle-targeting capability in vivo. When formulated into LNPs carrying VZV gE-mRNA, these lipids induced potent and durable humoral and cellular immune responses, outperforming the SM-102 benchmark. The study confirms the platform’s potential for developing safer, more effective mRNA vaccines with reduced off-target effects.100

Luan et al. systematically compared the effects of subcutaneous (s.c.) vs. intramuscular (i.m.) administration routes on the immunogenicity of VZV mRNA vaccine and protein subunit vaccine in mice.101 The study found that the immunization route did not produce statistically significant differences in the elicited immune response for both mRNA vaccine (15 μg) and the subunit vaccine (10 μg) adjuvanted with B2Q (a potent combination of CpG ODNs and QS21). In contrast, the subunit vaccine formulated with a conventional alum adjuvant showed route-dependent efficacy, performing poorly in general.101 However, another study by Jiang et al. presents a different perspective on the importance of administration routes. In their evaluation of the CVG206 mRNA vaccine, a direct comparison revealed that the intradermal (i.d.) administration offered a significant advantage over the i.m. administration by enabling substantial dose-sparing. Specifically, the results demonstrated that a much lower “1/3 dose” (or 1/6 dose) administered via the i.d. route induced humoral (IgG antibody) and cellular immune responses that were comparable to, or even superior than, those achieved by a full “1 dose” (or 1/3 dose) delivered via the i.m. route.88

Besides, Liu et al. also evaluated the immunogenicity of two combination mRNA-LNP vaccines for respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) and VZV in a mouse model.102 The main finding was that both combination strategies, mixing two separate vaccines or co-encapsulating both mRNAs in a single LNP, elicited humoral and cell-mediated immune responses comparable to those of the individual vaccines. This demonstrated the feasibility of a combination RSV/VZV mRNA vaccine, which could simplify immunization schedules, reduce costs, and improve vaccine coverage, especially for the elderly.102

Our laboratory optimized the heterologous immunization strategies of VZV mRNA vaccine (RNA-gE) and protein subunit vaccine (PS-gE) in VZV-primed middle-aged C57BL/6 mice, which model human adults aged 42–45 years. The immunization groups included homologous regimens (2 × RNA‑gE and 2 × PS‑gE) and heterologous regimens (RNA-gE > PS-gE and PS-gE > RNA-gE). Results indicated that the mRNA vaccine-primed regimens induced a Th1-biased immune response, characterized by a lower IgG1/IgG2c ratio, whereas the protein vaccine-primed regimens prompted a Th2-biased response with a higher IgG1/IgG2c ratio. Crucially, the cell-mediated immunity was significantly stronger in the mRNA vaccine-primed regimens.83 Transcriptome analysis results suggested that RNA-gE > PS-gE and 2 × RNA‑gE elicited a significantly stronger activation in immune-related signaling pathways, including innate immunity, cytokine/chemokine signaling, antigen presentation, and B/T cell activation (Data not published).

Predictability of pre-clinical reactogenicity and translational limitations

Pre-clinical safety assessments in mice, guinea pigs, and non-human primates (NHPs) consistently demonstrated favorable local and systemic tolerability for VZV gE mRNA-LNP candidates when compared with Shingrix®; including minimal local inflammatory infiltration, reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine release, and absence of overt organ toxicity.83,92,99,103 Nevertheless, these pre-clinical reactogenicity read-outs exhibit limited predictive power for human clinical reactogenicity, as exemplified by Moderna’s mRNA-1468. Despite encouraging pre-clinical safety signals, early-phase human data revealed a reactogenicity profile broadly comparable to Shingrix®, ultimately contributing to program discontinuation before Phase 3 progression.88

Multiple biological factors underlie this translational gap. First, species-specific differences in innate immune sensing govern responses to LNPs and modified mRNA.99,104 Rodents typically display lower baseline innate immune activation upon LNP administration relative to older adult humans, particularly the immunosenescent target population (≥50 years of age) for herpes zoster vaccines.105,106 Age-dependent remodeling of pattern-recognition receptor signaling, inflammasome activity, and cytokine output in human elderly populations cannot be fully recapitulated in young laboratory animals, even when using aged mouse cohorts.107 Second, pre-clinical studies frequently rely on short-term observational endpoints: histopathology, serum cytokine panels, and body-weight change within days post-immunization. These metrics poorly capture subjective human reactogenicity endpoints such as injection-site pain, myalgia, chills, and fatigue, which are partially mediated by neuro-inflammatory signaling and pain-response pathways that are difficult to quantify in animals.107 Notably, macaque transcriptomic studies have shown that Shingrix® up-regulates platelet-activation and pain-related gene signatures that are not readily observable in mouse models, highlighting the advantage of NHPs yet also acknowledging inter-species divergence in neuro-immune crosstalk.103 Third, vaccine dose scaling and LNP biodistribution further complicate translation.50 Many pre-clinical immunizations employ relatively high per-kg body weight RNA doses in small animals; direct linear extrapolation to human clinical doses is unreliable. Even when NHPs are used, inter-species differences in LNP protein-corona formation, cellular uptake by myeloid cells, and magnitude of local lymph-node innate stimulation may alter reactogenicity outcomes.108 Heterologous prime-boost regimens and combination vaccine formats (e.g., co-formulated VZV-RSV mRNA) may further modify local and systemic inflammatory signatures, whose safety can only be definitively assessed in human trials.102

Therefore, pre-clinical reactogenicity data should be interpreted as a screening tool to exclude overtly toxic LNP-mRNA constructs rather than as definitive evidence of improved human tolerability.109,110 Pre-clinical work can guide lead selection by ranking candidates for relative inflammatory potential (e.g., comparing serum pro-inflammatory cytokine magnitudes across different ionizable-lipid formulations), but cannot substitute for careful Phase I dose-finding in the target elderly population.111,112 Clinical protocols for next-generation HZ mRNA vaccines should prioritize granular collection of both objective adverse events and subjective solicited reactogenicity symptoms across multiple age strata (including adults ≥70 years old), to properly evaluate whether optimized mRNA-LNP platforms can achieve the long-sought goal of reduced reactogenicity relative to RZV.

Clinical studies

Preclinical studies have demonstrated the promise of mRNA-based vaccines against HZ. However, clinical development for these candidates remains in the early stages (Phase I or II, Table 2). Moderna has completed a Phase I/II clinical trial for its candidate, mRNA-1468, evaluating its safety, reactogenicity, and immunogenicity in adults 50 years of age and older (NCT05701800). The results indicated that mRNA-1468 induced humoral immunity comparable to Shingrix® at 1 month following the second dose, while gE-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses trended higher than Shingrix®, particularly at middle and higher dose levels. The two-dose intramuscular regimen (Days 1 and 57) was generally well tolerated with predominantly mild-to-moderate solicited reactions. However, the reactogenicity profile was not improved compared to Shingrix® (https://feeds.issuerdirect.com/news-release.html?newsid=5150137996547682&symbol=MRNA and https://feeds.issuerdirect.com/news-release.html?newsid=5369469295110030&symbol=MRNA). Despite these immunogenicity signals, the development of mRNA-1468 was discontinued by Moderna in November 2025 before advancing to Phase 3. Furthermore, clinical trials for Immorna’s mRNA vaccine IN001 were approved by the FDA in September 2023, authorizing the start of its Phase I and II study (ClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT06375512 and NCT07205796). Pfizer, in collaboration with BioNTech, is conducting a Phase I/II clinical trial to evaluate a novel mRNA-based vaccine for shingles (ClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT05703607). The study aims to enroll approximately 900 healthy participants aged 50 to 85 years old. The trial was initiated on January 25, 2023, and the entire study is projected to conclude by August 20, 2030. The development of srRNA vaccines for HZ, exemplified by the candidate JCXH-105 from Immorna, represents a significant advancement in next-generation vaccine technology. This platform was designed to produce a robust and durable immune response by enabling the vaccine’s RNA to replicate within host cells and exerting the adjuvant effect of srRNA itself. Initial clinical progress is promising, with a completed Phase 1 study establishing the initial safety and immunogenicity of JCXH-105 in adults aged 50–69 (ClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT05871541). The research has now advanced to a larger, active Phase 2 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT06581575).

Based on the documents from China’s CDE, the development of mRNA vaccines for shingles is highly active, with at least seven distinct candidates having received implicit clinical trial approval. These candidates include vaccines from AIM Vaccine (CXSL2500154) and a collaboration between Rongcan Biotech and Shanghai Institute of Biological Products (CXSL2500040), three lyophilized vaccines from Abogen Biosciences (CXSL2500001/2/3), and another lyophilized form from Rigeno and Ruiqi Biotech (CXSL2400514). In addition, an VZV mRNA vaccine from Zhifei Longcom (CXSL2500961) was applied on November 11, 2025, which has not yet gained implicit clinical trial approval.

Discussion

Herpes zoster (HZ), while curable, is an infectious disease that causes immense pain and imposes a significant financial strain on patients. It results from the reactivation of the latent VZV virus, typically manifesting as a debilitating dermatomal rash and PHN in a substantial proportion of patients. The primary management strategies for HZ are reactive, involving antiviral therapy for acute infection and often complex, multi-modal regimens for chronic neuropathic pain. The risk and severity of HZ are predominantly influenced by age-related immunosenescence, as well as immunosuppression from medical conditions or therapies.113–115 Consequently, vaccination stands as the most effective public health intervention to reduce the incidence and associated burden of this disease. The first-generation live-attenuated vaccine (Zostavax), while pioneering, demonstrated suboptimal and waning efficacy, particularly in the very elderly and immunocompromised individuals.116 The subsequent adjuvanted RZV (Shingrix®) marked a major advance, offering high and sustained efficacy. However, its widespread adoption is hindered by significant challenges, including limited production yield, high cost, and significant reactogenicity.117–119 Therefore, the main objectives of the development of new HZ vaccines are safety, low cost, good immunogenicity, and simple immunization procedures, driving the exploration of next-generation platforms such as mRNA vaccines.

This review summarized the rapid progress in mRNA-based HZ vaccine development. Preclinical studies consistently demonstrate that mRNA-LNP vaccines encoding the VZV gE antigen elicit robust humoral and cellular immune responses. A key finding across multiple studies is that these mRNA vaccines often induce a potent Th1-biased immune response shortly after immunization, which is comparable or even superior to that induced by RZV.82–86 Notably, the Th1-polarizing nature of mRNA vaccines is not unique to VZV but is a consistent feature of the platform, as demonstrated in other disease contexts.35,120–123 For instance, transcriptomic analysis of the SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccine revealed an early and dominant activation of MHC class I-related genes (e.g., Tap1/2, B2m, H2-D1/K1).122,123 This is driven by the intrinsic properties of nucleoside-modified mRNA-LNP formulations, which act as potent adjuvants activating innate immune pathways (e.g., RIG-I, TLRs) and promoting cytokines like IL-12 and type I interferons.122 In contrast, protein subunit vaccine or inactivated virus vaccine (e.g., ZF2001) tend to favor MHC class II presentation and Th2-biased responses.120,122,123 This temporal pattern aligns with the intrinsic mechanism of mRNA vaccines: the encoded antigen is translated intracellularly, leading to immediate processing and presentation via the MHC I pathway, which promotes robust CD8+ T cell activation and a Th1-biased response. In contrast, protein-based vaccine or inactivated virus vaccine present pre-formed antigens externally, preferentially activate MHC II pathways and tend to skew toward Th2 immunity.120,124–127

Substantial research efforts have been dedicated to optimizing the antigen design for mRNA vaccines. Researchers systematically screened and evaluated a panel of VZV gE mRNA vaccine candidates featuring various sequence modifications to identify the most immunogenic construct.84,87–89,91–96 The screening process involved designing sequences encoding the full-length gE protein, C-terminally truncated versions (e.g., deletions in the transmembrane or cytoplasmic domains), and specific point mutations targeting key functional motifs.84,87–89,91–96 These motifs included the AYRV sequence (involved in TGN targeting, with mutations like Y569A), the YAGL endocytosis motif (e.g., Y582A mutation), and the acidic SSTT cluster (phosphorylation site mutations like S593A, S595A, T596A, T598A), often tested in combinations (e.g., double mutants).88,89,91,96 The immunogenicity, particularly the cellular immune response, was comprehensively compared in mice and non-human primate models against benchmarks like the licensed subunit vaccine Shingrix®. The consensus from multiple studies indicates that a full-length gE sequence incorporating specific C-terminal mutations, notably the Y569A mutation (disrupting TGN targeting and promoting cell surface expression) combined with mutations in the SSTT motif, consistently elicited superior and more balanced humoral and cellular immune responses compared to truncated or wild-type gE constructs. This optimized construct (e.g., referred to as ZOSAL-BMQ, VZV-gEmD, or gE-M in different studies) induced significantly higher gE-specific IgG titers, a favorable Th1-biased antibody profile (higher IgG2c/IgG1 ratio), and robust polyfunctional CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses that were often stronger and more durable than those induced by Shingrix®.91,92,96 The enhanced immunogenicity is hypothesized to stem from improved mRNA stability, translational efficiency, and altered subcellular localization of the antigen (increased membrane presentation) due to the rational design, which facilitates better antigen presentation and T-cell priming.91,92,96 Concurrently, innovations in delivery systems, such as novel ionizable lipids and simplified LNP formulations, aim to enhance mRNA delivery efficiency, improve biodistribution with reduced off-target effects (e.g., liver tropism), and further improve safety profiles.93,97–100 The exploration of heterologous prime-boost regimens (e.g., mRNA prime followed by protein boost) and combination vaccines (e.g., RSV/VZV) also presents intriguing strategies to potentially enhance immunity or simplify vaccination schedules.83,102

Despite the highly promising preclinical data, the clinical development of mRNA HZ vaccines is still in its early stages. Candidates from Moderna (mRNA-1468), Pfizer/BioNTech, and other biotech companies are undergoing Phase I/II trials.24 Preliminary clinical data indicate that mRNA-1468 was generally well tolerated with reactogenicity broadly comparable to Shingrix.24 More clinical data on shingles mRNA vaccines are still needed, particularly with alternative LNPs and self-amplifying mRNA designs that could potentially lower the required vaccine dose. In addition, the long-term durability of immune protection conferred by mRNA vaccines requires thorough evaluation, as some preclinical data suggest that while the initial response may be stronger, the long-term memory might differ from that induced by protein-based vaccines.87 The superior safety profile observed in animal models must be conclusively demonstrated in large-scale human trials. Furthermore, the real-world effectiveness and the performance of these vaccines in key vulnerable populations, such as the very elderly and immunocompromised individuals, are yet to be established.

In conclusion, mRNA vaccine technology holds immense potential to revolutionize the prevention of herpes zoster. It offers a compelling combination of strong immunogenicity, a potentially improved safety and tolerability profile, rapid and scalable manufacturing, and design flexibility. The current preclinical and early clinical data are highly promising, suggesting that mRNA vaccines could potentially match or even surpass the high efficacy of RZV while mitigating its main drawbacks. Ultimately, a successful next-generation vaccine, independent of the vaccine platform (RZV or mRNA), should have a much reduced reactogenicity profile than Shingrix® without compromising the excellent efficacy of the current vaccine.
