Shared Decision Making as a Policy and Clinical Framework for the Responsible Introduction of Repetitive Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation Therapy in Japan
Authors: Yoshihiro Noda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) has expanded in Japan since its inclusion under public insurance in 2019. This review examines systemic barriers to equitable adoption, ethical and legal challenges, and proposes Shared Decision Making (SDM) as a clinical and policy framework to ensure patient-centered care. Methods A narrative policy and practice review was conducted, synthesizing evidence from peer-reviewed studies, registry data, government guidance, and professional society statements. Comparative analysis with the United States and Europe was used to contextualize Japan’s distinctive financing and governance arrangements. Results Implementation of rTMS in Japan remains concentrated in tertiary hospitals and metropolitan centers, with limited diffusion to rural areas. Strict eligibility criteria under public insurance restrict access, while private-pay clinics have proliferated with heterogeneous protocols and variable disclosure practices. These structural imbalances generate inequities in access, ethical tensions regarding transparency, and legal concerns about informed consent. Additional barriers include limited public awareness, variability in provider expertise, and constrained outpatient consultation time. International comparisons highlight broader indications and more flexible reimbursement abroad, supported by stronger governance structures. Conclusion Embedding SDM into both insured and private rTMS practice in Japan is essential to safeguard equity, transparency, and sustainability. A pragmatic SDM framework, incorporating standardized information materials, AI-enabled decision aids, multidisciplinary collaboration, and registry-linked accountability, can transform rTMS adoption into a model of patient-centered governance. Institutionalizing SDM will not only improve treatment concordance and patient trust in Japan but also provide lessons for global mental health policyKeywords: Treatment resistant depression, Shared decision making, Patient-centered care, Health policy, Decision making

INTRODUCTION

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) has emerged internationally as a non-invasive neuromodulation technique with demonstrated efficacy in the treatment of major depressive disorder, particularly in patients who do not respond adequately to pharmacotherapy [1]. Since its initial regulatory approval in the United States in 2008 [2] and subsequent adoption across Europe and other regions, rTMS has increasingly been incorporated into clinical practice as a viable alternative or adjunct to conventional treatments [3]. In Japan, public health insurance coverage for rTMS was introduced in 2019, marking a significant milestone in the institutionalization of this therapy within the national health system. Following this policy change, clinical adoption has expanded, but remains concentrated in tertiary hospitals and specialized psychiatric centers [4].

Despite this progress, several systemic challenges have become apparent. Regional disparities in access remain pronounced, with advanced facilities clustered in metropolitan areas while smaller hospitals in rural regions face barriers related to equipment costs and workforce shortages [5,6]. At the same time, the growth of private-pay clinics, often operated by non-specialists, has introduced heterogeneity in treatment protocols and disclosure practices, raising concerns about the consistency, transparency, and safety of care [5,6]. These structural imbalances highlight the tension between expanding therapeutic availability and ensuring equitable, evidence-based practice.

The Japanese experience also illustrates broader ethical and policy issues relevant to international audiences. As rTMS continues to diffuse globally, questions of patient autonomy, informed consent, and equitable access are increasingly salient. In particular, the divergence between public insured and self-pay systems in Japan underscores how financing and regulatory frameworks shape the conditions under which patients make treatment choices [5,7]. Without systematic safeguards, patients may be left to navigate complex decisions with incomplete or inconsistent information, potentially undermining both safety and trust in psychiatric care.

Against this backdrop, Shared Decision Making (SDM) has gained recognition as a critical framework for aligning clinical practice with patient-centered principles [8]. SDM moves beyond traditional informed consent by actively incorporating patients’ values, preferences, and life circumstances into the treatment planning process [9,10]. In the context of rTMS, where multiple therapeutic pathways exist and long-term evidence is still evolving, SDM provides a structured approach to ensure that choices are transparent, concordant with patient priorities, and ethically sound [8,11]. Given Japan’s dual financing structure and heterogeneous disclosure practices, SDM is not only beneficial but structurally indispensable for ensuring fairness and transparency in rTMS delivery.

This review therefore examines the challenges associated with the introduction of rTMS in Japan, situates these within international trends in neuromodulation therapy, and discusses the role and significance of SDM in realizing patient-centered psychiatric care [11]. By highlighting both structural barriers and methodological opportunities, the analysis aimed to contribute to ongoing global dialogue on how innovative treatments can be responsibly integrated into health systems while safeguarding patient agency and equity [5,7].

METHODS

This article employs a narrative policy and practice review methodology designed to integrate clinical evidence with health system, regulatory, and ethical perspectives. A structured literature search was conducted using PubMed and Google Scholar, covering publications up to October 2025. Search terms included “rTMS,” “transcranial magnetic stimulation,” “treatment-resistant depression,” “shared decision making,” and “Japan.” Studies were included if they provided empirical data, policy analyses, or professional guidance relevant to rTMS implementation in Japan; editorials without empirical grounding were excluded. Themes were identified through iterative review and integrated using a narrative synthesis approach.

Rather than following a narrowly clinical protocol, the approach emphasizes a cross-domain interpretive synthesis relevant to the governance of rTMS in Japan and internationally. The review focuses on the introduction and dissemination of rTMS therapy in Japan since its inclusion under public health insurance in 2019, situating this experience within broader global trends in neuromodulation policy. The objective was not to generate a meta-analytic estimate of efficacy, but to examine how financing arrangements, institutional capacity, professional standards, and patient-centered frameworks such as SDM interact to shape equitable access and responsible implementation.

Literature sources included peer-reviewed publications, registry reports, government guidance, professional society statements, and selected grey literature. Electronic searches were conducted using combinations of the above keywords, and the author prioritized systematic reviews, consensus statements, real-world registry reports, and recent observational studies.

Grey literature sources included the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) notifications, available registry summaries (for example, the transcranial magnetic stimulation Database Registry Consortium in Japan), professional society guidance, and news or organizational reports describing service provision and reimbursement. Where direct Japan-specific legal, regulatory, or case law materials were available and relevant, these were used to inform the Ethical and Legal Issues section; where such Japan-specific materials were not available or inconclusive, the analysis is presented as comparative and interpretative rather than a definitive legal opinion.

Selection and synthesis were conducted by the author (Y.N.) with iterative cross-checking of key policy documents; as a narrative policy review, formal meta-analysis and quantitative risk-of-bias assessment were not conducted, although methodological limitations of included studies were considered in the synthesis.

RESULTS

Implementation status of rTMS therapy in Japan

With international reports supporting the efficacy of rTMS for treatment-resistant depression [12], rTMS was reimbursed in Japan in 2019 following the MHLW notification, adoption progressed mainly at university hospitals and specialized psychiatric centers [7]. Registry-based data from Japan show that by 2024 approximately 2,600 patients had received rTMS therapy [13].

Nevertheless, several challenges to wider dissemination remain. Existing empirical studies vary in methodological rigor, sample sizes in Japanese registry studies remain modest, stimulation protocols differ across institutions, and long-term outcome data are limited. These limitations require cautious interpretation of the current evidence base.

Regional disparities in access remain pronounced: tertiary hospitals in urban areas have relatively advanced adoption, whereas small- and medium-sized hospitals in rural regions face barriers to implementation due to capital requirements and difficulty securing trained personnel [5]. Other barriers include strict eligibility and billing requirements for rTMS under public insurance and limited accessibility to institutions providing rTMS [4,7], which have contributed to a recent increase in TMS clinics operating as private practices, particularly in large cities, as a means of meeting unmet demand [14].

Operational heterogeneity among adopting institutions is also problematic; treatment protocols and staffing arrangements vary considerably. Patient-level barriers persist as well, since public awareness and understanding of rTMS remain insufficient and information about the therapy does not always reach patients appropriately. Consequently, clinicians bear a strong responsibility for explaining treatment options, and the importance of decision-support interventions that enable patients to make informed, value-concordant choices has grown. In summary, although structural progress has been made, rTMS diffusion in Japan remains constrained by regional and resource limitations, operational variability, and gaps in patient understanding [15]. Financing and coverage arrangements

The introduction of rTMS into Japan’s public insurance system in 2019 created a dual financing landscape: public insured care restricted to treatment-resistant depression, and a rapidly expanding private-pay sector with broader indications [16]. This divergence has shaped patient cost burdens, eligibility criteria, and transparency of information provision, with implications for fairness and sustainability [7,11].

Governance and accountability in service provision

Operational heterogeneity across institutions, particularly between university hospitals and private clinics, has produced variability in treatment protocols, disclosure practices, and clinician expertise. Inadequate or inconsistent accountability mechanisms have raised concerns about informed consent, professional competence, and institutional responsibility for patient safety [7,11].

Equity of access and regional distribution

Service availability remains concentrated in tertiary urban centers, while smaller hospitals and rural regions face barriers related to capital investment and workforce shortages. These disparities highlight systemic inequities in access to advanced psychiatric technologies, reflecting broader patterns of maldistribution in Japan’s health system [7,11].

Patient engagement and decision-making processes

Limited public awareness of rTMS, combined with time constraints in outpatient encounters, restricts patients’ ability to make informed, value-concordant choices [11,17]. The absence of standardized information materials and decision-support tools further undermines transparency and patient agency, reinforcing the need for structured SDM [8].

Comparative international context

Compared with the United States and Europe, Japan’s combination of restrictive indication criteria, concentration of services in tertiary urban centers, and dual public-private financing produces distinctive systemic constraints and imbalances in clinical practice. These structural features underscore the need for robust SDM as both a clinical and policy instrument to mitigate inequities and strengthen governance [4,14].

Specifically, since the Food and Drug Administration cleared rTMS in the United States in 2008 [2], its adoption as a standard treatment option for treatment-resistant depression has advanced, supported by expanded private insurance coverage and an accumulating body of clinical trials that have strengthened treatment infrastructure [12,18]. In Europe, regulatory frameworks and clinical guidelines vary by country, but many jurisdictions have set comparatively broader indications for rTMS; clinical use has therefore extended beyond major depressive disorder to investigational applications in other psychiatric and neurological conditions [19,20]. In Japan, rTMS was reimbursed under public insurance beginning in 2019, but eligibility has been narrowly restricted to treatment-resistant depression and service provision remains concentrated in tertiary urban centers [16]. Consequently, regional disparities in access are pronounced. The expansion of privately paid clinics with heterogeneous indications and uneven standards for disclosure and accountability further complicates the landscape [14].

DISCUSSION

Summary of findings

Japan’s experience with the introduction of rTMS demonstrates that the diffusion of novel psychiatric technologies is governed not only by clinical efficacy but also by financing arrangements, institutional capacity, and governance structures. Since public insurance coverage began in 2019, provision has expanded mainly within tertiary centers, resulting in persistent regional disparities in access. Concurrently, the rapid growth of private-pay clinics has introduced wide variability in indications, disclosure practices, and institutional accountability. Together, these systemic features, including restrictive insuredcare eligibility, service concentration, and Japan’s dual publicprivate financing structure, have generated inequities in access, inconsistencies in patient protection, and ethical tensions regarding transparency and patient autonomy [5,7,14].

In addition, evolving evidence on long-term efficacy and safety, limited public awareness, and constrained outpatient consultation time further complicate equitable delivery [17]. These challenges underscore the need for policy instruments that safeguard fairness, transparency, and sustainability.

This review also outlines a feasible SDM workflow that can be applied across both insured and private-pay settings to support transparent and value-concordant decision making.

Four overarching policy priorities follow:

1) Equity of access: Targeted investment in workforce development and infrastructure to reduce regional disparities and extend advanced psychiatric technologies beyond tertiary urban centers.

2) Transparency and accountability: Institutionalization of standardized information materials, disclosure practices, and registry-linked monitoring across both insured and private sectors to protect patient autonomy and trust.

3) Sustainability of financing: Reimbursement policies that balance financial protection with responsible expansion of indications so that diffusion of rTMS does not compromise long-term health-system viability.

4) Patient agency through SDM: Embedding SDM as routine practice to transform rTMS adoption into a governance mechanism that strengthens legitimacy, aligns care with patient values, and enhances trust in psychiatric services.

Ethical and legal governance

The divergence between public insured and self-pay rTMS services raises important ethical and legal questions. Patients entering private clinics may face substantial financial burdens and unclear benefit-risk tradeoffs if disclosure standards are inconsistent [21]. From a governance perspective, this dual system risks undermining patient autonomy and informed consent, which are foundational to medical ethics [7,21].

Legally, inadequate disclosure of risks, costs, or eligibility criteria can expose institutions to disputes over medical contracts and malpractice [21]. Publicly reimbursed rTMS is subject to strict billing and eligibility criteria under MHLW rules, whereas selfpay practice operates under looser institutional constraints. This disparity raises questions about the appropriateness of indication expansion and the transparency of care [7].

To mitigate these risks, standardized information materials, explicit disclosure requirements, and professional competence standards across both public insured and private sectors are essential [21]. Embedding SDM as a legal-ethical safeguard ensures that patients’ choices are informed, value-concordant, and resilient against disputes [11]. Policy implications include:

• Standardizing consent templates that explicitly include efficacy estimates, treatment burden, adverse effects, and cost scenarios.

• Aligning institutional billing practices with transparent eligibility checks.

• Encouraging professional societies to issue explicit disclosure and competence standards for self-pay clinics.

• Establishing registry-linked audit mechanisms to monitor adherence to disclosure standards and outcomes.

Financing and sustainability

Restrictive eligibility criteria under public insurance limit access to rTMS, while private-pay expansion raises concerns about affordability and sustainability [5,7,14]. Policymakers must balance the financial protection offered by insurance with the need to broaden indications responsibly. Aligning reimbursement policies with transparent eligibility checks and registrylinked monitoring could mitigate inequities and ensure that expansion does not compromise sustainability [13].

At the same time, financing arrangements must anticipate long-term system viability. Overly rapid expansion without governance safeguards risks destabilizing psychiatric care financing. Embedding SDM into reimbursement frameworks, by requiring documentation of patient values and preferences, could serve as a novel accountability mechanism that links financing to patient-centered legitimacy [11].

Equity and regional distribution

Persistent regional disparities in rTMS provision reflect broader structural inequities in Japan’s health system. Concentration of services in metropolitan centers leaves rural populations disadvantaged, reinforcing the need for targeted investment in workforce training and infrastructure. Equity of access should be a central policy objective, supported by mechanisms that incentivize diffusion beyond tertiary hospitals [5,7].

International comparisons suggest that equity can be improved through regional workforce redistribution, telemedicine integration, and mobile treatment units. Japan’s policy design must therefore consider not only reimbursement but also structural incentives that encourage providers to extend services to underserved areas [22-24].

SDM as a policy instrument

While prior studies in Japan have examined patient involvement and informed consent in psychiatric care, none have proposed SDM as an integrated framework spanning both clinical decision-making and system-level governance for rTMS. The present review therefore advances a novel conceptualization of SDM as a cross-cutting mechanism that addresses structural inequities arising from Japan’s dual financing system.

Beyond its clinical utility, SDM can function as a governance tool to strengthen transparency, accountability, and patient agency. By standardizing information provision and structuring patient–clinician dialogue, SDM offers a practical means of harmonizing patient experience across insured and private settings.

Operationalizing SDM under current structural and regulatory constraints

To translate SDM from a conceptual framework into routine clinical practice, a feasible workflow must align with Japan’s current regulatory and structural conditions.

Stepwise SDM process tailored for public insurance– based and private pay rTMS settings

1) Initial assessment and eligibility confirmation: The clinician explains the patient’s clinical status, treatment history, and eligibility for rTMS under both public insurance and private pay pathways, ensuring that the patient understands which options are genuinely available.

2) Presentation of available treatment pathways: The clinician outlines differences in protocol standardization, scheduling flexibility, and out-of-pocket costs, providing information permitted under current regulations.

3) Elicitation of patient priorities: The clinician invites the patient to express preferences regarding treatment intensity, scheduling constraints, financial considerations, and desired speed of improvement.

4) Option deliberation and clarification: Clinician and patient jointly review how each pathway aligns with the patient’s priorities, clarifying misunderstandings and providing balanced information.

5) Shared selection of a treatment plan: A mutually agreed upon treatment plan is selected and documented, including rationale and anticipated adjustments.

Transparent, patient-centered presentation of options, costs, and expected outcomes

1) Treatment options: Under public insurance, the protocol follows standardized high frequency left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex stimulation over 20–30 sessions. Private pay options may allow alternative protocols such as theta burst stimulation or accelerated schedules.

2) Cost structures: Public insurance covers most treatment costs with a fixed copayment. Private pay treatment requires full out-of-pocket payment but may offer greater flexibility.

3) Expected outcomes: Both pathways have evidence supporting effectiveness, but time to response and treatment intensity may differ.

Model scenario demonstrating how SDM reconciles system level differences

A patient requiring rapid symptom improvement due to work responsibilities is informed that public insurance offers standardized daily sessions with limited scheduling flexibility, whereas private pay clinics may offer accelerated protocols at higher cost. Through SDM, the patient selects the private pay accelerated protocol, prioritizing rapid functional recovery.

By institutionalizing SDM through standardized consent templates, decision-support tools, and multidisciplinary collaboration, health systems can ensure that patients make informed, value-concordant choices [25-27].

Embedding SDM across both insured and private rTMS settings can harmonize disclosure standards, reduce variability in patient experience, and reinforce trust in psychiatric services. Moreover, SDM can serve as a bridge between clinical practice and policy oversight, transforming patient dialogue into a mechanism of governance [8,11,25].

Limitations of SDM in rTMS implementation

While SDM is valuable, several limitations must be acknowledged:

• Evolving evidence base: Long-term efficacy and safety data remain incomplete, undermining reliability of information provision.

• Patient cognitive constraints: Severe depression may impair comprehension and judgment, limiting decision-making capacity.

• Clinician variability: Differences in training and communication skills affect the quality of SDM practice.

• Institutional time pressures: Outpatient consultations often last only 5–10 minutes in Japan, making meaningful SDM difficult.

These constraints risk reducing SDM to a formal gesture rather than a substantive process. Policy must therefore address training, consultation time, and institutional incentives to ensure authentic SDM practice [17,26,28].

Future of SDM in rTMS practice

As rTMS becomes more widely adopted, SDM is expected to evolve:

• Evidence accumulation will strengthen the informational foundation for patient-centered decisions.

• AI-enabled decision aids will allow personalization of information to each patient’s profile and values.

• Triangular SDM models involving patients, families, and clinicians will expand decision-making beyond the dyadic interaction.

• Pre-visit digital tools will mitigate time constraints by enabling patients to clarify values before consultations.

International policy comparisons

Compared with the United States and Europe, Japan’s restrictive coverage criteria and dual financing arrangements create distinctive systemic constraints. International experiences suggest that broader indications and more flexible reimbursement can facilitate diffusion, provided they are supported by robust governance and accountability mechanisms [12,30].

Japan’s case underscores the importance of coupling technological adoption with policy instruments, such as SDM, that safeguard equity and sustainability. Japan’s experience illustrates the risks of rapid diffusion of psychiatric technologies without adequate governance safeguards [4,5,7].

Policy implications

To address these challenges, several policy measures are recommended [11,13,16,21,30]:

• Standardization of information materials and consent processes across insured and private rTMS services.

• Registry-linked monitoring and audit mechanisms to ensure accountability and transparency.

• Professional society guidance on disclosure and competence standards for self-pay clinics.

• Targeted investment in workforce and infrastructure to reduce regional disparities.

• Integration of SDM into routine practice as both a clinical and policy requirement.

CONCLUSION

This review examined the current status and challenges of rTMS implementation in Japan and highlighted the essential role of SDM in promoting patient-centered care. Persistent disparities in access, widening variation in private-pay services, and inconsistent disclosure practices continue to hin-der informed and value-concordant decision making [12,29]. Embedding SDM into routine practice, supported by standardized information materials and structured communication, can ensure that treatment choices reflect both clinical evidence and patient priorities [31]. At the policy level, SDM also functions as a governance mechanism that enhances transparency, accountability, and equity across insured and private rTMS services [32,33]. Integrating SDM into the governance of rTMS may therefore support responsible innovation while maintaining fairness and sustainability in mental health care.
