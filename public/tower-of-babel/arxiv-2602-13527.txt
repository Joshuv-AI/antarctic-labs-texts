Bruno ideal and the variety of centers for singular germs of vector fields
María Martín-Vega, Daniel Panazzolo

Abstract
. Given a logarithmic analytic vector field ∂ \partial , we consider the formal ideal B ⁡ ( ∂ ) B(\partial) defined by the collinearity locus of the semi-simple and nilpotent components of ∂ \partial . Assuming that the eigenvalues of the linear part of ∂ \partial satisfy the so-called Bruno arithmetic condition, we prove that B ⁡ ( ∂ ) B(\partial) is in fact an analytic ideal. Moreover, ∂ \partial is analytically normalizable when restricted to this ideal. As a consequence, the vanishing locus V V of B ⁡ ( ∂ ) B(\partial) is an analytic variety, and the foliation defined by ∂ | V \partial|_{V} is analytically linearizable.



1.
Introduction

Based on ideas of A.D. Bruno, the aim of this paper is to study the existence of analytic invariant sets related to the convergence of the Poincaré-Dulac normalization for germs of singular analytic vector fields.

Let us start by briefly recalling some basic concepts and results of normal forms. The detailed definitions will be given in the next subsection.
A germ of singular vector field
\partial
is called a
Poincaré-Dulac normal form
(or simply
normal form
) if we can decompose it in the form

\partial=S+R

where:

(1)

S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}
is linear diagonal vector field

(2)

R
is a non-linear vector field with a nilpotent linear part.

(3)

S
and
R
commute, i.e. the Lie Bracket
[S,R]
vanishes.

As remarked by Martinet in
[
16
]
, these properties are equivalent to say that
S
and
R
are respectively the semi-simple and nilpotent components in the
Jordan decomposition
of
\partial
(see subsection
2.3
). This definition extends to the case where
\partial
is a singular
formal
vector field, i.e. to a vector field of the form
\partial=\sum f_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}
where each component
f_{i}
is a formal series without constant term.

Let us express the condition
[S,R]=0
more explicitly. Using the logarithmic basis
\{x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{n}
for vector fields, we can expand

R
in the form

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{{\underline{m}}\in M_{i}}a_{{\underline{m}}}\mathbf{x}^{{\underline{m}}}x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}

where
M_{i}
is a subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{i-1}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq-1}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n-i-1}
.
The condition
[S,R]=0
requires that the non-zero terms in the above expansion of
R
are such that

S(\mathbf{x}^{{\underline{m}}})=0
. In other words, that
\langle\lambda,{\underline{m}}\rangle=\sum\lambda_{i}m_{i}
vanishes. These terms are called
resonant
and the relations
\langle\lambda,{\underline{m}}\rangle=0
are called
resonances
. We say a resonance is
negative
if one of the components
m_{i}
of
{\underline{m}}
is equal to
-1
, and
positive
otherwise.

From the classical results by H. Poincaré
[
19
]
and H. Dulac
[
8
]
, it is known that every singular analytic vector field
\partial
is formally conjugate to a normal form. In other words, there exists a formal change of coordinates, which we identify to an automorphism
\Phi
of the ring of formal series, such that

\Phi\partial\Phi^{-1}=S+R

where
S,R
are
formal
vector fields satisfying the above conditions (1),(2) and (3). In this case, we say that
\Phi\partial\Phi^{-1}
is a
formal normal form
of
\partial
and that the conjugating automorphism defines a formal
normalization
.

The analogous problem of obtaining an
analytic
normalization presents two main difficulties. Either the normal form itself is not analytic—reflecting the fact that the formal series expansion of
R
given above has zero radius of convergence—or the conjugating automorphism
\Phi
fails to be analytic.

Let us now discuss one of the main results on analytic normalisation proved by Bruno in
[
2
]
.
We start by the introducing the following notation. To a vector of complex numbers
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
we associate the sequence
\omega_{k}=\omega_{k}(\lambda)
, indexed by
k\in\mathbb{N}
, defined by

\omega_{k}\;=\;\min\bigl\{\,|\langle\lambda,\underline{m}\rangle|\;:\;\underline{m}\in M_{1}\cup\cdots\cup M_{n},\ \underline{m}\text{ non-resonant},\ \|\underline{m}\|\leq 2^{k}\bigr\}.

where we note
\|{\underline{m}}\|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|m_{i}|
and the sets
M_{i}
are defined as above.

We now state the following conditions on a germ of singular vector field
\partial
:

\omega
-condition
:
We say that
\partial
satisfies the
Bruno’s arithmetic
\omega
-condition
(or simply the
\omega
-condition) if it has a normal form
S+R
with semi-simple part

S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}
such that the sequence
\omega_{k}=\omega_{k}(\lambda)
satisfies

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}-\frac{\log\bigl(\omega_{k}\bigr)}{2^{k}}<\infty.

Geometric
A
-condition
: We say that
\partial
satisfies the
geometric A-condition
if it has a normal form
S+R
such that

R=fS

for some formal series
f\in C[[\mathbf{x}]]
. In other words, the nilpotent component is
collinear
to the semi-simple component.

The
\omega
-condition is easily seen to be independent of the choice of
normal form, since the spectrum
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})

is clearly invariant under coordinate changes. As we shall see later, as a simple consequence of the uniqueness of the Jordan decomposition, the
geometric
A
-condition is also independent of the choice of normal form.
Consequently, both the
\omega
-condition and the geometric
A
-condition
are intrinsically determined by
\partial
.

Remark 1.1
.

We remark that the geometric
A
-condition formulated here is strictly stronger than the original
A
-condition formulated by Bruno, but it is better adapted to the formulation of our main result. We refer the reader to
[
2
]
or to
[
24
, sec. 10.3]
for the precise statement of the
A
-condition.

Theorem 1.2

(Bruno, 1971)
.

Let
\partial
be a germ of singular analytic vector field fulfilling the
\omega
-condition and the geometric
A
-condition. Then,
\partial
is analytically conjugated to a normal form.

This result of Bruno has been generalized in several different directions. For instance, L. Stolovitch proved a normalization result for foliations generated by perturbations of commuting semi-simple vector fields under conditions of complete integrability. C. Chavaudret
[
5
]
proved an analog of Bruno’s result for the normalization of a vector field in the vicinity of a
n
-torus.

We observe that, in the absence of resonances, the geometric
A
-condition is automatically satisfied and the
\omega
-condition implies that
\partial
is analytically conjugated to a linear diagonal vector field. In this case, we simply say that
\partial
is
analytically linearizable
.

In this context, the Theorem
1.2

represents a significant weakening the arithmetic conditions for linearizability previously imposed by other authors, such as C.-L. Siegel
[
22
]
and V.-A. Pliss
[
18
]
. We also note that such condition is satisfied by almost all tuples of complex number, and that Perez-Marco and Yoccoz
[
17
]
proved it to be a necessary and sufficient condition for analytic linearizability in dimension
n\leq 2
.

On the other hand, in the presence of resonances, the geometric
A
-condition is rarely satisfied and
imposes a strong restriction in order to guarantee analytic normalization.

In this setting, Bruno’s insight, which we want to investigate in this paper, can be summarized as follows: rather than imposing the geometric
A
-condition from the outset, one should consider the problem of analytic linearizability restricted to the
formal locus
where this condition holds.

To enunciate our main result, we need introduce the notion of
Bruno ideal
. In what follows, we will always assume that
\partial
is a
logarithmic vector field
, i.e. a vector field preserving the coordinate hyperplanes
x_{i}=0
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

Suppose initially that
\partial
is a (possibly formal) logarithmic vector field in
normal form with nonvanishing semisimple part
S
. Then
\partial
can be
written as

\partial=(1+f)\,S+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}g_{j}\,T_{j},

where

T_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{ji}\,x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}

is an arbitrarily chosen family of diagonal vector fields such that the set

\{S,T_{1},\ldots,T_{n-1}\}
is
\mathbb{C}
-linearly independent, and where

f,g_{j}\in\mathbb{C}[[x]]
are formal power series without constant term.
The normal form condition (3) stated above is equivalent to requiring

S(f)=S(g_{j})=0,\qquad j=1,\ldots,n-1.

where
S
acts on
\mathbb{C}[[x]]
as a derivation. In this setting, we define the
Bruno ideal
of
\partial
by

B(\partial)=\langle g_{1},\ldots,g_{n-1}\rangle\subset\mathbb{C}[[x]].

More generally, for an arbitrary singular vector field
\partial
, we define

B(\partial)
as follows: Choose a (formal) conjugated normal form

\delta=\Phi\partial\Phi^{-1}
, and define
B(\partial)
as the pullback
of
B(\delta)
under the coordinate change induced by
\Phi
. As we shall prove, it turns out
that the resulting formal ideal
B(\partial)
is independent of the choice of
the conjugated normal form and is therefore intrinsically attached to

\partial
. Additionally, such formal ideal is invariant by
\partial
, i.e.

\partial\big(B(\partial)\big)\subset B(\partial)

and we can consider the problem of reduction to normal form
modulo
B(\partial)
. Here, we refer the reader to subsection
2.5
for the precise definition of a normal form modulo an invariant ideal.

Our main result can now be stated as follows:

Main Theorem
.

Let
\partial
be an analytic logarithmic vector field satisfying the
\omega
-condition. Then, the ideal
B(\partial)
is analytic and there exists an analytic automorphism
\Phi
such that the conjugated derivation
\delta=\Phi\partial\Phi^{-1}
is in normal form modulo
B(\delta)
.

We recall that a formal ideal
I\subset\mathbb{C}[[x]]
is called
analytic
if it is generated by analytic germs, i.e. if

I=\big(I\cap\mathbb{C}\{x\}\big)\,\mathbb{C}[[x]]

Therefore, it follows from the Theorem that the vanishing locus of
B(\partial)
defines a germ of analytic subvariety
V(B(\partial))\subset(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)
, which is moreover invariant by
\partial
. We say such variety is the
Bruno variety
of
\partial
.

The following result is an important
dynamical
consequence of the theorem.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
\partial
be an analytic vector field satisfying the
\omega
-condition. Then,
\partial
defines a linear foliation in restriction to its Bruno variety.

1.1.
Previous works

In
[
3
]
and, more recently, in
[
4
]
, Bruno introduced two
formal ideals
\mathcal{B}(\partial)\supseteq\mathcal{A}(\partial)
that are
closely related to the ideal
B(\partial)
defined above.

In these references, he asserts that, if the eigenvalues of
S
(the semi-simple part of
\partial
) are pairwise commensurable then
\mathcal{A}(\partial)
is analytic. More generally, if these eigenvalues are purely imaginary and satisfy the
\omega
-condition, then ideal
\mathcal{B}(\partial)
is analytic.
Unfortunately, these assertions are not proved in the cited works, and we have
not been able to locate any complete proofs in the references quoted by Bruno.

Note that, under the additional assumption that
\partial
is
logarithmic, the ideal
\mathcal{B}(\partial)
originally defined by Bruno coincides with the ideal

B(\partial)
as defined above.

The Main Theorem is also closely related to a result of L. Stolovitch
[
23
]

on the existence of analytic invariant varieties, which itself generalizes to
higher dimensions a classical result of Dulac
[
7
]
.

More precisely, let us state Stolovitch’s result in the present logarithmic
setting. Suppose that all resonant monomials associated with the diagonal vector
field
S
belong to the semigroup generated by the monomials

x^{{\underline{m}}_{1}},\ldots,x^{{\underline{m}}_{s}}
, for some vectors

{\underline{m}}_{1},\ldots,{\underline{m}}_{s}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
.
Assume moreover that
S
satisfies the
\omega
-condition. Then, Stolovich proves that, up to
analytic conjugacy, any analytic logarithmic vector field with semisimple part

S
can be expanded in the form

\partial=S+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{s}g_{ij}(x)\,x^{{\underline{m}}_{j}}\right)x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}},

where the
g_{ij}
are analytic germs.

Note that Stolovitch’s result remains valid without the assumption that

\partial
is logarithmic, provided instead that all resonances are positive. We refer the reader to the original paper for the detailed definitions.
We also emphasize that the above expansion is particularly
useful for controlling the growth of solution curves of the vector field, since
it allows one to view them as
perturbations
of the resonant monomials.
Indeed, for each
j
, one has
\partial(x^{{\underline{m}}_{j}})
lying in the
ideal generated by

\langle x^{{\underline{m}}_{1}},\ldots,x^{{\underline{m}}_{s}}\rangle
. We observe however that the above expansion is not a
normal form
in the sense (1),(2),(3) since the non-linear part does not necessarily commute with
S
.

In the logarithmic setting, our main result implies Theorem
1.2
.
In fact, the proof will follow closely the inductive scheme originally introduced by Bruno in
[
2
]
,
but conveniently adapted and reformulated using the elegant approach developed by Martinet in his Bourbaki
survey
[
16
]
. In that survey, Martinet provides a detailed sketch
of the proof of Bruno’s theorem in the non-resonant case. His method relies on the
Jordan decomposition and on the natural
S
-graduation of the space of vector
fields, which significantly simplifies the analytic estimates required to
establish the analyticity of the conjugating map.

During the preparation of this work, we became aware of a recent preprint by
Romanovski and Walcher
[
20
]
, in which the authors propose
a simplified proof of Theorem
1.2
. Their approach makes
systematic use of the natural homogeneous
S
-graduation, in the same spirit
as Martinet’s method, but without using the formalism of Jordan decompositions.

In
[
9
]
, Ecalle uses the powerful formalism of mould-comould expansions and arborifications to give analternative proof of Theorem
1.2
in the non-resonant case (see also
[
11
]
). It would be interesting to study if Ecalle’s formalism can be adapted to prove our main theorem.

1.2.
Plan of the paper

In Section
2
, we introduce the basic concepts and definitions, as
well as several classical results that will be used in the proof of
the Main Theorem. We also prove that the first part of the Main Theorem (analyticity of
B(\partial)
) is indeed a consequence of the second part (existence of an analytic normalization modulo
B(\partial)
).

The proof of the theorem is divided into two clearly distinguished parts,
presented in Sections
3
and

4
. In
Section
3
, we establish a purely formal
normalization result by developing the inductive procedure required to reduce
a formal vector field to a normal form modulo its Bruno ideal.

In Section
4
, we show that, under the

\omega
-condition, it is possible to control the domains of analyticity of
the successive coordinate changes constructed in the previous section when
they are applied to an analytic vector field.

Finally, in Section
5
, we discuss several examples
of Bruno ideals and present some applications of the main result.

2.
Derivations and normal forms modulo ideals

In this section, we present some basic definitions and results that will be used in the paper. Since the normal form results will be explained in the formalism of a Jordan decomposition, we will hereby consider vector fields as
derivations
.

2.1.
Some facts about free modules and derivations

Let
R
be a ring
R
such that
\mathbb{Q}\subset R
and let
M
be a free
R
-module. We list some useful facts.

(M.1)

For each ideal
I\subset R
, the quotient module
M/IM
is a free module over the ring
R/I
.

(M.2)

The second exterior power
M\wedge M
is a free module over
R
.

The
coefficient ideal
of an element
m\in M
is the ideal

\Gamma(m)\subset R
generated by the coefficients of the expansion of
m

with respect to any arbitrary free basis
\{e_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in A}
of

M
.

The
ideal of collinearity
of two elements
m,n\in M
is the ideal

\Gamma(m\wedge n)
, i.e. the coefficient ideal of
m\wedge n
, seen as an
element of the second exterior power
M\wedge M
.

Consider now the
R
-module
{\operatorname{Der}}(R)
of derivations on
R
. Given an ideal
I\subset R
, we
denote by
{\operatorname{Der}}(-\log I)
the submodule of derivations
\partial\in{\operatorname{Der}}(R)
such that
\partial(I)\subset I
. In this case, we will also
say that
I
is
\partial
-invariant
or that
I
is
preserved
by

\partial
. Note that
{\operatorname{Der}}(-\log I)
contains
I\,{\operatorname{Der}}(R)

as a submodule.

A derivation
\partial\in{\operatorname{Der}}(-\log I)
induces a derivation in the
quotient ring
A=R/I
, which we will denote by
\partial_{A}
. Given an
element
f\in R
, we can write
(f\partial)_{A}=f_{A}\partial_{A}
, where
f_{A}

denotes the class of
f
in
A
.
The following fact will also be useful:

(D.1)

Suppose that
\partial_{1},\partial_{2}\in{\operatorname{Der}}(-\log I)
are such
that
(\partial_{1})_{A}=(\partial_{2})_{A}
in
A=R/I
. Then
\partial_{1}-\partial_{2}
belongs to the submodule
I{\operatorname{Der}}(R)
.

(D.2)

If
\partial\in{\operatorname{Der}}(-\log I)
then
\partial({\operatorname{rad}}(I))\subset{\operatorname{rad}}(I)
, where
{\operatorname{rad}}(I)
denotes the radical of
I
. Moreover, if
I
is radical and
I=P_{1}\cap\cdots\cap P_{n}
is its minimal prime decomposition, then
\partial(P_{i})\subset P_{i}

for each
i
. See for instance
[
21
]
.

2.2.
Formal and analytic logarithmic derivations. Graduations and Collinearity

We denote by
\mathcal{O}=\mathbb{R}\{x\}
or
\mathbb{C}\{x\}
the
ring of germs of real (resp. complex) analytic functions in
n
variables

x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.

Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}=\mathbb{R}[[x]]
or

\mathbb{C}[[x]]
be the completion of
\mathcal{O}
with respect to the
Krull topology defined by
\{\mathfrak{m}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Given an ideal
I\subset\mathcal{O}
, we denote by
I\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
the ideal generated by
I
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{}\mathcal{O}}
. Since
\mathcal{O}\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
is a
faithfully flat extension, we have

I\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\cap\mathcal{O}=I.

We say that an ideal
\tilde{J}\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
is

analytic
if it has the form
\tilde{J}=I\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}

for some
I\subset\mathcal{O}
.
Each element
f\in\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
is defined by a formal series

f=\sum_{\underline{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}a_{{\underline{m}}}x^{\underline{m}}

where
a_{\underline{m}}\in C
and
x^{\underline{m}}=x_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{m_{n}}
. We define the support of
f
by

\operatorname{supp}(f)=\{{{\underline{m}}}:a_{{\underline{m}}}\neq 0\}

and define the order and degree of
f
by

(1)

\mathrm{ord}(f)=\inf\{\|{\underline{m}}\|:{\underline{m}}\in\operatorname{supp}(\partial)\}\quad\text{ and }\quad\deg(f)=\sup\{\|{\underline{m}}\|:{\underline{m}}\in\operatorname{supp}(\partial)\}

where
\|{\underline{m}}\|=\sum_{i}|m_{i}|
. As usual we convention that
\deg(0)=-\infty
and
\mathrm{ord}(0)=\infty
. Note that
f
is a polynomial iff
\deg(f)<\infty
, or equivalently, if
\operatorname{supp}(f)
is finite.

We denote by
{\operatorname{Der}}\left(\mathcal{O}\right)
the module of derivations in
\mathcal{O}
. An element of

{\operatorname{Der}}\left(\mathcal{O}\right)
is fully determined by its action on the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. Hence, we can identify
{\operatorname{Der}}\left(\mathcal{O}\right)
with the
the free module of vector fields

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}

with coefficients
a_{i}\in\mathcal{O}
.
A derivation
\partial\in{\operatorname{Der}}\left(\mathcal{O}\right)
is called
local
if
\partial(\mathfrak{m})\subset\mathfrak{m}
. We also define

{\operatorname{Der}}_{\log}(\mathcal{O})\subset{\operatorname{Der}}\left(\mathcal{O}\right)
as the submodule of derivations preserving the
ideal
I_{\log}=(x_{1}\cdots x_{n})
. It is a free module with basis
\left\{x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\right\}
.

We define similarly the free modules
{\operatorname{Der}}_{\log}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\right)\subset{\operatorname{Der}}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\right)
of derivations and logarithmic
derivations in the ring
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
. Each derivation
\partial\in{\operatorname{Der}}\left(\mathcal{O}\right)
extends uniquely to a derivation in
{\operatorname{Der}}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\right)
. We say that a derivation
\partial
in
{\operatorname{Der}}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\right)
is
analytic
if
\partial
is the extension of a derivation
in
{\operatorname{Der}}\left(\mathcal{O}\right)
.

In this paper, we consider only logarithmic derivations. To simplify the notation, we will henceforth denote the modules

{\operatorname{Der}}_{\log}(\mathcal{O})
and
{\operatorname{Der}}_{\log}(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}})
simply by
\mathcal{D}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
, respectively.

We will frequently consider logarithmic derivations of the form

x^{{\underline{m}}}L(\lambda)

where
{\underline{m}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n},
and
L(\lambda):=\sum\lambda_{j}x_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
We will call them
monomial derivations
. Note that a monomial derivation acts on monomials by the formula

(2)

x^{\underline{m}}L(\lambda)\,\big(x^{\underline{n}}\big)=\langle\lambda,{\underline{n}}\rangle\,x^{{\underline{m}}+{\underline{n}}}

and Lie bracket of two monomial derivations is given by

(3)

\big[\,x^{{\underline{m}}}L(\lambda),\mathbf{x}^{{\underline{n}}}L(\mu)\,\big]=\langle\lambda,{\underline{n}}\rangle\,\mathbf{x}^{{\underline{m}}+{\underline{n}}}L(\mu)-\langle\mu,{\underline{m}}\rangle\mathbf{x}^{{\underline{m}}+{\underline{n}}}L(\lambda)

and in particular,
[L(\lambda),L(\mu)]=0
. We further observe that each derivation
\partial\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
has a unique expansion as a sum of monomial derivations,

(4)

\partial=\sum_{{\underline{m}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\mathbf{x}^{{\underline{m}}}\,L(\lambda_{{\underline{m}}})

with each
\lambda_{{\underline{m}}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
We call this the
monomial expansion
of
\partial
.

Based on this expansion, define the support of
\partial
by

\operatorname{supp}(\partial)=\{{{\underline{m}}}:\lambda_{{\underline{m}}}\neq 0\}

and define the order and degree of
\partial
by

(5)

\mathrm{ord}(\partial)=\inf\{\|{\underline{m}}\|:{\underline{m}}\in\operatorname{supp}(\partial)\}\quad\text{ and }\quad\deg(\partial)=\sup\{\|{\underline{m}}\|:{\underline{m}}\in\operatorname{supp}(\partial)\}

We say that
\partial
is
k
-flat
if
\mathrm{ord}(\partial)\geq k
. Note that this is equivalent to say that

\partial(\mathfrak{m})\subset\mathfrak{m}^{k+1}.

In analogy to the case of germs, we say that
\partial

polynomial
if
\deg(\partial)<\infty
, or equivalently, if
\operatorname{supp}(\partial)
is finite.

It will be also convenient to consider expansions of derivations with respect to a fixed set of diagonal derivations. A collection of diagonal derivations
L(\mu_{0}),\ldots,L(\mu_{n-1})
will be called a
logarithmic basis
if the vectors
\mu_{0},\ldots,\mu_{n-1}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
are linearly independent. In this case, each derivation

\partial\in\mathcal{D}
has a unique expansion

(6)

\partial=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}g_{i}\,L(\mu_{i})

with coefficients
g_{0},\ldots,g_{n}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
. We call it the
logarithmic expansion of
\partial
with respect to the basis
\{L(\mu_{i})\}
.

Remark 2.1
.

The transition between the monomial expansion (
4
) and the above expansion can be easily obtained by expressing each diagonal derivation
L(\lambda_{\underline{m}})
in the basis
\{L(\mu_{0}),\ldots,L(\mu_{n-1})\}
and then regrouping the terms.
In other words, it suffices to consider the
n\times n
matrix
\mathbf{T}
of change of basis between the canonical basis
\{e_{1},..,e_{n}\}
and the basis

\{\mu_{0},\ldots,\mu_{n-1}\}
of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

We shall frequently use the following fact: A diagonal derivation
S=L(\lambda)
induces a graduation

(7)

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\mathbb{C}}\operatorname{Gr}_{\alpha}(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}},S),

where
\operatorname{Gr}_{\alpha}(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}},S)
is the linear subspace of series
f\in\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
such that
S(f)=\alpha f
. We also observe that
S
induces a graduation on the formal derivations,

(8)

3.
Formal reduction to normal form modulo the Bruno ideal

The Proposition
2.15
from the last subsection implies that the Main Theorem will hold if we prove that, under the
\omega
-condition, any analytic derivation is analytically conjugated to a derivation in normal form modulo its Bruno ideal.

To prove this result, we adapt the Newton-type inductive scheme introduced by Bruno. The main idea is to construct a sequence of coordinate changes that successively transform
\partial
into a (relative) normal form, modulo truncations to jet spaces of increasing order. As in Newton’s method, the order of truncation is doubled at each step.

As mentioned in the introduction, we begin by describing how the normalization procedure works for formal derivations, for which (formal) convergence is straightforward to establish and requires no arithmetic assumptions.

3.1.
The adjoint operator

The inductive reduction to normal form relies on the invertibility of the following adjoint operator, acting on
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
,

\operatorname{ad}_{(1+f_{0})S}(\cdot)=[\,(1+f_{0})\,S,\,\cdot\,]

where
f_{0}
is a series belonging to
\mathfrak{m}\cap\operatorname{Gr}_{0}(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}},S)
. As a first remark, we observe that such operator restricts to a linear map

\operatorname{Gr}_{\alpha}(\widetilde{\mathcal{D}},S)\rightarrow\operatorname{Gr}_{\alpha}(\widetilde{\mathcal{D}},S)

for each
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, as it easily follows from (
9
). In other words,
\operatorname{ad}_{(1+f_{0})S}
is a graduated operator of degree
0
.
The following result will play a crucial role in the formal and analytic reduction to a normal form.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f_{0}
be as above and suppose that
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. Then,
\operatorname{ad}_{(1+f_{0})S}
restricts to an isomorphism of
\operatorname{Gr}_{\alpha}(\widetilde{\mathcal{D}},S)
. More precisely, its inverse is given explicitly by

\operatorname{ad}_{(1+f_{0})S}^{\,-1}\,x^{\underline{m}}L(\mu)=\frac{x^{\underline{m}}}{\alpha\,(1+f_{0})}\;L(\mu)+\frac{x^{\underline{m}}\,L(\mu)(f_{0})}{\alpha^{2}\,(1+f_{0})^{2}}\;S

where
L(\mu)
is any diagonal derivation and
x^{\underline{m}}
denotes a monomial in
\operatorname{Gr}_{\alpha}(\widetilde{\mathcal{O}},S)
.

Proof.

This is an straightforward computation using the following decomposition

\operatorname{ad}_{(1+f_{0})S}(U)=[(1+f_{0})S,U]=\alpha\,(1+f_{0})\cdot\left(U-\frac{U(f_{0})}{\alpha\,(1+f_{0})}S\right)

where
U\in\operatorname{Gr}_{\alpha}(\widetilde{\mathcal{D}},S)
. Note that the linear operator inside the parenthesis has the form
\mathbf{Id}-\mathbf{N}
with
\mathbf{N}
being the linear operator

U\mapsto\mathbf{N}(U)=\frac{U(f_{0})}{\alpha\,(1+f_{0})}S

and, since
S(f_{0})=0
, we have
\mathbf{N}^{2}=0
(i.e.
\mathbf{N}
is nilpotent of degree
2
). Furthermore, it commutes with the scalar operator of multiplication by
\alpha\,(1+f_{0})
. Therefore, the inverse of
\operatorname{ad}_{(1+f_{0})S}
can be written as
\frac{1}{\alpha\,(1+f_{0})}(\mathbf{Id}-\mathbf{N})
. This is precisely the expression given in the enunciate.
∎

Let us state a simple Corollary which will be used later. Given a derivation
\delta\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
, we denote by

\delta=\sum_{\alpha}\;\delta_{\alpha}

its decomposition with respect to the graduation given in (
8
), and let
\delta_{\ast}=\delta-\delta_{0}
denote the sum of those graduated components of
\delta
of non-zero degree.

Corollary 3.2
.

(1) Given a derivation
\delta\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
, the equation

\big[(1+f_{0})S,U\big]=\delta_{\ast}

has a solution
U\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
. Moreover, such solution is unique if we assume that
U=U_{\ast}
.

(2) If we further assume that
\mathrm{ord}(\delta)\geq K
, i.e. that
\delta\in\mathfrak{m}^{K}\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}
for some
K\in\mathbb{N}
, then the same holds for the unique solution defined above.

The second part of the enunciate is an immediate consequence of the explicit form
\operatorname{ad}_{(1+f_{0})S}^{\,-1}
given by the Lemma.

4.
Analytic reduction to normal form modulo the Bruno ideal

In this section, we prove that under the
\omega
-condition, the formal reduction to normal form constructed in the previous section is analytic, provided that
\partial
is analytic.

As we mentioned in the introduction, the key analytic estimates rely on the original ideas of Bruno, detailed in
[
2
]
. However, we will reformulate these estimates in terms of the natural
S
-graduation of the space of vector fields given by (
8
), in the spirit of Martinet’s survey
[
16
]
.

For the sake of clarity, the next subsection is devoted to establishing some basic general facts about majorant norms for analytic germs and analytic derivations.

4.1.
Analytic
r
-norms

We denote by
D_{r}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:|x_{i}|<r,\ i=1,\ldots,n\}
polydisk of radius
r>0
, and by
\mathcal{O}(D_{r})
the ring of series which are absolutely convergent on the closure
D_{r}
. In other words, a series

f=\sum_{{\underline{m}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}a_{{\underline{m}}}\mathbf{x}^{{\underline{m}}}

belongs to
\mathcal{O}(D_{r})
if its
r
-majorant norm
(or simply
r
-norm
)

\|f\|_{r}:=\sum_{{\underline{m}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}|a_{{\underline{m}}}|r^{\|{\underline{m}}\|}

is finite, where we note
\|{\underline{m}}\|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|m_{i}|
. Observe that each
f\in\mathcal{O}(D_{r})
is an analytic function on
D_{r}
, and hence defines an analytic germ. Reciprocally, every analytic germ admits a representative in
\mathcal{O}(D_{r})
for some
r>0
. We also have

\|f\cdot g\|_{r}\leq\|f\|_{r}\cdot\|g\|_{r}

which implies that
\mathcal{O}(D_{r})
is a Banach algebra with respect to the above norm.

Similarly, we define the
r
-norm for a logarithmic derivation
\partial
as follows. We consider its monomial expansion (see
4
)

\partial=\sum_{{\underline{m}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\mathbf{x}^{{\underline{m}}}L(\lambda_{{\underline{m}}})

and set

\|\partial\|_{r}:=\sum_{{\underline{m}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}r^{\|{\underline{m}}\|}\,\|\lambda_{{\underline{m}}}\|

where, as previously, we define
\|\lambda\|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}|
. We denote by
\mathcal{D}(D_{r})
the space of logarithmic derivations with finite
r
-norm. As above, we observe that each
\partial\in\mathcal{D}(D_{r})
defines a germ of analytic derivation, i.e. an element of
\mathcal{D}
. We also have

\|f\cdot\partial\|_{r}\leq\|f\|_{r}\cdot\|\partial\|_{r}

for all
f\in\mathcal{O}(D_{r})
and
\partial\in\mathcal{D}(D_{r})
.

Remark 4.1
.

Consider the expansion of
\partial
with respect to an arbitrary logarithmic basis
\{L(\mu_{0}),\ldots,L(\mu_{n-1})\}

\partial=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}g_{i}\,L(\mu_{i}),

as in subsection
2.2
. Then it follows from Remark
2.1
that there exists two constants
0<c<d
(depending only on
\mu_{0},\ldots,\mu_{n-1}
) such that

c\;\|\partial\|_{r}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\|g_{j}\|_{r}\,|\mu_{j}|\leq d\;\|\partial\|_{r}

In particular,
\partial\in\mathcal{D}(D_{r})
if and only if
g_{0},\ldots,g_{n-1}\in\mathcal{O}(D_{r})
.

We now list some useful properties of the
r
-norm.

(1)

Let
\varphi=(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n})\in\mathcal{O}(D_{\rho})^{n}
be such that
\max\{\|\varphi_{1}\|_{\rho},\ldots,\|\varphi_{n}\|_{\rho}\}\leq r
for some
\rho,r>0
. Then,
\varphi(D_{\rho})\subseteq D_{r}
and the composed function
f\circ\varphi=f(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n})
satisfies

\|f\circ\varphi\|_{\rho}\leq\|f\|_{r}

for all functions
f\in\mathcal{O}(D_{r})
.

(2)

Suppose that
\mathrm{ord}(f)\geq k
(i.e. that
f\in\mathfrak{m}^{k}
), and let
0<\rho<r
. Then, we have the following relation between the
\rho-
norm and the
r-
norm.

\|f\|_{\rho}=\sum_{{\underline{m}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}|a_{{\underline{m}}}|\rho^{\|{\underline{m}}\|}=\sum_{{\underline{m}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}|a_{{\underline{m}}}|\left(\frac{\rho}{r}\right)^{\|{\underline{m}}\|}r^{\|m\|}\leq\left(\frac{\rho}{r}\right)^{k}\|f\|_{r},

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
a_{{\underline{m}}}
vanishes for
\|{\underline{m}}\|<k
.

(3)

Similarly for
\partial\in\mathcal{D}(D_{r})
a derivation such that
\mathrm{ord}(\partial)\geq k
and
0<\rho<r
, we have

\|\partial\|_{\rho}\leq\left(\frac{\rho}{r}\right)^{k}\|\partial\|_{r}.

(4)

Given a monomial derivation
x^{{\underline{m}}}L(\lambda)
and a monomial
x^{{\underline{n}}}
, the identity (
2
) gives the estimate

(19)

\|x^{\underline{m}}L(\lambda)\,x^{\underline{n}}\|_{r}\leq|\langle\lambda,{\underline{n}}\rangle|\,\|x^{{\underline{m}}+{\underline{n}}}\|_{r}\leq\|{\underline{n}}\|\,\|x^{\underline{m}}L(\lambda)\|_{r}\,\|x^{\underline{n}}\|_{r}

where we have used the trivial inequality
|\langle u,v\rangle|\leq\|u\|\,\|v\|
.

Similarly, given two monomial derivations
x^{{\underline{m}}}L(\lambda),\,x^{{\underline{n}}}L(\mu)
, the identity (
3

for their Lie bracket:

\begin{split}\bigl\|\,\big[\,x^{{\underline{m}}}L(\lambda),\,x^{{\underline{n}}}L(\mu)\,\big]\,\bigr\|_{r}&\leq\|\lambda\|\,\|{\underline{n}}\|\,\bigl\|x^{{\underline{m}}+{\underline{n}}}L(\mu)\bigr\|_{r}\\
&\quad+\|\mu\|\,\|{\underline{m}}\|\,\bigl\|x^{{\underline{m}}+{\underline{n}}}L(\lambda)\bigr\|_{r}\\
&\leq\bigl(\|{\underline{n}}\|+\|{\underline{m}}\|\bigr)\,\bigl\|x^{{\underline{m}}}L(\lambda)\bigr\|_{r}\,\bigl\|x^{{\underline{n}}}L(\mu)\bigr\|_{r}\end{split}

(5)

More generally, if we consider two derivations

with monomial expansions

\|\,[H,K]\,\|_{r}\leq\sum_{{\underline{m}},{\underline{n}}}(\|\underline{m}\|+\|\underline{n}\|)\;\|\mathbf{x}^{{\underline{m}}}L(\lambda_{{\underline{m}}})\|_{r}\,\|\mathbf{x}^{{\underline{n}}}L(\mu_{{\underline{n}}})\|_{r}

In particular, if we assume that

and

\begin{split}\|[H,K]\|_{r}\leq(\,\deg(H)+\deg(L)\,)\;\|H\|_{r}\,\|K\|_{r}\end{split}

(6)

Similarly, if

is a polynomial function and

\|\partial\,\big(p)\|_{r}\leq\deg(f)\,\|\partial\|_{r}\,\|f\|_{r}

4.2.
Bounds on

-norms under conjugation

The following technical lemma provides the first key ingredient in the induction procedure for analytic normalization. Its purpose is to control the norm of a derivation under a coordinate change defined by the flow of a polynomial vector field. In general, such a norm cannot be controlled on a polydisk of fixed radius, since it tends to

as one approaches the boundary. The central idea is therefore to work with three carefully chosen nested polydisks, under the constraint that their radii do not shrink too much.

We emphasize that this result is a direct consequence of several technical lemmas established in

]

Let

, and consider three radii

\frac{\rho_{1}}{r}=\left(\frac{1}{C2^{k}}\right)^{C/2^{k}},\quad\frac{r}{\rho}=\left(\frac{1}{C2^{k}}\right)^{C/2^{k}}\Omega_{k}

for some constant

to be specified later and

some
sequence of real numbers satisfying
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\Omega_{k}=1
, also to be specified later.
Further, let

5.
Examples and applications of the Bruno ideal

We now present applications of the Main Theorem and its corollary, starting with two-dimensional vector fields.

Example 5.1
.

Let
\partial=S+R
be a formal logarithmic vector field with linear part

S=\lambda\,x\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+\mu\,y\frac{\partial}{\partial y},

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
and
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
.
Assuming that
\partial
is in normal form, the perturbation term
R
can be written as

R=GS+F\,y\frac{\partial}{\partial y},

where
F,G\in\mathbb{C}[[x,y]]
are formal power series without constant term having an expansion of the form

F=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(k,l)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\\
\lambda k+\mu l=0\end{subarray}}a_{k,l}x^{k}y^{l},\quad G=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(k,l)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\\
\lambda k+\mu l=0\end{subarray}}b_{k,l}x^{k}y^{l}.

The Bruno ideal is therefore given by

B(\partial)=\big\langle F\big\rangle
and we distinguish two cases:

•

Resonant case.

If
\mu/\lambda\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
, then writing
\mu/\lambda=-m/n
in irreducible terms, we have

F,G\in\mathbb{C}[[x^{n}y^{m}]]\cap\mathfrak{m}.

•

Non-resonant case.

If
\mu/\lambda\notin\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
, then
F=0
,
G=0
.

Let us now consider an arbitrary analytic vector field
\delta
that is formally conjugated to
\partial
, and examine the information provided by the Main Theorem.

Assume first that
\partial
is in the resonant case. In this situation, the
\omega
-condition is always satisfied, and therefore

V(B(\delta))\subset(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)

is a germ of an analytic variety.

We further distinguish two subcases.
If
F=0
, then
V(B(\delta))=(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
, and, up to an analytic change of coordinates, we may write

\delta=u\left(nx\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-my\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right),

for some analytic unit
u\in\mathbb{C}\{x^{n}y^{m}\}
. On the other hand, if
F\neq 0
, then
V(B(\delta))
defines a germ of an analytic variety with either one irreducible smooth component (if
\mu=0
) or two irreducible smooth components (if
\mu\neq 0
), intersecting transversely.

Finally, assume that
\partial
is in the non-resonant case and that the
\omega
-condition holds. Then
V(B(\delta))=(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
, and the Main Theorem implies that
\delta
is analytically linearizable.

We observe that the resonant case above with F=0 corresponds to a classical theorem of Dulac: if a germ of a two-dimensional vector field with non-vanishing linear part admits a formal first integral, then it also admits an analytic first integral.

More interesting examples arise in higher dimension.
The following result provides an alternative proof of Lyapunov’s theorem for logarithmic vector fields.
Consider a germ of real analytic vector field of the form

\partial=-y\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+x\frac{\partial}{\partial y}+\lambda z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+R

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{\ast}
and
R
is
1
-flat. By the Center Manifold Theorem,
\partial
always has a formal invariant manifold
\widetilde{W}^{c}
tangent to the
z=0
direction, called its
center manifold
. We say that
\partial
if formally integrable on its center manifold if the restriction of
\partial
to
\widetilde{W}^{c}
has a formal fist integral. On the other hand, we say that
\partial
is analytic integrable on its center manifold if
\widetilde{W}^{c}
is a germ of analytic variety and the restriction of
\partial
to
\widetilde{W}^{c}
has an analytic first integral.

Proposition 5.2

(Lyapunov’s theorem
[
14
]
)
.

\partial

is formally integrable on its center manifold if and only if it is analytic integrable on its center manifold.

Proof.

Suppose that
B(\partial)=0
, then the result is obvious since the foliation is necessarily linearizable, being
z=0
its analyitically integrable center manifold. Now suppose
B(\partial)\neq 0
and that the vector field is formally integrable in
\widetilde{W}^{c}
. Then it follows from the definition of
B(\partial)
that
\widetilde{W}^{c}
is an irreducible component of
B(\partial)
, therefore it is analytic.

∎

Related to Lyapunov’s theorem, we get also a relative easier proof for the following result, see
[
1
,
6
,
13
]
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\partial
be a vector field in the hypotheses of the previous Proposition. There is an infinite family

of cycles accumulating at the origin if and only if

Proof.

We only prove the direct implication since the converse is a direct consequence of the previous result. We note that there are many steps in common with respect to the proof in

Since the eigenvalues of the linear part of

We start making a cylindric blow-up centered at the stable manifold. The fiber
\pi^{-1}(0)
is the only cycle defined by the strict transform
\widetilde{\partial}
of
\partial
in
\pi^{-1}\Omega
. Based on this cycle, we can define the Poincaré map on an analytic transverse section
\Delta

\Delta

We will study the cycles of

The map

By the properties of the center manifolds

∎

Suppose that the linear part of
