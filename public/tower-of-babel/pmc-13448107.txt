E-scooter riders sustain more head and internal injuries than motorcyclists and cyclists in England and Wales
Authors: Garikai Kungwengwe, Michael W. Gach, Siri Gowda, Edelyne Tandanu, Douglas Donnachie, Zakhar Menkin, Simarjit Sandhar, David Bodansky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electric scooters are increasingly used across the United Kingdom, yet national evidence on injury patterns, severity and exposure-adjusted risk remains limited. This study combined the National Major Trauma Registry for England and Wales (trauma admissions, 2020–2022) with operator-reported rental incident data from 18 cities (2020–2024), covering more than 33 million rides and 77.8 million kilometres. The two sources were analysed separately, not linked; across 38,440 records combined (15,247 registry trauma patients and 23,193 operator-reported incidents), e-scooter trauma showed a distinct injury profile, with higher adjusted relative risks than motorcyclists of traumatic brain injury (RR 3.45, 95% CI 3.19–3.75), internal organ injury (1.46, 1.35–1.57) and vascular trauma (3.24, 2.72–3.86), but lower fracture risk (0.80, 0.74–0.85); traumatic brain injury risk also exceeded that of cyclists (1.74, 1.61–1.88). Traumatic brain injury accounted for over one third of adult injuries, and paediatric riders formed a larger share of the trauma burden than in comparator groups. Helmet use was rare, female sex was associated with higher injury severity, and exposure-adjusted collision rates varied with area-level deprivation. A standardised severity framework enables clearer comparison across transport modes and supports evidence-based regulation, infrastructure planning and targeted public health interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-59829-5.

Introduction

Electric scooters (e-scooters) have evolved rapidly from novelty devices to a common form of urban mobility.1 In the United Kingdom (UK), millions of privately owned e-scooters operate outside of regulatory frameworks that require registration, insurance and taxation, and these unregulated vehicles vastly outnumber those permitted under government-sanctioned rental trials.2 Many devices are modified to circumvent safety features, leaving policymakers uncertain about how to enforce the law and protect riders.

In the UK, e-scooter injury rates and patterns resemble those of pedal cyclists,3 and the injury burden has grown with rising e-scooter use, as documented by a systematic review of 34 studies.4 The Department for Transport (DfT) recorded more than 1,200 collisions involving e-scooters and 1,387 casualties in 2023, including six deaths and 416 serious injuries,5 but these statistics likely underestimate the true burden because incidents are not formally coded and must be extracted from free-text entries, capturing fewer than10% of events.6 Approaches to regulation vary internationally: some countries mandate helmets, impose speed caps or employ geofencing, whereas others have banned e-scooters outright.7 Case series suggest that riders of privately owned e-scooters may suffer more severe injuries than rental users or other vulnerable road users; however, such risks are typically reported per injured patient rather than per unit of distance travelled or exposure.5.

Existing data are predominantly derived from emergency departments and trauma registries, describing only the most severe cases.8 Injured riders are typically young and male,9 with frequent head injuries10 and a high burden of fractures and other musculoskeletal trauma;11,12 helmet use is rare.13,14 Heterogeneous case definitions and outcome measures hinder comparisons across jurisdictions and vehicle types.15 Comparative studies are scarce: a Finnish registry provided limited information on injury types or relative risks,16 while U.S. surveillance data reported higher injury and hospitalisation rates for e-scooter and e-bike riders than for cyclists but did not adjust for exposure or injury site.4 A nationwide UK cohort of 293 e-scooter and 2,538 bicycle trauma cases found that e-scooter riders were more likely to require care at a major trauma centre and to sustain serious head and limb injuries, yet it did not account for exposure or include motorcyclists.3 Evidence on factors influencing injury severity is similarly limited. One cross-sectional study linked road riding and frequent use to a two-fold increase in serious injury,17 whereas larger cohorts found that age was not an independent predictor after adjustment.18 Few investigations have integrated trauma registry records with usage data to generate exposure-adjusted injury rates or to consider socioeconomic status.19 Consequently, policy decisions often rely on extrapolated counts that overlook differences in exposure and social context.

This study therefore undertook a national comparison of injury patterns and relative risks across e-scooter, motorcycle and pedal-cycle collisions in the UK. By linking the National Major Trauma Registry (NMTR)20 with incident reports from the country’s largest e-scooter operator,21 the study generated age-stratified injury profiles by body region, injury type and severity, adjusting for socioeconomic status and exposure. Injuries were classified according to the ICD-10 Injury Mortality Diagnosis matrix to permit robust severity stratification and international comparability.22 The objectives were to compare injury patterns and severity between e-scooters, motorcycles and pedal cycles in the UK trauma population, and to examine associations between age, sex and deprivation and injury severity among e-scooter riders. It was hypothesised that e-scooter riders sustain more head and internal injuries than motorcyclists and pedal cyclists, and that male riders and those from more socioeconomically disadvantaged areas experience greater injury severity. By integrating minor with severe injuries and accounting for exposure, this study offers a comprehensive assessment of e-scooter safety to inform regulation and public health strategies.

Methods

A retrospective observational study was undertaken to compare injury patterns in e-scooter, motorcycle and pedal-cycle collisions using two complementary datasets. Moderate-to-severe injuries were extracted from the NMTR between 1 January 2020 and 31 December 2022; no data were available beyond this period, so events from 2023 to 24 were not analysed. NMTR captures patients admitted for 72 h or more, requiring intensive care or dying in hospital, providing near-complete ascertainment of moderate-to-severe injuries across England and Wales over the studied period. The two sources were deliberately selected to capture complementary segments of the injury spectrum: the NMTR characterises the pattern and severity of hospitalised, moderate-to-severe trauma, whereas the operator dataset supplies exposure denominators (rides and distance travelled) together with the high-frequency, lower-severity events and near misses that rarely reach trauma services. Neither source alone captures both the severity profile and the underlying population exposure; analysed in parallel, they allow injury patterns to be interpreted against the volume of riding from which they arise. Because their ascertainment, severity coding and observation windows differ fundamentally, the datasets are reported side by side rather than pooled, and estimates from one are not transferred quantitatively to the other.

To assess lower-severity events, rental e-scooter incident reports were obtained from Voi Technology™ (Stockholm, Sweden) spanning July 2020 to December 2024 across ten trial areas in 18 cities and comprising self-reported collisions and near-misses submitted via the operator’s mobile application, email or telephone. An important and recognised limitation is that these data relate only to rental e-scooters, since the operator does not collect information on privately owned vehicles. As a result, patterns observed in this dataset may not reflect those of private e-scooter users, limiting the generalisability of the findings. Because injury coding and ascertainment differed between NMTR and Voi, datasets were analysed separately and no linkage was attempted. To avoid ambiguity, three terms are used consistently throughout: an ‘incident’ is any reported event, including near misses and property damage; a ‘collision’ is an incident involving physical impact with another road user, a vehicle, a fixed object or the ground; and an ‘injury event’ denotes an incident resulting in bodily harm. By this definition, all NMTR records are injury events requiring hospital care, whereas Voi incidents span the full range from near misses to fatal injuries. Reporting follows the STROBE guidelines.23.

Data collection and processing

National Major Trauma Registry

Wheelchairs and other micromobility devices were excluded, as well as records with implausible ages (i.e., < 3 years for e-scooter riders or pedal cyclists; <10 years for motorcyclists) or missing data (n = 53). Available variables included age, helmet use, Injury Severity Score (ISS), Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) codes and free-text injury descriptions; sex and socio-economic status were not collected. Although discrete age bands were provided, these were collapsed into paediatric (< 18) and adult (≥ 18) strata to ensure sufficient counts in the oldest age groups during regression analysis. Free-text descriptions and AIS codes were independently mapped by three investigators to the ICD-10 IMD matrix;22 disagreements were resolved by consensus. This process consolidated heterogeneous diagnostic data into a standardised taxonomy of injury location (i.e., Body Region Levels I and II) and injury type (i.e., Nature of Injury), enabling cross-modality comparisons while improving interpretability, reproducibility and applicability to injury surveillance, clinical planning, public health and policy. Because the ICD-10 Injury Mortality Diagnosis matrix assigns each diagnosis to a single, fixed body-region and nature-of-injury category, this mapping is deterministic and reproducible once a code is agreed, allowing every summary category to be re-derived independently. Injuries were coded by the registry using the Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS), specifically the AIS 2008 dictionary (the 2005 revision incorporating the 2008 update) with the registry’s standard, longstanding UK-specific modifications to a small number of severity scores, from which the Injury Severity Score was derived. This single dictionary version was applied consistently by trained NMTR coders across the 2020–2022 study period, so no cross-version remapping was required.20 AIS severity categories were defined as mild (AIS 1 e.g., superficial wounds/contusions), moderate (AIS 2 e.g., closed extremity fractures or major ligament tears), serious (AIS 3 e.g., open or comminuted facial fractures), severe or critical (AIS 4–5 e.g., traumatic brain injury (TBI) requiring craniectomy or multiple haemorrhages with coma >6 h), and unsurvivable (AIS 6 e.g., aortic rupture). Injury counts were aggregated by vehicle type, age group, body region, injury type and AIS category; individual identifiers were unavailable, precluding identification of recurrent events.

Voi Technology™ self-reported safety incident data

This study analysed Voi’s incident reports from July 2020 to December 2024 for ten trial areas. These reports are voluntarily submitted by riders via the Voi app or customer-service channels and may include near misses, property damage, collisions or falls. Because they are self-reported and cannot be cross-checked, each report was treated as a unique event. Monthly ride-volume tables provided the number of rides and distance travelled by city. Case-level variables recorded trial area, city, rider age, sex, date of incident and user-reported injury severity. Injury severity followed DfT guidance: Level 0 (near miss/property damage only), Level 1 (minor physical injury such as bruises, cuts or sprains), Level 2 (moderate injuries including fractures, deep lacerations or concussion), Level 3 (severe injuries requiring surgery or serious medical treatment) and Level 4 (critical or fatal injuries). Reports missing city, date or severity were excluded (n = 258). City-level deprivation was quantified using two measures from the 2019 English Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD), an area-level measure of socioeconomic disadvantage that ranks small neighbourhoods in England and Wales from most to least deprived: (1) population-weighted average rank and (2) the proportion of Lower-layer Super Output Areas (LSOAs; small neighbourhood census units of around 1,500 residents) in the most-deprived decile.24.

Outcomes and predictors

For the NMTR dataset, the primary outcomes were the relative frequencies of injuries by body region and injury type across vehicle types; secondary outcomes were injury severity (AIS categories). Predictors in multivariable models included vehicle type (e-scooter, motorcycle, pedal cycle), age group, body region and injury type. Relative risks (RRs) estimated differences in injury counts between e-scooter riders and comparator groups after controlling for total injury counts and age. For the Voi dataset, the primary outcomes were collision rates per 10,000 rides and injury severity level (Levels 0–4). Predictors included city-level deprivation measures, rider sex, age band (16–19, 20–34, 35–44, ≥ 45 years), season, and city.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in Stata BE 19. Counts and percentages were summarised for categorical variables, and medians with interquartile ranges for continuous data. For the registry dataset, unadjusted relative risks (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were first calculated from contingency tables to compare injury frequencies across vehicle types. The analysis then adopted a two-stage modelling approach: Poisson models were used to screen for significant differences in injury distribution by body region and injury type; only categories with p < 0.05 were taken forward. Because injury counts varied more than expected under a Poisson distribution, negative binomial regression was used to estimate adjusted RRs, stratifying analyses by age group (i.e., adult vs. paediatric cases). Predicted frequencies were reported as events per 100 incidents. Because the registry does not record distance or trip exposure for motorcyclists or pedal cyclists, these negative binomial models used each mode’s total injury count as the offset, so the resulting relative risks describe differences in injury composition (events per 100 injuries within a mode) rather than incidence per unit of travel. Genuine exposure-adjusted rates (per 10,000 rides and per 10,000 km) were available only for rental e-scooters from the operator data and were modelled separately. The two sources shared no common identifiers and were not linked; their only point of alignment was a shared severity vocabulary, with registry injuries graded by AIS-derived categories and operator reports by the corresponding DfT severity levels. To maintain clinical clarity, multivariable analyses focused on the more granular Level II body regions of the IMD matrix. Ordinal logistic regression was used to explore independent risk factors for higher severity injury (i.e., AIS ≥ 4), with motorcyclists, adults, and lower-extremity injuries as reference categories. The proportional odds assumption was checked and satisfied.

For the self-reported Voi data, incident (collision) rates per 10,000 rides were calculated and their association modelled with city-level deprivation using weighted linear regression, weighting each city by the total distance travelled. Rides were chosen as the primary exposure denominator because each trip constitutes a discrete unit of risk exposure, encompassing the mounting, dismounting and junction-negotiation events at which most incidents occur, and because ride counts were recorded more completely and with less inter-city measurement variability than trip distances; distance-based rates (per 10,000 km) are nonetheless reported alongside them in Table S5 to permit comparison and to account for between-city differences in average trip length. Robust standard errors were used to account for differing variability across cities, and analyses were repeated after excluding any city that disproportionately influenced results. Injury severity was modelled (Levels 0–4) using ordinal logistic regression with rider age, sex, season and city as predictors; the reference groups were riders aged 16–19, male riders, winter and Liverpool (the largest trial city). The correlation of city-level odds of severe injury with deprivation was assessed using Spearman’s correlation.

To minimise false-positive findings in the face of multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted: Holm-Bonferroni correction when fewer than 20 tests were conducted and Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery rate adjustment when 20 or more tests were involved. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Missing data were handled by analysing only complete cases; no imputation was necessary. Because individual identifiers were not available, each record was treated as a distinct event, and repeat events could not be identified. An interrupted time-series analysis comparing pre-trial and post-trial periods was considered but could not be implemented because pre-2020 exposure and collision data were unavailable and Voi data could not be linked to comparator areas.

Results

National major trauma registry

Sample characteristics

The NMTR contained 15,247 patients, including 580 e-scooter riders, 7,027 motorcyclists and 7,640 pedal cyclists (Table 1). E-scooter riders accounted for only about 4% of the registry population, yet a larger share of their injuries occurred in children: 16.2% of e-scooter injuries were in riders < 18 years compared with 6.2% of motorcyclist injuries and 8.9% of pedal-cycle injuries. Injury severity scores were broadly similar across groups (median ISS 12.5 for e-scooters, 13.0 for motorcycles and 11.0 for pedal cycles), and in-hospital mortality was low (≈ 2.3–2.6%). Helmet use differed markedly between the cohorts: only 5.9% of e-scooter riders were documented as wearing helmets, versus 75.7% of motorcyclists and 45.0% of pedal cyclists. E-scooter riders had slightly higher mean head AIS scores (3.7 vs. 3.6), but absolute differences were small.

Differences in injury profiles between adult & paediatric riders

Across 62,654 recorded injuries, adults made up most cases in all cohorts (87.9% e-scooter, 94.7% motorcyclist and 92.9% pedal-cycle injuries) (Table S1). In univariate comparisons, adult e-scooter riders were less likely than children to sustain lower-extremity injuries (RR 0.61, 95% CI 0.47–0.79) or internal organ injuries (RR 0.81, 95% CI 0.71–0.94). Adult motorcyclists and pedal-cycle riders similarly had lower risks of head and neck injury (RR 0.63 and 0.74, respectively), including TBI (RR 0.58 and 0.67) or other head trauma (RR 0.66 and 0.81). Adults also had lower risks of abdominal injury, vascular injury and critical injury (AIS 5), reflecting the greater vulnerability of paediatric riders. Injury distribution and severity data are summarised in Fig. 1.

Multivariable analysis of injury location - Level II body region

All cross-modal relative risks below describe the composition of injuries among injured patients (events per 100 injuries within each mode), not the absolute risk of injury per rider or trip. After adjusting for age, adult e-scooter riders had markedly higher risks of TBI than adult motorcyclists (RR 3.45, 95% CI 3.19–3.75) and pedal cyclists (RR 1.74, 95% CI 1.61–1.88), corresponding to ≈ 36 TBI events per 100 e-scooter incidents (Fig. 2). Other head injuries were also more frequent among e-scooter riders (RR 2.31 vs. motorcyclists and 1.39 vs. cyclists), affecting ≈ 23 adults per 100 incidents. E-scooter riders had 1.7-fold higher risk of hip injury compared with motorcyclists (RR 1.66, 1.54 vs. 0.93 injuries per 100 incidents) and 80% higher risk of other lower-extremity injuries than pedal-cyclists (RR 1.82; 12.26 vs. 6.75 injuries per 100). Conversely, vertebral injuries were about 70% less frequent among e-scooter riders (RR 0.32) and occurred in only ≈ 2.6 per 100 incidents. Thoracic, pelvic & lower-back, and upper-extremity injuries were also less common, with RRs around 0.4–0.5 relative to adult motorcyclists and cyclists. Similarly, among paediatric riders, e-scooter use conferred a significantly higher risk of TBI compared with motorcycles (RR 2.39) or bicycles (RR 1.38), representing ≈ 43 cases per 100 incidents (Fig. 2). Lower-extremity trauma was more common than in pedal-cycle collisions (RR 1.75; ≈20 per 100), whereas vertebral, thoracic, upper-extremity and pelvic & lower-back injuries were substantially less frequent (RR ≈ 0.12–0.45). Abdominal injuries were ~ 60% less frequent among paediatric e-scooter riders than among cyclists in the same age group. A detailed breakdown of age stratified comparisons is provided in Tables S2 & S3, with absolute rates in Table S4. Estimates within paediatric strata and for rare injury categories are based on small event counts and are accompanied by wide confidence intervals; these should be interpreted with caution.

Multivariable analysis of injury type - nature of injury

When injuries were grouped by type, adult e-scooter riders had a lower risk of fracture than motorcyclists (RR 0.80, 95% CI 0.74–0.85) and pedal cyclists (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.84–0.96), with ≈ 43 fractures per 100 incidents versus 54 per 100 in motorcyclists and 48 per 100 in cyclists (Table 2). In contrast, internal organ injury occurred more often among e-scooter riders (RR 1.46 vs. motorcyclists; RR 1.36 vs. cyclists), with ≈ 36 adult cases per 100 incidents, and vascular trauma risk was markedly increased (RR 3.24 vs. motorcyclists; RR 1.66 vs. cyclists), though absolute rates were low (≈ 7.6 per 100 incidents). Small increases were noted for dislocations and open wounds, but these remained infrequent (≤ 2.5 per 100). Paediatric e-scooter riders showed a comparable pattern: fracture risk was lower than among motorcyclists (RR 0.75), whereas internal organ injury (RR 1.43) and vascular trauma (RR 2.19) were higher. Absolute rates mirrored these associations: fractures occurred in ≈ 34 per 100 paediatric e-scooter incidents versus ≈ 46 per 100 motorcyclist incidents, internal organ injury in ≈ 45 vs. 31 per 100, and vascular trauma in ≈ 8.8 vs. 4.0 per 100. There were no clear differences in dislocations or open wounds.

Risk factors for higher-severity injury

Relative to adult motorcyclists, pedal cyclists had 38% higher odds of severe injury (OR 1.38, 1.24–1.53), and paediatric riders had nearly double the odds (1.87, 1.57–2.23) (Fig. 3). The interaction between paediatric age and e-scooter use was protective (0.71, 0.53–0.95), indicating lower odds of high-severity trauma than in adult motorcyclists. Among e-scooter riders, internal organ injury (2.86, 1.18–6.92), other head injury (2.28, 1.66–3.12) and upper-extremity trauma (1.78, 1.19–2.67) were associated with higher severity, whereas TBI, vertebral, thoracic and pelvic/lower-back injuries showed no significant differences. For pedal cyclists, abdominal injury was linked to higher severity (2.07, 1.52–2.82), while thoracic (0.67, 0.59–0.75), TBI (0.85, 0.74–0.97), upper-extremity (0.78, 0.67–0.90) and pelvic/lower-back trauma (0.73, 0.58–0.93) were associated with lower severity compared with lower-extremity trauma in adult motorcyclists. Among children, abdominal (0.44, 0.30–0.63), other head (0.51, 0.41–0.65), pelvic/lower-back (0.58, 0.33–1.05), TBI (0.64, 0.51–0.79) and upper-extremity injuries (0.47, 0.34–0.65) were all associated with lower odds of severe injury, indicating generally less critical patterns across multiple regions.

Voi Technology™ self-reported safety incident data

Sample characteristics and exposure-adjusted incident rates

The Voi dataset comprised 23,193 reported incidents across more than 33 million trips covering 77.8 million km (Table 3). Riders had a median age of 27 years (IQR 23–34); 67.5% were male, and only 0.77% reported wearing helmets. Male riders had a 36% lower risk of collision than female riders (15.5 vs. 24.4 incidents per 100 riders), and adults had a 78% lower risk than children (18.4 vs. 83.3 per 100). Collision rates per 10,000 rides varied from 21.05 (Peterborough) to 2.83 (Cheltenham), and from 10.89 to 1.53 per 10,000 km (Table S5). Distance-weighted regression showed that each 10-place increase in the IMDR rank (indicating greater affluence) was associated with a 0.14-point reduction in incidents per 10,000 rides (p = 0.031), while the proportion of LSOAs was not significant. After excluding Liverpool, a single outlier city, the associations strengthened: a 10-rank rise in IMDR reduced incidents by 0.22 per 10,000 rides (p = 0.001), and a 10-percentage-point increase in the proportion of deprived LSOAs increased incidents by 1.24 per 10,000 rides (p = 0.013), equivalent to 22 fewer and 124 additional incidents per million rides, respectively (Fig. 4).

Predictors of injury severity

Ordinal logistic regression adjusting for sex, age, season and city (Table S6) showed that riders aged 20–69 had lower odds of severe injury than those aged 16–19 (OR 0.42–0.60), while those aged 70–79 did not differ significantly (OR 1.45, 0.27–7.81). Female riders had more than twice the odds of severe injury compared with males (OR 2.13, 1.65–2.77), increasing to threefold among women aged 60–69 (OR 3.10, 1.17–8.25). Relative to Liverpool, higher severity injuries were observed in Gloucester (OR 5.86, 2.82–12.17), Cheltenham (OR 2.82, 1.27–6.24), London (OR 2.68, 1.62–4.42), Northampton (OR 2.02, 1.75–2.34), Portsmouth (OR 1.37, 1.12–1.67) & Birmingham (OR 1.36, 1.19–1.55), while lower odds were seen in Bath (OR 0.68, 0.53–0.88) and Bristol (OR 0.67, 0.60–0.74). Linear regression identified an inverse relationship between IMDR and injury severity (β = 0.0056, p = 0.012), suggesting that more affluent areas reported comparatively more severe injuries (Table S7). No significant seasonal variation in severity was detected.

Discussion

By mapping NMTR data to standardised outcomes and aligning these records with operator-reported exposure, this study moved beyond descriptive case series to capture the full spectrum of injury severity and enable international comparisons.22 This integrated approach revealed previously under-explored trends and socioeconomic predictors: among e-scooter users, internal organ and vascular injuries were more frequent than in comparator groups; incidents were prevalent in more deprived regions, whilst injury severity was modestly associated with greater affluence; and female sex was associated with higher injury severity. A high proportion of underage users was also observed; despite sustaining fewer fractures, they accounted for a substantial share of craniofacial injuries,16 a predominance of head trauma has also been reported in trauma series from Germany25 and France.26 These findings emphasise the need for policies that address the socio-demographic determinants of e-scooter safety to prevent avoidable harm.

The spatial and socioeconomic disparities observed in e-scooter injuries are likely to be multifactorial. Geographic analyses indicate that crash risk concentrates in neighbourhoods with large working-age populations, high proportions of car-free households, and dense connectivity or nightlife nodes.27 These environments typify inner-city districts where congestion slows traffic and blunts impact forces. By contrast, affluent suburbs with wider roads and higher vehicle speeds see fewer crashes, but when collisions do occur, they transmit greater energy and yield more severe trauma.28 Built-environment typology studies show that incidents cluster in deprived urban areas, where dense intersection networks, limited greenspace, schools, and high entertainment employment density create multiple points of exposure.29 Conversely, collisions in outer suburban wards, characterised by lower population diversity but wider carriageways and fewer dedicated cycling lanes, are less frequent yet more likely to result in severe trauma.29 These contrasts suggest urban deprivation magnifies collision risk through exposure, while affluence magnifies severity through speed and infrastructure. Differences in data capture may accentuate them: serious injuries in affluent areas are more likely to present to trauma centres, whereas trivial incidents in deprived districts typically go unreported. Fewer than 10% of serious e-scooter injuries recorded in trauma registries appear in police datasets,6 and demographic analyses indicate that women and residents of deprived communities are more likely to seek medical care and report incidents.30 Voluntary reporting is likely to over-represent memorable or consequential events relative to minor falls and near misses, so the reported severity distribution probably overstates the average severity of all rental-scooter incidents; conversely, the most serious events may be under-captured where hospitalised or unconscious riders, or those attended directly by emergency services, never file an operator report. Because the propensity to report may itself vary with rider age, sex and city, with injury severity, and with area-level deprivation, the observed gradients in both incident rate and severity could be partly accentuated or attenuated by differential reporting, and are best read as patterns among reported rental-scooter incidents rather than unbiased estimates of absolute risk. These deprivation associations are, moreover, ecological: they link the characteristics of places, summarised at city level, to aggregate rates and must not be interpreted as the socioeconomic status of individual riders. They therefore argue for targeting high-risk environments and infrastructure rather than assuming a particular socioeconomic profile among those injured.

Even so, differential reporting patterns may not wholly account for the sex differences observed in this cohort. Female riders had more than twice the odds of severe trauma and a higher overall risk of injury compared with male riders, consistent with a US survey that found lower crash incidence but a 1.5-fold higher injury rate among women.30 Although DfT trial data show that more than 70% of UK e-scooter users are male,31 and casualty counts are correspondingly higher among men,5 women appear disproportionately represented in higher severity trauma.30 Survey evidence indicates that women are more likely to report lower riding frequency and confidence, and to ride on footpaths and unpaved surfaces, behaviours that nearly double risk of severe injury.30 Most e-scooters are also designed around male anthropometry, with handlebar height and centre of gravity placement demanding greater upper body strength, compounding these experiential and behavioural disadvantages.30 Overall, these factors suggest that gender disparities in e-scooter trauma arise from an interplay of vehicle design, exposure patterns, and route choice, rather than reporting bias alone, and set the stage for closer examination of the underlying injury mechanisms.

The disproportionate burden of head injury, and of traumatic brain injury in particular, likely reflects a convergence of behavioural, design and environmental factors rather than any single cause. Foremost is the rarity of helmet use, documented in only 5.9% of e-scooter riders in the registry and 0.77% of Voi users, leaving the cranium largely unprotected during a fall. The vehicle itself compounds this exposure: a standing posture places the rider’s centre of mass high and far forward, so that abrupt deceleration tends to pitch the body head-first over the handlebar, while the small-diameter wheels, often without suspension, and limited tyre contact characteristic of e-scooters are readily arrested by kerbs, potholes, tram tracks and other surface irregularities and provide little grip on wet or uneven ground. Experimental models of e-scooter falls against a kerb confirm that these dynamics generate head impacts capable of producing clinically important brain injury even at modest speeds.32 The predominantly urban riding context, in which such hazards are dense and collisions with vehicles, pedestrians and street furniture are common, further raises the probability of head-first impact.29 Together these factors offer a coherent, if non-causal, explanation for the elevated relative risk of traumatic brain injury observed across age groups, and reinforce the case for helmet promotion alongside design standards addressing wheel size, suspension and braking. These associations, including that with low helmet use, may also reflect unmeasured rider behaviours and risk-taking tendencies that the registry and operator data could not capture.

Visceral and vascular injuries were prominent, echoing a French series in which 13.4% of patients suffered abdominal trauma and 6.6% required endovascular repair.26 Biomechanical studies attribute this pattern to the high forward centre of gravity and small wheels without suspension that project riders over the handlebar during abrupt deceleration, a recognised cause of abdominal and vascular trauma.32 Finite-element modelling shows that rigid grip ends markedly increase abdominal wall compression, whereas rounded, energy-absorbing grips reduce it by 19%.33 Children are particularly vulnerable because their thinner abdominal walls and proportionally larger organs align with handlebar height; paediatric series report that abdominal contusions and lacerations almost invariably result from direct handlebar impact.34 These observations support design standards mandating deformable grips and enhanced braking to reduce energy transfer on impact.

A substantial portion of the NMTR data spans successive Covid-19 lockdowns, which may limit generalisability. Travel restrictions reduced motor traffic volumes by about one-fifth in 2020, likely lowering high-energy collisions while increasing the relative incidence of low-speed or single-vehicle accidents.35 Conversely, pedal-cycle traffic reached its highest levels since the 1960s, increasing exposure. Alcohol consumption shifted from bars and nightlife to home, reducing absolute drink-drive collisions, yet drink-drive casualties accounted for a slightly larger share of serious crashes.36 These pandemic-era disruptions suggest caution when extrapolating these findings to current conditions. Moreover, the Voi data reflect self-reported incidents involving rental e-scooters only, limiting generalisability. An estimated million privately owned e-scooters operate illegally in the UK, dwarfing the regulated rental fleet.6 Private devices are unregulated and may differ in handling and safety; a 2023 field study showed that these lighter, more portable scooters have substantially poorer braking than heavier rental models, increasing collision risk during critical events.37 Consequently, exposure estimates and incident patterns in the rental dataset probably underestimate the true incidence and severity of e-scooter injuries in the UK.

Public health and policy interventions should reflect these patterns. The high burden of head trauma alongside low helmet use reiterates the need for mandatory helmet legislation, supported by integrated or rental helmet systems and targeted enforcement for younger riders.38 The prevalence of internal organ and vascular injuries highlights high-energy collisions, supporting measures such as stricter speed limits, geofencing and temporal restrictions.39 Infrastructure improvements, including protected lanes, separation from motor traffic, better lighting and surveillance, are central to prevention and address safety concerns especially among women and disabled users.40 Active Travel England’s £200 million programme requires local authorities to demonstrate how investment improves safety for these groups, setting a precedent for equitable funding across micromobility projects.40 National trial extensions show the DfT emphasis on evidence before legalisation, with rental pilots authorised until May 2028.41 Improved coding of e-scooter incidents in police and hospital records, linked with operator data, is crucial for surveillance and evaluation.41 Local initiatives, such as London’s trial with lower speed limits, designated parking bays and geofenced zones, illustrate that regulatory design can reduce harm and improve compliance.42 Vehicle-side interventions such as in-app safety alerts, journey tracking and emergency access, should complement structural measures. Collectively, these regulatory and infrastructural priorities provide a framework for the safe and equitable integration of e-scooters into the UK transport system.

A key strength of this study is the complementary use of two datasets capturing different parts of the e-scooter safety spectrum. The NMTR provides near-complete ascertainment of moderate-to-severe trauma in England and Wales, enabling detailed cross-modal comparison of injury pattern and severity, while the Voi dataset contributes exposure denominators and a broader range of reported events within regulated rental schemes, including lower-severity incidents and near misses that trauma registries do not capture. Together, these sources provide a more informative picture of injury risk, severity and socioeconomic patterning than either could alone. Because the two datasets differ fundamentally in ascertainment, severity coding and time frame, they are complementary rather than directly comparable: the registry characterises the composition and severity of hospitalised trauma, whereas the operator data describe the frequency and distribution of reported events relative to exposure. Findings from one therefore contextualise, rather than validate or quantitatively extrapolate to, the other, and the two should not be read as a single combined estimate of injury burden. However, several limitations should temper interpretation. First, this was an observational, cross-sectional analysis and cannot establish causal relationships. Important determinants of injury risk and severity, including intoxication, speed, road conditions, route environment, crash mechanism and most protective behaviours, were unavailable or inconsistently recorded, leaving potential for residual confounding. Second, each dataset captures only part of the injury spectrum: NMTR is restricted to moderate-to-severe trauma, whereas Voi data are self-reported, unverified and derived from a single rental operator. Minor events are therefore likely to be under-reported, reporting may vary by rider, city and severity, and the Voi data should be interpreted as reported incident patterns within regulated rental schemes rather than the full population burden of e-scooter injury. Relatedly, the registry-based relative risks describe the composition of injuries among hospitalised trauma patients rather than the exposure-adjusted incidence of injury, and cross-modal comparisons should be read in that light. Exposure estimates were also limited to rental e-scooters and may not generalise to privately owned devices, which may differ in design, maintenance, modification status and rider profile. Third, anonymisation prevented person-level linkage within or between datasets, so repeat events, longer-term outcomes and direct matching of operator report to trauma cases could not be assessed. Fourth, deprivation analyses were conducted at city level; these ecological associations describe places rather than people, may not reflect individual-level socioeconomic risk, and must not be read as the deprivation status of the individual riders involved, so policy inferences should target high-risk environments rather than individuals. Finally, the NMTR study period overlapped with the Covid-19 pandemic, during which travel patterns, road use and injury mechanisms may have differed from post-pandemic conditions. Mapping free-text injuries to standardised anatomical categories may also have introduced non-differential misclassification, reducing precision for specific estimates, though unlikely to have altered the main patterns observed. Lastly, a number of estimates, particularly those within paediatric strata and for rare injury categories, rest on small event counts and carry correspondingly wide confidence intervals; these warrant cautious, hypothesis-generating interpretation. The data also lacked the granularity for two analyses suggested during review: operator records carried the calendar date but not the time of an incident, precluding analysis of time-of-day, weekday–weekend or night-time patterns, and the registry extract was structured as adult versus paediatric strata, with too few older riders (for example, 45 rental users aged ≥ 65) to support reliable stratification of older adults; both warrant dedicated study in future linked datasets.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of e-scooter injuries in the UK, pairing registry-based injury patterns across transport modes with exposure-adjusted incident rates for rental e-scooters, and is one of the few internationally to integrate trauma-registry and operator data. Because these datasets capture different injury severities and populations, conclusions about injury composition are drawn from the registry and those about exposure-adjusted incidence and deprivation from the operator data, rather than across the two. The findings highlight distinct patterns of severe injury: compared with motorcyclists, e-scooter riders had more than three times the risk of traumatic brain injury (RR 3.45) and vascular trauma (RR 3.24) and around 1.5 times the risk of internal organ injury (RR 1.46), alongside a disproportionate paediatric burden and a socioeconomic gradient in collision rates, showing that risk is not evenly distributed but shaped by age, sex and local context. Future work should test the effectiveness of safety interventions through quasi-experimental designs, investigate the behavioural and infrastructural factors that may underlie the observed differences in injury severity by sex and age group, such as riding exposure, route choice, vehicle ergonomics and access to protected infrastructure, and evaluate how evolving regulation affects injury trends; further work should also link exposure data to clinical outcomes and quantify the exposure of privately owned e-scooter users, which remains largely unmeasured. As e-scooter adoption expands across Europe, embedding rigorous public health evidence into forthcoming UK legislation will be critical to ensure that the benefits of micromobility are realised while minimising avoidable harm.
