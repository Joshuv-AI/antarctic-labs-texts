Abstract
We study separation axioms for X X -top-lattices (i.e. lattices L L for which a given subset X ⊆ L \ { 1 } X\subseteq L\backslash\{1\} admits a Zariski-like topology ). Such spaces are T 0 T_{0} and usually far away from being T 2 . T_{2}. We give graphical characterizations for an X X -top-lattice to be T 1 , T_{1}, T 1 4 , T_{\frac{1}{4}}, T 1 2 , T_{\frac{1}{2}}, T 3 4 T_{\frac{3}{4}} and provide several families of examples/counterexamples that illustrate our results. We apply our results mainly to the prime (resp. maximal, minimal) spectra of prime (resp. maximal, minimal) ideals of commutative (semi)rings.



Introduction

The prime spectrum

[

Several authors attempted to generalize the standard Zariski topology to the
spectra of special classes of submodules of a module over a (commutative) ring. That enriched the Theory of Modules with many new classes of (
co
)
prime
submodules (e.g.
[
42
]
,
[
27
]
). The main
problem was that the closed varieties in such spectra are
not
necessarily closed under finite unions
. Several authors tried to find
conditions under which such spectra have a Zariski-like topology. The first
attempts were by McCasland Smith (
[
34
]
,
[
35
]
), who
introduced the notion of
top modules
(see also Lu
[
32
]
).
Moreover, several authors, including Abuhlail (e.g.
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
), studied
Zariski-like topologies
for
different classes of
(co)prime submodules
of a module over a ring
(e.g.
[
15
]
,
[
41
]
). These investigations were extended by
several authors to special classes of
(co)prime subsemimodules
of a
given semimodule over a semiring (e.g.
[
12
]
,
[
28
]
).

This led Abuhlail and Lomp
[
7
]
(see also
[
6
]
) to
consider the more general framework of what they called an
X
-top
lattice
(a

for which an appropriate subset

attains a Zariski-like topology). Applications
are, in particular, to the lattice
Sub(_{R}M)
of sub(semi)modules of a
given left (semi)module

. For the special case

Abuhlail and Hroub
[

[
27

spectral
(in the sense of Hochster

26

Our goal in this paper is to initiate a

We recall the relevant definitions and results from Lattice Theory. We
follow

1.1
.

A

(L,\leq)
such that
the

of any two elements exist. For a

(L,\leq),
we set

a\vee b:=\sup\{a,b\}\text{ and }a\wedge b:=\inf\{a,b\}\text{ }\forall\ a,b\in L.

We call
(L,\wedge)
a
meet-semilattice
of
L
and
(L,\vee)
a

join-semilattice
of
L.
For
x,y\in X,
we set
{y||x}
iff
x

and
y
are
not comparable
(i.e.
x\nleqq y
and
y\nleqq x
).

Definitions 1.2
.

A lattice
\mathcal{L}=(L,\wedge,\vee)
is said to be a

(1)

bounded lattice
iff
L
has a
largest element

1
and
a
smallest element

0
.

(2)

complete lattice
iff arbitrary meets and joins of elements of

L
exist in
L
(in this case,
1=\bigvee\limits_{x\in L}x
and
0=\bigwedge\limits_{x\in L}x,
i.e. every complete lattice is bounded).

(3)

distributive lattice
iff
\forall\ x,y,z\in L
:

x\wedge(y\vee z)=(x\wedge y)\vee(x\wedge z)\text{ (equivalently }x\vee(y\wedge z)=(x\vee y)\wedge(x\vee z)\text{).}

1.3
.

Let
(L,\wedge)
be a
complete
meet-semilattice and
B,C\subseteq L.

We call
q\in C:

(completely)
B
-
irreducible in
L
iff for any
A\underset{\text{finite}}{\subseteq}B
(

) with
q\leq a
for all

we have:
\bigwedge\limits_{a\in A}a=q\Longrightarrow a=q
for some

(completely) strongly

iff for any
A\underset{\text{finite}}{\subseteq}B
(
A\subseteq B

\bigwedge\limits_{a\in A}a\leq q\Longrightarrow a\leq q
for some
a\in A.

With

(resp.

), we denote
the set of
B
-irreducible (resp. completely
B
-irreducible, strongly
B
-irreducible, completely strongly
B
-irreducible) elements of

We drop
the superscript

if it’s clear from the context.

1.4
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=(L,\wedge,\vee,1,0)

complete

We call

With

-minimal (barely

1.5

Let

Let

We recall the some definitions and notation from the
Theory
X
-Top Lattices.
We follow
[

(unless otherwise stated explicitly).

Notation
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=(L,\wedge,\vee,1,0)
be a complete lattice and
X\subseteq L\backslash\{1\}
. For any
a\in L
, we define

V_{X}(a)

:=

\{x\in X\ |\ a\leq x\};

D_{X}(a)

:=

We call
V_{X}(a)
the
variety
of

in
X.
Moreover, we set

V_{X}(L):=\{V_{X}(a)\ |\ a\in L\}\text{ and }\tau_{X}(L)=\{D_{X}(a)\mid a\in L\}.

We drop the subscript
X
if it’s clear from the context.

1.7
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=(L,\wedge,\vee,1,0)
be a
complete
lattice
and
X\subseteq L\backslash\{1\}.
Notice that
V_{X}(0)=X,

V_{X}(1)=\emptyset
and
V_{X}(L)
is closed under arbitrary intersections
as
\bigcap\limits_{a\in A}(V_{X}(a))=V_{X}(\bigvee\limits_{a\in A}a)
for
any
A\subseteq L.
We say that
\mathcal{L}
is an
X
-top lattice

[
7
]
iff
V_{X}(L)
is closed under finite unions (equivalently,
\tau_{X}(L)
is closed under finite intersections). In this case,
(X,\tau_{X}(L))
is a topological space, which we call a
Zariski-like
topology
).

Consider
X\subseteq L\backslash\{1\}
. For any
Y\subseteq X
and
a\in L,

we set

I_{X}(Y):=\bigwedge_{y\in Y}y\text{ and }\sqrt[X]{a}:=I_{X}(V_{X}(a)).

It can be shown that
\overline{Y}=V_{X}(I_{X}(Y))
(e.g.
[
5
, Lemma 1.8]
. We say that
a\in L
is
X
-
radical
iff
\sqrt[X]{a}=a

and denote the
set of
X
-radical elements
of
L
by
C^{X}(L)
, i.e.

C^{X}(L):=\{a\in L\text{ }|\text{ }a=\bigwedge_{y\in V_{X}(a)}y\}.

Clearly,
X\subseteq C^{X}(L)
and
(C^{X}(L),\wedge)
is a
meet-semilattice. We drop the superscript
X
if it’s clear from the context.

We recall a fundamental characterization of
X
-top lattices by Abuhlail and
Lomp
[
7
]
.

Theorem 1.8
.

(
[
7
,
Theorem 2.2
]
)
Let
\mathcal{L}=(L,\wedge,\vee,1,0)
be a complete lattice and
X\subseteq L\backslash\{1\}
. Then
\mathcal{L}
is an
X
-top lattice if and only if every
x\in X
is strongly
C^{X}(L)
-irreducible in
(C^{X}(L),\wedge)
(i.e.
X=SI^{C^{X}(L)}(X)
).

The following is a direct, but very useful, consequence of Theorem
1.8

especially in constructing examples and counterexamples.

Corollary 1.9
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=(L,\wedge,\vee,1,0)
be an
X
-top lattice
for some
X\subseteq L\backslash\{1\}
. If
Y\subseteq X,
then
L
is a
Y
-top lattice and the corresponding topology on
Y
is the induced subspace
topology.

Proof
.

We show first that
C^{Y}(L)\subseteq C^{X}(L)
. Let
a\in C^{Y}(L).
Notice
that
V_{Y}(a)\subseteq V_{X}(a)
as
Y\subseteq X,
hence,
\bigwedge V_{X}(a)\leq\bigwedge V_{Y}(a)=a
. Since,
a\leq\bigwedge V_{X}(a)
, we
conclude that
a=\bigwedge V_{X}(a)
. By the direct implication of Theorem
1.8
,
Y\subseteq SI^{C^{X}(L)}(X).
Since
C^{Y}(L)\subseteq C^{X}(L),

and both share the
same meet
, we conclude that
Y\subseteq SI^{C^{Y}(L)}(Y).
By the reverse implication of Theorem
1.8
,
L
is a

Y
-top lattice. Notice that
V_{Y}(a)=V_{X}(a)\cap Y
for any
a\in L
,
i.e. the corresponding topology on
Y
is the induced subspace topology on
Y\hookrightarrow X.\blacksquare

Let

be an
X

some
X\subseteq L\backslash\{1\}.
By Proposition
2.9
,
X
is
T_{0}
; however,
X
is
T_{1}
if and only if
K.\dim(X)=0.
This suggests
studying the separation axioms between
T_{0}
and
T_{1}.
While there are
many such separation axioms (cf.
[
13
]
,
[
14
]
), we limit
our attention in this paper to the so-called
quarter separation
axioms
:
T_{\frac{1}{4}},

T_{\frac{1}{2}}
and
T_{\frac{3}{4}}
as
defined in
[
37
]
.

For the convenience of the reader, we start by recalling and fixing the
needed definitions.

Definition 3.1
.

(e.g.
[
37
]
,
[
19
]
) We say that a topological space
X
is

(1)

T_{\frac{1}{4}}
iff any
x\in X
is closed
or
kerneled (i.e.
iff
X=Cl(X)\cup K(X)
).

(2)

T_{\frac{1}{2}}
iff any
x\in X
is closed
or
isolated (i.e.
iff
X=Cl(X)\cup Iso(X)
).

(3)

T_{\frac{3}{4}}
iff any
x\in X
is closed
or
regular open
(i.e. iff
X=Cl(X)\cup RO(X)
).

Remark 3.2
.

(e.g.
[
37
]
) Notice that for any topological space
X,
we have
RO(X)\subseteq Iso(X)\subseteq K(X),
whence

T_{1}\ \implies T_{\frac{3}{4}}\ \implies T_{\frac{1}{2}}\ \implies T_{\frac{1}{4}}\ \implies T_{0}.

Definition 3.3
.

(cf.
[
13
]
) Let
X
be a topological space. For
A,B\subseteq X,

we set
A\vdash B
iff there exists
U\underset{\text{open}}{\subseteq}X

such that
A\subseteq U
and
B\cap U=\emptyset.
We say that
X
is
T_{F}

iff for every
x\in X
and
F\underset{\text{finite}}{\subseteq}X\backslash\{x\},
either
\{x\}\vdash F
or
F\vdash\{x\}.

We begin by an easy, but very useful, diagrammatic characterization of
X
-top lattices for which
X
is
T_{\frac{1}{4}}.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
L
be an
X
-top lattice for some
X\subseteq L\backslash\{1\}.
The following are equivalent:

(1)

X
is a
T_{\frac{1}{4}};

(2)

K.\dim(X)\leq 1;

(3)

X
is
T_{F}.

Proof
.

(1\Longleftrightarrow 2)
We have

X\text{ is }T_{\frac{1}{4}}\Longleftrightarrow X\overset{\text{Definition}}{=}Cl(X)\cup K(X)\overset{\text{Lemma \ref{kerneled}}}{=}Max(X)\cup Min(X)\Longleftrightarrow K.\dim(X)\leq 1.

(2\Longrightarrow 3)
Assume that
K.\dim(X)\leq 1.
Let
x\in X
and
F\underset{\text{finite}}{\subseteq}X\backslash\{x\}.
If
F=\emptyset,

then
\{x\}\vdash F
as
x\in X
and
F\cap X=\emptyset.
Assume that
F\neq\emptyset
and
F:=\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\}\subseteq X\backslash\{x\}.

Case I:

x\in Min(X).
In this case,
x\in D(\bigwedge\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i})
(since
X
is strongly
X
-irreducible
in
C^{X}(L)
by Theorem
1.8
and
X\subseteq C^{X}(L)
). It’s obvious
that
D(\bigwedge\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i})\cap F=\emptyset.
So,
\{x\}\vdash F.

Case II:
x\in Max(X).
In this case,
V(x)=\{x\}\
whence
F\subseteq X\backslash\{x\}=D(x)
and
\{x\}\cap D(x)=\emptyset,

Proposition 3.18
.

Let
L
be an
X
-top lattice for some
X\subseteq L\backslash\{1\}.
Then

RO(X)=\{x\in X\mid\overline{D(x)}=X\backslash\{x\}\}=Iso(X)\cap\mathcal{E}(X).

Proof
.

Step I:
\ RO(X)\subseteq\{x\in X\mid\overline{D(x)}=X\backslash\{x\}\}.

Let
x\in RO(X),
i.e.
\{x\}=int(V(x)).
Since
\{x\}\underset{\text{open}}{\subseteq}X
and
D(x)\subseteq X\backslash\{x\},
it follows that
\overline{D(x)}\subseteq X\backslash\{x\}.
On the other hand, let
y\in X\backslash\{x\}.
If
U_{y}\in\mathcal{O}(y)
and
U_{y}\subseteq V(x),

then
y\in int(V(x))=\{x\},
a contradiction. It follows that
U_{y}\cap D(x)\neq\emptyset
for every
U_{y}\in\mathcal{O}(y),
whence
y\in\overline{D(x)}.
So,
X\backslash\{x\}\subseteq\overline{D(x)}.

Consequently,
X\backslash\{x\}=\overline{D(x)}.

Step II:
\ \{x\in X\mid\overline{D(x)}=X\backslash\{x\}\}\subseteq Iso(X)\cap\mathcal{E}(X).

Let
x\in X
be such that
\overline{D(x)}=X\backslash\{x\}.
Notice that
\{x\}=X\backslash\overline{D(x)}\underset{\text{open}}{\subseteq}X,
i.e.
x\in Iso(X).
Since
D(x)\subseteq X\backslash\{x\},
we have
E(x):=\bigwedge(X\backslash\{x\})\leq\bigwedge D(x).
On the other hand,
if
a_{x}:=\bigwedge D(x)\nleqq E(x),
then there exists
q\in X\backslash\{x\}\
such that
a_{x}\nleqq q
, i.e.
q\in D(a_{x})
and
D(a_{x})\in\mathcal{O}(q).
By assumption,
q\in X\backslash\{x\}=\overline{D(x)},

whence
D(a_{x})\cap D(x)\neq\emptyset,
a contradiction.

Step III:

Iso(X)\cap\mathcal{E}(X)\subseteq RO(X).

Let
x\in Iso(X)\cap\mathcal{E}(X).
By assumption,
\{x\}\underset{\text{open}}{\subseteq}X,
whence
x\in int(\overline{\{x\}}).
On the other
hand, let
q\in int(\overline{\{x\}})=int(V(x)).
Then
q\in D(a)\subseteq V(x)
for some
a\in L.
If
q\neq x,
then
q\in X\backslash\{x\}
and so
q\in V(E(x))=V(\bigwedge D(x))=\overline{D(x)}.
Since
D(a)\in\mathcal{O}(q),
there exists
h\in D(a)\cap D(x)\subseteq V(x)\cap D(x)=\emptyset,
a
contradiction. So,
\{x\}=int(\overline{\{x\}}),
i.e.
x\in RO(X).\blacksquare
