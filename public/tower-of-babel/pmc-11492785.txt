Predicting the effect of nosocomial infection prevention on neonatal mortality and hospital stay in Ethiopia: a prospective longitudinal study
Authors: Etagegn Shacho, Argaw Ambelu, Ayele Taye Goshu, Daniel Yilma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeonatal Nosocomial infections (NNIs) are a significant cause of morbidity and mortality for neonates in an intensive care unit. Neonatal causes of death in healthcare facilities are attributed to different factors. We aimed to investigate factors associated with NNIs, estimate the burden of NNIs, and assess how the prediction effects help to save medical mortality and length of hospital stay.MethodA prospective longitudinal study was conducted and data were collected from January 2022 to June 2022 from Jimma University Medical Center (JUMC). The data were gathered in a variety of ways, including an in-person interview with the patient’s caregiver, direct observations of neonatal patients, and a review of the study participants’ charts. This study includes patients aged 3 to 28 days who were admitted to the JUMC neonatal ward and stayed for at least 48 h. Multi-state model formulation and multivariate logistic regression were used for data analysis.ResultsA total of 545 neonates were included out of 688, and 30% (n = 164) of them acquired nosocomial infections (NIs); 98 (33%) of infected patients were born prematurely; and 71 (31.4%) were underweight at birth. NIs were higher in neonates with long hospital stay (AOR: 1.16, 95%CI: 1.13–1.20), use of urinary catheters (AOR: 3.09, 95%CI: 1.55–6.15), and undergoing surgical procedures (AOR: 2.42, 95%CI: 1.13–5.17). Patients who developed NIs had a higher risk of death (HR: 2, 95% CI: 1.31, 3.04). The burden of neonatal NIs was determined to have a risk of 0.3, a mortality rate of 9.6%, and an average duration of hospital stay of 14.6 days. Competing risk regression suggests that neonates with NIs have a significantly higher risk of death than those who are not infected (HR: 16.42, 95% CI: 8.70-30.98, p < 0.001). Assumed prevention that decreases the NIs rate in half would result in 101 lives and 1357 patient days saved from 10,000 neonatal inpatients.ConclusionUrinary catheterization and surgical procedure increased neonatal NIs. Longer hospital stay can increase the risk of NIs and can also result from the NIs. Our finding indicated that effective prevention of NIs could help reduce neonatal deaths and their hospital stays.

Introduction

The burden of child mortality worldwide is more than one-third accounted for by newborn deaths [1]. In many of the world’s low income countries, newborn mortality rates can range from 40 to 50 per 1000 live births [2]. In Ethiopia, neonatal mortality accounted for 42% of child death [3] and nearly 120,000 babies died within their first four weeks of life [4]. The Ethiopia Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS) 2016 reported a neonatal mortality rate of 29/1000 live births [5].

Neonatal mortality in developing nations is primarily caused by infections [6] and NNIs is one of the significant contributor for neonatal morbidity and mortality [7]. NNIs are hospital-acquired infections that are acquired postnatal from an environmental source [8]. NNIs occur after the third day or after the first 72 h of life [9]. NIs can be attributed to failures in knowledge and training regarding basic infection control processes, coupled with inadequate infrastructure, systems of care, and resources [10–12]. These gaps lead to severe consequences when devices such as intravenous catheters and ventilators are introduced without sufficient attention to mitigate the substantial risk of infection they entail [13–15].

NIs are often associated with various factors, such as overcrowded healthcare settings, inadequate hygiene practices, and the presence of antibiotic-resistant pathogens. Effective infection prevention measures play a crucial role in combating nosocomial infections, including proper hand hygiene, sterilization of medical equipment, waste disposal, and surveillance systems [16, 17].

NIs result in use of more hospital resources, delay treatment for critically ill patients, contribute to capacity shortages, increase mortality, and result in significant increases in health-care expenses due to longer hospital stays [18]. This is complicated since NIs increases the duration of hospital stay but at the same time, the risk of NIs increases with the duration of stay [19].

A recent report revealed that Healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) were associated with prolonged hospital stay by six or more days [20]. The reduction of inappropriate stays in hospitals could increase hospital productivity and reduce waiting lists, without endangering the quality of services [21]. However, this investigation is complicated by the reality that patients acquired NI during their stay in the hospital. When the patients’ stay longer in the health center, they have the probability of acquiring NI. This situation requires careful consideration in the study by treating NIs as an exposure that varies with time [22]. This bias happens when the hazards subordinate to time are not accurately addressed within the statistical analysis [23]. These issues require the selection of appropriate models, as well as a particular arrangement and processing of the data for analysis. We have introduced multi-state transition models to address the issues. A multi-state transition model offers comprehensive approaches to complex disease progression pathways, where patients may experience various intermediate endpoints. It is also beneficial to identify the specific advantage that a particular preventative measure might offer [24, 25]. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, no study has been conducted that looked at the effects of neonatal NIs with a particular transition to multi-state models with time to sequential adverse occurrences. Therefore, the objective of this study were to (i) identify determinants associated with neonatal NIs, (ii) estimate load of neonatal NIs, and (iii) predict the preventive effect on reducing hospital mortality and length of hospital stay.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a prospective longitudinal study which was conducted from January to June 2022. Patients who were admitted to the JUMC neonate ward and stayed for a minimum of 48 h, aged between 3 and 28 days, are included in the study. We rejected any neonate who was discharged within 48 h of admission, was less than three days old, had died, or had transferred to another hospital. According to the criteria, 143 neonatal patients were excluded from the study. Of the 143 neonates excluded, 107 were discharged within 48 h of admission, 28 were less than three days old and died, and 8 were less than three days old and moved to another hospital.

The neonatal ward is one of the hospital’s current intensive care unit services. The unit contains 20 neonatal beds and 14 kangaroo mother-care (KMC) beds. The unit also includes two incubators, ten radiant warmers, four continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) machines, phototherapy equipment, and an oxygen concentrator. In addition, there is pulse oximetry, a glucometer, and newborn resuscitation equipment [26]. Healthcare professionals collected the data in various forms. An in-person interview was scheduled for the patient’s caregiver, who answered questions about the mother’s residence, antenatal care visit, and admission status. The data collector made direct observations on neonatal patients, particularly for the presence of nosocomial infections. Nosocomial infections are identified by symptoms such as discomfort, fever, hypothermia, redness, warmth, breathing problems, coughing, and yellow or bloody mucus. The review was done on study participants’ charts for variables such as age at admission, neonate gender, gestational age, weight at birth, mode of delivery, diagnosis at admission, CPAP use, urinary catheter use, central line use, surgical procedure completed, discharge or death status, and date.

Variables and measurements

The response variables in this study were time to events such as discharge from the hospital, developing nosocomial infection and death. The explanatory qualitative variables include mode of delivery, sex, mother’s residence, admission status, CPAP use, urinary catheter use, surgical procedure performed and quantitative variables include age, length of stay in hospital, gestation age, and weight at birth.

Multivariate logistic regression and goodness of fit of the model

Multivariate logistic regressions were used in to identify the risk factors associated with neonatal nosocomial infections. After fitting the logistic model to categorical data, essential to assess the model’s appropriateness, adequacy, and applicability evaluate the model’s appropriateness, sufficiency, and relevance is essential. The study used the Hosmer and Lemeshow for goodness of fit tests and Pearson’s chi-square test (X2).

Multi-state model formulation

Multi-state models allow for data longitudinal analysis when an individual may experience several health events. According to [27, 28] a multistate model is deemed Markov if it assumes that the probability of changing to a new state depends only on the model’s current value. The linkages between incidence, prevalence, and mortality can be used to estimate the population disease burden in the multistate extended illness-death model.

An illustration of the flow of the multi-state extended disease death model is provided in Fig. 1. Patients begin in state 0, which is hospital admission, under this extended illness-death model. Not a single patient is infected at this moment. Until they leave the hospital without a NIs (state 2 if the patient is discharged alive, state 3 if the patient passes away), or until they get a NIs, the patients stay in this condition. State 1 if the patient is infected. Until they are released from the hospital alive (state 4) or pass away from a healthy illness while still in state 1, patients in state 1 are kept under observation in state 5. Five transitions were examined in this study: Entry (0) → Discharge (2), Entry (0) →NIs (1), Entry (0) → Death (3), NIs (1) → Discharge (4), and NIs (1) → Death (5).

Competing risk regression and cumulative incidence

In order to account for competing risks, competing risk regression (CRR) examines how predictors affect the cumulative incidence function. Competing risk regression evaluates how factors affect the likelihood of a specific cause of failure in a group of independent subjects, where each subject may fail for various reasons [29].

The risk of becoming ill within a specific population over a particular time period is expressed by cumulative incidence. This enables one to contrast the neonatal mortality risk before and after the introduction of a hypothetical intervention. The smaller the value of θ, the more effective the prevention of NIs. Results

Patient characteristics

Out of 688 neonate patients, 545 were included in the study during the period from January to June 2022. The median age at the time of admission was 5 days with a range of 25.83 days, which indicates that neonates were admitted at various ages. The length of stay has an interquartile range of 11 days (19 − 8 = 11) and a median value of 11 days. Based on this outcome, half of the patients stay in the hospital between 8 and 19 days. As a result, the variation recommended the need for individual care techniques. Of 545 neonates, 164 (30%) acquired nosocomial infection. 98 (33%) of infected patients had a premature birth, and 71 (31.4%) were underweight at birth. In 120 patients who used urinary catheters, 65.8% acquired nosocomial infections. The total number of infected patients not on CPAP was 52.4%. After the surgical procedure was completed, 68% of the neonates were infected. Thus, 32.8% of patients’ mode of delivery was normal and infected. Based on admission status, 31.4% of patients were referred for admission and infected (Table 2).

Cumulative incidence and competing risk regression

The cumulative incidence curve in Fig. 2a, which shows significance (P < 0.001), indicates that the overall likelihood of dying with NIs after six days has increased compared to not having NIs. Similarly, as shown in Table 4, there was a 16-fold increase in the risk of death in patients with NIs compared to those without NIs (HR: 16.42, 95% CI = 8.70–30.98). Additionally, the reduction in the risk of mortality for every week increases with gestation age (HR: 0.83, 0.76–0.90). According to Fig. 2b, the cumulative incidence of discharge from the hospital for patients without NIs was higher than for patients with NIs (P < 0.001).

Conversely, for every day that a neonate’s age increases at admission, the rate of discharge rises by 4.4%. When the LOHS increased, the rate of discharge from hospital can be decreased by 21.3%. Those without NIs had a greater discharge rate than those with NIs (Fig. 2; Table 4).

Predicting the effectiveness of NIs prevention

We analyzed data sets that contain the LOHS, infection, and outcome information required to conduct the analysis. 545 out of the 688 neonatal patients, whose medical records were collected for the data set fulfilled the requirements. Out of these, 164 acquired a NIs, with 138 being discharged and 26 died. Out of the non-infected neonates, 354 were discharged from hospital alive and 27 died. The average incidence of mortality (9.6%) and LOHS (14.6 days) in the absence of preventive measures indicate the presence of a burden on patients related to NIs (Table 5).

The transition rates were estimated from the data set, and the results of a hypothetical prevention effect are shown in below. The analysis shows that the death hazard ratio for patients who have acquired a nosocomial infection is higher and significant (HR: 2, 95% CI: 1.31, 3.04). The decrease in average LOHS after prevention was 14.48 days, and the mortality rate reduced to 8.6%. A reduction in deaths and patient stays can be achieved through effective preventions in which 101 lives and 1357 patient-days saved per 10,000 patients as the result of hypothetical prevention that halves the infection hazard (Table 6).

Discussion

Our study revealed a higher burden of NNIs in JUMC and use of catheter, having surgical procedure and longer duration of hospital stay of neonates increased the risk of NIs. Our prediction showed that effective prevention of NIs reduces neonatal mortality and hospital stay.

In this analysis the overall magnitude of NNIs was 30%, which was relatively higher compared with others studies in Ethiopia; 27.5% in Shashemene referral hospital reported [35], 18% in Gonder hospital [36] and studies in other counties; 21.4% at Mansoura University Hospital, Egypt [37], and 13.5% Ali Asghar children Hospital reported in Iran [38].

Neonates with extended hospital stays had a higher risk of NIs. This association has also been confirmed in previous published works [39–42]. This is due to a longer hospitalization leads to additional treatment and exposure to various sources of infection [43, 44]. We found neonates with urinary catheters had a three-fold increased risk of developing NIs compared to patients without catheterization. Other studies conducted in India [45], Morocco [46], Egypt [47], and China [48] had also showed similar findings. This could be due to microorganisms causing NIs to rise from the perineum by capillary action in the thin mucous film adjacent to the external catheter surface, microorganisms originating from the patient’s urinary flora, or the hands of medical staff during catheter insertion or manipulation of the collection system. When urine in the collection bag becomes contaminated or closed drainage fails, the bacteria that gained access to the catheter lumen through reflux can cause intraluminal contamination [49].

Neonates who had undergone surgical operations were found to be 2.42 times more likely to develop hospital-acquired infections than those who did not undergo surgical procedures. This finding was consistent with other studies conducted in Egypt [50], Iran [38] and Perak [51].

Surgical procedures increase the risk of Nosocomial infections due to various factors such as the duration of surgery, preoperative hospital stay, immune function, microbial contamination, proper adherence to aseptic techniques, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and meticulous surgical practices [52–54].

Our finding revealed neonates with nosocomial infection had a higher rate of death risk after six days. From the competing risk regression, we found NIs and gestation age as risk factors for in-hospital mortality. However, previous studies reported that age and NIs were significant risk factor for hospital mortality [55]. On the other hand, the age of the neonate at admission, NIs status, and LOHS were identified as factors for discharge alive. When the neonate was older at admission, the discharge rate increased. As LOHS grew, hospital discharge rates reduced in comparison. Patients without NIs had a higher discharge rate than those with NI.

However, other studies have suggested age, sex, and NIs are significant predictors of discharge from hospital [55]. The data estimates had a 0.3 risk of NNIs, a mortality rate of 9.6%, and an average length of stay 14.6 days. This resulted in infected patients experiencing longer hospital stays and increased mortality rates.

As a result, the most significant advantage of prevention was observed in this dataset. Several measures can help reduce the risk of nosocomial infections [56]. For instance, educational modules and in-service training courses have been successful in improving nurses’ knowledge and practices regarding nosocomial infection control measures [57]. Enhanced recovery after surgery programs, including antibiotic prophylaxis tailored to individual patient risk factors, surgery type, and drug characteristics, play a crucial role in preventing nosocomial infections after thoracic surgery [58]. Furthermore, standardized infection prevention practices, such as hand hygiene and environmental cleaning, along with team engagement and leadership involvement [59]. These interventions collectively contribute to decreasing the incidence of nosocomial infections and improving patient outcomes.

By using our models and assuming a theoretical prevention method could decrease the infection risk by 50% (q = 0.50), for every 10,000 patients, it would result in the saving of 101 deaths and 1357 patient days.

A key strength of the study was its ability to predict the effectiveness of preventing neonatal deaths and reducing hospital stays due to NIs using large sample sizes. There were no related previously available studies that applied competing risk regression to determine the factors associated with neonatal NIs and estimate the preventive impact of such infections. However, the study was limited as it was conducted at a single hospital, and variations in neonatal intensive care may affect the generalizability of the results across different hospital units.

Conclusion

Neonatal nosocomial infections are more prevalent after surgical procedures and urinary catheterization and in neonates with longer hospital stay. Neonates who acquire nosocomial infection have a longer hospital stay and a higher probability of dying. Our finding indicated that effective prevention of NIs could lead to a decrease in neonatal deaths and their hospital stays. Therefore, appropriate implementation of infection prevention in hospital can contribute for reduction of neonatal mortality related to nosocomial infection and save additional cost and resources lost due to longer stay of neonates in the hospital.
