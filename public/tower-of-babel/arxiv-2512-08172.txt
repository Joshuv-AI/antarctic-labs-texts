Security Analysis of Integer Learning with Errors with
Rejection Sampling

Abstract
At ASIACRYPT 2018, a digital attack based on linear least squares was introduced for a variant of the learning with errors ( LWE ) problem which omits modular reduction known as the integer learning with errors problem ( ILWE ). In this paper, we present a theoretical and experimental study of the effectiveness of the attack when applied directly to small parameter ILWE instances found in popular digital signature schemes such as CRYSTALS-Dilithium which utilize rejection sampling. Unlike other studies which form ILWE instances based on additional information obtained from side-channel attacks, we take a more direct approach to the problem by constructing our ILWE instance from only the obtained signatures. We outline and introduce novel techniques in our simulation designs such as modular polynomial arithmetic via matrices in ℝ \mathbb{R} , as well as algorithms for handling large sample sizes efficiently. Our experimental results reinforce the proclaimed security of signature schemes based on ILWE . We additionally discuss the implications of our work and digital signatures as a whole in regards to real-world applications such as in Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS).



Introduction

Digital signatures ensure the authenticity and validity of digital communications and interactions and are an essential component of any modern cryptographic protocol. In practice and implementation, algorithms currently used include EdDSA
14
, RSA
18
, and ECDSA
25
;
6

In recent years, quantum computing has become a major anticipated threat to currently used cryptographic systems (including digital signature schemes) due to quantum algorithms such as Shor’s algorithm
30
;
31
and Grover’s algorithm
12
;
13
. With this eventual threat, NIST has selected several post-quantum public key schemes and digital signature schemes for standardization which can be implemented on classical computers yet are resistant to attacks by quantum computers. Selected digital signature algorithms include CRYSTALS-Dilithium
10
(standardized in FIPS 204
20
), SPHINCS+
2
(standardized in FIPS 205
21
), and FALCON
26
(FIPS upcoming).

One of the most popular areas of post-quantum cryptography is lattice-based cryptography, which CRYSTALS-Dilithium and FALCON are both based on. For
m\geq 1
an integer, a subset
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a lattice if
\Lambda
is a discrete additive subgroup of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and no points in
\Lambda
are arbitrarily close to each other. Of particular interest in lattice-based cryptography is a class of lattices known as
q
-ary lattices. A lattice
\Lambda
is a
q
-ary lattice if
q\mathbb{Z}^{m}\subseteq\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{m}
. Given an integer matrix
\mathbf{A}
, a
q
-ary lattice may be defined more explicitly – for instance, via

\Lambda=\mathcal{L}_{q}^{\perp}(\mathbf{A})=\left\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\ :\ \mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}\equiv\mathbf{0}\mod q\right\}.

Several computationally hard problems arise from lattices such as the shortest vector (
SVP
),
\gamma
-approximate shortest vector (
\gamma
-
SVP
),
\gamma
-gap shortest vector (
\gamma
-
GapSVP
), closest vector (
CVP
), and bounded distance decoding (
BDD
) problems (see, e.g.,
24

LWE
) problem and the short integer solutions (
SIS
) problem. The basic idea of
LWE
involves solving noisy linear systems modulo some paramter
q
and is at least as hard as some hard lattice problems, a result proved by Regev in 2005
27
. Several popular variations of
LWE
exist and are used in practice such as module learning with errors (
MLWE
), which is described more in-depth alongside
SIS
in Section
2.3
.

In 2018, Bootle et al.
5
introduced a digital attack based on linear least squares which is effective against an integer version of the
LWE
problem known as integer learning with errors (
ILWE
) in which elements do not undergo reduction modulo
q
. Although not always immediately apparent,
ILWE
is used in some constructions of lattice-based digital signature schemes when paired with the technique of rejection sampling (explained in Section
3
). The attack in
5
is applied to a lattice-based signature scheme known as BLISS
9
using information obtained via side-channel attacks to formulate an
ILWE

In the signing algorithm of CRYSTALS-Dilithium one step is based on the security of
ILWE
with rejection sampling. Previous other studies have looked at the effectiveness of this least squares attack against CRYSTALS-Dilithium (see, e.g.,
15
;
32
;
7
;
4
;
3
;
8
). However, most of these attacks base themselves from information based on side-channel leakage. Furthermore, they do not look at the effectiveness of said attack for varying sizes of parameters. In this paper, we will look at a simpler but more direct approach to testing the attack from Bootle et al. against the
ILWE
structure prevalent in CRYSTALS-Dilithium without leaked information from side-channel attacks. This approach includes variations to parameters and underlying sampling distributions.

Our Contributions

We implement and apply simulations of the attack from Bootle et al.
5
to rejection sampling procedures present in schemes such as CRYSTALS-Dilithium to gauge the effectiveness of the attack. Unlike the approach in
5
and other aforementioned works which rely on information obtained via side-channel attacks, we opt for a more direct formulation of an
ILWE
instance using only the sample values obtained from the signing algorithm in CRYSTALS-Dilithium and similar schemes. In designing the implementation, we use novel techniques for transferring arithmetic in specific modules to matrix arithmetic over
\mathbb{R}
. We also introduce an algorithm to handle large sample sizes efficiently for the attacks from
5
. Our experimental results highlight the importance of the underlying sampling distributions used in
10
and help to highlight a range of parameters which are secure against these attacks without any assumptions of side-channel leakage that may occur.

Notation

Let

\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X^{n}+1)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{R}_{q}=\mathbb{Z}_{q}[X]/(X^{n}+1).

For a polynomial

(or

\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{1}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}|w_{i}|,\quad\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}w_{i}^{2}},\quad\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{0\leq i<n}\{|w_{i}|\}

respectively. For the vector
\mathbf{w}=(w^{(0)},\ldots,w^{(k-1)})\in\mathcal{R}^{k}
(or
\mathcal{R}_{q}^{k}
), we similarly have

\left\lVert\textbf{w}\right\rVert_{1}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\|w^{(j)}\|_{1},\quad\left\lVert\textbf{w}\right\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\|w^{(j)}\|_{2}^{2}},\quad\left\lVert\textbf{w}\right\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{0\leq j<k}\{\|w^{(j)}\|_{\infty}\}.

For

We denote

. When

\texttt{coeff}(\textbf{v})=(\texttt{coeff}(v^{(0)})||\dots||\texttt{coeff}(v^{(k-1)}))
. We also denote

as the Hamming weight of

, which is the number of non-zero coefficients of

. When
\textbf{v}=(v^{(0)},\dots,v^{(k-1)})\in\mathcal{R}^{k}
, we use the notation
\texttt{wt}(\textbf{v})=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\texttt{wt}(v^{(j)})
.

For

to denote the rounding of

to the closest integer, rounding down in the case of a tie. For a vector or polynomial

For a vector

as the

as the vector of length

of

For a set

as the uniform random sampling of

from the set

. For an arbitrary probability distribution

with standard deviation

Short Integer Solution Problem

We first introduce several popular problems in lattice-based cryptography which form the theoretical foundation for security. The first of which is the Short Integer Solution (SIS) problem, in which one must find a short integer combination of vectors equivalent to
0
modulo some parameter
q
. We state the problem more formally in Definition
2.1
below.

Definition 2.1
(Short Integer Solution (SIS))

Let
m,k,q
, and
\beta<q
be positive integers. For a randomly-chosen matrix
\mathbf{A}\leftarrow\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m\times k}
, the
SIS
problem is to find
\textup{{x}}\neq\textup{{0}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
such that
\mathbf{A}\textup{{x}}\equiv\textup{{0}}\mod q
and
\left\lVert\textup{{x}}\right\rVert\leq\beta
.

Here, the norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
is usually specified to be
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}
or
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty}
. Observe that the restriction
\beta<q
is necessary to avoid trivial solutions. If
\beta\geq q
, the solution
\textbf{x}=(q,0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
satisfies
\textbf{A}\textbf{x}\equiv\textbf{0}\mod q
for any
\textbf{A},m,k
. The problem in Definition
2.1
was first introduced by Ajtai in
1
, who shows the SIS problem is as at least as hard as solving some believed-to-be difficult problem for every lattice. More specifically, solving
SIS
parameterized by
m,k,q,\beta
with
A
is equivalent to solving
SVP
on the
q
-ary lattice
\mathcal{L}_{q}^{\perp}(\textbf{A})
. Furthermore, for
\textbf{A}\xleftarrow{\$}\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m\times n}
a solution
x
with
\left\lVert\textbf{x}\right\rVert\leq\beta
to
SVP
in the lattice
\mathcal{L}_{q}^{\perp}(\textbf{A})
gives a solution to
\beta\sqrt{n}
-
GapSVP
and
\beta\sqrt{n}
-
SIVP
on any
n
dimensional lattice. We refer the reader to
1
for more details.

In practical constructions of cryptosystems, a variation of SIS over modules known as module short integer solutions (
MSIS
) is used for advantages in efficiency and security. We outline the
MSIS
problem formally in Definition
2.2
below.

Definition 2.2
(Module Short Integer Solution (MSIS))

Let
m,k,n,q
, and
\beta<q
be positive integers. For a randomly-chosen matrix
\mathbf{A}\leftarrow\mathcal{R}_{q}^{m\times k}
, the
MSIS
problem is to find
\textup{{x}}\neq\textup{{0}}\in\mathcal{R}^{k}
such that
\mathbf{A}\textup{{x}}\equiv\textup{{0}}\mod(X^{n}+1,q)
and
\left\lVert\textup{{x}}\right\rVert\leq\beta
.

We remark that although we use the specific rings
\mathcal{R}_{q}
and
\mathcal{R}
,
MSIS
can be more generally defined for any polynomial
f(X)
of degree
n-1
for the rings
\mathbb{Z}_{q}[X]/(f(X))
and
\mathbb{Z}[X]/(f(X))
respectively.
SIS
and
MSIS
are particularly useful in cryptography to construct a one-way function (see, e.g.,
1
;
17
;
23
). In lattice-based signature schemes such as CRYSTALS-Dilithium, the the hardness of
MSIS
is needed to ensure crucial security assumptions hold such as strong unforgeability
10
.

Learning With Errors Problem

Another popular and widely used problem in lattice-based cryptography is the Learning With Errors (
LWE
) problem. The
LWE
problem, introduced by Regev in
27
, tasks the solver with finding a solution to a system of noisy linear equations modulo
q
. Formally,
LWE
can be defined as follows.

Definition 2.3
(Learning With Errors (LWE))

Let
\textup{{s}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{k}
fixed and
\chi
a probability distribution on
\mathbb{Z}
. Suppose
\textup{{A} }\xleftarrow{\$}\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m\times k}
and
\textup{{b}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{k}
is computed as

\textup{{b}}=\textup{{A}}\textup{{s}}+\textup{{e}}\mod q

for
\textup{{e}}\leftarrow\chi^{k}
.
Given
(\textup{{A}},\textup{{b}})\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m\times k}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{k}
, the
LWE
problem is to recover
s
.

Most commonly,
\chi
is chosen to be the discrete Gaussian distribution (i.e.,
\chi=\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}
). When
\chi=\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}
with
\sigma\geq 2\sqrt{k}
, Regev
27
shows solving LWE on average is at least as hard as solving some approximate lattice problems quantumly. In
22
, Peikert shows a similar result for a classical reduction given some restrictions on
q
. For more details on reductions relating to LWE, see e.g.
27
;
22
;
28
;
16
.

As in the case of
SIS
,
LWE
can be generalized to the module setting which we call module learning with errors (
MLWE
). We give the
MLWE
problem in Definition
2.4
below.

Definition 2.4
(Module Learning With Errors (MLWE))

Let
\textup{{s}}\in\mathcal{R}_{q}^{k}
fixed and
\chi
a probability distribution on
\mathcal{R}
. Suppose
\textup{{A} }\xleftarrow{\$}\mathcal{R}_{q}^{m\times k}
and
\textup{{b}}\in\mathcal{R}_{q}^{k}
is computed as

\textup{{b}}=\textup{{A}}\textup{{s}}+\textup{{e}}\mod(X^{n}+1,q)

for
\textup{{e}}\leftarrow\chi^{k}
.
Given
(\textup{{A}},\textup{{b}})\in\mathcal{R}_{q}^{m\times k}\times\mathcal{R}_{q}^{k}
, the
MLWE
problem is to recover
s
.

As with
LWE
, the distribution
\chi
in
MLWE
is commonly chosen to be discrete Gaussian (i.e.,
\chi=\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{n}
). In a similar to fashion to
MSIS
,
MLWE
is a well-known and extensively used problem in several areas of lattice-based cryptography. Most relevant to our work in this paper, the hardness of
MLWE
forms a significant foundation for security against key recovery attacks in signature schemes, such as CRYSTALS-Dilithium
10
.

We should note that in Definitions
2.3
and
2.4
, we respectively phrase
LWE
and
MLWE
as the problem of recovering a secret vector
s
. This is known as the
search
version of
LWE
or
MLWE
. However, the two problems could instead be given as a
decision
problem in which one must decide if
(\textup{{A}},\textup{{b}})
is constructed as described or sampled uniform randomly. Though we do not outline the decision version of the problems in this paper, solving the search version and the decision version of
LWE
(or
MLWE
) is actually equivalent.

A lesser known variant of the
LWE
problem which omits the modular reduction is introduced by Bootle et al. in
5
. This variant, known as integer learning with errors (
ILWE
), is given below as Definition

.

Definition 2.5
(Integer Learning With Errors (ILWE))

Let
\textup{{s}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
fixed and
\chi_{a},\chi_{e}
probability distributions on
\mathbb{Z}
. Suppose
\textup{{A} }\leftarrow\chi_{a}^{m\times k}
and
\textup{{b}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
is computed as

\textup{{b}}=\textup{{A}}\textup{{s}}+\textup{{e}}

for
\textup{{e}}\leftarrow\chi_{e}^{k}
.
Given
(\textup{{A}},\textup{{b}})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times k}\times\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, the
ILWE
problem is to recover
s
.

In comparison to the construction in
LWE
, observe that the entries of
b
=
As
+
e
are not reduced modulo
q
in
ILWE
. The reduction modulo
q
is a crucial step for ensuring the hardness of
LWE
, hence the
ILWE
problem is less used in the foundations of forming practical lattice-based cryptographic protocols. However, when paired with rejection sampling (to be discussed in Section
3
),
ILWE
-like constructions appear in lattice-based digital signatures such as BLISS and Dilithium.

Attacks on ILWE via Least Squares

After introducing the ILWE problem,
5
develops and outlines an effective attack against
ILWE
based on the linear least squares method (LSM). The attack is simple and elegant, yet quite effective so long as the number of rows
m
in the
ILWE
instance is large enough and the error distribution
\chi_{e}
is
subgaussian
:

Definition 2.6
(Subgaussian Variable)

Let
\tau>0
be a real number. A random variable
X
over
\mathbb{R}
is said to be
\tau
-subgaussian if for all
u\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{E}[\exp(uX)]\leq\exp\left(\frac{\tau^{2}u^{2}}{2}\right).

A
\tau
-subgaussian probability distribution is defined similarly.

The term subgaussian is a broad term that applies to several probability distributions common in
LWE
and
ILWE
. For instance, the discrete Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}
is
\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\pi}}
-subgaussian
and the uniform distribution over
[-\alpha,\alpha]\cap\mathbb{Z}
is
\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{2}}
-subgaussian

5
. We now summarize the LSM attack below as Algorithm
1
, which we will commonly refer to as the “LSM attack”.

\texttt{Attack.LSM}(\textbf{A},\textbf{b})

Input:

\textbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\textbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, matrices for an ILWE instance.

Output:

\tilde{\textbf{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
recovered secret.

Step 1.

Compute
\hat{\textbf{s}}:=(\textbf{A}^{\top}\textbf{A})^{-1}\textbf{A}^{\top}\textbf{b}
.

Step 2.

Compute
\tilde{\textbf{s}}=\lfloor\hat{\textbf{s}}\rceil
.

Step 3.

Return
\tilde{\textbf{s}}
.

5
show that if given sufficiently many

of
\textbf{A})

obtains
\hat{\textbf{s}}
satisfying
\left\lVert\hat{\textbf{s}}-\textbf{s}\right\rVert_{\infty}<1/2

\tilde{\textbf{s}}=\textbf{s}
. For the distributions
\chi_{a}
and
\chi_{e}
, let us denote
\sigma_{a}
and
\sigma_{e}

below adapted from Theorem 4.5 in

\chi_{a}
is

It can be shown that

actually does very well against

We should emphasize that this attack exploits the underlying mathematical structure of information known (or deduced) by a party, and hence we consider it as a digital attack. Although we will discuss the implications of this work and the broader impact in practical applications in Section

Rejection Sampling and Digital Signatures

Rejection sampling, introduced in
33
, is a common technique used in several digital signature algorithms to ensure independence of the secret key from signature components. The basic idea is to sample elements from some probability distribution and reject them with some probability. More specifically, for a given probability distribution
f
and for some positive constant
M\in\mathbb{R}
, a sample
u
is drawn from
f
and accepted with probability
g(u)/(M\cdot f(u))
for some other probability distribution
g
. This ensures that our samples actually follow the distribution
g
. This allows one to generate samples following the distribution
g
, while only accessing samples from the distribution
f
. Although this describes the basic idea of rejection sampling, we refer the reader to other works
33
;
9
for a more detailed and comprehensive foundation for rejection sampling.

CRYSTALS-Dilithium

One of the most popular post-quantum digital signature schemes is CRYSTALS-Dilithium
10
. In fact, the module-lattice-based digital signature standard (ML-DSA) standardized by NIST in 2022
20
is derived from CRYSTALS-Dilithium. The overall security of CRYSTALS-Dilithium is based on two well-known lattice problems: the MSIS (
2.2
) and MLWE (
2.4
) problems.
In the main signing algorithm, a rejection sampling procedure is used. We provide an overview of said rejection sampling loop below in Algorithm
3
. Note that we omit a large majority of the actual signing procedure for simplicity, as we will only be concerned with this rejection sampling procedure. The full signing algorithm can be found in
10
;
20
.

\texttt{Dilithium.Samples}(k,\textbf{s},\gamma,\rho,\beta)

Input:

k\in\mathbb{N}
module rank,

\textbf{s}\in\mathcal{R}^{k}
secret with coefficients in
[-\eta,\eta]
,

\gamma\in\mathbb{N}
parameter satisfying
\left\lVert\textbf{y}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\gamma
,

\rho\in\mathbb{N}
Hamming weight of
c
,

\beta\in\mathbb{N}
parameter for rejection sampling bound.

Step 1.

Sample uniform random
\textbf{y}\leftarrow\mathcal{R}_{q}^{k}
with each polynomial having coefficients in
[-\gamma+1,\gamma]
.

Step 2.

Sample random
c\leftarrow\mathcal{R}_{q}
with coefficients in
\{0,\pm 1\}
and Hamming weight
\rho
.

Step 3.

Compute
\textbf{z}:=\textbf{y}+c\textbf{s}\in\mathcal{R}^{k}
.

Step 4.

Reject if
\left\lVert\textbf{z}\right\rVert_{\infty}\geq\gamma-\beta
and go back to Step 2. Else, continue.

Step 5.

Return
(\textbf{z},c)
.

Algorithm 3:
Basic Overview of Dilithium’s Rejection Sampling Procedure

We provide some explanations and important observations for a few of these steps. In Step 2, we need to randomly sample an element of
\mathcal{R}_{q}
with coefficients in
\{0,\pm 1\}
and Hamming weight
\rho
. The standard method for this is to use the
SampleInBall
procedure outlined below in Algorithm
4
as done in
10
. This procedure (or similarly designed procedures) are fairly standard for hashing to a ball. In our later experiments, we use a basic implementation of this procedure to generate
c\in\mathcal{R}
by simply generating the coefficients vector of
c
via Algorithm
4
. Careful consideration and precaution should be used when implementing or using this procedure in practice however.

\texttt{SampleInBall}(\rho)

Input:

\rho\in\mathbb{N}
parameter.

Output:

\textbf{c}\in\{0,\pm 1\}^{n}
with Hamming weight
\rho
.

Step 1.

Initialize vector
\textbf{c}=\textbf{0}
of length
n
.

Step 2.

For
i:=(n+1)-\rho
to
n
do

j\xleftarrow{\$}\{1,\dots,i\}
,

\textbf{c}[i]=\textbf{c}[j]
,

\alpha\xleftarrow{\$}\{0,1\}
,

\textbf{c}[j]=(-1)^{\alpha}
.

Step 3.

Return
c
.

Algorithm 4:
Creating a Random Vector with
\rho
Entries
\pm 1
.

For Step 3, note that we compute
z
as an element in
\mathcal{R}^{k}
(i.e., without reducing coefficients modulo
q
). Although many specifications note that
z
should be computed modulo
q
, it actually does not make a difference. Since
\left\lVert\textbf{y}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\gamma
and
\left\lVert c\textbf{s}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\rho\cdot\eta
, it is clear that
\left\lVert\textbf{y}+c\textbf{s}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\gamma+\rho\cdot\eta<q/2
for essentially any reasonable choice of parameters. Hence reduction modulo
q
does not occur even when computing in
\mathcal{R}_{q}^{k}
.

In Step 4, the value
\gamma-\beta
serves as the rejection sampling bound. We should note that the parameter
\beta
is usually chosen as the maximum possible coefficient of
c\textbf{s}
for verification to be correct. In our later experiments, we will consider a slightly different case since we only are concerned with security of this key recovery attack. Instead of focusing solely on
\beta
, we will consider the more general bound
\gamma-\beta
which appears in the rejection sampling. For fixed
\gamma
, we choose
\beta
so that the rejection rate is roughly
50\%
(more specifically, between
40\%
and
60\%
most of the time). The change in
\beta
is most noticeable for smaller parameter experiments which is discussed in Section
5
, where the value of
\beta
may sometimes even be negative. This is acceptable for use in our simulations. We also slightly adjust
\rho
in our experiments, the Hamming weight of
c
for different scenarios.

BLISS

In
5
, which introduces the attack described in Section
2.4
, the authors apply the LSM attack to BLISS (Bimodal Lattice Signature Scheme)
9
. Though the structure of BLISS has a quite similar structure to Dilithium,
5
applies the LSM attack in a very different way than we will. Our formulation of an
ILWE
instance will be discussed later in Section
4.1
.
5
apply the attack to BLISS by using a known value of
\langle\textbf{z},c\textbf{s}\rangle
obtained via side-channel attacks. To best describe their approach, we introduce the main signing algorithm from BLISS (Algorithm
5
) which the attack is used on.

\texttt{BLISS.Sign}(\textbf{s},\texttt{pk},m,\zeta)

Input:

\textbf{s}=(s_{1},s_{2})\in\mathcal{R}_{2q}^{2}
secret,

\texttt{pk}\in\mathcal{R}_{2q}
public key,

m\in\mathcal{R}_{q}
message,

\zeta\in[0,2q-1]
with
\zeta\cdot(q-2)=1\mod 2q
.

Output:

(\textbf{z}=(z_{1},z_{2}^{\prime}),c)\in\mathcal{R}_{2q}^{2}\times\mathcal{R}
signature.

Step 1.

Sample
y_{1},y_{2}\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}^{n}
.

Step 2.

Compute
u:=\zeta\cdot\texttt{pk}\cdot y_{1}+y_{2}\mod 2q
.

Step 3.

Compute
c:=H(\lfloor u\rceil_{d}\mod p,m)\in\mathcal{R}
.

Step 4.

Sample
b\xleftarrow{\$}\{0,1\}
and compute

z_{1}:=y_{1}+(-1)^{b}s_{1}c
,

z_{2}:=y_{2}+(-1)^{b}s_{2}c
.

Step 5.

Continue with probability
1/(M\text{exp}(-\left\lVert\textbf{s}c\right\rVert_{2}^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}))\text{cosh}(\langle\textbf{z},\textbf{s}c\rangle))
. Else, go back to Step 1.

Step 6.

Compute
z_{2}^{\prime}:=(\lfloor u\rceil_{d}-\lfloor u-z_{2}\rceil_{d})\mod p
.

Step 7.

Return
(\textbf{z}=(z_{1},z_{2}^{\prime}),c)
.

Algorithm 5:
BLISS Signing Algorithm

We omit several finer details of Algorithm
5
. This includes generation of
pk
, the notation
\lfloor\cdot\rceil_{d}
, and some additional parameters like
M
and
p
.
H
is a hash to a ball (e.g.,
SampleInBall
). We refer the reader to
9
for a more comprehensive outlook on the BLISS signing algorithm. Observe however that the internal structure of the signing algorithm is quite similar to Dilithium, computing

\displaystyle z_{1}

\displaystyle:=y_{1}+(-1)^{b}s_{1}c,

\displaystyle z_{2}

\displaystyle:=y_{2}+(-1)^{b}s_{2}c,

with
\textbf{z}=(z_{1},z_{2})
,
\textbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2})
, and
\textbf{s}=(s_{1},s_{2})
. That is,
\textbf{z}\in\mathcal{R}^{2}
satisfies

\textbf{z}:=\textbf{y}\pm c\textbf{s}\in\mathcal{R}^{2}.

Though there are several other differences between the signing algorithms, we take particular notice of the rejection sampling step. In BLISS, signatures are rejection with some probability, whereas Dilithium rejects them based on the infinity norm of part of the sample.

To formulate an
ILWE
instance based on the structure of BLISS signatures,
5
show that
\langle\textbf{z},c\textbf{s}\rangle=\langle\textbf{a},\textbf{s}\rangle+e
with

\textbf{a}=(z_{1}c^{*},2^{d}z_{2}^{\prime}c^{*})\quad\text{and}\quad e=\langle z_{2}-2^{d}z_{2}^{\prime},s_{2}c\rangle.

Here,
c^{*}
is the conjugate of
c
with respect to the inner product. For many samples, an
ILWE
may be constructed with the matrix
A
with rows consisting of each
a
and the entries of
b
consisting of each
\langle\textbf{z},c\textbf{s}\rangle
recovered from side-channel attacks.

It can be shown that the
e
described above follows some bounded distribution
\chi_{e}
with variance

\sigma_{e}^{2}\approx\frac{2^{2d}}{3}(\delta_{1}+4\delta_{2})n\rho

for some
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
. On the other hand,
a
follows some distribution
\chi_{a}
with variance

\sigma_{a}^{2}=\rho\cdot\text{Var}(\mathcal{D}_{\sigma})

for the underlying discrete gaussian distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}
. The main observation to make is that generally we will have
\sigma_{e}^{2}>\sigma_{a}^{2}
. This is true for all the example parameter choices used in
5
.

Implementation Strategies

In this section, we outline our techniques for implementing our attack simulations. We specifically apply the attacks to outputs of Algorithm
3
. Said otherwise, given many samples of the form
(\textbf{z}_{i},c_{i})\in\mathcal{R}^{k}
with

\textbf{z}_{i}=\textbf{y}_{i}+c_{i}\textbf{s}\in\mathcal{R}^{k}

(1)

we wish to attempt to recover
s
using the attack from Bootle et al
and Gao. The attacks have been implemented in release R2023b (version 23.2) of
MATLAB
, which utilizes matrix operations over
\mathbb{R}
. For this to be successfully done, there are two techniques we use: converting polynomial arithmetic in
\mathcal{R}
to matrices over
\mathbb{R}
, and an algorithms for handling large sample sizes.

Arithmetic in
\mathcal{R}
via Matrices Over
\mathbb{R}

In formulating the
ILWE
instance and discussing algorithms of implementing the described attack, it will be useful to consider the problem of recovering
s
with many samples in the form of equation (
1
) as a problem over integer (and real) matrices. For any two polynomials
f\in\mathcal{R}
and
g\in\mathcal{R}
, write

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=f_{0}+f_{1}X+\dots+f_{n-1}X^{n-1},

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle=g_{0}+g_{1}X+\dots+g_{n-1}X^{n-1}.

Define the matrix
\textbf{F}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
and vector
\textbf{G}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
via

\textbf{F}=\begin{bmatrix}f_{0}&-f_{n-1}&-f_{n-2}&-f_{n-3}&\cdots&-f_{1}\\
f_{1}&f_{0}&-f_{n-1}&-f_{n-2}&\cdots&-f_{2}\\
f_{2}&f_{1}&f_{0}&-f_{n-1}&\cdots&-f_{3}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
f_{n-1}&f_{n-2}&f_{n-3}&f_{n-4}&\cdots&f_{0}\end{bmatrix},\quad\textbf{G}=\begin{bmatrix}g_{0}\\
g_{1}\\
g_{2}\\
\vdots\\
g_{n-1}\end{bmatrix}.

Here,
F
is simply the matrix representation of
f\in\mathcal{R}
under the ordered monomial basis
(1,X,\dots,X^{n-1})
of
\mathcal{R}
. We then claim that we can compute the coefficients of
fg\mod X^{n}+1
by the matrix-vector product using the defined
F
and
G
. That is,

\texttt{coeff}(fg\mod X^{n}+1)=\textbf{F}\textbf{G}.

In practice, computing the matrix-vector product is actually less efficient than computing the polynomial product and reducing modulo
X^{n}+1
. Computing
fg\mod X^{n}+1
takes
O(n\log n)
operations using a number theoretic transform (see, e.g.,
29
) whereas the product
F
G
is naively
O(n^{2})
. This matrix-vector product will be used for crafting our
ILWE
instance however.

We can extend the above ideas to the product
f\textbf{g}\in\mathcal{R}^{k}
for
f\in\mathcal{R}
and
\textbf{g}\in\mathcal{R}^{k}
in the following way. Write

\textbf{g}=(g^{(0)},g^{(1)},\dots,g^{(k-1)})\in\mathcal{R}^{k}

and let
\textbf{G}^{(j)}
be the coefficient vector of
g^{(j)}
. Define matrix
\tilde{\textbf{F}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{nk\times nk}
and vector
\tilde{\textbf{G}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{nk}
via

\tilde{\textbf{F}}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{F}&&&\\
&\textbf{F}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\textbf{F}\end{bmatrix},\quad\tilde{\textbf{G}}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{G}^{(0)}\\
\textbf{G}^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\textbf{G}^{(n-1)}\end{bmatrix}.

Then,

\texttt{coeff}(f\textbf{g}\mod X^{n}+1)=\tilde{\textbf{F}}\tilde{\textbf{G}}.

In regards to our specific problem, observe that each sample
(\textbf{z}_{i},c_{i})\in\mathcal{R}_{q}^{k}\times\mathcal{R}_{q}
may be represented by a matrix
\tilde{\textbf{C}}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{nk\times nk}
and a vector
\tilde{\textbf{Z}}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{nk}
. Supposing we have access to
m
samples, recovering
s
can then be done by solving an
ILWE
instance with

\textbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\textbf{C}}_{1}\\
\tilde{\textbf{C}}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{\textbf{C}}_{m}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{Z}^{mnk\times nk},\quad\textbf{b}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\textbf{Z}}_{1}\\
\tilde{\textbf{Z}}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{\textbf{Z}}_{m}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{Z}^{mnk}.

Handling Large Sample Sizes

The matrix
A
for the
ILWE
instance described has
mnk
rows, which clearly will be difficult to deal with for large sample sizes
m
. Computing the least squares estimator for a matrix of size
mnk\times nk
can be done in
O(mn^{2}k^{2})
. Below we show an algorithm with this complexity (Algorithm
6
), specifically catered to the structure of samples in
\mathcal{R}
with real matrices.

\texttt{Samples.LSM}(m)

Input:

m
number of samples used.

Output:

x
least squares estimator obtained for
m
samples.

Step 1.

Initialize the
(kn+1)\times(kn+1)
matrix
\textbf{B}:=\textbf{0}
.

Step 2.

For
i:=1
to
m
do

(a)
Get Sample
\textbf{D}:=(\tilde{\textbf{C}}||\tilde{\textbf{Z}})\in\mathbb{R}^{kn\times(kn+1)}
,

(b)
Compute
\textbf{B}:=\textbf{B}+\textbf{D}^{\top}\textbf{D}
.

Step 3.

Solve
\textbf{B}[1\text{:}kn,1\text{:}kn]\textbf{x}=\textbf{B}[1\text{:}kn,kn+1]
.

Step 4.

Return
x
.

Algorithm 6:
LSM Algorithm for Large Samples

In Step 2 of Algorithm
6
,
(\tilde{\textbf{C}}||\tilde{\textbf{Z}})
is the matrix representation of the sample
(\textbf{z},c)
. Recall also that
\textbf{B}[1\text{:}kn,1\text{:}kn]
denotes the submatrix of
B
consisting of the first
kn
rows and first
kn
columns and
\textbf{B}[1\text{:}kn,kn+1]
the submatrix consisting of the first
kn
rows and the last column of
B
. We now give a brief description of why Algorithm
6
works. For an
ILWE
instance with
A
and
b
, let
\textbf{M}=(\textbf{A}||\textbf{b})
. Then note that

\textbf{M}^{\top}\textbf{M}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{A}^{\top}\textbf{A}&\textbf{A}^{\top}\textbf{b}\\
\textbf{b}^{\top}\textbf{A}&\textbf{b}^{\top}\textbf{b}\end{bmatrix}.

If

\textbf{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\textbf{B}_{1}\\
\textbf{B}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\textbf{B}_{m}\\
\end{pmatrix},

then we have
\textbf{M}^{\top}\textbf{M}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\textbf{B}_{i}^{\top}\textbf{B}_{i}
. We can compute
\textbf{x}=(\textbf{A}^{\top}\textbf{A})^{-1}\textbf{A}^{\top}\textbf{b}
by solving a linear system with portions of
\textbf{M}^{\top}\textbf{M}
. This gives the Algorithm
6
above, as each
\textbf{B}_{i}
here is constructed with the matrix representations of the samples
\textbf{B}_{i}=(\tilde{\textbf{C}}_{i}||\tilde{\textbf{Z}}_{i})
.

Large Sampling Algorithms for SVD

Algorithm
6
can be easily modified to work for the attack introduced by Gao described in Section
2.5
as well. We give the modification as a separate algorithm in Algorithm
7
.

\texttt{Samples.SVD}(m)

Input:

m
number of samples used.

Output:

v
the last column of
V
in SVD obtained for
m
samples.

Step 1.

Initialize the
(kn+1)\times(kn+1)
matrix
\textbf{B}:=\textbf{0}
.

Step 2.

For
i:=1
to
m
do

(a)
Get Sample
\textbf{D}:=(\tilde{\textbf{C}}||-\tilde{\textbf{Z}})\in\mathbb{R}^{kn\times(kn+1)}

(b)

Step 3.

\textbf{B}=\textbf{V}\Sigma\textbf{V}^{\top}

Algorithm 7:
SVD Algorithm for Large Samples

For an
ILWE
instance, suppose the matrix
\textbf{M}=(\textbf{A}||-\textbf{b})
has an SVD
\textbf{M}=\textbf{U}\Sigma\textbf{V}^{T}
. Then an SVD of
\textbf{M}^{\top}\textbf{M}
is given by

\textbf{M}^{\top}\textbf{M}=\textbf{V}\Sigma^{\top}\Sigma\textbf{V}^{\top}.

In fact, any SVD of
\textbf{M}^{\top}\textbf{M}
gives rise to a matrix
V
for an SVD of
M
. So, we only need to compute
\textbf{M}^{\top}\textbf{M}
, then compute an SVD of
\textbf{M}^{\top}\textbf{M}
to run the attack. Though we do not outline the experimental results of this algorithm in our paper, we note that Algorithm
7
can in fact be used to apply the attack from Algorithm
2
for large sample instances of
ILWE
.

Experimental Model and Results

Attack Model

The strategies outlined in Sections
4.1
and
4.2
are used to simulate the two attacks outlined in Section
2.4
. We begin by describing our overall attack model used to evaluate the effectiveness of these attacks. Our workflow for these simulations follows the general framework below:

1.

Fix parameters
n,\rho,k,\eta
, randomly choose a secret
\textbf{s}\in\mathcal{R}^{k}
with coefficients in
[-\eta,\eta]
, and set
\gamma,\beta
so that the rejection sampling bound we reject roughly about 50% of our samples (through trial and error).

2.

For
m=10^{2}
,
10^{3}
,
10^{4}
, and
10^{5}
, obtain
m
samples
(\textbf{z},c)
in matrix representation using the implementation techniques from Section
4
.

3.

Run the attack from Section
2.4
to obtain our recovered
\tilde{\textbf{s}}
.

4.

Compute and return
\left\lVert\tilde{\textbf{s}}-\textbf{s}\right\rVert_{1}
, discarding the result if
\tilde{\textbf{s}}=\textbf{0}
.

The result we obtain with the above workflow is
\left\lVert\tilde{\textbf{s}}-\textbf{s}\right\rVert_{1}
. We choose the 1-norm to measure the “success” of an attack in an attempt to balance the number of slots recovered with the sizes of coefficients recovered. Notice furthermore that
\texttt{wt}(\tilde{\textbf{s}}-\textbf{s})\leq\left\lVert\tilde{\textbf{s}}-\textbf{s}\right\rVert_{1}
and
\left\lVert\tilde{\textbf{s}}-\textbf{s}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\left\lVert\tilde{\textbf{s}}-\textbf{s}\right\rVert_{1}
, so this result actually serves as a worst-case bound for both the difference in the largest coefficient and the number of slots recovered. If
\rho
and
\eta
are known, an obtained result
\tilde{\textbf{s}}
can be adjusted so that
\tilde{\textbf{s}}
has the correct weighting and its coefficients are in the correct range.

As mentioned in Section
4
, the attacks have been implemented in release R2023b (version 23.2) of
MATLAB
. We use a 13th Gen Intel Core i7-1355U 1.70 GHz processor to run the simulations. We should note however that the processor is largely irrelevant for these experimental results. Since we obtain a specific
\tilde{\textbf{s}}
resulting from the attack which we define out success from, processing power does not matter. A better processor will allow us to run the attack with more samples (which may result in better
\tilde{\textbf{s}}
), however the resulting
\tilde{\textbf{s}}
will be roughly the same so long as the same number of samples are used.

Applications and Broader Impact

The findings of this study extend beyond theoretical cryptanalysis and hold practical significance for multiple domains transitioning toward quantum-safe security infrastructures. As quantum computing continues to evolve, industries that depend on the confidentiality and authenticity of digital communication need to reassess the robustness of their cryptographic foundations. The insights derived from our analysis directly contribute to the information for this migration by reinforcing security assurances against recently developed digital attacks.

In the transportation domain, lattice-based digital signature schemes such as CRYSTALS-Dilithium are anticipated to serve as the foundation for authentication mechanisms across Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS), autonomous and connected vehicles, and Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) communication infrastructures. Ensuring secure message signing and verification among vehicles, roadside units, and cloud servers is essential to prevent message tampering in safety-critical environments. The resilience insights provided in this work can therefore help to support the design of secure communication protocols emphasizing low latency and small computational costs, which are important requirements for use in transportation infrastructures.

Beyond transportation, the implications of this research extend to a variety of critical infrastructure domains. In finance and digital banking, post-quantum digital signatures will safeguard transaction verification and blockchain-based systems against quantum-enabled forgeries. In healthcare, lattice-based schemes can protect patient telemetry and secure communication between medical devices and cloud servers where message authenticity is vital. The industrial and energy domains, which increasingly depend on distributed sensor networks and remote-control systems, can also benefit from our findings to strengthen authentication between edge devices and supervisory systems. Similarly, defense and aerospace applications, which demand long-term data confidentiality and secure command chains, can leverage
ILWE
-informed parameterization to anticipate and mitigate quantum-era threats.

Collectively, this work bridges the gap between mathematical cryptanalysis and real-world deployment, emphasizing how subtle variations in lattice-based signature constructions and parameter selections influence resiliency across security-critical domains. The results underscore the growing importance of post-quantum readiness as a cross-domain priority, guiding policymakers, engineers, and system designers toward cryptographic implementations that remain secure in the coming quantum era. Furthermore, this work will be important to different domain areas in terms of how post-quantum signature schemes, an integral part of secure post-quantum cryptography (PQC) communication, may minimize the computational and communication latency required for time-sensitive real-world applications.

Conclusions

In this paper, we’ve conducted an analysis of the effectiveness of the attack from Bootle et al.
5
in the context of rejection sampling procedures present in digital signature schemes such as CRYSTALS-Dilithium. In particular, we approach the key recovery problem as an instance of
ILWE
without any additional information obtained from side-channel attacks while using matrices in
\mathbb{R}
for representations of constructed polynomials in
\mathcal{R}
. Additionally, we introduce strategies for handling large sample sizes with both the least squares and SVD approaches. Our experimental results show ranges of parameters which are vulnerable to the least squares attack. The effectiveness of the attack when the underlying sampling distribution is changed emphasizes the importance of the standard distributions used. We note that, as expected, the attack is not effective for any parameters used in practice. Additionally, we outline and discuss the relevance of this work and
ILWE
-based digital signature constructions for practical deployment in critical transportation areas such as ITS and V2X communication. For future work, we plan to pair our simulation results with results for other strategies targeting
ILWE
, using this to develop small, robust ranges of parameters for digital signature schemes utilizing rejection sampling which may reduce communication latency and computational costs needed for use in ITS and other practical applications.
