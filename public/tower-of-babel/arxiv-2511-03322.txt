Calibrations for minimal surfaces with free boundary and Cheeger-type problems
Guy Bouchitté and Minh Phan

Abstract
. We study a problem of minimal surfaces with free boundary written in the form of a non convex minimization problem. Our aim is to characterize optimal solutions by finding a suitable calibration field. A natural upper bound of the infimum is given by a variant of the Cheeger problem that we solve explicitly proving the optimality thanks to the construction of a cut-locus potential. The comparison with the original problem is then discussed in detail.



Keywords:
Free boundary problems, Calibrations, Minimal surfaces, Shape derivative, Cheeger sets, cut-locus potential.

2020 Mathematics Subject Classification:

49J45, 49N15, 49Q10, 65K10, 90C26.

1.
Introduction

In all the paper,
D
is a given bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with Lipschitz boundary. For any subset
\Omega\subset D
, we denote by
|\Omega|:=\mathcal{L}^{N}(\Omega)
its area (the
N
-dimensional Lebesgue measure of
\Omega
) and by
P(\Omega)
its perimeter. Recall that, if
P(\Omega)<+\infty
, then
P(\Omega)=\mathcal{H}^{N-1}(\partial\Omega)
, i.e. the
(N-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of the (essential) boundary of
\Omega
.

Given
\lambda\geq 0
, our aim is to study the following variational problem:

\displaystyle\beta(\lambda)

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{\int_{D}\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}\;dx-\lambda\;|\{u\geq 1\}|\;:\;u\in W^{1,1}_{0}(D)\right\}.

(1.1)

By using a truncation argument, one checks easily that the infimum above is unchanged if we restrict the infimum to competitors
u

such that
0\leq u\leq 1
. The free boundary associated with such
u
is then the essential boundary of the set
\Omega=\Omega(u):=\{u=1\}
. Accordingly, the minimization problem (
1.1
) contains two terms in competition, on the one hand the minimal area of a parametrized surface with
\partial D\times\{0\}
and
\partial\Omega\times\{1\}
boundaries, and on the other hand the area of the unknown subset
\Omega
times the scaling factor
\lambda
.
An alternative point of view is to see (
1.1
) as the minimization of the shape functional:

J_{\lambda}:\ \Omega\subset D\mapsto J(\Omega)+(1-\lambda)\,|\Omega|
where:

J(\Omega)=\inf\left\{\int_{D\setminus\Omega}\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}\;dx\ :\ u=0\quad\text{\;on\;}\partial D,\quad u=1\quad\text{\;on\;}\partial\Omega\right\}.

(1.2)

Figure 1.
A surface with prescribed boundary
u=0
on
\partial D
and free boundary
\partial\{u=1\}
.

In this context, if
\Omega
is an optimal set for
J_{\lambda}
, then a solution
u
to (
1.1
) is obtained by solving

\displaystyle-\Div\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}}=0\quad\text{\;in\;}D\setminus\Omega,

(1.3)

\displaystyle u=0\quad\text{\;on\;}\partial D,\qquad u=1\quad\text{\;on\;}\partial\Omega,

where (
1.3
) encodes the zero mean curvature of the parametrized surface
z=u(x)
.

In fact, it is well known that the above nonlinear boundary value problem does not always have a solution (see for example
[
19
]
). In order to start with a well-posed problem, we replace the variational problem (
1.1
) by the following relaxed formulation, in which the class of competitors
u
is extended to possibly discontinuous functions (thus violating the boundary conditions):

\beta(\lambda)=\inf\left\{E_{\lambda}(u)\;:\;u\in BV(D)\right\}

(1.4)

where

\displaystyle E_{\lambda}(u):=\int_{D}\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}\;dx+\int_{D}|D^{s}u|+\int_{\partial D}|u|\;d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}-\lambda\;|\{u\geq 1\}|.

(1.5)

Here
BV(D)
denotes the subspace of functions
u\in L^{1}(D)
whose distributional gradient
Du
is a vector Radon measure of bounded total variation from
D
to
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. The Radon-Nikodym derivative of
Du
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
D
coincides with the a.e. defined approximate gradient
\nabla u
while
D^{s}u
denotes its singular part. Such functions
u
have a well defined trace in
L^{1}(\partial D)
(see
[
27
]
) and a well defined jump set
S_{u}:=\{u_{+}>u_{-}\}
where
u_{-}(x),u_{+}(x)
are the lower (resp. upper) approximative limits of
u
at any
x\in D
. It turns out that
S_{u}
is an
N-1
dimensional rectifiable subset of
D
such that

D^{s}u=(u_{+}-u_{-})\,\nu_{u}\mathcal{H}^{N-1}\text{\huge$\llcorner$}S_{u}+D^{c}u
where
\nu_{u}
is a normal unit vector to
S_{u}
; the remainder of
D^{s}
on the complement of
S_{u}
is the so called Cantor part that we will denote by
D^{c}u
.
For further details on the space
BV(D)
, we refer to
[
9
]
and also to
[
10
]
for classical relaxation issues in this space. It is easy to check that (
1.4
) admits solutions in
BV(D;[0,1])
and that the infima defined in
(
1.1
) and (
1.4
) coincide. However, the solution is not unique (see
[
11
]
in a simpler context), and characterizing a global minimum turns out to be a very difficult problem. This is due to the nonconvexity of the term
-\lambda|\left\{u\geq 1\right\}|
. In fact, if we restrict the definition of
E_{\lambda}(u)
to
u\geq 0
, we can rewrite this term as the integral
\lambda\,\int_{D}g(u)dx
where
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle g(t)=\begin{cases}-t&\text{\;if\;}t\leq 0\\
0&\text{\;if\;}0\leq t<1,\\
-1&\text{\;if\;}t\geq 1.\end{cases}

(1.6)

This integrand
g
is lower semicontinuous non-increasing and admits a jump at
t=1
. Its convexification
g^{**}

given below is such that
\{g^{**}<g\}=(0,1)
:

\displaystyle g^{**}(t)=-\min\{t,1\}.

(1.7)

In order to overcome this lack of convexity, we will use a duality recipe in dimension
N+1
which was developed in
[
4
]
for functionals of the kind
u\in W^{1,p}(D)\to\int_{D}(f(\nabla u)+g(u))\,dx

for general non-convex lower semicontinuous functions
g:\mathbb{R}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
, possibly admitting isolated discontinuity points.
In
[
4
]
, the integrand
f
was assumed to be convex and satisfying a
p
-growth lower bound with
p>1
. Recently this duality result has been extended to the case
p=1
where
f
has linear growth at infinity (see
[
6
]
). Moreover, if
f
is assumed to be positively one homogeneous (for example
f(\nabla u)=|\nabla u|
),
an
exclusion principle
was derived in
[
6
]
, namely: the minimizers of
\int_{D}(f(\nabla u)+g(u))\,dx
under a Dirichlet condition
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\partial D)
such that
g(u_{0})=g^{**}(u_{0})
cannot take values in the set
\{g^{**}<g\}
.

We are also interested in a variant of (
1.1
) where
the surface-area term is replaced by the total variation of
u
. In virtue of the inequalities
|z|\leq\sqrt{1+|z|^{2}}\leq 1+|z|
holding for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we infer that:

\displaystyle m(\lambda,D):=\beta_{0}(\lambda)-|D|\ \leq\ \beta(\lambda)\ \leq\ \beta_{0}(\lambda)\quad,

(1.8)

where

\displaystyle\beta_{0}(\lambda):=\inf\left\{\int_{D}(1+|\nabla u|)dx-\lambda|\left\{u=1\right\}|\;:\;u\in W^{1,1}_{0}(D),\;0\leq u\leq 1\right\}.

(1.9)

We are led to another free boundary problem where the PDE counterpart of (
1.3
) is given by

\displaystyle-\Div\left(\frac{\nabla u}{|\nabla u|}\right)=0\quad\text{\;in\;}D\setminus\Omega\ ,

while the relaxed functional to be minimized in
BV(D;[0,1])
becomes

\displaystyle E_{\lambda}^{0}(u):=|D|+\int_{D}|Du|+\int_{\partial D}|u|d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}+\lambda\,\int_{D}g(u)\,dx\ .

(1.10)

Here, two important observations underscore the role of this variant of the original problem:

•

The equality
E_{\lambda}=E_{\lambda}^{0}
holds on the subset
BV(D;\{0,1\})
. Indeed, if
u
is the characteristic function
{{\bf 1}\kern-2.79999pt\rm l}_{\Omega}
of a subset
\Omega\subset D
, then
\Omega
has a finite perimeter in
R^{d}
given by
P(\Omega)=\int_{D}|Du|+\int_{\partial D}|u|d\mathcal{H}^{N-1}
(in that case
\nabla u=0
a.e and
D^{c}u=0
). It follows that:

u={{\bf 1}\kern-2.79999pt\rm l}_{\Omega}\ \implies\ E_{\lambda}(u)=E_{\lambda}^{0}(u)=P(\Omega)-\lambda|\Omega|.

(1.11)

•

The non convex variational problem
\beta_{0}(\lambda)
in (
1.9
) satisfies the one–homogeneity assumption mentioned above. Thus, in virtue of
[
6
, Theorem 2.2]
and since
\{g^{**}<g\}=(0,1)
, any solution to
\beta_{0}(\lambda)
is of the form
u={{\bf 1}\kern-2.79999pt\rm l}_{\Omega}
. A consequence is that the left hand side of (
1.8
) coincides with the minimum of the following shape optimization problem:

\displaystyle m(\lambda,D)=\min\left\{P(\Omega)-\lambda|\Omega|\;:\;\Omega\subset D\right\}.

(1.12)

Note that the equality
\beta_{0}(\lambda)=|D|+m(\lambda,D)
can be also recovered by using the co-area formula. Actually the problem (
1.12
) has been studied in
[
7
,
8
]
as being in close relation with the celebratred Cheeger problem (see the survey
[
26
]
):

h_{D}:=\inf\left\{\frac{P(\Omega)}{|\Omega|}\;:\;\Omega\subset D\right\}.

(1.13)

In particular, one has
m(\lambda,D)=0
for every
\lambda\leq h_{D}
while
m(\lambda,D)<0
otherwise.

The main contributions of this paper are the following:

-

We show that the strict inequality
\beta(\lambda)<\beta_{0}(\lambda)
holds if

u\equiv 0
or
u\equiv 1
are not minimal for (
1.1
) (Theorem
3.2
). This latter condition is equivalent to require that
\lambda
is between two thresholds
\lambda_{0}
and
\lambda_{1}
.
By Lemma
3.1
, these thresholds satisfy the inequalities:

\displaystyle 0<\lambda_{0}\ \leq h_{D}\ \leq\frac{P(D)}{|D|}\ \leq\ \lambda_{1}\ \leq\ +\infty.

More precise estimates are obtained in Proposition
3.6
by using special calibration fields for the dual problem in dimension
N+1
associated with
(
1.1
). Numerical simulations are given for radial examples in dimension two.

-

We develop a theory of
\theta
-calibrability for solving
m(\lambda,D)
. In the two-dimensional case, we associate to any bounded convex open set
D
a specific potential
\rho:\overline{D}\to(0,R_{D}]
where
R_{D}
denotes the inradius of
D
. This potential is continuous, locally Lipschitz in
D
with a maximal plateau
\{\rho=R_{D}\}
which coincides with the
central subset
defined by
U_{D}:=\{x\in D:d(x,D^{c})=R_{D}\}
.
In
D\setminus\overline{U}_{D}
,
\rho
satisfies
\nabla\rho\not=0
a.e. and solves the boundary problem:

\displaystyle\Div\frac{\nabla\rho}{|\nabla\rho|}+\frac{1}{\rho}=0\quad,\quad\frac{\nabla\rho}{|\nabla\rho|}\cdot\nu_{D}=-1\quad\text{on $\partial D$}\,.

(1.14)

A very simple geometric construction of this so-called cut locus potential is described in
Section
4
, which allows us to make explicit a calibrating vector field for any
\lambda\geq h_{D}
. Then we deduce that the unique solution of
m(\lambda,D)
is given by
\Omega_{\lambda}=\{\rho>\frac{1}{\lambda}\}
. It coincides with the union of all balls of radius
\frac{1}{\lambda}
contained in
D
(see (
4.1
)),
so that we recover by a different method the result in
[
7
,
8
]
.

Before we finish this introduction, we want to mention that the idea of building a potential
\rho
associated with a general convex set
D
can be extended in higher dimensions. The same PDE as in (
1.14
) would be kept, and solutions to (
1.12
) would still be the upper level sets
\{\rho>\frac{1}{\lambda}\}
for every
\lambda<h_{D}
. In turn the geometric characterization that we found for
N=2
(by means of the normal distance to the cut locus of
D
) does not work any more for
N>2
. This suggests a very interesting open issue worth to explore in future work.
Note that here the potential
\rho
depends only of the shape of
D
(see Remark
4.8
), in contrast with the parametrized potentials introduced in
[
8
]
.

-

In Section
2
, we present briefly the duality recipe for the non-convex problem
\beta(\lambda)
. This leads to an
N+1
dimentional dual problem and to optimality conditions in terms of divergence free calibration vector fields.
In the case of the homogenous variant
\beta_{0}(\lambda)
, this dual problem reduces to a more classical formulation in dimension

.
The optimality of a subset of

for (

) is characterized by a

- calibrability condition.

-

In Section

, we discuss the occurrence of the inequality
\beta(\lambda)<\beta_{0}(\lambda)
according to the value of
\lambda
and to the geometric properties of
D
. Some numerical simulations are given in the case where
D
is of a disk in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

-

In Section
4
, we restrict ourself to the case of a convex subset
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. After a short background on Cheeger sets, we introduce the called cut-locus potential
\rho
and focus on the explicit construction of a vector field
q:D\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{2}
which calibrates the unique solution
\Omega_{\lambda}
of (
1.12
).

2.
Dual problems and calibrations

The variational problems associated respectively with
\beta(\lambda)
and
\beta_{0}(\lambda)
are non convex with linear growth. They enter the duality framework developed initially in
W^{1,p}(D)
for
p>1
(see
[
4
]
), further extended to
W^{1,1}(D)
and
BV(D)
in
[
6
]
, where
D
is a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. In these references a characterization of global minimizers is provided by using calibration fields defined in
L^{\infty}(D\times I;\mathbb{R}^{N+1})

where
I
is an open interval of
\mathbb{R}
such that
\overline{I}
contains the range of all solutions.
This section is devoted to state the dual problems in the particular case of
\beta(\lambda)
and
\beta_{0}(\lambda)
.
In both cases, the solutions range in
[0,1]
so that we take
I=(0,1)
and the searched calibration fields are of the form

\sigma(x,t)=\left(\sigma^{x}(x,t),\sigma^{t}(x,t)\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}\quad\text{where $(x,t)\in D\times(0,1)$}.

In the following we will denote

Q:=D\times(0,1).

2.1.
Dual problem of (
1.1
)

Following
[
4
]
, the dual formulation of the variational problem of (
1.1
) (or of its relax form (
1.4
)) is given by
the following formulation in
Q
(thus in dimension
N+1
).

\displaystyle\sup\left\{-\int_{D}\sigma^{t}(x,0)\,dx\;:\;\Div\sigma=0\text{\;in\;}Q,\;\sigma^{t}+\sqrt{1-|\sigma^{x}|^{2}}\geq 0\text{\;in\;}Q,\;\sigma^{t}(x,1)\geq\lambda-1\text{\;on\;}D\right\},

(2.1)

Adopting a fluid dynamics view point,
(
2.1
) can be interpreted as the maximization of the downward flow
\sigma=(\sigma^{x},\sigma^{t})
of an incompressible fluid (
\Div\sigma=0
in
Q
) through the bottom interface
D\times\{0\}
when it is subject to the pointwise non linear constraint
\sigma^{t}+\sqrt{1-|\sigma^{x}|^{2}}\geq 0
a.e. in
Q
while
\sigma^{t}(x,1)\geq\lambda-1
on the upper interface
D\times\{1\}
.

]

Remark 2.2
.

In order to explain how the results in

are applied to our case for deriving the dual problem (

), two comments are in order:

-

Following the general notations of
[

]
, the original primal problem (

), can be written as

\inf\left\{f_{\lambda}(u,\nabla u)\,dx\ :\ u\in W^{1,1}_{0}(D)\right\},

where
f_{\lambda}(t,z):=\sqrt{1+|z|^{2}}+\lambda g(t)
and

is the non convex function in (

Then, the bulk constraint on

. In our case, we have

f_{\lambda}^{*}(t,\sigma^{x})=\lambda\,g(t)-\sqrt{1-|\sigma^{x}|^{2}}\quad\text{if $|\sigma^{x}|\leq 1$}\ ,+\infty\quad\text{otherwise}.

. Hence the bulk condition holding a.e. in

.

-

In view of the discontinuity of

at

, an additional normal trace condition has to be imposed on the boundary interface

\sigma^{t}(x,1)\geq\inf f_{\lambda}^{*}(1,\cdot)=-f_{\lambda}(1,0)
(see condition (3.21) in

]
). In our case, we obtain
the
\lambda

\sigma^{t}(x,1)\geq\lambda-1
, which accounts the free boundary associated with the subset

Note that this condition holding a.e.

is well defined in the sense of normal traces of bounded functions

Remark 2.3
.

Here a short background is in order.
We recall that
BV(D)
is the set of functions
u
in
L^{1}(D)
whose distributional gradient
Du
is an element of
\mathcal{M}(D;\mathbb{R}^{N})
the set of Radon vector measures from
D
to
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. By considering only elements

[0,1]
,

BV(D;[,1])
.
For every function
u\in BV(D)
,

is a bounded Radon measure which can be decomposed into

\displaystyle Du=\nabla udx+D^{c}u+(u_{+}-u_{-})\nu_{u}d(\mathcal{H}^{N-1}\text{\huge$\llcorner$}S_{u})

(2.4)

where
\nu_{u}
denotes the Radon-Nikodým density of
Du
with respect to its total variation
|Du|
, i.e.
\nu_{u}:=dDu/d|Du|
. Note that
D^{c}u
is the Cantor part of the measure
Du
. The quantity
[u]:=u^{+}-u^{-}
is called the

of

across the interface
S_{u}
and the direction of the jump is given by
\nu_{u}
along
S_{u}
. Accordingly, the
complete graph
of function
u
, denoted by
\overline{G}_{u}
, is defined by

\displaystyle\overline{G}_{u}:=\bigcup_{x\in\Omega}\Big(\{x\}\times[u_{-}(x),u_{+}(x)]\Big).

For futher details , we refer to the monograph
[
9
]
.

It is an
N
-rectifiable subset of
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}
with an oriented unit normal denoted by
\widehat{\nu}_{u}
. This oriented normal
\widehat{\nu}_{u}
is
\mathcal{H}^{N}\text{\huge$\llcorner$}\overline{G}_{u}
a.e. determined by

\displaystyle\widehat{\nu}_{u}(x,t)=(\nu_{u}(x),0)\quad\text{\;for\;}x\in S_{u}\text{\;and\;}t\in[u_{-}(x),u_{+}(x)],

(2.5)

on the vertical part of
\overline{G}_{u}
, whereas, on the
approximately continuous part

G_{u}:=\{(x,\tilde{u}(x))\}
, it is identified as

\displaystyle\widehat{\nu}_{u}(x,\tilde{u}(x))=\frac{(\nabla u(x),-1)}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u(x)|^{2}}}

(2.6)

if

is approximately differentiable at
x
(with its approximate gradient
\nabla u(x)
), and it is horizontal, i.e.
\widehat{\nu}_{u}(x,\tilde{u}(x))=(\nu_{u}(x),0)
, at points in the support of the Cantor part of

. Notice that
\nu_{u}=dD^{c}u/d|D^{c}u|
holds
|D^{c}u|
-a.e in
D
. We remark also that the complete graph
\overline{G}_{u}
of functions

belonging to

agrees with the
continuous graph

, on which
\widehat{\nu}_{u}(x,u(x))
is given by (

).
Therefore, the normal trace of the calibration field

given in (
2.3
) satisfies the equality
\sigma\cdot\widehat{\nu}_{u}=1

on
G_{u}
(
\mathcal{H}^{N}
-a.e.).

2.2.
Dual problem of
\beta_{0}(\lambda)

By applying the same duality framework as for
\beta(\lambda)
, we obtain the relation:

\displaystyle\beta_{0}(\lambda)=\sup\left\{\int_{D}-\sigma^{t}(x,0)dx\;:\;\Div\sigma=0,\;|\sigma^{x}|\leq 1,\;\sigma^{t}+1\geq 0\text{\;in\;}Q,\;\sigma^{t}(x,1)\geq\lambda-1\text{\;on\;}D\right\}.

We recall that
m(\lambda,D)=\beta_{0}(\lambda)-|D|,
where
m(\lambda,D)
is the minimum of the shape optimization problem (
1.12
).
In order to simplify further computations, it is convenient to rewrite the duality relation above, after changing
\sigma^{t}
into
\sigma^{t}+1
, as follows:

\displaystyle m(\lambda,D)=\sup\left\{\int_{D}-\sigma^{t}(x,0)dx\;:\;\Div\sigma=0,\;|\sigma^{x}|\leq 1,\;\sigma^{t}\geq 0\text{\;in\;}Q,\;\sigma^{t}(x,1)\geq\lambda\text{\;on\;}D\right\}.

Since the pointwise constraints over

It is easy o check that

It follows that:

In turn, we are going to show that the latter inequality is an equality.

Reduction to dimension

By the exclusion principle mentioned in the introduction, optimal solutions

Theorem 2.4

Let

(2.9)

In the left hand side of (

Proof.

First we notice that, with

m(\lambda,D)=\inf\left\{\int_{D}(|\nabla v|+\lambda\,g^{**}(v))\,dx\ :\ v\in W^{1,1}_{0}(D)\right\}.

Indeed, since

Next we apply classical convex duality arguments. The perturbation function

being

\displaystyle h^{*}(q)

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\int_{D}p\cdot q\,dx-h(p):p\in L^{1}(D;\mathbb{R}^{N})\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\int_{D}\left(p\cdot q-|p+\nabla v|-\lambda g^{**}(v)\right)\,dx:(v,p)\in W^{1,1}_{0}(D)\times L^{1}(D;\mathbb{R}^{N})\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\int_{D}\left(\tilde{p}\cdot q-|\tilde{p}|\right)+\int_{D}\left(v\,\Div q-\lambda g^{**}(v)\right)\,dx:(v,\tilde{p})\in W^{1,1}_{0}(D)\times L^{1}(D;\mathbb{R}^{N})\right\}

Here above:

-

-

to pass to the last line, we decoupled the supremum in

+\infty&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

It is convenient now to change

2.3.
Relations with Cheeger problem and calibrability notions

We recall that the Cheeger constant of a bounded domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is given by

\displaystyle h_{D}:=\inf\left\{\frac{P(\Omega)}{|\Omega|}\ :\ \Omega\subset D,|\Omega|>0\right\}.

(2.11)

It is well known that the infimum here is actually a minimum and
any optimal
\Omega
for this geometric problem is called a Cheeger set of
D
. In case
D
is convex, it turns out that this set is unique (see for instance
[
14
]
,
[
24
]
,
[
26
]
) and we shall denote it by
C_{D}
. For a non convex
D
, the uniqueness fails; however, since a union of Cheeger sets is still a Cheeger set, the definition of
C_{D}
can be extended
(see
[
24
]
,
[
13
]
) by setting

\displaystyle C_{D}:=\bigcup\;\{\Omega\;:\;\Omega\text{\;is a Cheeger set of\;}D\}.

In this case,
C_{D}
is called the
maximal Cheeger set
.
If
D
has finite perimeter, we will use in many places an upperbound for
h_{D}
, namely the ratio

\lambda_{D}:=\frac{P(D)}{|D|}\ .

(2.12)

It satifies the inequality
\lambda_{D}\geq h_{D}
while the equality holds
if and only if
D
is a Cheeger set of him self; for brevity, we will say that
D
is
self-Cheeger
.

Lets us now come back to the parametrized geometric optimization problem (
1.12
). It consists, for every
\lambda\geq 0
, in minimizing the shape functional

J_{\lambda}(\Omega)\ :=\ P(\Omega)-\lambda|\Omega|\ ,\ \text{ $\Omega$ measurable subset of $D$}.

Here we set
P(\Omega)=+\infty
if
\Omega
is not of finite perimeter and by convention
P(\Omega)=0
if
|\Omega|=0
. In particular, we have

m(\lambda,D)=\inf J_{\lambda}\leq 0
for every
\lambda\geq 0
.

The existence of minimizers for
J_{\lambda}
follows directly from the direct method of Calculus of Variations. As it appears in the proof of Theorem
2.4
,

m(\lambda,D)
is also the mimimum of the following companion convex problem:

\widetilde{m}(\lambda,D):=\min\left\{F_{\lambda}(u):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|Du|-\lambda\int_{D}u\ dx\ :\ u\in BV(\mathbb{R}^{N};[0,1]),u=0\ \text{a.e. on\ }D^{c}\right\}\ .

The equality
\widetilde{m}(\lambda,D)=m(\lambda,D)
is a consequence of the corea formula
F_{\lambda}(u)=\int_{0}^{1}J_{\lambda}(\{u>t\})\,dt\geq m(\lambda,D)
from which it follows that:

u\ \text{solves}\ \widetilde{m}(\lambda,D)\iff\{u>t\}\ \text{solves}\ m(\lambda,D)\ \text{for a.e. $t\in(0,1)$}.

The main properties of the function
m(\lambda,D)
are summarized below:

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded set with finite perimeter. Then

(i)

The function
m(\cdot,D)
is concave continuous non increasing on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and satisfies

-(\lambda-h_{D})_{+}\,|D|\ \leq m(\lambda,D)\ \leq\min\{0,P(D)-\lambda|D|\}.

(2.13)

Therefore it holds
m(\lambda,D)=0
for every
\lambda\in[0,h_{D}]
whereas:

m(\lambda,D)<0\ \text{ for $\lambda>h_{D}$}\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{\lambda\to+\infty}m(\lambda,D)+\lambda|D|=P(D).

(2.14)

(ii)

Assume that
D
is a minimizer of
m(\lambda^{*},D)
for a suitable value
\lambda^{*}>0
. Then
\lambda^{*}\geq h_{D}
and
D
is the unique minimizer of
m(\lambda,D)
for any
\lambda>\lambda^{*}
.
In particular the concave function
m(\cdot,D)
has the linear behavior
m(\lambda,D)=m(\lambda^{*},D)-(\lambda-\lambda^{*})\,|D|
for every
\lambda\geq\lambda^{*}
.

(iii)

Let
\Omega\subset D
. Then we have
m(\lambda,D)\leq m(\lambda,\Omega)
while

m(\lambda,D)=m(\lambda,\Omega)
if
\Omega
is a minimizer of
m(\lambda,D)
.
If such is the case with a set
\Omega
of positive measure, then the ratio
\lambda_{\Omega}=\frac{P(\Omega)}{|\Omega|}
satisfies
\lambda_{\Omega}<\lambda
.

Proof.

In the assertion (i), the fact that
m(\cdot,D)
is monotone is obvious, while the concavity follows from writing
m(\cdot,D)
as the infimum of the family of affine functions
\lambda\to P(\Omega)-\lambda|\Omega|
, when
\Omega
runs over subsets
\Omega\subset D
. The relations (
2.13
) are obtained by noticing that
P(\Omega)\geq h_{\Omega}|\Omega|
. Thus,
since
|\Omega|\leq|D|
, the shape functional
J_{\lambda}
to be minimized satisfies

-(\lambda-h_{D})_{+}\,|D|\leq\,(h_{D}-\lambda)\,|\Omega|\ \leq J_{\lambda}(\Omega)\ \,\leq m(\lambda,D).

(2.15)

Then, by taking the infimum in
\Omega
(and recalling that
m(\lambda,D)\leq 0
), we get (
2.13
) which proves that
m(\cdot,D)
is finite, hence continuous by the concavity property.
If
\lambda\leq h_{D}
, the first inequality in (
2.13
) implies that
m(\lambda,D)\geq 0
, hence
m(\lambda,D)=0
since we know that
m(\cdot,D)\leq 0
.
If
\lambda>h_{D}
, we obtain directly that
m(\lambda,D)<0
since
J_{\lambda}(\Omega)=|\Omega|(h_{D}-\lambda)<0
holds whenever
\Omega
is a Cheeger set of
D
.
Moreover, the second inequality in (
2.13
) implies that

\limsup_{\lambda\to\infty}\,m(\lambda,D)+\lambda|D|\leq P(D).

(2.16)

In the opposite direction, let
(\lambda_{n})
be any sequence such that
\lambda_{n}\to+\infty
and choose a minimizer
\Omega_{n}
for
m(\lambda_{n},D)
.
Then, by the upperbound inequality (
2.16
), we have

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(P(\Omega_{n})+\lambda_{n}|D\setminus\Omega_{n}|\right)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(m(\lambda_{n},D)+\lambda_{n}|D|\right)\leq P(D)<+\infty.

It follows that
{{\bf 1}\kern-2.79999pt\rm l}_{\Omega_{n}}\to 1
in
L^{1}(D)
while
\limsup_{n\to\infty}P(\Omega_{n})\leq P(D)
. By the lower semicontinuity of the perimeter, we infer that

P(\Omega_{n})\to P(D)
so that
\lambda_{n}|D\setminus\Omega_{n}|\to 0
. Finally, we have proved that

m(\lambda_{n},D)+\lambda_{n}|D|\to P(D)
as wished.
The proof of assertion (i) is finished.

Lets us turn to the assertion (ii). If
D
solves
m(\lambda^{*},D)
, then
\lambda^{*}\geq h_{D}
since otherwise, by the assertion (i), we would have

m(\lambda^{*},D)=J_{\lambda^{*}}(D)=0
which is uncompatible with the inequality
J_{\lambda^{*}}(D)\geq(h_{D}-\lambda^{*})|D|
.
Next we observe that, if
\lambda\geq\lambda^{*}
, then for every
\Omega\subset D

\displaystyle J_{\lambda}(\Omega)\ =\ J_{\lambda^{*}}(\Omega)+(\lambda^{*}-\lambda)|\Omega|\ \geq\ J_{\lambda^{*}}(D)+(\lambda^{*}-\lambda)|D|\ =\ J_{\lambda}(D).

It follows that
D
is optimal also for
m(\lambda,D)
and that
m(\lambda,D)=m(\lambda^{*},D)-(\lambda-\lambda^{*})\,|D|
.
Futhermore, in view of the inequalities above, the optimality of a competitor
\Omega
for

requires that
(\lambda^{*}-\lambda)|\Omega|=(\lambda^{*}-\lambda)|D|
.
If we assume that
\lambda>\lambda^{*}
, this is possible only if

which means that

. Hence

is the unique solution to

.

Let us prove now the assertion (iii). If
\Omega\subset D
, the inequality

is clear since any admissible subset for

is also a competitor for
m(\lambda,D)
. In particular, if

m(\lambda,D)=m(\lambda,\Omega)=J_{\lambda}(\Omega)

If moreover

as claimed.
∎

\displaystyle|\rho(x^{\prime})-\rho(x)|\ \leq\ \frac{R}{d(B(x,\varepsilon),D^{c})}\,|x^{\prime}-x|.

(4.11)

Recalling that
\rho
is constant on
\overline{\Omega_{\frac{1}{R}}}
, we conclude that
\rho
is locally Lipschitz in
D
.

In a second step, we show (
4.8
). The fact that
\rho(x)=\tau(x)
for every
x\in\partial D
is a direct consequence of the equality

\gamma(x)=\tau(x)
(see (
4.5
)) and of the fact that
\{\delta\ :\ \alpha(x,\delta)\leq 0\}=[0,\gamma(x)]
.
Note that, if
x\in\partial D
, then
\alpha(x,\delta)=0
whenever
\delta\leq\tau(x)
and we know from Lemma (
A.4
) that
\alpha(x,\cdot)
is stricly increasing on
[\gamma(x),R]
.

Next we show that
\tau(x)\leq\frac{1}{\kappa_{\partial D}(x)}
at any
x\in\partial D
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that

\tau(x)>0
. Then, by the definition of
\tau(x)
, this means that
x\notin\Lambda_{D}
, hence
x\in\partial_{r}D
and
d(x,D^{\delta})=\delta
whenever
\delta\leq\tau(x)
. In particular the equality
d(x,D^{\tau(x)})=\tau(x)
means that the ball
B(x-\tau(x)\nu_{D}(x)
is contained in
D
and touches the boundary
\partial D
at
x
. It follows that
\kappa_{\partial D}(x)<+\infty
and that
0<\rho(x)=\tau(x)\leq\frac{1}{\kappa_{\partial D}(x)}
.

In a last step, we show that
\rho
is continuous in all
\overline{D}
. First we notice that
\rho
is lower semicontinuous since any strict upper level set

\{\rho>r\}
coincides with the open subset
\Omega_{\frac{1}{r}}
for every
r\in(0,R_{D})
, while it coincides with
D
for
r=0
and the empty set for
r\geq R_{D}
.

Let
x_{n}\in\overline{D}
such that
x_{n}\to x
. The case where
x\in\overline{U}_{D}
is easy since we have
\rho(x_{n})\leq\max\rho=R_{D}=\rho(x)
and
by the lower semicontinuity of
\rho
,
\rho(x)\leq\liminf\rho(x_{n})\leq\limsup\rho(x_{n})\leq\rho(x)
.
Therfore we can assume that
x\notin\overline{U}_{D}
. Then
x_{n}\notin\overline{U}_{D}
for large
n
and,
in virtue to the characterization (
4.6
), we have

\rho(x_{n})=d(x_{n},D^{\rho(x_{n})})

As
\rho(x_{n})\leq R
, we can assume that, up to extracting a subsequence,
\rho(x_{n})\to\delta^{*}
for some
\delta^{*}
.
By the continuity property given in the assertion (i) of Lemma
A.1
, passing to the limit in the equality above leads to the equality

\delta^{*}=d(x,D^{\delta^{*}})
. Then, by Lemma
A.4
, we have either
\delta^{*}=\rho(x)
or
\delta^{*}=0
.
If
\delta^{*}>0
, we are done since, in this case, the whole sequence
\rho(x_{n})
converges to the unique cluster point
\rho(x)
.
If
\delta^{*}=0
, then
\rho(x_{n})\to 0
and, thanks the lower semicontinuity of
\rho
, we infer that
\rho(x)=0
. Since
\rho(x)\geq d(x,D^{c})
while

\rho(x)=\tau(x)
on
\partial D
, this is possible only if
x\in\partial_{s}D
where
\tau(x)=0
. However, even in this case, we have
\rho(x_{n})\to\rho(x)
.

∎

Remark 4.5
.

Y. Li and L. Nirenberg proved in
[
25
]
that
\tau
is Lipschitz if
D
has a
C^{2,1}
boundary but
it is untrue for a general convex domain (even
C^{2,\alpha}
with
\alpha<1
is not enough).
We conjecture that for a general convex domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the cut-locus potential
\rho
belongs to
C^{0,\frac{1}{2}}(D)
, as it is the case for a square (see the example
4.6
below).

Example 4.6

(An explicit formula for
\rho
in the case of a square)
.

Let us consider the domain
D=(-1/2,1/2)^{2}
whose inradius is
R=\frac{1}{2}
. Hence
\rho=1/2
on the disk
U_{D}=\Omega_{2}=\{|x|\leq\frac{1}{2}\}
.
The cut-locus
\overline{\Lambda}
consists of the two diagonals of
D
whereas the Cheeger constant is
h_{D}=2+\sqrt{\pi}
. For every
x\in D\setminus\Omega_{2}
,
\delta=\rho(x)
is the unique
\delta
such that
x
belongs to the arc of circle
C^{\delta}=\partial\Omega_{\lambda}\cap D
.
Let us compute
\rho
in the north east quater
[0,1/2]^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{2}
. We set
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
, and
\delta=1/2-t
for
t\in(0,1/2)
. Then,
x\in C^{\delta}
(hence
\rho(x)=\frac{1}{2}-t
) if and only if
t
solves the equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(x_{1}-t)^{2}+(x_{2}-t)^{2}=(\frac{1}{2}-t)^{2}\\
x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{4}.\end{cases}

These equations determine a unique
t\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
given by

\displaystyle t=x_{1}+x_{2}-\frac{1}{2}-\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{2}-x_{1}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-x_{2}\right)}.

Accordingly we obtain the following expression for
\rho
for
x\in D
:

\displaystyle\rho(x)=\begin{cases}1-(|x_{1}|+|x_{2}|)+\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{2}-|x_{1}|\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-|x_{2}|\right)}&\text{if}\ x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\geq 1/4\\
\frac{1}{2}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

This computation confirms that
\rho
vanishes only at the vertices of
\overline{D}
where the curvature is infinite. It is positive and of class
C^{1}
inside
D
. After some computations, we get the following equality:

\displaystyle|\nabla\rho(x)|=\frac{\rho(x)}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{2}-|x_{1}|\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-|x_{2}|\right)}}\quad\text{on}\ \{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\geq 1/4\},

which clearly shows that
|\nabla\rho|
blows-up when approaching the sides of the square. The presence of the square term in the expression of
\rho(x)
indicates that we cannot expect better than the
C^{1/2}
regularity of
\rho
in
\overline{D}
.
We present some calculations for the normalized gradient of
\rho
on
D\setminus\overline{U}_{D}.

\displaystyle\nabla\rho(x)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-1-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}a(x)\\
-1-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\frac{1}{a(x)}\end{array}\right)\ \text{where}\quad a(x):=\frac{\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}-x_{2}}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}-x_{1}}}\ ,

The unit vector
q_{\rho}:=-\frac{\nabla\rho}{|\nabla\rho|}
is given by:

\displaystyle q_{\rho}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{C_{1}(x)^{2}+C_{2}(x)^{2}}}\left(\text{sgn}(x_{1})\,C_{1}(x),\text{sgn}(x_{2})\,C_{2}(x)\right),

\displaystyle C_{1}(x):=1+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}a(x)\quad\text{and}\quad C_{2}(x):=1+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\frac{1}{a(x)}.

From the expressions above, we can check that, if
x
approaches the vertical sides (i.e.
|x_{1}|\to 1/2
), then
a(x)\to+\infty
so that

q_{\rho}(x)\to({\rm sgn}(x_{1}),0)
; on the same way
q_{\rho}(x)\to(0,{\rm sgn}(x_{2}))
as
|x_{2}|\to 1/2
. Therefore the normal trace of
q_{\rho}
on
\partial D

satisfies the equality
q_{\rho}\cdot\nu_{D}=1
.
This property will be confirmed in the general case in the forthcoming Theorem
4.7
.

\rho
and of its normalized gradient on the first quarter
[0,1/2]^{2}
is displayed in Figure

are drawn in varied colors while, in the right one, the normalized gradient of
\rho
is represented (with a magnifying glass) in black streamlines starting

.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded convex domain of inner radius
R
and central set
U_{D}
. Then, on the open subset
D\setminus U_{D}=\{0<\rho<R\}
, the locally Lipschitz potential
\rho
is such that
\nabla\rho\not=0\ \text{ a.e.}
. Moreover the unit vector field
q_{\rho}:=-\frac{\nabla\rho}{|\nabla\rho|}

satisfies

\Div q_{\rho}=\frac{1}{\rho}\quad\text{in $D\setminus\overline{U}_{D}$}\quad\text{and}\quad q_{\rho}\cdot\nu_{D}=1\quad\mathcal{H}^{1}\ \text{a.e. on $\partial D\setminus\overline{U}_{D}$}.

(4.12)

Here above, the first equality is intended a.e. and in the sense of distributions in
D\setminus\overline{U}_{D}
.

Proof.

Note that the open subset
\{0<\rho<R\}
is indeed
D\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\frac{1}{R}}}
where
\rho
is not constant. On this subset, the vector field
\eta:=-\nabla\rho/|\nabla\rho|
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\eta(x)=\frac{x-\Pi_{\rho(x)}(x)}{\rho(x)}.

Owing to the expression above, it is clear that the equality
\eta(x)=\nu_{D}(x)
holds at any regular point of the boundary, that is for every
x\in\partial_{r}D\setminus\overline{U}_{D}
.
Indeed, at those points, we have
\rho(x)=\tau(x)
and
x-\Pi_{\rho(x)}(x)=\rho(x)\nu_{D}(x)
.

Next we are going to prove that
\Pi_{\rho(x)}(x)
is locally Lipschitz in
x
and hence, so is
\eta
.
We firstly claim that for every
\delta,\delta^{\prime}
satisfying
0<\delta^{\prime}<\delta
, for each
y\in D
, there is a constant
K_{\delta}
such that

\displaystyle|\Pi_{\delta}(y)-\Pi_{\delta^{\prime}}(y)|\leq K_{\delta}|\delta-\delta^{\prime}|.

(4.13)

It follows immediately that for every
x\in D\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\frac{1}{R}}}
,
x^{\prime}\in B(x,\varepsilon)\subset D
, keeping in mind (
4.11
),

\displaystyle|\Pi_{\rho(x)}(x)-\Pi_{\rho(x^{\prime})}(x^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq|\Pi_{\rho(x)}(x)-\Pi_{\rho(x^{\prime})}(x)|+|x-x^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\leq K_{\rho(x)}|\rho(x)-\rho(x^{\prime})|+|x-x^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\leq\left(K_{\rho(x)}+\frac{R}{d(B(x,\varepsilon),D^{c})}\right)|x-x^{\prime}|.

Hence, we obtain that
\eta
is locally Lipschitz on
D
provided
we show the validity of the claim (
4.13
).
Given
0<\delta^{\prime}<\delta
, for every
y\in D
, by Lemma
A.3
(ii),
\Pi_{\delta^{\prime}}(y)
is always in
M^{\delta}_{\delta^{\prime}}(\Pi_{\delta}(y))
, see Figure
12
for illustration. We obtain

\displaystyle|\Pi_{\delta}(y)-\Pi_{\delta^{\prime}}(y)|\leq|w-x|=\frac{|\delta-\delta^{\prime}|}{\cos\varphi(x)},

(4.14)

where
x=\Pi_{\delta}(y)
and
w
is the extreme point of
M^{\delta}_{\delta^{\prime}}(x)
in the complement of
D^{\delta^{\prime}}
(see Figure
12
).
By exploiting the assertion (iii) of Lemma
A.5
, there exists
K_{\partial D^{\delta}}>0
such that

\displaystyle K_{\partial D^{\delta}}=\min\left\{k_{\partial D}(s)\;:\;s\in\partial D^{\delta}\right\}=\min\left\{\cos^{2}\varphi(s)\;:\;s\in\partial D^{\delta}\right\}.

Since
x\in\partial D^{\delta}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\cos\varphi(x)}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{K_{\partial D^{\delta}}}}.

(4.15)

We then use the inequalities (
4.14
) and (
4.15
) to derive that the inequality (
4.13
) holds with
K_{\delta}=(K_{\partial D^{\delta}})^{-1/2}

Figure 9.
Divergence of
\eta
along
C^{\delta}
.

The next step is to prove that
\displaystyle\Div\eta=\frac{1}{\rho}
in
D\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\frac{1}{R}}}
. The vector field
\eta
is indeed the unit normal to the level sets
C^{\delta}=\{x\in D\;:\;\rho(x)=\delta\}
which are arcs of radius
\delta
. Given
x
and
x_{\varepsilon}
on
C^{\delta}
illustrated by Figure
9
. Let us evaluate locally divergence of
\eta
along directions
\eta
and
\eta^{\perp}
.
We recall that

\displaystyle(D\eta)h\cdot h:=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left\langle\frac{\eta(x+\varepsilon h)-\eta(x)}{\varepsilon},h\right\rangle

for some non null direction
h
.
Since
|\eta|=1
, we have

\displaystyle 2(D\eta)h\cdot\eta=\left\langle\frac{\eta(x+\varepsilon h)-\eta(x)}{\varepsilon},\eta(x+\varepsilon h)+\eta(x)\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[|\eta|^{2}(x+\varepsilon h)-|\eta|^{2}(x)\right]=0.

Thus, for
h=\eta
, we get

\displaystyle(D\eta)\eta\cdot\eta=0.

(4.16)

As
\eta
locally Lipschitz, there is some constant
M
such that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\eta(x+\varepsilon h)-\eta(x_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}\right|\leq\frac{M}{\varepsilon}|x+\varepsilon h-x_{\varepsilon}|=\frac{M\delta}{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{1}{\cos\theta_{\varepsilon}}-1\right)=\frac{M\delta}{\varepsilon}\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\delta^{2}}}-1\right)\sim\frac{M\varepsilon}{2\delta}.

Hence, for
h=\eta^{\perp}
,

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{\eta(x+\varepsilon h)-\eta(x)}{\varepsilon},h\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{\eta(x+\varepsilon h)-\eta(x_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon},h\right\rangle+\left\langle\frac{\eta(x_{\varepsilon})-\eta(x)}{\varepsilon},h\right\rangle

\displaystyle\sim\frac{M\varepsilon}{2\delta}+\frac{\sin\theta_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\sim\frac{M\varepsilon}{2\delta}+\frac{\tan\theta_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{M\varepsilon}{2\delta}+\frac{1}{\delta}.

We obtain

\displaystyle(D\eta)\eta^{\perp}\cdot\eta^{\perp}=\frac{1}{\delta}.

(4.17)

From the equations (
4.16
) and (
4.17
), we can derive that

\displaystyle\Div\eta=(D\eta)^{T}:Id=(D\eta)^{T}:(\eta\otimes\eta+\eta^{\perp}\otimes\eta^{\perp})=(D\eta)\eta\cdot\eta+(D\eta)\eta^{\perp}\cdot\eta^{\perp}=\frac{1}{\delta}=\frac{1}{\rho}.

As
\eta
is locally Lipschitz on the open set
\{0<\rho<R\}
, the equality above holds not only a.e. on this set but also in the distributional sense.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

In view of Theorem
4.7
,
\rho
solves the boundary value problem

\Div\frac{\nabla\rho}{|\nabla\rho|}+\frac{1}{\rho}=0\quad\text{in $D\setminus\overline{U}_{D}$}\ ,\quad\frac{\nabla\rho}{|\nabla\rho|}\cdot\nu_{D}=-1\quad\text{in $\partial D\setminus\overline{U}_{D}$}\ ,\quad\rho=R_{D}\quad\text{in $\partial U_{D}$ .}

In fact it can be shown that
\rho
is directly related to the following strictly convex variational problem

\min\left\{\int_{D}|\nabla u|+\int_{\partial D}|u|\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}-\int_{D}\ln(u)\,dx\;:\;u\in W^{1,1}(D)\right\},

whose unique solution
\overline{u}
admits a maximal plateau
\overline{u}=\frac{1}{h_{D}}
and coincides with
\rho
on the complement of the Cheeger set of
D
.
Studying this new minimization problem in dimension
N\geq 2
could help us understand the structure of solutions to
m(\lambda,D)
in the case of a general domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, especially when the convexity assumption of the domain
D
is removed.

4.2.
The set
\Omega_{\lambda}
is calibrable and solves
m(\lambda,D)

By exploiting the PDE (
4.12
) satisfied by the cut-locus potential of

,
we are now in position to derive, as corollaries of Theorem

, the calibrability and the optimality of the set
\Omega_{\lambda}
for

.

Corollary 4.9
.

Let
\lambda\geq h_{D}
. Then
\Omega_{\lambda}
defined by (
4.1
) solves
m(\lambda,\Omega_{\lambda})
. Therefore
\lambda\geq\lambda_{\Omega_{\lambda}}
and
\Omega_{\lambda}
is
\theta
-calibrable with constant
\theta\leq\lambda\,\lambda_{\Omega_{\lambda}}^{-1}
.

Proof.

By (
2.20
), it is enough to show that
\Omega_{\lambda}
solves
m(\lambda,\Omega_{\lambda})
. Let us apply the optimality conditions of the assertion (ii) of Theorem
2.4
in the case where
D=\Omega=\Omega_{\lambda}
. Then we are done if we can find
\overline{q}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{\lambda};\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that

\displaystyle|\overline{q}|\leq 1\quad\text{\;a.e. in\;}\Omega_{\lambda}\ ,\quad 0\leq\Div\overline{q}\leq\lambda\quad\text{\;a.e. in\;}\Omega_{\lambda}\ ,\quad\overline{q}\cdot\nu_{\Omega_{\lambda}}=1\quad\text{\;$\mathcal{H}^{1}$-a.e. on\;}\partial\Omega_{\lambda}

(4.18)

Such a vector field can be constructed on
\Omega_{\lambda}
by starting from a calibrating field of the Cheeger set of
D
. This semi-explicit construction is provided with the existence of a calibrating field for the Cheeger set of
D
, that means
\overline{q}=q_{h_{D}}
in
\Omega_{h_{D}}
where
q_{h_{D}}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega_{h_{D}};\mathbb{R}^{2})
satisfies

\displaystyle|q_{h_{D}}|\leq 1,\quad\Div q_{h_{D}}=h_{D}\quad\text{\;a.e. in\;}\Omega_{h_{D}},\qquad q_{h_{D}}\cdot\nu_{\Omega_{h_{D}}}=1\quad\text{\;$\mathcal{H}^{1}$-a.e. on\;}\partial\Omega_{h_{D}}.

We need now to construct
\overline{q}
on
\Omega_{\lambda}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{h_{D}}}
. The cut-locus potential
\rho
comes into play here.
Let us define
\overline{q}
on
\Omega_{\lambda}
as follows:

\displaystyle\overline{q}(x):=\begin{cases}q_{h_{D}}(x)&\ \text{if $x\in\Omega_{h_{D}}$}\\
-\frac{\nabla\rho(x)}{|\nabla\rho|(x)}&\ \text{if $x\in\Omega_{\lambda}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{h_{D}}}$}\end{cases}

(4.19)

The condition
|\overline{q}|\leq 1
is clearly satisfied. On the other hand, recalling that
\Omega_{h_{D}}=\{\rho>h_{D}^{-1}\}
,
\rho
is constant on the interface
\partial\Omega_{h_{D}}\cap D
while
h_{D}\leq\rho^{-1}\leq\lambda
in
\Omega_{\lambda}\setminus\Omega_{h_{D}}
. Therefore the normal trace of
\overline{q}
has no jump and the ditributional divergence
\Div\overline{q}
belongs to
L^{\infty}(\Omega_{\lambda};[h_{D},\lambda])
since

\Div\overline{q}=\begin{cases}h_{D}&\ \text{if $x\in\Omega_{h_{D}}$}\\
\frac{1}{\rho}&\ \text{if $x\in\Omega_{\lambda}\setminus\Omega_{h_{D}}$}\end{cases}.

Eventually, the condition
\overline{q}\cdot\nu_{\Omega_{\lambda}}=1
is fullfiled on
\partial\Omega_{\lambda}\cap D
where
\rho=\frac{1}{\lambda}
.
The same equality holds on the shared boundary piece
\partial\Omega_{\lambda}\cap\partial D
since, by (
4.12
), it holds
\eta=\nu_{D}
on
\partial D\setminus\overline{U}_{D}
.
Finally, the three conditions in (
4.18
) are satisfied.
∎

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
\lambda>h_{D}
. Then, the upper level set

\Omega_{\lambda}=\{\rho>\lambda^{-1}\}

is the unique solution to the problem
m(\lambda,D)
.

We remark that, according to Theorem
2.4
, the optimality of
\Omega_{\lambda}
for the problem
m(\lambda,D)
can be achieved by extending the construction
of the calibrating field
\overline{q}
defined in (
4.19
) from
x\in\Omega_{\lambda}
to
x\in D\setminus\Omega_{\lambda}
. This will be done in the next subsection.
Here we propose a direct proof based on the PDE satisfied by the cut-locus potential of
D
, as a consequence of Theorem
4.7
.
Note that a similar PDE proof appears in
[
8
, Proposition 4]
exploiting the solutions of a family of auxiliary problems where a Neumann boundary condition is implicitely imposed on
\partial D
.

Proof.

Let consider
F\subset D
a Borel subset with finite perimeter. We need need to show that
P(F)-\lambda|F|\geq P(\Omega_{\lambda})-\lambda|\Omega_{\lambda}|
.
Let
\overline{q}
be the vector field satisfying (
4.22
) that we constructed in the proof of Corollary
4.9
.
Then, setting
\delta=\frac{1}{\lambda}
, we have the equalities:

P(\Omega_{\lambda})-\lambda\,|\Omega_{\lambda}|=\int_{\Omega_{\lambda}}(\Div\overline{q}-\lambda)\,dx=\int_{\Omega_{\lambda}}\left(\frac{1}{\rho}-\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\,dx.

On the other hand, since
|\overline{q}|\leq 1
, we have:

\displaystyle P(F)-\lambda\,|F|

\displaystyle\geq\int_{F}(\Div\overline{q}-\lambda)\,dx=\int_{F}\left(\frac{1}{\rho}-\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{\lambda}}\left(\frac{1}{\rho}-\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\,dx+\int_{F\setminus\Omega_{\lambda}}\left(\frac{1}{\rho}-\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\,dx-\int_{\Omega_{\lambda}\setminus F}\left(\frac{1}{\rho}-\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\,dx

Since
\frac{1}{\rho}\geq\frac{1}{\delta}\geq 0
on
F\setminus\Omega_{\lambda}
and
\frac{1}{\rho}\geq\frac{1}{\delta}\leq 0
on
\Omega_{\lambda}\setminus F
, we deduce that

P(F)-\lambda\,|F|\geq\int_{\Omega_{\lambda}}\left(\frac{1}{\rho}-\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\,dx=P(\Omega_{\lambda})-\lambda|\Omega_{\lambda}|,

whence the optimality of
\Omega_{\lambda}
for
m(\lambda,D)
for every
\lambda\geq h_{D}
.

Let us prove now the uniqueness of the solution of
m(\lambda,D)
for
\lambda>h_{D}
. From the previous arguments, we know now that for all
\mu,\nu
such that
h_{D}<\mu<\lambda<\nu
, the sets
\Omega_{\mu}
and
\Omega_{\nu}
are minimal for
m(\mu,D)
and
m(\nu,D)
respectively. Then, by a well known comparison argument (see for instance
[
8
, Lemma 4 (i)]
), we have the inclusions
\Omega_{\mu}\subset\Omega\subset\Omega_{\nu}
for any
\Omega
solving
m(\lambda,D)
.
The equality
\Omega=\Omega_{\lambda}
follows by sending
\mu\nearrow\lambda
and
\nu\searrow\lambda
.

∎

\partial D^{\delta^{\prime}}
. By using (ii),
d(w,D^{c})=\delta^{\prime}
. The ball

contained in

touches
\partial D
at and only at

. In other words,

\partial_{r}D^{\delta^{\prime}}
. Besides, both

. It is easy to see that they coincide. We conclude that

\partial_{r}D^{\delta^{\prime}}
, a contradiction. The proof is complete.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

The following assertions hold true:

(i)

Given

\Pi_{\delta^{\prime}}(y)\in M^{\delta}_{0}(x)

.

Proof.

(i)
For each

\displaystyle z(t):=(1-t)x^{*}+tx.

\displaystyle\delta^{\prime}=|z^{*}-x^{*}|\geq d(z^{*},D^{c})\geq d(z^{*},\partial B(z^{*},|z^{*}-x^{*}|))=\delta^{\prime}.

Lemma A.4
.

Let
\alpha(x,\delta)
be a function defined on
\overline{D}\times[0,R]
by

\displaystyle\alpha(x,\delta):=d(x,D^{\delta})-\delta.

(A.2)

Then, for every
x\in D\setminus\overline{\Omega_{\frac{1}{R}}}
, the function
\alpha(x,\cdot)
is continuous on

and exhibits the following behavior:

\displaystyle\alpha(x,\delta)=\begin{cases}-\delta\qquad&\text{\;if\;}0<\delta\leq d(x,D^{c})\\
-d(x,D^{c})&\text{\;if\;}x\notin\Lambda_{D}\,\text{\;and\;}\,d(x,D^{c})<\delta<\gamma(x)\\
\text{\;is strictly increasing\;}&\text{\;on \;}[\gamma(x),R].\end{cases}

Therefore, if

,
\alpha(x,\cdot)
vanishes at the unique positive
\delta:=\rho(x)
where
\gamma(x)<\rho(x)<R
.

On the other hand, if
x\in\partial D
, then
\gamma(x)=\tau(x)
and
\alpha(x,\cdot)
vanishes on
[0,\tau(x)]
while
\alpha(x,\cdot)>0
on the possibly empty
inerval
(\tau(x),R]
.

Proof.

The continuity statement results from the assertion (i) of Lemma
A.1
.
If
0<\delta\leq d(x,D^{c})
, then
x
belongs to
\overline{D^{\delta}}
and
d(x,D^{\delta})=0
implies that
\alpha(x,\delta)=-\delta
.

Next, we assume that
x\notin\Lambda
and we prove that
\alpha(x,\delta)=-d(x,D^{c})
if
\delta
belongs to the interval
(d(x,D^{c}),\gamma(x))
.
Note that, by the definition (
4.4
), it holds
\eta(x)=0
if
x\in\Lambda_{D}
, making the latter interval is empty. Then it exits
\overline{x}\in\overline{\Lambda}

and

such that

\displaystyle\overline{x}=x+\zeta(x)\nabla d(x,D^{c})\ ,\quad|\overline{x}-x^{*}|=d(\overline{x},D^{c})=\gamma(x).

Since we assumed that

,

meets the segment

at a unique point

and

. By Lemma

(i), we deduce that

As a consequence, keeping in mind that

(ii)), we obtain

\displaystyle d(x,D^{\delta})=|x-w|=|x^{*}-w|-|x^{*}-x|=\delta-d(x,D^{c}).

So, we conclude that
\alpha(x,\delta)=d(x,D^{\delta})-\delta=-d(x,D^{c})
as claimed.

Finally, let us show that the continuous function
\alpha(x,\cdot)
is strictly increasing in

. If it is the case, then as

\delta

such that

\displaystyle\Pi_{\delta^{\prime}}(\Pi_{\delta}(x))=\Pi_{\delta^{\prime}}(x)=\Pi_{\overline{B(x,|x-\Pi_{\delta^{\prime}}(x)|)}}(\Pi_{\delta}(x)).

(A.3)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\delta}(x)=x+t_{\delta}\nabla d(x,D^{c}),

Lemma A.5
.

Let
D
be a convex domain of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For every
x\in\partial D
, we define

\displaystyle k_{\partial D}(x):=\frac{1+\nu_{D}^{+}(x)\cdot\nu_{D}^{-}(x)}{2}.

Then, we have

(i)

for every
x\in\partial D
,
0<k_{\partial D}(x)\leq 1
;

(ii)

k_{\partial D}(x)=1
for every
x\in\partial_{r}D
;

(iii)

\forall\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, the set
\left\{x\;:\;k_{\partial D}(x)<\varepsilon\right\}
is finite.

Proof.

We recall that the normal cone of
D
at
x
is given by

\displaystyle N_{D}(x):=\left\{a\nu_{D}^{+}(x)+b\nu_{D}^{-}(x)\;:\;a,b\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\right\}.

For every
x\in\partial D
, we denote by
\varphi(x)
the angle

\displaystyle\varphi(x):=\frac{1}{2}\angle(\nu_{D}^{-}(x),\nu_{D}^{+}(x)),

and by
T_{D}(x)
the tangent cone of
D
at
x

\displaystyle T_{D}(x):=

\displaystyle\cl\left\{s(y-x)\;:\;y\in D,\;s\geq 0\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{aT_{D}^{+}(x)+bT_{D}^{-}(x)\;:\;a,b\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\right\},

where
T_{D}^{\pm}(x)
are the left and right tangent unit vectors of
D
at
x
. We denote by
\psi(x)
the angle

\displaystyle\psi(x):=\frac{1}{2}\angle(T_{D}^{-}(x),T_{D}^{+}(x)).

Since tangent and normal cones are polar each other, it holds
\varphi(x)+\psi(x)=\frac{\pi}{2}
for every
x\in\partial D
.

Proof of (i):
clearly, as
D
is convex, we have

0\leq\varphi(x)<\frac{\pi}{2}
and
0<\psi(x)\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
.
Hence, for all
x\in\partial D
, it holds
0<k_{\partial D}(x)\leq 1
since

\displaystyle k_{\partial D}(x)=\frac{1+\cos 2\varphi(x)}{2}=\cos^{2}\varphi(x)=\sin^{2}\psi(x).

Proof of (ii):
As
D
is convex, its boundary
\partial D
admits at most countably many singular points. On the regular part
\partial_{r}D=\partial D\setminus\partial_{s}D
, we get
\nu_{D}^{-}(x)=\nu_{D}^{+}(x)
, i.e.
\varphi(x)=0
. Then,
k_{\partial D}(x)=1
.

Proof of (iii):
Let
\varepsilon
such that
0<\varepsilon<1
and denote

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}:=\{x\in\partial D\;:\;k_{\partial D}(x)<\varepsilon\}\quad,\quad N_{\varepsilon}:=\#(E_{\varepsilon}).

The function
h(\varphi)
defined by

\displaystyle h(\varphi):=\frac{1+\cos 2\varphi}{2},\quad\text{\;for\;}\varphi\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}).

is non increasing in
[0,\frac{\pi}{2})
, while
h(\varphi(x))=k_{\partial D}(x)
for every
x\in\partial D
.
There always exists
\ \varphi_{e}\in(0,\pi/2)
such that
h(\varphi_{\varepsilon})=\varepsilon
. We set

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}:=\{x\in\partial D\;:\;k_{\partial D}(x)<\varepsilon\}.

By the monotonicity of
h
, we deduce that
\varphi(x)>\overline{\varphi}
for every
x\in E_{\varepsilon}
.
It follows that

\displaystyle N_{\varepsilon}\,\overline{\varphi}<\sum_{x\in E}\varphi(x)\leq\sum_{x\in\partial D}\varphi(x)\leq 2\pi,

whence
N_{\varepsilon}\leq\frac{2\pi}{\overline{\varphi}}<+\infty
.
∎
