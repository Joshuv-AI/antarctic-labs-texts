Lindblad dynamics of open multi-mode bosonic systems: Algebra of quadratic superoperators, exceptional points and speed of evolution
Andrei Gaidash, Alexei D. Kiselev, Anton Kozubov, George Miroshnichenko

Abstract
Quantum dynamics of open continuous variable systems is a multifaceted rapidly evolving field of fundamental and technological significance. An important example is a multimode photonic system coupled to a Markovian bath so that temporal evolution of the density matrix is governed by the master equation of the Lindblad form. Theoretical analysis of the Lindblad dynamics complicated by intermode couplings such as dynamical (coherent) and environment-mediated (incoherent) interactions between the modes can be rather involved even for exactly soluble models. Our algebraic technique uses the Lie algebra of quadratic combinations of left and right superoperators to simplify both quantitative and qualitative analysis of intermode coupling-induced effects in multi-mode systems by eliminating jump superoperators and reducing the Lindblad equation to the form governed by the effective non-Hermitian Hamiltonian, H ^ eff \hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}} . We show that the spectral properties of the thermal bath Liouvillian are determined by the effective temperature independent Hamiltonan and employ jump-eliminating transformations for computing its eigenvalues and eigenoperators. It is found that the Liouvillian exceptional points (EPs) are the points in the parameter space where the matrix, H H , associated with H ^ eff \hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}} is nondiagonalizable. For the case of photonic polarization modes modeled as a bimodal bosonic system, we describe the geometry of EPs and apply our method to study how the intermode-couplings-induced effects such as EPs and temperature influence the dynamics of the speed of evolution that governs the quantum speed limit times of polarization qubit states.

Introduction

The importance of the problems related to
quantum dynamics of open systems
for quantum technologies
such as

cannot be overestimated.
The theory of quantum open systems
is essential to

the processes that underlie
transfer and storage of quantum information

An alternative and a widely used
master equation approach to quantum systems
that evolve in time
interacting with an environment
assumes that temporal evolution
of the reduced density matrix,
\hat{\rho}
,
is governed by the equation of motion of the general form

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{\rho}}{\partial t}=\hat{\mathcal{L}}\hat{\rho},

(1)

where
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
is the Liouvillian superoperator
(a linear mapping acting on the vector space of linear operators).
When the evolution is unitary, so that

\hat{\rho}(t)=\hat{U}(t)\hat{\rho}(0){\hat{U}}^{\dagger}(t)
,
where
\hat{U}(t)
is the unitary evolution operator
and the dagger denotes Hermitian conjugation,
the Liouvillian,
\hat{\mathcal{L}}=\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{H}
,
is determined by the generator of
\hat{U}
giving
the Hermitian Hamiltonian,
\hat{H}
, as follows

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{H}\hat{\rho}=-i[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}],\quad\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{H}=-i(\overleftarrow{\hat{H}}-\overrightarrow{\hat{H}}),

(2)

where
[\hat{A},\hat{B}]=\hat{A}\hat{B}-\hat{B}\hat{A}

is the commutator and the left (right) arrow over an operator

\overleftarrow{\hat{A}}
(
\overrightarrow{\hat{A}}
)
indicates the left-hand (right-hand) action
superoperator:

\overleftarrow{\hat{A}}:\>\hat{\rho}\mapsto{\hat{A}}\hat{\rho}\equiv\overleftarrow{\hat{A}}\hat{\rho}

(
\overrightarrow{\hat{A}}:\hat{\rho}\mapsto\hat{\rho}{\hat{A}}\equiv\overrightarrow{\hat{A}}\hat{\rho}
).
For the general case of nonunitary dynamics,
the Liouvillian is supplemented by the dissipative terms
describing loss of energy and information into the environment.

Interestingly, in the framework of measurement theory,

They can be split into two parts:

Note that this interpretation implies that the environment

When evolution is conditioned on the results of measurements

In Sec.

II
Algebraic approach

In this section, we show that
the Jordan-Schwinger approach
extended to the case of left and right superoperators
provides an efficient and natural way to describe
the algebraic structure of superoperators that
enter bosonic GKSL equations.

II.1
Model

Lindblad dynamics of a
N
-mode bosonic system
interacting with a thermal environment
is governed by the Lindblad (GKSL) equation
of the following form
(our notations are close to those used in Ref.
[
33
]
):

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{\rho}}{\partial t}=-i\sum_{n,m}^{N}\Omega_{nm}[\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{m},\hat{\rho}]

\displaystyle-\sum_{n,m}^{N}\bigl\{\Gamma_{nm}^{(-)}(\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{m}\hat{\rho}+\hat{\rho}\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{m}-2\hat{a}_{m}\hat{\rho}\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle+\Gamma_{nm}^{(+)}(\hat{a}_{m}\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}+\hat{\rho}\hat{a}_{m}\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}-2\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}\hat{a}_{m})\bigr\},

(3)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\pm}=\gamma_{\pm}\Gamma,\quad\Gamma=\Gamma_{-}-\Gamma_{+},\quad\gamma_{+}=\gamma_{-}-1=n_{T},

(4)

where
the dagger stands for Hermitian conjugation,

\hat{a}_{n}
(
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{n}
) is the annihilation (creation) operator of
n
th mode
and
n_{T}
is the mean number of thermal photons;
the effects of coherent (dynamical)
intermode couplings are introduced through
the frequency matrix

\Omega={\Omega}^{\dagger}
,
whereas
the
elements of the positive definite relaxation matrices

\Gamma_{\pm}>0

and
\Gamma=\Gamma_{-}-\Gamma_{+}>0

give the coupling constants of
the incoherent (environment-mediated) interaction
between the bosonic modes.

Our task now is
to put the thermal bath multimode Liouvillian
in a form suitable for subsequent algebraic analysis.
To this end,
we shall generalize considerations presented
in Ref.
[
95
,
32
]
to
the case of multimode systems
and introduce quadratic combinations
of left and right superoperators
of the following form:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{(-)}_{nm}=\overleftarrow{\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{n}\hat{a}_{m}}-\overrightarrow{\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{n}\hat{a}_{m}},

(5a)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{nm}^{(0)}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\overleftarrow{\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{n}\hat{a}_{m}}+\overrightarrow{\hat{a}_{m}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{n}}\right).

(5b)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{nm}^{(+)}=\overleftarrow{\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{n}}\overrightarrow{\hat{a}_{m}},\quad\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{nm}^{(-)}=\overleftarrow{\hat{a}_{m}}\overrightarrow{\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{n}}.

(5c)

The Liouvillian
can now be expressed in terms of
the superoperators (
5
)
as follows

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}=\sum_{n,m}^{N}\Bigl[-i\Omega_{nm}\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{(-)}_{nm}+2\bigl\{\Gamma_{nm}^{(0)}\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{nm}^{(0)}

\displaystyle+\Gamma_{nm}^{(+)}\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{nm}^{(+)}+\Gamma_{nm}^{(-)}\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{nm}^{(-)}\bigr\}+\delta_{nm}\Gamma_{nm}\Bigr],

(6)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}=-(\Gamma_{+}+\Gamma_{-})=\gamma_{0}\Gamma,\quad\gamma_{0}=-2n_{T}-1,

(7)

where

\delta_{nm}
is the Kronecker symbol.
Note that
the terms proportional to the jump superoperators

\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{nm}^{(+)}
and
\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{nm}^{(-)}

represent
the quantum jump part of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
,
whereas
the effective Hamiltonian
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(0)}=\sum_{n,m}^{N}\left[\Omega_{nm}+i\Gamma_{nm}^{(0)}\right]\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{m}-i\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits{\Gamma_{+}}.

(8)

The formula (
6
)
is the starting point of our analysis.
It gives the multimode Liouvillian
written as a linear combination
of the superoperators:

\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{(-)}_{nm}
,
\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{nm}^{(0)}
and

\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{nm}^{(\pm)}
given by Eq. (
5
).
An important point is that these superoperators
generate a Lie algebra.
However,
owing to a number of factors, such as large dimension,
the structure of this algebra is prohibitively complex.
In subsequent sections,
we describe the approach
that allows us to
clarify an overall picture behind the complicated algebraic structure
by greatly reducing the number of
commutation relations.

II.2
Jordan map

Now we briefly remind the reader
about the
well-known Jordan-Schwinger approach that uses the Jordan map
(more details can be found in, e.g., Chapter 5 of the book
[
96
]
)
given by

\displaystyle A\mapsto\hat{J}_{A}=\sum_{n,m=1}^{N}A_{nm}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{n}\hat{a}_{m},

(9)

where
A
is the
N\times N
matrix,
to introduce quadratic boson operators associated with matrices.
It is rather straightforward to check that these operators meet the commutation relations

\displaystyle[\hat{J}_{A},\hat{J}_{B}]=\hat{J}_{[A,B]}

(10)

and enjoy a number of useful properties
including the indentities

\displaystyle e^{\hat{J}_{V}}\hat{J}_{A}e^{-\hat{J}_{V}}=\hat{J}_{A^{\prime}},\quad A^{\prime}=e^{V}Ae^{-V},

(11a)

\displaystyle e^{\hat{J}_{V}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}e^{-\hat{J}_{V}}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(e^{V})_{ji}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j},

(11b)

\displaystyle e^{\hat{J}_{V}}\hat{a}_{i}e^{-\hat{J}_{V}}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(e^{-V})_{ij}\hat{a}_{j}

(11c)

that will be used in our subsequent calculations.

Our next step is to introduce the algebra of superoperators which
bears some resemblance to
the above algebra with
the commutation relations (
10
).
The idea is to use bilinear combinations
of the superoperators so that the commutation relations appear to be partly translated into the
change of the matrices of the coefficients.

Now we move on to discussing the basic algebraic
properties, whereas
the spectral problem and
other applications will be considered later on.

II.3
Superoperators associated with matrices and Liouvillian

Let us introduce the following notation:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{A}\equiv\sum_{n,m}A_{nm}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{nm},

(12)

where
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{nm}
is an arbitrary (presumably quadratic) superoperator labeled with two
indices and
A_{nm}
is the element of a complex-valued matrix
A
. Then, according to the above
notation, the superoperators have the following general properties:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{A}+\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{A}=(\hat{\mathcal{P}}+\hat{\mathcal{Q}})_{A},

(13)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{A}+\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{B}=\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{A+B},

(14)

\displaystyle c\cdot\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{A}=\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{c\cdot A}.

(15)

Another useful property is that, when the operators meet the commutation relations of the form:

\displaystyle[\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{ij},\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{nm}]=\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{im}\delta_{jn}\pm\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{jn}\delta_{im},

(16)

where
[A,B]=AB-BA
is the commutator, we have the following identity:

\displaystyle[\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{A},\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{B}]=\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{AB\pm BA},

(17)

which is analogous to the key property of the Jordan-Schwinger map given by Eq. (
10
).
However, as we will
see in the next section, the algebraic structure of superoperators associated with matrices has a
number of important distinctive peculiarities.

By using these superoperators,
the Liouvillian (
6
)
may alternatively be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}=-i\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{(-)}_{\Omega}+2\bigl(\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(0)}_{\Gamma_{0}}+\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(+)}_{\Gamma_{+}}+\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(-)}_{\Gamma_{-}}\bigr)+\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\Gamma\,\hat{\mathcal{I}},

(18)

where

\hat{\mathcal{I}}
is the identity superoperator.
Note that the conjugate (adjoint) of the Liouvillian superoperator,

\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}
, defined through the relation

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{A}\hat{\mathcal{L}}(\hat{\rho})\}=\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}(\hat{A})\hat{\rho}\}

(19)

is also of the form (
18
)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}=i\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{(-)}_{\Omega}+2\bigl(\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(0)}_{\Gamma_{0}}+\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(+)}_{\Gamma_{-}}+\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(-)}_{\Gamma_{+}}\bigr)+\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\Gamma\,\hat{\mathcal{I}},

(20)

where
\Omega
is changed to
-\Omega

and the jump parameters
\gamma_{+}
and
\gamma_{-}

are interchanged:
\gamma_{\pm}\to\gamma_{\mp}
.
Now we discuss the basic commutation
properties of the superoperators that enter the above expression for the Liouvillian.

II.4
Commutation relations

In this section, our goal is to clarify the algebraic structure of the superoperators. Since this
structure is determined by commutation relations, we begin with the case of superoperator

\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{(-)}_{nm}
,
\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{nm}^{(+)}
,
\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{nm}^{(-)}
, and
\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{nm}^{(0)}
and
deduce the relations for superoperators of the form (
12
) associated with matrices.

It is rather straightforward to obtain
the relations

\displaystyle[\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(0)}_{ij},\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\pm)}_{nm}]=\pm\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\pm)}_{im}\delta_{jn}+\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\pm)}_{jn}\delta_{im}),

(21a)

\displaystyle[\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(-)}_{ij},\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(+)}_{nm}]=\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(0)}_{im}\delta_{jn}+\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(0)}_{jn}\delta_{im}-

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\big(\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{(-)}_{im}\delta_{jn}-\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{(-)}_{jn}\delta_{im}\big),

(21b)

\displaystyle[\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{(-)}_{ij},\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(s)}_{nm}]=\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(s)}_{im}\delta_{jn}-\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(s)}_{jn}\delta_{im},\quad s\in\{0,\pm\}

(21c)

and employ the properties given by Eqs. (
16
) and (
17
) to derive
the following commutation relations

\displaystyle[\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(0)}_{A},\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\pm)}_{B}]=\pm\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\pm)}_{\frac{1}{2}\{A,B\}},

(22a)

\displaystyle[\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(-)}_{A},\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(+)}_{B}]=\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(0)}_{\{A,B\}}-\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{(-)}_{\frac{1}{2}[A,B]},

(22b)

\displaystyle[\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{(-)}_{A},\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(s)}_{B}]=\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(s)}_{[A,B]},

(22c)

\displaystyle[\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(0)}_{A},\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(0)}_{B}]=\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{(-)}_{\frac{1}{4}[A,B]},

(22d)

\displaystyle[\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{(-)}_{A},\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{(-)}_{B}]=\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{(-)}_{[A,B]},

(22e)

\displaystyle[\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\pm)}_{A},\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\pm)}_{B}]=0,

(22f)

where
\{A,B\}=AB+BA
stands for the anticommutator.

Formulas (
22
) describe the algebraic structure that somewhat
resembles
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
Lie algebra.
A direct sum of such algebras
is exactly the algebra corresponding to
the limiting case of noninteracting modes, where all the matrices that enter
Eq. (
18
) are diagonal
(
\{\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(0)}_{nn},\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(+)}_{nn},\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(-)}_{nn}\}

are the generators of
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
Lie algebra).
However, the effects of intermode couplings cannot generally be neglected, and thus the
matrices
\Omega
and
\Gamma
are not necessarily diagonal
(both are Hermitian and
\Gamma>0
). So, we focus our attention on this general case.

III
Spectral problem

In this section, we shall use the above algebraic relations
to solve the spectral problem for the Liouvillian (
6
).
Our analysis involves two steps:
(a) applying a transformation that casts the Liouvillian
into the diagonalized form which is solely expressed in terms of the
effective non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
and does not contain
terms proportional to the jump superoperators
\hat{\mathcal{K}}_{nm}^{(\pm)}
;
and (b) simplifying the effective Hamiltonian
so as to eliminate the inter-mode interactions.

III.1
Jump-eliminating transformations

Similarly to Ref.
[
32
]
,
we begin with algebraic identities for exponentiated
superoperators acting by similarity (adjoint action).
By using the commutation relations (
22
),
we have

\displaystyle e^{\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\pm)}_{B}}\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{(-)}_{A}e^{-\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\pm)}_{B}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{(-)}_{A}-\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\pm)}_{[A,B]},

(23a)

\displaystyle e^{\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\pm)}_{B}}\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(0)}_{A}e^{-\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\pm)}_{B}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(0)}_{A}\mp\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\pm)}_{\frac{1}{2}\{A,B\}},

(23b)

\displaystyle e^{\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\pm)}_{B}}\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\mp)}_{A}e^{-\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\pm)}_{B}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\mp)}_{A}\mp\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(0)}_{\{A,B\}}

\displaystyle+\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{(-)}_{\frac{1}{2}[A,B]}+\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\pm)}_{BAB}.

(23c)

From these identities,
the transformations determined by the jump operators,

\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\pm)}_{B}
,
modify a linear combination
of superoperators that enter the Liouvillian (
18
)
as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}=-i\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{(-)}_{\Omega}+2\sum_{\alpha\in\{0,\pm\}}\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\alpha)}_{\Gamma_{\alpha}}+\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\Gamma\,\hat{\mathcal{I}}

\displaystyle\mapsto\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}=-i\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{(-)}_{\Omega^{\prime}}+2\sum_{\alpha\in\{0,\pm\}}\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\alpha)}_{\Gamma^{\prime}_{\alpha}}+\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\Gamma\,\hat{\mathcal{I}}

\displaystyle=e^{\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\nu)}_{B}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}e^{-\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(\nu)}_{B}},\quad\nu\in\{+,-\},

(24a)

\displaystyle\Omega^{\prime}=\Omega+i[\Gamma_{-\nu},B],\quad\Gamma_{0}^{\prime}=\Gamma_{0}-\nu\{\Gamma_{-\nu},B\},

(24b)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\nu}^{\prime}=\Gamma_{\nu}+\frac{i}{2}[\Omega,B]-\frac{\nu}{2}\{\Gamma_{0},B\}+B\Gamma_{-\nu}B,

(24c)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{-\nu}^{\prime}=\Gamma_{-\nu},

(24d)

where the special case of thermal bath Liouvillians
is described by
the superoperator (
18
)
with
the matrices
\Gamma_{\alpha}=\gamma_{\alpha}\Gamma

and the coefficients
\gamma_{\alpha}
given
by Eqs. (
4
) and (
7
).

Now it is our task to
simplify the Liouvillian by eliminating
contributions coming from jump operators
with the help of compositions of
the above similarity transformations (
24a
).
These compositions are determined by
two jump superoperators:

\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(+)}_{B_{+}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(-)}_{B_{-}}
,
and one has to find the matrices,
B_{+}
and
B_{-}
, such that,
in the transformed Liouvillian

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{T}}\hat{\mathcal{L}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{-1}=

\displaystyle-i\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{(-)}_{\Omega^{\prime}}+2\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(0)}_{\Gamma_{0}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\Gamma\,\hat{\mathcal{I}}\equiv\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{d},\quad\Gamma_{0}^{\prime}<0

(25)

both matrices associated with
\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(+)}
and

\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(-)}
vanish.
An important point is that the operator of evolution

\mathrm{e}^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{d}t}
is required to be bounded from above at
t\geq 0
.
This places the above constraint
\Gamma_{0}^{\prime}<0
on
the relaxation matrix to ensure that
real parts of eigenvalues of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{d}
are nonpositive.

From Eq. (
24c
),
the conditions
\Gamma_{\pm}^{\prime}=0

will require solving algebraic Riccati equations
that, in general, do not have closed-form solutions.
Theoretical considerations detailed in Appendix
A

show that, in our case, the Riccati equations can be treated
analytically. In what follows, we will utilize
the results of Appendix
A
to derive
both the jump-eliminating transformations and the effective Hamiltonian.

We begin with the transformation of the form

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{+-}(A_{+},A_{-})=e^{\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(+)}_{A_{+}}}e^{\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(-)}_{A_{-}}}.

(26)

This is the case where
the condition
\Gamma_{-}^{\prime}=0

leads to the Riccati equation (
108
)
with
\nu=-1

and its solution can be taken in
the simplest form

A_{-}=I
(see Eq. (
110
)).
Then the resulting effective non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
with
\Gamma_{0}^{\prime}=-\Gamma

is given by (see Eq. (
112
))

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{d}=-i(\overleftarrow{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}}-\overrightarrow{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\dagger}})=\overleftarrow{\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}}+\overrightarrow{\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\dagger}},

(27)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\sum_{nm}(\Omega-i\Gamma)_{nm}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{n}\hat{a}_{m}

\displaystyle=\hat{J}_{H},\quad H=\Omega-i\Gamma,

(28)

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}=-i\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\hat{J}_{L},\quad L=-i\Omega-\Gamma.

(29)

Since
\Gamma_{-}^{\prime}=0
,
the jump-eliminating condition for the remaining recycling term
leads to a linear equation for the matrix
A_{+}

of the form (
115
)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{+}+\frac{i}{2}[\Omega^{\prime},A_{+}]-\frac{1}{2}\{\Gamma_{0}^{\prime},A_{+}\}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{+}-(LA_{+}+A_{+}{L}^{\dagger})/2=0.

(30)

According to Appendix
A

(see Eqs. (
117
)– (
121
))
its solution reads

\displaystyle A_{+}=-W_{+}=-2\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{L\tau}\Gamma_{+}e^{{L}^{\dagger}\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau.

(31)

An important point is that the matrix
W_{+}
is closely related to
the steady-state (equilibrium) density matrix

\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{ss}}

(
\hat{\mathcal{L}}\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{ss}}=0
).
More precisely,
it can be shown
[
84
]

that
the steady-state normally-ordered characteristic function
is Gaussian

\chi_{\mathrm{ss}}(\bm{\alpha})=\exp\{-\sum_{m,n}W_{mn}^{(+)}{\alpha}^{\ast}_{m}\alpha_{n}\}

and the corresponding steady-state statistical operator is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{ss}}=\frac{\exp\{-\hat{J}_{V_{\mathrm{ss}}}\}}{\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\exp\{-\hat{J}_{V_{\mathrm{ss}}}\}},\quad e^{V_{\mathrm{ss}}}=(W_{+})^{-1}+I.

(32)

For the differently ordered transformation

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{-+}(B_{-},B_{+})=e^{\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(-)}_{B_{-}}}e^{\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(+)}_{B_{+}}}

(33)

we cannot use Eq. (
113
) because, at
\nu=1
,
the relaxation matrix
\Gamma_{0}^{\prime}
(see Eq. (
111
))
is no longer negative definite.
In this case,
the properly defined effective Hamiltonian
can be obtained using
the solution given by Eq. (
122
).
By using relation (
123
),
similar to Eq. (
31
),
the matrix
B_{+}

\displaystyle B_{+}=(W_{-})^{-1}-I=-((W_{+})^{-1}+I)^{-1}=-e^{-V_{\mathrm{ss}}}

(34)

can be expressed in terms of
W_{+}
,
whereas equation for the matrix
B_{-}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{-}+\frac{i}{2}[\Omega^{\prime},B_{-}]+\frac{1}{2}\{\Gamma_{0}^{\prime},B_{-}\}=0

(35)

can be treated using
the method developed in Appendix
A

for solving Eq. (
115
).

We can now apply the above general results
to thermal bath Liouvillians with
the relaxation matrices given by Eq. (
4
).
This is the special case of our primary concern
that was briefly discussed at the end of Appendix
A
.
In this case, the matrices
W_{+}
and
W_{-}

are proportinal to the unity matrix:

W_{+}=n_{T}I
and
W_{-}=(n_{T}+1)I
.
As a result, a similar remark applies to the matrices

A_{\pm}
and
B_{\pm}
:

A_{\pm}=\alpha_{\pm}I
and
B_{\pm}=\beta_{\pm}I
.
So, the differently ordered jump-eliminating
transformations can be written in the following simplified form:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{+-}(\alpha_{+},\alpha_{-})\equiv\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{+-}=e^{\alpha_{+}\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(+)}_{I}}e^{\alpha_{-}\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(-)}_{I}},

(36)

\displaystyle\alpha_{+}=-n_{T},\quad\alpha_{-}=1,

(37)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{-+}(\beta_{-},\beta_{+})\equiv\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{-+}=e^{\beta_{-}\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(-)}_{I}}e^{\beta_{+}\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(+)}_{I}},

(38)

\displaystyle\beta_{+}=-e^{-z_{T}},\quad\beta_{-}=n_{T}+1=\frac{1}{1-e^{-z_{T}}}\equiv Z

(39)

and
the resulting diagonalized Liouvillian (
27
) is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{d}=\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{+-}(-n_{T},1)\hat{\mathcal{L}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{+-}^{-1}(-n_{T},1)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{-+}(Z,-e^{-z_{T}})\hat{\mathcal{L}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{-+}^{-1}(Z,-e^{-z_{T}})

\displaystyle=\overleftarrow{\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}}+\overrightarrow{\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\dagger}},\quad\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}=-\hat{J}_{i\Omega+\Gamma}.

(40)

In contrast to
the Hamiltonian (
8
)
that
depends on temperature
and determines the so-called semiclassical limit
where the action of quantum jumps is neglected
[
46
]
,
the effective Hamiltonian (
28
)
is related to the diagonalized Liouvillian (
40
)
and is temperature independent
(it does not depend on
n_{T}
).
Interestingly, in the zero-temperature limit
with
n_{T}=0
, the difference between the Hamiltonians vanishes.

For
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}
, we have the relation

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{d}^{\sharp}=\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{-+}(n_{T},-1)\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{-+}^{-1}(n_{T},-1)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{+-}(-Z,e^{-z_{T}})\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{+-}^{-1}(-Z,e^{-z_{T}})

\displaystyle=-i(\overrightarrow{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}}-\overleftarrow{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\dagger}})=\overrightarrow{\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}}+\overleftarrow{\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\dagger}}

(41)

that can be readily obtained from the conjugated versions of
Eqs. (
40
) and (
27
)
with the help of identities

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{\pm\mp}^{-1}(\alpha_{\pm},\alpha_{\mp})=\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{\mp\pm}(-\alpha_{\mp},-\alpha_{\pm}),

(42)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{\pm\mp}^{\sharp}(\alpha_{\pm},\alpha_{\mp})=\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{\pm\mp}(\alpha_{\mp},\alpha_{\pm}).

(43)

III.2
Spectrum, eigenoperators and superpropagators

Owing to Eq. (
40
),
the spectral problem for the Liouvillian (
18
)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}\hat{\rho}_{\lambda}=\lambda\hat{\rho}_{\lambda},\quad\lambda\in\mathrm{spec}(\hat{\mathcal{L}})

(44)

is directly related to the eigenvalue problem
for its diagonalized counterpart (
27
)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{d}\,\hat{\rho}_{\lambda}^{(d)}=\lambda\hat{\rho}_{\lambda}^{(d)}.

(45)

that can be easily solved provided the spectrum and eigenstates
of the effective Hamiltonian (
28
) are known.
More specifically,
given the eigenstates,
\left|\mu\right>
,
and eigenvalues,
\mu
,
of
\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}=-i\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}

with
\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}\left|\mu\right>=\mu\left|\mu\right>
,
the relations

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\lambda}^{(d)}=\left|\mu\right>\left<\nu\right|,\quad\lambda=\mu+{\nu}^{\ast}

(46)

where the asterisk denotes complex conjugation,
give the eigenmode
\hat{\rho}_{\lambda}^{(d)}

and the corresponding eigenvalue.
In what follows, we will often use the operator

\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}=-i\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}

(see Eq. (
29
)) as a convenient substitute for
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
.

We can now express the eigenoperator
\rho_{\lambda}
in terms of

\rho_{\lambda}^{(d)}
using the superoperators

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{+-}^{-1}\equiv\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{+-}^{-1}(-n_{T},1)=\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{-+}(-1,n_{T}),

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{-+}^{-1}\equiv\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{-+}^{-1}(Z,-e^{-z_{T}})=\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{+-}(e^{-z_{T}},-Z)

(47)

that enter Eq. (
40
) as follows

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\lambda}=\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{-+}^{-1}\hat{\rho}_{\lambda}^{(d)}=q_{\lambda}\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{+-}^{-1}\hat{\rho}_{\lambda}^{(d)},

(48)

where
q_{\lambda}
is the coefficient of proportionality
takes into account the difference between images of

\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{-+}^{-1}
and
\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{+-}^{-1}
that both belong to the one-dimensional
eigenspace.
Thus, the spectrum,
\mathrm{spec}(\hat{\mathcal{L}})
,
and the eigenoperators,
\hat{\rho}_{\lambda}
, are
governed by the spectral properties of the effective Hamiltonian (
28
).

Similarly, for eigenoperators of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}

and
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}_{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda}=\lambda\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda},\quad\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{d}^{\sharp}\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda}^{(d)}=\lambda\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda}^{(d)},

(49)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda}=\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{+-}^{\sharp}\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda}^{(d)}.

(50)

Note that, when
the eigenmodes of the diagonalized Liouvillian and its conjugate
are normalized using
the biorthogonality condition

\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\{\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{(d)}\hat{\rho}_{\lambda}^{(d)}\}=\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda}
,
the biorthogonality relations
for
\hat{\rho}_{\lambda}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda^{\prime}}

given by

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\{\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda^{\prime}}\hat{\rho}_{\lambda}\}=\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\{\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{+-}^{\sharp}[\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{(d)}]\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{-+}^{-1}\hat{\rho}_{\lambda}^{(d)}\}

\displaystyle=q_{\lambda}\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\{\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{+-}^{\sharp}[\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda}^{(d)}]\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{+-}^{-1}\hat{\rho}_{\lambda}^{(d)}\}=q_{\lambda}\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda}

(51)

depend on the coefficient
q_{\lambda}
.

III.2.1
Single-mode spectral problem

In order to illustrate the basic steps of our method,
it is instructive
to consider the simplest single-mode case with
N=1
, where

\mathcal{K}^{(\pm)}_{I}\equiv\mathcal{K}^{(\pm)}
and

\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}=-(i\Omega+\Gamma)\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}
.

In this case,
the solution of the spectral problem
for the diagonalized Liouvillian
reads

\displaystyle\lambda_{mn}=-i\Omega(m-n)-\Gamma(m+n),

(52a)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\lambda}^{(d)}=\hat{\rho}_{mn}^{(d)}=\left|m\right>\left<n\right|\equiv\hat{\Pi}_{mn},

(52b)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda}^{(d)}={[\hat{\rho}_{mn}^{(d)}]}^{\dagger}=\hat{\Pi}_{nm}=\hat{\sigma}_{nm}^{(d)}

(52c)

whereas the eigenoperators

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{mn}=e^{e^{-z_{T}}\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(+)}}e^{-Z\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(-)}}\left|m\right>\left<n\right|,

(53)

can be evaluated using the algebraic identity

\displaystyle e^{-Z\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(-)}}\left|m\right>\left<n\right|=\sum_{k=0}^{\min(m,n)}(-Z)^{k}\sqrt{\binom{m}{k}\binom{n}{k}}

\displaystyle\times\left|m-k\right>\left<n-k\right|.

(54)

The result

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{mn}=\sum_{k=0}^{\min(m,n)}\frac{(-Z)^{k}\sqrt{m!n!}}{k!(m-k)!(n-k)!}(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{m-k}\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{a}^{n-k}

(55)

can be conveniently expressed in terms of
the steady state eigenoperator

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{0}=e^{e^{-z_{T}}\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(+)}}\left|0\right>\left<0\right|=e^{-z_{T}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}}=:e^{-Z^{-1}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}}:,

(56)

where
:\ldots:
stands for normal ordering,
proportional to the density matrix of the thermal equilibrium state:

\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{eq}}=Z^{-1}\hat{\rho}_{0}
.
The expression for the eigenoperator,

\hat{\sigma}_{\lambda}=\hat{\sigma}_{nm}
,
of the adjoint Liouvillian

\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}

with the eigenvalue

\lambda={\lambda}^{\ast}_{nm}=\lambda_{mn}

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{nm}=\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{+-}^{\sharp}(-n_{T},1)\hat{\Pi}_{nm}=\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{+-}(1,-n_{T})\hat{\Pi}_{nm}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\min(m,n)}\frac{(-n_{T})^{k}\sqrt{m!n!}}{k!(m-k)!(n-k)!}(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{n-k}\hat{a}^{m-k}

(57)

can be derived with the help of Eq. (
54
)
and the relation

\displaystyle e^{\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(+)}}\left|0\right>\left<0\right|=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left|n\right>\left<n\right|=\hat{I}.

(58)

We can now briefly discuss
how the solution of the spectral problem
can be used to describe the evolution of
the density matrix in terms of eigenmodes.
To this end, in Appendix
B
, we have derived
the biorthogonality relations for the eigenoperators (
55
)
and (
57
) (see Eq. (
139
))

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{\sigma}_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\hat{\rho}_{mn}\}=q_{mn}\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}},\quad q_{mn}=Z^{m+n+1}.

(59)

So, the eigenmode expansion describing dynamics
of the density matrix takes the following form:

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(t)=\sum_{m,n=0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{\lambda_{mn}t}}{q_{mn}}\hat{\rho}_{mn}\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{\sigma}_{nm}\hat{\rho}(0)\}.

(60)

III.2.2
Multi-mode spectral problem

By contrast to the single-mode case,
solving the
spectral problem for the diagonalized
multimode Liouvillian (
27
) requires additional considerations
due to intermode interactions that complicate
the effective Hamiltonian (
28
) so that
the associated matrix
H
is non-diagonal.
We shall apply the algebraic relations

\displaystyle e^{\overleftarrow{\hat{V}_{+}}+\overrightarrow{\hat{V}_{-}}}\{\overleftarrow{\hat{H}_{+}}+\overrightarrow{\hat{H}_{-}}\}e^{-\overleftarrow{\hat{V}_{+}}-\overrightarrow{\hat{V}_{-}}}=\overleftarrow{\hat{H}_{+}^{\prime}}+\overrightarrow{\hat{H}_{-}^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\pm}^{\prime}=e^{\pm\hat{V}_{\pm}}\hat{H}_{\pm}e^{\mp\hat{V}_{\pm}}

(61)

to the special case with

\hat{H}_{+}=\hat{H}_{-}^{\dagger}=\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\hat{J}_{L}
,
so as to find the operator

\hat{V}_{+}=\hat{V}_{-}^{\dagger}\equiv\hat{V}=\hat{J}_{V}

that transforms the Hamiltonian into the diagonal form
(see Eq. (
11a
)):

\displaystyle e^{\hat{V}}\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}e^{-\hat{V}}=e^{\hat{J}_{V}}\hat{J}_{L}e^{-\hat{J}_{V}}=\hat{J}_{L_{d}}\equiv\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(d)},

\displaystyle L_{d}=e^{V}Le^{-V}=\mathrm{diag}(L^{(d)}_{1},\ldots,L^{(d)}_{N}).

(62)

The resulting diagonalized effective Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(d)}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}L^{(d)}_{k}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{a}_{k}=-\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Bigl(i\Omega^{(d)}_{k}+\Gamma^{(d)}_{k}\Bigr)\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{a}_{k},

(63)

where
\Omega^{(d)}_{k}=-\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits L_{k}^{(d)}

and

\Gamma^{(d)}_{k}=-\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits L_{k}^{(d)}
.
Note that, for creation operators, the corresponding
mapping is given by Eq. (
11b
).

For the diagonalized Liouvillian superoperator

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(d)}=\overleftarrow{\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(d)}}+\overrightarrow{{[\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(d)}]}^{\dagger}}

(64)

governed by the effective Hamiltonian (
63
),
eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenoperators of
are given by

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathbf{mn}}=-\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Bigl\{i\Omega^{(d)}_{k}(m_{k}-n_{k})

\displaystyle+\Gamma^{(d)}_{k}(m_{k}+n_{k})\Bigr\},\quad m_{k},n_{k}\in\mathbb{N},

(65)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\lambda}^{(d)}=\hat{\rho}_{\mathbf{mn}}^{(d)}=e^{-\hat{V}}\hat{\Pi}_{\mathbf{mn}}e^{-\hat{V}^{\dagger}},\>\hat{\Pi}_{\mathbf{mn}}=\left|\mathbf{m}\right>\left<\mathbf{n}\right|

(66)

where

\mathbf{m}\equiv(m_{1},\ldots m_{N})

(
\mathbf{n}\equiv(n_{1},\ldots n_{N})
)
is the multi-index
that labels
is the multimode Fock ket(bra)-state

\left|\mathbf{m}\right>=\left|m_{1}\right>\otimes\ldots\otimes\left|m_{N}\right>

(
\left<\mathbf{n}\right|=\left<n_{1}\right|\otimes\ldots\otimes\left<n_{N}\right|
).

The matrix density eigenoperators given
by Eqs. (
53
)
and (
57
)
that enter the expansion (
60
)
are generalized as follows

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\mathbf{mn}}=e^{e^{-z_{T}}\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(+)}_{I}}e^{-Z\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(-)}_{I}}e^{-\hat{V}}\hat{\Pi}_{\mathbf{mn}}e^{-\hat{V}^{\dagger}},

(67)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{nm}}=e^{\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(+)}_{I}}e^{-n_{T}\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(-)}_{I}}e^{\hat{V}^{\dagger}}\hat{\Pi}_{\mathbf{nm}}e^{\hat{V}}

(68)

so that it is rather straightforward to write down
the multimode version of Eq. (
60
):

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(t)=\sum_{\mathbf{m,n}}\frac{e^{\lambda_{\mathbf{mn}}t}}{q_{\mathbf{mn}}}\hat{\rho}_{\mathbf{mn}}\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{nm}}\hat{\rho}(0)\},

(69)

\displaystyle q_{\mathbf{mn}}=\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{nm}}\hat{\rho}_{\mathbf{mn}}\}.

(70)

Alternatively,
dynamics of the density matrix
can be described using the relations

\displaystyle e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{d}t}\left|\mathbf{m}\right>\left<\mathbf{n}\right|=e^{\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}t}\left|\mathbf{m}\right>\left<\mathbf{n}\right|e^{\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\dagger}t}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{(\hat{b}_{i}^{\dagger})^{m_{i}}}{\sqrt{m_{i}!}}\left|\mathbf{0}\right>\left<\mathbf{0}\right|\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\hat{b}_{i}^{n_{i}}}{\sqrt{n_{i}!}},

(71)

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{i}^{\dagger}=e^{\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}t}\hat{a}_{i}^{\dagger}e^{-\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}t}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}P_{ji}(t)\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger},

(72)

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}P_{ji}^{*}(t)\hat{a}_{j},\quad P(t)=e^{Lt}=e^{-(i\Omega+\Gamma/2)t}

(73)

in terms of the operators

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\mathbf{mn}}^{(1)}=e^{e^{-z_{T}}\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(+)}_{I}}e^{-Z\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(-)}_{I}}\hat{\Pi}_{\mathbf{mn}}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\rho}_{m_{i}n_{i}},

(74)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{nm}}^{(1)}=e^{\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(+)}_{I}}e^{-n_{T}\hat{\mathcal{K}}^{(-)}_{I}}\hat{\Pi}_{\mathbf{nm}}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\sigma}_{n_{i}m_{i}},

(75)

where
\hat{\rho}_{m_{i}n_{i}}

and
\hat{\sigma}_{n_{i}m_{i}}

are given by Eq. (
55
) and Eq. (
57
),
respectively,
and the matrix elements

\displaystyle U_{\mathbf{m^{\prime}m}}(t)=\left<\mathbf{m^{\prime}}\right|e^{\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}t}\left|\mathbf{m}\right>

(76)

that can be evaluated using Eq. (
72
).

So, the dynamics of our system
is governed by
the action of the superpropagator

e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}t}
on
the statistical operator
at the initial instant of time,
expressed in terms of
the products of single-mode operators
as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(t)=e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}t}\hat{\rho}(0)=\sum_{\mathbf{m,n,m^{\prime},n^{\prime}}}\frac{U_{\mathbf{m^{\prime}m}}(t)U_{\mathbf{n^{\prime}n}}^{*}(t)}{q_{\mathbf{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}}^{(1)}}

\displaystyle\times\hat{\rho}_{\mathbf{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}}^{(1)}\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{nm}}^{(1)}\hat{\rho}(0)\},

(77)

\displaystyle q_{\mathbf{mn}}^{(1)}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}q_{m_{i}n_{i}},

(78)

where

q_{m_{i}n_{i}}=\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{\sigma}_{n_{i}m_{i}}\hat{\rho}_{m_{i}n_{i}}\}=Z^{n_{i}+m_{i}+1}
.

An important point to be emphasized is that,
in contrast to Eqs. (
63

IV
Applications: Exceptional points and speed of evolution

The results of the previous section
demonstrate that our method
can be used as an efficient tool to
perform a comprehensive analysis of the
spectral properties of
the multi-mode bosonic Liouvillians (
3
).
As discussed in Sec.
I
,
such analysis will be of crucial importance for understanding
physical phenomena near exceptional points.
More generally, the eigenspectrum and the eigenoperators
are intimately related to
the intermode couplings-induced regimes of the Lindblad dynamics.
One of the natural ways to deal with these regimes
is to study how the inter-mode interactions
influence the speed of evolution
underlying the concept of the quantum speed
limit (QSL) time.

The latter is
defined as the minimal time needed for a system to perform a transition
between predetermined initial and final (target) states.
Quantum mechanics is known to dictate QSL bounds for the minimal evolution time,
which is inversely related to the evolution speed.
For the case of unitary evolution, the well-known
Mandelstam-Tamm
[
97
,
98
]
and
Margolus-Levitin
[
99
,
100
,
101
]
inequalities
provide general limits on the speed of dynamical evolution
and set a dynamical intrinsic time scale.
These inequalities have been extended to
the case of nonunitary dynamics of open quantum systems
using the geometric approach where
quantum speed limits are derived as upper bounds on the rate of
change of geometric measures of distinguishability
such as the relative purity
[
102
]
,
the Bures angle
[
103
]
,
the quantum Fisher information
[
104
]

(see also unifying considerations
presented in Refs.
[
105
,
106
]
).

It is rather straightforward to illustrate the basic steps of the
geometric approach assuming that the initial state is pure,

\hat{\rho}(0)=\left|\psi_{0}\right>\left<\psi_{0}\right|
,
and the measure of distinguishability is determined by
the Uhlmann fidelity.
In this case, we can use
the simplified expression for the fidelity

\displaystyle F(\hat{\rho}(0),\hat{\rho}(t))=\left<\psi_{0}\right|\hat{\rho}(t)\left|\psi_{0}\right>\equiv F(t)

(79)

and, with the help of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
show that the rate of change of the fidelity (
79
)

\displaystyle\Bigl|\frac{\partial F(t)}{\partial t}\Bigr|\leq v(t)

(80)

is bounded from above by the speed of evolution
defined using
the Hilbert-Schmidt metric
[
107
,
108
,
109
]
:

\displaystyle v(t)=\sqrt{\mathop{\rm Tr}\nolimits\Bigl[\frac{\partial\hat{\rho}(t)}{\partial t}\Bigr]^{2}}\equiv\Bigl\|\frac{\partial\hat{\rho}(t)}{\partial t}\Bigr\|_{2}.

(81)

From Eq. (
80
), we have the QSL inequality

\displaystyle t\geq t_{F}(t)=\frac{1-F(t)}{\langle{v}\rangle_{t}},\quad\langle{v}\rangle_{t}\equiv\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}v(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau

(82)

for the fidelity-based time-dependent QSL time
t_{F}
.
Note that similar reasoning is directly applicable to the case where
the geometric measure of distinguishability
is given by
the Hilbert-Schmidt distance

\|\hat{\rho}(t)-\hat{\rho}(0)\|_{2}
between the initial and
evolved states.

The literature on quantum speed limits is quite voluminous,
and more details on QSLs and their applications in the fields such as
quantum metrology and optimal control theory can be found
in, e.g., Ref.
[
110
]
.
The study of QSLs is one of the hot areas in current research:
the limits for time-resolved sensing of dynamical signals using qubit
probes are found be closely related to the QSL
[
111
]
;
bounds on the speed limit of quantum
evolution by unitary operators in arbitrary dimensions are established
in
[
112
]
;
an attainable quantum speed limit based
on the variation of quantum coherence
using the skew information as the coherence measure
was obtained in
[
113
]
;
the experimental verification to the
QSLs in various scenarios with a superconducting circuit
is presented in
[
114
]
.

For two-level open quantum systems that interact with
Markovian and non-Markovian environments,
the problem of evolution speed has been the subject of intense
studies
[
115
,
107
,
116
,
117
,
106
]
. A
systematic analysis of the most common quantum speed limit (QSL) bounds in the damped Jaynes-Cummings model was performed
in
[
118
]
. The problem of quantum metrology in the context of non-Markovian quantum
evolution of a two-qubit system was explored
in
[
119
]
.
Geometric quantum speed limits of a qubit subject to decoherence in an ensemble of
chloroform molecules were recently studied in a
nuclear magnetic resonance experiment
[
120
]
.

Compared to the case of finite-dimensional quantum systems,
the evolution speed of
bosonic multi-mode quantum systems representing a family of
open continuous-variable systems
has received much less attention
[
108
,
109
,
121
]
.
So, in this section, we show how the algebraic tools
developed in the previous sections
can be efficiently utilized to explore the problem
of the speed of evolution of
quantum polarization states
represented by bimodal bosonic systems
[
122
]
.

Lindblad dynamics of such systems can be regarded as a model
describing propagation of quantized polarization modes in an optical
fiber
[
33
]
.
In this model,
the intermode coherent (incoherent) interactions
originate from the effects of
the birefringence (the dichroism or polarization-dependent losses).
In Sec.
IV.1
, we shall use
the frequency and relaxation vectors
to encode channel anisotropy parameters
characterizing the effective Hamiltonian.
In the parameter space determined by these vectors,
we deduce the conditions that govern the geometry of the exceptional points.
In Sec.
IV.2
,
by assuming that the mean number of photons,
n_{T}
,
is small,
approximate formulas for the Liouvillian and the evolution superpropagator
describing the low-temperature regime
in the first-order (linear) approximation
are employed to perform numerical analysis of
the evolution speed of
the one-photon polarization states known as
the polarization qubit.

IV.1

The effective Hamiltonian (

\displaystyle H={\Omega}-i\Gamma=\sum_{k=0}^{3}(\omega_{k}-i\gamma_{k}){\sigma}_{k}=(\omega_{0}-i\gamma_{0}){\sigma}_{0}

\displaystyle+(\bm{\omega}-i\bm{\gamma},\bm{\sigma}),\quad{\sigma}_{0}\equiv\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(1,1),

(83)

\displaystyle{\sigma}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\

\bm{\omega}\equiv(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3})

\bm{\gamma}\equiv(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3})

\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}=\gamma(\sin\theta_{\Gamma}\cos\phi_{\Gamma},\sin\theta_{\Gamma}\sin\phi_{\Gamma},\cos\theta_{\Gamma})=\gamma\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma},

\displaystyle\bm{\omega}=\omega(\sin\theta_{\Omega}\cos\phi_{\Omega},\sin\theta_{\Omega}\sin\phi_{\Omega},\cos\theta_{\Omega})=\omega\mathbf{n}_{\Omega}.

It is rather straightforward to obtain
the matrix exponential for
{P}(t)

(see Eq. (

\displaystyle{P}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-iHt}=\mathrm{e}^{-(i\omega_{0}+\gamma_{0})t}\Bigl\{\cosh(qt){\sigma}_{0}

\displaystyle-\frac{\sinh(qt)}{q}(\bm{\gamma}+i\bm{\omega},\bm{\sigma})\Bigr\},

(86)

where

\displaystyle q=\sqrt{(\bm{\gamma}+i\bm{\omega},\bm{\gamma}+i\bm{\omega})}=\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{3}(\gamma_{k}+i\omega_{k})^{2}}.

(87)

From Eq. (
86

that exceptional points require
the rate parameter

given by Eq. (

to vanish,

Geometrically, this EP condition occurs when
the vectors
\bm{\gamma}
and
\bm{\omega}

are mutually orthogonal,
\bm{\gamma}\perp\bm{\omega}
,
and are identical in length,
|\bm{\gamma}|=|\bm{\omega}|
:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma},\mathbf{n}_{\Omega})=0,\quad\gamma=\omega.

(88)

At EP,
the expression for the matrix exponential (
86

\displaystyle{P}(t)|_{q=0}=\mathrm{e}^{-(i\omega_{0}+\gamma_{0})t}\Bigl\{{\sigma}_{0}-\gamma t(\mathbf{n}_{\Gamma}+i\mathbf{n}_{\Omega},\bm{\sigma})\Bigr\}

(89)

shows that a rapidly varying combination of exponential functions
appear to be
replaced with a function linearly dependent on time.

V
Conclusions and discussion

In this paper, we have studied the Lindblad dynamics of
multimode bosonic systems
governed by the GKSL equation of the form (
3
)
with relations (
4
) describing systems
interacting with the thermal environment.
This thermal bath case involves both the coherent (dynamical) and incoherent (environment-mediated)
intermode couplings represented by the frequency and relaxation matrices:

\Omega
and
\Gamma
, respectively.
At the heart of our general approach to dynamics is the Lie algebra of
quadratic combinations of the left and right
superoperators (see Eqs. (
5a
)–(
5c
)),

\{\overleftarrow{\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}},\overleftarrow{\hat{a}_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{N}

and

\{\overrightarrow{\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}},\overrightarrow{\hat{a}_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
,
with the commutation relations given by Eq. (
21
).

We have found that
introducing the superoperators associated with matrices
(see Sec.
II.3
and the commutation relations (
22
))
leads to a natural representation of the algebraic structure of the Liouvillian
(see Eq. (
18
))
and significantly simplifies analysis of this algebra.
This step can be regarded as an extension of the well-known
Jordan-Schwinger approach outlined in Sec.
II.2

to the case of superoperators.

We have derived the algebraic identities (
23
)
which are utilized
for computing the differently ordered
jump-eliminating transformations
(see Eqs. (
26
) and (
33
)).
that recast the Liouvillian (
18
) into the diagonalized
form (
25
), where contributions coming from the jump
superoperators (recycling terms) are eliminated.
These transformations are expressed in terms of solutions
to the algebraic Ricatti equations which are analyzed in the
Appendix
A
.
It turns out that these solutions essentially depend on
the matrix (
31
) closely related to the algebraic
structure of the steady state statistical operator
(see Eq. (
32
)).

As a result,
in the limiting case of thermal bath Liouvillians (
4
),
the jump-eliminating
transformations take the simplified form
of the superoperators
given by Eqs. (
36
)
and (
38
)
with the matrices proportional to unity matrix.
In this case,
the transformed Liouvillian,
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
,
and its conjugate,
\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{\sharp}
,
defined using relation (
19
)
are given by formulas (
40
)
and (
41
), respectively.

Our general result is that
the diagonalized Liouvillian (
27
) is completely determined
by the effective non-Hermitian Hamiltonian (
28
),

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
,
associated with the matrix
H=\Omega-i\Gamma

through the Jordan mapping (
9
).
In contrast to the approximate semiclassical Hamiltonian (
8
),
the effective Hamiltonian is temperature independent,
and the associated matrix

H
governs both the spectral properties of the Liouvillian and
Lindblad dynamics regimes.
In particular, at Liouvillian exceptional points,
this matrix cannot be diagonalized.

As an immediate consequence of the jump-eliminating procedure,
the spectral problem for the Liouvillian
and its conjugate can be analytically treated by expressing
the spectrum and eigenoperators in terms of
the eigenvalues and the eigenstates of the operator
proportional to the Hamiltonian,
\hat{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}=-i\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}

(see Eqs. (
44
)–(
51
)).
Efficiency of the method is demonstrated
by solving the spectral problem
(see Eqs. (
52
)– (
58
))
and deriving the eigenmode decomposition
of the matrix density evolved in time
(see Eq. (
60
))
for the single-mode Liouvillian closely related to the case of
noninteracting modes.
Note that
in Appendix
B
,
we have employed
the method of generating operators
as a tool that considerably simplifies
the derivation of the biorthogonality relations.

For the case where the matrix
H
is diagonalizable,
we have shown how to diagonalize
the effective Hamiltonian, solve the spectral problem,
and obtain the eigenmode expansion for
\hat{\rho}(t)

(see Eqs. (
61

70

We have also derived an alternative representation
for
\hat{\rho}(t)
that remains valid even if the matrix
H

is non-diagonalizable (see Eqs. (
71
)–(
70

The latter is the case where
a Liouvillian exceptional point comes into play.

In addition to the above spectral analysis,
we have shown how our algebraic technique
applied to bimodal bosonic systems
can be used to study
the intermode coupling-induced regimes
of the Lindblad dynamics of photonic polarization modes
and the related problem of the speed of evolution.
In this case, the matrices

and
\Gamma

expressed in terms of the Pauli matrices

\bm{\omega}
and
\bm{\gamma}

(see Eq. (

giving

\lambda_{\pm}=i\omega_{0}+\gamma_{0}\pm q

The formula (

is used to deduce two relations (

the geometry of
\bm{\omega}
and
\bm{\gamma}

[

A more complicated example of

.

In order to study the speed of evolution

In addition, our general results for

provide a useful tool for
the analysis of generalized

For linearly polarized qubit (

In Fig.

The peculiarity of the low-temperature approximation
with the Liouvillian and the superpropagator
expanded as power series in

is that
the corresponding approximate superoperators
acting on density matrices with finite-dimensional support
in the Fock basis will produce the operators whose support
is also finite-dimensional.
Thus, by using this approximation,
theoretical treatment of the Lindblad dynamics of
such states that
exemplify
extremes opposite to the well-known Gaussian states
can be performed within the realm of finite-dimensional models.

Our results are applicable to bosonic systems with an arbitrary number of modes,
so it is rather straightforward to
go beyond the limitations of the two-mode system
by taking into consideration additional modes.
For instance, such generalizations will be useful
when studying the dynamics of light
in systems such as twisted fibers
[

and open resonators with overlapping

[

This framework could
also be useful to study measurement-induced phase transitions

Among a wide range of potential applications,

computing multiple-time correlation functions using

(a recent discussion of related techniques can be found in Refs.

Our final remark is that, according to Appendix

the thermal bath constraints (

imposed on the relaxation matrices can be relaxed.
This opens up an opportunity to explore consequences
of applicability of Lindblad dynamics modeling to a wide range of processes.

Appendix A

For

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}^{\prime}=\Gamma_{0}-\nu\{\Gamma_{-\nu},A_{\nu}\}.

Since

\displaystyle\frac{i}{2}[\Omega,X_{\nu}]-\frac{1}{2}\{\Gamma,X_{\nu}\}+X_{\nu}\Gamma_{-\nu}X_{\nu}=0

\displaystyle\{\Gamma_{\nu},W_{\nu}^{-1}\}=-\frac{1}{2}\{LW_{\nu}+W_{\nu}{L}^{\dagger},W_{\nu}^{-1}\}
