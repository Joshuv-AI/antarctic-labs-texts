The role of tensor fascia lata (TFL) graft in wound treatment: a case report
Authors: Amin M. Yassin, Momen Mohamed, Mohamed Dirar, Abdelrhman Hamadnaalla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Combat-related injuries to the upper limb pose significant reconstructive challenges, particularly in resource-limited, conflict-zone settings. Innovative strategies are essential to preserve hand function, and the choice of an optimal reconstruction remains complex. Temporary wound dressings must foster healing and protect vital structures during the interval before definitive closure. This report evaluates the effectiveness of a tensor fascia lata (TFL) graft combined with negative-pressure wound therapy (NPWT) as a temporary dressing for complex wounds in austere environments.Case presentation:A 32-year-old right-hand-dominant soldier suffered shrapnel trauma to his left distal forearm, causing transection of the flexor tendons to the middle, ring, and little fingers, as well as the ulnar nerve and artery. Surgical exploration and repair were performed, after which a full-thickness TFL graft was applied as a dermal matrix substitute, in conjunction with intermittent NPWT at −100 mmHg. By the third week, robust granulation tissue was evident, allowing for successful skin grafting instead of flap reconstruction. At 7 weeks, the patient achieved more than 90 degrees of finger flexion with a QuickDASH score of 15.9%, indicating mild disability.Clinical discussion:The TFL graft’s collagen-rich extracellular matrix provides mechanical integrity and supports tissue hydration, angiogenesis, and tendon gliding. Combined with NPWT, the graft offers a vascularized scaffold, while NPWT evacuates fluids and promotes tissue approximation. This combination may substitute for commercial acellular dermal matrices at a substantially lower cost.Conclusion:TFL grafts, especially when integrated with NPWT, represent a robust, versatile, and practical solution for temporary wound coverage in complex injuries, particularly in resource-constrained settings.

Introduction

Upper limb trauma is frequently encountered in plastic surgery emergencies, with an increasing incidence due to global conflicts. Modern weaponry causes complex wounds that require advanced surgical approaches, including staged debridement, skeletal support, and revascularization. Early reconstruction benefits rehabilitation but is often delayed to minimize complications. Temporary dressings maintain wound protection and support healing until closure. ADMs are increasingly used as bioactive scaffolds that support tissue regeneration and provide structural support, particularly for wounds with exposed critical structures. TFL grafts are a versatile alternative for soft-tissue reconstruction. NPWT promotes healing when used as a temporary dressing but requires a protective layer over vital structures[2–4].

This report describes the innovative use of an autologous TFL graft combined with NPWT as a temporary dressing for complex forearm injuries in a low-resource, conflict-zone setting. Without access to commercial dermal matrices or free tissue transfer, this technique serves as a practical alternative. Its novelty lies in using TFL as a temporary dermal substitute, applying this method in a resource-limited conflict zone, and combining TFL with NPWT – an approach that, to the best of our knowledge, has not been previously reported in the literature.

Case presentation

A 32-year-old right-handed soldier sustained a shrapnel injury to his left forearm, producing a wound measuring 10 × 10 cm2 with exposed flexor tendons and neurovascular structures. He was unable to flex his ulnar fingers, had absent sensation in the ulnar nerve distribution, and reported numbness in the median nerve territory. Initial assessment at a field facility showed no bony involvement and normal blood tests. The wound was contaminated with devitalized tissue and foreign debris, requiring specialized plastic surgery intervention (Fig. 1). The patient was advised about the need for multiple surgeries, including tissue grafts and a distant abdominal flap, since free flap procedures were unavailable. He declined the abdominal flap, leading to a revised plan using a TFL graft and NPWT.

Under general anesthesia, thorough debridement and copious irrigation were performed. Examination revealed severed flexor digitorum profundus and superficialis tendons of the middle, ring, and little fingers, as well as the palmaris longus and flexor carpi ulnaris. The ulnar nerve was transected; the median nerve was contused but intact. The ulnar artery was injured and ligated. The ulnar nerve was repaired with Prolene 6-0 and the tendons with 3-0 (Fig. 1). A full-thickness tensor fascia lata (TFL) graft was harvested from the ipsilateral thigh through a 15-cm lazy-S incision. The graft measured 10 × 10 cm2, matching the defect dimensions, with a thickness of approximately 2–3 mm. It was fixed with interrupted 4-0 nylon sutures circumferentially to the wound margins. The donor site was closed primarily with a closed-suction drain, which was removed on postoperative day 2. Multiple 2-mm longitudinal pores were created in the graft in a staggered grid pattern, 1 cm apart, to facilitate drainage. Negative-pressure wound therapy (NPWT) was then applied (Fig. 2) Surgery lasted 2 hours, with approximately 100 mL of blood loss. No intraoperative difficulties were encountered beyond the injury’s complexity. NPWT was applied in intermittent mode at −100 mmHg, producing serous output of less than 20 mL per day. Dressings were changed every 4 days for 3 weeks (approximately five changes). Physiotherapy with passive flexion began on day three. The postoperative course was uncomplicated, with pain managed with oral analgesics.

By the end of the third week, the patient could flex his fingers beyond 90 degrees with diminished pain. The outer graft surface appeared dry and discolored; however, after bedside debridement of the superficial necrotic layer, the underlying bed was healthy and granulating, and the previously exposed tendon and nerve were adequately covered with vascularized tissue (Fig. 3). The wound was suitable for split-thickness skin grafting rather than a flap, simplifying reconstruction. The graft was successful with no significant complications. By week seven, the patient resumed daily activities, and a QuickDASH score of 15.9% reflected mild disability. Follow-up was limited due to the patient’s displacement by ongoing conflict. This work has been reported in line with the SCARE guidelines[5]. Discussion

Complex forearm wounds are difficult to reconstruct because of delicate structures and limited soft-tissue coverage. Large defects often require distant or free flaps, as local tissue is insufficient. Distant flaps entail donor-site morbidity, complex positioning, and prolonged immobilization, which may hinder hand rehabilitation. Free flaps, favored for their single-stage nature and thin, pliable characteristics, may be unfeasible in resource-limited settings because of a lack of microsurgical capability and high surgical demand.

The TFL graft has a long-standing role in reconstruction for tendons, ligaments, and protective shields in surgery. Its histological composition – rich in collagen and hyaluronic acid – promotes wound healing by providing strength, infection resistance, moisture retention, and tissue hydration. TFL also contains fibroblasts, mast cells, and telocytes that mediate inflammation and support tissue growth, angiogenesis, and regeneration. It is important to note that these effects represent established biological properties of fascia derived from prior experimental studies; they were not directly investigated or demonstrated in the present case[6]. Mika et al illustrated that fascia functions as an innervated organ, playing a role in motor control and proprioception[7]. The release of neural substances from nerves may assist in directing signals in damaged neural tissue while enhancing axonal regeneration. Portillo et al reviewed fascia lata applications in dental therapy, supporting its versatility as a biological graft material across disciplines[8]. Park documented the versatility of TFL allografts for soft tissue reconstruction in diverse clinical scenarios[9]. Vredenburgh et al confirmed the robust mechanical strength of TFL allografts for structural applications[10]. In our case, the TFL graft protected underlying structures and created a smooth surface, allowing early physiotherapy without tethering or adhesion. While the outer layer may dry, the inner layer remains moist, supporting granulation and tissue growth. This inner layer integrates with the wound bed and, after debridement of the outer surface, provides a healthy, vascularized base for skin grafting.

Acellular dermal matrices have become the gold standard for dermal substitution; however, their high cost is a significant barrier. A single 5 × 5 cm2 sheet of bovine ADM costs approximately $2266, with larger sheets costing proportionally more[4]. Autologous TFL graft harvesting significantly reduces costs by eliminating the need for commercial products, which is especially advantageous in low-income settings. However, it increases operative time and introduces donor-site concerns. ADM provides benefits such as standardized quality, predictable performance, no donor-site morbidity, and immediate, unlimited supply, but carries risks including seroma, hematoma, infection, and necrosis[4]. The donor-site morbidity of TFL harvest is generally well tolerated; Angelo and de Campos Azevedo reported that 95% of patients would undergo the procedure again, with functional effects of residual thigh symptoms being small and not clinically significant[11]. The most common donor-site complaints include pain, scarring, and seroma, with residual thigh symptoms in approximately 51% of patients, but rated as clinically insignificant on validated outcome measures[11].

Traditional dressings provide basic coverage but lack structural support and bioactivity for complex injuries, requiring frequent changes that increase infection risk. Flap reconstruction offers definitive coverage but can be delayed by contamination or resource limitations. Combining a TFL graft with NPWT bridges this gap, enabling management of complex wounds and improving outcomes by simplifying closure. NPWT optimizes wound conditions and has become a favored technique. The integration of NPWT with other methods has accelerated wound healing and gained popularity[12,13]. In our case, NPWT enhanced the TFL graft’s effectiveness by supporting cavity reconstruction and maintaining an optimal healing environment. The graft’s pores facilitated fluid drainage, reducing hematoma and seroma risk, while NPWT promoted tissue contact and eliminated dead space. Despite the wound being classified as dirty due to its combat-related nature, no infections were observed.

Both autologous TFL grafting and ADM have drawbacks but they successfully converted a complex wound into a manageable condition. The approach’s main advantages include a readily available, low-cost autologous graft with biohistological benefits, its combination with NPWT for optimal healing, and avoidance of complex flap procedures – particularly valuable in resource-limited settings. The QuickDASH score objectively measured functional outcome, indicating mild disability at 7 weeks. However, the brief 7-week follow-up, necessitated by patient relocation, restricts long-term assessment of sensory and motor function, tendon adhesion, graft durability, and late complications. Scar remodeling and nerve recovery were still in progress; meaningful evaluation typically requires 6–12 months. Limitations also include the absence of grip strength and formal sensory testing, a single-case design, and donor-site morbidity with longer operative time. Larger prospective studies with standardized, extended follow-up are needed to confirm generalizability and compare effectiveness and cost with alternatives.

Conclusion

The TFL graft, acting as an autologous dermal matrix, provides mechanical strength and essential biological components for wound healing. Combined with NPWT, it optimizes the wound environment, protects vital structures, and facilitates early mobilization, making complex wounds suitable for later skin grafting. This approach is especially valuable in resource-limited settings lacking commercial ADMs and free flap options. Larger studies with longer follow-up are needed to confirm its generalizability and to compare its effectiveness and costs with those of alternative methods.
