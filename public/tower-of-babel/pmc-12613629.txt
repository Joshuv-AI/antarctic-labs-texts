The application of the Attention-Relevance-Confidence-Satisfaction motivation model-based instructional design in advanced nursing practice course: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Jin Wang, Xiaofen Wang, Xiaofang Zhu, Ying Zhou, Meiyi Tao, Honghui Zhang, Pingping He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe advanced nursing practice course is a core course for graduate nursing students, aimed at enhancing their comprehensive clinical competence and professionalism. The Attention-Relevance-Confidence-Satisfaction motivation model provides a theoretical framework for instructional innovation by using systematic strategies to stimulate and sustain learning motivation. Instructional design innovations play a crucial role in enhancing graduate nursing students’ learning motivation, self-learning ability, and critical thinking.ObjectiveThe objective is to implement the instructional design based on the Attention-Relevance-Confidence-Satisfaction motivation model in the advanced nursing practice course and evaluate its influence on the learning motivation, self-learning ability, and critical thinking of graduate nursing students.MethodsThis study employed a mixed-methods approach, combining a single-group pre-test/post-test quasi-experimental design with descriptive qualitative research. From March to May 2024, 31 first-year graduate nursing students received a 9-week Attention-Relevance-Confidence-Satisfaction model-based instructional reform. Quantitative methods were used to assess learning motivation, self-learning ability, critical thinking, and academic performance, with comparisons made using paired t-tests. The qualitative component included a descriptive qualitative study with semi-structured individual interviews. This mixed-methods study aimed to objectively measure effectiveness through quantitative data and gain in-depth insights into students’ experiences and benefits via qualitative data.ResultsThe learning motivation and self-learning ability, as well as critical thinking of students exposed to Attention-Relevance-Confidence-Satisfaction model, were all improved. In addition, students achieved better test scores, expressed recognition and satisfaction with the course, and improved professional value recognition.ConclusionApplying the Attention-Relevance-Confidence-Satisfaction model-based motivational instructional design in advanced nursing practice course is feasible and has proved to be effective in increasing learning motivation, promoting self-learning ability, and cultivating critical thinking among graduate nursing students. Background

In 2010, China launched the Master of Nursing Specialist program to cultivate highly knowledgeable nursing professionals. The program emphasizes mastery of fundamental theories and systematic expertise, while fostering critical and analytical thinking. Graduates are expected to independently address common nursing issues and demonstrate strong comprehensive abilities. They are also expected to excel in providing high-quality care. As the healthcare system evolves rapidly, nursing students must pursue more systematic and advanced studies to effectively address future challenges in the field [1]. Advanced nursing practice is a core course that enables nursing graduate students to systematically acquire the theory and method of clinical advanced nursing practice, knowledge of specialty areas, exercise their thinking ability, and foster innovation [2]. Additionally, it enables nursing graduate students to apply theories, skills, and research abilities of a higher level to solve patient issues in clinical specialty areas. As a theoretical, practical, and innovative course, its teaching quality directly impacts the overall quality of student development [3].

Motivation is the internal force or cause that drives an individual to engage in specific behaviors. Learning motivation refers to students’ intrinsic impetus to take initiative in learning, thereby enhancing learning outcomes [4]. As a key factor in the learning process and a variable in teaching, strategies to improve motivation should be incorporated into educational programs [5]. Nursing graduate students have undergone extensive theoretical education and clinical internships from undergraduate to postgraduate levels, which may lead to academic burnout and thus reduce students’ learning motivation [6]. Self-learning ability is the independent capacity to proactively seek methods and master new knowledge, making it a critical competency in student learning [7]. Nursing students can update their professional knowledge to address patient demands in their future careers by developing self-learning ability [8]. Traditional teaching methods are predominantly centralized and instructor-led, fostering controlled learning that diminishes opportunities for independent study, thereby hindering the development of self-learning ability and adversely affecting students’ learning motivation [9]. During their education and clinical practice, nursing students frequently encounter challenges related to the appropriateness of clinical decisions. Critical thinking is essential for making sound judgments in these contexts [10]. It entails the ability to analyze, evaluate, and reason logically to form reasonable conclusions—a crucial skill for the twenty-first century generation [11]. Nursing students must therefore be proficient in critical thinking to effectively assess and resolve healthcare issues [12]. Moreover, during their training, postgraduate nursing students are highly vulnerable to various academic and curricular pressures. These can result in burnout, diminished professional identity, and even intentions to leave the profession—adversely affecting both professional recognition and career development [13]. In light of these challenges, the conventional teaching model proves insufficient across multiple domains of clinical nursing education [14]. Introducing innovative instructional approaches is therefore essential. To enhance learning from diverse perspectives, redesigning the teaching methodology of this course is imperative.

The Attention-Relevance-Confidence-Satisfaction (ARCS) motivation model, developed by John Keller and widely used in education, focuses on four types of motivation: Attention, Relevance, Confidence, and Satisfaction [15]. These four elements interact to form an integrated framework. The model is implemented through the motivational design of instructional processes and resources to stimulate and sustain students’ learning motivation [16]. The ARCS motivation model has been validated to be effective in improving learner motivation and learning outcomes in many studies [17, 18], such as pharmacy, English courses, bioinformatics, etc [19–21]. However, very few studies have reported on the use of the model in graduate nursing education, and limited study has examined the pedagogical application of using the model in advanced nursing practice course. Therefore, the effectiveness of the ARCS motivation model in advanced nursing practice course is unclear.

This study proposes the use of the ARCS motivation model to stimulate learning and thinking ability among graduate nursing students in an advanced nursing practice course and provide guidance for improving the quality of advanced nursing practice education. This study aimed to assess the impact of this instructional design on graduate nursing students’ learning motivation, self-learning ability, and critical thinking.

Materials and methods

Study design

A mixed-methods study was designed to examine the role of instructional design in the advanced nursing practice course based on the ARCS motivation model. The quantitative part employed a quasi-experimental study designed with pre-and post-intervention, and the qualitative part used a descriptive qualitative design with semi-structured individual interviews.

Participants and setting

Researchers recruited 31 first-year full-time nursing graduate students from a university in Southern China, all of whom agreed to participate in the study. The Advanced Nursing Practice course is a required class for first-year nursing graduate students, offered in their second semester. These students had already taken similar theory and nursing courses and had a certain foundational knowledge.

Participation in research was not a prerequisite for participation in this course. Registered students were invited to participate in research, but this was voluntary and will not affect academic performance whether they participate or not. Students were informed that the data collected was confidential and would only be used for research purposes. Classes were provided with information about the educational program of the course, including an explanation of the voluntary nature of their participation and the option to withdraw at any time. The data collection procedure was initiated with the consent of the participants. The ethical application was approved by the university’s ethics committee (approval number: [2024] 326).

Advanced nursing practice course introduction

In China, the advanced nursing practice course is a core course for graduate nursing students. It prepares nursing master’s students to apply high-level theories, skills, and research competencies to address patient issues within clinical specialties. The course requires not only solid theoretical knowledge and clinical skills, but also critical thinking and patient-centered decision-making abilities. The textbook used for this course is listed in the 13th Five-Year Plan issued by the National Health and Family Planning Commission, and is supplemented by a casebook as a teaching aid.

The course has complexity and involves the use of a multidisciplinary model to solve difficult clinical cases. The course content focuses on course introduction, advanced nursing practice development and its relevant theories, Advanced Practice Nurse development, individualized advanced nursing practice, group-based advanced nursing practice, advanced nursing practice in intravenous therapy, plumbing, wound stoma, and midwifery, international perspectives on advanced nursing practice, and advanced nursing practice research and applied cases. After the course, there will be 18 months of nursing clinical practice, and for students to achieve advanced nursing practice levels and improve their learning motivation, self-learning ability, and critical thinking.

Intervention program

A 9-week advanced nursing practice course of 36 classroom hours for 2 credits was conducted in 2024 for 31 first-year full-time graduate nursing students. This instructional design was based on a ten-step process of the ARCS motivation model proposed by Professor Keller [15] as a basis for designing a framework of motivational instructional design. During the course preparation, students completed a brief open-ended questionnaire solely for the teaching team to understand their learning needs and interests. Participation was voluntary and anonymous; responses were not used in the research analysis.

Adopting a student-centered approach, the team carefully identified limitations in the traditional course, conducted a comprehensive analysis of students’ learning status, and pursued research-informed improvements. Before the implementation of this intervention study, the teachers of this course team received theoretical training on the ARCS model. Implementation

This course adopted a teacher-guided, student-centered approach, and we have constructed the “4E” teaching process, which was a whole process, multi-stage online and offline blended teaching mode consisting of active Engagement before class, knowledge Exploration in class, knowledge Experience after class, and knowledge Expansion after class. We employed various teaching methods, including team-based learning (TBL), problem-based learning (PBL), and case-based learning (CBL), evidence-based thinking method, among others.

Active engagement before class

Before the class, the faculty team used the online learning platforms to upload relevant learning materials for the course, such as courseware, videos, cases, relevant literature, etc. and assigned pre-course tasks. Students could preview the online course, complete pre-course tasks, review past knowledge, and read real clinical cases through the online learning platforms. Teachers encouraged students to raise questions and ideas on the course platform after previewing the course so that teachers could provide timely answers to students’ questions.

Knowledge exploration in class

The ancient Chinese book “The Doctrine of the Mean” mentions, “Broadly learn, thoroughly ask, carefully think, debate wisely, and steadfastly put into the act [22]”. We have used this classic quotation to establish a step-by-step, inquiry-driven approach, encompassing “Learning, Asking, Thinking, Debating, and Acting”. (1) Learning: Teachers used teaching resources such as courseware, case studies, and videos to deliver a thorough presentation of the content around the teaching objectives. (2) Asking: After the teacher’s lesson, students shared their queries and took the initiative to summarise their problems to the teacher. (3) Thinking: Students completed a pre-course task assigned by the teachers, where students engaged in group discussions that were problem-driven, reviewed literature and information based on evidence-based evidence grading, created coursework, and reported back. (4) Debating: After completing the task debriefing, students debated the points made in the debriefing as well as the questions posed by the teacher. At the end of the debate, the teacher pointed out the strengths of the report and areas for improvement and encouraged students to judge it with a dialectical concept. At the end of the class, there would be a teacher’s evaluation and a student’s mutual evaluation. (5) Acting: The teacher assigned tasks after class, asking students to utilize the virtual simulation software developed by the teacher’s team to carry out relevant course exercises, as well as reviewing the content of the course. Additionally, after class, teachers would upload outstanding student learning outcomes to an online platform to facilitate continued mutual learning among students.

Knowledge experience after class

After class, students went to the school’s advanced nursing practice laboratory and affiliated hospitals for clinical practice. In this module, students selected cases based on faculty guidance and applied higher-order nursing theory, skills, and research competencies to patient problems in a clinical specialty area. Teachers encouraged students to articulate their insights and reflections on handling clinically complex cases through a written paper.

Knowledge expansion after class

After class, the teacher encouraged students to apply the knowledge acquired in the classroom to participate in academic conferences, helping them translate their reflections on complex clinical cases into research activities. In addition, teachers lead students to actively participate in community volunteering on themes such as ‘whole life care’ to better understand the needs of patients and promote health knowledge.

Evaluation

Quantitative and qualitative data were collected to gain a comprehensive understanding of the effectiveness of instructional design based on the ARCS motivation model. Students’ learning motivation, self-learning ability, and critical thinking were assessed both at the beginning and at the end of the course. The course has also been designed with a multi-form test to evaluate students’ mastery of knowledge. In addition, semi-structured interviews were conducted after the completion of the course.

Learning motivation

The Working Preference Inventory (WPI), developed by Amabile et al. in 1994, was designed to assess endogenous and exogenous motivation [23]. The inventory comprises two distinct versions tailored for adults and students, aimed at measuring work motivation and learning motivation, respectively. In 2006, Liping Chi introduced, revised, and culturally adapted the student version of the WPI to evaluate motivation among college students in China [24]. The revised version has 30 items, including two dimensions of endogenous motivation (self-determination, competence, task commitment, curiosity, enjoyment, and fun) and exogenous motivation (competition, evaluation, recognition, monetary or other incentives, and other constraints). The scale is scored on a four-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating stronger levels of motivation. The scale has good reliability and validity.

Self-learning ability

The Self-learning Ability Scale was adopted to anonymously evaluate students’ self-learning ability in nursing students [25]. The scale has 28 items in three dimensions, including self-management, information management, and learning cooperation. Among them, ten questions are self-management, 11 questions are information management, and seven questions are learning cooperation. A five-point Likert scale was used for rating from 1 (completely disagree) to 5 (completely agree), with total scores ranging from 28 to 140 points. Higher scores indicate better self-learning abilities. The total score of more than 84 indicates that the independent learning ability is higher than the medium level. Otherwise, it is lower than the medium level. The Cronbach’s coefficient of the scale was 0.8632, and the partial reliability of the total volume table was 0.7656. At present, the scale has been widely used by nursing students.

Critical thinking disposition

The Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory of Chinese Version (CTDI-CV) [26]was adopted to evaluate nursing students’ critical thinking ability. This scale includes seven dimensions, which were truth-seeking, open-mindedness, analysis ability, systematization ability, self-confidence in critical thinking, inquisitiveness, and cognitive maturity, with a total of 70 items. Each dimension consisted of 10 items and used a six-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). The total score ranged from 70 to 420, with scores over 280 indicating a positive critical thinking tendency. Each dimension that scores < 40 represented a weak disposition, and ≥ 40 indicated a positive disposition. The Cronbach’s alpha values ranged from 0.54 to 0.77 for the seven dimensions and 0.90 for the total scale.

A multi-form test

The multi-form test is designed to assess the students’ mastery and application of the course. The test is worth a total of 100 marks and consists of task debriefing, construction of a nursing intervention plan, and reflective diary writing. All three types of evaluation assessed students’ knowledge, skills, and humanistic qualities. Structured grading rubrics were developed for each assessment component. Except for the “student mutual evaluations” during task presentations, all other evaluations were independently conducted by the course instructor. Semi-structured interviews

Individual semi-structured interviews were conducted to collect a rich set of qualitative data to complement the quantitative information. The interviewer was a second-year postgraduate student in our research group who had received training in a university course on “Qualitative Research Methods”. Before the interview, the researcher built trust with the students, explained the purpose of the interview, and obtained their verbal consent for the study. To ensure the authenticity of the results, the interviewer repeatedly emphasized to the students that the results of the interview were not related to their academic performance, but only to understand their real learning experiences. Interviewees were selected from the recruited students until saturation was reached, and nothing new about the research topic emerged during the interviews. The interviews were held in classrooms or dormitories, based on the students’ preferences, fostering a comfortable atmosphere that allowed interviewees to engage without pressure, resulting in believable outcomes. Each interview lasted approximately 30 min and was transcribed immediately after the interview was recorded.

The interview outline included the following questions, “How do you feel about the advanced nursing practice course? What did you learn from the course? Does the course’s training program increase your learning motivation? Does the course improve your self-learning ability? Does the program’s curriculum develop your critical thinking? What have you learned from the course that will inspire your future work? “.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS 27.0 software. Frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation were used to describe demographic characteristics. Means were used to describe participants’theoretical scores. Normality tests were performed on the differences in the scores of the pre-test and post-test of each scale, and they all conformed to a normal distribution. Paired-sample t-tests were used to compare differences between pretest and post-test scores, with P < 0.05 indicating significance for all tests.

For the qualitative data, the Colaizzi analysis method was used [27]. The audio recordings were transcribed into text within 24 h. Subsequently, the two authors meticulously reviewed all the transcripts to familiarize themselves with the data. They extracted significant statements pertinent to the study objectives and analyzed the codes derived from the text. The codes were subsequently categorized into thematic groups, with comprehensive descriptions provided for each group. Ultimately, analogous viewpoints were consolidated to establish the conclusive themes. The NVivo 11.0 software facilitated data administration and analysis.

Quantitative results: A multi-form test

The passing score for the Advanced Nursing Practice course at this university was set at 60, with a score of 85 or higher categorized as excellent. The highest score on this test was 96.1 and the lowest score was 83.5, with an average score of 88.12. The results showed a 100% pass rate for the course and a high average score.

Qualitative results

When selected the interviewees, we took the gender and age of the students as well as their marks into account to ensure a diverse and representative sample. A total of 15 students were interviewed by independent interviewers. 3 categories were identified from the qualitative data: (1) Gain from learning, (2) Learning inspiration, (3) Course feedback.

Theme 1: gain from learning

The category of “learning gains” included 3 sub-themes. Through the use of the ARCS motivation model and a variety of teaching methods, the learning motivation, self-learning ability, and critical thinking of nursing students have been enhanced. In addition, the theory, skills, and humanistic qualities of the nursing students have been enhanced.

Improved learning motivation

After the course study, the nursing students gained a passion for learning. They were more willing to participate in learning activities under the ARCS motivation model. This development favored the students’ motivation and willingness to learn.

“The nursing major was not my first choice, I have always had little interest in the nursing profession and chose to go to graduate school for better employment. However, after the first advanced nursing practice course, I was very impressed with the course, and it gave me a deeper understanding of the nursing profession as well, so that I look forward to every class in this course later to give me a goal and motivation for my studies.”(N1).

Improved self-learning ability

Students claimed that the instructional design based on the ARCS model was able to significantly improve their self-learning ability. The course gave students multiple opportunities and ways to participate in the course and led to active learning.

“The advanced nursing practice course gave us a lot of opportunities to participate in learning, discussion, and marking, we worked in small groups to complete the debriefing task, the group members divided up the work and there was a high level of participation from everyone.”(N4).

Improved critical thinking

Teaching in this course broke down students’ inherent ways of thinking, and students learned to use evidence-based methods to find answers, think critically, and make clinical decisions that were most appropriate for their patients.

“In preparation for the task before the class, our group members summarized their learning, discussed with each other, reviewed evidence-based information, and ultimately came up with the results that best fit the debriefing topic.”(N6).

“This course breaks down the inherent rigid thinking. I have realized the importance of critical thinking and formulating problems. It is characterized by open-mindedness and active interaction, fostering a collaborative learning environment.”(N7).

Theme2: learning inspiration

The category of “Learning inspiration” included 2 sub-themes. Students were aware of the importance of the nursing profession in healthcare and recognized the breadth of the nursing profession’s prospects. In addition, they gradually attached importance to the concept of humanistic care and gained a broader understanding of the nursing discipline. The course broke down students’ prejudice about the nursing profession and built their professional confidence and recognition.

Thinking about future career development

This course broadened the student’s knowledge of the nursing profession. Nursing staff could not only maintain the health of patients through basic treatment means but also reduce the psychological pressure on patients through humanistic care. Students could upgrade from general nurses to clinical nurse specialists through their efforts, creating more medical value for patients.

“It is proved that there’s more to the nursing profession than just giving shots and handing out medication. There are so many more ways we can promote patient health and improve their quality of life.” (N5).

Improved professional value recognition

This course broke down students’ prejudice about the nursing profession, gave them a proper view of nursing professionalism and importance, and increased their sense of professional recognition. As nursing students, they needed to develop a sense of professional mission and responsibility to maintain patient health.

“I’ve always had a bit of bias against the nursing profession, and now my mind have changed. The nursing profession is an essential presence in the healthcare industry.” (N9).

Theme3: course feedback

The category of “Course feedback” included 1 sub-theme. The students who have participated in this course teaching reform highly approved of this instructional design and gave it a very high level of satisfaction.

Expressing recognition and satisfaction for the course arrangement

The teaching mode and method used in this course were novel, and the course form was rich, which aroused students’ interest. The arrangement of course tasks was reasonable, from shallow to deep, from easy to difficult. In the teaching process, a large number of real clinical cases were cited and scenario simulation was carried out, which changed the drawbacks of the students’ inability to apply theories to the clinic, cultivated the students’ thinking and practical ability, and received positive feedback from the students. As the sense of professional identity increased, it gradually built the student’s professional confidence. Students had a lot of opportunities to express their opinions in class, which enhanced their self-confidence.

“We are in the second semester of our first year of graduate study and have been educated in many graduate courses, but the teaching model of the advanced nursing practice course is by far the most novel and recognizable teaching style I have ever seen. I hope it can be widely applied in other courses.”(N14).

“I am greatly satisfied with this course, I rarely had the opportunity to express myself in class before, but here I am often able to voice my opinions and feel so much more confident in all of myself.”(N11).

Discussion

We used a mixed-methods approach to explore the impact of an instructional design based on the ARCS motivation model applied to an advanced nursing practice course on graduate nursing students’ learning motivation, self-learning ability, and critical thinking. Overall, both quantitative and qualitative results indicated that the instructional design using the ARCS model in this course produced positive effects among Chinese graduate nursing students. The six main positive impacts were as follows, including improved in learning motivation; improved in self-learning ability; improved in critical thinking; a high pass rate for the course; and higher course recognition and satisfaction.; higher professional value recognition.

Improved learning motivation

Learning motivation is a critical factor in education, significantly influencing the effectiveness of teaching programs [28]. Studies indicate that the ARCS model enhances nursing students’ learning attitudes cognitively and improves their self-efficacy, thereby fostering motivation [18, 29]. Furthermore, ARCS-based instruction helps students sustain and even increase their motivation throughout the course, including those already motivated at the outset [20]. This study developed a “4E” instructional model to comprehensively enhance students’ learning motivation through systematic pre-class, in-class, and post-class activities. By integrating clinical nursing videos, real-world cases, expert literature, and virtual simulation software, a dynamic and immersive learning environment was created, effectively strengthening students’ self-development willingness and both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation. Some studies have shown that the use of multiple teaching methods, such as TBL, PBL, CBL, evidence-based thinking method, in nursing education might enhance the problem-solving abilities and motivation of nursing students [30]. This study employed traditional and pedagogically reliable methods to help students build a fundamental understanding of the course, while also introducing these innovative methods. The combination of traditional and modern teaching strategies helped sustain students’ innate curiosity, stimulate enthusiasm for learning, and enhance motivation [31]. Our findings are consistent with the results of a study of 36 Chinese students, which found that the implementation of motivational teaching strategies based on the ARCS model can increase students’ motivation and promote their learning [32].

Improved self-learning ability

Self-learning is promoted in higher education and professional education, including in nursing education [33]. Self-learning ability is an essential component of lifelong learning for personal and professional development [34]. Our previous instructional approach relied on “spoon-feeding”, a method entirely directed by the teacher. This approach suppressed student autonomy, leading to passive learning, underdeveloped critical thinking skills, and low knowledge retention. In contrast, our new instructional design is student-centered, effectively stimulating students’ intrinsic motivation and fostering their capacity for self-learning. For example, it was previously reported that ARCS motivational teaching provided opportunities to stimulate and sustain students’ curiosity about the course, which in turn enhances their ability to engage in active learning [35–37]. Our findings showed that learning based on the ARCS model improved nursing students’ self-learning ability, which is similar to the results of a previous study [38]. This study provided students with rich curriculum resources, designed progressive group debriefing tasks, gave students space to think independently, stimulated students’ attention and curiosity about the course and holistically improved students’ self-learning ability [39]. It was worth noting that although students’ self-learning ability had improved, our results showed that students’ overall independent learning ability still needed to be improved. Maybe students were under long-term learning pressure, as well as being accustomed to the traditional mode of teaching, students still habitually accepted knowledge passively during the learning process, and measures should be taken to cultivate further improvement of students’ self-learning ability.

Improved critical thinking

In 1992, the American League for Nursing established critical thinking as a core competency required for nursing education, and in 1999, the American Association of Colleges of Nursing identified critical thinking as a core competency for nursing education. Critical thinking is closely related to nursing competence and patient clinical outcomes [40], and developing critical thinking enables graduates to excel academically [41], professionally [42], and in personal resilience [43]. Developing critical thinking in nursing students has a critical impact on their ability to make prompt and correct clinical decisions in their future employment.

Our study showed that students’ critical thinking skills improved by the end of this study, with all seven dimensions of critical thinking significantly improving by the end of the course. Some studies have found that factors that stimulate students’ critical thinking include simulations, case studies, and clinical experiences [44]. In our study, a large number of real clinical case studies, simulations, and clinical practices were conducted, the instructor encouraged students to search for a large amount of literature, look for evidence-based evidence to judge the grade of the literature, make correct clinical decisions. Critical thinking is key to successful clinical practice and simulation can help nursing students learn to think critically and become more confident [45]. After class, students used virtual simulation software to strengthen practical skills, and participated in clinical training at advanced nursing labs and affiliated hospitals during post-course practice. These pedagogical strategies enhanced students’ critical thinking, aligning with previous findings [46]. Additionally, an inquiry-based instructional framework titled “Learning, Asking, Thinking, Debating, and Acting” was implemented. Methods such as task debriefing, mutual teacher-student evaluation, brainstorming encouraged active thinking and debate, sustaining student initiative and engagement. This approach fostered analytical and critical skills, shifting away from traditional passive knowledge reception [47].

A high pass rate for the course

The scores for this study are in a multi-format approach to assess the learning outcomes of the students. The first component involved complex progressive tasks. The second part tasked students with constructing a clinical nursing intervention plan based on course content to assess applied knowledge. The third component entailed writing a reflective learning journal to deepen comprehension and promote the refinement of personal learning strategies. Results showed a 100% pass rate among students, and the class achieved a high average score, indicating that the motivational teaching strategy enhanced students’ understanding of theoretical content and ability to apply knowledge in practice, thus improving academic performance—consistent with previous findings [48]. In addition, some studies have shown that better academic performance is closely related to higher motivation [29, 49], and the results of this study show that this teaching reform can increase students’ motivation and improve their self-learning ability so that the improvement of comprehensive ability effectively stimulates students’ desire and ability to learn, which leads to improved performance [50]. Thus, it is verified that the instructional design based on the ARCS motivation model can enable students to achieve better results.

Higher course recognition and satisfaction

The results of our study showed that students recognized and were satisfied with the use of an instructional design based on the ARCS motivation model in advanced nursing practice courses. This course implemented a step-by-step instructional model with progressively difficult pre-class tasks, aligning with students’ cognitive patterns to provide positive feedback on learning outcomes and psychological needs. By integrating theoretical and practical elements, the course enhanced the clarity and engagement of nursing education, resulting in substantial student gains [51]. Furthermore, as students’ perception of ARCS-based motivational teaching positively influenced learning attitudes, we designed class activities and course structures consistent with the ARCS model. This approach increased motivation, improved attitudes toward learning, and promoted acceptance of the course design [52].

The design of this study also used a large number of online resources such as mass development of online course MOOC [53], RainClassroom, etc., which provided students with selective attention and deriving satisfaction from multiple sources. At the same time, students are able to receive positive feedback on their learning outcomes from teachers and peers in the classroom, and students gradually build self-confidence. Therefore, using an instructional design based on the ARCS motivation model is able to give students a high level of satisfaction with the course [54].

Higher professional value recognition

Nurses’ attitudes and decisions are significantly influenced by their professional values [55]. Professional nursing practice can only be achieved when nurses possess a clear understanding of their professional principles and consistently adhere to them [56]. In our pre-course learning survey, we found that students had a low level of professional value identification. Developing the professional values of nursing students is vitally important for the development of the nursing profession [57]. In this study, by integrating personal experiences into the classroom debriefing and learning process, students were able to reflect on and understand the core values of the nursing profession more deeply based on theoretical and practical learning. ARCS-based instructional design supports positive professional identity by creating contextualized learning environments that prepare students to cope with potential career challenges. Well-developed professional values contribute significantly to nurses’ early clinical decision-making [58]. Thus, it is essential for nursing educators and administrators to monitor students’ development of professional values and consider their impact when revising curricula [59].

This study deeply integrated the ARCS motivation model into the instructional design of an advanced nursing practice course, employing innovative teaching methods to enhance student learning. The results demonstrate that the ARCS-based approach improved students’ learning motivation, self-learning ability, and critical thinking, while also had a high pass rate for the course, greater course satisfaction, and stronger professional identity. This foundation better prepares students for future clinical practice.

Limitations and future directions

There are some shortcomings in this study. Firstly, we only examined the impact of the ARCS motivation model on Chinese postgraduate nursing student’s advanced nursing practice course, which has a small number of students, so the generalisability of our findings needs to be considered. Therefore, multi-center, large-sample studies in different cultural contexts are needed to confirm our findings. In addition, the quantitative analysis used a quasi-experimental study without a randomized controlled group, which reduced the credibility of the results. Therefore, it is necessary to test our findings with more rigorous randomized controlled trials in the future. Lastly, the current study only conducted pre- and post-course measurements, future longitudinal studies to examine the long-term effects of ARCS motivation model-based instructional design in the advanced nursing practice course on nursing students are warranted.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate the ARCS motivation model into an advanced nursing practice course for graduate students, evaluating its educational effectiveness. The ARCS-based instructional design enhanced first-year nursing graduates’ learning motivation, self-learning ability, and critical thinking. It proved feasible and was well-received, offering both personal and professional benefits. We recommend that educators incorporate such motivational approaches into nursing curricula and promote their use in other courses.
