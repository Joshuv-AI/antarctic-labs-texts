Optimizing methane flux prediction and key feature identification based on a novel hybrid machine learning model
Authors: Xinqin Gu, Li Yao, Xiang Xiao, Lifeng Wu, Haofeng Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryNatural landscape methane (CH4) emissions account for half of the global total, yet their quantification remains challenging due to high measurement costs and ecosystem complexity. In this study, the performance of three machine learning models, XGBoost (XGB), random forest (RF) and support vector machine (SVM) were assessed, to predict CH4 fluxes, using data from 36 FLUXNET-CH4 sites. Furthermore, the GWOPSO algorithm was applied to hybridize the XGB model to optimize input features and hyperparameters simultaneously. The results demonstrated that ensemble models (XGB and RF) significantly outperformed SVM. The hybrid model further improved prediction accuracy. Across most wetland types, it required only five key features to surpass the all-feature XGB model, reducing RMSE by 1.1%–10.8%. Soil temperature was identified as the most important predictive variable across most site types. Overall, this study recommends a reliable CH4 prediction method, which provides a reference for CH4 prediction of different site types.Subject areas: Environmental science, Environmental monitoring, Applied sciences

Introduction

As a potent greenhouse gas, methane (CH4) has a 100-year global warming potential 28–34 times greater than carbon dioxide.1 CH4 emissions from natural landscapes constitute roughly half of global CH4 contributions to the atmosphere. However, quantifying CH4 emissions faces two major challenges: first, traditional observation methods are costly with limited coverage2,3; second, the emission processes involve complex multi-feature interactions.4,5,6 Therefore, accurately quantifying CH4 emissions has become a key research priority.

Given machine learning’s capability to handle non-linear relationships, data mining technology such as support vector machine (SVM), artificial neural network (ANN), random forest (RF), and long short-term memory (LSTM) have been effectively employed in predicting CH4 emission2,7,8,9 and conducting spatial expansion analyses.10,11 For example, Yuan et al. developed a causality-enabled machine learning model for CH4 emission from 4 wetland types: bog, fen, marsh, and wet tundra.7 Peltola et al. used a RF machine learning algorithm to extend the eddy-related flux measurement data of CH4 at 25 sites to the northern hemisphere (north of 45°N) to estimate CH4 emissions from wetlands.10Based on eddy covariance (EC) flux measurements and satellite-derived environmental variables at a 10 km spatial resolution, Ying et al. produced the first daily-scale estimates of CH4.12 Chen et al. developed six distinct bottom-up machine learning models using in situ CH4 fluxes from both chamber measurements and the Fluxnet-CH4 network.13 Luo et al. used different time intervals to train the methane concentration data of the Alberta air regional site, and compared the prediction performance of different neural network models.14 Recently, XGBoost (extreme gradient boosting) has gained widespread adoption in diverse fields due to its higher computational efficiency and better ability to mitigate overfitting.15,16,17 How-ever, to date, no study has applied XGBoost for CH4 across various site types.

The optimal selection of input features for machine learning models remains a critical challenge in predicting CH4 emissions. Excessive features may induce overfitting through increased model complexity, while insufficient features compromise prediction accuracy by omitting key features. To avoid such issues, some researchers use principal component analysis (PCA) for feature dimensionality reduction.8 However, the linear combination characteristic of PCA causes the principal components to lose ecological interpretability, thus limiting mechanistic analysis. In contrast, tree-based models (e.g., RF, Gradient Boosting Trees) effectively retain critical features while eliminating redundancies through feature importance ranking and pruning algorithms.18,19 These models, integrated with shapley additive explanations (SHAP), further quantify feature-specific positive/negative contributions and interaction effects on the target flux, providing an effective approach to balance predictive performance with ecological interpretability.20

In addition to feature selection, the accuracy and efficiency of the model depend largely on the setting of hyperparameters. However, most machine learning models have limitations in parameter optimization, especially in complex nonlinear problems.21,22 Traditional optimization methods are often difficult to find the global optimal solution. The meta-heuristic algorithm can efficiently explore the parameter space and quickly identify the optimal parameter combination by simulating the evolutionary mechanism or swarm intelligence behavior in nature (such as firefly algorithm, particle swarm optimization (PSO), whale optimization algorithm (WOA), etc.).23,24,25 This algorithm not only overcomes the limitations of traditional methods, but also provides more flexible and powerful optimization capabilities for complex problems. Wu et al.26 designed a hybrid model based on extreme learning machine (ELM) and flower pollination algorithm (FPA) to predict monthly pan evaporation. Mohammadi and Mehdizadeh27 applied the WOA to optimize support vector regression (SVR), aiming to enhance monthly pan evaporation prediction capabilities. Similarly, El-Kenawy and Eid28 proposed a hybrid GWOPSO algorithm, which integrates the hierarchical exploration of GWO and the social utilization of PSO to solve the hyperparameter optimization problem in feature selection. By balancing global search ability and convergence efficiency, GWOPSO effectively navigates high-dimensional non-convex spaces. The algorithm was validated on 17 UCI datasets, achieving significant error reduction and feature reduction while maintaining robustness. Although the single iteration cost of GWOPSO is slightly higher than that of standard PSO or GWO, the total number of iterations is reduced owing to the accelerated convergence of the fusion mechanism, resulting in better overall efficiency.

Although optimization algorithms have achieved significant progress in the field of model tuning, their application in CH4 prediction model optimization remains relatively limited, particularly in studies that simultaneously perform feature selection and hyperparameter optimization. To address this gap, this study first systematically assessed the performance of three machine learning models (XGB, SVM, and RF) in predicting CH4 emissions under all-feature input conditions. Building on this foundation, this study innovatively constructed a hybrid optimization framework coupling a gray wolf-particle swarm optimization algorithm (GWOPSO) with the XGB model, enabling the simultaneous optimization of model input features and hyperparameter combinations. This approach not only significantly improved the accuracy of CH4 emission predictions but also revealed key driving features by quantifying feature importance across different site types. These findings provide a robust technical foundation and theoretical basis for developing high-precision CH4 prediction models tailored to different site types, offering new insights for the precise prediction and management of CH4 emissions.

Results

Statistical performance of all-feature models

The RMSE, mean absolute error (MAE) and R2 values of the 3 machine learning models at each station and their average values of different site types are shown in Figure 1 and Table 1, respectively. A comprehensive comparison incorporating bootstrap uncertainty quantification and statistical testing confirmed that ensemble learning models (XGB and RF) significantly and consistently outperformed the SVM model (p < 0.001). Specifically, the XGB model achieved a mean reduction in RMSE of 10.65 nmol m−2 s−1 (95% CI: 7.07 nmol m−2 s−1−15.04 nmol m−2 s−1), representing a 28.9% relative improvement, and a reduction in MAE of 9.18 nmol m−2 s−1 (95% CI: 5.97 nmol m−2 s−1−12.78 nmol m−2 s−1), a 37.1% improvement over the SVM model. The ΔR2 was 0.166 (95% CI: 0.138–0.192). Similarly, the RF model demonstrated mean absolute reductions of 10.12 nmol m−2 s−1 (95% CI: 6.77 nmol m−2 s−1−14.19 nmol m−2 s−1) in RMSE (27.5% relative improvement) and 8.87 nmol m−2 s−1 (95% CI: 5.85 nmol m−2 s−1−12.11 nmol m−2 s−1) in MAE (35.8% relative improvement). The absolute difference in R2 was 0.167 (95% CI: 0.141–0.191), equivalent to a 39.7% relative increase. All differences are statistically significant (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p < 0.001). These results indicate that both XGB and RF models exhibit both higher accuracy and stronger explanatory power in predicting CH4 emissions compared to SVM.

The study revealed significant performance variations across site types. All three models showed limited predictive capability in Drained and Upland types, with R2 values consistently below 0.268, indicating their inability to effectively capture CH4 emission patterns in these ecosystems. In contrast, the XGB model achieved superior performance in Marsh, attaining the highest R2 value of 0.803. The XGB model demonstrated a R2 of 0.5%–3.7% higher than the RF model (across site types: Fen, Marsh, Rice, Swamp, Upland), which highlighted its enhanced capacity to capture the complex relationships between CH4 emissions and input features.

Feature selection of XGB model based on the SHAP method

Initially, we developed the XGB model, incorporating all available features as inputs, with CH4 emissions served as the target output. Subsequently, we applied the SHAP method to compute the feature importance values, which are illustrated in Figure 2. Feature selection was performed independently for each site type, and the top five variables of SHAP importance were finally selected. Among them, land surface temperature (TS), normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), latent heat flux (LE), and gross primary productivity during daytime (GPP_DT) were selected in most cases and were key features of universality. The results indicated that TS was the most critical feature influencing CH4 emissions across six site types (Bog, Fen, Marsh, Rice, Swamp, and Wet tundra), with SHAP values ranging from 9.71 nmol m−2 s−1 (Bog) to 45.36 nmol m−2 s−1 (Swamp). Vegetation dynamics, represented by the NDVI, also played a significant role, ranking among the top three drivers in five site types (Bog, Fen, Marsh, Rice, Swamp), with the highest SHAP value of 11.06 observed in Marsh. Notably, LE exhibited exceptional importance in Swamp (SHAP = 28.75 nmol m−2 s−1), while GPP_DT was the dominant feature in Rice paddies (SHAP = 17.20 nmol m−2 s−1).

Heatmap of mean absolute Shapley (SHAP) values for XGB models using the all-feature set

To evaluate the SHAP-based feature-selected XGB model (S-XGB), it was compared with the Pearson-based feature-selected XGB model (P-XGB) (Table 2). The P-XGB model demonstrated RMSE values ranging from 13.006 to 98.292 nmol m−2 s−1, R2 values between 0.020 and 0.702, and MAE values of 6.215–69.756 nmol m−2 s−1 across different input combinations. Its accuracy peaked when using five input features across all site types. Comparative analysis revealed that the SHAP-based XGB model generally outperformed the Pearson-based approach, achieving 0.2%–22.7% lower RMSE with the same number of input features. The Pearson-based XGB model also underperformed compared to the all-feature XGB model (All-XGB). In contrast, the SHAP method captures nonlinear feature interactions, demonstrating superior advantages over traditional feature selection approaches.

Performance of the hybrid model

The SHAP method served as a model-agnostic approach for evaluating feature contributions. However, it exhibited theoretical limitations in feature selection due to its dependence on model quality-suboptimal models may yield unreliable SHAP value interpretations. To address this, our study proposed a dual optimization framework simultaneously optimizing feature combinations and XGB hyperparameters, ultimately developing enhanced predictive models. With the objective of model simplification, we constrained the feature subset to 2–5 optimal features (Table 3). Experimental results demonstrated that the CGWOPSO-XGB4 model achieved superior performance among all site types. For most site types, this optimized model with merely 5 features outperformed the baseline All-XGB model containing 16 features, reducing RMSE by 1.1%–10.8%.

Discussion

Prediction accuracy of machine learning models

In this study, we examined the prediction accuracy of various CH4 prediction models under all-feature input conditions. The results showed that the XGB and RF models significantly outperformed the SVM in terms of prediction accuracy. This finding is in line with the research results of Kim et al.8 in the field of CH4 gap-filling. They found through the analysis of five wetlands and rice fields that the RF model outperformed the SVM. Additionally, Irvin et al.9 indicated that in 17 wetland and rice field sites, decision tree-based models, such as XGB and RF, outperformed ANN in most sites. This further highlights the advantages of XGB and RF models in CH4 prediction.

This study revealed the mean NRMSE of different models (Figure 4A) and mean CH4 fluxes among 8 site types (Figure 4B). The results showed that sites with low CH4 emissions (Drained and Upland) have higher mean NRMSE, while high-emission sites (Marsh) correspond to the lowest mean NRMSE. Similarly, through the systematic comparative analysis of ten typical wetland ecosystems, Knox et al.2 found that the highest relative error is often related to low CH4 emission points, and the lowest relative error is related to high CH4 emission points. This may result from the phenomenon that, high-emission site types have more stable methanogenic community structures and regular hydrological conditions, which makes the response relationships between key driving features and CH4 fluxes easier for models to capture. In contrast, low-emission site types are affected by complex features such as intermittent flooding and fluctuations in root exudates, leading to a more nonlinear CH4 generation process and thus increasing model uncertainty.

Advantages of the hybrid model

Accurate prediction of CH4 flux is of great significance for quantifying the contribution of natural emission sources to climate change and providing key parameters for global carbon cycle models. However, due to differences in geographical factors such as altitude, topography, and climate, the impact of ecological features on CH4 flux varies across different regions.29 Therefore, selecting appropriate input feature combinations is crucial for accurately predicting CH4 flux.

At present, most studies focus on conducting correlation analysis, screening, and dimensionality reduction of input features before using the model. Common methods include Pearson correlation analysis, recursive feature elimination, and PCA.8 All of these methods involve screening features before the model completes its actual task, and whether the selected features can function well in the model can only be reflected through the final results of the model’s computation.

SHAP, as a post hoc interpretability method in machine learning, works by first obtaining the optimal computational results through model building and then calculating the contribution of each different feature in the model to the model output.30 Comparative analysis revealed that the SHAP-based XGB model generally outperformed the Pearson-based approach, achieving 0.2%–22.7% lower RMSE with the same number of input features. Furthermore, the top five features selected by the Pearson method largely differed from those identified by SHAP, indicating that the Pearson method primarily relies on linear relationships between features and target variables, making it unsuitable for feature selection in machine learning models. However, the SHAP method has certain theoretical flaws in feature selection, that is, it relies on the quality of the model. If the model quality is not optimal, its SHAP values will struggle to provide an accurate measure of feature contributions. Therefore, this paper attempts to optimize both the feature combinations and the parameter values of the XGB model simultaneously to obtain a more superior-performing model. The results showed that when comparing models with equivalent feature inputs (P-XGB, S-XGB, and CGWOPSO-XGB), the proposed hybrid approach consistently exhibited a tighter cluster distribution around the reference line and more optimal slope characteristics. Moreover, for most site types, this optimized model with only 5 features outperformed the baseline All-XGB model containing 16 features, reducing RMSE by 1.1%–10.8%.

Analysis of input features

This study identified the top three driving features based on SHAP mean values of the CGWOPSO-XGB4 models, elucidating key influences on CH4 emission predictions across diverse ecosystems (Table 4). TS emerged as a core feature in Bog, Drained, Fen, Marsh, and Swamp ecosystems, aligning with findings from Irvin et al.9 and Buzacott et al.30 These studies similarly identified TS as a primary feature through feature importance analysis, with its dominance likely attributable to methanogen metabolic sensitivity to soil thermal conditions.31 The pronounced contribution of total primary productivity obtained by the daytime approach (GPP_DT) in Bog, Marsh, and Upland ecosystems confirms the vcritical ecological process whereby plant photosynthetic products supply methanogenic substrates via root exudates.32

Notably, the NDVI exhibited marked significance as a vegetation coverage metric across six site types. This observation corroborates Buzacott et al.,30 who demonstrated that incorporating vegetation dynamic features like NDVI or leaf area index enhances CH4 emission predictions not only in grasslands but also across other land-use types. Such improvements may stem from vegetation dynamics regulating CH4 transport pathways33 and refining predictive accuracy.34

Of particular interest is the unique explanatory power of specific features in certain site types. For instance, the strong predictive capacity of net ecosystem exchange (NEE) in Drained reveals hydrologically mediated alterations in CH4 production mechanisms. This phenomenon likely arises from drainage-induced redox potential changes that modulate the balance between methanogenesis and CH4 oxidation, thereby substantially influencing net emissions.35

Conclusion

In this study, we systematically evaluated the performance of three machine learning models—XGBoost (XGB), RF, and support vector machine (SVM) in predicting CH4 emissions, using data from 36 sites in the FLUXNET-CH4 database. Furthermore, we hybridized the XGB model with the GWOPSO algorithm to optimize both the input features and the hyperparameters of the XGB model simultaneously. Key findings include.

Limitations of the study

The study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results.

1Factors such as seasonal variations, climate change effects (e.g., changes in precipitation and temperature patterns), and site-specific management practices (e.g., land-use adjustments or agricultural activities) may dynamically affect feature importance. Consequently, the stability of the key identified features, such as soil temperature and vegetation indices, under differing environmental or anthropogenic conditions remains uncertain.

Method details

Dataset and site description

This study selected 36 sites from the FLUXNET-CH4 database.36 These sites spanned tropical to boreal climates and encompass diverse and representative site types (Figure S1 and Table S1), including bog (5), drained (4), fen (7), marsh (7), rice (4), swamp (1), upland (4), and wet tundra (4). The site selection criteria were as follows: (1) at least one full year of measured flux data; (2) the selected sites must possess the necessary input data required for developing the relevant models in this study.

In this study, these features were selected for CH4 flux prediction, mainly based on two considerations: data availability and their direct or indirect effects on key biophysical processes of CH4 production, consumption and transport. Meteorological features including air temperature (TA), wind speed (WS), wind direction (WD), atmospheric pressure (PA), and vapor pressure deficit (VPD) regulate atmospheric transport and plant physiology3,37; energy balance features including incoming shortwave radiation (SW_IN), latent heat flux (LE), and sensible heat flux (H) affect substrate supply and microbial environment38,39; carbon flux features including net ecosystem exchange (NEE), ecosystem respiration (RECO), and gross primary production (GPP) reflect the state of the carbon cycle,39,40,41 with GPP directly related to the supply of methanogenic substrates. Additional features include friction velocity (USTAR), used for data quality control, and the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI). When more than one observation depth for soil temperature (TS) was available, multiple TS observations were integrated using the weighted average method. In addition, NDVI data are obtained from MODIS data in Google Earth Engine (Table S2).42

Extreme gradient boosting

The XGB model is a machine learning algorithm implemented in gradient boosting framework.43 The integration algorithm summarizes the modeling results of the sum of all the weak learners (Classification and regression tree, CART). The XGB model adopts the training method of continuous accumulation of multiple weak learners to optimize the objective function, that is, the XGB model builds one CART at a time, and the newly established CART score will be accumulated with all previous CART scores. The number of iterations is 450. The learning rate is 0.03. The subsample ratio is 0.6. The maximum depth of each tree is 6. The minimum sum of instance weight needed in a child node is 10. The number of threads is 2.

Random forest

Random forest (RF) is an ensemble algorithm proposed by Breiman.44 RF consists of multiple classification and regression tree (CART), and the average of these tree prediction results is the final prediction results. In flux prediction studies, the accuracy of RF is better than other machine learning algorithms.45,46 Given its performance in flux prediction, this study applies the RF algorithm for predicting CH4, and compares it with the XGBoost. The number of decision trees is 500. The number of randomly selected features used for each tree construction (mtry) is 4.

Support vector machine

The Support Vector Machine (SVM), proposed by Vapnik,47 represents a supervised learning framework within artificial intelligence, primarily designed for predictive analytics and pattern detection. This algorithm has gained significant traction in both regression tasks and forecasting applications. Distinctively, SVM operates through kernel-based methodology, employing mathematical transformation functions to implicitly project low-dimensional input data into higher-dimensional feature spaces. This characteristic enables complex nonlinear relationships to be modeled through linear techniques in transformed space. The penalty parameter is 4.0; the error tolerance is 0.01; and the parameter of the kernel function is 0.8; epsilon parameter is 0.01.

Shapley additive explanations

SHAP is an interpretability framework based on cooperative game theory.48 It calculates Shapley values of feature marginal contributions to provide local and global explanations for any machine learning model. In this study, SHAP values were used to quantify the contribution of each feature to CH4 prediction in the XGB model.

PSO algorithm

Particle swarm optimization (PSO) was inspired by the behavior of bird flocks, which was proposed by Kennedy and Eberhart.49 The algorithm simulates the movement of particles in a search space, where each particle represents a potential solution to the optimization problem. The particles move through the search space by adjusting their velocity and position based on their own experience and the experience of the swarm.

The female and male leaders hold the highest position and are referred to as alpha (α). The subordinate wolves that assist the leaders are known as beta (β) and represent the second level of the hierarchy. The third level is called delta (δ), and the remaining wolves are termed omega (ω), representing the lowest level of the hierarchy.

Model establishment and validation

The CH4 flux data and input features used in this study were based on daily temporal resolution. In terms of model verification, a 5-fold cross-validation method is used to evaluate the performance of the model. The specific process is as follows: all data are randomly divided into training set (80%) and test set (20%). In order to reduce the influence of the randomness caused by a single division on the results, the above division and verification process was independently repeated 5 times.

Uncertainty quantification

The uncertainty in the performance differences between ensemble models (XGBoost, RF) and the baseline model (SVM) was assessed using the bootstrap resampling method with 1000 iterations. The 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for the mean differences in RMSE, MAE, and R2 were reported.

Statistical significance testing

The statistical significance of the performance differences between models was formally tested using the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test. This test was applied to the paired performance metric distributions across all sites or site types. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All reported comparisons between ensemble models and the SVM model yielded p-values of less than 0.001.
