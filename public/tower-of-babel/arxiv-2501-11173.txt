How Many Cards Should You Lay Out in Quad-128: A Classification of Caps in AG ⁡ ( 7 , 2 ) \AG(7,2)
Kariane Calta, Timothy E. Goldberg, Lauren L. Rose

Abstract
. We define a cap in the affine geometry AG ⁡ ( n , 2 ) \AG(n,2) to be a subset in which every collection of four points is in general position. In this paper, we classify, up to affine equivalence, all caps in AG ⁡ ( 7 , 2 ) \AG(7,2) of size k ≥ 10 k\geq 10 . In particular, we show that there are two equivalence classes of 10 10 -caps and one equivalence class of 11 11 -caps, none of which are complete, and one equivalence class of 12 12 -caps, which are both complete and of maximum size.

1.
Introduction

Quads
is a pattern recognition game similar to the popular card game SET. A standard Quads deck consists of
64
cards, each with one, two, three, or four of the same symbol, in one of four colors. A complete Quads deck is shown in Figure
1
, with triangles, squares, circles, and hearts as symbols. The goal of the game is to find
quads
, collections of four cards that satisfy a specific pattern, namely that in each attribute (number, symbol, shape) the four cards are all the same, all different, or two pairs of types.

For example, the cards
1-red triangle
,
2-green squares
,
3-red hearts
, and
4-blue circles
form a quad, since the numbers are all different, there are two red and two green cards, and the shapes are all different.

Figure 1.
Quad-64 Deck

Quads cards can be coded with vectors in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{6}
, and easily generalized to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
. See
[
2
]
or
[
9
]
for details.

Once we do this, a quad has a nice mathematical interpretation.

Theorem 1.1
.

[
2
]

Four distinct cards
A,B,C,D
form a quad if and only if as vectors,
A+B+C+D=\mathbf{0}
.

This is a parallel to SET, where cards can be coded with vectors in
\mathbb{Z}_{3}^{4}
, and generalized to
\mathbb{Z}_{3}^{n}
. In this case, three distinct cards form a SET if and only if their vector sum is zero.

The celebrated
Cap Set Problem
is to find the maximum size of a SET-free collection of cards, called a
cap
. The answer is only known for
n\leq 6
, and the best known upper bound is
(2.756)^{n}
, due to Ellenberg and Gijswijt (
[
5
]
). See
[
4
]
or
[
6
, Chapter 9]
for a detailed introduction to the
Cap Set
problem.

In this paper, we study the analogous problem for Quads, but expand our study to the classification of all caps, not just those of maximum size. Quad-free sets are called
2
-caps
in
[
1
]
and
Sidon Sets
in
[
10
]
,
[
3
]
, and
[
7
]
, for example. The latter two papers make use of a natural connection between Sidon Sets and linear codes. In a slight abuse of definition, we call a quad-free set of cards a
cap
.

In
[
10
]
, Tait and Won found asymptotic bounds for the maximum size
M(n)
of a cap in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,2^{n/2}\leq M(n)\leq 1+\sqrt{2}\cdot 2^{n/2}.

A
complete cap
is a maximal quad-free set, but not necessarily of maximum size. In
[
8
]
, Redman, Rose, and Walker constructed a small complete cap in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
for each

.

The sizes of complete caps in dimensions
7
and
8
can be verified computationally, and the maximum caps sizes for dimensions
9
and
10
have been determined using libraries of even linear codes
[
7
]
.

In
[
2

, Crager et al. classified caps of up to
9
cards in any dimension, and all caps in
\AG(n,2)
for

, up to affine equivalence, using the fact that the encoding by vectors in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
is a model for the affine geometry
\AG(n,2)
.

This paper picks up where
[
2
]
leaves off. Our goal is to classify caps in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{7}
, which corresponds to the card deck Quad-128. One way to construct Quad-128 from the standard deck, Quad-64, is to add a half-attribute, say background color, with two states instead of four, as seen in Figure

Since caps in

(2)

(1)

2.
Fundamental notions in
\AG(n,2)

The set
\AG(n,q)
is the finite affine geometry of dimension
n
and order
q
. A standard model for
\AG(n,q)
consists of vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, where
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is the field of order
q
. In this model the affine subspaces (called
flats
) are just translates of linear subspaces. A detailed description of the geometry of
\AG(n,q)
, and in particular
\AG(n,2)
, can be found in
[
2
]
. For the convenience of the reader, we recall the most relevant definitions and properties here. Let
V
be a vector space over a field.

An
affine combination
of a set
A\subseteq V
is a linear combination of elements of
A
whose coefficients sum to
1
. The set
A
is
affinely independent
if no element is an affine combination of the others. The
affine span
of
A
, denoted
\aff(A)
, consists of all affine combinations of elements of
A
. The
flats
in
V
are the affine spans of non-empty sets. An
affine basis

B
for a flat
F
is a set of affinely independent elements whose (affine) span is
F
. The
dimension
of
F
is
\dim(F)=|B|-1
. Equivalently, if
F=W+v
, where
W
is a linear subspace and
v\in V
, then
\dim(F)=\dim(W)
.

An
affine transformation

T:V\rightarrow W
is a map of the form
T(x)=L(x)+b
where
L
is a linear map and
b\in W
is fixed. Affine transformations map affinely dependent sets to affinely dependent sets.
Two subsets are
affinely equivalent
if there is an affine isomorphism (invertible affine transformation) that maps one onto the other.

In the case of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
, the notion of an affine combination becomes much simpler.

Lemma 2.1
.

An affine combination of a set
S
in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
is exactly a sum of an odd number of elements of
S
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from the fact that the only scalars are the elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}=\{0,1\}
, and a sum of these scalars equals
1
if and only if there is an odd number of ones in this sum.
∎

It follows from the lemma above that the affine span of a set
S
in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
is the minimal superset of the
S
that is closed under sums of odd numbers of elements.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
. An
affine dependence relation
of elements of
S
is an expression of one element as an affine combination (i.e. sum of an odd number) of other elements, or any equation between sums of elements that can be rearranged into this form.

Remark 2.3
.

Since affine transformations map affinely dependent sets to affinely dependent sets, it follows that they preserve affine dependence relations. Hence if
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are affinely equivalent via some affine isomorphism
f
, then
f
maps the sets of all affine dependence relations in
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
to each other in a one-to-one fashion.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
.

(1)

The
dimension
of
S
is the dimension of
\aff(S)
.

(2)

A
basis
for
S
is a subset of
S
that forms an affine basis for
\aff(S)
.

(3)

The
dependent
set in
S
with respect to
B
is
D=S\setminus B
.

Remark 2.5
.

Note that if
S
is an
m
-dimensional subset with affine basis
B
, then
|B|=m+1
; i.e. an affine basis has one more element than the dimension of the subset.

We are primarily interested in the case where
S
is a
7
-dimensional subset
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{7}
of size
k
where
k\geq 10
. In this case
|B|=8
, and so
|D|=k-8\geq 2
.

Example 2.6
.

Let
S
be a set of size
10
with basis
B=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{8}\}
and
D=\{x,y\}
. Then
x
and
y
can each be written as a sum of an odd number of basis elements. Since
x,y\notin B
and
|B|=8
, each must be the sum of either three, five, or seven basis elements.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
be a subset with basis
B
and dependent set
D=\{x_{1},\ldots x_{r}\}
.

(1)

For
1\leq i\leq r
, the set
B_{i}
is the unique set of basis elements that sum to
x_{i}
.

(2)

For any
1\leq i_{j}\leq r
, we define
B_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{s}}=B_{i_{1}}\cap B_{i_{2}}\cdots\cap B_{i_{s}}
.

(3)

More generally, for any

to be the unique set of basis elements that sum to

.

Using the inclusion-exclusion principle, we can determine
|B_{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{s}}|
for any subset
\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{s}\}
of

with size

if we know the cardinalities of the every
B_{i_{1}\ldots i_{s}}
. Some of this numerical information is captured by the

and

of a set, defined below.

Definition 2.8
.

Let

be a set with basis

and dependent set

. We say

has

|B_{1}|\mbox{-}|B_{2}|\mbox{-}\cdots\mbox{-}|B_{m}|,

|B_{1}|\mbox{-}|B_{2}|\mbox{-}\cdots\mbox{-}|B_{r}|\mbox{-}(|B_{12}|,\ldots,|B_{1r}|,\ldots,|B_{(r-1)r}|),

where the pairs

.

Let

Remark 2.10
.

Note that if

Note that

Example 2.11
.

Suppose

Suppose further that

\displaystyle x_{1}

\displaystyle=a_{1}+\cdots+a_{5},

\displaystyle y_{1}

\displaystyle=b_{1}+\cdots+b_{5}

\displaystyle x_{2}

\displaystyle=a_{3}+\cdots+a_{7},

\displaystyle y_{2}

\displaystyle=b_{3}+\cdots+b_{7}.

Then we can build an affine isomorphism

between

The situation described in Example

will occur multiple times in this paper. We will often prove that, for a set with a basis of a certain type, we can always label the points so that the dependent elements have particular expressions as sums of basis elements. We will call this a

Lemma 2.12
.

If two subsets of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
have the same size and dimension, and have bases such that their dependent sets fit the same template, then the sets are affinely equivalent.

Remark 2.13
.

A surprising consequence of the results in this paper is that, for

Theorem 2.15

(Affine Basis Exchange Theorem)
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
be a set with basis
B
and dependent set
D=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{r}\}
.
Suppose that there exists
a\in(B_{1}\cap B_{2})\setminus(\cup_{i=3}^{r}B_{i})
.
Let
B^{\prime}=(B\setminus\{a\})\cup\{x_{2}\}
and

. Then:

(1)

B^{\prime}
is a basis for
S
with dependent set
D^{\prime}
.

(2)

B^{\prime}_{1}=(B_{1}\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}B_{2})\cup\{x_{2}\}
, where
\mathbin{\bigtriangleup}
denotes the symmetric difference operator.

(3)

B^{\prime}_{a}
=
B_{2}\setminus\{a\})\cup\{x_{2}\}
.

(4)

B^{\prime}_{i}=B_{i}
for

.

Proof.

Let
S
,
B
,
D
,

,
B^{\prime}
, and

be as in the statement of the theorem.

First note that
B^{\prime}
is also a basis for

because the elements of

are independent by definition, and

is independent of the elements of

since the only sum involving

also contains

.

We now give an explicit construction of the elements of

in terms of elements of
B^{\prime}
.
First, since

only appears in

and

. Thus,
B^{\prime}_{i}=B_{i}
for

.
Next, since

, we have

where
\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}\}=B_{2}\setminus\{a\}
.
Solving for

, we get

We have now written

3.
Quads and caps in
\AG(n,2)

The vector space
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
is a model for the finite affine geometry
\AG(n,2)
.
We now recall the key notions of

,

, and associated terms from
[
2

Definition 3.1
.

•

A
quad
in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
is a set of four elements that sum to zero, or equivalently an affine dependence relation among four elements.

•

A
cap

C
is a quad-free subset of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
.

•

The
first quad closure
of a subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
is

\qc_{1}(S)=S\cup\{x+y+z\mid\text{$x,y,z\in S$ are distinct}\}.

•

A cap
C
is
complete
if it is a maximal quad-free subset of
\aff(C)
, or equivalently if
\qc_{1}(C)=\aff(C)
.

•

A cap
C
is a
k
-cap
if
|C|=k
.

As we saw in the last section, a set
C
can be partitioned, in a non-unique way, into a basis
B
and a dependent set
D=C\setminus B
. Moreover, any element in
D
can be written uniquely as the sum of an odd number of basis elements. If
C
is a cap, then no four elements can sum to zero, so no element of
C
can be the sum of three basis elements.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
C
be a cap with basis
B
and dependent set
D
. Then each element of
D
can be written uniquely as a sum of an odd number of at least five elements of
B
.

Proof.

Let
C,B,D
be as in the lemma statement.
Let
x\in D
. Since
B
is a basis for
\aff(C)
, we know
x
can be written uniquely as an affine combination of elements of
B
. By Lemma
2.1
, this combination is a sum of an odd number of elements of
B
. This odd number cannot be one, since by assumption
x\notin B
. This odd number cannot be three either, for if
x=a+b+c
for distinct
a,b,c\in B
, then
a+b+c+x=\vec{0}
, so
\{a,b,c,x\}
is a quad in the cap
C
, a contradiction. Thus, the odd number must be at least five.
∎

-cap in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
.

(1)

If

(2)

4.
Caps of dimension
7

We now prove some general properties of
7
-dimensional caps, which will be used throughout the paper.

For the following lemmas, let

be a
7
-dimensional cap in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
with basis
B
and dependent set

.
Note that by Lemma
3.2

can be written uniquely as the sum of exactly five or seven basis elements.

First, we show that two dependent elements cannot both be sums of seven basis elements.

Lemma 4.1
.

At most one point in
D
can be a sum of seven basis elements.

Proof.

Suppose
x,y\in D
are both sums of seven basis elements. Then we can write
B=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{8}\}
such that

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=a_{1}+\cdots+a_{7},

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=a_{2}+\cdots+a_{8}.

Then
x+y=a_{1}+a_{8}
, which implies that
C
contains a quad, a contradiction. Hence
x
and
y
cannot both be sums of 7 basis elements.
∎

The next lemma shows that two sums of five must share either two or three basis elements.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in D
be sums of five basis elements. Then
|B_{12}|
equals
2
or
3
.

Proof.

By the inclusion-exclusion principle we have

8=|B|\geq|B_{1}\cup B_{2}|=|B_{1}|+|B_{2}|-|B_{12}|=10-|B_{12}|,

so
|B_{12}|\geq 10-8=2
.

Now suppose that
|B_{12}|=4
.
Then we can write
B=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{8}\}
such that

\displaystyle x_{1}

\displaystyle=a_{1}+\ldots+a_{5},

\displaystyle x_{2}

\displaystyle=a_{1}+\ldots+a_{4}+a_{6},

so
B_{12}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{4}\}
.
Then
x_{1}+x_{2}=a_{5}+a_{6}
which implies that
C
contains a quad, contradicting the fact that
C
is a cap. Hence
|B_{12}|\neq 4
.

Finally, since
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
we have
B_{1}\neq B_{2}
, so
|B_{12}|\neq 5
.

It follows that
|B_{12}|
equals
2
or
3
.
∎

The following lemma will be useful for constructing caps throughout the rest of the paper.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
, and
x_{3}
be distinct elements of

. Then
|B_{1}\cup B_{2}\cup B_{3}|=8
.

Proof.

Suppose
|B_{1}\cup B_{2}\cup B_{3}|\leq 7
. Then the subcap

with basis
B^{\prime}=B_{1}\cup B_{2}\cup B_{3}
and dependent set

has dimension at most

. But by Proposition
3.3
, since

is a cap of dimension at most

, we have

. This is a contradiction since
|D^{\prime}|=3
. Hence it must be that

.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in D
with
|B_{1}|=7
and
|B_{2}|=5
. Then
|B_{12}|=4
.

Proof.

By inclusion-exclusion, we have

8=|B|\geq|B_{1}\cup B_{1}|=|B_{1}|+|B_{2}|-|B_{12}|=12-|B_{12}|,

so
|B_{12}|\geq 12-8=4

Suppose

Then we can write
B=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{8}\}
such that

\displaystyle x_{1}

\displaystyle=a_{1}+\ldots+a_{7},

\displaystyle x_{2}

\displaystyle=a_{1}+\ldots+a_{5}.

But then

, which implies
C
contains a quad, a contradiction. Thus

.
∎

Corollary 5.5
.

There are two equivalence classes of 7 dimensional

Let
C
be a
10
-cap of dimension
7
. By Theorem
5.4
,
C
has a basis

8.

In this last section, we prove that there are no

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
C
be a
13
-cap of dimension
7
with basis
B
. Then

must have type
5\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}5

|B_{ij}|=2
for two disjoint pairs

Let
C

B
be as in the theorem statement. Since

, by Lemma
3.2
each element of the dependent set

is a sum of five or seven basis elements, and by Lemma
4.1
, at most one of is a sum of seven basis elements. Therefore

must have type
5\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}5
or
7\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}5

Suppose
B
has type
7\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}5
. Observe that for any pair
\{j,k\}\subseteq\{2,3,4,5\}
, the set
C_{1jk}=B\cup\{x_{1},x_{j},x_{k}\}
is an
11
-cap of dimension
7
with basis type
7\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}5
. By Lemma
6.1
, the extended type of such a cap must be

. This implies that

|B_{23}|=|B_{34}|=|B_{45}|=3.

Then
C_{2345}=B\cup\{x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5}\}
is a
12
-cap of dimension
7
with basis
B
of extended type
5\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}(3,3,3,3,3,3)
. By Lemma
7.2
, this is impossible. Therefore the basis

must have type
5\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}5
.

Now consider the numbers
|B_{ij}|
for pairs
\{i,j\}
in

4.2
we know that each of these numbers is
2
or
3
. If none or exactly one of these numbers is

is a
12
-cap of dimension

with basis

7.2
, this is impossible, so it must be that at least two of these numbers are

Applying Proposition
7.3
to each
12

, we conclude that if

Now we prove that no
13

Theorem 8.2
.

There are no
7
-dimensional
13
-caps.

Proof.

Let
C
be a
13
-cap of dimension
7
. By Theorem
8.1
, we know
C
has a basis
B
of type
5\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}5\mbox{-}5
, where
|B_{ij}|=2
for two disjoint pairs

|B_{ij}|=3

for each

2

\sum|B_{i}|=5(5)=25\quad\text{and}\quad\sum|B_{ij}|=2(2)+8(3)=28.

Suppose without loss of generality that
|B_{12}|=|B_{34}|=2

For any triple
\{i,j,k\}

11

C_{ijk}=B\cup\{x_{i},x_{j},x_{k}\}
to find that

|B_{ijk}|=\begin{cases}1&\text{if one of $|B_{ij}|,|B_{ik}|,|B_{jk}|$ is $2$;}\\

There are
\binom{5}{3}=10

For any quadruple

\displaystyle|B_{12345}|

\displaystyle=\left|\bigcup B_{i}\right|-\sum|B_{i}|+\sum|B_{ij}|-\sum|B_{ijk}|+\sum|B_{ijkl}|
