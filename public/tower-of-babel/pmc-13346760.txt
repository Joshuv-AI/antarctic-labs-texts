Associations between body fat percentage estimated by the CUN-BAE equation and cardiometabolic risk indicators in adults with intellectual disabilities
Authors: Claudio Farías-Valenzuela, Manuel Ortiz-Franco, Emilio Jofré-Saldía, Alexis Espinoza-Salinas, Sebastián Espoz-Lazo, Paloma Ferrero-Hernández, Eduardo R de Victo, Caroline Brand, Josivaldo de Souza-Lima, Gerson Ferrari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdults with intellectual disabilities (ID) often exhibit higher adiposity , increasing their risk of cardiometabolic conditions. Therefore, simple and non-invasive methods for estimating body fat in this population are needed. This study aimed to examine the associations between body fat percentage (BF%) estimated by the Clínic Universidad de Navarra–Body Adiposity Estimator (CUN-BAE) equation and cardiometabolic risk indicators in adults with ID. This cross-sectional study included 133 adults with ID (mean age 23.0 ± 6.7 years; 69.9% were men) from five special education centers in Santiago, Chile. BF% was estimated using the CUN-BAE equation. Cardiometabolic indicators included body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), neck circumference (NC), waist-to-height ratio (WtHR), and submandibular skinfold (SS thickness). Mean BF% was 15.6% in men and 27.7% in women, respectively. Significant positive associations were observed between BF% and BMI (men: β = 0.77, R² = 0.77; women: β = 0.54, R² = 0.52), WC (men: β = 0.29, R² = 0.56; women: β = 0.21, R² = 0.31), NC (men: β = 0.95, R² = 0.43; women: β = 0.57, R² = 0.17), WtHR (men: β = 53.02, R² = 0.62; women: β = 27.54, R² = 0.26), and SS (men: β = 0.99, R² = 0.45; women: β = 1.15, R² = 0.49). Overall, BF% estimated by the CUN-BAE equation demonstrated consistent relationships with multiple cardiometabolic indicators in both sexes. These findings suggest that the CUN-BAE equation is a practical tool for estimating body fat in adults with ID; however, further validation against direct measures of body composition is warranted.Keywords: Intellectual disability, Obesity, Body fat, Disability, Cardiometabolic risk, Body compositionSubject terms: Diseases, Endocrinology, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

People with intellectual disabilities (ID) represent a small proportion of individuals with disabilities, accounting for approximately 1–3% of the global population1. This population faces specific health-related challenges, including lower levels of physical fitness and a higher likelihood of overweight and obesity. The prevalence of overweight among adults with ID ranges from 52.0% to 67.2%2, affects up to 38.3%, which is higher than the 28% reported in the general population3.

Obesity is associated with numerous adverse health outcomes, increasing the risk of conditions related to metabolic syndrome, such as insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and hypertension4. Among individuals with ID, these negative effects are often exacerbated by reduced physical functioning, early declines in functional capacity, and a higher risk of dependency5.

In the assessment of obesity in populations with ID, various anthropometric methods have been used, including three-dimensional scanners6, bioelectrical impedance analysis7, and skinfolds measurements8. Although these methods are valid and accurate, their use often requires expensive equipment and trained personnel, limiting their widespread applicability. Therefore, there is a need for accessible and easy-to-use tools that can be reliably implemented across diverse settings.

In this context, several widely used anthropometric methods are available to assess cardiometabolic risk. These methods are valued for their simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and applicability in large-scale studies, and include indicators such as body mass index (BMI)9, waist circumference (WC)10, and waist-to-height ratio (WtHR)11. These measures are well-established as accessible and effective indicators for the early detection of obesity-related and metabolic risk factors. Due to their simplicity and practicality, these methods have also been applied to individuals with ID, facilitating the monitoring of cardiometabolic risk without the need for sophisticated equipment or highly specialized personnel12,13.

More recently, additional anthropometric indicators with similar practical advantages have been proposed, such as submandibular skinfold (SS)14 and neck circumference (NC)15, both of which have been validated as measures of adiposity and cardiometabolic risk in adults. Furthermore, previous studies have reported sex-related differences in cardiometabolic risk indicators among adolescents with ID16, as well as in body fat percentage (BF%) among adults with ID7, with higher values observed in women.

Based on these methods, several equations have been developed to estimate body fat percentage (BF%) using basic anthropometric measurements. For example, Bertapelli et al.17,18 developed equations to estimate BF% from BMI in pediatric and adolescent populations with ID. Similarly, the equation proposed by Gómez- Ambrosi et al.19, known as the Clínic Universidad de Navarra–Body Adiposity Estimator (CUN-BAE), has been widely used in various populations and has demonstrated utility, although it has not yet been validated in individuals with ID. A study by Chen et al.20 reported that the CUN-BAE equation may better predict multimorbidity than traditional cardiometabolic risk indicators such as BMI, WC, and WtHR. Similarly, a recent study in a representative sample of Chilean adults found that BF% estimated using the CUN-BAE equation was significantly associated with BMI and WC, with strong correlations observed in both men and women21. However, that study did not include individuals with ID.

Given this gap, it is reasonable to expect that BF% estimated by the CUN-BAE equation is associated with common anthropometric indicators of cardiometabolic risk in Chilean adults with ID, regardless of sex. Therefore, the present study aims to examine the associations between BF% estimated by the CUN-BAE equation and cardiometabolic risk indicators in adults with ID.

Methods

Study design and sample

This study was a cross-sectional, descriptive quantitative investigation. Data were obtained from the “Ludoinclusión®” project22, developed under the Vice-Rector’s Office for Outreach (Vicerrectoría de Vinculación con el Medio [VIME]) at the University of Santiago of Chile.

To determine statistical power based on the sample size of 133 participants, an analysis was conducted using G*Power version 3.1. The analysis was based on a bivariate correlation test assuming a normal distribution, with an alpha level of 0.05, a large effect size (r = 0.50; d = 1.15; f = 0.57), and a two-tailed test. The results indicated a statistical power (1 − β) of 0.98, suggesting a high probability of detecting significant associations.

From an initial sample of 156 participants, 23 were excluded due to failure to meet eligibility criteria or incomplete data. Finally, this study included 133 adults with ID, of whom 69.9% were male. The mean age of participants was 23.00 years (SD = 6.67). The sample was selected through convenience sampling from five special education centers in Santiago, Chile. The use of convenience sampling was justified by limited access to adults with ID in community settings and the need to conduct assessments within structured institutional environments. All individuals with complete anthropometric data who met the predefined inclusion criteria were included, and no additional selection procedures were applied.

The inclusion criteria were: male or female adults aged ≥ 18 years; a diagnosis of mild (IQ 50–69) or moderate (IQ 35–49) ID based on the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale-IV (WAIS-IV)23; independent mobility; regular participation in physical education activities; and absence of physical disability. Exclusion criteria included: severe or profound ID (IQ < 35), a diagnosis of Down syndrome and/or Prader–Willi syndrome, dependence in performing motor tasks, incomplete anthropometric assessments, or wheelchair use.

Institutional approval was obtained through the directors or coordinators of each participating center. Guardians provided informed consent for participation. The study was conducted in accordance with established ethical guidelines for research involving human participants24 and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Granada (Approval No. 2052 / CEIH / 2021).

Procedure

Data collection was conducted in April 2022 and 2023. Initial contact with directors and tutors occurred in March of each year. A meeting was held to explain study procedures, including objectives and evaluation protocols. During this meeting, parents and tutors reviewed and signed informed consent forms, ensuring voluntary and anonymous participation, with the option to withdraw at any time.

Assessments were scheduled during morning and afternoon sessions. Each participant was accompanied by a tutor who provided assistance as needed. To standardize procedures, evaluators underwent training prior to data collection. Anthropometric measurements were obtained in duplicate by two trained evaluators, both health professionals, following the standardized protocols of the International Society for the Advancement of Kinanthropometry (ISAK)25.The mean of the 2 measurements was used for analysis. Detailed procedures have been described elsewhere26,27. Although technical error of measurement and intraclass correlation coefficients were not calculated, standardized protocols, calibrated instruments, and previously validated procedures with high interrater reliability were used to ensure measurement consistency. Measurements were obtained in the following order: (i) body weight; (ii) height; (iii) WC; (iv) NC; (v) SS; (vi) BMI; and (vii) WtHR. Data were categorized by sex, considering participants’ age and sex.

Body fat percentage

Body weight (kg) and height (cm) were measured with participants barefoot and wearing light clothing using a calibrated digital scale with an integrated stadiometer (SECA®, model 769, Hamburg, Germany). Height was measured with participants standing in deep inspiration and with the head positioned in the Frankfurt plane. BF% was estimated using the CUN-BAE Eqs. 19,20:

BF% = – 44.988 + (0.503 × age) + (10.689 × sex) + (3.172 × BMI) – (0.026 × BMI2) + (0.181 × BMI × sex) – (0.02 × BMI × age) – (0.005 × BMI2 × sex) + (0.00021 × BMI 2 × age).

In this equation, sex was coded as 0 for men and 1 for women. Age was recorded in years, and BMI was calculated using Quetelet’s index (weight [kg]/height² [m²])28.

Cardiometabolic risk indicators

BMI (kg/m²) was calculated using the standard formula: weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²).

WC and NC were measured in centimeters using a non-extensible metal tape (CESCORF®, Porto Alegre, Brazil). WC was measured at the midpoint between the lower rib margin and the iliac crest at the end of normal expiration29. NC was measured with the participant standing in a relaxed position, with the head in the Frankfurt plane, just below the laryngeal prominence and perpendicular to the neck axis14. Given the characteristics of individuals with ID, additional verbal instructions, visual demonstrations, and tutor assistance were provided to ensure proper positioning, particularly for height measurement. Participants unable to complete all measurements were excluded from analyses.

WtHR was calculated as WC (cm)/height (cm). SS (mm) was measured using a Slim Guide caliper (Rosscraft, Surrey, Canada), following established protocols14.

Statistical analysis

Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Descriptive statistics are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR), depending on data distribution.

Comparisons between men and women were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for non-parametric variables and the independent samples Student’s t-test for parametric variables.

Correlations between BF% (CUN-BAE) and cardiometabolic indicators were assessed using Pearson’s (r) or Spearman’s (rho) coefficients, as appropriate. Correlation strength was classified as trivial (< 0.09), small (0.10–0.29), moderate (0.30–0.49), high (0.50–0.69), very high (0.70–0.89), or almost perfect (> 0.90)30.

Univariate linear regression models were used to examine associations between BF% and each cardiometabolic indicator. Regression assumptions were evaluated based on standardized residuals, with no major violations observed. To avoid multicollinearity, multivariable models including BMI and CUN-BAE simultaneously were not performed. Instead, separate univariate models were fitted, consistent with the study objective of assessing associations rather than independent effects. Results are presented as regression coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and coefficients of determination (R²).

All analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and JASP version 0.19.3 (Amsterdam, Netherlands). A two-sided significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted.

Results

The final sample included 133 participants with ID (n = 93 men; n = 40 women), with a mean age of 23.00 years (SD = 6.67). Significant sex differences (p < 0.05) were observed for selected cardiometabolic indicators, particularly NC and SS. BF% estimated using the CUN-BAE equation was 15.62% in men and 27.65% in women, with women showing a significantly higher BF% (+ 12.03%; p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Univariate linear regression models were used to examine the association between BF% (CUN-BAE) and cardiometabolic indicators, stratified by sex. Significant associations (p < 0.001) were found between BF% and BMI in both men (β = 0.77; 95%CI: 0.69–0.86; R² = 0.77) and women (β = 0.54; 95%CI: 0.37–0.70; R² = 0.52). Among men, strong associations were also observed for NC (β = 0.95; 95%CI: 0.72–1.17; R² = 0.43) and WtHR (β = 53.02; 95%CI: 44.44–61.60; R² = 0.62). In women, WC (β = 0.21; 95%CI: 0.10–0.31; R² = 0.31) and SS (β = 1.15; 95%CI: 0.77–1.53; R² = 0.49) showed the strongest associations.

In the total sample, each 1% increase in BF% was associated with increases of 0.73 kg/m² in BMI, 0.25 cm in WC, 0.35 cm in NC, 49 units in WtHR, and 1.33 mm in SS (Table 2).

Discussion

This study found that BF% estimated using the CUN-BAE equation was positively associated with all evaluated cardiometabolic risk indicators (BMI, WC, NC, WtHR, and SS) in both men and women with ID.

Mean BF% was lower than previously reported values in individuals with ID31, which may be explained by differences in age, associated syndromes, physical activity levels, and assessment methods. Our findings are consistent with those of Rimmer & Yamaki(30), who reported lower BF% among individuals with ID living in institutional settings compared with those living with their families. Institutional environments may promote structured routines, increased social participation, and reduced health-related barriers32, potentially contributing to the lower adiposity observed in this sample.

Previous studies have shown that anthropometric indicators such as BMI, WC, and NC are positively associated with cardiometabolic risk and are useful for obesity monitoring33. For example, Ferrero-Hernández et al. 14 reported significant associations among BMI, WC, NC, WtHR, and SS in individuals with ID. However, it is important to note that this study included children, adolescents, and adults, and therefore its findings should be interpreted with caution when applied specifically to adult populations. Additionally, composite indices derived from anthropometric measures have been proposed as alternative approaches to estimate body fat and cardiometabolic risk13.

In the present study, positive associations between BF% and cardiometabolic indicators were observed, particularly in men, with the exception of SS, which showed stronger associations in women. These findings support previous evidence indicating that the CUN-BAE equation is a practical and feasible method for estimating adiposity across populations19,20.

The CUN-BAE equation is derived from BMI, a well-established predictor of cardiometabolic risk34 and obesity in individuals with ID35. This supports its use as a simple and accessible proxy for body composition. For instance, Bertapelli et al.17 validated the Deurenberg equation against dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA)36, reporting strong correlations between BF% and BMI (r = 0.91). Similarly, our findings demonstrated strong correlations between BF% (CUN-BAE) and BMI in both sexes. However, not all studies support the use of BMI as an adiposity indicator in individuals with ID. Polfuss et al.37 reported discrepancies between BMI and DXA-derived fat estimates in children with Down syndrome and spina bifida, suggesting that accuracy may vary across populations and conditions.

Importantly, accumulating evidence suggests that CUN-BAE–derived BF% is not only a surrogate of adiposity but also a clinically relevant marker of cardiometabolic risk. Prospective findings from the Hordaland Health Study showed that CUN-BAE–estimated BF% is significantly associated with future cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes risk, with stronger associations observed in sex-stratified analyses38. In addition, higher CUN-BAE values have been linked to increased cardiovascular mortality and multimorbidity, reinforcing its potential utility as a cardiovascular risk indicator39. Furthermore, longitudinal evidence indicates that CUN-BAE–derived adiposity is independently associated with incident atrial fibrillation, highlighting its relevance for cardiovascular risk prediction beyond traditional anthropometric measures40.

The weaker correlations observed between BF% and indicators such as WC and WtHR in women highlight the importance of sex-specific differences in fat distribution. These differences may be related to lower central fat accumulation in women and sex-specific hormonal and physiological patterns41. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies reporting sex-based disparities in adiposity distribution and related health outcomes42.

Farias-Valenzuela et al.16 reported that women with ID present higher WtHR and lower physical performance, including reduced handgrip strength and poorer functional test results. Similarly, Pataky et al.43 showed that overweight and obesity are associated with reduced functional capacity, including impaired mobility and lower performance in the 6-minute walk test. Together, these findings reinforce the link between higher adiposity and reduced physical function.

These results highlight the need for simple, non-invasive, and cost-effective tools to assess body composition in clinical and educational settings. Compared with more complex methods such as bioelectrical impedance or three-dimensional scanners, which require specialized equipment and trained personnel44,45, the CUN-BAE equation represents a practical and time-efficient alternative for identifying obesity-related risk in individuals with ID.

The CUN-BAE equation has been previously validated against reference methods for body composition assessment, including DXA, in general populations, demonstrating strong correlations with directly measured BF% (e.g., r ≈ 0.88–0.89)38,46,47. These findings support its use as a practical and accessible estimator of adiposity in epidemiological studies. However, to date, validation studies in individuals with ID remain limited or absent. Therefore, the interpretation of BF% estimated by the CUN-BAE equation in this population should be made with caution, and further research comparing its performance against gold-standard methods, such as DXA, is warranted.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, the use of convenience sampling and the inclusion of only Chilean adults with ID limit the generalizability of the findings. Third, the absence of direct body composition measures (e.g., DXA) prevents validation of BF% estimates. Additionally, data on medication use that may influence body composition were not collected, which may have affected the results. Finally, the lack of standardized cut-off points for cardiometabolic risk indicators in adults with ID may limit the interpretation of findings.

Despite these limitations, this study has notable strengths, including the focus on an underrepresented population, sex-stratified analyses, and the use of multiple anthropometric indicators. These findings may contribute to improving health monitoring in adults with ID. Future studies should evaluate the applicability of the CUN-BAE equation across different levels of ID, specific syndromes, and social contexts, and incorporate direct body composition measures to further validate its use.

Conclusion

BF% estimated using the CUN-BAE equation was positively associated with all cardiometabolic risk indicators (BMI, WC, NC, WtHR, and SS) in both men and women with ID. These findings support the use of the CUN-BAE equation as a practical, accessible, and low-cost tool for estimating body fat and identifying cardiometabolic risk in this population. Further studies are warranted to validate its accuracy against direct body composition methods.
