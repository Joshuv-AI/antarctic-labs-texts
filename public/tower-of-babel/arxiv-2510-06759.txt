Fast-forwardable Lindbladians imply quantum phase estimation
Zhong-Xia Shang, Naixu Guo, Patrick Rebentrost, Alán Aspuru-Guzik, Tongyang Li, Qi Zhao

Abstract
Quantum phase estimation (QPE) and Lindbladian dynamics are both foundational in quantum information science and central to quantum algorithm design. In this work, we bridge these two concepts: certain simple Lindbladian processes can be adapted to perform QPE-type tasks. However, unlike QPE, which achieves Heisenberg-limit scaling, these Lindbladian evolutions are restricted to standard quantum limit complexity. This indicates that, different from Hamiltonian dynamics, the natural dissipative evolution speed of such Lindbladians does not saturate the fundamental quantum limit, thereby suggesting the potential for quadratic fast-forwarding. We confirm this by presenting a quantum algorithm that simulates these Lindbladians for time t t within an error ε \varepsilon using 𝒪 ⁡ ( t ​ log ⁡ ( ε − 1 ) ) \mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{t\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}\right) cost, whose mechanism is fundamentally different from the fast-forwarding examples of Hamiltonian dynamics. As a bonus, this fast-forwarded simulation naturally serves as a new Heisenberg-limit QPE algorithm. Therefore, our work explicitly bridges the standard quantum limit-Heisenberg limit transition to the fast-forwarding of dissipative dynamics. We also adopt our fast-forwarding algorithm for efficient Gibbs state preparation and demonstrate the counter-intuitive implication: the allowance of a quadratically accelerated decoherence effect under arbitrary Pauli noise.



Introduction.—
Quantum algorithms leverage the properties of quantum mechanics to achieve acceleration over classical rivals
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
. Quantum phase estimation (QPE)
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
;
Kitaev 1995
is a fundamental primitive at the core of many quantum algorithms
Dalzell et al. 2023
, enabling the estimation of eigenvalues and the preparation of eigenstates for unitary operators. QPE and its various variants
Wiebe and Granade 2016
;
Ding and Lin 2023
;
Higgins et al. 2007
serve as the foundation for numerous significant quantum applications, such as factoring large numbers
Shor 1994
, solving linear systems of equations
Harrow et al. 2009
, and estimating ground state energies of quantum Hamiltonians in condensed matter physics and quantum chemistry
McArdle et al. 2020
.

Simulating quantum dynamics
Feynman 2018
is one of the most important applications for quantum computing. QPE has deep connections with Hamiltonian dynamics, which can estimate eigenvalues and prepare eigenstates of a Hamiltonian
H
by querying Hamiltonian dynamics
e^{-iHt}
. To estimate eigenvalues within an error
\epsilon
, the required total Hamiltonian evolution time is
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
, which is known to achieve the Heisenberg limit
Zwierz et al. 2010
, a fundamental limit set by the energy-time uncertainty principle of quantum mechanics
Busch et al. 2007
;
Kijowski 1974
. This fundamental limit implies the Hamiltonian no fast-forwarding theorem
Atia and Aharonov 2017
;
Berry et al. 2007
, which explains why existing universal Hamiltonian simulation algorithms
Lloyd 1996
;
Berry et al. 2015
;
Low and Chuang 2017
generally have
\Omega(t)
complexity scalings.

Quantum dynamics is not restricted to closed systems. Lindbladians
Lindblad 1976
describe the dissipative dynamics of open quantum systems and havee recently been shown to be a transformative quantum algorithm primitive for various critical tasks, including preparations for the Gibbs states
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Chen et al. 2023b
;
Rouzé et al. 2024
and ground state
Ding et al. 2024
, optimizations
Chen et al. 2025
, optimal control
He et al. 2024
, and solving differential equations
Shang et al. 2024
. Given the importance of Lindbladian simulations
Kliesch et al. 2011
;
Cleve and Wang 2017
;
Li and Wang 2022
;
Ding et al. 2025
and the deep connections between QPE and Hamiltonian dynamics, it is therefore natural and important to explore the connections between QPE and Lindbladians and their implications for the Lindbladian fast-forwarding. Moreover, as quantum algorithms based on QPE and Lindbladians seem to be rather different, understanding their connections will also be vital for bridging unitary and dissipative quantum techniques and recognizing the strengths and weaknesses of these two primitives compared to each other.

In this work, we discover interesting and non-trivial connections between QPE and Lindbladians. Specifically, we demonstrate that there exist simple, purely dissipative Lindbladians whose simulation can be utilized for QPE tasks, estimating eigenvalues and preparing eigenstates of Hamiltonians. However, this Lindbladian-based QPE only achieves a standard quantum limit scaling
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
and thus, has not yet reached the minimal energy-time uncertainty. In other words, the natural dynamics of these open quantum systems do not fully exploit quantum advantages with respect to the estimation accuracy. This observation motivates the prospect of quadratic fast-forwarding for such Lindbladian dynamics.

Indeed, we propose a quantum algorithm that can simulate these Lindbladians of evolution time
\mathcal{O}(t)
with a complexity of only
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{t})
. Compared with the commonly used dilated Hamiltonian approach (see explanations in Ref.
Cleve and Wang 2017
) for Lindbladian simulation, our algorithm also has exponential advantages in terms of accuracy and number of ancilla qubits. While in the context of Hamiltonian dynamics, several special examples that enable fast forwarding have been found
Atia and Aharonov 2017
;
Gu et al. 2021
, to the best of our knowledge, this work gives the first example of Lindbladian fast forwarding. Notably, the reason for Lindbladian fast forwarding here is the non-reachability of the Heisenberg limit, which is in stark contrast to Hamiltonian cases, whose reason is that their special structures enable overcoming the Heisenberg limit
Atia and Aharonov 2017
.

Since our algorithm realizes quadratically fast-forwarding for Lindbladians that enable QPE tasks, it directly leads to a Lindbladian-based Heisenberg-limit (
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
) QPE. Therefore, we successfully bridge the standard quantum limit-Heisenberg limit transition to the fast-forwarding of dissipative dynamics. More interestingly, the fast-forwarding algorithm also unveils a surprising understanding of quadratic quantum speedup that seems to have nothing to do with quantum. The core of our fast-forwarding simulation algorithm is that the expected translation distance of
N
-step (
t
)
classical
random walk
Xia et al. 2019
is
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N})
(
\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{t}\right)
). Since QPE accounts for the Grover-type
Grover 1996
quantum advantage through amplitude estimation
Brassard et al. 2000
, thus, the
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N})
spreading of the
classical
random walk becomes exactly the source of quadratic quantum speedup.

Besides these main results, we also show that our fast-forwarding simulation algorithm can be applied to quantum Gibbs state preparations, which can match the state-of-the-art scaling in quantum singular value transformation
Gilyén et al. 2019
approach. Moreover, we find that our fast-forwarding simulation algorithm also works for a much broader class of Lindbladians, which we call the Choi commuting Lindbladians, that include arbitrary combinations of Pauli noise. This indicates that the decoherence effect under arbitrary Pauli noise can actually occur quadratically faster.

We want to mention here another work of Lindbladian fast-forwarding
Shang et al. 2025
. The fast-forwarding there is only for circuit depth, while keeping the query complexity or the total cost unchanged through increasing the number of ancilla qubits. In contrast, in this work, the Lindbladian fast-forwarding considers the total cost.

The

. This result also leads to the no-fast-forwarding theorem for Hamiltonian simulation

Atia and Aharonov 2017

In contrast with Hamiltonian dynamics (Schrödinger equation) describing closed quantum systems, the dynamics of open quantum systems with weak system-environment interactions can be described by Lindbladian dynamics
Lindblad 1976
, which takes the form

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathcal{L}[\rho(t)]

(4)

\displaystyle=-i[H_{I},\rho(t)]+\sum_{i}\left(F_{i}\rho(t)F_{i}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\rho(t),F_{i}^{\dagger}F_{i}\}\right),

where
\rho(t)
is the system density matrix,
H_{I}
is the internal Hamiltonian, and
F_{i}
are quantum jump operators describing dissipative interactions between the system and the environment. While the Lindbladian is no longer a unitary operation, it is still a completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) map
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
. The Lindbladian in Eq. (
4
) can be simulated through the dilated Hamiltonian simulation approach (see SM
C
). For example, consider a Lindbladian with
H_{I}=0
, and there is only a single jump operator

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathcal{L}_{F}[\rho(t)]=F\rho(t)F^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\rho(t),F^{\dagger}F\},

(5)

then the short-time dynamics of the dilated Hamiltonian
\tilde{F}=\begin{pmatrix}0&F^{\dagger}\\
F&0\end{pmatrix}
will approximate the short-time Lindbladian dynamics

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{a}\left(e^{-i\tilde{F}\sqrt{\tau}}(|0\rangle\langle 0|\otimes\rho(t))e^{i\tilde{F}\sqrt{\tau}}\right)\approx\rho(t+\tau).

(6)

Therefore, by repeatedly adding ancilla qubits and running short-time
\tilde{F}
dynamics, we are able to approximately simulate Eq. (
5
). For complexity, to simulate the Lindbladian Eq. (
5
) for time
t
within error
\varepsilon
, the required dilated Hamiltonian simulation time and number of ancilla qubits are

Hami.simu.time

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(t^{2}\varepsilon^{-1}),

(7)

Number of ancilla qubits

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(t^{3}\varepsilon^{-2}).

(8)

Note that more advanced Lindbladian simulation algorithms

;
Li and Wang 2022
can achieve
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(t\text{poly}\log(\varepsilon^{-1}))
and Ref.
Childs and Li 2016

Lindbladian as slow QPE.—
At first glance, coherent QPE and dissipative Lindbladians seem to be unrelated. However, our key observation is that if we set the jump operator

5

H

Quadratic fast-forwarding simulation.—
Since the natural evolution of the Lindbladian Eq. (

\displaystyle(\textbf{H}\otimes I)e^{-i\tilde{H}\sqrt{\tau}}|0\rangle|\psi\rangle=

Our Lindbladian fast-forwarding unveils non-trivial differences between unitary and dissipative dynamics (Fig.

Additional results.—
We provide two additional results.

Quantum Gibbs state preparation:
It turns out that our Lindbladian fast-forwarding algorithm can also be used for quantum Gibbs state preparation. Given a positive semidefinite Hamiltonian
H_{P}
with
\|H_{P}\|\leq 1
, our goal is to prepare the Gibbs state
\rho_{\beta}=e^{-\beta H_{P}}/Z_{\beta}
at the inverse temperature
\beta
with
Z_{\beta}=\mathrm{Tr}(e^{-\beta H_{P}})
the partition function. Based on Ref.
Shang et al. 2024

F=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{H_{P}}\otimes I&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}
, one can eventualy prepare the purification

of

. Since
F
is Hermitian, which satisfies our fast-forwarding requirements, as a result, through the state-of-the-art Hamiltonian simulation algorithm

, to prepare

within an error

is

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2^{n}}{Z_{\beta}}\beta\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}\right),

(19)

for
\beta>\log^{3}(\varepsilon^{-1})
. This result matches the previous state-of-the-art results shown in QSVT
Gilyén et al. 2019

Since their complexity mainly relies on the mixing time of the Lindbladian, which can vary greatly from case to case, we currently don’t know how to make a fair comparison with those results.

Choi commuting Lindbladians and Pauli noise:
In fact, our quadratic fast-forwarding algorithm can be applied to a much broader class of Lindbladians beyond Eq. (
9
).
For the following Lindbladian

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\sum_{i}\left(H_{i}\rho(t)H_{i}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\rho(t),H_{i}^{\dagger}H_{i}\}\right),

(20)

as long as
H_{i}
are Hermitian and
[H_{i}\otimes H_{i}^{*},H_{j}\otimes H_{j}^{*}]=0~\forall\,i,j
, we allow to achieve a fast-forwarding simulation with the required Hamiltonian simulation time also of order
\mathcal{O}\left(t^{1/2}\log^{1/2}(\varepsilon^{-1})\right)
. We name such Lindbladians the Choi commuting Lindbladians (See SM
I
). Importantly, for any Pauli operators
P_{i}
and

, we have
[P_{i}\otimes P_{i}^{*},P_{j}\otimes P_{j}^{*}]=0
satisfies the definiton of Choi commuting Lindbladians. Therefore, we obtain the surprising result that for any combination of Pauli noises, the resulting decoherence effect can actually happen in a quadratically faster speed. Since Pauli noise is the standard error model in the quantum error correction community
Lidar and Brun 2013
, our results suggest the potential applications of using quantum computers for exploring noisy quantum systems. Besides Pauli noise, such Choi commuting Lindbladians also cover other important classes such as maximal dephasing quantum master equations
Fagnola et al. 2019
.

Summary and outlook.—
In summary, we find a deep connection between QPE and Lindbladians, and its implications to fast-forwarding. Theorems
1
-
3
are logically self-consistent. Lindbladian Eq. (
9
) for QPE only gives a standard quantum limit scaling (Theorem
1
), which indicates the possibility of quadratic fast-forwarding (Theorem
2
). The fast-forwarding algorithm, in turn, improves QPE based on Eq. (
9
) to the Heisenberg limit (Theorem
3
). We unveil the stark differences of fast-forwarding in Hamiltonian and Lindbladian dynamics and give a new way of achieving quadratic quantum speedup stemming from the concentration of a classical random walk. We also show how to utilize the Lindbladian fast-forwarding for quantum Gibbs state preparations and its generalizations to Choi commuting Lindbladians, including arbitrary combinations of Pauli noise.

There are several interesting future directions. First, we can ask whether there are other types of Lindbladian fast-forwarding that can go beyond quadratic to exponential based on different mechanisms. Second, in SM
F
, we show that our Lindbladian fast QPE algorithm has something to do with the Kravchuk polynomial
Krawtchouk 1929
, which might draw special interest. Third, since the Lindbladian Eq. (
9
) we considered can help prepare diagonal quantum states on the eigenbasis of
H
, it naturally catalyzes the quantum Mpemba effect
Moroder et al. 2024
: an exponential speedup towards the thermal equalibrism can be achieved for diagonal input states compared to states with coherence on the eigenbasis of
H
. Further studies in this direction could lead to improvements on recent Lindbladian-based quantum Gibbs state preparation algorithms
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Chen et al. 2023b
;
Rouzé et al. 2024
. Forth, we hope that this work can inspire more research on the comparison between dissipative and coherent quantum algorithms, especially their advantages and disadvantages in different tasks. Finally, it is exciting to explore more quantum algorithms based on Lindbladians and open quantum systems, where new types of quantum speedup might be discovered.

Appendix A
Setup and preliminaries

In this work, the
n
-qubit Hamiltonian we consider has the form

\displaystyle H=\sum_{\alpha}h_{\alpha}\Pi_{\alpha},

(21)

where
\{h_{\alpha}\}\in[0,1]
are real eigenvalues satisfying
h_{\alpha}\neq h_{\beta},~\forall\alpha\neq\beta
, and
\{\Pi_{\alpha}\}
are the corresponding eigenspace projectors.

We also introduce two concentration results here.

Lemma 1
.

Given a binomial distribution
\bm{\Pr}[X=m]=\binom{N}{m}(1-p)^{N-m}p^{m}
and a positive
c
, we have the following inequality

\displaystyle\bm{\Pr}[|X-Np|\geq cN]=\sum_{|m-Np|\geq cp}\binom{N}{m}(1-p)^{N-m}p^{m}\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{Nc^{2}}{0.5p(1-p)+2c/3}\right).

(22)

Proof.

Bernstein’s inequality
Vershynin 2018
states that when
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
are i.i.d. variables with the mean
\mu
and the variance
\sigma^{2}
, and we have
|X_{i}-\mu|\leq R
for any
i
, then the following inequality holds

\displaystyle\bm{\Pr}\left[\left|\sum_{i}X_{i}-N\mu\right|\geq Nc\right]\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-Nc^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}+2Rc/3}\right).

(23)

Note that the binomial distribution can be generated from
N
independent Bernoulli coins with a probability
p
to get 1 and a probability
1-p
to get 0. Therefore, we can simply let
X_{i}
be these Bernoulli coins, resulting in
\mu=p
,
\sigma^{2}=p(1-p)/4
and
R=1
. Putting these parameters into Eq.
23
, the result is proved.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Given a binomial distribution
\bm{\Pr}[X=m]=\binom{N}{m}(1-p)^{N-m}p^{m}
and a positive
c
, we have the following inequality

\displaystyle\bm{\Pr}[|X-Np|\geq cN]=\sum_{|m-Np|\geq cp}\binom{N}{m}(1-p)^{N-m}p^{m}\leq 2e^{-2c^{2}N}.

(24)

Proof.

Here, the result is based on the Hoeffding inequality, which for example, can be found in Ref.
Vershynin 2018
.
∎

Appendix B
Brief review of standard quantum phase estimation (QPE)

Figure B.2:
The quantum circuit of standard quantum phase estimation
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
.

Here, we summarize the results of standard QPE that utilizes the quantum Fourier transform (QFT)
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
.

Given a state
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{\alpha}\Pi_{\alpha}|\psi\rangle=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}|\psi_{\alpha}\rangle
, QPE with
d
ancilla qubits first acts a controlled Hamiltonian simulation unitary
U_{H}
with the form

\displaystyle U_{H}=\sum_{x=0}^{2^{d}-1}|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes e^{-i2\pi Hx},

(25)

where
x=(x_{d-1}x_{d-2}\cdots x_{0})=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}x_{j}2^{j}
on the initial state
|\text{in}\rangle=\sum_{x=0}^{2^{d}-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{d}}}|x\rangle\otimes|\psi\rangle
. Next, QPE implements the QFT circuit
U_{\text{QFT}}
on the ancilla qubits. The output state can then be derived

\displaystyle|\text{out}\rangle\

\displaystyle=(U_{\text{QFT}}\otimes I_{n})U_{H}\sum_{x=0}^{2^{d}-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{d}}}|x\rangle\otimes|\psi\rangle

(26)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{2^{d}-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{d}}}U_{\text{QFT}}|x\rangle\otimes e^{-i2\pi Hx}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{2^{d}-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{d}}}U_{\text{QFT}}|x\rangle\otimes\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}e^{-i2\pi h_{\alpha}x}|\psi_{\alpha}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{2^{d}-1}\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{y=0}^{2^{d}-1}e^{i2\pi xy/2^{d}}|y\rangle\otimes\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}e^{-i2\pi h_{\alpha}x}|\psi_{\alpha}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}\sum_{y=0}^{2^{d}-1}\sum_{x=0}^{2^{d}-1}e^{-i2\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d})x}|y\rangle\otimes|\psi_{\alpha}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}\sum_{y=0}^{2^{d}-1}\frac{1-e^{-i2\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d})2^{d}}}{1-e^{-i2\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d})}}|y\rangle\otimes|\psi_{\alpha}\rangle.

Eigenvalue estimation.——
For the eigenvalue estimation task, we can see that when
|\psi\rangle=|\psi_{\alpha}\rangle
is an eigenstate of
H
, then Eq.
26
has the simplified form

\displaystyle|\text{out}\rangle=\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{y=0}^{2^{d}-1}\frac{1-e^{-i2\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d})2^{d}}}{1-e^{-i2\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d})}}|y\rangle\otimes|\psi_{\alpha}\rangle.

(27)

The probability of measuring
|y\rangle
is therefore

\displaystyle p_{y}\

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\frac{1-e^{-i2\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d})2^{d}}}{1-e^{-i2\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d})}}\frac{1-e^{i2\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d})2^{d}}}{1-e^{i2\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d})}}

(28)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\frac{e^{-i\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d})2^{d}}\sin(\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d})2^{d})}{e^{-i\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d})}\sin(\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d}))}\frac{e^{i\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d})2^{d}}\sin(\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d})2^{d})}{e^{i\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d})}\sin(\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d}))}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\frac{\sin(\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d})2^{d})^{2}}{\sin(\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d}))^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\frac{1}{\sin(\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d}))^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\frac{1}{\sin(\pi|h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d}|_{c})^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\frac{1}{4|h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d}|_{c}^{2}},

where the last line, we define the circular distance
\{|\theta|_{c}=\min\{\theta\mod 1,(1-\theta)\mod 1\}
, and we use the inequality

\displaystyle|\sin(\pi|\theta|_{c})|\geq 2|\theta|_{c}.

(29)

Thus, we have the failure probability of estimation within an error
\epsilon
has the upper bound

\displaystyle\bm{\Pr}\left[|h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d}|_{c}\geq\epsilon\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{y:|h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d}|_{c}\geq\epsilon}p_{y}

(31)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y:|h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d}|_{c}\geq\epsilon}\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\frac{1}{4|h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d}|_{c}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\cdot 2^{d}}\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s^{2}}ds+\frac{1}{2\cdot 2^{2d}\epsilon^{2}}=\frac{1}{2\cdot 2^{d}\epsilon}+\frac{1}{2\cdot 2^{2d}\epsilon^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{d}\epsilon}.

As a result, to have a success probability at least
1-\delta
, we require
2^{d}=\mathcal{O}(1/(\epsilon\delta))
. Since
U_{H}
has the decomposition

\displaystyle U_{H}=\sum_{x=0}^{2^{d}-1}|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes e^{-i2\pi Hx}=\prod_{j=0}^{d-1}\left(|0\rangle\langle 0|_{j}\otimes I+|1\rangle\langle 1|_{j}\otimes e^{-i2\pi H2^{j}}\right),

(32)

which is the successive multiplication of
j
th-ancilla qubit controlled
e^{-i2\pi H2^{j}}
unitaries for
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
, therefore, the cost of constructing
U_{H}
characterized by the Hamiltonian simulation time is
1+2+\cdots+2^{d-1}=2^{d}-1
and we have

\displaystyle\text{Hami.simu.time}=2^{d}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1}\delta^{-1}),

(33)

where the
\epsilon^{-1}
scaling is also called the Heisenberg limit.

Eigenstate preparation.——
For the eigenstate preparation task, we still consider Eq.
26
. Following the derivation of Eq.
28
, the probability of measuring
y
on the ancilla register is

\displaystyle p_{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\sum_{\alpha}|c_{\alpha}|^{2}\frac{\sin(\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d})2^{d})^{2}}{\sin(\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d}))^{2}}.

(34)

After the measurement, the remaining state on the system register becomes

\displaystyle|\phi_{y}\rangle=\frac{1}{2^{d}\sqrt{p_{y}}}\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}\frac{1-e^{-i2\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d})2^{d}}}{1-e^{-i2\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d})}}|\psi_{\alpha}\rangle,

(35)

whose overlap with an eigenstate
|\psi_{\beta}\rangle
is

\displaystyle|\langle\phi_{y}|\psi_{\beta}\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{|c_{\beta}|^{2}\frac{\sin(\pi(h_{\beta}-y/2^{d})2^{d})^{2}}{\sin(\pi(h_{\beta}-y/2^{d}))^{2}}}{2^{2d}p_{y}}=\frac{|c_{\beta}|^{2}\frac{\sin(\pi(h_{\beta}-y/2^{d})2^{d})^{2}}{\sin(\pi(h_{\beta}-y/2^{d}))^{2}}}{|c_{\beta}|^{2}\frac{\sin(\pi(h_{\beta}-y/2^{d})2^{d})^{2}}{\sin(\pi(h_{\beta}-y/2^{d}))^{2}}+\sum_{\alpha\neq\beta}|c_{\alpha}|^{2}\frac{\sin(\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d})2^{d})^{2}}{\sin(\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d}))^{2}}}.

(36)

If
h_{\beta}
is exactly known, we can then always let
h_{\beta}
be 0 by shifting the spectrum of
H
(adding identity). In this case, we will have

\displaystyle|\langle\phi_{y}|\psi_{\beta}\rangle|^{2}=0,~\forall y\neq 0.

(37)

Therefore, only when the measurement outcome is
y=0
, the eigenstate
|\psi_{\beta}\rangle
will have a non-zero contribution. For
y=0
, we have the measurement probability

\displaystyle p_{0}

\displaystyle=|c_{\beta}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\sum_{\alpha\neq\beta}|c_{\alpha}|^{2}\frac{\sin(\pi h_{\alpha}2^{d})^{2}}{\sin(\pi h_{\alpha})^{2}}\geq|c_{\beta}|^{2}.

(38)

Define
\Delta_{\beta}
as the gap between
h_{\beta}
and other eigenvalues, then the overlap has a lower bound

\displaystyle|\langle\phi_{0}|\psi_{\beta}\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{|c_{\beta}|^{2}2^{2d}}{|c_{\beta}|^{2}2^{2d}+\sum_{\alpha\neq\beta}|c_{\alpha}|^{2}\frac{\sin(\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d})2^{d})^{2}}{\sin(\pi(h_{\alpha}-y/2^{d}))^{2}}}

(39)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|c_{\beta}|^{2}2^{2d}}{|c_{\beta}|^{2}2^{2d}+\sum_{\alpha\neq\beta}|c_{\alpha}|^{2}\frac{1}{4|h_{\alpha}|_{c}^{2}}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|c_{\beta}|^{2}2^{2d}}{|c_{\beta}|^{2}2^{2d}+\sum_{\alpha\neq\beta}|c_{\alpha}|^{2}\frac{1}{4\Delta_{\beta}^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|c_{\beta}|^{2}}{|c_{\beta}|^{2}+(1-|c_{\beta}|^{2})\frac{1}{4(2^{d}\Delta_{\beta})^{2}}}.

To have an overlap at least
1-\zeta
, we require
2^{d}=\mathcal{O}(|c_{\beta}|^{-1}\Delta_{\beta}^{-1}\zeta^{-1/2})
. For the overall complexity, we need to account for the measurement probability
p_{0}
. Through amplitude amplification, we can use
\mathcal{O}(|c_{\beta}|^{-1})
queries on the preparation unitaries of
|\text{out}\rangle
to amplify
p_{0}
to
\mathcal{O}(1)
. As a result, the overall required Hamiltonian simulation time is

\displaystyle\text{Hami.simu.time}=\mathcal{O}(|c_{\beta}|^{-1}2^{d})=\mathcal{O}(|c_{\beta}|^{-2}\Delta_{\beta}^{-1}\zeta^{-1/2}).

(40)

Appendix C
Dilated Hamiltonian for Lindbladian simulation

The general form of a Lindbladian is

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathcal{L}[\rho(t)]=-i[H_{I},\rho(t)]+\sum_{i}\left(F_{i}\rho(t)F_{i}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\rho(t),F_{i}^{\dagger}F_{i}\}\right),

(41)

where
\rho(t)
is system density matrix,
H_{I}
is the internal Hamiltonian, and
F_{i}
are quantum jump operators. Here, we consider a special case where
H_{I}=0
and there is only a single jump operator
F

\frac{d\rho(t)}{dt}=\mathcal{L}_{F}[\rho(t)]=F\rho(t)F^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\rho(t),F^{\dagger}F\}.

(42)

For this special Lindbladian Eq.
42
, there is a simple Lindbladian simulation algorithm through dilated Hamiltonian

\tilde{F}=\begin{pmatrix}0&F^{\dagger}\\
F&0\end{pmatrix}.

(43)

The short-time evolution under
\tilde{F}
has the effect

\displaystyle e^{-i\tilde{F}\sqrt{\tau}}(|0\rangle\langle 0|\otimes\rho(t))e^{i\tilde{F}\sqrt{\tau}}

\displaystyle\approx\left(I-i\tilde{F}\sqrt{\tau}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{F}^{2}\tau\right)(|0\rangle\langle 0|\otimes\rho(t))\left(I+i\tilde{F}\sqrt{\tau}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{F}^{2}\tau\right)

(44)

\displaystyle\approx|0\rangle\langle 0|\otimes\rho(t)+\tau\tilde{F}(|0\rangle\langle 0|\otimes\rho(t))\tilde{F}-\frac{1}{2}\tau\tilde{F}^{2}(|0\rangle\langle 0|\otimes\rho(t))-\frac{1}{2}\tau(|0\rangle\langle 0|\otimes\rho(t))\tilde{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=|0\rangle\langle 0|\otimes\left(\rho(t)-\frac{1}{2}\{F^{\dagger}F,\rho(t)\}\tau\right)+|1\rangle\langle 1|\otimes F\rho(t)F^{\dagger}\tau.

By tracing out the ancilla qubit, we obtain

\displaystyle\rho(t)+F\rho(t)F^{\dagger}\tau-\frac{1}{2}\{F^{\dagger}F,\rho(t)\}\tau\approx\rho(t+\tau).

(45)

Therefore, by repeatedly adding ancilla qubits and running short-time evolution of
\tilde{F}
, we are able to simulate Eq.
42
.

Regarding the complexity, for a simulation of time
\tau=t/N
, dilated Hamiltonian simulation needs a time
\sqrt{\tau}=\sqrt{t/N}
, resulting in a total simulation time
T=N\sqrt{t/N}=\sqrt{Nt}
. It can be shown that to simulate the Lindbladian Eq.
42
for time
t
within error
\varepsilon
, we need
N=t^{3}/\varepsilon^{2}
, resulting in a total dilated Hamiltonian simulation time

\displaystyle T=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right).

(46)

Since
N
is also the number of ancilla qubits, the number of ancilla qubits is
\mathcal{O}(t^{3}\varepsilon^{-2})
.

Appendix D
Details on Lindbladian as slow QPE

D.1
Motivation

Consider the following special Lindbladian

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathcal{L}_{H}[\rho(t)]=H\rho(t)H-\frac{1}{2}\{\rho(t),H^{2}\},

(47)

where the single jump operator is the Hamiltonian
H
in Eq.
21
. We can express
\rho(0)
in terms of
\{\Pi_{\alpha}\}
:

\rho(0)=\sum_{\alpha\beta}\Pi_{\alpha}\rho(0)\Pi_{\beta}=\sum_{\alpha\beta}\rho_{0,\alpha\beta}.

(48)

Since we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\Pi_{\alpha}\rho(0)\Pi_{\beta}}{\mathrm{d}t}=h_{\alpha}h_{\beta}\rho_{0,\alpha\beta}-\frac{h_{\alpha}^{2}+h_{\beta}^{2}}{2}\rho_{0,\alpha\beta}

(49)

\displaystyle\rho_{0,\alpha\beta}\xrightarrow{t}e^{\left(h_{\alpha}h_{\beta}-\frac{h_{\alpha}^{2}+h_{\beta}^{2}}{2}\right)t}\rho_{0,\alpha\beta},

(50)

Therefore, the Lindbladian Eq.
47
has the solution

\displaystyle\rho(t)

\displaystyle=e^{\mathcal{L}_{H}t}[\rho(0)]=\sum_{\alpha\beta}e^{\mathcal{L}_{H}t}[\rho_{0,\alpha\beta}]=\sum_{\alpha\beta}e^{\left(h_{\alpha}h_{\beta}-\frac{h_{\alpha}^{2}+h_{\beta}^{2}}{2}\right)t}\rho_{0,\alpha\beta}.

(51)

Because we have
h_{\alpha}\neq h_{\beta}
for
\alpha\neq\beta
, therefore,

\displaystyle h_{\alpha}h_{\beta}-\frac{h_{\alpha}^{2}+h_{\beta}^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\leq|h_{\alpha}||h_{\beta}|-\frac{h_{\alpha}^{2}+h_{\beta}^{2}}{2}<0,~\forall\,\alpha\neq\beta,

(52)

\displaystyle h_{\alpha}h_{\beta}-\frac{h_{\alpha}^{2}+h_{\beta}^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle=0,~\forall\,\alpha=\beta.

(53)

Thus, the steady state
\rho_{\text{ss}}
preserves the coherence inside the subspace of
H
with the same energy while dissipating the coherence between the subspace of
H
with different energy

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{ss}}=\rho(\infty)=\sum_{\alpha}\rho_{0,\alpha\alpha}.

(54)

For QPE as shown in Section
B
, considering infinite accuracy, the output state, including both ancilla and the system, has the form

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{QPE}}=\sum_{\alpha\beta}|h_{\alpha}\rangle\langle h_{\beta}|\otimes\rho_{0,\alpha\beta},

(55)

where
|h_{\alpha}\rangle
is an ancilla computation basis state corresponding to the binary representation of the eigenvalue
h_{\alpha}
. By tracing out the ancillas, we can find that the reduced system Hamiltonian is also
\rho_{\text{ss}}
, which indicates a strong connection between these two seemingly vastly different quantum methods.

D.2
Lindbladian as slow QPE

We consider in more detail the short-time dilated Hamiltonian simulation Eq.
44
, we have

\displaystyle e^{-i\tilde{F}\sqrt{\tau}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-i\sqrt{\tau})^{k}}{k!}\tilde{F}^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-i\sqrt{\tau})^{2k}}{2k!}\tilde{F}^{2k}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-i\sqrt{\tau})^{(2k+1)}}{(2k+1)!}\tilde{F}^{2k+1}

(56)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-i\sqrt{\tau})^{2k}}{2k!}\begin{pmatrix}(F^{\dagger}F)^{k}&0\\
0&(FF^{\dagger})^{k}\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-i\sqrt{\tau})^{(2k+1)}}{(2k+1)!}\begin{pmatrix}0&(F^{\dagger}F)^{k}F^{\dagger}\\
(FF^{\dagger})^{k}F&0\end{pmatrix}.

F
has the singular value decomposition
F=W\Sigma V
, putting this into the above expression, we have

\displaystyle e^{-i\tilde{F}\sqrt{\tau}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-i\sqrt{\tau})^{2k}}{2k!}\begin{pmatrix}V^{\dagger}\Sigma^{2k}V&0\\
0&W\Sigma^{2k}W^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-i\sqrt{\tau})^{(2k+1)}}{(2k+1)!}\begin{pmatrix}0&V^{\dagger}\Sigma^{2k+1}W^{\dagger}\\
W\Sigma^{2k+1}V&0\ \end{pmatrix}

(57)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}V^{\dagger}\cos(\sqrt{\tau}\Sigma)V&0\\
0&W\cos(\sqrt{\tau}\Sigma)W^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}-i\begin{pmatrix}0&V^{\dagger}\sin(\sqrt{\tau}\Sigma)W^{\dagger}\\
W\sin(\sqrt{\tau}\Sigma)V&0\ \end{pmatrix}.

When
F=H
is Hermitian, we have

\displaystyle e^{-i\tilde{H}\sqrt{\tau}}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\sqrt{\tau}H)&0\\
0&\cos(\sqrt{\tau}H)\end{pmatrix}-i\begin{pmatrix}0&\sin(\sqrt{\tau}H)\\
\sin(\sqrt{\tau}H)&0\ \end{pmatrix}.

(58)

To simplify the presentation without loss of generality, we consider
\rho(0)=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
, which has the form

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle=\sum_{\alpha}\Pi_{\alpha}|\psi\rangle=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}|\psi_{\alpha}\rangle,

(59)

with
\sum_{i}|c_{i}|^{2}=1
. Acting Eq.
58
to
|0\rangle|\psi\rangle
, we have

\displaystyle e^{-i\tilde{H}\sqrt{\tau}}|0\rangle|\psi\rangle=|0\rangle\cos(\sqrt{\tau}H)|\psi\rangle-i|1\rangle\sin(\sqrt{\tau}H)|\psi\rangle.

(60)

By setting
\tau=t/N
and repeatedly implementing
e^{-i\tilde{H}}
for
N
times (with each time adding a new ancilla qubit), we have

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{i}e^{-i\tilde{H}_{i}\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}}\right)|0\rangle^{\otimes N}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{N}(-i)^{m}\sqrt{\binom{N}{m}}|\bar{m}\rangle\cos\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}H\right)^{N-m}\sin\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}H\right)^{m}|\psi\rangle

(61)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{N}|\bar{m}\rangle(-i)^{m}\sqrt{\binom{N}{m}}\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}\cos\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}h_{\alpha}\right)^{N-m}\sin\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}h_{\alpha}\right)^{m}|\psi_{\alpha}\rangle,

where
|\bar{m}\rangle
represents the Dicke state, the equal superposition of all
N-m
0s and
m
1s configurations.

The probability of measuring
|\bar{m}\rangle
is

\displaystyle p_{m}=\binom{N}{m}\sum_{\alpha}|c_{\alpha}|^{2}\cos\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}h_{\alpha}\right)^{2N-2m}\sin\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}h_{\alpha}\right)^{2m},

(62)

with the resulting state in the system having the form

\displaystyle|\phi_{m}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{m}}}(-i)^{m}\sqrt{\binom{N}{m}}\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}\cos\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}h_{\alpha}\right)^{N-m}\sin\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}h_{\alpha}\right)^{m}|\psi_{\alpha}\rangle.

(63)

the contribution (probability) of
|\psi_{\alpha}\rangle
in
|\phi_{m}\rangle
is

\displaystyle p_{m,\alpha}=|\langle\phi_{m}|\psi_{\alpha}\rangle|^{2}=\binom{N}{m}\frac{|c_{\alpha}|^{2}\cos\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}h_{\alpha}\right)^{2N-2m}\sin\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}h_{\alpha}\right)^{2m}}{p_{m}}.

(64)

D.2.1
Eigenvalue estimation

When
|\psi\rangle=|\psi_{\alpha}\rangle
is an eigenstate of
H
, we have

\displaystyle p_{m}

\displaystyle=\binom{N}{m}\cos\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}h_{\alpha}\right)^{2N-2m}\sin\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}h_{\alpha}\right)^{2m}

(65)

\displaystyle=\binom{N}{m}(1-q_{\alpha})^{N-m}q_{\alpha}^{m},

where we define
q_{\alpha}=\sin\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}h_{\alpha}\right)^{2}
. Therefore,
p_{m}
is exactly the binomial distribution. According to Lemma
1
, the measurement results will concentrate on
q_{\alpha}N
and we have

\displaystyle\bm{\Pr}[|m-Nq_{\alpha}|\geq cN]=\sum_{|m-Nq_{\alpha}|\geq cN}p_{m}\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{Nc^{2}}{0.5q_{\alpha}(1-q_{\alpha})+2c/3}\right).

(66)

Therefore, we can expect to use
m/N
to estimate
q_{\alpha}
. Since
\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}\ll 1
, we have
q_{\alpha}\approx\frac{t}{N}h_{\alpha}^{2}
. Therefore, an
\epsilon
-additive error estimation in
h_{\alpha}
corresponds to a
\frac{2t}{N}h_{\alpha}\epsilon
-additive error estimation in
q_{\alpha}
, which further corresponds to a
2th_{\alpha}\epsilon
-uncertainty (
c=\frac{2th_{\alpha}}{N}\epsilon
) in
m
and we have

\displaystyle\bm{\Pr}[|m-Nq_{\alpha}|\geq 2th_{\alpha}\epsilon]

\displaystyle\lesssim 2\exp\left(-\frac{4t^{2}h_{\alpha}^{2}\epsilon^{2}/N}{0.5\frac{t}{N}h_{\alpha}^{2}(1-\frac{t}{N}h_{\alpha}^{2})+\frac{4th_{\alpha}\epsilon}{3N}}\right)

(67)

\displaystyle=2\exp\left(-\frac{4t^{2}h_{\alpha}^{2}\epsilon^{2}}{0.5th_{\alpha}^{2}(1-\frac{t}{N}h_{\alpha}^{2})+\frac{4th_{\alpha}\epsilon}{3}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{4t^{2}h_{\alpha}^{2}\epsilon^{2}}{0.5th_{\alpha}^{2}+\frac{4th_{\alpha}\epsilon}{3}}\right)

\displaystyle=2\exp\left(-\frac{4th_{\alpha}\epsilon^{2}}{0.5h_{\alpha}+\frac{4\epsilon}{3}}\right).

We typically require an accuracy
\epsilon<h_{\alpha}
, which leads to

\displaystyle\bm{\Pr}[|m-Nq_{\alpha}|\geq 2th_{\alpha}\epsilon]=\sum_{|m-Nq_{\alpha}|\geq 2th_{\alpha}\epsilon}p_{m}\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{24}{11}t\epsilon^{2}\right).

(68)

Therefore, to have an estimation success probability at least
1-\delta
, we require the Lindbladian evolution time to be of order

\displaystyle\text{Lindlad.evo.time (t)}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2}\log(\delta^{-1})).

(69)

Since the Lindbladian dynamics of time
t
within an error
\varepsilon
is simulated through the dilated Hamiltonian with the simulation time to be
\mathcal{O}(t^{2}/\varepsilon)
as shown in Eq.
46
, therefore, the total dilated Hamiltonian simulation time required to fulfill the eigenvalue estimation task is

\displaystyle\text{Hami.simu.time (T)}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-4}\log^{2}(\delta^{-1})\varepsilon^{-1}),

(70)

where the
\varepsilon
dependence just affects the Lindbladian simulation error but will not contribute to the accuracy of the eigenvalue estimation. Therefore, the natural (purified) evolution of the Lindbladian Eq.
47
gives an eigenvalue estimation algorithm with standard quantum limit (
\epsilon^{-2}
), and the dilated Hamiltonian simulation algorithm for Eq.
47
gives an eigenvalue estimation algorithm that is quadratically worse (
\epsilon^{-4}
) than standard quantum limit.

D.2.2
Eigenstate preparation

Given
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}|\psi_{\alpha}\rangle
, if the eigenvalue
h_{\beta}
of the interested eigenstate
|\psi_{\beta}\rangle
is exactly known, then similar to the operation in standard QPE (Section
B
), we can then always let
h_{\beta}
be 0 by shifting the spectrum of
H
. By this, following Eq.
64
, we will have

\displaystyle p_{m,\beta}=\binom{N}{m}\frac{|c_{\beta}|^{2}\cos\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}h_{\beta}\right)^{2N-2m}\sin\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}h_{\beta}\right)^{2m}}{p_{m}}=0,~\forall m\neq 0.

(71)

Therefore, we only need to focus on the measurement event
m=0
. According to Eq.
62
, the probability of measuring
m=0
is

\displaystyle p_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}|c_{\alpha}|^{2}\cos\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}h_{\alpha}\right)^{2N}

(72)

\displaystyle=|c_{\beta}|^{2}+\sum_{\alpha\neq\beta}|c_{\alpha}|^{2}\cos\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}h_{\alpha}\right)^{2N}\geq|c_{\beta}|^{2}.

Let
\Delta_{\beta}
be the spectral gap between
h_{\beta}
and other eigenvalues, then after the measurement, the overlap between the resulting state and
|\psi_{\beta}\rangle
has the lower bound

\displaystyle p_{0,\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{|c_{\beta}|^{2}}{p_{0}}=\frac{|c_{\beta}|^{2}}{|c_{\beta}|^{2}+\sum_{\alpha\neq\beta}|c_{\alpha}|^{2}\cos\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}h_{\alpha}\right)^{2N}}

(73)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{|c_{\beta}|^{2}}{|c_{\beta}|^{2}+\sum_{\alpha\neq\beta}|c_{\alpha}|^{2}(1-\frac{t}{2N}h_{\alpha}^{2})^{2N}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|c_{\beta}|^{2}}{|c_{\beta}|^{2}+\sum_{\alpha\neq\beta}|c_{\alpha}|^{2}(1-\frac{t}{2N}\Delta_{\beta}^{2})^{2N}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|c_{\beta}|^{2}}{|c_{\beta}|^{2}+(1-|c_{\beta}|^{2})\left(1-\frac{1}{\frac{2N}{t\Delta_{\beta}^{2}}}\right)^{\frac{2N}{t\Delta_{\beta}^{2}}t\Delta_{\beta}^{2}}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|c_{\beta}|^{2}}{|c_{\beta}|^{2}+(1-|c_{\beta}|^{2})e^{-t\Delta_{\beta}^{2}}},

where in the second line, we use the Taylor approximation for
\cos\left(\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}h_{\alpha}\right)
since
\sqrt{\frac{t}{N}}\ll 1
, and in the last line, we use the fact that
(1-1/x)^{x}<e^{-1}
. As a result, to make the overlap
p_{0,\beta}
be at least
1-\zeta
, we therefore require the overall complexity

\displaystyle\text{Lindlad.evo.time (t)}=\mathcal{O}\left(|c_{\beta}|^{-1}\Delta_{\beta}^{-2}\log(\zeta^{-1})\right),

(74)

which corresponds to the dilated Hamiltonian simulation time

\displaystyle\text{Hami.simu.time (T)}=\mathcal{O}\left(|c_{\beta}|^{-1}\Delta_{\beta}^{-4}\log^{2}(\zeta^{-1})\varepsilon^{-1}\right),

(75)

where the
|c_{\beta}|^{-1}
comes from using amplitude amplification to amplify
p_{0}
and we ignore the additional logarithmic factors. Note that
\varepsilon
dependence just affects the Lindbladian simulation error but will not contribute to the accuracy of the eigenstate preparation.

W

\displaystyle A=\left\|(I_{n}\otimes\langle 1|)U_{f}(\textbf{H}^{\otimes n}\otimes I)|0\rangle^{\otimes n}|0\rangle\right\|_{2}=\left\|2^{-n/2}(I_{n}\otimes\langle 1|)\sum_{x}|x\rangle|f(x)\rangle\right\|_{2}=2^{-n/2}\sqrt{W}.

(107)

Therefore, judging whether
A
is zero or not can solve the decision problem. The amplitude estimation algorithm
Brassard et al. 2000
is as follows. We first construct the following unitary

\displaystyle U_{ae}=(I_{n+1}-2U_{f}(\textbf{H}^{\otimes n}\otimes I)|0\rangle^{\otimes n}|0\rangle\langle 0|^{\otimes n}\langle 0|(\textbf{H}^{\otimes n}\otimes I)U_{f}^{\dagger})(I_{n}\otimes Z),

(108)

and let
U_{ae}
replace
e^{-iH}
in QPE (either the standard QPE (Eq.
25
) or our fast-forwarding Lindbladian-based QPE (Eq.
E
)). Next, we set the input
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{x}|x\rangle|f(x)\rangle
. Then, running the QPE algorithms with queries on
U_{ae}
for at most
\mathcal{O}(2^{n/2})
times is sufficient to judge whether
W
is zero or not. The
\mathcal{O}(2^{n/2})
scaling is quadratically better than classical algorithms
\mathcal{O}(2^{n})
, and the reason for this quadratic speedup is exactly the Heisenberg limit scaling of QPE. Note that, for our Lindbladian-based QPE, we are doing the fast-forwarding simulation for the Lindbladian Eq.
47
with
H_{ae}
to be the jump operator where
H_{ae}
satisfies
e^{-iH_{ae}}=U_{ae}
.

Appendix H
Quantum Gibbs state preparation

Here, we describe how to use our fast-forwarding result for Gibbs state preparations. The basic idea is to combine our Lindbladian fast-forwarding simulation algorithm with the Lindbladian-based differential equation solver introduced in
Shang et al. 2024
. Given a positive semidefinite Hamiltonian
H_{P}
with
\|H_{P}\|\leq 1
, which is also the hidden assumption in previous algorithms
Gilyén et al. 2019
;
Chowdhury and Somma 2016
, the Gibbs state
\rho_{\beta}
and the partition function
Z_{\beta}
at the inverse temperature
\beta
are

\displaystyle\rho_{\beta}=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{P}}}{Z_{\beta}},~\quad Z_{\beta}=\mathrm{Tr}(e^{-\beta H_{P}}).

(109)

For
\rho_{\beta}
, we consider its symmetric purification state

\displaystyle|\rho_{\beta}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2^{n}}{Z_{\beta}}}(e^{-\beta H_{P}/2}\otimes I)|\Omega\rangle

(110)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2^{n}}{Z_{\beta}}}e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}H_{P}\otimes I}|\Omega\rangle,

where
|\Omega\rangle=2^{-n/2}\sum_{i}|i\rangle|i\rangle
.

Based on Ref.
Shang et al. 2024
, if we start from an initial state
|+\rangle\langle+|\otimes|\Omega\rangle\langle\Omega|
, and construct a Lindbladian of the form Eq.
42
, with the single jump operator having the form

\displaystyle F=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{H_{P}}\otimes I&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

(111)

then at the time
\beta/2
, the output density matrix will have the form

\displaystyle\rho_{out}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\cdot&e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}H_{P}\otimes I}|\Omega\rangle\langle\Omega|\\
|\Omega\rangle\langle\Omega|e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}H_{P}\otimes I}&\cdot\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\cdot&\sqrt{\frac{Z_{\beta}}{2^{n}}}|\rho_{\beta}\rangle\langle\Omega|\\
\sqrt{\frac{Z_{\beta}}{2^{n}}}|\Omega\rangle\langle\rho_{\beta}|&\cdot\end{pmatrix}.

(112)

Note that to construct
F
, we assume that we are given the access (or the block encoding) of
\sqrt{H_{P}}
. If we have access to the preparation unitary of the purification of
\rho_{out}
, which is true in our algorithm, then we can follow the amplitude amplification procedure introduced in Ref.
Shang et al. 2024
to finally prepare
|\rho_{\beta}\rangle
with
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\frac{2^{n}}{Z_{\beta}}})
queries to the preparation unitary.

Let us now consider the complexity. We can observe that the jump operator Eq.
111
is Hermitian, which meets our fast-forwarding requirements. Combining the fast-forwarding result Eq.
87
and the amplitude amplification, the total required Hamiltonian simulation time (
e^{-iFt}
) to prepare
|\rho_{\beta}\rangle
within an error
\varepsilon
is

Hami.simu.time

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2^{n}}{Z_{\beta}}}\beta^{1/2}\log^{1/2}(\varepsilon^{-1})\right),

(113)

where the
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}
symbol hides the additional
\log(\sqrt{\frac{2^{n}}{Z_{\beta}}})
factors.

To make a fair comparison with previous methods, we also need to consider the cost of implementing Hamiltonian simulation on quantum computers. To simulate a Hamiltonian
e^{-iFt}
of time
t
within an error
\varepsilon
, the state-of-the-art quantum singal processing technique needs to query the block encoding of
\sqrt{H_{P}}
for
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(t+\log(\varepsilon^{-1}))
times
Low and Chuang 2017
. Recall Fig.
E.3
, the whole controlled Hamiltonian simulation Eq.
79
is decomposed into
d^{\prime}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\log(t^{1.5}\varepsilon^{-1}))
single-control Hamiltonian simulations. To make sure the whole error is within
\varepsilon
, we can simply let each single-control Hamiltonian simulation be within an error
\varepsilon/d^{\prime}
. The resulting overall query complexity on
\sqrt{H_{P}}
to construct Eq.
79
within
\varepsilon
then becomes

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(t+d^{\prime}\log(\varepsilon^{-1}))=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(t+\log^{2}(\varepsilon^{-1})).

(114)

Putting this result in Eq.
113
, the final query complexity on
\sqrt{H_{P}}
to prepare
|\rho_{\beta}\rangle
(and therefore
\rho_{\beta}
) within an error
\varepsilon
becomes

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2^{n}}{Z_{\beta}}}\beta^{1/2}\log^{1/2}(\varepsilon^{-1})+\log^{2}(\varepsilon^{-1})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2^{n}}{Z_{\beta}}}\sqrt{\max\left(\beta,\log^{3}(\varepsilon^{-1})\right)\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}\right).

(115)

When
\beta>\log^{3}(\varepsilon^{-1})
, the complexity becomes
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2^{n}}{Z_{\beta}}}\sqrt{\beta\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}\right)
.

In comparison, given the
\sqrt{H_{P}}

Gilyén et al. 2019

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2^{n}}{Z_{\beta}}}\sqrt{\max\left(\beta,\log(\varepsilon^{-1})\right)\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}\right),

(116)

when
\beta>\log(\varepsilon^{-1})
, the complexity becomes
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2^{n}}{Z_{\beta}}}\sqrt{\beta\log(\varepsilon^{-1})}\right)
, which is also the result in another Gibbs state preparation algorithm
Chowdhury and Somma 2016
based on the
\sqrt{H_{P}}

We want to mention that the above procedure can also be changed into a partition function estimation algorithm

Appendix I
Generalizations of Lindbladian fast forwarding

Here, we give a much broader class of Lindbladians with Eq.
47
as a subset that can enable quadratic fast forwardings. We refer to this class of Lindbladians as the Choi commuting Lindbladians. The form of Choi commuting Lindbladians is

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathcal{L}_{C}[\rho(t)]=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\left(H_{i}\rho(t)H_{i}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\rho(t),H_{i}^{\dagger}H_{i}\}\right),

(117)

where we require
H_{i}
to be Hermitian and

[H_{i}\otimes H_{i}^{*},H_{j}\otimes H_{j}^{*}]=0,~\forall\,i,j\in[1,K].

(118)

To see why Eq.
117
enables fast forwarding, we need to introduce the vectorization picture where we map
\rho(t)=\sum_{ij}\rho_{ij}(t)|i\rangle\langle j|
to its Choi vector
\ket{\rho(t)\rangle}=\sum_{ij}\rho_{ij}(t)|i\rangle|j\rangle
. Under this vectorization picture, the Lindbladian Eq.
117
has the form

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\ket{\rho(t)\rangle}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=L_{C}\ket{\rho(t)\rangle}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\left(H_{i}\otimes H_{i}^{*}-\frac{1}{2}H_{i}^{\dagger}H_{i}\otimes I-I\otimes\frac{1}{2}H_{i}^{T}H_{i}^{*}\right)\ket{\rho(t)\rangle}

(119)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\left(H_{i}\otimes H_{i}^{*}-\frac{1}{2}H_{i}^{2}\otimes I-I\otimes\frac{1}{2}H_{i}^{*2}\right)\ket{\rho(t)\rangle}.

which has the solution

\displaystyle\ket{\rho(t)\rangle}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K}\left(H_{i}\otimes H_{i}^{*}-\frac{1}{2}H_{i}^{2}\otimes I-I\otimes\frac{1}{2}H_{i}^{*2}\right)t\right)\ket{\rho(0)\rangle}

(120)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{K}\exp\left(\left(H_{i}\otimes H_{i}^{*}-\frac{1}{2}H_{i}^{2}\otimes I-I\otimes\frac{1}{2}H_{i}^{*2}\right)t\right)\ket{\rho(0)\rangle},

where the equality in the second line is due to Eq.
118
. Eq.
120
indicates that
\rho(t)
can be prepared by the following channel

\displaystyle\rho(t)=e^{\mathcal{L}_{H_{K}}t}\left[e^{\mathcal{L}_{H_{K-1}}t}\left[\cdots e^{\mathcal{L}_{H_{1}}t}\left[\rho(0)\right]\right]\right],

(121)

with each
e^{\mathcal{L}_{H_{i}}t}[\cdot]
corresponds to the Lindbladian dynamics for a time
t

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=H_{i}\rho(t)H_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\{\rho(t),H_{i}^{2}\}.

(122)

We can find that each Lindbladian
e^{\mathcal{L}_{H_{i}}t}[\cdot]
fits the form of Eq.
47
and therefore, its simulation enables a quadratic Lindbladian fast forwarding with the complexity in Eq.
87
. As a result, the overall dynamics Eq.
117
can be simulated with the complexity

Hami.simu.time

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(Kt^{1/2}\log^{1/2}(\varepsilon^{-1})\right),

Number of ancilla qubits

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(K\left(\log(t)+\log(\varepsilon^{-1})\right)\right).

(123)

Here, the Hamiltonian simulation time is the overall simulation time accounting for all
e^{-iH_{i}t}
dynamics.

Fast decoherence.——
A direct and surprising corollary of the fast forwarding of Choi commuting Lindbladians is that the decoherence effect under arbitrary Pauli noise can occur quadratically faster. Consider the system experiences a Pauli-type noise with the Lindbladian description having the form

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho(t)}{dt}=\mathcal{L}_{P}[\rho(t)]=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\left(P_{i}\rho(t)P_{i}-\rho(t)\right).

(124)

While different Pauli operators might not commute with each other, we must have
[P_{i}\otimes P_{i}^{*},P_{j}\otimes P_{j}^{*}]=0
for arbitrary
i,j
. Therefore, arbitrary combinations of Pauli noises satisfy our definition of the Choi commuting Lindbladian, which indicates that the natural decoherence effect described by Eq.
124
can occur is a quadratically faster time.

Appendix J
Binomial distribution state preparation

In this section, we briefly discuss how to prepare the state

\displaystyle|S_{B}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{N}}}\sum_{m=0}^{N}\sqrt{\binom{N}{m}}|m\rangle.

(125)

The idea is to first show the closeness between the binary distribution and the discrete Gaussian distribution. Then, we apply the algorithm
Kitaev and Webb 2008
for the discrete Gaussian state preparation.

Given a Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
, Ref.
Kitaev and Webb 2008
proposed a method for preparing the following discrete Gaussian state

\displaystyle|\xi_{\sigma,\mu,N}\rangle=\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\xi_{\sigma,\mu,N}(m)|m\rangle,

(126)

where
\xi_{\sigma,\mu,N}(m)
is defined
Bauer et al. 2021
as

\displaystyle\xi_{\sigma,\mu,N}^{2}(m)=\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{f(\mu,\sigma)}e^{-\frac{(m+lN-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}},

(127)

with
f(\mu,\sigma)

\displaystyle f(\mu,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\vartheta(\pi\mu,e^{-2\pi^{2}\sigma^{2}})

(128)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\left(1+2\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\cos(2\pi l\mu)e^{-2\pi^{2}l^{2}\sigma^{2}}\right).

For
\xi_{\sigma,\mu,N}(m)
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\xi_{\sigma,\mu,N}^{2}(m)-\frac{1}{f(\mu,\sigma)}e^{-\frac{(m-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right|=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{f(\mu,\sigma)}\left(e^{-\frac{(m+lN-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}+e^{-\frac{(m-lN-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right).

(129)

As we will see later, we are interested in
\mu=N/2
and
\sigma^{2}=N/4
, which means
|m-\mu|\leq N/2
, and therefore,
|lN\pm(m-\mu)|\geq(l-1/2)N
. As a result, we have

\displaystyle\left|\xi_{\sigma,\mu,N}^{2}(m)-\frac{1}{f(\mu,\sigma)}e^{-\frac{(m-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{f(\mu,\sigma)}e^{-\frac{(l-1/2)^{2}N^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}

(130)

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{1}{f(\mu,\sigma)}e^{-\frac{N^{2}}{8\sigma^{2}}}+2\sum_{l=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{f(\mu,\sigma)}e^{-\frac{lN^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\frac{1}{f(\mu,\sigma)}e^{-\frac{N^{2}}{8\sigma^{2}}}+2\frac{1}{f(\mu,\sigma)}e^{-\frac{N^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}\frac{1}{1-e^{-\frac{N^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}}

\displaystyle=2\frac{1}{f(\mu,\sigma)}e^{-\frac{N}{2}}+2\frac{1}{f(\mu,\sigma)}e^{-4N}\frac{1}{1-e^{-2N}}

where in the second line, we use the property:
(l-1/2)^{2}>l
for
l\geq 2
, and in the third line, we use the sum formula for a geometric sequence. For
f(\mu,\sigma)
, we have

\displaystyle\left|f(\mu,\sigma)-\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\vartheta(\pi\mu,e^{-2\pi^{2}\sigma^{2}})

(131)

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\left|\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\cos(2\pi l\mu)e^{-2\pi^{2}l^{2}\sigma^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}e^{-2\pi^{2}l\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}e^{-2\pi^{2}\sigma^{2}}\frac{1}{1-e^{-2\pi^{2}\sigma^{2}}}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{\pi N/2}e^{-\pi^{2}N/2}\frac{1}{1-e^{-\pi^{2}N/2}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\exp(-N)),

putting which into Eq.
130
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\xi_{\sigma,\mu,N}^{2}(m)-\frac{1}{f(\mu,\sigma)}e^{-\frac{(m-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right|=\mathcal{O}(\exp(-N)).

(132)

Based on Eq.
132
, we obtain the relation between
\xi_{\sigma,\mu,N}^{2}(m)
and the probability distribution function (PDF) of the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
at
\mu=N/2
and
\sigma^{2}=N/4
.

\displaystyle\left|\xi_{\sigma,\mu,N}^{2}(m)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-\frac{(m-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\xi_{\sigma,\mu,N}^{2}(m)-\frac{1}{f(\mu,\sigma)}e^{-\frac{(m-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right|+\left|\frac{1}{f(\mu,\sigma)}e^{-\frac{(m-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-\frac{(m-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right|

(133)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\exp(-N)).

For the initial state
|S_{B}\rangle
Eq.
125
, we have
\{p_{m}=2^{-N}\binom{N}{m}\}
as the binomial distribution
\mathcal{B}(N,1/2)
. Regarding the relations between the binomial distribution and the PDF of
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 3

(De Moivre–Laplace Theorem
Petrov 2012
)
.

Let
S_{N}
be a random variable with binomial distribution
\mathcal{B}(N,p)
.
Let
\mu=Np
and
\sigma^{2}=Np(1-p)
.
Let
\phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-(x-\mu)^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}
be the PDF of
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})

Then, for
m\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}

\displaystyle\left|\Pr(S_{N}=m)-\phi(m)\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

(134)

Combining Lemma
3
and Eq.
133
, using the triangle inequality, we obtain that

\displaystyle\left|p_{m}-\xi_{\sqrt{N}/2,N/2,N}^{2}(m)\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

(135)

We now consider the total variance distance

\displaystyle d_{\text{tvd}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{N}\left|p_{m}-\xi_{\sqrt{N}/2,N/2,N}^{2}(m)\right|

(136)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{|m-N/2|\leq cN}\left|p_{m}-\xi_{\sqrt{N}/2,N/2,N}^{2}(m)\right|+\sum_{|m-N/2|>cN}\left(p_{m}+\xi_{\sqrt{N}/2,N/2,N}^{2}(m)\right)

\displaystyle\leq cN\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{N})+2e^{-c^{2}N}+2e^{-2c^{2}N},

where in the last line, we use Lemma
2
and the concentration property of the Gaussian distribution
Vershynin 2018

, we have

\displaystyle d_{\text{tvd}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\frac{\log^{1/2}(N)}{\sqrt{N}})+\mathcal{O}(1/N)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}).

(137)

Using

Recall that we have
N=t^{3}/\varepsilon^{2}

\displaystyle\||S_{B}\rangle-|\xi_{\sigma,\mu,N}\rangle\|_{2}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon),

(139)
