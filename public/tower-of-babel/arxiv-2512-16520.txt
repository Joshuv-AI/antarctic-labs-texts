Replica Keldysh field theory of quantum-jump processes: General formalism and application to imbalanced and inefficient fermion counting
Felix Kloiber-Tollinger, Lukas M. Sieberer

Abstract
Measurement-induced phase transitions have largely been explored for projective or continuous measurements of Hermitian observables, assuming perfect detection without information loss. Yet such transitions also arise in more general settings, including quantum-jump processes with non-Hermitian jump operators, and under inefficient detection. A theoretical framework for treating these broader scenarios has been missing. Here we develop a comprehensive replica Keldysh field theory for general quantum-jump processes in both bosonic and fermionic systems. Our formalism provides a unified description of pure-state quantum trajectories under efficient detection and mixed-state dynamics emerging from inefficient monitoring, with deterministic Lindbladian evolution appearing as a limiting case. It thus establishes a direct connection between phase transitions in nonequilibrium steady states of driven open quantum matter and in measurement-induced dynamics. As an application, we study imbalanced and inefficient fermion counting in a one-dimensional lattice system: monitored gain and loss of fermions occurring at different rates, with a fraction of gain and loss jumps undetected. For imbalanced but efficient counting, we recover the qualitative picture of the balanced case: entanglement obeys an area law for any nonzero jump rate, with an extended quantum-critical regime emerging between two parametrically separated length scales. Inefficient detection introduces a finite correlation length beyond which entanglement, as quantified by the fermionic logarithmic negativity, obeys an area law, while the subsystem entropy shows volume-law scaling. Numerical simulations support our analytical findings. Our results offer a general and versatile theoretical foundation for studying measurement-induced phenomena across a wide class of monitored and open quantum systems.

As an illustration of the formalism, we analyze imbalanced and inefficient

paper, we extend this analysis in two important directions:

(i) We consider

Ref.
Starchl et al. 2025
persists without fine tuning: the model exhibits no

\gamma_{\pm}>0
, the entanglement entropy obeys an area law. However, area-law

mean rate
\gamma=(\gamma_{+}+\gamma_{-})/2

and

, the system displays
signatures of quantum criticality and emergent conformal invariance. For scales
larger than

\mathrm{SU}(R)

\mathrm{SU}(2R)/\mathrm{Sp}(R)
, but the qualitative physics remains the

;
Fava et al. 2024
;

(ii) We examine

. This mass induces a correlation length

II
Quantum-jump processes

We begin by introducing the description of discrete-time quantum-jump processes
in terms of Kraus operators. In the main text, we focus on formal aspects; we
review the physical realization of quantum-jump processes in
Appendix
A
.

II.1
Kraus operators

We consider the dynamics of a fermionic or bosonic quantum system subjected to
continuous measurements. At time
t_{0}
the system is initialized in the pure
state
\lvert\psi_{0}\rangle
. The time interval from
t_{0}
to
t=t_{0}+T
is
partitioned into
N
time steps of duration
\Delta t=T/N
. Evolution during a
single time step is described by a set of Kraus operators
\hat{K}_{\alpha}

with
\alpha\in\mathsf{A}_{0}=\{0,\dotsc,A\}
. The operator
\hat{K}_{0}

corresponds to no quantum jump occurring during the time step and is defined
by
Jacobs 2014

\hat{K}_{0}=1-\mathrm{i}\left(\hat{H}\Delta t-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\hat{K}_{a}^{\dagger}\hat{K}_{a}\right)=1-\mathrm{i}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta t,

(1)

where
\hat{H}
is the Hamiltonian. No-jump evolution described by
\hat{K}_{0}

is continuous in the sense that
\hat{K}_{0}\to 1
for
\Delta t\to 0
. In
contrast, quantum jumps, which are described by the remaining Kraus operators

\hat{K}_{a}
with
a\in\mathsf{A}=\{1,\dotsc,A\}
, induce discontinuous
evolution with

\hat{K}_{a}=\sqrt{\gamma_{a}\Delta t}\,\hat{c}_{a}.

(2)

We do not impose any restrictions on the jump operators
\hat{c}_{a}
: they can be
arbitrary fermionic or bosonic operators. In terms of the jump operators, the
effective non-Hermitian Hamiltonian introduced in Eq. (
1
) is given by

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\hat{H}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\gamma_{a}\hat{c}_{a}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{a}.

(3)

Throughout, Greek indices label the full set of Kraus operators,
while Latin indices label only those corresponding to quantum jumps:

\alpha\in\mathsf{A}_{0}=\{0,\dotsc,A\},\qquad a\in\mathsf{A}=\{1,\dotsc,A\}.

(4)

The Kraus operators satisfy the completeness relation

\sum_{\alpha=0}^{A}\hat{K}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{K}_{\alpha}=1,

(5)

up to corrections that vanish in the continuous-time limit
N\to\infty
such
that
\Delta t\to 0
. Consequently, the set of Kraus operators defines a
quantum channel describing the deterministic evolution of the density matrix
over a single time step,

\hat{\rho}\mapsto\sum_{\alpha=0}^{A}\hat{K}_{\alpha}\hat{\rho}\hat{K}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}=\left(1+\mathcal{L}\Delta t\right)\hat{\rho},

(6)

starting from the initial pure state
\hat{\rho}_{0}=\lvert\psi_{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0}\rvert
. The Liouville superoperator or Lindbladian is

\mathcal{L}\hat{\rho}=-\mathrm{i}\left[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}\right]+\sum_{a=1}^{A}\gamma_{a}\left(\hat{c}_{a}\hat{\rho}\hat{c}_{a}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{c}_{a}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{a},\hat{\rho}\right\}\right).

(7)

In the continuous-time limit, Eq. (
6
) can be recast as a
Markovian quantum master equation in Lindblad form.

II.2
Quantum trajectories

Formally, a quantum-jump process is a stochastic unraveling of the quantum
channel (
6
). As a computational tool, such unravelings
find widespread application in the numerical simulation of open-system
dynamics
Plenio and Knight 1998
;
Daley 2014
. We begin by considering the unraveling
into pure-state quantum trajectories corresponding to perfectly efficient
detection of quantum jumps; modifications arising from finite detection
efficiency will be introduced further below in
Sec.
V
.

In such a stochastic unraveling, one Kraus operator
\hat{K}_{\alpha}
is drawn at
random and applied to the state during each time step. For a system in the state

\lvert\psi\rangle
, the operator
\hat{K}_{\alpha}
is chosen with probability

P_{\alpha}=\left\lVert\hat{K}_{\alpha}\lvert\psi\rangle\right\rVert^{2}=\langle\psi|\hat{K}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{K}_{\alpha}|\psi\rangle.

(8)

If
\alpha=0
meaning that no jump occurs during the time step, the system
evolves continuously according to

\lvert\psi\rangle\mapsto\frac{\hat{K}_{0}\lvert\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{P_{0}}}=\left[1-\mathrm{i}\left(\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}-\mathrm{i}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\gamma_{a}\langle\psi|\hat{c}_{a}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{a}|\psi\rangle\right)\Delta t\right]\lvert\psi\rangle,

(9)

Otherwise, for
\alpha=a\in\{1,\dotsc,A\}
, the system undergoes a
discontinuous quantum jump described by

\lvert\psi\rangle\mapsto\frac{\hat{K}_{a}\lvert\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{P_{a}}}=\frac{\hat{c}_{a}\lvert\psi\rangle}{\lVert\hat{c}_{a}\lvert\psi\rangle\rVert}.

(10)

While we introduce quantum-jump processes here from a formal perspective, a
microscopic derivation is presented in
Appendix
A
. There, we consider a minimal setup in
which the system is coupled to independent bosonic or fermionic baths, one for
each jump channel. Bath occupations are monitored continuously: Detection of no
excitation in any bath occurs with probability
P_{0}
and yields no-jump
evolution, whereas detection of an excitation in bath
a
, with probability

P_{a}
, induces the corresponding jump
Gardiner and Zoller 2014
;
Gardiner and Zoller 2015
;
Wiseman and Milburn 2009
;
Jacobs 2014
. Continuously measuring bath occupations thus
produces precisely the stochastic unraveling of Eq. (
6
)
described above. For this reason, we refer to the index
\alpha
specifying the
evolution during a given time step as a measurement outcome.

A quantum trajectory over
N
time steps is specified by a sequence of
measurement outcomes
\alpha_{n}
with
n\in\mathsf{N}=\{1,\dotsc,N\}
,
collected into the vector

\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{N})\in\mathsf{A}_{0}^{N}
. The
probability for this sequence is

P_{\bm{\alpha}}=\left\lVert\hat{K}_{\alpha_{N}}\dotsb\hat{K}_{\alpha_{1}}\lvert\psi_{0}\rangle\right\rVert^{2},

(11)

and the corresponding normalized state is

\lvert\psi_{\bm{\alpha}}\rangle=\left.\hat{K}_{\alpha_{N}}\dotsb\hat{K}_{\alpha_{1}}\lvert\psi_{0}\rangle\middle/\sqrt{P_{\bm{\alpha}}}\right..

(12)

This expression describes the evolution of the quantum state
conditioned

on a specific sequence of measurement outcomes. By contrast,
Eq. (
6
) captures the
unconditional
dynamics, in
which one averages over all possible outcomes.

Observables of interest are functions
f_{\bm{\alpha}}
of the
normalized state or, more generally, of the measurement record itself. The
average of
f_{\bm{\alpha}}
over the ensemble of trajectories is

\overline{f_{\bm{\alpha}}}=\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathsf{A}_{0}^{N}}P_{\bm{\alpha}}f_{\bm{\alpha}}.

(13)

For observables linear in the state, such as the expectation value

f_{\bm{\alpha}}=\langle\psi_{\bm{\alpha}}|\hat{O}|\psi_{\bm{\alpha}}\rangle
, this reduces to

\overline{\langle\psi_{\bm{\alpha}}|\hat{O}|\psi_{\bm{\alpha}}\rangle}=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\hat{O}\hat{\rho}),\qquad\hat{\rho}=\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathsf{A}_{0}^{N}}P_{\bm{\alpha}}\lvert\psi_{\bm{\alpha}}\rangle\langle\psi_{\bm{\alpha}}\rvert.

(14)

That is, the expectation value may be expressed in terms of the
ensemble-averaged density matrix. Nonlinear observables, such as the von Neumann
entropy of a subsystem, do not admit such a simplification.

This framework encompasses random projective
Poboiko et al. 2023
or generalized
measurements
Starchl et al. 2025
, performed at a fixed rate
\gamma
, as a
special case. In this setting, the rates
\gamma_{a}
in Eq. (
2
) are
identical and equal to the measurement rate,
\gamma_{a}=\gamma
, and the jump
operators are measurement operators and thus obey the completeness relation

\sum_{a=1}^{A}\hat{c}_{a}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{a}=1,

(15)

in addition to the Kraus-operator completeness
relation (
5
). The completeness
relation (
15
) leads to the
total probability of a jump occurring in a single time step being
state-independent,

P_{\mathrm{jump}}=\sum_{a\in\mathsf{A}}P_{a}=\gamma\Delta t.

(16)

Therefore, jumps may indeed be identified with measurements performed at a fixed
rate
\gamma
. For projective measurements, the measurement operators
additionally satisfy
\hat{c}_{a}=\hat{c}_{a}^{\dagger}=\hat{c}_{a}^{2}
and project
onto eigenspaces of the measured observable. Then, the
Liouvillian (
7
) takes the form

\mathcal{L}\hat{\rho}=-\mathrm{i}\left[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}\right]+\gamma\left(\sum_{a=1}^{A}\hat{c}_{a}\hat{\rho}\hat{c}_{a}-\hat{\rho}\right).

(17)

The completely mixed state
\hat{\rho}=1/D
, with
D
the Hilbert-space
dimension, is stationary under evolution generated by this Liouvillian and, for
spatially extended systems with local measurements, is generically the unique
steady state—independent of all system parameters, including the measurement
rate
\gamma
. Consequently, random projective measurements yield nontrivial
effects only in nonlinear trajectory-dependent observables. By contrast, for
general non-Hermitian jump operators, the steady state of
Eq. (
7
) is typically nontrivial, implying that both linear
and nonlinear observables depend sensitively on system parameters.

II.3
Rescaled Kraus operators and continuous-time limit

We have introduced quantum-jump processes in discrete time, which provides a
convenient starting point for the construction of a functional-integral
representation of the trajectory average (
13
)
presented in Sec.
IV
. However, in this construction, we
ultimately want to take the continuous-time limit. Therefore, we now discuss how
Eq. (
13
) can be rewritten in a form that is
suitable for taking this limit.

As we will see, taking the continuous-time limit is facilitated by introducing
rescaled Kraus operators,

\hat{J}_{\alpha}=\left.\hat{K}_{\alpha}\middle/\sqrt{q_{\alpha}}\right.,\qquad q_{0}=1-\Gamma_{0}\Delta t,\qquad q_{a}=\Gamma_{a}\Delta t,

(18)

which take the explicit form

\hat{J}_{0}=1-\mathrm{i}\left(\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}+\frac{\mathrm{i}\Gamma_{0}}{2}\right)\Delta t,\qquad\hat{J}_{a}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{a}}{\Gamma_{a}}}\,\hat{c}_{a}.

(19)

The rates
\Gamma_{a}
may be chosen freely. We will consider two natural
choices: a
uniform rescaling
with identical rates

\Gamma_{a}=\Gamma_{1}
, and a
detailed rescaling
with arbitrary

\Gamma_{a}
. Although these choices lead to slightly different intermediate
representations of the replica Keldysh field theory, the differences disappear
in the replica limit. Detailed rescaling includes the uniform case as a special
instance, yet we discuss both separately because they lend themselves to
different intermediate interpretations.

In terms of the rescaled Kraus operators, the trajectory probability

P_{\bm{\alpha}}
in Eq. (
11
) factorizes as

P_{\bm{\alpha}}=Q_{\bm{\alpha}}O_{\bm{\alpha}}
,
with

Q_{\bm{\alpha}}=\prod_{n=1}^{N}q_{\alpha_{n}},\qquad O_{\bm{\alpha}}=\left\|\hat{J}_{\alpha_{N}}\cdots\hat{J}_{\alpha_{1}}\lvert\psi_{0}\rangle\right\|^{2}.

(20)

The average of
f_{\bm{\alpha}}
can then be written as

\overline{f_{\bm{\alpha}}}=\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathsf{A}_{0}^{N}}Q_{\bm{\alpha}}O_{\bm{\alpha}}f_{\bm{\alpha}}.

(21)

This expression naturally suggests interpreting the average as being taken with
respect to the distribution
Q_{\bm{\alpha}}
, which is defined in
Eq. (
20
) as a product of the probabilities
q_{0}
and

q_{a}
, Eq. (
18
), for no jump and a jump of type
a
to
occur in a given time step. Under this interpretation, the quantity being
averaged is the product
O_{\bm{\alpha}}f_{\bm{\alpha}}
. It is
important to stress, however, that
Q_{\bm{\alpha}}
is
not
the
physical probability of the trajectory
\bm{\alpha}
; the physical
probability remains
P_{\bm{\alpha}}
.

We next rewrite Eq. (
21
) in a form suitable for
taking the continuous-time limit, first considering the case of uniform
rescaling and subsequently turning to the more general case of detailed
rescaling.

II.3.1
Uniform rescaling

For uniform rescaling, the probabilities
q_{a}
all take the same value

q_{1}=\Gamma_{1}\Delta t
. Then, the probability
Q_{\bm{\alpha}}
in
Eq. (
20
) depends only on the total number of jumps

M_{\bm{\alpha}}
, that is, the number of measurement outcomes

\alpha_{n}\neq 0
in the sequence
\bm{\alpha}
,

Q_{\bm{\alpha}}=Q_{M_{\bm{\alpha}}}=q_{0}^{N-M_{\bm{\alpha}}}q_{1}^{M_{\bm{\alpha}}}.

(22)

To perform the average over trajectories in
Eq. (
21
), we choose a different parameterization
of trajectories: instead of specifying the values of
\alpha_{n}
in each time
step, we fully determine a trajectory by specifying the total number of jumps

M
, the time steps
n_{m}
with
m\in\{1,\dotsc,M\}
or corresponding times

t_{m}=t_{0}+n_{m}\Delta t
at which the jumps occur, and the types of jumps

a_{m}\in\mathsf{A}
. We collect the time-step and jump indices in vectors

\mathbf{n}=\left(n_{1},\dotsc,n_{M}\right)
with
n_{1}<\dotsb<n_{M}
and

\mathbf{a}=\left(a_{1},\dotsc,a_{M}\right)
. These vectors uniquely determine
a vector
\bm{\alpha}_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{a}}
of length
N
: all
elements with index
n
not contained in
\mathbf{n}
vanish

\left(\bm{\alpha}_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{a}}\right)_{n}=0
,
corresponding to no-jump evolution, whereas

\left(\bm{\alpha}_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{a}}\right)_{n_{m}}=a_{m}
. We
can thus write the average (
21
) as

\begin{split}\overline{f_{\bm{\alpha}}}=\sum_{M=0}^{N}Q_{M}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathsf{A}^{M}}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathsf{O}_{M,N}}O_{\bm{\alpha}_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{a}}}f_{\bm{\alpha}_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{a}}},\end{split}

(23)

where the third sum is over all ordered
M
-tuples with each element in

\mathsf{N}=\{1,\dotsc,N\}
,

\mathsf{O}_{M,N}=\left\{\mathsf{N}^{M}:n_{1}<\dotsb<n_{M}\right\}.

(24)

As stated above, we want to bring this in a form that enables taking the
continuous-time limit. For the quantities

O_{\bm{\alpha}_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{a}}}
and

f_{\bm{\alpha}_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{a}}}
, this is achieved by noting
that they are functions of the jump times,

\mathbf{t}=\left(t_{1},\dotsc,t_{M}\right)
, with

t_{m}=t_{0}+n\Delta t\in[t_{0},t]
, and the jump indices,

O_{\bm{\alpha}_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{a}}}=O_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{t}),\qquad f_{\bm{\alpha}_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{a}}}=f_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{t}).

(25)

Furthermore, in the sums in Eq. (
23
), we first
remove the restriction of the elements of
\mathbf{n}
being ordered and extend
the summation over all
\mathbf{n}\in\mathsf{N}^{M}
. When we do this, we have
to include a factor
d_{\mathbf{n}}
that ensures that all elements
n_{m}
are
different, that is,
d_{\mathbf{n}}=0
if
n_{m}=n_{m^{\prime}}
for at least one pair
of elements, and
d_{\mathbf{n}}=1
otherwise. Further, for any given ordered
sequence
\mathbf{n}
, the unrestricted sum contains all possible
M!

permutations of the elements
n_{m}
. Therefore, we have to include a factor

1/M!
to compensate the resultant overcounting,

\overline{f_{\bm{\alpha}}}=\sum_{M=0}^{N}\frac{1}{M!}Q_{M}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathsf{A}^{M}}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathsf{N}^{M}}d_{\mathbf{n}}O_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{t})f_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{t}).

(26)

In the continuous-time limit, the sums over the components of
\mathbf{n}

become integrals. The factor
d_{\mathbf{n}}
has support on a set of measure
zero in the domain of integration
[t_{0},t]^{M}
, and may thus be dropped,

\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathsf{N}^{M}}d_{\mathbf{n}}=\frac{1}{\Delta t^{M}}\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{N}\Delta t\dotsb\sum_{n_{M}=1}^{N}\Delta t\,d_{\mathbf{n}}\sim N^{M}\prod_{m=1}^{M}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}t_{m}}{T}.

(27)

Combining the prefactor
N^{M}
with
Q_{M}/M!
in
Eq. (
26
), we obtain in the limit

N\to\infty
a Poisson distribution for the number of jumps during the
evolution time
T=t-t_{0}
,

\frac{N^{M}}{M!}Q_{M}=\frac{\left(\Gamma_{1}T\right)^{M}}{M!}\left(1-\frac{\Gamma_{0}T}{N}\right)^{N-M}\to\left(\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{\Gamma_{0}}\right)^{M}P_{M}(T),

(28)

where

P_{M}(T)=\frac{\left(\Gamma_{0}T\right)^{M}}{M!}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{0}T},

(29)

and we have used

\left(1-\frac{\Gamma_{0}T}{N}\right)^{N}\to\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{0}T},\qquad\left(1-\frac{\Gamma_{0}T}{N}\right)^{-M}\to 1.

(30)

Therefore, in the continuous-time limit,
Eq. (
26
) becomes

\overline{f_{\bm{\alpha}}}\sim\sum_{M=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{\Gamma_{0}}\right)^{M}P_{M}(T)\left(\prod_{m=1}^{M}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}t_{m}}{T}\sum_{a_{m}=1}^{A}\right)O_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{t})f_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{t}).

(31)

This expression is directly analogous to the average over random projective or
generalized measurements performed at a rate
\gamma

Poboiko et al. 2023
;
Starchl et al. 2025
. To make the analogy explicit, consider for concreteness a 1D
fermionic lattice of size
L
with local measurements. Quantum jumps correspond
to applying measurement operators, and the jump-type index
a
becomes a double
index, where
n
labels the measurement outcome and
l
labels the lattice
site. We focus here on projective measurements of local occupation numbers with
outcomes
n\in\{0,1\}
. For the choice
\Gamma_{1}=\gamma
and

\Gamma_{0}=L\Gamma_{1}
, Eq. (
31
)
takes the form

\overline{f_{\bm{\alpha}}}\sim\sum_{M=0}^{\infty}P_{M}(T)\left(\prod_{m=1}^{M}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}t_{m}}{LT}\sum_{l_{m}=1}^{L}\sum_{n_{m}=0}^{1}\right)O_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{t})f_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{t}).

(32)

This representation has a clear interpretation: when measurements occur at rate

\gamma
, the number of measurements in a time interval
T
is Poisson
distributed (
29
) with mean
\gamma T
. Conditional on
M

measurements, the measurement times are uniformly distributed in

t_{m}\in[t_{0},t]
, and one further averages over the measurement locations and
outcomes. This reproduces precisely the ensemble average employed in
Refs.
Poboiko et al. 2023
;
Starchl et al. 2025
.

More generally, an analogous interpretation applies to
Eq. (
31
) even for non-Hermitian
jump operators, provided one chooses
\Gamma_{0}=A\Gamma_{1}
. In this view, the
expression resembles an average over the number of jumps, their times, and
their types. It is important to stress, however, that this should be regarded
only as an interpretation suggested by the algebraic structure of
Eq. (
31
). Physically, jumps do not
occur at the externally chosen rate
\Gamma_{0}
. The true probability of a
trajectory with a given jump sequence is determined by
Eq. (
11
), which is manifestly independent of the
artificial rates
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{1}
introduced through the rescaling in
Eq. (
18
). In the replica Keldysh functional integral
developed in Sec.
IV
, we will see explicitly that these
rates indeed drop out.

Finally, we consider the special case that we average over an observable that
factorizes over jumps:

O_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{t})f_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{t})=\prod_{m=1}^{M}g_{a_{m}}(t_{m}).

(33)

Then, using

\prod_{m=1}^{M}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}t_{m}}{T}\sum_{a_{m}=1}^{A}g_{a_{m}}(t_{m})=\left[\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}}{T}\sum_{a=1}^{A}g_{a}(t^{\prime})\right]^{M},

(34)

Eq. (
31
) can be rewritten as

\overline{f_{\bm{\alpha}}}\sim\exp\!\left\{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\left[\Gamma_{1}\sum_{a=1}^{A}g_{a}(t^{\prime})-\Gamma_{0}\right]\right\}.

(35)

II.3.2
Detailed rescaling

For detailed rescaling with
\Gamma_{a}=\gamma_{a}
, the probability

Q_{\bm{\alpha}}
in Eq. (
20
) depends on the
number of jumps
M_{\bm{\alpha},a}
for each individual type of jump

a\in\mathsf{A}
,

Q_{\bm{\alpha}}=Q_{\mathbf{M}_{\bm{\alpha}}}=q_{0}^{N-M_{\bm{\alpha}}}\prod_{a=1}^{A}q_{a}^{M_{\bm{\alpha},a}},

(36)

where

\mathbf{M}_{\bm{\alpha}}=\left(M_{\bm{\alpha},1},\dotsc,M_{\bm{\alpha},A}\right)
,
and again
M_{\bm{\alpha}}=\sum_{a=1}^{A}M_{\bm{\alpha},a}

is the total number of jumps. We now choose a different parameterization of
trajectories: for each type of jump
a
, we specify the total number of jumps

M_{a}
, as well as the time steps

\mathbf{n}_{a}=\left(n_{a,1},\dotsc,n_{a,M_{a}}\right)
with

n_{a,1}<\dotsb<n_{a,M}
or equivalently the times

t_{a,m_{a}}=t_{0}+n_{a,m_{a}}\Delta t
at which the jumps occur. Each sequence

\mathbf{n}_{a}
is ordered, and we again include a factor

d_{\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{a}}}
that is unity when all
n_{a,m_{a}}
with

a\in\mathsf{A}
and
m_{a}\in\{1,\dotsc,M_{a}\}
are different, and zero
otherwise. By

\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{a}}=\left(\mathbf{n}_{1},\dotsc,\mathbf{n}_{A}\right)
we
denote the collection of all vectors
\mathbf{n}_{a}
. This collection uniquely
determines a vector
\bm{\alpha}_{\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{a}}}
of length

N
: we set
\left(\bm{\alpha}_{\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{a}}}\right)_{n}=a

for all
n
that are contained in
\mathbf{n}_{a}
. Using this parameterization of
trajectories, we can express the average (
21
) as

\overline{f_{\bm{\alpha}}}=\sum_{M_{1}=0}^{N}\sum_{M_{2}=0}^{N-M_{1}}\dotsb\sum_{M_{A}=0}^{N-\sum_{a=2}^{A}M_{a}}Q_{\mathbf{M}}\\
\times\sum_{\mathbf{n}_{1}\in\mathsf{O}_{M_{1},N}}\dotsb\sum_{\mathbf{n}_{A}\in\mathsf{O}_{M_{a},N}}d_{\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{a}}}O_{\bm{\alpha}_{\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{a}}}}f_{\bm{\alpha}_{\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{a}}}}.

(37)

In analogy to Eq. (
25
), we regard

O_{\bm{\alpha}_{\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{a}}}}
and

f_{\bm{\alpha}_{\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{a}}}}
as functions of

\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{a}}=\left(\mathbf{t}_{1},\dotsc,\mathbf{t}_{A}\right)
,
where for each type of jump
a
, the vector

\mathbf{t}_{a}=\left(t_{a,1},\dotsc,t_{a,M_{a}}\right)
collects the times
at which jumps occurs:

O_{\bm{\alpha}_{\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{a}}}}=O(\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{a}}),\qquad f_{\bm{\alpha}_{\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{a}}}}=f(\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{a}}).

(38)

Furthermore, as we did before for uniform rescaling, we can remove the
restrictions on the sums over
\mathbf{n}_{a}
if we compensate the resultant
overcounting by introducing factors of
1/M_{a}!
,

\overline{f}_{\bm{\alpha}}=\sum_{M_{1}=0}^{N}\sum_{M_{2}=0}^{N-M_{1}}\dotsb\sum_{M_{A}=0}^{N-\sum_{a=2}^{A}M_{a}}Q_{\mathbf{M}}\left(\prod_{a=1}^{A}\frac{1}{M_{a}!}\right)\\
\times\sum_{\mathbf{n}_{1}\in\mathsf{N}^{M_{1}}}\dotsb\sum_{\mathbf{n}_{A}\in\mathsf{N}^{M_{A}}}d_{\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{a}}}O(\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{a}})f(\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{a}}).

(39)

In the continuous-time limit
N\to\infty
, the sums over the components of the
vectors
\mathbf{n}_{a}
are converted to integrals,

\sum_{\mathbf{n}_{a}\in\mathsf{N}^{M_{a}}}\sim N^{M_{a}}\prod_{m_{a}=1}^{M_{a}}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}t_{m}}{T}.

(40)

The factors
N^{M_{a}}
can again be combined with other factors appearing in
Eq. (
39
) to yield a product of
Poisson distributions:

\begin{split}\left(\prod_{a=1}^{A}\frac{N^{M_{a}}}{M_{a}!}\right)Q_{\mathbf{M}}&=\prod_{a=1}^{A}\frac{\left(\Gamma_{a}T\right)^{M_{a}}}{M_{a}!}\left(1-\frac{\Gamma_{0}T}{N}\right)^{N-M_{a}}\\
&\to\mathrm{e}^{-\left(\Gamma_{0}-\sum_{a=1}^{A}\Gamma_{a}\right)T}\prod_{a=1}^{A}P_{a,M_{a}}(T),\end{split}

(41)

where

P_{a,M_{a}}(T)=\frac{\left(\Gamma_{a}T\right)^{M_{a}}}{M_{a}!}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{a}T}.

(42)

For the same reason as above, the factor
d_{\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{a}}}
can be
dropped in the continuous-time limit
N\to\infty
. Thus,
Eq. (
39
) becomes

\overline{f_{\bm{\alpha}}}\sim\mathrm{e}^{-\left(\Gamma_{0}-\sum_{a=1}^{A}\Gamma_{a}\right)T}\\
\times\left[\prod_{a=1}^{A}\sum_{M_{a}=0}^{\infty}P_{a,M_{a}}(T)\prod_{m_{a}=1}^{M_{a}}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}t_{a,m_{a}}}{T}\right]O(\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{a}})f(\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{a}}).

(43)

The exponential in the first line vanishes for the choice

\Gamma_{0}=\sum_{a=1}^{A}\Gamma_{a}
. Under this condition,
Eq. (
43
) admits the following
interpretation: jumps of type
a
may be viewed as occurring with rate

\Gamma_{a}
. For each jump type, one then averages over the number of jumps in
the interval
T
, which obeys the Poisson distribution (
42
) with
mean
\Gamma_{a}T
, and over the associated jump times, uniformly distributed in

t_{a,m_{a}}\in[t_{0},t]
. As in the case of uniform rescaling, this
interpretation is merely suggested by the structure of
Eq. (
43
). The actual distribution
of jump types and times is fixed by Eq. (
11
), which is
insensitive to the auxiliary rates
\Gamma_{\alpha}
introduced by the rescaling
prescription.

Analogous to the discussion for uniform rescaling, let us examine the special
case in which the observable factorizes over quantum jumps:

O(\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{a}})f(\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{a}})=\prod_{a=1}^{A}\prod_{m_{a}=1}^{M_{a}}g_{a}(t_{a,m_{a}}).

(44)

Substituting this expression into
Eq. (
43
) and using

\prod_{m_{a}=1}^{M_{a}}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}t_{a,m_{a}}}{T}g_{a}(t_{a,m_{a}})=\left[\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}}{T}g_{a}(t^{\prime})\right]^{M_{a}},

(45)

we arrive at

\overline{f_{\bm{\alpha}}}\sim\exp\!\left\{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\left[\sum_{a=1}^{A}\Gamma_{a}g_{a}(t^{\prime})-\Gamma_{0}\right]\right\}.

(46)

III
Replica formalism

Having shown, for both uniform and detailed rescaling, how the trajectory
average in Eq. (
21
) can be rewritten in a form
amenable to the continuous-time limit, we now specify the quantities whose
averages we aim to compute.

In the dynamics of quantum many-body systems under continuous measurements,
particular interest lies in observables that depend nonlinearly on the quantum
state (
12
). Prominent examples include
subsystem entropies and connected correlation functions. Such observables are
built from trajectory averages of products of quantum expectation values. The
latter can be expressed as expectation values with respect to the
k
-replica
density matrix defined as
1
1

1

For fermionic systems, the tensor product is
understood within the bosonic Hilbert space obtained via a Jordan–Wigner
transformation. Fermionic operators can then be implemented in the replicated
Hilbert space
Fava et al. 2023
;
Fava et al. 2024
.

\hat{\mu}_{k}(t)=\overline{\bigotimes_{r=1}^{k}\hat{\rho}_{r}(t)},

(47)

where the overbar again denotes the trajectory average, and the index
r
labels
identical copies or replicas of the original system. For instance, for two
operators
\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
,

\begin{split}\overline{\langle\hat{A}(t)\rangle\langle\hat{B}(t)\rangle}&=\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left[\hat{A}\hat{\rho}(t)\right]\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left[\hat{B}\hat{\rho}(t)\right]}\\
&=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left\{\left(\hat{A}_{1}\otimes\hat{B}_{2}\right)\overline{\left[\hat{\rho}_{1}(t)\otimes\hat{\rho}_{2}(t)\right]}\right\}\\
&=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left[\left(\hat{A}_{1}\otimes\hat{B}_{2}\right)\hat{\mu}_{2}\right].\end{split}

(48)

To evaluate the
k
-replica density matrix
\hat{\mu}_{k}(t)
, we first express a
single-replica density matrix as

\hat{\rho}_{r}(t)=\left.\hat{D}_{r}(t)\middle/\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left[\hat{D}_{r}(t)\right],\right.

(49)

with the unnormalized density matrix

\hat{D}_{r}(t)=\hat{J}_{r,\alpha_{N}}\dotsb\hat{J}_{r,\alpha_{1}}\lvert\psi_{r,0}\rangle\langle\psi_{r,0}\rvert\hat{J}_{r,\alpha_{1}}^{\dagger}\dotsb\hat{J}_{r,\alpha_{N}}^{\dagger},

(50)

where
\hat{J}_{r,\alpha}
are the Kraus operators (
19
) acting on
replica
r
, and
\lvert\psi_{r,0}\rangle
is the initial state in the corresponding
Hilbert space. Note that since each replica is an identical copy, we have

\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left[\hat{D}_{1}(t)\right]=\dotsb=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left[\hat{D}_{R}(t)\right]=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left[\hat{D}(t)\right].

(51)

The product of Kraus operators in Eq. (
50
)
describes the evolution from
t_{0}
to
t=t_{N}=t_{0}+N\Delta t
, which
justifies denoting this expression by
\hat{D}_{r}(t)
. For brevity, the explicit
dependence of
\hat{D}_{r}(t)
on
\bm{\alpha}
is suppressed.

We then insert Eq. (
49
) into Eq. (
47
) and employ
Eq. (
21
) to write the trajectory average. Noting
that the denominator in Eq. (
49
) is simply the quantity

O_{\bm{\alpha}}
defined in Eq. (
20
), namely

O_{\bm{\alpha}}=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}[\hat{D}(t)]
, we thus obtain

\begin{split}\hat{\mu}_{k}(t)&=\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathsf{A}_{0}^{N}}Q_{\bm{\alpha}}\frac{\otimes_{r=1}^{k}\hat{D}_{r}(t)}{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left[\hat{D}(t)\right]^{k-1}}\\
&=\lim_{R\to 1}\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathsf{A}_{0}^{N}}Q_{\bm{\alpha}}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{r=k+1,\dotsc,R}}\!\left[\bigotimes_{r=1}^{R}\hat{D}_{r}(t)\right],\end{split}

(52)

where the second equality implements the standard replica trick. Before this
step, the rates
\Gamma_{\alpha}
defined in Eq. (
18
)
cancelled term by term in the sum over
\bm{\alpha}
. This cancellation
is lost once we exchange the order of summing over trajectories with the limit

R\to 1
. For any
R>1
, individual terms retain explicit dependence on

\Gamma_{\alpha}
, so different choices of
\Gamma_{\alpha}
become equivalent
only after the replica limit is taken.

Averages such as Eq. (
48
) can then be obtained by introducing
source fields into the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
in Eq. (
1
) and
differentiating the corresponding
R
-replica Keldysh partition function with
respect to these sources,

Z_{R}(t)=\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathsf{A}_{0}^{N}}Q_{\bm{\alpha}}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left[\bigotimes_{r=1}^{R}\hat{D}_{r}(t)\right]=\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathsf{A}_{0}^{N}}Q_{\bm{\alpha}}\prod_{r=1}^{R}Z_{r}(t),

(53)

where
Z_{r}(t)=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left[\hat{D}_{r}(t)\right]
. After taking the
derivatives that generate the desired observables, the source fields have to be
set to zero. The replica limit
R\to 1
has to be taken only at the end of the
computation.

IV
Replica Keldysh partition function

Building on the groundwork laid in Secs.
II
and

III
, we now derive a functional‐integral representation
of the partition function (
53
) using the Keldysh
technique
Altland and Simons 2010
;
Kamenev 2023
;
Sieberer et al. 2016
;
Sieberer et al. 2025
;
Thompson and Kamenev 2023
. To begin, we consider a single factor
Z_{r}(t)
in the product
over replicas in Eq. (
53
). Using
Eq. (
50
),
Z_{r}(t)
takes the form

Z_{r}(t)=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left(\hat{J}_{r,\alpha_{N}}\dotsb\hat{J}_{r,\alpha_{1}}\lvert\psi_{r,0}\rangle\langle\psi_{r,0}\rvert\hat{J}_{r,\alpha_{1}}^{\dagger}\dotsb\hat{J}_{r,\alpha_{N}}^{\dagger}\right).

(54)

Within the Keldysh formalism, the products of Kraus operators acting on the ket
and bra components of
\lvert\psi_{r,0}\rangle\langle\psi_{r,0}\rvert
are interpreted as
describing evolution along the forward and backward branches of the closed time
contour. A functional‐integral representation follows from inserting
coherent-state resolutions of the identity between successive Kraus
operators. This construction yields fields
\psi_{\sigma,r}(t)
and

\psi_{\sigma,r}^{*}(t)
, defined on both Keldysh branches
\sigma=\pm
and for
each replica
r\in\{1,\dotsc,R\}
. Additional indices, such as those labeling
lattice sites, are left implicit. For bosonic systems the two fields are complex
conjugates, while for fermionic systems they are independent Grassmann
variables. We thus obtain

Z_{r}(t)=\int\mathrm{D}[\psi_{r}^{*},\psi_{r}]\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\left(S_{r,L}[\psi_{r}^{*},\psi_{r}]+S_{r,D}[\psi_{r}^{*},\psi_{r}]\right)}Z_{r,J}[\psi_{r}^{*},\psi_{r}].

(55)

The exponential factor describes continuous evolution and consists of two
contributions: first, a Liouvillian term
S_{r,L}[\psi_{r}^{*},\psi_{r}]
, which
accounts for unitary evolution generated by the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
and, as
discussed in Sec.
V
, acquires additional
dissipative contributions in the case of inefficient detection; second, a decay
term
S_{r,D}[\psi_{r}^{*},\psi_{r}]
associated with the anti-Hermitian part of
the effective Hamiltonian (
3
). The remaining factor,

Z_{r,J}[\psi_{r}^{*},\psi_{r}]
, encodes the effect of quantum jumps. Collecting
the contributions from all replicas, the full replica partition
function (
53
) can be written as

Z_{R}(t)=\int\mathrm{D}[\psi^{*},\psi]\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\left(S_{L}[\psi^{*},\psi]+S_{D}[\psi^{*},\psi]+S_{J}[\psi^{*},\psi]\right)},

(56)

where

S_{L}[\psi^{*},\psi]=\sum_{r=1}^{R}S_{r,L}[\psi_{r}^{*},\psi_{r}],\quad S_{D}[\psi^{*},\psi]=\sum_{r=1}^{R}S_{r,D}[\psi_{r}^{*},\psi_{r}],

(57)

and the jump contribution is defined by

\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}S_{J}[\psi^{*},\psi]}=\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathsf{A}_{0}^{N}}Q_{\bm{\alpha}}\prod_{r=1}^{R}Z_{r,J}[\psi_{r}^{*},\psi_{r}].

(58)

In the following, we derive explicit expressions for
S_{L}[\psi^{*},\psi]
,

S_{D}[\psi^{*},\psi]
, and
S_{J}[\psi^{*},\psi]
.

IV.1
Hamiltonian and dissipative Keldysh action

For continuous evolution, the Hamiltonian part of
\hat{J}_{r,0}
in
Eq. (
19
) produces, upon exponentiation, the standard Hamiltonian
contribution to the Keldysh action, while the remaining terms in
\hat{J}_{r,0}

give rise to a decay contribution. Accordingly, the Liouvillian and decay
components of the Keldysh action are

\begin{split}S_{L}[\psi^{*},\psi]&=\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\sigma\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left[\psi_{\sigma,r}^{*}(t^{\prime})\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\psi_{\sigma,r}(t^{\prime})-H_{\sigma,r}(t^{\prime})\right],\\
\mathrm{i}S_{D}[\psi^{*},\psi]&=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left[\sum_{a=1}^{A}\gamma_{a}D_{\sigma,r,a}(t^{\prime})-\Gamma_{0}\right].\end{split}

(59)

In these expressions,
H_{\sigma,r}(t)
and
D_{\sigma,r,a}(t)
denote matrix
elements in coherent states,

\displaystyle H_{\sigma,r}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\zeta_{\sigma}\psi_{\sigma}(t)|\hat{H}_{r}|\zeta_{\sigma}\psi_{\sigma}(t)\rangle}{\langle\zeta_{\sigma}\psi_{\sigma}(t)|\zeta_{\sigma}\psi_{\sigma}(t)\rangle},

(60)

\displaystyle D_{\sigma,r,a}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\zeta_{\sigma}\psi_{\sigma}(t)|\hat{c}_{r,a}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{r,a}|\zeta_{\sigma}\psi_{\sigma}(t)\rangle}{\langle\zeta_{\sigma}\psi_{\sigma}(t)|\zeta_{\sigma}\psi_{\sigma}(t)\rangle},

(61)

where
\zeta_{\sigma}=+1
for bosons and
\zeta_{\sigma}=\sigma
for fermions;
that is, as standard in the Keldysh formalism, the sign of fermionic fields on
the backward branch is flipped
Sieberer et al. 2025
.

According to Eq. (
2
), the probability of a quantum jump in a single
time step is
O(\Delta t)
. As a result, a typical trajectory consists of extended
periods of continuous evolution, described by
\hat{J}_{0,r}
in
Eq. (
19
), interrupted by isolated jumps represented by

\hat{J}_{a,r}
. In the continuous-time limit, the jump times form a set of
measure zero within the evolution interval
[t_{0},t]
, so that the integrals in
Eq. (
59
) extend over the entire evolution.

IV.2
Jump Keldysh action

Having discussed the Liouvillian and decay contributions to the Keldysh action,
which encode the continuous evolution, we next turn to the contribution due to
quantum jumps (
58
). Here we distinguish between uniform and
detailed rescaling.

IV.2.1
Jump Keldysh action for uniform rescaling

As discussed in Sec.
II.3.1
, for uniform rescaling, each
trajectory is specified by the jump times
t_{m}
and the jump types
a_{m}
. In
this case,
Z_{r,J}
is given by the product of coherent-state matrix elements
of the rescaled jump operators (
19
),

Z_{r,J}=\prod_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\gamma_{a_{m}}}{\Gamma_{1}}V_{r,a_{m}}(t_{m}),

(62)

with the vertex

V_{r,a}(t)=c_{+,r,a}(t)c_{-,r,a}^{\dagger}(t),

(63)

where
c_{+,r,a}(t)
and
c_{-,r,a}^{\dagger}(t)
are defined analogously to
Eqs. (
60
) and (
61
):

\begin{split}c_{\sigma,r,a}(t)&=\frac{\langle\zeta_{\sigma}\psi_{\sigma}(t)|\hat{c}_{r,a}|\zeta_{\sigma}\psi_{\sigma}(t)\rangle}{\langle\zeta_{\sigma}\psi_{\sigma}(t)|\zeta_{\sigma}\psi_{\sigma}(t)\rangle},\\
c_{\sigma,r,a}^{\dagger}(t)&=\frac{\langle\zeta_{\sigma}\psi_{\sigma}(t)|\hat{c}_{r,a}^{\dagger}|\zeta_{\sigma}\psi_{\sigma}(t)\rangle}{\langle\zeta_{\sigma}\psi_{\sigma}(t)|\zeta_{\sigma}\psi_{\sigma}(t)\rangle}.\end{split}

(64)

Since Eq. (
62
) factorizes over jumps, we can use
Eq. (
35
) to perform the
average over trajectories and thus derive the jump

58
), resulting in

\mathrm{i}S_{J}[\psi^{*},\psi]=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\left[\Gamma_{1}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\prod_{r=1}^{R}\frac{\gamma_{a}}{\Gamma_{1}}V_{r,a}(t^{\prime})-\Gamma_{0}\right].

(65)

It is straightforward to verify that terms containing
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{1}

cancel in the replica limit

when summing the decay and jump actions in
Eq. (
56
).

IV.2.2

The parameterization of trajectories for detailed rescaling is given at the

and the times

(66)

Inserting this in Eq. (

) and using

) to take the

\mathrm{i}S_{J}[\psi^{*},\psi]=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\left[\sum_{a=1}^{A}\Gamma_{a}\prod_{r=1}^{R}\frac{\gamma_{a}}{\Gamma_{a}}V_{r,a}(t^{\prime})-\Gamma_{0}\right].

(67)

V
Inefficient detection

In studies of measurement-induced phase transitions, measurements are usually
assumed to be perfect, meaning that they extract complete information about the
measured observable. Under such idealized conditions, the dynamics preserve the
purity of the quantum state. In any experimental realization, however,
imperfections are unavoidable
Ladewig et al. 2022
;
Minoguchi et al. 2022
;
Passarelli et al. 2024
;
Leung et al. 2025
;
Leung and Romito 2025
;
Paviglianiti et al. 2025
: states decohere due to imperfect
measurements, classical noise, and unwanted coupling to the environment, leading
to mixed-state dynamics. A particularly relevant example is inefficient
detection, where only a fraction of the quantum jumps is
registered
Gardiner and Zoller 2014
;
Gardiner and Zoller 2015
;
Wiseman and Milburn 2009
;
Jacobs 2014
. The
fraction of detected jumps is quantified by the detection efficiency

\eta\in[0,1]
, interpolating between the limit of fully efficient detection
with
\eta=1
, which yields pure-state trajectories, and that of fully
inefficient detection with
\eta=0
, where the dynamics become deterministic
and are governed by the Liouvillian (
7
).

The fate of measurement-induced phase transitions at finite
\eta
depends
sensitively on the structure of the jump operators. As discussed in
Sec.
II.2
, when jump operators represent random
projective measurements, the stationary state of the deterministic Lindbladian
dynamics is typically a featureless infinite-temperature state, regardless of
the measurement rate. Consequently, signatures of measurement-induced phase
transitions vanish entirely in the limit
\eta\to 0
. By contrast, for
non-Hermitian jump operators, the steady state is generically nontrivial even at

\eta=0
. The parameter
\eta
therefore provides a natural interpolation
between measurement-induced phase transitions of continuously monitored systems
and phase transitions in the nonequilibrium steady states of driven open quantum
matter.

We model a detector of finite efficiency
\eta\in[0,1]
by splitting each time
step
\Delta t
into two substeps of duration
\eta\Delta t
and

\left(1-\eta\right)\Delta t
, respectively
Jacobs 2014
. The Kraus
operators in Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
) are then modified as
follows: For the first time substep,

\hat{K}_{1,0}=1-\mathrm{i}\eta\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta t,\qquad\hat{K}_{1,a}=\sqrt{\eta\gamma_{a}\Delta t}\,\hat{c}_{a},

(68)

and for the second substep,

\hat{K}_{2,0}=1-\mathrm{i}\left(1-\eta\right)\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta t,\quad\hat{K}_{2,a}=\sqrt{\left(1-\eta\right)\gamma_{a}\Delta t}\,\hat{c}_{a}.

(69)

Inefficient detection is implemented by stochastically unraveling the evolution
during the first substep, while in the second substep we apply deterministic
averaged evolution. The discussion of trajectories in
Sec.
II.2
generalizes accordingly: For a system in a
mixed state
\hat{\rho}
, the probability for an evolution of type
\alpha
to
occur in a single time step is

P_{\alpha}=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left(\mathcal{K}_{2}\mathcal{K}_{1,\alpha}\hat{\rho}\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left(\mathcal{K}_{1,\alpha}\hat{\rho}\right),

(70)

where

\mathcal{K}_{1,\alpha}\hat{\rho}=\hat{K}_{1,\alpha}\hat{\rho}\hat{K}_{1,\alpha}^{\dagger},

(71)

and

\mathcal{K}_{2}\hat{\rho}=\sum_{a=1}^{A}\hat{K}_{2,a}\hat{\rho}\hat{K}_{2,a}^{\dagger}=\left[1+\left(1-\eta\right)\mathcal{L}\Delta t\right]\hat{\rho},

(72)

with the Liouvillian superoperator (
7
). Note that evolution
with
\mathcal{K}_{2}
conserves the trace; this is why
\mathcal{K}_{2}
may be
omitted after the second equality in Eq. (
70
).

A trajectory over
N
time steps is specified by a sequence of outcomes

\bm{\alpha}
of the measurements performed in each first time substep;
the probability for this sequence is

P_{\bm{\alpha}}=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left(\mathcal{K}_{2}\mathcal{K}_{1,\alpha_{N}}\dotsb\mathcal{K}_{2}\mathcal{K}_{1,\alpha_{1}}\hat{\rho}_{0}\right),

(73)

and the normalized state after
N
time steps is

\hat{\rho}_{\bm{\alpha}}=\left.\mathcal{K}_{2}\mathcal{K}_{1,\alpha_{N}}\dotsb\mathcal{K}_{2}\mathcal{K}_{1,\alpha_{1}}\hat{\rho}_{0}\middle/\sqrt{P_{\bm{\alpha}}}\right..

(74)

Having specified the discrete-time quantum jump process for inefficient
detection, we now discuss how the associated Keldysh field theory is modified as
compared to the one for efficient detection. As a preliminary step, it will
prove convenient to modify the factors
q_{\alpha}
appearing in the
definition (
18
) of rescaled Kraus operators for the first
substep by replacing
\Gamma_{\alpha}\to\eta\Gamma_{\alpha}
. In the second
substep, where the evolution is averaged, no rescaling is required.

We begin by examining how inefficient detection modifies the Liouvillian
contribution to the Keldysh action (
59
). Most importantly, the
factors
\mathcal{K}_{2}
appearing in Eq. (
74
)
generate additional terms in the action: evolution implemented by

\mathcal{K}_{2}
in Eq. (
72
) is governed by the Liouvillian
superoperator (
7
), which contains both coherent and
dissipative terms. The latter yield a contribution that can be obtained as
described in Refs.
Kamenev 2023
;
Sieberer et al. 2016
;
Sieberer et al. 2025
;
Thompson and Kamenev 2023
. We find

\!\!\!S_{L}[\psi^{*},\psi]=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left\{\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\sigma\left[\psi_{\sigma,r}^{*}(t^{\prime})\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\psi_{\sigma,r}(t^{\prime})-H_{\sigma,r}(t^{\prime})\right]\right.\\
\left.-\mathrm{i}\left(1-\eta\right)\sum_{a=1}^{A}\gamma_{a}\left[V_{r,a}(t^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma=\pm}D_{\sigma,r,a}(t^{\prime})\right]\right\}.

(75)

Note that the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
appears in the Kraus operators in both time
substeps in Eqs. (
68
) and (
69
) with prefactors

\eta
and
1-\eta
, respectively. Thus,
\eta
cancels in the sum of the
respective contributions to the Keldysh action. In contrast, the dissipative
part stemming from the Liouvillian (
7
) appears only in the
second time substep (
72
). Therefore, in Eq. (
75
) this
contribution appears with a prefactor
1-\eta
.

A second element of the Keldysh action associated with continuous evolution is
the decay term in Eq. (
59
). Under inefficient detection, this term
originates solely from the non-Hermitian part of the effective Hamiltonian in
the first time substep, Eq. (
68
). Although an analogous term also
appears in the second substep (
69
), its effect is already encoded
in Eq. (
75
). Consequently, the decay part of the Keldysh action is
simply multiplied by a factor of
\eta
:

\mathrm{i}S_{D}[\psi^{*},\psi]=-\frac{\eta}{2}\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left[\sum_{a=1}^{A}\gamma_{a}D_{\sigma,r,a}(t^{\prime})-\Gamma_{0}\right].

(76)

We now turn to the jump action. The discussion in
Sec.
II.3
of how to perform the trajectory average
in the continuous-time limit for uniform and detailed rescaling relies on
different ways of parameterizing quantum trajectories, which remain valid for
inefficient detection. The only modification is that due to the rescaling

\Gamma_{\alpha}\to\eta\Gamma_{\alpha}
, the exponents in
Eqs. (
35
)
and (
46
) acquire a
prefactor
\eta
. However,
\eta
drops out of the rescaled Kraus operators
corresponding to quantum jumps. The action that describes jumps is thus

\mathrm{i}S_{J}[\psi^{*},\psi]=\eta\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\left[\sum_{a=1}^{A}\Gamma_{a}\prod_{r=1}^{R}\frac{\gamma_{a}}{\Gamma_{a}}V_{r,a}(t^{\prime})-\Gamma_{0}\right].

(77)

The Keldysh field theory for quantum-jump processes with inefficient detection
is therefore defined by the functional integral in Eq. (
56
),
together with the three contributions to the Keldysh action given in
Eqs. (
75
), (
76
), and (
77
). This forms
one of the central results of our work. For
R>1
, the framework allows one to
evaluate trajectory averages of observables that are nonlinear in the quantum
state, such as the product of expectation values in
Eq. (
48
). Such quantities generally exhibit a nontrivial
dependence on the detection efficiency
\eta
; we will present concrete examples
in Sec.
VI
. In contrast, in the limit

R\to 1
, the construction reduces by design to the standard Lindblad-Keldysh
field theory of Refs.
Sieberer et al. 2016
;
Sieberer et al. 2025
;
Thompson and Kamenev 2023
;
Kamenev 2023
, which captures the dynamics averaged over all measurement
outcomes. In particular, in the sum of Eqs. (
75
),
(
76
), and (
77
), both
\eta
and the auxiliary rates

\Gamma_{\alpha}
cancel out.

VI
Imbalanced and inefficient fermion counting

We now apply the above formalism to the problem of imbalanced and inefficient
fermion counting. Specifically, we consider a 1D fermionic lattice system
subject to monitored fermion gain and loss, where the two occur at different
rates and only a fraction of the corresponding jumps is detected. Our analysis
extends the results of Ref.
Starchl et al. 2025
, which focused on the balanced
and perfectly efficient case. When gain and loss rates coincide, fermion
counting can be cast as a sequence of generalized measurements performed at
random times with a fixed rate, and the associated field theory was derived in
Ref.
Starchl et al. 2025
by adapting the framework introduced in
Ref.
Poboiko et al. 2023
for random projective measurements. In contrast,
handling imbalanced rates lies beyond that construction and requires the more
general approach developed in this work.

VI.1
Model

We consider free fermions on a 1D lattice of size
L
, with annihilation and
creation operators
\hat{\psi}_{l}
and
\hat{\psi}_{l}^{\dagger}
,
respectively. The Hamiltonian is

\hat{H}=\sum_{l,l^{\prime}=1}^{L}\hat{\psi}_{l}^{\dagger}H_{l,l^{\prime}}\hat{\psi}_{l^{\prime}}.

(78)

Our final results focus on nearest-neighbor hopping between sites
l
and

l\pm 1
, described by

H_{l,l^{\prime}}=-J\left(\delta_{l+1,l^{\prime}}+\delta_{l,l^{\prime}+1}\right),

(79)

where Kronecker deltas are understood modulo
L
to impose periodic boundary
conditions. In intermediate steps, however, we keep the discussion general and
leave the Hermitian matrix
H
unspecified.

Fermion counting involves two types of jump operators on each lattice site
describing fermion gain and loss,

\hat{c}_{+,l}=\hat{\psi}_{l}^{\dagger},\qquad\hat{c}_{-,l}=\hat{\psi}_{l},

(80)

with spatially uniform jump rates
\gamma_{\pm}
. Thus, in the notation of the
previous sections, the jump-type index
a
is replaced by the pair of indices

\alpha=\pm
and
l
.

For balanced rates,
\gamma_{+}=\gamma_{-}
, the stationary state of the
unconditional dynamics is a featureless infinite-temperature state, independent
of the jump rate
Starchl et al. 2025
. Sites are uncorrelated, and each site is
empty or occupied with equal probability, corresponding to a mean fermion
density of
n=1/2
. When
\gamma_{+}\neq\gamma_{-}
, the steady state remains
factorized over sites,

\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{ss}}=\prod_{l=1}^{L}\frac{1}{\gamma_{+}+\gamma_{-}}\left[\gamma_{+}\hat{n}_{l}+\gamma_{-}\left(1-\hat{n}_{l}\right)\right],

(81)

where
\hat{n}_{l}=\hat{\psi}_{l}^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}_{l}
. However, the fermion
density in the steady state is now generally different from
n=1/2
and given
by

n=\frac{\gamma_{+}}{\gamma_{+}+\gamma_{-}}.

(82)

Our model therefore provides a minimal example in which the stationary state of
the unconditional dynamics depends nontrivially on the jump rates.

In what follows, instead of specifying the rates
\gamma_{\pm}
, it is often
convenient to use the steady-state density
n
as a free parameter of the model,
together with the average jump rate

\gamma=(\gamma_{+}+\gamma_{-})/2.

(83)

VI.2
Replica Keldysh field theory

Having defined the model, we now apply the results of
Sec.
V
to derive the replica Keldysh field theory
for imbalanced and inefficient fermion counting.

VI.2.1
Fermionic replica Keldysh action

The field-theoretic description of imbalanced and inefficient fermion counting
is encoded in the functional-integral representation of the partition
function (
56
), with the action consisting of the
Liouvillian (
75
), decay (
76
), and jump
terms (
77
), where we set
\Gamma_{\pm}=\gamma_{\pm}
and

\Gamma_{0}=L\gamma
. To obtain the explicit form of the action, we evaluate
the coherent-state matrix elements in Eqs. (
60
), (
61
),
and (
64
), with the last of these determining the vertex in
Eq. (
63
). The calculation relies on fermionic anticommutation
relations to normal-order operators, and on the fact that coherent bra and ket
states are left and right eigenstates of creation and annihilation operators,
respectively. This yields

H_{\sigma,r}(t)=\sum_{l,l^{\prime}=1}^{L}\psi_{\sigma,r,l}^{*}(t)H_{l,l^{\prime}}\psi_{\sigma,r,l^{\prime}}(t).

(84)

The matrix elements (
61
) entering the Liouvillian and decay parts of the
action take the form

\begin{split}D_{\sigma,r,+,l}(t)&=1-\psi^{*}_{\sigma,r,l}(t)\psi_{\sigma,r,l}(t),\\
D_{\sigma,r,-,l}(t)&=\psi^{*}_{\sigma,r,l}(t)\psi_{\sigma,r,l}(t),\end{split}

(85)

and the vertices (
63
) associated with fermion gain and loss are

\begin{split}V_{r,+,l}(t)&=-\psi^{*}_{+,r,l}(t)\psi_{-,r,l}(t),\\
V_{r,-,l}(t)&=-\psi_{+,r,l}(t)\psi^{*}_{-,r,l}(t).\end{split}

(86)

The fermionic replica Keldysh action is the starting point for constructing a
long-wavelength effective field theory, formulated in terms of slow modes built
from bilinears of fermionic fields
Poboiko et al. 2023
;
Starchl et al. 2025
. These
bilinears are introduced as auxiliary bosonic degrees of freedom via a
generalized Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation. Following
Refs.
Poboiko et al. 2023
;
Starchl et al. 2025
, below we define the bilinears in the
symmetrized limit in which both fermionic fields are evaluated at the same time

t
. To prepare for the Hubbard-Stratonovich decoupling, we therefore rewrite
the action in a strictly time-local form using the “principal-value”
regularization scheme
Poboiko et al. 2023
;
Starchl et al. 2025
.

We begin with the matrix elements in Eqs. (
60
)
and (
61
). Although their form appears time-local in the continuous-time
limit, this is not strictly the case: before taking the limit, these expressions
involve matrix elements of
\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{r,l}\hat{\psi}_{r,l^{\prime}}

evaluated between coherent states separated by a single discrete time step. As a
first step, we follow the procedure of Ref.
Yang et al. 2023
to render these
matrix elements strictly time-local, which introduces an additive shift; as a
second step, we apply the principal-value regularization, which induces an
additional shift. Any such shift in Eq. (
84
) cancels when summing
over the Keldysh branch index in Eq. (
75
). The situation is
different for the matrix elements in
Eq. (
85
), which take the form

D_{\sigma,r,\pm,l}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\mp\psi^{*}_{\sigma,r,l}(t)\psi_{\sigma,r,l}(t).

(87)

Since the jump operators (
80
) are linear in the
fermionic fields, the procedure of Ref.
Yang et al. 2023
introduces no additive
shift in the matrix elements (
64
). Moreover, because the
vertices (
86
) involve products of fields residing on different
Keldysh branches, they are unaffected by the principal-value regularization; see
Ref.
Starchl et al. 2025
for details.

In what follows, it is convenient to work in the basis
Poboiko et al. 2025

\psi=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{+}\\
\psi_{-}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\psi^{\dagger}=\left(\psi_{+}^{\dagger},-\psi_{-}^{\dagger}\right),

(88)

where
\psi_{\pm}
and
\psi_{\pm}^{\dagger}
are column- and row-vectors in
replica space. This choice of basis is motivated by the following observations:
The usual Larkin-Ovchinnikov transformation of fermionic fields combines the
bosonic Keldysh rotation with a sign change of
\psi_{-}^{*}

Altland and Simons 2010
;
Kamenev 2023
. This sign change, allowed only for fermionic fields because

\psi_{-}
and
\psi_{-}^{*}
are independent Grassmann variables, greatly
simplifies the quadratic Hamiltonian part of the Keldysh action by reducing it
to the identity in Keldysh space. Here, we implement only the sign change, which
already provides a useful simplification, while omitting the bosonic
rotation. This choice proves convenient for constructing the NLSM target
manifold, whose structure is more transparent in this representation.

In the basis (
88
), the
vertices (
86
) take the compact form

V_{r,\pm,l}(t)=-\psi_{r,l}^{\dagger}(t)\sigma_{\pm}\psi_{r,l}(t),

(89)

where
\sigma_{\pm}=(\sigma_{x}\pm\mathrm{i}\sigma_{y})/2
. Moreover, only the sum
over branch indices of the coherent-state matrix elements in
Eq. (
87
) enters the action in Eqs. (
75
)
and (
76
). This sum can be written as

\sum_{\sigma=\pm}D_{\sigma,r,\pm,l}(t)=1\mp\psi_{r,l}^{\dagger}(t)\sigma_{z}\psi_{r,l}(t).

(90)

We now rewrite the full action in Eq. (
56
) using these compact
expressions. First, we decompose the action as

S=S_{L}+S_{D}+S_{J}=S_{H}+S_{M},

(91)

where
S_{H}
is the Hamiltonian part, the first line of Eq. (
75
),
while
S_{M}
contains the remaining terms due to measurements: the second line of
Eq. (
75
) together with Eqs. (
76
)
and (
77
).

The Hamiltonian action reads

S_{H}[\psi^{*},\psi]=\sum_{l,l^{\prime}=1}^{L}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,\psi_{l}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})\,G_{0,l,l^{\prime}}^{-1}\psi_{l^{\prime}}(t^{\prime}),

(92)

with

G_{0}^{-1}=\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}-H,

(93)

where
H
is the Hamiltonian matrix in Eq. (
78
).

The measurement action takes the form

S_{M}[\psi^{*},\psi]=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,\mathcal{L}_{M}[\psi_{l}^{*}(t^{\prime}),\psi_{l}(t^{\prime})],

(94)

with measurement Lagrangian

\mathrm{i}\mathcal{L}_{M}[\psi^{*},\psi]=-(1-\eta)\,\psi^{\dagger}\Lambda\psi+\frac{\eta\Delta\gamma}{2}\,\psi^{\dagger}\sigma_{z}\psi\\
+\eta\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\gamma_{\alpha}\prod_{r=1}^{R}\left(-\psi_{r}^{\dagger}\sigma_{\alpha}\psi_{r}\right)-\gamma\left[R+\eta\left(1-R\right)\right],

(95)

where the net gain rate is

\Delta\gamma=\gamma_{+}-\gamma_{-}.

(96)

In Eq. (
95
), the matrices
\sigma_{\pm}
,
\sigma_{z}
, and

\Lambda=\begin{pmatrix}1-2n&2n\\
2(1-n)&-1+2n\end{pmatrix},

(97)

with density given in Eq. (
82
), act nontrivially in Keldysh space; the
identity in replica space is left implicit. To obtain the form of
Eq. (
95
), we used

\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\gamma_{\alpha}\left(\sigma_{\alpha}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\sigma_{z}\right)=\gamma\,\Lambda.

(98)

The matrix
\Lambda
encodes the symmetrized equal-time limit of the exact
Green’s functions for a given mean fermion density
Starchl et al. 2025
. It will
play an important role in our analysis of the saddle-point structure of the
Keldysh action.

VI.2.2
Generalized Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation

The generalized Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation is implemented by inserting
the following identity into the functional
integral (
56
)
Poboiko et al. 2023
:

1=\int\mathrm{D}[\mathcal{G},\Sigma]\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\left[\left(\mathcal{G}+\mathrm{i}\psi\psi^{\dagger}\right)\Sigma\right]},

(99)

where
\mathcal{G}
and
\Sigma
are Hermitian
2R\times 2R
matrix fields. We
use
\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}
to denote a trace that acts in Keldysh, replica, lattice, and time
spaces. The integration over
\Sigma
yields a delta functional that enforces
the identity
\mathcal{G}=-\mathrm{i}\psi\psi^{\dagger}
. Together with the
principal-value regularization, this ensures that the expectation value

\langle\mathcal{G}(t)\rangle
coincides with the fermionic Green’s function
in the symmetrized equal-time limit,

\left\langle\mathcal{G}_{l}(t)\right\rangle=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\left(\langle\psi_{l}(t)\psi^{*}_{l}(t+0^{+})\rangle+\langle\psi_{l}(t)\psi^{*}_{l}(t-0^{+})\rangle\right).

(100)

The identity
\mathcal{G}=-\mathrm{i}\psi\psi^{\dagger}
further allows us to
recast the measurement Lagrangian (
95
) entirely in terms of

\mathcal{G}
. For the first two terms in (
95
), we use

\psi^{\dagger}\Lambda\psi=-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left(\Lambda\psi\psi^{\dagger}\right)=-\mathrm{i}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\Lambda\mathcal{G}),\quad\psi^{\dagger}\sigma_{z}\psi=-\mathrm{i}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\sigma_{z}\mathcal{G}).

(101)

Following
Poboiko et al. 2023
, we decouple the product over replicas in the third
term of Eq. (
95
) simultaneously in all slow channels by summing over
Wick contractions, which yields

\mathrm{i}\mathcal{L}_{M}[\mathcal{G}]=\mathrm{i}\left(1-\eta\right)\gamma\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\Lambda\mathcal{G})-\frac{\mathrm{i}\eta\Delta\gamma}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\sigma_{z}\mathcal{G})\\
+\mathrm{i}^{R}\eta\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\gamma_{\alpha}\mathop{\mathrm{det}_{R}}\!\left[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}\!\left(\sigma_{\alpha}\mathcal{G}\right)\right]-\gamma\left[R+\eta\left(1-R\right)\right],

(102)

where
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}
denotes a partial trace in Keldysh space, and
\mathop{\mathrm{det}_{R}}
is the
determinant in the remaining replica space. The measurement Lagrangian given in
Ref.
Starchl et al. 2025
for balanced and efficient fermion counting is recovered
by setting
\gamma_{+}=\gamma_{-}
and
\eta=1
. In the limit
R\to 1
,
Eq. (
102
) reduces to

\mathrm{i}\mathcal{L}_{M}[\mathcal{G}]=\gamma\left[\mathrm{i}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}(\Lambda\mathcal{G})-1\right].

(103)

With the measurement Lagrangian rewritten in terms of
\mathcal{G}
, the
fermionic integral (
56
) becomes Gaussian and can be evaluated
explicitly. This yields the partition function in the form

Z_{R}(t)=\int\mathrm{D}[\mathcal{G},\Sigma]\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}S[\mathcal{G},\Sigma]},

(104)

where the action is

S[\mathcal{G},\Sigma]=S_{0}[\mathcal{G},\Sigma]+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,\mathcal{L}_{M}[\mathcal{G}_{l}(t^{\prime})],

(105)

with the measurement Lagrangian (
102
) and

\mathrm{i}S_{0}[\mathcal{G},\Sigma]=\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\!\left[\ln\!\left(\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}-H+\mathrm{i}\Sigma\right)-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{G}\Sigma\right].

(106)

VI.2.3
Continuous symmetries of the replica Keldysh action

A prerequisite for understanding the long-wavelength behavior of our model is a
characterization of the global continuous symmetries of the replica Keldysh
action. This analysis will inform both the discussion of the Gaussian theory in
Sec.
VI.3
and the construction of the NLSM in
Sec.
VI.4
. Of particular importance are unitary field transformations,

\psi\mapsto\mathcal{R}\psi
with
\mathcal{R}\in\mathrm{U}(2R)
, since
breaking such symmetries leads to the appearance of massless Goldstone modes.

Due to the identity
\mathcal{G}=-\mathrm{i}\psi\psi^{\dagger}
, the matrix
field
\mathcal{G}
transforms as

\mathcal{G}\mapsto\mathcal{R}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{R}^{-1}
. When does such a
transformation constitute a symmetry? The action in Eq. (
106
)
is invariant under arbitrary transformations of
\mathcal{G}
provided
\Sigma

is transformed simultaneously as

\Sigma\mapsto\mathcal{R}\Sigma\mathcal{R}^{-1}
. Nontrivial constraints on
the symmetry group
G\leq\mathrm{U}(2R)
therefore arise from the measurement
Lagrangian (
102
) by requiring

\mathcal{L}_{M}[\mathcal{G}]=\mathcal{L}_{M}[\mathcal{R}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{R}^{-1}]
.
To specify these constraints, we analyze the measurement Lagrangian term by
term.

We first consider efficient detection with
\eta=1
, in which case the first
term in Eq. (
102
) vanishes. The second term, proportional to

\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\sigma_{z}\mathcal{G})
, is invariant if

\mathcal{R}\sigma_{z}\mathcal{R}^{-1}=\sigma_{z}.

(107)

This holds when
\mathcal{R}
is block-diagonal in Keldysh space,

\mathcal{R}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{V}_{+}&0\\
0&\mathcal{V}_{-}\end{pmatrix},

(108)

with
\mathcal{V}_{\pm}\in\mathrm{U}(R)
, so that

\mathcal{R}\in\mathrm{U}(R)\times\mathrm{U}(R)
.

The matrices
\mathcal{V}_{\pm}
are further constrained by the third term in
the measurement Lagrangian (
102
). Consider first the contribution
with
\alpha=-
. Inserting the transformed
\mathcal{G}
, the determinant in
replica space factorizes as

\mathop{\mathrm{det}_{R}}\!\left[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}\!\left(\sigma_{-}\mathcal{R}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{R}^{-1}\right)\right]=\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{det}_{R}}(\mathcal{V}_{+})}{\mathop{\mathrm{det}_{R}}(\mathcal{V}_{-})}\mathop{\mathrm{det}_{R}}[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}(\sigma_{-}\mathcal{G})].

(109)

The final factor appears also in the original expression in
Eq. (
102
). Hence,
\mathcal{R}
is a symmetry if the prefactor
reduces to unity, which is the case if

\mathop{\mathrm{det}_{R}}(\mathcal{V}_{+})=\mathop{\mathrm{det}_{R}}(\mathcal{V}_{-})
. The same condition follows from
the term with
\alpha=+
.

To determine the symmetry group
G
, note that the unitary group factorizes as

\mathrm{U}(R)=\mathrm{U}(1)\rtimes\mathrm{SU}(R)
, separating the global
phase from the special-unitary part. Because
\mathcal{V}_{+}
and

\mathcal{V}_{-}
must have equal determinants, only a single global

\mathrm{U}(1)
factor remains. Thus the symmetry group is

G\cong\mathrm{U}(1)\rtimes\left[\mathrm{SU}(R)\times\mathrm{SU}(R)\right].

(110)

The block-diagonal constraint (
108
) arises from the second term in
Eq. (
102
), which vanishes for balanced fermion counting with

\Delta\gamma=0
. We did not find a way to exclude the possibility that, for

\Delta\gamma=0
, the remaining third term might admit non-block-diagonal
symmetries. However, in particle-number-conserving models,
\mathcal{R}
can
indeed be shown to be block-diagonal
Starchl et al. 2025
;
Poboiko et al. 2025
. Since
fermion counting breaks particle-number conservation, the symmetry group of the
action is expected to be more restricted in this case. Therefore,
transformations
\mathcal{R}
should remain block-diagonal.

We now turn to inefficient detection with
\eta<1
. The first term in
Eq. (
102
) introduces the additional constraint

\mathcal{R}\Lambda\mathcal{R}^{-1}=\Lambda,

(111)

which forces
\mathcal{V}_{+}=\mathcal{V}_{-}
.
The symmetry group (
110
) is therefore reduced to

G\cong\mathrm{U}(R).

(112)

VI.2.4
Continuous symmetries in the operator formalism

We briefly digress to connect the symmetries of the Keldysh action to weak and
strong symmetries of the time-evolution superoperator
Buča and Prosen 2012
;
Albert and Jiang 2014
. A constructive approach to identifying operator symmetries is
presented in Ref.
Starchl et al. 2025
; instead, here we derive them from the
field-theoretic formulation.

The identity
\mathcal{G}=-\mathrm{i}\psi\psi^{\dagger}
, together with the
definition of the field vectors in Eq. (
88
) and the
block-diagonal form of
\mathcal{R}
in Eq. (
108
), implies that the
two blocks
\mathcal{V}_{\pm}
act separately on the field components

\psi_{\pm}
on the forward and backward branches of the closed time contour.
In general, field transformations that act independently on the two branches
correspond to strong symmetries of the time-evolution superoperator, whereas
transformations acting identically on both branches correspond to weak
symmetries
Sieberer et al. 2025
.

For
\eta=1
, we found that
\mathcal{V}_{\pm}\in\mathrm{U}(R)
with the
constraint
\mathop{\mathrm{det}_{R}}(\mathcal{V}_{+})=\mathop{\mathrm{det}_{R}}(\mathcal{V}_{-})
. The common
determinant defines a weak
\mathrm{U}(1)
symmetry. Aside from sharing this
determinant, the matrices
\mathcal{V}_{\pm}
remain independent, giving rise to
a strong
\mathrm{SU}(R)
symmetry. As we will see in Sec.
VI.4
, it
is precisely this strong
\mathrm{SU}(R)
symmetry that underlies the NLSM.

For
\eta<1
, we obtained the additional constraint

\mathcal{V}_{+}=\mathcal{V}_{-}
, reducing the symmetry group from
Eq. (
110
) to Eq. (
112
) and thus leaving only
a weak
\mathrm{U}(R)
symmetry. That inefficient detection breaks the symmetry
required for the NLSM description was already anticipated for balanced fermion
counting in Ref.
Starchl et al. 2025
. Furthermore, Ref.
Starchl et al. 2025
also
shows that two conditions are necessary for the strong
\mathrm{SU}(R)
symmetry
underlying the NLSM to emerge in free fermionic systems: (i) conservation of the
total particle number of the system and reservoirs, and (ii) conservation of the
purity of the state of the system. The latter requires keeping a complete
record of measurement outcomes, which is fulfilled only when
\eta=1
.

VI.3
Gaussian theory

Following Refs.
Poboiko et al. 2023
;
Starchl et al. 2025
, we first analyze the theory
within the Gaussian approximation. Specifically, we determine the
replica-symmetric saddle point of the Keldysh action and restrict the functional
integral (
104
) to quadratic fluctuations around this saddle
point. This approximation is valid for small
\gamma
and on short to
intermediate length scales.

VI.3.1
Saddle point and fluctuations

The saddle-point equations
\delta S/\delta\mathcal{G}=0
and

\delta S/\delta\Sigma=0
admit a unique replica-diagonal solution.
Replica-asymmetric or replicon saddle points, which form a finite-dimensional
manifold, will be discussed in Sec.
VI.4
. Setting
R=1
in numerical
prefactors, the replica-symmetric saddle point is

\mathcal{G}=-\mathrm{i}Q/2,\qquad\Sigma=\gamma Q,\qquad Q=\Lambda,

(113)

independent of
\eta
, and with
\Lambda
defined in Eq. (
97
).
Gaussian fluctuations around this saddle point can be parameterized as

\mathcal{G}=-\mathrm{i}\left(\Lambda+\delta Q_{\mathcal{G}}{}\right)\!\big/2,\qquad\Sigma=\gamma\left(\Lambda+\delta Q_{\Sigma}{}\right)\!\big/2,

(114)

where
\delta Q_{\mathcal{G}}
and
\delta Q_{\Sigma}
are
2R\times 2R
matrices. The Gaussian theory is
obtained by expanding the Keldysh action (
105
) to second order in

\delta Q_{\mathcal{G}}
and
\delta Q_{\Sigma}
as detailed in Refs.
Poboiko et al. 2023
;
Starchl et al. 2025
.

A key feature of fluctuations around the saddle point can be anticipated from
the discussion of symmetries in Sec.
VI.2.3
:
since the saddle point (
113
) partially breaks these
symmetries, certain types of fluctuations become massless Goldstone modes. The
manifold of these modes is the quotient
G/H
, where
G
is the symmetry group
of the Keldysh action, given by Eqs. (
110
)
and (
112
) for
\eta=1
and
\eta<1
, respectively, and

H
is the subgroup of
G
that leaves the saddle point invariant, as expressed
by Eq. (
111
). In Sec.
VI.2.3
, we
already identified
H\cong\mathrm{U}(R)
for inefficient detection.

It follows that for
\eta=1
, the Goldstone manifold
G/H\cong\mathrm{SU}(R)
is
nontrivial, whereas for
\eta<1
,
G=H
and the quotient
G/H
is
trivial. Hence, massless modes arise for
\eta=1
due to the strong

\mathrm{SU}(R)
symmetry discussed in Sec.
VI.2.4
, and
they acquire a mass when this strong symmetry is broken for
\eta<1
.

The physics of fluctuations of these Goldstone modes on the nonlinear manifold
is discussed in Sec.
VI.4
. But first, we focus on linearized
fluctuations. As we show, within this approximation, the massless modes for

\eta=1
generate algebraic correlations and logarithmic growth of
entanglement, whereas the mass term for
\eta<1
introduces a finite
correlation length.

VI.3.2
Connected density correlation function

As discussed in Sec.
III
, the nontrivial effects of
measurements become apparent in nonlinear observables. We focus here on the
equal-time connected density correlation function, defined in the operator
formalism and in terms of field expectation values as
Poboiko et al. 2023

\begin{split}C_{l,l^{\prime}}(t)&=\frac{1}{2}\overline{\left\langle\left\{\hat{n}_{l}(t),\hat{n}_{l^{\prime}}(t)\right\}\right\rangle}-\overline{\left\langle\hat{n}_{l}(t)\right\rangle\left\langle\hat{n}_{l^{\prime}}(t)\right\rangle}\\
&=C_{r,r,l,l^{\prime}}(t,t)-C_{r,r^{\prime},l,l^{\prime}}(t,t),\end{split}

(115)

where the correlation function of density fluctuations is

C_{r,r^{\prime},l,l^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})=\langle\delta\rho_{r,l}(t)\delta\rho_{r^{\prime},l^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle.

(116)

Permutation symmetry among replicas renders the specific choice of
r
and
r^{\prime}

in Eq. (
115
) irrelevant, provided that
r\neq r^{\prime}
. The
density fluctuations in Eq. (
116
) are related to the
\delta Q_{\mathcal{G}}
in
Eq. (
114
) via
Poboiko et al. 2023

\delta\rho_{r,l}(t)=-\frac{1}{4}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}\!\left[\sigma_{x}\delta Q_{\mathcal{G},r,r,l}(t)\right].

(117)

The correlation function (
115
) can be calculated explicitly
in the Gaussian approximation. In the steady state,
C_{l,l^{\prime}}(t)
becomes
time-independent and translationally invariant, allowing us to write

C_{l,l^{\prime}}(t)=C_{l-l^{\prime}}
. When calculating
C_{l}
, boundary effects due to
stopping the measurement process at finite time
t
must be taken into
account
Poboiko et al. 2023
. This leads to an expression for the correlation
function in momentum space,
C_{q}
, in terms of the solution of an integral
equation that depends on the two parameters
u=2l_{0}\sin(q/2)
and

\eta
, where

l_{0}=\frac{J}{\sqrt{2}\gamma}.

(118)

However, the leading asymptotic behavior of
C_{q}
for small momenta and small
detection inefficiency
\delta\eta=1-\eta
is correctly reproduced in the
bulk approximation
Poboiko et al. 2023
, in which the boundary condition in time
is neglected and we rescale
u\to 2u
and
\delta\eta\to 4\delta\eta
,
leading to the following expression for the correlation function:

C_{q}\approx n\left(1-n\right)\tilde{c}(ql_{0}),

(119)

with

\tilde{c}(u)=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}v\left\{\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}[b(2u,v)]-\left(1-4\delta\eta\right)\left\lvert b(2u,v)\right\rvert^{2}\right\}\\
\times\left(1-\left(1-4\delta\eta\right)^{2}\left\lvert b(2u,v)\right\rvert^{2}-4\left(1-4\delta\eta\right)n\left(1-n\right)\right.\\
\left.\times\left\{\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}[b(2u,v)]-\left(1-4\delta\eta\right)\left\lvert b(2u,v)\right\rvert^{2}\right\}\right)^{-1},

(120)

where

b(u,v)=\left[\left(1-\mathrm{i}v\right)^{2}+2u^{2}\right]^{-1/2}.

(121)

For low momenta
q\to 0
,
C_{q}
behaves as

C_{q}\sim\begin{cases}g_{0}q-4n\left(1-n\right)\left(ql_{0}\right)^{2},&\delta\eta=0,\\
\frac{g_{0}}{l_{0}}\sqrt{\left(ql_{0}\right)^{2}+\delta\eta},&\delta\eta\to 0,\end{cases}

(122)

with

g_{0}=\frac{\sqrt{2}n\left(1-n\right)l_{0}}{\sqrt{1-2n\left(1-n\right)}}.

(123)

Transforming to real space yields

C_{l}\sim\begin{cases}-\frac{g_{0}}{\pi l^{2}},&\delta\eta=0,\\
-\frac{g_{0}}{\sqrt{\pi l_{0}}}\frac{\delta\eta^{1/4}}{l^{3/2}}\mathrm{e}^{-l/\xi},&\delta\eta\to 0,\end{cases}

(124)

where

\xi=\frac{l_{0}}{\sqrt{\delta\eta}}.

(125)

For
\delta\eta=0
, this reproduces the result of Ref.
Starchl et al. 2025
:
Algebraic correlations arise as a manifestation of a massless Goldstone mode,
even though particle-number conservation is broken. For
\delta\eta>0
,
correlations are cut off at the scale
\xi
(
125
).

VI.3.3
Subsystem entropy

The second key nonlinear observable we consider is the subsystem entropy. For a
system in the state
\hat{\rho}
, the reduced density matrix of a contiguous
block of
\ell
sites is obtained by tracing out the remaining
L-\ell
sites,

\hat{\rho}_{\ell}=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}_{L-\ell}(\hat{\rho})
. The corresponding von Neumann
subsystem entropy is then

S_{\ell}=-\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left[\hat{\rho}_{\ell}\ln\!\left(\hat{\rho}_{\ell}\right)\right]}.

(126)

Efficient detection with
\eta=1
yields pure-state quantum trajectories,

\hat{\rho}=\lvert\psi\rangle\langle\psi\rvert
, making
S_{\ell}
a genuine measure of
entanglement. However, for inefficient detection with
\eta<1
, the
conditional state
\hat{\rho}
becomes mixed. Then, entanglement can be
quantified by the fermionic logarithmic negativity
Shapourian et al. 2017
;
Shapourian and Ryu 2019
, which we discuss further in Sec.
VI.6.4
.

The subsystem entropy can be computed using another property of quantum
trajectories: evolution under the quadratic Hamiltonian (
78
)
and linear jump operators (
80
) preserves
Gaussianity of the state. For a Gaussian state,
S_{\ell}
is obtained from
fluctuations of the subsystem particle number,

\hat{N}_{\ell}=\sum_{l=1}^{\ell}\hat{n}_{l}
, through a cumulant expansion
with coefficients given by the Riemann zeta function

\zeta(k)

Klich and Levitov 2009
:

S_{\ell}=2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\zeta(2k)C_{\ell}^{(2k)}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}C_{\ell}^{(2)}+\frac{\pi^{4}}{45}C_{\ell}^{(4)}+\dotsb.

(127)

We restrict ourselves to the leading term of the expansion, which involves the
second cumulant,

C_{\ell}^{(2)}=\overline{\langle(\hat{N}_{\ell}-\langle\hat{N}_{\ell}\rangle)^{2}\rangle}.

(128)

Inserting here the explicit expression for
\hat{N}_{\ell}
and using the
definition of the conditional density correlation
function (
115
), we obtain

S_{\ell}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}\sum_{l,l^{\prime}=1}^{\ell}C_{l-l^{\prime}}=\frac{2\pi}{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}q}{q^{2}}\,C_{q}\left[1-\cos(q\ell)\right].

(129)

The behavior of the subsystem entropy for large subsystem sizes,
\ell\gg l_{0}
,
follows by inserting the asymptotic form (
122
) of
the density correlation function. For efficient detection,
\delta\eta=0
, we
find

S_{\ell}\sim\frac{2\pi g_{0}}{3}\ln(\ell/l_{0}),\qquad\delta\eta=0.

(130)

That is, for efficient detection, the Gaussian theory predicts logarithmic
growth of entanglement on large scales, as in a 1D conformal field theory
(CFT)
Di Francesco et al. 1997
.

On short scales
\ell\lesssim l_{0}
, the system is expected to behave like a
Fermi gas at infinite temperature
Poboiko et al. 2023
, leading to volume-law
behavior of the entropy:

S_{\ell}\sim-\left[n\ln(n)+\left(1-n\right)\ln\left(1-n\right)\right]\ell.

(131)

The prefactor is not fully reproduced by
Eq. (
129
) since higher cumulants in
Eq. (
127
) become significant in this
case
Poboiko et al. 2023
. We expect volume-law behavior
S_{\ell}\sim\ell
to
persist for inefficient detection,
\eta<1
, also at large scales

\ell\gg l_{0}
. In particular, in the limit
\eta\to 0
,
Eq. (
131
) describes the exact subsystem entropy of the fully
mixed steady state (
81
).

Deviations from the CFT behavior (
130
) for

\eta=1
arising at short scales and, as discussed below, also at large scales
due to nonlinear fluctuations, can be characterized by the scale-dependent
effective central charge
Starchl et al. 2025
:

c_{\ell}=3\frac{\partial S_{\ell}}{\partial\!\ln(\ell)}.

(132)

Using the Gaussian prediction (
130
), one finds

c_{\ell}\sim 2\pi g_{0},\qquad\ell\to\infty,

(133)

with
g_{0}
defined in Eq. (
123
). Nonlinear fluctuations of Goldstone
modes drive a renormalization-group (RG) flow of
g_{0}
toward zero on large
scales, causing the effective central charge to vanish and the entanglement
entropy to cross over from logarithmic to area-law scaling. Next, we discuss how
this RG flow can be obtained from an NLSM description.

VI.4
Nonlinear sigma model

As anticipated in Sec.
VI.3.1
, the
replica-symmetric saddle point (
113
) breaks global
continuous symmetries of the Keldysh action, thereby giving rise to Goldstone
modes. For
\eta=1
, these modes span a nonlinear manifold, with fluctuations
within this manifold described by an NLSM, which we derive in the present
section.

In our discussion of the Goldstone-mode manifold, and hence of the target
manifold of the NLSM, we have thus far focused exclusively on continuous
symmetries of the Keldysh action. We have neglected an important discrete
symmetry, to which we now turn: particle-hole symmetry (PHS)
Jian et al. 2022
;
Fava et al. 2024
;
Poboiko et al. 2025
;
Starchl et al. 2025
;
Niederegger et al. 2025
.

VI.4.1
Particle-hole symmetry

The quadratic hopping Hamiltonian (
78
) exhibits PHS if there
exists a unitary matrix
U
with
UU^{*}=\pm 1
such that the Hamiltonian
matrix
H
satisfies
UH^{*}U^{\dagger}=-H

Ludwig 2016
. Owing to the
Hermiticity of
H
, this condition is equivalently written as

UH^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}U^{\dagger}=-H
. Here we focus on the case
UU^{*}=+1
,
which, together with unitarity of
U
, implies that
U
is symmetric,

U=U^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}
. By the Autonne-Takagi factorization, such a matrix can be
expressed as
U=R^{\dagger}R^{*}
, where
R
is unitary and we have used that
all singular values of
U
are equal to one. For the transformed Hamiltonian

H^{\prime}=RHR^{\dagger}
, we obtain

H^{\prime{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}=R^{*}H^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}R^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}=-R^{*}U^{\dagger}HUR^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}=-H^{\prime}.

(134)

Thus, PHS of
H
implies that
H^{\prime}
is skew-symmetric.

In what follows, we leave the explicit form of
H
unspecified in intermediate
expressions. However, for any
H
possessing PHS, we assume that we work in a
basis in which PHS reduces to skew symmetry. Our final results apply to the
Hamiltonian matrix (
79
) describing nearest-neighbor
hopping with real hopping amplitudes. For this case, the necessary basis
transformation corresponds to a gauge transformation of the field operators,

\hat{\psi}_{l}\mapsto\mathrm{i}^{l}\hat{\psi}_{l}
. This renders the hopping
matrix (
79
) purely imaginary,

H_{l,l^{\prime}}\mapsto-\mathrm{i}J\left(\delta_{l+1,l^{\prime}}-\delta_{l,l^{\prime}+1}\right).

(135)

PHS alters the target manifold of the NLSM
Jian et al. 2022
;
Fava et al. 2024
;
Poboiko et al. 2025
;
Starchl et al. 2025
;
Niederegger et al. 2025
. To determine this modification, we
first explicitly symmetrize the action with respect to PHS and then carry out
the generalized Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation on the symmetrized action.

We begin by considering the Hamiltonian contribution to the
action (
92
). Working in the basis of
Eq. (
88
) and leaving the summation over
\sigma
,

r
, and
l
implicit as matrix multiplication, the Hamiltonian term takes the

\psi^{\dagger}(t)H\psi(t)=\frac{1}{2}\psi^{\dagger}(t)\left(H-H^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\right)\psi(t)\\

where we have introduced the doubled field vectors

\Psi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\psi\\

The charge-conjugation matrix

88

S_{H}[\Psi]=\sum_{l,l^{\prime}=1}^{L}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,\bar{\Psi}_{l}(t^{\prime})\,G_{0,l,l^{\prime}}^{-1}\,\Psi_{l^{\prime}}(t^{\prime}).

(140)

devoid of structure in charge-conjugation space and is therefore manifestly

\sigma_{y}\Lambda^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\sigma_{y}=-\Lambda,\quad\sigma_{y}\sigma_{z}\sigma_{y}=-\sigma_{z},\quad\sigma_{y}\sigma_{\pm}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\sigma_{y}=-\sigma_{\pm},

\mathrm{i}\mathcal{L}_{M}[\Psi]=-\left(1-\eta\right)\bar{\Psi}\Lambda\Psi+\frac{\eta\Delta\gamma}{2}\bar{\Psi}\sigma_{z}\Psi\\
+\eta\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\gamma_{\alpha}\prod_{r=1}^{R}\left(-\bar{\Psi}_{r}\sigma_{\alpha}\Psi_{r}\right)-\gamma\left[R+\eta\left(1-R\right)\right].

(142)

This is formally equivalent to Eq. (

\psi\to\Psi
and
\psi^{\dagger}\to\bar{\Psi}

The presence or absence as well as the precise form of PHS depends on the
details of the model. In contrast, a chiral symmetry is always present, as it is
intrinsic to the Keldysh formalism

Guo et al. 2025

the Hamiltonian matrix

exhibits PHS, the model belongs to the chiral
orthogonal symmetry class BDI; if PHS is broken, it instead falls into the
chiral unitary class AIII.

Generalized Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation with PHS

We proceed with a generalized Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation of the

By Eq. (

The identity

VI.4.3
NLSM target manifold for efficient detection,
\eta=1

In Sec.
VI.3
, we derived the Gaussian theory by expanding
the action to quadratic order in fluctuations around the saddle
point (
113
). For simplicity, we chose the
replica-symmetric saddle point as the reference for this expansion. Applying
symmetry transformations to the replica-symmetric saddle point generates other
saddle points. The NLSM describes long-wavelength fluctuations of the Goldstone
modes within the manifold formed by these saddle points. Therefore, to derive
the NLSM, we first need to determine the structure of its target manifold,
meaning the manifold of saddle points, and obtain an explicit
parameterization. We anticipate that the symmetry underlying the NLSM
description is broken for inefficient detection with
\eta<1
, and hence we
first focus on the case of efficient detection with
\eta=1
.

As a preliminary step, we determine the replica-symmetric saddle point of the
particle-hole symmetrized action. Due to the factor
\beta
in
Eq. (
146
), the replica-symmetric solution to the
saddle-point equations is now

\mathcal{G}=-\mathrm{i}\beta Q/2,\qquad\Sigma=\gamma Q,\qquad Q=\Lambda,

(147)

where, as before,
\beta=1/2
for class BDI. For class AIII, we formally
recover Eq. (
113
) by setting
\beta=1
. Note that the
matrix
\Lambda
defined in Eq. (
97
) satisfies
condition (
144
), as follows from
Eq. (
141
):

\bar{\Lambda}=\mathcal{C}\Lambda^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{C}=\sigma_{y}\Lambda^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\sigma_{y}=-\Lambda.

(148)

We obtain the NLSM target manifold by applying continuous unitary
transformations to the replica-symmetric saddle
point (
147
). Specifically, we replace
Q=\Lambda
by

Q=\mathcal{R}\Lambda\mathcal{R}^{-1},

(149)

where
\mathcal{R}\in\mathrm{U}(2R)
for symmetry class AIII and

\mathcal{R}\in\mathrm{U}(4R)
for class BDI. In the latter case, the
transformations must additionally preserve the
condition (
148
), so that

Q=-\mathcal{C}Q^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{C}
. This requirement is satisfied if

\mathcal{R}^{-1}=\bar{\mathcal{R}}
, which, together with unitarity

\mathcal{R}^{-1}=\mathcal{R}^{\dagger}
, implies

\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{C}\mathcal{R}^{*}\mathcal{C}.

(150)

Equation (
147
), with
Q
given by
Eq. (
149
), parameterizes the NLSM manifold if the transformations

\mathcal{R}
satisfy two conditions: (i) They must be symmetries of the Keldysh
action. This ensures that if
Q=\Lambda
is a saddle point, then

Q=\mathcal{R}\Lambda\mathcal{R}^{-1}
is also a saddle point. The set of
such transformations forms a group
G
. (ii) They must act nontrivially on the
saddle point
\Lambda
. Transformations that leave
\Lambda
invariant satisfy
Eq. (
111
) and form a subgroup
H
. To isolate the transformations
that satisfy condition (ii), one must factor out the subgroup
H
. The resulting
target manifold of the NLSM is then
G/H
.

We first determine the restrictions on
\mathcal{R}
imposed by requirement (i).
Note that symmetries for class AIII have already been discussed in
Sec.
VI.2.3
. Here, we repeat the key points and
indicate the modifications required for class BDI.

The action (
146
) is symmetric under arbitrary
transformations. Therefore, nontrivial symmetry constraints on the group
G

arise solely from the measurement Lagrangian (
145
), where

\beta=1
for class AIII and
\beta=1/2
for class BDI. The explicit form
for class AIII is given in Eq. (
102
). Symmetry transformations must
satisfy
\mathcal{L}_{M}[\mathcal{G}]=\mathcal{L}_{M}[\mathcal{R}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{R}^{-1}]
.

As in Sec.
VI.2.3
, we check this condition term
by term. Considering efficient counting with
\eta=1
, the first term of the
measurement Lagrangian (
145
) vanishes. The second term is
invariant if
\mathcal{R}
is block-diagonal in Keldysh space, as given in
Eq. (
108
). For class AIII, the blocks are arbitrary unitary
matrices,
\mathcal{V}_{\pm}\in\mathrm{U}(R)
; for class BDI,
Eq. (
150
) imposes the constraint

\mathcal{V}_{\pm}=\tau_{y}\mathcal{V}_{\mp}^{*}\tau_{y}
, so that
\mathcal{R}

can be parameterized in terms of a single matrix

\mathcal{V}\in\mathrm{U}(2R)
as

\mathcal{R}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{V}&0\\
0&\tau_{y}\mathcal{V}^{*}\tau_{y}\end{pmatrix}.

(151)

Next, we consider the third term of the measurement Lagrangian, starting with
class AIII. As discussed in Sec.
VI.2.3
, this
term is invariant if
\mathop{\mathrm{det}_{R}}(\mathcal{V}_{+})=\mathop{\mathrm{det}_{R}}(\mathcal{V}_{-})
. This yields

\mathcal{R}\in G\cong\mathrm{U}(1)\rtimes\left[\mathrm{SU}(R)\times\mathrm{SU}(R)\right]
.

Turning to class BDI, we examine the contribution to the third term of the
measurement Lagrangian (
145
) with
\alpha=-
. Inserting here
the transformed matrix
\mathcal{G}
, the exponential factor becomes

\mathrm{e}^{\beta\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{CR}}\ln\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\sigma_{-}\mathcal{R}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{R}^{-1}\right)}=\mathrm{e}^{\beta\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{CR}}\ln\left(\tau_{y}\mathcal{V}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\tau_{y}\mathcal{V}\right)}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{CR}}\ln\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\sigma_{-}\mathcal{G}\right)}.

(152)

The final factor on the right-hand side is already present in
Eq. (
145
). Hence, invariance of the measurement Lagrangian
requires the first factor to reduce to unity. To rewrite this factor, we use the
following general relation for skew-symmetric
2D\times 2D
matrices
A
and

B

\mathop{\mathrm{pf}}(A)\mathop{\mathrm{pf}}(B)=\mathrm{e}^{\beta\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\ln\left(A^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}B\right)}=\left(-1\right)^{D}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(AB)},

(153)

where
\mathop{\mathrm{pf}}
denotes the Pfaffian. Here, we apply this to the
2R\times 2R

matrices
A=\tau_{y}
, with
1_{R}
left implicit, and

B=\mathcal{V}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\tau_{y}\mathcal{V}

Lagrangian thus requires

\mathrm{e}^{\beta\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{CR}}\ln\left(\tau_{y}\mathcal{V}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\tau_{y}\mathcal{V}\right)}=\left(-1\right)^{R}\mathop{\mathrm{pf}_{CR}}(\tau_{y})\mathop{\mathrm{pf}_{CR}}\!\left(\mathcal{V}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\tau_{y}\mathcal{V}\right)=1.

(154)

Using

\begin{gathered}\mathop{\mathrm{pf}_{CR}}(\tau_{y})=\mathop{\mathrm{pf}_{C}}(\tau_{y})^{R}=\left(-\mathrm{i}\right)^{R},\\
\mathop{\mathrm{pf}_{CR}}\!\left(\mathcal{V}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\tau_{y}\mathcal{V}\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{det}_{CR}}(\mathcal{V})\mathop{\mathrm{pf}_{CR}}(\tau_{y}),\end{gathered}

\mathop{\mathrm{det}_{CR}}(\mathcal{V})=1

contribution with
\alpha=+

\mathcal{V}\in\mathrm{SU}(2R)
and thus

\mathcal{R}\in G\cong\mathrm{SU}(2R)
.

We now turn to condition (ii) and determine the group
H
of transformations

\mathcal{R}
that leave the saddle point
\Lambda

explicit parameterization of the NLSM target manifold, obtained using

given by Eq. (

Q=\begin{pmatrix}1-2n&2nU\\

(156)

where, for AIII,

U=\mathcal{V}\sigma_{y}\mathcal{V}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\sigma_{y}

For class BDI, the saddle point
\Lambda

satisfying

\mathcal{V}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\sigma_{y}\mathcal{V}=\sigma_{y}

For both symmetry classes AIII and BDI, the NLSM target manifold becomes trivial
in the replica limit

is trivial, and only the replicon manifold is nontrivial. For
R=1
, the
theory describes the unconditional dynamics of free fermions with incoherent
gain and loss, which in the steady state (

correlations in space and time. Such behavior corresponds to the theory being
massive even for
\eta=1

Starchl et al. 2025
.

VI.4.4
Trivial target manifold for inefficient detection,
\eta<1

We now turn to inefficient detection with
\eta<1
. In this case, the first
term in the measurement Lagrangian (
145
), which contains

\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\Lambda\mathcal{G})
with coefficient

\mathcal{R}

However, this is precisely the condition that determines the group

discussed below Eq. (

Starchl et al. 2025

VI.4.5
Nonlinear sigma model Lagrangian

The general form of the NLSM action for
\eta=1
can be obtained by inserting
the parameterization of the NLSM manifold from Eq. (
156
) into
Eq. (
146
), taking the spatial continuum limit in which the
lattice-site index
l
is replaced by the continuous coordinate
x
, and
performing a gradient expansion

. This procedure does not,
however, account for the renormalization of the coefficients in the NLSM
Lagrangian that results from integrating out massive modes. To determine this
renormalization, we instead begin with the most general form of the NLSM
obtained from the gradient expansion of Eq. (
146
) but
leave its coefficients unspecified. Linearizing the NLSM action and matching it
to the Gaussian theory, after all massive fluctuations have been integrated out
there, then fixes these coefficients. Implementing this program yields the NLSM
Lagrangian for the replicon manifold:

\mathrm{i}\mathcal{L}_{R}[U]=-\frac{\beta g}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{CR}}\!\left(\frac{1}{v}\partial_{t}U^{-1}\partial_{t}U+v\partial_{x}U^{-1}\partial_{x}U\right),

(157)

where the bare value
g_{0}
of the dimensionless NLSM coupling
g
is given in
Eq. (
123
), and

v=2J\sqrt{1-2n\left(1-n\right)}.

(158)

As noted above:

AIII:

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle\in\mathrm{SU}(R),

(159)

BDI:

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=1/2,

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle\in\mathrm{SU}(2R)/\mathrm{Sp}(R).

(160)

Thus, the same NLSM arises for all values of
\gamma_{\pm}
. The physics
discussed in Ref.
Starchl et al. 2025
is therefore not restricted to balanced
gain and loss with
\gamma_{+}=\gamma_{-}
.

For
\eta<1
, the first term in Eq. (
145
) yields a mass
contribution proportional to the detection inefficiency

, which takes the form

\mathrm{i}\delta\mathcal{L}_{R}[U]=2n\left(1-n\right)\delta\eta\gamma\left[\beta\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{CR}}\!\left(U+U^{-1}\right)-2R\right].

(161)

This mass term suppresses long-wavelength fluctuations of

. We may thus
parameterize

with a Hermitian matrix
\Phi
and expand the
NLSM Lagrangian to second order,

\mathrm{i}\left(\mathcal{L}_{R}[\Phi]+\delta\mathcal{L}_{R}[\Phi]\right)=-\beta\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{CR}}\!\left[\frac{g}{2}\left(\frac{1}{v}\partial_{t}\Phi^{-1}\partial_{t}\Phi\right.\right.\\
\left.\left.\vphantom{\frac{1}{v}}+v\partial_{x}\Phi^{-1}\partial_{x}\Phi\right)+2n\left(1-n\right)\delta\eta\gamma\Phi^{2}\right].

This form of the NLSM Lagrangian is consistent with the low-momentum

VI.4.6
Renormalization-group flow of the NLSM

For
\eta=1
, the large-scale behavior of our model is governed by the RG flow
of the NLSM coupling constant
g
. The NLSM description and thus the RG flow is
identical to that obtained for balanced counting in Ref.
Starchl et al. 2025
. For
completeness, we briefly recall the relevant results. We then analyze how the RG
flow is modified by inefficient detection.

On scales
l\gtrsim l_{0}
, the NLSM coupling constant acquires a logarithmic
renormalization
Poboiko et al. 2023
;
Poboiko et al. 2025
,

g=g_{0}-\frac{1}{4\pi\beta}\ln(l/l_{0}),

(163)

where, as above,
\beta=1
for symmetry class AIII and
\beta=1/2
for class
BDI. This expression is valid in the weak-coupling regime
g\gg 1
, which,
according to Eq. (
123
), corresponds to a low rate of fermion gain and
loss. The flow reaches the strong-coupling regime, where
g
becomes of order
unity, at the characteristic scale

l_{*}=l_{0}\mathrm{e}^{4\pi\beta g_{0}}.

(164)

As discussed in Sec.
VI.3.3
, the renormalization
of
g
controls the long-distance behavior of the effective central charge.
Thus, the flow of
g
toward small values for
l\gtrsim l_{*}
implies that the
logarithmic growth of the entanglement
entropy (
130
) eventually saturates, leading to
area-law scaling for subsystem sizes
\ell\gtrsim l_{*}

Poboiko et al. 2023
.
Accordingly, our model exhibits no measurement-induced transition between
critical and area-law phases; all choices of
\gamma_{\pm}
yield area-law
entanglement beyond the scale
l_{*}
. Nevertheless, because
l_{*}
grows
exponentially with
g_{0}\sim J/\gamma
, the onset of area-law behavior falls far
outside the window accessible to our numerical simulations when
J/\gamma
is
large—even though such scales can be reached in principle
Fan et al. 2025
; see
Sec.
VI.6
for details.

For small but finite measurement inefficiency,

0<\delta\eta=1-\eta\ll 1
, the mass term in Eq. (
162
)
suppresses fluctuations of
\Phi
at large scales and thereby cuts off the RG
flow. The resulting infrared cutoff is the correlation length
\xi
of
Eq. (
125
), but with the microscopic scale
l_{0}\sim g_{0}

renormalized. Even though this renormalization modifies prefactors, the
functional dependence
\xi\sim\delta\eta^{-1/2}
remains correct, as we
confirm numerically in Sec.
VI.6
. Beyond the scale

\xi
, the long-wavelength behavior is governed by the Gaussian theory, with
g

replaced by its renormalized value. We examine this regime in detail below.

VI.5
RG-corrected Gaussian theory

To assess how the renormalization of
g
affects physical observables, we
incorporate the RG correction Eq. (
163
) into the Gaussian correlation
function (
122
)
Poboiko et al. 2023
;
Starchl et al. 2025
.
The infrared cutoff of the RG flow is set by the larger of the two scales:

l=1/q
, where
q
is the momentum at which the correlation function is
evaluated, or the correlation length (
125
) associated with the mass
generated by
\delta\eta>0
.

For
\eta=1
, inserting Eq. (
163
) into
Eq. (
122
) yields

\frac{C_{q}}{g_{0}q}\sim 1-4\sqrt{\frac{1-2n\left(1-n\right)}{2}}ql_{0}+\frac{1}{4\pi\beta g_{0}}\ln(ql_{0}).

(165)

In the limit
q\to 0
, the logarithmic renormalization dominates and suppresses

C_{q}/(g_{0}q)
, whereas the Gaussian approximation predicts

C_{q}/(g_{0}q)\to 1
as
q\to 0
. The momentum scale at which the logarithmic
term becomes comparable to the Gaussian contribution marks the onset of the
crossover from the regime in which the Gaussian theory is valid to the
fluctuation-dominated strong-coupling regime. Following
Ref.
Starchl et al. 2025
, we associate this crossover with the maximum of the
rescaled correlation function, which occurs at
q_{c}\sim\gamma^{2}
. The
corresponding length scale is
l_{c}\sim\gamma^{-2}
. Because
l_{c}
grows
only algebraically with
\gamma
, it remains accessible in numerical studies.

As shown in Ref.
Starchl et al. 2025
, the length scale
l_{c}\sim\gamma^{-2}

serves as the upper boundary of a critical window within which the model
exhibits emergent conformal invariance, as expected for a critical
phase
Alberton et al. 2021
. Its lower boundary is

l_{0}\sim\gamma^{-1}
(
118
). Within this interval, the system displays
signatures of conformal behavior, including algebraic decay of density
correlations,
C_{l}\sim l^{-2}
, and logarithmic entanglement scaling,

S_{\ell}\sim\ln(\ell)
. While these features also arise from the Gaussian
approximations in Eqs. (
124
)
and (
130
), the Gaussian central charge
Eq. (
133
) is valid only in the combined
limits
\gamma\to 0
and
\ell\to\infty
. For any finite
\gamma
,
approximately logarithmic entanglement growth appears instead on intermediate
scales where
c_{\ell}
varies slowly and reaches a maximum at

\ell=l_{m}\sim\gamma^{-3/2}
. The scaling of
l_{m}
ensures that this length
lies within the critical regime,
l_{0}<l_{m}<l_{c}
. Logarithmic entanglement
growth and additional hallmarks of conformal symmetry, including the universal
functional form of the mutual information, are captured by the renormalized
value of the effective central charge at its maximum
Starchl et al. 2025
.

For
\delta\eta>0
, the RG flow is terminated when
l\sim q^{-1}\sim\xi
. Using Eq. (
122
) at this scale gives

C_{q}\sim\frac{g_{0}}{\xi}\left[1-\frac{1}{4\pi\beta g_{0}}\ln(\xi/l_{0})\right]\sim\sqrt{\delta\eta},\quad\delta\eta\to 0.

(166)

This is consistent with Eq. (
165
): substituting

q\sim\xi^{-1}
into that expression and retaining only the leading terms for

\xi\gg l_{0}
reproduces the same scaling.

VI.6
Numerical simulations of quantum-jump trajectories

We validate our analytical results by comparing them with direct numerical
simulations of the model. Before presenting our findings in detail, we briefly
summarize the numerical methods employed, which differ from those used for
balanced and efficient fermion counting
Starchl et al. 2025

VI.6.1
Numerical methods

For balanced and efficient fermion counting, the rates of gain and loss jumps

\pm\mathrm{i}G_{BA}&-G_{BB}\end{pmatrix},

(174)

(175)

(176)

VII
Conclusions

In this work, we have developed a comprehensive replica Keldysh field-theory
framework for general quantum-jump processes in bosonic and fermionic many-body
systems. This framework encompasses, in particular, processes involving
non-Hermitian jump operators with state-dependent and thus time-varying
rates—going beyond the paradigmatic scenario of projective measurements
performed at a fixed, externally imposed rate. Moreover, our formalism unifies
the description of pure-state trajectories under efficient detection with the
mixed-state dynamics arising from inefficient monitoring. It thus provides a
versatile framework for exploring a broad range of measurement-induced
phenomena.

Applying this framework to imbalanced and inefficient fermion counting in a 1D
lattice system, we have established several key results. For imbalanced but
efficient detection, we confirmed that the qualitative picture observed
previously in the balanced case persists
Starchl et al. 2025
: there is no
measurement-induced phase transition, and entanglement obeys an area law on
sufficiently large scales, while an extended quantum-critical regime emerges
between parametrically separated scales
l_{0}\sim\gamma^{-1}
and

l_{c}\sim\gamma^{-2}
. This regime exhibits signatures of emergent conformal
invariance, including algebraic correlations and approximately logarithmic
entanglement growth.

We have further shown that a finite detection inefficiency
\delta\eta
,
corresponding to a nonzero fraction of undetected fermion gain and loss jumps,
introduces a correlation length
\xi\sim\delta\eta^{-1/2}
. On length scales
larger than
\xi
, quantum entanglement is strongly suppressed, as evidenced by
the fermionic logarithmic negativity, which obeys an area law. In contrast, the
subsystem entropy exhibits volume-law scaling due to the mixedness of individual
quantum trajectories.

More broadly, our results establish a direct conceptual and technical connection
between measurement-induced dynamics and the steady-state physics of driven open
quantum systems
Sieberer et al. 2016
;
Sieberer et al. 2025
;
Thompson and Kamenev 2023
;
Kamenev 2023
. For example, imbalanced and inefficient fermion counting in the
limit of inefficient detection constitutes a representative instance of driven
open quadratic fermionic systems with gain-loss dissipation
Prosen 2008
;
*Prosen2010
;
Barthel and Zhang 2022
. This class of systems has been studied extensively:
such models are known to host topologically nontrivial phases in their
nonequilibrium steady states
Bardyn et al. 2013
and to exhibit signatures of
non-Hermitian topology
Bergholtz et al. 2019
in their dynamics
Sayyad et al. 2021
,
including the non-Hermitian skin effect
Song et al. 2019
. Further paradigmatic
settings include systems with impurities, both in the bulk
Fröml et al. 2019
and
at the boundaries
Turkeshi et al. 2024
. The latter give rise to boundary-driven
models that provide a natural platform for studying quantum
transport
Žnidarič 2010
. For all of these scenarios, as well as for
related phenomena beyond the class of quadratic fermionic systems, our formalism
furnishes an analytical framework for exploring their unraveling into quantum
trajectories. This, in turn, paves the way toward shedding further light on the
question of whether universal properties exist that transcend the apparent
conceptual divide between measurement-induced pure-state dynamics and the
nonequilibrium steady states of driven open quantum systems.

Appendix A
Minimal physical model for quantum-jump trajectories

Here we outline a minimal physical model that gives rise to the discrete-time
dynamics governed by the Kraus operators in Eqs. (
1
)
and (
2
). The construction follows the standard quantum-optical
treatment of continuous monitoring
Gardiner and Zoller 2014
;
Gardiner and Zoller 2015
;
Wiseman and Milburn 2009
;
Jacobs 2014
.

We consider a generic system with Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
coupled to bosonic or
fermionic baths. The bath annihilation and creation operators are

\hat{b}_{a,k}
and
\hat{b}_{a,k}^{\dagger}
, where

a\in\mathsf{A}=\{1,\dotsc,A\}
indexes the baths and
k
labels discrete
bath modes of energies
\omega_{a,k}
. The operators satisfy

\left[\hat{b}_{a,k},\hat{b}_{a^{\prime},k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\right]_{\zeta}=\hat{b}_{a,k}\hat{b}_{a^{\prime},k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}-\zeta\hat{b}_{a^{\prime},k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{a,k}=\delta_{a,a^{\prime}}\delta_{k,k^{\prime}},

(177)

with
\zeta=+1
for bosons and
\zeta=-1
for fermions. The Hamiltonian of
bath
a
is

\hat{H}_{a}=\sum_{k}\omega_{a,k}\hat{b}_{a,k}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{a,k},

(178)

and the system-bath coupling is described by

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}=\mathrm{i}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\sum_{k}\left(g_{a,k}^{*}\hat{c}_{a}\hat{b}_{a,k}^{\dagger}-g_{a,k}\hat{b}_{a,k}\hat{c}_{a}^{\dagger}\right).

(179)

Bath
a
inherits its bosonic or fermionic statistics from the system operator

\hat{c}_{a}
. For example, in fermionic gain and loss processes governed by jump
operators (
80
), Eq. (
179
) represents
particle tunneling into and out of fermionic reservoirs
Starchl et al. 2025
.

All baths are assumed to start in the vacuum,
\hat{b}_{a,k}\lvert 0_{a}\rangle=0
, and
the joint initial state is

\lvert\Psi(t_{0})\rangle=\lvert\psi(t_{0}),0_{1},\dotsc,0_{A}\rangle
.

Following standard derivations of stochastic Schrödinger
dynamics
Gardiner and Zoller 2014
;
Gardiner and Zoller 2015
;
Wiseman and Milburn 2009
;
Jacobs 2014
, we move to
an interaction picture in which operators evolve under the bath
Hamiltonians (
178
), while the state evolves under

\hat{H}_{I}(t)=\hat{H}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}(t),

(180)

with

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}(t)=\mathrm{i}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\left[\hat{c}_{a}\hat{b}_{a}^{\dagger}(t)-\hat{b}_{a}(t)\hat{c}_{a}^{\dagger}\right],

(181)

and

\hat{b}_{a}(t)=\sum_{k}g_{a,k}\hat{b}_{a,k}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{a,k}t}.

(182)

The interaction-picture bath operators satisfy

\left[\hat{b}_{a}(t),\hat{b}_{a^{\prime}}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})\right]_{\zeta}=\delta_{a,a^{\prime}}\Gamma_{a}(t-t^{\prime}),

(183)

where

\Gamma_{a}(t)=\sum_{k}\left\lvert g_{a,k}\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{a,k}t}.

(184)

We now invoke the Markov approximation, assuming the bath correlation times are
short compared with characteristic system timescales, so that

\Gamma_{a}(t)=\gamma_{a}\delta(t).

(185)

In general,
\gamma_{a}
is complex. Its imaginary part, the Lamb shift,
contributes to the unitary evolution of the system and is typically absorbed
into
\hat{H}
. In the following, we neglect this Lamb-shift contribution.

The interaction-picture time evolution operator satisfies the integral equation

\hat{U}_{I}(t,t_{0})=\mathbbm{1}-\mathrm{i}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,\hat{H}_{I}(t^{\prime})\hat{U}_{I}(t^{\prime},t_{0}).

(186)

For simplicity, we set
t_{0}=0
. We integrate the integral equation in small
discrete time steps, such that
t=\Delta t
.

In the weak-coupling or Born approximation,
Eq. (
186
) can be solved perturbatively. Iterating
the integral equation to second order yields

\lvert\Psi_{I}(\Delta t)\rangle=\hat{U}_{I}(\Delta t,0)\lvert\Psi_{I}(0)\rangle=\lvert\Psi_{I}^{(0)}\rangle+\lvert\Psi_{I}^{(1)}\rangle+\lvert\Psi_{I}^{(2)}\rangle,

(187)

where
\lvert\Psi_{I}^{(0)}\rangle=\lvert\Psi_{I}(0)\rangle
. By using

\hat{b}_{a}(t)\lvert 0_{a}\rangle=0
, the first-order contribution can be written as

\begin{split}\lvert\Psi_{I}^{(1)}\rangle&=-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\mathrm{d}t\,\hat{H}_{I}(t)\lvert\Psi_{I}(0)\rangle\\
&=\left[-\mathrm{i}\hat{H}\Delta t+\sum_{a=1}^{A}\sqrt{\gamma_{a}}\hat{c}_{a}\Delta\hat{B}_{a}^{\dagger}(0)\right]\lvert\Psi_{I}(0)\rangle,\end{split}

(188)

where the quantum Itô increments are

\Delta\hat{B}_{a}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{a}}}\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,\hat{b}_{a}(t^{\prime}).

(189)

They satisfy the Markov-approximation commutation relation

\left[\Delta\hat{B}_{a}(t),\Delta\hat{B}_{a^{\prime}}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})\right]_{\zeta}=\Delta t\,\delta_{a,a^{\prime}}\delta_{t,t^{\prime}}.

(190)

The second-order contribution is

\begin{split}\lvert\Psi_{I}^{(2)}\rangle&=-\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime\prime}\,\hat{H}_{I}(t^{\prime})\hat{H}_{I}(t^{\prime\prime})\lvert\Psi_{I}(0)\rangle\\
&=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a=1}^{A}\gamma_{a}\hat{c}_{a}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{a}\Delta t\lvert\Psi_{I}(0)\rangle,\end{split}

(191)

where terms generating two reservoir excitations, which occurs with probability

O(\Delta t^{2})
, are omitted
Gardiner and Zoller 2015
.

So far, we have considered evolution from the initial time
t=0
to

\Delta t
. However, the evolution equation holds for arbitrary time steps from

t
to
t+\Delta t
. This is because the quantum Itô
increments (
189
) commute for different time steps
according to Eq. (
190
). The description in
terms of the commuting operators
\Delta\hat{B}(t)
in each time interval
suggests a decomposition of each bath Hilbert space into a product of Hilbert
spaces for individual time intervals
Gardiner and Zoller 2015
. This leads to an
important simplification of the description of the evolution process: evolution
up to time
t
does not affect the Hilbert spaces at later times; these remain
in the vacuum state. Thus, from Eq. (
187
), we obtain

\begin{split}\lvert\Psi_{I}(t+\Delta t)\rangle&=\left[1-\mathrm{i}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta t+\sum_{a=1}^{A}\sqrt{\gamma_{a}}\hat{c}_{a}\Delta\hat{B}_{a}^{\dagger}(t)\right]\lvert\Psi_{I}(t)\rangle\\
&=\left(\hat{K}_{0}+\sum_{a=1}^{A}\hat{K}_{a}\frac{\Delta\hat{B}_{a}^{\dagger}(t)}{\sqrt{\Delta t}}\right)\lvert\Psi_{I}(t)\rangle,\end{split}

(192)

with
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
defined in Eq. (
3
). The final form
makes explicit the Kraus operators in Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
).

We now incorporate continuous monitoring of the bath populations by performing,
in each time step, a projective measurement of

\hat{N}_{a}(t)=\frac{\Delta\hat{B}_{a}^{\dagger}(t)}{\sqrt{\Delta t}}\frac{\Delta\hat{B}_{a}(t)}{\sqrt{\Delta t}}.

(193)

Since all baths begin in vacuum, the allowed outcomes are either no excitation
or a single excitation in one bath. Multiple excitations occur with probability

O(\Delta t^{2})
and are neglected.

The probability of detecting no excitation is

P_{0}=\left\lVert\hat{K}_{0}\lvert\Psi_{I}(t)\rangle\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert\hat{K}_{0}\lvert\psi(t)\rangle\right\rVert^{2},

(194)

and the system state updates as

\lvert\psi(t)\rangle\mapsto\hat{K}_{0}\lvert\psi(t)\rangle/\sqrt{P_{0}}
. This corresponds to
continuous, no-jump evolution. The probability of detecting an excitation in
bath
a
is

P_{a}=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\left\lVert\hat{K}_{a}\Delta\hat{B}_{a}^{\dagger}(t)\lvert\Psi_{I}(t)\rangle\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert\hat{K}_{a}\lvert\psi(t)\rangle\right\rVert^{2},

(195)

and the corresponding update is

\lvert\psi(t)\rangle\mapsto\hat{K}_{a}\lvert\psi(t)\rangle/\sqrt{P_{a}}
, representing a
quantum jump. These probabilities and update rules reproduce precisely the
quantum-trajectory description of Eqs. (
8
),
(
9
), and (
10
).

For completeness, we note that Eq. (
192
) may also be recast as
a quantum stochastic Schrödinger equation for the system alone by introducing
stochastic increments
\Delta N_{a}(t)
that count bath excitations in each time
step
Gardiner and Zoller 2014
;
Gardiner and Zoller 2015
;
Wiseman and Milburn 2009
;
Jacobs 2014
.

Appendix B
Generalized Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation with PHS

In this appendix, we present a detailed and self-contained derivation of the
generalized Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation for systems with PHS,
culminating in Eq. (
145
), which expresses the measurement
Lagrangian in terms of the matrix field
\mathcal{G}
. In
Sec.
B.1
, we begin with an elementary evaluation of
Gaussian Grassmann integrals in the presence of PHS. This serves as the
foundation for the derivation of Wick’s theorem in Sec.
B.2
,
which is in turn required for establishing a useful identity that enables the
simultaneous decoupling of measurement vertices in all slow channels, as
discussed in Sec.
B.3
. The decoupled vertex is obtained by
performing all possible Wick contractions, which can be viewed equivalently as
evaluating a suitable Gaussian Grassmann integral. Our analysis also resolves a
missing prefactor in the corresponding formula reported in
Ref.
Poboiko et al. 2025
. Finally, exploiting the Gaussian integral
representation of the decoupled measurement Lagrangian, we derive
Eq. (
145
) in Sec.
B.4
.

B.1
Gaussian integrals with PHS

We aim to show that

Z=\int\mathrm{D}[\psi^{*},\psi]\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\left(\bar{\Psi}\mathcal{G}^{-1}\Psi+J^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\Psi\right)}=\mathop{\mathrm{Pf}}\!\left(-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{G}^{-1}\right)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}J^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{C}J},

(196)

where
\mathcal{G}
is a matrix in Keldysh, charge-conjugation, replica, and,
when applicable, position and discrete-time spaces. Thus,
\mathcal{G}
is a

4D\times 4D
matrix, where
4
is the dimension of the combined Keldysh and
charge-conjugation space, and
D=R
is the dimension of replica space or

D=RLN
when
N
discrete time steps are included. In the continuous-time
limit, matrix multiplication in time space becomes integration, and
\mathop{\mathrm{Pf}}

becomes a functional Pfaffian. When
D=R
,
\mathop{\mathrm{Pf}}
reduces to the ordinary
Pfaffian
\mathop{\mathrm{pf}}
. The spinors
\Psi
and
\bar{\Psi}
are defined in
Eq. (
137
) and satisfy the constraint

\bar{\Psi}=\Psi^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{C}
, with
\mathcal{C}
given in
Eq. (
139
). The source vector
J
has
4D
Grassmann components. Finally,
the integration measure is

\begin{split}\mathrm{D}[\psi^{*},\psi]&=\prod_{d=1}^{D}\mathrm{d}\psi_{+,d}^{*}\mathrm{d}\psi_{+,d}\mathrm{d}\psi_{-,d}^{*}\mathrm{d}\psi_{-,d}\\
&=\frac{1}{4^{D}}\prod_{d=1}^{D}\prod_{i=1}^{4}\mathrm{d}\Psi_{i,d}=\frac{1}{4^{D}}\mathrm{D}[\Psi].\end{split}

(197)

This ordering of fields is the natural one arising in the construction of the
Keldysh functional integral
Altland and Simons 2010
;
Sieberer et al. 2016
;
Sieberer et al. 2025
;
Thompson and Kamenev 2023
;
Kamenev 2023
.

To begin, we rewrite the quadratic form in the exponent as

\bar{\Psi}\mathcal{G}^{-1}\Psi=\Psi^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{G}^{-1}\Psi=\Psi^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{P}\Psi,\qquad\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{C}\mathcal{G}^{-1}.

(198)

Without loss of generality,
\mathcal{P}=-\mathcal{P}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}
is
skew-symmetric: any symmetric component of
\mathcal{P}
would cancel in the
quadratic form
\Psi^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{P}\Psi
due to the Grassmann nature
of
\Psi
. Note that skew-symmetry of
\mathcal{P}
is equivalent to the
condition (
144
) for
\mathcal{G}
. Being skew-symmetric,
even-dimensional, and generally complex,
\mathcal{P}
can be expressed in the
form

\mathcal{P}=U^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\Sigma U,\quad U^{-1}=U^{\dagger},\quad\Sigma=\bigoplus_{d=1}^{2D}\lambda_{d}\mathrm{i}\sigma_{y},\quad\lambda_{d}\geq 0.

(199)

We define

\Upsilon=U\Psi=V\Phi,\qquad V=\bigoplus_{d=1}^{2D}v,\qquad v=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
\mathrm{i}&-\mathrm{i}\end{pmatrix}.

(200)

Writing

\Phi=\left(\phi_{1},\phi_{1}^{*},\dotsc,\phi_{2D},\phi_{2D}^{*}\right)^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}
,
where
\phi_{d}
and
\phi_{d}^{*}
are independent Grassmann variables, and using

v^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathrm{i}\sigma_{y}v=\sigma_{y}
, we may further rewrite the
quadratic form as

\Psi^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{P}\Psi=\Upsilon^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\Sigma\Upsilon=\sum_{d=1}^{2D}\lambda_{d}\left(\Upsilon_{2d-1},\Upsilon_{2d}\right)\mathrm{i}\sigma_{y}\begin{pmatrix}\Upsilon_{2d-1}\\
\Upsilon_{2d}\end{pmatrix}\\
=\sum_{d=1}^{2D}\lambda_{d}\left(\phi_{d},\phi_{d}^{*}\right)\sigma_{y}\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{d}\\
\phi_{d}^{*}\end{pmatrix}=2\mathrm{i}\sum_{d=1}^{2D}\lambda_{d}\phi_{d}^{*}\phi_{d}=\mathrm{i}\phi^{\dagger}A\phi,

(201)

where

\phi=\left(\phi_{1},\dotsc,\phi_{2D}\right)^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}},\quad A=2\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(\lambda_{1},\dotsc,\lambda_{2D}).

(202)

Next, we express the source term
J^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\Psi
using
\phi
and

\phi^{*}
. Define the
4D\times 2D
matrices
P_{\pm}
as

P_{+}=\bigoplus_{d=1}^{2D}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},\qquad P_{-}=\bigoplus_{d=1}^{2D}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix},

(203)

so that
\Phi=P_{+}\phi+P_{-}\phi^{*}
. Then

J^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\Psi=J^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}U^{\dagger}V\Phi=-\mathrm{i}\left(\mu^{\dagger}\phi-\phi^{\dagger}\nu\right),

(204)

where

\mu^{\dagger}=\mathrm{i}J^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}U^{\dagger}VP_{+},\qquad\nu=-\mathrm{i}P_{-}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}V^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}U^{*}J.

(205)

We now transform the integration measure (
197
) to the
variables
\phi
and
\phi^{*}
. From Eq. (
200
),

\mathrm{D}[\psi^{*},\psi]=\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{Det}}(U)}{4^{N}\mathop{\mathrm{Det}}(V)}\mathrm{D}[\Phi].

(206)

Here
\mathop{\mathrm{Det}}
denotes a determinant over Keldysh, charge-conjugation, replica, and,
when applicable, position and discrete-time spaces. In the continuous-time
limit, it becomes a functional determinant; otherwise it reduces to an ordinary
determinant
\det
. Using

\mathop{\mathrm{Det}}(V)=\prod_{d=1}^{2D}\det(v)=\left(-\mathrm{i}\right)^{2D}=\left(-1\right)^{D},

(207)

and

\mathrm{D}[\Phi]=\prod_{d=1}^{2D}\mathrm{d}\phi_{d}\mathrm{d}\phi_{d}^{*}=\prod_{d=1}^{2D}\mathrm{d}\phi_{d}^{*}\mathrm{d}\phi_{d}=\mathrm{D}[\phi^{*},\phi],

(208)

we obtain

\mathrm{D}[\psi^{*},\psi]=\left(-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{D}\mathop{\mathrm{Det}}(U)\mathrm{D}[\phi^{*},\phi].

(209)

Substituting Eqs. (
201
), (
204
),
and (
209
) into Eq. (
196

Gaussian Grassmann integral

\begin{split}Z&=\left(-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{D}\mathop{\mathrm{Det}}(U)\int\mathrm{D}[\phi^{*},\phi]\,\mathrm{e}^{-\phi^{\dagger}A\phi+\mu^{\dagger}\phi+\phi^{\dagger}\nu}\\
&=\left(-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{D}\mathop{\mathrm{Det}}(U)\mathop{\mathrm{Det}}(A)\mathrm{e}^{\mu^{\dagger}A^{-1}\nu}.\end{split}

(210)

We now rewrite the prefactor in terms of the Pfaffian of
\mathcal{P}

Eqs. (
202
) and (

\mathop{\mathrm{Det}}(A)=\prod_{d=1}^{2D}2\lambda_{d}=4^{D}\mathop{\mathrm{Pf}}(\Sigma).

(211)

Thus,

\left(-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{D}\mathop{\mathrm{Det}}(U)\mathop{\mathrm{Det}}(A)=\left(-1\right)^{D}\mathop{\mathrm{Det}}(U)\mathop{\mathrm{Pf}}(\Sigma)\\
=\left(-1\right)^{D}\mathop{\mathrm{Pf}}\!\left(U^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\Sigma U\right)=\left(-1\right)^{D}\mathop{\mathrm{Pf}}(\mathcal{P})=\mathop{\mathrm{Pf}}(-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{P}).

(212)

Next, we express the exponent in Eq. (
210
) in terms of the
source vector
J
. Using Eq. (
205
),

\mu^{\dagger}A^{-1}\nu=J^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}U^{\dagger}VP_{+}A^{-1}P_{-}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}V^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}U^{*}J.

(213)

With the explicit forms of
A
and
P_{\pm}
in Eqs. (
202
)
and (
203
), respectively,

P_{+}A^{-1}P_{-}=\bigoplus_{d=1}^{2D}\frac{1}{2\lambda_{d}}\sigma_{+}.

(214)

Using
\sigma_{+}=(1+\mathrm{i}\sigma_{y})/2

vv^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}=\sigma_{z}
and
v\mathrm{i}\sigma_{y}v^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}=\sigma_{y}
,

VP_{+}A^{-1}P_{-}V^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}=\bigoplus_{d=1}^{2D}\frac{1}{4\lambda_{d}}\left(\sigma_{z}+\sigma_{y}\right)=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}\left(E^{-1}+\Sigma^{-1}\right),

(215)

where
\Sigma
is defined in Eq. (
199

E=\bigoplus_{d=1}^{2D}\lambda_{d}\mathrm{i}\sigma_{z}=E^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}.

(216)

Thus, using
U^{*}=\left(U^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\right)^{-1}

Eq. (
210

\begin{split}\mu^{\dagger}A^{-1}\nu&=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}J^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}U^{-1}\left(E^{-1}+\Sigma^{-1}\right)U^{*}J\\

(217)

Since

B.2
Wick’s theorem

We now use Eq. (
196
) to derive Wick’s theorem for systems with PHS.
To begin, we rewrite Eq. (
196
) as

\langle\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}J^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\Psi}\rangle=\int\frac{\mathrm{D}[\psi^{*},\psi]}{\mathop{\mathrm{Pf}}\!\left(-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{G}^{-1}\right)}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\left(\bar{\Psi}\mathcal{G}^{-1}\Psi+J^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\Psi\right)}=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}J^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{C}J}.

(218)

Expanding both sides in power series and matching terms involving the same total
power of components of
J
, here equal to
2F
, yields

\frac{1}{(2F)!}\langle\left(\mathrm{i}J^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\Psi\right)^{2F}\rangle=\frac{1}{F!}\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}J^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{C}J\right)^{F}.

(219)

In the following we require Wick’s theorem in the setting where all fields share
the same position and time while differing only in their Keldysh,
charge-conjugation, and replica indices. We combine these indices into a single
multiindex
\alpha
. Thus, we can rewrite Eq. (
219
) as

\frac{\left(-1\right)^{F}}{(2F)!}\sum_{\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{2F}}\langle\prod_{f=1}^{2F}J_{\alpha_{f}}\Psi_{\alpha_{f}}\rangle\\
=\frac{1}{4^{F}F!}\sum_{\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{2f}}\prod_{f=1}^{F}J_{\alpha_{2f-1}}\left(\mathrm{i}\mathcal{G}C\right)_{\alpha_{2f-1},\alpha_{2f}}J_{\alpha_{2f}}.

(220)

Consider now a fixed set of indices
\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{2F}
.
Equation (
220
) must continue to hold when we restrict the
sums to those terms involving only this particular set of indices. These
indices must be distinct; otherwise, the product over Grassmann variables
vanishes identically. Under this restriction, the sums run over all
permutations of the fixed indices. Let
\mathrm{S}_{2F}
denote the symmetric
group of permutations of
2F
elements. Then

\frac{\left(-1\right)^{F}}{(2F)!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{S}_{2F}}\langle\prod_{f=1}^{2F}J_{\alpha_{\sigma_{f}}}\Psi_{\alpha_{\sigma_{f}}}\rangle\\
=\frac{1}{4^{F}F!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{S}_{2F}}\prod_{f=1}^{F}J_{\alpha_{\sigma_{2f-1}}}\left(\mathrm{i}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{C}\right)_{\alpha_{\sigma_{2f-1}},\alpha_{\sigma_{2f}}}J_{\alpha_{\sigma_{2f}}}.

(221)

We first reorder the product on the left-hand side of Eq. (
221
).
Because pairs of Grassmann variables commute, the labels
\alpha_{\sigma_{f}}
may
be replaced by
\alpha_{f}
. The sum over permutations then yields
(2F)!
and
cancels the prefactor. Separating the
J
and
\Psi
variables gives

\prod_{f=1}^{2F}J_{\alpha_{f}}\Psi_{\alpha_{f}}=\left(-1\right)^{F}\left(\prod_{f=1}^{2F}J_{\alpha_{f}}\right)\left(\prod_{f=1}^{2F}\Psi_{\alpha_{f}}\right).

(222)

Next, we rearrange the product on the right-hand side of Eq. (
221
).
Separating the
J
factors from the factors of
\mathrm{i}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{C}

and using
\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}(\sigma)
for the sign of
\sigma
, we obtain

\prod_{f=1}^{F}J_{\alpha_{\sigma_{2f-1}}}J_{\alpha_{\sigma_{2f}}}=\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}(\sigma)\prod_{f=1}^{2F}J_{\alpha_{f}}.

(223)

Having aligned both sides of Eq. (
221
) with respect to the monomial

\prod_{f=1}^{2F}J_{\alpha_{f}}
, we compare coefficients and arrive at Wick’s
theorem:

\begin{split}\langle\prod_{f=1}^{2F}\Psi_{\alpha_{f}}\rangle&=\frac{1}{2^{2F}F!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{S}_{2F}}\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}(\sigma)\prod_{f=1}^{F}\left(\mathrm{i}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{C}\right)_{\alpha_{\sigma_{2f-1}},\alpha_{\sigma_{2f}}}\\
&=\frac{1}{2^{F}}\mathop{\mathrm{pf}}\!\left\{\left[\left(\mathrm{i}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{C}\right)_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}\right]_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in\{\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{2F}\}}\right\}.\end{split}

(224)

B.3
Decoupling of measurement vertices with PHS

As shown in Ref.
Poboiko et al. 2023
for symmetry class AIII, when
\mathcal{G}

in the generalized Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation (
99
) is
used to describe long-wavelength fluctuations, measurement vertices must be
decoupled simultaneously in all possible channels. This requirement is
satisfied by summing over all Wick contractions of measurement vertices. We
extend the reasoning of Ref.
Poboiko et al. 2023
to symmetry class BDI, which
clarifies the emergence of factors
\beta=1/2

Poboiko et al. 2025
.

We denote a point in spacetime by
\mathbf{x}=(l,t)
and the corresponding
momentum by
\mathbf{q}=(q,\omega)
, with
\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{x}=ql-\omega t
. For notational convenience, we work in a finite, continuous
spacetime volume so that the momenta
\mathbf{q}
are discrete. The integration
over
\Sigma
in Eq. (
143
) enforces

\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})=-\mathrm{i}\Psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})\bar{\Psi}_{\alpha^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x}).

(225)

To explicitly implement the assumption that
\mathcal{G}
represents a slow
mode, we rewrite this identity in momentum space and introduce a UV cutoff

\Lambda
. Moreover, we replace
\bar{\Psi}
by
\Psi^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}
by
multiplying
\mathcal{G}
by
\mathcal{C}
:

\left(\mathrm{i}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{C}\right)_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q})=\theta(\Lambda-\left\lvert\mathbf{q}\right\rvert)\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\Psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}/2)\Psi_{\alpha^{\prime}}(-\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}/2).

(226)

Consider now a local vertex with
2F
fields, contributing to the action as

S[\Psi]=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{x}\prod_{f=1}^{2F}\Psi_{\alpha_{f}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{1},\dotsc,\mathbf{k}_{2F}}\delta_{0,\sum_{f=1}^{2F}\mathbf{k}_{f}}\prod_{f=1}^{2F}\Psi_{\alpha_{f}}(\mathbf{k}_{f}).

(227)

To project this vertex onto slow modes, we retain only those contributions for
which the momenta are pairwise close,

\left\lvert\mathbf{k}_{f}-\mathbf{k}_{f^{\prime}}\right\rvert\lesssim\Lambda
. To enumerate all such
pairings, we start from
\left\lvert\mathbf{k}_{2f-1}-\mathbf{k}_{2f}\right\rvert\lesssim\Lambda
for
f\in\{1,\dotsc,F\}
and apply all permutations

\sigma\in\mathrm{S}_{2F}
. This procedure overcounts equivalent pairings:
each
\sigma
can be followed by
F!
pair permutations without changing the
pair structure, and reversing the order within each pair also leaves the pairing
unchanged. Correcting for this overcounting requires division by
2^{F}F!
:

S[\Psi]=\frac{1}{2^{F}F!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{S}_{2F}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{1},\dotsc,\mathbf{k}_{2F}}\delta_{0,\sum_{f=1}^{2F}\mathbf{k}_{f}}\\
\times\left[\prod_{f=1}^{F}\theta\!\left(\Lambda-\left\lvert\mathbf{k}_{\sigma_{2f-1}}-\mathbf{k}_{\sigma_{2f}}\right\rvert\right)\right]\left[\prod_{f=1}^{2F}\Psi_{\alpha_{f}}(\mathbf{k}_{f})\right].

(228)

We reorganize the product of fields:

\prod_{f=1}^{2F}\Psi_{\alpha_{f}}(\mathbf{k}_{f})=\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}(\sigma)\prod_{f=1}^{F}\Psi_{\alpha_{\sigma_{2f-1}}}(\mathbf{k}_{\sigma_{2f-1}})\Psi_{\alpha_{\sigma_{2f}}}(\mathbf{k}_{\sigma_{2f}}).

(229)

Define the momentum variables

\mathbf{K}_{f}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{k}_{\sigma_{2f-1}}-\mathbf{k}_{\sigma_{2f}}\right),\quad\mathbf{q}_{f}=-\left(\mathbf{k}_{\sigma_{2f-1}}+\mathbf{k}_{\sigma_{2f}}\right).

(230)

This yields

S[\Psi]=\frac{1}{2^{F}F!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{S}_{2F}}\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}(\sigma)\sum_{\mathbf{q}_{1},\dotsc,\mathbf{q}_{F}}\delta_{0,\sum_{f=1}^{F}\mathbf{q}_{f}}\\
\times\prod_{f=1}^{F}\theta\!\left(\Lambda-\left\lvert\mathbf{q}_{f}\right\rvert\right)\sum_{\mathbf{K}_{f}}\Psi_{\alpha_{\sigma_{2f-1}}}(\mathbf{K}_{f}-\mathbf{q}_{f}/2)\Psi_{\alpha_{\sigma_{2f}}}(-\mathbf{K}_{f}-\mathbf{q}_{f}/2).

(231)

Comparing with Eq. (
226
), we identify the structure of the slow
mode on the right-hand side. Transforming back to spacetime, we obtain

S[\Psi]=\frac{1}{2^{F}F!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{S}_{2F}}\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}(\sigma)\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{x}\prod_{f=1}^{F}\left(\mathrm{i}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{C}\right)_{\alpha_{\sigma_{2f-1}},\alpha_{\sigma_{2f}}}(\mathbf{x}).

(232)

When we now compare Eq. (
232
) with Wick’s
theorem (
224
), we observe that the right-hand sides match up
to the factor
1/2^{F}=\beta^{F}
appearing in the latter. This factor may be
absorbed by a redefinition of
\mathcal{G}
. Thus, the projection of the vertex
onto soft modes is equivalent to averaging the original vertex as in
Eq. (
218
), but with
\mathcal{G}
replaced by

\mathcal{G}/\beta
. Writing the vertex as

S[\Psi]=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{x}\,V[\Psi(\mathbf{x})]
, we obtain

V[\mathcal{G}]=\int\frac{\mathrm{D}[\psi^{*},\psi]}{\mathop{\mathrm{pf}}\!\left(-\mathrm{i}\beta\mathcal{C}\mathcal{G}^{-1}\right)}\,V[\Psi]\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\beta\bar{\Psi}\mathcal{G}^{-1}\Psi},

(233)

where all fields and matrices are evaluated at the same spacetime point, so the
Pfaffian in the denominator is taken only over Keldysh, charge-conjugation, and
replica spaces. The factor
\beta
on the right-hand side is missing in
Ref.
Poboiko et al. 2025
.

B.4
Generalized Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation with PHS

Building on the preceding results, we now apply the generalized
Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation to the particle-hole-symmetrized action

S=S_{H}+S_{M}
with the Hamiltonian action (
140
) and the
measurement Lagrangian (
142
).

We begin by rewriting the measurement Lagrangian (
142
) in terms of

\mathcal{G}=-\mathrm{i}\Psi\bar{\Psi}
. For the first two terms, we obtain

\bar{\Psi}\Lambda\Psi=-\mathrm{i}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\Lambda\mathcal{G}),\qquad\bar{\Psi}\sigma_{z}\Psi=-\mathrm{i}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\sigma_{z}\mathcal{G}).

(234)

To decouple the third term, we use Eq. (
233
). In preparation for
performing the Grassmann integral on the right-hand side, we express the
measurement vertex in exponential form, noting that any exponential containing

\bar{\Psi}_{r}\sigma_{\pm}\Psi_{r}
truncates after two terms. This yields

V_{\pm}[\Psi]=\prod_{r=1}^{R}\left(-\bar{\Psi}_{r}\sigma_{\pm}\Psi_{r}\right)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}(\mathrm{i}\epsilon)^{R}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\epsilon}\bar{\Psi}\sigma_{\pm}\Psi},

(235)

where the replica index
r
is implicitly summed in the exponent. This relation
recasts Eq. (
233
) as a Gaussian integral. Using
Eq. (
196
), we thus obtain

V_{\pm}[\mathcal{G}]=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}(\mathrm{i}\epsilon)^{R}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{pf}}\!\left[-\mathrm{i}\beta\mathcal{C}\left(\mathcal{G}^{-1}+\frac{1}{\beta\epsilon}\sigma_{\pm}\right)\right]}{\mathop{\mathrm{pf}}\!\left(-\mathrm{i}\beta\mathcal{C}\mathcal{G}^{-1}\right)}.

(236)

To simplify the ratio of Pfaffians, we use that for any skew-symmetric

4R\times 4R
matrix
A
,
\mathop{\mathrm{pf}}(A)^{-1}=\mathop{\mathrm{pf}}(A^{-1})
. We then apply
Eq. (
153
) with
D=2R
. After that, splitting the trace
over Keldysh, charge-conjugation, and replica spaces as
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{CR}}
, and
writing the prefactor as

\epsilon^{R}=\mathrm{e}^{\beta\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{CR}}[\ln(\epsilon)]},

(237)

we obtain

V_{\pm}[\mathcal{G}]=\mathrm{i}^{R}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{CR}}\ln\left[\epsilon\exp\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{\beta\epsilon}\sigma_{\pm}\mathcal{G}\right)\right]}.

(238)

To take the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, we expand
\exp\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}\ln
as a power series
in
1/\epsilon
. Note that since
\mathcal{G}
is a matrix in Keldysh,
charge-conjugation, and replica spaces,
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}
is only a partial trace, and

\exp\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}\ln
does not reduce to a determinant in Keldysh space. The expansion
of such a determinant in two dimensions would terminate at second order. While
this simplification does not generally occur when
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}
is a partial trace, the
appearance of
\sigma_{\pm}
causes the expansion to terminate already at first
order, as we show in the following.

We obtain a formal series expansion by using the expansions of the exponential
function and the logarithm:

\mathrm{e}^{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{\beta\epsilon}\sigma_{\pm}\mathcal{G}\right)}=1+\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(\beta\epsilon)^{K}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+K}}{n!}\\
\times\sum_{k_{1},\dotsc,k_{n}=1}^{\infty}\delta_{K,\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k_{i}}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}\!\left[(\sigma_{\pm}\mathcal{G})^{k_{i}}\right].

(239)

To simplify the final product, we note first that for any
2\times 2
matrices

A
and
B
, one can check by explicit calculation that

\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\sigma_{\pm}A\sigma_{\pm}B)=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\sigma_{\pm}A)\,\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\sigma_{\pm}B).

(240)

This factorization property can be seen to extend to partial traces by using the
Schmidt decomposition of
\mathcal{G}
,

\mathcal{G}=\sum_{\mu=0}^{3}\sigma_{\mu}\otimes\mathcal{G}_{\mu},

(241)

where
\sigma_{\mu}
are Pauli matrices in Keldysh space
\mathcal{G}_{\mu}
are

2R\times 2R
matrices in charge-conjugation and replica spaces. We find

\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}\!\left[(\sigma_{\pm}\mathcal{G})^{k}\right]=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}(\sigma_{\pm}\mathcal{G})^{k}.

(242)

Thus,

\mathrm{e}^{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{\beta\epsilon}\sigma_{\pm}\mathcal{G}\right)}=1+\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}c_{K}\left[\frac{1}{\beta\epsilon}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}(\sigma_{\pm}\mathcal{G})^{K}\right]^{K},

(243)

where

c_{K}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+K}}{n!}\sum_{k_{1},\dotsc,k_{n}=1}^{\infty}\delta_{K,\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k_{i}}.

(244)

Now consider a
1\times 1
matrix
A
such that

\det(1+A/\epsilon)=1+A/\epsilon
. On the other hand, we can write

\det(1+A/\epsilon)=\exp\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\ln(1+A/\epsilon)
and use the exact same
expansion as above—obviously, a factorization property analogous to
Eq. (
240
) applies also to
1\times 1
matrices. Then,
by comparing the coefficients of equal powers of
1/\epsilon
in

\det(1+A/\epsilon)=1+A/\epsilon
and the right-hand side of
Eq. (
243
), we find
c_{1}=1
and
c_{K}=0
for
K\geq 2
. Therefore,

\mathrm{e}^{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{\beta\epsilon}\sigma_{\pm}\mathcal{G}\right)}=1+\frac{1}{\beta\epsilon}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}(\sigma_{\pm}\mathcal{G}).

(245)

Inserting this into Eq. (
238
) and taking the limit
\epsilon\to 0

yields

V_{\pm}[\mathcal{G}]=\mathrm{i}^{R}\mathrm{e}^{\beta\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{CR}}\ln\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{K}}\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\sigma_{\pm}\mathcal{G}\right)}.

(246)

Finally, substituting Eqs. (
234
) and (
246
)
into Eq. (
142
) yields Eq. (
145
).

With the measurement Lagrangian decoupled, the remaining Grassmann integral in
the Keldysh partition function is quadratic and can be evaluated using
Eq. (
196
). Leaving the summation over lattice sites and integration
over time implicit, we obtain

\begin{split}\int\mathrm{D}[\psi^{*},\psi]\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\bar{\Psi}G^{-1}\Psi}&=\mathop{\mathrm{Pf}}\!\left(-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{C}G^{-1}\right)\\
&=\left(-1\right)^{RLN}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\left[\beta\ln\left(G^{-1}\right)\right]},\end{split}

(247)

where the Pfaffian is taken over Keldysh, charge-conjugation, replica, position,
and time spaces, and where
G^{-1}=G_{0}^{-1}+\mathrm{i}\Sigma
with
G_{0}^{-1}

defined in Eq. (
93
). In the second equality we used
Eq. (
153
) with
A=\sigma_{z}\mathcal{C}
and

B=\mathcal{C}G^{-1}
, and
D=2RLN
, where
LN
is the dimension of
position-time space, that is, lattice size times the number of time steps. The
sign arises from

\mathop{\mathrm{Pf}}(\sigma_{z}\mathcal{C})=\mathop{\mathrm{pf}_{KC}}(\sigma_{z}\mathcal{C})^{RLN}=(-1)^{RLN}.

(248)

We absorb this sign into the integration measure
\mathrm{D}[\mathcal{G},\Sigma]
.
The resulting Keldysh partition function is then given by
Eqs. (
104
) and (
105
), with
S_{0}
from
Eq. (
146
).

Appendix C
Numerical methods

In what follows we provide a description of the numerical procedure we use to
simulate quantum trajectories for imbalanced and inefficient fermion
counting. Our approach builds on the formulation of quantum-jump trajectories
for efficient detection in Sec.
II
together with
its extension to inefficient detection in Sec.
V
.

The dynamics proceed in discrete time steps
\Delta t
. For inefficient
detection, each step is further decomposed into two substeps of duration

\eta\Delta t
and
\left(1-\eta\right)\Delta t
, respectively. The first
substep implements measurement-conditioned evolution; its numerical treatment is
presented in Sec.
C.1
. The second substep implements
unconditional evolution, corresponding to an average over measurement outcomes;
the associated algorithm is detailed in Sec.
C.2
.
Additional implementation details for the no-jump evolution during the first
substep are provided in Sec.
C.3
.

For generality, we formulate the algorithm in terms of arbitrary gain and loss
jump operators,

\hat{c}_{+,l}=\sqrt{2}\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{L}B_{+,l,l^{\prime}}\hat{\psi}_{l^{\prime}}^{\dagger},\quad\hat{c}_{-,l}=\sqrt{2}\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{L}B_{-,l,l^{\prime}}\hat{\psi}_{l^{\prime}}.

(249)

Moreover, for later use, we denote the
l
th row and column of a matrix
A
by

A_{l,*}
and
A_{*,l}
, respectively, and introduce

M_{+,l}=\left(B_{+}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\right)_{*,l}B_{+,l,*}^{*},\qquad M_{-,l}=\left(B_{-}^{\dagger}\right)_{*,l}B_{-,l,*},

(250)

along with the Hermitian bath matrices

M_{+}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}M_{+,l}=B_{+}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}B_{+}^{*},\qquad M_{-}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}M_{-,l}=B_{-}^{\dagger}B_{-}.

(251)

The corresponding expressions for local gain and loss as in
Eq. (
80
) are obtained by setting

B_{\pm}=\sqrt{\gamma_{\pm}/2}.

(252)

C.1
Conditional evolution

During the first time substep, the evolution is conditioned on the measurement
outcome, and the algorithm samples different outcomes stochastically. However,
we do not directly implement the trajectory evolution described in
Secs.
II

and
V
. Instead, exploiting the locality of the
jump processes, we construct a more efficient scheme.

To begin, we outline why the first-order-in-time quantum-jump
method
Daley 2014
of Secs.
II

and
V
cannot be applied directly. The probability
that any jump occurs during a single time step, given the system state

\hat{\rho}(t)
, is the sum of the individual jump probabilities in
Eq. (
70
). With
\alpha=\pm
denoting gain and loss and

l
the site index,

P_{\alpha,l}=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left[\mathcal{K}_{1,\alpha,l}\hat{\rho}(t)\right].

(253)

Substituting here the jump superoperator (
71
) together
with Eq. (
68
) and the general gain and loss jump
operators (
249
), one obtains

\begin{split}P_{+,l}&=2\eta\Delta t\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left\{M_{+,l}\left[1-D(t)\right]\right\}=\eta\gamma_{+}\Delta t\left[1-D_{l,l}(t)\right],\\
P_{-,l}&=2\eta\Delta t\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left[M_{-,l}D(t)\right]=\eta\gamma_{-}\Delta tD_{l,l}(t),\end{split}

(254)

where
D(t)
is the single-particle density
matrix (
168
), the matrices
M_{\pm,l}
are
defined in Eq. (
250
), and the final equalities apply in the case of
local gain and loss. The total jump probability during the time step form
t
to

t+\Delta t
becomes

P_{\mathrm{jump}}=\sum_{\alpha=\pm}\sum_{l=1}^{L}P_{\alpha,l}=\eta\Delta t\left\{L\gamma_{+}-\Delta\gamma\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}[D(t)]\right\},

(255)

where
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}[D(t)]=\langle\hat{N}(t)\rangle
is the mean particle number.
Since
\langle\hat{N}(t)\rangle
is typically of order
L
, enforcing

P_{\mathrm{jump}}\ll 1
, as required for the validity of the first-order
method
Daley 2014
, forces
\Delta t\sim L^{-1}
, which is computationally
prohibitive.

To circumvent this limitation, we treat the local measurement processes at each
site independently. In each step, the lattice is traversed in a randomly chosen
order. For site
l
, the jump probability is

P_{\mathrm{jump},l}=\sum_{\alpha=\pm}P_{\alpha,l}=\eta\Delta t\left[\gamma_{+}-\Delta\gamma D_{l,l}(t)\right].

(256)

A uniformly distributed random number
r_{1}\in[0,1]
determines whether a jump
occurs: a jump is applied if
r_{1}<P_{\mathrm{jump},l}
. A second random
number
r_{2}\in[0,1]
determines whether the jump is gain or loss, with loss
selected if
r_{2}<\tilde{P}_{-,l}
, where

\tilde{P}_{\pm,l}=P_{\pm,l}/P_{\mathrm{jump},l}.

(257)

Otherwise the jump is gain. The corresponding transformation of the state is

\hat{\rho}(t)\to\left.\mathcal{K}_{1,\pm,l}\hat{\rho}(t)\middle/\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left[\mathcal{K}_{1,\pm,l}\hat{\rho}(t)\right]\right.,

(258)

which preserves Gaussianity. Hence the induced change in the single-particle
density matrix can be computed using Wick’s theorem. For a gain at site
l
,

\begin{split}D(t)\to D(t)+\frac{\left[1-D(t)\right]M_{+,l}\left[1-D(t)\right]}{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\{M_{+,l}\left[1-D(t)\right]\}}.\end{split}

(259)

and for a loss at site
l
,

D(t)\to D(t)-\frac{D(t)M_{-,l}D(t)}{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}[M_{-,l}D(t)]}.

(260)

For local gain and loss, multiplication by
M_{\pm,l}
merely selects specific
rows and columns of
D(t)
, allowing the updates to be implemented without
explicit matrix multiplications. In particular, for gain at site
l
, using the
notation of Eq. (
250
),

D(t)\to D(t)+\frac{[1-D(t)]_{*,l}[1-D(t)]_{l,*}}{1-D_{l,l}(t)}.

(261)

and for loss at site
l
,

D(t)\to D(t)-\frac{D_{*,l}(t)D_{l,*}(t)}{D_{l,l}(t)}.

(262)

After sweeping through the chain and applying all jumps, we carry out continuous
no-jump evolution generated by
\hat{K}_{1,0}
in Eq. (
68
). This
evolution acts on every lattice site, including those at which jumps occurred,
but the resulting error is negligible because a given site undergoes only
finitely many jumps in any finite time
Wiseman and Milburn 2009
. The continuous
evolution is

\hat{\rho}(t+\eta\Delta t)=\frac{\mathcal{K}_{1,0}\hat{\rho}(t)}{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left[\mathcal{K}_{1,0}\hat{\rho}(t)\right]}=\frac{\hat{K}_{1,0}\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{K}_{1,0}^{\dagger}}{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left[\hat{K}_{1,0}\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{K}_{1,0}^{\dagger}\right]}.

(263)

As shown in Sec.
C.3
, this can be recast in terms of the
single-particle density matrix as

D(t+\eta\Delta t)=U(\eta\Delta t)\\
\times\left\{D(t)+\mathrm{e}^{-\eta\Delta\gamma\Delta t}\left[1-D(t)\right]\right\}^{-1}D(t)U^{\dagger}(\eta\Delta t),

(264)

where
U(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}Ht}
. This completes the first time
substep.

Before discussing the unconditional evolution in the second substep, we
emphasize that treating local measurements independently allows us to choose a
time step that does not scale as
\Delta t\sim L^{-1}
. In practice, we select

\Delta t
such that the jump probability at a single site, estimated from
Eq. (
256
) with
\eta=1
and half filling as

P_{\mathrm{jump},l}=\gamma\Delta t
, is
0.0125\,\%
. We verified that the
results remain unchanged upon doubling or halving
\Delta t
.

We have not attempted a rigorous analysis of the error incurred by treating
local measurements independently. Instead, we performed numerical benchmarks:
observables linear in the state agree quantitatively with the unconditional
evolution that can be computed numerically exactly as described in
Sec.
C.2
. Furthermore, for balanced and efficient
fermion counting with
\gamma_{+}=\gamma_{-}
and
\eta=1
, our results agree
well with those of Ref.
Starchl et al. 2025
, obtained using a numerically exact
method
Daley 2014
. The implementation in Ref.
Starchl et al. 2025
relies on
the rates being balanced,
\gamma_{+}=\gamma_{-}
, in which case fermion counting
can be rephrased as a random generalized measurement. In that case, the waiting
time between jumps can be sampled from an exponential distribution, and the
evolution between jumps can be implemented through exact diagonalization.

C.2
Unconditional evolution

Unconditional evolution during the second time substep is governed by
Eq. (
72
). This equation corresponds to the first-order expansion in

\Delta t
of the evolution over the interval
\left(1-\eta\right)\Delta t

generated by the Liouvillian (
7
). In our numerical
implementation, we treat this interval exactly by evolving the state under the
master equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\rho}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathcal{L}\hat{\rho}(t),

(265)

for a duration
\left(1-\eta\right)\Delta t
. This master equation can be
recast as a closed equation of motion for the single-particle density
matrix (
168
), which admits an exact
solution
Starchl and Sieberer 2024
.

We consider the general quadratic Hamiltonian in Eq. (
78
) and
the gain-loss jump operators in
Eq. (
249
). The single-particle density
matrix obeys

\frac{\mathrm{d}D(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\mathrm{i}\left[ZD(t)-D(t)Z^{\dagger}\right]+2M_{+},

(266)

with

Z=H-\mathrm{i}M,\qquad M=M_{-}+M_{+}.

(267)

The solution to Eq. (
266
) with initial condition
D_{0}
at
t=0

is

D(t)=Q(t)D_{0}Q(t)^{\dagger}+R(t),

(268)

where

Q(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}Zt}.

(269)

To specify
R(t)
, write
Z=V\Lambda V^{-1}
, with

\Lambda=\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(\lambda_{1},\dotsc,\lambda_{L})
. Define the elementwise or
Hadamard product
A\circ B
via

\left(A\circ B\right)_{l,l^{\prime}}=A_{l,l^{\prime}}B_{l,l^{\prime}}.

(270)

Then

R(t)=-2\mathrm{i}\,V\left[\left(V^{-1}M_{+}V^{-\dagger}\right)\circ K(t)\right]V^{\dagger},

(271)

where

K_{l,l^{\prime}}(t)=\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\left(\lambda_{l}-\lambda_{l^{\prime}}^{*}\right)t}}{\lambda_{l}-\lambda_{l^{\prime}}^{*}}.

(272)

As noted above, our numerical implementation uses
Eq. (
268
) to evolve the single-particle density
matrix over the second substep, from the beginning time
t
to

t+\left(1-\eta\right)\Delta t
.

C.3
No-jump evolution

It remains to derive Eq. (
264
). For the jump operators
in Eq. (
249
), the effective
Hamiltonian (
3
) becomes

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\hat{H}-\mathrm{i}\left[\hat{H}^{\prime}+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(M_{+})\right].

(273)

where

\hat{H}^{\prime}=-\sum_{l,l^{\prime}=1}^{L}\hat{\psi}_{l}^{\dagger}\Delta M_{l,l^{\prime}}\hat{\psi}_{l^{\prime}},\qquad\Delta M=M_{+}-M_{-}.

(274)

For local gain and loss, this expression reduces to

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\hat{H}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\left(\Delta\gamma\hat{N}-L\gamma_{+}\right).

(275)

Up to first order in
\Delta t
, the no-jump Kraus operator in
Eq. (
68
) can therefore be written as

\hat{K}_{1,0}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\eta\hat{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta t}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\eta\hat{H}\Delta t}\mathrm{e}^{-\eta\hat{H}^{\prime}\Delta t}\mathrm{e}^{-\eta\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(M_{+})\Delta t}.

(276)

The final exponential drops out due to the normalization in
Eq. (
263
) and may hence be omitted. The remaining two
factors generate unitary and non-unitary evolution, respectively. We define

\hat{U}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{H}t},\qquad\hat{V}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\hat{H}^{\prime}t}=\mathrm{e}^{\Delta\gamma\hat{N}t/2}=\hat{V}^{\dagger}(t),

(277)

so that

\hat{K}_{1,0}=\hat{U}(\eta\Delta t)\hat{V}(\eta\Delta t).

(278)

The evolution in Eq. (
263
) then factorizes as

\hat{\rho}(t+\eta\Delta t)=\hat{U}(\eta\Delta t)\frac{\hat{V}(\eta\Delta t)\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{V}(\eta\Delta t)}{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left[\hat{V}(\eta\Delta t)\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{V}(\eta\Delta t)\right]}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(\eta\Delta t).

(279)

We first analyze the evolution generated by
\hat{V}(t)
. Consider the general
expression

\hat{\rho}(t)=\frac{\hat{V}(t)\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{V}(t)}{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left[\hat{V}(t)\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{V}(t)\right]},

(280)

which reduces to the form required in
Eq. (
279
) when we set

t=\eta\Delta t
and
\hat{\rho}_{0}=\hat{\rho}(t)
. Differentiating with
respect to
t
yields

\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\rho}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\left\{\hat{H}^{\prime},\hat{\rho}\right\}+2\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\left(\hat{H}^{\prime}\hat{\rho}\right)\hat{\rho}.

(281)

This equation can be rewritten as an evolution equation for the single-particle
density matrix (
168
). Since
\hat{\rho}(t)

in Eq. (
280
) is a normalized product of Gaussian
operators, it remains a normalized Gaussian state. Wick’s theorem therefore
applies, leading to

\frac{\mathrm{d}D}{\mathrm{d}t}=\left\{\Delta M,D\right\}-2D\Delta MD=\Delta\gamma\left(1-D\right)D.

(282)

To solve this equation, we assume that
D
is invertible and introduce the
matrix

Y=D^{-1}-1,\qquad D=\left(1+Y\right)^{-1}.

(283)

Using

\frac{\mathrm{d}D^{-1}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-D^{-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}D}{\mathrm{d}t}D^{-1},

(284)

we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}Y}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\mathrm{d}D^{-1}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\left\{\Delta M,Y\right\}.

(285)

The solution of this equation reads

Y(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta Mt}Y_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta Mt},

(286)

which yields

\begin{split}D(t)&=\left[1+\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta Mt}\left(D_{0}^{-1}-1\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta Mt}\right]^{-1}\\
&=\left[D_{0}+\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta\gamma t}\left(1-D_{0}\right)\right]^{-1}D_{0}.\end{split}

(287)

In Eq. (
279
), we require

t=\eta\Delta t
and
D_{0}=D(t)
. Finally, also the unitary evolution in
Eq. (
279
) can be reformulated in terms
of the single-particle density matrix using the approach of
Sec.
C.2
. We thus obtain
Eq. (
264
).
