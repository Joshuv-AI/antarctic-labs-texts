Thermal Bootstrap of Large- 𝑵 N Matrix Models via Conic Optimization
Sophia M. Adams

Abstract
This paper is aimed at improving thermal bootstrapping methods for matrix quantum mechanics and quantum field theory. The thermal energies of the large- N N one-matrix anharmonic oscillator and large- N N two-matrix anharmonic oscillator were bounded without logarithmic relaxation using the Quantum Information Conic Solver. For the one-matrix anharmonic oscillator, dual to a theory of “long strings”, stricter bounds yielded a value of the first long string excited energy within 0.001 % 0.001\% of the physical value and the first estimation from symmetry and self-consistency equations alone of the first long string coupling coefficient.



1
Background

Bootstrapping is a method of solving a theory by specifying conditions that the theory must satisfy, such as self-consistency equations and symmetries. Solving a theory with bootstrapping can be advantageous because it can produce tight, rigorous bounds on observables without much computational cost. In particular, matrix quantum mechanics (MQM) is an important bootstrapping target because some MQM theories may not be analytically solvable or reliably solvable through numerical or perturbative methods but may contain many symmetries. Additionally, MQM provides simplified, nonperturbative models that capture essential features of quantum gravity and black hole dynamics, such as black hole entropy in the BFSS matrix model (Ref.
PhysRevD.55.5112
).

In the thermal bootstrap, the main object of study is the thermal density matrix, constrained by symmetries and by the Kubo–Martin–Schwinger (KMS) condition that characterizes thermal equilibrium (Refs.
ArakiSewell1977
,
Sewell1977
). Bootstrapping a thermal state involves determining which combinations of correlators and expectation values are consistent with these constraints. In this work, the thermal energy is bootstrapped for the one-matrix and two-matrix anharmonic oscillator. From the one-matrix model, we estimate the long string ground state energy, the first long string excited state energy, and the first long string coupling coefficient, which characterize the structure of the thermal spectrum and the interaction strength in the matrix model’s dual long string description.

Previous thermal bootstrap studies have primarily used linear semidefinite programming (SDP) methods, which approximate nonlinear physical constraints by linear relaxations. While rigorous, such methods can fail to enforce nonlinear relations like the KMS condition exactly. To overcome this limitation, we explore the use of the Quantum Information Conic Solver (QICS) (Ref.
he2024qicsquantuminformationconic
), a nonlinear SDP solver designed to handle quantum conic constraints natively. The idea behind QICS is to extend the feasible space of SDPs to include nonlinear cone structures that naturally encode positivity and consistency conditions of quantum states. This allows us to impose nonlinear constraints directly, rather than approximating them through relaxations. In particular, QICS uses the Skajaa-Ye algorithm (Ref.
SkajaaYe2015
) to find the feasible space of a quantum relative entropy cone (which happens to correspond exactly to the form of the KMS constraint).

In this work, we take steps toward improving thermal bootstrapping methods for MQM by applying QICS to bootstrap the one-matrix and two-matrix anharmonic oscillators, achieving larger system sizes and tighter bounds than previously obtained through linear methods (Ref.
cho2025thermalbootstrapmatrixquantum
).

2
Models

In this paper, we are interested in the one-matrix and two-matrix anharmonic oscillator in the ’t Hooft large-
N
limit. For each bootstrap problem, we consider operators built as products of elementary matrices
X,P
(and other matrices in the two-matrix model). The length
L
is the maximum number of operators appearing in a single product. Increasing
L
allows longer operator products in the bootstrap and generally improves the precision of the resulting bounds.

2.1
One-Matrix

In one-matrix quantum mechanics, the quartic anharmonic oscillator Hamiltonian takes the form

H=\operatorname{Tr}(\frac{1}{2}P^{2}+\frac{1}{2}X^{2}+\frac{g}{N}X^{4})

(1)

where the canonical coordinate
X
and its conjugate momentum
P
are traceless
N\times N
Hermitian matrices. The gauged theory or “singlet sector” can be reformulated in terms of non-interacting fermions, while the ungauged theory contains “non-singlet sectors” dual to a theory of “long strings” (Refs.
Maldacena2005LongStrings
and
Balthazar_2019
) and is not known to be analytically solvable. One can derive bounds on the energy spectrum, which characterize the energy spectrum of long strings and long string interactions in the adjoint sector, by bootstrapping the ungauged theory at finite temperature where

E(\beta)\equiv\langle H\rangle_{\beta}\equiv\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}}(\rho_{\beta}H)=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}}(\frac{1}{Z(\beta)}e^{-\beta H}H)=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}}(\frac{1}{\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}}\left(e^{-\beta H}\right)}e^{-\beta H}H).

(2)

Due to large-
N
factorization,

\langle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{O}_{1})...\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{O}_{1})\rangle_{\beta}=\langle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{O}_{1})\rangle_{\beta}...\langle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{O}_{1})\rangle_{\beta},

(3)

so we only need to consider single-trace operators. Note that each trace is to be regarded as order
N
, and
X
and
P
are rescaled by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}
, allowing us to omit the
\frac{1}{N}
multiplying
X^{4}
in the Hamiltonian. As in Ref.
cho2025thermalbootstrapmatrixquantum
, we express the components of
X
and
P
as
X_{ab}=X^{A}T_{ab}^{A}
and
P_{ab}=P^{A}T_{ab}^{A}
where
T_{ab}^{A}
are the traceless Hermitian
SU(N)
generators satisfying the completeless relation
T_{ab}^{A}T_{cd}^{A}=\delta_{ad}\delta_{bc}-\frac{1}{N}\delta_{ab}\delta_{cd}
so that the canonical commutation relation
\frac{1}{N}[X^{A},P^{B}]=\frac{i}{N}\delta^{AB}
is equivalent to

\frac{1}{N}[X_{ab},P_{cd}]=\frac{i}{N}\delta_{ad}\delta_{bc}+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{N^{2}}).

(4)

We consider the same large-
N
simplifications as in Ref.
cho2025thermalbootstrapmatrixquantum
, including treating any remaining trace products as independent variables. We find bounds on the thermal energy by bootstrapping the system of operators
\mathcal{O}_{i}
in tuples of
X
and
P
up to maximum length
\frac{L}{2}
(e.g. for
L=6
,
O_{i}\in\{1,X,P,XP,PX,XXX,XXP,XPX,PXX,XPP,PXP,PPX,

PPP\}
) subject to the following constraints:

•

Semidefinite positivity of the moment matrix
\mathcal{M}_{ij}=\langle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{O}_{i}^{\dagger}\mathcal{O}_{j})\rangle_{\beta}

•

Parity symmetry,
\langle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{1}\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{2}\cdots\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{n})\rangle_{\beta}=0
with
n
odd and
\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{i}\in\{X,P\}

•

Time-reversal symmetry,
\operatorname{Re}(\langle\operatorname{Tr}(P_{1}P_{2}\cdots P_{n})\rangle_{\beta})=\operatorname{Im}(\langle\operatorname{Tr}(P_{1}P_{2}\cdots P_{m})\rangle_{\beta})=0
with
n
odd and
m
even

•

Reality conditions,
\langle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{O}_{i}^{\dagger})\rangle_{\beta}=\langle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{O}_{i})\rangle_{\beta}^{*}

•

Stationary state conditions,
\langle[H,\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{O}_{i})]\rangle_{\beta}=0

•

Cyclicity of the trace with large-
N
factorization,

\langle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{1}\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{2}\cdots\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{n})\rangle_{\beta}=\langle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{2}\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{3}\cdots\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{n}\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{1})\rangle_{\beta}

+\sum_{k=2}^{n}\langle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{2}\cdots\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{k-1})\rangle_{\beta}\langle\operatorname{Tr}([\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{1},\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{k}])\rangle_{\beta}\langle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{k+1}\cdots\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{n})\rangle_{\beta}

with
\mathcal{O^{\prime}}_{i}\in\{X,P\}

•

Normalization,
\frac{1}{N}\langle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathds{1})\rangle_{\beta}=1

•

Large-
N
commutation relations (Eq. (
4
))

•

KMS condition on a thermal state (Section
3
)

2.2
Two-Matrix

The two-matrix quartic anharmonic oscillator is described by the Hamiltonian

H=\operatorname{Tr}(\frac{1}{2}(P_{1}^{2}+P_{2}^{2})+\frac{1}{2}(X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2})-\frac{g}{N}[X_{1},X_{2}]^{2})

(5)

where
P_{1}
is the conjugate momentum of
X_{1}
and
P_{2}
,
X_{2}
. Bootstrapping the two-matrix model is ultimately motivated by the BFSS matrix model, conjectured to be dual to M-theory in the large-
N
limit
PhysRevD.55.5112
. Due to the computational cost associated with bootstrapping a large number of matrices and the fact that the thermal state is not supersymmetric, bootstrapping the full BFSS model, which has 9 bosonic and 16 fermionic matrices, is a much more nontrivial task using this method. Instead, we consider the bosonic BFSS model, dual to a theory of a black D0-brane in the large-
N
limit, truncated to two matrices. As an additional simplification, we include the mass term
\frac{1}{2}(X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2})
.

The two-matrix system made from tuples of
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
,
P_{1}
, and
P_{2}
is subject to the same constraints as the one-matrix model with an additional constraint that comes from swapping the
1
and
2
operator labels:
X_{1}\leftrightarrow X_{2}
,
P_{1}\leftrightarrow P_{2}
. Reformulating the model in terms of complex operators

P=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(P_{1}+iP_{2}),\quad\bar{P}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(P_{1}-iP_{2}),\quad X=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(X_{1}+iX_{2}),\quad\bar{X}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(X_{1}-iX_{2})

(6)

as in Ref.
lin2025highprecisionbootstrapmultimatrixquantum
allows us to view the
SO(2)
symmetry as a
U(1)
symmetry and make manifest additional constraints from
U(1)
charge conservation. In terms of complex operators, the Hamiltonian can be written

H=\operatorname{Tr}(P\bar{P}+X\bar{X}+\frac{g}{N}[X,\bar{X}]^{2})

(7)

since the system is invariant under the relabeling of barred and unbarred operators.

In practice, the
U(1)
charge of a term is the difference between the number of barred and unbarred operators, and charge conservation implies that any observable with a nonzero
U(1)
charge is zero. In this basis, we can consider the same constraints as in the real basis but can replace the parity constraint with the stronger
U(1)
charge constraint. Additionally, the time-reversal and operator relabeling constraints can be reinterpreted in the new basis as follows. We will refer to the original time-reversal transformation,

P_{1}\rightarrow-P_{1},\quad P_{2}\rightarrow-P_{2},\quad i\rightarrow-i,

(8)

as
T
and the new time-reversal-like transformation,

P\rightarrow-P,\quad\bar{P}\rightarrow-\bar{P},\quad i\rightarrow-i,

(9)

as
T^{\prime}
. Just as
T
implied constraints on the real and imaginary parts of expectation values of
X_{1},X_{2},P_{1},P_{2}
operators,
T^{\prime}
now implies these constraints on the expectation values of the complex operators. Namely, any observable with an even number of
P
and
\bar{P}
’s is purely real, while any observable with an odd number of
P
and
\bar{P}
’s is purely imaginary. This constraint significantly reduces the number of variables. However, the original
T
constraint reinterpreted in the new basis is still needed in order to obtain meaningful bounds on the energy. In terms of the complex operators,
T
becomes the transformation

X\rightarrow\bar{X},\quad\bar{X}\rightarrow X,\quad P\rightarrow-\bar{P},\quad\bar{P}\rightarrow-P,\quad i\rightarrow-i.

(10)

The relabeling constraint becomes a relabeling of barred and unbarred operators; however, in practice, we leave this constraint out since it neither improves the bounds nor reduces the number of free variables. In fact,
T^{\prime}
is a composition of this relabeling and
T
. The original relabeling constraint turns out to be equivalent to the new relabeling constraint when
U(1)
charge conservation is imposed.

3
Thermal Constraints

At finite temperature, the bootstrap problem can be formulated as a conic optimization problem where constraints take the form

\mathcal{M}\succeq 0,

(11)

representing a semidefinite cone and

\beta C\succeq A^{1/2}\log(A^{1/2}B^{-1}A^{1/2})A^{1/2},

(12)

representing an operator relative entropy cone for matrices

A_{ij}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}(\langle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{O}_{i}^{\dagger}\mathcal{O}_{j})\rangle_{\beta}-\frac{1}{N}\langle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{O}_{i}^{\dagger})\rangle_{\beta}\langle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{O}_{j})\rangle_{\beta}),

(13)

B_{ij}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}(\langle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{O}_{j}\mathcal{O}_{i}^{\dagger})\rangle_{\beta}-\frac{1}{N}\langle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{O}_{j})\rangle_{\beta}\langle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{O}_{i}^{\dagger})\rangle_{\beta}),

(14)

and

C=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\langle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{O}_{i}^{\dagger}[H,\mathcal{O}_{j}])\rangle_{\beta}

(15)

constructed from products of tuples of maximum length
\frac{L-4}{2}
(Ref.
cho2025thermalbootstrapmatrixquantum
). The operator relative entropy conic condition is equivalent (Refs.
ArakiSewell1977
,
Sewell1977
, and
Fawzi_2024
) to the Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) condition on a thermal state and is replaced by energy constraint equations,
\langle H\mathcal{O}_{i}\rangle=E\langle\mathcal{O}_{i}\rangle
, at ground state. While the ground state energy can be readily bounded using semidefinite programming, the KMS constraint is nonlinear and so requires semidefinite relaxation to turn it into a semidefinite programming problem. This is the approach taken in Ref.
cho2025thermalbootstrapmatrixquantum
and solves the thermal bootstrapping problem by relaxing the nonlinear operator relative entropy cone into a linear positive semidefinite cone by approximating the
\log
:

\log(x)\approx r_{m,k}(x):=2^{k}r_{m}\left(x^{1/2^{k}}\right),

(16)

where
m,k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,

r_{m}(x)\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}f_{t_{j}}(x),\qquad f_{t}(x)\equiv\frac{x-1}{t(x-1)+1},

(17)

and
t_{j}\in[0,1]
and
w_{j}>0
are the nodes and weights of the
m
-point Gauss–Radau quadrature, chosen so that

\int_{0}^{1}p(t)\,dt=\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}p(t_{j})

(18)

holds for any polynomial
p(t)
of degree
2m-2
, with
t_{1}=0.6
. The functions
r_{m,k}(x)
provide successively tighter rigorous upper bounds on
\log(x)
with increasing
(m,k)
(Ref.
cho2025thermalbootstrapmatrixquantum
). Alternatively, the operator relative entropy cone can be handled exactly using the Skajaa-Ye algorithm, as is implemented by the Quantum Information Conic Solver (QICS) (Ref.
he2024qicsquantuminformationconic
).

) with MOSEK. We also derive stricter bounds than what was previously achieved in Ref.

4.1.1
Comparison to the dual long string model

To extract energy eigenvalues, we compare the model to its dual long string description.

As in Ref.
cho2025thermalbootstrapmatrixquantum
, we consider the semiclassically quantized phase space defined by

J(e)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\lambda_{-}(e)}^{\lambda_{+}(e)}d\lambda\sqrt{2(e-v(\lambda))}

(19)

where

v(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}+g\lambda^{4}

(20)

and
\lambda_{\pm}(e)
are the turning points of
v(\lambda)
. The Fermi energy
e_{f}
is defined as the solution to
J(e_{f})=1
, and the ground state eigenvalue distribution is given by

\rho(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{2(e_{f}-v(\lambda))}.

(21)

Then in the adjoint sector at large-
N
the energies
\Delta_{n}
relative to the ground state and their eigenvectors
w_{n}(\lambda)
can be found from the following singular integral eigenvalue equation

\int_{\lambda_{-}(e)}^{\lambda_{+}(e)}d\lambda^{\prime}\rho(\lambda^{\prime})\frac{w_{n}(\lambda)-w_{n}(\lambda^{\prime})}{(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})^{2}}=\Delta_{n}w_{n}(\lambda)

(22)

with orthogonality
\int_{\lambda_{-}(e)}^{\lambda_{+}(e)}d\lambda\rho(\lambda)w_{n}(\lambda)=0
and normalization
\int_{\lambda_{-}(e)}^{\lambda_{+}(e)}d\lambda\rho(\lambda)|w_{n}(\lambda)|^{2}=0
conditions.

The numerical values of the energy eigenvalues were computed in Ref.
Marchesini:1978ud
and are listed below for
g=2
:

e_{0}=0.8654577

\Delta_{1}=2.1281936

\Delta_{2}=4.6201131

\Delta_{3}=7.0716122

\Delta_{4}=9.5258038.

As in Ref.
cho2025thermalbootstrapmatrixquantum
, the long string thermal partition function to 2-loop order in the planar limit is given by

\frac{-\operatorname{log}Z(\beta)}{N^{2}}=\sum_{n}\operatorname{log}(1-e^{-\beta\Delta_{n}})+\beta\sum_{j,k}\frac{h_{jkjk}}{(e^{\beta\Delta_{j}}-1)(e^{\beta\Delta_{k}}-1)}+\mathcal{O}(h^{2})

(23)

where

h_{abcd}=-\int_{\lambda_{-}(e)}^{\lambda_{+}(e)}d\lambda d\lambda^{\prime}\rho(\lambda)\rho(\lambda^{\prime})\frac{w_{c}^{*}(\lambda)w_{d}^{*}(\lambda^{\prime})(w_{a}(\lambda)-w_{a}(\lambda^{\prime}))(w_{b}(\lambda)-w_{b}(\lambda^{\prime}))}{(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})^{2}}

(24)

are coupling coefficients.

To calculate the long string coupling coefficients
h_{abcd}
, we numerically compute the eigenvectors
w_{n}(\lambda)
and evaluate Eq. (
24
) by approximating the integral as a discrete sum.

To evaluate Eq. (
19
) numerically and solve for the Fermi energy
e_{f}
, we use Mathematica’s NIntegrate and the closed form solution for the turning points.

We solve the integral eigenvalue equation (Eq. (
22
)), which has the form of a Fredholm integral equation of the second kind,

\int_{-\lambda_{\max}}^{\lambda_{\max}}K(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})\,w_{n}(\lambda^{\prime})\,d\lambda^{\prime}=\Delta_{n}\,w_{n}(\lambda),

(25)

using the Nyström method (Ref.
Atkinson1997
) with Gaussian quadrature.
Eq. (
25
) is approximated as a weighted sum over
Q
quadrature nodes
\lambda_{i}
with weights
\omega_{i}^{\text{quad}}
(with both nodes and weights supplied by Gaussian quadrature) so that it becomes a discrete matrix equation:

\sum_{j=1}^{Q}\omega_{j}^{\text{quad}}\,K(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j})\,w_{n}(\lambda_{j})=\Delta_{n}\,w_{n}(\lambda_{i}).

(26)

Since the kernel is symmetric,
K(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})=K(\lambda^{\prime},\lambda)
,
we use the Hermitian form

M_{ij}=\sqrt{\omega_{i}^{\text{quad}}\omega_{j}^{\text{quad}}}\,K(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}),\qquad M\,\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n}=\Delta_{n}\,\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n},

(27)

where
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{n}=\bigl(\sqrt{\omega_{1}^{\text{quad}}}\,w_{n}(\lambda_{1}),\sqrt{\omega_{2}^{\text{quad}}}\,w_{n}(\lambda_{2}),\ldots,\sqrt{\omega_{Q}^{\text{quad}}}\,w_{n}(\lambda_{Q})\bigr)^{\!\top}
.
To regularize the kernel’s short-distance singularity,
the diagonal elements
M_{ii}
are set to zero.
This yields a finite-dimensional symmetric matrix
M
whose discrete spectrum
converges to the eigenvalues
\Delta_{n}
as the number of quadrature points
Q

5
Discussion

A major roadblock in bootstrapping is numerical instability, which can occur when correlator coefficients differ from each other by a large factor. The precise origin of numerical instability is not well understood but often results in solvers struggling to find the feasible region and returning unphysical or no results. Although QICS enforces a stricter version of the thermal (KMS) condition than linear SDP methods and stricter constraints can improve numerical instability, its implementation in double precision ultimately limits the achievable accuracy for larger spaces. While QICS is able to produce rigorous bounds for the one-matrix bootstrap for system size up to at least
L=12
, it encounters numerical instability at
L=6
in the two-matrix bootstrap. Due to the rapid growth of the operator basis, the two-matrix bootstrap is a substantially more demanding problem and would likely benefit from the use of an arbitrary precision solver. Therefore, a method of solving the bootstrap that balances both precision and nonlinear constraints is still desired.

Beyond numerical issues, the results demonstrate that nonlinear conic methods can extend thermal bootstrapping beyond the reach of conventional linear relaxations. In the one-matrix case, QICS successfully reproduces and sharpens previous energy bounds, indicating that the nonlinear implementation of the KMS constraint meaningfully strengthens consistency conditions. For the two-matrix model, imposing additional symmetries, such as
U(1)
charge conservation, reduces the dimensionality of the constraint space enough to obtain bounds for system size
L=6
. Such improvements could eventually enable the extraction of higher long string excitations and couplings as well as observables in matrix models dual to theories of black holes.
