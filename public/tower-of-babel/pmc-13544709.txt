A 20-cm redundant vas deferens loop during late orchidopexy: aberrant ductal anatomy as an intraoperative pitfall in a 17-year-old male
Authors: Youssef Maachi, Zinedine Omar, Amine Slaoui, Tariq Karmouni, Abdelatif Koutani, Khalid Elkhader
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Cryptorchidism is one of the most common congenital anomalies of the male genitourinary tract. Anatomical variations of the spermatic cord structures, though underreported, may increase the risk of iatrogenic injury during orchidopexy.Case presentation:A 17-year-old male presented with lifelong unilateral cryptorchidism. Preoperative ultrasonography localized the testis in a high inguinal position with preserved vascular flow. Given the patient’s informed refusal of orchiectomy despite counseling on malignancy risk, elective orchidopexy was performed. During surgery, an unusually long, redundant vas deferens loop measuring approximately 20 cm was identified. Through systematic proximal-to-distal tracing of the vas deferens and meticulous mobilization of the entire redundant segment, the structure was fully preserved. The testis was fixed in a tension-free subdartos pouch. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, with confirmed scrotal position at the 6-month follow-up.Conclusion:This case highlights a rare but surgically significant anatomical variant that may simulate a fibrous band or a duplicated structure and may be inadvertently divided during orchidopexy. Full identification of the cords’ contents before dissection of the cords is critical to avoid obstructive azoospermia. Surgeons should maintain heightened vigilance for ductal anomalies, particularly in older patients with prolonged cryptorchidism, in whom anatomical distortion is more likely.

Introduction

Cryptorchidism, defined as the failure of one or both testes to descend into the scrotum by three months of age, is one of the most common anomalies of the male genitourinary system, affecting 1–4% of full-term male infants at birth; however, fewer than 1% still have an undescended testis beyond the first year of life[1]. Testes that remain undescended beyond 6 months of age rarely descend spontaneously and generally require surgical correction, with orchidopexy recommended between 6 and 18 months of age[2,3]. If left untreated, cryptorchidism carries risks of testicular malignancy, torsion, hernias, and subfertility[4]. In developing countries, however, presentation in adulthood remains common. The vas deferens is of particular surgical importance during orchidopexy, as inadvertent injury results in ipsilateral obstructive azoospermia. While orchidopexy is generally considered routine, anatomical variations of the spermatic cord can present unexpected intraoperative challenges[5]. This case report describes an unusual ductal variant and discusses the surgical principles necessary to prevent iatrogenic injury. It has been reported in accordance with the SCARE 2025 guidelines[6].

Case presentation

A 17-year-old male student presented with lifelong unilateral cryptorchidism. Surgical treatment had not been pursued during childhood due to limited access to pediatric surgical care, a pattern consistent with delayed presentation in resource-limited settings. He had no significant past medical or surgical history and no family history of cryptorchidism or genitourinary anomalies. Secondary sexual characteristics were normally developed. Physical examination revealed an empty hemiscrotum with no palpable testis in the inguinal region. Preoperative ultrasonography confirmed a viable testis in a high inguinal position with normal echotexture and preserved vascular flow (Fig. 1). Testicular volume measured 12 mL on the affected side and 20 mL on the contralateral side, confirming mild volume asymmetry consistent with prolonged maldescent and establishing the diagnosis of canalicular cryptorchidism. After extensive, informed counseling – including discussion of orchiectomy given the increased malignancy risk and limited fertility benefit of orchidopexy in postpubertal males – he expressed a clear preference for testicular preservation for psychological, cosmetic, and fertility-related reasons. Elective orchidopexy was performed under general anesthesia. An inguinal incision was made, the external oblique aponeurosis was incised, and the ilioinguinal nerve was identified and preserved. A patent processus vaginalis extending to the internal inguinal ring was identified and ligated at that level. The procedure was performed without optical magnification; total operative time was approximately 45 minutes. During systematic cord dissection, an unusually long redundant vas deferens loop measuring approximately 20 cm was identified (Fig. 2), representing a potential pitfall for inadvertent transection. Upon identifying this anomaly, the standard dissection sequence was suspended – no tissue division was performed until the entire course of the vas deferens had been traced and confirmed. The vas deferens was traced proximally toward the internal inguinal ring and distally toward the epididymal tail; a single continuous vas deferens was confirmed throughout, excluding duplication or accessory ductal structures. The redundant loop was fully mobilized with meticulous attention to vascular preservation. Given the postpubertal age and prolonged maldescent, the deferential artery was considered the primary collateral source of testicular perfusion; no vascular compromise was observed intraoperatively. The testis was placed in a subdartos pouch via a separate scrotal incision in a tension-free position, and the wound was closed in layers. Recovery was uneventful, with discharge on postoperative day 1. At 6-month follow-up, the testis was well positioned, with acceptable cosmetic results. Instructions were given for monthly self-examination and annual ultrasonographic surveillance, given the oncological risk. No complications were recorded; the outcome was classified as Clavien-Dindo grade 0. The clinical course is summarized in Figure 3. Discussion

Anatomical considerations

Ductal and epididymal anomalies in cryptorchidism are far more common than generally appreciated, occurring in 36–60% of cases depending on testicular position – yet they remain underrecognized intraoperatively. The 20 cm vas deferens loop encountered here exemplifies this risk: a surgically significant variant that may be mistaken for fibrous tissue and inadvertently transected, with potentially irreversible consequences for the patient’s reproductive system. The higher the testicular position, the greater the likelihood of such anomalies, and the greater the imperative for systematic anatomical identification before any tissue division. The prevalence of epididymal and ductal anomalies in cryptorchidism is strongly position-dependent: rates range from approximately 36% in inguinally located testes to over 60% in intra-abdominal positions, with testis-epididymis dissociation as the most common finding[7]. The high inguinal position in this case is consistent with this gradient, and the observed loop likely reflects a broader embryological disruption of paratesticular development.

The embryological basis of looping variants can be understood within the framework of testicular descent. The testis and epididymis originate from distinct structures – the genital ridge and the mesonephric (Wolffian) duct, respectively – and their union requires precise spatiotemporal coordination[5]. When gubernacular attachment occurs anomalously to the vas deferens rather than to the epididymal tail, descending traction acts on the vas, resulting in disproportionate elongation and looping, while the testis remains proximally arrested[5,7]. Experimental rodent data support this model, demonstrating that abnormal gubernacular-epididymal attachment reproducibly produces proximal vas elongation and cryptorchidism[5]. The 20 cm loop in this case likely represents an extreme expression of this mechanism, compounded by 17 years of somatic growth – a factor absent in the infant cases that constitute the majority of the published literature.

Management considerations in postpubertal cryptorchidism

Management of postpubertal cryptorchidism remains debated. While orchiectomy is guideline-endorsed when the testis is hypoplastic and the contralateral side is normal[4,8], the AUA classifies this as a Clinical Principle rather than a Standard, reflecting the importance of individualized decision-making[8]. Orchidopexy remains a defensible alternative when the testis demonstrates preserved volume and vascular flow on preoperative imaging.

Regarding spermatogenic function, prolonged cryptorchidism beyond puberty is associated with severe impairment of germ cell maturation, with histological studies documenting Sertoli-cell-only or maturation arrest patterns in a significant proportion of postpubertal cases[9]. In the present case, semen analysis was not performed; meaningful spermatogenesis was considered unlikely given the age and duration of maldescent. However, a near-normal testicular volume suggests preserved Leydig cell mass, and orchidopexy may confer endocrinological benefit by maintaining endogenous testosterone production[9] – a justification that, alongside the patient’s psychological and cosmetic preferences, supported the decision to proceed. Preserving vas deferens integrity remains essential regardless of spermatogenic potential, as iatrogenic vasectomy would compound an already compromised reproductive system[10].

The oncological risk warrants specific attention in this context. The relative risk of testicular germ cell tumor is estimated to be 2.75–8 times that of the general population, highest among those with high-inguinal or intra-abdominal positions and delayed correction[4]. Intraoperative biopsy to exclude germ cell neoplasia in situ (GCNIS) is guideline-recommended in postpubertal males undergoing orchidopexy[4]; it was not performed in this case, representing a recognized limitation. Beyond its oncological role, intraoperative biopsy would also have provided definitive histological assessment of testicular function – specifically the presence or absence of spermatogonia and Leydig cell integrity – information that cannot be inferred from volume or vascular flow alone. Its absence, therefore, limits both the oncological and functional characterization of this case. Annual ultrasonographic surveillance was instituted as an alternative monitoring strategy.

Clinical significance and surgical implications

The primary surgical risk of this variant is inadvertent division of the redundant loop, mistaken for cremasteric fibers, gubernacular attachments, or fibrous tissue during mobilization. Unilateral vas deferens injury may significantly impair reproductive outcomes in patients with concurrent contralateral anomalies[7,10], and even in postpubertal males with compromised spermatogenesis, preserving vasal integrity remains essential to avoid compounding an already impaired reproductive system with iatrogenic obstructive azoospermia[9,10].

First, at 17 years of age, this is the oldest patient in whom a looping vas deferens has been documented during orchidopexy – a context in which oncological risk and fertility concerns make anatomical vigilance particularly consequential. Second, the approximately 20 cm loop exceeds the length reported in any prior open inguinal case. Third, in this postpubertal male, the deferential artery represents the primary – and likely sole – collateral source of testicular perfusion, rendering inadvertent vas injury not merely a fertility risk but a threat to testicular viability itself. Surgical technique and principles

Successful management relied on four operative principles. First, all spermatic cord structures were systematically identified before any tissue division, with each traced along its full length prior to any surgical decision. Second, the vas deferens was sought early and traced both proximally toward the internal inguinal ring and distally toward the epididymal tail, allowing recognition of the redundant loop before any risk of injury arose. Third, adequate surgical time was allocated, permitting unhurried dissection rather than expedient management of what might appear to be a routine procedure. Fourth, vascular preservation was prioritized. Testicular blood supply derives from three sources: the internal spermatic artery, the deferential artery, and the cremasteric artery[15]. In high inguinal testes with prolonged maldescent, the cremasteric contribution is reduced, rendering the deferential artery the primary collateral – making any dissection below the lower pole of the testis a risk for testicular atrophy, even without vas transection. Gubernacular-sparing dissection and avoidance of energy devices near the vasal loop are therefore strongly recommended[15].

Our case adds to the literature reported by Hester and Kogan[5] and Cundy et al[15] by documenting the largest and latest-presenting looping vas variant in open inguinal orchidopexy, extending these principles to the postpubertal age group.

Study limitations

This case report carries inherent limitations: semen analysis was not performed; intraoperative biopsy to exclude GCNIS was omitted – representing a deviation from current guideline recommendations – and generalization from a single case is not possible.

Strengths

The case, substantiated by intraoperative photographic evidence and precise loop measurement, addresses the underreported dilemma of orchidopexy versus orchiectomy in postpubertal males.

Conclusion

This case documents a 20 cm redundant vas deferens loop encountered during late orchidopexy in a 17-year-old male – the largest and oldest-presenting case in the open inguinal literature. Complete proximal-to-distal identification of all cord structures before any tissue division allowed safe preservation of the vas and avoidance of obstructive azoospermia. Surgeons must anticipate ductal anomalies, identify them before dividing, and prioritize precision over speed – a level of vigilance that is as essential as technical execution.
