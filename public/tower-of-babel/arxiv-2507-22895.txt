Brain motor intention Extraction Amplifier: Non-invasive brain-muscle interface
Ye Sun, Bowei Zhao, Dezhong Yao, Rui Zhang, Bohan Zhang, Xiaoyuan Li, Jing Wang, Mingxuan Qu, Gang Liu

Abstract
Brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) enable real-time interaction between the brain and external devices by decoding neural signals. However, existing motor-based BCI paradigms, like motor imagery BCI, face challenges with imprecise labeling in real-world use. This mismatch between EEG signals and true behavioral intentions leads to pseudo-labels, undermining decoding accuracy and system robustness. To overcome this bottleneck, this paper first proposes a novel motor intention extraction framework based on a non-invasive brain-muscle interface (BMuI)( BCI = Brain Computer ​ Interface = Brain Muscle ​ ( BMuI ) × Muscle Computer ​ Interface \text{BCI}=\frac{\text{Brain}}{\text{Computer}}\text{ Interface}=\frac{\text{Brain}}{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bcancel{\text{Muscle}}}}(\text{BMuI})\times\frac{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bcancel{\text{Muscle}}}}{\text{Computer}}\text{ Interface} ). This method simulates the neural pathway from the brain to the muscles in order to capture and enhance the weak motor intention signals originating in the brain. It then uses EMG as a high-fidelity relay medium to achieve more accurate intention recognition and transmission. To systematically validate the feasibility and effectiveness of this approach, we conducted both offline experiments (to repeatedly verify feasibility) and online experiments (to construct a real-time interactive system and evaluate its performance). The results show that BMuI is feasible, achieving a prediction accuracy of 0.8314; in the online experiment, all participants are able to successfully control the Unity virtual arm.

Traditional motor-based BCIs, such as motor imagery BCIs (MI-BCIs)

;

;

In real-world applications, however, the correspondence between EEG and intent labels is often imprecise, resulting in the issue of pseudo-labels, which adversely affects decoding accuracy and system robustness.

While such systems are capable of distinguishing between different imagined states to some extent, they are inherently constrained by the low signal-to-noise ratio of EEG and their heavy reliance on discrete labels. As a result, their outputs tend to resemble label-based predictions rather than a fine-grained reconstruction of neural intention strength
Maiseli et al. 2023
;
Vilela and
Hochberg 2020
, temporal dynamics, or spatial features. Furthermore, the pseudo-label problem exacerbates decoding errors. In complex scenarios–such as multi-degree-of-freedom robotic control, precise prosthetic manipulation, and real-time interaction with the environment–this decoding approach struggles to deliver the high fidelity and multidimensional control required. Consequently, it hinders the deeper application and broader adoption of BCIs in areas such as rehabilitative medicine, intelligent assistive technologies, and human-computer collaboration
Bockbrader et al. 2018
.

To address the limitations of conventional BCIs-such as pseudo-label noise, limited decoding accuracy, and poor real-time performance–this paper first proposes a non-invasive brain-muscle interface (BMuI). Drawing inspiration from the physiological neural pathway connecting the brain to the muscles
AP 1986
, this approach redefines the BCI architecture by explicitly incorporating muscle activity as an intermediate representation. Specifically, it decomposes the conventional brain-computer interface into two functional modules: a brain-muscle interface (EEG to EMG) and a muscle-computer interface (EMG to device). While the latter has been extensively studied and is considered technically mature, the former remains largely unexplored. To bridge this gap, this study presents the first non-invasive BMuI framework that realises continuous EEG-to-EMG decoding with high temporal resolution, effectively filling a critical void in current BCI research.

The proposed BMuI establishes a precise, one-to-one temporal alignment between cortical and muscular signals, thereby enabling robust and fine-grained extraction of motor intention. Crucially, EMG not only serves as a high-fidelity carrier of descending motor commands but also functions as a physiological amplifier-transforming weak, noise-prone EEG signals into clearer, more stable representations. By capitalizing on this amplification effect, BMuI enables clearer and more stable decoding of motor intentions, demonstrating marked improvements in transmission accuracy and real-time performance. This provides effective support for achieving natural and continuous human-machine interaction.

The contributions of this paper are as follows:

1. This paper presents, for the first time, a motor intention extraction and amplification system–a non-invasive brain-muscle interface (BMuI). Unlike conventional BCIs, which directly map brain signals to external devices, our approach introduces muscle activity as an explicit intermediate variable. Specifically, we decompose the traditional motor-based non-invasive BCI into two sequential modules: a brain-muscle interface (EEG to EMG) and a muscle-computer interface (EMG to device)(see Fig.
1
D). While muscle-computer interfaces are well established and have achieved high accuracy, the brain-to-muscle interface remains largely unexplored. The BMI proposed in this work fills this critical gap by establishing a continuous, high-resolution mapping from EEG to EMG. Both offline and online experiments demonstrate the feasibility and effectiveness of this interface, offering a novel and physiologically grounded pathway for motor intention decoding within neural interface systems.

2. The BMuI proposed in this study achieves, for the first time, the continuous extraction of motor intentions from EEG signals with high temporal resolution. Simultaneously, it establishes a one-to-one correspondence between EEG and the predicted EMG, thereby enabling precise mapping of motor intentions. This groundbreaking development effectively overcomes the limitations associated with pseudo-labels in traditional methods–which often result in reduced accuracy and limited generalisability–and provides a novel technological pathway for motor intention decoding.

3. Although the EMG predicted by the BMuI exhibits significant correlation with experimentally recorded EMG signals, discrepancies remain. To address this issue, the present paper proposes an improved proportional mapping approach: a hybrid control strategy. A convolutional neural network (CNN) is first employed to decode the predicted EMG signals in order to classify the intended movement direction. Subsequently, the muscle with the most accurate prediction and clear physiological relevance is selected for proportional activation mapping. This strategy ensures effective integration with the BMuI framework. Building upon this, we developed, for the first time, an online, real-time neural feedback interface based on the BMuI. This system is capable of accurately decoding EEG, predicting corresponding EMG, and translating the predicted EMG into control commands to drive a Unity-based virtual arm in real time, enabling elbow flexion and extension.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section 2 presents the experimental results; Section 3 discusses the significance of the findings and provides a summary; and Section 4 details the design and implementation of the proposed method.

2.1
Offline experiment

This experiment aims to validate the feasibility of a brain-computer interface (BMuI) system that simulates the neural conduction pathway from the brain to the muscles, extracting motor intentions from EEG and using EMG as a medium for amplifying this information. A total of 37 healthy volunteers (aged 19 to 24) participated in the study, including 29 males and 8 females. All participants provided informed consent prior to the experiment and received appropriate compensation after each session.

Figure 2:
Brain-muscle interface paradigm, A: The situation of the subjects at the time of collection and the distribution of electrodes in the arm; B: Distribution of electrodes for electroencephalogram and electromyography acquisition; C: Experimental paradigm.

As shown in Fig.
2
, during the experiment, participants sat upright in a chair with both hands resting naturally on the table, palms placed and secured within a measurement device. Experimental tasks were presented via a screen positioned directly in front of the participant, who was required to perform specific actions as instructed. Meanwhile, the system simultaneously recorded EEG, EMG from the upper arm, and the exertion of force at the elbow.
The operator monitored EEG, EMG, and elbow force data in real time via an independent monitoring terminal to ensure the correctness of participants’ movements and the quality of the recorded signals, thereby enhancing the validity of the experiment and the reliability of the data. Each participant was required to perform six predefined movements, with each movement repeated ten times, resulting in a total of 60 samples per participant.

Figure 3:
Comparison of model predicted values (the EMG predicted based on EEG) with actual measured values.

Subsequently, the brain-muscle interface (BMuI) system was used to decode the EEG signals, extracting the embedded motor intentions and amplifying them using EMG signals as the carrier. The EMG signals predicted by the system-representing the extracted motor intentions-were then compared with the actual EMG signals, which reflect the motor commands transmitted from the brain to the muscles, to evaluate the accuracy and effectiveness of the BMuI system.

Specifically, Spearman correlation coefficient was employed as the evaluation metric to assess the degree of monotonic consistency between the EEG-based predicted EMG signals and the ground truth EMG recordings, thereby quantifying the system’s ability to accurately extract motor intentions from EEG. Furthermore, to verify the statistical significance of the extracted motor intentions, a one-sample t-test was conducted on the correlation coefficients to determine whether they were significantly greater than zero-the baseline representing the absence of effective motor intention extraction-thus validating the feasibility and practical potential of the proposed approach.

2.2
Online Application Experiment

To evaluate the feasibility of using BMuI for real-time extraction of motor intention, an online experiment was conducted. The BMuI system continuously decoded EEG in real time to predict corresponding EMG. The predicted EMG signals were proportionally mapped and used as control commands to drive a Unity-based virtual arm, enabling real-time control of elbow flexion and extension.

Ten healthy participants were recruited. At the outset, a personalised BMuI model was constructed for each participant using data collected under the same protocol as in the offline experiment. During data acquisition, participants were seated comfortably with their hands resting naturally on a table, palms secured within a measurement device. In response to on-screen cues, they were instructed to perform two predefined movements, each repeated 60 times, resulting in 120 samples per participant. Throughout the acquisition process, EEG, EMG, and force data were synchronously recorded, while an operator continuously monitored signal quality and task performance to ensure data reliability and experimental consistency.

In the subsequent online control phase, participants wore only an EEG cap. The system captured EEG signals in real time, decoded them using the personalised BMuI model to predict corresponding EMG activity, and translated the predictions into control commands via a proportional hybrid EMG control strategy, thereby enabling the virtual arm to perform elbow flexion and extension.

The primary performance metric was the time required for participants to complete each movement using the virtual hand. This study aimed to assess the practicality and effectiveness of decoding motor intention directly from EEG signals in real time using a BMuI system.

3
Discussion

This paper proposes a novel Brain-Muscle Interface (BMuI) that redefines the architecture of brain-computer interaction by explicitly incorporating muscle activity as an intermediate representation within the BCI framework. Unlike traditional BCIs, which map electroencephalography (EEG) directly to device control, our approach decomposes the interface into two distinct modules: a brain-muscle interface (EEG-to-EMG) and a muscle-computer interface (EMG-to-device). While the latter is well-established and known for its high reliability, the brain-muscle interface remains largely unexplored. To address this gap, we propose a motor intention decoder and amplifier inspired by the neurophysiological pathway from cortex to muscle. Specifically, due to skull attenuation, non-invasively recorded EEG is typically weak and highly susceptible to noise
Castermans
et al. 2013
. In contrast, EMG, as the peripheral manifestation of central motor commands
Tun
et al. 2021
;
Yang et al. 2010
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Xi et al. 2020
, offers a higher signal-to-noise ratio and more robust temporal characteristics. Leveraging this distinction, the proposed BMuI method decodes EEG in real time to predict corresponding EMG activity, effectively amplifying and extracting motor intention, and establishing a more direct and continuous mapping between cortical sources and motor outputs.

This study encompasses two main lines of investigation:

First, we propose and implement a novel framework termed the Brain-Muscle Interface (BMuI). In contrast to conventional motor-based BCI paradigms, which directly map EEG to control commands, BMuI introduces EMG as an intermediate physiological variable. This enables the system to be logically divided into two sequential modules: the brain-muscle interface (EEG to EMG) and the muscle-computer interface (EMG to device). While muscle-computer interfaces are relatively mature, the brain-muscle interface–as a critical yet underexplored bridge between neural intent and muscular output–has received limited systematic attention. The proposed BMuI addresses this shortcoming. Its conceptual design is inspired by the frequency-matching principle employed in steady-state visual evoked potential BCIs (SSVEP-BCIs) (see Figure
6
). In the SSVEP paradigm, visual stimuli at specific frequencies elicit EEG responses of the same frequency, thereby enabling a direct mapping between stimulus labels and user intention–thus achieving a “what you see is what you think” mechanism
Vialatte et al. 2010
;
Bin et al. 2009
;
Herrmann 2001
;
Cheng et al. 2002
. BMuI extends this principle to the motor BCI domain. Rather than relying on external stimuli, it exploits the intrinsic neurophysiological pathways of motor intention: signals originating in the cortex propagate via neural pathways to elicit muscular activity. This establishes a one-to-one correspondence between EEG and EMG, enabling a “what you think is the label” mechanism. As a result, BMuI avoids the pseudo-labelling issues found in conventional motor BCIs and significantly improves decoding accuracy.

Figure 6:
Analogy between SSVEP-BCI and the brain-muscle interface.A: Th mechanism of “What you see is what you think” at SSVEP-BCI; B: The mechanism of “what you think is the label” at the brain-muscle interface

Second, we propose a control strategy based on continuous prediction of EMG. Traditional motor BCIs typically adopt discrete classification schemes, whereas BMuI employs a continuous decoding paradigm that more accurately captures the temporal evolution and amplitude dynamics of neural motor commands. Instead of inferring discrete intention classes, the system estimates the EMG trajectory in real time, supporting high temporal resolution (on the millisecond scale) and fine-grained control. This continuous decoding enables output that more closely reflects the timing and magnitude of true neural commands, thereby achieving greater fidelity and responsiveness in control. Such capabilities promote more natural, smooth, and adaptive human-machine interaction, with strong potential for applications requiring precise dynamic motor control, such as prosthetics, robotic assistance, and neurorehabilitation.

To systematically assess the effectiveness and practicality of the proposed method, both offline and online experimental paradigms were employed. In the offline setting, EEG data from 37 participants were collected to train and validate the motor intention decoding model, evaluating its feasibility and generalisability at the population level. For the online experiments, a real-time virtual arm control system was developed using the Unity engine. The decoded EEG was continuously translated into control commands, driving a virtual arm to perform elbow flexion and extension, thereby evaluating system responsiveness and control precision in dynamic interactive scenarios.

In the offline experiments, bilateral arm muscles were chosen as EMG targets to facilitate comprehensive EEG-to-EMG mapping. In contrast, the online application used only the right upper arm as the decoding target, enabling direct EEG-based control of elbow movement in the right limb. Results from the offline experiments confirmed the technical feasibility of decoding EMG from EEG, while existing neurophysiological evidence of EEG-EMG coupling
Yang et al. 2010
;
Xi et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Bortel and Sovka 2006
further validated the BMuI framework. In the online experiments, all participants successfully controlled the virtual arm, completing the instructed tasks with consistency and precision. These findings demonstrate that the proposed BMuI method is not only feasible, but also effective in decoding motor intentions from EEG and transforming them into accurate and continuous control signals. This provides a robust foundation for the development of high-performance, intuitive, and neuro-adaptive interface technologies.

3.1
Summary

This paper first proposes a novel amplifier for extracting motor intentions from the brain-the Brain-Muscle Interface (BMuI). Grounded in the biological principle that muscular activity reflects the brain’s motor intentions, and leveraging the fact that EMG are typically stronger and more stable than EEG, the proposed BMuI decodes EEG signals to predict EMG activity. In doing so, it enables the real-time extraction and amplification of motor intentions directly from the brain. Both offline and online experimental results demonstrate the feasibility and effectiveness of this approach, achieving accurate intention decoding without relying on external behavioral labels. This method avoids the pseudo-labelling issues inherent in traditional motor-based brain-computer interface, and offers a fundamentally new technological pathway for brain-computer interface development.

4
Method

This paper utilized a wearable 16-channel non-invasive EEG system (g.Nautilus) to record brain activity from the sensorimotor areas of the frontal and parietal lobes, with a sampling rate of 500 Hz. EMG were collected via 12 channels, with six electrodes evenly positioned on each upper arm to cover the muscles involved in elbow movement. The EMG signals were sampled at 1000 Hz. Additionally, a pressure sensor was employed to record force signals at a sampling frequency of 6.6 Hz. The electrode placement is illustrated in Fig.

4.1
Signal preprocessing

Initially, the EEG, EMG, and force signals were temporally aligned through upsampling procedures. The EEG signals were then pre-processed using a common average reference (CAR) technique and band-pass filtered between 15-35 Hz. The CAR method involves computing the average signal across all channels to generate a reference, which is subsequently subtracted from each individual channel. The operation is mathematically described as:

x_{i}^{CAR}\left(t\right)=x_{i}\left(t\right)-\frac{1}{C}\sum_{j=1}^{c}{x_{j}}\left(t\right)

Where is the potential value of the th channel and is the total number of channels.

This processing step effectively removes common mode noise and enhances the spatial resolution of the signal.

For EMG signals, a band-pass filter ranging from 20 to 450 Hz was applied to eliminate DC offset while preserving the relevant muscle activity frequencies. This was followed by a 48-52 Hz Butterworth notch filter to remove power line interference at 50 Hz, thereby improving signal fidelity.

4.2
Signal Segmentation

The EEG, EMG, and force signals were segmented into 60 independent sessions. Within each session, the arm force signals were analysed to determine the participant’s force exertion state. Specifically, a predefined threshold was established, and any instance in which the force signal exceeded this threshold was identified as an active task period. Signal segments meeting this criterion were subsequently extracted for further analysis. This segmentation strategy ensured that only task-relevant and high-quality data were retained, thereby enhancing the reliability and validity of subsequent analyses.

4.3
Brain-muscle interface

This paper proposes a sliding window-based Transformer model specifically designed to capture the temporal dynamics and spatial characteristics of EEG signals, thereby enabling the generation of discriminative representations for accurate mapping to EMG (see Fig.
8
). Originally developed for natural language processing, the Transformer architecture is distinguished by its encoder-decoder framework and multi-head self-attention mechanism, which allow it to model long-range dependencies in sequential data and extract coherent global structures. In this study, we extend the capabilities of the Transformer to the domain of neural signal decoding, capitalising on its strength in capturing global temporal dependencies to model the internal structure of EEG within local temporal segments. The incorporation of a sliding window mechanism preserves temporal continuity across segments, enabling the model to progressively construct a continuous decoding pathway from cortical activity to muscular activation. This approach highlights the Transformer’s adaptability in modelling non-stationary, noisy, and temporally complex biological signals, thereby broadening its applicability to cross-modal neural decoding tasks.

4.4
Direction-Proportional EMG Control(DP-EMG Control)

Offline experiments have demonstrated that, although the BMuI can effectively predict the overall trend of EMG signals, a discrepancy remains between the predicted output and the actual EMG activity. This discrepancy limits the accuracy of conventional proportional mapping approaches based on EMG.

To overcome this limitation, we propose a hybrid control strategy (see Fig.
9
). First, a convolutional neural network (CNN) is employed to decode the spatiotemporal features of multi-channel EMG, producing discrete movement direction commands and thereby improving directional recognition accuracy. Second, the brachialis muscle–an elbow flexor with clear physiological relevance and the best prediction performance–is selected as the source for activation strength. Its EMG activity is used for proportional mapping to enable continuous control. This approach combines the CNN’s capacity for directional classification with the natural continuity of muscle activation-based control, thereby achieving a balance between precision and intuitive responsiveness.
