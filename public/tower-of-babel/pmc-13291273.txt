Effects of herbaceous root development on soil water infiltration and hydraulic properties in loess
Authors: Xiaochan Wang, Shijie Liu, Chaoyang Tian, Pan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Root development alters soil pore structure and connectivity, thereby affecting un-saturated water movement; however, its growth-stage-dependent effects on infiltration and hydraulic properties in loess remain poorly quantified. In this study, one-dimensional soil column infiltration experiments were conducted on Festuca arundinacea root–soil composites under root distribution conditions corresponding to different growth stages (T = 0, 35, 52, 76, and 99d). The results showed that total infiltration duration decreased significantly with increasing growth stage, indicating enhanced water transport capacity in loess. Saturated hydraulic conductivity showed a strong linear relationship with root biomass ratio (RB), while the other hydraulic parameters varied systematically with increasing RB and were well described by exponential functions. Based on the temporal evolution of root distribution, quantitative relationships between hydraulic parameters and growth stage were established and incorporated into the Richards equation. HYDRUS simulations agreed well with the measured wet-ting-front advance and volumetric water content changes. These findings indicate that herbaceous root growth stage is an important biological factor regulating infiltration behavior and hydraulic properties in loess, providing parameter support for studies of vegetation–soil–water interactions and soil water infiltration modeling under vegetation restoration on the Loess Plateau.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55875-1.

Introduction

Water infiltration is a key process in unsaturated soil hydrology, directly control-ling soil water redistribution, plant water uptake, rainfall runoff generation, and water cycling in slope ecosystems1. In the Loess Plateau region of China, where precipitation is limited and highly uneven in both space and time, soil infiltration not only determines the efficiency of rainfall utilization but also profoundly affects vegetation restoration, soil conservation, and slope stability2,3. Loess is characterized by a loose structure, uneven pore distribution, and pronounced softening upon wetting, and its infiltration behavior is therefore highly sensitive to changes in pore structure4,5. Therefore, clarifying the biological and structural factors that affect infiltration in loess is of great importance for understanding soil hydrological processes and the effects of vegetation restoration in the Loess Plateau region.

Vegetation roots are important biological factors regulating soil pore structure and water movement. Previous studies have shown that roots can significantly affect water infiltration in unsaturated soils by altering pore connectivity, forming preferential flow pathways, and increasing the proportion of medium and large pores6–12. Structural characteristics such as root diameter1,13–15, distribution depth16–18, and root content19–21 are closely related to improvements in soil pore structure and can further influence saturated hydraulic conductivity and the shape of the soil water characteristic curve (SWCC)10,22–24.

However, most existing studies have focused on the effects of static root traits, such as root biomass, root-influenced depth, and root structure at a single time point, on soil hydraulic properties9,21,25, whereas the temporal evolution of root architecture during plant growth and its hydrological effects remain relatively underexplored. In fact, root biomass, diameter, and distribution depth change continuously across growth stages, leading to stage-dependent variations in rhizosphere pore structure and soil hydraulic parameters19,26,27. Most relevant studies have so far focused on woody vegetation through field observations or numerical analyses28,29, whereas research on how root development in herbaceous plants affects water infiltration and hydraulic parameter dynamics in loess remains limited.

Under large-scale vegetation restoration, herbaceous roots have gradually become an important biological factor affecting pore structure and unsaturated water movement in loess25,30. Therefore, systematically revealing the quantitative relationships between herbaceous root development and the unsaturated hydraulic parameters of loess, such as the Van Genuchten model parameters, and clarifying how root structural evolution across growth stages influences soil water infiltration and hydraulic properties are of great significance for a deeper under-standing of vegetation–soil–water interactions and soil hydrological responses under ecological restoration in loess regions.

Based on the above, this study focused on F. arundinacea root–soil composites. Through root distribution investigation, one-dimensional soil column infiltration experiments, and unsaturated hydraulic parameter testing, the effects of herbaceous root development at different growth stages on soil water infiltration and hydraulic parameter evolution in loess were systematically investigated. Based on these results, quantitative relationships between hydraulic parameters and growth stage were established and incorporated into the Richards equation, and HYDRUS simulations were performed to evaluate the applicability and predictive capability of the proposed relationships for unsaturated infiltration under different growth stages.

Materials and methods

Basic properties of tested soil and root

The soil samples used in this study were collected from Bailuyuan, a representative area of the Loess Plateau in Xi’an, Shaanxi Province, China (Longitude: 109.096517°, Latitude: 34.214674°). This region is located in the transitional zone between the Guanzhong Basin and the southern edge of the Loess Plateau, with an annual average precipitation of approximately 500–700 mm. It is strongly influenced by a monsoon climate, with rainfall exhibiting marked seasonality, and short-duration, high-intensity rainfall events frequently occurring in summer33,34.

As shown in Fig. 1(a), the vegetation in this area is dominated by herbaceous cover and cropland, exhibiting typical surface cover characteristics of the Loess Plateau. A natural slope in a public land was selected for sampling (Fig. 1(b)). After removing the surface loose soil, undisturbed loess samples were collected manually from the exposed sidewall of the slope (Fig. 1(c)). The basic physical properties of the loess were then determined, as listed in Table 1. The particle size distribution curve of the test soil is shown in Fig. 2. The tested soil was composed predominantly of silt, followed by clay, while sand accounted for the smallest proportion, showing a typical particle size composition of loess.

Investigation of root distribution

Rainfall-induced shallow landslides in loess regions are predominantly distributed in areas covered by herbaceous vegetation, especially on the Loess Plateau, with previous studies reporting that approximately 69% of landslides occur in such areas31,32. F. arundinacea is, a commonly used herbaceous species for ecological slope protection on the Loess Plateau, is widely applied in soil–bioengineering practices such as highway, railway, and river embankment slope stabilization. Therefore, it was selected as a representative plant for this study to investigate the influence of herbaceous root development on soil water infiltration. To facilitate the collection of F. arundinacea roots at different growth stages and to observe their developmental patterns, a 30 m² rectangular planting area was established in this study (Fig. 3(a-b)). Roots of F. arundinacea at different growth stages were sampled, and their developmental characteristics are shown in Fig. 4. As shown in Fig. 4, during the 0–52 d stage, the root structure was relatively simple and had not yet differentiated into a complex architecture, while root length increased significantly with growth stage. When the growth stage reached T = 64 d, lateral root development became more pronounced, gradually forming a mature root network, and root length gradually stabilized at approximately 24 cm.

To further clarify the root distribution of F. arundinacea vegetation at different soil depths under different growth stages (T), layered sampling was conducted for F. arundinacea at different growth stages (0, 7, 15, 24, 35, 52, 64, 76, 88, and 99 d). First, the aboveground stems and leaves as well as the loose surface soil were removed. A ring knife (φ6.18 × 2 cm) was then used to collect soil samples containing F. arundinacea roots from the 0–24 cm soil layer. The samples were grouped at 4 cm depth intervals, i.e., 0–4 cm, 4–8 cm, 8–12 cm, 12–16 cm, 16–20 cm, and 20–24 cm, resulting in six groups (Fig. 3(c)). During sampling, the cutting edge of the ring knife was kept facing downward and perpendicular to the ground surface (Fig. 3(d-e)) to ensure sampling reliability. The collected samples were then sieved to separate the roots, and the adhering soil on the root surface was removed by washing. The roots were air-dried to constant weight and then weighed (Fig. 3(f)). On this basis, the root biomass ratio (RB) of the root–soil composites at different soil depths was calculated by Eq. (1).

Soil column infiltration experiment

In this study, a self-designed soil column infiltration apparatus (Fig. 5) was employed to conduct unsaturated water infiltration experiments on root–soil composites. The experimental setup mainly consisted of three components: an infiltration column system, a water supply system, and a data acquisition system, as illustrated in Fig. 5(a).

Fig. 5Soil column infiltration test system. (a): Infiltration device physical map and (b): moisture sensor layout diagram.

Soil column infiltration test system. (a): Infiltration device physical map and (b): moisture sensor layout diagram.

The infiltration column was a cylindrical container made of transparent acrylic, with a wall thickness of 1 cm, a height of 50 cm, and an inner diameter of 30 cm. A filter screen was installed at the bottom of the column to ensure uniform seepage distribution and to prevent the loss of fine particles. Four small openings were arranged on the sidewalls of the infiltration column (Fig. 5(b), through which volumetric water content sensors were installed. The sensors used were CSF13 series soil water content sensors manufactured by XingYi (China), with a measurement range of 0–60% and an accuracy of ± 2%. During the experiments, the data sampling interval was set to 2s.

Based on the root distribution characteristics of mature F. arundinacea, five growth stages (T = 0, 35, 52, 76, and 99 d) were selected in this study to prepare root–soil composite column specimens. To realistically reproduce the actual root distribution pattern, a plain loess layer was arranged from the bottom of the column to a depth of 0–16 cm, while the root–soil composite layer was placed at depths of 16–40 cm (Fig. 5(b)). The root–soil composite section was further divided into six layers, with each layer being 4 cm thick. The RB assigned to each layer was determined according to the root distribution measured in the section “Investigation of root distribution” (the RB values for each layer are listed in Table S1).

During the preparation of the soil column specimens, the natural loess was air-dried, crushed, and sieved through a 2 mm mesh. Deionized water was then uniformly sprayed onto the soil using a spray bottle to achieve the target water content. The moistened soil was subsequently sealed in plastic bags lined with plastic film and allowed to stand for 24 h to ensure uniform moisture distribution.

Based on the target dry density \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\rho\:}_{d}$$\end{document} (1.389 g/cm³), water content, cross-sectional area of the soil column, and the corresponding RB for each soil layer, the required masses of loose loess and F. arundinacea roots for each 2 cm-thick soil layer were calculated. The weighed loess and roots were thoroughly mixed to ensure homogeneity. The prepared loose soil mixture was then evenly placed into the infiltration column and compacted, resulting in a final layer thickness of 2 cm after compaction. After compaction, the surface of the soil layer was roughened to prevent layer separation during subsequent filling. This procedure was repeated layer by layer until a soil column specimen with a total height of 40 cm was formed.

After the preparation of the soil column specimens, the top of columns were sealed with plastic film and allowed to stand for 24 h. The initial volumetric water content of the soil column was then determined using the sensors (approximately 17.5% in this study), after which the infiltration experiments were conducted. Rainfall infiltration was applied using the water supply system, and to represent the short‑duration, high‑intensity summer rainfall commonly observed in the Bailuyuan area of the Loess Plateau, and based on previous studies21,35,36, the rainfall intensity was set to 60 mm/h.

During the experiments, the wetting front migration was visually monitored through the transparent wall of the infiltration column. The experiment was terminated when the wetting front reached a position 1 cm above the bottom of the column, as the column fixing device at the base was non-transparent and obstructed further visual observation.

Numerical calculation of soil column infiltration

Using the Hydrus-2D software39, two dimensional soil column models were established under different growth stages T (0, 35, 52, 76 and 99 d), as shown in Fig. 6. The model domain was set to 30 cm × 40 cm, consistent with the laboratory experiments. The computational domain was divided into seven layers: the upper layer (0–24 cm) was assigned as the root-soil composite and further divided into six layers with a thickness of 4 cm each, denoted as Materials 1–6; while the lower layer (24–40 cm) was assigned as bare loess (Material 7). The corresponding material parameters were specified for each layer. The computational domain was discretized using an unstructured triangular finite element mesh, allowing effective representation of spatial heterogeneity while maintaining numerical stability. The final mesh consisted of 1456 nodes and 2798 two dimensional elements, with local refinement applied in key regions to ensure adequate resolution of hydraulic gradients and infiltration processes. A free rainfall boundary condition was imposed at the top boundary, with a rainfall rate of 0.99 mL min⁻¹. The bottom boundary was specified as free drainage, while the lateral boundaries were defined as impermeable (Fig. 6). Observation points were placed at depths of 5, 10, 20, and 30 cm to monitor the temporal evolution of volumetric water content during infiltration. The infiltration process was terminated when the wetting front reached a depth of 39 cm.

Results

Evolution of root distribution

The distribution of root biomass ratio (RB) of F. arundinacea at different growth stages along the soil profile is shown in Fig. 7. As illustrated in Fig. 7, the root distribution range initially increases and then stabilizes with increasing growth stage. At T = 7 d, F. arundinacea roots were only present in the 0–4 cm soil layer; at T=15d, roots extended to the 0–8 cm layer; and at T=24d, they reached the 0–12 cm layer. When the growth stage reached 52 d, the root distribution spanned 0–24 cm, and for stages beyond T=52d, the distribution remained stable within 0–24 cm. The RB at each soil layer showed a general increasing trend with growth stage. Taking the 0–4 cm layer as an example, RB was minimal (0.0053%) at T = 7 d, increased markedly to 0.6586% at T=64d, and stabilized at approximately 0.8750% when T reached 88d. During the preparation of soil column specimens for different growth stages, the RB value assigned to each soil layer was based on these measurements (the RB values for each layer are provided in Table S1).

Characteristics of wetting fronts and water variation

In the soil column infiltration experiments, water was applied from the top of the column. During infiltration, the interface between the wetted zone and the relatively dry zone is defined as the wetting front21, whose migration process could be clearly observed through the transparent wall of the column. Owing to obstruction by the fixing device, the observable range of the wetting front was limited to the zone extending from the top of the soil column to a depth of 0–39 cm.

Figure 9 presents that the wetting front migration of soil column specimens with different growth stages (T) during the infiltration process. The results show that, as infiltration proceeded, the wetting front gradually migrated downward. In addition, root incorporation effectively enhanced water movement in the soil, thereby altering the wetting-front migration pattern in the soil column specimens.

As shown in Fig. 9(a), for the bare loess specimen, the wetting front reached a depth of 2.52 cm at an infiltration time of t = 10 min and advanced to 13.50 cm at t = 60 min. The total infiltration duration was 403.8 min. With increasing T, the infiltration rate increased markedly. For the specimen with T = 99 d (Fig. 9(e)), the wetting front reached 17.75 cm at t = 60 min, which was 4.25 cm deeper than that of the bare loess. At t = 120 min, the wetting front advanced to 25.87 cm, exceeding that of the bare loess (18.28 cm) by 7.59 cm. Meanwhile, the total infiltration duration was reduced to approximately 272.93 min, representing a reduction of 47.95% compared with that of plain loess. Overall, as the growth stage increased, the total infiltration duration gradually decreased, and the wetting-front advance distance increased at the same infiltration time.

Discussion

Temporal response analysis of unsaturated infiltration parameters

Previous studies have demonstrated that root systems of various vegetation types significantly influence soil hydraulic properties. Trees and shrubs with deep and extensive root systems can enhance infiltration and saturated hydraulic conductivity in unsaturated soils through the formation of macropores and preferential flow paths34,35,47. Other herbaceous plants primarily affect shallow soil layers, accelerating wetting front propagation and altering volumetric water content dynamics7,25. These studies highlight that the magnitude, depth, and spatial distribution of root effects vary among vegetation types, which should be carefully considered when evaluating soil water transport processes. The F. arundinacea roots used in this study are characterized by small diameters, dense distribution, and highly flexible networks. When incorporated into the soil, these roots, through mechanisms such as network formation and compression, promote the formation of local soil aggregates, alter the pore structure32. Specifically, root incorporation effectively increases the total porosity and the proportion of medium and large pores. This increase in medium‑to‑large pores enhances pore connectivity, while decreasing the uniformity of pore distribution10,40. Consequently, these changes modify the soil infiltration parameters, including \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{K}_{s}$$\end{document}, saturated water content, and residual water content32,40. The root distribution is closely related to its growth stage T. With increasing T, both the root-influenced depth and root content gradually increase. Based on Eqs. (7–11), the evolution of unsaturated infiltration parameters of the root–soil composite at different soil depths under the influence of F. arundinacea roots at different growth stages (0, 35, 52, 76, and 99 d) was calculated, as shown in Fig. 13.

Figure 13 presents the calculated results, during the growth stage of T = 0-52d, F. arundinacea roots affected only the soil layer within 0–12 cm, and their influence on the saturated hydraulic conductivity of the soil was limited (Fig. 13(a)). Taking T=35d as an example, the infiltration-promoting effect of roots was confined to the 0–8 cm soil layer. At 0–4 cm, the saturated hydraulic conductivity was approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:8.38\times\:{10}^{-5}$$\end{document}, which was only 10.22% higher than that of plain loess (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:8.38\times\:{10}^{-5}$$\end{document}); at 4–8 cm, it was \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:8.42\times\:{10}^{-5}$$\end{document}, representing an increase of only 1.53%. Meanwhile, parameters such as the air-entry value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:$$\end{document} and the shape parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:n$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:m$$\end{document} also changed only slightly. Therefore, under the growth stages of 35 and 52 d, the soil column infiltration tests exhibited only a slight reduction in total infiltration duration compared with plain loess, and the sensor response times at different soil depths remained close to those of plain loess.

Temporal response analysis of water infiltration

In this study, the hydraulic parameters of bare loess and root–soil composites at different growth stages were obtained using Eqs. (7–11) in HYDRUS. Based on these parameters, numerical simulations were conducted to determine the wetting front progression and the temporal changes in volumetric water content during infiltration.

Figure 14 presents a comparison between the soil column infiltration test results and the numerical simulation results for the root–soil composites. Under different growth stage T conditions, the simulated results agreed well with the measured data. Taking the root–soil composite specimen at T=99d as an example (Fig. 14(a)), the simulated wetting front reached 18.46 cm at an infiltration time of 60 min, differing from the measured value (17.75 cm) by only 0.11 cm. When the infiltration time increased to 210 min, the simulated wetting front reached 32.53 cm, with a difference of 0.52 cm from the measured value (33.05 cm). Further comparison of the total infiltration duration obtained from the numerical simulation, with the experimental results also showed great agreement. Both the simulated and measured total infiltration durations gradually decreased with increasing T.

Conclusion

This study combined root distribution investigation, one-dimensional soil column infiltration experiments, and unsaturated hydraulic parameter tests to systematically analyzed the effects of herbaceous root development at different growth stages on the infiltration process and hydraulic parameter evolution of loess. The main conclusions are as follows:

(i)An exponential function was used to quantitatively characterize the influence of vegetation growth stage on root distribution patterns. With increasing growth stage, the root distribution range first expanded and then gradually stabilized. When the growth stage exceeded =52d, the root distribution range became basically stable within the 0–24 cm soil layer. Meanwhile, the RB at different soil depths also increased rapidly at first and then gradually stabilized with increasing growth stage.(ii)Vegetation growth significantly affected water infiltration in loess by expanding the root distribution range and increasing RB. As the growth stage increased, the wetting-front advance rate gradually increased, whereas the total infiltration duration decreased markedly.
