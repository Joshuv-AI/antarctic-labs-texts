Bilateral Maxillary Fourth and Fifth Molars in a 17-Year-Old Female
Authors: Hikari Wakao, Takahiro Takekawa, Karen Gomi, Akinori Moroi, Koichiro Ueki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supernumerary teeth are relatively common in clinical practice; however, the occurrence of multiple supernumerary teeth in a bilaterally symmetrical pattern is rare. A 17-year-old female was referred to our department for extraction of impacted third molars. Panoramic radiography revealed two bilaterally symmetrical tooth-like structures adjacent to each maxillary third molar. Computed tomography confirmed that these were independent structures, leading to a diagnosis of bilateral maxillary fourth and fifth molars. In July 2023, the bilateral maxillary and mandibular third molars, together with the bilateral maxillary fourth and fifth molars, were extracted under general anesthesia. Gross examination of the extracted teeth revealed morphological features consistent with molar teeth. This case highlights the importance of accurate three-dimensional imaging and individualized treatment strategies in the management of complex and rare presentations of supernumerary teeth.

Introduction

The reported prevalence of supernumerary teeth (ST) ranges from 0.1% to 3.8%, and most cases remain asymptomatic [1-5]. ST are often incidentally detected during radiographic examinations performed for dental treatment, such as caries, periodontal disease, or malocclusion [6-8]. Two-dimensional (2D) radiographic imaging has limitations in the diagnosis of supernumerary teeth, whereas three-dimensional (3D) evaluation using computed tomography (CT) is essential for accurate localization and morphological assessment.

Previous reports have suggested that ST are associated with genetic factors and systemic conditions, including syndromes [1]. ST may cause various clinical complications, such as delayed eruption, displacement of adjacent teeth, crowding, and cyst formation. The most common site of occurrence is the maxillary midline region, followed by the maxillary molar region [9-14].

Among supernumerary molars, fourth molars that morphologically resemble adjacent molars have occasionally been reported, whereas fifth molars are extremely rare.

Case presentation

In April 2023, a 17-year-old female was referred to our department for extraction of impacted third molars. The patient had initially visited a local dental clinic, where impacted third molars were identified during a routine radiographic examination. Surgical management

In July 2023, the bilateral mandibular third molars and the bilateral maxillary third, fourth, and fifth molars were extracted under general anesthesia. Gross examination of the four maxillary supernumerary molars revealed morphological features consistent with molar teeth (Figure 7).

From left to right, the extracted specimens include the right maxillary fifth molar, the right maxillary fourth molar, the left maxillary fourth molar, and the left maxillary fifth molar.

Postoperative outcome

Intraoperative findings revealed no oroantral communication between the extraction sockets and the maxillary sinuses. Each extracted tooth was independent, with no evidence of synodontia or adhesion to the surrounding alveolar bone or adjacent teeth. Gross examination of the extracted teeth revealed morphological features consistent with molar teeth.

The postoperative course was uneventful. Postoperative panoramic radiography demonstrated satisfactory healing of the extraction sockets, with no retained fragments or other abnormalities (Figure 8).

Postoperative panoramic radiograph.

No abnormalities were observed at the extraction sites. The bilateral maxillary third, fourth, and fifth molars and the bilateral mandibular third molars had been completely removed.

The patient was followed for one year after surgery. At the one-year follow-up, clinical examination showed satisfactory healing, with no postoperative complications.

Written informed consent for the publication of this case and the accompanying images was obtained from the patient’s legal guardian, and assent was obtained from the patient.

Discussion

The occurrence of ST is believed to be influenced by genetic and environmental factors. These teeth are more prevalent in the permanent dentition than in the primary dentition and are observed more often in the maxilla than in the mandible [1-5]. Regarding site-specific distribution, reported frequencies are as follows: maxillary anterior region, 77.2%; maxillary molar region, 11.0%; mandibular premolar region, 5.2%; mandibular anterior region, 2.7%; maxillary premolar region, 2.6%; and mandibular molar region, 2.7% [9]. These variations in frequency across different sites have been attributed to several factors, including the theory of phylogenetic atavism and spatial variations within the successional dental lamina [9,12,13].

In a survey of children aged 5-15 years with ST, 4.6% had three or more supernumerary teeth [1]. Furthermore, five or more supernumerary teeth were observed in only 2.4% (6 of 251) of affected individuals [7]. Fujiwara et al. revealed that among patients with bilaterally symmetrical ST, the sites of occurrence were the anterior (73.3%; 33 cases), premolar (8.9%; 4 cases), and molar (8.9%; 4 cases) regions [8]. In the present case, four ST were observed in the bilateral maxillary third molar region, with two teeth occurring symmetrically on each side, a rare clinical presentation based on the number and the specific posterior location.

Systemic conditions associated with a high prevalence of ST include cleft lip and palate and cleidocranial dysplasia. Other reported associations include Gardner syndrome, chondroectodermal dysplasia (Ellis-Van Creveld syndrome), Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, and tricho-rhino-phalangeal syndrome [1]. CT is an essential diagnostic tool for ST, providing accurate 3D information regarding tooth orientation, local abnormalities, and relationships with adjacent anatomical structures. In this case, 3D CT reconstruction aided in reducing operative time and surgical invasiveness.

Compound and complex odontomas were considered in the differential diagnosis. However, each radiopaque structure appeared as an independent tooth-like structure with a recognizable crown and root morphology resembling that of a molar. These findings differed from the multiple small tooth-like structures typically observed in compound odontomas and the irregular radiopaque mass characteristic of complex odontomas. Therefore, based on the panoramic radiographic, CT, and gross morphological findings, the structures were diagnosed as bilateral maxillary fourth and fifth molars. However, histopathological examination of the extracted specimens was not performed; therefore, their dental tissue composition could not be microscopically confirmed, and odontomas could not be definitively excluded. This represents a limitation of the present case.

To our knowledge, only 10 cases of bilateral maxillary fourth molars, including the present case, have been reported in the literature [15-23] (Table 1).

These studies suggest that, in addition to genetic factors, racial and geographic factors may influence ST occurrence. Reportedly, variations exist in prevalence among racial groups [1,2,5,8,14], with a higher prevalence reported in Black populations (6.4%) than in White populations (0.9%). Similarly, other studies have indicated a higher prevalence in Asian populations (2.7%) than in White populations (1.6%). Notably, 4 of the 10 reported cases were from Japan. This relatively high frequency of reported fourth and fifth molars in East Asia, particularly Japan, suggests potential regional variation in their manifestation.

The presence of ST may cause malocclusion, including tooth displacement, crowding, delayed eruption, or impaction, as well as root resorption. Therefore, extraction is the preferred treatment when an adverse impact on adjacent teeth is expected [3,6]. Clinical factors associated with these complications include morphology, orientation, and the patient’s age [24]. Currently, there is no consensus on the optimal timing for ST extraction. The existing literature highlights two primary approaches: early intervention before root formation of permanent teeth and delayed extraction after root formation is complete [1,25].

Additionally, in cases where simultaneous extraction of the third and fourth molars is deemed high-risk, a staged approach has been proposed. This approach involves prioritizing the extraction of the third molar, followed by a period of clinical observation until the fourth molar erupts, at which point it is subsequently removed [26]. However, in some cases, extraction may be challenging, such as when the ST is fused to an adjacent tooth at the crown or root, or when it is located close to the root apex of an adjacent tooth or the nasal floor. In such cases, if permanent tooth eruption is favorable and no associated cystic or tumorous lesions are present, careful observation may be considered a treatment option. During follow-up, periodic radiographic examinations are required to confirm the position of the ST and to monitor for the development of cystic or tumorous lesions [27-29]. In this case, extraction was indicated based on a comprehensive preoperative assessment, in which we considered the potential for malocclusion in the permanent dentition and the risk that the ST could become a source of inflammation. Surgical planning using 3D CT enabled a minimally invasive extraction process, underscoring that the management of ST necessitates accurate 3D diagnosis and a tailored treatment strategy for each patient.

Conclusions

ST are influenced by genetic and environmental factors and are more commonly associated with permanent dentition and the maxilla. The present case, which involved four bilaterally symmetrical ST in the maxillary third molar region, represents a rare clinical presentation regarding the number of teeth and their posterior anatomical location. Accurate diagnosis using 3D CT was essential for a precise evaluation of anatomical relationships and facilitated a minimally invasive surgical approach. ST management should be individualized, considering tooth morphology, orientation, potential complications, and patient-specific factors. This case highlights the importance of accurate 3D imaging and tailored treatment strategies in the management of complex and uncommon presentations of ST.
