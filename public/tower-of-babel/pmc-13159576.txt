Continuing Medical Education as a Tool to Accelerate the Adoption of Extended-Duration Intravitreal Anti-VEGFs
Authors: Katie Robinson, Kevin Lester, Sasha Persaud, Charlene Depry, Simon Cooper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurpose: This study evaluated the impact of a comprehensive, curriculum-based continuing education (CE) programme on the adoption of second-generation intravitreal anti-VEGF therapies among ophthalmologists treating patients with diabetic macular oedema (DME) or neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD). Methods: A multimodal educational curriculum was developed for eye care providers, incorporating principles of adult learning and marketing strategies. The curriculum included 14 CE activities (didactic content, case scenarios, live meetings, interprofessional activities, and downloadable resources) delivered from July 2024 through April 2026. Surveys, assessing knowledge, attitudes, and clinical decision-making, were administered before, during, and after participation. Participants were categorised as single-activity learners (one activity) or multiple-activity learners (three or more activities). Statistical analyses compared knowledge acquisition, comfort, and adoption of second-generation anti-VEGF agents between groups. Results: As of September 2025, 5,975 providers completed at least one activity; 398 providers, with complete data, were analysed (289 single-activity, 109 multiple-activity learners). Both groups, post education, demonstrated improved knowledge and comfort with second-generation anti-VEGF agents. Multiple-activity learners had higher baseline knowledge and consistently selected second-generation agents more frequently in case scenarios. Single-activity learners showed greater knowledge gains, while multiple-activity learners maintained higher overall knowledge. The curriculum increased preparedness for future use of new therapies, particularly among low-frequency injectors (<9 injections/week), though some barriers to adoption persisted. Conclusion: A curriculum-based, multimodal CE programme can accelerate the adoption of innovative therapies by improving clinician knowledge, comfort, and clinical decision-making. Iterative, data-driven education is effective in bridging gaps between emerging science and real-world practice, ultimately supporting better patient outcomes in nAMD and DME care.

Introduction

Diabetic macular oedema (DME) and neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) are both on the rise in the United States, leading to a growing number of patients in need of treatment [1,2]. An ageing population and increasing diabetes prevalence have expanded the at-risk pool – nearly 20 million Americans have some form of AMD, and the prevalence of DME in working-age adults tripled from 2009 to 2018 [1]. Consequently, demand for intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy has surged. Ophthalmologists who administer anti-VEGF injections are performing millions of anti-VEGF treatments each year, with insurance claims for injections climbing dramatically over the past decade [3]. At the same time, clinical advances in the management of patients with nAMD or DME are evolving at a daunting pace. First-generation anti-VEGF agents like bevacizumab, ranibizumab, and aflibercept 2 mg have been routinely used for years. In the last few years, second-generation agents, faricimab and aflibercept 8 mg, have been approved to treat both patients with DME or nAMD. However, both options are still relatively new, and clinicians who inject intravitreal anti-VEGFs lack clarity regarding when, how, and in whom to use these agents. This workload underscores the critical need for efficient and up-to-date strategies for the management of patients with nAMD or DME.

Previously, we reported results from a survey of patients with DME or nAMD and ophthalmologists who treat these patients that highlighted the burden of disease and its treatment as experienced by patients [4]. Most patients with DME or nAMD receive intravitreal injections regularly but rely on others or public transport to attend appointments. Patients with DME mainly experience progressive vision loss and blurred vision, while patients with nAMD report more issues with night vision and low light. More patients with nAMD are dissatisfied with information and counselling than those with DME. Ophthalmologists often misjudge patients’ perspectives on treatment necessity, insurance challenges, anxiety, and transportation difficulties. Further, patients are dissatisfied with the counselling and education provided by clinicians, more so for patients with nAMD than those with DME [4].

Additionally, several recognised challenges in the diagnosis and long-term management of patients with nAMD or DME have been identified. On the diagnostic side, limited screenings have been shown to lead to late diagnosis or undiagnosed DME [5]. Once patients are in care, lack of adherence to the high injection frequency has been linked to poor real-world outcomes [6,7]. From the physician’s perspective, managing these conditions now entails complex decision-making, such as choosing among multiple anti-VEGF agents or determining how to extend injection intervals safely [8,9]. There is a limited consensus on the “best” approach for an individual case, especially when patients have an inadequate response to initial therapy. Resource constraints also play a role – retina clinics face high volumes, and integrating care (e.g. coordinating with endocrinologists for diabetic patients) can be difficult [10]. Overall, despite remarkable progress in therapies, ensuring each patient gets timely, sustained, and personalised care for nAMD or DME remains a multifaceted challenge.

Based on these challenges, a need for comprehensive education that is sustainable, data-driven, agile, engaging, and easy-to-navigate was identified. Continuing education (CE) based on a curriculum approach has been shown to be effective in improving knowledge, competence, and performance of clinicians; and is likely to have greater clinical effect than single education exposure [11,12]. Curriculum education, defined as a series of educational activities in which learners are tracked longitudinally and data from earlier activities is used to refine the content for subsequent activities, allows multi-modal serial education, providing a mix of live and online content hosted on a centralised platform, with downloadable patient/clinician resources on a variety of topics centred on one theme. This form of education also facilitates refinement of the messaging over time to ensure that it addresses current challenges and educational needs.

The objective of this study was to assess the impact of a CE curriculum on the adoption of second-generation anti-VEGF therapies by evaluating the knowledge and attitudes of ophthalmologists who participated in multiple online activities compared to those who participated in only one.

Methods

Design of the Education

The educational intervention was designed around a central learning platform with 14 different CE activities of multiple formats initiating in July 2024 and scheduled through April 2026. The research conducted prior to the education helped inform content development. Additionally, course faculty assisted in the refinement of the final learning objectives, agenda, and presentation of content. The format of the education collectively included: didactic content focused on the latest data,case scenario education to practice nuances in management of different scenarios of patients with nAMD or DME (eg, treatment-naïve, suboptimal initial response, treatment-refractory),live meetings at national conferences to add interaction and networking,interprofessional CE activity that included physician and optometry faculty to promote best practices for assessment, referral, and management within care teamsdownloadable clinician resources related to the importance of early detection, new options for treatment, and engaging in effective conversations.trigger-based marketing to promote learner engagement via personalised emails throughout the curriculum

Outcomes and Analysis

Multiple surveys were provided to the physicians before, during, and after they engaged in the education. All questions were created to highlight key messaging throughout the educational intervention. While Moore’s outcomes framework was not explicitly used, measures such as knowledge and performance were assessed and supplemented with measures such as self-reported comfort and preparedness to change, which can influence downstream behaviours. All surveys were created by AMA standards for multiple-choice questions and reviewed by programme faculty. Full survey instruments can be found in Appendix A. Data were collected electronically. For analysis, questions were mapped to a specific theme, and percentages were calculated across multiple questions. Baseline knowledge for multiple-activity learners was measured at each activity. Chi-square and t-test statistics were used to compare different groups throughout the study, as described in the results.

Results

Demographics of the Sample

As of September 2025, 5,975 eye care providers completed at least one component of the education; 1004 of those were US-based community physicians who indicated that they currently use anti-VEGF injections in their patients with nAMD/DME. This cohort was comprised of ophthalmologists who identified as either retina specialists (33%, n = 331) or general ophthalmologists (67%, n = 673)”. Of these clinicians, a subset of 398 was analysed as they had provided complete data on all aspects of the programme. Of this subset, 289 were classified as completing just one activity (“single-activity learners”) and 109 completed three or more activities (“multiple-activity learners”) (Table 1). These two groups were further categorised by the number of patients they see each week, the number of anti-VEGF injections they provide each week, and their primary practice setting. In order to understand the relationship between their practice setting and prior experience with these agents, learners were divided by the number of online activities they engaged in and the number of anti-VEGF injections they conducted per week (high = 21+; low = <9). High injectors were more comfortable than low injectors among single-activity learners, but levels of comfort were more moderate in the multiple-activity cohort, regardless of injection volume (Figure 1). Again, clinicians were divided by their number of weekly injections of anti-VEGF they conduct (high = 21+; low = <9). While high injectors indicated a higher frequency of use of second-generation anti-VEGF agents than low injectors, there was little difference between those who participated in multiple or single education activities (Figure 2). However, for low injectors, those who participated in multiple activities indicated lower use of second-generation agents than single-activity learners. Discussion

This study evaluated the impact of a comprehensive, curriculum-based CE programme on the adoption of second-generation anti-VEGF therapies among ophthalmologists treating nAMD and DME. The pre- and post-education assessment results demonstrated that both single-activity and multiple-activity learners improved their knowledge and comfort with these agents. Notably, multiple-activity learners entered the curriculum with higher baseline knowledge and showed consistent gains across all measured domains, including clinical data interpretation, switching strategies, and real-world application. In case-based scenarios, a higher proportion of multiple-activity learners selected second-generation agents compared to single-activity learners, suggesting that repeated engagement with the curriculum may foster greater openness to adopting innovative therapies. The findings reinforce the value of a multimodal, curriculum-based approach to CE. Unlike single-exposure educational interventions, the curriculum allowed for iterative learning, agile content adaptation, and sustained engagement. Learners could navigate content according to their needs, and the curriculum was responsive. After reviewing early outcomes, we were able to tailor and fine-tune content based on ongoing feedback and observed gaps. This adaptive approach not only improved knowledge acquisition but also supported meaningful changes in clinical attitudes and decision-making, particularly regarding the use of newer anti-VEGF agents. The integration of live meetings, case-based learning, and downloadable resources further enhanced the educational experience and facilitated interprofessional collaboration.

Interestingly, we found that while there were substantial knowledge gains for all learners, a greater effect was seen in the single-activity learners. Multiple-activity learners appeared to have a higher baseline knowledge level, reflecting either greater prior understanding of the material or incremental knowledge gain along their learning journey. Multiple exposures may help clinicians maintain high knowledge levels, but even a single exposure can bring learners up to par in key areas.

Using a curriculum-based approach in education can be logistically difficult. Clinicians have limited time for continuing education and have multiple patient types and therapeutic areas that they may have interest in keeping up to date. Effective marketing strategies were essential to address these barriers and make it clear that each component of the curriculum was interconnected and contributed to a cohesive learning experience. Approaches such as trigger marketing, the development of a recognisable brand, and a unified landing page were instrumental in nudging learners to return and engage with additional content. Despite these efforts, the sheer volume of material and the rapid pace of advancements in retina care required strategic prioritisation of new content and ongoing reinforcement of the curriculum’s value. These considerations highlight the importance of not only high-quality educational content but also thoughtful delivery and sustained outreach to maximise clinician participation and impact. Future education could even use a micro-learning approach to highlight aspects of each activity in the curriculum in a smaller time burden to encourage broader education for learners.

While the curriculum addressed many known challenges, the project also revealed new gaps. For example, despite overall gains, some low-frequency injectors remained less likely to adopt second-generation agents, indicating persistent barriers to change in certain practice settings. As discussed in a recent review, practice barriers may limit the best education from delivering real change in performance and patient outcomes [13]. Additionally, the need for ongoing education about integrating real-world data, best practices for first-line versus subsequent therapy selection, and strategies for patient engagement and counselling became apparent. These insights can inform future iterations of the curriculum and highlight areas for further research and resource development.

Limitations

Several limitations to this study should be acknowledged. First, the study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to recall and response biases. While the sample included a diverse group of clinicians using anti-VEGF agents, outcomes were limited to clinicians in the United States, potentially limiting generalisability. While knowledge and attitudes improved, the study did not directly measure changes in patient outcomes or long-term practice patterns. Additionally, the effect of systemic barriers such as access to injections or insurance coverage was not addressed here and could contribute to the reported practice behaviours. Future research should aim to address these limitations and explore the sustained impact of curriculum-based CE on clinical practice.

The lack of randomisation and the observational design limit causal inference. An alternative theory for these results would be that learners who did more procedures per week came to the education with a higher baseline knowledge and a greater interest in learning more. However, comfort in using these agents was similar for high injectors whether they were single- or multi-activity learners, and high injectors are using second-generation agents more frequently, even in the single-activity cohort. While these data suggest that increased comfort, more so than increased knowledge, is driving use of anti-VEGF agents, further research with matched samples and increased investigation of learner demographics would help interpret results.

Conclusion

The rapid evolution of therapeutic options for nAMD and DME, coupled with increasing patient volumes and resource constraints, underscores the need for efficient, up-to-date clinician education. By equipping providers with current evidence and practical strategies, curriculum-based CE can accelerate the adoption of innovations that may improve patient outcomes and reduce treatment burdens. The observed improvements in knowledge, comfort, and case-based decision-making suggest that such educational models can help bridge the gap between emerging science and real-world practice, ultimately benefiting both clinicians and patients. Collectively, these findings support the use of an intentional and longitudinal instructional design based on routine analytics and refinement of educational messaging to influence complex clinical decision-making.
