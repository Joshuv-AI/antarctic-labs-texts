Arteriovenous fistula presenting with progressive dyspnea and claudication: Clinical clues and surgical resolution
Authors: Vinojan Satchithanantham, Heseetha Thananchayan, Bavani Senthuran, Anton Jenil, Matheivaanan Sivalingam, Ketheeswary Sivasithamparam, Gajanthan Raveendran, Mahilrajan Ganesu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTraumatic arteriovenous fistulas often remain undiagnosed for extended periods, only becoming evident when complications arise.Case presentationA 65-year-old female presented with dyspnea and intermittent claudication of the left thigh and calf. She had a history of abdominal hysterectomy with critical care stay 30 years ago, likely involving femoral line insertion. Echocardiogram showed severe pulmonary hypertension and preserved ejection fraction. Examination revealed bruit in the left iliac fossa and groin. CT angiogram (CTA) showed a superficial femoral artery-vein fistula, external iliac vein aneurysm, and dilated inferior vena cava. Surgical repair was planned after MDT discussion. Under spinal anesthesia, retroperitoneal control of the external iliac artery was attempted but was difficult due to a large external iliac vein. Left groin was then explored, and control of the common femoral, superficial femoral, profunda femoris arteries and femoral vein was achieved. The fistulous tract was opened and repaired. Surgery was successful with the disappearance of the thrill and a favorable post-operative recovery.DiscussionProlonged critical care with probable femoral cannulation may have caused the arteriovenous fistula (AVF), though its location in the superficial femoral artery is slightly against. Here, AVF led to pulmonary hypertension, and steal syndrome with intermittent claudication. Chronic high-flow venous return caused an external iliac vein aneurysm. CTA confirmed the diagnosis, and surgical repair was preferred due to its location. Owing to pulmonary hypertension and poor tolerance to prolonged anesthesia, the venous aneurysm was left untreated.ConclusionsThis case underscores the need for timely diagnosis of AVFs.

Introduction

Arteriovenous fistula is an abnormal connection between artery and vein which can be congenital or acquired [1]. Acquired arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) can be categorized as either surgically created or resulting from vascular injury caused by trauma or medical procedures [2] including obtaining femoral access during cardiac catheterization and central venous line placements [1]. Most of them present late and the delayed diagnosis will result in development of a pseudoaneurysm, neuropathy, skin ulceration, chronic limb swelling, thromboembolic sequelae, limb loss, fistula rupture with hemorrhage, and cardiac overload with subsequent high output cardiac failure or endocarditis [3], [4].

Digital subtraction angiography, duplex and color Doppler ultrasonography, as well as CT or MR angiography, can be utilized for diagnosis [3]. Management options include percutaneous endovascular techniques or open surgical procedures [4].

Case presentation

A 65-year-old female presented with progressive dyspnea and disabling intermittent claudication involving her left thigh and calf affecting the quality of life. Her medical history included a total abdominal hysterectomy complicated with stay in the critical care 30 years ago, during which she would have undergone femoral line insertion. Last couple of years she was diagnosed to have severe pulmonary hypertension with preserved left ventricular function. On examination, her abdomen was soft with a lower midline scar. There was a significant bruit in the left iliac fossa and the groin. There was no obvious scar over the left groin. Left leg was mildly swollen with palpable pulses. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed an arteriovenous fistula between the proximal superficial femoral artery and the superficial femoral vein, (just distal to the origin) along with external iliac vein aneurysm (Fig. 1) and significant dilation of the inferior vena cava.Fig. 1External Iliac vein aneurysm in CTA.Fig. 1

This case was discussed in the vascular MDT and was decided to proceed with the surgical repair as it is better than a covered stent in the groin. The surgery was performed under spinal anesthesia. Initially it was attempted to achieve external iliac artery control in the retroperitoneal region but as the vein was very large it was found to be difficult to get into the artery. So, the left groin was explored with control of the common femoral, superficial femoral and profunda femoris arteries (Fig. 2). Subsequently femoral vein control also was achieved. Once the control is achieved the fistulous tract was opened up and the hole was repaired with 4 0 polypropylene. Disappearance of the thrill confirmed the successful repair. The patient was subsequently transferred to the surgical ICU and showed a favorable postoperative recovery. Her respiratory symptoms improved significantly following the operation. Follow up

During the one year follow up period, the patient showed significant clinical improvement, with a gradual reduction in the frequency and severity of claudication symptoms. Further her walking distance and exercise tolerance improved.

Discussion

This case highlights a complex presentation of an AVF following vascular trauma, with cardiovascular manifestations, ultimately leading to its diagnosis. This lady underwent abdominal hysterectomy 30 years ago and it led to prolonged stay in the critical care. She lost the clinical records and could not remember the events. She would have undergone femoral cannulation during that period. But the site of the fistula is slightly against, as it is in the origin of superficial femoral artery. When a patient presents with cardiac instability of uncertain aetiology with a previous history of an invasive procedure, one should carefully look for pulsatile mass, murmur, or thrill over the scars, and the development of varicosities in the affected extremity [2]. Nicoladoni-Branham sign could be elicited which will reveal the disappearance of bruit and thrill and normalize the pulse and heart rate when pressure is applied to the proximal part of the feeding artery [2]. According to a study from Germany, being female may increase the risk of developing an arteriovenous fistula after vascular puncture, as women generally have smaller vessel diameters. [4], [5]. In our patient AVF caused haemodynamic instability by increasing the preload, leading to pulmonary hypertension and severe respiratory symptoms. Furthermore, steal syndrome, caused by blood shunting away from superficial femoral artery, led to intermittent claudication. Chronic high-flow venous circulation likely contributed to the aneurysmal dilation of the external iliac vein.

Definitive diagnosis was achieved through imaging modalities, with CT angiography effectively delineating the vascular structures and fistula. Even though spontaneous closure has been reported in 30% of femoral AVF [4], our patient developed complications, necessitating an intervention. Treatment options for AVFs include endovascular embolization, covered stenting and surgical repair. Given this fistula was located in the groin, surgical repair was thought to be better than placing a covered stent. We did not want to repair the external iliac vein aneurysm as this patient had severe pulmonary hypertension and might not tolerate prolonged surgery and anesthesia.

After the surgery usually, a rapid resolution of cardiac symptoms will be noted [2] as our patient's respiratory symptoms improved and she is currently clinically well.

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of recognizing AVFs as a potential cause of unexplained cardiovascular symptoms. Early diagnosis and timely intervention are essential to prevent long-term complications such as heart failure and pulmonary hypertension, ultimately improving patient outcomes.
