Enhanced dust reduction method for solar panels application
Authors: Pooya Hooshyar, Ali Moosavi, Ali Nouri Borujerdi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introducing an innovative dual-layer coating technique to enhance solar panel durability against dust, this method uses a translucent aluminum zinc oxide conductive film to prevent accumulation through active dust repulsion. A secondary TiO2-infused coating, applied via a cost-effective sol-gel method, boosts anti-soiling capabilities in a passive manner. Comprehensive tests on dust accumulation, self-cleaning efficiency, mechanical robustness, UV-VIS transmission, and chemical resilience reveal promising results. These coatings improve glass clarity, reduce dust adhesion, and maintain energy production even in calm conditions. The effectiveness relies on the precise concentrations of aluminum nitrate and Titania nanoparticles, with annealing temperature affecting transmission rates. The coatings demonstrate highly desired resistance to both alkaline and acidic environments, ensuring consistent performance across various settings. This durability supports the widespread adoption of solar energy in diverse climates, advancing global sustainable energy efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-81183-7.

Introduction

During the upward rate of energy consumption over the globe, a suitable substitute for fossil fuels became an enormous global concern due to the shortage of fossil energy resources1,2. As a logical necessity, any alternative energy source must not only meet global demand but also prioritize human and environmental well-being. Solar energy stands out as a promising solution, significantly cutting CO2 emissions while being widely accessible. Its appeal lies in its environmental and economic advantages, making it the most cost-effective energy source. As a result, the global adoption of solar panels is rapidly increasing, highlighting their effectiveness and sustainability in addressing energy needs3. Indeed, a notable challenge in the implementation of solar panels is the daily accumulation of dust particles on their surfaces. These particles form a stubborn barrier that obstructs sunlight from reaching the photovoltaic (PV) surface, leading to a consequential drop in efficiency for solar cells. Lakashimi and Ramadas4studied the efficiency decline of coated solar panels due to soiling, with a concentration of 52 g/cm². The soiling materials included chalk, brick, coal, and sand. Their results showed that coal and sand caused the most significant efficiency losses, averaging 60% and 45% under 220 W/m² irradiation. However, when the irradiation doubled, the efficiency losses were reduced to 33% and 35%. Additionally, the abrasive dust particles, due to their hardness differences from the PV surface, can create scratches. These scratches scatter light, further reducing panel performance by deflecting sunlight from its absorption points5.

The methods devised to remove particles from the surface of solar cells are also categorized into two categories, active and passive. The first category includes high-cost parts which also are not economical due to their high-cost maintenance approaches. Washing with water that needs water to be used to wash the surface of solar panels. Due to the water crisis and high water consumption, washing solar panels is not recommended. Based on a recent investigation conducted by Lohrmann et al.6, electricity generation is heavily reliant on water usage, particularly for cleaning photovoltaic modules. The research shows that the availability of water resources is diminishing, making it imperative to find an alternative method for cleaning solar panels without water. Additionally, humidity exacerbates the issue of dust accumulation by promoting the coagulation of particles, further exacerbating the efficiency reduction in solar panels and solar sites. While manual cleaning methods such as rubbing the solar panels may seem intuitive, they often exacerbate the problem. The act of rubbing can intensify scratches on the surface, compromising long-term usage and integrity. Furthermore, these actions can introduce micro/nano scratches on the photovoltaic (PV) modules, serving as additional sources of light scattering. These scratches alter the angle of incidence of light, disrupting the optimal absorption and conversion of solar energy by the PV module. As Panat et al.7 utilized an electrostatic field to remove dust particles from solar panels – which is based on the inherent inducted electrostatic charge on the dust particles due to collision with each other and surfaces- it is evident that the electrostatic dust removal approach is an effective dust removal methodology. They used an electrical field that was deployed between a glass substrate and an aluminum sheet to remove dust particles from the substrate. As was mentioned, it is also necessary to know that as the dust particles are carried around by the wind, they get inducted electrical charge due to being rubbed into each other or other surfaces. The passive methods include a coating introduction on the solar panels and substrates. Using an anti-soiling coating would lead to a significant reduction in dust particles accumulation on the surface, meanwhile, alleviating the total transmission of the surface due to their anti-reflectivity properties. Many researchers have studied the impacts of active approaches. Al-Badra et al.8 took advantage of using a vibrator on solar panels to vibrate the module to remove dust particles. Kawamoto et al.9 also performed an electrostatic field by using electrical wiring beneath the solar surface to induct an electrical charge on dust particles to remove them from a tilted solar surface. As was reviewed by Ilse et al.10 the covalent bonding between settled dust particles and beneath substrate and also Vander Waals force as was regarded by Isaifan et al.11. It is widely postulated by other researchers some forces are acting between the settled dust particles and solar panels that are trying to stick the dust particles to the solar panels10,12,13. It is believed that using an anti-soiling coating would remove dust particles more easily due to the inherent feature of these coatings. Therefore, it is thought that the mentioned forces decrease when anti-soiling coatings are used. Completing the previous fact, Borah et al.14 found out that the soiling loss and its pattern vary with dust particle composition.

Another defining parameter of anti-soiling coating performance is surface morphology and the dust particle’s average diameter. This statement was fully confirmed by Bahattab et al.15. They investigated that as the size of dust particles decreases, the soiling effect and transmission loss on solar panels and substrates increases. The investigation that regarded the effects of four different sizes of dust particles in which that the fine size of dust particles was more effective. Dahlioui et al.16 investigated the water condensation impact on solar panels’ soiling losses. They concluded that by increasing the capillary force on solar panels for dust particles, particle adhesion got more severe (see Fig. 1). Conversely, in regions with sufficient precipitation, natural rainfall presents an effective solution for washing away dust particles from solar panels. It’s noteworthy that anti-reflectivity stands out as a desirable trait in thin films, wherein the reduction of solar light irradiance reflection occurs. This particular property plays a pivotal role in enhancing the efficiency of PV modules by minimizing the amount of sunlight reflected away from the surface, thus optimizing light absorption and energy conversion. As reviewed by Shanmugam et al.17 in their study, the anti-reflective property of thin films can be achieved by texturizing the surface. Another criterion discussed in their study was the gradual change of the refractive index (n), which represents the ratio of the velocity of light on a medium to that in air.

It is important to note that other studies involving active dust repulsion from PV surfaces did not assess the impact of using an additional anti-soiling coating on the conductive films. Guo et al.18 performed electrical dust repulsion aided with EDS (Electrodynamic Dust Shield) in which an electrodynamic cover was developed beneath a polymer dielectric cover. Their study led to a 16–33% enhancement in their results. Polizos et al.19 developed a hybrid anti-soiling coating that demonstrated a 39% improvement in dust repelling compared to an uncoated substrate. Their research concluded that there was no combined benefit of electrostatic and anti-soiling effects for use in the optical industry. Ota et al.20adopted a similar approach, examining the effects of an anti-soiling coating applied to a polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) substrate, which inherently possesses electrostatic dust repulsion capabilities. However, PMMA substrates are not commonly used in the solar panel industry. Their report indicates that this substrate is widely accepted as the base material for Fresnel lenses in concentrator photovoltaics (CPs). A thorough review of the existing literature reveals that research on anti-soiling coatings and electrostatic dust removal strategies has primarily focused on each approach separately, neglecting the potential benefits of their combined use. This study aims to fill that gap by investigating the synergistic effects of integrating both passive and active dust mitigation techniques on solar panels. Through the application of a double-functional coating onto a glass substrate, this research endeavors to unlock the enhanced efficiency and dust removal capabilities that can result from the convergence of these distinct approaches. It is worth noting that these coatings amalgamate the benefits of each other, fostering synergistic performance and presenting intriguing possibilities for diverse applications in relevant contexts21. This entails the application of a transparent conductive coating for active dust repulsion and an anti-soiling coating for passive dust removal. In contrast to previous studies using expensive methods to apply transparent conductive films for repelling dust from solar panels7, we utilized an optimized sol-gel technique for cost-effective coating production. Our rough double-layer coating, developed in this study, exhibits anti-reflection properties according to both criteria identified by Shanmugam et al.17 that as the refraction index gradually reduces, the textures of the anti-soiling coating - which initially receives light irradiation - enhance the anti-reflection ability of the coated samples.

Materials and methods

Materials

Titania solution with approximate 25 nm diameter particles was purchased from Unipolymat, Iran. Zinc acetate, aluminum nitrate, TEOS, monoethanol, diethanolamin, isopropanol, sulfuric acid, and sodium hydroxide were purchased from Merck. HCL (Hydrochloric acid) with 37% purity, was also obtained from Merck. Deionized water (DI water) was also supplied by domestic suppliers.

Conductive solution preparation

6.4 g of Al(NO3)3 was dissolved in 12 g of isopropanol with magnetic stirring at 30 °C, yielding solution (A). Then, 10 g of Diethanolamine was mixed with isopropanol to create a 0.2 molar solution, named (B), and stirred at 70 °C for 2 h. During the stirring, 30 g of Zinc acetate was gradually added over 10 min to create solution (C). Next, 4 g of solution (A) and 10 g of solution (C) were combined with solution (B) to achieve a weight ratio of Al to Zn of 0.2, followed by stirring for 2 h at 70 °C, resulting in solution (D). Finally, solution (D) was dip-coated onto soda-lime glass, with one side of the coated substrate washed with distilled water to prevent a double effect on light transmission. The schematic of the process of conductive solution preparation is presented in figure S.1.

Silica-based anti-soil solution preparation

9 g of TEOS was stirred with 23.8 g of methanol on a magnetic stirrer at room temperature for 1 h, yielding solution (F). Separately, 0.5 g of HCl was added to 5 g of DI water and stirred for 1 h at room temperature to create solution (G). Next, solution (F) was combined with 0.82 g of G and stirred at 45 °C for 24 h to produce solution (H). Subsequently, 9 g of TiO2 sol was stirred with 70 g of methanol at room temperature for 1 h to obtain solution (I). Solution (H) was then added dropwise to solution (I) while stirring to form solution (J). Finally, solution (J) was dip-coated onto a prepared sample previously coated with a light-transmitting conductive coating. As before, one side of the coated glass was washed with distilled water to prevent interference with light transmission. It is worth mentioning that the TiO2 nanoparticles do not contribute to the reaction in this process. The schematic of the process of this anti-soiling solution preparation is presented in figure S.2.

Glass substrate preparation

Glass substrates with the dimensions of 3 × 10 cm were washed with acetone and DI water. The plates were dried in an oven at 100 °C. Following the cleaning process, the glass substrates were treated with piranha solution (a mixture of concentrated sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) in approximately a 3:1 ratio) for 4 h to prepare them for the optimal coating process. The cleaning procedure was then repeated.

Film preparation

Anti-soiling film

Dip-coating method was deployed to introduce the film on the substrate with the down/upward speed of 5 cm/min. The dip-coated samples were dried in an oven at 100 °C, 120 °C, and 140 °C.

Conductive film

Dip-coating method was also used to apply the film on the substrate with the down/upward speed of 5 cm/min. the dip-coated samples were dried in an oven at 120 °C, 140 °C, and 160 °C. Figure 2 displays the films that have been deposited on the lime-glass substrate.

Test methods

Abrasion test

The test method was conducted to assess the adhesion of the prepared coating, a concern highlighted by several researchers22. To simulate the abrading condition in natural situations which is mainly based on dust particle movement (that is induced by the wind blowing, etc.…) on solar panels, as the same test was carried away by Li et al.23, Sandpaper (silicon sandpaper P600) is used for this purpose. To simulate real conditions, a 600-gram weight was put on samples with an anti-soiling coating, imitating normal surface forces like wind and gravity. A setup was used to scratch the samples in 50 cm cycles, repeated from one to eight times – as depicted in Fig. 3. Many studies from the literature review were used to conduct this test. Fakhri et al.24 conducted the same test to evaluate a superhydrophobic coating used for drag reduction. According to their study, the coating could resist up to 200 cm abrasion. Also, San Vicente et al.5 discovered that introducing an anti-soiling coating on glass samples would increase the mechanical resistance of glass. The scratches could increase the reflecting coefficient of substrate which leads to a significant efficiency reduction of solar panels.

It is important to know that as the scratches on solar panels increase in size and quantity, the dust adhesion becomes more obvious and observable. The free-body diagram of a dust particle is presented in Fig. 4, aiming to clarify the forces inserted into a dust particle. Roughness significands the effect of electrostatic force, ion force, and covalent force and makes dust repulsion more severe, because as the contact area of dust particles with the substrate increases, the aforementioned forces also increase. In the context of introducing the coatings discussed in this article, the external electrostatic force is strengthened due to the presence of the transmitter conductive oxide, thereby resulting in more efficient detachment of particles from the substrate. Additionally, when employing the anti-soiling coating, the forces causing dust particles to adhere to the substrate are diminished. In each case, the equilibrium of the forces gets disturbed leading to the particles moving and repelling from the substrate.

Ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy (UV-VIS) test

The experiment utilized a UV-VIS device to subject the surface to waves of diverse wavelengths, measuring sample transmission as the ratio of waves passing through to those exposed. This method scrutinized the transmission of glass samples coated with an anti-soiling coating, bare glass, and conductive light transmitter-coated samples. Specifically, it probed the impact of TiO2 and Aluminum nitrate dopants on the coatings’ transmission properties. Additionally, the investigation delved into the influence of annealing temperature variations on transmission. Through meticulous examination via UV-VIS testing, insights were gleaned into the nuanced interplay of these factors, elucidating their contributions to the overall performance and effectiveness of the coatings.

Soiling test

The coated samples were carefully placed in a custom-built soiling chamber specifically designed for this testing procedure. An 8-liter chamber utilized a high-speed impeller spinning at 200 rpm to create the perfect conditions for the experiment. With precision, 10 g of fine dust particles were introduced through a strategically placed hole above the impeller, creating a swirling cloud of dust within the isolated environment. After allowing the dust to settle for 5 min, the soiled samples were removed from the chamber and meticulously cleaned using pressurized air at 10 bars, ensuring a thorough and precise cleaning process. The test was done in 3 cycles. A graphical representation of the experimental setup can be observed in Fig. 5.

The dust particles utilized in the experiment were Kaolin, with diameters ranging from 400 nm to 10 μm. According to the research conducted by Alkharusi et al.25, larger dust particles are more easily removed. Consequently, it is the finer dust particles that contribute significantly to the overall soiling.

Chemical abrasion

In solar sites predominantly situated in urban and arid regions, frequent exposure to precipitation poses a significant challenge. This environmental phenomenon accelerates the degradation of thin films and coatings, rendering them less functional over time. To assess the durability of the produced anti-soiling coating under similar conditions, Yi et al.26conducted evaluations involving soaking tests in various solutions with differing pH levels. In this study, the anti-soiling coated samples underwent soaking in solutions such as ammonium, sodium hydroxide, locally supplied water, DI water, and phosphoric acid for 4 h. Subsequently, the transmission loss of these treated samples was compared with that of non-soaked samples following a soiling test conducted in a controlled chamber. This methodology mirrors the approach adopted by Yi et al.26, providing a comprehensive evaluation of the coating’s resistance to degradation under realistic environmental conditions26. Other researchers have also investigated how the resistance of the coated samples compares to that of uncoated ones when exposed to corrosive environments27.

Self-cleaning test

Photocatalytic degradation leads to the breakdown of pigments found in contaminants on surfaces. This process occurs through the exchange of electrons between the contaminants and the TiO2 present in the coating. In the conducted experiment, 1 cc of methylene blue solution at a concentration of 80 ppm was applied to three types of samples: one coated with both a light-transmitting conductive film and an anti-soiling layer, one uncoated, and one coated solely with a TiO2 solution with the same concentration provided in the anti-soiling coating. All samples were placed in a dark chamber equipped with a 160 W A-type UV lamp. After six hours, the transmission loss of the samples was measured and compared to their initial losses using UV-VIS testing.

Electrical dust repelling test

During the experiment, three samples underwent a 7 kV.m uniform electrical field exposure for 30 s: bare glass, glass coated with an anti-soiling coating and a light transmitter conductive film, and glass with a conductive light transmitter coating underneath and an anti-soiling coating on top. Images were captured post-repulsion to evaluate the ratio of the dust-covered area to the total area for each sample. The coatings were instrumental in maintaining field uniformity near the glass plate. Typically, encountering a nonconductive material causes electrical field lines to diverge, diminishing effectiveness, but the coatings countered this, improving field efficiency28. This divergence hampers the electrical field’s effectiveness, causing a net repulsion of dust particles. The glass substrate’s inherent nonconductivity exacerbates this issue, leading to inefficient dust removal. However, applying an aluminum zinc oxide (AZO)-based coating markedly diminishes this deviation to a fraction of its original magnitude. The experimental setup featured an aluminum sheet and holder, allowing for an adjustable distance between the sheet and the coated substrate via a screw and nut mechanism.

Water contact angle test

A water contact angle test is commonly used to evaluate coating surface energy. As reported by Drelich1, surfaces can be categorized into four groups based on water contact angle: superhydrophobic, hydrophobic, hydrophilic, and superhydrophilic, with increasing water contact angle. In light of this classification, the water contact angle test was conducted on three types of surfaces: bare glass, glass coated with an anti-soiling coating, and glass coated with a light transmitter conductive coating containing AZO nanoparticles. Using the Jikan-CAG-20 apparatus, 1 µl of DI water was dropped onto each coated substrate to measure the water contact angle. To minimize measurement error, the water contact angle was measured at five different points on each sample, and the average of these measurements was reported in the study.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) test

This test is performed to evaluate the coating quality on the glass substrate by Using the TESCAN 3 system from the Czech Republic. Each sample was covered with a thin gold layer (containing nanoparticles with an average diameter of 20 nm) to enhance the electrical conductivity for capturing better SEM images.

Results and discussion

The main goal of this article is to examine samples coated with a double-layered system. As a result, tests for self-cleaning, mechanical abrasion, chemical resistance, and electrical dust repulsion were conducted to assess the combined benefits of both layers. This research aims to develop a coating that actively and passively repels dust, adjusts to refractive index changes, enhances resistance to corrosive environments, improves scratch durability, and employs a simple production method. These objectives present innovative contributions that have not been addressed in previous studies.

UV-VIS test results

The transmission of coated glass samples is influenced by factors such as annealing temperature, dip-coating cycles, and dopant concentration (specifically, TiO2 nanoparticles for the anti-soiling substrate and aluminum nitrate for the light-transmitting conductive film). Test results indicated that adding 0.3 g of TiO2 improved substrate transmission by 1%, with the film thickness ranging from 450 nm to 500 nm. The coating comprised particles with an average size of 49 nm, and the overall film thickness was approximately 470 nm, as determined using the Beer-Lambert law29–31. The average transmission of coated glass in the range of 300 to 1100 nm is 90.23%, compared to 89.12% for bare glass. Coated lime glass transmits 90.34% of light at 450 nm, while bare glass transmits 89.56%. To ensure uniformity in the coating, the Beer-Lambert law was utilized. The SEM image of the coating was divided into 800 identical sections, with the light intensity of each section applied in the Beer-Lambert law. This approach yielded a maximum deviation from the average thickness of only 5%. Figure 6 presents the results of the numerical calculation of film thickness using the Beer-Lambert law.

The transmittance of the coated samples surpasses that of the uncoated glasses for two main reasons. First, there is a gradual transition in the refractive index from air to the glass substrate. By incorporating aluminum nitrate into the solution for the conductive film at ratios of 1%, 2%, and 3% relative to the zinc weight, it was determined that the 1% ratio provided the highest transmission. As a result, the annealing temperature experiment was conducted exclusively on the coating with a 1% aluminum-to-zinc ratio. According to Refs8,32–44, as the annealing temperature increases, the transmission on the quartz substrate rises to about 95% because the nanoparticles align along the (0 0 2) direction. This study tested annealing temperatures of 100 °C, 120 °C, and 160 °C, with 120 °C being the best (as shown in Fig. 8). At this temperature, the grain size of the nanoparticles increases, but the orientation changes more slowly, resulting in a slight decrease in transmission. It is important to note that at temperatures above 200 °C, the coating detached from the surface due to mismatched thermal expansion coefficients between the coating and the glass substrate. Thus, the annealing temperature was kept below this limit. Additional data is provided by Jamshidi et al.45.

The anti-soiling test result

The soiling test was performed in the presence of 37% relative humidity (RH) which accounts for room RH. This RH is undoubtedly lower in desserts or equal to it46, as the locations in which solar sites are located47. The test results of the experiment were compared with uncoated samples. The increased surface roughness of the glass due to the superhydrophilic coating led to better dust mitigation on the coated samples, as roughness plays a crucial role in reducing dust accumulation on surfaces48. This superhydrophilicity promotes water accumulation on the substrate, increasing capillary forces. However, at low relative humidity levels, the coated samples showed significantly reduced soiling losses. Self-cleaning test results

In the aforementioned test, the self-cleaning performance of coated samples was compared to that of a bare glass sample and a coated sample with a titania solution containing the same concentration of TiO2 as the anti-soiling coating. The results showed that bare glass experienced greater transmission loss compared to the anti-soiling coated sample, which, in turn, had higher transmission loss than the titania-coated sample. This indicates that the presence of silica particles can reduce the contact area between methylene blue and TiO2 nanoparticles, thereby decreasing the effectiveness of the titania photocatalytic reaction. The transmission achieved through photocatalytic reactions has the potential to significantly improve the performance of polluted solar panels by optimizing the relationship between absorbed light and the panels’ output50.

As depicted in Fig. 11, a 23% transmission gap is observed between both coatings, primarily attributed to the inclusion of silica nanoparticles in the coating. These silica nanoparticles are thought to diminish the contact area between TiO2 nanoparticles and methylene blue, resulting in reduced transmission recovery. To achieve full transmission recovery, it is necessary to expose the coated sample to UV irradiation for a longer duration than previously required.

Water contact angle test

Surfaces are arranged in four different categories based on their water contact angle. Ahmad et al.54 have discussed that the surfaces that possess water contact angle (WCA) < 5° the surface are called superhydrophilic and for 5°< WCA < 90° the surface is called hydrophilic. In the tests, the water contact angle of coatings could not be measured by set-up due to the lower quantity of its precision. So, WCA was measured using image processing software. The WCA of the anti-soiling coated sample was 4° and the other one was 26°; thus, the coatings were assumed to be superhydrophilic and hydrophilic, respectively. Chemical resistance test

In this test, all samples underwent immersion in basic/acidic solutions with different pH levels for four hours. The findings revealed that the double-folded coating (a coating applied on a sample previously coated with AZO before applying the titania-based coating) exhibited better resistance in basic solutions, likely due to the coating’s nanoparticle solution being in a basic state. After soaking, the samples were exposed to soiling in a chamber under different cycles. Following this, the soiled samples were tested using UV-VIS spectroscopy. In this test, the opaque nature of dust particles means that higher UV-VIS test results indicate fewer dust particles remaining on the sample. This is because the remaining dust blocks light transmission through the glass. Thus, the UV-VIS test results reflect the effectiveness of the coating’s anti-soiling properties. Interestingly, the coating soaked in sodium hydroxide exhibited higher transmission compared to the non-soaked one, possibly due to the enhanced coating roughness in that solution. Heat resistance test

As was described previously, the samples were introduced to 60°c temperature for a 6 h duration. The test results showed a 2% transmission reduction meanwhile the anti-soiling functionality stayed in a constant quality. This occurs due to the transformation of the anatase phase of TiO2crystals into the rutile phase56. Manickam et al.56 and Tian et al.57 also found out that the nano particles’ average diameter increases as the annealing temperature increases which is another reason for the transmission reduction of the coating, they also suggested that the reduction in anxiety energy of valence layer electrons corresponds to the improved self-cleaning performance.

Electrostatic dust removal

The application of a conductive coating can significantly augment the electromagnetic field in proximity to the substrate, enhancing its overall electromagnetic performance. This phenomenon can be understood through the fundamental principles of electromagnetism, particularly the behavior of electric field lines when they interact with different materials. When these field lines encounter non-conductive materials, they tend to diverge or spread out, leading to a notable reduction in electric field strength in that area. This reduction occurs because non-conductive materials do not allow free movement of electric charges, which means that the electric field can’t maintain its intensity as it would in conductive materials. In contrast, conductive coatings provide a path for electric charges to move freely, allowing the field lines to remain more concentrated and directed. This concentration of electric field lines near the surface of the conductive coating enhances the local electric field strength. Thus, the strategic application of conductive coatings not only mitigates the adverse effects of non-conductive substrates but also enables enhanced performance in electrical dust mitigation58,59. Therefore, in the case of glass, which is inherently non-conductive, the electrostatic field required for the suppression of dust particles tends to get weaker. Conversely, the presence of a conductive coating mitigates this decrease in the electrostatic field, thereby maintaining a more stable field strength. Panat et al.7used a 12 KV electromagnetic field at a distance of 1.5 cm and also their produced coating was deposited on the substrate via an expensive method of atomic layer deposition (ALD). However, the sol-gel-produced coating demonstrated an impressive ability to remove approximately 97% of dust from the coated sample, whereas a test on bare glass removed only about 70% of dust, calculated using an image processing code. The electromagnetic field applied was 7 KV, with an operating time of 30 s from a distance of 10 cm. This test specifically focused on data related to electrostatic dust repulsion, excluding other repellent methods. The efficient dust removal capability was achieved through a low-temperature annealing method, rendering the coating procedure more economically viable compared to previous research on light transmitter conductive coatings. Notably, the coating is cost-effective. The newly introduced light transmitter conductive oxide film demonstrated impressive effectiveness with a 97% dust repellency rate under a 7 KV external electrical field, notably lower than the voltage requirement in the referenced study7. In addition to its dust-repellent properties, this bi-functional coating offered the advantage of enhancing the transmission of the underlying glass substrate by incorporating an anti-soiling thin film. By integrating an anti-reflective feature, it effectively minimized unwanted reflections, enhancing the overall optical clarity. Moreover, the coating served a dual purpose as a protective barrier against dust particles when exposed to external forces like wind. This dual functionality rendered it highly suitable for applications in environments where dust accumulation is a significant concern. It is important to note that additional research is needed to reduce the voltage required for dust repulsion in electrical dust mitigation methods. While the current study has managed to lower this electrical voltage through coating process optimization, further reductions are necessary for larger applications, like solar farms. However, the improved electrical field may be suitable for smaller uses, such as residential solar panels. Additionally, the primary costs associated with these applications appear to be related to setup and maintenance, while energy consumption remains relatively low60,61.

Conclusion

A new bi-functional thin film was created by improving an anti-soiling coating with TiO2 nanoparticles, which significantly enhanced its effectiveness. The film also included an aluminum/zinc-based conductive layer designed to transmit light while enabling electrostatic dust removal. This dust removal process was achieved through low-temperature annealing, making the coating method more affordable than earlier studies. Notably, the conductive oxide layer achieved a 97% dust repellency rate with the use of just a 7 KV external electrical field, a considerably lower voltage compared to previous research. Furthermore, the electrical field showed strong dust removal capabilities, even at greater distances.

The coating demonstrated excellent resistance to both mechanical wear and chemical degradation, particularly in environments with pH levels higher than 7, ensuring its durability under typical solar field conditions. It also maintained its structure under fluctuating temperatures, which helped improve transmission and minimize reflections, thus enhancing optical clarity. Acting as a protective layer, the coating effectively prevented dust accumulation, making it particularly suitable for dusty environments. Experiments on different doping agent concentrations revealed that the coating successfully eliminated fine dust particles and outperformed bare glass in terms of dust removal efficiency.

The conductive layer efficiently repelled dust even without external assistance, though natural forces like wind and gravity further boosted its performance. The coating maintained optimal dust-repellent properties while achieving high light transmission, thanks to the integration of both active and passive dust mitigation methods. Its self-cleaning characteristics, durability, and low production cost make it a highly practical solution for solar applications. The coating improved light transmission by 1% and increased dust repellency by 27% using a 7 KV electrostatic field. Additionally, the double-coated sample resisted damage from solutions with pH values ranging from 4 to 14.

In conclusion, the hybrid coating presented in this study partially enhanced light transmission while effectively reducing dust buildup through a combination of passive and active strategies. This advanced level of dust reduction was achieved without sacrificing transparency, representing a substantial improvement over previously available coating.
