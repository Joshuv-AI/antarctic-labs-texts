IDP for 2-Partition Maximal Symmetric Polytopes
Su Ji Hong, George D. Nasr

Abstract
. We provide a framework for which one can approach showing the integer decomposition property for symmetric polytopes. We utilize this framework to prove a special case which we refer to as 2 2 -partition maximal polytopes in the case where it lies in a hyperplane of ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} . Our method involves proving a special collection of polynomials have saturated Newton polytope.



1.
Introduction

We say a convex polytope is a
lattice polytope
whenever it is the convex hull of integer points. Lattice polytopes have been studied under many different settings, such as mathematical optimization
[
TOG17
]
, projective toric varieties
[
Ful93
,
CLS24
]
, and tropical geometry
[
KKE21
]
. This work concerns that relating to the integer decomposition property (IDP). This property has been studied for a variety of reasons. For example, it often is intertwined with the study of Ehrhart polynomials for polytopes.

A common case of study is the Newton polytope of a polynomial. In this setting, one can ask if the corresponding polynomial has saturated Newton polytope (SNP). There is an established history of finding polynomials which are SNP—a survey can be found in
[
MTY19
]
. A classic example of this includes Schur polynomials, which will be relevant for this paper.

As is often the case, demonstrating that a polynomial has SNP in turn can be used to argue that the corresponding Newton polytope has IDP. This technique has been used more frequently in recent years—for instance, one can see
[
BGH+21
]
for a study on Schur polynomials and inflated symmetric Grothendieck polynomials, which
[
NNTDLH23
]
generalized this and other results by defining what they call
good symmetric functions
. Both cases leverage the SNP property of a polynomial to prove a corresponding Newton polytope has IDP.

The work of
[
NNTDLH23
]
involved adding together Schur functions, which has a symmetric Newton polytope. When we say a polytope
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
symmetric
, we mean that for any for any permutation
\pi
on
n
elements, we have
\pi\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}
, where
\pi
acts on the coordinates of points in
\mathcal{P}
. This leads us to wonder the following.

Problem 1
.

Do all symmetric lattice polytopes have IDP? If not, which ones do?

In this report, we focus on a special case of the aforementioned problem when the polytopes are what we call
2-partition maximal polytopes
. For now, we wait to define these until the the next section. By using the aforementioned strategy, we identify the symmetric polynomials whose sum gives rise to 2-partition maximal symmetric polytopes and then prove the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

If a 2-partition maximal symmetric polytope is contained in a hyperplane of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, then it has IDP.

We will provide necessary terminology and background in section
2
. In section
3
, we provide a general framework by which one can prove symmetric polytopes are IDP. Finally, in section
4
, we provide an outline for how we use the method from section
3
to prove Theorem
1.1
.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout we only work with
lattice polytopes
, which we have defined as the convex hull of integer points. We additionally define the following things for polytopes. Throughout,
\mathcal{P}
is a polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 2.1
.

•

The
dilation
of
\mathcal{P}
by
t\in\mathbb{N}
is

t\mathcal{P}:=\{tx:x\in\mathcal{P}\}.

•

\mathcal{P}
has the
integer decomposition property (IDP)
if for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in t\mathcal{P}
implies
\displaystyle x=\sum_{i=1}^{t}x_{i}
where
x_{i}\in\mathcal{P}
. The
x_{i}
need not be distinct.

•

The set
\operatorname{conv}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
is the convex hull of points
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}
.

•

Given a polynomial
\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}]
, the
support
of
f(x)
is

\operatorname{Supp}(f):=\{\alpha:c_{\alpha}\neq 0\}.

•

Given a polynomial
f(x)\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}]
, the
Newton Polytope
is

\operatorname{Newt}(f):=\operatorname{conv}(\operatorname{Supp}(f)).

•

We say a polynomial
f
has
saturated Newton polytope (SNP)
if every point in
\operatorname{Newt}(f)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is an exponent vector in
f
.

We will be interested in working with Schur polynomials and partitions, so we will additionally need the following. Throughout,
\lambda
and
\mu
are partitions.

Definition 2.2
.

•

Let
T
be a Young diagram for
\lambda
. A
semistandard
Young tableau

T
is a filling with entries from
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
that is weakly increasing along
rows and strictly increasing down columns.

•

The
content
of a semistandard Young tableau
T
is
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n})
where
\alpha_{i}
is the number of
i
’s appearing in
T
.

•

The
Schur polynomial

s_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}]
is

\displaystyle s_{\lambda}=\sum_{\alpha}K_{\lambda,\alpha}x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}x_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}

where
K_{\lambda,\alpha}
is the number of semistandard Young tableau of shape
\lambda
and content
\alpha
.

•

If
\lambda
and
\mu
are partitions with the same size, then the
dominance order

\trianglelefteq
, defined on partitions of the same size, is defined by
\lambda\trianglelefteq\mu
whenever
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i}
for all
k
. In this case, we say
\lambda
is
dominated
by
\mu
.

•

Given a symmetric polytope
\mathcal{P}
, let
\operatorname{LP}(\mathcal{P})
be the set of all lattice points of
\mathcal{P}
that are partitions and
\operatorname{MLP}(\mathcal{P})
be the set of pairwise
\trianglelefteq
-maximal partitions in
\operatorname{LP}(\mathcal{P})
.

•

For a polytope
\mathcal{P}
, we let
\operatorname{Ext}(\mathcal{P})
be the set of extreme points of
\mathcal{P}
.

We now define the polytopes of interest to this paper.

Definition 2.3
.

A symmetric polytope
\mathcal{P}
is
2-partition maximal
provided there are two partitions
\lambda
and
\mu
for which
\operatorname{MLP}(\mathcal{P})=\{\lambda,\mu\}
.

Example 2.4
.

Consider a symmetric polytope
\mathcal{P}
whose extreme points are permutations of
(10,2,1)
and
(7,6,0)
. See Figure

\mathcal{P}

s_{(10,2,2)}

(8,5,1)=\frac{7}{16}(10,2,2)+\frac{1}{16}(2,10,2)+\frac{1}{2}(7,7,0).

Furthermore,
\operatorname{MLP}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})=\{(10,2,2),(7,7,0),(8,5,1)\}

3.
Conjecture on Symmetric Polytopes and IDP

We now discuss how one can extend ideas in
[
BGH+21
,
NNTDLH23
]
for our purposes.
The authors of
[
BGH+21
,
NNTDLH23
]
used certain families of polynomials
s
which have SNP to show that
\operatorname{Newt}(s)
, which is symmetric, has IDP. To use these same strategies, we define
s
in the following way.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\displaystyle s:=\sum_{\lambda\in\operatorname{MLP}(\mathcal{P})}s_{\lambda}
. Then
\mathcal{P}=\operatorname{Newt}(s)
.

The definition of convex hull and Rado’s Theorem
[
Rad52
]
provides justification for why this Lemma holds, which we detail now. Given a symmetric polytope
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, if
\lambda
is a lattice point in
\mathcal{P}
, then so is
\pi(\lambda)
for all permutations
\pi
in
S_{m}
, the symmetric group on
m
elements. Then by the definition of Newton polytopes, we see that
\operatorname{Newt}(s_{\lambda})\subseteq\mathcal{P}
.
Thus, the support of
\displaystyle s=\sum_{\lambda\in\operatorname{LP}(\mathcal{P})}s_{\lambda}
contains all lattice points of
\mathcal{P}
, so
\mathcal{P}=\operatorname{Newt}(s)
. However, by Rado’s Theorem
[
Rad52
]
, we see that
\lambda\trianglelefteq\mu
if and only if
\operatorname{Newt}(s_{\lambda})\subseteq\operatorname{Newt}(s_{\mu})
. Thus, the support of
s
is equal to the support of
\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda\in\operatorname{MLP}(\mathcal{P})}s_{\lambda}
.

Returning now to our situation, we see that if
\lambda
and
\mu
are the partitions for a 2-partition maximal polytope
\mathcal{P}
and
s:=s_{\lambda}+s_{\mu}
, then
\operatorname{Newt}(s)=\mathcal{P}
.
To employ the methods of
[
BGH+21
,
NNTDLH23
]
, we will want to find a polynomial with SNP which is
t\mathcal{P}
for each choice of
t
. To this end, we define the following.

Definition 3.2
.

Given
k
partitions
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{k}
, not necessarily distinct, let
s:=s_{\lambda_{1}}+s_{\lambda_{2}}+\cdots+s_{\lambda_{k}}
. We define a new function
ts
, for
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. Let
K:=\{1,2,\dots,k\}
and
\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{K}{t}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)
be the set of multisubsets of
K
of size
t
. For
I\in\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{K}{t}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)
, let
\lambda_{I}:=\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}
. Then

ts:=\displaystyle\sum_{I\in\left(\kern-2.45836pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{K}{t}\right)\kern-2.45836pt\right)}s_{\lambda_{I}}.

Remark 3.3
.

Note when
k=2
,
ts
becomes

\sum_{i=0}^{t}s_{(t-i)\lambda_{1}+i\lambda_{2}}.

In general, we conjecture the following for this special choice of
ts
whenever
\mathcal{P}
is a symmetric polytope.

Conjecture 3.4
.

For
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, we have
\operatorname{Newt}(ts)=t\operatorname{Newt}(s)
and thus
t\mathcal{P}=\operatorname{Newt}(ts)
. Furthermore,
ts
has SNP for all
t
.

If this conjecture is true, then the set of lattice points of
t\mathcal{P}
is the support of
ts
. Then we can apply the following.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a symmetric polytope for which the prior conjecture holds true for
\displaystyle s=\sum_{\lambda\in\operatorname{MLP}(\mathcal{P})}s_{\lambda}
. (That is,
s
has SNP.) Then
\mathcal{P}
has the integer decomposition property.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a symmetric polytope for which the prior conjecture holds true. Let
t
be a positive integer. We want to show that every lattice points of
t\mathcal{P}
can be written as sum of
t
lattice points of
\mathcal{P}
. Let
\pi\in t\mathcal{P}
. Since
ts
has SNP, then
\pi
is an exponent vector of

ts=\displaystyle\sum_{I\in\left(\kern-2.45836pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{K}{t}\right)\kern-2.45836pt\right)}s_{\lambda_{I}},

that is, there is an
I:=\{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{t}\}
so that
\pi
is an exponent vector of
s_{\lambda_{i_{1}}+\lambda_{i_{2}}+\cdots+\lambda_{i_{t}}}
. Each exponent vector of
s_{\lambda_{i_{1}}+\lambda_{i_{2}}+\cdots+\lambda_{i_{t}}}
corresponds to a content vector of some semistandard tableau, which we denote
T
, for the partition
\lambda_{i_{1}}+\lambda_{i_{2}}+\cdots+\lambda_{i_{t}}
. We demonstrate that any such tableau can be decomposed into semistandard tableaux for
\lambda_{i_{1}}
,
\lambda_{i_{2}}
,
\dots
,
\lambda_{i_{t}}
, which we will denote
T_{i_{j}}
for
j
from
1
to
t
respectively. We define these using an inductive procedure, focusing on one row of each tableau at a time, starting with the last row for each.

Let
n
be the number of rows in
T
and let
(\lambda_{i_{j}})_{k}
denote the
k
th part of
\lambda_{i_{j}}
.
For each
j
, starting at
j=1
do the following.

(1)

Let
\ell
be the number of rows of
T
. Remove the first
(\lambda_{i_{j}})_{\ell}
columns from
T
and use them as the right-most columns of
T_{i_{j}}
. Note that these columns all have length
\ell
.

(2)

Subtract
(\lambda_{i_{j}})_{\ell}
from each entry of
\lambda_{i_{j}}
, truncating the last coordinate.

We now can repeat these steps by inducting on the length of the partitions to find the remaining columns for
T_{i_{j}}
.

Each
T_{i_{j}}
is a semistandard tableaux since columns of
T_{i_{j}}
come directly from columns of
T
and they are added from left-to-right.
Each
T_{i_{j}}
is also of shape
\lambda_{i_{j}}
. We prove this by inducting on the rows of
T_{i_{j}}
. Observe by step
1
of the above procedure that this is true for row
n
. After the first iteration of the two steps above is completed, note that the length of the partitions decreases. In general, suppose row
r
of
T_{i_{j}}
has
(\lambda_{i_{j}})_{r}
cells. Then the above procedure will add
(\lambda_{i_{j}})_{r-1}-(\lambda_{i_{j}})_{r}
columns from
T
to
T_{i_{j}}
, meaning that row
r-1
will have
(\lambda_{i_{j}})_{r-1}-(\lambda_{i_{j}})_{r}+(\lambda_{i_{j}})_{r}=(\lambda_{i_{j}})_{r-1}
cells. Furthermore, after the above steps are completed for row
r-1
,
T
will have length
r-2
, meaning that no further cells will be added to row
r-1
.

Consequently, each
T_{i_{j}}
has a shape that corresponds to
\lambda_{i_{j}}
, which means the content vector of
T_{i_{j}}
is a lattice point of
\mathcal{P}
since
s
has SNP. Thus
\pi
, which is the content vector of
T
, can be written as a sum of
t
lattice points of
\mathcal{P}
. This implies
\mathcal{P}
has IDP.

∎

As an example of an application for the prior Theorem, if
\mathcal{P}
is a symmetric polytope with two maximal lattice partitions so that Conjecture
3.4
holds, then each lattice points of

(t-k)\lambda+k\mu

Remark 3.7
.

One can apply the above framework to aforementioned results. For example, consider the work done by
[
BGH+21
]
. There, they define the symmetric Grothendieck polynomial
G_{\lambda}
of a partition
\lambda
and study
\mathcal{P}=\operatorname{Newt}(G_{\lambda})
. The polynomial
G_{\lambda}
is of the form

G_{\lambda}=\sum_{\mu}c_{\mu}s_{\mu}

where
c_{\mu}\in\mathbb{Z}
and the sum is over partitions
\mu
obtained by adding boxes to the Young diagram of
\lambda
. The partitions
\mu
in the above descriptions are not all maximal partitions. However, in
[
EY17
]
, they have shown that the
\mathcal{P}
is the convex hull of the support of
\displaystyle\sum_{\mu\in\operatorname{MLP}}s_{\mu}
.
In
[
BGH+21
]
, the authors showed that
\mathcal{P}
has IDP by observing that
t\mathcal{P}=\operatorname{Newt}(G_{t,t\lambda})
where
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
G_{t,t\lambda}
is the inflated symmetric Grothendieck polynomial. In addition, they showed that the
t\mathcal{P}
is the convex hull of the support of
\displaystyle\sum_{\mu\in\operatorname{MLP}(t\mathcal{P})}s_{\mu}
. Thus, one can define
s=\displaystyle\sum_{\mu}s_{\mu}
(with maximal lattice partition
\mu
) and apply the method outlined in this section to give an alternative proof that
\mathcal{P}
is IDP.

4.
2-Partition Maximal Polytopes and IDP

In this section we apply the method outlined in the prior section in our special case. Throughout,
\lambda
and
\mu
are pairwise maximal partitions corresponding to a 2-partition maximal polytope
\mathcal{P}
.
We will focus on the case where the size of
\lambda
and
\mu
are the same, leaving the other cases for future study.
Thus,
\mathcal{P}
lies in a hyperplane. We further restrict our study to the case where
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
, meaning that the length of
\lambda
and
\mu
is 3, as in this case we learn even more about the partitions
\lambda
and
\mu
.

Lemma 4.1
.

If the lengths of
\lambda
and
\mu
are 3 and both partitions have the same size, then

|\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i}|=1
for some
i\in\{1,2,3\}
.

Proof.

Suppose that we have
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})
and
\mu=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3})
so that
|\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i}|\geq 2
for all
i
. Without loss of generality, suppose
\lambda_{1}>\mu_{1}
. Thus, for
\lambda
and
\mu
to be maximal (and not comparable), we must have
\lambda_{3}>\mu_{3}
. Specifically, note that
\lambda_{3}>\mu_{3}+1
.

Now, let
\gamma:=(\mu_{1}+1,n-\mu_{1}-\lambda_{3},\lambda_{3}-1)
. Note that
\gamma
,
\lambda
, and
\mu
are pairwise maximal. We prove that
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}
.

First, consider the point
\iota:=(\mu_{1},n-\mu_{1}-\lambda_{3},\lambda_{3})
. Observe that
\iota\in\mathcal{P}
since
\mu
and
\lambda
dominate
\iota
. Thus, it is sufficient to show that
\gamma\in\operatorname{conv}(\lambda,\mu,\iota)
. To this end, we define a matrix
A=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&\mu&\iota\end{bmatrix}
, that is, a
3\times 3
matrix whose columns are
\lambda
,
\mu
, and
\iota
. We will demonstrate that
A^{-1}
exists and
A^{-1}\gamma
has non-negative entries which sum to
1
.

To proceed, we use Sage’s Matrix class to perform the necessary computations. These can be seen in Appendix
A
.
First, the determinant of
A
is

\big(\lambda_{3}(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2})-\mu_{3}(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})\big)(\lambda_{1}-\mu_{1})=n(\lambda_{3}-\mu_{3})(\lambda_{1}-\mu_{1})

which is nonzero since
\lambda_{1}>\mu_{1}
and
\lambda_{3}>\mu_{3}
, and so
A^{-1}
exists.

We next demonstrate that the entries of
A^{-1}\gamma
sum to
1
. After computation, we find that

A^{-1}\gamma=\begin{bmatrix}{1\over(\lambda_{1}-\mu_{1})}&{1\over(\lambda_{3}-\mu_{3})}&1+{\lambda_{2}-\mu_{2}\over(\lambda_{1}-\mu_{1})(\lambda_{3}-\mu_{3})}\end{bmatrix}.

If one adds the first two entries together, utilizing the fact that the size of
\lambda
and
\mu
are the same, we have

{1\over(\lambda_{1}-\mu_{1})}+{1\over(\lambda_{3}-\mu_{3})}={\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{3}-(\mu_{1}+\mu_{3})\over(\lambda_{1}-\mu_{1})(\lambda_{3}-\mu_{3})}={\mu_{2}-\lambda_{2}\over(\lambda_{1}-\mu_{1})(\lambda_{3}-\mu_{3})},

which when added the final entry of
A^{-1}\gamma
gives
1
. Now, since
\lambda_{1}>\mu_{1}
and
\lambda_{3}>\mu_{3}
, one can see the first two entries of
\gamma
are positive. Since the last entry is
1
minus the first two entries of
\gamma
, both of which are at most
{1\over 2}
due to our assumption that
|\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i}|\geq 2
, this means the final entry is non-negative. That is, we have demonstrated that
\gamma\in\operatorname{conv}(\lambda,\mu,\iota)\subseteq\mathcal{P}
. This means that
\{\lambda,\mu,\gamma\}\subseteq\operatorname{MLP}(\mathcal{P})
, but we assumed that
\mathcal{P}
is
2
-partition-maximal. This is a contradiction.

Thus, we know there exists an
i
for which
|\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i}|\leq 1
. However, we now further that this value can not be
0
. However, since the length of
\mu
and
\lambda
are 3, if one of their coordinates agree, then after removing these coordinates we are really considering partitions of length 2 with the same size. These partitions are always comparable, contradicting that our partitions are pair-wise not comparable.

∎

In the prior proof, we utilized the region
\operatorname{conv}(\lambda,\mu,\iota)
with
\iota:=(\mu_{1},n-\mu_{1}-\lambda_{3},\lambda_{3})
. This region has further utility.

Lemma 4.2
.

The interior of
\operatorname{conv}(\lambda,\mu,\iota)
contains all weakly decreasing lattice points in
\mathcal{P}
which are not contained in
\operatorname{Newt}(s_{\lambda})
and
\operatorname{Newt}(s_{\mu})
.

Proof.

For convenience we define
N_{\lambda}:=\operatorname{Newt}(s_{\lambda})
and
N_{\mu}:=\operatorname{Newt}(s_{\mu})
.

First, we demonstrate
\operatorname{Ext}(\mathcal{P})=\operatorname{Ext}(N_{\lambda})\cup\operatorname{Ext}(N_{\mu})
.
By Lemma
3.1
,
\mathcal{P}
is the convex hull of the exponent vectors for
s_{\lambda}
and
s_{\mu}
. Without loss of generality, suppose
\pi\in\operatorname{Ext}(\mathcal{P})
is an exponent vector of
s_{\lambda}
. If
\pi
were not an extreme point of
N_{\lambda}
, then it is either on the boundary or interior of
N_{\lambda}
. Then the same would be true for
\mathcal{P}
, which is a contradiction.
On the other hand, suppose
\pi\in\operatorname{Ext}(N_{\lambda})\cup\operatorname{Ext}(N_{\mu})
and yet
\pi\notin\operatorname{Ext}(N_{s})
. Without loss of generality,
\pi\in\operatorname{Ext}(N_{\mu})
, which implies that
\pi
is one of the permutations of
\mu
. By definition of
N_{s}
, if
\pi\not\in\operatorname{Ext}(N_{s})
, then none of the other permutations of
\pi
is an extreme point of
N_{s}
.Then
\operatorname{Ext}(N_{s})\subseteq\operatorname{Ext}(N_{\lambda})
. This implies that
N_{s}\subseteq N_{\lambda}
. Thus
\mu\in N_{s}
is a lattice point in
N_{\lambda}
, which means that
\mu
is dominated by
\lambda
. However, this contradicts that
\mu
and
\lambda
are not comparable.

Next, we demonstrate that
\iota=(\mu_{1},n-\mu_{1}-\lambda_{3},\lambda_{3})
is both on a facet of
N_{\lambda}
and
N_{\mu}
. Using
[
BGH+21
, Theorem 29]
, note that
\iota
is on the face defined by
x+y=\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}
, and so
\iota
is on a facet of
N_{\lambda}
, and
\iota
is on the face defined by
x=\mu_{1}
, so
\iota
is also on a facet of
N_{\mu}
.

The line
(\lambda_{3}-\mu_{3})x-(\lambda_{1}-\mu_{1})z=\mu_{1}\lambda_{3}-\mu_{3}\lambda_{1}
has both
\lambda
and
\mu
on it. We claim this line is a facet of
\mathcal{P}
, that is,
(x,y,z)\in\mathcal{P}
implies
(\lambda_{3}-\mu_{3})x-(\lambda_{1}-\mu_{1})z\leq\mu_{1}\lambda_{3}-\mu_{3}\lambda_{1}
. Note that for all permutations
(x,y,z)
of
\lambda
,
x\leq\lambda_{1}
and
z\geq\lambda_{3}
. Thus

\displaystyle(\lambda_{3}-\mu_{3})x-(\lambda_{1}-\mu_{1})z\leq

\displaystyle(\lambda_{3}-\mu_{3})\lambda_{1}-(\lambda_{1}-\mu_{1})\lambda_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{3}\mu_{1}-\lambda_{1}\mu_{3}.

Note that for all permutations
(x,y,z)
of
\mu
,
x\leq\mu_{1}
and
z\geq\mu_{3}
. Thus

\displaystyle(\lambda_{3}-\mu_{3})x-(\lambda_{1}-\mu_{1})z\leq

\displaystyle(\lambda_{3}-\mu_{3})\mu_{1}-(\lambda_{1}-\mu_{1})\mu_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{3}\mu_{1}-\lambda_{1}\mu_{3}.

We have demonstrated that the extreme points of
\mathcal{P}
satisfy the aforementioned inequality, and thus every point in
\mathcal{P}
does as well.

We now can finally demonstrate that the decreasing points of
\mathcal{P}
are contained in
\operatorname{conv}(\lambda,\mu,\iota)
. We first identify the facet defining inequalities of
\operatorname{conv}(\lambda,\mu,\iota)
. There are three facets: hyperplanes containing
\mu
and
\lambda
,
\iota
and
\mu
, and
\iota
and
\lambda
. That is, the points of
\operatorname{conv}(\lambda,\mu,\iota)
satisfy the following three inequalities:

(\lambda_{3}-\mu_{3})x-(\lambda_{1}-\mu_{1})z\leq\mu_{1}\lambda_{3}-\mu_{3}\lambda_{1},\,\,\,x\geq\mu_{1},\,\,\,\text{and }z\leq\lambda_{3}.

Let
(x,y,z)\in\mathcal{P}\setminus(N_{\lambda}\cup N_{\mu})
and
x\geq y\geq z
. Since
(x,y,z)\not\in N_{\lambda},
it must not satisfy at least one of the facet defining inequalities from
[
BGH+21
, Theorem 29]
:

\displaystyle x\leq\lambda_{1},

\displaystyle\hskip 8.5359ptx\geq\lambda_{3},

\displaystyle y\leq\lambda_{1},

\displaystyle\hskip 8.5359pty\geq\lambda_{3},

\displaystyle z\leq\lambda_{1},

\displaystyle\hskip 8.5359ptz\geq\lambda_{3}.

Since
x\geq y\geq z
, one of
x\leq\lambda_{1}
or
z\geq\lambda_{3}
must not be satisfied. However,
(x,y,z)\in\mathcal{P}
, which is the convexhull of permutations of
\lambda
and
\mu
. Since the coordinates of
\lambda
and
\mu
are at most
\lambda_{1}
,
x
,
y
, and
z
cannot be more than
\lambda_{1}
. Thus
x\leq\lambda_{1}
is always satisfied. Further,
z<\lambda_{3}
.

Similarly, since
(x,y,z)\not\in N_{\mu},

x\leq\mu_{1}
or
z\geq\mu_{3}
must not be satisfied. But as before, since the coordinates of
\lambda
and
\mu
are at least
\mu_{3},
we know that
z\geq\mu_{3}
. Thus
x>\mu_{1}
. Thus, we have demonstrated that any decreasing point in
\mathcal{P}
satisfies the three aforementioned facet defining inequalities.
∎

The utility of this result is two-fold. First, recall that ultimately our goal is to show that if
s=s_{\lambda}+s_{\mu}
, we have
ts
is SNP for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
. Since it is already known that
s_{\lambda}
and
s_{\mu}
are SNP, we need only be concerned with points outside the Newton polytope for these two points.
Beyond this, we also know that a point
\pi\in\mathcal{P}
if and only if a permutation of
\pi
is in
\mathcal{P}
. Thus, we may only concern ourselves with decreasing points outside of the Newton polytopes of
s_{\lambda}
and
s_{\mu}
, that is, the interior of the region
\operatorname{conv}(\lambda,\mu,\iota)
. With the prior Lemma in mind, we have the following from which Theorem
1.1
follows.

Theorem 4.3
.

The polynomial

ts=\sum_{i=0}^{t}s_{(t-i)\lambda+i\mu}

has SNP.

Proof.

We show
(x,y,z)\in\operatorname{Newt}(s_{(t-i)\lambda+i\mu})
for some
i
. By Lemma
4.1
, we know there exists a coordinate between
\lambda
and
\mu
which differs exactly by
1
. Without loss of generality, suppose
\lambda_{3}-\mu_{3}=1
. Then
t\mu_{3}\leq z\leq t\lambda_{3}
. Let
\alpha:=(t-(t\lambda_{3}-z))\lambda+(t\lambda_{3}-z)\mu
.
We claim that
(x,y,z)\in\operatorname{Newt}(s_{\alpha})
.

Observe the last coordinate of
\alpha
is
z
, since

\displaystyle(t-(t\lambda_{3}-z))\lambda_{3}+(t\lambda_{3}-z)\mu_{3}=

\displaystyle t\lambda_{3}-t\lambda_{3}\lambda_{3}+z\lambda_{3}+t\lambda_{3}\mu_{3}-z\mu_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t\lambda_{3}+z(\lambda_{3}-\mu_{3})-t\lambda_{3}(\lambda_{3}-\mu_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z,

where the last equality follows after implementing our assumption that
\lambda_{3}-\mu_{3}=1
.

By assuming
(x,y,z)
is weakly decreasing, recall by Lemma
4.2
we know that
(x,y,z)\in\operatorname{conv}(\lambda,\mu,\iota)
where
\iota:=(\mu_{1},n-\mu_{1}-\lambda_{3},\lambda_{3})
. Thus, we may write
(x,y,z)=at\lambda+bt\mu+ct\iota
with
a,b,c>0
and
a+b+c=1
. Since the last coordinates of
(x,y,z)
and
\alpha
are equal, we just need to show that
x
is less than the first coordinate of
\alpha
, that is,

at\lambda_{1}+bt\mu_{1}+ct\mu_{1}<(t-(t\lambda_{3}-z))\lambda_{1}+(t\lambda_{3}-z)\mu_{1}=t\lambda_{1}-(t\lambda_{3}-z)(\lambda_{1}-\mu_{1}).

Since
z=at\lambda_{3}+bt\mu_{3}+ct\lambda_{3}
, we have

t\lambda_{3}-z=t\lambda_{3}-(at\lambda_{3}+bt\mu_{3}+ct\lambda_{3})=t((1-a-c)\lambda_{3}-b\mu_{3})=t(b\lambda_{3}-b\mu_{3})=tb(\lambda_{3}-\mu_{3})=tb.

Thus,

\displaystyle t\lambda_{1}-(t\lambda_{3}-z)(\lambda_{1}-\mu_{1})=

\displaystyle t\lambda_{1}-tb(\lambda_{1}-\mu_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t(\lambda_{1}-b\lambda_{1}+b\mu_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t((1-b)\lambda_{1}+b\mu_{1}).

Note that since
\mu_{1}<\lambda_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle x=at\lambda_{1}+bt\mu_{1}+ct\mu_{1}<

\displaystyle at\lambda_{1}+bt\mu_{1}+ct\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t(b\mu_{1}+(a+c)\lambda_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t(b\mu_{1}+(1-b)\lambda_{1})=t\lambda_{1}-(t\lambda_{3}-z)(\lambda_{1}-\mu_{1}).

That is, we have now shown that
(x,y,z)
is dominated by
\alpha
.
∎
