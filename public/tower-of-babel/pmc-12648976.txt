Validation of Shona Symptom Questionnaire (SSQ-14), Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) and Generalised Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7) in Unguja Island, Zanzibar
Authors: Diana Ceccolini, Jermaine Dambi, Haji Fatawi, Suleiman Ali, Simon Kühnert, Lindsay Solera-Deuchar, Jabir James, Catherine Odhiambo, Peter McGovern, Whitney Taiwo, Ruth Verhey, Dixon Chibanda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDue to the dearth of resources in low- and middle-income countries, the use of culturally appropriate and psychometrically robust screening tools for common mental disorders (CMDs) administrable by lay health workers (LHWs) is critical to both identifying people that need care and collecting prevalence data to inform policy decisions. The Shona Symptom Questionnaire (SSQ-14), Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) and Generalised Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7) were validated as part of introducing the Friendship Bench in Zanzibar, Tanzania.MethodsThe present cross-sectional study was conducted at two primary healthcare units. The three screening tools were translated into Swahili and administered to a random sample of 475 participants alongside a standardised clinical assessment done by a psychiatrist, the gold-standard diagnostic technique to which the performance characteristics of the tools were compared. The structural validity of the tools was assessed via confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Reliability was measured using Cronbach’s alpha. Prevalence estimates of CMDs were derived from the clinical assessments.ResultsThe optimal cut-off point for the SSQ-14 was ≥ 7 (sensitivity = 76% and specificity = 70%); for the PHQ-9 was ≥ 5 (sensitivity = 69% and specificity = 63%); and for the GAD-7 was ≥ 3 (sensitivity = 69% and specificity = 64%). The area under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUC) indicated acceptable diagnostic accuracy, i.e., AUCSSQ-14 = 0.78, AUCPHQ-9 = 0.69 and AUCGAD-7 = 0.70. CFA yielded two- and one-factor structures for the PHQ-9 and GAD-7, and SSQ-14 respectively. The internal reliability of the tools was high, i.e.,αSSQ-14 = 0.82, αPHQ-9 = 0.81 and αGAD-7 = 0.83. The prevalence of CMDs, depression, and anxiety was of 25%, 18% and 8% respectively.ConclusionsThe tools are recommended for use by lay health workers in this setting and population. The high prevalence of CMDs reported highlights the importance of implementing evidence-based psychological interventions delivered by LHWs and reinforces the need for simple, validated, and culturally appropriate screening tools administrable by LHWs to do so, especially in low-resourced settings.Supplementary InformationfThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03584-1.Keywords: Translation, Validation, Screening tool, Global mental health, Common mental disorders, Friendship Bench, Zanzibar

Background

Depressive and anxiety disorders are the second and sixth leading causes of years lived with disability (YLDs) respectively, accounting for 10.9% of all-cause YLDs globally [1]. However, a significant mental healthcare gap exists, with up to 85% of those needing treatment not having equitable access to it [2–5]. Due to the general lack of mental health professionals, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), it is unlikely that the existing workforce will be able to close this considerable gap [6, 7]. Hence, alternative approaches have been devised, such as the use of trained and supervised lay health workers (LHWs) or community health volunteers (CHVs) to deliver brief psychological interventions [8]. This task-sharing approach is a viable way to close the care gap and has been widely recommended for implementation [8–14].

The Friendship Bench Programme, a [15–20] brief psychological intervention for generalised anxiety and depressive disorders, or common mental disorders (CMDs), delivered by trained and supervised CHVs, is a successful example of this approach and has consistently shown to be effective in reducing symptoms of anxiety and depression [15–23]. Since its development in Zimbabwe, it has been scaled up across the country [15] and replicated worldwide [24–27]. The programme, translated as Dawati Rafiki in Kiswahili, was also piloted in Zanzibar, Tanzania in 2019, with the aim of task-sharing mental healthcare in a context in which there are only approximately 1.31 mental healthcare professionals per 100,000 population [28]. Part of piloting Dawati Rafiki in Zanzibar, and for the first time in Tanzania generally, required the validation of culturally and linguistically appropriate screening tools for CMDs.

Validated screening tools with optimal diagnostic accuracy are fundamental for identifying people needing mental healthcare and increasing the access and coverage of task-shared mental healthcare initiatives such as Dawati Rafiki [29, 30]. They are also essential for accurately estimating prevalence in the region, particularly given the limited mental health workforce, as they can be administered by non-specialist staff, allowing screening at a larger scale to identify care needs and inform policy decisions on service provision to address the care gap. Up-to-date epidemiological data specific to Zanzibar is scarce, with the most recent prevalence estimates being from 2011 (N = 2637) [31]. A prevalence of CMDs derived from the General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12) [32, 33] of 6.8% was reported, with women having significantly higher rates (8.8% vs. 4.7%) compared to men. The prevalence was lower in rural (3.5%) compared to urban Unguja areas (6.7%). To the authors’ knowledge, more up-to-date epidemiological data in Zanzibar is inexistent. However, the 2021 GBD study estimated the prevalence of depressive disorders and anxiety disorders in Tanzania at 4.04%, and 3.90% respectively [1].

Brief screening tools are especially needed in resource-poor settings like Zanzibar, as overstretched healthcare workers do not usually have the time or training to carry out complex and time-consuming diagnostic interviews. These tools are also crucial for integrating mental healthcare into primary care, which is part of Zanzibar’s development vision for 2050 [34], as well as into community care, as they can be administered both by CHVs and non-mental health professionals. This, in turn, can also help address the care gap. Finally, validated screening tools are also vital for promoting and enhancing research, both through ease of administration, standardisation, and comparison across settings [30].

This study aimed to validate three mental health screening tools: the Shona Symptom Questionnaire (SSQ-14), the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), and the Generalised Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7). These measures were selected for parallel validation as they are routinely employed in replication efforts of the Friendship Bench programme, enabling comparison of data across diverse implementation settings. In addition, they are among the most widely used tools in sub-Saharan African contexts [35, 36]. A further aim of the study was to provide an updated estimate of the prevalence of CMDs in this setting, addressing the current lack of recent epidemiological data in the region.

Methods

Study design and location

The present cross-sectional study was conducted by Health Improvement Project Zanzibar (HIPZ) in collaboration with Zanzibar’s Ministry of Health (MoH), at two outpatient clinics and one district hospital on Unguja island.

Outcome measures

The Shona Symptom Questionnaire (SSQ-14)

The SSQ-14, the primary instrument used in the Friendship Bench Programme, is a 14-item screening questionnaire originally developed in the Shona language of Zimbabwe to identify CMDs [37]. Each item is scored dichotomously (0 = no, 1 = yes) with a maximum score of 14 and a cut-off score of ≥ 8, and refers to symptoms experienced over the week prior to the assessment. Items cover both common indicators of depression and anxiety and culturally specific idioms of distress derived from ethnographic and qualitative research in Harare. The tool has been widely used [18, 20–23, 38, 39], validated in primary care settings [40], and adapted for young adults and adolescents [41]. It has also been applied in mainland Tanzania with a cut-off score of ≥ 8 [42]. HIPZ and Dawati Rafiki employ a Kiswahili version of this measure, the Dodoso la Dalili Kwa Kiswahili (DDK-14).

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)

The PHQ-9 is a self-administered nine-item tool assessing each DSM-5 diagnostic criterion for Major Depressive Disorder [43, 44]. Items are rated on a four-point scale (0 = not at all, to 3 = nearly every day), referencing symptoms experienced over the two weeks prior to the assessment, with total scores ranging from 0 to 27 to indicate severity. It has been widely used, adapted, and validated across contexts, including mainland Tanzania with a cut-off score of ≥ 9 [45], demonstrating robust psychometric properties [40, 43, 45–51].

The Generalised Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7)

The GAD-7 is a self-administered seven-item questionnaire assessing anxiety symptoms over the two weeks prior to the assessment [52]. Items are scored from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), yielding a total score of 0–21 to indicate severity. It has been widely adapted and validated across diverse contexts [40, 53–58], in Kiswahili in Kenya [59], and used in mainland Tanzania with a cut-off score of ≥ 10 [60].

Translation procedures

Three mental health experts independently translated all three tools from English to Kiswahili. A two-day workshop attended by the translators, a clinical psychologist, the mental health coordinators from HIPZ and Zanzibar’s MoH, and the MoH health promotion unit coordinator was then held to review the translations. Discrepancies were discussed and resolved by consensus, resulting in a single version for each tool. To ensure cultural and linguistic appropriateness, five community members—an Imam, a teacher, a policymaker, a service user, and a delivery agent—participated in a five-hour focus group discussion. Drawing on their knowledge of Zanzibari Kiswahili, which differs from mainland Tanzanian Kiswahili, and local idioms of distress, participants reviewed the translations for each construct and refined them to ensure contextual equivalence with the originals. An expert panel then incorporated the feedback from the discussion to generate final versions of the adapted tools. Finally, the tools underwent back-translation to verify their semantic equivalence and ensure that the meaning of each item was preserved in the new language.

Study population and recruitment

Recruitment occurred at the two outpatient clinics every morning between October 11th and November 12th, 2021. Using a standardised script, a trained research assistant provided information about the study to waiting patients, who had previously been triaged by a nurse to ensure emergency cases were excluded from the study. Those willing to participate were then individually screened for eligibility and consented.

Inclusion criteria included being over 18; at the recruitment sites during the study period; and able to provide signed or ink thumb print consent. Exclusion criteria included: having severe cognitive impairment hindering full participation; having a prior diagnosis of any mental disorder; and needing emergency care at the triage stage. Eligible and consenting patients were randomly selected using an online random number generator until the required sample size was achieved.

Sample size

Following Wolf et al.’s [61] recommendations regarding required sample sizes for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), we assumed low factor loadings to achieve a parsimonious solution; and estimated 460 participants were needed. This estimate accounts for the number of latent variables to be compared for a two-factor CFA. In actuality, 475 participants were recruited to account for inconsistent responses during data cleaning.

Validation procedures

The screening tools were administered by the trained research assistants, who also collected sociodemographic data. Participants were then referred to a trained psychiatrist, blinded to the screening results, who formulated a psychiatric diagnosis based on a diagnostic interview guided by a standardised clinical assessment tool. A participant was considered to have a CMD if the psychiatrist diagnosed them with either generalised anxiety disorder, depression, or both.

As no Kiswahili version of a structured diagnostic interview existed, we developed a tool based on Kiswahili translations of the relevant modules of the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI; [62]) for diagnosing Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD) and Major Depressive Disorder (MDD). This was done to standardise diagnostic procedures in the local language. Diagnostic interviews conducted by qualified psychiatrists are widely recommended for cross-cultural validation of screening tools [30, 63], and the MINI was chosen because it aligns with the ICD-10 [64], the predominant diagnostic framework in this setting, and was considered most appropriate for use by the study psychiatrist.

Data collection, management and analysis

Sociodemographic data and screening outcomes were collected on a tablet. Data collected on tablets is retained by the research team for further analysis. Informed consent, psychiatric history and assessments were collected in paper format. These documents are preserved in the HIPZ offices, with restricted access to the principal investigator. STATA 17 [65] was used to conduct all analyses.

Structural validity

The factorial structure of the measures was first examined using exploratory factor analysis (EFA; see Additional file 1) and subsequently evaluated with confirmatory factor analysis (CFA; see Additional file 2 for Structural Equation Models for the CFA). Models derived from a priori literature were tested for the best fit using standard goodness-of-fit indices (see Additional file 3 for the indices and their interpretation). To the authors’ knowledge, no a priori factorial models exist for the SSQ-14; therefore, CFA was conducted only for a one-factor model for this tool. For both the PHQ-9 and GAD-7, most studies support a one-factor structure (PHQ-9: [66]; GAD-7:, consistent with their original design [44, 52]. However, there is no consensus on their optimal factor structure [67–69]. Consequently, both one-factor and two-factor models were compared for the PHQ-9 and GAD-7.

For all three measures, Model 1 represented a one-factor structure. The two-factor models derived from the literature were specified as follows: for the PHQ-9, Model 2 included a somatic factor (items 3, 4, 5) and a cognitive/affective factor (items 1, 2, 6, 7, 8, 9); Model 3 included a somatic factor (items 3, 4, 5, 7, 8) and a non-somatic factor (items 1, 2, 6, 9); and Model 4 included a somatic factor (items 3, 4, 5, 8) and a cognitive/affective factor (items 1, 2, 6, 7, 9) [67]. For the GAD-7, Model 2 comprised a somatic factor (items 4, 5, 6) and a cognitive/affective factor (items 1, 2, 3, 7) [69].

Criterion validity

The performance characteristics of the three tools were compared to a clinical diagnosis derived from a standardised clinical assessment. The SSQ-14, PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores were compared to a diagnosis of CMDs (depression and/or anxiety), depression, and anxiety respectively. The sensitivity and specificity and the positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) for different possible cut-off points of the tools were calculated. The sensitivity (the true positive rate) and 1-specificity (the false positive rate) for different cut-off points were then plotted on the x-axis and y-axis respectively to produce a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve.

Convergent validity

The convergent validity of the translated tools was assessed by exploring the correlation between SSQ-14, PHQ-9, and GAD-7 scores using the Pearson Correlation coefficient. The correlations are interpreted as follows: weak (r =.1- 0.39), moderate (r =.40- 0.69), strong (r =.7 −.89) and very strong (r=. 0.9 − 1.0) [70].

Internal reliability

The internal reliability of the tools was measured with the use of Cronbach’s alpha (α) and interpreted based on Cicchetti’s guidelines [71]. When Cronbach’s alpha is below 0.70, the level of clinical significance is unacceptable; between 0.70 and 0.79 is fair; between 0.80 and 0.89 is good; and above 0.90 is excellent.

Out of the 475 participants (Table 1), 117 (24.6%) were male and 358 (75.4%) were female, with ages ranging from 18 to 75 (M = 33.3, SD = 13.3). Most of the participants practised Islam (95.6%), with the rest practising Christianity (4.4%). The majority of the participants were currently married (67.4%), and out of those not currently married, some had never married (26.3%), and the rest were either separated, divorced, or widowed (6.3%). Few participants completed secondary education or further (34.3%); approximately a third of participants were unemployed (32.6%), and the majority were either employed or did housework (60.2%).

Structural validity of the SSQ-14

Although the one-factor model had a significant chi-squared test (p <.001) and a chi-squared to degrees of freedom ratio of more than two (χ2/df = 2.01) indicating a poor fit; the rest of the fit indices indicated an excellent fit, i.e., RMSEA = 0.046; pclose = 0.719; CFI = 0.938 and TLI = 0.926.

Structural validity of the PHQ-9

Four models were fitted, of which Model 2 displayed the best fit. Like all PHQ-9 models, Model 2 had had a significant chi-squared test (p <.001) and a chi-squared to degrees of freedom ratio of less than two (χ2/df = 1.75) indicating a mediocre fit. Similarly, like all PHQ-9 models, the rest of the fit indices indicated an excellent fit, i.e., RMSEA = 0.040; pclose = 0.799; CFI = 0.98 and TLI = 0.97. However, the comparative measures of fit were better for Model 2 (AIC = 8728.12; BIC = 8844.70).

Structural validity of the GAD-7

Two models were fitted, of which Model 2 displayed the best fit. Like Model 1, Model 2 had had a significant chi-squared test (p <.001) and a chi-squared to degrees of freedom ratio of more than two (χ2/df = 3.27) indicating a poor fit. However, Model 2 performed better in the other indices, i.e., RMSEA = 0.07; pclose = 0.078; CFI = 0.98; TLI = 0.97; AIC = 5672.92; BIC = 5764.51.

Prevalence estimates of common mental disorders

The prevalence of CMDs, depression and anxiety derived from the standardised clinical assessments was of 25.1% (n = 119, 95% CI: 21.2%, 29.0%); 17.7% (n = 84, 95% CI: 14.3%, 21.1%); and 7.6% (n = 36, 95% CI: 5.2%, 10.0%) respectively.

Discussion

Structural validity

No a priori factor structure models exist for the SSQ-14 in the literature, with previous studies mainly focusing on evaluating its diagnostic accuracy [37, 40, 41]. Therefore, only a one-factor model was fitted. However, the SSQ-14 aims to measure symptoms of both depression and anxiety, suggesting a two-factor structure might demonstrate better fit. The fit indices were conflicting. The model yielded a significant chi-squared test and a chi-squared to degrees of freedom ratio greater than two, indicating an unacceptable model fit. However, the chi-squared to degrees of freedom ratio was just above two and is prone to misfitting for large samples like ours [72–74]; and other indicators suggested a good or excellent fit. Our data supporting the one-factor structure may be explained by cultural expressions of mental health idioms, suggesting there might be a lack of differentiation between depression and anxiety symptoms among Zanzibar residents. Ethnological studies are needed to better understand the idioms associated with CMDs in this setting. Nonetheless, tool validation is an iterative process; therefore, the factorial structure of the tool should be explored further. For example, latent class analyses could be conducted to yield differential models and inform further structural validation. Nevertheless, the SSQ-14 exhibited adequate fit is recommended for use by lay health workers as the tool is iteratively improved.

A recent systematic review of the factor structure of the PHQ-9 found satisfactory evidence both for one- and two-factor models [67]. The present study compared a one-factor model to three two-factor models. Model 2 (one factor containing somatic symptoms (items 3, 4, and 5) and one containing cognitive/affective symptoms (items 1, 2, 6, 7, 8 and 9)) [67] displayed the best fit to the data. However, all models displayed a good fit, irrespective of the significant chi-squared tests for all models, as the chi-squared to degrees of freedom ratios were all less than two [74]. This supports the idea of a two-factor structure for the PHQ-9 but is discordant with previous research recommending its use as a unidimensional measure [66]. Cultural differences in the expression of depressive symptoms could explain the discrepancies with the posited single-factor structure.

There is no consensus on the factorial structure of the GAD-7, so we tested both one- and two-factor models. The literature suggests a two-factor model distinguishing somatic (items 4, 5, 6) and cognitive/affective (items 1, 2, 3, 7) symptoms may provide a better fit in certain settings [69], irrespective of the tool’s original structure, which aligns with our findings, as AIC and BIC were lower for the two-factor model. However, RMSEA and pclose values indicated that neither model achieved a truly good fit. CFA of the Luganda and Runyoro versions (N = 4107) among Ugandians aged 13–80 yielded a single-factor solution, producing comparable fit indices (e.g., RMSEA = 0.097, considered a poor-mediocre fit), though only the single-factor model was explored [75]. A study of South African first responders (N = 429) found excellent fit for the two-factor English version [76], potentially reflecting the higher educational level of that sample. Language differences and limited cultural expression in translations may explain the lack of robust fit, highlighting the need for further ethnographic research. In our study, item 7 (“feeling afraid, as if something awful might happen”) had the lowest factor loading (r =.4), consistent with the Ugandan study [75], suggesting future translation refinements could improve fit across settings. Overall, the CFA provides good evidence of the structural validity of the SSQ-14 and PHQ-9 in this setting, and satisfactory validity for the GAD-7.

Criterion and internal validity

The NPVs associated with the optimal cut-off points and prevalence estimates in this study indicate that the SSQ-14 and PHQ-9 screen 90%, and the GAD-7 screens 96% of true negatives as negatives. The high NPVs suggest that by using the current cut-off points, only 4–10% of people who do not need care will be incorrectly flagged as needing it due to false positives. This is particularly helpful in resource-constrained settings such as Zanzibar to ensure that limited mental health resources are prioritised for those most likely to benefit, minimising unnecessary referrals and reducing the burden on healthcare services. Conversely, the PPVs indicate that among those identified as positive, 46%, 29%, and 14% truly have the condition on the SSQ-14, PHQ-9, and GAD-7, respectively. The relatively low PPVs suggest that by using these cut-off points, 54%, 71%, and 86% of people flagged as positive do not actually have the condition, meaning that many referred for care may not need it. To address this, confirmatory assessments and careful referral management are essential to ensure that resources are prioritised for those truly in need, to avoid overburdening the already limited services, and avoid causing unnecessary anxiety or stigma to those falsely screened as positive.

Compared to the validation study of the three tools carried out in Zimbabwe [40], the PPVs in the present study are all lower, and the NPVs are all higher. The trade-off between the tools’ relatively low PPVs and high NPVs carries with itself public health implications. On the one hand, the high NPVs suggest the tools are effective in correctly identifying patients who do not require mental health care, which is helpful in this setting due to the lack of mental health workforce and health-related resources generally. Not providing care to individuals who do not need it will maximise and prioritise resources for those who do. Conversely, the low PPVs suggest the tools are less effective in correctly identifying patients who do require mental health care, which may result in them not receiving the care they need. Careful consideration should be given to the implications of these values in this setting, including how to avoid missing potential cases after a potentially false negative screening result.

The AUC values suggest that there is a 78% chance that someone that has CMDs would have a higher score on the SSQ-14 than someone that does not, a 69% chance that someone that has depression would have a higher score on the PHQ-9 than someone that does not, and a 70% chance that someone that has anxiety would have a higher score on the GAD-7 than someone that does not [77]. Tools with AUC values between 0.7 and 0.8 are considered to have acceptable diagnostic validity [77]. According to this interpretation and considering that the AUC values for both the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 lie on the threshold of acceptability, the authors can conclude that all tools have acceptable diagnostic validity in this setting.

Compared to the validation study of the three tools carried out in Zimbabwe [40], the current AUC values are all lower. The sensitivity and specificity of all tools were also lower in the current study, and the internal reliability was lower for the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 but not for the SSQ-14. However, the performance characteristics yielded from the present validation study are still superior to those yielded by utilising the cut-off points from the validation study carried out in Zimbabwe, suggesting that the new cut-off points improve the diagnostic validity of the measures in this setting.

The SSQ-14 displayed better criterion validity than the other two measures, likely because it has been devised for a setting and context more similar to that of Zanzibar than the PHQ-9 and the GAD-7, facilitating its adaptation. The SSQ-14 is also more user-friendly. It is the only tool with binary response options, which might have been more easily understood, especially considering the low educational level of the participants in this study. The scoring instructions for the SSQ-14 are also less complex than those for the PHQ-9 and GAD-7, making them more easily interpretable by LHWs. This tool may therefore be more appropriate for use in the current setting than the rest. Moreso, the SSQ-14 displayed acceptable construct validity as the scores were moderately to strongly correlated to PHQ-9 and GAD-7. The convergent validity demonstrated with widely used international screening tools provides further evidence of the utility of the SSQ-14, which is also comparable to a previous study conducted in Zimbabwe [40]. The translated PHQ-9 and GAD-7 perform as well diagnostically and psychometrically in Zanzibar, allowing for data collection essential for comparison with other regions. This is especially essential given the need to collect evidence of the effectiveness of task-shared interventions across different settings.

Prevalence of common mental disorders

According to the psychiatric assessments, the prevalence of CMDs, depression, and anxiety across the two outpatient clinics on Unguja Island was estimated to be of 25%, 18%, and 8% respectively. Compared to the last available estimates (2011), which found a prevalence of CMDs of 6.8% [31], the current estimates are significantly higher. The findings could be indicative of a significant increase in the prevalence of CMDs in this population, which is consistent with the global increases in the GBD attributable to CMDs [1]. The fact that our sample was predominantly female (75%) may have also contributed to a higher prevalence, however, even when compared only to the female-specific estimates from 2011, the current prevalence remains substantially higher. Alternatively, different methodological approaches between studies may account for the discrepancies.

For instance, the prevalence estimates reported in the 2011 survey [31] were derived from the GHQ-12 [32, 33], a screening tool, whereas the prevalence estimates reported in the present study were derived from standardised clinical assessments. The selected cut-off point of ≥ 3 for the GHQ-12 resulted in high specificity and reduced sensitivity, which may have produced a lower prevalence estimate relative to the true prevalence in the population. On the other hand, the use of standardised diagnostic interviews conducted by qualified psychiatrists has been widely recommended for cross-cultural validations of screening tools [30, 63]. The sampling technique also differed across studies. The 2011 survey [31] implemented a multi-stage sampling with probability proportional to size technique, ensuring that every individual in each Shehia had the same probability of selection. In contrast, the present study conducted random sampling within two public outpatient clinics.

Limitations

Our sample was drawn from public outpatient clinics in relatively urban areas of Unguja and may not fully reflect the general population. CMD prevalence has been shown to vary across care settings, with studies in mainland Tanzania suggesting that individuals attending religious or traditional healers may have up to twice the prevalence observed in primary care [78]. It also differs between urban and rural areas, with higher prevalence reported in urban settings, and by sex, with women, who made up 75% of our sample, being more likely to experience CMDs [31]. Recruiting participants seeking treatment for physical health conditions may have further influenced estimates, given the established link between poor physical and mental health [79]. Consequently, findings may be affected by selection bias, and differences across subgroups should be interpreted with some caution. Measurement invariance across key subgroups, such as sex, age, and educational level, was not tested, which would have helped determine whether observed differences reflect true variation or measurement factors.

Finally, all diagnoses were conducted by a single psychiatrist. While resource constraints necessitated this approach and a standardised tool was developed to support consistency, this may have introduced assessment bias. Although the psychiatrist was blinded to screening results, bias cannot be entirely excluded. Furthermore, the standardised tool, although derived from a gold-standard approach (the MINI) was not validated before use. These limitations suggest the findings should be interpreted with caution and generalised beyond this setting and population carefully. Due to the aforementioned methodological limitations regarding the sampling technique, future research should explore alternative options to carry out community-based screening of CMDs in this setting.

Conclusion and recommendations

The present study is the first to explore the structural validity, criterion validity, and internal reliability of the translated and adapted versions of the SSQ-14, PHQ-9, and GAD-7 in Zanzibar, Tanzania. Because no other screening tool has been validated in this population, the authors recommend the use of the SSQ-14 with a cut-off point of ≥ 7; the PHQ-9 with a cut-off point of ≥ 5; and the GAD-7 with a cut-off point of ≥ 3, for use by LHWs in primary healthcare populations in Zanzibar. The SSQ-14, with its superior performance characteristics and more straightforward response options and scoring instructions, is recommended with greater confidence for routine clinical and CHV use. The PHQ-9 and GAD-7 can be used for intermittent clinical evaluations and research purposes to facilitate the collection of data that is comparable across different settings.

Nevertheless, our results highlight the importance of follow-up studies to adapt these tools, especially the PHQ-9 and GAD-7, for better psychometric performance and diagnostic accuracy. For instance, item response theory techniques, such as differential item functioning, can be valuable in informing potential modifications. Measurement invariance testing across key subgroups could strengthen confidence in the applicability and robustness of the psychological instruments across diverse demographic groups. Further testing, such as test-retest reliability, could also enhance the evidence for the instruments’ reliability and validity in this population.

The lack of excellent fit and performance characteristics could also mean that the contextual and cultural differences between Zanzibar and the settings in which the tools were developed are so significant that they necessitate further formative research, such as ethnographic studies, qualitative interviews, and additional workshops. Earlier work on the validation of the SSQ-14 in Zimbabwe, for instance, emphasised a balance between emic and etic aspects of the tool, resulting in the SSQ being a culturally accepted tool across different settings in Zimbabwe.

This study has also been the first in over ten years to report on prevalence estimates of CMDs in this setting [31]. Having reliable and up-to-date epidemiological data in this setting has practical implications, as it provides more accurate information for bodies such as the MoH Zanzibar and HIPZ to consider when planning for service provision and estimating population needs. The high prevalence of CMDs reported reinforces the need to develop, implement, and scale up evidence-based brief psychological interventions delivered by LHWs such as Dawati Rafiki, and emphasises the importance of having culturally appropriate and validated screening tools which are easily administrable by LHWs to do so.
