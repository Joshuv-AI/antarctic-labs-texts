Eye-tracking and cognitive patterns in post-traumatic stress disorder: a scoping review
Authors: Pierluigi Diotaiuti, Giuseppe Spica, Francesco Di Siena, Elisa Cavicchiolo, Loiane Cristina De Souza, Alexandro Andrade, Stefania Mancone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: PTSD is associated with alterations in attentional processing and executive control, particularly in response to threat-related or emotionally salient cues. Early research emphasised sustained hypervigilance, whereas recent evidence indicates that attentional biases in PTSD are dynamic, context-dependent, and may also involve avoidance. Eye-tracking, especially combined with neurophysiological measures, provides fine-grained indices of visual attention and oculomotor control.Objective: This scoping review synthesises empirical findings on attentional bias and cognitive control in PTSD, focusing on eye-tracking paradigms—including antisaccade, free-viewing, and visual search tasks—and their neurobiological correlates, integrating advances published up to 2025.Method: A literature search was conducted in Scopus, PubMed, and PsycINFO from 2010 to 2025 using terms related to PTSD, eye-tracking, attentional bias, inhibition, and executive functioning, updated to include 2025 publications.Results: Eye-tracking studies suggest that PTSD is associated with heterogeneous and dynamic attentional patterns. While some studies report sustained attention to threatening stimuli, recent evidence also describes attentional avoidance under increased cognitive load, reduced visual exploration, and shortened scanpaths. Alterations in early perceptual processing have been documented in a small number of multimodal studies, with trauma-related sensory encoding differences and contextual-memory difficulties reported in specific paradigms. Attention bias variability, reflecting fluctuations between vigilance and avoidance, has been proposed as a relevant correlate of PTSD severity, with some studies suggesting added sensitivity of eye-tracking indices relative to reaction-time measures. Attentional patterns are further modulated by trauma type and symptom composition, indicating substantial interindividual variability.Conclusion: Eye-tracking can capture early and late attentional processes in PTSD with high temporal resolution, showing that attentional dysregulation may extend beyond hypervigilance to include avoidance and instability across contexts.

Introduction

Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) is a trauma-related psychiatric condition that may develop following exposure to life-threatening or severely distressing events. PTSD is defined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) as comprising four symptom clusters: intrusion, avoidance, negative alterations in cognitions and mood, and alterations in arousal and reactivity (American Psychiatric Association, 2013), a reorganisation of diagnostic criteria that reflected substantial revisions relative to earlier DSM editions (Friedman, 2013). Although exposure to traumatic events is common in the general population, only a subset of exposed individuals develops PTSD, suggesting that trauma exposure alone is not sufficient to explain the onset and persistence of the disorder (Ehlers & Clark, 2000; Kessler et al., 2005).

The risk of developing PTSD is not uniform across trauma-exposed populations. Higher vulnerability has been documented in women as well as in occupational groups with repeated trauma exposure, including military personnel and Veterans, first responders, and healthcare workers (Arena et al., 2025; Marmar et al., 2006; Olff, 2017; Sahebi et al., 2021; Wisco et al., 2022). In these occupational groups, PTSD has also been linked to broader neuroendocrine and structural alterations, such as reduced hippocampal volume and altered cortisol regulation in police officers with PTSD (Lindauer et al., 2006). These epidemiological and risk-related differences indicate that specific cognitive-affective and neurobiological mechanisms may contribute to individual variability in PTSD development and maintenance.

Among these mechanisms, alterations in attentional processing have received increasing theoretical and empirical interest. Cognitive models of PTSD have long proposed that trauma-related psychopathology is associated with selective processing of threat, such that individuals preferentially orient toward, maintain attention on, or fail to disengage from trauma-related or negatively valenced cues (Bar-Haim et al., 2007; Cisler & Koster, 2010; Ehlers & Clark, 2000). Early formulations mainly emphasised hypervigilance to threat, whereas more recent perspectives suggest that attentional responding in PTSD is more heterogeneous and dynamic for instance, attention bias to threat can be suppressed under conditions of acute life-threatening danger (Bar-Haim et al., 2010), involving not only vigilance and sustained engagement, but also delayed disengagement, attentional avoidance, and fluctuations between these patterns over time (Cisler et al., 2009; Lazarov et al., 2019). This broader perspective is important because attentional dysregulation may contribute both to persistent symptom activation and to difficulty adapting to changing environmental demands.

Within this framework, attentional bias in PTSD can be more precisely understood as the product of an altered interaction between stimulus-driven and goal-directed attentional systems. Bottom-up processes refer to the relatively rapid and automatic capture of attention by salient stimuli, especially cues that are biologically, emotionally, or motivationally significant. In PTSD, trauma-related or negatively valenced cues may acquire heightened salience, thereby increasing the likelihood of early orienting toward threat and continued competition for processing resources. Top-down processes, by contrast, refer to voluntary control mechanisms that support goal maintenance, inhibition of prepotent responses, flexible shifting of attention, and disengagement from stimuli that are no longer relevant to current task demands. According to Attentional Control Theory, anxiety-related conditions are characterised by an imbalance between these systems, such that excessive bottom-up capture by threat reduces the efficiency of top-down control, especially under conditions of emotional load or cognitive demand (Eysenck et al., 2007; Eysenck & Derakshan, 2011), a pattern consistent with earlier behavioural evidence that attentional control moderates the relationship between posttraumatic stress symptoms and threat-related attentional bias (Bardeen & Orcutt, 2011). Consistent with this framework, behavioural evidence in PTSD has directly linked reduced attentional control with heightened attentional bias toward threat-related material (Schoorl et al., 2014), supporting the theoretical link between top-down regulatory deficits and bottom-up threat capture in this population. Applied to PTSD, this framework suggests that attentional bias should not be reduced to a single phenomenon such as hypervigilance alone; rather, it may emerge at different stages of processing, including initial orienting, sustained attention, delayed disengagement, strategic avoidance, or instability across trials and contexts (Aupperle et al., 2012; Cisler & Koster, 2010). This attentional dysregulation may also carry functional consequences beyond visual processing itself, as attention bias toward threat has been associated with exaggerated fear responding and impaired fear extinction in PTSD (Fani et al., 2012b), suggesting a potential link between attentional and fear-learning mechanisms. This view is consistent with broader neurocognitive accounts describing PTSD as involving disrupted interactions between emotional reactivity and cognitive control systems, rather than isolated deficits in either domain alone (Hayes et al., 2012b).

Recent neurocognitive studies have attempted to identify a possible top-down dysfunction that could explain the hyperarousal observed in patients with PTSD. In a functional MRI study using a Go/No-Go inhibition task, Falconer et al. (2008) reported reduced recruitment of the right-lateralized cortical inhibitory network together with increased activation in striatal and somatosensory regions. Importantly, these findings do not constitute a direct measure of attentional bias; rather, they provide convergent neurofunctional evidence for the hypothesis that inhibitory-control systems may be less efficiently recruited in PTSD when salient input competes for processing. In theoretical terms, this pattern supports the view that enhanced salience processing may place greater demands on regulatory control, thereby making it more difficult for individuals with PTSD to suppress prepotent responses and redirect attention in a goal-consistent manner. This is consistent with broader translational evidence indicating that PTSD involves dysfunction across distributed brain circuits — including amygdala, prefrontal, and hippocampal systems — implicated in threat processing and regulatory control (Fenster et al., 2018). From this perspective, executive dysfunction is relevant to PTSD not merely as a broad neuropsychological deficit, but more specifically as a mechanism that may shape the regulation of attention in the presence of emotionally salient or threat-related stimuli (Aupperle et al., 2012).

This theoretical distinction is especially useful for interpreting the empirical heterogeneity of attentional findings in PTSD. If threat-related salience predominates early in processing, individuals may show vigilance or facilitated orienting toward trauma-related cues. If top-down control is insufficient to override this capture, attention may remain allocated to threat, leading to prolonged dwell time or difficulty disengaging. Conversely, when emotional material becomes difficult to regulate, attentional avoidance may emerge as a later, strategic attempt to reduce distress rather than as evidence against the presence of attentional bias. More recent accounts are therefore compatible with the view that PTSD involves not only sustained threat vigilance, but also oscillation between engagement and avoidance, as well as trial-by-trial instability in attentional allocation. Similar evidence of neuropsychological impairment, including attentional deficits, has been documented in female survivors of intimate partner violence with PTSD, relative to trauma-exposed women without PTSD (Stein et al., 2002), further underscoring the relevance of trauma type and gender in shaping cognitive and attentional outcomes. This expanded model provides a stronger conceptual basis for interpreting why different paradigms may reveal different manifestations of attentional dysregulation.

Historically, research on attentional bias in PTSD has relied mainly on reaction-time-based paradigms, especially the emotional Stroop task and the dot-probe task (MacLeod et al., 1986; Williams et al., 1996). These paradigms have made an important contribution to the field, but they infer attentional processes indirectly from behavioural responses and have produced inconsistent findings in PTSD, with studies variably reporting hypervigilance, avoidance, or null effects (Ashley et al., 2013; Bryant & Harvey, 1997; Cisler et al., 2011; Kimble et al., 2009). A major limitation of these methods is that they provide a relatively static and indirect estimate of attentional allocation, making it difficult to capture the temporal dynamics of initial orienting, sustained attention, disengagement, re-engagement, and attentional instability. By contrast, eye-tracking methods provide a more direct and fine-grained assessment of visual attention, allowing investigators to examine first fixation, fixation latency, dwell time, scanpath organisation, visual exploration, and variability in gaze allocation over time (Armstrong et al., 2013; Isaacowitz et al., 2006; Kimble et al., 2010). This methodological advantage over reaction-time paradigms has similarly been highlighted in eye-tracking research on anxiety disorders more broadly, including in developmental populations (In-Albon & Schneider, 2010). These features make eye-tracking particularly suitable for testing whether PTSD is characterised by sustained threat monitoring, avoidance, oscillation between vigilance and avoidance, or altered visual exploration under cognitive or emotional load.

Eye-tracking paradigms also map in theoretically meaningful ways onto different components of attentional functioning. Free-viewing and visual-search tasks are particularly informative for examining spontaneous orienting, sustained attention, avoidance, and visual exploration in the absence of strong response constraints. Modified dot-probe paradigms augmented with eye-tracking allow researchers to dissociate initial orienting from later maintained attention more precisely than reaction-time indices alone. Oculomotor paradigms such as the antisaccade task are especially relevant to the top-down component of attentional control, because they require participants to inhibit a reflexive gaze shift toward a salient peripheral stimulus and instead generate a voluntary saccade in the opposite direction (Coe & Munoz, 2017). Accordingly, increased antisaccade latency and error rates in PTSD can be interpreted not only as evidence of generic executive dysfunction, but more specifically as behavioural support for impaired top-down regulation in the presence of salient or emotionally loaded stimuli (Blekic et al., 2021; Reinhard et al., 2017).

Eye-tracking studies in PTSD have indeed begun to reveal a more complex attentional profile than early hypervigilance models alone would predict. Several investigations have documented prolonged dwell time on threatening, angry, fearful, or trauma-related stimuli in PTSD, supporting the presence of sustained attention to negatively valenced material (Kimble et al., 2010; Lazarov et al., 2022; Powers et al., 2019; Suarez-Jimenez et al., 2022; Veerapa et al., 2023). At the same time, more recent work suggests that attentional responding in PTSD may also involve avoidance, reduced visual exploration, shorter scanpaths, and variability in gaze allocation across trials or task conditions (Alon et al., 2023; Blekic et al., 2025; Lev et al., 2025). This is consistent with the idea that eye-tracking captures multiple stages of attentional processing rather than a single, static bias score. Studies integrating eye-tracking with neuroimaging or electrophysiological measures suggest that altered attentional processing in PTSD may be linked both to broader neural regulatory systems and to very early sensory-perceptual abnormalities in the processing of trauma-related cues (Suarez-Jimenez et al., 2022; Zidda et al., 2025).

In light of these developments, the present scoping review aims to map and synthesise the empirical literature using eye-tracking to investigate attentional processing in PTSD. Specifically, the review examines how eye-tracking studies have characterised vigilance to threat, sustained attention, attentional avoidance, and attention bias variability in PTSD. It also considers the extent to which eye-movement paradigms, such as antisaccade tasks, provide evidence of impaired inhibitory or executive control in response to emotionally salient stimuli, and identifies which eye-tracking paradigms, indices, and multimodal approaches have been most informative in this field. By focusing on these objectives, the review seeks to clarify the specific contribution of eye-tracking to the understanding of attentional dysregulation in PTSD and to identify the main conceptual and methodological directions emerging from this literature.

Methods

Study design

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley (2005), further refined by the Joanna Briggs Institute guidance for scoping reviews (Peters et al., 2020a) and updated in line with subsequent methodological refinements for the conduct of scoping reviews (Peters et al., 2020b), and reported following the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines (Tricco et al., 2018). A scoping review design was considered appropriate because the objective was to map the breadth, heterogeneity, and conceptual directions of the literature on eye-tracking and attentional processes in PTSD, rather than to perform a quantitative synthesis or formal quality appraisal. The literature is heterogeneous with respect to trauma populations, PTSD ascertainment methods, experimental paradigms, eye-tracking indices, and multimodal integrations such as EEG and fMRI.

Information sources and search strategy

A literature search was conducted in PubMed, PsycINFO, and Scopus to identify peer-reviewed empirical studies on PTSD, eye-tracking, attentional bias, and cognitive control. The final search covered studies published up to 2025 and was designed to capture empirical work using eye-tracking or eye-movement methodology in PTSD or trauma-exposed samples with PTSD-related analyses.

Search strings combined terms for the target disorder, eye-tracking methodology, attentional processes, and executive-functioning constructs. The core terms included: ‘post-traumatic stress disorder’ OR ‘PTSD’; ‘eye-tracking,’ ‘eye movements,’ ‘gaze,’ ‘oculomotor’; ‘attentional bias,’ ‘visual attention,’ ‘vigilance,’ ‘dwell time,’ ‘fixation,’ ‘scanpath,’ ‘disengagement,’ ‘attention bias variability’; and executive-functioning terms such as ‘inhibition,’ ‘inhibitory control,’ ‘attentional control,’ ‘cognitive control,’ ‘executive control,’ ‘executive functioning,’ ‘antisaccade,’ and, when relevant, ‘cognitive flexibility.’ Search syntax was adapted to the indexing conventions of each database.

Because the initial search strategy focused on the contemporary eye-tracking literature published between 2010 and 2025, an additional check was conducted to determine whether eligible PTSD eye-tracking studies had been published before 2010. This supplementary check used the same core concepts and did not identify additional empirical studies meeting the inclusion criteria. Accordingly, although the final included corpus consisted of studies published from 2010 onward, the lower bound reflected the distribution of eligible studies identified rather than an a priori exclusion of relevant pre-2010 evidence.

Eligibility criteria and study selection

Studies were eligible if they: (a) used eye-tracking methodology to examine attentional or oculomotor processes; (b) included participants with clinician-established PTSD, probable PTSD based on validated symptom cut-offs, subthreshold PTSD, or trauma-exposed samples in which PTSD symptoms were explicitly analyzed in relation to eye-tracking outcomes; and (c) employed paradigms relevant to attentional bias or cognitive control, such as free-viewing, visual search, antisaccade, emotional face viewing, or eye-tracking-enhanced dot-probe/Stroop-type paradigms.

Studies were excluded if they: (a) relied exclusively on reaction-time measures without eye-tracking; (b) did not include PTSD-relevant clinical or symptom-based analyses; (c) focused on non-empirical publication types such as reviews, editorials, or commentaries; or (d) provided insufficient methodological detail for interpretation.

Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts and then assessed full texts for eligibility. Disagreements were discussed and, when necessary, resolved with a third reviewer.

PTSD diagnostic ascertainment

Because diagnostic procedures and symptom-assessment strategies varied across the included studies, we explicitly extracted how PTSD was defined or operationalised in each article. Diagnostic ascertainment was categorised as: (1) clinician-established PTSD diagnosis based on DSM criteria or structured diagnostic interviews; (2) probable PTSD based on validated symptom cut-offs; (3) subthreshold PTSD; or (4) symptom-based grouping within trauma-exposed samples.

This categorisation was used to interpret findings cautiously and to avoid assuming full diagnostic equivalence across studies using different case-definition strategies. In particular, studies relying on formal diagnostic interviews were distinguished from studies using validated self-report PTSD symptom measures or symptom cut-offs to identify probable or subthreshold PTSD. Trauma-exposed samples were included only when PTSD symptoms were explicitly analyzed in relation to eye-tracking outcomes. This approach allowed the review to retain clinically relevant evidence while preserving methodological transparency regarding differences in PTSD ascertainment across the literature.

Data charting and extraction

For each included study, the following information was extracted: author/year, sample characteristics, trauma type, PTSD ascertainment method, study objectives, task paradigm, eye-tracking device and sampling rate, gaze variables, derived attentional constructs, key findings, neurobiological correlates when available, and study limitations.

Synthesis strategy

Given the substantial heterogeneity of the included studies, findings were synthesised narratively rather than quantitatively. The synthesis focused on recurring domains: sustained attention to threat, initial orienting and disengagement, inhibitory control/executive control, attentional avoidance, attention bias variability, and studies integrating neurobiological measures.

Results

The study selection process is summarised in Figure 1. A total of 132 records were initially identified through database searches. After duplicate removal, 118 records remained and were screened based on titles and abstracts. Of these, 89 were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria, for example because they did not use eye-tracking methodology, did not include PTSD-relevant clinical or symptom-based analyses, or had an unrelated focus. The full texts of the remaining 29 articles were assessed for eligibility. Twelve studies were further excluded because they lacked PTSD clinical diagnosis or symptom-based PTSD analyses, relied solely on reaction-time tasks without eye-tracking, provided insufficient methodological detail, or were non-empirical publication types such as reviews or editorials. As a result, 17 empirical studies met all inclusion criteria and were retained for final analysis. Table 1 below provides a detailed summary of the empirical studies included in the final review. Overview of included studies and paradigms

The final corpus comprised 17 empirical studies employing eye-tracking in the context of PTSD. Free-viewing or related visual-exploration paradigms represented the most common approach, being used in eight studies. Smaller subsets of studies employed antisaccade or oculomotor-control paradigms (n = 2), eye-tracking-enhanced dot-probe paradigms (n = 2), visual-search paradigms (n = 2), continuous-performance protocols with integrated eye-tracking (n = 1), and emotion-recognition tasks with eye-tracking (n = 1). Two studies used multimodal designs integrating eye-tracking with neurobiological measures, including fMRI or EEG. These categories were used descriptively and were not treated as strictly mutually exclusive, because some studies combined task paradigms with multimodal neurobiological measures or incorporated overlapping methodological features. Across studies, substantial heterogeneity was observed in trauma type, sample composition, PTSD ascertainment, eye-tracking indices, and task demands.

Sustained attention to negative and trauma-related stimuli

Across several studies, participants with PTSD showed longer dwell time or greater fixation allocation to negatively valenced, angry, fearful, or trauma-related stimuli than control groups. This pattern was reported in free-viewing, matrix-viewing, and eye-tracking-enhanced dot-probe paradigms, including Kimble et al. (2010), Powers et al. (2019), Suarez-Jimenez et al. (2022), Lazarov et al. (2022), Veerapa et al. (2023), and Alon et al. (2023). Evidence of increased attention to negative material was also observed in trauma-exposed control groups in Suarez-Jimenez et al. (2022) and Lazarov et al. (2022), suggesting that trauma exposure itself may be associated with altered gaze allocation to negative stimuli. However, the magnitude or clinical specificity of sustained attention to negative material was generally greater in PTSD samples, particularly in studies comparing PTSD, trauma-exposed healthy controls, and non-exposed healthy controls.

Initial orienting, disengagement, and temporal dynamics

Findings concerning initial orienting, disengagement, and temporal dynamics were examined primarily in relation to negative, threatening, emotionally salient, or trauma-related stimuli. Overall, these findings were less uniform than those concerning sustained attention. Kimble et al. (2010) reported a trend toward first fixations on trauma-related images, suggesting possible early orienting toward trauma-relevant material. Thomas et al. (2013) observed a dynamic temporal pattern during a six-second free-viewing task, with early attention to trauma-related cues during the first interval, relative avoidance during the middle interval, and later re-engagement. Powers et al. (2019) found that early and sustained attention to angry faces were differentially associated with PTSD symptom clusters, as described in greater detail in the subsection on symptom-related and trauma-related heterogeneity.

In the domain of oculomotor control, Blekic et al. (2021) used an emotional antisaccade paradigm involving emotionally salient negative information. Relative to healthy controls, the PTSD group showed faster initial orienting toward emotional stimuli, more antisaccade errors, greater intra-individual variability, and longer disengagement times. These findings indicate that emotionally salient stimuli may interfere with both inhibitory control and attentional disengagement in PTSD. Zidda et al. (2025) further documented delayed fixation to contextual information relative to trauma-related cues, suggesting that abnormalities in the temporal allocation of gaze may also involve reduced or delayed processing of contextual information when trauma-related material competes for attention.

Inhibitory control and executive control findings

Antisaccade and related oculomotor-control paradigms identified alterations in inhibitory control. Reinhard et al. (2017) found longer antisaccade latencies and higher error rates in PTSD, particularly for fearful-face stimuli. Blekic et al. (2021) similarly reported more antisaccade errors, greater intra-individual variability, and altered orienting to emotional stimuli. Hungerford et al. (2023), using the BEAM protocol in active-duty service members with mild traumatic brain injury, found modest associations between PTSD symptoms and oculomotor attention measures. This relatively modest association is somewhat unexpected given earlier neuropsychological evidence that PTSD in veteran populations is reliably associated with generalised attentional and memory deficits relative to trauma-exposed veterans without the disorder (Vasterling et al., 2002), suggesting that oculomotor indices and broader neuropsychological measures may not always converge, particularly in samples with comorbid conditions such as traumatic brain injury.

Attentional avoidance and reduced visual exploration

Several recent studies also described patterns consistent with attentional avoidance or reduced visual exploration. Blekic et al. (2025) reported reduced dwell time on emotional targets and shorter scanpaths in a Face-in-the-Crowd paradigm. Roy et al. (2022) found altered fixation and saccade patterns under cognitive load, and Thomas et al. (2013) documented within-trial shifts from early engagement to later avoidance. These findings are broadly consistent with earlier neuroimaging evidence linking attention bias to threat with altered activation in amygdala–prefrontal circuitry in PTSD, although that evidence was derived from reaction-time-based paradigms rather than direct eye-tracking measures (Fani et al., 2012a). These findings indicate that the direction of attentional allocation in PTSD was not limited to sustained engagement with threat across all paradigms.

Attention bias variability

Attention bias variability was examined in studies using free-viewing and combined eye-tracking/reaction-time paradigms. Alon et al. (2023), who compared participants with PTSD, trauma-exposed healthy controls, and non-exposed healthy controls, found that dwell time percentage differentiated the PTSD group from both trauma-exposed healthy controls and healthy controls. In contrast, attention bias variability was elevated in both trauma-exposed groups relative to healthy controls, suggesting that variability in attentional allocation may reflect trauma exposure more broadly, whereas sustained dwell time on negative material may be more specifically associated with PTSD status.

Lev et al. (2025) also included PTSD, trauma-exposed healthy control, and non-exposed healthy control groups and compared reaction-time-based and eye-tracking-based indices of attention bias variability. In that study, both reaction-time-based and eye-tracking-based attention bias variability were elevated in the PTSD group, and eye-tracking-based variability accounted for additional variance in PTSD symptom severity. These findings suggest that attentional instability may be clinically relevant in PTSD; however, its interpretation should take into account whether the observed differences distinguish PTSD specifically from trauma-exposed controls or instead reflect a broader effect of trauma exposure.

Multimodal and neurobiological findings

Two studies combined eye-tracking with neurobiological measures. Suarez-Jimenez et al. (2022) integrated eye-tracking with resting-state fMRI and related sustained attention to negatively valenced faces to patterns of resting-state functional connectivity involving the nucleus accumbens and orbitofrontal cortex. Zidda et al. (2025) combined eye-tracking with high-density EEG and documented early perceptual alterations together with delayed contextual gaze allocation and impaired associative memory. Given the limited number of multimodal studies, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary but informative for generating hypotheses about the neural and perceptual mechanisms underlying attentional dysregulation in PTSD.

Symptom-related and trauma-related heterogeneity

Several studies indicated that eye-tracking patterns varied according to PTSD symptom dimensions, trauma typology, and trauma-related clinical characteristics. Powers et al. (2019) examined 70 women using a modified dot-probe task with angry and neutral faces and recorded both initial fixation during the early phase of stimulus presentation and total dwell time across the full viewing interval. At the group level, women with PTSD showed longer dwell time on angry faces than controls. Symptom-level analyses further indicated that avoidance symptoms were associated with a greater likelihood of initially fixating angry faces, whereas re-experiencing symptoms were associated with longer sustained attention to angry faces. Thus, different PTSD symptom clusters were associated with distinct temporal components of threat processing: avoidance with early orienting and re-experiencing with prolonged attentional engagement.

Evidence of heterogeneity was also observed across trauma-exposed subgroups. Early neuropsychological research had already documented attentional dysfunction specifically among rape survivors with PTSD (Jenkins et al., 2000), foreshadowing the more recent eye-tracking evidence that trauma subtype shapes the direction and duration of attentional allocation. Lizarán Villalba et al. (2025) compared survivors of drug-facilitated sexual assault, survivors of non-drug-facilitated sexual assault, and healthy controls using a free-viewing task with emotional and neutral images. Participants exposed to drug-facilitated sexual assault showed greater sustained engagement with threatening material during later stages of processing, whereas the non-drug-facilitated sexual-assault group did not show the same pattern of emotional modulation. Within the trauma-exposed participants, avoidance symptoms were associated with reduced fixation time on emotional scenes, while dissociative symptoms were associated with increased fixation on neutral stimuli. These results indicate that both trauma subtype and symptom composition influenced the direction and duration of attentional allocation.

Flechsenhar et al. (2024) examined emotion recognition and gaze behaviour in participants with PTSD, major depressive disorder, somatic symptom disorder, and healthy controls. The PTSD group showed slower responses and more emotion-recognition errors, particularly when evaluating neutral faces. Across diagnostic groups, greater childhood trauma exposure was associated with longer saccade latencies and poorer recognition accuracy. These findings suggest that some visual-attentional abnormalities may be associated specifically with PTSD, whereas others may reflect broader developmental or transdiagnostic effects of childhood trauma.

These studies show that attentional dysregulation was not uniform across individuals with PTSD. Early orienting, sustained engagement, avoidance, and attention to neutral or ambiguous information varied according to symptom cluster, dissociation, trauma typology, and developmental trauma history. This is consistent with broader psychometric evidence indicating that PTSD symptoms are highly interrelated with general distress, complicating efforts to isolate cluster-specific mechanisms such as attentional dysregulation (Marshall et al., 2010).

Discussion

The present scoping review mapped 17 empirical studies using eye-tracking to investigate attentional processing, visual exploration, and oculomotor control in post-traumatic stress disorder and trauma-exposed samples. Overall, the available findings do not support a single, uniform attentional phenotype in PTSD. The comparatively most recurrent pattern was greater sustained allocation of gaze to negatively valenced, threatening, or trauma-related stimuli. Evidence regarding initial vigilance was less consistent, while a smaller number of studies documented delayed disengagement, attentional avoidance, reduced visual exploration, instability in attentional allocation, and impaired inhibitory control. These patterns varied across paradigms, stimulus types, processing stages, trauma-exposure groups, and symptom profiles.

The findings suggest that PTSD-related attentional dysregulation is better conceptualised as heterogeneous and context-sensitive than as a stable tendency toward hypervigilance alone. Eye-tracking is particularly valuable in this respect because it enables the temporal decomposition of attention into partially distinct processes, including initial orienting, sustained engagement, disengagement, later avoidance, re-engagement, visual-search organisation, and trial-by-trial variability. At the same time, the limited number of studies, marked methodological heterogeneity, and differences in PTSD ascertainment preclude strong conclusions regarding diagnostic specificity, causal mechanisms, or current clinical applicability.

Vigilance and sustained attention to threat

The most recurrent finding across the included studies concerned sustained attention to negatively valenced or trauma-related material. Several investigations reported longer dwell time, greater fixation allocation, or more persistent gaze toward angry, fearful, negative, or trauma-relevant stimuli in participants with PTSD. This pattern emerged across free-viewing, matrix-viewing, and eye-tracking-enhanced dot-probe paradigms, including the studies by Kimble et al. (2010), Powers et al. (2019), Suarez-Jimenez et al. (2022), Lazarov et al. (2022), Veerapa et al. (2023), and Alon et al. (2023).

These findings provide moderately recurrent evidence that PTSD may be associated with prolonged attentional engagement with emotionally negative information. The result is especially relevant because dwell time and related fixation measures provide a more direct index of visual allocation than reaction-time measures. Veerapa et al. (2023), for example, did not identify a significant attentional bias using reaction-time indices, whereas eye-tracking showed sustained attention toward negative scenes in the PTSD group, particularly at longer stimulus presentations. This finding illustrates how reaction-time tasks may fail to detect attentional processes that become visible when gaze is measured continuously.

However, sustained attention to negative material should not automatically be interpreted as a PTSD-specific marker. In studies that included trauma-exposed healthy controls, increased attention to negative stimuli was sometimes observed in both PTSD and trauma-exposed groups. Suarez-Jimenez et al. (2022) found greater dwell time on negatively valenced faces in both groups relative to non-exposed controls, while Lazarov et al. (2022) similarly reported increased sustained attention in trauma-exposed participants, although the effect was stronger in the PTSD group. Trauma exposure itself may therefore alter the salience or processing priority of negative information, while PTSD may be associated with a greater magnitude or persistence of this effect.

The evidence for initial vigilance, understood as rapid preferential orienting toward threat, was less consistent than the evidence for sustained engagement. Kimble et al. (2010) reported only a trend toward first fixations on trauma-related images, and other studies did not consistently identify PTSD-specific differences in first-fixation location or latency. Lazarov et al. (2022), for instance, found group differences in total dwell time but not in first-fixation dwell measures. Accordingly, the reviewed literature provides stronger support for altered maintenance of attention than for a generalised, automatic tendency to orient initially toward threat.

This distinction is theoretically important. Greater sustained attention may reflect prolonged engagement, difficulty disengaging, repeated re-engagement, or continued competition from emotionally salient information. These mechanisms cannot always be separated using total dwell time alone. Future studies should therefore avoid treating all increased gaze allocation as equivalent evidence of hypervigilance and should instead distinguish early orienting from later maintenance and disengagement processes.

Initial orienting, disengagement, and temporal dynamics

The findings concerning initial orienting and disengagement indicate that attentional abnormalities in PTSD may unfold dynamically over time. Rather than displaying a constant attentional preference or avoidance response, participants may shift between engagement, withdrawal, and re-engagement during the same trial or across task conditions.

Thomas et al. (2013) provided one of the clearest examples of this temporal complexity. During a six-second free-viewing task, participants with PTSD initially allocated attention to trauma-related cues, showed relative avoidance during the middle interval, and then re-engaged with the same material at a later stage. Participants with subthreshold symptoms showed a different profile characterised by more sustained engagement. These findings suggest that similar overall fixation totals may conceal distinct temporal trajectories and that PTSD-related attentional responding may involve oscillation rather than a stable bias in one direction.

Evidence of disengagement difficulty also emerged from oculomotor-control studies. Blekic et al. (2021) reported faster initial orienting toward emotionally salient stimuli, more antisaccade errors, greater intra-individual variability, and longer disengagement times in the PTSD group. These findings suggest that emotionally salient material may not only capture attention but may also interfere with the ability to redirect gaze according to task demands. Roy et al. (2022) similarly observed longer individual fixations and altered fixation and saccade rates under cognitive load, a pattern that may reflect reduced efficiency in disengaging from currently processed visual information.

Zidda et al. (2025) extended the analysis of temporal allocation by showing delayed fixation on contextual information relative to trauma-related cues. In that study, the gaze abnormality did not consist solely of increased attention to the trauma cue; it also involved reduced or delayed attention to the surrounding context. This distinction is relevant because adaptive threat processing requires not only detecting salient cues but also integrating them with contextual information that may indicate whether danger is current, probable, or no longer present.

Nevertheless, conclusions regarding initial orienting and disengagement remain tentative. Only a limited number of studies directly assessed these processes, and they used different tasks, stimuli, time windows, and operational definitions. Some findings may reflect trauma-related attentional capture, whereas others may reflect general emotional arousal, slowed information processing, or executive-control difficulty. The current evidence therefore supports the presence of altered temporal dynamics but does not yet establish a single PTSD-specific sequence of vigilance, disengagement failure, and avoidance.

Attentional avoidance and reduced visual exploration

Although early accounts of PTSD attentional bias emphasised vigilance and sustained monitoring of threat, several recent studies identified patterns consistent with attentional avoidance or reduced visual exploration. These findings do not necessarily contradict evidence of sustained attention. Avoidance may occur at a later processing stage, under increased cognitive load, in particular symptom subgroups, or in response to specific types of emotional material.

Blekic et al. (2025) found reduced dwell time on emotional targets and shorter scanpaths in a Face-in-the-Crowd visual-search paradigm. The PTSD group therefore appeared to sample less visual information and to reduce engagement with emotionally salient targets. Because the task required active search within a competitive visual array, the findings may reflect an avoidance strategy that becomes especially apparent when perceptual and cognitive demands are elevated.

Roy et al. (2022) also reported reduced fixation and saccade rates during a dual-task condition involving cognitive load. Although both PTSD and control participants reduced visual exploration while performing the concurrent task, the PTSD group showed a distinct pattern of longer individual fixations and altered search behaviour. This may indicate less flexible distribution of visual attention when processing resources are constrained. Thomas et al. (2013), in turn, showed that avoidance may emerge within a specific temporal interval rather than characterise the entire viewing period.

Avoidance should therefore be interpreted as a potentially task-dependent and temporally specific phenomenon. It may function as a strategic or partially automatic attempt to reduce emotional activation after an initial encounter with threatening material. Alternatively, reduced exploration may reflect cognitive overload, narrowed attentional scope, impaired search efficiency, or a failure to process contextual information rather than deliberate avoidance.

The available evidence is not yet sufficient to conclude that attentional avoidance is a stable characteristic of PTSD. The relevant studies remain few, recent, and methodologically diverse. Reduced dwell time and shorter scanpaths are not process-pure measures: they may reflect avoidance, reduced interest, faster detection, perceptual narrowing, fatigue, or inefficient visual search. Future studies should combine eye-tracking with subjective affect ratings, task-performance measures, psychophysiology, and experimental manipulations of cognitive load to clarify the functional meaning of reduced gaze allocation.

Attention bias variability

Attention bias variability has been proposed as an index of fluctuations in attentional allocation between threat and non-threat stimuli. Unlike mean bias scores, which summarise the average direction of attention across a task, variability measures aim to capture instability over time or across trials. This approach is conceptually relevant to PTSD because the disorder may involve alternating vigilance, avoidance, and re-engagement rather than a stable attentional preference. Early behavioural work using reaction-time-based indices had already linked greater attention bias variability to PTSD symptom severity (Iacoviello et al., 2014), providing the conceptual foundation for subsequent eye-tracking-based investigations of this construct.

Alon et al. (2023) found that dwell-time percentage differentiated participants with PTSD from both trauma-exposed and non-exposed healthy controls. Attention bias variability, by contrast, was elevated in both trauma-exposed groups relative to non-exposed controls. This pattern suggests that sustained attention to negative stimuli may have greater specificity for PTSD, whereas variability may partly reflect the broader consequences of trauma exposure.

Lev et al. (2025) compared reaction-time-based and eye-tracking-based attention bias variability and found that both indices were elevated in PTSD. Importantly, eye-tracking-based variability accounted for additional variance in PTSD symptom severity. This result suggests that continuous gaze measures may capture clinically relevant instability that is not fully represented by reaction-time indices.

Despite these promising findings, attention bias variability remains supported by a small number of studies. Its calculation also varies across the literature, and variability scores may be influenced by measurement error, trial number, task reliability, stimulus selection, and general response inconsistency. Elevated variability should not therefore be assumed to represent a specific oscillation between vigilance and avoidance unless the temporal sequence of gaze allocation is directly demonstrated.

Further research should establish the test–retest reliability of eye-tracking-based variability measures, their specificity relative to trauma exposure and other psychiatric conditions, and their incremental validity beyond mean dwell time, symptom severity, and general cognitive variability. At present, attention bias variability is best regarded as a promising but insufficiently replicated construct.

Inhibitory and oculomotor control

The antisaccade and related oculomotor-control studies provide preliminary evidence that PTSD may be associated with impaired inhibitory control, particularly in the presence of emotionally salient stimuli. Antisaccade tasks require participants to suppress a reflexive eye movement toward a peripheral cue and generate a voluntary saccade in the opposite direction. Performance therefore depends on the inhibition of a prepotent response, maintenance of task goals, and flexible control of gaze.

Reinhard et al. (2017) reported longer antisaccade latencies and higher error rates in participants with PTSD, particularly when fearful faces were presented. Antisaccade performance also predicted PTSD symptom severity. Blekic et al. (2021) similarly found more antisaccade errors, greater response variability, faster orienting to emotional stimuli, and longer disengagement times in the PTSD group. Together, these studies suggest that emotionally salient information may increase the demands placed on top-down control systems and interfere with the ability to regulate gaze according to task requirements.

Hungerford et al. (2023) found weaker associations between PTSD symptoms and oculomotor attention measures in active-duty service members with mild traumatic brain injury. Traditional neuropsychological measures showed stronger effect sizes, although the eye-tracking protocol identified distinct oculomotor patterns. This pattern is broadly consistent with earlier neuropsychological research documenting attentional and executive deficits in combat-related PTSD using standard cognitive testing (Crowell et al., 2002), suggesting that oculomotor and conventional neuropsychological indices may capture partially overlapping but not identical aspects of attentional dysfunction. These findings illustrate both the potential and the limitations of oculomotor measures. They may detect subtle aspects of visual attention and response control but may not always outperform broader neuropsychological assessments, particularly in clinically complex samples.

The available evidence therefore suggests preliminary convergence regarding altered inhibitory control, but the literature remains small. This convergence is consistent with earlier behavioural evidence using attentional-network paradigms, which similarly identified executive-control deficits (rather than deficits in alerting or orienting alone) as a distinguishing feature of PTSD (Leskin & White, 2007). This convergence extends beyond oculomotor paradigms: electrophysiological studies using Go/NoGo continuous performance tasks have similarly identified attention-related deficits in PTSD, indexed by altered event-related potentials during response inhibition (Shucard et al., 2008), further supporting the presence of impaired inhibitory control across multiple measurement modalities. Two of the principal studies used relatively limited clinical samples, and the larger study involved participants with mild traumatic brain injury, making it difficult to isolate PTSD-specific effects. It also remains unclear whether poorer antisaccade performance is primarily driven by emotional salience or reflects a more general executive-control difficulty.

Future research should compare emotional and non-emotional antisaccade conditions, include trauma-exposed control groups, assess comorbid depression, anxiety, dissociation, and traumatic brain injury, and examine associations with distinct PTSD symptom clusters. Longitudinal designs would also be needed to determine whether inhibitory-control abnormalities precede symptom persistence, emerge as a consequence of PTSD, or change with treatment.

Symptom-related and trauma-related heterogeneity

A major conclusion of the reviewed literature is that attentional patterns vary according to symptom composition, trauma typology, developmental history, and other clinical characteristics. This heterogeneity may help explain why different studies have identified apparently contrasting patterns of vigilance, sustained engagement, avoidance, and reduced exploration.

Powers et al. (2019) found that different PTSD symptom clusters were associated with distinct temporal components of attention. Avoidance symptoms were related to a greater likelihood of initially fixating angry faces, whereas re-experiencing symptoms were associated with prolonged dwell time. Although the association between avoidance symptoms and initial orientation to threat may appear counterintuitive, it is compatible with the possibility that individuals first detect threatening information and subsequently attempt to regulate distress through avoidance.

Lizarán Villalba et al. (2025) further demonstrated that attentional allocation varied across sexual-assault subgroups and according to avoidance and dissociative symptoms. Survivors of drug-facilitated sexual assault showed greater sustained engagement with threat at later processing stages, whereas the non-drug-facilitated assault group did not show the same pattern. Avoidance symptoms were associated with reduced fixation on emotional scenes, while dissociative symptoms were associated with greater attention to neutral stimuli. These findings indicate that trauma typology and symptom profile may influence both the direction and temporal course of gaze allocation.

Flechsenhar et al. (2024) showed that childhood trauma was associated with longer saccade latencies and poorer emotion-recognition accuracy across diagnostic groups. The PTSD group also demonstrated slower responses and greater difficulty classifying neutral facial expressions. Some visual-attentional alterations may therefore be linked specifically to PTSD, whereas others may reflect broader developmental or transdiagnostic consequences of early trauma. This is consistent with earlier behavioural evidence indicating that processing biases for emotional information are not uniform across clinical disorders in children and adolescents, but vary according to diagnostic category, including PTSD, depression, and generalized anxiety (Dalgleish et al., 2003).

The distinction between PTSD, trauma exposure, and broader psychopathology is especially important. Several studies included trauma-exposed healthy control groups, while others compared PTSD only with non-exposed controls. Effects that distinguish PTSD from non-exposed participants but not from trauma-exposed participants may reflect trauma-related adaptation rather than disorder-specific dysfunction. Similarly, studies including probable or subthreshold PTSD cannot be assumed to represent the same clinical population as studies using structured diagnostic interviews.

The reviewed evidence therefore supports a heterogeneous attentional phenotype. PTSD should not be treated as if all individuals display the same pattern of gaze behaviour. Symptom clusters, dissociation, trauma type, cumulative or developmental trauma exposure, comorbidity, and current cognitive load may all influence whether attention is directed toward, maintained on, redirected away from, or repeatedly shifted between emotional stimuli.

Future studies should move beyond simple case–control comparisons and examine dimensional symptom profiles, clinically meaningful subgroups, and interactions between trauma characteristics and task conditions. Person-centered approaches, latent-profile analysis, or longitudinal trajectory models may be useful for identifying distinct attentional phenotypes. However, such approaches will require considerably larger samples than those used in most studies included in this review.

Exploratory neurobiological findings

Only two included studies directly combined eye-tracking with neurobiological measures. These findings are informative but should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of an established neurobiological model of attentional dysregulation in PTSD.

Suarez-Jimenez et al. (2022) combined free-viewing eye-tracking with resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging. Participants with PTSD and trauma-exposed healthy controls showed greater sustained attention to negatively valenced faces than non-exposed controls. Within the PTSD group, greater dwell time on negative faces was associated with weaker resting-state connectivity between the nucleus accumbens and orbitofrontal cortex. The direction of the association differed in healthy controls. The authors interpreted this pattern as potentially reflecting altered integration between salience, reward, and regulatory processes.

Zidda et al. (2025) combined eye-tracking with high-density electroencephalography during the processing of trauma-related cues embedded in neutral contexts. Participants with PTSD showed altered early perceptual responses, delayed fixation on contextual information, and impaired associative memory. These findings suggest that trauma-related attentional dysregulation may emerge at early sensory-perceptual stages and may interfere with the integration of salient cues into their broader context.

These studies offer potential mechanistic explanations for sustained engagement with negative information and delayed contextual processing. However, they differ substantially in their neurobiological measures, tasks, and target processes. One examined resting-state connectivity in relation to gaze toward emotional faces, whereas the other investigated early electrophysiological processing and contextual memory. Their findings cannot yet be integrated into a single explanatory model.

Broader theories concerning altered salience processing, impaired contextualisation, and inefficient top-down regulation are compatible with these results, but they extend beyond what can be directly concluded from the included multimodal evidence. These theoretical accounts are broadly consistent with quantitative meta-analytic evidence of altered neural activity across salience, threat-processing, and regulatory brain regions in PTSD more generally (Hayes et al., 2012a). Replication in larger samples, task-based neuroimaging, longitudinal designs, and standardised multimodal protocols will be necessary before specific neural mechanisms can be linked reliably to eye-tracking phenotypes in PTSD.

Broader cognitive interpretation

The empirical patterns identified in this review can be situated within models proposing an altered interaction between bottom-up salience processing and top-down attentional control. Trauma-related and negatively valenced cues may acquire heightened motivational significance and therefore compete strongly for processing resources. This heightened salience may be reflected not only in attentional capture but also in excessive psychophysiological fear expression, proposed as an intermediate phenotype linking trauma exposure to clinical symptoms (Norrholm et al., 2015). At the same time, inefficient inhibitory or regulatory control may make it more difficult to disengage from these cues, redistribute attention, or integrate contextual information.

This framework may help explain why sustained engagement, delayed disengagement, avoidance, and attentional variability are not necessarily mutually exclusive. Rapid or sustained capture by threat may be followed by strategic withdrawal when emotional activation becomes difficult to regulate. Under increased cognitive demand, the same individual may show reduced exploration or narrowed attentional scope. Across trials, these shifts may appear as elevated attention bias variability.

Attentional Control Theory similarly proposes that anxiety-related conditions reduce the efficiency of goal-directed control when threat-related stimuli compete for attention. Applied to PTSD, this account suggests that abnormalities may be most evident under emotional or cognitive load, when inhibitory control and flexible shifting are required. The antisaccade and dual-task findings are compatible with this interpretation.

However, this broader cognitive account should be distinguished from the direct findings of the review. Not all included studies assessed bottom-up capture, top-down control, or emotional regulation explicitly. Moreover, similar gaze patterns may arise from different mechanisms. Longer dwell time may reflect difficulty disengaging, deliberate monitoring, stimulus relevance, or impaired search efficiency. Reduced fixation may reflect avoidance, perceptual narrowing, or reduced task engagement. Theoretical models are therefore useful for organising the findings but should not be treated as confirmed explanations.

Methodological implications of eye-tracking research

The reviewed studies illustrate the methodological value of eye-tracking for PTSD research. Compared with reaction-time paradigms, eye-tracking provides continuous and relatively direct information about where and when visual attention is allocated. This makes it possible to distinguish initial orienting from sustained attention, disengagement, re-engagement, visual exploration, and attentional instability.

Such distinctions are important because a single reaction-time bias score may arise from different attentional sequences. An apparently neutral average score, for example, could reflect no bias, alternating vigilance and avoidance, rapid engagement followed by withdrawal, or substantial interindividual variability. Eye-tracking can reveal these temporal patterns and may therefore clarify inconsistencies previously reported with emotional Stroop and dot-probe tasks.

The included studies also show that different paradigms provide information about different components of attention. Free-viewing tasks are useful for assessing spontaneous gaze allocation and sustained engagement. Dot-probe tasks augmented with eye-tracking permit comparison between direct gaze measures and traditional reaction-time indices. Antisaccade tasks assess inhibitory and oculomotor control. Visual-search tasks provide information about exploration strategies under competition or load. Multimodal paradigms can relate gaze patterns to neural or electrophysiological processes.

Nevertheless, the field remains highly heterogeneous. Studies differed in eye-tracking devices, sampling frequencies, calibration procedures, fixation algorithms, areas of interest, stimulus duration, emotional categories, time windows, and derived variables. Dwell time, first-fixation measures, scanpath length, gaze variability, and antisaccade errors were not always defined or calculated consistently. This limits direct comparison and prevents quantitative synthesis.

Attention bias variability poses a particular methodological challenge. Different computational approaches can produce different estimates, and variability scores may contain both meaningful attentional fluctuation and measurement noise. Studies should report the exact formula used, the number and duration of trials, data-cleaning procedures, reliability indices, and sensitivity analyses.

Future research would benefit from consensus-based reporting standards for eye-tracking studies in PTSD. At minimum, studies should report device characteristics, sampling rate, calibration accuracy, fixation-detection criteria, missing-data thresholds, area-of-interest definitions, stimulus timing, preprocessing steps, metric calculation, and reliability. Sharing analytic code and de-identified gaze data where ethically feasible would further improve transparency and reproducibility.

Limitations of the available evidence and of the review

Several limitations of the evidence base should be considered. First, the literature remains small, with only 17 studies meeting the inclusion criteria. Within individual domains, such as antisaccade performance, attentional avoidance, attention bias variability, and multimodal neurobiology, conclusions often depend on two or three studies.

Second, many studies used small samples. This limits statistical power, increases the likelihood of unstable effect estimates, and restricts the possibility of examining interactions between symptom clusters, trauma characteristics, sex, comorbidity, and task conditions. Small samples are especially problematic when studies calculate complex or highly variable eye-tracking indices.

Third, PTSD ascertainment differed substantially. Some studies used clinician-established diagnoses or structured interviews, whereas others relied on self-report cut-offs, probable PTSD, subthreshold symptoms, or dimensional symptom analyses in trauma-exposed samples. These approaches provide complementary information but should not be treated as clinically equivalent.

Fourth, the included populations were heterogeneous and sometimes highly selective. Several studies focused on military personnel or veterans, some included predominantly or exclusively women, and others examined specific trauma types such as interpersonal or sexual trauma. One study involved active-duty personnel with mild traumatic brain injury. These characteristics limit generalizability and may partly account for variation in findings. Notably, none of the included eye-tracking studies examined children or adolescents, despite existing behavioural and electrophysiological evidence that response-inhibition deficits associated with PTSD are also present in younger trauma-exposed populations, such as adolescent earthquake survivors (Wu et al., 2010). This developmental gap represents an important direction for future eye-tracking research, given that attentional and inhibitory-control mechanisms may follow distinct developmental trajectories.

Fifth, comorbid conditions were not assessed or controlled consistently. Depression, anxiety, dissociation, substance use, sleep disturbance, traumatic brain injury, and medication may influence gaze behaviour and cognitive control. Excluding common comorbidities can produce highly selected samples, whereas failing to measure them makes it difficult to determine whether observed effects are specific to PTSD.

Sixth, study designs were predominantly cross-sectional. Consequently, it is not possible to determine whether attentional abnormalities represent vulnerability factors, consequences of trauma exposure, correlates of current symptom severity, or mechanisms maintaining PTSD. Prospective studies beginning after trauma exposure and longitudinal studies across treatment are required to clarify temporal and causal relationships.

Seventh, the field lacks consistent evidence regarding test–retest reliability, convergent validity, and predictive validity. A measure may differentiate groups in a single study without being sufficiently stable or accurate for individual-level use. Reliability should therefore be established before eye-tracking indices are proposed as biomarkers or clinical tools.

The present review also has limitations. As a scoping review, its objective was to map the breadth and conceptual structure of the literature rather than to conduct a formal risk-of-bias assessment or meta-analysis. The heterogeneity of samples, paradigms, and outcome measures made quantitative synthesis inappropriate. Consequently, descriptions of stronger or more recurrent evidence are based on the number, consistency, and methodological characteristics of the included studies rather than on pooled effect sizes.

Although the search covered three major databases and was updated through 2025, relevant studies may have been missed because of differences in terminology, indexing, or publication status. Restriction to peer-reviewed empirical studies may also have increased the potential for publication bias. Finally, the interpretive categorisation of findings into vigilance, sustained attention, disengagement, avoidance, variability, and inhibitory control involves some conceptual overlap, because individual eye-tracking measures are not always specific to a single cognitive process.

Potential clinical perspectives

Eye-tracking has potential clinical relevance because it can characterise attentional processes that may not be apparent from self-report or reaction-time measures alone. However, the current evidence does not support its use as an established diagnostic, prognostic, or treatment-selection instrument in PTSD. Established evidence-based treatments for PTSD, including trauma-focused psychotherapies and pharmacological interventions, remain the primary standard of care (Difede et al., 2014), and eye-tracking should currently be regarded as a complementary research tool rather than a replacement for these approaches.

At present, the most plausible clinical contribution is as an adjunctive research and monitoring method. Eye-tracking could potentially identify whether an individual shows sustained engagement with threat, delayed disengagement, reduced contextual exploration, avoidance under load, or marked variability across time. Such profiles may eventually help refine individualised formulations, particularly if they prove stable, symptom-specific, and responsive to treatment.

Eye-tracking measures could also be used to evaluate mechanisms of change during trauma-focused therapy, cognitive interventions, exposure-based treatment, or attention-bias modification. According to emotional processing theory, effective exposure-based treatment requires activation of the fear structure alongside the incorporation of corrective information (Foa & Kozak, 1986); eye-tracking could potentially provide an objective index of whether such activation and engagement with corrective, contextual information occurs during treatment. Given that effective cognitive-behavioral treatments for PTSD are thought to operate through mechanisms such as fear extinction, cognitive restructuring, and emotional processing (Zalta, 2015), eye-tracking could offer an objective, process-level index of whether these underlying mechanisms are engaged during treatment. Repeated assessment might show whether treatment reduces sustained attention to threat, improves contextual exploration, increases inhibitory control, or stabilises attentional allocation. These possibilities remain hypothetical because few studies have examined longitudinal change or treatment sensitivity.

Attention-bias modification represents another possible area of development. Foundational experimental work demonstrated that inducing attentional biases can causally influence anxiety levels (Mathews & MacLeod, 2002), providing the conceptual basis for clinical applications aiming to modify attentional bias therapeutically. If specific and reliable gaze patterns are shown to contribute to symptom maintenance, gaze-contingent or feedback-based interventions could be designed to modify them. Feedback-based training approaches, such as neurofeedback protocols, have already shown promise in improving attentional and self-regulatory skills in other performance-related domains (Corrado et al., 2024), supporting the broader feasibility of biofeedback-informed strategies for modifying attentional patterns in clinical populations. Neurofeedback-based approaches have shown preliminary effectiveness in modifying attentional and hypervigilance-related patterns in other conditions characterised by heightened salience processing, such as chronic pain (Diotaiuti et al., 2024) and precision-sport performance regulation (Corrado et al., 2024), lending indirect support to the feasibility of feedback-based strategies for retraining attentional allocation in PTSD. However, intervention development should not assume that all patients require attention to be redirected away from threat. For individuals already showing avoidance, further reduction in threat engagement could be counterproductive. A clinically meaningful approach would need to distinguish persistent vigilance from strategic avoidance and support flexible, context-appropriate attention rather than movement in a single direction.

The clinical value of eye-tracking will ultimately depend on its incremental validity. A measure should provide information beyond established diagnostic interviews, symptom scales, neuropsychological tests, and clinical history. It should also be reliable, feasible, acceptable, and interpretable at the individual level. These requirements have not yet been met.

Future research directions

Future studies should use adequately powered and diagnostically well-characterised samples. Comparisons should include both trauma-exposed participants without PTSD and non-exposed controls, allowing researchers to distinguish disorder-related effects from broader consequences of trauma exposure. Where possible, studies should also include psychiatric comparison groups to test diagnostic specificity.

Greater standardisation is needed in task design, gaze metrics, and reporting. Researchers should distinguish clearly between initial fixation, sustained dwell, disengagement latency, scanpath organisation, avoidance, and variability. Time-resolved analyses should be preferred over single aggregate scores when the objective is to identify vigilance–avoidance sequences or other dynamic patterns.

Longitudinal designs are particularly important. Prospective studies following trauma-exposed individuals could establish whether gaze abnormalities predict PTSD onset or persistence, particularly given evidence that memory and executive dysfunctions are already detectable in the acute phase of PTSD (Lagarde et al., 2010), raising the question of whether analogous attentional and oculomotor abnormalities emerge early or develop progressively over the course of the disorder. Treatment studies could assess whether attentional indices change with symptom improvement and whether baseline patterns predict response to specific interventions. Existing randomized controlled trials of attention-bias modification in PTSD have so far yielded mixed clinical outcomes when relying solely on reaction-time-based bias indices (Schoorl et al., 2013), suggesting that future intervention trials could benefit from incorporating eye-tracking-based measures to more precisely characterise treatment-related changes in attentional allocation and to identify which patients are most likely to benefit from bias-modification approaches.

Multimodal studies should be expanded but should remain closely tied to clearly defined eye-tracking phenomena. Combining eye-tracking with EEG, fMRI, autonomic measures, and pupillometry may help distinguish early sensory capture, emotional arousal, cognitive control, and contextual integration. Larger samples and replication across laboratories will be essential.

Research on heterogeneity should examine symptom clusters, dissociation, developmental trauma, trauma typology, comorbidity, sex, age, and medication. Person-centered analyses may identify subgroups characterised by sustained vigilance, later avoidance, inhibitory-control difficulty, or marked instability. Such profiles should then be tested prospectively rather than inferred solely from cross-sectional group averages.

Finally, future work should establish psychometric properties. Test–retest reliability, internal consistency where appropriate, measurement invariance, convergent validity, discriminant validity, and sensitivity to clinical change should be reported systematically. Without this foundation, even theoretically informative eye-tracking findings cannot be translated responsibly into clinical practice.

Conclusion

The available eye-tracking evidence indicates that attentional processing in PTSD is heterogeneous and context-sensitive rather than adequately described by a uniform hypervigilance model. Increased sustained attention to negatively valenced or trauma-related stimuli was the most recurrent finding, whereas evidence for initial vigilance, avoidance, disengagement difficulties, attention bias variability, and inhibitory-control alterations was less consistent and derived from smaller or methodologically diverse subsets of studies. Eye-tracking therefore offers a valuable research approach for distinguishing the temporal components of attentional dysregulation that may remain obscured by reaction-time measures. However, the current evidence base is limited by methodological heterogeneity, small samples, and variable PTSD ascertainment. Eye-tracking should consequently be regarded as a promising tool for investigating attentional mechanisms and individual variability in PTSD, rather than as an established diagnostic or treatment-selection instrument.
