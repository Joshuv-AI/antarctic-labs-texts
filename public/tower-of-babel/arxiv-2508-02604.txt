Periodic robust robotic rock chop via virtual model control
Yi Zhang, Fumiya Iida, Fulvio Forni

Abstract
Robotic cutting is a challenging contact-rich manipulation task where the robot must simultaneously negotiate unknown object mechanics, large contact forces, and precise motion requirements. We introduce a new virtual-model control scheme that enables knife rocking motion for robot manipulators, without pre-planned trajectories or precise information of the environment. Motion is generated through interconnection with virtual mechanisms, given by virtual springs, dampers, and masses arranged in a suitable way. Through analysis and experiments, we demonstrate that the controlled robot behavior settles into a periodic motion. Experiments with a Franka manipulator demonstrate robust cuts with five different vegetables, and sub-millimeter slice accuracy from 1 mm to 6 mm at nearly one cut per second. The same controller survives changes in knife shape and cutting board height, and adaptation to a different humanoid manipulator, demonstrating robustness and platform independence.

The complex interaction among the knife, the material being cut, and the surrounding environment makes trajectory planning a non-trivial task. Furthermore, determining how to dynamically adjust the manipulator’s impedance in a task-relevant manner is an ongoing area of research.

Consider the ‘trivial’ task of cutting in a kitchen. The knife repeatedly comes into contact with objects of varying material properties, ranging from soft fruits, such as strawberries, to stiffer objects, such as pumpkins and a rigid cutting board.
The soft mechanics of food make accurate simulation challenging and this, in turn, complicates the application of learning- and optimization-based methods.

Accurate trajectory tracking and planning optimization do not seem to yield satisfactory results in the case of cutting.
Consider the rocking chop - a technique commonly used in vegetable cutting where the knife is rolled back and forth. Executing such a motion based on controlled predefined trajectories requires precise knowledge of the blade’s geometry to plan a corresponding trajectory

In contrast, it would be highly desirable to have a robust approach that does not depend on precise information, such as the knife geometry.
Conventional trajectory generation methods lack this generalization capability and typically rely on accurate vision sensing for re-planning

In this paper, we propose a novel approach that is less reliant on accurate information. Our approach is a virtual model controller (VMC)
[

23

capable of generating complex motions for contact-rich manipulation tasks. In our approach, the desired robot behavior emerges from the interaction between the robot’s physical mechanics, a virtual mechanism given by an organized collection of mass, springs, and dampers tailored to the task, and the environment. For cutting, these virtual components can realize the necessary motion without requiring explicit trajectory or force tracking.
The cutting action remains feasible even if the knife, cutting board or object changes, though the cutting performance may degrade and the resulting motion varies.
Moreover, by incorporating sensor feedback, cutting state can be monitored and virtual components can be optimized online to enhance cutting performance such as frequency and force needed for successful separation.
Importantly, our controller does not require extensive training data, and tuning is efficient thanks to the intuitive virtual mechanism design.

The central hypothesis of this work is that virtual model control offers a robust solution for interactive tasks, addressing the inherent limitations of trajectory-based methods, specifically their reliance on precise environmental information and the computational burden of adapting to changes. II
Related works

Predicting the mechanical interaction during food cutting is inherently challenging.
One of the reasons is that developing an accurate physical model of foodstuffs is complex.
The force required to cut through a food item depends on numerous factors, including ripeness, skin presence, orientation, slice thickness, and the knife’s rake angle and sharpness
[
24
,
25
,
26
,
27

.
Simply applying a high cutting force can yield imprecise cuts or even damage delicate produce such as tomatoes.
In earlier studies, cutting force was often treated as an external disturbance, with adaptive controllers employing position and velocity feedback to maintain predetermined trajectories
[
28
]
.
Impedance control was introduced to regulate cutting force directly, augmented by adaptive laws to accommodate time-varying desired positions and environments
[
29
]
.

Robotic cutting is commonly approached as a motion tracking problem, often informed by visual sensing.
A key challenge lies in formulating the cutting trajectory itself. While basic trajectories can be designed, more sophisticated cutting trajectories are harder to derive.
Remarkably,

[
19
]
obtained a smoothed path from camera data to separate meat muscle.

[
20
]
proposed a cut and slice trajectory adjusted through force sensing, to reduce damage to deformable objects.
More complex rolling (or rocking) actions have been addressed by leveraging the knife geometry to maintain a constant contact force
[
18
]
.

[
30
]
explored learning from demonstration, introducing a coupling term via the Udwadia–Kalaba method to suppress lateral blade movements.
Reinforcement learning is used in
[
16
]
to learn a cutting trajectory and impedance parameters.
Because developing an exact physical model of foodstuffs is challenging, learning-based approaches have also garnered attention. A series of works
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
employs data-driven model predictive control (MPC), where a neural network is used to predict future knife positions based on current position and force measurements, thus enabling real-time optimization of the reference force.
In
[
31
]
, variational autoencoders are used to model the cutting dynamics, though their primary goal is to detect material properties and thickness rather than to learn a cutting strategy.

The main difference with our approach is that our controller can generate complex rocking motion without requiring a predefined trajectory. Our controller design and deployment require minimal information about the food and the external environment (knife geometry, cutting board features). Furthermore, its design is not data-intensive and both design and deployment require minimal computing efforts. In turn, these features guarantee the robot achieves successful cutting, robustly handling different food types.

A second challenge involves managing unanticipated interactions with the environment.
Inaccurate camera-based detection of the object position, or variations in the table height, can lead to collisions or excessive forces if the robot strictly follows a fixed cutting path.
This often results in safety triggers when the commanded position is physically unachievable (e.g., below the cutting board).
To deal with the interaction, Yan et al.
[
32
,
18
]
adopt a hybrid scheme that switches to impedance or direct force control upon detecting contact with the cutting surface.
Their method segments the cutting process into distinct press, push, and slice phases, applying different controllers in each stage.
In our approach, the controller does not require the identification of different stages of cutting. Rocking is the result of the continuous interaction between robot mechanics, virtual model controller, and environment. Unanticipated interactions may degrade the performance,
but only large perturbations will disrupt the whole rocking motion.

A consistent benchmarking framework remains largely absent in the field of robotic cutting.
Although prior work often reports end-effector trajectory and force sensor data for individual cutting trials, these metrics are insufficient for real-world applications.
In particular, reliability—whether a robot can repeatedly cut and separate multiple slices with accurate thickness—remains understudied.
To address this gap, we quantify the performance of our controller against a set of relevant metrics such as cut frequency, cutting force, cutting speed, slice-thickness accuracy and variance.

III
Virtual mechanism design for cutting

III-A

Virtual model control in a nutshell

A virtual model controller consists of a (virtual) mechanical linkage combined with springs, dampers, and masses that, once coupled with the robot, shapes its behavior. It achieves this by imposing constraints on the motion of the robot and its reaction to the environment. The associated virtual forces are then realized through the robot’s available actuation, typically mapped into joint torques and executed by the motors at each joint
[
22
,
33
,
23
]
.
This mapping relies on the robot’s kinematics.

For fully actuated robot manipulator based on revolute joints, using
\boldsymbol{q}
to denote the joint coordinates of the robot, the force
\boldsymbol{f}_{i}
generated by a virtual component at a point
\boldsymbol{h}_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
of the robot is mapped into corresponding joint torques
\boldsymbol{\tau}
using the Jacobian matrix
J_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{h}_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})}{\partial\boldsymbol{q}}
.
From the principle of virtual work
[
34
]
, the torque command to emulate the forces of the virtual model controller is given by

\boldsymbol{\tau}=J_{1}(\boldsymbol{q})^{T}\boldsymbol{f}_{1}+J_{2}(\boldsymbol{q})^{T}\boldsymbol{f}_{2}+J_{3}(\boldsymbol{q})^{T}\boldsymbol{f}_{3}+\dots

(1)

Virtual model control is intrinsically stable, because the controlled robot is the interconnection of the passive mechanics of the robot and the passive virtual mechanics
[
23
]
.
Stability is guaranteed by passivity theory
[
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
,
41
]
.
Virtual model control provides an intuitive, task-oriented method for energy shaping and damping injection in robotics
[
42
,
43
]
.

The selection of spring and damper parameters and their geometric configuration also shapes the impedance of the robot
[
10
,
44
]
.
Although impedance control is typically implemented with linear springs and dampers, a nonlinear spring where force saturates can limit excessively rapid movements and enhance safety.
In this work, all springs in the virtual mechanism have a saturating nonlinear characteristic
between displacement and force given by

\boldsymbol{f}(k,\sigma,\boldsymbol{z})=\sigma\tanh(k|\boldsymbol{z}|/\sigma)\frac{\boldsymbol{z}}{|\boldsymbol{z}|},

(2)

where
k
is the stiffness parameter,
the maximum force is governed by
\sigma\geq 0
, and
\boldsymbol{z}
represents the spring displacement.

Experimental platform and cutting motion

IV-A

Robot platform and control architecture

The virtual model controller outlined in the previous section is implemented through
the VMRobotControl.jl package
[
45
]
, a platform-independent framework designed for virtual model control in robotic systems.
This package enables the construction and simulation of both the robot and virtual components.
Given the robot’s URDF representation,
the package offers several primitives to define the virtual model controller, to compute its dynamics, and to generate the torque commands for each joint.

Our experimental platform consists of a 7-DoF Franka Research 3 arm, with a maximum payload of 3 kg.
To collect force data, a ROBOTIQ FT 300-S force-torque sensor is mounted at the robot wrist, providing force and torque measurements at 100 Hz with a noise level of approximately 0.1 N.
A knife is rigidly connected to the sensor using a 3D-printed attachment as shown in Fig.
4
.
Thanks to the platform independence of the virtual model design, the cutting mechanism was also implemented on a humanoid robot.
Sciurus17, a lightweight and low-cost platform developed by RT Corp. Scirus17 features 17 degrees of freedom (DoF) distributed across its upper body (with 7 DoF per arm, 2 DoF for the head and 1 DoF for the torso).
The robot utilizes Dynamixel motors with current control, and the conversion from torque to current is approximated by means of a linear relation based on the performance characteristics of the motor.

V
Energy-based analysis of the periodic motion

Energy considerations provide insight into the emerging periodic motion.
The mechanical energy of the robot,
E_{R}
, satisfies the following standard passivity inequality
[
46
,
34
,
43
]

\dot{E}_{\mathrm{R}}\leq\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\tau}+\sum_{i}\dot{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{d}_{i}

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{q}
is the vector of joint coordinates of the robot manipulator,
\boldsymbol{\tau}
are the related motor torques, and
(\boldsymbol{s}_{i},\boldsymbol{d}_{i})
are additional co-located positions/forces variables, accounting for generic external actions to the robot.
The inequality takes into account unavoidable losses due to internal mechanical dissipation.

The controller is also a virtual mechanism, whose mechanical energy satisfies

\dot{E}_{\mathrm{VMC}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{6}\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{f}_{i}-W_{\mathrm{dissipation}}+W_{\mathrm{elastic}}.

(6)

W_{\mathrm{dissipation}}\geq 0

accounts for the power dissipated in the dampers of the virtual mechanism.
W_{\mathrm{elastic}}
captures
the power flow caused by the switching of the references
\boldsymbol{r}_{2,1}
,
\boldsymbol{r}_{2,2}
, and
\boldsymbol{r}_{S}
.

The negative sign of the term

-\sum_{i=1}^{6}\dot{\boldsymbol{p}}_{i}\boldsymbol{f}_{i}

in (
6
) is due to the mechanical interconnection between robot and virtual model controller. Through (
1
), each force
\boldsymbol{f}_{i}
acting on the robot exerts an equal and opposite force on the virtual mechanism. The total
energy of the controlled robot,

E=E_{\mathrm{R}}+E_{\mathrm{VMC}}
,
thus satisfies

\dot{E}\leq-W_{\mathrm{dissipation}}+W_{\mathrm{elastic}}+\sum_{i}\dot{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{d}_{i}

(7)

(
7
) shows that the energy of the controlled must continuously decrease if no external forces act on the robot (i.e.
\boldsymbol{d}_{i}=0
) and if we do not switch references
\boldsymbol{r}_{2,1}
,
\boldsymbol{r}_{2,2}
, and
\boldsymbol{r}_{S}
of the virtual mechanism.
The robot motion eventually settles in a resting position. For the gravity-compensated robot, this corresponds to a minimum of the elastic potential energy.
This is a classical result of passivity-based control
[
42
]
.

In our case, each time a reference is switched at a specific robot configuration, a bounded amount of energy is either injected into or removed from the system. This action continuously drives the controlled robot into motion.
Switching between the cutting and raising phases is governed by different triggers: the transition from raising the knife
to cutting is position-based, while the switch from cutting to raising is primarily triggered by the knife’s orientation. However, because the cutting board restricts the motion of the knife,
we can also treat the latter as a position-based trigger. When a switch occurs, the spring that was previously connected to
the reference
\boldsymbol{r}_{2,1}
is detached and reattached to
\boldsymbol{r}_{2,2}
, and vice versa.
Because of these reference switches, the virtual springs generate new forces, leading to non-zero velocities and thus new dissipation (
W_{\mathrm{dissipation}}>0
) in the virtual dampers.
The injected elastic energy is always bounded, due to the position-based switching mechanism.

The periodic cutting motion of the controlled robot results from the balance between the bounded energy injected through reference switching and the energy dissipated by virtual dampers, the internal dissipation of the robot, and the environment.
That is, neglecting internal dissipation and environment contributions, for any periodic motions of period
T
we have, for all
t
,

\int_{t}^{t+T}W_{\mathrm{elastic}}(\tau)d\tau=\int_{t}^{t+T}W_{\mathrm{dissipation}}(\tau)d\tau.

(8)

The overall system does not
accumulate energy indefinitely. The injected energy is either dissipated by the robot’s inherent damping and the virtual dampers, or
transferred into the environment (e.g., performing cutting work on the vegetable or contacting the board).
Hence, over multiple cycles, the system settles to a bounded
regime of energy.
Any surplus energy in the system regardless of its source ultimately appears as increased speed at switching or larger
cutting forces. Nonetheless, it is dissipated through robot’s
physical and virtual damping, or absorbed by subsequent contact with the board or vegetable, preventing unbounded
energy growth.

Further insights can be gathered by
looking at a toy model, given by a single mass and one degree of freedom. For a single controlled mass
we have

m\ddot{x}+c\dot{x}+k(x-r)=0,

(9)

where

m>0
is the mass,
c>0
is the damping coefficient, and
k>0
is the spring stiffness.
r
is the reference, switching between two values,
r\in\{r_{1},r_{2}\}
.

x
is the mass position.
The mechanical energy reads

E=\frac{1}{2}m\dot{x}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}k(x-r)^{2}

(10)

from which

\dot{E}=-c\dot{x}^{2}+W_{r}.

(11)

W_{r}
accounts for the flow of energy due to switching reference. For instance,
W_{r}
is zero unless a switch occurs. A switch at time
t
causes an instantaneous (and large!) flow of energy,
W_{r}\neq 0
. The amount of injected energy can be computed by looking at the left and right limits of the switching time
t
, denoted by
t^{-}
and
t^{+}
. For instance,

{E(t^{+})-E(t^{-})}=\frac{1}{2}k(x(t^{+})-r(t^{+}))^{2}-\frac{1}{2}k(x(t^{-})-r(t^{-})^{2}.

(12)

If
r\in\{r_{1},r_{2}\}
and switch times depend on
x
, this injection is always bounded. A periodic motion arises at the balance between injection of energy (switches) and dissipation (during motion).
Consider the case that a switch occurs at positions
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, as depicted in Fig.
7
.
This case mirrors the cutting/raising transitions in rocking motion.
Formally,

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=r_{1}\quad\text{if}\quad\bigl(\dot{x}\geq 0\,\wedge\,x\leq x_{1}\bigr)\;\text{or}\;\bigl(\dot{x}\leq 0\,\wedge\,x\leq x_{2}\bigr),

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=r_{2}\quad\text{if}\quad\bigl(\dot{x}\geq 0\,\wedge\,x\geq x_{1}\bigr)\;\text{or}\;\bigl(\dot{x}\leq 0\,\wedge\,x\geq x_{2}\bigr).

By plotting trajectories in the
(x,\dot{x})
phase plane (Fig.
8
), one observes that solutions converge to a closed curve.
Intuitively, if the mass moves too fast, the damper dissipates more energy, returning the system toward this closed curve.
Conversely, if motion is too slow, less energy is dissipated, and the system gains more momentum until it approaches the same closed curve.

A final analysis can be developed
experimentally.
Consider a generic cutting cycle
identified by the robot switching reference from ‘raising’ to ‘cutting’.
The total work done by the robot per cutting cycle can be approximated by the energy

E_{\mathcal{C}_{j}}\,=\,\sum_{t_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{j}\setminus\{t_{N}\}}\boldsymbol{F}\bigl(t_{i}\bigr)\bigl(\boldsymbol{x}(t_{i+1})-\boldsymbol{x}(t_{i})\bigr),

(13)

where
\boldsymbol{F}
is the force read from the force sensor placed at the end-effector while cutting.
\boldsymbol{x}
is its position.
The set

\mathcal{C}_{j}=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{N}\}
contains the time instants of cycle
j
at which sample measures are taken.
At each cycle,
E_{\mathcal{C}_{j}}
provides an estimate of the amount of work required to cut, considering all dissipations and interactions (internal, external, food, cutting board).

We start the experiment with the knife initially performing a pure rocking motion with no food. From the 7th to the 16th cycle, a carrot is introduced and cut by the knife, after which the carrot is removed.
As illustrated in Fig.
9
, the work done for cutting the carrot differs from the work done during baseline rocking motion. However, once the carrot is removed, the system returns to the energy level observed during pure rocking. This behavior shows how the virtual model controller
adjusts the robot motion to compensate for external perturbations, and consistently returns to prior energy balance when perturbations are removed.

We evaluated the versatility of the controller by cutting a variety of vegetables (spring onion, cucumber, carrot, courgette, and potato) under two conditions: (i) using a single set of control parameters for all food (uniform parameters), and (ii) with parameters tuned for each food type (food-tuned parameters), with the goal of minimizing the applied force. In fact, reducing the cutting force helps to achieve precise cuts and prolongs the lifespan of both the knife and the cutting board.
Larger items, such as cucumber, courgette, and potato, were cut in half to make them suitable for rocking and ensure stable placement on the cutting board. All vegetables were securely held to prevent motion during cutting. Results are summarized in Fig.
10
.

To quantitatively evaluate the cutting process, we have used the following metrics. Let
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{cut},i}=\{t_{1},\dots,t_{N_{i}}\}

denote the times at which a sample measure is taken during the cutting phase
i
. These belong to the time interval in which

\boldsymbol{p}_{b}
is connected to the reference
\boldsymbol{r}_{2,1}
, as shown in Fig.
3(c)
(left).
The average and peak forces during the
i
th cycle are computed from the
force-torque sensor reading, mounted at the robot wrist

F_{\text{avg},i}\,=\!\!\sum_{t\,\in\,\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{cut},i}}\!\!\frac{|\boldsymbol{F}(t)|}{N_{i}}\qquad\quad F_{\text{peak},i}\,=\max_{t\,\in\,\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{cut},i}}|\boldsymbol{F}(t)|.

(14)

Similarly, the average and maximum velocities of the robot wrist during cutting are estimated as follows.

v_{\text{avg},i}\,=\!\!\sum_{t\,\in\,\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{cut},i}}\!\!\frac{|\boldsymbol{v}(t)|}{N_{i}}\qquad\quad v_{\text{peak},i}\,=\max_{t\,\in\,\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{cut},i}}|\boldsymbol{v}(t)|,

(15)

where
\boldsymbol{v}
is the velocity of the wrist, computed using the robot kinematics.
Finally, cut frequency is defined as

f_{\text{cut}}\,=\,\frac{N_{\text{switch}}-1}{T},

(16)

where
N_{\text{switch}}
is the total number of transitions from the cut to the raise state that occur during the time interval
T
of a whole experiment. This frequency indicates how rapidly consecutive slices are produced.
Work for one slice is computed with (
13
).

When using a single set of control parameters for all foods, the force profiles in the
z
-axis (Fig.
10(a)
) were similar across all food types despite significant differences in stiffness.
This is because the control parameters were chosen to apply a sufficiently high force so that the intrinsic properties of the food had minimal impact on the resulting behavior.
Peak force is typically observed near the transition from cut to raise and varies with the energy absorption characteristics of each vegetable.
The level of ‘residual’ energy after cut typically results in a higher contact force before the knife is retracted.
With parameters tuned specifically for each food type, the force required for cutting spring onion and cucumber is noticeably reduced, while high force levels are needed for other vegetables.

shows the sliced vegetables at the end of experiments. By visual inspection, both (i) uniform and (ii) food-specific parameter selection
produce no noticeable difference in slice quality or accuracy.

VI-B

Cut accuracy

To evaluate the precision of our cutting mechanism, we cut courgette samples using various target thicknesses and cutting speeds. Two average cut frequencies were implemented for each thickness setting, approximately 0.51 and 0.94 cuts per second. Fig.
11
shows representative slices obtained under these conditions.

Cutting accuracy is assessed by measuring each cut in four distinct locations. Measurements are taken with care, avoiding deformation of the slice as much as possible.
We use three metrics to measure (i) if the desired thickness is achieved, (ii) if the accuracy is consistently achieved with continuous cutting, and (iii) if each slice is evenly cut.

For (i), we compare the average of all measurements
\Delta
with the desired one. We compute

\Delta=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Delta_{i}\qquad\quad\Delta_{i}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{4}\Delta_{i,j}

(17)

where
N
is the total number of slices,
\Delta_{i}
is the average thickness of slice
i
based on four thickness measurements

\Delta_{i,j}
.
For (ii),
we take the variance of the average thickness slices. Namely,

\sigma^{2}_{\Delta}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\Delta_{i}-\Delta\right)^{2}.

(18)

Higher variance indicates lower consistency. Finally,
for (iii), we take the variance of the four thickness measurements within each slice and compute its average across all slices:

\sigma^{2}_{\Delta}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma^{2}_{\Delta_{i}}\qquad\sigma^{2}_{\Delta_{i}}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{4}\left(\Delta_{i,j}-\Delta_{i}\right)^{2}.

(19)

In this case, a higher variance indicates lower evenness.

Fig.
11
shows that average thickness is close to the desired target. In particular, the slices are consistently slightly thinner than expected.
Several factors may contribute to this discrepancy: the knife’s movement matches the desired thickness without compensating for its own width, alongside the plastic deformation of the vegetable during cutting and moisture loss in the slices.
Despite these effects, achieving nearly one slice per second with such precision is comparable to or even exceeds human performance.

Faster cutting generally degrades performance in terms of average thickness, consistency, and evenness, as illustrated in Fig.
11
, except in the case of 1-mm slices.
The decreased performance at higher speeds is mainly due to the limited ability of the knife to accurately reach the target slicing position in a short time frame, resulting in fluctuations and positional adjustments during the cut.
This effect is minimized when cutting very thin slices. Additionally, 1-mm slices experience greater plastic deformation at slower cut speeds, potentially hindering the initiation and propagation of a clean cut.

VI-C

Cut robustness

The VMC approach demonstrates inherent robustness in handling environmental variations. As shown in Section
VI-A
, the same set of control parameters enabled successful cut of a variety of food items, from delicate spring onions to rigid potatoes.
In addition to these results, we tested the controller using three different knives, as shown in Fig.
12(a)
.
Knife 1 features an optimal blade curvature for rocking and has been used in all experiments so far.
Knife 2 has a different blade geometry, which is still suitable for rocking. In contrast, knife 3 is a carving knife and should not be used for rocking.
Results are shown in Fig.
12
. To assess robustness, we consider a uniform set of parameters for all knives.

Although all knives exhibited rocking motion under identical control parameters, the differences in blade geometry affected energy dissipation and resulted in different rocking features.
Knife 2 had a smaller blade than knife 1, resulting in a reduced energy dissipation through contact with the cutting board. This led to a higher residual energy after cutting, which turned into speed, that is, faster cuts.
Similar considerations apply to knife 3.
The progressive increase in force peaks for knives 2 and 3, respectively,
can be explained in a similar way: knives 2 and 3 are moving faster than knife 1, leading to stronger forces at contact with the cutting board.

We also demonstrate the ability of the controller to handle different heights of the cutting board.
Fig.
13(a)
illustrates the experimental setup, where the nominal cutting board height was modified by adding MDF layers underneath.
Fig.
13
shows that the rocking motion remained stable across all heights, without requiring any adjustments to the control parameters. The gradual decrease in peak force with increasing height can be explained by the knife having less distance to accelerate before switching.

Together, Figs.
12
and
13
provide a neat illustration of the difference between our virtual model controller and trajectory-based methods. The latter would require a precise knowledge of knife geometry and new path planning computations to take into account variations in cutting board heights. In contrast, because the rocking motion established by the virtual model controller is not programmed, it can easily comply with various settings.

VII
Conclusion and future works

We have proposed a new virtual model controller for rock cutting. The rocking motion has been discussed theoretically, through energy considerations, and experimentally.
Performance has been illustrated by cutting several vegetables, at several thicknesses and cut frequencies.
Robustness has been illustrated by using three different knives and several cutting board heights. Notably, the controller does not require any trajectory planning and relies on minimal information, both at design and deployment stage, and delivers consistent and reliable cutting performance. Experiment videos are available online:
https://youtu.be/67v-U4gp9_k
.

Although these results clarify the flexibility and reliability of our control strategy, there are still significant directions for improvement. At level of integration, the most pressing one is handling the full cutting pipeline. This entails autonomous pick and place of the food, its orientation on the cutting board, and the capability of holding food in place during cutting. It seems also crucial for the robot to recognize different foods and knives, and to measure in real-time the result of its action, during and after cutting.
More work is also needed at the level of the food-knife interaction. A key future direction is the systematic exploration of adaptation/learning of the control parameters, to improve performance. The idea is to exploit additional sensing, such as vision and tactile information, to optimize control parameters in real time.

Thanks to the intuitive design of our virtual mechanism, automatic control parameter adjustment can be performed at different levels of complexity.
Here, for completeness, we discuss an initial study in which the spring stiffness
k_{2}
is adjusted to achieve successful food separation while moderating the force exerted on food and board.
The overall strategy is extremely simple. We increase
k_{2}
if the knife does not achieve separation of food (by position sensing through robot and knife geometry). We reduce
k_{2}
if we detect excess overshoot on the knife handle (i.e. if the knife handle moves past the critical reference).
This leads to an error signal,
e
, capturing the mismatch between desired and achieved vertical position of the knife. We update
k_{2}
every six cutting cycles,
using the adaptation law

k_{2}^{(n+1)}=k_{2}^{(n)}+\alpha\,e,

where
\alpha
is the adaptation gain.

Figure
15
provides a brief illustration.
All control parameters are set as in the A-2 Fast cut in Table
I
, except for
k_{2}
, which is dynamically adapted.

k_{2}
starts from a low value, which is sufficient initially to cut through a carrot. From time
t=20
s, the carrot presents a harder consistency which requires adaptation to cut through as shown in Figure
15(b)
.

k_{2}
increases and, after
t=35
s, adaptation is completed and cutting is reestablished.
This brief illustration shows the potential of continuous learning combined with virtual model control. Here we have focused on a simple single parameter for simplicity, but we plan to develop a systematic approach in the future, leveraging vision and tactile sensing.
