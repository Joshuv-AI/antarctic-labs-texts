Posttraumatic iatrogenic meningoencephalocele following tympanoplasty: illustrative case

Iatrogenic meningoencephalocele extending into the temporal bone is a rare complication of middle ear surgery. Its development has been attributed to a combination of factors, including the size of the bony defect and intracranial pressure, although the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood.1–6 It is generally considered that the coexistence of both bony and dural defects is required for its occurrence.3,4 In most cases, bony and dural disruption results from surgical drilling of the temporal bone together with inflammatory extension from chronic otitis media, leading to simultaneous defects in bone and dura through which herniation develops.3,4,7–11

Traumatic meningoencephalocele has also been reported, although such cases most commonly occur in the ethmoid or frontal sinuses.12,13 Even when it arises within the temporal bone, previously published cases typically involved herniation into the middle ear due to fractures of the tegmen tympani accompanied by dural injury.14 These lesions are generally considered to result from skull base fractures that produce both bony and dural defects, allowing herniation into a preexisting anatomical space.

In the present case, we encountered a patient who developed a meningoencephalocele that herniated through a skull base fracture into the surgically created mastoid cavity following middle ear surgery with mastoidectomy. Reports of trauma-induced iatrogenic meningoencephalocele are exceedingly rare, and this case provides valuable insight into the underlying mechanisms of iatrogenic herniation. We therefore describe this case as well as review the relevant literature.

Illustrative Case

The patient was a teenage girl who was injured in a motorcycle traffic accident. She sustained a right acute subdural hematoma, traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage, contusions of the right frontal and temporal lobes, and fractures of the left temporal bone (petrous portion) and left parietal bone. She underwent an emergency craniotomy and hematoma evacuation for the right acute subdural hematoma at another neurosurgical center. Her general condition stabilized, and the postoperative course was uneventful. However, she had severe traumatic facial nerve palsy from the time of injury, as well as left-sided sensorineural hearing loss associated with the left temporal bone fracture (Fig. 1A–C). She was therefore referred to the otorhinolaryngology department at our institution for further evaluation and management. She subsequently underwent facial nerve decompression and tympanoplasty on day 53 after the injury. During this procedure, a left mastoidectomy was performed; however, the mastoid tegmen was preserved, and the middle cranial fossa dura was not exposed (Fig. 1D). She was discharged home on postoperative day 5, and her facial nerve palsy showed near-complete recovery during outpatient follow-up.

A–C:Axial CT image (A) and 3D reconstruction images (B and C) demonstrating a left temporal bone fracture involving the petrous portion after traffic trauma (arrowheads). The fracture extended to the mastoid tegmen (dotted circle). D:During the initial surgery, the mastoid tegmen bone was preserved (asterisk), and no dural exposure was identified.

However, at 12 months after surgery, follow-up imaging revealed a left meningoencephalocele, and she was referred to our neurosurgical department. Because no interval imaging studies had been performed until 12 months after surgery, it was difficult to determine when the meningoencephalocele developed postoperatively. At the initial neurosurgical evaluation, she exhibited no signs of infection or CSF leakage and no neurological deficits. CT and MRI demonstrated a bony defect in the mastoid tegmen, through which the inferior surface of the temporal lobe had herniated into the cavity created by the previous mastoidectomy (Fig. 2). Although the meningoencephalocele was asymptomatic, surgical repair was judged to be indicated in light of the potential future risks of meningitis and CSF leakage. Because there was no urgency, surgery was planned electively, and resection of the meningoencephalocele with reconstruction of the middle cranial fossa floor was performed 16 months after the mastoidectomy, in consideration of her academic schedule.

Right:T2-weighted MR image demonstrating herniation of the inferior surface of the temporal lobe through this defect into the cavity created by the previous mastoidectomy (arrow).

Surgery

Resection of the meningoencephalocele and dural reconstruction were performed via a middle cranial fossa approach, based on preoperative planning with 3D simulation (Fig. 3A), which was selected to allow a more reliable and watertight dural repair. The 3D model used for preoperative simulation was generated using GRID (Kompath) based on preoperative thin-slice heavily T2-weighted MRI (0.6-mm slice) and thin-slice CT (0.3-mm slice).

Intraoperative findings of middle cranial fossa reconstruction. A: Preoperative surgical simulation using a 3D model confirmed that the lesion could be accessed with minimal retraction of the temporal lobe via the middle cranial fossa approach. B:A sail-shaped skin incision was designed in the temporal region. C:After the craniotomy, the dura beneath the fracture line was found to be absent, exposing the cortical vein, which was wedged into the fracture gap (arrowheads). D: Along the fracture line, the inferior surface of the temporal lobe also lacked dural coverage and had herniated in a mushroom-like manner into the mastoidectomy cavity (arrowhead). E: Resection of the meningoencephalocele revealed a bony defect of the mastoid tegmen (arrow). F: The pedicled temporalis fascia graft was then placed to cover the middle cranial fossa floor defect (asterisk), and additional dural repair was performed using DuraGen.

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia. A sail-shaped skin incision was made (Fig. 3B), and after elevating the skin flap caudally by dissecting along the loose areolar tissue plane, a pedicled temporalis fascia graft was harvested. On reflecting the temporalis muscle, a fracture line from the initial trauma was identified in the temporal bone. A temporal craniotomy was then performed according to the preoperative simulation, which revealed a dural defect along the fracture line, with the cortical vein entrapped within the fracture. The cortical vein was carefully dissected from the surrounding bone and preserved (Fig. 3C). Next, dissection of the middle cranial fossa dura was performed. This revealed a dural defect corresponding to the fracture line of the mastoid tegmen, through which the temporal lobe had mushroomed into the mastoidectomy cavity (Fig. 3D). The herniated portion of the temporal lobe was coagulated and resected, and the meningoencephalocele filling the mastoidectomy cavity through the bony defect of the tegmen was aspirated and removed (Fig. 3E). The pedicled temporalis fascia was then sutured and secured deep to the dural defect of the middle cranial fossa, covering the underlying bony gap. DuraGen (Integra LifeSciences) was interposed between the pedicled fascia and the dura of the middle cranial fossa to achieve dural reconstruction (Fig. 3F). The dura surrounding the defect was markedly attenuated, making primary closure impossible. Because of concerns regarding infection, rigid reconstruction of the mastoid tegmen using artificial materials was avoided. The bone flap was replaced and fixed with titanium plates, and the wound was closed in layers.

Postoperative Course

Her postoperative course was uneventful, and she was discharged home on postoperative day 12 without complications. Follow-up MRI performed 1 year after surgery demonstrated no recurrence of the meningoencephalocele (Fig. 4), confirming the stability of the middle cranial fossa reconstruction.

Discussion

Observations

In this report, we described a case in which delayed bone and dural defects developed along the fracture line of the mastoid tegmen following trauma, ultimately resulting in a meningoencephalocele that herniated into the mastoidectomy cavity. Although iatrogenic meningoencephalocele after middle ear surgery has been reported, most cases are attributed to bony and dural defects secondary to inflammation extending from conditions such as chronic otitis media.3,4,7–10 Reports in which a skull base fracture serves as the underlying cause, as in the present case, are exceedingly rare. This case highlights that skull base fractures can be an important predisposing factor for iatrogenic meningoencephalocele and provides useful insights into both the pathophysiology of iatrogenic herniation and the perioperative considerations necessary when performing middle ear surgery in patients with temporal bone trauma.

In the present case, no obvious bony defect of the mastoid tegmen was identified at the time of tympanoplasty performed approximately 7 weeks after the injury, and the dura was not exposed intraoperatively. Therefore, the bony and dural defects were considered to have developed subsequently. In contrast, at the time of surgery for the meningoencephalocele, marked thinning and a broad defect of the dura were observed along the temporal bone fracture line. Based on these findings, it is presumed that a dural injury occurred at the time of trauma; however, because the arachnoid membrane remained intact, no symptoms indicative of dural violation, such as CSF leakage, were apparent in the acute phase.15 Following mastoidectomy, a cavity was created directly beneath the fracture site, eliminating structural support for the fractured area. The persistent gravitational load of the temporal lobe on the bone and dura, which had already been weakened by the fracture, likely led to progressive enlargement of the bony and dural defects, ultimately resulting in the development of a meningoencephalocele. It should be noted that intracranial pressure was not measured during the clinical course; therefore, it could not be determined whether elevated intracranial pressure had been present as a contributing factor in the development of the meningoencephalocele.

Iatrogenic meningoencephalocele following surgery for chronic otitis media is generally attributed to weakening of the bone and dura due to inflammatory extension,3,4,7–10 making its occurrence difficult to predict. In contrast, when a fracture is the underlying cause, as in the present case, the presence of a tegmen fracture can be identified preoperatively before performing mastoidectomy. This allows for an assessment of the potential risk of postoperative meningoencephalocele. In patients such as the one in the present case who exhibit a fracture of the mastoid tegmen, mastoidectomy should be performed with the recognition that an associated dural injury may already be present, thereby posing a risk of postoperative meningoencephalocele. In selected cases, it may be prudent to perform a simultaneous temporal craniotomy to directly assess for dural violation and to consider prophylactic reconstruction of the middle cranial fossa if necessary. Even when dural integrity is not evaluated intraoperatively, careful postoperative imaging follow-up is warranted in view of the potential risk of delayed meningoencephalocele. At the same time, attention should also be paid to the possible development of known delayed complications of temporal bone fractures, including meningitis, CSF leakage, and cholesteatoma.16

In the present case, the meningoencephalocele was treated via a middle cranial fossa approach. The use of 3D simulation during preoperative planning enabled us to determine the precise extent of craniotomy required to expose the fistulous site. Recent advances in simulation software allow not only accurate visualization of bony structures but also representation of soft tissue deformation, such as temporal lobe elevation, as demonstrated in this report.17–19 This facilitates preoperative simulation that closely approximates actual surgical maneuvers. In surgeries involving skull base manipulation, as in the present case, brain retraction is often unavoidable; however, excessive retraction carries the risk of brain retraction injury.20 The ability to preoperatively assess the degree of brain retraction required to reach the target lesion is therefore of substantial clinical value.18,19 Moreover, the use of simulation software enables surgeons to determine the minimal craniotomy necessary to access the lesion while keeping brain retraction to an absolute minimum. These advantages underscore the importance of incorporating preoperative simulation—particularly in operations for skull base pathologies in which retraction-related risks are inherent—into surgical planning.

Various reconstructive methods have been described, including the use of artificial materials, autologous bone grafts, and soft tissue for repair.4,9 In the present case, rigid reconstruction was not performed in view of the risk of infection and the limited extent of the bony defect. However, when the bony defect is extensive, rigid reconstruction should be considered. Given the potential risk of infection, reconstruction with autologous tissue may be preferable whenever feasible, and calvarial bone grafting may therefore represent a reasonable reconstructive option.4,9

Lessons

We reported a case of iatrogenic meningoencephalocele in which a skull base fracture was considered the underlying cause. In patients with tegmen fractures, as in the present case, an associated dural injury may be present; therefore, the risk of iatrogenic meningoencephalocele following mastoidectomy should be carefully recognized. Furthermore, when treating meningoencephalocele via a middle cranial fossa approach, the use of 3D simulation can facilitate accurate determination of the optimal craniotomy extent.
