Learning–Assessment Firewall approach: protecting learning safety and assessment integrity
Authors: Manar Al-lawama
Published: 2025

1. Background

One afternoon, following rounds, faculty received a routine WhatsApp message requesting that evaluations be submitted by the end of the day. What made me pause, however, was a response from a new part-time instructor: “Can someone guide me on this process? I was doing more teaching than assessing. Was I wrong?!” That simple, honest question surfaced a dilemma I had long internalized but never explicitly confronted. Despite my training in assessment and education, I realized how much I had normalized a system that blurred the boundaries between teaching and evaluation.

During clinical teaching, I foster a psychologically safe environment, encouraging students to ask questions, express uncertainties, and think aloud. I value their reasoning over correct answers, prioritizing process over product as the core of learning. Yet, at the end of the week, I must assign a numerical score counted into their academic record, shifting my role from facilitator to assessor.

Shifting between teacher and assessor is deeply disorienting. It applies ethical tension of fostering a safe learning environment while simultaneously evaluating students for grades. Can mistakes remain opportunities for growth, or will they count against them? How can students engage freely knowing they will be assigned marks?

That WhatsApp message shed light on multiple interconnected issues. Firstly, the expectations placed on part-time instructors, often hired to fill gaps in subspecialties and with minimal exposure to the students, are unrealistic [1]. They are typically brought in to teach, not to assess, yet are asked to make significant evaluative judgments with limited observation and support.

Secondly, the roles of facilitator and assessor are inherently in conflict. From a pedagogical standpoint, this also sends mixed signals. On one hand, we encourage openness and intellectual risk-taking during clinical learning [2]. On the other, we quietly observe and later assign grades that can affect academic progression, sometimes without students realizing they were being assessed at all. This undermines the trust required for effective learning.

Such blurred boundaries are not just problematic; they are pedagogically unsound. Summative assessment, by nature, is judgmental and high stakes. It requires consistency, objectivity, and sufficient sampling of a student’s performance. When conducted without clear criteria, adequate observation, or trained assessors, the results are vulnerable to bias and variability. More importantly, they compromise the educational contract between learner and teacher.

Looking back, I now see how my efforts to protect a safe, formative learning space may have been undercut by the very structure I work within. The role conflict becomes even more pronounced for part-time educators who are rarely briefed on institutional assessment standards and are left to rely on instinct and limited interaction. This places students at risk of being unfairly evaluated and instructors in an ethically uncomfortable position.

This is not an individual failing but a systemic design flaw. Educators need clear guidance and training to protect learning environments while maintaining the integrity of summative assessments. Grading students while simultaneously teaching them creates a hidden, high-stakes judgment that fuels ethical tension for educators.

The purpose of this paper is to propose the Learning–Assessment Firewall Framework as a conceptual solution to resolve the persistent role conflict between teaching and assessment in clinical education.

2. Proposed solution: Learning–Assessment Firewall Framework

I propose applying a framework to separate learning from assessment; Learning–Assessment Firewall Framework. This framework should be applied in medical schools as part of their standards that should also be recognized within accreditation processes. This framework emphasizes a deliberate separation between teaching and assessment roles, as well as the contexts in which they occur, thereby safeguarding both the integrity of learning and the fairness of summative evaluation.

1) Conceptualizing the Learning–Assessment Firewall Framework

In computer science, a firewall regulates information flow between two different zones: a trusted internal network and a public external network, deciding which information is blocked, which is filtered, and which is allowed through. Reflecting on our case, the firewall represents the separation between the (trusted network) which is the student learning zone, where learners are encouraged to explore, make mistakes, and grow without fear of immediate judgment. And the (public network) which is the summative assessment zone, where information becomes part of the official academic record and contributes to progression and certification.

The firewall is the deliberate system that filters and regulates what flows from the safe learning environment into the high-stakes assessment environment. i. Educator’s role

This framework emphasizes the separation of the educator’s teaching and assessment functions. Specifically, it distinguishes the role of the teacher, operating within the learning zone, from that of the assessor, operating within the summative assessment zone for the same cohort. In practice, this often involves teaching one group of students while assessing a different group. Such a cross-group approach safeguards the integrity of both processes: students can take intellectual risks in a psychologically safe learning environment, while assessments remain fair, objective, and free from bias.

Role separation under Learning–Assessment Firewall Framework does not require additional staff; it can be achieved through careful timetable design and role assignment. Students are divided into subgroups, each assigned to an educator for teaching. Within their own subgroup, educators provide assessment for learning—feedback on logbooks, case write-ups, and coursework—with no academic consequences. For summative purposes, the same educator serves as assessor for a different subgroup. In practice, this means that teaching groups are paired and rotated: while Educator A teaches Group 1 and assesses Group 2, Educator B teaches Group 2 and assesses Group 1. This switching ensures that formative teaching and summative assessment roles remain separate, reducing bias and ethical tension.

ii. Students’ performance

The framework regulates how different aspects of student activity are managed across the “Learning– Assessment Firewall,” determining what remains protected in the learning environment, what is subject to independent validation, and what is explicitly used as summative evidence for the assessment record. Student activities within the learning environment are handled in three distinct ways:

Blocked from crossing the firewall: Struggles with medical knowledge, clinical reasoning, or procedural skills remain within the trusted learning zone. Similar to that are formative assessment results. These represent opportunities for feedback and growth, not for summative consequences.

Filtered before crossing the firewall: Professionalism concerns raised by educators are reviewed through independent committee for validation and only confirmed issues are incorporated into summative decisions. However, the evaluation of students’ commitment, communication skills, and professionalism remains an integral part of the learning process with a predefined rubric that is used for feedback and mentorship purposes only.

Professionalism concerns should be filtered and validated through a structured and clearly defined process before they can influence summative outcomes. Programs should start by clearly communicating professional expectations to students and educators, including an explicit definition of what constitutes a professional breach or concern (e.g., confidentiality violation, disrespectful behavior). When such concerns arise, educators must document them using a unified reporting template and submit the report to an independent committee. The committee should review the documentation, invite the student for a hearing to ensure procedural fairness, and examine whether the concern reflects a recurring pattern rather than an isolated event. Based on this review, the committee will forward its recommendations to students’ affairs or the Dean, in line with the school’s governance structure, for any final action (e.g., remediation, monitoring, and so forth). Establishing this due-process pathway ensures fairness, transparency, and legal defensibility [3,4].

Allowed to cross the firewall: Designated summative outputs, such as portfolios, case write-ups, and procedural logbooks, are explicitly intended to cross the firewall, providing direct and objective evidence of milestone achievement not a graded course work.

3. Discussion

Learning–Assessment Firewall Framework represents a guide for institutions to build learning activities, feedback and formative assessment in a standardized, transparent, and fair way, while preserving the integrity of summative assessment free from bias. It reduces the role conflict and ethical tension experienced by educators, who no longer need to simultaneously foster a safe learning environment and assign high-stakes grades to the same learners [5]. It also helps learners experience greater psychological safety, which is essential for deep learning, as the fear of judgment by their teacher is alleviated [6]. This separation enhances assessment fairness and objectivity by reducing personal bias as educators are assessing the performance of their own students whose skills and knowledge might be partly contributed to the knowledge and skills of their educator. Furthermore, the firewall concept fits perfectly within competency-based learning as achieving competence is the goal and not how the student fulfilled that milestone within the acceptable predefined timeframe and the appropriateness for the student level of learning.

The Learning–Assessment Firewall complements, rather than contradicts, established frameworks such as Programmatic Assessment and Workplace-Based Assessment [7,8]. While these models emphasize continuous observation and the broad use of diverse assessment tools to improve validity, Learning–Assessment Firewall Framework sharpens the boundary between learning and assessment by ensuring that activities within the learning environment are treated strictly as assessment for learning—focused on feedback and milestone achievements without academic consequences such as grades, progression, or dismissal. As noted in programmatic assessment, multiple assessment points are essential across the learning journey [9]; however, Learning–Assessment Firewall Framework specifies that those occurring within learning sessions with educators serve solely to provide feedback and to judge the achievement of competencies, without contributing scores to summative evaluations. This responds directly to concerns about clerkship grading, which has been criticized for being stressful, inequitable, and unhelpful for learning, particularly for underrepresented students [10]. By clearly separating formative feedback from summative judgment, Learning–Assessment Firewall Framework fosters psychological safety, alleviates the ethical tension of educators acting simultaneously as mentors and assessors, and reinforces the role of assessment as a learning tool that supports safety and growth in medical education [11].

A simple question in a messaging group revealed how fragile the boundary between teaching and assessment can be. As educators, we must reflect deeply on our roles and advocate for systems that respect the integrity of both learning and assessment. By decoupling summative judgment from the learning process and implementing this practical firewall, clinical learning environments can maintain their integrity and support both fair assessment and effective learning. Furthermore, providing all faculty with clear expectations, training, and support to fulfill these distinct roles will uphold trust and professionalism.
