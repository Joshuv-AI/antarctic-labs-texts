Ligamentum teres tear with hyaline body formation: A rare cause of mechanical hip pain
Authors: Mahmoud Kortibi, Qusai Razzouk, Mohammad Issa, Mostafa Sagheer, Hamza Khaiata, Firas Berro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTraumatic injuries of the ligamentum teres (LT) are an underrecognized cause of hip pain and instability. Diagnosis is often delayed due to nonspecific symptoms and normal radiographs. Complete LT rupture associated with intra-articular hyaline body formation is rare and may result in chronic mechanical symptoms.Case presentationA 38-year-old female presented with progressive right hip pain and limping over two years following direct blunt trauma. Early recognition and appropriate surgical management can lead to excellent functional outcomes.

Introduction

The ligamentum capitis femoris, commonly known as the ligamentum teres (LT), is an intra-articular but extra-synovial structure extending from the acetabular cotyloid fossa and transverse acetabular ligament to the fovea capitis on the femoral head [1], [2], [3]. Although historically considered a vestigial or functionally unnecessary structure, increasing evidence highlights its role in hip biomechanics, stability, and nociception [4]. The LT plays a role during flexion with external rotation and during extension with internal rotation—helping to prevent femoral head subluxation at these terminal ranges [1]. It is vascularized primarily by the obturator artery, serving as a key contributor to femoral head perfusion during childhood and adolescence [1]. Diagnosis of LT pathology remains challenging due to nonspecific symptoms and limited reliability of imaging modalities. Magnetic resonance arthrography and computed tomography arthrography have shown greater accuracy in detecting LT abnormalities [1]. Clinically, patients often present with groin pain, hip instability, restricted motion, and pain provoked by the LT test—performed with the hip flexed to approximately 70°, abducted 30°, and rotated internally and externally [4]. Proposed functions include stabilization in upright posture [4], fine motor coordination [4], proprioception, nociception, and distribution of synovial fluid across the articular surfaces [3], [4]. LT injuries commonly arise from twisting mechanisms [3] and may coexist with labral tears or follow hip dislocation [3]. Arthroscopic evaluation has revealed that LT rupture is more prevalent than previously believed and often responds well to debridement or other arthroscopic interventions [2], [3]. Current classifications describe LT pathology as complete tears (type I), partial tears (type II), or degenerative tears (type III) [4]. Given the emerging understanding of LT function and its contribution to hip pain and instability, accurate diagnosis and appropriate management are essential to restoring joint function and reducing disability. Here we present the case of a 38-year-old female with progressive right-hip mechanical pain and an antalgic gait. She was diagnosed with ligamentum teres tear. Surgical intervention was performed, and the femoral head was stabilized. Postoperatively, the patient reported complete relief of her pain.

Case presentation

A 38-year-old female with no significant medical or surgical history presented with a two-year history of progressive right hip pain accompanied by a noticeable limp. The patient sustained a direct blunt trauma to the right hip after being kicked during a physical altercation. She experienced immediate pain but remained ambulatory. Over the following months, the symptoms progressively worsened, with increasing limp and mechanical symptoms including clicking and instability despite appropriate conservative management. Clinical examination revealed an antalgic gait and a positive Trendelenburg sign on the right. Hip mobility was markedly restricted, with more than a 60% reduction in internal and external rotation compared with the contralateral side. The pain pattern was mechanical, exacerbated by physical activity and alleviated by rest. Initial anteroposterior pelvic radiographs (Fig. 1) obtained at the onset of symptoms were unremarkable, demonstrating no evidence of fracture, dysplasia, or degenerative change. Subsequent magnetic resonance imaging of the right hip reported the presence of a joint effusion. A detailed review of the imaging by the treating orthopedic surgeon further identified a ligamentum teres tear (Fig. 2). Due to the mechanical nature of the symptoms, their progressive course, and the failure of nonoperative management, surgical intervention was indicated. A hip arthrotomy was performed using the modified Gibson approach (Fig. 3). Intraoperatively, a complete tear and absence of the ligamentum teres was identified, which facilitated anterior dislocation of the femoral head. Additionally, the joint effusion was found to consist of an accumulation of hyaline bodies located in the inferomedial aspect of the acetabular cavity. Hyaline bodies were predominantly localized to the inferior aspect of the acetabular fossa. Intraoperative assessment of the articular cartilage revealed mild chondral changes, including superficial fibrillation and erythema, confined to the inferior region of the femoral head and corresponding acetabular surface. These findings were consistent with localized mechanical irritation likely resulting from chronic instability and contact with intra-articular loose bodies. The limited extent of cartilage involvement may be attributed to the small size and smooth morphology of the hyaline bodies, which likely minimized widespread abrasive damage despite prolonged symptoms. The surgical procedure included excision of the hyaline bodies from the acetabular fossa (Fig. 4), followed by reduction of the dislocation and anatomic stabilization of the femoral head. The joint capsule was then securely closed (Fig. 5), and the wound was closed in layers with the application of sterile dressing. The postoperative course was uneventful. At the 8-month follow-up, the patient reported complete resolution of preoperative pain and limping. Clinical assessment demonstrated full, pain-free range of motion and a negative Trendelenburg sign. Discussion

This case illustrates the rare coexistence of a complete ligamentum teres (LT) tear with intra-articular hyaline bodies, resulting in chronic mechanical hip pain and progressive functional limitation. Although historically under recognized, LT pathology is increasingly implicated in hip stability, proprioceptive feedback, and nociception [4], [5]. When torn, the LT can act as a primary pain generator and source of persistent mechanical symptoms, aligning closely with the clinical course observed in this patient. The case is particularly relevant in a developing-world context, where hip arthroscopy—the current gold standard for evaluating intra-articular soft-tissue pathology—is not readily available. In such environments, open surgical approaches remain crucial for accurate diagnosis and effective treatment. The modified Gibson approach was selected due to its well-established safety profile, preservation of the medial femoral circumflex artery, and provision of extensive access to the hip joint, enabling direct visualization of the LT and complete excision of intra-articular loose bodies [8]. The concomitant presence of intra-articular hyaline bodies adds a notable dimension to this case. These structures are thought to arise from synovial metaplasia secondary to repetitive microtrauma or represent cartilage fragments detached due to chronic instability associated with LT tears [7]. Their removal, combined with restoration of femoral head stability, facilitated the patient's rapid and complete recovery. The limited and localized chondral changes observed intraoperatively suggest that small, smooth hyaline bodies may produce focal cartilage irritation rather than widespread degenerative damage, despite chronic mechanical instability. These excellent postoperative outcomes corroborate existing evidence that timely surgical intervention for symptomatic LT tears can yield optimal results [4], [7]. The combination of neglected LT tear and chronic hyaline body formation created a self-perpetuating cycle of instability and irritation, successfully interrupted through open debridement and stabilization. This report reinforces several important clinical principles, LT pathology should remain a diagnostic consideration in patients with chronic mechanical hip symptoms and unremarkable initial radiographs. A high index of suspicion is warranted, particularly when symptoms are mechanical and disproportionate to initial imaging findings. Hyaline intra-articular bodies, although rare, may indicate chronic joint instability and should prompt evaluation of underlying soft-tissue lesions [7]. In developing-world settings without routine arthroscopic access, the modified Gibson approach is a safe, effective, and biologically sound option for diagnosis and treatment [8]. Recent evidence from specialized centers indicates that the rate of avascular necrosis (AVN) following surgical hip dislocation for elective, non-traumatic indications are extremely low, with some series reporting rates approaching 0%, confirming the vascular safety of this approach [8]. Taken together, this case expands the understanding of LT-related pathology, highlights the continued relevance of open hip surgery in resource-limited environments, and emphasizes meticulous imaging review and individualized surgical planning when managing complex intra-articular hip disorders.

Conclusion

Complete ligamentum teres (LT) tears with intra-articular hyaline bodies emerge as an underdiagnosed driver of chronic hip instability, mechanical pain, and functional decline—yet they respond robustly to open surgical intervention when arthroscopy is unavailable. This case highlights their rare but potent coexistence, where a modified Gibson approach delivered safe vascular preservation, full joint access, LT debridement, and loose body removal, yielding complete recovery without complications. By interrupting the cycle of instability-driven synovial metaplasia and irritation, this intervention aligns with evidence that timely LT surgery optimizes outcomes. Clinicians must prioritize LT pathology in persistent mechanical symptoms despite normal radiographs, view hyaline bodies as red flags for soft-tissue instability, and embrace tailored open techniques like Gibson's modification in resource-limited settings. This report bridges high-resource evidence to global practice, urging heightened vigilance and holistic strategies to redefine management of complex hip disorders.
