Effect of smoking on vitamin D status in the adults of Henan, China: the role of HDL-C and LDL-C
Authors: Baowei Wang, Guojie Wang, Li Gao, Guojun Yang, Shixian Feng, Jiying Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmoking has been reported to be associated with vitamin D (VD) status, but the evidence is limited. A cross-sectional study was conducted aiming to examine whether there is any effect of smoking on VD status in the adults based on the data sourced from the chronic diseases and nutrition surveillance in Henan. Multiple linear and multinomial logistic regression models based on the sampling design and weight were created to estimate the association between smoking and serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D concentration (S_25(OH)D_C) and VD status, respectively. It was found that in men, smokers had significantly lower S_25(OH)D_C and higher proportions of VD deficiency (VDD) and VD insufficiency (VDI) than non-smokers (S_25(OH)D_C: 18.2 ng/ml vs 19.8 ng/ml; proportion of VDD: 17.3% vs 14.3%; proportion of VDI: 44.7% vs 38.3%). Multivariate regression analyses demonstrated that in men smoking was independently associated with S_25(OH)D_C and VD status, reducing S_25(OH)D_C by 1.90 ng/ml, and increasing the risk of VDD and VDI by 134% and 65%, respectively. Further analysis stratified by the levels of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) revealed that in men with low HDL-C and high LDL-C, smoking increased 8.24 and 4.08 times likely to having VDD and VDI, respectively. In conclusion, smoking reduced S_25(OH)D_C, and increased the risk of VDD and VDI in men, especially in men with low level of HDL-C and high level of LDL-C in Henan.Keywords: Smoking, Vitamin D, Association, Risk factor, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, HDL-C, LDL-CSubject terms: Diseases, Endocrinology, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Vitamin D (VD) is well-known for its significant role in maintaining bone health. Over the past decades, studies have demonstrated that beyond skeletal benefits, VD has extraskeletal functions such as immunomodulation, enhancing glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity, stabilizing the endothelium, providing anti-inflammatory effects, and influencing the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, and VD deficiency (VDD)/VD insufficiency (VDI) increases the risk of some infectious diseases like tuberculosis and pneumonia as well as some noncommunicable diseases, including hypertension, diabetes, obesity, breast cancer, cardiovascular diseases and others1–5. The human body has two sources of VD, exogenous intake from diet and endogenous synthesis. The endogenous synthesis is the primary source, occurring in the skin through the transformation of 7-dehydrocholesterol triggered by ultraviolet B (UVB) from sunlight and approximately contributing to 90% of the body’s VD requirements. The geographical and environmental factors such as latitude, altitude, and atmospheric pollution significantly affect solar UVB ray exposure and, consequently, VD synthesis in the human body5. The residents in the regions with high latitude, low altitude and high atmospheric pollution are at an increased risk of VDD/VDI due to relatively low UVB exposure, resulting from the reduced amount of UVB of sunlight reaching the earth surface in these regions. Besides the geographical and environmental factors, some lifestyles associated with the solar UVB ray exposure of the skin like dressing, outdoor and physical activities, as the proximate influencing factors, play a critical role in VD synthesis in the skin6. VDD/VDI has been reported a common health condition in many countries, and has been regarded as a major public health issue globally despite the fact that the low-latitude regions have an overall lower VDD/VDI prevalence than the high-latitude regions7,8. In a region with relatively small span of latitude, the lifestyles as modifiable risk factors at individual level become more important. Therefore, it is valuable to identify what lifestyles are the determinants of VD status for reducing the prevalence of VDD/VDI.

Tobacco smoking is an important factor of lifestyle influencing on health, and has extensively been confirmed as the risk factor of a number of diseases such as lung cancer, chronic heart disease, stroke and so on. Globally, one in five adults use tobacco, and tobacco use leads to millions of preventable deaths every year9. It is of great significance to explore whether there is a significant association between tobacco smoking and VD status, the two globally important public health issues.

The association between tobacco smoking exposure and VD has been reported in general population, sub-population or special population, but it is still controversial whether it reduces the level of serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D), and whether it is the risk factor of VDD/VDI. A meta-analysis published in 2021 including 24 studies with 11,340 participants showed that the level of 25(OH)D in smokers was lower than that in nonsmokers with a small combined effect amount (SMD: − 0.24; 95% CI − 0.31 to − 0.17), and smokers were more likely to have VDD/VDI than nonsmokers, but the combined effect amount of RR was very small (RR: 1.11; 95%CI 1.03–1.19)10. One recent report based on a large representative sampling population from the national health and nutrition examination surveys (NHANES) in USA from 2001 to 2014 indicated that tobacco smoke exposure was associated with VD status, which was a protective factor in the population aged 12–19 years and a risk factor in the population aged 20 years and above11, but the study based on the USA NHANES from 2009 to 2010 indicated that tobacco smoke exposure was a risk factor for VDD/VDI in US children and adolescents12. One recent report found that smoking at high altitude was associated with a significant increase in the serum 25(OH)D concentration, while at low altitude it was associated with a significant decrease13. Evidence from the Japan suggested that passive smoking exposure was a risk factor for VDD/VDI in children, but no dose–response relationship between the number of cigarettes smoked indoors and VDD/VDI14. The study based on the Korea NHANES from 2010 to 2014, representative population data, revealed that smoking was not independent risk factor in Korean adults15. We found two relevant studies with inconsistent results reported from China16,17. One demonstrated that smoking was associated with decreased serum vitamin D concentrations in older Chinese men16, whereas the other yielded contradictory findings in Chinese adults17. In addition, one study conducted among rural women of reproductive age in rural central China demonstrated that passive smoking might strengthen the effect of inadequate nutrition on the risk of VDD18.

Overall, the evidence on the association between tobacco smoke exposure and VD status are rather limited and inconsistent, so the effect of smoking on the VD status is not clear, and more studies are needed to verify the association11,13. The objective of this study was to explore whether there is any effect of smoking on serum 25(OH)D concentration and VD status in the adult residents after adjustment for potential confounding factors based on the population-representative data in Henan, the central part of China, for contributing the evidence to support public health initiatives addressing smoking and VDD/VDI.

Methods

Study setting and data source

This present study was a cross-sectional study and set in Henan, one province in China with the latitude 31°23′–36°22′N and longitude 110°21′–116°39′N, and was based on the data sourced from the chronic diseases and nutrition surveillance among the adult residents of Henan in 2018/2019, part of chronic disease and nutrition surveillance among the adults in China (2018), which was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the National Center for Chronic and Noncommunicable Disease Control and Prevention, Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (code: 201819 and date of approval: April 27, 2018). Informed consent was obtained from all the participants during the data collection, and all methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

The surveillance applied the multi-stage stratified cluster sampling method to select participants for a representative sample of the whole non-institutionalized adult residents in Henan. The entire province was stratified into rural area and urban area. The seven counties in the rural area and seven districts in the urban area in Henan which participated in the system of China chronic disease and risk factors surveillance were selected as the survey administration areas. These 14 counties or districts had been identified as representative of the whole province based on a comprehensive evaluation using the indicators including the proportion of the population aged 65 years or older, mortality rate, proportion of non-agricultural population, illiteracy rate and so on19. At the first stage, three subdistricts or towns were randomly selected from each selected district or county, and at the second stage, two neighborhoods or villages were randomly selected from each selected subdistrict or town. The first and second stages both used the population-size ordered systematic sampling. At the third stage, one residential group was randomly selected from each selected neighborhood or village, and at the fourth stage, 45 households which were randomly selected from each selected residential group. The third and fourth stages both used simple random sampling. All residents aged 18 years or older from the selected households were regarded as participants. Finally, a total of 8455 adult residents aged 18 years or older from 84 villages or neighborhoods in 42 subdistricts or townships were recruited as participants.

For all participants, the questionnaire-based interview was conducted to collect the demographic characteristics, life styles, etc., and anthropometry including weight, height, waist circumference (WC) and blood pressure (BP) were measured using the standard scales and procedures. Meanwhile, blood samples for all participants collected from antecubital vein were used for measuring the concentrations of serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D2 (25(OH)D2), 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 (25(OH)D3), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglycerides (TG), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), 2-h plasm glucose (2-h PG) during oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), etc.

Height was measured using a metal stadiometer with the accuracy of 0.1 cm. Weight was measured by G&G TC-200 K electronic scale with the accuracy of 0.1 kg. WC was measured with measuring tape, accurate to 0.1 cm. BP was measured using Omron HBP1300 electronic sphygmomanometer, accurate to 1 mmHg. All measuring instruments complied with national metrological certification requirements, and all measurement procedures were performed in accordance with the industry standard of the People’s Republic of China: Methods for Anthropometric Measurement in Human Health Surveillance (WS/T 424-2013).

For the detection of blood lipids and 25(OH)D, fasting antecubital venous blood was collected using vacuum blood collection tubes with separating gel and no anticoagulant. The samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to separate serum. The obtained serum was aliquoted, frozen and transported to the designated laboratory, and all tests completed within one month. TC was measured by the cholesterol oxidase-p-aminophenazone phenol method. TG were determined using the glycerol phosphate oxidase-4-chlorophenol method. HDL-C and LDL-C were assayed by homogeneous enzymatic colorimetric method. For plasma glucose measurement, fasting and 2-h-post-load antecubital venous blood was collected with vacuum tubes anticoagulated by sodium fluoride and potassium oxalate, respectively. After centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min to isolate plasma, the plasma samples were delivered to the local laboratories and assayed within 24 h. Plasma glucose was determined by hexokinase method.

Assessment of VD status and tobacco smoking exposure

VD status in participants was assessed based on serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D) concentration, which was calculated as the sum of serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D2 (25(OH)D2) and serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 (25(OH)D3) concentrations. Both of them were determined using high performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass (LC–MS) (API 4500MD, AB SCIEX, USA), with standard reference material 972a used for accuracy control. VD status was classified into VDD designated as serum 25(OH)D concentration < 12 ng/ml (30 nmol/L), VDI as serum 25(OH)D concentration between 12–20 ng/ml (30–50 nmol/L), and VD sufficiency (VDS) as serum 25(OH)D concentration > 20 ng/ml (50 nmol/L)20. Tobacco smoking exposure was classified as two levels, smoking (smokers) and non-smoking (non-smokers), based on whether the participants smoked or not when they were interviewed. Smoking included self-reported daily smoking and smoking but not daily, and non-smoking included never smoking and former smoking. Of the 8455 participants, one having missing information of tobacco smoking were excluded, and 8454 completing both assessment of VD status and tobacco smoking were included for this present analysis.

Covariates/potential confounders and category for the adjustment analysis

The Covariates/potential confounders for the adjustment analysis included sex, ethnicity, marital status, education attainment and occupation, residential altitude, alcohol use, sedentary time, history of possible vitamin D supplement (PVS), WC, Body mass index (BMI), hypertension, diabetes, TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, and TG. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2) (weight/height2). Hypertension was diagnosed at ≥ 140/90 or diagnosed before in the hospitals and taking anti-hypertension medication during the past two week. Sedentary time (> median), BMI (≥ 28 kg/m2), WC (men ≥ 90; women ≥ 85), TC (< 4.5 mmol/L), TG (< 1.7 mmol/L), HDL-C (men > 1.0 mmol/L; women > 1.3 mmol/L) and LDL-C (< 2.6 mmol/L) were categorized two levels for the analysis21. Participants who met at any one of criteria were diagnosed with diabetes as follows: FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, 2-h PG during OGTT ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, and a self-reported diagnosis of DM that was determined previously by a healthcare professional at the town-level/community health service center or above hospitals21.

Statistical analysis

During the Statistical Analysis, the complex sample survey design and sampling weight were considered for the analysis for obtaining the unbiased estimated results representing the adult residents of Henan. The average serum 25(OH)D concentration and its 95%CI were estimated for the adult residents as dividing the weighted total serum 25(OH)D concentration by the weighted total adult residents. The average serum 25(OH)D concentration between smoking and non-smoking was compared using t test, which was estimated with weighted simple linear regression with robust variance estimation of Taylor linearization. The weighted multiple linear regression models with robust variance estimation of Taylor linearization based on complex sampling design were created taking serum 25(OH)D concentration as dependent variable and smoking as independent variable to explore whether smoking is independently associated with serum 25(OH)D concentration after adjustment for the other studied factors. The linear regression coefficient (β) was used to measure the association. Variance inflation factors (VIF) were assessed to check multicollinearity among the variables. A VIF value greater than 10 was considered as an indication of multi-collinearity. However, no significant multicollinearity was observed. The prevalence rates/proportions of smoking, VDD and VDI in the adult residents were estimated, respectively, as dividing the weighted frequency of smoking, VDD and VDI by the weighted total adult residents, respectively. Design-based (DB) F statistic, which is turned into with non-integer degrees of freedom by using a second-order Rao and Scott correction for accounting for the survey design from the usual Pearson Chi-square statistic for two-way tables under the survey module, was used for the comparison of rates or proportions22. The sampling error was estimated using Taylor linearization. Weighted multinomial logistic regression model with robust variance estimation of Taylor linearization based on complex sampling design was applied to estimate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (95%CI) for measuring the association between smoking as independent variable and VD status including three categories, VDD, VDI and VDI as dependent variable. The category VDS was considered as reference category (baseline category) and specified as “0” and the remaining categories i.e. VDD and VDI were considered as alternative category. The potential confounding factors studied were included as independent variables in the models when multivariate regression was conducted. Stata survey data analysis module was employed for the analyses. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Average serum 25(OH)D concentration by smoking and sex

The average serum 25(OH)D concentration was estimated at 17.1 ng/ml (95%CI 16.1, 18.2) in the adults. Men exhibited an average concentration at 18.9 ng/ml (95%CI 18.0, 19.9), significantly higher than the level in women, which stood at 15.4 ng/ml (95%CI 14.2, 16.6) (t = 9.10, p < 0.001). The average serum 25(OH)D concentration was 18.2 ng/ml in smokers, significantly higher than that in non-smokers (16.7 ng/ml; t = 3.39, p = 0.005). After stratified by sex, the average serum 25(OH)D concentration was 18.2 ng/ml in male smokers, significantly lower than that in male non-smokers (19.8 ng/ml; t = 2.74, p = 0.018); the difference of the average serum 25(OH)D concentration between female smokers and female non-smokers was not significant (t = 0.18, P = 0.859). The conflicting result about the effect of smoking on serum 25(OH)D concentration between pre-stratification and post-stratification suggested that sex might be the effect modifiers (Table 2).

Linear regression analysis results

Taking serum 25(OH)D concentration as dependent variable, simple and multiple line regression analysis results both demonstrated that smoking was significantly associated with serum 25(OH)D concentration in men. Multiple linear regression analysis revealed that after adjustment for age and other factors studied, smoking was a significant independent determinant of serum 25(OH)D concentration in men with the adjusted β of − 1.90 (95%CI − 2.79, − 1.02), suggesting that serum 25(OH)D concentration was reduced by 1.90 ng/ml in male smokers compared to male non-smokers. In women, no association with statistical significance was found between smoking and serum 25(OH)D concentration with adjusted β and 95%CI of 0.63 (− 1.89, 3.14) (Table 3).

Estimated prevalence rate of smoking and distribution of VD status

The estimated prevalence rate of smoking was 26.9% (95%CI 23.7%, 30.3%) in the adults, and it was 54.8% (95%CI 47.0%, 62.3%) in men, far higher than that in women, which was 0.4% (95%CI 0.2%, 0.7%), implying that the overwhelming majority of smokers were men.

The estimated proportions of VDD and VDI was 17.4% and 44.8% in smokers, and 26.7% and 45.1% in non-smokers, respectively, and the difference in the distribution of VD status between smokers and non-smokers in the adults was significantly (DB F = 12.98, P < 0.001). After stratification by sex, it was significant in men (DB F = 4.97, P = 0.026), but not significant in women (DB F = 0.18, P = 0.798). In men, smokers had higher proportions of VDD and VDI, which were 17.3% and 44.7%, respectively, significantly higher than non- smokers (14.3% and 38.3%, respectively), suggesting that smoking might increase the risk of VDD and VDI in men (Table 4).

Multinomial logistic regression analysis results

Taking VD status as dependent variable, multivariate multinomial logistic regression analysis results demonstrated that smoking was an independent factor associated with VDD and VDI in the adults or in men only. With consideration of the category VDS as the base outcome (baseline category), the adjusted OR (AOR) of smoking estimated was 1.77 (95%CI 1.13, 2.78) for VDD and 1.58 (95%CI 1.19, 2.12) for VDI in the adults, and they were 2.34 (95%CI 1.14, 3.26) for VDD and 1.65 (95%CI 1.22, 2.23) for VDI in men, suggesting that smokers had a 134% and 65% increased likelihood of VDD and VDI compared to non-smokers in men, respectively. No significant association between smoking and VD status was identified in women (Table 5).

Modification effects by HDL-C and LDL-C in men

Modification effects on the associations between smoking and serum 25(OH)D concentration or VD status by HDL-C and LDL-C in men were examined based on stratified analysis using the multiple linear and multinomial logistic regression models, respectively. Based on the adjusted β And AOR estimated, it seems that the effect of smoking on serum 25(OH) concentration/ VD status was significant in men with low HDL-C or high LDL-C, but not in men with high HDL-C or low LDL-C. When considering HDL-C and LDL-C together, the stratified analysis showed that AOR (95%CI) of smoking was 8.24 (3.14, 21.62) for VDD and 4.08 (2.41, 6.91) for VDI in men with low HDL-C and high LDL-C, 1.19 (0.23, 6.17) for VDD and 1.03 (0.75, 1.42) for VDI in men with high HDL-C and Low LDL-C, 1.64 (0.55, 4.86) for VDD and 2.41 (0.78, 7.45) for VDI in men living with low HDL-C and Low LDL-C, and 2.80 (1.33, 5.87) for VDD and 1.64 (1.15, 2.32) for VDI in men with high HDL-C and high LDL-C, suggesting that the risk of smoking on VDD and VDI might differ with the levels of HDL-C and LDL-C in men, and the risk of smoking on VDD and VDI was highest in men with low HDL-C and high LDL-C, followed by either low HDL-C or high LDL-C. In men living with high HDL-C and low LDL-C, AOR (95%CI) was 1.19 (0.23, 6.17) for VDD and 1.03 (0.75, 1.42) for VDI, and adjusted β was − 0.33 (− 2.57, 1.90), which suggested that there might be little or no effect of smoking on VD status/serum 25(OH)D men without dyslipidemia (Table 6).

Discussion

Effects of smoking on VD status and modification effects by HDL-C and LDL-C

This present study examined the effects of smoking on serum 25(OH)D concentration and VD status in the adult residents of Henan, the central part of China, and found that overall smoking was significantly associated with serum 25(OH)D concentration and VD status in the adults, and after stratification by sex, smoking reduced serum 25(OH)D concentration and increased the risk to have unfavorable VD status in men, especially in men with high-level LDL-C or low-level HDL-C. The linear regression and logistic regression analysis results showed that smoking reduced serum 25(OH) concentration by 1.90 ng/ml and increased the risk of VDD by 134% and VDI by 65% in men, and reduced serum 25(OH)D concentration by 2.94 ng/ml and increased the risk of VDD by 724% and VDI by 308% in men with low-level HDL-C and high-level LDL-C after adjustment for potential confounding factors studied. Unfortunately, no significant association between smoking and VD was found in women.

The previous studies based on the data men and women combined in USA, UK, South Europe and Puerto Rican supported that smoking increased the risk of unfavorable VD status, and detected an increased risk of VDD/VDI in smokers with AOR ranging from 1.4 to 2.223–26. This present study showed that smoking was at increased risk of VDD/VDI, with AOR of 1.77 for VDD and 1.58 for VDI in the adults, which was consistent with the previous studies mentioned-above. A meta-analysis also supported that the smoking reduced the serum 25(OH) D and increased the risk of VDD/VDI in spite of a small effect10. One study in Greek reported that smoking was a significant determinant of serum 25(OH)D, increasing the likelihood of VDD by 60% in young men, and decreasing the 25(OH)D level by 4.3 ng/ml (p < 0.001) in male smokers compared to male non-smokers27. The study in south Chinese that smoking reduced serum 25(OH) D concentration by 0.96 ng/ml (3.85 nmol/L) in men aged 50 + years. By comparison, our study results demonstrated that smoking increasing the likelihood of VDD and VDI by 134% and 65%, respectively, and reduced serum 25(OH)D concentration by1.90 ng/ml in men, and the much higher effect was observed in men with low-level HDL-C or high-level LDL-C.

In women, the adjusted β (95%CI) was 0.63 (− 1.89, 3.14), and AOR (95%CI) was 0.40 (0.07, 2.40) for VDD and 1.00 (0.27, 3.69) for VDI based on the sex-stratification multivariate regression analysis results of this present study, suggesting that probably smoking is not a risk factor in women. The studies conducted in the pregnant smokers and non-smokers in the Spain and Iran28 didn’t draw the conclusion that tobacco exposure was an independent risk factor for unfavorable VD status in women. These previous studies support our conclusion that smoking reduced levels of serum 25(OH)D and increased the risk of unfavorable VD status in men, but not in women. However, it is quite different with the recent publication based on the USA NHANES data over the entire 14-year period (2001–2014), which demonstrated that the stronger association was observed in women than that in men despite of the finding that tobacco smoking exposure was associated with increased risk of VDD in men and women11. Two studies in Denmark and USA also revealed that tobacco smoking exposure significantly reduced levels of serum 25(OH)D in women29,30. It was reported that ethnicity/race was an effect modifier for the association between tobacco smoking exposure and VD status in the adults11,29. It may be the difference of race/ethnicity that led to the inconsistence between this present study and the studies mentioned-above. Bear in mind that the prevalence of smoking in women of this present study was only 0.4%, so it needed large sample size to confirm whether there is the association between smoking and VD in women. The far low smoking exposure rate in women led to an increased difficulty in drawing a convincing conclusion, so we are not confident whether the sample size was enough for exploring the association between smoking and VDD/VDI in women in this present study, and suggest to do a further study. The substantial difference in the prevalence rates of smoking between men and women is a prominent feature of smoking in Henan owing to the culture and tradition. Owing to the far low prevalence rate of smoking in women, smoking would have little impact on the prevalence of VDD/VDI in female women in Henan even if it might be associated with VDD/VDI.

The mechanism underlying how smoking reduces serum 25(OH)D concentration and increased the risk of VDD/VDI remains unclear, but several potential pathways have been suggested5,27: smoking may alter the hepatic metabolism of VD, reduces the intake of food rich in VD through changing the dietary taste; nicotine and Benzo[a]Pyrene in tobacco may decrease body storage of VD; skin aging resulted from smoking may reduce the cutaneous production of VD3; heavy metals like Cd and Pb in tobacco smoke may inhibit VD activation mediated by Cd and Pb poisoning; smoking usually accompanies by a unhealthy lifestyle with reduced sun exposure and thus affect the synthesis of VD.

Modification effect of HDL-C and LDL-C on the associations between smoking and serum 25(OH)D concentration or VD status by in men were examined in this present study. Based on stratified analysis results, the effect of smoking on serum 25(OH)D concentration or VD status differed with the level of HDL-C or LDL-C in men. The differences of adjusted β, AOR for VDD and AOR for VDI in the men between low HDL-C and high HDL-C groups, and between low LDL-C and high LDL-C groups were distinct despite that some of them had no statistical significance, suggesting HDL-C and LDL-C might be the effect modifiers for the association between smoking and VD status/serum 25(OH)D concentration in men. In this present study, the highest risk of smoking for VDD and VDI was observed in men with low HDL-C and high LDL-C, followed by those with either low HDL-C or high LDL-C, and little or no effect was observed in men with high HDL-C and low LDL-C, implying that smoking might primarily reduce 25(OH)D in men with low HDL-C or high LDL-C or dyslipidemia. Since smoking is linked to low HDL-C or high LDL-C, and the well-documented association between dyslipidemia and VD raises the possibility that dyslipidemia may directly contribute to reduced serum 25(OH)D levels, it provides a clue that smoking may reduce 25(OH)D level through increasing LDL-C or reducing HDL-C31–35. However, it remains unconfirmed whether VDD is a cause or effect of dyslipidemia33.

Strengths and limitations

This present study was based on the sample selected using the multi-stage stratified cluster sampling method, well-representing the adult residents in Henan, and the sampling weight and design was considered during the analysis, and attempted to investigate as many potential factors of VDD as possible for adjustment. Serum 25(OH)D, which is considered a stable and reliable marker of VD status, was used for assessing the VD status. Serum 25(OH)D concentration was determined by LC–MS/MS, which is the most reliable method for measuring the serum 25(OH)D concentration currently, and the standard reference material 972a was used as the accuracy control. Therefore, the conclusions in this present study were drawn about the effect of smoking on VD status is solid. In addition, to our knowledge, our finding that the effect of smoking on serum 25(OH)D concentration or vitamin D status is modified by HDL‑C and LDL‑C levels in men has not been documented in the existing literature. However, certain limitations of this present study need to be appropriately considered. For this present study, only one measurement (single blood test) of serum25(OH)D concentration for each participant was taken in the late fall or winter when people usually have reduced outdoor activities and little area of their skin exposed under the sunlight owing to low temperature and smog and haze, so it may not reflect long-term VD status. This present study is the cross-sectional design, so the associations presented between independent factors and outcome variables do not necessarily represent causal relationships. This present study was setting in the central part of China, most of the participants are Han ethnicity, and the prevalence of smoking was very small in women owing to the culture and tradition. Therefore, the data presented in this study might not be applicable to the general populations in other regions, and it should be cautious to extrapolate the conclusion of this present study to the residents in other regions. The factors such as intake of food rich in VD, sun exposure, clothing and seasonal variation that can affect VD status were not included in this present study, so we arbitrarily assumed that the distribution of these factors balanced between the smoking and non-smoking groups.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this present study, a population-based study set in Henan, the central part of China explored the effect of smoking on the level or status of VD in the adults based on a cross-sectional data and found that smoking was an independent determinant of serum 25(OH)D concentration and VD status, significantly increasing the likelihood of having reduced serum VD concentration and suffering from VDD/VDI in men, especially in men with low-level HDL-C and high-level LDL-C in Henan, the central part of China. It is suggested for the future study to explore whether smoking might reduce 25(OH)D level through reducing HDL-C or increasing LDL-C in men.
