The Centrality Paradox: Why Your Friends Are Always More Important
Rajat Subhra Hazra, Evgeny Verbitskiy

Abstract
. We revisit the classical friendship paradox which states that on an average one’s friends have at least as many friends as oneself and generalize it to a variety of network centrality measures. In particular, we show that for any irreducible, undirected graph G G , the “friends‐average” of degree, eigenvector‐centrality, walk‐count, Katz, and PageRank centralities exceeds the global average. We show that the result follows from the variational characterisation of the eigenvector corresponding to the Perron eigenvalue.



1.
Introduction

The
friendship paradox
was first noted by
Feld 1991
and it states that on average, your friends have more friends than you do. Although noted as a paradox, this can be easily established as a fact on any general graph with minimal assumptions. The phenomenon arises from a sampling bias, that is, high-degree nodes appear more frequently in local neighborhoods, increasing the average degree seen by a typical individual. The paradox is more than a curiosity and it influences our perception of popularity, influence, and norms in networks, and plays a role in phenomena such as the spread of diseases or misinformation. There has been many recent works which address the implications of the friendship paradox on perception, see for example
Eom and Jo 2014
,
Hodas et al. 2013
,
Cantwell et al. 2021
,
Kumar et al. 2024
,
Wilson 2010
.

The friendship paradox asserts that in any finite, undirected connected graph
G=(V,E)
,

\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{i\in V}\Bigl(\,\frac{1}{d_{i}}\sum_{j\sim i}d_{j}\Bigr)\geq\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{i\in V}d_{i}

where
d_{i}
denotes the degree of node
i
and
j\sim i
indicates adjacency. Equivalently, if one chooses a random node
i
and then picks one of its neighbors at random, that neighbor’s degree is stochastically at least as large as
d_{i}
. There are multiple ways to prove this result.

The degree of a node in a graph is itself a centrality measure. Centrality measures assign a numerical score to each node in a network to capture its relative importance; common examples include degree centrality, PageRank, eigenvector centrality, Katz centrality, and betweenness centrality, each highlighting a different facet of node’s influence. It is therefore natural to generalize the friendship paradox into a “centrality paradox,” which states that, on average, your friends are more central than you are. In recent years, such generalizations have been suggested. For example,
Higham 2019
analyzes a version of the centrality paradox in which one chooses a random edge (friendship) and then a random endpoint of that edge, and compares the average centrality of those sampled friends to the global average.
Cantwell et al. 2021
showed that if you replace “degree” by any nonnegative attribute
x_{i}
on each node
i
, then the difference between the average of
x
over randomly sampled neighbors and the global average of
x
is governed by the covariance between
x
and degree. In other words, whether your friends look “larger” on attribute
x
than the average person depends precisely on how strongly
x
is correlated with degree.

In this article we take a complementary perspective: rather than sampling an edge and one of its endpoints, we reformulate the paradox by averaging each node’s centrality over its neighbors. For
\mathbf{r}:V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
a centrality measure we compare the mean of
\{\mathbf{r}(j):j\sim i\}
for each
i
against the overall mean of
\mathbf{r}(i)
. With this “neighbor‐averaging” definition in place, we then prove that the resulting centrality paradox holds not only for degree, but also for eigenvector centrality, walk‐count centralities (powers of adjacency matrix), Katz centrality, and even PageRank on strongly connected directed graphs.

More recently,
Hazra et al. 2025a
,
Hazra et al. 2025c
,
Hazra et al. 2025b
have analyzed the
node‐level
friendship‐paradox bias in sparse random‐graph models. For each node
i
, they study

\Delta_{i}\;=\;\frac{1}{d_{i}}\sum_{j\sim i}d_{j}\;-\;d_{i}

and derive its distributional limits, concentration behavior, and dependence on the local tree‐like structure as the graph grows. In the context of centrality measures, one can analogously define

\Delta_{i}^{(\mathbf{r})}\;=\;\frac{1}{d_{i}}\sum_{j\sim i}r_{j}\;-\;r_{i}

for any nonnegative centrality
\mathbf{r}
. Characterizing the distribution, fluctuations, and structural dependencies of these
\Delta_{i}^{(\mathbf{r})}
in random‐graph and real‐world networks remains an open and promising direction for future research, which we further discuss in Section
5
.

Outline of the Paper

After this introduction, in Section
2
we set up notation for an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
and introduce the notion of centrality paradox and define five prototypical centrality measures (degree, walk-count, eigenvector, Katz and PageRank) that will be studied. In Section
4
we prove the centrality paradox for each of these cases, showing that on any connected graph the mean neighbor-centrality strictly exceeds the node’s own score. Section
4
then compares two different friend-averaging statistics (the node-averaged versus edge-sampled versions) and highlights when they agree or differ. Finally, in Section
5
we discuss limitations of our approach (in particular for distance-based measures), connections to sparse random-graph models via local weak limits, and promising directions for extending these results to more global or tree-like settings.

2.
Centrality Paradox

2.1.
Matrices and eigenvectors

Let
G=(V,E)
be a connected undirected graph (without self-loops) with
|V|=n
nodes and
|E|=m
edges. We label
V=\{1,\dots,n\}
. Write
A
for its adjacency matrix, so
A_{ij}
is the number of edges between
i
and
j
and
A
is symmetric with zeros on the diagonal.
The degree of node
i
is

d_{i}\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij},\quad\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})^{\top},\quad D=\mathrm{diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{n}).

Since
G
is undirected and connected,
A
is irreducible and nonnegative; its spectral radius
\rho(A)=\lambda_{1}>0
is a simple eigenvalue with associated left/right Perron eigenvectors
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}>0
(we normalize
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{1}=1
). Define the transition matrix for the simple random walk on the graph by

(1)

C\;=\;D^{-1}A\,,

which is row‐stochastic (
C\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}
) and irreducible. Thus
1
is its Perron eigenvalue of
C
with right eigenvector
\mathbf{1}>0
, and its unique positive left eigenvector
\mathbf{w}^{\top}
satisfies
\mathbf{w}^{\top}C=\mathbf{w}^{\top}
,
\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=1
. In fact
\mathbf{w}=d^{\top}/\|d\|_{1}
, since

\mathbf{d}^{\top}C\;=\;\mathbf{d}^{\top}D^{-1}A\;=\;\mathbf{1}^{\top}A\;=\;\mathbf{d}^{\top}.

2.2.
Centrality measures

A centrality measure
\mathbf{r}:V\to[0,+\infty)
is a non-negative function on vertices/nodes; we view
\mathbf{r}
is a non-negative column vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It assigns to each node a non‐negative score reflecting its “importance” within the network structure, by aggregating simple nodal statistics like counts of neighbours or walks, through a weighting function to capture how influence or connectivity decays with distance. We refer to the survey article
Saxena and Iyengar 2020
for the discussion of various centrality measures. Here we focus on the following important ones.

(1)

Degree centrality:
Degree centrality is the most straightforward way to study a node’s importance: it simply counts how many connections the node has. In an undirected network, this equals the total number of edges incident on the node. In a directed network, we distinguish between in-degree (the number of incoming links) and out-degree (the number of outgoing links).

(2)

Eigenvector centrality:

Defines a node’s score proportional to the sum of its neighbors’ scores, i.e. as the principal eigenvector of the adjacency matrix. It highlights nodes connected to other well‐connected nodes, and in friendship‐paradox terms, identifies those whose friends are themselves highly central.

(3)

Walk counts:
This is not in general considered as a centrality measure but plays a crucial role in many centrality measures (
Estrada 2010
). The rank is given through the number of walks of length
\ell
starting from a vertex.

(4)

Katz centrality:
Extends degree by counting all walks emanating from a node, exponentially down‐weighting longer walks via a parameter
\alpha<\frac{1}{\rho(A)}
, where
\rho(A)
is the spectral radius of the adjacency matrix. It captures both direct ties and indirect influence, but still emphasizes nearer neighbors more heavily.

(5)

PageRank centrality:

A variant of eigenvector centrality that assumes a random‐walk with occasional “teleportation” (restart) to any node with probability
1-\beta
. PageRank reflects both the quantity and quality of friends, and mitigates sink effects by redistributing rank.

Definition 2.1
.

Centrality paradox
: Given a finite connected graph
G=(V,E)
with
|V|=n
. If
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})^{\top}
be a node‐wise centrality, define

\overline{\mu}_{\mathbf{r}}\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(\,\frac{1}{d_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}r_{j}\Bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{\mathbf{r}}\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}.

We say that the
centrality paradox for

\mathbf{r}
holds if

\overline{\mu}_{\mathbf{r}}\;\geq\;\mu_{\mathbf{r}}.

Recall, the
C
matrix as defined in (
1
), then the centrality paradox is equivalent to

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{n}C_{ij}r_{j}-r_{i}\Bigr)\geq 0,

or, equivalently,

(2)

\langle\mathbf{1},C\mathbf{r}\rangle\geq\langle\mathbf{1},\mathbf{r}\rangle.

Theorem 2.2

(Centrality Paradox)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a connected, undirected graph on
n
vertices, with adjacency matrix
A
, degree vector
\mathbf{d}
, and Perron eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
. We consider the following centralities
\mathbf{r}
,

\displaystyle\text{Degree:}

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=A\,\mathbf{1},

\displaystyle\text{Walk‐count (order }\ell\text{):}

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=A^{\ell}\,\mathbf{1},

\displaystyle\text{Eigenvector:}

\displaystyle A\,\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}\,\mathbf{r},

\displaystyle\text{Katz:}

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=(I-\alpha A)^{-1}\mathbf{1},\quad 0<\alpha<1/\lambda_{1},

\displaystyle\text{PageRank:}

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=(1-\beta)\,C\,\mathbf{r}+\beta\tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{1},\qquad\beta\in(0,\,1).

Then in each case the centrality paradox holds. In other words, on any connected graph your “average friend” has higher centrality than you do.

Remark 2.3
.

In the next section we provide a detailed definitions of the centrality and provide the proofs for each of these individual centrality paradox.

The proofs depend crucially on the following variational formulation of the eigenvector corresponding to the Perron eigenvalue.

Theorem 2.4

(
Fiedler et al. 1985
)
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be irreducible and nonnegative, with the Perron eigenvalue
\lambda>0
, right eigenvector
\mathbf{u}>0
, and left eigenvector
\mathbf{v}>0
normalized so that
\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{u}=1
. Then for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}>0
with
\mathbf{x}\circ\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{u}\circ\mathbf{v}
(where
\circ
is the Hadamard product
\mathbf{x}\circ\mathbf{y}=(x_{1}y_{1},\ldots,x_{n}y_{n})^{\top}
),

\mathbf{y}^{\top}P\,\mathbf{x}\;\geq\;\mathbf{v}^{\top}P\,\mathbf{u}=\lambda,

and equality occurs if and only if
\mathbf{x}
is a scalar multiple of
\mathbf{u}
(whence
\mathbf{y}
is the corresponding inverse scalar multiple of
\mathbf{v}
). In particular, setting
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{u}
gives

\mathbf{u}^{\top}P\,\mathbf{v}\;\geq\;\mathbf{v}^{\top}P\,\mathbf{u}=\lambda,

with equality if and only if
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
are linearly dependent.

3.
Centrality Paradox for Specific Centralities

Below we expand briefly on each centrality’s definition and importance, then prove its paradox.

3.1.
Degree Centrality

Degree centrality is the simplest measure: a node’s centrality is the number of its immediate neighbors. It was introduced in social network analysis to quantify local influence and is widely used because of its interpretability and computational efficiency.
Here
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{d}=A\,\mathbf{1}
. Then

\langle\mathbf{1},\,C\,\mathbf{d}\rangle\;=\;\langle\mathbf{1},\,D^{-1}A\,\mathbf{d}\rangle\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{d_{i}}\,\bigl(A\,\mathbf{d}\bigr)_{i}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{d_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}\,d_{j},

while

\langle\mathbf{1},\,\mathbf{d}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}.

Thus

\langle\mathbf{1},\,C\,\mathbf{d}\rangle-\langle\mathbf{1},\,\mathbf{d}\rangle\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{d_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}d_{j}\;-\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}\Bigl(\sqrt{\tfrac{d_{j}}{d_{i}}}-\sqrt{\tfrac{d_{i}}{d_{j}}}\Bigr)^{2}\;\geq\;0.

Hence

\langle\mathbf{1},\,C\,\mathbf{d}\rangle\geq\langle\mathbf{1},\,\mathbf{d}\rangle,

with strict inequality unless all degrees are equal (i.e.
G
is regular). Instead of the symmetrization trick one can use the variational formulation of Theorem
2.4
to show this result as well.

Since
C
is irreducible,
1
is its simple Perron eigenvalue with right eigenvector
\mathbf{1}
and left eigenvector
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{d}/\|\mathbf{d}\|_{1}
. By Theorem
2.4
, choosing
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{w}
and
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{1}
gives

\langle\mathbf{1},\,C\mathbf{w}\rangle\;\geq\;\langle\mathbf{w},\,C\mathbf{1}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{w},\,\mathbf{1}\rangle=1,

i.e.

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\|\mathbf{d}\|_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}\,d_{j}\Bigr)\;\geq\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tfrac{d_{i}}{\|\mathbf{d}\|_{1}}=1,

or equivalently,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{d_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}d_{j}\;\geq\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i},

recovering the classical friendship paradox for degree.

3.2.
Eigenvector centrality

Eigenvector centrality (
Bonacich 2007
) assigns each node a value proportional to the sum of its neighbors’ values and it captures a node’s influence in terms of the influence of its neighbors. It other words, it solves

A\,\mathbf{r}=\lambda_{1}\,\mathbf{r},\qquad\|\mathbf{r}\|_{1}=1,\quad\mathbf{r}>0.

In eigenvector centrality a node with relatively few links can score highly if it ties directly into other well-connected or highly central nodes. Formally, each node’s centrality is defined to be proportional to the sum of the centralities of its neighbors, which means that nodes positioned close to the most prominent individuals or tightly knit communities naturally attain higher eigenvector scores.

Since
A
is symmetric,
\langle\mathbf{r},\,A\,\mathbf{1}\rangle=\langle A\,\mathbf{r},\,\mathbf{1}\rangle=\lambda_{1}\,\langle\mathbf{r},\,\mathbf{1}\rangle=\lambda_{1}.

We need to show (
2
) and hence we compute

\langle\mathbf{1},\,C\,\mathbf{r}\rangle\;=\;\langle\mathbf{1},\,D^{-1}A\,\mathbf{r}\rangle\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{d_{i}}\,(A\,\mathbf{r})_{i}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{1}\,r_{i}}{d_{i}}.

Meanwhile
\langle\mathbf{1},\,\mathbf{r}\rangle=1.
Thus we need

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{1}\,r_{i}}{d_{i}}\;\geq\;1,\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{r_{i}}{d_{i}}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}.

As
\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}=1
by the weighted harmonic–arithmetic mean inequality we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{r_{i}}{d_{i}}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\,d_{i}}\;=\;\frac{1}{\langle\mathbf{r},\,\mathbf{d}\rangle}\;=\;\frac{1}{\langle\mathbf{r},\,A\,\mathbf{1}\rangle}\;=\;\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}.

Therefore

\langle\mathbf{1},\,C\,\mathbf{r}\rangle\geq\langle\mathbf{1},\,\mathbf{r}\rangle
,
with equality if and only if all
d_{i}
coincide (i.e.
G
is regular).

3.3.
Walk‐Count

Walk‐count of order
\ell
counts the total number of walks of length
\ell
starting from each node. For a small
\ell
, it captures local connectivity; for larger
\ell
, it approximates eigenvector centrality as
\ell\to\infty
. The walk counts play a crucial role in various centralities such as the Katz and PageRank which we discuss later. See also
Estrada et al. 2009
where powers of matrices measure the communicability between nodes in a graph. Define

\mathbf{r}=A^{\ell}\,\mathbf{1},\qquad r_{i}=\bigl(A^{\ell}\,\mathbf{1}\bigr)_{i}.

Then

\langle\mathbf{1},\,C\,\mathbf{r}\rangle\;=\;\langle\mathbf{1},\,D^{-1}A\,A^{\ell}\,\mathbf{1}\rangle\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{d_{i}}\,(A^{\ell+1}\mathbf{1})_{i},\quad\langle\mathbf{1},\,\mathbf{r}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(A^{\ell}\mathbf{1})_{i}.

We will use the following result from
(
Eaves et al. 1985
, Corollary 3)
.
Since
W=A^{\ell}
is symmetric and nonnegative, and irreducible for
\ell\geq 1
we have that for any positive
\mathbf{x}
,

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}x_{i}\,W_{ij}\,x_{j}^{-1}\;\geq\;\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}W_{ij},

with equality if and only if all
x_{i}
equal. Taking
x_{i}=1/d_{i}
, we get

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{d_{i}}\,W_{ij}\,d_{j}\;\geq\;\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}W_{ij}\;=\;\mathbf{1}^{\top}W\,\mathbf{1}.

Hence

\langle\mathbf{1},\,C\,\mathbf{r}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{d_{i}}\,(A^{\ell+1}\mathbf{1})_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{d_{i}}(WA\mathbf{1})_{i}={\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{d_{i}}W_{ij}d_{j}}\geq\sum_{i,j}W_{ij}=\langle\mathbf{1},\,A^{\ell}\,\mathbf{1}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{1},\,\mathbf{r}\rangle,

with equality if and only if
d_{i}
are all equal (so
G
is regular). For
\ell=0
,
W=I
and equality holds trivially.

3.4.
Katz Centrality

Katz centrality (
Katz 1953
) measures influence by counting all walks, exponentially damped by length. Let
\lambda_{1}
be the largest eigenvalue of
A
and choose
0<\alpha<1/\lambda_{1}
and define

\mathbf{r}=(I-\alpha A)^{-1}\,\mathbf{1}\;=\;\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\alpha^{\ell}\,A^{\ell}\,\mathbf{1},

which converges entrywise because
\alpha\,\lambda_{1}<1
. Note

\langle\mathbf{1},\,C\,\mathbf{r}\rangle\;=\;\langle\mathbf{1},\,D^{-1}A\,\bigl((I-\alpha A)^{-1}\mathbf{1}\bigr)\rangle\;=\;\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\alpha^{\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(A^{\ell+1}\mathbf{1})_{i}}{d_{i}},

whereas

\langle\mathbf{1},\,\mathbf{r}\rangle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\alpha^{\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(A^{\ell}\mathbf{1})_{i}.

Therefore

\langle\mathbf{1},\,C\,\mathbf{r}\rangle-\langle\mathbf{1},\,\mathbf{r}\rangle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\alpha^{\ell}\Bigl[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tfrac{(A^{\ell+1}\mathbf{1})_{i}}{d_{i}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}(A^{\ell}\mathbf{1})_{i}\Bigr].

But by the argument above for walk-count centrality measure, one has

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tfrac{(A^{\ell+1}\mathbf{1})_{i}}{d_{i}}\;\geq\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}(A^{\ell}\mathbf{1})_{i},

with equality if and only if
G
is regular. Since
\alpha^{\ell}>0
, each summand is nonnegative and at least one is strictly positive when
G
is non-regular. Therefore

\langle\mathbf{1},\,C\,\mathbf{r}\rangle\geq\langle\mathbf{1},\,\mathbf{r}\rangle.

3.5.
PageRank Centrality (Directed Setting)

PageRank refines eigenvector centrality by dampening the effect that a highly central node has when it links to many others (
Page et al. 1999
). It evaluates each node’s importance based on both the quantity and quality of its inbound links, in particular, a link from a modestly connected page carries more weight than one from an out-degree “hub.” PageRank balances three factors: the number of incoming links, each linker’s tendency to distribute its opinion across its own outgoing edges, and the linking page’s own centrality. By incorporating this topology-driven feedback with a small probability of “teleporting” to any node, PageRank produces a stable measure of importance in complex networks.
In this subsection, we allow
G=(V,E)
to be a
strongly connected directed
graph on
n
nodes. Write
A
to be adjacency matrix, that is,

A_{ij}=1\text{ if there is a directed edge }i\to j,\text{ and }0\text{ otherwise}.

Define the out‐degree vector

d_{i}^{\mathrm{out}}\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij},\qquad D_{\mathrm{out}}=\mathrm{diag}\bigl(d_{1}^{\mathrm{out}},\dots,d_{n}^{\mathrm{out}}\bigr).

Since
G
is strongly connected,
d_{i}^{\mathrm{out}}>0
for all
i
. We reformulate the transition matrix
C
as follows

C\;=\;D_{\mathrm{out}}^{-1}A,\quad C_{ij}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{d_{i}^{\mathrm{out}}},&i\to j,\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Because
G
is strongly connected,
C
is irreducible and
C\,\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}
.

Fix a teleportation parameter
\beta\in(0,1)
and define the PageRank matrix

P\;=\;(1-\beta)\,C+\beta\,\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}.

Since
C
is irreducible and
\mathbf{v}>0
,
P
is also irreducible and row‐stochastic. Hence the Perron eigenvalue of
P
is
\lambda=1
.

The PageRank centrality
\mathbf{r}
is defined as the unique normalized (
\langle\mathbf{1},\mathbf{r}\rangle=1
)
solution of

(3)

\mathbf{r}^{\top}P=\mathbf{r}^{\top}.

We would like to prove the directed‐PageRank paradox, that is,

(4)

\overline{\mu}_{\mathbf{r}}\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{d_{i}^{\mathrm{out}}}\sum_{\,j=1}^{n}A_{ij}r_{j}\Bigr)\;\geq\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\;=\;\mu_{\mathbf{r}},

equivalently,
\langle\mathbf{1},\,C\,\mathbf{r}\rangle\;\geq\;\langle\mathbf{1},\,\mathbf{r}\rangle=1
.

Since
P
is irreducible and row‐stochastic, we can apply Theorem
2.4

with
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{1}
being the left and right eigenvectors, respectively:

(5)

\mathbf{1}^{\top}\,P\,\mathbf{r}\;\geq\;\mathbf{r}^{\top}\,P\,\mathbf{1}\;=\;\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{1}\rangle\;=\;1,

Using the definition of
P
one has

\mathbf{1}^{\top}\,P\,\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{1}^{\top}\Bigl[(1-\beta)\,C+\frac{\beta}{n}\,\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\Bigr]\mathbf{r}=(1-\beta)\,\mathbf{1}^{\top}\,C\,\mathbf{r}\;+\;\frac{\beta}{n}\,\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{1}(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\,\mathbf{r})=(1-\beta)\,\mathbf{1}^{\top}\,C\,\mathbf{r}\;+\;\beta.

Hence from (
5
) it follows

(1-\beta)\,\mathbf{1}^{\top}\,C\,\mathbf{r}+\beta\;\geq\;1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathbf{1}^{\top}\,C\,\mathbf{r}\;\geq\;1\;=\;\mathbf{1}^{\top}\,\mathbf{r}.

Hence this shows (
4
).

4.
Comparison of centrality paradox

In the discussion above we have focused on the “neighbour‐averaging” version of the centrality paradox, namely

\bar{\mu}_{r}\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{d_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}r_{j},

which compares each node’s own score to the average score of its neighbours. An alternative, equally natural way to weight friends is by sampling edges uniformly at random and then looking at the centrality of the endpoint reached. This leads to the
edge‐weighted average

\tilde{\mu}_{r}\;=\;\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\,r_{i}}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_{k}}\;=\;\frac{\mathbf{r}^{\top}\mathbf{d}}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{d}},

where
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})^{T}
is the degree vector. In this section we compare
\bar{\mu}_{r}
and
\tilde{\mu}_{r}
. Note that they agree when all the degrees
d_{i}
are same. The centrality paradox for some of the centrality measures in terms of
\tilde{\mu}_{r}
was studied in
Higham 2019
.

Recall the two “friend‐average” quantities for a centrality vector
\mathbf{r}
:

\tilde{\mu}_{\mathbf{r}}\;=\;\frac{\mathbf{r}^{\top}\mathbf{d}}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{d}}\;=\;\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{j}\,d_{j}}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_{k}},\qquad\bar{\mu}_{\mathbf{r}}\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{d_{i}}\sum_{j\sim i}r_{j}.

We can rewrite
\bar{\mu}_{\mathbf{r}}
by swapping the order of summation:

\bar{\mu}_{\mathbf{r}}\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{d_{i}}\sum_{j\sim i}r_{j}\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{j}\sum_{\,i:\,i\sim j}\frac{1}{d_{i}}.

Thus

\bar{\mu}_{\mathbf{r}}\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{j}\,a_{j},\quad\text{where}\quad a_{j}=\sum_{\,i:\,i\sim j}\frac{1}{d_{i}}.

Meanwhile,

\tilde{\mu}_{\mathbf{r}}\;=\;\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{j}\,d_{j}}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_{k}}\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{j}\,b_{j},\quad\text{where}\quad b_{j}=\frac{d_{j}}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_{k}}.

Therefore,

\bar{\mu}_{\mathbf{r}}-\tilde{\mu}_{\mathbf{r}}\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{j}\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{n}a_{j}-b_{j}\Bigr).

In general there is no fixed inequality between
\bar{\mu}_{\mathbf{r}}
and
\tilde{\mu}_{\mathbf{r}}
without further assumptions on
\mathbf{r}
or
G
; they coincide when
G
is regular (all
d_{i}
equal), in which case
\bar{\mu}_{\mathbf{r}}=\tilde{\mu}_{\mathbf{r}}=\mu_{\mathbf{r}}
. These two quantities in general need not coincide.

Example:
Consider the degree centrality and the star graph on
n
nodes. A simple calculation shows

\tilde{\mu}_{d}\;=\;\frac{(n-1)^{2}+(n-1)\cdot 1^{2}}{2(n-1)}\;=\;\frac{n}{2},\quad\bar{\mu}_{d}\;=\;\frac{1+(n-1)^{2}}{n},

so
\bar{\mu}_{d}>\tilde{\mu}_{d}
for
n\geq 3
.

In general connected graphs, it is unclear if there is an universal ordering between
\bar{\mu}_{d}
and
\tilde{\mu}_{d}
.

Now instead of degree centrality, if one considers the eigenvector centrality, then one can show in certain cases,
\tilde{\mu}_{r}>\bar{\mu}_{r}
.

Example.

Consider a line graph on
6
vertices:
v_{1}
v_{2}
v_{3}
v_{4}
v_{5}
v_{6}

The adjacency matrix is given by

A\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&0&0&0\\[6.0pt]
1&0&1&0&0&0\\[6.0pt]
0&1&0&1&0&0\\[6.0pt]
0&0&1&0&1&0\\[6.0pt]
0&0&0&1&0&1\\[6.0pt]
0&0&0&0&1&0\end{pmatrix}

The characteristic polynomial is given by
\lambda^{6}-5\lambda^{4}+6\lambda-1
and the eigenvalues are

\lambda_{k}=2\cos\left(\frac{\pi k}{7}\right),\quad k=1,2,\ldots,6,

with the corresponding right eigenvectors

\mathbf{v}^{(k)}=\begin{bmatrix}\sin\left(\dfrac{k\pi}{7}\right),\sin\left(\dfrac{2k\pi}{7}\right),\sin\left(\dfrac{3k\pi}{7}\right),\sin\left(\dfrac{4k\pi}{7}\right),\sin\left(\dfrac{5k\pi}{7}\right),\sin\left(\dfrac{6k\pi}{7}\right)\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

The Perron eigenvalue is
\lambda=\lambda_{1}=2\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{7}\right)\approx 1.80194
with the normalized eigenvector

{\mathbf{r}}=[0.0990,\quad 0.1785,\quad 0.2224,\quad 0.2224,\quad 0.1785,\quad 0.0990]^{\top}.

If we consider the degree centrality
d=[1,2,2,2,2,1]^{\top}
, then

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}_{d}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{1}\cdot 2+\frac{1}{2}(1+2)+\frac{1}{2}(2+2)+\frac{1}{2}(2+2)+\frac{1}{2}(2+1)+\frac{1}{1}\cdot 2}{6}=\frac{11}{6}

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}_{d}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+4+4+4+4+1}{1+2+2+2+2+1}=\frac{14}{10}.

We see that
\bar{\mu}_{d}>\tilde{\mu}_{d}
.
However, if we consider eigenvector centrality
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{d}^{(2)}=A\mathbf{d}
we have

\begin{gathered}\bar{\mu}_{\mathbf{r}}\approx 0.1799,\quad\bar{\mu}_{\mathbf{r}}\approx 0.1822,\\
\bar{\mu}_{\mathbf{d}^{(2)}}\approx 5.8333,\quad\bar{\mu}_{\mathbf{d}^{(2)}}\approx 5.875,\end{gathered}

i.e., the opposite inequalities hold:
\bar{\mu}_{\mathbf{r}}<\tilde{\mu}_{\mathbf{r}}
and
\bar{\mu}_{\mathbf{d}^{(2)}}<\tilde{\mu}_{\mathbf{d}^{(2)}}
.

5.
Discussion: Open directions

We now end with some discussions on the open directions. We have shown that, in every non‐regular connected graph, the average centrality of a node’s neighbors strictly exceeds that node’s own score for a wide class of measures such as degree, eigenvector, walk-count, Katz and PageRank centralities. This unified “centrality paradox” thus holds across both local and global functionals. Here are some of the crucial open areas which immediately emerge from the findings of this article.

(1)

Not all popular measures fit our current framework. Closeness and harmonic centrality depend on shortest-path distances rather than powers or resolvents of the adjacency matrix, so our bilinear–inequality arguments (which hinge on quadratic forms and spectral identities) do not immediately apply. Determining whether the neighbor-averaging paradox holds for these distance-based measures remains open and likely requires novel comparisons of inverse-distance sums to their global averages.

(2)

In case of sparse random graphs such as Erdős-Rényi, configuration model and preferential attachment model,
Hazra et al. 2025a
analyze the “friendship bias” for degree centrality. One can similarly define the bias for any centrality measure by

\Delta_{i}^{(r)}=\frac{1}{d_{i}}\sum_{j\sim i}r_{j}-r_{i}.

Now one can define the empirical measure

\mu_{\mathrm{bias},n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\Delta_{i}^{(r)}},

where
\delta
is the dirac-delta measure. This measure contains non-trivial information about the paradox. In the case of classical friendship paradox with degree centrality, using the tools of local weak convergence (
van der Hofstad 2024
) one can show the above measure converges and can be expressed explicitly in terms of some random trees. In contrast, eigenvector and Katz centralities are inherently global: their scores at every node reflect the full network. One cannot simply replace the finite graph by its local tree limit and still capture these spectral quantities. Although one can be hopeful as these methods have been used for various centrality measures to study their properties, for example
Garavaglia et al. 2020
studies the local weak convergence results for page-rank centrality. See also
van der Hofstad and Pandey 2024
for other applications of local weak convergence and centrality measures. So the open direction would be to study the centrality measures mentioned in this article and prove the convergence of
\mu_{\mathrm{bias},n}
.

(3)

A promising avenue is to blend our paradox inequalities with recent spectral analyses on sparse, tree-like graphs
Bordenave and Lelarge 2010
. These studies characterize the limiting behaviour of eigenvalues of large random adjacency or non-backtracking matrices via Galton–Watson trees. If one can study the eigenvector properties for some explicit trees, it may become possible to derive precise limit laws for
centrality paradox for eigenvectors. Also in sparse random graphs there is a subtle interplay of localization and delocalization of eigenvectors and it would be interesting to find connections of the eigenvector centrality paradox and such physical phenomenon.
