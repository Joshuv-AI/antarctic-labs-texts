MentalBench : A Benchmark for Evaluating Psychiatric Diagnostic Capability of Large Language Models
Hoyun Song Migyeong Kang Jisu Shin Jihyun Kim Chanbi Park, Hangyeol Yoo Jihyun An Alice Oh Jinyoung Han KyungTae Lim

Abstract
We introduce MentalBench , a benchmark for evaluating psychiatric diagnostic decision-making in large language models (LLMs). Existing mental health benchmarks largely rely on social media data, limiting their ability to assess DSM-grounded diagnostic judgments. At the core of MentalBench is MentalKG , a psychiatrist-built and validated knowledge graph encoding DSM-5 diagnostic criteria and differential diagnostic rules for 23 psychiatric disorders. Using MentalKG as a golden-standard logical backbone, we generate 24,750 synthetic clinical cases that systematically vary in information completeness and diagnostic complexity, enabling low-noise and interpretable evaluation. Our experiments show that while state-of-the-art LLMs perform well on structured queries probing DSM-5 knowledge, they struggle to calibrate confidence in diagnostic decision-making when distinguishing between clinically overlapping disorders. These findings reveal evaluation gaps not captured by existing benchmarks. All resources are publicly released. 1 1 1 https://github.com/HoyunS/MentalBench

Introduction

The global burden of mental disorders continues to increase
32
, motivating interest in whether large language models (LLMs) can support mental health–related analytical tasks
23
;
46
. Prior work suggests that LLMs can identify clinically relevant signals in text and generate plausible diagnostic hypotheses by leveraging mental health knowledge acquired during pretraining
24
;
49
;
28
. However, psychiatric diagnosis is a high-stakes, criteria-driven process, and it remains unclear whether existing evaluations meaningfully assess how LLMs apply diagnostic criteria under realistic conditions.

A central difficulty in psychiatric diagnosis arises from its inherently multi-criteria and boundary-sensitive decision structure. Diagnostic judgments are governed by standardized frameworks such as DSM-5, which require integrating symptom combinations, duration constraints, functional impairment, exclusion rules, and distinctions between clinically similar disorders
5
. In practice, these judgments are further complicated by incomplete or selectively reported patient information. As a result, evaluating diagnostic performance requires benchmarks that go beyond factual recall of diagnostic criteria and test how models make decisions when diagnostic boundaries are unclear.

Despite this need, existing mental health benchmarks remain limited. Importantly, this gap reflects not a lack of interest, but the inherent difficulty in constructing high-quality psychiatric diagnostic data. Strict privacy regulations, the absence of objective biomarkers, and the complexity of formal diagnostic logic make large-scale curation of expert-validated datasets exceptionally challenging
16
;
48
. Consequently, most benchmarks rely on social media data and self-reported labels
54
;
6
, which are ill-suited for evaluating DSM-grounded diagnostic judgments or systematic differential diagnosis. To date, no benchmark has been designed to rigorously assess psychiatric diagnostic decision-making in controlled yet clinically grounded settings.

In this work, we introduce
MentalBench
, a benchmark designed to address this gap. At its core is
MentalKG
, a psychiatrist-built and validated knowledge graph that explicitly encodes DSM-5 diagnostic criteria and differential diagnostic rules for 23 psychiatric disorders.
MentalKG
serves as a golden-standard logical backbone, constructed with direct expert involvement rather than automatically extracted from text. Leveraging this structured knowledge, we generate diagnostic scenarios through rule-consistent augmentation and subsequently review them with psychiatrists to ensure clinical plausibility. This design yields synthetic data with low annotation noise and enables fine-grained analysis: model errors can be disentangled into deficiencies in domain knowledge versus failures in applying diagnostic criteria.

Using
MentalKG
as a foundation,
MentalBench
comprises 24,750 synthetic clinical cases that systematically vary along two clinically critical axes: information completeness (from structured medical–chart–like descriptions to incomplete patient self-reports) and diagnostic complexity (from single-disorder cases to challenging differential diagnosis scenarios). These controlled variations allow us to evaluate not only whether a model can identify relevant diagnoses, but also whether it can appropriately determine the number of diagnoses to commit to in ambiguous settings.

Our experimental results reveal a nuanced limitation of current LLMs. While models demonstrate strong performance on structured, single-answer queries that directly probe DSM-5 knowledge, they struggle when faced with clinically similar disorders where the key challenge lies in deciding whether to commit to a single diagnosis or retain multiple plausible alternatives. This difficulty in calibrating diagnostic commitment, rather than a lack of factual knowledge, emerges consistently across model families and remains largely unexamined by existing benchmarks. Our contributions are threefold:

•

We construct
MentalKG
, a psychiatrist-built and validated knowledge graph encoding DSM-5 diagnostic criteria and differential diagnostic rules for 23 psychiatric disorders.

•

We introduce
MentalBench
, a clinically grounded benchmark of 24,750 synthetic diagnostic cases generated from expert-curated logic, enabling low-noise and interpretable evaluation.

•

We provide a systematic analysis showing that current LLMs struggle not with recalling diagnostic knowledge, but with calibrating diagnostic commitment in the presence of clinically overlapping conditions.

LLMs in Mental Health Diagnosis

Recent studies demonstrate that LLMs can identify clinically meaningful patterns in text and suggest potential diagnoses
51

43

These findings highlight the necessity of systematically evaluating LLMs’ psychiatric diagnostic capabilities before their integration into real-world clinical practice.

Benchmarks for Mental Health Diagnosis

Most existing benchmarks for mental health diagnosis rely on social media data

This reliance reflects the inherent difficulty of constructing high-quality psychiatric diagnostic datasets, given strict privacy regulations, the absence of objective biomarkers, and the complexity of formal diagnostic logic. Recent question-answering benchmarks

2.3
Question Answering Benchmarks in Medical Domain

In general medicine, benchmarks such as MedQA
18

Unlike general medical QA, psychiatric diagnosis requires deciding whether to commit to a single diagnosis or retain multiple plausible alternatives.
MentalBench
explicitly models this challenge by incorporating both informational uncertainty and diagnostic complexity, which are not addressed by existing medical benchmarks.

3
MentalKG

Due to the diagnostic complexity resulting from overlapping symptoms among psychiatric disorders, generating clinical cases without guiding psychiatric diagnostic criteria can produce clinically vague cases that lack diagnostic representativeness.
To ensure the clinical reliability of the generated cases, we construct
MentalKG
, a psychiatrist-validated knowledge graph that structurally formalizes DSM-5
2
diagnostic criteria. Our graph decomposes complex clinical diagnostic criteria into four distinct entity types: Symptom, Symptom Group, Disorder, and Differential Diagnosis.

3.1
Graph Components

Symptom Nodes.

MentalKG
comprises 84 symptom nodes (
S
) covering the diagnostic criteria for 23 mental disorders. Each symptom node contains two attributes: a textual description describing the clinical meaning of the symptom, and a set of subtypes representing its specific manifestations.

Symptom Group Nodes.

The DSM-5 often organizes symptoms into clinically relevant groups, such as
depressive
and
manic episodes
. To represent this hierarchy, we introduce 23 symptom group nodes (
G
), which aggregate related symptom nodes.

Disorder Nodes.

MentalKG
contains 23 disorder nodes (
D
) corresponding to specific mental disorders in DSM-5, which we select in consultation with psychiatrists based on two criteria: (1) high clinical prevalence, (2) need for differential diagnosis from other disorders. We map DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for each disorder in
MentalKG
to a structured graph representation, connecting disorder nodes (
D
) directly to symptom group nodes (
G
) to represent the symptoms required for diagnosis (details are in
Figure 1
). For more fine-grained diagnostic requirements, such as minimum symptom thresholds and duration constraints, we encode them as internal attributes of the disorder nodes. Through this process, the natural language descriptions of DSM-5 are effectively transformed into structurally formalized information in the graph.

Differential Diagnosis Nodes.

In clinical practice, patients’ self-reports rarely perfectly match the diagnostic criteria of a single disorder. Challenges often arise when symptoms overlap, or clinical evidence is inconclusive; for instance, a patient presenting with depression who also exhibits manic features requires careful differentiation between
Major Depressive Disorder
and
Bipolar II Disorder
. To address this, we introduce differential diagnosis nodes (
D_{d}
) connecting a primary suspicion (
D_{1}
) to an alternative (
D_{2}
) via a directed edge. This directionality captures scenarios where a patient resembles
D_{1}
, but specific confounding contexts (e.g., ambiguous duration or shared symptoms) necessitate the evaluation of
D_{2}
.

Each node internally encodes the differential diagnostic criteria using the following three attributes: (1)
Triggering Condition
, which specifies additional symptoms that prompt the need for differential diagnosis; (2)
Key Difference
, which represents the core points of divergence between the two disorders; and (3)
Discriminating Rules
, which specify conditional decision rules for determining the final diagnosis. For example, when depressive episodes co-occur with elevated mood, differential diagnosis between MDD and BD becomes necessary. The key difference lies in whether manic symptoms collectively satisfy the criteria for a Manic Episode: if so, BD is diagnosed; otherwise, MDD.

illustrates this example.

3.2
Expert Validation

The entire construction process of
MentalKG
was conducted in collaboration with two experts. Specifically, the psychiatrist validated the following aspects of the graph: (1) the clinical appropriateness of symptom subtypes within symptom nodes, (2) the alignment of the formalized diagnostic criteria with DSM-5 standards, and (3) the clinical validity of the differential diagnosis logic. Through iterative cycles of expert review and refinement, kg can structurally encode the complex diagnostic criteria and differential diagnostic logic of the DSM-5 rather than simply serving as a simple collection of disorder–symptom connections. A detailed validation process is provided in Appendix
A.3
.

Building upon
MentalKG

MentalBench
, a question-answering benchmark consisting of synthetically generated clinical cases that describe specific patients’ psychiatric symptom profiles and their corresponding diagnostic options. Following the generation pipeline illustrated in
Figure 2
, we generate four types of clinical cases across two diagnostic scenarios: (1) Single–Disease Identification (
Medical Chart
,
Patient Self-Report
) and (2) Differential Diagnosis (
Unambiguous
,

4.1
Single–Disease Identification

In this setting, we focus on generating clinical cases for single-disease identification, where each case represents a clinically typical presentation for only a single mental disorder. The following subsections detail the process of generating clinical cases for each type.

4.1.1
Symptom Profile Construction

Patients sharing the same psychiatric diagnosis often exhibit substantial clinical heterogeneity, characterized by diverse symptom combinations and varying durations. To capture this diversity in the generated clinical cases, we construct multiple symptom profiles for each disorder by sampling symptom configurations from
MentalKG
. We consider two clinical scenarios.

Type 1 – Medical Chart.

This type represents a clinical scenario in which a psychiatrist has completed a diagnostic interview with a patient and documented it in the medical chart. Under the assumption that all information necessary for diagnosis has been fully collected, we sample symptom combinations that strictly satisfy all DSM-5 diagnostic requirements, such as meeting the minimum symptom threshold (e.g., at least five required symptoms for MDD). We generate 5 seed profiles per disorder for this type.

Type 2 – Patient Self-Report.

This type reflects a scenario in which patients directly describe their subjective distress to clinicians in their own words. Motivated by the clinical reality that patients tend to focus on chief complaints while omitting secondary symptoms
12
;
26
, we sample incomplete symptom profiles from
MentalKG
that include core symptoms of the target disorder but fall below the DSM-5 diagnostic threshold (e.g., only three symptoms for MDD). In this type, we generate ten seed profiles per disorder.

Option Choices.

For each sampled symptom profile, we assign four diagnostic options. To evaluate whether models can discriminate between clinically similar disorders with a precise understanding of diagnostic criteria, distractors are selected from
MentalKG
as disorders that share overlapping symptoms with the target disorder.

4.1.2
Patient Profile Instantiation

We convert symptom profiles into concrete patient profiles by assigning demographic attributes and specific symptom manifestations. Demographic information is sampled from pre-generated combinations produced using Gemini-2.5-Pro, and one predefined subtype is selected for each symptom node. This process yields five distinct patient profiles per symptom profile, capturing variability in clinical presentation. Further details on the demographic sampling process are provided in Appendix
B.1
.

4.1.3
Clinical Case Generation

The initialized patient profiles are converted into natural language using two styles aligned with the scenario types in Section
4.1.1
. Profiles in the
Medical Chart
use a third-person perspective and clinical terminology to simulate psychiatrists’ notes, whereas profiles in the
Patient Self-Report
use a first-person perspective and colloquial language to reflect patients’ subjective reports. All scenarios are constrained to 150–200 words and instructed to avoid mentioning diagnostic labels. For each patient profile, we generate three scenarios per type using three LLMs—GPT-4.1, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Qwen3-235B—to mitigate model-specific stylistic bias.

We next construct differential diagnosis cases to evaluate models’ ability to distinguish among clinically similar disorders with overlapping symptom profiles. We generate two types of cases:
Ambiguous
, which maintain clinical plausibility for multiple disorders by leveraging shared symptoms, and
Unambiguous
cases, which incorporate explicit discriminative cues that resolve the case to a single diagnosis.

4.2.1
Symptom Profile Construction

We construct symptom profiles by injecting differential diagnosis logic defined in
MentalKG
into the predefined symptom profiles from Section
4.1.1
. This design choice is motivated by the fact that symptom profiles fully satisfying diagnostic criteria for a specific disorder typically do not require differential diagnosis, whereas partially satisfied profiles leave room for multiple potential diagnoses that can be shifted by subtle clinical nuances. We define two differential diagnosis scenarios:

Type 3 – Ambiguous.

These cases represent diagnostically ambiguous situations in which more than one disorder remains clinically plausible.
We construct these cases by injecting the
Triggering Condition
from the target differential diagnosis node, which introduces additional symptoms that allow multiple candidate diagnoses to remain plausible.

Type 4 – Unambiguous.

These cases represent diagnostic scenarios in which multiple disorders are initially plausible, but explicit discriminative cues enable the case to be resolved to a single diagnosis. We construct them by augmenting ambiguous symptom profiles with
Key Differences
and
Discriminating Rules
defined in the corresponding differential diagnosis nodes, yielding two variants per profile that resolve to either the original target diagnosis or the differential diagnosis.

Option Choices.

We include the target and differential diagnoses as the primary options, with two additional distractors selected from less related disorders to emphasize discrimination between the two targets. In
Ambiguous
cases, both target diagnoses are considered correct answers, whereas in
Unambiguous
cases, only one diagnosis is correct, as determined by the applied discriminating rules.

4.2.2
Clinical Case Generation

Differential diagnosis symptom profiles are instantiated into patient profiles using the same procedure as described in Section
4.1.2
.
For each profile, five patient variants are generated. All differential diagnosis cases are generated in the
Patient Self-Report
style using the pipeline described in Section
4.1.3
. Representative examples of these generated cases are illustrated in Figure
3
.

4.3
Data Statistics

We generate 24,750 clinical cases in total, comprising 1,725 for
Medical Chart
, 3,450 for
Patient Self-Report
, 6,525 for
Ambiguous
, and 13,050 for
Unambiguous
cases. Detailed statistics for each generation stage are summarized in

4.4
Expert Validation

All constructed seed profiles are reviewed by two psychiatrists to ensure the clinical reliability of the generated clinical cases. Profiles that insufficiently represented the target disorder or exhibited clinically ambiguous profiles are revised based on the feedback. To further assess the quality of the generated cases, two experts evaluate 220 randomly sampled scenarios across all scenario types using a 5-point Likert scale. The evaluation considers three dimensions:
Linguistic Naturalness
,
Diagnostic Validity
, and
Clinical Realism
.
With high inter-annotator agreement, the mean scores are 4.95, 4.44, and 4.89, respectively, indicating consistently high ratings across all evaluation dimensions. We report detailed results of expert validation in Appendix
B.2
.

Experiments and Analysis

We conduct a comprehensive evaluation of diverse LLMs on
MentalBench
to assess their psychiatric diagnostic capabilities in clinical settings.

5.1
Experimental Setup

We employ closed-source models from the GPT, Gemini, and Claude families, as well as open-source models including Qwen 2.5, Qwen 3, Gemma 3, Llama-3.1, and MentaLLaMA. Notably, MentaLLaMA
52
is a specialized model fine-tuned from Llama-2 for the mental health domain. For Types 1 and 2, models are instructed to select a single answer, whereas for Types 3 and 4, they are directed to choose one or more answers. Details of the model versions, prompts, and settings are in Appendix
D
. We measure accuracy using an exact match metric, recognizing a prediction as correct only if it identifies the complete set of ground-truth answers.

Performance Trends by Scale and Architecture.

Evaluation results show a clear hierarchy: proprietary models generally outperform open-source models, and performance generally improves with model scale, confirming that the benchmark effectively measures fundamental diagnostic capacity. Notably, MentaLLaMA significantly underperforms despite domain specialization, suggesting that its reliance on the older Llama-2 architecture and social media training data does not generalize to rigorous clinical diagnosis.

Scenario analysis highlights critical reasoning gaps. A universal performance drop from

5.4
Error Patterns Analysis

Models Struggle to Calibrate Diagnostic Commitment.

To investigate the reasoning failures driving the performance divergence observed in Table
1
, we conduct a fine-grained error analysis on
Type 3 (Ambiguous)
and
Type 4 (Unambiguous)
scenarios. We categorize errors into three distinct types: (1) Over-diagnosis, where the model predicts supersets of the ground truth (i.e., failing to rule out distractors); (2) Under-diagnosis, where the model predicts subsets (i.e., missing valid comorbidities); and (3) Incorrect, where the model predicts unrelated diagnoses.

Figure 7
illustrates the distribution of these error types, showing a fundamental failure to calibrate diagnostic commitment. Open-source models exhibit excessive commitment in
Type 4

Type 3
, resulting in frequent Under-diagnosis (insufficient commitment) by forcing single-label predictions even when clinical evidence supports multiple conditions. This divergence highlights that current models lack the adaptive reasoning required to achieve this calibration, resulting in oscillation between excessive inclusivity and rigid strictness depending on the model architecture. Analysis using other metrics is also presented in Appendix

6
Conclusion

We introduce
MentalBench
, a benchmark grounded in
MentalKG
for rigorously evaluating psychiatric diagnostic reasoning. While our experiments confirm that LLMs possess robust latent knowledge of DSM-5 criteria, they exhibit critical diagnostic failures when applied to clinical scenarios. We find that models struggle to handle information incompleteness, with performance collapsing when shifting from structured charts to subjective patient narratives. Furthermore, models fail to calibrate diagnostic commitment, oscillating between excessive inclusivity and rigid strictness, and lack the intrinsic constraint awareness to define decision boundaries without explicit prompting. These findings confirm that
MentalBench
poses a substantial diagnostic challenge to current state-of-the-art models. By establishing a rigorous evaluation framework,
MentalBench
advocates for future research to prioritize the adaptive, robust decision-making essential for real-world psychiatry.

Limitations

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations. First, the clinical cases in
MentalBench
are constructed as descriptive narratives rather than interactive dialogues. While real psychiatric diagnosis involves iterative clinician–patient conversations, our benchmark is intentionally designed to evaluate models’ ability to apply clinically grounded diagnostic criteria under controlled and interpretable scenarios. Future work will leverage the constructed knowledge graph to simulate doctor–patient interactions and assess additional diagnostic capabilities such as follow-up question selection.

Second, all clinical cases are generated exclusively in English, limiting generalizability across languages and cultural contexts where psychiatric symptoms may be expressed differently. However, the knowledge graph encodes DSM-5–based diagnostic logic in a language-independent manner, making its structure applicable across diverse linguistic and cultural settings. This allows future extensions to multilingual and culturally diverse benchmarks through prompt-level control.

Finally, our evaluation focuses on models’ final diagnostic outputs and observable error patterns, without directly examining whether their internal reasoning aligns with DSM-5 diagnostic criteria. While this design enables systematic analysis of how models apply diagnostic knowledge at the output level, it does not capture whether their intermediate reasoning processes follow clinically grounded diagnostic logic. In future work, we plan to augment
MentalBench
with explicit, criterion-level diagnostic reasoning, allowing the benchmark to more directly assess models’ diagnostic reasoning behaviors.

Ethical Considerations

MentalBench
includes only clinically grounded synthesized clinical cases and does not contain any real patient data, medical records, or personally identifiable information. While some synthesized cases may describe severe psychiatric symptoms, including suicidal ideation or self-harm, such content is presented as clinically descriptive accounts of symptoms that focus on patients’ reported experiences, without including explicit descriptions of suicidal methods or self-harm behaviors. Additionally, although
MentalBench
is designed to reflect a wide range of clinically realistic diagnostic scenarios, the benchmark does not capture the full complexity of real-world clinical practice, as discussed in the limitations. Consequently, the evaluation results do not guarantee real-world clinical performance and must be utilized under expert guidance.

Scope of
MentalBench

Table 2
presents the complete list of 23 disorders included in our work. To ensure a holistic evaluation of psychopathological reasoning, we selected representative disorders across a wide spectrum of diagnostic categories defined in the DSM-5. Our coverage encompasses Anxiety Disorders (e.g., GAD, Specific Phobia), Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders (e.g., OCD, Body Dysmorphic Disorder), Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders (e.g., PTSD, Adjustment Disorder), Mood Disorders (e.g., MDD, BD I and II), the Schizophrenia Spectrum (e.g., Delusional Disorder, Schizophrenia), Feeding and Eating Disorders (e.g., Anorexia Nervosa, Binge-Eating Disorder), and Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD).

Crucially, our selection criteria prioritize the replication of authentic challenges encountered in real-world clinical settings, specifically addressing the diagnostic ambiguities posed by symptom overlap and subtle phenomenological distinctions. Since precise differentiation among these conditions is a prerequisite for identifying the primary pathology and determining appropriate therapeutic interventions, we deliberately included disorders where accurate diagnosis hinges on fine-grained clinical details.

For instance, we explicitly distinguish between the three presentations of ADHD—Combined, Inattentive, and Hyperactive—to test the model’s ability to identify specific behavioral nuances. Furthermore, we address the complex comorbidities found at the intersection of mood and psychotic symptoms, which represent a significant diagnostic hurdle. To capture this, we include Schizoaffective Disorder (both Bipolar and Depressive types) alongside Mood Disorders with Psychotic Features (BD I with psychotic features and MDD with psychotic features). While these conditions often present with identical symptoms, accurate diagnosis relies on analyzing duration, symptom concurrence, and severity. By including these confounding cases, we rigorously test the LLM’s depth of expert-level psychiatric knowledge.

Regarding the target demographic, our benchmark focuses on adult clinical narratives. Consequently, we generally exclude neurocognitive and neurodevelopmental disorders (e.g., Alzheimer’s Disease) and conditions primarily diagnosed in early childhood (e.g., Conduct Disorder). However, we make a strategic exception for ADHD. Although classified as a neurodevelopmental disorder with childhood onset, ADHD is frequently first diagnosed in adulthood and serves as a critical differential diagnosis for various mood and psychotic disorders. Therefore, its inclusion is essential for a robust evaluation of adult mental health conditions.

A.3
Details of Expert Validation

To ensure the clinical reliability of the constructed
MentalKG
, the entire development process is conducted in collaboration with mental health experts. Specifically, the experts perform a rigorous validation focusing on three key aspects:

1) Clinical Appropriateness of Subtypes within Symptom Nodes

Experts verify whether the phenotypic manifestations (
Subtypes
) listed under each symptom node accurately represented the core clinical concept of that node. During this process, subtypes that are semantically ambiguous or exhibited significant overlap with other nodes are removed or reassigned. For instance,
Hopelessness
(S083), initially categorized as a subtype of the
Depressed Mood
(S024) node, is separated into a distinct symptom node to accurately implement the specific diagnostic criteria for
Persistent Depressive Disorder
.

2) Alignment with DSM-5 Standards

Experts review the structural connections between Symptom and Symptom Group nodes, as well as the logic encoded in node attributes, to ensure they faithfully reflect the formal diagnostic criteria of the DSM-5. During this review, experts manually encoded
Additional Requirements
(e.g., exclusion criteria) into disorder nodes to sharpen the decision boundaries between clinically similar conditions if needed.

3) Clinical Validity of Differential Diagnosis Logic

Since DSM-5 differential diagnosis guidelines are originally presented as unstructured text, we converted them into structured logical rules. Experts evaluate the discriminating rules within differential diagnosis nodes to ensure they appropriately capture real-world patterns of diagnostic confusion and the discrimination logic between paired disorders. Through this iterative review, ambiguous rules are refined or discarded, and specific conditional contexts are added to establish clearer discrimination criteria.

This rigorous validation process ensures that
MentalKG
successfully transforms the complex diagnostic systems and differential diagnoses of the DSM-5 across 23 disorders into a structured format with a high level of clinical fidelity.

Inter-Annotator Agreement Analysis

To assess the reliability of the expert ratings, we report Percent Agreement, Cohen’s
\kappa_{w}
(quadratic weighted;
7
), and Gwet’s AC2
15
. As shown in the table, we observe a discrepancy where high Percent Agreement (e.g., 93.2% for Linguistic Naturalness) is accompanied by low
\kappa_{w}
values (e.g., 0.041). This is well known as the
prevalence paradox
or
kappa paradox

11
, where chance-corrected agreement is artificially underestimated when rating distributions are highly skewed. Cohen’s
\kappa
penalizes agreement when the marginal distribution of ratings is highly skewed. However, in our case, the overwhelming prevalence of high scores (4s and 5s) inflates the probability of chance agreement (
P_{e}
), mathematically suppressing the
\kappa
value despite strong actual consensus between annotators.

To address this limitation, we adopt Gwet’s AC2 as our primary reliability metric, as it is robust to prevalence effects while maintaining chance correction
15
. The AC2 scores indicate substantial reliability (ranging from 0.499 to 0.691), supporting the conclusion that the high ratings reflect genuine consensus on the dataset’s high quality rather than random chance.

Types 3 and 4
), we employ Micro-average Precision, Recall, and F1-score.

Let
\mathcal{O}
denote the set of options for a given problem, and
N
be the total number of data samples. For the
i
-th sample, we define the set of ground-truth answers as
Y_{i}
and the set of model predictions as
\hat{Y}_{i}
. Unlike macro-averaging, which calculates metrics per class before averaging, the micro-average approach first aggregates True Positives (
TP
), False Positives (
FP
), and False Negatives (
FN
) across the entire dataset, and then calculates Precision, Recall, and F1-score based on these global counts.

The global
TP
,
FP
, and
FN
are defined as follows, where
\mathbb{I}(\cdot)
is the indicator function that returns 1 if the condition is true and 0 otherwise:

\displaystyle TP

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\mathbb{I}(o\in Y_{i}\land o\in\hat{Y}_{i})

(1)

\displaystyle FP

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\mathbb{I}(o\notin Y_{i}\land o\in\hat{Y}_{i})

\displaystyle FN

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\mathbb{I}(o\in Y_{i}\land o\notin\hat{Y}_{i})

Based on these aggregated values, Micro-average Precision (
P_{micro}
), Recall (
R_{micro}
), and F1-score (
F1_{micro}
) are calculated as:

\displaystyle P_{micro}

\displaystyle=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}

(2)

\displaystyle R_{micro}

\displaystyle=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}

\displaystyle F1_{micro}

\displaystyle=2\times\frac{P_{micro}\times R_{micro}}{P_{micro}+R_{micro}}

Overall, these findings reveal a calibration failure in diagnostic strictness. The persistent pattern of high recall but low precision shows that models favor inclusive prediction strategies that ensure the correct diagnosis is selected, yet fail to reliably exclude clinically similar distractors. This imbalance reflects an inability to internally regulate the trade-off between inclusivity and exclusion, leading to systematic over-diagnosis in unambiguous cases and under-diagnosis in ambiguous ones. As a result, current LLMs struggle to infer appropriate diagnostic cardinality from evidence alone, limiting their reliability in real-world clinical settings.
