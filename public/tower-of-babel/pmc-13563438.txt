Accelerating natural product discovery, characterization and engineering by biofoundries
Authors: Ling Hu, Yuwei Pan, Huimin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Covering: From early developments to the presentNatural product (NP) discovery is increasingly constrained by low-throughput screening, repeated rediscovery, and challenges in scaling genome mining-guided validation workflows. This highlight examines how automated biofoundries are accelerating NP discovery, characterization, and engineering through integrated design–build–test–learn (DBTL) cycles. We discuss recent advances in phenotype-first and genome-first discovery strategies enabled by robotics, high-throughput pathway reconstitution, and automated screening platforms. We further highlight emerging technologies, including cell-free biosynthesis, automated culturomics, programmable chassis engineering, and AI-assisted workflow orchestration, that may enable increasingly autonomous biofoundries for scalable exploration of NP chemical space and therapeutic discovery.

Introduction

Natural products (NPs) have long served as an indispensable source of pharmaceutical agents, lead compounds, sustainable agriculture, biomaterials, and industrial biotechnology.1 Traditional NP discovery strategies, primarily based on the cultivation of microorganisms and activity-guided isolation of abundant metabolites, have enabled the discovery of numerous clinically important molecules such as penicillin, lovastatin, and cyclosporine.1 More recently, advances in genome sequencing and bioinformatics have transformed the field by enabling the identification of cryptic biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs), substantially expanding the accessible biosynthetic landscape encoded in microbial genomes.2,3 Despite these advances, major bottlenecks remain in both cultivation-based and genome-guided NP discovery workflows. Conventional screening frequently suffers from the repeated rediscovery of known compounds and often requires labor-intensive purification and characterization. Meanwhile, even though genome-mining approaches can computationally predict thousands of candidate BGCs, experimental validation, heterologous expression, and functional characterization remain difficult to scale.

These challenges highlight the growing need for automated, scalable, and high-throughput platforms capable of accelerating NP discovery and characterization. In recent years, there has been increasing interest in integrating NP research with biological foundries (biofoundries). Biofoundries are highly automated and robotic facilities that enable rapid and efficient design–build–test–learn (DBTL) cycles for the engineering of biological systems and molecules for research and biotechnology applications.4,5 In 2019, the Global Biofoundries Alliance (GBA) was established to coordinate activities across biofoundries worldwide (https://www.biofoundries.org/), and currently comprises more than 40 members.6 Biofoundries have already demonstrated substantial impact in DNA assembly,7 protein engineering,8 synthetic biology,9 and antibody development.10 By contrast, the integration of NP research into biofoundries remains relatively underdeveloped despite being particularly well-suited for automation because of its reliance on large-scale DNA assembly, strain engineering, fermentation, and screening workflows.

Recent studies demonstrate that biofoundries can substantially accelerate multiple stages of NP research, including pathway reconstruction, high-throughput heterologous expression, phenotypic screening, and pathway engineering. In parallel, scalable automation enables the generation of increasingly large and standardized datasets that may support future machine learning (ML)-guided optimization and iterative DBTL cycles. In this Highlight, we summarize recent advances in biofoundry-enabled NP discovery, characterization, and engineering, with an emphasis on strategies implemented across the DBTL cycle, as shown in Fig. 1. We further discuss emerging opportunities, current challenges, and future directions for integrating NP research with biofoundry technologies.

Automation strategies

To date, only a limited number of studies have integrated NP discovery into highly automated biofoundry workflows. Current biofoundry implementations have primarily adapted two established NP discovery paradigms: phenotype-first discovery and genome-first discovery. In phenotype-first approaches, high-throughput screening directly links biological activity to genetic determinants. In genome-first approaches, candidate BGCs are computationally prioritized, reconstructed, and expressed in heterologous hosts to systematically access cryptic biosynthetic diversity. More recently, biofoundry workflows have also expanded toward scalable NP engineering and diversification through automated mutagenesis and combinatorial pathway design. Representative examples are summarized in Fig. 2 and Table 1, and their respective strengths and limitations are discussed below.

Phenotype-first discovery in automated workflows

In phenotype-first strategies, high-throughput screening is used to identify strains displaying a desired phenotype, followed by elucidation of the underlying genetic basis. Traditional workflows are limited by manual colony handling, inconsistent assay setup, and low throughput. Biofoundries overcome these bottlenecks by integrating robotics and automation into screening pipelines. Moffat et al. automated a transposon screen of Pseudomonas sp. Ps652ΔHCN, a potato-field isolate, to identify genes required for inhibiting the plant pathogen Streptomyces scabies11 (Fig. 2A). The workflow integrated automated identification and selection of ∼2880 Pseudomonas mutant colonies, robotic inoculation, and automated imaging analysis. Using an automated robotic platform, the authors dispensed non-pathogenic proxy S. coelicolor M145 spores into 120 individual 24-well plates, an approach only practicable through automation, and then robotically arrayed all mutants for antagonism assays. This setup eliminated the need for a traditional 96-solid pin replicator, which proved unsuitable because the large inhibition zone produced by neighboring colonies obscured mutants defective in metabolite production.

The automated assay ran in parallel across all mutants in about 2 weeks. Automated imaging flagged mutants that lost inhibitory activity, and sequencing of those hits linked the phenotype to a cryptic BGC for 7-hydroxytropolone, a BGC not readily predictable from genomic data12 (Table 1). Notably, preparing the 120 plates took only ∼10 hours of robot time, illustrating how automation can increase screening throughput by an order of magnitude while substantially reducing manual labor. Such standardized, robot-controlled assays also reduce human error and bias in colony picking and visual screening, and generate large, consistent datasets that could be mined by ML methods in future optimization cycles.

Notably, phenotype-first workflows do not directly identify the bioactive compound or its mechanism of action. Once a genetic hit is identified, conventional downstream workflows, including metabolite isolation, structural elucidation, and mechanistic characterization, are still required. In addition, designing robust automated phenotypic assays for more complex traits or non-model microbes is also difficult. Overall, the Moffat et al.11 study illustrates how biofoundry can dramatically scale up phenotype-driven NP discovery, while highlighting the need for broader assay standardization and integration of downstream analytics in future discovery pipelines.

Genome-first discovery and characterization enabled by biofoundries

Genome-first approaches begin with computational identification of candidate BGCs, followed by heterologous expression and metabolite characterization. Compared with phenotype-first approaches, genome-first strategies systematically access cryptic biosynthetic diversity and are particularly compatible with scalable automation due to their modular and highly parallelizable workflows.

In targeted discovery workflows, biofoundries are often used to systematically explore specific NP families with predictable biosynthetic logic. Yuan et al. established an automated high-throughput workflow for fungal terpenoid discovery using Aspergillus oryzae as a heterologous host13 (Fig. 2B). Terpenoids represent the largest class of natural products and constitute an important source of therapeutics, while fungi remain a rich reservoir of terpenoid biosynthetic diversity.14 In this workflow, 39 terpenoid BGCs were identified computationally using antiSMASH15 and subsequently refactored for heterologous expression in A. oryzae. Automated DNA assembly generated 208 engineered strains, as several clusters required multiple constructs. Fermentation followed by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis detected 185 unique terpenoid metabolites, and subsequent anti-inflammatory screening identified mangicol J, a bioactive sesterterpenoid (Table 1).

A second example is FAST-RiPPs, developed by Ayikpoe et al., which established a fast, automated, scalable, high-throughput platform for the discovery of ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified peptides (RiPPs)16 (Fig. 2B). RiPPs constitute a highly diverse NP family in which mature products are generated through post-translational modification of genetically encoded precursor peptides.17 RiPPs are particularly well-suited for genome-first biofoundry workflows because their substrates (e.g., precursor peptides) are directly encoded within BGCs and their biosynthetic transformations are often relatively predictable.17 The FAST-RiPPs platform integrated the genome-mining tool Rapid ORF Description and Evaluation Online (RODEO)18 with the Illinois Biological Foundry for Advanced Biomanufacturing (iBioFAB)19 to automate pathway refactoring and heterologous expression in Escherichia coli. The authors compiled 96 RiPP BGCs spanning diverse bacterial phyla and encompassing 383 biosynthetic genes. Using a modular “plug-and-play” strategy,20 approximately 86% of the clusters were successfully refactored and expressed in parallel, leading to the detection of 30 RiPP products across six classes, including lanthipeptides, lasso peptides, graspetides, glycocins, linear azol(in)e-containing peptides, and thioamitides. Several compounds displayed antimicrobial activity, including a class II lanthipeptide active against Klebsiella species at low micromolar concentrations (Table 1).

Genome-first workflows can also incorporate bioactivity-guided prioritization to improve the likelihood of identifying functional metabolites. One effective strategy is the use of self-resistance genes as indicators of potentially bioactive BGCs.21 Yuan et al. expanded this concept through FAST-NPS, a platform for the discovery of bioactive natural products from Streptomyces22 (Fig. 2C). FAST-NPS combined the Antibiotic-Resistant Target Seeker (ARTS)23 tool for identifying BGCs containing putative self-resistance genes with iBioFAB-enabled automated direct cloning, conjugation, and heterologous expression in Streptomyces lividans. Using antiSMASH15 and ARTS,23 the authors screened 11 Streptomyces genomes and identified more than 100 candidate BGCs containing putative resistance determinants. Through automated Cas12a-assisted precise targeted cloning (CAPTURE),24 105 prioritized BGCs ranging from 10–100 kb were directly captured from genomic DNA with an approximately 95% cloning success rate. Each cluster was subsequently introduced into S. lividans and analyzed by LC-MS. 23 metabolites were detected from 12 BGCs, and downstream bioassays demonstrated that five clusters produced compounds with antibacterial or antitumor activities, corresponding to eight bioactive metabolites in total (Table 1).

Collectively, these studies illustrate the major strengths of genome-first biofoundry workflows, including scalable BGC refactoring or direct cloning, parallel strain construction, and systematic exploration of cryptic biosynthetic diversity. Such workflows can also incorporate computation-guided prioritization to improve the likelihood of identifying functional metabolites. Nevertheless, several bottlenecks remain common across current platforms. First, metabolite characterization remains substantially lower throughput than upstream pathway construction and screening. Although biofoundries can rapidly generate large numbers of engineered strains, downstream purification, structure elucidation, and mechanistic studies remain labor-intensive. Recent advances in downstream analytics may help alleviate this bottleneck, including MS/MS-based molecular networking and automated dereplication workflows that prioritize fractions and reduce rediscovery,25 NMR/MS-based fraction-screening and computational NMR tools that support semiautomated structure annotation,26 and microcrystal electron diffraction (MicroED)-based approaches that enable structure determination from minute quantities of material.27 Nevertheless, integrating these technologies into scalable and closed-loop biofoundry workflows remains a significant challenge. Furthermore, heterologous expression success is often limited by host compatibility, missing cofactors, pathway regulation, or incomplete biosynthetic reconstruction. Addressing these limitations will require continued advances in analytical automation together with expanded chassis engineering.

NP pathway engineering and diversification

Beyond accelerating NP discovery, biofoundries also provide scalable platforms for NP engineering and diversification. Pathway engineering has emerged as an attractive strategy for NP diversification due to the modular architectures of many NP classes, especially RiPPs and polyketides. Compared with traditional chemical derivatization, biosynthetic engineering can access broader and more selective modifications through programmable manipulation of biosynthetic enzymes and pathways.17 These modular and programmable biosynthetic systems are highly compatible with biofoundry principles, enabling iterative DBTL cycles for high-throughput generation and screening of NP analogues.

Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of robotic systems for scalable NP engineering. Guo et al. developed a robotic workflow for the construction and screening of lanthipeptide variant libraries in E. coli, generating and evaluating more than 1700 lanthipeptide variants through automated mutagenesis and bioactivity screening28 (Table 1). This platform enabled rapid identification of variants with improved antimicrobial activity and demonstrated how automation can facilitate systematic exploration of NP sequence–function relationships. Such workflows are particularly valuable for RiPPs, where large combinatorial sequence spaces can be difficult to experimentally evaluate using traditional approaches. In addition, high-throughput DNA assembly platforms have also been developed to support scalable engineering workflows. For example, an integrated robotic plasmid construction platform was recently applied to the high-throughput assembly of combinatorial polyketide synthase part libraries, highlighting the potential of automated DNA synthesis infrastructures for NP pathway diversification.29 Collectively, these studies illustrate how automation and DBTL-compatible workflows may transform NP research from discovery-oriented pipelines into programmable engineering platforms.

Despite the broad applications of biofoundries in NP discovery, characterization, and engineering, current workflows remain only partially integrated, with limited feedback between experimental outputs and subsequent redesign cycles, highlighting the need for broader integration of interoperable experimental and computational methodologies.

Emerging opportunities

Although biofoundries have enabled increasingly scalable platforms for NP discovery and engineering, most systems remain fragmented and are not yet fully iterative or closed-loop. Limitations arise not only from the difficulty of adapting traditionally labor-intensive experimental workflows to high-throughput automation but also from the lack of integrated computational frameworks that can coordinate iterative DBTL cycles. In this section, we discuss enabling experimental and computational strategies that may help expand, integrate, and progressively close NP biofoundry workflows.

Experimental platforms to expand biofoundry workflows for NP research

Cell-free systems (CFS) are emerging as promising platforms for biofoundries because they reduce dependence on cellular hosts and are inherently compatible with automation, miniaturization, and high-throughput liquid handling. Traditional cellular expression workflows often slow iterative pathway engineering due to host incompatibility and complex cellular regulation. Recent studies have demonstrated that CFS can support rapid enzyme and pathway prototyping for NP discovery and biosynthesis, as well as flexible reaction optimization for NP diversification.30,31 For example, Liu et al. previously developed a unified biocatalysis (UniBioCat) system32 using an E. coli-based cell-free expression platform to co-express the precursor peptides and modification enzymes for rapid biosynthesis and engineering of RiPPs. Using UniBioCat, they successfully reconstituted the biosynthetic pathway for de novo salivaricin B production, generated single and double salivaricin B variants, and screened these mutants for enhanced bioactivity. The platform was further applied to the synthesis and bioactivity screening of 12 previously uncharacterized lanthipeptides, demonstrating the scalability and generalizability of CFS for rapid NP prototyping and functional screening. Although the predictable substrate and biosynthetic logic of RiPP BGCs make them particularly well-suited for cell-free biosynthesis (CFB), similar approaches have also been extended to nonribosomal peptide33 and polyketide34 systems, demonstrating that CFB is potentially generalizable for NP prototyping. In addition to widely used E. coli-based platforms, Streptomyces-derived CFS may provide improved compatibility with high-GC biosynthetic genes and actinomycete-specific expression machinery, offering a complementary option for prototyping NP pathways from Streptomyces.35 Beyond their utility for NP pathway prototyping, CFS are also intrinsically compatible with biofoundry workflows due to their standardized, liquid-handling-compatible and miniaturizable nature, enabling facile integration with automated liquid handling and high-throughput screening platforms.36 Together, CFS are promising platforms for rapid NP prototyping and scalable functional screening and accelerate the build/test steps within biofoundries.

Automated culturomics represents another emerging direction for expanding accessible microbial diversity.37 Although heterologous expression systems dominate current biofoundry applications owing to their programmability and scalability,38 native producers remain highly valuable because they preserve endogenous regulatory networks, self-resistance mechanisms, specialized precursor supplies, and cofactors that are often required for proper biosynthetic enzyme activity and metabolite production. These native contexts are often critical for accessing cryptic or chemically unique metabolites that cannot be readily reproduced in heterologous hosts.39 However, many environmentally derived or non-model microorganisms are slow-growing, genetically difficult to manipulate, or require highly tailored cultivation conditions, limiting their integration into systematic NP discovery pipelines.39 Automated culturomics has therefore emerged as a promising strategy to expand the searchable microbial and chemical space available for NP discovery while improving compatibility with biofoundry workflows. Huang et al. previously reported a ML-guided robotic culturomics platform that integrates an artificial intelligence (AI)-guided colony selection algorithm for increased taxonomic diversity with an automated colony-picking robot for high-throughput generation of isolates.40 Using this platform, 20 personalized isolate biobanks were generated, yielding a total of 26 997 isolates and 1197 high-quality draft genomes. This study enabled an automated, programmable framework that broadens the scope of environmental microbes that can be cultivated and screened. Overall, automated culturomics enables high-throughput, automated strain isolation and cultivation, laying the foundation for integrating culture-based NP discovery into biofoundries.

In parallel, advances in automated chassis engineering are improving the scalability of pathway optimization in non-model organisms. Zuo et al. described a robotic genetic manipulation process for rapid chassis engineering of the non-model organism Pichia pastoris.41 The authors demonstrated automated optimization of transformation conditions and high-throughput genome engineering in P. pastoris, achieving single-site and two-site genome-editing efficiencies of up to 94.6% and 36.3%, respectively. They further characterized 96 endogenous promoters and engineered P. pastoris to produce terpenoids α-santalene and α-santalol. This work highlights a scalable, programmable chassis engineering and standardized strain construction system that can support large-scale pathway manipulation and iterative DBTL optimization in alternative hosts.

Collectively, advances in cell-free systems, automated culturomics, and programmable chassis engineering may facilitate integration of diverse NP discovery and prototyping strategies, enabling more interconnected and iterative DBTL cycles within future biofoundries for NP research.

Integration of computational tools into DBTL cycles and AI-driven biofoundries

Despite advances in experimental technologies, integration of computational and AI-assisted frameworks will be essential for establishing fully closed-loop biofoundries for NP research. Numerous computational tools have been developed for specialized tasks across NP discovery workflows,42 including genome mining and BGC analysis (e.g., antiSMASH, BiG-SCAPE, DeepBGC, and BGC-Prophet),15,42 metabolite dereplication and structural characterization from MS or NMR data (e.g., CSI:FingerID, Spec2Vec, SMART, and DP4+),43 computational bioactivity prediction (e.g., discovery of halicin and abaucin),42 and computer-aided design for strain and pathway engineering across different microbial hosts (e.g. Galaxy-SynBioCAD and StreptoCAD).44,45 While these platforms have substantially accelerated individual stages of NP research, many current computational tools remain focused primarily on design-phase tasks with limited integration of downstream experimental feedback into iterative optimization and redesign processes. In particular, experimental outputs from genome-first discovery, phenotype-first screening, and NP engineering are often analyzed independently rather than reintegrated into adaptive learn and design phases. As a result, the learn phase remains comparatively underdeveloped in many current NP workflows, limiting the realization of fully closed-loop biofoundry systems.

Early biofoundry studies demonstrated how computational tools could be integrated with automated experimentation to support iterative DBTL cycles. Carbonell et al. demonstrated how an automated DBTL pipeline can accelerate microbial strain optimization for fine chemical production.46 Experimental data generated during the test stage were analyzed in the learn stage using predictive statistical methods to guide the next round of design. After two DBTL cycles, microbial production of the flavonoid (2S)-pinocembrin was improved by approximately 500-fold. The pipeline was subsequently extended to optimize an alkaloid biosynthetic pathway, illustrating its broader potential for rapidly engineering microbial production of valuable compounds.46 Complementing these statistically guided DBTL workflows, HamediRad et al. developed BioAutomata, the first AI-powered biofoundry that integrated machine learning with robotic experimentation to achieve autonomous optimization of a lycopene biosynthetic pathway.47 By using Bayesian optimization to iteratively design experiments executed by iBioFAB, BioAutomata explored less than 1% of the design space while outperformed random screening by 77%, providing the earliest demonstration of AI-driven closed-loop optimization in a biofoundry. More recently, generalized self-driving laboratories have further extended these concepts toward increasingly adaptive and autonomous DBTL workflows. Unlike protein engineering or metabolic engineering, where DBTL cycles are often guided by well-defined optimization objectives such as enzymatic activity or product titer, NP discovery frequently begins without a clearly defined target molecule or structure–activity relationship, making adaptive learning and iterative redesign substantially more challenging. Nevertheless, recent advances in self-driving laboratories have further demonstrated how automation, AI-guided experimental redesign, and active learning strategies may support iterative optimization and experimental design.8,48 Singh et al. recently established a generalized autonomous enzyme engineering platform that achieved a 90-fold improvement in substrate preference and a 16-fold improvement in enzymatic activity of the Arabidopsis thaliana halide methyltransferase through four iterative engineering cycles over 4 weeks.8,48 In this study, assay data generated during each cycle were used to train a low-N ML model that guided variant selection in subsequent rounds, thereby establishing a closed-loop DBTL workflow. Although these approaches have primarily been applied to pathway optimization, protein engineering and metabolic engineering systems, they can be adapted for NP discovery, characterization, and engineering. One potential strategy toward iterative NP DBTL cycles may involve closer integration of genome-first discovery, phenotype-first screening, and NP diversification platforms, allowing experimental outputs from one pipeline to iteratively inform subsequent design decisions in another.

Current development of AI-assisted tools for NP research is largely hindered by the limited availability of large, high-quality, standardized, integrated, and interoperable datasets.42 In contrast to protein engineering and metabolic engineering, NP discovery involves heterogeneous data types, including genomics, metabolomics, bioactivity screening, and pathway engineering outputs, which complicates iterative learning and model development. Consequently, the scalable and high-throughput nature of biofoundries can help address these limitations by enabling standardized data generation and facilitating integration of traditionally labor-intensive NP pipelines. Such datasets may accelerate the development of ML-assisted tools, which could in turn further enhance automation and iterative DBTL cycles. In addition, the diverse strategies used in NP discovery and diversification have resulted in many task-specific computational tools with limited interoperability across workflows. Future integration of these currently isolated platforms may require unified data architectures and AI-assisted orchestration frameworks capable of coordinating information flow and experimental feedback across multiple DBTL steps.

Beyond data analysis and experimental prioritization, closing the DBTL loop will also require computational frameworks that can translate learned outcomes into testable hypotheses and executable experimental workflows. Emerging scientific AI agents and automated protocol-generation tools may help connect experimental interpretation with laboratory execution by proposing follow-up experiments and converting selected designs into machine-readable instructions for robotic platforms.49,50 Although such systems remain at an early stage and have not yet been broadly demonstrated for NP research, they represent an important interface between the learn phase and subsequent design–build–test iterations.

Conclusions and perspectives

NPs have long served as an important source of pharmaceuticals, sustainable agriculture, biomaterials, and industrial biotechnology. From early phenotype-driven discovery to current high-throughput and increasingly automated engineering platforms, NP discovery has continuously evolved alongside advances in biological knowledge and experimental technologies. Recent integration of NP workflows into biofoundries has enabled scalable applications in direct cloning, strain engineering, pathway refactoring, phenotypic screening, and NP diversification. Nevertheless, as illustrated in Fig. 3, current biofoundries for NP research remain only partially integrated, with many processes still fragmented and reliant on substantial human intervention between DBTL steps. Accordingly, most existing systems remain within level 2 (partial automation, where multiple experimental steps or sub-workflows are automated but humans remain responsible for monitoring and downstream processes) and level 3 (conditional automation, where most workflow elements are automated and software-integrated, but human intervention is still required for selected tasks and coordination between modules) of the automation framework proposed by Stephenson et al.,51 highlighting the remaining gap between current biofoundries and fully autonomous NP discovery platforms.

Evolution of NP research from manual operations to automated biofoundries to autonomous biofoundries. Traditional NP discovery workflows rely on largely manual and fragmented strategies including phenotype-guided screening, genome mining of natural sources, and pathway engineering for NP diversification. Recent advances in biofoundries have enabled increasingly automated and scalable workflows integrating high-throughput experimentation, synthetic biology, and computational tools. Future autonomous biofoundries may further integrate AI-driven workflow generation and orchestration, active learning, multi-omics analysis, biosynthetic pathway elucidation and redesign, and automated DBTL cycles to accelerate NP discovery, characterization, and diversification with minimal human intervention. Automation levels are adapted from the framework proposed by Stephenson et al. and broadly indicate the approximate degree of automation represented by each stage.51

To move toward autonomous NP discovery platforms, advances in experimental and computational technologies must be accompanied by improved integration and orchestration across DBTL cycles. Community efforts toward FAIR (findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable)-compliant data will be important for connecting genomic, metabolomic, chemical, and bioactivity datasets and enabling more interoperable NP discovery pipelines.52 At the infrastructure level, digital twins and cloud-connected biofoundry architectures represent promising future directions for integrating distributed instrumentation, standardized data streams, and AI-driven decision-making into unified autonomous research environments.53 Although fully autonomous biofoundries with minimal human intervention for NP research are unlikely to be realized in the near future, we envision that continued advances in automation, standardized data generation, and AI-assisted workflow generation and orchestration will progressively transform current fragmented NP workflows into increasingly integrated and adaptive discovery platforms. Ultimately, future autonomous biofoundries for NP research may be capable of iteratively coordinating NP mining, characterization, engineering, and diversification within scalable closed-loop DBTL cycles, thereby accelerating the expansion of NP chemical space and therapeutic discovery.
