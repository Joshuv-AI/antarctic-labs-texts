Comparative evaluation of calcined paper mill sludge, montmorillonite, and their composite adsorbents for mature landfill leachate treatment
Authors: Elham Falahati Naghibi, Mohammad Shirmardi, Abdoliman Amouei, Seyed Mahmoud Mehdinia, Neda Ahmadzadeh Tori, Hajar Tabarinia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mature landfill leachate is difficult to treat because refractory dissolved organic matter produces high chemical oxygen demand (COD), intense color, and poor biodegradability. This study compared calcined paper mill sludge (PMS-A), montmorillonite (Mt), and a novel calcined PMS–Mt composite for simultaneous COD and color removal from mature landfill leachate. Adsorbents were characterized by SEM, EDAX, FTIR, nitrogen sorption, and point-of-zero-charge analysis, and evaluated in batch experiments. At the optimized conditions (pH ≈ 7, 80 g L⁻¹ adsorbent, 90 min, and 250 rpm), PMS-A achieved the highest color and COD removals (98% and 92%), followed by PMS–Mt composite (96% and 76%) and Mt (50% and 60%). Although Mt had the greatest specific surface area, its lower performance showed that surface chemistry and site accessibility were more influential than surface area alone. The pseudo-second-order and Elovich models provided the best fit to the kinetic data. COD adsorption was generally better described by the Freundlich isotherm, whereas color isotherm behavior differed among the adsorbents. These empirical fits were not interpreted as proof of a specific adsorption mechanism. The required dosage of 80 g L⁻¹ and energy-intensive calcination remain important scale-up constraints. Overall, PMS-A showed the strongest technical performance, supporting further work on dosage reduction, regeneration, continuous-flow operation, and techno-economic assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-67356-6.

Introduction

Municipal solid waste generation continues to increase worldwide as a result of urbanization, population growth, and changing consumption patterns1. Landfill leachate, produced when water percolates through deposited waste, is one of the most problematic by-products of solid waste disposal because it contains a complex mixture of dissolved organic matter, ammonia nitrogen, salts, metals, and emerging contaminants2–5. The composition of leachate varies with landfill age, waste composition, hydrology, and operational conditions; however, mature landfill leachate is typically characterized by low biodegradability, high color, and a high fraction of refractory organic matter4–7. In this context, COD and color are not single-contaminant indicators comparable to individual dyes or metal ions4,8. Instead, they represent the cumulative contribution of heterogeneous dissolved organic constituents, particularly humic- and fulvic-like substances with aromatic structures, high molecular weight, and negatively charged functional groups2,7,9. Their simultaneous removal is therefore important because these constituents contribute to oxygen demand, aesthetic pollution, reduced biodegradability, and interference with downstream treatment processes4,6. A wide range of treatment technologies has been investigated for landfill leachate, including biological treatment, coagulation–flocculation, advanced oxidation processes, electrochemical oxidation, membrane filtration, and adsorption2,6,10–15. Biological processes are generally more suitable for young leachate with high biodegradable organic content, whereas mature leachate often requires physicochemical or hybrid treatment due to the persistence of refractory dissolved organic matter6,10. Although advanced oxidation and membrane-based systems can achieve high removal efficiencies, their practical application may be constrained by chemical and energy demand, sludge production, membrane fouling, and operational cost11–15. Adsorption remains an attractive option as a pretreatment or polishing step because of its operational simplicity, process flexibility, and ability to remove diverse organic and inorganic constituents depending on adsorbent properties and wastewater chemistry3,16. The performance of an adsorbent depends on multiple physicochemical factors, including surface charge, pore structure, mineral composition, surface functional groups, ion-exchange capacity, and the accessibility of active sites16,17. Commercial activated carbon is widely used because of its high porosity and adsorption capacity, but its cost and dependence on virgin carbon precursors have encouraged the development of low-cost adsorbents derived from agricultural, mineral, and industrial wastes18–22. In leachate treatment, waste-derived adsorbents such as rice husk, bagasse, fly ash, red mud, cow dung ash, sewage sludge, steel slag, and paper waste sludge have been evaluated with varying degrees of success4,18–22. These materials offer a resource-recovery pathway, but their performance must be interpreted in relation to leachate complexity, required adsorbent dose, regeneration potential, and scalability rather than removal efficiency alone19,21,23. Paper mill sludge (PMS) is generated in large quantities by the pulp and paper industry and creates disposal challenges because of its high moisture content, variable organic/mineral composition, and large production volume23,24. Converting PMS into adsorbent materials has been proposed as a waste valorization strategy25–30. Thermal treatment of PMS can produce Ca-rich mineral phases and alkaline surface sites, which may promote the uptake of negatively charged organic constituents through electrostatic attraction, surface complexation, precipitation-like interactions, or pore-associated retention, depending on the composition of the adsorbate and solution chemistry27,29,31. In contrast, Mt is a layered aluminosilicate clay with cation-exchange capacity, surface hydroxyl groups, and tunable surface chemistry, making it useful for the adsorption of a broad range of aqueous contaminants32,33. Combining calcined PMS with Mt is therefore scientifically reasonable because PMS may contribute Ca-rich alkaline sites, while Mt may increase surface heterogeneity and provide additional hydroxyl- and aluminosilicate-based interaction sites27,32,34. Nevertheless, whether this combination improves COD and color removal from real mature landfill leachate compared with calcined PMS alone has not been systematically assessed. Previous studies have investigated paper mill sludge-derived adsorbents, clay-based adsorbents, and composite materials for wastewater and landfill leachate treatment. However, most studies have focused on individual adsorbent classes, synthetic wastewater or leachate, ammoniacal nitrogen removal, or activated carbon-based composites rather than real mature landfill leachate3,4,35–46. A detailed comparison of selected adsorbents reported for landfill leachate treatment is provided in Table S1 (Supplementary Information). To the best of our knowledge, a direct comparison of calcined PMS, Mt, and a calcined PMS–Mt composite for simultaneous COD and color removal from real mature landfill leachate has not been reported. Therefore, this study aimed to: (i) synthesize a calcined PMS–Mt composite and compare its performance with PMS-A and Mt; (ii) characterize the adsorbents using SEM, EDAX, FTIR, BET, and pHₚzc analyses; (iii) optimize key batch adsorption parameters, including pH, adsorbent dose, contact time, and stirring speed; and (iv) evaluate adsorption kinetics and equilibrium behavior using nonlinear model fitting. The study was designed not only to identify the most effective material among the tested adsorbents, but also to clarify whether Mt incorporation provides measurable performance advantages over calcined PMS alone.

Materials and methods

Leachate sampling

The leachate used in this study was collected in August 2024 from the former municipal waste disposal site, which included a composting facility, in the Anjilsi forest area, Babol County, Iran. This site, covering 29 hectares with a 30% slope, served as a disposal area for household and industrial waste for over 23 years, until its closure in the past two years. The site received approximately 300 tons of waste per day, of which only 70 tons were converted into compost. Although waste disposal activities have ceased, leachate production continues at the site (Table S2 in the Supplementary Information). Raw leachate was collected in 20-liter high-density polyethylene (HDPE) bottles and transported immediately to the Water and Wastewater Chemistry Laboratory at Babol University of Medical Sciences. No pretreatment (e.g., filtration or centrifugation) was applied before adsorption experiments. Samples were stored at 4 ± 1 °C, during which coarse suspended solids settled naturally, and the supernatant was used for subsequent experiments. The BOD₅/COD ratio was determined as an indicator of leachate biodegradability and to assess the applicability of the selected treatment process4,35.

Preparation of Mt

The Mt used in this study (Fig. S1 in the Supplementary Information) was procured from Sigma-Aldrich (catalog no. 281522, grade K10), and its physicochemical properties are summarized in Table S3 (in the Supplementary Information). The material was utilized directly in adsorption experiments without any pre-treatment or modification.

Synthesis of adsorbents

The PMS was washed with distilled water three times to remove soluble impurities, then dried in an oven at 100 °C for 48 h. The dried PMS was ground and sieved to obtain particles smaller than mesh size 80 (177 μm) (Fig. S2 in the Supplementary Information). Two types of precursors were prepared:

Two mass ratios of PMS to Mt (1:1 and 2:1) were tested in preliminary experiments. The 2:1 ratio showed higher removal efficiencies for both color and COD and was therefore selected for further studies. Optimization of other ratios was beyond the scope of this work.

To create an oxygen-free atmosphere, each precursor powder was placed inside a sealable cast-iron/steel reactor. The reactor was then purged with nitrogen gas through a small port to displace the internal air. Immediately after purging, the port was sealed, and the closed reactor was transferred to a muffle furnace. The sealed reactor was heated from room temperature to 800 °C at a rate of 20 °C min⁻¹, held at 800 °C for 2 h, and then cooled naturally to room temperature. The resulting materials were designated PMS-A and the composite and stored in airtight glass containers until use27,28,31,40. Figure 1. shows the three types of adsorbents used in this study.

Characterization of adsorbents

The morphology and surface structure of PMS-A, Mt, and the composite were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, VEGA3, TESCAN, Czech Republic). Elemental composition was determined by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS/EDAX, SAMX, France). Surface functional groups were identified using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Tensor II, Bruker, USA). Textural properties, including specific surface area, pore volume, and pore diameter, were determined using nitrogen adsorption–desorption analysis with a BET analyzer (Mini II, BELSORP, Japan).

Determination of adsorbents’ point of zero charge (pHpzc)

The pHpzc of the adsorbents was determined using the pH drift method. A 0.1 M KNO3 solution was prepared, and six aliquots of 50 mL were transferred into separate containers. Subsequently, 0.15 g of dried adsorbent was added to each container. The initial pH of the suspensions was adjusted to values ranging from 2 to 12 using 0.1 M HCl or NaOH solutions. The suspensions were then stirred at 150 rpm and maintained at room temperature for 48 h. After equilibration, the suspensions were filtered, and the final pH of the filtrates was measured. The difference between the final and initial pH values (ΔpH = pHfinal – pHinitial) was plotted against the initial pH, and the pHpzc was determined as the initial pH at which ΔpH equaled zero47.

Adsorption experiments, optimization and analytical methods

All adsorption experiments were carried out in batch mode. In a typical procedure, 25 mL of leachate was transferred to polyethylene bottles. The pH of the leachate was adjusted using H₂SO₄ or NaOH solutions, and the specified adsorbent dose was added. The bottles were then sealed and agitated on an orbital shaker at a constant speed and room temperature for a predetermined contact time to allow adsorption equilibrium to be reached. The effects of various parameters, including the type of adsorbent (PMS-A, Mt, and composite), initial pH (3–11), adsorbent dose (20–240 g L⁻¹), contact time (10–180 min) and stirring speed (50–350 rpm) on removal efficiency were investigated48. Initially, the optimum pH was determined for all three adsorbents at a fixed adsorbent dosage of 60 g L⁻¹ and a contact time of 60 min. Subsequently, the effect of adsorbent dosage was investigated at the optimized pH value. All optimization experiments were performed in triplicate, and results are reported as mean values. After the completion of the contact time, the samples were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatants were then filtered through a membrane filter with a pore size of 0.45 μm to remove residual adsorbent particles. The clarified samples were analyzed to determine COD concentration and color intensity34. COD was measured using the semi-closed reflux Standard Methods for the Examination of Water and Wastewater (APHA, 2017), applicable for concentrations ranging from 40 to 400 mg L⁻¹. When necessary, samples were diluted at a ratio of 1:10 to ensure compliance with the analytical range49. Leachate color was determined using the Hach 8025 method with a DR 5000 spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 465 nm and reported in platinum–cobalt (Pt–Co) units50.

Quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC)

A comprehensive QA/QC protocol was applied to ensure reliable and reproducible measurements. All glassware and digestion vials were acid-washed (20% H2SO4) and rinsed with deionized water prior to use to prevent contamination. COD analyses were performed according to Standard Methods (APHA, 2017), with each batch including reagent blanks and control samples49. The ferrous ammonium sulfate (FAS) titrant was standardized before use, and potassium hydrogen phthalate (KHP) was prepared at appropriate concentrations and used as a reference standard to verify the accuracy of the method. All measurements were conducted in triplicate and reported as mean values. For color analysis, the spectrophotometer (DR 5000) was calibrated using deionized water blanks prior to each measurement, and filtered samples were analyzed within the instrument’s working range. These procedures ensured acceptable precision, accuracy, and analytical transparency.

Adsorption kinetic models

Adsorption kinetics were evaluated using pseudo-first-order (PFO), pseudo-second-order (PSO), intraparticle diffusion (IPD), and Elovich models. These models were used to describe the time-dependent uptake behavior and to identify possible contributions of external mass transfer, intraparticle diffusion, and heterogeneous surface interactions52–57. The PFO model assumes that the adsorption rate is proportional to the difference between equilibrium and instantaneous adsorption capacity53. The PSO model is widely used in adsorption studies and frequently provides a good empirical description of uptake data; however, a good PSO fit alone does not prove that chemisorption is the dominant mechanism54,57. The IPD model was used to evaluate the contribution of diffusion within adsorbent pores, where a non-zero intercept indicates that intraparticle diffusion is not the sole rate-controlling step55. The Elovich model was used to describe adsorption on energetically heterogeneous surfaces, where the adsorption rate decreases with increasing surface coverage56. Adsorption equilibrium was analyzed using the Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin isotherm models58,59. The Langmuir model describes monolayer adsorption on a homogeneous surface with finite identical sites60. The Freundlich model is an empirical model used to describe adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces and non-uniform site-energy distributions61. The Temkin model considers adsorbate–adsorbent interactions and assumes that adsorption energy decreases with increasing surface coverage; however, its fitted parameters should be interpreted cautiously because dimensional and formulation issues have been discussed in recent adsorption literature62,63. Safety considerations

Landfill leachate was handled as a potentially hazardous environmental matrix. Laboratory coats, nitrile gloves, and protective eyewear were used throughout the experiments. COD digestion involving dichromate, concentrated sulfuric acid, and elevated temperature was conducted in a fume hood. Chemical and leachate wastes were disposed of according to institutional hazardous-waste management procedures.

Results and discussion

Characteristics of raw leachate

The leachate was alkaline, highly colored, and rich in refractory organic matter (Table 1). The mean COD and BOD₅ values were 2240 ± 452 and 233 ± 41 mg L⁻¹, respectively, corresponding to a BOD₅/COD ratio of 0.10 ± 0.02. This ratio confirms low biodegradability and is consistent with a mature leachate in which humic- and fulvic-like fractions constitute an important part of the residual organic load2,7,66. The color value of 3500 ± 959 Pt–Co suggests a substantial concentration of chromophoric dissolved organic matter7,9. The high electrical conductivity further demonstrates the presence of a concentrated ionic matrix that can compete with organic constituents for surface sites or alter electrostatic interactions2,6. Consequently, adsorption performance in this real matrix cannot be directly extrapolated from studies using single solutes or synthetic dye solutions2,3,15,66,67.

Characterization of the adsorbents

EDAX

EDAX analysis (Table S4 and Figure S3 in the Supplementary Information) showed that PMS-A was dominated by Ca and O, indicating the formation or enrichment of Ca-containing mineral phases during high-temperature treatment of PMS. This finding is consistent with previous reports showing that high-temperature-treated PMS can contain thermally stable Ca-based phases such as CaO, CaCO₃, CaS and, related mineral species27,68. These Ca-rich phases may contribute to the adsorption of negatively charged organic constituents through electrostatic attraction, surface complexation, or precipitation-like interactions, although the present EDAX data alone cannot distinguish among these pathways. Mt was mainly composed of O, Si, and Al, reflecting its aluminosilicate structure69. The composite showed combined elemental features of both PMS-A and Mt, confirming the formation of a chemically heterogeneous material. Overall, the EDAX results support the interpretation that PMS-A and the composite provide Ca-rich alkaline surfaces, whereas Mt contributes aluminosilicate-based surface sites.

FTIR

The FTIR spectra of PMS-A and the composite showed similar dominant bands, indicating that incorporation of Mt did not fundamentally alter the main mineral framework derived from calcined PMS (Fig. 2.). Both adsorbents exhibited intense carbonate-related bands at approximately 1426–1430, 875, and 714–715 cm⁻¹, confirming that CaCO₃ or carbonate-containing mineral phases were major surface components after thermal treatment27,29. Weak C–H bands at approximately 2872–2984 cm⁻¹ suggest extensive thermal decomposition of organic matter during calcination. Compared with PMS-A, the composite displayed additional bands associated with O–H, Si–O, and Al–O vibrations, which are consistent with the incorporation of Mt and the development of a more heterogeneous surface chemistry. These FTIR observations, together with the EDAX results, indicate that PMS-A provides a Ca-rich carbonate-dominated surface, whereas the composite combines carbonate-rich PMS-derived phases with Mt-related aluminosilicate functionalities. Overall, consistent with previous reports, adsorption performance appears to be primarily governed by accessible Ca-based active sites, while Mt mainly contributes to surface heterogeneity without substantially enhancing adsorption capacity27,34.

SEM

Figure 3. illustrates the surface morphology of PMS-A, the composite, and Mt at 20 μm magnification. All adsorbents exhibit relatively dense surfaces with limited visible porosity. However, PMS-A shows slightly higher surface irregularity and increased pore volume. This feature is mainly attributed to the higher organic content of PMS (i.e., cellulose, hemicellulose and lignin), which undergoes extensive thermal decomposition during pyrolysis at 800 °C, releasing volatile gases and generating additional pore spaces27,29. In the composite, the presence of Mt also may partially restrict pore development by occupying or blocking newly formed pores, leading to a more heterogeneous structure with lower apparent porosity compared to PMS‑A, as similarly observed in Mt‑based composites incorporating additional phases such as ZrO₂ aluminosilicates70. In contrast, Mt exhibits a dense, plate-like morphology typical of layered clay minerals44.

BET

BET analysis provided specific surface area, total pore volume, average pore diameter and BJH pore-structure parameters for PMS-A and the composite (Tables S5 and S6 in the Supplementary Information). PMS-A exhibited a BET surface area of 11.22 m² g⁻¹, a total pore volume of 0.0578 cm³ g⁻¹, and an average pore diameter of 20.6 nm. The composite showed a lower BET surface area of 5.67 m² g⁻¹ and a total pore volume of 0.0471 cm³ g⁻¹, but a larger average pore diameter of 33.25 nm. According to the IUPAC pore-size classification, both materials fall mainly within the mesoporous range of 2–50 nm57,71. The relatively larger pore diameter of the composite may facilitate the diffusion of relatively large organic molecules present in mature landfill leachate; however, this interpretation is inferred from pore-size data and should not be regarded as direct evidence of molecular diffusion behavior71–73. Mt has been reported to possess a relatively high specific surface area (250 m² g⁻¹) due to its layered clay structure (Table S3, in the Supplementary Information)74. Nevertheless, in the present study, Mt exhibited lower COD and color removal than PMS-A, indicating that BET surface area alone did not govern adsorption performance16. Surface charge, active-site chemistry, pore accessibility, and the nature of the organic matter in mature landfill leachate likely played more decisive roles. Because direct active-site accessibility and post-adsorption surface characteristics were not evaluated, this explanation should be considered a plausible interpretation rather than definitive mechanistic evidence. Overall, these results suggest that the superior performance of PMS-A was more likely associated with its Ca-rich alkaline surface properties than with high surface area, which is consistent with the observations reported by Wang et al.27.

pHpzc of the adsorbents

The pHpzc values of the adsorbents were determined from the intersection of the ΔpH curves with the zero-charge reference line (ΔpH = 0), as shown in Fig. 4. The pHpzc values were 12.0, 10.5, and 5.7 for PMS-A, the composite, and Mt, respectively. These results indicate that PMS-A and the composite possess strongly alkaline surface characteristics, whereas Mt exhibits a comparatively lower pHpzc consistent with aluminosilicate clay surfaces26,70,74. The high pHpzc values of PMS-A and the composite can be attributed to the Ca-rich composition of calcined PMS and the formation of alkaline mineral phases during thermal treatment27. Incorporation of PMS into the composite preserved the alkaline character of the material, resulting in a pHpzc substantially higher than that of Mt. These high pHpzc values suggest that PMS-A and the composite remain positively charged over a broad pH range below their pHpzc, which may favor interaction with negatively charged humic-like and chromophoric organic constituents in mature landfill leachate.

Effect of solution pH

Solution pH strongly influences adsorption by governing adsorbent surface charge, the ionization of dissolved organic matter, and the availability of active surface groups75. As shown in Fig. 5a, color removal was highest under acidic conditions (pH 3), reaching 98%, 95%, and 66% for PMS-A, the composite, and Mt, respectively. As the solution became more alkaline, color removal gradually decreased. This trend is consistent with enhanced protonation of surface sites and stronger electrostatic attraction toward negatively charged chromophoric organic compounds under acidic conditions. Conversely, under alkaline conditions (pH 11), decreased removal may be associated with surface deprotonation and stronger electrostatic repulsion between negatively charged dissolved organic matter and the adsorbent surfaces. The pHpzc values determined in the previous section help explain these observations. PMS-A and the composite retained favorable surface charge characteristics over a much broader pH range than Mt, whereas Mt was expected to become progressively less favorable for adsorption above its pHpzc. This difference is consistent with the substantially lower color removal observed for Mt throughout the investigated pH range. Similar pH-dependent behavior has been reported for Ca-rich paper sludge-derived adsorbents27. The highest COD removal was observed near neutral pH (≈ 7), reaching 78% (29 mg g⁻¹) and 72% (27 mg g⁻¹) for PMS-A and the composite, respectively (Fig. 5b). At higher pH, reduced COD removal may be associated with increased electrostatic repulsion between negatively charged organic constituents and deprotonated surface sites, together with competition from OH⁻ ions for available adsorption sites27. Mt showed comparatively limited COD removal throughout the investigated pH range, with only slight improvement under acidic conditions, possibly because protonation of its surface enhanced electrostatic attraction and ion-exchange interactions3. In addition to electrostatic effects, the Ca-rich carbonate phases identified by EDAX and FTIR may also contribute to the observed pH dependence. Under acidic conditions, partial dissolution or surface alteration of carbonate-containing phases (e.g., CaCO₃) may expose additional reactive Ca-containing sites and potentially modify surface reactivity27. However, because dissolved Ca²⁺ was not measured in the present study, any contribution from calcium release or related interactions should be regarded as a plausible interpretation rather than direct evidence. Likewise, although carbonate phases are expected to remain relatively stable under alkaline conditions, the present data do not permit direct assessment of their surface reactivity3,27,38. Highly acidic conditions may also induce partial protonation, aggregation, or structural transformation of humic-like chromophoric compounds, resulting in limited color and COD reduction independent of adsorption9. To evaluate this possibility, control experiments were performed without adsorbent under identical pH conditions (Fig. S4). pH adjustment alone resulted in negligible removal of both color and COD, with only a slight decrease observed at pH 3. Therefore, although limited acid-induced transformation of dissolved organic matter cannot be completely excluded, the substantially greater removals obtained in the presence of the adsorbents indicate that adsorption was the dominant removal process under the investigated conditions. Finally, pH 7 was selected for subsequent experiments because it provided the best balance between color and COD removal while remaining more representative of practical landfill leachate treatment conditions.

Effect of adsorbent dosage

Adsorbent dose is a key operational variable because it determines the number of available adsorption sites, solid–liquid contact area, and material consumption3. The effect of dose was evaluated from 20 to 240 g L⁻¹ at pH 7 (optimized pH), 60 min contact time, 250 rpm, and room temperature. Increasing the dose improved removal efficiency for all adsorbents (Fig. 5c and d). For color removal, increasing the dose from 20 to 120 g L⁻¹ increased removal from 43% to 99% for PMS-A, from 49% to 99% for the composite, and from 18% to 61% for Mt. For COD removal, PMS-A increased from 64% to 93%, the composite from 57% to 86%, and Mt to only 64% at 120 g L⁻¹. The improvement was most pronounced up to 80 g L⁻¹, after which additional increases produced only marginal gains. This plateau may reflect progressive underutilization of adsorption sites, particle aggregation, or overlapping of active surfaces at high solid loadings3,26. Although removal efficiency increased with dose, adsorption capacity decreased as dose increased because the same pollutant mass was distributed over a larger adsorbent mass. For example, COD adsorption capacity decreased from 72 to 17 mg g⁻¹ for PMS-A, from 64 to 16 mg g⁻¹ for the composite, and from 32 to 12 mg g⁻¹ for Mt as the dose increased from 20 to 120 g L⁻¹. Based on removal efficiency and marginal improvement beyond this point, 80 g L⁻¹ was selected as the operational dose for subsequent experiments. At this dose, PMS-A, the composite, and Mt achieved color/COD removals of 98%/86%, 96%/76%, and 50%/60%, respectively. Although the optimal dose (80 g L⁻¹) is higher than typical doses for commercial adsorbents (e.g., 5–10 g L⁻¹ for GAC), two considerations should be noted. First, PMS is an industrial waste with negligible feedstock cost, whereas GAC typically requires virgin carbonaceous feedstocks such as coal or lignocellulosic biomass (e.g., wood and coconut shells). Second, the primary goal of this study is to demonstrate the technical feasibility of converting a waste stream into a functional adsorbent, not to compete with GAC on a dose-to-dose basis.

Effect of contact time

The effect of contact time on the adsorption was evaluated ranging from 10 to 180 min under optimized conditions (pH ≈ 7 and an adsorbent dosage of 80 g L⁻¹), 250 rpm, and room temperature. As illustrated in Fig. 5e–f and Fig. S5 (in the Supplementary Information), all adsorbents exhibited a rapid adsorption phase at the initial stages, followed by a gradual decrease in adsorption rate as equilibrium was approached38. For color removal, PMS-A demonstrated the fastest and most efficient adsorption kinetics, with removal efficiency increasing from 77% at 10 min to 97% within 60 min and reaching 99% at 180 min. The composite followed a similar trend but with slightly lower efficiency, increasing from 74% to 87% at 60 min and reaching 91% at 180 min. In contrast, Mt showed significantly slower adsorption kinetics, with color removal increasing from 46% at 10 min to only 59% at 180 min. COD adsorption exhibited a distinct yet consistent kinetic pattern. PMS-A achieved rapid COD removal, reaching 86% within the first 30 min and 92% at 90 min, after which no significant increase was observed up to 180 min, indicating rapid attainment of adsorption equilibrium. The composite exhibited moderate kinetics, achieving 50% COD removal at 30 min and 71% at 90 min, after which it stabilized. Mt displayed the slowest COD adsorption behavior, with removal increasing gradually from 29% at 10 min to approximately 50% at 90 min, and reaching only 53% at 180 min. The pronounced differences in adsorption kinetics among the adsorbents may be attributed to variations in surface chemistry, pore structure, and accessibility of active sites. PMS-A and the composite possess more favorable pore networks, higher densities of functional groups, and higher pHpzc values, which collectively enhance mass transfer and promote rapid interaction between pollutants and adsorption sites. In contrast, Mt suffers from structural limitations and fewer effective binding sites, resulting in slower adsorption kinetics and lower removal efficiency. From an operational perspective, adsorption equilibrium for PMS-A and the composite was reached within 60–90 min, whereas Mt required longer contact times to achieve comparable removal efficiencies. Extending the contact time beyond 90 min led to only marginal improvements for all adsorbents, confirming equilibrium attainment. Accordingly, 90 min was selected as the optimal contact time, balancing adsorption efficiency and operational feasibility.

Effect of stirring speed

The effect of stirring speed was examined over a range of 50 to 350 rpm under optimized conditions (pH ≈ 7, adsorbent dosage of 80 g L⁻¹, and contact time of 90 min). For PMS-A and the composite, color removal efficiency increased with stirring speed up to 250 rpm, followed by a slight decline at 350 rpm (Fig. 5g). Specifically, color removal by PMS-A increased from 91% at 50 rpm to 99% at 250 rpm, while the composite showed a similar improvement from 94% to 98%. The initial enhancement can be attributed to reduced external mass transfer resistance and improved diffusion of chromophoric compounds to active adsorption sites3. The slight decline observed at 350 rpm may indicate that further increases in agitation provided no additional mass-transfer benefit under the experimental conditions3,76,77. In contrast, Mt exhibited substantially lower and less stable color removal efficiencies (46–56%) across the investigated stirring speeds. The maximum color removal (56%) occurred at 150 rpm, after which efficiency declined with increasing agitation. Although the exact reason was not investigated in the present study, this behavior may reflect differences in the adsorption characteristics of Mt compared with PMS-based adsorbents. The effect of stirring speed on COD removal (Fig. 5h) followed a similar but more pronounced trend for PMS-A and the composite. For PMS-A, COD removal increased from 76% at 50 rpm to a maximum of 93% at 250 rpm, before decreasing to 89% at 350 rpm. The composite showed a non-monotonic response, with COD removal decreasing from 82% to 64% at 150 rpm, increasing to 86% at 250 rpm, and declining again to 75% at 350 rpm. Although the cause of this non-monotonic trend was not specifically investigated, it may reflect different adsorption responses of heterogeneous organic fractions in mature landfill leachate to changes in mixing intensity, together with the heterogeneous surface characteristics of the composite. However, this interpretation requires further experimental verification. These observations suggest that moderate agitation provided favorable conditions for adsorption, whereas further increases in stirring speed did not result in additional improvement3,76. Mt displayed a distinct COD adsorption behavior compared to color removal. COD removal increased with stirring speed, reaching a maximum of 68% at 350 rpm, while the lowest efficiency (42%) occurred at 150 rpm. This observation may reflect differences in the physicochemical characteristics of the organic fractions contributing to COD and color. Unlike color, which is mainly associated with high-molecular-weight humic-like chromophores, COD represents a broader mixture of organic compounds with different molecular sizes and adsorption behaviors7,66. However, this hypothesis requires verification through molecular-weight fractionation or compositional analysis. Overall, increasing stirring speed enhanced color and COD adsorption by PMS-A and the composite up to an optimal value, beyond which no further improvement was observed or a slight decline occurred. PMS-A and the composite maintained high color removal efficiencies (> 90%) across all stirring speeds and exhibited optimal COD removal at moderate agitation. In contrast, Mt showed limited color adsorption and comparatively greater improvement in COD removal at higher stirring speeds. Considering both adsorption performance and operational stability, a stirring speed of 250 rpm was selected as the optimal condition.

Kinetic studies

The adsorption kinetics of color and COD onto PMS-A, the composite, and Mt were evaluated using PFO, PSO, IPD, and Elovich models (Fig. 6. and Table S7 in the Supplementary Information). For both pollutants, the PFO model provided a reasonable description of PMS-A and the composite adsorption (adjusted R² > 0.96), suggesting that rapid initial uptake and physical mass-transfer processes contributed to the early stage of adsorption53. However, the lower goodness of fit for Mt indicates that first-order kinetics alone did not adequately describe its adsorption behavior. Among the tested models, the PSO model provided the best fit, with high adjusted R² values (0.937–0.993), low SD values, and close agreement between experimental and calculated equilibrium capacities. This result indicates that surface-controlled uptake may have contributed to the adsorption process. However, PSO fitting alone cannot be used to conclude that chemisorption was the dominant mechanism54. The Elovich model also produced relatively high adjusted R² values (0.966–0.998), consistent with adsorption on energetically heterogeneous surfaces. However, as with PSO, Elovich fitting should be interpreted as a descriptor of heterogeneous uptake rather than direct proof of mechanism56. The IPD model indicated multi-stage adsorption for both color and COD. The non-zero intercepts (C) obtained for all adsorbents show that intraparticle diffusion was not the only rate-controlling step and that boundary-layer diffusion also contributed to the uptake process, with this contribution being more pronounced for color than for COD55. These kinetic results are consistent with the characterization data, which indicate that PMS-A and the composite possess more favorable surface charge and Ca-rich surface chemistry than Mt. Overall, the kinetic analysis supports heterogeneous, multi-step adsorption involving external mass transfer, intraparticle diffusion, and surface interactions, with PMS-A showing the most effective uptake behavior.

Isotherm modeling

The adsorption isotherms describe the equilibrium distribution of color and COD between the liquid and solid phases3,42. Experimental data were fitted using the Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin models (Fig. 7a–c for color, Fig. 7d–f for COD, and Table S7 in the Supplementary Information). For color adsorption, the Langmuir model provided the best description for PMS-A and the composite, showing high adjusted R² values (0.981 and 0.982, respectively) and relatively low SD values. The corresponding Qmax values were 74.35, 38.68, and 20.03 Pt-Co g⁻¹ for PMS-A, the composite, and Mt, respectively, indicating a higher adsorption capacity of PMS-A toward chromophoric constituents. Although the Langmuir model is commonly associated with monolayer adsorption on homogeneous surfaces, its statistical agreement was considered only as an equilibrium description rather than direct evidence of a specific adsorption mechanism. The Freundlich model also showed acceptable fitting for color adsorption, with nF values greater than unity for all adsorbents, indicating favorable adsorption and the potential contribution of heterogeneous adsorption sites. However, its lower fitting performance for PMS-A and the composite suggested that color adsorption was better represented by the Langmuir model under the studied conditions. In contrast, the relatively better performance of the Freundlich model for Mt suggests a greater contribution of heterogeneous adsorption sites on the clay surface. The Temkin model exhibited variable fitting behavior, with a particularly high adjusted R² value for the composite (0.997), suggesting differences in equilibrium behavior among the adsorbents. For COD adsorption, a different equilibrium behavior was observed. The Freundlich model provided the best fit for PMS-A (adjusted R² = 0.986), while Langmuir and Freundlich models showed comparable performance for the composite and Mt. The better Freundlich fitting for COD is consistent with the heterogeneous nature of dissolved organic matter in mature landfill leachate, where COD represents a mixture of chemically diverse organic compounds rather than a single adsorbate. The Temkin model showed lower or comparable fitting quality, indicating a limited ability to describe COD equilibrium behavior under the investigated conditions. The Langmuir-derived Qmax values for COD were 52.41, 32.55, and 15.70 mg g⁻¹ for PMS-A, the composite, and Mt, respectively, confirming the superior adsorption capacity of PMS-A.

Overall, the results of this study suggest that the removal of color and COD from mature landfill leachate was governed by multiple concurrent processes rather than a single mechanism. The superior performance of PMS-A is consistent with its Ca-rich surface chemistry and high pHpzc, which maintained a positively charged surface over the investigated pH range and favored interactions with negatively charged humic-like organic constituents27. The incorporation of Mt increased surface heterogeneity and introduced additional hydroxyl-bearing aluminosilicate functionalities, but these features did not compensate for the reduced contribution of Ca-rich adsorption sites after composite formation32,34. In addition, Ca-rich mineral phases derived from calcined PMS may have contributed through calcium-mediated interactions with dissolved organic matter and possibly by promoting the precipitation of organic constituents. Together, these findings indicate that surface chemistry and active-site accessibility played a more important role than specific surface area in governing adsorption performance. Because no post-adsorption surface characterization or dissolved calcium analysis was performed, these mechanistic interpretations should be regarded as plausible explanations rather than direct evidence. Consistent with these findings, adsorption alone did not completely remove the organic constituents responsible for COD. The residual COD is likely associated with low-molecular-weight, highly hydrophilic, and refractory organic compounds that exhibit limited affinity for the Ca-rich adsorption sites2,7. These fractions are likely to be more difficult to remove by adsorption alone and may require complementary post-treatment processes, such as advanced oxidation, to achieve further COD reduction.

Preliminary economic considerations

The proposed adsorbents offer both economic advantages and limitations. PMS, the primary feedstock, is an abundant industrial waste that is generally available at little or no cost, and its beneficial reuse may reduce disposal and environmental management costs. In addition, adsorbent preparation requires no chemical activation or modification, eliminating reagent costs. However, calcination at 800 °C for 2 h is the major cost component because of its high energy demand. Consequently, the economic feasibility of the process depends largely on local energy costs and production scale. Although the low feedstock cost may partially offset this energy requirement, a comprehensive techno-economic assessment, including energy consumption, production cost, regeneration efficiency, and life-cycle cost, is needed before large-scale implementation.

Conclusions

In this study, calcined paper mill sludge adsorbent (PMS-A), montmorillonite (Mt), and their composite (PMS-Mt) were evaluated for the removal of color and COD from mature landfill leachate. PMS-A exhibited the highest adsorption performance, which is likely associated with its Ca-rich surface, high pHpzc, and favorable surface properties, whereas the composite benefited from increased surface heterogeneity and mesoporous structure. Although Mt possessed a relatively high BET surface area, its adsorption performance was limited, indicating that surface chemistry and active-site accessibility were more influential than surface area alone. The kinetic and isotherm analyses indicated that adsorption behavior was influenced by adsorbent properties and the chemical complexity of the target pollutants. Despite the relatively high adsorbent dose required, the results demonstrate the potential of PMS as a promising precursor for preparing adsorbents for mature landfill leachate treatment and highlight its potential for valorization as an industrial by-product. Future studies should focus on improving adsorption capacity through adsorbent modification or process optimization to reduce material consumption, evaluating adsorbent regeneration, investigating continuous-flow column performance, conducting techno-economic assessments, and validating the process under pilot- or full-scale operating conditions.
