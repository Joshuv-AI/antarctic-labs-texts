Effects of quercetin in male rats’ testis after consumption of permethrin: a transmission electron microscopy (TEM) study
Authors: Omid Rajabzadeh, Atefeh Yaghoubi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPermethrin, a widely used pyrethroid insecticide, impairs male reproductive function through oxidative stress and endocrine disruption. This study evaluated the protective effects of quercetin against permethrin-induced testicular toxicity in adult male Wistar rats. Thirty rats were divided into five groups (n = 6): control, DMSO vehicle, permethrin (35 mg/kg/day orally), permethrin + quercetin (15 mg/kg/day orally), and quercetin alone, for 60 days. Permethrin exposure induced severe ultrastructural damage including mitochondrial swelling, nuclear chromatin condensation, and Sertoli cell detachment from the basement membrane—along with seminiferous tubule disorganization, reduced sperm motility and viability, increased sperm morphological abnormalities, and suppressed serum testosterone, FSH, and LH levels. Co-administration of quercetin significantly attenuated these adverse effects, preserving testicular cellular integrity, improving sperm quality, and partially restoring gonadotropin and testosterone levels. These findings demonstrate that quercetin exerts multifaceted protection against permethrin-induced testicular injury at hormonal, functional, histological, and ultrastructural levels, highlighting its potential as a protective agent in mitigating pesticide-associated male reproductive dysfunction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42877-2.Keywords: Permethrin, Quercetin, Testicular toxicity, Oxidative stress, Transmission electron microscopySubject terms: Cell biology, Drug discovery, Physiology, Zoology

Introduction

Infertility is a significant health concern worldwide1. Clinically, infertility is defined as the inability of a couple to achieve conception after actively trying for 12 months2. Globally, an estimated 48.5 million couples were affected by infertility in 20103. Reports indicate that around 10% to 15% of couples worldwide experience infertility4. Each year, 10% to 15% of couples attempting to conceive are unable to do so and require significant medical intervention3.

Infertility affects as many as 12% of men, with sexual dysfunction being a common issue among those of reproductive age. In certain cases, these conditions contribute to difficulties in conceiving5. Several factors contributing to male infertility are associated with reduced production of sperm exhibiting normal morphology and progressive motility. Among these, vascular conditions such as varicocele stand out as the most common cause, accounting for 49.4% of cases. According to semen analysis findings, the most frequently identified conditions were azoospermia, observed in 56.4% of cases, and oligozoospermia, which accounted for 24.5%6. Male factors are believed to account for 30–50% of infertility cases. A decline in fertility or complete infertility can arise from various causes, including testicular dysfunction, hormonal disorders, lifestyle influences such as smoking and obesity, congenital anatomical abnormalities, exposure to gonadotoxic substances, and the effects of aging, among other contributing factors2.

Medications prescribed for common health conditions can sometimes lead to sperm abnormalities and issues with sexual function. Addressing certain underlying causes of male infertility may help enhance semen quality and resolve sexual dysfunction associated with infertility5. Environmental pollution, particularly from insecticides, is a significant contributor to male infertility, as residues persist in the food chain, soil, and water sources7. According to previous studies, the household and agricultural use of insecticides and pesticides can cause damage to the nervous, respiratory, digestive, and immune systems, as well as organs such as the heart and liver8, and can also have carcinogenic and immunosuppressive effects9.

Pyrethroids are synthetic compounds derived from pyrethrum, a natural extract from Chrysanthemum flowers. Among these, permethrin, a type I pyrethroid, is commonly utilized as an insecticide10. Chronic exposure to pyrethroid-containing insecticides can disrupt androgen and sex hormone receptors, leading to androgen-related endocrine dysfunction. Omotoso et al. (2014) showed that permethrin causes abnormalities, including disruption of normal structure, a decrease in the population of mature sperm cells, and a reduction of interstitial spaces in rats8. Zhang et al. also suggested that the use of the insecticide permethrin disrupted the mitochondrial membrane of Leydig cells, reducing cholesterol transport to mitochondria and inhibiting the conversion of cholesterol to pregnenolone, thereby disrupting testosterone biosynthesis and subsequently decreasing testosterone production11. Treatment options encompass lifestyle adjustments, tailored or evidence-based medical interventions, and surgical procedures, all of which are designed to deliver significant enhancements in fertility outcomes2. In previous research, co-administration of naringenin supplementation improved the toxicity induced by permethrin in the reproductive system of male rats9.

Bioflavonoids, as antioxidants and anti-inflammatory agents, can serve as therapeutic supplements for male infertility under conditions of oxidative stress12. Polyphenolic compounds, as natural antioxidants, are increasingly being incorporated into functional food formulations with the aim of promoting human health and improving physical and cognitive performance13. Quercetin is a flavonol-type polyphenol found in a variety of fruits and vegetables, including apples with skin, cherries, berries, onions, broccoli, and raw spinach, as well as green and black tea leaves14. Quercetin has been reported to have several potentially advantageous health effects, such as antioxidant15, antihypertensive16, and antidiabetic17 activities. The antioxidant capacity of quercetin, its protective effects against environmental contaminants and toxic agents including heavy metals and its efficacy in the prevention and treatment of male infertility have been reported in most studies18. Although there is substantial evidence regarding the antioxidant effects of quercetin, its effects on the ultrastructural integrity of testicular tissue damaged by insecticides and on spermatogenic disorders remain incompletely characterized. Therefore, the current study aimed to provide a comprehensive analysis of the protective role of quercetin against permethrin-induced testicular toxicity in rats.

Material and methods

Materials

Permethrin (cis/trans ratio: 20/80) and DMSO (Dimethyl Sulfoxide) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Quercetin powder was dissolved in 20% glycerol and 0.9% normal saline, then stored in a dark falcon tube at 4°C for fresh use.

All experimental protocols involving animals were approved by the Lorestan University of Medical Sciences Ethics Committee (Approval No.: IR.LUMS.REC.1404.361). All methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant national guidelines and the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org) for reporting in vivo experiments.

Experimental design

Thirty male rats, weighing 250 ± 10 g, were randomly divided into five groups (n = 6 per group). Sample size determination: Based on prior reproductive toxicity studies using similar experimental designs and TEM-based endpoints8,9, a minimum of 5–6 animals per group was determined to provide adequate statistical power (≥ 80%) to detect significant differences in histological scores and sperm parameters, assuming an effect size of d = 1.2 and α = 0.05. Accordingly, we allocated n = 6 rats per experimental group, consistent with the 3Rs principle and established practices in rodent toxicology research. Thirty male Wistar rats (weighing 250 ± 10 g) were obtained from the Animal Breeding Center of Lorestan University of Medical Sciences, Khorramabad, Iran. All animals were client-owned by the institution and not privately owned; therefore, no additional owner permission statements were required beyond institutional ethical approval (IR.LUMS.REC.1404.361).

After 60 days of treatment, the rats were deeply anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). Under deep anesthesia, blood samples were collected directly from the left ventricle, and the animals were euthanized by exsanguination followed by testis excision. This method of euthanasia is consistent with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020) for terminal procedures under deep anesthesia.

Histological processing and semi-quantitative scoring

Histological evaluation was limited to seminiferous tubule cross-sections displaying identifiable stage-specific cellular associations of the spermatogenic cycle. To distinguish pathological changes from normal physiological variations such as the transient vacuolization typically observed in stages VII–VIII of the seminiferous epithelium, vacuolization or intercellular separation was deemed pathological only when it exceeded the morphological characteristics expected for that specific stage or was associated with additional signs of damage, including germ cell sloughing, nuclear pyknosis, or epithelial disorganization. Immediately after dissection, testicular tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 48 h at room temperature to ensure adequate preservation of cellular architecture. Tissues were then processed through a graded ethanol series (70% → 96% → 100%), cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax. Serial Sects. (5-μm thickness) were cut using a rotary microtome (Leica RM2125, Germany) and mounted on glass slides coated with Poly-L-lysine to prevent detachment during staining. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) using standard protocols. Histological evaluation was performed by two independent observers blinded to the experimental groups, using a light microscope (Olympus BX51) at × 200 and × 400 magnifications. To quantitatively assess spermatogenic integrity, 20 random cross-sections of seminiferous tubules per animal were evaluated and assigned a histological score1–10 based on the presence and organization of germ cells (spermatogonia, spermatocytes, spermatids), epithelial regularity, basement membrane integrity, and extent of vacuolization or intercellular separation adapted from established criteria in rodent testicular toxicity studies9. A score of 10 indicated full spermatogenesis with intact tubular architecture, while a score of 1 reflected complete atrophy. The mean histological score per animal was used for statistical analysis. A semi-quantitative histological scoring system (range: 1–10) was applied to assess testicular tissue integrity, based on the following morphological features: (i) height and stratification of the germinal epithelium, (ii) presence and organization of all germ cell types (spermatogonia, spermatocytes, spermatids), (iii) extent of pathological vacuolization (particularly adjacent to the basement membrane), (iv) basement membrane thickness and regularity, (v) presence of germ cell sloughing, and (vi) luminal sperm presence. Additionally, qualitative histopathological features including germ cell sloughing, Sertoli cell vacuolization, and interstitial edema were documented and used to support ultrastructural and functional findings.

Sperm morphology assessment

Sperm morphology was evaluated according to standard protocols with minor modifications (WHO, 2021). Briefly, the cauda epididymis of both testes were minced in 1 mL of pre-warmed normal saline (37°C) and incubated for 10 min to allow sperm to swim out. A 10-μL aliquot of the sperm suspension was smeared onto a clean glass slide, air-dried, and fixed in 96% ethanol for 30 min. Slides were then stained using hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) to enhance nuclear and cytoplasmic contrast. At least 200 spermatozoa per animal were examined under light microscopy (× 1000, oil immersion) and classified into the following categories: (i) normal head (smooth, oval, with an intact acrosomal cap), (ii) abnormal head (including round head, long head, and other irregular shapes), (iii) normal tail (straight, uniform thickness), and (iv) abnormal tail (twisted, coiled, or short tail). Results are expressed as the percentage of each morphological type relative to the total number of sperm counted.

Sperm motility and viability assessment

Sperm motility was evaluated immediately after epididymal dissection using a pre-warmed Neubauer hemocytometer under phase-contrast microscopy (× 200). At least 200 spermatozoa per sample were classified as: (i) progressively motile (sperm moving actively in a straight or large-circle pattern), (ii) non-progressively motile (sperm moving but not in a forward direction), or (iii) immotile (no movement observed). Sperm viability was assessed using the eosin–nigrosin staining method: live spermatozoa exclude the dye (white heads), while dead spermatozoa incorporate it (pink/red heads). At least 200 spermatozoa per slide were scored. Data are expressed as percentages.

Hormone assay

Blood samples were collected directly from the left ventricle of deeply anesthetized rats and immediately transferred into non-heparinized tubes. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 1,600 × g for 10 min and stored at − 70°C until analysis. Serum concentrations of testosterone, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and luteinizing hormone (LH) were measured using commercially available double-antibody radioimmunoassay kits (Immunotech, Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA, USA). The assay sensitivity was 0.025 ng/mL for all hormones.

Ultrastructural assay

To evaluate the impact of permethrin and the protective potential of quercetin on the cellular machinery of spermatogenesis, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed with a targeted, pathology-oriented approach. Specimens (~ 1 mm3) were excised from the mid-portion of each testis immediately after dissection to ensure anatomical consistency across all groups. Tissues were fixed in 2.5% phosphate-buffered glutaraldehyde (pH 7.4), post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide at 4°C, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin Sects. (30–50 nm) were cut using a Leica Ultracut microtome, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined using a LEO 906 transmission electron microscope (Zeiss, Germany) at the Histology Department, Faculty of Medicine, Iran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran.

Imaging was deliberately focused on cellular compartments critically involved in spermatogenesis and known to be vulnerable to oxidative and toxic insults, including:

Sertoli cells adjacent to the basement membrane, due to their essential role in maintaining the blood–testis barrier and supporting germ cell development;

Sertoli cells adjacent to the basement membrane, due to their essential role in maintaining the blood–testis barrier and supporting germ cell development;

Germ cells (spermatogonia, primary spermatocytes, and spermatids) at identifiable and comparable maturational stages, selected based on nuclear morphology, chromatin condensation pattern, and acrosomal development;

Germ cells (spermatogonia, primary spermatocytes, and spermatids) at identifiable and comparable maturational stages, selected based on nuclear morphology, chromatin condensation pattern, and acrosomal development;

Leydig cells in the interstitial compartment, assessed for mitochondrial integrity and secretory granule content as indicators of steroidogenic function;

Leydig cells in the interstitial compartment, assessed for mitochondrial integrity and secretory granule content as indicators of steroidogenic function;

Testicular microvasculature, to evaluate potential permethrin-induced endothelial injury.Within these categories, ultrastructural features recognized as sensitive markers of cellular stress such as mitochondrial swelling, nuclear chromatin condensation or pyknosis, cytoplasmic vacuolization, endoplasmic reticulum dilation, and detachment from the basement membrane were specifically documented. All micrographs were acquired at standardized magnifications, and two independent observers, blinded to experimental groups, confirmed that comparisons were made between anatomically and developmentally equivalent structures. Images were acquired using a LEO 906 transmission electron microscope (Zeiss, Germany) at the Histology Department, Faculty of Medicine, Iran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality of distribution and homogeneity of variances were verified using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. Since all variables met the assumptions of parametric testing, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess differences among the five experimental groups. When a significant main effect was detected (p < 0.05), Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post-hoc test was applied to determine pairwise group comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 26).

Results

Histological assay

Histological evaluation of seminiferous tubules revealed significant structural differences among experimental groups (Table 1). The control (9.45 ± 0.51), DMSO vehicle (9.33 ± 0.51), and quercetin-alone (9.66 ± 0.54) groups exhibited well-preserved testicular architecture, characterized by a stratified germinal epithelium containing all stages of germ cells (spermatogonia, primary spermatocytes, and spermatids), a regular basement membrane, and absence of pathological vacuolization (Fig. 1a,b; Fig. 2a,b, Figs. 3, 4; Fig. 5a,b). In contrast, the permethrin-treated group displayed severe histopathological disruption, with a significantly reduced mean histological score (5.66 ± 0.41; p < 0.001 vs. control). These tubules showed extensive vacuolization adjacent to the basement membrane, marked thickening of the basement membrane, widened intercellular spaces, disorganized germ cell layers, and focal germ cell sloughing (Fig. 3a,b). Co-administration of quercetin with permethrin markedly attenuated these structural damages, yielding a significantly higher histological score (7.33 ± 0.63) compared to the permethrin only group (p < 0.001). The seminiferous tubules in this group exhibited improved epithelial organization, reduced vacuolization, and preserved basement membrane integrity, although full restoration to control levels was not achieved (Fig. 4a,b).

Sperm morphology analysis

Assessment of sperm morphology revealed significant alterations in head and tail structure following permethrin exposure, which were partially mitigated by quercetin co-administration (Table 2). The permethrin-treated group exhibited a marked reduction in the proportion of spermatozoa with normal heads (45% vs. 75% in control; p < 0.05) and normal tails (50% vs. 80% in control; p < 0.05). Concurrently, abnormal head forms including round heads (15% vs. 5% in control) and total abnormal heads (25% vs. 10% in control) were significantly elevated (p < 0.05). Tail defects, particularly twisted tails (25% vs. 5% in control) and short tails (15% vs. 5% in control), also increased substantially (p < 0.05). Notably, co-administration with quercetin significantly improved sperm morphology compared to the permethrin-only group: the proportion of normal heads increased to 55% (p < 0.01), and normal tails rose to 60% (p < 0.01). However, the permethrin–quercetin group still displayed a higher incidence of round heads (20%) and short tails (15%) than the control group (p < 0.01 and p < 0.05, respectively), indicating incomplete restoration of sperm integrity. The quercetin-alone and DMSO vehicle groups showed morphology profiles comparable to the control, confirming the absence of inherent toxicity from either quercetin or the solvent.

Sperm motility and viability

Permethrin exposure significantly impaired sperm motility and viability compared to control groups (p < 0.05; Table 3). The permethrin-treated group exhibited a marked reduction in progressive motility (40% vs. 65% in control) and viability (50% vs. 70% in control), accompanied by a substantial increase in immotile spermatozoa (40% vs. 15% in control). Co-administration of quercetin partially reversed these adverse effects: progressive motility improved to 50% and viability increased to 60% (p < 0.05 vs. permethrin alone), although neither parameter fully returned to control levels. Notably, the quercetin-only group demonstrated superior sperm parameters, with progressive motility and viability reaching 70% and 80%, respectively—values comparable to or slightly exceeding those of the control group. The DMSO vehicle group showed no significant differences from controls, confirming its neutrality. These findings indicate that quercetin exerts a protective effect against permethrin-induced functional sperm damage, consistent with its observed ultrastructural and histological benefits.

Serum hormone profiles

Permethrin exposure significantly suppressed serum testosterone, FSH, and LH levels compared to control and vehicle groups (p < 0.05; Table 4). Specifically, the permethrin-treated group exhibited markedly reduced testosterone (1.0 ± 0.8 ng/mL vs. 2.5 ± 0.5 ng/mL in control), FSH (0.5 ± 0.8 ng/mL vs. 2.0 ± 0.7 ng/mL), and near-undetectable LH (0.000 ± 0.001 ng/mL vs. 1.000 ± 0.009 ng/mL), indicating profound hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis disruption. Co-administration of quercetin significantly attenuated these endocrine disturbances: testosterone levels increased to 2.0 ± 0.2 ng/mL (p < 0.05 vs. permethrin alone), and both FSH (1.5 ± 0.5 ng/mL) and LH (1.000 ± 0.002 ng/mL) were fully restored to levels comparable to the control group (p > 0.05). The DMSO vehicle and quercetin-alone groups showed hormone concentrations indistinguishable from controls, confirming the absence of solvent or flavonoid-related endocrine effects. These findings suggest that quercetin preserves gonadotropin secretion and Leydig cell steroidogenic function, likely by mitigating permethrin-induced oxidative damage in the testes and/or central regulatory centers.

Ultrastructural assay

All TEM comparisons were conducted between seminiferous tubules at anatomically and developmentally equivalent stages of the spermatogenic cycle, as confirmed by concurrent light microscopic staging and ultrastructural landmarks. Transmission electron microscopy revealed targeted ultrastructural alterations in key cellular components of spermatogenesis following permethrin exposure, which were significantly mitigated by quercetin co-administration. Sertoli cells, imaged adjacent to the basement membrane, exhibited preserved ultrastructure in control and DMSO groups, with euchromatic nuclei, intact nuclear envelopes, and close adhesion to a thin, regular basement membrane (Fig. 6A,B; Fig. 7A,B). In contrast, the permethrin group displayed Sertoli cell pathology characteristic of blood–testis barrier disruption: heterochromatic nuclei, extensive cytoplasmic vacuolization, abnormal nuclear envelopes, and detachment from the basement membrane (Fig. 8A). Co-administration with quercetin restored nuclear euchromatin, eliminated vacuolization, and re-established firm adhesion to the basement membrane (Fig. 8B). Germ cells at matched maturation stages showed severe oxidative damage in the permethrin group. Primary spermatocytes at equivalent maturation stages showed normal euchromatic nuclei and clearly defined plasma membranes in the control group (Fig. 9A), whereas those in the permethrin group exhibited intensely condensed chromatin and cytoplasmic vacuolation (Fig. 9B), while Spermatogonia located on the basement membrane exhibited spherical heterochromatic nuclei and intact nuclear envelopes in the control group (Fig. 10A), while those in the permethrin-treated group displayed pyknotic nuclei and perinuclear vacuolar spaces (Fig. 10B). Spermatids at comparable developmental stages showed spherical nuclei with intact acrosomal membranes and normal mitochondrial structure in the control group (Fig. 11A,C), whereas those in the permethrin-treated group revealed hyperchromatic nuclei, effaced nuclear membranes, and swollen mitochondria (Fig. 11B,D). In the permethrin–quercetin group, these features were markedly improved: germ cells showed euchromatic or normochromatic nuclei, intact nuclear membranes, and mitochondria of normal size and cristae structure, confirming functional preservation (Fig. 12A–C).

Discussion

Previous studies have shown that exposure to environmental pollutants such as permethrin-containing insecticides on a chronic basis adversely affects male fertility by interacting with androgenic and sex hormone receptors7,10. Importantly, to ensure biological relevance and comparability, all histological and ultrastructural assessments were restricted to tubules at matched stages of the seminiferous epithelial cycle. This approach minimizes misinterpretation of stage-dependent physiological features as toxicological endpoints.

In the present study, targeted transmission electron microscopy of cellular compartments critical to spermatogenesis including Sertoli cells adjacent to the basement membrane, germ cells at defined maturational stages, and interstitial Leydig cells revealed ultrastructural alterations in the permethrin-treated group compared to the control group. The observed alterations included cytoplasmic vacuolation, heterochromatin condensation with nuclear disorganization, and mitochondrial swelling. On the other hand, the ultrastructural features of cells in the quercetin-treated groups closely resembled intact cells. Additionally, histological assessment of the testis in both the control and quercetin-treated groups revealed that the testicular parenchyma contained seminiferous tubules lined with a stratified germinal epithelium. However, in the permethrin-treated groups, seminiferous tubules exhibited irregular morphology, enlarged intercellular spaces, and prominent vacuolization adjacent to the basement membrane features consistent with germ cell sloughing and Sertoli cell dysfunction.

Critically, our study now provides a comprehensive reproductive profile by integrating functional sperm parameters and endocrine status. Permethrin exposure significantly impaired sperm quality: progressive motility dropped to 40% (vs. 65% in control), viability fell to 50%, and morphological abnormalities increased markedly particularly abnormal heads (25% vs. 10%) and short/twisted tails (15–25% vs. 5%). Concurrently, serum testosterone (1.0 ng/mL), FSH (0.5 ng/mL), and LH (undetectable) levels were profoundly suppressed (p < 0.05), indicating severe disruption of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis and Leydig cell steroidogenesis.

Co-administration of quercetin significantly attenuated these adverse effects. Sperm progressive motility improved to 50%, viability rose to 60%, and normal head morphology increased to 55% (p < 0.05 vs. permethrin alone). Hormonally, LH was fully restored to 1.0 ng/mL, FSH increased to 1.5 ng/mL, and testosterone reached 2.0 ng/mL, demonstrating robust protection of both testicular and central endocrine function. These findings confirm that quercetin’s benefits extend beyond cellular architecture to functional fertility outcomes, a dimension not previously addressed in studies of permethrin–quercetin interactions. Our findings are strongly supported by recent evidence demonstrating that quercetin exerts its antioxidant and anti-apoptotic effects primarily through modulation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway and suppression of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress two interconnected mechanisms highly relevant to germ cell survival. Ding et al. (2023) showed that quercetin alleviates cadmium-induced renal apoptosis by inhibiting the PERK/eIF2α/ATF4/CHOP ER stress axis, thereby preserving mitochondrial integrity and reducing caspase activation31. Similarly, Shi et al. (2024) demonstrated that quercetin upregulates Nrf2 and its downstream target NQO1, while downregulating Keap1, to counteract oxidative stress and mitochondrial apoptosis in cadmium-exposed hepatocytes32. Although these studies focused on kidney and liver, the same pathways are critically active in testicular cells: our TEM data reveal precisely the types of damage targeted by these mechanisms mitochondrial swelling, RER dilation, nuclear condensation, and caspase-associated apoptosis.

Furthermore, Adiguzel et al. (2025) recently confirmed that quercetin suppresses ER stress markers (GRP78, CHOP, HSP70/90) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) in heavy metal–exposed tissues, while restoring redox balance via Nrf2 activation (33). Given that permethrin induces oxidative injury in Sertoli and germ cells as evidenced by disrupted organelle ultrastructure and elevated sperm abnormalities quercetin’s ability to stabilize ER homeostasis, enhance antioxidant defenses, and block mitochondrial apoptotic signaling likely underlies the structural and functional preservation observed in our study.

Mitochondrial swelling in permethrin exposed germ cells indicative of disrupted electron transport and ATP depletion was reversed by quercetin, consistent with its known role in stabilizing mitochondrial membranes and enhancing complex I/III activity21,22. The reduction in chromatin condensation and vacuolization further suggests attenuation of oxidative DNA damage and ER stress, corroborated by Priyanka et al.25 and Costa et al.23. Additionally, the detachment of Sertoli cells from the basement membrane in the permethrin group likely reflects blood–testis barrier (BTB) disruption, which may disable spermatogenesis28,29. Quercetin’s ability to preserve Sertoli cell germ cell adhesion aligns with its reported role in modulating tight junction proteins and reducing inflammatory signaling. conversely, the ultrastructural morphology of testicular cells in the quercetin-treated groups remained largely intact, resembling that of the control group. This conservation of cellular architecture supports the role of quercetin as a potent antioxidant that can alleviate permethrin-induced oxidative injury. Quercetin is known to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS), enhance endogenous antioxidant defenses (e.g., superoxide dismutase and glutathione peroxidase)26, and modulate anti-apoptotic signaling pathways such as Nrf2 and Bcl-227. Thus, the current findings amplify the therapeutic potential of quercetin in counteracting environmental toxicant–mediated testicular dysfunction.

Histological assessment confirmed ultrastructural observations. While the control and quercetin treated groups exhibited normal testicular parenchyma with well-organized seminiferous tubules, the permethrin exposed group displayed marked architectural irregularity, including flattened intercellular spaces within the germinal epithelium likely reflecting germ cell loss or junctional disruption. Serum levels of FSH and LH were significantly elevated in the permethrin–quercetin group compared to the permethrin group, with LH fully normalized. The suppression of gonadotropins in the permethrin group reflects HPG axis inhibition due to impaired testicular feedback. The recovery with quercetin indicates restored Leydig and Sertoli cell communication, further highlighting its systemic protective role. In summary, this study provides integrated ultrastructural, histological, sperm functional, and endocrine evidence that permethrin exposure disrupts testicular homeostasis at multiple levels, while quercetin, as a pure natural compound, confers significant cytoprotection. These results emphasize the role of dietary antioxidants in mitigating the reproductive toxicity of environmental pollutants and support the potential integration of quercetin-based strategies into preventive or adjunctive approaches for male infertility associated with pesticide exposure.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that chronic permethrin exposure induces multifaceted testicular toxicity in male rats, manifested as ultrastructural organelle damage, histological disorganization of seminiferous tubules, significant impairment of sperm motility, viability, and morphology, and profound suppression of serum testosterone, FSH, and LH levels. Concurrent administration of quercetin significantly attenuated these adverse effects, preserving mitochondrial and nuclear integrity, maintaining germinal epithelium architecture, improving functional sperm parameters, and restoring gonadotropin and testosterone concentrations. These findings provide integrated evidence that quercetin exerts comprehensive cytoprotective and endocrine-modulating effects against permethrin-induced reproductive toxicity, likely through its potent antioxidant and anti-apoptotic properties. While minimizing exposure to pyrethroid insecticides remains the primary preventive strategy, quercetin represents a promising natural adjunct for mitigating pesticide associated male infertility.
