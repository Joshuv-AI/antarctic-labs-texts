Physicochemical and Antibacterial Properties of a Mussel-Inspired Chlorinated Monomer Modified Dental Resin Matrix
Authors: Canwen Zhang, Jiayi Huang, Mengyu Huang, Yu Zhang, Haoyu Jiang, Manling Chen, Peining Liang, Cynthia Kar Yung Yiu, Kang Li
Published: 2026

Introduction

As a complex multifactorial oral disease, dental caries leads to the continuous demineralization and destruction of hard dental tissues, a process primarily driven by the metabolic acids of cariogenic biofilms.1 Statistical data indicates that the prevalence of dental caries among 12-year-old children ranges from 27% to 64%, while in adults it ranges from 26% to 83%.2 The occurrence of dental caries can also cause serious problems such as tooth impairment, masticatory dysfunction, pain, and aesthetic concerns.3 Currently, composite resin filling therapy is predominantly employed in clinical practice for the treatment of dental caries. Nevertheless, existing commercial resin systems lack antibacterial properties, enabling bacteria to infiltrate inside the resin-dentin interface, forming biofilm and resulting in acid production.4 This leads to interface damage and resin restoration detachment, with a 5-year failure rate approaching 20% and annual economic losses exceeding 5 billion dollars.5 Therefore, improving resin systems to enhance their antibacterial efficacy holds significant clinical relevance and research value.

Mussels are prevalent mollusks in the ocean. They excrete mussel protein via their byssus threads, establishing stable and long-lasting wet adhesive connections with rocks in the high-salinity and impact-susceptible humid marine environment.6,7 Motivated by the wet adhesion mechanism of mussels, several studies have endeavoured to utilize mussel-inspired compounds for dentin bonding in humid conditions to enhance the service lifespan of resin restorations.

Li et al8 developed a novel mussel-inspired monomer, namely dopamine methacrylamide (DMA), and found that pretreatment of dentin surfaces with DMA can effectively extend the durability of resin-dentin interface and inhibit the activity of matrix metalloproteinases in situ. Hu et al9 synthesized another mussel-inspired resin monomer, namely catechol-lysine-methacrylate (CLM). The results revealed that CLM could enhance the chemical stability of collagen and improve its resistance to enzymatic degradation, thus enhancing both immediate and aged bonding performance. Wu et al10 evaluated the bonding performance of oxidized N-2-(3,4-dihydroxylphenyl) acrylamide (OX-DAA), found that OX-DAA not only could interact with collagen to induce remineralization but also enhance the durability of resin-dentin interface. All these findings highlighted the immense clinical application potential of mussel-inspired compounds in dentin bonding.

However, these previously reported mussel-inspired monomers (such as DMA and CLM) primarily function as adhesion promoters and possess limited intrinsic antibacterial activity.8, 9, 10 Consequently, their independent applicability in biofilm-rich oral environments remains in doubt, as they cannot prevent cariogenic bacterial adhesion or secondary caries formation. Moreover, while most current research applies these compounds merely as pretreatment primers for dentin bonding, their direct incorporation into the bulk adhesive resin matrix and the subsequent effects on the physicochemical network remain unexplored.

Triclosan (2,4,4-trichloro-2-hydroxybenzyl ether) is a broad-spectrum antibacterial agent, with chlorine serving as its primary antibacterial component.11 It is widely used in oral hygiene products.12,13 and disrupts bacterial membranes, leading to bacterial demise at high concentrations.14,15 Additionally, triclosan encapsulated in nanocapsules or loaded onto chitosan was added to adhesive resins, resulting in reliable antimicrobial activity while maintaining interfacial stability.16 The structural characteristics of triclosan highlight that strategic chlorination of organic rings can significantly endow materials with potent antibacterial efficacy.

Therefore, to endow existing mussel-inspired compounds with antibacterial properties while preserving their polymerization capability within resin matrix, this study designed a novel biomimetic antibacterial monomer, designated as N-(2-chloro-4,5-dihydroxyphenethyl) methacrylamide (Cl-DMA). Cl-DMA was specifically developed to address the functional deficiencies of earlier monomers like DMA and CLM by substituting hydrogen atoms on the catechol benzene ring with chlorine, inspired by the antibacterial mechanism of triclosan. This molecular engineering establishes Cl-DMA as a dual-functional monomer that concurrently combines adhesive catechol chemistry with antimicrobial activity, distinguishing it from previously reported catechol-based monomers. Our previous investigations revealed that utilizing Cl-DMA solely as a dentin priming agent yielded substantial improvements in interfacial bond longevity and effectively suppressed Streptococcus mutans colonization. Furthermore, it presented a favourable safety profile for human dental pulp stem cells relative to standard Bis-GMA.17 Building upon these well-established safety and bonding profiles, the current study advanced its application by directly incorporating Cl-DMA into an experimental resin matrix based on a Bis-GMA/TEGDMA/HEMA comonomer system. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the effects of Cl-DMA on the physicochemical properties, antibacterial activity, and cytocompatibility of the modified resin, and to identify the optimal loading concentration for subsequent functional validation.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Briefly, the targeted Cl-DMA monomer was successfully prepared via the room-temperature halogenation of DMA utilizing N-chlorosuccinimide within a trifluoroacetic acid solvent system, followed by established extraction and purification steps, and the reaction process is shown in Figure 1.17 All the reagents were purchased from Aladdin. The structure of the synthesized compound was verified using attenuated total reflectance Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), proton nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H-NMR), and high-resolution mass spectrometry.

Schematic diagram of the Cl-DMA synthesis steps.

Bisphenol A diglycidylmethacrylate (Bis-GMA), triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (TEGDMA), 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA), camphorquinone (CQ), and ethyl-4-dimethylaminobenzoate (EDMAB) were purchased from Aladdin. The S. mutans strain (ATCC 700610) was acquired from Bai Ou Bowei Biotechnology.

Preparation of experimental resin samples

A baseline resin blend was formulated to serve as the control (0 wt%), comprising 35 wt% Bis-GMA, 40 wt% TEGDMA, and 24 wt% HEMA, alongside 0.5 wt% of both CQ and EDMAB as the photoinitiator system.18,19 Cl-DMA was subsequently incorporated into this base matrix at specified mass fractions of 1, 3, and 5 wt%. To ensure a homogeneous molecular distribution, the mixtures were magnetically stirred in a light-proof environment for 12 h. Solid specimens were fabricated according to ISO 4049 guidelines,20 either as discs (15 × 1 mm) or rectangular bars (25 × 2 × 2 mm), and photopolymerized using a commercial LED device (1200 mW/cm2, CuringPen, Eighteeth). Prior to testing, any surface irregularities were eliminated by wet-grinding the samples with 600-grit silicon carbide paper.

Physicochemical properties of Cl-DMA-modified resin matrix

The surface hydrophilicity of the resin discs (n = 6) was determined via static water contact angle measurements (LSA 100, LAUDA Scientific) utilizing a 5 μL droplet, following sequential surface polishing up to 0.5 μm with alumina suspension. For water sorption and solubility evaluations, discs (n = 6) were desiccated to a constant initial mass (M1), incubated in 10 mL of double-distilled water at 37°C for 1 week, weighed immediately to record the saturated mass (M2), and finally redesiccated to a constant mass (M3). The indices of water sorption and solubility were calculated following ISO 4049 guidelines.

The degree of conversion (DC) was monitored via FTIR spectroscopy (Alpha II, Bruker) by calculating the intensity ratio of the polymerizable aliphatic C=C bonds (1638 cm−1) against the stable aromatic C=C reference peak (1608 cm−1) before and after a 20-second light irradiation. The flexural strength and elastic modulus were evaluated via a three-point bending setup (ElectroPlus E3000, Instron) employing a 1.0 mm/min crosshead speed on bar-shaped specimens (25 × 2 × 2 mm). Immediately after fabrication, six specimens from each group were tested to obtain the baseline (0 day) mechanical properties. To evaluate long-term mechanical stability, an additional set of six specimens per group was immersed in 10 mL of double-distilled water at 37°C for 28 days, after which they were blotted dry and tested using the same three-point bending protocol.

Cl-DMA release profile and kinetics

To track the elution dynamics of Cl-DMA, pairs of resin discs from each experimental group were immersed in 10 mL of deionized water and maintained in a shaking incubator (100 rpm, 37°C) in triplicate. At predefined time intervals (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 7, and 14 days), 4 mL of the release medium was collected and immediately replenished with 4 mL of fresh deionized water to maintain a constant total volume (10 mL). After filtering the aliquots through a 0.22 μm PES membrane, exact monomer concentrations were quantified utilizing an HPLC system (Ultimate 3000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) monitored at 289 nm. The mobile phase comprised methanol and 0.4% aqueous formic acid flowing at 1 mL/min. To quantify the released monomer, a calibration curve was established using standard Cl-DMA solutions with concentrations ranging from 0.00025 to 0.0025 mg/mL (R2 > 0.99). The peak area at 289 nm was used to calculate the Cl-DMA concentration in the release medium at each time point.

To evaluate the release mechanism, the cumulative release data were fitted to the Higuchi model (Qt = KHt1/2) and Korsmeyer–Peppas model (ln(Mt/M∞) = ln(KKP) + n ln(t)), where Qt or Mt is the cumulative release amount at time t, KH and KKP are the release rate constants, and n is the diffusional exponent indicating the release mechanism.21,22

In vitro antimicrobial assessments

Following UV sterilization, the formulated resin discs were exposed to S. mutans (adjusted to approximately 107 CFU/mL) in 12-well microplates. The culture medium utilized was BHI broth supplemented with 2 wt% sucrose to encourage robust biofilm formation. After a 24 h anaerobic incubation at 37°C, both the surrounding planktonic suspensions and the surface-adherent biofilms were harvested. The biofilms were physically disrupted into homogeneous suspensions via a combination of ultrasonic agitation (50 W, 30 seconds) and vigorous vortexing. Bacterial viability was subsequently determined by standard serial dilution and CFU counting on agar plates.

To further corroborate metabolic activity, intact biofilms on the resin surfaces were incubated with CCK-8 reagent in the dark for 1 hour, and the optical density was recorded at 450 nm. Agar diffusion tests were conducted by placing sterilized samples onto preinoculated BHI agar plates, visually checking for the presence of inhibition halos after 24 hours. For structural visualization, glutaraldehyde-fixed and ethanol-dehydrated biofilm specimens were gold-sputtered and examined via scanning electron microscopy (Apreo S, Thermo Scientific). Additionally, the spatial distribution of viable and nonviable cells within the biofilm architecture was distinguished by treating the samples with a Live/Dead staining kit for 20 minutes and capturing Z-stack images through confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, FV3000, Olympus). The confocal images were analysed by BioImageL software (Faculty of Odontology, Malmo University) to yield the data of live/dead bacterial distribution.

Cytotoxicity assay

The potential cytotoxicity of the modified resin matrices was evaluated against L929 murine fibroblasts. Sample eluates were prepared by immersing the sterilized discs in complete cell culture medium (MEM containing 10% horse serum) for 24 hours, strictly maintaining a standard extraction ratio of 1 cm2/mL. Fibroblasts seeded in 96-well plates (1 × 104 cells/well) were subsequently treated with these specific extracts. Relative cellular metabolic viability was quantitatively evaluated utilizing the CCK-8 protocol after 1, 3, and 5 days of exposure. Moreover, cell membrane integrity was morphologically validated after 5 days of coculture employing a fluorescent Live/Dead assay. Three random fields of view per sample were captured under CLSM, and the live-to-total cell ratio was calculated utilizing ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All statistical processing was executed utilizing SPSS software (version 20.0, IBM). Depending on the homogeneity of variance across the collected data, either a one-way ANOVA coupled with the SNK posthoc test or the Games-Howell test was applied to distinguish statistical differences among groups. The significance threshold was strictly defined at P < .05.

Results

Characterization of Cl-DMA

Figure 2 summarizes the spectral data verifying the successful synthesis of the target functional monomer. The attenuated total reflectance-FTIR spectrum (Figure 2A) exhibits a distinct absorption band at 3373.7 cm−1, characteristic of the phenolic hydroxyl (O-H) stretching within the catechol moiety. Furthermore, the presence of the amide linkage is confirmed by the N–H and C=O vibrational peaks at 3148.4 and 1648.5 cm−1, respectively. Crucially, a new stretch at 805.1 cm−1 unambiguously indicates the successful grafting of the C–Cl bond onto the aromatic ring.

The FTIR (A), 1H NMR (B), and HRMS (C) characterization of Cl-DMA.

Proton NMR analysis (Figure 2B) provided further structural validation. The spectrum displays singlet signals at 9.19 and 9.08 ppm, corresponding to the hydroxyl protons adjacent to the aliphatic ring, alongside signals at 7.97 to 7.99 ppm assigned to the methylene-adjoined -NH protons. The emergence of these diagnostic peaks confirms the formation of the Cl-DMA structure. Finally, high-resolution mass spectrometry data (Figure 2C) revealed a definitive peak at m/z 256.0715 representing [M + H]+, which closely matches the theoretical calculation (256.0662, Δ = 0.0053 Da), conclusively proving the successful derivation of Cl-DMA.

Figure 5 plots the temporal leaching dynamics of the incorporated monomer over a 14-day window in an aqueous environment. The elution trajectory was characterized by an acute initial burst within the first 48 hours, smoothly transitioning into a steady, prolonged release phase (Figure 5A). Total cumulative release mass scaled proportionally with the initial monomer loading concentration (Figure 5B). When the cumulative release was expressed as a percentage of the total incorporated Cl-DMA (Figure 5C), the 14-day cumulative release percentages were 1.12% for the 1 wt% group, 2.20% for the 3 wt% group, and 1.50% for the 5 wt% group. Notably, although the 5 wt% group exhibited the highest absolute release mass (0.299 mg at 14 days), its percentage release was lower than that of the 3 wt% group, which is mathematically expected due to the normalization by the larger initial monomer reservoir in the 5 wt% formulation. Importantly, the cumulative release percentage profiles of all three groups (Figure 5C) exhibited similar overall shapes – rapid initial release followed by a gradual plateau – which is consistent with a diffusion-controlled mechanism. As detailed in Table, the release profiles demonstrated excellent linearity when fitted to both the Higuchi model (R2: 0.8904-0.9954) and the Korsmeyer–Peppas model (R2: 0.9302-0.9963). Crucially, the calculated diffusional exponents (n) for all modified matrices fell below the 0.45 threshold, confirming that the monomer transport mechanism is strictly governed by Fickian diffusion.

The antibacterial properties of Cl-DMA-modified resin matrix

The representative plate images of the planktonic bacterial suspension and the biofilm are presented in Figure 6A. The corresponding CFU counting results for the planktonic and biofilm states are presented in Figure 6B, C, respectively. For the planktonic state, the 5 wt% group showed a minor 0.3-log10 reduction in planktonic CFU compared with the control, while no significant differences were observed among the different Cl-DMA concentration groups (Figure 6B). In the biofilm state, all Cl-DMA-modified resin groups achieved an over 2.5-log10 reduction in viable bacteria, indicating significant bactericidal activity, while no significant differences were found among the various Cl-DMA concentration groups (Figure 6C).

Antibacterial results of Cl-DMA-modified resin. Parts (a-d) represent the control, 1, 3, and 5 wt% Cl-DMA groups, respectively. (A) The representative images of the CFU plating for both bacterial suspension and bacterial biofilm; (B) quantification of planktonic CFU; (C) quantification of biofilm CFU, showing a >2.5-log10 reduction in viable bacteria for all Cl-DMA-modified groups compared to the control; (D) CCK-8 metabolic activity assay; (E) inhibition zone test; (F) SEM observation of bacterial biofilm; (G) the CLSM images (2D overlay projections) of Streptococcus mutans biofilms and the corresponding biomass distribution of accumulated total, live and dead bacteria along the Z stack.

The CCK-8 assay indicated a concentration-dependent decrease in the proportion of viable bacteria with increasing concentrations of Cl-DMA, showing statistical significance (P < .05) (Figure 6D). This result is consistent with scanning electron microscopy observations (Figure 6F), which revealed a progressive reduction in the density and quantity of adhered bacterial biofilm as the Cl-DMA concentration increased. No apparent inhibition zones were observed around the resin discs in both the control group and the Cl-DMA groups (Figure 6E).

The live/dead bacterial staining results demonstrated a concentration-dependent effect of Cl-DMA. As its concentration increased, the proportion of dead bacteria rose significantly from 0.25% to 16.29%. In parallel, the total biomass of bacterial biofilms exhibited a sharp decrease, declining from nearly 200,000 μm3 in the control group to approximately 10,000 μm3 in the 1 wt% and 3 wt% Cl-DMA groups, and further dropping to around 2000 μm3 in the 5 wt% Cl-DMA group. Overall, the total bacterial biomass was reduced by close to 95%.

Cytotoxicity of Cl-DMA-modified resin matrix

The relative cell viabilities of L929 fibroblasts cultured with resin extracts for 1, 3, and 5 days are presented in Figure 7A. The cell viability decreased in a dose-dependent manner with increasing Cl-DMA concentrations. Across all observation periods, the 1 wt% and 3 wt% Cl-DMA groups maintained average cell viabilities ranging from 84.61% to 95.81% and 72.17% to 78.03%, respectively, both of which remained above the acceptable biosafety threshold (70%). However, the incorporation of 5 wt% Cl-DMA led to pronounced cytotoxicity, with the metabolic viabilities dropping to approximately 40.46% to 54.56%, representing a significant reduction compared to the control group.

To further evaluate cell membrane integrity, live/dead staining was quantitatively analysed (Figure 7B) and observed via CLSM (Figure 7C) after 5 days of incubation. Interestingly, the quantitative image analysis revealed that the membrane-intact cell viability (live cell ratio) remained exceptionally high (>90%) across all groups, with no significant differences observed even in the 5 wt% group (P > .05) (Figure 7B). Morphologically, the control, 1 wt%, and 3 wt% groups exhibited a dense, well-spread morphology with overwhelmingly green fluorescence. Despite the severe decline in metabolic activity detected by the CCK-8 assay, the 5 wt% group still maintained a relatively high degree of cell confluence, presenting predominantly green fluorescence with only a marginal increase in dead cells (Figure 7C).

Discussion

Despite the widespread use of mussel-inspired compounds in dentin bonding, their potential to inhibit cariogenic biofilms remains relatively limited due to their modest intrinsic antimicrobial efficacy.8, 9, 10 Therefore, inspired by the broad-spectrum antibacterial activity of triclosan,23, 24, 25 we successfully synthesized Cl-DMA by substituting hydrogen atoms on the catechol ring of DMA with chlorine (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Our findings demonstrate that while the incorporation of Cl-DMA imparts robust antibacterial properties, it simultaneously exerts a dose-dependent influence on the physicochemical stability and biocompatibility of the resin matrix.

The water sorption and solubility results revealed a progressive decrease in water sorption alongside a concurrent increase in solubility with higher Cl-DMA concentrations (Figure 4A, B). This observation might appear paradoxical, as water diffusion is typically the prerequisite for monomer leaching. However, this phenomenon is physically sound considering the dual role of Cl-DMA. On one hand, the chlorinated catechol groups enhance the overall hydrophobicity of the matrix, creating a steric and electronic barrier that retards bulk water diffusion into the deep resin network. Interestingly, despite this intrinsic hydrophobicity of the monomer, the surface contact angle remained unaffected across all groups (Figure 3). This indicates that the macroscopic surface energy is still predominantly governed by the bulk Bis-GMA/TEGDMA/HEMA cross-linked network, ensuring that the intrinsic bacterial adhesion propensity of the resin surface remains physically unaltered.26 On the other hand, the catechol group acts as a potent polymerization inhibitor by scavenging oxygen-derived radicals.27 This was evidenced by the significantly reduced DC in the 5 wt% group, with all groups remaining below 50%, suggesting the presence of a gel phase within the polymer network.28 This incompletely cross-linked network harbours a substantial pool of unreacted, leachable monomers, which directly contributes to the elevated solubility observed in the Cl-DMA-modified groups, particularly at 5 wt% (9.43 µg/mm3).

It is important to note that ISO 4049:2019 specifies water sorption (≤40 µg/mm3) and solubility (≤7.5 µg/mm3) limits for polymer-based restorative materials (typically filled composites and luting cements)20; our unfilled experimental resin matrix does not fall within this scope, and these strict limits are not directly applicable. Nevertheless, the elevated solubility, combined with the gel phase that provides preferential pathways for water ingress, facilitates hydrolytic degradation over time – a concern confirmed by the significant decline in mechanical properties after 28 days of water ageing (Figure 4D, E). Therefore, this represents an inherent limitation of the current unfilled formulation that should be addressed in future optimization studies, for instance by exploring alternative photoinitiator systems, adjusting the proportion of different resin monomers, or incorporating filler particles to improve conversion efficiency and long-term stability.

The 28-day water ageing experiment demonstrated that all groups, including the control, experienced a significant decline in flexural strength and elastic modulus after ageing. This overall reduction is consistent with the known hydrolytic degradation of Bis-GMA/TEGDMA/HEMA-based polymer networks, which are susceptible to water sorption and ester bond cleavage over time.29 Notably, although the 5 wt% group exhibited the lowest immediate mechanical properties, no significant differences were detected among the four groups after ageing. This suggests that the hydrolytic degradation of the base resin matrix may represent the dominant factor governing long-term mechanical performance, and that Cl-DMA incorporation up to 5 wt% does not further exacerbate degradation beyond that of the unmodified resin. Nevertheless, the substantial decline in mechanical properties across all groups highlights a critical limitation of the current unfilled resin formulation. In clinical applications, dental resins are typically reinforced with filler particles, which could substantially improve both immediate and aged mechanical performance. Future studies should therefore evaluate the Cl-DMA-modified resin in a filled composite or adhesive formulation, incorporating long-term ageing protocols to better simulate the complex oral environment.

The quantitative release profile of Cl-DMA further supports this localized leaching mechanism, demonstrating a highly predictable sustained-release behaviour. Mathematical fitting using the Korsmeyer–Peppas model yielded diffusional exponents (n)≤0.45 for all modified groups (Table), strictly defining the transport mechanism as Fickian diffusion driven by concentration gradients.30 The pronounced initial burst release observed within the first 48 hours is attributed to the rapid dissolution of these unreacted Cl-DMA monomers located at or near the resin surface. From a clinical perspective, this Fickian-driven profile may offer practical advantages. The early burst release provides an immediate localized antibacterial surge to eradicate residual bacteria during the restoration procedure, while the subsequent sustained release maintains a long-term inhibitory microenvironment without prematurely exhausting the active agent or totally compromising the structural integrity of the resin matrix.

The antimicrobial outcomes of this study suggest a dual-action mechanism: a dominant contact-killing effect supplemented by a localized release-killing effect. The lack of distinct inhibition zones around the resin discs is consistent with a contact-active surface, where the immobilized chlorinated catechol groups disrupt bacterial membranes upon direct contact. This mechanism explains the robust >95% reduction in biofilm biomass across all groups. However, the HPLC release data clearly demonstrate that a fraction of unreacted Cl-DMA is eluted from the matrix, particularly during the initial 48-hour burst phase. This localized release is likely responsible for the mild inhibitory effect observed against planktonic bacteria in the 5 wt% group (Figure 6B), as the higher monomer loading transiently elevated the local concentration above the minimum inhibitory threshold.31 Therefore, the antibacterial activity is best described as a synergistic combination of contact-dominant and localized release-based mechanisms.32 This contact-dominant antibacterial mode may be advantageous for dental resin formulations, as it potentially avoids excessive systemic leaching of antibacterial agents, reduces the risk of bacterial resistance, and exerts targeted bactericidal effects at the resin-dentin interface where biofilm-induced secondary caries frequently occurs. However, these potential benefits require further validation in in vivo models.

While the incorporation of Cl-DMA imparted robust contact-antibacterial properties to the resin matrix, the cytotoxicity evaluations revealed a dose-dependent effect on L929 fibroblasts. According to the ISO 10993-5 guidelines, both the 1 wt% and 3 wt% Cl-DMA groups exhibited acceptable biocompatibility, with cell viabilities consistently remaining well above the 70% threshold. The pronounced cytotoxicity observed solely in the 5 wt% group is highly correlated with the HPLC release kinetics and water solubility data.

A noteworthy phenomenon in this high-concentration group is the profound decoupling between its metabolic activity (Figure 7A) and membrane integrity (Figure 7B, C). While the quantitative CCK-8 assay unmasked a severe ∼50% reduction in mitochondrial dehydrogenase activity, the quantitative live/dead analysis definitively demonstrated that over 90% of the cells in the 5 wt% group maintained intact cell membranes, showing no statistical difference from the control group (Figure 7B).

This dual-assay paradox suggests that the cytotoxicity observed at 5 wt% may be primarily cytostatic rather than immediately cytocidal. Although our previous study demonstrated the fundamental safety of the Cl-DMA molecule as a dentin primer,17 its incorporation into the bulk resin at an excessively high concentration (5 wt%) leads to a massive initial monomer burst release. One possible hypothesis is that these leached methacrylamide monomers, alongside the reactive chlorinated catechol groups, induce severe cellular oxidative stress.33 This stress may force fibroblasts into a state of cell cycle arrest and metabolic dormancy before triggering acute cell membrane lysis. Visually and structurally, the cells remain attached and intact, creating an illusion of high biocompatibility, yet their metabolic functions are largely halted. However, we emphasize that this remains a hypothesis based on our current observations, and direct mechanistic studies, including ROS analysis, apoptosis assays, and mitochondrial function assessment, are required to confirm the exact mode of cytotoxicity.34 This finding underscores the importance of combining both structural imaging and quantitative metabolic assays to accurately establish the biosafety threshold. Based on these in vitro observations, restricting the functional monomer loading to ≤3 wt% appears prudent for this experimental formulation; however, the optimal concentration for clinical use should be further validated in in vivo settings.

Although this study successfully synthesized Cl-DMA and demonstrated its significant contact antibacterial effect at 3 wt% loading without compromising immediate physical, mechanical, and biocompatibility properties of experimental resin matrix, several limitations should be noted. First, all experiments were performed under short-term in vitro conditions. Although the 28-day water ageing experiment unmasked a universal decline in mechanical properties, long-term ageing evaluation is necessary to correlate chronic material degradation with long-term antibacterial efficacy. It is important to note that the antibacterial activity was evaluated only after 24 hours of biofilm formation. While the HPLC release data suggest a sustained release profile over 14 days, the corresponding long-term antibacterial efficacy, particularly against mature biofilms, remains to be demonstrated. Therefore, our current findings should be interpreted as proof-of-concept evidence for the short-term antibacterial potential of the Cl-DMA-modified resin, and caution must be exercised when extrapolating these data to long-term clinical scenarios. Secondly, the antibacterial evaluation was restricted to a monospecies S. mutans biofilm model. Since oral biofilms are polymicrobial and structurally complex, the current model does not fully reflect the dynamic clinical oral environment.35 Thirdly, although our previous study demonstrated that Cl-DMA used as a dentin primer significantly enhanced resin-dentin bond durability,17 the current strategy directly incorporates the monomer into the bulk resin matrix. This modification could substantially alter polymerization kinetics, monomer conversion, and interfacial interactions; thus, the previous priming bonding data cannot be directly extrapolated to the current system. The absence of an active resin-dentin bonding performance evaluation in the present study is a limitation that should be addressed in subsequent investigations.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this short-term in vitro study, incorporating up to 3 wt% Cl-DMA effectively endows the resin formulation with robust antibacterial properties against S. mutans while maintaining acceptable baseline physicochemical and biological performance. However, the significant decline in mechanical properties after 28-day water ageing indicates that the current unfilled resin formulation requires further optimization. The 3 wt% Cl-DMA formulation warrants further investigation, including long-term ageing, bonding performance evaluation, and multispecies biofilm models, to assess its potential for clinical application.
