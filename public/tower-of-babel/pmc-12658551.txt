Prevalence and impact of adverse childhood experiences and extrafamilial violence: a representative survey of adults in Northern Ireland
Authors: Colm Walsh, Lisa Bunting, Gavin Davidson, Ciaran Mulholland, Claire McCartan, Nicola Doherty, Mark Shevlin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAdverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are linked to poor outcomes in adulthood, but extrafamilial violence (EFV), such as bullying, community and collective violence, remains relatively under-researched. This study aimed to estimate the prevalence of both the commonly measured ACEs and EFV in Northern Ireland (NI), a postconflict region and assess the association with physical and psychological outcomes.MethodsA representative sample of 1200 adults completed a standardised assessment of childhood adversity (WHO Adverse Childhood Experiences International Questionnaire) covering 13 ACEs. Logistic regressions assessed the association between high ACE exposure (4+vs 0) and adult mental and physical health, health behaviours and service use, adjusting for age, gender and deprivation. Adjusted ORs (AORs), population attributable fractions and additional variance explained were reported.ResultsWhen EFVs were included alongside commonly measured ACEs, 60.0% (n=719) of the NI sample reported at least one ACE and 17.6% (n=212) reported four or more. EFVs were independently associated with poor mental health outcomes, including post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)/complex PTSD (CPTSD) (AOR=14.8, 95% CI (6.5, 33.8)) and anxiety (AOR=7.5, 95% CI 4.9 to 11.4), contributing up to 18.3% (n=41) of anxiety cases. EFVs were also linked to substance use, chronic pain and frequent health service contact. While the most commonly measured ACEs predicted involvement in the justice system, EFVs significantly improved model fit for most health outcomes.ConclusionsEFV is a common and impactful form of childhood adversity. Including EFVs in ACE models improves predictive accuracy, particularly for mental health. ACE research and policy frameworks should be broadened to reflect the added burden of EFV.Keywords: Mental Health, Violence, Adolescent, Public Health

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are associated with poor adult health and behaviour outcomes. Most ACE research has focused on intrafamilial adversity, most commonly measured by the core 10 items (C-10), with limited attention to extrafamilial violence (EFV) (community and collective violence). This is particularly salient in conflict-affected regions. The independent contribution of EFV to long-term outcomes remains underexplored.

Introduction

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) refer to a collection of potentially traumatic events experienced during childhood but remembered into adulthood.1 While research on child maltreatment has spanned more than 50 years,2 the seminal ACE study by Felitti et al3—later replicated in large population samples by Merrick et al4—highlighted the prevalence of childhood adversity and its association with adverse health and psychological outcomes in adulthood in the USA. The growth in ACE studies has been instructive, providing a shared language5 that has deepened understanding of the range of harms children may experience and their physical and psychological sequelae.6 Although estimates vary across contexts and research designs,7 the pooled global prevalence of exposure to at least one ACE is approximately 60%, and 16.1% for exposure to four or more ACEs.2 In the EU, the prevalence of four or more ACEs is estimated at 5.6%.8

Understanding the prevalence of ACEs is vital for prevention, given their consistent link with a wide range of negative outcomes.2 9 Felitti et al3 reported a dose-response relationship between cumulative ACEs and health outcomes, with four or more ACEs found to be an important level that was associated with elevated risk (see also Hughes et al10). A consistent research finding is that individuals reporting four or more ACEs are significantly more likely to develop diagnosable mental health conditions.11 12 Each additional ACE is associated with a 12.9% increase in the risk of multimorbidity,9 and each point on the ACE score adds a corresponding increase in the odds of justice system contact.13

In addition to more reliable prevalence estimates, recent research has increasingly examined mechanisms underlying the ACE–outcome relationships. Meta-analytical findings show that certain adversities, particularly violence-related ones, are more harmful14; some groups, such as females, are at greater risk of specific ACEs15; and that outcomes differ even among individuals exposed to the same events, with deprivation emerging as a key moderator.2 Despite two decades of progress, important gaps remain.

Violence is among the most common adverse experiences in childhood.16 17 While much of this occurs within the family,18 a significant share also occurs in extrafamilial settings.19 20 A recent study by the Youth Endowment Fund21 involving 10 000 children and young people in England and Wales found that 20% had been exposed to extrafamilial violence (EFV) in the past year. As with familial ACEs,22 EFV often overlaps with other forms of adversity: 65% of victims report polyvictimisation.18 These rates are likely even higher in conflict-affected settings.19 23 24

During what was the longest running conflict in Europe, as many as 30% of the population had exposure to conflict-related bereavement and/or injury in Northern Ireland (NI),25 with some research suggesting elevated rates of mental health disorders, particularly stress-related symptoms, compared with populations in non-conflict-related areas.26 27 Further, there is some evidence that during childhood, individuals may have been exposed to a range of adversities, inside (maltreatment and abuse) and outside of the family context (EFV, such as peer bullying, community violence and collective violence).20

However, a lack of reliable estimates on the prevalence of EFV in such contexts limits our understanding of children’s experiences and the comparative impact of different exposures.27 Based on the only large nationally representative study of lifetime adversity in the adult population of conflict-affected NI,28 the present study aimed to: (1) estimate the prevalence of individual ACEs, ACE domains and cumulative ACE exposures in the adult population; (2) assess the association between high (4+) ACE exposure and a range of mental health, physical health, health behaviour, criminal justice and health service related outcomes and (3) examine the additional impact of community violence above and beyond that attributable to ACEs using population attributable fractions (PAFs). In the analysis and discussion, we differentiate between familial adversities, most commonly measured via the Core-10 ACEs items, and extrafamilial adversities, which include collective and community violence. This study is also important given the historic and current high levels of social and economic deprivation relative to other countries of the UK and Europe and its emergence from decades of political violence.

Methods

Context

Data analysed in this study are based on the NI ACEs Survey, which used a stratified random probability sampling design with proportional allocation to assess childhood adversity among 1200 adults aged 18+ years (see Walsh et al28 and the online supplemental material for further details). Of the 2616 addresses issued, 261 were ineligible (eg, vacant or non-residential), leaving 2355 eligible addresses. A total of 1203 interviews were completed, yielding a conservative response rate of 51% of all eligible households. When households where the selected respondent was away during fieldwork or where status could not be confirmed are excluded from the denominator, the achieved response rate was 60%. The final sample provided close alignment with NI population demographics across age, gender, religion, ethnicity and deprivation (see online supplemental table S1), with no statistically significant differences between the population estimates and achieved sample, with regard to age (p=0.88), gender (p=0.48) or ethnicity (0.7).28 Comparisons with the NI Youth Wellbeing Survey showed closely matched ACE prevalence estimates for identically worded items, supporting the external validity of the data (see online supplemental table S2). Given this close alignment, sampling weights were not applied, as their use was unlikely to materially alter prevalence estimates or regression results.

Measures

Deprivation was measured using the Northern Ireland Multiple Deprivation measure, with deciles and quintiles used in analyses (1 being most deprived).29 Childhood adversity was measured using the Adverse Childhood Experiences International Questionnaire (ACE-IQ), adapted for NI.24 The ACE-IQ was developed by consensus via the WHO (2011) to provide a standardised way of capturing a wider set of adversities (abuse; parents; family dysfunction; EFV) via 13 categories, some of which are not routinely captured using the original ACE tool.3 Responses can be dichotomised (1=present; 0=absent), or captured as higher frequency experiences (eg, ‘many times’). The 13-item tool captures abuse, family dysfunction and EFV (community and collective violence).

Mental Health Outcomes–The International Trauma Questionnaire was used to generate a binary variable representing meeting the ICD-11 criteria for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) or complex PTSD (CPTSD).30 The seven-item version of the Warwick Edinburgh Mental Well-being Scale was used to measure mental well-being in adults.31 Scores range between 14 and 70, with scores below 20 used to indicate poor psychological well-being. The PHQ-4 was used as a concurrent measure of anxiety and depression with subscale scores greater than 3 being used to code for caseness.32

Physical health outcomes—Participants were asked if they had any physical health conditions or illnesses that had lasted, or were expected to last, for 12 months or more, and if they had ever been diagnosed with any of a list of 17 common chronic illnesses/diseases. Questions were derived from previous studies.33 34 Health conditions with at least 50 cases were retained in the main analyses (angina, asthma, cancer, chronic pain, type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, insomnia/poor sleep and rheumatoid arthritis), while those with fewer than 50 cases (eg, Alzheimer’s/dementia, ADHD, autism/Asperger’s, COPD, type 1 diabetes, epilepsy, kidney or liver disease, learning disability, stroke and ‘other’ specified conditions) were excluded from reporting due to very low prevalence.

Health behaviours, criminal justice involvement and health service use—Questions taken from the Bellis et al study33 and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention survey were used to assess smoking, drug use, poor diet, physical activity/sedentary behaviour, arrest and incarceration. Problematic substance use was measured using a combination of substances listed in the DUDIT screening tool35 and the NI Wellbeing survey.16 Items taken from the Client Service Receipt Inventory were used to collect service usage history.

Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to examine the prevalence of ACEs across individual ACE types and ACE domains, as well as cumulative ACE exposure in relation to maltreatment and household factors (core ACE items: C-10) and the addition of EFV (EFV+C-10; C-13). Frequencies and percentages were calculated to describe the distribution of ACE exposure, and cross-tabulations were used to assess the association between ACE group exposure and age, gender and deprivation.

Binary logistic regression models were used with a variable representing high ACE exposure (4+ACEs) as a predictor variable and mental health, physical health, health behaviours and healthcare utilisation as outcome variables with age (continuous), gender (dichotomous) and deprivation (continuous) as covariates. The estimates were reported as adjusted ORs (AORs) with 95% CIs.

The contribution of EFV-ACEs to various mental health, physical health, health behaviour and service use outcomes was estimated using PAFs and difference (Δ) in proportion of explained variance from regression models (ΔR²):

To make the results clearer, we looked at mental health outcomes where adversity had the strongest effect. We then calculated the predicted probabilities for problems like anxiety, depression and PTSD for men and women and those in different age groups, comparing those with no childhood adversities to those with four or more. These are presented as percentages and show the absolute differences in risk, making the findings easier to interpret than ORs alone. The age groups were chosen to reflect different levels of exposure to the ‘Troubles’ Age (postconflict (18–29 years), as a child (30–49 years) or as an adult (50+ years))—capturing generational differences in conflict exposure.

See the online supplemental material for a more detailed explanation of these measures, including the formulae used.

Results

Prevalence

Based on the distribution of all 13 ACEs, three in five adults reported at least one ACE (n=719, 60.0%; table 1). Within the parent/household domain, parental separation was the most common (n=194, 24.2%), followed by domestic abuse (n=163, 20.3%), with parental incarceration the least common (n=48, 6.0%). In the maltreatment domain, emotional neglect was most common (n=143, 17.8%), followed by sexual abuse (n=71, 8.8%), with physical neglect lowest (n=48, 6.0%). Within the EFV domain, collective violence was the most common (n=165, 20.5%), followed by bullying (n=76, 9.5%), and then community violence (n=60, 8.7%). Using the most common 10 ACE items (maltreatment and household adversity), 12.8% of respondents (n=154) reported four or more adversities. When EFVs were included, this rose to 17.6% (n=212).

ACEs and outcomes

Mental health: After controlling for age, gender and deprivation, adults with four or more ACEs had significantly higher odds of poor mental health outcomes than those with no ACE exposure. Specifically, they were more likely to report current anxiety (OR 7.48, 95% CI 4.91 to 11.39, χ²(6)=120.52, p<0.001), depression (OR 8.60, 95% CI 5.35 to 13.82, χ²(6)=114.17, p<0.001), poor mental well-being (OR 6.56, 95% CI 3.96 to 10.88, χ²(6)=87.16, p<0.001), an adult mental health diagnosis (OR 9.56, 95% CI 6.21 to 14.72, χ²(6)=175.28, p<0.001) and PTSD/CPTSD (OR 14.76, 95% CI 6.46 to 33.77, χ²(6)=101.30, p<0.001) (table 2).

Physical health: Four or more ACEs were also associated with adverse physical health outcomes. Respondents had greater odds of any ongoing physical health problem (OR 5.39, 95% CI 3.65 to 7.97, χ²(6)=290.71, p<0.001), angina (OR 2.32, 95% CI 1.10 to 4.89, χ²(6)=24.05, p<0.001), asthma (OR 2.75, 95% CI 1.31 to 5.78, χ²(6)=46.20, p<0.001), chronic pain (OR 5.12, 95% CI 2.49 to 10.55, χ²(6)=52.76, p<0.001), insomnia/poor sleep (OR 4.30, 95% CI 2.19 to 8.47, χ²(6)=31.07, p<0.001) and rheumatoid arthritis (OR 2.11, 95% CI 1.12 to 3.99, χ²(6)=32.72, p<0.001). Associations with cancer, diabetes and high blood pressure were not statistically significant (table 2).

Health behaviours: Adults with high ACE exposure also demonstrated greater likelihood of adverse health behaviours. They had higher odds of daily smoking or vaping (OR 2.59, 95% CI 1.79 to 3.75), χ²(6)=89.43, p<0.001), past-year drug use (OR 2.95, 95% CI 1.88 to 4.63, χ²(6)=89.38, p<0.001), drinking above recommended alcohol limits (OR 1.86, 95% CI 1.13 to 3.00, χ²(6)=33.34, p<0.001), low/no physical activity (OR 2.05, 95% CI 1.31 to 3.19, χ²(6)=51.58, p<0.001) and no daily fruit or vegetable consumption (OR 1.64, 95% CI 1.06 to 2.55, χ²(6)=22.70, p<0.001) (table 2).

Criminal justice involvement and health service use: High ACE exposure was strongly associated with criminal justice contact and service use. Adults with four or more ACEs had increased odds of ever being arrested (OR 8.30, 95% CI 4.86 to 14.10, χ²(6)=188.84, p<0.001), attending A&E in the past 3 months (OR 2.92, 95% CI 1.76 to 4.87, χ²(6)=23.30, p<0.001) and contacting their GP three or more times during the same period (OR 2.85, 95% CI 1.70 to 4.78, χ²(6)=27.35, p<0.001). Time spent in hospital was not significantly associated with ACE exposure (table 2).

The contribution of EFV (EFV-ACEs) to outcomes

Although adults with high ACE exposure showed substantially elevated odds of poor mental health outcomes, these individual-level effects translate into more moderate contributions at the population level. EFV ACEs, however, remained strong predictors of mental health outcomes than the most commonly used 10 ACEs (C-10), with higher and more significant ORs across all mental health conditions (see online supplemental table S3 for full model results). EFV accounted for 13.0%–18.3% of mental health cases in the NI population, with the highest contribution observed for anxiety, followed by ever having any mental condition in adulthood (17.5% and then depression (16.9%) (see table 3). Additionally, including EFV ACEs in the model significantly improved predictive accuracy, increasing explained variance (ΔR²) by 12.1%–20.7%. This reflects the fact that, while ACEs and EFV are powerful predictors for those affected, their overall population impact depends on how common such exposures are within the wider community.

EFV played a significant role in some physical health outcomes, although for many the results were non-significant (see online supplemental table S3). EFV ACEs accounted for 16.8% of cases reporting a current physical health problem and explained a high degree of variance (48.8%). They also contributed substantially to chronic pain (10.7%) and made a more modest contribution to insomnia (6.9%) (table 3).

For health behaviours, EFV ORs exceeded C-10 ACEs for smoking/vaping (PAF 10.1%), drug use (4.3%) and high alcohol intake, though drug and alcohol results were only marginally significant. No associations emerged for diet or physical activity. C-10 ACEs predicted arrests more strongly, while EFV showed stronger associations with frequent GP visits and A&E attendance, though PAFs were modest (4.5%–7.2%) (table 3).

A strong adversity-mental health gradient was evident for both women (figure 1) and men (figure 2). Among women with 4+ACEs, rates of anxiety (46.9% vs 10.6%), depression (35.4% vs 6.0%), PTSD/CPTSD (20.7% vs 1.7%) and mental health diagnosis (50.9% vs 9.8%) were far higher than in those with 0 ACEs, with well-being notably lower (27.7% vs 5.5%). Discussion

While overall ACE prevalence in this study aligns with global averages,2 rates in NI are higher than those reported in England and Wales33 and pooled EU estimates.2 8 In our sample, three in five participants reported at least one ACE (n=719, 60.0%), and nearly one in five reported four or more (n=212, 17.6%). Variation in the most commonly reported ACEs may reflect differences in measurement approaches, the limited EU evidence base and inconsistencies in which domains are captured across studies.7

Exposure to EFV was also relatively common yet may be under-recognised due to the dominant focus on familial harm and the normalisation of community violence in conflict-affected contexts.19 Bullying prevalence appeared lower than recent estimates, but this likely reflects the use of a frequency-based measure that captured more persistent exposure. Community violence was lower than in other conflict-affected studies,36 although age-stratified analyses suggest increasing exposure among younger cohorts.28 By contrast, collective violence exceeded both global (15.3%) and Serbian (3.3%) estimates.7 36

As in other contexts, ACE exposure was unequally distributed.33 37 The prevalence of four or more ACEs in the most deprived areas was nearly double that in the least deprived (n=117, 24.4% vs n=39, 12.8%), underlining the role of structural inequalities.4 38 39 This pattern is particularly relevant in NI, where deprivation rates remain elevated.40 41 Although longitudinal evidence is limited,42 findings support the view that poverty is both a cause and consequence of ACEs, suggesting that poverty-reduction initiatives should be designed with explicit ACE-awareness.

Consistent with prior research, increased ACE exposure was strongly associated with poor mental health.10,12 Overall prevalence of PTSD/CPTSD was 6.1% (n=73), close to the 5% rate previously reported among NI adolescents.16 The largest effect was observed for PTSD/CPTSD (OR=14.8), and this is unsurprising given that trauma exposure is a diagnostic entry point for PTSD; high ORs are therefore to be expected, particularly in conflict-affected contexts.19 28 Hughes et al’s meta-analysis reported global estimates for 4+ACEs of 3.7 for anxiety and 4.4 for depression, while our AORs were higher at 7.5 and 8.6, respectively.8 These findings support the expectation that elevated trauma exposure in NI translates into a high burden of mental health problems across the life course, but they may also reflect the broader range of adversities included in the current study, particularly EFV.

Individuals with 4+ACEs also had a significantly increased risk of ongoing physical health issues (5.4 times higher odds, 95% CI (3.65 to 7.97)) and of specific conditions, including angina (2.3 times), insomnia/poor sleep (4.3 times), chronic pain (5.1 times), asthma (2.8 times) and rheumatoid arthritis (2.1 times). These findings are broadly consistent with global estimates of ACE–physical health associations.10

Including EFV ACEs enhanced model explanatory power, consistent with polyvictimisation research.43 44 EFVs accounted for up to 18% of anxiety cases and contributed to 16.3% of reported poor health and 10.1% of chronic pain, with smaller effects on specific conditions. They were also more strongly associated than C-10 ACEs with smoking, drug use and alcohol use, although PAFs were modest (4.9%–6.9%). Interestingly, C-10 ACEs remained stronger predictors of lifetime arrest, despite criminogenic risks being higher among those with EFV. This suggests that contextual and demographic factors—such as social class, gender and community surveillance—may be more important in shaping justice involvement than trauma type alone.45 It is important to note that the high ORs observed for EFV reflect strong individual-level associations with poor outcomes, whereas the PAFs represent their population-level contribution. These two metrics capture different but complementary dimensions of impact: Ors may indicate how strongly exposure increases risk for individuals, while PAFs may account for how widespread that exposure is within the wider population. Consequently, exposures such as EFV can have powerful effects for those who are exposed and yet, at the same time, contribute more modestly to overall population prevalence because they are less common in that population.

Predicted probability analyses showed a clear ACE–mental health gradient across the sample, with some gendered effects. Women tended to have higher probabilities for anxiety, PTSD/CPTSD and lifetime diagnosis, while well-being was similarly reduced for both genders. Age-based comparisons did not reveal a consistent ‘conflict-era’ pattern, suggesting that the elevated risk is not strongly tied to the timing of Troubles exposure. This is consistent with the broader finding that EFV contributes substantially to poor mental health outcomes across the life course, but its effects may be more generalised than age-period-specific.

Limitations

As a retrospective, self-report study, the findings are subject to recall bias and potential under-reporting, particularly for sensitive experiences, such as abuse and violence. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; observed associations reflect correlations, not necessarily temporal or causal pathways. While the sample was representative of the NI population, subgroup analyses (eg, by ethnicity or gender identity) were limited by relatively small numbers. Finally, although the inclusion of EFV expands current ACE frameworks, the operationalisation of EFV may have omitted relevant community-level harms not captured by the ACE-IQ.

Conclusions

ACE research has largely focused on high-income countries. Implications and future directions

These findings underscore the need for integrated, trauma-informed approaches across health, social care and justice sectors in NI. Efforts to reduce poverty and inequality are also likely to reduce ACE prevalence, given the clear social patterning observed. Future research should examine ACE trajectories longitudinally to clarify causal pathways, explore resilience and protective factors alongside adversity, and test interventions that target both familial and community sources of harm. In conflict-affected settings, greater attention to EFV is essential to fully capture the burden of childhood adversity and inform effective prevention.
