Designing Algorithms for Entropic Optimal Transport from an Optimisation Perspective


Abstract
In this work, we develop a collection of novel methods for the entropic-regularised optimal transport problem, which are inspired by existing mirror descent interpretations of the Sinkhorn algorithm used for solving this problem. These are fundamentally proposed from an optimisation perspective: either based on the associated semi-dual problem, or based on solving a non-convex constrained problem over subset of joint distributions. This optimisation viewpoint results in non-asymptotic rates of convergence for the proposed methods under minimal assumptions on the problem structure. We also propose a momentum-equipped method with provable accelerated guarantees through this viewpoint, akin to those in the Euclidean setting. The broader framework we develop based on optimisation over the joint distributions also finds an analogue in the dynamical Schrödinger bridge problem.

1
Introduction

Given two probability distributions
\mu
and
\nu
over
{\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
respectively, the optimal transport (OT) problem concerns finding an
optimal
map that transforms samples from one to another.
The OT problem was originally proposed by Gaspard Monge in the 1780s to address the problem of finding a method to transport resources between a collection of sources and sinks, and was rediscovered in the early 1900s by Hitchcock, Kantorovich, and Koopmans with applications in designing transportation systems, coinciding with the birth of linear programming.
Recent advances in computing resources has renewed interest in the OT problem, both in the design of approximate methods for this problem suited for large-scale settings
(
Peyré and Cuturi 2019
)
, and in the development of a theoretical understanding of its properties
(
Villani 2003
;
Santambrogio 2015
)
.

The “optimality” in the OT problem is defined in terms of a cost function
c:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
The optimal value of the problem results in a notion of discrepancy between
\mu
and
\nu
that complements information-theoretic discrepancy measures like the total variation distance or the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence.
Formally, let
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
be the set of all joint distributions whose marginals are
\mu
and
\nu
.
The Kantorovich formulation of the OT problem is given by the following program:

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\iint c(x,y)\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)=:\mathrm{OT}(\mu,\nu;c)~.

(1)

A notable case is when
c(x,y)=\|x-y\|_{p}^{p}
where
\|\cdot\|_{p}
is the
\ell_{p}
-norm, and this corresponds to the
p
-Wasserstein distance between
\mu
and
\nu
raised to the
p^{th}
power.
The space of probability measures endowed with the
p
-Wasserstein distance takes on a rich Riemannian structure with implications in developing the metric theory of gradient flows
(
Ambrosio et al. 2008
)
.
Wasserstein distances have also found applications in image processing and operations research, which has motivated the design of efficient methods to compute these distances.
In modern machine learning and statistics however, methods based on solving the exact OT problem have not seen widespread use owing to both computational and statistical reasons resulting primarily from the high-dimensional nature of the problems involved.

These bottlenecks can surprisingly be alleviated by adding an entropy regularisation to the OT problem, referred to as the entropic optimal transport (eOT) problem.
More precisely, for a regularisation parameter
\varepsilon>0
, this is defined as

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\iint c(x,y)\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)+\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\mu\otimes\nu)=:\mathrm{OT}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu;c)~.

(2)

Above,
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\mu\otimes\nu)
is the KL divergence between
\pi
and the product distribution
\mu\otimes\nu
.
A popular algorithm for solving this problem is the
Sinkhorn
algorithm
(
Sinkhorn and Knopp 1967
)
, and this was recently popularised by
Cuturi 2013
by demonstrating it as a viable solution for solving the eOT problem over large datasets, and by extension for solving the OT problem approximately.
We refer the reader to
Peyré and Cuturi 2019
for a historical view of the method.
Although
Sinkhorn and Knopp 1967
proved that the method converges asymptotically, the first known non-asymptotic rate of convergence was given by
Franklin and Lorenz 1989
.
They do so by viewing the Sinkhorn algorithm as a matrix scaling method and leverage Hilbert’s projective metric in conjunction with Birkhoff’s theorem.
Other interpretations of the Sinkhorn algorithm have led to both asymptotic and non-asymptotic guarantees; see
Rüschendorf 1995
;
Di Marino and Gerolin 2020
;
Carlier 2022
for instance.
We highlight that
\mathrm{OT}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu;c)
is
biased
relative to
\mathrm{OT}(\mu,\nu;c)
as
\mathrm{OT}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\mu;c)\neq 0
.
To address this, a debiasing strategy is proposed in
Feydy et al. 2019
which results in the
Sinkhorn divergence
which is shown to metrize convergence in law.
In addition to the computational benefits of the Sinkhorn algorithm, the sample complexity to estimate the Sinkhorn divergence using samples from
\mu
and
\nu
scales much better than
\mathrm{OT}(\mu,\nu;c)
for a variety of costs
(
Genevay et al. 2019
;
Mena and Niles-Weed 2019
;
Chizat et al. 2020
)
.

Of relevance to this paper is a more recent interpretation of the Sinkhorn algorithm as an
infinite-dimensional optimisation procedure
, which originated with the mirror descent interpretation of the Sinkhorn algorithm in
Mishchenko 2019
for discrete spaces
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
.
This interpretation has been instrumental in obtaining assumption-free guarantees for the Sinkhorn algorithm and has led to a growing body of literature since its inception
(
Mensch and Peyré 2020
;
Léger 2021
;
Aubin-Frankowski et al. 2022
;
Deb et al. 2023
;
Reza Karimi et al. 2024
)
.

Summary of contributions

In this work, we draw inspiration from this refreshing viewpoint, and design new methods for the eOT problem with provable guarantees.
Notably, these guarantees do not place any specific assumptions on the domains
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
, or on the marginal distributions
\mu
and
\nu
.
A key mathematical object that we consider in ths design is the
semi-dual formulation
of the eOT problem which we describe in more detail in
Section

3
.
The semi-dual problem is a concave unconstrained program, which motivates the use of a steepest ascent procedure and with the Sinkhorn algorithm leads to an abstract class of methods.
Roughly speaking, these methods can be seen as iteratively minimising the discrepancy between
\nu
and the
{\mathcal{Y}}
-marginal of a joint distribution while ensuring the joint distribution maintains a certain form.

We show that these class of methods can be interpreted in a variety of ways as described in
Section

4
.
These interpretations come in handy when analysing a subclass of methods that can be seen as minimising the squared maximum mean discrepancy
(
Gretton et al. 2006
)
.
We find that the
{\mathcal{Y}}
-marginal of the sequence of joint distributions resulting from these methods converges to
\nu
at a rate that scales as
\frac{1}{N}
where
N
is the number of iterations, and consequently leads to an optimal coupling for the eOT problem due to the form of the joint distribution that is maintained.

We next show how we can use principles in finite-dimensional optimisation to adapt to growth conditions of the semi-dual to design other kinds of steepest descent methods, extending the class of methods described a priori for solving the eOT problem in
Section

5.1
.
These also lead to methods that provably optimise the semi-dual (and consequently solving the eOT problem) and converge at a rate that scales
\frac{1}{N}
under minimal assumptions.
We find that one of these methods can be accelerated and converge at a faster rate that scales as
\frac{1}{N^{2}}
under the same assumptions.
We finally discuss a path-space generalisation of the class of methods in
Section

3
for solving the dynamical Schrödinger bridge problem in
Section

5.2
.

Background

Notation

For a set
{\mathcal{Z}}
, the set of probability measures over
{\mathcal{Z}}
is denoted by
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{Z}})
.
Given a distribution
\pi\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}})
,
\pi_{{\mathcal{X}}}
and
\pi_{{\mathcal{Y}}}
are the
{\mathcal{X}}
-marginal and
{\mathcal{Y}}
-marginal respectively i.e., for
A\subseteq{\mathcal{X}},B\subseteq{\mathcal{Y}}
,

\pi_{{\mathcal{X}}}(A)=\int_{A\times{\mathcal{Y}}}\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)~;\quad\pi_{{\mathcal{Y}}}(B)=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}\times B}\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)~.

Given two distributions
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}}),\nu\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{Y}})
, we say that
\pi\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}})
is a coupling of
\mu
and
\nu
if
\pi_{{\mathcal{X}}}=\mu
and
\pi_{{\mathcal{Y}}}=\nu
.
For
\rho\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{Z}})
, we use
\mathrm{d}\rho
to represent its density.
For convenience, if
\rho
has a density w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure, we use
\rho
to also denote its density depending on the setting.

Let
\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{Z}})
be probability measures such that
\rho
is absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\rho^{\prime}
.
The KL divergence between
\rho,\rho^{\prime}
is denoted by
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\|\rho^{\prime}):=\int_{{\mathcal{Z}}}\mathrm{d}\rho\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\rho^{\prime}}
.
For a functional
{\mathcal{F}}:{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{Z}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we call
\delta{\mathcal{F}}(\rho)
its first variation at
\rho\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{Z}})
, and this is the function (up to additive constants) that satisfies
(
Santambrogio 2015
, Def. 7.12)

\langle\delta{\mathcal{F}}(\rho),\chi\rangle=\int_{{\mathcal{Z}}}\delta{\mathcal{F}}(\rho)(z)\mathrm{d}\chi(z)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{{\mathcal{F}}(\rho+h\cdot\chi)-{\mathcal{F}}(\rho)}{h}\qquad\forall~\chi\text{ such that}\int\mathrm{d}\chi(z)=0~.

For a measurable function
f:{\mathcal{Z}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, its
L^{p}
-norm w.r.t.
\rho
is denoted by
\|f\|_{L^{p}(\rho)}
, which is defined as
\left(\int_{{\mathcal{Z}}}|f(z)|^{p}\mathrm{d}\rho(z)\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}
.
When
\rho
is replaced with
{\mathcal{Z}}
as
\|f\|_{L^{p}({\mathcal{Z}})}
, then this is understood to be the
L^{p}
-norm of
f
w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure of
{\mathcal{Z}}
.
For another measurable function
g:{\mathcal{Z}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, the
L^{2}(\rho)
inner product is defined as
\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}=\int_{{\mathcal{Z}}}f(z)g(z)\mathrm{d}\rho(z)
.
As a special case, when the subscript in the norm and inner product are omitted as in
\|\cdot\|
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, then these correspond to the
L^{2}({\mathcal{Z}})
-norm and inner product respectively.
Following the notation in
Rudin 1987
, we use
L^{p}
to also denote the space of measurable functions whose
L^{p}
norm is finite.

2.1
The entropic optimal transport problem

We recall the definition of the eOT problem from
Eq.

2
below. For a lower-semi-continuous cost cost function
c:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, the primal formulation of the entropic optimal transport (abbrev. eOT) problem is given by

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\iint c(x,y)\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)+\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\mu\otimes\nu)=:\mathrm{OT}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu;c)~.

When
\varepsilon=0
, this corresponds to the (canonical) optimal transport problem.
On the other hand, it can be seen that as
\varepsilon\to\infty
, the solution to
\mathrm{OT}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu;c)
tends to the product measure
\mu\otimes\nu
.
The eOT problem is also directly related to the
static Schrödinger bridge problem

(
Léonard 2014
, Def. 2.2)
denoted by
\mathrm{SB}(\mu,\nu;\pi^{\textsf{ref}})
for the reference measure
\pi^{\textsf{ref}}
whose density is
\mathrm{d}\pi^{\textsf{ref}}\propto\exp\left(-\nicefrac{{c}}{{\varepsilon}}\right)\mathrm{d}(\mu\otimes\nu)
and marginals
\mu
and
\nu
.
Then, direct calculation gives

\mathrm{OT}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu;c)=\varepsilon\cdot\Big\{\underbrace{\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi^{\textsf{ref}})}_{\mathrm{SB}(\mu,\nu;\pi^{\textsf{ref}})}-\log Z_{\textsf{ref}}\Big\}~;~~Z_{\textsf{ref}}=\iint\exp\left(-\frac{c(x,y)}{\varepsilon}\right)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}\nu(y)~.

(3)

The above problem is a (strictly) convex minimisation problem since
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is convex, and
\pi\mapsto\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi^{\textsf{ref}})
is strictly convex.
Moreover, under certain regularity conditions, the eOT problem admits a unique solution
\pi^{\star}\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)

(
Léonard 2014
;
Nutz and Wiesel 2022
)
of the form

\mathrm{d}\pi^{\star}(x,y)=\exp\left(\phi^{\star}(y)-\psi^{\star}(x)-\frac{c(x,y)}{\varepsilon}\right)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}\nu(y)

(4)

where
\psi^{\star}
and
\phi^{\star}
are called
Schrödinger potentials
, and the optimal value of the problem is

\mathrm{OT}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu;c)=\varepsilon\cdot\left(\int\phi^{\star}(y)\mathrm{d}\nu(y)-\int\psi^{\star}(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right)~.

While the solution
\pi^{\star}
is unique, the Schrödinger potentials are unique only up to constants, that is, if
\psi^{\star}
and
\phi^{\star}
are Schrödinger potentials, then
\psi^{\star}+\beta
and
\phi^{\star}+\beta
are also Schrödinger potentials for any constant
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
The eOT problem has a dual formulation with zero duality gap, and is
unconstrained
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{OT}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu;c)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\cdot\sup\left\{\int\phi(y)\mathrm{d}\nu(y)-\int\psi(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.-\log\iint\exp\left(\phi(y)-\psi(x)-\frac{c(x,y)}{\varepsilon}\right)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}\nu(y)\right\}~.

(5)

Any solution of the dual problem above corresponds to a pair of Schrödinger potentials and vice versa
(
Nutz 2021
, Thm. 3.2)
.
Note that
\mathrm{OT}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu;c)=\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{OT}_{1}(\mu,\nu;\nicefrac{{c}}{{\varepsilon}})
, and hence without loss of generality, we focus on
\mathrm{OT}_{1}(\mu,\nu;\nicefrac{{c}}{{\varepsilon}})
in the rest of this work.
We denote the objective in the dual form of
\mathrm{OT}_{1}(\mu,\nu;\nicefrac{{c}}{{\varepsilon}})
by
D(\psi,\phi)
and use
\pi^{\star}
to denote the (primal) solution of
\mathrm{OT}_{1}(\mu,\nu;\nicefrac{{c}}{{\varepsilon}})
.

2.2
Related work

Traditional analyses view the Sinkhorn algorithm as either alternating projection on the two marginals
\mu,\nu
or block maximization on the two dual potentials
\psi,\phi
.
These render a linear convergence with a contraction rate of the form
1-e^{-\nicefrac{{\|c\|_{\infty}}}{{\varepsilon}}}
.
An important limitation of this analysis is that the rate becomes
exponentially slower
with growing
\|c\|_{\infty}
or decreasing
\varepsilon
.
Recently, several analyses have focused on the Sinkhorn algorithm in the setting where
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
are discrete spaces
(
Altschuler et al. 2017
;
Lin et al. 2022
;
Dvurechensky et al. 2018
)
.
More relevant to us is where
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
are continuous spaces where many probabilistic approaches have been taken for analyzing the Sinkhorn algorithm.
Most recently,
Chiarini et al. 2024
leverage the stability of optimal plans with respect to the marginals to obtain exponential convergence with unbounded cost for all
\varepsilon>0
, albeit under various sets of conditions on the marginals.
This relaxes the assumptions made in
Chizat et al. 2024
for semi-concave bounded costs while still maintaining a contraction rate that only deteriorates
polynomially
in
\varepsilon
.
We refer the reader to
Chiarini et al. 2024
for a more comprehensive literature review for analyses of the Sinkhorn algorithm.
In summary, the most recent analyses place assumptions on the growth of the cost, decay of the tails of
\mu,\nu
and/or log-concavity, to obtain exponential convergence guarantees.

In contrast, the advantage of taking the optimisation route i.e., viewing the Sinkhorn algorithm as performing infinite-dimensional mirror descent
(
Léger 2021
;
Aubin-Frankowski et al. 2022
;
Reza Karimi et al. 2024
)
is that it provides a guarantee under
minimal
assumptions.
From non-asymptotic guarantee standpoint, these aforementioned works furnish a discrete-time iteration complexity that scales as
\nicefrac{{1}}{{N\varepsilon}}
.
If the costs are additionally assumed to be bounded, then
Aubin-Frankowski et al. 2022
recover a contractive rate reminiscent of the classical Hilbert analysis.
In this work, we achieve the similar rates while significantly expanding the scope of algorithm design and shed more light on the eOT problem, unifying both the primal and dual perspectives.

Mensch and Peyré 2020
gives another mirror descent interpretation of Sinkhorn but the change of variable results in a non-convex objective which is hard to prove convergence for.
There has also been interest in designing alternative algorithms for the eOT problem, among them
Conforti et al. 2023
that designs Wasserstein gradient flow dynamics over the submanifold of
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
which borrow tools from SDEs and PDEs in its analysis.

3
A new class of methods for solving the eOT problem

In this section, we propose a class of methods for solving the eOT problem, termed

Φ

-match
.
As we explain later in this section, this new class of updates is derived by identifying commonalities between the Sinkhorn algorithm and another method that is based on the
semi-dual formulation
associated with the dual eOT problem in
Eq.

5
.
We begin by first introducing this semi-dual problem in
Section

3.1
and present this class of methods in
Section

3.2
.

3.1
The semi-dual problem in eOT

The semi-dual problem was originally discussed in
Genevay et al. 2016
, specifically to motivate the use of stochastic algorithms to solve the eOT problem in the setting where
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
are discrete spaces.
Later work by
Cuturi and Peyré 2018
studies this in more detail, while still primarily focusing on the discrete space setting.

We first define the following operations, in the notation of
Léger 2021
.
Let
\phi\in L^{1}(\nu)
and
\psi\in L^{1}(\mu)
.
Define

\phi^{+}(x):=\log\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\exp\left(\phi(y)-\frac{c(x,y)}{\varepsilon}\right)\mathrm{d}\nu(y)~;~~\psi^{-}(y):=-\log\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\exp\left(\psi(x)+\frac{c(x,y)}{\varepsilon}\right)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)~.

Importantly, from
Eq.

4
we see that any pair of Schrödinger potentials
(\phi^{\star},\psi^{\star})
corresponding to
\mathrm{OT}_{1}(\mu,\nu;\nicefrac{{c}}{{\varepsilon}})
satisfies
\psi^{\star}=(\phi^{\star})^{+}
and
\phi^{\star}=(\psi^{\star})^{-}
; this can be inferred by noting that
\pi^{\star}_{{\mathcal{Y}}}=\nu
and
\pi^{\star}_{{\mathcal{X}}}=\mu
.
Therefore, instead of solving the dual problem
Eq.

5
in two variables
\phi,\psi
, it would be sufficient to solve one of

\sup_{\phi\in L^{1}(\nu)}D(\phi^{+},\phi)\qquad\text{or}\qquad\sup_{\psi\in L^{1}(\mu)}D(\psi,\psi^{-})~.

Without loss of generality, we work with the maximisation problem over
\phi
, and this is referred to as the
semi-dual problem
for eOT.
Additionally, we note that for any
\phi\in L^{1}(\nu)
, the objective
J
of the semi-dual satisfies

J(\phi):=D(\phi^{+},\phi)=\sup_{\psi\in L^{1}(\mu)}D(\psi,\phi)=\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\phi(y)\mathrm{d}\nu(y)-\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\phi^{+}(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,.

(6)

The fact that
D(\phi^{+},\phi)=\sup_{\psi\in L^{1}(\mu)}D(\psi,\phi)
can be derived by using the fact that
\psi\mapsto D(\psi,\phi)
is concave and its first variation at
\phi^{+}
is
0
.
This leads to viewing the semi-dual problem for eOT as explicitly eliminating
\psi
via a partial maximisation of
D(\psi,\phi)
in the dual problem (
Eq.

5
).
To translate a dual potential
\phi\in L^{1}(\nu)
to a joint distribution over
{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}
, define
\pi(\phi,\phi^{+})
with density w.r.t.
\mu\otimes\nu
as

\mathrm{d}\pi(\phi,\phi^{+})(x,y)=\exp\left(\phi(y)-\phi^{+}(x)-\frac{c(x,y)}{\varepsilon}\right)\mathrm{d}\nu(y)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)~.

(7)

This is a valid probability density function over
{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}
as evidenced by the fact that its
{\mathcal{X}}
-marginal is always
\mu
.
Recall that this joint distribution with
\phi\leftarrow\phi^{\star}
in
Eq.

7
corresponds to the unique solution of the eOT problem
Eq.

4
by virtue of
(\phi^{\star})^{+}=\psi^{\star}
.

3.1.1
Properties of the semi-dual
J

We henceforth assume that
\mu
and
\nu
have densities w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure.
In this subsection, we state properties of the semi-dual
J
that underlie the methods that we propose and study in this section.
As a prelude, we state two general observations about the semi-dual
J
.

Fact 1

(Shift-invariance)
.

The semi-dual is invariant to additive perturbations of its argument.
Formally, for any
C\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\phi\in L^{1}(\nu)
,
J(\phi+C\cdot\bm{1})=J(\phi)
where
\bm{1}:x\mapsto 1
.
This is due to the fact that
(\phi+C\cdot\bm{1})^{+}=\phi^{+}+C\cdot\bm{1}
.

Fact 2

(First variation)
.

The first variation
\delta J
of the semi-dual
J
can be succinctly expressed in terms of the marginal
\pi(\phi,\phi^{+})_{{\mathcal{Y}}}

(
Léger 2021
, Lem. 1)
: for any
\phi\in L^{1}(\nu)
,

\delta J(\phi)(y)=\nu(y)-\pi(\phi,\phi^{+})_{{\mathcal{Y}}}(y)=\nu(y)-\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\exp\left(\phi(y)-\phi^{+}(x)-\frac{c(x,y)}{\varepsilon}\right)\nu(y)\mu(x)\mathrm{d}x~.

(8)

The following two properties play a crucial role in our analysis. The first is that the Bregman divergence of
J
is non-positive – in other words
J
is a concave functional.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\phi,\overline{\phi}\in L^{1}(\nu)
. Then,
J(\overline{\phi})-J(\phi)-\left\langle\delta J(\phi),\overline{\phi}-\phi\right\rangle\leq 0
.

The second property is a lower bound on the Bregman divergence, yielding the regularity condition that the semi-dual
J
does not grow arbitrarily.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\phi,\overline{\phi}\in L^{1}(\nu)
.
Then,

J(\overline{\phi})-J(\phi)-\langle\delta J(\phi),\overline{\phi}-\phi\rangle\geq-\frac{\|\overline{\phi}-\phi\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{Y}})}^{2}}{2}~.

3.2
From semi-dual gradient ascent to a new class of methods for eOT

Lemma

1
and
2
implies that one could ostensibly use a gradient ascent-like procedure to find a maximiser of the semi-dual
J
and consequently solve the eOT problem to within a desired tolerance.
More precisely, from an initialisation
\phi^{0}\in L^{1}(\nu)
, we can obtain a sequence of iterates
\{\phi^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
based on the recursion

\phi^{n+1}={\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{SGA}}(\phi^{n};\eta):=\phi^{n}+\eta\cdot\delta J(\phi^{n})~.

(
SGA
)

The update
{\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{SGA}}
was previously considered by
Genevay et al. 2016
for discrete spaces
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
, where
\phi
can be represented as a finite-dimensional vector.
In this setting,
Lemma

2
implies a standard notion of smoothness for the semi-dual
J

(
Nesterov 2018
, Chap. 2)
by the monotonicity of finite-dimensional norms.
This consequently results in an
assumption-free
non-asymptotic convergence guarantee for
SGA
with
\eta<2
.

When generalising to continuous spaces, a temporary setback towards establishing such rates of convergence for this update is that
\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{Y}})}\leq\|\phi\|_{L^{2}({\mathcal{Y}})}
is not generally true, thus leading to an “incompatibility”.
We rectify this by instead adopting an alternate perspective on
SGA
as minimising the “discrepancy” between the
{\mathcal{Y}}
-marginal of
\pi(\phi,\phi^{+})
and
\nu
.
From the form of
\delta J(\phi)
in
Eq.

8
, we can rewrite the update
SGA
as

{\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{SGA}}(\phi;\eta)=\phi+\eta\cdot\left(\nu-\pi(\phi,\phi^{+})_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\right)~.

(9)

Note that a fixed point
\phi^{\star}
of this update satisfies
\pi(\phi^{\star},(\phi^{\star})^{+})_{{\mathcal{Y}}}=\nu
.
This also corresponds to a maximiser of
J
since
\pi(\phi^{\star},(\phi^{\star})^{+})_{{\mathcal{Y}}}=\nu
which is equivalent to
\delta J(\phi^{\star})=0
.

Related to
Eq.

9
, the Sinkhorn algorithm corresponds to an update map
{\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{Sinkhorn}}

(
Reza Karimi et al. 2024
, Lem. 2)
that is defined as

{\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{Sinkhorn}}(\phi):=\phi-\left(\log\frac{\pi(\phi,\phi^{+})_{{\mathcal{Y}}}}{\nu}\right)~.

(
Sinkhorn
)

Through a little observation, it can be seen that both
{\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{SGA}}
and
{\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{Sinkhorn}}
are instantiations of a more general class of updates described below.
Let
\Phi:L^{1}({\mathcal{Y}})\to L^{\infty}({\mathcal{Y}})
be an operator that returns a positive function.
In this setting, it takes a density function (
L^{1}({\mathcal{Y}})
) and returns a function that is not necessarily a density (
L^{\infty}({\mathcal{Y}})
).
Then, we define the update
{\mathsf{M}}^{\Phi\textsf{-match}}
as

{\mathsf{M}}^{\Phi\textsf{-match}}(\phi;\eta)=\phi-\eta\cdot\left(\log\Phi(\pi(\phi,\phi^{+})_{{\mathcal{Y}}})-\log\Phi(\nu)\right)~.

(
\Phi
-match
)

From this, we see that (1) when
\Phi(f):f\mapsto e^{f}
, this recovers
SGA
; and (2) when
\Phi(f):f\mapsto f
, this recovers
Sinkhorn
with a general step size
\eta
(also called
\eta
-Sinkhorn in
Reza Karimi et al. 2024
).
In the next section, we provide different interpretations of

Φ

-match
in the primal space, i.e., over the space of distributions
\pi\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}})
as opposed to dual potentials
\phi\in L^{1}(\nu)
as we do here.
These interpretations not only lend to rates of convergence for
SGA
, but also a related collection of methods that are instances of the general

Φ

-match
framework.

4
Interpretations of
\Phi
-match

The interpretations of

Φ

-match
that we discuss here are motivated by recent work in understanding the Sinkhorn algorithm (
Sinkhorn
)
(
Aubin-Frankowski et al. 2022
;
Reza Karimi et al. 2024
)
.
In essence, these prior works view the Sinkhorn algorithm as minimising
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{{\mathcal{Y}}},\nu)
over a subset
{\mathcal{Q}}
of joint distributions over
{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}
.
This is defined as

{\mathcal{Q}}:=\left\{\pi:\exists~\phi\in L^{1}(\nu)\text{ such that }\pi(x,y)=\exp\left(\phi(y)-\phi^{+}(x)-\frac{c(x,y)}{\varepsilon}\right)\mu(x)\nu(y)\right\}~.

(10)

While
\pi^{\star}
belongs to
{\mathcal{Q}}
, this constrained set is
not
a convex subset of
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}})
owing to the factorisation structure.
Despite this, an intriguing observation about the Sinkhorn algorithm is that it ensures the iterates lie in this set
{\mathcal{Q}}
.
Here, we show that

Φ

-match
also operates in the same way while minimising an objective that is not the KL divergence but instead a discrepancy which depends on
\Phi
.
We specifically show that

Φ

-match
can be interpreted in the following two ways: (1) as an alternating projection scheme and (2) as a local greedy method analogous to gradient descent / mirror descent.
While

Φ

-match
is derived from the semi-dual, these interpretations do not involve the semi-dual and solely operate in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}})
.
This leads to rates of convergence for
SGA
and its kernelised version (

k

-SGA
) that we introduce later on.

Φ

-match
as iterative projections on
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}})

Consider the following projection operations.

\displaystyle\mathsf{project}_{{\mathcal{Y}},\nu}(\pi;\Phi)

\displaystyle:=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bar{\pi}}\left\{\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{\pi}\|\pi):\bar{\pi}_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\propto\pi_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\cdot\frac{\Phi(\nu)}{\Phi(\pi_{{\mathcal{Y}}})}\right\},

(11a)

\displaystyle\mathsf{project}_{{\mathcal{X}},\mu}(\pi^{\prime},\pi;\eta)

\displaystyle:=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bar{\pi}}\left\{\eta\cdot\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{\pi}\|\pi^{\prime})+(1-\eta)\cdot\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{\pi}\|\pi):\bar{\pi}_{{\mathcal{X}}}=\mu\right\}~.

(11b)

For a given
\pi\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}})
,
\mathsf{project}_{{\mathcal{Y}},\nu}
can be seen as correcting the
{\mathcal{Y}}
-marginal of
\pi
towards
\nu
, and the nature of this correction depends on
\Phi
.
On the other hand,
\mathsf{project}_{{\mathcal{X}},\mu}
finds a “midpoint” (which depends on the stepsize
\eta\in[0,1]
) while ensuring that the
{\mathcal{X}}
-marginal is
\mu
.
In the following lemma, we show that if
\pi\in{\mathcal{Q}}
(corresponding to some
\phi\in L^{1}(\nu))
, then applying the projections
Eqs.

11a
and
11b
successively is equivalent to updating
\phi
using

Φ

-match
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\phi^{0}\in L^{1}(\nu)
and let
\{\phi^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be the sequence of potentials obtained as
\phi^{n+1}={\mathsf{M}}^{\Phi\emph{\textsf{-match}}}(\phi^{n};\eta)
for
\eta in[0,1]
.
Then, the sequence of distributions
\{\pi^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
where
\pi^{n}=\pi(\phi^{n},(\phi^{n})^{+})
satisfy for every
n\geq 0

\pi^{n+1}=\mathsf{project}_{{\mathcal{X}},\mu}(\pi^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}},\pi^{n};\eta)\quad\text{where}~\pi^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}=\mathsf{project}_{{\mathcal{Y}},\nu}(\pi^{n};\Phi)~.

Φ

-match
as a local greedy method

For a map
{\mathcal{F}}:L^{1}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}})\to L^{\infty}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}})
, define

\mathsf{root}_{{\mathcal{X}},\mu}(\pi;{\mathcal{F}},\eta):=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bar{\pi}}\left\{\langle{\mathcal{F}}(\pi),\bar{\pi}-\pi\rangle+\eta^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{\pi}\|\pi):\bar{\pi}_{{\mathcal{X}}}=\mu\right\}~.

(12)

When
{\mathcal{F}}
is the first variation of a functional that measures the discrepancy between the
{\mathcal{Y}}
-marginal of
\pi
and
\nu
,
\mathsf{root}_{{\mathcal{X}},\mu}(\pi;{\mathcal{F}},\eta)
can be viewed as minimising a local first-order approximation of this functional, thereby approximately matching the
{\mathcal{Y}}
-marginal, while restricting the
{\mathcal{X}}
-marginal to be
\mu
.
The following lemma states that the successive projections defined in
Eqs.

11a
and
11b
correspond to a root finding procedure for
{\mathcal{F}}\leftarrow{\mathcal{V}}_{\Phi}
defined as

{\mathcal{V}}_{\Phi}(\pi)(x,y)=\log\Phi(\pi_{{\mathcal{Y}}})(y)-\log\Phi(\nu)(y)~.

(13)

Lemma 4
.

Let
\pi\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}})
be such that
\pi_{{\mathcal{X}}}=\mu
.
Then for
\eta\in[0,1]
,

\mathsf{project}_{{\mathcal{X}},\mu}(\mathsf{project}_{{\mathcal{Y}},\nu}(\pi;\Phi),\pi;\eta)=\mathsf{root}_{{\mathcal{X}},\mu}(\pi;{\mathcal{V}}_{\Phi},\eta)~.

The proofs of
Lemmas

3
and
4
are given in
Sections

A.2.1
and
A.2.2
respectively.
This leads to the following straightforward corollary about

Φ

-match
in the manner of
Lemma

3
.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\phi^{0}\in L^{1}(\nu)
and let
\{\phi^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be the sequence of potentials obtained as
\phi^{n+1}={\mathsf{M}}^{\Phi\emph{\textsf{-match}}}(\phi^{n};\eta)
for
\eta\in[0,1]
.
Then, the sequence of distributions
\{\pi^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
where
\pi^{n}=\pi(\phi^{n},(\phi^{n})^{+})
satisfy for every
n\geq 0

\pi^{n+1}=\mathsf{root}_{{\mathcal{X}},\mu}(\pi^{n};{\mathcal{V}}_{\Phi},\eta)~.

The above corollary shows that while the updates

Φ

-match
are themselves expressed in terms of the potentials
\phi
, the joint distributions are automatically constrained in the set
{\mathcal{Q}}
and solve
Eq.

12
at every iteration.

Recall from earlier that

Φ

-match
with
\Phi:f\mapsto f
and
\eta=1
coincides with
Sinkhorn
.
In this setting,
Lemma

3
recovers the classical iterative Bregman projection interpretation of the Sinkhorn method
(
Peyré and Cuturi 2019
, Remark 4.8)
.
The map
{\mathcal{V}}_{\Phi}
in
Eq.

13
is
\log\frac{\pi_{{\mathcal{Y}}}}{\nu}
in this case, which corresponds to the first variation of the functional
\rho\mapsto\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\|\nu)
.
This is of course, up to an additive constant which cancels out in the inner product in
Eq.

12
.
The equivalence in
Corollary

1
for the Sinkhorn method was originally derived in
Aubin-Frankowski et al. 2022
and generalised to an arbitrary step size
\eta\in(0,1)
in
Reza Karimi et al. 2024
.
Besides the KL divergence, another popular measure of discrepancy between distributions is the
\chi^{2}
divergence.
Setting
\Phi(f)=\exp\left(\frac{f}{\nu}-1\right)
, and
{\mathcal{V}}_{\Phi}
results in
{\mathcal{V}}_{\Phi}
coinciding with
\frac{\pi_{{\mathcal{Y}}}}{\nu}-1
, which is the first variation (up to an additive constant) of the functional
\rho\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\chi^{2}(\rho_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\|\nu)
.

4.1
Connection between
SGA
and Maximum Mean Discrepancy

The

Φ

-match
abstraction also permits a similar interpretation of
SGA
, which corresponds to
\Phi:f\mapsto e^{f}
in

Φ

-match
.
The map
{\mathcal{V}}_{\Phi}
(
Eq.

13
) in this case is
\pi_{{\mathcal{Y}}}-\nu
, which is the first variation of another notion of discrepancy between
\pi_{{\mathcal{Y}}}
and
\nu
given by a
Maximum Mean Discrepancy
(abbrev. MMD).
Let
k:{\mathcal{Z}}\times{\mathcal{Z}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a positive-definite kernel, and let
{\mathcal{H}}_{k}
be its RKHS.
We refer the reader to
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
for a more detailed exposition about kernels and their RKHS.
For
\xi\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{Z}})
, the mean function w.r.t. kernel
k
is defined as

{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}(\xi)(y):=\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}k(y,y^{\prime})\cdot\mathrm{d}\xi(y^{\prime})~.

The MMD (for a kernel
k
)
(
Gretton et al. 2006
)
is defined as

\mathrm{MMD}_{k}(\xi,\rho)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in{\mathcal{H}}_{k}\\
\|f\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{k}}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\left|{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}[f]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}[f]\right|=\|{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}(\xi)-{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}(\rho)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{k}}~.

The first variation of
\xi\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{MMD}_{k}(\xi,\rho)^{2}
is given by
{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}(\xi)-{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}(\rho)

(
Mroueh et al. 2019
, Lem. 1)
.
Moreover, for
characteristic
kernels
k

(
Fukumizu et al. 2004
)
, we have that
\xi\mapsto{\mathfrak{m}}_{\xi}
is a one-to-one mapping, which establishes that
\mathrm{MMD}_{k}
for such kernels is a metric over the space of probability measures.
Examples of characteristic kernels are the identity, Gaussian, and Laplace kernels.

Going back to the map
{\mathcal{V}}_{\Phi}
for
SGA
, this coincides with the first variation of

\rho\mapsto{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\rho_{{\mathcal{Y}}},\nu):=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{MMD}_{k_{\mathrm{Id}}}(\rho_{{\mathcal{Y}}},\nu)^{2}

for the identity kernel
k_{\mathrm{Id}}
defined as
k_{\mathrm{Id}}(y,y^{\prime})=1
iff
y=y^{\prime}
, since
{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}(\xi)(y)=\xi(y)
.
This also motivates the consideration of a more general update
{\mathsf{M}}^{k\textsf{-SGA}}
for iteratively minimising
{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\cdot;\nu)
for any characteristic kernel, and results in the following update:

{\mathsf{M}}^{k\textsf{-SGA}}(\phi;\eta):=\phi+\eta\cdot\left\{{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}(\nu)-{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}(\pi(\phi,\phi^{+})_{{\mathcal{Y}}})\right\}~.

(
k
-SGA
)

Due to the generality of the abstraction

Φ

-match
, we can also view

k

-SGA
as an instance of

Φ

-match
with the choice
\Phi_{k}(f):f\mapsto e^{{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}(f)}
where we overload
{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}(f):=\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}k(y,y^{\prime})\cdot f(y^{\prime})\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}
.
As a consequence,
Corollary

1
shows that the sequence of iterates
\{\phi^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
formed by

k

-SGA
results in a sequence of distributions
\{\pi(\phi^{n},(\phi^{n})^{+})\}_{n\geq 1}
formed by iteratively applying
\mathsf{root}_{{\mathcal{X}},\mu}
with
{\mathcal{F}}\leftarrow{\mathcal{V}}_{\Phi_{k}}
. This implication is essential for deriving non-asymptotic rates of

k

-SGA
in the next subsection.

k
-SGA
as kernelised
SGA

Since the map
{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}
is additive, i.e.,
{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}(f_{1}+f_{2})={\mathfrak{m}}_{k}(f_{1})+{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}(f_{2})
for suitable functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}(\delta J(\phi))(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}k(y,y^{\prime})\cdot\delta J(\phi)(y^{\prime})\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}k(y,y^{\prime})\cdot(\nu(y^{\prime})-\pi(\phi,\phi^{+})_{{\mathcal{Y}}}(y^{\prime}))\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}

\displaystyle={\mathfrak{m}}_{k}(\nu)(y)-{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}(\pi(\phi,\phi^{+})_{{\mathcal{Y}}})(y)~.

Then,

k

-SGA
can be equivalently written as

{\mathsf{M}}^{k\textsf{-SGA}}(\phi;\eta)=\phi+\eta\cdot{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}(\delta J(\phi))

and can be viewed as performing kernel smoothing
\delta J
before using it for the update.

4.1.1
Deriving non-asymptotic rates for
k
-SGA

With the new
{\mathcal{L}}_{k}
objective, we derive non-asymptotic rates for

k

-SGA
for a bounded, positive-definite kernel
k
here.
We state a key lemma from
Aubin-Frankowski et al. 2022
which characterises the growth of
{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\cdot;\nu)
relative to the entropy functional
H:\xi\mapsto\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\mathrm{d}\xi\log\mathrm{d}\xi
.
This, along with the convexity of
{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\cdot;\nu)
, is instrumental in establishing a rate of convergence for

k

-SGA
.

Proposition 1

(
(
Aubin-Frankowski et al. 2022
, Prop. 14)
)
.

Let
k:{\mathcal{Y}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a bounded, positive definite kernel where
c_{k}:=\sup_{y\in{\mathcal{Y}}}k(y,y)<\infty
.
Then for any
\xi,\overline{\xi}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{Y}})
,

0\leq\left\langle\delta{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\overline{\xi};\nu)-\delta{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\xi;\nu),\mathrm{d}\overline{\xi}-\mathrm{d}\xi\right\rangle\leq 2c_{k}\cdot\left\langle\delta H(\overline{\xi})-\delta H(\xi),\mathrm{d}\overline{\xi}-\mathrm{d}\xi\right\rangle~.

Consequently,

0\leq{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\overline{\xi};\nu)-{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\xi;\nu)-\langle\delta{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\xi;\nu),\mathrm{d}\overline{\xi}-\mathrm{d}\xi\rangle\leq 2c_{k}\cdot\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\overline{\xi}\|\xi)~.

We now state the formal guarantee for

k

-SGA
; the proof is given in
Section

A.2.3
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\phi^{0}\in L^{1}(\nu)
, and let
\{\phi^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be the sequence of potentials obtained as
\phi^{n+1}={\mathsf{M}}^{k\emph{\textsf{-SGA}}}\left(\phi^{n};\min\left\{\frac{1}{2c_{k}},1\right\}\right)
for a bounded, positive-definite kernel
k
.
Define the sequence of distributions
\{\pi^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
where
\pi^{n}=\pi(\phi^{n},(\phi^{n})^{+})
.
Then, for any
N\geq 1

{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\pi_{{\mathcal{Y}}}^{N};\nu)\leq\frac{\max\{2c_{k},1\}}{N}\cdot\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi^{0})\,.

(14)

Note that every
\pi^{n}
in the sequence of distributions generated by

k

-SGA
stays in
{\mathcal{Q}}
.
When the kernel
k
is characteristic,
Theorem

1
shows that
\pi^{n}_{{\mathcal{Y}}}
approaches
\nu
and consequently establishes a rate of convergence to the optimal coupling
\pi^{\star}
.
Suppose
\phi^{0}=\bm{0}
, and
\pi^{0}=\pi(\phi^{0},(\phi^{0})^{+})
.
Then from
Léger 2021
, we know that
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi^{0})\leq\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi^{\textsf{ref}})
where
\pi^{\textsf{ref}}
is the reference measure in
Eq.

3
.
Consequently, after
N
iterations, the rate we obtain from
Theorem

1
is

\frac{2c_{k}}{N}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi^{\textsf{ref}})}{\varepsilon}~.

This highlights the better dependence on
\varepsilon
compared to the more classical analyses of the Sinkhorn algorithm where the dependence on
\varepsilon
is of the form
e^{-\varepsilon^{-1}}
.
From this, we can directly infer that
SGA
results in a sequence of joint distributions whose
{\mathcal{Y}}
-marginals converge to
\nu
in the squared MMD defined by a bounded, positive definite kernel at a
\frac{1}{N}
rate
without
additional assumptions on either the marginal
\nu
or the cost function
c(\cdot,\cdot)
, since the bound is meaningful as soon as an optimal coupling exists
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi^{\textsf{ref}})<\infty
.

4.2
Additional remarks on
\Phi
-match

Φ

-match
as a mirror method

The interpretation of

Φ

-match
presented here is a generalisation of the mirror method perspective of
\eta
-Sinkhorn introduced in
Reza Karimi et al. 2024
.
Let
\varphi:{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
be given by
\varphi(\pi)=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi^{\textsf{ref}})
, where
\pi^{\textsf{ref}}
is the reference measure in the static Schrödinger bridge problem (
Eq.

3
).
Then,

\delta\varphi(\pi)(x,y)=\log\frac{\pi(x,y)}{\pi^{\textsf{ref}}(x,y)}~.

Following
Reza Karimi et al. 2024
, consider the restriction of
\varphi
to
\overline{{\mathcal{Q}}}=\{\pi\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}):\pi_{{\mathcal{X}}}=\mu\}
, and let
\varphi^{\star}
be the convex conjugate of this restricted
\varphi
.
From
Reza Karimi et al. 2024
, we have that for any suitably integrable
h
,

\delta\varphi^{\star}(h)(x,y)=\mu(x)\cdot\frac{\pi^{\textsf{ref}}(x,y)\exp(h(x,y))}{\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\pi^{\textsf{ref}}(x,y^{\prime})\exp(h(x,y^{\prime}))\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}}~.

(15)

Note that
\delta\varphi^{\star}(h)
is a valid density function over
{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}
since
\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\delta\varphi^{\star}(h)(x,y)\mathrm{d}y=\mu(x)
.
Additionally, as expected of a mirror map, we have
\delta\varphi^{\star}(\delta\varphi(\pi))=\pi
for any
\pi\in\overline{{\mathcal{Q}}}
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\phi^{0}\in L^{1}(\nu)
and let
\{\phi^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be the sequence of potentials obtained as
\phi^{n+1}={\mathsf{M}}^{\Phi\emph{\textsf{-match}}}(\phi^{n};\eta)
.
Then, the sequence of distributions
\{\pi^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
where
\pi^{n}=\pi(\phi^{n},(\phi^{n})^{+})
satisfy for every
n\geq 0

\pi^{n+1}=\delta\varphi^{\star}\left(\delta\varphi(\pi^{n})-\eta\cdot{\mathcal{V}}_{\Phi}(\pi^{n})\right)

(16)

where
{\mathcal{V}}_{\Phi}
is defined in
Eq.

13
.

Lemma

5
offers a mirror descent interpretation of

Φ

-match
in the spirit of a mirror descent method in finite-dimensional optimisation
(
Nemirovski and Yudin 1983
)
, where one uses mirror map to perform updates in the dual space.
We give the proof of
Lemma

5
in
Section

A.2.4
.

5
Extensions

Here, we build on the ideas in the preceding sections and present two extensions.
The first extension revisits the smoothness property of the semi-dual discussed previously and illustrates how this can be leveraged to design iterative methods for maximising the semi-dual with provable guarantees, which also allows us to design an accelerated version of one of these algorithms without having to place assumptions on
\mu
and
\nu
.
The second extension adapts

Φ

-match
for the dynamical Schrödinger bridge problem which generalises the static Schrödinger bridge problem to path measures.

5.1
Adapting to smoothness of the semi-dual
J

Signed semi-dual gradient ascent

As mentioned previously, the fundamental obstacle to establishing guarantees for
SGA
is the mismatch between the inner product in the Bregman divergence (which is in
L^{2}({\mathcal{Y}})
) and the squared growth (which is in
L^{\infty}({\mathcal{Y}})
).
This suggests a better-suited steepest ascent update involving
L^{1}({\mathcal{Y}})
(which corresponds to the dual norm of
L^{\infty}({\mathcal{Y}})
) that is defined by

\phi^{n+1}={\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{sign-SGA}}(\phi^{n};\eta):=\phi^{n}+\eta\cdot\|\delta J(\phi^{n})\|_{L^{1}({\mathcal{Y}})}\cdot\mathrm{sign}(\delta J(\phi^{n}))~.

(
sign-SGA
)

Note that for any
\phi\in L^{1}(\nu)\cap L^{\infty}({\mathcal{Y}})
,
{\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{sign-SGA}}(\phi;\eta)\in L^{1}(\nu)\cap L^{\infty}({\mathcal{Y}})
due to the form of
\delta J(\phi)
in
Eq.

8
.
This is because for any
\phi\in L^{1}(\nu)
,
\|\delta J(\phi)\|_{L^{1}({\mathcal{Y}})}=\|\pi(\phi,\phi^{+})_{{\mathcal{Y}}}-\nu\|_{L^{1}({\mathcal{Y}})}\leq 2
, and the sign function being bounded pointwise.
Also, for a sufficiently small
\eta>0
, the growth property implied by
Lemma

2
asserts that
J({\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{sign-SGA}}(\phi;\eta))\geq J(\phi)
since one can show

J({\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{sign-SGA}}(\phi;\eta))\geq J(\phi)+\eta\cdot\|\delta J(\phi)\|_{L^{1}({\mathcal{Y}})}^{2}-\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\cdot\|\delta J(\phi)\|_{L^{1}({\mathcal{Y}})}^{2}~.

This ascent property in conjunction with the concavity of
J
enables us to give a non-asymptotic convergence rate for
sign-SGA
with an “anchoring” step.
Let
y_{\texttt{anc}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
be an anchor point, and
\phi^{0}\in L^{1}(\nu)\cap L^{\infty}({\mathcal{Y}})
be such that
\phi^{0}(y_{\texttt{anc}})=0
.
Define the set

{\mathcal{T}}_{\phi^{0},y_{\texttt{anc}}}:=\{\phi\in L^{1}(\nu)\cap L^{\infty}({\mathcal{Y}}):\phi(y_{\texttt{anc}})=0,J(\phi)\geq J(\phi^{0})\}~.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\{\phi^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be the sequence of potentials generated according to the following recursion for
n\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\phi^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

\displaystyle={\mathsf{M}}^{\emph{\textsf{sign-SGA}}}(\phi^{n};\eta)

\displaystyle\phi^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\phi^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}-(\phi^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(y_{\emph{\texttt{anc}}})-\phi^{n}(y_{\emph{\texttt{anc}}}))\cdot\bm{1}

with
\phi^{0},y_{\emph{\texttt{anc}}}
as defined above.
Then, for all
N\geq 1
,
\phi^{N}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\phi^{0},y_{\emph{\texttt{anc}}}}
and for
\widetilde{\phi}^{\star}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}~\{J(\phi):\phi\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\phi^{0},y_{\emph{\texttt{anc}}}}\}
we have

J(\phi^{N})-J(\widetilde{\phi}^{\star})\geq-\frac{2\cdot\mathrm{diam}({\mathcal{T}}_{\phi^{0},y_{\texttt{anc}}};L^{\infty}({\mathcal{Y}}))^{2}}{N+1}~,\quad\text{where }~~\mathrm{diam}({\mathcal{S}};L^{\infty}({\mathcal{Y}})):=\sup_{\overline{\phi},\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}}\|\overline{\phi}-\phi\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{Y}})}~.

The first step applies
sign-SGA
, and the second step recenters the iterate to satisfy
\phi^{n+1}(y_{\texttt{anc}})=\phi^{n}(y_{\texttt{anc}})
.
The recentering does not affect the value of the semi-dual as noted previously in
1
, and if
\phi^{\star}\in L^{\infty}({\mathcal{Y}})
is a Schrödinger potential, then
\phi^{\star}-\phi^{\star}(y_{\texttt{anc}})\cdot\bm{1}
is also a maximiser of
J
, which lies in
{\mathcal{T}}_{\phi^{0},y_{\texttt{anc}}}
.
This shift-invariance also explains the use of the anchoring step: without anchoring, the superlevel set of
J
is unbounded.
We can hence infer that the sequence
\{J(\phi^{n})\}_{n\geq 1}
converges to the maximum of the semi-dual
J
. The proof of
Theorem

2
can be found in
Section

A.3.1
.

Projected semi-dual gradient ascent

When the cost function is bounded in a certain manner, it is possible to show that the semi-dual satisfies a different notion of smoothness, but non-uniformly depending on the “size” of the domain considered.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\phi,\overline{\phi}\in L^{2}(\nu)
be such that
\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{Y}})},\|\overline{\phi}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{Y}})}\leq B
for a given
B>0
.
Assume that the cost
c(\cdot,\cdot)\geq 0
.
Then,

J(\overline{\phi})-J(\phi)-\langle\delta J(\phi),\overline{\phi}-\phi\rangle\geq-\frac{\lambda(B)\cdot\|\overline{\phi}-\phi\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}^{2}}{2}~;~~\lambda(B)=e^{2B}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{(x,y^{\prime})\sim\mu\otimes\nu}\left[\exp\left(\frac{c(x,y^{\prime})}{\varepsilon}\right)\right]~.

While
Lemma

2
is a general statement, regularity condition
Lemma

6
is parameterised by a “size” parameter
B
, and hence it is useful to understand what a reasonable choice of
B
is for the purposes of solving the eOT problem.
If
B
is too small, then it is likely that the Schrödinger potential
\phi^{\star}
would not satisfy
\|\phi^{\star}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{Y}})}\leq B
.
Interestingly however, the Schrödinger potentials
\phi^{\star}
and
\psi^{\star}=(\phi^{\star})^{+}
inherit properties from the cost function
c(\cdot,\cdot)
, which allow us to determine a reasonable choice of
B
based on the cost function.
This is formalised in the following proposition.

Proposition 2

(
(
Di Marino and Gerolin 2020
, Lem. 2.7)
)
.

Consider the dual eOT problem defined in
Eq.

5
.
There exists Schrödinger potentials
\phi^{\star},\psi^{\star}
such that

\|\phi^{\star}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{Y}})}\leq\frac{3\|c\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}})}}{2};\quad\|\psi^{\star}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{X}})}\leq\frac{3\|c\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}})}}{2}~.

The intriguing aspect of this proposition is the lack of a dependence on the regularisation parameter
\varepsilon>0
.
This proposition also suggests that solving the semi-dual problem for eOT over the space of functions
\phi\in L^{2}(\nu)
such that
\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{Y}})}\leq\frac{3\|c\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}})}}{2}
is sufficient to recover a Schrödinger potential.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}_{B}:=\{\phi\in{\mathcal{L}}^{2}(\nu):\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{Y}})}\leq B\}\subset L^{1}(\nu)
.
We define the following operation

\phi^{n+1}={\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{proj-SGA}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{B}}(\phi;\eta):=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\overline{\phi}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{B}}~\left\|\overline{\phi}-\left(\phi+\eta\cdot\frac{\delta J(\phi)}{\nu}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}^{2}~.

(
proj-SGA
)

While this operation may appear fortuitous, this is actually a natural recommendation based on
Lemma

6
.
This is because it can be obtained as the solution to a truncated local quadratic approximation of the semi-dual given below (truncated due to the restriction to
{\mathcal{S}}_{B}
), which is inspired by ISTA
(
Beck and Teboulle 2009
)
:

{\mathsf{M}}^{\mathsf{proj-SGA}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{B}}(\phi;\eta)=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\overline{\phi}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{B}}~J(\phi)+\langle\delta J(\phi),\overline{\phi}-\phi\rangle-\frac{1}{2\eta}\cdot\|\overline{\phi}-\phi\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}^{2}~.

From
Lemma

6
, when the cost
c(\cdot,\cdot)
is non-negative and the step size satisfies
\eta\leq\lambda(B)^{-1}
,
J({\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{proj-SGA}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{B}}(\phi;\eta))\geq J(\phi)
for any
\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{B}
as

\displaystyle J({\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{proj-SGA}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{B}}(\phi;\eta))

\displaystyle\geq J(\phi)+\left\langle\delta J(\phi),{\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{proj-SGA}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{B}}(\phi;\eta)-\phi\right\rangle-\frac{\lambda(B)}{2}\|{\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{proj-SGA}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{B}}(\phi;\eta)-\phi\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq J(\phi)+\left\langle\delta J(\phi),{\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{proj-SGA}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{B}}(\phi;\eta)-\phi\right\rangle-\frac{1}{2\eta}\|{\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{proj-SGA}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{B}}(\phi;\eta)-\phi\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq J(\phi)~.

The final step uses the optimality of
{\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{proj-SGA}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{B}}(\phi;\eta)
.
Analogous to
sign-SGA
, the concavity of
J
results in the following non-asymptotic convergence guarantee for
proj-SGA
.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose
c(\cdot,\cdot)
is a non-negative cost function such that
\lambda(B)<\infty
.
Let
\{\phi^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be the sequence of potentials generated according to the following recursion for
n\geq 0
:

\phi^{n+1}={\mathsf{M}}^{\emph{\textsf{proj-SGA}}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{B}}(\phi^{n};\lambda(B)^{-1})~,

where
\lambda(B)
is defined in
Lemma

6
.
Then, for all
N\geq 1
,
\phi^{N}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{B}
and for
\widetilde{\phi}^{\star}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}\limits_{\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{B}}J(\phi)

J(\phi^{N})-J(\widetilde{\phi}^{\star})\geq-\frac{\lambda(B)\cdot\|\phi^{0}-\widetilde{\phi}^{\star}\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}^{2}}{2N}~\,.

Accelerated
proj-SGA

Note that the growth condition in
Lemma

6
that
proj-SGA
is conceptually based on is given in terms of
L^{2}(\nu)
which is a Hilbert space.
This encourages adopting the structure of FISTA
(
Beck and Teboulle 2009
)
to design an accelerated version of
proj-SGA
, which would lead to a rate of convergence that scales as
\nicefrac{{1}}{{N^{2}}}
.
This method is based on minor adjustments to handle the non-uniform growth condition in
Lemma

6
, and is presented below.
For initial values
\phi^{1}=\overline{\phi}^{0}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{B}
, and
t_{1}=1
:

\displaystyle\overline{\phi}^{n}={\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{proj-SGA}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{B}}(\phi^{n};\lambda(3B)^{-1})~;~~t_{n+1}=\frac{1+\sqrt{1+4t_{n}^{2}}}{2}~;

\displaystyle\phi^{n+1}=\overline{\phi}^{n}+\left(\frac{t_{n}-1}{t_{n+1}}\right)\cdot(\overline{\phi}^{n}-\overline{\phi}^{n-1})~.

(
proj-SGA++
)

Theorem 4
.

Suppose
c(\cdot,\cdot)
is a non-negative cost function such that
\lambda(B)<\infty
.
Consider the sequences
\{\phi^{n}\}_{n\geq 2},\{\overline{\phi}^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
generated according to
proj-SGA++
.
Then, for any
N\geq 1
,

J(\overline{\phi}^{N})-J(\widetilde{\phi}^{\star})\geq-\frac{2\cdot\lambda(3B)\cdot\|\overline{\phi}^{0}-\tilde{\phi}^{\star}\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}^{2}}{(N+1)^{2}};\quad\widetilde{\phi}^{\star}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{B}}\,J(\phi)~.

We give the proof of
Theorems

3
and
4
in
Sections

A.3.2
and
A.3.3
.
From
Proposition

2
, we know that the maximum value of the semi-dual
J
over
{\mathcal{S}}_{B}
for
B=\frac{3\|c\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}})}}{2}
is
J(\phi^{\star})
for the Schrödinger potential
\phi^{\star}
.
This implies that the sequences of semi-dual values generated by
proj-SGA
and
proj-SGA++
with the appropriate step sizes converge to
J(\phi^{\star})
at a
\frac{1}{N}
and
\frac{1}{N^{2}}
rate respectively.
A crude bound on
\lambda(B)
in this setting is given by
\lambda(B)\leq\exp\left(B\cdot(\varepsilon^{-1}+2)\right)
.

We would like to mention that this is not the only accelerated method for the eOT problem.
One can take advantage of the structure of
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
, particularly when they are discrete spaces to directly accelerate
{\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{SGA}}
instead of
{\mathsf{M}}^{\textsf{proj-SGA}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{B}}
analogous to accelerating gradient descent to minimise a convex, smooth function in finite dimension.
In that special case, the semi-dual is concave and also smooth in the canonical sense as implied by
Lemma

2
due to the monotonicity of norms, and this leads to a rate that scales as
\frac{1}{N^{2}}
.
Alternatively, one could possibly also design accelerated algorithms for minimising
\rho\mapsto{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\rho_{{\mathcal{Y}}};\nu)
subject to the constraint
\rho\in{\mathcal{Q}}
in contrast to
proj-SGA++
which is based on the semi-dual.
The constraint ensures that the solution has the form of the optimal coupling
\pi^{\star}
. However, designing an accelerated method for this problem in the flavour of accelerated MD for constrained optimisation can prove challenging, due to the non-convexity of the set
{\mathcal{Q}}
and the need for linear combination of past iterates when considering momentum.
In
Appendix

B
, we elaborate on the challenge of building acceleration based on this primal design.

We conclude this discussion by drawing a comparison to
how
the rates for
SGA
,
sign-SGA
, and
proj-SGA
have been established.
Specifically, the techniques used to prove
Theorems

2
,
3
and
4
are fundamentally different from
Theorem

1
.
This highlights the benefits of considering alternate viewpoints for establishing provable guarantees.

5.2
Path-space
\Phi
-match
for the Schrödinger Bridge problem

The eOT problem in the static Schrödinger bridge form (
Eq.

3
) can be viewed as finding a certain distribution in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}})
closest to another reference distribution in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}})
.
This can also be posed for distributions over curves
{\mathcal{C}}([0,T];{\mathcal{Z}})
for
{\mathcal{Z}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
more generally, and a path measure precisely captures the notion of a probability measure over trajectories.
For a stochastic process
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
with state space
{\mathcal{Z}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the path measure is a collection of distributions
({\mathbb{P}}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
where
{\mathbb{P}}_{t}
is the law of
X_{t}
.
The dynamical Schrödinger bridge problem is formulated to find the path measure with endpoints
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}=\mu
and
{\mathbb{P}}_{T}=\nu
that is closest to the reference path measure.
Formally,

\min_{{\mathbb{P}}}~\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}({\mathbb{P}}\|{\mathbb{P}}^{\textsf{ref}})\quad\text{such that }{\mathbb{P}}_{0}=\mu,{\mathbb{P}}_{T}=\nu~.

(17)

This is a (strictly) convex problem defined in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{C}}([0,T],{\mathcal{Z}}))
.
The classical result of
Föllmer 1988
shows that by the disintegration property of the KL divergence, the optimal path measure
{\mathbb{P}}^{\star}
that solves
Eq.

17
can be decoupled as

{\mathbb{P}}^{\star}_{\{0,T\}}=\pi^{\star}~;\quad{\mathbb{P}}^{\star}_{(0,T)|X_{0},X_{T}}={\mathbb{P}}^{\textsf{ref}}_{(0,T)|X_{0},X_{T}}

where
\pi^{\star}
is the solution to the (primal) eOT problem in the static Schrödinger bridge form (
Eq.

3
) with
\pi^{\textsf{ref}}={\mathbb{P}}^{\textsf{ref}}_{0,T}
, and
{\mathbb{P}}_{(0,T)|X_{0},X_{T}}
is the terminal-conditioned measure (also termed the bridge). Due to the above disintegration property and the form of the solution of the static Schrödinger bridge problem in
Eq.

4
, under certain regularity conditions (see
Léonard 2014
), there exists measurable functions
\bm{\psi}_{0}
and
\bm{\phi}_{T}
such that the relative density of
{\mathbb{P}}^{\star}
w.r.t.
{\mathbb{P}}^{\textsf{ref}}
satisfies

\frac{\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{P}}^{\star}_{[0,T]}}{\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{P}}^{\textsf{ref}}_{[0,T]}}((X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]})=\exp\left(\bm{\phi}_{T}(X_{T})-\bm{\psi}_{0}(X_{0})\right)~.

(18)

Moreover, if
{\mathbb{P}}^{\textsf{ref}}
is Markovian i.e., if the underlying stochastic process is a Markov process, then the factorisation in
Eq.

18
is necessary and sufficient for the characterisation of
{\mathbb{P}}^{\star}
and is unique.
This parallels the unique form of the optimal coupling form in
Eq.

4
.

An example of a reversible Markov process
{\mathbb{P}}^{\textsf{ref}}
is the reversible Brownian motion on
[0,T]
.
In this case, the density of
{\mathbb{P}}^{\textsf{ref}}_{\{0,T\}}
satisfies
{\mathbb{P}}^{\textsf{ref}}_{\{0,T\}}(x,y)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2T}\right)
.
Finding
\pi^{\star}
is equivalent to solving the eOT problem (
Eq.

2
) with cost
c(x,y)=\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2}
and
\varepsilon=T
, and the bridge in this setting corresponds to the classical Brownian bridge which draws a more substantive connection between eOT and the dynamic SB problem.
However, in general, it is not feasible to directly sample from the bridge, and this motivates the design of generic methods for solving
Eq.

17
.

Iterative Proportional Fitting

A popular algorithm for solving the dynamic Schrödinger bridge problem is commonly referred to as the
Iterative Proportional Fitting
(IPF) algorithm defined by the iteration below.

{\mathbb{P}}^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\overline{{\mathbb{P}}}}\left\{\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\overline{{\mathbb{P}}}\|{\mathbb{P}}^{n})~:~\overline{{\mathbb{P}}}_{T}=\nu\right\}~,\quad\mathbb{P}^{n+1}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\overline{{\mathbb{P}}}}\left\{\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\overline{{\mathbb{P}}}\|{\mathbb{P}}^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})~:~\overline{{\mathbb{P}}}_{0}=\mu\right\}~.

This can be viewed as the path-space analogue of the alternating projection form of the Sinkhorn algorithm.

Rüschendorf 1995
shows that the initial and final marginals of the sequence of iterates
\{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
generated by the above iteration with
{\mathbb{P}}^{0}={\mathbb{P}}^{\textsf{ref}}
converges to
\mu
and
\nu
respectively as
n\to\infty
, and
Bernton et al. 2019
gives a non-asymptotic rate of convergence that scales as
\frac{1}{N}
in the number of iterations
N
building off the result by
Rüschendorf 1995
.
In
Reza Karimi et al. 2024
, it is shown that this iteration is the solution to the following local greedy update:

{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}\left\{\langle\delta\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}({\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{T}\|\nu),\overline{{\mathbb{P}}}-{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathcal{C}}([0,T],{\mathcal{Z}}))}+\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\overline{{\mathbb{P}}}\|{\mathbb{P}}^{n})~:~\overline{{\mathbb{P}}}_{0}=\mu\right\}~.

From IPF to
path-
\Phi
-match

The similarities between the local greedy update above and
Eq.

12
motivate us to propose a path-space analogue of

Φ

-match
.
For
\eta\in[0,1]
, define

{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\overline{{\mathbb{P}}}}~\left\{\langle\overline{{\mathcal{V}}}_{\Phi}({\mathbb{P}}^{n}),~\overline{{\mathbb{P}}}-{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\rangle+\eta^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\overline{{\mathbb{P}}}\|{\mathbb{P}}^{n})~:~\overline{{\mathbb{P}}}_{0}=\mu\right\}~

(19)

where
\overline{{\mathcal{V}}}_{\Phi}({\mathbb{P}})=\log\Phi({\mathbb{P}}_{T})-\log\Phi(\nu)
.
When
\Phi(f)=f
,
\overline{{\mathcal{V}}}_{\Phi}
is the first variation of
\rho\mapsto\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho_{T}\|\nu)
, and
Eq.

19
recovers the interpretation of IPF stated above with
\eta=1
.
Similar to the equivalence in
Lemma

4
, it can be shown that this local greedy update can be expressed a sequence of alternating projections but in the space of path measures as written below.

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\overline{{\mathbb{P}}}}\left\{\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\overline{{\mathbb{P}}}\|{\mathbb{P}}^{n})~:~\overline{{\mathbb{P}}}_{T}\propto{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{T}\cdot\frac{\Phi(\nu)}{\Phi({\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{T})}\right\}

(
path-
\Phi
-match
(a))

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\overline{{\mathbb{P}}}}\left\{\eta\cdot\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\overline{{\mathbb{P}}}\|{\mathbb{P}}^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})+(1-\eta)\cdot\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\overline{{\mathbb{P}}}\|{\mathbb{P}}^{n})~:~\overline{{\mathbb{P}}}_{0}=\mu\right\}~.

(
path-
\Phi
-match
(b))

As it turns out,
20
(and therefore path measure update
Eq.

19
) can be implemented as updates on the drifts of a sequence of SDEs whose corresponding solutions are given by
\{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
. Let
{\mathbb{P}}^{\textsf{ref}}
be the strong solution of an SDE (hence Markovian) as

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=u^{\textsf{ref}}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\cdot\mathrm{d}B_{t}~,\quad X_{0}\sim\mu~.

(21)

By Girsanov’s theorem
(
Pavliotis 2014
, Chap. 3)
, the dynamical SB problem can be reduced to a minimisation problem over path measures
{\mathbb{P}}
induced by SDEs with a different drift vector field
(v_{t})_{t\geq 0}

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=v_{t}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\cdot\mathrm{d}B_{t}~,\quad X_{0}\sim\mu~.

(22)

We formally characterise this recursive update on
\{v_{t}^{n}\}_{n\geq 1,t\in[0,T]}
in the following proposition.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be obtained from
20
with
{\mathbb{P}}^{0}={\mathbb{P}}^{\emph{\textsf{ref}}}
.
Then for every
n\geq 1
,
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
is the path measure associated with the solution of the following SDE:

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=v_{t}^{n}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\cdot\mathrm{d}B_{t}~,\quad X_{0}\sim\mu~,

where
v_{t}^{0}=u^{\emph{\textsf{ref}}}
and for every
n\geq 0
,

v_{t}^{n+1}=v_{t}^{n}+2\eta\cdot(\nabla\log p_{t}^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}-\nabla\log p_{t}^{n})-2\cdot\nabla V_{t}

with
p^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{t}
and
p^{n}_{t}
denoting marginal densities of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
and
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
respectively. Above
V_{t}
is defined as

V_{t}(x):=-\log{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\exp\left(-\eta\cdot(1-\eta)\cdot\int_{t}^{T}\|\nabla\log p_{s}^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(Z_{s})-\nabla\log p_{s}^{n}(Z_{s})\|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right)~\right|~Z_{t}=x\right]

where the expectation is taken over the law of the SDE
(Z_{s})_{s\geq t}
given by

\mathrm{d}Z_{s}=[v_{s}^{n}(Z_{s})+2\eta\cdot(\nabla\log p_{s}^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(Z_{s})-\nabla\log p_{s}^{n}(Z_{s}))]~\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{2}\cdot\mathrm{d}B_{s}~;\;Z_{t}=x~.

The above proposition extends
Reza Karimi et al. 2024
for IPF to
20
.
The key difference here is that the path measure
{\mathbb{P}}^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
is defined by the time-reversal of the SDE with respect to
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
, but with initial distribution whose density is proportional to
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{T}\cdot\frac{\Phi(\nu)}{\Phi({\mathbb{P}}_{T}^{n})}
, instead of
\nu
as for the case of
\eta
-IPF.

Reza Karimi et al. 2024
discuss approaches for computing
\nabla V_{t}
through stochastic optimal control, and the other terms
\nabla\log p_{t}^{n+1/2},\nabla\log p_{t}^{n}
can be obtained through score-matching time-reversals as in the classical IPF algorithm where
\eta=1
. We give details of the proof of this proposition in
Section

A.3.4
.

The connection to

Φ

-match
can also be seen in the following manner.
Suppose
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
satisfies the factorisation form in
Eq.

18
with some
\bm{\phi}^{n}_{T}
and
\bm{\psi}^{n}_{0}
, and note that
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{0}=\mu
.
Then, one can show analogous to
Lemma

3
that
{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}
admits the factorisation form in
Eq.

18
where

\bm{\phi}^{n+1}_{T}=\bm{\phi}^{n}_{T}-\eta\cdot(\log\Phi({\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{T})-\log\Phi(\nu))~

and
\bm{\psi}_{0}^{n+1}
set to satisfy
{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}_{0}=\mu
is equivalent to the output of
20
.
From this, it can be seen that for each
n\geq 0
,
{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}_{\{0,T\}}
is the solution for the static Schrödinger bridge problem with marginals
(\mu,{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{T})
, and
{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}_{(0,T)|X_{0},X_{T}}={\mathbb{P}}^{\textsf{ref}}_{(0,T)|X_{0},X_{T}}
.
As a consequence, if
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{T}\to\nu
then
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{\star}
.
We expand on the value of this factorisation in the following discussion about a potential-space implementation of
20
.

A “dual” version of
path-
\Phi
-match

We investigate here a “dual” potential-space implementation which is based on the factorisation form in
Eq.

18
in contrast to the discussion above which was based on the path measures.
This connects to updates from

Φ

-match
for eOT, and is similar to the continuous flow of SDE viewpoint in
Reza Karimi et al. 2024
.
Let
\phi^{\star}
and
\psi^{\star}
be the Schrödinger potentials associated with the static problem (
Eq.

3
) with marginals
\mu,\nu
, and suppose
(f_{t}^{\star})_{t\geq 0}
and
(g_{t}^{\star})_{t\geq 0}
are solutions to the Kolmogorov forward and backward equations under
Eq.

21
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f^{\star}_{t}+\nabla\cdot(u^{\textsf{ref}}f^{\star}_{t})-\Delta f^{\star}_{t}=0~,

\displaystyle\quad f^{\star}_{0}(x)=e^{\psi^{\star}(x)}~,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}g^{\star}_{t}+u^{\textsf{ref}\top}\nabla g_{t}^{\star}+\Delta g^{\star}_{t}=0~,

\displaystyle\quad g^{\star}_{T}(y)=e^{\phi^{\star}(y)}~.

Then, according to
Léonard 2014
and the Markov property of
{\mathbb{P}}^{\textsf{ref}}
, we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{[k,l]}^{\star}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{[k,l]}^{\textsf{ref}}}((X_{t})_{t\in[k,l]})=f_{k}^{\star}(X_{k})g_{l}^{\star}(X_{l})\quad\forall~k<l\in[0,T]~.

Therefore with their dynamics fixed, knowing the two boundary functions
\bm{\phi}_{T}^{\star},\bm{\psi}_{0}^{\star}
(
Eq.

18
) gives us all the information about the optimal path measure
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
.
Taking hints, we keep our sequence of path measures
\{\mathbb{P}^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
in the factorized form involving
\bm{\phi}_{T}^{n},\bm{\psi}_{0}^{n}
alone:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{n}_{[0,T]}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\textsf{ref}}_{[0,T]}}((X_{t}^{n})_{t\in[0,T]})=\frac{\mathbb{P}^{n}_{0,T}}{\mathbb{P}_{0,T}^{\textsf{ref}}}(X_{0}^{n},X_{T}^{n})=\exp(\bm{\phi}^{n}_{T}(X_{T}^{n})+\bm{\psi}^{n}_{0}(X_{0}^{n}))~.

(23)

This implies that they always solve the SB problem for their own marginals, and consequently remain within the reciprocal and Markovian class.
If moreover,
\bm{\psi}_{0}^{n}=(\bm{\phi}_{T}^{n})^{+}
then we always have
\mathbb{P}^{n}_{0}=\mu
. In the lemma below, we derive alternative updates on the SDE drift by leverging
the corresponding dual Schrödinger potentials from the static setting, whose proof can be found in
Section

A.3.5
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\{\phi^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be the sequence of potentials obtained from

Φ

-match
.
For any
n\geq 1
, let
(g_{t}^{n})_{t\in[0,T]}
be the solution to the Kolmogorov backward equation

\partial_{t}g_{t}^{n}+\nabla g_{t}^{n\top}u^{\textsf{ref}}+\Delta g_{t}^{n}=0,\;\;g_{T}^{n}(y)=e^{\phi^{n}(y)}~.

The path measure
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
associated with the following SDE

\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{n}=\left\{u^{\emph{\textsf{ref}}}(X_{t}^{n})+\nabla\log g_{t}^{n}(X_{t}^{n})\right\}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\cdot\mathrm{d}B_{t}~,\quad X_{0}^{n}\sim\mu

(24)

factorises as
Eq.

23
and converges to
{\mathbb{P}}^{\star}
at the same rate as the sequence
\{\phi^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
converges to
\phi^{\star}
for the eOT problem.

Note that the additional drift in
Eq.

24
implies that
v_{t}
in
Eq.

22
is necessarily a gradient vector field.
Through a Cole-Hopf transform, the Kolmogorov backward equation can be translated to a PDE in
\phi_{t}^{n}=\log g_{t}^{n}
instead.
This is also evident from the Feynman-Kac formula, which prescribes that
(\phi_{t}^{n})_{t\in[0,T]}
can be expressed as
\phi_{t}^{n}(y)=\log\mathbb{E}^{\textsf{ref}}[e^{\phi^{n}(x_{T})}|x_{t}=y]
with respect to the the reference process
Eq.

21
.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we systemically synthesise a variety of viewpoints on algorithms for eOT – specifically those surrounding the Sinkhorn algorithm.
This synthesis, centered around infinite-dimensional optimisation, leads to a collection of novel methods based on either the primal or dual formulation of the eOT problem, allowing us to go beyond the classical Sinkhorn algorithm and IPF for the path-space / dynamic Schrödinger bridge problem.
We also see how the viewpoints contribute to provable guarantees for these methods, which notably are not based on any strict assumptions on the marginals
\mu,\nu
.
It would be interesting to see how these guarantees can be improved; going past the bounded kernel condition in
Theorem

1
, and bounded costs condition to establish a desirable form of smoothness for the semi-dual in
Lemma

6
.
While this not only encourages a new theoretical perspective for solving the eOT / Schrödinger bridge, we hope that this work also spurs the development of new practical methods other than the Sinkhorn / IPF algorithm and its derivatives for the eOT and Schrödinger bridge problems respectively.

If

Lemma 13
.

Let
(\hat{\pi}^{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the solution to the system

39a
and
39b
.
For kernel
k(y,y^{\prime})=1
iff
y=y^{\prime}
, it holds for any
r\geq 2
that

{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\hat{\pi}^{t}_{{\mathcal{Y}}},\nu)\leq\frac{r^{2}}{t^{2}}\cdot\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi(g^{0},(g^{0})^{+}),\pi^{\star})~,

where
{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\cdot;\nu)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{MMD}_{k}(\cdot,\nu)^{2}
.

Proof.

The proof is based on showing the decay of the following Lyapunov functional

V(\hat{\pi}_{y}^{t},g^{t},t)=\frac{t^{2}}{r}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\hat{\pi}_{y}^{t};\nu)+rD_{\varphi^{\star}}((f^{0}\oplus g^{t});(f^{0}\oplus g^{\star}))~,

where
D_{\varphi^{\star}}(h^{\prime};h)=\varphi^{\star}(h^{\prime})-\varphi^{\star}(h)-\left\langle\delta\varphi^{\star}(h),h^{\prime}-h\right\rangle
.
The time derivative of
V
is

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}V(\hat{\pi}_{y}^{t},g^{t},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2t}{r}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\hat{\pi}_{y}^{t};\nu)+\frac{t^{2}}{r}\langle\delta{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\hat{\pi}_{y}^{t};\nu),\dot{\hat{\pi}}^{t}\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+r\langle\dot{g}^{t},\nabla\varphi^{\star}((f^{0}\oplus g^{t}))-\nabla\varphi^{\star}((f^{0}\oplus g^{\star}))\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{2t}{r}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\hat{\pi}_{y}^{t};\nu)+t\langle\delta{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\hat{\pi}_{y}^{t};\nu),\frac{t}{r}\dot{\hat{\pi}}^{t}-\nabla\varphi^{\star}((f^{0}\oplus g^{t}))+\nabla\varphi^{\star}((f^{0}\oplus g^{\star}))\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{2t}{r}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\hat{\pi}_{y}^{t};\nu)-t\langle\delta{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\hat{\pi}_{y}^{t};\nu),\hat{\pi}^{t}-\pi^{\star}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{t(r-2)}{r}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\hat{\pi}_{y}^{t};\nu)\,.

Therefore if
r\geq 2
,
V
is decreasing with time, and we have

\displaystyle\frac{t^{2}}{r}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(\hat{\pi}_{y}^{t};\nu)

\displaystyle\leq V(\hat{\pi}_{y}^{t},g^{t},t)

\displaystyle\leq V(\hat{\pi}_{y}^{0},g^{0},0)

\displaystyle=rD_{\varphi^{\star}}((f^{0}\oplus g^{t}),(f^{0}\oplus g^{\star}))

\displaystyle=r\cdot\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi(g^{0},(g^{0})^{+}),\pi^{\star})~.

where in the last step, we used
Reza Karimi et al. 2024
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Although the rate above is
\mathcal{O}(1/t^{2})
, faster than MD ODE with
\mathcal{O}(1/t)
rate in

\hat{\pi}^{t}\rightarrow\pi^{*}
, only the convergence for the
{\mathcal{X}}
and the

is a weighted sum of

and since the weights increase very fast with

after running long enough we’d expect

for large

What we can conclude from this discussion is that despite the niceness of
{\mathcal{L}}_{k}
from a mirror descent standpoint (e.g., smooth and convex), the non-convexity of the constraint set we are optimizing over poses a fundamental challenge for acceleration, which involves interpolation between past iterates.
This also highlights the benefit of the semi-dual perspective for building momentum, as we do in

Appendix C
Semi-dual problem in OT

For completeness, here we briefly highlight the semi-dual problem associated with the OT problem (
Eq.

1
) as well.
The Monge-Kantorovich dual problem in OT
(
Villani 2003
, Chap. 1)
is given by

\mathrm{OT}(\mu,\nu;c)=\sup\left\{\int f(x)d\mu(x)+\int g(y)\mathrm{d}\nu(y):f(x)+g(y)\leq c(x,y)\right\}~.

(44)

The
c
-transform of a continuous function
\varphi
is defined as

\varphi^{c}(x)=\inf_{y}\;c(x,y)-\varphi(y)~.

From
Gangbo and McCann 1996
, any solution
(f^{\star},g^{\star})
that solves
Eq.

44
is a pair of functions that satisfies

f^{\star}=(g^{\star})^{c}\qquad g^{\star}=(f^{\star})^{c}~.

Consequently, the dual problem (
Eq.

44
) can be reduced to a problem over just one of
f\in L^{1}(\mu)
or
g\in L^{1}(\nu)

(
Jacobs and Léger 2020
, Thm. 1)
as

\mathrm{OT}(\mu,\nu;c)=\sup_{f\in L^{1}(\mu)}\int f(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)+\int f^{c}(y)\mathrm{d}\nu(y)~.

(45)

Semi-dual gradient ascent for OT

We would like to highlight here that iterative methods for maximising the semi-dual for the OT problem have also been previously proposed
(
Chartrand et al. 2009
;
Jacobs and Léger 2020
)
.

Chartrand et al. 2009
consider the
L^{2}
-gradient (equivalently, the Fréchet derivative) of the OT semi-dual, while recent work by
Jacobs and Léger 2020
use the
\dot{H}^{1}
-gradient defined with respect to the homogeneous Sobolev space
\dot{H}^{1}
which
Jacobs and Léger 2020
argue is better suited to the OT problem.

Appendix D
Additional remarks on

k

-SGA

D.1
Reparameterisation

Alternatively, one can also interpret

k

-SGA
as gradient ascent on a re-parametrised dual objective.
Let
\widetilde{J}:L^{1}(\nu)\to{\mathbb{R}}
be defined as

\widetilde{J}(\varphi)=J\left(\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}k(z,\cdot)\varphi(z)\mathrm{d}z\right)~.

Let
M_{k}:L^{1}(\nu)\to L^{\infty}({\mathcal{Y}})
be defined as
M_{k}(g)=\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}k(z,\cdot)g(z)\mathrm{d}z
, and note that this is linear.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\delta\widetilde{J}(\varphi)(y)

\displaystyle=\delta J(M_{k}(\varphi))[\delta M_{k}(\varphi)(y)]

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\delta J(M_{k}(\varphi))(z)\cdot k(z,y)\mathrm{d}z~.

A gradient ascent procedure for
\widetilde{J}
results in the iteration

\displaystyle\varphi^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\varphi^{n}+\eta\cdot\delta\widetilde{J}(\varphi^{n})

(46)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow M_{k}(\varphi^{n+1})

\displaystyle=M_{k}\left(\varphi^{n}+\eta\cdot\delta\widetilde{J}(\varphi^{n})\right)

\displaystyle=M_{k}(\varphi^{n})+\eta\cdot\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}k(w,\cdot)\cdot\delta\widetilde{J}(\varphi^{n})(w)\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle=M_{k}(\varphi^{n})+\eta\cdot\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}k(w,\cdot)\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\delta J(M_{k}(\varphi^{n}))(z)\cdot k(w,z)\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle=M_{k}(\varphi^{n})+\eta\cdot\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\delta J(M_{k}(\varphi^{n}))(z)\cdot k(z,\cdot)\mathrm{d}z~.

This shows that a sequence of
\{\varphi^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
obtained through
Eq.

46
can be mapped to
\{\phi^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
obtained from

k

-SGA
as
\phi^{n}=M_{k}(\varphi^{n})
for all
n\geq 0
.

D.2
Particle implementation of
k
-SGA

When
k
is a Gaussian or a Laplace kernel, we can use the form of
{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}
and independent samples from a Gaussian or Laplace distribution respectively to implement

k

-SGA
approximately.
This is because
{\mathfrak{m}}_{k}(f)(y)=C_{k}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{k}}\left[f(y+\xi)\right]
where the pair
(C_{k},{\mathcal{D}}_{k})
are

(C_{k},{\mathcal{D}}_{k})=\begin{cases}(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}},~{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma^{2}\cdot\mathrm{I}_{d})&\text{if }k(y,y^{\prime})=\exp\left(-\frac{\|y^{\prime}-y\|^{2}_{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\\
(2b)^{d},~\mathrm{Lap}(0,b)&\text{if }k(y,y^{\prime})=\exp\left(-\frac{\|y^{\prime}-y\|_{1}}{2b}\right)\end{cases}~.

As a result, the particle version of

k

-SGA
can be expressed as

\widehat{{\mathsf{M}}}^{k\textsf{-SGA}}(\phi;\eta)=\phi+\frac{\eta\cdot C_{k}}{N_{\text{samp}}}\cdot\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{samp}}}\nu(y+\xi_{i})-\pi(\phi,\phi^{+})_{{\mathcal{Y}}}(y+\xi^{\prime}_{i})\right\}~;\quad\{\xi_{i}\},\{\xi_{i}^{\prime}\}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}{\mathcal{D}}_{k}~.

Besides these, for a general
k
, it might be viable to consider the Stein kernel
(
Liu et al. 2016
)
.
The Stein kernel for a distribution
\rho
and with a base kernel
k
is given by

\displaystyle k_{\rho}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=k(x,x^{\prime})\cdot\langle\nabla\log\rho(x),\nabla\log\rho(x^{\prime})\rangle+\langle\nabla\log\rho(x),\nabla_{2}k(x,x^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\langle\nabla\log\rho(x^{\prime}),\nabla_{1}k(x,x^{\prime})\rangle+\nabla_{1}\cdot(\nabla_{2}k(x,x^{\prime}))~.

The key property of the Stein kernel is that

\int k_{\rho}(z,\cdot)\rho(z)\mathrm{d}z=0~.

When given an oracle to sample from
\pi(\phi,\phi^{+})_{\mathcal{Y}}
but not
\nu
, it would be useful to consider the Stein kernel
k_{\nu}
, and with this choice of
k\leftarrow k_{\nu}
,

k

-SGA
becomes

\phi^{n+1}=\phi^{n}-\eta\cdot\int_{{\mathcal{Y}}}k_{\nu}(z,\cdot)\pi(\phi^{n},(\phi^{n})^{+})_{{\mathcal{Y}}}(z)\mathrm{d}z~.
