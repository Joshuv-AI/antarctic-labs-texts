Moduli Theory of the r r -Braid Arrangement
Vance Blankers, Emily Clader, Iva Halacheva, Haggai Liu, Dustin Ross

Abstract
. We describe a family of hyperplane arrangements depending on a positive integer parameter r r , which we refer to as the r r -braid arrangements, and which can be viewed as a generalization of the classical braid arrangement. The wonderful compactification of the braid arrangement (with respect to its minimal building set) is well-known to yield the moduli space ℳ ¯ 0 , n \overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n} , and, in this work, we generalize this result, constructing a moduli space ℳ ¯ n r \overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n} of certain genus-zero curves with an order- r r involution that we identify with the corresponding wonderful compactification of the r r -braid arrangement. The resulting space is a variant of the previously studied moduli space ℒ ¯ n r \overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n} [ 2 ] , related via a change of weights on the markings.



1.
Introduction

For an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
of hyperplanes in
\mathbb{P}^{N}
, a wonderful compactification is, roughly speaking, a way of replacing
\mathbb{P}^{N}
by a different ambient variety in such a way that the complement of the hyperplanes is preserved but the arrangement
\mathcal{A}
itself is replaced by a divisor with normal crossings. There are a number of such compactifications, given by blowing up
\mathbb{P}^{N}
(in a prescribed order) along certain sets of subvarieties obtained via intersecting subsets of
\mathcal{A}
, known as building sets. The collection of building sets is partially ordered by inclusion, and it has a unique maximal and minimal element.

An important special case is when
\mathcal{A}
is the braid arrangement, which consists of the hyperplanes

H_{ij}=\{[x_{1}:\cdots:x_{n}]\;|\;x_{i}=x_{j}\}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n-1}

for all
0\leq i<j\leq n
, under the convention that
x_{0}=0
. In this case, the wonderful compactification with respect to the minimal building set coincides with the moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+2}
of genus-zero stable curves
[
4
, Section 4.3]
.

For any positive integer
r
, there is a natural generalization of the braid arrangement, which we refer to as the
r
-braid arrangement, consisting of the hyperplanes

H_{ij}^{k}=\{[x_{1}:\cdots:x_{n}]\;|\;x_{i}=\zeta^{k}x_{j}\}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n-1}

for all
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
and
0\leq k\leq r-1
, where
\zeta
denotes a fixed primitive
r
th root of unity. The primary goal of this paper is to give a modular interpretation of the wonderful compactification of the
r
-braid arrangement with respect to its minimal building set.

Specifically, we construct a moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}
parametrizing genus-zero stable curves with an order-
r
automorphism
\sigma
, equipped with two marked fixed points of
\sigma
and
n
marked orbits of
\sigma
. Our main theorem is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
r\geq 2
and
n\geq 1
. Then the wonderful compactification of the
r
-braid arrangement in
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
, with respect to its minimal building set, is the moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}
.

Remark 1.2
.

This space is closely related to the moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
constructed and studied in
[
2
,
3
,
1
]
, which has interesting connections to generalizations of the permutohedron and matroids. More precisely, the marked fixed points and all but one of the marked orbits in
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
are “light” in the sense that they are allowed to coincide with one another, whereas the marked points in
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}
are required to be all distinct. In this way,
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}
can be viewed as relating to
\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{r}_{n}
in much the same way that
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
relates to the Losev–Manin moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
, which parametrizes genus-zero curves with two “heavy” (non-coinciding) marked points and
n
light marked points.

The strategy for the proof of Theorem
1.1
is as follows. First, we study the combinatorics of the intersection lattice of the
r
-braid arrangement in
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
, relating it to a certain poset of decorated graphs. This allows us to describe the minimal building set explicitly as consisting of all subspaces spanned by a subset of the coordinate points

[1:0:\cdots:0],\;[0:1:0:\cdots:0],\;\ldots,\;[0:\cdots:0:1]

and at most one of the
r^{n-1}
points whose coordinates are all
r
th roots of unity. In particular, the desired wonderful compactification
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}
is the blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
along all such subspaces, in any inclusion-increasing order.

From here, we leverage the known perspective on both the moduli space of curves and the Losev–Manin moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
as similar iterated blow-ups. A key fact about Losev–Manin space is that, by virtue of its stability condition, the genus-zero curves being parametrized are chains of projective lines, as opposed to the arbitrary trees of projective lines that are parametrized by
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+2}
. This allows us to define an “
r
th power” map
q:\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
given by
[x:y]\mapsto[x^{r}:y^{r}]
on each
\mathbb{P}^{1}
in the chain. Using
q
, together with the above explicit perspective on the wonderful compactification of the
r
-braid arrangement as an iterated blow-up, we identify the wonderful compactification with a fiber product

\textstyle{F\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{q}
\textstyle{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{c}
\textstyle{\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n},}

in which
c
is a weight-reduction morphism. This fiber diagram allows us to construct a universal family over
F
of the objects parametrized by
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}
by combining the data of the universal families over
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+2}
and
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
. From here, the fact that
F
is the requisite moduli space follows.

2.
Wonderful compactifications

Wonderful compactifications were introduced by De Concini and Procesi
[
4
]
in the context of hyperplane arrangements in projective space and later generalized by Li
[
9
]
to more general arrangements of subvarieties in a smooth ambient variety. We review the necessary background in this section, referring the reader to such references as
[
4
,
6
,
9
]
for further details.

2.1.
Basic combinatorics

A
hyperplane arrangement
in
\mathbb{P}^{N}
is a finite collection

\mathcal{A}=\{H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}\}

of hyperplanes
H_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{N}
. The
intersection lattice
of
\mathcal{A}
is the collection
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}
of all subsets

H_{I}:=\bigcap_{i\in I}H_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{N}

for
I\subseteq[k]:=\{1,\ldots,k\}
. We consider
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}
as a poset under
reverse
inclusion, so that the unique minimal element is
\widehat{0}=H_{\varnothing}=\mathbb{P}^{N}
.

By the
complement
of an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
, we mean

Y^{\circ}:=\mathbb{P}^{N}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}.

One compactification of
Y^{\circ}
is, of course,
\mathbb{P}^{N}
itself, and
\mathbb{P}^{N}\setminus Y^{\circ}
is (the union of) the original arrangement. The hyperplanes in
\mathcal{A}
may not form a normal crossings divisor, however, and a wonderful compactification is, roughly speaking, a compactification
\overline{Y}
of
Y^{\circ}
in which
\mathcal{A}
is replaced by a divisor
\overline{Y}\setminus Y^{\circ}
with normal crossings. The way to produce such a compactification is, unsurprisingly, by blowing up
\mathbb{P}^{N}
at subspaces along which unwanted intersections of
\mathcal{A}
occur.

Since some subsets of
\mathcal{A}
may already intersect in normal crossings, one generally need only blow up at a subset of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}
in order to obtain a compactification with the requisite “wonderful” properties (some of which we describe below). In particular, the subsets of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}
that give rise to wonderful compactifications are as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a hyperplane arrangement in
\mathbb{P}^{N}
. A
building set
of
\mathcal{A}
is a subset
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}\setminus\{\widehat{0}\}
such that, for all
X\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}\setminus\{\widehat{0}\}
, there is an isomorphism of posets

[\widehat{0},X]\cong\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{maximal }Y\in\mathcal{G}\\
\text{with }Y\leq X\end{subarray}}[\widehat{0},Y].

Here,
\leq
denotes the reverse-inclusion order in
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}
, and
[A,B]
denotes the interval

[A,B]:=\{C\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}\;|\;A\leq C\leq B\}.

The condition of Definition
2.1
is automatically satisfied when
X\in\mathcal{G}
, so
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}\setminus\{\widehat{0}\}
is a building set (the
maximal building set
); this corresponds to the observation that one can obtain a wonderful compactification by blowing up
\mathbb{P}^{N}
at
every
intersection of the hyperplanes in
\mathcal{A}
. On the other hand, if the arrangement includes distinct hyperplanes
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
for which
X=H_{1}\cap H_{2}
and no other hyperplane in the arrangement meets
X
, then it is straightforward to verify that

[\widehat{0},X]\cong[\widehat{0},H_{1}]\times[\widehat{0},H_{2}].

Therefore, a building set can include
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
without including
X=H_{1}\cap H_{2}
; this corresponds to the observation that
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
already have normal crossings, so no blow-up along their intersection is needed. In Figure
1
, we contrast this situation with the situation where there is a third hyperplane
H_{3}
for which
H_{1}\cap H_{2}=H_{1}\cap H_{3}=H_{2}\cap H_{3}
, in which case a building set must contain this (non-transverse) intersection
X
since
[\widehat{0},X]
does not decompose as a product.

\textstyle{H_{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

Hyperplanes
H_{1},H_{2},H_{3}
in three-space that intersect at a common line
X=H_{1}\cap H_{2}\cap H_{3}
, and the corresponding poset
[\widehat{0},X]
, which has rank two but does not decompose as a product of two rank-one posets.

Once a building set is chosen, the precise definition of a wonderful compactification is as follows.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a hyperplane arrangement in
\mathbb{P}^{N}
, and let
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}\setminus\{\widehat{0}\}
be a building set. Choose an ordering of the elements of
\mathcal{G}
that is compatible with inclusion; that is, let

\mathcal{G}=\{G_{1},\ldots,G_{\ell}\}

in which
i\leq j
whenever
G_{i}\subseteq G_{j}
. Then the
wonderful compactification
of
\mathcal{A}
with respect to
\mathcal{G}
is the variety
\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{G}}
obtained by blowing up
\mathbb{P}^{N}
along
G_{1}
, then blowing up the result along the strict transform of
G_{2}
, then blowing up the result along the strict transform of
G_{3}
, and so on.

Among the “wonderful” properties of
\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{G}}
are the following
[
4
, Section 3]
:

•

Because
\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{G}}
is obtained from
\mathbb{P}^{N}
by blowing up only at subvarieties that do not meet
Y^{\circ}
, there is an inclusion

Y^{\circ}\hookrightarrow\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{G}}

as a dense open set; in other words,
\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{G}}
is a compactification of
Y^{\circ}
.

•

The boundary
\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{G}}\setminus Y^{\circ}
is a divisor with normal crossings.

•

The intersection-theoretic structure of the boundary can be read off combinatorially: namely,
\overline{Y}_{\mathcal{G}}\setminus Y^{\circ}
is a union of divisors
D_{G}
for nonempty
G\in\mathcal{G}
, and
D_{G_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap D_{G_{k}}
is nonempty if and only if
\{G_{1},\ldots,G_{k}\}
forms a “
\mathcal{G}
-nested set.”

The notion of “
\mathcal{G}
-nested set” is entirely combinatorial (see
[
4
, Definition 2.4]
or
[
5
, Definition 3.2]
); for example, when
\mathcal{G}
is the maximal building set,
\mathcal{G}
-nested sets are simply chains in the poset
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}\setminus\{\widehat{0}\}
.

2.2.
The minimal building set

In this paper, we will be primarily concerned with the minimal building set. To describe this more explicitly, refer to
X\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}\setminus\{\widehat{0}\}
as
decomposable
if there is an isomorphism of posets

\displaystyle[\widehat{0},Y_{1}]\times[\widehat{0},Y_{2}]

\displaystyle\overset{\cong}{\rightarrow}[\widehat{0},X]

(1)

\displaystyle\ (H_{1},H_{2})

\displaystyle\mapsto H_{1}\lor H_{2}=H_{1}\cap H_{2}

for some
Y_{1},Y_{2}<X
, and otherwise, refer to
X
as
indecomposable
.

Note that, for any
X\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}\setminus\{\widehat{0}\}
, one has

[\widehat{0},X]\cong\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{maximal}\\
\text{indecomposable }Y\leq X\end{subarray}}[\widehat{0},Y].

This fact follows from two observations:

(i)

Any interval
[\widehat{0},X]
can successively be decomposed into products of subintervals until the upper bounds on those subintervals are indecomposable; and

(ii)

Any element of a product of intervals that is not contained in one of the intervals is decomposable, as it can be decomposed by its factors in the product.

Thus, it follows that the set of indecomposable elements forms a building set. Conversely, it is straightforward from Definition
2.1
to see that any building set must contain all indecomposable elements, so the
minimal building set
is the set

\mathcal{G}_{\text{min}}=\Big\{\text{indecomposable elements of }\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}\setminus\{\widehat{0}\}\Big\}.

As an illustrative example, let us determine the minimal building set of the braid arrangement. These results are well-known to experts, but we summarize them here as they provide a helpful template for the setting of the
r
-braid arrangement to come.

Example 2.3
.

Recall from the introduction that the braid arrangement in
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
is the arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{n}
consisting of all hyperplanes

H_{ij}=\{[x_{1}:\cdots:x_{n}]\;|\;x_{i}=x_{j}\}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n-1}

for
0\leq i<j\leq n
, where we set
x_{0}=0
. Elements of the intersection lattice
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}_{n}}
can be naturally identified with set partitions of
\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
, in which the set partition

\{0,1,\ldots,n\}=S_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup S_{\ell}

corresponds to the linear subspace

(2)

\Big\{[x_{1}:\cdots:x_{n}]\;\big|\;x_{i}=x_{j}\text{ if }\{i,j\}\subseteq S_{k}\text{ for some }k\Big\}.

Let us denote a set partition by
\mathbf{S}=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{m}\}
, where we omit any parts of size
1
from the notation (since they do not contribute to the condition defining (
2
)), and let us denote the corresponding subspace by
X_{\mathbf{S}}
. If
m\geq 2
, then

[\widehat{0},X_{\mathbf{S}}]\cong[\widehat{0},X_{\mathbf{S}_{1}}]\times[\widehat{0},X_{\mathbf{S}_{2}}],

in which
\mathbf{S}_{1}
and
\mathbf{S}_{2}
are given by removing
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, respectively, from
\mathbf{S}
.

Thus, the indecomposable elements of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}_{n}}
correspond to set partitions with only one non-singleton part
S
. As subsets of
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
, these are of the form

X_{S}:=X_{\{S\}}=\Big\{[x_{1}:\cdots:x_{n}]\;\big|\;x_{i}=x_{j}\text{ if }\{i,j\}\subseteq S\Big\}

for any nonempty subset
S\subseteq\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
. Setting
p_{0}=[1:1:\cdots:1]
and, for
1\leq i\leq n
,

p_{i}=[0:\cdots:0:1:0:\cdots:0]

(with a
1
in the
i
th spot), then
X_{S}
can equivalently be expressed as the linear subspace spanned by
p_{i}
with
i\notin S
.

One finds, then, that the wonderful compactification of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
with respect to its minimal building set is the iterated blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
along all subspaces spanned by subsets of
\{p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}
, in inclusion-increasing order. This precisely coincides with Kapranov’s description
[
8
]
of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+2}
.

In the next section, we adapt the argument in the above example to give an explicit description of the minimal building set of the
r
-braid arrangement.

3.
The
r
-braid arrangement and its minimal building set

Fix a primitive
r
th root of unity
\zeta
. The
r
-braid arrangement
in
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
is the arrangement
\mathcal{A}^{r}_{n}
consisting of all hyperplanes of the form

H^{k}_{ij}:=\{[x_{1}:\cdots:x_{n}]\;|\;x_{i}=\zeta^{k}x_{j}\}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n-1}

for
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
and
0\leq k\leq r-1
. Note, in particular, that we allow
i=j
, but in this case we must insist that
k\neq 0
(as
H^{0}_{ii}
is all of
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
, not a hyperplane), and for such
k
,

H^{k}_{ii}=\{[x_{1}:\cdots:x_{n}]\;|\;x_{i}=0\}.

The goal of this section is to give an explicit description of the minimal building set of the
r
-braid arrangement along the lines of Example
2.3
. Here, as above, we denote by
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
the coordinate points in
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
. Furthermore, we denote
\mathbb{Z}_{r}=\{0,1,\ldots,r-1\}
, and for any
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
, we denote

p_{\mathbf{a}}=[\zeta^{a_{1}}:\zeta^{a_{2}}:\cdots:\zeta^{a_{n}}].

The description that we aim to prove is the following.

Proposition 3.1
.

The minimal building set of the
r
-braid arrangement in
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
consists of all subspaces spanned either by a subset
S
of the coordinate points
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
, or by
S\cup\{p_{\mathbf{a}}\}
for some
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}
.

To prove this, we first identify the intersection lattice
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}^{r}_{n}}
with a purely combinatorial lattice, in much the same way that the intersection lattice of the braid arrangement is identified with the lattice of set partitions. The combinatorial objects in this case are somewhat richer; we refer to them as
(r,n)
-graphs
and introduce them below.

3.1.
The lattice of
(r,n)
-graphs

Fix
r\geq 2
and
n\geq 1
. In what follows, a
complete graph
refers (as usual) to a simple graph with an edge between every pair of distinct vertices, and a
complete
r
-flower
refers to a non-simple graph with
r
edges between every pair of distinct vertices and
r-1
self-edges at each vertex.

Definition 3.2
.

An
(r,n)
-graph
is a graph
G
with vertex set

V(G)=[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}

and a fixed edge labeling

\displaystyle E(G)

such that
G
decomposes as a disjoint union of connected subgraphs

G=G_{0}\sqcup G_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup G_{b}

for some

, satisfying the following conditions:

(i)

G_{1},\ldots,G_{b}
are nonempty complete graphs, and their edge labels are
compatible
in the sense that for any triple of vertices

, we have

k_{i_{1}i_{3}}\equiv(k_{i_{1}i_{2}}+k_{i_{2}i_{3}})\mod r.

is a (possibly empty) complete

.

Lemma 3.4
.

There is an isomorphism of posets

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}^{r}_{n}}

G_{X}

Proof.

First, we must check that
G_{X}
is indeed an
(r,n)
-graph. To do so, let

Z_{X}=\{i\in[n]\;|\;x_{i}=0\text{ for all }x\in X\}.

Then
X\subseteq H_{ij}^{k}
for all
i,j\in Z_{X}
and all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
, so
G_{X}
contains the complete
r
-flower on vertex set
Z_{X}
with edge labeling as required by condition (ii) of Definition
3.2
. From here, let
G^{\prime}
be any other connected component of
G_{X}
. Then
G^{\prime}
is a simple graph, because if there were multiple edges (meaning
X\subseteq H^{k}_{ij}\cap H^{k^{\prime}}_{ij}
for some
k\neq k^{\prime}
) or self-edges (meaning
X\subseteq H^{k}_{ii}
for some
k\neq 0
), then their vertices would be in
Z_{X}
. Furthermore,
G^{\prime}
is a complete graph, since for any vertices
i\leq j\in V(G^{\prime})
, the fact that
G^{\prime}
is connected means that there exists a path in
G^{\prime}
from
i
to
j
. Denote the vertices in this path by

i=i_{1},i_{2}\ldots,i_{\ell}=j.

Then, by the definition of the edge labels on
G_{X}
, each
x\in X
satisfies

x_{i_{m}}=\zeta^{p_{i_{m}i_{m+1}}}x_{i_{m+1}}

for
m=1,\ldots,\ell-1
, where

p_{i_{m}i_{m+1}}:=\begin{cases}k_{i_{m}i_{m+1}}&\text{ if }i_{m}<i_{m+1}\\
-k_{i_{m}i_{m+1}}&\text{ if }i_{m}>i_{m+1}.\end{cases}

Adding these equations for all

shows that each
x\in X
satisfies
x_{i}=\zeta^{p_{ij}}x_{j}
with

p_{ij}:=\sum_{m=1}^{\ell-1}p_{i_{m}i_{m+1}}.

This means that
X\subseteq H_{ij}^{p_{ij}}
, so
G^{\prime}
contains an edge from
i
to
j
, proving that
G^{\prime}
is indeed a complete graph. Furthermore, applying this argument with
i=j
shows that we have

x_{i}=\zeta^{p_{ii}}x_{i}.

Since
i\notin Z_{X}
, there exists some
x\in X
with
x_{i}\neq 0
. Thus, the above equation is only possible if
p_{ii}\equiv 0\mod r
, and applying this to paths of length
\ell=3
proves that condition (i) of Definition
3.2
is satisfied. This shows that
G_{X}
is an
(r,n)
-graph, as claimed.

To see that the association
X\mapsto G_{X}
is bijective, let
G
be any
(r,n)
-graph, and let

S_{G}=\{(i,j,k)\in[n]\times[n]\times\mathbb{Z}_{r}\;|\;i\leq j\text{ and }\exists\text{ an edge in }G\text{ from }i\text{ to }j\text{ labeled }k\}.

Setting

X_{G}=\bigcap_{(i,j,k)\in S_{G}}H_{ij}^{k},

it is straightforward to see that the associations
X\mapsto G_{X}
and
G\mapsto X_{G}
are inverse.

Finally, to see that this bijection matches up the poset structures in
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}^{r}_{n}}
and
\Gamma^{r}_{n}
, note that by definition,
X\leq Y
in
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}^{r}_{n}}
if and only if
X\supseteq Y
. Thus, if
G_{X}
has an edge from
i
to
j
labeled
k
, then
X\subseteq H_{ij}^{k}
and hence
Y\subseteq H_{ij}^{k}
. This shows that
G_{Y}
also has an edge from
i
to
j
labeled
k
, so
G_{X}\leq G_{Y}
in
\Gamma^{r}_{n}
.
∎

In light of Lemma
3.4
, we can determine the minimal building set of the
r
-braid arrangement by instead determining the indecomposable elements of the poset
\Gamma^{r}_{n}
. These are characterized by the next lemma. To state the lemma, we refer to a connected component of an
(r,n)
-graph as
trivial
if it has no edges; in particular, note that if
G_{0}\neq\varnothing
, then it is always nontrivial. The minimal element
\widehat{0}
of
\Gamma^{r}_{n}
is the unique
(r,n)
-graph with no edges, meaning all of its connected components are trivial.

Lemma 3.5
.

An element
G\in\Gamma^{r}_{n}\setminus\{\widehat{0}\}
is indecomposable if and only if
G
has exactly one nontrivial connected component.

Proof.

We prove both directions by contrapositive. First, suppose that
G\in\Gamma^{r}_{n}\setminus\{\widehat{0}\}
has multiple nontrivial connected components
G_{i_{1}},\ldots,G_{i_{k}}
. These are not themselves
(r,n)
-graphs since their vertex set is a proper subset of
[n]
, but for any such graph
H
, we denote by
\widetilde{H}
the
(r,n)
-graph with edges and edge labels as in
H
, so that any vertices in
[n]\setminus V(H)
constitute trivial connected components in
\widetilde{H}
. We claim that

(3)

[\widehat{0},G]\cong\prod_{j=1}^{k}[\widehat{0},\widetilde{G_{i_{j}}}],

via the isomorphism

H\mapsto\big(\widetilde{H\cap G_{i_{j}}}\big)_{j=1}^{k}

with inverse given by

(H_{1},\ldots,H_{k})\mapsto H_{1}\vee\cdots\vee H_{k}

for
H_{j}\in[\widehat{0},\widetilde{G_{i_{j}}}]
. The fact that these are inverses is straightforward to check: given an element
H\in[\widehat{0},G]
and denoting
H_{j}:=\widetilde{H\cap G_{i_{j}}}
, we have

E(H)=E(H_{1})\cup\cdots\cup E(H_{k})

(with matching edge labels) and hence
H=H_{1}\vee\cdots\vee H_{k}
, whereas given an element
(H_{1},\ldots,H_{k})\in\prod[\widehat{0},\widetilde{G_{i_{j}}}]
with
H_{1}\vee\cdots\vee H_{k}=H
, we have

\widetilde{H\cap G_{i_{j}}}=H_{j}.

This proves (
3
) and hence verifies that
G
is decomposable.

Conversely, suppose that
G
is decomposable, so that

(4)

[\widehat{0},G]\cong[\widehat{0},K_{1}]\times[\widehat{0},K_{2}]

for (possibly disconnected) subgraphs
\widehat{0}<K_{1},K_{2}<G
.
The fact that
K_{1},K_{2}\neq\widehat{0}
means that they contain nontrivial connected components
H_{1},H_{2}
, respectively, and we claim that these are contained in disjoint connected components
G_{i_{1}},G_{i_{2}}
of
G
. In other words, the claim is that there are no edges of
G
connecting a vertex in
H_{1}
to a vertex in
H_{2}
, and this is straightforward to see: if
e
were such an edge, then the subgraph consisting only of the edge
e
would be in
[\widehat{0},G]
but not the join of a graph in
[\widehat{0},K_{1}]
and a graph in
[\widehat{0},K_{2}]
, which would violate (
4
).
∎

3.2.
Proof of minimal building set

Equipped with these results on
(r,n)
-graphs, we are prepared to prove the description of the minimal building set of the
r
-braid arrangement given above.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

By Lemmas
3.4
and
3.5
, the indecomposable elements of the lattice
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}^{r}_{n}}
are
X\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}^{r}_{n}}
such that
G_{X}
has exactly one nontrivial connected component. These are precisely those
X
of the form

(5)

X=\{[x_{1}:\cdots:x_{n}]\in\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\;|\;x_{i_{1}}=\cdots=x_{i_{\ell}}=0\}

for some
i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell}\in[n]
(if the nontrivial connected component is the complete
r
-flower) or

(6)

X=\{[x_{1}:\cdots:x_{n}]\in\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\;|\;\zeta^{k_{1}}x_{i_{1}}=\cdots=\zeta^{k_{\ell}}x_{i_{\ell}}\}

for some distinct
i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell}\in[n]
and some
k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}
(if the nontrivial connected component is not the complete
r
-flower). In the first case (
5
) above,
X
is equal to the subspace spanned by the coordinate points
p_{i}
for
i\notin\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell}\}
, and in the second case (
6
),
X
is equal to the subspace spanned by these same coordinate points together with the point
p_{\mathbf{a}}
given by

a_{i}=\begin{cases}-k_{i}\mod r&\text{ if }i\in\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell}\}\\
0&\text{ if }i\notin\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell}\}.\end{cases}

Thus, the indecomposable elements of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}^{r}_{n}}
are those subspaces spanned by a subset of the coordinate points
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}
and at most one root-of-unity point
p_{\mathbf{a}}=[\zeta^{a_{1}}:\cdots:\zeta^{a_{n}}]
, proving the proposition.
∎

Proposition
3.1
implies that the wonderful compactification of
\mathcal{A}^{r}_{n}
with respect to its minimal building set is the iterated blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
along all subspaces spanned by subsets of
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}
and at most one element of
\{p_{\mathbf{a}}\;|\;\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}_{r}^{n}\}
, in any inclusion-increasing order. Toward the ultimate goal of giving this blow-up a modular interpretation, we first devote the next section to reframing it as a fiber product of the usual moduli space of curves
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+2}
with the Losev–Manin space
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
.

4.
The wonderful compactification as a fiber product

We begin this section by recalling some preliminaries on Hassett’s moduli spaces of curves with weighted marked points, following
[
7
]
.

4.1.
Background on Hassett spaces

For any weight vector
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1]^{n}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}>2
, the moduli space
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,\mathbf{w}}
is a smooth Deligne–Mumford stack that provides an alternative compactification of
\mathcal{M}_{0,n}
in which the marked points are allowed to coincide in a controlled way prescribed by
\mathbf{w}
. Specifically, elements of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,\mathbf{w}}
are tuples
(C;p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
up to automorphism, where
C
is a genus-zero curve with at worst nodal singularities, and
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\in C
are marked points with assigned weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}
, satisfying the following conditions:

(i)

the sum of the weights of any collection of coinciding marked points is
\leq 1
;

(ii)

the sum of the weights at all special points (i.e. marked points or half-nodes) of any irreducible component is
>2
, where half-nodes are given weight
1
.

We refer to such curves as
\mathbf{w}
-stable
. Note, in particular, that
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n}
is recovered as the special case where
w_{i}=1
for all
i
.

Part of what makes Hassett spaces interesting is that certain choices of weight vector give rise to toric moduli spaces. A simple special case is the weight vector

\mathbf{w}_{\mathbb{P}}=(\underbrace{\epsilon,\ldots,\epsilon}_{n+1\text{ times}},1),

where
\epsilon=1/n
. In this case, the stability condition (ii) ensures that a
\mathbf{w}_{\mathbb{P}}
-stable curve has a single
\mathbb{P}^{1}
component. Choosing coordinates on this
\mathbb{P}^{1}
in which the last marked point is
\infty
and the second-to-last marked point is
0
, the data of a
\mathbf{w}_{\mathbb{P}}
-stable curve is a choice of the remaining
n
marked points (up to automorphism via scaling), which are complex numbers that do not all coincide with
0
by the choice of
\epsilon
. That is:

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,\mathbf{w}_{\mathbb{P}}}=\mathbb{P}^{n-1}.

A more interesting toric case is given by the weight vector

\mathbf{w}_{\text{LM}}:=(\underbrace{\epsilon,\ldots,\epsilon}_{n\text{ times}},1,1),

where again
\epsilon=1/n
. The resulting moduli space is denoted

\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}:=\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,\mathbf{w}_{\text{LM}}}

and is referred to as
Losev–Manin space

[
10
]
. The stability condition (ii) now forces that
\mathbf{w}_{\text{LM}}
-stable curves are chains of projective lines with the last two marked points on the two ends:

p_{n+1}
p_{n+2}

Each component of such a curve has two distinguished special points (the half-nodes or the “heavy” points
p_{n+1},p_{n+2}
), and one can choose coordinates so that these distinguished special points are
0
and
\infty
, with
0
being the one closer to the marked point
p_{n+1}
and
\infty
being the one closer to the marked point
p_{n+2}
. In these coordinates, we can view
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
, and there is an action of
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
on
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
in which
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
acts by sending the marked point
p_{i}
in each
\mathbb{P}^{1}
component to the point
t_{i}p_{i}
in the same component. In fact, this action endows
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
with the structure of a smooth toric variety: it is the toric variety associated to the braid fan
[
10
, Section 2]
.

Another perspective on
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
and why it is toric is given in terms of the
weight-reduction maps
between Hassett spaces. Namely, for any weight vectors
\mathbf{w}
and
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
with
w_{i}\geq w_{i}^{\prime}
for all
i
, there is a birational map

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,\mathbf{w}}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,\mathbf{w}^{\prime}}

given by iteratively contracting components of a
\mathbf{w}
-stable curve that fail to be
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
-stable. Applying this to the weight vectors
\mathbf{w}_{\text{LM}}
and
\mathbf{w}_{\mathbb{P}}
above, one obtains a map

d:\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n-1}.

Analyzing this map via
[
7
, Proposition 4.5]
, one sees that it is an iterated blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
along all subspaces spanned by subsets of the coordinate points, in dimension-increasing order. Because such subspaces are all torus-invariant, the fact that
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
is toric follows.

Along the same lines, there is a weight-reduction map

c:\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+2}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n},

which can be viewed as an iterated blow-up of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
along the strict transforms of all subspaces of
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
spanned by
[1:1:\cdots:1]
together with some subset of the coordinate points. The composition of the two reduction maps
d\circ c:\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+2}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
recovers the Kapranov description of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+2}
as an iterated blow-up discussed in Example
2.3
. (The crucial difference between this blow-up and the one discussed in the previous paragraph is that, since
[1:1:\cdots:1]
is not torus-invariant, the resulting blow-up is not toric. In this sense,
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
can be viewed as “the last toric blow-up on the way to
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+2}
.”)

4.2.
The wonderful compactification as a fiber product

In this section, we realize the wonderful compactification of the
r
-braid arrangement in
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
as a fiber product
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+2}\times_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}}\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
. The key insight that we require is that the
r
-braid arrangement is the preimage of the braid arrangement under the map
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
that takes the
r
th power of each coordinate. Since blow-ups commute with flat base change, we might then try to conclude that the wonderful compactification of the
r
-braid arrangement is the fiber product
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+2}\times_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
, where the maps are the iterated blow-up
d\circ c:\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+2}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
and the
r
th power map. However, this is not quite right: what goes wrong is that the coordinate hyperplanes happen to lie in the ramification locus of the
r
th power map, so instead of blowing up along these subspaces, the fiber product
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+2}\times_{\mathbb{P}^{n-1}}\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
blows up along nonreduced schemes supported on the coordinate subspaces. To remedy this, we first blow-up the coordinate spaces, obtaining
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
, and then we use the
r
th power map on
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
instead.

More precisely, since
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
is a smooth toric variety, there is a natural map
q:\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
that extends the
r
th power map on the torus. In the coordinate description of elements of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
from the previous subsection,
q
preserves the underlying curve
C
and the last two marked points, and it acts on the first
n
marked points by
[x:y]\mapsto[x^{r}:y^{r}]
. Observe that
q
is finite locally free of degree
(n-1)^{r}
: in the affine toric chart of any torus-invariant point of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
, the pullback
q^{*}
can be realized as the inclusion of polynomial rings

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}]

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}]

\displaystyle f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})

\displaystyle\mapsto f(x_{1}^{r},\dots,x_{n-1}^{r}),

which exhibits the codomain as a free module of rank
(n-1)^{r}
over the domain. Being finite locally free implies, in particular, that
q
is finite and flat. Note that the ramification locus of
q
is the toric boundary of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
.

With this notation in place, the main result of this section is the following.

Proposition 4.1
.

The wonderful compactification of
\mathcal{A}^{r}_{n}
with respect to its minimal building set is the fiber product
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}
in the following diagram:

\textstyle{\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{q}
\textstyle{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{c}
\textstyle{\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}.}

Proof.

We begin by setting up notation. Viewing
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
as the iterated blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
along the coordinate subspaces of
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
, let
\mathcal{Z}
be the collection of strict transforms of the linear subspaces in
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
spanned by
[1:\dots:1]
together with a set of coordinate points, and let
\mathcal{W}
be the collection of strict transforms of the linear subspaces in
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
spanned by one of the points
p_{\mathbf{a}}=[\zeta^{a_{1}}:\cdots:\zeta^{a_{n}}]
together with a set of coordinate points. As discussed in the previous subsection,
c:\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+2}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
is the iterated blow-up along the elements of
\mathcal{Z}
, in any inclusion-increasing order.

We aim to prove that
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{r}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
is the iterated blow-up along the elements of
\mathcal{W}
. Once we have accomplished this, we can combine it with the fact that
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
is the iterated blow-up along the coordinate subspaces of
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
to see that
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{r}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
is the iterated blow-up along the collection of linear subspaces spanned by a subset of the coordinate points and at most one of the points
p_{\mathbf{a}}
. By Proposition
3.1
, this is precisely the minimal building set of
\mathcal{A}^{r}_{n}
, from which we conclude that
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}
is the wonderful compactification with respect to this building set, as desired.

To prove that
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{r}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
is the iterated blow-up along the elements of
\mathcal{W}
, begin by decomposing the map
c
as follows:

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+2}=Y_{n-2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c_{n-3}}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y_{n-3}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c_{n-4}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\cdots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c_{1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c_{0}}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y_{0}=\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n},

where the map
c_{k}
is the blow-up of

along the union of the strict transforms of the
k
-dimensional elements of
\mathcal{Z}
. Denote the intermediate compositions of these blow-up maps by
\widehat{c}_{k}=c_{0}\circ\dots\circ c_{k-1}:Y_{k}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
, and let
\widetilde{Z}_{k}\subseteq Y_{k}
denote the union of the strict transforms of the
k
-dimensional elements of
\mathcal{Z}
:

\widetilde{Z}_{k}=\overline{\widehat{c}_{k}^{-1}(Z_{k}\setminus Z_{k-1})},

where
Z_{k}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
is the union of the
k
-dimensional elements of
\mathcal{Z}
. With this notation, we have
Y_{k+1}=\mathrm{Bl}_{\widetilde{Z}_{k}}(Y_{k})
.

Recursively define
X_{0}=\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
and
X_{k}=Y_{k}\times_{Y_{k-1}}X_{k-1}
, so that every square in the following diagram is a pullback:

\textstyle{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{r}=X_{n-2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{X_{n-3}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\cdots\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{X_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{X_{0}=\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{q}
\textstyle{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+2}=Y_{n-2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{Y_{n-3}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\cdots\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{Y_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{Y_{0}=\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}.}

We prove inductively that the map
X_{k}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
is the iterated blow-up along the elements of
\mathcal{W}
of dimension less than
k
; the desired result then follows from the
k=n-2
case.

To prove the induction step, consider the diagram

\textstyle{X_{k+1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{b_{k}}
\scriptstyle{q_{k+1}}
\textstyle{X_{k}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{q_{k}}
\textstyle{Y_{k+1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{c_{k}}
\textstyle{Y_{k}.}

Since
q
is flat and since flatness is preserved by base change,
q_{k}
is flat. Furthermore, since blow-ups commute with flat base change (see
[
12

) and since
Y_{k+1}=\mathrm{Bl}_{\widetilde{Z}_{k}}(Y_{k})
, it follows that
b_{k}
is a blow-up:

X_{k+1}=\mathrm{Bl}_{\widetilde{Z}_{k}\times_{Y_{k}}X_{k}}(X_{k}).

It remains to study the blow-up locus
\widetilde{Z}_{k}\times_{Y_{k}}X_{k}\subseteq X_{k}
; we must argue that it is equal to the strict transform of the union of the
k
-dimensional elements of
\mathcal{W}
. More precisely, after unraveling definitions, we must argue that

(7)

\overline{\widehat{c}_{k}^{-1}(Z_{k}\setminus Z_{k-1})}\times_{Y_{k}}X_{k}=\overline{\widehat{b}_{k}^{-1}(W_{k}\setminus W_{k-1})},

where
W_{k}
is the union of the

-dimensional elements of
\mathcal{W}
and
\widehat{b}_{k}=b_{0}\circ\cdots\circ b_{k-1}
. By definition of
\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathcal{W}
, it is not hard to see that
W_{k}=q^{-1}(Z_{k})
, and it then follows that
W_{k}\setminus W_{k-1}=q^{-1}(Z_{k}\setminus Z_{k-1})
. Since
W_{k}\setminus W_{k-1}
is disjoint from the blow-up locus of
\widehat{b}_{k}
and
Z_{k}\setminus Z_{k-1}
is disjoint from the blow-up locus of
\widehat{c}_{k}
, it then follows that

\widehat{b}_{k}^{-1}(W_{k}\setminus W_{k-1})=q_{k+1}^{-1}(\widehat{c}_{k}^{-1}(Z_{k}\setminus Z_{k-1})).

Lastly, since
q
is finite locally free, which is preserved by base change, so is
q_{k+1}
, and since none of the components of
Z_{k}
are contained entirely within the ramification locus of
q_{k+1}
, the equality (
7
) is a special case of the lemma below.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
q:X\rightarrow Y
be a finite locally free map of irreducible varieties and let
Z\subseteq Y
be a subset such that none of the irreducible components of the Zariski closure
\overline{Z}
lie entirely within the ramification locus of
q
. Then

\overline{Z}\times_{Y}X=\overline{q^{-1}(Z)}.

Remark 4.3
.

The lemma fails if a component of
\overline{Z}
lies within the ramification locus of
q
. For example, if
X=Y=\mathbb{A}^{1}
,
q(x)=x^{2}
, and
Z=\{0\}
, then the fiber product is a point of multiplicity two supported at the origin, while the closure of the preimage is a point of multiplicity one supported at the origin.

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

It suffices to prove the lemma in the case where
X
and
Y
are affine and
q^{*}:\mathbb{C}[Y]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[X]
is an inclusion that endows
\mathbb{C}[X]
with the structure of a free module over
\mathbb{C}[Y]
, since our hypotheses imply that
Y
has an affine open cover over which the restriction of
q
has this form. In this case,

\overline{Z}\times_{Y}X=\mathrm{Spec}\left(\frac{\mathbb{C}[Y]}{\mathcal{I}(Z)}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}[Y]}\mathbb{C}[X]\right)=\mathrm{Spec}\left(\frac{\mathbb{C}[X]}{\mathcal{I}(Z)\mathbb{C}[X]}\right)

and

\overline{q^{-1}(Z)}=\Spec\left(\frac{\mathbb{C}[X]}{\mathcal{I}(q^{-1}(Z))}\right).

Thus, we must prove that
\mathcal{I}(Z)\mathbb{C}[X]=\mathcal{I}(q^{-1}(Z))
. The inclusion
\mathcal{I}(Z)\mathbb{C}[X]\subseteq\mathcal{I}(q^{-1}(Z))
simply follows from the fact that the inclusion
q^{*}:\mathbb{C}[Y]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}[X]
maps
\mathcal{I}(Z)
into
\mathcal{I}(q^{-1}(Z))
, so the crux of the lemma is proving the other inclusion.

Let
g\in\mathcal{I}(q^{-1}(Z))
. Choose a basis
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
of
\mathbb{C}[X]
as a module over
\mathbb{C}[Y]
, and write
g=g_{1}f_{1}+\dots+g_{m}f_{m}
for some
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\in\mathbb{C}[Y]
. Note that
m
is the degree of the finite map
q
. Let
z\in Z
be any point over which
q
is unramified, and set
q^{-1}(z)=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}
. Evaluating
g
at
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
and noting that
g(x_{j})=0
and
g_{i}(x_{j})=g_{i}(z)
for all
i,j
, we obtain equations

0=g_{1}(z)f_{1}(x_{j})+\dots+g_{m}(z)f_{m}(x_{j})\;\;\;\text{ for }\;\;\;j=1,\dots,m.

In other words, the function

g_{1}(z)f_{1}+\dots+g_{m}(z)f_{m}\in\mathbb{C}[X]

vanishes at all the preimages
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
of
z
, which means that it descends to zero in the quotient

\frac{\mathbb{C}[X]}{\mathcal{I}(z)\mathbb{C}[X]}\cong\left(\frac{\mathbb{C}[Y]}{\mathcal{I}(z)}\right)^{m}\cong\mathbb{C}^{m}.

Given that the images of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}
in this quotient form a basis as a
\mathbb{C}
-vector space, we conclude that
g_{1}(z)=\cdots=g_{m}(z)=0
. Since this holds for every
z\in Z
over which
q
is unramified, and since these points form a dense subset of
\overline{Z}
by our hypotheses on
Z
, it then follows that
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\in\mathcal{I}(Z)
. The expression
g=g_{1}f_{1}+\dots+f_{n}g_{n}
then implies that
g\in\mathcal{I}(Z)\mathbb{C}[X]
, as desired.
∎

At this point, we have successfully identified the wonderful compactification of the
r
-braid arrangement with the fiber product
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}
of a diagram in which three of the corners are moduli spaces. It likely comes as no surprise, then, that
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}
has a modular interpretation, as well. We turn to this interpretation in the next and final section.

5.
The wonderful compactification as a moduli space

We begin this section by describing the moduli problem of interest, and we then prove that the fiber product
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}
is indeed a fine moduli space for this moduli problem. Throughout, as above, we fix a primitive
r
th root of unity
\zeta
. Furthermore, all schemes are assumed to be finite-type over
\mathbb{C}
.

5.1.
The moduli problem

Fix

and

. We will refer to the objects in our moduli space as

(r,n)
-curves
, to distinguish them from the

-curves studied in

Definition 5.1

A

-curve

distinct marked points (denoted by

,

, subject to the following conditions:

and

•

and

•

\sigma

•

the

is stable (i.e., it is an element of

to

and

the

-fixed points in the directions of

and
x^{-}
, respectively. In addition to the conditions above, we require that, in coordinates on

and

, the automorphism

is given by multiplication by the fixed primitive root of unity

.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let

\widetilde{c}:C_{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow C_{\mathcal{L}}
and
\widetilde{q}:C_{\mathcal{L}}\rightarrow C_{\mathcal{L}}
be the maps on curves induced by

and
q
. Define
\zeta:C_{\mathcal{L}}\rightarrow C_{\mathcal{L}}
to be multiplication by
\zeta
on each component of

. Then

(C_{\mathcal{M}}\times_{C_{\mathcal{L}}}C_{\mathcal{L}};\;\{(y_{i},\zeta^{j}z_{i})\},\;(y^{\pm},z^{\pm}),\;\sigma)

is a heavy
(r,n)
-curve, where
\sigma(y,z)=(y,\zeta z)
.

Proof.

For convenience, set
C=C_{\mathcal{M}}\times_{C_{\mathcal{L}}}C_{\mathcal{L}}
. Since
\widetilde{q}:C_{\mathcal{L}}\rightarrow C_{\mathcal{L}}
is a degree-
r
cyclic cover of
C_{\mathcal{L}}
that is fully ramified at the nodes and heavy marked points of
C_{\mathcal{L}}
, it follows that
C\rightarrow C_{\mathcal{M}}
is a degree-
r
cyclic cover of
C_{\mathcal{M}}
that is fully ramified over
y^{\pm}
, as well as over all nodes of
C_{\mathcal{M}}
that connect components in the chain from
y^{-}
to
y^{+}
. Up to isomorphism, there is a unique such cyclic cover
C\rightarrow C_{\mathcal{M}}
. More specifically,
C
is a nodal rational curve containing a central chain that we identify with
C_{\mathcal{L}}
, and for each tree
T\subseteq C_{\mathcal{M}}
that is contracted by
\widetilde{c}
, the curve
C
contains
r
identical such trees attached at simple nodes at the
r
points of
\widetilde{q}^{-1}(\widetilde{c}(T))\subseteq C_{\mathcal{L}}
.

Given this description of
C
, one sees that it has an order-
r
automorphism acting by multiplication by
\zeta
on the central chain
C_{\mathcal{L}}
and permuting the trees accordingly. The fiber product map
\widetilde{f}_{\mathcal{M}}:C\rightarrow C_{\mathcal{M}}
is the quotient by this automorphism, while the map
\widetilde{f}_{\mathcal{L}}:C\rightarrow C_{\mathcal{L}}
contracts the trees to the central chain, so this automorphism is the
\sigma
in the statement of the lemma.

Set

to be the endpoints of the central chain of

, corresponding to
z^{\pm}\in C_{\mathcal{L}}
. Furthermore, for each
i\in[n]
and

, set
z_{i}^{j}=\sigma^{j}(z_{i}^{0})
, where

is the unique element of the preimage

whose image under
\widetilde{f}_{\mathcal{L}}
is

. This data makes

into a heavy
(r,n)

and

, it agrees with the data in the statement of the lemma.

-curves. The next lemma, on the other hand, gives an explicit procedure for going in the other direction.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
(C;\{z_{i}^{j}\},x^{\pm},\sigma)
be a heavy
(r,n)
-curve.

•

Define
(C_{\mathcal{M}};\{y_{i}\},y^{\pm})\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+2}
by setting

, setting

to be the image of the orbit
\{z_{i}^{0},\dots,z_{i}^{r-1}\}
, and setting
y^{\pm}
to be the image of
x^{\pm}
.

•

Define
(C_{\mathcal{L}};\{z_{i}\},z^{\pm})\in\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
to be the image of

that forgets all but the marked points
\{z_{i}^{0}\}
.

Then

, and under this identification of curves,
z_{i}^{j}=(y_{i},\zeta^{j}z_{i})
,
x^{\pm}=(y^{\pm},z^{\pm})
, and
\sigma(y,z)=(y,\zeta z)
.

Proof.

Notice that the canonical maps
C\rightarrow C_{\mathcal{M}}
and
C\rightarrow C_{\mathcal{L}}
give rise to a canonical map

, by the universal property of fiber products. Given the explicit description of

in the proof of Lemma

, it is not hard to see that this map is an isomorphism of nodal curves, and that it identifies the marked points and automorphism as stated in the lemma.
∎

(r,n)
-curves, while Lemma

Proposition 5.5
.

There is a bijection

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{r}=\{\textrm{heavy }(r,n)\text{-curves}\}.

-curves over

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{n+1}^{r}
,
\sigma
, and
z_{i}^{j},x^{+},x^{-}
over
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{r}
specializes to the construction of Lemma
5.3
over
b
. Thus, the given data indeed defines a tautological family of heavy
(r,n)
-curves.
∎

5.4.
The tautological family is universal

We are now prepared to conclude with the following culminating result.

Theorem 5.7
.

The fiber product
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}
is a fine moduli space for the moduli problem of heavy
(r,n)
-curves. That is, for any base scheme
B
, pulling back the tautological family gives a bijection

\Bigg\{\text{morphisms }B\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}\Bigg\}\;\;\longrightarrow\;\;\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}\textstyle\text{families of heavy}\\
\textstyle(r,n)\text{-curves over }B\end{subarray}\right\}.

Proof.

To prove injectivity, suppose that two morphisms
f,g:B\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{r}
give rise to isomorphic families of heavy
(r,n)
-curves upon pulling back the tautological family. Then these isomorphic families restrict to isomorphic heavy
(r,n)
-curves over every
b\in B
. Proposition
5.5
then implies that the
f(b)=g(b)
for every
b\in B
, so
f=g
.

To prove surjectivity, let
\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{C};\{z_{i}^{j}\},x^{\pm},\sigma)
be a family of heavy
(r,n)
-curves over
B
. Upon taking the quotient of
\mathcal{C}
by
\sigma
, we obtain a family
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{M}}=(\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}};y_{i},y^{\pm})
of curves in
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+2}
, and thus a morphism
f_{\mathcal{M}}:B\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{0,n+2}
. Similarly, after contracting to the central chain of
\mathcal{C}
, we obtain a family
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}}=(\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{L}};z_{i},z^{\pm})
of curves in
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
, and thus a morphism
f_{\mathcal{L}}:B\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
. By the universal property of fiber products,
f_{\mathcal{M}}
and
f_{\mathcal{L}}
give rise to a morphism
f_{\mathcal{F}}:B\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{r}
. We claim that
\mathcal{F}\cong f_{\mathcal{F}}^{*}(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{taut}})
where
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{taut}}
denotes tautological family over
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{r}
.

By construction of
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{taut}}
as a fiber product, we can construct its pullback
f_{\mathcal{F}}^{*}(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{taut}})
from the families
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{M}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}}
:

f_{\mathcal{F}}^{*}(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{taut}})=(\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}\times_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{L}}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{L}};\;\{(y_{i},\zeta^{j}z_{i})_{i}^{j}\},\;(y^{\pm},z^{\pm}),\;\sigma).

The natural maps
\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}
and
\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{L}}
give rise to a map
\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}\times_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{L}}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{L}}
, and Lemma
5.4
implies that this map is a fiberwise isomorphism of heavy
(r,n)
-curves over
B
. Since
\mathcal{C}
is flat over
B
, the isomorphism on fibers implies that
\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}\times_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{L}}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{L}}
is an isomorphism (see, for example,
[
11
, Section 37.16]
), and we then conclude that
\mathcal{F}\cong f_{\mathcal{F}}^{*}(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{taut}})
.
∎

Because Theorem
5.7
shows that
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}
is a fine moduli space for heavy
(r,n)
-curves and Proposition
4.1
shows that
\overline{\mathcal{M}}^{r}_{n}
is the wonderful compactification of the
r
-braid arrangement with respect to its minimal building set, the proof of Theorem
1.1
is complete.
