Multi-omics analysis reveals seasonal variation in ovarian lipid metabolism associated with vitamin D3 in the muskrats
Authors: Wenjing Lu, Yuyue Dong, Zhaomei Dong, Yuning Xie, Haolin Zhang, Yingying Han, Dong Zhang, Yuning Liu, Qiang Weng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSeasonal reproduction is critical for animals adapting to environmental changes, yet the mechanisms driving these shifts remain incompletely understood. Gut microbiota are increasingly recognized as important modulators of reproductive health, but their roles in seasonal breeding species remain unclear.ResultsIn the muskrat (Ondatra zibethicus), a well-established seasonal breeder, morphological and histological analyses revealed enhanced follicular development during the breeding season (BS). Subsequent integrated ovarian transcriptomics and metabolomics identified that lipid metabolic processes were significantly enriched during the BS. This was validated by the increased expression of key lipid metabolic enzymes (e.g., DGAT1, CPT1A) and elevated triglyceride content in BS ovaries. Systemically, we found that serum vitamin D3 (VD3) and ovarian vitamin D receptor (VDR) expression were significantly elevated during BS. In contrast, analysis of upstream factors during the non-breeding season (NBS) showed a significant enrichment of gut Lactobacillus and a concurrent elevation of plasma lithocholic acid (LCA), a known VDR agonist. Correlation analyses confirmed that both Lactobacillus and LCA were inversely associated with serum VD3 levels. Critically, in vitro experiments demonstrated that VD3 treatment enhanced the expression of key lipid metabolism genes in muskrat granulosa cells.ConclusionsCollectively, our findings provide evidence for a gut microbiota-VD3-ovary axis that modulates seasonal ovarian lipid metabolism and function. This axis represents a new layer of host-microbiota interaction in reproductive physiology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12796-7.

Background

Lipid metabolism in the ovary is a dynamic and complex process affecting several critical processes of female reproduction, including folliculogenesis, oocyte maturation, and steroidogenesis [1]. De novo lipogenesis (DNL) has been shown to be present in granulosa cells (GCs) and might play important roles in cell proliferation and physiology [2]. The process of DNL involves the conversion of excess carbs into fatty acids by important factors such as acetyl-CoA, malonyl-CoA, acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), and fatty acid synthase (FAS) [3]. These fatty acids are processed and produced into triglycerides or cholesterol, and subsequently utilized in peripheral tissues, forming high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) particles [4, 5]. Several studies have found that DNL plays a crucial function in mammalian ovarian follicles, particularly GCs. For instance, transcriptomics analysis of granulosa and theca cells revealed that ovarian lipid metabolism was involved in follicle development and maturation [6]. Recent research indicates that fatty acid β-oxidation (FAO) is an essential source of energy during oocyte maturation [7]. The transport of fatty acids across the mitochondrial membrane by carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT1) is a key step in FAO [8]. Controlling lipid homeostasis is crucial for normal development and physiology, and disruption of lipid metabolism can lead to the development of some pathophysiologies such as polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) [9].

Vitamin D3 (VD3), a fat-soluble secosteroid, is a pivotal regulator of numerous physiological processes, including calcium homeostasis, immune function, and reproductive health [10]. Its primary source in mammals is endogenous synthesis in the skin, where 7-dehydrocholesterol is converted to pre-VD3 upon exposure to ultraviolet B (UVB) radiation from sunlight, followed by thermal isomerization to VD3. The reproductive system is one of the target tissues for VD3 [11]. Vitamin D receptor (VDR) has been identified in many reproductive organs, such as prostate, testis, uterus, and ovary (particularly GCs) [12].1α,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3 [1α,25(OH)2D3] acts as the active form of VD3 and exerts its biological effects through VDR [13]. It has been demonstrated that VD3-induced VDR signaling could potentially alleviate endoplasmic reticulum stress and improve 17β-estradiol secretion in the ovarian GCs [14]. VD3 plays essential roles in lipid metabolism through various mechanisms, including its effects on adipocyte differentiation, fat storage, lipid oxidation, and insulin sensitivity [15]. On the one hand, 1α,25(OH)2D3 has been reported to suppress fatty acid synthesis by inhibiting FAS, which in turn reduces lipid accumulation in breast cancer cells [16]. In contrast, other studies show that VD3 supply could promote lipid accumulation in prostate cancer cells by activating PPARα [17, 18]. VD3 was suggested to promote lipid accumulation in differentiating human keratinocytes but not in proliferating HaCaT cells [19]. In addition, it has been documented that serum VD3 levels are important in relation to reproductive function [12]. While there is growing evidence to suggest the impact of VD3 deficiency on lipid metabolism in women with PCOS, there is a paucity of studies focusing on the potential role of VD3 supply on ovarian lipid metabolism.

Gut microbiota plays crucial roles in the process of food digestion, energy metabolism, immune homeostasis, reproductive activity, and longevity of the host [20, 21]. Gut microflora has also been suggested to influence the female reproductive endocrine system, interfering with hormones such as estrogens, androgens, and insulin [22]. Studies have indicated that the influence of gut microbiota on reproductive organs was mainly through the circulatory transport of endocrine, oxidative stress, and inflammatory factors induced by the products or products of gut microbiota to the reproductive organs, affecting reproductive functions [23, 24]. Imbalanced gut microbiota can contribute to a range of diseases and ailments [25]. In addition, gut microbiota has an effect on the synthesis, metabolism, and transport of fat-soluble vitamins through the production of metabolites [26]. It has been reported that VD3 status and biological function might be affected by changes in gut microbiota [27]. Seasonal environmental variations, along with their impacts on host physiology and ecology, have the potential to drastically modify animal gut microbiota [28]. However, the effects of this altered gut microbiota on seasonally breeding mammals have not been studied in detail.

The muskrat (Ondatra zibethicus) is a semi-aquatic rodent with high economic and medicinal value, and it exhibits clear seasonal breeding patterns, with sexual activity occurring from March to October [29]. During this period, male muskrats develop their caudal abdominal scent glands, which secrete muskrat scent to stimulate female estrus [30]. While males remain continuously sexually active, females undergo cyclic estrus throughout the breeding season, highlighting the tight seasonal regulation of reproductive physiology [31]. Although seasonal changes in muskrat reproductive organs and musk glands have been documented, the metabolic mechanisms underlying female ovarian activity remain largely unexplored. In this study, we applied a multi-omics approach-combining ovarian transcriptomics and metabolomics with plasma metabolomics, gut microbiota profiling, and in vitro GCs experiments-to explore the metabolic basis of seasonal ovarian function, with a particular focus on the role of lipid metabolism and possible links to gut microbiota.

Methods

Animals and sample collection

The adult female muskrats weighing 700 ± 50 g were sampled from the Xinji muskrat breeding station (Hebei, China), in May (breeding season, BS, n = 21) and December (non-breeding season, NBS, n = 15). Female muskrats were sedated with a sodium pentobarbital (BioDee, Beijing, China) injection before being decapitated for euthanasia. Muskrat blood was immediately sampled in heparinized tubes and centrifuged, and supernatant was sampled for the following experiments. Ovaries and intestines were collected from the females. One part of ovarian samples was immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and the others were stored at -80 °C. Cecal contents were harvested and kept at -80 °C until DNA extraction. Animal experiments were permitted by Ethics Committee of Experimental Animals of Beijing Forestry University (EAWC_BJFU_202008). All experiments were carried out according to the Chinese Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Histological staining

Ovarian samples fixed for 24 h were removed from 4% paraformaldehyde solution. These samples were then dehydrated in graded ethanol (70%-100%) and xylene solutions and embedded in paraffin. The ovarian tissues (n = 3) were serially sectioned at 5 μm slices, with one section selected for every 5 sections. These ovarian sections were then deparaffinized and rehydrated in various alcohol gradients for observation of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining by light microscopy (Leica DMLB, Houston, USA). Follicles at different developmental stages were counted manually in the sections. To minimize statistical bias, the data collection process involved three blinded, independent personnel. Furthermore, to avoid repeated counting of the same follicle, only follicles with visible oocyte nuclei were counted.

Ultrastructure observation

One mm3 of ovarian samples were extracted and thereafter preserved in 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 1% osmic acid solution. It underwent ethanol and acetone dehydration, embedding media infiltration in gradient epoxy resin and acetone, and overnight heating at 70 °C. The ovarian sections were cut at 50–70 nm. Ovarian ultrastructure was examined by transmission electron microscope (H-7650, HITACHI, Tokyo, Japan). ImageJ software was used to analyze transmission electron microscope images for determining the number of lipid droplets. For each sample, three randomly regions were selected for analysis.

Oil red O staining

Ovarian frozen sections were washed 3 times with distilled water after being fixed for 15 min in 4% paraformaldehyde. Following a 10-min staining process with oil red O solution (B0023, Boerfu, Wuhan, China), the sections were rinsed with distilled water. After rinsing the sections with distilled water, identification was carried out using 75% alcohol. Hematoxylin staining was applied to ovarian sections for 5 min, followed by distilled water washing, differentiation solution washing, and tap water washing. The findings were examined using an optical microscope. In each sample, three randomly selected regions were analyzed to quantify the oil red O staining.

Culture and treatment of the muskrat GCs

In May, the ovarian tissues from one side of four muskrats were collected for GCs isolation and co-culture, with the experiment repeated three times. The muskrat GCs were isolated as previously described [32]. Muskrat ovaries were harvested, and mature follicles were punctured with a 1 mL syringe to release the GCs. The isolated cells were plated in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, NY, USA) with the addition of 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA) in 37 °C environment. After reaching 70%-90% confluence, the GCs were treated with 10 nM or 100 nM VD3 (Selleck, Houston, TX, USA) for 24 h. Then, the GCs were sampled for RNA isolation.

Transcriptomic analysis

Total RNA extraction from the muskrat ovaries group (n = 3) and GCs group (n = 3) was performed using the TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA) method. The RNA integrity number (RIN > 7.0) of the sequencing RNA was assessed using the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Sequencing was performed on the platform of Illumina NovaSeqTM 6000 (Illumine, USA), generating 300 bp paired-end reads. The clean reads required for subsequent analysis were obtained by filtering out low-quality reads through FastQC (v0.11.8) (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). Cleaned reads from each sample were assembled de novo into transcripts via Trinity (https://github.com/trinityrnaseq/trinityrnaseq) with default parameters. The assembled transcripts were clustered and de-redundant using Corset (https://code.g.oogle.com/p/corset-project/) [33], and then searched against the NCBI protein nonredundant (NR), Clusters of Orthologous Groups of proteins (COG), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases using Diamond to identify the proteins that had the highest sequence similarity with the given transcripts to retrieve their function annotations and a typical cut-off E-values less than 1.0 × 10− 5 was set. Transcript abundance was estimated using StringTie (v1.3.5) [34] and gene expression was normalized to Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) using featureCounts (v1.6.2) [35]. The DESeq2 software (v1.22.1) was employed to screen differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across various breeding seasons [36], set at an adjusted p-value < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg method) and log2|fold change| ≥ 1. The functional annotation and statistical enrichment analysis of the DEGs were conducted with a threshold q value (adjusted p value) < 0.05 based on the Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG database using GOseq software (v1.52.0) and KOBAS 3.0 software (https://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn), respectively.

Untargeted metabolomic analysis

Ovarian samples (n = 6) were homogenized, and the internal standard solution was added and shaken at 1500 rpm for 5 min. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C after 15 min on ice. Muskrat ovarian sample was centrifuged and supernatant was sampled for LC-MS analysis. Plasma metabolites of the muskrats (n = 6) were extracted as previously described [37]. LC-MS analysis was employed using Ultra High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (LC-30 A, Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with quadrupole time-of-flight (AB Sciex TripleTOF 6600). The ACQUITY Premier HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, USA) was used for hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography separation. Quality control (QC) samples were processed to monitor the stability.

To examine the overall metabolic differences among the groups, we employed principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA). Statistical significance was determined using Student’s t-test, and p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction method. We determined differential metabolites (DMs) between BS and NBS groups by applying the following criteria: VIP > 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 [38]. In addition, the functions of these metabolites and metabolic pathways were investigated using the KEGG database.

RNA extraction and RT-PCR analysis

Total RNA from the muskrat ovarian tissues (n = 3) and cultured cells (n = 3) was extracted with Trizol Reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and subsequently reverse transcribed into cDNA. The qPCR was performed on ABI PRISM 7500 Fast equipment (Applied Biosystems, USA). Primers used were shown in Table S1. β-actin was used as a standardized reference gene. Relative mRNA levels were quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

IHC was applied to the muskrat ovarian sections (n = 3). After the paraffin sections had been deparaffinized with xylene, gradient alcohol hydration was carried out. The citric acid-buffered solution was added and heated. The slices were then blocked with 10% goat serum and incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS 3 times, the sections were incubated with biotinylated secondary antibody and avidin-biotin complex for 30 min each. DAB peroxidase substrate was used, and the images were acquired under the microscope. A semi-quantitative score for positivity was assessed using ImageJ software based on optical density. Table S2 shows the antibodies utilized in the experiment.

Western blot analysis

Muskrat ovarian samples (n = 3) were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (Applygen) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Applygen). Protein was electrotransferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore Corporation, USA) after being electrophoresed on SDS polyacrylamine gels. Membranes were blocked in 5% fat-free milk for 1 h, and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Table S2 was described the antibodies and their dilution ratios. Membranes were cleaned with 1× TBST and then incubated for 1 h with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Bioss Biotechnology). Chemifluorescence was detected using the ImageQuant LAS 4000 microsystem (GE Healthcare Biosciences AB, Inc) and quantified using Image J.

Biochemical assays

The ovarian tissue was homogenized in 0.5 ml of precooled saline solution. These homogenates were centrifuged and the supernatant was sampled. Ovarian free fatty acids (FFA) and triacylglycerol (TG) contents (n = 3) were assessed using commercial assay kits (Beijian Xinchuangyuan Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Serum FFA, total cholesterol (TC), TG, LDL-C, and HDL-C contents (n = 6) were all obtained using the automated biochemical analyzer (TBA-120FR, Toshiba, Japan). Serum VD3 content (n = 3) was assayed using 1,25(OH)2D3 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) Kits (JM-02110R1; Jiangsu Jingmei, China).

Gut microbiota sequencing and analysis

The total genome DNA of the cecal contents (n = 6) was extracted using the CTAB technique. DNA content and purity were assessed using 1% agarose gel. To achieve a concentration of 1 ng/µL, DNA was diluted with sterile water. Bacterial 16S rRNA genes of 16S V3-V4 regions were amplified by PCR using primer pairs 341F (5’-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT- 3’). Sequencing libraries were created using the gTruSeq® DNA PCR-Free Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA) and index codes were added. Library was sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, USA), which yielded 250 bp paired-end reads. These paired-end reads were then combined with FLASH (v1.2.11) [39]. The sequences were analyzed using Uparse software (v7.0.1001) [40]. Differences in alpha diversity and composition of gut microbiota between BS and NBS groups were analyzed using T-test and Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and P-values were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg method [41]. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe, v1.1.2) analysis was used to search for biomarkers that would distinguish each host species. The significance of microbiota differences and similarities between BS and NBS groups were verified using MetaGenomeSeq analysis and ANOSIM analysis [42].

Statistical analysis

Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, USA), and the data were presented in graphs as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). Statistical comparisons between two groups were analysed using Student’s t-test (unpaired t-test), while those between multiple groups were subjected to One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post hoc analysis using SPSS 26 Software (SPSS Inc., USA). P < 0.05 was considered significant. Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed to determine the correlation between two variables.

Results

Seasonal phenotypic changes in the muskrat ovaries

The phenotypic differences of muskrat ovaries between BS and NBS were shown in Fig. 1 and Fig. S1. Ovarian weight and volume were significantly higher in BS compared to NBS (Fig. S1A, B). Histological examination further revealed distinct follicle development stages: primary follicles (PFs), secondary follicles (SFs), antral follicles (AFs), and corpus lutea (CL) were present in BS (Fig. 1A), whereas ovaries in NBS were mainly composed of PFs and SFs with only a few AFs (Fig. 1B). Quantification of serial H&E-stained sections confirmed higher numbers of growing follicles and CLs in BS (Fig. 1C). Oil Red O staining showed stronger lipid accumulation in GCs in BS (Fig. 1D), while little staining was observed in NBS (Fig. 1E). Quantitative results indicated that the relative total area of lipid droplets significantly increased in BS (Fig. 1F). Consistently, ultrastructural analysis demonstrated more abundant lipid droplets in GCs during BS compared with NBS (Fig. 1G-I). These results suggested that seasonal ovarian activity in muskrats was closely associated with lipid accumulation in GCs, providing a rationale to further examine the regulatory mechanisms of ovarian lipid metabolism.

Ovarian transcriptomic and metabolomic remodeling across seasons

To identify the molecular drivers of the observed phenotypic changes, we first performed transcriptomic analysis of the ovaries. The quality of the raw reads from muskrat ovarian samples was summarized in Table S3. The assembly statistics of transcript and unigene in the ovaries of muskrats were shown in Table S4. A total of 2,580 genes were differentially expressed between the BS and NBS groups, with 1,370 genes upregulated and 1,210 genes downregulated in NBS relative to BS (Fig. 2A). GO enrichment analysis showed that DEGs were associated with fatty acid biosynthetic process, lipid metabolism, and vitamin metabolism-related pathways (Fig. 2B, Table S5). KEGG analysis further highlighted energy metabolism-related pathways in regulation of seasonal changes in ovarian function, including PPAR, FoxO, and insulin signaling pathways (Fig. 2C, Table S6). Among genes involved in lipid metabolism, Cpt1a, Fox1, Pparg, and Scd1, which were important for lipid transportation and metabolism, were markedly up-regulated in BS group (Fig. 2D). In summary, these results suggest that seasonal variations in lipid metabolism are linked to ovarian function in the muskrats.

To determine whether these transcriptomic alterations were reflected at the metabolite level, we next conducted non-targeted metabolomic profiling of ovaries from the BS and NBS groups. Total ion chromatograms (TIC) results indicated high instrument stability and repeatability (Fig. S2). Correlation analysis of QC samples suggested that these data are accurate and reliable (Fig. S3A, B). From PCA score plots in positive (Fig. S3C) and negative ion mode (Fig. S3D), BS and NBS samples were totally overlapped. Partial least square discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) score plots showed that the BS group and NBS group were effectively divided into two categories in both ion modes (Fig. 3A, C). R2Y and Q2 values were greater than 0.9 and 0.5 respectively (Fig. 3B, D). The upward trend in regression line demonstrated the stable and effectiveness of the model. Class Ⅰ annotations of ovarian metabolites are shown in Fig. 3F, including fatty acyl (FA, 6.74%), glycerophospholipid (GP, 3.43%), glyceride (GL, 0.86%), bile acids (0.75%), and sphingolipid (SP, 0.49%). A total of 182 DMs were identified between NBS and BS, with 145 DMs up-regulated and 37 DMs down-regulated respectively (Fig. 3E). The heat map showed the expression profiles of DMs in each group (Fig. 3G). Importantly, KEGG pathway analysis of these metabolites also showed significant enrichment in fatty acid biosynthesis, citrate cycle, glycerophospholipid metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, etc. (Fig. 3H). Collectively, transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses consistently pointed to lipid metabolism remodeling as a core molecular feature underlying seasonal changes in the muskrat ovary.

Enhanced lipid metabolism in the breeding-season ovary

The integration of transcriptomic and metabolomic data suggested that lipid metabolism plays a central role in seasonal changes in muskrat ovarian function. To further evaluate this, we examined the expression and distribution of genes involved in lipid metabolism in the ovary. The mRNA levels of Dgat1, Lpl, Fas, Cpt1a, Acadl, and Hadh transcripts were remarkably decreased from BS to NBS (Fig. 4A), whereas there was no significant difference in Hsl. The distribution patterns of genes (DGAT1, CPT1A, ACADL, HADH, LPL, HSL, and FAS) involved in lipid metabolism were shown in Fig. 4B and Fig. S4. The positive DGAT1, CPT1A, ACADL, HADH, LPL, HSL, and FAS staining were present in the ovarian GCs. Meanwhile, the protein levels of DGAT1, LPL, CPT1A, HADH, LDLR, and PPARγ were increased in BS (Fig. 4C). Although serum TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, and FFA contents were not considerably different between BS and NBS (Fig. 4D), ovarian TG content was notably higher in BS than in NBS (Fig. 4E). Together, these findings indicate that ovarian lipid metabolism is specifically enhanced during BS, consistent with the transcriptomic and metabolomic signatures observed.

Seasonal variation of circulating VD3 and ovarian VDR expression

Given the transcriptomic enrichment of vitamin metabolism pathways and the observed enhancement of ovarian lipid metabolism across seasons, we next assessed whether VD3 signaling varies seasonally. Serum VD3 concentrations were higher in BS than in NBS (Fig. 5A). VDR signaling was found in the muskrat ovaries in both seasons (Fig. 5B). In the GCs and theca cells, VDR was clearly visible in the nucleus. VDR-positive staining was less prominent in luteal and interstitial cells than in GCs (Fig. 5C). Importantly, quantitative analysis showed that the total protein level of VDR in the ovary was also significantly higher during BS. (Fig. 5D). These observations indicate that the ovary is VD3-responsive and that systemic VD3 availability differs by season.

Plasma metabolomic profiles highlight seasonal differences in bile acids

To further investigate systemic metabolic factors potentially influencing VD3 availability, we analyzed plasma metabolomic profiles from BS and NBS groups. The TIC results showed high instrument stability and repeatability (Fig. S5). PCA score plot indicated that plasma metabolomic profile of BS group was clearly distinct from those of NBS group (Fig. 6E). PLS-DA score plots showed that the BS group and the NBS group were effectively divided into two categories in both ion modes (Fig. 6A, C). R2Y and Q2 of both PLS-DA models were above 0.9 and 0.8 respectively (Fig. 6B, D). The upward trend in regression line demonstrated the stable and effectiveness of the model. As shown in Fig. 6F, a total of 473 DMs were identified between BS and NBS, of which 275 DMs were up-regulated and 198 DMs were down-regulated respectively. These plasma DMs were classified into eight categories (Fig. 6G), including hormones and transmitters, lipids, peptides, vitamins and cofactors, steroids, etc.

Pathway enrichment analysis indicated that seasonal differences were concentrated in primary bile acid biosynthesis, steroid hormone biosynthesis, arachidonic acid metabolism, ABC transporters, etc. (Fig. 6J). In particular, steroid hormone–related metabolites were generally higher in BS (Fig. 6H), while metabolites linked to bile acid biosynthesis showed marked seasonal divergence (Fig. 6I). Notably, the concentration of lithocholic acid glycine conjugate (LCAGC), a secondary bile acid and known VDR ligand, was significantly higher in NBS than in BS, suggesting a potential inhibitory influence on systemic VD3 signaling.

Gut microbiota composition shifts between BS and NBS

Given the seasonal alteration of the gut-derived metabolite LCAGC, we next characterized the cecal microbiota composition to identify its potential source. 16 S rRNA sequencing revealed that the BS group has 103 unique OTUs, the NBS group has 222 unique OTUs, and the BS and NBS groups share 1,048 OTUs (Fig. S6A). The beta diversity displayed by PCoA revealed that BS group formed a unique cluster away from NBS group (Fig. S6B). The alpha diversity of gut microbiota did not differ significantly between the BS and NBS groups (Fig. 7A). Linked-bar plots demonstrated that BS and NBS muskrats have a significantly altered gut microbiota composition at phylum, family, and genus levels (Fig. 7B), respectively. In particular, the abundance of Lactobacillus increased from BS to NBS. The cladogram of LEfSe and histogram of LDA score revealed that Desulfobacterota, Ruminococcaceae, Oscillospiraceae, and Clostridia were the marker bacteria of BS group; Lactobacillus, Bacteroidales, Muribaculaceae, Limosilactobacillus, Bacilli, and Bifidobacterium were the marker bacteria of NBS group (Fig. 7C, D). Compared with the NBS group, gut microbiota of BS group had lower functionality of the majority pathways (Fig. 7E). Similarity percentage (Simper) analysis showed the top 20 bacteria and their abundances that contributed to the differences between BS and NBS groups (Fig. 7F). Notably, the analysis highlighted Lactobacillus and its related taxa (Limosilactobacillus, Bacilli) as the most significant biomarkers for the NBS group, consistent with the elevated plasma LCA levels during this period.

Associations among Lactobacillus, lithocholic acid, serum VD3, and ovarian TG

To formally test the relationships among the key seasonal variables identified in the gut, plasma, and serum, we next performed correlation analyses. The scatter plot and linear correlation result showed that Lactobacillus abundance exhibited a favorable positive correlation with LCAGC content in the muskrats (Fig. 8A, r = 0.75, P = 0.0074, n = 6). Meanwhile, both Lactobacillus abundance and LCAGC content in the muskrats were negatively correlated with VD3 level (Fig. 8B, C; r = -0.66, P = 0.019; r = -0.68, P = 0.016; n = 6). Additionally, ovarian TG content exhibited a favorable positive correlation with serum VD3 level in the muskrats (Fig. 8D, r = 0.97, P = 0.001, n = 3). These results establish a quantitative link between the dominant NBS gut microbe, a key bile acid metabolite, and the seasonal fluctuations of circulating VD3, as well as imply the action of circulating VD3 on ovarian lipid metabolism.

VD3 treatment promotes lipid metabolism in cultured GCs

Finally, to examine whether VD3 directly regulate lipid metabolism in ovarian GCs in vitro, we performed RNA-seq analysis of GCs treated with 10 nM VD3 and untreated GCs. The raw data of RNA-seq analysis were firstly processed for quality control and downstream analyses were based on the clean data with high quality (Table S7). The muskrat GCs from two groups were identified with 71,184 transcripts, of which 31,785 transcripts were found in untreated GCs and 9,403 transcripts were found in GCs treated with 10 nM VD3, with 29,996 transcripts for both groups (Fig. 9A). The volcano graphic reflected a total of 2,042 DEGs, with 1,474 genes down-regulated and 568 genes up-regulated (Fig. 9B). As shown in Fig. 9C, lipid metabolism-related pathways were observed in KEGG enrichment analysis. DEGs involved in lipid metabolism-related pathways were selected for heat map clustering analysis as shown in Fig. 9D. GO analysis revealed that a high number of transcripts were enriched in lipid binding, sphingolipid binding, phospholipid binding, phospholipid-translocating ATPase complex, and so on (Fig. 9E). As shown in Fig. 9F, treatment with VD3 significantly induces certain lipid metabolism-related genes in the muskrat GCs cultured in vitro. Treatment with 10 nM of VD3 significantly increased SREBF1, Hsl, Scd1, Dgat1, Cpt1a, and Acadl mRNA expression, while treatment with 100 nM of VD3 significantly increased Hsl and Acadl mRNA expression (P < 0.05). These results demonstrate that VD3 can promote the expression of key lipid metabolism genes in muskrat GCs under in vitro conditions.

Discussion

Seasonal environmental changes and their effects on host physiology and ecology can significantly alter animal gut microbiota. Nevertheless, the reproductive implications of these gut microbiota changes for seasonally breeding mammals remain to be explored. In this study, we investigated whether the gut microbiota-VD3 axis is relevant to seasonal regulation of ovarian lipid metabolism in muskrats by integrating multi-omics analyses.

The phenotypic alterations in the muskrat ovaries across various breeding seasons were consistent with other seasonal breeders, such as water voles [43] and wild ground squirrels [44], who also displayed marked seasonal variations in ovaries and oocytes that were unable to develop to their full potential in NBS. Meanwhile, oil red O staining was detected in the muskrat ovarian GCs in BS, whereas it was difficult to observe in NBS. Ultrastructural observations showed that there are many lipid droplets within the GCs in BS, whereas in NBS, there are few lipid droplets within the follicles. Meanwhile, ovarian TG content was notably increased in BS compared to NBS. The corpus luteum is known to have a higher lipid content due to its function in steroidogenesis, which may influence the overall TG measurements in ovarian tissue. Previous studies have established the evidence of lipid metabolism, particularly DNL, in goose follicles and additionally indicated its involvement in GCs function [2, 45]. In bovine ovarian follicles, seasonal variations in oocyte developmental competence were related to follicular fatty acid composition [46]. Based on our results, lipids might be associated with the potential regulation of follicle development of the muskrats in BS.

A variety of studies have indicated that lipid metabolism is linked to reproductive processes such as reproductive hormone synthesis and embryonic development [47, 48]. Transcriptomic analyses of the muskrat ovaries revealed that several genes related to lipid synthesis, catabolism and regulation were significantly differentially expressed between the BS and NBS groups. These DEGs were mainly concentrated in PPAR signaling pathway, ferroptosis, and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids. Metabolomics analyses further supported this finding, with DMs were remarkably enriched in glycerophospholipid metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis, steroid hormone biosynthesis. It was reported that PPAR signaling pathway was implicated in ovarian follicle development [49]. Exposure of zebrafish to polystyrene induced PCOS-like ovulatory dysfunction through effects on PPAR signaling pathway and dysregulation of lipid metabolism, including glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, arachidonic acid, and α-linolenic acid [50]. In rabbit GCs, deregulation of PPAR signaling pathway was consistent with the reduction of several metabolites such as glycerophosphocholine, arachidic acid, and palmitic acid, with implications for follicular development [51]. There were increased lipid metabolism and energy dynamics to cope with high energy demands of ovarian development in Acipenser sinensis [52]. In this study, the immunolocalization, gene expression, and protein expression results suggest that DNL and FAO processes in the muskrat ovaries were more active in BS, which are similar to the role that lipid metabolism plays in the follicle development in other animals. In sheep, locally enriched melatonin content in the ovaries up-regulated lipid metabolism-related signaling pathways and promoted lipid metabolism in the follicular cells, which in turn supported follicular development [53]. In bovine ovarian follicles, there was an increase in the abundance of lipid metabolism-related genes during the follicular to luteal phase transition [54], and free FAs and cholesterol were involved in the pre-ovulatory maturation of oocyte-cumulus complex and the ovulation process [55]. Up-regulation of FAO has been reported to benefit folliculogenesis and developmental competence of mouse follicles cultured in vitro [56]. In this study, the expression patterns of lipid metabolism-related genes in the muskrat ovaries were consistent with the results of transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses, suggesting that DNL and FAO processes were increased in the muskrat ovaries to provide energy for follicular-luteal transition in BS.

In this study, we supplemented cultured muskrat GCs in vitro with VD3, and reported the transcriptomic analysis of VD3-treated GCs and untreated GCs for the identification of the mechanism of VD3 regulating the muskrat GCs function. Our results showed that 10 nM VD3 supplementation up-regulated fatty acid β-oxidation and de novo lipogenesis of the muskrat GCs. However, 100 nM VD3 treatment did not significantly affect the muskrat GCs, possibly due to the cytotoxicity effects at higher doses. It has been reported that Scd1 is a multifunctional regulator of the fatty acid network that is essential for mouse oocyte maintenance and differentiation during early follicular genesis [47]. Our previous study has demonstrated the role of VD3 in steroidogenesis in the muskrat GCs [32]. It could be inferred that this might also be one of the ways in which VD3 regulates GCs function and indirectly affected follicular development of the muskrats.

VD3 is a pivotal regulator of numerous physiological processes, whose primary source in mammals is endogenous synthesis in the skin [57]. This process is highly dependent on seasonal photoperiod, with longer daylight hours in BS enhancing VD3 production, while shorter light exposure in NBS-such as the short photoperiod observed in muskrats during winter months-markedly reduces its synthesis. Additionally, dietary intake of VD3 from animal-based foods or supplements serves as a secondary source, though its contribution varies with seasonal food availability [10]. Given these dynamics, circulating VD3 levels typically exhibit pronounced seasonal fluctuations, aligning with reproductive cycles in photoperiod-sensitive species like the muskrat. Beyond these canonical pathways, recent research has implicated the gut microbiota as a potential modulator of VD3 homeostasis, possibly through the production of secondary bile acids like lithocholic acid (LCA), a known ligand of VDR [58]. 16 S rRNA-seq results showed that the abundance of Lactobacillus was increased and Lactobacillus was one of the characteristic bacteria in NBS. In this study, blood metabolomics data showed that primary bile acid biosynthesis, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and arachidonic acid metabolism were significantly enriched. Lithocholic acid glycine conjugate content was considerably higher in NBS than in BS, indicating that the level of LCA was increased in NBS. Lactobacillus could modulate the level of VD3 transporter in the enterocytes, thereby reducing serum VD3 levels [27], which was consistent with our previous report that VD3 level was reduced from BS to NBS [32]. In mice, disruption of gut microbiota and decreased VD3 levels, resulting in a decline in oocyte quality [59]. While sunlight and diet remain the predominant determinants of VD3 status, this study investigates their roles in driving seasonal ovarian lipid metabolism in muskrats, alongside the association with gut microbiota during periods of reduced light exposure.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings strongly suggest the involvement of ovarian lipid metabolism in regulating seasonal variations in muskrat ovarian function. We further show that that gut microbiota, particularly Lactobacillus, may contribute to these changes by modulating circulating VD3 levels and related metabolites. In vitro experiments confirmed that VD3 promotes lipid droplet catabolism and fatty acid oxidation in muskrat GCs (Fig. 10). To our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate high-throughput analyses of gut microbiota and plasma metabolites across breeding seasons in muskrats. Collectively, these results provide new ecological insights into the interplay between gut microbiota and ovarian physiology, laying a foundation for future improvements in reproductive management and conservation strategies for wildlife, including muskrats.

Nevertheless, this study is not without limitations. At the time this study was conducted, a reference genome was unavailable. Consequently, de novo assembly resulted in the absence of RefSeq-based annotations, as well as potential redundancy or fragmentation in the transcriptome. While the data strongly support associations and a plausible regulatory axis, much of the evidence linking gut microbiota to ovarian function via VD3 remains correlative. The direct relationships remain to be further experimentally validated. Furthermore, the environmental and nutritional variables naturally differed between BS and NBS, and were not manipulated independently. These factors might influence VD3-related measures and ovarian lipid metabolism.
