Association of mean blood glucose, glycemic variability, and their combined effect with 28-day mortality in critically ill ICU patients with acute myocardial infarction: a MIMIC-IV retrospective cohort study
Authors: Xinlin Luo, Jun Zhou, Fenghuan Hu, Dongzhou Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDysglycemia is frequent in critically ill patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and is linked to adverse outcomes. However, the relative contributions of sustained hyperglycemia and short-term glucose fluctuations, as well as their combined effects, remain incompletely defined in the intensive care unit (ICU). Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the independent and joint associations of mean blood glucose (MBG) and glycemic variability (GV) with 28-day mortality in critically ill ICU patients with AMI.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using the MIMIC-IV database, including adult ICU patients with AMI. MBG and GV, defined as the mean and standard deviation of all glucose measurements during ICU stay, were calculated prior to outcome occurrence. The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality. Multivariable Cox models were used with adjustment for demographics, comorbidities, laboratory variables, illness severity, and ICU treatments.ResultsAmong 1,817 patients, 374 (20.6%) died within 28 days. The mean age was 70.4±12.8 years, and 66.2% were male. Higher MBG and GV were independently associated with mortality [adjusted hazard ratio (HR) per 10 mg/dL increase: 1.15 (95% CI: 1.12–1.18) and 1.03 (95% CI: 1.01–1.06), respectively]. In joint analyses, patients with high MBG and low GV had the greatest risk. Risk patterns differed according to diabetes status.ConclusionsIn critically ill ICU patients with AMI, both MBG and GV are independently associated with short-term mortality, with a stronger association observed for MBG. Considering both metrics may improve risk stratification and support individualized glycemic management.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Higher mean blood glucose (MBG) and greater glycemic variability (GV) were independently linked to higher 28-day mortality in acute myocardial infarction patients.

• In patients without diabetes, the highest risk occurred with high MBG but low GV; MBG had an inverted U-shaped mortality relationship. In patients with diabetes, the highest risk was seen with both high MBG and high GV, where MBG correlated linearly with mortality. GV showed a linear association with mortality in both groups.

• The effect was stronger in elderly patients.

What is known and what is new?

• Dysglycemia was common in critical illness and tied to worse acute myocardial infarction outcomes via oxidative stress and inflammation. Prior studies often assessed MBG or GV separately.

• This study jointly evaluated MBG and GV, showing combined assessment improved prognostic value. Risk patterns differed by diabetes status: high MBG with low GV was riskiest in patients without diabetes, while high MBG with high GV was riskiest in patients with diabetes.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Using only one glucose metric may miss high-risk profiles, which differ in patients with and without diabetes. ICU management should integrate both MBG and GV for early risk stratification. Glycemic protocols should consider individualized targets based on diabetes status and combined MBG/GV profiles, not just mean glucose levels.

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains a major global health burden and continues to be associated with substantial short-term mortality despite advances in reperfusion therapy and critical care (1). Dysglycemia is common in patients with AMI and has been consistently linked to adverse outcomes (2,3). Acute hyperglycemia may exacerbate myocardial injury through oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, platelet activation, and vascular inflammation (4,5). In addition to sustained hyperglycemia, glucose fluctuations may impose further biological stress and contribute to adverse cardiovascular remodeling (6).

According to the American Diabetes Association (ADA) Standards of Care in Diabetes—2025 (7), a target blood glucose range of 140–180 mg/dL is recommended for most critically ill patients receiving insulin therapy, balancing the risks of hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia. Similarly, the 2023 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for acute coronary syndromes emphasize individualized metabolic management in patients with AMI (8). However, the optimal glycemic strategy in critically ill patients remains uncertain, and the long-standing debate between tighter and more moderate glucose control persists (9).

Most prior studies in AMI have focused on single glycemic metrics, whereas glycemic variability (GV) has been relatively underexplored (2,3,6). Evaluating mean blood glucose (MBG) or GV alone may not fully capture the complexity of dysglycemia during critical illness. A combined assessment may better reflect cumulative glycemic burden and short-term instability, thereby improving risk stratification.

We therefore conducted a retrospective cohort study using the MIMIC-IV database to examine the independent and joint associations of MBG and GV with 28-day mortality in critically ill ICU patients with AMI. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0324/rc) (10).

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was based on the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV, version 3.0), a publicly available, single-center database comprising detailed clinical data from patients admitted to the intensive care units of Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC), Boston, between 2008 and 2019 (11). The database includes records from 94,458 ICU admissions corresponding to 65,366 unique patients and integrates longitudinal information on demographics, diagnoses, laboratory measurements, treatments, and clinical outcomes across 523,740 inpatient encounters. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Access to the MIMIC-IV database was obtained after completion of the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) training program by one author (J.Z.; certification no. 65830225). As this study involved analysis of de-identified data, it was exempt from clinical trial registration. The study was designed as a retrospective observational cohort analysis in accordance with relevant reporting guidelines.

Patient selection was based on prespecified criteria. Eligible patients met all of the following inclusion criteria:

a diagnosis of AMI identified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes; and

a diagnosis of AMI identified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes; and

age >18 years at the time of ICU admission.

age >18 years at the time of ICU admission.

Patients were excluded if any of the following conditions were present:

the ICU admission was not the first for the index hospitalization;

the ICU admission was not the first for the index hospitalization;

the length of ICU stay was ≤2 days; or

the length of ICU stay was ≤2 days; or

data on survival status or glycemic measurements were unavailable.

data on survival status or glycemic measurements were unavailable.

The final analytic cohort consisted of 1,817 patients.

Data extraction and variable definitions

Clinical data were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database using structured query language (SQL) with Navicat Premium (version 16.2). Patient identification and variable extraction were performed using predefined SQL queries based on standardized database schemas and coding definitions. Extracted variables were prespecified and categorized as follows.

Demographic information included age, sex, marital status, race, ICU admission type, and body mass index (BMI). Pre-existing comorbidities were identified based on documented diagnoses and included congestive heart failure, diabetes mellitus, cerebrovascular disease, severe liver disease, hypertension, and cardiogenic shock.

Laboratory variables comprised white blood cell count, hemoglobin, platelet count, serum creatinine, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), cardiac troponin I (cTnI), N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), triglycerides, total cholesterol, and all recorded blood glucose measurements. Baseline laboratory values were defined as the first available measurements obtained after ICU admission. Disease severity at ICU admission was assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index, Acute Physiology Score III (APSIII), and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score.

ICU-related treatments included continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), mechanical ventilation (MV), insulin therapy, vasoactive agents, statins, β-blockers, calcium channel blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, thrombolytic therapy with alteplase, antiplatelet agents, anticoagulants, and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).

MBG was calculated as the average of all biochemical glucose measurements obtained during the ICU stay. GV was defined as the standard deviation (SD) of these glucose values. In addition, the coefficient of variation (CV) was calculated as (SD/MBG) × 100% as a supplementary measure of GV. Both metrics were calculated using glucose measurements obtained prior to the occurrence of death or censoring to minimize reverse causation. Hypoglycemia was defined as at least one blood glucose value <70 mg/dL during ICU admission. Follow-up time was calculated from ICU admission to death or censoring at 28 days, whichever occurred first.

Outcome

The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality. In the MIMIC-IV database, mortality information is derived from in-hospital records and linked follow-up data. For patients discharged before 28 days, survival status was determined using available follow-up information within the database. Follow-up time was calculated from ICU admission to death or censoring at 28 days, whichever occurred first. The secondary outcome was the occurrence of hypoglycemia during the ICU stay.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate based on their distribution. Between-group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance for normally distributed variables and the Kruskal-Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were reported as counts with percentages and compared using the Chi-squared test.

Missing data were addressed using multiple imputation. Variables with more than 40% missingness (including triglycerides, cholesterol, and NT-proBNP) were excluded a priori. For all remaining variables with less than 20% missingness, five multiple imputed datasets were generated and pooled for subsequent statistical analyses.

MBG and GV were categorized into tertiles based on their empirical distributions: MBG <121.83, 121.83–154.11, and ≥154.11 mg/dL; GV <23.82, 23.82–45.77, and ≥45.77 mg/dL. For interaction analyses, MBG and GV were further dichotomized, with the highest tertile defined as “high” and the two lower tertiles combined as “low”.

Univariable analyses were initially performed to explore associations between baseline clinical characteristics and 28-day mortality. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression was subsequently applied to quantify the associations of MBG, GV, and their combined categories with mortality. Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and existing literature and were retained in the final models if their inclusion resulted in a ≥10% change in the estimated associations of the primary exposures. Three hierarchical models with increasing degrees of adjustment were constructed. MBG and GV were analyzed as both continuous and predefined categorical variables.

Additional analyses were conducted using the CV as an alternative measure of GV. CV was analyzed as both a continuous variable and in tertiles using the same modeling strategy.

Additive interaction between MBG and GV was assessed using the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion (AP), and synergy index (SI), based on multivariable-adjusted hazard ratios derived from joint exposure categories.

The robustness of the primary findings was evaluated through sensitivity analyses using complete-case (non-imputed) datasets. Restricted cubic spline models were used to assess potential nonlinear relationships between MBG, GV, and mortality risk.

Survival probabilities were visualized using Kaplan-Meier curves stratified by MBG, GV, and their combined categories, with between-group differences assessed by the log-rank test. Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted according to age (<65 vs. ≥65 years), AMI type, sex, and diabetes status, and the results were summarized using forest plots.

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2) and Free Statistics software (version 2.0). A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 1,817 patients with AMI were included in the final analysis, among whom 374 (20.6%) died within 28 days. The patient selection process is illustrated in Figure 1, and baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. The mean age was 70.4±12.8 years, and 1,203 patients (66.2%) were male. Overall, 885 patients (48.7%) had diabetes, and 284 (15.6%) experienced at least one episode of hypoglycemia during the ICU stay. Regarding AMI subtype, 444 patients (24.4%) had ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and 1,373 (75.6%) had non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI).

Compared with survivors, non-survivors were older and had higher Charlson Comorbidity Index, APSIII, and SOFA scores. They were more likely to have comorbidities, including congestive heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, renal dysfunction, severe liver disease, hypertension, sepsis, and cardiogenic shock.

Notably, non-survivors had longer ICU stays but shorter total hospital lengths of stay than survivors.

In the overall cohort, higher MBG was independently associated with an increased risk of 28-day mortality [adjusted hazard ratio (HR) per 10 mg/dL increase, 1.15; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.12–1.18]. Relative to the lowest MBG tertile, mortality risk increased stepwise in Tertile 2 (HR, 2.14; 95% CI: 1.55–2.97) and Tertile 3 (HR, 3.58; 95% CI: 2.51–5.12; P for trend <0.001), with similar directionality observed among patients with and without diabetes.

GV was also independently associated with 28-day mortality in the overall cohort (adjusted HR per 10 mg/dL increase, 1.03; 95% CI: 1.01–1.06). In tertile analyses, patients in the highest GV category had a higher risk of death compared with those in the lowest category (HR, 1.90; 95% CI: 1.36–2.66; P for trend =0.001). In stratified analyses, the association between GV and mortality remained evident among patients without diabetes (adjusted HR per 10 mg/dL, 1.04; 95% CI: 1.01–1.07), whereas the tertile-based gradient was attenuated among patients with diabetes (Table 2).

In joint analyses, distinct risk patterns emerged by diabetes status. Among patients without diabetes, those with high MBG (≥154.11 mg/dL) and low GV (<23.82 mg/dL) had the highest risk of 28-day mortality compared with the low-MBG/low-GV reference group (HR, 4.14; 95% CI: 2.46–6.98). In contrast, among patients with diabetes, the greatest risk was observed in those with both high MBG (≥154.11 mg/dL) and high GV (≥45.77 mg/dL) (HR, 2.11; 95% CI: 1.29–3.48).

Restricted cubic spline analyses demonstrated differential exposure–response relationships by diabetes status (Figure 3). Among patients without diabetes, MBG exhibited a significant nonlinear (inverted U-shaped) association with 28-day mortality (P for nonlinearity =0.002), whereas the relationship was predominantly linear among patients with diabetes (P for nonlinearity =0.075) (Figure 3A,3B). In contrast, GV showed largely linear associations with mortality in both groups (Figure 3C,3D).

Additional analyses using the CV, both in the overall cohort and stratified by diabetes status, showed that the association with 28-day mortality was attenuated after multivariable adjustment and was no longer statistically significant (Table S2).

Additive interaction analyses did not demonstrate a positive synergistic effect between MBG and GV. The estimated RERI and SI suggested a tendency toward antagonism, indicating that the joint effect of high MBG and high GV was lower than expected based on their individual associations. Consistent findings were observed on the multiplicative scale, where the interaction term indicated attenuation rather than amplification of risk (Table S3).

Associations of MBG and GV with hypoglycemia

In the overall cohort, multivariable analyses showed significant associations between hypoglycemia and both MBG (adjusted HR, 0.93; 95% CI: 0.90–0.97) and GV (adjusted HR, 1.05; 95% CI: 1.02–1.07). In stratified analyses, the highest incidence of hypoglycemia was observed among patients with low MBG and high GV in both groups with and without diabetes (Table S4).

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of the findings, several sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, analyses using the complete-case (non-imputed) dataset, in which MBG and GV were categorized into the same tertiles as in the primary analysis, yielded results consistent with those obtained from the imputed dataset. Both MBG and GV, analyzed as continuous and categorical variables, remained significantly associated with 28-day mortality in multivariable models. In joint analyses, patients with high MBG and low GV continued to have the highest mortality risk relative to the low-MBG/low-GV reference group (Table S5). Second, in analyses restricted to patients aged 65 years or older, MBG and GV remained significantly associated with 28-day mortality across all modeling approaches. When analyzed as continuous variables, the adjusted HRs were 1.17 (95% CI: 1.13–1.20) for MBG and 1.06 (95% CI: 1.03–1.08) for GV. Within this older subgroup, those with high MBG and low GV had the highest mortality risk (HR, 2.81; 95% CI: 1.92–4.11) compared with the low-MBG/low-GV reference group (Table S6). Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of ICU patients with AMI, both MBG and GV were independently associated with 28-day mortality across diabetic and non-diabetic populations, regardless of whether these metrics were modeled as continuous or categorical variables. Importantly, their joint assessment provided incremental prognostic information beyond either metric alone and revealed distinct glycemic-mortality patterns according to diabetes status. Among patients without diabetes, elevated MBG accompanied by relatively low GV was associated with the highest mortality risk, whereas in patients with diabetes, concurrent elevations in both MBG and GV conferred the greatest risk of death.

Dysglycemia is common in critically ill patients and reflects stress-related disruptions of glucose homeostasis mediated by counter-regulatory hormones, including glucagon, cortisol, thyroid hormones, and growth hormone. Nutritional support in the ICU further amplifies glycemic instability and contributes to adverse outcomes (12). Previous studies (13) have shown that glucose metrics incorporating measurements across the entire hospitalization outperform admission glucose alone for risk prediction, with persistent hyperglycemia demonstrating stronger prognostic relevance than early or isolated elevations. In this context, MBG serves as a practical indicator of cumulative glycemic burden. Mechanistically, sustained hyperglycemia promotes oxidative stress and inflammation, impairs endothelial function and myocardial repair, and exacerbates ischemic injury (14), thereby increasing short-term mortality risk (5,6,15).

GV represents a complementary dimension of dysglycemia by capturing short-term glucose fluctuations that are not reflected by mean levels (16). Experimental and clinical studies have linked elevated GV to coronary plaque vulnerability and myocardial fibrosis (17,18), as well as adverse ventricular remodeling that predisposes to arrhythmia and hemodynamic instability (19,20). In addition, marked glucose variability may activate sympathetic-adrenal pathways, increasing susceptibility to both hypoglycemic and hyperglycemic episodes (21). Previous observational studies have reported associations between high GV and increased mortality or major adverse cardiovascular events following AMI, regardless of diabetes status. Collectively, these findings indicate that the prognostic value of GV is context dependent and is most informative when interpreted in conjunction with MBG rather than as an isolated metric (22).

Given the prognostic relevance of both MBG and GV, combining these metrics may improve risk stratification in critically ill patients, although evidence regarding their joint effects remains limited. Prior ICU studies have shown that among patients without diabetes, mortality increases with higher MBG and GV, with the greatest risk observed in those with high MBG but low GV (23,24). In contrast, associations between MBG, GV, and mortality appear weaker or more heterogeneous in patients with diabetes, with some studies identifying GV as the dominant predictor (23), while others emphasize stress hyperglycemia-related indices (25). These discrepancies may reflect distinct physiological responses to dysglycemia between patients with and without diabetes. Consistent with this concept, our findings indicate that in AMI patients without diabetes, both hyperglycemia and marked GV were associated with increased mortality, whereas in patients with diabetes, sustained hyperglycemia appeared to confer the greatest risk.

In the subgroup analysis, we observed differential associations of MBG and GV across age subgroups Among patients with AMI aged ≥65 years, elevated glucose levels and fluctuations were associated with a more pronounced increase in 28-day mortality. In contrast, GV did not significantly affect mortality among those aged <65 years. These findings may be attributable to vascular sclerosis, endothelial dysfunction (26,27), and increased susceptibility to glycemic abnormalities in older adults. However, they differ from the results reported by Su and Lu (19,28), indicating the need for further investigation to clarify this discrepancy.

While SD reflects absolute glucose dispersion, CV expresses variability relative to mean glucose and is intended to reduce dependence on average glycemic levels. However, in the context of marked dysglycemia, these metrics may capture partially distinct aspects of glycemic instability, which may explain their different behavior after adjustment.

Interestingly, interaction analyses did not support a synergistic effect between MBG and GV. Instead, both additive and multiplicative interaction metrics suggested a potential antagonistic relationship, whereby the joint effect of high MBG and high GV was less than expected based on their individual contributions.

This finding may reflect overlapping or competing biological pathways, such as shared mechanisms of oxidative stress and metabolic dysregulation, which may limit the incremental prognostic contribution of GV in the presence of substantial hyperglycemia. Alternatively, in the context of marked hyperglycemia, the effect of glucose variability may be attenuated, potentially reflecting a ceiling effect of metabolic stress.

This study has several strengths, including a large sample size, complete 28-day mortality follow-up, and a comprehensive joint evaluation of MBG and GV in critically ill patients with AMI, with stratified analyses according to diabetes status.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the retrospective, single-center design based on the MIMIC-IV database may limit generalizability; however, the large cohort and extensive adjustment for clinical and severity-related covariates help to strengthen the internal validity of the findings. Second, although the timing and frequency of glucose measurements varied across patients, we sought to mitigate potential time-related bias through comprehensive multivariable adjustment, nonlinear modeling, and consistent findings across subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Finally, as with all observational studies, causal inferences cannot be established, and residual confounding by unmeasured factors remains possible.

Conclusions

In critically ill patients with AMI, higher MBG was consistently associated with increased 28-day mortality and represented the most robust prognostic indicator. GV, assessed using standard deviation, was also associated with mortality; however, analyses using the coefficient of variation yielded attenuated associations after adjustment.

Interaction analyses did not support a synergistic effect between mean glucose levels and GV. Instead, the joint effect appeared to be less than expected based on their individual contributions, suggesting a complex interplay rather than simple additive risk.

Overall, integrating both the magnitude and pattern of glycemic dysregulation may improve risk stratification, although the independent contribution of relative GV should be interpreted with caution.
