A BAI1-PSTB-Hydrogel promotes diabetic wound healing by targeting mtDNA leakage and the cGAS–STING axis to alleviate endothelial senescence
Authors: Xianqi Wang, Rui Zheng, Wenjie Cheng, Yanan Xu, Xian-jie Xu, Heliang Fu, Dan Wu, Peiwen Wang, Yang Zhang, Huirong Wu, Jijun Chen, Peng Zhao, Shanshou Liu, Xiaoli Liang, Wen Yin, Kuo Shen, Junjie Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic Wound (DW) is one of the most prevalent complications in diabetes patients, impairing patient wellbeing and quality of life severely. The pathogenesis of DW remains incompletely understood, hindering the development of targeted therapies.MethodsWe analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing data and tissue samples from DW patients and healthy controls to investigate characteristic of DW. A high-glucose (30 mM) stimulation model was used to mimic Vascular endothelial cells （VECs) injury in vitro. We evaluated the effects of BAI1 on high-glucose–induced VECs dysfunction, cellular senescence, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) leakage, and cGAS–STING pathway activation. To enable topical wound delivery, BAI1 was loaded into mesoporous silica nanoparticles and encapsulated within a polyvinyl alcohol–alginate–tannic acid–borax composite hydrogel. A diabetic mouse wound model was established to assess the therapeutic efficacy of the BAI1-loaded hydrogel.ResultsSingle-cell sequencing and histopathological analyses revealed severe VECs injury in diabetic wounds, accompanied by STING activation and cellular senescence. In vitro, high glucose induced mtDNA leakage, cGAS–STING activation, and senescence in VECs. BAI1 treatment attenuated mtDNA leakage, suppressed STING activation, reduced senescence, and restored VECs function. The BAI1-loaded hydrogel significantly accelerated wound closure and improved healing quality in diabetic mice. Tissue analysis further demonstrated that the hydrogel reduced STING activation and senescence markers in wounds.ConclusionmtDNA leakage in VECs drive cellular senescence and contribute to impaired healing in diabetic wounds. BAI1 alleviates senescence by inhibiting BAX, thereby reducing mtDNA release and subsequent cGAS–STING activation. The BAI1-PSTB-Hydrogel is a promising topical dressing for DW, with broad clinical translation potential.

Introduction

DWs represent one of the most severe and prevalent complications of diabetes mellitus affecting approximately 15–25% of diabetic patients. DWs account for more than 85% of nontraumatic lower-limb amputations, imposing a tremendous economic burden on patients and society [1]. The pathophysiology of DWs is complex and involves a multifactorial interplay of metabolic dysregulation, immune dysfunction, vasculopathy, neuropathy, mechanical stress, and infection. These unfavorable factors collectively drive cellular dysfunction and lead to recalcitrant chronic wounds [2,3]. Despite advances in DWs management strategies, including glycemic control, debridement, offloading, advanced dressings, and regenerative medicine [4], the development of innovative therapies remains limited by an incomplete understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms. Hence, exploring the molecular mechanisms of DWs and developing targeted therapeutic measures are imperative.

Hyperglycemia-induced vasculopathy accounts for a central pathological hurdle to DWs healing [5,6]. As the vascular lining, VECs are not only structural pillars for maintaining barrier integrity and hemodynamic homeostasis but also the primary executors of angiogenesis. In addition, neovascularization is the foundation of tissue growth and repair, during which time VECs play a pivotal role [7]. However, injury to VECs and subsequent angiogenesis disorder caused by various adverse factors constitute the core pathological mechanism of a wide variety of diseases, including diabetic retinopathy, diabetic nephropathy and DWs. Specifically, metabolic disturbances (hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia), the accumulation of toxic products (e.g., advanced glycation end-products), and oxidative stress in the diabetic milieu impair VEC proliferation and migration, induce premature senescence or programmed cell death, and ultimately diminish the functional VEC pool [8,9]. Interventions targeting VEC apoptosis [10], pyroptosis [11], and ferroptosis [12,13] have shown promise in improving DWs healing, indicating that VECs are viable therapeutic targets. Nevertheless, the precise mechanism underlying VEC injury in DWs remains incompletely understood.

Cellular senescence is a cell state triggered by various stressors. In addition to its role in physiological aging and age-related pathologies, such as osteoarthritis, neurodegenerative diseases, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease [14], senescence is increasingly recognized as a critical player in the pathology of acute and chronic tissue injury [15]. Senescent cells are characterized by irreversible cell-cycle arrest, elevated senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity, a potent senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), mitochondrial dysfunction, and the upregulation of cell-cycle inhibitors such as p53, p21, and p16 [[16], [17], [18]]. Accumulating evidence now positions cellular senescence as a core pathophysiological alteration underlying impaired healing in DWs [17]. Consequently, targeting VEC senescence has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy [[19], [20], [21]]. Hyperglycemia-induced VEC senescence is increasingly viewed as a critical nexus linking metabolic dysregulation to vascular dysfunction and delayed ulcer healing. However, the mechanisms through which hyperglycemia accelerates VEC senescence remain elusive and warrant in-depth investigation.

Mitochondria play a pivotal role in maintaining cellular homeostasis [22]. Their dysfunction is recognized as a hallmark of cellular senescence [23]. Under stress or apoptotic conditions, increased permeability of minority mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (miMOMP) can lead to the opening of BAX/BAK macropores, resulting in the leakage of mitochondrial DNA into the cytoplasm. [24,25]. While minor mtDNA leakage may not induce cell death, it can trigger premature cellular senescence. Cytosolic mtDNA is sensed by cyclic GMP–AMP synthase (cGAS), which activates the adapter protein stimulator of interferon genes (STING) [26,27]. This activation initiates a pro-inflammatory response and drives the senescence program [28,29]. Notably, inhibiting mtDNA leakage or blocking the cGAS–STING pathway has been shown to suppress senescence [30]. However, a critical gap remains: the precise pathways governing mtDNA leakage in DWs are still unelucidated. Therefore, we hypothesize that blocking BAX-mediated mtDNA release and subsequent cGAS–STING activation can inhibit VEC senescence, thereby promoting the healing of DWs. To verify this hypothesis, we analyzed public single-cell transcriptomic data and observed significant enrichment of senescence signatures in VEC from DWs skin. BAI1, a mitochondrial stabilizer, inhibits BAX oligomerization and subsequent mtDNA leakage [31]. We confirmed in vitro that BAI1 alleviates high-glucose–induced VEC injury, mitigates mitochondrial damage, reduces mtDNA leakage, suppresses cGAS–STING activation, and attenuates cellular senescence. To bridge these findings toward clinical translation, we loaded BAI1 into mesoporous silica nanoparticles and encapsulated them within a polyvinyl alcohol–alginate–tannic acid–Borax composite hydrogel, creating a novel topical dressing. In a diabetic (db/db) mouse wound model, this dressing significantly accelerated wound closure and improved healing quality (Scheme 1). Histological analyses further demonstrated that the dressing reduced local oxidative stress, suppressed STING expression, and decreased tissue senescence levels. This study elucidates the critical role of VEC senescence in DWs pathogenesis and provides a strong rationale for the topical application of BAI1 as a promising therapeutic strategy.Scheme 1Schematic illustration of the proposed mechanism: BAX-dependent mtDNA leakage-mediated activation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway in endothelial cells of DWs.

Results

DWs tissues exhibit severe tissue damage, inflammatory responses and cellular senescence

To characterize the cellular alterations in DWs, we analyzed the single-cell transcriptomic dataset GSE165816 from diabetic wounds. Cells were classified into 14 distinct types (Fig. 1A) on the basis of known marker genes (Fig. S1A), including epithelial cells (keratinocytes and glandular cells), stromal cells (fibroblasts and smooth muscle cells), immune cells (M1 and M2 macrophages, T cells, B cells, mast cells, neutrophils, etc.), endothelial cells (VEC and lymphatic endothelial cells). We observed an increased proportion of immune cells and a decreased proportion of epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells in DW patients compared with those in healthy controls (Fig. 1B). To validate these sequencing findings, we collected skin tissue specimens from DWs patients who underwent debridement and from nondiabetic patients with the same age who underwent plastic surgery (healthy controls) for histological analysis (Table S1). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson trichrome, and myeloperoxidase (MPO) immunohistochemical staining demonstrated that DWs tissues displayed severe structural damage, increased inflammatory cell infiltration, reduced dermal collagen content, shortened and disorganized collagen fibers, and diminished skin appendages (hair follicles and sweat glands) (Fig. 1E). Immunohistochemistry for 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) indicated more pronounced DNA damage in DWs tissues (Fig. 1E). KEGG analysis also revealed enrichment in the Rap1 and p53 signaling pathways, which are involved in cell proliferation, apoptosis, and the cell cycle. Accordingly, immunofluorescence costaining for Ki67 and TUNEL showed fewer Ki67-positive cells and more TUNEL-positive cells in DWs tissues (Fig. 1F). These results collectively indicate that DWs tissues are characterized by severe damage, structural disruption, inflammatory infiltration, and DNA damage.

Gene-set enrichment analysis revealed a striking enrichment of cellular senescence pathways among the differentially expressed genes. The role of cellular senescence in wound repair has garnered substantial attention: in acute wounds, transiently senescent cells facilitate healing by secreting chemokines that recruit phagocytes, whereas in chronic wounds such as diabetic ulcers, senescent cells accumulate persistently and drive inflammation via the SASP, thereby impairing tissue repair. To delineate cell-type-specific contributions to the senescent milieu in DWs, we performed senescence gene-set scoring across all subclusters. Fibroblasts, macrophages, and VECs each exhibited elevated senescence scores (Fig. S1C). Immunofluorescence co-staining of clinical wound specimens for p16 with CD31, CD68, and α-SMA was then employed to evaluate the relative senescence burden across these lineages (Fig. S1D–F). Notably, p16 immunoreactivity was most prominently co-localized with CD31 in DW tissues, indicating that VECs undergo more pronounced senescence than macrophages or fibroblasts in the diabetic wound microenvironment. Collectively, these findings establish VEC senescence as a pivotal pathological feature of diabetic wound-healing impairment.

VECs undergo cellular senescence in DWs tissues

Given that impaired angiogenesis due to VEC dysfunction is a major contributor to non-healing wounds and that VEC senescence is prominently elevated in diabetic wound tissues, we focused our subsequent analysis on VECs [32]. On the basis of the marker molecules (Fig. S2A), we further subdivided endothelial cells into lymphatic endothelial cells, arterial endothelial cells, venous endothelial cells(VenEC) and microvascular endothelial cells. In addition, one population lacking a clear classification was defined by its high expression of XIST (Fig. 2A). Pseudotime analysis was performed to compare endothelial cells from DFU wounds and normal skin. Annotation of cell subpopulations revealed that venous endothelial cells and microvascular endothelial cells exhibited the greatest divergence between the two groups during the differentiation trajectory (Fig. 2B). Volcano plots displayed the differentially expressed genes within each endothelial cell subpopulation (Fig. 2C). Venous endothelial cells and microvascular endothelial cells represented the most abundant endothelial cell subpopulations in DW skin tissues (Fig. S2B) and also exhibited the highest number of differentially expressed genes (Fig. S2C). Notably, the abundance of venous endothelial cells was increased in DFU wounds, which may reflect reactive hyperplasia resulting from persistent vascular injury and repair during wound healing. Based on these observations, human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC) were selected as the in vitro cellular model. GO enrichment analysis of venous endothelial cells revealed significant enrichment of biological processes (Fig. 2D). KEGG enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes in venous endothelial cells identified significant enrichment of the cellular senescence signaling pathway (Fig. 2E). GSEA using a senescence-associated gene set demonstrated significant enrichment, accompanied by a markedly increased senescence score (Fig. 2F and G; Fig. S2D). We further validated these conclusions using immunofluorescence co-staining of clinical specimens. Co-staining for BAX (Fig. 2H), STING (Fig. 2I) or p16 (Fig. 2J) with the endothelial marker CD31 resulted in significantly higher expression of BAX, STING and p16 in CD31-positive cells from DWs tissues than in those from healthy skin. Furthermore, DWs tissues exhibited severe vascular damage with narrowed nascent vascular lumens. In summary, VEC in DWs skin exhibit increased expression of BAX, STING and p16 and undergo cellular senescence.

To determine whether VEC senescence directly contributes to the pathogenesis of diabetic wounds, we performed virtual knockout of the senescence-associated genes Cdkn1a and Foxo3 selectively in vascular endothelial cells using scTenifoldKnk. Gene Ontology enrichment analysis of the most significantly perturbed genes following each virtual knockout revealed marked enrichment in pathways controlling endothelial cell fate specification, endothelial cell differentiation, artery development, and arterial morphogenesis — processes intimately coupled to VEC proliferation and angiogenesis (Fig. S2E and F). Collectively, these findings establish VEC senescence as a central driver of the non-healing phenotype in diabetic wounds.

BAI1 mitigates high-glucose–induced VEC injury and mitochondrial damage

Given the observed oxidative stress, inflammation, and VEC senescence in DWs, and considering that BAI1 is a selective allosteric inhibitor of BAX that prevents its mitochondrial translocation and oligomerization, we sought to elucidate the underlying mechanism. We established an in vitro injury model by treating HUVEC with high glucose (HG, 30 mM) and evaluated the effects of BAI1 (schematics in Fig. 3A). To identify the cells as HUVEC, immunofluorescence staining for CD31 was first performed (Fig. S3A). A CCK-8 assay was used to determine the optimal glucose concentration for inducing injury, to rule out the influence of osmotic pressure, an equal amount of mannitol was added as a control (Fig. S3B) and the optimal concentration of BAI1 for rescue (Fig. S3C). CCK-8 assays indicated reduced cell viability in the HG group, which was restored by BAI1 co-treatment (Fig. 3B). VEC function was further assessed via scratch wound, tube formation, and Transwell migration assays. The scratch assay showed delayed wound closure in the HG group, which was rescued by BAI1 (Fig. 3C and D). Tube formation was significantly impaired by HG, and BAI1 restored tube length (Fig. 3E and F). The Transwell assay revealed reduced migratory capacity under HG conditions, which was improved by BAI1 treatment (Fig. 3G and H). To determine the effects of BAI1 on oxidative stress and mitochondrial function, we measured reactive oxygen species (ROS), mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), and DNA damage. Immunofluorescence and flow cytometry analyses revealed a significant increase in ROS levels in HG-treated VEC, which was attenuated by BAI1 (Fig. 3I and J). The MMP, which was assessed using a JC-1 probe, indicated mitochondrial depolarization in the HG group (increased green fluorescence and decreased red fluorescence), which was reversed by BAI1 (Fig. 3K and L). Immunofluorescence staining for 8-OHdG revealed increased DNA damage in the HG group, which was reversed by BAI1 (Fig. 3M). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of VEC showed normal mitochondrial morphology with intact cristae in the control group. HG treatment induced mitochondrial swelling, cristae disruption, and outer membrane dissolution. BAI1 co-treatment restored mitochondrial morphology, with improved cristae structure and membrane integrity (Fig. 3N). These findings indicate that HG induces ROS accumulation, mitochondrial damage, and DNA injury in VEC, all of which are alleviated by BAI1.

BAI1 alleviates high-glucose–induced VEC senescence

Since mitochondrial dysfunction plays a central role in regulating cellular senescence, we next assessed the expression of senescence markers. SA-β-gal staining showed a significant increase in the number of positive cells in the HG group, which was reduced by BAI1 (Fig. 4A). This finding was corroborated by an infrared senescence probe assay (Fig. 4B). Ki67 immunofluorescence staining revealed a decreased number of Ki67-positive cells in the HG-treated group, whereas an increased number of Ki67-positive cells was observed in the BAI1 co-treatment group (Fig. 4C). Western blot analysis revealed upregulation of the senescence markers p16, p21, and p53 in HG-treated VEC, and this upregulation was suppressed by BAI1 (Fig. 4D). Immunofluorescence staining for these proteins yielded consistent results (Fig. 4E). BAI1 reduces mtDNA leakage and inhibits cGAS–STING pathway activation

We next investigated whether the release of mitochondrial content as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), driven by BAX/BAK macropore formation (miMOMP), governs cellular senescence and SASP in VEC. To evaluate BAX oligomerization and activation, we performed immunofluorescence staining using an anti-activation-specific anti-BAX 6A7 antibody alongside the mitochondrial outer membrane marker TOM20, and imaged cells via superresolution structured illumination microscopy (SIM). High glucose exposure markedly enhanced BAX oligomerization and mitochondrial translocation, as indicated by increased colocalization of BAX 6A7 with TOM20; this effect was significantly suppressed by BAI1 treatment (Fig. 5A). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) targeting the mitochondrial D-loop region in cytosolic fractions confirmed a robust increase in cytosolic mtDNA levels in HG-treated cells, which was markedly attenuated by BAI1 coadministration (Fig. 5B). Immunofluorescence costaining with MitoTracker Red and a mitochondria-targeted mtDNA probe further revealed that HG challenge triggered pronounced cytosolic mtDNA leakage alongside severe mitochondrial network fragmentation, while BAI1 treatment reduced mtDNA leakage and partially restored mitochondrial morphology (Fig. 5C). The cGAS–STING pathway acts as a pivotal cytosolic DNA sensor that initiates downstream NF-κB signaling upon detection of double-stranded DNA. Western blot analysis demonstrated that HG treatment upregulated the protein expression of cGAS and STING, and increased the phosphorylation of TBK1, NF-κB, and IRF3, while reducing IκB phosphorylation. To enhance the rigor and translational relevance of our findings, we extended our analyses to a human dermal microvascular endothelial cell (HMEDC) model of hyperglycemia, selected based on single-cell RNA-sequencing data from DW. This model represents the most abundant endothelial cell subset with the highest number of differentially expressed genes in DFU wounds, thereby maximizing the physiological relevance to hyperglycemic vascular injury. SIM imaging revealed that HG treatment increased BAX oligomerization and colocalization with TOM20 in HMEDCs, which was inhibited by BAI1 (Fig. S4A). qRT-PCR analysis confirmed elevated cytosolic mtDNA (D-loop) levels in HG-challenged HMEDCs, which were reduced by BAI1 intervention (Fig. S4B). Consistent with our observations in HUVEC, Western blot analysis showed that HG upregulated the expression of BAX 6A7, cGAS, STING, and the senescence marker p21 in HMEDCs, and BAI1 treatment mitigated these increases (Fig. S4C). qPCR analysis of SASP genes IL‐6,IL‐8,TGF‐β,MCP‐1 further demonstrated that HG-induced upregulation was suppressed by BAI1 (Fig. S4D). Additionally, senescence-associated β-galactosidase SA‐β‐gal staining confirmed that HG enhanced senescence in HMEDCs, and this effect was alleviated by BAI1 (Fig. S4E), mirroring our findings in HUVEC. Collectively, these results demonstrate that BAI1 inhibits BAX-mediated mtDNA leakage, suppresses cGAS–STING pathway activation, and consequently attenuates senescence and SASP generation in distinct VEC models, highlighting its therapeutic potential for hyperglycemia-associated vascular senescence (Fig. 5G).

Inhibition of STING alleviates VEC senescence

To directly validate the functional role of the cGAS–STING pathway in mediating hyperglycemia-induced endothelial senescence and SASP generation, we employed lentiviral-mediated shRNA knockdown of STING (shSTING) in high glucose-treated HUVECs. Western blot analysis revealed that HG exposure robustly upregulated the expression of cGAS and STING and increased the phosphorylation of TBK1, p65, and IRF3—key downstream effectors of the cGAS–STING pathway. STING knockdown (shSTING) significantly abrogated these HG-induced increases in the expression of cGAS–STING signaling components, confirming the efficient blockade of pathway activation (Fig. 6A). At the transcriptional level, qPCR analysis of SASP-related genes (including MCP-1, TGF-β, TNF-α, IL-8, IL-6, and IL-1α) demonstrated that HG markedly increased their expression, whereas knockdown of STING effectively reversed this proinflammatory transcriptional program (Fig. 6B). Discussion

By integrating single-cell transcriptomics, histopathological validation of clinical specimens, in vitro cellular models, and in vivo animal experiments, this study systematically elucidated and validated a novel mechanism underlying the impaired healing of DWs. We demonstrated that a hyperglycemic microenvironment induces mitochondrial dysfunction, activates BAX/BAK-mediated mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), and triggers mtDNA leakage into the cytosol. This cytosolic mtDNA subsequently activates the cGAS-STING innate immune pathway, ultimately driving VEC senescence, cellular dysfunction, and delayed wound healing. Furthermore, we elucidate the therapeutic potential of BAI1, a selective allosteric BAX inhibitor and mitochondrial stabilizer, in reversing VEC senescence and preserving VEC function by reducing mtDNA leakage and suppressing cGAS-STING activation. To overcome drug delivery challenges and facilitate the translation of this discovery, we successfully developed an intelligent drug delivery system—the BAI1-PSTB-Hydrogel—which significantly accelerated wound closure in a diabetic mouse model. Collectively, our findings provide a fresh perspective on the pathophysiology of DWs and identify a promising therapeutic target for the development of novel anti-senescence therapies.

Mounting evidence underscores the pivotal role of cellular senescence in the pathogenesis of chronic DWs [33]. Interventions aimed at delaying senescence, eliminating senescent cells, or inhibiting the SASP have shown promise in promoting healing. For instance, Wei Xuerong et al. reported that the use of galactose-modified PLGA nanoparticles encapsulating Fe3O4, designed to target and clear senescent cells, accelerated wound repair [34]. Similarly, Priyadarshani Nadeeshika Samarawickrama et al. demonstrated that the senolytic compound ABT263 significantly enhanced wound healing in diabetic db/db mice by clearing senescent cells [35]. Our study aligns with and extends these findings. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealed a significant enrichment of senescence-related pathways in DWs tissues. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that hyperglycemia accelerates VEC senescence and impedes wound healing, whereas interventions targeting VEC senescence effectively promote repair. These results support the inhibition of VEC senescence as a valid therapeutic target for DWs. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealed that, compared with normal wound tissue, venous and microvascular endothelial cells exhibited distinct differentiation trajectories during DWs progression. Among skin endothelial cell subsets, both the proportion of cells and the number of differentially expressed genes were most pronounced, leading us to select HUVEC as the primary in vitro model. Key findings were further validated using Human primary dermal microvascular endothelial cells (HDMECs), whose trends were consistent with those observed in venous endothelial cells.

Mitochondrial DNA, a circular double-stranded molecule, normally resides within the mitochondrial matrix and plays crucial cellular roles [36]. Its integrity is maintained by a network involving DNA polymerases, repair enzymes, and mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM), and dysregulation of this network compromises mtDNA stability, leading to its leakage into the cytosol [37]. Additionally, impaired mitophagy and aberrant mitochondrial dynamics (fission and fusion) are associated with mtDNA leakage [[38], [39], [40]]. Permeabilization events, particularly MOMP—mediated by mPTP pore opening, BAX/BAK oligomerization, and VDAC oligomeric pores—are critical for mtDNA release. Once leaked, mtDNA acts as a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP), activating cytosolic DNA pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) such as cGAS, initiating the STING-dependent innate immune response, and ultimately inducing cellular senescence [41,42]. In support of this axis, studies have shown that targeting mtDNA leakage inhibits senescence and improves cell function in various contexts [43]. For example, Meng Yang et al. reported that hyperoxia-induced VDAC1-mediated mtDNA release activated the cGAS-STING pathway, leading to lung epithelial cell senescence [44]. Wei Feilong et al. reported that the Mfn2-mtDNA-cGAS-STING axis is crucial in microglia following spinal cord injury [45]. Research by Zhao Lan et al. revealed that bioinspired nanovesicles from NP cell membranes can achieve homologous targeting and deliver BAI1 to mitochondria, restoring mitochondrial integrity, suppressing SASP factor release, and improving conditions [46]. Our study is the first to demonstrate in the context of DWs that BAI1, by inhibiting BAX activation, reduces mtDNA leakage, suppresses cGAS-STING signaling, attenuates VEC senescence, and consequently promotes wound healing. This finding highlights a novel mechanistic pathway and a promising therapeutic strategy for DWs.

Direct inhibition of the cGAS-STING pathway can suppress excessive SASP and delay cellular senescence. However, this approach significantly compromises immune surveillance against pathogens and tumors [26]. It may impair overall immune responses and increase infection risk [47]. Furthermore, this pathway is essential for antitumor immunity. It promotes dendritic cell maturation, T cell infiltration, and type I interferon production, thereby enhancing immunotherapy efficacy [48].In contrast, targeting the upstream release of mtDNA is a superior strategy. mtDNA is a major endogenous ligand that activates cGAS-STING. Protecting mitochondrial integrity and reducing mtDNA leakage limit aberrant immune activation at its source. This method avoids the broad immunosuppression caused by direct pathway inhibition. Consequently, it controls pathological inflammation while better preserving the host's fundamental capacity for anti-infection and anti-tumor immune surveillance.

The irregular morphology of DWs, coupled with excessive inflammation and exudate, presents a significant clinical challenge for effective topical drug delivery [49]. Hydrogels, three-dimensional hydrophilic polymer networks formed via chemical or physical crosslinking, are considered ideal wound dressings for DWs because of their ability to maintain a moist environment, provide a barrier, absorb exudate, and serve as drug delivery vehicles [50]. To this end, we developed a facile yet high-performance composite hydrogel. Based on a polyvinyl alcohol-borax system and modified with sodium alginate and tannic acid, this material exhibits significantly enhanced mechanical properties and robust tissue adhesion while maintaining excellent biocompatibility [51]. BAI1 is a hydrophobic small-molecule inhibitor with poor penetration into the deep dermis. To address the need for sustained local delivery, we loaded it into MSNs and subsequently encapsulated these within the composite hydrogel. As reported in a recent study, hydrogels enable sustained release of encapsulated nanoparticles through matrix erosion and network relaxation [52]. In our system, the BAI1-loaded mesoporous silica nanoparticles are released from the PSTB hydrogel in a similar manner, where the release kinetics are modulated by crosslinking density and medium exchange, ensuring prolonged local retention of the therapeutic agent. To our knowledge, this is the first study to employ a BAI1-loaded hydrogel for DWs treatment. Our experimental results demonstrate that the BAI1-PSTB-Hydrogel accelerates wound closure and improves healing quality, while cell senescence was significantly attenuated with BAI1, confirming its efficacy as an advanced drug delivery system. This strategy overcomes the limitations of BAI1's hydrophobicity and poor local absorption. The porous structures of both the MSNs and the hydrogel facilitate the sustained release of BAI1 at the wound site. The incorporation of PSTB hydrogel endows the BAI1-PSTB-Hydrogel system with anti-infective properties, rendering it an ideal dressing material for complex diabetic wounds. Given the intricate pathophysiological characteristics of diabetic wounds, we further assessed the clinical translational potential of the BAI1-PSTB-Hydrogel using a murine model of infected wounds. In this model, we compared the BAI1-PSTB-Hydrogel with a clinically available growth factor gel. Our findings demonstrate that the BAI1-PSTB-Hydrogel outperforms the growth factor gel in mitigating tissue damage, improving collagen deposition, alleviating inflammatory infiltration, and accelerating wound closure. Coupled with its straightforward preparation process, this system provides a robust theoretical and practical foundation for the clinical translation of BAI1.

Study limitations and future perspectives

Although this study integrates multidimensional experiments to systematically elucidate the mechanism by which BAI1 regulates the BAX/mtDNA/cGAS-STING axis in diabetic wounds and validates the therapeutic potential of the BAI1-PSTB hydrogel, several limitations remain that warrant further investigation. First, this study primarily employs the small-molecule inhibitor BAI1 for pharmacological intervention on BAX to verify its role in inhibiting mtDNA leakage and cGAS-STING pathway activation. Although the experimental results support the feasibility of this mechanism, direct causal evidence at the genetic level is still lacking. Future studies could utilize endothelial cell-specific Bax knockout mouse models to further clarify the necessity and specificity of this signaling axis in vascular endothelial cell senescence and wound repair. Second, this study achieves topical drug delivery to the wound site through the local application of the BAI1-PSTB hydrogel. Histological analysis suggests that BAI1 exerts more pronounced effects on vascular endothelial cells; however, it remains unclear whether this hydrogel system enables selective delivery to endothelial cells. Future efforts may focus on developing active targeting strategies based on endothelial cell-specific markers to enhance cell specificity and therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, the BAI1-PSTB hydrogel demonstrates favorable wound healing efficacy and biocompatibility in mouse models, yet its long-term degradation behavior, pharmacokinetic profile, and safety in large animal models still require systematic evaluation. Considering the complexity of clinical application, subsequent studies should assess its efficacy and toxicology in animal models that more closely recapitulate human pathophysiology, and explore its potential for combination with existing clinical treatments such as debridement, negative pressure wound therapy, and growth factors.

Conclusion

This study establishes that VEC injury and accelerated senescence are pivotal drivers of impaired healing in DWs, and we pioneer the use of mesoporous silica nanoparticles to encapsulate the hydrophobic BAX inhibitor BAI1, and subsequently incorporate this construct into a PSTB hydrogel for effective topical delivery. The resulting BAI1-PSTB-Hydrogel significantly accelerates wound closure and enhances healing quality in a diabetic mouse model. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that BAI1 mitigates high-glucose–induced mitochondrial damage, reduces the cytosolic release of mtDNA, and thereby suppresses the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway and its downstream effectors, ultimately alleviating cellular senescence and dysfunction in VEC. Collectively, our work provides the first evidence that BAI1 exerts a potent anti-senescence effect on VEC under hyperglycemic conditions and confirms the therapeutic efficacy of its hydrogel-based formulation in promoting diabetic wound repair. These findings offer a robust mechanistic and translational foundation for the clinical development of BAI1 as a novel therapy for DWs.

Materials & methods

Analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing data

Publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from DWs and healthy control skin tissues were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (accession number GSE165816). Data processing, normalization, dimensionality reduction, clustering, and differential gene expression analysis were performed using the online bioinformatics tools of the Majorbio Cloud Platform (www.majorbio.com).

Data quality control and genome alignment

In summary, reads with poor-quality barcodes and UMIs were excluded and subsequently aligned to the reference genome. Reads that were uniquely aligned to the transcriptome and overlapped an exon by at least 50% were taken into account for UMI counting. Prior to quantification, UMI sequences underwent error correction, and valid barcodes were identified using the EmptyDrops method. Cell by gene matrices were generated by counting UMIs and calling cell barcodes. Batch effects were corrected using the Harmony algorithm.

Cell clustering

Each sample's cell by gene matrix was separately imported into Seurat version for downstream analysis (Tables S2 and S3).

To analyze differentially expressed genes that are upregulated, the expression values of each gene within a specific cluster were compared to those in other cells using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Various criteria were used to identify significantly upregulated genes. Initially, genes had to exhibit a minimum 2-fold overexpression in the target cluster. Secondly, they needed to be expressed in more than 25% of the cells in the target cluster. Finally, the p-value should be less than 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) is a globally recognized system for classifying gene functions, providing an evolving controlled vocabulary and precise definitions to thoroughly describe the characteristics of genes and their products across all organisms.

To investigate the role of the senescence phenotype in vascular endothelial cells, we performed virtual knockout of the senescence-associated genes CDKN1A and FOXO3 using the scTenifoldKnk R package (v 1.0.1) applied to single-cell transcriptomic data of vascular endothelial cells. Subsequently, over-representation analysis (ORA) was conducted on the significantly perturbed genes (p < 0.05) following virtual knockout to identify enriched biological functions. This analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler R package (v 4.14.6) with the GO_BP gene set from MSigDB 2026.1. The names of significantly enriched GO_BP terms, the corresponding gene counts, and the adjusted p-values were recorded (Tables S4 and S5).

Patient samples and histopathology

Skin tissue specimens were obtained from DWs patients who underwent surgical debridement and from non-diabetic individuals who underwent elective plastic surgery at the First Affiliated Hospital of Fourth Military Medical University (n = 6 per group). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Hospital's Institutional Review Board. All participants provided written informed consent. Individuals with significant comorbidities known to interfere with wound healing were excluded. There were no statistically significant differences in age, sex, or baseline characteristics between the control group and the patients with diabetic foot ulcers; additionally, the control group had no history of other chronic diseases, wound infections, or use of special medications.

The collected tissues were fixed, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. Sections were stained with H&E and Masson trichrome for general histology. Immunofluorescence (IF) and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining were performed to assess the expression of TUNEL, Ki67, 8-OHdG, myeloperoxidase (MPO), cGAS, STING, BAX, and CD31, following standard protocols.

Encapsulation of the BAI1 in mesoporous silica nanoparticles

Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) (Ruixi, Xi'an, China) were dispersed in deionized water. BAI1 (MedChemExpress) was dissolved in an ethanol-methanol (1:1 v/v) solution. The two solutions were combined and stirred vigorously for 24 h at room temperature. The mixture was then centrifuged, and the pellet was washed repeatedly to remove the unloaded drug. A cryoprotectant was added to the final pellet, which was subsequently lyophilized to obtain BAI1-loaded MSN (BAI1@MSN) powder for further use.

Characterization of the BAI1@MSNs: Morphological analysis

The morphology of the nanoparticles was examined by TEM (TF20, FEI, USA) and SEM (Sigma 300, ZEISS, Germany). For TEM imaging, a processed sample droplet was applied onto an ultrathin copper grid, stained with 1% phosphotungstic acid for 1 min, and air-dried prior to observation.

Characterization of BAI1@MSNs: Hydrodynamic size and zeta potential

A 10 μL aliquot of the nanoparticle dispersion (10 mg/mL) was diluted in 2 mL of pure water and homogenized by gentle pipetting. The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential were subsequently measured using a dynamic light scattering instrument (NanoBrook 90Plus PALS, Brookhaven Instruments, USA).

Characterization of BAI1@MSNs: confirmation of the chemical structure

The chemical structure of the loaded BAI1 was verified using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (TU-1810; Beijing Purkinje General Instrument Co., Ltd.).

Determination of the drug loading capacity of BAI1@MSNs

Standard Curve Preparation: A standard stock solution of BAI1 (1 mg/mL) was prepared in a methanol/chloroform (1:1, v/v) solvent mixture. Serial dilutions were analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to establish a linear correlation between the peak area and the drug concentration.

Quantification of Drug Loading: Following the drug loading procedure, the supernatant collected after centrifugation of the BAI1@MSNs suspension was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. The filtrate was analyzed by HPLC to determine the peak area corresponding to unencapsulated BAI1. This value was interpolated into the standard curve to calculate the amount of nonloaded drug. The drug loading capacity was then determined from the difference between the initial amount of BAI1 used and the amount remaining in the supernatant.

Preparation of the BAI1-PSTB-Hydrogel

The PSTB composite hydrogel was synthesized as follows. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) was dissolved in deionized water at 90 °C under constant stirring to obtain a homogeneous solution. Sodium alginate (SA) was then added to the PVA solution and thoroughly mixed. TA was subsequently introduced, and the mixture was stirred until complete dissolution occurred. The pH of the solution was adjusted to 8.0 using sodium hydroxide. The pre-prepared BAI1@MSN lyophilized powder was added to this mixture and stirred continuously to ensure uniform dispersion. Finally, a borax solution was added under rapid stirring to initiate crosslinking, resulting in the formation of the BAI1-loaded PSTB hydrogel (BAI1-PSTB-Hydrogel).

Rheological measurements

Rheological testing was conducted using an Anton Paar rheometer. The hydrogel was secured onto the rheometer disk and subjected to testing using a stainless steel parallel plate rotor with an 8 mm diameter. Testing commenced with a slow descent of the plate until a normal force of approximately 20 mN was achieved. Frequency sweep tests were conducted at a strain of 0.5%, covering a frequency range from 0.1 to 10 rad/s.

Swelling properties test

The swelling behavior of the BAI1-PSTB-Hydrogel was characterized. Briefly, a preweighed hydrogel sample (initial weight, W0) was immersed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The sample was then removed, and surface-adhered water was gently removed with filter paper. The weight of the swollen hydrogel (W1) was immediately recorded. The swelling ratio (SR) was calculated using the following equation: SR (%) = [(W1 − W0)/W0] × 100%.

Cell culture and treatment

We purchased primary HUVEC from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cell identity was confirmed by immunofluorescence staining for CD31. For in vitro experiments, HUVEC were divided into three groups: normal control (NC, cultured in Endothelial Cell Medium (ScienCell, USA), high glucose (HG, treated with medium containing 30 mM D-glucose for 120 h before further treatment), and HG + BAI1 (treated with 30 mM glucose plus 1.5 μM BAI1). Cells were incubated under these conditions for 120 h before being harvested for subsequent analyses.

HDMECs were purchased from SUNNCELL (catalog No. SNP-H345). The cells were divided into three groups: normal control (NC), cultured in human dermal microvascular endothelial cell complete medium (SUNNCELL, SNPM-H345); high glucose (HG), treated with medium containing 30 mM glucose; and high glucose plus BAI1 (HG + BAI1), treated with 30 mM glucose plus 1.5 μM BAI1. Cells were incubated under the indicated conditions for 120 h and then harvested for subsequent experiments.

CCK-8

HUVEC in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3000 cells per well. After overnight attachment, cells were treated according to the experimental groups. Following 24 h of treatment, the culture medium in each well was replaced with 100 μL of fresh medium containing 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime, C0037). After a 4-h incubation at 37 °C, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Tube formation assay

Matrigel (Corning, USA) was thawed overnight at 4 °C. A total of 200 μL of Matrigel was added to each well of a 24-well plate and allowed to polymerize at 37 °C for 20 min. HUVEC from different treatment groups were trypsinized, counted, and seeded onto the polymerized Matrigel at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in 500 μL of the respective treatment medium. After 3 h of incubation at 37 °C, tube networks were imaged using an EVOS M7000 fluorescent microscope. Total tube length and the number of branch points were quantified using ImageJ software.

Scratch wound assay

HUVEC were seeded into 6-well plates and grown to confluence. A sterile 200 μL pipette tip was used to create a linear scratch across the cell monolayer. Detached cells were washed away with PBS, and fresh serum-free medium containing the respective treatments was added. Wound closure was monitored and imaged at 0, 12, 24, and 48 h using an EVOS M7000 microscope. The remaining scratch area at each time point was measured using ImageJ software to calculate the migration rate.

Transwell migration assay

A cell suspension containing 2 × 104 HUVEC in 200 μL of serum-free medium was added to the upper chamber of a Transwell insert (8.0 μm pore size, Corning). The lower chamber was filled with 600 μL of complete growth medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum as a chemoattractant. After 24 h of incubation, cells remaining on the upper surface of the membrane were removed with a cotton swab. Cells that had migrated to the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted under a microscope.

Measurement of intracellular ROS

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA (Beyotime, S0033S). After treatment, cells were incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA in serum-free medium at 37 °C for 20 min. After washing, fluorescence images were captured immediately using an EVOS M7000 fluorescent microscope. For quantitative analysis, cells were trypsinized, washed, and analyzed by flow cytometry (FACSCalibur, BD Biosciences).

Assessment of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm)

Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using the JC-1 assay kit (Beyotime, C2003S). After treatment, cells were incubated with JC-1 working solution at 37 °C for 20 min. After washing, cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope. Mitochondrial depolarization is indicated by a shift from red (JC-1 aggregates, high ΔΨm) to green fluorescence (JC-1 monomers, low ΔΨm). The red/green fluorescence intensity ratio was also quantified by flow cytometry.

TEM

HUVEC from different treatment groups were trypsinized, pelleted, and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) overnight at 4 °C. Samples were post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Mitochondrial ultrastructure was examined and imaged using a transmission electron microscope (EM420, Philips).

Western blot analysis

After treatment, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (New Cell & Molecular Biotech) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails on ice. Lysates were sonicated and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (AccuRef Scientific, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE on 4-20% gradient gels and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against: P16 (CST, 18769S), P21 (CST, 2947S), P53 (CST, 2524S), β-actin (Proteintech, 81115-1-RR), BAX 6A7 (Santa Cruz, sc-23959), cGAS (CST, 79978S), STING (CST, 13647S), p-TBK1 (CST, 5483S), TBK1 (CST, 38066S), p-IκB (CST, 2859S), IκB (CST, 4812S), p-NF-κB (CST, 3033S), NF-κB (Proteintech, 80979-1-RR), p-IRF-3 (CST, 2859S), IRF-3 (CST, 4812S). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (AccuRef Scientific, China) and imaged.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells grown on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope. The primary antibodies used were the same as listed for Western blotting.

SA-β-gal staining

Cellular senescence was detected using a SA-β-gal staining kit (Beyotime, C0602) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After treatment, cells were fixed and incubated with the SA-β-gal staining solution overnight at 37 °C (without CO2). Blue-stained senescent cells were visualized and counted under a bright-field microscope (EVOS M7000).

Generation of stable STING knockdown cell lines

Stable STING knockdown lines of HUVEC cells were generated via short hairpin RNA (shRNA) lentiviral vectors with shSTING RNA and shNC RNA. The primer sequences of shSTING and shNC were as follows: shSTING,

Forward: 5′- GATCCGACTCCTCATCAGTGGTATGGATCATTCAAGAGATGATC CATACCACTGATGAGGAGTCTTTTTTG -3′,

Reverse: 5′-AATTCAAAAAAGACTCCTCATCAGTGGTATGGATCATCTCTTGAATGATCCA TACCACTGATGAGGAGTCG -3′, shNC, Forward: 5’: GATCCGTTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGTAATTCAAGAGATT ACGTGACACGTTCGGAGAATTTTTTC -3′,

Reverse: 5′ ATTGAAAAAATTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGTAATCTCTTGAATTAC GTGACACGTTCGGAGAACG -3’.

HEK293T cells were transfected using pMD2.G and the sPAX2 packaging system to produce the lentivirus. At 48 and 72 h following transfection, the supernatant was collected and filtered through 0.45 μm a syringe filter (Millipore, No. SLHAR33SS). Then, the lentivirus containing shSTING RNA and shNC RNA was used to infect wild-type HUVEC and stable STING knockdown lines (HUVEC KD STING) were selected with puromycin at the concentration of 10 μg/mL.

Cytoplasmic DNA extraction

Cells cultured in T75 flasks at the logarithmic growth phase were trypsinized, and the resulting cell suspension was equally divided into two Eppendorf (EP) tubes, designated Tube 1 (for total DNA extraction) and Tube 2 (for cytoplasmic DNA extraction). Cells were pelleted by centrifugation. For Tube 1, the supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 220 μL of PBS, 10 μL of RNase Solution, and 20 μL of Proteinase K. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 15 min, followed by the addition of 250 μL of Buffer GB and thorough mixing by vortexing. The sample was then incubated in a 65 °C water bath for 30 min, after which DNA was purified using a spin column. For Tube 2, the supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 250 μL of Buffer A. Following a 10-min incubation at room temperature, the suspension was centrifuged to pellet the cells, and the supernatant was transferred to a new tube. A subsequent centrifugation step was performed to remove cellular debris and organelles, and the resulting supernatant was collected. To this supernatant, 20 μL of Proteinase K was added and mixed by vortexing, followed by the addition of 250 μL of Buffer GB, again with vortex mixing. The sample was incubated in a 65 °C water bath for 30 min, and DNA was purified using a spin column. For both tubes, the DNA purification procedure was as follows: 180 μL of anhydrous ethanol was added to the digested sample and vortexed for 20 s. The mixture was transferred to a gDNA spin column placed in a 2-mL collection tube. After discarding the flow-through, 650 μL of Washing Buffer A was added to the column, followed by centrifugation. The flow-through was discarded, and 650 μL of Washing Buffer B was added to the column, followed by another centrifugation step. After discarding the flow-through, the column was centrifuged again to remove residual buffer. The column was then transferred to a new collection tube, and 30–100 μL of Elution Buffer pre-heated to 70 °C was applied to the membrane. Following a 3-min incubation at room temperature, DNA was eluted by centrifugation. DNA extracted from Tube 1 was used as an internal control for the corresponding Tube 2 samples.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA from HUVEC and HDMECs was extracted using trizol reagent (Invitrogen) and then reverse-transcribed into cDNA by a PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Further quantitative PCR was performed using cDNA templates, primers, PCR Mix (#11141ES60, Yeasen), and SYBR Green Mix (#11201ES08, Yeasen). Specific primer pairs were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology (Beijing, China). The primer sequences are shown in Table S6. GAPDH and mtDNA (D-loop) were used as internal references for coding genes and mtDNA, respectively. Final relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2-△△CT method.

Establishment of the animal model and experimental treatment

Animals and Ethical Considerations: Male WT C57BL/6 mice weighing 20- 25g and aged 8-12 weeks were obtained from the Animal Center of the Fourth Military Medical University. Male db/dbmice aged 8 - 12 weeks were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). All mice were housed in a standard animal room under controlled environmental conditions. The indoor temperature was maintained at 20-25°C, the relative humidity was kept at 40-70%, and the light/dark cycle was 12 h/12 h. During the rearing process, mice had free access to food and water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee and Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Fourth Military Medical University. Animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

To establish a diabetic wound healing model, anesthesia was induced with 5% isoflurane gas (RWD Life Science Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) and maintained with 2.5% isoflurane gas delivered through a facemask. The skin on the back of mice was depilated under general anesthesia. The surgical sites were disinfected using iodine ethanol and then wiped with 70% ethanol. A wound in the dorsal skin was created using an 8 mm diameter circular biopsy punch, extending deep into the subcutaneous tissue. The experiment was divided into four groups (n = 6 per group): C57BL/6 mice were used as the Wild-Type control group, and db/db mice were randomly divided into three groups: Diabetic (db/db), Diabetic + PSTB-Hydrogel, and Diabetic + BAI1-PSTB-Hydrogel. The wounds of mice in the WT and Diabetic groups were immobilized using only silicone rings. Mice in the Diabetic + PSTB-Hydrogel group were treated with hydrogel-covered wounds, and mice in the Diabetic + BAI1-PSTB-Hydrogel group were treated with hydrogel-covered wounds loaded with BAI1. The wound healing process was observed and recorded using a macroscopic imager. The remaining area of the wound was measured using ImageJ.

To establish an infected wound model, anesthesia was induced with 5% isoflurane and maintained with 2.5% isoflurane delivered via a facemask. Under general anesthesia, the dorsal skin of the mice was depilated. The surgical site was disinfected with iodine ethanol and then wiped with 70% ethanol. A full-thickness excisional wound with an 8 mm diameter was created on the dorsal skin using a circular biopsy punch, extending deep into the subcutaneous tissue. C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6 per group): NC, infected wound, growth factor, and BAI1-PSTB-Hydrogel. In the NC group, wounds were immobilized using a silicone ring only. In the remaining three groups, 10 μL of Staphylococcus aureus (1 × 107 CFU/mL) was inoculated onto the wound for 2 days to establish a biofilm infection. The infected wound group was treated with normal saline, the growth factor group was treated with a growth factor-containing gel covering the wound, and the BAI1-PSTB-hydrogel group was treated with the BAI1-loaded hydrogel covering the wound. The wound healing process was observed and recorded using a macroscopic imager. The remaining wound area was measured using ImageJ software.

For the in vivo cellular uptake assay, mice were anesthetized, and the dorsal hair was removed. The skin on the back was disinfected by swabbing with a cotton ball soaked in iodophor. A full-thickness circular wound was created at the center of the dorsum using an 8-mm biopsy punch. FITC-labeled MSNs were incorporated into a FITC-PSTB-Hydrogel, which was then applied to the cutaneous wound. Twenty-four hours after application, skin tissues were harvested, embedded in OCT compound, and sectioned into 8 μm thick frozen sections for subsequent immunofluorescence staining.

Histological and immunofluorescence analysis of wound tissues

The skin tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned into 6 μm-thick slices. Sections were stained with H&E and Masson trichrome for histological analysis.

Immunofluorescent staining was applied to paraffin-embedded wound tissue sections following dewaxing, hydration, and antigen retrieval. Following overnight incubation at 4 °C in a moist environment, the sections were exposed to 5% bovine serum albumin and sealed for half an hour. After removing the excess primary antibody, we applied the diluted fluorescent secondary antibody to the wound slices. The slices were kept in the dark for an hour at room temperature. DAPI staining was performed for 5–10 min to label the nuclei. The slices were observed with a fluorescent microscope EVOS M7000. The following primary antibodies were used: Ki67, TUNEL, 8OHdG, cGAS, STING, BAX, and CD31.

Detection of wound ROS: The levels of ROS in wound tissues were assessed by staining frozen sections for ROS. The frozen sections were defrosted at room temperature while maintaining controlled moisture levels. The sections were incubated with ROS dye solution in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min. Next, the slides were subjected to three washes on a decolorizing table, each lasting 5 min. The samples were covered with anti-fluorescence quenching coverslips and subsequently examined under a fluorescence microscope.

Biocompatibility evaluation

In vitro: Hydrogel extracts were obtained by immersing sterile hydrogels in endothelial cell medium for 24 h at 37 °C, followed by filtration (0.22 μm). HUVEC were cultured in the hydrogel extract or standard medium for 24 h, and cell viability was assessed using a Calcein-AM/PI double-staining kit (Beyotime, C2015S).

In vivo: C57BL/6 mice were injected intraperitoneally with 200 μL of PBS or BAI1-PSTB-Hydrogel suspension. On days 7 and 14 post-injection, blood was collected for complete blood count and liver/kidney function tests. Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) and skin at the injection site were harvested for H&E staining to assess potential histopathological changes.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least three times. The results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software Inc.). Comparisons between two groups were made using an unpaired two-tailed t-test, and for multiple group comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett post hoc test was applied. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.
