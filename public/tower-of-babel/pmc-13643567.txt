The Unmet Need: When the Standard Armamentarium Falls Short

Tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is far from an innocent bystander in heart failure. Affecting an estimated 1.6 million patients in the United States alone, significant TR carries a substantial prognostic burden—mortality rates of 40% to 70% within 1 to 4 years of diagnosis—compounded by recurrent hospitalizations for right heart failure, renal insufficiency, and hepatic congestion. Over the past decade, the management of symptomatic high-risk patients has undergone a fundamental shift, moving decisively away from therapeutic nihilism toward an expanding transcatheter toolkit.1

While guideline-endorsed transcatheter repair and orthotopic replacement have substantially broadened the therapeutic options for high-risk patients,2, 3, 4, 5, 6 a discrete and clinically important subset remains without suitable options—patients with coaptation gaps too large for repair, annuli exceeding device sizing limits, or prior tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER) precluding reintervention. For this true “no-option” population, heterotopic caval valve implantation (CAVI) has emerged as a complementary strategy: Rather than addressing the native valve directly, it isolates the right atrium from systemic venous backflow by deploying bioprosthetic valves within the superior vena cava (SVC) and inferior vena cava (IVC).7,8

A Distinct Therapeutic Niche

The case reported by Henes et al9 in this issue of JACC: Case Reports merits attention not merely as a technical first-in-human milestone, but as an illustration of a conceptual distinction of growing clinical relevance: Heterotopic transcatheter tricuspid intervention is a therapy for right heart failure and systemic venous congestion, not for tricuspid valve anatomy per se.1,2 This distinction carries meaningful implications for both patient selection and outcome interpretation.

When a heterotopic device is deployed within the caval veins, the tricuspid valve regurgitation persists. What is altered is the hemodynamic consequence: Retrograde propagation of elevated right ventricular and right atrial pressures into the systemic venous bed is interrupted. Hepatic, renal, and splanchnic circulations are shielded from venous hypertension; diuretic responsiveness may be restored; and the viscous cycle of congestion-driven organ dysfunction may be attenuated.3,10 Within this physiological context, procedural efficacy is appropriately measured not by residual TR grade, but by the degree of decongestion, functional recovery, and end-organ protection achieved. This paradigm shift has direct implications for the design of follow-up protocols and endpoint selection in future heterotopic device registries.1

Bicaval Approaches

The TricValve (P+F Products & Features) deploys 2 independent self-expanding bioprosthetic valves in the SVC and IVC and represents the most extensively studied heterotopic platform to date. The TRICUS EURO study reported 94% procedural success, with 79.4% of patients achieving NYHA functional class I or II at 6 months, alongside significant improvements in quality of life.11 Real-world data from the TricBicaval registry corroborated these findings, reporting 96.1% intraprocedural success and sustained functional benefit at 1 year.12 The principal anatomical constraint of TricValve is the requirement for sufficient caval oversizing—a prerequisite that excludes patients with substantially enlarged IVC dimensions.9 The device is currently being evaluated in the ongoing TriCAV II trial, planned to enroll 500 patients in the United States (NCT06137807).

The TRICENTO G2 (MEDIRA GmbH) extends the bicaval principle by integrating the SVC and IVC components into a single continuous stent-graft frame. This uninterrupted architecture isolates the entire right atrium from regurgitant ventricular pressure—a distinction from TricValve's 2 independent valves—and may afford more complete protection of the systemic venous bed. The first-generation TRICENTO demonstrated feasibility and evidence of right ventricular reverse remodeling in a multicenter registry but was ultimately withdrawn following stent fractures attributable to elevated right atrial pressure.7 The G2 incorporates a reinforced frame engineered to withstand pressures up to 45 mm Hg, an enlarged lateral aperture, and transitions to an off-the-shelf design available in 2 standardized sizes.9 Importantly, the continuous anchoring zone accommodates patients otherwise excluded from TricValve by insufficient IVC oversizing—the precise anatomical scenario encountered in the present case.

Crosscaval Approaches

The Trillium (Innoventric) bridges the right atrium from the SVC to the IVC through a single fabric-covered stent incorporating 3 fenestrations at the tricuspid annular level to maintain right ventricular filling. In a multicenter feasibility study of 20 patients, the device achieved 100% procedural success, with significant reductions in central venous pressure and improvement in NYHA functional class at 30 days.13 Data from a formal pivotal study recently reported by Lurz et al1 provide the first structured evidence base for crosscaval heterotopic therapy.

The UNICA (Innoventric) employs a single bare-metal stent carrying 2 bioprosthetic valves—positioned at the IVC–RA and SVC–RA junctions, respectively—and a sealing skirt at the right atrial floor. The bare central stent body preserves transseptal access for future interventions if required. In the first-in-human application, peak central venous pressure fell immediately from 29 to 12 mm Hg, hepatic vein flow reversal resolved, and the patient achieved NYHA functional class II at 60-day follow-up, with a total device deployment time of 7 minutes.14

Where Does Heterotopic Intervention Fit?

The patient who stands to benefit most from a heterotopic approach is one in whom venous congestion and its sequelae—refractory edema, recurrent pleural effusion, diuretic resistance, early hepatic dysfunction—constitute the dominant burden of disease, rather than TR as a stand-alone anatomical problem. The case presented by Henes et al9 exemplifies this phenotype precisely: an 81-year-old man with recurrent cardiac decompensation, advanced right heart failure, and a history of surgical mitral repair and prior T-TEER, in whom every established therapeutic pathway had been exhausted. The prompt elimination of the v-wave on right heart catheterization, resolution of hepatic vein flow reversal, and recovery of TAPSE, right ventricular fractional area change, and right ventricular strain within 5 days are consistent with decongestion-mediated right ventricular unloading—the intended and physiologically anticipated heterotopic mechanism—rather than structural valvular correction.

A brief historical perspective is instructive. The earliest heterotopic experience employed off-label balloon-expandable SAPIEN valves deployed in the IVC alone. The randomized TRICAVAL trial established both the proof of concept and a critical limitation: Single IVC coverage provides only partial hemodynamic relief, as SVC backflow remains unaddressed.15,16 This finding catalyzed the development of dedicated bicaval and crosscaval systems providing simultaneous bilateral venous coverage. Whether decongestion achieved through heterotopic intervention translates into durable survival benefit remains an open and important question.

The evidence base for heterotopic CAVI is continuously growing. The randomized TRICAVAL trial established proof of concept while exposing the insufficiency of single-IVC coverage. Dedicated bicaval systems have since yielded more consistent results: The TRICUS EURO study reported 94% procedural success and 79.4% of patients in NYHA functional class I-II at 6 months, with real-world TricBicaval data confirming sustained benefit at 1 year.11,17 The TRICENTO multicenter registry demonstrated right ventricular reverse remodeling, while first-in-human data for the crosscaval Trillium and UNICA devices confirm hemodynamic feasibility across both architectural concepts.1,7,14 Across all platforms, the consistent finding is symptomatic and functional improvement; survival data from adequately powered trials remain absent.

Conduction Management in a Heterotopic Context

Among the procedural considerations highlighted by the Henes et al9 case is the management of periprocedural third-degree atrioventricular block and the decision to implant a leadless transcatheter pacemaker (Micra TPS, Medtronic). Conduction disturbances of this severity are not an anticipated complication of heterotopic intervention, in contrast to orthotopic TTVR, where mechanical proximity to the right bundle branch constitutes a recognized risk. The detection of intermittent atrioventricular block during preprocedural right heart catheterization in this patient supports a preexisting substrate rather than a device-attributable event.9 The implantation of a leadless pacing system—deployed through the stent graft into the right ventricle without traversing the tricuspid annulus—was both technically uncomplicated and strategically appropriate, circumventing the risk of chronic lead-prosthesis interaction. This experience adds to an emerging body of evidence favoring leadless pacing as the preferred strategy when permanent pacing is required in the context of transcatheter tricuspid intervention.18,19

Conclusions

The first-in-human implantation of the TRICENTO G2 reported by Henes et al9 extends the reach of heterotopic intervention to patients excluded from both conventional transcatheter repair and orthotopic replacement. More broadly, the case reinforces a principle central to heterotopic therapy: The therapeutic target is the failing right heart and the congested systemic venous circuit, not the valve leaflets themselves. As heterotopic CAVI transitions from compassionate use to randomized validation—and from European feasibility to further global scalability—the field now requires prospective registries and pivotal trials to define which patients derive the most meaningful and durable benefit, establish hard clinical endpoints beyond functional class, and position CAVI as a deliberate, protocol-driven strategy for the patient with medically refractory venous congestion rather than an intervention of last resort.
