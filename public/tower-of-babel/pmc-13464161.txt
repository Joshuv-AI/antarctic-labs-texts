JDS Communications special issue: Pasture-based dairy systems—Definition, diversity, and future direction
Authors: JR Roche
Published: 2026

Interest in pasture-based dairy systems in the Journal of Dairy Science has noticeably increased since the mid-1990s (Figure 1), reaching >30 manuscripts per year for much of the past decade. The expansion in publications likely reflects the convergence of volatile milk prices and input costs, consumer interest in pasture-derived dairy products, increased scrutiny of farmed-animal welfare, the pressure to quantify nutrient losses and GHG emissions in all systems, and the need to identify cows, forages, and technologies suited to grazing systems. The subject distribution in Figure 2 confirms that nutrition and production remain the dominant themes, with 308 documents of the 825 (37%), though published subjects have broadened into milk quality and processing, genetics and genomics, health and welfare, reproduction, and farm systems and economics—themes that form the basis of the invited reviews in this special issue.

The author distribution (Figure 2) indicates that this growth has been driven primarily by research groups in countries in which grazing has remained biologically and economically important to dairy production. Analysis of first- and last-authorship positions across the 825 articles identified in the Scopus query reveals a relatively concentrated leadership structure: the 20 authors with the greatest combined first- and last-author contribution collectively led 254 articles in these positions (31%). Researchers based in New Zealand and Ireland dominated both the first- and senior-authorship rankings, together accounting for the majority of leadership contributions among the top 20, reflecting the central role of these 2 countries in pasture-based dairy research published in this journal, especially in recent decades. The invited authors for this special issue reflect this publication leadership.

For the purpose of this special issue, a pasture-based system is defined by the primacy of grazed pasture in the cow's diet—a system in which pasture is the dominant feed resource for the lactating cow, as well as her primary environment. This special issue was developed to define pasture-based dairy systems in temperate regions more clearly and to strengthen the scientific basis for their study, comparison, and improvement. It distinguishes systems built around intensively managed grazed pasture from extensive grazing; niche, low-input models; and mixed or housed systems in which grazing is present but not nutritionally or operationally central. Its premise is not that pasture is a universal solution, but that pasture-based dairying is a definable, measurable, and improvable system in which progress depends on better cows, better forages, better measurement, stronger environmental accounting, and management systems that retain grazed pasture as the central dietary and behavioral feature of the lactating cow.

For me, part of the motivation for this special issue was personal. As an editor, reader, and conference attendee, I became frustrated by submissions and presentations discussing challenges and opportunities for “pasture-based” systems, when, in practice, the submitters' and presenters' foci were on extensive grazing systems, with little active pasture management; niche, low-input systems proposed as alternatives to “intensive dairying,” or housed and mixed systems in which grazing was present but not central to the lactating cow's diet or daily environment.

I am not disputing the value of this research nor the importance of these farming systems, but they should not be conflated with pasture-based dairying as practiced in temperate environments and where intensively managed grazed pasture supplies most of the diet of the lactating cow and determines the rhythm of herd management. This distinction matters because the inputs, constraints, and outcomes of intensively managed pasture-based systems differ fundamentally from those of systems that merely include grazing or are idealistically driven toward extensive features. Forages are selected and managed differently, cows require different genetic attributes, nutrient cycling occurs across a grazed landscape, and farm decisions are organized around pasture supply, animal behavior, product identity, and environmental risk. Without this distinction, conclusions about nutrition, genetics, welfare, milk composition, environmental impact, and farm-system economics for pasture-based systems will be weakened.

The papers in this special issue reveal that pasture-based dairy systems are neither simple nor static. They are dynamic biological systems in which the cow, the sward, the soil, the climate, the milk producer, and the consumer interact. The contributing authors examine pasture-based dairying as an integrated biological, economic, environmental, and social system. Horan et al. (2026) provide a conceptual basis for defining pasture-based systems in temperate regions, whereas other contributions address forage selection (Donaghy et al., 2026), pasture allowance, supplementation (Roche et al., 2026), transition-cow nutrition (Crookenden and Heiser, 2026), cow genetics (Berry, 2026), fertility (Butler et al., 2026), health, welfare (Fisher et al., 2026), cow-calf contact, virtual fencing, precision technologies, artificial intelligence (AI; Eastwood et al., 2026), milk composition, product authentication (Magan et al., 2026), nutrient loss (McDowell et al., 2026), methane mitigation (Kay et al., 2026), agrivoltaics, and farm economics.

Collectively, these papers demonstrate that pasture-based systems are not low-management or low-technology systems. They are dynamic systems in which pasture supply and nutritive value vary with season, climate, species composition, soil fertility, and grazing management, and in which cows must harvest feed, maintain health and reproductive performance, express normal behavior, and produce milk in an outdoor environment prone to change.

The future will depend on better forage and animal genetics suited for the system, better transition and reproductive management, robust welfare assessment, technologies suited to outdoor environments, defensible product-authentication methods, and environmental accounting that reflects whole-system performance. The purpose of this special issue is, therefore, not to defend pasture-based dairying as a single model, but to define it more clearly and identify the science needed to improve it.
