Exploring the association between medication, patient-specific factors and delirium in hospitalised patients: a 10-year retrospective, population-based cohort study in Iceland
Authors: Anita E Weidmann, Julia A Schintler, Reto Stauffer, Martin I Sigurðsson, Freyja Jónsdóttir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to identify potential significant statistical associations between the development of delirium, medication and several other patient-specific factors across a 10-year retrospective longitudinal real-world dataset.DesignA retrospective, population-based cohort study.SettingThe National University Hospital of Iceland.ParticipantsAll patients≥18 years hospitalised between 2010 and 2020.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was a diagnosis of delirium within 2 years of a patient filling a prescription for a specific medication. The exposure was pre-admission medication use or a pre-existing condition. Secondary outcomes included mortality, length of hospital stay, readmission rate and frequency of additional delirium diagnosis.ResultsA total of 85 942 admissions and readmissions were included in the dataset, which comprised 1066 variables. The cohort comprised 55 495 patients (51.5% male) with a median [IQR] age of 71 years [58, 82]. Throughout the study, 3533 patients were diagnosed with delirium at least once over the 10-year study period. A statistically significant association (χ2 test, p<0.001) between the delirium (n=3533) and the non-delirium group (n=51 926) was identified for hyperpolypharmacy (≥10 medications), prescribed proton pump inhibitors, opioids, beta-lactam antibiotics, anticoagulants, statins and selective beta-blockers. Delirium was associated with an extended hospital stay, an increased readmission rate and increased mortality. No significant statistical association was found between delirium and the use of tricyclic antidepressants (p=0.121), nor with the presence of type I diabetes (p=0.216). On an annual average, the prevalence of patients who had obtained a delirium diagnosis within the previous year and were subsequently diagnosed again with delirium during their admission was 25.7%.ConclusionNumerous medications and other patient-specific risk factors have been identified, which underlines the complexity of delirium development. To strengthen patient safety and hinder the development of delirium, an increased emphasis is needed to review medication use and improve our understanding of medication risk associated with delirium cause, treatment and prevention. Specific prescribing/deprescribing advice should be developed to inform prescribing practice and patient safety.Keywords: Delirium, Medication Review, Risk Factors

Introduction

Delirium is defined as a condition not wholly attributable to a current neurocognitive disorder. It must be associated with additional cognitive impairment, including deficits in memory, orientation, language or visual-spatial abilities, according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition.1 It is characterised by an abrupt onset of disorientation and has been independently associated with a twofold increase in risk of death, a 2.4-fold increase in risk of institutionalisation and a 12.5-fold increase in risk of dementia.2 3 Even though delirium affects one-third of patients over the age of 70 in general medical settings, it is frequently overlooked, resulting in an underdiagnosed condition.4 5 Direct 1-year healthcare costs are estimated to be between $143 and $152 billion, assuming that 20% of older hospitalised patients develop delirium.5 The precise mechanism by which delirium develops remains unclear. It is thought to arise as an interplay between various predisposing and precipitating factors, which span a complex range of social, environmental, non-pharmacological and pharmacological factors.16,9 These include but are not limited to older age, dementia, a high burden of comorbidities, surgery, acute illness, infections, medication, intoxication, inflammation or sleep dysregulation.4 9 Medications are recognised risk factors for delirium and are considered to be the causative factor in up to 39% of delirium cases.10 11 A summary of reported mechanisms through which medication contributes to the development of delirium in adults published as part of a systematic review of guidelines across 114 Neurological and Medical Societies stated drug interactions, drug withdrawal, adverse effects, polypharmacy, renal impairment, pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic changes as some of the contributing mechanisms.12 The review points out that medication is inconsistently presented as a predisposing or precipitating factor with no clear categorisation given. Medications that cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB), increase BBB permeability (eg, in renal failure) or have anticholinergic or dopaminergic activity are most likely to cause delirium.13,15 Sedatives (n=24/80%), opioids (n=22/73.3%), psychoactive drugs (n=21/70%)+anticonvulsants (n=14/46.7%), anticholinergic agents (n=20/66.7%), antihistamines (n=18/60%) and steroids (n=16/53.3%) were among the most frequently cited drug classes mentioned across the guidelines.12

While antipsychotic use is by far the most frequently used approach to pharmacological treatment of delirium, their use is controversial.16 Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have failed to show an association between antipsychotic use and the duration of severity of delirium.17,19 Both the Beers Criteria and the Screening Tool of Older Persons Prescriptions in older adults with high fall risk (STOPPFall) risk tool recommend the withdrawal of antipsychotics in older adults unless they are at risk of significant harm to self and others.19 Additionally, there is a distinct paucity of specific information about therapeutic alternatives for medication causing delirium. Strategies centre mainly on discontinuation of medication, being mindful of the anticholinergic potential of individual medication and choosing a newer generation of medication within a medication class,1019,22 while many studies have been conducted on medication use and delirium, with differing levels of quality.15 A recent yet systematic review carried out by the same research team that conducted the present study summarised 106 out of 3867 available original studies on medication-induced delirium and highlighted the need for large epidemiological studies investigating the associations between medication, delirium and other confounding factors.23 The present study aimed to identify potential significant statistical associations between the development of delirium, medication and several other patient-specific factors across a 10-year retrospective internal medicine longitudinal real-world dataset. The study set out to prove the hypothesis that there is a positive correlation between a spectrum of drug classes previously unreported in the literature and a diagnosis of delirium. In addition, we hypothesise that there is a positive correlation between a diagnosis of delirium and length of stay (LOS), readmission rate, co-morbidity index, frailty and mortality over time.

Method

Study design

A retrospective population-based cohort study design.

Study setting

Landspítali University Hospital, Reykjavik, Iceland, serves as the secondary care for around 75% of Iceland, and the tertiary hospital for the whole of the Icelandic population (total population (2020): 289 99024 and it has 700 beds with approx. 20 000 patients each year.

Study population

Admissions and readmissions of adult patients≥18 years to the internal medicine specialty wards at the Landspítali University Hospital in Iceland between 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2020, with a follow-up of clinical outcomes which was carried out until 17 March 2022.

Patient database

Data on a total of 1066 variables were included across the entire Icelandic internal medicine database. The creation of the database (figure 1) has been previously outlined by Jonsdottir et al.25 The approval for the establishment of the database was granted on 21 September 2021 by the National Bioethics Committee of Iceland (VSN-21-179).

Graphical presentation of all information sources used in the development of the aggregated Icelandic internal medicine database 2010–2020. ATC, Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical; ICD 9/10, International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems – 9th/10th revision

Medication data

Medication use was defined as any medication filled in the year prior to the patient’s admission and the year post-discharge. The Icelandic Prescription Registry of the Directorate of Health provides real-time information on all prescription medication usage, excluding over-the-counter medications. The accuracy of the prescription database is assessed regularly and is estimated to be 95%. Information on the medications was coded with the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification.26

Clinical data

The internal medicine database includes clinical data on admitted patients by internal medicine specialties, including information on a patient’s characteristics such as age, gender and internal medicine specialty responsible for the admission. Even though the new International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems – 11th revision came into effect in January 2022, the comorbidities of patients in the database were retrieved from hospital and primary care records using International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems – 10th revision (ICD-10) to ensure coding equivalence over the database period 2010–2020.27 28 A comorbidity was considered as present for a patient if a clinical diagnosis was made prior to admission. The comorbidity and frailty burden was assessed by calculating the Elixhauser comorbidity index (EI) and the Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS).29 30 The medication use categories used in this study were non-polypharmacy (≤5), polypharmacy (5–9) and hyper-polypharmacy (≥10).31 Mortality was gathered from the Icelandic Cause of Death Register. A diagnosis of delirium was documented via the ICD codes R41 (R41.0, R41.1, R41.2, R41.3, R41.8) and F05 (F05.0, F05.1, F05.8, F05.9) (Box 1). A diagnosis of delirium (R41.* or F05.*) within the year (365 days) prior to admission was defined as a prior diagnosis of delirium.

Outcome

The primary outcome was a hospital coding for delirium within 2 years of a patient being exposed to a specific medication. Secondary outcomes included mortality (group comparison of patients who had received a delirium diagnosis during the period in which they died and the other group who did not receive a diagnosis of delirium in the period of their demise), LOS (sum of days in hospital over a 2 year period), readmission rate (readmission within 30 days yes/no) and frequency of delirium coding.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was undertaken from June 2023 to April 2024. All patient data used for this analysis were de-identified prior to statistical analysis. All statistical analyses and data processing were conducted using the statistical programming language R (V.4.5.2), using dplyr (V.1.2.0) and tidyr (V.1.3.2) for data aggregation, tableone (V.0.13.2)32 33 for univariate analysis and lmtest (V.0.9.40) with sandwich (V.3.1.1) clustered covariance estimates. Descriptive statistics were used to report the prevalence of specific medication use, pre-existent conditions and the incidence of at least one delirium diagnosis. Study participants were divided into two groups: patients who obtained a diagnosis of delirium within a 2-year period and patients who did not. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to estimate the odds of receiving a delirium diagnosis, adjusting for age, sex, temporal changes (including offsets for 2-year periods) and maximum frailty score. This grouping facilitated the assessment of whether certain illnesses or medications were associated with higher rates of delirium diagnosis. Covariates were selected based on clinical judgement, existing literature and prior peer-review feedback. Only variables considered baseline characteristics and potential confounders were included. Variables representing post-exposure events or defined as secondary outcomes were excluded from the final multivariable models to avoid overadjustment. Mostly all covariates are binary, except the following: number of medication (counts), LOS (continuous; hours; days), days till next admission (counts), polypharmacy category (categorical with three levels; nominal), HFRS class (categorical with three levels; nominal) and Charlson comorbidity index (counts). Multicollinearity among predictors was assessed using generalised variance inflation factors (GVIFs). Adjusted GVIF values were all close to 1 and below conventional thresholds for concern, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity among included covariates. Two medication variables (ticlopidine and prostaglandins) were excluded from the final models due to quasi-complete separation (no delirium events among exposed patients) and very small exposure counts, which resulted in unstable and non-informative estimates. χ2 tests were used to identify possible dependencies between delirium (yes/no) and categorical variables, while two-sample t-tests were used for continuous variables with normal distributions to test for the mean difference between both groups. For variables presented with median and IQR, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied. A significance level of α=0.05 was used for all statistical tests.

To evaluate whether mortality among patients diagnosed with delirium during a 2-year period was higher than in those without such a diagnosis, a series of one-sided binomial tests was performed, with Bonferroni correction applied to adjust for multiple hypothesis testing.34 The same procedure was used to assess whether a diagnosis of delirium (ICD codes F05* or R41*) within 365 days prior to admission increased the likelihood of receiving a new F05* diagnosis (figure 2). This was calculated on an annual interval. Patient-level data were aggregated over 2 years to minimise missingness; remaining variables with missing values were excluded, and analyses used complete-case data. No variables with missing data were included in the analysis.

Study reporting

Study reporting adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline reporting of observational studies in epidemiology.35

Ethics approval

Several ethical authorisations were obtained for the establishment of the Internal medicine database and the ICD-10-diagnoses dataset. The ethical approval was granted by the Icelandic National Bioethics Committee (VSN-21-179), the Data Protection Authority of Iceland, that waived individual consent.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the patient cohort

The study cohort included 55 459 2-year aggregated admissions. Of the cohort, 28 559 were male (51.50%) with a median age at admission [IQR] of 71 years [58, 82] and a median LOS in hospital [IQR] of 168.47 hours (7 days) [3, 19]. (table 1). For the entire cohort, the most prevalent comorbidity was hypertension (52.67%, n=29 208), followed by a benign cancer diagnosis (36.1%, n=19 994) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (29.56%, n=16 393). The most frequently filled medications in the year prior to admission were medications for the central nervous system (84.8%, n=47 043), cardiovascular system (79.35%, n=20 417) and anti-infective medications (67.9%, n=37 639).

Clinical characteristics and clinical outcomes of the patient cohort with delirium

Table 2 compares the patient characteristics, comorbidities and clinical outcomes for the entire cohort, stratified by whether patients were diagnosed with delirium (n=3533) or not (n=51926). Patients with delirium were older with a median age [IQR] of 82 [75-87] when compared to patients without delirium, 70 [57 - 81] (Kruskal-Wallis test, p<0.001). On average, the maximum LOS in hospital was found to be significantly longer for patients with a delirium diagnosis. The mean sum of LOS in hospital was 407.72 hours for delirium patients compared to 163.82 hours for non-delirium patients (two-sample t-test, p<0.001). The number of days until the next admission [IQR] was 72 [14, 316] days for patients with delirium and 144 [19, 672] days for those without. An analysis excluding delirium diagnoses based solely on F05 (excluding R41) yielded comparable results.

Comorbidities

The median EI (IQR) for patients with delirium was 8 [3, 14] compared with a median of 5 [0,11] for patients without a delirium diagnosis [Kruskal-Wallis test, p<0.001]. The mean Elixhauser score for delirium patients was 9.02 compared with a mean score of 6.54 for patients without delirium diagnosis. The same applies to the Charlson comorbidity index, where delirium patients on average scored 3.14 compared with non-delirium patients scoring 2.62 (two-sample t-test, p<0.001). The median values of the maximum Charlson comorbidity indexes for both delirium and non-delirium patients were 2, but with a difference in the IQR with [1, 5] and [0, 5], respectively (Kruskal-Wallis test, p<0.001).

The most common comorbidities among patients with delirium versus patients without delirium were hypertension (68.3% (n=2414) vs 51.6% (n=26 794)), followed by a benign cancer diagnosis (44.5% (n=1571) vs 35.5% (n=18 423)), COPD (32.4% (n=1146) vs 29.4% (n=15 247)), ischaemic heart disease (37.6% (n=1327) vs 27.8% (n=14 458)), malignant cancer diagnosis (25.0% (n=885) vs 20.8% (n=10 824)), cerebrovascular accident (12.9% (n=457) vs 8.2% (n=4276)), kidney disease (12.6% (n=446) vs 8% (n=4154)), organic psychiatric disease (27.5% (n=973) vs 3.9% (n=2040)), anxiety disorder (6.6 .% (n=233) vs 4.5% (n=2335)), mood disorder (6.8% (n=241) vs 4.1% (n=2106)) and dementia (6.9% (n=243) vs 1.8% (n=953)). Further comorbidities are shown in table 2.

Frailty

Patients who received a delirium diagnosis had a statistically significant higher HFRS. The HFRS in the study cohort was classified as low (≤5) in 42.0% (n=23 297) of cases, medium (6-15) in 43.2% (n=23 960) and high (>15) in 14.8% (n=8202) (table 2).

Mortality

On average, mortality was 8.43% (n=137 per 2 years) in the delirium group, compared with 6.17% (n=452 per 2 years) in the non-delirium group. Mortality among patients with delirium was significantly higher in each period (binomial tests with Bonferroni correction, p<0.001), except for the 2018–2019 period (p=1.000) and 2020 (p=0.0879), which only depicts data from 1 year (figure 2).

Medication use

Patients with delirium were more likely to have hyper-polypharmacy (≥10), 73.3% (n=2588) versus 68.4% (n=35 532) of those without delirium. However, there was no statistical significance for an association of polypharmacy (5–9 medications) or patients taking five medication or less with delirium (χ2 test, p<0.001).

A number of medications exhibit a significant association with delirium (χ2 test, p<0.001) including medication for the central nervous system (87.1% (n=3078) vs 84.7% (n=43 965)), cardiac medication (82.1% (n=2900) vs 79.1% (n41096)), anti-infective medication (71.0% (n=2510) vs 67.7% (n=35 129)), opioids (61.5% (n=2174) vs 58.1% (n=30 168)), gastric medication (58.4% (n=2064) vs 53.3% (n=27 674)), respiratory medication (50.6% (n=1787) vs 46.3% (n=24 042)), anticoagulants (50.0% (n=1765) vs 45.2% (n=23 461)), hormones (52.7% (n=1861) vs 47.1 (n=24 460)), antiplatelet medication (31.6% (n=1118) vs 27.9% (n=14 486)), antiviral medication (8.6% (n=303) vs 7.0% (n=3609)), benzodiazepines (38.9% (n=1374) vs 34.6% (n=17 989)), medication for the musculoskeletal (34.7% (n=1226) vs 32.1. Within each drug class significantly associated with delirium (online supplemental table 1), the majority of cardiac medication (C01) was significantly associated with delirium (1b). Within the other drug classes selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (N06AB) (1a), beta-lactam antibiotics and quinolones (J01C) (1c), proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) (A02BC) (1d), antihistamines (R06) (1e), heparin (B01AB01), enoxaparin (B01AB05), factor Xa – inhibitor (B01AF), apixaban (B01AF02) (1f), cortico- and glucocorticoids (H02) (1g) as well as clopidogrel (B01AC04) and acetylsalicylic acid (B01AC06) (1h), insulin (A10A) (5i), nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (M01A), paracetamol (N02BE01), allopurinol (M04AA01) and any parkinsonian medication (N04) (1k) were all significant. In contrast, tricylic antidepressants (N06AA) (1a) and all antidiabetic medication other than insulin showed no significant difference between the delirium group and those without (1i).

Multivariable analysis of factors associated with delirium

After adjustment for age, sex and maximum frailty score with a multivariable logistic regression analysis, the patient had increased odds of receiving a delirium diagnosis for the following medications: hormones, glucocorticoids and PPIs, and the following comorbidities: alcohol abuse, organic psychiatric disease and substance misuse (table 3 and online supplemental table 1).

Association between pre-diagnosed delirium and future consecutive delirium

Patients diagnosed with delirium (F05*) during their admission showed a significantly higher prevalence of having received a delirium diagnosis (R41*/F05*) in the 365 days prior to admission (binomial tests with Bonferroni correction, p<0.001). The average prevalence for the group of patients who had already obtained a delirium diagnosis 365 days prior to admission is much higher at 25.7% (2010–2020), while the patient group who did not obtain a diagnosis 365 days prior to admission amounted to an average of 3.5% (2010–2020; figure 3).

Discussion

The study identified numerous significant associations between the occurrence of delirium and the use of medications such as opioids, beta-lactfiguream antibiotics, anticoagulants, statins and selective beta-blockers; a diverse range of comorbidities with hypertension, COPD, cancer, dementia and kidney as well as ischaemic heart disease being predominantly significant as well as anxiety and mood disorders. Alongside the findings that a diagnosis of delirium is associated with an extended hospital stay, an increased re-admission rate, increased mortality, hyper-polypharmacy (≥10 medication) and an increased risk of future delirium events following the primary occurrence, the findings support previous literature reports. Less well reported is the significant association with PPIs and delirium. Contrary to published literature, no statistical association was found between delirium and the use of tricyclic antidepressants or type I diabetes.

The statistically significant association between the intake of PPIs and delirium appears to contradict published literature, which mainly reports a decreased incidence of delirium, when compared with histamine h2-receptor antagonists (H2-blockers).36,39 The observed association between PPI use and delirium in our univariate analysis should be interpreted with caution, given that these results may be subject to confounding by underlying clinical frailty, comorbidities or higher polypharmacy, as with all univariate analyses. A potential mechanism for PPI-induced delirium may be linked to alterations in gut microbiota composition and physiological function resulting from PPI use.40 These alterations can lead to overgrowth of opportunistic pathogens like Enterobacteriaceae, Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Klebsiella.41 An increased abundance of both Klebsiella and Pseudomonas in the gut have been linked to delirium in postoperative patients.41 42 In addition an elevated gut mucosa, permeability may precipitate an augmented risk of enteric sepsis, which may, in turn, give rise to clinical complications.40 While an intriguing observation, further research is required to substantiate these findings. Literature also hypothesises that statins have a protective effect against delirium due to their pleiotropic properties.43 One potential mechanism for the significant delirogenic effects of statins reported by our findings is its modulation of nitric oxide production in an inflammatory state, which may contribute to the development of cerebral ischaemia by altering cerebral autoregulation.43 This results in an altered blood flow, which may lead to hypoperfusion and inadequate oxygenation thereby increasing the risk for delirium.43 These observed associations between statin use and delirium in our univariate analysis should also be interpreted with caution, like the PPIs, given that these results may be subject to confounding by underlying clinical frailty, comorbidities or higher polypharmacy, as with all univariate analyses.

In general, these discrepancies may be attributed to the fact that these medications are employed in standard treatment regimens and are administered on a regular basis, and therefore, these findings may also be influenced by underlying conditions, fraility or the prescribing patterns in Iceland. To evaluate whether the findings were influenced by key factors such as age, gender and underlying frailty, a multivariable analysis was conducted. Although we adjusted for these factors known to be strongly associated with delirium, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. Multivariable analyses adjusting for polypharmacy are shown in online supplemental S2. Because polypharmacy is a downstream intermediate in the causal pathway from medication use to delirium, adjusting for it can lead to overadjustment or collider bias; thus, directed acyclic graphs guided confounder selection in epidemiology emphasises upstream factors, such as age, sex, frailty and cognition, over intermediates, such as polypharmacy.44

The multivariable analysis revealed increased odds of delirium associated with the use of medications like hormones, glucocorticoids and PPIs. An association was also found for patients with the following comorbidities: alcohol abuse, organic psychiatric disorders and substance misuse. The reasons may be that alcohol and substance misuse is less associated with increased age. However, it is also conceivable that these drugs exert an effect that has not yet been identified. Further difference to existing literature reports is the lack of statistical association between delirium and the use of tricyclic antidepressants in the patient cohort analysed in this study. Tricyclic antidepressants have a high anticholinergic burden, and it is widely acknowledged that tricyclic antidepressants, such as clomipramine and amitriptyline, exhibit a high affinity for muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, which consequently elevates the risk of medication-induced delirium.45 46 The results of this study highlight the importance of medication review for high-risk patients, such as those at risk of developing delirium or recently hospitalised. When conducting medication review, clinicians should reassess the use of potentially deliriogenic drugs, especially for vulnerable patients like those of advanced age or on polypharmacy. Incorporating this into medication review could help improve medication safety by enabling early detection of delirium risk.

The large number of significant findings in this analysis confirms the complex and multifaceted aetiology of delirium. This prompts the question of how much is actually understood about the factors that precipitate delirium and whether its consequences are given due consideration. This deficit in knowledge compels researchers to identify and implement novel machine learning prediction tools and innovative validation tools for delirium, thereby ensuring the best patient outcomes in a cost-effective manner.47,49 The findings of this study are in line with the hypothesis that a history of delirium may be a risk factor for a subsequent delirium diagnosis, despite the potential for diagnosis misclassification from a lacking incentive to use delirium codes in clinical practice. The prevalence of delirium differed significantly between patients who had already been diagnosed with delirium and those who had not yet received a diagnosis, across all age groups. A retrospective study of 8591 intensive care unit (ICU) admissions to the Vanderbilt University Medical Centre between 1 January 2014 and 31 December 2016, with existing delirium on admission, reported the incidence and associated factors of a recurrence of delirium.50 The findings indicate that 12.4% (n=1067) of patients experienced a recurrent episode of delirium in the ICU within a median time frame of 37 [12–96] hours after the initial episode concluded (negative Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU>48).50 In addition, the risk of dementia is increased by 20% with each additional episode of delirium among patients who have already experienced one episode of delirium, and mortality is increased by 39% in older hospitalised patients according to a retrospective cohort study of a longitudinal dataset including 650 590 patients aged ≥65 years51,54. Consequently, the need for effective treatment and prevention strategies is important for clinical practice as cognitive damage resulting from delirium may be severe and longer lasting. The findings of this research also indicated a notable increase in mortality rates among patients who received a delirium diagnosis when compared with those who did not.

Strengths and limitations

The large-scale real-world database analysis, including 55 459 2-year aggregated admissions, is one of the key strengths of this study and the ability to link a nationwide prescriptions database with clinical data from both hospital and primary care. Allowing for comprehensive examination of the entire tertiary care and the majority of secondary care of internal medicine patients in Iceland and there is no loss to follow-up of patients. The patient-associated medical data, which have been documented across a range of inpatient and outpatient healthcare settings, allow unprecedented correlation with multiple risk factors. Furthermore, the available data cover a significant retrospective study period (2010–2020), and the aggregation mode of this study may enable an interpretation of long-term medication intake on delirium. It should, however, be noted that this analysis is subject to several limitations. First, the aggregation into 2-year periods facilitates the identification of potential associations between delirium and other variables; however, this approach inevitably led to some loss of detail and may have reduced temporal precision, potentially obscuring short-term trends. Data were obtained from multiple healthcare settings; however, before analysis, all available information was harmonised and aggregated at the individual patient level across sources over the 2-year study period. Because most patients contributed data from more than one source and source-specific information was often complementary rather than overlapping, observations could not be meaningfully attributed to a single cluster. Consequently, clustering by data source was not feasible in the regression analyses, and we acknowledge that any residual within-source correlation cannot be entirely excluded. Additionally, the retrospective design of the study depends on the data collected and documented in the healthcare system for clinical purposes. A limitation is that we might not fully capture the severity of presenting complaints, such as mild versus severe pneumonia. In addition, the presence of a statistical association does not necessarily imply a causal relationship. Another limitation is the lack of information on the use of over-the-counter medications. Additionally, the use of information of medication filling from the prescription database may overestimate medication use as there is a lack of information on medication adherence. Furthermore, the lack of information on medication dosages and duration of exposure in the database limits the interpretation of the findings and may influence the observed associations. In addition to this, data on ethnicity and socioeconomic status were not available in the database. Although Iceland has a relatively homogeneous population with universal access to healthcare, the socioeconomic status may still contribute to medication use and the risk of delirium. The absence of these variables may therefore contribute to residual confounding. Regarding the coding of delirium, it is important to consider that there may be no incentive to code this condition, and that, in the majority of cases, hyperactive delirium is coded. Moreover, there may be a tendency to diagnose delirium in patients who have already received a prior diagnosis, which could introduce a potential bias in coding and reporting. It is also essential to consider that no adjustment was made for further comorbidities. Delirium was identified using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes F05 and R41; however, the R41 code is less specific and may capture organic cognitive disturbances. Nonetheless, re-analysis using only F05 ICD codes produced similar results. Excluding it entirely could lead to the omission of relevant cases of delirium that are not otherwise coded as F05. Even though there is an association with clinical outcomes such as more extended hospital stay and mortality, it is unclear if the diagnosis of delirium is the main driving factor or if delirium serves as a marker for increased frailty and comorbidity.

Conclusion

This study lends support to the proposal that delirium should not be viewed as a homogeneous disease, as has been the case in numerous clinical studies to date.55 Numerous medication risk factors have been identified which underlines the complexity of medication influences on delirium development. Future studies need to incorporate more in-depth clinical information regarding the illness severity, medication dosages and duration of the medication exposure to further assess these potential associations and causal pathways. To strengthen patient safety and hinder the development of delirium, an increased emphasis is needed to review medication use, preferably with a multidisiplinary approach involving internal medicine specialists and clinical pharmacists and improve our understanding of medication risk associated with delirium cause, treatment and prevention. Particular attention should be directed towards patients who previously obtained a delirium diagnosis on hospital admission.
