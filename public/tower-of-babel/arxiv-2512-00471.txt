Out-of-time correlation functions in single-body systems
Andrew C. Hunt

Abstract
In the study of quantum chaos, ‘out of time ordered correlators’ (OTOCs) are commonly used to quantify the rate at which quantum information is scrambled. This rate has been conjectured by Maldecena et al. to obey a universal, temperature dependent bound. Recent studies have shown that instantons, delocalised structures that dominate tunnelling statistics over barriers, reduce the growth rate of OTOCs. For the case of the symmetric double well, this reduction ensures the bound is maintained for OTOCs generated using ring polymer molecular dynamics (RPMD), a method with approximate dynamics but exact quantum statistics. In this report we set out to further understand the role of the instanton in the enforcement of the Maldacena bound and test whether RPMD is sufficient to satisfy the bound. We also investigate the impact of coherence on the flattening of of OTOCs by contrasting bounded with scattering systems. For the scattering system we observe a significantly smaller OTOC growth rate than that of the analogous bounded system, and a flattening in growth rate as time progresses. We attribute the first effect to influence of the Boltzmann operator, and the second to interference caused by anharmonicity of the potential, but require further for verification of these conclusions. In our studies of RPMD, we find counterexamples showing that it is not sufficient to satisfy the bound. In light of these results, we develop a theory for OTOCs using (analytically-continued) Matsubara dynamics, revealing significantly different dynamical behaviour around the instanton compared to the predictions of RPMD. The instanton is found to be stationary in all coordinates but its collective angle Φ 0 \Phi_{0} , and fluctuations about it no longer resemble that of classical dynamics on a first order saddle as in RPMD. Advancing this theory could further help elucidate the origin of the Maldacena bound and serves as a starting point in the development of novel instanton-based quantum rate theories.

Chapter 1
Introduction

Classical chaos investigates the complex and unpredictable behaviours of dynamical systems. It is observed in a vast range of systems, from the dynamics of biological systems
[
26
]
to the orbits of celestial objects
[
29
]
and is the cornerstone on which the ergodic hypothesis of classical statistical mechanics lies
[
4
]
. A commonly used diagnostic of classical chaos is ‘exponential sensitivity to small changes in initial conditions’, resulting in the ‘butterfly effect’.

The Heisenberg uncertainty principle forbids the specification of both position and momentum simultaneously in quantum systems. This presents a challenge for an analogous ‘butterfly effect’ like description of quantum chaos, as initial conditions can not all be specified. In addition to this, due to the linearity of the time-dependent Schrödinger equation, chaotic behaviour analogous to that of the corresponding classical phase space variables cannot arise in the expectation values of Hermitian operators.
For these reasons, the concept of ‘quantum chaos’ analogous to the classical case does not exist. Instead, quantum chaos refers to the study of quantized systems whose classical counterparts exhibit chaotic behaviour and the investigation of how the localisation of initial wavefunctions spreads throughout quantum systems over time, also known as ‘scrambling’.

‘Out-of-time ordered correlation functions’ (OTOCs) were originally introduced by Larkin and Ovchinnikov in their discussion of semiclassical approaches to superconductivity
[
20
]
. Following this, OTOCs have been reintroduced and extensively studied

Chapter 2
OTOCs

An ‘out-of-time ordered correlation function’ (OTOC) is a widely used measure of information scrambling in quantum systems
[
12
]
. This chapter begins with an examination of the Lyapunov exponent and its connection to classical chaos. We then discuss the relationship between chaos and scrambling, extending analysis to quantum systems. Following this, we introduce ‘ring polymer molecular dynamics’ (RPMD), by which semiclassical OTOCs may be calculated.

2.1
Chaos in classical systems

In the study of dynamical systems, the Lyapunov exponent
\lambda
is used to quantify the sensitivity of a system property with respect to slight changes in initial conditions. Consider a one-dimensional classical system with Hamiltonian

H(p,q)=T(p)+V(q)\text{,}

(2.1)

where the kinetic energy
T(p)=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}
,
V(q)
is the potential energy and
p
and
q
are the momentum and position respectively. If a trajectory is chaotic,

|\delta q_{t}|\approx|\delta q|e^{\lambda t}\text{,}

(2.2)

where
\delta q
is an initial perturbation in
q
and
\delta q_{t}
is the corresponding change in
q
at time
t
. A system is commonly
[
13
]
considered to be chaotic if the difference between two typical (i.e. not periodic orbits or stationary points) trajectories grows exponentially with time, indicated by a positive exponent in Eq.(
2.2
).

A more general metric of sensitivity to initial perturbations is the ‘stability matrix’

\boldsymbol{M}(t)\equiv\begin{pmatrix}M_{pp}&M_{pq}\\
M_{qp}&M_{qq}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}{\partial p_{t}}/{\partial p}&{\partial p_{t}}/{\partial q}\\
{\partial q_{t}}/{\partial p}&{\partial q_{t}}/{\partial q}\end{pmatrix}\text{.}

(2.3)

Each element of
\boldsymbol{M}
gives the sensitivity of the system phase space variables
x\in q,p
at time
t
with respect to in infinitesimal perturbation at time
0
. Note here that for
\delta q\to 0
, exponential growth rate of
M_{qq}
of Eq.(
2.3
) corresponds to the Lyapunov exponent in Eq.(
2.2
).

By differentiating the equations of motion for
p_{t}
and
q_{t}
with respect to
p
and
q
we obtain

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\boldsymbol{M}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-V^{\prime\prime}(q_{t})\\
m^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{M}\text{.}

(2.4)

The potential energy curvature
V^{\prime\prime}(q)\equiv\frac{{\rm d}^{2}V}{{\rm d}q^{2}}
generalises to the Hessian in the case of a system of multiple dimensions. When trajectories pass through regions for which
V^{\prime\prime}(q)<0
, Eq.(
2.4
) shows that the elements of the stability matrix grow exponentially, giving a positive Lyapunov exponent. For this reason even non-chaotic systems, containing areas of negative potential curvature, may still exhibit globally positive Lyapunov exponents.

In this report, we focus mainly on non-classically chaotic ‘barrier scrambling’ models. The simplest example of this is a double well, where only the trajectories in the local vicinity of the barrier are chaotic.

2.2
Thermally-averaged classical scrambling

We denote thermal expectation values at inverse temperature
\beta
as
\left<\cdot\right>_{\rm cl}
. For the one-dimensional system of Sec.
2.1
,

\left<\cdot\right>_{\rm cl}\equiv\frac{1}{hZ_{\rm cl}}\int{\rm d}p\,{\rm d}q\>\cdot e^{-\beta H(p,q)}\>\text{ ,}

where
h
is Plank’s constant, and the classical partition function

Z_{\rm cl}=\frac{1}{h}\int{\rm d}p\,{\rm d}q\>e^{-\beta H(p,q)}\text{.}

The integration limits are infinite in general unless stated otherwise throughout this thesis, with multiple integral signs abbreviated to a single one when possible.

To obtain a thermally-averaged Lyapunov exponent, one could construct

\begin{split}C_{1}(t)=\left<\left(\frac{\partial q_{t}}{\partial q}\right)\right>_{\rm cl}\text{,}\end{split}

(2.5)

then measure the exponential growth rate of the correlation function. Following integration by parts though, Eq.(
2.5
) gives

C_{1}(t)=\beta\left<V^{\prime}(q)\>q_{t}\right>_{\rm cl}\text{.}

(2.6)

This is the form of a two-time correlation function, which due to the Cauchy-Schwarz rule of weighted inner-products is bounded such that
\forall t

\left|\left<V^{\prime}(q)\>q_{t}\right>_{\rm cl}\right|\leq\sqrt{\left<V^{\prime}(q)^{2}\right>_{\rm cl}\left<q^{2}\right>_{\rm cl}}\text{.}

(2.7)

This bounding nullifies any possibility of long-term exponential growth, as also observed in other normal two-time correlation functions. For this reason it is more useful to consider,

\begin{split}C_{\rm cl}(t)&=\hbar^{2}\left<\left(\frac{\partial q_{t}}{\partial q}\right)^{2}\right>_{\rm cl}\text{,}\end{split}

(2.8)

which can no longer be rearranged into a multi-time correlation function. The presence of
\hbar^{2}
in Eq.(
2.8
) is due to quantum-classical correspondence as will be explained in the following section. We extract the classical thermal Lyapunov exponent
\lambda_{\rm cl}
as

\lambda_{\rm cl}:=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\ln C_{\rm cl}(t)\text{,}

(2.9)

where
t\in I
, the time interval within which
C_{\rm cl}(t)
grows exponentially. Here,
\lambda
is assumed to be time-independent, but this may not always be true. In practice, the Lyapunov exponent is calculated as an average of the early-time exponential growth of
C_{\rm cl}(t)
as calculation at later times becomes exponentially more computationally expensive.

At late times, we can calculate the growth rate of
C_{\rm cl}(t)
for barrier scrambling systems by considering linearised dynamics about fixed points in phase space, following the approach of Xu
et al.
​​
[
41
]
. As mentioned in Sec.
2.1
, we consider a one-dimensional system with a single saddle point in
V(q)
which we call the ‘barrier’. We position the barrier at
q=0
and give curvature
\sqrt{\frac{1}{m}V^{\prime\prime}(0)}=i\omega_{\rm b}
.

Due to the form of Eq.(
2.4
), at late times the growth of Eq.(
2.8
) is dominated by trajectories that spend all time in the vicinity of the barrier. In this region of configuration space, the dynamics corresponding to the exact Hamiltonian
H(p,q)
can be generated by an approximate Hamiltonian

\begin{split}H(p,q)\approx H_{\delta}(p,q)&:=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}-\frac{1}{2}m\omega_{\rm b}^{2}q^{2}\text{.}\quad\quad\left|q\right|<\delta\ll 1\end{split}

(2.10)

Applying the transformation

\begin{pmatrix}a^{+}\\
a^{-}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2m\omega_{\rm b}}}\begin{pmatrix}1&m\omega_{\rm b}\\
1&-m\omega_{\rm b}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}p\\
q\end{pmatrix}\text{,}

(2.11)

we use Eq. (
2.10
) to generate decoupled equations of motion for these new variables as

a^{\pm}_{t}=a^{\pm}\>e^{\pm\omega_{\rm b}t}\text{.}

(2.12)

We now define the constraint function

S(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\quad\quad|x|>\delta\\
1&\quad\quad|x|\leq\delta\end{array}\right.

(2.13)

with which we constrain
q
and
q_{t}
(which can now be written as functions of
a^{\pm}
and
a^{\pm}_{t}
respectively) within the thin strip of phase space surrounding the saddle point. We therefore write
C_{\rm cl}
at late times in terms of trajectories that have spent all time in the vicinity of the barrier as

\begin{split}C_{\rm cl}(t\to\infty)&=\hbar^{2}\int{\rm d}a^{+}{\rm d}a^{-}e^{-\beta H_{\delta}}\left(\frac{\partial q_{t}}{\partial q}\right)^{2}S(a^{+}-a^{-})S(a^{+}e^{\omega_{\rm b}t}-a^{-}e^{-\omega_{\rm b}t})\text{.}\end{split}

(2.14)

At large
t
, the product of constraint functions is non-zero for
|a^{+}|<\delta\exp(-\omega_b t)
and
|a^{-}|<\delta\exp(-\omega_bt)+\delta\simeq\delta
. Also, using the equations of motion of
a^{\pm}
,

\begin{split}\lim_{t\to\infty}\left[\frac{\partial q_{t}}{\partial q}\right]&=e^{\omega_{\rm b}t}\text{,}\quad\quad\left|q\right|<\delta\ll 1\end{split}

(2.15)

such that

\begin{split}C_{\rm cl}(t\to\infty)&=\hbar^{2}e^{2\omega_{\rm b}t}\int_{-\delta e^{-\omega_{\rm b}t}}^{\delta e^{-\omega_{\rm b}t}}{\rm d}a^{+}\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}{\rm d}a^{-}\>e^{-\beta H_{\delta}(a^{+},a^{-})}\\
&\sim e^{\omega_{\rm b}t}\text{.}\end{split}

(2.16)

The exponentially shrinking limits on
{\rm d}a^{+}
reduce the growth of
C_{\rm cl}(t)
at late times by a factor of two. We conclude that

\lambda_{\rm cl}\approx\omega_{\rm b}\text{,}

(2.17)

for one-dimensional classical barrier scrambling systems at late times. We therefore can verify that
C_{\rm cl}
for barrier scrambling grows exponentially at late times and following similar arguments, see that
C_{1}
will not.

2.3
Quantum scrambling

It is well known that commutators between operators
\hat{W}
and
\hat{V}
in quantum mechanics correspond to Poisson brackets
[
10
]
between variables
w
and
v
in the
\hbar\to 0
limit;

\lim_{\hbar\to 0}\frac{1}{i\hbar}[\hat{W},\hat{V}]=\{w,v\}.

(2.18)

Therefore, for
\hat{W}=\hat{q}
and
\hat{V}=\hat{p}
, Eq.(
2.8
) is the classical limit of

\begin{split}C_{\rm qm}(t)&=\left<[\hat{q}_{t},\hat{p}]^{\dagger}[\hat{q}_{t},\hat{p}]\right>_{\rm std}\text{.}\end{split}

(2.19)

When possible we will abbreviate
C_{\rm qm}(t)
as
\left<[\hat{q}_{t},\hat{p}]^{2}\right>_{\rm std}
for legibility, where the absolute square of the commutator is implied. The
t
-subscripts indicate that operators have been evolved in time up to
t
(i.e.
\hat{q}_{t}\equiv e^{\frac{it}{\hbar}\hat{H}}\hat{q}\>e^{-\frac{it}{\hbar}\hat{H}}
). For thermal expectation values, we now use the quantum thermal average

\left<\cdot\right>_{\rm std}\equiv\Tr{\hat\rho\:\cdot}\text{,}

(2.20)

for which
\hat{\rho}=Z_{\rm qm}^{-1}e^{-\beta\hat{H}}
is the thermal density matrix and the quantum canonical partition function
Z_{\rm qm}=\Tr{e^{-\beta\hat H}}
.

The thermal density matrix can be spread between operators in the expression of Eq.(
2.19
). This spreading, referred to as ‘regularisation’, does not alter the classical limit. One commonly
[
6
]
used regularisation of
C_{\rm qm}
is the ‘symmetrised’ version for which pairs of operators are separated by
\hat{y}^{2}=\hat{\rho}^{1/2}
.

\left<[\hat{q}_{t},\hat{p}]^{2}\right>_{\rm sym}:=\Tr{\hat y^2[\hat q_t,\hat p]^\dagger\hat y^2 [\hat q_t,\hat p] }\text{.}

(2.21)

Other forms of regularisation are possible, and will be introduced when appropriate.

2.4
A bound on quantum scrambling

Quantum OTOCs have been extensively studied the field of many-body physics. In this context, there are two main timescales relevant to the growth of OTOCs. The first is the dissipation time
t_{\rm d}
which gives the timescale on which the memory of local interactions is lost in the system. This timescale is commonly related to the decorrelation time of two-point correlators. The second is the scrambling time
t_{\rm s}
, the timescale for which any initial information (in the form of wavefunction localisation) fed into the system is irretrievably spread.

For systems in which there is a hierarchy in timescales such that
t_{\rm d}\ll t_{\rm s}
, some display OTOCs which grow exponentially, analogously to classical mechanics,

\left<[\hat{W}_{t},\hat{V}]^{2}\right>\sim e^{\lambda_{\rm qm}t}\text{.}\quad\quad\quad\quad t_{\rm d}<t\ll t_{\rm s}

(2.22)

Here,
\lambda_{\rm qm}
is commonly referred to as the ‘quantum Lyapunov exponent’.

In 2016, Maldacena
et al.
conjectured a universal (system independent) upper bound on this exponent
[
21
]

\lambda_{\rm qm}\leq\frac{2\pi}{\beta\hbar}\text{,}

(2.23)

which we refer to as the ‘Maldacena bound’. Due to the reciprocal dependence on
\hbar
in Eq.(
2.23
), all classical systems trivially satisfy this bound.
Following this, Tsuji
et al.
re-derived this bound
[
39
]
using the assumption that exponential growth of the OTOC was independent of regularisation.

Although both proofs require relatively stringent criteria and assumptions to be valid, numerical studies have all so far shown that both quantum many-body
[
19
,
2
,
28
,
1
]
and single-body systems
[
16
,
17
]
, including barrier scrambling systems
[
15
]
, satisfy the bound. This consistency suggests that, even though the Maldacena bound was introduced in the context of many-body physics, it may be a fundamental property of all quantum systems.

RPMD approach to OTOCS

Ring polymer molecular dynamics (RPMD)
[
8
]
is a method introduced by Manolopoulos
et al.
using the fictitious dynamics of ‘ring polymers’ to approximate ‘Kubo-regularised’ time correlation functions (TCFs). These dynamics
have the decency

1

W.H. Miller
to satisfy quantum detailed-balance.
Following the introduction of RPMD it was pointed out by Althorpe
et al.
that it is an approximation to Matsubara dynamics
[
18
]
, a theory by which Fourier smoothing of the path integral gives quantum dynamics without real-time coherence. In this section we introduce the basic concepts behind RPMD, and state the form of an RPMD OTOC. For a detailed derivation see Ref.
[
36
]
.

In our discussions here, we continue to use the Hamiltonian of Eq.(
2.1
), with hats on corresponding quantum operators. Generalisation to multiple dimensions is straightforward. Due to Feynman’s path integral formulation of quantum statistical mechanics
[
11
]
the quantum partition function, at inverse temperature
\beta

Z_{\rm qm}=\lim_{N\to\infty}Z_{N}\text{,}

(2.24)

where

Z_{N}=\frac{1}{(2\pi\hbar)^{N}}\int{\rm d}\boldsymbol{p}\>{\rm d}\boldsymbol{q}\>e^{-\beta_{N}H_{N}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})}\text{,}

(2.25)

with
\beta_{N}=\beta/N
. Equation (
2.25
) is an imaginary time path integral, discretized into
N
imaginary-time slices, each of which is given a set of phase space variables {
p_{n},q_{n}\}
. We abbreviate integrals over multiple coordinates (and will continue to do so in later sections) as
{\rm d}\boldsymbol{x}\equiv{\rm d}x_{1}{\rm d}x_{2}...{\rm d}x_{N}
. The ring polymer Hamiltonian,

H_{N}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=\underbrace{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left[\frac{p_{n}^{2}}{2m}+V(q_{n})\right]}_{{{T_{N}(\boldsymbol{p})+V_{N}(\boldsymbol{q}})}}+S_{N}(\boldsymbol{q})

(2.26)

is a sum over a ring of identical classical systems with Hamiltonian
H(p_{n},q_{n})
with coordinates adjacent in imaginary time connected by the spring potential

S_{N}(\boldsymbol{q})=\frac{m}{2(\beta_{N}\hbar)^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(q_{n}-q_{n+1})^{2}\text{.}\quad\quad(q_{N+1}\equiv q_{1})

(2.27)

In RPMD, the quantum partition function
Z_{\rm qm}
is approximated by
Z_{N}
for some finite
N
. The quantum statistics of RPMD are exact in the
N\to\infty
limit (where the discrete paths
\boldsymbol{q}
become functions of imaginary time
q(\tau)
of period
\beta\hbar
), but in practice
N
is treated as a convergence parameter, commonly ranging from
16
-
128
[
14
]
. The ring polymer Hamiltonian,
H_{N}
, is used to generate artificial, classical dynamics - ‘ring polymer molecular dynamics’. In other words, for some system property
f(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})
time evolution is given by

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}f(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=\mathcal{L}_{\rm rp}f(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})\text{,}

(2.28)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\rm rp}\cdot\equiv\left\{H_{N}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}),\cdot\right\}
and
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
is the Poisson bracket corresponding to the
2N
-dimensional ring polymer phase space.

The RPMD approximation to Kubo-regularised correlation functions can be shown to be exact in the high-temperature, harmonic and
t\to 0
limits
[
14
]
. In addition to these pleasant properties, RPMD preserves the quantum Boltzmann distribution, known as ‘detailed balance’. To demonstrate this we consider some RPMD TCF between operators
A
and
B
,

\begin{split}C_{\rm AB}^{\rm rp}:=&\>\frac{1}{(2\pi\hbar)^{N}Z_{N}}\int{\rm d}\boldsymbol{p}\>{\rm d}\boldsymbol{q}\>e^{-\beta_{N}H_{N}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})}A(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})\>e^{\mathcal{L}_{\rm rp}t}B(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})\\
=&\>\frac{1}{(2\pi\hbar)^{N}Z_{N}}\int{\rm d}\boldsymbol{p}\>{\rm d}\boldsymbol{q}\>e^{-\beta_{N}H_{N}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})}B(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})\>e^{-\mathcal{L}_{\rm rp}t}A(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})\text{,}\end{split}

(2.29)

where the second line in Eq.(
2.29
) is obtained by expanding
e^{\mathcal{L}_{\rm rp}t}
as a Taylor series in
t
, then applying integration by parts to move all derivatives applied on
B(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})
onto
e^{-\beta H}A(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})
and rearranging. The manipulation of Eq.(
2.29
) implies detailed balance,

C_{AB}^{\rm rp}(t)=C_{BA}^{\rm rp}(-t)\text{.}

(2.30)

By choosing
A\!\!=\!\!1
, detailed balance in RPMD TCFs implies

C_{1B}^{\rm rp}=C_{B1}^{\rm rp}\equiv\left\langle\hat{B}\right\rangle_{\rm std}\text{,}

(2.31)

as
C_{B1}
is simply the path integral formulation of the exact quantum thermal expectation value of
\hat{B}
.

Following Sadhasivam
[
36
]
, we take the Kubo regularisation of a quantum OTOC as

\left<[\hat{q}_{t},\hat{p}]^{2}\right>_{\rm kubo}:=\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\beta}{\rm d}\lambda\>\Tr{\hat\rho^{\:-(\beta-\lambda)}[\hat q_t,\hat p]^\dagger\hat\rho^{\:-\lambda}[\hat q_t,\hat p]}\text{,}

(2.32)

in which the density matrix is ‘smeared’ between each commutator. This regularised form of correlation functions in general ensures that the TCF is real and time-reversible. The RPMD approximation of Eq.(
2.32
) is

\left<\left(\frac{\partial X_{\!0\,t}}{\partial X_{\!0}}\right)^{2}\right>_{\rm rp}:=\frac{1}{(2\pi\hbar)^{N}Z_{N}}\int{\rm d}\boldsymbol{p}\>{\rm d}\boldsymbol{q}\>e^{-\beta_{N}H_{N}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})}\left(\frac{\partial X_{\!0\,t}}{\partial X_{\!0}}\right)^{2}\text{,}

(2.33)

wherein the centroid coordinate

X_{\!0}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}q_{n}\text{,}

(2.34)

the arithmetic mean of the positions of each identical classical system. Note that the form of Eq.(
2.34
) is exactly the same as a multi-dimensional classical OTOC in the
2N
-dimensional phase space defined by
H_{N}
.
By considering Taylor expansions about
t=0
, it can be shown that the RPMD agrees with the Kubo-regularised quantum OTOC up to
O(t^{6})
.

As they obey quantum detailed balance, RPMD and Matsubara dynamics conserve the quantum Boltzmann distribution
[
40
]
, in contrast to other common semiclassical methods such the family based on Initial Value Representation
[
38
]
. Ref.
[
35
]
argues that the Maldecena bound is enforced due to conservation of these statistics. We will further explore and test this claim in the following chapter.

Chapter 3
RPMD OTOCs

Sadhasivam
et al.
have shown that formation of ‘instantons’ in symmetric barrier scrambling systems reduces the exponential growth rate of RPMD OTOCs
[
35
]
at low temperatures, such that they satisfy the Maldacena bound. In this chapter, we first outline their proof, then examine specific examples where it may break down, exploring the reasons for these exceptions.

3.1
Multidimensional classical scrambling

In this section we generalise the result of Sec.
2.2
to a single particle classical system of
N
dimensions. We will use this result in the next section for the
N
-dimensional system defined by RPMD. We now define the stability matrix

\boldsymbol{M}(t):=\frac{\partial\{\boldsymbol{p}_{t},\boldsymbol{q}_{t}\}}{\partial\{\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}\}}

(3.1)

where the phase space variables
\boldsymbol{p}
and
{\boldsymbol{q}}
are now
N
-dimensional and our Hamiltonian

H(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=\frac{\boldsymbol{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{p}}{2m}+V(\boldsymbol{q})\text{.}

(3.2)

We obtain the equations of motion for the stability matrix as

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\boldsymbol{M}(t)=\boldsymbol{A}(t)\boldsymbol{M}(t)\text{,}

(3.3)

where

\boldsymbol{A}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}&-\boldsymbol{V^{\prime\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q}_{t})\\
m^{-1}\boldsymbol{1}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\end{pmatrix}\text{.}

(3.4)

The Hessian at time
t
, denoted as
\boldsymbol{V^{\prime\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q}_{t})\equiv\frac{\partial^{2}V(\boldsymbol{q}_{t})}{\partial\boldsymbol{q}_{t}\partial\boldsymbol{q}_{t}}
. We use
\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{1}
as the
N\times N
zero and identity matrices respectively.

For a fixed point in dynamics at
\{\boldsymbol{p}_{t},\boldsymbol{q}_{t}\}=\{\boldsymbol{\tilde{p}},\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}}\}\>\forall t
meaning that the stability matrix propagator
\boldsymbol{A}(t)\to\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}}
, a constant. The equation of motion of the stability matrix at this point,
\boldsymbol{\tilde{M}}
, is therefore governed by a first order linear differential equation. This makes each element of
\boldsymbol{\tilde{M}}
a linear combination of exponential terms, with growth rates determined by the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i},i\in\{\pm 1,\pm 2\cdots\pm N\}
of
\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}}
.

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\tilde{M}}_{ij}(t)&=\sum_{i}c_{ij}\>e^{\lambda_{i}t}\text{,}\end{split}

(3.5)

which means that in the late-time limit,

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\tilde{M}}_{ij}(t\to\infty)&\sim e^{\lambda_{\rm max}t}\text{,}\end{split}

(3.6)

provided that
c_{ij}\neq 0
for
i
corresponding to
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{\rm max}
, the eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}}
with largest positive real part. In practice it is found that
c_{ij}=0
is only ever true in special cases where a prohibitive symmetry constraint exists, which is not encountered in this report.

As
\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}}
is block antidiagonal, its eigenvalues satisfy
[
37
]

\det{\lambda_i^2\mt{1}+\frac{1}{m}\mt{V''}(\vc{\tilde q})}=0

(3.7)

meaning that we may relate these eigenvalues to those of the mass-weighted Hessian eigenvalues as

\displaystyle\lambda_{\pm i}=\pm\eta_{i}\text{,}

(3.8)

where

\eta_{i}:=\sqrt{-\frac{1}{m}\frac{\partial^{2}V(\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}})}{\partial\xi_{i}^{2}}}

(3.9)

and
\xi_{i},i\in\{1,2\cdots N\}
are the normal modes of the Hessian.
We therefore conclude that the max growth rate of the stability matrix at fixed points in multi-dimensional systems is equal to the most negative eigenfrequency of the mass-weighted Hessian at that point.

Following the same logic as in Sec.
2.2
we now consider the dynamics about the fixed point. We use the constraint function from Eq.(
2.13
) to define the ‘survival probability’

P(t)=\int{\rm d}|\boldsymbol{z}|\prod_{i=1}^{2N}S(|z_{i}|)S(|z_{i\>t}|)\text{,}

(3.10)

which indicates the probability that a trajectory, starting in a region of phase space where the stability matrix grows at the rate defined by
\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}}
, remains in that region at time
t
. Here,
\{\boldsymbol{z}\}
is some complex coordinate system made from
\{\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}\}
corresponding to the eigenvectors of
\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}}
centered at
\{\boldsymbol{\tilde{p}},\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}}\}
. As we know the dynamics about this fixed point in these coordinates,

\begin{split}P(t)&=\int{\rm d}|\boldsymbol{z}|\prod_{i=1}^{2N}S(|z_{i}|)S(|z_{i}\>e^{\lambda_{i}t}|)\\
&\sim\prod_{\Re{\lambda_i}>0}e^{-\lambda_{i}t}\text{.}\end{split}

(3.11)

Combining Eq.(
3.6
) with Eq.(
3.11
) gives the late-time growth rate of a barrier scrambling OTOC in an
N
-dimensional space as

\begin{split}C(t\to\infty)&\sim e^{2\lambda_{\rm max}t}\prod_{\Re{\lambda_i}>0}e^{-\lambda_{i}t}\text{,}\end{split}

(3.12)

a result analogous to that obtained by Xu
et al.
in
[
41
]
. This result can be written in terms of the normal mode frequencies using Eq.(
3.8
) to give our final result

\begin{split}C(t\to\infty)&\sim e^{2\eta_{\rm max}t}\prod_{\eta_{i}>0}e^{-\eta_{i}t}\text{.}\end{split}

(3.13)

Note that for the case of a first order saddle in potential, the growth rate of
C(t\to\infty)
simplifies to
\eta_{\rm max}
, the negative eigenfrequency of the mass-weighted Hessian.

3.2
Instantons and the bound on chaos

RPMD is classical mechanics in
2N
-dimensional phase space, for which the new effective ‘ring polymer potential’

U_{N}(\boldsymbol{q})=V_{N}(\boldsymbol{q})+S_{N}(\boldsymbol{q})\text{,}

(3.14)

using the definitions established in Sec.
2.5
. Due to the presence of
\beta_{N}
in the potential (in the springs between adjacent beads), the potential energy landscape defined by
U_{N}
is temperature dependent.

For barrier scrambling systems, above the ‘crossover temperature’

T_{\rm c}=\frac{\hbar\omega_{b}}{2\pi k_{B}}\text{,}

(3.15)

there is a first order saddle in
U_{N}
corresponding to all of the beads located at the top of the barrier (see the red configuration in Fig.
3.1
). We refer to this configuration as the ‘classical saddle’, for which we may use Eq.(
3.13
) to calculate the late-time growth of an RPMD OTOC as

\lambda_{\rm rp}\approx\omega_{b}\text{.}\quad\quad\quad\quad T>T_{c}

(3.16)

Due to the form of Eq.(
2.27
), as
T
is decreased below
T_{c}
, the springs holding adjacent beads become so weak that a delocalised structure now becomes the first order saddle in
U_{N}
, with a lower potential energy (see the blue configuration in Fig.
3.1
). This structure is known as the ‘instanton’, which we in general denote with a tilde (i.e.
\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}}
corresponds to
\boldsymbol{q}
valued at the instanton).

The proof of Sadhasivam begins with the observation that trajectories that pass over the barrier below
T_{c}
fluctuate about the instanton. Hence, using Eq.(
3.13
) the growth of the OTOC

\lambda_{\rm rp}\approx\eta_{0}\text{,}\quad\quad\quad\quad T<T_{c}

(3.17)

where

\eta_{0}=\sqrt{-\frac{1}{m}\frac{\partial^{2}U_{N}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}})}{d\xi_{0}^{2}}}\text{,}

(3.18)

and
\xi_{0}
is the unstable normal mode of the RPMD Hessian at the instanton.
In the case of a symmetric barrier,
\xi_{0}
is primarily directed along the centroid coordinate
X_{0}
(defined in Eq.(
2.34

\begin{split}\eta_{0}&\approx\sqrt{-\frac{1}{m}\frac{\partial^{2}U_{N}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}})}{\partial X_{\!0}^{2}}}\\
&=\sqrt{-\frac{1}{m}\frac{\partial^{2}V_{N}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}})}{\partial X_{\!0}^{2}}}.\end{split}

(3.19)

The second equality in Eq.(
3.19
) follows due to there being no coupling between springs in
S_{N}
and
X_{0}
in Eq.(
2.27
).
Now as we know the instanton is a first order saddle in
U_{N}
, any displacements perpendicular to
X_{0}
must be stable with respect to perturbations, that is

\frac{\partial^{2}U_{N}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}})}{\partial{X_{k}}^{2}}\geq 0\quad\quad k=\pm 1,\pm 2\cdots

(3.20)

where the orthogonal basis vectors

{X_{k}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}T_{kn}q_{n}\text{,}

(3.21)

and the
N\times N
discrete Fourier transform matrix

{T_{kn}}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\frac{2}{N}}\cos\left(\frac{2kn\pi}{N}\right)&k=-1,-2,-3\cdots\\
\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}}&k=0\\
\sqrt{\frac{2}{N}}\sin\left(\frac{2kn\pi}{N}\right)&k=1,2,3\cdots\end{cases}

(3.22)

These coordinates are eigenvectors of the Hessian corresponding to
S_{N}
with
N\to\infty

\left(\frac{2k\pi}{\beta\hbar}\right)^{2}\!\!\!
. Due to
U_{N}=V_{N}+S_{N}
, Eq.(
3.20
) implies

\frac{1}{m}\frac{\partial^{2}V_{N}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}})}{\partial{X_{k}}^{2}}\geq-\left(\frac{2k\pi}{\beta\hbar}\right)^{2}\quad\quad k=\pm 1,\pm 2\cdots

(3.23)

Now it can be shown using Eq.(
3.21
) that

\frac{\partial^{2}V_{N}(\boldsymbol{q})}{\partial X_{0}^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}V_{N}(\boldsymbol{q})}{\partial X_{k}^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}V_{N}(\boldsymbol{q})}{\partial X_{-k}^{2}}\right]\quad\quad k=\pm 1,\pm 2\cdots

(3.24)

Combining Eq.(
3.23
) with Eq.(
3.24
) we have

-\frac{1}{m}\frac{\partial^{2}V_{N}(\boldsymbol{q})}{\partial X_{0}^{2}}\leq\left(\frac{2\pi}{\beta\hbar}\right)^{2}\text{,}

(3.25)

which, given the assumption of Eq.(
3.19

\eta_{0}\leq\frac{2\pi}{\beta\hbar}\text{,}

(3.26)

making the growth of RPMD OTOCs satisfy the Maldacena bound.

3.3
Breaking the bound

In this section we illustrate two examples for which RPMD OTOCs violate the Maldacena bound. The first is a system for which instantons do not form, due to there being no saddle in potential. The second example is where the assumption of Eq.(
3.19

3.3.1
Systems without instantons

Here we define the two-dimensional classical Hamiltonian
H(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})=T(\boldsymbol{p})+V(\boldsymbol{q})

3.4
Boltzmann averaging of unstable mode

It follows from Sec.
3.3.2
that due to projections of the unstable mode into the non-centroid modes of the instanton, the
t\to\infty
growth of the RPMD OTOC will violate the Maldacena bound.

For early times, on the timescale on which quantum OTOCs grow exponentially, it is observed (compare
\eta_{0}
with
\lambda_{\rm rp}
in Fig.
3.5
) that the RPMD OTOC grows at a rate significantly lower than that of
\eta_{0}
. We suggest that is due to short-time thermal averaging of the unstable mode softening the early growth of the RPMD OTOC.

To measure the thermal average of the unstable mode we define

\eta_{\beta}^{\rm rp}:=\sqrt{-\frac{1}{m}\left<\frac{\partial^{2}U_{N}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}})}{\partial\xi_{0}^{2}}\delta(\xi_{0})\right>_{\rm rp}}\text{,}

(3.29)

where
\xi_{0}
is the negative frequency normal mode of the ring polymer potential
U_{N}
in the instanton configuration
\boldsymbol{\tilde{q}}
, and

\left<\cdot\right>_{\rm rp}:=\frac{1}{(2\pi\hbar)^{N}Z_{N}}\int{\rm d}\boldsymbol{p}\>{\rm d}\boldsymbol{q}\cdot e^{-\beta_{N}H_{N}(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q})}

(3.30)

is the ring polymer phase space average.

In the following sections, we construct an expression for the unstable mode (following the work of Althorpe) then test whether this phenomenon is adequate to make RPMD OTOCs in asymmetric systems satisfy the bound on the timescales relevant to the quantum OTOC growth.

3.4.1
Geometry of the instanton and the unstable mode

In the construction of the unstable mode, we consider the
N\to\infty

\boldsymbol{q}
becomes infinite dimensional, and thus can be equivalently represented as
q(\tau)

\tau

\beta\hbar

3.5
Conclusion

In this chapter we have shown that the infection of springs into ring polymer molecular dynamics can lead to violations of the Maldacena bound for RPMD OTOCs. The results of this chapter
do not contradict
the claim of Ref.
[
35
]
that the Maldecena bound is enforced due to quantum Boltzmann statistics: thermal quenching of scrambling was consistently observed in the reduction of
\lambda_{\rm rp}
versus
\lambda_{\rm cl}
in all models considered. Rather, the results indicate that to properly test this theory, it must be done in a framework for which the underlying dynamics are accurate.

The appropriate theory for this further work is Matsubara dynamics, but due to a severe sign-problem
[
18
]
, there is no computationally feasible way to directly calculate a Matsubara OTOC. In the next chapter, we instead suggest an indirect method to calculate the late time growth of the Matsubara OTOC, avoiding this issue.

Chapter 4
Matsubara OTOCs

In the previous chapter we demonstrated that RPMD is an inadequate method to satisfy the Maldacena bound. In this chapter we illustrate the relationship between RPMD and Matsubara dynamics, then construct a method for the calculation of the late-time growth of a Matsubara OTOC. We focus only on systems at temperatures below
T_{c}
as above this temperature all saddle-point systems trivially satisfy the bound.

4.1
Relation of RPMD to Matsubara dynamics

RPMD considers the dynamics of jagged ring polymers, for which the number of beads
N
is a convergence parameter. Matsubara dynamics however describes that of smooth loops in imaginary time in the limit of
N\to\infty
, but with the number of Fourier modes (in this context referred to as Matsubara modes) having been kept at a finite
M
. In this limit, the coordinates and momenta can, following the logic of Eq.(
3.31
), be written as a Fourier series in terms of the
M
modes (which we assume to be odd) as

q_{\rm mats}(\tau)=Q_{0}+\sqrt{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\mu}\left\{Q_{n}(\tau_{0})\sin\omega_{n}(\tau_{0}+\tau)+Q_{\bar{n}}(\tau_{0})\cos\omega_{n}(\tau_{0}+\tau)\right\}\text{.}

(4.1)

We remind the reader that though the non-centroid modes are independent of
\tau
, they do depend on the choice of imaginary time origin
\tau_{0}
. We have defined
\mu=(M-1)/2
for notational convenience, and abbreviate the whole path as
\boldsymbol{Q}
, as knowledge of these
M
modes specifies
q_{\rm mats}(\tau),\>\forall\tau
. The expression corresponding to
p_{\rm mats}(\tau)
is analogous to Eq.(
4.1
).

Following Willat​
[
40
]
​ we can analytically continue the Matsubara time-correlation function between
A(\boldsymbol{Q})
and
B(\boldsymbol{Q})
to give

C_{AB}^{[M]}=\frac{1}{(2\pi\hbar)^{M}Z_{M}}\int{\rm d}\boldsymbol{Q}\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{P}\>e^{-\beta R_{M}(\boldsymbol{Q},\boldsymbol{P})}A(\boldsymbol{Q})\>e^{\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{M}t}B(\boldsymbol{Q})\text{,}

(4.2)

where
R_{M}
denotes the
N\to\infty
limit of the ring polymer Hamiltonian
H_{N}
, evaluated over the path defined by the Matsubara modes in Eq.(
4.1
).

R_{M}\equiv\lim_{N\to\infty}H_{N}=\oint_{\beta\hbar}\frac{p_{\rm mats}(\tau)^{2}}{2m}{\rm d}\tau+S_{E}[\boldsymbol{Q}]\text{.}

(4.3)

Here,
U_{N}
has become the Euclidean action
S_{E}
, as defined in Eq.(
3.32
), evaluated over the path
q_{\rm mats}(\tau)
.

The only difference between Eq.(
4.2
) and the
N\to\infty
limit of the corresponding RPMD correlation function is the Fourier smoothing of the paths integrated over (due to finite
M
), and the complex Liouvilllian

\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{M}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm rp}+i\mathcal{L}_{\rm I}\text{.}

(4.4)

The real part
\mathcal{L}_{\rm rp}

\mathcal{L}_{\rm rp}=\sum_{n=-\mu}^{\mu}\left\{\frac{P_{n}}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial Q_{n}}-\frac{\partial S_{E}[\boldsymbol{Q}]}{\partial Q_{n}}\frac{\partial}{\partial P_{n}}\right\}\text{,}

(4.5)

generates the classical dynamics of RPMD in the space of the
M
Matsubara modes, whilst the imaginary part

\mathcal{L}_{\rm I}=\sum_{n=-\mu}^{\mu}\omega_{n}\left(P_{\overline{n}}\frac{\partial}{\partial P_{n}}-Q_{\overline{n}}\frac{\partial}{\partial Q_{n}}\right)

(4.6)

propagates trajectories into the complex plane, making the dynamics unstable and theory computationally infeasible in all but some applications
[
30
]
.

Removal of
\mathcal{L}_{\rm I}
in analytically continued Matsubara therefore gives ‘smoothed RPMD’, which suggests that some spurious artefacts observed in RPMD may be removed by inclusion of this extra part. In this chapter we consider these dynamics, when linearised about a fixed point, to obtain a late-time approximation to the growth of a Matsubara OTOC.

4.2
Complex instantons

The complex Liouvillian cannot be written in terms of a Poisson bracket, hence there is no Hamiltonian associated with the complex dynamics and therefore the Matsubara phase space variables
\{\boldsymbol{Q},\boldsymbol{P}\}
are not governed by Hamilton’s equations of motion. Consequently, the intuition of ‘zero momentum + zero potential gradient
=
fixed point’ from classical mechanics is no longer applicable. Therefore, in this section we consider the action of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{M}
on the stationary instanton configuration
\{\boldsymbol{Q},\boldsymbol{P}\}=\{\boldsymbol{\tilde{Q}},\boldsymbol{0}\}
to see whether it remains a fixed point in analytically continued Matsubara dynamics.

We start by re-writing the Eq.(
4.4
) as

\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{M}=\sum_{n=-\mu}^{\mu}\left\{\mathcal{L}_{Q_{n}}+\mathcal{L}_{P_{n}}\right\}+i\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\tau_{0}}\text{,}

(4.7)

where we have used the chain rule and Eq.(
4.1
) to define the imaginry time translation operator

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\tau_{0}}=-\sum_{n=-\mu}^{\mu}\omega_{n}\left(P_{\overline{n}}\frac{\partial}{\partial P_{n}}+Q_{\overline{n}}\frac{\partial}{\partial Q_{n}}\right)\text{.}

(4.8)

We have packaged up the other operators as

\mathcal{L}_{Q_{n}}=\mathchoice{\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!15}$\displaystyle\frac{P_{n}}{m}$}}{\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!15}$\textstyle\frac{P_{n}}{m}$}}{\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!15}$\scriptstyle\frac{P_{n}}{m}$}}{\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!15}$\scriptscriptstyle\frac{P_{n}}{m}$}}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial Q_{n}}\text{,}

(4.9)

and

\mathcal{L}_{P_{n}}=\mathchoice{\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!15}$\displaystyle\left(-\frac{\partial S_{E}[\boldsymbol{Q}]}{\partial Q_{n}}+2i\omega_{n}P_{\overline{n}}\right)$}}{\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!15}$\textstyle\left(-\frac{\partial S_{E}[\boldsymbol{Q}]}{\partial Q_{n}}+2i\omega_{n}P_{\overline{n}}\right)$}}{\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!15}$\scriptstyle\left(-\frac{\partial S_{E}[\boldsymbol{Q}]}{\partial Q_{n}}+2i\omega_{n}P_{\overline{n}}\right)$}}{\hbox{\pagecolor{gray!15}$\scriptscriptstyle\left(-\frac{\partial S_{E}[\boldsymbol{Q}]}{\partial Q_{n}}+2i\omega_{n}P_{\overline{n}}\right)$}}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial P_{n}}\text{.}

(4.10)

This definition of Eq.(
4.7
) shows the Liouvillian as being made up of three types of operation. The first two,
\mathcal{L}_{Q_{n}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{P_{n}}
, when applied on any function involve the multiplication by a prefactor (highlighted with
grey boxes
for clarity) which when evaluated at the stationary instanton equal zero. For the last operation however, this is not necessarily so. We may illustrate this by applying it on any non-centroid position mode, for example

i\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\tau_{0}}Q_{1}=-i\omega_{1}Q_{\bar{1}}\text{,}

(4.11)

which is evidently not zero in the instanton configuration. We therefore conclude that, unlike in RPMD, the instanton in Matsubara dynamics is not stationary, as the position modes ‘whizz off’ into the complex plane when evolved in time.

We now define imaginary-time translation invariant (ITTI) properties as some function of the phase space variables
g(\boldsymbol{P},\boldsymbol{Q})
for which

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\tau_{0}}\>g(\boldsymbol{P},\boldsymbol{Q})=0\text{.}

(4.12)

An example of an ITTI coordinate already encountered in this report is
R_{n}
as seen in Sec.
3.4.2
. In App.
10
, we show that application of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{M}
conserves imaginary time translation invariance of functions, meaning that all ITTI properties of the instanton are stationary in time under the dynamics of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{M}
. With this property in mind, we define the coordinate transformation from the Fourier modes to the ‘angular modes’

\{\boldsymbol{P},\boldsymbol{Q}\}\to\{P,\boldsymbol{\Pi},\boldsymbol{L},Q,\boldsymbol{R},\boldsymbol{\Phi}\}\text{.}

(4.13)

Here the centroid variables are unchanged (
\{P_{0},Q_{0}\}\to\{P,Q\}
) and the fluctuation coordinates

\begin{split}R_{n}&=\sqrt{Q_{n}^{2}+Q_{\overline{n}}^{2}}\\
\Phi_{n}&=\sum_{k=1}^{\mu}T_{nk}\phi_{k}\text{,}\quad\quad\quad\quad\left[\phi_{n}=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\arctan\left(\frac{Q_{n}}{Q_{\overline{n}}}\right)\right]\end{split}

(4.14)

with
T_{nk}
as the
\mu\times\mu
discrete Fourier transform matrix as defined in Eq.(
3.22
). The conjugate angular and radial momenta to
\{\boldsymbol{\Phi},\boldsymbol{R}\}
are
\{\boldsymbol{L},\boldsymbol{\Pi}\}
.

It can be shown that every single angular coordinate and conjugate momentum (except
\Phi_{\!0}
, the totally symmetric linear combination of
\phi_{n}
’s) is ITTI and therefore is stationary in the instanton configuration. As
\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{M}
preserves ITTI symmetry, it maps all ITTI functions to those of the same symmetry. This means that the dynamics of coordinates of this symmetry are also all independent of
\Phi_{\!0}
.

The dynamics of
\Phi_{\!0}
can be understood by applying the Liouvillian

\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{M}\Phi_{\!0}=-i\sqrt{\mu}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\sum_{n=-\mu}^{\mu}\frac{L_{n}}{mR_{n}^{2}}\text{,}

(4.15)

where the conjugate momenta to
\phi_{n}

L_{n}=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\left(P_{n}Q_{\overline{n}}-P_{\overline{n}}Q_{n}\right)\text{.}

(4.16)

One can show that these are also ITTI, hence all further powers in
\left(\overline{\mathcal{L}}_{M}\right)^{n}\Phi_{\!0}(\boldsymbol{Q})
are also invariant, meaning that they are not a function of
\Phi_{\!0}
. Changing
\Phi_{\!0}
therefore has
no effect
on the dynamics of the other coordinates. The movement of
\Phi_{\!0}\leftarrow\Phi_{\!0}+\Delta
also has no effect even on its own dynamics, other than a translation by
\Delta
such that
\Phi_{\!0\,t}\leftarrow\Phi_{\!0\,t}+\Delta,\>\forall t
.

We conclude that the instanton is a fixed point in analytically continued Matsubara dynamics in all but its redundant coordinate
\Phi_{\!0}
when represented in angular coordinates. In the next section we use this result to approximate the late-time growth of Matsubara OTOCs.

4.3
Matsubara OTOCs at ‘fixed’ points

In angular coordinates, we define the Matsubara stability matrix as

\boldsymbol{M}_{\rm mats}(t):=\frac{\partial\>\{P_{t},\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{t},\boldsymbol{L}_{t},Q_{t},\boldsymbol{R}_{t},\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{t}\}}{\partial\>\{P,\boldsymbol{\Pi},\boldsymbol{L},Q,\boldsymbol{R},\boldsymbol{\Phi}\}}\text{.}

(4.17)

Using the complex Liouvillian, we construct the equations of motion of
\boldsymbol{M}_{\rm mats}
as

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\boldsymbol{M}_{\rm mats}(t)=\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm mats}(t)\boldsymbol{M}_{\rm mats}(t)\text{.}

(4.18)

As the instanton is stationary in all but the redundant coordinate, the propagation matrix in the stationary instanton configuration
\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}}_{\rm mats}
is again constant, allowing us to calculate the growth of the instanton stability matrix from its eigenvalues. It is important to note that if one were to make the same construction under Fourier mode coordinates, the propagation matrix would no longer be stationary in the instanton geometry, making the following analysis impossible. We demonstrate this construction and discuss the insights gained in Appendix
9
.

Due to the redundant nature of
\Phi_{\!0}
, as discussed in Sec.
4.2
, we know that
\widehat{\underline{\Phi_{\!0}}}
must be an eigenvector of
\boldsymbol{A}_{\rm mats}
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}=0
. We can therefore calculate the survival probability, just as in Sec.
3.1
, but now without constraining
\Phi_{\!0}
, as

\begin{split}P_{\rm mats}(t)&=\int{\rm d}|\overline{\boldsymbol{z}}|\prod_{i=1}^{M-1}S(|\overline{z}_{i}|)S(|\overline{z}_{i\,t}|)\text{,}\end{split}

(4.19)

where
\{\overline{z}\}
are the eigenvectors of
A
with
\widehat{\underline{\Phi_{\!0}}}
removed. The fluctuations about the saddle obey equations of motion
\overline{z}_{t}=\overline{z}e^{\overline{\lambda}_{i}t}
where
{\overline{\lambda}_{i}}
are the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}}
with
\lambda_{0}
removed. This gives

\begin{split}P_{\rm mats}(t)&=\int{\rm d}|\overline{\boldsymbol{z}}|\>\prod_{i=1}^{M-1}S(|\overline{z}_{i}|)S(|\overline{z}_{i}e^{\overline{\lambda}_{i}t}|)\\
&\sim\prod_{\Re{\lambda_i}>0}e^{-\lambda_{i}t}\text{,}\end{split}

(4.20)

meaning that the late-time growth of
C_{\rm mats}
is

\begin{split}C_{\rm mats}(t\to\infty)&\sim e^{2\max(\lambda)t}\prod_{\Re{\lambda_i}>0}e^{-\lambda_{i}t}\text{,}\end{split}

(4.21)

which has the same form as the expression as for the multidimensional classical case. In the next section, we construct
\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}}_{\rm mats}
for
M=3
(
\mu=1
).

4.5
Conclusion

Given that the 3-mode model is an extremely simplistic representation of an instanton, it is not appropriate at this point to draw any concrete conclusions about whether Matsubara OTOCs will adhere to the Maldacena bound. Instead it is intriguing to note the significant differences in dynamics between the two sets of results due to the complex Liouvillian, motivating further work on the model.

Chapter 5
Scattering OTOCs – ‘SOTOCs’

Throughout the analysis of the previous chapters it was found that quantum OTOCs very rarely grow exponentially in the case of low-dimensional systems. The fast onset of coherence due to interference is often blamed for this, as quantified by the ‘Ehrenfest time’
\tau_{\rm ehr}
. In this chapter we look at the growth of OTOCs in systems for which
\tau_{\rm ehr}
is infinite, specifically scattering systems. No comparable studies have been identified in existing literature, hence the methodology employed will also be a topic of discussion.

5.1
The model

For our unbounded system, we consider a one-dimensional scattering system with Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}+V(\hat{q})\text{,}

(5.1)

where the unperturbed Hamiltonian

\hat{H}_{0}=\frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2m}\text{,}

(5.2)

and

V(\hat{q})=\begin{cases}0&\quad\left|q\right|>\frac{L}{2}\>\>\left(=\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{8g}}\right)\\
g\hat{q}^{4}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}\hat{q}^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{4}}{64g}&\quad\left|q\right|\leq\frac{L}{2}\quad{\rm.}\\
\end{cases}

(5.3)

A plot of this potential energy surface is shown in the right panel of Fig.
5.4
(red), with a superimposed plot of the symmetric double well from which it is derived (dotted blue). We use
\lambda=2
and
g=0.08
throughout the chapter. From now on we refer to
V(q)
of Eq.(
5.3
) as the ‘scattering potential’ and the double well potential as the ‘bounded potential’.

Since the potential vanishes for
|q|>L/2
, the eigenstates in these regions must be linear combinations of the free particle wavefunction. We therefore use the Numerov method
[
24
]
to numerically calculate the wavefunctions within the box, taking the free particle wavefunctions as boundary conditions. This matching to these asymptotic solutions is referred to as using ‘scattering boundary conditions’ in literature. This generates
\{\bra{x}\ket{\psi_p}\}
such that:

\hat{H}\ket{\psi_p}=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}\ket{\psi_p}\text{.}\quad\quad\forall p\in\mathbb{R}

(5.4)

As there exist no bound states in the scattering system, the scattering states of Eq.(
5.4
) forms a complete, orthogonal set
[
22
]
.

5.2
Problems with matrix element calculation

Due to the infinite extent of the scattering states, it is impossible to calculate matrix elements by ‘brute force’ integration as was possible over a finite DVR grid for bounded systems (outlined in App.
7.2
).
The matrix elements are singular, as shown by this example:

Consider calculation of the matrix element
\bra{\psi_p}\hat{p}\ket{\psi_p}
. This integral can be separated in
x
as

\begin{split}\bra{\psi_p}\hat{p}\ket{\psi_p}=-i\hbar\left(\int\limits_{\>\>|x|\leq L/2}\!\!\!\!\!\!{\rm d}x\>\phi_{p}^{*}(x)\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\phi_{p}(x)\>\>+\!\!\int\limits_{\>\>\>|x|>L/2}\!\!\!\!\!\!{\rm d}x\>\phi_{p}^{*}(x)\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}x}\phi_{p}(x)\right)\text{,}\end{split}

(5.5)

where we have defined

\phi_{p}(x)=\bra{x}\ket{\psi_p}\text{.}

(5.6)

The first integral of the two is finite and is much like that of the integral over a finite DVR grid. The second integral however diverges, since

\phi_{p}(x>L/2)\sim e^{ipx/\hbar}\text{.}

(5.7)

This is not surprising as plane wave states are not normalisable.

5.3
Using wavepackets to calculate SOTOCs

The form of
\hat{H}_{0}
allows us to calculate OTOCs analytically in regions of space for which
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}
. For example,

\begin{split}[\hat{q}_{t},\hat{p}]&=e^{it\hat{H}/\hbar}[\hat{q},\hat{p}]e^{-it\hat{H}/\hbar}\quad\quad[\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}]\\
&=i\hbar\text{,}\end{split}

(5.8)

therefore for all possible regularizations, the quantum OTOC

\begin{split}\left<[\hat{q}_{t},\hat{p}]^{2}\right>&=\hbar^{2}\text{.}\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad[\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}]\end{split}

(5.9)

This means that if we construct a basis localised in
q
, we can trace over the basis functions that contribute to the growth of the OTOC (in the vicinity of the barrier) and leave out those that do not.

The position eigenstates at
q
in the position representation are

\bra{x}\ket{q}=\delta(x-q)\text{,}

(5.10)

and in the momentum representation is

\bra{p}\ket{q}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}e^{-\frac{ip}{\hbar}q}\text{,}

(5.11)

giving a uniform distribution in
p
(as expected from the Heisenberg uncertainty principle). The infinite range of momenta means any real time evolution on the initial states will result in a spread of the wavefunction throughout all space.

Fortunately, we are interested in thermal OTOCs, for which the Boltzmann operator damps contributions of high momentum states in the basis. We can calculate this damping of Eq.(
5.10
) analytically for
|x|>L/2
where
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}
,

\bra{x}e^{-\frac{\lambda\hat{H}_{0}}{2}}\ket{q}=\frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}\sqrt{\frac{4m\pi}{\lambda}}e^{\>-\frac{m}{\lambda}\left(\frac{x-q}{\hbar}\right)^{2}}\text{,}

(5.12)

which can be transformed into the momentum representation as

\bra{p}e^{-\frac{\lambda\hat{H}_{0}}{2}}\ket{q}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}e^{\>-\frac{ip}{\hbar}q-\frac{\lambda p^{2}}{4m}}\text{,}

(5.13)

showing that the Boltzmann operator gives an exponential cutoff in momentum, giving initial states far from the barrier a finite ‘range’. We show an example of this in Fig.
5.2
where the evolved wavepackets are unable to reach the barrier in the time
t
. This makes possible the setup of an OTOC calculation in which we only include the states that interact with the barrier.

We therefore calculate the thermal ‘scattering OTOC’ (SOTOC) as

\begin{split}S_{\lambda}(t)&=\frac{1}{Z_{s}}\int_{q\in X}{\rm d}q\>\bra{q}e^{-\frac{\lambda\hat{H}}{2}}[\hat{q}_{t},\hat{p}]^{\dagger}e^{-(\beta-\lambda)\hat{H}}[\hat{q}_{t},\hat{p}]e^{-\frac{\lambda\hat{H}}{2}}\ket{q}\text{.}\end{split}

(5.14)

The interval
X
defines a partial trace over the position basis such that all contributions outside of it do not involve wavepackets hitting the barrier in the time interval of interest
[0,t_{\rm max}]
. We similarly define the partition function as

Z_{s}:=\int_{q\in X}{\rm d}q\bra{q}e^{-\beta\hat{H}}\ket{q}\text{.}

(5.15)

The SOTOC is calculated using wavepackets as

\begin{split}S_{\lambda}(t)&=\frac{1}{Z_{s}}\int_{q\in X}{\rm d}q\>\left[\bra{L_{\lambda q}(t)}\ket{L_{\lambda q}(t)}+\bra{R_{\lambda q}(t)}\ket{R_{\lambda q}(t)}\right.\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-\left.2{\rm Re}\left[\bra{R_{\lambda q}(t)}\ket{L_{\lambda q}(t)}\right]\>\right]\end{split}

(5.16)

where the evolved wavepackets

\ket{L_{\lambda q}(t)}:=e^{-\frac{\beta-\lambda}{2}\hat{H}}\hat{p}\>e^{\frac{it}{\hbar}\hat{H}}\>\hat{q}\>e^{-\frac{it}{\hbar}\hat{H}}e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}\hat{H}}\ket{q}

(5.17)

and

\ket{R_{\lambda q}(t)}:=e^{-\frac{\beta-\lambda}{2}\hat{H}}e^{\frac{it}{\hbar}\hat{H}}\>\hat{q}\>e^{-\frac{it}{\hbar}\hat{H}}\>\hat{p}\>e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}\hat{H}}\ket{q}\text{.}

(5.18)

Because
e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}\hat{H}}\ket{q}
is localised in both position and momentum, the evolved wavepackets can be propagated numerically, without the need for an infinite grid. In App.
11
we give details of this procedure.

From Eq.(
5.14
), we calculate the standard and symmetrised SOTOCs with
S_{\beta}
and
S_{\beta/2}
respectively. By noting that
S_{\lambda}(t)=S_{\beta-\lambda}(t)
, we obtain the Kubo-regularised SOTOC as

\begin{split}S_{\rm kubo}(t)&=\frac{2}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\beta/2}{\rm d}\lambda\>S_{\beta-\lambda}(t)\text{.}\end{split}

(5.19)

5.4
Discretizing bases

Eq.(
5.14
) involves an infinite, continuous range of states that theoretically must to be integrated over. In this section, we present the necessary approximations required to make basis discrete, allowing integration over a finite grid making the method computationally feasible.

We first discretize the eigenbasis
\{\ket{\psi_p}\}
by adopting a discrete spectrum spaced by
\delta_{p}
and introducing a maximum momentum cutoff
p_{\rm max}=N_{p}\delta_{p}
,

\begin{split}\bra{x}\hat{\mathds{1}}\ket{x'}&=\int{\rm d}p\bra{x}\ket{\psi_p}\bra{\psi_p}\ket{x'}\\
&\simeq\sum_{n=-N_{p}}^{N_{p}}\phi_{n\smash{\delta_{p}}}(x)\,\phi_{n\smash{\delta_{p}}}^{*}(x^{\prime})\delta_{p}\text{.}\end{split}

(5.20)

To discretise
\{\ket{q}\}
, the localised basis over which the SOTOC is integrated, we use

5.6
Conclusion

In the OTOCs of scattering systems investigated, there is a noticeable decrease in the exponential growth as time progresses. As the Ehrenfest time is infinite in these systems, this flattening must result from some other interference effect in dynamics, which we suspect may be due to anharmonicities in the scattering potential. The high-temperature correspondence of Fig.
5.4
verifies the results of the SOTOC, and suggests that the reason for the meagre exponential growth of the thermal SOTOC in Fig.
5.3
is due to the imaginary time propagation of the Boltzmann operator. However, it should be emphasised that the conclusions drawn here are somewhat unclear, and require further research to be verified.

Chapter 6
Conclusions and further work

In this report, we aimed to address two main questions. The first was to understand the influence of coherence on the flattening of quantum OTOCs. We compared the OTOCs of bounded and unbounded systems, outlining a methodology to calculate SOTOCs, thermal OTOCs of scattering systems specifically focusing on the contribution of wavepackets interacting with the potential barrier. We found that the exponential growth rate of the SOTOC was significantly lower than that of the analogous bounded system, which, following comparison with the results of the same systems at infinite temperature, we concluded was due to the action of the Boltzmann operator. For the OTOCs of unbounded systems we also observed a decrease in growth rate as time progressed, which we expect is consequence of the highly anharmonic scattering barrier, but require further research for verification. Though these results indicate that the Ehrenfest time is not solely responsible for the flattening of OTOCs, the precise mechanism still remains unclear, and will require further investigation to be understood.

The second question addressed the relationship between the Maldacena bound and the instanton, and whether RPMD is sufficient to satisfy the bound. The results of Chapter
3
revealed that for systems lacking instantons RPMD produced OTOCs that, despite growing at a significantly lower rate than in the classical case, may still fail to satisfy the bound. In addition to this it was shown that some asymmetric systems for which instantons do form may also fail to satisfy the bound, even in the short-time limit when thermal quenching of the OTOC growth is significant. These results together provide overwhelming evidence that RPMD is not guaranteed to satisfy the Maldacena bound: though the bound may be of quantum statistical origin, conservation of the quantum Boltzmann distribution is not sufficient for it to be obeyed.

These results suggested that a more sophisticated approach with accurate dynamics, Matsubara dynamics, would be necessary to understand how the bound is enforced. In Chapter
4
we outlined an approach for the calculation of Matsubara OTOCs, by linearising the analytically continued Matsubara dynamics about the instanton. In considering the dynamics of the instanton itself, we uncovered that, unlike in RPMD, the instanton is not a fixed point, but instead is stationary in all coordinates but the collective angular coordinate
\Phi_{0}
.
The minimal working example we tested for Matsubara OTOCs revealed dynamics that were markedly different to those of RPMD, showing a new perspective on the instanton that has not been seen before in literature. The theory presented in Chapter
4
enables the developments of more complicated (
M>3
) Matsubara OTOC models, which we believe could shed light on the origin of the Maldacena bound in barrier scrambling systems and may also provide insights for the general case.

Just as the instanton governs the growth of OTOCs in barrier scrambling systems, it also controls the rate of quantum tunneling over barriers
[
31
]
, and is therefore the backbone of several reaction rate theories. This suggests that the further insight gained in this report regarding the nature of the instanton and dynamics of fluctuations about it could help inspire the construction of new rate theories, and aid further understanding of existing ones, providing a promising direction for further research.
