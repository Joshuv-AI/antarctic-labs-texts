Artificial intelligence in emergency medicine: from algorithmic performance to real-world clinical integration — understanding the implementation gap

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Artificial intelligence (AI)–driven clinical decision support systems (CDSS) promise to enhance diagnostic accuracy, risk stratification, and workflow efficiency. Background

Emergency medicine operates in an environment characterized by urgency, diagnostic uncertainty, and limited resources, where clinicians must rapidly identify high-risk patients and make critical decisions with incomplete information. Conventional clinical prediction tools, such as the HEART and TIMI risk scores, provide structured guidance based on predefined variables but are inherently static and unable to adapt continuously to evolving patient conditions or complex nonlinear relationships among clinical parameters. In contrast, artificial intelligence (AI)-driven clinical decision support systems (AI-CDSS) integrate electronic health records, physiological signals, laboratory data, and medical imaging to generate dynamic, real-time predictions. By recognizing complex patterns beyond conventional statistical approaches, AI has demonstrated considerable potential to improve diagnostic accuracy, facilitate earlier detection of clinical deterioration, enhance risk stratification, and optimize emergency department (ED) workflow [1–4].

The application of AI in emergency medicine has expanded rapidly over the past decade, encompassing AI-assisted triage, sepsis prediction, stroke detection, electrocardiogram interpretation, medical imaging analysis, pulmonary embolism detection, and operational forecasting within emergency departments. Many of these models report excellent retrospective performance, frequently achieving Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC) values between 0.85 and 0.95 while outperforming traditional scoring systems in controlled datasets [5, 6]. Despite these promising findings, only a limited number of AI systems have been successfully integrated into routine emergency care, highlighting a persistent gap between algorithm development and real-world clinical implementation [5, 6].

A major reason for this implementation gap is the discrepancy between retrospective algorithmic performance and prospective clinical effectiveness. AI models often demonstrate strong internal and external validation using historical datasets, yet their performance may decline after deployment because of differences in patient populations, disease prevalence, institutional workflows, documentation practices, and clinician behavior [5–7]. Consequently, high discrimination metrics alone cannot be considered sufficient evidence for widespread clinical adoption. Increasingly, methodological guidance emphasizes that retrospective validation should represent only an early stage of evidence generation, with prospective clinical evaluation required to establish safety, usability, workflow integration, and real-world effectiveness before large-scale implementation [8].

Current AI research also tends to prioritize statistical performance metrics such as AUROC, sensitivity, and specificity while comparatively underreporting calibration, external validation, subgroup equity, workflow impact, clinician acceptance, and patient-centered outcomes. Although high predictive accuracy is essential, improvements in discrimination do not necessarily translate into reductions in mortality, diagnostic delay, hospital length of stay, or healthcare costs. Contemporary perspectives therefore advocate that AI should ultimately be evaluated according to its ability to improve meaningful clinical outcomes rather than computational performance alone [9–11].

Beyond methodological limitations, successful implementation of AI in emergency medicine remains challenged by algorithmic bias, dataset shift, limited explainability, regulatory uncertainty, data governance, cybersecurity concerns, workflow disruption, and resource constraints. Addressing these barriers requires transparent reporting, continuous post-deployment monitoring, equitable model performance, and close collaboration between clinicians, informaticians, healthcare organizations, and AI developers [6–16].

This narrative review synthesizes current evidence on AI-based clinical decision support systems in emergency medicine by evaluating major clinical applications, critically examining diagnostic performance beyond conventional accuracy metrics, analyzing implementation barriers that limit translation from algorithm to bedside, and proposing practical strategies for human–AI collaboration. By emphasizing patient-centered outcomes and implementation science rather than algorithmic performance alone, this review aims to provide a clinically relevant framework for the safe, effective, and sustainable integration of AI into emergency care. Methods

This narrative review was conducted in accordance with the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA), emphasizing a clearly defined objective, transparent methodology, and balanced critical synthesis of the available literature. The review was designed to evaluate the clinical applicability, implementation feasibility, and translational impact of artificial intelligence (AI)–based clinical decision support systems (AI-CDSS) in emergency medicine rather than to perform quantitative evidence pooling.

A structured literature search was performed in PubMed, Scopus, and Embase for English-language publications published between January 2015 and December 2025. Search terms included combinations of artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning, emergency medicine, emergency department, clinical decision support systems, triage, sepsis prediction, stroke detection, electrocardiogram interpretation, medical imaging, workflow integration, implementation science, and implementation barriers. Boolean operators were applied to optimize search sensitivity and specificity. To capture recent methodological, regulatory, and implementation perspectives, relevant guidance documents and consensus statements from major health organizations were also reviewed.

Eligible studies included peer-reviewed investigations reporting retrospective or prospective validation of AI applications in emergency department settings, studies evaluating diagnostic, prognostic, or operational AI systems, implementation analyses, and landmark methodological publications with direct clinical relevance. Studies limited to technical algorithm development without clinical application, conference abstracts without full-text publication, opinion articles lacking empirical evidence, and non-validated preprints were excluded.

Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full-text articles for eligibility, with disagreements resolved through consensus. A total of 122 studies met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the final synthesis. Extracted information included study design, study population, AI application, reference standard, validation methodology, performance metrics (including discrimination and calibration where available), implementation characteristics, and reported clinical or workflow outcomes. Because of substantial heterogeneity in study design, AI methodologies, outcome measures, and validation strategies, quantitative meta-analysis was not considered appropriate. Instead, findings were synthesized narratively, integrating evidence on diagnostic performance with implementation science principles to provide a clinically relevant assessment of AI deployment in emergency medicine.

AI applications in emergency medicine

Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a promising tool for addressing diagnostic uncertainty, time-sensitive decision-making, and increasing operational demands within emergency departments (EDs) [1–12]. Most AI applications function as clinical decision support systems integrated into electronic health records (EHRs), medical imaging platforms, bedside monitoring systems, or operational management tools [2–12]. Rather than replacing clinical judgment, these systems are designed to assist clinicians by facilitating earlier risk identification, improving diagnostic accuracy, prioritizing high-acuity patients, and optimizing workflow efficiency [1–12, 17, 18]. Although numerous AI models have demonstrated encouraging diagnostic performance during development and retrospective validation, their translation into routine clinical practice has been considerably more variable [5, 6, 10, 11, 14, 17]. Consequently, evaluating AI applications requires consideration of both algorithmic performance and real-world implementation [5, 6, 10, 11, 14, 17]. Major AI applications in emergency medicine and reported clinical impact

AI-assisted triage and risk stratification

Early identification of patients at risk of clinical deterioration remains one of the primary objectives of emergency department triage [19–22]. AI-assisted triage systems utilize demographic information, vital signs, laboratory findings, previous medical history, and continuously updated electronic health record data to generate dynamic risk estimates for patient deterioration [19–22]. Unlike conventional rule-based triage systems, machine learning algorithms continuously update predictions as new clinical information becomes available, allowing individualized and adaptive risk assessment [2, 6, 17, 18]. These models have also been applied to predict emergency department crowding, hospital admission, resource utilization, and patient prioritization [19, 20].

One of the principal advantages of AI-assisted triage is the earlier identification of patients whose initial clinical presentation may appear stable despite an increased risk of deterioration [19, 21, 22]. Several retrospective studies have demonstrated improved discrimination compared with traditional triage systems [19, 21, 22]. However, improved sensitivity may also increase false-positive alerts, resulting in overtriage, unnecessary investigations, alert fatigue, and additional cognitive burden for clinicians [6, 12, 17]. Consequently, successful implementation requires careful calibration of alert thresholds to balance early detection with acceptable specificity [6, 11, 17].

Among prospective implementations, the Triage-GO system represents one of the largest real-world applications of AI-supported emergency department triage [23]. Similar findings have been reported for AI-based deterioration prediction systems evaluated in hospitalized patients, demonstrating improved early detection and clinical response [24]. The study reported improvements in patient prioritization and several workflow-related outcomes following implementation [23]. Nevertheless, because the evaluation employed a pre–post observational design rather than randomized allocation, the reported improvements may have been influenced by temporal confounding, workflow modifications, staffing changes, or other operational factors unrelated to the AI intervention itself [11, 23]. Therefore, although Triage-GO provides encouraging evidence for clinical implementation, additional multicenter randomized studies remain necessary to establish definitive causal benefits on patient-centered outcomes [10, 11, 23].

Sepsis and early warning systems

Sepsis prediction represents one of the most extensively investigated applications of AI in emergency medicine [7–28]. Machine learning models typically analyze continuously updated electronic health records, laboratory trends, and physiological monitoring data to identify patients at risk of developing sepsis before conventional clinical recognition [22, 25–28]. Numerous retrospective studies have reported earlier identification of sepsis and superior discriminative performance compared with traditional rule-based screening systems [22, 25–28].

Despite these encouraging findings, evidence supporting improved clinical outcomes following implementation remains limited [7, 10, 11, 17]. Most published studies involve retrospective validation or single-center cohorts, whereas randomized implementation trials demonstrating reductions in mortality, intensive care unit admission, or hospital length of stay remain relatively uncommon [7, 10, 11, 17]. Furthermore, excessive false-positive alerts may contribute to unnecessary antibiotic administration, increased healthcare utilization, and antimicrobial stewardship concerns, highlighting the need to balance early detection with clinical specificity [6, 7, 12].

The Targeted Real-Time Early Warning System (TREWS) illustrates both the promise and limitations of AI-assisted sepsis detection [25, 29]. Although TREWS demonstrated successful prospective deployment across multiple hospitals and was associated with earlier clinician recognition and treatment of sepsis, its evaluation was observational rather than randomized [29]. Consequently, physician judgment, unmeasured clinical decision-making, and selection bias may have influenced the reported outcomes, making it difficult to determine the independent contribution of the AI system [11, 29]. These findings reinforce the need for prospective randomized implementation studies capable of demonstrating meaningful improvements in patient-centered outcomes [10, 11, 29].

Stroke detection and neuroimaging

Stroke detection represents one of the most mature applications of AI in emergency medicine [30–32]. Automated computed tomography (CT) interpretation systems rapidly identify intracranial hemorrhage, large-vessel occlusion, and early ischemic changes, thereby facilitating timely diagnosis and treatment [30–32]. Several studies have demonstrated reductions in imaging interpretation time, improved radiology workflow, and shorter door-to-needle or door-to-intervention intervals following AI implementation [30–32].

Although these systems have demonstrated encouraging performance in both retrospective and prospective settings, diagnostic accuracy may vary across institutions because of differences in imaging protocols, scanner characteristics, disease prevalence, and patient populations [30–34]. Consequently, continuous quality monitoring, external validation, and periodic recalibration remain essential to maintain reliable performance across diverse clinical environments [6, 35, 36].

ECG and cardiac decision support

Artificial intelligence–enabled electrocardiogram (AI-ECG) systems have demonstrated considerable promise for detecting arrhythmias, acute coronary syndromes, atrial fibrillation, and occult structural heart disease through automated analysis of ECG waveforms [9, 37–39]. Several deep-learning models have achieved diagnostic performance comparable with or exceeding that of junior clinicians when evaluated using retrospective datasets [37–39].

However, performance comparisons consistently demonstrate that AI systems do not uniformly outperform experienced cardiologists or emergency physicians [5, 9, 10]. Consequently, AI should function as an adjunct to clinical decision-making rather than a replacement for physician judgment [1, 9, 10]. Successful implementation depends on clinician oversight, transparent reporting, and appropriate interpretation of AI-generated recommendations [6, 9, 10, 17].

The implementation gap is particularly evident in AI-assisted detection of non–ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). During retrospective external validation, the DAMI-ECG model achieved excellent diagnostic discrimination, with an AUROC of 0.915 [40]. However, prospective evaluation across multiple emergency departments in the ROMIAE study demonstrated a decline in performance, with an AUROC of 0.814 [41]. This reduction likely reflects the fundamental difference between AI and routine emergency practice. AI-ECG algorithms evaluate only electrical waveforms, whereas emergency physicians integrate ECG findings with clinical history, physical examination, laboratory investigations, imaging results, and bedside assessment [9, 10]. Notably, prospective evaluation demonstrated no clinically significant superiority of AI over the widely used multimodal HEART score, illustrating that excellent retrospective algorithmic performance does not necessarily translate into improved real-world clinical effectiveness [11, 41, 42].

Imaging-based AI systems

Beyond stroke detection, AI has been applied extensively to identify intracranial hemorrhage, pulmonary embolism, pneumonia, and other radiological abnormalities [33– [32, 43, 44]. These systems aim to reduce missed diagnoses, accelerate radiology triage, and improve workflow efficiency within emergency departments [30–33, 44]. Internal validation studies frequently report excellent discrimination; however, diagnostic performance commonly declines during external validation because of dataset shift, variations in disease prevalence, imaging protocols, and differences between institutions [5, 6, 33, 35].

An important methodological limitation of many imaging studies is the widespread use of reader-study designs. In these evaluations, physicians interpret isolated medical images without access to clinical history, laboratory findings, physical examination, or other contextual information [5, 43]. The widely cited CheXNet study, for example, compared AI performance with physicians interpreting chest radiographs alone [43]. Although this methodology standardizes image interpretation, it does not accurately reflect routine emergency medicine practice, where diagnoses are inherently multimodal and integrate clinical presentation with imaging findings [5, 10]. Consequently, reader-study designs may overestimate the apparent superiority of AI compared with real-world emergency physicians [5, 11, 45, 46]. Continued multicenter validation, prospective implementation studies, and ongoing performance monitoring remain essential to ensure that imaging AI systems provide sustainable clinical benefit across diverse healthcare settings [11, 35, 36].

Diagnostic performance: beyond AUROC

The Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC) is among the most frequently reported metrics for evaluating AI-based clinical decision support systems because it reflects a model’s ability to discriminate between patients with and without a given outcome [5, 35, 36, 47]. However, excellent discrimination alone does not guarantee clinical usefulness [10, 11]. A model with a high AUROC may still demonstrate poor calibration, limited generalizability, or inadequate clinical utility when deployed in routine emergency care [6, 11, 35, 36]. Consequently, model evaluation should extend beyond statistical accuracy to determine whether predicted probabilities correspond to actual patient outcomes and whether AI-assisted decisions improve clinical management [11, 35, 36, 47].

Although many AI models report excellent retrospective performance, discrimination metrics alone cannot determine whether implementation will reduce mortality, shorten hospital length of stay, accelerate diagnosis, improve patient safety, or optimize resource utilization [5, 10, 11, 14, 17]. Similarly, statistically significant improvements in predictive performance do not necessarily translate into meaningful improvements in patient-centered outcomes [10, 11]. Operational consequences—including alert fatigue, unnecessary downstream investigations, workflow disruption, increased cognitive burden, and resource consumption—are frequently underreported despite their substantial influence on successful implementation [6, 11, 12, 14, 17].

An additional limitation of current AI evaluation is that many algorithms are compared against isolated computational metrics or single-modality physician assessments rather than routine multimodal clinical practice [5, 11–46]. Emergency physicians integrate patient history, physical examination, laboratory investigations, imaging findings, physiological monitoring, and clinical judgment when making diagnostic decisions [9, 10]. In contrast, many AI systems analyze only a single data modality, such as an electrocardiogram or medical image [37–44]. Consequently, retrospective comparisons may overestimate AI performance while underrepresenting the complexity of real-world clinical decision-making [5, 11–46].

Increasingly, experts advocate that AI should be evaluated according to its impact on patient-centered outcomes rather than algorithmic performance alone [10, 11]. Prospective implementation studies and randomized controlled trials comparing AI-assisted care with current standards of clinical practice provide substantially stronger evidence than retrospective validation studies [8, 23, 29, 48, 49]. Future evaluations should therefore incorporate calibration, external validation, subgroup fairness, workflow integration, clinician acceptance, cost-effectiveness, and clinically meaningful outcomes, including mortality, diagnostic timeliness, length of stay, and healthcare resource utilization [13], 48– [8, 11]. Ultimately, the objective of AI in emergency medicine is not to maximize AUROC, but to produce measurable improvements in patient care while supporting safe, equitable, and effective clinical decision-making [1, 6, 10, 12, 15, 16].

Implementation challenges

Despite encouraging advances in diagnostic performance, translating artificial intelligence (AI) into routine emergency department (ED) practice remains challenging [5, 6, 10, 11, 14, 17]. Successful implementation depends not only on algorithmic accuracy but also on ethical, operational, organizational, regulatory, and economic factors that influence clinical adoption [6, 12, 14–49], 16– [8]. Consequently, many implementation barriers arise from healthcare systems rather than the AI models themselves [6, 10, 14, 17]. Implementation barriers and system-level considerations

Algorithmic bias

Algorithmic bias occurs when AI models perform unequally across different patient populations because training datasets inadequately represent demographic, geographic, or clinical diversity [13, 16, 35]. Such bias may result in reduced diagnostic accuracy among underrepresented populations and contribute to healthcare disparities [13, 15, 16]. Importantly, statistical significance or excellent predictive performance does not necessarily translate into equitable or clinically meaningful outcomes [10, 11]. Even highly accurate models may increase false-positive alerts, unnecessary investigations, workflow disruption, or clinician burden if implementation challenges are overlooked [6, 11, 12, 17]. Fairness assessment, subgroup validation, and continuous monitoring across diverse patient populations should therefore become integral components of AI evaluation before and after deployment [13], 48– [8, 16, 35].

Generalizability and dataset shift

AI models frequently demonstrate reduced performance when deployed outside the institutions in which they were developed [5, 6, 35, 36]. Differences in patient demographics, disease prevalence, documentation practices, imaging protocols, and clinical workflows contribute to reduced external validity [6, 35, 36]. Furthermore, model performance may deteriorate over time because of evolving treatment guidelines, seasonal disease patterns, and changing healthcare practices—a phenomenon commonly referred to as dataset shift or model drift [6, 14, 17]. Unlike traditional clinical scoring systems, machine learning models require continuous performance surveillance, periodic recalibration, and external validation to maintain reliable performance throughout their clinical lifecycle [48– [8, 36, 47].

Workflow integration

Emergency departments operate under substantial time pressure, making workflow integration a critical determinant of AI adoption [6, 14, 17]. Systems requiring additional documentation, frequent interface changes, manual data entry, or repeated overrides are unlikely to achieve sustained clinician acceptance regardless of predictive performance [6, 14, 17]. Similarly, excessive notifications may contribute to alert fatigue, reducing clinician responsiveness to both AI-generated and conventional clinical alerts [6, 12, 17]. Successful implementation therefore requires seamless integration within existing electronic health record systems, minimal disruption of established workflows, intuitive user interfaces, and alignment with routine clinical decision-making processes [8, 14–49].

Explainability versus actionability

Many high-performing AI systems rely on complex deep-learning architectures that function as “black-box” models, limiting transparency regarding how predictions are generated [4, 45, 46]. Limited explainability may reduce clinician confidence, particularly in emergency medicine, where diagnostic decisions must often be justified rapidly and under significant clinical uncertainty [6, 45, 46]. Recent advances in explainable AI attempt to identify features contributing to model predictions; however, standardized methods for presenting these explanations remain limited [45, 46].

Importantly, explainability should not be considered synonymous with clinical actionability [11–46]. A prediction may be statistically accurate and partially interpretable while still failing to provide information that changes patient management [10–46]. Conversely, an AI system with limited interpretability may still support clinical decision-making if it consistently improves meaningful patient outcomes [10, 11]. Consequently, future AI evaluation should assess not only whether clinicians understand model predictions but also whether those predictions facilitate timely, appropriate, and effective clinical interventions [8, 10–46, 48, 49].

Regulatory and medico-legal uncertainty

The rapid evolution of AI has introduced important legal and regulatory challenges regarding accountability for AI-assisted clinical decisions [15, 16, 48, 49]. Questions remain regarding responsibility when recommendations conflict with physician judgment or when adverse outcomes occur following AI-supported care [15, 16]. Regulatory approval should therefore be regarded as the beginning rather than the endpoint of clinical oversight [8, 16, 48, 49]. Adaptive AI systems require ongoing post-market surveillance, performance auditing, transparent governance structures, and clearly defined accountability among developers, healthcare organizations, regulators, and clinicians to ensure continued safety after deployment [8, 16, 48, 49].

Economic and resource constraints

Financial and infrastructural limitations represent major barriers to AI implementation, particularly in resource-constrained healthcare settings [6, 14, 16, 17]. Deployment frequently requires software licensing, computing infrastructure, cybersecurity protection, interoperability with electronic health records, technical maintenance, and dedicated clinical informatics expertise [14–17]. These costs may exceed the capabilities of many emergency departments, especially in low- and middle-income countries [16, 17]. Without scalable implementation strategies and equitable access to digital infrastructure, widespread adoption of AI risks further widening existing disparities in emergency healthcare delivery [13, 16, 17].

Data governance and cybersecurity

AI systems depend on continuous access to large volumes of patient data, making robust data governance and cybersecurity essential for safe implementation [15, 16]. Healthcare organizations must protect patient confidentiality through secure data storage, encryption, audit trails, controlled access, and clearly defined data stewardship policies [15, 16]. Additional challenges include interoperability between heterogeneous health information systems, proprietary algorithms that limit transparency, and increasing cybersecurity threats targeting healthcare infrastructure [6, 14–16]. Strong governance frameworks should therefore extend beyond regulatory compliance to include continuous monitoring of data quality, model performance, privacy protection, and cybersecurity resilience throughout the AI lifecycle [8, 15, 16, 48, 49].

Human–AI collaboration model

Emerging evidence suggests that the greatest value of artificial intelligence (AI) in emergency medicine lies in augmenting rather than replacing clinician expertise (Fig. 3) [1, 5–12, 17]. Emergency care requires rapid decision-making under conditions of diagnostic uncertainty, incomplete information, competing priorities, and ethical complexity. Although AI systems can rapidly analyze large volumes of clinical data and generate highly accurate risk estimates, they lack contextual understanding, situational awareness, ethical reasoning, and the experiential judgment that characterize expert clinical practice [1, 5, 6, 9, 10, 45, 46]. Consequently, AI should be regarded as a clinical decision support tool rather than an autonomous decision-maker [1, 2, 6, 9, 10, 17].

The primary role of AI is to enhance clinicians’ ability to recognize patients at increased risk of deterioration, identify diagnostic patterns that may otherwise be overlooked, and support operational decision-making within busy emergency departments [9, 19, 21, 22, 37–39]. Instead of providing prescriptive recommendations, AI systems should generate probabilistic risk estimates or alerts that assist clinicians in prioritizing patient assessment and resource allocation [6, 10, 11, 14, 17]. Final clinical decisions should remain based on integration of AI outputs with patient history, physical examination, laboratory findings, imaging studies, bedside assessment, and clinician experience [9–11]. In this collaborative model, computational efficiency complements—rather than replaces—human clinical reasoning [1, 9, 10].

Effective human–AI collaboration also requires structured governance mechanisms [8, 16, 48, 49]. When AI recommendations differ from clinician judgment, discrepancies should undergo systematic review to determine whether disagreement reflects model limitations, data quality issues, or opportunities for improving clinical practice [8, 16, 45, 46, 48]. Such feedback mechanisms promote clinician trust while reducing both excessive reliance on AI (automation bias) and inappropriate dismissal of accurate AI recommendations (algorithm aversion) [6, 17, 45, 46]. Maintaining meaningful human oversight remains essential for ensuring patient safety, professional accountability, and ethical decision-making [6, 9, 10, 16, 48, 49].

Successful implementation further depends on continuous post-deployment monitoring [8, 14–49]. AI performance should be routinely evaluated for calibration, discrimination, fairness across patient populations, dataset shift, and impact on clinically meaningful outcomes [13], 48– [8, 11, 35, 36]. Governance frameworks should clearly define responsibilities for healthcare organizations, clinicians, informatics specialists, and AI developers while supporting periodic model recalibration, bias auditing, cybersecurity oversight, and transparent reporting of performance [8, 15, 16, 48, 49]. Continuous feedback between frontline clinicians and development teams enables iterative refinement of AI systems while preserving clinician autonomy and accountability [8, 14–49].

Proposed collaborative AI integration model

This review proposes a three-pillar framework for the safe integration of AI into emergency medicine:

Risk amplification

AI continuously analyzes multimodal clinical data to identify patients at increased risk of deterioration, prioritize high-acuity cases, and enhance early recognition of time-sensitive conditions [9, 19, 21, 22, 37–39]. Its primary function is to amplify risk detection rather than replace diagnostic authority [1, 9, 10].

Contextual interpretation

Clinicians retain ultimate responsibility for decision-making by integrating AI-generated predictions with clinical history, physical examination, laboratory investigations, imaging findings, patient preferences, and professional judgment [9–11]. AI informs—but never dictates—clinical action [1, 6, 9, 10].

Continuous audit and learning

Healthcare organizations should establish ongoing monitoring of model performance, calibration, fairness, workflow impact, patient-centered outcomes, and cybersecurity [13], 48– [8, 11, 15, 16, 35, 36]. Regular auditing, governance, and model recalibration ensure that AI systems remain accurate, equitable, transparent, and clinically relevant throughout their operational lifecycle [8, 15, 16, 48, 49].

Together, these three pillars establish a collaborative framework in which AI enhances diagnostic precision, supports clinical decision-making, and improves emergency care while preserving human judgment, ethical responsibility, and system-level accountability [1, 6, 9, 10, 16, 48, 49].

Future directions

Future research in emergency medicine should move beyond demonstrating high retrospective diagnostic accuracy toward establishing whether artificial intelligence (AI) improves meaningful patient outcomes in routine clinical practice [10, 11]. While retrospective validation remains an essential step in algorithm development, implementation science should become the primary focus of future investigations [10, 11, 14, 17]. Prospective multicenter studies conducted across diverse healthcare environments—including urban, rural, academic, and resource-limited emergency departments—are needed to evaluate the generalizability, safety, and real-world effectiveness of AI-assisted clinical decision support systems [8, 23, 29, 36, 48, 49]. Outcome measures should extend beyond diagnostic discrimination to include mortality, time to treatment, length of hospital stay, intensive care unit admission, healthcare costs, resource utilization, clinician workload, and patient safety [10, 11, 23, 29].

Future evaluations should also examine how AI influences emergency department workflow and human decision-making [10, 11, 14, 17]. In addition to measuring diagnostic performance, studies should assess clinician acceptance, usability, cognitive workload, alert fatigue, workflow integration, and operational efficiency [6, 8, 14, 17]. These implementation outcomes are essential for determining whether AI genuinely improves clinical practice or simply introduces additional complexity [10, 11, 17]. Continuous post-deployment monitoring should become a routine component of AI implementation to detect dataset shift, calibration drift, performance degradation, and unintended consequences that may emerge over time [8, 14–36]. Periodic model recalibration, external validation, and transparent reporting of real-world performance should therefore accompany regulatory approval throughout the AI lifecycle [48– [8, 36, 47].

Ensuring equitable and trustworthy AI requires systematic assessment of fairness across diverse patient populations [12, 13, 16]. Future studies should routinely perform subgroup analyses according to age, sex, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, geographic setting, and other clinically relevant characteristics to identify potential algorithmic bias and disparities in model performance [12, 13, 16, 35]. Adoption of standardized reporting frameworks—including TRIPOD-AI for prediction model development and validation, CONSORT-AI for randomized clinical trials, and SPIRIT-AI for trial protocol reporting (Table 3)—will improve methodological transparency, reproducibility, and comparability across studies while reducing reporting heterogeneity [8, 47–49].

Reporting and governance frameworks for AI evaluation

Finally, successful implementation will depend on strengthening AI literacy among emergency medicine professionals [6, 10, 17]. Clinicians should be equipped to understand model development, interpret probabilistic predictions, recognize algorithmic limitations, identify potential bias, and participate actively in governance, auditing, and quality improvement initiatives [6], 48– [8, 16, 17, 35, 45, 46]. Meaningful progress will require close collaboration among clinicians, informaticians, researchers, healthcare organizations, regulators, and AI developers [6, 14–49]. By combining rigorous implementation science, standardized reporting, continuous governance, and patient-centered evaluation, future research can facilitate the safe, equitable, and sustainable integration of AI into emergency medicine while ensuring that technological innovation translates into measurable improvements in clinical care [8, 10, 11, 16, 48, 49].

Ethical and equity implications in global emergency care

The implementation of artificial intelligence (AI) in emergency medicine raises important ethical, legal, and equity considerations, particularly in the context of existing global healthcare disparities [6–13, 15, 16]. Although high-income countries (HICs) are generally better positioned to adopt AI-enabled clinical decision support systems because of established digital infrastructure, low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) often face substantial barriers related to limited financial resources, workforce capacity, health information technology, and regulatory support [16, 17]. Without deliberate efforts to promote equitable implementation, AI has the potential to widen rather than reduce disparities in emergency care [13, 16].

Effective AI deployment depends on reliable internet connectivity, interoperable electronic health records, secure data infrastructure, computational resources, and ongoing technical support [14–17]. These prerequisites remain inconsistently available across healthcare systems, particularly in resource-constrained emergency departments [16, 17]. Consequently, healthcare institutions with advanced digital infrastructure may benefit from earlier risk identification, improved workflow efficiency, and enhanced diagnostic support, whereas under-resourced facilities may continue to rely on conventional approaches [6, 14, 17]. Promoting equitable access therefore requires scalable and cost-effective implementation strategies, including cloud-based deployment, open-source technologies, interoperable software platforms, and AI systems capable of functioning in low-resource environments with limited bandwidth [14, 16, 17].

Dataset representativeness represents another major ethical challenge [12, 13, 16, 35]. Many contemporary AI models are developed and validated primarily using datasets from high-income countries, limiting their external validity when deployed in populations with different disease epidemiology, healthcare delivery systems, demographic characteristics, and clinical workflows [5, 6, 35, 36]. Underrepresentation of populations from the Global South may increase algorithmic bias and reduce predictive accuracy, potentially reinforcing existing health inequities [12, 13, 16]. Future AI development should therefore prioritize geographically and demographically diverse training datasets, routine external validation across multiple healthcare settings, and transparent reporting of subgroup performance to ensure equitable clinical benefit [13], 48– [8, 16, 35, 36].

Ethical implementation extends beyond algorithmic fairness to include patient autonomy, informed consent, data ownership, privacy protection, transparency, and accountability [15, 16, 48, 49]. Governance frameworks should be adapted to local legal, cultural, and healthcare contexts while ensuring responsible stewardship of patient data and appropriate oversight of AI-assisted clinical decision-making [8, 15, 16, 48, 49]. Ultimately, equitable AI implementation requires international collaboration among researchers, healthcare organizations, policymakers, and technology developers to ensure that advances in AI strengthen global emergency care systems rather than further fragmenting access to high-quality healthcare [6, 14, 16, 17].

Strengths and limitations of the review

This narrative review provides a comprehensive synthesis of current evidence regarding artificial intelligence–based clinical decision support systems in emergency medicine, with particular emphasis on the gap between algorithmic performance and real-world clinical implementation [5, 6, 10, 11, 14, 17]. A major strength of this review is its integration of diagnostic performance, implementation science, and health system considerations rather than focusing solely on algorithm development or predictive accuracy [8, 10, 11, 14–49]. The review critically examines workflow integration, algorithmic bias, explainability, regulatory governance, economic barriers, and post-deployment monitoring while synthesizing evidence across multiple clinical applications, including triage, sepsis prediction, imaging analysis, and AI-assisted electrocardiography [6–49]. Furthermore, the proposed human–AI collaboration model offers a practical conceptual framework for integrating AI into emergency medicine while preserving clinician oversight and accountability [1, 6, 9, 10, 16, 48, 49].

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. As a narrative review, this study does not include quantitative meta-analysis or formal assessment of pooled diagnostic accuracy because of substantial heterogeneity in study designs, AI methodologies, validation strategies, and reported outcome measures [5, 35, 47]. The included evidence comprises both retrospective and prospective investigations of varying methodological quality, reflecting the evolving nature of AI research in emergency medicine [5], 48– [8, 47]. Additionally, the literature search was restricted to English-language publications indexed in major biomedical databases, which may have excluded relevant evidence published in other languages or regional journals. Finally, because many AI systems remain in early phases of clinical implementation, evidence regarding long-term patient outcomes, cost-effectiveness, and post-deployment performance remains limited [10–29]. Despite these limitations, this review provides a clinically relevant and implementation-focused synthesis of current evidence while identifying key priorities for future research and responsible integration of AI into emergency care [8, 10, 11, 16, 48, 49].

Conclusions

Artificial intelligence (AI) has progressed from experimental research to early-stage clinical implementation in emergency medicine, demonstrating considerable potential to improve triage, sepsis prediction, imaging interpretation, electrocardiogram analysis, and operational decision-making. Although many AI-based clinical decision support systems achieve excellent diagnostic performance in retrospective studies, strong algorithmic accuracy alone is insufficient to justify widespread clinical adoption. Current evidence indicates that improvements in patient-centered outcomes, including mortality, diagnostic timeliness, length of stay, and healthcare resource utilization, remain limited.

Future progress should shift from optimizing algorithms to optimizing implementation. AI systems should be evaluated not only by discrimination metrics such as the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC), but also by calibration, external validation, workflow integration, fairness, clinician acceptance, cost-effectiveness, and real-world clinical impact. Continuous post-deployment monitoring, transparent reporting, and robust governance frameworks are essential to ensure safe, equitable, and sustainable implementation.

Rather than replacing clinicians, AI should function as a collaborative decision support tool that augments human expertise while preserving professional judgment and ethical responsibility. Ultimately, the success of AI in emergency medicine will depend on its ability to produce measurable improvements in patient outcomes, strengthen healthcare systems, and support high-quality emergency care.
