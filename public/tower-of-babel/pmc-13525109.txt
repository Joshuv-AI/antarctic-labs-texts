Response to Letter to the Editor for AO Spine Clinical Practice Recommendations for the Management of Spinal Tuberculosis
Authors: Julian Scherer
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We thank the correspondents for their thoughtful and well-referenced letter, and we are grateful for the care with which they engaged with our review. 1 The distinction they draw between spondylitis that is tuberculous but fails to resolve, and destructive granulomatous spondylitis that was never tuberculous in the first place, is a genuinely important one for any surgeon managing this disease, and we do not dispute the clinical substance of their argument. Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease and chronic granulomatous disease are real and under-recognised causes of exactly the diagnostic uncertainty our review was written to address, and the case they cite — an adolescent treated empirically for spinal tuberculosis who was ultimately found to have Aspergillus fumigatus osteomyelitis and underlying CGD — is a sobering illustration of how costly that blind spot can be.

We would, however, like to clarify why we have not incorporated host immune competence into our recommendations, since the reason is methodological rather than a disagreement with the correspondents’ clinical reasoning. This article was written under the Global Spine Journal’s Knowledge Forum Reviews format, which follows a specific and deliberately narrow process: the AO Spine Knowledge Forum Trauma and Infection group selects a small number of studies addressing discrete clinical decision points, and for each one we provide a clinical rationale, a summary of findings, a structured methodological critique, and a GRADE-based recommendation. Every recommendation in our Table is therefore traceable to a specific reviewed study and its documented evidence quality. This is not incidental to the format; it is the format. The value of a Knowledge Forum review, as distinct from a general narrative review or a clinical guideline, is that a reader can see exactly which evidence underlies each recommendation and exactly how confident the panel is in it.

None of the four studies we selected — Altini et al on MRI versus FDG-PET/CT, Hu et al on the laboratory prediction model, Tang et al on surgical approach, or Mittal et al on imaging-defined healed status — addressed host immunodeficiency, MSMD, CGD, or fungal co-infection in any form.2-5 Had we added a host-directed recommendation on the strength of the correspondents’ letter, we would have been issuing a GRADE-graded recommendation with no underlying reviewed study, no documented evidence quality, and no panel assessment behind it. That would not simply be an extension of our review; it would be a different kind of claim altogether, and one that misrepresents the evidentiary basis the rest of the article is built on. We think it is important to hold that line even when, as here, the suggested addition is clinically sound, because the format’s credibility depends on every recommendation being answerable to a specific piece of graded evidence.

We would note that this concern is not entirely foreign to our review. Our methodological critique of the Hu et al laboratory model already records that its derivation cohort excluded patients with HIV infection and autoimmune disease, and that its individual markers — including tuberculosis antibody and interferon-γ release assay results — depend on host immune response. The correspondents have correctly identified that we stopped short of following that observation to its natural conclusion. That is a fair criticism of the discussion, even if it does not, in our view, justify a new graded recommendation.

We would therefore suggest two things. First, the point the correspondents raise is better characterised as a limitation of the current evidence base than as evidence for a new recommendation: none of the studies available to a Knowledge Forum review addressing imaging, laboratory diagnosis, surgical approach, or healed status in spinal tuberculosis have yet been designed or reported in a way that allows host immune status to be evaluated as a variable, and that gap is worth naming explicitly as a direction for future work. Second, we agree that host immune competence is an important enough topic in its own right — spanning diagnostic reasoning, culture practice, referral thresholds, and downstream decisions about live vaccination and family counselling — that it deserves a dedicated evaluation on its own evidentiary terms, rather than being appended to a review whose scope was defined before the question was raised. We would welcome a future Knowledge Forum topic built around exactly the literature the correspondents cite, including the MSMD and CGD series and the case they describe, assessed with the same transparency we have tried to apply here.

We thank the correspondents again for a letter that sharpens rather than undermines the individualised approach we set out to advocate, and we hope this response clarifies why we have chosen to acknowledge their point in this way rather than by amending our recommendations directly.
