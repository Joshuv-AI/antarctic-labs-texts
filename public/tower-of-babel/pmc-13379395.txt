Host serum-circulating flavivirus NS1 co-opts mosquito miRNA processing to suppress early immune defense
Authors: Yifei Li, Chunlai Cui, Jinjin Ding, Yiguan Wang, Jiaqi Yun, Fang Li, Sibao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFlaviviruses pose growing global health threats and are transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes. Yet, how these viruses overcome early mosquito immunity remains unclear. Here, we reveal a cross-host immune evasion strategy in which the flavivirus NS1 protein, abundantly secreted into infected human serum, is taken up by mosquitoes during blood feeding and rapidly internalized into midgut epithelial cells—prior to viral replication. NS1 hijacks the mosquito spliceosome-associated factor Prp19 to promote miR-275-5p maturation, which suppresses the caspase gene CASPS19, blocking apoptosis and enabling early viral replication. Engineered mosquitoes expressing a miR-275 sponge show enhanced resistance to dengue and Zika viruses. These findings uncover a mechanism by which a viral protein presented in infected host blood reprograms vector gene regulation, establishing a paradigm of cross-species immune manipulation by flaviviruses.Subject terms: Viral vectors, Dengue virus, Entomology, Virus-host interactions

Introduction

Flaviviruses, including dengue virus (DENV) and Zika virus (ZIKV), are transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes and pose an escalating global public health threat, with nearly four billion people at risk of infection1. Notably, DENV infections have shown a persistent upward trend and are projected to increase substantially in the future2. DENV causes illnesses ranging from mild fever to life-threatening dengue hemorrhagic fever (DHF) and dengue shock syndrome (DSS)3, particularly in secondary infections. ZIKV infection during pregnancy is linked to congenital malformations and neurodevelopmental abnormalities4. Driven by expanding mosquito populations, global warming, and increased human mobility, flavivirus infections have become an escalating public health concern. In the absence of a licensed vaccine or an approved antiviral drug5, vector control remains the cornerstone of current prevention efforts.

In mosquitoes, the midgut epithelium constitutes the primary site of viral infection and serves as the first physical defense barrier against viral establishment. Following ingestion of an infected blood meal, successful viral replication in the midgut is a critical prerequisite for subsequent systemic infection and eventual virus transmission6. During infection in mosquito cells, flaviviruses encounter complex immune responses, including RNA interference (RNAi), apoptosis, and activation of immune signaling pathways such as Toll, IMD (Immune deficiency), and JAK-STAT pathways7. RNAi is an evolutionarily conserved posttranscriptional gene-silencing mechanism in all eukaryotes8. The small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) pathway is activated during viral replication, when viral double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) intermediates are generated9,10, functioning as the important antiviral defense mechanism in insects11. To counteract these processes, many viruses encode viral suppressors of RNAi (VSRs) to evade antiviral RNAi12. The DENV nonstructural protein NS2A inhibits the Dicer-dependent siRNA production, and VSR-deficient DENV strains exhibit severe replication defects in mosquitoes13. Other insect viruses also encode VSRs to counteract antiviral RNAi14. Nonetheless, the activity of these VSRs is limited to later stages of infection, after the virus has successfully entered host cells and initiated replication.

The early phase of viral infection, including attachment, receptor binding, and cellular entry, is a critical determinant of viral fate15. To successfully establish infection, flaviviruses must overcome the mosquito’s midgut immune defenses during the initial stage to ensure viral replication16. However, the molecular mechanisms by which viruses subvert early antiviral defenses in mosquitoes remain poorly understood.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small non-coding RNAs that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally and are involved in host–pathogen interactions. In this work, we identify Aedes miR-275-5p as a key regulator promoting early flavivirus infection. Flavivirus exposure induces miR-275-5p, which suppresses antiviral apoptosis by targeting the caspase gene CASPS19. We further show that the secreted flavivirus nonstructural protein NS1, abundantly present in the blood of infected vertebrate hosts, is ingested by mosquitoes during blood feeding and internalized into midgut epithelial cells prior to viral replication. NS1 promotes miR-275-5p maturation by interacting with the mosquito spliceosome-associated protein Prp19. This NS1–Prp19–miR-275–CASPS19 axis enables the virus to suppress apoptosis and establish early infection. These findings reveal a paradigm in which a viral protein present in infected host blood manipulates gene regulation in the invertebrate vector to subvert innate immunity. Notably, depletion of miR-275-5p with a miRNA sponge enhances mosquito resistance to DENV and ZIKV infections.

Results

miR-275-5p facilitates DENV infection of Ae. aegypti

To investigate the role of mosquito miRNAs in the early immune response to virus infection, we knocked down Dicer1, which encodes the RNase III enzyme essential for canonical miRNA maturation17. Silencing of Dicer1 resulted in reduced susceptibility to DENV2 infection (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1a), highlighting the importance of miRNAs in mosquito-virus interactions. The mosquito midgut acts as the initial barrier to viral invasion18. To delineate dynamic changes in miRNA expression during early viral infection, we performed sRNA sequencing (sRNA-seq) to profile the miRNA transcriptome in the midguts of female Ae. aegypti at 2, 6, and 24 h after ingestion of a DENV2-infected bloodmeal. Comparative analysis revealed significant differences in the miRNA expression landscape in DENV2-infected midguts relative to uninfected controls (Fig. 1b). Differential expression analysis identified aae-miR-275-5p, aae-miR-71-5p, and aae-miR-317 as among the significantly upregulated miRNAs at early time points examined (2, 6, and 24 hpi) (Fig. 1b, c, Supplementary Fig. 1b, c, d).

Further functional investigation showed that transfection with antagomirs (chemically modified single-stranded microRNA antagonist) targeting miR-275-5p or miR-71-5p resulted in a substantial reduction in viral load in Ae. aegypti Aag2 cells, while inhibition of miR-317 had no detectable effect on viral replication (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 1e, f, g). We next examined the in vivo relevance of these findings by injecting female mosquitoes with antagomirs against miR-275-5p or miR-71-5p followed by DENV2 infection (Fig. 1e). Consistent with the cell-based assays, depletion of miR-275-5p or miR-71-5p significantly reduced viral load in mosquitoes. Notably, inhibition of miR-275-5p led to a marked decrease in viral prevalence, whereas inhibition of miR-71-5p had no detectable effect on viral infection rate. We thus prioritized miR-275-5p for subsequent mechanistic investigation. Increasing miR-275-5p abundance through agomir (chemically modified double-stranded microRNA analogue) treatment enhanced viral replication (Fig. 1f, g, Supplementary Fig. 1h). Moreover, immunofluorescence assays in Aag2 cells showed that miR-275-5p antagomir reduced the number of DENV2-infected cells, while overexpression with agomir led to an increase of infected cells (Fig. 1h). These data show that DENV infection activates miR-275-5p expression, which in turn facilitates viral infection of Ae. aegypti.

miR-275-5p targets the caspase-related gene CASPS19

To elucidate the mechanisms by which miR-275-5p modulates DENV2 infection, we employed three target prediction algorithms—microTarget, PITA and miRanda—to identify putative target genes. 3’UTR, 5’UTR, and coding sequences in the Ae. aegypti genome was analyzed for potential miRNA-target interactions. We found that miR-275-5p could base pair with some mosquito immunity-related genes, including Rel1 (encoding Relish-like transcription factor), APG18B (an autophagy-related gene) and CASPS19 (a caspase gene) (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Table 1). To validate the interaction between miR-275-5p and its candidate target genes, we performed target verification assay ex vivo using luciferase reporter assays. DNA fragments surrounding predicted target sites were PCR-amplified and separately cloned into the downstream of the Renilla luciferase stop codon in the psiCHECK-2 plasmid. Dual luciferase reporter assays revealed that miR-275-5p significantly silenced the expression of AAEL003444 (annotated as CASPS19), a gene encoding a short caspase (Fig. 2a, b). Notably, when the region homologous to the seed sequence of miR-275-5p was mutated in the CASPS19 reporter construct, luciferase activity was unaffected by miR-275-5p (Fig. 2b). We also observed a significant increase in CASPS19 expression following injection of miR-275-5p antagomir in mosquitoes (Fig. 2c), while CASPS19 expression was significantly reduced following injection of miR-275-5p agomir (Fig. 2d). Other predicted target genes were excluded from further analysis due to inconsistencies between ex vivo and in vivo results (Supplementary Fig. 2a–f). These results suggest that CASPS19 is directly targeted by miR-275-5p in its 3’UTR region, leading to the suppression of its expression.

Depletion of CASPS19 enhances DENV infection

To investigate the role played by CASPS19 on DENV2 replication, Aag2 cells transfected with dsCASPS19 were infected with DENV2. Repression of CASPS19 strongly increased DENV2 titers (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. 3b). Similarly, knockdown of CASPS19 facilitated DENV2 abundance in mosquitoes (Fig. 2f). These findings suggest that CASPS19 is involved in antiviral defense against DENV2 infection.

CASPS19 is a homologue of Drosophila Decay, a member of the cysteine protease family that is closely related to the effector caspases Dcp-1 and Drice, which mediate apoptosis in Drosophila19 (Supplementary Fig. 3a). We hypothesized that CASPS19 may likewise participate in the apoptotic process of mosquitoes. To test this hypothesis, we outlined the core apoptosis pathway in Ae. aegypti based on the well-characterized pathway in D. melanogaster (Fig. 2g)20. In this pathway, initiator caspases, including ARK (Apaf-1 Related Killer) and Dronc, are activated by a death signal and subsequently cleave and activate effector caspases, including CASPS7, CASPS8, and CASPS1621. The inhibitor of apoptosis protein 1 (IAP1) suppresses both initiator and effector caspases to prevent inappropriate apoptosis, whereas IMP facilitates IAP1 degradation22,23. We found that RNAi-mediated knockdown of IMP, ARK, or DRONC can reduce CASPS19 transcript abundance (Fig. 2h, Supplementary Fig. 3b). Depletion of CASPS19 led to a reduction of the RNA abundance of three major effector caspase genes CASPS7, CASPS8, and CASPS16 (Fig. 2i), suggesting that CASPS19 functions upstream in the effector caspase cascade and responds to the activation of initiator caspases. Interestingly, the IAP1 transcript abundance also decreased in CASPS19-depleted cells, indicating a potential negative feedback mechanism.

To further assess apoptosis, dsCASPS19-treated mosquito midguts were subjected to TUNEL staining after DENV2 infection. Apoptosis was reduced following CASPS19 depletion compared to control conditions (Fig. 2j). Additionally, a decrease of miR-275-5p abundance enhanced apoptosis, while its induction via agomir injection led to a reduction in apoptotic activity in mosquito midguts (Fig. 2j). Infected midgut lysates were further processed to analyze caspase activity at 2 dpi (days post infection). Mosquito midguts injected with dsCASPS19 showed reduced caspase activity (Fig. 2k). In contrast, treatment with miR-275-5p antagomir increased caspase activity, whereas miR-275-5p agomir administration decreased it (Fig. 2k). Together, these results indicate that CASPS19 is involved in cell apoptosis and miR-275-5p regulates apoptosis by promoting CASPS19 degradation.

Mosquito apoptosis suppresses virus infection

Apoptosis, a form of programmed cell death, plays a crucial role in maintaining cellular homeostasis by eliminating pathogen-infected cells. As a crucial innate immune defense mechanism, apoptosis confers a reduction in virus propagation24. However, some viruses exploit apoptosis during their replication cycles or even utilize viral proteins to cleave caspase25, depending on the specific virus-vector interactions. To explore apoptosis’ role in mosquito antiviral defense, Aag2 cells were treated with the apoptosis agonist, procaspase-activating compound-1 (PAC-1), or the pan caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK, before being infected with DENV2. PAC-1 treatment reduced virus load, whereas Z-VAD-FMK treatment enhanced viral infection (Supplementary Fig. 3c), suggesting that apoptosis can restrict virus infection. Moreover, inhibition of apoptosis gene expression impaired caspase activity and promoted DENV2 replication (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 3d). These data indicate that apoptosis serves as an important antiviral factor in mosquitoes.

To investigate whether miR-275-5p regulates virus infection via cell apoptosis, Aag2 cells were transfected with either the miR-275-5p agomir or antagomir in the presence of PAC-1 or Z-VAD-FMK, followed by DENV2 infection. Compared to cells treated only with PAC-1, knockdown of miR-275-5p in the presence of PAC-1 increased host resistance to DENV2. Conversely, in cells exposed to Z-VAD-FMK, miR-275-5p knockdown decreased resistance to the virus (Fig. 3b). In contrast, treatment with the miR-275-5p agomir partially reversed the suppression of viral replication induced by PAC-1 and increased viral load compared to cells treated only with Z-VAD-FMK (Fig. 3c). Notably, the effect of the miR-275-5p agomir or antagomir on viral infection was tightly coordinated with caspase activity (Supplementary Fig. 3e,f). Moreover, the knockdown of CASPS19 counteracted the antiviral activity of PAC-1 while it enhanced DENV2 infection in cells treated with Z-VAD-FMK (Fig. 3d). Collectively, these findings suggest that CASPS19 is integral to the regulation of cell apoptosis. We propose that DENV2 infection exploits miR-275-5p to circumvent innate apoptotic responses, thereby facilitating viral propagation.

Previous studies have reported that apoptosis occurs at later stages of flavivirus infection, whereas flaviviruses can block apoptotic cell death during the early phases of infection26,27. In line with these findings, we observed that cell apoptosis was not activated at 2 d post DENV infection (Fig. 3e). Notably, suppression of miR-275-5p alleviated the virus-induced inhibition of apoptosis at 2 dpi (Fig. 3e), indicating that miR-275-5p functions as a critical factor exploited by DENV to attenuate apoptosis during early infection.

The NS1 protein increases miR-275-5p abundance

Following DENV2 infection, mosquito miR-275-5p expression increased threefold during the first 2 h post-infection and subsequently declined (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 1e). Most viral particles may remain as intact virions within the gut lumen, which likely have not yet entered host cells to release their genomes and replicate after 2 h post-ingestion. As such, we assume that the structural proteins (capsid, membrane and envelope) on the viral surface28,29, and the secreted nonstructural protein NS1 that is abundant in patient sera, are likely responsible for inducing infection signals to the host cell30,31.

To determine the potential involvement of these proteins, we synthesized C, E, M, NS1, and control GFP proteins using a eukaryotic cell-free expression system (Supplementary Fig. 4a). These proteins were individually added to blood meals, fed to mosquitoes, and midgut miR-275-5p abundance was quantified 2 h post-feeding. Compared to GFP controls, NS1 significantly increased miR-275-5p abundance (Fig. 3f) and enhanced virus infection in cells (Fig. 3g). Consistent with these results, NS1 protein stimulated miR-275-5p expression in Aag2 cells in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3h). Western blot and immunofluorescence assays showed that NS1 entered mosquito midgut cells and Aag2 cells, localizing within both cytoplasmic and nuclear compartments as early as 2 h after infection (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c, d). Besides, NS1 is robustly detected in the midgut epithelium at 2 h post-infection (Fig. 3i). Notably, NS1-mediated miR-275-5p induction correlated with a significant suppression of CASPS19 expression and a concomitant reduction in cell apoptosis (Fig. 3j–l, Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). Conversely, the structural proteins E, M, and C failed to significantly influence miR-275-5p abundance in mosquitoes (Fig. 3f). Together, these results indicate that NS1 protein in patient sera specifically activates mosquito miR-275-5p expression, thereby suppressing apoptosis and facilitating flavivirus infection in mosquitoes.

Biogenesis of miRNAs involves the transcription of a stem-loop RNA (pri-miRNA) by RNA polymerase II, followed by processing through the Drosha-Pasha complex to generate a precursor miRNA (pre-miRNA). This pre-miRNA is then exported to the cytoplasm for further processing by Dicer, producing the mature miRNA duplex17. To decipher the mechanism by which NS1 promotes miR-275-5p production, Aag2 cells were treated with Actinomycin D (ACTD) to suppress cellular transcription. Following this treatment, cells were supplemented with either NS1 protein or GFP as a control. After a two-hour incubation, miR-275-5p abundance was measured. Interestingly, miR-275-5p abundance was significantly higher in cells treated with NS1, even in the presence of ACTD (Fig. 3m, Supplementary Fig. 4i), suggesting that NS1 enhances the production of mature miR-275-5p independently of transcriptional activation. To further clarify this mechanism, we examined the processing of miR-275-5p primary transcript. Using specific pri-miR-275 qPCR primers (Supplementary Table 2) targeting the stem-loop structure at the 3’ end sequenced by 3’RACE, we found that the presence of NS1 protein promoted the processing of pri-miR-275 into mature miR-275-5p (Fig. 3n). Notably, after DENV2 infection, pri-miR-275 abundance also dramatically declined at 2 hours post infection (Fig. 3o). These results indicate that NS1 in the serum of patients promotes miR-275-5p maturation rather than enhancing pri-miR-275 transcription.

NS1 binds to the host pre-RNA processing factor 19 (Prp19)

To further explore how NS1 regulates miR-275-5p biogenesis, we investigated proteins that physically associate with NS1. Immunoprecipitation of NS1 from Aag2 cell extracts revealed specific interactions with several proteins involved in RNA processing, metabolism, splicing, and stability (Supplementary Fig. 4j). Among these, Prp19, a conserved eukaryotic RNA splicing factor essential for activation and stabilization of the spliceosome32, was found to directly interact with NS1 under the treatment of RNase, indicating that Prp19 is a prominent NS1-interacting partner (Fig. 4a). B52 (AAEL012621), another protein identified during screening, did not bind NS1 and served as a negative control (Fig. 4a). We further confirmed that the co-localization of Prp19 and NS1 in cells is consistent with their possible interaction in vivo (Supplementary Fig. 4k).

Prp19 is known to be involved in mRNA processing, spliceosome activation, genome stability, DNA damage response, and protein ubiquitination32. Repression of Prp19 expression led to a marked reduction of miR-275-5p abundance and increase of both CASPS19 and caspase activity (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b, d, e, f), suggesting that Prp19 contributes to miR-275-5p regulation and apoptosis suppression. Significantly, Prp19 repression led to an accumulation of pri-miR-275 (Supplementary Fig. 5c), highlighting its role in miR-275-5p maturation. Further investigation revealed that over-expression of Prp19 into Aag2 cells increases mature miR-275-5p abundance and decreased pri-miR-275 expression (Supplementary Fig. 5g,h). To assess the role of Prp19 during virus infection, we injected mosquitoes with dsRNA targeting Prp19 and subsequently infected them with DENV2. Prp19 depletion led to the reduction of viral loads (Supplementary Fig. 5i,j), indicating that Prp19 is crucial for viral infection in mosquitoes.

To directly evaluate whether Prp19 is required for NS1-induced miR-275-5p activation, we repressed Prp19 in mosquitoes and introduced NS1 into the blood meal. Quantification of miR-275-5p in dissected midguts revealed that Prp19 is essential for NS1-mediated activation of miR-275-5p (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 4l). Notably, NS1 addition reduced pri-miR-275 levels, an effect that was partially reversed by Prp19 depletion (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 4l). These data indicate that production of mature miR-275-5p requires the association of NS1 with Prp19.

To explore the mechanism by which Prp19 promotes miR-275-5p maturation, we hypothesized that Prp19 directly binds to the miR-275-5p primary transcript RNA to facilitate its cleavage. RNA extracted from NS1- and Prp19-immunoprecipitated Aag2 cells was analyzed for miRNA processing intermediates. Pre-miR-275 was detected in both NS1 and Prp19 immunoprecipitants (Fig. 4c). However, pri-miR-275 was absent from these immunoprecipitants. This is likely due to the rapid cleavage from pri-miR-275 into pre-miR-275 following its binding to the complex containing NS1 or Prp19. Electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA) confirmed that Prp19 directly binds to pri-miR-275 (Fig. 4D), and NS1 enhances this interaction (Fig. 4e). NS1 administration enhanced miR-275-5p abundance and decreased pri-miR-275 levels in Prp19-transfected cells compared to Prp19 transfection alone (Fig. 4f, g). Under actinomycin D treatment, NS1 expression in Prp19-tranfected cells show a reduction in pri-miR-275 levels (~50% decrease) compared with Prp19 alone. The findings indicate that NS1 facilitates the association of Prp19 with pri-miR-275, leading to enhanced processing of the mature miR-275-5p.

In insects, pri-miRNAs are processed into pre-miRNAs by the RNaseⅢ enzymes Drosha and Pasha before being exported into the cytoplasm for further processing33. Repression of Drosha and Pasha led to the accumulation of pri-miR-275 and a decrease of miR-275-5p abundance (Fig. 4h, i). Notably, Prp19 was attenuated to enhance pri-miR-275 cropping in Drosha- and Pasha-depleted cells compared with dsGFP-treated cells (Fig. 4h), thereby decreased the mature miR-275-5p abundance (Fig. 4i), indicating that Prp19-mediated cleavage of pri-miR-275 partially depends on the canonical miRNA processing pathway.

Depletion of miR-275-5p with a miRNA sponge enhances mosquito resistance to virus infection

Given the proviral role of miR-275-5p in DENV2 infection, we engineered a transgenic Ae. aegypti strain designed to suppress miR-275-5p expression34. The transgene encodes a miRNA-sponge consisting of 10 tandem repeats of complementary miR-275-5p sequences, each separated by a four-nucleotide spacer and including a four-nucleotide central bulge to enhance stability and efficacy35. To ensure specific expression in the mosquito midgut post-bloodmeal, this miRNA sponge construct was placed downstream of the carboxypeptidase (Cp) promotor36 and cloned it into a piggyBac plasmid containing a GFP gene under the control of the 3×P3-promoter for visual screening (Fig. 5a). Following microinjection into early-stage embryos, transgenic mosquitoes were successfully identified through GFP fluorescence (Fig. 5b). The engineered mosquitoes had significantly reduced miR-275-5p abundance compared to wild type mosquitoes after blood meal (Fig. 5c), validating the genomic integration and effective transcription of the miRNA sponge construct (Fig. 5d, e). This strain was designated miR-275-SP. Consistent with the functional role of miR-275-5p, engineered mosquitoes showed increased CASPS19 transcript abundance and elevated caspase activity relative to wild type after viral infection (Fig. 5f, g). Notably, DENV2 infection rates and viral loads were substantially decreased in miR-275-SP engineered mosquitoes (Fig. 5h), indicating that suppression of miR-275-5p significantly restrict DENV transmission. Moreover, miR-275-SP transgenic mosquitoes exhibited enhanced resistance to a broad spectrum of flaviviruses, including DENV2, DENV4, and ZIKV (Fig. 5i, j). Importantly, the engineered miR-275-5p sponge line displayed no detectable defects in survival, fecundity, and egg viability, indicating no overt fitness cost (Supplementary Fig. 5k, l, m).

Discussion

To establish infection and replicate, viruses must counteract their host’s immune responses. In mammals, flaviviruses such as dengue virus (DENV) evade innate immune responses through multiple strategies, including viral RNA and protein modification and interference with host signaling pathways37. For instance, the DENV NS4B protein antagonizes the vertebrate interferon (IFN) pathway by preventing STAT dimerization and nuclear translocation38. In invertebrates, DENV elicits a relatively weak immune response compared to bacterial pathogens, suggesting that flaviviruses have evolved strategies to counteract mosquito defenses39. Notably, effective viral replication in the midgut depends on the early suppression of innate defenses in epithelial cells. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying this process in mosquitoes remain poorly understood, and the specific effectors the viruses employ as well as the pathways within the hosts they target are currently unknown. In this study, we show that DENV hijacks the mosquito miRNA miR-275-5p to inhibit apoptosis, thereby facilitating early infection. Mechanistically, this process is mediated by the secreted viral protein NS1, which enters mosquito midgut epithelial cells shortly after blood feeding and interacts with the spliceosome-associated protein Prp19 to promote the biogenesis of miR-275-5p through enhanced processing of its primary transcript.

During the early phase of infection, viruses must rapidly suppress host defenses to ensure initial replication. In mammals, this is achieved in part by early cleavage of MAVS, a key adaptor in the antiviral signaling cascade40. In mosquitoes, the mechanisms for early immune suppression by flaviviruses remain elusive. We identified NS1—a secreted viral protein highly abundant in the serum of infected individuals (up to 15,000 ng/ml) and acquired during blood feeding—as a key early-acting viral effector30. We confirmed the presence of NS1 in our C6/36-derived DENV2 inoculum by western blotting (Supplementary Fig. 4g). We further quantified soluble NS1 in the virus preparation used for infectious blood meals by ELISA and measured a concentration of ~1133.44 ng/mL (Supplementary Fig. 4h). Notably, this level falls within the range reported in dengue patient sera (70‒15,000 ng/mL), supporting the physiological relevance of NS1 levels in our experimental system. Our study demonstrates that NS1 rapidly enters midgut epithelial cells within 2 h of blood ingestion, a stage at which most viral particles likely have not yet entered these cells. Previous studies showed that NS1 internalization occurs via clathrin-mediated endocytosis in mammalian cells41. NS1 was reported to be associated with reduced expression of ROS and JAK-STAT pathway genes around 18 h post-infection, a time coinciding with established viral replication in midgut cells42. However, the molecular mechanism underlying the immune suppression and whether NS1 plays a causal role in modulating these pathways remained unresolved. Our study discovers that NS1 acts at a much earlier stage, entering midgut epithelial cells within 2 h after blood ingestion and co-opting mosquito miRNA processing to promote the maturation of miR-275-5p. This leads to direct suppression of apoptosis—a key antiviral defense—facilitating initial viral replication. Our findings suggest that the previously observed downregulation of ROS and JAK-STAT-related genes at later time points may reflect an indirect outcome of NS1-enabled viral amplification, rather than a direct effect of NS1 on these pathways. Thus, our work reveals an early-acting mechanism by which NS1 modulates mosquito immunity during the critical window of midgut infection. While our data support a causal role for NS1 in promoting viral infection in experimental systems, future work incorporating dengue patient–derived samples could further strengthen the physiological relevance of this mechanism in natural human infection.

As a key component of the innate immune response, apoptosis eliminates infected or damaged cells to suppress viral infection. Insects employ apoptotic responses to restrict viral infections, including baculoviruses in Lepidoptera43, Drosophila C virus in fruit fly44, and Sindbis viruses in mosquitoes45. Here, we demonstrate that repression of apoptosis enhances DENV and ZIKV infection in mosquitoes, as shown by increased viral loads following pan-caspase inhibition and reduced infection upon caspase activation. While previous studies suggested that flaviviruses block apoptotic cell death at the early stage of virus infection27, our findings reveal that apoptosis is actively suppressed during the early stages of DENV infection, a critical window for viral establishment.

We further show that this early apoptotic suppression is mediated through NS1-dependent promotion of miR-275-5p maturation. miRNA biogenesis involves RNA polymerase II-mediated transcription of pri-miRNAs, followed by Drosha–Pasha-mediated cleavage into pre-miRNAs. Accessory proteins such as p68, p72, RNA-binding proteins like hnRNP1, and spliceosome-associated components facilitate the efficient processing of specific pri-miRNAs46,47. In our study, we identified Prp19—an essential spliceosome-associated factor—as an NS1-interacting protein that promotes pri-miR-275 processing. Although Prp19 lacks canonical RNase or RNA-binding domains, its WD40 repeat domain has been demonstrated to mediate RNA binding48. Prp19 may directly associate with pri-miR-275 through its WD40 repeat domain and facilitates its processing into mature miR-275-5p, suggesting Prp19 as an accessory factor in canonical miRNA biogenesis.

Previous studies have established miR-275-3p as the dominant mature strand induced after blood feeding, with essential roles in regulating SERCA and maintaining midgut physiology and homeostasis49,50. In contrast, our data show that early DENV infection selectively increases the otherwise minor miR-275-5p strand, whereas miR-275-3p abundance remains largely unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 6a) and does not measurably contribute to early infection phenotypes (Supplementary Fig. 6b,c). This pattern is consistent with a context-dependent bias in miRNA arm usage during infection, rather than a proportional increase of both strands, highlighting an additional layer of post-transcriptional regulation that can be exploited by pathogens51–53. We propose that the arbovirus exploits the post-prandial induction of pri-miR-275 through the NS1–Prp19 axis, biases miR-275 biogenesis toward production of miR-275-5p. Importantly, because miR-275-3p abundance is not detectably altered by NS1 manipulation or Prp19 expression (Supplementary Fig. 6d-f), this strategy allows the virus to modulate a proviral regulatory factor without broadly perturbing the canonical miR-275-3p–SERCA pathway that is critical for midgut homeostasis. Such selective rewiring of host miRNA output enables precise immune evasion while minimizing disruption of essential physiological processes, a feature that may favor efficient early replication and sustained transmission.

In summary, our study identifies flavivirus NS1 as a key viral effector that subverts mosquito immunity to facilitate early infection. Specifically, NS1 enters midgut epithelial cells soon after blood feeding and promotes miR-275-5p maturation by interacting with Prp19, leading to suppression of the caspase gene CASPS19 and apoptosis inhibition, in this way allowing the virus to evade early antiviral responses and establish infection. Importantly, engineered Ae. aegypti mosquitoes expressing a miR-275-5p sponge exhibit enhanced resistance to DENV2 and ZIKV infection, highlighting the translational potential of targeting this pathway to interrupt arbovirus transmission.

Methods

Mosquitoes, cells and viruses

Ae. aegypti (Rockefeller strain) mosquitoes were reared at 27 °C with 70 ± 5% relative humidity under a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle. HEK293T cells for luciferase assay were grown in DMEM (HyClone) containing 10% FBS. C6/36 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 (Cytiva) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco). Aag2 cells were cultured in Schneider’s Drosophila Medium (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% FBS. Insect cells were incubated at 28 °C, while mammalian cells were kept at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. DENV2 (New Guinea C strain), DENV4 (1036 strain) and Zika virus (ZJ03 strain) were propagated in C6/36 cells.

Plasmid construction for tagged expression of Prp19 and DENV proteins

To construct the Prp19-expressing plasmid in mosquito cells, the full-length cDNA of Prp19 was cloned into the pAc5.1 vector (Invitrogen) with a Flag epitope tag fused to its C-terminus. The Prp19-expressing plasmid was constructed by inserting the Prp19 cDNA into the pET28A vector (Invitrogen), incorporating a C-terminal 6×His tag. Four individual DENV2 genes were cloned into pF3A WG (BYDV) Flexi® Vector (Promega), each with a N-terminal 3×V5 tag, and used for in vitro translation via the TNT SP6 High-Yield Wheat Germ Master Mix (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Generation of transgenic mosquitoes expressing a bloodmeal-inducible miR-275 sponge

The construct includes ten tandem repeats of miR-275-5p binding sites driven by the midgut-specific carboxypeptidase (Cp) promoter, with SV40 polyadenylation (SV40) terminator. 10×aae-miR-275-sponge sequence (5′-GTCTCGGTTCAAATTAGCGCG-3′) was synthesized by Sangon Biotech and placed under the control of the bloodmeal-inducible carboxypeptidase (cp) promoter. The GFP marker gene driven by the eye-specific 3×P3 promoter, enabled transgenic screening under fluorescent microscope. The resulting plasmid, piggyBac [3×P3-GFP, cp-10×miR-275-5p sponge], was microinjected into approximately Ae. aegypti 1000 embryos along with a helper plasmid at final concentration of 0.15 and 0.3 μg/ml, respectively, diluted in a solution of 5 mM KCl and 0.1 mM NaH2PO4 (pH 6.8). Microinjections were performed as previously described54. G0 adults were crossed with wild-type mosquitoes, and G1 progeny were screened for GFP fluorescence. Transgenic insertions were confirmed by PCR using genomic DNA extracted with the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen).

Small RNA library construction and sequencing

Midguts from Ae. aegypti mosquitoes were dissected at 2, 12, and 24 h post-DENV2 infection. Total RNA was extracted from pooled midguts (n = 30 per time point) and used for small RNA library construction using the Illumina TruSeq Small RNA kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions by Majorbio Corporation. Briefly, 3′ and 5′ RNA adapters were ligated to small RNAs, followed by reverse transcription using random primers. The resulting cDNA products were amplified by PCR with primers complementary to the adapter sequences. The PCR amplicons were size-separated on a 6% Novex TBE PAGE gel to enrich for sRNA (18–32 nt). Small RNA libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform using NovaSeq Reagent Kit. The expression level of each miRNA was quantified as transcripts per million reads (TPM). Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2, and miRNA with |log2 FC | ≥ 1 and p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Candidate miRNAs identified through the bioinformatics screening were subsequently validated using independent samples by quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR). Only miRNAs whose differential expression was confirmed by qRT-PCR (p-value < 0.05, Student’s t-test) were subjected to further functional investigation.

Prediction of microRNA targets

Candidate targets of miR-275-5p in Ae. aegypti were predicted using three computational algorithms: PITA55, miRanda56, and microTar57. Prediction thresholds were set as follows: miRanda score ≥ 140 (default), PITA ΔΔG ≤ 0, and microTar energy score ≥ 0.5. Gene annotations and 3′ UTR, 5′ UTR, and coding sequence (CDS) regions were obtained from VectorBase.

RNA isolation and quantitative Real-Time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from dissected midguts and carcasses using RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa) and resuspended in nuclease-free water. Genomic DNA was removed using DNase I (TaKaRa), and cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (TaKaRa). For miRNA analysis, cDNA was reverse transcribed using the miRcute miRNA qPCR Detection kit (Tiangen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. qPCR reactions were performed using Taq pro qPCR SYBR Green MasterMix Kit (Vazyme). All reactions were run in triplicate, and relative expression levels were calculated by normalizing to Actin for mRNAs and U6 for miRNAs.

Antagomirs, agomirs, and dsRNA treatment

Chemically modified antagomirs (single-stranded) and agomirs (double-stranded, stabilized) targeting miR-275-5p were synthesized by RiboBio (Guangzhou, China). Double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) was synthesized using the MEGAscript RNAi kit (ThermoFisher) with a T7 promoter sequence (5’ ends-TAATACGACTCACTATAGGG-3′) and purified using the MEGACLEAR kit (ThermoFisher). dsGFP was used as a negative control. At 3 d post-eclosion, mosquitoes were microinjected with 69 nl of agomir (50 μM), antagomir (50 μM), or dsRNA (1.5 ng/μl) using a Nanoject II microinjector (Drummond). Mosquitoes were allowed to recover for 3–4 days prior to blood feeding.

Membrane blood feeding

Three-day-old female mosquitoes were maintained on 10% sucrose for 48 h before blood feeding. Virus stocks were prepared as clarified supernatants from DENV2-infected C6/36 cell cultures and were used without further purification. Defibrinated sheep blood was mixed in a 1:1 ratio with virus. The mosquitoes were then allowed to feed on an infectious blood meal using a membrane feeding system at 37 °C. Fully engorged mosquitoes were sorted and maintained at 27 °C with 70 ± 5% relative humidity.

Luciferase report assay

Target validation was performed as previously described58. The psiCHECK-2 reporters were constructed by inserting the putative miR-275-5p target in the psiCHECK-2 vector (Promega). HEK293T cells were co-transfected with 100 ng of each psiCHECK-2 reporter construct and 100 nM of either a synthetic miR-275-5p agomir or a Negative Control miRNA agomir (miR-ON agomir-NC #24, RiboBio) using Attractene Transfection Reagent (Qiagen). The cells were collected and lysed at 48 h post transfection, and firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega). Each sample was assayed in triplicate, and experiments were independently repeated three times.

TUNEL staining and immunohistochemistry

Mosquito midguts were dissected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS. For the midguts containing undigested blood, they were opened and flattened with a tungsten needle in DEPC-treated water to remove the blood bolus. The midguts were then permeabilized with 20 μg/mL proteinase K for 5 min at room temperature. After two PBS washes, TUNEL staining was performed using the TUNEL Apoptosis Detection Kit (Vazyme), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, samples were incubated with equilibration buffer followed by labeling mix containing terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) for 60 min at 37°C, washed three times with PBS. The samples were counterstained with DAPI.

Caspase activity assay

Mosquito midguts were dissected in PBS 24 h post-viral infection. Blood boluses were removed by gently opening each midgut in DEPC-treated water. Each midgut was then transferred to 50 μL of Schneider’s medium and mixed with 50 μL of Caspase-Glo® 3/7 reagent (Promega)59. Aag2 cells were washed three times with PBS and then suspended in 100 μL PBS followed by the addition of 100 μL Caspase-Glo® 3/7 reagent. Samples were incubated at room temperature for 0.5–2 h, and caspase activity was measured by luminescence.

Protein expression and purification

Prp19 was expressed as a 6×His-tagged fusion protein from the pET28A vector (Invitrogen) in E. coli BL21 cells. Protein was purified using Ni-NTA affinity chromatography. The column was equilibrated with 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and 25 mM imidazole. Protein was eluted using a linear gradient of imidazole from 25 to 500 mM and purified using a 3 kDa molecular weight cutoff filter (Amicon® Ultra). Protein concentration was measured using BCA Protein Quantification.

Western blot

Cells were lysed with RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail and incubated on ice for 1 h. Cell lysates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were mixed with protein loading buffer and boiled for 10 min. The samples were separated by SDS-PAGE on a 10% gel. Proteins were transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% Tween-20, pH 7.5) and then incubated with the indicated primary antibody (Flag or V5 antibody, Genscript, 1:1000) at room temperature for 2 h, washed three times with TBST, and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Goat anti-Mouse IgG, Invitrogen, 1:10,000 or Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG, Abcam, 1:10,000). Protein bands were visualized with SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

For western blot detection of NS1 in the DENV2 inoculum, C6/36 cells were mock-infected (Supernatant) or infected with DENV2 (DENV2 inoculum). Viral particles from culture supernatant were pelleted and analyzed by immunoblotting using antibodies against the DENV2 envelope protein (E, GeneTex GTX127277) and NS1 (Abcam ab41623).

Northern blot analysis

Mosquitoes were injected with dsPrp19 or a control dsRNA (dsGFP). At 48 h post-injection, mosquitoes were fed with blood containing GFP or recombinant DENV2 NS1 protein (1 µg/mL). Midguts were dissected 2 h post-feeding. Total RNA ( ≈ 20 µg per sample) was separated by denaturing agarose gel electrophoresis and transferred to a nylon membrane. The membrane was then cross-linked and hybridized overnight with a biotin-labeled RNA probe (Sangon Biotech). Hybridization signals were detected using the Chemiluminescent Biotin-labeled Nucleic Acid Detection Kit (Beyotime).

Immunofluorescence detection

Aag2 cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, washed three times with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS (PBST) for 10 min. Cells were then blocked with 2% BSA in PBST for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Flag or V5 antibody, Genscript, 1:200) diluted in 2% BSA/PBST. After washing three times with PBS, cells were incubated with secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse, Alexa Fluor 555 goat anti-Rabbit, or Alexa Fluor 647 goat anti-mouse; 1:200 dilution, Beyotime Biotech) and counterstained with DAPI for 1 h at room temperature. Images were acquired using a Leica TSC SP8 STED 3X confocal microscope.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

For co-immunoprecipitation, Aag2 cells were incubated with NS1 protein or Rabbit IgG for 2 h. The cell supernatant was removed and washed 3 times with PBS. Total proteins extracted with NP40 lysis buffer were subjected to Co-IP assays. Protein A/G magnetic beads (Invitrogen) were incubated with anti-NS1 antibody (Gene Tex) for 2 h at 4 °C. The antibody-conjugated beads were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the extracted protein lysates. After five washes with IP buffer using a magnetic rack, bound protein complexes were eluted by boiling in SDS loading buffer for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was subjected to LC–MS/MS analysis.

Identification of NS1-binding Candidates

The raw mass spectrometry files were processed with MaxQuant 1.5.5.1 and searched against the uniprotkb_Aedes_aegypti_20640_2023_07_31 database. The false discovery rate (FDR) for peptides and proteins was set to <1%. Our primary screening criterion was designed to maximize the recovery of potential interactors. Therefore, we considered all proteins with a minimum of one unique peptide in the NS1 pull-down as initial candidates. To rigorously control for nonspecific binders, any protein detected in the IgG control sample (even with a single spectrum) was considered a contaminant and discarded. The candidate list was subsequently subjected to direct binding assays.

Pull down assay

3×Flag- NS1-binding candidates were separately co-incubated with NS1-3×V5 in the presence of protease inhibitor cocktail (1:100 dilution, Beyotime Biotech) and RNaseA (1:100 dilution, Thermo Scientific) for 3 h at 4 °C. Protein complex was then incubated with anti-Flag magnetic beads for 3 h at 4 °C. The dissociated protein was washed three times with IP Buffer (Beyotime Biotech) using a magnetic rack. Bead-bound complexes were eluted by boiling in SDS sample buffer for 10 min. The supernatant was collected, and proteins were analyzed by Western blotting.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

A Cy5-labeled single-stranded RNA probe containing pre-miR-275 and flanking sequence (20 nt upstream and 25 nt downstream of the cleavage sites) was incubated with purified GFP, NS1, Prp19 proteins at 25°C for 1 h in EMSA binding buffer (Beyotime Biotech) supplemented with 5% glycerol. The resulting mixtures were subjected to electrophoresis using a non-denaturing 6% polyacrylamide gel in 0.5× TBE buffer. Gel was exposed and imaged using a chemiluminescent detection device (Amersham Typhoon5).

Statistical analyses

The statistical significance of the difference in nonparametric data between two treatments was analyzed using the Mann-Whitney test. Other statistical significance was calculated using a two tailed Student’s t-test for unpaired comparisons between two groups or one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) between more than two groups. A value of P < 0.05 was statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 5.00 for Windows (GraphPad software).
