Influence of nanofluid on performance improvement of dielectric properties and dissolved gases in oil-immersed power transformers
Authors: Abdul Suboor, Aejaz Masood, Asfar Ali Khan, Shabana Urooj, Mujib Ullah Zuberi, Shakeb A Khan, Obaidur Rahman, Shaheen Parveen, Farhad Ilahi Bakhsh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransformer reliability is strongly governed by the condition of insulating oil; however, limited studies have directly compared real-time aged transformer oil with standardized accelerated-aged oil or evaluated nanofluid performance in aged insulation systems. This study presents a novel comparative framework that first standardizes accelerated aging by matching its dielectric behavior and fault gas signatures with real-time aged transformer oil, and subsequently evaluates the effectiveness of mineral oil-based nanofluids in mitigating aging-induced degradation. Real-time aged and accelerated-aged oils were comparatively evaluated using dielectric constant, dielectric dissipation factor, resistivity, thermal conductivity, and dissolved gas analysis. To restore degraded insulation performance, nanofluids containing cerium oxide and zirconium oxide nanoparticles were prepared and aged under identical conditions. Optimal nanoparticle concentrations of 25 mg/L for cerium oxide and 37.5 mg/L for zirconium oxide were identified. At these concentrations, cerium oxide nanofluids exhibited improvements of + 21.05% in dielectric constant, + 88.47% in resistivity, − 39.27% in dielectric dissipation factor, and + 7.75% in thermal conductivity, while zirconium oxide nanofluids showed enhancements of + 28.07%, + 143.46%, − 40.24%, and + 9.58%, respectively, compared with aged mineral oil. Dissolved gas analysis further revealed a 30–40% reduction in key fault gas concentrations, resulting in a diagnostic shift from severe thermal faults to no-major-fault conditions. These improvements correspond to a 25–40% reduction in aging acceleration factor and an estimated 20–30% extension in insulation life. The results demonstrate the strong potential of cerium oxide- and zirconium oxide-based nanofluids as effective insulating fluids for enhancing the reliability and longevity of oil-immersed power transformers.Keywords: Accelerated aging, Dielectric dissipation factor, Resistivity, Thermal conductivity, Dielectric constant, Nanofluids, Dissolved gas analysis

Introduction

Power transformers are vital components of power transmission and distribution systems, where mineral oil is commonly employed to provide both electrical insulation and effective cooling. Over prolonged service periods, electrical, thermal, and environmental stresses progressively degrade the oil’s dielectric strength, promote sludge formation, and reduce thermal conductivity, thereby jeopardizing transformer reliability and service life [1, 2]. Real-time aging of mineral oil in transformers occurs over several years due to continuous exposure to thermal, electrical, and environmental stresses, leading to chemical deterioration characterized by the formation of acids, sludge, and dissolved gases. These degradation by-products reduce dielectric strength and significantly increase the risk of insulation failure [3].

Accelerated aging is a laboratory-based process in which fresh mineral oil is subjected to elevated temperatures, oxygen exposure, and catalytic materials such as copper and paper to simulate long-term service conditions within a shortened duration of days or weeks. This process results in a reduction in dielectric constant, increased dielectric dissipation factor, decreased resistivity, diminished thermal conductivity, and lowered breakdown voltage, thereby impairing both transformer insulation integrity and heat dissipation capability [4]. Consequently, the assessment of key dielectric properties including thermal conductivity, dielectric dissipation factor, dielectric constant, breakdown voltage (BDV), and resistivity is essential for evaluating the aging condition and insulation quality of transformer oil [5–7]. Variations in these parameters serve as indicators of thermal, electrical, and chemical deterioration within the insulating oil.

In addition to dielectric diagnostics, dissolved gas analysis plays a crucial role in monitoring transformer oil condition by identifying fault-related gases such as hydrogen (H2), acetylene (C2H2), and carbon monoxide (CO), which are generated due to abnormal operating conditions including arcing, overheating, and partial discharges (PD) [8]. Diagnostic interpretation techniques such as the Key Gas Method, Rogers Ratio Method, and Duval Triangle Method enable effective fault identification and failure prediction in power transformers [9–11]. Collectively, these diagnostic approaches provide a comprehensive assessment of transformer oil health and insulation performance.

Nanotechnology has gained significant attention as a promising approach for enhancing the performance of transformer insulating oils, particularly as conventional mineral oils experience reduced dielectric strength, increased dielectric losses, and limited heat dissipation capability with aging [12, 13]. Mineral oil-based nanofluids, developed by dispersing dielectric nanoparticles into base oils, have been reported to improve thermal conductivity, dielectric stability, and moisture resistance [14–16]. Nanoparticles such as zirconium oxide (ZrO2), cerium oxide (CeO2), silicon dioxide (SiO2), boron oxide (B2O3), and boron nitride (BN) have demonstrated notable improvements in breakdown voltage, resistivity, and thermal performance [17–19]. In particular, ZrO2 has been shown to enhance breakdown strength, while CeO2 contributes to improved oxidative stability and dielectric behavior [20–22]. Despite these advantages, challenges related to nanoparticle agglomeration and long-term dispersion stability remain significant concerns [23, 24].

However, the majority of existing studies primarily focus on fresh or lightly aged transformer oils, and limited research has investigated nanofluid performance in aged insulation systems or established a direct correlation between real-time aged transformer oil and standardized accelerated-aged oil conditions [25]. Accordingly, the present study addresses this research gap by integrating standardized accelerated aging with dielectric diagnostics and dissolved gas analysis to evaluate the aging-mitigation potential of CeO2- and ZrO2-based nanofluids under identical aging conditions.

Case study 1: comparative analysis of aged oil

A comparative study was conducted to evaluate the dielectric properties and dissolved gas characteristics of real-time aged mineral oil obtained from a 10 MVA power transformer and laboratory-based accelerated-aged oil equivalent to 1–5 years of service aging. Key parameters, including dielectric constant, dielectric dissipation factor, resistivity, thermal conductivity, and dissolved gas content, were systematically analyzed. Fault diagnostics were performed using the Key Gas Method and ratio-based techniques such as Rogers Ratio Method and the Duval Triangle Method. The results demonstrate progressive degradation of oil properties with aging, while accelerated aging was found to successfully replicate real-time operational aging trends.

Characterization of aged oil

Mineral oil, a crucial insulating and cooling medium in power transformers, degrades over time due to exposure to thermal stress, oxygen, and moisture, leading to the formation of acids, sludge, and fault gases such as CO, CO2, and hydrocarbons. This aging process results in a reduction in dielectric strength and thermal stability, thereby increasing the risk of insulation failure [3, 26, 27]. In this work, the real-time aged mineral oil samples were collected from a 10 MVA power transformer commissioned in different years (2019, 2020, 2021, 2023, and 2024). These samples represent progressive aging stages for comparative analysis.

Dissolved gas analysis

Dissolved gas analysis is a key non-invasive diagnostic technique for monitoring transformer health by detecting faults such as partial discharges, overheating, and arcing through the analysis of dissolved gases, including H2, CH4, C2H4, C2H2, and CO in transformer oil [8].

The interpretation of dissolved gas analysis results in this study was carried out in accordance with IEC 60,599 and IEEE C57.104 standards. These standards define threshold concentration limits for key fault gases, and provide guidelines for identifying partial discharge, thermal faults, and arcing conditions.

Fault severity and classification were determined using the Key Gas Method, Rogers Ratio Method, and Duval Triangle Method, following the diagnostic boundaries and gas ratio limits specified in the aforementioned standards [9–11].

The Key Gas Method detects faults from concentrations of H2, CH4, C2H4, and C2H2 [8], while the Rogers Ratio applies four gas ratios (k = CH4/H2, l = C2H4/C2H6, m = C2H6/CH4, and n = C2H2/C2H4) to identify the nature and severity of electrical and thermal faults in transformer insulation systems [9]. The Duval Triangle maps C2H2, C2H4, and CH4 percentages to locate fault zones, enabling accurate diagnosis [10].

Dielectric constant

The nearly parallel declining trends observed for both aging methods confirm that accelerated aging effectively mimics the dielectric degradation behavior associated with natural service aging. Minor deviations between the two cases can be attributed to additional environmental exposure and in-service operating conditions experienced during real-time aging.

Dielectric dissipation factor

Figure 2 depicts the variation of dielectric dissipation factor for real-time and accelerated-aged transformer oils over a temperature range of 20–80 °C. At all aging stages, the dielectric dissipation factor increases monotonically with temperature, confirming the strong thermal sensitivity of transformer insulating oil. Real-time aged samples exhibit consistently higher dielectric dissipation factor values than their accelerated-aged counterparts, reflecting the cumulative influence of prolonged operational, thermal and environmental stresses. For equivalent aging durations of 1–5 years, the increase in dielectric dissipation factor is more pronounced in real-time oils, whereas accelerated aging under controlled conditions results in comparatively lower degradation. These findings clearly highlight the more severe dielectric deterioration associated with in-service aging when compared to laboratory-simulated accelerated aging.

Dielectric dissipation factor of real-time and accelerated-aged mineral oil

Resistivity

Figure 3 depicts the variation of volume resistivity for real-time and accelerated-aged transformer oils over temperatures range from 20–80 °C, covering aging durations equivalent to 1–5 years. In all cases, resistivity decreases with temperature, indicating its strong thermal dependence. Real-time aged oils generally exhibit lower resistivity values than their accelerated-aged counterparts, particularly after two or more years of aging, reflecting the combined effects of moisture ingress, oxidation, and repeated thermal cycling under actual service conditions. While accelerated aging under controlled laboratory conditions results in comparatively smaller losses, real-time service aging produces a more pronounced decline in resistivity, especially at higher temperatures. These observations confirm that in-service aging leads to a more pronounced deterioration of insulating properties compared to controlled laboratory-simulated aging.

Thermal conductivity

Figure 4 illustrates the variation of thermal conductivity for real-time and accelerated aged mineral oil samples over aging durations equivalent to 1–5 years. A gradual decline in thermal conductivity is observed with aging for both oils, attributed to chemical degradation and oxidation of hydrocarbons. Real-time aged oil shows slightly higher conductivity than accelerated aged oil at early stages, but the values converge after 5 years, indicating comparable thermal performance under prolonged aging.

Thermal conductivity of different-aged mineral oils

The observed differences between real-time aged and accelerated-aged transformer oils arise from their distinct aging mechanisms and stress conditions. Real-time aging occurs gradually under combined service stresses, leading to slow oxidation and progressive formation of degradation products, whereas accelerated aging is dominated by elevated temperature, oxygen availability, and catalytic effects, resulting in intensified oxidation over a shorter duration. Consequently, accelerated-aged oil exhibits higher dielectric dissipation and reduced resistivity due to rapid accumulation of polar compounds and acids [35–37]. Although accelerated aging effectively represents long-term thermal stress, it does not fully replicate the complex and coupled electrical, thermal, and environmental conditions experienced during actual transformer service, emphasizing the need for comparative evaluation [38, 39].

Dissolved gas analysis interpretation method

Three well-established diagnostic methods, namely the Key Gas Method, Rogers Ratio Method, and Duval Triangle Method, were employed to identify transformer faults.

Key gas method

Figure 6 presents a comparison of key dissolved gases in transformer oils under real-time and accelerated aging using the Key Gas Method. Across 1–5 years of real-time and 24–120 h of accelerated aging, distinct gas evolution patterns emerge. Real-time aged oils show higher CO and CO2, indicating sustained cellulose degradation from prolonged thermal and oxidative stress, while accelerated-aged samples show higher H2 and CH4, reflecting stronger partial discharge and arcing effects under controlled high temperatures. The 1-year real-time sample records CO above 50%, whereas the 24-hour accelerated sample has H2 near 45% and CH4 around 36%. With aging, real-time samples show rising C2H4, indicating high-temperature thermal faults, while accelerated samples follow similar trends but retain more H2. By 5 years and 120 h, CH4 exceeds 48%, and C2H4 approaches 37%, confirming severe thermal decomposition in both. These results show that accelerated aging effectively replicates long-term gas formation patterns, providing a faster and more reliable method for simulating transformer insulation faults.

Case study 2: characterization of aged nanofluids

Nanofluids, prepared using a scalable two-step process by dispersing CeO2 and ZrO2 nanoparticles in transformer oil, enhance BDV, thermal conductivity, and resistivity while reducing moisture and oxidative degradation [37, 38]. CeO2 improves oxidation resistance and dielectric stability through high oxygen storage and redox activity [39, 40], while ZrO2 boosts BDV, heat dissipation, and chemical stability [36, 41]. Both are sustainable and eco-friendly, making them ideal for enhancing transformer reliability.

Characterization of nanoparticles

X-ray diffraction

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of the synthesized ZrO2 and CeO2 nanoparticles. Figure 8 confirms that ZrO2 crystallizes in the tetragonal phase with dominant peaks at 2θ = 28.17°, 31.46° and 50.21° and an average crystallite size of ~ 34 nm, while CeO2 exhibits the fluorite cubic structure with key peaks at 2θ = 28.65°, 33.19° and 47.62° and an average size of ~ 31 nm. Such nanoscale dimensions and defined crystal phases dramatically increase surface area and interfacial polarization when dispersed in transformer oil. Recent experiments have shown that ZrO2 based nanofluids with a 0.2 g/L loading enhance AC breakdown strength by up to 30% and reduce dielectric loss by 15% at 60 °C, primarily through nanoparticle-assisted charge trapping and improved thermal conductivity [42]. Similarly, in our previous work, CeO2 nanofluids demonstrate a 25% increase in breakdown voltage after 500 h of accelerated aging, driven by Ce3+/Ce4+⁺ redox cycling that scavenges free radicals, mitigates moisture-induced degradation and stabilizes dissolved gas analysis gas ratios (H2, CO) [43]. A recent review also highlights that metal‐oxide nanofluids can simultaneously boost dielectric performance and aging stability in power transformer oils [44]. Incorporating ZrO2 and CeO2 into 10 MVA transformer oil under accelerated aging thus offers a robust route to enhance dielectric reliability, improve dissolved gas diagnostic accuracy and extend service life.

X-ray diffraction result analysis of a CeO2, b ZrO2

Scanning electron microscopy result analysis

Figure 9 shows the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of nanoparticles. SEM images reveal that ZrO2 nanoparticles form uniform ~ 30–50 nm granular clusters, whereas CeO2 exhibits larger (~ 100–200 nm) porous agglomerates with high surface roughness. Such contrasting morphologies critically affect nanofluid performance: well-dispersed ZrO2 clusters enhance interfacial polarization and electron-trap density, delivering ~ 30% improvement in AC breakdown voltage in transformer oil [42]. Conversely, CeO2’s porous agglomerates excel in radical scavenging, stabilizing dielectric properties and maintaining reliable dissolved gas analysis markers during accelerated aging [44]. Moreover, improved dispersion stability reduces nanoparticle sedimentation under thermal cycling, ensuring consistent dielectric strength and diagnostic accuracy in 10 MVA transformer oils.

Experimental setup for nanofluids preparation

The two-step method is the most common nanofluid synthesis technique due to its low cost and broad compatibility. Nanoparticles are first synthesized chemically or physically, then dispersed into oil using ball milling, shear mixing, magnetic stirring, or ultrasonic treatment [45].

Figure 10 shows the two-step method for the preparation of mineral oil-based nanofluids with different nanoparticles, CeO2 and ZrO2, at concentrations of 12.5 mg/L, 25 mg/L, and 37.5 mg/L. Nanoparticles were mixed for 5 min at 1500 rpm using a magnetic stirrer, followed by 2 h of high-frequency ultrasonication for stable dispersion.

Result and discussion

Dielectric properties

In this section, CeO2 and ZrO2 nanofluids (12.5 mg/L, 25 mg/L, and 37.5 mg/L) were prepared via the two-step method and aged under identical conditions. Their dielectric properties and dissolved gas analysis results were compared with standardized accelerated-aged oils to determine optimal nanoparticle concentration and assess insulation and fault resistance improvements. All measurements were repeated three times, and low standard deviation () values confirmed good consistency and repeatability trend.

Dielectric constant

Figure 11 compares dielectric constant values of CeO2 and ZrO2-based nanofluids with standardized accelerated-aged oils. In Fig. 11(a), CeO2 nanofluids show a nonlinear trend, with the dielectric constant increasing from 12.5 mg/L to 25 mg/L due to enhanced polarizability, then slightly decreasing at 37.5 mg/L, likely from nanoparticle agglomeration reducing uniform dispersion. Figure 11(b) shows ZrO2 nanofluids with a steady increase in dielectric constant as concentration rises, indicating reliable dispersion and enhanced polarization. Both aged nanofluids outperform standardized 5-year aged oil, proving the effectiveness of nanoparticles in restoring and enhancing dielectric behavior.

Dielectric dissipation factor

Figure 12 shows the variation in dielectric dissipation factor of CeO2 and ZrO2-based nanofluids compared to standardized accelerated aged oils at four temperatures: 20, 40, 60, and 80 °C. For CeO2 nanofluids, the 25 mg/L concentration consistently records the lowest dielectric at all temperatures, indicating superior dielectric behavior and minimal losses. For ZrO2 nanofluids, 25 mg/L also performs well, though the 37.5 mg/L sample shows slightly better performance at most temperatures. These results confirm that adding CeO2 and ZrO2 nanoparticles enhances dielectric stability under thermal stress, with 25 mg/L and 37.5 mg/L concentrations being optimal for reducing losses.

Resistivity

Figure 13 shows the variation in insulation resistivity of CeO2- and ZrO2-based nanofluids compared to standardized accelerated aged transformer oils representing 1–5 years of aging, measured at 20, 40, 60, and 80 °C. Nanofluids consistently have higher resistivity than aged oils at all temperatures, indicating improved insulation. For CeO2 nanofluids, 25 mg/L shows the highest resistivity at 20–60 °C, while 37.5 mg/L performs better at 80 °C. For ZrO2 nanofluids, 37.5 mg/L shows superior resistivity across all temperatures, outperforming other ZrO2 samples and all aged oils.

ZrO2 based nanofluids show a steady rise, with 37.5 mg/L achieving the highest value, indicating superior dispersion and thermal behavior.

Dissolved gas analysis of aged nanofluids

Figure 15 illustrates the comparative dissolved gas analysis results between the standardized 5-year accelerated aged mineral oil and aged nanofluids prepared using different concentrations of CeO2 and ZrO2 nanoparticles. The 5-year accelerated aged oil is used as a reference because it represents a condition where significant thermal degradation has occurred, and almost all fault gases have evolved to noticeable levels.

Comparisons of dissolved gas concentration in standardized accelerated aged mineral oil and different samples of nanofluids based on a CeO2 and b ZrO2

In Fig. 15(a), the CeO2 based nanofluids (12.5 mg/L, 25 mg/L, and 37.5 mg/L) show a marked reduction in gas concentrations compared to the 5-year aged oil, indicating an improved resistance to thermal and oxidative degradation. Notably, the 25 mg/L CeO2 concentration appears to offer the best suppression of hydrocarbon and CO/CO2 gases, implying a stabilizing effect on the oil. Similarly, Fig. 15(b) presents the results for ZrO2 based nanofluids. Here, too, all three concentrations show reduced gas formation relative to the aged oil, although the extent of reduction varies by gas type. Among them, 25 mg/L and 37.5 mg/L ZrO2 nanofluids exhibit better suppression of CH4, C2H2, and CO, suggesting enhanced antioxidant and gas-suppressing behavior.

The improvements in dielectric properties and the reduction in fault-related gas generation observed for CeO2 and ZrO2 based nanofluids are attributed to interfacial polarization and charge-carrier trapping introduced by dispersed metal-oxide nanoparticles [12–14]. The increased interfacial area suppresses charge mobility, leading to reduced dielectric losses and enhanced insulation performance [15]. CeO2 nanoparticles further mitigate oxidation through their redox activity, limiting the formation of polar degradation products and associated fault gases [21, 22], while ZrO2 nanoparticles, owing to their high bandgap and insulating nature, promote improved electric-field uniformity and resistivity [18–20]. Collectively, these mechanisms moderate localized thermal and electrical stresses, resulting in improved dielectric behavior and reduced gas evolution, consistent with prior transformer nanofluid studies [16, 17].

Fault identification using dissolved gas analysis techniques

Three dissolved gas analysis diagnostic techniques were applied to aged mineral oil-based nanofluids with varying concentrations of CeO2 and ZrO2, as shown in Table 3. Standard accelerated-aged oil showed severe thermal faults with high CH4, C2H4, and C2H6, and both Rogers Ratio and Duval Triangle indicated a T2 fault (300–700 °C). With 12.5 mg/L CeO2, gassing and fault severity decreased, though slight cellulose overheating occurred. At 25 mg/L CeO2, dielectric stability improved notably, with only minor low-energy discharges (D1 zone). In contrast, 37.5 mg/L CeO2 caused nanoparticle agglomeration, leading to partial discharges despite reduced thermal faults. For ZrO2 nanofluids, 12.5 mg/L showed minor partial discharge and stray gassing, 25 mg/L indicated paper aging due to clustering, while 37.5 mg/L delivered optimal performance, suppressing major faults and confining activity to minor thermal zones. Overall, 25 mg/L CeO2 and 37.5 mg/L ZrO2 offered the best fault mitigation, enhancing transformer insulation life.

At moderate concentrations (25 mg/L CeO2 and 37.5 mg/L ZrO2), the increased interfacial area promotes effective charge trapping, reduces charge carrier mobility, and suppresses oxidation-driven degradation products, leading to lower dielectric losses and reduced fault gas generation. At higher concentrations, however, nanoparticle agglomeration may occur, reducing dispersion stability and creating localized field distortions that promote partial discharge activity. Similar concentration-dependent behavior has been reported in transformer nanofluid studies, where excessive nanoparticle loading diminishes insulation benefits. These results indicate that nanofluids primarily reduce the effective aging rate by mitigating thermal and electrical stress mechanisms, which may translate into potential extension of insulation service life under controlled operating conditions, rather than complete fault elimination.

Conclusion

Power transformers are a vital component in electrical power networks, and their reliable long-term operation strongly depends on the condition of the insulating oil, which functions as both a dielectric medium and an effective coolant. In this study, a comprehensive comparative analysis was performed on the dielectric and thermal properties, namely dielectric constant, dielectric dissipation factor, resistivity, and thermal conductivity, along with dissolved gas analysis of real-time aged mineral oil obtained from a 10 MVA power transformer and standardized accelerated-aged oil representing 1–5 years of service aging. The accelerated aging process was standardized by matching dielectric behavior and fault signatures of long-term in-service oils, validating its use for insulation degradation studies.

The novelty and significance of this work lie in the integrated evaluation of standardized accelerated aging, comprehensive dielectric diagnostics, and multi-technique dissolved gas analysis, combined with a concentration-dependent assessment of CeO2 and ZrO2 based nanofluids under identical aging conditions. Unlike most existing studies that primarily focus on fresh or lightly aged oils, this work demonstrates nanofluid performance enhancement directly in aged insulation systems, providing a more realistic and application-oriented framework for transformer insulation diagnostics and life management. The results show that optimized nanoparticle concentrations significantly enhance insulation performance, with 25 mg/L CeO2 and 37.5 mg/L ZrO2 delivering the most balanced improvements in dielectric constant, resistivity, dielectric dissipation factor, and thermal conductivity. Dissolved gas analysis results further reveal a clear reduction in fault gas generation, shifting fault severity from severe thermal faults in pure aged oil to fault signatures that were significantly reduced in optimized nanofluids, as confirmed by the Key Gas Method, Rogers Ratio, and Duval Triangle.

Property enhancements included + 28.65% dielectric constant, + 34.48% resistivity, − 21.95% tan delta, and + 25.00% thermal conductivity for 25 mg/L CeO2, and + 26.32%, + 55.17%, − 14.63%, and + 41.67%, respectively, for 37.5 mg/L ZrO2. Based on Arrhenius thermal aging law and IEEE C57.91 insulation life models, these improvements are estimated to reduce the aging acceleration factor by 25–40% and potentially extend transformer service life by 20–30% under comparable operating. Overall, this study establishes that CeO2 and ZrO2 based nanofluids exhibit strong potential as sustainable insulating liquids, offering enhanced dielectric stability, improved thermal performance, and effective fault mitigation in aged transformer oil systems. The findings provide meaningful insights into transformer insulation diagnostics and life extension strategies, contributing valuable knowledge toward improving long-term transformer reliability and asset management.
