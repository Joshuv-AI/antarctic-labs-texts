On finite-temperature Fredholm determinants
Oleksandr Gamayun, Yuri Zhuravlev

Abstract
We consider finite-temperature deformation of the sine kernel Fredholm determinants acting on the closed contours. These types of expressions usually appear as static two-point correlation functions in the models of free fermions and can be equivalently presented in terms of Toeplitz determinants. The corresponding symbol, or the phase shift, is related to the temperature weight. We present an elementary way to obtain large-distance asymptotic behavior even when the phase shift has a non-zero winding number. It is done by deforming the original kernel to the so-called effective form factors kernel that has a completely solvable matrix Riemann-Hilbert problem. This allows us to find explicitly the resolvent and address the subleading corrections. We recover Szegő, Hartwig and Fisher, and Borodin-Okounkov asymptotic formulas.

Introduction and motivation

Quantum integrable systems attract much attention among researchers due to the possibility of precisely addressing non-perturbative physical phenomena ranging from AdS/CFT to condensed matter
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. The exact evaluation of the correlation function even in the integrable system remains a challenge because of the necessity to perform summation over the form factors
[
4
,
5
]
. At zero temperatures, one can use numerical summation
[
6
]
or settle with the effective field theory methods
[
7
]
. For non-zero temperatures, or more generically for finite entropy states, more sophisticated methods were developed: the quantum transfer matrix approach
[
8
]
and related thermal form factors series
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
; Leclair-Mussardo approach
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
and its generalizations in terms of the thermodynamic form factor program
[
15
,
16
]
, the quench action
[
17
]
, and the ballistic fluctuation theory
[
18
]
among many others.

At large coupling constants, closed expressions exist for the correlation functions of integrable systems in terms of Fredholm determinants of the generalized sine kernels
[
4
]
.
Their asymptotic behavior can be computed via the non-linear steepest descend analysis of the corresponding Riemann-Hilbert problem
[
19
,
20
]
.
This method is quite technically involved
[
21
]
, therefore in
[
22
,
23
]
we introduced a heuristic method of
effective form factors
to extract large time and long distance asymptotic of two-point functions in XY-spin chain
[
22
]
, the system of one-dimensional anyons
[
24
]
, the mobile impurity
[
25
,
26
]
, and Hubbard model
[
27
]
.
The method’s main idea is to extract the asymptotic behavior not from the thermodynamic expression of the Fredholm determinants but rather from the scaling analysis with the system size of the appropriately modified form factor series. We have checked the validity of this approach numerically, but the connection with the non-linear steepest descend method remained elusive.
In this paper, we partially solve this problem. More specifically, we address the static (equal time) case for which the typical two-point function can be expressed as a Fredholm determinant acting on
L_{2}(\mathds{S}^{1})
(or
\mathds{S}^{1}
for shortness)

T_{x}[\theta]=\det_{\mathds{S}^{1}}\left(1+\hat{S}\right)

(1)

here by
\mathds{S}^{1}
we mean a set
\{q\in\mathds{C},|q|=1\}
, an action of the operator
\hat{S}
is specified by its kernel
S(q,p)

(\hat{S}f)(q)=\int\limits_{\mathds{S}^{1}}S(q,p)f(p)dp=\int\limits_{|p|=1}S(q,p)f(p)dp.

(2)

The kernel differs from the traditional sine kernel by a prefactor given by the weight function
\theta(q)
, specifically

S(q,p)=\frac{\theta(q)}{2\pi i}\frac{e_{+}(p)e_{-}(q)-e_{+}(q)e_{-}(p)}{p-q},\qquad e_{\pm}(q)=q^{\pm x/2}.

(3)

The Fredholm determinant is understood as the standard Fredholm series

T_{x}[\theta]=1+\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int\limits_{(\mathds{S}^{1})^{n}}\det(S(q_{j},q_{k}))_{j,k=1}^{n}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}dq_{j}.

(4)

The other
definitions and the effective ways to compute Fredholm determinants can be found in Ref.
[
28
]
.

Analogously, the continuous counterpart is a
finite-temperature
sine kernel, acting on the real line (
L_{2}(\mathds{R})
).

T_{x}[\theta]=\det_{\mathds{R}}\left(1+\hat{S}\right),\qquad S(q,p)=\frac{\theta(q)}{2\pi}\frac{\sin(x(p-q))}{p-q}.

(5)

Originally, this type of kernel arose in the studies of one-dimensional Bose gas for the correlation functions of the impenetrable bosons
[
4
]
.
In that case, the prefactor
\theta(q)
is proportional to the Fermi weight
\theta(q)\sim(e^{(q^{2}-\mu)/T}+1)^{-1}
, where
T
is the temperature and
\mu
is a chemical potential.
We will not fix the specific form of
\theta(q)
but rather discuss asymptotic for the quite generic class of smooth functions decaying fast enough at the infinities.
Recently there was a lot of interest in exploring the properties of
finite-temperature
deformations of the sine kernel determinants
[
29
,
30
]
.
Partially, it is connected with the fact that the corresponding Riemann-Hilbert problem turns out to be much simpler than in the
zero-temperature
case when the contour degenerates to the set of arcs or intervals
[
31
]
.

Essentially, since
\theta(q)
decays fast enough at infinity, the case of
\mathds{R}
is completely equivalent to
\mathds{S}^{1}
so we will focus mainly on considering (
1
). The answer for the continuous kernels can be obtained by introducing the radius of
\mathds{S}^{1}
and then sending it to infinity with the appropriate rescating of
x
. We are not going to discuss the possible peculiarities of taking such a limit, we just mention that the results for the asymptotic behavior in the mobile impurity problems
[
25
]
suggest that everything seems to work without any subtleties.

The great advantage of the
\mathds{S}^{1}
case is that the Fredholm determinant (
1
) is nothing but a Toeplitz determinant (see
[
32
]
or Sec. 5 in
[
22
]
)

T_{x}[\theta]=\det_{0\leq n,m\leq x-1}c_{n-m},\qquad\quad c_{k}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\limits_{\mathds{S}^{1}}\frac{dq}{q^{k+1}}e^{2\pi i\nu(q)}.

(6)

with

e^{2\pi i\nu(q)}=1+\theta(q).

(7)

Throughout the rest of the paper, we assume the connection (
7
) and will treat
\nu(q)
and
\theta(q)
on equal footing.
We assume that the phase shift
\nu(q)
is a smooth function with a possible non-zero winding number around
\mathds{S}^{1}
.
The large
x
asymptotic of the Toeplitz determinant for these types of symbols is known to be given by Szegő formulas
[
33
,
34
]
and its generalization by Hartwig and Fisher
[
35
,
36
]
. The subleading corrections can be found in a form that is widely referred to as the Borodin-Okounkov formula
[
37
]
.
By trying to reproduce the effective form factor asymptotic for the determinant (
1
) we re-derive these results in elementary fashion.
It turns out that the effective form factors lead to such a deformation of the kernel that the corresponding Riemann-Hilbert problem can be solved exactly.
As a sequence of that the resolvent is known explicitly and the subleading corrections can be reproduced systematically and presented in a closed form.

The paper is organized as follows: in the next section (
2
), we introduce the main notations and explain how the Riemann-Hilbert problem can be solved explicitly for the deformed kernels.
In Sec. (
3
) we proceed to the evaluation of the Fredholm determinant by the means of computing its variation with respect to the phase shift. In Sec. (
4
) we consider the subleading corrections, and in Sec. (
5
) the connection to the effective form factors is thoroughly discussed. The last section contains concluding remarks and possible future directions.
Various technical aspects and alternative formulations can be found in the Appendices.

2
Riemann-Hilbert Problem

In this section, we introduce main notations and give the formula for the resolvent of the kernel (
1
).
Let
\theta(q)
in (
1
) be a meromorphic function in
\mathds{CP}^{1}
. We additionally assume that the
function
\nu(q)
in (
7
) doesn’t have singularities on
\mathds{S}^{1}
, but might have
a winding number
\mathfrak{n}

\mathfrak{n}=\oint_{\mathds{S}^{1}}d\nu(q)\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\mathds{S}^{1}}dq\frac{\theta^{\prime}(q)}{1+\theta(q)}.

(8)

The winding number measures the difference between the number of zeros and the number of poles of
1+\theta(q)
within
\mathds{S}^{1}
, or the difference between the number of poles and zero outside
\mathds{S}^{1}
.
Therefore, if
\mathfrak{n}<0
there are at least
|\mathfrak{n}|

\mathds{S}^{1}
. We order them by the absolute value
|z_{1}|>|z_{2}|\dots>|z_{-\mathfrak{n}}|>1
,
1+\theta(z_{k})=0

For clarity, we assume that all zeroes are distinct even by their absolute value, but the proper limiting procedures can easily lift this restriction in the final answers.
We want to deform the contour
\mathds{S}^{1}
in (
1
) into
\mathcal{C}
such that it will encircle all of the zeroes
\{z_{k},\,k=1,\dots,-\mathfrak{n}\}
but none of the poles of
\theta(q)
(see Fig. (
1
)).
By doing this the value of the Fredholm determinant remains unchanged, but the winding number of the new contour is zero

T_{x}[\theta]=\det_{\mathcal{C}}(1+\hat{S}),\qquad\oint_{\mathcal{C}}d\nu(q)=0.

(9)

Similarly if
\mathfrak{n}>0
we find zeroes within
\mathds{S}^{1}
,
1>|z_{1}|>|z_{2}|\dots>|z_{\mathfrak{n}}|
,
1+\theta(z_{k})=0
, and deform the contour
\mathds{S}^{1}\to\mathcal{C}
to exclude them.

Our next step is to deform the kernel. Namely,
we introduce the deformed Fredholm determinant
\tau_{\mathcal{C}}[\nu]
defined on the contour

\tau_{\mathcal{C}}[\nu]=\det\limits_{\mathcal{C}}(1+\hat{V}_{\mathcal{C}})

(11)

\text{v}_{-}(q)=q^{-x/2},\qquad\text{v}_{+}(q)=\text{v}_{-}(q)w_{\mathcal{C}}(q),\qquad w_{\mathcal{C}}(q)=q^{x}+\int\limits_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.5038pt}{\resizebox{488250}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.50458pt}{$\displaystyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.5038pt}{\resizebox{488250}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.50458pt}{$\textstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{496717}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21754pt}{\resizebox{581946}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.09773pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle<$}}}}}}}\frac{dk}{2\pi i}\frac{k^{x}}{k-q}\frac{\theta(k)}{1+\theta(k)}.

(12)

Here the contour
\mathcal{C}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\displaystyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\textstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{453071}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.19157pt}{$\scriptstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{520837}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle<$}}}}}}

1

The idea of such a deformation is that the resolvent for this kernel defined as

(1+\hat{V}_{\mathcal{C}})(1-\hat{R})=1.

(13)

R(q,p)=\frac{\sqrt{\theta(p)}\sqrt{\theta(q)}}{2\pi i}\frac{f_{+}(p)f_{-}(q)-f_{+}(q)f_{-}(p)}{p-q}.

(14)

The functions

and

can be related by the integral equations

(1+\hat{V}_{\mathcal{C}})\sqrt{\theta}f_{\pm}=\sqrt{\theta}\text{v}_{\pm},\qquad(1-\hat{R})\sqrt{\theta}\text{v}_{\pm}=\sqrt{\theta}f_{\pm}.

(15)

These integral equations can be rewritten as a Riemann–Hilbert problem (RHP). To do this, we, following Slavnov
[
38

\displaystyle|V(q)\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\text{v}_{-}(q)\\
-\text{v}_{+}(q)\end{array}\right),\qquad\langle V(q)|=(\text{v}_{+}(q),\text{v}_{-}(q)),

\displaystyle|F(q)\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}f_{-}(q)\\

\chi_{\mathcal{C}}(q)=\mathds{1}-\int\limits_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{dk}{2\pi i}\frac{\theta(k)|F(k)\rangle\langle V(k)|}{k-q}.

(20)

In these notations, the resolvent reads

R(p,q)=\frac{\sqrt{\theta(p)}\sqrt{\theta(q)}}{2\pi i}\frac{\langle F(p)|F(q)\rangle}{p-q}.

(21)

3
Variational formulas

In this section using the exact form of the resolvent, we evaluate the tau function (
10
).
To do this we consider the variation
\nu\to\nu+\delta\nu
such that the winding number around the contour
\mathcal{C}
remains zero.
The variation of the determinant can be computed as

\delta\ln\tau_{\mathcal{C}}[\nu]={\rm Tr}(1-\hat{R})\delta V_{\mathcal{C}}.

(30)

The variation of the kernel reads

\delta V_{\mathcal{C}}(q,p)=V_{\mathcal{C}}(q,p)\frac{\delta\theta(p)}{2\theta(p)}+\frac{\delta\theta(q)}{2\theta(q)}V_{\mathcal{C}}(q,p)+\frac{\sqrt{\theta(p)\theta(q)}}{2\pi i}q^{-x/2}p^{-x/2}\int\limits_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.5038pt}{\resizebox{488250}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.50458pt}{$\displaystyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.5038pt}{\resizebox{488250}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.50458pt}{$\textstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{496717}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21754pt}{\resizebox{581946}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.09773pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle<$}}}}}}}\frac{dk}{2\pi i}\frac{\delta\theta(k)}{(1+\theta(k))^{2}}\frac{k^{x}}{(k-q)(k-p)}.

(31)

The trace of the first two terms can be reduced to the computation of the resolvent on the diagonal. Indeed, using
(1-\hat{R})\hat{V}_{\mathcal{C}}=\hat{R}
we obtain

\delta\ln\tau_{\mathcal{C}}[\nu]=\int\limits_{\mathcal{C}}dk\delta\theta(k)\frac{R(k,k)}{\theta(k)}+\int\limits_{\mathcal{C}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.5038pt}{\resizebox{488250}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.50458pt}{$\displaystyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.5038pt}{\resizebox{488250}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.50458pt}{$\textstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{496717}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21754pt}{\resizebox{581946}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.09773pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle<$}}}}}}}}dk\delta\theta(k)\frac{k^{x}}{(1+\theta(k))^{2}}\frac{M(k,k)}{2\pi i},

(32)

where we have denoted by
M(k_{1},k_{2})
the following combination

M(k_{1},k_{2})\equiv\int\limits_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{dqdp}{2\pi i}\frac{\sqrt{\theta(q)}q^{-x/2}}{k_{1}-q}(1-\hat{R})(q,p)\frac{\sqrt{\theta(p)}p^{-x/2}}{k_{2}-p}.

(33)

One can easily express these integrals in terms of the solutions of the RHP (see (
A
) for details).

M(k_{1},k_{2})=\frac{\chi_{22}(k_{1})\chi_{12}(k_{2})-\chi_{12}(k_{1})\chi_{22}(k_{2})}{k_{1}-k_{2}}

(34)

or using explicit presentation (
25
)

M(k_{1},k_{2})=-e^{\Omega_{\mathcal{C}}(k_{1})+\Omega_{\mathcal{C}}(k_{2})}\frac{b_{\mathcal{C}}(k_{1})-b_{\mathcal{C}}(k_{2})}{k_{1}-k_{2}}.

(35)

Here we should use either
\chi_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\displaystyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\textstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{453071}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.19157pt}{$\scriptstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{520837}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle>$}}}}}}
or
\chi_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\displaystyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\textstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{453071}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.19157pt}{$\scriptstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{520837}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle<$}}}}}}
, and correspondingly
b_{\mathcal{C}}
depending on the position of
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
. In particular, for
k_{1}=k_{2}=k\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\displaystyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\textstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{453071}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.19157pt}{$\scriptstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{520837}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle<$}}}}}}
using L’Hôpital’s rule we obtain

M(k,k)=-e^{2\Omega_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.5038pt}{\resizebox{488250}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.50458pt}{$\displaystyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.5038pt}{\resizebox{488250}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.50458pt}{$\textstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{496717}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21754pt}{\resizebox{581946}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.09773pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle>$}}}}}}(k)}\partial_{k}b_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\displaystyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\textstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{453071}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.19157pt}{$\scriptstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{520837}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle>$}}}}}}(k).

The resolvent at the diagonal can be easily evaluated with the explicit form of the

(see Eqs. (

It is convenient to present it in a form that can be continued into the domain

so that integration over

(36)

Altogether the variation reads

\delta\ln\tau_{\mathcal{C}}[\nu]=\int\limits_{\mathcal{C}}dq\delta\nu(q)\left(\frac{x}{q}+\int\limits_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.5038pt}{\resizebox{488250}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.50458pt}{$\displaystyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.5038pt}{\resizebox{488250}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.50458pt}{$\textstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{496717}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21754pt}{\resizebox{581946}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.09773pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle<$}}}}}}}\frac{\nu(k)dk}{(k-q)^{2}}+\int\limits_{\mathcal{C}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.5038pt}{\resizebox{488250}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.50458pt}{$\displaystyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.5038pt}{\resizebox{488250}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.50458pt}{$\textstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{496717}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21754pt}{\resizebox{581946}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.09773pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle>$}}}}}}}\frac{\nu(k)dk}{(k-q)^{2}}\right).

(37)

Here we have replaced the variation in
\theta
to the variation in
\nu
using Eq. (
7

Taking into account that for

,
\tau_{\mathcal{C}}[\nu]=1
we obtain

\boxed{\tau_{\mathcal{C}}[\nu]=\exp\left(x\int\limits_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{dq}{q}\nu(q)-\frac{1}{2}\int\limits_{\mathcal{C}}\int\limits_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\frac{\nu(k)-\nu(q)}{k-q}\right)^{2}dk\,dq\right).}

(38)

This formula gives the leading asymptotic for the Toeplitz determinant of the symbol with the properties indicated at the beginning of the Sec. (

Let us discuss equivalent ways to present this formula and how certain known cases can be recovered from it. First, we notice that with the functions
\Omega_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\displaystyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\textstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{453071}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.19157pt}{$\scriptstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{520837}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle>$}}}}}}
and
\Omega_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\displaystyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\textstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{453071}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.19157pt}{$\scriptstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{520837}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle<$}}}}}}
from Eq. (
27
) we can rewrite

\int\limits_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{\nu(q)dq}{q}=\int\limits_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{(\Omega_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\displaystyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\textstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{453071}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.19157pt}{$\scriptstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{520837}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle>$}}}}}}(q)-\Omega_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\displaystyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\textstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{453071}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.19157pt}{$\scriptstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{520837}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle<$}}}}}}(q))dq}{2\pi iq}=\Omega_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\displaystyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\textstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{453071}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.19157pt}{$\scriptstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{520837}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle>$}}}}}}(0).

(39)

Here in the last integral we have shrinked the contour
\mathcal{C}
into inside for
\Omega_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\displaystyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\textstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{453071}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.19157pt}{$\scriptstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{520837}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle>$}}}}}}
and outside for
\Omega_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\displaystyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\textstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{453071}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.19157pt}{$\scriptstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{520837}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle<$}}}}}}
. Using integration by parts we can express
\tau_{\mathcal{C}}
as

\tau_{\mathcal{C}}[\nu]=\exp\left(x\Omega_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\displaystyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\textstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{453071}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.19157pt}{$\scriptstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{520837}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle>$}}}}}}(0)+\int\limits_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{dq}{2\pi i}\Omega^{\prime}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\displaystyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\textstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{453071}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.19157pt}{$\scriptstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{520837}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle<$}}}}}}(q)\Omega_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\displaystyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\textstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{453071}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.19157pt}{$\scriptstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{520837}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle>$}}}}}}(q)\right).

(40)

For zero winding number we put

and present
\nu(q)

\nu(q)=\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}q^{n}\nu_{n}/(2\pi i)

which is nothing but the strong Szegő formula for the asymptotic of the Toeplitz determinant.

The absolute value in the logarithm reflects that while integrating by parts the obtained expressions should be considered as principal value.

Now let us turn to the case of a non-zero winding number of

w.r.t.
\mathds{S}^{1}
. We focus on the negative values

. To do so we introduce the jump

(43)

(44)

where the integration contours lie slightly above (below) the unit circle for
\omega_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\displaystyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\textstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{453071}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.19157pt}{$\scriptstyle>$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{520837}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle>$}}}}}}

(\omega_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\displaystyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.39435pt}{\resizebox{415990}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.52972pt}{$\textstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{453071}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.19157pt}{$\scriptstyle<$}}}}}{\raisebox{-0.21756pt}{\resizebox{520837}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.13684pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle<$}}}}}})

These functions solve the scalar RHP with the modified jump

6
Summary and outlook

To summarize, we have found closed expressions for the large
x
asymptotic of the finite temperature sine kernel Fredholm determinants.
To address the non-zero winding number we have considered the deformation of the contours on which the original determinant is defined along with the deformation of the kernel that corresponds to the effective form factors approach. To clarify this connection was our original motivation.
In (
C
) we have presented an alternative way, where one keeps the contour, deforms only the kernel, and still gets a completely solvable Riemann-Hilbert problem. In the end, our approach turned out to be similar to the approach in
[
31
]
, but the advantage of our case is that we have found the resolvent explicitly. Notice that we could have considered more general kernels by deforming
v_{\pm}(q)\to v_{\pm}(q)e^{\pm g(q)}
.
Even though the connection with the Toeplitz determinant would be lost in this case, all the procedures for finding the resolvent and asymptotic remain mostly unchanged. However, achieving the full asymptotic expansion might be unreachable because of the properties of the function
g(\lambda)
, which need to be specified case by case.

The variational formula was an important step in our derivation. This, in our opinion, is the best approach to deal with the generic function
\nu(q)
,
while in specific cases the differentiation over particular parameters might be preferable.
