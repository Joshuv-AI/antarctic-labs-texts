Impact of venous thromboembolism on mortality in hospitalized patients with inflammatory bowel disease: analysis of the MIMIC-IV database, 2008 to 2022
Authors: Kuang-Te Wang, Min-I Su, Wei-Ru Chiou, Chuan-Lei Chao, Yi-Fan Huang, Chun-Han Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Background

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), encompassing Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), is characterized by chronic inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract [1]. While the primary manifestations of IBD are localized to the digestive system, a significant proportion of patients experience extraintestinal manifestations (EIMs), affecting various organ systems [2]. EIMs can range from joint and skin conditions to hepatobiliary and ocular disorders [3–5]. Studies suggest that up to 50% of individuals with IBD will develop at least one EIM during their lifetime [2]. Among these, venous thromboembolism (VTE), a serious vascular complication involving blood clot formation, stands out as a major contributor to morbidity and mortality in patients with IBD [2, 6].

Epidemiological data reveal a meaningful association between IBD and VTE. Patients with IBD are at a two- to four-fold increased risk of VTE, including deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), compared to the general population [7–9]. The risk is not limited to venous events, as IBD patients also face an elevated risk of arterial thromboembolic complications, including stroke and mesenteric ischemia [9–11]. The prevalence of thromboembolic events in IBD patients ranges from 1% to 8% in clinical studies, and as high as 3%–41% in post-mortem studies [9, 12]. The pathogenesis of VTE includes chronic inflammation, platelet dysfunction, and acquired risk factors. Active disease and immobility during hospitalization or surgery exacerbate this risk. Recent global burden analyses indicate that overall IBD‑related mortality, although gradually declining over the last two decades, still exceeds that of the general population [13]. Consequently, understanding and mitigating specific lethal complications, most notably VTE, has become a primary objective in contemporary IBD care.

VTE impact in patients with IBD

Several studies have examined the impact of VTE in hospitalized patients with IBD. A study using the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) found that inpatients with IBD have higher odds of DVT, PE, portal vein thrombosis (PVT), and mesenteric ischemia than patients without IBD [14]. Patients with IBD with associated DVT, PE, and mesenteric ischemia had increased colectomy and morbidity rates as well as higher healthcare costs [8, 15]. Another study showed that among hospitalized patients with IBD, the risk of readmission with VTE after discharge is high within 60 days of discharge, suggesting that thromboembolism risk extends beyond hospitalization alone [16].

However, while prior studies highlight the increased risk and adverse outcomes associated with VTE in hospitalized patients with IBD, comprehensive, large-scale studies focusing on the precise mortality rates associated with VTE specifically among hospitalized patients with IBD are lacking. Although some studies report that VTE is associated with significantly increased mortality in hospitalized patients with IBD, more in-depth studies are needed [2]. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate associations between VTE and mortality (in-hospital and overall mortality) in hospitalized patients with IBD to better understand the full impact of thromboembolism on mortality, expected to refine preventative strategies and improve clinical outcomes for this population.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study was conducted using data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) V.3.1 Database, 2008–2022 which is a comprehensive, publicly available critical care dataset that includes data of 364,627 non-institutionalized patients and 546,028 hospitalized patients treated in the Emergency Department (ED) and Intensive Care Units (ICU, CCU) in a single center, Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, Boston, MA, USA, from 2008 to 2022 [17, 18]. The dataset comprises de-identified patient information, including demographic data, vital signs, laboratory test results, medications, and clinical outcomes, making it an invaluable resource for analyzing trends and outcomes in critical care [19].

Study population

Hospitalized adult patients with IBD were included. Diagnosis of IBD was determined using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), 9th and 10th Revisions. Patients with ulcerative colitis were identified using the ICD-10 codes K51, K510x, K512x, K513x, K518x, K519x, and the corresponding ICD-9 codes 556.0, 556.1, 556.2, 556.3, 556.4, 556.5, 556.6, 556.8, and 556.9. ICD-10 codes K50, K500x, K501x, K508x, and K509x were used to identify patients diagnosed with CD, along with ICD-9 codes 555.0, 555.1, 555.2, and 555.9. Data from patients younger than 18 years, those lacking complete hospitalization records, those with missing data or repeated data (e.g., second and third hospitalizations) of those with multiple admissions were excluded in the present study. After exclusions, 2790 patients formed the analytic sample. Figure 1 depicts the patient selection process.

Flowchart of patient selection

Additionally, the database includes out-of-hospital mortality data, enriching the scope of long-term follow-up. Long‑term mortality was ascertained via the Social Security Death Index linkage available in MIMIC‑IV, which captures more than 95% of out‑of‑hospital deaths in the United States, ensuring robust follow‑up. Following the completion of web-based training and the successful completion of the Protecting Human Research Participants examination, author Min-I Su (Record ID: 42219644) obtained the necessary approval to access and extract data from the MIMIC-IV database (https://github.com/MIT-LCP/mimic-iv) [18].

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Mackay Memorial Hospital (Number 24MMHIS460e). The IRB waived informed consent of the included patients because the research involved analysis of anonymized, retrospective data.

Covariates

Patients with VTE were identified using the ICD-10 codes I822, I8221x, I8222x, I8229x, I823, I824, I8240x, I8241x, I8242x, I8243x, I8244x, I8245x, I8246x, I8249x, I824Yx, I824Zx, I825, I8250x, I8251x, I8252x, I8253x, I8254x, I8255x, I8256x I8259x, I825Yx, I825Zx, I826, I8260x, I8261x, I8262x, I827, I8270x, I8271x, I8272x, I82A, I82A1x, I82A2x, I82B, I82B1x, I82B2x, I82C, I82C1x, I82C2x, I828, I8281x, I8289x, and I829x, and the corresponding ICD-9 codes 556.0, 4532, 4533, 4534, 45,340, 45,341, 45,342, 4535, 45,350, 45,351, 45,352, 4536, 4537, 45,371, 45,372, 45,373, 45,374, 45,375, 45,376, 45,377, 45,379, 4538, 45,381, 45,382, 45,383, 45,384, 45,385, 45,386, 45,387, and 45,389. In addition, patients’ demographic data were collected, including age, sex, laboratory tests, and comorbidities. Laboratory tests performed included measurements of white blood cells (WBC), red blood cells (RBC), hemoglobin, platelets, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, sodium, potassium, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine transaminase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP). Comorbidities included VTE, Clostridium difficile infection, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, coronary arterial disease (CAD), stroke, chronic kidney disease (CKD), diabetes mellitus (DM), myocardial infarct (MI), congestive heart failure (CHF), peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, chronic pulmonary disease, rheumatic disease, renal disease, cancer and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) scores. Medications, including corticosteroids (Prednisolone, Methylprednisolone, Prednisone or Budesonide ER [UCERIS]), biologics (Infliximab, Adalimumab [Humira], Cimzia, Golimumab [Simponi], Vedolizumab [Entyvio], Natalizumab [Tysabri], Ustekinumab [Stelara], Risankizumab [Skyrizi] or Upadacitinib[Rinvoq]), immunomodulators (Sulfasalazine, Pentasa, Azathioprine, Mercaptopurine or Cyclosporine) and anticoagulants (Warfarin, Coumadin, Dabigatran, Pradaxa, Rivaroxaban, Xarelto, Apixaban, Edoxaban or Savaysa), were also assessed.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as means and standard deviation (SD). Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages (%). The χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used to test comparisons between groups for categorical variables, whereas the students’ test was used for comparisons of continuous variables. In-hospital mortality and long-term mortality were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier survival curves. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used for univariate and multivariable analyses. Models were constructed to explore associations between VTE and mortality. Model 1 was adjusted for sex and age. Model 2 was adjusted for demographic factors, including sex, age, laboratory results (ALT, ALP, AST, hemoglobin, WBC, RBC, sodium, potassium, creatinine, platelets) and CCI scores. Model 3 was adjusted for Clostridium difficile infection, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, CAD, stroke, CKD, DM, myocardial infarct (MI), CHF, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, chronic pulmonary disease, rheumatic disease, renal disease, and malignant cancer. The proportional hazards assumption for the Cox regression models was tested using Schoenfeld residuals. A p-value greater than 0.05 indicated that the assumption was met. All statistical analyses were performed using R (V.4.2.3) and SPSS (V.20). A threshold of p < 0.05 (two sided) was established as statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics and comparisons between VTE and non-VTE groups

This study included 2,790 hospitalized adult patients with IBD, including only first admissions. During the follow-up period of 696.27 ± 1126.29 days, the mean duration of total hospital stays for the whole study population was 7.33 ± 9.93 days (Table 1). Table 1 also presents the baseline characteristics of the study population, grouped by VTE status (with/without). Patients’ mean age was 52.62 years (SD 19.23), and 47.46% were male. Patients in the VTE group were significantly older than those in the non-VTE group (60.66 vs. 52.26, P < 0.001). Additionally, the VTE group had significantly lower hemoglobin values (10.99 vs. 12.2 g/dL, P < 0.001), red blood cell count (3.81 vs. 4.19 K/uL, P < 0.001), and serum sodium concentration (136.51 vs. 137.78 mEq/L, P = 0.012), while their Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) scores were higher (3.79 vs. 2.4, P < 0.001) than those of the non-VTE patients.

Regarding comorbidities, the VTE group had a significantly higher prevalence of CAD (18.8% vs. 10.66%, P = 0.010), stroke (6.84% vs. 1.53%, P = 0.001), cerebrovascular disease (12.82% vs. 3.89%, P < 0.001), and cancer (19.66% vs. 6.36%, P < 0.001).

The VTE group had a significantly longer median hospital stay (15.81 vs. 6.96 days, P < 0.001). and markedly higher in-hospital mortality and overall mortality (34.19% vs. 16.01%, P < 0.001) than did the non-VTE group (10.26% vs. 2.28%, P < 0.001).

Survival analysis and risk assessment

Figures 2 and 3 display Kaplan–Meier survival curves, showing that patients with IBD who have VTE have a significantly higher mortality risk compared to those without VTE. Cox regression analysis (Table 2) demonstrated that VTE was significantly associated with increased in-hospital mortality (HR 4.013, 95% CI 2.160–7.454, P < 0.001) and overall mortality (HR 2.471, 95% CI 1.786–3.419, P < 0.001). Even after adjusting for potential confounders—including sex, age, liver function enzymes (ALT, ALP, AST), hematological parameters (hemoglobin, WBC, RBC, platelets) and biochemistries (serum sodium, potassium, creatinine), CCI scores, and comorbidities (Clostridium difficile infection, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, CAD, stroke, CKD, DM, MI, CHF, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, chronic pulmonary disease, rheumatic disease, renal disease, and cancer)—VTE remained a significant predictor of both in-hospital mortality (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 2.205, 95% CI 1.127–4.315, P = 0.021) and overall mortality (aHR 1.758, 95% CI 1.270–2.433, P = 0.001). Subsequent analysis of Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs) for all covariates showed that all values were well below the conventional cutoff of 10, ranging from 1.03 to 6.336. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed for all Cox models using Schoenfeld residuals. The assumption was satisfied for both in-hospital mortality (P = 0.382) and long-term mortality (P = 0.149) (Fig. S1), indicating the models were appropriate for the data. The survival curves diverged within the first 30 days and continued to separate over the three‑year follow‑up, highlighting a sustained long‑term impact of VTE.

Discussion

The present study is the first to examine the impact of VTE on both in-hospital and post-discharge long-term mortality in a large, national database of inpatients with IBD. While mortality remains a critical outcome to monitor in hospitalized patients with IBD, predicting it accurately has been a persistent challenge, primarily due to its relatively low prevalence in this population [6], in which low incidence complicates the development of robust predictive models and may result in underestimating the impact of certain risk factors [6].

Previous studies have sought to identify key predictors of in-hospital mortality in inpatients with IBD, typically using conventional logistic regression [6, 15]. Several consistent risk factors have emerged from those previous studies, including that increasing age is a significant predictor of mortality [6, 20]; Also, frailty—defined as a state of increased vulnerability to stressors—is shown to independently predict adverse outcomes, including in-hospital mortality and readmission [6, 20]. Furthermore, the requirement for IBD-related surgery during hospitalization has been associated with increased mortality, likely reflecting the complexity and severity of such cases [6, 20]. Likewise, sepsis and septic shock—systemic inflammatory responses to infection—are well-established predictors of mortality, underscoring the increased susceptibility of IBD patients to infectious complications [20, 21]. Malnutrition, common in IBD due to chronic inflammation and malabsorption, further exacerbates the risk of adverse outcomes [6]. Moreover, UC has been linked to a higher inpatient mortality rate than CD in some cohorts, although these findings are context dependent [22]. In addition to general risk factors, specific clinical conditions and complications have been associated with higher mortality rates in hospitalized patients with IBD. Both venous and arterial thromboembolism have emerged as critical contributors to mortality in this population [7, 21].

The pathogenesis of VTE and arterial thromboembolism in IBD is complex and multifactorial. The primary mechanisms driving this association include chronic inflammation, platelet dysfunction, and acquired risk factors. Chronic inflammation disrupts hemostatic balance by upregulating procoagulant factors (e.g., fibrinogen, factors V, VII, VIII, X, XI, XII) [11, 23, 24] and reducing fibrinolytic activity through decreased tissue plasminogen activator and increased plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 [11, 24]. It also promotes procoagulant microparticles and tissue factor release, further increasing the prothrombotic tendency [24]. Platelet dysfunction in IBD is associated with quantitative and qualitative platelet abnormalities. Patients often exhibit thrombocytosis and hyperactive platelets with increased aggregation, adhesion, and elevated markers, including P-selectin and CD40L [23, 25]. Platelet-leukocyte aggregates also form more frequently in IBD patients, contributing further to clot formation [24]. These mechanisms, combined with acquired risk factors such as fluid depletion, smoking, hypertension, metabolic syndrome, immobility, surgery, and hospitalization, significantly heighten the risk of VTE, which, in turn, contributes substantially to patient mortality [1, 2, 7–9, 25, 26]. Importantly, massive PE and disseminated microthrombi can precipitate acute right‑heart failure and multi‑organ dysfunction, thereby translating the prothrombotic setting into excess mortality. Potential intervention points along this cascade include aggressive anti‑inflammatory therapy, early patient mobilization, and prompt pharmacologic prophylaxis.

Results of the present study show VTE to be a strong and independent predictor of both in-hospital and long-term mortality. Although we did not specifically examine whether VTE directly caused mortality, the statistical association between the presence of VTE and increased mortality rates is strong, emphasizing the importance of VTE management in hospitalized patients and the potential need for intensive monitoring consistent with a growing body of literature demonstrating the association between IBD and thromboembolic events [1, 8, 14, 21–23, 27]. Hospitalization and surgery further exacerbate these prothrombotic conditions due to immobilization, tissue trauma, and increased inflammatory burden.

Zhang et al. [28] develop machine learning (ML)-based prediction model for risk stratification of 1-year mortality in ICU patients admitted to IBD who extracted from the MIMIC-IV database. Furthermore, previous studies [21, 22] reported a 2.1-fold (odds ratio) increase in in-hospital mortality among hospitalized IBD patients with VTE compared to non-IBD patients with VTE, even after adjusting for confounders. The present study expanded these findings and further demonstrated that VTE was identified as an independent predictor of both in-hospital mortality (aHR 2.205) and long-term mortality (aHR 1.758) over a median follow-up period of 696.27 days, indicating that VTE impacts the mortality of IBD patients not only during hospitalization but also after discharge. This underscores the prognostic importance of VTE in this high-risk hospitalized population with IBD.

Clinical guidelines have recognized the elevated VTE risk in hospitalized IBD patients and strongly advocate for thromboprophylaxis. Protective effects of long-term anticoagulation and antiplatelets have been demonstrated in terms of mortality and healthcare utilization among patients with IBD have been shown in a retrospective study [29]. Several consensus statements emphasize that IBD patients face a significantly greater risk of VTE than individuals without IBD [7, 8, 21, 27]. During acute flares, this risk is even more pronounced, and hospitalization further amplifies the danger—some studies estimate a 6-fold increase in VTE risk during hospitalized flares compared to nonhospitalized ones [7, 22, 27, 30]. However, despite reasonable awareness of the increased risk of thrombosis in hospitalized IBD patients, many gastroenterologists may not use pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis. A nationwide survey in the United States showed that only 34.6% physicians would give pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis to a hospitalized patient with ulcerative colitis [31, 32], underscoring a substantial care gap.

Given the risk of VTE-related in-hospital mortality and long-term mortality in hospitalized IBD patients, we strongly recommend routine pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis in hospitalized IBD patients, particularly during acute disease flares or in the perioperative setting [1, 8, 27]. The studies also suggest pharmacologic prophylaxis with low-molecular-weight heparin, low-dose unfractionated heparin, or fondaparinux in patients hospitalized with IBD for acute illness or major surgery, regardless of the reason for admission [1, 7, 14, 27]. For those with moderate to severe flares and no significant bleeding, anticoagulant thromboprophylaxis is strongly recommended. Even in cases of nonsevere gastrointestinal bleeding, the continued use of anticoagulants is supported, acknowledging the high baseline thrombotic risk in this population [1, 27, 31, 32]. Nguyen et al. [33] suggest that for IBD patients with unprovoked VTE, the advantages of prolonged anticoagulation in preventing recurrent VTE surpass the risks of bleeding complications [33]. Furthermore, a recent international consensus statement emphasized the need for extended prophylaxis post-discharge in selected high-risk patients, though this approach should be individualized based on risk stratification tools incorporating factors such as frailty, prior VTE, active disease, and ongoing immobility [34]. Specifically, extended anticoagulation might be more suitable for patients who experienced VTE without active disease or other temporary triggering factors [33].

Of note, the association between VTE and mortality is especially significant in older patients, those with more comorbid burden, lower levels of hemoglobin and sodium, and those with concomitant or cardiovascular disease. Although the retrospective nature of this study limits the ability to establish a definitive cause-and-effect relationship, these findings could have potential implications for clinical practice. Firstly, conducting an early risk assessment upon admission—considering factors like age, existing health conditions, cancer history, lab results, and mobility—can help pinpoint patients who might benefit from medication-based thromboprophylaxis, along with mechanical measures where suitable. It is recommended to evaluate the risks of clotting and bleeding daily to maintain safety. Secondly, because VTE is associated with increased long-term mortality, extended preventive treatment after hospital discharge may be appropriate for certain high-risk groups, such as individuals with a history of VTE, active malignancy, persistent immobility, or multiple comorbidities. However, additional randomized controlled trials are needed to provide definitive recommendations. From a health-economic standpoint, even though we did not perform a direct cost-effectiveness analysis, patients with VTE had longer hospitalizations and increased mortality, implying that effective prevention could both enhance patient outcomes and decrease healthcare resource utilization. Future studies incorporating formal cost-effectiveness analyses and prospective evaluation of risk-stratified prophylaxis strategies will be needed to confirm these potential benefits and inform guideline development.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations to the interpretation of results. First, the single-center (patients are all from the emergency department and intensive care units of Beth Isreal-Deaconess Medical Center, Boston, MA, USA), retrospective study design and reliance on the MIMIC-IV database alone supports generalization of results to the MIMIC population but may restrict the broader applicability of the findings to other populations, either institutionally or geographically. Secondly, certain patient data were missing in the database, increasing the potential for selection bias in the data collection and analysis and the inability to account for all possible confounding factors. Thirdly, despite the use of various models to adjust for confounders, unidentified or unmeasured variables associated with mortality rates may still exist, necessitating cautious interpretation of the findings. Furthermore, detailed information for the disease activity of IBD (e.g., Crohn’s Disease Activity Index, Ulcerative Colitis Endoscopic Index of Severity, Mayo Clinic Score for Ulcerative Colitis, active inflammation and hypercoagulability) was not available in the database; however, these factors may drive VTE risk in IBD patients [35, 36]. These scoring systems are crucial to predicting mortality in patients with IBD but were not used in the MIMIC-IV survey so could not be considered in this study. Fourth, reliance on administrative ICD codes without confirmatory imaging introduces the possibility of misclassification of both IBD and VTE events. Frailty has been described as an important risk factor for mortality among IBD patients; however, we could not assess patients’ frailty via this database. Fifth, the MIMIC database is unable to distinguish between pre-hospitalization and post-hospitalization VTE so could not determine that neither pre-hospitalization VTE or post-hospitalization VTE is associated with an increased risk of mortality. Lastly, information regarding the timing and dosage of pharmacologic treatment for either IBD or VTE was inconsistent from patient to patient in this database, so it was not evaluated.

Conclusion

Findings of the present study affirm that VTE is a significant and independent predictor of mortality in hospitalized IBD patients, regardless of the presence of other comorbidities. The presence of VTE correlates significantly not just with short-term in-hospital mortality but also with long-term mortality post-discharge, indicating the need for prolonged, individualized care strategies to mitigate long-term risks. Future prospective studies are still needed to evaluate risk‑stratified, extended‑duration prophylaxis regimens and determine the safety–efficacy balance of novel oral anticoagulants in patients with IBD in the post‑discharge setting.
