The Symmetry Coefficient of Positively Homogeneous Functions
Max Nilsson ⋆, Pontus Giselsson ⋆

Abstract
The Bregman distance is a central tool in convex optimization, particularly in first-order gradient descent and proximal-based algorithms. Such methods enable optimization of functions without Lipschitz continuous gradients by leveraging the concept of relative smoothness, with respect to a reference function h h . A key factor in determining the full range of allowed step sizes in Bregman schemes is the symmetry coefficient, α ⁡ ( h ) \alpha(h) , of the reference function h h . While some explicit values of α ⁡ ( h ) \alpha(h) have been determined for specific functions h h , a general characterization has remained elusive. This paper explores two problems: ( i ) deriving calculus rules for the symmetry coefficient and ( ii ) computing α ⁡ ( ‖ ⋅ ‖ 2 p ) \alpha(\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}^{p}) for general p p . We establish upper and lower bounds for the symmetry coefficient of sums of positively homogeneous Legendre functions and, under certain conditions, provide exact formulas for these sums. Furthermore, we demonstrate that α ⁡ ( ‖ ⋅ ‖ 2 p ) \alpha(\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}^{p}) is independent of dimension and propose an efficient algorithm for its computation. Additionally, we prove that α ⁡ ( ‖ ⋅ ‖ 2 p ) \alpha(\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}^{p}) asymptotically equals, and is lower bounded by, the function 1 / ( 2 ​ p ) 1/(2p) , offering a simpler upper bound for step sizes in Bregman schemes. Finally, we present closed-form computations for specific cases such as p ∈ { 6 , 8 , 10 } p\in\{6,8,10\} .

Notation and Definitions

The notation we use is standard and follows for example
[
21
]
or
[
5
]
.

Let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the scalar product on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, given by
\langle x,y\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}
denote the set of symmetric positive definite matrices of size
n\times n
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
,
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
Y
equal either
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
or
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to Y
is said to be
positively homogeneous of degree

p

over

S
if
f(\lambda x)=\lambda^{p}f(x)
holds for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
and all
x\in S
such that
\lambda x\in S
. When
S=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the function
f
is simply said to be
positively homogeneous of degree

p
.

Given a function
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
, the
effective domain
of
h
is the set
\dom h\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid h(x)<\infty\}
. A proper and convex function
h
is said to be
essentially smooth

[
21
, Section 26]
if the following three properties hold:

(i)

S\coloneq\Int(\dom h)
is non-empty,

(ii)

h
is differentiable on
S
,

(iii)

for all sequences
\left\{x^{(i)}\right\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
in
S
converging to a point of
\bdry S
, then
\lim_{i\to\infty}\left\lVert\nabla h\left(x^{(i)}\right)\right\rVert_{2}=\infty
.

Equivalently, if
h
is proper, closed and convex, then
h
is essentially smooth if and only if
\partial h
is a single-valued mapping
[
21
, Theorem 26.1]
, i.e., if
\partial h(x)
contains at most one element for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The function
h
is said to be a
Legendre function

[
21
, Section 26]
if
h
is a proper, closed, and convex function that is strictly convex on
\Int(\dom h)
and essentially smooth. Recall that, by
[
21
, Theorem 26.5]
,
h
is a Legendre function if and only if
h^{*}
is a Legendre function.

Given a function
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
that is differentiable on
\Int(\dom h)
, the
Bregman distance

D_{h}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is defined as

D_{h}(x,y)\coloneq\begin{cases}h(x)-h(y)-\langle\nabla h(y),x-y\rangle&\text{if }y\in\Int(\dom h),\\
\infty&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

for all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Note that when
h
is a Legendre function, then
D_{h}(x,y)>0
holds for all
x,y\in\Int(\dom h)
such that
x\neq y
, and if
D_{h}(x,y)=0
holds for some
x,y\in\Int(\dom h)
, then
x=y
.

The
symmetry coefficient
of a Legendre function
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is defined as

\alpha(h)\coloneq\inf\left\{\left.\frac{D_{h}(x,y)}{D_{h}(y,x)}\right|x,y\in\Int(\dom h)\text{ and }x\neq y\right\}.

(1)

Since the symmetry coefficient is only defined for Legendre functions, essential smoothness implies that there exists points
x,y\in\Int(\dom h)
such that
x\neq y
, and so the infimum in (
1
) ranges over a non-empty set. Furthermore, the strict convexity of
h
over
\Int(\dom h)
implies that both the numerator and denominator in (
1
) are always strictly positive.

We will refer to the property
\alpha(h)=1
as
h
having
perfect symmetry
, following
[
2
]
.

Preliminaries

The following proposition collects some basic operations under which the symmetry coefficient is preserved. This result will be used multiple times throughout this paper. Note that the property
\alpha(h)=\alpha(h^{*})\in[0,1]
was shown in
[
2
]
.

Proposition 2.1
:

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a Legendre function and let
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
and
L\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be nonsingular. Then,

\alpha(h)=\alpha(h^{*})=\alpha(h+\langle b,\cdot\rangle+c)=\alpha(h(\cdot-x_{0}))=\alpha(\lambda h)=\alpha(h\circ L)\in[0,1].

(2)

Proof.

For the first equality, see
[
2
, Section 2.3]
. For the rest of the equalities, let us define
\hat{h}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
by

\hat{h}(x)\coloneq\lambda h(Lx-x_{0})+\langle b,x\rangle+c

(3)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
L
is nonsingular, the mapping
\phi:\Int(\dom\hat{h})\to\Int(\dom h)
, defined by
\phi(x)\coloneqq Lx-x_{0}
for all
x\in\Int(\dom\hat{h})
, is bijective. Since
h
is a Legendre function, we get that
\hat{h}
is a proper, closed, and convex function that is strictly convex on
\Int(\dom\hat{h})
. Moreover, since
L
is nonsingular, we get from subdifferential calculus that
\partial{\hat{h}}(x)=\lambda L^{\top}\partial\hat{h}(Lx-x_{0})+b
holds for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Therefore,
\partial\hat{h}
is a single-valued mapping, which in turn implies that
\hat{h}
is essentially smooth
[
21
, Theorem 26.1]
. All in all,
\hat{h}
is a Legendre function. Furthermore,

\displaystyle D_{\hat{h}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\hat{h}(x)-\hat{h}(y)-\langle\nabla\hat{h}(y),x-y\rangle

\displaystyle=\lambda h(Lx-x_{0})-\lambda h(Ly-x_{0})+\langle b,x-y\rangle-\langle\lambda L^{\top}\nabla h(Ly-x_{0})+b,x-y\rangle

\displaystyle=\lambda\bigl(h(Lx-x_{0})-h(Ly-x_{0})-\langle\nabla h(Ly-x_{0}),(Lx-x_{0})-(Ly-x_{0})\rangle\bigr)

\displaystyle=\lambda D_{h}(\phi(x),\phi(y))

holds for all
x,y\in\Int(\dom\hat{h})
. Therefore,
\alpha(\hat{h})=\alpha(h)
and the rest of the equalities in (
2
) hold. Finally, the inclusion in (
2
) follows since
\min\{D_{h}(x,y)/D_{h}(y,x),D_{h}(y,x)/D_{h}(x,y)\}\in(0,1]
holds for all
x,y\in\Int(\dom h)
such that
x\neq y
.
∎

The following proposition provides a general lower bound of the symmetry coefficient of
h\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{m}h_{i}
, as referenced in
C4
. It is general in the sense that we only assume that the functions
h_{i}
are Legendre. The lower bound, given by (
4
), will be central in proving
Proposition

3.4
,
Theorem

4.1
and
Theorem

5.2
. In the latter two results, the symmetry coefficient will actually coincide with this lower bound:
\min_{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}}\left\{\alpha(h_{i})\right\}
.

Proposition 2.2
:

Let
h_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a Legendre function for each
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
and suppose that
\cap_{i=1}^{m}\Int(\dom h_{i})
is non-empty. Then,
h\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{m}h_{i}
is a Legendre function and

\alpha(h)\in\left[\min_{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}}\left\{\alpha(h_{i})\right\},1\right].

(4)

Proof.

Since
\cap_{i=1}^{m}\Int(\dom h_{i})
is non-empty,
[
21
, Theorem 6.5]
implies that
\Int(\dom h)=\cap_{i=1}^{m}\Int(\dom h_{i})
, after identifying the relative interior of
\dom h
as simply the interior of
\dom h
. Therefore,
h
is proper, closed, and strictly convex on
\Int(\dom h)
. By
[
21
, Theorem 23.8]
,
\partial h=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\partial h_{i}
and so
\partial h
is a single-valued mapping, which implies that
h
is essentially smooth
[
21
, Theorem 26.1]
. Therefore,
h
is a Legendre function.

Let
x,y\in\Int(\dom h)=\cap_{i=1}^{m}\Int(\dom h_{i})
such that
x\neq y
, then

\frac{D_{h}(x,y)}{D_{h}(y,x)}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}D_{h_{i}}(x,y)}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}D_{h_{i}}(y,x)}.

For each
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
we have, by definition, that
D_{h_{j}}(x,y)/D_{h_{j}}(y,x)\geq\alpha(h_{j})\geq\min_{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}}\left\{\alpha(h_{i})\right\}
and so

\sum_{j=1}^{m}D_{h_{j}}(x,y)\geq\sum_{j=1}^{m}D_{h_{j}}(y,x)\min_{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}}\left\{\alpha(h_{i})\right\}.

By
Proposition

2.1
,
\alpha(h)\in[0,1]
, therefore (
4
) holds.
∎

If
h
is separable, i.e., if
h(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}h_{i}(x_{i})
holds for all
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, then
Proposition

2.2
can be refined.

Proposition 2.3
:

Let
h_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a Legendre function for each
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be defined by
h(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}h_{i}(x_{i})
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Then,
h
is a Legendre function and

\alpha(h)=\min_{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}}\{\alpha(h_{i})\}.

Proof.

One could easily show that the function
h
is proper, closed, and strictly convex on
\Int(\dom h)=\Pi_{i=1}^{m}\Int(\dom h_{i}).
Since
\partial h=\Pi_{i=1}^{m}\partial h_{i}
, the mapping
\partial h
is single-valued which implies that
h
is essentially smooth
[
21
, Theorem 26.1]
. Therefore,
h
is a Legendre function.

Let
x,y\in\Int(\dom h)
such that
x\neq y
. Let
\mathcal{J}\coloneq\{j\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}\mid x_{j}\neq y_{j}\}
. By assumption,
\mathcal{J}
is non-empty. For each
j\in\mathcal{J}
we have that
D_{h_{j}}(x_{j},y_{j})/D_{h_{j}}(y_{j},x_{j})\geq\alpha(h_{j})\geq\min_{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}}\left\{\alpha(h_{i})\right\}
and so

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}D_{h_{j}}(x_{j},y_{j})\geq\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}D_{h_{j}}(y_{j},x_{j})\min_{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}}\left\{\alpha(h_{i})\right\}

which implies that

\displaystyle\frac{D_{h}(x,y)}{D_{h}(y,x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}D_{h_{i}}(x_{i},y_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}D_{h_{i}}(y_{i},x_{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}D_{h_{j}}(x_{j},y_{j})}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}D_{h_{j}}(y_{j},x_{j})}\geq\min_{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}}\left\{\alpha(h_{i})\right\}.

It remains to show that
\alpha(h)\leq\min_{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}}\{\alpha(h_{i})\}
. Let
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
be arbitrary. Let
x,y\in\Int(\dom h)
such that
x_{j}\neq y_{j}
and
x_{i}=y_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}\setminus\{j\}
. We then have that

D_{h}(x,y)/D_{h}(y,x)=D_{h_{j}}(x_{j},y_{j})/D_{h_{j}}(y_{j},x_{j}).

Therefore,
\alpha(h)\leq\alpha(h_{j})
and since
j
was arbitrary we are done.
∎

R1

If
h
is Legendre and positive homogeneous of degree
p>1
over
\Int(\dom h)

\implies\dom h=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

A function
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
can be Legendre
or
positively homogeneous of degree
p>1
over
\Int(\dom h)
, without necessarily having full effective domain. But if
h
is both Legendre and positively homogeneous, then we can show that
\dom h=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Furthermore, in that case, the functions
h,\nabla h,
and
D_{h}
can be shown to be positively homogeneous of degree
p,p-1,
and
p
, respectively, see
Lemma

3.2
.

R2

The function
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is a strictly convex quadratic function with
\dom h=\mathbb{R}^{n}

\iff

\alpha(h)=1
.

Of particular interest is the left implication, since the right implication is a straightforward calculation. A variant of this result has been shown for the special case when
n=1
,
\dom h=\mathbb{R}_{++}
, and
h
assumed to be twice continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{R}_{++}
in
[
6
]
. In our argument (see the proof of
Proposition

3.3
) we do not put any such extraneous assumptions on
h
. In fact, our argument assumes only that
h
is a Legendre function.

R3

If
h_{i}
is positively homogeneous of degree
p_{i}>1
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, where the degrees satisfy
p_{1}<p_{2}\leq\dots\leq p_{m-1}<p_{m}
, then
\alpha(h)\leq\min\{\alpha(h_{1}),\alpha(h_{m})\}
.

This will be proved, using
Lemma

3.2
, in
Proposition

3.4
. In the coming section, we will frequently combine this upper bound with the lower bound of
Proposition

2.2
, in order to fully determine the symmetry coefficient for some special cases.

The Domains of Positively Homogeneous Legendre Functions (R1)

We turn to proving
R1
, which will be done in
Proposition

3.1
. Before that, we provide two lemmas:
Lemma

3.1
and
Lemma

3.2
. The former does not assume that the function
h
is Legendre and describes how the positive homogeneity of
h
over a set
S
implies positive homogeneity of
\nabla h
and
D_{h}
over
S
and
S\times S
, respectively. The latter does assume that
h
is Legendre and positive homogeneous over
\Int(\dom h)
and describes how this implies that
h
must have full domain. These will be combined to show
Proposition

3.1
.

Lemma 3.1
:

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be differentiable on an open set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Suppose that
h
is positively homogeneous of degree
p
over
S
, then
\nabla h
is positively homogeneous of degree
p-1
over
S
and
D_{h}
is positively homogeneous of degree
p
over
S\times S
.

Proof.

If
S=\emptyset
then the statement is vacuously true. Otherwise, let
x\in S
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
be such that
\lambda x\in S
. The function
h\circ(\lambda\Id)
is differentiable at
x
, and after differentiating we get
\lambda\nabla h(\lambda x)=\lambda^{p}\nabla h(x)
which gives that
\nabla h
is positively homogeneous of degree
p-1
over
S
.

Let also
y\in S
such that
\lambda y\in S
. Then

\displaystyle D_{h}(\lambda x,\lambda y)

\displaystyle=h(\lambda x)-h(\lambda y)-\langle\nabla h(\lambda y),\lambda x-\lambda y\rangle

\displaystyle=\lambda^{p}h(x)-\lambda^{p}h(y)-\lambda^{p}\langle\nabla h(y),x-y\rangle=\lambda^{p}D_{h}(x,y)

and so
D_{h}
is positively homogeneous of degree
p
over
S\times S
.

∎

Lemma 3.2
:

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a Legendre function. Suppose that
h
is positively homogeneous of degree
p
over
\Int(\dom h)
, then
\dom h=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\Int(\dom h)
and define
t_{0}
as the infimum over the non-empty interval

C\coloneqq\{t\in\mathbb{R}_{++}\mid tx\in\Int(\dom h)\}.

Since
\Int(\dom h)
is open,
t_{0}<1
. By
[
21
, Theorem 6.1]
,
\lambda x\in\Int(\dom h)
holds for all
\lambda\in(t_{0},1)
and so
t_{0}x\in\Cl(\dom h)
. By
Lemma

3.1
,
\left\lVert\nabla h(\lambda x)\right\rVert_{2}=\lambda^{p-1}\left\lVert\nabla h(x)\right\rVert_{2}
holds for all
\lambda\in(t_{0},1)
. Therefore,
\lim_{\lambda\downarrow t_{0}}\left\lVert\nabla h(\lambda x)\right\rVert_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and by the relative smoothness of
h
we have that
t_{0}x\not\in\bdry(\Int(\dom h))
. However, since
t_{0}x\in\Cl(\dom h)
we get that
t_{0}x\in\Int(\dom h)
. The only way this can happen is if
t_{0}=0
, since otherwise
t_{0}
would not be the infimum over
C
. In a similar way, the supremum of
C
is
\infty
. Therefore, if
x\in\Int(\dom h)
then
\lambda x\in\Int(\dom h)
holds for all
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
. In particular, we conclude that
0\in\Int(\dom h)
.

Now for any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
take
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
small enough such that
\lambda y\in\Int(\dom h)
. Therefore,
\lambda^{-1}\lambda y=y\in\Int(\dom h)
and
\dom h=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
∎

Proposition 3.1
:

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a Legendre function. Suppose that
h
is positively homogeneous of degree
p
over
\Int(\dom h)
. Then,
h
is differentiable, and
h,\nabla h
, and
D_{h}
are positively homogeneous of degree
p,p-1
, and
p
, respectively.

Proof.

From
Lemma

3.2
, we get that
\dom h=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and so
h
is differentiable on
\Int(\dom h)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The conclusion now follows from letting
S=\mathbb{R}^{n}
in
Lemma

3.1
.
∎

Perfect Symmetry (R2)

We turn to proving
R2
, which will be done in
Proposition

3.3
. Before that, let us take a moment to motivate why it was natural to assume that
p>1
in
Lemma

3.1
,
Lemma

3.2
and
Proposition

3.1
.

Proposition 3.2
:

Let
p\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
and let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a Legendre function. Suppose that
h
is positively homogeneous of degree
p
over
\Int(\dom h)
. Then,
p\neq 1
.
Furthermore,

(i)

if
p\in(0,1)
, then
\alpha(h)=0
,

(ii)

if
p\in(1,2)
, then there exists a Legendre function
\hat{h}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
which is positively homogeneous of degree
q\in(2,\infty)
such that
\alpha(h)=\alpha(\hat{h})
.

Proof.

Suppose that
p=1
. Let
x\in\Int(\dom h)
and take
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{++}\setminus\{1\}
such that
\lambda x\in\Int(\dom h)
. This can be done since
\Int(\dom h)
is non-empty and open. Then, for any
\theta\in(0,1)
we get that

h((1-\theta)x+\theta\lambda x)=h((1-\theta+\theta\lambda)x)=(1-\theta)h(x)+\theta h(\lambda x),

(5)

where we in the second equality used that
1-\theta+\theta\lambda>0
and
h
is positively homogeneous of degree
1
. Clearly, (
5
) contradicts the strict convexity of
h
over
\Int(\dom h)
and so
p\neq 1
follows.

(i)

Suppose now that
p\in(0,1)
. Let
x\in\Int(\dom h)
and follow the argument as in
Lemma

3.2
to get that
\lambda x\in\Int(\dom h)
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
. The only difference is that
\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\left\lVert\nabla h(\lambda x)\right\rVert_{2}=\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\lambda^{p-1}\left\lVert\nabla h(x)\right\rVert_{2}=\infty
and so
0\notin\Int(\dom h)
. Since
h
is closed,
h(0)=\liminf_{y\to 0}h(y)\leq\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}h(\lambda x)=\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\lambda^{p}h(x)=0
and so
0\in\dom h
. In particular, this implies that
\dom h
is not open and by
[
2
, Proposition 2]
we have that
\alpha(h)=0
.

(ii)

By
Lemma

3.2
, we have that
\dom h=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By
[
21
, Theorem 26.5, Corollary 15.3.1]
, the conjugate function
h^{*}
is Legendre and is positively homogeneous of degree
q=p/(p-1)\in(2,\infty)
, therefore the statement holds with
\hat{h}\coloneqq h^{*}
.

∎

As
Proposition

3.2
shows, we can often assume that the degree of positive homogeneity
p
is greater than or equal to
2
. For instance, if
p\in(1,2)
then when analyzing the symmetry coefficient of
\frac{1}{p}|\cdot|^{p}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, we can just as well analyze the symmetry coefficient of
\frac{1}{q}|\cdot|^{q}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
1/p+1/q=1
and
q\in(2,\infty)
, since
\left(\frac{1}{p}|\cdot|^{p}\right)^{*}=\frac{1}{q}|\cdot|^{q}

[
21
, Section 12]
. This is precisely what we will do in
Sections

4
and
5
.

Let us now concentrate on the case
p=2
, covered in
Proposition

3.3
. First, let us recall in
Theorem

3.1
the converse of Euler’s Theorem for homogeneous functions. This theorem is classical, but we include a proof for the sake of completion. The proof idea comes from
[
1
, Exercise 9.3.9]
.

Theorem 3.1

(Converse of Euler’s Theorem for Homogeneous Functions)
:

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be differentiable on
\Int(\dom h)
. Suppose that
\Int(\dom h)
is a convex set and that
ph(x)=\langle\nabla h(x),x\rangle
holds for all
x\in\Int(\dom h)
, then
h
is positively homogeneous of degree
p
over
\Int(\dom h)
.

Proof.

If
\Int(\dom h)=\emptyset
then the statement is vacuously true. Otherwise, fix
x\in\Int(\dom h)
. Define
\phi:(t_{0},t_{1})\to\mathbb{R}
by
\phi(t)=h(tx)-t^{p}h(x)
for all
t\in(t_{0},t_{1})
, where
t_{0}
and
t_{1}
is the infimum and supremum of the non-empty set
S\coloneqq\{t\in\mathbb{R}_{++}\mid tx\in\Int(\dom h)\}
, respectively. Since
\Int(\dom h)
is open,
t_{0}<t_{1}
and
1\in(t_{0},t_{1})
. Since
S
is a convex set,
(t_{0},t_{1})\subset S
. Therefore,
\phi
is differentiable on
(t_{0},t_{1})
, and

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla h(tx),x\rangle-pt^{p-1}h(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\langle\nabla h(tx),tx\rangle-pt^{p-1}h(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}ph(tx)-pt^{p-1}h(x)=\frac{p}{t}\phi(t)

holds for all
t\in(t_{0},t_{1})
. This differential equation has the solution
\phi(t)=Ct^{p}
, for any
C\in\mathbb{R}
. The condition
\phi(1)=0
gives that
C=0
and so it follows that
h
is positively homogeneous of degree
p
over
\Int(\dom h)
.
∎

Proposition 3.3
:

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a Legendre function. Then
\alpha(h)=1
if and only if
h
is a strictly convex quadratic function with
\dom h=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
h
is a strictly convex quadratic with
\dom h=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e., that there exists some
Q\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}
,
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that

h(x)=\frac{1}{2}\langle x,Qx\rangle+\langle b,x\rangle+c

holds for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By
Proposition

2.1
, the symmetry coefficient of
h
equals the symmetry coefficient of
\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}^{2}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, which has the associated Bregman distance

\displaystyle D_{\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\langle y,x-y\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
D_{\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}
is symmetric about its arguments, we get from (
1
) that
\alpha\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)=1
.

Now, suppose that
\alpha(h)=1
. By
Proposition

2.1
, we may without loss of generality suppose that
0\in\Int\dom h
,
h(0)=0
, and
\nabla h(0)=0
. We aim to show that there exists some
Q\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}
such that
h(x)=\frac{1}{2}\langle x,Qx\rangle
holds for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By assumption,

D_{h}(x,y)=D_{h}(y,x)

(6)

holds for all
x,y\in\Int(\dom h)
. We get that

\displaystyle 2h(x)-\langle\nabla h(x),x\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(h(x)-h(0)-\langle\nabla h(0),x\rangle\right)-\left(h(0)-h(x)-\langle\nabla h(x),-x\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=D_{h}(x,0)-D_{h}(0,x)=0

holds for all
x\in\Int(\dom h)
, where we in the last equality used (
6
) for
y=0
. Therefore,

h(x)=\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla h(x),x\rangle

(7)

holds for all
x\in\Int(\dom h)
. By
Theorem

3.1
,
h
is positively homogeneous of degree 2 over the set
\Int(\dom h)
. By
Lemma

3.2
,
\dom h=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla h(x),y\rangle-\langle\nabla h(y),x\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla h(x),x\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla h(y),y\rangle-\langle\nabla h(y),x-y\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\left(\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla h(y),y\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla h(x),x\rangle-\langle\nabla h(x),y-x\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\left(h(x)-h(y)-\langle\nabla h(y),x-y\rangle\right)-\left(h(y)-h(x)-\langle\nabla h(x),y-x\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=D_{h}(x,y)-D_{h}(y,x)=0

holds for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where we in the second equality used (
7
) and in the last equality used (
6
). Therefore,

\langle\nabla h(x),y\rangle=\langle\nabla h(y),x\rangle

(8)

holds for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In particular,

\langle\nabla h(\lambda_{1}x_{1}+\lambda_{2}x_{2}),y\rangle=\langle\lambda_{1}x_{1}+\lambda_{2}x_{2},\nabla h(y)\rangle=\langle\lambda_{1}\nabla h(x_{1})+\lambda_{2}\nabla h(x_{2}),y\rangle

holds for all
x_{1},x_{2},y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. Setting

y=\nabla h(\lambda_{1}x_{1}+\lambda_{2}x_{2})-(\lambda_{1}\nabla h(x_{1})+\lambda_{2}\nabla h(x_{2}))

gives that

\langle\nabla h(\lambda_{1}x_{1}+\lambda_{2}x_{2})-(\lambda_{1}\nabla h(x_{1})+\lambda_{2}\nabla h(x_{2})),y\rangle=\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=0,

i.e.,
\nabla h
is linear. Since
\nabla h
is also continuous
[
5
, Corollary 17.43]
we have that
h(x)=\frac{1}{2}\langle x,Qx\rangle
for some
Q\in\mathbb{S}^{n}

[
5
, Proposition 2.58]
. Since
h
is strictly convex,
{Q\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{n}.}

∎

The Symmetry Coefficient of Sums of Positively Homogeneous Functions (R3)

Finally, we turn to proving
R3
.

Proposition 3.4
:

Let the function
h_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be Legendre and positively homogeneous of degree
p_{i}\in(1,\infty)
over
\Int(\dom h_{i})
, for each
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. Suppose that
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
is a sequence satisfying
p_{1}<p_{2}\leq\dots\leq p_{m-1}<p_{m}
. Then,
h=\sum_{i=1}^{m}h_{i}
is a Legendre function and

\alpha(h)\in\left[\min_{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}}\{\alpha(h_{i})\},\ \min\{\alpha(h_{1}),\alpha(h_{m})\}\right].

(9)

Proof.

By
Proposition

3.1
,
\dom h_{i}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
holds for each
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
and so
h
has full domain and is a Legendre function. Let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be such that
x\neq y
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\frac{D_{h}(\lambda x,\lambda y)}{D_{h}(\lambda y,\lambda x)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda^{p_{i}}D_{h_{i}}(x,y)}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda^{p_{i}}D_{h_{i}}(y,x)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\frac{D_{h_{1}}(x,y)+\sum_{i=2}^{m}\lambda^{p_{i}-p_{1}}D_{h_{i}}(x,y)}{D_{h_{1}}(y,x)+\sum_{i=2}^{m}\lambda^{p_{i}-p_{1}}D_{h_{i}}(y,x)}=\frac{D_{h_{1}}(x,y)}{D_{h_{1}}(y,x)},

(10)

where the first equality follows from
Proposition

3.1
and the third equality follows from the fact that
p_{i}-p_{1}>0
holds for all
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. Similarly,

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\frac{D_{h}(\lambda x,\lambda y)}{D_{h}(\lambda y,\lambda x)}=\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\frac{D_{h_{m}}(x,y)+\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\lambda^{p_{i}-p_{m}}D_{h_{i}}(x,y)}{D_{h_{m}}(y,x)+\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\lambda^{p_{i}-p_{m}}D_{h_{i}}(y,x)}=\frac{D_{h_{m}}(x,y)}{D_{h_{m}}(y,x)}.

(11)

Combining (
10
) and (
11
) gives that
\alpha(h)\leq\min\{\alpha(h_{1}),\alpha(h_{m})\}
. The lower bound of
\alpha(h)
in (
9
) is given by
Proposition

3.4
.
∎

Corollary 3.1
:

Let the functions

and

. Then,

is a Legendre function and

Proof.

Apply
Proposition

3.4
with

.
∎

Remark 3.1
:

We will now argue the necessity of the condition

in

. Let

, then

gives that

.

The Symmetry Coefficient of
|\cdot|^{p}

In this section we will consider the symmetry coefficient of the one-dimensional Legendre and positively homogeneous function
|\cdot|^{p}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
for some
p>1
. Central to this study is the function
f_{p}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

f_{p}(u)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{|u|^{p}-pu+(p-1)}{(p-1)|u|^{p}-p\operatorname{sgn}(u)|u|^{p-1}+1}&\text{ if }u\neq 1,\\
1&\text{ if }u=1,\\
\end{cases}

(14)

for all
u\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, when
p>2
we will consider the function
g_{p}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

g_{p}(u)\coloneqq u^{2(p-1)}-(p-1)^{2}u^{p}-2p(p-2)u^{p-1}-(p-1)^{2}u^{p-2}+1

(15)

for all
u\in[0,1]
. In fact, we will show in
Proposition

4.1
that computing
\alpha(|\cdot|^{p})
is equivalent to computing the extremum points of
f_{p}
. Furthermore, when
p>2
this is equivalent to computing the roots of the function
g_{p}
over its domain
[0,1]
. Even though
f_{p}
is generally non-convex and non-concave, we will in
Proposition

4.2
show that if
p>2
, then
f_{p}
is quasiconcave on
[-1,1]
and is uniquely maximized over
[-1,1]
by some point in
(-1,0)
. This lets us recast the non-convex and non-concave problem of naively computing
\alpha(|\cdot|^{p})
via (
1
) to a one-dimensional quasiconcave problem over a compact interval, as implemented in
Algorithm

1
.

Not only will the study of
f_{p}
lead to an efficient way of computing
\alpha(|\cdot|^{p})
, it will also let us show that the function
\psi:(1,\infty)\to(0,1]
defined by

\psi(p)=\alpha(|\cdot|^{p})
, for all
p\in(1,\infty)
, is strictly increasing on the interval
(1,2)
and strictly decreasing on the interval
(2,\infty)
. This is shown in
Corollary

4.3
. When combining this with the bounds found in
Proposition

3.4

Proposition 4.1
:

Let
p>1
and let
f_{p}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
as in (
14
). Then, the denominator in (
14
) is positive, i.e.,

(p-1)|u|^{p}-p\operatorname{sgn}(u)|u|^{p-1}+1>0

holds for all
u\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{1\}
. Furthermore,
f_{p}
is continuous, differentiable on
(-1,1)
, positive, and satisfies
f_{p}(u^{-1})=f_{p}(u)^{-1}
for all
u\neq 0
. Moreover, the symmetry coefficient of
|\cdot|^{p}
is given by

\alpha(|\cdot|^{p})=\min\biggl\{\min\bigl\{f_{p}(u),f_{p}(u)^{-1}\bigr\}\mid u\in[-1,1]\biggr\}.

(16)

Proof.

First, note that
|\cdot|^{p}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is proper, closed, differentiable on
\mathbb{R}
and strictly convex, and so it is a Legendre function. Let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
x\neq y
, then we have that

\displaystyle\frac{D_{|\cdot|^{p}}(x,y)}{D_{|\cdot|^{p}}(y,x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{|x|^{p}-|y|^{p}-p\operatorname{sgn}(y)|y|^{p-1}(x-y)}{|y|^{p}-|x|^{p}-p\operatorname{sgn}(x)|x|^{p-1}(y-x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{|x|^{p}+(p-1)|y|^{p}-p\operatorname{sgn}(y)|y|^{p-1}x}{(p-1)|x|^{p}+|y|^{p}-p\operatorname{sgn}(x)|x|^{p-1}y}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{|u|^{p}-pu+(p-1)}{(p-1)|u|^{p}-p\operatorname{sgn}(u)|u|^{p-1}+1}&\text{ if }y\neq 0\text{ and }u\coloneqq x/y,\\
(p-1)^{-1}&\text{ if }y=0,\\
\end{cases}

(17)

where the last equality for
y\neq 0
is obtained by dividing the numerator and denominator by
|y|^{p}
and simplifying.
By the strict convexity of
|\cdot|^{p}
, we have that
D_{|\cdot|^{p}}(y,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
holds for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
such that
x\neq y
, and since the first of the two expressions in (
17
) equals
f_{p}(u)
for
u\neq 1
, we have that the denominator in (
14
) is positive,

Two uses of l’Hospital’s rule give that

\displaystyle\lim_{u\to 1}f_{p}(u)

\displaystyle=\lim_{u\to 1}\frac{u^{p}-pu+(p-1)}{(p-1)u^{p}-pu^{p-1}+1}

\displaystyle=\lim_{u\to 1}\frac{pu^{p-1}-p}{(p-1)pu^{p-1}-(p-1)pu^{p-2}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{u\to 1}\frac{(p-1)pu^{p-2}}{(p-1)^{2}pu^{p-2}-(p-2)(p-1)pu^{p-2}}=1

and thus
f_{p}
is continuous.

By the definition of
f_{p}
in (
14
) and the fact that the denominator in (
14
) is positive, the function
f_{p}
is differentiable on the set
(-1,1)
. By strict convexity of
|\cdot|^{p}
, we have that
D_{|\cdot|^{p}}(x,y)>0
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
such that
x\neq y
, and so
f_{p}
is positive. If
u\neq 0
, then by (
17
)

\displaystyle f_{p}(u)=\frac{D_{|\cdot|^{p}}(|u|,\operatorname{sgn}(u))}{D_{|\cdot|^{p}}(\operatorname{sgn}(u),|u|)}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{D_{|\cdot|^{p}}(\operatorname{sgn}(u),|u|)}{D_{|\cdot|^{p}}(|u|,\operatorname{sgn}(u))}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{D_{|\cdot|^{p}}(|u|u^{-1},|u|)}{D_{|\cdot|^{p}}(|u|,|u|u^{-1})}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{|u|^{p}D_{|\cdot|^{p}}(u^{-1},1)}{|u|^{p}D_{|\cdot|^{p}}(1,u^{-1})}\right)^{-1}=f_{p}(u^{-1})^{-1}

(18)

where the fourth equality follows the fact that
D_{|\cdot|^{p}}
is positively homogeneous of degree
p
, as shown in
Proposition

3.1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\alpha(|\cdot|^{p})

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\left.\frac{D_{|\cdot|^{p}}(x,y)}{D_{|\cdot|^{p}}(y,x)}\right|x,y\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and }x\neq y\right\}

\displaystyle=\min\biggl\{\inf\bigl\{f_{p}(u)\mid u\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{1\}\bigr\},(p-1)^{-1}\biggr\}

\displaystyle=\inf\biggl\{\min\bigl\{f_{p}(u),f_{p}(u)^{-1}\bigr\}\mid u\in\mathbb{R}\biggr\}

\displaystyle=\min\biggl\{\min\bigl\{f_{p}(u),f_{p}(u)^{-1}\bigr\}\mid u\in[-1,1]\biggr\},

where we in the second equality used (
17
), and in the third equality used that
f_{p}>0
and that
f_{p}(0)=p-1
, and in the last equality used (
18
) combined with the fact that
f_{p}
is continuous.
∎

\displaystyle=p\left(cu_{p,c}^{p-1}+c-(u_{p,c}+1)\right)+u_{p,c}+1.

(i)

In the case

p\left(cu_{p,c}^{p-1}+\underbrace{c-(u_{p,c}+1}_{>0})\right)+u_{p,c}+1

(ii)

. Let

be such that

\displaystyle g_{1,p,c}(u_{p,c})

\displaystyle=p\left(cu_{p,c}^{p-1}+c-(u_{p,c}+1)\right)+u_{p,c}+1

\displaystyle<p\left(cu_{p,c}^{p-1}+c-(u_{p,c}+1)\right)+2

\displaystyle<p\left(cu_{p,c}^{p-1}+\tfrac{c-2}{2}\right)+2

\displaystyle<p\tfrac{c-2}{4}+2

\displaystyle<0,

where the equality is (

(iii)

Theorem 5.1

Suppose that

\displaystyle D_{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}^{p}}(x,y)

Appendix A

In this section, we compute closed form expressions of

for some even positive integers

be an even integer and recall the polynomial

g_{p}(u)\coloneqq u^{2(p-1)}-(p-1)^{2}u^{p}-2p(p-2)u^{p-1}-(p-1)^{2}u^{p-2}+1

over

as a polynomial over

4.2

is equivalent to finding a closed form expression of the unique root of

in the interval

Despite the polynomial being of degree

and

Note that

is a

(1,0,\dots,0,-(p-1)^{2},-2p(p-2),-(p-1)^{2},0,\dots,0,1)

forms a palindrome. This observation allows us to make use of
tetrahedral numbers
in our results, where the
n
th tetrahedral number is defined as

Te_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{n}T_{k},

and
T_{k}
is the
k
th
triangular number
:

T_{k}\coloneqq\frac{k(k+1)}{2}.

For
n\leq 0
, we define
Te_{n}=T_{n}=0
and we note that the following identities hold for all integers

:

\displaystyle T_{n}

\displaystyle=T_{n-1}+n,

(64)

\displaystyle Te_{n}

\displaystyle=Te_{n-1}+T_{n}.

(65)

Proposition A.1
:

Let
p\geq 4
be a positive even integer, then

g_{p}(u)=(u+1)^{4}\sum_{k=0}^{2(p-3)}Te_{\min\{k,2(p-3)-k\}+1}(-1)^{k}u^{k}

(66)

holds for all

.

Proof.

Fix
p\geq 4
. Let

(u+1)^{4}\sum_{k=0}^{2(p-3)}Te_{\min\{k,2(p-3)-k\}+1}(-1)^{k}u^{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{2(p-1)}c_{i}u^{i}

hold for all
u\in\mathbb{R}
and some coefficients
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. First, we note that
c_{0}=1
. For any
i\in\{1,\dots,p-3\}
, we get that

c_{i}=(-1)^{i}(Te_{i+1}-4Te_{i}+6Te_{i-1}-4Te_{i-2}+Te_{i-3}),

which after an evaluation gives that
c_{i}=0
for
i\in\{1,\dots,p-3\}
, since in general

Te_{n}-4Te_{n-1}+6Te_{n-2}-4Te_{n-3}+Te_{n-4}=0

. Moreover, it is straightforward to verify that

c_{p-2}=(-1)^{p-2}(Te_{p-3}-4Te_{p-2}+6Te_{p-3}-4Te_{p-4}+Te_{p-5})=-(p-1)^{2}\\

and

c_{p-1}=(-1)^{p-1}(Te_{p-4}-4Te_{p-3}+6Te_{p-2}-4Te_{p-3}+Te_{p-4})=-2p(p-2),\\

where we here used twice that

is even. Since the right hand side of (

) is a product of two palindromic polynomials, we have that

is also a palindromic polynomial, i.e.,

must hold for all

, and so (

) holds.
∎

It will at this point be convenient to define, for any even integer
p\geq 4
, the palindromic polynomials

h_{1,p}(u)\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{p-4}T_{\min\{k,p-4-k\}+1}(-1)^{k}u^{k}

and

h_{2,p}(u)\coloneqq(-1)^{\frac{p}{2}-1}(p-1)u^{\frac{p}{2}-1}+\sum_{i=0}^{p-2}(-1)^{i}u^{i}

for all
u\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proposition A.2
:

Let
p\geq 4
be a positive even integer, then

h_{1,p}(u)h_{2,p}(u)=\sum_{k=0}^{2(p-3)}Te_{\min\{k,2(p-3)-k\}+1}(-1)^{k}u^{k}

(67)

holds for all
u\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\sum_{i=0}^{2(p-3)}c_{i}u^{i}\coloneqq h_{1,p}(u)h_{2,p}(u)
holds for all
u\in\mathbb{R}
and some coefficients
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. We have that

\displaystyle h_{1,p}(u)h_{2,p}(u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{p-2}\sum_{j=0}^{p-4}(-1)^{i+j}T_{\min\{j,p-4-j\}+1}u^{i+j}

(68)

\displaystyle\ +(p-1)\sum_{j=0}^{p-4}(-1)^{\tfrac{p}{2}-1+j}T_{\min\{j,p-4-j\}+1}u^{\tfrac{p}{2}-1+j}.

(69)

Let
k\in\{0,\dots,\tfrac{p}{2}-2\}
. Then (
68
), but not (
69
), will contribute to
c_{k}
. Since,
\min\{k,p-4-k\}=k
, we get that

c_{k}=(-1)^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k}T_{j+1}=(-1)^{k}Te_{k+1}.

Let us now cover the case when
k\in\{\tfrac{p}{2}-1,\dots,p-3\}
, using induction over
k
. Let us fix
k=\tfrac{p}{2}-1
. When expanding, there will be contributions from both the right hand side of (
68
) and (
69
) to
c_{k}
. From (
68
) we get the terms

(-1)^{k}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\tfrac{p}{2}-2}T_{j+1}+T_{p-4-k+1}\right)

(70)

and from (
69
) we get
(-1)^{k}(p-1)T_{1}
, which when added to (
70
) yield

\displaystyle c_{k}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\tfrac{p}{2}-2}T_{j+1}+T_{\tfrac{p}{2}-2}+(p-1)T_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\left(Te_{\tfrac{p}{2}-1}+T_{\tfrac{p}{2}-1}-(\tfrac{p}{2}-1)+(p-1)\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\left(Te_{\tfrac{p}{2}-1}+T_{\tfrac{p}{2}-1}+\tfrac{p}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}Te_{k+1},

where the second and final equalities use (
64
) and (
65
).

Let us now suppose that the induction hypothesis
c_{k-1}=(-1)^{k-1}Te_{k}
holds for some
k\in\{\tfrac{p}{2},\dots,p-3\}
. We will now show that
c_{k}=(-1)^{k}Te_{k+1}
. Similarly expanding the right hand side of (
68
) and (
69
) we get

\displaystyle c_{k}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\tfrac{p}{2}-2}T_{j+1}+\sum_{j=\tfrac{p}{2}-1}^{k}T_{p-3-j}+(p-1)T_{k-\left(\tfrac{p}{2}-1\right)+1}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\tfrac{p}{2}-2}T_{j+1}+\sum_{j=\tfrac{p}{2}-1}^{k-1}T_{p-3-j}+T_{p-3-k}+(p-1)T_{k-\tfrac{p}{2}+2}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\tfrac{p}{2}-2}T_{j+1}+\sum_{j=\tfrac{p}{2}-1}^{k-1}T_{p-3-j}+T_{p-3-k}+(p-1)\left(T_{(k-1)-\tfrac{p}{2}+2}+k-\tfrac{p}{2}+2\right)\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\left(Te_{k}+T_{p-3-k}+(p-1)\left(k-\tfrac{p}{2}+2\right)\right),

where we in the third equality use (
64
) and in the last equality we use the induction hypothesis:

\displaystyle c_{k-1}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k-1}Te_{k}

\displaystyle\implies Te_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\tfrac{p}{2}-2}T_{j+1}+\sum_{j=\tfrac{p}{2}-1}^{k-1}T_{p-3-j}+(p-1)T_{(k-1)-(\tfrac{p}{2}-1)+1}.

Finally,

\displaystyle T_{p-3-k}+(p-1)(k-\tfrac{p}{2}+3)

\displaystyle=\frac{(p-3-k)(p-2-k)}{2}+\frac{(p-1)(2k-p+4)}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}=T_{k+1}

and so
c_{k}=(-1)^{k}\left(Te_{k}+T_{k+1}\right)=(-1)^{k}Te_{k+1}.
By induction,
c_{k}=(-1)^{k}Te_{k+1}
holds for all
k\in\{\tfrac{p}{2}-1,\dots,p-3\}
.

Since both
h_{1,p}
and
h_{2,p}
are palindromic polynomials, the resulting polynomial
h_{1,p}h_{2,p}
must also be palindromic. Moreover, the right hand side of (
67
) is a palindromic polynomial, implying that the result that
c_{k}=(-1)^{k}Te_{k+1}
for
k\in\{0,\dots,p-3\}
along with the palindromic property shows the equality (
67
).
∎

Combining
Proposition

A.1
and
Proposition

A.2
we have reduced the problem of finding a closed form expression of
\alpha(|\cdot|^{p})
, for even integers
p\geq 4
, to finding a root of the polynomials
h_{1,p}
or
h_{2,p}
with degrees
p-4
and
p-2
, respectively. This problem may seem intractable when
p-2\geq 5
, i.e., when
p\geq 8
(since
p
is an even integer). However, we will further explore the palindromic properties of
h_{1,p}
and
h_{2,p}
, allowing us to handle the cases with
p=8
and
p=10
.

The study of palindromic polynomials, also called
reciprocal
polynomials, has a rich history. See for instance
[
16
]
, where it was shown that for palindromic polynomials
h
of even degree
d
, there exists a polynomial
\tilde{h}
of degree
d/2
such that
h(u)=u^{d/2}\tilde{h}(u+u^{-1})
holds for all
u\neq 0
. Since both of our polynomials
h_{1,p}
and
h_{2,p}
are palindromic of even degrees, we apply this method to reduce the problem of finding a zero to
h_{1,p}
and
h_{2,p}
to the one of finding a zero of the polynomials
\tilde{h}_{1,p}
and
\tilde{h}_{2,p}
of degree
(p-4)/2
and
(p-2)/2
, respectively.
Since roots to polynomials up to degree 4 can be computed in closed form, the problem becomes tractable for
p=8
and
p=10
. The case of
p=12
seems to be intractable, since the positive real roots we seek are the real roots of
h_{2,12}
, which has degree
5
.
