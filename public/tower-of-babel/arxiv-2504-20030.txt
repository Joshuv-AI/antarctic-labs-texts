Allele trees for the mother-dependent neutral mutations model and their scaling limits in the rare mutations regime
Airam Blancas, María Clara Fittipaldi, Saraí Hernández-Torres

Abstract
The mother-dependent neutral mutations model describes the evolution of a population across discrete generations, where neutral mutations occur among a finite set of possible alleles. In this model, each mutant child acquires a type different from that of its mother, chosen uniformly at random. In this work, we define a multitype allele tree associated with this model and analyze its scaling limit through a Markov chain that tracks the sizes of allelic subfamilies and their mutant descendants. We show that this Markov chain converges to a continuous-state Markov process, whose transition probabilities depend on the sizes of the initial allelic populations and those of their mutant offspring in the first allelic generation. As a result, the allele tree converges to a multidimensional limiting object, which can be described in terms of the universal allele tree introduced by Bertoin in [ 2 ] .

Introduction

The Bienaymé-Galton-Watson (BGW) branching process is a classical model for the evolution of population size, in which each individual produces a random number of offspring independently of others, according to a fixed offspring distribution. The BGW process tracks the number of individuals across generations, indexed by discrete time, where generations represent the number of ancestral steps between an individual and the original ancestor.

BGW processes have been extensively studied, and their variations extend the model’s applicability beyond population size, allowing for the modeling of a wide range of phenomena that shape population structure. Among the key forces driving population change, gene mutations play a crucial role. Given a specific gene, mutations give rise to different versions, referred to as
alleles
. Research in computational biology and genetics has highlighted the need for models that consider a finite number of alleles; see
[
9
,
11
,
14
]
.

The
mother-dependent neutral mutations model
,
introduced in
[
3
]
, addresses this need by modeling the genealogy of a population undergoing neutral mutations with a finite number of alleles (see Subsection
1.1
). In this work, we analyze its long-term allelic structure in the following sense.
Alleles classify the population’s genealogy according to genetic type, leading to a partition into
allelic subfamilies
, which are encoded in a
multitype allele tree
(defined in Section
4
). Our main focus is on the allele tree and its scaling limit.

Our analysis considers a population modeled by mother-dependent neutral mutations, where population growth is in the critical regime, the mutation rate is small, and the number of original ancestors is large (see Hypotheses (H
1
), (H
2
), and (H
3
) below).

Before stating our main theorem, we first define the mother-dependent neutral mutations model as a multitype BGW process.

1.1
Mother-dependent neutral mutations model

The
mother-dependent neutral mutations model
describes the evolution of a population with a finite number of alleles,
where random mutations alter the genotype of individuals without affecting their offspring distribution–and hence without influencing population size dynamics. Because of this property, these mutations are referred to as
neutral
.

Children who inherit the same allele as their mother will be referred to as
clones
, while those who acquire a different allele will be called
mutants
.
In the mother-dependent neutral mutations model, population size follows a standard BGW process, and mutations arise along the ancestral lines of the population. A parameter
r\in(0,1)
controls the
mutation probability
,
with mutations occurring randomly within the population and independently of one another.
Whenever a mutation event occurs, the mutant child does not inherit the mother’s allele but instead acquires a different allele chosen uniformly at random from a finite set of possible alleles. Throughout this work, let
d
be the number of alleles, which we label as
[d]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,d\}
.
Then, the joint distribution of mutant children follows a multinomial distribution, which we introduce next.

We denote by
\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i},i\in[d])

the offspring distribution of our population, where each
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}
is a measure valued on
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
. The measure
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}
specifies the offspring distribution (by type) of an individual of type
i
.
Since we assume that the mutations in our model are neutral, any individual, regardless of type, follows a common offspring distribution
\mu
for its
total number of children
. We thus assume that
\mu
is a probability measure supported on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, satisfying
\mu(0)+\mu(1)<1
(to avoid trivial cases).

For
i\in[d]
, the measure
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}
defines the distribution of the random vector
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i}
, corresponding to the offspring of an individual of type
i
. According to our description above, the probability that an individual of type
i
begets
\mathbf{v}=(\mathbf{v}(1),\ldots,\mathbf{v}(d))\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
children is

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}(\mathbf{v})&=\mathbb{P}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i}=\mathbf{v})\\
&=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}\left[(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i}{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i}{(d)})=(\mathbf{v}(1),\dots,\mathbf{v}(d))\right]\\
&=\mu(|\mathbf{v}|)\binom{|\mathbf{v}|}{\mathbf{v}(1),\dots,\mathbf{v}(d)}(1-r)^{\mathbf{v}(i)}\prod\limits_{j\neq i}\left(\dfrac{r}{d-1}\right)^{\mathbf{v}(j)}.\\
\end{split}

(1)

In the equation above,
\mu_{i}({\mathbf{v}})
is the probability that an individual of type
i
gives birth to
|\mathbf{v}|
children, with
\mathbf{v}(j)
of them being of type
j
.

Equation (
1
) implies that, among the
\mu(|\mathbf{v}|)
children, each undergoes a mutation with probability
r\in(0,1)
, independently of the others. The types of mutant children are assigned according to a multinomial distribution.
Then the random variable

\xi^{(+)}\overset{d}{=}\xi^{(+,i)}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i}(j),\qquad i\in[d],

(2)

follows distribution
\mu
, which is the same for all types of individuals.

In the mother-dependent neutral mutations model, the population is described by a
d
-type BGW process
\{\mathbf{Y}=(\mathbf{Y}_{k},k\in\mathbb{N}_{0});\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{a}}^{\boldsymbol{\mu},r}\}
. Here, we indicate that the process
\mathbf{Y}
has
\boldsymbol{\mu}
as its offspring distribution (as defined in (
1
)), with mutation rate
r\in[0,1]
, and starts with
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
individuals. That is,
\mathbf{a}(i)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
represents the initial number of individuals of type
i
. Since the relevant parameter for the measure
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is the mutation rate
r
, we will use the notation
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{a}}^{r}
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\mathbf{Y}_{k}
describes the allelic configuration of the population in the
k
-th generation (also referred to as the
k
-th level), so that for each
i\in[d]
,
\mathbf{Y}_{k}(i)
denotes the number of individuals of type
i
in the
k
-th level.
The process

\mathbf{Y}\coloneqq(\mathbf{Y}_{k},k\in\mathbb{N}_{0})

is a discrete-time Markov chain, recursively defined for each

by

\mathbf{Y}_{0}(j)=\mathbf{a}(j)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{Y}_{k+1}(j)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\mathbf{Y}_{k}(i)}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k+1,\ell}^{i}{(j)},\qquad\text{ for all }k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(3)

In the definition above,
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i}_{k+1,\ell}(j)
denotes the number of children of type
j
in the
(k+1)
-th level begotten by the
\ell
-th individual of type
i
in level
k
.
The random vectors
(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i}_{k,\ell})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
are independent copies of the random vector

defined in (

Theorem 1.1
.

Consider a sequence of rescaled allele trees

\mathscr{A}^{(n)}=\left((n^{-2}\mathcal{A}^{(n)}_{u},\mathcal{C}^{(n)}_{u},n^{-1}\boldsymbol{d}^{(n)}_{u}):u\in\mathbb{U}\right)

where each
\mathscr{A}^{(n)}

\left(n^{-2}\mathcal{A}^{(n)}:u\in\mathbbm{U}\right)\Longrightarrow\left(\mathcal{Y}_{u}:u\in\mathbbm{U}\right)\qquad n\to\infty,

\nu(dz)=\dfrac{c}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}z^{3}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{c^{2}y}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}dz,\qquad z>0,

(9)

and random initial population
\theta_{1}
which follows the inverse Gaussian distribution
\inverseGauss\left(\frac{1}{c},\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)
.

\mathscr{A}^{(n)}\Longrightarrow\left(\left(\mathcal{Y}_{u},\mathcal{C}_{u},\sum\limits_{i\neq\mathcal{C}_{u}}\frac{c}{d-1}\mathcal{Y}_{u}\mathbf{e_{i}}\right):u\in\mathbb{U}\right),\qquad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

1.3
Relation to previous work

The mother-dependent neutral mutations model is a variation of the
BGW process with neutral mutations
, introduced and studied by Bertoin in
[
2
,
1
]
. Both models describe neutral mutations, but the BGW process with neutral mutations assumes an infinite number of alleles, where each mutation event produces a novel allele.
This process is modeled as a BGW process in which each child has a probability
r\in[0,1]
of undergoing a mutation, thereby introducing a novel allele.

This work follows a similar approach but extends it to the mother-dependent neutral mutations model.
In particular, we generalize the scaling scheme discussed in
[
2
]
to the multidimensional case.
In that framework, the number of individuals is rescaled, and allelic generations are treated as a discrete-time parameter. While our main result can be viewed as a multidimensional extension of
[
2
, Theorem 1]
, there are significant differences between
[
2
]
and the present work.

One such difference lies in the construction of the allele tree. Here, we extend the notion of the allele tree to a multitype setting (see Section
4
), where special care must be taken with the order in which descendant allelic populations are recorded.

A second key difference is the role played by the clone-mutant Markov chain, defined in Section
3
. One of our main tools is Proposition
5.2
, which describes the limit behavior of this chain. Interestingly, the limit depends on the initial population and the types present therein–a distinction that is not possible in
[
2
]
, since allele types cannot reappear in that setting. However, when we look at the limiting behavior of the multitype allele tree, we recover an object that can still be constructed from the tree-indexed CSBP

\mathscr{Y}
defined by Bertoin in
[
2
]
.

2
Background on multitype BGW processes

In this section, we consider a general
d
-type BGW process

\{\mathbf{Y}=(\mathbf{Y}_{k},k\in\mathbb{N}_{0});P_{\mathbf{a}}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\}

and study its induced genealogical structure.
In this case, the genealogy is represented by a finite plane tree, where each vertex is assigned a color corresponding to its type.

We begin by introducing labeling systems that encode both genealogical and type information for a
d
-type BGW process.
We then introduce notation to identify allelic subfamilies, that is, subtrees consisting of individuals of the same type.
Next, we present the branching property of
d
-type BGW processes, along with a generalization that will be useful in this work.
We conclude the section by discussing how our notation extends from trees to forests.

2.1
Plane trees

For non-empty set of vertices
V
, a
plane tree

\mathbf{t}\subset V\times V
is a directed and connected planar graph without loops. If the set of vertices is finite, we refer to
\mathbf{t\textbf{}}
as a
finite plane tree
.
The set of plane trees is denoted by
\mathcal{T}
. A plane tree
\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{T}
determines a set of vertices, which we denote by
V(\mathbf{t})
.

A directed edge in
\mathbf{t}
indicates a parent-child relationship, where the direction of an edge is from the child to the parent. Therefore, the out-degree of each vertex is either 0 or 1.
Within a tree, there is a unique vertex
\rho(\mathbf{t})
with an out-degree of 0, and we refer to this special vertex as the
root
. Whenever the context is clear, we simply write
\rho
.
Otherwise, there is exactly one edge out of
u
pointing to a vertex
v
. This means that
u
is a child of
v
, or equivalently, that
v
is the parent of
u
.
If two vertices
u
and
w
share
v
as their parent, we say that
u
and
w
are
siblings
.

A
branch
is a sequence of vertices
u_{0},\ldots,u_{n}
where
u_{i}
is the parent of
u_{i+1}
for all
i=0,\ldots,n-1
. A branch is also known as a
genealogical line
.

We will often refer to a plane tree simply as a
tree
. A finite collection of trees is a called a
forest
.

2.2
Labelling a multitype BGW tree

In this subsection, we define and establish notation for these trees and introduce two labeling systems for BGW processes: the Ulam-Harris notation, and the breadth-first order.
The Ulam-Harris notation preserves the genealogical structure, while the breadth-first order arranges individuals based on a breadth-first search over the finite plane tree.
Our notation is consistent with that in
[
13
, Section 6.2]
and
[
5
]
.

Consider a tree
\mathbf{t}
rooted at
\rho
.
The
Ulam-Harris notation
assigns labels to the individuals in
\mathbf{t}
from the set of finite sequences of positive integers

\mathbbm{U}\coloneqq\cup_{n\geq 0}\mathbb{N}^{n}
. These labels track the ancestral lineage of each individual starting from the root
\rho
.

The length of the sequence in
\mathbb{U}
indicates the level of the individual within the tree.
Specifically, the label
\emptyset
represents the root
\rho
at level 0, while any individual
u=u_{1}\ldots u_{k}
has a label that reflects their genealogy in the
k
-th level of the tree.

The Ulam-Harris notation describes parent-child relationships and ancestry within the tree. If
u=u_{1}...u_{k}\in\mathbf{t}
, then
uj=u_{1}...u_{k}j
represents the
j
-th children of
u
.

We denote the
length
of the sequence by
|u|=k
. The
level
of an individual is the number of edges from the root to
u
.
Thus, the root has length
0
and level
0
. Note that vertices with equal length belong to the same level.

The Ulam-Harris naturally defines ancestry relationships.
For
u,v\in\mathbf{t}
, we say that
v
is an
ancestor
of
u
(or equivalently,
u
is a
descendant
of
v
) if there exists
w\in\mathbbm{U}
such that
u=vw
. This relationship is denoted by
v\preceq u
(or
u\succeq v
). In particular, note that
u\preceq u
for any
u\in\mathbf{t}
.

In the
breadth-first order
, we traverse the tree from its root to its last level, moving across each level in order from left to right. If
\mathbf{t}
contains at least
k
vertices, the
k
-th vertex of
\mathbf{t}
is denoted by
u_{k}(\mathbf{t})
. When no confusion arises, we denote the
k
-th vertex by
u_{k}
.

2.3
Colored trees and subtrees

A plane tree is a graphical representation of a BGW process.
If we now consider a multitype BGW process, the information on the types of individuals can be represented coloring each vertex.

We now consider

The set of

-type plane trees is denoted by

.
We associate with each

a mapping

. For

, the integer

is called the type (or color) of

, where the color of each vertex indicates the function

.
The pair

and refer to it as the

of a BGW process.

Definition 2.1
.

Fix a type

. A subtree
\mathbf{s}
of type

in

is a maximal connected subgraph of

satisfying the following conditions:

are of type

;

(ii)

either the root

has no parent or the type of its parent is different from

; and

(iii)

if

is a child of

so that
v\notin V\left(\mathbf{s}\right)
and

, then
C_{\mathbf{t}}(v)\neq i
.

Subtrees of type

in
(\mathbf{t},C_{\mathbf{t}})
are ranked according to the Ulam-Harris order of their roots in
\mathbf{t}
and are denoted by
\mathbf{s}_{1}^{(i)},\mathbf{s}_{2}^{(i)},\dots,\mathbf{s}_{k}^{(i)},\dots
. The forest
\mathbf{f}^{(i)}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{s}_{1}^{(i)},\mathbf{s}_{2}^{(i)},\dots,\mathbf{s}_{k}^{(i)},\dots\}
is called the subforest of type

of
(\mathbf{t},C_{\mathbf{t}})
.

2.4
General branching property

The branching property of BGW processes describes a natural conditional independence arising from their genealogical structure. For
d
-type BGW processes, this property states the following for any level
k
: conditioned on the number of individuals and their types in the
k
-th level,
v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}
, the subtrees rooted at
v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}
are independent.

The branching property immediately implies the following.
For each
i\in[d]
, let
{P}_{\mathbf{a}(i)\mathbf{e}_{i}}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
denote the law of a
d
-type BGW process starting with
\mathbf{a}(i)\geq 0
individuals of type
i
and offspring distribution
\boldsymbol{\nu}
. If
\mathbf{a}=(\mathbf{a}(1),\ldots,\mathbf{a}(d))
, the branching property implies that
P_{\mathbf{a}}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
is equal in law to the convolution of the measures
P_{\mathbf{a}(1)\mathbf{e}_{1}}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}},\dots,P_{\mathbf{a}(d)\mathbf{e}_{d}}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
.

In Theorem
2.2
, we state a stronger version of the branching property. To prepare for this result, we will follow
[
8
, Section 2]
to introduce concepts originally defined in
[
6
]
and applied for instance in
[
10
]
. The general branching property has also been established for other variants of BGW processes; for example, for BGW in varying environment
[
4
, Proposition 2.1]
.

Let
\mathbf{t}
be a
d
-type tree rooted at
\rho
. A
stopping line

L
in
(\mathbf{t},C_{\mathbf{t}})

is a set of vertices of
\mathbf{t}
satisfying the following condition:
every branch starting at the root contains at most one vertex in
L
.
Hence, if we consider two different vertices in a stopping line
L
, then neither can be a descendant of the other.

From trees to forests

For a finite, non-empty set of vertices

is a directed planar graph without loops.

For each tree
\mathbf{t}
, the only vertex with an out-degree of

\rho(\mathbf{t})
.
The collection of roots within a forest
\mathbf{f}
is collectively referred to as the

\mathbf{t}
.

Since a forest may consist of multiple connected components,
we first need to label the trees in a forest

\mathbf{f}
.
To do so, we assign an order to the trees and label them as

\mathbf{t}_{1}(\mathbf{f}),\mathbf{t}_{2}(\mathbf{f}),\ldots,\mathbf{t}_{\ell}(\mathbf{f}),\dots

(11)

(or simply
\mathbf{t}_{1},\mathbf{t}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{t}_{\ell},\dots

In the case of the breadth-first order, we define the labeling for the whole forest by enumerating with
\rho_{1}
the root of
\mathbf{t}_{1}(\mathbf{f})
, then we perform breadth-first order tree by tree. This enumeration is performed tree by tree, so that, once we have completed the

-th tree we continue with the

-th. Note that the root of the

-th is labeled with

The definition of a stopping line is extended to forests by saying that

3
Clone-mutant Markov chain

In this section, we define a Markov chain which keeps track, for each allelic generation, of the number of clone and mutant individuals. This Markov chain is one of our main tools for the analysis of the allelic structure within the mother-dependent neutral mutations model.

Definition of the clone-mutant Markov chain

Let
(\mathbf{t},C_{\mathbf{t}})\in\mathcal{T}_{d}

d

\{\mathbf{Y}=(\mathbf{Y}_{k},k\in\mathbb{N}_{0});\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{a}}^{r}\}

\mathbf{M}_{0}=\mathbf{a},\qquad\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{a}}^{r}\text{-a.s.}

In the mother-dependent neutral mutations model, once a mutation occurs, the mutant offspring must differ in type from its mother. Consequently, mutants in the

(12)

for all

Proof.

Let

Let us consider the set of all individual in the allelic generation

Step 1

Rank the mothers in

Step 2

Inside each block from Step 3, rank the nodes as follows:

–

Arrange the nodes in increasing order of their type.

–

If there are ties among nodes of the same type, break them by ranking in decreasing order of the size of their corresponding subtrees.

The ranking from Step 4 defines the ordered pairs

5
Asymptotic behavior

In this section, we analyze the asymptotic behavior of the allelic structure of a population with mother-dependent neutral mutations.

We begin by proving the convergence of the clone-mutant Markov chain. Recall that Lemma

(\mathbf{T}_{0},\mathbf{M}_{1})

\inverseGauss\left(\mu,\lambda\right)
with parameters
\mu,\lambda>0

Let

\mathbf{Y}^{(n)}=(\mathbf{Y}^{(n)}_{k},k\in\mathbb{N}_{0})
be a mother-dependent neutral mutation model with law
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{a}^{(n)}}^{r(n)}
, as defined in (
5
), for each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

Throughout this section, we assume Hypotheses (H
1
), (H
2
), and (H
3
) from Subsection
1.2
in the Introduction.

We begin with the convergence of the transition probabilities of the clone-mutant Markov chain, starting from a large single-type population. In this case, it suffices to assume Hypotheses (H
2
) and (H
3
), and assume an initial population consists of
n
of type
j\in[d]

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume the regime of Hypothesis (H
2
) and (H
3
) on the sequence of mother-dependent neutral mutation models
\left(\mathbf{Y}^{(n)},n\in\mathbb{N}\right)

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, suppose
\mathbf{Y}^{(n)}
follows the measure
\mathbb{P}_{n\mathbf{e}_{j}}^{r(n)}
, and let

\left((\mathbf{T}_{k}^{(n)},\mathbf{M}^{(n)}_{k+1})\,;\,k\in\mathbb{N_{0}}\right)
be the associated clone-mutant Markov chain. Then we have the following.

(i)

Let

\theta_{1}=\inf\{t\geq 0:\sigma\mathbf{B}_{t}(j)+ct=1\}\sim\inverseGauss\left(\tfrac{1}{c},\tfrac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)

be the first hitting time of level
1

\left(n^{-2}T^{(n)}_{0},n^{-1}\mathbf{M}^{(n)}_{1}\right)\overset{\mathcal{D}}{\Longrightarrow}\left(\theta_{1},\sum\limits_{i\in[d]}\tfrac{c}{d-1}\theta_{1}\mathbf{e}_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{i\neq j\}}\right),\qquad\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty.

For each
i\neq j
, the
i
-th component satisfies
\tfrac{c}{d-1}\theta_{1}\sim\inverseGauss\left(\tfrac{1}{d-1},\tfrac{c}{\sigma^{2}(d-1)}\right)

(ii)

The joint moment generating function of

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}^{r(n)}_{n\mathbf{e}_{j}}\left[e^{\mathbf{q}(j)n^{-2}T_{0}^{(n)}}e^{\langle\mathbf{q},n^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{1}^{(n)}\rangle}\right]=e^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{j}(\mathbf{q})},

\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{j}(\mathbf{q})=\kappa\left(\mathbf{q}(j)+\sum\limits_{i\neq j}\frac{c}{d-1}\mathbf{q}(i)\right)\coloneqq\tfrac{c}{\sigma^{2}}\left(1-\sqrt{1-\tfrac{2\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}}\left(\mathbf{q}(j)+\tfrac{c}{d-1}\sum\limits_{i\neq j}\mathbf{q}(i)\right)}\right)

(24)

for each

such that

\theta_{1}
above, which follows an inverse Gaussian distribution.

5.2
can be found in Subsection

Proposition 5.2
.

Assume (H
1
), (H
2
) and (H
3
), and consider the law
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{a}^{(n)}}^{r(n)}
. Then

\left(n^{-2}\mathbf{T}^{(n)}_{0},n^{-1}\mathbf{M}^{(n)}_{1}\right)\overset{\mathcal{D}}{\Longrightarrow}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{y}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right),\qquad\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty,

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{y}}
is a vector of
d
independent random variables such that
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{y}}(j)\sim\inverseGauss\left(\tfrac{\mathbf{y}(j)}{c},\tfrac{\mathbf{y}(j)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)
, and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(j)=\tfrac{c}{d-1}\sum\limits_{i\neq j}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{y}}(i)
for each
j\in[d]
.
Moreover, the full clone-mutant Markov chain satisfies

\left(\left(n^{-2}\mathbf{T}^{(n)}_{k},n^{-1}\mathbf{M}^{(n)}_{k+1}\right);k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\right)\Longrightarrow((\mathbf{W}_{k},\mathbf{Z}_{k}),k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}),\qquad\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty,

(25)

where
((\mathbf{W}_{k},\mathbf{Z}_{k}),k\in\mathbb{N}_{0})
is a continuous-state homogeneous Markov chain whose reproduction measure is given by:

\begin{split}\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}^{r(n)}_{\mathbf{a}^{(n)}}\left[e^{\langle\mathbf{x},n^{-2}\mathbf{T}_{0}\rangle}e^{\langle\mathbf{z},n^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{1}\rangle}\right]=e^{\langle\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\kappa}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\rangle}\end{split},

where the function

\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{j}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\coloneqq\kappa(\mathbf{x}(j)+\tfrac{c}{d-1}\sum\limits_{i\neq j}\mathbf{z}(i)),\qquad j\in[d],

valid for all

Here,
\kappa
is the cumulant from (
24

Indeed,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}^{c}_{\mathbf{y}}\left[e^{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{W}_{k+1}\rangle}e^{\langle\mathbf{z},\mathbf{Z}_{k+1}\rangle}|\mathbf{W}_{k}=\mathbf{u},\mathbf{Z}_{k}=\mathbf{v}\right]=e^{\langle\mathbf{v},\boldsymbol{\kappa}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\rangle}\end{split}.

Once we have the limit behavior of the mutant-clone Markov chain, we have everything to prove our main result Theorem
1.1
.
We finish by formally describing the limit object defined as a tree-indexed CSBP.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
a>0
and let
\nu
be a measure on
(0,\infty)
and satisfying
\int(1\wedge y)\nu(dy)<\infty
.
A tree-indexed CSBP with reproduction measure
\nu
and initial population size
a
is a process
(\mathcal{Z}_{u}:u\in\mathbb{U})
indexed by the Ulam-Harris tree
\mathbb{U}
and taking values in the non-negative reals
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
The distribution of this process is characterized inductively on its levels, as follows,

(i)

\mathcal{Z}_{\varnothing}=a
almost surely;

(ii)

let every
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Conditionally on the previous levels
(\mathcal{Z}_{v}:v\in\mathbb{U},|v|\leq k)
,
the sequences
(\mathcal{Z}_{uj})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
defining the offspring of vertices
u\in\mathbb{U}
at level
|u|=k
are independent, and each sequence
(\mathcal{Z}_{uj})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
follows the distribution of the atoms of a Poisson random measure on
(0,\infty)
with intensity
\mathcal{Z}_{u}\nu
, where these atoms are repeated according to their multiplicity, ranked in decreasing order, and completed by an infinite sequence of zeros if the Poisson measure is finite.

As stated in the main theorem, the limiting object is a tree-indexed CSBP with reproduction measure

\nu(dz)=\dfrac{c}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}z^{3}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{c^{2}y}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}dz,\qquad z>0,

and random initial population
\theta_{1}\sim\inverseGauss\left(\frac{1}{c},\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)
.

Remark 5.4
.

Note that Theorem

assumes Hypotheses (H
2
) and (H

In the more general case (when the initial population includes a large number of individuals possibly of all available types) we additionally require Hypothesis (H

Theorem

for each
\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\bar{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{i}
.
From this, we conclude that

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{a}(i)\mathbf{e}_{i}}(\mathbf{T}_{0}(i)=k,\mathbf{M}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\ell})=\frac{\mathbf{a}(i)}{k}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}^{*k}(\boldsymbol{\ell}+(k-\mathbf{a}(i))\mathbf{e}_{i}).

We now compute the moment generating function of
(\mathbf{T}_{0},\mathbf{M}_{1})
.
By the branching property, if
\mathbf{Y}_{0}=\mathbf{a}
, then for every

\displaystyle\varphi_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{a}}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{x}(j)^{\mathbf{T}_{0}(j)}\mathbf{y}(j)^{\mathbf{M}_{1}(j)}\right]

(29)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\prod_{r=1}^{\mathbf{a}(i)}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}}\left[\mathbf{x}(i)^{\mathbf{T}_{0,r}(i)}\prod_{j\neq i}\mathbf{y}(j)^{\mathbf{M}_{1,r}(j)}\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}}\left[\mathbf{x}(i)^{\mathbf{T}_{0}(i)}\prod_{j\neq i}\mathbf{y}(j)^{\mathbf{M}_{1}(j)}\right]\right)^{\mathbf{a}(i)}

(31)

i\in[d]
, the families
\left(\mathbf{T}_{0,r}(i),r\in[\mathbf{a}(i)]\right)
and
\left((\mathbf{M}_{1,r}(j),j\in[d]),r\in[\mathbf{a}(i)]\right)

\varphi_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).

Moreover, from (
28
) and (
29

\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
and
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in[0,1]^{d}

\displaystyle\varphi_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\varphi_{i}^{\mathbf{a}(i)}(\mathbf{x}(i),\mathbf{\bar{y}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left[\sum\limits_{\mathbf{k}(i)\mathbf{a}(i)}^{\infty}\frac{\mathbf{a}(i)}{\mathbf{k}(i)}\sum\limits_{\mathbf{v}_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}^{*\mathbf{k}(i)}\big(\mathbf{v}_{i}+(\mathbf{k}(i)-\mathbf{a}(i))\mathbf{e}_{i}\big)\mathbf{x}(i)^{\mathbf{k}(i)}\prod\limits_{j\neq i}\mathbf{y}(j)^{\mathbf{v}_{i}(j)}\right].

\begin{split}\varphi_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})&=\sum\limits_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left[\frac{\mathbf{a}(i)}{\mathbf{k}(i)}\mathbf{x}(i)^{\mathbf{k}(i)}\sum\limits_{\mathbf{v}_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}^{*\mathbf{k}(i)}(\mathbf{v}_{i}+(\mathbf{k}(i)-\mathbf{a}(i)\mathbf{e}_{i})\prod\limits_{j\neq i}\mathbf{y}(j)^{\mathbf{v}_{i}(j)}\right]\\

On the other hand, using again the definition, we also have

Comparing the two expressions,
we deduce that for all
\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{a},\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
we have

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{a}}\left(\mathbf{T}_{0}=\mathbf{k},\mathbf{M}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\ell}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\mathbf{a}(i)}{\mathbf{k}(i)}\sum\limits_{(\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{d})\in W_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}^{*\mathbf{k}(i)}(\mathbf{v}_{i}+(\mathbf{k}(i)-\mathbf{a}(i))\mathbf{e}_{i}).

∎

(32)

&+x\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial s_{i}}(\varphi_{i}(x,\mathbf{y}^{i}),\mathbf{y}^{i})\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi_{i}}{\partial y_{j}y_{k}}(x,\mathbf{y}^{i})\\

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}\left[\mathbf{M}_{1}(j)\mathbf{M}_{1}(k)\right]=&\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i}(i)\right)^{2}\right]\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}\left[\mathbf{M}_{1}(j)\right]\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}\left[\mathbf{M}_{1}(k)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i}(i)\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i}(k)\right]\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}\left[\mathbf{M}_{1}(j)\right]\\

&+\frac{1}{1-m_{ii}}\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i}(j)\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i}(k)\right],\end{split}

which leads to the identity

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{e}_{i}}\left[|\mathbf{M}_{1}|^{2}\right]=\\

and this concludes the proof. ∎

&=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{\left(\mathbf{x}(j)+\tfrac{c}{d-1}\sum\limits_{i\neq j}\mathbf{z}(i)\right)\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{y}}(j)\right\}\right],\end{split}

and the result follows from the moment generating function of the inverse Gaussian
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathbf{y}}(j)\sim\inverseGauss\left(\tfrac{\mathbf{y}(j)}{c},\tfrac{\mathbf{y}(j)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)
.

Finally, by Lemma
3.2
, the sequence

\left\{(\mathbf{T}_{k},\mathbf{M}_{k+1}):k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\right\}
defines a time-homogeneous Markov process.
Therefore, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}^{r(n)}_{\mathbf{a}^{(n)}}\left[e^{\langle\mathbf{x},n^{-2}\mathbf{T}_{k}\rangle}e^{\langle\mathbf{z},n^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{k+1}\rangle}\Big|\mathbf{T}_{k-1}=\mathbf{v}^{(n)},\mathbf{M}_{k}=\mathbf{u}^{(n)}\right]=\mathbb{E}^{r(n)}_{\mathbf{v}^{(n)}}\left[e^{\langle\mathbf{x},n^{-2}\mathbf{T}_{0}\rangle}e^{\langle\mathbf{z},n^{-1}\mathbf{M}_{1}\rangle}\right],\end{split}

and if
\tfrac{\mathbf{v}^{n}}{n}\rightarrow\mathbf{v}
, we conclude the desired convergence stated in (
25
). ∎
