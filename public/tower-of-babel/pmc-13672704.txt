Sequential Exposure to Acute, Intermediate, and Late Synovial Fluid Fracture Hematoma after Ankle Fracture Promotes Cartilage Damage in Vitro Partially Attenuated by Anti Inflammatory Agents
Authors: Evan Fedock, Nicholas B Allen, Alexandra Hunter Aitchison, Will Sharp, Olivia Blaber, Zachary Aman, Austin Cates, Lucas Domberg, Samuel B Adams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA) following intra-articular fractures (IAFs) remains a common sequela after injury. Cartilage degeneration after injury is influenced by multiple factors, including elevated proinflammatory cytokines produced by synovial fluid fracture hematoma (SFFH). Targeting this inflammatory response may reduce cartilage damage. This study evaluated cartilage injury and inflammatory mediator changes caused by SFFH, assessing whether commercially available anti-inflammatory agents can suppress the inflammatory cascade implicated in PTOA and reduce chondrocyte death.MethodsAnkle synovial fluid was collected from 54 patients with IAFs. Human cartilage discs harvested from talar allografts were cultured in standard chondrocyte media (SCM) and initial media was collected. Explants were randomized into five groups: (1) control (SCM only), (2) sequential exposure SFFH representing acute (days 0–2), intermediate (days 3–9), and late (≥10) phases, (3) Group 2 with interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra), (4) Group 2 with dexamethasone, and (5) Group 2 with doxycycline. After exposure, spent media was collected. Samples were analyzed for cytokines, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), C-terminal telopeptides of type II collagen (CTX-II), and sulfated glycosaminoglycans (sGAGs), with histological assessment performed to evaluate viability and proteoglycan content.ResultsSFFH exposed cartilage showed increased levels of proinflammatory cytokines, MMPs, and cartilage breakdown products compared to controls. Treatment with IL-1Ra, dexamethasone, or doxycycline all reduced inflammatory cytokines and MMP levels. Safranin O staining, indicating proteoglycan content, was lowest in group 2. No significant difference in chondrocyte survival was observed among groups.ConclusionSequential exposure to SFFH creates a sustained proinflammatory and catabolic environment that promotes biochemical cartilage degradation. IL-1Ra, dexamethasone, and doxycycline reduced inflammatory cytokine and MMP concentrations while cartilage matrix degradation markers remained elevated and histologic preservation was not significantly improved. These findings suggest that suppression of inflammatory pathways alone may be insufficient to fully prevent cartilage injury under cumulative SFFH exposure.Clinical RelevanceAcute anti-inflammatory therapy following IAF may modulate inflammatory and catabolic mediator production within the post-fracture joint environment. Further in vivo investigation is required to determine whether these biological effects translate into durable cartilage preservation or altered PTOA risk.Level of EvidenceLaboratory study.Keywords: Ankle, Fracture, PTOA, Post-Traumatic Osteoarthritis, Inflammatory, Cytokines, Matrix-Metalloproteinase, IL-1Ra, Doxycycline, Chondrocyte, Cartilage, Damage

Introduction

Unlike hip and knee osteoarthritis (OA), ankle OA is caused by trauma in nearly 80% of cases. 1 It also affects a younger population, with presentation an average fourteen years earlier than hip or knee OA. 2 Because ankle OA most commonly arises as a sequela of trauma, its natural history can be studied from the moment of trauma. Understanding the progression of intra-articular fractures (IAFs) to posttraumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA) may reveal a time-dependent therapeutic “window” for disease-modifying intervention. However, comprehensive care still requires a deeper understanding of the physiologic and structural joint changes that follow injury. As consistent relationships between residual articular surface displacement following fracture and PTOA have not been firmly established, recent investigations have shifted from studying exclusively anatomic alignment toward examining the complex biological fracture environment. 3

Fracture severity, specifically the magnitude of articular cartilage damage, is a positive predictor of PTOA development. 1 Although the standard treatment of IAFs focuses on anatomic reduction, restoration of joint congruence, and mechanical alignment, mechanical correction alone does not halt inflammatory processes that damage otherwise uninjured cartilage. Following IAF, cartilage is rapidly exposed to hemarthrosis and a surge of proinflammatory mediators including tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 (IL-1), and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), within the synovial fluid, which contribute to chondrocyte apoptosis, necrosis, and cartilage loss.4-8 Recent studies have comprehensively characterized the temporal composition of synovial fluid fracture hematoma (SFFH), demonstrating dynamic changes in inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, matrix metalloproteinases, and cartilage degradation products across the acute, intermediate, and late phases following intra-articular fracture. 9 These studies provide the biologic basis for the sequential SFFH model used in the present investigation.

While studies have characterized the temporal composition, these phases have largely been studied independently despite cartilage being exposed to a dynamic, continuously evolving inflammatory environment following intra-articular fracture. Consequently, the deleterious effects of SFFH exposure to articular cartilage across a clinically relevant time course remain incompletely understood. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the effects of sequential exposure to acute, intermediate, and late SFFH on human cartilage explants and determine whether clinically available anti-inflammatory agents could attenuate these cumulative responses. We hypothesized that sequential exposure would promote persistent cartilage injury and that interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra), dexamethasone, and doxycycline would partially mitigate these effects.

Materials and Methods

Acquisition of Synovial Fluid Fracture Hematoma

Duke University Medical Center Institutional Review Board approval was obtained, and fluid samples were collected from patients with ankle intra-articular fractures at the time of surgical intervention, as previously described. 10 As outlined in Figure 1, cartilage discs were initially cultured in standard chondrocyte media (SCM) for three days to establish baseline conditions. Subsequently, samples were exposed to acute (days 0–2), intermediate (days 3–9), and late (>9 days) SFFH phases, simulating a defined clinical progression timeline. On day 12, discs were washed, returned to SCM for an additional three-day recovery period, and then collected for histological analysis. Parallel experimental groups were cultured in the presence of IL-1Ra, dexamethasone, or doxycycline, with sequential dosing to assess their therapeutic efficacy in mitigating inflammation-induced cartilage damage. These treatment durations were selected to approximate the clinically achievable interval between injury and definitive operative fixation following intra-articular ankle fracture, during which pharmacologic modulation of the evolving inflammatory environment may be feasible.

Experimental design and sequential SFFH exposure protocol. 1) Cartilage discs were harvested from fresh allograft tali domes. 2) The discs were cultured in SCM for 3 days. 3) The spent media was removed and stored for later analysis. 4) Cartilage explants were cultured over 12 days with sequential exposure to SFFH: 4A (days 0–2, acute), 4B (days 3–5, intermediate), 4C (days 6–9, intermediate), and 4D (≥10, late). Control discs (Group 1) were maintained in standard chondrocyte media, while experimental groups received phase-specific SFFH. Treatment groups were exposed to SFFH alone (Group 2), SFFH with IL-1Ra (Group 3), SFFH with dexamethasone (Group 4), or SFFH with doxycycline (Group 5). 5) The discs were removed from the SFFH culture environment and washed 3 times. 6) The discs were cultured for 3 days in fresh SCM. 7) Media from the culture was collected and stored for analysis. 8) Cartilage discs underwent Safranin O staining and TUNEL analysis

Collection and Culture of Human Cartilage Discs

Human talar cartilage discs were obtained and processed as previously described. 10 Briefly, 5-mm cartilage explants were created from the articular surface of 6 fresh human tali using a biopsy punch. Donor tissue was obtained from a tissue bank from specimens deemed unsuitable for implantation due to size mismatch but that passed all disease screening. Explants were weighed, washed three times, and maintained in SCM consisting of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, nonessential amino acids, HEPES buffer, penicillin/streptomycin, and L-ascorbic acid 2-phosphate.

Explants from each of the six donor tali were randomly assigned and evenly distributed across all experimental groups such that each treatment group contained explants from every donor. Consistent with the previously published cartilage explant model on which this study was based, cartilage explants were analyzed as the experimental unit. 10 Potential donor-related variability was minimized through even distribution of donor explants across treatment groups, normalization of biomarker concentrations to explant weight, and comparison with each explant’s baseline biomarker concentrations.

Explants were equilibrated in culture for 3 days at 37°C and 5% CO2 prior to experimental manipulation, after which baseline “initial” spent media were collected. Following baseline media collection, cartilage explants were cultured for 12 days under their respective conditions with routine media changes. Upon completion of treatment, explants were thoroughly washed to remove residual treatment components and transferred to fresh wells containing SCM. Discs were then cultured for an additional 3 days. This recovery period was included to allow residual SFFH and treatment agents to be removed while permitting continued release of cartilage-derived biomarkers prior to endpoint analyses, consistent with previously published cartilage explant methodology. 10 Final spent media were subsequently collected and stored for downstream analyses.

Creation and Culture of Human Cartilage Discs with SFFH with IL-1Ra, Dexamethasone, and Doxycycline

To simulate the time-dependent inflammatory milieu following intra-articular fracture, human talar cartilage explants were sequentially exposed to early, intermediate, and late SFFH. This model reflects the dynamic progression of pro-inflammatory and catabolic signaling observed in vivo. Cartilage explants were exposed to SFFH under defined treatment conditions, with evaluation of three therapeutic agents targeting inflammatory and matrix-degrading pathways. Specifically, 20 μg/ml IL-1Ra, 39 μg/ml dexamethasone, and 25 μg/ml doxycycline were administered based on their known anti-inflammatory and anti–MMP properties to assess their ability to mitigate cartilage degradation across these phases.

Analysis of Cytokines, MMPs, and sGAG

Quantification of inflammatory cytokines and MMPs in spent media was performed using multiplex immunoassays, as previously described. 10 The analytes assessed included pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines as well as MMPs implicated in cartilage degradation. Cartilage degradation was further evaluated by measuring CTX-II using a commercially available competitive immunoassay, consistent with established methods. 10

Sulfated Glycosaminoglycan Analysis

sGAG concentration in initial and final spent media samples was quantified using the tetravalent cationic dye Alcian blue in accordance with manufacturer instructions (Cat# BP-004; Kamiya Biomedical Company). 10

Histological Preparation

Following completion of culture, cartilage explants were fixed, processed, and embedded as previously described. 10 5 μm sections were obtained perpendicular to the articular surface for histologic evaluation and used for subsequent Safranin O and TUNEL evaluation.

Safranin O Staining

Proteoglycan content was assessed using Safranin O staining according to established protocols. 10 Stained sections were imaged using light microscopy at a 5× objective, and staining intensity was quantified using ImageJ software. 10 Analysis was performed on defined cartilage zones, and values were normalized across samples to enable comparison between treatment groups. Histologic assessment was performed independently by three blinded investigators experienced in cartilage histologic evaluation. Formal inter-rater reliability testing was not performed. Analyses were performed using cartilage explants as the experimental unit. No a priori power analysis was performed.

Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl Transferase dUTP Nick End Labeling Assay

Chondrocyte apoptosis was evaluated using a TUNEL assay performed according to manufacturer instructions and prior validated protocols. 10 Sections were processed, labeled, and imaged using fluorescence microscopy.

Quantitative analysis of TUNEL-positive and total nuclei (DAPI) was performed using ImageJ, applying predefined intensity and size thresholds. Cell viability was calculated as the proportion of non-apoptotic cells relative to total cells, consistent with previously described analytical methods. 10

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 6.01). Group differences were evaluated using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with treatment group and media collection time point as the two factors, followed by Holm–Sidak multiple comparisons testing, consistent with previously described methods. 10 Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 (*), with significance levels denoted as follows P < 0.01 (**), P < 0.001 (***), and a P value < 0.0001 (****). Data are displayed as the mean ± the standard error of the mean. If the concentration of a cytokine or MMP was measured to be below the manufacturer’s lower limit of detection (LLOD), the values were designated as one-half LLOD for statistical analysis.9-12 If more than half of the samples in any given category, such as “IL-2 from initial spent media,” were below the LLOD, the analyte was not included in the statistical analysis. Biomarkers excluded for this reason included IFN-γ and IL-12p70. 10 The cartilage disc weight was used to normalize cytokine, MMP, sGAG, and CTX-II concentrations. Initial and final spent media concentrations were analyzed using the two-way ANOVA described above. To quantify treatment-related changes, normalized differences between final and initial concentrations were also calculated and reported. Following procedures from previous studies, cytokines, MMPs, sGAG, CTX-II, safranin O staining, and TUNEL staining were compared for control group followed by a comparison reference group (SFFH), and the treatment groups, i.e. IL-1Ra, dexamethasone, and doxycycline. 10

Results

Cytokine, MMP, and Cartilage Breakdown Product Concentration in Experimental Groups

The concentrations of analytes in the initial and final spent media were used to compare the cartilage response to SFFH and the treatments. IL-1β, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12, IL-13, TNF-α, MMP-1, MMP-2, MMP-3, MMP-9, MMP-10, CTX-II (P < 0.01), and sGAGs (P < 0.05) all had statistically significant elevated concentrations in the samples cultured in SFFH compared with the control group. This finding is consistent with previous studies. 9 When comparing the SFFH culture samples to the samples of SFFH plus treatment (IL-1Ra, dexamethasone, or doxycycline), all of the aforementioned analytes except CTX-II and sGAGs displayed significantly decreased concentrations (P < 0.0001). CTX-II and sGAGs, however, had a significantly increased concentration in the samples treated with either doxycycline, or dexamethasone (P < 0.01). Cytokine and MMP concentrations are reported in pg/mL, CTX-II concentrations in ng/mL, and sGAG concentrations in µg/mL. SFFH-exposed cartilage discs demonstrated elevated cytokine, TNF-α, and MMP levels compared to controls. Treatment with IL-1Ra, dexamethasone, or doxycycline reduced these inflammatory and catabolic markers, although IL-2 did not follow this overall trend. Despite suppression of inflammatory mediators, cartilage matrix degradation markers, CTX-II and sGAG, remained elevated across treatment groups, indicating persistent matrix degradation despite cytokine and MMP suppression.

There was no significant difference in chondrocyte viability in any of the groups, as illustrated in Figure 4. That is, the control, SFFH culture group, and SFFH culture groups with treatments, all had similar chondrocyte viability. Though not statistically significant, the control (no exposure to SFFH) group had higher relative Safranin O staining intensity, and therefore higher proteoglycan concentration when compared to the other groups. No significant differences in relative intensity were identified between the SFFH culture group and the groups of samples cultured in SFFH and treated with either IL-1Ra, dexamethasone, or doxycycline. Discussion

The principal finding of this study is that sequential exposure to temporally evolving SFFH induces sustained inflammatory and catabolic responses in human cartilage tissue explants, and that these effects are only partially attenuated by clinically available anti-inflammatory interventions. By exposing the same cartilage explants to acute, intermediate, and late SFFH in sequence, this model more closely recapitulates the cumulative inflammatory burden experienced by cartilage during the post-fracture period, extending prior work that evaluated each SFFH phase in isolation. Although IL-1Ra, dexamethasone, and doxycycline significantly reduced SFFH-induced inflammatory cytokine and MMP production, cartilage matrix degradation markers remained elevated and histologic preservation was not significantly improved. These findings indicate that reductions in inflammatory cytokines and MMPs were not accompanied by measurable structural preservation within the relatively short duration of this study. However, the absence of detectable histologic differences at this acute time point should not be interpreted as evidence that biologic cartilage injury was absent or that longer-term matrix degeneration would not subsequently occur. Collectively, these findings suggest that sequential exposure to a clinically relevant inflammatory insult is sufficient to induce cartilage damage independent of concomitant mechanical injury at the time of trauma, and that targeted suppression of selected inflammatory pathways alone may be insufficient to fully prevent cartilage injury following cumulative exposure to the post-fracture inflammatory milieu.

IL-1Ra (Anakinra), an FDA-approved biologic for rheumatoid arthritis, has shown chondroprotective effects by blocking IL-1 signaling, reducing downstream cytokine release, and preventing cartilage degradation in both clinical and preclinical models.13,14 Similarly, dexamethasone was included as a treatment group due to its clinical relevance and availability. As a corticosteroid with strong anti-inflammatory properties, it is widely used in emergency settings to manage acute joint inflammation. 15 Prior studies have demonstrated its ability to suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α while upregulating anti-inflammatory mediators like IL-10, thereby contributing to preservation of cartilage integrity under inflammatory conditions.10,16 Doxycycline, though an antibiotic, also functions as a matrix metalloproteinase inhibitor, with studies in osteoarthritis models demonstrating reductions in cartilage destruction, subchondral bone changes, and cyst formation. 17 Together, these agents represent clinically relevant examples of how targeted anti-inflammatory and anti-catabolic strategies can attenuate inflammatory and catabolic mediator production. 18

Whereas Allen et al. evaluated the biologic effects of isolated acute, intermediate, and late SFFH environments, the present study investigated cartilage responses to sequential exposure across all inflammatory phases, thereby more closely modeling the temporal evolution of the post-fracture joint environment. To our knowledge, this represents one of the most clinically relevant and reproducible ex vivo models currently available for investigating the cumulative effects of the evolving post-fracture inflammatory environment on human articular cartilage. Consistent with prior studies, sequential exposure produced elevations of inflammatory cytokines, MMPs, CTX-II, and sGAG. 10 Continuous treatment exposure was selected to model a clinically realistic therapeutic strategy that could potentially be initiated shortly after injury and maintained until definitive fixation, which commonly occurs approximately 10–12 days after fracture. 19

The findings of this study demonstrate that selected anti-inflammatory agents, specifically IL-1Ra and doxycycline, partially attenuate the spike of pro-inflammatory cytokines, MMPs, and cartilage breakdown products that occur after cartilage is exposed to SFFH. Dexamethasone also reduced several analyte levels, though its effects were less consistent. These results suggest that timely administration of intra-articular anti-inflammatory agents may modulate selected inflammatory pathways implicated in PTOA development. However, persistent elevations in CTX-II and sGAG despite cytokine and MMP suppression indicate that additional mechanisms likely contribute to cartilage injury. Consequently, cytokine suppression alone should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of cartilage protection. Looking ahead, clinically available agents such as IL-1Ra (Anakinra, SOBI, Stockholm, SE; marketed as Kineret) or MMP inhibitors like doxycycline warrant further investigation as potential strategies to modulate post-fracture inflammatory responses following IAF. Preclinical models, such as sustained IL-1Ra delivery in murine PTOA, already support this approach, demonstrating reduced synovitis and cartilage degradation.14,17

The inflammatory composition of SFFH has previously been characterized in detail, including temporal alterations in cytokines, chemokines, matrix metalloproteinases, and cartilage degradation products. 9 The present study therefore did not seek to re-characterize these established inflammatory profiles, but instead used this previously validated sequential SFFH model to evaluate the cumulative biologic response of cartilage to evolving post-fracture conditions. Potential contributors, not studied, may have also contributed to our findings including red blood cell degradation products, iron-mediated oxidative stress, complement activation, damage-associated molecular pattern signaling, and other soluble mediators. Consequently, the present findings establish biological association rather than direct causation. Future studies incorporating more comprehensive characterization of SFFH composition may provide additional mechanistic insight into pathways driving cartilage degeneration following fracture.

While this study demonstrated that selected anti-inflammatory agents can partially attenuate inflammatory responses resulting from SFFH, future work should explore additional treatment strategies. For instance, joint lavage to remove SFFH may prove to be an equally effective and potentially more cost-efficient option. 10 Moreover, long-term investigations are necessary to determine whether early intra-articular interventions following ankle fracture translate into meaningful clinical outcomes, such as reduced rates of PTOA development and improved long-term joint function. Together, these directions may help refine early, minimally invasive approaches to preserve cartilage health and better understand strategies for modifying PTOA risk.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. This study utilized an in vitro cartilage explant model that does not incorporate synovial tissue, subchondral bone, vascular responses, systemic immune influences, and other factors that contribute to PTOA development in vivo. Although fresh donor tali without gross articular abnormalities were utilized, pre-existing donor-specific variation in cartilage quality cannot be completely excluded. The present study was not designed to characterize the specific biologic mediators responsible for cartilage injury. Outcome measures primarily consisted of secreted biomarkers and limited histologic assessment. RNA sequencing, gene-expression analyses, intracellular signaling studies, and broader proteomic characterization were not performed. Previous investigations have demonstrated that SFFH alters chondrocyte gene-expression profiles, suggesting biologic responses not captured by the current endpoints. Accordingly, the inability to detect differences in terminal cartilage degradation markers should not be interpreted as evidence that cartilage biology remained unchanged. 9

Furthermore, downstream histologic changes often lag behind early molecular and metabolic alterations in cartilage. Consequently, the absence of significant differences in Safranin O staining or TUNEL analysis after approximately two weeks of culture does not exclude ongoing biologic processes that may ultimately contribute to progressive cartilage degeneration and PTOA. Further in vivo studies are necessary to determine whether modulation of inflammatory mediators translates into durable cartilage protection from IAF.

In conclusion, sequential exposure to temporally evolving SFFH produces a sustained inflammatory and catabolic environment that contributes to early cartilage degradation following intra-articular fracture. By exposing the same human cartilage explants to acute, intermediate, and late SFFH in sequence, this study provides a more clinically representative model of the cumulative inflammatory burden experienced by cartilage during the post-fracture period, extending prior work that evaluated individual SFFH timepoints in isolation. Although IL-1Ra, dexamethasone, and doxycycline reduced inflammatory mediator production, these interventions did not fully preserve markers of cartilage matrix integrity or histologic cartilage structure under cumulative SFFH exposure. Collectively, these findings suggest that post-fracture inflammation alone, even in the absence of concomitant mechanical cartilage injury, may be sufficient to promote cartilage damage and that targeted inflammatory modulation alone may be insufficient to fully mitigate this process. This work establishes a clinically relevant ex vivo platform for studying human cartilage responses to sequential post-fracture inflammatory exposure and supports future investigation into combination therapies and optimized treatment strategies aimed at improving cartilage preservation after intra-articular fracture.
