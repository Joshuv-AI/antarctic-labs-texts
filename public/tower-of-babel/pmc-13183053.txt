The association between Life’s Crucial 9 and cardiometabolic multimorbidity among US older adults: Research based on machine learning
Authors: Xueying Wei, Lingxiao Zheng, Qianhui Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To explore the association between Life’s Crucial 9 (LC9) and cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) in American adults aged ≥60 years, as well as key LC9 components. Using data from the National Health and National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2005 to 2018, 4913 participants were included. Weighted logistic regression, restricted cubic splines, subgroup analyses, and 4 machine learning models (e.g., XGBoost) were used. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the curve, and SHAP analysis was used to interpret the optimal model. Each 10-point increase in LC9 was associated with a 48% lower CMM risk (OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.47–0.58, P < .001), with a significant nonlinear relationship. XGBoost exhibited the best predictive performance (area under the curve = 0.829). Fasting blood glucose, serum creatinine (Scr), and the poverty income ratio were the top predictive factors. Higher LC9 scores are strongly and nonlinearly linked to reduced CMM risk in older adults. XGBoost combined with SHAP is a useful tool for CMM risk stratification, highlighting blood glucose level as a key factor for CMM based on LC9.

1. Introduction

Against the backdrop of a rapidly aging global population, research into and management of the health of older adults has become critically important, particularly in light of the increasing burden of age-related comorbidities.[1] Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) refers to the simultaneous presence of 2 or more cardiometabolic disorders (CMDs), such as diabetes, myocardial infarction (MI), and stroke.[2] According to reports, the incidence of CMM in the population aged ≥ 60 years is several times higher than that in the population aged ≥ 40 years.[3] Compared with a single CMD, CMM imposes a greater health burden. Individuals diagnosed with CMM exhibit a substantially reduced life expectancy, with estimated losses ranging from 12 to 15 years at age 60, along with a markedly elevated all-cause mortality risk, reported to be 3.7–6.9 times higher compared to those without CMM.[4] Therefore, the development and implementation of targeted strategies for the prevention and management of CMM represent a critical priority with substantial implications for global public health policy and practice.

The American Heart Association proposed a cardiovascular health indicator including scores of multiple lifestyles, called Life’s Simple 7 (LS7) in 2010, which was developed into Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) in 2020.[5] A novel cardiovascular health metric, Life’s Crucial 9 (LC9), was recently formulated through the incorporation of depression symptom assessment into the existing LE8 framework.[6] The association between LC9 score and other health outcomes, such as periodontitis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, stroke, and cardiorenal syndrome.[7-10] All these studies indicated a strong negative correlation between LC9 scores and the risks of the above health outcomes. In addition, prior research has demonstrated a significant inverse association between higher LE8 scores and the prevalence of CMM.[11] However, the correlation between LC9 and CMM has not yet been fully explored.

In the field of epidemiological research, traditional statistical methods are often limited in their ability to manage high-dimensional datasets and to model complex nonlinear relationships and interaction effects among variables.[12] In contrast, ML offers distinct advantages for addressing these challenges owing to its flexibility in capturing intricate patterns and dependencies within data. Consequently, ML techniques are increasingly being integrated into medical and public health research.[13] Furthermore, ML algorithms are particularly effective in identifying the most influential exposure variables from a large set of predictors, thereby facilitating the prioritization of risk factors and enhancing resource allocation.[14] This capability supports evidence-based clinical decision making and contributes to the optimization of limited healthcare resources.

To address these gaps, we investigated the association between LC9 scores and CMM using data in older adults from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). We established a large-scale cross-sectional study and used advanced ML technology. Owing to the serious limitations of ML in explaining the direction of correlations between variables, we additionally applied SHapley Additive exPlans (SHAP) analysis to address this issue to identify the variables included in LC9, which are most closely associated with CMM in older adults.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. The study design and population

The NHANES is a recurring cross-sectional survey designed to collect comprehensive health and nutritional data from a nationally representative sample of the noninstitutionalized US population. For this study, we utilized data sourced from publicly available NHANES repositories (www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/). All survey procedures strictly adhered to the federal ethical guidelines and were reviewed and approved by the institutional review board.

We initially included 13,481 participants aged ≥60 years from the NHANES 2005 to 2018 cycles. After applying the exclusion criteria, 5422 individuals with incomplete CMM assessment data were removed. From the remaining 8059 participants, an additional 2590 participants were excluded because of insufficient data for calculating LC9 scores. Thereafter, 556 participants with missing covariate information were excluded, resulting in a final analytical sample of 4913 individuals included in the study (Fig. 1).

2.2. Assessment of LC9 scores and CMM

The calculation of LC9 scores involves 9 variables: blood pressure, total cholesterol, blood sugar, tobacco, body mass index, physical activity, diet, sleep, and mental health.[15] The detailed calculation method for the scores is presented in Table S1. Healthy eating patterns were evaluated using the 2015 Healthy Eating Index (HEI-2015). Dietary data were the average of the 2 dietary survey data sets. The detailed calculation method for the HEI-2015 is presented in Table S2. Other laboratory indicators and physical examinations were conducted according to strict measurement standards. Mental health was evaluated using the Patient Health Questionnaire 9 (PHQ-9). The average of the 9 scores for each research subject was the LC9 score.

CMM is characterized by the coexistence of CMDs including diabetes, MI, and stroke. Diabetes was defined as any of the following criteria: self-reported physician diagnosis, current use of glucose-lowering medications, or fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL or hemoglobin A1c ≥ 6.5%.[16] Information on MI and stroke was obtained from self-reported medical histories. A CMM is characterized by the coexistence of 2 or more CMDs.

2.3. Covariates

Because LC9 already includes many lifestyle factors, behavioral factors, and laboratory indicators, the covariates in this study only included demographic variables such as age, sex, race, poverty income ratio (PIR), education and marital status, drinking status, cancer, chronic kidney disease (CKD), serum uric acid (UA), and serum creatinine (Scr).[17,18] Except for age, UA, and Scr, which were continuous variables, all other variables were categorical. Please refer to Table S3 for detailed definitions of each covariate.

2.4. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.5.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Statistical significance was set P < .05. Examination weights from the Mobile Examination Center were applied in our analyses.[19] Continuous variables were depicted using weighted means (standard deviation), and categorical variables were expressed as counts (weighted percentages). We contrasted the distribution differences of the variables between the CMM and non-CMM groups using t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. To examine the association between LC9 scores and CMM, we conducted weighted logistic regression analyses to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).[20] Three sequential models were fitted: crude model (model 1), model adjusted for demographic factors (model 2), and fully adjusted model incorporating all covariates (model 3). To assess potential nonlinear dependencies, restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was employed.[21] Additionally, subgroup analyses were performed to investigate the potential interaction effects between LC9 scores and other covariates on the prevalence of CMM.[22]

Subsequently, to comprehensively investigate the associations between the 9 components of LC9 scores, covariates, and CMM, we employed ML methodologies. The entire dataset was randomly partitioned into a training set (70%) for model development and a test set (30%) to assess generalization performance. Given the substantial class imbalance between the non-CMM and CMM groups, which could adversely affect model training, we applied the synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) to balance the 2 classes to a 1:1 ratio within the training set.[23] Random Forest, k-nearest neighbors (KNN), neural network (NNET), and extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) were chosen to train the model. By performing 10-fold cross-validation and grid search on the training set to adjust the hyperparameters, the optimal hyperparameters are obtained and then retrained on the training set to predict the test set. The model performance was evaluated using multiple metrics, including the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity and specificity.[24] The algorithm with the best performance was included in further analysis. Finally, the SHAP analysis was used to evaluate the importance of each variable and the direction of their association with CMM.[25]

3.2. Association of LC9 scores with CMM risk

The results of the weighted logistic regression models are presented in Table 2. A 10-point increase in LC9 scores was associated with a 48% lower CMM risk after considering all covariates (OR = 0.52, 95%CI 0.47–0.58, P < .001). When LC9 scores were converted into categorical variables, the results showed 52%, 66%, and 91% decreased risks in the higher quartile (Q2, Q3, and Q4) versus the lowest quartile (OR = 0.48, 95%CI: 0.35–0.66, P = .004; OR = 0.34, 95%CI: 0.25–0.46, P = .004; OR = 0.09, 95%CI: 0.05–0.16, P < .001), with a significant dose-response trend (P for trend < .001).

The results of the RCS regression model are shown in Figure S2. After adjusting for all covariates, higher LC9 scores were nonlinearly associated with a lower risk of CMM (P for overall < .001 and P for nonlinear = .021).

3.3. Subgroup analyses

We examined the associations between LC9 scores and CMM across subgroups stratified by sex, age (60–69 years and ≥70 years), race, education, PIR, marital status, drinking status, CKD, and cancer (Fig. S3). In the majority of these subgroups, higher LC9 scores were significantly associated with a reduced risk of CMM, which was consistent with the primary analysis. Notably, no significant interaction was observed between LC9 scores and any of the covariates (All P > .05), indicating that the protective association of LC9 scores against CMM remained consistent and robust across diverse population strata.

The performance of the optimal hyperparameters determined through 10-fold cross-validation and grid search on the training set in the test set is shown in Table 3. Overall, all 4 algorithms performed well, with AUC values above 0.70 and accuracy rates reaching 0.60. Among the models evaluated, the XGBoost algorithm demonstrated superior performance, achieving an AUC of 0.829, an accuracy of 0.780, and high sensitivity and specificity, indicating a robust discriminative capability. Figure 1 shows the receiver operating characteristic curves of the 4 algorithms in detail. Therefore, we chose the XGBoost algorithm for the next step of the analysis.

3.5. SHAP analysis

We then established an SHAP analysis based on the XGBoost algorithm. Figure 2 shows the results of the SHAP analysis for the prediction of the CMM. Figure 2A shows a honeycomb plot that displays the global feature importance and the direction of each feature’s impact on the model’s prediction. Each dot represents a single individual in the dataset. The features were ranked vertically from top to bottom based on their global importance. The top 5 most important variables were blood glucose, Scr, UA, PIR, and physical activity. The X-axis (SHAP value) indicates the impact of a feature on the prediction for that individual; a positive SHAP value increases the predicted risk of CMM, whereas a negative value decreases it. Blood glucose level is the most important predictive factor. Higher values of blood glucose score, age, UA, Scr, and physical activity score were associated with an increased risk of CMM. Conversely, a higher PIR value is associated with a reduced risk of CMM. Figure 2B shows the force plot that explains the model’s prediction for a single randomly selected individual. The base value E[f(x)] = 0.113 was the average prediction of the model. For this specific individual, features such as age (78 years) and blood glucose score (30) significantly increased their predicted risk (+0.0213 and +0.0687, respectively). However, features such as a PHQ-9 score of 100 and a tobacco score of 100 decreased their risk (−0.0295 and −0.0169, respectively).

4. Discussion

In this study, we explored the relationship between dietary LC9 scores and the risk of CMM in older adults using data from the NHANES 2005 to 2018. Weighted logistic regression models and RCS analysis indicated a significant negative correlation between LC9 scores and the risk of CMM. Subgroup analyses indicated that this association was robust across all subgroups. Importantly, ML methods were employed to complement traditional statistical approaches, leveraging their respective strengths – with traditional models providing robust inference and hypothesis testing, while ML techniques excel at prediction and pattern recognition in complex datasets. The XGboost algorithm exhibited the best predictive performance for the test set. In addition, Shap analysis further confirmed that the blood glucose score was the most closely associated variable with CMM among all variables. These findings substantiate a significant inverse relationship between elevated LC9 scores and the risk of CMM in older adults.

Our findings align with and extend previous research on cardiovascular health metrics and multimorbidity. The inverse association between LC9 scores and CMM risk is consistent with studies focusing on its predecessor, LE8, which also reported protective effects against CMM.[11,26,27] The incorporation of depressive symptoms via the PHQ-9 into the LC9 score enhances its clinical utility, reflecting the growing recognition of mental health as a critical component of cardiovascular wellness.[28] Moreover, older adults are more likely to suffer from CMD. This study aimed to elucidate the association between LC9 and CMM, with a particular focus on the role of age-related factors in older adults, to provide new medical recommendations to promote cardiovascular health in older adults.

It is widely recognized that individuals are characterized by a heterogeneous set of attributes, encompassing demographic factors (e.g., age, gender), behavioral and lifestyle variables, as well as a range of clinical biochemical indicators. Although multivariate regression models can incorporate multiple variables simultaneously, they have limitations in handling high-dimensional data and identifying complex interaction effects between variables.[29] The application of ML models in this study addresses the notable limitations of traditional statistical methods in handling complex high-dimensional datasets.[30] Our modeling methodology rigorously adheres to the establishment of best practices for predictive analytics. In accordance with these standards, we leveraged a suite of 4 distinct ML algorithms to ensure a comprehensive and robust analysis. Among the 4 algorithms, XGBoost performed the best. XGBoost is an advanced and efficient ML algorithm that belongs to the gradient boosting framework for ensemble learning. It has performed well in many epidemiological studies.[31-33] We further used SHAP analysis to reveal that the variable most closely associated with CMM among all variables is the blood glucose score, and the lower the score, the greater the risk of developing CMM. High blood glucose levels can directly damage endothelial cells, leading to abnormal function.[34] Blood vessels are more prone to constriction, accelerating thrombus formation and leading to the occurrence of cardiovascular diseases (CVD).[35] Under hyperglycemia, oxidative stress and inflammation intensify, leading to insulin resistance and accelerating the occurrence of diabetes.[36,37] In summary, the blood glucose metric demonstrated the strongest association with CMM, not only among the 9 essential health scores but also across all variables examined in this study. Therefore, actively monitoring the blood sugar levels of the older adults may be an important measure for improving cardiometabolic health.

This study had several notable strengths. First, we explored the association between LC9 and CMM in older adults, providing new insights into the applicability of LC9 in specific populations. Second, the utilization of data from the NHANES, a large-scale, nationally representative survey with a complex sampling design, enhances the generalizability of our findings to the noninstitutionalized US older adults. Third, the application of both traditional weighted logistic regression and advanced ML approaches, complemented by the SHAP analysis, provides a comprehensive and robust analytical framework. This multi-method strategy not only confirmed a strong and independent inverse association between LC9 scores and CMM but also identified and interpreted the most influential predictive features, such as blood glucose, thereby bridging traditional epidemiology with interpretable artificial intelligence.

Despite these strengths, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional nature of the NHANES data precludes the establishment of causal relationships between the LC9 scores and CMM risk. Second, despite meticulous adjustment, residual confounding may persist due to unmeasured factors such as genetic predisposition. Third, although SMOTE was employed to mitigate class imbalance, the relatively limited number of CMM cases compared with controls may still influence the stability and generalizability of the ML models. In addition, certain variables, including a history of myocardial infarction and stroke, relied on self-reporting, which is susceptible to recall bias. Finally, due to the large number of participants lacking LC9 data, the sample size of this study is limited, which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Future research should prioritize prospective cohort studies or intervention trials to corroborate our findings and establish causality.

5. Conclusion

In summary, our analysis elucidates a significant association between LC9 and CMM risk in older US people. Importantly, ML analysis revealed that blood glucose played a dominant role in all variables. Further research is required to validate our conclusions.
