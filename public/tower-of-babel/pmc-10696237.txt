Introduction

Fractures of the tibial plateau pose a complex clinical entity in orthopedic trauma. They are often associated with high-energy trauma and can lead to significant complications, such as joint dysfunction and deformity. Optimal management of these fractures involves accurate assessment, appropriate classification, and tailored surgical intervention [1,2].

However, the management of tibial plateau fractures in patients with unfused growth plates remains a challenge. The persistence of open growth plates in the knee region in adults is a rare presentation, and its presence can significantly influence treatment options and outcomes [3,4].

In this article, we present a case report of a tibial plateau fracture in a 23-year-old young adult with an unfused growth plate. The complexity of managing this fracture was further compounded by the presence of the non-fused growth plate, necessitating a carefully planned and adapted approach.

The objective of this case report is to highlight the unique challenges associated with managing tibial plateau fractures in patients with open growth plates in adults. We will discuss the diagnostic evaluation, fracture classification, treatment options considered, and the outcomes achieved.

We hope that this clinical observation will contribute to a better understanding of this rare presentation of tibial plateau fractures and improve the management of such patients.

Methods

This retrospective case report evaluates a patient diagnosed with a tibial plateau fracture and an unfused growth plate. The examination and treatment were carried out at the 32nd Pavilion of Orthopedics and Traumatology, Ibn Rochd Hospital, Casablanca.

The documentation and presentation of this work adhere to The SCARE 2020 guideline: updating consensus Surgical Case Report (SCARE) guidelines. [10], ensuring compliance with standards for high-quality reporting in case reports.

Case presentation

A 23-year-old male patient was evaluated after being struck by a car, resulting in a closed knee injury.

On physical examination, significant swelling of the left knee was noted, with loss of bony and muscular landmarks, including the anterior tibial tuberosity, patella, and knee condyles (Fig. 1) and with a valgus deformity. The patient experienced complete functional impairment and reported tenderness upon palpation or movement of the left knee. However, there were no neurovascular deficits noted, with intact sciatic nerve and palpable pedal and retro-malleolar pulses.

Clinical appearance showing the knee valgus deformity.

Radiographic assessment, including anteroposterior and lateral views of the knee, revealed a Salter and Harris type 4 fracture of the proximal tibia (see Fig. 2). Although the fracture was significant, the patient's growth plate remained intact, as evidenced by a hand X-ray to determine skeletal maturity, which was approximated to be 15 years, give or take six months. It was intended to perform a CT scan prior to the procedure, but due to its unavailability at our facility during that period and the patient's limited financial means, we had no choice but to proceed with the treatment plan based on the information available (Fig. 3).

preoperative radiograph.

Bone age radiograph of the left hand and wrist.

The patient was admitted to the hospital for the placement of a rigid knee orthosis. They received level 2 analgesic treatment, consisting of codeine and paracetamol, and a prophylactic dose of 4000 IU sodium enoxaparin, administered as a single daily injection. After proper anesthetic preparation, an open reduction and internal fixation were carried out through an external approach to the knee. A T-shaped anatomical plate with three holes, all of which were used, along with two compression screws, was utilized for osteosynthesis.

Postoperatively, a satisfactory alignment was confirmed on a follow-up X-ray (Fig. 4).

Postoperative radiograph.

The patient experienced a smooth recovery, with the polyvinyl chloride (PVC) Redon drain, size 16, being removed on the first postoperative day, and the wound healing cleanly without any indications of infection. Immobilization with a rigid knee orthosis was employed for pain relief for one week only. After three months, the patient was cleared for full weight-bearing, and passive rehabilitation commenced at the three-week mark.

At a 6-month follow-up, the patient exhibited a favorable clinical outcome, with a pain-free knee and normal joint range of motion (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Tibial plateau fractures are intricate injuries that necessitate precise management to ensure positive outcomes. In this case report, we detail the uncommon presentation of a tibial plateau fracture in a 23-year-old patient whose growth plate remained intact. The challenge of addressing this fracture was heightened by the necessity to account for the patient's age and the potential implications for growth and lasting outcomes [1,2].

Classifying tibial plateau fractures is essential in directing treatment choices. Based on the Salter-Harris classification system, our patient's fracture was designated as type 2. This classification assists clinicians in selecting the most suitable treatment modality and in forecasting the prognosis contingent on the fracture's configuration [3].

When addressing tibial plateau fractures, the primary objectives are to reestablish joint alignment, secure reliable fixation, encourage prompt mobilization, and circumvent potential complications. Surgical measures are typically mandated to ensure proper repositioning and stabilization of the fractured segments. In this instance, we conducted an open reduction and internal fixation via an external approach to the knee. An anatomical T-shaped plate and compression screws were employed. This technique facilitated precise alignment and robust stabilization, fostering ideal healing and facilitating the patient's return to normal function [4,5].

In younger patients still possessing open growth plates, safeguarding the growth plate is paramount, as its premature closure can induce growth anomalies and malformations. The intact status of our patient's growth plate was corroborated by a hand X-ray, revealing a skeletal age of approximately 15 years ±6 months. This crucial insight guided our therapeutic choices to ensure that the growth potential was not compromised [6].

Post-surgery care and rehabilitation play a pivotal role in achieving desired outcomes post tibial plateau fractures. Initiating movement early, progressive weight-bearing, and a regimented rehabilitation program are pivotal in reestablishing knee functionality and limiting complications. For our patient, unrestricted weight-bearing was sanctioned at the three-month mark, and a non-aggressive rehabilitation program was kickstarted three weeks post-surgery. The six-month follow-up revealed a pain-free knee exhibiting normal joint mobility [7].

Given the infrequency of tibial plateau fractures in young adults retaining an intact growth plate, formulating definitive assertions based on a solitary case remains a challenge. Nevertheless, this case underscores the imperative of a collaborative strategy, encompassing both orthopedic and pediatric orthopedic expertise, when managing such intricate fractures. Augmented research and additional case accounts are essential to refine our grasp of the best treatment protocols and long-haul consequences for this distinct patient group [8,9].

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case report highlights the successful management of a tibial plateau fracture in a 23-year-old patient with a preserved growth plate. The complexity of the fracture, coupled with the patient's young age and the potential impact on growth, necessitated careful consideration and a multidisciplinary approach. The utilization of an open reduction and internal fixation technique, guided by appropriate classification and surgical principles, resulted in a favorable outcome with pain-free knee function and normal joint range of motion at the 6-month follow-up.

The preservation of the growth plate was of utmost importance to ensure optimal growth potential and prevent growth disturbances. The identification of the preserved growth plate through skeletal age assessment aided in treatment planning and allowed for fracture fixation without interference with future growth.

This case report contributes to the existing literature by providing insights into the management of tibial plateau fractures in young patients with open growth plates. It emphasizes the importance of accurate classification, appropriate surgical techniques, and postoperative rehabilitation in achieving successful outcomes. Further studies and case reports are warranted to expand our understanding of optimal management strategies and long-term outcomes in this specific patient population.
