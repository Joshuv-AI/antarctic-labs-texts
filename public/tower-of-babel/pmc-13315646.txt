Impact of partial cannabis legalisation in Luxembourg: An interdisciplinary perspective
Authors: Nadine Berndt, Serge Schneider, Stefania Oliverio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn 2023, Luxembourg partially legalised cannabis, permitting home cultivation and private-domain use. The current study aimed to evaluate the short-term impacts of this reform on consumption patterns, market dynamics, product potency, and cannabis-related driving-under-the-influence cases (DUI).MethodsAn interdisciplinary approach was applied, combining data from cannabis seizures, wastewater analysis, toxicological data from DUI cases, and a WEB-based survey among adults who indicated to have used cannabis in the past 12 months. Indicators were compared before and after the policy change.ResultsMost indicators suggested a limited overall impact of partial legalisation in the year following the reform. Tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) concentrations in seized herbal and resin samples remained stable, as did THC metabolite loads in wastewater. Results of the WEB-based survey indicated little change in patterns of use, prices, or acquisition channels, with most survey respondents continuing to purchase from illicit sources. Only a minority of respondents reported personal or intended future cultivation. In contrast, cannabis-related DUI cases increased significantly. Although average THC concentrations in blood samples remained unchanged, elevated levels of THC metabolites suggested a potential increase in chronic use among some individuals.ConclusionPartial cannabis legalisation in Luxembourg appears to have had only moderate impacts on market dynamics, patterns of use, and product potency during the first year, but was associated with an increase in cannabis-related DUI cases. Similar to findings in other countries, new initiation of cannabis consumption was limited, while people with an established pattern of frequent use may have intensified their use. These findings highlight the need for continued monitoring of impaired driving, cannabis consumption patterns, and emerging trends in self-cultivation, alongside public education on health risks.Keywords: Cannabis policy reform, drug market, wastewater analysis, driving under the influence, consumer surveys

Background

For many decades, cannabis has remained the most commonly consumed illicit drug in Europe and worldwide. Recent surveys show that 8.4% of European adults (aged 15–64 years) have used cannabis in the last year, with around 1.5% estimated to use cannabis on a daily or almost daily basis (European Union Drugs Agency, 2025a). Similar observations have been documented in Luxembourg with surveys indicating that 5.4% of adults aged 15–64 had used cannabis during the past year (Berndt et al., 2025) and 21.4% of pupils aged 11–18 had used cannabis at least once in their lifetime (Catunda and Mendes, 2024). Cannabis is further associated with the most seizures and drug law offences, confirming its high availability on European markets (European Union Drugs Agency, 2025a).

To respond to the use of cannabis and its illicit market, several countries have revised, or are considering revising, their regulatory frameworks governing recreational cannabis use (Manthey et al., 2021, Belackova et al., 2025). From a legal and economic perspective, revisions are generally driven by efforts to remove cannabis from public spaces, reduce drug-related crime and violence, provide a regulated market for cannabis and cannabis-derived products, and generate economic benefits (Auriol et al., 2023, Kilmer, 2019, Rogeberg, 2018). From a public health perspective, the aim is to raise awareness of the dangers of regular cannabis use and thus help reduce adverse health outcomes (Pearlson et al., 2021). In 2013, Uruguay was the first country to regulate recreational cannabis. Since this pioneering move, policies have evolved worldwide and led, amongst other developments, to the creation of legal or decriminalised cannabis markets in the Americas, several countries in Europe and South Africa (EMCDDA, 2023; European Union Drugs Agency, 2025a).

In July 2023, Luxembourg implemented a legal framework authorising the home cultivation of cannabis. Since 21st July 2023, up to four cannabis plants may be legally cultivated per household, the place of cultivation must be the place of habitual residence, and the plants must not be visible from public spaces. Personal consumption of cannabis is permitted in the private sphere only. Objectives of the new law are to reduce the share of cannabis trade by drug dealers, shift its consumption from the public to the private domain, and to reduce the health risks of potential contaminated cannabis products from the illicit market. While consumption, possession, transport, and acquisition in the public domain remain illegal, the new law has further eased the criminal procedures and penalties for cannabis use and possession in public. A simplified legal procedure, including a 145€ fine, applies if up to 3 g of cannabis are seized in public. For minors (< 18 years), possession and consumption remain prohibited. The legal limit for driving under the influence of cannabis stayed unchanged, with the existing level of detection and punishment remaining at 1 ng/mL THC in serum (Ministère d’Etat, 2023). Prior to the reform adopted in July 2023, cannabis was governed by a predominantly prohibitionist legal framework in Luxembourg, under which its production, distribution, possession, and use were generally subject to criminal sanctions, with limited exceptions for medical or scientific purposes. However, following the 2001 amendment to the Law of 19th February 1973, the use and possession of minor quantities for personal consumption had already been decriminalised, being punishable only by administrative or pecuniary penalties rather than custodial measures (European Monitoring Centre for Drugs and Drug Addiction, 2023; Le Gouvernement du Grand-Duché de Luxembourg, 2026).

Cannabis regulatory changes emphasise the critical need for systematic monitoring and evaluation of public health and social consequences of the evolving policies, thereby informing decision makers and guiding evidence-based drug policy formulation. Indicators from different domains may be evaluated to gain better insights in the impact of a cannabis policy change (Fischer et al., 2019). As such, although general population surveys are considered as the primary and most widely accepted method to assess cannabis use prevalence rates (Goodman et al., 2019), persons who use cannabis may often be underrepresented in national or household surveys due to stigma, privacy concerns, or sampling biases (Levy et al., 2022, Skliamis et al., 2022). Targeted surveys, such as the European WEB Survey on Drugs (EWSD), represent a useful methodological tool for capturing data from individuals who directly engage with cannabis markets, enabling detection of policy-responsive shifts in consumption patterns, such as changes in frequency of use or acquisition behaviours, that may not be observable through general population surveys (Barratt et al., 2017, European Union Drugs Agency, 2025b).

The quality and the quantity of seized samples provide essential information about illicit market shifts, while an important goal of a cannabis law change is to reduce the black market (Bouchard et al., 2025). In Europe, cannabis seizures remain at historically high levels and current research suggests that legalisation may not be consistently associated with an increase or decrease in seizure rates in Europe (Manthey et al., 2023). Research findings from the US showed that black markets persisted after legalisation, possibly because overregulation „may create an environment in which operating in the illegal market may be a reasonable option“ (Song, 2019). Considering that private small-scale growers may fear high investment costs, and may lack the space, time, or knowledge to implement self-supply (Ministère de la Santé et de la Sécurité Sociale et al., 2025), the cheap and easy to access black market may remain an attractive alternative (Belackova et al., 2015).

Another indicator gaining importance in recent years and allowing real-time insight into geographical and temporal trends of cannabis is the analysis and quantification of cannabis residues in wastewater. Monitoring drugs and their metabolites in wastewater has proven a valuable, non-invasive tool to estimate drug consumption in selected areas and population. Although results are prone to high uncertainty levels due to drug instability, unknown excretion rates, varying water flow rates, etc. (Bijlsma et al., 2024), the wastewater based epidemiology is unbiased and, if carried out in a consistent way, allows to monitor consumption trends and compare results obtained at different places and periods of time (Yi et al., 2023).

One more relevant indicator providing insight into the impact of a policy change is the number of cases of driving under the influence of drugs (DUID) and here specifically, cannabis. From 2020 to mid−2025, over 100 review articles on cannabis consumption and driving are listed in PubMed. The recurring issue is whether legalisation of recreational cannabis may lead to a change in car traffic accidents. While most studies report a positive correlation with legalisation being associated with increases in rates of fatal motor vehicle collisions (Windle et al., 2021), Voy (2023) reported that some evidence suggests that recreational cannabis legalisation led to fewer fatal and serious injury collisions, although others do not report a relationship at all between cannabis consumption and risks of having an accident (Voy, 2023). This shows that in general, mixed results, predominantly from the US, were acquired so far (Farrelly et al., 2023) and no clear evidence about road safety and traffic accidents can be drawn (González-Sala et al., 2023; Jin et al., 2025), even though a rise in acute THC-related intoxications have been reported (Allaf et al., 2023).

To date, there is a lack of insight about the impact of the partial cannabis legalisation implemented in Luxembourg. A study published by the Ministry of Health and Social Security on the initial effects of Luxembourg’s cannabis policy reform conducted two months after the law change, revealed a limited impact on overall consumption patterns, with most persons who use cannabis reporting no change in use, a minority reporting a slight increase in frequency, and a minority reporting initiation of home cultivation of cannabis plants (Ministère de la Santé et de la Sécurité Sociale, 2025). The current study aims to fill this gap and enhance the understanding of the situation after the law change implemented on 21 July 2023 combining epidemiological indicators on cannabis one year before and one year after the partial legalisation. Our goal was to evaluate different consumption and market indicators including cannabis patterns of use and acquisition habits, drug seizures, DUID cases, and wastewater residues to obtain a robust estimation of the impact of the policy change in Luxembourg.

Methods

Seized cannabis and hashish samples

Samples included in the study were fresh or dried herbal and resin samples only. All samples originated from nationwide police seizures. They were collected from 21/07/2022–21/07/2024. Seizures made by the customs officers, however, were excluded from the study as they might have been transiting samples.

Joints and all other mixtures containing tobacco were excluded as determination of THC content in the cannabis part of the mixture was not feasible. Moreover, young plants without flowers, degraded samples, or samples without an indication of the date of seizure were excluded from the study. In accordance with UNODC recommendations (Cole et al., 2025), duplicate analyses were carried out. Gas chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry (GC-MS) was used to confirm the presence of cannabinoids and to exclude presence of other compounds, such as synthetic cannabinoids or other new psychoactive substances (NPS). High-performance liquid chromatography with diode array detector (HPLC-DAD) was used for the quantification of THC using an accredited method.

Cannabis metabolite in wastewater

Wastewater analysis began in January 2023. Three treatment plants, serving approximately 52,000 people (about 8% of the Luxembourgish population), were included in the study. The wastewater treatment plants were selected as they had an accredited time-dependent sampling system, and they roughly represented the Luxembourgish general population (urban/rural, demographic and social mixture). Samples were collected once a week on different weekdays. They were collected in sterile glass bottles and stored at −20°C prior to analysis. After filtration, solid phase extraction was used to clean and concentrate 11-nor−9-carboxy-Δ9-THC, the major THC metabolite. Quantification was performed using deuterated 11-nor−9-carboxy-Δ9-THC and a validated LC-QToF method (liquid chromatography coupled to quadrupole time-of-flight spectrometry).

Driving under the influence of drugs and positive cannabis tests among drivers

For the study, samples collected by the police during the period of one year before (21st July 2022–20 th July 2023) and one year after legal change were included (21st July 2023–20 th July 2024), with dates based on the initial police reports. According to standard police procedure, both blood and urine samples were obtained whenever possible and then transferred to the forensic toxicology laboratory for analysis. All samples were screened for the presence of alcohol, drugs and other psychotropic substances via gas chromatography coupled to mass single-quadrupole mass spectrometry (GC-MS) in urine (if available); confirmatory analysis and quantification were performed in serum using GC-MS or liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). When urine was available, screening for THC-COOH, the main THC metabolite, was carried out via GC-MS. Quantification in serum of THC, its active metabolite 11-THC-OH, and its inactive metabolite THC-COOH was performed via GC-MS following solid-phase extraction (SPE) extraction and derivatisation. In absence of urine, all serum samples were analysed via GC-MS and quantified using the above-mentioned procedure.

In this study, results for THC, the major active marker for cannabis consumption, and THC-COOH, the marker for chronic cannabis consumption, are reported. Results negative for THC but positive only for THC-COOH were excluded to reduce bias resulting from a long interval between time of the alleged events and sampling or between consumption and sampling. Statistical analyses, including Student t-tests for comparisons, were performed using R Studio (Version 4.2.2).

Targeted WEBsurvey

Data from a targeted WEB-based survey among persons who use cannabis complemented the objective indicators. While the data on seizures, wastewater analyses, and driving under the influence reports reflect population-level trends in use and availability, they are not able to capture behaviours, perceptions, acquisition behaviour, and cultivation among people who use cannabis. Triangulating survey responses with epidemiological and enforcement data enhances the understanding of the impact of cannabis legislation on both individual behaviours and broader population-level patterns. The European WEB Survey on Drugs, coordinated by the EUDA, collected data during 2024 from people who use drugs, aged 18 years or older, across several countries. Details of the methodology can be found elsewhere (European Union Drugs Agency, 2025b).

The survey was also implemented in Luxembourg and administered in English, German, and French, specifically targeting adults who indicated to have used substances for recreational purposes in the past 12 months residing in Luxembourg. The survey was administered between June and July 2024. Respondents were self-selected, and recruitment strategies relied on convenience sampling via social media campaigns, press releases, and promotion of the survey by drug-treatment and harm-reduction centres. More than 1000 people accessed the survey.

Based on their reported drug use, respondents were presented with a module on one or more substances, including cannabis. An additional national cannabis module on patterns of use and acquisition behaviours was added to the 2024 survey to assess the impact of the national cannabis policy reform, thereby complementing the national epidemiological surveillance system. The additional module assessed self-reported changes in cannabis use after the law change, engagement in cannabis cultivation, the likelihood of growing cannabis plants in the future, and perceptions of changes in cannabis market prices, potency and quality.

Statistical analyses

Seizures and wastewater analysis. For the seizures, as the legal limit in Luxembourg is 1% of THC of dried material, only samples with THC > 1% were included in the study. All statistical tests, (means, determination of outliers, variance) before and after legalisation were carried out using, Prism (Graphpad®) versions 10.5 and 10.6.

Driving under the influence of drugs and positive cannabis tests among drivers. For the comparison of THC and THC-COOH levels before and after legalisation, paired Student T test for equal variance (homoscedastic) was performed with R Studio (Version 4.2.2).

Targeted WEB survey. For the analysis of the WEB-based survey data, cases with missing values were excluded. Following data cleaning, the final analytic sample comprised 911 participants. Descriptive analyses were conducted to examine the prevalence of responses related to key cannabis-related behaviours and perceptions. Specifically, frequency analyses were performed for the following variables: changes in cannabis consumption; initiation of obtaining cannabis from cultivation; first-time cannabis cultivation by oneself or household members; likelihood of future cannabis cultivation; perceived changes in the quality or potency of cannabis products from other illicit sources; and perceived changes in the price of cannabis products from other illicit sources. All analyses were performed using R Studio (Version 4.4.1).

Ethics statement

The study was not reviewed by a national institutional ethics committee, as the WEB-based survey was designed, coordinated, and approved by the European Union Drugs Agency (formerly the European Monitoring Centre for Drugs and Drug Addiction), and all responses were collected fully anonymously. The analyses of drug seizures, wastewater monitoring, and indicators such as driving under the influence of drugs and positive cannabis tests among drivers were based on routine national surveillance and standard data collection procedures.

Results

Quantity and quality of seized cannabis and hashish samples

The first year after the partial legalisation, we observed a 12.7% decrease in samples seized by the national judicial police. This drop was almost entirely due to herbal cannabis samples, whose number fell from 905 to 667 samples (−26.3%), whereas the number of resin samples only decreased from 1291 to 1250 (Table 1).

Before the law change, average THC levels were 14.5% for herbal cannabis and 32.3% for resin. After partial legalisation, an increase was observed for herbal cannabis rising to 15.8%, and for resin to 32.8% (Table 1). The increase in THC content in seized herbal cannabis samples has continued a well-documented national trend observed over several years (from 11.8% in 2020–14.3% in 2023) and does not appear to be related to the change in the legal status of cannabis (Berndt et al., 2025).

THC content in cannabis resin remained consistently > 30% during the past years (30.6% in 2021, 32.2% in 2022, 31.8% in 2023). For cannabis resin samples, the change in law did not reveal any significant change in THC content (p > 0.05).

Cannabis metabolite in wastewater

Seventy-seven samples were analysed in the three wastewater treatment plants before the change in law, and 155 samples were analysed after. Using the Grubbs outlier test, two outliers, one before and one after the partial legalisation, were discarded. The average THC-COOH content (in mg/1000 persons/day, not corrected for metabolism or excretion rates) increased from 99.2 before to 103.1 after the change of law. Using an unpaired t-test, no significant change in THC-COOH levels was observed (p = 0.05). The results are presented in Table 2.

Targeted WEB survey

The non-representative sample (n = 911) comprised individuals aged 18–68 years (mean = 29.9) who reported using at least one substance other than nicotine in the past year. The largest age group was 25–34 years (39.0%), followed by the age group of 18–24 years (27.4%), 35–44 years (23.7%), and individuals aged 45 years and above (10%). The majority of respondents identified as men (64.0%), while 34.0% identified as women, 0.7% as transgender or non-binary, and 1.3% did not provide answer. Over half of the sample (53.6%) had completed higher education, whereas 41.5% reported secondary education as their highest qualification. The remaining respondents reported a primary education level (4.9%). Most participants were employed (68.4%), 19.2% were students, and 12.4% reported another type of occupation, being unemployed, or receiving social benefits.

Overall, only a minority of the respondents who indicated to have used cannabis in the past 12 months reported an increase in consumption after the law change, while use remained unchanged for most respondents. Slightly more than one-third of respondents reported having started their own cultivation or obtaining cannabis from other persons who cultivate it. The majority, however, indicated that they had not changed their source of acquisition since the law change.

More than half of the respondents indicated that growing their own cannabis in the future was likely or very likely; about one-fifth indicated that they did not plan to grow cannabis. Furthermore, changes in the quality, potency, or price of cannabis products from the illicit market were reported by only a few respondents who indicated to have used cannabis . Discussion

In this study, data from cannabis seizures by the national police, loads of cannabis residues in wastewater, analyses of DUI cases, and a web survey among cannabis users were evaluated with the aim of assessing the impact of the partial cannabis legalisation enacted on 21 July 2023 in Luxembourg. Overall, most indicators suggested that the overall impact of the law change was low. No statistically significant changes were observed in THC concentrations in herbal or resin seizures, and THC metabolite loads in wastewater remained largely stable when comparing the year preceding and the year following the policy change. Similarly, no significant change in THC levels in DUI cases was detected, and the majority of persons who indicated to use cannabis reported that they did change their consumption habits, continued to purchase cannabis from the illicit market, and did not perceive changes in potency or price. However, some observations warrant further investigation.

First, the decrease in seizures was largely attributable to a reduction in herbal cannabis samples, while the number of seized resin samples remained almost stable. This pattern may indicate that a growing proportion of people who use cannabis obtain herbal cannabis through personal cultivation, as suggested by the WEB-based survey, in which approximately one-third of respondents reported sourcing cannabis from own cultivation or someone else who cultivates it following the legislative change. Because self-cultivated cannabis is likely to be consumed privately, this may result in fewer opportunities for law enforcement to detect and seize such material. The planting and harvesting of herbal cannabis is relatively simple, whereas the production of cannabis resin requires a greater technical effort beyond cultivation and drying in which the plants are processed, a step many persons who use cannabis may not be prepared or equipped to undertake. This higher level of complexity may limit the extent to which individuals produce resin themselves, which could explain the absence of a comparable decrease in seized resin samples. Our results align with findings from a US study reporting that recreational cannabis legalisation was associated with a decrease in cannabis seizure rates (Meinhofer and Rubli, 2021), with the decline linked to shifts in law enforcement priorities and reduced illicit market activity.

Second, analysis of wastewater data revealed no significant change in cannabis consumption before or after the legal reform. European monitoring data from 2024 indicated a decline in cannabis residues in wastewater across several cities: out of 51 cities, 25 reported a decrease and only 13 reported an increase in cannabis metabolite levels (European Monitoring Centre for Drugs and Drug Addiction EMCDDA, 2023). These findings differ from those obtained in Washington State, where a three-year study observed an increase in cannabis consumption, as measured by THC-COOH levels in wastewater, although it remained unclear whether the rise reflected a higher number of people who use cannabis, higher potency, or more frequent use (Burgard et al., 2019).

In contrast to wastewater indicators and product potency in seizures, significant rise in cannabis-related DUI cases was observed (+22%). While increased enforcement or reporting rather than more widespread or riskier consumption could explain the higher number of DUI cases, Luxembourgish authorities confirmed that the frequency of police checks did not increase after the law change. Thus, our results may reflect broader acceptance of cannabis use in daily activities such as driving. Since cannabis use has been legalised in the private domain for adult residents, it may be mistakenly perceived as legal in public, coupled with a lower inhibition threshold for activities such as driving. Lack of knowledge regarding the toxicological effects of cannabis, including absorption and elimination in biological fluids (e.g., half-life in blood or urine), especially after regular consumption, may also contribute to careless use. Although similar hypotheses have been raised in the US for countries with recreational and medical cannabis legalisation, evidence suggested that perceived safety actually reduced self-reported cannabis-related DUI cases (Dutra et al., 2022).

Although the increase in cannabis-related DUI cases in Luxembourg following partial legalisation may suggest a rise in impaired driving incidents, previous studies have reported that, despite associations between cannabis use and increased fatal crashes, consistent evidence of greater driving impairment has not been demonstrated (González-Sala et al., 2023, Hultgren et al., 2024, Windle et al., 2022). Average THC levels found in cannabis-related DUI cases in this study support this trend, as no significant changes in THC blood concentrations were observed before and after partial legalisation.

We observed a significant increase in THC-COOH, the marker of chronic cannabis use, while THC blood levels remained stable. Despite there not being an official THC-COOH concentration cut-off to distinguish occasional from regular cannabis consumption, values at or above 100 ng/mL in serum are generally considered to be associated with chronic cannabis use (Zinka et al., 2019). Results in this study showed that while THC-COOH mean and median concentrations were already elevated before implementation of the new cannabis law, the observed significant rise in THC-COOH concentrations suggests that a subset of the population may have increased regular consumption following the policy reform. These findings are consistent with international observations indicating limited evidence of new user initiation after recreational cannabis law changes, but a rise in chronic use among existing persons who use cannabis (Lachance et al., 2022).

Findings from the targeted WEB survey indicated that the majority of persons who indicated to use cannabis did not change their consumption patterns, aligning with studies suggesting that legal reforms rarely result in substantial increases in recreational use among existing users (Hall and Lynskey, 2020, McDonald et al., 2025a). A study by McDonald et al. (2025b) found in a longitudinal Canadian cohort that cannabis use frequency increased marginally in the overall sample and that changes were highly dependent on baseline use, with frequent users often decreasing their consumption after legalisation. Only a small proportion of persons who use cannabis reported an increase in their consumption after the law change, consistent with findings from Canada and several US states (Farrelly et al., 2023, Pearlson et al., 2021). Clear trends have not yet been observed in Europe among countries that have moved forward with a change in their recreational cannabis policy. Over one-third of respondents engaged in personal cultivation or sourced cannabis from others who cultivated it, reflecting a behavioural shift that may also be observed in other European jurisdictions with authorised personal cultivation (European Union Drugs Agency, 2025a). However, results from our WEB survey revealed that most persons who use cannabis maintained their previous acquisition sources, indicating a yet limited impact on existing cannabis supply networks. Future intentions to cultivate cannabis plants were high, reflecting findings from Uruguay, where legalisation was associated with an increased interest in self-cultivation (Queirolo et al., 2016). Notably, perceived changes in cannabis product potency and price were minimal, contrasting with concerns regarding the illicit market adaptation and findings from a Canadian study where people who use cannabis had increasingly favourable perceptions of legal cannabis products compared to illegal cannabis products regarding quality, but to a lesser extent for price, as legal cannabis was perceived to be more expensive than illegal cannabis (Wadsworth et al., 2022).

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the study findings. First, only short-term impacts following the 2023 reform are reflected that do not capture longer-term changes (beneficial or harmful) in behaviour or market dynamics, while these may emerge as partial legalisation becomes established over time. Second, the multiple data sources used within the study come along with inherent methodological constraints. Data from cannabis seizures may be influenced by law enforcement practices and not accurately reflect overall market availability or potency, whereas wastewater analysis, while providing near real-time and location-specific estimates, may be affected by uncertainties in population size estimation and metabolite stability (e.g., temperature, pH of the water, and microbial activity) (Barbosa de Oliveira et al., 2023). The observed increases in cannabis-related DUI cases may partly reflect changes in testing practices rather than actual increases in impaired driving, and while THC metabolite levels may suggest patterns of repeated or chronic use, they do not provide direct evidence of impaired driving. The WEB-based survey is subject to selection and self-report biases meaning that the sample may not be representative of the broader population of people who use cannabis. Additionally, the cross-sectional nature of the survey limits causal inference regarding changes in individual behaviour over time. Finally, the absence of a control group limits the ability to attribute the observed changes of specifically to the policy reform, as other external factors may have influenced the study results. In spite of these limitations, a key strength of this study is its interdisciplinary design, integrating multiple complementary data sources to assess the short-term impacts of cannabis policy reform. Notably, it is the first study in Luxembourg to provide systematic insights into market dynamics, product potency, cannabis-related DUI cases, and consumption patterns.

Conclusions

One year after the partial legalisation of cannabis in Luxembourg, no clear overall change in cannabis consumption among existing users, acquisition patterns, product potency, or market value has been observed. Stable wastewater indicators, consistent habits among people who use cannabis, and unchanged THC concentrations in seized and consumed products provide no evidence of increased cannabis consumption.

Partial legalisation in Luxembourg appears to have had a limited impact on overall consumption patterns, market dynamics, and product characteristics, but has been accompanied by a rise in detected drug-driving incidents, potentially due to behavioural changes among a subset of users associated with limited knowledge of the short- and long-term effects of cannabis. These findings suggest that the legal reform may have influenced behaviour in specific high-risk groups, even when broader indicators remained stable.

Ongoing monitoring is recommended to assess longer-term impacts, particularly as intentions to grow cannabis may indicate future shifts in consumption patterns, thereby contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of how use evolves following legislative changes. Further research is needed to evaluate public health outcomes, especially regarding cannabis-impaired driving, chronic use, and public understanding of legal limits and health risks. Monitoring should also focus on evolving market trends, including self-cultivation practices and potential changes in illicit supply chains, as well as the extent to which policy objectives are achieved over time in Luxembourg.
