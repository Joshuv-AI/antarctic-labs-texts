Health-related outcomes among adults with cerebral palsy: a scoping review
Authors: Yeo Wool Kim, Ha Na Jeong, Gi Won Choi, Sun Ju Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdvances in medical technology have extended the life expectancy of individuals with cerebral palsy (CP), resulting in a growing adult population facing long-term physical, psychological, and social health-related challenges. This underscores the need for a comprehensive understanding of their health-related outcomes.ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify and map the health-related outcomes assessed and the corresponding measurement instruments used in studies involving adults with CP.MethodsNine electronic databases were searched. The final search was completed on May 26, 2025. The studies were screened according to predefined criteria. To map the identified outcomes, data were organized into health-outcome levels: domain, sub-domain, outcome, and measurement. Frequency analysis was then performed to summarize the distribution of outcomes and measurement instruments across the included studies.ResultsA total of 16,820 records were identified (16,811 through database searches and 9 through manual searches), of which 159 studies met the inclusion criteria. The analysis identified four domains, 18 sub-domains, and 67 outcomes, represented by 348 distinct measurement instruments. Most studies focused on younger adults, and research on older adults was underrepresented. Most of the outcomes were concentrated within the physical domain. Mental, social, and overall domains were less frequently addressed, and CP-specific measurement instruments were rarely applied.ConclusionsThis study provides an overview of the health-related outcomes reported in adults with CP. It underscores the need for the broader inclusion of older adults, continued expansion into non-physical domains, where research interests have been increasing but remain limited, and the consistent use of common measurement instruments, together with the development and wider application of CP-specific measurement instruments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-05001-5.

Background

Cerebral palsy (CP) originates from non-progressive brain lesions that occur during the fetal or infant period [1]. Advances in medical technology and treatment environments have markedly reduced early mortality, resulting in a steadily increasing proportion of individuals with CP who survive into adulthood [2, 3]. Recent evidence has shown that the life expectancy of adults with CP is approaching that of the general population [4, 5]. However, these trends have been primarily documented in high-income countries, whereas patterns in many low- and middle-income countries remain unclear because of limited population-based registry data [6]. In this context, beyond its traditional definition as the most common motor disability in childhood, CP is increasingly recognized as the most common early-onset lifelong neurodevelopmental condition, characterized by activity limitations resulting from impaired development of movement and posture [1, 7].

However, extended survival has revealed a disproportionate burden of health problems in adulthood. Adults with CP experience cardiovascular, metabolic, and neurologic disorders 1.3–5.8 times more often than adults without CP [8]. The premature deterioration of musculoskeletal function and mobility has also been reported, with declines observed as early as the third decade of life [9]. Furthermore, the early onset of secondary complications contributes to accelerated aging [7].

These challenges are not limited to physical health. Adults with CP have a markedly higher prevalence of depression, anxiety, and other mental health disorders than the general population [10]. Social participation is also significantly restricted by limited environmental support and constrained autonomy [11]. These multidimensional burdens are closely linked to excess healthcare utilization and costs, with resource use being approximately 2.5–3 times higher than that among people without CP [8]. Lifetime medical costs are estimated to exceed USD 1.2 million per individual with CP and reach approximately USD 15 billion in aggregate in the United States [12]. Adults with CP face diverse and complex health challenges [10]. Therefore, it is necessary to identify and comprehensively map the health-related outcomes and measurement instruments used in studies on adults with CP, so that future research and practice can better prioritize needs and guide targeted interventions.

A previous systematic review identified the outcome measures used in studies on adults with CP published between 2000 and 2017 [13]. However, a bibliometric analysis of CP research conducted up to 2022 revealed that publications from 2018 to 2022 account for approximately two-thirds of the total output in the preceding 15 years [14], indicating that an update of health-related outcomes in research on CP among adults is needed.

From a classification perspective, outcomes in the previous systematic review were classified using the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) framework [13]. Additionally, an ICF Core Set, specifically for adults with cerebral palsy, has recently been developed [15]. However, the ICF is composed of numerous codes, requires substantial expertise, and its practicality is limited when many measurement items do not fit the existing codes or require additional classification, which may result in a low linking rate [16, 17]. This limitation may be addressed by mapping the outcomes within a broader conceptual framework and classifying them according to the findings, rather than following a fixed code. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines health as “a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being” [18]. This macroscopic WHO concept of health provides a useful framework for understanding and managing the complex health issues among adults with CP. This definition is particularly valuable for understanding the health of individuals with disabilities as it incorporates mental and social dimensions that are often overlooked [19]. Accordingly, this review maps health-related outcomes into four domains (i.e., physical, mental, social, and overall) and categorizes them into sub-domains using a data-driven approach.

Building on these conceptual considerations, this study aimed to map health-related outcomes addressed in studies on adults with CP. The specific objectives are to (1) identify health-related outcomes reported in studies involving adults with CP, (2) categorize these outcomes into specific domains, and (3) examine how these outcomes have been measured.

Methods

Study design

This study is a scoping review conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodological guidance for conducting scoping reviews [20] and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist [21]. The protocol was preregistered in the Open Science Framework (OSF) (No: 10.17605/OSF.IO/S9MNF).

This scoping review was designed according to the Population–Concept–Context (PCC) framework. The PCC elements were defined as follows: Population – adults with CP; Concept – studies assessing, reporting, or discussing health-related outcomes; and Context – any healthcare, community, or research setting. The types of sources included all study designs.

In this study, we operationally defined a health-related outcome as a conceptual construct representing physical, mental, and social aspects of health, in accordance with the WHO definition of health [18]. The term “outcome” refers to the construct being measured (e.g., pain, depression, gross motor function), whereas “measurement instruments” refer to the tools or scales used to operationalize these constructs.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in collaboration with a medical information specialist. The full search queries based on the PCC framework are presented in Appendix 1. The following nine electronic databases were searched from their inception to May 26, 2025: PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, PsycINFO, Web of Science, Scopus, Research Information Sharing Service (RISS), and Korean Studies Information Service System (KISS). The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary and natural language keywords. Additionally, the reference lists of the included studies and relevant systematic reviews were manually screened to identify additional eligible articles.

Study selection

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) adults aged ≥ 18 years diagnosed with CP, (2) assessment, reporting, or discussion of health-related outcomes, (3) conducted in any healthcare, community, or research setting, and (4) any study design, including grey literature, provided that at least the abstract was accessible. No language restrictions were applied.

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) inclusion or comparison of non–cerebral palsy populations, (2) participants under 18 years, unclear adult age range, or inclusion of child-specific factors, (3) no specific health-related outcomes reported (including qualitative studies, narrative literature, systematic reviews, expert opinions, and commentaries), (4) focus on specific medical treatments (e.g., surgery, medication, electrical or brain stimulation), prognosis, or complications, (5) reporting only employment or language outcomes, (6) primary objective to estimate the prevalence or incidence of specific comorbidities, or (7) book publications or sources for which even the abstract was not accessible.

All the identified studies were imported into EndNote [22] and uploaded to Covidence [23] for de-duplication and screening. Each record was independently screened by two reviewers at both the title/abstract and full-text stages. Four authors (YW, HN, GW, and SJ) conducted the screening in pairs, with each record assessed independently by two reviewers according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion among all four authors. When full texts were unavailable, eligibility was determined based on abstract screening alone (n = 19, 11.9%). Studies without an available abstract were excluded. The entire selection process is summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Data extraction and mapping

Data extraction

Data were extracted using a predefined data-extraction form developed for this review. The form captured study characteristics (title, author, journal, year of publication, country, publication type, and study design), participant characteristics (population description, sample size, and age), and all reported health-related outcomes together with their corresponding measurement instruments. General study characteristics were not considered health-related outcomes.

All outcomes were extracted verbatim as reported in the original studies. To ensure extraction consistency, 19 of the 159 included studies (12%) were randomly selected and independently extracted by two researchers (YW and HN). From these studies, 104 outcomes were identified. Three discrepancies(e.g., whether the Borg Scale should be classified as a health-related outcome or as a measure of program feasibility, inclusion of caregiver-related variables, and refinement of variable naming) were detected (2.9%) and resolved through discussion with a third researcher (SJ). As the calibration exercise demonstrated sufficient inter-rater agreement, the first author (YW) proceeded to extract data from the remaining studies.

Inductive outcome mapping process

All outcomes were extracted verbatim and subsequently mapped into progressively higher levels of abstraction. The mapping process proceeded in three stages: (1) development of standardized outcome name codes, (2) mapping of standardized outcome name codes into sub-domains, and (3) mapping of sub-domains into domains. The inductive outcome mapping process and an illustrative classification example are presented in Fig. 2.

The inductive outcome mapping process and an illustrative classification example

The development of standardized outcome name codes and mapping of sub-domains were conducted entirely inductively from the extracted data. The WHO definition of health [18] was applied solely at the domain level to structure the highest-order mapping.

From the outset, mutual exclusivity was established as a structural design principle of the taxonomy. Each outcome concept was assigned to a single sub-domain and a single domain in order to minimize conceptual redundancy and preserve analytic clarity.

All mapping stages were initially conducted by the first author (YW) and documented in an Excel analytic codebook. Upon completion of each stage, materials were circulated to all co-authors (SJ, HN, and GW) for independent review. Structured meetings were then held to evaluate classification decisions in relation to predefined mapping principles to confirm consistency. Consensus was defined as unanimous agreement. When disagreement occurred, classification was re-evaluated. Revisions were incorporated before progression to subsequent stages.

Step 1: Development of standardized outcome name codes

Following verbatim extraction, outcome terminology was standardized to reduce linguistic variation. This process was governed by four explicit rules applied consistently throughout the analysis.

First, when studies explicitly used a clearly defined conceptual term, the standardized outcome name code retained the original conceptual terminology.

Second, when a study did not explicitly define a conceptual term, the measurement instrument was used as the primary reference for defining the standardized outcome name code.

Third, when similar or variably labeled concepts were assessed using the same measurement instrument across studies, a single standardized outcome name code was assigned based on the measurement instrument name. However, if the measurement instrument clearly assessed distinct constructs, outcomes were classified separately according to the specific construct measured.

Fourth, for multi-construct instruments, mapping was conducted at the subscale level whenever subscale scores were reported. When only total scores were available and subscale-level mapping was not feasible, mapping was based on the primary construct represented by the instrument. In such cases, the instrument’s multi-construct nature was retained.

Step 2: Sub-domain-level mapping

Standardized outcome name codes were mapped to higher-level sub-domains based on conceptual and functional similarity. Sub-domains were inductively developed and refined through team consensus, with efforts to minimize conceptual overlap.

Step 3: Domain-level mapping

Sub-domains were subsequently mapped into higher-level domains. Domain structuring was aligned with the WHO definition of health [18], encompassing physical, mental, and social well-being. A distinct overall health domain was additionally included to accommodate global or composite assessments (e.g., self-rated health or overall quality of life) that did not map exclusively to a single well-being dimension.

Data analysis

Extracted data related to the characteristics of included studies were categorized by the countries in which the studies were conducted, population description, language, publication type, study design, and publication year. Population descriptions were classified according to specific participant subgroups reported in the study, and publication years were grouped into 10-year intervals. Extracted health-related outcomes were mapped into domains, sub-domains, outcomes, and measurement levels. Frequencies and percentages were calculated for all categorized data. Frequency analysis and visualization were performed using Microsoft Excel 2024 and the R statistical software, version 4.5.1.

Results

Selection of eligible studies

A total of 16,811 records were identified through the database searches. After removing duplicates (8,110), 8,701 records were screened by title and abstract, and 8,202 records were excluded. The full texts of 499 articles were then assessed for eligibility, and 342 were excluded. In addition, nine records were identified through manual searches, of which two were included. In total, 159 studies were included in the final analysis, as shown in the PRISMA-ScR flow diagram (Fig. 1). The list of included studies is presented in Appendix 2.

Characteristics of the included studies

The included studies were conducted across a range of countries. The largest proportion was conducted in the USA (n = 47, 29.6%), followed by South Korea (n = 28, 17.6%), Australia (n = 15, 9.4%), Sweden (n = 13, 8.2%), the Netherlands (n = 9, 5.7%), and Norway (n = 8, 5.0%). Smaller numbers of studies were conducted in Denmark, Canada, Japan, Spain, Turkey, the UK, Israel, Brazil, Germany, Greece, Hong Kong, Iran, Ireland, Italy, and South Africa. In addition, four multi-country studies were identified, including studies conducted in South Korea and Japan (n = 2, 1.3%), the UK and Ireland (n = 1, 0.6%), and France, Germany, Italy, Portugal, and Sweden (n = 1, 0.6%). Three studies did not report the recruitment country. Some studies specifically targeted subgroups of adults with cerebral palsy. Among these, young adults constituted the most frequently reported subgroup (n = 15; 9.4%), followed by spastic type (n = 9; 5.7%), ambulant (n = 5; 3.1%), dyskinetic (n = 3; 1.9%), and other specific subgroups (each ≤ 1.3%). Most studies were published in English (n = 138; 86.8%), and the remainder in Korean (n = 21, 13.2%). Regarding publication type, articles were predominant (n = 127; 79.9%), followed by conference abstracts (n = 19; 11.9%), dissertations (n = 10; 6.3%), and trial registries (n = 3; 1.9%). In terms of study design, cross-sectional studies were most common (n = 103; 64.8%), followed by experimental (n = 34; 21.4%), longitudinal (n = 20; 12.6%), and scale development studies (n = 2; 1.3%). Regarding publication year, 72 studies (45.3%) were published between 2010 and 2019, 50 (31.4%) between 2020 and 2025, 33 (20.8%) between 2000 and 2009, and four (2.5%) between 1995 and 1999, as shown in Table 1.

The Mental domain consisted of three sub-domains, 11 outcomes, and 40 measurements. The Social domain included five sub-domains, 14 outcomes, and 37 measurements. The Overall domain contained three sub-domains, four outcomes, and 25 measurements.

Domain-level frequency

The frequencies of the outcomes extracted at the domain level are shown in Fig. 3. The Physical domain was reported most frequently (n = 149; 93.7%), ranking first among all domains. The Social domain followed (n = 45; 28.3%), while the Mental (n = 42; 26.4%) and Overall (n = 41; 25.8%) domains were less frequently addressed, ranking third and fourth, respectively.

The number of studies addressing the Mental domain increased from two in the 1990s to 10 in the 2000s, 14 in the 2010s, and 16 in the 2020s. Meanwhile, the number of studies addressing the Social domain increased from two in the 1990s to seven in the 2000s, 17 in the 2010s, and 19 in the 2020s. As the counts for the 2020s covered only publications up to May 26, 2025, these numbers are expected to increase further.

Sub-domain-level frequency

The frequencies of the outcomes extracted at the sub-domain level are presented in Table 3. The Motor sub-domain was most frequently addressed (n = 93; 58.5%), followed by Body structures (n = 82; 51.6%) and Mobility (n = 73; 45.9%). Symptoms ranked fourth (n = 50; 31.4%). Psychological distress, Quality of life, and Medical conditions each accounted for 17.6% (n = 28) and shared fifth place. Daily living (n = 19; 11.9%), Participation (n = 17; 10.7%), and Communication (n = 14; 8.8%) followed. The least represented sub-domains were Psychological Resources and Interpersonal (each n = 8; 5.0%), Life satisfaction (n = 7; 4.4%), and Environment (n = 5; 3.1%).

Outcome-level frequency

The frequency of outcomes extracted at the outcome level (≥ 7 occurrences) is presented in Table 4. Gross motor function was the most frequently reported outcome (n = 80, 50.3%), followed by Pain (n = 43, 27%) and Walking function (n = 27, 17%). Anthropometric measures and Health-related quality of life each accounted for 13.2% (n = 21), ranking fourth. Relevant morbidity (n = 19; 11.9%), Activities of daily living (n = 19; 11.9%), Physical activity (n = 18; 11.3%), Range of motion (n = 18; 11.3%), and Fatigue (n = 18; 11.3%) were also common. Gait analysis and Depression each represented 10.7% (n = 17). Other outcomes such as Muscle tone (n = 16, 10.1%), Balance and functional mobility (n = 15; 9.4%) followed. The remaining outcomes, including Physical function, Manual ability, Muscle strength, Balance, Speech and communication function, Participation in social roles and activities, and Healthcare services utilization, were each reported in less than 9% of the studies. Measurement-level frequency

The frequency of measurement instruments extracted at the measurement level (≥ 6 occurrences) is presented in Table 5. The Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS, including original and Expanded & Revised versions) was the most frequently used measurement instrument (n = 78, 49.1%), followed by Weight- and height-related indices (n = 21, 13.2%) and Relevant morbidity (n = 16, 10.1%). Goniometric range of motion and the Short Form Health Survey (SF-36, SF-12v2) each accounted for 6.9% (n = 11). Manual Ability Classification System (MACS), 6-Minute Walking Test (6MWT), and Communication Function Classification System (CFCS) each represented 6.3% (n = 10). Other measurement instruments, including the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, bone mineral density measurement, 11-point Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) for pain intensity, and healthcare service utilization derived from medical records or questionnaires, were reported with slightly lower frequencies (n = 9, 5.7%). Discussion

A total of 16,820 records (16,811 from database searches and nine from manual searches) were screened, of which 159 studies addressed health-related outcomes among adults with CP. The extracted outcomes were mapped into four analytical levels: domain, sub-domain, outcome, and measurement. Across these 159 studies, a total of four domains encompassing 18 sub-domains and 67 outcomes operationalized through 348 distinct measurement instruments were identified.

From the perspective of the study participants, the included studies revealed that certain subgroups were selectively targeted based on diagnostic subtype, functional status, or age group. In particular, distinct patterns were observed in the age-based classifications. Most studies focused on young adults (15 studies), while only a few addressed older age groups, including one study each involving participants over 40 years of age and middle-aged adults, and two studies examining older adults. Among the age groups, studies involving older adults were the least frequently reported. Individuals who reach older age with long-term disabilities are classified as those aged with disabilities, representing a rapidly growing population with unique healthcare needs [24]. As the average life expectancy of individuals with CP continues to increase, a growing proportion of adults with CP fall into this category, prompting heightened calls for research addressing aging and its associated outcomes in this population [24, 25]. Despite these calls for further research, older adults remain relatively underrepresented in the current literature on adults with CP.

From the perspective of study mapping, at the domain level, the Physical domain emerged as the most frequently investigated within the included studies. In contrast, studies addressing the Social, Mental, and Overall domains were considerably fewer. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidelines for adults with CP emphasize that mental health problems and emotional difficulties should be managed in parallel with physical health issues, recognizing that their impact can be equally significant [26]. It also underscores the importance of promoting functional abilities and social participation by removing barriers and supporting independent living skills [26]. Although multidimensional perspectives on health have been discussed in prior literature, outcomes related to non-physical domains were less frequently reported in the included studies. However, the findings of this review revealed that the number of studies addressing these domains has already surpassed that of the previous decade, even though the data from the 2020s included only publications up to May 2025. The findings indicate that studies addressing mental, social, and overall domains have increased in more recent publications within the included studies.

The sub-domain and outcome levels of the present review were compared with the chapter-level and second-level categories, respectively, of the earlier ICF-linking review by Benner et al. [13]. At the sub-domain level, both studies consistently reported Mobility; Sensory functions and pain, which correspond to Symptoms in the present taxonomy; Neuromusculoskeletal and movement-related functions, which correspond to Motor; and Structures related to movement, which correspond to Body structures, as high-frequency areas. As an added feature of the present review, Psychological distress and Quality of life were among the highest-frequency sub-domains.

At the outcome level, both studies consistently reported Sensation of pain; Walking, which corresponds to Walking function and Gait analysis in the present taxonomy; Mobility of joint functions, which corresponds to Range of motion; and Structure of the lower extremities, which corresponds to Anthropometric measures, as high-frequency areas. In the present review, Gross motor function also ranked highly, likely reflecting that the GMFCS was not used in the frequency analysis as an outcome in the review conducted by Benner et al. [13]. In addition to this difference, the present review also identified Health-related quality of life, Activities of daily living, Physical activity, Fatigue, and Depression as frequently measured outcomes.

Taken together, while outcomes related to mobility, movement functions, and pain were prominent in both reviews—consistent with the definition of CP as a motor disability [7]— the present review, which incorporated more recent studies, demonstrated growing attention to psychosocial and patient-reported outcomes, reflecting a broader, more multidimensional view of health. This shift can be interpreted as the academic community’s response to previously unmet needs. As reported by Gross et al. [27], while clinicians often target outcomes such as decreased muscle tone or improved range of motion, people with CP and their families consistently prioritized participation, quality of life, and function in daily life [27]. This trend parallels the paradigm shift in rehabilitation medicine from the biomedical to the biopsychosocial model [28], reflecting that quality of life, rather than survival alone, has become the central concern in CP [29]. It also suggests that adults with CP are increasingly recognized not merely as individuals with motor impairments, but as a population experiencing broader psychological and social challenges that become particularly visible in adulthood. Building on this broader conceptualization, clinical practice may benefit from embracing a multidimensional approach to health assessment. Ideally, such assessments would be accompanied by interventions specifically designed to improve these outcomes in adults with CP. In particular, the complex needs of this population would be met by specialist multidisciplinary teams, as recommended by the NICE guidelines [26].

At the measurement level, both studies consistently reported gross motor function, pain measures, walking performance measures (6-Minute Walk Test, 10-Meter Walk Test), joint mobility assessments (goniometric range of motion), and generic health/function instruments (Short Form Health Survey, Barthel Index, Functional Independence Measure) as high-frequency measures. Although the relative rankings differed, a largely similar set of measurements was used with high frequency across both studies. In particular, the use of the Short Form Health Survey, Barthel Index, and Functional Independence Measure closely parallels that observed in outcome reviews of individuals with acquired brain lesions such as traumatic brain injury and stroke [30]. This consistency suggests that measurement practices in CP research have remained relatively stable over time, and the overlap with instruments commonly used in other neurological conditions indicates that most measures applied are generic rather than CP-specific. In line with this observation, CP-specific measurement instruments were rarely utilized. Measurement instruments specifically designed to capture health-related outcomes of CP, such as the Cerebral Palsy 6-Dimension (CP-6D) and Cerebral Palsy Quality of Life (CPQOL), were used in only one of the included studies [31]. Beyond their limited use, a more fundamental issue is that the CPQOL was originally developed for children and adolescents [32], and the CP-6D was derived from the CPQOL as a simplified preference-based measure subsequently applied to adults [33]. CP-specific instruments tailored to adults therefore remain scarce. However, adults with CP have a unique and heterogeneous life course and corresponding health needs that differ from those of children [34, 35], underscoring the need to develop and apply outcome measures specifically targeted to this population.

Additionally, of the 348 measurement instruments identified across the included studies, only 27 were used six or more times, thus indicating that the overwhelming majority were used only sporadically. This lack of consistency may hinder comparability and standardization across research findings, limiting the ability to synthesize evidence across studies and to inform evidence-based practices. For children with CP, the ICF Core Sets [36] are accompanied by a corresponding toolbox of measures that operationalizes how to assess these categories [37]. Contrastingly, although the ICF Core Sets for adults with CP have recently been established [15], consensus on the corresponding measurement approaches has yet to be fully established, suggesting that addressing this gap would be valuable.

Lastly, this study has some limitations. First, as a scoping review, the methodological quality of the included studies was not assessed, which may have limited the interpretation of the evidence and the strength of the conclusions drawn. Second, as Korean databases were included in the search process, studies originating from Korea may have been relatively overrepresented, potentially influencing the distribution of the research topics or measurements identified. Third, 19 studies (11.9%) were included as abstract-only records as part of our effort to incorporate grey literature. Because abstracts may not comprehensively report all measured outcomes, some outcomes may have been underreported in the frequency analysis. Fourth, because the taxonomy was developed primarily through a researcher-driven inductive mapping process (with the exception of the domain level, which was structured according to the WHO definition of health [18]), classification decisions inherently involved interpretive judgment. Although structured and iterative procedures were applied, some degree of misclassification cannot be entirely excluded. Finally, as this review reports the frequency within the included studies, a higher frequency does not necessarily indicate greater clinical importance or priority.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive overview of health-related outcomes and measurement instruments used among adults with CP. The findings identify the most frequently reported outcomes and instruments and provide useful guidance for selecting appropriate measures in future research and clinical practice.

This study has several implications for practice and research. First, there is a notable lack of studies focusing on older adults with CP. As life expectancy increases, both research and practice should address aging-related outcomes and provide age-appropriate care. Second, although interest in the mental, social, and overall aspects of health has been increasing, these domains remain insufficiently explored. A multidimensional perspective is required to promote holistic understanding and care. Third, the consistent use of common measurements, along with the development and broader application of CP-specific instruments, is needed. Establishing standardized condition-specific measurement instruments will enhance research comparability and enable more accurate clinical assessments, thereby contributing to individualized and evidence-based interventions.
