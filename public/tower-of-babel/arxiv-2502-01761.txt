Matrix systems, algebras, and open maps
Stephan Weis

Abstract
. Every state on the algebra M n \mathrm{M}_{n} of complex n × n n\times n matrices restricts to a state on any matrix system. Whereas the restriction to a matrix system is generally not open, we prove that the restriction to every *-subalgebra of M n \mathrm{M}_{n} is open. This simplifies topology problems in matrix theory and quantum information theory.

1.
Introduction

In the work of Choi and Effros
[
14
]
, a
matrix system

on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a complex linear subspace
\mathcal{R}
of the full matrix algebra
\mathrm{M}_{n}

that is self-adjoint (the conjugate transpose
A^{\ast}
of every
A\in\mathcal{R}
lies
in
\mathcal{R}
) and contains the
n\times n
identity matrix
\mathds{1}_{n}
, see also

[
5
,
42
]
. Let
\mathcal{R}
be a matrix system on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. If

\mathcal{R}
is closed under matrix multiplication we call it a

*-subalgebra
of
\mathrm{M}_{n}
. The dual space to
\mathcal{R}
is denoted by

\mathcal{R}^{\ast}:=\{\ell:\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{C}\mid\text{$\ell$ is $\mathbb{C}$-linear}\}
and the cone
of positive semidefinite matrices in
\mathcal{R}
by
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{R})
. The

state space
of
\mathcal{R}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{R})

\displaystyle:=\{\ell\in\mathcal{R}^{\ast}\mid\forall A\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{R}):\ell(A)\geq 0,\ell(\mathds{1}_{n})=1\}\,.

The restriction
\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\to\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{R})
,
\ell\mapsto\ell|_{\mathcal{R}}
of states to

\mathcal{R}
is continuous and affine. Its analytic properties would be perfectly
clear if it were not for the openness that fails in Example
1.1
. Let

K,L
be subsets of some Euclidean spaces endowed with their relative topologies

[
27
]
. A map
f:K\to L
is
open at

x\in K
if the image
of every neighborhood of
x
in
K
is a neighborhood of
f(x)
in
L
. The
map
f
is
open
if it is open at every point in
K
.

It is helpful to represent states as matrices. The antilinear isomorphism

\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{R}^{\ast}
,
A\mapsto\langle A,\,\cdot\,\rangle
restricts by
Lemma
2.2
to the affine isomorphism

\textstyle r_{\mathcal{R}}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})\to\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{R})\,,\quad\rho\mapsto\langle\rho,\,\cdot\,\rangle\,,

where
\langle A,B\rangle:=\tr(A^{\ast}B)
is the Frobenius inner
product of
A,B\in\mathrm{M}_{n}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{R})

\displaystyle:=\{A\in\mathcal{R}\mid A^{\ast}=A\}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{R})^{\vee}

\displaystyle:=\{A\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{R})\mid\forall B\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{R}):\langle A,B\rangle\geq 0\}\,,

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})

\displaystyle:=\{\rho\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{R})^{\vee}\mid\tr(\rho)=1\}\,.

Generalizing a term of von Neumann algebras
[
10
]
,
we refer to the elements of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})
as
density matrices
. The inclusion

\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{R})\subset\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{R})^{\vee}
can be strict. For example, the density matrix

\diag(-1,5,2)/6
of the matrix system spanned by
\mathds{1}_{3}
and
\diag(1,-1,0)
is
indefinite. It is well known, see Rem.
2.8
, that the identity

\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{A})^{\vee}
holds for every *-subalgebra
\mathcal{A}
of
\mathrm{M}_{n}
.

Example 1.1
.

We write block diagonal matrices as direct sums, for instance

\textstyle\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&c\end{pmatrix}=A\oplus c\in\mathrm{M}_{3}\quad\text{for every}\quad A\in\mathrm{M}_{2}\,,\quad c\in\mathbb{C}\cong\mathrm{M}_{1}\,.

Denoting the imaginary unit by
\mathrm{i}\in\mathbb{C}
and the
Pauli matrices
by

\textstyle X:=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad Y:=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\mathrm{i}\\
\mathrm{i}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad Z:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\,,

we define the matrix system
\mathcal{R}:=\spn_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathds{1}_{3},X\oplus 1,Z\oplus 0)
.
The orthogonal projection of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
onto the line spanned by

\ket{+}:=(1,1){}^{\mathrm{T}}/\sqrt{2}
is written
\ket{+}\!\!\bra{+}
in

Dirac’s notation

[
7
,
11
]
.
The open set
\mathcal{O}:=\{\ell\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{M}_{3})\mid\ell(0\oplus 1)>0\}

contains

\textstyle\omega_{\lambda}:=r_{\mathrm{M}_{3}}\big[(1-\lambda)\ket{+}\!\!\bra{+}\oplus\lambda\big]\,,\quad\lambda\in(0,1]\,.

So
\mathcal{O}|_{\mathcal{R}}:=\{\ell|_{\mathcal{R}}:\ell\in\mathcal{O}\}
contains
\omega_{\lambda}|_{\mathcal{R}}

but none of the restrictions
\ell_{\theta}|_{\mathcal{R}}
of

\textstyle\ell_{\theta}:=r_{\mathrm{M}_{3}}\big[\frac{1}{2}\big(\mathds{1}_{2}+\cos(\theta)X+\sin(\theta)Z\big)\oplus 0\big]\,,\quad\theta\in(0,2\pi)\,.

Specifically,
\ell_{\theta}
has the value
\ell_{\theta}(A_{\theta})=1
at

A_{\theta}:=\cos(\theta)(X\oplus 1)+\sin(\theta)Z\oplus 0
and

\ell(A_{\theta})\leq(\cos(\theta)-1)\ell(0\oplus 1)+1
holds for every

\ell\in\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{M}_{3})
. Hence

\textstyle\ell|_{\mathcal{R}}(A_{\theta})-\ell_{\theta}|_{\mathcal{R}}(A_{\theta})\leq(\cos(\theta)-1)\ell(0\oplus 1)<0\,,\quad\ell\in\mathcal{O}\,.

This shows that
\mathcal{O}|_{\mathcal{R}}
is not a neighborhood of
\omega_{\lambda}|_{\mathcal{R}}

as
\lim_{\theta\to 0}\ell_{\theta}|_{\mathcal{R}}=\omega_{\lambda}|_{\mathcal{R}}
. In conclusion,

\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{M}_{3})\to\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{R})
,
\ell\mapsto\ell|_{\mathcal{R}}
is not open at

\omega_{\lambda}
for any
\lambda\in(0,1]
.

Asking where
\mathcal{S}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\to\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{R})
,
\ell\mapsto\ell|_{\mathcal{R}}
is open is the
same, by Coro.
2.7
b), as inquiring at which density
matrices the orthogonal projection

(1.1)

\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})

is open. The map (
1.1
) is defined in (
2.2
) and
(
2.3
) below. For now suffice it to say that it is a restriction
of the orthogonal projection of
\mathrm{M}_{n}
onto
\mathcal{R}
.

and Leake et

Theorem 1.2
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is a *-subalgebra of
\mathrm{M}_{n}
, then the orthogonal projection

\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
is open.

Thm.
1.2
can simplify the problem (
1.1
), and
related continuity problems, if
\mathcal{R}
is included in a *-subalgebra of

\mathrm{M}_{n}
smaller than
\mathrm{M}_{n}
as we show in Sec.
8
.
Examples from quantum information theory are presented in
Sec.
9
.

Thm.
1.2
is proved in Sec.
6
. The main ideas
are that
\mathcal{A}
is a direct sum of full matrix algebras and that
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})

is stable and highly symmetric. Thereby, a convex set
K
is
stable

if the
midpoint map

\textstyle K\times K\to K\,,\quad(x,y)\mapsto\frac{1}{2}(x+y)

is open. A convex set is always (except Rem.
1.3
) understood to
be included in a Euclidean space. Debs
[
20
]
proved the stability
of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
for
\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{M}_{n}
, Papadopoulou
[
41
]
achieved
it for *-subalgebras
\mathcal{A}
of
\mathrm{M}_{n}
, and we do it for
real

*-subalgebras
\mathcal{A}
of
\mathrm{M}_{n}
in Sec.
5
using Clausing’s
work on retractions
[
15
]
. The analogue of Thm.
1.2

is established for the algebra
\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
of real
n\times n
matrices
in Sec.
7
. The Secs.
2
and
3

collect preliminaries.

Remark 1.3
.

a)

Vesterstrøm
[
52
]
proved that the restriction of states
to the center of a von Neumann algebra is open. Thm.
1.2

is a noncommutative analogue in the finite-dimensional setting.

b)

Stability is a meaningful concept in optimal control

[
41
,
58
]
and quantum information theory

[
48
]
because of the following “CE-property”. A
compact convex set
K
is stable if and only if for every continuous
function
f:K\to\mathbb{R}
, the envelope
f^{\vee}(x):=\sup\{h(x):h\leq f\}
,

x\in K
is continuous, see
[
52
,
39
]
and

[
48
]
. Here, the supremum is taken over all continuous
affine functions

whose graphs lie below the graph of
f
.

2.
States and density matrices

The aim of this section is to translate openness questions from states to
density matrices.
In the sequel, we refer to matrix systems and *-subalgebras synonymously as

complex matrix systems
and
complex *-subalgebras
, respectively.
In addition, we introduce real *-subalgebras as they have a greater variety
of state spaces than the complex ones. See, however,
Rem.
2.4
.

A
real matrix system
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a real linear subspace
\mathcal{R}
of
\mathrm{M}_{n}

that is self-adjoint and contains
\mathds{1}_{n}
. Let
\mathcal{R}
denote a real matrix
system on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We endow
\mathcal{R}
with the Euclidean scalar product

(2.1)

\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{R}\,,\quad(A,B)\mapsto\mathrm{Re}\langle A,B\rangle\,,

where
\langle A,B\rangle=\tr A^{\ast}B
and
\mathrm{Re}(a+\mathrm{i}b)=a
is the real part of
a complex number,
A,B\in\mathrm{M}_{n}
,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. The positive cone, space of hermitian
matrices, dual cone, and space of density matrices are defined verbatim to their
respective complex counterparts defined in Sec.
1
, and are denoted
by

\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{R}),\quad\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{R}),\quad\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{R})^{\vee},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})\,.

We call
\mathcal{R}
a
real *-subalgebra
of
\mathrm{M}_{n}
if
\mathcal{R}
is closed under
matrix multiplication.

Generalizing a definition from real algebras
[
32
, Sec. 4.5]
,
we define the
real state space
of
\mathcal{R}
as

\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{R}):=\{\ell\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{R},0}^{\ast}\mid\forall A\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{R}):\ell(A)\geq 0,\ell(\mathds{1}_{n})=1\}\,,

where

\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{R},0}^{\ast}:=\{\ell:\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{R}\mid\mbox{$\ell$ is $\mathbb{R}$-linear},\forall A\in\mathcal{H}^{-}(\mathcal{R}):\ell(A)=0\}

is the space of real functionals vanishing on the skew-hermitian
matrices

\mathcal{H}^{-}(\mathcal{R}):=\{A\in\mathcal{R}:A^{\ast}=-A\}\,.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
\mathcal{R}
is a real matrix system on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, then the map

r_{\mathbb{R},\mathcal{R}}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})\to\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{R}),\quad\rho\mapsto\mathrm{Re}\langle\rho,\,\cdot\,\rangle

is a real affine isomorphism between compact convex sets.

Proof.

As
\mathcal{R}
is the orthogonal direct sum
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{R})\oplus\mathcal{H}^{-}(\mathcal{R})
,
the real linear isomorphism
[
23
, Sec. 67]

r_{\mathbb{R},\mathcal{R}}:\mathcal{R}\to\{\ell:\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{R}\mid\mbox{$\ell$ is $\mathbb{R}$-linear}\}\,,\quad A\mapsto\mathrm{Re}\langle A,\,\cdot\,\rangle

restricts to the real linear isomorphism
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{R})\to\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{R},0}^{\ast}
.
Restricting
r_{\mathbb{R},\mathcal{R}}
further, we obtain an injective map whose
domain is
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})
. So, it suffices to prove

r_{\mathbb{R},\mathcal{R}}(\rho)\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{R})
for

\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})
, and that
r_{\mathbb{R},\mathcal{R}}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})\to\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{R})
is
surjective. Both assertions are straightforward to verify.
The convex set
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})
is compact, see
[
44
, Lemma 3.3]
,
because
\mathds{1}_{n}
lies in the interior of
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{R})
in the topology of

\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{R})
. The convex set
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{R})
is compact as it is the image of a
compact set under a continuous map.
∎

Returning to complex functionals, we consider the real vector space

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{her}}^{\ast}:=\{\ell:\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{C}\mid\mbox{$\ell$ is $\mathbb{C}$-linear},\forall A\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{R}):\ell(A)\in\mathbb{R}\}

of complex functionals taking real values on hermitian matrices.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
\mathcal{R}
is a complex matrix system on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, then the map

r_{\mathcal{R}}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})\to\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{R})\,,\quad\rho\mapsto\langle\rho,\,\cdot\,\rangle

is a real affine isomorphism between compact convex sets.

Proof.

The complex antilinear isomorphism
r_{\mathcal{R}}:\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{R}^{\ast}
,

A\mapsto\langle A,\,\cdot\,\rangle
restricts to a real linear isomorphism

\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{R})\to\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{her}}^{\ast}
. Furthermore,

\alpha:\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{R},0}^{\ast}\to\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{her}}^{\ast}\,,\quad\alpha(\ell)[A+\mathrm{i}B]:=\ell(A)+\mathrm{i}\ell(B)\,,\quad A,B\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{R})\,,

is a real linear isomorphism, whose inverse is given by

\ell\mapsto\mathrm{Re}\circ\ell
. So, the following diagram commutes.
(Note that two arrows in opposite directions denote a bijection.)

\textstyle{\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{R})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{r_{\mathcal{R}}}
\scriptstyle{r_{\mathbb{R},\mathcal{R}}}
\textstyle{\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{R},0}^{\ast}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\alpha}
\textstyle{\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{her}}^{\ast}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{Re}}

Moreover, if
\ell_{1}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{R},0}^{\ast}
and
\ell_{2}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{her}}^{\ast}

satisfy
\ell_{2}=\alpha(\ell_{1})
, then

\ell_{1}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{R})
holds if and only if
\ell_{2}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{R})
. Hence,
the claim follows from Lemma
2.1
.
∎

Example 2.3
.

Real *-subalgebras of
\mathrm{M}_{n}
have a richer class of state spaces than the
complex ones. The
Bloch ball

[
7
, Sec. 5.2]

\textstyle\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{2})=\big\{\frac{1}{2}(\mathds{1}_{2}+c_{X}X+c_{Y}Y+c_{Z}Z)\colon c_{X},c_{Y},c_{Z}\in\mathbb{R},c_{X}^{2}+c_{Y}^{2}+c_{Z}^{2}\leq 1\big\}

is a three-dimensional Euclidean ball of radius
1/\sqrt{2}
. The set of
density matrices of the algebra
\mathrm{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
of real
2\times 2
matrices
is the great disk

\textstyle\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))=\big\{\frac{1}{2}(\mathds{1}_{2}+c_{X}X+c_{Z}Z)\colon c_{X},c_{Z}\in\mathbb{R},c_{X}^{2}+c_{Z}^{2}\leq 1\big\}

of the Bloch ball
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{2})
. There is no complex *-subalgebra of
\mathrm{M}_{n}

in any dimension
n
whose state space is a disk.

Remark 2.4
.

Real and complex matrix systems have the same families of state spaces. If

\mathcal{R}
is a real or complex matrix system on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, then the real matrix
system of hermitian matrices
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{R})
has the same set of hermitian
matrices and hence the same set of density matrices as
\mathcal{R}
. Conversely,
any real matrix system
\mathcal{R}
included in
\mathcal{H}(\mathrm{M}_{n})
has the same same set
of hermitian matrices and the same set of density matrices as the complex
matrix system
\mathcal{R}\oplus\mathrm{i}\mathcal{R}
. The set of density matrices of a real
or complex matrix system
\mathcal{R}
is affinely isomorphic to the state space of

\mathcal{R}
by Lemma
2.1
or
2.2
, respectively.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{R}_{2}
be real matrix systems on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and let
\mathcal{R}_{2}\subset\mathcal{R}_{1}
.
We abbreviate

\mathcal{H}_{i}:=\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{R}_{i})
,

\mathcal{C}_{i}:=\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{R}_{i})
,

\mathcal{C}_{i}^{\vee}:=\mathcal{C}^{\vee}(\mathcal{R}_{i})
,

\mathcal{D}_{i}:=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{i})
,

\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R},i}:=\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{R}_{i})
,

\mathcal{S}_{i}:=\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{R}_{i})
,

r_{\mathbb{R},i}:=r_{\mathbb{R},\mathcal{R}_{i}}
, and

r_{i}:=r_{\mathcal{R}_{i}}
,
i=1,2
.
Usually, the orthogonal projection of
\mathcal{R}_{1}
onto
\mathcal{R}_{2}
is the idempotent
self-adjoint linear map
\mathcal{R}_{1}\to\mathcal{R}_{1}
whose range is
\mathcal{R}_{2}
. Reducing the
codomain to the range, we get a map

(2.2)

\pi:\mathcal{R}_{1}\to\mathcal{R}_{2}\,,

whose value at
A\in\mathcal{R}_{1}
is specified by the equations

\mathrm{Re}\langle A-\pi(A),B\rangle=0
for all
B\in\mathcal{R}_{2}
. We refer to
\pi
as
the
orthogonal projection
of
\mathcal{R}_{1}
onto
\mathcal{R}_{2}
in this paper. The
notation
\mathcal{R}_{1}\to\mathcal{R}_{2}
conveying domain and range is useful especially in
Sec.
6
. The adjoint of
\pi
is the embedding

\mathcal{R}_{2}\to\mathcal{R}_{1}
,
A\mapsto A
. If
\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
are complex matrix
systems, then the Frobenius inner product induces the same orthogonal
projection as the Euclidean scalar product (
2.1
).

As
\mathcal{R}_{i}
is the orthogonal direct sum of the spaces of its hermitian and
skew-hermitian matrices,
i=1,2
, the map (
2.2
) restricts to

\pi:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}
, which we denote (aware of the notational imprecision)
by the same symbol
\pi
. The value of
\pi
at
A\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
is specified by

\langle A-\pi(A),B\rangle=0
for all
B\in\mathcal{H}_{2}
.

A
cone
in a Euclidean space

(E,\langle\!\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\!\rangle)
is a subset
C
of
E

that is closed under multiplication with positive
scalars. A
base
of a cone
C
is a subset
B
of
C
such that

0\not\in\aff B
and such that for all nonzero
x\in C
there exist

y\in B
and
s>0
such that
x=sy
holds. Note that we have

B=C\cap\aff B
for every base
B
of a cone
C
. The set

M^{\vee}:=\{x\in E\mid\forall y\in M\colon\langle\!\langle x,y\rangle\!\rangle\geq 0\}

is a closed convex cone for every subset
M\subset E
, called the

dual cone
to
M
. Regarding duality of convex cones, we refer
to
[
45
, Sec. 14]
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{R}_{2}
be real matrix systems on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
\mathcal{R}_{2}\subset\mathcal{R}_{1}

and let
\pi:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}
denote the orthogonal projection.
Then
\mathcal{C}_{2}^{\vee}=\pi(\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\vee})
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}=\pi(\mathcal{D}_{1})
holds.

Proof.

This lemma and its proof are similar to
[
44
, Prop. 5.2]
.
The inclusions
\pi(\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\vee})\subset\mathcal{C}_{2}^{\vee}
and

\mathcal{C}_{2}\supset(\pi(\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\vee}))^{\vee}
are straightforward to verify and imply

\pi(\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\vee})\subset\mathcal{C}_{2}^{\vee}\subset[\pi(\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\vee})]^{\vee}{}^{\vee}\,.

Since
[\pi(\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\vee})]^{\vee}{}^{\vee}
is the closure of the convex cone

\pi(\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\vee})
, it suffices to show that
\pi(\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\vee})
is closed.
By
[
44
, Lemma 3.1]
, this would follow if
\pi(\mathcal{D}_{1})

was a compact base of
\pi(\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\vee})
. As
\mathds{1}_{n}
lies in the interior
of
\mathcal{C}_{1}
in the topology of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, we know that
\mathcal{D}_{1}
is a compact
base of
\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\vee}
, see
[
44
, Lemma 3.3]
. Hence

\pi(\mathcal{D}_{1})
is a compact base of
\pi(\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\vee})
provided we establish
that
0\not\in\aff\pi(\mathcal{D}_{1})
. But this is clear from
\pi
being
trace-preserving:

\tr(\pi(A))=\langle\mathds{1}_{n},\pi(A)\rangle=\langle\mathds{1}_{n},A\rangle=\tr(A)\,,\quad A\in\mathcal{H}_{1}\,.

This completes the proof of
\mathcal{C}_{2}^{\vee}=\pi(\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\vee})
. The identity

\mathcal{C}_{2}^{\vee}=\pi(\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\vee})
and the fact that
\pi
is trace-preserving
imply
\mathcal{D}_{2}=\pi(\mathcal{D}_{1})
.
∎

Lemma
2.5
shows that the orthogonal projection

\pi:\mathcal{R}_{1}\to\mathcal{R}_{2}
restricts to the map

(2.3)

\pi:\mathcal{D}_{1}\to\mathcal{D}_{2}\,,

which we call the
orthogonal projection
of
\mathcal{D}_{1}
onto
\mathcal{D}_{2}
.
To avoid any possible confusion, the orthogonal projections
\mathcal{R}_{1}\to\mathcal{R}_{2}
and

\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}
are written without function labels from here on
(Lemma
3.7
is an exception).

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{R}_{2}
be real matrix systems on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that

\mathcal{R}_{2}\subset\mathcal{R}_{1}

and let
\pi:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{1})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{2})
denote the orthogonal projection.

3.
Direct convex sums

This section addresses affinely independent convex sets, their convex hulls,
and maps defined thereon. Let

\Delta_{m}:=\{(s_{1},\dots,s_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\mid\forall i:s_{i}\geq 0,s_{1}+\dots+s_{m}=1\}

denote the probability simplex, and

\textstyle\Delta_{m}(\epsilon,s_{1},\dots,s_{m}):=\Delta_{m}\cap\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}(s_{i}-\epsilon,s_{i}+\epsilon)

the open hypercube of edge length
2\epsilon
centered at

(s_{1},\dots,s_{m})\in\Delta_{m}
.

A family of convex subsets
K_{1},\dots,K_{m}
of a Euclidean space is

affinely independent
if every point in their convex hull can be
expressed by a unique convex combination
s_{1}x_{1}+\dots+s_{m}x_{m}
. This
means that
(s_{1},\dots,s_{m})\in\Delta_{m}
is unique and
x_{i}\in K_{i}
is
unique for all
i
for which if
s_{i}>0
,
i=1,\dots,m
. The

direct convex sum

[
1
]
of a family
K_{1},\dots,K_{m}

of affinely independent convex sets is defined as their convex hull

K_{1}\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}\cdots\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}K_{m}:=\conv(K_{1}\cup\dots\cup K_{m})\,.

If
K_{1},\dots,K_{m}
is a family of affinely independent compact convex sets,
then their direct convex sum is compact
[
45
, Thm. 17.2]
.
A compactness argument allows us to describe a base of open neighborhoods.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
K_{1},\dots,K_{m}
be affinely independent compact convex subsets of a
Euclidean space, and let
x_{i}\in K_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,m
, and

(s_{1},\dots,s_{m})\in\Delta_{m}
. Let
I:=\{i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\mid s_{i}>0\}
,

\delta:=\min_{i\in I}s_{i}
, and define

\begin{array}[]{r@{\hskip 2pt}l}O_{I}(\epsilon,(A_{i})_{i\in I}):=\{t_{1}y_{1}+\dots+t_{m}y_{m}\mid\thinspace&(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})\in\Delta_{m}(\epsilon,s_{1},\dots,s_{m}),\\
\thinspace&\mbox{$\forall i\colon y_{i}\in K_{i}$ and ($i\in I\Rightarrow y_{i}\in A_{i}$)}\}\end{array}

for every
\epsilon\in(0,\delta]
and open set
A_{i}
in the relative
topology of
K_{i}
containing
x_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,m
. Then the family

\{O_{I}(\epsilon,(A_{i})_{i\in I})\}
is a local base of open neighborhoods at

s_{1}x_{1}+\dots+s_{m}x_{m}
in the relative topology of
K_{1}\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}\cdots\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}K_{m}
.

Proof.

As
K:=K_{1}\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}\cdots\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}K_{m}
is a metric space, it suffices to show that

O:=O_{I}(\epsilon,(A_{i})_{i\in I})
is open and there are arbitrary small
sets of this form.

Let
B_{i}:=A_{i}
if
i\in I
and
B_{i}:=K_{i}
if
i\not\in I
,
i=1,\dots,m
.
Then the set

\textstyle U:=\Delta_{m}(\epsilon,s_{1},\dots,s_{m})\oplus\bigoplus_{i}B_{i}

is open in the relative topology of the compact set

\widetilde{K}:=\Delta_{m}\oplus\bigoplus_{i}K_{i}
. The complement

U^{\complement}:=\widetilde{K}\setminus U=\underbrace{\textstyle\Delta_{m}(\epsilon,s_{1},\dots,s_{m})^{\complement}\oplus\bigoplus_{i}K_{i}}_{C:=}\cup\bigcup_{j\in I}\underbrace{\textstyle\Delta_{m}\oplus(\bigoplus_{i\neq j}K_{i})\oplus A_{j}^{\complement}}_{C_{j}:=}

is compact. Hence, its image under the continuous surjective map

\textstyle\beta:\widetilde{K}\to K\,,\quad((t_{i})_{i=1}^{m},(y_{i})_{i=1}^{m})\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}y_{i}

is compact. We prove that
O=\beta(U)
is open in
K
by showing that

\beta(U)
is disjoint from
\beta(U^{\complement})
. Let

u:=(t_{1},\dots,t_{m},y_{1},\dots,y_{m})\in U\,.

Then
\beta(u)\not\in\beta(C)
follows as the vector
(t_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
is
uniquely determined by
\beta(u)
. For every
j\in I
we have
s_{j}>0
,
hence
t_{j}>0
by the definition of
O
. Thus,
y_{j}
is uniquely determined
by
\beta(u)
, which proves
\beta(u)\not\in\beta(C_{j})
.

Let
|M|:=\sup_{x,y\in M}|y-x|
denote the diameter of a set
M
. The
distance of
x:=s_{1}x_{1}+\dots+s_{m}x_{m}
from a point
t_{1}y_{1}+\dots+t_{m}y_{m}

in
O
is bounded by

\textstyle\sum_{i}\left(|s_{i}-t_{i}||x_{i}|+t_{i}|x_{i}-y_{i}|\right)\leq\epsilon\sum_{i}|x_{i}|+\sum_{i\in I}t_{i}|A_{i}|+\epsilon\sum_{i\not\in I}|K_{i}|\,.

Choosing open sets
(A_{i})_{i\in I}
with diameters at most
\epsilon
,
we obtain

\textstyle|O|\leq 2\sup_{y\in O}|x-y|\leq 2\epsilon\left(\sum_{i}|x_{i}|+1+\sum_{i\not\in I}|K_{i}|\right)\,,

which completes the proof, as the compact sets
K_{i}
have finite
diameters and as
\epsilon
can be chosen arbitrarily small.
∎

Let
E_{1},\dots,E_{m}
be Euclidean spaces. We consider
E_{i}
as a
subspace of the direct sum
\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}E_{j}

via
the
embedding

\textstyle E_{i}\to\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}E_{j}\,,\quad x\mapsto(\underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{\makebox[0.0pt]{\scriptsize$i-1$ zeros}},x,\underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{\makebox[0.0pt]{\scriptsize$m-i$ zeros}})\,,\quad i=1,\dots,m\,.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
K_{i}\subset E_{i}
and
L_{i}\subset F_{i}
be compact convex subsets of Euclidean
spaces
E_{i}
and
F_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,m
, such that
K_{1},\dots,K_{m}
are affinely
independent in
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}E_{i}
and
L_{1},\dots,L_{m}
are affinely
independent in
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}F_{i}
. If
f_{i}:K_{i}\to L_{i}
is a map,

i=1,\dots,m
, then a map

(3.1)

\textstyle f_{1}\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}\cdots\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}f_{m}:K_{1}\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}\cdots\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}K_{m}\to L_{1}\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}\cdots\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}L_{m}

is well defined by

\textstyle s_{1}x_{1}+\dots+s_{m}x_{m}\mapsto s_{1}f_{1}(x_{1})+\dots+s_{m}f_{m}(x_{m})\,.

If
f_{i}
is open and surjective for
i=1,\dots,m
, then
f_{1}\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}\cdots\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}f_{m}

is open.

Proof.

The map is well defined as the sets
K_{1},\dots,K_{m}
are affinely independent
and the sets
L_{1},\dots,L_{m}
are affinely independent. Regarding the openness
of
f_{1}\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}\cdots\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}f_{m}
, it suffices to show that the images of the members
of a base of the relative topology of
K_{1}\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}\cdots\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}K_{m}
are open.
Using the base of Lemma
3.1
, we have

f_{1}\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}\cdots\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}f_{m}[O_{I}(\epsilon,(A_{i})_{i\in I})]=O_{I}[\epsilon,(f(A_{i}))_{i\in I}]\,,

which is open in
L_{1}\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}\cdots\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}L_{m}
. The required identity of
f_{i}(K_{i})=L_{i}

for every
i\not\in I
is a consequence of
f_{i}
being surjective.
∎

We call the map (
3.1
) the
direct convex sum
of the maps

f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
. Next, we recall a sufficient condition for the affine
independence of convex sets
[
16
]
.

Remark 3.3
.

Let
K_{i}
be a convex subset of a Euclidean space
E_{i}
such that

0\not\in\aff K_{i}
holds for
i=1,\dots,m
. Then
K_{1},\dots,K_{m}

are affinely independent in the direct sum
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}E_{i}
.

Returning to matrix systems, we consider a real matrix system
\mathcal{R}_{i}

on
\mathbb{C}^{n_{i}}
,
i=1,\dots,m
. The direct sum
\mathcal{R}:=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{R}_{i}
is
a real matrix system on
\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}+\dots+n_{m}}
. The Frobenius inner product of

(A_{i})_{i=1}^{m},(B_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\in\mathcal{R}
is

\langle(A_{i})_{i=1}^{m},(B_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\langle A_{i},B_{i}\rangle
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{i}
be a real matrix system on
\mathbb{C}^{n_{i}}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{R}_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathcal{R}_{m})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{R}_{1})\oplus\dots\oplus\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{R}_{m})\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\vee}(\mathcal{R}_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathcal{R}_{m})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}^{\vee}(\mathcal{R}_{1})\oplus\dots\oplus\mathcal{C}^{\vee}(\mathcal{R}_{m})\,,

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathcal{R}_{m})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{1})\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}\cdots\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{m})\,.

Proof.

The first identity is clear. The second one follows by induction from
m=2
,
a case that is easy to verify. By Rem.
3.3
and because

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{i})
is included in the hyperplane of trace-one matrices

,
the sets
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{1}),\dots,\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{m})
are affinely independent in the direct
sum
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{R}_{i})
. The third identity follows from the second
one by enforcing the trace to be one.
∎

B^{\ast}&C\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad A\in\mathcal{H}(M_{p})\,,\quad B\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times q}\,,\quad C\in\mathcal{H}(M_{q})

is positive semidefinite if and only if the top left block
A
is positive
semidefinite, the range of
B
is included in the range of
A
, and the

generalized Schur complement

M/A=C-B^{\ast}A^{-}B
is positive
semidefinite, where
A^{-}
is a
generalized inverse
of
A
, that is
to say,
A^{-}\in M_{p}
and
AA^{-}A=A
holds
[
25
]
.

Proposition 6.3
.

The orthogonal projection
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{p+q})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{p}\oplus\mathrm{M}_{q})
is open.

Proof.

Lemma
6.1
proves the claim when
\mathcal{R}:=\mathrm{M}_{p+q}
and

\mathcal{A}:=\mathrm{M}_{p}\oplus\mathrm{M}_{q}
. The set of density matrices
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{p+q})
is stable
by Rem.
5.1
b). The reflection
\gamma:\mathrm{M}_{p+q}\to\mathrm{M}_{p+q}
at
the subspace
\mathrm{M}_{p}\oplus\mathrm{M}_{q}
generates a group of order two.

We show that
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{p+q})
is invariant under
\gamma
. In block form,
the reflection reads

\textstyle\gamma:\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
D&C\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}A&-B\\
-D&C\end{pmatrix}\,,

where
A\in\mathrm{M}_{p}
,
B\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times q}
,
C\in\mathrm{M}_{q}
, and
D\in\mathbb{C}^{q\times p}
.
The space of hermitian matrices is invariant under
\gamma
, which restricts to

\textstyle\mathcal{H}(\mathrm{M}_{p+q})\to\mathcal{H}(\mathrm{M}_{p+q})\,,\quad M=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
B^{\ast}&C\end{pmatrix}\mapsto M^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}A&-B\\
-B^{\ast}&C\end{pmatrix}\,,

where
A\in\mathcal{H}(\mathrm{M}_{p})
,
B\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times q}
, and
C\in\mathcal{H}(\mathrm{M}_{q})
. By
Rem.
6.2
, the map
M\mapsto M^{\prime}
preserves the positive
semidefiniteness since
M
and
M^{\prime}
have the same diagonal blocks and
both off-diagonal blocks differ in a sign, so that
M^{\prime}/A=M/A
holds.
The map
\gamma
preserves the trace. The set of fixed points

\mathrm{M}_{p}\oplus\mathrm{M}_{q}=\{A\in\mathrm{M}_{p+q}:\gamma(A)=A\}
is a *-subalgebra of
\mathrm{M}_{p+q}
.
∎

Corollary 6.4
.

The orthogonal projection

\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n_{1}}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathrm{M}_{n_{k}})

where

Proceeding by induction, we observe that the orthogonal projection

\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n_{1}})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n_{1}})

\textstyle\pi_{k}:\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n_{1}+\dots+n_{k}})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n_{1}}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathrm{M}_{n_{k}})

Hence, by Lemma
2.5
, the map

(6.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n_{1}+\dots+n_{k+1}})

(for

∎

6.1

7.
Real *-subalgebras of
\mathrm{M}_{n}

Every real *-subalgebra of
\mathrm{M}_{n}
is *-isomorphic
[
21
, Thm. 5.22]

to a direct sum of algebras of real, complex, and quaternionic

q
-by-
q
-matrices of various sizes
q
. We are here interested in the
algebra
\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
of real
n\times n
matrices.

Proposition 7.1
.

The orthogonal projection
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R}))
is open.

Proof.

Lemma
6.1
proves the claim when
\mathcal{R}:=\mathrm{M}_{n}
and

\mathcal{A}:=\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. The set of density matrices
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})
is stable
by Rem.
5.1
b). The reflection
\gamma:\mathrm{M}_{n}\to\mathrm{M}_{n}
at
the real subspace
\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
generates a group of order two.

The algebra
\mathrm{M}_{n}

is the orthogonal direct sum
\mathrm{M}_{n}=\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and the
reflection at the real subspace
\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
reads

\textstyle\gamma:\mathrm{M}_{n}\to\mathrm{M}_{n}\,,\quad A+\mathrm{i}B\mapsto A-\mathrm{i}B\,,\quad A,B\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\,.

The orthogonal transformation
\gamma
preserves the space of hermitian matrices,
which is the orthogonal direct sum

\mathcal{H}(\mathrm{M}_{n})=\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathrm{i}\,\mathrm{Skew}_{n}(\mathbb{R})

of the space of real symmetric matrices

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle:=\{A\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\colon A{}^{\mathrm{T}}=A\}=\mathcal{H}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R}))

and the space of skew-symmetric matrices

\displaystyle\mathrm{Skew}_{n}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle:=\{A\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\colon A{}^{\mathrm{T}}=-A\}\,.

We prove that
\gamma
preserves the trace and the positive semidefiniteness
on the space of hermitian matrices. Let
A,C\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and

B,D\in\mathrm{Skew}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. Then

\tr(A+\mathrm{i}B)=\tr(A)
shows that the trace is preserved. It is well known
that a matrix is positive semidefinite if and only if its inner product
with the square of every hermitian matrix is nonnegative. Thus

\displaystyle\langle A-\mathrm{i}B,(C+\mathrm{i}D)^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle A+\mathrm{i}B,(C-\mathrm{i}D)^{2}\rangle

shows that
A-\mathrm{i}B
is positive semidefinite if
A+\mathrm{i}B
is positive
semidefinite. Clearly,
\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})=\{A\in\mathrm{M}_{n}:\gamma(A)=A\}
is a real
*-subalgebra of
\mathrm{M}_{n}
.
∎

As the orthogonal projection
\mathrm{M}_{n}\to\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is the entrywise real part,
we denote by
\mathrm{Re}:\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R}))
the orthogonal projection
of
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})
onto
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R}))
.

Example 7.2
.

We consider the chain
\mathrm{M}_{3}\supset\mathrm{M}_{2}\oplus\mathrm{M}_{1}\supset\mathrm{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathrm{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R})

of real *-subalgebras of
\mathrm{M}_{3}
. The orthogonal projections

\textstyle\mathrm{M}_{3}\longrightarrow\mathrm{M}_{2}\oplus\mathrm{M}_{1}\longrightarrow\mathrm{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathrm{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R})

restrict by Lemma
2.5
to

\textstyle\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{3})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{2}\oplus\mathrm{M}_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{2}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathrm{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R}))\,.

The map
\pi_{1}
is open by Prop.
6.3
. By
Lemma
3.7
, the map
\pi_{2}
is the direct convex sum

\textstyle\mathrm{Re}\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}\idty:\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{2})\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}\{1\}\to\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))\oplus_{\mathrm{c}}\{1\}

of
\mathrm{Re}:\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{2})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
and the identity map
\idty:\{1\}\to\{1\}
.
The map
\mathrm{Re}
is open by Prop.
7.1
, hence
\pi_{2}
is open
by Prop.
3.2
. The orthogonal projection

\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{3})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathrm{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R}))
is open, as it is a composition of
two open maps.

8.
Topology simplified by algebra

Thm.
1.2
can simplify topology problems. Given topological spaces

K,L
, a map
f:K\to L
is
continuous

[
27
]
at
x\in K
if
the preimage of every neighborhood of
f(x)
in
L
is a neighborhood of
x

in
K
.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{R}_{2}
be real matrix systems on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that

\mathcal{R}_{2}\subset\mathcal{R}_{1}
. Let
\pi_{1}:\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{1})
and

\pi_{2}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{1})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{2})
denote the orthogonal projections and assume the
orthogonal projection
\pi_{2}\circ\pi_{1}:\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{2})
is open. Let

f:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{2})\to T
be a map to a topological space
T
. Let
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{1})
.

a)

The map
f\circ\pi_{2}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{1})\to T
is open at
\rho
if and only if

f:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{2})\to T
is open at
\pi_{2}(\rho)
.

b)

The map
f\circ\pi_{2}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{1})\to T
is continuous at
\rho
if and
only if the map
f:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{2})\to T
is continuous at
\pi_{2}(\rho)
.

Proof.

The orthogonal projection
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{2})
equals indeed

\pi_{2}\circ\pi_{1}
by Lemma
2.5
.

We begin with the implication “
\Rightarrow
” of a). If
\mathcal{N}_{2}\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{2})

is a neighborhood of
\pi_{2}(\rho)
, then

\textstyle f(\mathcal{N}_{2})=(f\circ\pi_{2})\circ\pi_{2}^{-1}(\mathcal{N}_{2})

is a neighborhood of
f(\pi_{2}(\rho))
because
\pi_{2}
is continuous and

f\circ\pi_{2}
is open at
\rho
. Regarding the implication “
\Leftarrow
”,
we choose a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{1}\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{1})
of
\rho
. Then

\textstyle f\circ\pi_{2}(\mathcal{N}_{1})=f\circ(\pi_{2}\circ\pi_{1})\circ\pi_{1}^{-1}(\mathcal{N}_{1})

is a neighborhood of
f(\pi_{2}(\rho))
because
\pi_{1}
is continuous,

\pi_{2}\circ\pi_{1}
is open, and
f
is open at
\pi_{2}(\rho)
.

To prove b) we choose a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{T}\subset T
of
f(\pi_{2}(\rho))
.
Regarding the implication “
\Rightarrow
”, the preimage

\textstyle f^{-1}(\mathcal{N}_{T})=(\pi_{2}\circ\pi_{1})\circ\pi_{1}^{-1}\circ(f\circ\pi_{2})^{-1}(\mathcal{N}_{T})

is a neighborhood of
\pi_{2}(\rho)
, because
f\circ\pi_{2}
is continuous
at
\rho
, the map
\pi_{1}
is continuous, and
\pi_{2}\circ\pi_{1}
is open.
Regarding the implication “
\Leftarrow
”, the preimage

\textstyle(f\circ\pi_{2})^{-1}(\mathcal{N}_{T})=\pi_{2}^{-1}\circ f^{-1}(\mathcal{N}_{T})

is a neighborhood of
\rho
, as
f
is continuous at
\pi_{2}(\rho)
,
and
\pi_{2}
is continuous.
∎

Remark 8.2

(Simplifying openness problems)
.

Let
\pi:\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})
be the orthogonal projection to a real
matrix system
\mathcal{R}
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and let
\mathcal{A}
be a *-subalgebra of
\mathrm{M}_{n}

such that
\mathcal{R}\subset\mathcal{A}
. Then
\pi=\pi_{\mathcal{R}}\circ\pi_{\mathcal{A}}
factors into
the orthogonal projections
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}:\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
and

\pi_{\mathcal{R}}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})
. For every
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})
the map

\pi
is open at
\rho
if and only if
\pi_{\mathcal{R}}
is open at

\pi_{\mathcal{A}}(\rho)
.

Indeed, the map
\pi
factors by Lemma
2.5
. The second
claim follows from Lemma
8.1
a), by letting
\mathcal{R}_{1}:=\mathrm{M}_{n}

and
\mathcal{R}_{2}:=\mathcal{A}
, and by taking
\pi_{\mathcal{R}}
as the map
f:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{2})\to T
.
The assumptions of the lemma are met since
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}
is open by
Thm.
1.2
.

The following examples demonstrate the use of Rem.
8.2
.
Continuity problems are a topic of Sec.
9
below.

Example 8.3
.

Keeping the notation of
\pi=\pi_{\mathcal{R}}\circ\pi_{\mathcal{A}}
from
Rem.
8.2
, we take
\mathcal{R}:=\spn_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathds{1}_{3},X\oplus 1,Z\oplus 0)

\mathcal{A}:=\mathrm{M}_{2}\oplus\mathrm{M}_{1}
.

By Ex.
3.5
a), the map
\pi_{\mathcal{R}}

8.2
, the map

\pi:\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{3})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})

\pi_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(\mathcal{G}_{0})

To describe
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(\mathcal{G}_{0})

\bra{\varphi}&c\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&c\end{pmatrix}\,,

where

,

\lambda\in[0,1]

Using the generalized Schur complement (Rem.

), one
verifies that the fiber of

\textstyle\rho(\lambda,z):=\begin{pmatrix}(1-\lambda)\ket{+}\!\!\bra{+}&z\ket{+}\\
\overline{z}\bra{+}&\lambda\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\,,\quad|z|^{2}\leq\lambda(1-\lambda)\,,

where
|z|
denotes the absolute value of
z\in\mathbb{C}
. In conclusion, the
orthogonal projection
\pi:\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{3})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})
is not open at any point
of

\textstyle\pi_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(\mathcal{G}_{0})=\{\rho(\lambda,z)\colon z\in\mathbb{C},|z|^{2}\leq\lambda(1-\lambda),\lambda\in(0,1]\}

and open at every point in the complement.

c)

We verify a claim made in Ex.
4.6
. The segment

\mathcal{G}=\big[\ket{+}\!\!\bra{+}\oplus 0,0\oplus 1\big]
is the fiber of

\pi_{\mathcal{R}}
over
M=\frac{1}{2}(\ket{+}\!\!\bra{+}\oplus 1)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})
by
Ex.
3.5
b), so
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(\mathcal{G})
is the fiber of

\pi=\pi_{\mathcal{R}}\circ\pi_{\mathcal{A}}
over
M
. As recalled in part a) above, the map

\pi
is open at
\ket{+}\!\!\bra{+}\oplus 0
but not open at any point of

\pi_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(\mathcal{G}_{0})
. We now observe that
\pi
is not open at any point
in the relative interior of
\pi^{-1}(M)
, as we have the chain of
inclusions

\displaystyle\textstyle\hphantom{{}={}}\pi_{\mathcal{A}}\Big(\ri\big((\pi_{\mathcal{R}}\circ\pi_{\mathcal{A}})^{-1}(M)\big)\Big)=\ri\Big(\pi_{\mathcal{A}}\big((\pi_{\mathcal{R}}\circ\pi_{\mathcal{A}})^{-1}(M)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\textstyle=\ri(\pi_{\mathcal{R}}^{-1}(M))=\ri(\mathcal{G})=\mathcal{G}_{0}\setminus\{0\oplus 1\}\subset\mathcal{G}_{0}\,,

whose first equality holds by
[
45
, Thm. 6.6]
.

Example 8.4
.

The recipe of Rem.
8.2
helps analyze the openness of
the orthogonal projection
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}(P,Q))
to the real matrix system

\textstyle\mathcal{R}(P,Q)=\spn_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathds{1}_{n},P,Q)

generated by two orthogonal projections
P,Q\in\mathrm{M}_{n}
, that is to say, matrices
satisfying
P=P^{2}=P^{\ast}
and
Q=Q^{2}=Q^{\ast}
. It is well known that the matrix
system
\mathcal{R}(P,Q)
is included in a surprisingly small *-subalgebra of
\mathrm{M}_{n}
,
see Coro. 2.2 in the survey
[
8
]
by Böttcher and
Spitkovsky, and the references therein. More precisely, there exists a unitary

n\times n
matrix
U
, nonnegative integers
m_{1}\leq 4
and
m_{2}
, and positive
integers
k_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,m_{1}
satisfying
k_{1}+\dots+k_{m_{1}}+2m_{2}=n
, such
that

is included in

\textstyle\mathcal{A}:=\big(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\mathrm{M}_{1}\otimes\mathds{1}_{k_{i}}\big)\oplus\big(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m_{2}}\mathrm{M}_{2}\big)\,.

Since

is a homeomorphism of

, the

\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}(P,Q))

and
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})
are equivalent. The second one is substantially
simplified by the method of Rem.
8.2
as
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
has a
rather simple shape. It is the direct convex sum of several three-dimensional
Euclidean balls and a simplex of dimension at most three by
Lemma
3.4
and Ex.
2.3
. This observation should also
simplify the strong continuity problem for the numerical range
W(P+\mathrm{i}Q)
.

9.
Continuity in quantum information theory

We discuss continuity problems of entropic inference maps and of measures
of correlation. We assume that
\mathcal{R}
is a real matrix system on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
that, without loss of generality (see Rem.
2.4
), we
have
\mathcal{R}\subset\mathcal{H}(\mathrm{M}_{n})
.

Example 9.1

(Maximum entropy inference I)
.

The purpose of the maximum entropy inference method is to update a prior
probability distribution if new information becomes available in the form of
constraints that specify a set of possible posterior probability
distributions. The preferred posterior is that which minimizes the relative
entropy from the prior subject to the available constraints, see Chap. 8 in
Caticha’s book
[
12
]
and the references therein. An analogous
quantum mechanical inference method can be defined by replacing probability
distributions with density matrices and the standard relative entropy with
the Umegaki relative entropy. The axiomatic foundations of the maximum
entropy inference method were settled for probability distributions in the
1980’s, see Chap. 6 in
[
12
, pp. 157–160]
. More than 30 years
later, the axioms of the quantum inference are still a matter of discussion

[
3
]
but a new approach appeared in the work of Vanslette
recently
[
51
]
.

Linear constraints
4
4

4

Linear constraints can be defined in terms of expectation values.
Let
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
be hermitian
n\times n
matrices such that

\mathcal{R}=\spn_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathds{1}_{n},A_{1},\dots,A_{k})
. The observables represented by

A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
have the
expectation values

v(\rho)=(\langle\rho,A_{1}\rangle,\dots,\langle\rho,A_{k}\rangle){}^{\mathrm{T}}
if

\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})
is the system state
[
7
]
. The
fiber
\pi^{-1}(\sigma)
over
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})
is the set of

\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})
whose expectation values are
v(\rho)=v(\sigma)
, see
Lemma
4.1
.

on
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})
are defined by the orthogonal projection

\textstyle\pi:\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})\,.

The
relative entropy

S:\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\times\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\to[0,+\infty]

is an asymmetric distance. It is defined by

S(\rho_{1},\rho_{2}):=\tr[\rho_{1}(\log(\rho_{1})-\log(\rho_{2}))]
if the range
of
\rho_{1}
is included in the range of
\rho_{2}
and by

S(\rho_{1},\rho_{2}):=+\infty
otherwise, for all
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})
.
Let
\tau\in\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})
, the
prior
, be a density matrix of
maximal rank
n
. Then

\textstyle\phi_{\tau}:\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\to\mathbb{R}\,,\quad\rho\mapsto-S(\rho,\tau)

is continuous and strictly concave. So the
maximum entropy inference map

(9.1)

\textstyle\Psi_{\tau}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\,,\quad\sigma\mapsto\argmax_{\rho\in\pi^{-1}(\sigma)}\phi_{\tau}(\rho)

is well defined, see
[
53
, Def. 1.1]
and the references therein.
Discontinuities of this inference map
[
56
]
aroused interest in
theoretical physics

[
13
,
26
,
36
,
57
]
. The map
(
9.1
) is continuous for commutative real matrix systems
\mathcal{R}
,
for example for the inference of probability distributions mentioned above.

We quote
[
53
, Thm. 4.9]
.

Theorem 9.2
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})
. Then
\Psi_{\tau}
is continuous at
\sigma
if and
only if
\pi
is open at
\Psi_{\tau}(\sigma)
.

In what follows, continuity and openness problems will be simplified by
factoring
\pi=\pi_{\mathcal{R}}\circ\pi_{\mathcal{A}}
through a *-subalgebra
\mathcal{A}
of
\mathrm{M}_{n}

that includes
\mathcal{R}
, using the notation of Rem.
8.2
.

Example 9.3

(Maximum entropy inference II)
.

Before simplifying the openness problem of
\pi
, we describe the set

\Psi_{\tau}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}))
of posteriors, as the openness only matters for
points in this set by Thm.
9.2
. Recalling
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{R})
,
we define

\textstyle\mathcal{E}_{\tau}(\mathcal{R}):=\big\{\frac{e^{\log(\tau)+A}}{\tr(e^{\log(\tau)+A})}\mid A\in\mathcal{R}\big\}\,.

The manifold
\mathcal{E}_{\tau}(\mathcal{R})
is known as a
Gibbsian family
or

exponential family
, see

[
56
,
38
,
53
,
43
]
and the
references therein. By (D5) in
[
53
]
we have

(9.2)

\textstyle\Psi_{\tau}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}))=\{\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n}):\inf_{\rho_{2}\in\mathcal{E}_{\tau}(\mathcal{R})}S(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})=0\}\,.

The right-hand side of (
9.2
) is the

reverse information closure
or
rI-closure

[
18
]

of
\mathcal{E}_{\tau}(\mathcal{R})
, which is a subset of the Euclidean closure of

\mathcal{E}_{\tau}(\mathcal{R})
.

a)

If
\tau=\mathds{1}_{n}/n
is the uniform prior, then

\textstyle\phi_{\tau}(\rho)=-S(\rho,\mathds{1}_{n}/n)=S(\rho)-\log(n)\,,\quad\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})

is the
von Neumann entropy

S(\rho):=-\tr[\rho\log(\rho)]
up to a
constant. By functional calculus,
\mathcal{E}_{\tau}(\mathcal{R})
is included in
\mathcal{A}
and
so is the set of posteriors
\Psi_{\tau}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}))
as per (
9.2
),
because
\mathcal{A}
is closed. Rem.
8.2
then shows that for every

\rho\in\Psi_{\tau}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}))
the openness of
\pi
at
\rho
is equivalent
to the openness of
\pi_{\mathcal{R}}=\pi|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}
at
\rho

(the same conclusion is true for every prior
\tau
in
\mathcal{A}
). This can
simplify the problem if
\mathcal{A}
has a simpler structure or a smaller dimension
than
\mathrm{M}_{n}
. An example is given in Ex.
8.4
above.

b)

If the prior
\tau
lies outside of
\mathcal{A}
then
\mathcal{E}_{\tau}(\mathcal{R})
is disjoint
from
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
, again by functional calculus. Rem.
8.2

then shows that for every
\rho\in\Psi_{\tau}(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}))
the openness of
\pi

at
\rho
is equivalent to the openness of
\pi_{\mathcal{R}}
at
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}(\rho)
.
This is an even greater simplification than in part a) above, because the
analysis of
\pi
on
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})
is reduced to that of
\pi_{\mathcal{R}}
on

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
.

c)

Independently of
\mathcal{A}
, it sometimes helps that the posterior

\Psi_{\tau}(\sigma)
is contained in the relative interior of the fiber

\pi^{-1}(\sigma)
over
\sigma
for every
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})
and prior

\tau
by Coro. 5.7 and Lemma 5.8 in
[
53
]
. As an example, the
orthogonal projection
\pi:\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{3})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})
to the real matrix
system
\mathcal{R}
in Ex.
8.3
is not open anywhere in the relative
interior of the fiber over a certain point
M
and open at every point
in the complement of that fiber. Thm.
9.2
then shows that

\Psi_{\tau}
is discontinuous at
M
and continuous everywhere else in
the ellipse
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})
for every prior
\tau
.

From here on, we assume the prior
\tau:=\mathds{1}_{n}/n
be uniform. Using the
von Neumann entropy
S=\phi_{\tau}+\log(n)
, we write the inference map
(
9.1
) from Ex.
9.1
as

\textstyle\Psi:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\,,\quad\sigma\mapsto\argmax_{\rho\in\pi^{-1}(\sigma)}S(\rho)\,.

We also write
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R}):=\mathcal{E}_{\tau}(\mathcal{R})
for the exponential family of
Ex.
9.3
.

Example 9.4

(Maximum entropy inference III)
.

An important example from physics is the real matrix system of

local Hamiltonians

[
13
,
59
]
.

Every unit
i\in\Omega:=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
of an
N
-qubit system is associated
with a copy
\mathcal{A}_{i}
of the algebra
\mathrm{M}_{2}
. The subsystem with units in a subset

\nu\subset\Omega
is associated with the tensor product algebra

\mathcal{A}_{\nu}:=\bigotimes_{i\in\nu}\mathcal{A}_{i}
, whose identity we denote by
\mathds{1}_{\nu}
.
We have
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}=\mathrm{M}_{n}
for
n=2^{N}
. The algebra
\mathcal{A}_{\nu}
embeds into

\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}

via
the map
\mathcal{A}_{\nu}\to\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
,

A\mapsto A\otimes\mathds{1}_{\bar{\nu}}
, where
\bar{\nu}=\Omega\setminus\nu
is the
complement of
\nu
. Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a family of subsets of
\Omega
.
A
\mathfrak{g}
-local Hamiltonian
is a hermitian matrix in
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
of the
form

\textstyle\sum_{\nu\in\mathfrak{g}}A_{\nu}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\bar{\nu}}\,,\quad A_{\nu}\in\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{A}_{\nu})\,,\quad\nu\in\mathfrak{g}\,.

We denote the real matrix system of all
\mathfrak{g}
-local Hamiltonians by
\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{g}}

and the orthogonal projection by
\pi_{\mathfrak{g}}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{g}})
.

The
partial trace

\tr_{\bar{\nu}}:\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}\to\mathcal{A}_{\nu}
is the adjoint of
the embedding
\mathcal{A}_{\nu}\to\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
and satisfies

\langle A\otimes\mathds{1}_{\bar{\nu}},B\rangle=\langle A,\tr_{\bar{\nu}}(B)\rangle
for
every
A\in\mathcal{A}_{\nu}
,
B\in\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
. The partial trace
\tr_{\bar{\nu}}(\rho)

of
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})
is a density matrix of
\mathcal{A}_{\nu}
called

reduced density matrix
. Let

\textstyle\red_{\mathfrak{g}}:\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}\to\prod_{\nu\in\mathfrak{g}}\mathcal{A}_{\nu}\,,\quad A\mapsto[\tr_{\bar{\nu}}(A)]_{\nu\in\mathfrak{g}}

denote the map from
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
to the cartesian product of the algebras

(\mathcal{A}_{\nu})_{\nu\in\mathfrak{g}}
that assigns reduced density matrices.

Linear constraints on
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})
have been defined in terms of reduced
density matrices, see
[
38
,
13
]
and

[
59
, Sec. 1.4.2]
. This is formalized in the following diagram,
which commutes by formula (19) in
[
55
]
. (Obvious restrictions
of the domain and codomain of
\red_{\mathfrak{g}}
are omitted in the sequel.)

\textstyle{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{\mathfrak{g}}}
\scriptstyle{\red_{\mathfrak{g}}}
\textstyle{\red_{\mathfrak{g}}[\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})]}
\textstyle{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{g}})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\red_{\mathfrak{g}}}

The fiber
\pi_{\mathfrak{g}}^{-1}(\sigma)
over
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{g}})
is the set of
all
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})
such that
\red_{\mathfrak{g}}(\rho)=\red_{\mathfrak{g}}(\sigma)
.
Thm.
9.2
proves that the pullback
\Xi:=\Psi\circ\red_{\mathfrak{g}}^{-1}

of the inference map
\Psi
under
\red_{\mathfrak{g}}^{-1}
is continuous at

(\rho_{\nu})_{\nu\in\mathfrak{g}}\in\red_{\mathfrak{g}}[\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})]
if and only if

\red_{\mathfrak{g}}
is open at
\Xi[(\rho_{\nu})_{\nu\in\mathfrak{g}}]\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})
.

Chen et al.
[
13
, Ex. 4]
discovered a discontinuity of
\Xi

for
N=3
qubits and
\mathfrak{g}:=\{\{1,2\},\{2,3\},\{3,1\}\}
at

(\rho_{\nu})_{\nu\in\mathfrak{g}}\in\red_{\mathfrak{g}}[\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})]
, where

\textstyle\rho_{\nu}:=\frac{1}{2}(\ket{00}\!\!\bra{00}+\ket{11}\!\!\bra{11})\,,\quad\nu\in\mathfrak{g}

and they offered an interesting interpretation in terms of phase transitions.
The map
\red_{\mathfrak{g}}
being open
5
5

5

The openness of
\red_{\mathfrak{g}}
at
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})
is
a priori

weaker than the continuity of
\Xi
at
\red_{\mathfrak{g}}(\rho)
. The
continuity means that any sufficiently small change of
\red_{\mathfrak{g}}(\rho)

can be matched by an arbitrarily small change of
\rho

inside
the
image of
\Xi
and not just anywhere in
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})
.
Somewhat surprisingly, the two propositions are equivalent by
Thm.
9.2
.

at
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})
means that any sufficiently small change of

\red_{\mathfrak{g}}(\rho)
in
\red_{\mathfrak{g}}[\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})]
is matched by an
arbitrarily small change of
\rho
within
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega})
. Conversely,
if the openness fails, then there are arbitrarily small changes of

\red_{\mathfrak{g}}(\rho)
that can only be matched by changes of
\rho
beyond
some strictly positive threshold (in the metric sense). Loosely speaking,
a small change of a subsystem abruptly changes the entire system. Such
behavior is associated with phase transitions. It motivates every attempt
to study the openness of
\pi_{\mathfrak{g}}
. This should be done by tuning the
interaction pattern
\mathfrak{g}
to a concrete system. Whether enclosing

\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{g}}
into a *-subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
could simplify this
problem, as suggested by Exa.
9.3
, is not yet clarified.

We finish this paper with a map whose continuity is more subtle than
that of the inference.

Example 9.5

(Entropy distance I)
.

The
entropy distance
from the exponential family
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R})
is
defined by

(9.3)

\textstyle d:\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\to\mathbb{R}\,,\quad\rho_{1}\mapsto\inf_{\rho_{2}\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R})}S(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})

and equals the difference

(9.4)

\textstyle d(\rho)=S(\Psi\circ\pi(\rho))-S(\rho)\,,\quad\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})

between the value of the von Neumann entropy at
\rho
and the maximal value
on the fiber of
\pi
that contains
\rho
, see p. 1288 in
[
53
]
.
Formula (
9.4
) suggests studying the continuity of
d
through
the
rI-projection

\textstyle\Pi:\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})\,,\quad\Pi:=\Psi\circ\pi\,.

As per Def. 5.2 and equation (D8) in
[
53
]
, the density matrix

\Pi(\rho_{1})
is the generalized rI-projection of

\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})
to
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R})
, which is a well-known concept in
probability theory
[
18
]
, and which is defined as follows.
A sequence
(\tau_{i})\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})

rI-converges
to

\rho_{2}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})
if
\lim_{i}S(\rho_{2},\tau_{i})=0
holds. If every sequence

(\tau_{i})\subset\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R})
satisfying
\lim_{i}S(\rho_{1},\tau_{i})=d(\rho_{1})

rI-converges, independently of the sequence, to a unique
\rho_{2}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})
,
not necessarily in
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R})
, then
\rho_{2}
is the

generalized rI-projection
of
\rho_{1}
to
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R})
.

We quote from Lemma 5.15 and Lemma 4.5 in
[
53
]
.

Lemma 9.6
.

a)

For every
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})
the rI-projection
\Pi
is continuous
at
\rho
if and only if the entropy distance
d
is continuous at
\rho
.

b)

For every
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})
, the inference map
\Psi
is continuous
at
\sigma
if and only if
d
is continuous at every point in the fiber

\pi^{-1}(\sigma)
.

Example 9.7

(Entropy distance II)
.

The entropy distance from the exponential family
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{g}})
of local
Hamiltonians (Ex.
9.4
) is interesting because it quantifies many-body
correlations. Amari
[
4
]
and Ay
[
6
]
studied this type of
correlation measures in probability theory. Linden et al.
[
33
]

introduced it to quantum mechanics as a difference of von Neumann entropies like
formula (
9.4
), see also
[
59
, Sec. 1.4.2]
. Zhou

[
60
]
proved the equality of the two representations (
9.3
)
and (
9.4
) for density matrices of maximal rank
n
, see also

[
38
]
; the equality is true without rank restrictions as
stated in Ex.
9.5
.

Proposition 9.8
.

For every
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})
, the entropy distance
d
is continuous at
\rho

if and only if its restriction
d|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}
is continuous at
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}(\rho)
.

Proof.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{M}_{n})
. By Lemma
9.6
a), the entropy distance
d

is continuous at
\rho
if and only if the rI-projection
\Pi
is continuous at

\rho
. The inference map

\textstyle\Psi^{\mathcal{A}}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\,,\quad\sigma\mapsto\argmax_{\eta\in\pi_{\mathcal{R}}^{-1}(\sigma)}S(\eta)

has the same values as
\Psi
, whose image
\Psi(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{R}))
is included in

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
by Ex.
9.3
a). Therefore,

\Pi=\Psi\circ\pi=\Psi\circ\pi_{\mathcal{R}}\circ\pi_{\mathcal{A}}

is continuous at
\rho
if and only if
\Psi^{\mathcal{A}}\circ\pi_{\mathcal{R}}\circ\pi_{\mathcal{A}}

is continuous at
\rho
, if and only if
\Pi^{\mathcal{A}}\circ\pi_{\mathcal{A}}
is
continuous at
\rho
, where

\Pi^{\mathcal{A}}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})\,,\quad\Pi^{\mathcal{A}}:=\Psi^{\mathcal{A}}\circ\pi_{\mathcal{R}}\,.

Since
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}
is open by Thm.
1.2
, it follows from
Lemma
8.1
b) that
\Pi^{\mathcal{A}}\circ\pi_{\mathcal{A}}
is continuous at

\rho
if and only if
\Pi^{\mathcal{A}}
is continuous at
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}(\rho)
. By
equation (
9.4
),

d|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}(\eta)=S(\Psi^{\mathcal{A}}\circ\pi_{\mathcal{R}}(\eta))-S(\eta)\,,\quad\eta\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})

holds. Hence,
\Pi^{\mathcal{A}}
is continuous at
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}(\rho)
if and only if

d|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})}
is continuous at
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}(\rho)
, again by Lemma 5.15 1)
in
[
53
]
.
∎

Example 9.9

(Entropy distance III)
