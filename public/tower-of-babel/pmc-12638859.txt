RNF115 upregulation by YBX1-dependent m5C modification promotes hepatocellular carcinoma progression
Authors: Jianhao Li, Han Chen, Lang Bai, Hong Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
5-methylcytosine (m5C) RNA modification plays critical roles in post-transcriptional regulation and cancer progression. However, the downstream effectors of the m5C reader protein YBX1 and their mechanistic contribution to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remain largely undefined. In this study, we performed transcriptome-wide m5C profiling and observed significant heterogeneity in m5C modification patterns in HCC, with differentially methylated genes broadly distributed across the genome except for chromosome Y. We found that YBX1 is markedly upregulated in HCC and significantly correlated with poor patient prognosis. Functionally, YBX1 promoted tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro and in vivo. By integrating m5C MeRIP-seq and YBX1 RIP-seq data, we identified RNF115 as a direct YBX1 target regulated in an m5C-dependent manner. Mechanistic experiments demonstrated that YBX1 binds to m5C-modified motifs within the 3’ untranslated region (3’UTR) of RNF115 mRNA, thereby enhancing its stability and translation and resulting in elevated RNF115 protein expression. Clinical cohort analyses revealed that RNF115 overexpression is associated with advanced tumor stage, increased recurrence and metastasis, and poor prognosis in HCC patients. Functional assays in vitro and in vivo confirmed that RNF115 is essential for HCC malignant phenotypes, and RNF115 overexpression rescues the inhibitory effects of YBX1 knockdown. Further mechanistic studies showed that the YBX1/m5C–RNF115 axis promotes HCC progression primarily through activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Collectively, our findings uncover a novel epitranscriptomic mechanism by which YBX1 drives HCC malignancy via m5C-dependent stabilization of RNF115, highlighting the YBX1/m5C–RNF115 axis as a promising diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target for HCC.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, and its incidence is projected to exceed one million new cases by 20251,2. Despite advances in surgical resection, locoregional treatments, and molecular targeted therapies, the long-term prognosis for HCC remains unsatisfactory. Although up to 30% of patients initially respond to the multi-kinase inhibitor sorafenib, acquired resistance typically develops within six months, resulting in a persistently poor 5-year survival rate3. These challenges underscore the urgent need to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying HCC pathogenesis and identify more effective therapeutic targets4,5.

Dynamic RNA modifications have emerged as critical regulators of post-transcriptional gene expression, impacting diverse physiological and pathological processes6. Among these modifications, 5-methylcytosine (m5C) is abundantly present in mRNA as well as various non-coding RNAs, including tRNA, rRNA, snRNA, and eRNA7–9. m5C modifications are mainly catalyzed by three types of proteins: methyltransferases (writers), demethylases (erasers), and reader proteins9. The NSUN family of methyltransferases, particularly NSUN2, exhibits elevated expression in multiple cancers, including HCC, contributing to increased m5C levels and promoting tumor progression and drug resistance10. Erasers, such as the TET family of enzymes, mediate RNA demethylation, thereby rendering the modification reversible11. Reader proteins, including ALYREF and YBX1, recognize and bind to m5C sites, critically influencing processes such as mRNA export, stability, and translation initiation11. YBX1, in particular, has been shown to target several oncogenes with m5C modifications, such as HDGF12, CHD313, HGH114, ENO115 and SMOX16, thereby enhancing their stability and contributing to cancer progression. Research indicates that YBX1 recognizes 60% of mRNAs modified by m5C12. While YBX1’s oncogenic role in HCC has been confirmed, its specific function in targeting m5C-modified mRNAs in HCC remains inadequately understood17.

In this study, we performed a comprehensive analysis of m5C modification patterns in HCC and observed distinct methylation profiles, with differentially methylated genes distributed across all chromosomes except the Y chromosome. We further demonstrated that YBX1 is markedly upregulated in HCC and functionally promotes malignant progression both in vitro and in vivo. Through integrative multi-omics and mechanistic studies, we identified RNF115 as a direct YBX1 target, regulated via m5C-dependent stabilization of its mRNA. Clinical cohort analyses and in vitro/in vivo experiments confirmed that RNF115 is overexpressed in HCC and is essential for tumor growth and metastasis. Rescue assays further showed that RNF115 overexpression partially reversed the suppressive effects of YBX1 knockdown. Mechanistically, we revealed that the YBX1/m5C-RNF115 axis drives HCC progression primarily by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Collectively, our findings uncover a novel epitranscriptomic mechanism underlying HCC malignancy and highlight the YBX1/m5C-RNF115 signaling axis as a promising diagnostic and therapeutic target.

Results

The landscape of genetic characteristics of m5C modification in HCC

To explore the characteristics of m5C modification in HCC, we systematically analyzed the differences of m5C modifications in HCC mRNA between tumor and adjacent tissues based on RNA-BisSeq data. Our analysis revealed significant heterogeneity in m5C modification profiles between HCC and adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1A). Chromosomal mapping demonstrated that genes exhibiting differential m5C modification were broadly distributed across all chromosomes except the Y chromosome, with chromosomes 1 and 19 harboring the greatest number of differentially modified genes (Fig. 1B). Analysis of m5C-related regulatory factors showed that both mRNA and protein levels of DNMT3B, NSUN2, NSUN4, YBX1, NOP2, DNMT3A, DNMT1, ALYREF, and NSUN5 were significantly upregulated in HCC tissues, whereas NSUN6 expression was markedly decreased (Fig. 1C). Somatic mutation assessment indicated that mutations in the 16 m5C regulators were infrequent in HCC (7.85%) (Fig. S1A). Their chromosomal locations are illustrated in Fig. S1B. CNV analysis identified significant amplification of ALYREF, NSUN2, DNMT3B, and NSUN5 in both HCC and pan-cancer contexts (Fig. S1C and D). Furthermore, YBX1 expression was elevated in advanced-stage tumors, and its high expression predicted poorer overall survival in both TCGA and ICGC cohorts (Fig. 1D and E). Upregulation of YBX1 significantly promoted HCC cell proliferation, DNA synthesis, and colony formation, whereas YBX1-silencing exerted the opposite effect (Fig. 2D–I). Migration and invasion assays further demonstrated that silencing YBX1 markedly impaired, while ectopic expression enhanced, the migratory and invasive capacities of HCC cells (Fig. 2J–M). YBX1 enhances RNF115 mRNA stability and translational efficiency via m5C modification

To identify YBX1 downstream targets regulated via m5C, we employed a multi-omics strategy combining m5C MeRIP-seq, m5C RNA-BisSeq, YBX1 RIP-seq, and CRISPR-mediated YBX1 knockout datasets (Fig. 3A). Intersection analysis yielded seven candidate genes, among which RNF115 was found to be significantly overexpressed at both mRNA and protein levels in HCC (Fig. 3B). Correlation analysis of bulk and single-cell RNA-seq data showed that RNF115 expression positively correlated with YBX1 and NSUN2 in HCC (Fig. S2 A–F). Fluorescent immunostaining of HCC tissue microarrays further validated a strong positive correlation between YBX1 and RNF115 protein expression (Fig. 3C and D). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses demonstrated that both YBX1 and RNF115 are independent prognostic risk factors for HCC (Fig. S3A). Moreover, ROC curve analysis showed that YBX1 and RNF115 possess high diagnostic accuracy for HCC across multiple datasets (Fig. S3B–I). Similarly, NSUN2 expression was significantly elevated in advanced TNM stages (III and IV) compared to early stages (I and II) and consistently associated with worse overall survival across multiple independent cohorts (Fig. S3J–M), highlighting the clinical significance of key players in this m5C regulatory axis. Furthermore, we observed that the silencing of YBX1 or NSUN2 led to a substantial decrease in RNF115 expression. Conversely, ectopic expression of either YBX1 or NSUN2 resulted in a significant increase in RNF115 levels, as evidenced by western blot analysis (Fig. 3E and G). Moreover, in HCC cells, the YBX1-MT and NSUN2-MT mutants increased RNF115 expression levels, whereas the YBX1-WT and NSUN2-DM did not (Fig. 3F and H). Given YBX1’s known role in mRNA stabilization, we assessed its impact on RNF115 mRNA stability. As anticipated, actinomycin D treatment revealed that RNF115 transcripts had shorter half-lives in YBX1 knockdown cells, while YBX1 overexpression prolonged transcript stability (Fig. 3I). Then, RNC-qPCR analysis in HCC cell lines showed that YBX1 knockdown reduced the translation efficiency of RNF115 (Fig. S4A). Moreover, wild-type YBX1 (YBX1-WT), but not the mutant form (YBX1-MT), was able to enhance RNF115 translation (Fig. S4B). These findings further confirm that YBX1 enhances RNF115 expression by stabilizing its mRNA. We further utilized the m5C Finder and RBPDB databases to identify m5C methylation sites on RNF115 mRNA that potentially bind to YBX1. The results revealed three potential YBX1-binding m5C sites located in the 3’UTR of the RNF115 transcript (Fig. 3J). We constructed luciferase reporter plasmids containing the wild-type RNF115 sequence (RNF115-WT), an m5C site-mutant version (RNF115-MT), and several single-point-mutated RNF115 variants (Fig. 3K). Knockdown of YBX1 significantly decreased luciferase activity in both the RNF115-WT and single-point-mutant groups, but had no effect on the triple-point-mutated RNF115-MT group (Figs. 3L and S4C–F). In addition, RIP-qPCR analysis confirmed that YBX1 protein directly binds to RNF115 mRNA, but not to the RNF115-MT mRNA, suggesting that the m5C modification site is critical for YBX1–RNF115 mRNA interaction (Fig. S4G–H). Furthermore, mRNA stability assays revealed that YBX1 knockdown did not significantly affect the decay rate of RNF115-MT transcripts, indicating that the absence of the m5C modification abolishes YBX1-mediated regulation of transcript stability (Fig. S4I). RNF115 overexpression in HCC and its association with poor prognosis

To investigate the clinical significance of RNF115 in HCC, we analyzed RNF115 expression in the HX cohort using IHC. The findings demonstrated a significant overexpression of RNF115 in HCC tissues relative to adjacent non-tumor tissues. (Fig. 4A and B). Additionally, high RNF115 expression was closely associated with clinical malignant progression of HCC, including advanced BCLC stage (Fig. 4C and D), larger tumor size (≥7 cm), elevated PIVKA-II levels, recurrence, and metastasis (Fig. 4C–F, Table S4). Kaplan–Meier analysis further revealed that high RNF115 levels were associated with lower overall survival rates in HCC patients (Fig. 4G–I). Moreover, univariate and multivariate Cox analyses identified RNF115 expression level and BCLC stage as independent risk factors for the prognosis of HCC (Table S5). Knocking down RNF115 suppresses HCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in both in vitro and in vivo models

To investigate the function of RNF115, we designed and constructed three shRNAs targeting RNF115, which were transfected into Hep3B and Huh7 cells. Western blot analysis showed that shRNF115-2 significantly inhibited RNF115 protein expression in HCC cells, leading to its selection for further functional assays (Fig. 5A and B). CCK-8, EdU staining, and colony formation assays showed that RNF115 knockdown significantly inhibited HCC cell proliferation, DNA synthesis, and colony formation compared to cells transfected with the NC control (Fig. 5C–E). Furthermore, silencing RNF115 reduced the migration and invasion abilities of HCC cells (Fig. 5F and G). Following RNF115 knockdown, the expression levels of mesenchymal markers associated with epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), including N-cadherin, Vimentin, and MMP2, were significantly reduced, while the epithelial marker E-cadherin was upregulated (Fig. 5H). To evaluate the oncogenic role of RNF115 in vivo, we established a xenograft mouse model by subcutaneously injecting HCC cells infected with either shRNF115 or shNC. Consistent with the in vitro findings, the tumor growth rate and average tumor weight in the RNF115 knockdown group were significantly lower than those in the shNC group (Fig. 5I). Furthermore, IHC analysis indicated that tumors from the RNF115 knockdown group displayed weaker Ki-67 staining compared to the shNC group (Fig. 5J). YBX1 promotes HCC malignant phenotypes via RNF115

Based on our results showing that YBX1 facilitates the mRNA stabilization of RNF115 in an m5C-dependent manner, we proposed that the YBX1/RNF115 pathway is implicated in the progression of HCC. To test this hypothesis, we performed rescue assays and discovered that reintroducing RNF115 partially restored the proliferation, DNA synthesis, colony formation, migration, and invasion capabilities of YBX1-deficient HCC cells (Fig. 6A–F). Further in vivo subcutaneous xenograft experiments demonstrated that overexpression of RNF115 could partially recover the proliferative capacity of YBX1-deficient HCC cells (Fig. 7A–C). IHC analysis confirmed that the expression of Ki-67, which was diminished in the YBX1 knockdown group, was also partially restored by RNF115 overexpression (Fig. 7D–F). YBX1/m5C-RNF115 axis promotes HCC progression via activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the tumor-promoting role of the YBX1/m5C-RNF115 axis in HCC, we performed a series of exploratory analyses. GSVA revealed that high expression of both YBX1 and RNF115 was associated with the activation of multiple oncogenic pathways, including the unfolded protein response, G2/M checkpoint, MYC target gene activation, DNA repair, and PI3K/AKT signaling. Among these, the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway was of particular interest due to its well-documented role in HCC progression and therapeutic potential (Fig. 8A and B). We therefore selected this pathway for further investigation. Consistently, GSEA demonstrated that the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway was significantly enriched in HCC tissues with high YBX1 and RNF115 expression (Fig. 8C). GSEA based on multiple independent HCC datasets demonstrated that the PI3K/AKT pathway was significantly enriched in HCC tissues compared to non-tumor liver tissues (Fig. S5A–D). These findings align with previous studies reporting that aberrant activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway plays a pivotal role in the initiation and progression of hepatocellular carcinoma and is strongly associated with poor clinical outcomes. Therefore, targeting the PI3K/AKT pathway may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for HCC patients18,19. Further validation showed that YBX1 knockdown caused a reduction in the expression levels of p-AKT and p-PI3K. Additionally, the inhibitory effects on p-AKT and p-PI3K were reversed by reconstituting RNF115 (Fig. 8D). To further investigate whether the YBX1/m5C-RNF115 axis mediated activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway contributes to HCC cell proliferation, we employed MK-2206, a selective inhibitor of AKT phosphorylation20. The data revealed that MK-2206 effectively suppressed the proliferation ability induced by RNF115 overexpression (Fig. S6A–C). Discussion

Aberrant m5C modification has garnered increasing attention due to its emerging relevance in disease pathogenesis; however, the precise biological functions and underlying mechanisms of m5C-modified mRNAs in HCC remain insufficiently elucidated21. Here, we systematically characterized the role of the m5C reader YBX1 in HCC cell proliferation and metastasis. Further investigation revealed that YBX1 stabilizes RNF115 mRNA by recognizing m5C modification, thereby promoting its expression. RNF115 is significantly overexpressed in HCC, with high levels correlating strongly with poor prognosis. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that the YBX1/m5C-RNF115 axis promotes HCC malignancy, likely via activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway (Fig. 8E). Collectively, our study not only identifies a novel prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target for HCC but also provides fresh insights into the epitranscriptomic regulation of liver cancer, offering promising avenues for the development of targeted therapies.

Consistent with previous reports, we found that the levels of m5C modification exhibit marked heterogeneity in HCC tissues10. Our chromosomal localization analysis further revealed that genes with altered m5C modifications are broadly distributed across all chromosomes except the Y chromosome. It has been reported that the Y chromosome harbors only 78 protein-coding genes and exhibits relatively low transcriptional activity22,23. Moreover, increasing evidence suggests that the Y chromosome undergoes various degrees of transcriptional silencing in multiple cancer types, including HCC24. Interestingly, data from the HCCDB database also failed to detect significant differential expression of Y-linked genes in HCC tissues25. These observations collectively raise the intriguing possibility that the transcriptional silencing of the Y chromosome may underlie the observed reduction in m5C modification of Y chromosome-related genes in HCC. This hypothesis merits further exploration to unravel the epigenetic mechanisms underlying Y chromosome inactivation in HCC.

Research by Chen et al. demonstrated that up to 60% of m5C-modified mRNAs are recognized by YBX1, mediating diverse functional outcomes12. YBX1 has also been implicated in tumor progression, such as in bladder cancer, by stabilizing oncogenes like HDGF12. Consistent with these findings, we observed a pronounced upregulation of YBX1 at both transcript and protein levels in HCC, which was robustly associated with advanced disease stage and poor clinical outcome. Functional studies demonstrated that YBX1 knockdown suppresses, while its overexpression enhances, HCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. These results indicate that YBX1 may contribute to HCC progression via m5C-mediated regulation of downstream target gene expression.

To systematically identify mRNAs regulated by YBX1 through m5C-dependent mechanisms, we integrated multiple public high-throughput datasets. We utilized m5C MeRIP-seq to identify genes with m5C modifications, screened m5C RNA-BisSeq data for genes with differential m5C modification levels between HCC and adjacent normal tissues, and employed YBX1 RIP-seq to select YBX1-specific binding genes. Additionally, we identified YBX1 knockout targets by selecting genes whose expression significantly decreased following YBX1 CRISPR knockout. Based on expression differences in HCC and adjacent tissues, we chose RNF115 for further validation. Through a series of experiments and analyses. Through comprehensive experimental validation, we confirmed that YBX1 interacts with m5C-modified sites in the 3’-UTR of RNF115 mRNA, thereby enhancing its stability and translational efficiency in HCC cells. These findings reveal that RNF115 acts as a YBX1 target in HCC via m5C modification.

RNF115, also known as breast cancer-associated gene 2 (BCA2), has been predominantly studied for its oncogenic roles in lung adenocarcinoma26, gastric cancer27, breast cancer28, and thyroid cancer29. However, its function as a YBX1 target via m5C modification in HCC remains unclear. Unlike SMOX, which drives tumorigenesis via polyamine metabolism16, HDGF, which promotes angiogenesis and proliferation through growth factor signaling12, CHD3, which regulates transcription via chromatin remodeling13, and ENO1, which enhances glycolysis15, RNF115 functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase that directly modulates protein homeostasis through the ubiquitin-proteasome system27. This unique versatility enables RNF115 to serve as a critical integrator linking post-transcriptional RNA modifications to protein homeostasis, cellular signaling, DNA repair, and immune regulation30. YBX1, as a multifunctional RNA-binding protein, is known to participate in key cancer-related processes, including proteostasis, stress responses, DNA damage repair, and activation of transcription factors such as MYC31. The co-overexpression of YBX1 and RNF115 suggests a synergistic regulatory axis, potentially orchestrating multiple cancer hallmarks such as sustained proliferative signaling, resistance to apoptosis, genomic instability, and disruption of proteostasis. Given the broader regulatory scope and central positioning of RNF115 within this axis, it likely plays a more integrative role in tumor progression compared to other YBX1 targets.

In the present study, we demonstrated that RNF115 is not only highly expressed in HCC but also closely associated with advanced stage and poor survival. Functional experiments, both in vitro and in vivo, revealed that RNF115 silencing markedly inhibited the proliferation and metastasis of HCC cells. Importantly, restoration of RNF115 expression was able to partially rescue the defects in proliferation and invasion caused by YBX1 knockdown, indicating that RNF115 acts as a crucial downstream effector mediating the oncogenic effects of YBX1 in HCC. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that other YBX1 target genes may also contribute to tumor progression. Therefore, further studies are warranted to delineate the comprehensive regulatory network governed by YBX1 and to identify additional downstream effectors involved in HCC pathogenesis. Moreover, advanced experimental models, such as orthotopic liver cancer models and patient-derived xenograft models, as well as investigations into how the YBX1/m5C-RNF115 axis influences sensitivity to sorafenib, will be essential for facilitating the clinical translation of our findings.

Mechanistically, our data suggest that the YBX1/m5C-RNF115 axis may be involved in the activation of several oncogenic pathways, including the unfolded protein response, G2/M checkpoint regulation, MYC targets, DNA repair, and PI3K/AKT signaling. Among these, the PI3K/AKT pathway attracted particular attention, as it is aberrantly activated in approximately 50% of HCC patients and is closely associated with poor prognosis and resistance to sorafenib19,32. Our experimental results confirmed that the YBX1/m5C-RNF115 axis directly regulates the phosphorylation status of PI3K and AKT. Moreover, rescue experiments with the AKT phosphorylation inhibitor MK-2206 demonstrated that inhibition of AKT signaling effectively abrogated the pro-proliferative effects induced by RNF115 overexpression. These findings further support the notion that the YBX1/m5C-RNF115 axis promotes HCC progression at least in part through modulation of the PI3K/AKT pathway. Literature indicates that combination strategies targeting the PI3K/AKT pathway represent a promising approach to overcoming drug resistance and toxicity33. Our study provides preliminary evidence of a synergistic regulatory mechanism among RNA methylation, ubiquitination, and the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, offering new theoretical insights and potential avenues for the development of future combination targeted therapies. While our study focused on the PI3K/AKT pathway, RNF115, as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, likely modulates multiple oncogenic pathways. Previous studies showed that RNF115 activates Wnt/β-catenin signaling through APC ubiquitination in lung cancer, and other RNF family members regulate MAPK signaling in various cancers34–37. These potential multi-pathway effects may contribute to RNF115’s potent oncogenic activity in HCC. Future ubiquitinome profiling studies will be essential to fully characterize the signaling network regulated by the YBX1/m5C-RNF115 axis, potentially revealing additional therapeutic targets for combination strategies.

In conclusion, our study reveals that the YBX1/m5C-RNF115 axis is a crucial driver of HCC progression via activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Our findings underscore the central role of this regulatory axis in HCC pathogenesis and highlight its promise as a therapeutic target. Targeting the YBX1/m5C-RNF115 axis, either alone or in conjunction with PI3K/AKT pathway inhibitors, offers a compelling strategy for the development of more effective therapies against HCC.

Methods

Data source and preprocessing

Bulk RNA-seq, proteomics sequencing data, and YBX1 CRISPR RNA-seq datasets were systematically collected from public repositories, including The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)38, the International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC)39, and The National Omics Data Encyclopedia (NODE)40,41. To ensure cross-cohort comparability, all transcriptomic data were normalized using transcripts per million (TPM). Somatic mutation profiles were analyzed using the R package ‘maftools’. The hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) single-cell RNA sequencing dataset (GSE149614) was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO)42. RNA bisulfite sequencing (RNA-BisSeq) data were sourced from Song et al.10. RNA-BisSeq enables single-nucleotide resolution mapping of m5C modifications via bisulfite conversion of RNA molecules. The m5C methylation level at each site was defined as the methylation ratio, calculated as the proportion of non-converted cytosines to the total sequencing depth. All experimental protocols, data processing workflows, and quality control measures followed the guidelines described in the original studies. To analyze global m5C dynamics, we systematically compared methylation ratios at all detected sites between HCC tissues and paired adjacent normal tissues, aiming to elucidate global alterations in m5C modification patterns associated with HCC. Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-seq, GSE249045) and RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (RIP-seq, GSE264220) datasets were also retrieved from GEO16. Detailed cohort and sample information is summarized in Table S1.

Copy number variation (CNV) analysis

CNV data were obtained from GISTIC2-processed TCGA Pan-Cancer Atlas results43. Genes related to m5C regulation were selected for further analysis. CNV status was defined as gain for GISTIC scores >0.05 and loss for scores <−0.0544. Gain and loss frequencies were calculated per gene in TCGA-LIHC.

Somatic mutation analysis

Somatic mutation data of hepatocellular carcinoma (TCGA-LIHC) were downloaded using the TCGAbiolinks R package45. The mutation profiles of m5C-related genes were processed and visualized using the maftools package, including oncoplot representations46.

Chromosomal localization analysis

To visualize the genomic distribution of m5C regulators, we performed chromosomal mapping based on the human reference genome (hg38) using the RCircos R package to generate circular ideograms47. Linear chromosomal ideograms were further constructed using the RIdeogram package to show the distribution of genes with significantly altered m5C levels between tumor and adjacent tissues48. Log₂-fold changes (FCs) were color-coded, and the number of such genes per chromosome was overlaid as a line plot.

Clinical specimens

We collected 72 paired cancer and adjacent non-cancerous tissue samples between March 2018 and July 2019 at West China Hospital for the HX cohort. Tissue microarrays for 31 liver cancer samples were purchased from Superchip Biotech. The study complied with the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, with all protocols approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (approval number 2016-91).

Cell lines and cell culture

HCC cell lines Huh7, Hep3B, Huh6, and HepG2 were sourced from Procell Life Science & Technology (Wuhan, China). These cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) with added fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator.

Lentivirus infection and plasmid transfection

YBX1 knockdown (shYBX1) and overexpression (oe-YBX1) lentiviruses were developed by Hanheng Technology Corp (Shanghai, China), while RNF115 knockdown (shRNF115) and overexpression (oe-RNF115) lentiviruses were produced by Vigene Biosciences (Jinan, China). Lentiviral infections were conducted as previously reported49. The plasmids PCMV-YBX1-WT, PCMV-YBX1-Mut (W65A), pcDNA3.1-NSUN2-WT, and pcDNA3.1-NSUN2-Mut (C271/321A) were generously provided by Dr. Xiangyi Sun from the Department of Pharmacy at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University.

Western blot

Cell lysates were prepared using RIPA buffer (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). A detailed list of the primary antibodies used for Western blotting is provided in Table S2. Immune complexes were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence detection reagents (Beijing, China).

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescent

The staining procedures and scoring criteria for immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) were carried out as previously detailed. The primary antibodies used for IHC staining were the same as those employed in the aforementioned Western blotting. Images from the IHC staining were captured using an Olympus BX63 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Immunofluorescence tissue microarrays were analyzed with Aipathwell software (Servicebio, Wuhan, China), with relative expression calculated as the product of fluorescence intensity and positive area. Comprehensive information about the antibodies is provided in Table S2.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was measured using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, BIMAKE, Houston, TX, USA), following the procedures described previously50.

Transwell assay

The transwell assay was conducted as previously described. Three random fields of view were selected and captured using an Olympus microscope (Japan)16.

Mouse xenograft model

NCG mice (NOD coisogenic, genetically engineered, immunodeficient) were procured from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). Huh7 cells (1 × 10⁶), stably transfected with lentivirus, were suspended in 100 µL of a 1:1 DMEM–Matrigel mixture and then subcutaneously injected into 5–6-week-old NCG mice. The remaining experimental steps were conducted as previously described50.

RNA stability assay

To assess the half-life of RNF115 mRNA, actinomycin D (5 µg/mL; Sigma, USA) was added to the cell culture medium. Cells were harvested at designated time points, and total RNA was isolated for RT-qPCR analysis.

Ribosome nascent-chain complex (RNC)-mRNA extraction

Cells were pretreated with 0.1 mg/mL cycloheximide (CHX; MCE, USA) at 37 °C for 15 min to stabilize ribosome–mRNA complexes. After treatment, cells were gently scraped from the culture dishes and lysed in ice-cold RB buffer. The lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min to ensure complete lysis and then transferred to pre-chilled 2 mL microcentrifuge tubes. After centrifugation at 16,200 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove insoluble debris, the clarified supernatants were carefully layered onto 20 mL of sucrose cushion buffer (30% sucrose in RB buffer) and subjected to ultracentrifugation at 185,000 × g for 5 h at 4 °C. The resulting ribosome–nascent chain complexes were collected as pellets, and RNC-associated mRNAs were extracted using TRIzol reagent. The purified RNC-mRNAs were subsequently subjected to RT-qPCR analysis.

RNA binding protein immunoprecipitation (RIP)

RIP assay was performed using the RIP Kit (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Briefly, cell lysates were incubated with specific antibodies against YTHDF1 and IgG. Then, RNA-protein immunoprecipitation complexes were subjected to six washes, followed by incubation in a digestion buffer containing proteinase K to degrade proteins. Finally, RNA was isolated using phenol-chloroform extraction and further purified before being analyzed by RT-qPCR.

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

RNA was extracted from cells and mouse epidermal tissues using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, NY, USA) and converted to cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, NY, USA). The PCR primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) (Table S3). The experiments were analyzed by the Roche LightCycler 480Ⅱ (Roche, BSL, Switzerland). The data were calibrated by the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Reporter gene assays

RNF115-WT and RNF115-MT (C1438/2008/2047A) luciferase reporter plasmids were synthesized and inserted into the GV272 vector by Genechem (Shanghai, China). Cells were seeded in six-well plates and transfected with 2 µg of the luciferase reporter plasmid and 200 ng of the TK Renilla luciferase reporter. Luciferase activity in the cell lysates was measured using the Dual-Glo® Luciferase Assay System (Promega).

Correlation analysis

The single-cell RNA-seq dataset GSE149614 was retrieved from the GEO database. Given our focus on the intrinsic progression mechanisms of HCC, we specifically analyzed malignant hepatocytes from tumor tissue samples. These malignant hepatocytes were identified and extracted according to the established annotation criteria described in the original study39. Following cell extraction, dimensionality reduction was performed using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) to visualize the cellular heterogeneity. YBX1 and RNF115 expression levels were quantified within the malignant hepatocyte population, and correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate their co-expression patterns. Statistical significance of the correlation coefficient was determined to establish the relationship between YBX1 and RNF115 expression specifically in HCC tumor cells. In addition, pan-cancer and HCC-specific correlation analyses were conducted using the GPSAdb platform (https://www.gpsadb.com/)51.

Pathway enrichment analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed using the clusterProfiler R package to identify relevant biological pathways. To further explore functional differences between groups, we performed gene set variation analysis (GSVA) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), using the “h.all.v2022.1.Hs.symbols.gmt” gene set from the MSigDB database, implemented via the GSVA and clusterProfiler R packages52–54.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.3). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons between two groups, a two-sided unpaired Student’s t-test was used. For multiple group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was performed. Survival analysis was carried out using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. The p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using appropriate methods where applicable. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels were denoted as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.
