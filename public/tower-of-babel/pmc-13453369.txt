A Qualitative Exploration of the Barriers and Facilitators for Physical Activity Among Pregnant and Postpartum Women in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Wafa Alahmari, Murali Perumbakkam Subramanian, Amani Alhazmi, Samiah Alqabbani, Madawi Alotaibi, Afrah Almuwais
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhysical activity is vital for the health of mothers and their newborns during pregnancy and postpartum. However, many pregnant women do not meet recommended activity levels.AimThis study investigates the barriers and facilitators affecting physical activity in pregnant and postpartum women in Saudi Arabia to inform targeted interventions.MethodsA qualitative descriptive approach guided this study. Twenty participants were recruited through purposive convenience sampling from antenatal and postnatal clinics at Alyamamh Hospital in Riyadh until data saturation was achieved. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews lasting 40 to 50 minutes, focusing on experiences, barriers, and motivators related to physical activity. The interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using NVivo 20 software via thematic analysis. The research team consisted of rehabilitation sciences professionals who engaged in reflexive practices throughout data collection and analysis.FindingsParticipants had diverse socioeconomic backgrounds, with ages ranging from 23 to 39. Pregnant participants were between 9 and 36 weeks of gestational age, while postpartum participants were within four weeks of delivery. Five key themes emerged: Navigating Physical and Emotional Barriers, Motivated by Health and Well-being, Misinformation and Missed Opportunities, Safe and Practical Choices, and Broader Implications.Discussion and ConclusionsThis study examines the challenges and facilitators of physical activity among pregnant and postpartum women in Saudi Arabia. Culturally relevant interventions and improved healthcare support can enhance maternal and neonatal health and promote physical activity during these critical periods.

Introduction

Engaging in physical activity during pregnancy and postpartum significantly improves maternal and neonatal health. Substantial evidence shows that maintaining an active lifestyle during this crucial period enhances glucose regulation, reduces the risk of gestational diabetes, improves cardiovascular health, and optimizes birth outcomes.1–3 For postpartum women, regular physical activity accelerates recovery, supports weight management, and improves mental well-being, including lowering the risk of postpartum depression.2,4

Despite these well-documented benefits, most pregnant women fail to meet recommended physical activity guidelines. Studies report alarmingly low adherence rates worldwide, including among Saudi women.5–7 In Saudi Arabia, cultural, societal, and environmental barriers further discourage physical activity, contributing to high levels of sedentary behavior among women.8,9 Traditional gender roles, modesty norms, and the scarcity of gender-segregated exercise facilities exacerbate this problem.10

At the same time, facilitators like home-based exercise programs and increased awareness of physical activity’s benefits are emerging as practical solutions. The wider literature underscores the importance of culturally tailored interventions in overcoming these barriers for Saudi women.11,12 Culturally sensitive and practical, home-based exercise programs now serve as an effective option, particularly in contexts where public modesty prioritizes privacy.13 Research shows that many Saudi women prefer exercising at home, and this method effectively addresses their cultural and logistical constraints.10,13 Awareness of the health benefits associated with physical activity during pregnancy continues to grow among Saudi women, and this trend positively influences their participation in such activities. Studies reveal that women who understand how exercise impacts their health and that of their children tend to remain active.14,15

Social support networks actively enhance participation in physical activity by providing motivation and reducing barriers. Although pre-pregnancy activity levels influence the barriers and facilitators of physical activity,16 there is no substantial evidence examining these dynamics specifically among pregnant and postpartum women in Saudi Arabia. Exploring these factors remains crucial for developing targeted interventions that promote physical activity within this demographic. This study investigates the barriers and facilitators influencing physical activity among pregnant and postpartum women in Saudi Arabia. By identifying and addressing these factors, the research aims to support the development of targeted interventions that improve maternal and child health outcomes. A qualitative approach was selected because it is particularly suited to exploring subjective experiences, attitudes, and sociocultural determinants of health behavior, and is recommended for understudied populations where quantitative measures may not capture the full depth of lived experience. This study adopted a qualitative descriptive methodology, which seeks to provide a comprehensive summary of participants’ experiences while remaining close to their own accounts.17 The study was informed by a constructionist orientation, which assumes that meanings and understandings are socially constructed through individual experiences and interactions, and therefore aims to describe participants’ perspectives without imposing higher-order theoretical interpretation.17,18

Methods

Participants were recruited using convenience sampling from antenatal and postnatal clinics at Alyamamh Hospital in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Recruitment ceased upon data saturation, with a total of 20 participants included. The sample encompassed a diverse range of ages, educational backgrounds, and reproductive histories. A sample of 20 participants is consistent with published guidance indicating that qualitative descriptive studies typically achieve data saturation within sample sizes ranging from approximately 12 to 30 participants, depending on study scope and participant heterogeneity. Data saturation was assessed iteratively during analysis and confirmed following the 18th interview, with two additional interviews conducted to verify saturation, defined as the point at which no new codes or themes emerged across consecutive interviews.

Data were gathered through semi-structured interviews lasting 40–50 minutes. The interview guide (Appendix Figure 1) comprised open-ended questions tailored to explore individual experiences, barriers, and motivators related to physical activity. Interviews were conducted in private, comfortable settings to encourage open dialogue. The interview guide was developed by the research team following a systematic review of the relevant literature and in consultation with a qualitative research expert; it was piloted with two participants (whose data were excluded from the final analysis), with minor wording adjustments made to improve clarity and flow. All interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ prior written consent. Verbatim transcription was performed by a trained research assistant, and transcripts were cross-checked against audio recordings by a second team member to ensure accuracy; discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Princess Nourah bint Abdulrahman University (24–0587), which complies with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was secured from all participants, including publication of anonymized direct quotes and emphasizing their right to withdraw at any point without repercussions. Data confidentiality and participant anonymity were strictly maintained throughout the study.

Interviews were transcribed verbatim and analyzed using NVivo 20 software. A qualitative descriptive methodology was employed to investigate barriers and facilitators to physical activity among women during pregnancy and postpartum in Saudi Arabia. While being inductive and dynamic, this methodology does not involve deep theorization or provide any theoretical view. However, it allows for an in-depth understanding of the participants’ perspectives and experiences, particularly in understudied populations.17 The findings were grouped into broader themes based on constructionism, which describes the perspectives of the participants rather than explaining them. Initial open coding was conducted independently by two researchers, followed by iterative discussions to resolve discrepancies and reach consensus on code categories and emerging themes. In keeping with Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis framework, agreement was achieved through collaborative discussion and consensus rather than numerical inter-rater metrics. To ensure research transparency, all researchers engaged in reflexive bracketing discussions before data collection and maintained reflective memos throughout the analytical process, acknowledging their backgrounds as health professionals in rehabilitation sciences and the potential influence of this on data interpretation.

Researcher Reflexivity

All members of the research team are health professionals in rehabilitation sciences with clinical experience in maternity care settings. Awareness of this positionality is important, as professional backgrounds and clinical assumptions may influence data interpretation. To mitigate such influence, the research team engaged in systematic bracketing discussions prior to data collection, in which each researcher explicitly articulated their preconceptions and expectations regarding women’s physical activity during the perinatal period. Reflective memos were maintained individually throughout the data collection and analytical processes, documenting ongoing reflexive engagement with the data. These reflexive practices encouraged continual examination of how researchers’ assumptions, professional experiences, and disciplinary perspectives might shape data interpretation and theme development. These measures were adopted to strengthen the credibility, dependability, and transferability of the findings, in accordance with Lincoln and Guba’s criteria for trustworthiness in qualitative research.19

Findings

A total of twenty pregnant and postpartum women were recruited from Alyamamh Hospital in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. This diverse sample reflected various socioeconomic backgrounds, providing valuable insights into the multifaceted experiences of women during pregnancy and the postpartum period. Pregnant participants ranged in gestational age from 9 weeks (early stages) to 36 weeks (late third trimester). Postpartum participants were within the first four weeks after childbirth.

The participants represented diverse reproductive histories. Some were first-time mothers, while others had experienced up to four previous births. Participant Characteristics

Participants’ age range was between 23 to 39 years. Pregnant women were generally older, with the majority aged 30–36 years, while postpartum participants were mostly aged 25–31 years. The focus was on the immediate postnatal period, as postpartum women were all within the first four weeks after delivery. Pregnancy histories varied, with some women experiencing their first pregnancy and others having up to four previous births. The average number of previous births among postpartum participants was approximately 2.8. Educational attainment showed differences between the two groups: bachelor’s degrees were common in both, but postpartum women were more likely to have completed only high school. Five major themes emerged from the analysis. Notably, the sample included both primiparous and multiparous women, representing diverse reproductive experiences. The study was not designed to compare experiences according to parity or gestational stage; this limitation is further discussed in the Limitations section.

Five major themes emerged from the thematic analysis: (1) Navigating Physical and Emotional Barriers, (2) Motivated by Health and Well-being, (3) Misinformation and Missed Opportunities, (4) Safe and Practical Choices, and (5) Broader Implications. Theme 1: Navigating Physical and Emotional Barriers

This theme captures the physical discomforts, emotional fears, and practical burdens that constrained women’s engagement in physical activity during pregnancy and the postpartum period. The barriers identified were closely interconnected and formed a consistent pattern across participants’ accounts. Concerns about exercise safety frequently emerged, particularly during pregnancy, where women expressed fears that physical activity could negatively affect fetal well-being or pregnancy outcomes. I feel like it could cause a miscarriage, especially in the early months. (Participant 5)I’m worried about premature delivery or the baby’s position being affected. (Participant 14)

I feel like it could cause a miscarriage, especially in the early months. (Participant 5)

I’m worried about premature delivery or the baby’s position being affected. (Participant 14)

Alongside these concerns, physical discomfort was commonly described as a significant obstacle to maintaining an active lifestyle. Pregnant participants reported musculoskeletal discomfort, including back and pelvic pain, whereas postpartum women emphasized the physical challenges associated with recovery, particularly following cesarean delivery. Back pain and pelvic pain make it hard to engage in any exercise. (Participant 13) The cesarean wound makes it impossible to do anything at first. (Participant 15)

Back pain and pelvic pain make it hard to engage in any exercise. (Participant 13)

The cesarean wound makes it impossible to do anything at first. (Participant 15)

Practical demands associated with caring for a newborn further restricted opportunities for physical activity during the postpartum period. Participants described difficulties balancing exercise with childcare responsibilities and household commitments. I don’t have time with all the baby’s needs and household chores. (Participant 16)

I don’t have time with all the baby’s needs and household chores. (Participant 16)

In addition to time constraints, fatigue and reduced motivation were frequently reported, further limiting women’s willingness and ability to engage in physical activity. I feel tired all the time, and getting started with exercise feels overwhelming. (Participant 6)

I feel tired all the time, and getting started with exercise feels overwhelming. (Participant 6)

Taken together, these findings illustrate how emotional concerns, physical limitations, and competing caregiving responsibilities interact to reduce engagement in physical activity during both pregnancy and the postpartum period. Rather than operating independently, these barriers often reinforced one another, creating a cumulative burden that constrained women’s participation in regular physical activity.

Theme 2: Motivated by Health and Well-Being

This theme reflects the perceived physical and psychological benefits that motivated women to engage in physical activity during the perinatal period. Two subthemes emerged, highlighting distinct motivational factors during pregnancy and postpartum.

A. Pregnancy Facilitators

Pregnant participants frequently viewed physical activity as a means of preparing for childbirth and maintaining overall physical and emotional well-being. Exercise was perceived as beneficial for improving flexibility, enhancing physical readiness for labour, and supporting a healthier pregnancy experience. Exercises like pelvic stretches help prepare me for labor and make it easier. (Participant 12) I want to maintain muscle flexibility and remain active for a smoother delivery. (Participant 8)

Exercises like pelvic stretches help prepare me for labor and make it easier. (Participant 12)

I want to maintain muscle flexibility and remain active for a smoother delivery. (Participant 8)

B. Postpartum Facilitators

In contrast, postpartum participants primarily associated physical activity with recovery, weight management, and improved psychological well-being. Exercise was often viewed as an important strategy for restoring physical fitness and supporting emotional health following childbirth. It helps me lose weight and boosts my self-esteem. (Participant 10) Exercise is crucial in preventing postpartum depression. (Participant 9)

It helps me lose weight and boosts my self-esteem. (Participant 10)

Exercise is crucial in preventing postpartum depression. (Participant 9)

Overall, participants perceived physical activity as offering benefits that extended beyond physical health alone. The desire to facilitate childbirth, recover after delivery, improve self-confidence, and protect mental well being emerged as powerful motivators encouraging participation in physical activity during the perinatal period.

This theme highlights the influence of misinformation, limited knowledge, and inadequate professional guidance on women’s physical activity behaviours. Participants frequently reported uncertainty regarding safe exercise practices and often relied on informal information sources when making decisions about physical activity. The findings suggest that the absence of clear, evidence-based information created uncertainty and reinforced misconceptions regarding physical activity. Participants consistently described missed opportunities for receiving guidance from healthcare professionals, highlighting the need for routine counselling and education throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period.

Theme 4: Safe and Practical Choices

This theme reflects women’s preference for physical activities perceived as safe, manageable, and compatible with the physical demands of pregnancy and postpartum recovery. Participants consistently favoured low impact forms of exercise that could be easily incorporated into daily routines. I walk because it feels safe and easy to do. Conversely, activities perceived as physically demanding or potentially harmful were often avoided. I avoid exercises that might strain my body, such as heavy lifting. (Participant 4) I don’t do anything like running or jumping — it’s too risky. (Participant 7)

These accounts indicate that perceptions of safety strongly influenced women’s exercise choices. Activities such as walking, stretching, and yoga were viewed as practical and appropriate options, suggesting that interventions promoting accessible, low-impact forms of physical activity may be particularly acceptable to women during the perinatal period.

Theme 5: Broader Implications

This theme captures the broader sociocultural and healthcare factors that shaped women’s experiences and decisions regarding physical activity. Two subthemes emerged: Cultural Norms and Family Influences, and Healthcare Gaps.

A. Cultural Norms and Family Influences

Participants described how cultural expectations and family beliefs frequently influenced attitudes toward physical activity during pregnancy and postpartum. While some women obtained information and encouragement through family and social networks, others reported receiving advice that discouraged exercise. I learned about exercises from friends and women in my family. (Participant 17) In our culture, people think pregnancy is a time to rest, not to move around much. (Participant 14) My family thinks I should avoid exercise because it might harm the baby. (Participant 11)

These findings suggest that family members and broader cultural norms play an important role in shaping women’s perceptions of physical activity, either by supporting participation or reinforcing restrictive beliefs.

B. Healthcare Gaps

Participants also identified substantial gaps in the guidance provided by healthcare professionals regarding physical activity during pregnancy and postpartum. There’s no program or plan provided for physical activity—it feels like I have to figure it out myself. (Participant 13) Even after delivery, I didn’t receive any advice on how to gradually start exercising. (Participant 16) If my doctor tells me it’s safe, I’ll do it. (Participant 18)

Notably, participants expressed a strong willingness to engage in physical activity when recommendations came from trusted healthcare professionals. These findings highlight the potential value of integrating structured physical activity counselling into routine antenatal and postnatal care, thereby providing women with the information and reassurance needed to engage confidently in safe physical activity.

Discussion

This study explored the barriers and facilitators influencing physical activity among pregnant and postpartum women in Saudi Arabia. The analysis identifies five central themes: managing physical and emotional challenges, motivations tied to health and well-being, gaps in information and guidance, preferences for safe and feasible exercise options, and broader societal and healthcare considerations. The findings reveal how personal, cultural, and systemic factors intertwine to shape physical activity behaviors during these critical life stages. Participants recognized the health benefits of exercise but faced significant barriers, such as physical discomfort, fear, time constraints, and inadequate guidance, that often limited their participation. The study highlights culturally relevant facilitators, including low-impact and home-based exercise options, as promising ways to promote physical activity in this population.

The findings align with global evidence that underscores how physical and emotional challenges—such as fatigue, back pain, pelvic discomfort, and fears of miscarriage or preterm labor—prevent women from engaging in physical activity during pregnancy and postpartum periods.16,20 In Saudi Arabia, cultural and environmental factors exacerbate these challenges. For example, modesty norms and a lack of gender-segregated exercise facilities discourage many women from participating in physical activities.10 A similar study conducted in the United Kingdom among active pregnant and postpartum women highlighted additional barriers, including pregnancy-related body changes and a lack of childcare options, which often made participation in physical activities challenging.21 These findings are further supported by qualitative evidence demonstrating that both previously active and inactive women experience dynamic changes in physical activity behavior during and after pregnancy, influenced by individual, social, and environmental factors, highlighting the need for tailored and sustained interventions across the perinatal period.22 This reinforces the importance of addressing logistical constraints alongside cultural and societal barriers. Critically, physical activity behaviors among the study participants appear to be shaped not only by individual motivation but also by broader sociocultural norms that define acceptable maternal roles in the Saudi context. Cultural expectations framing pregnancy as a period of rest, reinforced by familial advice and community attitudes, effectively constrain women’s agency even when personal motivation to exercise exists. This aligns with socioecological frameworks which emphasize that individual health behaviors are embedded within and constrained by interpersonal, institutional, and community-level factors.23

Participants frequently cited the perceived health benefits of physical activity as a strong motivator, echoing findings from previous studies. Many women viewed exercise as a way to ease the process of labor, recover more quickly during the postpartum period, and improve mental well-being,2,4 establishing the importance of integrating education on exercise’s health benefits into antenatal care thereby playing a critical role in increasing participation rates. This study also contributes new insights into the impact of misinformation and reliance on non-medical sources for exercise guidance. Although researchers have observed this issue globally, it becomes particularly pronounced in Saudi Arabia due to the scarcity of structured prenatal exercise programs.12 Women’s preference for low-impact activities, such as walking and yoga, underscores how perceived safety influences exercise choices during pregnancy, consistent with global trends.9 Furthermore, the observed gap between awareness of physical activity benefits and actual practice among participants should be understood not merely as an information deficit, but as evidence that knowledge alone is insufficient to change behavior when deeply held cultural expectations, familial norms, and informal information sources reinforce restriction. Effective behavior change interventions must therefore target the sociocultural environment alongside individual-level health literacy, incorporating social cognitive theory principles and addressing structural determinants of maternal physical activity.24,25

The study’s qualitative design stands out as a key strength, as it allowed for a detailed understanding of the lived experiences of pregnant and postpartum women. Including women from diverse educational and reproductive backgrounds enhances the relevance of the findings within the Saudi context. However, the study does have limitations. The sample size, while appropriate for qualitative research, may not fully represent the diverse experiences of women across Saudi Arabia’s regions. Additionally, conducting data collection in a single hospital may limit how well the findings apply to other healthcare settings. Self-reported data also introduce the potential for recall bias, particularly regarding physical activity behaviors. Furthermore, the gestational age of pregnant participants at the time of interview ranged from 9 to 36 weeks, which may have introduced variability in physical capacity and perspective; gestational age should therefore be considered a potential confounding factor in interpreting findings. The findings are context-specific to Saudi women receiving care at a single tertiary hospital in Riyadh and should not be generalized beyond similar sociocultural and healthcare contexts without further investigation. A further limitation is that the study was not designed to compare experiences according to parity (primiparous versus multiparous women) or gestational stage. Although participants represented diverse reproductive histories, the sample size and exploratory qualitative design did not permit robust subgroup analysis. Future studies should specifically examine how parity and stage of pregnancy influence perceptions of physical activity.26–28

The findings highlight an urgent need for culturally tailored interventions to promote physical activity among pregnant and postpartum women. Healthcare providers must receive training to offer personalized advice on safe and effective exercise practices during routine antenatal and postnatal care. Public health campaigns can utilize digital platforms to share accurate, evidence-based information and combat widespread misinformation. On a broader scale, policymakers must prioritize developing gender-segregated, climate-controlled recreational facilities to address environmental barriers.

Future research should focus on evaluating how effectively these interventions increase physical activity participation. Longitudinal studies could explore how physical activity behaviors evolve throughout pregnancy and postpartum periods. Researchers should also investigate how social support networks and technology-driven solutions, such as mobile health applications, might contribute to increasing physical activity levels. Importantly, future qualitative and mixed-methods studies should stratify analyses by parity (primiparous versus multiparous) and gestational trimester, as these factors likely shape women’s perspectives on physical activity in meaningfully different ways. Studies conducted in other regions of Saudi Arabia and in Muslim-majority populations with similar sociocultural contexts would also help to assess the transferability of these findings.

Conclusions

This study provides new insights into the complex challenges and enablers that shape physical activity behaviors among pregnant and postpartum women in Saudi Arabia. Despite the persistence of personal and cultural barriers, the findings highlight the potential of targeted, culturally appropriate interventions to overcome these obstacles and promote maternal and neonatal health. By improving healthcare guidance, enhancing infrastructure, and building community support, stakeholders can empower women to engage in physical activity during these critical life stages. Addressing these factors is essential not only for improving individual health outcomes but also for achieving broader public health objectives.
