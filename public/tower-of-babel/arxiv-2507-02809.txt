Block triangular preconditioning for inverse source problems in time-space fractional diffusion equations
Monoswini Majumdar, Stefano Serra-Capizzano, Rosita L. Sormani

Abstract
The current work investigates the effectiveness of block triangular preconditioners in accelerating and stabilizing the numerical solution of inverse source problems governed by time-space fractional diffusion equations (TSFDEs). We focus on the recovery of an unknown spatial source function in a multi-dimensional TSFDE, incorporating Caputo time-fractional derivatives and the fractional Laplacian. The inherent ill-posedness is addressed via a quasi-boundary value regularization, followed by a finite difference discretization that leads to large, structured linear systems. We develop and analyze a block triangular preconditioning strategy that mimics the coefficient matrix, while simplifying its structure for computational efficiency. Numerical experiments using the GMRES solver demonstrate that the proposed preconditioner significantly improve convergence rates, robustness, and accuracy, making it well-suited for large-scale, real-world inverse problems involving fractional modeling.



Keywords: Fractional diffusion equations, inverse problems, preconditioning, GMRES, quasi-boundary value regularization

Introduction

In the present study, we consider the inverse problem of identifying the unknown source term
f(\mathbf{x})
of the following multi-dimensional time-space fractional diffusion equation

\begin{cases}\gamma_{1}(\mathbf{x},t)D_{t}^{\beta}u(\mathbf{x},t)+\gamma_{2}(\mathbf{x},t)(-\Delta)^{\frac{\omega}{2}}u(\mathbf{x},t)=f(\mathbf{x})q(t),&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,\;t\in(0,T),\\
u(\mathbf{x},t)=0,&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega^{\mathsf{c}},\;t\in(0,T),\\
u(\mathbf{x},0)=\rho(\mathbf{x}),&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,\\
u(\mathbf{x},T)=\mu(\mathbf{x}),&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,\end{cases}

(1)

in which
T>0
is the upper bound of the time interval and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
d
a positive integer, is an open and bounded domain with piecewise smooth boundary, whose complement in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is denoted by
\Omega^{\mathsf{c}}
. In the main equation,
\gamma_{1}(\mathbf{x},t)
and
\gamma_{2}(\mathbf{x},t)
are continuous functions representing variable coefficients,
q(t)>0
is a time-dependent known term, and
\beta\in(0,1)
is the order of the left Caputo fractional derivative, which is defined as

D_{t}^{\beta}u(\mathbf{x},t):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{u_{\tau}(\mathbf{x},\tau)}{(t-\tau)^{\beta}}\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

where
\Gamma
is the Euler Gamma function and
u_{\tau}
indicates the partial derivative of
u(\mathbf{x},\tau)
with respect to
\tau
. Moreover, the fractional Laplacian of order
\omega\in(1,2)
is defined as the hypersingular integral

(-\Delta)^{\frac{\omega}{2}}u(\mathbf{x},t):=c_{\omega}\,\text{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{u(\mathbf{x},t)-u(\mathbf{y},t)}{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{d+\omega}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y},

(2)

where

c_{\omega}:=\frac{2^{\omega}\Gamma\left(\frac{\omega+d}{2}\right)}{\pi^{d/2}\left|\Gamma(-\omega/2)\right|}

is a normalization constant,
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}
is the Euclidean distance between the points
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
, and P.V. denotes the Cauchy principal value, which is necessary due to the singularity of the integrand at
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}
. Finally,
\rho(\mathbf{x})
and
\mu(\mathbf{x})
are given functions representing the initial condition and the final state observation, respectively. The unknowns in this system are the solution
u(\mathbf{x},t)
and the source function
f(\mathbf{x})
.

Time-Space Fractional Diffusion Equations (TSFDEs) have received significant attention in recent years, due to their effectiveness in modeling complex and anomalous diffusion processes across various scientific domains. TSFDEs incorporate fractional derivatives in both time and space, allowing them to overcome some limitations of classical diffusion equations and to capture long-range dependencies and memory effects observed in heterogeneous and disordered media.

On the computational front, the numerical discretization of TSFDEs often leads to large-scale, dense, and ill-conditioned linear systems, with high computational and memory demands that necessitate the development of efficient numerical solvers. Numerous high-accuracy discretization schemes have been developed to handle the intrinsic nonlocality of both Caputo and Riesz derivatives. In
[
4
]
it was shown that banded block triangular preconditioners, designed via Kronecker product splittings, are particularly efficient when the time-fractional order is near one. These preconditioners exploit the Toeplitz and Hessenberg structure of the discretized matrices and preserve their sparsity to an extent, enabling fast matrix-vector products. Further contributions in
[
8
]
introduced a two-step splitting iteration method and the corresponding preconditioner. Their theoretical analysis indicated that the preconditioned matrix can be expressed as a combination of an identity matrix, a low-rank matrix, and a small-norm matrix, effectively clustering the spectrum and improving the convergence of Krylov subspace methods such as the GMRES. These advancements have enabled TSFDEs to be effectively applied in fields such as geophysics, biomedical imaging, quantitative finance, and image processing.

One key computational challenge in this domain lies in solving inverse source problems, where the objective is to recover an unknown spatial source term from noisy final time observations. These problems are typically ill-posed, requiring the use of regularization techniques like the quasi-boundary value method to ensure stable solutions, particularly where source terms are recovered from noisy final-time data. To address these challenges, block preconditioning techniques have emerged as effective tools to enhance the convergence of iterative solvers, such as GMRES, used in the numerical solution of these systems. In
[
12
]
, a modified quasi-boundary regularization method was proposed. The authors constructed a matrix-based formulation with a block Toeplitz structure and employed efficient preconditioned solvers to obtain stable numerical solutions under data noise.

This paper investigates the role of block triangular preconditioners in accelerating and stabilizing the numerical solution of inverse source problems governed by TSFDEs, focusing on the multi-dimensional setting with variable coefficients. In the slightly simpler constant coefficient setting, this problem was studied in
[
10
]
, using block-diagonalizable preconditioners that mimick the coefficient matrix structure, and in
[
11
]
through a variational approach.

Here, we build on the foundation established in
[
10
]
to develop and analyze a block triangular preconditioning strategy for efficiently solving the inverse source problem (
1
), addressing the recovery of the unknown spatial source function
f(\mathbf{x})
from noisy terminal data. This mathematical model is ill-posed, therefore it is regularized using the quasi-boundary value method. Then, we discretize the regularized forward problem using a finite difference scheme, which leads to a large block linear system containing multi-level Toeplitz structures. A block triangular preconditioner is proposed, designed to mimic the structure of the system matrix but eliminating upper off-diagonal blocks for computational efficiency. The GMRES method is used to solve the linear system and the proposed preconditioners are shown to accelerate convergence significantly, confirming that block triangular preconditioning is a robust and efficient approach for solving this type of inverse source problems.

The paper is structured as follows. After briefly introducing some notation and preliminary definitions in Section
2
, in Section
3
we regularize the continuous ill-posed problem through the quasi-boundary value method and in Section
4
we discretize the resulting well-posed equation via a finite difference scheme. In Section
5
, we present our proposed preconditioners and in Section
6
we perform several numerical experiments and simulations under varying conditions. The results validate the effectiveness of the block triangular preconditioners in improving eigenvalue clustering and reducing GMRES iterations. Finally, in Section
7
we draw conclusions.

2
Notation and preliminaries

In this work, we deal with multi-level matrix structures of size
n_{1}n_{2}\ldots n_{d}
with
d,n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}\in\mathbb{N}
, meaning that the entries can be partitioned into blocks of size
n_{1}
, which can be further subdivided into smaller blocks of size
n_{2}
, and so on, until the inner level of size
n_{d}
is reached. To effectively address the elements, we adopt the multi-index notation, with the following definitions and rules.

Multi-index notation.

A multi-index is a vector
(m_{1},\ldots,m_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. It is denoted with the corresponding bold cursive letter
{\boldsymbol{m}}
, while
{\boldsymbol{0}}
,
{\boldsymbol{1}}
, etc., denote vectors of all zeros, ones, and so on. The size will be clear from context. Operations and relations are performed componentwise, and
{\boldsymbol{h}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{k}}
represents the multi-index range
\{{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:{\boldsymbol{h}}\leq{\boldsymbol{j}}\leq{\boldsymbol{k}}\}
: when we write
{\boldsymbol{j}}={\boldsymbol{h}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{k}}
we mean that
{\boldsymbol{j}}
varies in this range according to the standard lexicographic ordering. Moreover, the product of all the components of
{\boldsymbol{m}}
is denoted by
N({\boldsymbol{m}}):=m_{1}m_{2}\ldots m_{d}
. Using these conventions, any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{N({\boldsymbol{n}})}
is expressed as
\mathbf{x}=\big[\,x_{{\boldsymbol{i}}}\,\big]_{{\boldsymbol{i}}={\boldsymbol{1}}}^{\boldsymbol{n}}
, while a
d
-level matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{N({\boldsymbol{n}})\times N({\boldsymbol{n}})}
takes the compact form
A=\begin{bmatrix}\;a_{{\boldsymbol{i}}{\boldsymbol{j}}}\;\,\end{bmatrix}_{{\boldsymbol{i}},{\boldsymbol{j}}={\boldsymbol{1}}}^{\boldsymbol{n}}
. The multi-index notation aligns with the structure of a multi-level matrix in a natural way, since
(i_{1},j_{1})
represents the outer block structure, and so on, until
(i_{d},j_{d})
indexes the inner level. For instance, in a 2-level matrix the element
({\boldsymbol{i}},{\boldsymbol{j}})
is found as the
(i_{2},j_{2})
entry of the block in position
(i_{1},j_{1})
.

Multi-level Toeplitz matrices.

A particular type of multi-level matrix structure is found in multi-level Toeplitz matrices, whose element in position
({\boldsymbol{i}},{\boldsymbol{j}})
depends only on the difference
{\boldsymbol{i}}-{\boldsymbol{j}}
. They can be expressed as
\begin{bmatrix}t_{{\boldsymbol{i}}-{\boldsymbol{j}}}\end{bmatrix}_{{\boldsymbol{i}},{\boldsymbol{j}}={\boldsymbol{1}}}^{{\boldsymbol{n}}}\;\in\mathbb{C}^{N({\boldsymbol{n}})\times N({\boldsymbol{n}})}
for any fixed
{\boldsymbol{n}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
. In the case where
d=1
, it is simply a Toeplitz matrix with constant entries along the diagonals, while for
d=2
it is a block Toeplitz matrix in which each block has a Toeplitz structure. More in general, a
d
-level Toeplitz matrix is a block Toeplitz matrix whose blocks have
(d-1)
-level Toeplitz structure. When the elements
t_{\boldsymbol{k}}
,
{\boldsymbol{k}}={\boldsymbol{1}}-{\boldsymbol{n}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{n}}-{\boldsymbol{1}}
, are the Fourier coefficients of a given complex-valued function
f\in L^{1}\big([-\pi,\pi]^{d}\big)
, the Toeplitz matrix is said to be generated by
f
.

3
Regularization

We start by regularizing the ill-posed problem given in (
1
), in order to obtain an equivalent well-posed formulation. We follow the quasi-boundary value method applied in
[
10
]
, also known as the non-local boundary value method. It is a classical regularization technique used for stabilizing ill-posed inverse problems, particularly when the terminal data is corrupted by noise. Unlike standard Tikhonov regularization, this approach modifies the boundary condition rather than the operator, offering better preservation of problem structure. It has been successfully applied to time-fractional diffusion equations in works such as
[
13
]
, and it is well-suited for problems where the final state measurement is uncertain or incomplete.

Suppose that the measurement of the final time data
\mu(\mathbf{x})=u(\mathbf{x},T)
in Equation (
1
) is contaminated by noise of level
\varepsilon>0
and denote the noise-contaminated measurement by
\mu_{\varepsilon}
. We assume that

\|\mu-\mu_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\varepsilon,

(3)

where
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}}
represents the
L^{2}
norm on
\Omega
. Applying the quasi-boundary value method to (
1
) leads to the following well-posed regularized problem

\begin{cases}\gamma_{1}(\mathbf{x},t)D_{t}^{\beta}v(\mathbf{x},t)+\gamma_{2}(\mathbf{x},t)(-\Delta)^{\frac{\omega}{2}}v(\mathbf{x},t)=\tilde{f}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})q(t),&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,\;t\in(0,T),\\
v(\mathbf{x},t)=0,&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega^{\mathsf{c}},\;t\in(0,T),\\
v(\mathbf{x},0)=\rho(\mathbf{x}),&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,\\
v(\mathbf{x},T)=\mu_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})-\lambda\gamma_{2}(\mathbf{x},t)(-\Delta)^{\frac{\omega}{2}}\tilde{f}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}),&\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,\end{cases}

(4)

in which
\lambda>0
is the regularization parameter,
v(\mathbf{x},t)
represents an approximation of
u(\mathbf{x},t)
, and
\tilde{f}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}
is an approximation of
f(\mathbf{x})
depending on
\lambda
and
\varepsilon
.

The choice of
\lambda
directly affects the balance between fitting the observed data and maintaining a stable, accurate solution. If
\lambda
is too small, the method focuses too much on matching the data exactly, which can lead to overfitting, capturing noise and producing unstable or oscillatory results. On the other hand, if
\lambda
is too large, the method emphasizes stability too much and may overly smooth the solution, causing important features of the true source to be lost or misrepresented. Optimal selection can be guided by techniques such as the L-curve method or the discrepancy principle, depending on the noise level in the data.

4
Discretization

In this section, we follow the finite difference method used in
[
10
]
to discretize (
4
), generalizing it to the variable coefficients setting and assuming that the spatial domain
\Omega
is an open hyperrectangle in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The analysis can be extended to non-Cartesian domains using immersion techniques; see for instance
[
9
]
for an application in the setting of a general bounded convex domain.

Let
S
be a positive integer representing the number of time steps and define the corresponding grid of step size
\Delta t
as

\Delta t:=\frac{T}{S},\qquad t_{s}:=s\Delta t,\qquad s=0,1,\ldots,S.

Recalling that
\beta\in(0,1)
, the L1 formula
[
14
]
is adopted to approximate the Caputo fractional derivative:

D_{L1}^{\beta}v(\mathbf{x},t_{s})=\frac{\Delta t^{-\beta}}{\Gamma(2-\beta)}\left[b^{(\beta)}_{0}v(\mathbf{x},t_{s})-\sum_{m=1}^{s-1}\left(b^{(\beta)}_{s-m-1}-b^{(\beta)}_{s-m}\right)v(\mathbf{x},t_{m})-b^{(\beta)}_{s-1}v(\mathbf{x},t_{0})\right],

(5)

in which for
m=0,1,2,\dots
the coefficients
b_{m}^{(\beta)}
are given by

b^{(\beta)}_{m}=(m+1)^{1-\beta}-m^{1-\beta}.

Under mild hypothesis, an error estimate is available for this formula, see
[
10
, Lemma 2.1]
or
[
14
, Theorem 1.6.1]
.

To deal with the fractional Laplacian (
2
), a fractional centered difference scheme is employed. Assuming that
\Omega:=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(a_{i},b_{i})
and setting, for simplicity, a unique step size
h
in all the
d
spatial directions, let
n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{d}\in\mathbb{N}
be the number of step size in each direction. We define the points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

x^{(1)}_{j_{1}}:=j_{1}h,\qquad x^{(2)}_{j_{2}}:=j_{2}h,\qquad\cdots\qquad x^{(d)}_{j_{d}}:=j_{d}h,\qquad{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},

and, making use of the multi-indices
{\boldsymbol{n}}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{d})
and
{\boldsymbol{j}}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{d})
, we denote the corresponding discrete mesh in
\Omega
as

\Omega_{h}:=\big\{\big(x^{(1)}_{j_{1}},x^{(2)}_{j_{2}},\dots,x^{(d)}_{j_{d}}\big)\mid{\boldsymbol{j}}={\boldsymbol{1}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{n}}\big\}.

Then, the discrete fractional Laplacian is constructed as

(-\Delta_{h})^{\frac{\omega}{2}}v\big(x^{(1)}_{j_{1}},\dots,x^{(d)}_{j_{d}},t\big)=\frac{1}{h^{\omega}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{l}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}a^{(\omega)}_{\boldsymbol{l}}v\big(x^{(1)}_{j_{1}}+l_{1}h,\,\dots\,,x^{(d)}_{j_{d}}+l_{d}h,\,t\big),

(6)

where
{\boldsymbol{l}}
represents a multi-index
{\boldsymbol{l}}=(l_{1},l_{2},\ldots,l_{d})
and
a^{(\omega)}_{\boldsymbol{l}}
,
{\boldsymbol{l}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, are the Fourier coefficients of the
d
-variate function

g({\boldsymbol{\theta}}):=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}4\sin^{2}{\left(\frac{\theta_{i}}{2}\right)}\right]^{\frac{\omega}{2}},\qquad{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\ldots,\theta_{d}),

(7)

defined by the formula

a^{(\omega)}_{\boldsymbol{l}}=\frac{1}{{(2\pi)}^{d}}\int_{{[-\pi,\pi]}^{d}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}{4\sin^{2}{\left(\frac{\theta_{i}}{2}\right)}}\right]^{\frac{\omega}{2}}e^{-{\mathrm{i}}{\boldsymbol{l}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\qquad{\mathrm{i}}^{2}=-1,\quad{\boldsymbol{l}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}l_{i}\theta_{i}.

(8)

It was proved in
[
7
]
that the approximation (
6
) converges to the fractional Laplacian with order
\gamma\leq 2
, under mild conditions concerning the smoothness of
v(\mathbf{x},t)
.

To compute the Fourier coefficients, in the one-dimensional case where
d=1
we have the explicit formula

a_{l}^{(\omega)}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left[4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\right]^{\frac{\omega}{2}}e^{-{\mathrm{i}}l\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{{(-1)}^{l}\Gamma(\omega+1)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\omega}{2}-l+1\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{\omega}{2}+l+1\right)}.

(9)

Moreover, in this case the following properties are satisfied.

Lemma 1

(
[
10
, Lemma 2.2]
)
.

Let
\omega\in(1,2)
and let
a^{(\omega)}_{l}
be defined as in (
9
) for
l\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then,

(a)

a^{(\omega)}_{0}
is the only nonnegative coefficient, i.e.,

a^{(\omega)}_{0}=\frac{\Gamma(\omega+1)}{\Gamma(\frac{\omega}{2}+1)^{2}}\geq 0,\qquad a^{(\omega)}_{l}=a^{(\omega)}_{-l}<0,\quad\forall l\neq 0;

(b)

for any
l\in\mathbb{N}
, the following recursive expression holds

a^{(\omega)}_{l+1}=\left(1-\frac{\omega+1}{\frac{\omega}{2}+l+1}\right)a^{(\omega)}_{l};

(c)

the Fourier coefficients sum to zero, i.e.,

\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}a^{(\omega)}_{l}=0.

On the other hand, in multi-dimensional settings we compute the coefficients
a_{\boldsymbol{l}}^{(\omega)}
numerically through the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT).

To construct the finite difference scheme, we define

\begin{cases}e_{0}^{(\beta)}=b_{0}^{(\beta)},\\
e_{m}^{(\beta)}=b_{m}^{(\beta)}-b_{m-1}^{(\beta)}=(m+1)^{1-\beta}-2m^{1-\beta}+(m-1)^{1-\beta},&\qquad 1\leq m\leq S-1;\end{cases}

then, combining (
4
), (
5
) and (
6
) and omitting the truncation errors, we obtain, for
s=1,\ldots,S
,

\begin{cases}\frac{\Delta t^{-\beta}\gamma_{1,{\boldsymbol{j}}}^{(s)}}{\Gamma(2-\beta)}\left[e^{(\beta)}_{0}v_{\boldsymbol{j}}^{(s)}+\sum_{m=1}^{s-1}e^{(\beta)}_{s-m}v_{\boldsymbol{j}}^{(m)}-b^{(\beta)}_{s-1}v_{\boldsymbol{j}}^{(0)}\right]+\frac{\gamma_{2,{\boldsymbol{j}}}^{(s)}}{h^{\omega}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{l}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}a^{(\omega)}_{\boldsymbol{l}}v_{{\boldsymbol{j}}+{\boldsymbol{l}}}^{(s)}=\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{j}}q^{(s)},&{\boldsymbol{j}}={\boldsymbol{1}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{n}},\\
v_{\boldsymbol{j}}^{(s)}=0,&{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus[{\boldsymbol{1}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{n}}],\\
v_{\boldsymbol{j}}^{(0)}=\rho_{\boldsymbol{j}},&{\boldsymbol{j}}={\boldsymbol{1}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{n}},\\
v_{\boldsymbol{j}}^{(S)}=\mu_{\varepsilon,{\boldsymbol{j}}}-\frac{\lambda\gamma_{2,{\boldsymbol{j}}}^{(S)}}{h^{\omega}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{l}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}a^{(\omega)}_{\boldsymbol{l}}\tilde{f}_{{\boldsymbol{j}}+{\boldsymbol{l}}},&{\boldsymbol{j}}={\boldsymbol{1}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{n}},\end{cases}

(10)

where
v_{\boldsymbol{j}}^{(s)}
denotes the numerical approximation of
v\big(x^{(1)}_{j_{1}},x^{(2)}_{j_{2}},\dots,x^{(d)}_{j_{d}},t_{s}\big)
and similarly

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{j}}

\displaystyle:=\tilde{f}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}\big(x^{(1)}_{j_{1}},x^{(2)}_{j_{2}},\dots,x^{(d)}_{j_{d}}\big),

\displaystyle\qquad q^{(s)}

\displaystyle:=q(t_{s}),

\displaystyle\gamma_{1,{\boldsymbol{j}}}^{(s)}

\displaystyle:=\gamma_{1}\big(x^{(1)}_{j_{1}},x^{(2)}_{j_{2}},\dots,x^{(d)}_{j_{d}},t_{s}\big),

\displaystyle\gamma_{2,{\boldsymbol{j}}}^{(s)}

\displaystyle:=\gamma_{2}\big(x^{(1)}_{j_{1}},x^{(2)}_{j_{2}},\dots,x^{(d)}_{j_{d}},t_{s}\big),

\displaystyle\rho_{\boldsymbol{j}}

\displaystyle:=\rho\big(x^{(1)}_{j_{1}},x^{(2)}_{j_{2}},\dots,x^{(d)}_{j_{d}}\big),

\displaystyle\mu_{\varepsilon,{\boldsymbol{j}}}

\displaystyle:=\mu_{\varepsilon}\big(x^{(1)}_{j_{1}},x^{(2)}_{j_{2}},\dots,x^{(d)}_{j_{d}}\big).

5
Matrix formulation and preconditioning

Now, let us write the scheme found above in matrix form. We define the coefficient

\eta=\eta(\beta,\Delta t):=\Delta t^{\beta}\Gamma(2-\beta),

the vectors

\mathbf{v}^{(s)}:=\begin{bmatrix}v_{\boldsymbol{j}}^{(s)}\end{bmatrix}_{{\boldsymbol{j}}={\boldsymbol{1}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{n}}}^{T},\qquad\tilde{\mathbf{f}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\end{bmatrix}_{{\boldsymbol{j}}={\boldsymbol{1}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{n}}}^{T},\qquad\boldsymbol{\rho}:=\begin{bmatrix}\rho_{\boldsymbol{j}}\end{bmatrix}_{{\boldsymbol{j}}={\boldsymbol{1}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{n}}}^{T},\qquad\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\varepsilon}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{\varepsilon,{\boldsymbol{j}}}\end{bmatrix}_{{\boldsymbol{j}}={\boldsymbol{1}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{n}}}^{T},

and the diagonal matrices

D_{1}^{(s)}:=\diag_{{\boldsymbol{j}}={\boldsymbol{1}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{n}}}\gamma_{1,{\boldsymbol{j}}}^{(s)},\qquad D_{2}^{(s)}:=\diag_{{\boldsymbol{j}}={\boldsymbol{1}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{n}}}\gamma_{2,{\boldsymbol{j}}}^{(s)},\qquad s=1,\ldots,S.

Therefore, the numerical scheme (
10
) can be reformulated as follows:

\begin{cases}D_{1}^{(s)}\left(e_{0}^{(\beta)}\mathbf{v}^{(s)}+\sum_{m=1}^{s-1}e_{s-m}^{(\beta)}\mathbf{v}^{(m)}\right)+\frac{\eta}{h^{\omega}}D_{2}^{(s)}B_{\boldsymbol{n}}\mathbf{v}^{(s)}-\eta q^{(s)}\tilde{\mathbf{f}}=b_{s-1}^{(\beta)}D_{1}^{(s)}\boldsymbol{\rho},&s=1,\ldots,S,\\
\mathbf{v}^{(S)}+\frac{\lambda}{h^{\omega}}D_{2}^{(S)}B_{\boldsymbol{n}}\tilde{\mathbf{f}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\varepsilon},\end{cases}

where
B_{\boldsymbol{n}}
is the
d
-level Toeplitz matrix of size
N({\boldsymbol{n}})\times N({\boldsymbol{n}})
representing the fractional Laplacian, with elements
a_{\boldsymbol{l}}^{(\omega)}
given by (
8
). In other words,
B_{\boldsymbol{n}}
is the
d
-level Toeplitz matrix generated by the function
g(\boldsymbol{\theta})
in
(
7
)
. Note that, since
g(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is real-valued and even,
B_{\boldsymbol{n}}
is a real symmetric matrix.

The unknowns in the scheme above are
\tilde{\mathbf{f}}
and
\mathbf{v}^{(s)}
for
s=1,\ldots,S
. By gathering the equations for all the time levels and the regularization equation in one large linear system, we get

A_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{z}

(11)

where

A_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc|c}e_{0}D_{1}^{(1)}+\frac{\eta}{h^{\omega}}D_{2}^{(1)}B_{\boldsymbol{n}}&\mathrm{O}_{\boldsymbol{n}}&\dots&\mathrm{O}_{\boldsymbol{n}}&-\eta q^{(1)}I_{\boldsymbol{n}}\\
e_{1}D_{1}^{(2)}&e_{0}D_{1}^{(2)}+\frac{\eta}{h^{\omega}}D_{2}^{(2)}B_{\boldsymbol{n}}&\ddots&\vdots&-\eta q^{(2)}I_{\boldsymbol{n}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\mathrm{O}_{\boldsymbol{n}}&\vdots\\
e_{S-1}D_{1}^{(S)}&\cdots&e_{1}D_{1}^{(S)}&e_{0}D_{1}^{(S)}+\frac{\eta}{h^{\omega}}D_{2}^{(S)}B_{\boldsymbol{n}}&-\eta q^{(S)}I_{\boldsymbol{n}}\\
\hline\cr\mathrm{O}_{\boldsymbol{n}}&\cdots&\mathrm{O}_{\boldsymbol{n}}&I_{\boldsymbol{n}}&\frac{\lambda}{h^{\omega}}D_{2}^{(S)}B_{\boldsymbol{n}}\end{array}\right],

in which
I_{\boldsymbol{n}}
is the identity matrix of size
N({\boldsymbol{n}})
and
\mathrm{O}_{\boldsymbol{n}}
is the zero matrix, while

\mathbf{y}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{v}^{(1)}\\
\mathbf{v}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{v}^{(S)}\\
\hline\cr\tilde{\mathbf{f}}\end{array}\right],\qquad\mathbf{z}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}b_{0}D_{1}^{(1)}\boldsymbol{\rho}\\
b_{1}D_{1}^{(2)}\boldsymbol{\rho}\\
\vdots\\
b_{S-1}D_{1}^{(S)}\boldsymbol{\rho}\\
\hline\cr\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\varepsilon}\end{array}\right].

Implementation.

To solve the linear system, we employ the MATLAB built-in GMRES function, to which we supply a custom routine for the computation of the matrix-vector product
\mathbf{z}=A_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}\mathbf{y}
, where

\mathbf{y}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{y}^{(S+1)}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{z}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{z}^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{z}^{(S+1)}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{y}^{(s)},\mathbf{z}^{(s)}\in\mathbb{C}^{{\boldsymbol{n}}}.

The algorithm we implement follows this scheme:

1.

For
s=1,\ldots,S

(a)

Compute
\mathbf{w}^{(s)}:=D_{1}^{(s)}\big(e_{s-1}\mathbf{y}^{(1)}+\ldots+e_{0}\mathbf{y}^{(s)}\big)
in
O\big(sN({\boldsymbol{n}})\big)
operations;

(b)

Compute
\frac{\eta}{h^{\omega}}D_{2}^{(s)}B_{\boldsymbol{n}}\mathbf{y}^{(s)}
in
O\big(N({\boldsymbol{n}})\log N({\boldsymbol{n}})\big)
operations without explicitly constructing the matrix, by embedding
B_{\boldsymbol{n}}
in a larger
d
-level circulant matrix and exploiting the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). Then, add the result to
\mathbf{w}^{(s)}
in
O\big(N({\boldsymbol{n}})\big)
operations;

(c)

Subtract
\eta q^{(s)}\mathbf{y}^{(s)}
in
O\big(N({\boldsymbol{n}})\big)
operations to obtain
\mathbf{z}^{(s)}
.

2.

For
s=S+1

(a)

Compute
\frac{\lambda}{h^{\omega}}D_{2}^{(S)}B_{\boldsymbol{n}}\mathbf{y}^{(S+1)}
in
O\big(N({\boldsymbol{n}})\log N({\boldsymbol{n}})\big)
operations using the FFT;

(b)

Add
\mathbf{y}^{(S)}
in
O\big(N({\boldsymbol{n}})\big)
operations to obtain
\mathbf{z}^{(S+1)}
.

The overall computational complexity of this algorithm is
O\big(SN({\boldsymbol{n}})\log SN({\boldsymbol{n}})\big)
.

5.1
Preconditioning proposal

We now turn to our preconditioning strategy, aiming to reduce the condition number of the coefficient matrix or to cluster the spectrum at 1. Based on the structure of
A_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}
, we propose the following lower block triangular preconditioner to ensure computational efficiency:

P_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc|c}e_{0}D_{1}^{(1)}+\frac{\eta}{h^{\omega}}D_{2}^{(1)}B_{\boldsymbol{n}}&\mathrm{O}_{\boldsymbol{n}}&\dots&\mathrm{O}_{\boldsymbol{n}}&\mathrm{O}_{\boldsymbol{n}}\\
e_{1}D_{1}^{(2)}&e_{0}D_{1}^{(2)}+\frac{\eta}{h^{\omega}}D_{2}^{(2)}B_{\boldsymbol{n}}&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\mathrm{O}_{\boldsymbol{n}}&\vdots\\
e_{S-1}D_{1}^{(S)}&\cdots&e_{1}D_{1}^{(S)}&e_{0}D_{1}^{(S)}+\frac{\eta}{h^{\omega}}D_{2}^{(S)}B_{\boldsymbol{n}}&\mathrm{O}_{\boldsymbol{n}}\\
\hline\cr\mathrm{O}_{\boldsymbol{n}}&\cdots&\mathrm{O}_{\boldsymbol{n}}&I_{\boldsymbol{n}}&\frac{\lambda}{h^{\omega}}D_{2}^{(S)}B_{\boldsymbol{n}}\end{array}\right],

where we simply replaced the elements in the upper right section of
A_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}
with zeros.

P_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}
clearly offers an accurate spectral approximation of the coefficient matrix, since it holds
A_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}=P_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}+R_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}
, where
R_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}
is a matrix of rank at most
N({\boldsymbol{n}})
. Therefore,

P_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}^{-1}A_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}=I_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}+P_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}^{-1}R_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S},

where
I_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}
is the identity of size
(S+1)N({\boldsymbol{n}})
and
P_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}^{-1}R_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}
has rank at most
N({\boldsymbol{n}})
. In other words, the preconditioned matrix is a low-rank correction of the identity, hence the eigenvalues will be clustered at 1.

Implementation.

The block triangular structure allows us to implement a forward substitution algorithm to solve linear systems of the form
P_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{z}
, with

\mathbf{y}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{y}^{(S+1)}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{z}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{z}^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{z}^{(S+1)}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{y}^{(s)},\mathbf{z}^{(s)}\in\mathbb{C}^{{\boldsymbol{n}}}.

The algorithm is structured as follows:

1.

For
s=1

(a)

Compute
e_{0}D_{1}^{(1)}+\frac{\eta}{h^{\omega}}D_{2}^{(1)}B_{\boldsymbol{n}}
in
O\big(N({\boldsymbol{n}})^{2}\big)
flops;

(b)

Solve the associated linear system with right hand term
\mathbf{z}^{(1)}
, using the MATLAB backslash operator, to obtain
\mathbf{y}^{(1)}
in
O\big(N({\boldsymbol{n}})^{3}\big)
flops.

2.

For
s=2,\ldots,S

(a)

Compute
e_{0}D_{1}^{(s)}+\frac{\eta}{h^{\omega}}D_{2}^{(s)}B_{\boldsymbol{n}}
in
O\big(N({\boldsymbol{n}})^{2}\big)
flops;

(b)

Compute the right hand term
\mathbf{z}^{(s)}-D_{1}^{(s)}\big(e_{s-1}\mathbf{y}^{(1)}+\ldots+e_{1}\mathbf{y}^{(s-1)}\big)
in
O\big(sN({\boldsymbol{n}})\big)
flops;

(c)

Solve the associated linear system with the backslash operator to obtain
\mathbf{y}^{(s)}
in
O\big(N({\boldsymbol{n}})^{3}\big)
flops.

3.

For
s=S+1

(a)

Compute
\frac{\lambda}{h^{\omega}}D_{2}^{(S)}B_{\boldsymbol{n}}
in
O\big(N({\boldsymbol{n}})^{2}\big)
flops;

(b)

Compute the right hand term
\mathbf{z}^{(S+1)}-\mathbf{y}^{(S)}
in
N({\boldsymbol{n}})
flops;

(c)

Solve the associated linear system with the backslash operator to obtain
\mathbf{y}^{(S+1)}
in
O\big(N({\boldsymbol{n}})^{3}\big)
flops.

Note that both the coefficient matrix and the preconditioner need not be explicitly assembled, allowing us to bypass the construction phase, except for the Toeplitz matrix
B_{\boldsymbol{n}}
which can be constructed just once during the setup.

Remark 1
.

The most computationally demanding steps in the above procedure are those involving the solution of linear systems that contain the dense Toeplitz matrix
B_{\boldsymbol{n}}
. In the constant coefficient case, i.e., when
D_{1}^{(s)}=\gamma_{1}I_{\boldsymbol{n}}
and
D_{2}^{(s)}=\gamma_{2}I_{\boldsymbol{n}}
for all the time instants
s=1,\ldots,S
, we can overcome this difficulty by substituting
B_{\boldsymbol{n}}
with structured matrices that spectrally approximate
B_{\boldsymbol{n}}
and allow for more efficient inversion through fast transforms. A detailed analysis of this constant coefficient subcase is provided in
[
10
]
, where the authors propose preconditioners that preserve the structure of the coefficient matrix by replacing every occurrence of
B_{\boldsymbol{n}}
in
A_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}
with its
\tau
and Strang preconditioners
[
3
]
.

This approach can be directly extended to our block triangular preconditioning strategy by substituting each instance of
B_{\boldsymbol{n}}
in the definition of
P_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}
with its
\tau
and Strang preconditioners. Let us denote the preconditioners constructed in this way as
T_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}
and
S_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}
, respectively. This procedure allows one to solve the aforementioned linear systems by leveraging the diagonalizability of circulant and
\tau
matrices, respectively via the Fourier and Sine matrices, and the associated fast transforms
[
15
]
. Clearly,
P_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}
provides a more accurate spectral approximation of the coefficient matrix than
T_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}
and
S_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}
, since the preconditioned matrix is a low-rank correction of the identity. As a result, it is expected that preconditioning with
P_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}
would lead to a lower number of iterations when using the GMRES method. However, thanks to the computational efficiency offered by fast transforms,
T_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}
and
S_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}
would reasonably yield lower CPU times than
P_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}
, making them the most competitive. In particular,
T_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}
is expected to deliver a superior performance, as the
\tau
preconditioner more accurately captures the behavior of the small eigenvalues of
B_{\boldsymbol{n}}
compared to the circulant Strang preconditioner. Given that this subcase has already been extensively studied in
[
10
]
, with minor differences with respect to our setting, we will not delve further into it.

Unfortunately, the computational benefits of the Strang and
\tau
preconditioners cannot be exploited in the variable coefficients case. In fact, the circulant or
\tau
structure is lost when they are multiplied by
D_{2}^{(s)}
. Hence, we restrict our attention to the preconditioner
P_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}
, since it provides the most accurate spectral approximation of
A_{{\boldsymbol{n}},S}
.

6
Numerical experiments

We conclude our analysis by numerically testing the efficiency of the proposed strategy. Our aim is to assess the preconditioner’s effectiveness in clustering the eigenvalues at 1 and improving the condition number, and also in terms of iterations and CPU time required by the GMRES method. Furthermore, we evaluate the accuracy of the method in reconstructing the source term. All tests were run on MATLAB R2022b, using a PC with the following specifications: Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-9750H CPU @2.60GHz 2.59 GHz and 16 GB of RAM.

6.1
Problem setting

Let us consider the one-dimensional version of the time-space fractional diffusion equation (
4
), with

\displaystyle\Omega=(0,\pi),\qquad T=1,

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(x,t)=te^{x},\qquad\gamma_{2}(x,t)=t^{2}x,

\displaystyle q(t)=t^{2},\qquad\rho(x)=0.

To construct the final time data
\mu(x)=v(x,T)
, we fix the exact space source term

f(x)=x\sin(x)

and numerically solve the direct problem

\begin{cases}\gamma_{1}(x,t)D_{t}^{\beta}u(x,t)+\gamma_{2}(x,t)(-\Delta)^{\frac{\omega}{2}}u(x,t)=f(x)q(t),&x\in\Omega,\;t\in(0,T),\\
u(x,t)=0,&x\in\Omega^{\mathsf{c}},\;t\in(0,T),\\
u(x,0)=\rho(x),&x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

In other words, we solve the linear system obtained by applying the same discretization technique used in Section
4
, which is

\widehat{A}_{n,S}\mathbf{y}=\widehat{\mathbf{z}}

where

\widehat{A}_{n,S}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}e_{0}D_{1}^{(1)}+\frac{\eta}{h^{\omega}}D_{2}^{(1)}B_{n}&\mathrm{O}_{n}&\dots&\mathrm{O}_{n}\\
e_{1}D_{1}^{(2)}&e_{0}D_{1}^{(2)}+\frac{\eta}{h^{\omega}}D_{2}^{(2)}B_{n}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\mathrm{O}_{n}\\
e_{S-1}D_{1}^{(S)}&\cdots&e_{1}D_{1}^{(S)}&e_{0}D_{1}^{(S)}+\frac{\eta}{h^{\omega}}D_{2}^{(S)}B_{n}\end{array}\right],

\mathbf{y}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{v}^{(1)}\\
\mathbf{v}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{v}^{(S)}\end{array}\right],\qquad\widehat{\mathbf{z}}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}b_{0}D_{1}^{(1)}\boldsymbol{\rho}+\eta q^{(1)}\mathbf{f}\\
b_{1}D_{1}^{(2)}\boldsymbol{\rho}+\eta q^{(2)}\mathbf{f}\\
\vdots\\
b_{S-1}D_{1}^{(S)}\boldsymbol{\rho}+\eta q^{(S)}\mathbf{f}\end{array}\right],

where
\mathbf{f}
denotes the sampling of
f(x)
over the spatial mesh.

Then, to simulate the contamination of
\mu(x)
by noise as in (
3
), we perturb it by adding a random quantity
\varepsilon\delta(x)
, where
\delta(x)
is randomly selected in the interval
(-1,1)
and the noise level is set to
\varepsilon=0.01\cdot\|\mu\|_{2}
. Therefore, we have
\mu_{\varepsilon}(x)=\mu(x)+\varepsilon\delta(x)
and our regularized model problem takes the form

\begin{cases}\gamma_{1}(x,t)D_{t}^{\beta}v(x,t)+\gamma_{2}(x,t)(-\Delta)^{\frac{\omega}{2}}v(x,t)=t^{2}\tilde{f}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x),&x\in(0,\pi),\;t\in(0,1),\\
v(x,t)=0,&x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus(0,\pi),\;t\in(0,1),\\
v(x,0)=0,&x\in(0,\pi),\\
v(x,1)=\mu_{\varepsilon}(x)-\gamma_{2}(x,t)\lambda(-\Delta)^{\frac{\omega}{2}}\tilde{f}_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x),&x\in(0,\pi).\end{cases}

Conclusions

We investigated the effectiveness of block triangular preconditioners for accelerating and stabilizing the numerical solution of inverse source problems governed by time-space fractional diffusion equations (TSFDEs). Specifically, we addressed the recovery of an unknown spatial source function in a multi-dimensional TSFDE incorporating Caputo time-fractional derivatives and the fractional Laplacian. To tackle the inherent ill-posedness of the problem, we employed a quasi-boundary value regularization approach, followed by a finite difference discretization. The resulting linear systems are characterized by various levels of structure, which we exploited to design and analyze a block triangular preconditioning strategy. Numerical experiments using the GMRES solver shown that the proposed preconditioner significantly improves convergence rates, robustness, and accuracy, making it well-suited for large-scale, real world inverse problems involving fractional models.

As future research directions, we identify two promising open questions:

a)

an analysis of the eigenvalue and singular value distributions in the Weyl sense of the resulting matrix-sequences, both original and preconditioned, using the framework of Generalized Locally Toeplitz (GLT) sequences

]
;

b)

the study of item a) within the context of block-structured matrix analysis, as developed in
[

]
.

Pursuing these directions may lead to the development of even more effective preconditioning techniques and provide a deeper understanding of the spectral properties of the matrix sequences arising from such fractional inverse problems.
