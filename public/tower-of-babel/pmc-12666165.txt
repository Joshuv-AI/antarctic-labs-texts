Feasibility of screening for heart failure in Africa: barriers and facilitators in a low-resourced setting
Authors: Neusa Jessen, Albertino Damasceno, Pardeep Jhund
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeart failure (HF) is of worldwide public health concern, yet with higher morbidity and mortality in low- and middle-income countries compared with high-income nations, posing a significant burden to the already overstrained and unprepared health systems in many countries.In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), while community level data is sparse, a number of hospital-based HF studies have been conducted, highlighting the distinct presentation of HF when compared with data from other regions of the world. It is imperative that more data on the precise nature of HF in Africa are obtained, but multiple barriers exist to obtaining reliable data. In the present commentary, we discuss characteristics of HF in Africa, their potential impact on conducting screening studies at the community level and describe the strategies that we have incorporated in the MAPUTO-HF (The Maputo Antecedents, Prevalence and Urban Transition Of Heart Failure Project: feasibility of a population-based study in a low-resource African setting) study, a feasibility study that was designed to overcome these barriers and facilitate HF research in SSA in both a rural and urban setting.

The burden of heart failure in Africa

While heart failure (HF) is of worldwide public health concern, the burden of disease is greatest in low- and middle-income countries, with prolonged hospitalisations and higher morbidity and in-hospital and 1-year mortality compared with high-income countries.1 2 Global population-based estimates show that the prevalence of HF varies from 1% to 4% with increasing trends despite the better therapies and very high mortality.3 However, global estimates of HF prevalence usually do not include data from African countries due to the lack of population level studies on HF epidemiology conducted in the continent. Yet, families, communities and healthcare systems in Africa suffer due to the weight of this burden, while many countries are unprepared. Management of HF poses an enormous challenge to African countries, due to the low availability of diagnostic tools and therapies for HF care,4 which is competing with highly prevalent communicable diseases in settings with limited resources. Consequently, 1-year mortality rates due to HF are higher than other regions of the world (34% in Africa vs 23% in India, the region with the next highest rate).5 It is imperative that more data on the precise nature of HF in Africa are obtained, but in these low-resource settings, multiple barriers exist to obtaining reliable data on the prevalence and aetiology of HF on which to base healthcare decisions for Africa.

While community level data are sparse, a number of hospital-based HF studies have been conducted in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) in the last two decades. These have highlighted the marked differences in presentation, aetiology and comorbidities when compared with data from other regions of the world6 (table 1). Hospital admissions are lower in African hospitals than expected when compared with other regions, and prognosis is poorer.3 This reflects the socioeconomic conditions of patients and families and the underdeveloped healthcare systems in SSA. Estimates of the prevalence of HF according to left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) are scarce among studies including African patients. The Inter-CHF (INTERnational Congestive Heart Failure Study) study,7 which enrolled 5813 patients between 2012 and 2014, included a total of 1294 patients with HF from five African countries (Mozambique, Nigeria, South Africa, Sudan and Uganda).8 It found a heterogeneous distribution of LVEF between these countries: HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) was observed in 54% of patients overall, with Nigeria having the greatest proportion with HFrEF (80%). HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction was observed in 30% of patients overall, with the greatest proportion in Sudan (28%). The proportion of patients with HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is much lower in these African countries when compared with the 50% seen in developed countries. In fact, only 16% had HFpEF and the highest proportion of patients with HF with HFpEF was in Uganda at 31%. Such findings may reflect variations in diagnostic criteria, use of echocardiographic modalities such as measurement of diastolic function and use of strain that are more labour-intensive and less widely available, leading to underestimation of the prevalence of HFpEF. More likely, they reflect the very different epidemiology and distribution of risk factors and comorbidities that track with ejection fraction and HF. Most notably, patients with HF in Africa present at their most productive years of life (at around two decades younger than observed in developed nations), with more diverse aetiologies such as communicable diseases like HIV, tuberculosis, opportunistic infections related to HIV and aetiologies that are less common in developed countries such as rheumatic heart disease. Of note, patients with rheumatic valvular heart disease keep a normal ejection fraction until later stages of evolution and may represent a considerable portion of the patients with HFpEF in Africa.9 Hypertensive heart disease is the most common cause of HF, and with the epidemiological transition, ischaemic heart disease is now the second most common cause. These differences, coupled with differences in risk factors such as diabetes and obesity, all serve to make the epidemiology of HF in Africa unique. Furthermore, patients in Africa will often present with more advanced disease.10 While there are some data like those above, the true epidemiology of HF is still largely unexplored in such settings, due to the lack of systematic and detailed community level epidemiological studies of HF. So, why is it difficult to conduct population level studies in Africa to screen for HF and what can be done to facilitate the collection of data?

Barriers to conducting population level HF studies in Africa

Several issues make the task of conducting high-quality research in African community settings a challenge. Many of the issues are related to what we currently know about the epidemiology of HF in Africa (table 1), while others are due to the healthcare systems and resources and socioeconomic factors. As noted above, there are several studies of patients hospitalised with HF as hospitalised cohorts are easier to obtain with patients being readily available to researchers. For instance, researchers are usually also the attending clinicians and can incorporate studies into busy clinical practice, and during admission, patients and families are more amenable to cooperating in research and diagnostic testing and are more available. After discharge, patients tend to be lost to follow-up, either due to lack of resources (to buy medications, food or for transport to return to clinics), migration or poor understanding or misconceptions regarding their health problem and the need for chronic treatment. As such, losses for follow-up are common,11 which can be overcome by obtaining several contact numbers and scheduling frequent visits when follow-up is necessary for the study. While conducting population level surveys in Africa is possible, screening at a community level is a much harder task.

Strategies to overcome barriers to conduct HF research in African countries: building from prior experiences to set-up a HF feasibility study in a low-resourced African setting

Mozambique, one of the poorest countries in the world, is characterized by the typical population distribution in Africa with more younger people in the population than older people. It has crowded urban areas (where Westernised diets and sedentary behaviours are rapidly being adopted), poor peri-urban and very poor rural areas (with low access to healthcare and high levels of communicable diseases and poor nutrition) (figure 1). Prior collaborations with multicentre HF studies have developed a research infrastructure that is now able to use prior experience in the region to undertake community level screening for HF. Using previous experience of conducting surveys, local knowledge of healthcare and communities, we are setting out to describe the epidemiology of HF in SSA.

Given the above challenges of conducting screening and other epidemiological studies of HF in Africa, we have designed a feasibility study (The Maputo Antecedents, Prevalence and Urban Transition Of Heart Failure Project: feasibility of a population-based study in a low-resource African setting (MAPUTO-HF)) to develop methods to overcome these barriers and facilitate HF research in SSA in both a rural and urban setting. The MAPUTO-HF study was designed to consider these challenges and incorporate strategies to overcome them as described below.

Including a community engagement and involvement strategy: Prior experience suggests that it is crucial to engage and involve community leaders (chiefs, religious leaders and traditional leaders) from the beginning of the study (before recruitment commences) to gain acceptance and collaboration of the communities. These stakeholders have influence with the population and can facilitate (or difficult) contact with communities. Gaining their trust is essential for an effective community engagement and involvement. Several strategies have been identified to help build trust.12 Community leaders should be meaningfully involved in all steps of the research, from planning to implementation, through meetings conducted along the study (to openly discuss the purpose and procedures of the study, how the population should be approached, engaged and involved and to clarify any doubts), sharing of knowledge, having staff embedded within the community by training and involving community members in some study procedures such as participant recruitment. From our experience, giving the opportunity of key stakeholders to participate in study procedures is also a very effective way of gaining their trust and posterior positive feedback to the communities. It is also important to give feedback of study developments to the community and to keep promises made.

Careful construction of protocols, case report forms and definitions: Protocols are designed to maximise data collection and minimise time for participants. Region-specific information on aetiology and comorbidities accounting for local issues such as housing and use of fuels in houses for cooking and heating as well as exposures such as heavy metals in mining work will be recorded to seek out potential region-specific factors associated with HF. Case definitions are adapted from international guidelines to ensure accurate case definition. Local cardiologists working with international HF specialists to determine aetiology and provide region-specific knowledge of the diagnosis and aetiology of HF will help improve case ascertainment.

Concluding remarks

The epidemiology of HF in Africa is complex and distinct to many other regions of the world. The knowledge that we currently have is derived from multiple cross-sectional studies often of hospitalised patients with prevalent HF. Conducting screening studies of HF in Africa is challenging due to multiple cultural, societal and epidemiological aspects of HF which are inherently linked and therefore less is known about the community epidemiology of HF. While there are numerous social, cultural and systemic barriers to conducting screening studies in HF in Africa, we believe that designing studies to overcome these barriers is possible. Only by designing specific screening studies will we be able to ascertain accurate estimates of the prevalence and aetiology and comorbidities of patients with HF. Once these data are available, then localised solutions to the prevention and treatment of HF can be designed and implemented to reduce the growing burden and high mortality of HF in Africa.
