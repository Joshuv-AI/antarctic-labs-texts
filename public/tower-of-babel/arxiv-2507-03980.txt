Combination generators with optimal cache utilization and communication free parallel execution
Xi He, Max A. Little

Abstract
We introduce an efficient and elegant combination generator for producing all combinations of size less than or equal to K K , designed for exhaustive generation and combinatorial optimization tasks. This generator can be implemented to achieve what we define as optimal efficiency : constant amortized time , optimal cache utilization , embarrassingly parallel execution , and a recursive structure compatible with pruning-based search. These properties are difficult to satisfy simultaneously in existing generators. For example, classical Gray code or lexicographic generators are typically list-based and sequentially defined, making them difficult to vectorize, inefficient in cache usage, and inherently hard to parallelize. Generators based on unranking methods, while easy to parallelize, are non-recursive. These limitations reduce their applicability in our target applications, where both computational efficiency and recursion are crucial. Our generator overcomes these challenges through three key features: a recursively blocked array layout enabling full vectorization, a divide-and-conquer structure supporting embarrassingly parallel execution, and automatic memory management handled internally by the algorithm. We adapt Bird’s algebra of programming -style calculation to derive our algorithms, a formalism for developing correct-by-construction programs from specifications. As a result, all generators in this paper are first formulated in their clearest specification, and efficient definitions are derived constructively through equational reasoning, resulting in concise and elegant divide-and-conquer definitions. Beyond presenting a combination generator, we extend our approach to construct generators for K K -permutations, nested combinations of combinations, and nested permutation-combination structures. To the best of our knowledge, the literature has not previously reported generators for these nested structures. We also develop sequential variants that produce configurations in Gray code-compatible orders—such as the revolving door ordering—which are particularly useful for constructing nested generators.

A commonly used method in combinatorial optimization involves a program
that eliminates partial configurations before extending them to complete
ones, which is applicable only when configurations are generated recursively.
. We believe that combinatorial generators designed with four key
factors—
generation efficiency
,
recursiveness
,
parallelizability
,
and
simplicity
—can satisfy the above criteria. Below, we
provide justifications for this claim and explain why existing methods
often fail to meet these requirements.

•

Generation efficiency
: First, most combinatorial structures
have configuration spaces that grow exponentially (or worse) with
input size. Consequently, any generator for such objects inherently
has exponential (or worse) complexity. As
Ruskey 2003

noted, an ideal combinatorial generator should have constant amortized
time—that is, generating each configuration should take a constant
amount of time on average, regardless of the input size. Second, algorithmic
structure should be compatible with modern hardware. Array-based,
fully vectorized implementations make better use of multi-level caches
and GPUs. In contrast, pointer-based structures like linked lists
suffer from poor cache performance and are harder to parallelize.

•

Recursiveness
: Recursive generation follows
Bellman 1954
’s
principle of optimality, which allows solutions to small subproblems
to be combined into larger solutions. Though originally for optimization,
this idea applies broadly
(
Bird and Hinze 2003
)
—for instance,
both combinations
\left[0,2,3\right]
and
\left[1,2,3\right]

can be built by extending the common sub-combination
\left[2,3\right]
.
Thus, generating
\left[2,3\right]
first and then extending it is
more efficient. In optimization, recursion also enables early pruning
of suboptimal configurations before they are fully constructed—something
non-recursive methods, such as unranking-based approaches, cannot
support.

•

Parallelizability
: Unranking-based approaches are easy to
parallelize but lack recursion and efficiency. A better solution is
to use a divide-and-conquer (D&C) style recursion, which breaks the
problem into smaller, independent subproblems. These subproblems can
be processed in parallel and mapped neatly onto modern memory hierarchies,
especially when combined with recursively blocked arrays
(
Gupta et al. 2000
)
.
However, most existing methods either lack this D&C form or do not
use arrays. For example, Gray code generators are sequential and thus
hard to parallelize. Perhaps only
Jeuring 1993
’s combination
generator has a D&C structure. However, due to its list-based structure,
Jeuring’s implementation does not lend itself naturally to a recursively
blocked array layout.

•

Mathematical elegance and simplicity
: A simple recursive
generator is much easier to implement and reason about. For example,
while
Eades et al. 1984
;
Ruskey 1988
provide insightful
analyses of combination generation, their algorithms are too complex
for practical use. As
Knuth 2011
remarked “
Eades et al. 1984

makes an interesting exercise in programming with recursive coroutines.
Unfortunately, however, none of the known constructions are sufficiently
simple to describe in a short space, or to implement with reasonable
efficiency. Perfect combination generators have therefore not yet
proved to be of practical importance.”

In this paper, we present an efficient D&C generator for generating
all
k
-combinations for
0\leq k\leq K
. It satisfies all the desirable
properties outlined above. Beyond introducing this generator, we extend
it to support related combinatorial objects, including
K
-permutations,
and the
nested combinations
/
permutations
. To the best
of our knowledge, no prior work has reported the generator for nested
combinations/permutations, except for trivial approach of composing
two separate generators. All generators presented in this paper are
derived constructively, making them simple and easy to implement.
Furthermore, we describe how to incorporate Gray code ordering into
these nested generators. This is especially useful when inner combinations
encode meaningful information.

3.1
Divide-and-conquer version

Vandermonde’s identity
is an identity for binomial coefficients,
which states that

C_{K}^{N+M}=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\left(C_{K-k}^{N}C_{k}^{M}\right)

(1)

The combinatorial proof of Vandermonde’s identity is constructive

original problem

can be decomposed into two

,

independent
subproblems

If we replace the sum and product operations in (

\mathit{nestedPerms}\left(K,D\right)=\left\langle\mathit{setEmpty}\left(D\right),\mathit{kperms}\left(K\right)\cdot!\left(D\right)\right\rangle\cdot\mathit{kcombs}\left(D\right),

(25)

The nested permutation generator (NPCG) will be almost same as the
NCCG, the proof of it will be almost same as NCCG except the cross
product
\circ
operator is replaced to
\merge
. The D&C generator
of (
25
) is then defined as

\displaystyle\mathit{nestedPerms}

\displaystyle\left(K,D,\left[\;\right]\right)=\left(\mathit{empty}\left(D\right),\left[\left[\left[\;\right]\right],\left[\>\right]^{K}\right]\right)

(26)

\displaystyle\mathit{nestedPerms}

\displaystyle\left(K,D,\left[x\right]\right)=\left(\mathit{single}\left(x,D\right),\left[\left[\left[\;\right]\right],\left[\>\right]^{K}\right]\right)

\displaystyle nestedPerms

\displaystyle\left(K,D,\mathit{xs}\cup\mathit{ys}\right)=\left(\mathit{setEmpty}\left(D,\mathit{pss}\right),\mathit{npss}\right),

where
\mathit{pss}=\mathit{kpermsAlg}\left(D,\mathit{pss}_{1},\mathit{pss}_{2}\right)
,
such that
\mathit{nestedPerms}\left(K,D,\mathit{xs}\right)=\left(\mathit{pss}_{1},\mathit{npss}_{1}\right)

and

\mathit{netedPerms}\left(K,D,\mathit{ys}\right)=\left(\mathit{css}_{2},\mathit{ncss}_{2}\right)
,
and
ncss
is defined as

\mathit{ncss}=\begin{cases}\left[\left[\left[\;\right]\right],\left[\>\right]^{K}\right]&!\left(D,\mathit{pss}\right)=\left[\;\right]\\
\mathit{kpermsAlg}_{3}\left(K,\mathit{kpermsAlg}_{3}\left(K,npss_{1},npss_{2}\right),\mathit{kperms}\left(K,!\left(D,\mathit{pss}\right)\right)\right)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(27)

where
\mathit{kpermsAlg}_{3}\left(K,\mathit{pss}_{1},\mathit{pss}_{2}\right)=\mathit{convol}\left(\cup,\merge,\mathit{pss}_{1},\mathit{pss}_{2}\right).

5
Conclusion

We have proposed several efficient D&C generators for producing
K
-combinations,
permutations, and nested combinations. In theory, these generators
can all be implemented to deliver the optimal efficiency on modern
hardware.

These generators have broad applications in combinatorial optimization
and exhaustive enumeration. For example, in the cell enumeration problem
in combinatorial geometry, the geometric analysis provided by
Gerstner and Holtz 2006

for enumerating cells of an arrangement
perfectly

matches

the
K
-combination generator
\mathit{kcombs}
. The way it organizes
combinations—grouping combinations of the same size into a single
list—perfectly aligns with the need to enumerate cells of an arrangement.
Although we have not yet conducted experiments to test the performance
of cell enumeration algorithms based on
\mathit{kcombs}
, it is
difficult to imagine whether any new approach in the future could
outperform this one in the task of cell enumeration. Our assumptions
regarding the superior efficiency stem from the perspective that this
geometric representation of hyperplanes is more efficient than the
classical one given by
Avis and Fukuda 1996
, which requires running
a linear program to determine the hyperplane. In contrast, our representation
relies only on matrix inversion, and the combination generator
\mathit{kcombs}

we propose is, by definition, could be the most efficient one available.

The nested combination generators,
\mathit{nestedCombs}
and
\mathit{nestedPerms}
,
also have promising applications in combinatorial optimization. Many
fundamental machine learning models can be expressed using combinatorial
structures involving nested combinations or permutations
(
He and Little 2025
;
He et al. 2025
;
He 2025
)
,
and thus can be optimized efficiently by adapting the combinatorial
generators introduced in this paper.

In theory, when carefully implemented on modern hardware, these generators
will be among the fastest available for producing these combinatorial
structures. Future work could include a comparative performance evaluation
in a controlled environment. Another promising direction is to incorporate
alternative Gray code orderings, beyond the revolving door method,
into the nested generators.
