Addressing migrant-specific barriers to accessing health services through primary health care in host countries in Latin American and the Caribbean
Authors: James Fitzgerald, Ernesto Báscolo, Gustavo Rosell de Almeida, Natalia Houghton, Rachel Jarboe, Julie Issa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT

The Region of Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC) encompasses 20 countries and seven territories stretching from Mexico to the southern tips of Argentina and Chile. It is a culturally rich terrain marked by significant bio- and sociocultural diversity, including approximately 50 million Indigenous People from 500 different ethnic groups with distinct languages and worldviews.1,2 However, LAC faces deep inequalities and vast disparities between social development and economic progress, including in healthcare systems. Moreover, migration has historically always existed in the region with current movements characterized by a south–south pattern within LAC2 combined with a south-north pattern from LAC, moving towards North America. Stark inequities have traditionally forced people to seek economic opportunities abroad yet recent crises, such as political and social upheavals in Haiti, Central America, and Venezuela, have driven large-scale migration. The case of Venezuela represents one of the largest exoduses in LAC's, and the world's recent history with over 7.7 million displaced citizens.2,3 Furthermore, environmental degradation and climate change have increasingly contributed to a substantial rise in migration as well as health risks for migrants in the Region, and it is predicted to accelerate in coming years.1

This complex mixture of interrelated factors negatively impacts an array of migrant determinants of health perpetuating and worsening situations of vulnerability. Additionally, it is important to highlight that many migrants include populations that are already in situations of vulnerability such as the poor, women and girls/adolescents, Indigenous Peoples, and the LGBTQI + community. The primary health care (PHC) approach is vital to protect migrant health as it offers comprehensive, accessible, community-based care that is key for managing both acute and chronic conditions.4 Mainstream healthcare models appear to be the most efficient, equitable and accessible for migrants although they may lack the capacity to adapt to situations of large-scale migration and reach migrants during public health crises.5 It is imperative to build strong and inclusive health systems based on a PHC approach with the perspective of attending to the unique health needs and challenges that migrants may face in accessing health services, in a manner that does not compromise the health of host populations.

Furthermore, regional data on unmet healthcare needs in the Americas reveal a complex landscape of challenges that have considerable implications for PHC and the overall health of populations, particularly migrants. The wide variation in unmet healthcare needs range from 9.9% to 73.3% across countries, underscoring severe inequalities in healthcare access.6 This disparity is particularly relevant for migrants, who often move from lower to higher-income countries seeking better opportunities. However, even in high-income countries, migrants may face significant barriers to accessing healthcare, contributing to the existing 23% of the population with unmet needs in these nations. Income disparities within countries further exacerbate the challenges faced by vulnerable populations, including many migrants, who are frequently in lower income brackets due to factors like language barriers and limited job opportunities and thus, disproportionally affected. The COVID-19 pandemic also highlighted the vulnerability of migrant populations during health crises and although civil society organizations, NGOs, and international organizations often fill in the gaps to attend to migrant access barriers,5, 6, 7 this points to the need for more inclusive and resilient healthcare systems, and policies that address specific migrant health needs.

Household survey data shows organisational barriers emerge as the most common cause of unmet healthcare needs in LAC, followed by financial barriers. For migrants, these barriers can be particularly daunting and significantly harder to overcome. For example, financial barriers can be especially challenging for migrants who may not have access to health insurance or may be working in low-wage, and often high-risk, jobs without benefits. Organisational barriers may include complex healthcare systems that are difficult to navigate, especially for migrants unfamiliar with the local language and customs and who lack interpretation and cultural mediation services. Aspects such as preferences for traditional medicines and language barriers have often been identified as major acceptability issues4,6,8 Migrants also often encounter stigma, discrimination and xenophobia which is further worsened and perpetuated by negative sociopolitical narratives and media discourse, also potentially leading to their abuse and limiting equitable treatment.9

Additionally, migrants, particularly the undocumented, struggle with legal or administrative constraints, insufficient information, and limited knowledge about navigating the health system and their rights.2,7,10,11 Building trust in a new healthcare system can be challenging for migrants especially if they have experienced discrimination, or fear that their immigration status may affect their access to care and even lead to their detention and/or deportation. Consequently, this could undermine trust in healthcare providers which is essential for healthcare access.2,6 Women from disadvantaged backgrounds face additional barriers related to financial constraints, distance to health facilities, and cultural norms that require permission to seek care. This intersectionality of access barriers, including ethnic and gender inequities, further complicates the situation for migrants.6 Such precarious conditions related to factors such as legal status, ethnicity, employment, gender, and living conditions severely impact migrant mental health and intersect to affect their health outcomes, overall.2

A key component of PHC for migrants is providing respectful care, void of any stigma or discrimination. For this, it is necessary to establish systems that offer culturally and linguistically adaptive services by providing appropriate material and culturally trained health professionals. This includes educating health professionals to treat migrants with dignity and communicate in a clear and empathetic manner, while also implementing protocols to address specific migrant health risks related to discrimination, among other factors.4,9 Providing health literacy programs for this population would also allow migrants to better navigate the system and understand their basic health rights, empowering them to advocate for themselves.6 Efforts to enhance respectful care and communication would build trust in health workers and the health system and increase access and acceptability for migrants.

Although many countries in the Region of the Americas have made progress towards achieving Universal Health based on a PHC approach, there are varying levels of advancement and challenges that continue to exist related to the inclusion of migrants. Some of these challenges include, for example, a lack of migrant health data limiting the development of strong policies and plans that effectively include migrant health needs, a shortage and poor distribution of qualified health workers at the PHC level, restricted financing in the health sector with competing health priorities, or limited intersectoral approaches necessary to address migration and health in a comprehensive manner. Inclusive and sustainable financing schemes to expand access to PHC services to migrants, such as the one implemented in Colombia (by far the largest receiving country of Venezuelan migrants), have shown to have had a positive impact on millions of migrants. Some multisectoral initiatives have also been implemented in the region, such as the “Operation Welcome” (Operação Acolhida) program in Brazil, for example, which provides support and access to services including and beyond healthcare such as education, work, and opportunities for pathways to more permanent residency, addressing related determinants of migrant health.5 Therefore, despite the challenges, these and several other countries in the region are taking robust measures to simultaneously meet both the local and migrant population basic health needs.

PHC-based systems play a crucial role in providing comprehensive and quality health services to migrants, which should be provided regardless of legal status. The design and implementation of these systems should emphasise cultural competence, addressing and overcoming common access barriers often faced by migrants such as acceptability and financial barriers. Addressing the unmet healthcare needs of migrants in the Americas requires a multifaceted approach that considers the complex interplay of economic, organizational, cultural, and social factors.6,9 Policymakers must reinforce targeted intersectoral approaches that recognize these broader social determinants of migrant health based on quality and reliable data, which includes social participation of both migrant and host populations and anticipates potential gaps in policy implementation.2,8, 9, 10 By including migrant health in all policies and strengthening PHC systems to be more inclusive, accessible, and responsive to the needs of diverse populations, including migrants, countries in the Americas can work towards reducing healthcare disparities and improving overall population health.
