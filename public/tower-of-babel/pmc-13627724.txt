Multilayer mesoporous vesosomes of block copolymer
Authors: Mian Wang, Jun Ho Hwang, Huangyan Shen, Xihuang Zheng, Sidan Tian, Senbin Chen, Hyeong Jun Kim, Weihua Li, Eunji Lee, Renhua Deng, Jintao Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultilayer mesoporous vesosomes uniquely combine hollow multilayer shells with ordered mesopores, providing a basis for multilevel gating, controlled release, and programmed reaction cascades, yet robust construction remains challenging. Herein, we report a molecular engineering strategy based on 3D-confined self-assembly to produce multilayer vesosomes with nearly hexagonally ordered shell perforations. Selective association of pentadecylphenol (PDP) and 1,5-dibromopentane (DBP) with the poly(4-vinylpyridine) block precisely tunes block-copolymer volume fraction to stabilize an unconventional perforated lamellar morphology, while reversible hydrogen bonding enables region-selective pore generation. Experiments and simulations reveal cooperative roles of confinement and hydrogen bonding in directing morphological evolution, with DBP further refining assembly through electrostatic and cross-linking interactions. The resulting multilayer mesoporous vesosomes feature a pyridine-rich corona and show iodine capture (1.26 g g−1), good recyclability, and faster uptake than nonporous multilayer analogues. This work offers a versatile route to complex hollow architectures and clarifies key determinants governing their formation.Subject terms: Self-assembly, Supramolecular polymers, Polymers

Introduction

Vesicles are closed aqueous micro-compartments formed by the self-assembly of amphiphilic molecules1–5. To surpass the functional limitations of conventional simple vesicles, the concept of the vesosome was introduced in the mid-1990s, inspired by biological multivesicular bodies6. These hierarchical assemblies are primarily categorized into two topologies: (i) nested vesosomes, where an outer lumen encapsulates multiple independent smaller vesicles derived from trapped aqueous droplets, forming a disordered, multicompartment architecture; and (ii) multilayer vesosomes, characterized by onion-like shells separated by liquid spacers7,8. While early research prioritized lipid-based nested systems to minimize leakage, multilayer architectures have gained prominence9,10. Their stacked interfacial barriers and tunable interlayer spacing not only maximize cargo retention and stability but also enable compartmentalized loading and programmed release11–13. This combination of structural robustness and functional versatility establishes them as superior platforms for advanced drug delivery and nanoreactors14,15.

Self-assembly of amphiphilic block copolymers (BCPs) serves as a versatile platform for constructing multilayer vesosomes16–18. In many systems, these hierarchical architectures emerge under kinetically controlled, non-equilibrium conditions: variations in selective-solvent composition and exchange protocols drive morphological transitions, eventually trapping the shells as chain mobility becomes progressively restricted19,20. Representative examples include onion-like multilayer aggregates formed by poly(tert-butyl acrylate)-block-poly(2-vinylpyridine) (PtBA-b-P2VP) in acidic aqueous media, which demonstrate the critical role of ionization state in regulating curvature and stacking21. Similarly, phase-diagram and pathway studies on model systems such as polystyrene-block-poly(acrylic acid) (PS-b-PAA) reveal that the accessibility of multilayer morphologies is strictly governed by solvent quality, block architecture, and solvent-exchange kinetics22–24. More recently, other advanced fabrication strategies have been developed25. For instance, polymerization-induced self-assembly (PISA) provides a direct route to onion-like multilayer structures26–28, while liquid-solid interfacial fusion assembly (LSIFA) enables the precise fabrication of uniform and tunable uni- and multilayer vesosomes29. Despite these advances, the walls of multilayer vesosomes in reported systems are typically non-mesoporous. A key next objective is to engineer multilayer vesosomes in which each shell incorporates stable, ordered, through-going mesopores. Such an architecture would preserve the structural benefits of stacked barriers while facilitating rapid mass transport and improving interfacial accessibility. Conceptually, this target structure aligns with the perforated-lamella morphology, effectively bridging a multilayer framework with permeable transport channels. Realizing this specific phase motif within a multishelled architecture represents a critical frontier in vesicular design.

In this context, emulsion-based three-dimensional confined self-assembly (3D-CSA) offers a promising platform by directing BCP organization inside droplets under geometric confinement coupled to solvent evaporation/solidification, a setting that naturally favors particles with well-defined nanostructures30–37. In some cases, these droplet-derived microparticles can be converted into porous structures via self-emulsification or post-processing methods, such as selective swelling38–47. Previous studies using 3D-CSA have successfully yielded closed multilayer vesosomes without mesopores, including hydrogen-bonding-assisted multilayer particles, BCP capsules with structure-dependent release and stimuli-responsive photonic microparticles48–51. Unlike conventional solution assembly, the phase-separation behavior in droplets parallels that in bulk systems, which theoretically renders the perforated-lamella accessible52–57. However, accessing the target perforated-lamella phase in vesosomes remains elusive due to its intrinsic instability. Theory and simulation suggest that the perforated-lamella state occupies an extremely narrow thermodynamic window58–61, so its experimental realization typically demands stringent control over the assembly pathway. In practice, the hollow framework and shell curvature must be maintained while the nascent through-pore network is stabilized against the thermodynamic driving forces that otherwise promote relaxation into simpler lamellar or cylindrical states. As a result, experimental demonstrations of multilayer vesosomes featuring ordered, through-going mesopores have remained scarce over the past two decades.

Here, we demonstrate the fabrication of multilayer vesosomes with ordered mesopores via an emulsion-based 3D-CSA strategy, empowered by synergistic molecular engineering (Fig. 1). We combine reversible hydrogen bonding from 3-pentadecylphenol (PDP) and cross-linking from 1,5-dibromopentane (DBP) to program the volume fraction and the effective packing parameter (peff) of polystyrene-block-poly(4-vinylpyridine) (PS-b-P4VP), thereby steering its assembly into the elusive perforated-lamella morphology. Subsequent selective removal of PDP generates near-hexagonal mesopores across every shell while exposing pyridinic interfaces, yielding a unique architecture visualized by 3D electron tomography. Experiments and SCFT modeling together elucidate the cooperative roles of molecular packing control and spatial confinement in stabilizing the perforations. The proposed mechanism is further evidenced by the controlled synthesis of ABn monolayer mesoporous vesicles (n = 1–8) and the capture of evolutionary intermediates. The resulting multilayer mesoporous vesosomes, featuring a reactive P4VP corona, exhibit an iodine uptake capacity of 1.26 g g⁻1, 81.6% capacity retention over repeated cycles, and faster uptake kinetics than onion-like multilayer vesosomes without mesopores.

Results

Synthesis and structure of multilayer mesoporous vesosomes

Ordered multilayer mesoporous vesosomes were prepared by emulsion-solvent evaporation driven 3D-CSA. In order to precisely tune the packing parameter required for the unconventional morphology, perforated-lamella, a molecular engineering strategy by combining side-group grafting and cross-linking was adopted. First, the BCP PS409-b-P4VP171 (73.9 wt% PS) was dissolved in a good solvent (e.g., chloroform), and PDP was added to form comb-like supramolecules, denoted as PS-b-P4VP(PDP)1.2, via hydrogen bonding to the pyridine units. DBP was then introduced as a crosslinker to yield PS-b-P4VP(PDP)1.2/DBP0.4, where 1.2 and 0.4 denote the molar ratios of PDP and Br equivalents (from DBP) to pyridine units, respectively. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) of the PS-b-P4VP(PDP)1.2/DBP0.4 complex (Supplementary Fig. 1, see Supplementary Information) revealed critical interactions: the characteristic pyridine ring vibration of non-bonded P4VP at 1598.9 cm−1 underwent distinctive peak splitting into doublet signals at 1600.2 cm−1 and 1589.2 cm−1 upon PDP binding, confirming hydrogen-bond formation. Concurrently, the emergence of a new absorption band at 1662.5 cm−1 provided direct evidence for quaternization between DBP’s halogens and P4VP’s nitrogen atoms. The PS-b-P4VP(PDP)1.2/DBP0.4 solution was emulsified into an aqueous polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) solution to generate emulsion droplets, which produced BCP microparticles48 with microphase structures through solvent (chloroform) evaporation.

A key step is the reversibility of PDP-P4VP hydrogen-bonding in ethanol/water, which enables selective swelling and removal of PDP without dissolving the PS-rich framework48. The mobility of the PS block at room temperature is limited because of its poor solubility (in ethanol) and high glass transition temperature (Tg), which is important for the generation of a stable frame for the mesoporous vesosomes. Thus, the ethanol-induced structural evolution mainly arises from selective swelling of the P4VP-domain and PDP extraction within the preorganized multilayer framework. In parallel, partial cross-linking by DBP furnishes sufficient fixation of the P4VP domains to retain curved lamellae and the multishell framework during swelling. As PDP is released, the polar domains selectively disassemble/reorganize, transforming perforated lamellae into mesoporous lamellae. Thus, the synergy of reversible supramolecular association and mild cross-linking under 3D confinement yields multilayer mesoporous vesosomes. Their accessible pore/shell interfaces are enriched in P4VP/pyridyl groups, while the inner domains are mainly PS (Fig. 2a).

The TEM image (Fig. 2b) reveals a multishelled architecture in which every shell presents a periodic in-plane fine structure, with shell thickness h1 = 37.1 ± 3.2 nm and in-layer period d1 = 71.9 ± 2.4 nm. In agreement, the SEM image (Fig. 2c) resolves hexagonally arranged surface pores with pore diameter l₀= 22.5 ± 1.7 nm and in-layer period d2 = 72.5 ± 3.6 nm; the close match d1 ≈ d2 indicates that the TEM contrast reflects the pore lattice observed by SEM, and that the periodic motif is intrinsic to each shell rather than an imaging artifact. Furthermore, elemental mapping (Fig. 2d) demonstrates uniform distributions of C, N and Br across individual particles; accordingly, the pyridinic nitrogen lining the mesopores provides accessible sites for post-functionalization. In addition, nitrogen adsorption-desorption (Supplementary Fig. 2) exhibits a type-IV isotherm, confirming mesoporosity. The multilayer perforated vesosomes exhibit a much higher Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) specific surface area (125.89 m2 g−1) than the multilayer vesosomes without mesopores (11.27 m2 g−1), consistent with the open, interconnected through-pores of perforated lamellae versus the more closed multilamellae in onion structures (Fig. 2e). The sorption-derived pore size is ≈ 17 nm, smaller than l0, which is consistent with differences between gas-phase models and real pore geometry, potential drying-induced shrinkage/partial closure, and the 3D interconnection of through-pores in perforated lamellae that broadens the apparent distribution. The pore size can also be tuned by varying the molecular weight of the BCP. When PS409-b-P4VP171 was replaced with PS586-b-P4VP200 under otherwise identical conditions, multilayer mesoporous vesosomes with a larger pore diameter of 24.4 ± 3.2 nm were obtained (Supplementary Fig. 3). The in-layer periods measured from SEM and TEM images also increased to 73.1 ± 4.5 nm and 74.0 ± 2.5 nm, respectively.

A TEM tilt series was acquired from −60° to +56° (Movie S1), and representative raw frames are shown in Fig. 3a. The aligned series was reconstructed tomographically (Movie S2), yielding a 3D volume in which xy-orthoslices extracted at successive z-positions (Fig. 3b) reveal a shell-by-shell repetition of the same ordered porous motif through the particle thickness. Thus, the contrast modulation that defines the in-plane lattice persists along the z-axis, indicating that the pores traverse the shell rather than terminating internally. Orthogonal projections of the full volume (top, bottom, and side views; Supplementary Fig. 4) further corroborate the radial continuity of the multilayer framework. For spatial registry, we separately reconstructed the outermost shell and the interior domains and then merged the volumes (Fig. 3c), showing that the shells are nested concentrically and share a common pore-lattice orientation over a large angular sector. To interrogate connectivity, serial reslices were generated through the 3D volume (Movies S3–S5), which provide a more faithful interpretation than single orthoplanes; complementary orthoslice views along the x, y, and z axes (Movies S6–S8) confirm continuous, percolating mesoporosity within each shell. Taken together, these observations show that each particle consists of a nested, multilayer skeleton in which every shell contains hexagonally arranged through-pores, consistent with an ordered perforated-lamella architecture. In further support, an SEM image of a partially fractured (incomplete) vesosome directly exposes the internal perforated lamellar shells (Supplementary Fig. 5), providing complementary real-space evidence for the multilayer, through-pore framework.

Formation mechanism of multilayer mesoporous vesosomes

Control of packing-parameter by hydrogen-bonded side groups and cross-linking

To elucidate the formation mechanism of multilayer mesoporous vesosomes, the effect of introducing DBP was first assessed. Notably, the presence of two halogen atoms in a single DBP molecule allows it to quaternize two pyridine groups, serving as a crosslinker within the P4VP microdomain. Thus, DBP plays a dual role: quaternization increases the polarity of P4VP-rich domains and contributes to packing-parameter regulation, while cross-linking stabilizes the perforated lamellar framework during ethanol treatment and PDP extraction. Although earlier studies identify DBP as a cross-linker that enhances the stability of P4VP domains62, its specific role in adjusting the packing parameter remained unclear. Accordingly, microparticle morphologies with and without DBP were compared under otherwise identical 3D-CSA conditions, together with their morphologies after ethanol treatment (Fig. 4a–f). In the absence of DBP, the supramolecular PS-b-P4VP(PDP)1.2 self-assembles under identical 3D-CSA conditions into onion-like microparticles (Fig. 4b), which can yield multilayer vesosomes without mesopores (Fig. 4c). By contrast, in the PS-b-P4VP(PDP)1.2/DBP0.4 system, centrifugation in different solvents gives clearly distinguishable outcomes: in ethanol, the particles readily form a visible pellet, whereas in water they remain largely suspended and no pellet is observed (Supplementary Fig. 6). UV-vis of the ethanol supernatant confirms PDP removal after centrifugation (Supplementary Fig. 7), supporting supramolecular swelling/removal as the origin of pore space. TEM images of the water-treated particles reveal that the originally onion-like lamellae become locally perforated, with P4VP-rich domains penetrating into the PS-rich shells (Fig. 4e). Subsequent ethanol treatment further enlarges these perforations and promotes the coalescence of internal voids into 3D-interconnected mesopores and hollow interiors, ultimately yielding multilayer mesoporous vesosomes (Fig. 4f). Notably, light intermolecular cross-linking between P4VP segments constrains interfacial chain splay and suppresses local area fluctuations, effectively lowering the interfacial area per chain (a0↓)63. At the same time, quaternization increases the polarity of P4VP and its affinity toward ethanol, so that a larger P4VP-rich volume is retained during selective swelling and PDP removal (v↑). Within the qualitative framework of the packing parameter, we therefore consider an effective packing parameter peff = v/(a0 lc): the coupled v↑ and a0↓ shifts move the system beyond the symmetric-lamella window (peff ≈ 1) into the perforated-lamella regime under droplet confinement, enabling multilayer mesoporous vesosomes rather than onion-like stacks63.

Additionally, supramolecular interactions of bottlebrush polymers with hydrogen-bonding side groups frequently give rise to distinctive self-assembly behaviors64, and open the possibility of tuning the effective packing parameter (peff). FT-IR first quantifies the extent of supramolecular association. As the PDP/P4VP molar ratio x increases, the pyridine ring vibration at ca. 1598.8 cm−1 shifts progressively (Fig. 4g) and approaches a plateau at x ≈ 2.0. From x = 0.6 to x = 3.0, the position of this band moves monotonically, reflecting increased hydrogen-bond loading. At x = 1.2, the shift remains below the saturation level, whereas the spectra at x = 2.0 and x = 3.0 are nearly identical, indicating that hydrogen-bond saturation is essentially reached by x ≈ 2.0. This FT-IR trend thus provides an experimental handle on the hydrogen-bond loading for tuning domain volumes under 3D-CSA. In parallel, the particle morphologies evolve coherently with x: starting from spherical and cylindrical states at low x, passing through lamellae and semi-perforated intermediates, and reaching ordered perforated-lamella shells near x ≈ 1.2; at higher x, inverse-type structures (e.g., inverse cylinders/hexosomes) emerge as peff > 1 becomes dominant. These coupled trends are summarized in Fig. 4h as a conceptual mapping linking x (hydrogen-bond loading) to an increasing P4VP(PDP)-rich effective volume fraction (fP4VP(PDP)) and a corresponding upward shift in peff, highlighting the narrow composition window for forming multilayer mesoporous vesosomes with perforated lamellae. The basic structural units and how x steers the effective packing are summarized in the schematic of PS-b-P4VP(PDP)x/DBP0.4, while representative experimental outcomes are consistent with the trends already established in the TEM survey (Supplementary Fig. 8).

A narrow window near the perforation regime also yields hierarchical byproducts featuring perforated outer shells and helical inner columns; their yield and 3D confirmation are documented in Supplementary Figs. 9, 10 and Movies S9 and S10. Occasional incomplete particles further reveal the nested (Supplementary Fig. 11), inside-out stacking of the hierarchical framework. The emergence of typical hexosome structures (Supplementary Fig. 12) further confirms the effectiveness of packing-parameter control in this system. Unlike the multilayer mesoporous vesosomes with ordered surface mesopores (Fig. 1c), these hexosome structures show clear cylindrical domains in SEM images and no continuous perforated lamellar shell. Notably, the multilayer perforated region at x = 1.2, with a PS volume fraction (fPS) of ≈ 0.38, falls within the perforation-favoring window extracted from theory. Finally, the block ratio r = Mn(PS)/Mn(P4VP) imposes an additional constraint: when r approaches or falls below unity, systems at x > 1 yield inverse spherical, non-porous particles rather than perforated lamellae (Supplementary Fig. 13 and Table S2), underscoring that supramolecular loading (x) and molecular architecture (r) must be co-tuned.

The incorporation of PDP triggers supramolecular assembly with P4VP via hydrogen bonding, leading to significant volumetric expansion of the P4VP-PDP complex. This results in a phase volume inversion where the P4VP-PDP phase surpasses PS in volume fraction, despite the higher molecular weight of PS. Effect of confinement degree

Here, we therefore investigate the confinement effect as a governing parameter and use the droplet size (or equivalently R/Rg) to program pore multiplicity within a single perforated shell, thereby bridging the monolayer mesoporous vesicle family to the multilayer mesoporous vesosomes formed at larger R. These ABn-type monolayer mesoporous vesicles are mainly observed in smaller particles under stronger confinement, where only one perforated shell can be accommodated. They represent the strong-confinement limit of perforated-lamella formation and provide a simplified structural model for understanding how curvature and packing frustration regulate pore formation in confined vesosomal architectures. Figure 5a schematizes the confinement pathway during solvent evaporation: varying the initial emulsion droplet radius R tunes the effective confinement strength (R/Rg), which in turn dictates how many perforations can be accommodated within a monolayer shell. Representative TEM images (Fig. 5b) reveal a systematic series of ABn-type monolayer mesoporous vesicles spanning single-pore, nanoring, double-, triple-, four-, five-pore, seven-pore and eight-pore states (denoted S1, S2, S2', S3, S4, S5, S7, and S8). Additional examples demonstrating the prevalence and diversity of these structures are provided in Supplementary Fig. 14.

Compared with the multilayer mesoporous vesosomes in Fig. 2 (weaker confinement), the ABn monolayer mesoporous vesicles form under stronger confinement (Fig. 5c). Consistently, the vesicle radius increases monotonically from 50.8 ± 2.6 nm to 93.1 ± 2.8 nm as the pore number grows from 1 to 8 (Fig. 5d), indicating that larger cavities allow the shell to host more perforations. The pore diameter is 22.3 ± 2.5 nm, and the pore-wall thickness is 38.3 ± 3.1 nm. In addition, several particles have noncircular (anisotropic) boundaries, as indicated by the yellow dashed circles in Supplementary Fig. 15. The outer edge deviates from a circle (slightly elongated, faceted, or undulated), which is consistent with a competition between interfacial area minimization and in-plane stretching or packing penalties. To rationalize these patterns, Fig. 5e correlates the pore number n with the predicted inter-pore angle(s) θ and presents the corresponding molecular models for each ABn topology, which indicate the underlying point arrangement and nearest-neighbor connectivity.

Complementing the experiment, we performed SCFT-based calculations under spherical confinement using the framework employed elsewhere in this work, with the confinement size (cavity radius R) explicitly fixed. By scanning x and R/Rg, the simulations yield twelve distinct structures spanning the experimentally observed sequence from single-pore to multipore states (up to nine pores). The resulting free-energy landscape (Fig. 5f) and the simulated structural progression (Fig. 5g) jointly rationalize confinement-selected pore multiplicity. Specifically, Fig. 5f presents the Helmholtz free-energy difference for vesicles with different pore numbers at various confinement levels R/Rg. ΔF/(nkBT) exhibits a non-monotonic dependence on pore number: introducing pores initially lowers the free energy by releasing packing frustration and better accommodating curvature, but beyond an intermediate pore multiplicity, the free energy increases again due to the rapidly growing interfacial area and in-plane stretching penalties. Consequently, each confinement level features an optimal pore-number window, consistent with the experimentally observed discretized pore multiplicities. Consistent with this energetic picture, Fig. 5g summarizes the simulated self-assembly pathway under increasing confinement, showing how the system evolves among competing ABn topologies as R/Rg varies. The simulated structures and pore multiplicities closely match the TEM/SEM observations in Fig. 5b, while a few additional simulated states (e.g., S2′, S5′) appear only rarely or transiently in experiments, consistent with a narrow kinetic window at high confinement. Taken together, the theory provides direct evidence that confinement selects pore multiplicity and geometry: as R decreases, the system cannot build many shells and instead minimizes free energy by multiplying perforations within a single shell. This ABn monolayer family represents the strong-confinement limit of the same mechanism that, at larger R, yields multilayer perforated lamellae. Therefore, the monolayer mesoporous vesicles provide a simplified structural model for understanding how perforated lamellae nucleate, accommodate curvature, and further develop into multilayer mesoporous vesosomes when the confinement R increases.

Beyond morphology, the multilayer perforated system exhibits simple, predictive geometric-scaling relations that link confinement (droplet size), shell number, and pore multiplicity. These relations arise from pore-packing geometry under spherical curvature and provide compact formulas that anticipate how structure evolves as size changes. Experimentally, multilayer mesoporous vesosomes with different sizes and layer numbers are readily observed. Figure 6a schematically illustrates vesosomes with varying shell numbers and defines the key geometric parameters. Figure 6b shows representative TEM and SEM images of multilayer mesoporous vesosomes with 2–6 shells. A broader survey (Fig. 6c) indicates that the shell number increases with the vesosome radius, from 109.8 ± 6.7 nm to 340.5 ± 14.2 nm. As established above, the shell number in multilayer mesoporous vesosomes is tunable. Although the manual membrane-emulsification method used here cannot yet achieve uniform size control, this limitation could be addressed in the future by optimizing SPG membrane emulsification.

Adsorption performance of multilayer mesoporous vesosomes

In addition, the multilayer mesoporous vesosomes expose abundant pyridinic groups continuously along both the external surfaces and internal pore walls, providing chemically accessible binding sites for functional species65. In addition to this chemical functionality, their perforated-lamella architecture offers a pronounced structural advantage: as shown above, the multilayer mesoporous vesosomes possess a much higher specific surface area than multilayer vesosomes without mesopores, implying more efficient transport through abundant mesoporous pathways. Taking iodine adsorption as an example, Fig. 7a schematically illustrates the fundamental difference in mass-transfer behavior. This structural distinction is directly reflected in the adsorption behavior. Upon exposure to iodine vapor at 25 °C, a thin film of multilayer mesoporous vesosomes rapidly darkens (Fig. 7b), indicating fast interfacial uptake. The uptake kinetics further quantify this advantage (Fig. 7c): at identical sorbent mass, the multilayer vesosomes reach an adsorption capacity of 0.67 g g−1 within 30 min, whereas the control group achieves only 0.41 g g−1 over the same period. Importantly, the final capacity is up to 1.26 g g−1, and the multilayer mesoporous vesosomes also retain 81.6% of their initial capacity after five adsorption-desorption cycles (Fig. 7d), confirming good structural stability and regenerability. Aqueous adsorption experiments using KI3 solutions further highlight the kinetic benefit of the perforated multilayer architecture (Fig. 7e). At equal sorbent mass, both materials reduce the KI3 concentration from 100 mg L−1 to approximately 2.45 mg L−1 (≈97.6% removal). This terminal concentration (~2.45 mg L−1) is near the UV-vis quantification limit (Supplementary Fig. 16). However, the multilayer mesoporous vesosomes reach near-complete depletion within ~4 h, whereas the control group requires ~8 h to reach a comparable residual concentration. Overall, these results demonstrate that embedding ordered mesopores within multilayer shells enables faster iodine uptake without sacrificing ultimate capacity. The combination of high surface accessibility and efficient transport pathways establishes multilayer mesoporous vesosomes as a structurally optimized platform for rapid iodine capture, and suggests broader potential for designing hierarchical sorbents where kinetics is the critical performance metric.

Discussion

In summary, we have successfully realized the synthesis of multilayer vesosomes with ordered mesopores by innovatively combining 3D-CSA with a molecular engineering strategy. Our systematic investigation, supported by theoretical simulations, reveals that the cooperative effect of hydrogen bonding and cross-linking is the key determinant for the formation of the unconventional perforated-lamella morphology. The utility of these multilayer mesoporous vesosomes was demonstrated through their application as high-performance adsorbents. With a rich, accessible P4VP corona, the multilayer mesoporous vesosomes deliver 1.26 g g−1 iodine uptake and 81.6% retention over five cycles, while exhibiting faster uptake kinetics than non-mesoporous multilayer vesosomes. This work not only provides a practical and versatile pathway to complex ordered hollow multishelled architectures but also clarifies the critical factors governing their formation. Improved size uniformity can be achieved in the future by optimizing the membrane emulsification technique. We anticipate that the resulting architecture will uniquely integrate two highly desirable structural features. Such a hierarchical framework is expected to enable multilevel gating, hierarchical controlled release, and programmed reaction cascades.

Methods

Materials

PS409-b-P4VP171 (Mn = 69 kg/mol, Mw/Mn = 1.15), PS586-b-P4VP200 (Mn = 82 kg/mol, Mw/Mn = 1.04), PS94-b-P4VP95 (Mn = 19.8 kg/mol, Mw/Mn = 1.08) and PS163-b-P4VP466 (Mn = 66 kg/mol, Mw/Mn = 1.15) were purchased from Polymer Source, Inc. Their molecular weights and compositions were determined by supplier-provided GPC/SEC and 1H NMR data, respectively. For PS409-b-P4VP171, the PS weight fraction was 73.9 wt%. Poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA, average Mw = 13-23 kg mol−1, 87–89% hydrolyzed) was purchased from Aldrich. 3-Pentadecylphenol (PDP, purity >90%), 1,5-dibromopentane (DBP, purity 98%) was purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Chloroform (analytical grade), iodine (I2, purity 99.7%), and potassium iodide (KI, purity 99.0%) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Absolute ethanol (EtOH, purity 99%) was purchased from Shanghai Titan Scientific Co., Ltd. Deionized water (Millipore Milli-Q grade) with a resistivity of 18.0 MΩ was used in all the experiments. Materials were used without further purification except for PDP. PDP was purified by recrystallization in n-hexane.

Preparation of initial BCP Microparticles by 3D-CSA

PS-b-P4VP and PDP were separately dissolved in chloroform to prepare stock solutions with concentrations of 10.0 mg mL−1. For a typical preparation of PS-b-P4VP(PDP)1.2/DBP0.4 microparticles, 0.5 mL of the PS-b-P4VP solution, containing 5.0 mg of PS409-b-P4VP171, was mixed with 0.45 mL of the PDP solution, containing 4.53 mg of PDP. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 h to form PS-b-P4VP(PDP)1.2 supramolecules, where the subscript represents the molar ratio of PDP to pyridine units of P4VP. DBP was prediluted by chloroform to 10 vol% before addition. The amount of DBP was controlled to provide bromine atoms at 0.4 equivalents relative to the pyridine units of P4VP, corresponding to 0.60 mg of DBP. The mixture was further stirred at room temperature for at least 4 h to obtain the PS-b-P4VP(PDP)1.2/DBP0.4 solution. Then, 0.1 mL of the mixed solution was emulsified with 1.0 mL of PVA aqueous solution (5.0 mg mL−1) by using a membrane-extrusion emulsification technique. The emulsion was collected in an open vial (10 mL), and microparticles were generated after natural evaporation of chloroform for at least 48 h at 30 °C.

Preparation of multilayer mesoporous vesosomes

Approximately 100 μL of the above-obtained microparticle dispersion (containing approximately 1.0 mg of microparticles) was mixed with 900 μL of ethanol. The mixture was shaken for approximately 30 s to ensure homogeneous mixing and then centrifuged (14000 r min−1 for 10 min). Afterwards, the precipitate was dispersed in ethanol by brief ultrasonication for approximately 30 s and centrifuged to remove the supernatant. This ethanol washing step was repeated twice. The whole ethanol treatment process was performed at room temperature. Finally, both PVA and PDP were removed, yielding multilayer mesoporous vesosomes.

Preparation of monolayer mesoporous vesicles

The preparation of monolayer mesoporous vesicles is similar to that of multilayer mesoporous vesosomes, except that the membrane-extrusion emulsification step for forming the initial BCP microparticles requires an increased number of passes, at least 30. Here, one pass is defined as one single passage of the emulsion through the membrane in the membrane-extrusion emulsification device.

Preparation of multilayer vesosomes without mesopores

The procedure was identical to that used for preparing the multilayer mesoporous vesosomes, except that DBP was not added and the ethanol-treatment step was extended. Specifically, 100 μL of the obtained initial BCP microparticle dispersion was centrifuged to remove the supernatant, and the precipitate was then immersed in 1 mL of ethanol for 24 h at room temperature.

Iodine adsorption test

For iodine vapor adsorption, 5 mg of microspheres was placed in a sealed container with excess iodine crystals without direct contact and kept at 25 °C. At selected times, the sample was weighed after removing surface iodine crystals to determine the iodine uptake. For KI3 solution adsorption, 5 mg of microspheres was added to 0.2 g L−1 KI3 aqueous solution and kept at 25 °C. At selected times, the supernatant was collected after centrifugation and analyzed by UV-vis spectroscopy to determine the residual KI3 concentration, adsorption capacity, and removal efficiency.

Characterization

The internal structure of the particles was observed by using a Hitachi HT7700 transmission electron microscope (TEM) operated at an accelerating voltage of 100 kV. Element mapping was measured by an energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) by using a Talos F200X high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) operated at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. The TEM models for 3D reconstruction of microparticles were JEM-1400/JEM-2100 (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), and refinement of the initial alignment and tomographic reconstruction of the tilt series were performed in IMOD. 3D visualization of the final volumes was carried out in UCSF Chimera and Amira-Avizo Software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The surface morphology of the particles was observed by using a Hitachi SU8010 scanning electron microscope (SEM) operated at an acceleration voltage of 1.0 kV. Before TEM and SEM characterization, the samples were stained with iodine vapor for 1 h. UV-vis spectra were detected by spectrophotometers (TU-1810 and UV-2700). The specific surface area and pore size were analyzed by using an ASAP 2420-4MP automatic specific surface area and porosity analyser. Hydrogen-bonding and quaternization interactions were analyzed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR, Bruker VERTEX 70).

Theoretical modeling via SCFT

Free energy functional and wall density profile

To predict the equilibrium morphologies of the supramolecular BCP system in spherical confinement, we employed self-consistent field theory (SCFT) under the Gaussian chain approximation. The experimental system was coarse-grained as an AB diblock copolymer with C blocks grafted onto the B block, where the A, B, and C blocks represent PS, P4VP, and PDP/DBP-associated side groups, respectively. The volume fraction of the A block was fixed at fA = 0.74, corresponding to the PS fraction of PS409-b-P4VP171 used in the experiment. The segregation strengths were set as χABN = χACN = 25 and χBCN = 0, where χ is the Flory-Huggins interaction parameter. The volume fraction of the C block (fC) was varied to represent the PDP/P4VP molar ratio x. The above theoretical predictions successfully identified eighteen distinct morphological families arising from spherical confinement self-assembly. Six additional phases emerge in the PS-b-P4VP(PDP)x/DBP0.4 system as x varies: spherical phase microspheres, two cylindrical phase variants, lamellar structures exhibiting either budding or onion-like layering, and multilayer mesoporous vesosomes (perforated lamellar microparticles). Twelve configurations correspond to ABn-type monolayer mesoporous vesicles exhibiting progressive pore multiplication: from monoporous (single pore) and nanoring architectures through biporous and triporous intermediates, culminating in structures with nine symmetrically arranged pores. This evolutionary sequence reflects confinement-driven entropy minimization as cavity dimensions contract. Quantitative agreement with experimental characterization was observed across all predicted architectures. This concordance validates the SCFT parameterization and establishes confinement geometry and copolymer composition as orthogonal control axes for hierarchical microstructure engineering.
