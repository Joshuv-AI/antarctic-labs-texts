Abstract
In this paper, we propose a random gradient-free method for optimization in infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces, applicable to functional optimization in diverse settings. Though such problems are often solved through finite-dimensional gradient descent over a parametrization of the functions, such as neural networks, an interesting alternative is to instead perform gradient descent directly in the function space by leveraging its Hilbert space structure, thus enabling provable guarantees and fast convergence. However, infinite-dimensional gradients are often hard to compute in practice, hindering the applicability of such methods. To overcome this limitation, our framework requires only the computation of directional derivatives and a pre-basis for the Hilbert space domain, i.e., a linearly-independent set whose span is dense in the Hilbert space. This fully resolves the tractability issue, as pre-bases are much more easily obtained than full orthonormal bases or reproducing kernels — which may not even exist — and individual directional derivatives can be easily computed using forward-mode scalar automatic differentiation. We showcase the use of our method to solve partial differential equations à la physics informed neural networks (PINNs), where it effectively enables provable convergence.



1
Introduction

Core to many applications is the solution of functional optimization problems; formally speaking, these are of the form

\argmin_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{R}(h),

(1)

where
\mathcal{H}
is a separable Hilbert space (e.g.,
L^{2}
space, or a Sobolev space), and
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
is a Fréchet differentiable risk functional, often convex.
Prominent examples include machine learning tasks such as supervised learning and generative modeling, optimization-based PDE solvers and broader inverse problems.

To solve problems like (
1
), practitioners typically introduce a finite-dimensional parametrization
\theta\mapsto h_{\theta}
of the functions
h\in\mathcal{H}
and then optimize over
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
using some variant of gradient descent.
This approach, however, is limited by the representation capacity of the chosen parametrization; moreover, because the parametrization is typically nonlinear (e.g., neural networks), even if
\mathcal{R}
is convex, the composition
\theta\mapsto\mathcal{R}(h_{\theta})
is most likely not, increasing the complexity of the resulting optimization problem.
To overcome these issues, we perform gradient-based optimization
directly in the function space

\mathcal{H}
, since its Hilbert space structure gives the existence of gradients as Riesz representers of Fréchet derivatives.
By doing so, we may retain convexity of the risk and also avoid the limited representation power of any specific parametrization.

However, gradient-based optimization in infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces is challenging.
For one, functional gradients themselves are often hard to compute exactly, being defined as solutions to variational problems involving the directional derivatives of the risk.
Secondly, it is not generally clear how to represent infinite dimensional objects computationally.
Thirdly, in the realm of stochastic gradient methods, controlling the variance of an infinite dimensional random element usually requires more care than that of a finite dimensional random vector.

In this paper, we adopt the functional optimization perspective, but we bypass exact gradient computation by taking inspiration from random gradient-free optimization methods.
This is a class of algorithms that build stochastic gradient estimators based only on directional derivative computations, which are easily available through forward mode automatic differentiation.
We adapt this framework to be applicable in infinite dimensions, yielding a general algorithm for minimizing Fréchet differentiable risk functionals over separable Hilbert spaces, with convergence guarantees in the convex case.

1.1
A motivating example: solving PDEs through gradient descent

Consider solving the following PDE with Dirichlet boundary conditions:

\begin{cases}L[u](\boldsymbol{x})=0\quad&\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega,\\
u(\boldsymbol{x})=f(\boldsymbol{x})\quad&\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(2)

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a bounded, open set with a smooth boundary and
L
is an
\ell
-th order differential operator.
For simplicity, in this section we will assume that
L
is a linear operator with bounded coefficients.
Recently, a fast growing body of research
[
2
,
15
,
14
,
19
]
has been dedicated to study deep learning algorithms that find approximate solutions to (
2
).
These algorithms work by minimizing a functional of the form

\mathcal{R}(h)=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert L[h]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\lVert h-f\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2},

(3)

where
h=h_{\theta}
is taken to be a neural network with fixed architecture, parameterized by
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
for some large
D\in\mathbb{N}
.
The idea is to minimize
\mathcal{R}(h_{\theta})
over
\theta
through variants of gradient descent in the parameter space.

We propose to see (
3
) as acting directly on functions, as opposed to a loss over finite-dimensional parameters.
Our main motivation for doing so is preserving the convexity of the risk when
L
is linear.
1
1

1

If
L
is nonlinear we may lose convexity, but still obtain a problem that is simpler than what a non-linear parametrization would result in.

This is also aligned with a recent surge in the
first optimize, then discretize
paradigm in the scientific ML literature, cf.
[
10
,
11
]
, which tries to emulate functional optimization algorithms when optimizing a parametric ansatz
h_{\theta}
to solve (
2
).
The functional perspective has also influenced recent works in statistical inverse problems
[
6
]
and nonparametric instrumental variable regression
[
5
]
.

To proceed, we must first specify the domain
\mathcal{H}
of the risk
\mathcal{R}
.
Due to the
nature of problem (
2
), we take
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{H}^{\ell}(\Omega)
,
the Sobolev space of order
\ell
and exponent
2
in
\Omega
— that is, the space of all
h\in L^{2}(\Omega)
whose weak derivatives exist up to order
\ell
and lie in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, which we endow with the usual inner product
\langle h,h^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{\ell}(\Omega)}~=~\sum_{j}\langle D^{(j)}h,D^{(j)}h^{\prime}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
.
2
2

2

We denote the
j
-th derivative of
h
by
D^{(j)}h
, which we interpret as a
d^{j}
-dimensional array of real numbers.

Thus, solving the PDE (
2
) becomes the optimization problem

\argmin_{h\in\mathbb{H}^{\ell}(\Omega)}\mathcal{R}(h),

(4)

where
\mathcal{R}
is given by (
3
).

To solve this problem under the functional paradigm, the most direct approach would be to
employ gradient descent in
\mathbb{H}^{\ell}(\Omega)
.
By definition, if
\mathcal{R}
is Fréchet differentiable, the gradient
\nabla\mathcal{R}(h)
is the unique element of
\mathbb{H}^{\ell}(\Omega)
satisfying
\langle\nabla\mathcal{R}(h),v\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{\ell}(\Omega)}=D\mathcal{R}(h;v)
for all
v\in\mathbb{H}^{\ell}(\Omega)
, where

D\mathcal{R}(h;v)=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{R}(h+\delta v)-\mathcal{R}(h)}{\delta}

(5)

is the directional derivative of
\mathcal{R}
at
h
in the direction
v
.
Thus, to obtain
\nabla\mathcal{R}(h)
we first need to compute
D\mathcal{R}(h;v)
, which is straightforward due to the linearity of
L
:

D\mathcal{R}(h;v)=\int_{\Omega}L[h](\boldsymbol{x})L[v](\boldsymbol{x})\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}+\int_{\partial\Omega}(h(\boldsymbol{x})-f(\boldsymbol{x}))v(\boldsymbol{x})\hskip 1.3pt\sigma(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}),

where
\sigma
is the surface measure on
\partial\Omega
.

We can now see that the functional
D\mathcal{R}(h;\cdot)
is linear and continuous in the
\mathbb{H}^{\ell}(\Omega)
-norm.
Furthermore, the map
h\mapsto D\mathcal{R}(h;\cdot)
, from
\mathbb{H}^{\ell}(\Omega)
to its dual, is also continuous, implying that
\mathcal{R}
is Frechét differentiable
[
13
]
.
Therefore, the gradient
\nabla\mathcal{R}(h)
is well defined, and finding it is equivalent to solving the following problem:

\displaystyle\text{Find $g\in\mathbb{H}^{\ell}(\Omega)$ such that}

(6)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\int_{\Omega}D^{(j)}g(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot D^{(j)}v(\boldsymbol{x})\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=\int_{\Omega}L[h](\boldsymbol{x})L[v](\boldsymbol{x})\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}+\int_{\partial\Omega}(h(\boldsymbol{x})-f(\boldsymbol{x}))v(\boldsymbol{x})\hskip 1.3pt\sigma(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}),

\displaystyle\text{for all $v\in\mathbb{H}^{\ell}(\Omega)$}.

This is a variational problem for which no general closed form solution exists, creating the need to look beyond exact gradients in order to solve (
4
).

1.2
Background: random gradient-free methods

To avoid the computation of exact gradients, an existing branch of the literature constructs “random gradient-free methods,” which leverage stochastic gradient estimators based on computations of directional derivatives.
Hence, though these methods are not derivative-free in a strict sense, they are gradient-free.
So far, this approach has only been considered for
finite dimensional
optimization problems. In infinite dimensions, existing methods exhibit prohibitive limitations.

If
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
, then random gradient-free methods estimate the gradient
g\triangleq\nabla\mathcal{R}(h)
as

\widehat{g}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}D\mathcal{R}(h;v_{m})v_{m},

(7)

where
v_{1},\ldots,v_{M}
are i.i.d. vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with zero mean and identity covariance matrix, cf.
[
12
]
.
This is thus an unbiased estimator of
g
that, under additional assumptions on
v
, has finite second moment:
\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert\widehat{g}\right\rVert^{2}]<+\infty
. For example, in the convenient case where
v_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})
, one may show that
\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert\widehat{g}\right\rVert^{2}]=\lVert g\rVert^{2}\left(\frac{M+d+1}{M}\right)
.
This setup already exhibits a couple of issues that may arise when
\mathcal{H}
is high/infinite dimensional:

(i)

The expression for
\mathbb{E}[\lVert\widehat{g}\rVert^{2}]
presented above suggests taking the sample size
M
to be proportional to
d
. As
d\to+\infty
, this becomes infeasible.

(ii)

Many of the usual choices for the distribution of the
v_{i}
’s have no infinite-dimensional analogue: there is no standard Gaussian distribution
[
3
]
, no uniform measure over the sphere
[
24
]
, and no Rademacher distribution (since sequences of
\pm 1
’s are not square-summable).
These are distributions used, for example, in
[
8
,
12
,
20
]
for the finite dimensional scenario.

(iii)

More broadly, if a random element
v
in an infinite dimensional Hilbert space satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}]<+\infty
, then it necessarily has a compact covariance operator, which thus cannot be the identity — a necessary condition for the unbiasedness of
\widehat{g}
in (
7
).
In fact, since eigenvalues of compact operators concentrate around zero
[
18
, Theorem 4.25]
, we have
\left\lVert T-I\right\rVert\geq 1
for any compact operator
T:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
.

(iv)

Generally speaking, it is difficult to design unbiased gradient estimators
\widehat{g}
whose second moment is guaranteed to be finite for any gradient
g
.

In this paper, we resolve these issues by proposing a novel random gradient-free algorithm that is suitable for infinite-dimensional optimization.
By first sampling a finite dimension
K
and then sampling random directions
v_{i}
from a specific
K
-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{H}
, we are able to overcome issues
(ii)
and
(iii)
;
then, by choosing the sample size
M
proportional to the random dimension, we bypass issue
(i)
.
Finally, by introducing an appropriate preconditioning of the gradient descent process we are able to control the variance of our gradient estimates, thus resolving issue
(iv)
.

Remark 3

Note that if

Proof.

From (

For any distribution

over

\frac{t_{i}}{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[t_{K}\mid K\geq i]\leq t_{i},\quad\text{for all}\quad i\in\mathbb{N}.

Proof.

The upper bound is obvious, since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[t_{K}\mid K\geq i]=\sum_{j=i}^{\infty}t_{j}\frac{\mathbb{P}[K=j]}{t_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t_{i}}\sum_{j=i}^{\infty}t_{j}(t_{j}-t_{j+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t_{i}}\sum_{j=i}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}\big(t_{j}^{2}-t_{j+1}^{2}\big)+\frac{1}{2}(t_{j}-t_{j+1})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{t_{i}}\sum_{j=i}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}\big(t_{j}^{2}-t_{j+1}^{2}\big)=\frac{t_{i}^{2}}{2t_{i}}=\frac{t_{i}}{2},

3.1
Formal setup and parameters

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded open set with a boundary smooth enough to admit a trace operator

5

5

We recall that the trace operator is the proper extension of the “restriction to the boundary” operator over continuous functions to Sobolev spaces.
It exists, for example, if the boundary of
\Omega

[
7

\Trace:\mathbb{H}^{1}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\partial\Omega)

For the sake of concreteness, we consider general PDEs with Dirichlet boundary conditions:
6
6

6

Other types of boundary conditions, such as Neumann or periodic boundary conditions, can be treated analogously.

\begin{cases}L[u](\boldsymbol{x})=0&\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega,\\
u(\boldsymbol{x})=f(\boldsymbol{x})&\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\Lambda\subset\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(21)

Here, the boundary condition is interpreted in the trace sense and

is a, possibly non-affine,
\ell

L[u](\boldsymbol{x})=F(\boldsymbol{x},u(\boldsymbol{x}),Du(\boldsymbol{x}),\ldots,D^{(\ell)}u(\boldsymbol{x})),

We assume that

for all

This is easily checked if

\mathcal{R}(h)=\frac{1}{2}\lVert L[h]\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{2}\lVert(\Trace[h]-f)\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)},

(22)

We will also consider the case of nonlinear

To be applied in this setting, our framework requires a pre-basis

To obtain one, we will take advantage of the theory of Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHSs) and their smoothness properties.
Recall the Matérn kernel

16

\mathrm{Matern}_{\nu,\eta}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}\nu\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\right\rVert}{\eta}\right)^{\nu}K_{\nu}\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}\nu\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\right\rVert}{\eta}\right),\qquad\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\Omega,

\Gamma
is the usual

\Gamma

When

16

Proof.

The proof is given in
Section

A.5

∎

So, as long as we have a countable set of points

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced a random gradient-free method for optimization in infinite-dimensional separable Hilbert spaces, avoiding the computational challenges common to functional gradient descent.
By using only directional derivatives and a pre-basis, our approach offers a practical and broadly applicable framework for solving functional optimization problems.
We believe that our work expands the applicability of functional gradient methods, particularly to settings with specialized losses and for which functional gradients would need to be computed accordingly.
In the future it would be interesting to consider variants such as constrained, mirror and accelerated versions of our algorithm.

A.1
Proof of
Proposition

1

The fact that
\mathbb{E}[v\mid K]=0
is obvious from

\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{z}\mid K]=\mathbf{0}

To show that

\mathbb{E}[\langle v,h\rangle]=0

This is easily obtained if we use the tower property of conditional expectations to compute

Lemma 2

For any

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{\langle h,e_{i}\rangle^{2}}{t_{i}}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathbf{1}\{K\geq i\}\langle h,e_{i}\rangle^{2}}{t_{i}}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

Proof of
Proposition

3

The fact that
\mathcal{B}
forms a linearly independent set follows directly from the positive-definiteness of the kernel
\mathrm{Matern}_{\nu,\eta}
.
To show
\mathcal{B}
has dense span, the main result we will use is the following theorem, which is an easy consequence of
[
16
, Equation (4.15)]
,
[
23
, Corollary 10.48]
(see also the discussion before Corollary 11.33) and the extension theorem for fractional Sobolev spaces
[
9
, Theorem A.4]
:

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathrm{Matern}_{\nu,\eta}
be the Matérn kernel on
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with smoothness parameter
\nu
, and let
s=\nu+d/2
.
Then, the RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{\nu,\eta}
generated by
\mathrm{Matern}_{\nu,\eta}
is equivalent to
\mathbb{H}^{s}(\Omega)
, meaning that
\mathcal{H}_{\nu,\eta}=\mathbb{H}^{s}(\Omega)
as a set of functions and their norms are equivalent: there exist constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
such that

c_{1}\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}^{s}(\Omega)}\leq\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\nu,\eta}}\leq c_{2}\left\lVert h\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}^{s}(\Omega)}\quad\text{for all}\quad h\in\mathcal{H}_{\nu,\eta}.

Now we will prove
Proposition

3
.
We start with some notation: If
(\mathcal{E},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathcal{E}})
is a given normed space and
E\subset\mathcal{E}
, we denote by
\closure(E;\mathcal{E})
the closure of
E
with respect to
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\mathcal{E}}
.
In this notation, we want to show that, under the assumptions of
Proposition

3
, we have
\closure(\spann\mathcal{B};\mathbb{H}^{\ell}(\Omega))=\mathbb{H}^{\ell}(\Omega)
.
The idea will be to show that this closure actually contains a Sobolev space of order at least
\ell
, which will be dense in
\mathbb{H}^{\ell}(\Omega)
.
We divide the proof into four steps:

First, let
s=\nu+d/2
, which is greater or equal to
\ell
, by assumption.
We claim that
\closure(\spann\mathcal{B},\mathbb{H}^{\ell}(\Omega))\supset\closure(\spann\mathcal{B},\mathbb{H}^{s}(\Omega))
.
Indeed, since the inclusion
\mathbb{H}^{s}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathbb{H}^{\ell}(\Omega)
is continuous
[
9
, Theorem 3.27]
, convergence in
\mathbb{H}^{s}(\Omega)
-norm implies convergence in
\mathbb{H}^{\ell}(\Omega)
-norm.
Next, by
Theorem

4
, we have
\closure(\spann\mathcal{B},\mathbb{H}^{s}(\Omega))=\closure(\spann\mathcal{B},\mathcal{H}_{\nu,\eta})
.
Thirdly, let
\bar{\mathcal{B}}=\{\Phi(\boldsymbol{x})\}_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega}
.
We then have
\closure(\spann\mathcal{B},\mathcal{H}_{\nu,\eta})=\closure(\spann\bar{\mathcal{B}},\mathcal{H}_{\nu,\eta})=\mathcal{H}_{\nu,\eta}
.
Indeed, for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega
, let
\{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be such that
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\to\boldsymbol{x}
as
i\to+\infty
.
Now compute:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathrm{Matern}_{\nu,\eta}(\boldsymbol{x},\cdot)-\mathrm{Matern}_{\nu,\eta}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i},\cdot)\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\nu,\eta}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathrm{Matern}_{\nu,\eta}(\boldsymbol{x},\cdot),\mathrm{Matern}_{\nu,\eta}(\boldsymbol{x},\cdot)\rangle+\langle\mathrm{Matern}_{\nu,\eta}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i},\cdot),\mathrm{Matern}_{\nu,\eta}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i},\cdot)\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad-2\langle\mathrm{Matern}_{\nu,\eta}(\boldsymbol{x},\cdot),\mathrm{Matern}_{\nu,\eta}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i},\cdot)\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Matern}_{\nu,\eta}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x})+\mathrm{Matern}_{\nu,\eta}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})-2\mathrm{Matern}_{\nu,\eta}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{i}),

where we have used the reproducing property of
\mathcal{H}_{\nu,\eta}
in the last step.
Now, note that
\mathrm{Matern}_{\nu,\eta}
is continuous in
\Omega\times\Omega
, which implies that
\lim\mathrm{Matern}_{\nu,\eta}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})=\lim\mathrm{Matern}_{\nu,\eta}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})=\mathrm{Matern}_{\nu,\eta}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x})
, thus
\left\lVert\mathrm{Matern}_{\nu,\eta}(\boldsymbol{x},\cdot)-\mathrm{Matern}_{\nu,\eta}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i},\cdot)\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\nu,\eta}}^{2}
goes to zero as
i\to+\infty
.
This proves the first equality.
The second one is the known fact that an RKHS can be constructed as the closure of the
\spann
of the image of the feature map with respect to the RKHS norm, see
[
23
, Section 10.2]
.
Finally, we have shown that
\mathbb{H}^{s}(\Omega)=\mathcal{H}_{\nu,\eta}\subset\closure(\spann\mathcal{B},\mathbb{H}^{\ell}(\Omega))
.
Now, recall that since
\Omega
is open, bounded and has a Lipschitz boundary, we know that
C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})
is a dense subset of
\mathbb{H}^{t}(\Omega)
for every
t\geq 0

[
9
, Theorem 3.29]
.
Therefore,
\closure(\mathbb{H}^{s}(\Omega),\mathbb{H}^{\ell}(\Omega))\supset\closure(C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega}),\mathbb{H}^{\ell}(\Omega))=\mathbb{H}^{\ell}(\Omega)
.

A.6
Proof of
Proposition

4

Start by noting that

\lvert\tilde{H}(x,p)\rvert\leq x^{2}+B\bar{c}^{2}+\bar{c}\left\lvert p\right\rvert

(39)

for any
x,p\in\mathbb{R}
.
Hence, if
h\in\mathbb{H}^{1}(\Omega)
, then the map
(t,x)\mapsto\tilde{H}(x,\partial_{x}h(t,x))
is a member of
L^{2}(\Omega)
, implying that
\mathcal{R}(h)
is well-defined for any
h\in\mathbb{H}^{1}(\Omega)
.
Furthermore, it is easy to see that
\tilde{H}
is concave in
p
, implying that
\mathcal{R}
is not a convex function of
h
.

To show
\mathcal{R}
is Fréchet differentiable, we first note that the partial derivative of
\tilde{H}
with respect to
p
is given by

\partial_{p}\tilde{H}(x,p)=\begin{cases}-\frac{p}{2B},\text{ if }\left\lvert p\right\rvert\leq 2B\bar{c},\\
-\bar{c}\sgn(p),\text{ if }\left\lvert p\right\rvert>2B\bar{c},\end{cases}

where
\sgn(p)
is the sign function, that is,
\sgn(p)=p/\left\lvert p\right\rvert
for
p\neq 0
and
0
for
p=0
.
Thus,
\partial_{p}\tilde{H}(x,p)
is continuous and bounded by
\bar{c}
in absolute value.
Now we take arbitrary
h,u\in\mathbb{H}^{1}(\Omega)
and
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
to compute

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(h+\delta u)

\displaystyle\hskip 8.5359pt=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{-\bar{x}}^{\bar{x}}\big[\partial_{t}[h+\delta u](t,x)+\tilde{H}(x,\partial_{x}[h+\delta u](t,x))\big]^{2}\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}x\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\bar{x}}^{\bar{x}}\big[[h+\delta u](t,x)-Gx^{2}\big]^{2}\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 8.5359pt=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{-\bar{x}}^{\bar{x}}\big[\partial_{t}[h+\delta u](t,x)+\tilde{H}(x,\partial_{x}h(t,x))+\delta\hskip 1.3pt\partial_{p}\tilde{H}(x,p_{\delta}(t,x))\hskip 1.3pt\partial_{x}u(t,x)\big]^{2}\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}x\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\bar{x}}^{\bar{x}}\big[[h+\delta u](t,x)-Gx^{2}\big]^{2}\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}x,

where
p_{\delta}(t,x)
lies between
\partial_{x}h(t,x)
and
\partial_{x}[h+\delta u](t,x)
, and is due to the Mean Value Theorem.
Now we expand:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(h+\delta u)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\mathcal{R}(h)+\delta\Bigg\{\int_{0}^{T}\int_{-\bar{x}}^{\bar{x}}\big[\partial_{t}h(t,x)+\tilde{H}(x,\partial_{x}h(t,x))\big]

\displaystyle\hskip 170.71652pt\cdot\bigg[\partial_{t}u(t,x)+\partial_{p}\tilde{H}(x,p_{\delta}(t,x))\partial_{x}u(t,x)\bigg]\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}x\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt+\int_{-\bar{x}}^{\bar{x}}[h(t,x)-Gx^{2}]u(t,x)\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}x\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\hskip 48.36958pt+\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\Bigg\{\int_{0}^{T}\int_{-\bar{x}}^{\bar{x}}[\partial_{t}u(t,x)+\partial_{p}\tilde{H}(x,p_{\delta}(t,x))\partial_{x}u(t,x)]^{2}\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}x\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt+\int_{-\bar{x}}^{\bar{x}}u(t,x)^{2}\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}x\Bigg\},

obtaining:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{R}(h+\delta u)-\mathcal{R}(h)}{\delta}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{-\bar{x}}^{\bar{x}}\big[\partial_{t}h(t,x)+\tilde{H}(x,\partial_{x}h(t,x))\big]

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt\cdot\bigg[\partial_{t}u(t,x)+\partial_{p}\tilde{H}(x,p_{\delta}(t,x))\partial_{x}u(t,x)\bigg]\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}x\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\int_{-\bar{x}}^{\bar{x}}[h(t,x)-Gx^{2}]u(t,x)\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\frac{\delta}{2}\left\{\int_{0}^{T}\int_{-\bar{x}}^{\bar{x}}[\partial_{p}\tilde{H}(x,p_{\delta}(t,x))\partial_{x}u(t,x)]^{2}\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}x\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}t+\int_{-\bar{x}}^{\bar{x}}u(t,x)^{2}\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}x\right\}.

Thus, since
\partial_{p}\tilde{H}
is bounded in absolute value by
1
and also continuous, we can apply dominated convergence as we let
\delta\to 0
to get

\displaystyle D\mathcal{R}(h;u)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{R}(h+\delta u)-\mathcal{R}(h)}{\delta}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{-\bar{x}}^{\bar{x}}\big[\partial_{t}h(t,x)+\tilde{H}(x,\partial_{x}h(t,x))\big]

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\cdot\bigg[\partial_{t}u(t,x)+\partial_{p}\tilde{H}(x,\partial_{x}h(t,x))\partial_{x}u(t,x)\bigg]\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}x\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\int_{-\bar{x}}^{\bar{x}}[h(t,x)-Gx^{2}]u(t,x)\hskip 1.3pt\mathrm{d}x.

Again, since
\partial_{p}\tilde{H}
is bounded and
\tilde{H}
satisfies (
39
), we conclude that

is a bounded linear functional on
\mathbb{H}^{1}(\Omega)
for any
h\in\mathbb{H}^{1}(\Omega)
.
Furthermore, by using Cauchy-Schwarz as well as the fact that both
\tilde{H}
and
\partial_{p}\tilde{H}
are Lipschitz continuous in
p
, one may show that the map
h\mapsto D(h;\cdot)
, from
\mathbb{H}^{1}(\Omega)
to its dual, is also continuous.
This implies the Fréchet differentiability of
\mathcal{R}
