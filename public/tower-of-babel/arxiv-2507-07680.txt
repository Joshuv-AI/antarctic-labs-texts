Temporal modulation of second harmonic generation in ferroelectrics by a pulsed electric field
Atsushi Ono

Abstract
We revisit the relationship between electric polarization modulation Δ ​ P \Delta P in ferroelectrics induced by a low-frequency pulsed electric field and the corresponding second harmonic intensity modulation Δ ​ I SH \Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}} . Using nonlinear response theory, we derive their time-domain expressions linear in the pulse amplitude, revealing that not only the electric field but also its time derivative contributes to Δ ​ I SH \Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}} . Furthermore, even when the time-derivative component is negligible, Δ ​ I SH \Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}} can be in antiphase with the pulsed field and thus with Δ ​ P \Delta P . These theoretical predictions are further supported by real-time simulations of a model for electronic ferroelectrics. Our results demonstrate that the commonly assumed relation Δ ​ P ∝ Δ ​ I SH \Delta P\propto\Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}} can break down under certain conditions, reflecting the complex-valued and frequency-dependent nature of nonlinear dynamical susceptibilities.

In the quest for dynamic control of symmetry and order in condensed matter, SHG now plays an increasingly pivotal role.

To analyze SHG signals, the following phenomenological expression has been widely used not only under equilibrium conditions but also in nonequilibrium photoexcited states:

\displaystyle P_{i}(2\omega)=\sum_{jk}\tilde{\chi}_{ijk}^{(2)}(2\omega;\omega,\omega)E_{j}(\omega)E_{k}(\omega).

(1)

Here,
E_{i}(\omega)
and
P_{i}(2\omega)
denote the
i
-component of the probe electric field at frequency
\omega
and the induced polarization at frequency
2\omega
, respectively, and
\tilde{\chi}_{ijk}^{(2)}
is the second-order susceptibility tensor.
The intensity of SHG is proportional to the square of
P_{i}(2\omega)
.
The term “phenomenological” is used here because
\tilde{\chi}_{ijk}^{(2)}
coincides with the microscopic response function derived from time-dependent perturbation theory only when the probe electric field is strictly monochromatic (i.e., a continuous wave).

Even when the probe is approximately monochromatic, additional approximations are often made in discussing the polarization modulation
\Delta P
induced by a pump pulse
E_{\mathrm{pump}}
(indices omitted for simplicity).
It is commonly assumed that the second-order susceptibility changes as
\tilde{\chi}^{(2)}\to\tilde{\chi}^{(2)}+\Delta\tilde{\chi}^{(2)}
, with
\Delta\tilde{\chi}^{(2)}\propto E_{\mathrm{pump}}
, and hence that the resulting change in the second harmonic intensity is

\displaystyle\Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}}\propto\bigl[\tilde{\chi}^{(2)}+\Delta\tilde{\chi}^{(2)}\bigr]^{2}-\bigl[\tilde{\chi}^{(2)}\bigr]^{2}\approx 2\tilde{\chi}^{(2)}\Delta\tilde{\chi}^{(2)}\propto E_{\mathrm{pump}}.

(2)

Since
\Delta P\propto E_{\mathrm{pump}}
, one then infers that
\Delta P\propto\Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}}
.
In this picture, the contribution of
\Delta\tilde{\chi}^{(2)}\sim\tilde{\chi}^{(3)}E_{\mathrm{pump}}
, where
\tilde{\chi}^{(3)}
is the third-order susceptibility, is effectively absorbed into
\tilde{\chi}^{(2)}
.
However
\Delta\tilde{\chi}^{(2)}
should, in principle, be evaluated as a convolution of
\tilde{\chi}^{(3)}
with the full spectra of the probe and pump fields, so it is not obvious that
\Delta\tilde{\chi}^{(2)}
remains strictly in phase with
E_{\mathrm{pump}}
.
This subtlety appears to have received little attention in the literature, except for a pioneering study by Timm and Bennemann
Timm and Bennemann 2004
.

In this paper, we revisit SHG that is temporally modulated by a pulsed electric field whose frequency is much lower than the probe frequency.
In Sec.

, we derive a general expression for
\Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}}
based on microscopic nonlinear response theory.
In Sec.
III
, we validate this expression using real-time simulations of a model ferroelectric system and demonstrate cases in which the relation
\Delta P\propto\Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}}
fails.
Sections
IV
and
V
provide the discussion and summary, respectively.
In Appendix, we present the spectral representation of the nonlinear susceptibility used for a quantitative comparison between theory and simulation.

II
General considerations

We consider the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(t)=\mathcal{H}_{0}+\mathcal{V}(t),

(3)

where
t
is time,
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is the unperturbed Hamiltonian, and
\mathcal{V}(t)
is the time-dependent perturbation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(t)=-E(t)P,

(4)

with
E(t)
the external electric field and
P
the electric polarization operator.
The expectation value of the electric polarization is given by

\displaystyle\langle P\rangle(t)=\Tr[\rho(t)P],

(5)

where
\rho(t)
is the density matrix of the system.

According to time-dependent perturbation theory, the
n
th-order response of the polarization to the electric field is expressed as

\displaystyle\langle P\rangle^{(n)}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}t_{n}\,\chi^{(n)}(t;t_{1},\dots,t_{n})E(t_{1})\cdots E(t_{n}).

(6)

Here, the
n
th-order susceptibility
\chi^{(n)}
is defined by

\displaystyle\chi^{(n)}(t;t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}^{n}\varTheta(t-t_{1})\varTheta(t_{1}-t_{2})\cdots\varTheta(t_{n-1}-t_{n})

\displaystyle\quad\times\left\langle[[\cdots[[\hat{P}(t),\hat{P}(t_{1})],\hat{P}(t_{2})],\cdots],\hat{P}(t_{n})]\right\rangle_{0},

(7)

where
\langle\bullet\rangle_{0}=\Tr[\rho(-\infty)\bullet]
is the expectation value in the initial state,
\varTheta
is the unit step function, and
\hat{P}
is the interaction-picture operator defined by

\displaystyle\hat{P}(t)=\mathcal{U}_{0}(t,-\infty)^{\dagger}P\mathcal{U}_{0}(t,-\infty),

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{0}(t,-\infty)=\mathcal{T}\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathcal{H}_{0}\,\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\right),

(9)

with
\mathcal{T}
the time-ordering operator.
Throughout this paper, we set the Dirac constant
\hbar=1
.
Since Eq. (
7
) is invariant under time translation,

\displaystyle\chi^{(n)}(t;t_{1},\dots,t_{n})=\chi^{(n)}(t+T;t_{1}+T,\dots,t_{n}+T)

(10)

for any
T
, we introduce an
n
-variable susceptibility:

\displaystyle\chi^{(n)}(\bar{t}_{1},\dots,\bar{t}_{n})=\chi^{(n)}(0;-\bar{t}_{1},\dots,-\bar{t}_{n}),

(11)

where
\bar{t}_{i}=t-t_{i}
for
i=1
to
n
.
The
n
th-order susceptibility in the frequency domain is given by

\displaystyle\chi^{(n)}(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\bar{t}_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}\bar{t}_{n}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{1}^{+}\bar{t}_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{n}^{+}\bar{t}_{n}}\chi^{(n)}(\bar{t}_{1},\dots,\bar{t}_{n}),

(12)

with
\omega_{i}^{+}=\omega_{i}+\mathrm{i}\eta
, where
\eta>0
is a broadening factor.
Since
\hat{P}
is Hermitian,
\chi^{(n)}
in Eq. (
12
) satisfies the relation

\displaystyle\chi^{(n)}(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})=\chi^{(n)}(-\omega_{1},\dots,-\omega_{n})^{*}.

(13)

The Fourier transformation of
\langle P\rangle^{(n)}(t)
yields

\displaystyle\langle P\rangle^{(n)}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\langle P\rangle^{(n)}(t)

(14)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}\omega_{n}}{(2\pi)^{n-1}}\delta(\omega_{1}+\cdots+\omega_{n}-\omega)

\displaystyle\quad\times\chi^{(n)}(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})E(\omega_{1})\cdots E(\omega_{n}),

(15)

where
\delta
represents the delta function.
Unlike Eq. (
1
), Eq. (
15
) is valid for arbitrary
E(\omega)
.

In what follows, we discuss the temporal modulations in the polarization and the second harmonic (SH) intensity induced by the following external electric field:

\displaystyle E(t)=E_{\mathrm{IR}}\cos(\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}t)+E_{\mathrm{THz}}(t),

(16)

where the first and second terms represent an infrared (IR) probe and a terahertz (THz) pump, respectively.
In the frequency domain, the electric field is expressed as

\displaystyle E(\omega)

\displaystyle=E_{\mathrm{IR}}\pi[\delta(\omega-\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})+\delta(\omega+\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})]+E_{\mathrm{THz}}(\omega).

(17)

II.1
Electric polarization

Setting
E_{\mathrm{IR}}=0
and substituting Eq. (
17
) into Eq. (
15
), we obtain the well-known expression for the polarization change linear in
E_{\mathrm{THz}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\langle P\rangle^{(1)}(\omega)=\chi^{(1)}(\omega)E_{\mathrm{THz}}(\omega).

(18)

When
E_{\mathrm{THz}}(\omega)
is sharply peaked around
\omega=0
,
\chi^{(1)}(\omega)
can be approximated by its value at
\omega=0
, yielding

\displaystyle\langle P\rangle^{(1)}(\omega)\approx\chi^{(1)}(\omega=0)E_{\mathrm{THz}}(\omega).

(19)

Since
\chi^{(1)}(\omega=0)
is real-valued, as follows from Eq. (
13
), there is no phase delay between the polarization and the pulsed field.
Accordingly, in the time domain, we arrive at the relation

\displaystyle\Delta P(t)\equiv\langle P\rangle^{(1)}(t)\propto E_{\mathrm{THz}}(t).

(20)

II.2
Second harmonic generation

We now consider the second-order response of the IR probe under the THz pump pulse described by Eq. (
16
).
For the response in the absence of the pump pulse, by substituting Eq. (
17
) into Eq. (
15
) and extracting the dominant contributions near
\omega=\pm 2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle P^{(2)}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{4}\bar{\chi}^{(2)}(\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})E_{\mathrm{IR}}^{2}\delta(\omega-2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\pi}{4}\bar{\chi}^{(2)}(-\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},-\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})E_{\mathrm{IR}}^{2}\delta(\omega+2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}).

(21)

Similarly, the pump-induced response linear in
E_{\mathrm{THz}}
can be expressed in terms of the third-order susceptibility as

\displaystyle P^{(3)}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\bar{\chi}^{(3)}(\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},\omega-2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})E_{\mathrm{IR}}^{2}E_{\mathrm{THz}}(\omega-2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{8}\bar{\chi}^{(3)}(-\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},-\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},\omega+2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})E_{\mathrm{IR}}^{2}E_{\mathrm{THz}}(\omega+2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}).

(22)

In Eqs. (
21
) and (
22
), we define the symmetrized susceptibility as

\displaystyle\bar{\chi}^{(n)}(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})=\sum_{s\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\chi^{(n)}(\omega_{s(1)},\dots,\omega_{s(n)}),

(23)

where
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
represents the symmetric group of degree
n
.

The far field of the electric dipole radiation, which is proportional to the second derivative of
P(t)
, is defined as follows (up to a multiplicative constant):

\displaystyle E_{\infty}(t)=-\ddot{P}(t),\quad E_{\infty}(\omega)=\omega^{2}P(\omega).

(24)

By substituting Eq. (
17
) into Eq. (
24
), we obtain

\displaystyle E_{\infty}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{4}E_{\mathrm{IR}}^{2}\Bigl[(2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})^{2}\bar{\chi}^{(2)}(\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})\delta(\omega-2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})

\displaystyle\quad+(-2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})^{2}\bar{\chi}^{(2)}(-\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},-\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})\delta(\omega+2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{8}E_{\mathrm{IR}}^{2}\Bigl[\omega^{2}\bar{\chi}^{(3)}(\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},\omega-2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})E_{\mathrm{THz}}(\omega-2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})

\displaystyle\quad+\omega^{2}\bar{\chi}^{(3)}(-\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},-\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},\omega+2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})E_{\mathrm{THz}}(\omega+2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})\Bigr].

(25)

To simplify notation, we introduce the following shorthand:

\displaystyle\bar{\chi}_{2}

\displaystyle=\bar{\chi}^{(2)}(\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}),

(26)

\displaystyle\bar{\chi}_{3}

\displaystyle=\bar{\chi}^{(3)}(\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},0),

(27)

\displaystyle\bar{\chi}_{3}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\partial\bar{\chi}^{(3)}(\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},\nu)}{\partial\nu}\right|_{\nu=0}.

(28)

The third-order susceptibility is then expanded around
\omega=\pm 2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}
as

\displaystyle\bar{\chi}^{(3)}(\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},\omega-2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})

\displaystyle\approx\bar{\chi}_{3}+(\omega-2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})\bar{\chi}_{3}^{\prime},

(29)

\displaystyle\bar{\chi}^{(3)}(-\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},-\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},\omega+2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})

\displaystyle=\bar{\chi}^{(3)}(\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},-\omega-2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})^{*}

\displaystyle\approx\bar{\chi}_{3}^{*}-(\omega+2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})\bar{\chi}_{3}^{\prime*},

(30)

where we have used Eq. (
13
).
By performing the inverse Fourier transformation of
E_{\infty}(\omega)
and neglecting second- and higher-order derivatives of
E_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)
, we obtain the time-domain expression for the far field:

\displaystyle E_{\infty}(t)

\displaystyle=\biggl\{\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}^{2}|\bar{\chi}_{2}|\cos(\phi_{2})+\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}^{2}|\bar{\chi}_{3}|\cos(\phi_{3})E_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)-\bigl[\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}|\bar{\chi}_{3}|\sin(\phi_{3})+\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}^{2}|\bar{\chi}_{3}^{\prime}|\sin(\phi_{3}^{\prime})\bigr]\dot{E}_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)\biggr\}E_{\mathrm{IR}}^{2}\cos(2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}t)

\displaystyle\quad+\biggl\{\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}^{2}|\bar{\chi}_{2}|\sin(\phi_{2})+\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}^{2}|\bar{\chi}_{3}|\sin(\phi_{3})E_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)+\bigl[\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}|\bar{\chi}_{3}|\cos(\phi_{3})+\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}^{2}|\bar{\chi}_{3}^{\prime}|\cos(\phi_{3}^{\prime})\bigr]\dot{E}_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)\biggr\}E_{\mathrm{IR}}^{2}\sin(2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}t),

(31)

where
\phi_{2}=\arg\bar{\chi}_{2}
,
\phi_{3}=\arg\bar{\chi}_{3}
, and
\phi_{3}^{\prime}=\arg\bar{\chi}_{3}^{\prime}
.

The appearance of the time derivative of
E_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)
in Eq. (
31
) can be attributed to two distinct origins.
The first arises from the fact that the far field
E_{\infty}(t)
is proportional to the second derivative of the polarization, as shown in Eq. (
24
).
Specifically, the term
\omega^{2}E_{\mathrm{THz}}(\omega\mp 2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})
in Eq. (
25
) leads to time derivatives of
E_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)
through its inverse Fourier transformation:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\omega^{2}E_{\mathrm{THz}}(\omega\mp 2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\omega\mp 2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})t^{\prime}}E_{\mathrm{THz}}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\mp 2\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}t}\bigl[4\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}^{2}E_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)\pm 4\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}\dot{E}_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)-\ddot{E}_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)\bigr].

(32)

The second origin is the frequency dependence of the third-order susceptibility
\bar{\chi}^{(3)}(\pm\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},\pm\omega_{\mathrm{IR}},\omega\mp 2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})
.
When
\chi^{(3)}
is expanded around
\omega=\pm 2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}
, as in Eqs. (
29
) and (
30
), the linear term in
\omega\mp 2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}
produces a contribution proportional to
\dot{E}_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)
via inverse Fourier transformation:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\omega^{2}(\omega\mp 2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})E_{\mathrm{THz}}(\omega\mp 2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\omega\mp 2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})t^{\prime}}}{\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}}E_{\mathrm{THz}}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\omega\mp 2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})t^{\prime}}\dot{E}_{\mathrm{THz}}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{\mp 2\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}t}\bigl[4\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}^{2}\dot{E}_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)\pm 4\mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}\ddot{E}_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)-\dddot{E}_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)\bigr],

(33)

which is reflected in the terms involving
\bar{\chi}_{3}^{\prime}
in Eq. (
31
).

Since Eq. (
31
) takes the form of
E_{\infty}(t)=A(t)\cos(2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}t)+B(t)\sin(2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}t)
, where
A(t)
and
B(t)
are slowly varying functions of time, the SH intensity is given by the time-averaged square of the field:
\overline{E_{\infty}(t)^{2}}\approx[A(t)^{2}+B(t)^{2}]/2
.
This leads to the following expressions for the SH intensity in equilibrium,

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{SH}}=\frac{1}{2}\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}^{4}|\bar{\chi}_{2}|^{2}E_{\mathrm{IR}}^{4},

(34)

and for the temporal modulation of the SH intensity linearly induced by the pulsed field,

\displaystyle\Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}}(t)=\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}^{4}|\bar{\chi}_{2}|E_{\mathrm{IR}}^{4}\biggl\{|\bar{\chi}_{3}|\cos(\phi_{2}-\phi_{3})E_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)

\displaystyle+\biggl[\frac{|\bar{\chi}_{3}|}{\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}}\sin(\phi_{2}-\phi_{3})+|\bar{\chi}_{3}^{\prime}|\sin(\phi_{2}-\phi_{3}^{\prime})\biggr]\dot{E}_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)\biggr\}.

(35)

Therefore, the relative change in the SH intensity,
\Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}}/I_{\mathrm{SH}}
, can be expressed in the form

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}}}{I_{\mathrm{SH}}}=C_{0}E_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)+C_{1}\frac{\dot{E}_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)}{\omega_{\mathrm{THz}}},

(36)

where the coefficients
C_{0}
and
C_{1}\equiv C_{10}+C_{11}
are given by

\displaystyle C_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{2|\bar{\chi}_{3}|}{|\bar{\chi}_{2}|}\cos(\phi_{2}-\phi_{3}),

(37)

\displaystyle C_{10}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\omega_{\mathrm{THz}}|\bar{\chi}_{3}|}{\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}|\bar{\chi}_{2}|}\sin(\phi_{2}-\phi_{3}),

(38)

\displaystyle C_{11}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\omega_{\mathrm{THz}}|\bar{\chi}_{3}^{\prime}|}{|\bar{\chi}_{2}|}\sin(\phi_{2}-\phi_{3}^{\prime}).

(39)

A notable result is that, unlike the polarization change
\Delta P(t)
in Eq. (
20
), the SH intensity change
\Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}}(t)
depends not only on
E_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)
but also on its time derivative
\dot{E}_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)
.
Furthermore, even if the time-derivative component
C_{1}
is negligible, the sign of
C_{0}
can be negative; that is,
\Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}}(t)
can be in antiphase with
E_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)
.
Consequently, the commonly assumed relation
\Delta P(t)\propto\Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}}(t)
does not generally hold.

to

, followed by an evaluation of its second- and third-order susceptibilities using the spectral representations in Sec.

to verify the theoretical prediction provided by Eq. (

).

III.1
Model

Figure 1:
(a) Electric polarization
\langle\sigma^{z}\rangle_{0}
in the ground state.
The dotted curve indicates the exact result,
[1-(h_{x}/J_{z})^{2}]^{1/8}
.
(b) Absolute values and (c) phases of
\bar{\chi}_{2}
and
\bar{\chi}_{3}
, with
\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}=E_{\mathrm{g}}/2
.
The broadening factor is set to
\eta/J_{z}=0.1
.

\phi_{2}
is undefined for
h_{x}/J_{z}\geq 2
.

We consider the one-dimensional Ising model with a transverse field, whose Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}=-J_{z}\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{z}\sigma_{i+1}^{z}-h_{x}\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{x},

(40)

where
\sigma_{i}^{\alpha}\ (\alpha=x,y,z)
denotes the Pauli matrix at site
i
.
This spin model captures the essential features of electronic ferroelectricity
Ishihara 2010
;
Ikeda et al. 2005
: the Ising interaction
J_{z}
stabilizes the ferroelectric phase with
\langle\sigma_{i}^{z}\rangle_{0}\neq 0
, while the transverse field
h_{x}
favors the quantum paraelectric phase with
\langle\sigma_{i}^{z}\rangle_{0}=0

Naka and Ishihara 2010
;
Hotta 2010
.
Hereafter, we employ the mean-field (MF) approximation to make the problem tractable, which also enables us to derive explicit expressions for the nonlinear susceptibilities.
The MF Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathrm{MF}}=-h_{z}\sigma^{z}-h_{x}\sigma^{x},\quad h_{z}=2J_{z}s_{z},

(41)

where the order parameter
s_{z}=\langle\sigma^{z}\rangle_{0}
is the electric polarization, and the site index has been omitted.
The MF Hamiltonian is diagonalized by a unitary transformation
U_{y}(\theta)^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathrm{MF}}U_{y}(\theta)
with
U_{y}(\theta)=\exp(-\mathrm{i}\theta\sigma^{y}/2)
and
\theta=\arctan(h_{x}/h_{z})
, yielding

\displaystyle U_{y}(\theta)^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\mathrm{MF}}U_{y}(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon_{-}&0\\
0&\varepsilon_{+}\end{bmatrix}{},\quad\varepsilon_{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{h_{z}^{2}+h_{x}^{2}}.

(42)

The ground state and excited state are given by
|0\rangle=U_{y}(\theta)|{\uparrow}\rangle
and
|1\rangle=U_{y}(\theta)|{\downarrow}\rangle
, respectively, where
|{\uparrow}\rangle
and
|{\downarrow}\rangle
are the eigenstates of
\sigma^{z}
satisfying
\sigma^{z}|{\uparrow}\rangle=|{\uparrow}\rangle
and
\sigma^{z}|{\downarrow}\rangle=-|{\downarrow}\rangle
.

The order parameter
s_{z}
is determined by the self-consistent equation

\displaystyle s_{z}=\langle\sigma^{z}\rangle_{0}=\frac{2J_{z}s_{z}}{\sqrt{4J_{z}^{2}s_{z}^{2}+h_{x}^{2}}}

(43)

at zero temperature.
The solution of Eq. (
43
) is

\displaystyle s_{z}=\begin{cases}\hfil\sqrt{4J_{z}^{2}-h_{x}^{2}}\Big/(2J_{z})&(2J_{z}>h_{x}>0)\\
\hfil 0&(h_{x}>2J_{z}>0),\end{cases}

(44)

which is plotted in Fig.
1
(a), along with the exact solution
Pfeuty 1970
for reference.
The ground state is ferroelectric for
h_{x}/J_{z}<2
and paraelectric for
h_{x}/J_{z}>2
.
The energy gap between
|0\rangle
and
|1\rangle
can be expressed as

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{g}}=\varepsilon_{+}-\varepsilon_{-}=\begin{cases}\hfil 4J_{z}&(2J_{z}>h_{x}>0)\\
\hfil 2h_{x}&(h_{x}>2J_{z}>0).\end{cases}

(45)

The matrix elements of the polarization operator
P=\sigma^{z}
are

\displaystyle\langle 0|P|0\rangle=-\langle 1|P|1\rangle=\langle\sigma^{z}\rangle_{0},

(46)

\displaystyle\langle 0|P|1\rangle=\begin{cases}\hfil-h_{x}/(2J_{z})&(2J_{z}>h_{x}>0)\\
\hfil-1&(h_{x}>2J_{z}>0).\end{cases}

(47)

IV
Discussion

The most significant assumption in our theory is the linear approximation of the third-order susceptibility, as given in Eqs. (
29
) and (
30
).
Provided this approximation holds, Eq. (
35
) offers a quantitative estimate of the SH intensity change and should be valid even in more complex systems, including real materials.
In the MF transverse-field Ising model discussed in Sec.
III
, by considering the
h_{x}
dependence of Eqs. (
46
) and (
47
), we suppose that
C_{0}
vanishes when the magnitude of the polarization expectation value
\langle 0|P|0\rangle
becomes comparable to that of the transition dipole moment
\langle 0|P|1\rangle
.
However, the universality of this behavior is still an open question.
It is therefore important to apply our formulas to other systems to determine whether the time-derivative component can dominate and whether
\Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}}(t)
remains in phase with
E_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)
.

If one extends beyond the linear approximation to include second-order terms, Eq. (
25
) yields contributions of the form
\omega^{2}(\omega\mp 2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})^{2}E_{\mathrm{THz}}(\omega\mp 2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}})
, which in the time domain correspond to second- and higher-order time derivatives of
E_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)
.
Experimental detection of these contributions would be highly challenging with current experimental capabilities.
Additionally, if
\Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}}
depends nonlinearly on
E_{\mathrm{THz}}
, the present formulation breaks down, and a higher-order perturbative expansion becomes necessary.
However, there is no reason to expect that the simple relation
\Delta P\propto\Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}}
holds in such strongly nonlinear regimes.

In this work, we have discussed the modulation of the polarization and the SH intensity by an external electric field pulse.
Other intriguing second-order phenomena, such as optical rectification and the bulk photovoltaic effect, are also described by the second-order response function.
Similarly, the linear modulation of these effects can be described by the third-order susceptibility.
Although in Eqs. (
21
) and (
22
) we focused on contributions around
\omega\approx\pm 2\omega_{\mathrm{IR}}
, an analogous discussion could address the response near
\omega\approx 0
.
A detailed investigation of this aspect is left for future work.

V
Summary

We investigated the temporal modulation of the electric polarization
\Delta P(t)
and second harmonic intensity
\Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}}(t)
, both of which are linearly induced by a low-frequency electric field pulse
E_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)
.
Using nonlinear response theory, we derived general expressions for
\Delta P
and
\Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}}
, demonstrating that
\Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}}
can contain terms proportional not only to
E_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)
but also to its time derivative
\dot{E}_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)
.
Through an analysis of the transverse-field Ising model within the mean-field approximation, we showed that for most parameter regimes,
\Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}}
is predominantly proportional to
E_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)
, in agreement with common assumptions.
We also found that the time-derivative component can become significant under certain conditions, and our simulations quantitatively agree with the theoretical predictions.
Furthermore, we showed that when the time-derivative component is negligible,
\Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}}
can nonetheless be in antiphase with
E_{\mathrm{THz}}(t)
; that is,
\Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}}
can decrease while
\Delta P
increases, and vice versa.
These findings reveal that the commonly assumed relation
\Delta P(t)\propto\Delta I_{\mathrm{SH}}(t)
does not always hold, thereby enabling a more accurate analysis of the ultrafast dynamics in ferroelectrics driven by electric field pulses.
