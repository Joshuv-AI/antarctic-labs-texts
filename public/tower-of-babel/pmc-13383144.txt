Targeted next-generation sequencing for microbial and clinical diagnosis: a prospective controlled comparison between sputum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid in patients with community-acquired pneumonia
Authors: Xin Feng, Min Du, Shaobin Feng, Yujuan Li, Yin Zhao, Dong Zhang, Xi Yong, Zhuangwen Pu, Jiawei Wu, Jun Niu, Jianming Luo, Yuni Xiang, Tong Chen, Hangyong He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate identification of pathogens causing community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) in adults is a crucial step for successful diagnosis and treatment. However, some patients cannot tolerate bronchoscopy, or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) cannot be obtained in a timely manner. Currently, targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) is increasingly used for detecting pathogens. As an easily accessible lower respiratory tract specimen, the diagnostic efficacy of sputum tNGS in detecting pathogens causing CAP remains unclear.Methods and designBetween August 2023 and May 2025, 181 patients clinically diagnosed with CAP were enrolled from the Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine, the People’s Hospital of Nanbu County, Sichuan Province. Sputum and BALF samples were collected from each patient on the same day. The samples underwent tNGS to compare the diagnostic performance of sputum tNGS and BALF tNGS in laboratory microbial detection and pathogen identification.ResultsIn total, 181 adult patients were included in this study, comprising 98 males (54.1%) and 83 females (45.9%). Finally 175 patients were diagnosed with CAP, and 6 patients had non-infectious diseases. Concerning laboratory microbial detection, the overall microbial detection rates of sputum tNGS and BALF tNGS were 96.1% (174/181) and 95.5% (173/181), respectively. Using the results of BALF tNGS microbial detection as the reference, sputum tNGS showed high overall consistency for bacterial detection (Kappa value: 0.71). Consistency was lower for fungal and viral detection (Kappa values: 0.47, 0.44). Furthermore, using the comprehensive clinical judgment criteria for pathogenic microorganisms as the gold standard, the diagnostic accuracy of sputum tNGS and BALF tNGS for pathogens leading to CAP was evaluated. The overall concordance rates of sputum compared to BALF tNGS for diagnosing pathogenic bacteria, fungi, viruses, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and atypical pathogens were 0.70 vs. 0.72, 0.93 vs. 0.93, 0.81 vs. 0.85, 0.97 vs. 0.98, and 0.99 vs. 0.98, respectively. Cost-effectiveness analysis indicated that the cost of sputum tNGS was significantly lower than that of BALF tNGS and the conventional method of pathogen detection (p < 0.0001).ConclusionsThe diagnostic accuracy of sputum tNGS for microorganisms causing CAP is comparable to that of BALF tNGS. It is easy to implement and has lower costs, suggesting its value as an alternative to BALF tNGS or conventional microbiological tests for the early and rapid diagnosis of CAP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13616-9.

Background

Rapid, accurate, and comprehensive identification of pathogens causing community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is crucial for selecting appropriate antibiotic regimens and improving prognosis. However, a definitive etiology remains elusive in 19%-62% of adults with CAP [1]. Recently, next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology has shown significant potential in diagnosing infectious diseases [2]. Compared to mNGS, targeted NGS (tNGS) offers several advantages; for instance, its detection sensitivity is not affected by the human genome and background microorganisms, it requires smaller amounts of samples, and it can simultaneously detect both DNA and RNA pathogens. It also offers lower costs and standardized workflows [3]. Furthermore, tNGS and mNGS exhibit comparable diagnostic performance in detecting microorganisms in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) [4]. Therefore, tNGS holds promise as an efficient and low-cost novel method of early diagnosis for CAP.

BALF specimens are currently considered the optimal sample for detecting the etiology of pneumonia [5]. However, in clinical practice, patients with CAP do not routinely undergo bronchoscopy to obtain BALF specimens for etiological screening. This approach also necessitates the presence of bronchoscopy equipment. Besides, the wait time for scheduling the procedure often compromises the timeliness of sample collection.

Sputum and BALF samples are both lower respiratory tract specimens, but sputum specimens can be obtained non-invasively and easily. Previous studies have indicated no significant difference in the sensitivity and specificity of mNGS between BALF and sputum samples in terms of pathogen detection. However, the clinical application of mNGS is susceptible to interference from human genomic DNA and considerably increases healthcare costs [6]. For patients who cannot tolerate or are not suitable candidates for bronchoscopy to obtain BALF, particularly those with severe CAP who are not intubated, sputum represents a more readily accessible and ideal lower respiratory tract specimen for early diagnosis. Nevertheless, the results of the sputum test are susceptible to interference from colonizing microorganisms in the upper respiratory tract and oral cavity. This may lead to results that do not perfectly reflect the etiology of the lower respiratory tract infection. Specifically, it may not represent the condition of infected alveoli, resulting in false positive and false negative findings.

Therefore, further clinical validation is needed to assess whether the results of tNGS conducted on sputum specimens offer good microbial detection concordance and consistent clinical diagnostic accuracy for identifying causative pathogens compared to tNGS conducted on BALF specimens. We hypothesized that in patients with CAP, tNGS performed on sputum from deep lower respiratory tract, including induced sputum, obtained through standardized procedures, and on BALF samples, will show high concordance in detecting causative pathogens. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic value of sputum tNGS in patients with CAP by comparing the concordance of microbial detection results and causative pathogen identification between sputum and BALF samples collected on the same day from the same patient. The results will provide robust evidence supporting the use of sputum tNGS as a new method for early, rapid, efficient, and low-cost clinical diagnosis of CAP.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a single-center, prospective, self-controlled study. The study enrolled all hospitalized patients clinically diagnosed with CAP in the Department of Respiratory Medicine, the People’s Hospital of Nanbu County, from August 2023 to May 2025.

The inclusion criteria were as follows:meeting the diagnostic guidelines for adult CAP in China [7]: (i) community-onset; (ii) pneumonia-related clinical manifestations, such as newly developed cough, expectoration, or worsening of pre-existing respiratory symptoms, with or without purulent sputum, chest pain, dyspnea, and hemoptysis, fever, lung consolidation and/or auscultatory moist rales, and peripheral blood white blood cell count > 10 × 109 /L or < 4 × 109 /L with or without left shift; (iii) chest imaging showing new patchy infiltrates, lobar or segmental consolidation, ground-glass opacities, or interstitial changes, in the absence or presence of pleural effusion. Sample collection

(1) Sputum Specimen Collection: Patients rinsed their mouths twice with normal saline. Thereafter, healthcare personnel gently percussed the patient’s back to induce coughing, and sputum specimens were collected in sterile cups. For patients with insufficient sputum production or who could not expectorate, sputum induction was conducted to obtain lower respiratory tract specimens. Relevant operational guidelines were followed for induced sputum collection [8]. First, patients inhaled 200-400 μg of salbutamol 10 minutes before nebulization to prevent wheezing during sputum induction. Patients were instructed to rinse their mouths with water and blow their noses. After nebulization with 3% sodium chloride injection for 10-15 minutes, patients were requested to rinse their mouths again, discard saliva as much as possible, and cough forcefully to expectorate deep sputum into a sterile container. If no sputum was produced, nebulization was repeated with 4% sodium chloride injection for another 5–10 minutes, and patients were instructed to repeat forceful coughing to expectorate deep sputum into a sterile container.

(2) BALF Specimen Collection: BALF samples were collected from all enrolled patients using a flexible electronic bronchoscope. Under topical anesthesia, the bronchoscope was advanced to the bronchopulmonary segment corresponding to the radiologically evident lesion. Next, bronchoalveolar lavage was performed in the selected segment. Sterile isotonic saline was instilled through the bronchoscope biopsy channel using a syringe, in aliquots of 25-50 mL each, to reach a total volume of 100 mL. The fluid was aspirated immediately after each instillation using a negative pressure of 50-100 mmHg (1 mmHg = 0.133 kPa), achieving a recovery rate of 40%-60%. The pooled BALF was collected in a sterile cup. Under sterile conditions, 20 mL of BALF was separated for assay. Specific procedures followed the technical specifications for BALF detection [9].

The BALF and sputum specimens were divided into two portions. One portion was used for smear microscopy, staining, and microbial culture within 2 hours of collection. The other portion of sputum and paired BALF specimens were stored in a refrigerator at 4 °C, and tNGS was completed within 24 hours of collection. Qualified BALF specimens met the following criteria: no contamination with macromolecular airway secretions, recovery rate > 40%, viable cells accounting for >95% of total cells, red blood cells < 10% (excluding trauma/hemorrhage factors), epithelial cells < 3%-5%, and smear showing intact cell morphology without deformation and uniform distribution [9]. Sputum samples from all patients were evaluated through microscopic examination. Gram staining was also conducted on purulent portions. Samples with fewer than 10 squamous epithelial cells (SECs) per low-power field, or a white blood cell/SEC ratio ≥ 10 and >5 microorganisms per field at 100× magnification, were considered qualified sputum samples [10]. Statistical analysis

First, the normality of data was assessed. Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages, and continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Non-normally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range) [M (QL, QU)]. The chi-square test was employed to compare categorical variables. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was utilized to compare continuous variables not conforming to a normal distribution. McNemar’s exact test was applied for paired binary data. Subsequently, 2 × 2 contingency tables were constructed to calculate the positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), sensitivity, and specificity of tNGS in paired sputum and BALF samples from the same patients. Kappa analysis was performed to calculate the degree of agreement. The strength of agreement was interpreted based on Kappa values following the criteria proposed by Landis and Koch [17]: <0.00 as ‘Poor’; 0.00–0.20 as ‘Slight’; 0.21–0.40 as ‘Fair’; 0.41–0.60 as ‘Moderate’; 0.61–0.80 as ‘Substantial’; 0.81–1.00 as ‘Almost perfect’. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data analysis and figure generation were done using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0; GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

In total, 181 patients clinically diagnosed with CAP were enrolled in this study. The participants included 98 males (54.1%) and 83 females (45.9%), with a mean age of 56.77 ± 18.25 years. Among the sputum specimens, 55.2% were induced sputum samples, and 44.8% were spontaneously expectorated sputum samples. A definitive diagnosis of CAP was established for 175 patients, while 6 patients were diagnosed with non-infectious diseases. Head-to-head Comparison of tNGS microbial detection results in paired sputum and BALF specimens from the same patient

The microbial concordance between paired sputum and BALF tNGS testing for the included patients is illustrated in Fig. 4. tNGS detected microorganisms in both sputum and BALF specimens from 168 specimens (92.8%, 168/181). In 2 patients (2/181, 1.1%), no microorganisms were detected by tNGS in either sputum or BALF specimens. In 5 cases (5/181, 2.7%), microorganisms were detected by tNGS only in BALF, with completely negative sputum tNGS results. Conversely, in 6 cases (6/181, 3.3%), microorganisms were detected by tNGS only in sputum, with completely negative BALF results (Supplementary Table 2). Among the 168 patients whose microorganisms were detected in both sputum and BALF samples, the results of tNGS were completely concordant (identical microbial profiles) in 49 specimens (49/168, 29.1%). Only in 4 patients (4/168, 2.3%), sputum and BALF showed completely discordant tNGS results (Supplementary Figure 1). Partial concordance (at least one identical microorganism detected) was observed in 115 patients (115/168, 68.4%) (Table 2, Supplementary Figure 2). The positivity rates for Candida, Epstein-Barr virus, and Herpes simplex virus were significantly higher in sputum specimens than in BALF specimens. After excluding these three microorganisms that commonly colonize the upper respiratory tract, the results of sputum and BALF specimens became completely concordant in 23 out of 115 partially concordant cases. Combining these 23 cases with the 49 double-positive specimens with complete concordance, and the 2 double-negative specimens, the overall complete concordance rate between sputum and BALF was 40.8% (74/181) (Supplementary Figure 3). Among the remaining 92 partially concordant paired specimens, tNGS detected more microbial species in sputum than in BALF in 52.1% (48/92) of cases. The composition of additional microorganisms detected in sputum specimens was as follows: viruses in 41% of cases (predominantly rhinovirus, SARS-CoV-2, and Influenza A virus), bacteria in 17% of cases (predominantly Haemophilus influenzae, Staphylococcus aureus), fungi in 17% of cases (predominantly Aspergillus species and Pneumocystis jirovecii), and multiple microorganisms simultaneously detected in 25% of cases. Conversely, tNGS detected more microbial species in BALF than in sputum in 38% (35/92) of these partially concordant specimens. The microbial profile detected additionally in BALF included: bacteria in 34% of cases (predominantly Haemophilus influenzae, Staphylococcus aureus), fungi in 26% of cases (predominantly Aspergillus species), viruses in 14% of cases (predominantly Adenovirus, Influenza A virus), and multiple microorganisms simultaneously detected in 26% of cases. Evaluation of value of sputum tNGS for microbial detection when considering BALF tNGS results as the reference standard

The overall sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of sputum tNGS for bacterial detection were 0.86, 0.85, 0.88, and 0.83, respectively, with a kappa value of 0.71 (Table 3). Notably, sensitivity reached 100% for Bordetella pertussis and Escherichia coli. Among mycobacteria, Mycobacterium tuberculosis showed a sensitivity of 70%, a specificity of 100%, and a concordance rate of 0.80. Sputum tNGS exhibited high sensitivity (88%) and specificity (99%) for detecting atypical pathogens. In this study, the results of sputum and BALF tNGS were completely concordant for Chlamydia psittaci and Legionella species. Discussion

We present the first prospective, head-to-head comparative study evaluating concordance and diagnostic consistency between sputum and BALF tNGS for detecting causative pathogens in CAP. Our study yielded several key findings: 1) After excluding microorganisms commonly colonizing the upper respiratory tract, the overall complete concordance rate of microbial detection between sputum and BALF tNGS was only 41%; 2) Using BALF tNGS as the reference for microbial detection, sputum tNGS exhibited substantial agreement for bacteria, atypical pathogens, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis, but only moderate agreement for fungi and viruses; 3) Using comprehensive clinical diagnosis as the gold standard for determining causative pathogens, sputum and BALF tNGS showed comparable diagnostic performance for bacteria, viruses, fungi, and atypical pathogens causing CAP. However, sputum tNGS exhibited high specificity but low sensitivity for diagnosing Mycobacterium tuberculosis; 4) The cost and turnaround time of sputum tNGS were significantly superior to those of BALF tNGS.

The etiological diagnosis of CAP has long faced a dilemma. BALF offers high diagnostic accuracy but is invasive, costly, and needs a long turnaround time [18]. On the other hand, sputum culture is non-invasive and economical but suffers from limited sensitivity [19]. The question arises whether sputum tNGS technology can achieve diagnostic performance comparable to BALF while retaining its non-invasive advantages. Previous studies utilizing multiplex PCR technology compared pathogen detection results in paired sputum and BALF specimens from pediatric patients with CAP, revealing high concordance for Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Influenza A virus, Influenza B virus, and RSV, with kappa values of 0.885, 0.944, 0.854, and 0.854, respectively [20]. However, there is a lack of high-quality evidence systematically comparing the performance of sputum and BALF in the context of tNGS technology. Therefore, this study provides the first direct, head-to-head evidence addressing this question through a prospective, paired design.

In this study, the overall microbial detection rates of sputum and BALF tNGS were 96.1% and 95.5%, respectively. This aligns with the results of Shi et al. [21], who reported comparable pathogen detection rates between paired sputum and BALF mNGS (BALF: 94.1%; sputum: 98.5%). In our study, 51% of patients showed partial concordance regarding microorganisms detected in BALF and sputum tNGS (at least one identical microorganism). Among them, 52% exhibited more microorganisms in sputum tNGS than in BALF tNGS. Viruses, particularly Rhinovirus, SARS-CoV-2, and Influenza A virus, accounted for 41% of cases. This finding has two important clinical implications: on one hand, sputum tNGS may lead to overdiagnosis due to the detection of viruses carried in the upper respiratory tract that are not causing CAP, specifically in the early phase of infection. On the other hand, it may help identify potential pathogens that could progress to CAP, offering early warning value [22, 23]. This likely warrants the relatively low concordance between sputum and BALF tNGS at the microbial level in our study.

We also introduced comprehensive clinical diagnosis as the reference standard to evaluate diagnostic performance to validate the accuracy of sputum and BALF tNGS in diagnosing the pathogens causing CAP. The results revealed comparable sensitivity between the two methods for detecting pneumonia-causing microorganisms, with good concordance between sputum and BALF tNGS (McNemar p > 0.05). However, some differences existed across microbial types. The sensitivities of sputum and BALF tNGS for bacterial, fungal, and viral infections were 0.71 vs. 0.75, 0.67 vs. 0.92, and 0.94 vs. 0.87, respectively. In contrast, Wei et al. [24] reported sensitivities of 92%, 86%, and 95% for detecting these three types of pathogens using BALF tNGS. The lower sensitivity for bacterial and fungal infections in our study compared to that reported by Wei et al. may be due to differences in study populations. Wei et al. included immunocompromised patients with more complex pathogen profiles, whereas we enrolled the general population with normal immune function. For diagnosing viral pneumonia, the sensitivity of sputum tNGS was slightly higher than that of BALF tNGS in our study. Previous studies have indicated that sputum tNGS can detect viral infections earlier than BALF tNGS. Furthermore, some infections initially invade the upper respiratory tract or concentrate there in the early stages of the disease [23]. For viruses causing pneumonia or severe pneumonia, such as the influenza virus and SARS-CoV-2, treatment or close monitoring for progression to pneumonia is warranted. For detected Rhinovirus or Respiratory syncytial virus, further evaluation integrating the patient’s immune status is necessary to prevent the progression of upper respiratory infection to pneumonia. Regarding diagnostic performance for pathogenic fungi, the sensitivity of sputum tNGS was slightly lower than that of BALF tNGS in our study. Therefore, in clinical practice, a positive sputum tNGS result requires careful consideration of high-risk host factors (immune status), characteristic imaging findings (halo sign, air crescent sign on CT), other microbiological evidence (G/GM tests, culture), and clinical course [25]. Concurrently, this finding may be constrained by the limited number of fungal-positive cases in our study, warranting further validation with larger sample sizes.

The present study indicated that the diagnostic sensitivity of sputum tNGS for Mycobacterium tuberculosis was lower than that of BALF tNGS. A meta-analysis reported that the sensitivity and specificity of BALF mNGS for diagnosing pulmonary tuberculosis were 73 and 98%, while the sensitivity and specificity of sputum mNGS were 60 and 99%, respectively [26]. The sensitivity of BALF tNGS in our study was notably higher than that reported in previous studies (96% vs. 73%). In our study, the diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of sputum tNGS for M. tuberculosis were generally consistent with the results of the above-mentioned meta-analysis (73% vs. 60%, 100% vs. 99%). The lower sensitivity of sputum tNGS compared to BALF in this study may be attributable to factors such as anatomical limitations, patient-related factors, and sample size. In patients with lesions in their alveoli or distal airways (e.g., early-stage pulmonary tuberculosis), pathogens may not be effectively expelled with sputum [27]. Additionally, false-negative results are more likely in paucibacillary pulmonary tuberculosis when the pathogen load in sputum falls less than the detection threshold. In contrast, BALF directly provides samples from the infected site in the lower respiratory tract, potentially increasing the detection rate of M. tuberculosis [28, 29]. Therefore, our findings suggest that a negative sputum tNGS result for M. tuberculosis cannot rule out infection. For patients with high clinical suspicion, it is necessary to incorporate other tests (e.g., sputum acid-fast staining, Xpert MTB/RIF, or blood T-SPOT.TB). In addition, bronchoscopy can help obtain BALF for relevant etiological examination when indicated.

This study also found that compared to BALF sampling, sputum specimen collection significantly reduced the need for invasive procedures and lowered medical costs while improving timeliness. It also resolved the need for bronchoscopy and anesthesia, thereby reducing patient discomfort and preventing complications. Economic analysis revealed that the consumable and operational costs were lower for sputum specimens, aligning with patient-centered and cost-effective clinical practice. This approach may shorten hospital stays and reduce overall medical expenditures. Integrating sputum tNGS into the routine diagnostic workflow for adult CAP holds promise in terms of the health economy.

This study had several limitations. First, as a single-center study, the risk of selection bias remains high; therefore, future studies should include larger, multicenter cohorts to validate these findings. Additionally, the presence of some detected microorganisms could not be confirmed due to the limitations of conventional etiological testing techniques.

Conclusions

Sputum tNGS offers good clinical diagnostic value for CAP caused by pathogenic bacteria, viruses, and atypical pathogens. It holds promise as an alternative first-line method to the commonly used BALF tNGS or CMT for the early diagnosis of pathogens causing CAP. However, it should be noted that the diagnostic sensitivity of sputum tNGS for fungi and Mycobacterium tuberculosis is lower than that of BALF tNGS. Sputum tNGS is superior to BALF tNGS in terms of safety, patient comfort, timeliness, and cost-effectiveness, particularly for patients with contraindications to bronchoscopy.
