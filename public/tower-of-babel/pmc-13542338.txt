Waders in a sea of debris: a global overview
Authors: Yada Trapletti‐Lanti, Mónica Expósito‐Granados, Elizabeth Znidersic, Marga L. Rivas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe impact of plastic pollution on ecosystems and marine fauna is well documented, although research into its effects on waders (also known as shorebirds) remains limited. Given that waders are exposed to coastal marine litter, this exposure could be a significant factor in the decline of their populations. This study aims to assess the global distribution of plastic ingestion in waders and investigate regional differences in their exposure. Our analysis included 30 studies, examining 316 digestive tracts and 1114 faecal samples. Only four studies analysed plastic in wader nests or entanglements. Eastern Asia exhibited the highest prevalence of plastic ingestion in waders, although no significant differences were detected among coastal regions. Descriptive patterns suggest that this trend may be influenced by intensive industrial activity, high population density, and pollution along migratory flyways. Southern Asia had the highest prevalence of plastics in faecal samples. No statistically significant differences were observed across wader families; however, descriptive results indicated elevated ingestion frequencies in Haematopodidae and Scolopacidae, while Recurvirostridae and Haematopodidae tended to show higher plastic prevalence in faecal samples. This study highlights the need for standardised sampling protocols and continued research on the effects of plastic on wader populations, which could contribute to their conservation.

INTRODUCTION

Plastic pollution is recognised as a serious threat to ecosystems globally (Eriksen et al., 2014; Gall & Thompson, 2015; Worm et al., 2017), with an associated impact on marine biodiversity (López‐Martínez et al., 2021; Tekman et al., 2022). Common plastics persist for centuries due to their non‐biodegradable nature, with significant accumulation in rivers and oceans (Eriksen et al., 2014; Rossi, Scherer & Petry, 2019). Plastics account for between 60% and 80% of all marine litter, with the highest concentrations found in areas of intense human activity (Barnes et al., 2009). Coastal regions, which are characterised by high population densities, host the majority of the world's population and, as a result, experience elevated levels of pollution. This is mainly due to the discharge of wastewater into marine waters, both directly and indirectly (Santhaseelan et al., 2021). The pervasiveness of microplastics in aquatic environments and their small size renders them particularly susceptible to ingestion by marine organisms. This can result in bioaccumulation along the food chain, increasing the risk of exposure for waterfowl (Jiang et al., 2024; Lam et al., 2022; Li et al., 2021).

Previous studies have identified plastic ingestion as a cause of death in 701 marine species, with 354 others dying due to entanglement (Kühn & Franeker, 2020). Among affected groups, seabirds are highly threatened by plastic pollution (Clark et al., 2023). Their susceptibility stems from specific traits, including movement patterns, diet, feeding behaviours, and morphological characteristics (Wilcox, Van Sebille & Hardesty, 2015). Although the interaction between seabirds and plastic pollution has been extensively studied, other bird groups, such as waders, have received considerably less attention despite also being impacted (Athira et al., 2024; Ballejo et al., 2021; Carlin et al., 2020; D'Souza et al., 2020; Lourenço et al., 2017; Luna, Gil‐Delgado & Bernat‐Ponce, 2022). Waders (also known as shorebirds) are an integral part of coastal and wetland ecosystems and can be indicators of the health of these ecosystems. Their feeding habits, such as foraging in mud and intertidal zones, expose this group of birds to significant risks of ingesting macroplastics (>5 mm) and microplastics (<5 mm in size).

Currently, many wader populations are in decline due to factors including habitat loss and degradation, climate change or loss of food resources (Andres et al., 2012; Rosenberg et al., 2019; Smith et al., 2020). However, few studies have assessed the impacts of interaction with and exposure to plastic pollution on waders (Flemming et al., 2022; Gregory, 2009). Recent findings suggest significant levels of microplastics in wader tissues, often exceeding concentrations in their surrounding environment, indicating an enrichment effect (Liu et al., 2023; Lourenço et al., 2017). Similar patterns have been observed in other species associated with coastal ecosystems, such as herbivorous snails (Saley et al., 2019). Correlations between plastic concentration in wader faeces and their feeding locations suggest that high levels of marine debris on sandy beaches may influence the invertebrate assemblages that constitute their primary diet (Mylius et al., 2023). Moreover, some studies suggest an association between an increased abundance of microplastics and the decline in coastal bird populations (Gilbert et al., 2016; Smith et al., 2020; Studds et al., 2017). Lavers, Bond & Hutton (2014) and Wilcox et al. (2015) highlighted that plastic ingestion can lead to sublethal effects, such as reduced body condition and impaired juvenile survival in seabirds, however, no evidence of this association has been observed in waders yet. These findings suggest that the accumulation of plastics not only affects individual health but may also compromise population viability over time; a concern that could extend to waders.

While some previous studies have reviewed the impact of plastic pollution on waders, no comprehensive synthesis has been conducted that includes ingestion, defecation, incorporation into nests, and entanglement. Through integrating these distinct dimensions, this study could provide a broader understanding of how plastics impact wader populations globally.

To achieve this synthesis, we conducted bibliometric and data analyses of the scientific literature to assess the impact of plastic pollution on waders. Specifically, our objectives were: (i) to assess the global presence of plastics in wader populations through ingestion, defecation, incorporation into nests, and entanglement; (ii) to examine the different methodologies used to analyse plastic presence; and (iii) to identify research gaps in protocols for sampling and characterising ingested plastics, to guide future conservation efforts.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Literature review

We conducted a descriptive review of the global literature on waders and their interaction with plastic pollution, covering studies published up to November 2024. The search was performed in the Scopus, Web of Science, and Science Direct databases using the search terms: (‘wader*’ OR ‘shorebird*’ OR ‘plover’ OR ‘Charadriiformes’) AND (‘plastic’ OR ‘microplastic’ OR ‘litter’). The search, restricted to titles, abstracts and key words, yielded 392 documents, spanning from 1971 to 2024. After removing duplicate articles (leaving 276), we applied the following exclusion criteria based on the screening of titles and abstracts: we excluded (i) conference papers, short communications, press notes, and editorial material; (ii) studies that were unrelated to wader species; and (iii) studies that lacked information on interactions with plastics. This screening process reduced the data set to 58 articles. In the next step, we conducted a full‐text evaluation of these 58 articles. Studies that did not provide information on the amount of plastics ingested, present in faeces, incorporated into nests, or entanglement, as well as those without specific data on wader species, were excluded from our analysis. This resulted in a final data set of 30 articles.

Taxa

Our study considered wader species belonging to the order Charadriiformes, specifically those in the suborders Charadrii, Scolopaci, and Chionidi. There are approximately 255 recognised wader species (Gill, Donsker & Rasmussen, 2021). Our research identified interactions with plastic in the Burhinidae, Charadriidae, Haematopodidae, Scolopacidae, and Recurvirostridae families, comprising a total of 47 species.

Data extraction

We extracted data using a systematic methodology to code and organise our database, focusing on the following variables: general information of the article (authors, year of publication, and year of study), location data, taxonomic information, sample size (N), sampling methods and plastic data [i.e. plastic frequency of occurrence (hereafter FO), type, colour, size, mass, and composition of polymer].

In the included studies, the presence of plastics was assessed using different sampling methods such as necropsies, stomach washings, regurgitates, analysis of faeces or pellets (Athira et al., 2024), by identifying the presence of plastics in nests (Luna et al., 2022), or documenting instances of entanglement in plastic debris (Donnelly‐Greenan, Nevins & Harvey, 2019). In general, studies that performed necropsies also usually indicated the portion of organ/portion of digestive tract analysed (e.g. gular pouch, gut, gizzard, proventriculus, intestines and regurgitates).

The metrics collected from articles were those suggested by Provencher et al. (2017), and included the FO (defined as the percentage of individuals or faecal samples that contained plastic), and the characterisation of each item, number of plastic items, mean mass, median mass, mass SD or SEM, mass range, and plastic type (fibres, fragments, pellets, films, filaments, beads, and foams, size and colour). Additionally, we recorded the techniques for plastic polymer identification and the types of polymers. Mean FO values were calculated as weighted means using sample size (N) as weights.

Articles addressing plastic incorporation into nests or entanglement were not included in the statistical analyses, as they lacked FO data or other comparable metrics.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were conducted using R Studio software, version 4.2.2 (R Core Team, 2022). To model differences in plastic ingestion FO across region and families, we used a quasibinomial generalised linear model (GLM), as this method is particularly suited to proportion data with potential overdispersion. The response variable in the model was the FO of plastic, while the explanatory variables (predictors) were region, family, and species. Overdispersion was assessed by dividing the residual deviance by the residual degrees of freedom. When overdispersion was detected, models were refitted using a quasibinomial error distribution. In cases where samples sizes were too small or expected frequencies too low, chi‐squared tests (or Fisher's exact tests when appropriate) were applied as an alternative to assess differences among categories (e.g. polymer type, colour or shape). The significance level was set at 0.05.

RESULTS

Worldwide results

Out of the 30 articles included in this study, most (N = 18) analysed plastic FO in the digestive tract. Eight studies examined plastic FO in faeces, two studies analysed plastics in the nests of waders, and two studies investigated entanglement. A total of 316 digestive tracts, 1114 faecal samples, 103 nests, and 55 entangled individuals were analysed. Data were collected from coastal regions across the world: North America (N = 316), South America (N = 107), southern Europe (N = 438), eastern Asia (N = 19), southern Asia (N = 79), western Africa (N = 585), southern Africa (N = 3), and Australia (N = 41). The number of studies and sample sizes varied substantially among regions, indicating an uneven representation of data sources across the dataset.

For the analysis of the mean FO of plastic found in digestive tracts, only studies reporting plastic ingestion were considered; therefore, sample sizes (N) refer exclusively to ingestion data. Overdispersion was detected in the binomial models; therefore, quasibinomial GLMs were applied. No significant differences were detected among coastal regions (North America, South America, southern Europe, eastern Asia, southern Africa, and Australia) (quasibinomial GLM, P > 0.05) (see online Supporting Information, Table S1). Eastern Asia, 73.7 ± 39.9% (N = 19) (mean ± SD), was the region with the highest frequency of plastics ingestion in waders followed by North America, 42.9 ± 31.7% (N = 172) (Fig. 1, Table S2).

For analyses of plastic FO in the faeces, only studies analysing faecal samples were included, and N refers to the number of faecal samples. No significant differences were found among the different coastal regions (southern Europe, southern Asia, western Africa, and Australia) (P > 0.05). The region with the highest plastic FO in waders' faeces was recorded in southern Asia (100.0 ± 0.0%, N = 27) followed by the Australian coastal region (95.0 ± 5.0%, N = 30) and southern Europe, 75.8 ± 12.9% (N = 410) (Fig. 1).

The only article that quantified the presence of plastics in wader nests (Luna et al., 2022), reported an average FO of 12.50% in a total of 24 nests of black‐winged stilt (Himantopus himantopus). However, another study mentioned the presence of plastics in the nests of two species, piping plover (Charadrius melodus) and American oystercatcher (Haematopus palliatus), but did not provide FO data (Grant et al., 2019). Similarly, only two studies addressed entanglement in waders. One study reported an average of two entangled individuals recorded per year for willet (Tringa semipalmata) (Donnelly‐Greenan et al., 2019). The other study documented 16 injury cases in lesser sand plover (Charadrius mongolus), representing 27.5% of all recorded injury cases in the study area, although no FO values were provided (Aarif et al., 2021).

Plastic presence by family and species

For plastic presence in digestive tracts, no significant differences were found among families or species (quasibinomial GLM, P > 0.05, Table S1). However, descriptive results suggest that the highest FO of plastic ingestion was observed in the families Haematopodidae (57.8 ± 48.2%) and Scolopacidae (38.4 ± 34.4%) (Table 1, Fig. 2). The most globally studied wader family was Scolopacidae with a total of 218 individuals analysed, and the species with the highest sampling effort was red phalarope (Phalaropus fulicarius) (N = 109), showing a FO of plastic ingestion of 59.6 ± 18.3% (Tables S2, S3). The family with the least research was Burhinidae, represented by only one study with three samples. For some species, no plastics were found when analysing their digestive tract, however the number of samples for these species was small (N ≤ 25). For example, the collared plover (Charadrius collaris), the semipalmated plover (Charadrius semipalmatus), the common snipe (Gallinago gallinago), and the Wilson's phalarope (Phalaropus tricolor) had no evidence of plastic in their digestive tracts (Table S3).

For the FO of plastics in faeces, no significant differences were found among families (P > 0.05, Table S1). Descriptive results indicated comparatively higher FO in the families Recurvirostridae 84.0 ± 13.6% (N = 152), and Scolopacidae, 48.0 ± 21.8% (N = 681). The most studied family again was Scolopacidae and the least studied family was Haematopodidae. No data were obtained for the family Burhinidae (Table 1, Fig. 2). The most studied species was sanderling (Calidris alba) with a FO in faeces of 54.6 ± 22.9% (N = 171). Several species exhibited plastic FO exceeding 80%, notably the lesser sand plover (N = 3) and the hooded plover (Thinornis cucullatus) (N = 15), both with a FO of 100%. Similarly, the pied oystercatcher (Haematopus longirostris) (N = 15) had a high plastic FO of 90%, while the pied avocet (Recurvirostra avosetta) (N = 144) had a FO of 84.5% (Table S3).

A significant difference was found between ingested plastics and those found in faeces (χ 2 = 24.57, df = 1, P < 0.01). The mean percentage of plastic FO found in faeces was 56.3 ± 25.6%, higher than that found in digestive tracts (40.1 ± 39.2%) (Fig. 3).

Sample processing

To examine the presence of plastics in the digestive tract of waders, most studies focused on analysis of the entire gastrointestinal tract, representing 38.9% (N = 7) of the reviewed articles. However, some studies analysed only certain parts of the digestive tract, such as the stomach (27.8%; N = 5), stomach and gizzard (11.1%; N = 2), or upper digestive tract in isolation (5.6%; N = 1). The remaining articles, 16.7% (N = 3), did not specify which part of the digestive tract was analysed. The most commonly used protocols for the analysis of plastics were those proposed by Provencher et al. (2017, 2019) and Van Franeker, Meijboom & De Jong (2004).

In both digestive tract and faecal plastic analyses, samples were typically digested with potassium hydroxide (KOH) to remove organic matter, filtered, and then oven dried.

Physical and chemical characterisation

Most studies did not determine the average size or type of plastics present in the specimens. Specifically, 63.3% of the studies (N = 19) did not characterise the type of plastic, 66.7% (N = 20) did not identify the colour, and only 26.7% of the studies (N = 8) performed polymer composition analysis using chemical analyses techniques such as Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) or Raman spectroscopy (Table S4). Most of these studies did not use a contamination control (90.0%, N = 27).

In terms of plastic ingestion, the average number of items ingested per specimen was 3.6 ± 6.7 items (N = 972), and the average mass of items was 0.08 ± 0.11 g (N = 972). No significant differences were detected among the plastic shapes found in waders (χ 2 = 9.84, df = 6, P > 0.05) or among the colour of the items (χ 2 = 18.98, df = 11, P > 0.05). Fragments and pellets were the most frequent shape, representing 30.7% and 24.1% of the total type of plastics respectively (Fig. 4A). There were no records of macroplastics. The predominant colour was green (58.0%) followed by white and blue, 50.0% and 46.0% respectively (Fig. 4B). Percentages are not mutually exclusive, as individual studies may report more than one plastic category. For the analysis of the composition of plastics, only three studies used FTIR. However, only two of these studies [Teboul et al. (2021) and Zhu et al. (2019)] provided composition data, finding 46.0% polyethylene and 36.5% polypropylene microplastics.

Radial plots of main plastic frequency of occurrence (FO) (%) of (A) plastic shapes and (B) colour in the digestive tract (pink line) and faecal samples (blue line) in waders.

For plastics found in faeces, the average number of items per faeces sample was 1.7 ± 0.6 items (N = 118). There were significant differences among the plastic shape identified (χ 2 = 60.46, df = 3, P < 0.01), with fibres being the most frequent shape of plastic (98.27 ± 9.68%) (Fig. 4A). Significant differences were found among the colour (χ 2 = 18.50, df = 5, P < 0.01), with blue being the most frequent colour identified in faeces (60.1%) (Fig. 4B). Of the total of eight studies on the presence of plastics in faeces, five used FTIR. Only one study, Athira et al. (2024), provided data on the types of polymer, reporting that the most predominant was polystyrene at 42.6%, followed by polypropylene at 12.8%.

Significant differences were found between plastic ingestion and presence in faecal samples of waders, for both the shape of plastics and their colour (χ 2 = 1670.3, df = 8, P < 0.01; χ 2 = 99.59, df = 13, P < 0.01, respectively) (Fig. 4).

DISCUSSION AND FUTURE RECOMMENDATIONS

Global presence of plastic

Our study has shown that plastics have a significant impact on waders, with widespread pollution found in various regions worldwide. Although no significant differences in plastic ingestion were detected among regions, the data suggest trends that may reflect variations in pollution intensity, local plastic sources, or ecological conditions. Our findings indicate relatively higher FO values of plastic ingestion in eastern Asia and North America, which is consistent with previous studies conducted in the North Pacific and adjacent regions, including the South China Sea (Zhu et al., 2019) and the East China Sea (Liu et al., 2023). The southern Asia coastal region, specifically India, is well known for high levels of microplastic pollution in its rivers, coasts (Robin et al., 2020), and islands (Dharani et al., 2003). Coastal areas recorded concentrations of up to 3.58 particles m−3, making microplastics available to wader species through direct ingestion and indirect consumption via bioaccumulation (Madhav et al., 2020).

The southern European coast is also a high‐risk area for plastic impact on wader populations. This is due to the North Atlantic and the Azores currents, which transport plastics from North America to the European coasts, leading to their accumulation along the coasts of Spain and Portugal (Cole et al., 2011). Our results show that 75.8% of waders in southern Europe contain plastics in their faeces, similar to a FO of 62.3% recorded by Coentro (2022) on the coast of Portugal and 73.7% by Lourenço et al. (2017) in the southern European coast. Additionally, plastic ingestion FO of 42.9% was found in waders from the North American coastal region, possibly attributable to the high availability of pollutants deposited in the sediment by the Atlantic Ocean current.

The lowest values derived from digestive tract analyses were recorded in southern Europe (0%), followed by Australia (18.1%), South America (26.2%) and southern Africa (33.3%). In contrast, FO values obtained from faecal samples were generally higher, with the lowest value reported for western Africa (38.7%). Given that coastal regions are the main areas of plastic accumulation, from sources including river basins, in situ waste accumulation, recreational activities and tourism (Thushari, Chavanich & Yakupitiyage, 2017; Thushari & Senevirathna, 2020), these numbers are likely influenced by limited research and monitoring efforts in these areas (Thushari & Senevirathna, 2020). In general, coastal areas with high human activity are hotspots for plastic pollution, which aligns with the elevated ingestion rates observed in wader populations from these regions (Jiang et al., 2024).

Waders and plastic pollution

This study focused on the prevalence of interactions between waders and plastic pollution on a global scale. Out of the 47 documented species, two are considered globally threatened: the lesser sand plover (endangered) and the hooded plover (vulnerable) according to IUCN (2024). Both these species exhibited a 100% FO of plastics in faeces, although with small sample sizes (Table S2). A high incidence of plastics in faeces among Recurvirostridae (84.0%) and Haematopodidae (90.0%) families, underscores concerns about the potential conservation threats posed by marine litter to these families (Flemming et al., 2022).

Although no significant differences were detected among wader families, descriptive patterns may be observed, potentially influenced by ecological and biological factors, including feeding habits, body size, and specific foraging or migratory strategies. For instance, the Haematopodidae family exhibits a highly specialised diet of molluscs, which are known vectors for microplastic contamination due to their ability to accumulate plastic particles in their tissues (Naji, Nuri & Vethaak, 2018). Similarly, species within the Recurvirostridae family usually feed in shallow waters where floating and suspended plastic particles are more available and may be ingested accidentally along with their prey (Hamilton, 1975; Martin et al., 2017). Such dietary specialisation, coupled with their relatively large body size, may explain the higher rates of plastic ingestion observed (Jâms et al., 2020). Additionally, migratory behaviours might influence exposure, as waders traverse regions with varying levels of plastic pollution, potentially accumulating plastics from different sites during long‐distance migrations (Samaraweera, Chandrajith & Jayasena, 2022). However, it will be necessary to conduct continuous monitoring to address such suggestions.

The most studied species was the sanderling (N = 182), followed by the dunlin (Calidris alpina) (N = 155), and the pied avocet (N = 144). Several studies suggest that populations of sanderlings are declining due to climatic and anthropogenic issues (Barona & Podestá, 2024; Payne, 2010; Thomas, Kvitek & Bretz, 2003). Thus, these species could be useful bio‐indicators for the presence of plastics in coastal and intertidal environments.

However, it is important to note that we only have information for 18% of the 255 species of waders, and it may not be representative of all waders. Further detailed studies are needed on these birds, which inhabit environments that are highly exposed to pollution, and may therefore be important sentinels.

Standardised protocols, global gaps and future recommendations

Our study highlights that research on the impact of plastic pollution on wader populations involves several challenges related to the characterisation and standardisation of methods, as well as notable research gaps in this field. Regarding sampling methods, our study differentiated between samples analysed in the digestive tract and those analysed in the faeces. The results showed the FO in faeces was nearly 50% greater than in digestive tracts. As recommended by Provencher et al. (2017), we also suggest the analysis of the whole digestive tract in cases where the individuals are already dead, such as stranded birds found on beaches. This approach could help to identify whether these differences arise from biases due to analysis of only part of the digestive system. Additionally, cross‐contamination during faecal sample collection may be common. Therefore, it is recommended also to collect samples of the surrounding sediment when collecting faeces (Lourenço et al., 2017; Mylius et al., 2023; Ward et al., 2022).

Inconsistency in data collection and reporting complicates comparisons. For example, we observed that colour descriptions were inconsistent across studies; terms like ‘clear’ or ‘light’ were used ambiguously. This inconsistency extended to plastic typologies, with some articles distinguishing between ‘film’ and ‘laminar’, whereas others grouped these under broader categories. Although the negative impacts of microplastics on birds are increasingly documented (Carlin et al., 2020; Drever et al., 2018; Lourenço et al., 2017), significant research gaps persist regarding the characterisation of the types of plastic found in waders (Flemming et al., 2022; Mansfield et al., 2024; Puskic, Lavers & Bond, 2020). Over 60% of the articles reviewed did not identify the type of plastic, colour or chemical composition. Advanced techniques such as FTIR and Raman spectroscopy are essential for detailed plastic characterisation and can provide valuable information on the origin and composition of the plastics found. Additionally, our statistical models showed substantial overdispersion, most likely due to the very limited number of available studies. This reinforces the need for more studies to enable more robust comparisons.

We found only two articles that analysed plastics in wader nests (Donnelly‐Greenan et al., 2019; Luna et al., 2022), whereas numerous studies have been conducted on plastics in the nests of gulls, European shags (Gulosus aristotelis), and shearwaters (Buxton et al., 2013; Lato et al., 2021; Lopes et al., 2020; Thompson et al., 2020; Votier et al., 2011; Yorio et al., 2022).

The implementation of standardised protocols, while adapting them to local contexts, is essential to ensure consistency and effectiveness in studying the impact of plastic pollution on wader populations.

Environmental implications

The study of the impact of plastic pollution on waders has several important ecological and environmental implications. Ingestion of plastics can cause internal injuries, reduce nutrient uptake and cause stomach blockage which may negatively affect body condition and individual survival, as reported in other birds (Pierce et al., 2004; Wang et al., 2021). In addition, the use of plastic debris as nesting materials may affect chick health and survival (Votier et al., 2011). However, no studies have been conducted on this in waders.

Waders play a crucial role in controlling populations of invertebrates in coastal habitats. Their population decline can consequently lead to ecological imbalance, affecting biodiversity and ecosystem health. Thus, due to their sensitivity to environmental changes, waders can act as bio‐indicators of ecosystem health (Amat & Green, 2010), potentially reflecting the extent of plastic pollution in coastal and marine ecosystems.

The study of the effects of plastic pollution on waders provides important information for the conservation of these species and highlights the link between wildlife health and environmental quality.

CONCLUSIONS

(1)This study reveals a pervasive influence of plastic pollution on waders across different global regions.(2)No significant differences in the FO of plastic were detected across geographical areas, families, or shape of ingested plastic. However, the data showed trend toward higher FO in wader digestive tracts in coastal regions of North America and eastern Asia. Australia and South America had lower values, although this may reflect limited sampling efforts.(3)Descriptive results suggest that the most affected family might be Haematopodidae. This family, as well as an apparently high plastic FO in endangered or vulnerable species such as the lesser sand plover and hooded plover, highlight the urgent need for targeted conservation measures.(4)This study also identified substantial gaps in research coverage, with only 18% of wader species studied, few geographical locations covered, and inconsistencies in sampling and analytical methodologies. The absence of standardised protocols for plastic characterisation, combined with limited polymer analysis and contamination controls, restricts our ability to make robust cross‐regional comparisons.(5)To address these challenges, we advocate for the adoption of standardised protocols, comprehensive studies on underrepresented species, and the incorporation of new sampling areas into global monitoring efforts. These actions are essential to improving our understanding of the ecological impacts of plastic pollution on waders and to guiding effective conservation strategies in coastal and intertidal habitats.

Australia and South America had lower values, although this may reflect limited sampling efforts.

(3)Descriptive results suggest that the most affected family might be Haematopodidae. This family, as well as an apparently high plastic FO in endangered or vulnerable species such as the lesser sand plover and hooded plover, highlight the urgent need for targeted conservation measures.
