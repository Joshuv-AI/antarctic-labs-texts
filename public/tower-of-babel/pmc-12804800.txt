Enhancing recall in asphalt pavement crack segmentation using a HoughNet-extended U-Net architecture with reconstruction head
Authors: István Reményi, Ámon Kiss, Zoltán Kárász, János Botzheim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate recognition of cracks in asphalt pavements is fundamental to proactive maintenance and infrastructure safety management. From the repairing perspective, the identification process of the ruined surface areas on the pavement is more about finding the cracks and their location without requiring precise curve estimation. If we treat the problem as a binary semantic segmentation, we can translate this statement as the recall capability is higher ranked than the given model’s precision, where one of the main challenges is the varying crack widths. In this paper, we focus on modifying a standard U-Net network with the voting-based segmentation head called HoughNet, created for object detection and segmentation tasks, and with an additional reconstruction head for pixel-level information preservation. While combining them with the most appropriate loss functions, we measured performance on two datasets with slightly different crack characteristics. We showed that the model trained and evaluated on the Crack500 dataset can find more relevant cracked surface parts with a similar magnitude of precision compared to the competitor architectures. To see the limitations of the concept, we also report results on GAPS384 with thinner crack lines, where the concept can shift the model performance towards finding the best F1 Scores at both the dataset and image scale.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Pavement erosion occurs when the surface material is affected by weather, traffic load, or other damaging factors. It causes deformities such as cracks and potholes, weakening its structural toughness. Here, we focus on detecting the thin lines of cracks in the camera’s images of the inspected areas. A reliable online monitoring system can speed up road maintenance services, minimizing human involvement in decision-making. The relative amount of damaged areas to the remaining surface parts is a key factor in evaluating pictures, so we selected to solve the detection via binary semantic segmentation. One of the main challenges is that the recorded images are burdened with noise. The uneven surface is full of tiny parts of crushed stone, and dust builds up between them, even inside cracks. This results in the ambiguity of the coloring in both segments, so the otherwise continuous crack lines have a fractal-like border and perceived discontinuities. So, as a system requirement, we found that the system should find the essential local features in the images and that the recall capability tends to be more important than the precision. Currently, semantic segmentation of pavement cracks focuses on improving accuracy, robustness, and real-time performance for automatic pavement defect detection across varying crack characteristics. In recent years, most studies have used deep learning models, such as the encoder-decoder-like U-Net. Innovations include architectural modifications and multi-path feature fusion to capture the crack details better and to reduce noise. The models were extended with an attention mechanism or with hybrid concepts that combine spatial and frequency-domain features. Despite the presented improvements, the main challenge remains due to noisy backgrounds and limited annotated datasets, the models usually struggle with class imbalance because cracks in images are sparse and irregular. There is a clear gap in improving feature extraction techniques that specifically enhance crack edge sharpness and the continuity required for correct semantic segmentation1 . Great potential lies within the feature extraction module design where the dynamically adaptability to varying crack morphologies is a must. To improve edge detection and crack continuity, more efficient strategies can be used which integrate multi-scale and multi-domain features.Since we are treating this as a segmentation module for an online monitoring system, such as the workflow for the UAV application2 or support for maintenance systems3, we also had restrictions on inference performance. Although we have investigated the impact of applying the self-attention layer in the model, convolutional kernels can be easily vectorized, and thus, computational overhead can be minimized. We selected U-Net as the general model architecture for the investigation. To measure the performance of our method, we selected two publicly available online benchmark data sets. Crack5004 has various types of cracks with different widths, and the second is GAPS3844, where the extent of the deformities is thinner, making the task even more challenging. Evaluating the effects of HoughNet-based segmentation and additional image reconstruction head modification, we found that the the interaction among neighboring pixels and the preservation of fine texture details containing the original image’s fine details through the network layers can be beneficial for the model performance, especially the metric recall for wider crack use cases and precision for thinner deformities. Here, we showed that the U-Net model can be straightforwardly extended with a voting-based feature mapping module to improve semantic details and segmentation recall.

Related works

According to recent studies, deep learning is preferred among solutions for the crack segmentation task1,5. A wide range of concepts is already available, from classical fully connected architecture design6 through edge detection-based models7,8 to more complex designs. DeepCrack has a hierarchical architecture for multi-scale feature encoding, so local and global information can be mixed within the information flow towards segmentation map generation9. The U-Net was one of the first encoder-decoder-style architectures with excellent performance. Since its introduction, it has become one of the most popular solutions due to the skip connection concept that implements multi-resolution feature extraction10,11. In this domain, it is also commonly used with various extensions. To improve generalization capabilities, Lau et al.12 used a pre-trained ResNet-34 as an encoder for the U-Net. Similarly, Di et al. applied ResNet-50 for the same task13 and in RUC-Net, a ResNet-18 was selected with further modification of the loss function to address the class imbalance14. As preprocessing steps, Nguyen et al.15 included classical image processing solutions. To capture the fine details, attention was included in model design16,17 and the implementation of the few-shot concept18. CrackUNet follows a so-called network-in-network structuring to reduce parameter count and, instead of standard convolution layers, it applies a deformable kernel to provide uneven sampling that can capture patterns of fragmented shapes19. A recent article extends the encoding capabilities with topology-based pattern extraction based on a snake dynamic convolution concept. To overcome an over-segmentation problem, an additional alignment module is introduced in CPCDNet, resulting in a trainable shape-aware solution20. UP-CrackNet follows the unsupervised learning methodology to train a U-Net architecture with a generator-discriminator concept borrowed from GANs21. Lu et al. created an automated system to extract 3D pavement texture features from 2D images and predict Mean Texture Depth (MTD), vital for skid resistance. Their work include 3D data acquisition from images, advanced multi-aspect texture feature-based 3D processing and multivariate models like Gradient Boosting Tree (GBT) linking features to MTD. They achieved less than relative errors in field tests, surpassing traditional methods22. For skid resistance in mind, in this study the authors used asphalt slab images with varying textures and friction data measured by a Dynamic Friction Tester to create their dataset. For friction prediction, they developed interpretable image indicators, capturing polishing effects. The raw pavement images undergo algorithmic processing to derive robust texture features modeling surface condition and safety risks. Their methodology starts with an image preprocessing like binary conversion. Then for multi-resolution texture extraction it applies wavelet transforms and employs machine learning to correlate features with the measeured friction data23. Having an outlook on defect detection, a recent paper introduced differentiable Non-Maximum Suppression implemented based on the Sinkhorn-Knopp matching, which enables end-to-end gradient optimization. The Sinkhorn algorithm is increasingly used in computer vision tasks requiring matching or alignment, including segmentation. As solving entropy-regularized matrix optimal transport problems, it can produce assignment between elements. This supports differentiable feature matching in models and offers trainable post-processing, applicable to precise texture or defect tasks in road imagery24. For multi-scale pavement analysis, Guo et al. employed a distance-adaptive variational formulation for super-resolving low-resolution images using depth cues. It formulates super-resolution as a spatially-varying inverse problem in depth-variant scenarios, such as those common in real-world imagery. The variational framework formulates super-resolution with distance-adaptive priors, enabling handling of depth variations that traditional methods overlook offering a practical extension to multi-scale, depth-sensitive applications25.

Methods

For our study, we selected the original U-Net, an encoder-decoder style architecture, with skip-connections at each different resolution level that provide enriched information exchange between the downstream and upstream parts. These consist of 2D convolutional, maximum pooling, and deconvolutional layers that operate with kernel sizes and , organized into UpSampling and DownSampling blocks10. We used this as a baseline for our investigations and measured how a loss function selection affects the model performance (Cross Entropy, Focal, Dice, and Tversky). The first addition was the application of the HoughNet concept. By modifying the layers of the semantic segmentation head, we can add the ability to neurons so that they can decide where they want to belong in their local neighborhood limited by a voting field placed around them26,27. The Generalized Hough Transform, which uses visual evidence from feature maps to generate class-conditional votes to identify object centers in a log-polar vote field, served as the model for HoughNet. A visual evidence map, bounding box dimensions (width, height), and center offsets are the three prediction heads that cover the image plane after the network processes an input image using a backbone CNN (such as ResNet) to produce the necessary feature maps. The voting mechanism is implemented by the evidence map head, while the latter two can be thought of as a stack of convolutional layers with RELU activations. Figure 1 shows the vision of the authors that they constructed an evidence map for every class C, a tensor of [] size where the entries hold a score value of how strongly they want to contribute in their own vote field with the R region count. The contributions are summed for each class at each pixel position, resulting in a segmentation map (which Samet et al. called an object presence map due to its primary use case in object detection). Vote accumulation is used on the resulting maps computed via as , where is the class-conditional vote field, p and q are evidence locations. The vote field is mapping evidence at q to thee hypothesized centers at p using a log-polar representation. For long-range voting relative to object scale s the radial distance and the angle are discretized into bins. This design collects votes from distant parts (in our case, the long crack parts). Votes are weighted by evidence strength , sigmoid-activated heatmap values from convolutional predictions. The final output detections are selected based on local maxima in exceeding a threshold. The results are then gets associated w,h and offset predictions refining the bounding box , where corrects grid discretization. Non-maximum suppression (NMS) merges overlapping peaks per class. Training uses focal loss on evidence, smooth L1 on dimensions/offsets, and a binary cross-entropy variant on presence maps to encourage peak formation. This integrates local (nearby pixels) and global (distant structures) cues effectively, boosting AP on small objects by 2.5 points over baselines like CenterNet. In our investigation, we used the original voting field setup.

Dataset and metrics

In this paper, we evaluated the results on two different data sets. Crack500 initially has 500 high-resolution pavement images with binary semantic segmentation labels covering the cracked regions. This alone contains an extreme class imbalance in pixel count between eroded and intact surface areas. To cancel this out, crack-affected regions were cropped and grouped into the 1896 train, 384 validation, and 1124 test set4. Following the guideline of Kim et al.29, the resolution of the images was converted to size , since the best registered performance was achieved at this resolution. In case of images, we used a Gaussian blur with a kernel as a preprocessing step to reduce the noise level, then the nearest sampling for resizing, and finally the centered cropping at the given resolution. The labels were only resized and cropped with the same sampling strategy. The second data set that we used is called GAPS384. In its first edition, Eisenbach et al. collected samples with different surface marks such as oil stains, road signs, etc., and used an automatic labeling procedure to generate bounding boxes for cracked regions. Yang et al. subsampled these images and performed a semantic segmentation labeling annotation. The data set contains 409 train and 100 test images4,30. To keep our tests consistent, here we used the same resizing policies. Figure 3 contains samples from both data sets showing the differences between crack sizes and their layouts.

Results on Crack500

As our first evaluation step on the Crack500 data set, we calculated the performance of different loss functions with the standard U-Net model (upper part of Table 3). For this report, we used five training runs for every mean value and standard deviation calculation. The Cross-Entropy loss is optimizing for the error measured as the average of the differences between two binary distributions at every pixel location. Focal loss is an extension of the Cross-Entropy; it tries to focus on the poorly predicted entries via its weighting mechanism. Usually, this makes it a good candidate for unbalanced data sets, primarily when we focus only on the precision and specificity. Dice loss prepares an intersection map between the output and ground-truth segmentation samples, causing higher IoU and F1 Score results. Tversky measures the error as a weighted version of the IoU metric, which can purposely prefer FPs or FNs. We voted not to interfere with a priori assumptions and used 1.0 for both FP and FN weights. Despite the lack of intentional favoritism, Tversky produced the highest recall value with slightly worse precision. A similar trend can be observed when the HoughtNet segmentation head modification was applied (lower part of Table 3). Combined with the Cross-Entropy, all metrics reached higher values, Focal loss suffered a slight performance degradation, and Dice also benefited. These losses received a lower FNR score and, therefore, better recall and IoU values. Tversky also gained in FPR, F1 Score, and IoU, and had the lowest FNR and the highest recall overall, while its precision was affected by its FPR. When we examined the model output, we found that the reason was the inaccurate border lines of the crack segment and its tendency to draw thicker lines (Fig. 4). For these reasons, we continued our investigation with Tversky as the segmentation loss function.

Results on GAPS384

With different crack characteristics, GAPS384 can give models another type of challenge. For further evaluation, we selected the crack pixels according to the corresponding ground-truth masks in the train split of each dataset, and applied the Brunner-Munzel test on the resulting samples. Conclusion

The real-world crack segmentation problem has challenges, such as uneven segmentation geometries, data set imbalances, different weather conditions, and overall noise presence. Under these circumstances, we tried to create a model that works well in identifying the cracked regions and was formulated so that recall was preferred instead of focusing on the high precision scores. We found that we could train a model with better recall performance using the reformulated segmentation head, an additional regression head, and the most suitable loss functions. By using convolutional kernels, the computation requirements did not change significantly, making the extension feasible for monitoring applications. We demonstrate that a Hough-style voting head, when combined with a reconstruction branch and Tversky loss, can be tuned to maximize recall for long, irregular crack structures under strong class imbalance. Our modifications increase recall and AIU on Crack500 at the cost of reduced precision and F1. The generalization capability of this concept was also reviewed by applying a more challenging data set. Although the results show high potential compared to other methods, improvements are needed. Due to the distribution shift in the data, the measured performance dropped in every metric. This sets our research orientation; the hyperparameter optimizations and raising model complexity are among our following tasks. Another future direction is the generalization investigation in more challenging scenarios, including shadows, cluttered backgrounds, poor lighting, road signs, asphalt pavement boundaries such as curbstones, low contrast, and environmental factors that affect segmentation accuracy.
