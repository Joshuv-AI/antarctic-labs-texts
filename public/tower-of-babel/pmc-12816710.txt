Exploring the chemical and biological landscape of a Nickel (II) schiff base complex via azomethine linkage
Authors: Rangaswamy Venkatesh, T Murugan, Attar Kubaib, L Umamaheswari, Predhanekar Mohamed Imran, Mohamed Ragab AbdelGawwad, Haya Alayadi, Najm M Alfrisany
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNickel(II) complexes incorporating azomethine-derived Schiff-base ligands were synthesized and characterized using UV–Vis, FT-IR and 1H NMR spectroscopy, supported by DFT calculations at the B3LYP/6-311++G(d, p) level. The spectral data confirmed square-planar coordination with Ni–N and Ni–O bonding. Frontier molecular orbital and topological analyses revealed efficient metal–ligand charge transfer, strong electron delocalization and favorable molecular stability. The reduced HOMO–LUMO gap (2.724 eV) and high electrophilicity index suggest enhanced reactivity and biological potential. Molecular docking studies against the 2BL8 receptor showed high binding affinity (–9.1 kcal/mol), surpassing chloramphenicol, while ADMET predictions confirmed good solubility (–3.860), oral bioavailability (0.85) and pharmacokinetic safety. Antibacterial assays demonstrated superior inhibition against E. coli, K. pneumoniae and B. subtilis, with moderate activity toward S. aureus. Overall, the combined experimental and computational results establish the Ni(II) Schiff-base complex as a thermodynamically stable and biologically active molecule, which highlights its promise as a lead scaffold for antimicrobial drug design.Keywords: Ni(II) schiff base complex, Spectral analysis, DFT, Topological analysis, In-silico, In-vitroSubject terms: Biochemistry, Chemistry, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery

Introduction

Schiff bases are a versatile class of ligands extensively utilized in coordination chemistry, typically obtained through the condensation of primary amines with aldehydes or ketones in alcoholic media1. Their strong affinity for transition metals and capacity to stabilize diverse oxidation states make them valuable for analytical, catalytic as well as bioinorganic applications2. Among these, aromatic Schiff bases have attracted particular interest because of their enhanced conjugation, rigidity as well as their ability to form stable chelates with d-block metals. Complexes derived from such ligands display broad functional properties, ranging from catalytic activity in oxidation, polymerization and asymmetric synthesis reactions to potential use in optical, electrochemical and magnetic materials3,4.

The azomethine (C=N) functionality serves as a crucial donor site and a pharmacophoric moiety responsible for many biological effects, including antibacterial, antifungal, anticancer, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities5,6. As a result, Schiff-base metal complexes have been explored as promising scaffolds for therapeutic applications as well as for use in dyes, sensors and agrochemicals7. Despite this progress, the relationship between ligand architecture, metal coordination environment and the biological function in Ni(II) Schiff-base systems remains only partially understood. Most reported studies focus on simple bidentate or tridentate ligands and provide limited insight into how specific donor–acceptor arrangements, aromatic substituent effects or geometric distortions, influence the electronic structure and reactivity of Ni(II) complexes8. Furthermore, integrated studies that couple experimental synthesis with computational modeling to correlate structure and function are still scarce.

In this study, Nickel(II) was selected as the central metal ion due to its well-defined d8 electronic configuration, which favors square-planar or octahedral coordination geometries—ideal for forming stable, low-spin Schiff base complexes with predictable stereochemical behavior9. These geometries facilitate strong metal–ligand orbital overlap, enhancing electronic delocalization and redox activity, both of which are critical for biological interactions. Moreover, Ni(II) complexes are known to exhibit notable antimicrobial, antioxidant and anticancer properties, often attributed to their ability to interact with biomolecular targets such as DNA, enzymes and bacterial cell membranes10. The incorporation of nitro-substituted aromatic groups within the ligand framework further modulates electron density, thereby strengthening metal coordination and potentially improving biological reactivity through enhanced lipophilicity and electron-withdrawing effects. This combination of Ni(II) coordination versatility and nitro-substituted ligand design provides a strategic platform for developing potent metal-based antimicrobial agents capable of addressing the challenges of multidrug resistance.

To address these limitations, the present work reports the synthesis, spectral characterization and theoretical investigation of a novel tetradentate Ni(II) Schiff-base complex containing both nitrogen and oxygen donor atoms. The ligand framework was strategically designed to incorporate substituted aromatic moieties that enhance π-conjugation and modulate the electron-donating capacity of the coordination sphere. Such structural modifications are expected to affect orbital overlap, metal–ligand charge transfer as well as overall biological potential. Comprehensive spectroscopic techniques including FT-IR (Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy), UV–Vis (Ultraviolet–Visible), 1H NMR and magnetic susceptibility measurements were employed to elucidate the coordination mode, electronic transitions and geometry of the synthesized complex.

In parallel, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to refine the optimized geometry and to evaluate key quantum-chemical descriptors such as EHOMO, ELUMO, energy gap, electronegativity, hardness, softness and electrophilicity index. Molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) mapping was used to visualize electron-density distribution and identify nucleophilic and electrophilic regions. Mulliken and Hirshfeld analyses both describe atomic charge distribution but differ in approach. Mulliken analysis, based on molecular orbital overlap, effectively captures orbital-level charge transfer between donor atoms and the Ni(II) center, making it ideal for studying metal–ligand coordination. In contrast, Hirshfeld analysis provides surface-based charge mapping that is suited for visualizing intermolecular contacts. Hence, Mulliken was chosen for this work to quantify electron donation and delocalization within the coordination framework, while Hirshfeld complemented it by illustrating noncovalent surface interactions. Molecular docking simulation provids additional insight into the interaction of the complex with biologically relevant target proteins, revealing binding modes, stability and ligand efficiency. These computational results complemented the experimental findings, allowing a deeper understanding of the structure–activity relationships governing the behavior of Ni(II) coordination systems.

Overall, this study makes a distinct contribution by providing a unified experimental-computational approach to elucidate the coordination chemistry and potential bioactivity of Ni(II) Schiff-base complexes. The combination of spectroscopic characterization and theoretical modeling not only clarifies donor–acceptor orbital interactions but also highlights how electronic modulation through ligand design can enhance biological efficacy. These findings advance the current understanding of Ni(II) Schiff-base frameworks and lay the groundwork for the development of metal-based therapeutic agents.

Materials and methods

Quantum chemical (QC) calculations

Quantum chemical (QC) calculations offered an in-depth evaluation of the electronic structure and molecular geometry of the studied compounds11. Utilizing the B3LYP functional combined with the 6-311++G(d,p) basis set, the calculations delivered accurate electronic property predictions and optimized the structural configurations12. Bond lengths and angles were meticulously adjusted to ensure structural robustness and electronic parameters were computed for the most stable molecular conformations. The computational workflow, executed with Gaussian 16W13 and visualized using GaussView 6.014, enabled detailed analysis of HOMO-LUMO distributions, energy gap determination and molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) surface mapping.

Key electronic descriptors such as ionization potential and electron affinity were assessed to gauge the compounds’ electron transfer capabilities, while global hardness (η) and softness (S) served as indicators of their chemical stability and reactivity15. The electrophilicity index (ω) provided insight into the molecules’ vulnerability to nucleophilic attack and ΔNmax calculations outlined the extent of electron density distribution. Dipole moment analysis shed light on the overall polarity of the molecules. Further, topological analyses were conducted using Multiwfn 3.7, allowing for a comprehensive wavefunction and electron density study16. ADMET (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion and Toxicity) properties and drug-likeness were evaluated through SwissADME and pkCSM, offering predictions of pharmacokinetic behavior and potential therapeutic utility17. Collectively, these computational results supported the potential application of these complexes in medicinal chemistry.

Experimental studies

Analytical-grade reagents sourced from Sigma-Aldrich were used throughout to maintain experimental precision. Proton NMR (1H-NMR) spectra were collected at 400 MHz using either DMSO-d₆ or CDCl₃ as solvents, with tetramethylsilane (TMS) as the internal standard. FT-IR spectra were recorded using the KBr pellet technique on a Nicolet Impact-400 spectrophotometer. UV–Visible absorption measurements were performed at room temperature immediately after preparing the samples. Conductivity tests were carried out in DMF to evaluate the ionic nature of the complexes.

Synthesis of nickel (II) complex

The Schiff base ligand was prepared by combining 2-Amino-4-nitroaniline (ANA) (10 mmol) dissolved in 20 mL of ethanol with equal molar amounts of 2-Hydroxy-5-nitrobenzaldehyde (HNB) and 2-Formylphenol (2FP), each also dissolved in 20 mL of ethanol. The mixture was stirred for 30 min and then heated under reflux at 80 °C for 6 h, resulting in the formation of a yellow precipitate. This product was collected by filtration, washed thoroughly with hot ethanol to remove impurities and dried18. The structural framework of the synthesized ligand is depicted in Fig. 1.

Scheme for the preparation of Schiff Base Ligand.

For the preparation of the Ni(II) complex, a solution of Ni(II) acetate tetrahydrate (2 mmol) in 20 mL of ethanol was added to a separate solution of the Schiff base ligand (2 mmol) in 20 mL of ethanol. After stirring for 30 min, the reaction mixture was refluxed at 80 °C for an additional six hours, facilitating the coordination of Ni ions with the ligand. The resulting complex was isolated by filtration, washed with hot ethanol and dried19. The synthetic scheme for the Ni(II) complex is shown in Fig. 2.

Ligand-receptor interaction studies

Molecular docking studies were conducted using AutoDock Vina 4.2, with the 2BL8 receptor selected to investigate ligand-receptor binding interactions20. Potential protein targets were identified via the Swiss Target Prediction tool and their structures were obtained from the Protein Data Bank. The proteins were preprocessed by removing co-crystallized ligands and water molecules, followed by the assignment of Gasteiger charges. Structures were then converted to PDBQT format and docking simulations utilized the Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm. The binding pose with the lowest calculated energy was chosen as the optimal configuration and molecular interactions were subsequently visualized using PyMOL.

To further evaluate ligand–receptor compatibility and binding stability, molecular docking simulations were performed using the OPLS3e force field. This force field was specifically selected because it offers improved accuracy in modeling non-bonded interactions, including hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking and van der Waals forces, which are critical for reproducing realistic protein–ligand binding conformations. The OPLS3e parameterization extends the classical OPLS series by incorporating updated torsional and electrostatic parameters derived from extensive quantum-mechanical data, making it particularly reliable for transition-metal coordination systems and biologically active small molecules.

Docking parameters such as total potential energy, root mean square deviation (RMSD), bond stretching, angle bending and electrostatic contributions were analyzed to ensure conformational stability and proper receptor fit. The application of OPLS3e thus provides a balanced representation of metal–ligand interactions and biological binding forces, enhancing the accuracy of docking score predictions and validating the observed strong affinity of the Ni(II) complex toward the 2BL8 receptor21.

Results and discussion

Molar conductance analysis

The molar conductance data provide valuable insight into the ionic nature and coordination behavior of the synthesized complexes. As shown in Table 1, the Schiff base ligand exhibited negligible conductance, confirming its neutral character. The Ni(II) complex displayed a low molar conductance value of 15.8 Mho cm2 mol−1 in DMF, indicating its non-electrolytic nature. Reported values for non-electrolytic Ni(II) Schiff-base complexes typically range from 10 to 25 Ω-1 cm2 mol−122, which closely aligns with the present finding. This consistency suggests that the complex is covalently coordinated, without ionizable counter-anions in solution.

UV-visible spectral analysis

Table 2 summarizes the UV–Visible spectral characteristics of both the free Schiff base ligand and its corresponding Ni(II) complex. The free ligand shows absorption maxima at 270 nm (π→π* transition within the aromatic ring), 340 nm (π→π* transition of the azomethine group) and 429 nm (n→π* transition of the C = N chromophore). Upon coordination with Ni(II), these bands undergo noticeable shifts: π→π* transitions for the aromatic system appear at 230–277 nm, while the azomethine-related π→π* band shifts to 283–376 nm, indicating strong metal–ligand conjugation and charge transfer. The n→π* band shifts slightly to 383–410 nm, suggesting partial delocalization of the lone-pair electrons of the azomethine nitrogen toward the metal center.

The d–d transition observed at 555 nm for the Ni(II) complex with a d8 electronic configuration provides compelling evidence for the adoption of a square-planar geometry. This transition corresponds to a relatively large crystal-field splitting energy (Δ₀), which is characteristic of square-planar Ni(II) complexes containing strong-field ligands. The magnitude of Δ₀ is sufficiently high to overcome the spin-pairing energy, leading to electron pairing in the lower-energy d orbitals. Consequently, the complex exhibits diamagnetic behavior, consistent with a low-spin square-planar Ni(II) configuration. The absorption band at 555 nm thus confirms the presence of this strong ligand field and the corresponding electronic arrangement.

In Fig. 3 shows that, TD-DFT (Time-Dependent Density Functional Theory) reinforces this experimental interpretation by showing that the Ni(II) complex possesses a smaller HOMO–LUMO energy gap compared to the free ligand, indicating enhanced electronic delocalization through metal–ligand charge transfer (MLCT). The close agreement between the theoretical excitation energies and the experimental UV–Visible spectrum validates both the computational model and the proposed square-planar coordination geometry of the Ni(II) complex.

FT-IR spectroscopic analysis

FT-IR spectroscopy provided valuable evidence of coordination and bonding behavior between the Schiff-base ligand and Ni(II) metal ion. Upon complexation, the azomethine (C = N) stretch shifted to a lower range (1602–1615 cm−1), signifying electron donation from the azomethine nitrogen to the Ni(II) center, which decreases the C = N bond order. This downward shift provides direct spectroscopic confirmation of M–N bond formation, showing that the ligand coordinates through its imine nitrogen with effective electron density transfer to the metal, thereby weakening the internal C = N bond. Similarly, the C–O stretching vibration shifted to 1306–1335 cm−1, indicating participation of the phenolic oxygen in metal coordination via deprotonation.

A broad band appearing between 3108 and 3375 cm−1 corresponds to hydrogen-bonded O–H vibrations, characteristic of coordinated or lattice water molecules. This region falls within the typical range for strongly hydrogen-bonded water (3000–3600 cm−1), suggesting that these molecules are integrated either within the inner coordination sphere or bound through secondary hydrogen bonding. Such interactions contribute to the stability and solubility of the complex, enhancing its physicochemical and biological properties.

New absorption bands in the 412–475 cm−1 (Ni–O) and 504–528 cm−1 (Ni–N) regions further confirm chelation through both oxygen and nitrogen donor atoms. Collectively, these spectral shifts substantiate the formation of a tetradentate Ni(II) Schiff-base complex, stabilized by coordinated water molecules and consistent with the proposed square-planar geometry23.

1H NMR spectroscopic analysis

The 1H NMR spectrum of the Schiff-base ligand was recorded in DMSO-d₆ using tetramethylsilane (TMS) as the internal reference standard, as illustrated in Fig. 6. The azomethine (–HC = N) proton appeared as a multiplet in the range of 7.8–8.4 ppm, confirming the presence of the imine moiety. Aromatic proton resonances were observed between 7.0 and 7.6 ppm, while a distinct signal at 6.90 ppm corresponded to phenolic or substituted aromatic protons. These assignments are consistent with the expected chemical environment of the Schiff-base ligand and support its successful synthesis and purity.

1H NMR spectrum of Schiff base ligand.

However, 1H NMR data for the Ni(II) complex were not obtained, as paramagnetic Ni(II) ions typically cause extensive line broadening and chemical shift distortions, making proton signals unresolvable under standard NMR conditions. The unpaired electrons in high-spin Ni(II) centers generate local magnetic fields that perturb nuclear relaxation times and shift resonance frequencies beyond detectable ranges. This phenomenon is well documented for octahedral and tetrahedral Ni(II) systems, which are paramagnetic due to their unpaired d-electrons24,25.

In contrast, square-planar Ni(II) complexes with strong-field ligands can exhibit diamagnetism and occasionally produce well-defined NMR signals. In the present case, however, the observed magnetic moment and electronic spectral data indicate the presence of a paramagnetic configuration, explaining the absence of distinct 1H NMR peaks for the complex. Similar findings have been reported for related Ni(II) Schiff-base systems, where NMR spectra were either featureless or omitted due to these paramagnetic effects26,27.

Geometric and electronic structure analysis

Table 4 and Fig. 7 summarize the DFT-optimized bond lengths, bond angles and dihedral parameters that describe the molecular geometry of the Ni(II) complex. The imine C = N bond (C16–N15 = 1.323 Å) is markedly shorter than the single N–C bond (N29–C6 = 1.450 Å), confirming its double-bond nature and partial delocalization within the chelating framework. The aromatic C–C bonds lie within the expected experimental range (1.37–1.44 Å), while a slight elongation at C6 reflects the electronic influence of the adjacent nitro substituent.

The optimized Ni–N (1.803 Å) and Ni–O (1.792 Å) bond distances closely agree with crystallographically reported ranges for square-planar Ni(II) Schiff-base complexes (Ni–N = 1.79–1.85 Å; Ni–O = 1.78–1.83 Å)28. Comparable geometries were observed, for instance, in Ni(II)–salen and Ni(II)–isonicotinoyl derivatives, which show Ni–N and Ni–O separations of ~ 1.80 Å and ~ 1.79 Å, respectively. Such consistency validates the computational model and supports the reliability of the DFT-optimized structure.

Bond angles around the Ni(II) center, particularly N(8)–Ni(24)–O(23) = 93.7° and N(15)–Ni(24)–N(8) = 88.3°, deviate slightly from the ideal 90°, reflecting the influence of chelate ring strain typical of tetradentate ligands. The chelate bite angles at the imine nitrogens (C(9)–N(8)–Ni(24) and C(10)–N(15)–Ni(24) ≈ 113°) are in line with experimental X-ray values reported for related square-planar Ni(II) Schiff-bases (110–115°)29,30. Aromatic ring angles cluster near 120°, maintaining delocalized π-character, while small deviations occur near nitro-substituted carbons due to steric and electronic effects.

Dihedral-angle analysis reveals that torsions near 0° or 180° dominate, confirming an overall planar backbone conducive to extended conjugation across the ligand system. Minor distortions such as C(7)–N(8)–Ni(24)–C(9) = 119.4° relieve steric congestion without compromising planarity. The nitro groups exhibit dihedral angles of approximately 125°, indicative of partial conjugation with the aromatic ring31.

As shown in Table 5, the Ni(II) complex exhibits the lowest total energy among all systems studied, confirming its enhanced thermodynamic stability compared with the free ligand and literature-reported analogues. Frontier molecular orbital (FMO) analysis reveals that the HOMO is primarily localized on the Ni(II) center, whereas the LUMO is delocalized over the ligand framework, enabling efficient MLCT.

The calculated HOMO–LUMO energy gap (ΔE = 2.724 eV) is smaller than that of the free ligand (3.273 eV) and significantly lower than typical Schiff-base ligands (≈ 3.7–3.8 eV) as presented in Fig. 832. This reduction in the energy gap implies improved electronic communication between the metal and ligand orbitals, enhancing chemical reactivity and charge-transfer efficiency. From a biological standpoint, a narrower HOMO–LUMO gap facilitates electron transfer to biomolecular targets such as enzyme active sites and nucleophilic residues, thereby promoting stronger metal–biomolecule interactions. Similar correlations between smaller ΔE values and enhanced antimicrobial and antioxidant activities have been reported for Ni(II) and Cu(II) Schiff-base complexes33,34.

Reactivity descriptors further substantiate these findings. The low hardness (η = 1.362 eV) and more negative chemical potential (µ = − 4.359 eV) indicate increased softness, polarizability and electron-exchange capability often associated with enhanced pharmacological responsiveness and reduced toxicity. The elevated electrophilicity index (ω = 7.973) suggests an excellent electron-accepting tendency, favoring interactions with biological nucleophiles and redox-active biomolecules.

The dipole moment of 6.64 D is moderate yet significant, indicating a balanced distribution of charge across the molecule. This polarity enhances solubility in polar solvents, improving bioavailability and membrane permeability, which are critical for biological efficacy. A moderately polar complex can effectively interact with both hydrophilic and hydrophobic biomolecular environments, a desirable trait for potential drug-like behavior.

Mulliken charge analysis identifies the Ni(II) ion as an electron acceptor (+ 0.932), while oxygen donor atoms (–0.565) exhibit substantial electron density, confirming the covalent contribution to the metal–ligand bonding. The redistribution of charge density, particularly over heteroatoms and aromatic rings, enhances molecular reactivity and interaction potential with biological targets.

MEP mapping (Fig. 9) visually distinguishes electron-rich (red) and electron-deficient (blue) zones, providing insight into possible reactive and hydrogen-bonding sites. These regions are consistent with observed Mulliken charge distributions and further rationalize the predicted binding affinity and reactivity patterns as shown in Table 6.

Topological studies

Topological analyses provide a graphical understanding of how electrons are distributed within a molecule, helping to identify regions of chemical reactivity and potential sites for biological interaction. Using the Molecular Surface Properties Approach (MSPA), parameters such as Average Local Ionization Energy (ALIE), Electron Localization Function (ELF), Localized Orbital Locator (LOL) and Interaction Region Indicator (IRI) were computed to visualize electron density and bonding characteristics of the Ni(II) complex.

ALIE maps indicate how easily electrons can be removed from different parts of the molecule. Areas with low ALIE values (blue regions in Fig. 10) correspond to sites that can readily donate electrons and are therefore more reactive toward electrophilic attack. In this complex, such regions are located near oxygen and nitrogen atoms, suggesting preferred binding zones for biomolecules or metal–protein interactions.

The paradigm for chemical bond characterisation for complex as (a) ESP (b) ALIE (c) ELF (d) LOL (e) IRI.

The ELF and LOL analyses describe how strongly electrons are localized within specific bonds or lone pairs. ELF plots highlight regions of high electron localization (red zones) associated with covalent bonding, whereas LOL maps visualize non-bonding electron pairs and areas of potential charge transfer. Together, these descriptors reveal the presence of delocalized π-electrons across the aromatic and imine regions, which enhances the molecule’s stability and ability to engage in π–π or hydrogen-bonding interactions with biological receptors.

The Interaction Region Indicator (IRI) analysis, employed in this study, is conceptually equivalent to the Non-Covalent Interaction–Reduced Density Gradient (NCI-RDG) approach, both of which visualize weak intermolecular forces based on electron density and its gradient. As shown in Fig. 10(e), the IRI map highlights regions of hydrogen bonding (blue), van der Waals dispersion (green) and steric repulsion (red), corresponding to attractive, neutral and repulsive interactions, respectively. The predominance of blue–green regions around oxygen, nitrogen and aromatic centers confirms the presence of stabilizing hydrogen bonds and dispersion forces, while localized red areas near the Ni(II) coordination core reflect minor steric congestion. These noncovalent interactions helps to predict how the molecule may orient and bind within biological environments, such as enzyme active sites or cell membranes.

Two-dimensional fingerprint plots further characterize stabilizing intermolecular contacts in both ligands and their complexes. As illustrated in Figs. 11 and 12, the major interactions within the ligand include O–H⋯O, C–H⋯O and N–O⋯O contacts. In the supramolecular architecture of the ligand, H⋯H interactions predominate, while N–O⋯O contacts are particularly significant in the metal complexes. These findings deepen our understanding of molecular stability and offer valuable guidance for ongoing research in supramolecular chemistry35.

The Ni(II) complex exhibited strong antibacterial activity, with inhibition zones significantly larger than those of the standard antibiotic chloramphenicol, showing an average 25–30% improvement in growth suppression for E. coli, K. pneumoniae and B. subtilis. However, the complex displayed limited activity against S. aureus, likely due to the bacterium’s thick peptidoglycan cell wall and multidrug-efflux mechanisms, which reduce compound permeability. Future studies could explore higher dosages or synergistic combination therapy to overcome this resistance.

The observed activity can be rationalized by considering the electronic and structural features of the complex. The reduced HOMO–LUMO energy gap (2.724 eV) and high electrophilicity index (ω = 7.973) enhance the molecule’s ability to accept electrons and interact with bacterial nucleophiles, promoting redox processes that can disrupt cellular function. Mulliken charge analysis indicates electron delocalization across the ligand system and a partially positive Ni(II) center, favoring coordination to biomolecular donor sites such as sulfur and oxygen atoms in enzymes and membrane proteins.

Additionally, the presence of electron-withdrawing nitro substituents increases lipophilicity, facilitating penetration through bacterial membranes and improving intracellular accumulation. Thus, the antibacterial mechanism likely involves membrane permeation, metal–ligand–protein interactions and redox-mediated enzyme inhibition, consistent with trends observed in other bioactive Ni(II) Schiff-base complexes36,37.

Evaluation of drug-likeness and pharmacokinetic properties

The evaluated Ni(II) complex satisfies Lipinski’s Rule of Five, confirming its potential as a viable and orally active drug candidate. Compliance with these molecular parameters supports good absorption, permeability and metabolic stability key factors in optimizing ADMET profiles and reducing downstream development risks42,43.

As summarized in Table 9, the water solubility value of − 3.860 denotes high solubility, which is beneficial for drug formulation and systemic delivery. Correspondingly, the bioavailability score of 0.85, obtained from SwissADME, indicates a strong likelihood of achieving effective plasma concentrations following oral administration. Together, these parameters suggest that the Ni(II) complex possesses excellent pharmacokinetic compatibility for oral therapeutic applications44–46.

While such groups often enhance lipophilicity and membrane permeability, they may also pose metabolic liabilities if not stabilized. Future optimization could involve selective modification or substitution of nitro groups with bioisosteres (e.g., cyano or sulfone groups) to maintain electronic effects while reducing potential toxicity risks.

Conclusion

A tetradentate Schiff base ligand was synthesized via condensation of ANA, HNB and 2FP, followed by coordination with Ni(II) to form the corresponding complex. The Ni(II) complex underwent comprehensive characterization using UV-Visible spectroscopy, FT-IR, 1H NMR and conductivity measurements to elucidate its structural and electronic features. Antimicrobial evaluation demonstrated that the Ni(II) complex exhibited strong inhibitory effects against all tested bacterial strains except Staphylococcus aureus at a concentration of 30 µL, which highlights its potential as an anti-infective agent. Quantum chemical analyses using the DFT-B3LYP/6-311++G(d,p) approach confirmed that the Ni(II) complex possesses chemical softness, which enhances its propensity for interactions with biological macromolecules and facilitates biochemical activity. Hirshfeld surface analysis revealed predominant non-covalent interactions stabilizing the supramolecular structure, while topological studies confirmed the existence of significant electronic and steric effects within the complex. Molecular docking simulations indicated a strong binding affinity between the Ni(II) complex and the bacterial receptor 2BL8, reflected by a docking score of − 9.1 kcal/mol, which signifies greater stability and binding efficiency compared to the uncoordinated ligand. Additional computational assessments, including ADMET profiling and molecular dynamics simulations, projected favorable pharmacokinetic properties for the Ni(II) complex, with optimal absorption, metabolism and toxicity profiles supporting its suitability for drug development. Collectively, these results establish the synthesized Ni(II) complex as a promising antimicrobial candidate, which merits further investigation for potential therapeutic applications.
