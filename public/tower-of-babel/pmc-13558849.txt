High‐Resolution Diffusion Tensor Imaging of the Hippocampus and Associations With Neuropsychiatric Symptoms in Lewy Body Diseases
Authors: Alexandra S. Budd, Kevin Grant Solar, Myrlene Gee, Krista Nelles, Brooklyn Mankasingh, Zahinoor Ismail, Eric E. Smith, Michael Martyna, Fang Ba, Sanjay Kalra, Christian Beaulieu, Richard Camicioli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTParkinson's disease (PD) encompasses motor and neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS) including depression, anxiety, and apathy. Cognitive subtypes—PD with mild cognitive impairment (PD‐MCI) and PD with dementia (PDD)—and the related Dementia with Lewy Bodies (DLB) exist within the spectrum of Lewy body disease (LBD). While NPS are a major comorbidity, established imaging biomarkers are not available, nor is the neuronal basis well known. Additionally, prevalence of NPS may be linked to cognitive status. The hippocampus is implicated in the development of NPS and may undergo microstructural changes that are not reflected in volume changes. High‐resolution diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) was used to identify hippocampal changes in LBD compared to healthy controls (HC) and examine the relationship with NPS within the LBD group, both through association with DTI measures as well as comparison of cognitive subgroups. The hippocampus was manually segmented in 38 LB spectrum participants (aged 58–87 years, 32% female) and 35 HC (aged 50–83 years, 54% female) on high‐resolution (1 × 1 × 1 mm3) DTI. The LBD group (LBD‐All) was divided into LBD‐Cognitively Impaired (LBD‐CI) and LBD‐Cognitively Unimpaired (LBD‐CU). Hippocampal volume, mean diffusivity (MD), and fractional anisotropy (FA) were extracted and compared across groups. Relationships between significantly different hippocampal measures and depression, anxiety, and apathy were explored within the LBD‐All group. Hippocampal FA was lower in LBD‐All relative to HC; however, no differences in MD or volume were observed. Hippocampal FA was positively associated with depression scores within LBD‐All. There were no differences in volume, MD, or FA between LBD‐CI and LBD‐CU. Lower hippocampal fractional anisotropy in the LBD spectrum relative to HC potentially reflects microstructure alterations; however, higher hippocampal FA in LBD with more severe depression suggests more complex microstructural changes such as altered synaptic density, Lewy neurite organization, or neuroplastic changes.

Introduction

The Lewy body disease (LBD) spectrum includes Parkinson's disease (PD), PD with mild cognitive impairment (PD‐MCI), PD with dementia (PDD), and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB). PD is the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disease (Beitz 2014), affecting an estimated 572 per 100,000 people over the age of 45 in North America (Marras et al. 2018). DLB is less common than PD, making up an estimated 5% of dementia cases in older patient groups (Hogan et al. 2016).

The essential feature of PD is parkinsonism, defined by bradykinesia in combination with rest tremor or with rigidity (Postuma et al. 2015). While PD is most recognized by these motor features, non‐motor features such as cognitive impairment, neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS), sleep disorders, autonomic symptoms, gastrointestinal symptoms, sensory symptoms, and others are also present (Chaudhuri et al. 2006; Postuma et al. 2015). Most patients with PD experience at least one non‐motor symptom (Hinkle and Pontone 2020) and these can have a greater impact on quality of life than motor symptoms (Prakash et al. 2016).

Neuropsychiatric symptoms are highly prevalent in PD (Mueller et al. 2018). In particular, depression, anxiety, and apathy can occur before motor symptoms (Nagy and Schrag 2019; Schapira et al. 2017) with approximately 35% of PD patients diagnosed with clinically significant depression (Reijnders et al. 2008). The prevalence of anxiety and apathy is not as well known, with ranges of between 13% and 43% of PD patients reporting anxiety (Dissanayaka et al. 2014) and 14%–70% reporting apathy (Nagy and Schrag 2019).

Cognitive impairment is a key non‐motor feature of PD (Hinkle and Pontone 2020) with dementia affecting approximately 80% of surviving patients 20 years beyond diagnosis (Hely et al. 2008). The degree and timing of cognitive decline are used to subtype PD. Specifically, if there is gradual decline in cognition without impairment of functional independence, an individual may be diagnosed with PD‐MCI (Litvan et al. 2012). A diagnosis of PDD requires the presence of dementia that develops gradually in established PD and impacts the individual's activities of daily living (ADLs) (Emre et al. 2007). If motor dysfunction and dementia appear within 1 year of each other, a patient would be classified as having DLB rather than PDD. Additionally, DLB can present with additional clinical features such as hallucinations, fluctuations in cognition, parkinsonism, and REM sleep behavior disorder (McKeith et al. 2017).

While PD is known for the loss of dopaminergic neurons within the substantia nigra, the hippocampus is highly susceptible to damage (Braak et al. 2004). The limbic system, which includes the hippocampus, is central to emotional regulation and behavior (Price and Drevets 2010). Both cognition and NPS have been linked to structural changes in the hippocampus (Ott et al. 2019), indeed NPS may be a consequence of adjusting to memory impairments from hippocampus dysfunction. Neuroimaging has been used to investigate the hippocampus in LBD, with measures of hippocampal volume among the most popular. Studies of hippocampal volume in the early stages of PD are inconsistent, while late stage disease seems to be accompanied by hippocampal atrophy (Banwinkler et al. 2022). Furthermore, changes in hippocampal volume have been associated with cognitive impairment, depression, and psychosis (Banwinkler et al. 2022). Research on the hippocampus in LBD is expanding, driven by studies beyond volume, due to advances in neuroimaging techniques such as diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) (Laurell et al. 2025), which generates diffusion metrics indicative of, yet not specific to, tissue microstructure (e.g., axonal/cell packing, extracellular space). Lewy body‐related histopathology in the hippocampus is primarily the deposition of alpha‐synuclein in Lewy neurites and Lewy bodies. There is evidence that synuclein damages white matter, in the case of PD (Yang et al. 2023) hence an imaging method such as DTI, which is routinely used to study white matter changes, is appropriate. High‐resolution DTI (i.e., 1 mm3 isotropic voxel volume) produces mean diffusion‐weighted images with sufficient definition allowing for manual segmentation of the hippocampus while minimizing partial volume effects on the diffusion metrics such as fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) (Treit et al. 2018) and has not been yet applied to LBD.

The first aim of this study was to use 1 mm isotropic DTI to investigate differences in hippocampal volumes, MD, and FA between participants with LBD (PD, PD‐MCI, PDD, and DLB) and healthy controls (HC). The second aim was to examine associations between NPS (depression, anxiety, and apathy), which are common features of LBD that affect quality of life, and hippocampal volumes and diffusion metrics, first within LBD participants and then comparing those with cognitive impairment (i.e., PD‐MCI, PDD, and DLB) and those without. We hypothesized that FA and volume would be lower while MD would be higher in the LBD group compared to HC, and that this effect would be more pronounced in those with cognitive impairment. We also hypothesized that more severe NPS would be associated with lower FA and hippocampal volumes, and higher MD values.

Methods

Participants

Participants were enrolled as part of the multicenter Canadian Consortium on Neurodegeneration in Aging's (CCNA) Comprehensive Assessment of Neurodegeneration and Dementia (COMPASS‐ND) study (Chertkow et al. 2019) and the Functional Assessment and Vascular Reactivity (FAVR)‐II study (Beaudin et al. 2022; McCreary et al. 2020; Peca et al. 2013); however, only the local cohort recruited in Edmonton, Alberta, was included in this study as these individuals underwent local imaging sequences in addition to the nationwide, multicenter study protocol. Additional healthy controls were included from a local normative development and aging study (Solar et al. 2021) that used the identical DTI protocol.

All participants provided informed consent for the collection and use of their data by the respective studies. All projects received ethics approval from the University of Alberta Research Ethics Board. A total of 40 participants (24 PD, 10 PD‐MCI, and 6 LBD [3 DLB/3 PDD]) were recruited into the LBD group (LBD‐All) through the University of Alberta Parkinson and Movement Disorders Program or community referral. Two participants in the PD group were excluded due to poor MRI quality, resulting in a total of 38 participants in the LBD‐All group.

Healthy controls (7 through FAVR‐II and 8 through COMPASS‐ND) were recruited from the community. An additional 20 HC participants were recruited through a local normative development study for a total of 35 controls with similar ages to the LBD‐All group.

Diagnoses of PD (Postuma et al. 2015), PD‐MCI (Litvan et al. 2012), PDD (Dubois et al. 2007; Emre et al. 2007; Postuma et al. 2015), and DLB (McKeith et al. 2017) were based on previously established criteria (Chertkow et al. 2019) applied by an experienced neurologist (coauthor R.C.). Exclusion criteria for participants in the COMPASS‐ND and FAVR‐II studies included previous traumatic brain injury or the presence of other diseases that significantly impact brain function (e.g., multiple sclerosis, Huntington's disease, or brain malignancies), symptomatic stroke within the past year, current substance use disorder, lack of a study partner, lack of proficiency in English, a Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score < 13, and any contraindications for MRI (Chertkow et al. 2019). Exclusion criteria for HC also included the presence of significant medical, psychiatric, or neurological conditions. Healthy controls for the local normative study were excluded if they reported any history of brain injury or any psychiatric or neurological disorders.

Participants diagnosed with PD‐MCI, PDD, or DLB were grouped together in the LBD‐Cognitively Impaired group (LBD‐CI), and the remaining participants with PD were placed in the LBD‐Cognitively Unimpaired (LBD‐CU) group. A total of 22 and 16 participants were included in the LBD‐CU and LBD‐CI groups, respectively.

Clinical Assessments

Participant sex (biological variable, assigned at birth) and age (at the time of the MRI scan) were recorded as well as years of education. For all individuals in the LBD‐All group and HC recruited by the COMPASS‐ND and FAVR‐II studies, general measures of health and cognition, such as cardiovascular risk measured using the Framingham Risk Score (FRS) (D'Agostino et al. 2008), and cognitive fluctuations as measured by the Mayo Fluctuations Scale (Ferman et al. 2004) were also included. Additionally, we included a measure of olfaction using the Brief Smell Identification Test (B‐SIT) (Aqrabawi and Kim 2018; Menon et al. 2013). For individuals in the LBD‐All group, we obtained disease duration in years, age at disease onset in years, the Movement Disorder Society‐sponsored revision of the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale part III total (MDS‐UPDRS‐III Total) (Goetz et al. 2007) evaluated in the ON state, Levodopa Equivalent Daily Dose (LEDD) in mg (Tomlinson et al. 2010), and Hoehn and Yahr (HY) stage (Hoehn and Yahr 1967). Neuroimaging

Image Acquisition

All participants underwent MRI at the Peter S. Allen MR Research Centre at the University of Alberta on a 3T Siemens Prisma (Erlangen, Germany) using a 64‐channel head coil. High‐resolution hippocampal DTI was obtained for all participants using a protocol (Treit et al. 2018) with the following parameters: single‐shot 2D EPI sequence (GRAPPA factor 2, 6/8 PPF, A/P phase encode), with FoV = 220 mm × 216 mm, matrix = 220 × 216, BW = 1420 Hz/px, 20 axial‐oblique slices parallel to the long axis of the hippocampus with 1 mm thickness and no gap, voxel size = 1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm with no interpolation, TE = 72 ms, TR = 2800 ms, diffusion time = 29 ms, b = 500 s/mm2 with 10 monopolar gradient directions with 10 averages, and 10 non‐diffusion‐weighted image (DWI) volumes, acquired in 5:18 min. Pre‐scan normalize was used to correct for B1 inhomogeneity and provide better visualization of the hippocampus. All image processing was performed with MRTrix3 (Tournier et al. 2019) using methods previously described (Solar et al. 2021) to produce mean DWIs, MD, and FA maps (Figure 1). A T1‐weighted anatomical whole‐brain scan was also obtained with the following parameters: FoV = 256 × 256 mm2, slice thickness = 1 mm, voxel size = 1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm, TR = 2300 ms, TE = 2.98 ms, TI = 400 ms, GRAPPA factor 2, and acquisition time = 5:21 min. Total intracranial volume (TIV) was obtained from the T1 anatomical images using FreeSurfer 6.0 (Fischl 2012) running on the CBRAIN platform (Sherif et al. 2014).

Hippocampal Segmentation

The right and left hippocampi of all participants were manually segmented by a single rater (coauthor A.S.B.), blinded to group status, on anonymized images using high‐resolution mean DWIs in ITK‐SNAP software (Yushkevich et al. 2006) as described in previous studies (Solar et al. 2021; Treit et al. 2018). Image quality was visually assessed to ensure hippocampi were appropriately captured within the slices obtained, then hippocampi were segmented according to the European Alzheimer's Disease Consortium and Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative Harmonized protocol (Boccardi et al. 2015; Frisoni et al. 2015), including the fimbria, alveus, and tail of the hippocampus, but excluding the subiculum (Solar et al. 2021). Tracing was performed along the axial‐oblique plane, then adjusted in the sagittal and coronal planes to ensure accurate delineation (Figure 1). After completing all segmentations, the same rater reviewed each one and adjusted as needed to improve accuracy and consistency across all participants. Volumes, FA, and MD were extracted from each whole hippocampus segmentation.

Reliability Analysis

The hippocampi of 14 participants were re‐segmented by the same rater (coauthor A.S.B.) to determine intra‐rater reliability and segmented by another experienced rater (coauthor K.G.S.) to determine inter‐rater reliability. Intra‐class correlation coefficients (ICC), obtained using software designed for evaluating medical image segmentations (Taha and Hanbury 2015), indicated both good inter‐rater (ICC = 0.862) and intra‐rater (ICC = 0.853) reliability (Koo and Li 2016).

Statistical Analysis

Participant Characteristics

Demographic characteristics and clinical measures are summarized as mean and standard deviation or by count of participants in categories, as required. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to evaluate normality for continuous variables. Comparison between HC and LBD‐All or LBD‐CU and LBD‐CI groups was made using the two‐sided two‐sample t‐test for continuous variables with normal distributions, the Wilcoxon test for continuous variables with non‐normal distributions, and Pearson's chi‐squared test for categorical variables. The p values reported for group descriptive parameters have not been corrected for multiple comparisons as these measures are provided to summarize the study populations.

Comparison of Diffusion Metrics Between Groups

Statistical analysis visualized data before conducting confirmatory data analysis (Fife 2020; Fife and Rodgers 2022). All statistical analysis was performed using R version 4.3.1 running in RStudio 2023.06.0 Build 421. The Flexplot package (Fife 2019) in R was used for visualization as well as model construction and comparison. As the DTI measures are hierarchical and not independent, meaning that right and left hippocampi are nested within each participant, mixed‐effects models (via the lmer4 package (Bates et al. 2015) in R) were used (Greenland 2000). As a consequence, a side factor (right or left) is included in all statistical models. The age dependence of hippocampal volume, MD, and FA was explored first using Locally Estimated Scatterplot Smoothing (LOESS). Using the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) for model comparisons (Schwarz 1978), the best polynomial model was determined for hippocampal volume, MD, or FA, comparing linear, cubic, and quadratic dependencies on age, in line with previous findings that have demonstrated non‐linear relationships between hippocampal diffusion metrics and age (Solar et al. 2021). The preferred model had a smaller BIC, compared to the less favored model (i.e., ΔBIC is negative). Sex was included as a factor in all the models. The significance of the main effect of group (HC or LBD‐All) and any interactions was determined by ΔBIC and the estimated p value for mixed models, obtained from the lmerTest library in R (Kuznetsova et al. 2017), by comparing a full model that included the effect or interaction of interest to a reduced model with the effect or interaction removed. An effect was determined to be significant if the ΔBIC was negative or if p ≤ 0.05 after correction with the False Discovery Rate (FDR) method for multiple comparisons across volume, MD and FA. In addition to testing for group effects, a paired t‐test was used to examine left and right hippocampal volumes to enable comparisons with the literature.

Association Between Hippocampal Measures Neuropsychiatric Symptoms

Relationships between NPS within the LBD‐All group were only considered for hippocampal measures that were significantly different between HC and LBD‐All. Two approaches were used. The primary analysis implemented regression models, and the secondary analysis dichotomized participants for NPS presence into “yes” or “no” based on established cut‐off values (Table 1). Specifically, for the primary regression analysis, confined to the LBD participants, we employed mixed‐effects models with the Geriatric Depression Scale 30‐item (GDS‐30), Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7‐item scale (GAD‐7), and Apathy Inventory Scale as predictors, adjusting for age and sex. The associations between diffusion metrics and NPS measures were evaluated using the following statistics: the NPS score regression coefficient, 95% confidence interval for the coefficient, and model comparison of a full model that included the NPS score and a reduced model with this effect removed, evaluated using ΔBIC as well as the estimated p value (FDR corrected).

For the secondary analysis, in LBD participants, cut‐offs were GDS‐30 > 11 for depression (Yesavage et al. 1982), GAD‐7 > 10 for anxiety (Spitzer et al. 2006), and Apathy Inventory Scale > 0 for apathy (Robert et al. 2002). Using a published algorithm (Guan et al. 2023; Ismail et al. 2017), the Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire (NPI‐Q) was used to derive the presence or absence of Mild Behavioral Impairment (MBI), which has demonstrated relevance in synucleinopathies (Yoon et al. 2019). Presence of each MBI‐C subdomain was defined as a score > 0. Again, ΔBIC and estimated p value (FDR corrected) are reported for model comparisons, with a negative ΔBIC or corrected p‐value ≤ 0.05 indicating a significant difference.

Results

Participants

Of the 38 individuals with LBD diagnoses included in the analysis, 12 (32%) were female. In the control group, there were 35 individuals, of which 19 (54%) were female (Table 2). The sex distribution was significantly different between the LBD‐All and HC groups (p = 0.05). The LBD‐All group was not significantly older than the HC group (HC: 66.7 ± 6.8 years, range 50–83 years; LBD‐All: 69.5 ± 7.2 years, range 58–87 years; p = 0.08). Olfaction, as measured by the B‐SIT, was impaired in the LBD‐All participants compared to HCs (p = 0.002) consistent with LBD pathology (Doty 2012). The LBD‐All participants did not differ significantly from HCs on the Mayo Fluctuations Scale (p = 0.06) or the MoCA (p = 0.1), but performed significantly worse on the specific cognitive domains of memory, executive function, and processing speed (p < 0.001 for all z‐scores). Within the LBD‐All group, the LBD‐CI participants were older (LBD‐CU: 66.8 ± 6.2 years, range 58–78 years; LBD‐CI: 73.2 ± 7.0 years, range 62–87 years; p = 0.006) with greater cardiovascular risk (FRS: p = 0.01). The LBD‐CU group fared better than the LBD‐CI group on all cognitive measures (p < 0.001 for MoCA, memory z‐score, and processing speed z‐score; p = 0.002 for executive function z‐score) except for Mayo Fluctuations Scale (p = 0.3). The HC and LBD‐All group did not differ significantly in the proportion of each group taking medication for NPS (p = 0.06); however, HCs exhibited fewer NPS as measured by NPI‐Q Severity Total Score (p < 0.001) and some of the MBI subdomains (decreased motivation: p = 0.01, emotional affective dysregulation: p = 0.001, impulse dyscontrol: p = 0.04). With respect to depression, anxiety, and apathy, the LBD‐All group scored higher on all these measures compared to the HC group (GDS‐30: p = 0.004, GAD‐7: p = 0.05, and Apathy Inventory Score: p = 0.008). Within the LBD group, there were no differences in NPS measures between the LBD‐CU and LBD‐CI group except for Apathy Inventory Score (p = 0.002). The LBD‐CU and LBD‐CI groups did not differ in disease duration or LEDD (p = 0.9 for both measures); however, the LBD‐CI had a later age of onset compared to the LBD‐CU group (p = 0.02), with greater motor impairment (p = 0.03 for MDS‐UPDRS III) and greater severity of disease (p = 0.04 for HY stage).

Model Building and Covariate Selection

The hippocampus volumes, MD, and FA for the HC and LBD‐All groups are shown in Figure 2 and Table 3. Statistical models were explored to determine if age, TIV (for hippocampal volumes), or any interactions were needed in the final analyses. For all three hippocampal metrics, LOESS suggested a dependence on age (Figure S1 and Table S1). Hippocampal volumes declined with age, and adding a linear age term improved model fit (no age dependence vs. linear: ΔBIC = −2.9, p = 0.005). Similarly, adding a linear age term improved model fit for MD, which had a positive relationship with age (no age dependence vs. linear: ΔBIC = −0.58, p = 0.02), as did adding a linear age term for FA, which declined with age (no age dependence versus linear: ΔBIC = −4.03, p = 0.003). Quadratic or cubic dependence on age was not preferred over a linear dependence for all measures (Table S1). With respect to hippocampal volumes, adding a linear term for TIV further improved model fit (no dependence on TIV vs. linear: ΔBIC = −5.45, p = 0.001). While including sex did not significantly improve model fit for volume or FA, it did improve fit for MD (volume: ΔBIC = +3.73, p = 0.3; MD: ΔBIC = −1.25, p = 0.05; FA: ΔBIC = +2.58, p = 0.1), and was included in all models because (i) HC and LBD‐All groups had significantly different sex distributions, and (ii) NPS has been found to vary with sex (Couture et al. 2024). Interaction terms did not improve model fit for any hippocampal metric (Table S2). To summarize, our model exploration indicates that hippocampus volume, MD, and FA all have a linear dependence on age, and sex is included for the reasons stated above. Additionally, hippocampus volume is linearly dependent on TIV.

Mixed Models Group Comparisons

To determine if there was a difference between the HC and LBD‐All participants, models were compared with and without the group factor. A group effect was only consistently favored for FA, indicating FA (and not MD or volume) varied between groups, with lower hippocampal FA in LBD‐All compared to HC (Table 3). Within the LBD cohort, hippocampal volume, MD, and FA did not significantly differ between LBD‐CI and LBD‐CU after FDR correction (Table 3). The right hippocampus was larger than the left across both HC and LBD‐All participants (by 7.7% and 6.7% respectively, paired t‐test: p < 0.001), consistent with prior literature (Malykhin et al. 2008; Nobis et al. 2019; Pedraza et al. 2004; Voineskos et al. 2015; Wellington et al. 2013).

Association Between Fractional Anisotropy and Neuropsychiatric Measures in the Lewy Body Group

When exploring the associations between hippocampal FA and NPS scores (Figure 3a–c and Table 4), only depression (GDS‐30) was significantly associated: specifically, FA was higher with greater GDS‐30 score (FDR‐corrected p = 0.03). NPI Severity, Anxiety (GAD‐7), and apathy (Apathy Inventory Score) were not significantly associated with FA (FDR‐corrected p = 0.08 for all). A secondary, threshold‐based analysis (Figure 3d–f and Table 5) in which LBD‐All participants were divided into those above and below previously defined cut‐offs for each NPS confirmed the pattern. Participants above the cut‐off for GDS‐30 scores, suggesting clinically significant depression (Yesavage et al. 1982), had significantly higher hippocampal FA compared to those below the cut‐off. No differences emerged when comparing participants above and below cut‐offs for clinically significant anxiety (Spitzer et al. 2006) or apathy (Robert et al. 2002).

Discussion

The first objective of this study was to identify differences in hippocampal volume, MD, and FA between HC and LBD participants. Hippocampal FA was significantly lower in the LBD‐All group compared to HC; however, we did not find a group difference in volume or MD. Our second objective was to investigate the associations between hippocampal measures and NPS for participants within the LBD group. We focused on FA because this was the only measure showing a significant between‐group difference. We found that hippocampal FA was positively associated with severity of depression as measured by GDS‐30. When participants were grouped by presence or absence of depression using a threshold score of greater than 11 on GDS‐30, the LBD‐All participants with depression had a higher average hippocampal FA than those without depression. No significant associations between hippocampal FA and anxiety, as measured by the GAD‐7, or apathy, as measured by the Apathy Inventory Score, emerged. Also, we did not find an association with MBI‐C subdomains after FDR correction, though apathy showed a trend.

To our knowledge, this is the first study reporting significant differences in hippocampal FA between HC and LBD participants. Other studies looking at hippocampal FA between these groups have found no significant differences (Hopeet al. 2019; Kamagata et al. 2017; Kim et al. 2013; Pyatigorskaya et al. 2016; Schulz et al. 2018). While we did not find a difference in hippocampal volumes between HC and LBD groups, smaller hippocampal volumes in PD and PDD have been reported (Camicioli et al. 2003; Laakso et al. 1996); however, the hippocampus is relatively preserved in LBD compared to Alzheimer's disease (Burton et al. 2009). One study reported no difference in hippocampal volume between PD without dementia and controls (Carlesimo et al. 2012). Similarly, MD did not significantly differ between our LBD and HC groups; however, previous studies looking at the difference between hippocampal MD in LBD participants compared to controls either showed that MD was increased in those with LBD (Carlesimo et al. 2012; Kim et al. 2013; Pyatigorskaya et al. 2016) or there was no difference between groups (Kamagata et al. 2017; Schulz et al. 2018). One difference between prior studies and ours is the DTI protocol. The higher resolution of our protocol (1 mm3) allows for better visualization and delineation of the hippocampus, as well as mitigating partial volume effects, which can result in increased MD when atrophy is present and may account for the higher MD in LBD groups reported in the literature compared to our findings. Another difference is the method used to delineate the hippocampus. In studies that used manual segmentation, either only the hippocampus body was measured (Camicioli et al. 2003) or the subiculum was included (Laakso et al. 1996). Other studies (Carlesimo et al. 2012; Hope et al. 2019; Kim et al. 2013; Pyatigorskaya et al. 2016) utilized automated segmentation methods in FSL (FIRST) (Patenaude 2007; Patenaude et al. 2011) or FreeSurfer (Fischl 2012) that include the subiculum, in contrast to our method which did not. A recent study (Ye et al. 2024) highlighted the association between volume loss in the subiculum and LBD.

To our knowledge, only one prior study has looked at correlations between non‐motor symptoms and hippocampal FA using an region of interest method (Wei et al. 2016). They found hippocampal FA was negatively correlated with the Non‐Motor Symptoms Scale mood score, whereas we found a positive correlation with a depression measure, the GDS‐30. Different symptom scales may have played a role in these contrasting results, as well as differences in study population. Wei and colleagues (Wei et al. 2016) had a slightly larger sample (43 PD) with HY ranging from 1 to 4, whereas HY ranged from 1 to 3 in our study, with most participants scoring 2. In addition, the average disease duration for their cohort was shorter than ours (4.6 vs. 6.3 years).

While we did not find a significant difference in hippocampal FA in LBD participants who were grouped based on the presence or absence of each MBI‐C subdomain, our results prior to FDR correction suggest that symptoms associated with decreased motivation may also be associated with FA. Interestingly, while the decreased motivation subdomain does not correspond to depressive symptoms per se, a number of the questions are overlapping (Ismail et al. 2017), which may account for why we see similar associations. The lack of significance in our study may be partially due to the inclusion of questions concerning apathy in this MBI domain while the GDS‐30 does not query apathy specifically.

While higher FA is generally considered to be good, unexpectedly, our results suggest that more severe depressive symptoms were associated with higher, rather than lower, hippocampal FA. Our measurement method has good inter‐rater reliability and we observed lower hippocampal FA with increased age in the HC group, consistent with a study in HC using the same imaging and segmentation methods (Solar et al. 2021); therefore, our results may be explained by considering that higher FA in gray matter can arise from lower complexity of fiber orientation or loss of crossing fibers. In depression, there may be a loss of complexity in certain pathways within the hippocampus, simplifying local geometry and increasing FA. Taken together, these findings point to a process of neuronal remodeling within the hippocampal pathways along with symptoms of depression in participants with LBD.

The unexpected trend of higher FA associated with more severe depression should be considered in the following context: (1) while DTI is most commonly used for white matter, the hippocampus is composed of both white and gray matter, (2) higher FA in a neurodegenerative disease group is not unprecedented, and (3) FA is an indirect measure of underlying tissue characteristics and should be interpreted with caution.

With regard to the first point above, DTI is most commonly used to examine white matter (WM) because it allows for virtual mapping of highly anisotropic major tracts and provides metrics like MD and FA indicating non‐specific changes in tissue microstructure. Interpretation of DTI metrics in gray matter (GM) is not as straightforward, but can indicate changes in extracellular space, cellular barriers, or dendritic branching. Interpretation of DTI metrics in the hippocampus is complicated as it is largely composed of GM, but also has WM features including the fimbria and alveus (Weston et al. 2015). In addition, GM is particularly susceptible to partial volume effects because it contains convolutions filled with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) (Weston et al. 2015). Some have argued that, compared to FA, MD is a better (or at least more easily interpretable) measure for GM (Chiapponi et al. 2013). The LBD group in our study had significantly lower FA than the HC group, suggesting the hippocampi of participants in the LBD group underwent a structural change that lowered FA relative to HCs; but within the LBD group, depression was associated with higher FA.

With respect to the second point, higher FA in PD compared to HCs is not unprecedented. For example, Mole et al. (Mole et al. 2016) reported higher FA along motor pathways in PD compared to HCs. The authors argue that early compensation within those regions, such as axonal sprouting or neuronal re‐routing in response to decreased neuronal input, might lead to higher FA. A functional connectivity study of MBI in PD reported increased connectivity with higher MBI in circuits involving the hippocampus (Lang et al. 2020), complementing our findings. The authors suggest a compensatory mechanism may be responsible. However, PD is not diagnosed until symptoms become clinically apparent, long after pathology has appeared in the brain and compensatory mechanisms begin to break down (Holtbernd et al. 2021). Consequently, while FA may be higher in early PD relative to HC, it could decline over time (Holtbernd et al. 2021). While we did not find that the LBD group had higher FA than the controls, it may be that a compensatory mechanism was not sufficient to elevate FA above controls, but was enough to result in our observation of a higher FA with respect to greater depression within the LBD group alone.

With respect to the third and final point, FA is an indirect measure of underlying tissue orientation; further evidence is needed to determine the cause of any changes (Jones et al. 2013). Interpretation of a measure obtained on the scale of millimeters as a direct indication of processes occurring on the cellular level (on the scale of microns) should be made with extreme caution. As indicated in the discussion above, the hippocampus is a mix of white and gray matter, which could lead to unexpected trends in FA.

Our statistical models for hippocampal FA and volume included linear dependence on age, in contrast to other reports of a non‐linear dependence (Carlesimo et al. 2012; Malykhin et al. 2017; Nobis et al. 2019; Solar et al. 2021; Walhovd et al. 2005; Yang et al. 2013). A previous life span study (Solar et al. 2021) reported a quadratic relationship between age and both hippocampal volume and FA, while we found a linear dependence, a difference which may result from our focus on an older population. The same study also reported a cubic dependence for MD, which contrasts with our finding of a linear age dependence; however, as can be seen in figure 5 of that prior published study (Solar et al. 2021), the hippocampal MD decreases most notably between the ages of 5 to 25 years, and remains fairly constant between 25 and 75 years. The minimum age in our population was 50 years; therefore, our finding is not necessarily inconsistent. Some studies assume a linear dependence between hippocampal measures and age (Den Heijer et al. 2012; Pereira et al. 2014), while a meta‐analysis reported no dependence on age for hippocampal volume (Tan et al. 2016). Different findings in the literature support our approach of determining the best age dependence using our data, rather than assuming the degree of dependence based on the literature. Again, due to the relatively narrow age range in our study, a different polynomial may provide a better fit than the ones used for a lifespan study.

While we included sex in our statistical models and found that sex improved our model for MD, sex differences in the hippocampus are not well‐established in the literature, with mixed findings reported (Malykhin et al. 2017; Narvacan et al. 2017; Nobis et al. 2019; Solar et al. 2021; Tan et al. 2016) or assumed dependence or independence on sex (Anblagan et al. 2018; Carlesimo et al. 2010; Den Heijer et al. 2012; Langnes et al. 2020; Pereira et al. 2014; Yang et al. 2013). In particular for hippocampal volume, intracranial volume is occasionally used as a surrogate for sex in statistical models (Walhovd et al. 2005), with one report suggesting hippocampal volume loss is accelerated in older females compared to males (Nobis et al. 2019). The interaction between age and sex with respect to diffusion metrics is not often considered in the literature; however, a few reports have indicated that this interaction is not significant (Malykhin et al. 2017; Narvacan et al. 2017).

A key strength of this study comes from direct segmentation of the hippocampus on mean DWI data, eliminating the need for co‐registration between DWI and T1, resulting in increased measurement accuracy by reducing registration error and partial‐volume contamination from adjacent CSF/tissue (Treit et al. 2018). Additionally, accuracy was improved through the use of blinded manual segmentation, the gold standard to which automated segmentation is compared (Patenaude et al. 2011).

While we used validated measures for NPS, one limitation is the inherent subjectivity of neuropsychiatric tools. Within the LBD participants, there is potential heterogeneity with respect to insight regarding disease‐related changes. Some participants may be more aware of neuropsychiatric changes and report them, resulting in a more varied response from this population with respect to NPS scores. We have somewhat mitigated this issue by also including the NPI‐Q which is evaluated by the study partner rather than the participant, although study partners are also heterogeneous with respect to observation of neuropsychiatric symptoms. A limitation with respect to hippocampus volumes is the use of TIV from FreeSurfer, which is known to be biased (Klasson et al. 2018); however, this measure is easily obtained and validated to serve as a nuisance variable to control for variations in head size in volumetric studies (Buckner et al. 2004). Another limitation of this study is the small sample size. Furthermore, we cannot know if there was co‐pathology with amyloid‐β, which is characteristic of Alzheimer's disease and may be associated with cognitive impairment in PD and DLB (Compta et al. 2011). Given the cross‐sectional study design, inferences about how NPS and hippocampal changes evolve over time are constrained. Future longitudinal studies could provide more insight regarding higher FA with worsening depression.

Another direction that could be taken is using measures of associated GM regions adjacent to the hippocampus proper, such as the entorhinal cortex and the subiculum, as well as associated limbic WM tracts (e.g., the fornix, cingulum, and uncinate fasciculus) (Pascalau et al. 2018) to compare any diffusion changes that may occur along with NPS.

The current study fills a gap in the literature regarding the knowledge of hippocampal diffusion and LBD. Few studies have looked at how LBD impacts hippocampal microstructure, and this study suggests the possibility of remodeling occurring in individuals with LBD. Furthermore, this study looks at the association between NPS and diffusion. As NPS broadly impact quality of life in individuals with LBD, it is crucial to study their relationship with the brain, which, with continued research, could lead to early identification of individuals predisposed to NPS for early intervention.

Conclusions

Our study shows that hippocampal FA is altered in the LBD spectrum. The relationship between diffusion changes and NPS in LBD has been relatively understudied compared to motor symptoms, despite their importance for quality of life. Our study suggests a two‐level relationship between depression and hippocampal FA in the Lewy body spectrum of disorders: FA was lower overall in participants with LBD than in HCs, yet higher FA was linearly associated with depression severity within the LBD group. These opposing directions demonstrate that, beyond diffuse neurodegeneration, a complex process of neuronal remodeling may be responsible for changes within the hippocampus in LBD.
