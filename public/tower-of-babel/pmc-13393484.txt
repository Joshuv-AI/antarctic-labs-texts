Relative handgrip strength mediates the association between metabolic syndrome and health-related quality of life among Korean adult women
Authors: Park HyoEun, Na Jin-Hee, Kim SeokHwan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to examine the association between metabolic syndrome and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) among Korean adult women and to investigate the mediating role of relative handgrip strength in this relationship.ResultsData from 8771 women aged 19–80 years from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2016–2018) were analyzed. Metabolic syndrome was significantly associated with lower HRQoL (B = − 0.006, p = .041). Relative handgrip strength showed a significant positive association with HRQoL (B = 0.031, p < .001) and a negative association with metabolic syndrome (B = − 0.027, p = .009). The indirect effect of metabolic syndrome on HRQoL through relative handgrip strength was statistically significant (B = − 0.001, 95% CI: −0.0013 to − 0.0001), indicating a partial mediating effect.

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a cluster of metabolic abnormalities, including abdominal obesity, hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, and hypertension, and is a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and premature mortality worldwide [1, 9]. The prevalence of MetS has increased steadily, with a particularly pronounced rise among women after menopause [2, 7]. Beyond its physiological consequences, MetS has been associated with substantial impairments in health-related quality of life (HRQoL), including reduced physical functioning, activity limitations, chronic fatigue, and psychosocial distress. However, few studies in South Korea have comprehensively examined the impact of MetS on women’s HRQoL, and empirical research exploring mediating mechanisms involving modifiable physical factors remains limited.

HRQoL reflects individuals’ perceived physical, mental, and social well-being and has become a core outcome indicator in modern healthcare as the focus has shifted from survival to quality of life [3, 4]. Handgrip strength (HGS) has emerged as a simple yet reliable biomarker of overall muscular strength and general health, and previous studies have shown that lower HGS is associated with cardiovascular disease, cognitive decline, depression, and reduced quality of life [5, 11]. Recent evidence further indicates that lower HGS is linked to a higher prevalence of MetS and an increased risk of physical limitations and poor HRQoL [6].

Metabolic syndrome has been associated with reduced health-related quality of life (HRQoL), particularly through limitations in physical functioning, increased fatigue, and decreased daily activity. These functional impairments may contribute substantially to poorer perceived health and well-being [30]. Handgrip strength is a widely used indicator of overall muscle strength and physical function and has been linked to both metabolic health and HRQoL. Relative handgrip strength, which adjusts grip strength for body size, has been proposed as a more sensitive indicator of metabolic health than absolute grip strength. Lower grip strength has been associated with a higher prevalence of metabolic syndrome as well as poorer physical and psychosocial aspects of quality of life. Therefore, grip strength may represent an important functional factor in understanding the relationship between metabolic syndrome and HRQoL [30]. This relationship is particularly relevant for women, who are more vulnerable to age- and hormone-related muscle loss across the life course. Reduced muscle strength in women has been associated not only with physical functional decline but also with decreased independence, self-efficacy, and social participation [6]. Several studies have suggested that HGS may function as a mediating variable in the relationship between MetS and HRQoL. For example, Meng et al. [12] reported that MetS indirectly influenced HRQoL through HGS, highlighting the importance of muscular strength as a pathway linking metabolic abnormalities to quality of life.

Despite these findings, studies examining the structural relationships among MetS, HGS, and HRQoL in South Korea remain scarce, particularly those focusing on adult women and explicitly testing mediating effects. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to examine the association between metabolic syndrome and health-related quality of life among South Korean adult women and to investigate the mediating role of handgrip strength in this relationship. By clarifying this mechanism, the study aims to provide empirical evidence to support multidimensional intervention strategies that integrate metabolic risk management with muscle strength enhancement to improve women’s quality of life.

Methods

Study participants and data collection

This study utilized data from the 7th Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES VII, 2016–2018), conducted nationwide by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency. A probability proportional sampling method was employed to select participants from across the country. Based on relevant study variables, a total of 8,771 women were selected as the final study sample (Fig. 1). The variables used in the analysis were derived from prior research and consisted of six items: the dependent variable (health-related quality of life), the independent variable (metabolic syndrome), the mediating variable (relative handgrip strength), and general characteristics including sociodemographic factors (marital status, education level, and income level).

Operational definitions and measurement tools

Health-related quality of life (dependent variable)

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) was measured using the EuroQol-5 Dimension (EQ-5D) instrument developed by the EuroQoL Group [13], which is widely used for clinical and health economic evaluations. The EQ-5D assesses health status across five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each dimension is rated on three levels (1 = no problems, 2 = some problems, 3 = severe problems), generating 243 possible health states (3⁵).

Health states were converted into a single index score ranging from 0 (death) to 1 (perfect health) using a value set. HRQoL can be expressed either as a profile across the five dimensions or as a composite index score, with the latter providing an overall assessment of health status [14]. While earlier studies in Korea commonly applied UK-based value sets due to the lack of national weights [15], this study calculated EQ-5D index scores using a Korean population-based value set developed by Kang et al. [16].

Metabolic syndrome (independent variable)

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) was defined according to the modified National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP-ATP III) criteria proposed by the American Heart Association and the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute [16]. Participants were classified as having MetS if they met three or more of the following criteria [17]: abdominal obesity (waist circumference ≥ 90 cm for men and ≥ 85 cm for women), triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL, HDL cholesterol < 40 mg/dL for men and < 50 mg/dL for women, blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg, and fasting blood glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL.

Relative handgrip strength: mediating variable

Handgrip strength was measured using a digital grip strength dynamometer (T.K.K 5401, Japan). Participants stood upright and were instructed to squeeze the dynamometer with maximum effort for three seconds. Starting with the dominant hand, both hands were alternately tested three times, with a 60-second rest period following measurements for both hands. Dominant hand strength was defined as the maximum value among the three trials with the dominant hand. Absolute handgrip strength was calculated as the sum of the highest values from each hand [18]. Relative handgrip strength was defined as the absolute grip strength divided by body mass index (BMI) [18]. In this study, relative handgrip strength was used as the mediating variable.

Marital status, educational level, income level: general characteristics

This study aimed to examine the effect of MetS on HRQoL, and therefore general characteristics were only used to identify the frequency and distribution of variables related to HRQoL. Four characteristics were included: age, marital status, educational level, and income level.

Marital status was coded as follows: separated, widowed, divorced, and others were classified as unmarried (0); cohabiting with a spouse was classified as married [1].

Educational level was categorized as: elementary school or less (0), middle school (1), high school (2), and college or higher (3).

Income level was categorized as: low (0), lower-middle (1), upper-middle (2), and high (3).

Statistical analysis

For statistical analysis, SPSS/PC+ version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used. Descriptive statistics were performed to summarize participants’ general characteristics.

Pearson’s correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships among the main variables.

To examine the mediating role of relative handgrip strength in the association between metabolic syndrome (MetS) and health-related quality of life (HRQoL), mediation analysis was conducted using Hayes’ PROCESS macro (Model 4) [19]. PROCESS macro Model 4 was selected because it represents a simple mediation model that estimates the indirect effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable through a single mediator. In this model, metabolic syndrome was entered as the independent variable, HRQoL (EQ-5D index score) as the dependent variable, and relative handgrip strength as the mediator.

Age, body mass index (BMI), marital status, education level, and income were included as covariates because these sociodemographic and health-related factors are commonly associated with metabolic syndrome and HRQoL in population-based studies [21, 26, 30].

The indirect effect of metabolic syndrome on HRQoL through relative handgrip strength was estimated using a bootstrapping procedure with 5,000 resamples [20]. The mediation effect was considered statistically significant if the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the indirect effect did not include zero.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The mean (SD) HRQoL score for married individuals was 0.952 (0.093), which was significantly higher than that for unmarried individuals (0.919 (0.136), p <.001). Similarly, the mean (SD) relative handgrip strength was higher in married individuals (2.000 (0.476)) than in unmarried individuals (1.850 (0.529)). Higher education levels were associated with higher HRQoL scores (p <.001). In particular, those with a college degree or higher showed the highest mean EQ-5D scores. A similar pattern was observed for relative handgrip strength, with higher values reported among individuals with higher education levels. Higher income levels were also associated with better quality of life (p <.001). Participants in the higher income group reported substantially higher EQ-5D scores compared to those in the lower income group. Likewise, relative handgrip strength increased with higher income levels. Notably, individuals with higher socioeconomic status exhibited consistently higher HRQoL and relative handgrip strength (p <.001).

Correlations among the main variables

Pearson’s correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships among the main variables. Age was positively correlated with body mass index (BMI) (r =.247, p <.001) and negatively correlated with relative handgrip strength (r = −.479, p <.001) and health-related quality of life (EQ-5D) (r = −.360, p <.001). BMI was negatively correlated with relative handgrip strength (r = −.521, p <.001) and health-related quality of life (r = −.155, p <.001). In contrast, relative handgrip strength showed a significant positive correlation with health-related quality of life (r =.318, p <.001)[Table 2].

The results showed that metabolic syndrome had a significant negative effect on relative handgrip strength (B = − 0.027, p <.01). Relative handgrip strength, in turn, had a significant positive effect on health-related quality of life (B = 0.031, p <.001). In addition, the direct effect of metabolic syndrome on health-related quality of life remained statistically significant (B = − 0.006, p <.05).

The indirect effect of metabolic syndrome on health-related quality of life through relative handgrip strength was − 0.001. The significance of the indirect effect was tested using a bootstrapping procedure with 5000 resamples. The 95% confidence interval ranged from − 0.0013 to − 0.0001, which did not include zero, indicating that the indirect effect was statistically significant.

These findings suggest that relative handgrip strength partially mediates the relationship between metabolic syndrome and health-related quality of life. In other words, metabolic syndrome is associated with lower handgrip strength, which in turn contributes to a reduction in health-related quality of life. According to these results, handgrip strength plays a significant mediating role in the effect of metabolic syndrome on health-related quality of life (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study analyzed 8,771 adult women aged 19–80 years to examine the effect of metabolic syndrome (MetS) on health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and to test the mediating effect of handgrip strength (HGS) in this relationship. The findings revealed that MetS was significantly and negatively associated with HRQoL, while HGS partially mediated this relationship. Thus, the adverse impact of MetS on HRQoL was partly explained by a decline in HGS, and HGS also functioned as an independent positive factor. These results highlight the need to consider not only MetS but also HGS in the development of HRQoL improvement programs, suggesting the utility of HGS as a mediating variable.

Relationship between metabolic syndrome and HRQoL

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a cluster of metabolic risk factors, including abdominal obesity, hypertension, hyperglycemia, hypertriglyceridemia, and low HDL cholesterol, which substantially increase the risk of cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes. In women, particularly after menopause, hormonal changes elevate the risk of MetS and are closely associated with declines in health-related quality of life (HRQoL). Previous studies comparing sex differences have consistently shown that women with MetS experience significantly lower HRQoL than women without MetS, whereas such differences are often less evident or absent among men [6, 21, 22, 30].

Several international studies have reported that the detrimental effects of MetS on HRQoL are more pronounced in women. For example, stronger associations between MetS and impaired HRQoL were observed among women in Iran and Bangladesh, particularly in physical health domains [23, 24]. Other studies have similarly demonstrated that MetS is a consistent predictor of lower HRQoL, with effects being especially significant among women [22, 25, 26]. Research conducted in non-urban populations further confirmed these sex-specific differences, showing that women with MetS had a greater risk of poor HRQoL compared to men [27].

Postmenopausal women, especially those more than 10 years beyond menopause, have been shown to report substantially lower HRQoL than premenopausal women [28]. In addition, persistent MetS has been associated with marked declines in mental HRQoL, particularly in vitality and mental health domains [29]. Systematic reviews and population-based studies across diverse settings consistently conclude that MetS is associated with impaired HRQoL, with stronger and more consistent effects observed in women [30–32]. Similar findings have been reported among immigrant and clinical populations, further reinforcing the vulnerability of women with MetS to diminished HRQoL [33].

Collectively, these findings align with and support the present study, underscoring the substantial negative impact of MetS on women’s quality of life [21–26, 30].

Mediating role of handgrip strength

Handgrip strength (HGS) is a well-established indicator of overall muscular strength and age-related decline in physical function. In this study, HGS partially mediated the association between metabolic syndrome (MetS) and health-related quality of life (HRQoL), indicating that the negative impact of MetS on HRQoL was partly explained by reduced muscle strength. This finding suggests that HGS functions not only as a marker of physical function but also as an independent protective factor influencing quality of life.

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated the clinical value of HGS. Lower HGS has been associated with adverse outcomes including depression, falls, functional disability, and premature mortality [34], while women with lower HGS have shown a higher risk of MetS [35]. Large cohort studies further reported that decreases in HGS were associated with increased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [6], a finding confirmed across multi-country cohorts [36, 37].

HGS has also been identified as a predictor of chronic disease outcomes. Higher HGS has been linked to lower diabetes incidence in women [38], whereas lower HGS has been associated with multimorbidity [39]. In Korean adults, relative HGS adjusted for body weight or body mass index was more strongly associated with MetS and HRQoL than absolute HGS, particularly among women [40]. A systematic review reported that low HGS was associated with a substantially higher risk of MetS, with stronger associations observed in women [41]. In addition, women with lower HGS demonstrated poorer HRQoL across pain, mobility, and daily activity domains [42], and similar associations have been reported in specific clinical populations such as breast cancer survivors [43].

Emerging mediation studies further support the role of HGS as a pathway linking metabolic and functional health outcomes. Previous research has shown that HGS mediates associations between general health status and cardiovascular risk [44], as well as relationships involving sleep duration, metabolic rate [45], multimorbidity, anemia [46], and mental health outcomes [47]. Intervention studies reinforce these findings, demonstrating that combined aerobic and resistance exercise programs can improve HGS, physical function, and HRQoL among women with MetS [48].

Taken together, these findings support the conclusion that MetS negatively affects HRQoL and that this relationship is partially mediated by HGS. This underscores the importance of interventions that address not only metabolic risk factors but also muscle strength maintenance and enhancement to improve quality of life among women with MetS.

Policy implications

The findings of this study suggest that, in managing the health of adult women, it is essential not only to prevent and manage MetS but also to improve quality of life through the maintenance and enhancement of muscular strength. Therefore, public health policies should integrate muscle-strengthening programs alongside strategies for the prevention of MetS in women. For instance, implementing strength training programs to improve handgrip strength may help reduce the risk of metabolic syndrome and ultimately enhance quality of life.

These discussions offer important implications for the development of effective health management strategies for adult women based on the study’s results. Future research should aim to conduct more comprehensive analyses that include a broader age range and male participants. Although clinically meaningful cutoff values for relative handgrip strength have not yet been firmly established, the mean values observed in this study may serve as preliminary reference data for future research and clinical applications.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, due to the cross-sectional design of the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, causal relationships among metabolic syndrome, handgrip strength, and health-related quality of life cannot be established. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal. Longitudinal studies are warranted to clarify the temporal and causal pathways underlying these relationships.

Second, health-related quality of life was assessed using the EQ-5D, which is a self-reported measure and may be subject to reporting bias. Although the EQ-5D is a widely validated and commonly used instrument in population-based studies, subjective perceptions of health status may not fully capture objective functional limitations.

Third, although key sociodemographic variables were adjusted for in the regression models, residual confounding by unmeasured factors such as physical activity level, dietary habits, or comorbid conditions cannot be completely excluded. These factors may influence both metabolic syndrome and muscle strength and could affect health-related quality of life.

Finally, this study focused exclusively on adult women in South Korea; therefore, the findings may not be directly generalizable to men or to populations in other cultural or ethnic contexts. Future studies including diverse populations and comparative analyses by sex are needed to enhance the generalizability of the results.

Conclusion and implications

This study examined the association between metabolic syndrome and health-related quality of life among adult women in South Korea and investigated the mediating role of handgrip strength using nationally representative data. The findings demonstrated that metabolic syndrome was significantly associated with lower health-related quality of life, and that handgrip strength partially mediated this relationship.

These results suggest that the adverse impact of metabolic syndrome on quality of life operates not only directly but also indirectly through reduced muscle strength. By identifying handgrip strength as a significant mediating factor, this study extends existing evidence beyond simple associations and highlights the importance of functional health in understanding health-related quality of life among women.

These findings highlight grip strength as a functional pathway linking metabolic syndrome and health-related quality of life. Monitoring and improving muscular strength may therefore represent an important strategy for mitigating the quality-of-life burden associated with metabolic syndrome among women.

Strengthening programs aimed at improving muscular function may play a meaningful role in enhancing well-being among women with metabolic syndrome. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to confirm causal pathways and to further explore sex-specific mechanisms across diverse populations.
