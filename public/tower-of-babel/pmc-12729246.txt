Development of a centralized progress management system using lean thinking and efforts to improve operations
Authors: Seiji Yahata, Isao Tanaka, Masami Takeuchi, Yasuhiro Goto, Isao Shiina, Satoshi Nakayama, Kazuo Shimura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, we improved MRI room operations by applying Lean Thinking principles with a strong emphasis on leadership and ICT integration. The primary objectives were to enhance MRI appointment slot utilization, reduce patient waiting times, and minimize referrals to external facilities. Lean methodologies such as Kaizen, 5 S, Value Stream Mapping, Standard Work, Spaghetti Chart, and Root Cause Analysis were used to identify inefficiencies and implement structured improvements. A centralized ICT-based management system was introduced to enable real-time tracking and enhance workflow coordination. Leadership played a key role in fostering staff engagement and continuous improvement. MRI workflows were analyzed using Lean tools to identify operational bottlenecks and streamline processes. A structured improvement approach was developed through brainstorming sessions and weekly short meetings to refine management practices. The ICT system facilitated real-time monitoring of patient progress and scheduling adjustments. The 5 S methodology was employed to improve workspace organization, while Kaizen supported incremental daily improvements based on staff input. Following these interventions, MRI appointment reservations increased by 26% and completed examinations grew by 19%, with further daily management refinements contributing additional improvements of 34% and 28%, respectively. Patient waiting times were significantly reduced from an average of 45 days to 5 days, and monthly external referrals for MRI examinations declined from 300 to 50. These outcomes demonstrate that the integrated application of Lean Thinking and ICT not only substantially enhanced operational efficiency, reduced patient delays, and optimized resource utilization but also improved patient access and workflow coordination. Leadership, combined with a supportive and collaborative culture, played a key role in empowering team members to engage in continuous improvement. Open communication, the active valuation of staff input, and the delegation of responsibilities contributed to establishing a sustainable model for process enhancement. Although challenges such as the need for continuous adjustments in daily management and the complexity of coordinating staff schedules while maintaining patient care remained, these results confirm that Lean Thinking originally developed in manufacturing can be effectively adapted to healthcare settings to enhance productivity, service accessibility, and overall operational performance.

Introduction

Lean Thinking is a method of operational improvement based on the Toyota Production System. In 2016, Klein et al. investigated the utilization of Lean Thinking to improve the throughput of computed tomography (CT) scans in emergency medical care, leading to its application in the healthcare field [1, 2]. Klein et al. conducted a behavioral analysis and formulated an improvement plan based on Lean Thinking for the operating system, focusing on the workflow from ordering a non-contrast-enhanced CT scan in emergency medical care to the completion of the scan and the patient’s return to the examination room. This approach successfully enhanced throughput efficiency [1].

Despite the demonstrated effectiveness of Lean Thinking in emergency medical care, challenges remain in improving daily operational efficiency in medical imaging departments, such as MRI examination departments. These departments frequently encounter prolonged waiting times, limited resource availability, and a reliance on outsourcing urgent examinations to external facilities. Such inefficiencies negatively impact patient experience, delay treatment, and impose financial burdens on healthcare institutions. Addressing these operational inefficiencies is essential to meet increasing patient demand, minimize unnecessary expenditures, and enhance the overall quality of care. However, existing research on Lean Thinking has primarily focused on emergency settings, leaving its application to routine operations in MRI departments largely unexplored.

This study explores the application of Lean Thinking beyond emergency care settings to improve both throughput and operational efficiency in daily medical imaging workflows. The MRI examination department of the Tokyo Women’s Medical University Hospital, which faced operational inefficiencies, was selected as the target for improvement.

At this facility, MRI examinations were conducted using six units—four in the outpatient center and two in the ward examination room. However, limitations in scheduling flexibility made it difficult to conduct examinations at necessary times. In urgent cases, outsourcing to external facilities was often required, leading to an increased burden on institutional management due to the rising number of outsourced examinations.

To address these challenges, this study applies Lean Thinking to routine MRI operations, an area where operational inefficiencies are both prevalent and impactful. By incorporating Lean tools and leveraging Information and Communication Technology (ICT) to manage workflow and resource allocation, this study aims to develop and validate a sustainable model for improving daily MRI operations and throughput. The integration of ICT is intended not only to streamline processes but also to provide real-time data for enhanced decision-making. Through this approach, the study contributes to both the academic discussion on Lean Thinking in healthcare and the development of practical solutions to critical challenges in medical imaging departments.

A review of existing studies on facility operation systems and throughput improvement using Lean Thinking in MRI departments revealed a lack of research in this area [3]. Given this gap, the study investigates whether Lean Thinking can be effectively utilized to enhance operations and throughput in daily MRI examination workflows. Furthermore, recognizing the potential of ICT in optimizing these processes, a new ICT system was developed to facilitate operational improvements and efficiency gains.

Based on this background, this study conducted a behavioral analysis and developed an improvement plan using Lean Thinking in the MRI department’s daily operations. The goal was to enhance the utilization rate of MRI examination slots, reduce the number of waiting days for reservations, and minimize the outsourcing of examinations to external facilities.

In 2015, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare issued a proposal titled “Healthcare 2035.” The proposal’s content is not a generalized list of existing initiatives but a vision 20 years into the future. To achieve this objective, the basic principles and values that should be respected in healthcare and the direction of change required are discussed from the perspective of 2035 [4, 5].

Within this “Healthcare 2035 Proposal,” “Lean Healthcare: Enhancing the Value of Health Care” is listed as the core pillar [4]. This “lean healthcare” is a way of thinking that applies the concept of lean thinking to healthcare. Therefore, providing high-value services at lower costs is necessary, and this is positioned as lean healthcare” [4].

The Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare aims to build a world-class sustainable healthcare system by 2035. Based on the idea of “better medical care at a lower price,” the concept of “lean healthcare” is expected to become an important initiative in Japan’s healthcare in the future.

Dedicated literature review

Dr. H.L. Bhaskar has contributed extensively to the academic and practical development of Business Process Reengineering (BPR), Lean Thinking, and Total Quality Management (TQM), particularly within the context of Industry 4.0 and manufacturing. Across nine publications, he builds a comprehensive body of knowledge integrating radical process transformation with continuous improvement philosophies and modern digital tools.

His most recent work proposes the B2Lean framework, a hybrid model that fuses the structural overhaul of BPR with the operational refinement of Lean Thinking, showing measurable benefits in cycle time, defect reduction, and process flexibility [6]. In parallel, Bhaskar and his co-authors explore how process mining tools crucial in Industry 4.0 can enhance real-time visibility and process diagnostics. This techno-functional perspective ensures that both technical capacity and business usability are considered when selecting tools [7].

In a related study, Bhaskar evaluates a wide range of BPM technologies (including RPA, process modeling, and optimization engines) in light of their fit for Industry 4.0 settings. He identifies successful adoption factors such as interoperability, scalability, and analytic capabilities [8].

Bhaskar’s foundational works address the conceptual framing of BPR. He argues for BPR not as a one-off restructuring tool, but as a sustained, process-based management practice supported by IT, leadership, and change readiness [9, 10]. While the integration of ICT can significantly enhance process efficiency and data visibility in healthcare settings, it is important to acknowledge its inherent limitations. Bhaskar critically argues that ICT alone cannot drive meaningful or sustained transformation without concurrent changes in organizational structure, workflows, and culture [10]. Over-reliance on technology may lead to automation of inefficient processes, resulting in limited performance gains. Moreover, ICT systems often fail to capture the tacit knowledge and human interactions that are crucial to high-quality service delivery. Thus, for ICT implementation to be truly effective, it must be embedded within a broader framework of process reengineering and organizational alignment [10]. This perspective underscores the need to view ICT not as a standalone solution, but as an enabler that must be integrated with human-centered and systemic change efforts.

He also emphasizes the strategic synergy between BPR and quality systems, particularly TQM, showing how radical process reengineering can complement and amplify quality-driven continuous improvement [11–13]. Building on evidence that Total Quality Management (TQM) significantly strengthens a firm’s market orientation—and that both, in turn, are positively associated with organizational performance—Bhaskar suggests a mechanism that directly supports the sustainability of Lean transformations [11]. TQM’s emphasis on standardized problem solving, continuous improvement, and leadership supported quality routines institutionalizes behaviors that keep Lean practices from reverting to pre change habits. Simultaneously, market orientation keeps the definition of “value” continuously calibrated to evolving patient/customer needs and competitive signals, ensuring that Lean’s waste reduction and flow improvements remain strategically relevant rather than purely internal efficiency plays. Together, TQM builds the durable cultural and procedural backbone for daily Lean work, while market orientation supplies the outward facing feedback loop that preserves alignment and renews improvement targets an interplay consistent with the empirically observed links among TQM, market orientation, and performance.

Additionally, Bhaskar empirically validates the interconnection between TQM, market orientation, and firm performance. His findings support the notion that market-informed TQM initiatives drive performance more effectively [11]. He also constructs a conceptual maturity model for TQM that serves as a platform for later reengineering or digitalization [14].

Collectively, these works form an integrative view of BPR—not as an isolated practice but as a dynamic, scalable methodology aligned with organizational performance, digital tools, and quality systems.

In addition, recent research cited in this study has demonstrated the effectiveness of Lean Thinking in specific diagnostic contexts. For example, Klein et al. (2016) applied Lean strategies to emergency CT workflows, while Özçelik et al. (2016) studied Lean applications in MRI-Tomography centers [1, 2]. Furthermore, Womack’s foundational work on Lean Production [15] and later applications in radiology scheduling and catheterization laboratories [16, 17] highlight the method’s cross-sector adaptability. These studies underscore that Lean Thinking can enhance operational efficiency, reduce waste, and improve service quality not only in manufacturing but also in healthcare.

Importantly, leadership and organizational culture have been identified as key enablers of sustainable Lean implementation. Liker emphasized the development of leadership at all levels [18], and Tortorella et al. showed that task-oriented and relational leadership behaviors are critical during various Lean phases [19]. These findings are directly reflected in this study’s implementation, which incorporated Daily Management, cross-functional team structures, and ICT solutions to create a replicable and scalable improvement model in MRI operations.

Materials and methods

Conventional operations improvement methods and lean thinking

Previous studies on Lean Thinking in healthcare, such as those by Klein et al. and Özçelik et al., have predominantly focused on applying Lean Thinking within specific diagnostic contexts, such as emergency CT scans, and often combined Lean with Six Sigma approaches [1, 2]. In contrast, this study explores its application to daily MRI operations in non-urgent settings, demonstrating its potential as a sustainable method for continuous workflow improvements and waste elimination. Originally proposed by J. P. Womack in 1990, Lean Thinking classifies all work processes based on whether they add value. The primary objective is to systematically eliminate waste and enhance productivity. The aim is to eliminate waste thoroughly and improve productivity [15]. In the field of healthcare, this concept can be used to improve patient waiting time, treatment delays, operational efficiency, cost, processing capacity, quality of medical care, and work environment.

While Lean Thinking is widely employed in both the automotive and healthcare industries, the specific tools and methodologies utilized differ depending on the sector. A comparative analysis of representative tools and their respective applications is provided in Table 1.

In this study, four analytical tools were employed: Value Stream Mapping, Spaghetti Chart, Standardized Work, and Root Cause Analysis. Following this analysis, an improvement plan was developed through brainstorming sessions. To sustain continuous Kaizen, daily management practices were implemented to support ongoing enhancements. Additionally, the 5 S methodology was applied to execute and refine the improvement plan.

Traditional operational improvement methods primarily focus on reducing operation time, which constitutes only a small fraction of total work time. Given that value-adding tasks typically account for less than 5% of total work time, even a 50% reduction in this category results in only a marginal decrease in overall work duration [16] (Fig. 1). Lean Thinking, in contrast, prioritizes the reduction of non-value-adding activities, leading to a substantial decrease in total operation time. This approach facilitates the delivery of high-quality services while significantly improving performance [17]. Furthermore, this study distinguishes itself by incorporating a centralized ICT system to enable real-time MRI schedule monitoring and optimize resource utilization. This structured framework presents a replicable model for other healthcare institutions seeking to enhance operational efficiency.

Data collection method

This study was conducted in three phases based on a workflow improvement project carried out at Tokyo Women’s Medical University Hospital.

Phase 1: collection of baseline data

Baseline examination data conducted over one year from January to December 2016 were collected. The data were extracted from the Radiology Information System (RIS).

Eight items were extracted: examination date, reservation time, examination site, reservation location, reception time, examination start time, examination end time, and examination location. These items were analyzed to assess the operational status.

Between January and April 2016, the movements of radiologic technologists, nurses, physicians, and nurse assistants were randomly plotted. At the same time, the time required for preparation and examination was measured and plotted for each item.

Phase 2: planning of workflow improvements

From March to August 2017, over a six-month period, the baseline data collected in Phase 1 were used to create Lean tools such as Value Stream Mapping, Spaghetti Charts, and Standardized Work.

Using these tools, issues were identified through Root Cause Analysis.

The extracted issues were reviewed by a team of 14 members, including 12 radiologic technologists with 5 to 20 years of experience and 2 engineers specializing in workflow improvement, to develop specific improvement plans.

Phase 3: implementation and evaluation of improvement plans

First implementation period: January to December 2018

During this period, a 15-minute “Daily Management” meeting was held weekly to continuously discuss improvement ideas.

Second implementation period: January to December 2019

Based on the results of the first phase, further improvement plans were developed and implemented.

Development of a centralized progress management system.

We developed a centralized progress management system to improve the productivity of the radiological technician team in the MRI department.

With conventional radiological information systems, it was unable to check detailed information such as how many patients have already completed the reception process, who is undergoing pre-examination interviews, who is available for examination, and how long it will take for patients undergoing examinations to complete. Therefore, it was difficult to understand how work progressed across the entire examination room, and the time from examination preparation to the end of the examination was unnecessarily long. Therefore, we have newly developed a centralized progress management system that receives information from the radiology information system in real-time and confirms in detail the status from reception to the start and end of the examination.

The system extracts the necessary data from database columns in the radiology information system, converts it into information, and displays the information on the screen using a web browser.

In addition to understanding the detailed status of each MRI by utilizing the centralized progress management system, it is possible to understand the patient’s preparation status in detail from interviews before injection and completion of examination preparation. Therefore, an optimal patient allocation for MRI examination based on the situation is possible.

As a result, we could shorten patient waiting times, improve patient satisfaction, and improve the efficiency of the overall operations, thereby creating an optimal examination environment.

Daily management

After implementing the improvement plan, the process should not be completed. Instead, daily management should be implemented, which is a key activity to continue evaluation and review, identify further problems and areas for improvement, and continue repeating the lean thinking cycle. In the present study, we held a weekly 15-min meeting (Daily Management) and used a dedicated sheet (Action Tracker), as shown in Fig. 9, to discuss daily management and further improvement plans.

In this study, the project leader prioritized raising awareness and motivating staff through daily management, focusing on cultivating a proactive approach to problem-solving. Team members were encouraged to openly share challenges and issues during meetings, creating an environment where not only the leader but also each staff member could voice their concerns and contribute solutions. The leader also assigned younger staff to take responsibility for specific issues, encouraging them to take an active role in problem-solving as stakeholders. This approach fostered a collaborative culture where staff saw themselves as vital contributors to operational improvements and embraced the MRI department’s challenges as their own.

Leadership and culture

In this study, leadership was instrumental in creating the conditions under which Lean initiatives could be implemented and sustained in daily MRI operations. Department leaders provided clear direction, ensured access to the necessary resources, and actively participated in the improvement cadence, which fostered a culture of transparency and continuous problem-solving.

Practically, leadership established short, regular Daily Management huddles focused on data (slot utilization, wait days, external referrals) and rapid follow-up. Each action item had an owner and due date, and progress was reviewed the following week, turning meetings from reporting into decision-making forums.

Leaders also delegated ownership to younger staff for specific improvement streams (e.g., pediatric sedation MRI scheduling, breast MRI protocol coordination). This not only accelerated local problem-solving but functioned as on-the-job leadership development, expanding the cadre of people capable of running Lean routines.

Cross-functional collaboration was formalized: MRI technologists, nurses, and administrators met around the same visual data (the centralized progress board), which reduced handoff loss and siloed optimization. The mirror monitor arrangement enabled experienced staff to supervise remotely and coach in real time without compromising safety.

While the importance of leadership and culture for Lean sustainability is well recognized in prior research, our findings add a setting-specific insight: aligning leadership engagement with major capital change (e.g., the installation of a new MRI system) created a shared sense of renewal and purpose, which measurably increased staff motivation and accelerated adoption of new work standards [20].

Finally, leaders made failure-tolerant experimentation explicit (e.g., trialing dual-booking where appropriate and revising start times), with pre-defined rollback criteria. This safeguarded patient safety while allowing rapid learning cycles an essential cultural feature for sustaining Lean beyond a one-off project.

Theoretical foundation and conceptual integration

To frame the intervention more broadly, the results of this study can be understood through the lens of an integrated Business Process Reengineering (BPR), Lean Thinking, and Information and Communication Technology (ICT) framework. As shown in Figure xx, BPR focuses on radical redesign of core processes, while Lean emphasizes continuous waste elimination and staff-driven process improvement. When these two methodologies are combined with ICT which enables real-time data visualization and operational coordination a powerful synergy is created. This integrative model aligns with recent healthcare transformation paradigms that emphasize both structural redesign and iterative improvement supported by digital solutions. In our study, this approach allowed for system-wide optimization rather than isolated enhancements, supporting both efficiency and adaptability Fig. 14.

Increase in the number of examination reservation slots and examinations performed

In this study, the implementation of an operational improvement plan using Lean Thinking led to a 26% increase in the number of examination reservation slots and a 19% increase in the number of examinations performed in 2018 compared to before the operational improvements. Further improvements made in 2019 led to an increase of 34% in reservation slots and 28% in examinations performed compared to 2018. A key characteristic of Lean Thinking is that operational improvements are not static; rather, they are sustained through the continuous repetition of the Lean cycle. In 2019, this approach resulted in an additional increase of eight and nine points, respectively, in reservation slots and examinations performed.

Continuous operational improvement is supported by daily management, which is discussed further below.

Reduction in the number of waiting days for reservations and outsourcing

Operational improvements significantly reduced the number of waiting days for reservations. In 2018, the average waiting time for reservations decreased to 10 days. This reduction was primarily attributed to the ability to reserve and perform examinations as early as the next business day, including procedures such as brain MRI, brain MRI + MRA, contrast MRI, gallbladder pancreatic MRCP, and whole-body MRI. Further analysis through daily management highlighted that waiting times increased due to limited reservation slots for breast MRI and pediatric sedation MRI. By increasing the number of available slots for breast MRI, reassessing pediatric sedation MRI slots, and reallocating them as necessary, it was possible to reduce waiting times to an average of five days by 2019.

Despite these improvements, some challenges remained. For instance, when no examination slots were available on a particular day, the next available reservation would be the following week. Additionally, overlapping holidays and vacations still led to extended waiting times.

Regarding outsourced examinations, the number of outsourced tests decreased significantly as a result of the increased number of reservations and examinations performed in the hospital.

The number of outsourced examinations dropped from 300 per month to 50 per month, representing an 83% decrease.

Most of the outsourced examinations were performed at affiliated hospitals, and therefore, outsourcing to unaffiliated hospitals was effectively eliminated. The reduction in waiting days and outsourced examinations contributed to improved satisfaction levels across clinical departments.

Productivity in MRI departments

The implementation of Lean Thinking in the MRI department resulted in a 28% increase in the number of examinations performed compared to pre-improvement levels, leading to a corresponding increase in productivity. The number of radiological technologists was reduced from 12 to 9, thanks to a restructured team. This reduction in staff contributed to a 33% improvement in productivity. Taking the reduction in radiological technologists into account, overall productivity increased by 71%.

While staff reductions typically result in decreased productivity, the improvements implemented in this study not only mitigated this effect but also contributed to a significant rise in the number of examinations performed. A key factor was the centralized progress management system, which allowed for monitoring patient preparation and MRI room progress—tasks that were previously difficult to track using the radiological information system. This system also enabled optimal patient assignment based on real-time circumstances.

Additionally, the installation of mirror monitors enabled skilled staff in both MRI rooms to oversee examination progress and assist less experienced operators. This created a support system that ensured safe examination procedures, improved productivity, and contributed significantly to medical safety.

Traditionally, Lean initiatives aimed at improving productivity in radiology departments have been undertaken exclusively by internal medical staff. However, such efforts are often limited to adjustments in staff allocation and task reorganization, restricting the scope of potential operational improvements [2, 21, 22].

In contrast, this study adopted a collaborative approach by involving external MRI system and ICT vendors from the early stages of the Lean process. Their technical expertise enabled the development and implementation of innovative solutions such as a centralized progress management system and real-time mirror monitoring that extended beyond the scope of conventional staff-led improvements. This multidisciplinary collaboration significantly broadened the impact of Lean implementation and contributed to substantial gains in operational efficiency.

However, Lean implementation in healthcare settings is often accompanied by several inherent challenges, including staff resistance, cultural incompatibility, sustainability of improvements, maintaining patient centered care, and resource limitations.

In our study, we proactively addressed these challenges. To mitigate staff resistance, we actively engaged radiological technologists and support staff from the early stages of Lean implementation. Staff were invited to participate in brainstorming sessions, and their input was incorporated into the improvement plans. This approach fostered a sense of ownership and reduced skepticism, making the Lean process more collaborative.

To overcome organizational cultural barriers, we secured strong top-down support from hospital leadership and formed cross-departmental teams to dissolve siloed communication. This promoted a culture of transparency, continuous improvement, and collective problem-solving.

To ensure sustainability, we standardized new workflows and incorporated continuous monitoring through Daily Management. Key performance indicators (KPIs) such as number of examinations and waiting time were tracked weekly using the Action Tracker. This allowed for ongoing evaluation and timely corrective actions. In addition, younger staff were assigned responsibility for specific issues, cultivating future leaders in Lean operations.

Patient-centeredness was maintained by including patient satisfaction and examination accessibility as core outcome metrics. By shortening waiting times and improving appointment flexibility, we enhanced both operational efficiency and the quality of patient experience. Feedback from patients was also considered during the redesign of pre-examination processes.

Lastly, despite limited resources, we utilized ICT solutions such as the centralized progress management system and mirror monitors to improve productivity without increasing manpower. This technology-enabled approach was key to sustaining improvements without placing additional burden on staff.

Additionally, this study uniquely highlights that aligning Lean initiatives with the timing of major capital investments such as the introduction of a new MRI system can significantly enhance team motivation and shared commitment. This strategic alignment created a shared vision among stakeholders, increasing engagement and ownership of Lean activities. By implementing Lean improvements concurrently with the installation of a new MRI, staff members perceived a sense of renewal and progress, fostering an environment conducive to operational innovation.

We believe these practical strategies for overcoming Lean-related challenges can be generalized and applied to other medical departments aiming to adopt Lean methodologies. Sharing such lessons may aid in broader implementation and adaptation across healthcare institutions.

For example, Virginia Mason Medical Center in the United States applied Lean hospital-wide and reported an 85% reduction in diagnostic turnaround time and over $2 million in annual inventory savings [23]. In Denmark, Lean initiatives in radiology cut CT waitlists from 12 to 4 weeks and boosted daily throughput by six additional exams without added staffing. During these meetings, the current status following the implementation of operational improvements was assessed, and further issues as well as potential areas for enhancement were identified. This structured approach enabled a continuous cycle of improvement and evaluation.

To gain a comprehensive understanding of the operational status of the MRI department, several indices were examined, including the total number of examinations performed per week, the total number of examinations conducted in each MRI room per week, and the examination time categorized by anatomical parts. Lean tools such as Value Stream Mapping, Spaghetti Diagrams, Standardized Work, and Root Cause Analysis all of which were utilized in this study are widely recognized in healthcare process improvement for their ability to visualize processes and identify inefficiencies. Each tool offers distinct advantages but also comes with certain limitations as reported in the literature. For example, Value Stream Mapping provides a comprehensive visualization of the entire patient workflow and helps uncover wasteful steps, yet it can be time consuming and its output may depend on the accuracy of collected data and the perspective of the analysts involved. In healthcare settings, one limitation of VSM is that it may be difficult to apply consistently due to variations in patient conditions and needs. Similarly, Spaghetti Diagrams effectively map staff and patient movements to reveal unnecessary motion in the workplace, thereby highlighting opportunities to streamline physical flow. However, a spaghetti map alone does not convey the time or frequency of each movement, and complex care environments can make such diagrams cumbersome to interpret without additional context. Standardized Work is a Lean method that ensures each task is performed in a consistent, best-practice manner, which can improve safety, quality, and efficiency in healthcare. The challenge with standardized workflows is maintaining flexibility: strict standardization can be counterproductive if it doesn’t allow adaptation to individual patient needs, so it should be created by those who do the work and serve as a basis for continuous improvement rather than as an inflexible rule. Root Cause Analysis (RCA), including techniques like the “5 Whys,” provides a systematic approach to dig deeper into underlying causes of operational problems. This tool’s strength is in preventing superficial fixes by addressing fundamental issues; however, its effectiveness relies on thorough investigation and an unbiased, multidisciplinary perspective. If an RCA is rushed or performed with narrow viewpoint, it can lead to misinterpretations or incomplete solutions. Notably, no single Lean tool is sufficient by itself the tools are most powerful when used in combination to provide complementary insights. In our project, this combination of VSM for mapping the overall process, spaghetti diagrams for spatial flow, standardized work for task consistency, and RCA for problem-solving ensured a holistic understanding of the MRI workflow.

In this study, we also took deliberate measures to prevent errors, misinterpretation, or inappropriate actions when applying these Lean tools. First, analyses and mapping exercises were conducted by a multidisciplinary team of stakeholders (including radiological technologists, nurses, and administrators) rather than by individuals in isolation. Involving an interprofessional team in the analysis provided multiple viewpoints and helped minimize personal bias, which aligns with recommended practices for thorough root cause analysis. This collective review ensured that the Value Stream Map and Spaghetti Diagram accurately reflected the reality of the MRI unit’s operations, and that conclusions drawn from them were agreed upon by various professionals. Second, we established standardized procedures for how each tool would be used in the project. For instance, the team followed a step-by-step protocol for creating the Value Stream Map (defining process steps, collecting data, validating the map) and for conducting RCA (using a structured “5 Whys” approach with verification of each causal assumption). By standardizing the way we applied these tools, we reduced variability in the analysis process itself and made the improvement workflow transparent and repeatable. Third, we provided training and education sessions on Lean methodology and the specific tools to all team members involved in the project. This ensured that everyone had a clear understanding of how to use and interpret the tools correctly for example, training staff on how to track their activities for the spaghetti diagram and how to identify waste in the flow, as well as educating them on the goals of standardized work documentation. Finally, any findings and proposed improvements identified with the tools were subject to multi disciplinary review and validation during our Daily Management meetings before implementation. In these brief weekly meetings, the team jointly examined the proposed changes (such as adjustments in scheduling or workflow) to verify that they were supported by data and frontline experience. This step created an additional safeguard against misinterpretation, as any questionable analysis result would be questioned and clarified by the group. Through these combined precautions team based analysis, standardized tool usage, staff training, and collective review we aimed to prevent missteps and ensure that Lean interventions led to genuinely effective and appropriate improvements.

In our study, leadership commitment was reflected in cross-departmental collaboration, empowerment of younger staff, and integration of ICT solutions. These actions linked directly to our results sustained operational improvements, shorter waiting times, and reduced outsourcing demonstrating that engaged, context-specific leadership behaviors drive measurable Lean outcomes.

Furthermore, recent studies have reinforced the central role of leadership and organizational culture in sustaining Lean transformations. For example, strong leadership commitment including clear communication and active involvement has been shown to foster the supportive environment essential for successful Lean initiatives [24]. In healthcare settings, leadership together with an improvement oriented culture can directly drive process improvements and also empower staff to sustain continuous improvement efforts [25]. Conversely, a lack of leadership support or adequate Lean expertise is frequently identified as a barrier to maintaining long-term Lean gains in organizations [26]. These recent findings align with our approach of nurturing a participative, continuous-improvement culture under committed leadership to ensure that the operational gains achieved through Lean Thinking are maintained over time.

To support replication in other healthcare settings, we propose a structured Lean-ICT implementation roadmap consisting of five key phases: (1) strategic alignment with institutional investments, (2) cross-functional team building with external vendors, (3) visualization and diagnosis using Lean tools, (4) implementation of ICT-supported operational solutions, and (5) daily management and KPI tracking for sustainability. This phased roadmap provides a practical and adaptable guide for other institutions aiming to integrate Lean Thinking with digital health infrastructure.

However, when other institutions attempt to implement these improvements, they should be mindful of potential pitfalls. For instance, if frontline staff do not fully embrace the new processes, improvements may not advance in practice. In addition, the initial cost of introducing the required ICT infrastructure can pose a major hurdle, especially for facilities with limited budgets. Finally, even if such a system is formally introduced, there remains a risk that it becomes a mere formality and is not actively sustained in daily operations.

Generalizability and future research directions

While this study was focused on MRI operations, the integrated BPR–Lean–ICT model has strong potential for generalization to other diagnostic imaging areas (e.g., CT, X-ray, ultrasound) and clinical departments such as surgery, emergency, or outpatient care. International case studies have demonstrated similar improvements using Lean or BPR in various contexts, suggesting that our centralized ICT-supported framework could provide value across diverse hospital settings.

Future research should explore the long-term sustainability of such improvements, especially in maintaining staff engagement and adapting to evolving clinical environments. Moreover, challenges such as user interface complexity of ICT systems, interdepartmental coordination, and initial implementation costs must be addressed. Emerging technologies such as AI-based predictive scheduling and IoT-enabled patient flow tracking—may offer the next stage in process optimization. Investigating the role of digital transformation in Lean healthcare (“Healthcare 5.0”) could provide a fruitful direction for subsequent studies.

Framing results using BPR evaluation metrics

To provide a comprehensive assessment of the intervention outcomes, we further structured our results using the four evaluation metrics commonly employed in Business Process Reengineering (BPR): cost, time, quality, and flexibility.

Cost: The number of radiological technologists was reduced from 12 to 9 without compromising service quality, resulting in improved productivity and personnel cost optimization. Moreover, the reduction of outsourced MRI examinations from 300 to 50 per month significantly reduced institutional expenditure related to external imaging services.

Time: A key achievement was the reduction of MRI reservation waiting time from 45 days to 5 days. Streamlined workflows and optimized scheduling directly shortened the cycle time from patient referral to examination completion. The ICT-supported centralized progress management system further reduced examination preparation time and improved overall scheduling efficiency.

Quality: Medical safety and examination quality were maintained or improved despite staff reductions, owing to enhanced training, standardization of pre-examination interviews, and real-time monitoring tools such as the mirror monitor system. Patient satisfaction was also improved through shorter wait times and more responsive examination availability.

Flexibility: The new team structure allowed for flexible role allocation, enabling technologists to cover multiple responsibilities depending on real-time needs. The scheduling system was modified to accommodate dynamic slot allocation, including the use of cancellation slots and adjustment for special cases such as pediatric sedation or breast MRI.

This BPR-based evaluation framework reinforces that our intervention not only achieved operational efficiency but also aligned with broader institutional goals for high-value, patient-centered healthcare transformation.

Beyond the immediate operational results, this study advances the theoretical and managerial understanding of Lean healthcare. Conceptually, the BPR–Lean–ICT integration can be viewed as a layered model: BPR provides the structural redesign, Lean ensures iterative staff-driven improvement, and ICT offers real-time visibility and coordination. This tripartite framework, as illustrated in Fig. 18, aligns with established theories of organizational change, such as Kotter’s stages of transformation and Schein’s model of cultural embedding, thereby situating the study within a broader academic discourse.

From a practical standpoint, the key factors identified here − (1) inclusion of IT and vendor experts in cross-functional teams, (2) synchronization of Lean with major capital investment, (3) daily KPI-based management, and (4) visible leadership commitment - as summarized in Table 6, can serve as a reproducible checklist for other institutions. Future projects could apply this checklist to diverse settings such as CT, surgery, or outpatient clinics, adapting the same principles to different operational challenges.

In comparing our work with prior research, some relevant parallels and key distinctions emerge. Akdag, Çalışkan, and Toma (2017) conducted a Lean process redesign in a radiology department, using value-stream mapping to identify and reduce non-value-added processes [27]. While this work focused on workflow diagnostics and Lean tools, it did not report on the inclusion of external technical specialists such as IT or device vendor experts to co-design ICT or equipment-based solutions. Similarly, recent studies on technology integration in nursing and administrative healthcare settings have shown benefits of digitalization for efficiency and documentation (e.g., Technology integration in nursing and healthcare administration, 2019), yet the involvement of specialized device/IT engineers in the improvement team remains rare [28].

The 2025 study by Capacho-Alfonso et al. develops a model for big data and health technology adoption, integrating technical, organizational, social, ethical, and legal dimensions. However, their model is largely at the organizational or policy level rather than grounded in hands-on operational redesign of imaging operations with on-site technical intervention [29].

Our study adds new knowledge in several respects: (1) It empirically demonstrates that including device and IT specialists in the cross-functional Lean team enables solutions (hardware layout changes, ICT dashboards, mirror monitoring, etc.) that clinical staff alone would not conceive. (2) It situates the BPR–Lean–ICT integration as a layered model (Fig. 18), showing how external technical expertise forms a crucial component in achieving sustained productivity and cultural change. (3) It provides a replicable checklist (Table 6) that other imaging departments or diagnostic units can use to guide team composition, investment timing, and leadership involvement—filling a gap in prior work which often lacks such operational detail.

Conclusions

In this study, we examined the possibility of improving MRI room operations through behavior analysis and Lean Thinking, based on the Toyota Production System. Key to our success was the role of leadership and a supportive culture that empowered team members to engage actively in continuous improvement efforts. Notably, after implementing the improvement plan, the number of examination reservation slots and examinations performed increased by 26% and 19%, respectively, demonstrating a significant enhancement in throughput and patient accessibility. Furthermore, by making detailed improvements through Daily Management, the reservation slots and examinations performed increased by 34% and 28%, respectively, compared to before improvements.

The leadership’s commitment to fostering a collaborative and problem-solving culture in the MRI department was instrumental in sustaining these improvements. By encouraging open communication, giving each team member a voice, and assigning responsibilities to younger staff, the department established a culture where staff actively contributed to identifying and addressing challenges. This cultural shift contributed to a sustainable model for integrating Lean and ICT in healthcare, providing substantial productivity gains while reducing staffing requirements, marking an innovative advancement in lean healthcare research. This study contributes to the healthcare field by demonstrating that Lean Thinking, typically applied in manufacturing, can be adapted to medical settings to enhance operational efficiency.

Specifically, our approach significantly increased the utilization rate of MRI reservation slots, reduced the average waiting days to five, and decreased the need for outsourcing examinations to other facilities, thereby improving service accessibility and clinical department satisfaction.

However, implementing Lean Thinking in a medical context presented certain challenges, such as the need for continuous, detailed adjustments through Daily Management and the complexity of coordinating staff schedules without disrupting patient care. These difficulties required ongoing engagement from staff and a commitment to iterative improvements, highlighting that such operational adjustments may require time to achieve full optimization.

This study highlights how integrating Lean Thinking with ICT contributes to improved hospital management by enhancing efficiency, reducing costs, and optimizing resource allocation. These findings support data-driven decision-making and promote sustainable operational improvements in healthcare institutions.

Our findings demonstrate that Lean Thinking can be effectively applied beyond manufacturing to non-urgent medical settings. Moreover, the integration of Lean Thinking and ICT not only improves operational efficiency but also enhances staff engagement and teamwork, providing new insights for Lean Healthcare research.

The study also highlights two practical considerations that may guide other institutions seeking similar improvements. First, forming cross-functional teams that deliberately include IT and vendor specialists expands the scope of possible solutions and accelerates the adoption of ICT-enabled workflows. Second, synchronizing Lean implementation with major equipment investments provides a powerful mechanism for aligning leadership priorities with frontline practice, thereby enhancing staff motivation and sustaining cultural change.

To facilitate replication, we propose a practical checklist of four key factors (Table 6) and a conceptual model of BPR–Lean–ICT integration (Fig. 18). Together, these managerial insights and theoretical contributions provide a transferable framework that can be adapted to diverse healthcare settings beyond MRI operations.

This study confirmed that Lean Thinking and ICT integration effectively improve MRI operations. However, optimizing the implementation process and assessing applicability to other medical departments remain future challenges. Further research should evaluate long-term impacts, including patient satisfaction and overall healthcare quality improvements.

In addition, future studies should evaluate this framework through multi-center implementations and long-term assessments. Such investigations would provide stronger evidence of generalizability across different healthcare contexts and further clarify its impact on patient-centered outcomes, organizational culture, and healthcare quality at scale.

This study has several limitations. First, the investigation was conducted in a single MRI department at one institution, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other settings. Second, although short-term improvements following the application of Lean methods were observed, further research is needed to assess the long-term sustainability and the impact on staff motivation. Additionally, the effectiveness of the ICT system may vary depending on its operational status and changes in the healthcare environment, warranting more detailed evaluation in future studies.”

Future work could explore similar integrations across other departments, assessing Lean’s adaptability and operational impact in diverse healthcare settings. Additionally, longitudinal studies are recommended to evaluate the long-term impact of these operational changes on patient satisfaction and healthcare delivery efficiency.
