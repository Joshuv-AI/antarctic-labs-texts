Ground-station and ERA5-Land evaluation for heat-induced labour loss assessment across developed-economy regions in Pacific Asia
Authors: Po-Yen Lai, Wee Shing Koh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobal warming is increasing extreme heat events, directly impacting labour health and economic productivity. High-resolution global reanalysis datasets, such as ERA5-Land, are enabling worldwide and regional assessments of heat-related labour impacts. This study systematically examines the reliability and bias of ERA5-Land-derived labour loss estimates across diverse Köppen-Geiger climate zones (A, C, D) and land cover types (urban and natural land cover) at 37 sites (including 36 WMO stations) in advanced-economy regions of Pacific Asia (Japan, Korea, Taiwan, and Singapore) from 2014 to 2023. Using the physics-based Liljegren wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) model and established exposure-response functions, we quantified annual cumulative labour loss and productivity loss while accounting for spatial labour distribution. A comparison of WBGT‑derived labour‑loss estimates from 37 ground‑station datasets with ERA5‑Land-driven estimates indicates strong concordance in temperate zones (R2 ≈ 0.85) and urban areas (R2 ≈ 0.94), contrasted by a systematic underestimation in tropical regions (up to ~ 18%, R2 ≈ 0.27). Incorporating a weighting approach that integrates population density and labour force participation rate (LFPR) significantly improves the assessment of the effective labour force scale, with ERA5-Land’s R2 in tropical regions increasing from 0.27 to 0.70. Based on our findings, we recommend (1) application of ERA5-Land in temperate and urban local-climate-zone (LCZ)-dominant dominant areas with standard caution, (2) for macro-level, climate- or land-cover-aggregated assessments, a 20% safety margin for tropical climate class (Köppen-Geiger Type A) and a 15% adjustment for natural environments to address underestimation uncertainty; and (3) for fine-scale, coupled climate × land-cover applications, context-specific correction (e.g., 31.55% for tropical natural areas) where localised land-cover data is available. These guidelines refine the operational use of reanalysis data for precise heat-risk assessments, and inclusion of population-density weighting in standard assessments, which improves index performance by 15–50%. While these guidelines refine the use of reanalysis data and offset policy under-protection risks, future applications must still account for microclimatic heterogeneity and the inherent assumptions of uniform sector-specific labour distributions. Introduction

Over recent years, the frequency and intensity of extreme heat events have increased significantly across multiple regions globally, with mid- to low-latitude cities frequently surpassing historical temperature records during summer months, thereby exposing outdoor workers to dangerously high thermal stress environments that threaten both health and productivity1,2. However, the assessment of outdoor thermal stress extends beyond air temperature alone, requiring comprehensive consideration of multiple environmental factors, including solar radiation, relative humidity, and wind speed, all of which collectively influence human thermal stress. Consequently, the Wet-Bulb Globe Temperature (WBGT), which integrates these meteorological elements, has been widely adopted by the International Labour Organization and ISO 7243 standards3 as a core thermal safety indicator4. In urban environments, the interaction between heat island effects and social vulnerability further amplifies thermal stress impacts, particularly in areas characterised by high building density and low vegetation coverage, which often correlate with lower socioeconomic conditions2,5. Extreme temperatures not only increase occupational injury and illness rates but also significantly elevate related healthcare and compensation costs4. To address observational data gaps, researchers have extensively utilised ERA5 reanalysis data6 for continuous thermal exposure monitoring7; however, while ERA5’s spatial resolution of approximately 0.25° represents an improvement over earlier products, it remains insufficient for accurately characterising block-scale topographic variations, building shading effects, and vegetation distribution patterns, resulting in biases in microscale heat island assessments and limiting its contribution to urban thermal risk research8,9.

Recent advances in ERA5 and ERA5-Land datasets have enabled comprehensive assessments of thermal comfort indices such as Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI) and WBGT across diverse climate zones10. However, ERA5 exhibits systematic biases in near-surface temperature and relative humidity, particularly in complex terrain and tropical regions11,12, necessitating validation for policy applications. While machine learning approaches and urban-scale bias correction methods can improve reanalysis reliability7, significant limitations persist: ERA5-Land shows reduced accuracy in complex terrain and high-elevation regions compared to ERA512, and near-surface meteorological variables systematically underestimate heat extremes compared to ground observations13.

Heat-induced labour loss directly impacts national gross domestic product (GDP) and public health14, making it a critical indicator for policy assessment. Parsons et al.1 pioneered the integration of ERA5 data with WBGT exposure-response functions to quantify heat-induced labour loss across different industries under extreme heat conditions, predicting that labour force losses will increase exponentially in the coming decades, particularly in tropical regions14, under high-emission scenarios such as SSP3-7.015. In regions lacking dense meteorological observation networks, ERA5 provides indispensable continuous climate data for such assessments; however, its spatial resolution is insufficient to capture urban microclimate heterogeneity, and systematic biases persist compared to in-situ observations, particularly in areas with dense buildings or complex terrain, leading to uncertainties in heat-induced labour loss estimates. More critically, current research generally lacks systematic validation against heat-induced labour loss derived from ground-based meteorological observations, leaving the reliability of reanalysis-based assessment results uncertain across different regional and industrial contexts. While advanced methods such as statistical downscaling, regional calibration, and surface parameter integration have been attempted to improve accuracy, their applicability and associated uncertainties across different climate zones, urban morphologies, and demographic distributions require further systematic investigation to support more precise and localised urban climate adaptation decision-making, particularly in densely populated and economically developed regions such as Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and Singapore16.

To evaluate its reliability in capturing heat-induced labour productivity loss, this study employs the ERA5-Land17 dataset. Compared to ERA5, ERA5-Land provides a finer spatial resolution (0.1°), enabling a more detailed capture of land surface variations in regions with complex terrain, such as mountainous, urban, and coastal areas18,19. We systematically compare ERA5-Land data with observations from 37 ground-based weather stations across Singapore, Taiwan, South Korea, and Japan that simultaneously measure air temperature, wind speed, relative humidity, and solar radiation. Considering that heat-induced labour productivity loss (HLPL) is influenced by wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT), and that the method for calculating WBGT directly affects assessment results, this study employs the physics-based model proposed by Liljegren et al.20 rather than simplified approaches adopted in1 to better approximate real-world conditions. The research focuses on the 2014–2023 period, representing the hottest decade in recorded history21, and analyses Köppen-Geiger climate classifications22, land cover types23, and population24 and labour force25 distributions at each station location to evaluate heat-induced labour loss characteristics and differences over the monitoring period, ultimately determining whether reanalysis data can provide a reliable scientific basis for future regional thermal risk management, labour policy development, and heat-risk-related economic losses.

Studied region

A total of 37 meteorological stations were included in the study domain, providing a robust latitudinal transect from Changi, Singapore at 1.37°N to Sapporo, Japan at 43.06°N. This spatial coverage extends across approximately 42 degrees of latitude, fully encompassing the transitional gradient from subtropical to temperate climatic zones. Station elevation ranges from near sea level (2.5 m) at Fukuoka to 772.4 m at Daegwallyeong, thereby ensuring comprehensive topographical representation within the dataset.

Of these, 36 sites are official World Meteorological Organization (WMO) stations, each assigned a unique WMO identifier, which assures data standardisation, traceability, and reliability. Hsinchu station (WMO ID: 46757) is excluded from the 2021 statistical analyses due to a lack of available data for that year. Although Choa Chu Kang is not listed among WMO stations, it is retained in this study as one of only two Singapore sites with continuous surface solar radiation observations for the study period, which is essential for heat stress analysis in tropical urban areas.

Data collection and preprocessing

Meteorological data

To collect ground-based meteorological observations from 37 sites, we obtained data specifically intended for academic research from the following government agencies: the Korea Meteorological Administration26, Japan Meteorological Agency27, Central Weather Administration in Taiwan28, and National Environment Agency in Singapore29. The dataset spans 2014 to 2023 and includes hourly records of the following 4 variables: air temperature at 2 m (T2m), relative humidity (RH2m), wind speed at 10 m (u10m), and global horizontal irradiance (GHI) or surface downward shortwave radiation (SSRD).

ERA5-Land dataset is provided by the Copernicus Climate Change Service in GRIB format30. To ensure a direct and fair comparison of heat stress conditions at each site, we selected the nearest ERA5-Land grid cell corresponding to each observation site when extracting the relevant data for useful four variables. Notably, in the ERA5-Land dataset, global horizontal irradiance (GHI) is equivalent to the surface downward shortwave radiation (SSRD), and the 10-m wind speed (u10m) can be derived as the magnitude of the wind vector, calculated by the root mean square of U10m and V10m fields.

Socioeconomic data

To assess the heat impact on labour productivity loss, this study primarily incorporates two socioeconomic variables: population density and labour force participation rate. The spatial distribution of the population is estimated using data from the WorldPop website24. Due to its 1-km data grid resolution, we can further estimate the total population within the ERA5-Land grid cell where the station is located. Labour force participation rates are derived from the International Labour Organization (ILO) estimates25, which provide the number of workers engaged in physically demanding sectors, including agriculture, forestry, fisheries, and construction; for each country. By integrating these datasets, we can quantify the vulnerability of economically active populations to heat stress at the corresponding ERA5-Land grid.

Climate type and land cover data

The table further classifies each station according to the Köppen-Geiger climate classification system22, denoting both the climate code and the corresponding regime. A clear latitudinal pattern is evident: tropical climates (Type A), including tropical rainforest (Af; Singapore) and tropical monsoon/savanna (Am, Aw; southern Taiwan), are present at lower latitudes. Temperate climates (Type C), comprising humid subtropical (Cfa) and monsoon-influenced humid subtropical (Cwa) types, are most widespread—dominating northern Taiwan, most of Korea, and southern to central Japan. Continental temperate climates (Type D), including both hot-summer (Dfa) and warm-summer (Dfb) variants, are found at higher latitudes in northern Korea and northern Japan.

The final column in the table presents the “Urbanicity” index for each site, providing a quantitative measure of urbanisation derived from the proportion of urban land cover within the corresponding ERA5-Land grid cell. The urban land cover fraction is specifically calculated based on the spatial proportion of Local Climate Zones (LCZ) 1–10 (built-up classes) as a percentage of total grid area23. Based on this metric, sites are classified as urban-dominated (Urbanicity > 0.5) such as Taichung (0.97), Nagoya (0.97), Tainan (0.89), Sapporo (0.87), and Seoul (0.86), all situated in major metropolitan cores; or as natural-cover (Urbanicity < 0.5), exemplified by sites such as Dawu (0.01), Naze (0.04), and Jeju (0.20), which correspond to rural or coastal environments.

These parameters follow the recommendations in Parsons et al.1 and have been widely adopted in previous studies assessing occupational heat stress36,37.

Importantly, we use the standard outdoor WBGT, rather than the simplified sWBGT, because sWBGT does not account for solar radiation, which is a critical determinant of outdoor heat stress. Our approach better reflects actual environmental conditions, particularly in sun-exposed work environments. This exposure-response model assumes that the severity of labour productivity loss is directly determined by the WBGT exposure. For example, at a WBGT of 20 °C, the predicted productivity loss is minimal (< 1%); at 27 °C, the loss increases to 10%; at approximately 32.5 °C, the workforce experiences a 50% reduction in capacity; and at around 38 °C, the loss fraction rises to 90%. These characteristic values illustrate the nonlinear and accelerating relationship between increasing heat stress and decreasing work ability. Labour productivity loss computation

To accurately quantify regional impacts on labour productivity loss of heat exposure, this study extends the basic labour loss fraction framework by integrating spatial population distribution and labour force participation data. This approach reflects actual economic vulnerability patterns across different regions. Population distribution data are sourced from WorldPop global high-resolution gridded datasets (approximately 100-m resolution), which utilise machine learning algorithms to integrate satellite imagery and census records24. Labour force participation rates are obtained from the International Labour Organization (ILO) global statistical database, providing labour market statistics by country25.

The spatial weighting factor (W) is computed as the product of the gridded population density (P), sourced from 100-m WorldPop data aggregated to 0.1°, and the regional Labour Force Participation Rate (LFPR), sourced from ILO for heavy-manual sectors, i.e., . P identifies heat exposure magnitude, while LFPR identifies the economic sensitivity of the effective at-risk workforce. This converts theoretical labour loss percentages into quantifiable labour force losses, enabling direct linkage to regional GDP impacts through labour productivity relationships. It is worth noting that this study primarily investigates whether ERA5-Land can accurately assess the impact of heat stress on labour productivity. Therefore, we did not directly calculate the GDP for specific regions; instead, this effect was considered within the linear regression analysis for data comparison in the next stage.

This labour-population-weighted approach enables identification of regions with varying economic vulnerabilities under identical heat exposure conditions due to differences in population density and labour participation characteristics. The methodology supports evidence-based resource allocation for climate adaptation strategies by prioritising areas where heat-induced productivity losses could lead to a significant economic impact, providing precise spatial targeting for urban climate resilience planning.

Analysis and assessment

This study employs a comprehensive three-tier analytical framework integrating ground-based observational data with ERA5-Land reanalysis datasets to systematically evaluate climate change impacts on labour productivity. The methodology combines Köppen-Geiger climate classification and land cover characteristics to assess the spatiotemporal patterns of heat-induced labour force losses through standardised performance metrics.

The primary analytical metric examines station-specific annual Labour Loss Fraction accumulations from 2014 to 2023, calculated for the 6:00–20:00 working period. The time frame is accommodated for regional differences in work schedules, encompassing the most probable working hours (8:00–17:00) plus flexible periods (6:00–8:00 and 17:00–19:00). This approach maintains consistency across the entire study area and period, including all calendar days without excluding public holidays or weekends.

Labour loss fraction statistical comparison

Linear regression methodology correlates annual cumulative labour loss fraction values with corresponding Köppen-Geiger climate types and land cover classifications for each station. This analysis validates ERA5-Land’s capacity to accurately represent ground-observed cumulative labour losses while identifying heat stress distribution patterns across distinct climate and land surface zones, thereby quantifying productivity loss magnitudes under varying climatic and land surface conditions.

Labour productivity loss statistical comparison

The labour productivity loss analysis extends the basic framework by incorporating the labour-population-weighted approach established in previous sections. This enhanced regression analysis employs demographic weighting factors to evaluate ERA5-Land’s ability to capture realistic heat-induced economic losses, providing more accurate representations of regional vulnerability patterns.

Bergen Metrics computation

Model performance evaluation utilises the Bergen Metrics comprehensive assessment framework38, an emerging climate model evaluation tool based on generalised p-norm mathematical architecture with L1 norm ( p=1) identified as the optimal evaluation criterion. The framework employs non-parametric clustering techniques to integrate multiple error metrics, effectively overcoming limitations associated with single or limited indicator assessments. Through comprehensive analysis of diverse error measures, including root mean square error, bias, and correlation coefficients, Bergen Metrics provide objective and robust model performance evaluations by quantifying agreement between ground observations and ERA5-Land datasets. Discussion

Impact of climate zonal differences in assessing cumulative heat-induced labour loss fraction

Quantitative analysis using the Köppen-Geiger climate classification demonstrates pronounced variability in the accuracy of ERA5-Land reanalysis data across different climate zones. As shown in Fig. 2, in temperate regions, ERA5-Land exhibits high reliability for evaluating heat-induced labour productivity losses, with an R2 of 0.85 and a regression slope of 0.83. This performance is consistent with results from Mediterranean climate studies39. Conversely, in tropical climates, ERA5-Land systematically underestimates cumulative labour loss ratios by up to 17.8% compared to observed values and shows markedly reduced agreement (R2 = 0.27). Such regional discrepancies are also evident in the normalised root mean square error (nRMSE), rising from 22% in temperate regions to 25% in the tropics (see Table 2). These findings are paralleled in European research, where higher errors were reported in southern versus central/northern regions, reflecting the challenges reanalysis models face with complex convective processes and climatic heterogeneity40. For continental climates, ERA5-Land slightly overestimates losses with a relatively lower R2 of 0.57, indicating limited predictive capacity, likely linked to adaptation dynamics as reported for continental climate zones1.

Impact of urbanisation and land cover in assessing cumulative heat-induced labour loss fraction

Land cover type substantially influences the quantitative accuracy of ERA5-Land data, as visualised in Fig. 3. In urban-dominated areas, model agreement with observations reaches R2 = 0.94 and an average difference of merely 1.5%. In contrast, natural-cover areas exhibit lower concordance (R2 = 0.69) and a discrepancy of 13.9%. The enhanced performance in urban contexts is consistent with findings that urban heat island effects produce more spatially homogeneous thermal environments, thereby enhancing model accuracy. Conversely, the greater land-atmosphere feedback complexity in rural and natural landscapes likely limits the performance of current reanalysis products41. This discrepancy reflects fundamental physical constraints of the model. As noted by Muñoz-Sabater et al.17, the offline configuration of ERA5-Land, while computationally efficient, does not assimilate surface observations directly and may therefore miss small-scale heterogeneity in natural landscapes.

In addition, orographic smoothing at 9‑km resolution limits the model’s ability to resolve microclimatic thermal extremes in complex terrain, where ground stations frequently register peak heat conditions that are averaged out at the 0.1° grid scale. Unlike the relatively thermally homogeneous urban environments (R² = 0.94), natural-cover regions are governed by more intricate land-atmosphere feedback, including spatially variable evapotranspiration and soil moisture dynamics that modulate surface energy partitioning. To account for these sub-grid uncertainties in policy applications, we interpret the 15% adjustment coefficient as a conservative safety margin relative to the observational baseline, ensuring that regional risk assessments explicitly incorporate these inherent modelling limitations.

Impact of population density weighting in assessing cumulative heat-induced labour productivity loss

This research introduces an approach by incorporating population and labour force participation rates as weighting factors in the analysis (see Fig. 4). This adjustment aligns the assessment more closely with the effective at-risk workforce within each region. The population-weighted analysis notably increases statistical agreement, with R2 improvements in the tropics from 0.27 to 0.70 (159%), and similar enhancements in continental and natural-cover regions. The Bergen Metrics (see Table 2) further quantifies these gains, with error reductions up to 52% in the tropics. These results support previous findings that accounting for population heterogeneity can substantially improve the precision of public health and labour impact assessments42.

Quantitative basis for policy and regional suitability

Although ERA5-Land demonstrates systematic underestimation or overestimation in certain regions, it generally meets national accuracy standards, particularly in temperate and urban areas where the coefficient of determination (R2) exceeds 0.85. From a policy perspective, the implications can be divided into two main aspects. First, regarding the cumulative labour loss fraction, this indicator does not directly account for the absolute number of potentially affected workers; nevertheless, it is inherently valuable for regional heat risk management and the formulation of labour-related policies. Quantitative analysis indicates that ERA5-Land systematically underestimates cumulative labour loss by 17.8% in tropical regions (Fig. 2b) and 13.9% in natural-cover areas (Fig. 3b). To manage these systematic biases, we propose a hierarchical uncertainty framework for policy guidance. On an aggregate level, we define a 20% safety margin for tropical regions and a 15% adjustment coefficient for natural-cover landscapes. These margins are established as conservative buffers to encompass the overall underestimation uncertainty relative to the ground-truth observation baseline. For instance, a 20% margin implies that reanalysis results should be adjusted (e.g., cumulative labour loss from ERA5) to approximate the actual observed risk. For more granular applications, our analysis (i.e., Table S1 in Appendix) reveals that within the tropics, this underestimation varies significantly, ranging from 6.67% in urban cores to 31.55% in natural environments, providing practitioners with a tiered approach for precision risk management. Furthermore, the statistical reliability of ERA5-Land, quantified through metrics such as nRMSE and the Bergen Metric, provides a rigorous basis for determining the effectiveness of these regional interventions. A higher nRMSE (e.g., 0.25–0.31) represents a policy under-protection risk. For example, if ERA5-Land predicts a WBGT of 32 °C, falling below the 33 °C ‘High’ heat stress trigger defined by the Singapore Ministry of Health (MOH)43, a systematic 18% underestimation could mask a real-world scenario where exposure already exceeds 33°C. Failing to trigger mandatory safety protocols due to this discrepancy leaves the workforce vulnerable. Therefore, we interpret the 15–20% safety margins as resilience buffers necessary to encompass these inherent modelling limitations. Conversely, the Bergen Metrics serve as a policy confidence index; its substantial reduction from 1.47 to 0.71 via labour-population weighting confirms that the assessment is most robust precisely where the workforce is most concentrated. By utilising these metrics as quantitative suitability markers, we ensure that the proposed safety margins function as effective resilience buffers against environmental modelling limitations. Accurate regional estimations derived from adjusted reanalysis data provide valuable insights to inform protective labour policies and public health measures and have been documented to contribute to a reduction in heat-related disease incidence during working hours44.

Second, the cumulative labour productivity loss derived from ERA5-Land exhibits comparatively higher statistical accuracy. This enhanced accuracy facilitates more precise assessment of areas with varying levels of economic vulnerability under heat exposure conditions. Consequently, this data supports targeted allocation of labour resources within climate adaptation strategies, thereby providing precise spatial identification critical for urban climate resilience planning.

Limitations and future directions

Although the study period (2014–2023) encompasses the historically warmest decade, and ERA5-Land has demonstrated robust performance in estimating heat-induced labour productivity loss in urban and temperate areas across Pacific Asia, future accelerated climate change may introduce greater uncertainties and amplify biases, potentially limiting the model’s ability to comprehensively assess extreme climate scenarios. Moreover, the spatial resolution of ERA5-Land (0.1°) may be insufficient to resolve microclimatic variations in complex natural terrains or densely built urban cores. This limitation is particularly evident when comparing Meteorological observations from a single station to grid-based data, a common challenge in point-to-grid validation. Additional constraints arise from incomplete meteorological records at several monitoring stations, especially in tropical and remote regions of Pacific Asia where station density is relatively low45. This can critically impact data assimilation processes during ERA5 generation6, thereby reducing the accuracy of high-resolution spatiotemporal meteorological simulations produced by ERA5-Land30. Future studies should prioritise next-generation global reanalysis datasets, such as ERA646, and employ high-resolution (< 5 km) regional models, particularly for the tropical Pacific Asia region. Integrating advanced satellite remote sensing data will also be critical for real-time bias correction and data assimilation47. To address data scarcity in tropical and remote areas with limited WMO station coverage, frameworks should incorporate high-density local observation networks, including crowdsourced meteorological data48 and IoT systems49. These localised sources can resolve the microclimatic heterogeneities often lost in 0.1° grid averages, providing a robust basis for real-time correction. Collectively, these advancements are expected to improve spatial representation and predictive accuracy, potentially reducing systematic errors to below 5%.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the high-resolution global reanalysis dataset ERA5-Land for estimating heat-induced labour productivity loss in developed-economy regions in Pacific Asia, using comparative analysis with 37 ground-based meteorological stations over the historically warm decade from 2014 to 2023. Three key aspects were assessed: differences across Köppen-Geiger climate classifications, the impact of urbanisation and land cover, and the effectiveness of population density weighting.

These quantitative results highlight the region-specific applicability of ERA5-Land. In temperate climate zones, ERA5-Land showed strong agreement with ground observations (R2 = 0.85), suggesting its suitability to inform policymaking. In contrast, the dataset significantly underestimated heat-induced cumulative labour loss fraction in tropical regions (by up to 17.8%, R2 = 0.27), underscoring limitations in modelling complex convective conditions. Urban areas demonstrated higher predictive accuracy (R2 = 0.94) than areas with natural land cover (R2 = 0.69), indicating the positive impact of urban heat island effects on model performance.

The labour-population weighting approach functions as a strategic spatial filter. By aligning thermal signals with the effective labour force scale, it prioritises high-exposure urban zones where ERA5-Land performs optimally. This resulted in R2 in tropical regions improving from 0.27 to 0.70 (a 159% improvement), offering a practical approach for policy application, as the model is most accurate precisely where the workforce is most concentrated.

Based on these findings, we propose a differentiated, region-specific framework: (1) application with standard caution in temperate and urban environments; (2) a 20% safety margin for tropical regions and a 15% adjustment for natural environments to buffer against high-level underestimation uncertainty; (3) a granular correction (e.g., 31.55% for tropical natural areas) where localised land-cover data is available; and (4) the inclusion of labour-weighting in standard assessments can improve index performance by 15–50%, thereby mitigating the policy under-protection risk, where model inaccuracies (nRMSE) might cause a failure to trigger safety advisories. This improvement simultaneously bolsters decision confidence, quantified by the Bergen Metric, supporting better-informed policy interventions in heat-vulnerable occupational sectors.

It is important to explicitly acknowledge the limitations of this approach. First, the spatial weighting factor assumes a relatively uniform distribution of the labour force within the 0.1° grid cells based on national averages, which may not perfectly capture block-level occupational demographics. Second, the current exposure-response function primarily targets heavy manual labour, highlighting the need for future integration of sector-specific physiological models. Finally, despite its high resolution, ERA5-Land’s orographic smoothing may still mask extreme microclimatic variations and localised anthropogenic heat.

Despite limitations such as orographic smoothing, ERA5-Land remains a highly useful tool for national-scale policy support. Future research can focus on: (1) the application of next-generation reanalysis products, e.g., ERA6, and the implementation of higher-resolution regional models in tropical regions of Pacific Asia; (2) the integration of advanced satellite remote sensing for real-time bias correction and data assimilation; and (3) the integration of local high-density observation networks, including crowdsourced meteorological data and IoT sensor arrays, to resolve small-scale variations in sparse-station regions. These advancements will further improve the accuracy of targeted public health interventions and resilient occupational health policies.
