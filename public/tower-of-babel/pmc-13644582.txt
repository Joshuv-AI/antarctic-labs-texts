Radiotherapy followed by metformin neoadjuvant therapy drives superior pancreatic cancer ferroptosis and augments anti-tumor immunity
Authors: Haitao Zhu, Yi Gong, Xin Wang, Zining Zhang, Bo Wang, Wenjin Xu, Yuting Su, John E. Eriksson, Dongqing Wang, Shijie Wei, Yanfang Liu, Xiang Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although neoadjuvant therapy has achieved higher levels of resectability and improved the survival outcomes of pancreatic carcinoma patients, cancer cells' resistance to conventional cell death has limited its clinical efficiency. Thus, developing an optimal sequence of novel neoadjuvant therapy modalities to boost cancer cell death is urgently needed. The current work addresses this issue by developing a combination of radiotherapy with metformin as a novel neoadjuvant therapy and investigating its optimal sequencing treatment to boost cancer cell ferroptosis. Of the three treatment regimens investigated, the sequential treatment with RT followed by metformin (i.e. as opposed to metformin followed by RT, or simultaneous treatment with both RT and metformin) was most effective in eliciting ferroptosis in vitro and in animal models. Mechanically, RT followed by metformin is dependent on the inhibition of the AMPK-GOT1 axis, which further leads to the arrest of cancer cells in G1/S phase, and ultimately ferroptosis. Moreover, using a combination of patient samples and mouse models, RT followed by metformin enhanced the anti-tumor immune cells (IFNγ+CD8+T cells, DC cells, and M1 macrophage) infiltration and cytokines (IFN-γ and IL-2) level, inhibited the pro-tumor immune cells (PD-1+TIM3+ CD8+T cells and M2 macrophage) infiltration and cytokines (TGF-β and IL-10) level. Overall, the study developed an optimal sequencing of neoadjuvant therapy modality that induced superior cancer cell ferroptosis and boosted anti-tumor immunity, which presented a promising therapeutic approach for refractory tumors.

Introduction

Neoadjuvant therapies have achieved considerable progress in enhancing the resectability level and improving the survival rates of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) patients [1]. Regardless, PDAC is still one of the greatest challenges in oncology, with the overall survival rate remaining around 11% and most patients ultimately suffer disease relapse [2]. The failure of the neoadjuvant therapy is ascribed to various factors, such as the low efficiency of treatment modality and sequence, systemic cytotoxicity to the immune system, as well as the innate resistance ability of cancer cells to the conventional regulated cell death [3,4]. Thus, there is still an unmet need for developing a novel neoadjuvant therapy with an optimal sequence to boost cancer cell death.

Ferroptosis is a novel regulated form of cell death characterized by uncontrolled lipid peroxidation [5]. Distinct from the traditional cancer therapy-induced conventional cell death, induction of ferroptosis not only suppresses tumor growth but holds potential for augmenting immune response and surmounting traditional therapy resistance [6,7]. Thus, ferroptosis has been implicated as a robust modality in refractory cancer treatment [8,9]. Depending on generating excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lipid peroxidation, promoting the biosynthesis of phospholipids (PUFA-PLs), as well as impairing antioxidant responses mediated by glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), radiotherapy (RT) induced ferroptosis displayed a promising strategy for treating various refractory tumors [[10], [11], [12]]. Thus, the use of RT induced ferroptosis as a component of neoadjuvant therapy presents itself as a fruitful alternative for investigation.

Cancer cells exposed to RT typically could activate several cytotoxic signaling pathways and cytoprotective mechanisms. Accordingly, cancer cells may employ multiple ferroptosis defense systems, such as ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1), to counteract ferroptosis and reduce their susceptibility to RT. Combination therapy strategies are therefore helpful to enhance RT-induced ferroptosis and achieve a more effective cure for tumors. Various anticancer drugs have been used in concert with RT in order to help suppress these mechanisms. DNA damage response (DDR) inhibitors, for example, have been shown to be effective in killing radioresistant cancer cells [13]. The potent cytoprotective effects of 3-methyladenine, an autophagy inhibitor, have also been observed to enhance radiosensitivity in preclinical models of hepatocellular carcinoma and esophageal cancer [14]. Although these strategies are promising in elevating the RT efficacy, their subsequent translation to the clinic has been hindered by concerns over safety. Metformin, the most widely used oral hypoglycemic agent in clinical practice, is becoming a hot topic in oncology research. Metformin has been proven to activate AMP-activated protein kinases (AMPK) and increase the intracellular levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [[15], [16], [17]]. It has also been shown to regulate levels of intracellular iron and glutaminase metabolism, which can also influence tumorigenesis [18,19]. Given the critical roles of both ROS and iron in ferroptosis, we were therefore intrigued to explore the possibility of a synergistic relationship between metformin and RT in the induction of ferroptosis as a viable form of neoadjuvant therapy.

To test this hypothesis, we treated pancreatic cancer cells with three distinct metformin and RT combination schedules, namely: RT followed by metformin (RT + M), metformin followed by RT (M + RT), and treatment with both RT and metformin simultaneously (RT & M). Our results showed that the RT + M schedule induced significantly more ferroptotic cell death and augmented anti-tumor immunity in vitro and in vivo compared with the other two regimens of treatment. Moreover, the AMPK/GOT1 axis arrested the cancer cells in G1/S phase, which was the underlying mechanism involved in this process. Overall, these findings demonstrated that the RT + M treatment sequence, as a novel neoadjuvant therapy, had promising clinical implications for the treatment of refractory tumors.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

Human pancreatic cancer cells (Panc1, PaTu 8988t, BxPC-3, MIA-PaCa-2), human fibrosarcoma cells (HT1080), mouse pancreatic cancer cells (Panc02), rat cardiomyocytes (H9C2), and mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEF) were obtained from the Cell Bank of the China Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Human pancreatic duct epithelial cells (HPDE6-C7) were obtained from the WHELAB (Shanghai, China). Human pancreatic stellate cells (PSC) were obtained from the Fenghui Biotechnology Company (Hunan, China). All cells were maintained in a 37 °C incubator at 5% CO2. Panc1, PaTu 8988t, BxPC-3, MIA PaCa-2, HT1080, H9C2, MEF, and HPDE6-C7 cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM). Panc02 and PSC cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI-1640) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, ExCell FSP500, USA) and antibiotics (80 U/mL penicillin, 0.08 mg/mL streptomycin). All the cell culture media were purchased from Key GEN Biotechnology Company (China).

Cultured cancer cells were treated with the indicated conditions as follow: RT + M group cells received RT (4, 8 Gy) 24 h prior to metformin (5 mM, 10 mM) for 48 h; RT & M group cells received simultaneous RT (4, 8 Gy) and metformin (5 mM, 10 mM) for 48 h; M + RT group cells received 24 h metformin (5 mM, 10 mM) prior to RT (4, 8 Gy) for 48 h.

Reagents

Metformin hydrochloride (#HY-17471A), L-Buthionine-(S, R)-sulfoximine (BSO; #HY-106376), Ferrostatin-1(Fer-1; #HY-100579), Liproxstatin-1 (Lip-1; #HY-12726), AUZ 454 (#HY-15004), BSO(#HY-106376A), CDC25B-IN-1(#HY-126246) were purchased from Med Chem Express (MCE, USA). Compound C (#ab120843) was purchased from Abcam (UK). C11-BODIPY (#RM02821) was purchased from ABclonal (China). The programmed cell necrosis induction Kit was purchased from Beyotime (China).

RNA sequencing and Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

About 2 µg RNA per sample was used for the RNA sample preparations. Sequencing libraries were generated using a NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (#E7530L, NEB, USA), and index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. Libraries were prepared as follows. mRNA was purified from total RNA using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. Fragmentation was carried out using divalent cations under elevated temperature in NEBNext First Strand Synthesis Reaction Buffer (5X). Strand cDNA was synthesized using a random hexamer primer, RNase H, buffer, dNTPs, and DNA polymerase I. Library fragments were purified with a QiaQuick PCR kit and eluted with EB buffer, followed by end repair, A-tail, and adapter addition. The target product was extracted for PCR, and the library was constructed. The gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) analysis used Cluster Profiler’s gseGO command. For the GSEA, gseGO parameters were set to repeat 10,000 times, nPerm = 1000, minGSSize = 10, and maxGSSize = 1000.

X-ray irradiation treatment

X-ray irradiation was delivered at a dose rate of approximately 2, 4, 6, or 8 gray (Gy) per minute using a 23EX Medical High Energy Linear Accelerator (Varian, USA), installed at The Affiliated Hospital of Jiangsu University. Local irradiation of tumors was achieved via lead shielding or collimation.

Colony formation assay

Six-well plates were seeded with 1000–2000 cancer cells per well. Cells were treated with or without RT, RT + M, RT & M, and M + RT, respectively. Cell medium was changed every 2–3 days following treatment. Following 10–14 days of incubation, colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature, washed twice with PBS, and stained with crystal violet for 20–30 min at room temperature. The number, size, and dimensions of the clone were analyzed.

Lipid peroxidation assay

Cancer cells were seeded in six-well plates with 1 × 105 cells per well, and further treated with or without RT, RT + M, RT & M, and M + RT, respectively. About 2 µM C11-BODIPY was then added, and cells were incubated for 15–20 min at 37 °C. Labeled cells were trypsinized and resuspended in PBS plus 5% FBS. Oxidation of the polyunsaturated butadienyl portion of C11-BODIPY resulted in a shift of the fluorescence emission peak from ∼590 nm to ∼510 nm proportional to lipid ROS generation, as analyzed by flow cytometry (BECKKMAN COULTER, USA). Data was processed using Flow Jo 10 software [20].

GSH and MDA assay

GSH and MDA levels of cancer cells or tumor tissues were measured with a GSH kit (A006-2-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) or MDA kit (A003-1-2, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China), respectively. The cells or tumor tissues were collected, suspended in PBS, and then ultrasonically disintegrated or homogenized. Following the instructions of the kit, quantification of the GSH and MDA content in samples was determined by colorimetry (OD: GSH = 405 nm, MDA = 532 nm). All experiments were performed as described previously [21].

Cell cycle assay

Cancer cells were seeded in six-well plates with 1 × 105 cells per well, and further treated with or without RT, RT + M, RT & M, and M + RT, respectively. Cells were then treated with 70% cold ethanol for 2 h at 4 °C to break membranes, processed with a cell cycle kit (KGA512, Key GEN Bio TECH, China), and then analyzed by flow cytometry (BECKKMAN COULTER, USA). Data was processed using Flow Jo 10 software.

Western blot

Cancer cells or tumor tissues were lysed on ice for 30 min and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Western blot assays were performed as described previously [21]. Antibodies were used as following: anti-human GPX4 (Abcam, # ab41787, 1:1000), anti-human SLC7A11 (Proteintech, # 26864-1-AP, 1:1000), anti-human GOT1 (Proteintech, # 14886-1-AP, 1:1000), anti-mouse GOT1 (Proteintech, # 60317-1-Ig), anti-human CDK2 (Proteintech, # 10122-1-AP, 1:1000), anti-human CDK4 (Proteintech, # 11026-1-AP, 1:1000), anti-human CDK6 (Proteintech, # 14052-1-AP, 1:1000), anti-human Phospho-AMPKα (Thr172) (CST, # 2535,1:1000), and anti-human AMPK (CST, # 5831, 1:1000). Secondary antibodies (either anti-rabbit or anti-mouse) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Invitrogen).

RNAi transfection

Cancer cells were seeded in six-well plates with an overnight cell count of 60–70%. To generate knockdown and knockout cells, cells were infected with a lentivirus carrying shRNA and then selected for puromycin (1 µg/ml) for 10–14 days. These stable cell lines were preserved in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 0.75 µg/ml puromycin for further experiments. The shRNA sequences are as follows: shControl: 5’-GTTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGTT-3’ shGOT1-1: 5’-GCGTTGGTACAATGGAACAAA-3’ shGOT1-2: 5’-GCTAATGACAATAGCCTAAAT-3’ shGOT1-3: 5’-GGAACAGGTGCACTTCGAATT-3’

GOT1 overexpression experiment

The mammalian expression plasmid hGOT1-OE (FC-3668) was purchased from the Fubio Company (Suzhou, China). Panc1 cells were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 60%–70% per well. The next day, cells were infected with the above vector. 24 h later, the virus solution was added again. After 48 h of infection, GOT1 overexpression cells were screened with medium containing 2 µg/mL of puromycin. After 6–7 days of continuous screening, cells were prepared for subsequent use.

AMPK knockout experiment

The AMPKα1/α2 knockout cell line was constructed using the dual-sgRNA CRISPR/Cas9 method. Two sgRNAs targeting PRKAA1 and PRKAA2 were synthesized into a dual-sgRNA system fragment and ligated to the lentiCRISPRv2 vector. The virus transfection procedure is the same as described above. The sgRNA sequences are as follows:

PRKAA1: 5’-AGAAGCAGAAACACGACGGG-3’

PRKAA2: 5’-AGAAGCAGAAGCACGACGGG-3’

TEM imaging

To observe the subcellular structure, cancer cells treated with various agents were harvested and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 24 h at 4 °C, and subsequently fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h. Specimens were dehydrated in a graded series of acetone and embedded in epoxy resin. After ultramicrotomy, ultrathin sections were stained with uranyl acetate for 15 min and modified with lead citrate for 5 min. Finally, the subcellular structure was observed by TEM using a Hitachi HT7800 microscope (Japan).

Xenograft studies

Animal studies were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Jiangsu University (UJS-IACUC-AP-2024031507). Five-week-old C57BL/6 female mice (Changzhou Cavins Laboratory Animal Company) were used as experimental subjects and cultured in the standard environment of the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangsu University.

Panc02 cells (1.5 × 105) were injected subcutaneously into the right dorsal flanks of C57BL/6 mice. When the tumor volume reached 50–100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into eight groups (five mice per group): untreated (control), M (100 mg/kg), RT (8 Gy), RT (8 Gy) + Fer-1 (1 mg/kg), M (100 mg/kg) + RT (8 Gy), RT (8 Gy) + M (100 mg/kg), RT (8 Gy) & M (100 mg/kg), RT (8 Gy)+ M (100 mg/kg) + Fer-1 (1 mg/kg). Tumor volume and growth rate were monitored every 2 days for 2 weeks.

For the immunity assessment, fresh mouse tumor tissues were stored in tissue preservation solution at 4 °C and analyzed within 4 hours. To facilitate dissociation, the tissues were digested with a mixture of 20 mg/ml Dispase II, 20 mg/ml lysozyme, and 20 mg/ml collagenase at 37 °C with rotation for 60 min, followed by filtration through a 70 µm cell strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension. Red blood cells were lysed at room temperature for 10 min. For surface staining, the single-cell suspension was stained with directly conjugated antibodies in the dark at 4 °C for 30 min. For intracellular staining, cells were first stained for surface antigens, then fixed and permeabilized using the Cytofix/Cytoperm kit (BD Biosciences), followed by staining for intracellular proteins. A total of 1 × 106 cells per sample were used for further analysis, following the official protocol from https://www.biolegend.com. Cells stained with isotype control antibodies served as negative controls, and Zombie dye was used to detect viable cells. Data were acquired by flow cytometry and analyzed using FlowJo software. The antibodies used for flow cytometry were anti-CD45 FITC (BioLegend, #103108, 1:200), anti-CD8a BV605 (BioLegend, #100743, 1:200), anti-PD-1 APC/Cyanine7 (BioLegend, #135223, 1:200), anti-TIM3 PE (BioLegend, #134003, 1:200), and anti-FNγ Percp/Cy5.5 (BioLegend, #505822, 1:200).

For the rechallenged experiments, 5 × 106 Panc02 cells treated with the following group (10 mice per group): (1) untreated (control), (2) RT + M, (3) RT + M+ Fer-1, and then were inoculated subcutaneously in 150 µl of phosphatebuffered saline (PBS) into the lower right flanks of C57BL/6J mice. About 7 days later, 1 × 106 untreated Panc02 cells were inoculated into the contralateral lower flank. The percentage of tumor-free mice was monitored every 2 days for 4 weeks.

Patient population

This study was reviewed and approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of Affiliated Hospital of Jiangsu University (SWYXLL20190225-3). As a retrospective study, the data of patients (n = 78) from the Department of Radiation Oncology, the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangsu University, were gathered. Following the exclusion criteria, 18 patients were enrolled. The basic characteristic of patients was acquired by telephone interview, as shown in Table S1.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) analysis

Tumor tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin wax. Tissue sections of 4 µm thickness were cut off for IHC analysis. Sections were subsequently dewaxed and then incubated with anti-human GOT1 (Proteintech, #14886-1-AP), anti-mouse GOT1 (Proteintech, #60317-1-Ig), and anti-mouse CD8 (Proteintech, #66868-1-Ig), and anti-mouse CD11c (Abclonal, #A1508), respectively, at 4 °C overnight. After being stained with hematoxylin, the sections were sealed with neutral resin, scanned and analyzed under an Olympus microscope.

Human pancreatic cancer tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned (4 µm). After deparaffinization and antigen retrieval in citrate buffer, sections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100, blocked with 5% BSA, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-rabbit Ki-67 (Proteintech, #28074-1-AP) and anti-mouse GOT1 (Proteintech, #60317-1-Ig). Species-matched Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h, followed by DAPI nuclear counterstaining. Slides were imaged using fluorescence microscopy.

Biochemical assay

The concentrations of IFN-γ (R&D Systems, MIF00), IL-2 (R&D Systems, M2000), IL-10 (DAKEWE, 1211002), and TGF-β (DAKEWE, 1217102) in the serum samples were measured using ELISA according to the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Protein structure prediction and interaction site analysis

The amino acid sequence of the protein of interest was retrieved from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). The retrieved sequence was subsequently submitted to the AlphaFold3 server to predict its three-dimensional structure in complex with potential interacting partners. From the five generated models, the model with the highest ranking score (Model 0) was selected for further analysis. The intermolecular interaction sites within the selected model were identified by analyzing residue contacts and spatial proximity at the binding interface. For visualization, the structure was rendered using the open-source PyMOL software (Version 2.6), where specific amino acid residues involved in the interactions are explicitly displayed.

Software and statistical analysis

Flow cytometry data processing was performed using Flow Jo 10 software. Quantifications of western blot and colony formation assay were performed using ImageJ software (NIH, v.2.0.0). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. The unpaired Student’s t-test was used to compare two experimental groups, and a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare consecutive results of multiple experimental groups. For all experiments, P < 0.05 was considered significant. All experiments were performed using at least three independent biological replicates and reported as mean ± standard error of the mean.

Results

Radiotherapy followed by metformin (RT + M) sequential administration elicits superior ferroptosis

As radiotherapy (RT) has been confirmed to induce ferroptosis in several cancer cell lines [12], we first assessed the sensitivity of pancreatic cancer cell lines to RT induced ferroptosis. According to the previous publication and our primary experiments, we treated the human pancreatic cancer cell lines with 8 grays (Gy) X-ray irradiation. Following treatment, Lipid ROS significantly increased in the PaTu 8988t, BxPC-3, MIA PaCa-2, rather than Panc1, indicating that Panc1 was more resistant to RT induced ferroptosis than other cell lines (Fig. S1a). Moreover, colony formation ability of Panc1 cells was mildly inhibited in the presence of 4 Gy irradiation, which could not be reversed by ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), a ferroptosis inhibitor (Figs. 1a and S2a). Interestingly, compared to the control group(untreated), the expression levels of GPX4, a ferroptotic cell death indicator, increased after irradiation with 4 Gy, but did not significantly change in response to 8 Gy (Fig. S1b). Next, we determined the concentrations of metformin used for treating Panc1 cells and normal cells (PSC, HPDE6-C7, H9C2, MEF) based on both our own preliminary experiments and a previous publication [20]. CCK8 results revealed that 5 and 10 mM doses of metformin had little effect on normal cells (Fig. S1c), but induced a half-lethal effect in Panc1 cells (Fig. S1d). About 5 and 10 mM metformin also inhibited the colony-forming ability of Panc1 cells, which could not be attenuated in the presence of Fer-1 (Figs. 1a and S2a). Furthermore, treatment with 5 and 10 mM metformin did not significantly alter the levels of GSH, lipid ROS, MDA, as well as the SLC1A11 and GPX4 expression level (Fig. 1b-e), suggesting that metformin alone failed to induce ferroptosis in Panc1 cells at these concentrations. Based on these findings, we chose 5 and 10 mM doses of metformin for the subsequent experiments.

To assess the synergistic effects metformin and RT on the induction of ferroptosis, we subjected Panc1 cells to the following treatment regimens: (1) RT + M (24 h RT prior to treatment with metformin); (2) RT & M (simultaneous treatment with both RT and metformin); (3) M + RT (24 h of metformin treatment prior to RT). The results showed that the RT + M group had a significantly higher ability to inhibit colony formation compared to the other groups (Figs. 1a and S2a, S2b). Notably, only RT + M inhibited colony formation ability could be reversed by administering Fer-1 and liproxstatin-1 (Lip-1), another ferroptosis inhibitor (Figs. 1a and S2a, S2b). Similar results were obtained in HT1080 and Panc02 cancer cells (Fig. S3). Compared to the other groups, RT + M treatment was also unique in the increased levels of lipid ROS and malondialdehyde (MDA), an end product of lipid peroxidation, and decreased levels of GSH and GPX4, while it had no effect on the SLC7A11 expression level (Figs. 1b-e and S2c). Distinct from the other groups, the morphological compositions of cancer cells in the RT + M group also showed ferroptosis characteristics, including smaller mitochondria with more condensed membrane density, and reduced mitochondrial cristae, both of which are distinct from features typically associated with autophagy or necrosis (Figs. 1f and S2d). These findings suggest that sequential treatment with RT followed by metformin (RT + M) is the most effective neoadjuvant therapy regimen for boosting cancer cell ferroptosis.

G1/S phase arrest is the underlying mechanism of RT + M neoadjuvant therapy-induced ferroptosis

To further understand the underlying mechanism responsible for the synergistic effect of metformin on radiotherapy induced ferroptosis, RNA sequencing and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) were performed and the results revealed that cell cycle checkpoint signaling was significantly altered in Panc1 cells following RT + M treatment compared to the untreated group cells (Fig. 2a). Flow cytometry analysis showed that 8 Gy irradiation caused an increase in the percentage of cells in the G2/M phase, indicating that cells in this phase were more resistant to radiotherapy (Fig. 2b). Notably, treatment with metformin (5 mM or 10 mM) had no significant effect on the cell cycle (Figs. 2b and S4a). Interestingly, compared to the RT, RT & M, and M + RT groups, RT + M resulted in a significantly larger decrease in the G2 phase percentage and nearly a 20% increase in the G1/S phase percentage (Figs. 2b and S4a). The observed increase in the G1/S phase percentage could be attributed to either cancer cells in this phase being more resistant to RT + M therapy, or RT + M inducing cancer cell arrest. Treated Panc1 cancer cells with BSO, a ferroptosis inducer, also increased the G1/S phase cell percentage (Fig. S4b), and BSO-induced Panc1 cancer cell ferroptosis was enhanced in the presence of AUZ454, a G1/S phase arrest enhancer (Fig. S4c). To further validate this hypothesis, we assessed the expression levels of the G1/S phase progression regulators cyclin-dependent kinase 2 (CDK2), cyclin-dependent kinase 4 (CDK4), and cyclin-dependent kinase 6 (CDK6). As expected, metformin (5 mM or 10 mM) treatment had no effect on the CDK2, CDK4, and CDK6 expression, while RT + M treatment significantly decreased the expression levels of CDK2, CDK4, and CDK6, further supporting the discovery that Panc1 cells were arrested in the G1/S phase (Fig. 2c). To further validate G1/S phase cell arrest was an underlying cause of ferroptosis induced by RT + M, CDC25B-IN-1, a G1/S phase arrest inhibitor, was used. RT + M treatment induced increased lipid ROS and MDA levels, decreased GSH levels, as well as downregulation of GPX4 protein expression levels, all of which were significantly reversed in the presence of CDC25B-IN-1 (Fig. 2d-g). These findings suggested that G1/S phase arrest is a crucial factor in RT + M-induced ferroptosis in Panc1 cells.

Glutamate oxaloacetate transaminase 1 (GOT1) responded for RT + M neoadjuvant therapy induced G1/S phase arrest

GOT1 can function as both a redox homeostasis regulator and G1/S checkpoint cofactor [21,22]. We wondered whether GOT1 was also an important regulator in RT + M-induced G1/S phase arrest and ferroptosis. Immunofluorescence assay revealed that pancreatic carcinoma samples expressed a higher level of GOT1 than the normal pancreatic tissues (Fig. S5a). Also, higher levels of GOT1 were observed in Panc1 and BxPC-3 cancer cells than in PaTu 8988t and MIA PaCa-2 (Fig. S5b). Compared to the control group, single RT or metformin treatment has no effect on the GOT1 expression level, while RT + M treatment significantly inhibited GOT1 at protein expression levels (Fig. 3a), rather than RNA expression levels (Fig. S5c). The similarity expression change level of GPX4 and SLC7A11 can also be detected (Figs. 1e and S5c). To further validate the role of GOT1 in RT + M-induced G1/S phase arrest, we constructed a stable GOT1 overexpression (OE-GOT1) Panc1 cell line (Fig. S5d). Compared to control parental Panc1 cells, RT + M treatment induced increased lipid ROS and MDA levels, as well as decreased GSH levels and GPX4 expression levels, all of which were reversed in the OE-GOT1 Panc1 cells (Figs. 3b-e and S5e). Moreover, RT + M treatment induced the downregulation of CDK4, which was reversed in OE-GOT1 Panc1, while no significant difference existed for CDK2 and CDK6 (Fig. 3e). Furthermore, cell cycle analysis revealed that RT + M treatment induced the G1/S phase arrest, which was reversed in the OE-GOT1 Panc1 cells (Figs. 3f and S5f-h). To further validate the role of GOT1 on G1/S arrest, a GOT1 knockdown Panc1 cell line was established (Fig. 3g). Upon 8 Gy X-ray irradiation, the GOT1 knockdown Panc1 cell line displayed lower levels of intracellular GSH, higher levels of MDA, as well as the lower expression levels of CDK2, CDK4, CDK6, and GPX4 than the control parental Panc1 cells (Fig. 3h-j), which is similar to the effects of treatment with RT + M. RT + M neoadjuvant therapy down-regulated the GOT1 depending on AMPK phosphorylation

As a known sensor of metformin, we speculated that AMPK may play an important role upstream of GOT1 in the mechanistic pathway responsible for RT + M-induced G1/S arrest. Interestingly, both metformin and RT + M treatment enhanced the levels of p-AMPK (Figs. 4a and S6a), which was reversed in the presence of Compound C, an inhibitor of AMPK phosphorylation (Figs. 4b and S6a). Furthermore, RT + M treatment down-regulated GOT1 protein expression, which was also reversed in the presence of Compound C (Fig. 4b). To further elucidate the relationship between GOT1 and AMPK, western blot revealed that the phosphorylation level of AMPK was unchanged in the GOT1 knockdown cells (Fig. S6b), while GOT1 phosphorylation level was significantly inhibited in the AMPK knockout cancer cells (Fig. S6c), indicating that AMPK was upstream of GOT1. We next explored the role of AMPK phosphorylation in RT + M treatment-induced ferroptosis. Colony forming ability assay demonstrated that inhibition of AMPK phosphorylation rescued RT + M treatment-induced cell death (Figs. 4c and S6e). Moreover, Compound C partly reversed RT + M-induced G1 arrest and promoted cancer cells to enter the G2/M phases (Figs. 4d andS6f).

To further investigate the role of AMPK in ferroptosis induced by RT + M, an AMPK knockout Panc1 cell line was established (Fig. S6g), which displayed a lower intracellular lipid ROS level compared to control cells under the RT + M treatment (Figs. 4e and S6h). In summary, these results demonstrate that metformin enhances radiotherapy-induced ferroptosis as a result of regulation of G1/S arrest via the AMPK-GOT1 axis.

RT + M neoadjuvant therapy drives superior ferroptosis and anti-tumor immunity in vivo

To validate our in vitro observation, C57BL/6 mice bearing Panc02 subcutaneous tumor were established and then randomly divided into the following treatment groups: untreated (control), RT, M, RT + Fer-1, M + RT, M & RT, RT + M, RT + M + Fer-1 (Fig. 5a). Compared to the control group, all other treatment groups showed inhibition of tumor growth in vivo, with this effect most pronounced in the RT + M group (Fig. 5b-d). Moreover, the suppression of tumor growth by RT + M treatment could be reversed using Fer-1 (Fig. 5b, 5d). Immunohistochemistry assay revealed that RT + M treatment significantly downregulated the GOT1 expression level (Fig. 5e). Compared to treatment with RT alone, RT + M significantly decreased GPX4, GOT1, CDK2, CDK4, and CDK6 protein expression levels (Fig. 5f), GSH level (Fig. 5g), and also increased levels of MDA (Fig. 5h). Thus, it is clear that the in vitro results translated well to treatment in vivo, where a combination of RT followed by metformin was the most effective treatment regimen in suppressing tumor growth by inducing ferroptosis. The hallmark of immunogenic cell death is the formation of immunological memory that initiates a rapid recall response when the same antigen appears. To determine whether RT + M treatment elicited such a memory response, we carried out the rechallenged experiments (Fig. 6d). RT + M-treated Panc02 cells were injected subcutaneously into the right flanks of C57BL/6J mice. About 7 days later, the mice were rechallenged with live Panc02 cells injected into the opposite flank. The results revealed that RT + M-treated Panc02 cells protected mice from tumor rechallenge and that this long-distance protection was curtailed in the presence of Fer-1 (Fig. 6e). These findings indicate that RT + M treatment could suppress tumor growth through both driving cancer cell ferroptosis and inducing CD8+ T cell-dependent immune memory.

To verify the clinical significance of sequential treatment with radiotherapy and metformin, patients who suffered from the combination treatment of radiotherapy and metformin were enrolled. However, due to the limitation of sample size, Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed no significant difference between the RT and RT + M combination therapy (Fig. 6f).

Discussion

The study showed conclusively that radiotherapy followed by metformin (RT + M) was the best novel neoadjuvant therapy [i.e. as opposed to metformin followed by radiotherapy (M + RT) or simultaneous treatment with both (RT & M)] inducing ferroptosis in cancer cells and eliciting the anti-tumor immunity (Fig. 7). Moreover, it was shown that RT + M combination therapy exerted this effect by arresting cells in G1/S phase. It was also shown that this effect on the cell cycle was regulated by the AMPK-GOT1 axis. Our study highlights the therapeutic combination of RT + M as a promising candidate for clinical translation to treat refractory tumors.Fig. 7Schematic showing RT followed by metformin drove superior cancer cells ferroptosis and elicited anti-tumor immunity.Fig 7 dummy alt text

Effective neoadjuvant therapy is critical for improving pancreatic carcinoma’s overall survival rate, but it is still a formidable challenge. Although Radiotherapy is used in more than 50% of tumor patients for neoadjuvant therapy purposes, the net effect was unsatisfactory. TGF-β inhibitor, fresolimumab, which has been used in combination with RT in phase II clinical trials to treat metastatic breast cancer with great efficacy and acceptable toxicity profiles [23]. In addition to the potential toxicity and side effects, administration schedules also play a critical role in determining therapeutic efficacy, which was also confirmed in the present study. Different therapeutic sequences displayed markedly distinct profiles in the induction of ferroptosis in vitro and in vivo. However, our clinical data could not confirm our in vivo and in vitro results. This may be ascribed to several factors, i) the present clinical trial belonged to the retrospective study, which has the intrinsic limitations, such as the susceptibility to bias and poor data quality. Some of the enrolled participants may inaccurately recall past events, which leads to omissions or distortions in information during data collection. Critically, enrolled tumor patients received metformin prior to RT, resulting in a sample that is not representative of the target population. Also, basic characteristic data of the enrolled samples were often incomplete or missing, compromising their reliability, which may lead to erroneous conclusions and hinder accurate determination of causal relationships.; ii) the present study participants were from a single center with a small sample size, which may result in biased results, unreliable conclusions, and difficulty in reflecting the real world. Thus, the exact clinical value of RT + M treatment still needs a prospective clinical trial with a large cohort from multiple centers and a thorough investigation in the future. Also, the enrolled patient baseline characteristics and treatment time should be carefully considered.

The cell cycle has long been considered to be an important factor in resistance to radiotherapy. Numerous studies have shown that CDK inhibitors can be combined with radiotherapy, resulting in a delay to DNA damage repair and an enhancement of apoptosis [14,21]. The precise mechanistic relationship between cell cycle arrest and ferroptosis, however, has yet to be investigated in detail. This outstanding issue was addressed by our current study, by first establishing that cancer cells in the G2 phase showed resistance to ferroptosis. G1 and S phase cancer cells, however, were shown to have a much greater susceptibility towards ferroptosis, which is likely a result of their requirement for large amounts of raw materials for DNA and protein synthesis, which can cause imbalances in intracellular redox levels. This is in stark contrast to cells in the G2 phase, where DNA synthesis and subsequently intracellular redox levels exist in relative equilibrium.

As a sensor of cellular energy, AMPK activation is closely related to the energy stress, especially the ATP/AMP ratio change [24]. In addition to the canonical activator, AMPK could also be regulated by transforming growth factor-β-activated kinase 1, lysosomal damage and autophagy, and glucose deprivation. Moreover, AMPK could also be phosphorylated at Thr172 in response to various DNA-damaging agents, such as ionizing radiation [25]. In the present study, both radiotherapy (DNA damage) and metformin (glucose and mitochondrial) could activate the AMPK. AMPK activation could function as either a tumor suppressor or a tumor promoter, depending on the surrounding context and the downstream signals. AMPK has now been reported to phosphorylate more than 100 distinct target proteins, which ultimately impact almost all aspects of cell metabolism and function [26,27]. The present study revealed that under the RT + M treatment condition, GOT1 is downstream of the AMPK. The canonical function role of GOT1 is to support mitochondrial metabolism and facilitate oxidative phosphorylation (OxPHOS) [22]. GOT1 is dispensable for cancer cells’ ATP/AMP ratio regulation, energy stress, and survival, especially in the Kras mutation PDAC cancer cells. In addition, GOT1 is essential for redox balance, cell cycle progression, and adaptive labile iron release [28]. Considering the functional overlap between GOT1 and AMPK, GOT1 as a downstream signal of AMPK and the underlying mechanisms of RT + M treatment are reasonable. However, there was no direct evidence of GOT1 and AMPK regulation reported previously. In the present study, molecular docking and IP results confirmed that AMPK displayed high affinity with GOT1 and further phosphorylated it. However, the comprehensive molecular mechanisms deserve deeper investigation.

Conclusion

In summary, we treated pancreatic cancer cells with three distinct metformin and RT combination schedules, namely: RT followed by metformin (RT + M), metformin followed by RT (M + RT), and treatment with both RT and metformin simultaneously (RT & M). We discovered that the RT + M schedule induced significantly more ferroptotic cell death and augmented anti-tumor immunity in vitro and in vivo compared with the other two regimens of treatment. Moreover, the AMPK/GOT1 axis arrested the cancer cells in G1/S phase, which was the underlying mechanism involved in this process. Overall, these findings demonstrated that the RT + M treatment sequence, as a novel neoadjuvant therapy, had promising clinical implications for the treatment of refractory tumors.
