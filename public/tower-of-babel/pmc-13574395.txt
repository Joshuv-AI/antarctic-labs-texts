Association of gestational diabetes with other lactation-related disorders
Authors: Chunyan Yang, Wenhui Song, Kaosheng Lu, Wei Dang, Jing Li, Yaning Wang, Meijia Zhang, Xiaoxue Cheng, Xiaohua Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is associated with adverse metabolic outcomes and may also be related to postpartum breast and lactation disorders. Observational studies are susceptible to confounding and reverse causation. We used a 2-sample Mendelian randomization design to examine the association between genetic liability to GDM and other disorders of the breast and lactation associated with childbirth. Genetic liability to GDM was associated with higher odds of a composite phenotype of breast and lactation disorders associated with childbirth. Replication using independent samples and more specific clinical outcomes is required. Summary statistics for GDM and the FinnGen outcome “other disorders of breast and lactation associated with childbirth” were obtained from the Integrative Epidemiology Unit open genome-wide association study resource. single nucleotide polymorphisms associated with GDM (P < 5 × 10−6) were clumped (R2 < 0.001) within 10,000 kb. The inverse-variance weighted method was the primary analysis; Mendelian randomization-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode analyses were complementary methods. Heterogeneity, directional horizontal pleiotropy, leave-one-out, and Steiger directionality analyses were performed. Nineteen single nucleotide polymorphisms were retained; F statistics ranged from 21.08 to 288.04. Genetic liability to GDM was associated with higher odds of the outcome in the inverse-variance weighted analysis (odds ratio [OR], 1.50; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.08–2.09; P = .0165). The weighted median (OR, 1.76; 95% CI, 1.09–2.84; P = .0216) and weighted mode estimates (OR, 1.98; 95% CI, 1.21–3.26; P = .0148) were directionally consistent. There was no statistical evidence of heterogeneity or directional horizontal pleiotropy. The Steiger test supported the direction from GDM to the outcome (P = 1.30 × 10−11).

1. Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a frequent metabolic complication of pregnancy, and its implications may extend beyond childbirth.[1]

Previous observational studies have reported lower rates of exclusive breastfeeding and a greater likelihood of formula supplementation among women with GDM.[2,3] These findings should be interpreted cautiously because maternal adiposity before pregnancy, obstetric events, perinatal care, and socioeconomic circumstances may influence both GDM and breastfeeding outcomes. In addition, metabolomic studies have described altered lipid profiles and differences in human milk oligosaccharides in women with GDM.[4,5] Whether these changes contribute directly to lactation-related disorders, however, remains uncertain.

Recent reviews and trials have also underscored the heterogeneity of GDM phenotypes, management strategies, and longer-term maternal cardiometabolic outcomes.[6–9] Mendelian randomization (MR) uses inherited genetic variation as instrumental variables and can reduce, but not eliminate, confounding and reverse causation when its assumptions are met. We used this approach to examine whether genetic susceptibility to GDM is associated with the FinnGen phenotype “other disorders of breast and lactation associated with childbirth.”

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

This study used a 2-sample MR design. Valid causal inference requires that the selected genetic variants are associated with GDM, are independent of factors that confound the GDM-outcome association, and affect the outcome only through GDM. The study workflow is shown in Figure 1.

2.2. Data sources

Genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics were accessed through the Integrative Epidemiology Unit OpenGWAS resource. The exposure was gestational diabetes (dataset identifier: finn-b-GEST_DIABETES; 5687 cases and 117,892 controls). The outcome was other disorders of the breast and lactation associated with childbirth (dataset identifier: finn-b-O15_BREAST_LACT_OTHER_DIS; 214 cases and 119,155 controls). Both datasets were derived from Finnish biobank-based GWAS resources.[10] No individual-level data were accessed.

Because the exposure and outcome were obtained from related Finnish biobank-based resources, participant overlap could not be excluded. This possibility was considered in the interpretation of the results.

2.3. Instrument selection and harmonization

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with GDM at P < 5 × 10−6 were selected as instruments. Variants were clumped using R2 < 0.001 within a 10,000-kb window. Outcome associations were extracted without proxy SNPs. Exposure and outcome data were harmonized using action = 2; palindromic SNPs with ambiguous allele frequencies were excluded. Instrument strength was assessed using F statistics calculated as beta2/standard error (SE)2; F statistics > 10 were considered to indicate a low risk of weak-instrument bias.

2.4. Statistical analysis and directionality

The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method was the primary estimator. MR-Egger regression, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode analyses were performed as complementary estimators. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran Q statistic, and directional horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using the MR-Egger intercept. Leave-one-out analysis assessed whether exclusion of an individual SNP materially changed the IVW estimate. SNP-specific forest, funnel, and scatter plots were generated for visual inspection.

For directionality, SNP–trait correlations for the binary exposure and outcome were calculated from log odds ratios (ORs), effect allele frequencies, and case-control sample sizes. The Steiger test was performed using mr steiger 2. A higher variance explained in GDM than in the outcome, together with a small Steiger P value, was interpreted as support for the GDM-to-outcome direction.

All analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.6.3).

3. Results

3.1. Instrument characteristics

Nineteen SNPs were included after linkage disequilibrium clumping and harmonization. All retained variants had F statistics > 10, ranging from 21.08 to 288.04 (mean, 41.92).

3.2. Primary MR findings

In the primary IVW analysis, genetically predicted liability to GDM was associated with higher odds of other disorders of the breast and lactation associated with childbirth (beta, 0.406; SE, 0.169; OR, 1.50; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.08–2.09; P = .0165). The weighted median estimate (OR, 1.76; 95% CI, 1.09–2.84; P = .0216) and weighted mode estimate (OR, 1.98; 95% CI, 1.21–3.26; P = .0148) were consistent in direction. The MR-Egger and simple mode estimates were also positive but less precise and did not reach statistical significance (Table 1 and Fig. 2).

3.3. Sensitivity analyses

There was no statistical evidence of heterogeneity according to either the IVW analysis (Q = 17.95, df = 18, P = .459, I2 = 0.00%) or MR-Egger regression (Q = 17.70, df = 17, P = .408, I2 = 3.93%). The MR-Egger intercept did not provide statistical evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy (intercept, −0.040; SE, 0.081; P = .628). In the leave-one-out analysis, removal of any single SNP did not materially alter the overall IVW estimate (Fig. 3). The funnel plot was broadly symmetrical, although visual inspection alone cannot exclude horizontal pleiotropy (Fig. 4).

4. Discussion

This 2-sample MR analysis found that genetic liability to GDM was associated with higher odds of the composite FinnGen phenotype of other disorders of breast and lactation associated with childbirth. The IVW estimate was supported directionally by the weighted median and weighted mode analyses; MR-Egger and simple mode estimates were less precise. These findings should not be interpreted as proof that GDM directly causes any specific postpartum breast condition.

Metabolic perturbations in GDM, including altered lipid profiles and differences in human milk oligosaccharides, may help frame possible biological pathways.[4,5] Clinical studies in women with GDM have also examined breastfeeding outcomes and postpartum glucose status, although these associations may reflect obstetric, metabolic, and social-care factors in addition to GDM itself.[2,3,11–13] However, the outcome is a registry-based composite phenotype and cannot distinguish mastitis, delayed lactogenesis, low milk supply, or difficulties that are primarily related to clinical and social care.

This study has several strengths. It used a genetic design that is less susceptible to reverse causation than conventional observational analyses. All 19 instruments had F statistics > 10. In addition, the primary estimate was evaluated using several complementary MR estimators, and the sensitivity analyses did not identify statistical evidence of substantial heterogeneity, directional horizontal pleiotropy, or undue influence of a single SNP. These findings improve the internal consistency of the analysis but do not verify every MR assumption.

This study has several limitations. The genetic instruments were selected at P < 5 × 10−6, rather than at the conventional genome-wide significance level. Although all instruments had acceptable strength and the sensitivity analyses did not indicate substantial pleiotropy, bias arising from the relatively relaxed selection threshold cannot be entirely dismissed. In addition, the exposure and outcome GWAS were obtained from Finnish biobank populations. Some degree of sample overlap may therefore have occurred, and caution is needed when applying the findings to populations with different ancestral backgrounds. The outcome was defined using a broad registry category and included relatively few cases. Consequently, the estimates had limited precision, and specific breast or lactation disorders could not be examined separately. Because only summary statistics were available, analyses stratified by parity, body mass index, delivery mode, glucose- lowering treatment, medication use, or breastfeeding support were not possible. Variation in the diagnostic criteria and phenotype definitions used for GDM may further influence the interpretation of the results.[14] Finally, the estimates represent the effect of genetically proxied susceptibility to GDM and should not be interpreted as the effect of a clinical diagnosis or a particular treatment.

Replication in independent and preferably nonoverlapping datasets, using clinically specific lactation outcomes, is needed. Prospective and mechanistic studies will be required to determine whether metabolic health before or during pregnancy influences postpartum breast and lactation outcomes.

5. Conclusion

Genetic liability to GDM was associated with higher odds of a composite phenotype of breast and lactation disorders associated with childbirth. The directionality analysis supported the GDM-to-outcome direction; however, replication in independent datasets with specific clinical outcomes is needed before drawing implications for prevention or postpartum care.
