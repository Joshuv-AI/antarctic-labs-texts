Mirror Mean-Field Langevin Dynamics
Anming Gu, Juno Kim

Abstract
The mean-field Langevin dynamics (MFLD) minimizes an entropy-regularized nonlinear convex functional on the Wasserstein space over ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , and has gained attention recently as a model for the gradient descent dynamics of interacting particle systems such as infinite-width two-layer neural networks. However, many problems of interest have constrained domains, which are not solved by existing mean-field algorithms due to the global diffusion term. We study the optimization of probability measures constrained to a convex subset of ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} by proposing the mirror mean-field Langevin dynamics (MMFLD), an extension of MFLD to the mirror Langevin framework. We obtain linear convergence guarantees for the continuous MMFLD via a uniform log-Sobolev inequality, and uniform-in-time propagation of chaos results for its time- and particle-discretized counterpart.

Introduction

In this work, we study the problem of minimizing an entropy-regularized functional on the space of probability measures constrained to a convex subset
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\mathcal{L}(\mu):=F(\mu)+\lambda{\operatorname{Ent}}(\mu),

(1)

where
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})
,
F:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})\to\mathbb{R}
is a linearly convex functional, and
\operatorname{Ent}(\mu)=\int\log\frac{d\mu}{dx}d\mu
is the entropy of
\mu
. Such problems arise naturally in statistical inference and machine learning, such as when studying infinite-width limits of neural networks
[
MMN18
,
NWS22
,
SWON23
,
TS24
]
, tensor decomposition
[
CB18
]
, sparse spikes deconvolution
[
CB18
]
, density estimation
[
SWN23
]
and discrepancy minimization
[
GBR+12
,
SWN23
]
. Often, a nonconvex optimization problem of a particle system can be formulated as an instance of (
1
) by lifting to the space of measures.

In the simplest case, we wish to sample from a distribution
\mu_{*}\propto e^{-f/\lambda}
for a potential
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, which corresponds to (
1
) where
F(\mu)=\int fd\mu
is linear. When
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a classical approach is to employ a discretization of the Langevin dynamics

dX_{t}=-\nabla f(X_{t})dt+\sqrt{2\lambda}dB_{t}.

(2)

The trajectory
\mu_{t}=\operatorname{Law}(X_{t})
can be interpreted as the Wasserstein gradient flow minimizing the KL divergence with respect to
\mu_{*}

[
JKO98
]
; this remarkable connection has led to many fruitful developments in both sampling and optimization, see e.g.
[
Dal17
,
Wib18
,
Che23
]
for an exposition. The convergence of (
2
) in various metrics (Wasserstein, TV, KL,
\chi^{2}
, Rényi) has been extensively analyzed under strong log-concavity or functional inequalities such as log-Sobolev or Poincaré
inequalities
[
Wib18
,
Wib19
,
VW19
,
DM17
,
DM19
,
LZT21b
,
CEL+22
,
MHFH+23
]
.

In the constrained or ill-conditioned case, however, (
2
) cannot be applied as the diffusion term will cause mass to escape
\mathcal{X}
. This problem can be solved by imposing an alternative non-flat geometry on
\mathcal{X}
and running the mirror Langevin dynamics (MLD), the analogue of mirror descent for sampling
[
HKRC18
,
ZPFP20
]
. By introducing a barrier function
\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, the mirror map
\nabla\phi
induces an isometry between the primal space
\mathcal{X}
with the Hessian metric
\nabla^{2}\phi
, and the dual space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the metric
\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}
. This allows us to apply a transformed diffusion in the dual space:

X_{t}=\nabla\phi^{*}(Y_{t}),\quad dY_{t}=-\nabla f(X_{t})dt+\sqrt{2\lambda\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{t})}dB_{t}.

(3)

The convergence of (
3
) under a mirror Poincaré inequality have been established in
[
CLGL+20
]
and various discretization schemes have been analyzed in
[
ZPFP20
,
Jia21
,
AC21
,
LTVW22
]
. In particular,
[
AC21
]
proposed a discretized mirror-Langevin algorithm which guarantees vanishing bias under mild assumptions on the mirror map.

Returning to the general problem (
1
), when
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, this can be solved efficiently by running the mean-field Langevin dynamics (MFLD)
[
NWS22
,
Chi22
]
extending (
2
), given by the McKean-Vlasov process

dX_{t}=-\nabla\frac{\delta F(\mu_{t})}{\delta\mu}(X_{t})dt+\sqrt{2\lambda}dB_{t},

(4)

which is the Wasserstein gradient flow minimizing
\mathcal{L}
. The convergence of (
4
) and its time discretization has been established under a log-Sobolev inequality (LSI) in
[
NWS22
]
. Following works have studied
propagation of chaos

[
Szn91
]
, that is, the approximation error induced when
\mu_{t}
is replaced by a finite particle system. A dedicated line of work has obtained error bounds which converge to zero uniformly for all time as the number of particles
N\to\infty
by leveraging uniform LSI
[
CRW23
,
SNW23
,
SWN23
]
. Recent works have further shown that these errors can be made independent of the LSI constant
[
Nit24
,
CNZ24
,
NLK+25
]
.

The same analyses may be applied if
\mathcal{X}
is a complete Riemannian manifold without boundary, e.g. a hypersphere or torus. However, the problem for general
\mathcal{X}
has remained open. Nonetheless, many applications of MFLD require the domain of optimization to be (implicitly or explicitly) constrained, e.g., trajectory inference
[
CZHS22
,
GCG25
]
, computation of Wasserstein barycenters
[
Chi23
,
VC23
]
, computation of discrepancy measures
[
SWN23
]
or signal deconvolution
[
CB18
]
with bounded support, and optimization of neural networks
[
NWS22
,
NLK+25
]
with constrained parameters. A more detailed overview of related works is provided in Appendix
A
.

1.1
Contributions

We propose the
mirror mean-field Langevin dynamics
(MMFLD), which combines mirror Langevin dynamics (
3
) with mean-field Langevin dynamics (

) to solve the constrained distributional optimization problem (

The Euclidean norm on

. We use

\int fdx

\int fd\mu

2
Preliminaries

In order to develop our main object of interest, the mirror mean-field Langevin dynamics, we first provide discussion of the mirror Langevin dynamics and mean-field Langevin dyamics as a primer.

2.1
Mirror Langevin Dynamics

The mirror Langevin dynamics (MLD) has been extensively studied in the context of constrained sampling problems
[
ZPFP20
,
Jia21
,
LTVW22
]
. Suppose we want to sample from a probability distribution
\mu
supported on a convex set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We assume
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and has density
\mu\propto e^{-f/\lambda}
for some differentiable
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Certainly, one way is to run the Langevin dynamics (or algorithm) (
2
) and project onto
\mathcal{X}
. However, one undesirable behavior of this projection step is that this will necessarily put positive mass onto
\partial\mathcal{X}
; see the numerical experiments in Section
5
. Alternatively, we can enforce the distribution to stay in
\mathcal{X}
by changing the geometry of the underlying space.

Towards that end, let
\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a thrice-differentiable strictly convex function of Legendre type
[
Roc97
]
. We require
\|\nabla\phi(x)\|\to\infty
and
\nabla^{2}\phi(x)\to\infty
as
x
approaches
\partial\mathcal{X}
, as this ensures the diffusion remains inside the domain
\mathcal{X}
. We call
\nabla\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
the
mirror map
and
\mathcal{Y}=\nabla\phi(\mathcal{X})=\mathbb{R}^{d}
the
dual space
. Further, define
\phi^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
to be the Legendre dual (convex conjugate) of
\phi
, given as
\phi^{*}(y)=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\langle x,y\rangle-\phi(x)
.

Lemma 2.1

(Properties of
\phi
)
.

The following hold:

(i)

\nabla\phi(\mathcal{X})=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the gradient map
\nabla\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is bijective and
\nabla^{2}\phi(x)\succ 0
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

(ii)

\nabla\phi^{*}(y)=\arg\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\langle x,y\rangle-\phi(x)
.

(iii)

\nabla\phi^{*}=(\nabla\phi)^{-1}
, so
\nabla\phi(\nabla\phi^{*}(y))=y
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\nabla^{2}\phi(x)=\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(\nabla\phi(x))^{-1}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

The mirror Langevin dynamics (MLD) in primal space satisfies the SDE

\begin{cases}X_{t}&=\nabla\phi^{*}(Y_{t})\\
dY_{t}&=-\nabla f(X_{t})dt+\sqrt{2\lambda\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{t})}dB_{t},\end{cases}

(5)

and its density
\mu_{t}=\operatorname{Law}(X_{t})
satisfies the Fokker-Planck PDE

\frac{\partial\mu_{t}}{\partial t}=\lambda\nabla\cdot\left(\mu_{t}[\nabla^{2}\phi]^{-1}\nabla\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\mu}\right).

Observe that this is equivalent to the Riemmanian Langevin dynamics in the primal space with metric given by the Hessian
\nabla^{2}\phi
, or in the dual space with metric
\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}
, as
(\mathcal{X},\nabla^{2}\phi)
is isometric to
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\nabla^{2}\phi^{*})
. This ensures we obtain the same the convergence guarantees in both the primal and dual spaces.

For the well-posedness of (
5
), we will require standard Lipschitz conditions on
f
, e.g. see
[
Øks10
]
. Additionally, we require that
\|[\nabla^{2}\phi(x)]^{1/2}-[\nabla^{2}\phi(x^{\prime})]^{1/2}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq O(\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{2})
for every
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
, see e.g.
[
ZPFP20
]
.
1
1

1

In the sequel, we will also implicitly assume this Frobenius norm bound for the mirror mean-field Langevin dynamics as well.

Using Itô’s formula, we can obtain a representation of (
5
) purely in terms of
X_{t}
; however, it depends on the third-derivative of
\phi
, see
[
Che23
, Exercise 10.1]
. Alternatively, we can reformulate the MLD entirely in the dual space as the following SDE:

dY_{t}=-\nabla f(\nabla\phi^{*}(y))dt+\sqrt{2\lambda[\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(Y_{t})]^{-1}}dB_{t}.

The distribution
\tilde{\mu}_{t}=\operatorname{Law}(Y_{t})
is the pushforward of
\mu_{t}
under the mirror map:
\tilde{\mu}_{t}=(\nabla\phi)_{\sharp}\mu_{t}
.

To analyze convergence, we start by introducing the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence and the relative Fisher information. Denote the KL divergence as

\operatorname{KL}(\mu\,||\,\nu):=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\frac{\mu}{\nu}\log\frac{\mu}{\nu}\right]=\int\mu\log\frac{\mu}{\nu}

and the relative Fisher information (modified by the mirror map) as

\operatorname{FI}(\mu\,||\,\nu):=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left\langle\nabla\log\frac{\mu}{\nu},[\nabla^{2}(\phi)]^{-1}\nabla\log\frac{\mu}{\nu}\right\rangle.

(6)

We will require the stationary distribution
\mu_{*}
to satisfy a mirror log-Sobolev inequality (MLSI):

Assumption 1

(Mirror log-Sobolev inequality)
.

There exists a constant
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}>0
such that
\mu_{*}
satisfies a mirror log-Sobolev inequality with constant
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}
, that is, for every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})
, it holds that
\operatorname{KL}(\mu\,||\,\mu_{*})\leq\frac{1}{2C_{\mathsf{LSI}}}\operatorname{FI}(\mu\,||\,\mu_{*})
.

Under this assumption, we can prove the following convergence guarantee:

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\{\mu_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
denote the evolution of (
5
) and
\mu_{*}
denote its stationary distribution. Under Assumption
1
, for
t\geq 0
, it holds that

\operatorname{KL}(\mu_{t}\,||\,\mu_{*})\leq e^{-2C_{\mathsf{LSI}}\lambda t}{\operatorname{KL}(\mu_{0}\,||\,\mu_{*})}.

A proof is given in
[
Jia21
]
, but for completeness, we provide a short proof in Appendix
B.1
. Note that
[
CLGL+20
]
proved convergence in
\chi^{2}
-divergence under a mirror Poincaré inequality, which is implied from MLD via the Brascamp-Lieb inequality
[
BL76
]
. It is also mentioned the stronger mirror LSI may not hold in general
[
OV00
]
. However, we must work with the mirror LSI in the mean-field setting; although it is conjectured the convergence of (unconstrained) mean-field Langevin dynamics in
\chi^{2}
-divergence should hold under a Poincaré inequality, this is as of yet unknown, see for example the discussion in the conclusion of
[
NWS22
]
.

2.2
Mean-Field Langevin Dynamics

Suppose we want to minimize the following entropy-regularized functional:

\mathcal{L}(\mu):=F(\mu)+\lambda{\operatorname{Ent}}(\mu),

(7)

where
F:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
is a linearly convex functional (Definition
2
),
\operatorname{Ent}(\mu):=\int\log\frac{d\mu}{dx}d\mu
is the entropy of
\mu
, and
\lambda>0
is the temperature parameter. We start by defining the first variation of
F
.

Definition 1

(First variation)
.

We say
F:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
admits a first variation at
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if there exists a continuous function
\frac{\delta F(\mu)}{\delta\mu}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for any
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have

\frac{dF(\mu+\epsilon(\mu^{\prime}-\mu))}{d\epsilon}\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}=\int\frac{\delta F(\mu)}{\delta\mu}(\mu^{\prime}-\mu)dx.

(8)

If the first variation of
F
exists, it is unique up to a constant.

To solve (
7
), we consider the mean-field Langevin dynamics (MFLD):

dX_{t}=-\nabla\frac{\delta F(\mu_{t})}{\delta\mu}(X_{t})dt+\sqrt{2\lambda}dB_{t},

(9)

a McKean-Vlasov process where
\mu_{t}=\operatorname{Law}(X_{t})
and
B_{t}
is the standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. It follows that
\mu_{t}
solves the nonlinear Fokker-Planck PDE

\frac{\partial\mu_{t}}{\partial t}=\nabla\cdot\left(\mu_{t}\nabla\frac{\delta F(\mu_{t})}{\delta\mu}\right)+\lambda\mu_{t}=\lambda\nabla\cdot\left(\mu_{t}\nabla\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\hat{\mu}_{t}}\right).

(10)

To ensure existence and convergence, the following assumptions are required.

Assumption 2

(Lipschitz and smoothness)
.

There exist constants
M_{1},M_{2}>0
such that for any
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds that
\left\|\nabla\frac{\delta F(\mu)}{\delta\mu}(x)\right\|_{2}\leq M_{1}
and

\left\|\nabla\frac{\delta F(\mu)}{\delta\mu}(x)-\nabla\frac{\delta F(\mu^{\prime})}{\delta\mu}(x^{\prime})\right\|_{2}\leq M_{2}(W_{2}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})+\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{2}).

Here
W_{2}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
is the
2
-Wasserstein distance between measures
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})
,

W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\mu^{\prime}):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{2}d\pi(x,x^{\prime}),

where
\Pi(\mu,\mu^{\prime}):=\{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})\mid(P_{x})_{\sharp}\pi=\mu,(P_{y})_{\sharp}\pi=\mu^{\prime})\}
is the set of transport plans, and
P_{x}(x,y):=x
,
P_{y}(x,y):=y
are the projections onto the first and second coordinates, respectively.

Definition 2
.

We say a functional
F:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
is linearly convex if for every
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\alpha\in[0,1]
, it holds that
F(\alpha\mu+(1-\alpha)\nu)\leq\alpha F(\mu)+(1-\alpha)F(\nu)
.

Assumption 3
.

F
is linearly convex and (
7
) admits a minimizer
\mu_{*}
.

For example, Assumption
3
holds if
F
is of the form
\sum_{i}\ell_{i}(\int f_{i}d\mu)
for convex losses
\ell_{i}
. Then the following can be shown:

Theorem 2.3
.

Under Assumptions
2
and
3
, the minimizer
\mu_{*}
of (
7
) is unique and its density satisfies
\mu_{*}\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{\delta F(\mu_{*})}{\delta\mu}\right)
.

The convergence analysis of the MFLD relies on the proximal Gibbs distribution
[
NWS22
,
Chi22
]
.

Definition 3

(Proximal Gibbs distribution)
.

For each
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we define
\hat{\mu}
to be the Gibbs distribution such that
\hat{\mu}\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{\delta F(\mu)}{\delta\mu}\right)
.

We briefly remark that for the
linear
Langevin dynamics, the proximal Gibbs distribution coincides with the stationary distribution
\mu_{*}
.

Next, we require the following
uniform
log-Sobolev inequality. For many cases of interest, the LSI can be obtained via the Bakry-Émery criterion
[
BE85
]
, Holley-Stroock perturbation
[
HS87
]
, or from Lipschitz perturbations due to Aida-Shigekawa
[
AS94
]
; see also
[
SWN23
]
.

Assumption 4

(Uniform LSI)
.

Suppose there exists a constant
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}>0
such that for every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the proximal Gibbs distribution
\hat{\mu}
satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with constant
C_{\mathsf{LSI}}
, that is,
\operatorname{KL}(\mu\,||\,\hat{\mu})\leq\frac{1}{2C_{\mathsf{LSI}}}\operatorname{FI}(\mu\,||\,\hat{\mu})
.

Here, the relative Fisher information is as in (
6
), except without the modifier
\nabla^{2}(\phi)^{-1}
.
[
NWS22
,
Chi22
]
used the entropy sandwich inequality (see Lemma
B.1
) to prove the following:

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the evolution described by (
10
). Under Assumptions
2
-
4
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mu_{t})-\mathcal{L}(\mu_{*})\leq e^{-2C_{\mathsf{LSI}}\lambda t}(\mathcal{L}(\mu_{0})-\mathcal{L}(\mu_{*})).

The discretization of MFLD has also been studied in
[
NWS22
,
SWN23
,
Nit24
]
; we refer to these works for a detailed analysis.

3
Mirror Mean-Field Langevin Dynamics

Now with the necessary technical background, we introduce our main object of study. Suppose we want to minimize (
7
) subject to the additional constraint that
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})
for a convex set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\underset{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})}{\arg\min}\,\mathcal{L}(\mu):=F(\mu)+\lambda{\operatorname{Ent}(\mu)},

(11)

where the domain of
F
and its first-variation are accordingly modified from (
7
), e.g., now we have
F:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})\to\mathbb{R}
, and its first variation at
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})
is a function
\frac{\delta F(\mu)}{\delta\mu}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. As we remarked in the introduction, restriction to a convex domain is a natural and frequently studied constraint in various optimization problems
[
CZHS22
,
Chi23
,
VC23
,
GCG25
]
.

For this problem, we introduce the
mirror mean-field Langevin dynamics
(MMFLD), which is defined as the following (primal) SDE over
\mathcal{X}
:

\begin{cases}X_{t}&=\nabla\phi^{*}(Y_{t})\\
dY_{t}&=-\nabla\frac{\delta F(\mu_{t})}{\delta\mu}(X_{t})dt+\sqrt{2\lambda\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{t})}dB_{t},\end{cases}

(12)

where
\mu_{t}=\operatorname{Law}(X_{t})
. Similar to the mirror Langevin and mean-field Langevin settings, it is easy to check that (
13
) corresponds to the following Fokker-Planck PDE:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mu_{t}=\lambda\nabla\cdot\left(\mu_{t}[\nabla^{2}\phi]^{-1}\nabla\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\hat{\mu}_{t}}\right).

(13)

Next, we introduce the following notions of local and dual norms.

Definition 4

(Local and dual norms)
.

Given a
C^{2}
strictly convex function
\phi
, the local norm at
x\in\operatorname{int}\operatorname{dom}\phi
with respect to
\phi
is defined as
\|u\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(x)}=\langle u,\nabla^{2}\phi(x)u\rangle^{1/2}
. The local dual norm is similarly defined as
\|u\|_{[\nabla^{2}\phi(x)]^{-1}}=\langle u,[\nabla^{2}\phi(x)]^{-1}u\rangle^{1/2}
.

To ensure existence and convergence, we will modify Assumption
2
to the following. Note that these correspond to the mean-field generalization of Assumptions 3, 6 from
[
Jia21
]
.

Assumption 5

(Relative Lipschitz and smoothness)
.

The following hold.

(i)

For all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})
, it holds that
\frac{\delta F(\mu)}{\delta\mu}
is differentiable with

\left\|\nabla\frac{\delta F(\mu)}{\delta\mu}(x)\right\|_{[\nabla^{2}\phi(x)]^{-1}}\leq M_{1}.

(ii)

For all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\frac{\delta F(\mu)}{\delta\mu}(x)-\nabla\frac{\delta F(\mu^{\prime})}{\delta\mu}(x^{\prime})\right\|_{[\nabla^{2}\phi(x^{\prime})]^{-1}}\leq M_{2}(W_{2,\phi}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})+\|\nabla\phi(x)-\nabla\phi(x^{\prime})\|_{[\nabla^{2}\phi(x^{\prime})]^{-1}}).

Here, we use the modified Wasserstein distance

W_{2,\phi}^{2}(\mu,\mu^{\prime}):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}\int\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{[\nabla^{2}\phi(x^{\prime})]^{-1}}^{2}d\pi(x,x^{\prime}).

It is then straightforward to see that (
11
) admits a unique minimizer; see Appendix
B.2
for a proof.

Theorem 3.1
.

Under Assumptions
3
and
5
, (
11
) is well-posed and admits a unique minimizer
\mu_{*}
which satisfies
\mu_{*}\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{\delta F(\mu_{*})}{\delta\mu}\right)
.

Recall that convergence analysis of the mean-field Langevin dynamics utilizes the entropy sandwich inequality
[
NWS22
,
Chi22
]
. We will also require this. Remarkably, the statement will hold exactly in the constrained setting as well–see Lemma
B.1
.

Now we can prove the convergence of the mirror mean-field Langevin dynamics in continuous time. The proof, which mirrors that of ordinary MFLD
[
NWS22
]
, is provided in Appendix
B.4
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\{\mu_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be the evolution described by (
13
). Under Assumptions
4

4.1
Obtaining the Discretized MMFLD

We now derive the time- and space- (i.e. particle-) discretized version of MMFLD by motivating the empirical dynamics from an alternative objective. Indeed, the KL term in (

\mu^{(N)}\in\mathcal{P}^{(N)}
be a distribution of
N

Numerical Experiments

Figure 1
:
(a)-(c) MMFLD v.s. projected MFLD for optimizing a nonlinear mean-matching objective over the unit simplex; (d)-(f) with an additional boundary potential preventing mass accumulation.

In this section, we conduct a simple experiment demonstrating the utility of (discretized) MMFLD. We consider optimization on the unit simplex
\Delta_{d}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,|\,\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}=1,\;x_{i}\geq 0\}
with
d=3
. A natural choice for the mirror map is the entropy function
\phi(x)=\sum_{i}x_{i}\log x_{i}
, whose dual is given by
\phi^{*}(y)=\log\sum_{i}\exp y_{i}
; see
[
BT03
]
for details. We aim to minimize the nonlinear functional

\displaystyle F(\mu)=\left\lVert\int_{\Delta_{3}}xd\mu(x)-q\right\rVert^{2}+\beta\int_{\Delta_{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\log\frac{1}{x_{i}}d\mu(x).

The first term is a mean-matching score for a fixed target
q\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, while the second term is a potential barrier preventing collapse towards the boundary
\partial\Delta_{3}
. As a baseline, we also implement projected MFLD, where particles are projected back onto the simplex after each update (
9
). The diffusion step of MMFLD is simulated in one step, resulting in similar runtimes. Both algorithms are run using
50
K particles with
\eta=3\times 10^{-3}
,
\lambda=0.1
.

Figure
1
(a)-(c) show the loss curves and final particle distribution of both algorithms when
\beta=0
. In particular, MFLD exhibits accumulation of mass along the boundary characteristic of projection methods (Figure
1
b), which can lead to excess error or overfitting. To avoid this trivial failure case, we also consider adding a small barrier preventing convergence to the boundary by setting
\beta=10^{-4}
(Figure
1
d-f). We observe a drastic effect on projected MFLD, as particles are strongly repelled from the boundary whenever they come close; in contrast, MMFLD maintains a more even distribution as the dual geometry already ensures the particles are well-behaved near the boundary. In both experiments, MFLD initially converges faster but MMFLD is able to achieve significantly smaller loss, thus demonstrating higher stability and optimality for constrained optimization.

6
Conclusion

We introduced the mirror mean-field Langevin dynamics to solve distributional optimization problems constrained to a convex subset
\mathcal{X}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Moreover, we obtained linear convergence rates for the continuous-time flow and also proved uniform-in-time propagation of chaos guarantees for the time- and particle-discretized setting. An important direction for future work is to generalize to the mirror Poincaré setting as in
[
CLGL+20
]
since it is more natural from a geometric viewpoint
[
Che23
]
; this will require studying the mean-field Langevin dynamics under a Poincaré inequality as well
[
NWS22
]
.

Appendix A
Related Work

MLD and constrained sampling.

For the problem of constrained sampling, the mirrored Langevin dynamics was first proposed by
[
HKRC18
]
. Then,
[
ZPFP20
]
introduced the more general mirror Langevin diffusion with Hessian-dependent covariance and its Monte Carlo discretization, the mirror Langevin algorithm (MLA). In continuous time,
[
CLGL+20
]
show exponential convergence of MLD under a mirror Poincaré inequality relative to the modified metric. In particular, this implies convergence of the Newton-Langevin diffusion when the mirror map equals the (strictly convex) potential
f
.

The convergence of the discretized algorithm is more difficult to analyze compared to the ordinary Langevin dynamics.
[
ZPFP20
]
showed that the MLA iterates contract to a Wasserstein ball around the target density, but with a potentially non-vanishing bias.
[
AC21
]
proposed a different discretization of MLD which achieves vanishing bias, but assumed an exact computation of the Hessian Riemannian throughout the diffusion process.
On the other hand,
[
LTVW22
]
showed vanishing bias of MLA under more restrictive assumptions on the mirror map based on
[
LWME19
,
LZT21a
]
; and
[
GV22
]
utilized an additional second-order self-concordance assumption (cf. Assumption
7
) and extensive Riemannian geometry for an exact discretization analysis on Hessian manifolds. Furthermore, Metropolis-adjusted (high accuracy) variants of MLA have been proposed to
[
SWW24a
,
SWW24b
]
obtain algorithms whose stationary distributions are unbiased.

MFLD and propagation of chaos.

Initial mean-field analyses of two-layer neural networks were studied independently by several groups
[
MMN18
,
CB18
,
SS20
,
HRÅÅS21
]
, showing either weak convergence or linear convergence under restrictive assumptions, e.g. the mean-field interaction is small or the optimization is over-regularized
[
EGZ19
]
. The linear convergence of the MFLD (under mild conditions) was first shown in
[
NWS22
,
Chi22
]
, leveraging the log-Sobolev inequality of the proximal Gibbs measure. Whereas
[
NWS22
]
studied the time-discretization and offered a primal-dual analysis,
[
Chi22
]
studied the annealed dynamics where
\lambda_{t}\to 0
in (
4
).

Following works focused on proving propagation of chaos bounds for the finite particle dynamics, with the noisy gradient descent of finite-width neural networks as a prominent application. While earlier arguments indicated the possibility of error blowing up exponentially in time
[
MMN18
]
, uniform-in-time guarantees were obtained in the case of super-quadratic regularization by
[
SNW23
]
and for more natural quadratic regularization
[
CRW23
,
SWN23
,
KZC+24
]
via a uniform-in-
N
LSI
[
Wan24
,
CNZ24
]
. Moreover, this error was shown to be independent of the LSI constant in
[
Nit24
]
and the corresponding convergence analysis was further refined in
[
CNZ24
,
NLK+25
]
, respectively relaxing quadratic regularization to strongly convex regularization and improving the propagation of chaos results using a directional LSI. Also, improved sample complexity results whave been established for certain classification problems
[
SWON23
,
MHWE25
]
.

\displaystyle\qquad-\nabla\cdot\left(\nu_{t}\int\nu_{0|t}\pi_{0}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda\nabla\cdot\left(\nu_{0|t}\int G_{0}(\mathbf{x})\nabla\nu_{t}+\nu_{t}G_{0}(\mathbf{x})N\nabla F(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}_{0})\right)-\nabla\cdot\left(\nu_{t}\int\nu_{0|t}\pi_{0}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda\nabla\cdot\left(\nu_{0|t}\int\left(\nu_{t}G_{0}(\mathbf{x})\nabla\log\frac{\nu_{t}}{\mu_{*}^{(N)}}(\mathbf{x})\right)d\mathbf{x}_{0}\right)-\nabla\cdot\left(\nu_{t}\int\nu_{0|t}\pi_{0}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}_{0}\right).

(19)

Now we have

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{L}^{(N)}}{dt}(\nu_{t})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}^{(N)}(\nu_{t})}{\delta\mu^{(N)}}(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial\nu_{t}}{\partial t}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\lambda\int\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}^{(N)}(\nu_{t})}{\delta\mu^{(N)}}(\mathbf{x})\nabla\cdot\left(\nu_{0|t}\int\nu_{t}G_{0}(\mathbf{x})\nabla\log\frac{\nu_{t}}{\mu_{*}^{(N)}}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}_{0}\right)d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\qquad-\int\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}^{(N)}(\nu_{t})}{\delta\mu^{(N)}}(\mathbf{x})\nabla\cdot\left(\nu_{t}\int\nu_{0|t}\pi_{0}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}_{0}\right)d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=-\lambda\int\nu_{0|t}\int\left\langle\nabla\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}^{(N)}(\nu_{t})}{\delta\mu^{(N)}},\nu_{t}G_{0}\nabla\log\frac{\nu_{t}}{\mu_{*}^{(N)}}\right\rangle d\mathbf{x}_{0}d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\qquad+\int\nu_{t}\left\langle\nabla\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}^{(N)}(\nu_{t})}{\delta\mu^{(N)}},\int\nu_{0|t}\pi_{0}d\mathbf{x}_{0}\right\rangle d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=-\lambda^{2}\int\nu_{0|t}\int\left\langle\nabla\log\frac{\nu_{t}}{\mu_{*}^{(N)}},\nu_{t}G_{0}\nabla\log\frac{\nu_{t}}{\mu_{*}^{(N)}}\right\rangle d\mathbf{x}_{0}d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\qquad+\lambda\int\nu_{t}\left\langle\nabla\log\frac{\nu_{t}}{\mu_{*}^{(N)}},\int\nu_{0|t}\pi_{0}d\mathbf{x}_{0}\right\rangle d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=-\lambda^{2}\underset{\nu_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\bigg\|\nabla\log\frac{\nu_{t}}{\mu_{*}^{(N)}}\bigg\|_{G}^{2}+\lambda\underset{\nu_{0t}}{\mathbb{E}}\left\langle\pi,\nabla\log\frac{\nu_{t}}{\mu_{*}^{(N)}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq-\lambda^{2}\underset{\nu_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\bigg\|\nabla\log\frac{\nu_{t}}{\mu_{*}^{(N)}}\bigg\|_{[\nabla^{2}\phi]^{-1}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\underset{\nu_{0t}}{\mathbb{E}}\|\pi\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi}^{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\underset{\nu_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\nabla\log\frac{\nu_{t}}{\mu_{*}^{(N)}}\right\|^{2}_{[\nabla^{2}\phi]^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq-\lambda^{2}C_{\mathsf{LSI}}{\operatorname{KL}(\nu_{t}\,||\,\mu_{*}^{(N)})}+\frac{1}{2}\underset{\nu_{0t}}{\mathbb{E}}\|\pi\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}\phi}

\displaystyle\leq-\lambda C_{\mathsf{LSI}}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(N)}(\nu_{t})-\mathcal{L}^{(N)}(\mu_{*}^{(N)})\right)+\frac{1}{2}\underset{\nu_{0t}}{\mathbb{E}}\|\pi\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}\phi},

(20)

using (
19
), integration by parts, Young’s inequality, and Lemma
B.1
.

Now using Assumptions
5
,
7
, and (
16
), for

\displaystyle M=\exp\left(\frac{2c_{1}D}{\sqrt{c_{2}}}\right),\quad\xi_{t}=\frac{1-\exp(-\frac{1}{16c_{1}^{2}t})}{(1-c_{1}(tM_{1}+2\sqrt{td}))^{2}}+\exp\left(-\frac{1}{16c_{1}^{2}t}+\frac{2c_{1}D}{\sqrt{c_{2}}}\right),

we may bound

\displaystyle\underset{\nu_{0t}}{\mathbb{E}}\|\pi\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}\phi}

\displaystyle=\underset{(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{t})\sim\nu_{0t}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\pi^{i}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i})}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq M_{2}^{2}\underset{(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{t})\sim\nu_{0t}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\nabla\phi(\mathbf{x}_{0}^{i})-\nabla\phi(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i})\|_{[\nabla^{2}\phi(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i})]^{-1}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq M_{2}^{2}\underset{(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{t})\sim\nu_{0t}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\nabla\frac{\delta F(\mu_{\mathbf{x}_{0}})}{\delta\mu}(\mathbf{x}_{0}^{i})-\nabla\frac{\delta F(\mu_{\mathbf{x}_{t}})}{\delta\mu}(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i})\right\|_{[\nabla^{2}\phi(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i})]^{-1}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq M_{2}^{4}\underset{(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{t})\sim\nu_{0t}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[NW_{2,\phi}^{2}(\mu_{\mathbf{x}_{0}},\mu_{\mathbf{x}_{t}})+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{0}^{i}-\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i}\right\|_{[\nabla^{2}\phi(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i})]^{-1}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2M_{2}^{4}\underset{(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{t})\sim\nu_{0t}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{0}^{i}-\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i}\right\|_{[\nabla^{2}\phi(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i})]^{-1}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2M_{2}^{4}\underset{(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{t})\sim\nu_{0t}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|-t\nabla\frac{\delta F(\mu_{\mathbf{x}_{0}})}{\delta\mu}(\mathbf{x}_{0}^{i})+\!\sqrt{2\lambda}\int_{0}^{t}[\nabla^{2}\phi(\mathbf{x}_{s}^{i})]^{1/2}dB_{s}\right\|_{[\nabla^{2}\phi(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{i})]^{-1}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 4Nt^{2}M_{1}^{2}M_{2}^{4}\xi_{t}+8\lambda tdNM_{2}^{4}M

\displaystyle=4NM_{2}^{4}(t^{2}M_{1}^{2}\xi_{t}+2\lambda tdM).

(21)

Here we have used Assumption
5
, Itô’s isometry, Jensen’s inequality, and Lemma
B.2
. Note that
\xi_{t}\leq M
deterministically, so for
t\in[0,\eta]
, we have
\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{0t}}\|\pi\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi}^{2}\leq N\delta_{\eta}
, where
\delta_{\eta}:=2\eta M_{2}^{4}M(\eta M_{1}^{2}+2\lambda d)
. By combining (
20
) and (
21
), we see that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathcal{L}^{(N)}(\nu_{t})-\mathcal{L}^{(N)}(\mu_{*}^{(N)})-\frac{N\delta_{\eta}}{2C_{\mathsf{LSI}}\lambda}\right)\leq-C_{\mathsf{LSI}}\lambda\left(\mathcal{L}^{(N)}(\nu_{t})-\mathcal{L}^{(N)}(\mu_{*}^{(N)})--\frac{N\delta_{\eta}}{2C_{\mathsf{LSI}}\lambda}\right).

Noting that
\nu_{\eta}=\mu_{k+1}^{(N)}
and
\nu_{0}=\mu_{k}^{(N)}
, Grönwall’s inequality yields

\mathcal{L}^{(N)}(\nu_{k+1})-\mathcal{L}^{(N)}(\mu_{*}^{(N)})-\frac{N\delta_{\eta}}{2C_{\mathsf{LSI}}\lambda}\leq\exp\left(-C_{\mathsf{LSI}}\lambda\eta\right)\left(\mathcal{L}^{(N)}(\nu_{k})-\mathcal{L}^{(N)}(\mu_{*}^{(N)})-\frac{N\delta_{\eta}}{2C_{\mathsf{LSI}}\lambda}\right).

Iterating yields

\mathcal{L}^{(N)}(\nu_{k})-\mathcal{L}^{(N)}(\mu_{*}^{(N)})-\frac{N\delta_{\eta}}{2C_{\mathsf{LSI}}\lambda}\leq\exp\left(-C_{\mathsf{LSI}}\lambda\eta k\right)\left(\mathcal{L}^{(N)}(\nu_{0})-\mathcal{L}^{(N)}(\mu_{*}^{(N)})-\frac{N\delta_{\eta}}{2C_{\mathsf{LSI}}\lambda}\right),

from which the claim follows.
∎

Lemma B.2
.

Under Assumptions
5
and
7
, and consider the updates
X_{t}^{i}=\nabla\phi^{*}(Y_{t}^{i})
following (
16
), it holds

\frac{1}{M}[\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{t}^{i})]^{-1}\preceq[\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{t}^{i})]^{-1}\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{0}^{i})[\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{t}^{i})]\preceq M[\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{t}^{i})]^{-1}

in expectation with respect to the Brownian motion for

M=\frac{1-\exp(-\frac{1}{16c_{1}^{2}t})}{(1-c_{1}(tM_{1}+2\sqrt{td}))^{2}}+\exp\left(-\frac{1}{16c_{1}^{2}t}+\frac{2c_{1}D}{\sqrt{c_{2}}}\right)

if
t\leq\min\left\{\frac{1}{2c_{1}M_{1}},\frac{1}{16c_{1}^{2}d}\right\}
, and deterministically bounded by
M=\exp\left(\frac{2c_{1}D}{\sqrt{c_{2}}}\right)
. We use the convention
M=1
when
c_{1}=0
and
D=\infty
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.1
, it implies from
[
NN94
]
that for
\|Y_{t}^{i}-Y_{0}^{i}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(Y_{0}^{i})}\leq\frac{1}{c_{1}}
, it holds

(1-c_{1}\|Y_{t}^{i}-Y_{0}^{i}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(Y_{0}^{i})})^{2}\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(Y_{0}^{i})\preceq\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(Y_{t}^{i})\preceq\frac{1}{(1-c_{1}\|Y_{t}^{i}-Y_{0}^{i}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(Y_{0}^{i})})^{2}}\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(Y_{0}^{i}).

Since
\nabla\phi(X_{t}^{i})-\nabla\phi(X_{0}^{i})=Y_{t}^{i}-Y_{0}^{i}=-t\nabla\frac{\delta F(\mu_{0})}{\delta\mu}(X_{0}^{i})+\sqrt{2\lambda t\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{0}^{i})}\cdot Z_{0}^{i}
, we have

\displaystyle\|Y_{t}^{i}-Y_{t}^{0}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(Y_{0}^{i})}

\displaystyle=\left\|-t\nabla\frac{\delta F(\mu_{0})}{\delta\mu}(X_{0}^{i})+\sqrt{2\lambda t\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{0}^{i})}Z_{0}^{i}\right\|^{2}_{[\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{0}^{i})^{-1}]}

\displaystyle=t^{2}\left\|\nabla\frac{\delta F(\mu_{0})}{\delta\mu}(X_{0}^{i})\right\|^{2}_{[\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{0}^{i})]^{-1}}+2\lambda t\|Z_{0}^{i}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq t^{2}M_{1}^{2}+2\lambda t\|Z_{0}^{i}\|_{2}^{2}.

Using
\chi^{2}
concentration, we have

\displaystyle\Pr[\|Z_{0}^{i}\|_{2}^{2}\geq(\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{\delta})^{2}]\leq\exp(-\delta)

for
t\leq\min\left\{\frac{1}{2c_{1}M_{1}},\frac{1}{16c_{1}^{2}d}\right\}
, and with probability at least
1-\exp(-d)\geq 1-\exp\left(-\frac{1}{16c_{1}^{2}d}\right)
over the draw of
Z_{0}^{i}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\|Y_{t}^{i}-Y_{0}^{i}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(Y_{0}^{i})}\leq tM_{1}+2\sqrt{td}<\frac{1}{c_{1}}.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle(1-c_{1}(tM_{1}+2\sqrt{\lambda td}))^{2}I_{d}\preceq\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{0}^{i})^{1/2}[\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{t}^{i})]^{-1}\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{0})^{1/2}

\displaystyle\preceq\frac{1}{(1-c_{1}(tM_{1}+2\sqrt{\lambda td}))^{2}}I_{d}.

For the remaining probability
\exp\left(-\frac{1}{16c_{1}^{2}d}\right)
, consider the function

\displaystyle g_{i}(s):=\langle u,\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(Y_{0}^{i}+s(Y_{t}^{i}-Y_{0}^{i}))u\rangle=:\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(Y_{s}^{i})[u,u].

From self-concordance, we have

\displaystyle|g_{i}^{\prime}(s)|

\displaystyle=|\nabla^{3}\phi^{*}(Y_{s}^{i})[u,u,Y_{t}^{i}-Y_{0}^{i}]

\displaystyle\leq 2c_{1}\|Y_{t}^{i}-Y_{0}^{i}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(Y_{s})}\|u\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(Y_{s}^{i})}

\displaystyle=2c_{1}\|Y_{t}^{i}-Y_{0}^{i}\|_{\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(Y_{s})}\cdot g_{i}(s)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2c_{1}}{\sqrt{c_{2}}}\|Y_{t}^{i}-Y_{0}^{i}\|_{2}\cdot g_{i}(s)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2c_{1}}{\sqrt{c_{2}}}D\cdot g_{i}(s),

which implies
|{\log g(1)}-\log g(0)|\leq\frac{2c_{1}D}{\sqrt{c_{2}}}
. Then, we deduce

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{2c_{1}D}{\sqrt{c_{2}}}\right)\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(Y_{0}^{i})\preceq\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(Y_{t}^{i})\preceq\exp\left(\frac{2c_{1}D}{\sqrt{c_{2}}}\right)\nabla^{2}\phi^{*}(Y_{0}^{i})

and

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{2c_{1}D}{\sqrt{c_{2}}}\right)I_{d}\preceq\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{0}^{i})^{1/2}[\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{t}^{i})]^{-1}\nabla^{2}\phi(X_{0}^{i})^{1/2}\preceq\exp\left(\frac{2c_{1}D}{\sqrt{c_{2}}}\right)I_{d}.

Thus, we deduce that, in expectation with respect to
Z_{0}^{i}
,
M
is upper bounded by

M=\frac{1-\exp(-\frac{1}{16c_{1}^{2}t})}{(1-c_{1}(tM_{1}+2\sqrt{\lambda td}))^{2}}+\exp\left(-\frac{1}{16c_{1}^{2}t}+\frac{2c_{1}D}{\sqrt{c_{2}}}\right),

which goes to
1
as
t\to 0
. This concludes the proof.
∎
