Comparative efficacy and safety of lenvatinib, sintilimab combined interventional therapy vs bevacizumab, sintilimab combined interventional therapy in intermediate and advanced hepatocellular carcinoma: a multicenter retrospective study
Authors: Yi-Xin Yuan, Lin Xu, Shu-Min Fu, Xiao-Huan Lai, An Li, Kan Liu, Yong-kang Xu, Ye Mao, Sheng-lan Huang, Jian-Bing Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystemic therapy combined with local intervention has demonstrated promising efficacy in patients with Unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (uHCC). Bevacizumab plus sintilimab combined interventional treatment (BSIT) and lenvatinib plus sintilimab combined interventional treatment (LSIT) are widely used in clinical practice in China, but the optimal option remains unclear. This study aimed to compare the efficacy and safety of BSIT and LSIT in intermediate and advanced uHCC.MethodsPrimary endpoints were progression-free survival (PFS) and objective response rate (ORR); secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), disease control rate (DCR), and treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs). Propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to reduce bias. Kaplan–Meier and multivariable Cox analyses were used to assess survival and prognostic factors.ResultsAfter PSM, 160 patients were assigned to BSIT (n = 80) and LSIT (n = 80). Survival outcomes, ORR, and DCR were similar between groups. Grade 3/4 AEs were comparable, except gastrointestinal bleeding, which was higher in BSIT. No grade 5 events occurred. Multivariate analysis revealed that extrahepatic metastasis and number of intrahepatic lesions independently predicted progression-free survival, while ECOG performance status was associated with overall survival.ConclusionsSintilimab plus lenvatinib or bevacizumab with interventional therapy showed comparable efficacy and tolerable safety in intermediate–advanced uHCC.Keywords: Unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma, Interventional treatment, Molecular targeted therapy, Immunotherapy, PD-L1 inhibitors

Introduction

Primary liver cancer is the sixth most common malignancy and the third leading cause of cancer death worldwide [1, 2]. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) constitutes the majority of cases, with over 80% diagnosed at advanced stages, making them ineligible for curative treatments and resulting in poor prognosis [3].

In recent years, systemic therapy for intermediate to advanced uHCC has achieved substantial progress, particularly through combinations of targeted agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors. The IMbrave150 trial established atezolizumab plus bevacizumab as a standard first-line regimen, confirming the benefit of integrating immunotherapy with anti-angiogenic therapy [4]. Similarly, the ORIENT-32 trial demonstrated that sintilimab combined with the bevacizumab biosimilar IBI305 significantly prolonged OS and PFS, providing an effective treatment option for Chinese patients with uHCC. Based on these results, the combination of sintilimab and bevacizumab biosimilar has been incorporated into the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) guidelines as a recommended first-line systemic therapy for uHCC [5]. Meanwhile, Lenvatinib demonstrated non-inferiority to sorafenib in the REFLECT study and has since become a standard first-line option in systemic therapy for hepatocellular carcinoma [6]. Moreover, retrospective analyses suggest that the combination of lenvatinib with sintilimab achieves favorable ORR and DCR, further supporting the role of combined targeted and immune-based strategies in this setting.

Locoregional interventional therapy remains central in HCC management. Both National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and Chinese guidelines recommend transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) as standard therapy for intermediate to advanced uHCC [7]. In recent years, several studies have demonstrated that hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC) can serve as an effective locoregional strategy for controlling tumor progression in intermediate and advanced HCC [8–10]. Moreover, the combination of TACE and HAIC has been explored in patients with a high tumor burden or macrovascular invasion, showing significant survival benefits [11, 12]. Locoregional therapy combined with systemic treatment has the potential to further enhance tumor response and prolong overall survival. Notably, the LEAP-012 and EMERALD-1 trials have shown that combining TACE with antiangiogenic agents or tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) can significantly improve clinical outcomes [13, 14]. Furthermore, retrospective studies have indicated that the combination of HAIC-FOLFOX with PD-1 inhibitors and TKIs may improve survival in Chinese patients with HCC, particularly those with a high tumor burden [15].

According to international guidelines, including those from the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL), the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD), and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), the current standard first-line systemic therapies for unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma comprise atezolizumab plus bevacizumab, durvalumab plus tremelimumab, or nivolumab plus ipilimumab. In China, however, treatment practices differ because of variations in drug accessibility, insurance reimbursement policies, and national guideline recommendations—particularly those issued by the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO). During the study period (May 2022 to Mar 2025), the combination of sintilimab with either bevacizumab biosimilar (IBI305) or lenvatinib had been incorporated into the CSCO guidelines as recommended first-line options for unresectable HCC. Consequently, these two regimens—BSIT and LSIT—were widely adopted in real-world clinical practice, often administered together with interventional approaches such as transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) or hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC). Despite their widespread use, robust multicenter evidence directly comparing BSIT and LSIT remains limited. Therefore, this multicenter real-world retrospective study was designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of these two regionally prevalent combination strategies in patients with intermediate to advanced hepatocellular carcinoma, aiming to provide clinically relevant evidence to guide treatment decision-making in this specific context.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective study included patients with uHCC who received interventional therapy combined with either sintilimab plus bevacizumab or sintilimab plus lenvatinib at three medical centers in China—The Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University, The First Affiliated Hospital of Gannan Medical University, and The Fifth People’s Hospital of Ganzhou—between May 2022 and Mar 2025. Inclusion criteria were: [1] patients aged 18–75 years; [2] Patients with HCC confirmed by imaging standards or histological diagnosis; [3] Child-Pugh class A or B; [4] ECOG performance status of 0 or 1; [5] at least one target lesion evaluable by RECIST 1.1 criteria; [6] No prior treatment for HCC; [7] No other malignancies; [8] At least two cycles of BSIT or LSIT; Exclusion criteria were: [1] severe esophagogastric varices or coagulation dysfunction; [2] Treatment interruption or regimen change not due to tumor progression; [3] history of severe heart disease, renal disease, and other underlying diseases that may preclude treatment tolerance; [4] Incomplete baseline information; Laboratory data were collected within three days prior to the initiation of treatment. Baseline imaging assessments, including contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT), were performed within seven days before treatment initiation and subsequently repeated every 2 to 3 months. All imaging evaluations were independently reviewed by two radiologists.

Esophagogastric fundus varices

patients in the BSIT group underwent esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) prior to the administration of bevacizumab to assess the presence and severity of esophagogastric varices. Esophagogastric varices were assessed according to the Japanese Research Society for Portal Hypertension (JRSPH) “Form” (F) classification: F1 represents small, straight varices that flatten with insufflation; F2 indicates moderately enlarged, tortuous varices; and F3 describes markedly enlarged, nodular varices. Management strategies in our cohort were aligned with variceal severity. Patients with F1 varices underwent surveillance with or without non-selective β-blockers, while F2–F3 varices received endoscopic prophylaxis. Notably, Patients with F3 varices or red signs were excluded from the BSIT group due to high bleeding risk.

Treatment procedure

TACE

The femoral artery was accessed using the Seldinger technique. A 5-French catheter was inserted into the celiac trunk or superior mesenteric artery for angiographic evaluation, followed by the selective advancement of a 2.7-French microcatheter into the tumor-feeding artery. Oxaliplatin (75 mg/m²) and 5-fluorouracil (750 mg/m²) were slowly infused through the microcatheter into the tumor-supplying artery. Subsequently, an emulsion consisting of iodized oil (10–20 mL) mixed with doxorubicin (20 mg/m²) was administered, followed by embolization of the tumor vasculature. The specific doses of chemotherapeutic agents and iodized oil were individualized based on a comprehensive assessment of body surface area, tumor size and location, and liver function status. Upon completion of the procedure, the arterial puncture site was compressed to achieve hemostasis and covered with a pressure bandage for 6 h. After returning to the ward, patients were closely monitored for post-embolization syndrome, including fever, chills, abdominal pain or distension, nausea, and vomiting. Careful attention was also given to detecting any bleeding or hematoma formation at the puncture site.

HAIC

The femoral artery was punctured using the Seldinger technique, and a catheter was advanced into the hepatic artery under digital subtraction angiography (DSA) guidance. The FOLFOX regimen was then administered intra-arterially through the catheter. This regimen consisted of oxaliplatin (85 mg/m²) infused over 3 h on day 1, folinic acid (400 mg/m²) administered over 3–5 h on day 1, and a bolus of 5-fluorouracil (400 mg/m²) followed by a continuous infusion of 2400 mg/m² over 46 h. Upon completion of the chemotherapeutic infusion, both the catheter and sheath were removed.

TACE + HAIC

Multinodular, massive, and diffuse hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) often involve multiple feeding arteries and collateral circulations. During interventional procedures, selected tumor-feeding arteries are targeted for transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) based on the tumor’s vascular anatomy and the patient’s liver function. A catheter is then super-selectively advanced into the identified feeding arteries. Following the embolization, the patient returns to the ward to receive the FOLFOX regimen, administered as previously described.

Bevacizumab plus sintilimab treatment

Sintilimab and bevacizumab were administered 3 to 5 days after completion of the interventional therapy. Patients received intravenous infusions of sintilimab at a fixed dose of 200 mg and bevacizumab at 15 mg/kg body weight every three weeks. Treatment was continued until disease progression or the occurrence of unacceptable toxicity.

Lenvatinib plus sintilimab treatment

Lenvatinib was administered orally at an initial dose of either 8 mg (≤ 60 kg) or 12 mg (> 60 kg) depending on their body weight until disease progression. Sintilimab intravenously at a dose of 200 mg every 3 weeks. Lenvatinib was administrated on the first day of interventional therapy and Sintilimab was intravenously injected 3 to 5 days after completion of the interventional therapy. If patients experienced intolerable toxicities, the doses of lenvatinib were either reduced or discontinued until the AEs were resolved. Treatment selection

The treatment protocols in this study were developed in accordance with the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) guidelines and the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging system. For locoregional therapy, the treatment modality was determined through multidisciplinary discussion and imaging assessment by at least two senior physicians. TACE was indicated for patients with preserved liver function and without complete main portal vein occlusion, particularly those with nodular or massive lesions showing well-defined margins and rich vascularity. HAIC was selected for patients unsuitable for conventional TACE, especially those with diffuse infiltrative tumors or multiple (> 5–10) nodules. The combined TACE–HAIC approach was reserved for complex cases involving a large tumor burden or multiple feeding arteries. Regarding systemic therapy, the choice between BSIT and LSIT followed CSCO guideline recommendations and was determined by multidisciplinary evaluation, taking into account drug accessibility, hepatic function, and bleeding risk. Specifically, the sintilimab–bevacizumab combination was used as first-line therapy for patients without high bleeding risk, whereas lenvatinib-based regimens were preferred for those with increased bleeding risk. In patients without contraindications, the final choice between regimens was made through shared decision-making, considering both physician recommendations and patient preference.

Propensity score matching analysis

In this study, PSM was applied to minimize selection bias and balance baseline characteristics between the two treatment groups. Propensity scores were estimated for all patients using a logistic regression model that included the following potential confounding variables: Age, Gender, ECOG-PS, Cirrhosis, Etiologies, Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), Child–Pugh stage, portal vein tumor thrombus (PVTT), Max tumor diameter, Number of intrahepatic lesions, Extrahepatic metastasis. A 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching algorithm was used with a caliper width of 0.2.

Outcome assessment

The primary endpoints of this study were progression-free survival (PFS) and objective response rate (ORR), while the secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), disease control rate (DCR) and treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs). PFS was defined as the duration from the initiation of treatment to either radiographic disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. OS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to death from any cause. Tumor responses were assessed according to both RECIST 1.1 and modified RECIST (mRECIST) criteria and categorized as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD). ORR was defined as the proportion of patients achieving either CR or PR, while DCR was defined as the proportion achieving CR, PR, or SD. TRAEs were graded in accordance with the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the R statistical software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation Inc., Vienna, Austria) and SPSS version 27.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR), depending on the data distribution, and compared using the Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. PFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed with the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to identify independent prognostic factors associated with PFS and OS. Variables with a P-value < 0.05 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Patient characteristics

Between May 2022 and June 2024, 204 patients with uHCC were enrolled from 288 diagnosed cases. Among them, 99 received BSIT, and 105 received LSIT (Fig. 2). Before PSM, significant differences existed in alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), BCLC stage, and portal vein tumor thrombus (PVTT). After PSM, baseline characteristics were well-balanced (all p > 0.05). Most patients were male, under 60 years old, HBV-positive, and had cirrhosis and over half were BCLC stage C. Notably, whereas one and two patients with a Child-Pugh score of 9 were present in the BSIT and LSIT groups before PSM, respectively, none of them remained in either cohort after PSM, thereby substantially enhancing the comparability of the subsequent efficacy and safety analyses. Efficacy

Before and after PSM, there were no statistically significant differences in median overall survival (mOS) or median progression-free survival (mPFS) between the BSIT and LSIT groups (all p > 0.05). After matching, mOS was 22.60 months for BSIT and 22.27 months for LSIT (HR: 0.873; p = 0.578); mPFS was 11.87 and 10.93 months, respectively (HR: 1.053; p = 0.786; Fig. 3). According to mRECIST and RECIST 1.1 criteria, no significant differences in ORR or DCR were observed between the BSIT and LSIT groups (all p > 0.05; Table 2). Based on mRECIST, ORR and DCR were 66.3% and 95% for BSIT, and 73.8% and 95% for LSIT. By RECIST 1.1, the values were 40% and 93.7% for BSIT, and 53.8% and 93.7% for LSIT. Univariate and multivariate analyses of prognostic factors for survival are summarized in Table 3. For PFS, multivariate analysis identified number of intrahepatic lesions (HR = 1.43, p = 0.039) and extrahepatic metastasis (HR = 1.56, p = 0.013) as independent predictors. For OS, multivariate analysis showed that ECOG performance status (HR = 1.83, p = 0.005) was an independent prognostic factor. Forest plots were used to compare outcomes across subgroups. For both OS and PFS, the BSiIT and LSiIT groups demonstrated comparable therapeutic efficacy across all subgroups (p > 0.05), indicating that both treatment regimens yielded similar clinical benefits regardless of patient subgroup (Fig. 5).

Advanced hepatocellular carcinoma with major vascular invasion (Vp3-Vp4)

The survival curves indicated that among patients with major vascular invasion (VP3-VP4), there were no statistically significant differences between the BSIT and LSIT groups in either mPFS (BSIT: 8.80 months; 95% CI: 6.67–12.93 vs. LSIT: 10.33 months; 95% CI: 7.33–14.67; HR: 0.903; 95% CI: 0.562–1.452; p = 0.673) or mOS (BSIT: 20.5 months; 95% CI: 12.57–NA vs. LSIT: 16.63 months; 95% CI: 12.97–32.03; HR: 0.938; 95% CI: 0.521–1.691; p = 0.832; Fig. 3). In terms of treatment response, the LSIT group demonstrated a significantly superior ORR compared with the BSIT group, according to mRECIST 1.1 criteria (74.5% vs.57.1%; p = 0.037; Fig. 6).

Safety of treatment

TRAEs are summarized in Table 4. No fatal TRAEs occurred. While the overall incidence of any-grade AEs was similar between groups, significant differences were observed in specific TRAEs. Hypertension (any grade:31.4% vs. 17.2%; p = 0.02) and hand-foot syndrome (any grade: 24.8% vs. 2.0%; p < 0.001) was more frequent in the LSIT group, whereas gastrointestinal bleeding (any grade: 17.2% vs. 5.7%; grade 3/4: 9.1% vs. 1.9%) were more common in the BSIT group (all p < 0.05). Among 17 patients in the BSIT group who experienced gastrointestinal bleeding, most events (14 cases, 82.4%) were variceal in origin and related to esophagogastric varices secondary to portal hypertension, while only a few (3 cases, 17.6%) were due to other causes. Other common AEs included abdominal pain, elevated AST/ALT, and vomiting. All AEs were manageable. The distribution of any-grade and grade 3/4 adverse AEs is illustrated in Fig. 7.

Dissussion

This multicenter retrospective study compared the efficacy and safety of BSIT versus LSIT in patients with uHCC. No significant differences were observed in median PFS, median OS, ORR, DCR or subgroup outcomes between groups. Nonetheless, the BSIT group showed numerically longer mOS (22.60 vs. 22.27 months) and mPFS (11.87 vs. 10.93 months). Independent prognostic factors included ECOG performance status, number of intrahepatic lesions, and extrahepatic metastasis. Safety profiles differed significantly between groups for hypertension, hand–foot skin reaction and gastrointestinal bleeding.

TACE is a cornerstone locoregional treatment for intermediate to advanced uHCC. Its combination with systemic agents has further improved outcomes. Recent phase III trials, including LEAP-012 (TACE + lenvatinib + pembrolizumab; mPFS: 14.6 months) and EMERALD-1 (TACE + durvalumab + bevacizumab; mPFS: 15.0 vs. 8.2 months, p = 0.032; ORR: 43.6% vs. 29.6%), highlight the promising strategy of combining TACE with immunotherapy and targeted therapy [13, 14].

Combining HAIC with TACE synergistically improves intrahepatic tumor control. TACE reduces tumor blood supply, prolonging retention and enhancing penetration of HAIC-delivered chemotherapy, which increases tumor necrosis and delays progression [16]. Furthermore, HAIC plus systemic therapy exerts dual effects: HAIC induces tumor cell death, releasing antigens and enhancing immunogenicity to improve the tumor microenvironment [17]. On the other hand, anti-VEGF agents promote vascular normalization, increasing perfusion and drug delivery [18, 19].

Although the BSIT group exhibited numerically longer mOS and mPFS than the LSIT group, Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in survival outcomes before or after PSM. Moreover, ORR and DCR were comparable between groups, as assessed by both RECIST 1.1 and mRECIST criteria. The outcomes of this study appear more favorable compared to other reported triple combination therapies involving interventional treatments, targeted agents, and immunotherapy. For instance, Zhu et al. conducted a study comparing TACE combined with a PD-(L)1 inhibitor and a targeted agent versus TACE alone in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), reporting a mOS of 19.2 months and a mPFS of 9.5 months in the combination group [20]. Similarly, Chang et al. investigated HAIC combined with a PD-1 inhibitor and lenvatinib versus PD-1 inhibitor plus lenvatinib alone, and found that the triple combination achieved an mOS of 19.3 months and an mPFS of 9.6 months [15].

Portal vein tumor thrombosis (PVTT) is a major prognostic factor in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). An updated analysis of the IMbrave150 study reported that patients with major vascular invasion achieved a mOS of only 7.6 months and a mPFS of 5.4 months following treatment with atezolizumab plus bevacizumab (A + T) [21]. Previous studies have shown that both TACE and HAIC can improve outcomes in HCC patients with PVTT [22, 23]. Our center previously demonstrated that HAIC combined with lenvatinib and PD-1 inhibitors yielded a mOS and mPFS of 14.3 and 8.8 months, respectively—superior to the IMbrave150 outcomes [23]. In the current subgroup analysis of patients with Vp3–4 PVTT, the LSIT group had longer mPFS (10.33 vs. 8.80 months), while the BSIT group showed superior mOS (20.50 vs. 16.63 months).

Several factors may explain these findings: First, Lenvatinib, a multi-targeted TKI, inhibits FGFR and VEGFR potentially enabling better control of progressive lesions and longer PFS [24, 25]. Second, Lenvatinib combined with PD-1 inhibitors enhances antiangiogenic and immunomodulatory effects [26]. Additionally, Bevacizumab might mitigate TACE/HAIC-related ischemia-reperfusion injury and inflammation, supporting long-term survival [27, 28]. Lastly, adverse events commonly associated with Lenvatinib, such as hand–foot skin reaction and hypertension, may lead to early treatment discontinuation, potentially compromising the efficacy of combination regimens. In contrast, the bevacizumab-based regimen may offer better tolerability, allowing for more sustained treatment and improved therapeutic outcomes. In addition, the LSIT group showed a significantly higher ORR than the BSIT group (mRECIST: 74.5% vs. 57.1%; p = 0.037), indicating its potential advantage in disease control for patients with extensive intrahepatic burden.

The forest plot indicated comparable therapeutic efficacy between the two combination regimens across all subgroups. This consistency may be due to shared antiangiogenic mechanisms between lenvatinib and bevacizumab, as well as their similar synergistic effects when combined with immunotherapy or locoregional treatments [29].

In terms of adverse events TRAEs, no significant differences were observed in the incidence of most adverse events between groups, regardless of grade. However, the BSIT group exhibited a higher incidence of grade 3/4 gastrointestinal bleeding (9.1% vs. 1.9%; p = 0.02). Despite routine pretreatment gastroscopy to exclude varices, some BSIT patients still experienced bleeding, necessitating regular endoscopic surveillance and vigilant monitoring during treatment. Conversely, hand–foot syndrome was significantly more common in the LSIT group, both overall (24.8% vs. 2.0%; p < 0.001) and for grade 1/2 events (22.9% vs. 2.0%; p < 0.001), underscoring the need for prophylactic measures and prompt symptomatic management to avoid dose reduction or treatment disruption.

In our study, gastrointestinal bleeding occurred more frequently in the BSIT group, which underwent pre-treatment endoscopic evaluation (EGD) before systemic therapy. This finding likely reflects two interacting mechanisms. In hepatocellular carcinoma, the extent of portal vein tumor thrombosis (PVTT) is a key hemodynamic driver of bleeding risk. PVTT at or above VP3 obstructs portal flow, elevates portal pressure, and promotes high-risk esophagogastric varices prone to rupture [30, 31]. Meanwhile, bevacizumab impairs VEGF-mediated endothelial repair and compromises vascular integrity, further lowering the bleeding threshold. Thus, in the portal hypertensive milieu induced by advanced PVTT, anti-VEGF therapy may act as a permissive cofactor for hemorrhage [32].These considerations highlight the need for endoscopic assessment and optimization before initiating bevacizumab-containing regimens and support risk-adapted management strategies incorporating PVTT grade into bleeding risk estimation and prophylaxis planning. Clinically, patients with VP3–VP4 disease should undergo careful endoscopic screening, receive guideline-based primary prophylaxis, and be closely monitored during treatment, given the synergistic effect of advanced PVTT and bevacizumab exposure on GI bleeding [30, 31].

While this study provides valuable real-world evidence regarding the BSIT and LSIT regimens, several important limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a retrospective analysis conducted across multiple centers within a single healthcare system, our findings require external validation in broader and more diverse populations. Moreover, although BSIT and LSIT are widely used in clinical practice in China, they are not currently endorsed as first-line systemic therapies by major international guidelines. The absence of head-to-head prospective comparisons with internationally established standards—such as atezolizumab plus bevacizumab or durvalumab plus tremelimumab—precludes any definitive conclusions regarding the relative efficacy of these regimens. In addition, because of the retrospective and multicenter nature of this study, detailed dosing data for lenvatinib were not consistently available across all centers. Consequently, the relative dose intensity (RDI) could not be reliably assessed, which may slightly limit evaluation of potential dose–response relationships in this setting. Taken together, our results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than establishing therapeutic equivalence or superiority. Future prospective, multicenter randomized trials are warranted to validate these findings and to clarify the global positioning of these combination strategies in the treatment landscape for advanced hepatocellular carcinoma.

Conclusion

BSIT and LSIT demonstrated comparable clinical benefits in patients with intermediate to advanced uHCC. Overall, TRAEs were manageable; however, patients receiving the BSIT regimen should be closely monitored for potential bleeding complications.
