Occipital interhemispheric transtentorial approach for the quadrangular lobule of the cerebellum
Authors: Miguel Ossuna, Aderaldo Costa Alves Junior, Marco Antônio Zanini, Pedro Tadao Hamamoto Filho

Introduction

The occipital interhemispheric approach was first described by Glasauer in 1970, combining the parieto-occipital interhemispheric approach to the pineal region described by Heppner in 1959 with the suboccipital transtentorial approach described by Poppen in 1966. In this technique, the occipital lobe is mobilized laterally along the interhemispheric fissure and the tentorium is opened parallel to the straight sinus up to its free edge [1–3].

This approach represented a significant advancement in the safe treatment of pineal region lesions and, after refinements, was extended to dorsal brainstem, splenium of the corpus callosum, and posterior thalamic lesions. Another potential application of OITT is the treatment of superior cerebellar lesions, specifically in quadrangular lobule, as it constitutes the floor of the posterior incisural space [4]. However, there are few reports describing the use of OITT for lesions within the cerebellar quadrangular lobe.

Relevant surgical anatomy

The posterior interhemispheric fissure approach exposes the medial cortical surface of the parietal and occipital lobes, the superior portion of the parahippocampal gyrus, and the splenium of the corpus callosum. The falx cerebri containing the straight and inferior sagittal sinuses can be visualized medially [5].

Inferior to the splenium, the pineal and parapineal regions, pulvinar of the thalamus, dorsal midbrain, quadrigeminal cistern, and tentorial incisura are exposed. Opening the tentorium widens the exposure to the superior vermis and quadrangular lobule of the cerebellum.

The quadrangular lobe of the cerebellum is an important subdivision of the cerebellar hemisphere, located in the anterior lobe. It lies between the primary fissure posteriorly and the precentral fissure anteriorly, occupying the superior surface of the cerebellar hemisphere.

Key venous structures in this region include the confluence of the inferior sagittal sinus, the internal cerebral veins and the basal veins of Rosenthal, which form the vein of Galen [6, 7].

Surgical technique

Positioning

The patient is placed in a three-quarter prone (park-bench) position, with the side of the lesion facing downward and the head flexed toward the floor. The occipital protuberance is the highest point, and the targeted occipital region lies inferior to the contralateral occipital lobe. This positioning allows gravity-assisted retraction of the occipital lobe following craniotomy, minimizing the need for fixed retractors (Fig. 1).

Skin incision

Neuronavigation can used to plan a wide craniotomy. A reversed "J-shaped" skin incision is marked to encompass the intended craniotomy margins.

Craniotomy

A wide craniotomy is performed, crossing the midline and extending below the inion–asterion line, exposing the torcular, superior sagittal sinus, and transverse sinuses. This facilitates the separation of the ipsilateral occipital lobe from the falx and tentorium.

Durotomy

A X-shaped dural opening is performed, with dural flaps reflected to the edges of the craniotomy. Cortical veins are identified, which are less frequent in this region and generally enter the superior sagittal sinus at an obtuse angle nearby the superior limit of the craniotomy. These veins must be preserved to avoid a non-negligible risk of venous infarction.

Microsurgery

The occipital lobe is gently retracted from the falx and tentorium and protected with cottonoid patties. Dissection of the interhemispheric fissure is carried out under the microscope, exposing the splenium of the corpus callosum, the entry of the vein of Galen into the straight sinus, and the tentorial incisura. CSF is released by opening the cisterns. The tentorium is coagulated as posteriorly as possible (1.5 cm anterior to the transverse sinus) and opened with a #11 blade parallel to the straight sinus (1.5 cm lateral), extending to the free edge. If necessary, the falx and contralateral tentorium can be opened to allow bilateral exposure. 4–0 sutures can be used to retract the tentorium upward, increasing the surgical corridor. After cutting and retracting the tentorium, the cerebellar surface can be clearly identified from the vermis (medially) to the quadrangular lobe (laterally) (Fig. 2).

Lesion resection

Debulking is alternated with circumferential dissection to achieve safe lesion removal.

Indications

Lesions of the superior cerebellum, either midline (superior vermis) or paramedian (quadrangular lobule), ideally located just beneath the tentorium (Fig. 3).

Benefits

This approach uses a natural anatomical corridor that allows wide exposure without manipulation of eloquent areas and with minimal injury to neural tissue. It enables easy identification of deep vascular structures. Being a supratentorial approach, it reduces muscle trauma, postoperative pain, and the risk of CSF fistula.

Specific information for the patients

Patients should be counseled on the risk of hemianopia. In most cases, the deficit is transient, with spontaneous recovery in a few weeks. As with any cerebellar lesions—particularly neoplastic lesions—postoperative, dysmetria, ataxia, and impaired coordination may occur, especially when there is significant extension toward the midline.

There is also a risk of venous infarction due to injury to superficial cortical veins draining into the interhemispheric fissure, which may lead to postoperative ischemia or hemorrhage. Additionally, injury to deep venous structures carries a risk of significant bleeding, which can result in intraoperative hemodynamic instability and, in severe cases, necessitate interruption of the procedure.

Postoperative management of the patients

Routine prophylactic antibiotics are administered to reduce the risk of surgical site infection. Careful attention should be paid to the possibility of CSF leakage, with close monitoring of the wound and surgical drains. Standard measures for thromboembolism prophylaxis should be implemented. On physical examination, particular emphasis should be placed on the assessment of visual fields and cerebellar function.

Conclusion

OITT is a safe and low-morbidity route for lesions within the cerebellar quadrangular lobe, as it uses a natural anatomical corridor, avoids manipulation of eloquent areas, and minimizes injury to normal brain tissue.
