Waste-to-energy approach by transforming municipal solid waste into biohydrogen and biomethane through dark fermentation with the yield of biofertilizer
Authors: K V Sreedharan, Debabrata Barik, Ayyar Dinesh, D Shanmugapriya, M Santhamoorthy, N Ashok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents an integrated waste-to-energy strategy for the sustainable conversion of the biodegradable organic fraction of municipal solid waste (BOFMSW) into biohydrogen (Bio.H2) and biomethane (Bio.CH4) through a two-stage continuous stirred dark fermentation (DF) process. The first-stage bioreactor was inoculated with Clostridium Thermocellum selectively enriched in a 2-bromoethanesulfonic acid (BESA) medium, and the influence of bimetallic ion catalysts NiCl2 + FeCl2 and NiCl2 + FeSO4 was evaluated at various concentrations (25, 50, 75, and 100 mg/L). The catalyst combination NiCl2 + FeCl2 at 75 mg/L produced the maximum Bio.H2 yield of 3162 L, representing a 69% enhancement compared with the catalyst-free substrate. At this optimal catalytic concentration, the percentage of H2 in the gas composition was 69.26%. The second-stage bioreactor utilized the effluent from the first stage for Bio.CH4 generation, achieving the highest cumulative yield of 729 L at 50 mg/L NiCl2 + FeCl2, which was 59% higher than that of the catalyst-free substrate. The two-stage process achieved an overall COD removal efficiency of 93.18%, demonstrating the system’s effective capacity for energy recovery. Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) and Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM) analyses confirmed the biochemical and morphological degradation of complex organics into volatile fatty acids, illustrating efficient substrate conversion and microbial proliferation. The final digested slurry, rich in nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, was found suitable for use as a bio-fertilizer, supporting nutrient recycling and soil enrichment. This integrated process not only improved energy recovery efficiency but also achieved near-zero waste discharge, combining waste-to-energy and waste-to-resource approaches. The developed system demonstrates strong potential for pilot-scale implementation of Bio.H2, and Bio.CH4 for co-production from municipal solid waste (MSW), offering a circular bioeconomy pathway toward low-carbon, sustainable urban waste management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51324-1.Keywords: Waste-to-energy, Biohydrogen, Clostridium thermocellum, Dark fermentation, Green energy, 2-bromoethanesulfonic acid (BESA), FTIR, FESEM analysisSubject terms: Biotechnology, Environmental sciences, Microbiology

Introduction

The sharp rise in global population, coupled with industrialization and urbanization, has led to a persistent rise in energy demand. Presently, this huge energy demand is met mainly with fossil fuels and a smaller contribution from renewable fuels. Considering the benefits of renewable fuels, the present era needs to explore more about the clean fuels of renewable nature instead of fossil fuels. At the same time, it can be predicted that the depletion of fossil fuel reservoirs will bring huge future crises in energy needs. Additionally, the use of fossil fuels increases greenhouse gas emissions and climate change, posing significant environmental and socio-economic problems. In concern to this, the transition toward sustainable, renewable, environmentally friendly energy systems has emerged as a worldwide priority to secure long-term energy security and mitigate adverse environmental impacts1,2. Bio.H2 production from organic and municipal waste emerges as an innovative renewable energy strategy, addressing both clean energy generation and effective waste management. Considering the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDG), Bio.H2 generation from municipal waste directly supports SDG 7 (Affordable and Clean Energy) and SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production), while also contributing to SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities) and SDG 13 (Climate Action) by enabling circular waste management and reducing greenhouse gas emissions3,4.

Hydrogen (H2) is widely recognized as a promising alternative to fossil fuels, as it is renewable and possesses high energy content and zero carbon emissions, which makes less impact on the environment compared to other fuels5. It has a much higher heating value than hydrocarbon-based petroleum fuels. In addition to this, it is lightweight, has good mixability with air, and a high combustion rate with no carbon emission, making it a suitable alternative to fossil fuels and other renewable fuels for efficient power generation6. Additionally, H2 can also be used as fuel in fuel cells to directly extract electricity without combustion. Many researchers have explored the H2 production using various proven technologies, which include steam methane reforming, coal gasification, water electrolysis, solid oxide electrolysis, thermochemical water splitting, biomass gasification, dark fermentation (DF), photo-fermentation, bio photolysis, and microbial electrolysis. Most of these technologies require substantial energy input, which, to some extent, remains economically unviable and environmentally unsustainable in some of the cases7,8. However, the quality and quantity of H2 generated depend on the robustness of the process chosen, without considering the economic aspect. As presently the world is facing issues for the disposal and handing of the huge organic wastes generated from the household and industries, it can be considered that, a low-cost technology such as DF may be a prime choice to handle a larger portion of waste to convert useful energy (Bio.H2) and biofertilizer, simultaneously without adversely affecting the ecosystem and improving the soil productivity9–12.

Extensive studies have been conducted by researchers to explore the possibility of generating bioelectricity from biowaste and Bio.H2 from waste by using various technologies. A study conducted by Idris et al.13 demonstrated the synthesis of palm kernel shell–derived reduced graphene oxide anodes modified with metal oxides (TiO2 and ZnO) to enhance microbial fuel cell (MFC) performance. The PKS-rGO/ZnO anode exhibited the highest power density (43.2 mW m−2) and bioremediation efficiency (93%), confirming the potential of agro-waste–based electrodes for cost-effective and scalable MFC industrialization. In another research, Idris et al.14 developed sustainable anodes from palm kernel shell–derived graphene oxide and reduced graphene oxide for MFCs aimed at simultaneous bioelectricity generation and formaldehyde bioremediation. The PKS-rGO anode exhibited superior electrochemical performance, delivering higher power density (24.47 mW m−2), lower internal resistance, and enhanced electron transfer. Moreover, it achieved an 87% formaldehyde removal efficiency, demonstrating the feasibility of bio-waste–derived electrodes for eco-friendly waste water treatment and energy recovery.

Idris et al.15 demonstrated the feasibility of simultaneous naphthalene biodegradation and bioelectricity generation using a biowaste-powered MFC equipped with palm kernel shell–derived graphene-based anodes. The PKS-rGO anode exhibited superior electrochemical performance, achieving a power density of 35.11 mW m−2 and an 85.5% naphthalene removal efficiency, confirming the effectiveness of agro-waste–derived electrodes in pollutant remediation and energy recovery. In a subsequent study, the authors16 further enhanced MFC performance by hybridizing PKS-rGO with metal oxides (TiO2 and ZnO) to fabricate advanced anodes. The PKS-rGO/ZnO electrode delivered the highest power density (45.4 mW m−2) and biodegradation efficiency (94.3%), with Bacillus and Niallia species identified as key exoelectrogens. These findings highlight the potential of modified biomass-derived electrodes for high-performance, sustainable MFC applications in organic pollutant treatment.

Bio.H2 can be produced through various biological processes, including photo fermentation, microbial electrolysis, and DF. Among these, DF is considered an efficient and cost-effective biological process, in which the breakdown of organic substances by microbial action under anaerobic conditions in the absence of light will take place to produce Bio.H2 gas and other valuable by-products17. DF, in particular, is widely studied for its operational simplicity, which offers a promising pathway for sustainable energy generation, as it can convert a wide range of organic substrates, including complex waste streams such as MSW, into Bio.H218. Bio.H2 production by DF has attained growing interest due to its significant potential for industrial energy applications. Though DF is a promising method for Bio.H2 production, it has many drawbacks also, which limit this process to laboratory-scale production19. Low substrate conversion efficiency, metabolic inhibition, and low yield are the main problems associated with the DF process20. Formation and accumulation of various by-products, such as Volatile Fatty Acids (VFA), which inhibit the microbial activity that leads to reduced Bio.H2 yield. The difficult task of maintaining a complex microbial consortium is a crucial aspect on which the reliability and success of the DF process depends21,22.

Researchers Becerra-Quiroz et al.23 evaluated DF as a sustainable route for valorizing the organic fraction of municipal solid waste (OFMSW) using life cycle assessment and circular economy principles. The study identified an optimal operational condition of 10 gVS/L organic loading with a substrate–inoculum ratio of 1, demonstrating that substrate–inoculum balance significantly governs Bio.H2 yield. Scale-up analysis for Bogotá projected an energy recovery potential of over 2.36 GWh per day, confirming the energetic and environmental feasibility of large-scale Bio.H2 production from urban waste. Florio et al.24 investigated the influence of inoculum-to-substrate ratio on mesophilic DF of OFMSW for Bio.H2 production. Using thermally pretreated sewage sludge as inoculum, the study revealed that an ISR of 0.07 achieved the highest Bio.H2 yield and process stability. The findings validate OFMSW as a viable feedstock and highlight the critical role of inoculum management in optimizing Bio.H2 production efficiency.

Gómez-Aquino et al.25 demonstrated that consecutive intermittent feeding in a sequencing batch reactor significantly enhances Bio.H2 production from the OFMSW. Frequent pulse feeding reduced the lag phase and achieved the highest Bio.H2 accumulation (365.5 NmL) and productivity (340.3 NmL L−1 d−1), while promoting caproate formation. The study highlights how controlled feeding strategies regulate microbial interactions and metabolite pathways to improve DF performance. Raj et al.26 provided a comprehensive review on transforming MSW into sustainable energy and high-value bioproducts under a green biorefinery framework. The authors emphasized that organic-rich MSW can serve as a low-cost feedstock for producing biofuels, organic acids, compost, and chemicals. The study underscores MSW valorization as a pathway toward energy security, environmental protection, and a resilient circular bioeconomy.

By adopting and utilizing a viable and efficient biological technology, it can convert the biodegradable organic fraction of municipal solid waste (BOFMSW) into a valuable gaseous product, such as Bio.H2 and Bio.CH4 and other byproducts, such as bio-fertilizers, which can also address the waste management issues to a major extent. BOFMSW is valuable due to its rich composition that favors the growth of Bio.H2-producing organisms. Among renewable energy generation options, production of Bio.H2 also attracted attention due to its high energy content and zero carbon emissions upon combustion, and holds immense promise as a clean energy source for power generation and transportation27.

Design of bioreactors has a crucial role in stimulating microbial growth and activity, the innovation of which, in turn, boosts Bio.H2 production28. Among various types of the reactor types, continuous stirred tank reactors, either run in batch or continuous mode, are especially appropriate for anaerobic digestion (AD) of substrates because they can effectively handle high solid concentrations29,30. Furthermore, implementing a two-stage AD process by splitting it into two distinct stages significantly improves overall efficiency31,32. Specially developed robust microbial strains can enhance Bio.H2 yield by reducing competing metabolic pathways33. Using an enriched specific H2-forming bacterial culture, and thereby optimizing microbial community growth, can enhance the Bio.H2 production34. The efficiency and rates of Bio.H2 yield can be substantially enhanced by the addition of catalysts35. Careful control of operational parameters and fermentation conditions is required to make this process efficient and viable.

Catalysts of certain metal ions and their combinations facilitate better electron transfer and thereby improve the efficiency of microbial metabolic pathways involved in Bio.H2 production during DF process, leading to higher gas yields. The addition of metal ion catalysts also optimizes the breakdown of complex organic compounds of BOFMSW into simpler molecules, boosting Bio.H2 production. Researchers Mishra et al.36 looked into how Ni/Fe2O3 bimetallic nanoparticles were used to generate Bio.H2 in a fermentation process. They observed that nanoparticles could improve electron transfer in systems that use microbes. When they added Ni/Fe2O3, which was 5% of the total weight and 150 mg per liter, the amount of Bio.H2 produced increased by 51.24% compared to the base study without bimetallic nanoparticles. They also studied the microbes that were present, which were Enterobacter spp. And they observed some other types of microbes that are taking part in Bio.H2 production, but they were not classified. This indicates that the community of microbes was changed when Ni/Fe2O3 nanoparticles were added. The Ni/Fe2O3 nanoparticles really made a difference in the production of Bio.H2 and were recommended as an efficient nano-catalyst that promotes glycolysis and hydrogenase activity for sustainable Bio.H2 enhancement. Researchers Pandit et al.37 emphasized that coupling DF with microbial electrolysis systems can overcome the low Bio.H2 yields of standalone DF. The review highlights the role of hydrogenase enzymes, electroactive microbes, and advanced catalysts in enhancing electron transfer and Bio.H2 recovery. DF–MEC (Microbial Electrolysis Cell) integration is presented as a promising route for high-yield, waste-to- Bio.H2 conversion. Boni et al.38 investigated the role of iron in fermentative Bio.H2 production from organic and food waste, revealing its critical influence on microbial metabolism. Iron supplementation significantly enhanced Bio.H2 yields, with a twofold increase for organic waste at 1000 mg L−1 Fe2+ and a maximum of 1736 mL from food waste at 50 mg L−1. The study confirms iron as a key micronutrient for optimizing DF performance.

Another study by Karadag and Puhakka39 demonstrated that supplementation of iron and nickel significantly enhances anaerobic Bio.H2 production in a continuous stirred tank reactor. Both metals increased Bio.H2 yield by up to 71%, shifting metabolic pathways toward butyrate formation while suppressing ethanol and by-product generation. Microbial analysis revealed dominance of Clostridium butyricum, with nickel reducing community diversity but improving Bio.H2 productivity. Sekoai and Daramola40 evaluated the influence of Fe2+, Mg2+, Ca2+, and Ni2+ on dark fermentative Bio.H2 production using suspended and immobilized mixed cultures. The highest H2 fraction (45.21%) and yield (292.8 mL H2 g−1 TVS) were achieved with immobilized cells supplemented with Fe2+ at 1000 mg L−1. The process also attained 52.3% COD removal, demonstrating the dual benefit of enhanced energy recovery and wastewater treatment. Zheng et al.41 investigated the effects of Fe2+ and Na+ on Bio.H2 production by three fermentative bacterial strains, revealing that optimal Fe2+ significantly enhanced Bio.H2 generation in all cultures. The highest performance was observed for E. harbinense ZGX4 at 0.05 g L−1 Fe2+ (2170 mL L−1 H2), while Na+ selectively stimulated E. harbinense and C. butyricum but inhibited E. cloacae. The study highlights strain-specific metal ion responses and the potential of micronutrient control for maximizing Bio.H2 yields.

It can be noted that, Bio.H2 can be produced most efficiently by using various colonies of microbes such as Clostridium, Bacillus, Enterobacter, and other microbes42–46. However it is observed that, few researchers have explored the study on microbes with catalyst using food waste, agricultural waste and other wastes, however none of the researchers have investigated the use of Clostridium Thermocellum as an anaerobic microbe with BOFMSW as a feed extracted from MSW with the aid of bimetallic catalytic ion combinations, NiCl2 + FeCl2 and NiCl2 + FeSO4, at various concentration ratio (25–100% in step of 25%) in a two stage reactor, which produces both the Bio.H2 gas and Bio.CH4 gas, thereby establishing an integrated waste-to-energy system. Additionally, the Bio.H2 extracted from the reactor was about 69.26% purity, indicating a higher quality compared to other studies. In addition to this, the two-stage reactor was fabricated in the authors’ laboratory with indigenous design parameters and specifications for continuous feeding and a maximum organic loading rate of 10 kg of BOFMSW. The fabricated reactor gives a higher gas yield and a complete digestion of the feedstock in a short span of time, and makes the nutrient-rich biofertilizer without any toxic and harmful components at the exit.

Materials and methods

Substrate preparation

The feedstock utilized in this investigation comprised MSW, which was collected from the municipal dumping yard nearest to the author’s university. The collected MSW primarily comprised biodegradable organic materials as the dominant fraction, followed by plastics, paper, textiles, glass, metals, and other inert components. Before use, biodegradable organic materials were segregated from the mixture of MSW using multiple separation techniques. Initially, the MSW was air-dried for 12 h at 95 °C using an electric hot air oven. After drying, manual segregation and physical screening were performed. This was done to remove large debris and unwanted materials, including plastics, paper, and textiles. Ferrous metallic components were identified and separated using a magnetic method. Non-ferrous metal components were manually picked and removed. Glass and other inert materials, such as stones, sand, and ceramics, were separated by density and texture differences through visual inspection and sieving over a metal grid of different grid sizes. The detailed segregation method was adopted as described in the literature47–49. Finally, the leftover waste was the biodegradable organic fraction, which mainly consisted of food waste, kitchen waste, rotten vegetables, meat, and fish waste. Afterward, the leftover biodegradable organic fraction of Municipal Solid Waste (BOFMSW) was subjected to a series of tests and characterization to ensure its feasibility for use as a feedstock in the reactor for the yield of H2 and CH4 gas. For this purpose, a sample of BOFMSW was prepared by grinding it in a mixture grinder by adding a small amount of water. The COD and BOD were analyzed in a diluted sample, while other parameters were analyzed in a dry sample. The properties of the extracted BOF from MSW are given in Table 1.

For boosting the Bio.H2 yield from BOFMSW, it needs to be pretreated for enhanced microbial activity and to promote the hydrolysis of organic matter. Initially, the BOFMSW was physically pretreated by grinding to reduce particle size and to increase surface area, facilitating faster microbial degradation and improving the overall digestibility of the substrate. After grinding, the BOFMSW was diluted with water at a 1:1 ratio by weight to form a slurry for optimal consistency50. The mixture was then subjected to a mechanical stirring at 200 rpm for 30 min to further reduce particle size and to increase the surface exposure, thereby promoting greater solubility and improving fermentative efficiency51. It was then thermally pretreated by heating at 140 °C for 30 min at a pressure of 2 bar in a closed reactor to suppress any unwanted microbes present in it. Also, the thermal treatment disinfects the feedstock. Additionally, the thermal pretreatment helps to enhance substrate degradation by breaking down macromolecular carbohydrates into simpler nutrients. These metabolically accessible forms promote faster and more efficient growth of H2-producing bacteria. After the thermal treatment, the feedstock slurry was allowed to cool in a controlled and hygienic environment to a temperature of 30 °C. To further enhance the hydrolysis, alkaline pretreatment was carried out using sodium hydroxide, which has superior efficiency in promoting protein solubility and lipid hydrolysis. In addition to this, sodium hydroxide helps to maintain the pH value of the BOFMSW in the range of 5.5 to 6.5, which is highly desirable for Bio.H2 production. Additionally, the alkaline pretreatment breaks down lignocellulosic biomass, facilitating microbial access to structural carbohydrates and creating favorable conditions for microbial and enzymatic activity. In this investigation, the species for Bio.H2 production was Clostridium Thermocellum, which was isolated from the digestate slurry of a pre-existing biomethane plant. The isolation method is explained in the subsequent sections below.

Seed culture preparation

Clostridium Thermocellum microorganism, was isolated from a pre-existing digestate slurry selectively enriched by 2-bromoethanesulfonic acid (BESA) medium (C2H5BrO3S). Researchers demonstrated that the addition of BESA effectively suppresses H2-consuming methanogenic activity in anaerobic consortia, thereby enhancing fermentative Bio.H2 production52–57. BESA used in this study effectively inhibited methanogenesis for selectively enriching cellulolytic, Bio.H2 producing Clostridium species from digestate slurry containing a diverse microbial community. For this purpose, the digestate slurry was filtered using Whatman Filter Paper (size: 2.5 µm) in order to retain only the microbial-rich fractions that favor the specific microbial enrichment. To deactivate non-spore forming methanogens and to enrich spore-forming Bio.H2 producing microorganisms like Clostridium species, the digestate slurry was subjected to heat pretreatment at 90 °C for 30 min. After heat treatment, the slurry was allowed to cool down to about 50 °C. BESA was then added to the pretreated slurry at a concentration of 4 g/L for further efficient inhibition of methanogens and to prevent Bio.H2 consumption without affecting the fermentative bacteria significantly. The slurry was then incubated under anaerobic, thermophilic conditions with Whatman No. 1 Filter Paper, which acted as a primary carbon source, favoring organisms capable of cellulose hydrolysis. After an initial growth period of about 5–7 days, a portion of the culture was repeatedly transferred into a fresh filter paper containing cellulose medium supplemented with BESA under anaerobic conditions. This process was repeated for several cycles, and the repeated cellulose based transfers progressively diluted the non-cellulolytic, slower-growing organisms while cultivating Clostridium Thermocellum. This selective enrichment and selective growth achieved by regulating the bacterial composition resulted in obtaining a seed culture dominated by cellulolytic, Bio.H2 producing Clostridium Thermocellum.

Microbial proliferation was evaluated under a microscopic test in which a dominant specific Bio.H2 producing bacterial cluster was identified. To confirm it, the morphological and cultural characteristics were analyzed by using a microscope and anaerobic agar media. The microscope with a 100× magnification capacity was used to capture images of a diverse microbial population, prominently featuring gram-positive, rod-shaped, spore-forming thermophilic anaerobic bacteria as the dominant species. Figure 1 presents microscopic images of the seed culture. Additionally, to confirm that the isolated anaerobic thermophilic bacterium, 16S rRNA gene sequencing was carried out, on comparing the results with NCBI with BLAST software, the microorganism was found to exhibit ≥ 99% similarity with Clostridium sp. with accession number NR_074629.1. Catalyst selection and its properties

For a higher yield of Bio.H2 from BOFMSW, combined catalysts (1:1 ratio) such as NiCl2 + FeCl2 and NiCl2 + FeSO4 were chosen in equal quantity as bi-metallic ion catalyst for this investigation. The presence of trace elements in these bi-metallic ion catalysts significantly contributes to the synthesis of microbial enzymes, which enable the biological conversion of BOFMSW into Bio.H2. These metal ion catalysts exhibit their effectiveness in breaking down substrates by streamlining the DF process quickly, while optimizing the efficiency.

The combination of NiCl2 + FeCl2 and NiCl2 + FeSO4 acts as an effective metal ion catalyst to enhance Bio.H2 production under DF, particularly when BOFMSW is used as feedstock. Ni2+ ions serve as essential cofactors in hydrogenase enzymes, supporting enzymatic activity, hydrogenase synthesis, and the conversion of sugars into Bio.H2 and VFA. Fe2+ ions facilitate electron transfer, activate hydrogenase and other redox enzymes, and aid in the breakdown of cellulose and hemicellulose into fermentable sugars, thereby promoting hydrolysis and acidogenic pathways. The synergistic effect of Ni2+ and Fe2+ creates a balanced redox environment, optimizing microbial metabolism and growth, while minimizing the formation of inhibitory compounds. This dual metal system directs microbial populations toward H2-producing bacteria, such as Clostridium Thermocellum, and enhances glycolysis and pyruvate conversion, ultimately improving fermentation efficiency and Bio.H2 yields35,58–61. FTIR spectroscopy analysis

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis was carried out to examine the biochemical alterations in the chemical composition of the BOFMSW substrate during DF. This was achieved by analyzing the infrared spectrum before and after the DF process. FTIR was used to study various functional groups present in the BOFMSW substrate before and after AD. The FTIR spectra were obtained using a Perkin-Elmer spectrometer with an absorption wavelength range of 400–4000 cm−1.

When analyzing the chemical composition of substrates using FTIR, the intensity of absorbance increases proportionally with the concentration of either a pure compound or a specific functional group. The results revealed that transmittance rates were lower in substrates post-DF, as they transmitted fewer infrared rays. This reduction occurred due to compositional changes during AD, leading to greater infrared absorption. In BOFMSW containing more organic compounds, distinctive stretching vibrations typically appear in the mid-infrared region, between 4000 and 1500 cm−1. Vibrations occurring below 1500 cm−1, primarily bending modes, form a unique molecular fingerprint that can reveal valuable structural information for identification. Each peak displayed in the graph represents a distinct molecular vibration used to identify functional groups in the BOFMSW substrate. Functional groups consist of specific atomic arrangements within molecules that contribute to chemical transformations. These atoms exhibit characteristic vibrational frequencies, which can be measured through FTIR spectroscopy. The variations observed in the FTIR spectrum before and after DF indicate the biochemical transformations that took place during the process.

FTIR spectroscopy was performed on samples before and after DF for the BOFMSW substrate and the BOFMSW substrate supplemented with bimetallic catalyst, aiming to investigate biochemical changes in both scenarios. The functional group region, positioned on the left side of the spectrum, corresponds to the stretching vibrations of functional groups, whereas the fingerprint region on the right side represents bending vibrations and complex molecular interactions. A comparison of the spectra of the BOFMSW substrate with and without catalytic addition highlights changes in peaks associated with specific functional groups. In both cases, these variations signify the chemical transformations that occurred during DF. A decrease or disappearance of peaks before and after DF indicates the breakdown of complex organic compounds and carbohydrates into simpler substances and nutrients during AD, demonstrating that such changes facilitate Bio.H2 production.

Figures 3, 4, and 5 illustrate FTIR analysis of the BOFMSW substrate before and after DF. The analysis indicates that the O–H stretching vibrations peak of BOFMSW substrate before DF is 3295.06 cm−1, whereas the O–H stretching peaks for BOFMSW substrate supplemented with catalysts of (NiCl2 + FeCl2) and (NiCl2 + FeSO4) after DF are 3309.69 cm−1 and 3309.53 cm−1, respectively. Experimental setup development

The experimental setup for this study was developed as a two-stage continuous stirred anaerobic bioreactor system, with the first stage dedicated to Bio.H2 production and the second stage focused on Bio.CH4 generation. Figure 6 presents the schematic flow diagram of the experimental procedure, whereas the layout of the experimental setup is shown in Fig. 7. The technical specifications and configuration details of the experimental setup are given in Table 5. The H2 and CH4 bioreactors were constructed from stainless steel, with respective capacities of 20 L and 10 L. Each bioreactor was equipped with heaters, temperature sensors, pH sensors, and pressure gauges to regulate and monitor essential parameters such as temperature, pH, and gas pressure. Additionally, level indicators tracked substrate levels, and electrically operated stirrers ensured uniform distribution of nutrients, seed culture, and temperature within the reactors. The experimental setup included designated mechanisms for substrate input, sampling, effluent drainage, and gas collection via a water displacement method. An isolation valve was integrated on the interconnection line between the reactors, allowing for the transfer of anaerobically digested effluent from the H2 bioreactor to the CH4 bioreactor for further Bio.CH4 production. To prevent H2-producing bacteria from being carried over, a UV disinfector was installed along this interconnection line. By incorporating a CH4 bioreactor following a H2 bioreactor, simultaneous production of Bio.H2 and Bio.CH4 from the same substrate was ensured, thereby maximizing resource utilization. Effluents from the H2 bioreactor contain energy-rich organic compounds, including VFA, which can contribute to Bio.CH4 production. Directly releasing these effluents into the environment could lead to unintended anaerobic decomposition, resulting in CH4 emissions that contribute to global warming and soil pollution. Therefore, the experimental setup ensured that the effluents were transferred to the CH4 bioreactor after each process cycle, optimizing energy recovery, enhancing resource efficiency, and promoting sustainable waste utilization.

H2 bioreactor operation

10 L of BOFMSW substrate slurry, already prepared as per “Substrate preparation” section, was fed through the feeding port into the 20 L capacity H2 bioreactor. 500 ml of inoculum containing the seed culture was added to the bioreactor through the substrate feeding port with the help of a sterilized syringe. This was done in a pure anaerobic condition to avoid the reactor from coming into contact with the outside environment. In order to ensure a complete anaerobic condition, the headspace of the reactor was flushed with nitrogen gas for about 15 min, and the lid was tightly sealed using rubber sealing and fastenings. All valves of the reactor were kept in a closed position. In order to facilitate the uniform distribution of the seed culture throughout the substrate, it was stirred gently using an electrically operated stirrer.

The bioreactor was kept under anaerobic conditions for an HRT of 50 days in the absence of oxygen and light, for DF. The pH of the substrate was regularly monitored and maintained at 5.5 by adding sodium hydroxide solution as required; at the same time, the temperature of the reactor was maintained constant at 40 °C by adjusting the temperature controller. Bioreactor operation was continuously monitored, and the operating parameters were closely observed. The substrate was continuously stirred in order to properly mix it and distribute the seed culture and nutrients evenly, ensuring homogenization and optimal growth of microorganisms. During the DF process, the injected microbes, Clostridium Thermocellum grew through continuous nutrient consumption in the substrate. This activity led to substrate degradation and the release of Bio.H2, through enzymatic reactions which occurred in combination with carbon dioxide and organic acids. The gas formation in the reactor was initiated on the 4th day, which was detected by the bourdon tube pressure gauge (Range 0–1 bar, 0.05 bar accuracy) installed on the reactor. The partial pressure of the produced gas was gradually increased in the subsequent days up to 0.3 bar. However, from the 15th day onward, the formation of the gas was faster, and it continued up to the 35th day, and at the 35th day, the pressure in the reactor was elevated to 0.9 bar. To confirm the gas constituents, a sample of gas was collected from the reactor and tested with the help of the H2 detector (Model: GT 8000, range 0–100%, accuracy ± 1%). Before the experiment, the sensor was calibrated in order to ensure the accuracy of the data. This gas sensor operates primarily on the catalytic combustion principle to accurately measure H2 gas percentage. The sensing element of the gas sensor consists of two beads, one is an active bead coated with platinum as a catalytic material, and the other is a reference bead without a catalyst. These two beads are arranged in a Wheatstone bridge circuit for recording the electrical resistance that gives the data about the H2 percentage. During the test, when H2 gas was allowed to pass through the sensor, the H2 molecules were oxidized by the action of platinum and released heat in accordance with the exothermic reaction. The exothermic process raises the temperature and electrical resistance of the active bead relative to the reference bead. The resulting resistance change unbalances the Wheatstone bridge, producing a voltage signal proportional to the H2 concentration. The detector’s onboard electronics amplify and linearize this signal to display the gas concentration in percentage by volume on a digital screen.

After 35 days of substrate digestion, the rate of pressure rise was a bit slower, indicating a complete digestion phase that yielded less gas. This indicates that a fresh substrate feed is needed for the microbes to produce the gas. For that purpose, about 800 mL of digested substrate effluents from the H2 bioreactor were anaerobically transferred to the CH4 bioreactor through the UV disinfector by opening the discharge valve of the H2 bioreactor. At the same time, 800 mL of fresh pretreated substrate containing BOFMSW was added to the H2 bioreactor through the feeding port to supplement nutrients for the growth and survival of the Clostridium Thermocellum microorganisms.

In order to produce gas regularly, continuous feeding was adopted from the 36th day onwards in the H2 bioreactor, and subsequently, digested effluent was transferred from the H2 bioreactor to the CH4 bioreactor for the action of methanogen to the remaining nutrients and converting the BOFMSW to a complete biofertilizer. A similar procedure was followed to operate the reactor while introducing the catalysts into the feedstock substrate, as explained in “Mixed inoculum preparation (Feedstock + seed culture + catalyst)” section. The effect of the catalyst on the Bio.H2 yield has been explained in "Catalyst selection and its properties" section. CH4 bioreactor operation

During the operation of the H2 bioreactor under continuous feeding mode using BOFMSW, 800 mL of digested substrate effluents were transferred daily from the H2 bioreactor to the CH4 bioreactor through a UV disinfector. The UV disinfector emits UV rays to deactivate the H2-forming bacteria in the effluents. The effluent level in the CH4 bioreactor increased daily, and it was allowed to reach up to 50% of the total volume. Then, a culture containing methanogenic microorganisms was added to the CH4 bioreactor for AD and production of biogas from the predigested substrate. Additionally, a provision was also provided for the CH4 bioreactor for external feeding of BOFMSW as and when required to maintain the nutrition level in the reactor for the growth and survival of the methanogens. During the investigation, the CH4 bioreactor was kept under anaerobic conditions. The pH and the temperature were monitored, which were maintained at 6.5 and 40 °C, respectively, considering as an optimal condition62. Since the effluent already contained the metal ion catalysts that were added during the first stage reaction (i.e., H2 bioreactor), this had triggered a higher yield of Bio.CH4 due to the action of supplementation of transition metal salts. The transition of metal salts enhanced the biogas yield primarily by stimulating enzyme-mediated redox reactions involved in hydrogenesis and methanogenesis. The Ni2+ ions stimulate the electron transfer and stabilize the hydrogenase active center, thus improving the acidogenic phase and enhancing Bio.CH4 yield. The Fe2+ ion plays a dual role in electron transport and enzyme activation. Fe2+ triggers the formation of iron-sulfur (Fe–S) clusters and cytochromes, which maintain intracellular redox balance. The combined action of Ni2+ and Fe2+ accelerates the activity of Ni–Fe hydrogenases, thereby enhancing proton reduction and increasing electron flow toward free H2 to form CH4. In addition to this, the action of SO42− influences the ionic strength, thereby providing a soluble sulfate ion that stimulates the sulfate reducing pathway and indirectly triggers the microbial competition in the reactor between methanogens and sulfate reducing bacteria, and promotes microbial synergy, improving biogas yield. After 15 days of digestion, the effluent was removed from the CH4 bioreactor and converted into organic fertilizer. Degradation of BOFMSW

During the digestion process, BOFMSW has undergone various anaerobic actions in which biological degradations were carried out with the action of catalysts and microorganisms in both H2 and CH4 bioreactors. It was observed that, during the biochemical reaction of catalyst-free substrate in the H2 bioreactor, the pH of the substrate was reduced from 4.81 to 4.46. This was due to the formation of organic acids during DF. Turbidity was seen to increase from 431 to 462 NTU, which indicates microbial growth. Total alkalinity was increased from 748 to 783, which was due to the accumulation of organic acids. Total acidity increased from 0.25 to 0.29%, which was due to the formation of VFA and other organic acids during the DF. Ammonia concentration was seen to increase from 125 to 157 mg/L, which was due to the release of nitrogen as a result of the breakdown of proteins. Total carbohydrates were seen to have decreased from 242,000 to 45,017 mg/L, which was due to the consumption of carbohydrates by the microbial community and the conversion of this into products of DF. The total lipid level was seen to decrease from 9418 to 8532 mg/L, due to microbial breakdown into simpler compounds. The protein level was seen to decrease from 20,884 to 9765 mg/L, as it breaks down due to microbial activity. The total fat level decreased slightly from 12,500 to 10,304 mg/L, which was due to its conversion into simpler molecules. Total suspended solids decreased from 113,216 to 38,417 mg/L due to the conversion of suspended solids into Bio.H2, soluble compounds, and carbon dioxide as a result of the breakdown of complex organic matter by the enzymes produced by microorganisms. COD decreased from 46,235 to 24,166 mg/L, indicating that the degradation of the organic matter in the BOFMSW into Bio.H2 and other by-products due to microbial action during the DF. BOD decreased from 19,350 to 11,263 mg/L, which indicates organic matter in the BOFMSW substrates was consumed and degraded by microbial action. The elemental carbon was seen to decrease from 48.35 to 29.48%, which was due to the breakdown of organic matter, in which carbon is a major elemental component. Elemental H2 was seen to increase from 6.21 to 7.43%, which was due to microbial metabolic activity. The nitrogen content was seen to increase from 1.7 to 3.16%, which is mainly due to protein breakdown and ammonia release, combined with carbon loss to gaseous products, which concentrates nitrogen in the residual substrate. Sulfur content decreased from 0.13 to 0.11% due to the conversion of sulfur compounds into hydrogen sulfide. FESEM analysis

Microbial community analysis associated with this investigation was essential to identify the types of microorganisms present, microbial diversity, and their abundance. DF depends on the metabolic activities of a multispecies microbial community. Microbial analysis is pivotal in understanding and optimizing DF for efficient Bio.H2 production, particularly when employing complex substrates like BOFMSW and specialized additives like Clostridium Thermocellum and metal ion catalysts. The addition of metal ion catalysts was intended to enhance the activity of key enzymes involved in Bio.H2 production. Microbial analysis helped to assess the activities of these enzymes, especially hydrogenases and cellulases, for their effectiveness and overall metabolic efficiency. Supplementing microbial communities with metal ion catalysts like (NiCl2 + FeCl2) and (NiCl2 + FeSO4) significantly influenced their growth, metabolism, and community structure. The metal ion combination (NiCl2 + FeCl2) optimized the enzymatic performance, metabolic energy, enhanced microbial growth rate, and biomass yield. (NiCl2 + FeSO4) facilitated in catalytically influencing the microbial activities and supported dominant microbial strains. Microbial analysis enabled the characterization of the microbial community involved in tracking the dominant species of Clostridium Thermocellum, a thermophilic and highly efficient cellulose-degrading bacterium. Microbial analysis facilitated tracking the effective colonization and proliferation of this strain. Clostridium Thermocellum is a key Bio.H2 producer, and its enzymatic potential to degrade lignocellulosic biomass of BOFMSW is crucial. It is known for its powerful cellulase system, and its enzymatic activity is instrumental in determining the functional performance of the DF process. Microbial analysis also helped to assess microbial activities that provided insight into the metabolic pathways involved in the DF process. The analysis facilitated the precise monitoring of the microbial community, the assessment of the effectiveness of additives like Clostridium Thermocellum and metal ion catalysts, and the refinement of process parameters to maximize Bio.H2 yield while minimizing the formation of unwanted byproducts.

Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FESEM) analysis was conducted on the dry BOFMSW substrate sample after DF, using an electron voltage of 2 kV. This analysis aimed to investigate the morphological changes that occur due to microbial activities, the resultant substrate degradation, and the microbial communities involved in DF. The sample for FESEM analysis was collected after the completion of the initial experiment under continuous mode operation, ensuring optimization of the DF process with respect to microbial activity in subsequent studies. FESEM analysis generated detailed images of the surface topology of the digested substrate and a high-resolution image of microbial growth and structures within it, at varying magnification levels ranging from 500 to 25000X. FESEM images reveal clusters of microbial communities responsible for fermentative Bio.H2 production and substrate biodegradation. High-resolution FESEM images distinctly revealed the rod-shaped structure of Clostridium Thermocellum bacteria, which appeared in chain formations and clustered arrangements. This formation is the characteristic of Clostridium species, which often aggregate to enhance enzymatic activity and substrate utilization, thus optimizing Bio.H2 yield during DF. The chain formations suggest active cell division and growth, while the clustered arrangements indicate microbial interactions that facilitate efficient DF. The FESEM images for BOFMSW with and without catalyst showing Clostridium Thermocellum bacterial adhesion is shown in Fig. 8. Surface morphology of the BOFMSW substrate in the FESEM images shows fractured, eroded, and porous layers that reveal the enzymatic hydrolysis and acidogenic activities that occurred due to microbial breakdown of substrate during DF. The FESEM analysis confirmed Clostridium Thermocellum as the dominant microbial community involved in the DF process, playing a crucial role in metabolic activity, hydrolysis of the BOFMSW substrate, and a significant increase in Bio.H2 production.

Results and discussion

Bio.H2 yield with BOFMSW (continuous feeding mode)

Effect of catalyst on cumulative Bio.H2 yield

The Bio.H2 yields, both with and without catalysts, are illustrated in Figs. 9 and 10. It can be observed that with catalyst free BOFMSW substrate, Bio.H2 production commenced on the 4th day onward and showed a gradual increase up to the 35th day. From the 36th day onward, when fresh substrate was introduced daily, gas production increased noticeably compared to previous days. This suggests that continuous feeding accelerates microbial activity due to the consistent and frequent nutrient supplementation from the BOFMSW substrate, promoting higher Bio.H2 yields compared to single-batch feeding. The cumulative maximum Bio.H2 yield recorded under continuous feeding mode was 1871 L, with a peak daily yield of 76 L per day. The H2 content in the gas composition was determined to be 54.71% using a gas detector. The results indicate that continuous substrate feeding maintained the bioreactor in a stable state, an essential condition for sustained Bio.H2 production. This steady-state environment allowed microorganisms to thrive in their optimal growth phase, ensuring persistent microbial activity and enhanced Bio.H2 generation. Moreover, continuous feeding enables more efficient substrate utilization. Unlike batch feeding, where effluents containing valuable organic compounds are discharged into the environment, continuous feeding repurposes these effluents in the CH4 bioreactor, leading to the production of high-energy Bio.CH4 gas. Given these advantages, continuous substrate feeding is considered preferable for enhanced Bio.H2 production.

Combined effect of clostridium thermocellum and metal ion catalysts

The experimental findings reveal that the two-stage bioreactor system incorporating Clostridium Thermocellum enriched seed culture and metal ion catalytic combinations NiCl2 + FeCl2 and NiCl2 + FeSO4 have contributed to a significant and positive effect on the overall yield of Bio.H2.

In the first stage, the efficient breakdown of complex carbohydrates into fermentable sugars was facilitated by the highly specialized, unique enzyme produced by the cellulolytic microorganisms of Clostridium Thermocellum. The key functions of consolidated bioprocessing activities, such as enzyme production, substrate hydrolysis, and DF, were performed by this spore-forming anaerobic bacterium. During metabolism, pyruvate was converted into a highly reactive molecule formed by linking an acetyl group, resulting in the production of reduced ferredoxin. The formation of Bio.H2 was facilitated by this electron-rich ferredoxin by transferring its electrons to a hydrogenase enzyme. During DF stage, the metal ion catalysts, NiCl2 + FeCl2 and NiCl2 + FeSO4, interacted with BOFMSW substrates by functioning as cofactors for key enzymes in the complex microbial metabolism activities. NiCl2-activated hydrogenase enzymes, which are crucial for Bio.H2 generation, whereas FeCl2 and FeSO4 enhanced redox balance and enzyme function, thereby improving the metabolic efficiency. The combination of these metal ions boosted the Bio.H2 yield by synergistic enzymatic activation and a robust catalytic environment. Ni2+ acted as an essential cofactor and key component for hydrogenase enzymes, and enhanced electron transfer during DF. Fe2+ is involved as a cofactor which was an essential constituent of the active site of hydrogenase enzymes. These enzymes were responsible for the reduction of protons into molecular H2. Adequate concentration of Fe2+ was an essential factor for the proper functioning of these hydrogenase enzymatic activities. Formation of Iron-sulfur clusters in the presence of Fe2+ has catalyzed an efficient electron flow towards the generation of Bio.H2. Fe2+ enhanced the microbial activity and electron transfer by regulating the metabolic pathways for efficient Bio.H2 production. Ni2+ and Fe2+ acted as cofactors for enzymes involved in glycolysis, the anaerobic metabolic pathway, where glucose was broken down into pyruvate. The presence of these ions enhanced the complex enzymes produced by Clostridium Thermocellum and degraded the cellulose effectively. The synergetic effect of Ni2+ and Fe2+ resulted in a balanced catalytic effect, thereby an efficient conversion of complex carbohydrates in the BOFMSW substrates into Bio.H2 was achieved. Bio.H2 production was catalyzed by Fe2+ through hydrogenase activation, whereas Ni2+ performed as a cofactor for key enzymes, thus ensuring efficient metabolism. The metallic ion combination facilitated the efficient breakdown of the substrate during the initial phase of the DF process with the combined support of Ni2+and Fe2+ by its hydrogenase activities. Experimental findings show that the combined influence of Clostridium Thermocellum, which facilitated efficient degradation of the complex BOFMSW substrate, and the metal ion catalysts, which effectively enhanced hydrogenase enzyme activities, resulted in a significant increase in Bio.H2 yield.

In the second stage, the digested substrate containing VFA and other intermediaries from the first stage, following Bio.H2 production, was converted into Bio.CH4 by methanogenic microorganisms. The metal ion catalysts added in the first stage were also catalytically influenced in this methanogenic stage. Ni2+ and Fe2+ ions played a crucial role as micronutrients for methanogenic activities. The combined effect of these ions enhanced the growth and activities of methanogens, resulting in an efficient conversion of VFA into Bio.CH4.Fe2+ played the role of enzyme cofactor in pathways of methanogenesis activities for converting VFA into Bio.CH4. Efficient electron transfer during the process of syntrophic metabolism was facilitated by Fe-containing proteins.

The observations revealed that an enhanced Bio.H2 yield was obtained in the first stage by conversion of BOFMSW substrate with specific seed culture and addition of metal ion catalysts. An efficient breakdown of complex substrate in this stage facilitated the production of necessary resources for second stage microbial community. Efficient conversion of VFA into Bio.CH4 was achieved in the second stage as a result of the continued action of catalysts, supporting the growth of methanogens. The selected metal ions acted as essential micronutrients and cofactors enabling the highly efficient functioning of the complex microbial consortium, thereby enhancing the yield of both Bio.H2 and Bio.CH4.

The second-stage methanogenic reactor exhibited a high Bio.CH4 production rate, indicating rapid conversion of fermentation intermediates produced during DF. Evaluation of overall process performance and efficiency demonstrates the superior effectiveness of two stage phase-separated digestion relative to conventional single stage DF systems.

Conclusion

A sustainable conversion of MSW into high-energy-content gaseous fuels through a two-stage integrated DF-AD process was rendered viable in this research study. This study establishes BOFMSW as a viable feedstock for the simultaneous production of Bio.H2 and Bio.CH4. The first-stage bioreactor, dedicated to Bio.H2 production, leverages a seed culture of Clostridium Thermocellum enriched in BESA medium, sourced from digestate slurry. The synergy between Clostridium Thermocellum enriched seed culture and metal ion catalysts enhanced the substrate hydrolysis and energy yields, validating an integrated and scalable route for sustainable waste-to-energy transformation. The strategic use of bimetallic ion catalyst combination NiCl2 + FeCl2 significantly enhanced Bio.H2 yield, achieving a 69% improvement at its optimal concentration of 75 mg/L, compared to catalyst free BOFMSW substrate. Following Bio.H2 generation, the residual digested substrate was effectively reutilized in the second-stage reactor for Bio.CH4 production, ensuring maximum energy recovery from the municipal solid waste. A 59% enhancement in Bio.CH4 yield was achieved by utilizing the digested substrate containing NiCl2 + FeCl2 at its optimal concentration of 50 mg/L. The achievement of an overall COD removal efficiency of 93.18%, clearly demonstrates the improved process performance. Additionally, the final digested substrate was evaluated for its nutrient profile, and its favorable NPK values confirmed its suitability to be used as a biofertilizer, supporting agricultural sustainability and closing the loop in a circular bioeconomy model. This sequential approach not only improves energy conversion but also ensures effective resource utilization. By transforming MSW into biofuels and agricultural inputs, this approach mitigates waste disposal concerns while promoting environmental sustainability. The findings reaffirm the feasibility of integrating waste-to-energy and waste-to-resource frameworks, contributing to a circular economy where MSW is harnessed for renewable energy and soil enrichment.

This integration of waste-to-energy conversion aligns with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals by promoting renewable energy generation, addressing environmental concerns, enhancing resource efficiency, promoting clean energy, and reducing dependency on fossil fuels. Research findings point to the prospect of large-scale Bio.H2 production and effective Bio.CH4 conversion, endorsing the feasibility of a dual-stage bioreactor system in moving forward with renewable energy solutions. The findings from this study also emphasize the potential of DF as a scalable and eco-friendly biotechnology for sustainable waste management, contributing to global efforts in transitioning towards cleaner energy solutions.

Future scope

Future research could focus on further advancing and refining microbial strain particularly Clostridium Thermocellum, while simultaneously optimizing affordable metal ion catalysts to enhance substrate conversion efficiency and to maximize Bio.H2 and Bio.CH4 yields. Scaling the capacities to industrial-scale levels is essential to validate performance, refinement, and improvement in reactor configurations to establish operational feasibility in municipal solid waste valorization. Integration with hybrid waste-to-energy systems, such as coupling DF with advanced microbial fuel cell (MFC) technology or microbial electrolysis, could further boost energy recovery. In addition, systematic evaluation of the digested substrate as biofertilizer through nutrient profiling and extended field experiments will establish its agricultural utility. Finally, the integrated technical and economic analysis and life cycle assessment should be undertaken to determine sustainability, cost-effectiveness, and commercialization potential, ensuring that the process is viable for large-scale municipal solid waste management and renewable energy generation.
