Unlocking the potential of gold nanoparticles with insights into green synthesis, mechanisms, characterization and multifunctional applications
Authors: Hansa Gul, Nasir Assad, Zahida Nasreen, Maria kanwal, Muhammad Nauman Khan, Ahmed Vandy, Muhammad Naeem-ul-Hassan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gold nanop articles (AuNPs) have garnered significant attention in biomedical research owing to their unique physicochemical properties, including biocompatibility, tunable stability, and versatile surface chemistries. However, conventional physical and chemical synthesis methods often depend on hazardous reagents and energy-intensive conditions, raising environmental and health concerns regarding their use. Green synthesis has emerged as a promising eco-friendly alternative, employing biological entities such as plants, bacteria, fungi, and algae as natural biofactories. This review explores advances in the biogenic synthesis of AuNPs and outlines the mechanisms of bioreduction and capping mediated by diverse biomolecules. A detailed overview of characterization techniques, including spectroscopy and microscopy, is provided to demonstrate the relationship between nanoparticle properties and their biomedical efficacy. The multifunctional applications of green-synthesized AuNPs are examined, with an emphasis on their antibacterial, antifungal, antiviral, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties. Mechanistic insights, including membrane disruption, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, DNA damage, and apoptosis induction, are discussed. Finally, this review highlights the challenges hindering large-scale production and clinical translation, including the need for standardized protocols and mechanistic understanding, while offering perspectives on future research directions. These findings establish a foundation for innovations in nanomedicine, underscoring the importance of bio-based synthesis for safe and effective healthcare solutions.

Introduction

Nanotechnology has emerged as one of the most rapidly evolving fields in modern science, providing innovative solutions across diverse sectors, including medicine [1], energy [2], agriculture [3], catalysis [4], and environmental management [5]. Recent advancements have highlighted nanotechnology as a “consumer-centric” revolution, offering sustainable opportunities for disease identification and global health improvement [6, 7]. Nanomaterials are broadly categorized based on their composition and dimensionality, including carbon-based [8], polymeric structures such as nanosponges [9, 10], lipid-based [11], and metallic structures. Among the diverse range of engineered nanomaterials, gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) hold a unique position because of their exceptional physicochemical properties. These include a high surface-to-volume ratio, tunable optical characteristics such as surface plasmon resonance, and remarkable chemical stability [12–14]. These attributes make AuNPs highly versatile in applications ranging from biosensing and drug delivery to imaging and therapeutics [15, 16]. AuNPs exhibit remarkable catalytic and biological activities compared to bulk gold, sparking significant interest in both fundamental research and translational medicine [17, 18].

Conventional synthesis employs chemical reducing agents (e.g., citrate, NaBH₄) or physical methods (laser ablation, thermal decomposition). These methods offer precise control; however, they pose toxicity, environmental, and scalability limitations [19]. Green synthesis utilizes biological systems, such as plants, bacteria, fungi, and algae, as natural biofactories, providing both reducing and capping agents under mild conditions [18, 20, 21]. However, these methods are energy-intensive, often require organic solvents, and employ toxic reducing or stabilizing agents. The use of sodium borohydride, hydrazine, and surfactants introduces the risk of environmental pollution and cytotoxicity, thereby restricting their biomedical applicability [22, 23]. Furthermore, scaling up such methods is not always economically feasible or sustainable. These limitations have accelerated the search for alternative synthetic strategies that combine efficiency, reproducibility, and environmental safety [24].

In this context, green synthesis has emerged as a powerful paradigm in nanotechnology [25, 26]. By harnessing biological entities, such as plants, bacteria, fungi, and algae, researchers have developed eco-friendly routes for producing AuNPs under mild conditions [6, 27]. These bioreactors supply reducing biomolecules, including phenolics, flavonoids, alkaloids, terpenoids, sugars, proteins, and enzymes, which not only reduce Au3+ ions to Au0 but also act as natural capping agents, thereby enhancing colloidal stability and functionalization [28–30]. For example, variations in metabolite composition—such as high flavonoid content versus terpenoid-rich extracts—can lead to the formation of anisotropic structures, such as nanorods, triangles, and decahedra. This highlights that biological systems are not merely passive reducing agents but active determinants of nanoparticle architecture and functionality [31–33].

Despite significant progress, a major challenge in green synthesis is the lack of reproducibility and standardization. Variability in biological composition, influenced by factors such as plant species, extraction method, pH, temperature, and precursor concentration, leads to inconsistent NPs characteristics [11]. This batch-to-batch variability limits the ability to scale up production and hampers direct comparisons between studies. Furthermore, the absence of quantitative comparative analyses between green-and conventionally synthesized AuNPs remains a critical gap in the literature.

Recent high-impact reviews (2023–2025) have extensively summarized the synthesis strategies and applications of AuNPs; however, they remain largely descriptive and lack an integrated critical analysis of mechanistic pathways, biological system efficiency, and structure–activity relationships. In particular, limited attention has been paid to identifying which biological systems provide optimal control over nanoparticle properties and the reasons thereof [34]. Additionally, the interplay between capping biomolecules and biological activity, as well as the transformation of nanoparticle identity through protein corona formation in physiological environments, remains underexplored.

The biomedical potential of green-synthesized AuNPs is vast and well documented. Studies have reported antibacterial and antifungal activities mediated by mechanisms such as cell wall disruption, ROS generation, membrane depolarization, and interference with DNA replication [35, 36]. Antiviral properties of AuNPs arise from blocking viral entry, disrupting membrane potentials, and inhibiting nucleic acid synthesis, with promising results against pathogens such as HIV, HSV, and influenza [20]. Perhaps most strikingly, anticancer applications of AuNPs have demonstrated selective cytotoxicity toward cancer cell lines, often via mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS induction, and caspase-mediated apoptosis [37]. These multifunctional therapeutic properties not only highlight the value of green AuNPs as broad-spectrum agents but also reduce the likelihood of resistance development compared to conventional drugs [38]. Emerging evidence also suggests the involvement of reactive nitrogen species (RNS) and immune-modulatory pathways, further expanding the therapeutic scope of AuNPs. However, the formation of protein corona in biological systems can significantly alter nanoparticle behavior, affecting targeting efficiency, toxicity, and overall therapeutic performance.

In the past ten years, green synthesis has been a widely used approach towards the production of AuNPs that can take special morphologies and have exclusive biomedical applications [39]. Nonetheless, a number of obstacles persist to limit large-scale translation despite the fact that it is eco-friendlier and has an interdisciplinary appeal [40]. The primary challenge lies in the difficulty of controlling the size, shape, and uniformity of NPs. Variations in synthesis conditions and metabolites between different plant or microbial sources often lead to the formation of heterogeneous nanoparticle populations. Standard protocols are also lacking, which hinders comparative studies and reproducibility [41]. Mechanistic insights have also been incomplete, with the exact metabolites involved being unclear so that they cannot be predicted, synthesized or produced in a controlled manner [42].

This review aims to fill this gap by providing a comprehensive and unified overview of AuNPs synthesis through biological routes. The review systematically compares conventional and green synthesis methods, focusing on the mechanisms of bioreduction and stabilization. It explores the key biological resources used in nanoparticle synthesis and examines characterization techniques that establish structure–function relationships. Additionally, this review highlights key challenges, including standardization, scalability, and toxicological considerations, such as protein corona formation and bioaccumulation. By integrating recent advancements and identifying unresolved questions, this work seeks to provide a more structured and critical framework for advancing the field of green-synthesized AuNPs toward clinical and industrial applications. Recent bibliometric analyses further demonstrate the rapid expansion and diversification of AuNP-related research across multiple interdisciplinary domains, including green synthesis, drug delivery, biosensing, anticancer therapy, antimicrobial applications, toxicology, and theranostics. Synthesis of gold nanoparticles

Several methods have been developed to synthesize AuNPs, thereby allowing control over their physicochemical properties. AuNPs can be synthesized using top-down or bottom-up methods. The top-down approach begins with bulk materials, which are broken down into nanoscale particles through physical, chemical, or mechanical processes.

These include the Turkevich, Brust, seed-mediated growth, biological, and sonochemical methods. The Turkevich method, introduced in 1951, is one of the most widely used techniques for synthesizing spherical AuNPs, typically ranging in size from 1 to 20 nm [44]. In contrast, the Brust–Schiffrin method (1994) utilizes a two-phase system to produce smaller, thiol-stabilized NPs (1.5–5 nm); however, it requires the use of toxic organic solvents, such as toluene [45].

AuNPs can be synthesized using either top-down or bottom-up methods. The top-down approach begins with bulk materials, which are broken down into nanoscale particles through physical, chemical, or mechanical processes [46]. This is simple, but it is hard to generate very small, well-defined NPs and potentially distort surface chemistry [47]. Conversely, the bottom-up strategy builds NPS starting from atoms or small molecular building blocks, leading to better control over size and morphology. This strategy can be physical, chemical, or biological in nature [48, 49]. Recent comprehensive reviews have further categorized these methods based on their operational processes, highlighting how interdisciplinary collaboration drives progress while addressing scalability and reproducibility challenges [50].

Conventional synthesis methods

Conventional synthesis methods for AuNPs primarily involve chemical and physical techniques that have been widely employed due to their effectiveness in controlling particle size, shape, and yield [51, 52]. Among chemical approaches, the Turkevich method (using trisodium citrate as a reducing and capping agent) and the Brust–Schiffrin method (using thiol ligands and sodium borohydride) are the most established protocols [53]. These methods reduce gold ions (Au3+) into elemental gold (Au0) under controlled conditions, leading to nucleation and growth of NPs [54, 55]. While these methods provide excellent control over morphology and monodispersity, they often rely on toxic chemicals, organic solvents, high temperatures, or strong reducing agents like NaBH₄ and hydrazine, which present environmental and biological risks [56]. A significant technical drawback of chemical synthesis is the “chemical footprint” left by surfactants, such as cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) [57]. These ligands often remain adsorbed to the NP surface, leading to inherent cytotoxicity that limits their direct clinical application without exhaustive purification steps.

Physical methods, such as laser ablation, ultrasonication, thermal decomposition, and microwave irradiation, can also be used to synthesize AuNPs without chemical reducing agents. However, these methods often demand sophisticated equipment, high energy input, and are less accessible for large-scale or biomedical applications [18].

Despite their precision and reproducibility, conventional synthesis routes face challenges including limited biocompatibility, residual toxicity, and environmental burden, thereby motivating the shift toward green and biogenic synthesis approaches that utilize naturally derived materials and ambient conditions [24] (Fig. 1).

A comparative assessment of these pathways reveals that while conventional chemical methods remain the ‘gold standard’ for strict monodispersity and geometric reproducibility, they are limited by the ‘chemical footprint’ left by toxic surfactants, such as CTAB. As indicated in Table 1, biogenic synthesis offers a paradigm shift by utilizing the ‘metabolic complexity’ of organisms [58, 59]. These natural capping agents not only stabilize AuNPs but also impart intrinsic biological identities—such as antioxidant or antimicrobial properties–that chemical ligands cannot replicate without complex secondary functionalization. However, one of the major challenges of green synthesis is reproducibility. Variations in plant species, geographical origin, harvest season, extraction method, metabolite composition, precursor concentration, pH, temperature, and reaction time can significantly influence nanoparticle size, morphology, surface chemistry, and biological activity. Consequently, identical synthesis protocols may produce AuNPs with different physicochemical properties in different laboratories. Improving reproducibility requires standardized extraction procedures, quantitative metabolite profiling, controlled reaction parameters, and comprehensive physicochemical characterization to ensure consistent nanoparticle quality and facilitate clinical translation.

Mechanistic distinction of AuNPs from other nanoparticle systems

Compared with widely studied NPs systems such as AgNPs, ZnO, TiO₂, iron oxide, and silica NPs, AuNPs exhibit distinct mechanistic advantages arising from their chemical inertness, localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR), tunable surface functionalization, and relatively lower intrinsic cytotoxicity. AgNPs, for example, exert antimicrobial activity mainly through rapid Ag⁺ ion release and strong oxidative stress, which can increase nonspecific toxicity toward mammalian cells [60]. In contrast, AuNPs are less prone to uncontrolled ion release under physiological conditions and therefore generally show greater physicochemical stability and biocompatibility. Similarly, ZnO and TiO₂ NPs rely largely on photocatalytic ROS generation, whereas AuNPs interact with biological systems through broader non-photocatalytic mechanisms, including membrane interaction, protein binding, enzyme modulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, photothermal conversion, and surface-mediated signaling. In addition, the surface chemistry of AuNPs allows efficient conjugation with biomolecules, drugs, peptides, antibodies, and nucleic acids through thiol, amine, and electrostatic interactions, making them especially useful for targeted delivery and theranostic applications [59]. Their LSPR behavior further supports optical imaging, biosensing, photothermal therapy, and SERS-based detection. Therefore, the biomedical relevance of AuNPs arises from a unique combination of plasmonic behavior, surface tunability, biological stability, and multifunctional therapeutic capability rather than nanoscale size alone [61].

Biogenic synthesis methods

The biogenic or “green” synthesis of AuNPs has emerged as a sustainable and environmentally friendly alternative to conventional physical and chemical methods. Unlike traditional approaches that often require high energy inputs or toxic reducing agents, biogenic synthesis harnesses natural biological resources, such as plants, bacteria, fungi, and algae, as bio-factories for NP production. Organisms like bacteria, fungi, and algae utilize their inherent metabolic machinery to reduce Au³⁺ ions and stabilize the resulting NPs, often in a single, environmentally benign step [62]. These organisms provide a rich repertoire of metabolites, including phenolics, flavonoids, terpenoids, polysaccharides, proteins, and enzymes, which simultaneously act as reducing and stabilizing agents. This not only facilitates NP formation under mild, aqueous conditions but also improves their biocompatibility by imparting biologically derived surface coatings [63]. The diversity of biological systems employed offers wide control over particle size, shape, stability, and functionality, while often conferring additional therapeutic properties.

General mechanism of biogenic AuNPs synthesis

The biosynthesis of AuNPs is a relatively simple, eco-friendly process that generally occurs under ambient conditions, without the need for elevated temperature or pressure. Typically, a biological extract derived from bacteria, fungi, algae, or plants is added dropwise into an aqueous solution of chloroauric acid (HAuCl4), were mixing initiates NPs formation. The appearance of a characteristic color change serves as a visual indicator of AuNP synthesis [64]. Although biogenic AuNP synthesis has traditionally been described using a simplified two-step model consisting of bioreduction followed by stabilization (capping), recent mechanistic studies indicate that these processes are often simultaneous, overlapping, and kinetically interconnected [65]. Therefore, the classical two-step mechanism should be considered a simplified conceptual framework rather than a strictly sequential process. In many biological systems, reduction, nucleation, growth, and surface capping occur concurrently, with biomolecules participating dynamically throughout NPs formation.

Nucleation, growth, and stabilization (capping)

The reduced Au0 atoms nucleate to form clusters, which grow into nanoscale particles. Simultaneously, biomolecules cap the forming AuNPs, providing colloidal stability and preventing aggregation The role of capping biomolecules extends far beyond simple NPs stabilization. Biomolecules such as flavonoids, phenolic acids, proteins, polysaccharides, terpenoids, alkaloids, and enzymes adsorb onto the AuNP surface through hydroxyl, carbonyl, amine, sulfhydryl, and carboxyl functional groups, thereby generating steric and electrostatic barriers that prevent NPs aggregation. This capping layer directly influences colloidal stability, hydrodynamic size, zeta potential, and dispersion behavior under physiological conditions. Importantly, the nature of the biomolecular corona also determines cellular interaction, protein adsorption, immune recognition, and biological activity. For example, polyphenol-rich coatings may enhance antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity through free radical scavenging, whereas protein- or polysaccharide-based capping can improve biocompatibility, serum stability, and drug-loading efficiency. Similarly, specific phytochemicals may modulate membrane interaction, ROS generation, and apoptotic signaling, thereby influencing antibacterial and anticancer efficacy. Therefore, capping biomolecules should not be considered passive stabilizers; rather, they function as active determinants of NPs biological identity, therapeutic performance, and biosafety [65–68]. Although the green synthesis of AuNPs has traditionally been described as a sequential process involving bioreduction, nucleation, growth, and subsequent capping, recent mechanistic studies indicate that these events frequently occur simultaneously or partially overlap rather than proceeding as discrete steps. During nanoparticle formation, biomolecules present in the biological extract can simultaneously reduce Au³⁺ ions, promote nucleation, adsorb onto the nanoparticle surface, and stabilize the growing particles. This dynamic process is better explained by nucleation–growth kinetic models, such as the LaMer model, in which reduction kinetics, supersaturation, nucleation, particle growth, and biomolecule adsorption are interconnected. Consequently, the classical stepwise mechanism should be regarded as a simplified conceptual framework, whereas the actual biosynthesis process involves continuous and overlapping physicochemical and biochemical events whose extent depends on the composition of the biological extract and reaction conditions.

Advanced mechanistic insights

Recent literature suggests that AuNP formation is not always a sequential two-step process. In hybrid systems involving Polyoxometalates (POMs), the reduction of Au³⁺ can occur through in-situ photoreduction, where the capping agent itself is activated by light to transfer electrons to the gold ions. Furthermore, we must distinguish between kinetic stabilization (where capping happens during growth) and post-synthetic ligand exchange, where labile molecules like citrates are displaced by stronger, multi-dentate inorganic clusters to enhance long-term stability [69].

The following subsections critically discuss the mechanisms of plant, bacteria, fungi, and algae-mediated synthesis, along with their advantages and clinical potential [70, 71].

Plant-mediated synthesis and mechanism

A safe, sustainable, scalable synthesis of AuNPs is through plant synthesis, a one-pot, one-step reaction. Aqueous extraction is followed by the use of different parts of plants, such as leaves, fruits, bark, flowers, seeds, and roots, etc. [72]. This extract is then mixed with a solution of a gold salt (HAuCl 4 ) in which a color change is generally observed in a few minutes to hours, indicating the formation of NPs [73]. Plant-mediated synthesis, in contrast to chemical methods, has a benefit of the Circular Bioeconomy in that agricultural waste is used as biofactories, which radically lowers the cost of high-purity reagents [74].

Bioactive phytochemicals, which are naturally reducing and stabilizing agents, are abundant in plants, the alkaloids, flavonoids, saponins, steroids, and tannins. Most importantly, these metabolites are so-called Shape-Directing Agents. Further examination shows that the geometry is determined by the ratio of particular phytochemicals: extracts with high flavonoid-to-terpenoid ratios prefer to form spherical particles as they are uniformly passivated on the surface and extracts with high terpenoid concentrations prefer to form triangles and decahedrals as they selectively bind to the facets of the gold crystal [75].

The biochemical process involves two-electron reduction of Au3+ to Au0 and is mainly accomplished by hydroxyl and glucoside groups of phenolics such as salicin. Although older models indicated a two-step process (reduction and capping), kinetic studies indicate that these two processes are concurrent and overlapping, and that the biological cap is built even as the gold nuclei are growing, which would provide excellent colloidal stability relative to citrate-capped AuNPs [76]. Indicatively, Equisetum diffusum produces stable spherical AuNPs (~ 69 nm) with effective antidiabetic activity [77]. Together, this array of metabolites not only leads to NP synthesis but also confers a unique “Biological Identity” with effects on cellular uptake through a mechanism called Protein Corona modulation [78].

Fungi-mediated synthesis and mechanism

Fungi are excellent candidates for large-scale production of metallic NPs because of their simplicity, high scalability, cost-efficiency, and ease of downstream processing. Their filamentous growth allows for high biomass yield, while their cell walls contain functional groups such as amine, carboxyl, sulfhydryl, hydroxyl, and phosphate that act as ligands to chelate metal ions and promote reduction. Fungi also serve as efficient platforms for large-scale, low-cost, and eco-friendly synthesis of AuNPs owing to their filamentous growth, which ensures high biomass yield. In addition, fungal systems secrete a range of reductive proteins and enzymes, such as ATPase, β-1,3-glucanase, hemicellulases, and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, that facilitate NPs formation [79]. Several fungal species, including Penicillium chrysogenum, Fusarium oxysporum, and Verticillium sp., have been shown to mediate the biosynthesis of diverse metallic NPs, notably those of platinum, silver, and titanium. [80]. Mechanistically, the reduction of Au³⁺ to Au⁰ is primarily driven by electron transfer from Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide (NADH) and NADPH-dependent oxidoreductases. Recent studies have identified that flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD)-dependent glutathione reductase plays a pivotal role in this transformation [81, 82]. Examples of unique species that are used to produce highly stable or DNA-decorated NPs with promising biomedical applications include Alternaria sp [83] , Colletotrichum sp., and Penicillium rugulosum [84, 85]. Yeasts, particularly Yarrowia lipolytica and Candida albicans, have been reported to synthesize spherical extracellular AuNPs, offering advantages for bioimaging, food safety, and environmental applications.[86, 87] (Table 2). A critical advantage of this biogenic route is the high level of protein retention, which leads to the development of a “pre-formed” Protein Corona. Mechanistically, proteins and enzymes utilize Van der Waals forces to adhere to the gold surface or alternatively attach through bonds between sulfur and nitrogen atoms within the protein structure [82]. This intrinsic functionalization establishes the nanoparticle’s biological identity and significantly increases biocompatibility for drug delivery applications [88]. The protein retention in fungal extracts is quite high, and thus, the protein Corona is formed pre-formed to increase biocompatibility with drug delivery applications [89, 90].

Bacterial synthesis and mechanism

The ability of bacteria to efficiently utilize their metabolism and strong enzymes has made them efficient biofactories in the synthesis of AuNPs. The Streptomyces species are also among them and are highly appreciated due to their rich secondary metabolism. AuNPs (10–50 nm) of spherical and triangular shapes can be synthesized by Streptomyces sp.[102]11(Table 3). Mechanistically, bacteria can produce AuNPs through extracellular and intracellular mechanisms, the adsorption of gold onto cell surfaces has been reported [103] or secreted enzymes, reduce Au(III) to Au(0), mainly with the aid of NADPH-dependent reductases, including nitrate reductase [104]. Similar enzymatic mechanisms have been demonstrated in various bacterial systems such as Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Rhodopseudomonas capsulata, Pseudomonas putida, and Pseudomonas fluorescens [62, 105]. Proteolytic enzymes have additionally been proposed to contribute to NPs formation in some Actinobacteria, indicating a supplementary role alongside reductive metabolic pathways. Intracellularly, the Au 3+ ions traverse bacterial cellular barriers through ion gates or carriers, and the bio-slender organic cytoplasmic enzymes, proteins, antioxidants, sugar, and amino acids, reducing and stabilizing the NPs. NADH-dependent enzymes and intracellular antioxidants play key roles in generating stable and monodispersed AuNP, as exemplified by the case of Deinococcus radiodurans and Lactobacillus kimchius, which are non-pathogenic [106–108].

AuNPs have also been synthesized using gram-negative bacteria strains. In previous studies, Shewanella oneidensis was reported to reduce 1 mM HAuCl₄ in an extracellular environment at 30 °C within 48 h, resulting in the formation of spherical AuNPs with an average size of approximately 12.5 nm. The biogenic AuNPs further exhibited potent bactericidal activity against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus [109, 110]. Conversely, intracellular biosynthesis of irregularly shaped AuNPs (50–100 nm) by Vibrio alginolyticus at 40 °C within 24 h yielded biogenic AuNPs with pronounced cytotoxic activity against human colon adenocarcinoma (HCA-7) cells, demonstrating the therapeutic relevance of bacterial systems in nanoparticle-mediated cancer therapy [111]. One important analytical distinction between bacterial and plant-derived AuNPs is that of the cell wall. The gold surface adsorbed onto the negatively charged cell surface in bacteria serves as a primary nucleation site, and octahedral or cubic structures can be grown precisely as they are not readily synthesized by plants [112]. In addition, non-pathogenic strains such as Lactobacillus kimchius use NADH-dependent pathways to produce stable, biocompatible AuNPs that can be used directly as probiotics to deliver drugs [106–108].

Algae-mediated synthesis and mechanism

The algae have become the sustainable biofactories in the synthesis of AuNPs, as they have a wide variety of biomolecular structures, including sulfated polysaccharides, pigments, and proteins. Algae are preferred over land plants because their biomass growth is ten times faster and they offer unique “Chemical Repertoires” like fucoidans and alginates that prevent NP aggregation in high-salinity biological environments [120, 121].

The reaction mechanism is based on the reduction of the Au3+ ions to the elemental Au0 in a probable occurrence in the mitochondrial membrane [122] with the help of functional groups in the algal biomass. Investigation on Chlorella vulgaris suggests that upon interaction with Au³⁺ ions, an initial reduction to Au0 occurs, followed by coordination with sulfur-bearing residues, facilitating nucleation and stabilization of AuNPs [22]. Fucoidan extracted from Turbinaria decurrens facilitated the synthesis of predominantly spherical AuNPs (4.5–59 nm) that demonstrated robust antioxidant capacity and selective cytotoxicity against HepG2 liver cancer cells [123]. The best stability has been optimized in other species such as Galaxaura elongata (3.85–77.13 nm) [124] and Tetra Stoechospermum marginatum (18.7–93.7 nm) [125]. Comprehensive mechanistic understanding shows the sulfonated polysaccharides in genera such as Turbinaria conoides guide anisotropic growth via a facet-selective binding which compels triangles or stars [86]. A summary of these various algal species and their ROS-mediated therapeutic capabilities is presented in Table 4. Overall, algae-mediated synthesis provides a sustainable and biologically rich route for AuNP fabrication, where sulfated polysaccharides, pigments, proteins, and other algal metabolites contribute to Au³⁺ reduction, NPs stabilization, morphology control, and biological activity. However, further standardization of algal species selection, extraction conditions, reaction parameters, and characterization metrics is required to improve reproducibility and enable reliable biomedical translation. Although plants, fungi, bacteria, algae, and other biological systems can all successfully synthesize AuNPs, their suitability depends on the intended biomedical application. Plant extracts are particularly attractive for antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer applications because they are rich in polyphenols, flavonoids, and other phytochemicals that remain associated with the nanoparticle surface and may enhance biological activity. Fungal systems generally provide higher nanoparticle yields owing to abundant extracellular enzymes and proteins, making them advantageous for large-scale production. Bacterial synthesis offers precise control through defined metabolic pathways but raises concerns regarding endotoxins and purification. Algal systems produce biocompatible NPs enriched with polysaccharides and pigments, which may improve stability and antioxidant properties, although productivity is often species-dependent. Therefore, the choice of biological source should be guided not only by synthesis efficiency but also by the desired physicochemical properties, therapeutic application, scalability, and biosafety.

Optimization parameters and shape control mechanisms

The reproducibility and precision of biogenic AuNP synthesis are governed by the combined influence of pH, temperature, precursor concentration, reaction time, biological extract composition, and capping behavior. These variables determine the reduction kinetics of Au³⁺ to Au⁰, nucleation density, growth rate, surface stabilization, and NPs morphology [59].

A critical determinant of shape evolution is facet-selective binding. Capping biomolecules do not bind uniformly to the gold surface. Instead, metabolites such as flavonoids, phenolics, terpenoids, proteins, polysaccharides, or other functional ligands may selectively adsorb onto specific crystal planes, such as the (111) or (100) facets. This selective masking restricts growth on certain planes while allowing gold atoms to deposit on unprotected surfaces, leading to anisotropic structures such as triangles, hexagons, rods, stars, or truncated particles. Such anisotropic AuNPs are particularly important for plasmonic applications, including SERS-based biosensing, because sharp edges and tips generate localized electromagnetic “hot spots.” Among synthesis parameters, pH is one of the most influential regulators of morphology. [69, 133]. Under acidic conditions, the reduction rate is generally slower, producing fewer nucleation sites and allowing larger or anisotropic particles to develop. In contrast, alkaline conditions promote deprotonation of phenolic and hydroxyl groups, accelerate Au³⁺ reduction, and produce a rapid nucleation burst, usually resulting in smaller and more spherical particles. Temperature acts as a kinetic accelerator; increasing temperature enhances reduction and nucleation rates, often producing smaller particles with improved uniformity. However, excessive temperature may destabilize biological capping molecules and alter surface chemistry [18, 134].

Precursor concentration must also be balanced against the reducing capacity of the biological extract. High HAuCl₄ concentration can promote secondary growth, aggregation, or polydispersity if insufficient capping molecules are available, whereas lower precursor concentrations often favor controlled nucleation and uniform particle formation. Reaction time further contributes to shape evolution: early-stage nuclei may develop into anisotropic structures, while prolonged reaction periods can promote Ostwald ripening, aggregation, or transformation toward more thermodynamically stable morphologies [134, 135].

Reproducibility remains a central limitation in translating green AuNP synthesis from laboratory-scale preparation to standardized biomedical production. Unlike chemical synthesis, where reagent purity, stoichiometry, and reaction kinetics can be tightly controlled, biogenic synthesis depends on complex and variable biological matrices. Differences in species, geographical origin, seasonal collection, plant part, microbial strain, growth conditions, extraction solvent, extract concentration, pH, temperature, reaction time, and precursor-to-extract ratio can alter the availability of phenolics, flavonoids, proteins, polysaccharides, enzymes, and other reducing or capping molecules [136]. These variations directly affect reduction kinetics, nucleation density, capping efficiency, particle size, morphology, crystallinity, surface charge, and ultimately biological activity [137, 138].

Therefore, reproducible green synthesis requires more than reporting color change or UV-vis confirmation. It requires standardized extraction procedures, quantitative profiling of major reducing and capping biomolecules, controlled reaction parameters, and batch-wise validation using integrated characterization techniques such as UV-vis, FTIR, TEM/SEM, XRD, DLS, and zeta potential analysis. Design of Experiments-based optimization and predictive modeling may further improve batch-to-batch consistency and support scalable, regulatory-compatible production of biogenic AuNPs.

Characterization techniques for biogenic AuNPs: an analytical perspective

The characterization of green-synthesized AuNPs requires an interpretive approach that links physicochemical features with biological performance. In biogenic systems, characterization is not limited to confirming NPs formation. It must explain how particle size, morphology, crystallinity, surface chemistry, hydrodynamic behavior, and protein corona formation influence stability, cellular uptake, toxicity, and therapeutic activity. Unlike chemically synthesized AuNPs, green-synthesized AuNPs are surrounded by a biologically derived surface layer composed of phytochemicals, proteins, polysaccharides, enzymes, and other metabolites. This biomolecular coating functions as more than a stabilizing cap. It shapes colloidal behavior, immune recognition, biological identity, and structure–function relationships [59]. Therefore, an integrated characterization cascade is necessary, in which each technique is interpreted beyond its descriptive role. The analytical relevance of major characterization techniques is summarized in Table 5.

Spectroscopic analysis: evidence of bioreduction and surface capping

UV-visible spectroscopy provides the first indication of AuNP formation through the appearance of the LSPR band. However, this result should be interpreted analytically rather than used only as visual confirmation. A plasmon band around 520–540 nm usually supports the formation of small spherical AuNPs, whereas red shifting or peak broadening may indicate particle enlargement, anisotropic morphology, or aggregation. Therefore, UV-vis data should be cross-validated with microscopic and hydrodynamic analyses [139].

FTIR analysis is equally important but should not be treated as a simple list of functional groups. Shifts in hydroxyl, carbonyl, amine, sulfhydryl, and protein-related bands provide evidence that specific biomolecules participate in Au³⁺ reduction and NPs stabilization. This interpretation is essential because the biological cap can contribute directly to antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, or anticancer activity. Therefore, FTIR should be discussed as a bio-fingerprinting tool that links extract chemistry with NPs function [140].

Microscopic and diffraction analysis: morphology, crystallinity, and purity

TEM and SEM provide direct evidence of particle morphology, dispersion, and size distribution. Their analytical value lies in linking NPs geometry with biological activity. Smaller particles generally show higher surface interaction and cellular uptake, whereas anisotropic particles may display stronger plasmonic behavior and different membrane-interaction profiles. XRD and SAED analyses confirm the crystalline nature and face-centered cubic structure of metallic gold, while EDX validates elemental composition and helps exclude precursor contamination. Together, TEM/SEM, XRD/SAED, and EDX provide stronger evidence than any single method alone [137, 138].

Stability and bio-interface assessment

DLS and zeta potential are critical for evaluating the biological behavior of green-synthesized AuNPs. DLS measures hydrodynamic diameter, which is often larger than TEM-derived size because it includes the hydrated biomolecular capping layer and possible soft corona. Therefore, a difference between TEM and DLS values should not automatically be considered an error; it may indicate successful surface capping or aggregation in aqueous medium. Zeta potential provides insight into colloidal stability, where values above approximately ± 30 mV generally indicate stronger electrostatic stabilization [141, 142]. However, zeta potential can change after exposure to serum or biological fluids because proteins adsorb onto the NPs surface and form a protein corona. This corona can transform the original “green identity” of the AuNPs into a new biological identity that influences biodistribution, toxicity, immune interaction, and targeting efficiency .

Overall, characterization of biogenic AuNPs should follow an integrated analytical cascade. UV-vis confirms plasmonic formation, FTIR identifies active capping biomolecules, TEM/SEM defines morphology, XRD/SAED confirms crystallinity, EDX verifies elemental purity, and DLS/zeta potential evaluate colloidal and biological-interface behavior [143]. Although individual characterization techniques provide valuable information, none is sufficient to fully establish the physicochemical properties of green-synthesized AuNPs. TEM accurately determines particle size and morphology but provides limited information on colloidal behavior in biological media. In contrast, DLS measures the hydrodynamic diameter, which includes the biomolecular capping layer and any adsorbed solvent molecules, often resulting in larger particle sizes than those observed by TEM. Similarly, FTIR confirms the presence of surface biomolecules but cannot independently identify the specific compounds responsible for reduction and stabilization. Therefore, reliable characterization of green AuNPs requires an integrated interpretation of complementary techniques, enabling a more accurate understanding of nanoparticle structure, stability, surface chemistry, and biological performance.

Biomedical applications of AuNPs

Antibacterial activity

Bacterial infections remain a significant global health burden worldwide, and the increase in antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [112, 144]. The proportion of resistant bacteria strains is now a major proportion of infections that are becoming more difficult to cure [145], and the world is already predicted to experience up to 10 million deaths annually unless preventive measures are put in place [146]. Bacterial resistance via activation of efflux pumps, alteration of the target site, and enzyme catalyzed degradation of drugs has made numerous antibiotics less effective.

Nanotechnology is a good alternative solution in this regard. AuNPs have attracted significant attention among other metallic NPs because of their outstanding stability, biocompatibility, and anti-bacterial activity [112]. AuNPs have several mechanisms of antimicrobial action, such as disturbance of bacterial cell membrane, production of ROS, and interference with DNA replication and protein synthesis [147]. In addition, AuNPs can be used as an effective drug-delivery solution, improving the effectiveness of traditional antibiotics and decreasing the dosage needed [148]. AuNPs provide a safer and more manageable antibacterial therapeutic agent; thus, they can be used as a successful alternative in treating resistant bacterial infection [77].

Mechanism of antibacterial action

The growth-inhibitory action of AuNPs begins with their adsorption and spontaneous aggregation on the negatively charged bacterial cell surface through strong electrostatic interactions. This interaction causes disruption of the cell membrane, leakage of intracellular contents, and eventual collapse of the bacterial structure [149–151]. After attachment, AuNPs penetrate bacterial cells and disrupt membrane-associated functions, including efflux pump activity and electron transport, thereby inhibiting ATP synthesis and impairing cellular respiration [152]. One of the primary pathways is the generation of ROS, i.e., O2− and OH radicals that induce oxidative stress resulting in lipid peroxidation, oxidative protein damage, and oxidative nucleoid lesions like DNA strand breaks and mutations [153, 154]. Simultaneously, AuNPs also directly bind to thiol and amine groups and break disulfide bonds, interfere with protein folding, and inactivate enzymes [155]. Internalization of the NPs also interacts with DNA, preventing replication and transcription and causing apoptosis-type death [156]. Moreover, AuNPs trigger membrane depolarization and unstable Ca2+ influx, which also affects survival [157]. In combination, these processes-membrane destabilization, respiratory suppression, ROS production, protein dysfunction, DNA damage, and ion imbalance-synergistically occur and are highly dependent on the size, shape, and capping agent of NPs [158] as shown in Fig. 3.

Antifungal activity

Fungal infections have risen significantly in recent years, particularly among immunocompromised individuals [169]. Such infections are mostly detected at their later stages, causing high mortality rates [170]. Every year, fungal diseases affect about 150 million individuals worldwide, killing about 1.7 million of them [169], and by 2023, there were more than 6.5 million cases and 3.8 million deaths due to them [171]. This increased incidence has led to the wide application of antifungal agents. Though Antifungal Resistance (AFR) is a natural entity [172], its increased rate is largely caused by excessive and improper usage of antifungals in human beings, animals, agriculture, and the environment, thus disrupting the One Health balance [173]. Like antibiotics, antifungals can be purchased over the counter, which further increases resistance and threat to health in the world [174].

Nanotechnology has, in this regard, turned out to be an innovative approach to overcome antifungal resistance [175]. A new frontier in this regard is AuNPs, which have multiple mechanisms to be applied to improve therapeutic efficacy and drug resistance. Although direct antifungal action has variable efficacy against resistant strains like Candida glabrata, the catalytic and drug-delivery properties of AuNPs are distinctive, which allows these materials to serve as a potent platform to develop next-generation synergistic antifungal therapies [174].

Mechanism of antifungal activity

The antifungal effect of AuNPs can be explained by a multifocal approach, disabling fungal homeostasis in several ways. Externally, the outer cell wall or chitin layer is broken down, or disruption of the Cell wall & chitin layer. The interaction and damage of macromolecules is achieved through binding with important macromolecules, including the blockage of synthesis of β -glucan, which affects cellular structure integrity and causes cellular damage [176]. At the same time, they act on the cell membrane by preventing the production of ergosterol, a necessary part of the cell membrane to maintain its fluidity and activity. Once AuNPs enter these outer defenses or internalize within the cell, a cascade of intracellular harm is experienced. One of the major routes is the elicitation of oxidative stress by using ROS, a well-established method of killing fungal organisms such as C. albicans [177].

This massive oxidative stress is contributive to various other harmful processes by itself. Direct AuNP and oxidative stress interactions may contribute to the protein denaturation of vital structural and enzymatic proteins. They also attack mitochondria, a block in the electron transport chain, crippling the cellular respiration and energy production. In addition, Au-NPs are capable of disrupting the protein-synthesis machinery, resulting in disassembly of ribosomal complex. And, lastly, the NPs may find access to the nucleus, interfering with DNA synthesis through the attachment to nucleic acids, or the generation of ROS-induced strand breaks [178], as shown in Fig. 4. This physical destabilization, coupled with a multidimensional toxic effect, means that AuNPs have a pronounced antifungal effect against several pathogenic fungi, such as C. albicans, C. tropicalis, Aspergillus flavus, and A. fumigatus [179–181]. Antiviral activity

Worldwide, viral infections pose a significant health concern, and there are few effective treatments for the majority of infections. Recently, AuNPs have garnered considerable interest as antiviral agents because they may also inhibit the viral life cycle by interacting with a variety of chemical groups via their physicochemical properties. AuNPs have been shown to bind viral surface proteins, prevent interactions with host cell receptors, disrupt membrane potentials to prevent viral entry, neutralize viral particles, and disrupt intracellular nucleic acid replication [190]. These effects have been reflected in several studies. For example, garlic extract-mediated AuNPs inhibited measles virus (MeV) in Vero cells by obstructing viral adsorption, with the help of bioactive organosulfur components, such as allicin, diallyl sulfide, diallyl disulfide, diallyl trisulfide, ajoene, allyl-cysteine, and allyl-cysteine sulfoxide [191, 192]. AuNPs derived from Sargassum wightii were found to inhibit both HSV-1 and HSV-2 with no cytotoxicity [193], and AuNPs were found to prevent post-entry replication of foot-and-mouth disease virus by obstructing intracellular synthesis of RNA [194]. Functionalized AuNPs can be used to augment antiviral activity: AuNPs functionalized with sulfonate groups inhibited influenza virus attachment due to multivalent interactions [195], whereas AuNPs with HIV gp120-blocking sulfate-ended ligands blocked HIV entry and prevented HIV infection of T cells [196]. These studies collectively underscore the anti-viral potential of AuNPs through extracellular and intracellular mechanisms of action, including receptor blockade, membrane disruption, and nucleic acid inhibition, with broad-spectrum, biocompatible anti-viral outcomes [194, 197].

Mechanism of antiviral activity on AuNPs

The mechanism of action of AuNPs is a multi-pronged approach to disrupting the viral life cycle. One of the primary antiviral mechanisms of AuNPs involves the prevention of viral attachment and cellular entry, whereby the NPs physically block viral binding sites and hinder their interaction with host cell receptors. This occurs through direct contact with the viral surface molecules, including the gp120 envelope glycoproteins of HIV, or through attachment to host cell receptors [196, 198]. Multivalent binding is usually amplified, whereby AuNP molecules with adsorbed ligands (e.g., sulfonated groups) interact with several binding sites on the virus, resulting in a potent interaction and rigorous structural deformation with irreversible inactivation [195, 199].

After viral entry into the host cell, AuNPs interfere with subsequent stages of the viral replication cycle, disrupting genome replication, protein synthesis, and virion assembly. AuNPs can suppress viral uncoating by interfering with the binding of viral capsid components. This interaction increases the structural stability of the virion, thereby preventing the release of viral genetic material into the host cytoplasm [200, 201]. In addition, direct antiviral effects of AuNPs against viral replication include direct antiviral actions by interfering with the activity of essential viral enzymes, such as polymerases, to produce new viral RNA or DNA [202, 203]. Lastly, AuNPs may interfere with the late events of infection by impairing the formation and egress of new viral particles by the host cell, thus spreading the infection [204, 205]. These multi-phenomenal properties in targeting the virus at multiple stages highlight the potential of AuNPs, with a multifactorial role in antiviral therapy [206] (Fig. 5).

Anticancer activity

Green-synthesized AuNPs are becoming one of the most promising nanoplatforms for cancer therapy because of their stability, biocompatibility, and powerful antitumor potential, including cytotoxicity against a wide spectrum of cancerous cells [207, 208]. Anticancer activity against various cancer cell lines has been repeatedly demonstrated in numerous in vitro studies [209–211]. In a related investigation, AuNPs synthesized using Citrus macroptera fruit extract displayed significant anticancer activity against MDA-MB-468, A549, and HepG2 cell lines [212]. Taxus baccata, Marsdenia tenacissima, and Acer truncatum, and Lonicera japonica, Abies spectabilis, and Gymnema sylvestre-mediated AuNPs have been used as anticancer agents against HT-29 (colon), PANIC-1, AGS (gastric), and HeLa (cervical) [209]. Garlic and onion extracts have been used for AuNP synthesis, exhibiting strong cytotoxic activity against MCF-7 breast cancer cells [213]. Further promising sources for AuNP synthesis have also been identified in both marine and terrestrial extracts. A549 lung, [214] KB-3-1 cervical [215] and MCF-7 breast [216] cancer cells were inhibited by AuNPs produced by Halymenia pseudofloresii, bovine serum albumin, Hypnea valentiae, Sonneratia alba fruits, jellyfish venom proteins, and Nothapodytes foetida leaves [214, 217, 218]. Microbes-mediated AuNPs have been found to be useful antitumor agents against liver cancer cells [219]. Streptomyces griseus-synthesized AuNPs inhibit both breast (MCF-7) and colon (HCT-116) cancer cells [220]. Similarly, treatment with red algae (Halymenia pseudofloresii)-mediated AuNPs led to the death of lung cancer cells through the production of significant amounts of ROS [212]. Sargassum glaucescens-mediated AuNPs inhibited breast (MDA-MB-231), cervical (HeLa), leukemia (CEM-ss), and liver (HepG2) cells [221]. Sargassum incisifolium-derived AuNPs suppressed MCF-7 and HT-29 cells without environmental toxicity [222]. A key report by Wang et al. [223] showed that Phyllanthus emblica fruit extract and Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis-synthesized AuNPs suppressed AGS gastric carcinoma cells. Green-synthesized AuNPs derived from diverse biological sources exhibit strong and selective cytotoxicity against multiple cancer cell types, highlighting their potential as safe and effective agents for targeted cancer therapy.

Mechanism of anticancer activity on AuNPs

AuNPs exert their anticancer effects through multiple intracellular pathways that disrupt vital cellular processes, as shown in Fig. 6. AuNPs bind to nuclear DNA directly, interacting with nitrogenous bases and DNA-phosphate residues, and causing widespread genetic damage in the form of oligo-nucleosomal fragments and oligo-nucleosomal fragmentation, the classic hallmarks of apoptotic progression [224, 225]. This DNA interruption inhibits replication and repair mechanisms, deterring the cell cycle of cancer cells, primarily at the G0/G1 and S stages, thus halting further reproduction [226]. Following prolonged injury and constitutive checkpoint failure, intrinsic apoptotic signals are activated; that is, Bcl-2 family-mediated regulation and eventual activation of caspase-9 and caspase-3, the major apoptotic executors [227, 228]. Taken together, this mechanism reveals a clear pathway whereby DNA damage induced by AuNPs leads to growth abrogation and subsequent death by caspase-mediated apoptosis [209]. AuNPs exhibit cytotoxic action through the formation of ROS [229], damage to DNA[230], and triggering of the caspase cascade, resulting in apoptotic and mitochondrial abnormalities [231]. According to reports, ROS generation, mitochondrial malfunction, and apoptosis triggered by caspase are caused by AuNPs [223]. Anti-inflammatory activity

Green-synthesized AuNPs have gained recognition as effective anti-inflammatory agents owing to the synergistic action of their nanoscale properties and the bioactive phytochemicals derived from plants and microbes. These biomolecules not only mediate eco-friendly synthesis but also act as capping agents, improving stability, biocompatibility, and therapeutic efficacy. Biomolecules and AuNPs promote macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, suppressing TNF-α and iNOS while enhancing IL-10 secretion. In addition, they inhibit core signaling pathways such as NF-κB, MAPK, and JAK/STAT, blocking cytokine and COX-2 expression [247], while reducing ICAM-1 levels to prevent leukocyte adhesion and tissue infiltration. Acting as antioxidants, AuNPs also scavenge ROS and preserve mitochondrial integrity, thereby disrupting the vicious cycle of oxidative stress and inflammation. Several biogenic AuNPs have demonstrated potent anti-inflammatory properties (Table 9). Muniyappan et al. [248] reported that curcumin-capped AuNPs synthesized with an average size of 20 nm exhibited 94% inhibition in a human red blood cell membrane stabilization assay, highlighting their strong anti-inflammatory potential by effectively preventing hemolysis and protecting cellular integrity against inflammatory stress. Similarly, Gao et al. [249] demonstrated that AuNPs synthesized from Citrus sinensis (orange) with an average size of 13.65 nm showed an IC50 value of 82.91 µg/mL in protein denaturation inhibition, further confirming the ability of plant-mediated AuNPs to attenuate inflammatory responses by suppressing protein denaturation, a key process in the inflammatory cascade. In another study, Kwak, et al.[95] revealed that AuNPs derived from Suaeda japonica, with sizes ranging from 20 to 30 nm, effectively reduced the generation of nitric oxide (NO) and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), two key mediators of inflammation, thereby confirming their role in downregulating inflammatory signaling pathways. Euphrasia officinalis-derived AuNPs (5–30 nm) suppressed NO, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 release in macrophages [250]. Chaetomium globosum AuNPs (23 nm) inhibited 15-lipoxygenase activity by 68.1%, reducing leukotriene synthesis [247]. Likewise, Capsicum annuum-mediated AuNPs (20–30 nm) stabilized cell membranes by inhibiting hemolysis with an IC₅₀ of 619.4 µg/mL [251]. These findings demonstrate that green-synthesized AuNPs exert potent anti-inflammatory effects through multifaceted mechanisms involving oxidative stress suppression, cytokine regulation, and membrane stabilization. Their biocompatibility and efficacy position them as promising candidates for the development of safe, nanoscale therapeutics in inflammation-related disorders.

Mechanism of anti-inflammatory action on Au NPs

AuNPs exert anti-inflammatory activity through multiple complementary mechanisms that modulate immune responses at both the cellular and molecular levels. AuNPs reprogram macrophages from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 type, thereby suppressing mediators such as TNF-α, IL-6, and iNOS while enhancing IL-10 and TGF-β secretion, which aids tissue repair [252–254]. AuNPs also inhibit central inflammatory signaling pathways, particularly NF-κB, MAPK, and JAK/STAT, thereby reducing transcription and release of cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and enzymes like COX-2 [255]. In addition, they decrease leukocyte adhesion and migration by downregulating adhesion molecules, such as ICAM-1, on endothelial cells, which prevents infiltration into inflamed tissues and subsequent damage [256]. Another key mechanism is their antioxidant role: AuNPs scavenge ROS, restore mitochondrial function, and prevent excessive ROS generation, thereby breaking the feedback loop between oxidative stress and inflammation [257–260]. These multi-targeted effects demonstrate that AuNPs alleviate inflammation not by blocking a single pathway, but by orchestrating the broad regulation of immune cell activity, signaling cascades, adhesion processes, and oxidative stress, as depicted in Fig. 7[261].

Anti-oxidant activity

Green-synthesized AuNPs are recognized as potent antioxidants, owing to the combined effects of their metallic core and bioactive capping agents, which enhance free radical scavenging activity. Rhus coriaria extract-mediated AuNPs (16–21 nm) exhibited high radical scavenging activity, with 85.7% DPPH and 96.8% ABTS in cell-based assays [268], whereas algae-mediated AuNPs (5–15 nm) showed an SC50 of 18.1 mg. Similarly, marine algae-based AuNPs (e.g.; Hizikia fusiformis (Hfs, ~ 18 nm)[269]; and tea polyphenol-derived AuNPs (~ 26 nm; [270] exhibited stronger antioxidant effects than their respective extracts, strongly suggesting a multi-component effect of phytochemicals and nanoscale effects. In addition to their phytochemical effects, AuNPs have distinctive physicochemical properties, including a high surface area, electrical conductivity, and catalytic redox behavior, which allows AuNPs to directly scavenge free radicals and catabolize ROS [271, 272]. AuNPs are more stable and have a longer bioavailability and cellular targeting when compared to conventional antioxidants [273]. Mechanically, their surface catalytic nature can be used to neutralize radicals through electron transfer and display intrinsic catalase-like activities in breaking down H2O2 to produce oxygen and water, further increasing antioxidant potential [274]. These characteristics make AuNPs worthy of consideration not only as a potential treatment for antioxidant therapy but also for a broader range of applications in biomedical systems, including biosensing, drug delivery, cancer therapy, and antimicrobial interventions [260].

Mechanism of antioxidant action

The antioxidant mechanism of green-synthesized AuNPs results from the synergistic effect between the metallic core and surface-bound phytochemicals from biological extracts. These NPs act through multiple pathways to combat oxidative stress. AuNPs directly scavenge ROS, including superoxide (O₂⁻), hydroxyl radicals (•OH), and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), by serving as electron donors, thereby neutralizing free radicals before they cause cellular damage. This is made possible by their high surface-to-volume ratio and redox-active surface, which enhance their interaction with radicals [272].

AuNPs demonstrate enzyme-mimetic activities, particularly catalase-like and peroxidase-like functions, allowing them to break down hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen. This catalytic behavior improves antioxidant performance and contributes to cellular redox balance. The surface-bound phytochemicals, such as flavonoids and polyphenols, contribute additional antioxidant activity by donating hydrogen atoms or electrons, thereby interrupting oxidative chain reactions [272].

In addition to direct ROS neutralization, AuNPs have been shown to activate cellular antioxidant pathways. AuNPs can upregulate nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) signaling, leading to increased expression of endogenous antioxidant enzymes, including glutathione peroxidase (GPx), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase [281]. Moreover, their improved physicochemical stability and bioavailability compared to traditional antioxidants ensure prolonged circulation and targeted cellular uptake. These combined effects make green AuNPs powerful therapeutic candidates for oxidative stress-related conditions and multifunctional biomedical applications. Toxicological considerations of green-synthesized AuNPs

Despite the significant biomedical potential of AuNPs, their clinical translation is often hindered by concerns regarding toxicity and long-term safety. Although green synthesis reduces the use of hazardous chemicals and provides biologically capped NPs that are generally considered more biocompatible, the particles themselves can still exhibit harmful effects depending on their physicochemical properties. Several studies have shown that AuNPs are readily internalized by cells through mechanisms such as endocytosis or direct penetration of the plasma membrane, after which they interact with proteins, nucleic acids, and intracellular organelles. One of the most widely reported pathways of toxicity is the overproduction of ROS, which induces oxidative stress. This process leads to lipid peroxidation, damage to proteins, mitochondrial dysfunction, and DNA strand breaks. The accumulation of oxidative damage often results in apoptosis through activation of caspase cascades or necrosis at higher exposure levels. In addition, AuNPs are known to bind thiol and amine groups in proteins, thereby altering enzyme structure and function, whereas their interactions with DNA can disrupt replication and transcription, further compromising cell viability [285].

A critical factor in AuNP toxicity is the formation of the ‘Protein Corona.’ Once AuNPs enter systemic circulation, the ‘green’ capping agents are partially replaced by host serum proteins. This transition determines the nanoparticle’s biological identity, cellular uptake, and biodistribution in organs, such as the liver and spleen. While ROS-mediated damage is the primary toxicity pathway, recent studies have also highlighted RNS mechanisms, in which AuNPs induce nitric oxide (NO) fluctuations, leading to peroxynitrite-mediated protein nitration and subsequent cell death [69].

In vivo studies also reveal that the toxicological effects of AuNPs extend beyond the cellular level, as NPs distribute widely after systemic administration and accumulate in major organs. The liver, spleen, kidneys, and lungs act as primary sites of deposition, reflecting clearance by the reticuloendothelial system. Accumulation in these tissues may cause chronic inflammation, hepatic stress, renal impairment, and hematological alterations. Particularly concerning are smaller AuNPs, generally below 10 nm, which have been shown to cross the blood–brain barrier and potentially exert neurotoxic effects. The degree of toxicity is strongly influenced by factors such as particle size, shape, and surface chemistry. For example, smaller NPs penetrate cells more efficiently and can cause greater cytotoxicity, while rod-shaped or anisotropic particles may inflict more membrane disruption than spherical forms. Positively charged AuNPs often exhibit higher toxicity because of their strong electrostatic interactions with negatively charged cellular membranes, whereas natural capping agents derived from plant metabolites or microbial proteins tend to enhance compatibility by stabilizing the nanoparticle surface and reducing direct membrane damage.

Dosage and exposure time are equally critical determinants of toxicological profiles. At low concentrations, AuNPs may be relatively benign or even therapeutically beneficial; however, increasing the dose often amplifies ROS production, disrupts mitochondrial function, and triggers apoptotic or necrotic cell death. Chronic exposure or repeated administration may also lead to long-term tissue accumulation, raising questions about excretion and biodegradability. These uncertainties highlight the importance of designing safe therapeutic regimens based on standardized toxicological data rather than isolated in vitro or short-term studies. Unfortunately, variations in synthesis protocols, biological capping agents, and experimental conditions have made it difficult to compare toxicological results across laboratories.

Several strategies have been proposed to mitigate the toxic effects of AuNPs. Surface functionalization with polymers, such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) or natural biomolecules, has been shown to reduce immunogenicity, prolong circulation times, and minimize aggregation. Encapsulation of NPs in delivery vehicles, such as liposomes, hydrogels, or biodegradable scaffolds, can also restrict off-target accumulation and reduce systemic toxicity. Importantly, green synthesis methods may offer protective advantages, as the phytochemicals or microbial metabolites used as reducing and stabilizing agents often possess antioxidant properties that counteract ROS-mediated damage. However, comprehensive toxicological testing remains indispensable.

Looking forward, toxicology remains one of the central challenges that must be addressed before green-synthesized AuNPs can transition from laboratory research to clinical practice. Future work should integrate multi-omics approaches and advanced imaging to map NP distribution and clearance in living systems, and long-term animal models are required to define safe dosage ranges and identify chronic side effects. Establishing standardized protocols for in vitro and in vivo testing will also be essential to harmonize findings across studies and facilitate regulatory approval. Ultimately, the promise of green-synthesized AuNPs lies not only in their eco-friendly production and broad therapeutic potential, but also in demonstrating that AuNPs can be used safely and reproducibly in humans. Addressing toxicological uncertainties is therefore not an obstacle but an opportunity to advance the field toward clinically viable and industrially scalable nanomedicine. Therefore, future toxicological evaluations of green-synthesized AuNPs should assess protein corona composition under physiologically relevant conditions, as the in vivo biological identity of NPs is determined by the acquired corona rather than the original green capping layer.

Conclusion and future perspectives

Green-synthesized AuNPs have emerged as promising nanomaterials for biomedical applications because they combine the intrinsic physicochemical advantages of AuNPs with the functional contribution of biologically derived capping molecules. Compared with conventional synthesis, biological routes offer safer, more sustainable, and potentially more biocompatible approaches by using plants, fungi, bacteria, algae, and other natural resources as reducing and stabilizing systems. These biological sources not only mediate Au³⁺ reduction and NPs stabilization but also influence particle size, morphology, surface chemistry, colloidal stability, and biological activity.

The therapeutic potential of green-synthesized AuNPs is broad, including antibacterial, antifungal, antiviral, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant activities. However, their biological performance depends strongly on synthesis conditions, capping biomolecules, shape, size, surface charge, and interaction with physiological environments. Therefore, the biomedical value of green AuNPs should not be judged only by their successful synthesis or color change, but by their reproducibility, mechanistic clarity, structure–activity relationship, stability, toxicity profile, and translational feasibility.

Despite considerable progress, several barriers still restrict clinical and industrial translation. The most important limitations include batch-to-batch variability, incomplete identification of active reducing and capping biomolecules, inconsistent characterization protocols, limited quantitative comparison with chemically synthesized AuNPs, and insufficient in vivo safety data. Protein corona formation, bioaccumulation, organ distribution, clearance behavior, and long-term toxicity also require deeper investigation because these factors can significantly alter NPs identity and biological response after exposure to blood or serum.

Future research should focus on developing standardized green synthesis protocols supported by quantitative metabolite profiling, controlled reaction parameters, and integrated characterization methods. Comparative studies using matched particle size, surface charge, assay conditions, and biological models are needed to evaluate green AuNPs against conventionally synthesized or citrate-capped AuNPs more rigorously. In addition, advanced approaches such as Design of Experiments, machine-learning-assisted optimization, omics-based corona analysis, and validated in vitro–in vivo toxicological models may improve reproducibility and predictive performance.

Future research should focus on developing standardized green synthesis protocols supported by quantitative metabolite profiling, controlled reaction parameters, and integrated characterization methods. Comparative studies using matched particle size, surface charge, assay conditions, and biological models are needed to evaluate green AuNPs against conventionally synthesized or citrate-capped AuNPs more rigorously. Although direct comparisons between green-synthesized and chemically synthesized AuNPs have been reported in a limited number of studies, differences in nanoparticle size, morphology, surface chemistry, capping agents, synthesis protocols, and biological assay conditions make quantitative cross-study comparisons difficult. Consequently, standardized head-to-head investigations employing identical experimental conditions are required to establish definitive differences in biological efficacy, toxicity, cellular uptake, and therapeutic performance. In addition, advanced approaches such as Design of Experiments, machine-learning-assisted optimization, omics-based corona analysis, and validated in vitro–in vivo toxicological models may improve reproducibility and predictive performance.

Overall, green-synthesized AuNPs represent a valuable platform for sustainable nanomedicine, but their future success depends on moving from descriptive synthesis reports toward standardized, mechanistically informed, and quantitatively comparable studies. Establishing clear links between biological source, capping chemistry, NPs properties, therapeutic mechanism, and safety profile will be essential for translating green AuNPs from laboratory-scale studies to reliable biomedical and clinical applications.
