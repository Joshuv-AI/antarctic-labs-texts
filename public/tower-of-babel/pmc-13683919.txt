Designing Engagement in Dementia Care: Embodied Participation, Situated Adaptation, and Co-Design-In-Practice
Authors: Yen-Fu Chen, Chaw-Mew Hung, Yen-Ting Yao, Shih-Tsun Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study explores how a tactile and play-based intervention, the Colorful Counting Brickers, fostered emotional and social engagement among older adults with dementia in a Taiwanese long-term care setting informed by relationally grounded care practices. Grounded in person-centered care and theories of embodied selfhood, the research employed a four-phase design process including rapid ethnographic immersion, iterative design development, expert consultation, and in-situ implementation. Thematic analysis of observations, caregiver interviews, and written reflections revealed that engagement was not a fixed behavioral response, but an emergent and relational process shaped by sensory interaction, peer reciprocity, and caregiver facilitation. Three key dimensions emerged. First, embodiment was evident as residents engaged through tactile exploration, affective responses, and gestural interaction. Second, relationality enabled peer reciprocity, including imitation, turn-taking, and spontaneous assistance. Third, immediacy was apparent as caregivers adjusted facilitation strategies in real time through emotional attunement and embodied familiarity. The study introduces the concept of co-design-in-practice to describe these situated and informal design contributions enacted by caregiving staff. This research contributes to the field of dementia care by reframing caregiving as a form of design agency and by emphasizing the relational and culturally embedded nature of engagement. It advocates for a shift from designing isolated products to designing with and through everyday care practices. This perspective informs a more inclusive and practice-sensitive model of participatory dementia innovation that recognizes the experiential expertise of caregivers and the lived realities of those they support.

Introduction

Dementia progressively affects memory, cognition, communication, emotional expression, and social participation. Within long-term care settings, many individuals experience growing isolation, apathy, and withdrawal, particularly as communication increasingly shifts to embodied, post-verbal, and nonverbal modes (Bartlett & O’Connor, 2010; Kitwood, 1997; Quinn & Blandon, 2017). In response to these psychosocial challenges, a substantial body of research has explored activity-based interventions designed to stimulate the senses, foster interpersonal connection, and support residual agency (Camp & Skrajner, 2004; Treadaway et al., 2014; van der Ploeg et al., 2013).

More recent scholarship has reframed what meaningful engagement involves: rather than a stimulus-driven behavioral response, engagement is increasingly understood as bound up with personal meaning, identity, capability, and the everyday conditions of care (Goodall et al., 2021; Harding et al., 2024). This shift has been accompanied by growing attention to the embodied and aesthetic dimensions of care, where sensory contact, material qualities, and relational presence shape how people with dementia connect with others and sustain a sense of self (Fleetwood-Smith et al., 2022; Owen et al., 2024). These developments suggest that dementia design should not only attend to the properties of interventions, but also to the relational and practical conditions through which they are enacted in care settings.

This study is situated within Taiwan’s long-term care system, where dementia care is shaped by distinct cultural and relational values. Compared with many Western care models that place greater emphasis on individual autonomy and explicit negotiation, Taiwanese care practices are often informed by collectivist orientations and the cultural concept of xiao (filial piety). According to Yeh and Bedford (2003), filial piety encompasses both reciprocal dimensions of gratitude, emotional intimacy, and voluntary care, and hierarchical dimensions of obligation and respect. Within institutional care settings, these values manifest in caregiving approaches characterized by tacit responsiveness, embodied familiarity, and intuitive adaptation. These qualities are often enacted through gesture, pacing, and affective attunement rather than through explicit verbal instruction. This culturally grounded care ecology provides a valuable context for examining how design interventions are not merely implemented but actively co-shaped through the relational practices of care staff.

Despite growing recognition of the importance of relational, embodied, and aesthetic engagement in dementia care, three interrelated gaps remain in how design and participation are currently understood. First, while recent scholarship has increasingly attended to how people with dementia engage with material environments (Fleetwood-Smith et al., 2022; Owen et al., 2024), designed interventions are still often treated as relatively stable artifacts whose value resides primarily in their material or functional properties. Less attention has been paid to how their meaning and effectiveness emerge through situated use, caregiver interpretation, and ongoing adaptation in everyday care settings. Second, although participatory and co-design research in dementia care has expanded considerably in recent years, the creative and interpretive work of frontline care staff is still rarely conceptualized as a form of design participation. Recent reviews suggest that participation is often concentrated in structured development activities, needs assessment, or usability evaluation, rather than in the ongoing enactment and adaptation of interventions in practice (Messina et al., 2025; Reyes et al., 2023). Third, many established participatory design frameworks in dementia care continue to rely on assumptions about collaboration that are more commonly foregrounded in Western design traditions, such as verbal articulation, structured workshops, and explicit consensus-building. Although recent work has called for more inclusive and situated forms of involvement (Groothuijse et al., 2024; Reyes et al., 2023), insufficient attention has been paid to culturally grounded care practices that operate through tacit knowledge, relational sensitivity, and embodied improvisation (Björgvinsson et al., 2012; Light & Akama, 2014).

This study addresses these gaps by examining how a tactile and play-based intervention, the Colorful Counting Brickers, fostered emotional and social engagement among older adults with dementia in a Taiwanese long-term care setting. Rather than treating the designed artifact as a finished product, we investigate how its meaning and impact were actively shaped through the adaptive practices of caregiving staff.

We introduce the concept of co-design-in-practice to describe how caregivers continuously adapt interventions through embodied familiarity and emotional attunement. By foregrounding this form of design participation, which occurs embedded within routine care rather than in formal design workshops, this study contributes to more culturally attuned and practice-sensitive models of dementia design.

The study was guided by the following research questions:1. How do older adults with dementia emotionally and socially engage with a play-based, sensory activity in a long-term care setting?2. What forms of peer interaction and social reciprocity emerge during such engagement?3. How do caregivers adapt, interpret, and shape the activity experience within the context of everyday care?

Theoretical Framework

This section elaborates the theoretical foundations that inform our understanding of engagement in dementia care and introduces the concept of co-design-in-practice that emerged through our analysis.

Foundational Perspectives: Person-Centered Care and Embodied Selfhood

Person-Centered Care: Principles and Implementation

Person-centered care (PCC) offers both a conceptual framework and a practical orientation for addressing the complexities of engagement in dementia care. Kitwood (1997) introduced the notion of ‘positive person work,’ emphasizing the preservation of identity through emotional affirmation and social interaction. Building on this foundation, Brooker’s (2003, 2006) VIPS model highlights the importance of valuing individuals, understanding their unique perspectives, and fostering supportive social environments.

Empirical studies have shown that PCC interventions can reduce agitation, neuropsychiatric symptoms, and depression, and improve quality of life (Kim & Park, 2017). However, research also suggests that while PCC is widely endorsed as a guiding philosophy, its implementation varies significantly across care settings. The effectiveness of PCC often relies on staff responsiveness, organizational culture, and capacity to adapt practices to local contexts (Brooker & Latham, 2015; Edvardsson et al., 2010). Recent critical dementia studies have called for approaches that move beyond individualistic framings of personhood to foreground relational, cultural, and structural dimensions of care (Bartlett, 2016; Kontos et al., 2017).

Embodied Selfhood and Communication

Embodied theories of selfhood further enrich the person-centered perspective by proposing that identity persists not only through memory or language but also through gesture, habit, and affective rhythm. Kontos (2005) introduced the concept of embodied selfhood to describe how self-expression can be enacted through the body, even when verbal communication is limited. Later scholars have expanded on this idea through related concepts such as body memory (Fuchs, 2020) and the inter-embodied self (Jenkins, 2014).

These perspectives emphasize the importance of sensory and motor engagement, including mirroring, touch, and rhythmic interaction, in sustaining presence and facilitating social connection. This orientation reframes communication as a transforming capacity, shifting from verbal-linguistic modes to embodied, sensory, and affective registers (Quinn & Blandon, 2017). Empirical research supports this perspective by showing that nonverbal, bodily-centered interactions can evoke emotional responses and promote participation among people living with dementia (Kontos & Martin, 2013).

Social Citizenship and Agency

In parallel with these perspectives, the framework of social citizenship positions individuals with dementia as capable of agency and participation. Rooted in disability studies and theories of civic inclusion, this approach understands engagement not only as a therapeutic objective but also as a fundamental social right (Bartlett, 2016; Bartlett & O’Connor, 2010). Agency in this context is enacted relationally through processes of recognition, reciprocity, and shared activity. Empirical studies indicate that when activities are thoughtfully scaffolded and socially responsive, people living with dementia can take initiative and make meaningful contributions to collective interactions (Dupuis et al., 2012; Phinney et al., 2007).

Analytical Lenses: Design, Materiality, and Situated Action

These foundational perspectives on dementia care call for flexible, context-sensitive design approaches that move beyond fixed protocols or prescriptive methods. We draw on design research frameworks that emphasize how meaning and participation emerge through actual use and situated practice.

Key influences include design-in-use (Redström, 2008), which recognizes that design continues through user interaction; material participation (Buse & Twigg, 2016, 2018), which positions objects as active mediators in care relationships; and infrastructuring (Björgvinsson et al., 2012), which extends design beyond discrete project phases to ongoing sociotechnical evolution. Everyday design practice perspectives (Duque Hurtado, 2018; Pink et al., 2016) further highlight how people engage in design thinking and adaptation within daily life contexts.

This orientation also resonates with design ethnography accounts that foreground how design is accomplished through ongoing, situated practices rather than discrete workshops or predefined outputs (Pink et al., 2022). In care contexts, such everyday designing is often inseparable from maintaining homeliness and relational comfort, where seemingly minor adjustments carry ethical and affective weight (Duque et al., 2019). These perspectives help clarify why caregivers’ micro-adaptations in dementia care can be understood as a distinctive mode of co-design.

Defining Key Terms

Before introducing our synthesizing concept, we clarify three central terms. Relational refers to engagement emerging through interpersonal dynamics and shared presence rather than individual cognitive response. Situated emphasizes how meaning and action are shaped by specific contextual conditions including physical environment, social relationships, cultural norms, and temporal rhythms. Embodied describes knowledge and participation enacted through the body, gesture, sensory experience, and affective attunement.

Co-Design-In-Practice: A Synthesizing Concept

Defining Co-Design-In-Practice

Building on these foundations, we introduce co-design-in-practice, a conceptual framework developed through our six-year ethnographic engagement in this dementia care setting. This framework emerged abductively: patterns in caregiver practice that existing design concepts did not fully capture led us to develop new conceptual language. Co-design-in-practice describes how caregivers continuously adapt technologies and interactions within the flow of daily care. We offer this framework as both our study’s theoretical contribution and the lens through which we organize our empirical findings.

Co-design-in-practice describes the process through which design researchers and caregiving staff collaboratively develop relational technologies (mediating artifacts). Once these technologies enter the care setting, caregivers, drawing upon their embodied familiarity with residents and their capacity for emotional attunement, engage in ongoing, real-time adaptations of both material environments and interaction strategies within daily care activities such as feeding, bathing, companionship, and comforting.

This form of design activity does not occur in formal design workshops or structured co-creation sessions. Instead, it is embedded within routine caregiving activities, manifesting as subtle adjustments in caregiver-resident interactions: a shift in hand gesture, a slowing of verbal rhythm, a repositioning of objects within reach. These design contributions do not produce prototypes or design specifications in the conventional sense; rather, they take form as micro-adjustments in posture, timing, spatial arrangement, and affective quality. These design moves are enacted, embodied, and often tacit.

Core Mechanisms

Embodied familiarity refers to the tacit, bodily knowledge caregivers develop through prolonged interaction with specific residents. This includes knowing how a particular resident prefers to be touched, what pace of movement feels comfortable, which sensory cues elicit positive responses, and how their body responds to different forms of engagement. This knowledge is not acquired through formal training but through accumulated experience of being-with and caring-for specific individuals.

Emotional attunement describes the capacity to sense and synchronize with residents’ affective states in real time. Caregivers adjust their emotional tone, pacing, and presence to match or gently shift the resident’s emotional register. This attunement operates through nonverbal channels such as facial expression, vocal prosody, breathing rhythm, and physical proximity, enabling communication that continues even when language fails.

Unlike conventional participatory design, which positions users as collaborators in discrete design phases, co-design-in-practice recognizes caregivers as situated designers whose creative adaptations occur continuously within the temporal and relational flow of care. It differs from traditional participatory design in three key respects: (1) it does not occur in formal workshops but is embedded in routine care activities; (2) it produces not prototypes or documentation but embodied adjustments in interaction quality; and (3) it relies not primarily on verbal articulation but on tacit, sensory, and affective knowledge.

Illustrative Examples

Illustrative examples include: caregivers adjust pacing based on residents’ breathing patterns (embodied attunement), create improvised visual cues when standard materials prove inadequate (material improvisation), or reconfigure activity rules to accommodate mixed abilities at the same table (situated adaptation). These adaptations draw on both professional knowledge and relational familiarity developed through ongoing care relationships.

Methods

Research Design

This study employed a qualitative, design-led research approach that combined rapid ethnography, practice-based prototyping, and in-situ evaluation. Grounded in the principles of person-centered care and participatory design, the methodological process was organized into four iterative phases: (1) contextual immersion, (2) design development, (3) expert consultation, and (4) field implementation. Throughout these phases, the research emphasized situated inquiry, guided by close attention to care routines, affective signals, and relational dynamics within the lived environment of dementia care.

Phase 1: Contextual Immersion

The research began with a two-week period of rapid ethnographic immersion in a dementia care unit within a government-operated long-term care facility in northern Taiwan. This phase was situated within an intergenerational co-living initiative and allowed the research team to closely observe daily rhythms, caregiving routines, and resident behaviors. Through participant observation, the team engaged in both physical and reflective activities alongside residents and staff, documenting affective responses, sensory preferences, and moments of nonverbal interaction.

Several key patterns emerged. Residents showed positive responses to tactile variation and rhythmic activities, whereas instruction-heavy interactions often led to disengagement. Informal conversations with caregiving staff further revealed contextual challenges, including short attention spans, sensory impairments, and the need for flexible facilitation strategies. These early insights informed the development of a culturally grounded and cognitively accessible design direction.

Phase 2: Design Development

Drawing on insights from the ethnographic phase, the research team designed the Colorful Counting Brickers, a tactile and modular play object inspired by dice. Each Bricker was constructed from EVA foam and shaped as a truncated octahedron, measuring approximately 15 centimeters in width. The surface featured textured dot patterns and vibrant colors intended to enhance both sensory engagement and visual attention (see Figure 1). The team developed four distinct activity formats: Color Matching, Tactile Counting, Stacking Game, and Bricker Passing. Each format was designed to support different cognitive and social capacities among participants. In addition to the Brickers as the core tactile artifact, the intervention included simple facilitation guidelines available for caregivers’ reference (e.g., suggested activity formats with color and stacking variations, role prompts for supporting different cognitive capacities). These guidelines served as flexible scaffolds that caregivers could adapt, supplement, or modify based on residents’ momentary capacities and group dynamics. The guidelines were not distributed to residents; rather, they supported caregivers’ preparation and in-the-moment facilitation decisions.Figure 1.The Colorful Counting Brickers: Designed with tactile concavities and bright colors to support sensory engagement

The iterative prototyping process incorporated input from care workers and occupational therapists. Their feedback guided refinements to features such as grip size, color saturation, and the overall flow of interaction. Although formal co-design protocols were not implemented, the contributions of caregiving staff played a critical role in shaping both the material characteristics and the facilitation strategies of the activity. Informed by the concept of co-design-in-practice, we interpret these situated adaptations as informal yet meaningful forms of design participation. This approach aligns with a design-in-use perspective, where the meaning and function of an intervention are continuously redefined through everyday caregiving practice.

Phase 3: Expert Consultation

Before implementation, three dementia care specialists were invited to evaluate the prototype through hands-on interaction and semi-structured interviews. They assessed its design suitability, usability, and potential facilitation methods. The experts confirmed that the activity aligned with principles of Montessori-based approaches and recommended specific adaptations, including the incorporation of verbal prompts, background music, and the gradual introduction of tasks.

Based on the expert feedback, minor adjustments were made to improve visual clarity and ensure tactile consistency. In addition, facilitation guides were developed to support implementation across diverse care settings and to assist caregivers in adapting the activity to varying levels of resident ability.

Phase 4: Implementation and Evaluation

Setting and Sample

The Colorful Counting Brickers were introduced and tested in situ across six pilot sessions conducted between October 2024 and March 2025 at a government-operated long-term care facility in northern Taiwan. During this phase, the artifacts were deployed within the everyday environment of the care unit, allowing the research team to observe how residents and caregivers engaged with them in the course of routine care. This facility was selected for its engagement with intergenerational co-living initiatives and its care approach informed by culturally grounded values including relational harmony and embodied familiarity. Sessions were integrated into existing recreational programming within the care facility.

The participant group comprised 13 residents (all male), with ages ranging from 70 to 91 years. Based on the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR), participants presented with moderate to severe dementia (CDR scores 2–3). Care was provided through a team-based model wherein each resident was supported by 3–4 designated caregivers who rotated shifts, with individual caregivers often supporting multiple residents. This structure enabled caregivers to develop embodied familiarity with specific residents while also building shared knowledge about facilitation approaches across the resident group.

Sessions were facilitated by members of the unit’s caregiving team, whose composition varied across sessions. Caregivers possessed a range of specialized experience in dementia care, with the majority having over five years and some over ten years.

Observation and Documentation

During these sessions, the research team adopted a non-intrusive observational role. Of the six sessions, two were directly observed by the research team. The remaining sessions were documented through caregiver-generated reports and informal debriefings. This hybrid documentation approach enabled the study to gather both direct field insights and emic interpretations provided by caregiving staff, within the practical constraints of the care environment. Data sources included field notes, informal caregiver reflections, and post-session interviews with three members of the caregiving team.

Although only two sessions were observed directly due to ethical and logistical limitations, this constraint was offset by the richness of practitioner narratives. This reliance on caregiving staff as co-interpreters of engagement aligns with the study’s commitment to emic perspectives. Triangulation across caregiver accounts, expert interviews, and thematic analysis supported the analytical robustness of the findings.

The aim of the study was not to produce clinically generalizable results. Instead, the focus was on how engagement unfolded in practice, including how caregivers adapted activities, how residents responded emotionally and socially, and how moments of connection emerged through gesture, pacing, and interaction with the materials.

In this study, residents’ participation was approached as post-verbal and embodied rather than primarily verbal. We treated residents’ moment-to-moment actions (e.g., initiating touch and exploration, redirecting attention, refusal or withdrawal, affective shifts, turn-taking gestures, and peer-to-peer prompting) as interactional cues that shaped how caregivers adapted pacing, rules, and facilitation roles in situ. These cues were captured through the available field notes from directly observed sessions, caregiver reflections and session logs, and debrief interviews, and were coded alongside caregiver actions to trace how co-design moves emerged through everyday care practice.

The dataset was analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s (2006) six-phase thematic analysis, guided by the co-design-in-practice framework established in the theoretical section. Our analytical approach attended specifically to the three dimensions of co-design-in-practice: immediacy (real-time responsiveness to resident states), embodiment (design knowledge carried through bodily action), and relationality (design embedded in caregiver-resident relationships). We coded for instances of embodied familiarity, emotional attunement, and adaptive redesign within everyday care activities.

Two researchers independently coded the materials, identified recurring patterns, and collaboratively synthesized themes related to emotional engagement, peer interaction, and facilitation dynamics. To enhance analytical trustworthiness, the team employed coder triangulation and iterative review.

Although the structure and degree of formality varied across sources, their integration allowed for a more nuanced and contextually grounded interpretation of the data. In particular, caregiver reflections, despite being informal and unstandardized, were considered essential for capturing experiential insights rooted in everyday practice, which might not have emerged through structured observation alone.

Ethical Considerations

This study received ethical approval from the Research Ethics Committee of National Taiwan University (Approval Nos. NTU-REC 201806ES053 and 202405ES098). All research procedures followed ethical guidelines for working with vulnerable populations. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal representatives. Particular attention was given to emotional well-being, autonomy, and relational sensitivity when working with older adults with dementia. All identifying information has been anonymized to ensure participant confidentiality.

Findings

The analyses in this section draw upon a composite dataset consisting of two sessions directly observed by the research team and four additional sessions documented through caregiver reflections and post-session interviews. Although the research team was not physically present for every session, the narratives generated by caregiving staff offered rich, practice-informed accounts that complemented and extended the observational data. This hybrid documentation approach reflects the study’s commitment to capturing engagement as it occurs within the flow of everyday care. It also positions caregivers not only as facilitators but as co-interpreters of relational dynamics and emotional expression.

The findings are organized into three themes that address the study’s research questions and illuminate the three dimensions of co-design-in-practice established in the theoretical framework: immediacy (real-time responsiveness), embodiment (design knowledge through bodily action), and relationality (design embedded in relationships). These dimensions are woven throughout the empirical accounts that follow.

Theme 1: Emotional Resonance and Sensory Engagement

Throughout the Bricker sessions, one of the most consistently observed outcomes was the spontaneous expression of emotion, particularly among residents with limited verbal ability. Although verbal communication was minimal, participants frequently demonstrated affective responses through nonverbal cues such as smiling, gentle laughter, sustained eye contact, and shifts in bodily orientation. These emotional reactions were often triggered by the sensory and visual qualities of the Brickers, especially during moments of tactile engagement and rhythmic activity.

The Brickers were intentionally designed to support both sensorimotor participation and emotional accessibility. As lightweight, palm-sized objects featuring bright colors and concave textures, they encouraged intuitive interaction through gestures such as stacking, aligning, and passing. Unlike conventional game tools, they did not rely on complex rules or verbal instruction, which enabled residents to engage through embodied familiarity rather than cognitive processing.

During one of the directly observed sessions, several residents displayed noticeable shifts in affect. One resident, who was typically disengaged, smiled and laughed softly while stacking a yellow Bricker. A caregiver witnessing this moment commented, “This is the first time I’ve seen him smile this week.”

Similar emotional responses were reported in caregiver reflections from other sessions. One caregiver noted, “Some residents usually don’t respond much, but when we placed the Brickers in front of them, they started to smile, make eye contact, and even pick up the block on their own.”

Caregivers often attributed these emotional responses to the Brickers’ intuitive tactility and visual appeal. They described the objects as “easy to hold,” “not too slippery,” and “visually inviting.” The geometric form and vibrant colors supported low-pressure exploration and helped sustain residents’ attention in ways that felt natural and unforced. One caregiver remarked, “The colors really draw their attention. Residents were more focused when we guided them to match or stack the pieces.”

These material qualities supported micro-moments of attentional presence and affective attunement, particularly when accompanied by relational cues from caregivers. Activities such as rhythmic passing, gentle stacking, and color matching became more than routine tasks. They created opportunities for shared attention, emotional lightness, and moments of intersubjective recognition. As one caregiver reflected, “For the first time in days, I felt the room become light. There was shared laughter, even between residents who usually sit quietly.”

Although often brief, these moments contributed to an emotionally generative atmosphere. Caregivers played a central role in shaping this environment through affective pacing, vocal modulation, and attentive presence. One caregiver explained, “We try to sense their mood before pushing further. If someone seems uneasy, we back off or try a simpler task. The point is to let them enjoy, not feel pressure.”

These findings suggest that even simple, tactile materials can evoke emotional resonance when situated within a relationally responsive care context. Rather than locating emotional engagement in the materials themselves, this theme underscores how it emerges through the interplay of design, bodily participation, and interpersonal care.

Theme 2: Peer Interaction and Social Reciprocity

In addition to enabling individual emotional expression, the Brickers also facilitated moments of peer interaction and emergent social reciprocity. These exchanges, although often subtle and brief, revealed residents’ enduring capacity for shared attention, coordinated rhythm, and mutual recognition. Such forms of interaction are often undervalued in conventional understandings of advanced dementia, where social potential is frequently overlooked or underestimated.

One of the most striking patterns observed was the spontaneous emergence of peer assistance. Residents with relatively preserved communicative abilities frequently offered verbal or gestural support to others without any prompting from staff. In one session, a participant pointed to a Bricker and gently suggested to a neighbor, “Put this one here.” In another, a soft-spoken resident began verbally explaining how to stack the blocks. As one caregiver reflected, “Some of them were helping others. One of the uncles who usually doesn’t talk much suddenly taught another how to stack. It felt like he became a little teacher.”

These forms of peer interaction were enabled by the activity’s accessible and low-pressure structure. Rather than depending on verbal instruction or abstract reasoning, the tasks supported embodied coordination through actions such as stacking, passing, and color matching. These movements facilitated social alignment through gesture, observation, and shared attention. Certain game formats, such as turn-taking or collaborative color identification, naturally encouraged synchronized participation. As one caregiver recalled, “He wasn’t responding to instructions, but he watched the person next to him stacking, then slowly picked up a brick and mirrored the action.”

The small-group setting further supported relational familiarity and emotional safety. Residents who were seated with familiar peers or regular caregiving staff exhibited greater openness to interaction and participation. As one caregiver observed, “They listen more when it’s someone they know, like a regular caregiver or a familiar peer. It’s a kind of emotional security.”

Although often subtle, these moments of reciprocity signaled a sustained capacity for social connection. The Brickers functioned not only as cognitive supports but also as relational mediators, enabling shared rhythms, imitation, and gentle acts of assistance. Within care environments that are frequently characterized by isolation, modest behaviors such as waiting, observing, and mirroring can be understood as meaningful expressions of interpersonal agency.

By emphasizing interaction rather than instruction, and attunement rather than performance, the activity fostered conditions in which peer co-presence could naturally emerge. These findings underscore the importance of designing for relational affordances, where participation is supported through material engagement and the scaffolding effects of familiar group dynamics.

Theme 3: Facilitation as Situated Adaptation

Across all sessions, caregiving staff played a central role in sustaining resident engagement. Their contributions extended beyond task facilitation to include co-creation, emotional mediation, and adaptive improvisation. Rather than adhering to scripted procedures, staff engaged in a form of co-design-in-practice, responding to residents’ needs through real-time interpretation and relational adjustment. This theme is constituted by five interrelated sub-themes that capture the distinct modes of caregiver adaptation: (1) attunement to emotional and relational cues, (2) personalization and differentiation in practice, (3) improvisation and material reinvention, (4) team-based facilitation, and (5) reflective practice as embedded learning.

Attunement to Emotional and Relational Cues

Staff demonstrated consistent attentiveness to residents’ emotional states and affective signals, adjusting their facilitation approach accordingly. Participation was not imposed, but negotiated moment by moment, often through soft verbal prompts, silence, or subtle shifts in pacing. As one caregiver explained, “We try not to push them, especially if we sense they’re about to get upset. It’s important to avoid triggering emotional stress points.”

This sensitivity reflected an embodied familiarity with residents and a cultivated responsiveness to nonverbal communication.

Personalization and Differentiation in Practice

Instead of delivering the activity in a standardized format, caregivers adapted both the content and level of complexity to match participants’ cognitive and sensory capacities. As one caregiver observed, “Some residents get bored if it’s too simple, but others need hand gestures or step-by-step demonstration to know what to do. So we adjust depending on who’s at the table.”

These differentiated strategies revealed a form of pedagogical intuition grounded in close observation and a commitment to inclusive engagement.

Improvisation and Material Reinvention

In the absence of pre-prepared instruction cards or a fixed facilitation script, caregivers created their own visual aids and adapted rules to support participation. One caregiver recounted, “We didn’t have the instruction cards, so I just made some myself. If we give each person a task, they focus better.” These improvised artifacts included color-coded area cards and simple paper guides that indicated where to place the Brickers. Such creative adaptations functioned as facilitation scaffolds, particularly when verbal instruction was insufficient. Consistent with the patterns described in Theme 2, the activity’s low-pressure structure and material engagement also supported residents’ embodied coordination through turn-taking, observation, and mirroring.

These acts of improvisation exemplify how facilitation evolved as a situated and creative process, shaped by the dynamics of the moment and the needs of each group. These adaptive practices reflected not only creative problem-solving but also a strong sense of ownership, reinforcing caregivers’ role as situated designers within the flow of everyday care.

Team-Based Facilitation and Distributed Attunement

Each session involved a primary facilitator supported by additional staff who provided one-on-one guidance and ensured resident safety. This distributed model of facilitation enabled a more responsive approach to diverse resident needs. As one caregiver noted, “Sometimes I feel like I need three pairs of hands. When others help, it really makes a difference.”

At the same time, variations in staff presence or unfamiliar team compositions occasionally disrupted the rhythm of engagement, highlighting the relational infrastructure upon which effective facilitation depends.

Reflective Practice as Embedded Learning

Following each session, caregivers engaged in informal debriefings and peer discussions. Although not formally documented, these reflections informed future adjustments and reinforced a sense of shared learning and mutual support. As one caregiver shared, “I always ask myself after each session… did it go well, what can be improved? Sometimes I ask my colleague for feedback too.”

These iterative learning loops positioned staff as reflective practitioners, continuously refining their practice in response to real-time experience.

Synthesis

Together, these themes demonstrate that meaningful engagement is co-produced through the interplay of design affordances, embodied participation, and relationally responsive care. Emotional expression, social reciprocity, and sustained attention did not arise from the materials alone but were shaped through the ongoing work of adaptation, interpretation, and coordination. The Brickers functioned not only as activity tools but also as flexible mediators of connection, whose meaning and use were actively shaped by those who enacted them.

These findings empirically illustrate the three dimensions of co-design-in-practice. Immediacy is evident in caregivers’ moment-to-moment attunement to residents’ emotional states and their real-time adjustments to pacing, tone, and task complexity (Theme 3). Embodiment manifests in residents’ tactile engagement with the Brickers and caregivers’ use of gesture, demonstration, and bodily presence to facilitate participation (Themes 1 and 2). Relationality emerges through the sustained familiarity between caregivers and residents, the peer interactions that unfolded during sessions, and the team-based coordination that supported distributed attention and care (Themes 2 and 3). Together, these dimensions reveal how engagement in dementia care arises not from designed objects alone but through the ongoing relational and embodied work of caregiving staff.

The following discussion situates these findings within broader theoretical frameworks of person-centered care, embodied selfhood, and design-in-use. Their implications for dementia care and participatory design are examined with attention to the lived complexities of long-term care environments.

Discussion

Reframing Engagement as Relational, Embodied, and Situated

Traditional models of engagement in dementia care often conceptualize participation as an individual response to external stimuli, typically assessed through metrics such as task completion or sustained attention (Douglas et al., 2018). While such frameworks have informed many activity-based interventions, they risk reducing engagement to a binary of activation versus passivity. This study proposes an alternative understanding of engagement as a co-constructed phenomenon that emerges through the interplay of people, materials, and care ecologies.

Our findings suggest that meaningful engagement should be understood not as a fixed outcome but as a situated and relational process shaped by emotional expression, embodied interaction, and interpersonal attunement. This perspective aligns with Kitwood’s (1997) person-centered approach, which emphasizes the social foundations of identity and the centrality of emotional affirmation. It also resonates with Kontos’ (2005) theory of embodied selfhood, which foregrounds gesture, rhythm, and sensory expression as core modes of meaning-making. In our study, residents engaged not only with the Brickers but also with caregivers and peers, using smiles, mirroring, and shared movement to participate in socially significant ways.

Furthermore, these findings extend the concept of social citizenship in dementia (Bartlett & O’Connor, 2010) by illustrating how even modest actions, such as turn-taking, helping a peer, or attending to group rhythm, can be understood as expressions of agency. These forms of participation were not solely dependent on cognitive ability but emerged from the relational and material conditions that made engagement possible.

Design as a Medium of Situated Participation

Rather than viewing design as a fixed intervention, this study conceptualizes it as a relational medium whose meaning is continuously shaped through use. The Colorful Counting Brickers did not function as isolated tools, but as catalysts for sensory stimulation, emotional expression, and social interaction. Their intuitive tactility, vibrant colors, and modular rhythm aligned with principles drawn from Montessori-based and DementiAbility approaches (Camp, 1999; Elliot, 2019). However, their significance was realized in practice, specifically through the ways caregivers introduced, adapted, and sustained them within everyday care contexts.

Although the Brickers were intentionally designed with sensory affordances and intuitive engagement in mind, their effectiveness emerged not from these material features alone, but from the ways they were flexibly enacted in everyday care. Their value was co-produced through the dynamic, emotionally attuned actions of caregivers.

This interplay between structured design and improvisational use was central to the intervention’s success. The materials offered a scaffold for engagement, yet it was the caregivers’ interpretive adjustments, including modulating pacing, modifying rules, and creating visual cues, that made the activities meaningful. These adaptations were not deviations from the design intent but were essential to how the intervention became situated, relationally responsive, and culturally grounded in practice.

Accordingly, we argue that sustainable dementia design must move beyond standardized usability metrics and instead foster what we describe as design within practice, an approach in which care workers animate, reinterpret, and sustain interventions through situated facilitation that is emotionally responsive and contextually embedded.

This perspective aligns with Redström’s (2008) notion of design-in-use, where the function and meaning of an artifact are continuously redefined in situ through real-time adjustment and relational interaction. Suchman’s (1987) theory of situated action further reinforces this view, suggesting that effective engagement is not the product of predetermined protocols but emerges through moment-to-moment responsiveness to social and environmental cues.

Our findings also resonate with Buse and Twigg’s (2016, 2018) work on material participation, which emphasizes how objects act as mediators of social life within care settings. The Brickers served as relational scaffolds that evoked shared attention, invited imitation, and facilitated co-presence. In doing so, they translated abstract design principles into lived, affectively rich interactions.

Taken together, these dynamics invite an expanded understanding of participation, shifting the focus from discrete user engagement with objects to a broader conception of embedded interpretation and co-performance by caregivers, as explored in the following section.

Caregivers as Situated Agents of Co-Design-In-Practice

While traditional participatory design (PD) typically emphasizes structured collaboration through workshops, co-ideation sessions, or formal prototyping phases, this study proposes the concept of co-design-in-practice as a conceptual extension. This lens captures emergent, informal, and embodied forms of design participation embedded in the routines of dementia care. It recognizes care workers as active design contributors, not through conventional design stages, but through real-time improvisation, adaptive facilitation, and intuitive reinterpretation of materials.

Rather than diminishing the rigor of PD, this framing responds to ongoing calls within design research (e.g., Björgvinsson et al., 2012; Light & Akama, 2014) to recognize practice-based and micro-level forms of design agency. It acknowledges that in care settings, where time constraints, power asymmetries, and communication barriers often inhibit formal co-design, practice-driven participation may offer a more viable and culturally appropriate model of engagement. Within this view, caregiving becomes not only an affective and relational act, but also a site of situated design work.

Although this study did not employ formal participatory design methods such as workshops or structured design sessions, it was informed by a conceptual commitment to participatory principles. Recent work in design for dementia (Niedderer et al., 2017) and reflections on diverse forms of participation (Hendriks et al., 2014) suggest that participatory processes can unfold through everyday interactions and relational practices, not only through formal co-design events. Our approach aligns with this broader understanding of participation, recognizing that care workers’ adaptive practices constitute a form of situated design work. What traditional PD might accomplish through workshops, we observe emerging through the embodied, relational labor of daily care.

Building on this perspective, we do not propose co-design-in-practice as a secondary or diminished mode of participation, but rather as an equally legitimate, culturally attuned and contextually grounded form of design agency, particularly in settings where formal co-design is impractical or ethically inappropriate. This framing recognizes how care staff creatively adapt materials and reconfigure interactions during use, not as part of a discrete design phase but as an integral and ongoing part of relational care practice.

The ways in which caregivers adjusted pacing, simplified rules, improvised prompts, and created visual aids exemplify the principles of design-in-use (Redström, 2008) and situated action (Suchman, 1987), where meaning and utility are continuously shaped through real-time engagement. These actions were not peripheral to the design process but central to how the intervention became meaningful and resonant in context.

This reframing positions caregivers not as passive implementers, but as situated co-constructors of engagement. Their interpretive labor, grounded in embodied knowledge and relational sensitivity, transformed the Brickers from static artifacts into dynamic and socially meaningful experiences. These insights contribute to recent conversations in participatory design that call for a broader understanding of participation beyond formalized methods (Bannon & Ehn, 2012; Light & Akama, 2014). By extending participatory design to encompass situated, practice-based adaptations, this study affirms the legitimacy of caregiving work as a form of everyday design agency.

The Three Dimensions of Co-Design-In-Practice in Action

The findings illustrate how co-design-in-practice operates through three interrelated dimensions: immediacy, embodiment, and relationality. Each of these dimensions contributes distinctly to the emergence of meaningful engagement in dementia care.

Immediacy refers to caregivers’ real-time responsiveness to residents’ affective and behavioral cues. This dimension was evident throughout the sessions, as staff continuously adjusted pacing, tone, and task complexity based on moment-to-moment observations. Rather than following predetermined scripts, caregivers enacted what Suchman (1987) describes as situated action, responding fluidly to the unfolding dynamics of each interaction. This form of immediacy requires not only observational skill but also emotional sensitivity and practical judgment cultivated through sustained caregiving relationships. It transforms facilitation from a procedural task into an improvisational practice attuned to the relational present.

Embodiment describes how design knowledge is carried and enacted through bodily action. Both residents and caregivers participated through gesture, touch, rhythm, and spatial positioning. Residents engaged with the Brickers through tactile exploration, mirroring, and coordinated movement, while caregivers used demonstration, hand-over-hand guidance, and postural adjustments to scaffold participation. This embodied dimension aligns with Kontos’ (2005) theory of embodied selfhood, which positions the body as a site of meaning-making that persists beyond verbal communication. Our findings extend this concept by showing how caregivers’ embodied practices, including their pacing, gestures, and affective presence, constitute a form of embodied design work that animates and sustains engagement.

Relationality emphasizes how design participation is embedded within caregiver-resident relationships and the broader social ecology of care. The effectiveness of adaptive strategies depended on the relational infrastructure of the care unit, including the continuity provided by designated caregiver teams, the emotional safety cultivated through familiar group compositions, and the trust developed through sustained interaction. This relational dimension resonates with Jenkins’ (2014) concept of inter-embodied identity, wherein selfhood and agency emerge through shared practices and mutual recognition. In the context of dementia care, relationality is not simply a backdrop for engagement but a constitutive condition. It is through relationships that materials acquire meaning, activities become safe to explore, and participation feels possible.

Together, these three dimensions reveal co-design-in-practice as a holistic, relationally embedded process. Immediacy ensures temporal responsiveness, embodiment grounds participation in sensory and gestural experience, and relationality situates these practices within networks of care and trust. This triadic framework offers a conceptual tool for understanding how engagement in dementia care emerges not from interventions alone but through the situated, embodied, and relational work of caregiving.

Designing for Relational Care: Implications for Practice and Research

This study offers important implications for both dementia care practice and design research. First, it emphasizes that interventions should be evaluated not only by usability or clinical outcomes but also by their capacity to support emotional presence, social reciprocity, and relational safety. Measures of engagement must account for subtle, embodied forms of interaction, such as eye contact, attuned silence, and rhythmic synchronization, which may reflect well-being in ways that are not captured by conventional metrics.

Second, the study affirms the role of care workers as active co-constructors of engagement. Their practical expertise, emotional sensitivity, and adaptive creativity represent essential design resources that should be supported, documented, and meaningfully integrated into design processes. Care environments should therefore be understood as dynamic spaces of design-in-practice, where improvisation and informal reflection continually shape how interventions take root and unfold.

For designers, this calls for a shift in perspective: care settings should be approached not simply as user environments, but as relational systems in which objects, routines, and human interactions co-evolve. Design efforts should prioritize open-ended, low-threshold artifacts that invite adaptation and improvisation. In addition, training programs and institutional policies should recognize and support caregivers as relational designers who generate and sustain meaningful participation through situated, embodied practice.

Toward Situated Design Inquiry in Dementia Care

The practice of relational facilitation observed in Taiwanese dementia care, characterized by affective attunement, group rhythm, and embodied expressions of xiao (filial piety), offers more than a localized care strategy. It presents a culturally grounded paradigm of design participation.

Unlike Euro-American co-design traditions that often emphasize explicit negotiation and structured ideation, this model foregrounds tacit knowledge, relational harmony, and intuitive responsiveness. Co-design, in this context, is not a discrete event but an ongoing relational dynamic, enacted through silence, gesture, and context-sensitive improvisation.

Such practices challenge dominant assumptions in the co-design literature that equate participation with verbal articulation or formal consensus. These relational practices resonate with the idea of moral attunement (Light & Akama, 2014), in which care and participation are enacted through culturally situated moral orientations rather than explicit deliberation. In the Taiwanese care context, such orientations are shaped by relational norms like xiao (filial piety), which emphasize tacit responsibility, emotional attentiveness, and deference to social harmony. This culturally situated moral dynamic can also be understood through Jenkins’ (2014) concept of inter-embodied identity, whereby selfhood and agency emerge relationally through shared bodily practices and affective attunement. Together, these perspectives highlight alternative epistemologies of care that foreground communal responsibility, embodied sensitivity, and moral obligation as legitimate design forces.

This research advances a situated model of design inquiry that bridges theoretical frameworks with the grounded realities of care. It demonstrates how concepts such as design-in-use, material participation, and co-design-in-practice can be operationalized through the everyday actions of caregivers in long-term dementia care. At the same time, it invites critical reflection on the cultural specificity of these frameworks.

In advancing this culturally grounded model of design participation, this study also engages with recent scholarship on care aesthetics and sensory qualities in dementia settings. Research on care aesthetics (Thompson, 2022), everyday aesthetics in institutional care (Fleetwood-Smith et al., 2022), and the aesthetic dimensions of domestic dementia care (Fox et al., 2025) has demonstrated how sensory, spatial, and relational qualities shape lived experience and well-being. Our findings extend this perspective by showing that aesthetic engagement in dementia care emerges not solely from designed objects or environmental qualities but through the ongoing relational and embodied practices of caregivers. Materials become meaningful through interpretation, adaptation, and attunement, which are processes that constitute a form of aesthetic labor integral to relational care.

Although many of the theoretical constructs cited originate from Euro-American contexts, this study reinterprets them through Taiwanese care practices. It shows how global concepts can be recontextualized within culturally informed routines, thereby contributing to a broader movement toward culturally sensitive, practice-based dementia research (Buse & Twigg, 2018; Treadaway et al., 2020).

This recontextualization draws on relational norms embedded in Taiwanese care settings, including collectivist values, interpersonal attunement, and embodied expressions of respect and care. Caregivers’ intuitive adaptation and affective calibration reflected not only technical competencies but also culturally resonant values of familial duty (xiao) and emotional harmony. These practices gave rise to forms of engagement that stand in contrast to Western models premised on verbal articulation and individual autonomy. By attending to these cultural textures, the study not only localizes global design theories but also expands them, offering alternative relational paradigms for understanding agency, participation, and co-design-in-practice in dementia care.

Rather than proposing a fixed design model, the study advances a processual and relational framework that foregrounds emotional resonance, situational adaptability, and the co-creative labor of care. It affirms that meaningful engagement is not merely an outcome to be measured, but a quality of interaction to be supported, interpreted, and sustained through design, caregiving, and shared presence.

This orientation also points to a methodological implication: capturing co-design-in-practice requires analytic forms that can attend to shifting roles, frictions, and negotiations across moments and sessions, particularly when participation is expressed through sensory, affective, and relational forms rather than speech. Following calls for ‘method stories’ in participatory dementia design (Hendriks et al., 2015), future work could more explicitly trace how residents’ embodied responses and caregivers’ situated judgments co-constitute participation over time, making visible the subtle, distributed, and relational work through which engagement is sustained.

In recognizing both the micro-design labor of caregivers and the cultural values that shape their actions, this study advocates for a relational turn in dementia design research, one that values not only what is designed but also how it is lived, interpreted, and sustained within the everyday textures of care. By bridging cultural practices and theoretical frameworks, this study contributes to a more pluralistic understanding of engagement in dementia care.

Conclusion

This study examined how a play-based, multisensory intervention, the Colorful Counting Brickers, supported emotional and social engagement among older adults with dementia in a Taiwanese long-term care setting. Its central contribution is conceptual: we introduce co-design-in-practice to name a form of design participation that has been largely overlooked in dementia research. Rather than occurring only in workshops or structured co-creation sessions, this design work is enacted by caregiving staff through moment-to-moment adjustments in pacing, gesture, verbal prompting, and spatial arrangement, grounded in embodied familiarity and emotional attunement. Operationalized through the dimensions of immediacy, embodiment, and relationality, the framework reframes caregiving not as the implementation of designed objects but as an ongoing, situated act of design in its own right.

This reframing carries both theoretical and practical significance. Theoretically, it extends participatory design and design-in-use scholarship by locating design agency within the relational and culturally embedded routines of care, including Taiwanese values such as xiao (filial piety). In doing so, it broadens prevailing assumptions that associate participation primarily with verbal articulation, formal consensus, or structured design events. Practically, it invites designers, care organizations, and policymakers to recognize caregivers as relational designers and to support open-ended, adaptable artifacts rather than prescriptive tools. While the study does not claim clinical generalizability, its findings point to the value of further inquiry across diverse cultural, institutional, and policy settings.

Ultimately, this study calls for a shift from designing objects to designing with and through care. In dementia contexts, meaningful engagement arises not only from what is offered, but also from how it is taken up moment by moment, in emotional, social, and relational terms, by those who live and care together.
