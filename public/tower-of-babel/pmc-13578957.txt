Differential perforin sensitivity drives lineage-dependent resistance to CAR T-cell killing in blood cancer cell lines∗

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

TO THE EDITOR:

Cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) play a crucial role in cancer control by eliminating cancerous cells primarily through perforin/granzyme-dependent cytotoxicity. Activated CTLs form an immunological synapse with antigen-presenting target cells and release perforin and proapoptotic serine proteases, granzymes, into the synaptic cleft, with perforin rapidly forming transmembrane pores that enable granzymes to enter the target cell cytosol and induce apoptosis.1 Although perforin is essential for CTL cytotoxicity, individual granzymes are partially redundant.

CTL cytotoxicity is harnessed in adoptive cell therapies of cancer, such as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell immunotherapy. This approach has shown remarkable success against B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia and against diffuse large B-cell lymphoma2 but is less effective in other hematological cancers such as acute myeloid leukemia (AML)3 and solid tumors.4

Resistance to CTL cytotoxicity and CAR T-cell therapy can arise through mechanisms that disrupt perforin/granzyme-dependent cytotoxicity.5 Earlier studies using crude perforin-containing granule extracts suggested that primary myeloid, but not lymphoid cancer cells, were more resistant to lysis, with varying susceptibility among individual patients.6,7 Whether the sensitivity of different hematological cancers to perforin bestows resistance to immune killing remains unresolved.

A primary challenge in comparing the sensitivity of various tumor types to CAR T-cell killing is the lack of shared target antigens and variability in their expression levels. Thus, unable to select a universal antigen receptor, we have taken a different approach by selecting 20 human cell lines representing myeloid (10), lymphoid B-cell (6), and lymphoid T-cell (4) lineages (supplemental Table 1). To standardize antigen exposure, we transduced the CD19− cell lines (myeloid and lymphoid T cells) with truncated human CD19 (hCD19t) comprised of the extracellular and transmembrane domains only.8 hCD19t-expressing cells were sorted to derive populations with comparable levels of hCD19t expression (supplemental Figure 1). Anti-hCD19 CAR T-cell cytotoxicity was assessed using a 4-hour 51Cr release assay, revealing marked differences in target-cell susceptibility (Figure 1A; supplemental Methods). Two cell lines, Kasumi I and RPMI 8226, were initially considered but excluded from further analysis because their death was mediated by mechanisms other than perforin-dependent cytotoxicity. Specifically, cell death was poorly inhibited by the Ca2+ chelator EGTA (supplemental Figure 2), which prevents perforin-mediated cell death but does not affect FAS- (CD95) or tumor necrosis factor α death receptor–mediated apoptosis. CD107a (LAMP-1) externalization on CAR T cells, a direct surrogate marker of cytotoxic granule exocytosis, was comparable across targets, indicating intact synapse formation and degranulation and arguing against defective CAR-target interactions as the cause of differential cell death (Figure 1B; supplemental Figures 3-5).

Next, we compared the sensitivity of the 20 cell lines to purified recombinant perforin.10 We observed a similar pattern: the cell lines resistant to CAR T-cell killing were also resistant to perforin-mediated lysis (Figure 1C). Both the Michaelis-Menten constant (Km) of immune-mediated killing and the 50% inhibitory concentration of perforin lysis differed by an order of magnitude between the most and least sensitive cell lines and were strongly correlated (Pearson r = 0.7; ∗∗P = .001; Figure 1D).

To assess the effect of perforin in the context of the immune synapse, we used single-cell time-lapse microscopy to visualize live synapses formed between CAR T and target cells. In this assay, perforin-mediated membrane disruption was detected by propidium iodide entering the cytosol and fluorescing upon binding to cytosolic RNA.11 We compared hCD19t-transduced sensitive Jurkat E6-1 cells with the resistant HL60 and PL21 cell lines. Consistent with our previous findings, membrane disruption, measured using a direct readout of perforin pore formation, was markedly reduced in the CAR T-cell–resistant cell lines (Figure 1E-F).

As perforin does not inflict cell death as a sole agent but relies on synergy with granzymes, it was important to determine whether the differential sensitivity to CAR T cells might be related to differential cell death signaling in the target cell cytosol. Murine granzyme B, the most potent cytotoxic granzyme, uses a distinct apoptotic pathway in that it directly processes procaspases-3 and -7 to their active forms, whereas human granzyme B primarily activates caspases through the intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway by cleaving the proapoptotic BH3-only protein twice daily. As mouse granzyme B cleaves twice daily with only ∼3% the efficiency of human granzyme B,12, 13, 14 we hypothesized that using murine anti-CD19 CAR T cells to target human cell lines expressing the extracellular domain of murine CD19 (mCD19t; supplemental Figure 6) would reveal contributions to cell death that are independent of their susceptibility to perforin-mediated membrane damage.

Our results showed that killing by mouse anti-mCD19 CAR T cells (Figure 2A) paralleled that of human CAR T cells and was again consistent with variable sensitivity to perforin-mediated lysis (Figure 2B), exhibiting a strong correlation (Pearson r = 0.7; ∗∗P = .008; Figure 2C). Of note, all cell lines in the resistant group were of myeloid lineage (Figure 2D). To assess whether primary myeloid cells are intrinsically resistant to perforin, we exposed peripheral blood mononuclear cells from healthy donors to increasing concentrations of purified recombinant perforin and assessed the progressive depletion of B cells, T cells, and monocytes, as an indicator of their perforin susceptibility. Monocytes withstood the osmotic challenge induced by perforin better than lymphoid cells, displaying a similar level of resistance to that observed in control perforin-resistant THP1 cells (Figure 2E). Collectively, these findings suggest that relative tolerance to perforin is an inherent property of myeloid cell lineages.

Our findings provide evidence that myeloid-derived cancers possess a lineage-dependent mechanism of perforin resistance, inherited from their healthy progenitors. The observation that normal cells of distinct lineages differ in their susceptibility to CTL-mediated cytotoxicity also suggests the existence of previously unrecognized intrinsic mechanisms regulating lineage-specific sensitivity to CTL-mediated cytotoxicity.

Plasma membrane adaptations that may protect against perforin in cancer cells and protect CTLs against their own toxin include the externalization of negatively charged phosphatidylserine.15,9 Flow cytometry measurements using a fluorescently tagged marker for phosphatidylserine, annexin V, produced no evidence of its increased exposure in resistant cell lines (supplemental Figure 7A-B). More recently, filamin A has been suggested to play an important role in CTL and target cell resistance to perforin (through regulating cell softness)16; however, we found no correlation between filamin A expression and perforin sensitivity (supplemental Figure 7C-D). These findings indicate that the resistance mechanism(s) in myeloid cells are distinct from or more complex than those in CTLs. This mechanistic difference raises the possibility of selectively targeting perforin resistance in myeloid malignancies without disrupting CTL homeostasis.

Although the mechanism of tumor cell resistance to CTL-mediated immunotherapy remains under investigation, the up to 10-fold reduction in immune killing observed here is likely to be biologically important. For comparison, inherited perforin mutations that impair CTL function to a similar degree can result in severe immune dysregulation.17 In the context of AML, perforin resistance represents a previously unrecognized mechanism of immune evasion, complementing the well-established immunosuppressive bone marrow microenvironment. These findings are consistent with the limited efficacy of CTL-based immunotherapies in AML. This work establishes a conceptual framework for dissecting the determinants of immune-mediated cytotoxicity in AML, guiding future research into novel therapeutic targets. The detailed characterization of 20 cell lines presented here also provides a valuable reference for analyzing primary patient samples in future studies and may inform investigations of other hematological or solid malignancies.

Limitations of this study include the primary use of established cell lines, and further validation in primary patient samples will be important to confirm the physiological relevance of these findings. Previous studies have reported reduced sensitivity of primary AML samples to perforin-mediated lysis,6,7 consistent with our observations in myeloid cell lines and healthy primary monocytes. Furthermore, although our data identify perforin sensitivity as a major determinant of immune-mediated killing, the mechanistic basis underlying differential perforin resistance remains unclear and warrants further investigation.
