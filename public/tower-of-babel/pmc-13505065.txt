Subway network accessibility and carbon balance: evidence from China’s dynamic centrality analysis
Authors: Gao Ziming, Wang Yuanyuan, Lv Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmid the ongoing transition toward low-carbon urban development, subway networks, as a modern mode of transportation, have become a key focus of academic attention. However, existing research suffers from two critical gaps: first, a predominant focus on either carbon emissions or carbon absorption in isolation, rather than their equilibrium; second, a simplistic “point‑line” physical assessment of subways that overlooks network‑level accessibility and topology. This study addresses these gaps by adopting the carbon balance ratio (the ratio of terrestrial carbon absorption to anthropogenic emissions) as the outcome variable and employing Social Network Analysis to construct dynamic, station-level subway network centrality metrics in China. Empirical findings reveal that a one‑unit increase in county‑level subway network accessibility significantly improves the carbon balance ratio. This effect is strongest in economically underdeveloped regions, areas with compact spatial structures, and zones with lower residential electricity consumption. Mechanism analysis reveals a tri‑phase contribution: (1) during construction, subways preserve green space and carbon absorption capability by avoiding surface disruption; (2) during operation, they induce modal shifts that reduce transport emissions; and (3) through industrial transformation, their carbon benefits are amplified by supportive institutions, market mechanisms, and technological capabilities. By systematically integrating carbon balance measurement with network‑level subway accessibility, this study offers a comprehensive empirical framework and provides policy insights for integrating rail transit into low‑carbon spatial governance strategies.Keywords: Subway network, Carbon balance, Transportation infrastructure, Green development

Introduction

Driven by the low-carbon transformation of cities, reducing emissions from the transportation sector has become a critical breakthrough. Subway systems, with their advantages of high passenger capacity and high energy efficiency, play a crucial role in optimizing urban spatial structure, guiding residents toward green travel, and promoting the low-carbon restructuring of industrial layouts. However, existing literature on the carbon balance effects of subways remains limited. China is undergoing urbanization at an unprecedented pace and has developed a comprehensive urban rail transit system, including subways, trams, light rail, monorails, and maglev systems. According to the Statistical Bulletin on Transportation Development (2024), China’s urban rail transit operates 72,300 vehicles, carries 32.209 billion passengers annually, and spans a total route length of 10,923.8 km. This provides a unique research context for analyzing the impact of modern transportation infrastructure on carbon balance.

Despite this potential, existing research exhibits significant gaps in understanding how the development of subway networks comprehensively affects urban carbon balance. A critical oversight is the predominant focus on either carbon emissions or carbon absorption in isolation, rather than on their equilibrium. The carbon balance ratio (defined as the ratio of terrestrial carbon absorption to anthropogenic carbon emissions) offers a more integrated measure of regional carbon metabolic status [1]. Unlike simple emission metrics, it captures the dual dynamics of human-induced carbon outputs and the ecosystem’s residual capacity to offset them [2]. This ratio is particularly relevant for evaluating infrastructure interventions, as it reflects whether a development strategy contributes to a net ecological surplus or merely shifts the locus of emissions. Empirically, studies on urban carbon dynamics have increasingly recognized the importance of accounting for both emission sources and absorption capacities when assessing policy effectiveness [2–4]. Accordingly, this study adopts the carbon balance ratio as its core dependent variable, enabling a more comprehensive assessment of how subway network development influences the equilibrium between urban emissions and natural carbon absorption.

To systematically investigate this relationship, this study develops a theoretical framework that distinguishes three temporally nested mechanisms through which subway networks affect carbon balance: the construction phase, the operational phase, and the industrial transformation phase. During construction, the underground nature of metro systems minimizes surface land occupation and preserves existing vegetation, soil carbon pools, and urban green spaces—a distinct advantage over road networks that fragment ecosystems. During operation, subway networks induce a large-scale modal shift from private vehicles to low-carbon rail transit, directly reducing transportation-related emissions. Over the longer term, the industrial transformation phase captures how improved connectivity reshapes economic geography: service sectors agglomerate around transit hubs, while manufacturing relocates to peripheral areas, with the carbon benefits of this spatial restructuring contingent upon institutional support, market mechanisms, and technological capabilities. These three phases form a progressive causal chain that collectively enhances regional carbon balance. Figure 1 presents a schematic overview of this analytical framework.

Analytical Framework

Regarding the socio-economic effects of subways, existing research indicates that subway construction can enhance regional accessibility, guide the agglomeration of population and employment around subway stations, increase land values and housing prices along subway lines, and reshape urban spatial form [5, 6]. Furthermore, as a low-carbon mode of transportation, subways can significantly reduce carbon emissions and air pollution [7, 8]. Leveraging their advantages of punctuality, speed, and high passenger capacity, subways significantly shorten residents’ travel time, alleviate traffic congestion, improve commuting efficiency, mitigate to some extent the social inequality arising from job-housing separation, and enhance residents’ accessibility to urban public services and their sense of well-being [9, 10]. A review of the existing literature reveals insufficient attention to the carbon effects arising from the spatial network evolution of subways, with most studies focusing on carbon emissions [11, 12], while the mechanisms through which subway network expansion influences carbon balance across multiple dimensions, including construction, operation, and industrial transformation, remain underexplored.

In terms of research methods, existing studies have predominantly employed approaches such as difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, and spatial difference-in-differences to investigate the socio-economic effects of subways [7, 13, 14]. Some studies have also used subway mileage as a key explanatory variable [6]. However, these methods are largely confined to capturing the binary or linear effects of whether a subway line is opened or the length of the line, thereby overlooking the interconnections, hierarchical structures, and functional differentiation among nodes within the subway network. By employing Social Network Analysis (SNA), this study aims to more comprehensively capture the impact of the spatial structural evolution of subway networks on regional carbon balance and its transmission mechanisms, addressing key questions such as: how does the dynamic evolution of subway network topology reshape patterns of spatial accessibility, and how do these spatial restructuring effects subsequently influence the carbon balance ratio?

The innovative contributions of this study are reflected in three key aspects. First, it substantially extends existing research on the influencing factors of carbon balance and the socio-economic impacts of metro systems. By adopting the carbon balance ratio as the outcome variable, this study moves beyond prior work that examines carbon emissions or carbon absorption in isolation, offering a more integrated assessment of how infrastructure development affects the equilibrium between anthropogenic emissions and natural absorption. Theoretically, this research extends Transit-Oriented Development (TOD) theory and low-carbon transportation systems theory by conceptualizing subway networks as a “space-energy-economy” coupled system. Rather than treating subways merely as a mode of transport, the framework recognizes them as spatial organizers that simultaneously influence land use patterns, energy consumption behaviors, and economic structures across the full infrastructure life cycle. Empirically, this study supplements the understanding of carbon balance effects arising from the spatial development of subway networks, with its research focus covering cities and counties in China that have opened subways, moving beyond previous case studies limited to individual cities, and enriches the analysis of factors influencing both carbon reduction and carbon absorption through public transportation development.

Second, surpassing the simplistic “point-line” physical attribute analysis prevalent in existing subway-related studies, this research pioneers the application of complex network analysis methods to evaluate metro accessibility at the national scale. By constructing station-level connectivity networks across all metro cities in China and aggregating centrality metrics to the county/district level, we provide a systematic and comparable assessment of subway network development. This methodological innovation, which integrates Social Network Analysis (SNA) with carbon balance accounting, offers a more economically meaningful indicator with enhanced real-world explanatory power than conventional binary measures of metro presence.

Third, from the integrated perspectives of ecology, lifestyle, and production, this paper develops a multi-level empirical analysis framework to investigate how subway network accessibility influences carbon balance. This framework systematically examines the underground space advantage and green resource preservation effect during construction, the eco-friendly mobility and transportation behavior shift effect during operation, and the industrial agglomeration transformation and energy efficiency improvement effect—moderated by institutional support, market mechanisms, and technological capabilities—during the industrial transformation phase. Collectively, these contributions expand the analytical perspective on the environmental impacts of transportation infrastructure and provide significant policy implications for optimizing urban green transportation systems and refining spatial governance strategies oriented toward low-carbon transition. The findings also offer insights for understanding the carbon reduction multiplier effects of intercity subway networks through network extension and functional decentralization, thereby contributing to the pursuit of carbon neutrality goals in metropolitan regions and urban agglomerations.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Sect. 2 presents the research background and literature review. Section 3 elaborates the theoretical framework and develops the research hypotheses. Section 4 details the research design, including data sources, variable construction, and the empirical model. Section 5 reports the baseline results, causal inference, robustness checks, and heterogeneity analyses. Section 6 explores the three mechanisms underlying the main effect. The final section concludes with policy recommendations and research implications.

Research background and literature review

Socioeconomic impacts of subway systems

In recent years, scholars worldwide have extensively examined the socioeconomic impacts of subway systems, revealing multidimensional effects spanning innovation-driven development, spatial restructuring, industrial transformation, and improvements in quality of life.

Regarding innovation, existing research underscores that subways not only reshape human capital mobility by reducing commuting time and costs but also enhance employee well-being, strengthen corporate talent attraction, and facilitate localized knowledge spillovers among universities, research institutions, and enterprises [15]. In terms of spatial restructuring, subway construction has proven effective in curbing urban sprawl, redirecting population and resources toward station-adjacent clusters, reorganizing job-housing spatial relationships, and optimizing intensive land use [16]. On industrial transformation, subway-induced land value appreciation along transit corridors accelerates the relocation of traditional industries and fosters the agglomeration of capital- and technology-intensive service sectors, thereby boosting regional total factor productivity and advancing industrial structure [17, 18]. For residents’ livelihoods, subways reduce both the economic and temporal burdens of commuting, mitigate congestion costs through enhanced transportation accessibility, and unlock consumption potential [9, 19].

Despite the rich body of research on the multidimensional socioeconomic effects of metro systems, a significant gap remains: existing studies predominantly focus on the direct emission-reduction benefits during metro operation, failing to systematically examine the integrated impact of subway networks on regional carbon balance across their full life cycle. The construction phase, operational phase, and the subsequent industrial transformation induced by subway development constitute a complex causal chain whose ultimate contribution to carbon balance remains inadequately quantified. This study addresses this gap by proposing and empirically testing a three-stage theoretical framework (construction, operation, industrial transformation) that captures the full sequence of carbon balance effects, moving beyond a narrow focus on operational emission reductions.

Evaluation of transportation system networks

Regarding transportation infrastructure networks, existing research can be divided into two categories: intercity studies and intracity studies. For intercity networks, studies have estimated transportation costs using highly disaggregated transportation network distribution data, characterizing the expansion and construction of intercity networks [20–22]. For intracity networks, transportation infrastructure has been evaluated by deriving transport flows between nodes based on U.S. highway networks and the Seattle road network [23]. Other studies have employed machine learning methods to assess the green effects of transportation infrastructure layouts through simulation analyses involving private vehicles, buses, subways, and charging stations [24]. Regarding urban metro spatial networks, current research primarily focuses on natural science case studies of individual cities, including investigations into network topological structures, coupling characteristics, and the identification of critical nodes. Specific examples from China include studies on metro systems in Beijing, Nanjing, and Shenyang [18, 25–27].

A critical research gap persists in this domain: existing studies lack a systematic and comprehensive framework for evaluating subway spatial connectivity networks, particularly at the national scale. Current research predominantly focuses on road transportation or the macro-level integrated development of transportation infrastructure, failing to construct physical connectivity network models based on the geographical location of metro stations, the linkage relationships between lines, and subterranean transfer accessibility. Moreover, no study has employed Social Network Analysis (SNA) methods to quantitatively assess the overall structural characteristics and connectivity efficiency of subway networks across all metro cities in China. This study fills this gap by constructing a dynamic, station-level subway connectivity network covering all metro cities nationwide, and by aggregating station centrality metrics to the county/district level, thereby enabling a comparable assessment of the holistic and networked development level of subway systems.

Influencing factors of carbon balance

Existing research has extensively explored the intrinsic drivers of carbon budgets within key natural ecosystems vital for maintaining global carbon balance, such as forests, grasslands, and wetlands [1, 28]. Climate change is widely recognized as the primary natural driver disrupting global and regional carbon balance patterns, with impact pathways that are multidimensional and complexly intertwined [29–31]. Regarding direct impacts, sustained temperature increases profoundly affect biogeochemical cycling processes, accelerating the decomposition rate of soil organic carbon [31]. Regarding indirect impacts, climate change exerts profound disturbances by triggering extreme events such as droughts and wildfires, which severely suppress vegetation photosynthetic efficiency and inflict devastating damage on ecosystem structure and function [32, 33].

Correspondingly, human activities have become the dominant force driving the persistent imbalance in carbon budgets [34]. The extensive expansion of the industrial sector and its over-reliance on fossil energy are widely acknowledged as core anthropogenic factors driving the surge in carbon emissions [35, 36]. Rapid urbanization processes directly consume natural surfaces with significant carbon absorption functions, such as green spaces and agricultural land, while also altering the spatial structure and ecological functions of terrestrial ecosystems [31, 37]. Notably, cities exert a decisive influence on local and regional carbon fluxes through their spatial structure, transportation organization patterns, and infrastructure layout [1].

Despite rich existing research achievements on the impacts of both natural and anthropogenic factors on carbon balance, a significant research gap remains. The systematic exploration of the direct and indirect mechanisms through which urban underground space development enhances carbon absorption capacity and overall carbon balance levels has not received sufficient attention. Current research predominantly focuses on the direct benefits of metros in reducing transportation carbon emissions, yet rarely delves into how the spatial structure of subway networks indirectly affects carbon balance, such as through altering land use patterns and influencing the persistence of ecological spaces. Furthermore, existing studies lack a comparative analysis of the differential impacts that subway network expansion versus road network expansion exert on surface vegetation.

Subway and carbon balance

In the existing literature, studies that directly take carbon balance as the dependent variable and examine the impact of subway networks on it remain rare. However, scholars have conducted relevant analyses from different perspectives. First, some studies focus on the impact of subway systems on carbon emissions, finding that subways, as a green transportation mode, can effectively replace high-carbon travel modes such as private cars by virtue of their advantages of large passenger capacity, electric power, and low unit energy consumption [7]. A case study in Shenzhen confirmed that prefabricated composite subway stations have a significant inhibitory effect on greenhouse gas emissions, while research on the multi‑modal transportation system in the Guangdong‑Hong Kong‑Macao Greater Bay Area further quantified and validated its carbon emission reduction effects [38, 39]. Second, other studies focus on the impact of subways on urban carbon sinks. Existing research finds that during subway construction, land disturbance, vegetation destruction, and surface subsidence risks can lead to carbon sink losses [8, 65]. At the same time, the predominantly underground nature of subways reduces direct encroachment on surface ecosystems, and by transferring a large amount of traffic and commercial functions underground, it releases above‑ground land resources that can be used for new public green spaces and ecological plazas, thereby creating spatial conditions for the expansion of urban carbon sinks [44]. Furthermore, considering that rapid and disorderly urban expansion during urbanization is an important cause of green space fragmentation and carbon sink loss, the Transit‑Oriented Development (TOD) model, which relies on subway construction to guide intensive spatial development, can curb low‑density urban sprawl and effectively protect ecological land along transit corridors [16, 45].

The above studies mostly focus on the carbon emission reduction effects during the operation phase of subways or the impacts on local carbon sinks during the construction period, failing to incorporate carbon emissions and carbon sinks into a unified analytical framework of carbon balance. Regarding the research sample, existing evidence mainly comes from case studies of single cities (e.g., Shenzhen, Shanghai), lacking systematic causal inference covering multiple cities and large samples, thus limiting the external validity of the conclusions. Cities differ significantly in subway network scale, development stage, urban morphology, etc., making it difficult to generalize findings from a single case to a broader range of regions. Meanwhile, how the spatial structural evolution of subway networks comprehensively affects regional carbon balance through multiple channels such as construction, operation, and industrial transformation still lacks systematic theoretical integration and empirical testing.

This study addresses these gaps by: (1) comparing the effects of subway accessibility versus road network density on carbon balance, directly demonstrating their divergent impacts on green space preservation; (2) identifying and quantifying three distinct mechanisms through which subways affect carbon balance; and (3) employing a multi-layered identification strategy combining instrumental variable and Heckman two-stage selection model to establish causality, thereby providing rigorous empirical evidence on the net contribution of subway networks to regional carbon balance.

Theoretical analysis

Subway networks serve as the backbone infrastructure of modern cities. Their influence on urban carbon balance is profound and multi-layered. This influence goes beyond the simple technical advantage of electric propulsion. It represents a fundamental restructuring of urban spatial organization that systematically intervenes in how regions manage carbon [5]. Road networks typically expand by consuming surface land, fragmenting ecosystems, and inducing low-density sprawl. Subway systems offer a fundamentally different development pathway. By placing transportation arteries underground and concentrating development around stations, subways intervene in three critical domains of the carbon cycle. These domains are the preservation of natural carbon absorption capacity during construction, the transformation of travel behavior during operation, and the reshaping of industrial spatial logic over the long term.

These three domains form a nested and progressively deepening causal chain. The construction phase establishes the ecological foundation by safeguarding existing carbon absorption capability and creating spatial conditions for new ones. The operational phase directly reduces transport emissions by altering behavioral choices [40]. The industrial transformation phase addresses production by restructuring economic geography in ways that depend on the broader institutional, market, and technological context [41–43]. Together, these pathways constitute a systemic approach to carbon balance enhancement. Subway networks are not merely transportation infrastructure. They are spatial organizers that can systematically reconfigure urban carbon dynamics. Based on this overarching logic, the paper proposes its first hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1

The development of subway spatial connectivity networks increases the regional carbon balance ratio.

Construction phase: carbon absorption preservation

The carbon absorption preservation mechanism during the construction period stems from the inherent spatial advantage of the subway system, which is primarily located underground and does not occupy surface land resources. This strategic spatial choice minimizes disturbance to vegetation layers, soil organic carbon pools, and groundwater cycles [44], avoiding the loss of green space and ecological fragmentation typically caused by road expansion [45], thereby effectively protecting the storage capacity and absorption function of existing natural resources. In addition, the enclosed nature of subway operations suppresses the negative ecological corridor effects that often impair surface transportation infrastructure, including noise pollution, exhaust emissions, and human activity [8, 46].

Beyond this preservation function, the Transit-Oriented Development paradigm anchored by subway networks actively shapes urban expansion. Through floor area ratio regulations and mixed land-use policies, this approach directs growth toward compact zones around stations. It curbs low-density sprawl that characterizes road-oriented development. This spatial constraint protects critical carbon absorption systems at the urban periphery, including forests, wetlands, and agricultural land [44]. The intensive development around stations also creates land surpluses. These surpluses can be allocated to artificial carbon absorption systems such as embedded green spaces, ecological corridors, and vertical greening [32, 42, 43]. These distributed absorption nodes form a green infrastructure network when interconnected. This network structurally preserves regional carbon absorption capacity.

Operational phase: active emission reduction

The operational carbon reduction mechanism stems from the subway system’s capacity to alter travel behavior. Traditional transport economics frames travel choice as a trade-off between time and monetary costs. Subway networks introduce a richer set of value dimensions that reshape passenger utility. Compared to private cars or buses, subways offer advantages such as high efficiency, punctuality, and large passenger capacity [47], which reduce uncertainty during commuting and allow residents to allocate saved time to work, leisure, and family life, thereby enhancing their quality of life and sense of well-being [7, 48]. As commuting costs decline and travel reliability improves, an increasing number of people shift from taxis, ride-hailing services, and private cars to subways, replacing previous high-carbon travel modes [40].

Powered by electricity, subways have a clear low-carbon advantage over fuel-powered vehicles. Under the same passenger volume, the energy intensity of subways is far lower than that of private cars and taxis [7]. When a large volume of travel demand shifts from surface transportation to underground rail transit, the overall carbon emissions of the urban transportation sector are reduced, achieving a positive synergy between travel efficiency and carbon reduction. Furthermore, the reshaping of residents’ travel behavior during subway operation not only directly contributes to carbon reduction but also subtly cultivates a low-carbon travel awareness among residents, strengthening their reliance on and identification with public transportation [49], thereby further amplifying the core role of subways in the low-carbon transformation of urban transportation.

Industrial transformation phase: upgrading and efficiency leap

The industrial transformation mechanism reflects the metro network’s capacity to reshape economic geography. However, the carbon benefits of this spatial restructuring are not automatic. They depend on the broader context within which subway networks operate. Specifically, the effectiveness of subway networks in reducing production-side carbon emissions is shaped by three foundational conditions: the institutional environment, the functioning of market mechanisms, and the level of technological capabilities.

For the services sector, improved accessibility reconfigures commercial geography. Essential consumer services form high-density clusters along rail corridors. This enhances convenience and reduces the need for energy-intensive dedicated shopping trips [49]. Knowledge-intensive services concentrate around interchange hubs. These hubs leverage the metro’s talent attraction effect to build specialized human capital pools. This spatial differentiation optimizes service value chains. It achieves broad coverage efficiency for basic services while strengthening innovation synergies for advanced services [50]. Ultimately, this reduces the embodied carbon per unit of service output. For the manufacturing sector, the subway network drives a process of spatial filtering and agglomeration. As transportation conditions improve, land costs in central urban areas rise significantly, prompting high-energy-consumption, low-value-added industries to gradually relocate outward. This spatial reconfiguration, driven by improved accessibility, facilitates the agglomeration of related industries in specific areas, fosters the construction of cooperation networks among firms in the field of environmental governance, and promotes the co-development of green technology research platforms and the shared use of pollution control facilities [5, 43]. When industrial agglomeration reaches a certain scale, it triggers economies of scale in environmental governance, which reduce the average cost of pollution abatement and enable more efficient resource utilization.

These spatial transformations do not occur in isolation. Their carbon benefits are amplified or constrained by three contextual conditions. The first condition is institutional support. Local environmental policies determine the direction and stringency of industrial regulation. Where governments demonstrate strong commitment to low-carbon development through consistent policy signals, the relocation of industry is more likely to result in genuine emission reductions rather than mere displacement of pollution [41]. Moreover, the integration of subway systems with other forms of low-carbon infrastructure, such as bike-sharing networks, extends the reach of rail transit and enhances its attractiveness as a substitute for private vehicle use. Such institutional arrangements create an enabling environment that aligns spatial restructuring with environmental objectives.

The second condition is market mechanisms. Land price differentials between central and peripheral areas serve as the primary economic driver of industrial relocation. Subway development generates capitalization effects that increase land values around stations, widening the gap between city centers and suburbs [42]. This widening gap strengthens the market incentive for manufacturing firms to move to lower-cost peripheral locations [5]. Furthermore, the degree of industrial concentration that emerges from this spatial filtering determines the magnitude of agglomeration economies. When firms cluster together, they can share infrastructure, reduce transaction costs, and benefit from localized knowledge spillovers, all of which contribute to lower carbon intensity.

The third condition is technological capabilities. The presence of green innovation capacity determines whether the knowledge flows enabled by subway networks translate into actual emission reductions. Regions with a strong base of green patents possess a stock of technological solutions that can be diffused through labor mobility and inter-firm interactions [43]. Similarly, the underlying energy efficiency of the regional economy shapes the baseline carbon intensity of production. Where productivity is already high and energy use is efficient, the spatial reorganization facilitated by subways can build on an existing foundation of best practices. Where these capabilities are weak, the potential for carbon improvements may remain unrealized.

These three conditions operate in concert. Institutional support provides the policy framework that guides industrial transition. Market mechanisms supply the price signals that drive efficient spatial allocation. Technological capabilities offer the knowledge and efficiency gains that convert spatial restructuring into measurable carbon reductions. Together, they constitute the enabling environment within which subway networks fulfill their potential to enhance carbon balance through industrial transformation.

The three mechanisms outlined above form a nested and mutually reinforcing system. The construction phase establishes the ecological foundation by safeguarding existing carbon absorption capacity and creating space for new ones. The operational phase directly reduces transport emissions by reshaping travel behavior. The industrial transformation phase addresses the production side by facilitating spatial restructuring whose carbon benefits depend on the presence of supportive institutions, well-functioning markets, and strong technological capabilities. These pathways are not isolated. The compact urban form supported by subway networks reduces both transport distances and the ecological footprint of urban expansion. The agglomeration economies that emerge from spatial restructuring enhance the viability of green infrastructure investments. The flow of workers enabled by the network diffuses the green technologies that emerge from innovation hubs. Together, these interactions create a system where the carbon balance effect of subway development is greater than the sum of its parts.

Based on this integrated theoretical framework, which distinguishes the aggregate effect from the three distinct mechanisms through which it operates, the paper proposes its second hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2

Subway networks enhance regional carbon balance through three mechanisms. First, they preserve and enhance carbon absorption capacity during the construction phase. Second, they reduce transport emissions by shifting travel behavior during the operational phase. Third, they promote industrial spatial restructuring, the carbon benefits of which are shaped by institutional, market, and technological conditions.

Research design

Data

This study covers the period from 2011 to 2023. Subway station information, including station names, planned routes, opening dates, and district or county locations, was compiled from publicly available sources such as the China Urban Rail Transit System Encyclopedia, the China Association of Metros, and the Rail Transit Network website. Based on this information, subway connectivity network data were constructed through a combination of web crawling and manual collation.

Carbon-related data are drawn from multiple sources. The baseline carbon balance ratio uses carbon emissions estimates from the EDGAR database, which provides high-resolution emissions data covering energy consumption and multiple sectors, including industry, transportation, and construction. Carbon absorption calculations rely on land use data sourced from the China Land Cover Dataset (CLCD), the first annual land cover product for China constructed using the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform with Landsat imagery. Net Primary Productivity (NPP) data used in robustness checks are obtained from MODIS annual NPP datasets, with quality assurance filters and GLC2000 land cover masks applied following established methods.

Socioeconomic data at the county, district, and city levels, including regional GDP, population, and the number of industrial enterprises above designated size, were sourced from the China Statistical Yearbook, the China City Statistical Yearbook, and the China Regional Statistical Yearbook. Road network data were obtained from OpenStreetMap (OSM) and cross-verified with Gaode Map road network data using ArcGIS extraction and processing. Vector geographic information on river systems and administrative boundaries was obtained from the National Geomatics Center of China, while slope data originated from the China Geospatial Conditions Monitoring Cloud Platform. The housing price data is sourced from the Juhui Database.

Provincial-level transportation indicators, including rail transit passenger volume, private passenger vehicle ownership, and taxi passenger volume, were derived from the China Transport Statistical Yearbook and the China Automotive Industry Yearbook. Bike-sharing entry dummies were manually collected from local government policy documents and news reports. Nighttime lighting data for Chinese administrative regions were acquired from the National Geophysical Data Center website of the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration.

Policy-related variables were constructed from legal and policy documents. Environmental regulation intensity was measured by the frequency of low-carbon related terms in municipal government work reports. Low-carbon city pilot status was manually coded from legal documents. Both are from the Peking University Law Database. Green patent data were obtained from the National Intellectual Property Administration and filtered using the WIPO IPC Green Inventory to identify environment-related patents.

Subway network development and node centrality metrics

To rigorously capture the spatial configuration and nodal importance of urban rail transit systems, this study adopts a complex network analysis approach. This methodology allows for a nuanced quantification of a station’s functional role within the broader network, moving beyond simple binary indicators of metro access. The analytical framework is structured as follows. We define subscripts c for city, m for county or district, t for year, and i for an individual metro station that is operational in a given year. For each unique “city-year” observation, we construct an unweighted, undirected graph representing the subway network. This involves transforming official subway route maps into binary station adjacency matrices, where a value of 1 denotes a direct connection between two adjacent stations on the same line (i.e., immediate predecessors or successors), and 0 otherwise. While these station-level metrics offer valuable insights into nodal characteristics, the empirical objective of this study is to assess the impact of the collective network accessibility at the county/district level on regional carbon balance. To achieve this, we construct a primary accessibility index by aggregating the closeness centrality of all operational stations within a given county or district. Closeness centrality is chosen as the core metric for aggregation because it most directly embodies the concept of accessibility that is central to our theoretical framework—the ease with which a station (and thus the area it serves) can reach the entire urban system. To ensure the robustness of our findings and to explore other dimensions of network influence, we also compute three alternative county/district-level metrics for use in sensitivity analyses: (1) the average closeness centrality, which normalizes for the number of stations; (2) the aggregated degree centrality; and (3) the aggregated betweenness centrality.

A critical methodological decision is that all metrics are constructed based on the actual operational status of each metro station in each specific year. This dynamic dataset comprises 394 “city-year” observations, tracking the progressive opening of stations over time. This approach offers two distinct advantages over studies that rely on static planning documents or long-term infrastructure blueprints. First, it provides an accurate representation of the network’s evolving operational reality. By using actual opening dates, the analysis can more cleanly isolate the causal impact of the metro system’s presence and its expanding connectivity from the influence of concurrent government planning priorities or industrial policies that might be correlated with planned, but not yet realized, infrastructure.

Second, leveraging the precise timing of station inaugurations introduces significant and credible temporal variation. The phased commissioning of stations within a single line (e.g., Nanning Metro Line 2: Jinxiang Station to Xijin Station opened on December 28, 2017, followed by Tanzze Station to Yudong Station on November 23, 2020; Wuhan Metro Line 8: Jintan Road Station to Liyuan Station opened on December 26, 2017, Yezhihu Station to Junyuncun Station on November 6, 2019, and Hubei Daily Station to Mahu Station on January 2, 2021), generates year-to-year changes in network topology that are exogenous to short-term fluctuations in local socioeconomic conditions. This granular, time-varying data on network evolution provides a much stronger foundation for causal inference than a simple binary indicator of whether a line has been planned, thereby ensuring a more accurate and reasonable empirical analysis.

Causal inference

Endogenous problem and instrumental variable estimation

While the baseline results in Table 2 establish a robust positive association between subway network accessibility and carbon balance ratios, this relationship may be subject to endogeneity concerns. Two primary sources of bias could affect the consistency of estimates. First, reverse causality may arise if regions with stronger environmental awareness or greater fiscal capacity simultaneously invest in subway infrastructure and implement other carbon reduction policies. Second, omitted variable bias could occur if unobserved factors, such as local planning capacity or institutional quality, influence both metro development and carbon management outcomes.

To address these concerns, this paper employs an instrumental variable (IV) approach. The selected instruments are interaction terms between two time-invariant geographic variables: the inverse of urban slope variability (Slope−1c), the inverse of river density (River−1c) and a one-period lagged dummy variable indicating pre-existing population agglomeration (Dum_scalemt-1). A county/district is defined as densely populated if its population exceeds one million. River density is computed from vector geographic data as total river length normalized by administrative area.

The validity of these instruments rests on two conditions, namely relevance and exclusion. Regarding relevance, slope variability and river density directly reflect the complexity of urban terrain. Steep slopes and densely distributed rivers significantly increase the technical challenges and construction costs of subway projects, including tunnel excavation and bridge construction, thereby constraining the expansion of subway networks [65]. After applying reciprocal transformation, both variables exhibit a positive correlation with subway network accessibility. The lagged population dummy captures the demand-side logic of metro planning. Governments prioritize subway deployment in areas where population concentration has already formed. By interacting geographic constraints with population agglomeration, the instruments simultaneously reflect supply-side feasibility and demand-driven planning, aligning closely with the actual decision-making process of subway network development.

Regarding the exclusion restriction, a potential concern is that natural geographic attributes such as slope and river density could influence carbon balance through channels other than subway networks. This paper addresses this concern through a multi-layered identification strategy. First, the geographic variables used in the instruments are time-invariant. By including county fixed effects in all regressions, any direct effect of these time-invariant characteristics on carbon balance is absorbed, ensuring that the identifying variation stems solely from the interaction between geographic attributes and a time-varying population agglomeration dummy. Second, geographic variables are transformed into proxies for “construction difficulty,” emphasizing their unidirectional technical constraints on subway engineering rather than reflecting regional economic endowments. This transformation ensures that these variables capture only the engineering feasibility of subway construction, independent of other environmental influences. Third, the instrument interacts geographic attributes with a one-period lagged dummy for densely populated areas. This design restricts the instrument’s effect to regions with established population concentration, excluding areas where terrain might independently shape economic development without concurrent metro planning, while also mitigating reverse causality from contemporaneous economic activities. Fourth, the baseline regressions include a rich set of control variables that directly block alternative pathways. Specifically, we control for county-level economic indicators (e.g., economic output, population size) that might be influenced by the instruments or act as intermediate channels, as well as variables capturing agricultural activity and land use. These controls absorb any residual influence of topography on carbon balance through agricultural practices or cropland distribution. Collectively, these measures ensure that the exclusion restriction is satisfied.

Selection bias problem and Heckman two-stage estimation

Another potential threat to identification arises from sample selection bias. Metro stations are not randomly distributed across counties and districts. Areas with zero operational stations may differ systematically from those with metro access due to lower population density, geographical constraints, or insufficient infrastructure investment. If these unobserved characteristics also correlate with carbon balance, restricting the analysis to districts with metro stations could yield inconsistent estimates.

To address this concern, this paper employs the Heckman two-stage selection correction model. The first stage uses a Probit model to estimate the probability that a county/district has at least one operational metro station in a given year. The dependent variable Dum_Submt is a binary indicator equal to 1 if the district has a metro station and 0 otherwise. The independent variables include the same instrument sets used in the IV approach along with the full set of control variables and fixed effects. From this first-stage regression, we compute the Inverse Mills Ratio (IMRmt), which captures the selection hazard. The second stage then re-estimates the baseline model with IMRmt included as an additional regressor.

Column (3) of Table 3 presents the first-stage Probit results. Both instrument sets are positive and statistically significant, further supporting their relevance for explaining metro station presence. Column (4) reports the second-stage results. The coefficient on Sub_netmt remains positive and statistically significant at the 1% level. This estimate is consistent with the baseline OLS results in Table 2, column (3). The coefficient on IMRmt is negative and statistically significant at the 1% level. The negative sign suggests that unobserved factors making metro station placement more likely are associated with lower carbon balance ratios. Areas selected for metro development tend to be more urbanized and industrialized, which inherently exert downward pressure on carbon balance. However, despite the significant selection correction, the core coefficient on subway network accessibility remains robust in both sign and magnitude.

Taken together, the IV and Heckman results provide strong evidence that the positive effect of subway network development on carbon balance is causal. The findings survive rigorous corrections for endogeneity and sample selection, reinforcing the validity of the theoretical framework and the policy relevance of the estimated effects.

Modifying the assessment framework for subway networks

To comprehensively measure subway network accessibility, this paper replaces the core explanatory variable with four alternative centrality metrics, each capturing a distinct dimension of network connectivity.

Column (4) employs average closeness centrality, calculated as the sum of station level closeness centrality divided by the number of stations in the district. This measure normalizes for the number of stations, capturing the average accessibility level per station. This specification directly addresses the potential concern regarding network size, isolating the quality of connectivity from the quantity of infrastructure. The coefficient remains positive and statistically significant, confirming that the baseline effect is not driven solely by districts with large numbers of stations but rather reflects the underlying connectivity quality.

Column (5) replaces the core explanatory variable with aggregated degree centrality. Degree centrality measures the number of direct connections each station maintains, capturing the local connectivity and transfer potential of each node. Aggregated degree centrality thus reflects the overall transfer density of the district, emphasizing the frequency of line intersections and the presence of interchange hubs. This metric is particularly relevant for capturing the network effects that arise from multi-line connectivity, where transfer stations generate disproportionately large accessibility benefits through enabling seamless connections between lines. Column (6) employs aggregated betweenness centrality. Betweenness centrality measures the extent to which a station lies on the shortest paths between other station pairs, capturing its role as a network bridge or intermediary. Aggregated betweenness centrality thus reflects the importance of stations in facilitating cross-network travel and reducing system-wide travel distances. Stations with high betweenness centrality are critical for network connectivity, as their presence or absence can substantially affect overall network efficiency. This metric captures the strategic importance of stations in connecting different parts of the network. Both alternative specifications yield positive and statistically significant coefficients, confirming that the positive effect of subway networks on carbon balance is robust to different conceptualizations of network centrality.

Exclusion of potentially confounding samples

Two potential sample composition issues could bias the estimates. First, regions without any operational metro stations throughout the entire sample period may differ systematically from metro-connected regions in ways that are not fully captured by control variables. These regions, typically smaller cities or rural counties, may face distinct economic and environmental conditions that could affect the carbon balance through channels unrelated to subway development. Second, China comprises four province-level municipalities—Beijing, Shanghai, Tianjin, and Chongqing—which stand apart from other cities due to their larger built-up areas, higher populations, and significant roles in politics, economy, science, culture, and transportation. Given their unique characteristics and disproportionately large number of metro stations, their inclusion in the analysis may introduce biases to the results.

To address potential estimation bias caused by the persistent absence of metro stations in some areas, columns (8) systematically excludes cities without operational metro systems throughout the sample period. This restriction ensures that the sample consists only of regions where subway development was technically and institutionally feasible, allowing for a more meaningful comparison. The coefficient signs and significance levels remain consistent with the baseline estimates, demonstrating empirical robustness and indicating that the results are not driven by the inclusion of regions that never developed subways.

To address concerns regarding the influence of the four province-level municipalities, column (9) excludes samples from these municipalities. Given the disproportionately large number of metro stations and the unique administrative status of these cities, their inclusion could potentially drive the results. After excluding these observations, the coefficient on Sub_netmt remains positive and statistically significant, with a magnitude comparable to the baseline estimate. This indicates that the exclusion of these municipalities does not materially affect the main findings, confirming that the results are not driven by these major metropolitan outliers.

Taken together, the robustness checks presented in Table 4 provide strong evidence that the positive relationship between subway network accessibility and carbon balance is not an artifact of specific measurement choices, sample composition, or the treatment of network topology. The consistency of the results across nine alternative specifications reinforces the empirical validity of the baseline findings and supports the theoretical framework that subway networks enhance regional carbon balance.

Heterogeneous impact

Heterogeneity of regional economy

The theoretical framework above posits that subway networks enhance carbon balance through construction-phase preservation, operational-phase modal shifts, and industrial transformation. The magnitude of these effects, however, may vary systematically with the level of regional economic development. From a theoretical perspective, the marginal benefit of green infrastructure investment is expected to be larger in regions where existing carbon intensity is high but the potential for transition remains untapped. Conversely, economically developed regions may face structural constraints that limit the additional contribution of any single intervention.

To examine this heterogeneity, the sample is divided into high and low economic development groups based on the median per capita GDP of counties and districts. Columns (1) and (2) of Table 5 present the regression results for the two subsamples. In regions with lower economic development, the coefficient on Sub_netmt is 0.030 and statistically significant at the 1% level, indicating that subway network accessibility substantially improves carbon balance in these areas. In contrast, for economically developed regions, the coefficient is not statistically significant.

This pattern can be explained by the distinct structural characteristics of developed versus developing regions. Economically developed areas typically exhibit high levels of urbanization that have already significantly reduced natural carbon absorption spaces such as green spaces and wetlands, limiting the potential for further preservation effects during the construction phase. Moreover, these regions often possess mature but carbon-intensive industrial structures that exhibit strong path dependence, with production processes and energy systems locked into fossil fuel-based configurations. The presence of entrenched infrastructure and established commuting patterns further constrains the marginal impact of new transit investments. In less developed regions, by contrast, urban form is still evolving, providing greater scope for subway networks to shape spatial development patterns, preserve ecological spaces, and induce modal shifts before high carbon lock-in occurs.

Notably, this finding also helps address potential concerns about selection bias. One might expect that subway networks are preferentially developed in economically advanced regions, and if such selection were driving the results, the estimated effect should be larger in those areas. The observation that the carbon balancing effect is actually stronger and more significant in less developed regions suggests that the results are not driven by the endogenous placement of subway infrastructure in wealthier areas. Instead, the findings indicate that the carbon reduction potential of subway networks is more closely tied to the transitional capacity and untapped environmental gains available in developing regions, rather than to pre-existing economic advantages. This pattern reinforces the interpretation that the estimated effects reflect genuine carbon balancing mechanisms rather than spurious correlations arising from the non-random distribution of metro systems.

Heterogeneity of spatial compactness

Spatial compactness, which characterizes the degree of agglomeration of economic activities within a city, represents a fundamental determinant of transportation system efficiency and carbon outcomes. From a theoretical perspective, compact urban forms generate economies of scale in infrastructure provision, reduce average travel distances, and create conditions conducive to public transit ridership. The synergy between compact spatial structure and rail transit systems is particularly pronounced, as dense development patterns align naturally with the high capacity, fixed route nature of subway networks.

To verify the heterogeneity of spatial compactness, this paper constructs a Normalized Spatial Spin Index based on the methodological frameworks of Angel [67] and Harari [68]. This index quantifies spatial compactness using nighttime light data. The specific construction method is as follows: Cities are partitioned into 25 grid-cell polygons. Points are randomly distributed within the lit area of each polygon, and the nearest neighbor distance d*n is calculated. Equation (11) is applied to compute the spin index representing economic agglomeration intensity. The weighted average of the top ten economic activity polygons characterizes the city’s spatial configuration. Equal Area Circle (EAC) standardization eliminates area bias, yielding a comparable compactness measure.

Based on the median NSpin value, the sample is divided into high and low spatial compactness subgroups for heterogeneity testing. Columns (3) and (4) of Table 5 present the results. In high compactness cities, the coefficient on Sub_netmt is 0.039 and statistically significant at the 1% level. In low compactness cities, the coefficient is not statistically significant. This disparity confirms that the carbon balancing effect of subway networks is substantially amplified in spatially compact cities.

The underlying mechanism operates through two reinforcing channels. First, geographic concentration of economic activities in compact cities reduces energy transmission losses and avoids redundant infrastructure construction, creating systematic efficiencies in energy utilization and pollution control. Second, compact spatial morphology aligns inherently with the topological structure of metro systems, enabling more efficient station coverage with shorter feeder distances. This spatial transportation synergy increases public transit modal share and maximizes the carbon balance capacity of subway networks. In sprawling cities with dispersed development patterns, the catchment areas of metro stations are limited, and the mismatch between transit supply and travel demand constrains the effectiveness of subway investments.

Heterogeneity of electricity consumption

The third dimension of heterogeneity examines how residential energy consumption patterns moderate the carbon balancing effect of subway networks. This analysis is grounded in behavioral economics and the theory of green preferences, which suggests that individuals with lower baseline energy consumption tend to exhibit higher environmental awareness and greater responsiveness to green infrastructure investments. From a theoretical perspective, energy consumption behavior reflects underlying preferences, habits, and attitudes toward resource conservation that shape the elasticity of travel mode substitution in response to transit improvements.

To test this heterogeneity, the sample is divided into high and low energy consumption groups based on the median annual per capita residential electricity consumption at the county/district level. Electricity consumption serves as a proxy for household energy intensity and, by extension, for the prevalence of energy-conscious behaviors and environmental preferences. Columns (5) and (6) of Table 5 present the results. In low electricity consumption regions, the coefficient on Sub_netmt is 0.031 and statistically significant at the 1% level. In high electricity consumption regions, the coefficient is not statistically significant.

This pattern can be explained by the concept of green preferences in behavioral economics. Residents in low electricity consumption areas are likely to possess stronger environmental awareness and a greater predisposition toward energy-conserving behaviors. These green preferences translate into higher acceptance of low carbon transportation alternatives, making the modal shift from private vehicles to public transit more elastic with respect to improvements in subway accessibility. When a new transit option becomes available, individuals with pre-existing environmental preferences are more likely to adopt it, generating larger reductions in transportation emissions per unit of infrastructure investment.

Furthermore, low energy consumption regions exhibit more malleable energy demand structures that are easier to transition toward low carbon alternatives. The formation of environmentally friendly daily habits is reinforced through behavioral inertia and social demonstration effects, creating a positive feedback loop that sustains green lifestyles. Once established, these preferences create favorable conditions for subway systems to achieve their full carbon reduction potential. In high electricity consumption regions, by contrast, entrenched energy-intensive lifestyles and weaker environmental preferences dampen the responsiveness to transit infrastructure improvements, limiting the marginal carbon benefit of subway network expansion.

Mechanism analysis

Construction phase: underground space advantage and green resource preservation effect

The theoretical foundation for the construction phase mechanism rests on a fundamental distinction between underground and surface transportation infrastructure. Metro systems are predominantly built beneath the ground, which minimizes direct occupation of surface land and reduces disturbance to existing vegetation, soil carbon pools, and urban green spaces. In contrast, road networks require extensive surface land acquisition, leading to fragmentation of natural habitats and permanent loss of green cover. This underground advantage suggests that subway development can preserve carbon absorption capacity that would otherwise be diminished by equivalent surface transportation expansion. The net effect on carbon balance therefore depends on whether the emission reductions generated by metro operations outweigh any localized construction disturbances.

To empirically examine this mechanism, this paper adopts a life cycle perspective that compares subway networks with traditional road infrastructure. Road network density is calculated as the ratio of total road length to area within each grid, drawing on the method of Roche [69]. This allows for a direct comparison of how subway accessibility versus road density affect carbon outcomes. Table 6 presents the results across five specifications.

Operational phase: eco-friendly mobility and transportation behavior shift

The operational phase mechanism is grounded in travel behavior theory and the economics of modal choice. Subway systems alter the relative attractiveness of transportation modes by offering a combination of speed, reliability, and low monetary cost that differs fundamentally from surface alternatives. According to random utility frameworks, individuals choose among travel options based on perceived costs and benefits. When subway accessibility improves, the utility of rail transit increases relative to private vehicles and taxis. This shift in relative attractiveness generates substitution effects, where travelers switch from high carbon modes to low carbon rail transit. The theoretical prediction is that improved subway accessibility should increase rail ridership while simultaneously reducing the use of taxis and private cars, thereby lowering transportation-related emissions.

To empirically examine this impact, this paper employs three core explanatory variables: rail transit passenger volume (Traff_subwaymt, unit: million passenger trips), taxi passenger volume (Traff_taximt, unit: million passenger trips), and private passenger vehicle ownership (Traff_carmt, unit: million vehicles). These variables were calculated by multiplying provincial annual totals by the corresponding county/district GDP weights and were incorporated into the baseline regression equations for analysis.

Industrial transformation phase: industrial agglomeration transformation and energy efficiency improvement

The industrial transformation mechanism operates through the spatial restructuring of economic activities and the diffusion of green technologies. Subway networks reduce commuting costs and enhance connectivity, which alters the location choices of firms and workers. This spatial reorganization can generate structural changes in the economy that affect carbon outcomes [70]. Theoretically, improved accessibility should facilitate the concentration of service industries around transit hubs while encouraging manufacturing to relocate to peripheral areas where land costs are lower. This spatial differentiation can reduce overall carbon intensity by shortening travel distances and enabling cleaner production patterns. However, whether these structural changes translate into actual carbon reductions depends on how subway networks interact with local institutions, market conditions, and technological capabilities. To capture these complex interactions, this paper adopts a moderation framework. Instead of directly testing the mediating effects of industrial structure, we examine how the relationship between subway accessibility and carbon balance is moderated by six key factors that represent institutional support, market mechanisms, industrial organization, and technological capacity. Each of these factors is expected to strengthen the carbon reduction effect of subway networks, creating a reinforcing cycle where subways enable the conditions that in turn amplify their own impact.

Moderating effects of institutional support and complementary infrastructure

The first set of moderators captures how external support systems shape the effectiveness of subway networks. These include local government commitment to environmental goals and the availability of complementary transportation infrastructure.

Column (1) examines the moderating role of local government environmental regulation intensity, measured by the frequency of low carbon related terms in municipal government work reports. Following established methods, we extract keywords such as “carbon reduction” and “low carbon” from annual government reports and calculate their total count per thousand words. This measure captures the degree of local policy commitment to green development. The interaction term between this regulation intensity and subway accessibility is positive and statistically significant at the 10% level. This result reveals a bidirectional reinforcing mechanism. On the one hand, subway networks provide a physical infrastructure through which environmental regulations can be effectively implemented, as improved connectivity reduces the cost for firms to comply with stricter environmental standards. On the other hand, strong regulatory commitment creates institutional conditions that guide subway development toward carbon reduction outcomes, ensuring that the spatial reorganization enabled by rail transit aligns with environmental objectives.

Column (2) examines the moderating role of bike sharing availability, measured by a dummy variable indicating whether Dockless bike sharing services have entered the city. Bike sharing serves as a last mile solution that extends the catchment area of subway stations and increases the accessibility benefits of rail transit. The interaction term between bike sharing and subway accessibility is positive and statistically significant at the 1% level. This finding suggests a mutually reinforcing relationship. Subway networks generate concentrated passenger flows that make bike sharing services economically viable, while bike sharing expands the effective service area of subway stations, allowing more residents to use rail transit for their daily commutes. The combination of these two modes creates a seamless multimodal system that enhances the substitution effect against private vehicle use.

Moderating effects of market mechanisms and industrial organization

The second set of moderators captures how market forces and industrial structure shape the carbon impact of subway networks. Column (3) examines the moderating role of land price differentials, measured by the Gini coefficient of land transaction prices across districts within a city. This indicator captures the magnitude of rent gradients between city centers and peripheral areas. Land price data are obtained from land market transaction records, including negotiated and listed land sales, with unit prices measured in yuan per hectare. The interaction term between the land price Gini coefficient and subway accessibility is positive and statistically significant at the 1% level. The theoretical logic here operates through two channels. Subway development generates capitalization effects that increase land values around stations, widening the price gap between central and peripheral areas. This widening gap creates stronger market incentives for manufacturing firms to relocate to lower-cost peripheral areas. At the same time, the existence of a steep land price gradient provides clear price signals that guide the spatial reorganization enabled by subway networks, ensuring that improved accessibility translates into efficient relocation patterns.

Column (4) examines the moderating role of industrial geographic concentration, measured by the Aiginger and Davies [71] concentration index for secondary industry. This index is calculated as the sum over districts of the share of secondary industry output multiplied by the logarithm of that share, with higher values indicating greater concentration. The interaction term between industrial concentration and subway accessibility is positive and statistically significant at the 10% level. This finding reflects an agglomeration mechanism. Subway networks lower transportation costs for workers and inputs, facilitating the concentration of manufacturing in specialized industrial parks on the urban periphery. When industrial activities cluster together, firms can share pollution control facilities, adopt cleaner production technologies more efficiently, and benefit from knowledge spillovers about energy-saving practices. These agglomeration economies transform the spatial reorganization enabled by subways into measurable carbon reductions.

Moderating effects of technological innovation and energy efficiency

The third set of moderators captures how technological capabilities determine the carbon outcomes of subway-induced economic activity. Column (5) examines the moderating role of green technological innovation, measured by the logarithm of green patent application quantity. Green patents are identified using the WIPO IPC Green Inventory classification, which covers areas such as alternative energy, energy conservation, and waste management. The interaction term between green patent applications and subway accessibility is positive and statistically significant at the 1% level. This result suggests a complementary relationship. Subway networks facilitate the flow of people and ideas, accelerating the diffusion of green technologies across firms and industries. When a region has a strong base of green innovation, the knowledge spillovers enabled by subway connectivity are more likely to translate into actual improvements in production processes and emission reductions. At the same time, the presence of advanced green technologies provides the content that makes the knowledge diffusion function of subway networks economically meaningful.

Column (6) examines the moderating role of green total factor productivity, measured using the SBM GML model [72, 73]. This method accounts for both desirable outputs such as gross regional product and undesirable outputs, including industrial wastewater, waste gas, and solid waste. Factor inputs include capital, labor, and energy consumption. The interaction term between green productivity and subway accessibility is positive and statistically significant at the 10% level. This finding captures an efficiency spillover mechanism. Subway networks enable workers and managers to move more easily between firms, facilitating the transmission of best practices in energy management and production organization. In regions with higher baseline energy efficiency, these interactions are more likely to result in productivity improvements that reduce carbon intensity. Conversely, the availability of efficient firms within a region provides the reference points that make the knowledge diffusion function of subways effective.

Taken together, the results in Table 8 demonstrate that the carbon balancing effect of subway networks is not uniform across contexts but is systematically strengthened by supportive institutions, complementary infrastructure, clear market signals, industrial agglomeration, and strong technological capabilities. These findings confirm that subway networks function not only as transportation infrastructure but also as institutional enablers, market amplifiers, and technology diffusers. The moderation patterns reveal bidirectional reinforcement: subway networks enable the conditions that enhance their own effectiveness, while those conditions in turn guide subway development toward carbon reduction outcomes. This mutual reinforcement suggests that realizing the full carbon benefits of subway investment requires coordinated policy efforts across transportation planning, environmental regulation, land management, industrial policy, and innovation support.

Conclusions

As urbanization accelerates, subways, with their advantages of large passenger capacity and high energy efficiency, have become the core backbone of urban low-carbon transportation systems. Therefore, scientifically evaluating the net effect of subway networks on carbon balance and identifying its heterogeneous impacts under different network structures is of great theoretical and practical significance for optimizing urban transportation strategies and achieving the goals of carbon peaking and carbon neutrality. This study investigates the causal relationship between subway network development and regional carbon balance using data from Chinese counties and districts between 2011 and 2023. The baseline results show that a one-unit increase in county-level subway network accessibility raises the carbon balance ratio by 2.1% points. The effect is strongest in economically underdeveloped regions, areas with compact spatial structures, and zones with lower residential electricity consumption. These heterogeneous patterns suggest that the carbon benefits of subway development are most pronounced where transitional potential is high and carbon lock-in is relatively low.

The mechanism analysis identifies three distinct phases through which subway networks influence carbon balance, with the operational phase emerging as the most important contributor. During the construction phase, subway networks preserve green space and carbon absorption capacity by avoiding the surface disruption caused by road networks. This preservation effect is significant but primarily prevents losses rather than actively creating new ones. During the operational phase, improved accessibility attracts riders to rail transit and reduces both taxi usage and private vehicle ownership. The magnitude of emission reductions from this modal shift is substantial and constitutes the largest single contribution to carbon balance improvement. During the industrial transformation phase, the carbon benefits of spatial restructuring are amplified by supportive institutions, well-functioning land markets, industrial agglomeration, and strong technological capabilities. This phase represents a longer-term structural channel whose effects depend on enabling conditions.

This study makes several contributions to the literature. First, by adopting the carbon balance ratio as the outcome variable, it moves beyond studies that examine emission or absorption in isolation, offering a more integrated assessment of infrastructure effects. Second, it introduces a network-based measure of subway accessibility that captures the topological structure of urban rail systems, overcoming the limitations of binary station indicators. Third, it develops a unified empirical framework that tests three temporally nested mechanisms, demonstrating that the carbon benefits of subways arise from a combination of preservation, behavioral change, and structural transformation.

Based on these findings, we propose four policy recommendations with explicit attention to implementation.

First, integrate carbon balance objectives into subway planning with clear institutional responsibility. Municipal transportation commissions should take the lead in embedding carbon balance targets into rail network master plans. This requires establishing interdepartmental coordination mechanisms that bring together transportation authorities, natural resource bureaus, and ecological environment agencies. For new subway projects, environmental impact assessments should explicitly quantify carbon balance outcomes, including both construction-phase green space preservation and operational-phase emission reductions. Ecological offset requirements should be enforced, ensuring that any unavoidable vegetation loss during construction is compensated through restoration or enhancement measures within the same corridor.

Second, strengthen the complementary infrastructure and institutional conditions that amplify subway carbon benefits. Municipal governments should prioritize the development of bike-sharing networks and pedestrian facilities that extend the catchment area of subway stations, particularly in peripheral areas where last-mile connectivity is weakest. Environmental regulation should be aligned with transit investment, using the improved accessibility provided by subways to phase out high-emission industries and to enforce stricter emission standards. Land management authorities should use the land value increments generated by subway development to fund ecological restoration in station-adjacent areas, creating a self-reinforcing cycle where infrastructure investment finances its own environmental benefits.

Third, use market mechanisms to guide industrial relocation and agglomeration. Local governments should leverage the land price gradients intensified by subway development to facilitate the voluntary relocation of energy-intensive manufacturing to specialized industrial parks on the urban periphery. These parks should be designed to enable shared pollution control facilities and energy cascading, converting the agglomeration economies enabled by subways into measurable carbon reductions. Zoning regulations should reserve land around transfer hubs for high-value service sectors, maximizing the carbon productivity gains from service sector agglomeration.

Fourth, develop green innovation diffusion channels that leverage the connectivity provided by subway networks. Science and technology bureaus should establish innovation networks that connect universities, research institutes, and firms located along rail corridors, using the mobility enabled by subways to accelerate knowledge spillovers. Green technology demonstration zones should be located near subway stations to maximize visibility and accessibility. Tax incentives for green commuting should be offered to firms located within station catchment areas, allowing them to reduce employee commuting emissions while lowering their own environmental compliance costs.

The findings of this study have implications beyond China. For rapidly urbanizing regions worldwide, the results suggest that subway networks can contribute to carbon balance not only through direct emission reductions but also through the preservation of green space and the spatial restructuring of economic activities. However, realizing these benefits requires coordinated policy efforts that integrate transportation planning with environmental regulation, land management, and innovation policy. Future research should extend the analysis to longer time horizons, incorporate climate variability into carbon absorption estimates, and examine how the interactions between subway development and urban form evolve over the full infrastructure life cycle.
