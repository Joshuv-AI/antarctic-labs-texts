Assessment of the modified glasgow prognostic scale and inflammatory parameters as a new prognostic indicator in pulmonary thromboembolism patients
Authors: Şehmus Işık, Tarık Kılıç, Ayşe Füsun Topçu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and objectiveAcute Pulmonary Embolism (PE) is a common and life-threatening condition, necessitating early prognostic evaluation. While various prognostic scoring systems exist, most lack parameters reflecting the inflammatory aspect of PE. This study aimed to assess the prognostic significance of inflammatory markers, particularly the modified Glasgow Prognostic Scale (mGPS), and their relationship with established prognostic tools like the Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index (PESI).Materials and methodsWe retrospectively analyzed 436 patients diagnosed with acute PE and hospitalized in the Department of Chest Diseases at Dicle University Medical Faculty between January 2020 and January 2024. Patients with incomplete records were excluded, leaving 334 for final analysis. mGPS scores were calculated and categorized as 0, 1, or 2. We examined associations between mGPS scores, PESI, 30-day and 90-day mortality, and other inflammatory parameters.ResultsThe mean age of the cohort was 61.52 ± 17.12 years. Patients with an mGPS score of 2 had significantly higher PESI scores (p < 0.001) and increased 30-day and 90-day mortality rates (p < 0.001). A strong, positive correlation was observed between mGPS and PESI (r = 0.536, p < 0.001). ROC analysis identified albumin (AUC: 0.878) and oxygen saturation (AUC: 0.854) as the best-performing predictors for PESI classification. A predictive model combining mGPS, oxygen saturation, and systolic blood pressure outperformed sPESI in mortality prediction (AUC: 0.748, cut-off: 2.5).ConclusionThe mGPS score is a significant prognostic marker for 30- and 90-day mortality in PE patients and outperforms sPESI in predicting PESI scores. Our simplified model incorporating mGPS, oxygen saturation, and systolic blood pressure provides a practical and effective alternative to existing tools for prognostic evaluation in acute PE.Keywords: Pulmonary embolism, Mortality, Inflammation, Acute phase proteins

Introduction and objective

In recent years, there has been a significant increase in the average life expectancy of human beings with a relative decrease in infectious diseases. Concurrent with this phenomenon, there has been a notable rise in the prevalence of chronic conditions, most notably cardiovascular diseases.

Thrombosis constitutes the underlying pathology of three major cardiovascular diseases: ischemic heart disease, stroke, and venous thromboembolism [1]. Venous thromboembolism (VTE), clinically manifesting as deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or pulmonary embolism (PE), is the third most common acute cardiovascular syndrome worldwide after myocardial infarction and stroke. VTE is associated with approximately 10 million cases per year and their burden of mortality and morbidity [2].

Acute PE usually occurs when thrombus fragments from the deep veins of the lower extremities occlude the pulmonary artery and its branches. However, both the respiratory and cardiovascular systems are affected. Pulmonary circulation, and thus perfusion, is primarily impaired in pulmonary embolism.

Physiopathological changes in PE include shortness of breath, pleuritic chest pain, and painful swelling of the lower limbs [3]. In studies, acute dyspnea is the most common symptom, but chest pain, syncope (5–17%), hemoptysis (7.6–9.6%), and orthopnea (1%) are less common [3–6].

In addition, hemodynamic instability due to an acute increase in pulmonary artery pressure is the major cause of death in acute pulmonary embolism [7].

This hemodynamic instability that occurs in acute PE is accompanied by respiratory disturbances due to ventilation/perfusion mismatch. In addition to mortality due to sudden hemodynamic instability in patients with acute PE, early mortality in the first 30 days can be high (10%) [8]. This has led to the need to identify high-risk patients at an early stage and to follow them more closely. The main risk stratification system used for this is the Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index (PESI), which combines factors such as age, vital signs and comorbidities, and its relatively simplified version, the sPESI [9, 10].

The Glasgow Prognostic Scale (GPS) is a scoring system for the detection of systemic inflammation, especially in patients with malignancy and trauma [11–13]. The mGPS is widely utilized in clinical practice owing to its practical nature and its reliance on commonly assessed laboratory parameters.

Risk scoring systems are important for predicting early mortality in acute PE within 30 days. However, current scoring systems need to be simplified. In this study, we aimed to investigate the correlation of mGPS with PESI score in terms of prognosis by retrospective analysis of patients diagnosed with acute PE.

Methods

The aim of this study was to compare the mGPS with the PESI score to investigate 30-day and 90-day mortality in patients who were hospitalized in Dicle University Faculty of Medicine, Department of Chest Diseases and Respiratory Intensive Care Unit between 01.01.2020–01.01.2024 and whose acute PE diagnosis was confirmed by BTPA. The study encompassed a comprehensive evaluation of descriptive data, including age, gender, demographic characteristics, comorbid chronic diseases (e.g., diabetes mellitus, hypertension, malignancy, etc.), smoking habits, and risk factors.

A comprehensive evaluation was conducted of the vital signs documented at the time of diagnosis, as well as the diagnostic laboratory tests performed.

According to the 2019 Acute Pulmonary Embolism guidelines established by the European Respiratory Society and the European Society of Cardiology, patients were classified according to their mortality risk classification and clinical prognostic scoring [14]. According to the 30-day early mortality risk of patients, those with hemodynamic instability are high risk; those with sPESI 1 or higher and/or PESI score 86 or higher and increased cardiac troponin levels or findings of RV dysfunction on echocardiography are medium-high risk; Patients with high sPESI and PESI scores but normal troponin levels and no evidence of RV dysfunction on echocardiography are classified as moderate-low risk; finally, patients with sPESI 0 and PESI 85 or below are classified as low risk [14].

According to the mGPS, a CRP value below 10 mg/L was assigned a value of 0 points, a CRP value greater than 10 mg/L and an albumin level less than 35 g/L was assigned a value of 1 point, and a CRP value greater than 10 mg/L and an albumin level greater than 35 g/L was assigned a value of 2 points. In our study, conventional standard CRP tests were analyzed.

The duration of hospitalization, the administration of thrombolytics, and the development of complications during hospitalization were evaluated and recorded.

The exclusion criteria for the study were as follows: Patients under the age of 18, those with missing CRP, albumin, hemograms at the time of diagnosis, patients with missing imaging studies, patients with missing PESI and sPESI scores, and patients with missing vital signs records were excluded from the study.

This study is retrospective. This study was approved by Dicle University Faculty of Medicine, Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee on 14.02.2024 with number 120. Our study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

The minimum required sample size was determined to be 326, assuming an effect size of 0.4100772, an alpha level of 0.05, and a power of 0.95.

In the study, patient groups were classified according to certain characteristics. Scale data were shown as mean±standard deviation, median, minimum and maximum. Categorical data were expressed as number and percentage. The Chi-square test was used to determine whether there was a statistical difference between the groups of categorical data. The conformity of the data to normal distribution was determined by Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, Kurtosis and Skewness, coefficient of variation and histogram examination. Continuous and normally distributed data were compared using Student-t test. Mann-Whitney U test was used for pairwise comparisons of data that did not fit the normal distribution. Kruskal-Wallis test was used for triple comparisons and evaluated with Bonferroni correction.

The present study sought to elucidate the correlation between the mGPS and PESI scores, as well as with other biological markers such as CRP, albumin, NLR, and systolic TA. Kendal’s Tau correlation analysis was conducted to calculate the correlation coefficient and to assess the relationship between categorical variables, at least one of which was not normally distributed.

Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed with various parameters to predict the low and high PESI score. The analysis yielded a set of parameters deemed suitable for inclusion in a multivariate logistic regression analysis model. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test was employed to assess the model’s fit.

ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the cut-off values and the efficiency of the model.

All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Science) 27.0 Windows package program and p < 0.05 was taken as the critical decision-making value.

Results

The study’s sample population included 334 out of 436 acute patients diagnosed with PE and treated at the Dicle University Chest Diseases Service and Respiratory Intensive Care Unit between 01.01.2020 and 01.01.2024. 102 patients were excluded from the study due to missing data elements.

The mean age of the patients was 61.52 years (± 17.12). Of the 334 acute PE patients, 53.6% were female (n = 179) and 155 were male (46.4%). Furthermore, 69.8% of the patients had never smoked (n = 233), while 18.9% were active smokers (n = 63).

Of the patients, 116 (34.7%) had no history of comorbidity at the time of diagnosis, while 65.3% had a comorbidity. The mean systolic TA was 121.21 ± 18.82 mmHg, and the mean pulse rate was 96.31 ± 18.23 bpm. The mean CRP level was 67.6 mg/L (± 114.9 mg/L) with a maximum value of 476 mg/L. The mean albumin level was 32.0 ± 6.4 mg/dL. According to the early mortality risk classification of patients, 157 (47.0%) patients were classified as intermediate-low risk (see Table 1).

In the multivariate logistic regression model, the sPESI score was found to have a statistically significant impact on predicting whether the PESI score would be low or high (p = 0.005). According to this model, a higher sPESI score increased the likelihood of a high PESI score by 5.18 times (OR: 5.180; 95% CI: 1.662–16.140). Another parameter in the model, the presence of RV dysfunction on echocardiography, was also determined to increase the likelihood of a high PESI score by 4.2 times (p = 0.007) (OR: 4.201; 95% CI: 1.487–11.867).

A further significant variable in the model was the presence of comorbid conditions (p < 0.001). The presence of any specified comorbidity increased the risk of a high PESI score by 8.3 times (OR: 8.249; 95% CI: 3.262–20.861). Additionally, low oxygen saturation was found to be another factor that increased the likelihood of a high PESI score (p = 0.003). A 1% decrease in oxygen saturation was associated with an 11% increase in the probability of a high PESI score (OR: 0.886; 95% CI: 0.818–0.959).

The parameter with the highest contribution to the model was the mGPS score (p < 0.001; Wald: 39.431). A 1-point increase in the mGPS score increased the likelihood of a high PESI score by 32.3 times (OR: 32.270; 95% CI: 10.911–95.442) (see Table 6).

As a result of these analyses, 3 parameters were selected to create a new model to predict mortality. Accordingly, systolic TA < 110 mmHg = 1 point; saturation < 92%=1 point; mGPS score of 2 points = 2 points. Patients were scored accordingly. ROC analysis was performed to compare the effect of this scoring result, PESI and sPESI on mortality. Discussion

Acute pulmonary embolism (PE) is a highly fatal condition that requires prompt intervention, making the early prediction of prognosis critically important. To address this, various prognostic scoring systems have been developed. However, many of these existing scoring systems are complex, and almost none include parameters that assess the inflammatory aspect of the disease. Moreover, most of them fail to integrate laboratory, radiological, and clinical findings comprehensively. Therefore, in our study, we aimed to emphasize the inflammatory aspect of the disease by primarily evaluating the mGPS scoring system, along with other inflammatory parameters, and assessing their effectiveness in combination with radiological and clinical criteria.

Analysis of the demographic and descriptive data of our patients revealed a mean age of 61.52 ± 17.12 years, with a median age of 65.5 years. These findings are consistent with values reported in similar studies in the literature. For instance, a study investigating various biomarkers for early mortality prediction, which included 697 patients, reported a mean age of 62 ± 16.7 years [15]. Similarly, in a retrospective study by Ma et al., involving 248 patients and examining 30-day mortality, the mean age was found to be 66.7 years [16]. Age is a significant factor in both the prognosis and the occurrence of acute pulmonary embolism (PE). Studies have demonstrated that patients with acute PE are generally over 50 years of age, with its incidence increasing with age in both sexes [17, 18].

In this study, early mortality (30-days) was found to be 8.7% (n = 29), while 90-days mortality during follow-up was 12.3% (n = 41). These results were consistent with most studies in the literature. In a study by Norton et al., which evaluated 245 patients, the 28-day and 6-month mortality rates were reported as 8.0% and 18.0%, respectively [19]. Additionally, in a study by Jovanovic et al. examining inflammatory biomarkers for early mortality prediction in acute PE, the 30-day mortality rate was found to be 8.9% [15].

In a study conducted in Turkey by Büyükşirin et al., the 30-day mortality rate was reported as 11.8% [20]. The discrepancy between their findings and the results of our study may be explained by the higher proportion of male patients in their cohort, as acute PE generally exhibits a higher mortality rate in men. Similarly, another study from Turkey by Duman et al., which investigated inflammation and mortality in acute PE, reported a 30-day mortality rate of 1.0% and a 6-month mortality rate of 5.9% [21]. The lower mortality rates observed in their study compared to ours may be attributed to the exclusion of high-risk patients from their analysis.

In the study conducted by Ma et al., the mean heart rate was reported as 94 (86–108) bpm in the mortality group and 88 (80–96) bpm in the non-mortality group. Similarly, respiratory rate in the same study was found to be 23 (20–25) bpm in the mortality group and 21 (20–23) bpm in the non-mortality group. These findings indicate that both heart rate and respiratory rate were higher in patients who experienced mortality [16].

In the present study, the mean of systolic blood pressure (SBP) was 121.21 ± 18.81 mmHg, respiratory rate was 19.97 ± 5.13 bpm, heart rate was 96.31 ± 18.23 bpm, and oxygen saturation was 88.47 ± 9.01%. A statistically significant relationship was found between mortality and SBP, respiratory rate, heart rate, and oxygen saturation. Overall, our findings were consistent with similar studies in the literature.

In another study investigating inflammatory parameters in acute PE, patients with a systolic blood pressure (SBP) below 90 mmHg were found to have significantly higher neutrophil counts [22]. Similarly, in a study examining the relationship between another inflammatory parameter, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and acute PE prognosis, a high NLR (cut-off: 5.12) was associated with higher respiratory rates and heart rates, as well as lower oxygen saturation and SBP [23]. In a comparable study, when the NLR cut-off was set at 4.4, it was also found to be associated with lower SBP and higher heart rates [24].

In relation to this, our analysis of vital signs in patients with varying mGPS scores, an indicator of inflammatory status, revealed that the group with an mGPS score of 2 was associated with higher heart rates, respiratory rates, and lower oxygen saturation. This relationship was statistically significant. These findings indicate that the analysis of vital signs and mGPS in relation to mortality yielded consistent results. This suggests that the mGPS score provides mortality-related results consistent with vital signs in acute PE.

In a study investigating the relationship between laboratory findings and mortality in patients with acute PE, elevated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were found to be associated with 30-day and 180-day mortality, as well as prolonged hospital stays [21]. Similarly, in another study by Büyükşirin et al., elevated CRP and neutrophil levels, along with decreased lymphocyte counts, were associated with 30-day mortality [20]. In a study conducted by Özcan et al., elevated CRP and decreased albumin levels were also associated with 30-day mortality [25]. Furthermore, Norton et al. identified significant associations between elevated CRP, NLR, decreased albumin levels, and 28-day mortality [19].

This study compared laboratory findings with mortality and mGPS scores. In the group of patients with fatal outcomes, hemoglobin and albumin levels were found to be significantly lower, while CRP, NLR, urea, and creatinine levels were significantly higher. Similarly, when laboratory parameters were compared based on mGPS scores, hemoglobin, albumin, potassium, and calcium levels were significantly lower in the group with an mGPS score of 2. Conversely, CRP, NLR, and urea levels were significantly higher in the mGPS score 2 group.

In acute VTE, the increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines leading to thrombotic activation constitutes a significant predisposing factor for acute PE. Among these pro-inflammatory cytokines, TNF-alpha and IL-6 play a crucial role in the development of this thrombotic state, both directly and through the mediation of CRP. A study has demonstrated that increased CRP levels lead to an increase in adhesion molecules, consequently enhancing thrombotic activity [26]. Since mGPS is one of the indicators of the patient’s inflammatory status, our study found that inflammatory laboratory parameters were associated with the severity of the disease.

In this study, we compared mGPS with various right ventricular (RV) failure findings, including RV/LV > 1, RV dilation, tricuspid insufficiency (TI), increased pulmonary artery (PA) diameter, and the McConnell sign. As a result, in patients with RV failure findings on echocardiography (ECHO), mGPS score 2 was found to be significantly more frequent. In a related study by Soylu et al., a statistically significant relationship was observed between high NLR and RV/LV ratio > 1 [24]. In acute PE, an RV/LV end-diastolic ratio > 0.9 on ECHO is a significant parameter for mortality. Moreover, RV dilation in patients with intermediate (submassive) 30-day mortality risk is also an important factor for early mortality risk. In conclusion, the association between mGPS score and ECHO findings may predict the impact of mGPS on mortality.

This study investigated the impact of mGPS score, PESI, and sPESI on 30-day and 90-day mortality. In this regard, for 30-day mortality, all patients who died had an mGPS score of 2. Additionally, in the group with low PESI and sPESI scores, there was only one patient who died. Thus, an mGPS score of 2 encompassed all the deceased patients. This pattern was also observed for 90-day mortality. When statistically analyzing 30-day and 90-day mortality with mGPS, PESI, and sPESI, patients with an mGPS score of 2, PESI score ≥ 86, and sPESI score ≥ 1 had significantly higher 30-day and 90-day mortality rates.

In a study conducted by Duman et al., the effects of inflammatory parameters on 30-day, 180-day, and 12-month mortality in acute PE were examined. It was found that elevated CRP levels were associated with 30-day, 90-day, and 12-month mortality [21].

Similarly, in a study by Liu et al. involving 101 patients, the relationship between albumin levels and mortality in acute PE was investigated, and low albumin levels were found to be associated with mortality [27].

In a study conducted by Büyükşirin et al., it was found that elevated CRP levels had a significant impact on early mortality in acute PE [20]. In another study, the average albumin levels in the mortality group were reported as 3.2 ± 0.6 g/dL, while in the non-mortality group, the average albumin levels were 3.7 ± 0.5 g/dL. This difference was found to be statistically significant. In the same study, the average CRP levels for the mortality and non-mortality groups were reported as 15.8 mg/dL (4.8–24.6) and 5.1 mg/dL (2–10), respectively, and this difference was also found to be statistically significant [25].

In a study conducted by Jovanovic et al., the relationship between CRP levels and adverse outcomes caused by acute PE during hospitalization was investigated. The mean CRP level in the group with adverse outcomes was reported as 80.4 mg/L (51.0–179.1.0.1), while in the other group, it was 41.2 mg/L (16.0–95.8.0.8). According to these results, higher CRP levels were found to be associated with more frequent adverse outcomes [15].

In a study investigating 28-day and 6-month mortality in acute PE, both 28-day and 6-month mortality were found to be associated with higher CRP levels. The same study also identified a significant relationship between low albumin levels and 28-day and 6-month mortality [19].

Multiple meta-analyses and studies in the literature have investigated the prognostic power of PESI and sPESI in predicting outcomes in acute PE patients. Most of these studies concluded that both sPESI and PESI are effective in predicting mortality in acute PE [28–32]. Consistent with these findings, our study demonstrated that an sPESI score of 1 or higher and a PESI score of 86 or higher were significantly associated with 30-day and 90-day mortality.

PESI and sPESI scores are validated scoring systems for predicting the prognosis of acute PE, and numerous studies have demonstrated that echocardiographic evidence of RV dysfunction is also a significant prognostic indicator in acute PE. In analyses involving these prognostic markers, the majority of high-risk patients were found to have an mGPS score of 2. This finding suggests that the mGPS score, which accurately identifies high-risk patients similar to PESI and sPESI—both more complex systems—or echocardiographic markers requiring specialized expertise, can serve as a simpler yet effective alternative for prognostic assessment.

In this study, patients were classified based on their 30-day mortality risk. All high-risk patients were classified as having an mGPS score of 2. Additionally, 10.3% (n = 8) of low-risk patients also had an mGPS score of 2. Statistical analysis revealed that patients with an mGPS score of 2 had a significantly higher 30-day mortality risk (Table 3).

In a study evaluating inflammatory parameters according to 30-day mortality risk classification for acute PE, the mean CRP levels were reported as 6.6 ± 4 mg/dL for the low-risk group, 8.9 ± 4.8 mg/dL for the intermediate-low-risk group, 11.4 ± 4.6 mg/dL for the intermediate-high-risk group, and 13.5 ± 7.1 mg/dL for the high-risk group. These findings demonstrated a significant correlation between increased 30-day mortality risk classification and elevated CRP levels. Similarly, albumin levels were reported as 3.9 ± 0.7 g/dL, 3.7 ± 0.8 g/dL, 3.2 ± 0.6 g/dL, and 3.1 ± 0.5 g/dL for the low, intermediate-low, intermediate-high, and high-risk groups, respectively, indicating a significant association between higher risk levels and lower albumin levels [33].

In this study, the correlation between the PESI score and the mGPS score, as well as the correlation of each with CRP, albumin, systolic BP, and NLR, was analyzed. A positive, strong, and significant correlation was observed between the PESI and mGPS scores (r = 0.536, p < 0.001). This indicates that as the mGPS score increases, the PESI score also rises. When comparing the PESI score with the inflammatory parameter CRP, a positive but weak and significant correlation was identified. For albumin, a significant, moderate, and negative correlation with the PESI score was found. Despite CRP and albumin being components of the mGPS score, neither showed a strong individual correlation with the PESI score. This correlation emerged only when these two parameters were combined in the mGPS score.

No studies in the literature specifically examined the correlation between the mGPS score, CRP, or albumin and the PESI score. However, studies comparing various inflammatory parameters were reviewed. In a study by Phan et al., the relationship between NLR and PESI/sPESI was evaluated through correlation analysis. A positive, moderate, and significant correlation between NLR and PESI was reported (r = 0.270, p < 0.01) [34]. Similarly, in our study, a weak, positive, and significant correlation was found between the PESI score and NLR.

The predictive power of the mGPS score for mortality or prognostic parameters has not been evaluated through logistic regression analysis in the literature. However, various studies have employed other inflammatory parameters. In a multivariate logistic regression model developed for predicting 30-day mortality in acute PE, NLR (OR: 1.132, 95% CI: 1.044–1.228; p = 0.003), hemoglobin (OR: 0.942, 95% CI: 0.907–0.979; p = 0.003), and the sPESI score (OR: 1.936, 95% CI: 1.121–3.345; p = 0.018) were reported as significant predictors [16].

In the analysis conducted by Duman et al., a Cox regression model was developed to predict 180-day mortality in acute PE. NLR and age were identified as significant predictors. For 180-day mortality, the model included NLR ≥ 3.25 (HR: 2.77, 95% CI: 1.48–5.18; p = 0.001) and age (per 1-year increase; HR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.04–1.09; p < 0.001) as significant parameters [21].

Similarly, in the study by Liu et al., a model for predicting 30-day mortality identified albumin (OR: 0.846, 95% CI: 0.762–0.939; p = 0.002) and NLR (OR: 1.171, 95% CI: 1.073–1.277; p < 0.001) as independent predictors [27].

In our study, logistic regression analysis was conducted using various parameters to develop a model that could potentially replace the PESI score. The dependent variable in this analysis was planned as having a PESI score ≤ 85 points or ≥ 86 points. Thus, the aim was to compare the predictive power of different parameters in determining whether the PESI score was low or high. In a study by Kahya et al., which investigated the relationship between OSAS and acute PE, PESI scores (low and high) were compared with OSA scores [35]. Similarly, in our study, univariate analyses were performed to evaluate the predictive power of sPESI, the presence of RV dysfunction on echocardiography, comorbidities, DVT presence, systolic blood pressure, oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, heart rate, NLR, and mGPS score in predicting whether the PESI score was low or high. Subsequently, multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed using parameters that were significant in the univariate analysis. The final model included sPESI score, RV dysfunction on echocardiography, presence of additional comorbidities, oxygen saturation, and mGPS score as predictors for distinguishing between low and high PESI scores. Among these, the most powerful parameter in the model was the mGPS score.

When the studies to date are analyzed, the effect of mGPS score on mortality in acute PE patients with stable course (massive emboli were not included) was examined in the study by Çelik et al. which is the only study in which the effect of mGPS score on mortality was examined. Accordingly, for the multivariate logistic regression performed in terms of mortality prediction, the odds ratio (OR) was found to be 2.120 (1.089–3.754)(p = 0.011) if the mGPS score was 1 point. The OR was reported to be 3.350 (1.457–5.376)(p < 0.001) if the mGPS score was 2 points. Once more, the most powerful variable in the multivariate model created in this study was reported to be an mGPS score of 2 [36]. This finding aligns with the results of our study. However, the observed discrepancy in Odds Ratios might be attributable to the exclusive inclusion of stable Acute PE patients in the pertinent study.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis results showed that we planned to create a new scoring system for predicting mortality in acute PE patients. We aimed for this scoring model to include laboratory parameters that are not included in the PESI score, to be less complex and to include the inflammatory aspect of acute PE. Accordingly, the most efficient model emerged with the combination of mGPS, systolic TA and saturation. In this model, there was no need for any imaging or further investigations and we were not dependent on the patient’s history. In addition, this model also took into account the effect of the inflammatory aspect of the disease, which is always in the background, on the prognosis. Accordingly, our model had a lower AUC value than PESI. However, it had a higher AUC value than sPESI. This showed that our new model is both practical and useful for clinical use and that it gives better results than a widely used prognostic system such as sPESI.

We found only one study in the literature directly related to the prediction of mortality in acute PE patients by mGPS score. In a study of 258 patients in our country, a statistically significant correlation was found between mGPS score and mortality in stable acute PE patients. mGPS score mean was reported as 0.55 ± 0.67 for patients without mortality and 1.09 ± 0.62 for the mortality group. Accordingly, the mean mGPS score was significantly higher in the mortality group (p < 0.001). However, evaluating the mGPS score as scalar data and reporting it as mean may not give statistically strong results. Statistical analysis by categorizing the mGPS scores as in our study will give more accurate results. In the same study, the mean CRP level was reported as 2.1 ± 2.8 mg/dL in patients who did not die and 4.2 ± 5.1 mg/dL in patients who died. Therefore, CRP level was found to be statistically significantly higher in patients with mortal prognosis. In the same study, the mean Albumin value was reported to be 3.71 ± 0.57 g/dL in patients with a non-mortal course and 3.69 ± 0.61 g/dL in patients with a mortal course. This difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.103). However, since massive acute PE patients, rheumatologic diseases, chronic renal failure patients, chronic liver failure patients were excluded in this study, it may not reflect the mortality rate for acute PE clearly. In this study by Çelik et al., the AUC value (area under the curve) for PESI was reported as 0.761 and 0.792 for mGPS in the ROC analysis performed in terms of the predictive power of mortality. However, no cut-off value was mentioned in the study [36].

There was only one study in the literature on the use of the mGPS score to predict prognosis in patients with acute PE until the time of our study. In their study, hemodynamically unstable patients, that is, high-risk patients, were not included in the study. In addition, there was no direct analysis of the relationship between mGPS score and PESI score. Therefore, our study is the only study that includes all patient groups (low, intermediate-low, intermediate-high and high risk), examines the relationship between mGPS and PESI score, investigates the efficacy of mGPS, PESI and sPESI in predicting mortality in acute PE patients and is the study with the largest patient population.

Conclusions

In the present study, mGPS scoring was found to be superior and more usable than sPESI scoring in terms of mortality in patients with acute PE.

In this study, we retrospectively examined the impact of the mGPS score and other inflammatory parameters on the prognosis of acute pulmonary embolism (PE). We found that the mGPS score is a significant predictor of both mortality and PESI scores. Furthermore, we concluded that inflammatory parameters, which are often underrepresented in prognostic scoring systems for acute PE, can be effectively integrated through this scoring model.

To date, only one study has explored the impact of the mGPS score and other inflammatory parameters on acute PE mortality. However, that study was limited to patients with stable acute PE. Thus, our study stands out as the only one in the literature with both a larger patient cohort and greater sample diversity.

Limitations

Our study provides valuable contributions to the literature. However, there are certain limitations to our study, including its single-center design. The retrospective nature of the study can also be considered a limitation to some extent. Furthermore, the lack of analysis of patient data related to treatment can be considered a limitation.

As a result of all these findings, there is a clear need for more studies, especially randomized controlled trials to evaluate the prognostic impact of inflammatory parameters and the mGPS score in patients with acute PE.
