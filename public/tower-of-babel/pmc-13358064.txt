Ultrahigh efficiency solar evaporation through orchestrated multiphase flow
Authors: Ruolan Tang, Wanqi Chen, Bo Yang, Jiantao Wang, Bo Zhang, Banghe Lv, Hai-Le Yan, Zongbin Li, Song Li, Peitao Liu, Xing-Qiu Chen, Liang Zuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Solar-driven interfacial evaporation (SDIE) represents a sustainable solution to alleviate global water scarcity. While holding great promise, developing energy-efficient and salt-resistant systems remains a critical challenge. Here, we address this issue by establishing a multiphase-flow dynamics framework that couples water replenishment, vapor dissipation, salt rejection, and heat transfer. An integrated evaporation system is designed using bimodal porous polyvinyl alcohol–polyvinyl pyrrolidone hydrogels for synchronized water supply and salt reflux, perforated Juncus effusus stems to facilitate vapor generation and escape, and flat-band λ-Ti3O5 powders for broadband solar absorption. Under one-sun irradiation, the system achieves an exceptional evaporation rate of 11.2 kg m−2 h−1 (normalized to the top-illumination projected area) and an apparent efficiency of 278.3% (defined as the ratio of total energy gain from incident solar irradiation and environmental heat harvesting to solar input). Notably, it operates stably in ~15 wt.% saline water without salt crystallization. Outdoor tests under natural sunlight yield a daily freshwater production of 39.8 L m−2 (normalized to the top-illumination projected area). This work presents a robust and scalable approach to sustained solar desalination by resolving energy, water, vapor, and salt management in SDIE systems.

Introduction

‌Freshwater scarcity represents a critical global challenge, posing severe threats to both ecosystems and human populations‌1. Addressing this crisis demands innovative and sustainable technological solutions‌, ‌among which seawater desalination stands as the most viable long-term strategy‌2–4. ‌Although mature technologies such as reverse osmosis and multi-stage flash distillation have been widely deployed, ‌solar-driven interfacial evaporation (SDIE) has garnered substantial research interest owing to its environmental benignity and economic feasibility, especially for decentralized applications in remote and off-grid regions‌5–11. Recent advances in SDIE systems have approached theoretical solar-to-vapor efficiency limits through high-performance photothermal materials for broadband solar absorption12,13, architectural optimization to minimize thermal dissipation, and interfacial water confinement to reduce vaporization enthalpy14,15. Nevertheless, two key constraints hinder practical applications: dilute solar flux density ( < 1 kW m−2) and thermodynamic salt crystallization.

From the perspectives of energy transfer and mass transport, an ideal SDIE system relies on the synergistic integration of four essential processes: (i) enhanced energy harvesting beyond solar input, (ii) continuous interfacial water supply, (iii) unobstructed vapor diffusion, and (iv) sustainable salt rejection. To achieve this, four key aspects must be addressed methodically. First, elevated structures enhance the capture of environmental energy by increasing surface area and optimizing thermal gradients16,17. However, such designs simultaneously compromise capillary water delivery and exacerbate salt crystallization at the evaporation interface18,19. Thus, a trade-off between energy-harvesting efficiency and water/salt transport must be carefully managed. Second, hierarchically porous matrices are employed to sustain continuous water replenishment and salt recirculation. Specifically, macropores support efficient fluid transport and salt cycling, whereas micropores facilitate capillary-driven water flow20,21. Despite these merits, precise regulation of pore architectures remains an unresolved challenge, limiting the system’s optimization potential. Third, interconnected pore architectures are crucial for enabling efficient vapor migration from internal pores to outer surfaces. Yet, the persistence of stagnant “dead zones” highlights the urgent need for engineered vapor-dissipation pathways22,23. Resolving this issue is pivotal for improving overall vapor diffusion efficiency. Fourth, vertical channel configurations provide a strategy for sustainable salt reflux, aimed at mitigating persistent crystallization challenges24–26. However, the coupled dynamics of salt diffusion and water supply in elevated structures remain poorly quantified, presenting a critical knowledge gap in system design. Collectively, optimizing these four aspects necessitates precise orchestration of multiphase transport and coupled water-vapor-salt-heat dynamics within solar evaporators.

Hydrogel-based evaporators represent a versatile SDIE platform, endowed with unique merits including autonomous buoyancy, superior mechanical flexibility, and tailorable hydrophilicity27,28. Importantly, tunable three-dimensional (3D) porous networks of hydrogels provide distinct advantages, enabling: (i) water state regulation through molecular-level engineering of network topology to modify interfacial water constitutions, (ii) fluid transport enhancement via controlled pore architectures to facilitate interfacial water supply and salt reflux, and (iii) hybrid system integration with solar absorbers, natural scaffolds, and vapor-extraction chimneys to optimize heat transfer and vapor dissipation29–31. Despite these promising features, current strategies remain predominantly empirical, lacking a deep understanding of the coupled water-vapor-salt-heat transport dynamics. This knowledge gap substantially hinders the rational optimization of materials and architectures, thereby calling for physics-governed design frameworks to systematically advance SDIE systems.

Guided by multiphase-flow dynamics and heat transfer principles, we developed a high-aspect-ratio hydrogel-based λ-Ti3O5/PVA/PVP/JE (TPPJ) evaporator system (Fig. 1a) that integrates three synergistic innovations. (i) A bimodal pore architecture, fabricated via directional freezing-induced phase separation of λ-Ti3O5-laden polyvinyl alcohol (PVA)/polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP) hydrogels, establishes dual-scale vertical channels for efficient capillary water delivery and passive salt rejection (Fig. 1b). (ii) Bioinspired vapor chimneys, formed by integrating perforated Juncus effuses (JE) stems into the hydrogel matrix, provide extra vapor nucleation sites and facilitate chimney-effect-assisted vapor escape (Fig. 1c). (iii) An inverted conical cavity design concurrently reduces optical reflectance, enlarges evaporative surface area, and refines thermal gradients to enhance energy utilization (Fig. 1c). Under one-sun irradiation in an open environment, the optimized TPPJ configuration achieves an ultrahigh evaporation rate of 11.2 kg m−2 h−1, while exhibiting stable salt resistance over 100 hours of operation in ~ 15 wt.% saline water. Moreover, the integrated solar desalination device yields an outdoor daily freshwater production of 39.8 L·m−2 under natural sunlight (8 h). Notably, both the evaporation rate and freshwater output are consistently normalized to the top-illumination projected area, enabling direct comparison with conventional evaporators. Through coordinated multiscale architecture design, this work resolves the long-standing challenge of coupled water-vapor-salt-heat transport in SDIE systems, unlocking a scalable route toward real-world solar desalination applications.Fig. 1Conceptual architecture of λ-Ti3O5/PVA/PVP/JE (TPPJ) evaporator.a High-aspect-ratio TPPJ evaporator composed of λ-Ti3O5 powders, PVA/PVP hydrogels, and perforated JE stems, enabling environmental energy harvesting beyond incident solar input. b Vertically aligned bimodal pore channels created by controlled phase separation during directional freezing, promoting water transport and salt reflux (highlighted in the dashed rectangle). c Incorporated internal chimney-like pathways derived from perforated JE stems, enhancing interfacial evaporation and facilitating vapor escape.

Results and discussion

Tailored bimodal pore hydrogel via directional freeze-drying

Adequate water supply is crucial for efficient solar-driven evaporation and persistent salt cycling. High-aspect-ratio evaporators often suffer from insufficient water replenishment, particularly under intense solar irradiation or high-salinity conditions. To address this need, hierarchical porous hydrogels with varied PVP/PVA mass ratios—denoted PPH-0 (0:1), PPH-1 (1:9), PPH-2 (1:4), PPH-3 (3:7), PPH-4 (2:3), and PPH-5 (1:1)—were synthesized via directional freeze-drying (Methods section and Supplementary Fig. 1). Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that PPH-0 (pure PVA) exhibited uniformly arranged vertical channels with cross-sectional pore diameters of around 9 µm (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Figs. 2–5). Upon incorporation of PVP into PVA (PPH-1 to PPH-5), a consistent bimodal pore structure emerged, featuring both large and small macropores in cross-section (Fig. 2b, c). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy confirmed the acetalization reactions between hydroxyl (─OH) groups of PVA and aldehyde (─CHO) groups of Glutaraldehyde (GA), along with hydrogen bonding interactions between PVA─OH and PVP─C = O moieties (Supplementary Figs. 6, 7). The bimodal pore architecture stemmed from phase separation in the PVA/PVP blends during gelation, driven by disparities in polymer miscibility and local crosslinking density. Elevated PVP content promoted network densification in polymer-rich regions, while concurrently generating large macropores in water-rich domains and small macropores within polymer-dense zones32. Swelling behavior analyses of the PPH hydrogels verified their water transport capacity (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9, and Supplementary Table 2). The equilibrium swelling rate increased with PVP content, peaking at PPH-5. This behavior is attributable to the enhanced hydrophilicity from polar carbonyl (C = O) groups along PVP chains. Compared with the disordered porous structures produced by conventional freeze-casting, vertically aligned channels created via directional freeze-casting significantly improved the swelling capacity of all PPH samples.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and Raman spectroscopy revealed that the intermediate water content in hydrated PPH samples increased with higher PVP content (Fig. 2e–g, Supplementary Figs. 10–12, and Supplementary Table 3). The vaporization enthalpies of interfacial water decreased from 1765 J g−1 for hydrated PPH-0 to 1521 J g−1 for hydrated PPH-5, in sharp contrast to that of bulk water (2475 J g−1) (Fig. 2h, Supplementary Fig. 13, and Supplementary Table 4). This reduction consistently supports the cluster-evaporation mechanism as the underlying physical origin, since fewer hydrogen bonds need to be broken per escaping entity compared to the dissociation of individual water molecules in bulk water. Molecular dynamics simulations further corroborated this interpretation, showing that the average number of hydrogen bonds per H2O molecule decreased to 2.800 and 2.766 in PVP- and PVA-rich environments, respectively, markedly lower than 3.072 in bulk water (Supplementary Fig. 14). Beyond water management, the porous architecture of PPH substrates also enhanced solar absorption. Incorporating 10.71 wt.% λ-Ti3O5 into PPH-0 and PPH-5 (Fig. 2i and Supplementary Fig. 15) yielded broadband solar absorptivities of 97.3% and 98.5%, respectively, exceeding that of pure λ-Ti3O5 powders4. Given its superior hydration and optical properties, PPH-5 was selected as the model hydrogel framework for synergistic optimization of energy-mass transport.

Biomimetic internal vapor-activation via embedded JE

Rapid vapor dissipation is another key requirement for maximizing solar evaporation efficiency. However, conventional SDIE systems often face performance limitations due to constrained vapor pathways. Inspired by vascular plant transpiration, we introduced lightweight JE aerenchyma tissues as embedded components for vapor activation and transport. As shown in Fig. 3a–c, pristine JE stems feature large, interconnected macropores ( ~ 50 μm in diameter) arranged in hollow star-shaped structures. Composed primarily of α-cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin (Supplementary Fig. 16), the material is inherently superhydrophobic, with a water contact angle of 162.8° (Supplementary Fig. 17). This extreme water repellency prevents liquid infiltration into the core region, thus preserving low-resistance vapor diffusion pathways. In addition, the superhydrophobic interfaces enable point-contact wetting, which facilitates unimpeded vapor diffusion and low-adhesion droplet roll-off. These properties work synergistically to suppress vapor back-condensation, sustain continuous vapor release, and enhance vapor transport efficiency. Synergetic energy-mass transfer for efficient evaporation

Efficient and sustainable interfacial evaporation relies on well-balanced synergy between energy transfer and mass transport. As demonstrated above, PVP incorporation into PVA forms a bimodal pore framework that enhances water transport. Meanwhile, pre-perforated JE stems create complementary hydrated shells for vapor generation and hollow cores for vapor release. Leveraging these synergistic design elements, a series of cylindrical evaporators was fabricated to achieve optimal energy-mass coupling (Supplementary Figs. 20–26). These evaporator series include: PPHs (hydrogel-only baseline systems), TPPHs (λ-Ti3O5@PPH-5), TPPCs (10.71 wt.% λ-Ti3O5@PPH-5 with empty vertical cavities), TPPJs (10.71 wt.% λ-Ti3O5@PPH-5 with perforated JE segments), and TPPJ-5-H (10.71 wt.% λ-Ti3O5@PPH-5 with five alkali-treated perforated JE segments).

During steady-state solar evaporation, energy input derives from two primary sources: direct solar irradiation and net thermal exchange with the environment38,39. Accordingly, maximizing solar absorption while suppressing parasitic heat losses becomes critical. With identical evaporator dimensions (1.4 cm diameter × 4 cm exposure height), evaporation rates under one-sun irradiation (normalized to the top-illumination projected area) rose from 3.12 kg m−2 h−1 for PPH-5 to a peak of 4.91 kg m−2 h−1 for TPPH-4 (10.71 wt.% λ-Ti3O5@PPH-5). The TPPH-4 setup achieved an average solar reflectance of 1.5% (compared to 3.6% for λ-Ti3O5 particles), which was further reduced to 0.53% upon incorporation of an inverted conical light-trapping cavity at the top. This design minimized reflective losses to 2.9 J h−1 against a total solar input of 554.2 J h−1.

Thermal losses in 3D evaporators are governed by Stefan-Boltzmann, Fourier, and Newton’s laws, necessitating precise thermal gradient control. Through optimizing the exposure height (10 cm) and conical cavity geometry (0.9 cm diameter × 5 cm depth), the high-aspect-ratio TPPH evaporators achieved a refined thermal profile: 30.5 °C at the top surface and 20.5 °C at the lateral surface (Supplementary Fig. 26). This architecture suppressed radiative and convective losses at the top surface to 13.1 J and 16.6 J, respectively, with negligible conductive loss. Crucially, the lateral surface temperature (20.5 °C) remained below ambient temperature (24.5 °C), enabling a net ambient energy gain of 677.4 J h−1. These results are consistent with recent studies highlighting the regulatory role of ambient air in columnar evaporators via thermal interactions at the top and sidewall surfaces40.

In addition, the temperature distributions and evaporation efficiencies of both TPPH and TPPJ evaporators were analyzed (Supplementary Figs. 31 and 32, and Supplementary Tables 7–12). To quantify energy contributions to evaporation, we define the apparent efficiency (η) as the ratio of total harvested energy (including incident solar irradiation and ambient thermal energy) to solar input. The TPPJ-5 configuration delivered a peak apparent efficiency of 278.3% (Fig. 4b), demonstrating substantial ambient heat capture far exceeding the supplied solar energy. Quantitative analysis showed that the system captured 554.2 J from solar input, along with an additional 1010.7 J from the environment via passive heat exchange. This environmental contribution corresponds to 182.37% of the solar input, highlighting the dominant role of ambient heat transfer under tested conditions. These results underscore the critical function of ambient thermal harvesting enabled by the 3D architectural design. Accordingly, TPPJ-5 achieves an optimal trade-off among efficient vapor transport, consistent water supply, and structural integrity within high-aspect-ratio geometries.

To assess the operational stability of TPPJ-5 evaporators, long-term indoor evaporation tests were performed on a single unit using 15.3 wt.% saline water (Supplementary Figs. 33–39). The system operated continuously for 10 days under a diurnal cycle of 10 hours of one-sun illumination and 14 h of dark conditions. Throughout the test, a stable evaporation rate of 11.12 ± 0.26 kg m−2 h−1 was maintained, without surface salt precipitation (Fig. 4c). In addition, the tested unit retained excellent flexibility and elasticity, demonstrating strong mechanical durability. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping revealed a gradual increase in Na+/Cl– distribution from the bottom to the top of the evaporator following the test. Structural analysis further confirmed that the hydrogel matrix retained its integrity and remained in close contact with the embedded JE segments.

Superhydrophobic JE-enhanced chimney effect

Rapid vapor dissipation is indispensable for sustaining high evaporation fluxes in elevated 3D evaporators. Conventional chimney-assisted systems facilitate vapor removal by leveraging externally driven buoyancy-induced convection within macroscale gas transport channels40–43. Recent work on 3D-printed hierarchical evaporators30 has demonstrated that internal bottom-to-top gas pathways generate directional airflow, which thins the vapor boundary layer, suppresses flow stagnation, and delivers dry ambient air to the evaporation interface. In contrast, the TPPJ system adopts an integrated architecture in which perforated JE stems simultaneously expand vapor-generation area, provide low-resistance escape pathways, inhibit salt accumulation via Janus interfaces, and support the hydrogel matrix. The intrinsic superhydrophobicity of JE stems prevents liquid intrusion and vapor recondensation, ensuring uninterrupted vapor flow. Localized heating generates internal density and pressure gradients, which promote directional vapor movement along these structure-guided pathways. Crucially, the TPPJ system operates as a coupled multiphase-flow framework that integrates vapor escape, mass transfer, and interfacial heat localization within a single architectural unit. Thus, its enhanced evaporation performance stems not only from chimney-like air convection but also from the synergistic coordination of internal vapor dynamics, mass-energy regulation, and structural integration.

To experimentally visualize the JE-enhanced chimney effect, schlieren imaging was conducted on three TPPJ configurations: TPPJ-0 (no JE), TPPJ-1 (one JE), and TPPJ-5 (five JEs) (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 40). TPPJ-1 exhibited distinct, upward-directed vapor plumes (absent in TPPJ-0), confirming chimney-effect-driven convective flow. With lateral vortex disturbances minimized, TPPJ-5 demonstrated stronger, more vertically oriented vapor streams, highlighting the collective enhancement of convective transport enabled by multiple perforated JE channels. These observations provide direct visual evidence of the perforated JE-enhanced chimney effect.

To clarify the role of superhydrophobicity in vapor transport, we performed comparative experiments: (i) TPPJ vs. TPPC, and (ii) TPPJ-5 vs. TPPJ-5-H (Supplementary Figs. 41 and 42). For the TPPC series, increasing the number of empty cavities modestly improved vapor escape relative to TPPC-0 (cavity-free), but their overall performance remained inferior to the counterpart TPPJ devices with perforated hydrophobic JE segments. Under one-sun irradiation, evaporation rates were measured at 9.85 kg m−2 h−1 (TPPC-5, five empty cavities), 11.21 kg m−2 h−1 (TPPJ-5, five hydrophobic JEs), and 9.24 kg m−2 h−1 (TPPJ-5-H, five hydrophilic JEs). These results demonstrate that the superhydrophobicity of perforated pristine JE stems is essential for suppressing vapor recondensation and ensuring efficient vapor transport in solar-driven systems.

To understand the underlying mechanism, a ‌simplified Multiphysics model‌ was developed to simulate ‌temperature distributions and gas flow dynamics in chimney structures‌ (Supplementary Fig. 43). The model was applied to three single-channel evaporators: (i) TPPC-1 with a hollow hydrophilic channel, (ii) TPPB-1 with a hollow hydrophobic channel, and (iii) TPPJ-1 with a perforated hydrophobic JE channel. In TPPJ-1 (Fig. 4e), warm, humid air ascended through the perforated JE channel (Fig. 4f, g), generating a negative pressure at the evaporation front (chimney effect). This drew in dry ambient air, inducing top-of-channel convection and activating internal evaporation surfaces. In addition, vapor flux in the bottom vortex “dead zone” was higher on the right side than on the left, demonstrating hydrophobic JE’s ability to mitigate stagnation. Pressure analysis along the gas channel (Supplementary Figs. 44 and 45) showed that the JE channel’s bottom inlet pressure was ~ 0.04 Pa, increasing to − 1.19 Pa at the top outlet, confirming the chimney effect’s role in generating negative pressure relative to the surroundings and enhancing air intake through the channel. In contrast to TPPB-1 and TPPC-1, TPPJ-1’s porous JE architecture produced pulsating airflow that penetrated the vapor layer, markedly enhancing evaporation and heat removal.

Marangoni convection-driven salt reflux

Effective salt rejection is necessary for rapid and sustained solar desalination. Among various strategies, Marangoni-effect-driven salt rejection is widely acknowledged as a highly efficient approach to mitigate salt accumulation in solar desalination systems44. The salt-rejection performance of the TPPJ-5 configuration was evaluated through an in situ test by depositing salt crystals on its top surface, with the bottom immersed in simulated saline solution and real seawater, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 46 and 47). The downward dissolution behavior of salt deposits was tracked (Supplementary Movie 4), validating the robust salt-rejection capability inherent to this evaporator design.

The outstanding anti-salt fouling performance of TPPJ evaporators arises from synergistic ion management via three complementary mechanisms. First, solar-driven evaporation induces continuous capillary flow that dynamically dissolves incipient surface salt, preventing localized supersaturation at the evaporation interface. Second, the bimodal pore architecture establishes lateral and vertical salinity gradients that drive diffusive ion redistribution from high-salinity regions to low-salinity pathways, facilitating efficient salt backflow into the bulk solution45,46. Third, a stable thermal gradient across the evaporation surface and underlying bulk brine triggers sustained Marangoni convection, which actively promotes downward salt reflux and mitigates salt accumulation at the evaporation surface44,47–50. These coupled processes form a self-regulating ion-transport network that enables long-term, salt-resistant operation under high-salinity conditions. In addition, perforated superhydrophobic JE stems introduce Janus interfaces that inhibit capillary wicking and brine infiltration, confining ion transport to defined hydrophilic paths and effectively preventing surface salt accumulation.

Scalability and outdoor performance of TPPJ evaporators

As demonstrated above, the high-aspect-ratio TPPJ-5 architecture (1.4 cm diameter × 10 cm exposure height) achieved four key objectives: (i) suppressing lateral vapor stagnant zones (“dead zones”), (ii) establishing a well-defined vertical thermal gradient, (iii) minimizing convective/radiative heat losses, and (iv) maximizing environmental heat harvesting. To evaluate structural scalability, we tested two large-scale evaporator variants: with and without fifteen perforated pristine JE segments. Both variants were fabricated by scaling the diameter from 1.4 cm to 4 cm while reducing the exposure height from 10 cm to 6 cm. Under one-sun irradiation, the evaporation rates reached 3.31 kg m−2 h−1 (no JE) and 4.93 kg m−2 h−1 (fifteen JEs). Notably, under identical dimensions and wind-free conditions, both configurations outperformed the prior system51 (evaporation rate ≈ 2.5 kg m−2 h−1), confirming the TPPJ architecture retains high effectiveness even upon scaling (Supplementary Fig. 51).

For large-scale applications, a modular array design composed of independently operating TPPJ units offers a feasible solution, circumventing the inherent limitations of monolithic structures while enabling efficient broad deployment (Supplementary Figs. 52 and 53). To assess the impact of array integration on per-unit evaporation performance, we conducted controlled indoor desalination experiments under strictly identical conditions: one-sun top-only illumination, 24.5 °C (ambient temperature), and natural seawater from the Bohai Sea in Shandong, China. Under these conditions, a standalone TPPJ-5 achieved a freshwater production rate of 8.0 kg m−2 h−1, while the eight-unit array maintained a rate of 7.2 kg m−2 h−1, both normalized to the top-illumination projected area. Array deployment thus causes only a marginal reduction in performance, preserving per-unit efficiency at levels comparable to those of standalone devices.

To evaluate outdoor performance, we designed a portable solar desalination device with two core components: (i) an evaporation subsystem integrating forty-five TPPJ evaporators for solar steam generation, and (ii) a condensation subsystem utilizing cooling water to facilitate vapor condensation (Fig. 5a). Outdoor experiments were conducted on the main campus of Northeastern University in Shenyang, China (Supplementary Figs. 54 and 55). From a broader scalability perspective, we examine the key limitations and trade-offs inherent to 3D evaporators, particularly the challenge of maintaining performance consistency across replicated units and the need to balance structural complexity with cost-effectiveness. While array integration enables scalable deployment, system-level considerations, including airflow distribution and vapor management, must be prioritized. Recent theoretical studies have identified vapor accumulation, ambient humidity, and airflow conditions as critical constraints on the scalability of 3D evaporator arrays52. Our experimental results demonstrate that the TPPJ design effectively mitigates these limitations by integrating internal vapor-dissipation pathways, thereby preserving per-unit performance in modular arrays and enabling stable outdoor operation under varying environmental conditions. In this regard, the present work provides an experimental complement to recent scale-up theories and highlights a viable structural strategy for scalable 3D evaporation. Although the current experimental scale is constrained by practical laboratory and field-testing limitations, the findings hold significant implications for guiding future scalable designs.

To benchmark performance, we compare the outdoor daily freshwater output of our system against existing hydrogel-based, array-type, and high-aspect-ratio evaporators (Fig. 5d). Comprehensive data on indoor water evaporation rates and efficiencies under one-sun irradiation, as well as outdoor daily freshwater yields under natural sunlight, are provided in Supplementary Table 13. Material cost analysis indicates that a 1 m2 TPPJ-5 evaporator requires approximately $8.05 in materials based on current industrial pricing. When evaluated against established cost-effectiveness metrics53 for 3D evaporators (Supplementary Tables 14–15), the TPPJ-5 system ranks second (1391.3 g h−1 $−1). Crucially, its high-aspect-ratio structural design significantly reduces spatial footprint while enabling comparable freshwater production. This combination of efficiency, economy, and space-saving architecture underscores its strong potential for scalable and deployable solar desalination solutions.

In summary, we report a holistic TPPJ evaporation system that orchestrates multiphase flow dynamics and heat transfer, enabling the unification of energy harvesting, water supply, vapor escape, and salt reflux. This integrated design delivers an ultrahigh evaporation rate of 11.2 kg m−2 h−1 under one-sun irradiation in an open environment, alongside an outdoor daily freshwater yield of 39.8 kg m−2 under natural sunlight—both normalized to the top-illumination projected area—while maintaining long-term operational stability. This exceptional performance stems from the synergistic integration of a bimodal porous PVA/PVP hydrogel matrix for rapid water transport and salt reflux, bioinspired perforated JE channels for efficient vapor generation and release, an inverted conical cavity geometry that refines temperature gradients, and a high-aspect-ratio design that extends energy harvesting beyond solar input. This coordinated strategy overcomes the fundamental challenge of water-vapor-salt-heat coupling, offering a scalable pathway toward practical solar desalination.

Methods

Chemicals and raw materials

Pristine JE stems were purchased from Jiangxi Juncus Effusus Co., Ltd, China. All other chemicals and raw materials were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China. These include titanium dioxide powders (rutile-TiO2, 100 mesh, 99.9 wt.% metals basis), PVA particles (molecular weight 13,000 − 23,000), PVP particles (molecular weight 58,000), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37 wt.%), glutaraldehyde solution (GA, 50 wt.% in H2O), sodium chloride (NaCl, ≥ 99.5 wt.%), and Lugeshi staining (Lugol’s iodine solution, 5 wt.%).

High-purity λ-Ti3O5 powders were prepared by sintering rutile-TiO2 nanoparticles at 1150 °C for 1 h in a hydrogen atmosphere with a flow rate of 1.5 L min−1, followed by slow cooling to room temperature with a cooling rate of 3 °C min−1. Certain quantities of the as-prepared λ-Ti3O5 powders were mixed into PVA-based hydrogel evaporators, acting as solar absorbers.

Pristine JE stems were cut into two distinct lengths: 4 cm and 10 cm. The 4 cm segments were individually assigned for water contact angle measurements and microstructural characterizations. Some of these segments were laterally wrapped with cotton wool ( ~ 1 mg), with one end immersed in a Lugol’s iodine solution for 3 h. After removing the cotton wool, they were immediately subjected to XCT imaging. As for the 10 cm segments, individual specimens were laterally wrapped with cotton wool ( ~ 2 mg) and subsequently used to fabricate TPPJ evaporators and for in situ optical tracking of water ingress behavior.

Synthesis of PVA/PVP and λ-Ti3O5/PVA/PVP hydrogels

First, 1.0 g of PVA particles was added to 20 mL of deionized (DI) water at 65 °C. After stirring 30 min to ensure complete dissolution, the suspension was cooled to room temperature, forming Solution A0. Second, predetermined amounts of PVP particles were separately dissolved in Solution A0 within 10 min, with PVP/PVA mass ratios of 1:9, 1:4, 3:7, 2:3, and 1:1. This yielded the PVA/PVP aqueous solutions designated as Solution A1, A2, A3, A4, and A5. Third, 0.24 g of λ-Ti3O5 particles was dispersed in 750 μL of each Solution A0 − A5, forming λ-Ti3O5/PVA/PVP aqueous dispersions (Solution B0 − B5). Fourth, varying quantities of λ-Ti3O5 particles (0.06 g, 0.12 g, 0.18 g, 0.24 g, and 0.30 g) were individually dispersed in 750 μL of Solution A5, forming λ-Ti3O5/PVA/PVP aqueous dispersions (Solution C1 − C5). Finally, before gelation, 281.26 μL of GA solution was added to each of Solutions A0 − A5, B0 − B5, and C1 − C5. All mixtures underwent bath sonication to achieve homogeneous distribution of components.

Each as-prepared solution was poured into a cylindrical glass mold, followed by the addition of 1.5 mL HCl (1.2 mol L−1) per mold. To initiate gelation and directional freezing, the mold was placed in a refrigerator freezer, with a low-temperature metal block positioned underneath to facilitate directional solidification. After freezing at − 40 °C for 2 h, the gels were freeze-dried at − 60 °C for 20 h. The resulting dried hydrogels were then submerged in DI water at 80 °C overnight to remove any unreacted chemicals. The as-synthesized hydrogel samples were systematically labeled PPH-0 to PPH-5, TPPH-B0 to TPPH-B5, and TPPH-C1 to TPPH-C5, corresponding to the precursor solutions A0 − A5, B0 − B5, and C1 − C5. Control hydrogel samples were additionally fabricated via conventional freeze-drying for comparison.

Fabrication of TPPJ evaporators

The initial steps for preparing TPPJ evaporators were identical to those used for synthesizing TPPH-C4 hydrogels. A varying number (1, 3, 5, or 7) of perforated pristine JE segments wrapped with cotton wool were placed vertically in a prepared glass mold. Solution C4 was mixed with 1.5 mL HCl (1.2 mol L−1) and immediately transferred to the glass mold. During gelation, a taper-type plastic die was pressed at the top of the gels. The subsequent directional freezing and freeze-drying procedures were also identical to those employed for TPPH-C4 hydrogels. The as-fabricated evaporators had a diameter of 1.4 cm, with an inverted conical cavity (0.9 cm diameter × 5 cm depth) at the apex. After freeze-drying, they were submerged in DI water at 80 °C overnight to remove any unreacted chemicals. Finally, the fully swollen TPPJ evaporators were washed with DI water to ensure cleanliness and then delivered to solar evaporation tests.

Fabrication of TPPC and TPPJ-5-H evaporators

TPPC evaporators were fabricated following the same procedure as for TPPJ evaporators, except that iron wires were vertically inserted into the glass molds in place of perforated pristine JE segments. After freeze-drying, these iron wires (1, 3, 5, or 7 per mold) were withdrawn to form open vertical cavities within the TPPC evaporators. All other fabrication parameters and geometric dimensions remained identical to those of the TPPJ evaporators. TPPJ-5-H evaporators were prepared by substituting the perforated superhydrophobic JE segments in the TPPJ-5 design with perforated hydrophilic JE segments. Before assembly, the hydrophilic segments were obtained by alkali-treating perforated pristine JE segments in a 4 wt.% NaOH solution for 3 minutes, according to an established method54.

Materials characterizations

The morphologies of PPH and JE samples were characterized using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (JEOL JEM7001F) operated at 15 kV. The chemical compositions and elemental valence states of PPHs were analyzed via x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (Thermo Fisher Scientific K-Alpha) with an Al-Kα x-ray source (1486.6 eV). The functional groups of PPHs and pristine JE stems were identified using an attenuated total reflection Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS10). The diffuse reflectance spectra of λ-Ti3O5 powders and λ-Ti3O5/PPH mixtures were measured using an ultraviolet−visible−near-infrared spectrometer (Shimadzu UV-3600i Plus) equipped with an integrating sphere (ISR-3100). The water contact angles of PPHs and JE stems were measured with an optical contact angle system (SL200KS). Raman spectral analysis (with 532 nm laser excitation) was conducted to investigate the enhanced formation of IW by hydrogels. The vaporization enthalpies of bulk water and IW in hydrated hydrogels were determined using both the thermometric method and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC25, TA Instruments). The porosities of PPHs and the characteristics of their open pores were evaluated by mercury intrusion porosimetry (AutoPore V9620).

In situ water adsorption photographs of pristine JE segments were captured using an advanced opto-digital microscope (Olympus DSX500). Water ingress into JE segments was examined via energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping. EDS mapping was performed using an Oxford Instruments Ultim40 detector mounted on a JEOL JSM-7001F field-emission scanning electron microscope. The system operated at an acceleration voltage of 15 kV and a working distance of 10 mm. Micro-XCT imaging was employed to visualize the water distribution within the segments. XCT measurements were performed using a ZEISS Xradia 610 Versa system, with a relatively low tube voltage of 50 kV and an operating power of 4.5 W to minimize beam-induced heating while maintaining sufficient contrast for structural reconstruction. Each sample was positioned at a fixed working distance from the X-ray source, achieving voxel sizes of 3 µm or 0.7 µm, which define the scan resolution. Upon completion of the scans, XCT images were processed, and the reinforcements were separated from the matrix using Avizo software.

Experimental setup for water uptake and lifting test

A fully dried PPH-5 sample (1.4 cm diameter ×;16 cm height) was vertically placed using insulating polyethylene (PE) foam as a holder, with its lower end immersed in DI water (25 °C) by 2 cm. This configuration was analogous to that used in the indoor solar evaporation tests on evaporators with an exposure height of 10 cm. During the swelling process, the water-rising height and gross weight of the wetted hydrogel sample were carefully monitored. The data from the dry to hydrated state were used to evaluate the mean tortuosity of PPHs with vertically aligned channels.

Experimental setup for validating the chimney effect

The chimney effect in TPPJ devices was validated using an off-axis single-mirror schlieren imaging system. This optical setup utilizes a knife-edge as a half-plane spatial filter to modulate light passing through the vapor flow field, enabling visualization of refractive-index gradients caused by vapor convection. The system is configured with a spherical concave mirror (203 mm diameter, 750 mm focal length), a point light source (positioned at twice the focal length of the concave mirror), and a camera. The reflected and focused beam is directed onto the knife-edge positioned at the focal plane of the imaging lens. When light passes through regions of non-uniform vapor density, local variations in refractive index deflect the beam. The knife-edge selectively blocks a portion of these deflected rays, converting angular deflection into light-dark intensity contrasts that form characteristic schlieren patterns.

Experimental setup for indoor solar evaporation

The indoor solar evaporation performance of hydrogel-based evaporators was evaluated under one-sun irradiation (AM1.5 G, 1 kW m−2) in an open environment. A xenon lamp (Solar-300, China Education Au-light Co., Ltd.) was used as the simulated solar source, and the solar flux was calibrated with a light power meter (CEL-NP2000-2, China Education Au-light Co., Ltd.). Individual evaporators were inserted into a PE foam board, either as a single unit or as part of an array. Each integrated device was then placed in a beaker containing 50 mL (or 500 mL) aqueous solution and exposed to one-sun top illumination. An aperture was attached to control the amount of light incident on the device. The measurements were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions with constant temperature ( ~ 24.5 °C) and relative humidity (RH ≈ 33%). The mass change of the aqueous solution was monitored over time using a balance with an accuracy of 0.1 mg (HC-5003, China Huachao High-tech Instrument Co., Ltd.). All infrared photographs were taken using an infrared thermal camera (Ti400, Fluke, USA)4. Both the evaporation rates and freshwater productivities were consistently normalized to the top-illumination projected area of the tested evaporators. Unlike thermal efficiency, the term “apparent efficiency” was employed to quantify energy contribution, defined as the ratio of total energy gain—from both incident solar irradiance and environmental heat harvesting—to solar input.

Long-term indoor and outdoor solar evaporation experiments

Long-term indoor solar evaporation experiments were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions in an open environment, using either simulated ~ 15 wt.% saline water or real seawater collected from the Bohai Sea in Shandong, China. An optimized TPPJ-5 evaporator (1.4 cm diameter × 10 cm exposure height) featuring an inverted conical cavity (0.9 cm diameter × 5 cm depth) at its apex was subjected to one-sun top illumination. During each experiment, the system operated continuously for 10 consecutive days, with 10 h of daytime one-sun illumination and 14 h of dark conditions daily. The mass change of the saline solution and the surface state of the TPPJ-5 evaporator were monitored in real time. To assess salt reflux behavior, ion concentrations (K+, Ca2+, Na+, Mg2+) in the bulk solution were measured using an inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometer (Avio500, Perkin Elmer Co., Ltd.) before and after the operation.

Outdoor solar evaporation experiments were performed using a homemade portable solar desalination device. Forty-five TPPJ evaporators were embedded in a single PE foam sheet and floated on either simulated 3.5 wt.% saline solution or real seawater in a container. Cooling water circulation was employed to facilitate condensation of water vapor on the condenser inner walls. All experiments were conducted at the main campus of Northeastern University, Shenyang, China. From 08:00 to 16:00, the intensity of natural sunlight on the transparent quartz glass roof of the solar desalination device and the cumulative freshwater production were continuously monitored. Meanwhile, the temperatures of TPPJ evaporators, cooling water, and ambient environment, as well as the humidity levels inside and outside the device, were recorded. Ion concentrations in the water before and after purification were evaluated.

Modeling of heat and mass transfer

Heat and moisture transport in two-phase flow was simulated using COMSOL Multiphysics 6.3 by analyzing the evolution of water saturation. A 2D computational domain was constructed based on the geometry of the experimental prototypes, comprising two coupled regions: the gas region and the sample-and-water region, which included the exposed chimney and a bulk water reservoir. The computational domain was discretized with approximately 35338 nodes, featuring a minimum element size of 0.01 mm. Solar irradiation was simulated by applying a heat flux of Qs = 1 kW m−2 at the top of the chimney. Multiphysics simulations were performed to investigate the surface temperature and airflow fields of three sample types containing a single vertical channel: (i) a hollow hydrophilic channel (TPPC-1), (ii) a hollow hydrophobic channel (TPPB-1), and (iii) a perforated hydrophobic JE segment (TPPJ-1). The model incorporated laminar flow, moisture transport in air, and heat transfer in humid air.
