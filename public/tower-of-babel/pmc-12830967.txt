Association of diabetes, obesity, and their co-occurrence with suicide ideation in Korean adults using nationally representative data from 2019 to 2021
Authors: Shiva Raj Acharya, Sudarshan Subedi, Diwash Timilsina
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGiven the surging suicide rates in Korea, identifying the key contributing factors is crucial to mitigating associated risks. We analyzed nationally representative data from the Korea Health Panel Survey (KHPS) spanning three years (2019–21) to estimate the association of diabetes, obesity, and their co-occurrence with suicide ideation in Korean adults. After screening, a total of 32,166 eligible adults aged ≥ 19 years were included. Suicide ideation and diabetes were assessed via binary responses, whereas obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m²) was classified according to the Korean Society for the Study of Obesity (KSSO). Multilevel-adjusted regression models and subgroup analyses were performed. The prevalence of obesity, diabetes, and suicide ideation was 30.5% (95% CI: 30.0–31.0%), 14.4% (95% CI: 14.0–14.8%), and 5.5% (95% CI: 5.2–5.7%), respectively. Obesity (OR: 1.16, 95% CI: 1.02–1.31), diabetes (OR: 1.28, 95% CI: 1.09–1.52), and co-occurrence (OR: 1.28, 95% CI: 1.04–1.61) were associated with an increased risk of suicide ideation. Diabetic (OR: 1.36, 95% CI: 1.07–1.64), and obese females (OR: 1.45, 95% CI: 1.21–1.74), and younger adults with obesity (OR: 1.53, 95% CI: 1.12–1.98) exhibited higher odds of suicide ideation. This study demonstrates a strong positive link between obesity, diabetes, and their co-occurrence with suicide ideation. Introduction

Suicide remains a pressing global health issue, contributing to over 700,000 deaths annually and ranking as the fourth leading cause of death among adults1. South Korea, in particular, faces a severe burden, with suicide rates reaching 24.1 per 100,000 population—more than double the OECD average2. While mental health conditions such as depression are well-recognized contributors, emerging evidence highlights the influence of diabetes and obesity on suicide ideation3–7. Therefore, it is crucial to underline the importance of examining both mental and physical health determinants of suicide ideation.

Globally, diabetes affects 537 million adults and is linked to a 1.5–2.5 times higher risk of suicide ideation, driven by factors such as disease burden, complications, and comorbid depression4,8,9. Similarly, obesity has been reported to increase the odds of depression and suicide ideation by 55%10 and 31%11, respectively. However, evidence remains conflicting. For instance, a U.S.-based study found no significant association between obesity and suicide ideation12, whereas Klinitzke et al. demonstrated a lower risk of suicide among overweight and obese individuals13. Prior Korean studies also suggest that sociocultural pressures surrounding body image further exacerbate mental health challenges for both obese and underweight individuals5,14–16, with 1.18 and 1.32 times higher odds of suicide ideation, respectively, compared to those with a healthy weight5. The substantial prevalence of diabetes (12–15%)17 and obesity (30–40%)18 in South Korea further underscores the need for a comprehensive assessment of how the co-occurrence of these conditions influences suicide ideation9,19. However, existing studies are limited by methodological limitations, including small sample sizes, restricted to specific populations rather than the general adult population, and insufficient inclusion of multiple confounders, such as socioeconomic status, lifestyle behaviors, and prior chronic and mental health conditions, all of which may affect both physical and psychological outcomes12,15,19–23. Furthermore, most studies explore these conditions in isolation, with limited exploration of their co-morbid influence or demographic-specific analyses5,6,9,12,19. These gaps have hindered a nuanced understanding of the potential relationship between obesity, diabetes, and suicide ideation, particularly in populations with high societal pressures related to body appearance and chronic disease management. Therefore, this study aims to expand on prior work by examining the association of obesity and diabetes, both independently and concurrently with suicide ideation, utilizing large, nationally representative samples and multilevel-adjusted models. The findings offer significant insights into the multifaceted relationship between these conditions, informing tailored health strategies and interventions to mitigate suicide risk among vulnerable groups.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective study utilized nationally representative data from the Korea Health Panel Survey (KHPS) from 2019 to 2021 (Version 2.2). The KHPS is jointly conducted by the Korea Institute for Health and Social Affairs (KIHASA) and the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS). This survey provides a comprehensive dataset on health conditions, disease prevalence, healthcare usage, and health-related behaviors. Participants were selected using a stratified cluster sampling approach with probability-proportional-to-size to ensure representation of the Korean population. Data collection involved in-person interviews conducted during household visits. The KHPS was designed to represent Korea’s general population using the 2015 Population and Housing Census, employing multistage probability-proportional and stratified cluster sampling. Accordingly, this study utilized the KHPS 2019–21 datasets (Ver. 2.2) and accounted for survey design by applying the weight variables as specified in KHPS procedures24. Further details on the KHPS are described elsewhere24,25. Of the initial pool of 41,145 participants from the KHPS 2019–2021, we selected and analyzed 32,166 adults (excluded, n = 8,979) who met all the inclusion criteria, which were as follows: (1) individuals aged 19 years or older, (2) availability of data on suicide ideation, and (3) availability of information on diabetes, anthropometric measurements (height and weight), and other covariates.

This study involved analysis of publicly available secondary data, accessed with approval (2019–21 Ver. 2.2) from KHPS at https://www.khp.re.kr:444/eng/main.do. We obtained approval from KIHASA and NHIS to access and use the data for this study (approval date: October 8, 2024). This study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki for studies involving human participants. In the KHPS, written informed consent was provided by all participants or their legal representatives. The KHPS adheres to strict protocols to ensure participant autonomy and confidentiality.

Outcomes measures

Suicide ideation

The main outcome of the study was suicide ideation. In KHPS, suicide ideation was assessed by asking the participants, “In the past year, have you ever had thoughts of wanting to die or commit suicide?”. The responses were binary (yes/no). For this study, participants with suicide ideation were defined as those who reported having thoughts of suicide.

Diabetes and obesity

The KHPS collected information on participants’ health conditions, including diabetes, by asking the participants, “Are you currently suffering from diabetes that has been diagnosed by a doctor?”24. Participants with diabetes were defined as those who reported having the condition. The survey measured participants’ height (cm) and weight (kg), which were used to determine BMI levels (kg/m²) and assess obesity status. BMI levels were categorized as underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–22.99 kg/m²), overweight (23–24.99 kg/m²), and obese (≥ 25 kg/m²), following the Korean Society Guidelines for Obesity in Korean adults26. In this study, adults with a BMI of 25 kg/m² or greater were classified as having obesity.

Covariates

We incorporated several covariates, identified in prior studies7,9,14,16,19,27, that may influence suicide ideation, diabetes, and obesity: (a) demographic and socioeconomic factors: age (years), gender (male, female), education status (middle school or below, high school, higher), residency area (urban, non-urban), marital status (yes, no), employment (yes, no); (b) lifestyle factors: physical activity (yes, no), current smoking status (yes, no), and alcohol intake [never, sometimes (less than or once a month)/frequently (2–4 times or more per month)], and (c) health-related factors: hypertension, stress, depression, and anxiety. The KHPS includes the data on participants’ walking activity, including the number of active walking days in the past week and total daily walking minutes. We calculated the total weekly walking time in minutes and classified participants as physically active or not based on the WHO guidelines for adults, with physically active defined as > 150 min of moderate to intense walking per week28. Stress levels were measured using a four-point scale: “very much,” “a lot,” “a little bit,” and “rarely.” For this study, participants reported “very much” or “a lot” stress levels were classified as the presence of stress. Depression was assessed by asking, “During the past year, have you experienced feelings of excessive depression lasting more than two weeks in a row?”. Anxiety was assessed by asking, “During the past year, have you experienced excessive anxiety or worry that interfered with your daily life for more than six months?”. Hypertension was assessed by asking participants whether they are currently suffering from hypertension that has been clinically diagnosed by a doctor29. The responses for hypertension, depression, and anxiety were recorded as binary variables (yes/no).

Statistical analyses

Results were presented using descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, and mean ± SD. The data’s normality was assessed through the Q-Q plots and the Shapiro-Wilk test. The differences between groups for categorical and continuous data were evaluated using the Chi-square test and Student’s t-test, respectively. Multilevel adjusted binary logistic regression models were used to examine the association between diabetes, obesity, and suicide ideation. Model 1 (the basic model) was adjusted for demographic factors, including age, gender, and marital status. Model 2 (the intermediate model) included adjustments for demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related factors, such as age, gender, marital status, education status, residency area, employment, BMI, hypertension, depression, anxiety, and stress. Model 3 (the full model) was further adjusted for lifestyle factors, including physical activity, smoking, and alcohol intake, in addition to the variables in Model 2. The odds ratios (ORs) for suicide ideation based on the co-occurrence of diabetes and obesity were estimated and presented using a forest plot. We further performed a subgroup analysis to examine the relationship between diabetes, BMI categories, and suicide ideation across sex and age groups. Additionally, the relationship between obesity severity and the risk of suicide ideation was evaluated based on adult BMI classes: Class I (25.0–29.9 kg/m²), Class II (30.0–34.9 kg/m²), and Class III (≥ 35.0 kg/m²), according to the WHO guidelines for the Asia-Pacific region30.

Nagelkerke’s R² and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) were used to assess model fit. Multicollinearity was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with all values below 5. Statistical analysis was performed using SAS Ver.9.4. A significance level of p < 0.05 was set, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated.

Results

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of participants based on the prevalence of suicide ideation. Among the 32,166 participants included in the analysis, the mean age was 57.54 ± 16.53 years. The prevalence of suicide ideation, obesity, and diabetes was 5.5% (95% CI, 5.2 − 5.7%), 30.5% (95% CI, 30.0–31.0%), and 14.4% (95% CI, 14.0–14.8%), respectively. Participants with diabetes exhibited significantly higher rates of suicide ideation (19.2% vs. 14.1%, p < 0.001). Additionally, suicide ideation was prevalent among females, married individuals, those who were overweight or obese, residents of urban areas, unemployed individuals, those with a middle school or lower education level, current smokers, and those with occasional or frequent alcohol intake (all p < 0.05).

The association between obesity, diabetes, and suicide ideation was assessed using multilevel logistic regression models, as shown in Table 2. The results indicated that both obesity and diabetes were significantly associated with suicide ideation. Across all three adjusted models, diabetes increases the risk of suicide ideation [OR, 95% CI: basic model (1.47, 1.28–1.65); intermediate model (1.24, 1.08–1.45); full model (1.28, 1.09–1.52)]. In the case of obesity, higher odds of suicide ideation were observed as more covariates were included in the analysis [OR, 95% CI: basic model (1.12, 1.03–1.24); intermediate model (1.15, 1.05–1.27); full model (1.16, 1.02–1.31)]. Participants with Class II obesity exhibited 1.46- and 1.31-fold greater odds of suicide ideation compared to those who were non-obese (OR, 95% CI: 1.46, 1.15–1.95) and those with Class I obesity (OR, 95% CI: 1.31, 1.11–1.82), respectively (Supplementary File 1). Although participants with Class I and Class III obesity also demonstrated higher odds of suicide ideation compared to non-obese individuals, these associations were not statistically significant (Supplementary File 1).

Figure 1 presents the subgroup analysis examining the association between diabetes, BMI levels, and suicide ideation across sex and age groups. Among females, those who were underweight (OR, 95% CI: 1.79, 1.32–2.59) or obese (OR, 95% CI: 1.45, 1.21–1.74) had higher odds of suicide ideation compared to those with normal weight (Fig. 1B). For males, the underweight group (OR, 95% CI: 1.73, 1.14–2.47) was significantly associated with suicide ideation (Fig. 1A). Females with diabetes (OR, 95% CI: 1.36, 1.07–1.64) had higher odds of suicide ideation compared to males with diabetes (OR, 95% CI: 1.23, 1.03–1.57). When stratified by age, younger adults (≤ 50 years) who were underweight (OR, 95% CI: 1.84, 1.20–2.73) or obese (OR, 95% CI: 1.53, 1.12–1.98) (Fig. 1C), and older adults (> 50 years) who were underweight (OR, 95% CI: 1.69, 1.21–2.33) or diabetic (OR, 95% CI: 1.30, 1.09–1.54) exhibited increased odds of suicide ideation (Fig. 1D).

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between diabetes, obesity, and suicide ideation within the Korean population. The findings revealed that both diabetes and obesity were associated with increased odds of suicide ideation across all regression models, even after adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related factors. Additionally, the co-presence of diabetes and obesity further elevated the odds of suicide ideation. These findings highlight the importance of considering these conditions as independent factors for suicide ideation.

Despite notable differences in the prevalence rates of diabetes (14.4%), obesity (30.5%), and suicide ideation (5.5%) observed in the study, a significant association was identified among these conditions. These prevalence rates of diabetes, obesity, and suicide ideation are also consistent with prior studies from Korea5,17,18,31,32. Consistent with these findings, studies utilizing samples from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) have also highlighted diabetes as a significant factor influencing suicide ideation among Korean adults3,4,9. This suggests that individuals with diabetes experience not only the physiological burden of chronic disease but also heightened mental health stressors and poor disease management6,9. The established link between depression and suicide7,9,33,34 suggests that diabetes-related stress and anxiety may contribute to depression6, which is prevalent in the Korean diabetic population35. This bidirectional relationship further amplifies the risk of suicide ideation, particularly when diabetes and depression coexist9.

The present study identified a positive association between obesity and suicide ideation, with obese adults demonstrating higher odds (OR: 1.16), consistent with prior research reporting ORs ranging from 1.18 to 2.545,11,19,36. However, the relationship between suicide ideation and obesity is complex. Social stigma related to body weight, particularly among individuals with obesity, may contribute to the psychological distress that can trigger suicidal thoughts5,16. Additionally, obesity-related mental health issues could also stem from self-perception and repeated failures in weight control efforts14,37. An important factor to consider is the distinction between self-perceived and clinically diagnosed obesity15,19. Suicide ideation often depends on how individuals perceive their condition and its consequences37,38. Individuals who perceive themselves as obese frequently attempt weight control, and these efforts have been linked with suicide ideation15. Moreover, the low prevalence of physical activity, a common feature among obese individuals—may contribute to both obesity and mental health issues, compounding the risk of suicide ideation38. However, a contrasting finding was also observed in our study that underweight individuals, particularly females, exhibited higher odds of suicide ideation. This may be explained by cultural and societal pressures regarding body image, where underweight individuals, especially women, experience heightened psychological stress from societal expectations or dissatisfaction with their bodies, contributing to suicide ideation5,15,16. Furthermore, neurochemical imbalance may also explain the observed associations. Diabetes and obesity have been linked to alterations in insulin and serotonin levels, which may play a role in the increased risk of suicide ideation observed in individuals with these health conditions3,39. As supported by our study, the co-existence of these conditions further elevates the odds of suicide ideation and may ultimately influence this mechanism9. However, this significant association disappeared after additional adjustments for lifestyle factors in Model 3. This attenuation likely reflects the confounding and mediating roles of health-related behaviors such as physical activity, smoking, and alcohol consumption40,41. These lifestyle factors are closely linked to both metabolic and mental health conditions, influencing inflammatory processes, stress responses, and neuroendocrine regulatory pathways associated with suicidal behaviors10,42,43. However, given the nature of our study design, which limits the ability to establish causal relationships, further longitudinal cohort and clinical observational studies are needed to explore the mechanisms underlying the reciprocal interactions between obesity, diabetes, and suicide ideation6,14.

The subgroup analysis provided valuable insights into the differing associations between obesity, diabetes, and suicide ideation across sex and age groups. Notably, both female and male participants with either obesity or diabetes demonstrated higher odds of suicide ideation, but the magnitude of the risk differed. Obese and underweight females were more likely to exhibit suicidal ideation, whereas among males, only underweight individuals showed a significant association. This finding aligns with other studies suggesting that women are more likely to internalize societal pressures regarding body image, contributing to increased mental health issues, including suicide, as discussed above14,16. For males, the growing societal expectations to maintain a lean physique and engage in bodybuilding may create psychological stress when these standards are not met44. Thus, gender differences in environmental and psychological factors may explain the increased suicide ideation associated with both underweight and obesity in females16,20. In younger adults, both underweight and obese individuals exhibited higher odds of suicide ideation, potentially due to the pressures of navigating early adulthood with body image concerns20,37. For older adults, the mental health challenges associated with managing chronic conditions like diabetes, compounded by the physical and social challenges of aging, may account for the observed increase in suicide ideation in this subgroup45. Interestingly, the absence of a strong association between co-morbid diabetes and obesity in the full model may indicate that lifestyle factors (physical activity, smoking, and alcohol intake) may mitigate the mental health risks associated with these co-existing conditions38,46. This finding suggests that interventions —including tailored lifestyle and dietary plans for the concurrent management of obesity and diabetes, along with routine mental health assessments for individuals with these conditions—could be critical for providing timely care and reducing the risk of suicide22.

The strength of this study is derived from its use of three years of large-scale, nationally representative samples and the inclusion of multilevel adjusted regression models. Furthermore, subgroup analyses by sex and age provide a nuanced assessment of how the relationship between obesity, diabetes, and suicide ideation varies across different groups. However, the study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective, cross-sectional design, it limits the ability to establish causal relationships between obesity, diabetes, and suicide ideation. Second, suicide ideation was assessed using a single self-reported question, an approach that has also been used in multiple prior studies3,4,9,47. However, this method may be prone to underreporting due to social stigma and may not capture the full range or intensity of suicidal thoughts. In contrast, other studies in Korea and globally have used structured tools, such as the Suicide Screening Questionnaire-Observer Rating (SSQ-OR)48, the Ask Suicide-Screening Questions (ASQ)49, and the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS)50, which provide more nuanced measures, including severity, intent, and associated behaviors, and are often administered by psychiatrists. Differences in assessment methods may partly explain variations in prevalence estimates and reported associations across studies. Therefore, future investigations should consider clinically oriented, comprehensive instruments rather than relying solely on self-reported responses to better understand the complexity of suicide ideation and clarify its relationship with diabetes, obesity, and their co-occurrence. Third, this study is a secondary analysis of the KHPS datasets (2019–21 Ver. 2.2), which limits the author’s control over the study design. Fourth, the self-reported data for diabetes and other study covariates may introduce bias. Fifth, debate persists regarding BMI’s accuracy in assessing obesity, as it may misestimate visceral fat51. Future studies should consider multiple obesity indicators—such as visceral fat mass, waist circumference52, mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC), and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR)—to provide a more comprehensive evaluation. Sixth, the study did not account for genetic predispositions, medication use, and environmental factors53–55, all of which may influence suicide ideation. Particularly in Korea, where a significant portion of the population utilizes Korean Medicine (KM), existing evidence suggests that KM use may influence both obesity and suicide ideation56,57 and may therefore influence their interrelationship. Additionally, cultural and social support factors—both of which have been shown to have a significant association with suicide58—were not considered in the analysis. Seventh, sleep problems and sleep quality, which are also known to be linked with suicide ideation59, were not accounted for. Eighth, the KHPS 2019–2021 dataset also includes the period of the COVID-19 pandemic, during which the pandemic had a significant impact on mental health both globally and in Korea60. Although the KHPS was conducted with strict measures and precautions during this time, the pandemic itself may have influenced the prevalence of suicide ideation and other mental health issues within the study population. Finally, our findings may not be fully generalizable to populations outside of Korea due to cultural, socioeconomic, and healthcare differences that could affect health outcomes in other regions. Therefore, while this study offers valuable insights, further research should aim to address these aforementioned limitations to advance the understanding of these complex relationships.

In conclusion, the presence of obesity and diabetes, either in isolation or co-occurrence, is linked to an increased odds of suicide ideation. This association is more pronounced in females and younger adults. The findings underscore the importance of considering obesity and diabetes along with their co-occurrence as key factors when developing targeted interventions to mitigate suicide ideation. Further clinical observational studies integrating multiple obesity metrics, accounting for gender differences, and considering genetic, cultural, and environmental factors are essential to better elucidate these findings.
