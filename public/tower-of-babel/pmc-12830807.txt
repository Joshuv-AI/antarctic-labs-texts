Comparison of multi-miRNA models from donor-matched plasma and cerebrospinal fluid as candidate biomarkers for Alzheimer’s disease prediction
Authors: Ursula S. Sandau, Jack T. Wiedrick, Trevor J. McFarland, Siting Chen, Joseph F. Quinn, Julie A. Saugstad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We discovered and validated miRNA biomarkers for Alzheimer’s disease (AD) in human cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). However, as more easily accessible biofluids are preferred for clinical assays, we compared the performance of the AD miRNAs in CSF to plasma to determine their potential use as AD biomarkers in this readily available biofluid. We obtained 320 donor- and date-matched normal control (NC) and AD CSF and plasma samples from the AD Neuroimaging Initiative, then analyzed 57 candidate AD miRNAs in both biofluids by RT-qPCR. For analysis, we divided the sample sets into 80% for discovery and 20% for validation. We then used predictive modeling of the 57 candidate AD miRNAs in the discovery phase to develop AD classifiers for each biofluid that showed similar performance in both CSF and plasma. However, in the validation phase AD classification performance was only maintained for the plasma models. When the plasma miRNA models were combined with clinical predictors (APOE genotype, age, sex, years of education) there was a boost in classification performance to a level comparable to the CSF proteins (Aβ42, t-tau, p-tau181) alone, which suggests that plasma miRNA assays combined with clinical information could serve as an alternative to more invasive CSF-based measurements. We also developed a parsimonious model based on two miRNAs that were top ranked and had increased (miR-23a-3p) and decreased (miR-423-5p) expression in AD. The ratio of these top two miRNAs classified AD with similar performance to all 57 miRNAs jointly and was sensitive to changes in cognitive score. These results support that an assay consisting of only two plasma miRNAs can classify AD from NC, and is informative about the disease stage reflected in cognitive scores.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33589-0.

Introduction

The 2024 World Health Organization reports that over 55 million people currently have dementia worldwide, and every year there are nearly 10 million new cases1. The most common form of dementia is Alzheimer’s disease (AD) which accounts for an estimated 60–80% of cases2. Currently, the only U.S Food & Drug Administration (FDA) approved disease-modifying therapies are monoclonal antibodies that target and remove Amyloid-beta (Aβ) from the brain2,3. One of which, lecanemab, is being tested in the asymptomatic stage of AD for slowing amyloid progression and/or cognitive decline in the AHEAD 3–45 Study4. Based on the 2025 AD drug development pipeline, there are currently 138 drugs being assessed in 182 clinical trials as disease-modifying biological agents or small molecule drugs, cognitive enhancing agents, or treatments for neuropsychiatric symptoms5. The mechanism of action for many of the drugs in Phase 2 or 3 clinical trials extends beyond Aβ or tau pathology to synaptic plasticity, neurotransmitter receptors, and inflammation5. Despite advancements in the development of new therapies, challenges to effectively treat AD patients include early diagnosis for timely intervention as well as monitoring therapeutic response and/or disease progression. The development of blood-based biomarkers has the potential to address these challenges as they enable greater accessibility and routine monitoring with reduced health care costs, compared to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) or imaging biomarkers. Recently, plasma measurements of tau (microtubule-associated protein), specifically the phosphorylated form of p-tau217 to non-phosphorylated tau ratio have been shown to have equivalent performance to the FDA approved CSF tests for classifying Aβ-Positron Emission Tomography (PET) and tau-PET status6. Also, stand-alone plasma p-tau217 measurements predict the development of cognitive impairment up to 10 years prior to onset in cognitively normal individuals7,8. Recently, the FDA approved the use of the Lumipulse G pTau217/Aβ 1–42 Plasma Ratio test that measures the ratio of p-tau217 to Aβ42 in plasma to diagnosis Aβ plaques in symptomatic adults 55 years and older9. While the clinical efficacy of plasma tau and Aβ tests establishes the utility for blood-based assays in AD6–10, novel blood biomarkers indicative of other pathophysiological changes, such as synaptic plasticity, are still needed to serve as a valuable component to monitoring therapeutic efficacy in clinical trials11.

MiRNAs are small non-coding RNAs that regulate the translation of messenger RNAs into proteins. Their importance is evidenced by a 2025 PubMed query of “miRNA” that returned over 187,000 publications, and by the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine award for the discovery of miRNAs and their roles in post-transcriptional gene regulation12. In rodent and/or cell culture models of AD, miRNAs regulate multiple aspects of AD pathophysiology including tau hyperphosphorylation, Aβ peptide production by the proteolytic processing of amyloid precursor protein (APP), synaptic loss, neuronal apoptosis, and neuroinflammation13–22. For example, increased levels of miR-125b-5p or miR-34a-5p decreased Aβ-induced neurotoxicity via a β-site amyloid precursor protein cleaving enzyme 1 (BACE1) dependent mechanism23–25. Further, an increase in miR-23b-3p inhibits tau hyperphosphorylation, Aβ42 peptide production, and neuronal apoptosis via inhibition of glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β)26. Rodent and cell models have also established that miRNA expression levels are altered by Aβ or tau pathology27. In humans with AD, changes in miRNA expression levels are dependent on the burden of Aβ and tau pathology as well as brain region28. Importantly, pioneering miRNA biomarker studies established that AD associated changes in postmortem human brain miRNA levels were detectable in antemortem CSF and/or plasma29,30. Subsequently, numerous candidate miRNA biomarkers for AD have been reported with reproducible differential expression of 36 miRNAs in plasma and three miRNAs (miR-125b, miR-146a, miR-135a) in the brain, CSF, and plasma31.

In our seminal CSF RT-qPCR miRNA studies, we developed an analysis pipeline that used multiple statistical methods, including machine learning, to prioritize miRNAs and then generate multi-miRNA models that classify AD vs. normal controls (NC) acquired from the Oregon Health & Science University Layton Aging and AD Center23. We then validated our miRNA analysis pipeline in a new and independent cohort of AD and NC CSF samples acquired from the University of California, San Diego Shiley-Marcos AD Research Center and demonstrated that the stand-alone classification performance of either the multi-miRNA models (AUC = 0.820) or the donor Aβ42:total tau CSF ratio (AUC = 0.861) was improved when combined into a multi-marker model consisting of miRNAs plus Aβ42:tau measurements (AUC = 0.903)25. We ultimately demonstrated that a model consisting of five CSF miRNAs classifies mild cognitive impairment (MCI) from NC and the performance of both the miRNAs (AUC = 0.705) and Aβ42:total tau CSF ratio (AUC = 0.758) was improved when combined (AUC = 0.813)24. Combinations of plasma or serum miRNAs have also been identified as candidate panels for AD diagnosis or to predict the conversion of MCI to AD32–34. Together, these studies demonstrate the potential for miRNAs to serve as an additional positive component of multi-marker models that increase the performance of biomarkers in order to indicate early brain changes in AD.

We chose to continue the use of RT-qPCR for our studies based on outcomes from a multisite biofluid and collaborative study wherein three independent laboratories examined the extracellular vesicle and RNA composition of pooled normal CSF samples and CSF from five major neurological disorders35. Based on our outcomes and experiences from these studies, we provided a recommended approach for consideration in collaborative, multi-site studies to evaluate CSF samples, included our finding of more miRNAs detected in the RT-qPCR arrays relative to those in the small RNASeq assays. This likely resulted from the fact that sequencing detects other small RNA fragments that are not miRNAs. For example, we found that the sequencing data included a large number of reads for transfer RNA fragments rather than miRNAs, while the arrays specifically targeted for miRNAs. In addition, differences in the number of miRNAs detected may result from the fact that all total RNA from 1.0 mL of CSF was used for the RT-qPCR assays, while the small RNASeq used 3.67 ng of RNA from 2.0 mL of CSF for each assay.

Herein, the goal of this study was to directly compare the performance of 57 miRNAs previously identified as candidate CSF biomarkers for AD in a new cohort of donor- and date-matched CSF and plasma from AD and NC participants. To date, limited studies have directly compared miRNA profiles in participant-matched CSF and blood from donors with AD28,36,37. Compelling results from these studies demonstrate that miRNAs altered in AD CSF tend to be unique from those altered in their matched plasma or serum28,36. Thus, we sought to determine if miRNA expression data generated from CSF vs. plasma perform comparably in multi-marker models to predict AD from NC. We obtained 320 samples from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative 2 (ADNI 2) that we divided into discovery (80% of samples) and validation (20% of samples) cohorts. We used two machine learning methods, Bayesian model-averaging (BMA) and elastic-net regression (ENR), to generate predictive models for each biofluid based on a set of 57 miRNAs identified in our27,35,38–40 and other AD studies41–58. We found that the performance of the miRNAs was comparable between the CSF and plasma in the discovery cohort. However, only the plasma models maintained performance in the validation cohort. Further, multi-marker models that combined plasma miRNAs, clinical predictors, and Aβ42:total tau CSF ratio had the most optimal performance (AUC = 0.958). Also, the performance of a more clinically useful model based on the ratio of the two top plasma miRNAs altered in AD (miR-23a-3p and miR-423-5p) was similar to that of models incorporating all 57 miRNAs, and was sensitive to changes in Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores. Together, our data demonstrates that multi-miRNA models based on plasma data exceed the performance of CSF based data. We further demonstrate that an assay consisting of two differentially expressed plasma miRNAs has the potential to both diagnosis AD, and to track cognitive decline as a marker of AD progression.

Results

Participant demographics and clinical characteristics

CSF and plasma samples from 160 participants (80 AD and 80 NC; 320 total) were obtained from ADNI 2. Nearly all of the samples were donated within six months, and all within 1 year, of the initial clinical visit (Supp Fig. 1a). 95% of the matched samples differed by less than 30 days of donation, and three donors (2%) differed by more than 60 days (Supp Fig. 1b). Based on quality control assessment of the RT-qPCR, 32 of the 320 samples (10%) were excluded due to technical failures. Of the samples included, 132 had matched CSF and plasma samples both analyzable by RT-qPCR (264 total). 11 CSF samples did not have an analyzable plasma sample, and 13 plasma samples did not have an analyzable CSF sample. Thus, the final study cohort consisted of CSF from 75 NC and 68 AD (143 total participants), and plasma from 74 NC and 71 AD (145 total participants) for a total of 288 samples (Table 1).

For the 288 samples, ~ 70% of the NC participants did not have an APOE-4 allele, while ~ 65% of the AD participants had at least one APOE-4 allele (Table 1, Supp Fig. 2a), which is expected in AD59,60. Age distributions were also matched between the AD and NC cohorts (Table 1, Supp Fig. 2b). There was a slightly higher proportion of males in the AD cohort; however, all groups were well represented (Table 1, Supp Fig. 2c). The participant cohort also included a mild but possibly expected difference in education levels (Std Diff 0.4), with NC tending to have more education (Table 1, Supp Fig. 2d). Further, MMSE scores among the AD ranged from 21 to 26 (CSF mean ± SD: 23.1 ± 1.9; plasma: 23.2 ± 1.9), with somewhat greater proportion toward 21, while MMSE scores among the NC ranged from 21 to 30 (CSF: 28.4 ± 1.9; plasma: 28.5 ± 1.9) but showed much greater proportion toward 30 (Table 1, Supp Fig. 3a).

Within each biofluid the demographics and clinical characteristics of the NC and AD samples included an analysis comparing the standardized mean difference or standardized count difference (Table 1). These standardized difference measurements (Std Diff) are informative about the direction and magnitude of differences in the variable being assessed with absolute values > ~ 0.2 regarded as potentially discriminative for disease state61,62. As expected, the NC and AD had robust differences for MMSE (Std Diff − 2.8), APOE genotype (0.8), and CSF Aβ42 (−0.5), total tau (t-tau) (1.3), and phosphorylated tau (p-tau181) (1.0) levels, similar in both the CSF and plasma cohorts. The NC and AD groups were well balanced for age, sex, and the number of excluded samples, as evidenced by low Std Diff values (Table 1). Importantly, the Std Diff values for the same characteristic were in all cases quantitatively similar between the two biofluid cohorts (e.g., MMSE Std Diff in CSF: −2.80; in plasma: −2.83). Note that the minor sample exclusions did not affect the Std Diffs for these clinical measures. This similarity between the two biofluid cohorts provides assurance that the specific sample exclusions within each cohort did not induce any bias in the composition of either cohort. Thus, the CSF and plasma cohorts can be used to fairly compare miRNA biomarker performance across biofluids.

Selection of miRNAs for use in machine learning to develop multi-miRNA models

We used our prior experience measuring human miRNAs by RT-qPCR in both CSF and plasma27,35,38–40, noting which miRNAs had a high probability of amplification and showed reasonable test-retest stability in both biofluids. Based on our published data and an extensive literature search41–58, we prioritized 57 miRNAs that had repeatedly shown consistent associations (increased or decreased) with dementia severity and predictive power for discriminating AD from NC individuals either singly or (more often) in combination with other miRNAs. We also included 6 human miRNAs as negative and positive controls, plus 1 C. elegans miRNA (cel-miR-39-3p) as a spike-in positive control. Full details regarding the assays (miRBase and ThermoFisher probes) are presented in Supp Table 1. To improve reliability of the RT-qPCR, each miRNA was run in triplicate on a custom 384-well card that included simultaneous measurement of samples from two participants (64 selected miRNAs x 3 replicate wells x 2 participant samples = 384 wells). The selected miRNAs fell into nine categories (Supp Table 1a): (1) 17 with confirmed AD association in CSF at minimum; (2) 3 with suspected but as-yet-unreplicated AD association based on our own testing; (3) 15 that we have not previously tested rigorously in CSF but that are reliably recoverable in plasma; (4) 11 similar to category (3) that additionally have reported associations to dementia-related phenotypes in the literature; (5) 4 that are novel additions to the TaqMan™ Advanced miRNA Human arrays and that we have not previously tested; (6) 7 with suspected null association to any AD-related process in CSF but that have reported associations to dementia-related phenotypes in the literature; (7) 4 that are reliably measurable in both biofluids and confirmed by own experience to be unassociated with AD for use as endogenous normalizer controls because of their known stable expression across different samples (https://assets.thermofisher.com/TFS-Assets/GSD/Reference-Materials/identifying-mirna-normalizers-white-paper.pdf); (8) 2 with known lack of expression in both CSF and plasma for use as negative controls; and (9) 1 cel-miR-39-3p spike-in positive control. For miRNA annotation we used miRBase v22.1 accession (Supp Table 1b) and provides corresponding detailed information regarding each miRNA probe in the array and their performance in the assay (Supp Table 1c).

Performance of miRNA assays in CSF compared to plasma

To determine whether the potential AD miRNA biomarkers we discovered and validated in CSF23–25 can also perform in plasma to classify AD from NC, we first assessed the quality of amplification measurements for each of the 57 candidate AD miRNAs in both biofluids by RT-qPCR. MiRNA measurements in plasma were superior to CSF in showing better sensitivity for low-concentration miRNAs, a greater proportion of miRNAs with acceptable quality-control (QC) metrics, and more consistent technical replicates (Supp Fig. 4a, b; Supp Table 1c, 2). For each miRNA we assessed and compared the log fold changes in expression for AD vs. NC in both CSF and plasma (Supp Table 2). Interestingly, we observed essentially no correlation between the CSF and plasma association values (Fig. 1a, r = 0.08). We also assessed the classification of AD vs. NC using individual miRNAs. For both biofluids the AUC is never very high for individual miRNAs (largest value is ~ 0.7 for miR-423-5p in plasma) and CSF miRNAs had lower AUC values on average (Supp Table 2).

Next, we used multi-miRNA predictive modeling to assess the performance of the 57 candidate miRNAs as biomarkers for AD classification in either CSF or plasma. To do this, the total sample cohorts were randomly divided into two groups: a Discovery and a Validation group (Supp Table 3). For the CSF group, the Discovery included n = 59 NC and n = 56 AD, while the Validation included n = 16 NC and n = 12 AD (Supp Table 3a). For the Plasma group, the Discovery included n = 59 NC and n = 57 AD while the Validation included n = 15 NC and n = 14 AD (Supp Table 3b). The discovery cohort was used to fit the model and the validation cohort used to check the predictive performance of the model on new samples that the model has not been trained on63. The discovery and validation cohorts generally had similar representation across the patient demographic and clinical characteristics from Table 1. Bayesian model-averaging (BMA) classifiers for AD were built by incorporating expression data from the discovery cohort for all 57 candidate miRNAs. The BMA classifiers showed similar predictive performance for AD in both CSF and plasma for the discovery cohort (area under the curve, AUC = ~ 0.78) (Fig. 1b). However, in the validation the performance was only maintained for plasma (AUC = 0.77), whereas in CSF it degraded by > 10 points (AUC = 0.65). We corroborated these results in the elastic-net regression (ENR) analysis of the same 57 miRNAs in the same two cohorts, again seeing similar performance for both biofluids in the discovery cohort (AUC = ~ 0.82) that was maintained in the plasma validation cohort (AUC = 0.82) but degraded by > 10 points in the CSF validation cohort (AUC = 0.70) (Supp Fig. 5). These results support that RT-qPCR measurements of miRNAs in plasma and the models based on these measurements are more reliable and less cohort-dependent than RT-qPCR measurements of miRNAs in CSF.

Importance ranking of miRNA predictors of AD in CSF and plasma

We next sought to determine the importance of individual CSF or plasma miRNAs to predictive models of AD. We used BMA to select the top 1,000 models for predicting AD using CSF (Fig. 2a) or plasma (Fig. 2b). Positive (blue) and negative (orange) coefficient signs in the figures reflect whether an increase or decrease of relative expression in the miRNA, respectively, is associated with AD in the context of the model at hand. Thus, it is expected that reliable predictors will have a stable sign (positive or negative) across models. The importance of a miRNA predictor is indexed by the posterior inclusion probability (PIP) from BMA. The PIP measures how likely it is that a variable should be included in the “true” model, given the available data. In CSF, 38 of the 57 miRNAs had a PIP > 0.01, which was our threshold for inclusion in the rank order (Fig. 2a). The PIP rank order of miRNAs is top to bottom in the figures, with age and sex (male) at the very bottom (Fig. 2a, b). Across the top 1,000 CSF models (horizontal axis), the 38 miRNAs and age adjustment were reasonably stable, but the direction of the sex adjustment was not consistent. Also, the CSF model with the highest posterior probability among all considered models contained only age and sex, and no miRNAs (Fig. 2a). In contrast, in plasma 56 of the 57 miRNAs had a PIP > 0.01 and were included in the rank order (Fig. 2b). The coefficient signs for the plasma miRNAs were similarly stable, and the direction of the sex adjustment was also far more stable than in the corresponding CSF models. Importantly, the highest-probability plasma models all contain at least two miRNAs that tend to have opposite coefficient signs as well as stable age and sex adjustments (Fig. 2b best model: miR-423-5p negative coefficient, miR-23a-3p positive coefficient).

We ranked the miRNAs by overall importance to AD prediction models by correlating miRNA PIPs from BMA with miRNA performance rank from ENR. CSF has a better rank correlation in importance between the two consensus methods (Fig. 2c, r = 0.81) vs. plasma (Fig. 2d, r = 0.60), mainly because there are fewer disagreements between BMA and ENR concerning which CSF miRNAs were of average rather than high performance. However, there was far worse agreement on which specific few miRNAs were most important for AD prediction. The circled miRNAs in the figures are those that contribute most to the bend in the curvilinear relationship between the BMA and ENR predictor importance scores; ideally just a few miRNAs should cause an extreme bend, signaling a sharp focus on those miRNAs in both modeling approaches. In CSF there are 16 circled miRNAs (Fig. 2c) vs. 7 circled miRNAs in plasma (Fig. 2d), with 1 in particular (miR-423-5p) causing most of the bending. There was also steeper linear correlation in the region of highest BMA predictor importance in the right half of the plasma figure (Fig. 2d) compared to the CSF figure (Fig. 2c). This pattern supports that plasma allows more resolution for AD predictor selection than CSF as the more important predictors appear more distinct from the less important predictors, regardless of selection method.

Predictive performance of plasma multi-miRNA models for AD across demographic subpopulations

Concluding that the plasma miRNAs have superior RT-qPCR performance and predictive models for AD compared to CSF miRNAs, we next assessed the generalizability of the plasma BMA model (incorporating all 57 miRNAs) across four demographic factors: APOE-4-positive status, adjacent decades of age, participant sex, and levels of educational attainment. We found that neither APOE-4-positive status nor age showed any appreciable difference in the miRNA model performance for predicting AD (Fig. 3a, b). Sex was a potential moderator of AD classification accuracy (Male AUC = 0.83 vs. Female AUC = 0.73), but the effect was not significant at this sample size (Fig. 3c, p = 0.1). Moreover, AD prediction models trained in a single sex were not reproducible in the validation cohorts, regardless of classifier method (Supp Fig. 6). Our moderation analysis also hinted very weakly that the accuracy of the miRNA-based AD prediction could possibly be higher in cohorts with lower average educational attainment (Fig. 3d, p = 0.3), meaning that prediction errors were larger for more educated individuals, who incidentally tended to show less AD prevalence in this participant cohort (Table 1). We also performed a sensitivity analysis for the association between years of educational attainment and AD diagnosis, CSF Aβ42:t-tau ratio, or predicted probability of AD from the plasma miRNA BMA model. In this analysis, there was a modest inverse association of education attainment with both AD diagnosis (p = 0.09) and the CSF Aβ42:t-tau ratio (p = 0.05) (data not shown). However, years of education attainment was not associated with the predicted probability of AD from the plasma miRNA BMA model (p = 0.47) (data not shown). We also assessed the robustness of the prediction model to missing or incomplete age information, even when an age adjustment is expected because the most significant risk factor for AD is aging64,65. In one version of this sensitivity analysis, we purposely “incorrectly” entered all ages into the original classifier as 75 (approximate cohort mean age) and evaluated the AUC in the discovery and validation cohorts, and found almost no difference in performance compared to using accurate age (Fig. 3e: Assuming age 75). In another version, we trained a new classifier ignoring age and applied that to the cohort, and again found very little difference in predictive performance (Fig. 3e: Ignoring age). We obtained similar results using the CSF BMA classifier, and in both biofluids using the ENR classifier instead (data not shown). Together our moderation analyses for APOE genotype, age, sex, and educational attainment demonstrate that the miRNA information used in our BMA classifier was robust to the age of the participant and appropriately accounts for the known genetic risk factor of APOE allele configuration, but its accuracy may be sensitive to some demographic differences, especially sex.

Predictive performance of plasma multi-miRNA models for AD in combination with participant clinical data

We previously showed that AD prediction models are improved by combining CSF miRNAs with Aβ42:t-tau ratio measures into a multi-marker model23–25. Here we comprehensively compared the performance of the BMA plasma all 57 miRNAs model to models containing only weak clinical information (APOE genotype, age, sex, years of education) and models based on stronger CSF protein data (combination of CSF Aβ42, t-tau, p-tau181 measurements) both singly and in combination to identify where combining classifiers may improve diagnostic accuracy. In the discovery cohort the plasma multi-miRNA model (AUC = 0.787) was superior to the weak clinical information (e.g., APOE genotype, AUC = 0.739) (Fig. 4a; Table 2). CSF proteins alone showed stronger performance (AUC = 0.892) than either the clinical information or plasma multi-miRNA model, but there were progressive increases in performance upon addition of miRNAs (AUC = 0.938) and miRNAs + clinical predictors (AUC = 0.958) (Fig. 4a; Table 2). Importantly, the performance of miRNAs + clinical information without CSF proteins was like the CSF proteins alone (AUC = 0.864 vs. 0.892) (Fig. 4a; Table 2), which suggests that plasma miRNA assays combined with clinical information could serve as an alternative to more invasive CSF-based measurements. However, the patterns in the validation cohort were complicated by an unreliability of the clinical information for assessing AD risk (Fig. 4b; Table 2), which likely reflects the small sample size of the validation cohorts. However, the miRNA-based classifier showed stable performance overall across the discovery and validation cohorts, both singly (AUC 0.787 discovery vs. 0.765 validation) and in combination with the CSF proteins (AUC 0.938 discovery vs. 0.867 validation), where approximately half of the total attenuation was explained by the attenuation of CSF protein performance itself (Table 2).

Performance of top two plasma miRNA model as a predictor for AD

Lastly, we sought to determine if a small number of plasma miRNAs could have similar predictive performance to the full plasma model incorporating all 57 miRNAs. To identify the top miRNA candidate biomarkers for AD, we correlated the coefficient signs and magnitude ranks obtained from BMA and ENR. MiR-23a-3p was identified as the top-ranked miRNA with increased expression in AD, and miR-423-5p as the top-ranked miRNA with decreased expression (Fig. 5a). These miRNAs were also the only miRNAs appearing in the highest-probability model identified by BMA in the plasma cohort (Fig. 2b). Moreover, the posterior distribution of model size obtained from BMA (Supp Fig. 7) showed that most of the probability is concentrated on models between 2 and 5 miRNAs in size. We examined the expression ratio of miR-23a-3p to miR-423-5p because their opposite direction of association with AD would drive the ratio toward greater values similar to the CSF Aβ42:t-tau ratio. This parsimonious model showed AD predictive performance in the discovery cohort (Fig. 5b, AUC = 0.771) similar to using information from all 57 miRNAs (AUC = 0.787) and that was maintained in the validation (Fig. 5b, AUC = 0.743). We also performed a sensitivity analysis of the top two miRNA ratio model, full BMA classifier, or CSF Aβ42:t-tau ratio for discriminating clinical changes in MMSE score. The two miRNA ratio (ΔMMSE = − 1.5 [− 2.3, − 0.7] p < 0.001), full miRNA model (ΔMMSE = − 2.3 [− 3.5, − 1.1] p < 0.001), and CSF Aβ42:t-tau ratio (ΔMMSE = − 2.9 [− 3.9, − 1.9], p < 0.001) all predicted a drop in MMSE scores (data not shown). The change in MMSE score was somewhat less sensitive to the two miRNA ratio compared to either the full miRNA model or the CSF Aβ42:t-tau ratio; thereby, reflecting somewhat less discriminating power for clinical changes in cognitive function. Nevertheless, these results demonstrate that an assay consisting of only two plasma miRNAs classified AD diagnosis nearly as accurately as a full panel of miRNAs and remained informative about disease stage as reflected by cognitive scores.

Discussion

Developing effective and early treatment strategies for AD relies on the ability to diagnose individuals during the prodromal stage where therapies would be most effective. In addition, biomarkers that are sensitive to AD progression would be useful for monitoring the efficacy of therapeutic interventions66. In line with this, our previous studies demonstrated the use of CSF multi-miRNA models to classify AD vs. MCI vs. NC, showing that these models were sensitive to changes in MMSE score23–25. While these studies demonstrated the potential for CSF miRNAs to serve as biomarkers for AD, the use of a more readily accessible biofluid such as blood increases patient accessibility and is more economically feasible66. Thus, here we determined whether plasma miRNAs have the same potential as CSF miRNAs to serve as AD biomarkers by examining multi-miRNA model performance in donor- and date-matched CSF and plasma samples from AD and NC participants.

Our data demonstrates that plasma is an optimal biofluid for miRNA RT-qPCR assays vs. CSF. From a technical standpoint, the plasma miRNAs had better QC performance, higher censoring threshold, and more reliable technical replicates (Supp Fig. 4). Regarding the multi-miRNA models, plasma had better concordance between BMA and ENR predictions (plasma AUC = 0.96 vs. CSF AUC = 0.87; data not shown) and was more robust to missing or inaccurate age (Fig. 3e; CSF data not shown). Also, a much larger proportion of miRNAs were included among the ranks of informative predictors for plasma than for CSF (Fig. 2a, b), and the top miRNAs were better resolved in plasma than in CSF (Fig. 2c, d). We also used two independent machine learning methods to show that combinations of plasma or CSF miRNAs have comparable classification performance in the discovery cohort but importantly that performance in the validation cohort was only maintained for the plasma models (Fig. 1b, Supp Fig. 5). In an independent study, we examined the longitudinal stability of miRNAs in plasma, which is important for development of a miRNA biomarker, and found that ~ 50% of the AD CSF miRNAs were stable in plasma isolated biweekly from healthy participants over a 3-month period27. These findings demonstrate that plasma carries a useful set of miRNA information for developing robust models for AD that perform across cohorts.

In line with two prior studies that used donor-matched CSF and plasma28,36, we found that there was little correlation in the effects of miRNAs in AD CSF vs. AD plasma (Fig. 1a). For example, miR-223-3p and miR-423-5p had the greatest positive and negative effect for AD in plasma and a near zero AD association coefficient when measured in CSF. While miR-30b-5p and miR-185-5p had the greatest positive and negative effect for AD in CSF and negligible effect for AD in plasma (Fig. 1a, Supp Table 2). These data suggest that AD-related changes in the miRNA expression for each biofluid may be mediated through different biological pathways. In line with this, a published literature review, reported that out of the 137 miRNAs differentially expressed in AD blood and 166 miRNAs in AD CSF only three miRNAs (miR-125b-5p, 146a, 135a) are in common31. In our study, we identified 16 miRNAs as being most important to CSF models (Fig. 2c) and seven miRNAs as most important to plasma models (Fig. 2d) with an overlap in four miRNAs (miR-101-3p, 125b-5p, 22-3p, 92b-3p). In line with the aforementioned review31, miR-125b-5p achieved the highest overall rank in both CSF and plasma (Fig. 2), albeit with decreases in CSF expression and increases in plasma expression being associated with AD (Supp Table 2). Together, these results demonstrate that candidate miRNA biomarkers for AD are generally biofluid dependent.

The seven miRNAs that contribute most to the AD classification models (miR-101-3p, 125b-5p, 22-3p, 23a-3p, 223-3p, 423-5p, and 92b-3p) have all been previously implicated as altered in humans with AD28,49,51,53,67–72 with miR-125b-5p, 23a-3p, 223-3p, 423-5p, and 92b-3p being differentially expressed in AD brain13,73,74 and miR-125b-5p, 22-3p, 23a-3p, 223-3p, and 423-5p in AD plasma or plasma extracellular vesicles51,67,68,75. Of these, miR-23a-3p and miR-423-5p were our top upregulated and downregulated miRNAs in plasma, respectively (Fig. 5a). We also showed that the ratio of these two miRNAs had a similar AUC for AD, as compared to models with all 57 miRNAs (Fig. 5b). In prior studies, miR-23a-3p was reported as increased in neuron-derived EVs isolated from AD plasma and the hippocampus of late onset AD donors74,75. Also, in line with our results, miR-423-5p was previously reported as decreased in the frontal lobe white matter from AD donors76. Furthermore, a multi-marker model containing miR-423-5p, miR-199a-5p, miR-199b-5p, and miR-3184-5p as well as the long non-coding RNAs NEAT1, XIST, and KCNQ1OT1 were identified as a candidate biomarker for the cross-talk between AD and diabetes mellitus45. MiR-423-5p levels have also been shown to significantly correlate with both APOE−4 status in CSF and donor age in plasma EVs and CSF77,78. In contrast to our results and these prior studies74–76, other human AD studies have reported a decrease in miR-23a-3p, increase in miR-423-5p or no change in expression of either miRNA in plasma, plasma EVs, serum, and/or CSF36,37,67,79,80. These differences may be attributed to methods used to quantify miRNA expression, Cq normalization, and analysis methods. Furthermore, the composition of the cohorts used for biomarker discovery and validation studies may impact the net effect of fold changes considering that miRNA expression levels can be affected by other factors such disease severity, genetics, sex, age, and educational status.

We also examined the generalizability of the plasma miRNA models across populations regardless of APOE−4 status, age, sex, and educational attainment. We performed moderation analyses for these factors based on their established relevance to AD. The greatest risk factors for AD include APOE-4 genotype, age, and sex2,65. Age is the primary risk factor for developing AD, with an approximate doubling of AD prevalence every five years beginning at age 6564,65. Of the 6.9 million people aged 65 and older with AD, ~ 60% are females2. The increased incidence in females has been suggested to be in part due to increased life expectancy in women. However, a reduction in the reproductive period for females has been related to increased dementia risk81. In line with the impact of these factors on AD, we previously showed that miRNA expression in human CSF extracellular vesicles is altered by AD, APOE−4 status, and sex40, in agreement with other reports of APOE−4 and sex-dependent effects on miRNA expression82.

In our moderation analyses, we showed that neither APOE−4 status nor age impact the prediction accuracy of the plasma miRNA models (Fig. 3a, b). However, sex was a potential moderator of AD classification accuracy, with an ~ 10-point AUC discrepancy in performance between males and females (Fig. 3c, p = 0.1). In further investigation, we showed that AD prediction models trained in a single sex were not reproducible (AUC = ~ 0.50) in the validation cohort (Supp Fig. 6). Based on these results we believe that sex does not confound our AD prediction models, but it will require studies with larger sample numbers to fully elucidate the impact of sex. With respect to educational attainment our data suggests a potential endogeneity bias in the current cohort. That is, the miRNAs did not correlate with education, and their prediction of AD was independent of education level, but the errors of prediction were larger for individuals with more education (Fig. 3d), who tend to show less AD prevalence in our cohort (Table 1). This pattern could arise if individuals with more years of education were less likely to receive an AD diagnosis, despite an AD-like plasma miRNA signature. This is plausible given that educational attainment is a component of cognitive reserve, which could delay memory loss despite the presence of AD pathology83. We believe that a useful accounting of the relationship of education to AD risk will require long-duration cohort studies free of selection bias. Altogether, we did not find strong evidence of lack of generalizability of miRNA-based AD risk prediction across key subpopulations, suggesting that some combination of plasma miRNA expression measurements may serve as a general use classifier for predicting AD pathology. However, one limitation to the current study is that the cohort did not contain a sufficient representation of the five racial groups to perform a moderation analysis across race. Another limitation is that we did not include an analysis for associations between the plasma miRNAs and co-morbidities that are prevalent in aging population and relevant to AD, such as cardiovascular disease. Furthermore, we did not assess the specificity of the plasma multi-miRNA models for AD vs. other neurodegenerative diseases and/or AD related dementias. To this end, we are currently assessing the performance of the plasma multi-miRNA models for AD vs. Parkinson’s disease and dementia with Lewy bodies. Future large-scale studies that include participant samples from different racial groups are also warranted to establish the translatability across race.

We also compared the performance of our BMA plasma miRNA model to that of models containing only clinical information or CSF Aβ42:t-tau (Fig. 4; Table 2). Importantly, the miRNA-based classifier showed stable performance across the discovery and validation cohorts both alone and in combination with the CSF proteins. Moreover, the boost in performance from adding miRNAs to CSF proteins remained broadly consistent for both cohorts, suggesting that the miRNAs are adding important information for the prediction of AD to known clinical biomarkers. However, it is important to note that the discovery view of the performance of the “strong” models (e.g., CSF Aβ42:t-tau) is almost certainly overly optimistic because all of them attenuated considerably in the validation. However, the plasma miRNAs alone showed the least attenuation, indicating a general robustness that, when combined with the large advantage in ease of sampling and measurement of plasma miRNAs, depicts their practical promise for AD risk prediction.

We also identified a parsimonious model consisting of the ratio of the top two plasma miRNAs (miR-23a-3p, miR-423-5p) that performs nearly as well as the full BMA model with 57 miRNAs (Fig. 5b). This top two ratio model was sensitive to changes in MMSE score, albeit to a lesser degree than the full BMA plasma model and CSF Aβ42:t-tau ratio. A strength of this model is the opposite-sign relationship between the two predictors, allowing the miRNAs to form a sensitive ratio due to the inverse correlated movement of the numerator and denominator. It is also likely that the performance of this miRNA model may be improved by including additional miRNAs. In support of this hypothesis, a recent study used ENR to identify a model consisting of six miRNAs that classified MCI cases based on the Amyloid Tau Neurodegeneration (ATN) framework84. And another study with a limited number of participants with AD, MCI with probably early AD, and controls identified a set of six miRNAs in plasma as candidate miRNA biomarkers for AD51. Indeed, our own BMA analysis suggested that four or five informative miRNAs may be an optimal number for prediction purposes (Supp Fig. 7). Also, future studies that assess a parsimonious plasma miRNA model in combination with plasma p-tau217 to Aβ42 ratio measurements9 may identify an optimal blood-based assay with boosted performance compared to either stand-alone measure. A plasma assay that combines miRNAs, tau, Aβ42 information may provide great utility for monitoring drug response in clinical trials since many Phase 2 or 3 drugs target neurotransmitter receptors, inflammation, synaptic plasticity/neuroprotection, metabolism/bioenergetics, proteostasis/proteinopathies, neurogenesis, growth factors/hormones, oxidative stress, vasculature, APOE/lipids, circadian rhythm, and gut-brain axis5. In line with this, a target prediction and Ingenuity Pathway analysis using miR-23a-3p and miR-423-5p identified that the top 25 diseases or functions within the Neurological Diseases category that are likely to be affected by the miRNAs include cognitive impairment, dementia, tauopathy, AD, and degenerative dementia (Supp Table 4). Also, the top 10 Canonical Pathways with a significant overrepresentation of mRNA targets to miR-23a-3p and/or miR-423-5p include G-protein coupled receptor signaling, neurexins and neuroligins, synaptogenesis, apoptosis, and lipid metabolism by PPARα (Supp Table 4). In other studies, miR-23a-3p was shown to directly regulate the expression of SNAP-25, a protein crucial for synaptic transmission and neurotransmitter release that implicated in AD85,86. MiR-423-5p is an autophagy associated miRNA, proposed as a plasma EV biomarker for brain aging77.

In conclusion, our data from donor- and date-matched CSF and plasma demonstrates that plasma miRNAs have the same potential as CSF miRNAs to serve in multi-miRNA models for AD prediction. Importantly, we used two independent machine learning methods in a discovery (80% of samples) and validation (20% of samples) to demonstrate that (1) miRNAs which contribute the most to predictive models are biofluid dependent and (2) that multi-miRNA models using plasma miRNA data are more robust to validation compared to using CSF miRNA data. Herein, we split the ADNI 2 samples into discovery and validation cohorts based on a prioritization of statistical power. Requirements for the discovery cohort was sufficient group size to allow for recovery of actionable differences, and the validation cohort was sufficient size to capture a representative span of population variance (Supp Table 3). Using these criteria, in the discovery cohort the AUC confidence intervals allowed reasonably fine discrimination of relative performance among competing classifiers and despite the small size, the validation cohort could mostly resolve an AUC = 0.75 (weakest classifier) from the reference value of 0.50. However, future studies in a larger, more diverse, independent cohort of plasma samples are needed to assess the performance of multi-miRNA models both alone and in combination with plasma p-tau217 to Aβ42 ratio measurements. Furthermore, studies aimed to identify the miRNAs that contribute to predictive models to diagnose AD vs. AD related dementias and to track disease progression and therapeutic response are warranted. However, prior to clinical integration of novel blood-based assays the Global CEO Initiative on Alzheimer’s Disease BBM (blood-based biomarkers) Workgroup recommends the development of evidence-based guidelines, training for health-care professionals, patient education and outreach, and biomarker performance in community-based studies87.

Methods

Participant samples

Participant samples and data were obtained from ADNI 288, an ongoing large multicenter project comprising over 50 research sites across North America. Data used in the preparation of this article were obtained from the ADNI database (adni.loni.usc.edu). The ADNI was launched in 2003 as a public-private partnership, led by Principal Investigator Michael W. Weiner, MD. The primary goal of ADNI has been to test whether serial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET), other biological markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessment can be combined to measure the progression of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and early Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Detailed information about ADNI, screening protocols, and visit procedures can be found at http://adni-info.org/.

For this study, we obtained donor- and date-matched CSF and plasma samples from 80 NC and 80 AD; all participants provided informed consent. The participants for this study were scored using the expanded ADNI 2 Summary of Key Eligibility Criteria, inclusion/exclusion criteria as follows: Cognitively Normal: MMSE scores between 24 and 30 (inclusive), a CDR of 0, non-depressed, non-MCI, and non-demented, education adjusted scores on delayed recall of one paragraph from Wechsler memory Scale Logical Memory II (≥ 9 for 16 or more years of education, ≥ 5 for 8–15 years of education, ≥ 3 for 0–7 years of education); Significant Memory Concern: MMSE scores between 24 and 30 (inclusive), a significant subjective memory concern reported by subject, informant, or clinician, CCI score ≥ 16 (based on first 12 questions), a CDR of 0, non-depressed, non-MCI, and non-demented, education adjusted scores on delayed recall of one paragraph from Wechsler Memory Scale Logical Memory II (≥ 9 for 16 or more years of education, ≥ 5 for 8–15 years of education, ≥ 3 for 0–7 years of education); Early MCI: MMSE scores between 24 and 30 (inclusive), a subjective memory concern reported by subject, informant, or clinician, objective memory loss measured by education adjusted scores on delayed recall of one paragraph from Wechsler Memory Scale Logical Memory II (≥ 16 years: 9–11; 8–15 years: 5–9; 0–7 years: 3–6), a CDR of 0.5, absence of significant levels of impairment in other cognitive domains, essentially preserved activities of daily living, and an absence of dementia; Late MCI: MMSE scores between 24 and 30 (inclusive), a subjective memory concern reported by subject, informant, or clinician, objective memory loss measured by education adjusted scores on delayed recall of one paragraph from Wechsler Memory Scale Logical Memory II (≥ 16 years: ≤8; 8–15 years: ≤4; 0–7 years: ≤2), a CDR of 0.5, absence of significant levels of impairment in other cognitive domains, essentially preserved activities of daily living, and an absence of dementia; Mild AD: MMSE scores between 20 and 26 (inclusive), a CDR of 0.5 or 1.0, and meets NINCDS/ADRDA criteria for probable AD89. All studies were approved by the OHSU Institutional Review Board Committee, IRB #00009707. We certify that the study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards as laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Participant cohort and data

We obtained 320 donor- and date-matched CSF and plasma samples from 160 participants enrolled in the ADNI 2 cohort (https://adni.loni.usc.edu/) on 08.23.2023, with approximate joint balancing of AD diagnosis status and self-reported sex. Study variables on all participants included ADNI enrollment and visit dates, biofluid draw dates, date of birth, year of AD diagnosis where applicable, self-reported sex (categorized as female or male only; accepted as a proxy for chromosomal sex designation), self-reported race and ethnicity (“Asian”, “Black”, and “White”, with “Hispanic” reported only among “White”), years of education completed, marital and working status concurrent with the biofluid draw date(s), concurrent cognitive performance per the MMSE, and APOE genotype (from which we determined APOE−4 status). We also obtained Aβ42, t-tau, and p-tau181 protein data on CSF assayed from the same draw from each participant. The platform used to measure CSF Aβ42 peptides in the ADNI 2 cohort was the xMAP AlzBio3 from Fujirebio90 which quantifies AD biomarkers in human CSF samples. It utilizes xMAP technology for multiplex analysis, allowing for the simultaneous detection of Aβ42, t-tau, and p-tau181. Note that we imputed t-tau values for three participants who were missing these measurements, using values from a linear regression model of t-tau as predicted by p-tau181 and additional clinical information (APOE-4 status, age, sex, MMSE, and AD diagnosis). In sensitivity analyses of AD prediction models comparing use of the imputed values (replacement of missing cells with values based on information from the observed portion of the data set; thus, no information was discarded) to listwise deletion (complete removal of a record with any missing values), we found that the models had similar operating characteristics either way, with no appreciable difference in the results, thus we used the imputed values in all subsequent analyses.

RNA isolation

We first compared three miRNA isolation kits for biofluids to identify the kit that would perform best for both CSF and plasma. We tested the MagMax MirVana kit, the Qiagen miRNeasy kit, and the Norgen Urine miRNA total RNA isolation kit. We found that the Norgen Urine miRNA kit yielded the most miRNAs with the lowest average Cq when used with an input volume of 250 µL and provided the most comparable results with plasma modestly outperforming CSF. Thus, total RNA was isolated from 250 µL of CSF and plasma using the Urine miRNA Purification Kit (29000, Norgen, Ontario, Canada) following the manufacturer’s protocol. We eluted the plasma in 50 µL and the CSF in 30 µL, as these amounts were shown to adequately equalize the RNA concentrations from each biofluid. Prior to RNA isolation, participant samples were de-identified and randomized into batches. Each RNA isolation batch consisted of matched CSF and plasma samples from 6 donors (12 samples total). Two batches of RNA were isolated per day for a total of 27 batch groups needed to run 320 participant samples. To mitigate within-participant RNA isolation and RT-qPCR batch effects, matched CSF and plasma samples from the same donor were processed for RT-qPCR in the same batch and on the same day. Following the addition of lysis buffer to the sample, an exogenous cel-miR-39-3p spike-in control (Thermo Fisher Scientific (TFS), Waltham, MA) was added at a final concentration of 3 pM. RNA was eluted with 30 µL (CSF) and 50 µL (plasma) of nuclease free water. The miRNA concentration of each sample was measured using the Qubit miRNA Assay kit (TFS) and read using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (TFS).

MiRNA RT-qPCR profiling

MiRNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using the TaqMan™ Advanced miRNA cDNA Synthesis kit (A28007, TFS). Briefly, 3 µL of total RNA was 3’ poly-adenylated, followed by a 5’ adaptor ligation step and reverse transcription. The resulting cDNA (5 µL) was added to a 14-cycle universal miR-amplification step. The miR-amplification reaction was diluted 1:10 with nuclease free water, then 220 µL of the diluted cDNA was mixed with 220 µL of nuclease free water and 440 µL of TaqMan™ Fast Advanced master mix (4444556, TFS). The mix (100 µL) was loaded into each of the eight ports of a TaqMan™ Advanced Human miRNA Custom Card (TFS). Each card was assayed using a QuantStudio™ 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (4485695, TFS) with the aid of an Orbitor RS2 Microplate Mover (ORB2001, TFS) to increase throughput and ensure each participant’s samples were assayed on the same day. Additionally, the RT-qPCR assays included two no-template controls: one water + spike-in and one water + spike-in without reverse transcriptase, to identify any assay(s) with spurious amplification (Supp Table 1C). The RT-qPCR data generated for each participant and the no template control data was imported into ExpressionSuite Software version 1.3 (TFS) for automatic baselining and thresholding, with maximum Cq set to 40 cycles. Then each sample’s data was exported from ExpressionSuite as an individual data file. There were 320 data files in total: 160 for CSF and 160 for plasma. All expression data was subsequently analyzed using Stata version 18.0 software.

MiRNA RT-qPCR quality control metrics

For each RT-qPCR well, we examined a variety of QC metrics exported by ExpressionSuite software. A well was considered adequately measured if the AmpScore was ≥ 1 and the CqConf was ≥ 0.8. Wells that did not meet this standard (or were labeled “Undetermined” by QuantStudio) were considered missing measurements, regardless of Cq value. Wells with acceptable QC but Cq > 34 (in CSF) or > 35 (in plasma) were considered censored values and given special handling using censoring-aware methods in the analysis (see Normalization below). For noncensored wells we examined the appropriateness of the baseline separation from the recorded Cq value and compared our QC assessments with other qualitative flags exported by QuantStudio, finding nothing unusual. As a final fidelity check we (1) examined the wells for two negative control miRNAs (miR-499a-3p and miR-647) to verify that no sample had a Cq value recorded for either of those with acceptable QC and (2) verified that the spike-in positive control (cel-miR-39-3p) and primary endogenous normalization reference control (miR-16-5p) were both completely observed with acceptable QC in all wells for all samples. The other endogenous normalizer control miRNAs (miR-24-3p, miR-92a-3p, miR-204-5p) showed excellent performance in both biofluids, with complete observation (meeting acceptable QC) for miR-24-3p and near-complete observation for miR-92a-3p (complete in plasma, 8% dropout in CSF) and miR-204-5p (4% dropout in plasma, 1% dropout in CSF). Given our built-in redundancy of normalizer control miRNAs and model-based methods of normalization that can handle noninformative missing data (see Normalization below), we considered this small amount of normalizer measurement dropout acceptable.

Excluded samples

After completing the RT-qPCR, we identified samples that did not perform well. There were three distinct subsets of aberrant measurement profiles: (1) low miRNA yield associated with large or missing Cq values across the entire array (n = 9; 2.8%); (2) an abnormal Cq distribution shift away from the cohort center of all miRNAs that was inconsistent with the spike-in measurements and suggested RNA concentration irregularities that occurred prior to the spike-in (n = 17; 5.3%); and (3) large Cq values for spike-in wells only with no corresponding shift in the Cq values for the human miRNAs assayed (n = 11; 3.4%). We defined low yield formally as a measurement profile containing > 15% missing values (approximately the 97th percentile of missing value burden). We defined anomalous Cq distribution shift as a global median divergence > 3.3 (spike-in) standard deviations separated from the spike-in median for the sample. Spike-in outliers were defined as samples having a large residual and high leverage score in a regression of the spike-in mean on the RT-qPCR run order. The technical definition was a studentized residual > 2 in magnitude coupled with a DFITS outlier-detection value > 2 x sqrt(1/N), the conventional rule-of-thumb cutoff, where N = 320 is the sample size. Other processing events that sometimes coincided with sample exclusion were visible evidence of hemolysis or technical failures. Altogether, a total of 32 (10%) samples displaying one or more of the three types of aberrant profile were excluded for a final count of 288 samples in the study.

Cq calibration

For technical calibration, all raw Cq values were adjusted for RNA isolation differences via median-alignment of the cel‑miR‑39‑3p spike‑in control reactions as an exogenous calibration factor. This adjustment represented the largest contribution to the overall normalization, with ~ 60% of the total technical variance explained by differences in spike-in control values. After technical calibration, all Cq values for the cel‑miR‑39‑3p spike-in (being exactly aligned by median) were removed from the dataset and did not contribute to later normalization steps. For further endogenous calibration of Cq distributions across samples we identified 17 miRNAs in CSF and 53 in plasma that were always present with acceptable QC in 100% of the samples and used these to align the Cq distributions across all donors. For each biofluid, we calculated the median over the per-biofluid always-observed miRNAs and modeled both biofluid medians as a function of a 7-knot restricted cubic spline of the RT-qPCR run order using a linear mixed-effects regression model including random intercepts per donor. Residuals from this model were subtracted from the corresponding Cq values for all miRNAs to align the Cq distributions across all donors. Note that this calibration strategy preserves biofluid differences and time drift for later correction via downstream normalization procedures.

Batch calibration

Batch calibration was performed to remove residual technical artifacts in the Cq measurements attributable to processing order (i.e., the run sequence of samples in the RT-qPCR assays). This step operates on the values resulting from the previous step (where isolation and inherent concentration differences were adjusted for using the spike-in and always-observed miRNA median). For the RNA isolation, each individual donor’s CSF and plasma were isolated on the same day in groups of 12 to minimize any potential isolation batch effects. For the RT-qPCR assays, each paired biofluid RNA (CSF and plasma from same donor) was run on the same day to minimize any potential batch effects. Each array card contained two CSF donor samples, or two plasma donor samples. The biofluids were run separately on array cards to ensure that automatic threshold and baselining was correct for each biofluid, given that plasma contains higher levels of RNA than CSF. Since the RNA isolations and RT-qPCR runs for both biofluids were done in parallel (both participant samples always paired in the same processing batch) order-related processing effects (e.g., time drift of measurements) are expected to affect both biofluids similarly. To account for these, biofluid differences were subtracted out using an absorbing linear regression, which enforced a grand mean of zero across the Cq distribution. We then fit a linear mixed-effects regression model including the same 7-knot restricted cubic spline of the RT-qPCR run order as above, interacted with a biofluid selector to allow for biofluid-specific adjustments to the fit, and additionally including indicators for known abnormal events or conditions noted during processing, with isolation batches entered as random intercepts. Fitted values from this regression were subtracted from the corresponding Cq values for all miRNAs as the adjustment for batch and order-related effects. Batch calibration adjustments were relatively minor for our dataset, accounting for only roughly 2% of the total process variance.

Normalization

Following the Cq Calibration and Batch Calibration steps above, we performed a Cq normalization using a mixed-effects model on the endogenous miRNA normalizer Cq values only. The model included quadratic effects of age (interaction effect of age with itself, to model the effect of differing ages rather than assuming the effect is linear for all ages), fully-interacted indicators for sex and AD diagnosis to adjust out study factors, and modeling the miRNA-sample interaction using crossed random effects. We used this model to estimate the residual differences between different samples (irrespective of biofluid) as BLUPs (best linear unbiased predictions); the BLUP values were subtracted from the fully calibrated Cq values as endogenous normalizer level effects across samples, normalizing the Cq values to those levels. Note that the two biofluids are also normalized by this step, so that miRNA expression is now relative to the normalizer specific to each biofluid. The resulting normalized Cq values were then recentered to the global median of the input Cq value distribution. This final normalization step was the second-largest contributor to the calibration and normalization adjustments, accounting for approximately 10% of the nuisance variance in the data. After normalization, > 70% of the total Cq variance was attributable to differences in mean expression across miRNAs (expected variance), 3% was attributable to sample-miRNA interactions (the biological signal), and < 1% to differences in mean expression across samples (meaning the normalization was highly successful); the remainder of ~ 25% is residual variance (the measurement noise). We verified that the normalized Cq values of the normalizer miRNAs themselves had essentially flat profiles and noted that the normalization achieved a substantial amount of variance reduction: 87% of the variance in median Cq values across samples was removed in CSF, and 93% in plasma.

Cq censoring and imputation

Censored Cq values (see MiRNA RT-qPCR Quality Control Metrics above) are viewed as quantitatively unreliable because their measurement falls outside the linear range of the assay. These were treated as interval observations, defined as an unobserved value falling between an upper and a lower limit. For the purposes of regression on the Cq values as an outcome predicted by AD status these can be handled as interval observations directly via modeling (see Statistical Analysis of Individual MiRNAs below). However, when the Cq values are used as predictors of AD status (see Statistical Modeling of AD Classifiers) the interval observation must be collapsed to a single most-likely value falling within the interval (because each miRNA is given only a single predictor weight). To accomplish this, we used a Bayesian multilevel interval regression model that considers all replicate wells for the sample jointly and naturally accounts for the different character of censored values in the context of non-censored values for the same miRNA when making predictions for AD. For the Bayesian prior distribution on the biofluid-specific mean level for the miRNA we used a Student’s t distribution with 4 degrees of freedom and a (wide and uninformative) scale of 10, and for the log of the root mean square error we used a (similarly uninformative) half-Cauchy distribution with the same scale of 10. Random effects for individual samples were assigned a zero-centered prior normal distribution with default conjugate inverse-gamma hyperprior (shape and scale parameters both 1/100) for the random-effect variance. The imputed value for a censored observation was taken as the posterior biofluid-specific grand mean for the miRNA plus the posterior mean of the random effect, based on 10,000 Markov chain Monte Carlo draws from the posterior distribution, following a burn-in run of 2500 draws. Note that the rank correlation between the predictions from this model and the naïve means of the corresponding replicate wells was 0.99, reflecting almost complete preservation of rank ordering among samples, but the model prediction is less biased because it accounts for censoring, which prevents it from overestimating the expression level. This bias correction was very necessary for CSF, where censoring was common, but less so for plasma, which had small amounts of censoring.

Prior to imputation the normalized Cq values, both censored and uncensored, are converted to an expression scale using inverted Cq units so that positive values mark higher expression. Non-censored Cq values with acceptable QC were used as exact measurements (both bounds of the interval are the same normalized Cq value). Censored Cq values with acceptable QC were treated as falling between the biofluid-specific censoring threshold (lower bound) and the average (upper bound) of the normalized Cq value and the maximum normalized Cq value across all observations on all miRNAs (the “observed maximum”, a value that will be strictly less than the maximum cycle number of 40, specified as a machine setting). Missing Cq values were treated as falling between the observed maximum (lower bound) and the maximum cycle number (upper bound of 40). Non-missing Cq values with unacceptable QC were treated as missing measurements for most purposes, but in this context the recorded Cq (with all normalization adjustments) was used as the lower bound and the maximum cycle number (40) as the upper bound. Finally, all the interval bounds were subtracted from the maximum cycle number of 40 to invert the scale.

Statistical analysis of individual miRNAs

Individual miRNAs were assessed one by one for association with AD in CSF and plasma separately using the same multilevel model specification. All models considered the miRNA expression value as an outcome predicted by AD diagnosis status, adjusted for age and sex and used the Huber–White heteroskedasticity-robust standard error estimator, with a random effect of sample to account for correlation among the replicate wells for the same sample. For miRNAs having any censored values in the biofluid we adopted a multilevel interval regression model (that accounts for censored and missing Cq values) incorporating both the lower and upper bounds of expression for the censored values, and for miRNAs with no censored values we used an ordinary linear mixed-effects model instead. The statistic of interest was the coefficient on the AD diagnosis status term, which represents the log2 fold change in the miRNA expression associated with AD vs. NC status, adjusted for age and sex.

Statistical modeling of AD classifiers with discovery and validation cohorts

Prior to predictive modeling we divided each biofluid into discovery and validation cohorts. The discovery cohort comprising 80% of the samples was used as a “training set” to optimize an in-sample prediction model based on the miRNA profile information. The validation cohort comprising the remaining 20% of the samples was used as an independent out-of-sample set for rigorous, transparent, and reliable testing of the prediction model. The division of the study cohort was random within biofluid types and balanced by AD diagnosis status so that the sample prevalence of AD was the same for each biofluid. The same discovery and validation splits were fit to each prediction model and used to assess its in-sample and out-of-sample performance. This is to ensure that all models are trained on the same information and tested against the same standard, allowing direct comparisons between performance metrics.

We next used two prediction modeling strategies to develop AD classifiers based on all available miRNA profile information in each biofluid: BMA and ENR that are examples of regularized regression strategies designed to maximize out-of-sample predictive accuracy while minimizing model size. BMA uses shrinkage of variable importance via prior constraints on the space of possible solutions, in terms of model size and weighting of the covariance between predictors. ENR uses cross-validation techniques to optimize penalty parameters that select or deselect predictors in either a harder or softer way depending on the optimal penalty configuration. Both approaches allow for straightforward ranking of variables according to their importance for predicting the outcome and can generate a predictive index that collapses all the miRNA information down to a single standalone score. We assessed performance of the model on a particular cohort by evaluating the AUC under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve of the predictive index when applied to the miRNA profiles in the cohort. In both modeling approaches we forced age and sex into all candidate models as adjustment terms.

Our implementation of BMA was a linear probability model for AD status using a beta-binomial prior on the model size with mean 4 (miRNA predictors), a Zellner g-prior on the coefficient matrix with g = ~ 6.85, and noninformative wide priors for the model constant (AD prevalence) and residual variance. The choice of hyperparameter constants was determined by functions of the available miRNA predictors (n = 57) and the mean sample size of the training split of each biofluid cohort (n = 115). Specifically, g = ~ 6.85 was chosen as the base-2 logarithm of the sample size, and the mean model size was chosen as 1 + floor(2 x log(57) – log(115)), reflecting a preference for increasing model size at roughly the rate of the square root of sample size (the “information”). The Markov chain Monte Carlo sampled 1 million draws of the model posterior (each draw comprising a candidate model) following a burn-in of 20,000 draws. Quantities from the BMA solution were the posterior mean coefficients for each miRNA predictor in each sampled model and the PIP (a variable importance measure) for all miRNA predictors based on their representation in the posterior distribution. Finally, posterior estimates of most-probable model sizes (i.e., how many miRNAs were typically needed for good predictions) were obtained for selecting priority candidate models.

Our implementation of ENR used a generalized linear model with logit link for predicting binary AD diagnosis status, considering a grid of ridge-LASSO mixing parameter values spanning 0 (pure ridge regression), 0.001, 0.01, and 0.1, using 10-fold cross-validation to select optimal LASSO penalties for each mixing parameter value and selecting the combination of parameters corresponding to the absolute minimum cross-validation deviance criterion across all combinations. Predictor importance for each miRNA was defined as the absolute magnitude rank of the miRNA under the ridge (mixing parameter = 0) solution, and coefficient values for comparing with BMA posterior estimates were taken from the ridge solution as well, but note that the final predictive index may come from a different solution.

Variance of predictive performance across demographic subpopulations

To assess the generalizability of the miRNA-based AD classifiers across various demographic subpopulations, we evaluated the predictive performance in key subsets of the cohort: APOE−4 + vs. APOE−4-, older vs. younger, males vs. females, and lower vs. higher educational attainment. For each factor we used probit regression to adjust the distribution of the predicted probabilities of AD among NC individuals for all other factors (to align the baseline risk across subgroups) and then tested for moderation of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve by the factor of interest. The power of the subgroup divisions to explain variance in the predicted probabilities of AD across individuals in the cohort was scored in terms of improved model fit of classifier accuracy to the predicted probabilities. If the AUC in each subgroup induced by levels of the dividing factor is substantively similar to the AUC in the full cohort (where the dividing factor is ignored) then the factor has low explanatory power and model fit is not worse by ignoring it. However, if the AUC differs by a salient amount, then the factor has enough explanatory power that model fit is better when the factor is accounted for. For educational attainment, we additionally considered the association of years of education to (1) the AD diagnosis status itself, (2) the ratio of Aβ42 to t-tau) in CSF, and (3) the predicted probability of AD calculated from the miRNA-based BMA classifier, in order to probe the sensitivity of AD markers to large changes in educational attainment. We used a structural equation modeling framework to simultaneously adjust all classifiers to the same age and sex background and account for shared information and residual correlations among them.

Robustness of AD classifiers to age adjustment

Since all our models include an age adjustment, we sought to determine whether this adjustment had a major influence on the results. We tested the sensitivity of the AD classifier models to missing or incomplete age information by evaluating two alternative versions of each classifier in each biofluid: one where the original classifier was used for training but all input ages for predictions were assumed to be 75 (the approximate mean age in our cohort), and another where age was left out of the model (and a new classifier trained ignoring age). We assessed the stability of the model in response to these perturbations by comparing the in-sample and out-of-sample predictive performance of each version with the performance of the original model that used accurate age.

Comparisons to clinical predictors and/or CSF protein assays

We compared the predictive performance of the plasma miRNA AD classifier from BMA to that of known or proposed clinical predictors of AD (APOE genotype, age, sex, years of education), and to that of a combination of CSF protein assays (Aβ42, t-tau, p-tau181) at times used in the clinical diagnosis of AD91,92. Additionally, we evaluated the improvements in predictive performance from combining these different clinical classifier types together. We considered and compared all combinations of one or more of these classifiers (i.e. combinations of the BMA prediction, the clinical predictors, and/or the CSF proteins) by both in-sample and out-of-sample AUC.

Selection of a parsimonious “Top two Ratio” model

We selected the two miRNAs (miR-23a-3p and miR-423-5p) appearing in the highest-probability model (in ~ 7% of the cumulative posterior density) identified by BMA in the plasma cohort. A weighted log ratio was formed by summing the normalized expression values (on the Cq = log2 scale) using the corresponding BMA predictor coefficients from the highest-probability plasma miRNA model as weights in the sum; note that this resulted in a log ratio (and not a log product) because the miRNAs had opposite associations with AD (miR-23a-3p positive and miR-423-5p negative). The log ratio was exponentiated (base 2) and rescaled by 100: the “top two” plasma miRNA ratio = 100 × 2 0.21 ∆Cq(miR−23a−3p) – 0.41 ∆Cq(miR−423−5p). The resulting ratio was oriented toward positive association with AD diagnosis status. We compared the predictive performance of this parsimonious model that uses as few predictor variables as possible to the more elaborate classifiers that used all the miRNA information to assess whether it may be competitive. We additionally assessed the ability of the parsimonious model to differentiate changes in clinical MMSE scores, comparing it to that of the predicted probabilities of AD from the full BMA classifier and to that of the Aβ42:t-tau measures in CSF, using a structural equation modeling framework to simultaneously adjust all classifiers to the same age and sex background, and also account for shared information and residual correlations among them.

MiRNA target prediction and pathway analysis

We used TargetScan 7.2 and miRDB to predict targets for miR-23a-3p and miR-423-5p. As pathway analysis is most effective for predictions generated from a limited gene set, predicted targets were excluded if they had a Cumulative Weighted Context Score (CWCS) > −0.3 in TargetScan or a target score of < 80 in miRDB. Pathway analysis was then performed using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA; QIAGEN Inc), excluding cancer-related tissues and cell lines to avoid knowledge bias towards cancer.
