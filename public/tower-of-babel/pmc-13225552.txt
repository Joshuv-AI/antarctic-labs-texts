Predicting depressive symptoms in older adults with hearing impairment development and validation of NHANES-based machine-learning models: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Lunyu Shang, Shuaiyu Jiang, Qi Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Depressive symptoms frequently co-occur with hearing impairment in older adults, yet available prediction tools for this high-risk subgroup remain limited in practicality and applicability. We developed and internally validated prediction models for clinically relevant depressive symptoms among older adults with hearing impairment. We analyzed cross-sectional data from 5 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey cycles (2005–2006, 2009–2010, 2011–2012, 2015–2016, and 2017–2018). Adults aged ≥60 years with hearing impairment were identified using the 2021 World Health Organization criterion (better-ear pure-tone average at 0.5/1/2/4 kHz ≥20 dB). Depressive symptoms were defined as a Patient Health Questionnaire-9 score ≥10. Candidate predictors included sociodemographic factors, comorbidities, lifestyle behaviors, and sensory/functional limitations; missing covariates were handled using multiple imputation. Participants were randomly split into training and validation sets (7:3). Predictors were selected using the Boruta algorithm followed by least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression, yielding 6 predictors (age, poverty–income ratio, self-rated health, sleep disorder, memory difficulty, and physical functional limitation). Logistic regression and 6 machine-learning algorithms were developed. Model performance was assessed by discrimination (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC]), calibration (calibration curves and Brier score), and clinical utility (decision-curve analysis). The analytic sample included 2123 participants, of whom 154 (7.25%) had depressive symptoms. In the validation set, AUCs across models ranged from 0.799 to 0.862. Logistic regression achieved the highest discrimination (AUC 0.862, 95% CI 0.800–0.911) with good calibration (Brier score 0.053) and favorable net benefit across clinically relevant thresholds. A nomogram, a dynamic nomogram, and an online calculator were developed to facilitate individualized risk estimation. Sensitivity analyses showed broadly consistent results, except for attenuated discrimination in the severe hearing-loss subgroup. A parsimonious 6-variable model demonstrated strong discrimination, good calibration, and potential clinical utility for risk stratification and targeted screening among older adults with hearing impairment. External validation and prospective evaluation are warranted before clinical implementation.

1. Introduction

Population aging is accelerating worldwide, making the physical and mental health of older adults a growing public health priority. Hearing impairment is one of the most common sensory deficits in later life and affects a substantial proportion of adults aged ≥60 years.[1–6] In high-income settings, adult-onset hearing loss ranks among the leading causes of years lived with disability.[7] Beyond reduced audibility, hearing impairment can limit communication, social participation, and daily functioning, and is associated with loneliness, functional decline, and poorer quality of life.[6,8,9] These consequences may increase vulnerability to depressive symptoms through reduced social engagement, greater dependence, and worsening overall health status.[9,10]

Depression is prevalent and disabling in older adults and contributes substantially to the global burden of disease.[11] A growing body of epidemiological evidence links hearing impairment to late-life depression across different populations and study designs.[12,13] However, depressive symptoms are often under-recognized in routine care, and communication barriers and comorbidities may further complicate timely detection among older adults with hearing impairment.[13] From a clinical and public health perspective, it is therefore important not only to document the association, but also to identify which individuals within this high-risk subgroup are most likely to experience clinically relevant depressive symptoms and may benefit from targeted screening and early support.

In parallel, prediction models for depressive symptoms and related mental health outcomes have expanded rapidly, driven by large-scale population datasets and advances in machine-learning methods.[14–19] Nevertheless, several limitations remain for prediction in older adults with hearing impairment. Most published models target general older populations or other high-risk groups and only indirectly incorporate hearing status, leaving their transportability to hearing-impaired older adults uncertain. In addition, definitions of hearing impairment and depressive symptoms vary across studies, including self-reported sensory difficulty versus audiometric criteria and different screening scales and thresholds, which undermines comparability and generalization. Many studies still prioritize discrimination, while calibration and decision-analytic utility are less consistently reported, limiting confidence in risk estimates for screening decisions. Finally, hearing-specific, user-friendly tools that support individualized risk stratification in routine care and community screening remain uncommon.

To address these gaps, we used multi-cycle NHANES data to develop and validate prediction models for clinically relevant depressive symptoms among older adults with hearing impairment defined using the 2021 World Health Organization (WHO) audiometric criterion. Depressive symptoms were defined using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 threshold for clinically relevant symptoms. We compared an interpretable logistic regression (LR) model with several machine-learning algorithms, and evaluated model performance comprehensively in terms of discrimination, calibration, and decision-curve net benefit. We further translated the best-performing model into a nomogram and an online risk calculator to facilitate individualized risk estimation. This study aimed to develop and validate prediction models rather than to infer causal effects of predictors.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

This cross-sectional study used data from 5 NHANES cycles (2005–2006, 2009–2010, 2011–2012, 2015–2016, and 2017–2018). These cycles were selected because they included both the depression questionnaire and the audiometry examination, which are required to define depressive symptoms and audiometry-based hearing impairment.

NHANES is an ongoing, nationally representative survey of the U.S. civilian, noninstitutionalized population conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) using a multistage probability sampling design. Data are collected through standardized home interviews and examinations in mobile examination centers under uniform protocols and quality control procedures. The primary aim of this study was to develop and validate prediction models rather than to infer causal effects of predictors. In line with this objective, analyses for model development and validation were conducted without applying NHANES sampling weights. We used only publicly available, de-identified NHANES data.

2.2. Study population

Hearing status was determined using NHANES audiometric examinations. Hearing impairment was defined according to the 2021 WHO criteria[20] as a better-ear pure-tone average (PTA) ≥20 dB at 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 kHz (WHO grade ≥1).

Participants were eligible if they were aged ≥60 years, completed the audiometry examination with sufficient thresholds to calculate the better-ear PTA, and met the WHO 2021 definition of hearing impairment (WHO grade ≥1). Accordingly, we excluded participants who were aged <60 years, had missing or insufficient audiometric data that precluded PTA calculation, had normal hearing (WHO grade = 0), or had missing Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) data. In total, 2123 participants were included, and the participant selection process is summarized in Figure 1. For the small proportion of missing covariate values among included participants, multiple imputation was applied to reduce potential bias from complete-case analysis.

2.3. Outcome variable

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), with each item scored from 0 to 3 (total score: 0–27).[21] The PHQ-9 is a widely used depression screening instrument with established reliability and validity across diverse populations.[22,23] Clinically significant depressive symptoms were defined as a PHQ-9 total score ≥10.[24] The outcome for prediction modeling was binary (presence vs absence of depressive symptoms).

2.4. Predictor variables

Candidate predictors were prespecified based on prior literature and data availability in NHANES, covering sociodemographic characteristics, health behaviors/lifestyle factors, health status and chronic conditions, and other relevant covariates. All predictors were obtained from standardized NHANES questionnaires and examinations.

Sociodemographic variables included age, sex, race/ethnicity, educational attainment, marital status, living alone, and family poverty income ratio (PIR). Health behaviors and lifestyle variables included body mass index, waist circumference, alcohol consumption, smoking status, sedentary behavior, average daily sleep duration, and hearing-aid use. Health status and chronic disease variables included hearing loss severity, hypertension, diabetes, cancer/malignancy, cardiovascular disease, arthritis, hypercholesterolemia, hyperlipidemia, visual impairment, and sleep disorders. Other covariates included self-rated health, physical functional limitations, health insurance coverage, self-reported memory difficulty and tinnitus. Detailed definition criteria for each predictor was shown Supplemental materials.

2.5. Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.2). Continuous variables with approximately normal distributions were summarized as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using independent-samples t-tests (continuous variables) and chi-square tests (categorical variables). A 2-sided P <.05 was considered statistically significant for descriptive comparisons.

2.6. Feature screening and model development

The analytic dataset was randomly split into a training set and a validation set (7:3; n = 1487 and n = 636, respectively). Feature selection was performed in the training set using the Boruta algorithm followed by LASSO regression, and the predictors retained after this sequential procedure were used for subsequent modeling. Using the selected predictors, we developed 7 candidate models, including LR, decision tree (DT), random forest, support vector machine (SVM), artificial neural network (ANN), XGBoost, and LightGBM, with LR as the baseline. For non-LR models, hyperparameters were tuned by grid search with 10-fold cross-validation in the training set.

Model performance was evaluated in the independent validation set in terms of discrimination (ROC/AUC), calibration (calibration curves and Brier score), and clinical utility (decision-curve analysis [DCA]). Classification metrics derived from the confusion matrix (accuracy, precision, specificity, recall, and F1 score) were also reported. The final model was selected based on overall performance and interpretability, and was further translated into a nomogram and an online calculator for individualized risk estimation.

2.7. Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of the final LR model, we performed prespecified sensitivity analyses targeting key analytic choices, including missing-data handling, outcome definition, and hearing impairment severity. Using the same 6 predictors and LR specification as in the main analysis, we repeated model fitting in the training set and evaluated performance in the validation set under 4 alternative settings: complete-case analysis with listwise deletion of missing predictors; defining depressive symptoms as PHQ-9 ≥8; defining depressive symptoms as PHQ-9 ≥12; and restricting the cohort to severe hearing loss (WHO grade ≥2; better-ear PTA ≥35 dB) while keeping PHQ-9 ≥10 as the outcome. Discrimination and overall accuracy were summarized using AUC and Brier score (Supplemental Table 3).

2.8. Ethical approval

NHANES study protocols were approved by the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. All participants provided written informed consent. This study was a secondary analysis of publicly available, de-identified NHANES data and therefore did not require additional institutional review board approval at our institution.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics

The 2123 older adults with hearing impairment were randomly split into a training set (n = 1487) and a validation set (n = 636) (Supplemental Table 1). Depressive symptoms were present in 154 participants (7.25%), with similar prevalences in the training (7.26%) and validation (7.23%) sets (P = .98). Overall baseline characteristics were well balanced between the 2 sets, with a small difference observed only for marital status (P = .006).

Within the training set, participants with depressive symptoms showed a less favorable health profile (Table 1). They were younger and more likely to report poorer general health, memory difficulties, and sleep disorder, and they had higher prevalences of visual impairment and physical functional limitation and a lower PIR (all P <.05). They also differed in alcohol drinking, sleep duration, and sedentary behavior, and had higher prevalences of arthritis, hypertension, tinnitus, and diabetes (all P <.05), whereas living alone did not differ (P = .54). Hearing-loss grade, race/ethnicity, education level, marital status, and hearing-aid use were generally comparable between groups (all P ≥.05).

3.2. Predictive variable screening

To identify a parsimonious and stable set of predictors, feature selection was conducted exclusively in the training set, while the validation set was kept untouched for subsequent performance evaluation. We first applied the Boruta algorithm to screen candidate variables and identified 11 important predictors (Supplemental Fig. 1). These Boruta-confirmed predictors were then entered into a LASSO LR with 10-fold cross-validation. Rather than adopting λ_min or λ_1se, we selected an intermediate penalty value between the 2 to achieve a balance between model parsimony and predictive stability (Fig. 2). This sequential procedure yielded 6 core predictors: age, self-rated health, memory difficulty, sleep disorder, physical functional limitation, and family PIR. Collectively, these predictors represent complementary domains, including overall health and functional status, cognitive and sleep-related problems, socioeconomic position, and aging, and were subsequently used to develop and validate all prediction models.

3.3. Model construction and performance evaluation

Seven models were trained using the 6 core predictors, with non-LR models tuned by 10-fold cross-validation and grid search (Supplemental Table 2). In the validation set, discrimination was good overall (AUC, 0.799–0.862), with LR achieving the highest AUC (Fig. 3; Table 2). All models showed acceptable calibration (Brier score, 0.052–0.064), and LR demonstrated favorable overall calibration (Supplemental Fig. 2). DCA suggested favorable net benefit for ANN, DT, LR, and SVM across a wide range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 4). Considering overall validation performance and interpretability, LR was selected as the final model.

3.4. Sensitivity analyses

Overall findings were robust to alternative analytic specifications (Supplemental Table 3). Using complete-case data yielded similar discrimination to the main analysis (AUC 0.885 vs 0.862). When redefining depressive symptoms using different PHQ-9 cutoffs, discrimination remained acceptable (AUC 0.801 for PHQ-9 ≥8 and 0.823 for PHQ-9 ≥12). In contrast, restricting the sample to severe hearing loss (WHO grade ≥2; PTA ≥35 dB) attenuated discrimination (AUC 0.708), with wider uncertainty due to the reduced sample size and event count.

3.5. Model interpretability

Based on the final LR model, we translated the regression coefficients into a nomogram for individualized risk estimation (Fig. 5). The nomogram assigns points to each of the 6 predictors; the total score corresponds to the linear predictor and the estimated probability of depressive symptoms. To facilitate practical implementation, we further developed an interactive web-based nomogram that automatically outputs the predicted risk after users enter the 6 predictor values (Supplemental Fig. 3). The web tool is available at https://hearing-depression-risk-prediction.shinyapps.io/dynnomapp/.

4. Discussion

Using 5 NHANES cycles (2005–2018) and the 2021 WHO audiometric criteria for hearing impairment, we developed and validated prediction models for clinically relevant depressive symptoms (PHQ-9 ≥10) in adults aged ≥60 years with hearing impairment. In the independent validation set, LR provided the most balanced overall performance, with high discrimination (AUC = 0.862) and good calibration (Brier score = 0.053). We operationalized the final model into a static nomogram, a dynamic web-based nomogram, and an online calculator to facilitate individualized risk estimation. Importantly, this study focused on risk prediction and stratification rather than causal inference; included predictors should therefore be interpreted as markers that improve predictive accuracy, not necessarily as causal determinants of depressive symptoms.

Our findings align with prior evidence linking hearing loss to late-life depressive symptoms across diverse datasets and study designs.[25,26] Beyond confirming an overall association, we highlight substantial heterogeneity in depression risk within a population that already has objectively defined hearing impairment.[9,27,28] This within-subgroup stratification is clinically relevant because it shifts the emphasis from testing population-level associations to identifying individuals who may benefit most from screening and targeted follow-up in hearing-related or geriatric care settings.

4.1. Key predictors and potential mechanisms

By integrating LASSO selection, the Boruta algorithm, and an interpretable LR framework, we identified 6 stable predictors spanning sociodemographic, health, functional, behavioral, and socioeconomic domains. Several predictors – poorer self-rated health, functional limitation, sleep disorder and memory difficulty – cluster around global vulnerability and diminished reserve, consistent with a biopsychosocial view of late-life depression.[29] Lower income (PIR) further indicate reduced social and material support, which may amplify stress exposure and barriers to care.

Multiple pathways may contribute to these patterns. Sensory impairment can increase effortful listening, fatigue, and stress, potentially affecting mood regulation.[30–32] Hearing impairment may also constrain communication and participation, contributing to loneliness and social isolation, which are consistently linked to depressive symptoms.[33,34] In addition, co-occurring functional limitations, sleep disturbance, and cognitive complaints may reflect shared neurobiological or health-related mechanisms that elevate depression risk.[30] While these interpretations are consistent with existing evidence, the present cross-sectional design does not establish temporal ordering, and the predictors should primarily be viewed as practical indicators for risk stratification.

4.2. Model performance and interpretability

We compared LR with 6 machine-learning algorithms (DT, random forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, SVM, and ANN). Although several complex models achieved near-perfect training performance, they showed greater degradation in the validation set, suggesting weaker generalizability in this sample. In contrast, LR remained stable, delivered the highest AUC, and showed favorable calibration. These results are consistent with broader clinical prediction literature indicating that machine-learning approaches do not uniformly outperform well-specified regression models, particularly when sample size, outcome prevalence, and signal-to-noise ratio constrain generalization.[35]

From an implementation perspective, LR also offers transparent coefficients, straightforward updating/recalibration, and interpretable risk communication. Decision-curve analysis indicated consistent net benefit across clinically relevant thresholds, supporting potential utility for prioritizing screening or follow-up. Translating the LR model into nomograms and an online tool further improves accessibility for clinical and public health use.

4.3. Clinical implications and potential implementation

The final model relies on routinely obtainable information and can be integrated into hearing-related assessments, comprehensive geriatric evaluations, or community health checkups to identify individuals at elevated risk of depressive symptoms. Such stratification may improve the efficiency of screening and referral, particularly in settings where mental health resources are limited. Moreover, the predictors highlight potentially modifiable vulnerability domains (e.g., sleep problems, functional limitation, and reduced social support), which may help clinicians and public health practitioners tailor follow-up assessments and supportive interventions. Notably, the tool is intended to support screening and risk communication; confirmation of depression and treatment decisions should be based on clinical evaluation.

4.4. Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include the use of multi-cycle NHANES data with standardized audiometry and a validated depression screening instrument, adoption of the 2021 WHO hearing impairment criteria, and a transparent modeling pipeline that combined stability-oriented feature selection with systematic algorithm comparison. The final LR model balanced discrimination, calibration, and interpretability, enabling practical deployment via nomogram-based tools. We further strengthened transparency by conducting prespecified sensitivity analyses across alternative missing-data handling, outcome thresholds, and hearing-loss severity definitions (Supplemental Table 3).

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and limits conclusions about temporal ordering; the relationship between hearing impairment and depressive symptoms may be bidirectional.[26] Second, PHQ-9 is a screening tool rather than a diagnostic interview, and several predictors were self-reported, which may introduce misclassification and reporting bias. Third, although multiple imputation was used for a small proportion of missing covariates among included participants, we excluded individuals with missing audiometry or PHQ-9 data; such exclusions may introduce selection bias and affect transportability. Additionally, model performance was lower when restricted to severe hearing loss (WHO grade ≥2), suggesting potential case-mix differences and limited transportability to this subgroup; larger samples and subgroup-specific updating or recalibration may be needed before use in individuals with more advanced hearing impairment. Fourth, outcome imbalance may influence threshold-dependent metrics; while we reported multiple performance measures and decision-analytic utility, threshold selection should be tailored to the intended application and resource context. Fifth, NHANES is designed to be nationally representative under its complex sampling framework; however, survey weights and design variables were not explicitly incorporated into the primary prediction modeling as reported here, so population-level prevalence estimates and national generalizability should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, external validation in independent cohorts and non-U.S. settings remains necessary, and model updating or recalibration may be required before broader implementation.

4.5. Conclusions and future directions

Future work should externally validate and, if needed, recalibrate the model in independent cohorts and diverse settings, including clinical and institutionalized populations. Longitudinal studies are warranted to evaluate performance for incident depressive symptoms and to determine whether integrating the tool into real-world workflows improves screening uptake, referral, and patient-centered outcomes.

5. Conclusion

In this NHANES-based cross-sectional study of U.S. adults aged ≥60 years with objectively defined hearing impairment, we developed and validated models to predict clinically relevant depressive symptoms (PHQ-9 ≥10). The LR model demonstrated the most balanced performance in the independent validation set (AUC = 0.862; Brier score = 0.053) and was translated into a nomogram and an online calculator to support individualized risk estimation. This tool may facilitate risk stratification and targeted screening in clinical and public health settings. External validation and, where necessary, recalibration are required before broader implementation in other populations and care contexts.
