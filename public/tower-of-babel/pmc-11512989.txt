The pluripotent-to-totipotent state transition in mESCs activates the intrinsic apoptotic pathway through DUX-induced DNA replication stress
Authors: Shunze Jia, Xinpeng Wen, Minwei Zhu, Xudong Fu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe pluripotent mouse embryonic stem cell (mESCs) can transit into the totipotent-like state, and the transcription factor DUX is one of the master regulators of this transition. Intriguingly, this transition in mESCs is accompanied by massive cell death, which significantly impedes the establishment and maintenance of totipotent cells in vitro, yet the underlying mechanisms of this cell death remain largely elusive. In this study, we found that the totipotency transition in mESCs triggered cell death through the upregulation of DUX. Specifically, R-loops are accumulated upon DUX induction, which subsequently lead to DNA replication stress (RS) in mESCs. This RS further activates p53 and PMAIP1, ultimately leading to Caspase-9/7-dependent intrinsic apoptosis. Notably, inhibiting this intrinsic apoptosis not only mitigates cell death but also enhances the efficiency of the totipotency transition in mESCs. Our findings thus elucidate one of the mechanisms underlying cell apoptosis during the totipotency transition in mESCs and provide a strategy for optimizing the establishment and maintenance of totipotent cells in vitro.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-024-05465-z.Keywords: Totipotency, R-loops, Intrinsic apoptosis, Pmaip1, DNA replication stress

Introduction

Totipotency refers to the ability of a cell to generate all cell types in a new organism [1]. This pivotal attribute holds immense promise in the field of regenerative medicine and in vitro propagation techniques. However, the limited availability of murine totipotent embryos greatly impedes totipotency research. To circumvent this limitation, researchers have established in vitro totipotent cell models, such as ‘2-cell-like cells’ (2C-like cells, 2CLCs), as alternative tools to study totipotency [2–5]. The 2CLCs, which is a rare and unstable totipotent-like population in the pluripotent mESCs culture, exhibit similar epigenetic/transcriptomic features and developmental potential of totipotent 2-cell (2C) embryos (such as the expression of 2C transcripts MERVL and Zscan4), making them an ideal model to investigate totipotency [2]. To overcome the scarcity and instability of 2CLCs, several groups have managed to establish stable totipotent-like cell models from mESC culture. Totipotent blastomere-like cell (TBLC) is constructed through splicing inhibitor pladienolide B (PlaB) [6]. Totipotent-like stem cell (TLSC) is established using a chemical cocktail of epigenetics [7]. Totipotent potential stem (TPS) and chemically induced totipotent stem cells (ciTotiSCs) are generated through a combination of small molecules [8, 9]. These refined totipotency cell models offer a robust foundation for advancing totipotency research.

Intriguingly, the establishment of totipotency in vitro concomitantly induces cell death in culture. For instance, our previous study demonstrated that prolonged activation of the 2C-like transition triggers cell death pathways [10]. Similarly, other research has reported that 2C-like conversion correlated with DNA damage and cell death [7, 11]. Additionally, while the TBLC model can be maintained for multiple passages, PlaB treatment still reduces cell proliferation at lower doses [6]. Currently, cell death triggered by the totipotency transition poses a significant obstacle in the establishment and culture of totipotent cells in vitro, yet the underlying molecular mechanisms remain largely elusive.

In this study, by exploring multiple totipotency transition models, we uncovered that cell death correlated with totipotency transition is largely mediated by intrinsic apoptosis. The apoptotic process was predominately attributed to the upregulation of DUX, which leads to the accumulation of replication stress (RS). Consequently, the RS activated p53 and its downstream target, PMAIP1/NOXA, a BH3-only protein, and induced intrinsic apoptosis through the Caspase-9 and Caspase-7. Importantly, the depletion of Caspase-7 or the administration of the pan-caspase inhibitor Z-VAD decreased the ratio of apoptosis induced by the totipotent transition and facilitated the totipotency transition in mESCs. These findings reveal one of the molecular mechanisms underlying the totipotency-transition-induced cell death and suggest that inhibiting the DUX-induced cell death could be used to optimize the establishment and culture of in vitro totipotent cells.

Materials and methods

mESC culture

The mESC-E14 cell line, which carries the MERV-L-LTR-tdTomato reporter, was kindly provided by the laboratory of Jin Zhang from Zhejiang University. To establish the inducible totipotency transition model, we infected the reporter cell line with lentivirus carrying inducible Dux (Addgene, 138320) and subsequently performed clone selection as we described previously [12]. The DUX-KO mESC cell line L13 and L19 was kindly provided by the laboratory of Man Zhang from Guangzhou Laboratory. The mESCs were cultured on 0.1% gelatin-coated plates with standard leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF)/serum medium containing 10% FBS (HAKATA, HB-FBS-500), 1,000 U/ml mouse LIF (Biolegend, 554002), 0.1 mM non-essential amino acids (Gibco, 11140), 0.055 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco, 21985023), 2 mM GlutaMAX (Gibco, 35050), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco, 11360) and penicillin/streptomycin (100 U/ml) (Gibco, 15140) in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 ℃. The ESC lines were maintained by daily medium changes and regular passage every other day.

For chemical treatment, the Doxycycline hydrochloride (YEASEN, 60267ES10), TSA (Targetmol, T6161), PlaB (Targetmol, T16551), ATMi (Beyotime, SC9136), ATRi (Targetmol, T3032), NAC (Beyotime, S0077), and SOD (Beyotime, S0086) were dissolved and added to the culture medium according to the manustructure’s instructions.

Flow cytometry and apoptosis detection

Flow cytometry analysis was conducted using the BD Fortessa, and Annexin V-FITC/7-AAD (YEASEN, 40311ES50) was stained according to the manustructure’s instructions. Data and images were analyzed and generated using FlowJo (V10) software. The statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 7.

RNA isolation and qPCR

Cellular RNA was isolated using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, RC112). Subsequently, complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the HiScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, R212 and Q712). qPCR was performed on an XN-1000 V instrument (ABI), and the relative quantification of gene expression was determined using the 2−∆∆ CT method with a normalization to the reference gene Gapdh. The statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 7. Primers and other oligos utilized in the qPCR are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

RNA interference

For siRNA construction, the DNA oligos containing the targeted sequences were synthesized and annealed utilizing the T7 RNAi Transcription Kit (Vazyme, TR102). Subsequently, the interference sequences were transfected into the cells using the Lipo6000 transfection reagent (Beyotime, C0526). After the transfection process, the cells were collected to conduct subsequent assays.

For shRNA cell line construction, gene knockdown was accomplished using the pLKO.1 system. The specific shRNA sequences employed can be found in Supplementary Table S2. Lentivirus production was carried out utilizing the psPAX2-PMD2.G system in 293T cells. Subsequently, mESCs were infected with the lentivirus for a duration of 48 h in a medium supplemented with 1 µg/ml Polybrene. Following the 2-day infection period, the cells were cultured in a medium containing 1 µg/ml puromycin for an additional 8 days to facilitate the selection of infected cells.

Bulk RNA-Seq analysis

For overlap analysis, the differentially expressed genes in TBLC [6] and Dux-induced mESCs [12] were acquired from the published article.

For Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) analysis, the raw read counts were downloaded from the GEO database (TLSC, GSE166204; siSnrpb1, GSE168728), and differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 [13] in R (v4.3.2). The GSEA analysis was calculated using clusterProfiler [14].

ATAC-seq analysis

The SRA files of mESC that were treated with 1 μm doxorubicin (doxR) or vehicle control were downloaded from GSE168687 using the SRA Toolkit. The raw reads were trimmed using Trim Galore [15], and were aligned to mm10 using bowtie2 [16]. The BAM files were transferred to the BW files using samtools [17] and deeptools [18]. The final BW files were visualized by IGV [19].

ChIP-seq analysis

For overlap analysis, the p53 binding peaks were downloaded from GSE168688. The peak files were annotated using ChIPseeker [20], and only the peaks annotated at the promoter region were selected for overlap analysis.

For p53 binding visualization, the clean reads of p53 ChIP-seq were aligned to mm10 using bowtie2 [16]. The BW files were generated using samtools [17] and deeptools [18].

scRNA-seq analysis

The read counts of TBLC and PSC were downloaded from GSM5195024 and were merged by Seurat v4 [21]. Briefly, outlier cells that were satisfied by the metrics: log (UMI counts) (> 3 MADs, low end) and log (percent mitochondrial read count + 1) (> 3 MADs, high end) were filtered out. Read counts were normalized using NormalizeData () function. The top 2,000 most variable genes summarized by PCA were visualized in a two-dimensional UMAP embedding using RunUMAP () function.

Immunofluorescence

The cultured cells were cultivated on coverslips coated with gelatin and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (diluted in phosphate-buffered saline, PBS) at room temperature for a duration of 10 min. After fixation, cell permeabilization was carried out using 0.5% Triton X-100 (in PBS) for 10 min at room temperature, followed by a 30-minute incubation in a blocking buffer (3% bovine serum albumin, BSA, in PBS). Subsequently, the cells were subjected to incubation with the primary antibody (S9.6 Anti-DNA: RNA hybrid antibody: ActiveMotif, 65684; Cytochrome C: Wanleibio, WL02410; MuERVL-Gag: HUABIO, ER50102), suitably diluted in the blocking buffer, at room temperature for 1 h and underwent three PBS-T washes (PBS containing 0.02% Triton X-100). The cells were then incubated with secondary antibodies (ABflo® 647-conjugated Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L), 1:200, ABclonal, AS060; FITC-conjugated Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L), 1:200, ABclonal, AS011; 594-conjugated Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L), 1:200, ABclonal, AS039) in the blocking buffer for 1 h at room temperature, followed by three PBST washes. Nuclear DNA was counterstained with DAPI (Sigma, D9542). The visualization and capture of images were conducted using a Research-grade fluorescence inverted Microscope (Olympus FV3000). The statistical analysis of R-loops nuclear immunofluorescence intensity was performed using ImageJ (Fiji).

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the ATP luminescent cell viability assay (Beyotime, S0027) or CCK8 (Beyotime, C0037). These two assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the absorbance was detected by a microplate reader (TECAN, SPARK).

Dot blot assay

The accumulation level of R-loops was detected using a Dot Blot assay. The experiment was conducted according to the published protocol [22]. Cells were collected from a 6-well plate and lysed using a hypotonic buffer, followed by centrifugation of the nuclei at 1000 rcf for 5 min. Proteinase K was used to digest histones, and genomic DNA was purified using phenol: chloroform: alcohol (25:24:1, pH 8.0). The Dot Blot assay was performed on a methanol-pretreated 0.2 μm PVDF membrane. After UV crosslinking, the primary antibody (S9.6, ab234957, Abcam) was used to detect the R-loops signal.

Results

Totipotency transition leads to apoptosis byDux

To investigate the molecular determinants regulating the cell death process during the totipotency transition in the mESC culture, we first used two chemical compounds, PlaB [6] and trichostatin A (TSA) [2, 8], to induce totipotency transition in mESC. The mESCs used contain a MERVL (a 2C repeats)-promoter-driven tdTomato transgene to indicate totipotent cells [23–25].

Upon totipotency induction, we detected an increased cell death (Fig. S1a). Cell death can be classified into various types, including apoptosis, necrosis, and so forth [26], and we found that the apoptosis gene set is enriched in the transcriptomes of multiple totipotent cells, including TBLC and TLSC (Fig. S1b). Therefore, we hypothesized that totipotency-transition-induced cell death is an apoptotic process. To test our hypothesis, we performed flow cytometry analysis using Annexin V-FITC/7-AAD staining and western blotting for biochemical markers of apoptosis. We observed that both PlaB and TSA treatment increased the apoptosis ratio in the cell culture (Fig. 1a, S1c). These results indicate that totipotency transition induces apoptosis.

We next focused on the downstream mediator for the totipotency-transition-associated apoptosis. Of note, we found that the early apoptosis populations display a higher MERVL::tdTomato signal (Fig. S2a). In addition, we found that the MERVL+ totipotent cells exhibited an increased apoptotic rate (Fig. 1a-b). These results suggested that totipotency-induction-associated apoptosis occurs primarily in the totipotent cells. Since DUX is one of the mast regulators for totipotency induction in mESCs [27, 28], and the overexpression of DUX causes toxicity in myoblasts [29], we hypothesized that the totipotency-induction-associated apoptosis is induced by the upregulation of DUX. To test this hypothesis, we used our previously constructed cell line with doxycycline (Dox)-inducible Dux transgene [12]. DUX overexpression has been reported to induce totipotent 2CLC [27, 28]. Compared to our PlaB or TSA treatment (Fig. 1a-b), genetic induction of Dux induced a higher apoptosis and cell death ratio in the mESC culture along with a higher induction of Dux and totipotency-transition (Fig. 1c-e, S2b), indicating that Dux is related to the apoptosis associated with totipotency induction. We further examined whether the knockout (KO) of the Dux could rescue the apoptosis caused by PlaB and TSA. The results showed that the cell death and apoptosis induced by PlaB or TSA was alleviated in Dux-KO mESCs compared to that of wild-type mESCs (Fig. 1f, S1c, S2c-d) [30]. These results suggested that the apoptosis associated with totipotency induction is mediated through the upregulation of DUX.

A recent research suggested that ROS induction was associated with the induction of totipotency [31]. Although ROS can induce apoptosis [32], our results showed that the antioxidant (SOD and NAC) treatment could not rescue the totipotency-transition-associated cell death (Fig. S2e), while the pan-caspase inhibitor Z-VAD significantly decreased cell death (Fig. S2e). These results suggest that ROS is not a major mediator for totipotency-transition-associated death and further indicate that totipotency-induced cell death is majorly apoptosis.

Totipotency-transition-associated apoptosis is mediated by the accumulation of R-loops, replication stress, and p53

The transcription factor DUX induced massive transcriptional activation of totipotent transcripts in mESCs [27]. We observed that Dux KO abolished the totipotency-transition-induced cell death as well as the transcriptional activation effect of DUX on totipotent transcripts (Fig. 1f, S2c), suggesting DUX-induced apoptosis is related to its transcriptional effect in mESCs. Transcription induces R-loops, three-stranded structures that harbor an RNA–DNA hybrid [33]. The accumulation of R-loops, when confronted with DNA replication, results in replication fork slowing and DNA RS [34], which can ultimately lead to cell apoptosis [35]. Based on this information, we hypothesized that the overexpression of DUX may cause the formation of R-loops during the totipotency transition and lead to apoptosis through the accumulation of R-loops.

To confirm our hypothesis, we first detected whether the overexpression of DUX could generate R-loops. We found that R-loops accumulated upon DUX induction, and DUX-induced 2CLCs exhibited an upregulated R-loops signal (Fig. 2a-c), indicating that DUX induction positively correlates with R-loops structure in cells. The accumulation of R-loops could result in an increased cellular RS [36–38]. Accordingly, we found that the overexpression of DUX leads to RS, indicated by the increased levels of the RS markers P-RPA32 and DNA damage marker γH2AX (Fig. 2d). In addition, we found that RNase H1 treatment, which prevents the persistence of R-loops [39, 40], decreases the expression level of P-RPA32, γH2AX and apoptotic cell death upon DUX induction (Fig. 2e, S3a-b). These results suggested that DUX at least partially triggers DNA replication stress and cell death through the formation of R-loops.

DNA RS can lead to apoptosis through the stabilization of p53 [41–43]. To examine whether p53 is involved in DUX-induced apoptosis in the mESC culture, we first assessed the protein levels of p53 at various time points upon DUX induction. Our results revealed a time-dependent increase in p53 levels following DUX induction (Fig. 2f). In addition, PlaB-induced totipotent TBLCs also displayed transcriptional enrichment in the p53 pathway (Fig. S4a). These results suggested that the totipotency transition in mESCs is accompanied by an activated p53. Furthermore, we observed that the downregulation of Trp53 resulted in a decrease in the apoptosis rate in the 2CLCs or mESC culture upon DUX induction (Fig. 2g, S4b-c). Collectively, these results suggest that DUX induces apoptosis through the stabilization of p53.

The BH3-family protein PMAIP1 mediates DUX-mediated apoptosis

The transcription factor p53 modulates apoptosis through transcriptional activation of a subset of cell death-associated genes [44]. To molecularly characterize the downstream targets of p53-mediated apoptosis induced by the totipotency transition, we overlapped the target promotor region of p53 ChIP-seq [45], the differentially expressed genes upon totipotency transition induced by spliceosomal repression [6], and the differentially expressed genes induced by DUX [12]. We collected fifty common genes (Fig. 3a). Among these fifty common genes, we focused on the cluster that is uniquely upregulated in the DUX-induced totipotent 2CLCs (Fig. 3b), as DUX-induced totipotent 2CLCs exhibited the highest apoptotic ratio (Fig. 1d-e). In the 2CLC cluster, a small group of genes is uniquely upregulated in 2CLC, including Plk2, Ubc, Btg2, and Pmaip1. Among these genes, Pmaip1, also known as NOXA in humans, belongs to the BH3-only BCL-2 family and can directly modulate apoptosis [46].

To explore the role of PMAIP1 in DUX-induced apoptosis, we first examined the expression pattern of Pmaip1 upon DUX induction. We observed that Pmaip1 is activated upon treatment with Dox, PlaB, or TSA, as well as in totipotent TBLCs. (Fig. 3d-f, S5a). Intriguingly, both the perturbations of R-loops or Trp53 normalized the activation of Pmaip1 in DUX-overexpressed mESCs (Fig. S5b-c). In addition, the Pmaip1 promoter region was opened and bounded by p53 under DNA damage conditions (Fig. 3c). These results collectively suggest that the activation of Pmaip1 in DUX-expressed mESCs is mediated by R-loops and p53.

We next examined the effect of Pmaip1 on the DUX-induced apoptosis during the totipotency transition. We found that the Pmaip1 knockdown led to a decreased apoptosis ratio and cell death upon DUX induction compared to the control cell line (Fig. 3g, S5d-g). These data suggest that PMAIP1 mediates the DUX-induced apoptosis.

Taken together, our results suggest that PMAIP1 is activated by p53 during the totipotency transition in mESCs and mediates the DUX-induced apoptosis.

DUX-induced apoptosis is mediated by the intrinsic Caspase9-Caspase7 pathway

PMAP1, as a BH3-only protein, has the capability to trigger the activation of BAX and BAK. This activation induces mitochondrial membrane permeabilization and the liberation of cytochrome c into the cytoplasm. The cytosolic cytochrome further facilitates the assembly of the apoptosome complex, ultimately leading to the activation of initiator caspase of intrinsic apoptosis (Caspase 9) [47]. Since DUX-induced apoptosis is mediated by PMAIP1, we asked whether this apoptosis belongs to intrinsic apoptosis.

Firstly, our results showed that cytochrome c was induced and released into the cytoplasm upon DUX induction (Fig. 4a, S6a), indicating that DUX promotes the activation of the intrinsic apoptotic process [48]. Furthermore, Casp9 knockdown mitigated the apoptosis in DUX-induced totipotent cells (Fig. 4b, S6b-c). By contrast, the depletion of Casp8, an initiator caspase of extrinsic apoptosis, did not inhibit apoptosis in totipotent cells (Fig. S6d). These results collectively indicate that DUX induces intrinsic apoptosis.

The intrinsic pathways end at the point of the execution phase considered the final pathway of apoptosis [49]. Lastly, to investigate the role of executor caspase in apoptosis during totipotency-transition, we knockdown Casp3, Casp6, and Casp7 genes, respectively (Fig. S7a). Importantly, we found that the knockdown of Casp7 reduces the apoptosis induced by DUX, while Casp3/6 knockdown did not modulate the DUX-induced apoptosis (Fig. 4c, S7b-c). These data suggest that DUX-induced intrinsic apoptosis is executed by the Caspase-7.

Notably, we observed that Casp7 knockdown increased MERVL+ totipotent cell ratio in mESC culture upon DUX induction or PlaB/TSA treatment (Fig. 4d, S8a) and decreased the apoptosis in the MERVL+ totipotent cells induced by PlaB/TSA (Fig. S8b). In addition, the treatment of apoptosis inhibitor, Z-VAD, also promotes the expression of totipotent transcripts during the totipotency transition (Fig. S8c). These results suggest that the inhibition of DUX-induced apoptosis facilitates the establishment of totipotent cells in vitro.

Discussion

The establishment of inducible totipotent cell lines in vitro provides an instrumental tool to the study of totipotency [6–9]. However, the totipotency transition correlates with massive cell death [10, 11], and the underlying mechanisms are not fully understood. To fill in this gap, we used three previously reported methods to induce the totipotency transition in mESCs and revealed one of the molecular mechanisms governing the cell death associated with totipotency transition (Fig. 4e). Specifically, we found that during the transition from pluripotent-to-totipotent state transition in mESCs, the transcription factor Dux caused accumulated R-loop structure, which led to DNA RS. In response to DNA RS, p53 was stabilized, promoting the intrinsic apoptotic process by upregulating PMAIP1.

The cell death poses a significant challenge in the establishment of stem cell lines. Overcoming this cell death is crucial for generating and maintaining stem cell culture in vitro. For instance, the suppression of cell death via inhibiting the JNK pathway is important to the recreation of plasticity and cell proliferation in the process of human chemically induced pluripotent stem cell establishment [50]. Likewise, CHIR 99,021 is added to promote cell viability in the process of TPS cell line induction [8]. In this study, we also provided evidence suggesting that overcoming DUX-induced apoptosis could promote the establishment of totipotency in the mESC culture (Fig. 4d, S8c).

Our work uncovered that the upregulation of DUX positively correlates with the formation of R-loops, which leads to DNA damage and apoptosis. An R-loop is a structure that forms when a single-stranded RNA molecule hybridizes with one strand of double-stranded DNA during transcription [51]. As DUX causes massive transcriptional activation in mESCs [52], we speculated that the transcriptional activation effect of DUX induced the accumulation of R-loops, which might disrupt normal DNA replication in mESCs, leading to DNA RS. Notably, although our results showed that Dux induction positively correlates with R-loops formation in mESCs, we do not have evidence demonstrating that DUX directly induces R-loops. The precise relationship between R-loops and Dux requires further investigation. In addition, it is worth investigating whether DUX induces apoptosis in mESCs through alternative mechanisms other than R-loops, as our results do not rule out the possibility. Intriguingly, the abnormal accumulation of DUX in embryos induces embryo developmental arrest [53], yet the underlying mechanisms remain to be revealed. It is worth investigating whether DUX affects embryos through R-loops and DNA RS.

p53 has been demonstrated as a key driver of the death of damaged cells [54]. Our results indicated that p53 is stabilized during the totipotency transition and mediated DUX-induced apoptosis through activating Pmaip1. Interestingly, p53 can also activate DUX and promote 2C-like transition [45]. These results suggest that there is a positive regulatory loop between p53 and DUX during the totipotency transition in mESCs. It is plausible that p53 may also contribute to DUX-induced cell death by augmenting the expression of DUX. Additionally, these results suggest that it is not ideal to prevent totipotency-transition-induced apoptosis in mESCs through targeting p53, as p53 also affects totipotency transition.

Effector caspases, including Caspase-3, Caspase-6, and Caspase-7, are responsible for initiating the hallmarks of the degradation phase of apoptosis [55, 56]. Our results showed that Caspase7, instead of Caspase3/6, mediated DUX-induced apoptosis. As Caspase-7 exhibits distinct substrates in terms of executing apoptosis compared to Caspase-3 and − 6 [57–60], it is intriguing to explore the specific substrates and mechanisms underlying Caspase-7-mediated apoptosis upon DUX induction.
