Beyond physical access: disability-inclusive primary health care as a test of health equity in Sub-Saharan Africa
Authors: Mohamed Daud Mohamed, Yakub Burhan Abdullahi, Abdikadir Ahmed Hassan, Iqro Omar Ibrahim, Liban Abdullahi Sudi, Nuradin Abdullahi Sheikh Rashid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Disability inclusion in primary health care is not a peripheral issue but a fundamental test of whether health systems in sub-Saharan Africa are advancing health equity and universal health coverage. Persons with disabilities experience persistent and overlapping barriers across the care pathway, including physical inaccessibility, unaffordable services, inaccessible information, poor provider communication, stigma, weak referral systems, and limited access to rehabilitation and assistive technology. These barriers are intensified by poverty, gender inequality, rural residence, and under-resourced health systems, producing avoidable inequities in access, quality, continuity, and financial protection. This commentary argues that disability-inclusive primary health care should be reframed from a narrow focus on physical access to a broader agenda of health-system accountability. Drawing on recent evidence from sub-Saharan Africa, it highlights how exclusion is embedded in routine system design, financing, workforce capacity, data systems, and governance. The paper further argues that meaningful inclusion requires disability-disaggregated monitoring, accessible communication, reasonable accommodation, provider training, strengthened referral pathways, financing for rehabilitation and assistive products, and formal participation of persons with disabilities and their representative organizations in planning and oversight. Disability-inclusive primary health care is therefore not a specialist add-on, but a core measure of whether universal health coverage reforms are genuinely equitable, people-centered, and resilient. Embedding disability inclusion into routine primary health care performance systems offers a practical pathway to stronger accountability and more inclusive health systems across sub-Saharan Africa.

Background

Disability is not a marginal concern but a core test of whether health systems deliver on their commitments to health equity and universal health coverage [1]. The WHO Global Report on Health Equity for Persons with Disabilities frames disability-related health inequities as avoidable differences produced by unfair social, economic, and health-system conditions, rather than by impairment alone [1]. Persons with disabilities experience higher mortality, morbidity, limitations in functioning, unmet health needs, poor quality of care, stigma and discrimination, unaffordable services, inaccessible facilities, information not provided in usable formats, poor communication with providers, and weak referral systems [1–3].

In sub-Saharan Africa, these inequities are intensified by chronically under-resourced primary healthcare systems, reliance on out-of-pocket payments, limited and poorly equipped rehabilitation services, and fragile referral pathways, particularly in low-spending settings [3–5]. Physical inaccessibility, negative attitudes, and communication barriers in facilities further constrain primary-level care, including sexual, reproductive, and maternal health [6, 7]. These disadvantages are rarely experienced in isolation. Disability commonly intersects with poverty, gender, rural residence, age, and chronic conditions, compounding exclusion and amplifying vulnerability to unmet health needs and catastrophic health expenditure [2–4]. Simultaneously, sub-Saharan African countries are investing in universal health coverage and primary health care reforms, including increased primary care spending and health system strengthening; however, persistent non-financial barriers continue to limit equitable access for vulnerable groups [8–10]. Against this backdrop, disability-inclusive primary healthcare should be treated as a critical test of whether health equity and universal health coverage reforms in sub-Saharan Africa are genuinely inclusive, accountable, and people-centered [3].

Beyond physical access: the layered barriers facing persons with disabilities

Disability inclusion in primary health care in Sub-Saharan Africa cannot be reduced to questions of ramps, transport, or facility design. While physical accessibility is essential, it represents only one dimension of a complex web of barriers that persons with disabilities encounter throughout the care pathway. Evidence demonstrates that these barriers begin before reaching a facility, with challenges in recognizing health needs and deciding to seek care often compounded by limited health information, low health literacy, and dependence on caregivers for decision-making—particularly among children and those with cognitive or sensory impairments [4–6]. Upon attempting to access services, persons with disabilities face direct and indirect financial obstacles including consultation fees, diagnostic costs, medicines, rehabilitation expenses, assistive technologies, transport costs, caregiver time, and the burden of repeated visits [4, 11, 12]. These economic constraints are especially acute for women and children with disabilities living in poverty or rural areas [6, 11].

Communication barriers further undermine the provision of equitable care. Inaccessible health information, such as the lack of sign-language interpretation or materials not available in Braille or alternative formats, limits informed consent and reduces trust between patients and providers [5, 6, 12]. Poor provider communication skills and a lack of reasonable accommodation exacerbate these issues, leading to suboptimal care quality [2, 12]. Stigma, discrimination, negative provider attitudes, lack of privacy, and low disability competence discourage care-seeking and worsen patient experiences across all domains of primary health care, including sexual and reproductive health, maternal health, child health, non-communicable disease management, mental health care, rehabilitation referral pathways, assistive technology access, and emergency response [4, 11]. Weak referral systems and limited rehabilitation capacity further entrench exclusion after the initial point of contact [5, 12].

These barriers are not isolated problems but reflect the persistent design of many health systems around an implicit “standard patient”—one who can see, hear, move independently, communicate without support, read written information unaided, travel easily to facilities, pay out-of-pocket costs without hardship, and advocate for themselves. This model systematically excludes persons with diverse mobility, sensory, communication, cognitive, psychosocial, and support needs [5, 12]. Addressing only physical access risks perpetuating inequity; instead, disability inclusion must be treated as a core design principle for primary health care and universal health coverage—not an optional add-on [4, 5, 12].

Disability-inclusive primary health care: from special services to routine care

Primary health care (PHC) is the most appropriate and equity-oriented platform for disability-inclusive care in sub-Saharan Africa because it is the level at which universal health coverage (UHC) becomes meaningful for households and communities [10]. Focusing on persons with disabilities is essential to realize UHC, given their greater health needs, higher risk of exclusion, and systematic barriers to coverage, quality, and affordability of care [13]. Disability inclusion cannot be confined to specialist or charitable services or separate vertical programs but must be embedded in routine PHC functions, including health promotion, disease prevention, and first-contact care [13].

Integrated PHC can support the early identification of disability-related needs, inclusive health promotion, accessible communication, respectful care, immunization, maternal and child health, and sexual and reproductive health and rights [6]. PHC is also a critical entry point for non-communicable diseases and mental health care, where persons with disabilities often experience unmet needs and poorer quality of services [13].

Making disability inclusion operational requires coordinated action across PHC actors and levels. Community health workers and rural PHC providers in sub-Saharan Africa report that persons with disabilities face geographic, financial, attitudinal, and communication barriers, underscoring the need for disability-sensitive outreach, health education, and accessible facility environments [3, 11]. Evidence from Ghana shows that improving PHC for persons with disabilities requires more affordable services, increased availability of providers, better system navigation support, and disability-friendly infrastructure and equipment [12].

PHC also connects households and communities to rehabilitation and assistive technology through referral and follow-up. In South Africa, efforts to embed rehabilitation referral recommendations into national PHC treatment guidelines demonstrate how standardized pathways from primary care to rehabilitation can strengthen service delivery and increase referrals [14]. Yet persistent gaps in referral pathways, assistive device availability, and provider capacity in rural districts highlight the need for stronger linkage between PHC, rehabilitation, and social support systems [15].

Through coordinated community health workers, facility providers, referral systems, and district health management teams, disability-inclusive PHC can connect households, communities, facilities, rehabilitation services, and social protection while anchoring health-system accountability for persons with disabilities. However, PHC will not become genuinely disability-inclusive unless disability is systematically measured, financed, supervised, and monitored as part of routine health system accountability for UHC [13].

From access barriers to system accountability

Disability exclusion in primary health care (PHC) in Sub-Saharan Africa is not only an issue of physical or logistical access but also a failure of health system accountability [2]. What is not measured, funded, supervised, and monitored remains invisible to planners, facility managers, and universal health coverage (UHC) reforms, perpetuating inequity and undermining the right to health for all [16]. Barriers to inclusion are deeply embedded across multiple levels of the health system, including workforce capacity, information systems, financing mechanisms, governance structures, and service delivery; however, interventions to address these barriers are often fragmented and insufficiently integrated into routine performance systems [2].

Embedding disability inclusion within everyday health system performance requires disability-disaggregated data in routine health information systems, service readiness assessments, facility accessibility audits, inclusive quality-of-care indicators, health worker training, supportive supervision, referral tracking, follow-up, accessible complaints and feedback mechanisms, reasonable accommodation, and accessible communication [16, 17]. Financing must also explicitly cover rehabilitation services and assistive technologies because services that are not budgeted are unlikely to be sustained [18]. Without these elements, persons with disabilities remain uncounted in monitoring frameworks and are unaccounted for in resource allocation decisions [2].

Accountability requires more than describing barriers: primary PHC systems must be able to detect who is being missed by services, which services are inaccessible or underutilized, whether referrals are completed, whether reasonable accommodations are provided, and whether persons with disabilities experience respectful, continuous, and usable care [16]. This demands robust data collection at all levels—facility-based audits and community-level feedback—and the integration of disability indicators into national monitoring and evaluation frameworks [17].

Crucially, accountability cannot be achieved without the meaningful participation of persons with disabilities and their representative organizations in all aspects of health planning, facility assessment, monitoring, evaluation, service redesign, and policy development [19]. Participation must not be reduced to symbolic consultation but must be recognized as essential: persons with disabilities are best placed to identify hidden barriers within services, test usability in real-world contexts, and hold systems accountable for change [17]. Their involvement ensures that reforms are grounded in lived experiences rather than assumptions or external perspectives [19].

Disability-inclusive primary health care will remain aspirational unless inclusion is embedded into everyday health-system performance systems, such as data, financing, supervision, quality improvement, community feedback, and governance [16].

Policy priorities for disability-inclusive primary health care in Sub-Saharan Africa

Disability-inclusive primary health care (PHC) in Sub-Saharan Africa must be embedded into routine health-system governance, financing, information, and service-delivery arrangements rather than delivered through parallel or time-limited projects [16]. Table 1 summarises how key health-system barriers can be reframed as PHC/UHC responses and equity benefits. Ministries and districts should integrate disability-disaggregated indicators into PHC information systems and universal health coverage (UHC) monitoring so that gaps in coverage, quality, and financial protection for persons with disabilities are visible and can be acted upon [2]. Facility managers can then use these data to prioritize progressive accessibility upgrades such as ramps, pathways, toilets, signage, and adapted examination spaces in high-volume PHC facilities, guided by structured audits that quantify gaps and identify low- and medium-cost improvements [20].

Communication accessibility and reasonable accommodation should be addressed alongside the built environment by ensuring accessible health information, user-friendly communication formats, and practical tools that support staff in identifying and addressing communication barriers [17]. Disability-inclusive, respectful, rights-based training and supervision must be institutionalized for all cadres to tackle negative attitudes, low confidence, and limited disability knowledge while providing concrete skills for accommodation, informed consent, and referral [21]. Rehabilitation and assistive technology should be planned and financed as integral PHC functions, with clear referral pathways linking community and primary-level services to district rehabilitation, and with attention to chronic underfunding and logistical constraints that currently limit access [5]. Given the consistent evidence that direct and indirect costs are major barriers to accessing needed rehabilitation, financial protection reforms must explicitly include PHC, rehabilitation, assistive products, and transport [5].

Across these priorities, the participation of persons with disabilities and organizations of persons with disabilities in planning, monitoring, evaluation, and service redesign should be formalized through co-design processes and routine use of disability awareness and accessibility tools grounded in lived experience [17]. Finally, disability inclusion must be embedded in emergency preparedness, outbreak response, conflict and displacement responses, and climate-related disaster planning, in light of evidence that persons with disabilities were largely overlooked in African COVID-19 responses and experienced exacerbated access barriers [22].

Conclusion

Disability-inclusive primary health care is one of the clearest tests of whether health equity and universal health coverage in sub-Saharan Africa are being implemented in meaningful rather than rhetorical ways. The evidence in this manuscript shows that exclusion is not produced by impairment alone, nor solved by ramps alone; it is sustained by health systems that are not designed, financed, monitored, or governed to include persons with disabilities. Physical barriers, communication failures, provider stigma, weak referral systems, inadequate rehabilitation access, and the invisibility of disability within routine data and accountability structures together reproduce avoidable inequities in care. A genuine equity response therefore requires disability inclusion to be embedded in everyday primary health care functions: service delivery, workforce training, financing, information systems, quality improvement, community feedback, and governance. Progress will depend on moving from descriptive recognition of barriers to measurable system accountability, with disability-disaggregated data, reasonable accommodation, inclusive monitoring frameworks, and the formal participation of persons with disabilities in service design and oversight. In this sense, disability-inclusive primary health care is not a niche agenda. It is a practical and ethical benchmark for whether sub-Saharan African health systems are becoming more just, more responsive, and more truly universal.
