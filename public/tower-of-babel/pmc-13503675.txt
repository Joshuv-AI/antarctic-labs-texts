Clinical-radiomic model development for local recurrence prediction in stereotactic body radiation therapy-treated lung cancer: comparative analysis with radiobiological models
Authors: Bao-Tian Huang, Pei-Xian Lin, Ying Wang, Li-Mei Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prediction of local recurrence (LR) after stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) for lung cancer remains challenging, and conventional radiobiological models have shown limited predictive performance. This study aimed to develop a clinical-radiomic model for LR prediction and compare its performance with existing radiobiological models. A total of 153 SBRT-treated lung cancer patients were retrospectively analyzed. Radiobiological models were used to estimate 2-year and 3-year local control (LC) probabilities, which were subsequently converted to LR probabilities using the relationship P(LR) = 1 − P(LC) for comparison with other models. Radiomic feature selection and model construction were performed using LASSO regression with tenfold cross-validation. A clinical-radiomic model was built by integrating clinical factors with the radiomic score (Radscore), followed by nomogram development. Model performance was evaluated using ROC analysis and calibration plots, with internal validation performed through bootstrap resampling. Clinical utility was evaluated using bootstrapped ROC, calibration, and decision curve analysis (DCA). The clinical-radiomic model showed higher AUC values than the radiobiological and radiomic models alone for both 2-year and 3-year LR predictions (all P < 0.05). Nomogram-derived risk scores differed significantly between the recurrence and non-recurrence groups. Bootstrap resampling suggested stable discriminatory performance, acceptable calibration, and potential decision-analytic performance. The clinical-radiomic model may show potential for individualized LR risk stratification in lung cancer patients receiving SBRT. Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide1. However, not all patients with early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) are suitable candidates for surgery. For surgically ineligible patients with early-stage NSCLC, SBRT has emerged as a standard treatment option2. This technique enables the delivery of hypofractionated high-dose radiation, achieving elevated biologically effective doses (BED) while sparing adjacent organs at risk (OARs)3,4. Despite the excellent efficacy of SBRT, local recurrence (LR) still occurs in approximately 13.0–18.8% of patients5,6. Notably, patients who experience LR and subsequently undergo salvage therapy may achieve survival outcomes comparable to those of patients in the original SBRT-treated cohort7. These findings underscore the need for more accurate predictive models to better stratify patients at a high risk of developing LR after SBRT.

Currently, prediction of LR after SBRT mainly relies on two main approaches: conventional radiobiological dose–response models and imaging-based radiomic models. Conventional radiobiological models generally consider BED10 > 100 Gy as the most widely accepted threshold for reducing the risk of LR8. To improve predictive accuracy, several models incorporating both clinical and dosimetric factors have been proposed to estimate 2- and 3-year LR probabilities after SBRT9–14. However, our previous external validation study found that these models showed limited predictive accuracy, with the highest area under the curve (AUC) reaching only 0.62515, indicating the need for more effective prediction strategies. Radiomic models, which extract high-dimensional quantitative features from CT and other imaging modalities, have shown promising value for predicting LR in lung cancer16–20. However, evidence regarding whether the integration of radiomic and clinical information can further improve LR prediction compared with conventional radiobiological models after SBRT remains limited.

Therefore, the aim of the present study is to develop a clinical-radiomic model that integrates clinical factors with radiomic features for LR prediction after SBRT and to systematically compare its predictive performance with conventional radiobiological and radiomic models.

Materials and methods

Study population

The study cohort consisted of 153 patients with primary or metastatic lung cancer who were retrospectively enrolled. All patients received SBRT at the Cancer Hospital of Shantou University Medical College between July 2011 and January 2021. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Cancer Hospital of Shantou University Medical College (Approval No. 2022112). Written informed consent for participants was waived in accordance with national legislation and institutional requirements. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histologically confirmed primary or metastatic lung cancer treated with SBRT; and (2) availability of complete baseline clinical data and follow-up records.

Radiotherapy treatment

Radiotherapy protocols were implemented according to previously established institutional guidelines, using risk-adapted dose schedules based on tumor volume and proximity to OARs15. Typical dose schedules included 12.5 Gy × 4 (50 Gy total), 10.0 Gy × 5 (50 Gy total), 25 Gy × 1 (25 Gy total), 10.0 Gy × 4 (40 Gy total), and 12.0 Gy × 4 (48 Gy total), selected according to volumetric and spatial relationships between the tumor and OARs. All the dose schedules used in the study were listed in Supplementary Table 1. Treatment planning was performed using Eclipse v10.0 (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA) from July 2011 to September 2019 and v15.5 from October 2019 to April 2021. Dose calculation was performed using the Anisotropic Analytical Algorithm (AAA, version 10.0 for Eclipse v10.0 and version 15.5 for Eclipse v15.5), with tissue heterogeneity correction incorporated. Image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) was delivered with a TrueBeam LINAC using RapidArc or intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT). Intra-fractional setup errors were corrected using cone beam computed tomography (CBCT), while pre-treatment fluoroscopic imaging was used in selected cases to assess tumor motion trajectories and ensure accurate alignment with the planning target volume (PTV).

Follow-up

Follow-up protocols were carried out according to institutional standards. Imaging surveillance consisted of contrast-enhanced CT scans every two months during the first year, followed by semi-annual assessments thereafter21. The most recent follow-up was completed in April 2024. LR was diagnosed primarily based on histopathological confirmation and enhanced CT findings. Radiological evidence of progressive enlargement of the primary tumor or adjacent areas on two sequential CT scans ≥ 6 months apart was used as a diagnostic criterion. Pathological examination was performed when LR was strongly suspected. When biopsy was not feasible, diagnosis was established based on contrast-enhanced CT or PET/CT scans. Clinical symptoms (e.g., cough, chest pain) and oncologist-assessed physical signs served as supplementary diagnostic indicators22. For the 2-year endpoint, only patients with 24 months of follow-up or documented LR within 24 months were included. For the 3-year endpoint, only patients with 36 months of follow-up or documented LR within 36 months were included. Patients with death, loss to follow-up, or recurrence-free but had insufficient follow-up duration were excluded from the corresponding endpoint analysis. Consequently, all 153 patients were included in both endpoint analyses. For patients who developed LR, follow-up time was defined as the interval from treatment completion to the occurrence of LR, whereas patients without LR were assigned a follow-up time of 36 months.

Data collection

We collected comprehensive clinical and demographic data, including baseline clinical variables (gender, age, and clinical stage), anatomical characteristics (primary or metastatic status and histological type), anthropometric measurements (body mass index [BMI]), and tumor dimensions (maximum diameter [MD]), gross tumor volume [GTV], and PTV).

Laboratory parameters were also recorded, including standard blood counts such as lymphocyte count, neutrophil count, platelet count, and hemoglobin concentration (Hb). Inflammatory biomarkers included the lymphocyte ratio (LCR), the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR).

In addition, radiotherapy-related parameters were collected, including the prescribed dose (PD), prescribed biological effective dose (BEDPD), maximum dose in PTV (PTVmax), maximum biological effective dose in PTV (BEDPTVmax), fractional dose, treatment fractions, and treatment duration. The BED was calculated using the linear-quadratic (LQ) model with an α/β ratio of 10 Gy. The formula used was: BED = n × d × [1 + d/(α/β)], where n represents the number of fractions and d the fractional dose.

Radiobiological models

Three radiobiological models were applied to estimate the 2-year LR probability: the Liu model, Klement model, and the Ohri model. For 3-year LR probability prediction, four models were employed: the Liu model, Klement model, Gucken model, and the Santiago model. The Liu model focuses on tumor regrowth locally following radiotherapy and can be applied to predict treatment outcomes over different follow-up duration11. The Klement model utilizes Bayesian inference to estimate cure rates and LR probabilities as a function of time14. The Ohri model, Gucken model, and the Santiago model adopt logistic regression frameworks to describe tumor control probability (TCP) as a sigmoidal function of the BED12,13,23. All calculations were performed using an in-house MATLAB R2021b (MathWorks, USA) program. Model parameters were obtained either from the original publications or through direct communication with the authors. The formulas and parameters for each radiobiological model were summarized in Table 1. Because the radiobiological models reported in the literature provide estimates of local control (LC) probability only, LR probability was derived as the complementary probability of LC, i.e., P(LR) = 1 − P(LC). This transformation enabled direct comparison with the other models and ensured consistent outcome representation throughout the manuscript.

Radiomic model

CT image acquisition and region of interest (ROI) segmentation

Patients underwent contrast-enhanced chest CT imaging using a Brilliance Big Bore scanner (voxel size 1.0 × 1.0 × 3.0 mm3; tube voltage: 120 kVp; tube current: 350 mA; convolution kernel: B-kernel convolution; reconstruction matrix: 512 × 512). The scanning protocol covered the entire thoracic cavity, from the lung apex to the diaphragmatic base. Post-acquisition, DICOM images were imported into the Eclipse treatment planning system (Varian Medical Systems) for ROI segmentation. In the present study, radiomic analysis was performed based on the intrapulmonary target lesion delineated on simulation CT images used for SBRT planning. The GTV was contoured by a radiation oncologist with 10 years of experience and subsequently reviewed and checked by a senior radiation oncologist with 20 years of experience. The target was generally delineated using CT pulmonary window settings; however, soft tissue window with contrast was additionally referenced when necessary to avoid the inclusion of adjacent vessels, atelectasis, or mediastinal or chest wall structures within the GTV.

Radiomic feature extraction

Radiomic features were automatically extracted using the open-source Python package PyRadiomics (version 3.13.3, https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io/en/latest/index.html)24. Image processing followed the Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI)25. A total of 1409 features were extracted, including first-order features, shape features, and texture features, from both original and filtered images. The filtered image sets included wavelet, Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG), square, squareRoot, logarithm, exponential, and gradient images, with a sigma value of 1.0. Sigma values determine the level of texture emphasis: lower sigma highlights fine textures (short-distance gray-level variations), whereas higher sigma emphasizes coarser textures (long-distance variations). Texture features included the gray level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM), gray level dependence matrix (GLDM), gray level run length matrix (GLRLM), gray level size zone matrix (GLSZM), and neighboring gray tone difference matrix (NGTDM)26. All CT images and segmentation were resampled to an isotropic voxel size of 1 × 1 × 1 mm3. Gray-level discretization was performed with a fixed bin width of 25 for texture feature extraction.

Radiomic feature selection and radiomic model development

To ensure feature reproducibility, intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) analysis was performed to evaluate the stability of radiomic features. A temporal partitioning strategy was adopted by comparing feature consistency between two independent chronological cohorts: cohort 1 (76 tumors imaged between January 2012 and August 2013) and cohort 2 (76 tumors imaged between September 2013 and April 2021). Features with ICC > 0.40 were retained for further analysis. Next, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to remove redundant features, and only those with a Pearson correlation coefficient < 0.90 were kept. To address the variability in feature scales, Z-score normalization was applied to standardize the data. Feature selection and model construction were then performed using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression algorithm. The method applies a regularization parameter (λ) to shrink regression coefficients towards zero, effectively excluding non-contributory features. The optimal λ was determined through tenfold cross-validation by selecting the value that minimized the cross-validation error. The radiomic score (Radscore) was calculated as the weighted sum of the selected feature names and their corresponding coefficients. Finally, the radiomic model was successfully constructed based on the selected features.

Clinical-radiomic model development

Binary logistic regression was performed for both univariate and multivariate analysis to assess the association between collected variables and 2- and 3-year LR status after SBRT. First, univariate logistic regression was applied to evaluate whether clinical, laboratory, and radiotherapy-related parameters were potential predictors of 2- and 3-year LR. Variables with P < 0.10 in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate model using a forward stepwise selection method, with the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) used to determine the optimal model fit27. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in the multivariate analysis. The clinical-radiomic model was subsequently constructed by integrating the Radscore with the independent risk factors identified through multivariate logistic regression analysis. In addition, given the previously reported association between BEDPTVmax and LR risk following SBRT11–13, BEDPTVmax was also incorporated into the final clinical-radiomic model regardless of its statistical significance in the multivariate analysis. To assess potential multicollinearity, tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) values were calculated. A tolerance < 0.1 and a VIF > 10 were considered indicative of multicollinearity28.

Model performance comparison

The performance of the radiobiological models, the radiomic model, and the clinical-radiomic model was assessed using multiple metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-Score, sensitivity, and specificity. Discriminative ability was evaluated by calculating the area under the curve (AUC) values of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. ROC curves of different models were generated using MedCalc software (MedCalc, Version 20.015, MedCalc Software Ltd). Sensitivity and specificity were automatically derived from the software, and predictive accuracy was calculated according to the recommended threshold values. Model calibration was further examined using calibration plots to evaluate the agreement between predicted and observed outcomes. A 45-degree calibration curve indicates perfect concordance between predicted probabilities and actual outcomes.

Nomogram construction and bootstrap validation of the clinical-radiomic model

A nomogram was developed based on the clinical-radiomic model to visually depict the 2- and 3-year LR probabilities. Total nomogram scores were calculated separately for the recurrence and non-recurrence groups to assess the discriminative capacity of the model. In the absence of an external validation cohort, internal validation was performed using 500 bootstrap resampling iterations to evaluate model stability and predictive performance. Bootstrapped ROC curves, calibration plots, and decision curve analysis (DCA) curves were generated. DCA was used to quantify the net clinical benefit of the predictive models across a range of threshold probabilities, thereby evaluating their potential clinical utility.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or as the median (P25, P75) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency (percentage). For model performance comparison, ROC curves were generated using MedCalc software. A P value < 0.05 indicated statistically significant discriminative ability. Differences in AUC values between models were assessed using the DeLong test. The P value was calculated using the Z-test method, with a P value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, with P > 0.05 indicating no significant difference between observed and predicted probabilities. Calibration plots were generated using OriginPro 9.1.0 (OriginLab Corporation, USA).

All remaining statistical analyses were performed using R software (Version 4.4.2). For radiomic feature selection, ICC analysis was conducted with the ICC package, and LASSO regression was implemented using the glmnet package. Waterfall plots were generated using the ggplot2 package. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis were carried out using the glm package. The nomogram of the clinical-radiomic model was developed with the rms package. Radscore points in the recurrence and non-recurrence groups were plotted using the ggplot2 package, and group differences were assessed with the Mann–Whitney U test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. ROC curves were generated using the ggROC package, while calibration curves were produced using the val.prob and calibrate functions within the rms package. DCA curves were created with the dcurves package.

Results

Patient characteristics and treatment outcome

A total of 153 patients with lung cancer who met the predefined eligibility criteria were retrospectively included. Comprehensive clinical data and detailed follow-up records were available for analysis. Patient and treatment characteristics were summarized in Table 2. With a median follow-up duration of 36 months, 38.6% (59/153) of patients experienced LR within two years after radiotherapy, while 43.1% (66/153) had developed LR within three years. The reported median follow-up duration of 36 months represented the fixed endpoint time used for analysis rather than the actual clinical follow-up duration. The 2-year and 3-year LR rates observed in this cohort were higher than those generally reported in the literature for patients treated with lung SBRT.

Radiomic model development

From the CT images, a total of 1,409 radiomic features were initially extracted. After reproducibility assessment using ICC analysis, 661 features with ICC > 0.40 were retained for further evaluation. Pearson correlation analysis was then applied to remove redundant features, resulting in 162 features selected for LASSO regression. A correlation heatmap of these features was shown in Fig. 1. For the 2-year predictive model, LASSO regression identified eight features at lambda.min and three features at lambda.1se with nonzero coefficients. For the 3-year predictive model, eight features at lambda.min and five features at lambda.1se were identified. To ensure model simplicity and facilitate future clinical relevance, three features (lambda.1se) were used to build the 2-year model, and five features (lambda.1se) were used to build the 3-year model. Clinical-radiomic model development

The clinical variables included in the 2-year and 3-year predictive models, as identified through univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses, were presented in Tables 4 and 5, respectively. In the multivariate analysis, clinical stage, PTV volume, and lymphocyte count were independently associated with 2-year LR, whereas clinical stage and MD were independently associated with 3-year LR. Given the previously reported association between BEDPTVmax and LR risk following SBRT11–13, it was incorporated into the clinical-radiomic model construction. Accordingly, the 2-year clinical-radiomic model was built by integrating clinical stage, PTV volume, lymphocyte count, BEDPTVmax, and the Radscore. The 3-year clinical-radiomic model was constructed by combining clinical stage, MD, BEDPTVmax, and the Radscore. Multicollinearity analysis results for variables in the final model were presented in Table 6, indicating no evidence of multicollinearity, as tolerance values were > 0.1 and VIF values were < 10. The AUC values for the clinical-radiomic model were 0.845 (95% CI 0.781–0.908) for 2-year LR prediction and 0.833 (95% CI 0.768–0.897) for 3-year LR prediction (Fig. 3A,B).

Performance comparison of the radiobiological, radiomic, and the clinical-radiomic models

The discriminative performance of all models was evaluated using AUC values, as summarized in Table 7. For 2-year prediction, the AUC values for the Liu, Klement, Ohri, radiomic, and the clinical-radiomic models were 0.624 (95% CI 0.542–0.701), 0.566 (95% CI 0.484–0.646), 0.633 (95% CI 0.552–0.710), 0.763 (95% CI 0.688–0.828), and 0.845 (95% CI 0.781–0.908), respectively. For 3-year prediction, the AUC values for the Liu, Klement, Gucken, Santiago, radiomic, and the clinical-radiomic models were 0.625 (95% CI 0.543–0.702), 0.575 (95% CI 0.494–0.655), 0.618 (95% CI 0.536–0.696), 0.618 (95% CI 0.536–0.696), 0.775 (95% CI 0.701–0.839), and 0.833 (95% CI 0.768–0.897), respectively. The clinical-radiomic model showed higher AUC values than all the evaluated radiobiological and radiomic models alone for both 2-year and 3-year LR predictions (all P < 0.05). ROC curves of the radiobiological, radiomic, and the clinical-radiomic models were shown in Fig. 3A,B, while the AUC comparisons were illustrated in Fig. 3C,D. Model performance metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-Score, sensitivity, and specificity were summarized in Table 7. Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, with P > 0.05 indicating good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities. As shown in Table 8, all models showed good calibration performance except for the Klement model in 2-year LR prediction. Calibration curves for all models were displayed in Fig. 4.

Nomogram construction and bootstrap analysis of the clinical-radiomic model

A nomogram was successfully constructed based on the 2-year and 3-year clinical-radiomic models (Fig. 5A,B). To further exhibit the clinical potential of the model, total nomogram points for individual patients were calculated and visualized using boxplots (Fig. 5C,D). Patients were exploratorily stratified according to the median nomogram score observed in the study cohort (90.58 for the 2-year model and 82.03 for the 3-year model). These values were used solely for descriptive risk-group comparison and should not be interpreted as clinically validated thresholds. In the absence of an external validation cohort, model performance was internally validated using bootstrap resampling with 500 iterations. The discriminative ability and calibration of the clinical-radiomic models were evaluated using ROC curves, calibration plots, and DCA curves (Fig. 6). The mean bootstrapped AUC values for the 2-year and 3-year clinical-radiomic models were 0.848 (95% CI 0.786–0.909) and 0.834 (95% CI 0.772–0.897), respectively. The bootstrapped ROC curves and calibration plots indicated good discrimination and calibration performance. The DCA showed that the clinical-radiomic model may provide net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities, approximately 10–90% for both models, suggesting potential clinical usefulness within this range. However, these thresholds are exploratory in nature and require further validation in independent cohorts before any clinical interpretation.

Discussion

Compared with previous studies that primarily focused on radiobiological models, our findings provide preliminary evidence that the clinical-radiomic approaches may offer potential value for individualized risk stratification in patients with lung cancer undergoing SBRT. To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly compare radiobiological, radiomic, and the clinical-radiomic models within the same cohort. We found the clinical-radiomic model exhibited significantly better discriminative performance compared with both the radiobiological models and the radiomic model alone for both 2-year and 3-year LR prediction. Historically, LR prediction has relied on the BED10 > 100 Gy threshold8. Although more recent radiobiological models incorporating dosimetric parameters and tumor size have been proposed10–14, their performance has remained modest, with the highest AUC reaching only 0.625 in our external validation15. Few studies have examined the integration of clinical factors with radiomic signatures (Radscore) for 2-year and 3-year LR prediction. Our results suggest that the clinical-radiomic model exhibited stable discriminatory performance, acceptable calibration, and potential decision-analytic performance based on the internal bootstrap resampling validation. Importantly, most variables included in the model are readily available in routine clinical practice, suggesting that it may warrant further investigation as a potential tool for individualized risk assessment. However, given the lack of external validation, the model should currently be considered a potential predictive approach rather than a tool ready for routine clinical decision-making.

This proposed model may provide a potential framework for future investigations of risk-adapted management strategies after SBRT. From an exploratory perspective, patients predicted to have a high risk of LR could be prioritized for evaluation in future prospective studies investigating whether closer imaging surveillance (such as shorter follow-up intervals or earlier PET/CT evaluation, e.g., every 3–4 months instead of every 6 months) may facilitate the potential timely detection of disease progression. Additionally, such risk stratification might offer a hypothesis-generating framework for future clinical trials investigating advanced management, including dose escalation during radiotherapy or the incorporation of adjuvant systemic therapies (such as targeted therapy or immunotherapy) in strictly selected high-risk cohorts. In contrast, patients with a low predicted risk of LR might eventually be evaluated for routine surveillance schedules (e.g., biannual follow-up CT), potentially reducing unnecessary imaging examinations and healthcare burdens. The proposed model may serve as a basis for future studies focusing on external validation, model refinement, and assessment of generalizability across different patient populations. However, no conclusions regarding clinical utility or decision-making can be drawn from the present single-center retrospective study. Further independent external validation is required before any consideration of clinical application.

Emerging evidence further supports the potential of radiomics to improve prognostic accuracy in lung cancer radiotherapy. Several recent investigations have reported that radiomic features derived from multimodal imaging serve as independent predictors of LR after SBRT. Dissaux et al. identified two PET/CT-derived radiomic features significantly associated with LR in early-stage NSCLC patients treated with SBRT29. Other investigations have shown that PET-CT can also predict regional recurrence more accurately than clinical models alone19. Similarly, CT-based radiomics has shown comparable prognostic value: Lafata et al. reported that two pretreatment CT features were significantly associated with LR after SBRT16, and Isoyama-Shirakawa et al. found radiomic scores derived from treatment planning CT were significant factors for 3-year recurrence patterns18. Consistent with these studies, our results reinforce the prognostic utility of radiomic features for predicting LR in lung cancer after SBRT. Importantly, our findings suggest that integrating clinical and radiomic variables may improve predictive performance compared with the radiomic model alone. This observation is aligned with other studies17,30–32 and suggests that incorporating multi-omics features can substantially enhance the accuracy of predictive models for radiation therapy33.

While the study found that the radiomic model outperformed radiobiological models, considerable debate remains regarding the standardization and reproducibility of radiomic biomarkers. Numerous studies have emphasized that variations in CT acquisition parameters, reconstruction algorithms, radiation dose, and reconstruction settings can profoundly affect the stability and reproducibility of radiomic features34,35. Addressing these challenges requires strict standardization of feature extraction procedures and adherence to established reporting guidelines. In the study, the radiomic feature extraction process was fully compliant with the recommendations of the IBSI. Several preprocessing steps were implemented, including data resampling, gray-value discretization for texture features, and Z-score normalization, to enhance feature robustness. More importantly, the ICC-based screening method used in the study diverged from conventional approaches. It is generally recognized that the major barrier to the clinical implementation of radiomics lies in temporal and inter-institutional variability, whereby feature values can differ across centers or time periods. To address this, we assessed feature stability by comparing two chronological cohorts of 76 tumors each (January 2012–August 2013 vs. September 2013–April 2021), rather than using the traditional ICC test based on repeated manual segmentation by two radiologists. This methodological innovation provides a more comprehensive evaluation of feature stability across time periods and patient cohorts. The consistency of three radiomic features across both the 2-year and 3-year models further supports the feasibility and stability of this approach. These findings offer insights for researchers working on radiomic modeling and may help improve feature reproducibility, ultimately advancing the clinical application of radiomics.

Despite our efforts to compare the predictive accuracy of radiobiological and radiomic models and to develop a clinical-radiomic model with improved performance, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the representativeness of the findings. External validation using multi-institutional cohorts will be necessary in future studies to assess the generalizability of the model. Additionally, because feature selection was performed using the full dataset before model evaluation, the reported AUC values may be susceptible to optimistic bias resulting from potential overfitting and data leakage. Furthermore, given the absence of external validation and full-pipeline validation, the limitation regarding such possible optimistic bias should be acknowledged. Second, the retrospective design may introduce selection bias, potentially affecting the validity of the conclusions. Third, the observed LR rates in the present study were higher than those reported in many previous SBRT studies, which may be partly attributable to the inclusion of both primary and metastatic lung tumors. Specifically, metastatic lesions accounted for 39.22% of the study population, thereby increasing the biological and clinical heterogeneity of the cohort. (1) From a radiobiological perspective, metastatic tumors often exhibit distinct genomic landscapes or altered tumor microenvironments. Furthermore, metastases derived from certain radioresistant primaries (e.g., colorectal cancer or renal cell carcinoma) were typically associated with higher LR compared to primary tumors. Nevertheless, current evidence suggests that after SBRT. Guckenberger et al. reported no significant difference in LR between primary lung tumors and lung metastases treated with SBRT36, while Park et al. also found no significant association between tumor location and overall survival; the inclusion of metastatic lesions may not compromise the treatment outcome, or treatment-related toxicity following SBRT for NSCLC37. Although detailed subgroup analyses were not explicitly performed in the present study, our observations were generally consistent with findings reported in the literature. Nevertheless, given the heterogeneity of the study population, including both primary and metastatic lesions, the potential impact of lesion type on model performance cannot be excluded and should be further evaluated in future studies. (2) Additionally, the present study included patients treated with various dose schedules in a real-world clinical setting, which may also have influenced LR outcomes. The median prescribed BED in our cohort was 95.2 Gy10, slightly lower than the commonly recommended threshold of 100 Gy10, partly because some metastatic lesions received relatively low-dose palliative treatment regimens. However, the inclusion of both high- and low-dose regimens may be considered a potential strength of the study, as it facilitated a more comprehensive assessment of radiobiological model performance across different regions of the dose–response curve. (3) We would like to emphasize that LR events determined based on histopathological confirmation and enhanced CT findings in the present study were comparable to those reported by other cancer centers. In addition, histopathological examination was performed whenever LR was strongly suspected during follow-up. Fourth, although calibration curves and Hosmer–Lemeshow tests displayed acceptable model calibration, more comprehensive calibration metrics such as calibration slope, calibration intercept, and Brier score were not evaluated in the present study, which may reduce the overall reliability and limit the comprehensiveness of model evaluation. Fifth, the study used contrast-enhanced CT, but potential variability in contrast enhancement and its effect on radiomic feature stability is not evaluated in the current study. The impact of contrast enhancement on radiomic features warrants further investigation in future studies. Sixth, regarding clinical variables, our model primarily included clinical stage, tumor size, laboratory parameters, and radiotherapy-related factors. Future studies could enhance model performance by incorporating additional clinically relevant variables, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) status and pulmonary function indicators.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the clinical-radiomic model integrating clinical parameters with the Radscore showed significantly better discriminative performance compared with both the radiobiological models and the radiomic model alone for predicting 2-year and 3-year LR status. These findings suggest that radiomics-based approaches, particularly when combined with clinical variables, may provide potential value for individualized risk stratification in lung cancer patients undergoing SBRT. However, further prospective multicenter studies with independent external validation are necessary to evaluate the reproducibility and generalizability of the model before routine clinical application can be considered.
