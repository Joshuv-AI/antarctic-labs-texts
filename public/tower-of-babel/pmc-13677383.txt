Requirement of phenylalanine and phenylalanine + tyrosine in broiler chickens fed purified amino acid diets and assessment of phenylalanine as a source of tyrosine
Authors: KB Nelson, SW Hufford, RL Drysdale, LS Parisi, MB Williams, LJ Urban, JC Valencia Beltran, MT Kidd, BW Parsons
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere is a paucity of information on the efficacy of Phe as a source of Tyr in chickens, a question that must be considered when expressing requirements for Phe and Tyr jointly (i.e. Phe + Tyr). The objectives of these experiments were to establish Phe and Phe + Tyr dose-response relationships in male Cobb 500 broilers using purified amino acid diets and to assess the efficacy of Phe as a source of Tyr. In Experiment I, Phe + Tyr requirement estimates were determined in diets devoid of Tyr. Dietary treatments were created by supplementing L-Phe in graded increments of 0.10% to achieve a range of 0.50 to 1.20% Phe. In Experiment II, Phe requirement estimates were determined using diets that contained excess Tyr (0.60%). Dietary treatments were created by supplementing L-Phe in graded increments of 0.10% to achieve a range of 0.20 to 0.90% Phe. In Experiment III, the efficacy of Phe as a source of Tyr was determined using a slope-ratio bioassay. Seven dietary treatments were created by supplementing 0.06, 0.12, and 0.18% of either L-Phe or L-Tyr to a purified basal diet that was devoid of Tyr and contained 0.60% Phe. There were 5 replicate pens of 5 male broilers per treatment in each experiment. In Experiments I and II, there was a quadratic effect of Phe on BW gain and gain:feed (P < 0.05). Linear one-slope broken-line regression analysis for gain:feed yielded an estimated Phe + Tyr requirement of 0.93% (R2 = 0.96) in Experiment I and a Phe requirement of 0.44% (R2 = 0.96) in Experiment II. In Experiment III, BW gain increased linearly in response to supplementation of both L-Phe and L-Tyr (P < 0.05). The estimated efficiency of L-Phe conversion to L-Tyr using multiple linear regression of BW gain on total Phe + Tyr intake ranged from 96.5 to 97.9% on a weight and molar basis. In conclusion, modern broilers can efficiently utilize Phe as a source of Tyr.Keywords: Broiler, Amino acid, Phenylalanine, Tyrosine, Requirement

Introduction

Early work characterizing animal responses to amino acids (AA) determined that Phe is indispensable for the rat (Womack and Rose, 1934), chicken (Almquist and Grau, 1944) and pig (Mertz et al., 1952). Moreover, Womack and Rose (1946) and Grau (1947) determined that Tyr is dispensable when sufficient Phe is present for both rats and chickens, respectively. In the liver, and to some extent the kidney, Phe is irreversibly hydroxylated to form Tyr via tetrahydrobiopterin-dependent Phe hydroxylase during the initial step of Phe catabolism (Kaufman, 1957, 1958 and 1961; Hsieh and Berry, 1979). This metabolic relationship is the basis for expressing animal requirements for Phe and Tyr as Phe + Tyr. Since Tyr cannot be converted to Phe, however, it is essential that there is sufficient Phe to meet the Phe requirement. When Phe is in excess of the Phe requirement, it can be converted to Tyr to meet the total Phe + Tyr requirement (Lartey and Austic, 2008; 2009). If an animal inefficiently utilizes Phe as a Tyr source, uncorrected Phe + Tyr requirements would be misleading for diet formulation. Grau (1947) reported that Phe and Tyr are equally efficacious as Tyr sources for chickens. Sasse and Baker (1972) quantified Phe as a Tyr source in chickens using a slope-ratio bioassay and determined that the efficacy was 105 and 96% when expressed on equivalent weight or molar basis, respectively. However, Elkin et al. (1980) reported that when dietary Phe + Tyr was suboptimal, an equimolar substitution of Phe for Tyr depressed BW gain and feed efficiency by approximately 45% and 22%, respectively. Furthermore, Gloaguen et al. (2014) reported an inefficiency of Phe as a Tyr source more recently for piglets.

A dietary deficiency of either Phe or Tyr will limit protein synthesis and depress animal growth. Moreover, Tyr is a precursor for the synthesis of various biologically relevant compounds, including catecholamines (e.g., dopamine, epinephrine and norepinephrine), melanin, and thyroid hormones (e.g., triiodothyronine and thyroxine). Although Phe + Tyr is not likely to be deficient in current commercial broiler chicken diets, efforts to reduce dietary CP could place more pressure on the Phe + Tyr requirement, which has not been extensively researched in modern broiler chickens (Maynard et al., 2020; Kidd et al., 2021). The variation in estimated efficacy of Phe as a Tyr source in the current literature questions the validity of expressing individual requirements for Phe and Tyr as Phe + Tyr for broiler chickens. If Phe is markedly inferior as a source of Tyr, Phe + Tyr requirement estimates would be confounded by the dietary Tyr content.

Therefore, the objectives of these experiments were to establish Phe and Phe + Tyr dose-response relationships in broiler chickens and to assess the efficacy of Phe as a source of Tyr using purified AA diets.

Materials and methods

All practices and procedures utilized in these experiments were approved by the University of Arkansas Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Fayetteville, AR, U.S.; Protocol number 25050).

Diets and experimental design

A total of 3 experiments were conducted herein. In all experiments, male Cobb 500 by-product chickens (off-sex of female parent-stock) were sourced from a local hatchery (Cobb-Vantress, Inc., Siloam Springs, AR, U.S.). Birds were housed in clean battery cages on raised wire floors in a temperature and humidity-controlled room. The lighting schedule was as follows: 24 h of light on d0, 23L:1D from 1 to 4 d-of-age, 20L:4D from 5 to 15 d-of-age, and 18L:6D from d16 until the end of the experiment. Each cage (61 cm × 61 cm) was equipped with pressurized nipple-drinkers and a feed trough. Birds were allowed access to water and feed ad libitum. A nutrient adequate corn and soybean meal-based crumble starter diet was offered during the pre-experimental phase. Prior to the start of the experimental phase, birds were fasted overnight. On d 12 (Experiments I and II) or d 11 (Experiment III), birds were weighed and allotted to cages (5 birds per cage) to equalize BW among treatments. The d 12 average BW for Experiments I and II were 335 and 336 g per bird, respectively, and d 11 average BW for Experiment III was 274 g per bird, respectively. Dietary treatments were assigned to cages (5 replicate cages per treatment) following a completely randomized design. Experimental diets were offered ad libitum until 19 d post-hatch in Experiments I and II or 18 d post-hatch in Experiment III.

In each experiment, a common basal diet was used to make the dietary treatments (Table 1). Basal diets were similar among experiments. Dietary treatments were purified cornstarch-based mash diets that contained a free AA mixture. The decision to omit dextrose during formulation was made to avoid spontaneous Maillard reactions with the free AA mixture that could reduce AA bioavailability. The free AA mixture composition is found in Table 1 and was based on the mixture used by Sasse and Baker (1972), with the exception that AA levels were optimized for modern broiler chickens (1.14% digestible Lys). Dietary treatments were created by supplementing various levels of L-Phe and L-Tyr at the expense of Solka Floc to the basal diets. Representative diet samples were analyzed for CP and AA concentrations (University of Missouri, Columbia, MO, U.S; Method 990.03 and 982.3 E [a, b and c]; AOAC International, 2007).

Experiment I was conducted to estimate the Phe + Tyr requirement in diets devoid of Tyr. The basal diet in this experiment contained 0.50% Phe (1.14% Lys) and L-Phe was added to achieve levels of 0.60, 0.70, 0.80, 0.90, 1.00, 1.10, and 1.20% Phe (Table 1). Experiment II was conducted to estimate the Phe requirement in diets with excess Tyr (0.60%). The basal diet in this experiment contained 0.20% Phe (1.14% Lys) and L-Phe was added to achieve levels of 0.30, 0.40, 0.50, 0.60, 0.70, 0.80, and 0.90% Phe. Experiment III was conducted to determine the efficacy of Phe as a source for Tyr. The difference between estimated requirements for Phe (Experiment I) and Phe + Tyr (Experiment II) were utilized for Experiment III, as this provided the range of dietary Phe levels where Phe would be converted to Tyr by the birds. In Experiment III, the basal diet contained 0.60% Phe and was devoid of Tyr. To the basal diet, 0.06, 0.12, and 0.18% of either L-Phe or L-Tyr were added. Statistical analyses

All data were analyzed using SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, U.S.). Data in all experiments were subjected to a one-way ANOVA via PROC GLM for a completely randomized design and means were separated using Tukey’s HSD test. Nutrient requirements were estimated using classic statistical models in Experiments I and II, where data were subjected to linear, quadratic, and linear one-slope broken line regressions via PROC NLIN or PROC GLM (Robbins et al., 2006). Data in Experiment III were subjected to multiple linear regression analyses via PROC GLM. Prior to multiple linear regression analyses, linear responses to Phe and Tyr were assessed individually. For multiple linear regression analyses, BW gain was regressed against total Phe + Tyr intake on both weight and molar basis. The efficacy of Phe as a Tyr source was determined using the slope-ratio method. The efficacy value for Tyr was set at 100% and the regression coefficient (slope) for Phe was divided by the regression coefficient (slope) for Tyr to determine the efficacy of Phe as a Tyr source (Littell et al., 1995). For all analyses, the cage served as the experimental unit and each experiment had 5 replicate cages per treatment with 5 birds per cage. Significance was declared at P < 0.05 for all analyses.

Results and discussion

Experiment I was conducted to determine Phe + Tyr requirement estimates of broiler chickens from 12 to 19 d-of-age fed purified AA diets devoid of Tyr. Live performance data for Experiment I are presented in Table 3. The BW gain, feed intake, and gain:feed differed among dietary treatments (P < 0.05). Positive quadratic responses were observed for BW gain (P < 0.05, R2 = 0.93), feed intake (P < 0.05, R2 = 0.91), and gain:feed (P < 0.05, R2 = 0.96) as Phe was increased from 0.50 to 1.20%. Linear one-slope broken line regressions are shown in Figure 1 for BW gain and gain:feed. The estimated Phe + Tyr requirement for BW gain was 1.02% (P < 0.05, R2 = 0.93) and gain:feed was 0.93% (P < 0.05, R2 = 0.96) using the broken line model.

A summary of the estimated Phe and Phe + Tyr requirements determined in Experiments I and II for broiler chickens fed purified AA diets from 12 to 19 d-of-age are shown in Table 5. The estimated Phe and Phe + Tyr requirements for BW gain and gain:feed ranged from 0.44 to 0.46 for Phe and 0.93 to 1.02% for Phe + Tyr using the broken line model. This is in good agreement with Sasse and Baker (1972) who used the same model and similar diets with crossbred chickens. For example, Sasse and Baker (1972) reported values of 0.50% for Phe and 0.95% for Phe + Tyr for optimal BW gain. Furthermore, both the present study and the study by Sasse and Baker (1972) indicate that Phe and Tyr each account for approximately half of the Phe + Tyr requirement. Estimated ideal AA ratios relative to Lys are also presented in Table 5. The ideal AA ratio for Phe + Tyr ranged from 82 to 96% across the various models, with the estimate being 92% for the quadratic-broken line model. The ideal Phe ratio ranged from 39 to 60% and was 47% for gain:feed using the intersect from the quadratic and broken line regressions.

Published requirement estimates for Phe + Tyr in modern broiler chickens are limited. Franco et al. (2017) estimated that an ideal AA ratio relative to Lys for Phe + Tyr of 107% was sufficient for optimal BW gain from 8 to 17 d-of-age in male Cobb 500 broiler chickens when using the linear broken line model. This estimate is higher than the ideal AA ratios determined in the present study (Table 5). The cause for this discrepancy may be partially due to differences in the basal diets used, where Franco et al. (2017) fed diets containing corn, broken rice, soybean meal, fish meal, and meat and bone meal, whereas purified AA diets were utilized in the present study. Another important consideration is the digestible Lys level in the diets, as this can substantially affect the ideal AA ratio if Lys is in excess. Franco et al. (2017) utilized a lower digestible Lys content of 1.05% compared with 1.14% in the present study. Digestible Lys could not be restricted too much in the present study without interfering with the primary objectives of Experiments I and II, which were the determination of the absolute Phe and Phe + Tyr requirements so that the appropriate titration window could be selected for assessment of Phe conversion to Tyr in Experiment III. It should also be noted that there are many more non-linear models to estimate nutrient requirements than those evaluated in Experiments I and II herein. Supplementary data is available for those interested in investigating models other than the classic ones utilized in the present study.

In another study by Roth et al. (2001), it was found that a reduction in Phe from 0.80 to 0.52% in diets containing adequate Tyr did not negatively influence the growth performance of male broilers from 8 to 28 d-of-age, suggesting the Phe requirement may be below 0.52%. This is in good agreement with Experiment II (Table 5). Interestingly, when reducing Phe in the diets, Roth et al. (2001) did report poor feather quality. Poor feather quality was also observed in the experiments herein when dietary Phe was inadequate, albeit feather quality was not quantified. Further research evaluating the Phe + Tyr requirement is warranted with longer experimental periods, more replicates per treatment and birds per replicate, and commercially relevant diets to confirm and refine the estimates in Table 5 for commercial broiler chickens. For precise determination of the requirement of Phe + Tyr for BW gain, higher levels than 1.2% may also be needed to identify a clear plateau.

Experiment III was conducted to determine the efficacy of Phe as a Tyr source. Data from Experiments I and II were utilized to select a range of L-Phe supplementation that would yield linear responses in growth that was also between the requirement for Phe + Tyr (Experiment 1) and Phe (Experiment 2). A selection of levels between the 2 requirements was done to ensure that supplemental L-Phe consumed by the birds would be utilized to meet the Tyr requirement. Live performance data for Experiment III are presented in Table 6. The BW gain, feed intake, and gain:feed increased with increasing levels of both L-Phe and L-Tyr (P < 0.05). Treatments supplemented with L-Phe or L-Tyr were not different (P > 0.05) at common inclusion levels (0.06, 0.12, or 0.18%) for any parameters assessed. Multiple linear regression analyses of total Phe + Tyr intake regressed against BW gain are presented on a weight and molar basis in Figures 3A and 3B, respectively. Linear responses were observed for BW gain with increasing consumption Phe + Tyr intake expressed as g of intake (P < 0.05, R2 = 0.98; Figure 3A) or moles of intake (P < 0.05, R2 = 0.98; Figure 3B).

The estimated efficacy of Phe as a Tyr source is presented in Table 7. The slopes determined for Phe and Tyr were 79.2 ± 1.87 and 80.9 ± 1.82 g BW gain per g of Phe + Tyr intake, respectively, but did not differ from one another (P > 0.05). The slope obtained for Phe was then divided by the slope obtained for Tyr, which revealed that the efficacy of Phe as a source of Tyr on a weight basis for BW gain was 97.9%. Differences in molecular weights of Phe (165.19 g/mol) and Tyr (181.19 g/mol) must be considered for valid comparisons of efficacy (Creek, 1968). Sasse and Baker (1972) converted the determined efficacy for Phe from a weight basis to a molar basis by multiplying the efficacy estimate determined of a weight basis by 0.91 (165.19/ 181.19). This resulted in a reduced estimated efficacy from 105% for Phe conversion to Tyr on a weight basis to 96% on a molar basis. In the present study, rather than performing a calculation on the final estimated efficacy on a weight basis, a new regression model was generated in which the input variable was changed from g of Phe + Tyr intake to moles of Phe + Tyr intake (Table 7). This accounted for differences in Phe consumption from the basal diet as feed intake increased with supplemental L-Phe or L-Tyr. When performing the regression using moles of Phe + Tyr intake, the slopes generated for Phe and Tyr were 13,232.6 ± 314.59 and 13,713.2 ± 309.99 g BW gain per mole of intake, respectively, and the slopes were different (P < 0.05). By performing the same calculation using the ratio of the slopes, the efficacy of Phe as a source of Tyr on a molar basis for BW gain was 96.5%. This value is remarkably similar to the value of 96% reported by Sasse and Baker (1972). Data from the present study indicates that there is a slight difference between efficacy estimates for Phe as a source of Tyr on a weight vs. molar basis, but this appears to be minimal in chickens. Furthermore, the estimated efficacy ranging from 97 to 98% demonstrates that Phe can be efficiently utilized by chickens to meet their Tyr requirement.

Gloaguen et al. (2014) reported numerical reductions in ADFI and ADG in piglets when the proportion of Phe relative to Tyr was increased despite the diets containing equivalent but suboptimal Phe + Tyr levels. Elkin et al. (1980) observed substantial growth depression in chickens when Tyr was replaced with Phe in diets containing equivalent but suboptimal Phe + Tyr levels. Elkin et al. (1980) suggested that the inefficiency of Phe as a Tyr source was not attributed to Phe hydroxylase activity as determined in vitro. It is important to note that reduced growth observed in the aforementioned studies was accompanied by reduced feed intake as Phe replaced Tyr. It is not abnormal for feed intake to vary in studies assessing efficacy of AA sources, particularly when purified AA diets are used which likely exacerbate palatability effects. For example, Graber and Baker (1971) observed that chickens fed Met adequate diets supplemented with Met had reduced feed intake in comparison to those fed Cys supplemented diets, despite determining that Met was 92% efficacious as a Cys source on a molar basis. Furthermore, in Experiment III in the present study (Table 6), feed intake was numerically lower at each level of L-Phe supplementation compared with L-Tyr and the same trend was observed for BW gain and gain:feed. In humans, L-Phe is bitter, whereas L-Tyr has little flavor (Wu, 2021). Thus, the palatability differences in Phe and Tyr can lead to differences in feed intake. The effect of Phe supplementation on feed intake of chickens has been documented but is inconsistent (Okumura et al., 1980; Edmonds and Baker, 1987; Lartey and Austic, 2008; 2009). When assessing efficacy, it is imperative that feed intake is either controlled or accounted for using regression approaches, as BW gain and gain:feed differ as intake of a limiting nutrient increases. Therefore, efficacy can be underestimated simply due to reduced intake of the limiting nutrient, rather than reduced efficacy of the test source.

In the study by Elkin et al. (1980), feed intake and subsequently Phe + Tyr intake can be calculated using the BW gain and gain:feed information provided in their third experiment. When Phe + Tyr was below the requirement (70 or 85% of the requirement), feed intake for Phe treatments was 40 to 94 g/chicken lower compared with the equivalent treatment containing both Phe + Tyr. If the Phe + Tyr intake is then calculated and BW gain is regressed on Phe + Tyr intake, the estimated efficacy of Phe as a source of Tyr on a weight basis is 98.3%. This estimated value from the data provided by Elkin et al. (1980) is almost identical to the estimated efficacy obtained herein of 97.9% on a weight basis (Table 7). Therefore, data obtained by Sasse and Baker (1972) and Elkin et al. (1980) for older genetic lines of chickens is in agreement with this study conducted using modern genetic lines of broiler chickens. Overall, both old and new studies confirm that Phe can be efficiently utilized as a source of Tyr in chickens.

Conclusion

In conclusion, Phe was highly efficacious as a Tyr source on both a weight (97.9%) and molar (96.5%) basis. Thus, it can be reasonably suggested that there should be no consequence of applying both a Phe and a Phe + Tyr minimum constraint for the formulation of reduced CP broiler diets, regardless of how much of the Phe + Tyr requirement is accounted for by L-Phe. Should it become economically favorable to include feed-grade AA beyond L-Ile, ideal ratios for Phe and Phe + Tyr, as well as their position in order of AA limitation, should be investigated further. Data herein provides initial estimates of Phe and Phe + Tyr requirements for broiler chickens from purified AA diets from 12 to 19 d-of-age that can assist with the selection of AA levels to evaluate in longer broiler trials using diets that are more commercially relevant. Moreover, L-Phe should be adequate to meet Phe or Phe + Tyr requirements under the circumstance that either are feed formulation nutrient matrix pressure points.
