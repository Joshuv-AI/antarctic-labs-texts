In-hospital cardiopulmonary resuscitation with balloon occlusion of the descending aorta: protocol of the randomised controlled CPReboa study
Authors: Lisa-Marie Gaube, Esther D Sparks, Manuela Iten, Jürgen Knapp, Monika Fürholz, Joerg C Schefold, Lukas Hunziker Munsch, Matthias Hänggi, Gert Krummrey, Martin Müller, Wolf E Hautz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCardiac arrest (CA) remains a major health burden with poor survival and poor neurological outcomes despite decades of advances in resuscitation science. Although high-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation is essential, it provides only limited cerebral and coronary perfusion, and current strategies relying on high-dose epinephrine may adversely affect cerebral microcirculation. Resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta (REBOA) has emerged as a potential adjunct to improve coronary and cerebral perfusion without compromising microvascular blood flow. Although animal studies and small human case series suggest physiological and clinical benefits of REBOA in CA, randomised clinical trials are lacking.Methods and analysisThis prospective, randomised controlled, single-centre clinical trial investigates the effect of REBOA during the treatment of CA. The planned study duration is 36 months, with a total of 98 patients to be randomised to either standard advanced life support (ALS) or ALS plus REBOA. Adult patients with an in-hospital CA, successful placement of a femoral artery introducer sheath and any electrical cardiac activity in the initial rhythm analysis are eligible for inclusion. Exclusion criteria comprise traumatic CA, asystole as the initial rhythm, pregnancy and CA occurring on intensive care units, in the operating room or in cardiac catheter laboratory. The primary outcome is sustained return of spontaneous circulation lasting for at least 20 min. Secondary and exploratory outcomes include survival, neurological outcome, changes in aortic blood pressure, end-tidal CO₂ and near-infrared spectroscopy values as well as causes of death and vascular complications related to the intervention. Statistical analyses will be performed on a modified intention-to-treat basis.Ethics and disseminationThe study protocol (Version 2.1, 11.02.2026) was approved by the Cantonal Ethics Committee of Cantone Bern (2025-D0108). Study results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals.Trial registration numberNCT07434726.Keywords: Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation; Emergency Service, Hospital; Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest

Introduction

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest

Cardiac arrest (CA) is a major public health burden.1–3 In Switzerland, out-of-hospital CA (OHCA) occurs in 76 out of 100 000 inhabitants every year4 of which two-thirds receive resuscitation attempts.5 Decades of research as well as treatment guidelines, public education and professional emergency medical services (EMS) aimed at increasing survival rates and have improved outcome in patients suffering from OHCA. However, despite all efforts, OHCA remains the third leading cause of death in Europe,4 and outcomes are poor. Today, <10% of patients in OHCA receiving cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) survive to hospital discharge.2–5 Survival with a favourable neurological outcome is achieved even less frequently.6 7 High-quality chest compressions are a prerequisite for return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) and good neurological outcome after CA because during time of arrest, the chest compressions provide blood flow and thus oxygenation to the heart and brain. However, even optimally performed CPR generates only up to 30% of normal cerebral and coronary perfusion.8 Coronary blood flow is indispensable for a possible ROSC.9 10 Current treatment strategies to achieve ROSC during CA further include a prominent role for the frequent and high-dose administration of epinephrine, which is known to impair microvascular cerebral blood flow and potentially worsen brain injury due to ischaemia.11–14 Therefore, better strategies to achieve ROSC during advanced cardiac life support (ACLS) are required.

Resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta in cardiac arrest

Resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta (REBOA) has been suggested as one such strategy to improve CA therapy. REBOA was primarily developed as a technique to stop massive haemorrhage in trauma patients.15 16 In non-traumatic CA, the use of REBOA can increase the mean arterial pressure (MAP), coronary and cerebral blood flow and perfusion pressure and therefore may contribute to achieving ROSC and a favourable neurological outcome.17–21 REBOA has therefore been termed ‘mechanical epinephrine’ because it achieves similar effects in the circulation as epinephrine but does not seem to impair cerebral microcirculation.22 Animal studies as well as case series in humans of REBOA in non-traumatic CA indeed demonstrated an increase in carotid blood flow and cerebral regional oxygen saturation as well as a reduced cerebral vascular resistance.23–25 These findings suggest that REBOA could be a beneficial addition to conventional advanced life support (ALS) in sustained CA.

Feasibility in the in-hospital cardiac arrest settings

However, the establishment of REBOA in OHCA under ongoing CPR necessitates extensive infrastructure in a time-critical and highly complex situation. A small case series demonstrated feasibility of REBOA implementation in this setting, although with considerable effort and mostly in patients with OHCA transported to a hospital.24 26 Here, REBOA can be done, but is likely to be late. One article currently in press reports on the results of the REBOARREST trial, a randomised controlled trial (RCT) in patients with OHCA randomised to receive ACLS alone or ACLS+REBOA. The authors did not find any significant benefit of one group over the other with respect to sustained ROSC, but their data confirm the difficulty in investigating REBOA in the OHCA setting. The median time from arrest to aortic occlusion was 47 min in the intervention group.27

We therefore suggest that the effect of REBOA, before being tried in OHCA with its complex logistical challenges, should be evaluated in such as transport in-hospital CA (IHCA) first, where most logistical challenges of the OHCA setting can be addressed much easier or are simply inexistent, such as transport. Evaluating REBOA in IHCA may therefore provide a benchmark of the upper limit of potential benefit of the intervention in CA. The findings and procedural requirements for the use of REBOA observed in this trial will inform an RCT for OHCA to further investigate the potential benefit on survival and neurological outcome or render such a trial unnecessary if a benefit of REBOA cannot even be established in the logistically less challenging IHCA setting.

Preclinical evidence

Several studies have examined the effect of continuous balloon occlusion of the aorta during non-traumatic CA in animal models. Occlusion of the aorta with and without selective aortic arch perfusion increases coronary artery blood flow and perfusion pressure19 21 28–32 as well as cerebral perfusion pressure and blood flow,

19 21 23 28 30 33–35 leading to improved rates of ROSC, 24-hour and 48-hour survival and neurological function in animals.19 29 31 33 34 To our knowledge, no conflicting data that fail to show the described effects have been published, leading to the suggestion that further research should concentrate on the question whether these promising findings also apply to humans.17 18 36–38

Clinical evidence to date

Clinical evidence regarding REBOA in non-traumatic CA is currently limited to case reports39 40 and smaller feasibility studies.24 26 Gamberini et al were able to establish balloon occlusion in 18 out of 20 patients with refractory OHCA. Eleven of these had suffered a non-traumatic CA. Sustained ROSC was achieved in five patients (four of whom were in non-traumatic CA). Again, the long duration of an average of 46 min from EMS alert to REBOA placement may have contributed to the fact that four patients were declared brain dead and another died later from recurrent CA.41

Brede et al demonstrated in a simulation training programme that the implantation of a REBOA in non-traumatic OHCA in a prehospital setting can be performed within an adequate time frame and does not negatively impact the quality of ALS measures.42 A feasibility study by the same group on the use of REBOA in non-traumatic OHCA followed in the same year. REBOA was successful for all 10 patients, with ROSC achieved in 60% and one patient survived past 30 days. The significant increase in end-tidal CO2 indicated improved organ perfusion during aortic occlusion. No negative impact on the ALS quality was also observed.26

A feasibility study on the use of REBOA in OHCA published by our team in 2020 demonstrated successful occlusion in 9 out of 15 patients who reached the emergency department (ED) in refractory CA. ROSC was achieved in two out of nine patients shortly after aortic occlusion. One possible explanation for the limited effect in this study could be that, on average, the patients had already been in CA for nearly an hour by the time aortic occlusion was initiated.24 Studies from Korea further indicate that among 15 patients with OHCA who underwent concurrent central venous catheter and REBOA placement, coronary perfusion pressure increased substantially within minutes after aortic occlusion.25

We currently know of six studies investigating the use of REBOA in non-traumatic CA (table 1). Four of these are already recruiting. Only the large multicentre study planned in Korea and the REBOARREST trial from Norway plan to investigate the use of REBOA in OHCA with substantial patient numbers. Our Swiss study so far has been able to include only two patients in 2 years. A key challenge of that study is the difficulty of getting a dedicated study team to the site of the OHCA without delaying treatment at the scene or transport of the patient.

Thus far, no prospective randomised trials have evaluated the effect of REBOA on CA. As shown in Section "Feasibility in the in-hospital cardiac arrest settings", the establishment of REBOA in OHCA under ongoing CPR necessitates extensive infrastructure in a time critical and highly complex situation or risks coming too late (or both).

We therefore suggest that the effect of REBOA, before being attempted in OHCA, should be evaluated in IHCA first, where most logistical challenges of the OHCA setting can be addressed much easier or are simply inexistent. Evaluating REBOA in IHCA may therefore provide a benchmark of the upper limit of potential benefit of the intervention.

Objectives

The overall objective of the study is to investigate whether aortic occlusion improves ROSC rates and survival in patients suffering from non-traumatic CA and explore changes of blood pressure and end-tidal CO2 in patients randomised to the intervention.

The primary objective is to investigate the effect of REBOA on successful resuscitation (defined as sustained ROSC >20 min). The secondary objective is to determine survival to hospital discharge, neurological outcome and survival to 6 months. Exploratory outcomes in the intervention group include the effect of endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta on common indicators of CPR quality, such as blood pressure and end-tidal CO2 at 1, 5 and 10 min after aortic occlusion compared with immediately before occlusion and changes in brain perfusion measured by near-infrared spectroscopy. We further aim to understand long-term effects of the intervention. The safety objective is to assess safety of the REBOA under ongoing CRP in IHCA and report device and access-related problems and complications as well as neurological outcomes of survivors.

Methods and analysis

Clinical investigation design

This single-centre prospective randomised controlled clinical trial, in an in-hospital setting where placement of a CE-certified medical device (pREBOA-PRO Catheter) for temporary endovascular occlusion of the aorta, will enrol patients suffering a non-traumatic IHCA over 36 months. Patients qualifying for the study are included when no ROSC is achieved with basic life support (BLS)/ALS provided according to the current guidelines until a femoral arterial sheath has been successfully placed during resuscitation and if there are no contraindications (see exclusion criteria below).

Pre-study/feasibility

Feasibility was tested by retrospectively analysing IHCA at Inselspital. We obtained data from our data warehouse. Data included all patient files from 1 January 2020 until 23 March 2024 that had a CA or resuscitation documented in their electronic record. All patients suffering from IHCA during that period were screened using the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and their cases were analysed. During the time span, 113 IHCA were identified (figure 1). None of the patients were pregnant during the time of CA, and none had traumatic CA. Moreover, 21 patients who had asystole as primary rhythm were excluded. After excluding patients with a do-not-resuscitate (DNR) order (n=10), 82 patients remained as potential REBOA candidates.

Patient flow chart of the feasibility study. CA, cardiac arrest; ICU, intensive care unit; IHCA, in-hospital cardiac arrest; OHCA, out-of-hospital cardiac arrest; OR, operating room.

During the analysed period, 76 of 113 patients suffering from IHCA were male and 37 females. The mean age was 71.4 years. Five patients were outpatients suffering a CA, for example, during outpatient chemotherapy. 79 patients had IHCA on the ward, 24 in the intermediate-care unit (IMCU) and 10 had a CA elsewhere on hospital grounds.

Almost two-thirds of patients suffering from IHCA had a non-shockable initial rhythm, with pulseless electrical activity being the most frequent primary rhythm during CA (40.7%). 21 patients had asystole as their primary rhythm, and in 14, the first rhythm was unknown or not documented.

When excluding patients with a DNR order (n=10), 72.8% of patients with IHCA achieved ROSC at some stage of ALS measures. A bit over half of those were admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU). Ultimately, only about a third of patients (35.9%) survived to hospital discharge and to 30 days after CA.

A cardiac problem was the suspected cause of death in 57 patients and a pulmonary issue in another eight patients. 14 patients died from aspiration and/or hypoxia. Anaphylactic or septic shock was the most likely cause of death in eight fatal IHCA cases, while seven patients died from other causes.

Hospitals operated under COVID-19 restrictions throughout much of the study period. This would have had an impact on the number of inpatients and consequently the frequency of IHCA. Therefore, a slightly higher rate of IHCA is to be expected in the planned prospective trial.

Sample size

Data to support sample size estimation for this study are limited. One recently published RCT in Norway, the REBOARREST trial, investigates the use of REBOA in OHCA and provides a published study protocol.43 The authors postulate a risk ratio of 2 for the primary endpoint sustained ROSC >20 min. Based on results of our feasibility study, the ROSC rate for IHCA is approximately 70% (not necessarily sustained ROSC), and the admission rate for this patient population to ICU is approximately 41%, suggesting that the rate of sustained ROSC lies between these values.

To adopt a more conservative approach, we attenuated the risk ratio proposed by Brede et al43 and recalculated the required sample size accordingly. A total of 49 patients per group (n=98 total) would be required to detect a 25% absolute difference in sustained ROSC (defined as ROSC lasting >20 min), assuming a control group incidence of 60%, risk ratio of 1.4, power of 80% and two-sided alpha level of 0.05 and 1:1 randomisation.

Study procedure

Standard resuscitation procedure during in-hospital cardiac arrest

If an IHCA on a normal ward or in the public area of the hospital is identified, the resuscitation team is alerted according to the hospital’s standard protocol. The in-hospital resuscitation team initiates ALS measures as soon as they reach the patient in CA. Decisions regarding the course, continuation or cessation of resuscitation efforts or the use of advanced treatment strategies such as extracorporeal CPR (eCPR) are made by the resuscitation team based on hospital standards and are not affected by this study. Femoral arterial access and arterial blood gas analysis are part of standard monitoring in advanced resuscitation care.

The CPReboa study team consists of at least two members and is alerted in addition to the hospital’s resuscitation team. At least one of them is an experienced clinician specifically trained in the study procedures. The study team takes over arterial cannulation typically performed by resuscitation teams. After successful placement of the arterial access sheath, arterial blood pressure is measured at the side port of the sheath and an arterial blood gas analysis is obtained.

Study specific procedures

After femoral arterial access is established, inclusion and exclusion criteria for the study will be assessed by the REBOA study team. Patients who will fulfil all the inclusion criteria and do not meet the exclusion criteria will be included into the study. A pre-made, numbered sealed envelope will be opened to allocate patients to either the intervention or the control group. This is marked as timepoint T0. In the control group, standard ALS will continue to be performed according to the resuscitation team in charge of the patient. The REBOA study team will assist these efforts under the direction of the resuscitation team. In the intervention group, a REBOA catheter will be placed through the arterial sheath into the femoral artery and advanced into the descending thoracic aorta. Once the catheter is positioned (T1), a second arterial pressure measurement line will continuously monitor blood pressure at the tip of the catheter, starting before the occlusion of the aorta. Then, the balloon will be inflated with the amount of NaCl 0.9% needed to obtain complete occlusion of the aorta, which will be marked T2. Complete occlusion of the aorta will be verified by cessation of the pulsatile blood pressure curve at the femoral arterial access sheath while pulsatility of the blood pressure curve at the catheter’s tip remains. After successful REBOA placement and balloon inflation, ALS will be continued for at least 5 min (T3).

Thereafter, all decisions regarding further treatment or cessation of resuscitation will be made by the treating resuscitation team according to standard clinical practice in both intervention and control groups. After ROSC in the intervention group, stepwise balloon deflation will be performed by removing volume from the balloon in mL-wise steps and under close monitoring of the arterial blood pressure. If a patient in the intervention group re-arrests during or after balloon deflation, the balloon may be inflated again at the discretion of the study physician a maximum of two additional times. In this case, T1 remains the same, re-occlusion of the aorta will be recorded as T1’ (or T1’’ for second re-occlusion). For patients in the intervention group who develop a balloon-dependent circulation, a careful deflation strategy will be attempted in conjunction with physiology-guided decision making, involving partial occlusion, management of catecholamines, cardiovascular support such as Impella or eCPR and revascularisation.

If ROSC can be achieved with the procedure, we expect it to occur immediately or shortly after balloon insufflation. Therefore, a follow-up period of 10 min after aortic occlusion seems practical to document physiological changes or ROSC.

Further medical treatment as well as concurrent treatment during the study intervention will be carried out by the resuscitation team and subsequently by the intensive care unit staff in the event of ROSC according to standard protocols and guidelines and in the same manner as for patients outside the study. Specifically, if the resuscitation team in charge of the patient decides to proceed to eCPR at any point during the resuscitation, the femoral arterial access obtained will be used for eCPR cannulation and thus potentially speed up eCPR installation. For patients randomised to the intervention group and intended to receive eCPR, REBOA will be removed after venous cannulation is obtained to facilitate arterial cannulation.

Although previous simulation trainings suggest REBOA to be established before any potential eCPR, if and when study procedures conflict with eCPR, eCPR will take priority.

Vital signs assessment

Blood pressure measurement is routinely performed invasively via the femoral arterial sheath using a transducer system. A second blood pressure measurement is performed invasively at the catheter tip using the designated port of the REBOA catheter and a second transducer system in the intervention group.

End-tidal CO2 measurement, the standard CO2 cuvette of the defibrillator, typically used in ALS, is used for patients who are intubated. Cerebral oxygenation is assessed non-invasively with near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) using adhesive electrodes placed on the patient’s forehead, employing a portable NIRS monitor. All vital signs will be logged in real time and exported in full time resolution into the study database later. Vital signs for discrete timepoints (inclusion, randomisation and 1, 5, 10 and 30 min after randomisation) will be abstracted by trained study nurses to reflect the measurements at these timepoints.

Quality control

A written Standard Operating Procedure including working instructions for the safe handling of the REBOA catheter will be handed to all study staff. Appropriate training in handling the REBOA catheter beforehand by the investigators responsible for conducting the trial is mandatory. The study intervention may only be performed by qualified personnel experienced with vascular puncture and REBOA. An independent Data Safety Monitoring Committee will oversee trial conduct and assess trial safety at predefined timepoints.

Data management

For each patient included, a code will be generated using a consecutive number. The data detailed in the study protocol will be first gathered into a tablet-based electronic Case Report Form (representing the source data) for those data not collected in the hospital’s electronic health record. Thereafter, coded data as well as all data available from the hospital’s electronic record will be entered into the study database RedCap, a password-protected, full-audit log database provided by the Clinical Trials Unit of Bern University (for data codebook, see online supplemental file 1)

Randomisation and blinding

Trial participants will be randomised 1:1 using variable block sizes generated by an independent, blinded biostatistician. Allocation concealment will be ensured using sequentially numbered, sealed envelopes, with the randomisation list and block sizes accessible only to the statistician. After study inclusion, group assignment will be determined by opening the next concealed envelope carried by the study team to the resuscitation site, containing the study ID and corresponding treatment allocation.

This is an open-label study because investigators cannot be blinded to the study procedure. However, study staff assessing long-term outcomes will be blinded towards the patient group allocation, as will be the trial statistician. The members of the resuscitation team and the REBOA team are predefined by a duty roster and are not selected based on the individual IHCA case.

Follow-up

Follow-up assessments will include survival status (alive yes/no) during ICU stay (TI), at hospital discharge (TD), at 30 days (TM) and at 6 months (TF). Neurological outcomes will be evaluated at TD and TF using the Cerebral Performance Category, modified Rankin scale and EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level questionnaire. Days alive and outside the ICU will be recorded at TD and TM. In non-survivors, causes of death will be documented at TI, TD and TF. Cerebral imaging findings, if available, and adverse events will be assessed at TD and TF. Because adverse events are common in patients who are resuscitated, we will specifically monitor patients for access site complications such as haematoma or vascular complications such as dissection, lower limb ischaemia, infection and any complications pertaining to the aorta.

Consent procedure

Gathering informed consent from patients in CA is not possible. Because ongoing CPR and the intervention evaluated in this trial are extremely time-sensitive, there will also be no time to contact family members or next of kin to obtain a statement of wishes expressed by the patient while he or she was in a state of capacity. Therefore, the physician member of the study team will ensure that there will be no concerns with respect to patients’ interests within the team attending to the CA (usually 2–3 nurses and at least one additional physician not involved in the study). Deferred consent will later be obtained from survivors who regain capacity to consent. In non-survivors and survivors who do not regain capacity to consent before hospital discharge, relatives will be informed about study participation and study procedures. In accordance with Swiss legislation and the regulatory approval of this trial, no deferred consent will be obtained from relatives in non-survivors or survivors who do not regain capacity to consent to mitigate the very high risk of survivorship bias in this trial. This procedure was approved by the Cantonal Ethics Committee of Berne. It accounts for the study characteristics and further aims to reduce the burden on relatives of study participants who are already burdened with the unexpected CA of the patient and whose consent would only relate to the use of data already obtained rather than study participation itself, because all study procedures have already been concluded when relatives are informed.

Statistical analysis

We hypothesise that ALS with REBOA will result in more sustained ROSC than ALS alone. All analyses will be conducted according to modified intention-to-treat principle and will be performed in Stata/MP V.18. The efficacy population will include all randomised patients (intention to treat), except for patients who were in asystole as the primary rhythm (modification). Those patients would represent an erroneous inclusion, as asystole as initial rhythm is an exclusion criterion.

Baseline characteristics will be summarised using appropriate descriptive statistics: categorical variables as counts and percentages and continuous variables as mean±SD deviation or median with IQR, depending on the normality of the distribution.

Primary analysis

The primary analysis will use a Bayesian logistic regression model with treatment as the only predictor. In this model, the log odds of sustained ROSC are expressed as a linear function of the treatment group. Results will be presented as the mean of the posterior distribution, together with 95% credible intervals for the OR. We will also report the posterior probability that the OR is >1 (indicating that REBOA improves the outcome) and the probability that it is <1 (indicating possible harm). No formal probability threshold for declaring success will be used, as this is an exploratory efficacy trial.

To improve precision and assess robustness to chance imbalance, we will refit the model adjusting for initial rhythm (shockable vs non-shockable).

Because REBOA is operator-dependent, we will further evaluate potential learning effects. For this sensitivity analysis addressing the learning curve, we will include the logarithm of the operator-specific case order as a covariate in the Bayesian logistic regression model. In this model, the log odds of sustained ROSC will be expressed as a function of treatment, the logarithm of case order. This approach allows us to assess whether increasing operator experience influences the observed treatment effect.

Secondary analysis

Secondary endpoints will be analysed using Bayesian generalised linear models appropriate to the type of outcome. Binary outcomes, such as survival, will be analysed using Bayesian logistic regression with the same prior structure as in the primary analysis. Time-to-event outcomes, such as survival to hospital discharge, will be analysed using Bayesian proportional hazards or accelerated failure-time models.

Count outcomes, such as the number of complications, will be modelled using Bayesian Poisson or negative binomial regression as appropriate. Continuous outcomes, such as hospital or ICU length of stay, will be analysed using Bayesian linear regression. All results will be presented with 95% credible intervals, and no adjustment will be made for multiple comparisons, as these secondary analyses are exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Patients receiving eCPR will be analysed as randomised (intention to treat). An exploratory analysis will assess the ability of REBOA to serve as a bridge to eCPR and as an enabler of successful eCPR (ie, eCPR with intact neurological survival).

Missing data

Missing data for the primary endpoint (sustained ROSC ≥20 min) are expected to be minimal because the outcome is assessed during resuscitation and documented in real time; therefore, the primary analysis will be performed in the modified intention-to-treat population using available data without imputation, while missing baseline covariate data used in adjusted models will be handled using multiple imputation with chained equations. For secondary and follow-up outcomes, the extent of missing data will be described and, if relevant, addressed using multiple imputation and sensitivity analyses including per-protocol and as-treated analyses, conservative assumptions for missing outcomes, and mixed-effects models for repeated physiological measurements.

Methodological limitations

The single-centre design of the study is a current key shortcoming and limits generalisability of results. We address this limitation by actively searching for additional suitable study sites. Exclusion of patients in the ICU, operating room and cardiac catheterisation laboratory may systematically remove patients with the highest likelihood of successful early intervention and favourable neurological recovery. We deliberately chose to exclude these patients precisely because of this characteristic to ensure our findings can be used to inform future research in the OHCA setting. However, patients with IHCA are more likely to have complex underlying comorbidities, non-cardiac aetiologies of arrest and advanced systemic illness compared with patients with OHCA, which limits the direct applicability of our trial results to the OHCA setting.

Ethics and dissemination

The CPReboa trial protocol (Version 2.1, 11.02.2026) has received regulatory approval from Cantonal Ethics Committee of the Canton of Bern (2025-D0108) and can be assessed at http://cpreboa.org. The trial is registered at https://clinicaltrials.gov (NCT07434726). The reported feasibility study was deemed exempt from full ethical review by the same committee (Req-2024-00644).

All individual deidentified participant data (including data dictionaries) collected in this trial will be shared on reasonable request with researchers eligible to work with coded individual biological, non-genetic health data under Swiss legislation. Eligibility will be determined by Cantonal Ethics Committee of Bern. This determination will require submission of a research plan to the ethics committee. The research plan must include the name and contact information of the responsible researcher, a research question, a detailed description of the planned analyses and a data handling section. Data will be provided as electronic files in STATA file format as exported from the RedCap study database.

Data will become available on publication of the trial results and remain available for 5 years thereafter. Requests for data sharing should be addressed to the corresponding author of the relevant publication or the trial’s sponsor. The trial’s study protocol is available on the trial website at http://cpreboa.org. The statistical analysis plan will become available on the trial website and in the Zenodo registry before inclusion of the last patient into the trial. The study findings will be published at clinicaltrials.gov and in a peer-reviewed scientific publication.
