IdeaBlocks: Expressing and Reusing Exploratory Intents for Design Exploration with Generative AI CCS: Human-centered computing Interactive systems and tools
DaEun Choi, Kihoon Son, Jaesang Yu, Hyunjoon Jung, Juho Kim

Abstract
. Generative AI opens new possibilities for design exploration by rapidly generating images aligned with user goals. However, our formative study (N=7) revealed three key limitations hindering designers’ broad and efficient exploration when interacting with these models. These include difficulty expressing open-ended exploratory intent, lack of continuity in exploration, and limited support for reusing or iterating on previous ideas. We propose IdeaBlocks, where users can express their exploratory intents to generative AI with structured input and modularize them into Exploration Blocks . These blocks can be chained for continuous, non-linear exploration and reused across contexts, enabling broad exploration without losing creative momentum. Our user study with 12 designers showed that participants using IdeaBlocks explored 2.13 times more images with 12.5% greater visual diversity than the baseline. They also developed ideas in more iterative and continuous patterns, such as branching, chaining, and revisiting ideas. We discuss design implications for future tools to better balance divergent and convergent support during different phases of exploration, and to capture and leverage exploratory intents more effectively.

1.
Introduction

Design exploration is an essential step in creative process
(
Eckert and Stacey 2000
)
. Designers collect diverse references, discover what they like, and use those inspirations to develop optimal solutions
(
Herring et al. 2009
;
Lee et al. 2010
;
Ritchie et al. 2011
;
Müller-Wienbergen et al. 2011
)
. This exploration process involves an iterative cycle between divergent and convergent thinking
(
Brophy 2001
;
Liu et al. 2003
;
and 2016
)
.
During the divergence phase, designers aim to consider as many possibilities as possible. Otherwise, they risk falling into a limited solution space, commonly known as design fixation
(
Jansson and Smith 1991
;
Cross 2004
)
.
In the convergence phase, designers want to identify what they like, integrate them, and refine them into a single concrete outcome
(
Finke et al. 1992
)
.
Previous research has supported this iterative process by providing tools that help users broadly explore alternatives
(
Cai et al. 2023
;
Zhang and Banovic 2021
;
Kang et al. 2021
)
, easily navigate vast design spaces
(
Matejka et al. 2018
;
Suh et al. 2024
)
, and merge multiple inspirations into a single outcome
(
Choi et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2023a
)
.

Recent advancements in generative AI have introduced new opportunities to further enhance design exploration. Image generation models, in particular, enable designers to rapidly produce tailored visual content from single prompts, supporting fast iteration and convergence when their design goal is clearly defined.
However, recent studies reveal that current interactions with these models often fall short in fostering creative divergence.
One major reason is text-based prompting, a predominant mode of interaction. Designers often struggle to articulate abstract concepts clearly through text, causing models to interpret ambiguous prompts in literal or conventional ways, resulting in narrow and predictable results
(
Ko et al. 2023
)
. It can also cause users to be confined to initial outputs, reinforcing design fixation
(
Wadinambiarachchi et al. 2024
)
.
Therefore, in this paper, we aim to investigate how generative AI can better support divergent design exploration, while preserving or even enhancing the benefits of efficiency.

While recent generative models have the technical capability to support both divergent and convergent exploration, we hypothesized that limitations lie in how users interact with them. To examine this, we conducted a formative study with 7 participants, focusing on their difficulties in expressing, evolving, and managing their exploration intentions when using generative models. Our findings revealed three key challenges.
First, participants struggled to express their open-ended
exploratory intent
. They often didn’t have clearly defined goals at early exploration stages and only had rough ideas about areas of interest and potential direction or range of the exploration. Yet, existing tools offered no way to articulate such open-ended intent, making it hard to steer the model toward broad exploration.
Second, participants struggled to make incremental progress while preserving previously successful outcomes. Without a way to isolate and build on successful elements, users felt hesitant to explore further and get to an early fixation on suboptimal outputs.
Third, there was a lack of support for reusing or evolving prior intentions over time. As explorations grew more complex, users found it difficult to recall past ideas or revisit promising directions. Without a structured way to return to earlier exploration, users often restarted from scratch, limiting iterative refinement and disrupting creative flow.

To address these challenges, we propose IdeaBlocks, a system that enables users to structure their exploratory intents into modular units called
Exploration Blocks
and interact with them on a canvas-based interface. Each
Exploration Block
encapsulates a user’s area of interest, direction, and scope of exploration, providing a flexible way to interact with generative AI models. Users can freely manipulate and connect these blocks on a canvas to form non-linear exploration paths, supporting broad, continuous, and iterative idea development over time. IdeaBlocks also provides features for reusing previously created blocks and paths, enabling designers to efficiently revisit, adapt, and extend their ideas. By externalizing exploratory intents into modular and manipulable objects, IdeaBlocks supports users in organizing and navigating their creative thinking process in more breadth and efficiency.

We conducted a within-subjects study with 12 designers comparing IdeaBlocks with a prompt-based baseline system without intent structuring or reuse support.
Our results demonstrated that participants using IdeaBlocks engaged in more efficient and diverse exploration. Specifically, participants gave 1.77 times more inputs to the model, facilitated by easier expression of their exploratory intent, and they could generate 2.13 times more images within the same given time. Their image generation rate increased progressively over time, indicating more sustained exploration driven by active reuse of previous intentions. They also produced 12.5% more visually diverse outcomes and reported higher satisfaction with both the number and diversity of their results. Linkography analysis
(
Goldschmidt 2014
)
additionally revealed that their exploration was more structured and connected with higher entropy, indicating IdeaBlocks encourages broader, more creative, and less linear idea development.
We further discuss how to enhance intent-aware design exploration tools through dynamic balancing between divergent and convergent efficiency.

Our contributions are threefold:

•

Results from a formative study providing an understanding of the limitations of using generative AI for design exploration and three design goals for the design of IdeaBlocks.

•

IdeaBlocks, a system with novel interaction techniques for generative design exploration workflows that externalizes exploratory intents into manipulable and reusable units, supporting non-linear and iterative exploration.

•

Findings from a user study demonstrating that IdeaBlocks enhances efficiency, diversity, and continuity in design exploration compared to traditional methods.

2.
Related Work

In this section, we review related research across two main areas: (1) tools that support creative design exploration and (2) systems that model and reuse user intent in generative workflows.

2.1.
Tools for Design Exploration

Design exploration plays a foundational role in the creative process, enabling designers to understand the problem space, discover relevant inspiration, and generate novel ideas
(
Shneiderman 2000
;
Eckert and Stacey 2000
)
. Designers often collect reference images from various sources, draw insights from them, and reinterpret these materials into new concepts
(
Herring et al. 2009
)
. With the rise of generative AI, recent research has investigated how generative models can support exploratory process in design workflows. Ko et al.
(
Ko et al. 2023
)
studied how graphic designers interact with large-scale text-to-image generation models (LTGMs), identifying both opportunities and limitations and offering guidelines for effective AI-assisted design tools. Building upon these insights, researchers have developed systems that integrate AI with interactive interfaces to aid design exploration.

2.1.1.
Supporting Exploration through Generation and Recombination

Many tools aim to enhance divergent exploration by allowing users to easily browse and navigate design spaces. For example, Dream Lens
(
Matejka et al. 2018
)
offers visualizations of generative design datasets for intuitive exploration. MetaMap
(
Kang et al. 2021
)
decomposes reference images to support structured and nuanced exploration. Similarly, Zhang et al.
(
Zhang and Banovic 2021
)
utilize GANs to present a diverse range of image outputs. DesignAID
(
Cai et al. 2023
)
also supports broader design space exploration using LLM and image generation models.

Another line of work supports the generation of design variations, which is a common strategy to solve ill-defined design problems
(
Simon 1996
)
. Tools such as Gem-Ni
(
Zaman et al. 2015
)
and Mixplorer
(
Kim et al. 2022b
)
enable users to generate parallel design variations efficiently and expand their creative solution space.

Other tools emphasize converting reference materials into new outputs, focusing on the convergent phase. CreativeConnect
(
Choi et al. 2024
)
, PopBlends
(
Wang et al. 2023a
)
, and Artinter
(
Chung and Adar 2023
)
support the recombination of multiple reference materials into a new visual output. FashionQ
(
Jeon et al. 2021
)
facilitates divergent and convergent thinking by externalizing cognitive operations through AI-driven visualizations. Luminate
(
Suh et al. 2024
)
supports hybrid ideation workflows by integrating generative AI with structured user interactions.

Building on these insights, IdeaBlocks adopts the principle of supporting flexible transitions between divergent and convergent ideation by modularizing exploratory intent into blocks. Unlike prior tools that treated exploration actions or outputs as monolithic, our modularized approach enables users to isolate specific aspects of their ideas to be especially valuable in iterative exploration.

2.1.2.
Managing and Navigating the Design Exploration Process

Another line of research is to help designers manage their exploration results, particularly through the use of mood boards
(
Eckert and Stacey 2000
;
Cassidy 2011
)
. Many computational systems have been proposed to help designers to build interactive mood boards, such as Funky Wall
(
Lucero 2015
)
, SemanticCollage
(
Koch et al. 2020
)
, May AI
(
Koch et al. 2019
)
, DesignPrompt
(
Peng et al. 2024
)
, and MoodCubes
(
Ivanov et al. 2022
)
.

Design ideation is inherently non-linear, often involving branching ideas, merging directions, and revisiting past work. Among prior work that supports such processes, Expandora
(
Choi et al. 2025
)
enables users to navigate prompt-generated ideas through a mindmap interface, while Graphologue
(
Jiang et al. 2023
)
visualizes LLM interactions as node-link diagrams to support contextual dialogue exploration. In addition, systems like DeckFlow
(
Croisdale et al. 2023
)
, Sensecape
(
Suh et al. 2023
)
, XCreation
(
Yan et al. 2023
)
, and OptiMuse
(
Zhou et al. 2024a
)
adopt canvas- or graph-based interfaces that help users express, organize, and reflect on ideation paths through spatial structuring and iterative manipulation.

While these systems support non-linear workflows, they often lack mechanisms for explicitly maintaining conceptual continuity across branches or managing how evolving ideas contribute to overall exploration direction. Our approach addresses these limitations by enabling users to track, reorganize, and reuse evolving design structures across iterations.

2.2.
Intent Modeling and Reusing in Creative Systems

As generative AI becomes increasingly integrated into creative workflows, designers are required to express their intentions primarily through text prompts. However, text modality often poses limitations, especially during open-ended creative ideation, where user intentions are often vague or evolve dynamically. Recent work has explored this user’s struggle with prompt articulation
(
Mahdavi Goloujeh et al. 2024
;
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
)
, highlighting the need for systems that better support the expression, resolution, and reuse of exploratory intent.

2.2.1.
Expressing and Resolving Creative Intents

Recent research has introduced systems to help users more effectively and accurately express their creative intent to generative models. GenQuery
(
Son et al. 2024b
)
utilized the generative model’s ability as an expression channel of a user’s intent during the image search process. Promptify
(
Brade et al. 2023
)
supports users in refining their creative intent through iterative prompt exploration with LLM suggestions and an image clustering interface. PromptCharm
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
supports this iterative refinement loop using multi-modal prompt engineering and refinement. DesignPrompt
(
Peng et al. 2024
)
and StyleFactory
(
Zhou et al. 2024b
)
suggest a novel way of incorporating multimodal inputs for expressing preferences over aesthetic attributes.
PromptMap
(
Adamkiewicz et al. 2025
)
and Patchview
(
Chung and Kreminski 2024
)
offer structured alternative interfaces instead of natural language prompts, providing more interpretable ways to communicate creative goals.

However, in design exploration settings, users often begin with open-ended ideas with uncertainty regarding their goals or how to articulate them clearly. Prior research has highlighted the challenges of supporting creativity when user needs are not specified enough
(
Terry and Mynatt 2002
;
Shneiderman 2007
)
. To address this, recent systems aim to resolve uncertain or evolving intent through more exploratory or proactive interactions. Hahn et al.
(
Hahn et al. 2024
)
propose a way to show the current understanding of models on the user’s prompt and let users specify them more by asking clarification questions.
CARE
(
Peng et al. 2025
)
supports the user’s personalized LLM query refinement when the user’s intention is vague through a multi-agent LLM framework.
RePrompt
(
Wang et al. 2023b
)
automatically refines emotionally ambiguous prompts to align generated images with intended expressions better and clarify the vague intent.
Dreamsheets
(
Almeda et al. 2024
)
allows users to compose spreadsheets to encourage users to explore and discover what they want during prompt engineering. Stylette
(
Kim et al. 2022a
)
suggests a way to interpret users’ high-level styling goals into low-level CSS properties and values and let them explore and apply them.

While prior works attempt to resolve vague user intent by refining the user’s input prompts, our approach embraces the inherent uncertainty and exploratory nature of early-stage ideation. Through a formative study, we analyzed how users express open-ended exploratory goals, and designed IdeaBlocks to allow them to express their ambiguous intent directly, using structured and lightweight inputs.

2.2.2.
Reifying and Reusing Creative Intents

Another line of research explores how users’ expressed intents can be retained and reused. Drawing from the instrumental interaction model
(
Beaudouin-Lafon 2000
)
, recent systems such as AI-Instruments
(
Riche et al. 2025
)
and Memolet
(
Yen and Zhao 2024
)
propose reifying prompts or interactions into tangible objects that can be manipulated, reused, or recombined. Kim et al.
(
Kim et al. 2023
)
suggested cells, generators, and lenses framework for modularizing LLM input, model, and output. IntentTagger
(
Gmeiner et al. 2025
)
also uses a notion of Intent Tags to store user intents and interact with them. This enables users to externalize their generative processes, reflect on them, and build upon them over time. IntentTuner
(
Zeng et al. 2024
)
structures user intent into reusable specifications to guide the model’s fine-tuning process.

Such approaches are also relevant in the design exploration process, where exploratory intents often reflect each designer’s broader aesthetic preferences or strategic directions that naturally carry over multiple ideas. Supporting the reuse of such intents can help them move more quickly, maintain coherence across variations, and better reflect on their evolving creative thinking. For example, Brickify
(
Shi et al. 2025
)
allows users to create persistent design tokens, facilitating the reuse and recombination of elements throughout the design process

IdeaBlocks extends this notion by focusing specifically on exploratory reuse, where the reuse of exploratory intents are across time and branches within a design space. Unlike prior work that focuses on editing or refining specific outputs,
Exploration Blocks
presents a modular unit of intent that can be chained, adapted, and reused across the long-term process of exploration.

3.
Formative Study

To better understand the limitations of using current generative AI tools in supporting early-stage design exploration, we conducted a formative study, especially focused on the challenges designers face when expressing and evolving their exploratory intent to the models.
We recruited seven participants (6 female, 1 male; age M = 24.86 and SD = 3.34) through an online recruitment post. All participants had prior experience using image generation models for design tasks and had worked on at least three graphic design projects before. Four participants held a bachelor’s degree in design, two were graduate students majoring in design, and one had no formal educational background in design. Details about the participants are in Appendix
A.1
. The study lasted for an hour, and participants were compensated approximately $17 USD.

3.1.
Study Procedure

Participants were asked to explore diverse mascot character design concepts for their company (for practitioners) or department (for students) using image generation models for 40 minutes. This task was chosen as it’s open-ended to encourage divergent thinking and, at the same time, it’s familiar to all participants with diverse backgrounds.

We provided a custom web-based image generation system to facilitate think-aloud during the study session. The system allowed users to generate four images at a time from a text prompt, modify parts of images via inpainting, edit images using text prompts (without inpainting), and request variations. We used Stable Diffusion XL
(
Podell et al. 2023
)
for image generation, InstructPix2Pix
(
Brooks et al. 2023
)
for editing, and DALL·E 2 API
1
1

1

https://openai.com/index/dall-e-2/
for inpainting and generating variation.
Whenever the participants used one of the generative features, the tool prompted them to articulate their current intentions and expectations through pop-up messages. After they looked at the results, the tool prompted them to reflect on whether the results reflected their intention well. In total, we collected 158 data points, each consisting of a participant’s expressed intent, system action, and assessment of how well the outputs aligned with their original goal.
Following the session, we conducted a 15-minute semi-structured interview to further investigate the challenges they experienced.

3.2.
Findings

We conducted a thematic analysis of the think-aloud transcripts, user inputs, and interview data to identify key challenges designers faced while exploring ideas with generative AI tools. The analysis was carried out after each participant session, and this process continued until saturation was reached.

3.2.1.
Difficulty in Expressing Exploratory Intents

We found that participants struggled to convey what they wanted to explore to the models through text prompts or other interaction methods. Design exploration often begins with an open-ended mindset, where designers have broad ideas and seek to explore diverse possibilities through generated images. In such cases, they exhibit
exploratory intents
, which we identified as consisting of three components: the
area of interest
(what to explore), the
direction
(how to explore it), and the
range
(how broadly to explore it).

For the
area of interest
and
direction
, participants could sometimes express them when they could be described textually (e.g., “cat-shaped mascot”, “laboratory background”), but this became challenging when the target of the exploration was visual in nature. For example, when exploring image styles, participants often struggled to translate their visual preferences into words. As P1 noted,
“I have a certain style in mind, but it’s hard to describe it in words.”
. Instead, they entered vague prompts such as “soft drawing style,” which lacked the specificity needed to guide the model as they wanted.

Expressing how much of a variation they wanted (
range
) in the results was even more problematic. Current interfaces offer no explicit control over how varied the outputs should be. To work around this, participants tried out generic modifiers like “various styles” or “doing something” in their prompts (P2, P3) or just used the variation function (P1, P3, P5, P6, P7) without explicit input to the model. These strategies were unreliable as they often produced outputs that were either too homogeneous or random, failing to meet their expectations for meaningful diversity aligned with their underlying intention.

3.2.2.
Lack of Continuity & Flexibility in Exploration

Designers typically explore across multiple image properties and gradually layer their decisions step-by-step. In traditional exploration, this often involves collecting reference images for each property individually, compiling them together on the mood board, and manually combining desirable elements into a final concept
(
Eckert and Stacey 2000
;
Cassidy 2011
)
. Participants using generative AI in our study desired a similar process but in a more automated way: starting with one property (e.g., character shape), choosing a preferred direction, and then moving on to another (e.g., image style) while
maintaining
the previously chosen direction.

However, participants felt like they couldn’t do this incremental exploration. During exploration, participants often encountered images with certain aspects they liked, and they wanted to build on those successes by exploring additional elements without losing what had already worked well. One common strategy was to append new descriptions about the new element to the previously used text prompt (P1, P2, P3). However, as each text-to-image generation is independent, reusing the same prompt did not reliably reproduce the previously satisfying components. As a result, the part of the image participants hoped to preserve often changed unexpectedly.
Other participants tried maintaining continuity by using inpainting on resulting images (P3, P7). This method gave them partial control over which region to change. Still, it was only effective when the exploration target could be localized to a specific area. However, many properties that the participants wanted to explore are not tied to a specific region, such as image style or character pose. This makes it difficult for participants to isolate and evolve just one conceptual property while keeping others intact.

Overall, this lack of continuity in exploration created a sense of fragility in the exploration process. Even when participants wanted to explore further, they often hesitated as they worried that it might overwrite parts they were already satisfied with. As a result, they decided to settle for their suboptimal outputs.

3.2.3.
Limited Support for Reusing and Iterating on Intentions

Participants also faced challenges in tracking and reusing their previously expressed exploratory intentions. As their exploration sessions got longer, it became increasingly difficult to remember which ideas they had already tried and what their original motivations were. P4 noted,
“It was hard to refer back to previous intentions. I couldn’t remember what idea I had in mind when generating an image, and I didn’t know how to keep track of it.”
This was because the interface provided only a linear history of prompts and outputs, and participants struggled to locate specific stages in their exploration or return to earlier concepts.

Additionally, participants desired to reuse certain intentions across the session, especially those that reflect their consistent preferences. For example, P3 remarked,
“There are overarching image style preferences that don’t change much. I wish I could express those once and apply them throughout the session. If the results are too far from them, I lose interest.”
Through observation, we observed two levels of reuse needs: (1) globally persistent preferences that apply across the session and (2) locally scoped intents specific to each idea branch. However, current tools lack mechanisms for managing or reapplying these intentions effectively, leading to inefficiencies in both divergent and convergent phases of ideation.

3.3.
Design Goals

Based on the findings, we derived three design goals (DGs) for the system to address the challenges faced by designers during the use of the generative models for efficient and broad design exploration:

•

DG1. Allowing explicit expression of
exploratory intents
by specifying the target property, direction, and range of exploration in both text and image channels. This ensures that users can explore broad possibilities aligned with their exploratory intents.

•

DG2. Enabling non-linear, step-by-step generation where new explorations build upon previously explored properties. This allows users to maintain continuity across steps, layering new ideas without losing earlier exploration.

•

DG3. Enabling the reuse of previous explorations for efficiency and creativity. The system should track how the user’s intention evolves and allow users to apply or adapt past directions to new contexts.

4.
IdeaBlocks

Based on the derived design goals, we implemented IdeaBlocks, a generative AI-based system that supports designers in creative exploration by enabling structured, iterative, and non-linear workflows.
At the core of IdeaBlocks is the concept of an
Exploration Block
—a modular unit that captures a user’s
exploratory intent
for a specific design property, including both the direction and the desired level of typicality. This structure (Figure
3
) includes the three key components of
exploratory intent
identified in our formative study: the area of interest (defined by selecting a property), the exploration direction (specified via a keyword input), and the exploration range (controlled through the typicality slider). To address the challenge of articulating visual properties and abstract directions through text alone, IdeaBlocks augments user input with system-generated textual and visual suggestions, enabling users to refine their intent by selecting, modifying, or building on representative examples.

Through this structure, IdeaBlocks enables users to explicitly define their exploratory intent and modularize it into
Exploration Blocks
(DG1). These blocks can be chained to preserve prior exploration and incrementally build the next exploration upon it, supporting continuity throughout the process (DG2). Additionally, previously explored blocks and paths are stored as a reusable format, allowing users to efficiently revisit and evolve their ideas over multiple iterations (DG3).

4.1.
Interface Walkthrough & Features

The system includes a canvas interface where users can craft and interact with
Exploration Blocks
(Figure
2
). In this section, we describe the key features of IdeaBlocks using a user scenario of John, a concept artist designing a mascot character for a music festival.

4.1.1.
Generate a List of Properties

The exploration begins by entering a design topic. Based on this, IdeaBlocks suggests 8 design properties that are relevant to the topic, using GPT-4o (See Appendix
D.1
for prompt). These properties are displayed in the left panel (Figure
2
(a)) and serve as the primary dimensions for exploration. Properties are classified as either text-based (e.g., ”Character’s Entity”) or image-based (e.g., ”Image Style”). Users can also delete the suggested properties and add custom properties on the fly to suit their specific needs.

John wants to design a mascot for the music festival organized by his team. To start, he inputs his design goal into IdeaBlocks. The system then presents a list of suggested properties to explore in the left panel, including “Mascot’s Entity”, “Image Style”, and “Background”. Each property represents a distinct creative dimension that John can explore, providing him with a structured yet flexible approach to his design process.

This allows users to flexibly control the breadth of their exploration depending on their current needs.

4.2.1
.
Users can iteratively refine these suggestions by deleting or requesting similar or distant suggestions. The generated images corresponding to each suggestion are then displayed in
Result Block
next to their respective
Exploration Block
.

Each
Exploration Block
, along with its settings and outcomes, is stored and summarized in the left panel, allowing users to track their exploration process. These stored blocks can also be reused for further exploration, which will be described in Section

To support efficient and flexible exploration, IdeaBlocks provides multiple features that enable users to revisit and reuse earlier exploratory intents (DG 3).

Exploration Blocks

Once pasted, the system automatically creates blocks with appropriate settings and continues the exploration process with those blocks automatically. This enables users to efficiently branch off from a new starting point while preserving the structure of a familiar design trajectory.

4.2.
Technical Details & Pipeline Evaluation

IdeaBlocks was built as a web-based system using ReactJS
2
2

2

https://react.dev/
and React Flow
3
3

3

https://reactflow.dev/
. IdeaBlocks also includes two pipelines for (1) translating the user’s exploratory intention into diverse suggestions and (2) generating images with previous exploration history. The technical details of these pipelines are discussed in the following sections.

4.2.1.
Exploratory Intention into System-Generated Suggestions

IdeaBlocks incorporates a multi-stage pipeline to generate suggestions based on the user’s input about the design topic, property, direction, and typicality level, tailored to either text or image properties.

Pipeline Design

First, IdeaBlocks creates a diverse pool of candidate suggestions. A two-step prompting strategy is used to systematically cover a controlled spectrum from typical to highly atypical suggestion candidates.
Given the input direction, IdeaBlocks prompts GPT-4o to generate ten alternative directions that span a range of divergence—from literal extensions of the original input direction to more distant interpretations of it (see prompts in Appendix
D.2.1
). For each alternative direction, the system queries LLM to produce multiple detailed and distinct suggestions aligned with that particular direction(see prompts in Appendix
D.2.2
). This process yields a total of 100 candidate suggestions for text-based properties (ten suggestions per direction) and 50 candidate suggestions for image-based properties (five suggestions per direction).

Next, the system filters candidate suggestions based on the typicality level specified by the user. Here, typicality is defined by how closely each suggestion aligns with the user’s input direction, measured via co-occurrence-based similarity between input direction and each candidate suggestion: for text, using GloVe
(
Gan et al. 2022
;
Pennington et al. 2014
)
; and for images, using the CLIP score
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
. Candidates are first ranked by this alignment, then evenly divided into subgroups, from which we select the one that matches the desired typicality.

Finally, from the filtered candidates, we select four representative suggestions to be given to the user via K-means clustering based on semantic similarity between each candidate. For text-based suggestions, clustering is performed using Sentence-BERT
(
Reimers and Gurevych 2019
)
text embeddings, and for image-based suggestions, we employ ViT-B/32 model-based CLIP visual embeddings
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
. The centroids of these four clusters are then presented as the final suggestions.

Pipeline Evaluation

We evaluated the system’s performance across three dimensions: (1) the relevance of suggestions to the input direction, (2) the diversity among representative suggestions, and (3) the validity of the typicality filtering of the suggestions. Our evaluation involved 400 suggestions across five distinct sets of properties and direction keywords (three text-based, two image-based).

To assess the relevance, we compared IdeaBlocks’s suggestions against two comparison sets: highly aligned and randomly generated (generated by LLM without applying our two-step approach). We calculated the embedding-based cosine similarity of those three sets relative to the original input direction. IdeaBlocks’s suggestions achieved average similarity scores that balanced relevance and inspiration (ours: 0.287 vs. aligned: 0.335, random: 0.182 for texts and 0.231 vs. aligned: 0.239, random: 0.208 for images).

We further examined whether our pipeline for selecting four representatives yields a sufficiently diverse set. We compared our pipeline’s suggestions against the random selection condition and calculated the same embedding-based similarity measure within each set. Our pipeline-selected representatives showed higher diversity than random selections, achieving average similarity scores of 0.322 vs. 0.403 for texts and 0.685 vs. 0.707 for images, confirming our system’s capability to produce relevant and diverse recommendations.

We also evaluated how well our pipeline’s assigned typicality levels align with human judgment. We recruited three raters, each with experience in at least three design projects, to annotate the typicality levels for a randomly selected set of 100 suggestions (60 text-based, 40 image-based) from the evaluation set described above. Inter-rater reliability (IRR) measured by Krippendorff’s Alpha was 0.75 for text-based and 0.69 for image-based suggestions, indicating substantial agreement. Final labels were established by averaging participant ratings. For text-based suggestions, the system’s typicality scores achieved a Pearson correlation of 0.68 and a Spearman correlation of 0.67 with human annotations—indicating a moderately strong alignment. For image-based suggestions, we observed a Pearson correlation of 0.59 and a Spearman correlation of 0.58, showing a moderate alignment. Overall, these results demonstrate our system’s ability to deliver relevant, diverse, and accurately aligned design explorations.

4.2.2.
Generating Images with Exploration History

To create images that incorporate both previous exploration history and the user’s new exploratory intent, IdeaBlocks composes prompts using a two-step LLM pipeline. First, IdeaBlocks prompts the LLM to extract detailed descriptions of previously explored properties from the most recent image in the connected block sequence (see Appendix
D.3.1
). This serves as a lightweight memory of prior visual context. Next, it retrieves details about the user’s new exploration intent: for text-based properties, it uses the provided suggestion directly; for image-based properties, it again prompts GPT-4o to generate a description of the new visual concept. Using these collected descriptions (both historical and new), GPT-4o synthesizes a text-to-image prompt by integrating key elements of each description (see prompt in Appendix
D.3.2
). This prompt is passed to DALL-E 3
4
4

4

https://openai.com/index/dall-e-3/
to generate the final image.

5.
Evaluation

We conducted a within-subjects comparative study with 12 participants to assess how well IdeaBlocks supports users to express and interact with their exploratory intents. Additionally, we analyzed its impact on users’ exploration patterns and the efficiency and diversity of their exploration outcomes.

Our study aimed to answer the following research questions:

•

RQ1. How effectively does IdeaBlocks support users in expressing and reusing their exploratory intentions?

•

RQ2. How does the use of IdeaBlocks influence users’ exploration process and patterns?

•

RQ3. How does IdeaBlocks impact the efficiency and diversity of users’ exploration?

5.1.
Participants

We recruited 12 participants (5 females, 7 males; M = 25, SD = 1.71) through an online recruitment post. All participants had a background in design, either through education or professional experience. Six participants were graduate students majoring in design, while three were final-year undergraduates in the same field. Two participants had already graduated with a design degree, and one had no formal degree but had experience working as a graphic designer. Details about the participants are in Appendix
B.2
. The study lasted about 1.5 hours, and participants were compensated approximately $34 USD.

5.2.
Baseline System

We designed a baseline system as a generative AI-based design exploration system with the same canvas-based interface but without key features that support explicitly expressing and reusing exploratory intent. The screenshot of the baseline interface is presented in Figure
9
in the Appendix.

In the baseline system, users enter a text prompt describing their desired exploration, and the system generates four corresponding images using DALL-E 3, the same image generation model as IdeaBlocks. However, unlike IdeaBlocks, the baseline does not provide structured input for specifying the direction or range of exploration. It also excluded features that support reusing prior exploration, such as the intent history sidebar and the block/path copy-and-paste functionality.

While both systems support non-linear exploration through a canvas interface, their branching mechanisms differ. In IdeaBlocks, newly added Exploration Blocks inherit the properties of linked blocks, preserving the context of prior explorations and allowing iterative refinement while maintaining exploration history. In contrast, although the baseline system allows users to add and link new blocks, it only copies the text prompt from the preceding block without retaining any contextual structure, providing no support for automatic intent chaining.
To balance the absence of IdeaBlocks’s LLM-based pipelines, such as interpreting user’s exploratory intent input, intent reuse, and contextual chaining, we provided access to ChatGPT
5
5

5

https://chat.openai.com/
in the baseline condition.
This setup allowed us to examine how IdeaBlocks’s integrated support for expressing, structuring, and reusing exploratory intent affects users’ design exploration processes compared to a more open-ended, prompt-driven approach.

5.3.
Study Procedure

Participants performed two rounds of design exploration tasks, each using both IdeaBlocks and the baseline system. The task involved generating diverse design concepts for one of two design topics:
“An illustration for a book cover of a detective novel set in a future city”
or
“A mascot for a summer night urban film festival.”
These topics are intended to not have a single, universally recognized visual representation and involve a comparable range of design elements to explore. To control for order effects, the order of both the design topic and the system use were counterbalanced across participants.

Each round began with a 10-minute tutorial on the given system, during which participants practiced with a sample design topic (
“Cute animal illustration”
) to familiarize themselves with the interface. They then proceeded with the exploration task for 20 minutes while thinking aloud to share how their ideas developed throughout the process. After completing each round, participants filled out a post-task survey. Following both sessions, we conducted a 15-minute semi-structured interview to discuss their experiences, compare the two conditions, and examine how the tools influenced their exploration process. The full set of interview questions is provided in Appendix
B.3
.

5.4.
Measures

We collected qualitative and quantitative measures to assess how the system supported users’ exploratory ideation. After each session, participants completed a post-task survey, providing 7-point Likert scale ratings on how well the system supported intent expression and reuse, efficient and diverse idea exploration process, and their satisfaction with the exploration outcomes. The survey also included the Creativity Support Index (CSI)
(
Cherry and Latulipe 2014
)
and NASA-TLX questionnaire
(
Hart and Staveland 1988
)
.
In addition to survey responses, we analyzed behavioral logs capturing timestamped user actions such as block creation, image generation, and use of other system features. We derived quantitative measures from these logs, such as the number and speed of images generated or the diversity of outputs. We also analyzed the structural characteristics of user exploration using linkography
(
Goldschmidt 2014
)
, capturing branching patterns, convergence, reuse, and connectivity between ideas.

6.
Result

Our findings indicate that IdeaBlocks significantly enhanced users’ ability to express and reuse their exploratory intent. It also supported more interconnected and structured patterns of idea exploration, resulting in greater efficiency and a broader diversity of concepts explored. We present our results in alignment with our three research questions.

We used the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for all Likert-scale survey responses. To analyze log data, we first ran a Shapiro-Wilk test for normality and applied either a paired t-test or a Wilcoxon test accordingly.

7.
Discussion

We propose IdeaBlocks, a system that helps graphic designers to explore design ideas by easily expressing and reusing their exploratory intents. Based on our findings, we suggest several design implications for future creativity support tools.

7.1.
How IdeaBlocks Changed the Design Exploration Process

Current prompt-based generative AI tools often constrain users to a monolithic and linear trial-and-error process. They struggle to directly manipulate specific parts of the image or express precise actions through text prompts
(
Masson et al. 2024
)
, making it hard to isolate, adjust, or recombine elements over time. This limits the iteration and reflection during the design exploration process.
This process is wholly different from how designers traditionally explore design ideas. Designers often use design reference platforms (e.g., Pinterest
6
6

6

https://pinterest.com/
) to gather sets of references for each design element and use mood boards to recombine them and track their progress. This modular and incremental structure supports more natural exploration, particularly in the early, open-ended ideation phase.

IdeaBlocks brings this modularity and iterations into the generative AI workflow. By allowing users to break down their intent into modular blocks for each property, chaining and reusing them, the system mirrors the flexible and iterative nature of traditional workflows.
At the same time, IdeaBlocks also has the potential to go beyond existing workflows by externalizing exploratory intent into a structured, reusable format, relieving designers from manually interpreting and linking disparate references. This reduced cognitive overhead gave users greater clarity over their evolving ideas and direction.

These features can also lead to new features designed for future creativity support tools. Since IdeaBlocks stores the underlying structure of the user’s explorations, it could be extended to provide real-time feedback based on the accumulated interaction data. For example, the system could analyze exploration patterns using linkograph-style methods (e.g.,
(
Smith et al. 2025
)
) to suggest unexplored directions or prompt users to revisit promising paths. Furthermore, systems could even learn individual exploration styles and proactively recommend the next steps or generate ideas tailored to users’ creative processes. Such directions would transform creative support tools from simple prompt-response systems into dynamic and process-aware collaborators.

7.2.
Imbalance between Divergent and Convergent Efficiency

While IdeaBlocks effectively helped users articulate their intent and explore a diverse range of ideas, our findings also revealed a key imbalance between how well it supported different stages of the creative process.
Specifically, the system effectively facilitated divergent exploration by enabling users to easily express their open-ended ideas and translate them into broad outcomes. However, it has limited support for convergent efficiency when users want to narrow down toward a specific outcome after exploring broadly.

IdeaBlocks follows a structured pipeline: users provide structured input, which the system then interprets and expands upon in a largely system-driven manner.
This enabled fast and wide exploration, as users could review more images and generate more diverse outcomes compared to the baseline. These benefits reflect high divergent efficiency, where users can explore broadly within a limited time. However, participants reported limitations in refining and converging into a specific outcome once they had a clearer vision. As the interaction was largely system-driven, users had limited control over steering suggestions beyond basic filtering (e.g., deleting or re-generating suggestions).
P8 stated,
“When I don’t have a clear idea in mind, I like the system suggestions. But once I do have a specific vision, it’s difficult to control each block precisely and get the exact image that I want.”
This suggests that while IdeaBlocks lowers the barrier to divergent exploration and iterations, it lacks sufficient support for
refinement
, which is also an essential aspect of the convergent phase of the creative process
(
Finke et al. 1992
)
.

This imbalance may explain why participants did not perceive their exploration process as significantly more efficient or creative despite the fact that they explored more outcomes with higher diversity (as reflected in the survey results). Without sufficient support for convergent efficiency, participants may have felt disconnected from the creative process, reducing their overall sense of agency and satisfaction. This echoes the findings from Stylette
(
Kim et al. 2022a
)
, VRCopilot
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
, and Kobiella et al.
(
Kobiella et al. 2024
)
, which showed that in open-ended creative tasks, lack of agency and controllability negatively affect the satisfaction about the results.

This could be addressed in future creative support systems by adding more interactive features that can be readily adjusted to users’ evolving needs. For example, letting users control the level of system autonomy dynamically (i.e., starting with system-driven recommendations but enabling finer manual adjustments when needed) would be helpful. Additionally, multimodal expression channels (e.g., sketching) can add more nuanced control to the current structured inputs (i.e., direction and typicality range) when the user desires more control over the output.

7.3.
Implications for Designing Intent-Aware Creativity Support Tools

Through the design of IdeaBlocks and user study, we suggest several design implications for future creativity support systems that aim to utilize users’ exploratory intents more effectively and flexibly.

To better support creative workflows, systems should distinguish between different types of design intent as they require distinct support strategies.
Visual properties, especially the style of the overall image, are usually related to users’ global preferences that persist across multiple ideas or projects. In our study, users frequently reused these properties via block-copying actions without modification. These visual properties can be treated as relatively stable, allowing systems to support cross-project reuse. For example, Brickify
(
Shi et al. 2025
)
, Piet
(
Shi et al. 2024
)
, and VST
(
Warner et al. 2023
)
enable users to transfer visual elements (color, styles) from one design to another. Future systems could go beyond direct transfer by inferring users’ global visual preferences from exploration patterns and allowing designers to reapply these preferences across multiple projects.

In contrast, semantic properties, which are typically delivered through text channels, were more context-dependent and often shifted entirely from one idea branch to another. For these properties, reusing was less about replicating specific content and more about reproducing past exploration behaviors in a new context. To support this, IdeaBlocks employed few-shot prompting with LLM to approximate and adapt previous intents, but future systems could support this process more precisely.
Previous work has suggested ways to analyze users’ creative activity to provide more personalized guidance
(
Son et al. 2022
;
De Peuter et al. 2023
)
. Building on this, future systems can incorporate a real-time pipeline with test-time adaptation mechanisms to learn from users’ exploration patterns and generalize users’ strategies into high-level structures, which can be transferred across different domains for reuse.

Second, future systems could benefit from capturing more contextual information than structured intent inputs. While IdeaBlocks relied on direction and typicality as primary modes of intent expression, participants’ actions often reflected more nuanced and tacit motivations. This is especially common in design domains where decision-making is primarily driven by implicit knowledge and judgment
(
Son et al. 2024a
)
. These underlying intentions are not always explicitly verbalized, but they strongly shape exploration behavior
(
Polanyi 2009
)
. Capturing such tacit cues, for example, through lightweight think-aloud prompts, could help systems better understand why users proceeded with exploration in such a manner, possibly leading to more adaptive and personalized intent-aware creative support.

Beyond the graphic design domain, the concept of modular
exploratory intent
can be extended to various creative tasks, such as writing, music production, and video editing. These domains also include iterative ideation, branching, and evolving intent. A system sharing design principles with IdeaBlocks could support broader creative exploration. For example, in writing, blocks might represent narrative directions, and in programming, they could represent different implementation approaches. This structured yet flexible representation of intent can help users externalize, recombine, and revisit ideas across sessions.

7.4.
Limitations and Future Work

Our work has several limitations that future research can address. In our user study, participants worked on each exploration task with a given system for 20 minutes, which is shorter than the usual design exploration in real-world settings. As a result, findings related to time-based behavioral patterns, such as changes in image generation rate, may differ in longer usage scenarios. Future work could integrate IdeaBlocks into actual design projects to better understand how user behaviors evolve over extended use and how they compare to those observed in controlled lab studies.

In addition, we conducted our evaluation with design students and early-career professionals, which may not reflect the behaviors and expectations of more diverse user groups, including experienced designers from the industry or individuals from non-design backgrounds. Future work could investigate how different levels of expertise, domain knowledge, or familiarity with generative tools influence how users interact with and benefit from IdeaBlocks.

Another limitation of our formative study is that it focused on a specific type of graphic design (mascot character design), which may not fully capture the needs of other design domains with distinct exploration patterns, such as UI/UX or architectural visualization. These domains involve different design constraints and workflows and may require IdeaBlocks to support additional types of inputs for exploratory intent expression. Future work should explore how the system can generalize across diverse design contexts and even extend beyond graphic design to other creative domains.

8.
Conclusion

This paper proposes IdeaBlocks, a system designed to enhance design exploration by providing a structured mechanism for expressing and reusing explorative intent. We identified three key limitations in generative AI tools: (1) the lack of explicit channels for conveying exploration strategies, (2) the difficulty in maintaining exploration continuity, and (3) the absence of reusing the previous exploration history.
To address these challenges, IdeaBlocks enables the modularized representation of exploratory intent through exploration blocks. Specifically, IdeaBlocks provides structured input mechanisms for expressing exploratory intents better, supports the chaining of blocks for continuous and parallel exploration, and facilitates context-aware copy-pasting of blocks and paths to enhance iterative design exploration. Through a comparative user study, we found that IdeaBlocks significantly improved users’ ability to express and reuse exploratory intent (RQ1), led to more structured and interconnected exploration behaviors (RQ2), and enhanced both the efficiency and diversity of generated design outcomes (RQ3). However, we also identified a gap between intent expression and fine-grained control over outputs, suggesting the need for better support in convergent refinement within creativity support systems.
