Abstract
​In the Disjoint Shortest Paths problem one is given a graph G G and a set 𝒯 = { ( s 1 , t 1 ) , … , ( s k , t k ) } \mathcal{T}\!=\!\{(s_{1},t_{1}),\ldots,(s_{k},t_{k})\} of k k vertex pairs. The question is whether there exist vertex-disjoint paths P 1 , … , P k P_{1},\ldots,P_{k} in G G so that each P i P_{i} is a shortest path between s i s_{i} and t i t_{i} . While the problem is known to be 𝖶 ⁡ [ 1 ] \mathsf{W}[1] -hard in general, we show that it is fixed-parameter tractable on planar graphs with positive edge weights. Specifically, we propose an algorithm for Planar Disjoint Shortest Paths with running time 2 𝒪 ⁡ ( k ​ log ⁡ k ) ⋅ n 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) 2^{\mathcal{O}(k\log k)}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)} . Notably, our parameter dependency is better than state-of-the-art 2 𝒪 ⁡ ( k 2 ) 2^{\mathcal{O}(k^{2})} for the Planar Disjoint Paths problem, where the sought paths are not required to be shortest paths.



1
Introduction

In the
Disjoint Paths
problem, we are given a graph
G
together with a set
\mathcal{T}=\{(s_{1},t_{1}),\ldots,(s_{k},t_{k})\}
of
k
pairs of pairwise distinct vertices, called
terminals
. The task is to decide whether there are pairwise vertex-disjoint paths
P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}
in
G
so that each
P_{i}
connects
s_{i}
with
t_{i}
. Being perhaps one of the most important computational problems of topological nature,
Disjoint Paths
was studied by Robertson and Seymour within their Graph Minors project, which resulted in establishing fixed-parameter tractability of the problem on general graphs.
More precisely, Robertson and Seymour proposed an
f(k)\cdot n^{3}
-time algorithm for a computable function
f

[
27
]
. The dependency on
n
in the time complexity has been subsequently improved to quadratic
[
20
]
, and very recently to almost linear-time
[
22
]
.

The main idea behind essentially all the contemporary algorithms for
Disjoint Paths
is the
irrelevant vertex technique
: iteratively find vertices that can be safely removed from the instance without changing the answer (hence they are called
irrelevant
), until the instance becomes so simple that it can be solved directly. Arguing that a given vertex is irrelevant typically relies on delicate rerouting arguments, which makes the technique not applicable to a range of interesting variants of the problem. In fact, some of those variants become computationally much harder, and their treatment requires other tools.

The variant that we investigate in this paper is
Disjoint Shortest Paths
. Here, we are given an instance
(G,\mathcal{T})
of
Disjoint Paths
where
G
is edge-weighted, and the task is to decide the existence of a solution
P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}
such that each
P_{i}
is a
shortest
path between
s_{i}
and
t_{i}
. Techniques based on finding irrelevant vertices are rather difficult to apply in the context of
Disjoint Shortest Paths
, because rerouting arguments used to reason about the irrelevance of a vertex typically need to modify a solution without paying attention to the lengths of the paths. As a result, the complexity of
Disjoint Shortest Paths
still remains quite an uncharted territory.

Disjoint Shortest Paths
were first studied by Eilam-Tzoreff
[
17
]
in 1998, who proved polynomial-time solvability for
k=2
. Later, Bérczi and Kobayashi
[
8
]
investigated the directed variant of the problem, and they gave a polynomial-time algorithm for
k=2
on general digraphs and an
n^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
-time algorithm for planar digraphs, thereby putting the directed planar case in the complexity class
\mathsf{XP}
. A major development was delivered by Lochet
[
23
]
, who gave an
\mathsf{XP}
algorithm, with a running time of
n^{\mathcal{O}(k^{5^{k}})}
, on general undirected graphs with unit weights.
Subsequently, Bentert, Nichterlein, Renken, and Zschoche
[
7
]
improved the exponent in the running time to
2^{\mathcal{O}(k\log k)}
.
Unfortunately, it was also proved in
[
7
,
23
]
that the problem is
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard, hence the existence of an FPT algorithm is unlikely. However, so far it was open
[
30
]
whether such an algorithm can be designed in the setting of planar graphs.

Our contribution.

In this work we answer the question stated above in the affirmative by proving the following theorem. Here
Planar Disjoint Shortest Paths
is the restriction of
Disjoint Shortest Paths
to planar undirected graphs
equipped with positive edge weights.

Theorem 1.1
.

The
Planar Disjoint Shortest Paths
problem can be solved in time
2^{\mathcal{O}(k\log k)}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
.

Remarkably, the achieved parameter dependency
2^{\mathcal{O}(k\log k)}
is better than state-of-the-art
2^{\mathcal{O}(k^{2})}
for
Planar Disjoint Paths

[
11
,
24
]
.
This suggests that the shortest-paths variant might be easier on planar graphs, in contrast to the general setting where
Disjoint Paths
is FPT but
Disjoint Shortests Paths
is
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard.
A similar phenomenon occurs for
Disjoint Paths
on directed graphs with
k=2
: the basic problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
[
18
]
whereas the shortest-paths variant is polynomial-time solvable
[
8
]
.
Observe that it is important to restrict to only positive edge weights, because allowing for zero weights would give a problem more general than basic
Disjoint Paths.

It is known that
Disjoint Shortest Paths
on undirected graphs generalizes
Disjoint Paths
on acyclic digraphs
[
2
,
23
]
.
Moreover, the corresponding reduction preserves planarity so
Planar Disjoint Shortest Paths
is at least as hard as
Disjoint Paths
on planar DAGs (
Lemma

3.4
).
As the
Disjoint Paths
problem is fixed-parameter tractable even on general planar digraphs
[
12
]
, such a reduction does not entail any meaningful lower bound.
However, it suggests that
Planar Disjoint Shortest Paths
may exhibit a behavior typical for problems on planar DAGs, and
provides a valuable hint that guides our analysis (see
Section

2
for details).

Another consequence of the aforementioned reduction that is the
Planar Edge-Disjoint Shortest Paths
problems is
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard because
Edge-Disjoint Paths
is
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard on planar DAGs
[
10
]
.
On the other hand,
Edge-Disjoint Paths
is FPT even on general graphs
[
20
]
.
Hence the requirement on the paths to be shortest makes the problem harder also in the planar edge-disjoint setting, giving yet another contrast to
Theorem

1.1
.

Amiri, Golshani, Kreutzer, and Siebertz
[
4
]
considered
Disjoint Paths
on a subclass of planar DAGs that admit an upward drawing.
They showed that this variant can be solved in time
2^{\mathcal{O}(k\log k)}\cdot n
, which improved upon the double-exponential parameter dependency for general planar digraphs
[
12
]
.
They asked whether that result can be extended to all planar DAGs.
As a direct consequence of
Theorem

1.1
and
Lemma

3.4
, we answer their question affirmatively when it comes to parameter dependency.

Corollary 1.2
.

The
Disjoint Paths
problem can be solved in time
2^{\mathcal{O}(k\log k)}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
on planar DAGs.

Other related work.

Recently, there have been a few other works investigating different aspects of the complexity of
Disjoint Shortest Paths
.
A variant of the problem with congestion was studied by Amiri and Wargalla
[
5
]
, followed by Akmal and Wein
[
3
]
. Bentert, Fomin, and Golovach
[
6
]
studied the natural optimization version, in which one does not need to connect all the terminal pairs, but rather maximize the number of pairs connected.
Very recently, Akmal, Vassilevska Williams, and Wein
[
2
]
considered the fine-grained complexity of the problem and improved several running times in the cases known to be polynomial-time solvable.

A closely related variant of the
Disjoint Paths
problem, where lengths of paths are also considered, is
Min-Sum Disjoint Paths
. In this variant, we seek a solution
P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}
to a given instance
(G,\mathcal{T})
of
Disjoint Paths
that minimizes the sum of the lengths of the paths
P_{i}
. The complexity of this variant is even more poorly understood than that of
Disjoint Shortest Paths
. It follows from the results of
[
7
,
23
]
that the problem is
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard on general graphs, but membership in
\mathsf{XP}
is currently unknown. Björklund and Husfeldt gave a randomized polynomial-time algorithm for
k=2

[
9
]
, but the polynomial-time solvability of the case
k=3
remains open, even in the setting of planar graphs. Recently, an
FPT
algorithm for the problem in a very specific class of planar graphs — grid graphs — was proposed by Mari, Mukherjee, Pilipczuk, and Sankowski
[
25
]
.
In addition, the problem becomes tractable on planar graphs with certain arrangements of terminals
[
14
,
21
,
29
]
.

2
Techniques

To outline the ideas behind our algorithm, we begin by discussing the

2^{\mathcal{O}(k^{2})}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
-time algorithm for
Planar Disjoint Paths
(not necessarily shortest) by Lokshtanov, Misra, Pilipczuk, Saurabh, and
Zehavi
[
24
]
.
To this end, we first need to go back even further in time, to the
n^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
-time algorithm by Schrijver from 1994
[
28
]
, which in fact even applies to the directed setting.
This algorithm is divided into two phases: first, one guesses the
homology class
of the solution, out of
n^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
many, and then given a suitable representation of that homology class,
finds a solution in that class (as long as one exists) via a reduction to a specific polynomial-time solvable 2-CSP problem, called
Homology Feasibility
.
Roughly speaking, two solutions
\mathcal{P},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
are homologous if
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
can be obtained from
\mathcal{P}
(and vice versa) by a series a modifications that “pull” some part of a solution through some internal face.
During that process a solution may temporarily lose vertex- or even edge-disjointedness and so it is more convenient to work with homologies of
algebraic flows
, objects more general than families of disjoint paths (see
Section

6.4
).
In essence, to enumerate the homology classes, Schrijver considered a Steiner tree
T
spanning the terminal vertices and analyzed the flow obtained by pushing all the paths onto
T
.
Then, in order to pin down the homology class of such a flow
\mathcal{P}
it is sufficient to specify for each
spinal path

Q
of
T
the number of times
\mathcal{P}
crosses
Q
, where a spinal path of
T
is a maximal subpath whose internal vertices have degree
2
.
The maximal number of such passes is
n
and the number of spinal paths
Q
to consider is
\mathcal{O}(k)
; this yields the
n^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
bound.

The main idea behind the
2^{\mathcal{O}(k^{2})}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
-time algorithm of
[
24
]
is that it suffices to inspect only
2^{\mathcal{O}(k^{2})}
homology classes.
This is challenging because a solution might “wind” around some terminal vertex,
forming a deep “spiral”, and solutions which differ in the depth of such a spiral are not homologous.
Taking into account that there are
\Omega(k)
potential sources for spirals, we may a priori obtain
n^{\Omega(k)}
different homologies.
Addressing this issue required incorporating two more techniques.
First, Adler et al.
[
1
]
had shown that one can reduce the treewidth
\mathbf{tw}
of input graph
G
to
2^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
using the irrelevant vertex technique.
This bounds the size of a maximal grid appearing in
G
and simplifies the analysis of spirals.
The second ingredient originates from the fixed-parameter algorithm for
Directed Planar Disjoint Paths
, due to Cygan, Marx, Pilipczuk, and Pilipczuk
[
12
]
.
Consider a directed plane graph
D
with outer face
C_{1}
and a distinguished internal face
C_{2}
and let
{\cal Q}
be a collection of disjoint paths connecting
C_{1}
with
C_{2}
.
Then for any other such collection of paths
\mathcal{P}
of the same size, one can reroute
\mathcal{P}
to almost match the
winding number

of
{\cal Q}
, while preserving the endpoints of the paths in
\mathcal{P}

[

, Lemma 4.8]
, see
Figure

1
.
Effectively, these two ideas reduce the problem to the case where each spinal path
Q
of the considered Steiner tree
T
has length
\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{tw})
, which in total gives the bound
\mathbf{tw}^{\mathcal{O}(k)}=2^{\mathcal{O}(k^{2})}
on the number of enumerated homology classes.

As observed by Bérczi and Kobayashi
[
8
]
, the second phase of Schrijver’s algorithm can be adapted to work with
Planar Disjoint Shortest Paths
. That is, the problem can be solved in polynomial time once we guess a correct homology class.
For
s_{i},t_{i}\in V(G)
we can define a directed acyclic graph, called the
(s_{i},t_{i})\text{-}\mathsf{dag}
, consisting of the oriented edges of
G
that lie on some shortest
(s_{i},t_{i})
-path in
G
.
Then it is sufficient to annotate the instance of
Homology Feasibility
with labels indicating that only the edges of the
(s_{i},t_{i})\text{-}\mathsf{dag}
can accommodate the path
P_{i}
.
However, the main issue remains: how to bound the number of relevant homology classes as a function of
k
. Here, the main obstacles can be phrased as follows:

The known rerouting arguments break down for the
Planar Disjoint Shortest Paths
problem, because different edges may lie on shortest paths between different pairs of terminals. (Or more formally, belong to different digraphs
(s_{i},t_{i})\text{-}\mathsf{dag}
, for
i=1,2,\ldots,k
). In particular, there is no treewidth reduction technique available for
Planar Disjoint Shortest Paths
.

Therefore, we need to design a completely new strategy to handle the “deep spirals” that contribute to the non-FPT number of homology classes that need to be considered.

A

in a graph is a path that is a shortest path between its endpoints.
. That is, every spinal path

Consider some path
P
from a solution that winds around some terminal with respect to
T

This means that
P
crosses some spinal path

of

several times in the same direction.
Since
P
is also a geodesic, this gives rise to a rather peculiar subgraph of

More formally, let
u,v\in V(Q)\cap V(P)
be two consecutive crossing points of
Q
and
P
.
Then the distance between
u
and
v
along
Q
must be equal to their distance along
P
.
Consequently, when the number of such crossings is large, then there are multiple non-equivalent (in the topological sense) ways to reach the interior of the spiral from the exterior via a shortest path: on each crossing one can choose to follow either
Q
or
P
. Now comes the key observation:

The situation described above implies the existence of a ring-shaped region
R

that covers almost the entire spiral and in which the structure of shortest
(s_{i},t_{i})
-paths is the same for each terminal pair
(s_{i},t_{i})
separated by the spiral; see
Figure

In other words, the digraphs
(s_{i},t_{i})\text{-}\mathsf{dag}
and
(s_{j},t_{j})\text{-}\mathsf{dag}
coincide inside
R
, whenever
s_{i},s_{j}
lie outside the spiral and
t_{i},t_{j}
lie inside the spiral, or vice versa.
In addition, it turns out that if
s_{i},t_{i}
are not separated by the spiral, then no shortest
(s_{i},t_{i})
-path intersects
R
.
As a consequence, we can neglect the structure of shortest paths inside
R
and just treat this part of the graph as a DAG on which the problem behaves like
Directed Planar Disjoint Paths
.
This enables us to take advantage of the rerouting argument from
[
12
]
inside the region
R
, to approximately (with constant additive error) find the winding number of the solution within
R
.

In order to turn this idea into an algorithm, we need to engineer the definition of such a “ring-shaped region”
R
(formalized later as a “
\mathsf{dag}
-
\mathsf{ring}
”) in such a way that:

(a)

it is safe to perform rerouting in
R
,

(b)

the existence of
R
is implied by a “winding behavior”,

(c)

any solution path can enter
R
only once and then it must exit
R
on the other side,

(d)

there exists a decomposition of the input graph using
\ell=\mathcal{O}(k)
pairwise-disjoint regions
R_{1},\dots,R_{\ell}
which cannot be extended by any additional region
R_{\ell+1}
,
and

(e)

such a decomposition can be effectively computed.

These properties ensure that a solution cannot form any large spirals outside the regions
R_{1},\dots,R_{\ell}
.
This enables us to extract the subgraphs where spiraling is possible and analyze them by treating their
shortest paths structures as DAGs.
We exploit this decomposition to refine the Steiner tree
T
to only use paths with “correct” winding numbers within each
R_{i}
.
By property (c), we can count the number
q
of subpaths that traverse
R_{i}
in any solution.
Next, we compute an arbitrary linkage
\mathcal{Q}
of size
q
that connects the two sides of
R_{i}
.
The rerouting argument from the work of Cygan et al.
[
12
]
ensures that
any solution can be tweaked to almost match (within constant additive error) the winding number of
\mathcal{Q}
within
R_{i}
.
Therefore, if we tweak
T
to go alongside
\mathcal{Q}
, then some solution is guaranteed to have only few “non-trivial” crossings with
T
in this area (
Figure

).

Once we are equipped with such a refined Steiner tree
T
, we show that provided there exists some solution
\mathcal{P}
, there is also a solution
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
for which number of non-trivial crossings with
T
is bounded by a function
f(k)
.
By a careful analysis, one can show that there are only
f(k)^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
many homology classes with this property, and so it is sufficient to enumerate and consider only those classes.
Notably, we are able to estimate
f(k)=k^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
,
which gives only
2^{\mathcal{O}(k\log k)}
relevant homology classes; less than
2^{\mathcal{O}(k^{2})}
for the standard
Planar Disjoint Paths
problem.
To this end, we take advantage of the fact that geodesic paths can only cross in a monotone fashion.
When
Q
is a path in
T
that is disjoint from the regions
R_{1},\dots,R_{\ell}
and
P
is a solution path, we can inspect a “crossing pattern” of
P
and
Q
.
As now we can assume that
P\cup Q
exhibits no “winding behavior”, it remains to consider the situation when
P
alternatively crosses
Q
from the ”left” and from the ”right”.
Such crossings form structures resembling “handles” and
each non-trivial handle must enclose some terminal vertex.
Thanks to the monotonicity of crossings,
the number of non-trivial handles of
P

Q
can be estimated as
\mathcal{O}(k)
(see
Figure

By considering all solution paths
P
and all paths

from

\mathcal{O}(k^{3})
.

Organization of the proof.

We begin with
Section

Then, in

Next, in

to count the number of “non-trivial crossings” between a path

\mathcal{P}
.
In

This section culminates with

Section

\mathcal{P}
.

In
Section

.

Preliminaries

We refer the reader to
[
15
]
for standard graph-theoretic terms not explicitly defined here.
We use letter
G
to denote undirected graphs and
D
for directed graphs (digraphs).
A directed acyclic graphs is called a DAG.
We denote the set of vertices as
V(G)
(resp.
V(D)
) and the set of edges (resp. arcs) as
E(G)
(resp.
A(D)
).
For two sets
V_{1},V_{2}\subseteq V(G)
, we denote by
E(V_{1},V_{2})
the set of edges in
G
with one endpoint in
V_{1}
and the other in
V_{2}
.
For an undirected graph
G
, we denote by
\vec{E}(G)
to the set of all orientations of its edges, i.e.,
\vec{E}(G)=\{(u,v),(v,u)\mid uv\in E(G)\}
.
For two sets
V_{1},V_{2}\subseteq V(G)
, we write
\vec{E}(V_{1},V_{2})
to denote the set
\{(u,v)\mid uv\in E(G),u\in V_{1},v\in V_{2}\}
.
A set
S\subseteq V(G)
is a
(V_{1},V_{2})
-separator
in
G
if
S
intersects every path connecting a vertex from
V_{1}
to a vertex in
V_{2}
.

Plane graphs and duals.

A
plane embedding
of a graph
G
is given by a mapping from
V(G)
to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and a mapping that associates with each edge
uv\in E(G)
a simple curve in the plane connecting the images of
u
and
v
, so that the curves given by two distinct edges can intersect only in a common endpoint.
A graph is called
planar
if it admits a plane embedding.
A
plane graph
is a graph with a fixed plane embedding, in which we identify the vertices with their images in the plane.
Similarly, we can consider plane multigraphs in which we allow for parallel edges and loops.
Here, the image of a loop is a simple closed curve.
A
face
in a plane graph (resp. multigraph)
G
is a maximal arc-connected subset of the plane minus the image of
G
.
We say that a vertex or an edge lies on a face
f
if its image belongs to the closure of
f
.
In every plane graph (resp. multigraph) there is exactly one face of infinite area, referred to as the
outer face
.
The set of faces of
G
will be denoted by
F(G)
.

The
dual multigraph

G^{\star}
has the face set
F(G)
of
G
as the vertex set, and for every edge
e
of
G
we introduce the
dual edge

e^{\star}
connecting the two faces lying on the two sides of
e
. (Note that these two faces might coincide in case
e
is a bridge, in which case
e^{\star}
is a loop.)
G^{\star}
admits a natural embedding in the plane in which every
f\in F(G)
is mapped to an arbitrary point chosen within
f
, and dual edges are embedded so that every edge
e\in E(G)
crosses its dual edge
e^{\star}
at a single point.

A directed multigraph
D
is called planar (resp. plane) if the underlying undirected multigraph is planar (resp. plane).
The dual
D^{\star}
of
D
is a directed plane multigraph defined analogously as above but taking into account the orientations of arcs.
An arc
a\in D
that has face
f_{1}
to the left (with respect to its orientation) and
f_{2}
to the right corresponds to the arc
a^{\star}=(f_{1},f_{2})
in
D^{\star}
.

Geometry.

For a set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
we denote its closure by
\mathsf{cl}(X)
.
For a simple closed curve
S
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
we write
\mathrm{Disc}(S)
to denote the bounded connected component of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus S
.
Given two simple closed curves
S,S^{\prime}
in the plane, we say that
S
and
S^{\prime}

cross
if both connected components of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus S
intersect
S^{\prime}
.
For two simple closed curves
S_{1},S_{2}
that do not cross, we denote by
\mathrm{Ring}(S_{1},S_{2})
the union of all connected components
X
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus(S_{1}\cup S_{2})
such that
\mathsf{cl}(X)
intersects both
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
.

Geodesics.

Given a graph
G
with positive edge weights
w\colon E(G)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
and a set
F\subseteq E(G)
,
we use
w(F)
to denote
\sum_{e\in F}w(e)
.
Also, given
s,t\in V(G)
, we use
d_{G}(s,t)
(or just
d(s,t)
when it does not lead to confusion) to denote the minimum value of
w(E(P))
over all paths
P
in
G
connecting
s
and
t
(if no such path exists, we set
d_{G}(s,t)=\infty
).

A
geodesic
in a graph
G
is a path that is a shortest path between its endpoints.
For a path
P
and
u,v\in V(P)
we denote by
P[{u,v}]
the subpath of
P
between
u
and
v
.

Observation 3.1
.

If
P
is a geodesic in a graph
G
and
u,v\in V(P)
then
P[{u,v}]
is also a geodesic.

A path
P
can be oriented in two ways.
A fixed orientation of
P
induces a linear order on
V(P)
denoted
\leqslant_{P}
.
When we refer to an oriented
(x,y)
-path
P
, we consider it with orientation for which
x
is the first element in the order
\leqslant_{P}
and
y
is the last element.
For
z\in V(P)
we abbreviate
P[\cdot,z]=P[x,z]
and
P[z,\cdot]=P[z,y]
.

Observation 3.2
.

Let
P
be an oriented shortest
(x,y)
-path in a graph
G
,
u,v\in V(P)
satisfy
x\leqslant_{P}u<_{P}v\leqslant_{P}y
, and
Q
be a shortest
(u,v)
-path in
G
.
Then the concatenation of paths
P[{x,u}]+Q+P[{v,y}]
also forms a shortest
(x,y)
-path.

We state a crucial property of geodesics in graphs with positive edge weights, which will be exploited repeatedly in the analysis of the crossing patterns.

Lemma 3.3

(Monotonicity of crossings)
.

Let
G
be an undirected graph with positive edge weights
w\colon E(G)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
Next, let
P,Q
be oriented geodesics in
G
and
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\in V(P\cap Q)
.
If
v_{1}<_{Q}v_{2}<_{Q}v_{3}
then
either
v_{1}<_{P}v_{2}<_{P}v_{3}

or
v_{1}>_{P}v_{2}>_{P}v_{3}
.

Proof.

Suppose that the claim does not hold.
Then either
v_{3}
is an internal vertex of
P[{v_{1},v_{2}}]

or
v_{1}
is an internal vertex of
P[{v_{2},v_{3}}]
.
Assume w.l.o.g. the first scenario.
Note that
P[{v_{1},v_{2}}]
is a geodesic by
Observation

3.1
.
Let
Q^{\prime}
be obtained from
Q
be replacing the subpath
Q[{v_{1},v_{2}}]
with
P[{v_{1},v_{2}}]
.
Then
Q^{\prime}
should also be a geodesic due to
Observation

3.2
.
But the vertex
v_{3}
appears twice on
Q^{\prime}
so it is not a simple path and hence cannot be a geodesic because the length of any cycle in
G
is positive.
∎

3.1
Problem definition and basic facts

Disjoint Shortest Paths

Parameter:

k

Input:
An undirected graph
G
with positive edge weights and a collection
\mathcal{T}=\{(s_{1},t_{1}),\dotsm,(s_{k},t_{k})\}
of pairs of vertices.

Task:
Find vertex-disjoint paths
P_{1},\dots,P_{k}
so that
P_{i}
is a shortest
(s_{i},t_{i})
-path for each
i\in[k]
.

For comparison, in the
Disjoint Paths
problem we do not require
P_{i}
to be a geodesic.

For an undirected graph
G
an
oriented subgraph
of
G
is a directed graph with the vertex set
V(G)
and an arc set contained in
\vec{E}(G)
.
It will later be convenient to analyze the problem from the following perspective, where oriented subgraphs of
G
encode the metric structure.

Disjoint Annotated Paths

Parameter:

k

Input:
An undirected graph
G
, a collection
\mathcal{T}=\{(s_{1},t_{1}),\dotsm,(s_{k},t_{k})\}
of pairs of vertices and a collection
\mathcal{D}=\{D_{1},\ldots,D_{k}\}
of oriented subgraphs of
G
.

Task:
Find vertex-disjoint paths
P_{1},\dots,P_{k}
so that
P_{i}
is an oriented
(s_{i},t_{i})
-path in
D_{i}
for each
i\in[k]
.

In
Planar Disjoint Shortest Paths
(
PDSP
) and
Planar Disjoint Annotated Paths
(
PDAP
) we assume that the input graph
G
is planar and that it is provided as a plane graph, that is, with a fixed embedding.

The following reduction from
Disjoint Paths
on DAGs to
Disjoint Shortest Paths
in undirected graphs is known, see e.g.
[
23
, Theorem 2.2]
. Here we note that it also works in the planar setting.

Lemma 3.4
.

There is polynomial-time algorithm that, given a planar acyclic digraph
D
with a collection of vertex pairs
\mathcal{T}_{D}
, outputs a planar undirected graph
G
with a collection of vertex pairs
\mathcal{T}_{G}
so that
|\mathcal{T}_{G}|=|\mathcal{T}_{D}|
and
(D,\mathcal{T}_{D})
is a yes-instance of
Disjoint Paths
if and only if
(G,\mathcal{T}_{G})
is a yes-instance of
Disjoint Shortest Paths
.

Proof.

First, we check if for every
(s_{i},t_{i})\in\mathcal{T}_{D}
there exists an
(s_{i},t_{i})
-path in
D
; if no, we can output a trivial no-instance.
Next, fix some topological order
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
of vertices in
D
.
Let
G
be obtained from
D
by transforming each arc
(v_{i},v_{j})
into an undirected edge
v_{i}v_{j}
and subdividing it into
j-i
unit-length edges.
Clearly
G
remains planar.
Observe that for any
i<j
there is a 1-1 correspondence between
directed
(v_{i},v_{j})
-paths in
D
and undirected
(v_{i},v_{j})
-paths in
G
of length
j-i
, which are shortest
(v_{i},v_{j})
-paths in
G
.
Since for each
(s_{i},t_{i})\in\mathcal{T}_{D}
the vertex
t_{i}
is reachable from
s_{i}
in
D
,
their distance in
G

equals their distance in the fixed topological order.
Hence there is also a 1-1 correspondence between solutions to
(D,\mathcal{T}_{D})
and solutions to
(G,\mathcal{T}_{G})

∎

Lemma

3.4
gives a parameter-preserving reduction from
Disjoint Paths
on planar DAGs to
Planar Disjoint Shortest Paths
.
Since the first problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
[
4
]
, we know that the latter is as well.

Shortest paths DAGs.

We next define
shortest paths DAGs
for given pairs of terminals. A slightly different variant of this definition was considered in
[
2
]
.

Definition 3.5

(Shortest paths DAGs)
.

Let
G
be an undirected graph with positive edge weights
w\colon E(G)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
s,t\in V(G)
.
We define
(s,t)\text{-}\mathsf{dag}
as the oriented subgraph of
G
with arcs defined as follows:
(u,v)\in A((s,t)\text{-}\mathsf{dag})
if
uv\in E(G)
and
d_{G}(s,u)+w(uv)+d_{G}(v,t)=d_{G}(s,t)
.

The following lemma is a straightforward consequence of the definition of shortest paths DAGs (as already observed in
[
2
]
).

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
G
be an undirected graph with positive edge weights
w\colon E(G)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
s,t\in V(G)
.
Also let
P
be an oriented
(s,t)
-path in
G
.
Then
P
is a geodesic in
G
if and only if
P
is a path in
(s,t)
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}
.

Proof.

For the forward implication observe that every (oriented) edge
(u,v)
of
P
belongs to
A((s,t)\text{-}\mathsf{dag})
by definition and therefore
P
is a path in
(s,t)
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}
.
To prove the second implication, we proceed by induction on the number
\ell
of edges in
P
.
The claim clearly holds for
\ell\leqslant 1
and so we assume
\ell\geqslant 2
.
Let
v
be the second vertex on
P
.
Since
(s,v)\in A((s,t)\text{-}\mathsf{dag})

we have
d_{G}(s,t)=w(sv)+d_{G}(v,t)
.
By the inductive assumption the path
P[v,t]
is a shortest
(v,t)
-path so its length equals
d_{G}(v,t)
.
Consequently, the length of
P
is
d_{G}(s,t)
.
∎

Since we work with positive edge weights, no
(s,t)
-walk in
(s,t)
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}
may contain a cycle.
This implies that
(s,t)
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}
is indeed a DAG.

Observation 3.7
.

Let
G
be an undirected graph with positive edge weights
w\colon E(G)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
s,t\in V(G)
. Then
(s,t)
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}
does not contain directed cycles.

Next, we make note of the following fact that is a direct consequence of
Lemma

3.6
.

Observation 3.8
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{T})
be an instance of
Disjoint Shortest Paths
and
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
be an instance of
Disjoint Annotated Paths
for
\mathcal{D}=\{(s_{i},t_{i})\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\mid i\in[k]\}
.
Consider a family
\mathcal{P}
of vertex-disjoint paths
P_{1},\dots,P_{k}
so that
P_{i}
is an
(s_{i},t_{i})
-path.
Then
\mathcal{P}
is a solution to
(G,\mathcal{T})
if and only if it is a solution to
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
.

3.2
Nice instances

It will be convenient to restrict ourselves to instances with somewhat regularized structure.
The following definition refers to instances of both problems (
PDSP
and
PDAP
).

Definition 3.9
.

An instance
(G,\mathcal{T})
is called nice if the following conditions hold.

1.

G
is a connected plane graph.

2.

\mathcal{T}
is a collection of vertex pairs from
G
in which no vertex appears more than once.

3.

Every vertex appearing in
\mathcal{T}
has degree 1.

Additionally, for
PDSP
we require one more condition.

4.

Each edge
e\in E(G)
appears as an arc in
(s,t)\text{-}\mathsf{dag}
for some
(s,t)\in\mathcal{T}
.

We begin by reducing a general instance of the problem to a nice one.

Lemma 3.10
.

There is an algorithm that, given an instance
(G,\mathcal{T})
of
PDSP
, outputs, in polynomial time, one of the following:

•

either a report that
(G,\mathcal{T})
is a no-instance, or

•

a collection
\mathcal{I}
of nice instances of
PDSP
,
where
|\mathcal{I}|=\mathcal{O}(k+|G|)
and

(G,\mathcal{T})
is a yes-instance if and only if every instance in
\mathcal{I}
is a yes-instance.

Proof.

We assume that there is no pair
(s,t)\in\mathcal{T}
such that
s
and
t
are in different connected components of
G
and that no vertex appears in
\mathcal{T}
more than once (since otherwise, we report that
(G,\mathcal{T})
is a no-instance).
Also, we modify the given graph
G
as follows:
for every terminal vertex
s
, we add a new vertex
s^{\prime}
to
G
, adjacent only to
s
and replace
s
with
s^{\prime}
in the corresponding pair in
\mathcal{T}
.
We extend the weight function
w
to assign weight 1 to the new edge
ss^{\prime}
(this choice is arbitrary).
Next, we remove all edges of
G
that do not appear as arcs in any
(s,t)
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}
, for all
(s,t)\in\mathcal{T}
. To do this, for every
u,v\in V(G)
, we compute their distance in
G
(this can be done in polynomial time) and therefore we can compute the directed graph
(s,t)
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}
, for each
(s,t)\in\mathcal{T}
. Observe that after the removal of these edges, the number of connected components of the graph that contain a terminal vertex can grow by at most
k
.

The algorithm then constructs a collection of instances
\mathcal{I}
, one for each connected component of
G
that contains some terminal vertex, i.e.,
\mathcal{I}
is the set of instances
(H,\mathcal{T}_{H})
, where
H
is a connected component of
G
(that contains a terminal vertex) and
\mathcal{T}_{H}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
consists of the pairs from
\mathcal{T}
with both vertices in
H
.
For every
(H,\mathcal{T}_{H})\in\mathcal{I}
, compute a plane embedding of
H
(this can be done in linear time, see
[
19
]
and
[
26
]
).
Note that thus, each instance in
\mathcal{I}
satisfies
Definition

3.9
∎

4
Rings

In order to define the “ring-shaped regions” as explained in
Section

2
,
for every bipartition of the set of vertices appearing in
\mathcal{T}
, we need to distinguish the pairs of
\mathcal{T}
that are “split” in such partition. In fact, our constructions involve not only the set of terminal vertices but also an additional set of vertices, which correspond to the branching nodes of the Steiner tree composed of geodesics on which we will work on. These notions are formalized in the following definitions.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
G
be a graph and
\mathcal{T}
be a set of pairs of vertices of
G
.
We say that a vertex set
\widehat{T}\subseteq V(G)

is a terminal-superset of
\mathcal{T}
if
\widehat{T}
contains all vertices appearing in
\mathcal{T}
, and possibly some more.

Definition 4.2

(Splitting partitions of terminals)
.

Let
G
be a graph,
\mathcal{T}
be a set of pairs of vertices of
G
, and
\widehat{T}\subseteq V(G)
be a terminal-superset of
\mathcal{T}
.
For a partition
(X,Y)
of
\widehat{T}

we define
\mathsf{split}_{\mathcal{T}}(X,Y)
as the set of pairs
(s,t)
such that
s\in X
,
t\in Y
and either
(s,t)
or
(t,s)
belongs to
\mathcal{T}
.
We say that
(X,Y)
is a splitting partition if
\mathsf{split}_{\mathcal{T}}(X,Y)\neq\emptyset
.
We also define
\mathsf{same}\text{-}\mathsf{side}(X,Y)

as the collection of pairs
(s,t)\in\widehat{T}\times\widehat{T}
with
s\neq t
,
for which
\{s,t\}\subseteq X
or
\{s,t\}\subseteq Y
.

4.1
DAG-cuts

We next proceed to defining
dag-cuts
for bipartitions of a terminal-superset
\widehat{T}
of
\mathcal{T}
,
which correspond to cuts of the original graph where the cut edges should satisfy the following property: these edges belong to oriented shortest paths for
every
split pair of
\mathcal{T}
but not for pairs of vertices of
\widehat{T}
that are not split.

Definition 4.3

(DAG-cuts)
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{T})
be a nice instance of
PDSP
and
(X,Y)
be a splitting partition of a terminal-superset
\widehat{T}
of
\mathcal{T}
.
Also, let
W_{X},W_{Y}\subseteq V(G)
be two disjoint vertex sets with
X\subseteq W_{X}
and
Y\subseteq W_{Y}
.
We define a
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cut}
as a partition
(V_{X},V_{Y})
of
V(G)
with the following properties.

1.

W_{X}\subseteq V_{X}
and
W_{Y}\subseteq V_{Y}
.

2.

For each
(v,u)\in\vec{E}(V_{X},V_{Y})

and each
(t,t^{\prime})\in\mathsf{split}_{\mathcal{T}}(X,Y)
, we have that
(v,u)\in A((t,t^{\prime})\text{-}\mathsf{dag})
.

3.

For each
(v,u)\in\vec{E}(V_{X},V_{Y})

and each
(t,t^{\prime})\in\mathsf{same}\text{-}\mathsf{side}(X,Y)
,

\{(v,u),(u,v)\}\cap A((t,t^{\prime})\text{-}\mathsf{dag})=\emptyset
.

See
Figure

4
for an illustration of a dag-cut.

An example of a dag-cut. The vertices depicted as squares correspond to the set
V_{X}
while the vertices depicted as discs correspond to the set
V_{Y}
.

Whenever
P
is a shortest
(t,t^{\prime})
-path for some
(t,t^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}
,

corresponds to a directed
(t,t^{\prime})
-path in
(t,t^{\prime})\text{-}\mathsf{dag}
.
If
(t,t^{\prime})\in\mathsf{same}\text{-}\mathsf{side}(X,Y)
then

clearly cannot use any edges from an

.
Otherwise, all these edges are oriented in the same direction in
(t,t^{\prime})\text{-}\mathsf{dag}
.
Consequently,
P
can cross an
(X,Y)\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cut}
at most once.

Observation 4.4
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{T})
be a nice instance of
PDSP
,
(X,Y)
be a splitting partition of some terminal-superset
\widehat{T}
of
\mathcal{T}
, and

be a set of edges connecting the two sides of an

.
Then the following holds:

1.

if

is a shortest

-path for some

then

if

-path for some

Relating dag-cuts to cycles in the dual.

We show that dag-cuts correspond to cycles of the dual graph that separate the vertices of the two different parts of the dag-cut.
In order to show this, we first prove that
in a dag-cut, the corresponding vertex sets in the partition induce connected subgraphs of
G
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{T})
be a nice instance of
PDSP
and
(X,Y)
be a splitting partition of a terminal-superset
\widehat{T}
of
\mathcal{T}
.
Also, let
W_{X},W_{Y}\subseteq V(G)
be two disjoint vertex sets with
X\subseteq W_{X}
and
Y\subseteq W_{Y}
.
For every
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cut}

(V_{X},V_{Y})
in
(G,\mathcal{T})
, it holds that
G[V_{X}]
and
G[V_{Y}]
are connected.

Proof.

Due to property (1) of
Definition

4.3
, we have that
X\subseteq W_{X}\subseteq V_{X}
and
Y\subseteq W_{Y}\subseteq V_{Y}
.
Since
(X,Y)
is splitting, there is a pair
(s,t)\in\mathcal{T}
such that one vertex belongs to
X
and the other to
Y
.
Assume without loss of generality that
s\in X
and
t\in Y
.

We first prove that
G[V_{Y}]
is connected.
Let
z
be a vertex in
V_{Y}
.
We show that there is a
(z,t)
-path in
G[V_{Y}]
.
Since
G
is connected, there is a
(z,t)
-path
P
in
G
.
We orient
P
from
z
to
t
and this orientation induces a linear order
\leqslant_{P}
on
V(P)
.
Let
v
be the first (with respect to
\leqslant_{P}
) vertex of
P
that is in
V_{X}
; if such a vertex does not exist then
P
is the claimed path in
G[V_{Y}]
.
Let
e=wv\in E(P)
such that
w<_{P}v
(
w\in V_{Y}
).
By
Definition

4.3
,

(v,w)\in A((s,t)\text{-}\mathsf{dag})
.
Therefore, there is a shortest
(s,t)
-path
Q
in
G
that contains the edges
e
.
Observe that, by definition, all edges of
Q
appear in
A((s,t))\text{-}\mathsf{dag}
as arcs oriented towards
t
.
The latter implies that every vertex of
Q[w,t]
belongs to
V_{Y}
. Thus, the concatenation
P[z,w]+Q[w,t]
is a
(z,t)
-path in
G[V_{Y}]
.

To prove connectivity of
G[V_{X}]
, we work analogously.
In particular, we pick an arbitrary vertex
z\in V_{X}
and the first vertex
v\in V_{Y}
in a
(z,s)
-path
P
and we show the following: if
w
is the neighbor of
v
in
P
(occurring before
v
in
P
), for every shortest
(s,t)
-path
Q
passing through
wv
,
V(Q[s,w])
should be a subset of
V_{X}
and therefore the concatenation
P[z,w]+Q[w,s]
is a
(z,s)
-path in
G[V_{X}]
.
∎

Let us now give a formal definition of separability by a cycle in the dual.
Let
G
be a plane graph, let
A
and
B
be two disjoint subsets of
V(G)
.
We say that a cycle
C
of the dual
G^{\star}

separates

A
and
B
if
A
and
B
belong to different connected components of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus C
.

Lemma 4.6

(Folklore)
.

Let
G
be a connected plane graph, let
(A,B)
be a partition of
V(G)
. If
G[A]
and
G[B]
are connected subgraphs, then there is a (unique) cycle
C
in the dual
G^{\star}
that separates
A
and
B
.

The existence of cycles in the dual corresponding to dag-cuts in the primal is stated in the following lemma, which is a direct implication of
Lemma

4.5
and
Lemma

4.6
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{T})
be a nice instance of
PDSP

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{T})
be a nice instance of

be a splitting partition of a terminal-superset

\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cut}

and

and

.

and

4.2
DAG-rings

Using the notion of dag-cuts, we are now ready to define
dag-rings
, which correspond to pairs of dag-cuts whose cycles bound a ring-shaped region where all edges are in (oriented) shortest paths between split pairs of terminals.

Definition 4.10

(DAG-rings)
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{T})
be a nice instance of
PDSP
and
(X,Y)
be a splitting partition of a terminal-superset
\widehat{T}
of
\mathcal{T}
. Also, let
W_{X},W_{Y}\subseteq V(G)
be two disjoint vertex sets with
X\subseteq W_{X}
and
Y\subseteq W_{Y}
.
A
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}

is a partition
(U_{X},U_{\mathsf{mid}},U_{Y})
of

with the following properties.

1.

(U_{X},U_{\mathsf{mid}}\cup U_{Y})
is a
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cut}
.

2.

(U_{X}\cup U_{\mathsf{mid}},U_{Y})
is a
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cut}
.

3.

The set
E(U_{X},U_{Y})
is empty.

4.

For each
e_{1}\in E(U_{X},U_{\mathsf{mid}})
,
e_{2}\in E(U_{\mathsf{mid}},U_{Y})
, and each
(t,t^{\prime})\in\mathsf{split}_{\mathcal{T}}(X,Y)
there exists a shortest
(t,t^{\prime})
-path
P
in
G
such that
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}\subseteq E(P)
.

We say that a pair
(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
of
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cuts}

represents
a
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}

(U_{X},U_{\mathsf{mid}},U_{Y})
if
\gamma=(U_{X},U_{\mathsf{mid}}\cup U_{Y})
and
\gamma^{\prime}=(U_{X}\cup U_{\mathsf{mid}},U_{Y})
.

See
Figure

5
for an illustration of a dag-ring.

appear as arcs in the

\text{-}\mathsf{dag}
of any pair

.

P[t,z_{2}]+Q[z_{2},w_{3}]+P^{\prime}[w_{3},t^{\prime}]
which is a shortest
(t,t^{\prime})
-path in
G
that contains the edges
e_{1}
and
e_{4}
.
∎

Using
Lemma

4.14
, we reduce the computation of a maximal dag-ring to the computation of maximally pushed dag-cuts.

Lemma 4.15
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{T})
be a nice instance of
PDSP
and
(X,Y)
be a splitting partition of a terminal-superset
\widehat{T}
of
\mathcal{T}
.
Also, let
W_{X},W_{Y}\subseteq V(G)
be two disjoint vertex sets with
X\subseteq W_{X}
and
Y\subseteq W_{Y}
.
Suppose that there exists at least one
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}
.
Then there exists a maximal
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}
, which can be found in polynomial time.

Proof.

Let
(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
be a pair of
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cuts}
that represent a
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}
with
\gamma_{1}\sqsubseteq\gamma_{2}
; such a pair is assumed to exist in the statement of the lemma.
Also, let
\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\gamma_{2}^{\prime}
be two
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cuts}
such that for every other
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cut}

\gamma^{\prime}
, we have
\gamma_{1}^{\prime}\sqsubseteq\gamma^{\prime}\sqsubseteq\gamma_{2}^{\prime}
; such
\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\gamma_{2}^{\prime}
exist because of
Lemma

4.9
.
Therefore, we have that
\gamma_{1}^{\prime}\sqsubseteq\gamma_{1}\sqsubseteq\gamma_{2}\sqsubseteq\gamma_{2}^{\prime}
, which implies, due to
Lemma

4.14
, that
(\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\gamma_{2}^{\prime})
represents a
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}
.
Maximality of this latter dag-ring follows from the fact that for every

(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cut}

\gamma^{\prime}
, we have

\gamma_{1}^{\prime}\sqsubseteq\gamma^{\prime}\sqsubseteq\gamma_{2}^{\prime}
.
Also, due to
Lemma

4.9
,
\gamma_{1}^{\prime}
and
\gamma_{2}^{\prime}
can be computed in polynomial time.
Note also that
Item

4
of
Definition

4.10
can be verified in polynomial time by checking if for every pair of edges
e_{1}=(u_{1},v_{1}),e_{2}=(u_{2},v_{2})\in E(G)
with
u_{1}\in U_{X},v_{1}\in U_{\mathsf{mid}}
and
u_{2}\in U_{\mathsf{mid}},v_{2}\in U_{Y}
, and every
(t,t^{\prime})\in\mathsf{split}_{\mathcal{T}}(X,Y)
, we have
d_{G}(t,t^{\prime})=d_{G}(t,u_{1})+w(e_{1})+d_{G}(v_{1},u_{2})+w(e_{2})+d_{G}(v_{2},t^{\prime})
.
The existence of a
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}
implies that this check gives will give a positive answer for every choice of
e_{1},e_{2},
and
(t,t^{\prime})
.
Conversely, if this check failed it would mean that there exists no
(W_{X},W_{X})\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}
in
G
at all.
∎

4.3
Exhaustive ring decompositions

We are now ready to define ring decompositions,
which are used in order to define areas where we can tame the winding number of solutions, as explained in
Section

2
.

Definition 4.16
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{T})
be a nice instance of
PDSP
and
\widehat{T}
be a terminal-superset of
\mathcal{T}
.
A collection of tuples
\mathcal{R}=(X^{i},Y^{i},\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{i})_{i=1}^{\ell}

is called a ring decomposition of
(G,\mathcal{T},\widehat{T})
when the following hold.

1.

For each
i\in[\ell]
,
(X^{i},Y^{i})
is a splitting partition of
\widehat{T}
and
(\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{i})
represents a
(X^{i},Y^{i})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}
.

2.

For each distinct
i,j\in[\ell]
the sets
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{i})
and
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1}^{j},\gamma_{2}^{j})
are disjoint.

The
family of partitions
of
\mathcal{R}
is the set
\{(X^{i},Y^{i})\colon i\in[\ell]\}
,
which we denote by
\Pi_{\mathcal{R}}
,
while the
family of dag-cuts
of
\mathcal{R}
is the set

\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}\{\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{i}\}
,
which we denote by
\Gamma_{\mathcal{R}}
.

A tuple
(X,Y,\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
is an extension of
\mathcal{R}
if
\mathcal{R}\cup\{(X,Y,\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\}
is also a ring decomposition of
(G,\mathcal{T},\widehat{T})
.
We call a ring decomposition exhaustive if it admits no extension.

Computing exhaustive ring decompositions.

We next show how to compute extensions of ring decompositions. For this, we will use some facts on crossing of closed curves in the plane.
The next observation follows directly from the fact that the sets
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{i})
and
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1}^{j},\gamma_{2}^{j})
are asked to be disjoint for each distinct
i,j\in[\ell]
in
Definition

4.16
, together with the fact that each ring of the form
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
is an open set and
\mathrm{Cycle}(\gamma)
and
\mathrm{Cycle}(\gamma^{\prime})
are its two boundaries.

Observation 4.17
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{T})
be a nice instance of
PDSP
and
\widehat{T}
be a terminal-superset of
\mathcal{T}
.
Also, let
\mathcal{R}
be a ring decomposition of
(G,\mathcal{T},\widehat{T})
.
Then for every two
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{R}}
,

\mathrm{Cycle}(\gamma)
and
\mathrm{Cycle}(\gamma^{\prime})
do not cross (as closed curves on the plane).

We also use the following known fact for simple closed curves on the plane.

Lemma 4.18

(Folklore)
.

Let
S_{1},S_{2}
be two simple closed curves on the plane.
Suppose there exist points
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
S_{1}
separates
\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
from
\{x_{3},x_{4}\}
whereas
S_{2}
separates
\{x_{1},x_{3}\}
from
\{x_{2},x_{4}\}
.
Then
S_{1},S_{2}
cross.

The next lemma shows that the computation of an extension of a ring decomposition for some fixed splitting partition
(X,Y)
boils down to finding
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cuts}
for some vertex supersets
W_{X},W_{Y}

of
X
and
Y
, respectively. Moreover, such sets
W_{X},W_{Y}
can be computed in polynomial time.

Lemma 4.19
.

There is a polynomial-time algorithm that, given a ring decomposition
\mathcal{R}
of
(G,\mathcal{T},\widehat{T})
and a splitting partition
(X,Y)
of
\widehat{T}
,
such that neither
(X,Y)
nor
(Y,X)
belongs to the family
\Pi_{\mathcal{R}}
of partitions of
\mathcal{R}
,
outputs one of the following:

•

either a correct report that there are no
(X,Y)
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cuts}

\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
such that
(X,Y,\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
is an extension of
\mathcal{R}
, or

•

two disjoint sets
W_{X},W_{Y}\subseteq V(G)
with
X\subseteq W_{X}
and
Y\subseteq W_{Y}
such that for every pair
(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
of
(X,Y)
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cuts}
,
(X,Y,\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
is an extension of
\mathcal{R}
if and only if
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
are
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cuts}
.

Proof.

We use
(X^{i},Y^{i},\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{i})_{i=1}^{\ell}
to denote the ring decomposition
\mathcal{R}
.
We first check how
(X,Y)
relates to the other partitions in
\Pi_{\mathcal{R}}
.

If
(X,Y)
“crosses” some partition
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\in\Pi_{\mathcal{R}}
, i.e., both
X
and
Y
contain vertices of both
X^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}
, then we report that no extension of
\mathcal{R}
with this partition
(X,Y)
is possible.
To see this, let
v_{X,X^{\prime}},v_{X,Y^{\prime}},v_{Y,X^{\prime}},v_{Y,Y^{\prime}}
be vertices of
V(G)
that are in
X\cap X^{\prime},X\cap Y^{\prime},Y\cap X^{\prime},
and
Y\cap Y^{\prime}
, respectively.
Also, let

\gamma_{1}^{\prime}
and
\gamma_{2}^{\prime}
be two
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cuts}
such that
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\gamma_{2}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{R}
and suppose that there are
(X,Y)
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cuts}

\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
such that
(X,Y,\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
is an extension of
\mathcal{R}
. Then, observe that

\mathrm{Cycle}(\gamma_{1}^{\prime})
separates
\{v_{X,X^{\prime}},v_{Y,X^{\prime}}\}
and
\{v_{X,Y^{\prime}},v_{Y,Y^{\prime}}\}
while
\mathrm{Cycle}(\gamma_{1})
separates
\{v_{X,X^{\prime}},v_{X,Y^{\prime}}\}
and
\{v_{Y,X^{\prime}},v_{Y,Y^{\prime}}\}
.
By
Lemma

4.18
,

\mathrm{Cycle}(\gamma_{1}^{\prime})
and
\mathrm{Cycle}(\gamma_{1})
cross (as closed curves on the plane), a contradiction to
Observation

4.17
. Therefore, we can safely report that, in this case, no extension of
\mathcal{R}
with this partition
(X,Y)
is possible.

From now on, we can assume that for every
i\in[\ell]
,
either
X^{i}
or
Y^{i}

does not
intersect both
X
and
Y
.
In other words,
one of the following holds: (a)
X^{i}\subseteq X
or (b)
Y^{i}\subseteq Y
or (c)
Y^{i}\subseteq X
or (d)
X^{i}\subseteq Y
; the cases (b) and (d) are obtained from (a) and (c) by swapping the roles of
X^{i}
and
Y^{i}
.
Keep in mind that since
(X,Y),(Y,X)\notin\Pi_{\mathcal{R}}
,
it cannot happen that
X=X^{i}
or
X=Y^{i}
, for any
i\in[\ell]
; thus, exactly one of the cases among (a)-(d) occurs for each
i\in[\ell]
.
For every
i\in[\ell]
, let

\displaystyle Z_{X}^{i}:=\begin{cases}X^{i},&\text{if $X^{i}\subseteq X$,}\\
Y^{i},&\text{if $Y^{i}\subseteq X$,}\\
\emptyset,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle Z_{Y}^{i}:=\begin{cases}X^{i},&\text{if $X^{i}\subseteq Y$,}\\
Y^{i},&\text{if $Y^{i}\subseteq Y$,}\\
\emptyset,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Intuitively,
Z_{X}^{i}
corresponds to the part of the partition
(X^{i},Y^{i})
that is contained in
X
(if such exists), while
Z_{Y}^{i}
corresponds to the part that is contained in
Y
(if such exists).

Also, for every
i\in[\ell]
, if
Z_{X}^{i}\neq\emptyset
(resp.
Z_{Y}^{i}\neq\emptyset
), we set

B_{X}^{i}
(resp.
B_{Y}^{i}
) to be the set of all vertices of
G
that are in the same connected component of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{i})
as
Z_{X}^{i}
(resp.
Z_{Y}^{i}
); if
Z_{X}^{i}
or
Z_{Y}^{i}
is the empty set then we also set
B_{X}^{i}
and
B_{Y}^{i}
to be the empty set, respectively.
We finally set
W_{X}:=X\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}B_{X}^{i}
and
W_{Y}:=Y\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}B_{Y}^{i}
.
It is easy to check that all above can be computed in polynomial time.

We now show that
(X,Y,\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
is an extension of
\mathcal{R}
if and only if
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
are
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cuts}
.
For this, it suffices to show that
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
is disjoint from the other rings
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{i})
,
i\in[\ell]

if and only if
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cuts}
. For every
i\in[\ell]
, let
R_{i}
denote
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{i})
.

For the forward direction, i.e., that disjointedness of rings implies that
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
are
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cuts}
, we argue as follows.
Let
\Delta_{X}
(resp.
\Delta_{Y}
) denote the component of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{2}}\setminus\mathsf{cl}(\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}))

that contains
X
(resp.
Y
).
Keep in mind that
\Delta_{X}
and
\Delta_{Y}
are open sets.
The fact that for every
i\in[\ell]
,
R_{i}\cap\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=\emptyset
implies that every
R_{i},i\in[\ell]
is either a subset of
\Delta_{X}
or a subset of
\Delta_{Y}
.
For each
i\in[\ell]
, let
\Delta^{i}
be the connected component of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus R_{i}
that is disjoint from
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
.
Now notice that either
\Delta^{i}\cap\widehat{T}\subseteq X
or
\Delta^{i}\cap\widehat{T}\subseteq Y
,
which implies that either
\Delta^{i}\subseteq\Delta_{X}
or
\Delta^{i}\subseteq\Delta_{Y}
, respectively.
Note also that for every
i\in[\ell]
,
B_{X}^{i}\subseteq\Delta^{i}
and
B_{Y}^{i}\subseteq\Delta^{i}
; from this we get
W_{X}\subseteq\Delta_{X}
and
W_{Y}\subseteq\Delta_{Y}
. Therefore, if
(U_{X},U_{\mathsf{mid}},U_{Y})

is the
(X,Y)
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}
represented by
(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
, then
W_{X}\subseteq U_{X}
and
W_{Y}\subseteq U_{Y}
.
This, in turn, implies that

(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
are also
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cuts}
.

In the other direction, suppose towards a contradiction, that
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
are
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cuts}
and there is
i\in[\ell]
such that
R_{i}\cap\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\neq\emptyset
.
Since the boundaries of
R_{i}
and
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
correspond to cycles of the dual
G^{\star}
,
there is a vertex of
G
that belongs to
R_{i}
that also belongs to
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
.
Consequently, either
\mathrm{Cycle}(\gamma_{1}^{i})
or
\mathrm{Cycle}(\gamma_{2}^{i})
crosses
\mathrm{Cycle}(\gamma_{1})
or
\mathrm{Cycle}(\gamma_{2})
.
This implies that
there is a vertex of
W_{X}\cup W_{Y}
in
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
.
This yields a contradiction to the fact that
\mathrm{Cycle}(\gamma_{2})
and
\mathrm{Cycle}(\gamma_{1})
separate
W_{X}
and
W_{Y}
, which holds because of
Lemma

4.7
.
Therefore,
\mathcal{R}\cup\{(X,Y,\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\}
is a ring decomposition of
(G,\mathcal{T},U)
.
∎

With
Lemma

4.19
in hand, we are now ready to show the main result of this subsection, i.e., that given a terminal-superset
\widehat{T}
, an exhaustive ring decomposition can be computed in time
2^{|\widehat{T}|}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
. Moreover, this ring decomposition should consist of
\mathcal{O}(|\widehat{T}|)
many rings.

Proposition 4.20
.

There exists an algorithm that, given a nice instance
(G,\mathcal{T})
of
PDSP
and a terminal-superset
\widehat{T}
of
\mathcal{T}

with
|\widehat{T}|=r
, runs in time
2^{r}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, and outputs an exhaustive ring decomposition of
(G,\mathcal{T},\widehat{T})
containing at most
2r-2
tuples.

Proof.

We will compute a sequence
(\mathcal{R}_{0},\mathcal{R}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{R}_{\ell})
of ring decompositions of
(G,\mathcal{T},\widehat{T})
with the following property.
For each
i\in[\ell]
the ring decomposition
\mathcal{R}_{i}
comprises
i
tuples and it is obtained from
\mathcal{R}_{i-1}
by inserting a tuple
(X^{i},Y^{i},\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{i})

which is an extension of
\mathcal{R}_{i-1}
and maximizes
|\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{i})\cap V(G)|
(breaking ties arbitrarily).
We start from an empty ring decomposition
\mathcal{R}_{0}
and terminate the process when no more extension is available.
Then clearly
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}
is exhaustive.

Claim 4.21
.

For every
i\in[\ell]
, if
(X^{i},Y^{i},\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{i})
is an extension of
\mathcal{R}_{i-1}
, then neither
(X^{i},Y^{i})
nor
(Y^{i},X^{i})
belongs to
\Pi_{\mathcal{R}_{i-1}}
.

Proof.

For every
h\leqslant i
, let
R_{h}
denote
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1}^{h},\gamma_{2}^{h})
.
Suppose towards a contradiction that either
(X^{i},Y^{i})
or
(Y^{i},X^{i})
belongs to
\Pi_{\mathcal{R}_{i-1}}
and let
j<i
be minimum possible so that
(X^{i},Y^{i})=(X^{j},Y^{j})
or
(X^{i},Y^{i})=(Y^{j},X^{j})
.
Since
(X^{i},Y^{i},\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{i})
is an extension of
\mathcal{R}_{i-1}
, we have that
R_{i}
and
R_{h}
are disjoint, for every
h<i
. Therefore, w.l.o.g. we can assume that
\gamma_{1}^{i}\sqsubseteq\gamma_{2}^{i}\sqsubseteq\gamma_{1}^{j}\sqsubseteq\gamma_{2}^{j}
and thus
X^{i}=X^{j}
and
Y^{i}=Y^{j}
.
Note that since
(X^{i},Y^{i})=(X^{j},Y^{j})
,
\gamma_{1}^{i}
is also an
(X^{j},Y^{j})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cut}
.
Therefore, due to
Lemma

4.14
,
we get that
(\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{j})
also represents an
(X^{j},Y^{j})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}
.

We next claim that
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{j})
is disjoint from the ring
R_{j^{\prime}}
, for every
j^{\prime}<j
.
To see why this holds, suppose towards a contradiction that there is a
j^{\prime}<j
such that
R_{j^{\prime}}
intersects

\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{j})
.
Due to
Observation

4.17
,

R_{j^{\prime}}
does not intersect
\mathrm{Cycle}(\gamma_{1}^{i})
or
\mathrm{Cycle}(\gamma_{2}^{j})
and therefore
R_{j^{\prime}}
is a subset of
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{j})
.
Also, since
\gamma_{1}^{j^{\prime}},\gamma_{2}^{j^{\prime}}
are
(X^{j^{\prime}},Y^{j^{\prime}})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cuts}
for some splitting partition
(X^{j^{\prime}},Y^{j^{\prime}})
, both components of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\mathsf{cl}(R_{j^{\prime}})
contain at least one terminal. This, in turn, implies that the sets
X^{i}
and
Y^{j}
belong to different components of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\mathsf{cl}(R_{j^{\prime}})
and therefore either
X^{j^{\prime}}=X^{i}
(and
Y^{j^{\prime}}=Y^{j}=Y^{i}
) or
X^{j^{\prime}}=Y^{j}=Y^{i}
(and
Y^{j^{\prime}}=X^{i}
),
a contradiction to minimality of
j
.

Given that
(\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{j})
represents an
(X^{j},Y^{j})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}
and

\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{j})
is disjoint from the rings
R_{j^{\prime}}
, for each
j^{\prime}<j
, we get that
(X^{j},Y^{j},\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{j})
is also an extension of
\mathcal{R}_{j-1}
.
Moreover, since both
R_{i}
and
R_{j}
are subsets of
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{j})
, we have
|R_{j}\cap V(G)|<|\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{j})\cap V(G)|
.
This contradicts the fact that
(X^{j},Y^{j},\gamma_{1}^{j},\gamma_{2}^{j})
is an extension of
\mathcal{R}_{j-1}
that maximizes
|\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1}^{j},\gamma_{2}^{j})\cap V(G)|
.
∎

Claim 4.22
.

Given
\mathcal{R}_{i-1}
we can compute
\mathcal{R}_{i}
in time
2^{r}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
.

Proof.

The algorithm iterates over all splitting partitions
(X,Y)
of
\widehat{T}
that are different from
(X^{j},Y^{j})
and
(Y^{j},X^{j})
for every
j<i
; there are less than
2^{r}
many of them. Because of
Claim

4.21
, we know that it is sufficient to focus only on partitions that are different from
(X^{j},Y^{j})
and
(Y^{j},X^{j})
for every
j<i
. For a fixed
(X,Y)
, our goal is to compute two
(X,Y)
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cuts}

\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
such that
(X,Y,\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
is an extension of
\mathcal{R}
while maximizing
|\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\cap V(G)|
, or report that no such extension (with
(X,Y)
) exists. Invoking the algorithm of
Lemma

4.19
for
(X,Y)
, we either correctly report that no extension with
(X,Y)
is possible, or obtain two disjoint sets
W_{X},W_{Y}\subseteq V(G)
with
X\subseteq W_{X}
and
Y\subseteq W_{Y}
such that for every pair
(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
of
(X,Y)
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cuts}
,
(X,Y,\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
is an extension of
\mathcal{R}
if and only if
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
are
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cuts}
. Note also that, by definition, a maximal
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}
represented by two
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cuts}

\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\gamma_{2}^{\prime}
is also maximizing
|\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\gamma_{2}^{\prime})\cap V(G)|
. Due to
Lemma

4.15
such a maximal
(W_{X},W_{Y})
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}
can be found in polynomial time.
∎

Claim 4.23
.

Suppose that the process terminates at iteration
\ell
.
Then
\ell\leqslant 2r-2
.

Proof.

For every
i\in[\ell]
,
let
D_{i}
be the bounded component of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\mathsf{cl}(\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{i}))
. Let also
K
denote the set
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathsf{cl}(\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{i}))
and let
D_{0}
be the component of
K
that is not contained in any
D_{i}
,
i\in[\ell]
. Since for each distinct
i,j\in[\ell]
the rings
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1}^{i},\gamma_{2}^{i})
and
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1}^{j},\gamma_{2}^{j})
are disjoint, we have that if
D_{i}\cap D_{j}\neq\emptyset
, then either
D_{i}\subseteq D_{j}
or
D_{j}\subseteq D_{i}
.

We consider a graph
H^{\prime}
that has one distinguished vertex
v_{0}
corresponding to
D_{0}
and a vertex
v_{i}
for each set
D_{i},i\in[\ell]
.
Two vertices
v_{i},v_{j}\in V(H)
for distinct
i,j\in[\ell]
are adjacent if
D_{i}\subseteq D_{j}
or
D_{j}\subseteq D_{i}
, but there is no
h\in[\ell]\setminus\{i,j\}
such that
D_{i}\subseteq D_{h}\subseteq D_{j}
or
D_{j}\subseteq D_{h}\subseteq D_{i}
, while
v_{0}
is adjacent to all vertices
v_{i},i\in[\ell]
for which there is no
h\neq i
such that
D_{i}\subseteq D_{h}
.
Note that
H
can be viewed as a tree rooted at
v_{0}
; every other vertex
v_{i}
has exactly one parent, that is the vertex
v_{j}
corresponding to the set
D_{j}
for which it holds that there is no other
h
different than
i
and
j
so that
D_{i}\subseteq D_{h}\subseteq D_{j}
.

Let
\ell_{1}
denote the number of leaves of
H
and let
\ell_{2}
denote the number of vertices of degree two of
H
.
We claim that
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}\leqslant r
. Indeed, the leaves of
H
correspond to disjoint sets
D_{i}
, each containing at least one element from
\widehat{T}
.
Moreover, for every vertex
v_{i}
of
H
that has exactly one child
v_{j}
, we get that
D_{i}\setminus D_{j}
intersects
\widehat{T}
. To see why this holds, observe that if
D_{i}\setminus D_{j}
does not intersect
\widehat{T}
, then
D_{i}\cap\widehat{T}=D_{j}\cap\widehat{T}
and therefore
(X_{i},Y_{i})
is equal to either
(X_{j},Y_{j})
or
(Y_{j},X_{j})
, a contradiction to
Claim

4.21
.
In summary, we have
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}
disjoint sets, each of which contains some element from
\widehat{T}
.

Therefore,
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}\leqslant|\widehat{T}|=r
. Moreover, using standard arguments, it is easy to see that the number of vertices of degree more than two in
H
is upper-bounded by
\ell_{1}-1
and therefore
|V(H)|\leqslant 2\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}-1\leqslant 2r-1
, which implies that
\ell\leqslant 2r-2
.
∎

Because of
Claim

4.22
, the overall algorithm runs in time
2^{r}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
and because of
Claim

4.23
the number of tuples in the obtained exhaustive ring decomposition is at most
2r-2
.
∎

4.4
Winding creates rings

In this subsection, we show that if we find a candidate solution that winds around a geodesic many times, then this situation gives rise to a dag-ring. This result will be applied in the presence of an exhaustive ring decomposition, in order to rule out large winding outside rings (see
Proposition

5.8
).
In order to state this result, we first define (winding) handles of paths.

Definition 4.24
.

Let
P,Q
be paths in a plane graph
G
. We say that
P
is a handle of
Q
if the endpoints of
P
are internal vertices of
Q
and
P
is internally disjoint from
Q
.

When
P
is a handle of
Q
, we denote by
C(Q,P)
the unique cycle contained in
Q\cup P
.
We say that
P
is a winding handle of
Q
if the endpoints of
Q
lie in different connected components of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus C(Q,P)
.
Otherwise
P
is called a regular handle of
Q
.

Proposition 4.25
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{T})
be a nice instance of
PDSP
,
\widehat{T}
be a terminal-superset of
\mathcal{T}
, and
Q
be a geodesic in
G
.
Also, let
P
be an oriented shortest
(x,y)
-path for some
(x,y)\in\mathcal{T}
and suppose that there exist
h
disjoint subpaths
P_{1},P_{2},\dots,P_{h}
of
P
, where
h>10\cdot(|\widehat{T}|+1)
, which are located on
P
in this order and each of them forms a winding handle of
Q
.
Then there exist:

1.

indices
\ell,r\in[h]
such that
\mathrm{Ring}(C(Q,P_{\ell}),C(Q,P_{r}))
does not contain any vertices from
\widehat{T}
,

2.

a splitting partition
(X,Y)
of
\widehat{T}
with
x\in X
,
y\in Y
, and

3.

a pair
(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
of
(X,Y)
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cuts}
with

\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\subseteq\mathrm{Ring}(C(Q,P_{\ell}),C(Q,P_{r}))

that represents an
(X,Y)
\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}
.

Proof.

We present the proof via a series of claims that reveal the structure of the subgraph
Q\cup P
gradually.
This allows us to accumulate notation that is used only within this proof.

For
i\in[h]
let us denote the two vertices of
V(P_{i})\cap V(Q)
as
v^{\prime}_{i},v^{\prime\prime}_{i}
, ordered in such a way that
v^{\prime}_{i}<_{P}v^{\prime\prime}_{i}
(see
Figure

6
).
Note that
v^{\prime\prime}_{i}<_{P}v^{\prime}_{i+1}
.
By
Lemma

3.3
we can choose an orientation of
Q
so that
v_{1}^{\prime}<_{Q}v_{1}^{\prime\prime}<_{Q}<v_{2}^{\prime}<_{Q}v_{2}^{\prime\prime}<_{Q}\dots<_{Q}v_{h}^{\prime\prime}
(that is, the relations
<_{Q}
and
<_{P}
coincide on this vertex set).
Let
x_{q},y_{q}
be the first and the last vertex of
Q
with respect to this orientation.

By the definition of a winding handle, for each
i\in[h]
the cycle
C(Q,P_{i})
separates the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
into two connected components, one containing
x_{q}
and one with
y_{q}
.
Let
R_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
denote the connected component of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus C(Q,P_{i})
containing
x_{q}
.

Claim 4.26
.

For each
i\in[h-1]
it holds that
R_{i}\subseteq R_{i+1}
.

Proof.

Because
v_{i}^{\prime\prime}<_{Q}v_{i+1}^{\prime}
and
v_{i}^{\prime\prime}<_{P}v_{i+1}^{\prime}
we infer that
C(Q,P_{i})
and
C(Q,P_{i+1})
are disjoint.
Next,
Q[x_{q},v_{i}^{\prime}]
is disjoint from
C(Q,P_{i+1})
so
v_{i}^{\prime}\in R_{i+1}
and consequently the entire cycle
C(Q,P_{i})
is contained in
R_{i+1}
.
This means that one of the connected components of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus C(Q,P_{i})
must be contained in
R_{i+1}
as well.
This component cannot contain
y_{q}
(which lies outside
R_{i+1}
) so it must be the one containing
x_{q}
, that is,
R_{i}
.
∎

Note that
\mathsf{cl}(R_{i})=R_{i}\cup C(Q,P_{i})
and
that
V(C(Q,P_{i}))
forms a
(V(C(Q,P_{i-1})),V(C(Q,P_{i+1})))
-separator
for every
i\in[2,h-1]
.
Next, we establish a crucial property of
Q\cup P
that will facilitate applying rerouting arguments.

Claim 4.27
.

Let
i,j\in[h]
be such that
j\geqslant i+2

and
u\in V(C(Q,P_{i}))
,
w\in V(C(Q,P_{j}))
.
Then there exists a shortest
(u,w)
-path in
G
that goes through
v_{i}^{\prime\prime}
and
v_{j}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

First suppose that both
u,w
belong to
V(P)
.
By
Observation

3.1
, the subpath
P[u,w]
is a shortest
(u,w)
-path.
We have
u\leqslant_{P}v_{i}^{\prime\prime}<_{P}v_{j}^{\prime}\leqslant_{P}w

so
P[u,w]
is the sought path.
The case when
u,w\in V(Q)
is analogous.

It remains to consider
u\in V(P)
,
w\in V(Q)
; again the symmetric case is analogous.
Let
J
be some shortest
(u,w)
-path.
Note that
V(C(Q,P_{i+1}))
is a
(u,w)
-separator
so there is
z\in V(J)\cap V(C(Q,P_{i+1}))
.

Case (1):
z\in V(P)
.
By
Observation

3.2
the concatenation
J^{\prime}=P[u,z]+J[z,w]
is a shortest
(u,w)
-path.
But
u\leqslant_{P}v_{i}^{\prime\prime}<_{P}z
so

v_{i}^{\prime\prime}\in V(J^{\prime})
.
Next, we take advantage of the fact that
v_{i}^{\prime\prime}\in V(Q)
and
v_{i}^{\prime\prime}<_{Q}v_{j}^{\prime}\leqslant_{Q}w
.
The concatenation
J^{\prime}[u,v_{i}^{\prime\prime}]+Q[v_{i}^{\prime\prime},w]

is a shortest
(u,w)
-path the goes through both
v_{i}^{\prime\prime}
and
v_{j}^{\prime}
.
See
Figure

6
for an illustration.

Case (2):
z\in V(Q)
.
Similarly as above, we obtain that
J^{\prime\prime}=J[u,z]+Q[z,w]
is a shortest
(u,w)
-path.
We have
z<_{Q}v_{j}^{\prime}\leqslant_{Q}w
so
v_{j}^{\prime}\in V(J^{\prime\prime})
.
Finally, we consider the geodesic
P[u,v_{j}^{\prime}]+J^{\prime\prime}[v_{j}^{\prime},w]
which visits
v_{i}^{\prime\prime}\in V(P[u,v_{j}^{\prime}])
.
The claim follows.
∎

.

E

(v_{1}^{\prime\prime},w)
-path so

(u,w)

As a consequence of this claim, we show that shortest paths cannot go “back and forth”.

Claim 4.28
.

Let
i\in[h-2]
and
u,w\in V(C(Q,P_{i}))
.
If
J
is a shortest
(u,w)
-path then
V(J)\cap V(C(Q,P_{i+2}))=\emptyset
.
Similarly, when
u,w\in V(C(Q,P_{i+2}))
then

V(J)\cap V(C(Q,P_{i}))=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Consider the first case.
Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
there exists
z\in V(J)\cap V(C(Q,P_{i+2}))
.
Clearly,
z\neq v_{i}^{\prime\prime}
because the latter vertex lies on
C(Q,P_{i})
.
By
Claim

4.27
, there exists a shortest
(u,z)
-path that goes through
v_{i}^{\prime\prime}
.
Because the edge weights are positive,
d_{G}(u,z)
must be strictly greater than
d_{G}(u,v_{i}^{\prime\prime})
.
Analogously, it holds that
d_{G}(w,z)>d_{G}(w,v_{i}^{\prime\prime})
.
Since
z
lies on a shortest
(u,w)
-path, we infer that
d_{G}(u,w)=d_{G}(u,z)+d_{G}(w,z)>d_{G}(u,v_{i}^{\prime\prime})+d_{G}(w,v_{i}^{\prime\prime})
.
This contradicts the triangle inequality and therefore such
z
cannot exists.

The case when
u,w\in V(C(Q,P_{i+2}))
and
z\in V(J)\cap V(C(Q,P_{i}))
is analogous but this time we exploit the fact that the path
J
goes through
v_{i+2}^{\prime}
, again because of
Claim

4.27
.
∎

Next, we
identify an interval
[\ell,r]\subseteq[1,h]
for which
R_{r}\setminus R_{\ell}
does not contain any elements from
\widehat{T}
.

Claim 4.29
.

There exists a partition
(X,Y)
of
\widehat{T}
with
x\in X
,
y\in Y
and an interval
[\ell,r]\subseteq[h]
such that
r-\ell=10
and
R_{i}\cap\widehat{T}=X
for each
i\in[\ell,r]
.

Proof.

By
Claim

4.26
we have
R_{i}\subseteq R_{j}
for any
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant h
so
|R_{i}\cap\widehat{T}|\leqslant|R_{j}\cap\widehat{T}|
.
The sequence
a_{i}=|R_{i}\cap\widehat{T}|
can increase at most
|\widehat{T}|
times.
Since
h>10\cdot(|\widehat{T}|+1)
there exist indices
\ell,r\in[h]
such that
a_{\ell}=a_{r}
and
r=\ell+10
.
Then
R_{i}\cap\widehat{T}
coincides for each
i\in[\ell,r]
.
We set
X=R_{r}\cap\widehat{T}
,
Y=\widehat{T}\setminus X
.

Now we argue that
x\in X
.
If
x\not\in X
then
x\not\in R_{r}
and the subpath

P[x,v_{\ell}^{\prime}]
would have to intersect
C(Q,P_{r})
.
Suppose there exists
z\in V(P[x,v_{\ell}^{\prime}])\cap V(C(Q,P_{r}))
.
Then
x\leqslant_{P}z<_{P}v_{\ell}^{\prime}
but on the other hand
v_{\ell}^{\prime}<_{P}v_{r}^{\prime}\leqslant_{P}z
what yields a contradiction.
The argument that
y\in Y
is symmetric.
∎

Next, we observe that when two vertices are either both “outside” or both “inside” then no shortest paths between them can intersect
C(Q,P_{\ell+2})
or
C(Q,P_{r-2})
.

Claim 4.30
.

Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
(resp.
x_{1},x_{2}\in Y
) and
J
be a shortest
(x_{1},x_{2})
-path.
Then
J
is disjoint from
C(Q,P_{\ell+2})

(resp.
C(Q,P_{r-2}))
.

Proof.

Consider
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
.
Note that
V(C(Q,P_{\ell}))
is a
(X,V(C(Q,P_{\ell+2}))
-separator. If
V(J)
intersected
V(C(Q,P_{\ell+2}))
then
J
would contain a subpath (still geodesic) between two vertices from
V(C(Q,P_{\ell}))
visiting a vertex from
V(C(Q,P_{\ell+2}))
.
This is impossible due to
Claim

4.28
.
The case with
x_{1},x_{2}\in Y
is analogous.
∎

Next, we show that given two vertices lying on the “closed ring” between
C(Q,P_{r-2})

and
C(Q,P_{\ell+2})
, the corresponding arc appears in the shortest paths DAGs for (oriented) shortest paths between split pairs of terminals.

Claim 4.31
.

Let
u,w
be vertices of
G
which lie in
\mathsf{cl}(R_{r-2}\setminus R_{\ell+2})
and
x_{1}\in X,\,y_{1}\in Y
.
If
(u,w)\in A((x_{1},y_{1})\text{-}\mathsf{dag})
then there exists a shortest
(x_{1},y_{1})
-path that visits
v_{\ell}^{\prime\prime},u,w,v_{r}^{\prime}
, in this order.

Proof.

Consider a shortest
(x_{1},y_{1})
-path
J
that goes through the edge
uw\in E(G)
with
u<_{J}w
.
The path
J[x_{1},u]
must visit a vertex from
V(C(Q,P_{\ell}))
and one from
V(C(Q,P_{\ell+2}))
.
We apply
Claim

4.27
to this subpath to find a geodesic with the same endpoints that visits
v_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}
.
By the replacement argument (
Observation

3.2
) we obtain a shortest
(x_{1},u)
-path
J^{\prime}
that goes through
v_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}
.
We exploit the same argument to obtain that
J^{\prime}+J[u,y_{1}]
is a shortest
(x_{1},y_{1})
-path that goes through
v_{\ell}^{\prime\prime},u,w
, in this order.
Analogously we can tweak the subpath between
w
and
y_{1}
to visit
v_{r}^{\prime}
.
∎

Claim 4.32
.

Let
u,w
be vertices of
G
which lie in
\mathsf{cl}(R_{r-2}\setminus R_{\ell+2})
and

x_{1},x_{2}\in X,\,y_{1},y_{2}\in Y
.
Then
(u,w)\in A((x_{1},y_{1})\text{-}\mathsf{dag})
if and only if
(u,w)\in A((x_{2},y_{2})\text{-}\mathsf{dag})
.

Proof.

Claim

4.31
implies that when

(u,w)\in A((x_{1},y_{1})\text{-}\mathsf{dag})
then
(u,w)\in A((v_{\ell}^{\prime\prime},v_{r}^{\prime})\text{-}\mathsf{dag})
.
The opposite implication holds as well: we can modify any shortest
(x_{1},y_{1})
-path to go through
v_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}
and
v_{r}^{\prime}
(by the same claim) and then we can replace the subpath from
v_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}
to
v_{r}^{\prime}
with any shortest
(v_{\ell}^{\prime\prime},v_{r}^{\prime})
-path.
In summary, the condition
(u,w)\in A((x_{1},y_{1})\text{-}\mathsf{dag})
is equivalent to
(u,w)\in A((v_{\ell}^{\prime\prime},v_{r}^{\prime})\text{-}\mathsf{dag})
which is equivalent to
(u,w)\in A((x_{2},y_{2})\text{-}\mathsf{dag})
.
∎

Now we orient the edges lying in the closure of
(R_{r-2}\setminus R_{\ell+2})
to be consistent with any shortest path that goes through this region, when oriented towards
Y
.

Figure

7
illustrates where particular objects are located with respect to

Claim 4.33
.

Let
U=V(G)\cap\mathsf{cl}(R_{r-2}\setminus R_{\ell+2})
.
We can orient the edges of

1.

For each
(u,w)\in A(D)

(x_{1},y_{1})\in\mathsf{split}_{\mathcal{T}}(X,Y)

(u,w)\in A((x_{1},y_{1})\text{-}\mathsf{dag})
.

For each
(u,w)\in A(D)
and
(x_{1},y_{1})\in\mathsf{same}\text{-}\mathsf{side}(X,Y)
it holds that neither
(u,w)
nor
(w,u)
belongs to
A((x_{1},y_{1})\text{-}\mathsf{dag})
.

Proof.

By
Claim

4.30
when
(x_{1},y_{1})\in\mathsf{same}\text{-}\mathsf{side}(X,Y)
then no shortest
(x_{1},y_{1})
-path can go through either

Since the instance is nice, every edge

(x_{1},y_{1})

This must happen for some

Hence we can construct

(x,y)\text{-}\mathsf{dag}
(recall that

) induced by

.
Then

3.7
.
∎

Claim 4.34
.

Let
i\in[\ell+2,r-4]
.
The set
U
admits a partition
(U_{X},U_{Y})

such that

,

U\setminus R_{i+2}\subseteq U_{Y}

and for each
(u,w)\in\vec{E}(U_{X},U_{Y})

(u,w)\in A(D)
.

Proof.

Note that the assumption

is needed to ensure that

V(G)\cap\mathsf{cl}(R_{i+2}\setminus R_{i})\subseteq U
.

Suppose that such a partition does not exists.
Then
D
admits a directed path from

to
U\cap\mathsf{cl}(R_{i})
.
This path must connect a vertex from

to one in
V(C(Q,P_{i}))
.
By
Claim

4.33
(

) this means that there exists a shortest
(x,y)
-path

that visits a vertex

Then

.

4.28
.
∎

Claim 4.35
.

For each
i\in[\ell+2,r-4]

there exists an
(X,Y)\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cut}

(V_{X},V_{Y})
in

V(G)\cap\mathsf{cl}(R_{i})\subseteq V_{X}
and

V(G)\setminus R_{i+2}\subseteq V_{Y}
.

We apply
Claim

4.34

to obtain a partition
(U_{X},U_{Y})
of
U

and consider the edge set

.
We define
V_{X}
as the reachability set of
V(G)\cap\mathsf{cl}(R_{i})
in the graph

and
V_{Y}=V(G)\setminus V_{X}
.
From

4.29
we know that
X\subseteq V_{X}
.

We argue that

5
Estimating the number of crossings

5.1
Homotopy

In this section we formalize the idea of counting the number of “non-trivial crossings” between a path
Q
and a solution
\mathcal{P}
, mentioned in
Section

2
.
To this end, we employ the concept of homotopy which, similarly as homology, detects similarities among topological objects.
However, we find homotopy to be a more intuitive language to conduct the reasoning, hence we will rely on it.
First, we identify a kind of handles which correspond to “trivial crossings”.

Definition 5.1

(Empty regular handle)
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{T})
be a nice instance of
PDSP
(resp.
PDAP
).
For a path
Q
in
G
and a regular handle
H
of
Q
we define
D(Q,H)
as the connected component of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus C(Q,H)
that does not contain the endpoints of
Q
.
We say that handle
H
is empty if
D(Q,H)
does not contain any terminal from
\mathcal{T}
.

We can use an empty regular handle
H
of
Q
to “pull” the path
Q
through
D(Q,H)
, obtaining a path that is in some sense topologically equivalent with respect to the terminal set.
The following definition formalizes this idea using the notion of homotopy.
We remark that our definition of homotopy deviates slightly from the one used in
[
24
]
.

Definition 5.2

(Homotopy)
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{T})
be a nice instance of
PDSP
(resp.
PDAP
),
Q
be a path in
G
and
H
be an empty regular handle of
Q
.
Next, let
q_{1},q_{2}
be the endpoints of
Q
and
r_{1},r_{2}
be the endpoints of
H
so that
q_{1}<_{Q}r_{1}<_{Q}r_{2}<_{Q}q_{2}
for some orientation of
Q
.
We define the path
Q^{H}
as the concatenation
Q[q_{1},r_{1}]+H+Q[r_{2},q_{2}]
.
We say that
Q^{H}
has been obtained from
Q
by a pull operation.

We say that path
Q_{2}
is homotopic
to
Q_{1}
if
Q_{2}
can be obtained from
Q_{1}
by a (possibly empty) series of pull operations.

Note that our notion of homotopy preserves the endpoints of path
Q
as well the first and the last edge on
Q
, because the attachments of a handle are always internal vertices of
Q
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Homotopy forms an equivalence relation.

Proof.

Transitivity is obvious.
Symmetry follows from the fact that when a
(v_{1},v_{2})
-path
H
is an empty regular handle of
Q
then
Q[v_{1},v_{2}]
is an empty regular handle of
Q^{H}
.
∎

We are in position to formalize the idea of counting non-trivial crossings between a path
Q
and a solution
\mathcal{P}
.
Intuitively, a crossing should be considered trivial when it can be avoided by some continuous shift of
Q
, which is formalized using homotopy.
Effectively we would like to count only those crossings that cannot be avoided by any tweak of
Q
.

For a subgraph
Q
of
G
we define
\partial(Q)
as the subset of edges in
E(G)\setminus E(Q)
with at least one endpoint in
V(Q)
.
Note that when
Q
is a path then any chord of
Q
belongs to
\partial(Q)
even though both its endpoints belong to
V(Q)
.

Definition 5.4
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{T})
be a nice instance of
PDSP
(resp.
PDAP
),
\mathcal{P}=(P_{1},\dots,P_{k})
be some solution to
(G,\mathcal{T})
,
and
Q
be some path in
G
.
We define
\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)
as the number of edges incident to
Q
that are used by
\mathcal{P}
, that is,

\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left|E(P_{i})\cap\partial(Q)\right|.

Furthermore, we define the combinatorial load
\mathsf{comb}\text{-}\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)
as the minimum
\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},\widehat{Q})
over all paths
\widehat{Q}
that are homotopic to
Q
.

Figure 8:

There are three
P_{1}
-handles of
Q
: the two marked in bold are regular handles and one winding handle is dotted.
The handle
H
is empty as the set
D(Q,H)
does not contain any terminals.
The path
Q^{H}=Q[q_{1},r_{1}]+H+Q[r_{2},q_{2}]
is homotopic to
Q
.
We have
\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)=20
and
\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q^{H})=10
.

In
Section

6.2
we will extend the definition of load to the case where
Q
is a dual path and introduce topological and algebraic counterparts of combinatorial load.

For paths
P,Q
we say that a handle
H
of
Q
is a
P
-handle
whenever
H
is a subpath of
P
.
For a path family
\mathcal{P}
we say
H
is a
\mathcal{P}
-handle
when it is a
P
-handle for some
P\in\mathcal{P}
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{P}
be as in
Definition

5.4

and
Q
be a geodesic in
G
.
Next, let
H
be a
\mathcal{P}
-handle of
Q
.
Then
\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q^{H})<\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)
.

Proof.

Let
v_{1}<_{Q}v_{2}
be the endpoints of
H
.
Consider any edge
e\in E(\mathcal{P})\cap\partial(Q^{H})
.
The edge
e
cannot be incident to an internal vertex of
H
because
H
is a subpath of a path from
\mathcal{P}
.
Suppose that
e\in E(Q)
.
Then
e
is an edge between one of the endpoints of
H
and a vertex
v_{3}
satisfying
v_{1}<_{Q}<v_{3}<_{Q}v_{2}
.
But this contradicts the monotonicity of crossings (
Lemma

3.3
).
Hence
e\in\partial(Q)
and
e
also contributes to
\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)
.
On the other hand,
H
contains at least one edge (note that
H
might possibly consist of a single edge) that contributes to
\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)
but not to
\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q^{H})
.
The lemma follows.
∎

The following lemma will facilitate estimation of
\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
P,Q
be paths in a graph
G
and
\ell
be the number of
P
-handles of
Q
.
Then
|E(P)\cap\partial(Q)|\leqslant 2\ell+6
.

Proof.

Consider
e\in E(P)\cap\partial(Q)
.
There are 3 scenarios.

•

e
is incident to one of the endpoints of
Q
; there are at most 4 such edges.

•

e
belongs to one of two subpaths of
P
between an endpoint of
P
and the closest vertex that is an internal vertex of
Q
; there are at most 2 such edges.

•

e
belongs to a
P
-handle of
Q
; there are
2\ell
such edges.

The lemma follows.
∎

5.2
Crossings outside rings

We now explain how an exhaustive ring decomposition comes in useful for estimating the number of “non-trivial crossings”.
We will use
Proposition

4.25
to justify that a geodesic
Q
disjoint from all the rings in the decomposition cannot have too many winding
\mathcal{P}
-handles for any solution
\mathcal{P}
.
We will also bound the number of non-empty regular
\mathcal{P}
-handles using the following fact.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
Q,P
be geodesics in graph
G
.
Next, let
H_{1},H_{2}
be two distinct regular
P
-handles of
Q
.
Then
D(Q,H_{1})\cap D(Q,H_{2})=\emptyset
.

Proof.

From the definition of a handle we obtain that
H_{1},H_{2}
are internally disjoint subpaths of
P
.
They may potentially intersect only at a common endpoint.
Consider some orientation of
P
.
Let
v_{1},u_{1}
be the endpoints of
H_{1}
and
v_{2},u_{2}
be the endpoints of
H_{2}
ordered in such a way that
v_{1}<_{P}u_{1}\leqslant_{P}v_{2}<_{P}u_{2}
.
By
Lemma

3.3
we know that we can orient
Q
so that
v_{1}<_{Q}u_{1}\leqslant_{Q}v_{2}<_{Q}u_{2}
.
Let
q_{2}
be the last vertex on
Q
with respect to this orientation.
Observe that
Q[u_{2},q_{2}]\cap C(Q,H_{1})=\emptyset
so
u_{2}
belongs to the same connected component of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus C(Q,H_{1})
as
q_{2}
.
Consequently,
u_{2}\not\in D(Q,H_{1})
.
Since both
Q[v_{2},u_{2}]
and
P[v_{2},u_{2}]
are disjoint from
C(Q,H_{1})\setminus\{u_{1}\}
we get that
C(Q,H_{2})\cap D(Q,H_{1})=\emptyset
.
Therefore, either
D(Q,H_{1})
is fully contained in
D(Q,H_{2})
or they are disjoint.
By a symmetric argument we obtain
C(Q,H_{1})\cap D(Q,H_{2})=\emptyset
which rules out the first option.
The lemma follows.
∎

We say that a path
Q
intersects an
(X,Y)\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}

(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
if
Q\cap\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{T})

be a nice instance of
PDSP
and
\mathcal{P}=(P_{1},\dots,P_{k})
be some solution to
(G,\mathcal{T})
.
Next, let
\widehat{T}
be some terminal-superset of
\mathcal{T}
,
\mathcal{R}
be an exhaustive ring decomposition for
(G,\mathcal{T},\widehat{T})
and
\widehat{Q}
be a geodesic in
G
which does not intersect any
\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}
from
\mathcal{R}
.
Then
\mathsf{comb}\text{-}\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},\widehat{Q})\leqslant\mathcal{O}(|\widehat{T}|^{2})
.

Proof.

We begin by showing that the properties of
\widehat{Q}
are preserved by certain pull operations.

Claim 5.9
.

Let
H
be an empty regular
\mathcal{P}
-handle of
\widehat{Q}
.
Then
\widehat{Q}^{H}
is a geodesic and it does not intersect any
\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}
from
\mathcal{R}
.

Proof.

The path
H
is a geodesic as a subpath of a path from
\mathcal{P}
.
Then
Observation

3.2
implies that
\widehat{Q}^{H}
is a geodesic.
Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
H
intersects

\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
where
(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
represents some
\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}
from
\mathcal{R}
.
By
Observation

4.4
(1),
H
can cross
\gamma
at most once hence
\gamma
separates the endpoints of
H
.
But these endpoints lie on
\widehat{Q}
so some edge in
\widehat{Q}
belongs to
\gamma
.
This contradicts the assumption that
\widehat{Q}
is disjoint from
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
.
As a consequence, we infer that
H
as well as
\widehat{Q}^{H}
are disjoint from all the rings in
\mathcal{R}
.
∎

While
\widehat{Q}
has an empty regular
\mathcal{P}
-handle
H
, we replace
\widehat{Q}
by
\widehat{Q}^{H}
.
The claim above ensures that the conditions of the proposition are maintained.
By
Lemma

5.5
we know that this process must terminate at some point.
Let
Q
denote the path obtained in the end of this process; then we know that
Q
has no empty regular
\mathcal{P}
-handles.
Since
Q
is homotopic to
\widehat{Q}
we have
\mathsf{comb}\text{-}\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},\widehat{Q})\leqslant\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)
.
Therefore, is suffices to count the number of
\mathcal{P}
-handles of
Q
that are either non-empty regular handles or winding handles.

Claim 5.10
.

For each
i\in[k]
there are at most
2k
non-empty regular
P_{i}
-handles of
Q
.

Proof.

Let
P_{i}^{1},\dots,P_{i}^{t}
be the collection of non-empty regular
P_{i}
-handles of
Q
.
By
Lemma

5.7
the sets
D(Q,P_{i}^{j})_{j=1}^{t}
are pairwise-
disjoint.
Next, non-emptiness means that each set
D(Q,P_{i}^{j})
contains some terminal from
\mathcal{T}
.
There are only
2k
terminals which yields an upper bound on
t
.
∎

We move on to the analysis of the winding handles.
Let
\widehat{k}=|\widehat{T}|
. Note that
2k\leqslant\widehat{k}
.

Claim 5.11
.

For each
i\in[k]
there are at most
20\cdot(\widehat{k}+1)
winding
P_{i}
-handles of
Q
.

Proof.

Suppose, for the sake of contradictions, that there are
20\cdot(\widehat{k}+1)+1
winding
P_{i}
-handles of
Q
.
Then there is a family of
10\cdot(\widehat{k}+1)+1
many such handles that are vertex-disjoint.

Let
P_{i}[Q]
be the subpath of
P_{i}
between its first and last intersection with
Q
, inclusively.
We apply
Proposition

4.25

to obtain that there exists a region
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
two winding handles
P_{i}^{1},P_{i}^{2}\subseteq P_{i}[Q]
of
Q
, and a splitting partition
(X,Y)
of
\mathcal{T}

such that (i) the boundary of
U
is
C(Q,P_{i}^{1})\cup C(Q,P_{i}^{2})
, (ii)
U
contains no vertex from
\widehat{T}
, (iii) there exists an
(X,Y)\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}

(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
with
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\subseteq U
.

We argue that
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
is disjoint from all the rings in
\mathcal{R}
.
Recall that
Q
is disjoint from all the rings in
\mathcal{R}
.
By
Observation

4.4
(1) whenever the path
P_{i}
enters any
\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}
through a
\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cut}

\gamma
then it cannot cross
\gamma
again.
Since no
\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cut}
from
\mathcal{R}
separates any vertices on
Q
, the path
P_{i}[Q]
must be also disjoint from all the rings in
\mathcal{R}
.
Since
P_{i}^{1},P_{i}^{2}
are subpaths of
P_{i}[Q]
, we infer that
C(Q,P_{i}^{1}),C(Q,P_{i}^{2})
are also disjoint from all the rings in
\mathcal{R}
.

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
U
intersects
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4})
where
(\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4})
represents a
\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}
from
\mathcal{R}
.
Since
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4})
is disjoint from the boundary of
U
, it must be that
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4})\subseteq U
.
Let
\Delta_{3}
denote
\mathrm{Disc}(\mathrm{Cycle}(\gamma_{3}))
.
Recall that
\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4}
are
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\text{-}\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{cuts}
for some splitting partition
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
so in particular
\Delta_{3}
contains at least one terminal.
Consequently,
\Delta_{3}
cannot be fully contained in
U
, which contains no terminals.
Likewise, the complement of
\Delta_{3}
also cannot be contained in
U
.
Because the boundary of
U
is a union of two connected sets
C(Q,P_{i}^{1}),C(Q,P_{i}^{2})
, exactly one of them must be contained in
\Delta_{3}
.
This means that
\gamma_{3}
separates a pair of vertices from
Q
and this contradicts the assumption that
Q
is disjoint from
\mathrm{Ring}(\gamma_{3},\gamma_{4})
.

We have established that no
\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}
from
\mathcal{R}
intersects
U
.
Consequently,
(X,Y,\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
forms an extension to the ring decomposition
\mathcal{R}
.
This contradicts the assumption that
\mathcal{R}
is exhaustive.
The claim follows.
∎

We are in position to estimate
\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)
.
By Claims
5.11
and
5.10
, the number of
P_{i}
-handles of
Q
is at most
20\cdot(\widehat{k}+1)+2k
, for each
i\in[k]
.
Then by
Lemma

5.6
we have
|E(P_{i})\cap\partial(Q)|\leqslant 40\cdot(\widehat{k}+1)+4k+6\leqslant 46\cdot(\widehat{k}+1)
.
We conclude that
\mathsf{comb}\text{-}\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},\widehat{Q})\leqslant\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)\leqslant k\cdot 46\cdot(\widehat{k}+1)=\mathcal{O}(\widehat{k}^{2})
.
∎

5.3
Rerouting inside a ring

In order to deal with paths traversing a
\mathsf{dag}\text{-}\mathsf{ring}
, we will rely on a rerouting argument by Cygan et al.
[
12

, which in turn is based on the work by Ding et al.

We now state the rerouting argument that will allow us to bound the winding numbers of the solution paths inside a DAG-ring.

5.5
Geodesic Steiner tree

We are in position to construct a Steiner tree that will work as a blueprint for enumerating homology classes, as discussed in

We start with an arbitrary Steiner tree spanning the terminals and iteratively refine it using an exhaustive ring decomposition.
Then we will take advantage of Propositions

Before proceeding to the statement of the next result, we define

6
Retrieving a solution via homology

In this section we complete the proof of
Theorem

1.1
by showing how to reduce the considered problem, with the help of
Proposition

5.21
, to an FPT number of subproblems conforming to the algebraic framework of Schrijver
[
28
]
. We remark that while the idea of searching for a solution to the
Disjoint Paths
problem of a fixed topological shape using the auxiliary algebraic problem of
Homology Feasibility
is due to Schrijver, the concrete implementation of the reduction needed here, and in particular the fine bounds on the number of homology classes that need to be considered, are new to this work.

6.1
Group-labelled graphs

First, we need to introduce an algebraic language for speaking about the topology of sets of paths in plane-embedded graphs. This language — of group-labeled graphs — was used by Schrijver in his work on vertex-disjoint paths in planar digraphs
[
28
]
. As mentioned, we will eventually use some of his results.

Oriented walks.

Throughout this section we fix
G
to be a connected multigraph together with an embedding in the plane. Note that in
G
we allow both parallel edges (edges connecting the same pair of endpoints) and loops (edges connecting a vertex with itself, embedded as closed curves).
Recall that the set of faces of
G
is denoted by
F(G)
.

An
oriented walk
in
G
is a sequence
\vec{W}=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{p})
of elements of
\vec{E}(G)
such that the tail
a_{i+1}
is the head of
a_{i}
, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p-1\}
. The
reversal
of
\vec{W}
is
\vec{W}^{-1}=(a_{p}^{-1},\ldots,a_{1}^{-1})
. Oriented walks in the dual graph
G^{\star}
are defined in the same way. An oriented walk is an
oriented path
if it visits every vertex of the graph at most once. Every path
P
in
G
gives rise to two oriented paths, called the
orientations
of
P
.

Groups and labellings.

We will use the multiplicative notation for groups: if
\Gamma
is a group, the result of applying the binary group operation to elements
a,b\in\Gamma
will be denoted by
a\cdot b
or just
ab
, the inverse of an element
a\in\Gamma
will be denoted by
a^{-1}
, and the identity element of
\Gamma
will be denoted by
1_{\Gamma}
.

Suppose
\Sigma
is a finite alphabet. We define
\overline{\Sigma}=\{a,a^{-1}\colon a\in\Sigma\}
and a mapping
\mathsf{reduce}\colon\overline{\Sigma}^{\star}\to\overline{\Sigma}^{\star}
as follows: for
w\in\overline{\Sigma}^{\star}
,
\mathsf{reduce}(w)
is obtained by exhaustively removing subwords of the form
aa^{-1}
or
a^{-1}a
for
a\in\Sigma
. (It is well-known and easy to see that the result of such exhaustive removal is unique.) Thus, the image of
\mathsf{reduce}
can be endowed with an associative binary operation
\cdot
defined as
u\cdot w=\mathsf{reduce}(uw)
, with identity element being the empty word and the inverse operation defined by reverting the word and replacing every symbol by its inverse. Thus, the image of
\mathsf{reduce}
becomes the
free group
on generators
\Sigma
, which we will denote by
\langle\Sigma\rangle
. All groups used in this paper will be of this form.

For a group
\Gamma
, a
\Gamma
-labelling
of
G
is a function
\lambda\colon\vec{E}(G)\to\Gamma
satisfying

\lambda(a^{-1})=(\lambda(a))^{-1}\qquad\textrm{for each arc }a\in\vec{E}(G).

If
\vec{W}=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{p})
is an oriented walk in
G
, then we define

\lambda(\vec{W})=\lambda(a_{1})\cdot\lambda(a_{2})\cdot\ldots\cdot\lambda(a_{p}).

The labelling
\lambda
naturally gives rise to a
\Gamma
-labelling
\lambda^{\star}
of
G^{\star}
, defined as

\lambda^{\star}(a^{\star})=\lambda(a)\qquad\textrm{for each arc }a\in\vec{E}(G).

Then
\lambda^{\star}
can be extended to walks in
G^{\star}
in the same way as this was done for
G
.

Characteristic words.

Suppose now that
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
is an instance of
Planar Disjoint Annotated Paths
, where
\mathcal{T}=\{(s_{1},t_{1}),\ldots,(s_{k},t_{k})\}
.
We define the alphabet
\Sigma=\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}=\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
, and we let
\Gamma=\Gamma_{\mathcal{T}}=\langle\Sigma\rangle
be the free group with generators

. Then, given a solution
\mathcal{P}=(P_{1},\ldots,P_{k})
to
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
, where every

is considered an oriented path that starts in

and ends in

, we define the
\Gamma
-labelling

of

as follows: for an arc

, set

•

\lambda_{\mathcal{P}}(a)=i
if some

traverses

•

\lambda_{\mathcal{P}}(a)=i^{-1}
if some

traverses

•

\lambda_{\mathcal{P}}(a)=1_{\Gamma}
if no

traverses

.

It is straightforward to check that thus,
\lambda_{\mathcal{P}}
is a
\Gamma
-labelling of

.

Now, if
\vec{W}
is an oriented walk in the dual

, then we define the
characteristic word
of
\mathcal{P}
with respect to

as

\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{W})=\lambda^{\star}_{\mathcal{P}}(\vec{W}).

Intuitively speaking,

gathers information about the crossings of
\vec{W}
by the paths of
\mathcal{P}
when
\vec{W}
is traversed along its orientation. Every counter-clockwise crossing with

contributes with

contributes with

, and consecutive occurrences of

cancel out. Note that

An oriented walk is an

if it begins and ends at the same vertex, and otherwise all the vertices traversed by it are pairwise different and different from the beginning. An oriented cycle is

if one of the two regions in which it divides the plane does not contain any terminals from

. We will use the following straightforward claim, which can be imagined as a discrete analogue of Green’s Theorem.

Lemma 6.1
.

If
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
is a nice instance of

and

Choose a starting vertex anywhere on

be the sequence of consecutive arcs of

6.2
Topological load and algebraic load

Before we continue, we need to make a reinterpretation of the notion of the minimum load of a path
Q
with respect to a family of paths
\mathcal{P}
(see
Definition

5.4
). Intuitively, it will be more convenient to work with a definition that considers
Q
fixed and applies transformations to
\mathcal{P}
, rather than the other way round, as was the case in
Definition

5.4
. Also, we need
Q
to be a path in the dual graph
G^{\star}
, rather than in
G
itself.

Load of dual paths.

First, we need to extend the notions of (minimum) load also to paths in the dual graph
G^{\star}
. This is done in a natural manner as follows. Suppose
(G,\mathcal{T})
is a nice instance of
PDSP
and
Q
is a path in
G^{\star}
. We define (empty) handles of
Q
as before, except that a handle of
Q
is a path in
G^{\star}
instead of in
G
. This gives an obvious extension of the notions of pull operations and of homotopy to paths in
G^{\star}
(see
Definition

5.2
). Now, for a path
Q
in
G^{\star}
and a solution
\mathcal{P}
of
(G,\mathcal{T})
, we define
\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)
to be the number of edges of
Q
that cross an edge of
\bigcup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}E(P)
, and again
\mathsf{comb}\text{-}\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)
is defined as the minimum
\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},\widehat{Q})
among paths
\widehat{Q}
homotopic to
Q
.

Topological load.

First, we introduce a notion of load that is almost equivalent to that of
Definition

5.4
, but relies on untangling the path family
\mathcal{P}
rather than modifying the path
Q
. The notion is defined in topological terms, so we need a few definitions.

A
curve
in the plane is a homeomorphic image of the interval
[0,1]
. Two curves
P,Q
are in

if
P\cap Q
consists of a finite number of points, none of which is an endpoint of
P
or
Q
, and for every point
x\in P\cap Q
there is an open neighborhood
N
of
x
such that
N-P
has two connected components, both intersecting
Q
, and
N-Q
has two connected components, both intersecting
P
(intuitively,
P
and
Q
cross at
x
so that they appear in an interlacing manner around
x
).

For a positive integer
k
, a
k
-configuration
is a pair
{\mathcal{C}}=(\mathcal{P},Q)
, where
Q
is a curve,
\mathcal{P}
is a family of
k
pairwise disjoint curves, and
Q
is in general position with each
P\in\mathcal{P}
. Thus,
{\mathcal{C}}
naturally gives rise to a plane multigraph
H_{\mathcal{C}}
defined as follows:

•

The vertex set of
H_{\mathcal{C}}
consists of the endpoints of the curves of
\mathcal{P}\cup\{Q\}
and the intersection points of
Q
with the paths of
\mathcal{P}
.

•

Let a
segment
be a maximal subcurve of
Q
or any
P\in\mathcal{P}
that does not contain any vertex of
H_{\mathcal{C}}
in its interior; there are naturally
Q
-segments (contained in
Q
) and
\mathcal{P}
-segments (contained in paths of
\mathcal{P}
). For every segment

, introduce an edge

to

connecting the two endpoints of

; thus

naturally has

-edges and
\mathcal{P}
-edges. The segment

is deemed to be the embedding of

in the plane, thus making

a plane multigraph.

Now, we consider the following operation of

: as long as

contains a face of length

homeomorphic to an open disc, say with vertices

, replace the three-edge path consisting of
Q
-edges incident to
u
and
v
with a single
Q
-edge with the same endpoints, and replace the three-edge path consisting of
\mathcal{P}
-edges incident to
u
with a single
\mathcal{P}
-edge with the same endpoints, so that the new edges do not intersect; see
Figure

10

\widetilde{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
. We define the
topological load
of configuration
{\mathcal{C}}
, denoted
\mathsf{top}\text{-}\mathsf{load}({\mathcal{C}})
as the number of vertices of
\widetilde{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
that are not the endpoints of the paths of
\mathcal{P}\cup\{Q\}
.

A visualization of the simplification operation.

-edges are depicted in blue and

-edges are depicted in red.

The following lemma links the topological load with the notion of combinatorial load considered in

.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
be an instance of
Planar Annotated Disjoint Paths
,
\mathcal{P}
be a solution to
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
, and

be a path in

. Then
(\mathcal{P},Q)
is a configuration and
\mathsf{top}\text{-}\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)\leqslant\mathsf{comb}\text{-}\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)

Proof.

That
(\mathcal{P},Q)
is a configuration is obvious. For the claimed inequality, the key observation is the following: if
\widehat{Q}
is homotopic to
Q
, then
\widetilde{H}_{(\mathcal{P},Q)}=\widetilde{H}_{(\mathcal{P},\widehat{Q})}
. To see this statement, first note that it suffices to give a proof under the assumption that
Q
and
\widehat{Q}
differ by one pull operation, which moreover involves an empty handle that delimits a single face of
G^{\star}
. Indeed, every pull operation can be presented as a composition of pull operations as above. Now, under this assumption, it is easy to see that either
H_{(\mathcal{P},Q)}
and
H_{(\mathcal{P},\widehat{Q})}
are equal, or they differ by one simplification operation. In both cases, we have
\widetilde{H}_{(\mathcal{P},Q)}=\widetilde{H}_{(\mathcal{P},\widehat{Q})}
.

Coming back to the proof of the claimed inequality, let
\widehat{Q}
be the path homotopic to
Q
such that
\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},\widehat{Q})=\mathsf{comb}\text{-}\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)
. By the observation of the previous paragraph, we have
\widetilde{H}_{(\mathcal{P},Q)}=\widetilde{H}_{(\mathcal{P},\widehat{Q})}
, hence
\mathsf{top}\text{-}\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)=\mathsf{top}\text{-}\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},\widehat{Q})
. Finally, we clearly have
\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},\widehat{Q})\geqslant\mathsf{top}\text{-}\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},\widehat{Q})
, because
H_{(\mathcal{P},\widehat{Q})}
has at least as many vertices as
\widetilde{H}_{(\mathcal{P},\widehat{Q})}
. By combining the three relations above we conclude that
\mathsf{top}\text{-}\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)\leqslant\mathsf{comb}\text{-}\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)
, as claimed.
∎

Algebraic load.

Next, we introduce another notion of load. This time, the notion is tailored to the toolbox of Schrijver, and therefore is algebraic in nature.

Let
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
be a nice instance of
Planar Annotated Disjoint Paths
,
\mathcal{P}
be a solution to this instance, and
Q
be a path in
G^{\star}
. We define the
algebraic load
of
Q
with respect to
\mathcal{P}
, denoted
\mathsf{alg}\text{-}\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)
, as

\mathsf{alg}\text{-}\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)=|\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Q})|,

where
\vec{Q}
is any orientation of
Q
. (The words
\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Q})
for the two orientations are inverses of each other, hence they have the same length.) We have the following easy observation.

Lemma 6.3
.

For every nice instance
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
of
Planar Annotated Disjoint Paths
, solution
\mathcal{P}
to this instance, and path
Q
in
G^{\star}
, we have
\mathsf{alg}\text{-}\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)\leqslant\mathsf{top}\text{-}\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)
.

Proof.

Let
\vec{Q}
be any orientation of
Q
and let
w
be the word over
\overline{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{T}}
consisting of the indices or their inverses of consecutive paths of
\mathcal{P}
crossed along
\vec{Q}
, so that
\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Q})=\mathsf{reduce}(w)
. Observe that in the definition of the topological load of the configuration
(\mathcal{P},Q)
, every consecutive operation of simplification corresponds to removing some pair of consecutive symbols of the form
ii^{-1}
or
i^{-1}i
from
w
. Therefore, the number of non-endpoint vertices left in
\widetilde{H}_{(\mathcal{P},Q)}
after applying simplifications exhaustively cannot be smaller than the number of symbols left in
w
after applying the cancellations exhaustively (i.e., applying the
\mathsf{reduce}(\cdot)
operator). It follows that
\mathsf{alg}\text{-}\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)=|\mathsf{reduce}(w)|\leqslant\mathsf{top}\text{-}\mathsf{load}(\mathcal{P},Q)
, as claimed.
∎

We note that the inequality provided by
Lemma

6.3
cannot be reversed: it is possible that the algebraic load of a path
Q
is significantly smaller than its topological load. We leave the easy task of finding examples to the reader.

6.3
Skeletons

We now gather all the observations obtained so far to prove a key proposition: given an instance of
Planar Annotated Disjoint Paths
, we can compute a “skeleton” — a tree in the dual connecting all the terminals — with the property that if there is a solution, then there is also a solution that crosses this skeleton a small number of times. The number of crossings will be expressed in terms of the topological load. The reader might think of skeletons as of the terminal-connecting trees provided by
Proposition

5.21
, except for technical modifications that make them live in the dual of the graph, rather than in the primal.

We proceed to formal details.
Let
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
be a nice instance of
Planar Annotated Disjoint Paths
. Recall that then each terminal
t
from
\mathcal{T}
has degree
1
in
G
, hence there is a single face
f_{t}\in F(G)
incident to
t
. Let
F_{\mathcal{T}}
be the set of those
terminal faces

f_{t}
for terminal vertices
t
from
\mathcal{T}
. A
skeleton
of
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
is an inclusion-wise minimal tree
K
that is a subgraph of
G^{\star}
and contains all the faces of
F_{\mathcal{T}}
. A vertex
f
of

is called
principal
if it belongs to
F_{\mathcal{T}}
or has degree at least
3
in
K
, and
non-principal
otherwise. A
spinal
path of

is a maximal path in

whose all internal vertices are non-principal. The set of all spinal paths of

will be denoted by

, and we let

be the set of all orientations of spinal paths of

|\mathcal{T}|=k

|{\cal Q}(K)|\leqslant 4k-3
and
|\vec{\cal Q}(K)|\leqslant 8k-6
.

Now if
\mathcal{P}
is a solution to
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
, then the
topological load
of a skeleton

with respect to
\mathcal{P}
is defined as the maximum topological load (with respect to
\mathcal{P}
) among the spinal paths of

. The algebraic load of
K
with respect to
\mathcal{P}
is defined analogously.

By a slight abuse of notation, throughout the rest of this section, we see every collection
\mathcal{D}
of oriented subgraphs as mapping from arcs to sets of indices.
Precisely,
given a collection
\mathcal{D}=(D_{1},\ldots,D_{k})
of oriented subgraphs of a graph

, for every arc

we use
\mathcal{D}(a)\subseteq\overline{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{T}}
to denote the set defined as follows:
for each

if

.

With these definitions in place, we can state and prove our proposition.

Proposition 6.4
.

There exists an algorithm that, given a nice instance
(G,\mathcal{T})
of
PDSP
with
k=|\mathcal{T}|
, runs in time

has no solution, or outputs a nice instance

•

If
(G,\mathcal{T})

(G^{\prime},\mathcal{T}^{\prime},\mathcal{D})

such that the topological load of

\mathcal{P}
is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(k^{3})
.

•

If
(G^{\prime},\mathcal{T}^{\prime},\mathcal{D})

.

Proof.

We first apply the algorithm of
Proposition

5.21

6.4
Enumerating the homology classes

In this section we describe how the algebraic framework of Schrijver
[
28
]
can be used in combination with
Proposition

6.4
to solve the
Planar Disjoint Shortest Paths
in fpt time, thereby proving
Theorem

1.1
. First, we need to recall the said framework, which requires several auxiliary definitions.

6.4.1
Homology Feasibility

In the
Homology Feasibility
problem we will be working with plane
\Gamma
-labelled multigraphs, as discussed in
Section

6.1
, where
\Gamma=\langle\Sigma\rangle
is a fixed free group over some finite set of generators
\Sigma
. Recall that this means that the elements of
\Gamma
are words over
\overline{\Sigma}=\{a,a^{-1}\colon a\in\Sigma\}
that are
reduced
, that is, do not contain any symbol and its inverse in succession.
For two elements
a,b
of
\Gamma
, we say that
a
is a
subword
of
b
if there exist
c,d\in\Gamma
so that
b
is the concatenation of
c
,
a
,
d
, in this order. Note that this entails
b=cad
in the group
\Gamma
, but is not the same. We say that a set
A\subseteq\Gamma
is
hereditary
if whenever
b\in A
, all the subwords of
b
belong to
A
as well. Also, we will denote
A^{-1}=\{a^{-1}\colon a\in A\}
. Note that if
A
is hereditary, so is also
A^{-1}
.

Let
G
be a connected plane multigraph. A
\Gamma
-shift
of
G
is a function
\psi\colon F(G)\to\Gamma
. (Recall here that
F(G)
is the set of faces of
G
.)
Given a
\Gamma
-shift
\psi
and a
\Gamma
-labelling
\lambda
of
G
, we may apply
\psi
to
\lambda
to obtain a new
\Gamma
-labelling
\psi[\lambda]
defined as follows: for all
a\in\vec{E}(G)
, if
a^{\star}=(f,f^{\prime})
, then we set

\psi[\lambda](a)=\psi(f)\cdot\lambda(a)\cdot\psi(f^{\prime})^{-1}\qquad\textrm{for every arc }a\in\vec{E}(G).

It is straightforward to verify that
\psi[\lambda]
is again a
\Gamma
-labelling of
G
.

Next, for the purpose of setting restrictions on the sought labellings, we introduce the following definition. A
domain mapping
is a mapping that assigns to every arc
a\in\vec{E}(G)
its
domain

\Delta(a)
and to each vertex
v\in V(G)
its
domain

\Delta(v)
so that

•

\Delta(a^{-1})=\Delta(a)^{-1}
for each
a\in\vec{E}(G)
,

•

\Delta(v)=\Delta(v)^{-1}
for each
v\in V(G)
, and

•

hereditary sets
\Delta(x)
for all
x\in V(G)\cup\vec{E}(G)
.

We say that a
\Gamma
-labelling
\lambda

conforms
to the domain mapping
\Delta
if

•

for every
a\in\vec{E}(G)
, we have
\lambda(a)\in\Delta(a)
, and

•

for every vertex
v\in\vec{E}(G)
and a sequence of arcs
a_{1},\ldots,a_{p}\in\vec{E}(G)
with heads
v
that are consecutive in the cyclic order around
v
, we have
\lambda(a_{1})\lambda(a_{2})\ldots\lambda(a_{p})\in\Delta(v)
.

For simplicity we assume that the domains are always given on input explicitly, as list of elements, even though in the framework of Schrijver one can even only assume oracle access to those sets.

Now, an instance of the
Homology Feasibility
problem consists of the following:

•

a connected plane graph
G
;

•

a
\Gamma
-labelling
\lambda
of
G
, where
\Gamma
is a free group on
k
given generators; and

•

a domain mapping
\Delta
; and

•

a set of
fixed faces

R\subseteq F(G)
.

The question is whether there exists a
\Gamma
-shift
\psi
of
G
such that

•

\psi(f)=1_{\Gamma}
for every fixed face
f\in R
; and

•

\psi[\lambda]
conforms to
\Delta
.

Any
\psi
satisfying the first condition above will be called
R
-stable
.
The following statement summarizes the results of Schrijver that will be of use for us.

Theorem 6.6

(follows from
[
28
]
)
.

The
Homology Feasibility
problem can be solved in time
N^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, where
N
is the total length of the bit encoding of the input.

A few words of explanation are in place, as the work of Schrijver
[
28
]
does not contain a precise statement as above. The method of Schrijver relies on a reduction of the
Homology Feasibility
problem on a graph
G
to the
Cohomology Feasibility
problem on its dual graph
G^{\star}
. The planarity of input is only used in this reduction: Schrijver’s polynomial-time algorithm for
Cohomology Feasibility
works on general graphs. In Schrijver’s setting, a priori there is no domain mapping
\Delta
defined on vertices, but in the proof (see the proof of
[
28
, Proposition 5]
) he extends the dual graph to an “extended dual”, where he introduces additional arcs between any pair of faces incident to a common vertex
u
and puts non-trivial constraints on those arcs in order to emulate the vertex-disjointness constraints for the sought paths. Setting those constraints to
\Delta(u)
in our notation exactly corresponds to the restrictions expressed in our definition of
Homology Feasibility
. Similarly, in Schrijver’s setting, there is only one fixed face
r
that is required to have value
1_{\Gamma}
in the sought shift. However, we can again easily extend this to the possibility of requiring that every face from a given set
R
is mapped to
1_{\Gamma}
on the level of
Cohomology Feasibility
, by picking any
r\in R
, asking it to be mapped to
1_{\Gamma}
as described in the setting of Schrijver, and in the instance of
Cohomology Feasibility
adding arcs
(r,r^{\prime})
for all
r^{\prime}\in R\setminus\{r\}
with
\lambda(r,r^{\prime})=1_{\Gamma}
and
\Delta(r,r^{\prime})=\{1_{\Gamma}\}
. These additional arcs impose the condition that all faces of
R
are assigned the same value in the shift.

Clean vertices.

Before we continue, let us introduce an important invariant of labellings that is maintained under the application of shifts. Suppose
G
is a connected plane multigraph and
\lambda
is a
\Gamma
-labelling of
G
, where
\Gamma
is a group.

We call a vertex
u
of
G

clean
under
\lambda
if

\lambda(a_{1})\cdot\lambda(a_{2})\cdot\ldots\cdot\lambda(a_{p})=1_{\Gamma},

where
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{p})
is the sequence of all arcs with head
u
, ordered clockwise around
u
. Note that there are
p
such sequences depending on the choice of the arc to start the sequence, but the definition does not depend on this choice: the product above will be equal to
1_{\Gamma}
for one of them if and only if it is equal to
1_{\Gamma}
for all of them.

The observation is that the cleanliness of a vertex is preserved under applying a shift.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
\Gamma
be a group,
G
be a connected plane multigraph,
\lambda
be a
\Gamma
-labelling of
G
, and
\psi
be a
\Gamma
-shift of
G
. Suppose a vertex
u
is clean under
\lambda
. Then
u
is also clean under
\psi[\lambda]
.

Proof.

Let
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{p})
be the sequence of arcs with head
u
ordered clockwise around
u
, and let
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{p})
be the sequence of faces incident to
u
so that
f_{i}
lies between
a_{i}
and
a_{i+1}
in the sequence
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{p})
(indices behave cyclically). Then

\displaystyle\psi[\lambda](a_{1})\cdot\psi[\lambda](a_{2})\cdot\ldots\cdot\psi[\lambda](a_{p})

\displaystyle=\psi(f_{p})\lambda(a_{1})\psi(f_{1})^{-1}\cdot\psi(f_{1})\lambda(a_{2})\psi(f_{2})^{-1}\cdot\ldots\cdot\psi(f_{p-1})\lambda(a_{p})\psi(f_{p})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\psi(f_{p})\cdot\lambda(a_{1})\cdot\lambda(a_{2})\cdot\ldots\cdot\lambda(a_{p})\cdot\psi(f_{p})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\psi(f_{p})\cdot\psi(f_{p})^{-1}=1_{\Gamma},

hence
u
is clean under
\psi[\lambda]
.
∎

And we also note that in labellings originating from solutions to the
Planar Annotated Disjoint Paths
problem, all the non-terminal vertices are clean.

Lemma 6.8
.

Suppose
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
is a nice instance of
Planar Annnotated Disjoint Paths
and
\mathcal{P}
is a solution to
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
. Then every non-terminal vertex of
G
is clean with respect to
\lambda_{\mathcal{P}}
.

Proof.

Suppose
u
is a non-terminal vertex of
G
. Then under
\lambda_{\mathcal{P}}
, either all the arcs with head
u
are mapped to
1_{\Gamma}
, or all but two are mapped to
1_{\Gamma}
, and the remaining two are mapped to
i
and
i^{-1}
for some
i\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}
. In both cases, clearly
u
is clean.
∎

Finally, we observe the following statement about solutions to carefully prepared instances of
Homology Feasibility
implying the existence of solutions to
Planar Annotated Disjoint Paths
. We remark that the used trick to encode vertex-disjointness constraints is due to Schrijver, see
[
28
, Proof of Proposition 5]
.

Lemma 6.9
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
be a nice instance of
Planar Annotated Disjoint Paths
, where
|\mathcal{T}|=k
, and
(G,\lambda,\Delta,R)
be an instance of
Homology Feasibility
over the group
\Gamma=\Gamma_{\mathcal{T}}
, satisfying the following properties:

•

For every terminal pair
(s_{i},t_{i})\in\mathcal{T}
, we have
\lambda(a(s_{i}))=\lambda(a(t_{i}))=i
, where
a(s_{i})
is the unique arc with tail
s_{i}
and
a(t_{i})
is the unique arc with head
t_{i}
.

•

Every non-terminal vertex of
G
is clean with respect to
\lambda
.

•

\Delta(v)\subseteq\{1_{\Gamma_{\mathcal{T}}}\}\cup\{i,i^{-1}\colon i\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}\}
for all
v\in V(G)
.

•

\Delta(a)\subseteq\{1_{\Gamma_{\mathcal{T}}}\}\cup\{i\colon i\in\mathcal{D}(a)\}\cup\{i^{-1}\colon i\in\mathcal{D}(a^{-1})\}
for all
a\in\vec{E}(G)
.

•

F_{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq R
.

Then if
(G,\lambda,\Delta,R)
has a solution, then so does
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
.

Proof.

Let
\psi
be the
R
-stable
\Gamma
-shift that is a solution to
(G,\lambda,\Delta,R)
, and let
\lambda^{\prime}=\psi[\lambda]
. Next, for each
i\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}
, let
H_{i}
be the directed graph consisting of all arcs
a\in\vec{E}(G)
with
\lambda^{\prime}(a)=i
. Note that for every arc
a\in\vec{E}(G)
, the word
\lambda^{\prime}(a)
has length at most
1
by the assumption about the domain mapping
\Delta
. Therefore, for every edge
e
of
G
, either both orientations of
e
receive
1_{\Gamma}
under
\lambda^{\prime}
and none them belongs to any of the graphs
H_{i}
, or these orientations receive
i
and
i^{-1}
, in which case one of them belongs to
H_{i}
.

We first observe that by the assumed property of the domain mapping
\Delta
, the graphs
H_{i}
are pairwise vertex-disjoint. Indeed, suppose that a vertex
v
is incident to arcs belonging to at least two different graphs
H_{i}
. Then there is a sequence of arcs
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{p}
, all with head at
v
and consecutive in the cyclic order around
v
, such that for some
i\neq j
, we have
\lambda^{\prime}(a_{1})=i^{\pm 1}
,
\lambda^{\prime}(a_{p})=j^{\pm 1}
, and
\lambda^{\prime}(a_{2})=\lambda^{\prime}(a_{3})=\ldots=\lambda^{\prime}(a_{p-1})=1_{\Gamma}
. Then we have
\lambda^{\prime}(a_{1})\lambda^{\prime}(a_{2})\ldots\lambda^{\prime}(a_{p})=i^{\pm 1}j^{\pm 1}
, which in all four cases cannot belong to
\Delta(v)
, because by assumption, all words in
\Delta(v)
have length at most
1
. This is a contradiction with
\psi
being a solution.

Next, observe that since
\psi
is
R
-stable and
F_{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq R
, we have that
\lambda^{\prime}
and
\lambda
agree on arcs incident to the terminals, so
a(s_{i})
and
a(t_{i})
belong to
H_{i}
, for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Further, as every non-terminal vertex is clean with respect to
\lambda
, by
Lemma

6.7
the same can be also said about
\lambda^{\prime}
. From this it follows that every vertex of
H_{i}
except for
s_{i}
and
t_{i}
is
balanced
in
H_{i}
: it has the same number of outgoing and incoming arcs. Therefore, every (weakly) connected component of
H_{i}
has an Eulerian closed walk, except for one component that contains both
s_{i}
and
t_{i}
, which contains an Eulerian walk that starts at
s_{i}
and ends at
t_{i}
. By exhaustively shortcutting cycles on this walk, we obtain a path
P_{i}
leading from
s_{i}
to
t_{i}
that is entirely contained in
H_{i}
. As the graphs
H_{i}
are pairwise vertex-disjoint, the same also holds for the paths
P_{i}
. Finally, by the way we defined
\Delta
on the arcs of
\vec{E}(G)
, it follows that
i\in\mathcal{D}(a)
for each arc
a
traversed on
P_{i}
(oriented from
s_{i}
to
t_{i}
). We conclude that
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{i}\colon i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\}
is a solution to
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
.
∎

6.4.2
Reducing
PDSP
to
Homology Feasibility

We now proceed to the main technical ingredient of this section: we show how given an instance of
Planar Annotated Disjoint Paths
together with the skeleton
K
provided by
Proposition

6.4
and suitable characteristic words for every spinal path of
K
, the search for a solution yielding the prescribed characteristic words can be reduced to solving an instance of
Homology Feasibility
. To a large extent this boils down to lifting the arguments of Schrijver
[
28
, Section 3.3]
, but we need to be much more careful, because we need to bound the number of obtained homology types using the bound on the topological load of the skeleton
K
.

In the following, a mapping
w\colon\vec{{\cal Q}}(K)\to\Gamma
, where
\Gamma
is a group, shall be called
regular
if
w(\vec{Q})^{-1}=w(\vec{Q}^{-1})
for each
\vec{Q}\in\vec{{\cal Q}}(K)
.

Proposition 6.10
.

There exists an algorithm that, given a nice instance
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
of
Planar Annotated Disjoint Paths
, a skeleton
K
of
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
, and a regular mapping
w\colon\vec{{\cal Q}}(K)\to\Gamma_{\mathcal{T}}
, in polynomial time either concludes that there is no solution
\mathcal{P}
satisfying
\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Q})=w(\vec{Q})
for each
\vec{Q}\in\vec{{\cal Q}}(K)
, or constructs an instance
(G,\xi,\Delta,F_{\mathcal{T}})
of
Homology Feasibility
such that the following two implications hold:

•

If the instance
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
has a solution
\mathcal{P}
such that for every
\vec{Q}\in\vec{{\cal Q}}(K)
we have
\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Q})=w(\vec{Q})
, then the instance
(G,\xi,\Delta,F_{\mathcal{T}})
has a positive answer.

•

If the instance
(G,\xi,\Delta,F_{\mathcal{T}})
has a positive answer, then the instance
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
has a solution.

Proof.

Throughout the proof we denote
\Gamma=\Gamma_{\mathcal{T}}
and
\Sigma=\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}
for convenience.
Recall that
G^{\star}
is the dual of
G
and the mapping
e\mapsto e^{\star}
maps every edge of
G
to the corresponding (dual) edge of
G^{\star}
. We may also apply this mapping to the edges of
G^{\star}
by setting
(e^{\star})^{\star}=e
. For a set of edges
X
(of
G
or
G^{\star}
), we denote
X^{\star}=\{e^{\star}\colon e\in X\}
. This notation can be naturally extended also to arcs of
\vec{E}(G)
and
\vec{E}(G^{\star})
; note that then
(a^{\star})^{\star}=a^{-1}
.

Recall that
{\cal Q}(K)
is the set of (undirected) spinal paths of
K
and
\vec{{\cal Q}}(K)
consists of the orientations of the paths from
{\cal Q}(K)
, and we have
|{\cal Q}(K)|\leqslant 4k-3
. For each path
Q\in{\cal Q}(K)
, pick an arbitrary edge
e_{0}(Q)
of
Q
, and let
e(Q)=(e_{0}(Q))^{\star}
. (Note that
e_{0}(Q)
is an edge of
G^{\star}
, while
e(Q)
is an edge of
G
.) If
\vec{Q}
is any of the two orientations of
Q
, then we let
a(\vec{Q})
be the orientation of
e(Q)
so that
a(\vec{Q})^{\star}=a_{0}(\vec{Q})
, where
a_{0}(\vec{Q})
is the orientation of
e_{0}(Q)
that belongs to (matches the orientation of)
\vec{Q}
.

Next, let
W
be the set of vertices consisting of all the terminals of
V_{\mathcal{T}}
, and all the endpoints of the edges
e(Q)
for
Q\in{\cal Q}(K)
; thus
|W|\leqslant 9k-6
.
Let

H=G-\bigcup_{Q\in{\cal Q}(K)}E(Q)^{\star}.

Observe that since
K
is a tree in the dual graph
G^{\star}
,
H
stays connected. Therefore, we may pick
L
to be an inclusion-wise minimal connected subgraph of
H
that contains all the vertices of
W
. Clearly,
L
is a tree whose every leaf is a vertex of
W
.

Now, let
\widehat{L}
be the graph obtained from the tree
L
by adding all the edges
e(Q)
, for
Q\in{\cal Q}(K)
. Thus,
\widehat{L}
is a subgraph of
G
, hence every face of
\widehat{L}
is the union of a subset of faces of
G
. We now observe various properties of
\widehat{L}
in a series of claims.

For the first one, let
R
be the set of principal vertices of
K
, or equivalently, the faces of
G
that are the endpoints of the paths of
{\cal Q}(K)
. Recall that
F_{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq R
.

Claim 6.11
.

Every face of
\widehat{L}
contains at most one face belonging to
R
.

Proof.

Consider any distinct
f,f^{\prime}\in R
and let
Q
be any path of
{\cal Q}(K)
such that
Q
is contained in the unique
(f,f^{\prime})
-path in
K
. Observe that
e(Q)
together with the unique path in
L
connecting the endpoints of
e(Q)
forms a cycle in
\widehat{L}
that has the faces
f
and
f^{\prime}
on the opposite sides. Thus,
f
and
f^{\prime}
cannot be contained in the same face of
\widehat{L}
.
∎

The next claim is crucial: we show that every solution to the instance
(G,\mathcal{T})
can be shifted so that only the edges of
\widehat{L}
get non-trivial labels, and the orientations of the edge
e(Q)
get labels corresponding to the characteristic words of the orientations of
Q
, for every
Q\in{\cal Q}(K)
. We shall call a shift
\psi\colon F(G)\to\Gamma

R
-stable
if
\psi(f)=1_{\Gamma}
for all
f\in R
.

Claim 6.12
.

Suppose
\mathcal{P}
is a solution to the instance
(G,\mathcal{T})
. Then there exists an
R
-stable shift
\psi
satisfying the following conditions:

•

\psi[\lambda_{\mathcal{P}}](a)=1_{\Gamma}
whenever
a
is an orientation of an edge that does not belong to
\widehat{L}
; and

•

for every
\vec{Q}\in\vec{{\cal Q}}(K)
, we have
\psi[\lambda_{\mathcal{P}}](a(\vec{Q}))=\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Q})
.

Proof.

For every face
g
of
\widehat{L}
, let
G^{\star}_{g}
be the subgraph of
G^{\star}-E(\widehat{L})^{\star}
induced by all the faces of
G
contained in
g
. Clearly,
G^{\star}_{g}
is connected and, by
Claim

6.11
, at most one vertex of
G_{g}
belongs to
R
. Let
r_{g}
be this vertex in case it exists, and otherwise let
r_{g}
be any vertex of
G^{\star}_{g}
.

Further, since
K
is a tree that is a subgraph of
G^{\star}
,
G^{\star}_{g}\cap K
is a forest that is a subgraph of
G^{\star}_{g}
. Let then
S_{g}
be any spanning tree of
G^{\star}_{g}
that contains
G^{\star}_{g}\cap K
. We root
S_{g}
in
r_{g}
.

We now define the shift
\psi
as follows. Consider any face
f
of
G
. Let
g
be the face of
\widehat{L}
that contains
f
; equivalently,
f\in V(G^{\star}_{g})
. Let
\vec{Z}_{f}
be the (unique)
(r_{g},f)
-path contained in
S_{g}
, oriented from
r_{g}
to
f
. Finally, we set

\psi(f)=\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Z}_{f}).

It remains to show that the shift
\psi
defined in this manner satisfies all the requested properties.

First, observe that if
f\in R
, then
f=r_{g}
. Hence,
\vec{Z}_{f}
is a one-vertex path and
\psi(f)=\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Z}_{f})=1_{\Gamma}
, as required.

Second, consider any edge
e
of
G
that does not belong to
\widehat{L}
, and any orientation
a
of
e
. Then
a^{\star}=(f,f^{\prime})
, where
f,f^{\prime}
are two faces of
G
that belong to
G^{\star}_{g}
for some single face
g
of
\widehat{L}
. If
e^{\star}
belongs to
S_{g}
, then
\vec{Z}_{f^{\prime}}
is
\vec{Z}_{f}
with
(f,f^{\prime})
appended, or
\vec{Z}_{f}
is
\vec{Z}_{f^{\prime}}
with
(f^{\prime},f)
appended; by symmetry assume the former. Then
\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Z}_{f^{\prime}})=\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Z}_{f})\cdot\lambda(a)
, hence

\psi[\lambda_{\mathcal{P}}](a)=\psi(f)\cdot\lambda(a)\cdot\psi(f^{\prime})^{-1}=\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Z}_{f})\cdot\lambda(a)\cdot\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Z}_{f^{\prime}})^{-1}=1_{\Gamma}.

Next, assume that
e^{\star}
does not belong to
S_{g}
. Then letting
\vec{Z}
be the longest common prefix of
\vec{Z}_{f}
and
\vec{Z}_{f^{\prime}}
, we can decompose
\vec{Z}_{f}
as the concatenation of
\vec{Z}
and
\vec{Z}_{f}^{\prime}
, and
\vec{Z}_{f^{\prime}}
as the concatenation of
\vec{Z}
and
\vec{Z}_{f^{\prime}}^{\prime}
, so that the concatenation of
\vec{Z}^{\prime}_{f}
,
a
, and
(\vec{Z}^{\prime}_{f^{\prime}})^{-1}
is an oriented cycle, say
\vec{C}
. Since
\vec{C}
is entirely contained in

, it is a non-separating cycle, hence by

6.1
we conclude the following:

1_{\Gamma}=\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{C})=\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Z}^{\prime}_{f})\cdot\lambda(a)\cdot\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Z}^{\prime}_{f^{\prime}})^{-1}.

By left-multiplying both sides by
\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},Z)
and right-multiplying by
\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Z})^{-1}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle 1_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Z})\cdot\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Z}^{\prime}_{f})\cdot\lambda(a)\cdot\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Z}^{\prime}_{f^{\prime}})^{-1}\cdot\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Z})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Z}_{f})\cdot\lambda(a)\cdot\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Z}_{f^{\prime}})^{-1}=\psi(f)\cdot\lambda(a)\cdot\psi(f^{\prime})^{-1}=\psi[\lambda_{\mathcal{P}}](a),

as required.

Finally, consider any
\vec{Q}\in\vec{{\cal Q}}(K)
. Then the beginning and the end of
\vec{Q}
are two faces
r_{g},r_{g^{\prime}}\in R
, where
g,g^{\prime}
are distinct faces of
\widehat{L}
. Let

; then

and

. Observe that removing

from
\vec{Q}
breaks
\vec{Q}
into two subpaths
\vec{Q}^{\prime}
(with beginning
r_{g}
and end

) and
\vec{Q}^{\prime\prime}
(with beginning

and end

). By construction,
\vec{Q}^{\prime}
and
\vec{Q}^{\prime\prime}
are contained in trees

and

, respectively (after forgetting orientations), hence

\psi(f)=\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Q}^{\prime})\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\psi(f^{\prime})=\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Q}^{\prime\prime})^{-1}.

We conclude that

\displaystyle\psi[\lambda_{\mathcal{P}}](a(\vec{Q}))

\displaystyle=\psi(f)\cdot\lambda_{\mathcal{P}}(a(\vec{Q}))\cdot\psi^{-1}(f^{\prime})=\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Q}^{\prime})\cdot\lambda^{\star}_{\mathcal{P}}(a_{0}(\vec{Q}))\cdot\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Q}^{\prime})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Q}^{\prime})\cdot\lambda^{\star}_{\mathcal{P}}(a_{0}(\vec{Q}))\cdot\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},(\vec{Q}^{\prime})^{-1})=\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Q}),

as required.
∎

Now, call a
\Gamma
-labelling
\xi
of
G

compact
if the following conditions are satisfied:

•

\xi(a)=1_{\Gamma}
for every

that is an orientation of an edge not belonging to
\widehat{L}
;

•

every non-terminal vertex of
G
is clean with respect to
\xi
;

•

for every terminal pair
(s_{i},t_{i})\in\mathcal{T}
, we have
\xi(a_{s_{i}})=i^{-1}
and
\xi(a_{t_{i}})=i
, where
a_{s_{i}}
and
a_{t_{i}}
are the unique arcs with head

and
t_{i}
, respectively.

In terms of this definition,

6.12
implies that every labelling originating in a solution to
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
can be shifted to a compact labelling, as explained formally in the next claim.

Claim 6.13
.

Suppose
\mathcal{P}
is a solution to the instance
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
. Then the
R
-stable shift
\psi
provided by
Claim

6.12
for
\mathcal{P}
is compact.

Proof.

The first condition of compactness follows directly from
Claim

6.12
, the second condition follows from
Lemma

6.7
combined with
Lemma

, and the third condition is ensured by the

-stability of

.
∎

We next observe that the space of compact labellings is already very restricted: knowing the values on arcs

for
\vec{Q}\in\vec{\cal Q}(K)
— which are fixed by the given mapping

— is enough to reconstruct the whole compact labelling.

Claim 6.14
.

There exists at most one compact
\Gamma
-labelling
\xi
of
G
such that
\xi(a(\vec{Q}))=w(\vec{Q})
for all
\vec{Q}\in\vec{\cal Q}(K)
. Moreover, this compact labelling, or a conclusion about its non-existence, can be computed in polynomial time.

Proof.

Let
L^{\prime}
be
L
with all the terminals of
V(\mathcal{T})
removed; thus
L^{\prime}
is also a tree. Let
\xi
be a labelling as in the lemma statement.
Observe that the restrictions imposed on
\xi
fix the labels of all the arcs except for the orientations of the edges of
L^{\prime}
:

•

For every arc
a
that is an orientation of an edge outside of
\widehat{L}
, we have
\xi(a)=1_{\Gamma}
.

•

For every arc of the form
a(\vec{Q})
for some
\vec{Q}\in\vec{\cal Q}(K)
, we have
\xi(a(\vec{Q}))=w(\vec{Q})
.

•

For every arc incident to a terminal, say belonging to the

th pair of
\mathcal{T}
, its value under
\xi
is

or

, depending on the orientation and whether it is the source or the target terminal.

It remains to show that the values on the orientations of the edges of

can be also uniquely determined. Root

at any vertex

. We process the edges of

in a bottom-up manner, at each step determining the values of the orientations of an edge incident to a leaf, say

. Thus, at the moment of processing,

is incident to exactly one edge

whose orientations have values under

should be clean with respect to

(because the terminals of

are not in

), there is a unique way of fixing the values of

from

.

At the end, all the values of
\xi

, possibly except for the root

. Hence, we verify whether

is clean. If this is the case, we may output

∎

Call a solution
\mathcal{P}
to
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})

compatible
if
\mathsf{char}(\mathcal{P},\vec{Q})=w(\vec{Q})
for each
\vec{Q}\in\vec{{\cal Q}}(K)
.
We are ready to present the algorithm to produce the instance of

promised in the lemma statement:

•

Run the algorithm of
Claim

6.14
to compute a compact
\Gamma
-labelling
\xi
of
G
such that
\xi(a(\vec{Q}))=w(\vec{Q})
for all
\vec{Q}\in\vec{\cal Q}(K)
. If the algorithm concluded that there is no such labelling
\xi
, then terminate and return the conclusion that there is no compatible solution.

•

Output the instance
(G,\xi,\Delta,F_{\mathcal{T}})
, where
\Delta
is defined by setting
\Delta(a)=\{1_{\Gamma_{\mathcal{T}}}\}\cup\{i\colon i\in\mathcal{D}(a)\}\cup\{i^{-1}\colon i\in\mathcal{D}(a^{-1})\}

and
\Delta(v)=\{1_{\Gamma}\}\cup\{i,i^{-i}\colon i\in\Sigma\}
for each

.

We are left with proving that if the algorithm returns the conclusion about the non-existence of a compatible solution
\mathcal{P}
, then this conclusion is correct, and otherwise the computed instance
(G,\xi,\Delta,F_{\mathcal{T}})
has the required properties.

First, suppose that
\mathcal{P}
is a compatible solution to
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
. Let
\psi
be the
R
-stable shift provided by
Claim

6.12
for
\mathcal{P}
. Then denoting
\xi=\psi[\lambda_{\mathcal{P}}]
, we have that
\xi
satisfies
\xi(a(\vec{Q}))=w(\vec{Q})
for all
\vec{Q}\in\vec{\cal Q}(K)
, by the conditions asserted by
Claim

6.12
and the compatibility of
\mathcal{P}
. Further,
\xi
is compact by
Claim

6.13
. By
Claim

6.14
, we conclude that the algorithm of this claim actually succeeds in constructing
\xi
; this in particular shows that the conclusion about the non-existence of a compatible solution is always correct. Now as
\xi=\psi[\lambda_{\mathcal{P}}]
, we have
\lambda_{\mathcal{P}}=\psi^{-1}[\xi]
, where
\psi^{-1}
is defined by
\psi^{-1}(f)=\psi(f)^{-1}
for all

. Noting that
\psi^{-1}
is also
R
-stable, and recalling that
F_{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq R
, we see that
\psi^{-1}
witnesses that the instance
(G,\xi,\Delta,F_{\mathcal{T}})
of
Homology Feasibility
has a solution.

Second, suppose the instance
(G,\xi,\Delta,F_{\mathcal{T}})
of
Homology Feasibility
has a solution. By the compactness of
\xi
and the construction of
\Delta
, the instance
(G,\lambda,\Delta,F_{\mathcal{T}})
satisfies the prerequisites of

. Hence, from this lemma we infer that the instance
(G,\mathcal{T},\mathcal{D})
of
Planar Disjoint Annotated Paths
also has a solution.
∎

The next proposition pipelines
Proposition

6.4
with
Proposition

6.10
, giving a way to reduce solving an instance of
Planar Disjoint Shortest Paths
to solving a number of instance of
Homology Feasibility
.

Proposition 6.15
.

There exists an algorithm that, given a nice instance
(G,\mathcal{T})
of
Planar Disjoint Shortest Paths
with
|\mathcal{T}|=k
, in time
2^{\mathcal{O}(k\log k)}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}

computes a family
\Phi
of instance of
Homology Feasibility
with the following properties:

•

|\Phi|\leqslant 2^{\mathcal{O}(k\log k)}
.

•

If the instance
(G,\mathcal{T})
has a solution, then at least one of the instances
I\in\Phi
has a solution.

•

If any of the instances
I\in\Phi
has a solution, then so does
(G,\mathcal{T})
.

Let us note that
Proposition

6.15
is very easy if one is content with a worse bound of
2^{\mathcal{O}(k^{4}\log k)}
on the size of
\Phi
and the running time. Namely, do the following:

•

Apply the algorithm of
Proposition

6.4
, obtaining an instance
(G^{\prime},\mathcal{T}^{\prime},\mathcal{D})
of
Planar Annotated Disjoint Paths
with
|\mathcal{T}^{\prime}|=k
and its skeleton
K

•

For every regular mapping
w\colon\vec{{\cal Q}}\to\Gamma_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}
that has only words of length
\mathcal{O}(k^{3})
in its image, apply
Proposition

6.10
to
w
and include the obtained instance of
Homology Feasibility
in
\Phi
. As
|\vec{{\cal Q}}(K)|\leqslant\mathcal{O}(k)
and
|\overline{\Gamma}_{\mathcal{T}}|=2k
, there are only
2^{\mathcal{O}(k^{4}\log k)}
such mappings.

Therefore, the main matter in the proof of
Proposition

6.15
will be how to reduce the number of relevant mappings to
2^{\mathcal{O}(k\log k)}
by a more careful analysis. Importantly, we will use the fact that
Proposition

6.4
provides an upper bound on the topological load of the obtained skeleton, and not just on the algebraic load.

Proof of
Proposition

6.15
.

We first prepare some simple combinatorial tools.
For a positive integer
p
, we denote
[p]=\{1,\ldots,p\}
.
A
matching
in
[p]
is a set
M
consisting of pairwise disjoint subsets of
[p]
, each of size
2
. We say that
a,b\in[p]
are
matched
in
M
if
\{a,b\}\in M
. A matching
M
is
non-crossing
if there are no indices
a<b<c<d
such that
a
is matched with
c
and
b
is matched with
d
. Denoting
V(M)=\bigcup M
, we call
M

perfect
if
V(M)=[p]
.

An
block
in
[p]
is a contiguous subset of
[p]
, that is, a subset
B
such that for all
a\leqslant b\leqslant c
, that
a,c\in B
implies also
b\in B
. A
division
of
[p]
is a partition of
[p]
into blocks. For a division
{\mathcal{R}}
of
[p]
, a matching
M
in
[p]
is called
{\mathcal{R}}
-jumping
if for all
a,b
matched in
M
,
a
and
b
do not belong to the same block of
{\mathcal{R}}
.
We will use the following combinatorial bound.

Claim 6.16
.

Let
p
be a positive integer and let
{\mathcal{R}}
be a division of
[p]
with
|{\mathcal{R}}|=r
. Then the number of different perfect non-crossing
{\mathcal{R}}
-jumping matchings in
[p]
is bounded by
p^{\mathcal{O}(r)}
, and they can be enumerated in time
p^{\mathcal{O}(r)}
.

Proof.

Consider a perfect non-crossing
{\mathcal{R}}
-jumping matching
M
in
[p]
.
Call
a\in[p]

left
if
a
is matched in
M
with some
b>a
, and
right
otherwise. As
M
is
{\mathcal{R}}
-jumping, no pair of
M
is contained in a single block of
{\mathcal{R}}
. As
M
is additionally non-crossing, every block
B
of
{\mathcal{R}}
can be partitioned into a prefix
B^{\prime}
and a suffix
B^{\prime\prime}
, both possibly empty, so that elements of
B^{\prime}
are right and the elements of
B^{\prime\prime}
are left. Let
\ell(B)
be the length of
B^{\prime}
.

The observation now is that the mapping
\ell
allows to uniquely reconstruct the matching
M
. Indeed, construct a word
w
of length
p
over the alphabet
\{(,)\}
by taking
[p]
, replacing every left element by
(
and every right element by
)
, and writing the obtained symbols in the natural order. This can be done in a unique way knowing the mapping
\ell
, as this mapping entirely describes which elements of
[p]
are left and which are right. Then
w
must be a valid bracket expression, and
M
is given by pairs of matching brackets.

Since the number of blocks is
r
, the number of possible mappings
\ell
is bounded by
p^{\mathcal{O}(r)}
, which by the above observation translates to the same bound on the number of considered matchings. The enumeration statement follows by considering all possible mappings
\ell
and outputting all suitable matchings corresponding to them.
∎

With these preliminaries settled, let us proceed with the proof of the lemma. The first step is to apply

, and thus obtain an instance

of

with

. We denote
\overline{\Sigma}=\overline{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}

for brevity. Recall that by
{\cal Q}(K)

K
, and
\vec{\cal Q}(K)
are the orientations of paths from
{\cal Q}(K)
.

Let us consider any solution
\mathcal{P}
to
(G^{\prime},\mathcal{T}^{\prime},\mathcal{D})
such that the topological load of

with respect to
\mathcal{P}
is
\mathcal{O}(k^{3})
. Each path

is considered to be oriented from

to

. We consider the plane drawing consisting of the tree

and paths from
\mathcal{P}

: a closed curve

When

2|\mathcal{T}|=2k

, we have

.

For

, we analogously define the word

, subwords

for
\vec{Q}\in\vec{\cal Q}(K)
, length

, and division

. By the construction of

and

that assigns to each

Observe that the solution
\mathcal{P}
witnesses that the instance

obtained by applying

The observation now is that there are only

candidates for the word

. For this, consider construction of the following two matchings

and

•

Every crossing of a path

•

For every segment of

. Match those two positions in

Thus,

is a perfect matching,

matches all the positions except for those corresponding to the terminal symbols, and

consists of
k
disjoint paths. The

connects the positions of

corresponding to

and

(which hence contain symbols

and

.

and

The division

The labelling of

•

The matchings

and

-jumping, and such that

matches all positions except for those corresponding to the terminal symbols. By

•

Once

and

so that the endpoints of every path are always labelled with a pair of opposite elements (

Thus, we can enumerate

, which gives us also

Now, define

.

7
Conclusion

We have classified the
Planar Disjoint Shortest Paths
problem as fixed-parameter tractable by presenting a
2^{\mathcal{O}(k\log k)}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
-time algorithm.
An intriguing question is whether this problems is indeed easier than
Planar Disjoint Paths
, or can one improve the running time
2^{\mathcal{O}(k^{2})}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
for the latter.
Our algorithm involves a careful preparation of a Steiner tree spanning the terminals which facilitates enumerating the relevant homology classes of a solution.
Is it possible to build such a Steiner tree for
Planar Disjoint Paths
that would accommodate only
2^{o(k^{2})}
relevant homology classes?
