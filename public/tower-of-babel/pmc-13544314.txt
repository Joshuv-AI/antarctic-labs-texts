Gravidity-dependent protection against placental malaria is associated with enhanced natural killer cell-mediated antibody-dependent cellular activation: a nested cohort study
Authors: Savannah N. Lewis, Alea Delmastro, Mary Lopez-Perez, Sonia Presti, Adam S. Kirosingh, Felistas Nankya, Kenneth Musinguzi, Jimmy Kizza, Abel Kakuru, Michael Ofori, James Dressman, Sean C. Bendall, Grant Dorsey, Philip J. Rosenthal, Moses R. Kamya, Kattria van der Ploeg, Lars Hviid, Prasanna Jagannathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundPlacental malaria disproportionately affects primigravid women and is a leading cause of morbidity in endemic settings. IgG specific to VAR2CSA, a placenta-binding Plasmodium falciparum protein, accumulates with successive pregnancies, but protective mechanisms remain incompletely understood. We investigated whether natural killer antibody-dependent cellular activation (NK-ADCA) was acquired in a gravidity-dependent manner and associated with improved birth outcomes.MethodsWe characterised second trimester NK-ADCA and VAR2CSA-specific antibody features among pregnant women in Eastern Uganda (NCT04336189). NK-ADCA (n = 53) against VAR2CSA-expressing P. falciparum-infected erythrocytes was assessed using primary NK cells with autologous or pooled donor plasma with characterised IgG Fc-fucosylation profiles. VAR2CSA-specific IgG abundance and Fc-fucosylation (n = 71) were quantified by ELISA and mass spectrometry. Responses were compared across gravidity and associations with placental malaria and birth weight determined.FindingsNK-ADCA increased significantly with gravidity (median 31.0% in multigravida [n = 20] vs. 11.7 in primigravid [n = 18], p = 0.0014) and correlated with reduced placental malaria severity (rs = −0.28, p = 0.043) and higher birth weight (rs = 0.52, p = 1 × 10−4). NK cell phenotypes did not differ across gravidities; instead, gravidity-dependent NK-ADCA was associated with VAR2CSA-specific IgG abundance (rs = 0.50, p = 0.00014) and reduced Fc-fucosylation (rs = −0.69, p = 1.2 × 10−8). Depletion of VAR2CSA-specific antibodies reduced NK-ADCA in multigravid but not primigravid plasma. A CD56neg adaptive-like NK subset (CD85j+FcεRIγ−NKG2C−) mediated most NK-ADCA and was preferentially activated by Fc-afucosylated antibodies.InterpretationGravidity-dependent protection against placental malaria was associated with enhanced NK-ADCA driven by accumulation of Fc-afucosylated VAR2CSA-specific antibodies, identifying NK-ADCA as a potentially important component of protective immunity to placental malaria.FundingThis work was supported by the National Institutes of Health, the Blavatnik Family Foundation, the Stanford University Office of the Vice Provost for Graduate Education, the Stanford Maternal and Child Health Research Institute, and the Danish Research Council for Development Research.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed from database inception to September 30th, 2025, combining terms for placental malaria (“placental malaria”, “malaria in pregnancy”, “pregnancy-associated malaria”, “VAR2CSA”), gravidity and parity (“gravidity”, “parity”, “primigravid”, “multigravid”), antibody Fc features (“IgG”, “Fc”, “afucosylation”, “fucosylation”, “glycosylation”), and natural killer cell effector function (“natural killer cell”, “NK cell”, “antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity”, “ADCC”, “ADCA”, “FcγRIIIa", “CD16″); reference lists of relevant articles were also hand-searched for additional sources. We restricted inclusion to English-language publications. No formal quality assessment tool was applied; the overall body of evidence was of moderate quality, comprising predominantly observational cohort studies and mechanistic studies using NK cell line-based assays, with limited data from primary NK cells, small sample sizes across most studies, heterogeneous outcome definitions, and restricted geographic representation. Protection from malaria-related adverse birth outcomes increases with successive pregnancies in malaria-endemic settings. Prior studies have established that antibodies to VAR2CSA, the Plasmodium falciparum placenta-binding protein, accumulate with increasing gravidity. However, systematic reviews have found inconsistent associations between VAR2CSA-specific antibody levels and pregnancy outcomes, suggesting antibody quantity alone does not predict protection. Recent work demonstrated that VAR2CSA-specific IgG becomes increasingly Fc-afucosylated with gravidity, and that this modification correlates with activation of NK cell lines and protection against low birth weight. NK cell-mediated antibody-dependent cellular activation (NK-ADCA) has also emerged as a correlate of protection against symptomatic malaria in children and adults in endemic settings, with evidence suggesting that adaptive-like NK cell subpopulations exhibit enhanced NK-ADCA following malaria exposure. Therefore, we investigated whether gravidity-dependent protection from placental malaria is associated with VAR2CSA-specific IgG Fc-afucosylation, ADCA mediated by adaptive-like NK cells, or their combined activity in malaria-exposed pregnant women.

Added value of this study

This study provides direct evidence that, in malaria-exposed pregnant women, primary NK cell-mediated ADCA increases with gravidity and is associated with improved pregnancy outcomes. Using samples from a randomised clinical trial in Eastern Uganda, we demonstrated that gravidity-dependent increases in NK-ADCA were driven by accumulation of Fc-afucosylated VAR2CSA-specific antibodies rather than by changes in NK cell phenotypes or function. Depletion of VAR2CSA-specific antibodies from multigravid donor plasma reduced NK-ADCA activity, confirming the antigenic specificity of this response. Importantly, we identify CD56neg NK cells with adaptive-like phenotypes as the primary mediators of ADCA in malaria-exposed pregnant women and demonstrate that these cells are preferentially activated by Fc-afucosylated antibodies. The magnitude of NK-ADCA measured during the second trimester was associated with reduced placental malaria severity and higher birth weight.

Implications of all the available evidence

These findings suggest that NK cell-mediated ADCA against VAR2CSA-expressing parasites is a key mechanism underlying gravidity-dependent protection against placental malaria. VAR2CSA-specific antibodies likely confer protection through dual mechanisms: Fab-mediated inhibition of placental sequestration and Fc-mediated enhancement of cellular activation and cytotoxicity. The importance of Fc-afucosylation of IgG for ADCA highlights this post-translational modification as a potential target for future therapeutic investigation, and suggests that vaccine strategies for placental malaria may benefit from optimising not only the quantity and epitope targeting of VAR2CSA-specific IgG but also its Fc-fucosylation profile. The enrichment of adaptive-like CD56neg NK cells in the ADCA response provides additional evidence for malaria-specific NK cell adaptation and suggests that these cells may represent important effectors against P. falciparum-infected erythrocytes. These results support the potential development of therapeutics and vaccines targeting antibody-dependent cellular mechanisms to protect vulnerable populations in malaria-endemic regions.

Introduction

Malaria is a persistent global health threat despite decades of control efforts.1 During pregnancy, malaria is a major cause of morbidity and mortality, with an estimated one out of three pregnant women in sub-Saharan Africa infected with P. falciparum in 2023.1 Although repeated infections in early life result in substantial clinical immunity in endemic settings,2 pregnant women have a higher risk of malaria-related morbidities compared to non-pregnant women.3 During pregnancy, P. falciparum-infected erythrocytes (IEs) express VAR2CSA, a member of the P. falciparum erythrocyte membrane protein 1 (PfEMP1) family, which binds chondroitin sulphate A (CSA) expressed on placental tissue.4,5 This promotes IE sequestration in the placenta,6 resulting in a number of adverse and potentially life-threatening outcomes for both the mother and child.3,7,8

In endemic settings, women experiencing their first pregnancy are at an especially high risk of malaria-related adverse birth outcomes, but this risk decreases with successive pregnancies.3,7,9 VAR2CSA-specific antibodies are acquired in a gravidity-dependent manner and have been associated with improved mother and newborn outcomes in some, but not all studies.10, 11, 12, 13 It has long been thought that acquired protection against placental malaria is mediated by neutralising VAR2CSA-specific IgG that prevents IE sequestration in the placenta.4,14, 15, 16 However, a systematic review found largely inconsistent associations between levels of VAR2CSA-specific antibodies and pregnancy or birth outcomes.17 Recent work suggests that VAR2CSA-specific antibody Fc-domain modifications contribute to gravidity-dependent protection from placental malaria among women living in endemic settings.18,19 Increasing plasma donor gravidity was correlated with loss of VAR2CSA-specific IgG Fc-fucosylation,18,19 and this Fc-afucosylation was associated with protection against low birth weight in Ghanian women.18 Fc-afucosylation greatly increases IgG affinity for Fcγ receptor III (FcγRIII, CD16) and consequently enhances Fc-dependent effector functions, such as antibody-dependent cellular activation (ADCA)/cytotoxicity (ADCC), and/or phagocytosis (ADCP).20, 21, 22 Indeed, only Fc-afucosylated VAR2CSA-specific IgG from malaria-exposed Ghanian women induced a robust ADCA response in NK92 cells.18,19 However, direct evidence regarding the impact of Fc-afucosylation of VAR2CSA-specific IgG on ADCA mediated by primary NK cells is lacking,23 as well as the degree to which NK cell subpopulations and ADCA activity recently implicated in protection from malaria in other contexts are relevant to protection against placental malaria.24, 25, 26, 27

In the present study, we leveraged data, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), and plasma from pregnant women enrolled in a clinical trial of intermittent preventative treatment of malaria in pregnancy (IPTp) regimens. We performed in vitro functional assays, multiparameter flow cytometry, and antibody profiling to determine whether ADCA mediated by NK cells (NK-ADCA) is acquired in a gravidity-dependent manner. We further sought to determine whether specific immunologic features of either NK cells or antibodies correlated with improved outcomes of malaria in pregnancy.

Methods

Ethics statement

Samples and data were obtained from HIV-uninfected pregnant women 16 years of age or older enrolled in the “Optimal Chemopreventive Regimens to Prevent Malaria and Improve Birth Outcomes” Clinical Trial (“DPSP” Clinical Trial, NCT04336189).28 The study protocol, including sample collection and analysis, was approved by the Makerere University School of Biomedical Sciences Research Ethics Committee (SBS 714), the Uganda National Council for Science and Technology (HS 2746), the Uganda National Drug Authority (CTC 0135/2020), and the University of California San Francisco Human Research Protection Program (19-29105). The University of California San Francisco Committee on Human Research acted as a single IRB for Stanford University for these protocols. Sample collection of Ghanaian plasma samples from non-pregnant malaria-exposed women was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Noguchi Memorial Institute for Medical Research, University of Ghana (study 038/10–11) and by the Regional Research Ethics Committees, Capital Region of Denmark (protocol H-4-2013-083).29 All participants provided written informed consent before enrolment.

IRB approval (IRB 41197) was obtained for the analysis of deidentified patient samples for all studies at Stanford University. The study adhered to the ethical principles of the 2024 Declaration of Helsinki.

Sex and gender considerations

All study participants were pregnant women. Biological sex (female) was an explicit inclusion criterion for enrolment in the DPSP clinical trial and was ascertained from medical records and clinical assessment at enrolment. Gender was not separately collected as a variable in this study. Because all participants share the same biological sex, sex-stratified analyses were not performed and sex was not included as a covariate. The study population reflects a context where pregnancy-associated malaria disproportionately affects women of reproductive age, and all findings are therefore specific to this population.

Study design, participants, sub-cohort selection, and outcomes measured

Samples from pregnant women for this study were obtained from women enrolled in two cohorts. The first cohort, the DPSP clinical trial, was a double-blinded, randomised, phase III trial that tested the hypothesis that IPTp with the combination of sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) plus dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine (DP) would significantly reduce the risk of adverse birth outcomes compared to IPTp with SP or DP alone.28 Enrolment for the study began in December 2020 and ended in December 2023, and the study was conducted from December 2020 to July 2024 in Busia District, Eastern Uganda, where malaria transmission is perennial and intense.28,30 Pregnant women were eligible for this clinical trial if they were within 12–20 weeks of gestation with viable singleton pregnancies, at least 16 years old, HIV uninfected, lived in Busia District, willing to deliver at the study hospital, and provided written informed consent. Study drugs were administered every four weeks after enrolment starting at 16 weeks of gestation. Eligible participants who were enrolled after 16 weeks of gestation were started on a study drug at their enrolment visit. Participants were followed through 28 days postpartum. Delivery and birth outcome data from the DPSP clinical trial are shown in Fig. 1.

To generate NK-ADCA, serology, and immunophenotyping data, a study sub-cohort (N = 75, n primigravid = 30, n secundigravid = 15, n multigravid = 30) was randomly selected from DPSP participants who completed the trial, had live births, and had PBMCs and plasma collected at enrolment available for use. Data from four donors were subsequently removed due to poor sample quality, resulting in a final cohort of 71 donors (primigravid = 27, secundigravid = 15, multigravid = 29). Clinical data for the sub-cohort is available in both Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. S1. All 71 donors were included in serological assays. NK-ADCA assay data was available for 53 donors for autologous and pooled FucHigh/FucLow opsonizing plasma conditions, and 54 donors for pooled primigravid/multigravid opsonizing plasma, following exclusions based on assay-level quality control criteria. Per-assay denominators and exclusions are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2. Samples were randomised across assay plates to minimise batch effects.

To investigate the impact of VAR2CSA-specific IgG Fc-fucosylation on NK-ADCA, we leveraged an additional set of plasma samples from a separate cohort of malaria-exposed women with known Fc-fucosylation levels described in a previous study.19 These samples were obtained from a cross-sectional study of non-pregnant adult Ghanaian women in Assin Foso, Ghana, a rainforest area described as having intense perennial malaria transmission, during the 2011 July through August rainy season.29 Individuals with similar total levels of VAR2CSA-specific IgG were used to create pools of plasma with relatively high (average 78% Fc-fucosylation across N = 9 donors [n nulligravid = 2, n primigravid = 2, n secundigravid = 3, n multigravid = 2]) and low (average 27% Fc-fucosylation across N = 11 donors [n nulligravid = 1, n primigravid = 2, n multigravid = 8]) Fc-fucosylation of VAR2CSA-specific IgG.19

Parasite culture and isolation for functional assays

The P. falciparum clones 3D7 (catalogue no. MRA-102, contributed by Daniel J. Carucci) and CS2 (catalogue no. MRA-96, contributed by Stephen J. Rogerson) were obtained through BEI Resources, NAID, NIH. 3D7 parasites were cultured as previously described by our group.25,31 CS2 parasites were cultured in pH 6.75 RPMI 1640 medium containing 2 mM glutamine, 25 mM HEPES, 25 mM sodium bicarbonate, 1% gentamicin, 250 μM hypoxanthine, 0.5% Albumax, and 5% human AB serum as previously described.32 Both cultures were maintained at 2% haematocrit, with CS2 cultures specifically using blood from an anonymous adult O− CMV− donor provided by the Stanford Blood Center. Both cultures were treated weekly with 5% D-sorbitol to maintain synchronicity. Gelatin flotation (0.7% gelatin in pH 6.75 RPMI 1640 medium containing 25 mM HEPES, 25 mM sodium bicarbonate, 1% gentamycin, and 250 μM hypoxanthine) was performed twice weekly to enrich for knob-expressing parasites in CS2 cultures.33

Both cultures were certified to be free of mycoplasma contamination using the MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza, catalogue no. LT07-318) prior to purification. Erythrocytes infected with trophozoite and schizont-stage parasites were purified from cultures with 10% or above parasitaemia using MACS cell separation LD columns (Miltenyi Biotec, catalogue no. 130-042-901) and were cryopreserved in glycerolyte until further use.

Quantification of VAR2CSA expression in infected erythrocytes

Cryopreserved CS2 and 3D7 P. falciparum schizonts enriched for knob expression in vitro were thawed, washed, and resuspended in incomplete RPMI medium. Thawed cells were aliquoted into 96-well plates (200,000 cells per well) and pelleted. VAR2CSA-specific human monoclonal antibodies (PAM1.4 and PAM2.8, described and validated in previous work)34 diluted to 10 μg/mL were added to plated cells and incubated 30 min at 4 °C. After washing three times with 1% BSA in 1X PBS, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated rabbit anti-human IgG Fcγ diluted in SYBR Green (1:10,000 dilution, Life Technologies, catalogue no. S7563) for 30 min at 4 °C. After washing three times with 1% BSA in 1X PBS, cells were resuspended in 1% BSA in 1X PBS before acquiring on an Attune NXT flow cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Representative plots of PAM1.4 and PAM2.8 labelling of CS2 and 3D7 infected erythrocytes (IEs) are provided in Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. S3A, respectively. All fluorophore-conjugated antibodies were validated by the manufacturer for use in flow cytometry on human peripheral blood mononuclear cells; clone, catalogue number, RRID, manufacturer, and working dilution for each antibody are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Titration and specificity testing were performed in our laboratory prior to experimental use.

NK cell antibody-dependent cellular activation assay

After thawing, PBMCs were plated at 400,000 cells per well of a U-bottom 96-well plate and primed overnight in 200 IU/mL of IL-2 (IS premium grade, Miltenyi Biotec catalogue no. 130-097-745) in complete RPMI-1640 medium (supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum [FBS], 100 IU penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, 1 mM HEPES, and 2 mM l-glutamine) in an incubator set to 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

The following morning, cryopreserved knob-enriched CS2-IEs were thawed, counted, and opsonized in plasma diluted to 10% in serum-free RPMI medium for 1 h at 37 °C (5% CO2). Sources of plasma included: autologous plasma (same PBMC donor); pooled plasma from primigravid and multigravid participants across DPSP (n = 20 donors per pool), and pools of plasma from Ghanian donors with similar levels of VAR2CSA-specific IgG, but with either relatively high (average 78% Fc-fucosylation across N = 9 donors [n nulligravid = 2, n primigravid = 2, n secundigravid = 3, n multigravid = 2]) or low (average 27% Fc-fucosylation across N = 11 donors [n nulligravid = 1, n primigravid = 2, n multigravid = 8]) Fc-fucosylation of VAR2CSA-specific IgG.19 After washing, opsonized CS2-IEs were added to primed PBMCs at a 1:2 effector to target ratio (800,000 CS2s per well) in RPMI medium containing 10% FBS. After adding APC anti-CD107a (which measures NK cell degranulation and serves as a proxy for cytotoxic activity35), GolgiStop (0.6 μL per mL, containing monensin, BD Biosciences, catalogue no. 554724), and GolgiPlug (0.6 μL per mL, containing brefeldin A, BD Biosciences, catalogue no. 555029), PBMCs and opsonized CS2-IEs co-incubated for 5 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

After stimulation, cells were washed and stained with the following surface antibodies or dyes in 1X PBS for 30 min at room temperature: eFluor 450 anti-CD85j, Brilliant Violet 510 anti-CD14, Brilliant Violet 510 anti-CD19, LIVE/DEAD Fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain (1:200 dilution, Thermo Fisher catalogue no. L34957), VioBright V600 anti-NKG2A, Brilliant Violet 605 anti-CD16, Brilliant Violet 711 anti-CD56, Brilliant Violet 785 anti-CD57, PE anti-NKG2C, AlexaFluor 700 anti-CD7, and APC-H7 anti-CD3. FIX & PERM Medium A (Thermo Fisher Scientific catalogue no. GAS001S100) was then added to the surface antibody cocktail and incubated for an additional 10 min at room temperature. After washing in FACS Buffer (1X PBS containing 0.5% bovine serum albumin-BSA- and 0.5 mM EDTA), cells were stained for 20 min in the dark with FITC anti-FcεRγ subunit and PerCP Cy5.5 anti-IFN gamma diluted in FIX & PERM Medium B (Thermo Fisher Scientific catalogue no. GAS002S100). Cells were then washed and resuspended in FACS Buffer. Samples were acquired on an Attune NXT flow cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and analysed using FlowJo v10.10.0 Software (Becton Dickinson & Company). Clone, catalogue number, RRID, manufacturer, and working dilution for each antibody are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

We analysed CD107a+ cells to characterise the NK cell subpopulations and associated cellular phenotypes degranulating during ADCA. We further stratified analyses of CD107a+ cells by CD16 expression, since NK cells activated through CD16 lose surface expression of this receptor.36 Data were analysed using RStudio (R version 4.5.0, RStudio version 2025.05.1+513 [Posit Software]), and SPICE (version 6.1 [NIAID]).37

NK cell immunophenotyping

NK cell immunophenotyping was performed on negative control conditions (uninfected erythrocytes) from the NK cell antibody-dependent cellular activation assay. Raw data (.fcs) files of live CD3− CD14− CD19− CD7+ single cells (gating strategy shown in Supplementary Fig. S2B) were exported out of FlowJo v10.10.0 Software (Becton Dickinson & Company) for analysis. CD16, CD56, CD57, CD85j, NKG2A, NKG2C, FcεRIγ NK cell surface and intracellular markers from the ADCA panel described in NK cell antibody-dependent cellular activation assay were included in clustering analyses. To reduce oversampling bias in higher cell count samples, downsampling was performed using a function that randomly selects events within a user-defined threshold. For the present analysis, that threshold was set to 1500 events. Samples with low cell counts (<50% of downsampling cutoff) were excluded from analysis. Preprocessing, k-means clustering, pseudobulk multi-dimensional scaling, differential abundance testing, dimensionality reduction, and data visualisations were produced using the R package CATALYST.38 The phenotypic marker expression signatures and relative abundance of each identified cluster was compared against the raw data to ensure biological accuracy, and overgeneralised clusters were further sub-clustered if deemed necessary.

Cytokine stimulation assay

After thawing, PBMC samples were plated and primed overnight as described in NK cell antibody-dependent cellular activation assay. The following day, a cocktail containing IL-12 (2.5 ng/mL, R&D Systems, catalogue no. 219-IL-005), IL-15 (10 ng/mL, PeproTech, catalogue no, 200-15-10UG), and IL-18 (0.25 μg/mL, R&D Systems, catalogue no. 9124-IL-010) in complete RPMI-1640 medium was added to the cells. Cells were stimulated for 18 h at 37 °C. GolgiPlug (0.6 μL per mL, containing brefeldin A, BD Biosciences, catalogue no. 555029) was added to the cells after 1 h of stimulation.

After stimulation, cells were washed and stained with the following surface antibodies or dyes in 1X PBS for 30 min at room temperature: Brilliant Violet 510 anti-CD14, Brilliant Violet 510 anti-CD19, LIVE/DEAD Fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain (1:200 dilution, Thermo Fisher catalogue no. L34957), Brilliant Violet 605 anti-CD16, Brilliant Violet 711 anti-CD56, AlexaFluor 700 anti-CD7, APC-H7 anti-CD3. FIX & PERM Medium A (Thermo Fisher Scientific catalogue no. GAS001S100) was then added to the surface antibody cocktail and incubated for an additional 10 min at room temperature.

After washing in FACS Buffer (1X PBS containing 0.5% bovine serum albumin-BSA- and 0.5 mM EDTA), cells were stained with Brilliant Violet 421 anti-TNFα and Brilliant Violet 785 anti-IFNγ for 20 min in the dark diluted in FIX & PERM Medium B (Thermo Fisher Scientific catalogue no. GAS002S100). Cells were then washed and resuspended in FACS Buffer before acquisition on an Attune NXT flow cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Data were analysed using FlowJo v10.10.0 Software (BD Life Sciences). More information about the fluorophore-conjugated antibodies used for flow cytometry experiments can be found in Supplementary Table S1.

Antigen-specific antibody assays

Plasma was assayed for malaria-specific IgG using a fucose-sensitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for antigen-specific IgG (FEASI) as originally described for SARS-CoV-2 spike-specific IgG39 and validated by us for VAR2CSA-specific IgG.18,40 This assay involves two ELISAs, one to quantify specific IgG (IgG ELISA) and a second to test its affinity for FcγRIIIa (FcγRIIIa-IgG ELISA). Antigens used in these assays were full-length VA2CSA ectodomain (IT4VAR04) produced in baculovirus-transfected insect cells41 and recombinant 3D7-MSP2 purified from Escherichia coli.42 For both assays, Nunc MaxiSorp 96-flat-bottom plates (BioLegend catalogue no. 423501) were coated overnight with VAR2CSA (75 ng per well) or MSP2 (25 ng per well) in 1X PBS at 4 °C. The following day, plates were washed three times with PBS-T (0.05% Tween-20 in 1X PBS) before blocking (1% casein in 1X PBS) for 2 h at 37 °C. Plates were then washed three times with PBS-T before adding DPSP donor plasma diluted in Antibody Buffer (0.1% casein in 1X PBS). Plasma incubated for 1 h at room temperature while shaking, then wells were washed five times with PBS-T.

For IgG ELISA, anti-human IgG Fc-HRP (SouthernBiotech catalogue no. 2048, RRID:AB_2795688; validated by the manufacturer for ELISA applications and confirmed to produce a dose-dependent signal with serially diluted calibrator plasma in our laboratory) diluted 1:50,000 in Antibody Buffer was incubated for 1 h at room temperature while shaking. For FcγRIIIa-IgG ELISA, biotinylated FcγRIIIa (Sino Biological catalogue no. 10389-H27H1-B-50) diluted to 1 μg/mL in Antibody Buffer was added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature while shaking. After washing plates five times in PBS-T, Pierce streptavidin poly-HRP (Thermo Scientific catalogue no. 21140) diluted to 0.1 μg/mL in Antibody Buffer was added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature while shaking.

Both plates were washed five times with PBS-T before developing. Plates for both assays were developed with Pierce 1-Step Ultra TMB substrate (Thermo Scientific catalogue no. 34029) diluted 50% with deionised water in the dark. MSP2 and VAR2CSA assays developed for 10 and 15 min, respectively. After adding 1 M sulphuric acid solution, absorbance was read at 450 nm an 540 nm using a SpectraMax iD3 multi-mode microplate reader. The representative optical density (OD) of each well was taken as the difference between 450 nm and 540 nm readings.

Pooled plasma from multigravid Ugandan women was serially diluted and used as a calibrator from which a standard curve and arbitrary unit (a.u.) values were calculated. This pooled sample was assigned a value of 100 a.u. and was used at the same dilution range in all assays for comparison. An additional set of plasma samples (n = 1 malaria-naive, n = 4 DPSP donors) and a blank were included on each plate to monitor batch effects between independent experiments. The standard curve and a.u. calculations were performed using a logistic-log function software platform developed by Ed Nieuwenhuys (publicly available at https://github.com/ednieuw/ELISA-logit-regression).

For all assays, each DPSP donor plasma sample was probed with three serial dilutions in duplicate. The geometric mean of all six a.u. values was taken as the representative sample a.u. for samples whose OD within range of the blank and the calibrator sample. Samples were assayed again with adjusted dilutions if the OD was out of range, and a sample was probed again at the same dilutions if the coefficient of variation between replicates exceeded 25%. The ratio of IgG- and FcγRIIIa-IgG ELISA a.u., referenced in the text as the FEASI ratio or FcγRIIIa -IgG/IgG, represents the fraction of Fc-afucosylated antigen-specific IgG from the total antigen-specific IgG pool for each sample. This fraction was then multiplied by 100. Calculations of 0 or that exceeded 100 were assigned values of 1 and 99, respectively.

For VAR2CSA DBL1x-2x ELISAs, Nunc MaxiSorp 96-flat-bottom plates (BioLegend catalogue no. 423501) were coated overnight with DBL1x-2x VAR2CSA protein (2 μg/mL) in 1X PBS at 4 °C. The following day, plates were washed with PBS-T three times before blocking in Blocking Buffer for 2 h at 37 °C. DPSP donor plasma diluted 1:100 in Antibody Buffer was added and left to incubate for 1 h at room temperature while shaking. Plates were then washed three times with PBS-T. Anti-human IgG Fc-HRP (SouthernBiotech catalogue no. 2048, RRID:AB_2795688) diluted 1:50,000 in Antibody Buffer was incubated for 1 h at room temperature while shaking. After plates were washed three times with PBS-T, TMB substrate was added to incubate for 15 min in the dark. The reaction was stopped after 15 min with 1 M sulphuric acid, and plates were read at 450 nm with an Absorbance 96 plate reader (Byonoy). Samples were analysed in duplicate; the average absorbance was calculated and multiplied by the dilution factor of 100. Average absorbance values below 0 were assigned a value of 0.

Depletion of VAR2CSA-specific antibody concentration in donor plasma

To reduce the relative concentration of VAR2CSA-specific antibodies from donor plasma, Nunc MaxiSorp 96-flat-bottom plates (BioLegend catalogue no. 423501) were coated overnight with full-length VAR2CSA (75 ng per well) in 1X PBS. The following day, plates were washed three times with PBS-T (0.05% Tween 20 in 1X PBS) before blocking (1% casein in 1X PBS) for 2 h at 37 °C.

Plates were washed three times with PBS-T, then 100 μL of donor plasma diluted to 10% in incomplete RPMI-1640 medium (same medium used to dilute plasma and opsonize IEs for NK-ADCA assays) was added to the first well and incubated for 1 h at room temperature while shaking. The remaining VAR2CSA-coated wells without plasma were kept hydrated in 100 μL PBS-T during this incubation. After 1 h, the 10% plasma was transferred to the next well after the PBS-T used to keep the well hydrated was aspirated off. Wells that previously contained 10% plasma were kept hydrated in 100 μL PBS-T throughout the remaining incubations. This process was repeated for a total of seven incubations. After the seventh incubation, 10% donor plasma was transferred from the last VAR2CSA-coated well to a cryotube and was stored at −80 °C for future use in NK-ADCA assays. Individual plasma samples were run in duplicate to ensure sufficient opsonizing volume remained for NK-ADCA. PBMCs from two Ugandan adults who voluntarily donated blood to Tororo District General Hospital were used for these experiments, which were performed as described in NK cell antibody-dependent cellular activation assay.

After the 10% plasma was removed from the final well, plates were washed five times with PBS-T. An ELISA was subsequently performed as described in Antigen-specific antibody assays to quantify the anti-VAR2CSA IgG captured from donor plasma by this approach.

MALDI-MSI analysis of N-linked glycans on antigen-specific antibodies

Matrix assisted laser desorption ionisation mass spectrometry imaging (MALDI-MSI) of antigen-specific antibody N-glycosylation was conducted using a modified affinity array-based approach, as previously described.43,44

Nexterion Slide H 3-D slides (SCHOTT NORTH AMERICA INC) were allowed to equilibrate at room temperature prior to staining. VAR2CSA protein (both IT4 full-length ectodomain41 and 3D7 DBL1x-2x45) was diluted using 1X PATH buffer (Grace Bio-Laboratories) prepared with high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)-grade water to desired concentration (133.33 μg/mL for IT4 full-length ecdomatin and 762.56 μg/mL for 3D7 DBL1x-2x). Using a 24 well module for spot guidance with the functionalized side of the slide up, 0.75 μL of antigen was spotted in duplicate in each well area. After spotting, the slide incubated for 1 h at room temperature in a pre-equilibrated humidity chamber. Post-incubation, the slide was left to dry for 30 min. The 24 well module was then attached to the slide, and the wells were washed twice with 200 μL of 1X PBS. Each well was blocked with 100 μL of 3% BSA in 1X PBS for 1 h at room temperature in a humidity chamber. The slides were washed twice with 200uL of 1X PBS. Then, 100 μL of each plasma sample diluted to 5% in 1X PBS was added to each well, and incubated for 2 h in the humidity chamber at room temperature on an orbital shaker set to 100 revolutions per minute. Wells were washed three times with 200 μL of 1X PBS before dipping in HPLC-grade water for 30 s. The slides were dried and stored in a desiccator prior to spraying.

To release N-glycans from captured antibodies, PNGase F enzyme (PNGase F PRIME-LY, N-Zyme Scientifics, distributed by Bulldog Bio) diluted to 0.1μg/μLin HPLC-grade water was sprayed onto the slide using an HTX M3+ Sprayer (HTX Technologies). Spraying parameters consisted of 10 passes at 25 μL/min, 1200 mm/min, 45 °C, 3 mm spacing between passes with 10 psi nitrogen gas. Once spraying was complete, slides were incubated in a preheated humidity chamber for 2 h at 37 °C. Next, α-cyano-4-hydroxycinamic acid (CHCA, 7 mg/mL in 50% acetonitrile/0.1% trifluoracetic acid) was sprayed using the same HTX M3+ sprayer with the following parameters: 10 passes at 70uL/minute, 1300 mm/min, 79 °C, 2.5 mm spacing between passes with 10 psi nitrogen gas.

N-glycan imaging was conducted using a timsTOF-flex MALDI-QTOF mass spectrometer (Bruker) operated in positive ion mode at a m/z range of 800–4000. Images were collected using a SmartBeam 3D laser operated at 10,000 Hz at a 50 × 50 μm laser spot size. Arrays where imaged at a raster of 150 μm with 500 laser shots per pixel.

Data were imported into SCiLS Lab 2025b (Bruker) and normalised to total ion count. Each spot was assigned to its own individual region by manually circling the corresponding spot in SCiLS software. Initial N-glycan peak extraction was conducted using theoretical mass values based on known hexose compositions. Peak values were adjusted to match measured peaks. Subsequently, maximum peak values were extracted and used for N-glycan absolute intensities for each region. Each slide was spotted with one well of duplicate antigen-only spots in 1X PBS. For each slide, the average signal from antigen-only spots was used to subtract the background signal from all sample spots. After antigen subtraction, if the peak maximum of an individual N-glycan was less than 1 arbitrary unit (au), the value was converted to 1 au to avoid 0 or negative values for statistical analysis. If a N-glycan was less than 2 au for more than 20% of samples, it was removed from further analysis. Relative intensity values were calculated by dividing the intensity values of each individual N-glycan by the summed intensities of all N-glycans included per spot. Averaged relative intensity values were calculated per sample. N-glycans included in the analysis are shown in Supplementary Table S4 (VAR2CSA-specific antibodies) and 5 (VAR2CSA DBL1x-2x-specific antibodies), and N-glycoforms were built with GlycoWorkBench.

Outcomes

Our primary analyses explored associations between NK-ADCA immunological data and gravidity (categorical and continuous). Secondary analyses explored associations between NK-ADCA data and 1) Placental malaria, defined either as (A) histopathological evidence of placental malaria, or (B) percentage of high-power frames with parasite pigment deposition in placental fibrin46; 2) Infant birth weight, either as a (A) continuous measure in grams, z-scored, or (B) categorically by gestational age (small for gestational age [SGA, below 10th percentile] or not). Other malaria-related adverse birth outcomes, including preterm birth, low birth weight, and neonatal mortality, were not analysed because they were too infrequent in the sub-cohort to permit meaningful statistical comparisons. Analyses of NK cell phenotypic subsets, including subset-level evaluation of NK-ADCA, were exploratory.

Statistics and visualisations

Statistical analyses for the present study were performed using STATA (version 19 [StataCorp LLC]), SPICE (version 6.1 [NIAID]),37 and/or RStudio (R version 4.5.0, RStudio version 2025.05.1+513 [Posit Software]).

The primary comparison of interest was NK-ADCA between primigravid and multigravida women. Based on our own data,24 we estimated that the mean frequency of CD107a+ NK cells in primigravid women would be 25% (standard deviation of 13%). Studying 30 women per group, with 80% power and alpha of 0.05, the minimum detectable difference in the percentage of CD107a+ NK cells would be approximately 10%. A smaller subset of secundigravid women were included to evaluate whether there were graded differences across gravidity.

Percentages of CD107a+ NK cells are reported as percentage of the parent NK cell population and were not background subtracted due to relatively low background in experimental conditions (refer to Fig. 2C). Box plots depict the data in quartiles, with the box representing the interquartile range, middle line reflecting the median of the data, and the lower and upper tails depicting quartile 1 and 4, respectively. Scatter plots depict individual data points, with the dark grey line showing the data fit to a linear model and the shaded light grey area around it representing the 95% confidence interval. These figures were generated using the R packages ggpubr (version 0.6.0) and cowplot (version 1.1.3).

The heat map, dot plot, and pie chart figures describing NK-ADCA-activated (CD107a+ CD16+/−) NK cells in Figs. 2 and 5 were generated in SPICE.37 The pseudobulk-level multi-dimensional scaling (pbMDS), differential abundance testing, heatmaps, and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projections (UMAPs) from unsupervised analysis of NK cell phenotypes in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S4 were generated using the R package CATALYST38 (refer to NK cell immunophenotyping for more detailed information).

Comparisons of cellular phenotypes, relative antibody levels, and percentages of CD107a+ cells between groups were performed using Mann–Whitney U tests. Comparisons between NK-ADCA opsonizing conditions where the same donors were represented in both were made using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Relationships between different parameters from the same donor were determined using Spearman's correlation. Linear and logistic regression models used to determine the relationship between immunologic data measured at enrolment and clinical data (placental malaria and birth weight). Both univariable and multivariable models were utilised as above, with non-normally distributed immune percentages log transformed resulting in approximately normal variable distributions. Multivariable linear and logistic regression models were adjusted for potential confounders, including IPTp arm, parasitaemia status at the time of sampling, maternal age, and gravidity (modelled as a dichotomous variable, primigravid vs. non-primigravid) (Supplemental Fig. S5). Two multivariable models were assessed: one accounting for IPTp and parasitaemia at the time of sampling, the second additionally adding maternal age and gravidity. Linear models were evaluated for adherence to standard assumptions, including linearity, normality of residuals, and constant variance. Given the relatedness of many immunologic measurements and the hypothesis-driven nature of these analyses, p-values were not globally adjusted for multiple comparisons; results were interpreted based on the consistency, magnitude, and plausibility of effects across complementary assays. Only the comparison of NK-ADCA across gravidity and its pre-specified secondary associations with placental malaria and birth weight were planned a priori; all other analyses were exploratory and should be considered hypothesis-generating. Two-sided p-values were calculated for all test statistics and p < 0.05 was considered significant. Analyses of association with gravidity and clinical outcomes were conducted only after all laboratory data had been fully processed and quality-controlled, reducing the potential for operator bias during data acquisition and gating. Missing data arose exclusively from sample quality failures and assay-level quality control; no imputation was performed, and each assay was analysed using all donors with evaluable data for that assay, with per-assay denominators reported throughout.

Results

NK cell-mediated ADCA increased with donor gravidity and was correlated with higher birth weight

We used data and samples from a clinical trial cohort of pregnant Ugandan women28 to study the role of NK cells in gravidity-dependent immunity to placental malaria. In the trial, the overall prevalence (Fig. 1A) and severity (Fig. 1B)46 of placental P. falciparum infection decreased significantly with increasing gravidity independent of IPTp treatment arm. Expressed as percentages, 79.2% of placentas from primigravid women had any histopathological evidence of placental malaria, with an average of 11.6% of high-power fields with parasite pigment deposition in placental fibrin (Fig. 1A and B). In secundigravid and multigravid women, 57.7% and 34.0% of placentas, respectively, had histopathological evidence of placental malaria, with 5.56% and 2.27% of high-power fields with pigment deposition in placental fibrin (Fig. 1A and B). Furthermore, the proportion of infants born small for gestational age declined significantly with increasing gravidity (Fig. 1C), whereas offspring birth weights increased significantly (Fig. 1D). Precisely, 32.9% of live infants born to primigravid mothers were small for gestational age, compared to 23.4% and 16.8% for secundigravid and multigravid mothers, respectively (Fig. 1C). Mean birth weight increased from 2913 g in primigravid women to 3053 and 3190 g in secundigravid and multigravid women (z-scored data visualised in Fig. 1D). Previous work has shown that Fc-afucosylation of PfEMP1-specific IgG is associated with enhanced NK-ADCA responses by NK92 cells against IgG-opsonized IEs and with clinical protection from P. falciparum malaria during pregnancy.18,19 To study whether NK-ADCA varied across gravidity and was associated with protection from placental malaria, we selected a random sub-cohort of participants based on gravidity from the parent study (N = 71, Table 1, Supplementary Fig. S1 and S2) to test these observations with primary NK cells and matched (autologous) plasma. In this sub-cohort, the prevalence and severity of placental malaria decreased with increasing gravidity similar to the parent cohort, although the prevalence of SGA was highest among secundigravid women, likely in part due to the smaller sample size (Table 1, Supplementary Fig. S1). We performed in vitro NK-ADCA assays by opsonizing CS2-IEs in 10% donor plasma before incubating with autologous (same-donor) PBMCs. Next, we investigated whether levels of NK-ADCA in second trimester enrolment samples were correlated with outcomes at delivery. Women with histopathological evidence of placental malaria at delivery had lower NK CD107a expression at enrolment compared to those without placental malaria (Supplementary Table S2). Higher NK CD107a expression was also associated with lower density placental malaria infection (Rs = −0.28, p = 0.043 [Spearman's correlation], Fig. 2E). Associations with NK-ADCA and placental malaria were similar after adjustment for IPTp arm parasitaemia at the time of enrolment, but not after adjustment for gravidity and maternal age (Supplementary Table S2).

NK cell CD107a expression was positively and significantly associated with birth weight of their offspring (Rs = 0.52, p = 1 × 10−4 [Spearman's correlation], Fig. 2F, Supplementary Fig. S3D). Consistent with these data, mothers who delivered small for gestational age infants had lower NK cell CD107a expression than those who delivered infants who were of average size for gestational age (p = 0.018 [Mann–Whitney U test], Fig. 2G). Associations with NK-ADCA and birth weight were similar after adjustment for IPTp arm, parasitaemia at the time of enrolment, gravidity, and maternal age, and were clinically meaningful: each 1 standard deviation increase in NK-ADCA (13.8 percentage points) was associated with a 0.55 standard deviation higher birth weight Z-score (Supplementary Table S3).

Together, these data show that NK-ADCA against VAR2CSA-expressing IEs was higher in multigravid than primigravid women, and higher NK-ADCA was associated with improved pregnancy outcomes.

NK cell cellular phenotypes and functional capacity were similar across gravidity

Although NK-ADCA against VAR2CSA-expressing IEs increased with gravidity, it was unclear whether NK cells, plasma, or a combination of both mediated this effect. We first assessed whether there were differences in NK cell phenotypes between primigravid, secundigravid, and multigravid women by performing unsupervised clustering analysis on gated NK cells (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. S4A). This clustering identified CD56bri-like and CD56dim NK cell subpopulations, as well as an expanded CD56neg population previously reported in malaria-exposed PBMC donors (Fig. 3A).25,31 CD56dim NK cells of any phenotype composed approximately 60% of the NK cell compartment, followed by CD56neg at 15.8% overall (Fig. 3A). We next evaluated whether NK cell function differed by gravidity. When we compared ADCA in response to CS2-IEs opsonized in pooled primigravid or multigravid donor plasma, NK cell CD107a expression was similar across PBMC donor gravidity within the same opsonizing condition (Fig. 3E). As an additional assessment of NK cell effector function, we stimulated PBMCs from primigravid and multigravid donors with a pro-inflammatory cytokine cocktail (IL-12, IL-15, and IL-18) and quantified the resulting NK cell cytokine response. This approach induced a robust cellular response (Supplementary Fig. S4D), but again, the percentages of IFNγ+ and TNFα+ NK cells were similar between primigravid and multigravid donors (Fig. 3F). Thus, increasing donor gravidity did not appear to impact NK cell phenotypes or function.

To determine whether VAR2CSA antigen–antibody interactions were responsible for gravidity-dependent increases in NK-ADCA, we next screened DPSP donor plasma for IgG specific to the full-length VAR2CSA ectodomain using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Relative amounts of VAR2CSA-specific IgG, quantified in arbitrary units (a.u.), increased with increasing gravidity (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Fig. S6A). Furthermore, anti-VAR2CSA IgG levels significantly correlated with levels of autologous NK-ADCA (Rs = 0.5, p = 0.00014 [Spearman's correlation], Fig. 4C). However, unlike NK-ADCA, VAR2CSA IgG levels were not significantly correlated with placental malaria or birth weight (Supplementary Fig. S6B–6E, Supplementary Table S2, Supplementary Table S3). This supports the notion that the mere presence of VAR2CSA-specific IgG is not protective against placental malaria, and that clinical protection involves induction of NK-ADCA to improve birth outcomes.17,18 IgG levels to MSP2, an immunogenic merozoite-stage P. falciparum antigen, were not positively correlated with gravidity or with NK-ADCA (Supplementary Fig. S7A and B).

To confirm that VAR2CSA-specific IgG was important for NK-ADCA of VAR2CSA-positive IEs, we depleted VAR2CSA-specific antibodies from plasma by serially incubating plasma in plates coated with VAR2CSA protein (Fig. 4D). Anti-VAR2CSA IgG levels decreased across cumulative incubations (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, removing VAR2CSA-specific antibodies greatly reduced the magnitude of NK-ADCA in response to multigravid, but not primigravid, opsonizing plasma (Fig. 4F). This may suggest that NK-ADCA induced by primigravid plasma is due to the presence of other antibodies with specificity to non-VAR2CSA P. falciparum antigens. Last, we performed ADCA assays with IEs from 3D7 P. falciparum, which unlike CS2 P. falciparum, was not selected for VAR2CSA expression during in vitro culture (Supplementary Fig. S3A). In contrast to data with CS2-IEs, there was no significant difference in NK cell degranulation when 3D7-IEs were opsonized in pooled primigravid and multigravid plasma (Supplementary Fig. S8). Thus, VAR2CSA-specific antibodies in multigravid plasma influence the magnitude of NK-ADCA against CS2-IEs.

Fc-afucosylation of VAR2CSA-specific IgG increases with gravidity and enhances NK-ADCA

NK-ADCA is fundamentally regulated by the ability of the NK cell Fc receptor, CD16a (FcγRIIIa), to bind the antibody Fc domain. Fc receptor binding is influenced by antibody glycosylation,20 and it has been established that antibody Fc-afucosylation has a dramatic enhancing effect on NK-ADCA.21,22 Fc-afucosylation of VAR2CSA-specific IgG potently induced ADCA in the NK92 cell line,18,19 but this has not been documented with primary NK cells from malaria-exposed pregnant women. We therefore opsonized CS2-IEs with two pools of plasma containing similar total VAR2CSA-specific IgG levels, but with or without marked Fc-fucosylation of VAR2CSA-specific IgG,19 and tested their ability to induce ADCA using DPSP donor NK cells. We quantified Fc-fucosylation of VAR2CSA-specific antibodies in DPSP donor plasma by fucose-sensitive ELISA for antigen-specific IgG (FEASI).39 FEASI substitutes the anti-human IgG secondary antibody used in ELISA with recombinant CD16a (FcγRIIIa), which binds IgG in a Fc-fucosylation-dependent manner.39 The ratio of the FcγRIIIa to IgG ELISA (FEASI ratio: FcγR-IgG/IgG) therefore reflects the fraction of total VAR2CSA-specific IgG that is Fc-afucosylated. Consistent with previous reports,18,19,40 we found that Fc-afucosylation of anti-VAR2CSA IgG increased significantly with plasma donor gravidity (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Fig. S9B). To investigate whether antibodies specific to different binding domains of VAR2CSA exhibit variable fucosylation trends, and what impact they may have on NK-ADCA, we employed matrix assisted laser desorption ionisation mass spectrometry imaging (MALDI-MSI) to measure fucosylation of antibodies specific to the full-length VAR2CSA ectodomain or the DBL1x-2x domain through an affinity array-based approach.43,44 41 unique glycans were identified for full-length and 66 for DBL1x-2x-specific VAR2CSA-specific antibodies across donors (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5). Consistent with FEASI data, the percentage of fucosylated antibodies specific to the full-length VAR2CSA ectodomain decreased with gravidity (Fig. 5C). Measurements of VAR2CSA-specific antibody fucosylation were significantly correlated between MALDI-MSI and FEASI (Fig. 5D). Both FEASI (Fig. 5E) and MALDI-MSI (Fig. 5F) data were associated with autologous NK-ADCA, indicating that VAR2CSA-specific IgG Fc-afucosylation likely influences NK-ADCA. Consistent with NK-ADCA data, we also observed a significant association between increasing VAR2CSA-specific IgG Fc-afucosylation and lower percentages of placental high-power fields with malaria pigment in fibrin (Fig. 5G and H, Supplementary Table S2). However, we did not find Fc-afucosylation to be significantly associated with infant size in this cohort (Supplementary Fig. 9C–E, Supplementary Table S3). Intriguingly, both levels and fucosylation of antibodies specific to the DBL1x-2x domain of VAR2CSA were not consistently associated with either gravidity or NK-ADCA: antibody levels significantly correlated with NK-ADCA but not gravidity, whereas fucosylation significantly correlated with gravidity but not NK-ADCA (Supplementary Fig. S10). Fc-afucosylation of antibodies against the non-pregnancy associated P. falciparum MSP2 antigen was not associated with donor gravidity or NK-ADCA (Supplementary Fig. S7C and D).

Lastly, we analysed whether opsonizing conditions with differential anti-VAR2CSA IgG Fc-fucosylation activated distinct NK cell populations. This analysis also further stratified CD107a+ cells by CD16 expression to determine how antibody Fc domain binding affinity impacts CD16 downregulation observed during NK-ADCA.36 CD56neg NK cells were the primary responding population in both plasma pools (Fig. 5I). However, CD16+ cells composed the greater fraction of responding cells in the Highfuc condition, whereas CD16− cells composed the greater fraction of responding cells in the Lowfuc condition (Fig. 5I). This is consistent with increased CD16 engagement under higher affinity Fc-FcγRIIIa binding conditions. Consistent with earlier data in autologous plasma (Fig. 2D), the most abundant responding subpopulation across both CD16 populations was the CD56neg CD85j+ FcεRIγ− NKG2C− subset (Fig. 5J). This combination of phenotypic markers has been previously described in adaptive-like NK cells from malaria-exposed adults and children.24,25,31,47 Interestingly, Highfuc plasma was associated with greater degranulation across all CD16+ cell subsets, including the CD85j+ FcεRIγ− NKG2C− subset, whereas Lowfuc plasma was associated with greater degranulation across all CD16− cell subsets (Fig. 5J).

Altogether, our findings corroborate and extend existing data indicating that VAR2CSA-specific IgG becomes increasingly Fc-afucosylated with gravidity in malaria-exposed pregnant women. We also observed that Fc-afucosylated anti-VAR2CSA IgG enhanced NK-ADCA and preferentially activated CD56neg NK cell subpopulations with adaptive-like phenotypes.

Discussion

In this investigation leveraging samples from a clinical study of pregnant women in Uganda, we found that NK-ADCA against VAR2CSA-expressing P. falciparum-infected erythrocytes mediated by NK cells increased with gravidity. Gravidity was not associated with differences in NK cell phenotypes or effector function, leading us to investigate the ability of donor plasma to regulate NK-ADCA. In donor plasma, levels and the degree of Fc-afucosylation of VAR2CSA-specific IgG were positively associated with both gravidity and NK-ADCA. CD56neg NK cells comprised the majority of the ADCA-mediating NK cell population in malaria-exposed pregnant women, and plasma containing Fc-afucosylated anti-VAR2CSA IgG elicited more activation from this subpopulation specifically. Higher NK-ADCA from samples collected during the second trimester was associated with reduced severity of placental malaria and significantly higher birth weight, suggesting that Fc-dependent effector mechanisms associated with Fc-afucosylated VAR2CSA-specific IgG may contribute to protection from placental malaria.

Multifunctionality of antibodies specific to Plasmodium antigens is an increasingly recognised component of protection against malaria. Specifically, the ability of Plasmodium-specific antibody Fc domains to engage immune cell Fc receptors has been repeatedly associated with a reduced risk of disease in natural infection and human challenge studies.18,24, 25, 26, 27,48,49 The glycosylation status of Plasmodium-specific antibodies is highly relevant in this context, as different antibody glycoforms have unique structural properties that determine and regulate their ability to engage different Fc receptors.20 In IgG antibodies, the Fc fucose glycan determines the strength of CD16a (FcγRIIIa) binding, which consequently impacts ADCA outcomes: the presence of fucose destabilises a critical carbohydrate–carbohydrate interaction involved in high-affinity binding, and the loss of this fucose dramatically enhances ADCA.21,22 Although Plasmodium-specific antibody glycosylation has not been widely studied, emerging evidence suggests that it is impacted by both the type of antigen and duration of malaria exposure. A recent report found that PfEMP1-specific IgG becomes increasingly Fc-afucosylated with increasing age and that this is associated with reduced risk of anaemia in a cohort of Ghanaian children.48 In pregnancy, Fc-afucosylation of VAR2CSA-specific IgG increased with gravidity and was important for Fc-dependent effector functions, specifically NK-ADCA.18,19 Consistent with these studies, we found that Fc-afucosylation of VAR2CSA-specific IgG increased with gravidity in malaria-exposed Ugandan women, whether measured by FEASI or MALDI-MSI. We further show that Fc-fucosylation was associated with protection against placental malaria and regulated the magnitude of primary NK cell ADCA using pools of plasma from Ghanaian women with similar levels of VAR2CSA-specific IgG.18,19

By performing these assays with primary NK cells, we could observe that the Fc-afucosylated IgG pool elicited more activation from CD56neg NK cells than of any other NK cell subpopulation. Although less abundant than CD56dim NK cells, our data using DPSP and Ghanaian plasma showed that the same CD56neg phenotypic subset, with a CD85j+ FcεRIγ− NKG2C− phenotype, comprised the majority of the ADCA response. We and several other groups have reported that ADCA mediated by NK cells with adaptive-like phenotypes are positively associated with both malaria exposure and clinical immunity.24,25,31,47 Concerning CD56neg NK cells specifically, a previous study of NK cells from malaria-exposed Ugandan children by our group demonstrated that this subpopulation has an enhanced ability to mediate ADCA against IEs compared to conventional CD56dim cells, and also found that CD56neg ADCA activity positively correlates with protection from symptomatic malaria.24 The most abundant ADCA-mediating CD56neg populations were NKG2C−, which further distinguishes them from the characteristic NKG2C+ adaptive-like NK cells described extensively in the context of human cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection.50, 51, 52, 53 We suspect that CMV seropositivity is relatively high in our study population,54 and this is reflected by the presence of some NKG2C+ subsets (NKG2A− NKG2C+ CD57+ FcεRIγ− CD56dim and NKG2A+ NKG2C+ CD57− CD56bri-like).50, 51, 52, 53,55 Together, these data offer more evidence supporting the existence of functionally and phenotypically distinct malaria-adapted NK cells.56,57 Our findings also suggest that enhanced NK-ADCA observed in malaria-endemic settings may reflect the convergence of two immune features associated with malaria: CD56neg adaptive-like NK cell subpopulations with intrinsically higher ADCA capacity and Plasmodium-specific antibodies that promote high-affinity Fc-FcγRIIIa interactions.

In our study sub-cohort, elevated autologous NK-ADCA from second trimester samples was associated with reduced severity of placental malaria and higher infant birth weight. Although NK-ADCA was regulated by levels of VAR2CSA-specific IgG and its Fc-fucosylation status, only the magnitude of NK-ADCA remained significantly associated with improved infant weight after adjusting for treatment. This indicates that acquisition of VAR2CSA-specific antibodies alone is not sufficient to garner protection against the adverse outcomes of placental malaria, but that their ability to induce Fc-dependent effector functions such as ADCA is an important component of protection.17,18 FEASI and MALDI-MSI data that used full-length VAR2CSA and the CSA-binding fragment offer a more nuanced perspective: NK-ADCA was associated with polyclonal Fc-afucosylated full-length VAR2CSA-specific antibodies, but not with afucosylated antibodies specific to VAR2CSA DBL1x-2x. Antibody responses to this specific domain have neutralising potential and therefore can prevent sequestration of IEs to the syncytiotrophoblast.58 Perhaps VAR2CSA-specific antibodies protect against placental malaria through distinct mechanisms influenced by their domain specificity, ability to induce Fc-dependent effector functions, or both.59,60 This highlights the need for future VAR2CSA epitope mapping studies to identify important antibody epitopes for both neutralising and non-neutralising activity.

We identified one study of NK cell cytotoxicity in malaria-exposed pregnant Gabonese women that also documented a gravidity-associated increase in NK-ADCA against IEs, but the authors only studied CD56+ cells, as was conventional at the time, and correlated IE lysis with plasma cortisol and prolactin levels, not VAR2CSA-specific antibodies.61 Existing evidence concerning the ability of peripheral blood NK cells to mediate ADCA at the maternal–foetal interface during placental malaria is limited and indirect. Placental malaria histology studies have had conflicting results regarding the presence of NK cells in the inflamed intervillous space,62,63 but studies of postpartum malaria-exposed Kenyan women showed that CD56+ CD3− NK cells were enriched in placental blood relative to peripheral blood.64,65 IFNγ produced by NK cells in placental blood was linked to reduced placental malaria in one cohort.65 Neither CD16 staining nor functional assays were performed with placental blood NK cells in either study, which leaves their identity as resident or circulating cells, along with their potential to mediate ADCA, unknown. Regardless of the extent of NK cell activity at the maternal–foetal interface, NK cells can clear opsonized IEs circulating in peripheral blood and possibly the spleen, and they likely also access the maternal blood syncytiotrophoblast interface where VAR2CSA-expressing IEs accumulate during placental malaria.3 Furthermore, FcR-expressing monocytes and neutrophils occupy the maternal–foetal interface during infection, and despite their associations with placental malaria pathology,3,7,61,66, 67, 68 they have also been theorised to contribute to protective immunity due to their abilities to mediate ADCA/ADCC, and/or ADCP.60,62,69 Fc-afucosylated opsonizing antibodies would also enhance the Fc-dependent effector functions of these cell types.20,59,70

There were limitations to this study. Although we validated this NK cell gating strategy with CITE-seq data in previous work,25 it still may permit a degree of contamination by other cells due to flow cytometry panel size constraints. Furthermore, the precise ontology of CD56neg NK cells remains unresolved, although we and others have shown they align closely to conventional peripheral blood NK cells and have relatively few similarities with innate lymphoid cells (ILCs).25,57 NK-ADCA dynamics in peripheral blood may differ from those at the maternal–foetal interface, and future studies should evaluate placental blood NK cells. DPSP study samples were selected at random based on gravidity when IPTp assignments were blinded, and although they were subsequently shown to be relatively balanced across treatment arms, malaria-related adverse birth outcomes were impacted by treatment.28,71 However, associations were similar after multivariable adjustment for IPTp arm and parasitaemia at enrolment (Supplementary Table S2, Supplementary Table S3). Though associations between immune measures and placental malaria lost significance after adjustment for maternal age and gravidity, this attenuation may reflect over-adjustment bias rather than true confounding, as immune measures likely lie on the causal pathway through which gravidity confers protection against placental malaria rather than operating as an independent confounder (Supplementary Fig. S5). Previous work from our group showed that elevated levels of IL-10+ IFNγ+ malaria-specific Type 1 regulatory T cells in primigravid donors were associated with both parasitaemia and placental malaria, indicating that other non-NK lymphocytes also influence risk of adverse pregnancy and birth outcomes in malaria-exposed pregnant women.72 Concurrent SARS-CoV-2 infection may have contributed to adverse birth outcomes, specifically birth weight and size for gestational age, given the timing of the parent clinical trial in relation to the COVID-19 pandemic.73 The relatively small study sub-cohort limited our ability to investigate relationships between NK-ADCA or serological data with less common malaria-associated adverse birth outcomes, including preterm birth and low birth weight. It is also possible that the size of the cohort obscured significant findings: we feel this is particularly evident in the insignificant association we report here between anti-VAR2CSA IgG Fc-afucosylation by FEASI and birth weight (Supplementary Fig. 9C and D), as a recent study in malaria-exposed pregnant Beninese women found these parameters to be significantly associated.18 Finally, our mechanistic and immunophenotyping analyses were exploratory and warrant confirmation in independent cohorts.

Placental malaria remains a serious threat to global progress against malaria, and immunological features of naturally acquired protection can inform the design of medical interventions. The present analysis identified an association between gravidity-related protection from placental malaria and NK cell-mediated ADCA. In the context of placental malaria, VAR2CSA-specific antibodies may act through both neutralising and Fc-dependent effector mechanisms, though causal inference requires further experimental validation. These insights can inform the development of therapeutics and vaccines for malaria-exposed pregnant women to help control this devastating disease.

Data sharing statement

Deidentified participant data and experimental data generated in this study—including flow cytometric quantification of NK cell marker expression, NK cell antibody-dependent cellular activation (NK-ADCA), cytokine stimulation assay results, ELISA and FEASI optical density and arbitrary unit values, and MALDI-MSI N-glycan intensities—along with a data dictionary, will be made available to qualified researchers upon reasonable request following publication. Requests will be reviewed on a case-by-case basis and require submission of a study protocol to the corresponding author (PJ; prasj@stanford.edu), along with execution of a data access agreement. Data access may be subject to restrictions to protect participant confidentiality and to comply with the terms of institutional review board approvals governing the DPSP clinical trial. Data will not contain direct identifiers. Raw flow cytometry files (.fcs) are available upon request and subject to the same access process described above.

Code sharing statement

Upon reasonable request, analytic code used for statistical analyses and visualisations (R and Stata scripts) will be made available. Contact the corresponding author (PJ) for more information.
