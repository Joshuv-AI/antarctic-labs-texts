Association between systolic blood pressure trajectories and successful aging in middle-aged and elderly Chinese individuals: a longitudinal study
Authors: Yijing Xin, Xifeng Qian, Yanmin Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to prospectively assess the relationship between systolic blood pressure (SBP) trajectories and successful aging in middle-aged and elderly Chinese individuals.MethodsA total of 1,960 participants from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) were included. SBP measurements from 2011, 2013, and 2015 were used to identify trajectory patterns via a latent category trajectory model (LCTM). Successful aging was assessed in 2020 and defined as the absence of major chronic diseases, preserved physical function, high cognitive performance, favorable mental health, and active social engagement. Logistic regression was applied to evaluate associations between SBP trajectories and successful aging.ResultsThree SBP trajectories were identified: stable low, stable high, and unstable high. Over the 9-year follow-up, 89 participants (4.5%) met the criteria for successful aging. Compared with the stable low group, adjusted odds ratios (95% CI) for successful aging were 0.490 (0.293–0.817) for the stable high group and 0.099 (0.014–0.727) for the unstable high group. Stratified and sensitivity analyses supported these findings.ConclusionsBoth stable high and unstable high SBP trajectories were significantly associated with reduced likelihood of successful aging. These findings highlight the importance of longitudinal SBP monitoring in promoting healthy aging in midlife and beyond.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25709-2.

Introduction

The global population is aging rapidly, with the proportion of individuals aged 60 and above projected to reach 2.1 billion (22%) by 2050[1]. Against this backdrop, the United Nations declared 2021–2030 as the “Decade of Healthy Aging”[2]. Healthy or successful aging, based on Rowe and Kahn’s conceptual model, is characterized by the absence of major diseases and disabilities, preserved cognitive and physical function, and sustained social engagement [3]. Individuals meeting these criteria tend to maintain better overall well-being [4]. Therefore, identifying modifiable risk factors is essential for promoting successful aging.

While traditional epidemiological research often examines risk factors in relation to single, disease-specific outcomes (e.g., cardiovascular disease, cognitive decline, or functional impairment), linking them to the multi-dimensional, composite outcome of “successful aging” represents a more innovative and comprehensive approach. This holistic perspective acknowledges that health in later life is not merely the absence of disease, but a complex interplay of physical, cognitive, and social well-being. From a public health standpoint, identifying risk factors that impact successful aging provides a more valuable target for interventions. Such interventions can be designed to promote overall thriving and quality of life across multiple domains, rather than solely preventing isolated conditions, thereby aligning more closely with the overarching goals of the Decade of Healthy Aging. Therefore, identifying modifiable risk factors that influence this composite outcome is essential for promoting successful aging.

Hypertension is among the most prevalent and modifiable cardiovascular risk factors, with rising global incidence [5]. Elevated blood pressure (BP) is not only linked to cardiovascular disease but also to cognitive decline [6, 7]. However, single-point BP measurements may fail to reflect long-term physiological changes, as BP levels fluctuate over time. Recent research has emphasized the significance of BP trajectories in predicting adverse health outcomes [8–12].

Despite growing attention to BP trajectories in clinical research, their relationship with the multi-dimensional composite of successful aging remains insufficiently explored. This study aimed to prospectively investigate the association between systolic blood pressure (SBP) trajectories and successful aging among middle-aged and older adults in China, using data from the nationally representative China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS).

Methods

Data source and study population

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study is a nationally representative panel survey covering 28 provinces, 150 counties, and 450 villages across mainland China. Detailed information on the study design and blood sample collection has been described elsewhere [13–15]. The baseline survey was conducted in 2011, followed by four waves of follow-up in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052–11015). This study included individuals aged 45 years and older in China. This specific age range was chosen to capture individuals transitioning into middle and older adulthood, which is a critical period for the development of chronic conditions and the establishment of health trajectories relevant to successful aging. For this analysis, data from the 2011 and 2020 waves were utilized. Of the 17,705 individuals enrolled at baseline, participants were excluded if they lacked baseline data on age, sex, or blood pressure (2011, 2013, 2015), or if information on successful aging in 2020 was missing. After applying these criteria, 1,960 participants were included in the final analytic sample (Fig. 1).

Assessment of successful aging

This study defines successful aging in accordance with Rowe and Kahn’s theoretical framework [16], encompassing five core domains: (1) absence of major chronic diseases, (2) intact physical function, (3) high cognitive performance, (4) good mental health, and (5) active social engagement. The assessment criteria for each domain are outlined below:

Absence of major diseases: Participants were asked whether they had been diagnosed by a physician with any major chronic conditions, including diabetes, cancer, chronic lung disease, heart disease, or stroke, based on national disease burden data and previous literature [4, 17, 18]. Those reporting none of these conditions were considered free of major chronic diseases.

Preserved Physical Function: Physical functioning was measured using the Activities of Daily Living (ADL) scale [19], which assesses independence in six basic tasks: dressing, bathing or showering, eating, getting in or out of bed, using the toilet, and controlling bladder or bowel movements. Participants requiring assistance with any of these activities were classified as having impaired physical function.

High cognitive function: Cognitive performance was evaluated using three instruments: the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS-10), a 10-word immediate and delayed recall test, and a figure-drawing task. TICS-10 includes five orientation items (year, month, date, season, and day of the week) and serial subtraction of 7 from 100, with a total of 10 point. Word recall involves memorizing and recalling 10 words immediately and after a delay, with the average of the two scores used (maximum 10 points). Figure drawing evaluates visuospatial ability by asking participants to replicate two overlapping pentagons (1 point). The composite score ranges from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating better cognition. Following prior studies [17, 20], individuals scoring at or above the sample median were categorized as having high cognitive function [19, 21].

Good mental health: Mental health was assessed using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) [22]. Scores range from 0 to 30, with higher values indicating more severe depressive symptoms. A score below 10 was defined as indicative of good mental health [19, 21].

Active participation in social activities: Social participation was determined based on involvement in any of the 11 types of activities assessed in the CHARLS questionnaire during the past month. These activities included, but were not limited to, socializing with friends, playing games (e.g., Mahjong, chess, cards), volunteering or charity activities, or participating in community club events [23]. Engagement in at least one activity qualified participants as socially active.

Systolic blood pressure trajectory model

BP, including SBP and diastolic blood pressure (DBP), was measured three times using a sphygmomanometer after the participant had rested in a seated position for over five minutes, following a standardized protocol. The average of the three readings was used as the final BP value. Measurements were obtained in 2011, 2013, and 2015. To analyze longitudinal SBP trends, a latent class trajectory model (LCTM) was applied [24], allowing SBP trajectories to be modeled as a binomial function of time. LCTM is a person-centered statistical technique that identifies unobserved (latent) subgroups of individuals who follow similar patterns of change over time. This approach allows for the categorization of individuals into distinct trajectory groups based on their longitudinal SBP measurements.The optimal number of trajectory groups was determined using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with model selection based on a posterior probability >0.7 for each group and a minimum group size of 2% of the total sample [25]. The BIC values for models with 2, 3, and 4 groups were 50456.75, 50295.43, and 50183.30, respectively. While the 4-group model showed a slightly lower BIC, we ultimately selected the 3-group model based on a balance of statistical fit, clinical interpretability, and the substantial size of each identified trajectory group. The 3-group model provided a parsimonious and clinically meaningful classification of SBP patterns. The selected 3-group model yielded a log-likelihood of −25090.86, with 15 parameters (npm). The estimated proportions of participants in each of the three identified trajectories were approximately 56.9% (Trajectory 1), 35.2% (Trajectory 2), and 7.9% (Trajectory 3).

Covariates

Covariate selection was based on factors previously associated with successful aging, including age, sex (male/female), body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol consumption, education level (low/middle/high), marital status (married/other), and use of antihypertensive medications. Education levels were categorized as low (primary school or below), middle (middle to high school), and high (high school or above). BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m²) and classified according to Chinese standards into underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal (18.5–23.9 kg/m²), overweight (24.0–27.9 kg/m²), and obese (≥ 28.0 kg/m²)[17]. Hypertension was defined as systolic BP ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic BP ≥ 90 mmHg, self-reported diagnosis, or current use of antihypertensive drugs.

Statistical analysis

Participants were classified into three groups according to SBP trajectories. The distribution of continuous variables was examined for normality. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed variables were reported as median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons for continuous variables were performed using the independent samples t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate; categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

Logistic regression models were used to evaluate the association between SBP trajectories and successful aging. Stratified analyses were further conducted by age (< 60 vs. ≥60 years), sex, smoking status, alcohol use, and weight status (overweight or obese).

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 21.0) and R (version 4.3.2). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Systolic blood pressure trajectory

Based on changes in SBP, three distinct trajectory classes were identified (Fig. 2). T1: Stable Low – SBP remained consistently around 115 mmHg. T2: Stable High – SBP fluctuated within a high range of 138–145 mmHg. T3: Unstable High – SBP initially declined from 175 mmHg to 157 mmHg, followed by a further decrease to 151 mmHg. The proportion of participants in each trajectory group was 56.9% for T1, 35.1% for T2, and 7.9% for T3.

Association between systolic blood pressure trajectories and successful aging

Supplementary Table S2 presented the baseline characteristics of participants stratified by successful aging. This table clearly shows that individuals who achieve successful aging tend to be slightly younger, more often male, have higher education levels, and critically, exhibit significantly better cardiovascular health indicators, characterized by lower SBP, lower prevalence of hypertension, and no use of anti-hypertensive medication at baseline (all P < 0.05).

Table 2 presents the association between SBP trajectories and successful aging. The proportions of participants achieving successful aging were 6.0%, 3.0%, and 0.6% in the stable low, stable high, and unstable high groups, respectively (Fig. 3). After adjusting for potential confounders, compared with the stable low group, the adjusted odds ratios (ORs) for successful aging were 0.490 (95% CI: 0.293–0.817) for the stable high group and 0.099 (95% CI: 0.014–0.727) for the unstable high group. A similar pattern was observed when examining the five individual domains of successful aging. Compared to those in the stable low SBP group, participants in the stable high and unstable high SBP groups had significantly lower odds of being free of major chronic diseases. Additionally, the stable high SBP group showed significantly lower odds of having no physical functional impairment, whereas this association did not reach statistical significance for the unstable high SBP group. For maintaining high cognitive function, only the unstable high SBP group showed significantly lower odds, whereas the stable high SBP group did not reach statistical significance.

Subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses were performed to examine potential effect modification in the association between SBP trajectories and successful aging. No statistically significant interactions were observed after adjusting for all relevant confounders (Table 3). It is important to note that due to the relatively small number of successful aging cases and the inherent reduction in sample size within subgroups, subgroup analyses should be interpreted with extreme caution and are considered exploratory rather than definitive. These findings are hypothesis-generating and warrant validation in larger, adequately powered studies. Additionally, the associations between elevated SBP trajectories and reduced odds of successful aging remained robust in sensitivity analyses, including models with additional adjustment for antihypertensive medication use and models excluding participants who used antihypertensive drugs (Table S3).

Discussion

In this longitudinal cohort study of middle-aged and elderly Chinese individuals, we examined the association between SBP trajectories and successful aging. Three distinct SBP trajectories were identified: stable low, stable high, and unstable high. Compared to participants with the stable low SBP trajectory, those in the stable high and unstable high trajectories had 51% and 90.1% lower odds of achieving successful aging, respectively. These findings highlight the critical role of monitoring SBP changes in middle-aged and older adults, offering new insights for promoting successful aging.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first prospective study to investigate the association between SBP trajectories and healthy aging in older adults. Given that BP fluctuates over time, assessing BP across multiple time points provides a more accurate representation of its impact on healthy aging than a single baseline measurement. Previous studies have primarily focused on the relationship between SBP and specific health outcomes in adults, finding that elevated SBP is associated with increased risks of cardiovascular disease (CVD) [8], stroke [11], frailty [25], renal function progression [26], and cognitive impairment [27]. For instance, a multi-trajectory analysis from a Chinese geriatric physical examination cohort demonstrated that sustained high levels of both SBP and DBP were linked to a higher risk of incident CVD [8]. Another study, using BP measurements at two time points, showed that progression from normotension or prehypertension to hypertension was associated with an increased CVD risk [28]. Additionally, a study identifying four SBP trajectories from age 55 to 106 revealed that patterns of high but decreasing or rapidly increasing SBP were associated with higher risks of stroke and mortality [11]. Findings from the CHARLS study indicated that SBP trajectories characterized by remission followed by re-elevation, or consistently high levels, were associated with increased frailty risk [25]. In patients with chronic kidney disease, elevated SBP trajectories assessed at three time points were linked to worse renal outcomes compared to stable SBP [26]. Our study extends this literature by evaluating healthy aging comprehensively—incorporating chronic disease status, cognitive function, physical and mental function, and social participation. This multifaceted approach provides more robust evidence linking SBP trajectories to overall health in older adults. Healthy aging is influenced by various factors such as regular physical activity, balanced nutrition, healthy body weight, and other lifestyle habits [29]. Even after adjusting for key covariates including sex, education, marital status, smoking, drinking, and body type, our findings consistently showed that stable high and unstable high SBP trajectories were associated with significantly lower odds of successful aging compared to stable low trajectories. Moreover, subgroup analyses revealed no significant effect modification by these covariates.

Our study revealed distinct SBP trajectories—stable low, stable high, and unstable high—each carrying unique implications for successful aging. The stable low SBP trajectory represents an ideal scenario, underscoring the importance of maintaining optimal blood pressure from middle age to prevent adverse health outcomes. This trajectory serves as a benchmark for successful cardiovascular health and emphasizes the long-term benefits of sustained healthy lifestyle choices and effective early BP management. Conversely, the stable high SBP trajectory highlights the persistent and often silent risk associated with chronically elevated blood pressure. Individuals on this trajectory, while seemingly stable, face continuous physiological stress that significantly diminishes their likelihood of achieving successful aging. This pattern emphasizes the critical need for consistent, long-term pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions to lower and maintain BP within healthy ranges, even if the levels appear “stable” over time. The unstable high SBP trajectory is particularly concerning and warrants more detailed attention in real-world clinical settings. Fluctuations in SBP, even if the average is high, may indicate suboptimal BP control, greater physiological dysregulation, or increased vascular stiffness, all of which contribute to a higher risk of adverse outcomes. This instability poses diagnostic and management challenges, as single-point measurements might not capture the true burden of hypertension. From a real-world perspective, this trajectory suggests that patients with fluctuating high BP may require more frequent monitoring, personalized treatment adjustments, and enhanced patient education on medication adherence and lifestyle modifications to stabilize their BP and mitigate long-term risks.

These trajectory findings move beyond the traditional focus on single blood pressure measurements, offering a more dynamic and nuanced understanding of cardiovascular risk. In clinical practice, understanding a patient’s SBP trajectory can enable more personalized and proactive interventions. For instance, identifying an “unstable high” pattern could prompt clinicians to investigate medication adherence, assess for secondary hypertension, or recommend ambulatory BP monitoring to get a more accurate picture of daily fluctuations. For public health, these findings can inform targeted screening programs, emphasizing the need for regular BP checks not just at a single point, but over an extended period, particularly for middle-aged adults. Promoting awareness of SBP patterns and the cumulative effect of even seemingly minor elevations or fluctuations can empower individuals to take a more active role in their cardiovascular health, thereby fostering a greater chance of achieving multi-dimensional successful aging. Our results underscore that the journey of SBP over time, not just its destination at a single point, is a critical determinant of healthy longevity.

The unstable high SBP trajectory presents a particularly complex and intriguing pattern. While characterized by overall high and fluctuating SBP, our analysis revealed a notable declining trend within this group over the study period. This observation warrants careful interpretation. It is plausible that individuals in this consistently participating and data-complete group were more health-conscious or had better access to healthcare. Upon diagnosis or increasing awareness of their elevated SBP, they might have initiated or intensified anti-hypertensive medication or adopted significant lifestyle modifications. This proactive health management could contribute to a reduction in SBP over time. However, as indicated in our baseline characteristics (Table 1), only 32% of participants in the “Unstable High” group reported taking anti-hypertensive medication at baseline. This suggests that while medication likely contributed for some, it might not be the sole explanation for the entire group’s trend, and highlights a critical public health gap in hypertension management. Another important statistical phenomenon is regression to the mean. Individuals whose SBP was unusually high or experienced temporary spikes at earlier time points might naturally show a tendency towards their average SBP over time. Given the “unstable” nature of this trajectory, it is possible that some measurements were outliers, and subsequent readings simply moved closer to their individual long-term mean, appearing as a group-level decline. While not a physiological improvement per se, this statistical effect can contribute to the observed trend. Despite this observed decline, the “unstable high” trajectory remains a high-risk pattern due to its inherent variability and generally elevated levels. From a clinical perspective, identifying such a pattern should prompt clinicians to investigate medication adherence, assess for secondary hypertension, and recommend more frequent monitoring, potentially including ambulatory BP monitoring, to capture the true burden of fluctuations. For public health, these findings underscore the need for early and consistent BP management strategies, as well as addressing barriers to medication adherence, even for those whose SBP might show a later decline. Our results emphasize that the journey of SBP over time, not just its destination at a single point, is a critical determinant of healthy longevity.

It is well established that BP tends to increase with age, a change that may serve as a compensatory mechanism to maintain adequate perfusion of vital organs such as the heart, brain, and kidneys. In our study, we identified three distinct SBP trajectories among participants. The stable low group maintained a mean SBP of approximately 115 mmHg, indicative of normotension. In contrast, the stable high group had SBP levels fluctuating between 138 and 145 mmHg, while the unstable high group exhibited an initial decline from 175 mmHg to 157 mmHg, followed by a further decrease to 151 mmHg. Regarding hypertension prevalence, 10.6%, 61.0%, and 100.0% of individuals in the stable low, stable high, and unstable high trajectory groups, respectively, were classified as hypertensive. However, only 4.8%, 23.1%, and 32.3% of individuals in these respective groups reported taking antihypertensive medication, indicating a relatively low rate of pharmacological intervention across all trajectories. Prior research has underscored the importance of antihypertensive medication adherence. For instance, one study demonstrated that consistent use of antihypertensive drugs was associated with slower cognitive decline in Chinese community-dwelling middle-aged and older adults [30]. Moreover, the SPRINT trial found that intensive SBP control reduced the risk of mild cognitive impairment by 19% among adults aged ≥ 50 years with hypertension but without diabetes or stroke history [31]. In our analysis, the association between elevated SBP trajectories and lower odds of successful aging remained robust even after additional adjustment for antihypertensive medication use or after excluding individuals taking such medications. This suggests that while antihypertensive therapy is important, the SBP trajectory itself may independently influence healthy aging outcomes. These findings highlight the need for future research to investigate non-pharmacological strategies—such as lifestyle modification, diet, physical activity, and stress management—to better manage long-term BP patterns and support successful aging in older populations.

Our study also examined the associations between SBP trajectories and the five individual components of successful aging. Participants with stable high or unstable high SBP trajectories had significantly lower odds of being free from major chronic diseases, maintaining intact physical function, and exhibiting high cognitive function, compared to those in the stable low trajectory group. However, no significant associations were observed between SBP trajectories and either good mental health or active participation in social activities. Unlike single-point BP measurements, SBP trajectories capture long-term trends and are independently associated with the onset, progression, and outcomes of various chronic conditions. Through complex multisystem mechanisms involving the vascular, metabolic, cardiac, and renal systems, elevated SBP trajectories can increase arterial stiffness, promote insulin resistance, contribute to kidney damage, and induce cardiac remodeling—factors that collectively heighten the risk of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease [32, 33]. The observed association between SBP trajectories and impaired physical function may be mediated by multiple physiological pathways. Hypertension has been shown to reduce capillary density in skeletal muscle, thereby impairing oxygen and nutrient delivery and accelerating declines in muscle strength, physical performance, and endurance [34]. A two-year longitudinal study found that hypertension was associated with slower gait speed [35], and other studies have reported a link between hypertension and reduced grip strength [36]. Similarly, SBP trajectories have been associated with cognitive decline. Individuals with persistently elevated SBP are at greater risk for cognitive deterioration [37], and greater variability in SBP and DBP has also been independently linked to cognitive decline [38]. The underlying mechanisms are complex and likely include cerebrovascular damage, disruption of the blood–brain barrier, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and brain atrophy [39]. In contrast, the lack of association between SBP trajectories and mental health or social participation in our study suggests that these dimensions of successful aging may be influenced by factors beyond blood pressure regulation. The relationship between BP and mental health remains inconclusive, with mixed evidence. Some studies have reported that higher SBP is associated with fewer depressive symptoms, greater well-being, and reduced emotional brain activity [40], while others have found conflicting results[41, 42].. These inconsistencies may reflect the multifactorial and bidirectional nature of the relationship, influenced by genetic, psychological, behavioral, and environmental factors. In our study, the lack of association between SBP trajectories and good mental health or active participation in social activities suggests that these dimensions of successful aging may be influenced by factors other than blood pressure alone. For instance, mental health and social participation are often shaped by a complex interplay of psychological, social, and environmental factors, such as social support, life satisfaction, and community engagement, which may not be directly influenced by SBP trajectories. Future research should further explore the underlying mechanisms linking SBP trajectories to different aspects of successful aging. Longitudinal studies with detailed assessments of psychological, social, and lifestyle factors are needed to elucidate the complex pathways involved. Additionally, interventions targeting multiple dimensions of successful aging, including both physiological and psychosocial factors, should be developed and tested to optimize health outcomes in older adults.

This study has several strengths, including its high-quality longitudinal design based on data from a nationally representative Chinese sample, spanning nine years. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the observational design precludes the establishment of definitive causal relationships between SBP trajectories and successful aging. Second, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce recall bias or misclassification. Third, while we adjusted for numerous potential confounders, the possibility of residual or unmeasured confounding remains. Fourth, the focus on middle-aged and older Chinese individuals may limit the generalizability of the findings to other age groups or populations of different ethnicities. A significant limitation of our study is the potential for selection bias, arising from the substantial attrition of participants from the initial cohort to the final analytical sample. Out of 17,705 participants in the initial database, our final analytical sample comprised 1,960 individuals who met all inclusion criteria and had complete data for the variables of interest. Our comparative analysis (as presented in Supplementary Table S1) revealed notable and statistically significant differences in several key baseline characteristics between the included and excluded participants. These differential baseline characteristics suggest that our analytical sample may not be fully representative of the broader middle-aged and older adult population in China. The included participants tend to be younger, more female, less educated, and more likely to be married and smoke. Consequently, the external generalizability of our findings regarding SBP trajectories and successful aging might be limited, as our sample may represent a specific subpopulation with distinct demographic and socioeconomic profiles. While we have transparently presented these differences, future research with more complete longitudinal data collection or employing advanced statistical methods to account for missingness (e.g., multiple imputation, inverse probability weighting) could further mitigate this bias and enhance the representativeness of the study population. Nevertheless, within our carefully selected analytical cohort, the internal validity of the observed associations remains robust, providing valuable insights into the relationship between SBP trajectories and successful aging among this specific group. Another significant limitation pertains to the statistical power of our study. The relatively low number of successful aging cases (n = 89) in our analytical sample, means that some analyses, particularly complex models or subgroup analyses, may be underpowered to detect true associations. The findings from such analyses should still be interpreted with considerable caution and viewed as exploratory. Future research with larger cohorts might be able to capture and analyze such rare but potentially important trajectory patterns. Despite controlling for a comprehensive set of sociodemographic and health-related covariates, we acknowledge the potential for unmeasured confounding. Specifically, the regional environment (e.g., urban/rural disparities, access to healthcare, local socioeconomic development, cultural norms) could significantly influence both SBP trajectories and successful aging outcomes, and may also be correlated with SBP levels. These unmeasured factors could potentially confound the observed associations. For instance, individuals in more developed urban areas might have different SBP trajectories due to lifestyle factors (e.g., diet, stress) but also better access to healthcare for managing BP, simultaneously influencing their successful aging. The inability to fully capture and adjust for these complex environmental influences is a limitation, and future research should aim to incorporate more detailed community-level data to further elucidate these relationships.

Conclusions

Stable high and unstable high SBP trajectories are associated with lower odds of successful aging, particularly its physiological dimension, compared to the stable low trajectory group. These findings highlight the importance of monitoring longitudinal changes in SBP among middle-aged and older adults and offer novel insights into strategies for promoting successful aging by maintaining physiological health.
