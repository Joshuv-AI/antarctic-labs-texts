Pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia of the penis: A rare mimic of squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Mohammed Taher Mujahid, Vikas Kumar Panwar, Gautam Shubhankar, Ankur Mittal
Published: 2024

INTRODUCTION

Pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia (PEH) is a rare benign lesion that occurs on the penis, characterized by hyperplastic epithelial proliferation, mimicking squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). The exact aetiology is unknown, but it results from an abnormal hyperplastic response to chronic inflammation or trauma, leading to an overgrowth of epithelial cells. Clinically it presents as a solitary, slow-growing lesion on the penis, and may appear as a papule, plaque, or ulcer. It may be painful or asymptomatic. It can occur anywhere on the penis, but most commonly on the glans or shaft. Histopathology is essential for definitive diagnosis, showing hyperplastic epithelial proliferation with no atypia or invasion. Treatment generally involves wide local excision or circumcision.

Here, we are presenting a rare case of PEH of the penis in a 64-year-old patient, mimicking an SCC highlighting the importance of thorough evaluation, diagnostic challenges and surgical intervention in managing such penile masses. The patient’s history of diabetes and lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) added complexity to the case, emphasizing the need for multidisciplinary care.

CASE SUMMARY

A 64-year-old diabetic male presented with a one-year history of penile swelling, accompanied by obstructive LUTS. Despite the topical medications, the swelling progressed. General physical examination was normal. Upon regional clinical examination, the patient exhibited a large, indurated penile mass measuring approximately 5 cm×4 cm involving almost the entire penile shaft, with fowl smelling purulent discharge (Fig. 1). Bilateral palpable, multiple, discrete, non-tender inguinal nodes were noted. Haematological, biochemical, and ultrasonographic evaluation of the upper tracts was normal. Uroflowmetry showed a maximum flow (Qmax) of 4.1 mL/second, a box pattern, raised post-voiding residual volume of 180 mL. A suprapubic catheter (SPC) was inserted because of a large obstructive penile mass. A wedge biopsy of the lesion revealed features suggestive of PEH. The endocrinologist’s opinion was taken and blood sugar levels were controlled.

Upon clinical examination a large penile mass involving the glans, external urethral meatus and reaching up to the proximal penile shaft is seen.

We discussed the diagnosis and treatment plan with the patient. Antibiotics were given for 1 week as per pus culture & sensitivity reports. He underwent total penectomy with Johanson’s permanent perineal urethrostomy under spinal anaesthesia. A circumferential incision was made at the root of the penis. Layers of incisions were developed to expose Buck’s fascia, corpora cavernosa and spongiosum. A total penectomy was carried out, ensuring meticulous haemostasis. A vertical perineal incision allowed access to the bulbospongiosus muscle. Under guidance from a bougie inserted through the SPC site, an incision was made over the bulbar urethra. The urethral margin was meticulously sutured to the skin over a Foley catheter. The postoperative course was uneventful. SPC and per urethral catheter were removed 1 week later. The patient is currently voiding well.

Histopathology showed stratified squamous epithelial lined tissue, focally ulcerated and focally hyperplastic forming tongue-like growths, subepithelium showed chronic inflammatory infiltrate but no evidence of malignancy or invasion, suggesting the final diagnosis of PEH (Fig. 2).

Histopathological examination under 20× magnification on H&E stain showing stratified squamous epithelial lined tissue, focally ulcerated and focally hyperplastic forming tongue-like growths. Subepithelium showing chronic inflammatory infiltrate but no evidence of malignancy or invasion.

We recommended regular follow-up appointments to monitor for potential recurrence, along with annual clinical examinations. Additionally, patient was educated on self-examination techniques and the importance of early detection.

DISCUSSION

PEH is an extremely rare condition that mimics SCC, accounting for less than 1% of penile lesions. Typically affects adults (40–70 years).

In a study by Cubilla et al. [1], 10 cases of pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia of the penis were diagnosed out of 300 cases of penile lesions. These lesions were found to be well-differentiated, non-verruciform, frequently multicentric, and preferentially originating from the penile foreskin mucosa of older males. Another Indian study found 5 cases of PEH among 250 patients with penile lesions, representing 2% of cases [2]. Indian studies report a mean age range of 40–55 years, with a range of 25–70 years [1,2,3].

Chronic inflammation and trauma are proposed risk factors, although the exact aetiology remains unclear [1,2]. Human papilloma virus infection has been implicated [3,4]. A study reported 15 cases of PEH over 10 years, highlighting its rarity [5]. Uncircumcised individuals may be more prone to PEH, as reported in Indian studies [1,2].

The number of studies and cases is limited due to the rarity of PEH.

PEH typically presents as well-demarcated plaques or nodules of varying sizes. The lesions often appear light purple or erythematous and can have a finely papular or verrucoid (wart-like) texture. Lesions are commonly found on the glans penis and are usually asymptomatic but can sometimes cause itching, burning, or discomfort.

Large penile mass can involve the urethra and can lead to bothersome LUTS and obstructive uropathy. It may get infected and require antibiotics. Insertion of a SPC may be necessary for preoperative optimization and infection control, ensuring an uneventful postoperative course and facilitating effective wound healing.

A wedge biopsy will reveal PEH and rule out malignancy. Special stains may be used to exclude infectious causes and confirm the diagnosis.

Ultimately penile mass needs excision with adequate surgical margins. This is achieved by partial penectomy if an adequate stump can be preserved for a urinary stream. Total penectomy with permanent Perineal urethrostomy may be needed in cases where the penile stump is inadequate after excision of the mass, as it was required in our case.

Histopathology shows marked irregular hyperplasia of the epidermis, which can appear as vegetative or verrucoid growth. Prominent dermal acute and chronic inflammation is often present, with focal extravasation of red blood cells and scattered eosinophils [2]. Focal microabscesses and thickening of the stratum corneum (hyperkeratosis) and the epidermis (acanthosis) are common [1].

Unlike SCC, PEH does not show cytological atypia. Conditions like lichen sclerosis, genital warts, and chronic inflammatory dermatoses can also present similarly and are considered in the differential diagnosis.

Complications include recurrence, scarring, meatal stenosis (if circumcision is performed), psychological distress.

Understanding these clinical features and the importance of histopathological examination can aid in the accurate diagnosis and management of PEH, preventing unnecessary aggressive treatments for what is ultimately a benign condition.

CONCLUSIONS

This case highlights the rarity, diagnostic challenges, need for thorough evaluation, preoperative suprapubic diversion, and importance of a multidisciplinary approach in treatment planning and managing such rare penile masses. The patient’s history of diabetes and LUTS added to the complexity, emphasizing the need for meticulous surgical planning and execution. The successful outcome underscores the effectiveness of total penectomy with perineal urethrostomy in such challenging cases.
