Capturing Financial Burden Concerns in an ALS Multidisciplinary Clinic
Authors: Nikhita Pathapati, Minh Nguyen, Jaden Ju, Mahita Maddukuri, John Kiehl, Elizabeth Kvale, Aaron P. Thrift, Sarah Berth, Astrid Grouls
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsAmyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is associated with substantial financial burden. How financial concerns are documented or addressed in ALS multidisciplinary clinics (MDC) is unclear. We aimed to characterize financial concerns among people living with ALS (pALS) and how they are identified and documented by multidisciplinary clinic staff.MethodsWe performed a retrospective electronic medical record (EMR) review for people living with ALS (pALS) attending at least two visits at the MDC, identifying documentation of financial concerns across all MDC notes throughout 2024. Semi‐structured interviews with MDC staff explored experiences with financial concern discussions and documentation. Rapid qualitative analysis was used to analyze interview content.ResultsAmong 67 pALS with at least 2 MDC visits, 45 (67.2%) had ≥ 1 documented financial concerns. Financial concerns were most documented in neurology physician notes and portal messages (both 77.8%) and included insurance denials, medication costs, disability/Medicare navigation, caregiving expenses, and home modifications. Social work and patient portal notes provided greater detail than other staff notes. Staff interviews highlighted insurance and equipment costs as frequent financial concerns. Four qualitative themes emerged: insurance challenges and reliance on non‐insurance resources, financial barriers limiting care and quality of life, limited proactive conversations/documentation, and the central role of social work.DiscussionFinancial concerns are common among pALS but are inconsistently discussed and documented in the EMR, often arising through asynchronous communication or social work. Integrating financial screening and promoting proactive multidisciplinary documentation may improve identification of financial concerns and support more equitable, person‐centered ALS care.

Introduction

“Financial toxicity” first described the cost burden of cancer care as a clinically relevant adverse effect [1]. Financial toxicity is associated with reduced medication adherence, poorer health‐related quality of life, increased symptom burden and psychological distress, and increased mortality among cancer patients [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Mounting evidence demonstrates that cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and other chronic illnesses, including neuromuscular conditions, similarly are associated with financial toxicity [9, 10, 11, 12].

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) presents substantial costs to patients and families from the period leading up to diagnosis through the care journey [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. In the US, many people living with ALS (pALS) get their care in multidisciplinary clinics (MDC), in which pALS see team members representing multiple health‐care disciplines.

Research among people with cancer shows that cost conversations occur in about 22% of encounters and that cost‐saving initiatives are frequently mentioned [18]. Rates of such conversations in ALS MDCs are unknown. We aimed to characterize financial concerns among pALS and how they are identified and documented by MDC staff.

Methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Baylor College of Medicine (BCM) (protocol H‐55807). pALS and staff were recruited from the BCM ALS MDC, an academic, urban clinic serving predominantly insured persons who typically come from throughout Texas and parts of Louisiana.

Retrospective Chart Review

pALS seen at the BCM ALS MDC were eligible for inclusion if they had an established diagnosis of ALS per neurologist documentation, and at least two lifetime clinic visits with at least one in 2024. At least two visits were selected as an inclusion criterion to ensure pALS established their primary ALS care with the MDC.

Evaluators (MN, NP, JJ, MM) reviewed all staff notes, patient messages, and telephone encounters that occurred with MDC staff from January to December 2024. Notes by medical providers seen by pALS within other medical specialties or for reasons unrelated to their ALS diagnosis were excluded. Notably, there are some staff members (particularly pulmonology, palliative care, and social work) who are not seen by pALS at all MDC visits. Which patients had or had not established or recently seen each staff member in MDC was not separately captured.

Evaluators reviewed each note for documentation of financial concern, including difficulties with cost, insurance, pay, and affordability. Auto‐populated text was excluded unless modified or referred to by the notewriter. Evaluators captured the direct quote and note type. Quotes were reviewed using an iterative, consensus‐based approach to develop categories of financial concern with discrete definitions.

Interviews

We conducted qualitative interviews with MDC staff practicing at the BCM ALS MDC for at least 6 months to ensure ability to speak to their routine practice. All patient‐facing staff members associated with the clinic were approached for inclusion, with two personal follow‐up invitations sent to non‐responders. Participants provided verbal consent to a recorded videoconferencing discussion of their experiences with financial concerns for patients in the MDC, including how conversations arise, what concerns are raised, and how staff respond to and document these discussions.

Rapid qualitative analysis was used. Recordings were reviewed using a structured matrix organized by question. Evaluators (MN, NP, AG) independently reviewed and populated a matrix, applying an inductive approach to identify recurring concepts and patterns within each question domain. Evaluators met to synthesize findings and resolve discrepancies through consensus. Common themes were identified based on the convergence of responses across participants.

Quotes presented throughout this manuscript have been edited for brevity and clarity, taking care to preserve meaning.

Results

Chart Review Findings

Of 106 electronic medical records (EMR) reviewed, 67 (63.2%) met the study inclusion/exclusion criteria. Of these 67 pALS, 45 (67.2%) had ≥ 1 documented financial concern in 2024. Most pALS were insured (n = 65, 97.0%), male (n = 36, 53.7%), and White (n = 48; 71.6%). Average age was 62 ± 11.2 years.

Documentation of financial concerns most frequently appeared in neurology physician notes and patient portal messages (Table 1). Patient portal messages and social work notes provided greater detail, such as longitudinal context and the impact on daily functioning, than neurology physician notes. The most commonly documented financial concerns were insurance denials, medication affordability, challenges navigating disability and Medicare benefits, and costs related to caregiving, equipment, and home modifications (Table 2).

Staff Interview Findings

Fourteen staff members were interviewed, including neurology, pulmonology, neuropsychology, nutrition, speech‐language pathology, respiratory therapy, clinic coordinators, social work, and durable medical equipment and ALS Association representatives. Most staff reported caregivers (n = 12, 86%) initiated cost conversations rather than starting cost conversations themselves. Staff reported insurance and equipment‐related costs (n = 12, 86% each) were the predominant financial concerns, followed by medication (n = 7, 50%) and transportation expenses (n = 4, 29%). Staff described recommending external resources to assist patients, most often the ALS Association (n = 12, 86%), state‐level resources (n = 7, 50%), loans/grants (n = 4, 29%), and philanthropic organizations (n = 3, 21%).

Four themes arose in staff interviews: insurance‐related challenges and reliance on non‐insurance resources, financial barriers limiting care, limited proactive discussion and documentation of financial concerns, and the central role of social work in addressing financial needs (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we found that financial concerns among pALS span multiple domains, are raised by pALS and caregivers rather than staff, and are often captured outside of typical physician documentation. Financial concerns were brought up by most pALS, aligning with high rates of financial burden previously identified [9, 14, 15]. MDC staff perspectives on common financial burdens align with concerns raised in documentation. Insurance challenges and medication and equipment affordability were common documentation themes, consistent with prior literature that identified costs among pALS [13, 14, 15, 16, 17].

Despite staff awareness of the problem, financial concerns were often identified and documented outside in‐person clinic visits. Asynchronous communication, particularly patient portal messages and telephone encounters, were a substantial proportion of documented financial concerns, often providing greater depth and context than physician notes. This is consistent with work showing that portal messaging plays an important and complex role in chronic disease management and captures needs that are missed during time‐limited clinic visits [19, 20, 21]. When ALS care needs evolve rapidly, such between‐visit documentation may represent an underutilized opportunity for earlier identification and intervention.

These findings align with literature demonstrating that people with chronic, life‐limiting illnesses frequently experience financial distress that is underrecognized and under‐addressed [9, 10, 11, 12]. In oncology, the negative impact of financial toxicity on symptoms and survival has prompted efforts to measure and screen for financial distress using standardized tools [6, 22]. In contrast, ALS care lacks disease‐specific approaches to financial screening, despite evidence that ALS‐related costs are uniquely broad [13, 14, 15, 16, 23]. This gap may place a disproportionate burden on pALS and caregivers to initiate cost conversations.

Our staff interviews demonstrated their and pALS' potential discomfort with, or deference to, initiating cost‐related discussions, shifting the burden towards caregivers. Existing literature corroborates that both patients and physicians face barriers to discussing cost, such as discomfort, time, and uncertainty [5, 24]. Furthermore, the emphasis on family visits in the ALS MDC may make it challenging for individuals to privately voice concerns if they are worried about upsetting others. Clinicians are also uncertain about their role in mitigating financial toxicity, consistent with our providers' reliance on social work [24, 25].

Multiple limitations are noted, in particular that this single‐center experience may not be generalizable to ALS MDC centers nationwide. Staff and pALS populations may vary nationwide. Our patient population is generally insured and experiences likely vary in Veteran and safety net populations. Furthermore, though duration and stage of illness impact costs, we did not document some common ALS‐specific measures for our clinic population, such as ALSFRS‐R or time since symptom onset, and these may differ from other sites. We also relied on MDC staff recall and documentation in the EMR rather than direct observation of clinic encounters or patient interviews. Finally, pALS' financial concerns were not systematically queried, so the results of the EMR review would not have captured all financial concerns.

Though single‐site, this study identifies potential system‐level gaps in the recognition and documentation of financial concerns across ALS MDCs. Similar work in other MDCs would clarify how cost concerns are addressed nationwide. Integration of pALS and caregiver perspectives on in‐clinic financial burden discussions is also needed. Embedding ALS‐relevant financial screening into MDCs, with shared responsibility among staff to identify and address discipline‐specific cost concerns, may improve recognition and support of financial burden throughout the disease course. Regular multidisciplinary meetings that review between‐visit communication may identify pALS experiencing persistent financial concerns and barriers.

Conclusion

Current operations in MDCs may rely on pALS‐ and caregiver‐initiated discussions of cost concerns, which increases the risk for under‐addressed financial concerns. This has potential ramifications for symptom burden, treatment adherence, and quality of life. Given the unique nature of ALS progression and care needs, specific strategies to proactively invite cost discussion may be helpful to pALS and their families and reduce the burden on pALS and caregivers to raise their concerns.
