Abstract
. We study travelling wave solutions of a generalised Korteweg-de Vries-Burgers equation with a non-local diffusion term and a concave-convex flux. This model equation arises in the analysis of a shallow water flow by performing formal asymptotic expansions associated to the triple-deck regularisation (which is an extension of classical boundary layer theory). The resulting non-local operator is a fractional type derivative with order between 1 1 and 2 2 . Travelling wave solutions are typically analysed in relation to shock formation in the full shallow water problem. We show rigorously the existence of travelling waves that, formally, in the limit of vanishing diffusion and dispersion would give rise to non-classical shocks, that is, shocks that violate the Lax entropy condition. The proof is based on arguments that are typical in dynamical systems. The nature of the non-local operator makes this possible, since the resulting travelling wave equation can be seen as a delayed integro-differential equation. Thus, linearisation around critical points, continuity with respect to parameters and a shooting argument, are the main steps that we have proved and adapted for solving this problem.



Keywords.
non-local evolution equation, fractional derivative, travelling waves, non-classical shocks

1.
Introduction

In this paper we study existence of undercompressive travelling waves of the following one-dimensional evolution equation:

(1.1)

\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}u^{3}=\partial_{x}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[u]+\tau\partial_{x}^{3}u\,,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\,,\ t\geq 0

with
\tau>0
and
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[\cdot]
is the non-local operator here applied to a real valued function
g
,

(1.2)

\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[g](x)=d_{\alpha}\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{g^{\prime}(y)}{(x-y)^{\alpha}}\,dy\,,\quad\mbox{with}\quad 0<\alpha<1\,,\quad d_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}>0\,.

Observe that this operator only acts on the variable
x
. Here
\Gamma
denotes the Gamma function.

Equation (
1.1
) with
\alpha=1/3
and a flux function (
u^{3}
in (
1.1
)) which is either a quadratic or a cubic polynomial, has been derived from one (quadratic flux) and two (cubic flux) layer shallow water flows by performing formal asymptotic expansions in the framework of the triple-deck boundary
layer theory (see, e.g.
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
and
[
27
]
). In
[
27
]
numerical simulations suggest the existence of travelling waves that violate the entropy condition in the two-layer model. Such solutions resemble the inner structure, in such a particular limit, of small amplitude shock waves for the original shallow water problem, that are not entropic or non-classical (see e.g.
[
16
]
). In this manuscript we aim to study rigorously the existence of such travelling wave solutions for (
1.1
), which has a cubic flux and thus corresponds to the two-layer model when
\alpha=1/3
.

We recall, that existence and stability of travelling waves for (
1.1
) with a quadratic flux function have been established in
[
2
,
3
]
(
\tau=0
) and in
[
1
]
(
\tau>0
), see also
[
11
]
. The results of
[
2
]
and
[
1
]
form part of building blocks in the proof of our main theorem, as we shall describe below.

We recall that hyperbolic conservation laws exhibit discontinuous solutions, whose discontinuities or shocks may travel with constant speed. These solutions belong to the class of weak solutions. An extra condition is necessary to select a unique solution for the Cauchy problem. The most common way to derive uniqueness conditions is to use vanishing diffusion arguments (see e.g.
[
26
]
). In particular, for scalar conservation laws admissible shocks result from constructing travelling wave solutions of the regularised parabolic equation. There are, however, other types of natural regularisations of hyperbolic conservation laws. Moreover, different regularisations of hyperbolic conservation laws might lead to different weak solutions. Examples describing this situation are scalar conservation laws with a non-genuinely nonlinear flux (neither convex nor concave), such as a cubic flux. In this case, shocks violating the classical Lax entropy condition, see
[
17
]
, can be constructed by introducing a diffusive-dispersive regularisation. This kind of solutions is defined by Hayes and LeFloch
[
16
]
as non-classical shock waves, others refer to them as undercompressive, see
[
13
]
and the references therein. Our aim is thus to show the existence of such solutions for the non-local version of these regularisations (
1.1
). For a complementary study of non-classical shock waves for a scalar conservation law with local diffusion and non-local dispersion see
[
23
]
.

We notice that the parameter
\tau
results from a choice in the rescaling. Analogous to
[
17
]
we can consider the equation in the following form

\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}u^{3}=\varepsilon\partial_{x}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[u]+\delta\partial_{x}^{3}u\,,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\,,\ t\geq 0

where
\varepsilon
and
\delta
are positive constants that act as control parameters. This means that depending on their relative size either diffusion (
\delta\ll\varepsilon^{2/\alpha}
) or dispersion (
\delta\gg\varepsilon^{2/\alpha}
) dominates in the limit of both
\varepsilon
and
\delta\to 0
. The parameter
\tau
results from the scaling
(x,t)\to(\varepsilon^{1/\alpha}x,\varepsilon^{1/\alpha}t)
so that
\tau=\delta/\varepsilon^{2/\alpha}
. It is when this parameter is of order one when we expect to get solutions that violate the entropy condition.

We introduce the travelling wave variable
\xi=x-ct
with wave speed
c
and
look for solutions
u(x,t)=\phi(\xi)
of (
1.1
) which connect
two different far-field real values
\phi_{-}
and
\phi_{+}
. A straightforward
calculation shows that if
\phi
depends on
x
and
t
only through the
travelling wave variable, then so does
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[\phi]
, and the travelling wave problem becomes

(1.3)

-c\phi^{\prime}+(\phi^{3})^{\prime}=(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[\phi])^{\prime}+\tau\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}.

Here
′
denotes differentiation with respect to
\xi
. We can then integrate
(
1.3
) with respect to
\xi
to arrive at the following travelling wave equation:

(1.4)

\tau\phi^{\prime\prime}+\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[\phi]=h(\phi)\,,\quad\mbox{where}\quad h(\phi):=-c(\phi-\phi_{-})+\phi^{3}-\phi_{-}^{3}\,,

where we have used

(1.5)

\lim_{\xi\to-\infty}\phi(\xi)=\phi_{-}

and

(1.6)

\lim_{\xi\to\infty}\phi(\xi)=\phi_{+}\,.

If
\phi^{\prime}
decays to zero fast enough as
\xi\to\pm\infty
, then we obtain the Rankine-Hugoniot condition

(1.7)

c=\frac{\phi_{+}^{3}-\phi_{-}^{3}}{\phi_{+}-\phi_{-}}=\phi_{+}^{2}+\phi_{-}^{2}+\phi_{-}\phi_{+}\,,

that we assume throughout this manuscript.

One expects that travelling wave solutions correspond to classical shock waves in the limit of the diffusive and dispersive terms tending to zero (in the right order -dispersion followed by diffusion tending to zero- or at the right asymptotic rate) if the Lax entropy condition
[
21
, Chapter II.1]
is satisfied, which for the current non-linear flux reads:

(1.8)

3\phi_{+}^{2}<c<3\phi_{-}^{2}\,.

In this manuscript, however, we investigate the existence of travelling wave solutions that do not satisfy (
1.8
). In particular, we shall look for solutions that satisfy

(1.9)

c<3\min\{\phi_{-}^{2},\phi_{+}^{2}\}\,.

We assume without loss of generality that
\phi_{+}<\phi_{-}
(observe that the equation is invariant under the change
\phi\to-\phi
).

The roots of
h(\phi)
are
\phi_{+}
,
\phi_{-}
and
\phi_{c}:=-(\phi_{-}+\phi_{+})
. We require that

(1.10)

\phi_{+}<\phi_{c}<\phi_{-}

so that

(1.11)

h^{\prime}(\phi_{-})>0\,,\quad h^{\prime}(\phi_{c})<0\quad\mbox{and}\quad h^{\prime}(\phi_{+})>0\,,

which is equivalent to the condition (
1.9
).
We observe that this in particular implies that
\phi_{-}>0
and
\phi_{+}<0
.

Under these assumptions, travelling wave solutions of (
1.4
) with (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) correspond to
non-classical shocks

in the sense described earlier. On the other hand, solutions that satisfy (
1.4
) with (
1.5
) and

(1.12)

\lim_{\xi\to\infty}\phi(\xi)=\phi_{c}

correspond to classical shocks (with the same wave speed). We expect, as in the local case

(see e.g.
[
17
]
), the possibility of solutions satisfying (
1.6
) to be a distinguished limit in the sense that there is a unique value of
\tau
that allows such connection, whereas there is an open set of values of
\tau
that allows solutions to satisfy (
1.12
). We recall that this last possibility corresponds to the classical shock admissibility condition (
1.8
) if we replace
\phi_{+}
by
\phi_{c}
in the notation. Moreover, if the only zeros of
h
where
\phi_{-}
and
\phi_{c}
with (
1.11
), this would be the only possible travelling wave solutions that can be constructed in both the local and the non-local case (see
[
1
]
).

There is a further necessary condition for existence of (
1.4
)-(
1.6
) on the values
\phi_{-}
and
\phi_{+}
, namely that
\phi_{+}+\phi_{-}>0
, which implies that

(see (
1.7
)), in particular. We shall show this below (see Lemma

), and this is a consequence of the results of
[
1
]
.

Let us state our main theorem:

Theorem 1.1

(Existence of undercompressive travelling waves)
.

Let
\phi_{-}
and
\phi_{+}\in\mathbb{R}
such that (
1.10
) with
\phi_{c}=-(\phi_{+}+\phi_{-})
holds and such that

(1.13)

\phi_{+}+\phi_{-}>0.

Then, there exists
\tau>0
such that (
1.4
)-(
1.6
) has a unique solution (up to a shift in
\xi
) in
C_{b}^{3}(\mathbb{R})
.

The uniqueness of

is an open question.

The paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
, we give some preliminary results. First, in Section
2.1
, we give some lemmas with estimates related to the non-local operator. In Section

, we establish the existence of solutions of (

) that satisfy (

) and prove that there are only three possible behaviours that such trajectories will have as

or to

as

, or they tend to

as

for some finite value

. This allows us to approach the problem via a shooting argument, with shooting parameter

. With this in mind, in Section

we define three disjoint sets of

) where only classical solutions exist, it was conjectured that for small enough values of

).

In Section

In Appendix

Finally, we point out that for the proof we need some results on the linearised equations around

2.
Preliminary results

2.1.
The non-local operator and some elementary lemmas

Let us first recall some basic properties of the fractional differential operator

\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[\cdot]
. Since it can be written as the convolution of
g^{\prime}
with

\theta(x)x^{-\alpha}/\Gamma(1-\alpha)
(where
\theta
is the Heaviside function),
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[\cdot]

is a pseudo-differential operator with symbol

(2.1)

\frac{ik\sqrt{2\pi}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\mathcal{F}\left(\frac{\theta(x)}{x^{\alpha}}\right)(k)=\left(b_{\alpha}+ia_{\alpha}\,\mbox{sgn}(k)\right)|k|^{\alpha}\,,

i.e.

\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[u])(k)=\left(b_{\alpha}+ia_{\alpha}\,sgn(k)\right)|k|^{\alpha}\hat{u}(k)

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the Fourier transform

\mathcal{F}(\varphi)(k)=\hat{\varphi}(k)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-ikx}\varphi(x)\,dx\,,

and the coefficients
a_{\alpha}
and
b_{\alpha}
are given by

a_{\alpha}=\sin\left(\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}\right)>0\,,\qquad b_{\alpha}=\cos\left(\frac{\alpha\pi}{2}\right)>0\,,\quad\mbox{for}\quad 0<\alpha<1,

(we refer to
[
5
]
for the details of the computation to obtain (
2.1
)).
The operator on the right-hand side of (
1.1
) is then a pseudo-differential operator with symbol

(2.2)

\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha})=-\left(a_{\alpha}-ib_{\alpha}\,sgn(k)\right)|k|^{\alpha+1}\,,

which is dissipative in the sense that the real part of (
2.2
) is negative.

For
s\geq 0
we shall adopt the following notation for the Sobolev space of square integrable functions,

H^{s}:=\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}):\,\|u\|_{H^{s}}<\infty\}\,,\qquad\|u\|_{H^{s}}:=\|(1+|k|^{2})^{s/2}\hat{u}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\,,

and the corresponding homogeneous norm

\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}:=\||k|^{s}\hat{u}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\,.

Using that
(a_{\alpha}^{2}+b_{\alpha}^{2})=1
it is easy to see that

\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}=\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{s+\alpha}}
, and this suggests that one can interpret

\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[\cdot]
as a derivative of order
\alpha
. We also observe
that
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[\cdot]
is a bounded linear operator from
H^{s}
to
H^{s-\alpha}
, for all
s\geq 1
.

For
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
, let
C^{m}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
denote the set of functions, whose derivatives up to order
m
are continuous and bounded. Then one can also infer that
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[\cdot]
is a bounded linear operator from
C_{b}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
to
C_{b}(\mathbb{R})
. As explained in
[
2
]
, this can be easily seen by splitting the domain of integration in (
1.2
) into
(-\infty,x-M]
and
[x-M,x]
for some positive
M>0
. Then integration by parts in the first integral shows the boundedness of
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[\cdot]
. Moreover, we will need the following improved estimate:

Lemma 2.1
.

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
, let
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
g\in C_{b}^{1}(-\infty,x)
, then for every
z\in\mathbb{R}
with
z\leq x

|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[g](z)|\leq C_{\alpha}\left(\sup_{y\in(-\infty,z]}|g(y)|\right)^{1-\alpha}\left(\sup_{y\in(-\infty,z]}|g^{\prime}(y)|\right)^{\alpha}

where

C_{\alpha}=d_{\alpha}\left(\frac{2(2\alpha)^{-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}\right)\,.

In particular, if
g\in C_{b}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, then
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[g]\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R})
with

\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[g]\|_{\infty}\leq C_{\alpha}\,\|g^{\prime}\|_{\infty}^{\alpha}\,\|g\|_{\infty}^{1-\alpha}\,.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the estimate for Riesz-Feller operators, see e.g.
[
4
, Proposition 2.4]
. Let
z\leq x
, and let us denote, for simplicity,

A=\left(\sup_{y\in(-\infty,z]}|g(y)|\right)\quad\mbox{and}\quad A^{\prime}=\left(\sup_{y\in(-\infty,z]}|g^{\prime}(y)|\right)\,.

Then

(2.3)

\begin{split}|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[g](z)|=&d_{\alpha}\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{g^{\prime}(z-s)}{s^{\alpha}}\,ds\right|\\
\leq&d_{\alpha}\left|\int_{0}^{M}\frac{g^{\prime}(z-s)}{s^{\alpha}}\,ds\right|+d_{\alpha}\left|\int_{M}^{\infty}\frac{g^{\prime}(z-s)}{s^{\alpha}}\,ds\right|\,.\end{split}

We estimate the first integral by taking the supremum in
g^{\prime}
and computing the remaining integral, thus

\left|\int_{0}^{M}\frac{g^{\prime}(z-s)}{s^{\alpha}}\,ds\right|\leq A^{\prime}\,\int_{0}^{M}\frac{ds}{s^{\alpha}}=\frac{A^{\prime}}{1-\alpha}M^{1-\alpha}.

In the second integral, we first integrate by parts and pull out the supremum of
g
to deduce

\left|\int_{M}^{\infty}\frac{g^{\prime}(z-s)}{s^{\alpha}}\,ds\right|\leq\alpha\left|\int_{M}^{\infty}\frac{g(z-s)}{s^{\alpha+1}}\,ds\right|+AM^{-\alpha}\leq 2AM^{-\alpha}.

Using these estimates in (
2.3
) gives

(2.4)

|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[g](z)|\leq d_{\alpha}\left(\frac{A^{\prime}}{1-\alpha}M^{1-\alpha}+2AM^{-\alpha}\right)\,.

An easy computation shows that the minimum of the right-hand side of (
2.4
) is attained at
M=2\alpha A/A^{\prime}
and this implies the first statement. The second is a consequence of the first by taking the supremum over all values in
\mathbb{R}
.
∎

In some instances we shall need to split the integral operator (
1.2
) as follows

(2.5)

\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[g](x)=d_{\alpha}\int_{-\infty}^{x_{0}}\frac{g^{\prime}(y)}{(x-y)^{\alpha}}\,dy+d_{\alpha}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\frac{g^{\prime}(y)}{(x-y)^{\alpha}}\,dy\,,\quad\mbox{for some}\quad x_{0}<x\,,

and treat the first term as a known function, whereas the second one can be viewed as a
left-sided Caputo derivative, see e.g.
[
18
]
, which we denote by
\mathcal{D}_{x_{0}}^{\alpha}[\cdot]
, indicating that the integration is from a finite value

, i.e.
g\in C^{1}_{b}([x_{0},\infty))
and
\alpha\in(0,1]
.

Notice that the first term on the right-hand side of (

), which is a function of

, is not equal to
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[g](x_{0})
, which is a number for fixed

.

We shall use the following technical lemma several times:

Lemma 2.2
.

For all
\phi\leq-\phi_{-}
(
<0

2\phi^{3}\leq h(\phi)<C_{h}\phi^{3}(<0)\quad\mbox{and}\quad(0<)H(\phi)-H(\phi_{-})<C_{H}\phi^{4}

The proof is an elementary calculus exercise. It is important to recall that constants here only depend on

Existence of trajectories that satisfy (

with
\tilde{\xi}<0
and

sufficiently large, that is based on linearisation about
\phi=\phi_{-}
as

The statement can be proved as in

Statement (ii) follows by the same arguments as in the proof of

As a final remark, let us mention a crucial difference for the cubic flux. For the quadratic flux one can show that solutions remain bounded and that this implies the existence of a limit value as
\xi\to\infty
and therefore the only possible connection is to the constant value
\phi_{+}

). However, in the cubic case we cannot show that solutions of (

(2.8)

H(\phi):=\int_{0}^{\phi}h(y)\,dy=-c\frac{\phi^{2}}{2}+\frac{\phi^{4}}{4}+A\phi\,,\quad\mbox{with}\quad A=c\phi_{-}-\phi_{-}^{3}\,.

The difference
H(\phi)-H(\phi_{-})
being non-negative is a necessary condition for existence (see Lemma

below). In the quadratic case (and, more generally, for a genuinely non-linear flux) the corresponding primitive has a zero
\bar{\phi}<\phi_{+}
that gives a lower bound of the solutions, because in the interval
(\phi_{+},\phi_{-})
,
H(\phi)-H(\phi_{-})>0
. In the current case this is no longer true;
H(\phi)-H(\phi_{-})>0
is satisfied in
(-\infty,\phi_{-})
, then, the existence of a lower bound is not guaranteed by this argument.

In the next lemmas we summarise the conditions on

:

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\phi
be a solution of (

) that satisfies (

) and let
(-\infty,\xi_{exist})
be its interval of existence, where
\xi_{exist}\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
. Then,

\int_{-\infty}^{\xi}\phi^{\prime}(y)\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[\phi](y)\,dy\geq 0\,,\quad\forall\xi\in(-\infty,\xi_{exist}).

Moreover, the integral vanishes if and only if
\phi\equiv\phi_{-}
.

As a consequence, for all
\xi\in(-\infty,\xi_{exist})
, the following holds:

(2.9)

0\leq\frac{\tau}{2}(\phi^{\prime}(\xi))^{2}+\int_{-\infty}^{\xi}\phi^{\prime}(y)\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[\phi](y)\,dy=H(\phi(\xi))-H(\phi_{-})\,.

The first statement appears in the proof of
[
1
, Lemma 4]
(see also
[
11
]
for a similar result) and adapts the arguments of
[
22
, Chapter 9]
. The second part of the lemma follows by multiplying (
1.4
) by
\phi^{\prime}
and integrating with respect to
\xi
. The condition (
2.9
) is on the primitive of the corresponding non-linear function of the equation, here (
2.8
).

Next, we show (
1.13
), which is a necessary condition on the far-field values for a solution of (

)-(
1.6
) to exist.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\phi_{-}
and
\phi_{+}
satisfy (
1.10
). Then, the inequality

(2.10)

H(\phi_{+})-H(\phi_{-})>0

is a necessary condition to obtain a global solution
\phi
of (
1.4
) that satisfies both (
1.5
) and (
1.6
). Moreover, (
2.10
) is equivalent to:

(2.11)

c<\phi_{-}^{2}\quad\mbox{which implies}\quad\phi_{-}+\phi_{+}>0\,.

Proof.

Suppose that
\phi
is a global solution of (
1.4
) satisfying (
1.5
) and (
1.6
). Then (
2.9
) holds, and taking the limit
\xi\to\infty
yields

H(\phi_{+})-H(\phi_{-})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi^{\prime}(\xi)\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[\phi](\xi)\,d\xi\geq 0.

We observe that
H(\phi)>H(\phi_{-})
for all
\phi\neq\phi_{-}
, and in particular (
2.10
) holds by (
1.10
). The assertion (
2.11
) follows from (

) by elementary computations.
∎

Next we show that if a trajectory
\phi
that satisfies (
1.5
) becomes unbounded, then it cannot oscillate below a certain value:

Lemma 2.5

(Non-oscillatory behaviour)
.

Let
\phi\in C^{3}_{b}(-\infty,0)
be a solution as constructed in Theorem
2.1
(i)-(ii). If the continuation of
\phi
becomes unbounded, then there exists
\xi^{*}\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
such that
\lim_{\xi\to(\xi^{*})^{-}}\phi(\xi)=-\infty
.

Proof.

Since
\phi(\xi)<\phi_{-}
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
and, by assumption,
\phi
is unbounded, there must exists
\xi^{*}\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
such that

\liminf\limits_{\xi\to(\xi^{*})^{-}}{\phi(\xi)}=-\infty
. We have to prove that
\lim\limits_{\xi\to(\xi^{*})^{-}}{\phi(\xi)}=-\infty
.

We argue by contradiction, and assume that
\displaystyle\lim_{\xi\to(\xi^{*})^{-}}{\phi(\xi)}\neq-\infty
and does not exist. Then, by regularity,
\phi
becomes unbounded in an oscillatory fashion as
\xi
increases. This means that there exists a sequence of local minima as follows:
\{\xi^{n}_{min}\}_{n\geq 0}
is increasing and satisfies
\xi^{n}_{min}\to(\xi^{*})^{-}
,
\phi^{\prime}(\xi^{n}_{min})=0
,
\phi^{\prime\prime}(\xi^{n}_{min})>0
and
\phi(\xi^{n}_{min})<-\phi_{-}
for all
n\geq 0
and
\{\phi(\xi^{n}_{min})\}_{n\geq 0}
is a monotone decreasing sequence with
\lim_{n\to\infty}{\phi(\xi^{n}_{min})}=-\infty
.

Observe that then also
h(\phi(\xi_{min}^{n}))<0
for all
n\geq 0
, and this gives

(2.12)

\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[\phi](\xi_{min}^{n})=h(\phi(\xi_{min}^{n}))-\tau\phi^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{min}^{n})<0\quad\text{for all}\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Lemma
2.1
gives a bound for the fractional derivative in terms of
\phi
and its first derivative. Namely, there exists
C_{\alpha}>0
(independent of
\tau
) such that

\left|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[\phi](\xi)\right|\leq C_{\alpha}\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(-\infty,\xi)}^{1-\alpha}\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(-\infty,\xi)}^{\alpha}\ \ \text{for all}\ \xi\in(-\infty,\xi^{*})

and, in particular, for each
\xi=\xi_{min}^{n}
we get the lower bound

(2.13)

0>\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[\phi](\xi_{min}^{n})\geq-C_{\alpha}\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(-\infty,\xi_{min}^{n})}^{1-\alpha}\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(-\infty,\xi_{min}^{n})}^{\alpha}.

On the other hand, considering Lemma
2.2
and that
\phi^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{min}^{n})>0
, we get the upper bound

(2.14)

\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[\phi](\xi_{min}^{n})<h(\phi(\xi_{min}^{n}))<-C_{h}|\phi(\xi_{min}^{n})|^{3}=-C_{h}\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(-\infty,\xi_{min}^{n})}^{3}.

Now, combining (
2.13
) and (
2.14
), we obtain

-C_{\alpha}\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(-\infty,\xi_{min}^{n})}^{1-\alpha}\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(-\infty,\xi_{min}^{n})}^{\alpha}\leq-C_{h}\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(-\infty,\xi_{min}^{n})}^{3}

which is equivalent to

(2.15)

\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(-\infty,\xi_{min}^{n})}^{\alpha+2}\leq\frac{C_{\alpha}}{C_{h}}\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(-\infty,\xi_{min}^{n})}^{\alpha}.

We obtain an upper bound on
\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(-\infty,\xi_{min}^{n})}
using Lemma
2.3
and Lemma
2.2
, as follows:

\frac{\tau}{2}\left(\phi^{\prime}(\xi)\right)^{2}\leq H(\phi(\xi))-H(\phi_{-})\leq C_{H}\phi^{4}(\xi)\leq 2\left(\phi(\xi_{min}^{n}))\right)^{4},\quad\forall\xi\in(-\infty,\xi_{min}^{n})

which implies

(2.16)

\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(-\infty,\xi_{min}^{n})}^{2}\leq\frac{4}{\tau}\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(-\infty,\xi_{min}^{n})}^{4}.

Finally, combining (
2.15
) and (
2.16
) implies for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
that

\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(-\infty,\xi_{min}^{n})}^{2-\alpha}<\tau^{-\alpha/2}C,

with
C=2^{\alpha}\frac{C_{\alpha}}{C_{h}}
. But this contradicts that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\phi(\xi_{min}^{n})=-\infty
and such sequences of local minima cannot exists. Thus it must be that
\lim_{\xi\to(\xi^{*})^{-}}\phi(\xi)=-\infty
.

∎

Next we show that
\xi^{*}
of the previous lemma is a finite value.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\phi\in C^{3}_{b}(-\infty,0)
be a solution as constructed in Theorem
2.1
(i)-(ii). If the continuation of
\phi
becomes unbounded, then there exists a finite
\xi^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(2.17)

\lim_{\xi\to(\xi^{*})^{-}}\phi(\xi)=-\infty,

and, therefore,
\phi
cannot be extended to
\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, the asymptotic behaviour of
\phi
is given by,

(2.18)

\lim_{\xi\to(\xi^{*})^{-}}{\left|\phi(\xi)\right|(\xi^{*}-\xi)}=\sqrt{\tau}C

where
C>0
is a constant independent of
\tau
.

Proof.

Since
\phi
is unbounded, by Lemma
2.5
there exist
\xi^{*}\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
and
\xi_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(2.19)

\displaystyle\lim_{\xi\to(\xi^{*})^{-}}\phi(\xi)=-\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\phi^{\prime}(\xi)<0,\ \forall\xi\in(\xi_{1},\xi^{*}).

It is now convenient to rewrite the equation (
1.4
), as a first order system by making the change of variables

u(\xi)=\phi(\xi)
and
v(\xi)=\phi^{\prime}(\xi)

for
\xi\in(-\infty,\xi^{*})
. This gives:

(2.20)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle{u^{\prime}=v,}\\
\displaystyle{v^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\tau}\left(h(u)-\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[u]\right).}\end{cases}

We first notice that there exist some
\xi_{0}\in[\xi_{1},\xi^{*})
and
C_{v}>0
such that

(2.21)

-\infty<u(\xi)<-\phi_{-}\quad\forall\xi\in[\xi_{0},\xi^{*}),\quad u(\xi_{0})\leq u(\xi)<\phi_{-}\quad\forall\xi\leq\xi_{0}

and

(2.22)

v(\xi)<-C_{v},\quad\forall\xi\in(\xi_{0},\xi^{*}).

The bounds (
2.21
) hold by (
2.19
). Let us show (
2.22
): If
v
becomes unbounded there is nothing to prove, again by (
2.19
). On the other hand, if
v
is bounded, we get an upper bound of
v^{\prime}(\xi)
, which diverges to
-\infty
as
\xi\to(\xi^{*})^{-}
. Indeed, applying Lemma
2.1
, Lemma
2.2
and an estimate like (
2.16
) yield

\begin{split}v^{\prime}(\xi)=\frac{1}{\tau}h(u(\xi))-\frac{1}{\tau}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[u](\xi)&\leq\frac{C_{h}}{\tau}u(\xi)^{3}+\frac{2^{\alpha}C_{\alpha}}{\tau^{1+\alpha/2}}|u(\xi)|^{1+\alpha}\\
&=u(\xi)^{3}\left(\frac{C_{h}}{\tau}-\frac{2^{\alpha}C_{\alpha}}{\tau^{1+\alpha/2}}\frac{1}{|u(\xi)|^{2-\alpha}}\right).\end{split}

The right-hand side of this inequality tends to
-\infty
as
\xi\to(\xi^{*})^{-}
, therefore,
\lim_{\xi\to(\xi^{*})^{-}}v^{\prime}(\xi)=-\infty
. This implies
(\ref{v:bounds})
. We can adjust the value of
\xi_{0}
by taking it closer to
\xi^{*}
as necessary so that both bounds hold in the same interval.

Once (
2.21
) and (
2.22
) are established for
\xi\in(\xi_{0},\xi^{*})
, we introduce the variables

z:=\frac{1}{u}<0\quad\mbox{and}\quad w:=-\frac{v}{u^{2}}\geq 0

in such interval.

We also change the independent variable, in order to absorb
z
in the derivative, as follows:

(2.23)

s=s_{0}-\int_{\xi_{0}}^{\xi}\frac{dy}{z(y)},\quad s_{0}>0.

Notice that
z<0
, thus
s
is strictly increasing with respect to
\xi
.

The system (
2.20
) then reads:

(2.24)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{dz}{ds}=-zw,\\
\displaystyle\frac{dw}{ds}=-2w^{2}+\frac{1}{\tau}\left(h\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)-\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\left[\frac{1}{z}\right]\right)z^{3}.\end{cases}

CASE I:
We first analyse the possibilities of ‘extinction’ and of ‘blow-up’ for
w
at a finite
s
.
Let us assume the former: there exists a finite
\bar{s}>s_{0}
such that
\lim_{s\to\bar{s}}w(s)=0
.
Then, at
\bar{\xi}\leq\xi^{*}
, given by
\bar{s}=s_{0}-\int_{\xi_{0}}^{\bar{\xi}}u(y)dy
, either
v(\bar{\xi})=0
or
\lim_{\xi\to(\bar{\xi})^{-}}u(\xi)=-\infty
. The former contradicts (
2.22
). The latter case implies that either
\bar{s}
is infinite, which gives a contradiction, or that
u
is integrable in
(\xi_{0},\bar{\xi})
. In that case, we have that
\bar{\xi}=\xi^{*}<\infty
, but also that for
\xi\in(\xi_{0},\xi^{*})
there are very small constants
\varepsilon_{1}>0
and
\varepsilon_{2}>0
, such that

(2.25)

\varepsilon_{1}<-\frac{u^{\prime}(\xi)}{u(\xi)^{2}}<\varepsilon_{2}

and integrating gives

\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{2}(\xi-\xi_{0})+1/u(\xi_{0})}<u(\xi)<\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{1}(\xi-\xi_{0})+1/u(\xi_{0})}

so that
\xi^{*}\leq\xi_{0}-\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{u(\xi_{0})}
. The constant
\varepsilon_{1}
tends to
0
as
\xi\to(\xi^{*})^{-}
(because we have only used the definition of
w
and the limit
w\to 0
in the estimate (
2.25
)), implying that
\xi^{*}=\xi_{0}
, but this gives a contradiction, with the choice of
\xi_{0}
. This implies that
w
cannot tend to
0
at a finite value.

Let us assume now that
w
exhibits ‘blow-up’. Then there exists a finite
s^{*}>s_{0}
such that

\lim_{s\to s^{*}}w(s)=\infty
. Thus, for
\xi\in(\xi_{0},\xi^{*})
sufficiently close to
\xi^{*}
,

-\frac{u^{\prime}(\xi)}{u^{2}(\xi)}\gg 1

and this, integrating over
(\xi_{0},\xi)
, gives

u(\xi)<\frac{1}{\xi-\xi_{0}+1/u(\xi_{0})},

but, this implies that
s^{*}
cannot be finite (using the above bound in the integral of (
2.23
) gives
\infty
as lower bound) and
w
does not blow up.

CASE II:
Let us now assume that
w
is defined for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
. We shall show that
\exists\lim_{s\to\infty}w(s)<\infty
, and then apply the definition of the new variables and integrate to get the result. First, we obtain estimates from (
2.24
).

Integrating the first equation in (
2.24
), we get the following:

(2.26)

z(s)=z_{0}e^{-\int_{s_{0}}^{s}w(s)\,ds}\rightarrow 0^{-}\quad\mbox{as}\quad s\to\infty.

This limit is clear if
\int_{s_{0}}^{\infty}w(s)\,ds=+\infty
. If
\int_{s_{0}}^{\infty}w(s)\,ds<+\infty
, in particular
\lim_{s\to\infty}w(s)=0
, then an argument as for the extinction of
w
gives that either
\lim_{s\to\infty}v(s)=0
, which is in contradiction with (
2.22
), or that
\lim_{s\to\infty}u(s)=-\infty
, which also implies that
z(s)\to 0
as
s\to\infty
.

Now, we observe that
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[u]<0
for all
\xi\in(\xi_{0},\xi^{*})
. To prove this we first split
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[u](\xi)
as follows,

\frac{1}{d_{\alpha}}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[u](\xi)=\int_{-\infty}^{\xi_{0}}\frac{v(y)}{(\xi-y)^{\alpha}}\,dy+\int_{\xi_{0}}^{\xi}\frac{v(y)}{(\xi-y)^{\alpha}}\,dy.

The second integral is negative due to (
2.22
). And the first is also negative, since integrating by parts and using (
2.21
) we get:

\begin{split}\int_{-\infty}^{\xi_{0}}\frac{v(y)}{(\xi-y)^{\alpha}}\,dy=&-\alpha\int_{-\infty}^{\xi_{0}}\frac{u(y)}{(\xi-y)^{\alpha+1}}\,dy+\frac{u(\xi_{0})}{(\xi-\xi_{0})^{\alpha}}\\
<&\ \alpha|u(\xi_{0})|\int_{-\infty}^{\xi_{0}}\frac{dy}{(\xi-y)^{\alpha+1}}+\frac{u(\xi_{0})}{(\xi-\xi_{0})^{\alpha}}=0.\end{split}

Let us now get a lower bound for
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[1/z]=\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[u]
by rewriting Lemma
2.1
in terms of
z
and
w
. We have two cases for all
s>s_{1}
for some
s_{1}\geq s_{0}
large enough:

(2.27)

0<-\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\left[\frac{1}{z}\right]\leq D(z,w):=\begin{cases}C^{\prime}_{\alpha}\left|\frac{1}{z}\right|^{1-\alpha},\quad&\mbox{if}\ |v|\ \text{stays bounded},\\
C_{\alpha}\left|\frac{1}{z}\right|^{1+\alpha}(\sup_{s>s_{1}}w)^{\alpha},\quad&\mbox{if}\ |v|\ \text{becomes unbounded},\end{cases}

for some
C^{\prime}_{\alpha}
,
C_{\alpha}>0
.

We now get bounds for the non-linear term using (
2.26
):
Let
s_{2}\geq s_{0}
large enough such that for all
s>s_{2}

|z(s)|<\min\left\{\frac{c}{-(c\phi_{-}-\phi_{-}^{3})},\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\right\},

then

(2.28)

0<\frac{1}{\tau}-\frac{c}{\tau}z^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\tau}z^{3}h\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)=\frac{1}{\tau}-\frac{1}{\tau}(c-(c\phi_{-}-\phi_{-}^{3})z)z^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\tau}\ \mbox{for all}\ s>s_{2}.

With (
2.27
) and (
2.28
) we have the following bounds on
dw/ds
using the second equation in (
2.24
):

(2.29)

\frac{1}{\tau}-\frac{c}{\tau}z^{2}-2w^{2}-\frac{1}{\tau}(-z)^{3}D(z,w)\leq\frac{dw}{ds}\leq\frac{1}{\tau}-2w^{2},\quad\forall s>\max\{s_{1},s_{2}\}.

In order to prove that
w\to C>0
as
s\to\infty
, we now argue by contradiction. First, we assume that
w
becomes unbounded, then (
2.29
) implies that
w
is decreasing as long as
w>1/\sqrt{2\tau}
, but this contradicts that
0<w(s)
becomes unbounded. Now, we assume that the limit is finite with
C=0
, since also
\lim_{s\to\infty}z(s)=0
, these implies that
dw(s)/ds>0
for all
s
large enough. This contradicts that
C=0
and
w(s)>0
.

The last possibility that we have to exclude is that
w
oscillates without limit. Let
M=\sup_{s>\max\{s_{1},s_{2}\}}w(s)<\infty
, then using (
2.29
) we get there exists
C>0
and
s_{3}\geq\max\{s_{1},s_{2}\}
such that

(2.30)

\frac{1}{\tau}-2w(s)^{2}-C|z(s)|^{2-\alpha}\leq\frac{dw(s)}{ds}\leq\frac{1}{\tau}-2w(s)^{2}\quad\mbox{for all}\quad s>s_{3}.

Here we have used (
2.27
) and noticed that for
|z|
small enough we have
|z|^{2+\alpha}
,
|z|^{2}<|z|^{2-\alpha}
.

Observe that if
M<1/\sqrt{2\tau}
, no oscillations are possible in the limit,
because there exists
s_{4}\geq s_{3}
such that

0<\frac{1}{\tau}-2M^{2}-C|z(s)|^{2-\alpha}\leq\frac{dw(s)}{ds}\quad\text{for all}\quad s>s_{4}.

Then, in this case, we obtain the desired result:
\lim_{s\to\infty}w(s)=M>0
and is finite.

Now, if
M=1/\sqrt{2\tau}
, there exists
s\in\mathbb{R}
such that
w(s)<1/\sqrt{2\tau}
, because we are assuming that the limit of
w
does not exist. If
M>1/\sqrt{2\tau}
, then by (
2.30
), with
s>s_{3}
,

\frac{dw(s)}{ds}\leq\frac{1}{\tau}-2w(s)^{2}\leq 0,\quad\text{as long as}\quad\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\tau}}\leq w(s)\leq M.

This means that on the intervals of
s
for which
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\tau}}\leq w(s)\leq M
,
w
is not increasing, so
w
cannot oscillate in this range. This implies that
w(s)\in(0,1/\sqrt{2\tau}]
, for all
s>s_{4}
with
s_{4}>s_{3}
large enough, oscillating without limit.

Then, there exists also an increasing sequence
\{\overline{s}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}>s_{4}
, where local minima of
w
are attained, with
dw(\overline{s}_{n})/ds=0
,
0<w(\overline{s}_{n})\leq 1/\sqrt{2\tau}
and
\overline{s}_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Let the sequence
\{\delta_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be defined by evaluating the right-hand side of (

) at each

,

0<\delta_{n}:=\frac{1}{\tau}-2w^{2}(\overline{s}_{n})<\frac{1}{\tau}.

Again there are two possibilities: either
\{\delta_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is bounded from below by a positive constant
K
, or
\delta_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. In the former case, we get from (
2.30
) that for all

,

K-C|z(\overline{s}_{n})|^{2-\alpha}\leq\frac{dw}{ds}(\overline{s}_{n})=0.

Applying (
2.26
) as
n\to\infty
, we deduce that there exists
n_{0}\geq 0
such that the left hand side is strictly positive
for all
n\geq n_{0}
, a contradiction.

If
\delta_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, then
w(\overline{s}_{n})\to 1/\sqrt{2\tau}
as
n\to\infty
. This means that this sequence of local minima is converging to the supremum of
w
on

, but this contradicts that
w
oscillates without a limit and, moreover, this implies that
\lim_{s\to\infty}w(s)=1/\sqrt{2\tau}
.

With the limit of
w
and taking into account its regularity, we get that there exists positive constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
proportional to

, such that for all
\xi>\xi_{0}

C_{1}<-\frac{u^{\prime}(\xi)}{u^{2}(\xi)}<C_{2}.

Then, integrating over the interval
(\xi_{0},\xi)
for
\xi<\xi^{*}

Now, we take
R
depending on
\eta
, once that
\tau
and
\delta
(taken as small as necessary) are fixed:

R(\eta)=(\sigma\eta)^{\alpha/2},\quad\text{for}\quad\sigma\in(0,1).

We take
\sigma
such that
R(\eta)\leq 1
. In particular for each
\eta
we have

(4.33)

\sigma<\eta^{-1}.

Then we can say that

\frac{1}{R^{\alpha}}\leq\left(\frac{1}{R^{\alpha}}\right)^{2/\alpha}=\frac{1}{R^{2}}.

The previous estimate and inequality (
4.32
) applied to (
4.31
) yield, for some order one constants,

(4.34)

\Phi_{\tau}(\eta)\leq\delta v(\eta)+\frac{R^{2}}{2\tau}\frac{C_{1}}{(\eta-R)^{\alpha+1}}+\frac{R^{2}}{2\tau}\frac{C_{2}|\Phi_{\tau}(\eta-R)|}{(\eta-R)^{\alpha}}+\frac{C_{3}}{\alpha}\frac{1}{R^{2}}-\frac{C_{4}}{\alpha}\frac{1}{\eta^{\alpha}}.

Therefore, we can deduce the following upper bound from (
4.34
) and using (
4.33
), where the worst case scenario is

\left(1-\frac{C_{2}}{2\tau}\sigma^{\alpha}\right)\Phi_{\tau}(\eta)\leq\frac{C_{5}}{\eta^{\alpha}},

for some
C_{5}>0
of order one, and, therefore, it is sufficient to take
\sigma
small enough such that

(4.35)

\sigma<\tau^{1/\alpha}\,.

Since, we can choose
\delta=\Phi_{\tau}(0^{+})
arbitrarily small once
\tau
is fixed and we have (
4.30
), we can conclude that for
1\ll\eta\ll\delta^{-2/\alpha}
(large enough but in this range), we can take
\sigma
satisfying (
4.33
) and (
4.35
), then, there exists
C>0
such that

(4.36)

\Phi_{\tau}(\eta)\leq\frac{C}{\eta^{\alpha}}\quad\mbox{for}\quad 1\ll\eta\ll\delta^{-2/\alpha}.

Finally, taking the limit
\xi_{0}\to\infty
implies that
\delta\to 0
so we can increase the range of
\eta
in the limit and we obtain the result.

Appendix A
Continuous dependence on
\tau

In this section, we prove the continuous dependence on the parameter
\tau
using the general theory for functional differential equations, e.g. see

[
15
, §2]
and
[
24
]
. After rewriting the problem as a functional differential equation, we check that the necessary hypotheses are fulfilled in order to apply the auxiliary lemmas and the continuous dependence result from
[
15
, §2]
.

First, we rewrite (
1.4
) as a system of first-order delay functional differential equations

\begin{cases}\phi^{\prime}&=\psi,\\
\psi^{\prime}&=\frac{1}{\tau}h(\phi)-\frac{d_{\alpha}}{\tau}\displaystyle{\int_{-\infty}^{\xi}\frac{\psi(y)}{(\xi-y)^{\alpha}}\,dy.}\end{cases}

In order to study the continuous dependence of solutions on
\tau
, we add
\tau
as an independent variable.
However, it is easier to consider instead of
\tau>0
its inverse
\nu:=1/\tau
such that the augmented system of first-order differential equations reads

(A.1)

\begin{cases}\phi^{\prime}&=\psi,\\
\psi^{\prime}&=\nu h(\phi)-\nu\ d_{\alpha}\int_{-\infty}^{\xi}\frac{\psi(y)}{(\xi-y)^{\alpha}}\,dy,\\
\nu^{\prime}&=0.\end{cases}

In order to frame this system as one of functional differential equations with finite delay, we split the integral term at
-r
for some
r>0
:

\int_{-\infty}^{\xi}\frac{\psi(y)}{(\xi-y)^{\alpha}}\,dy=\int_{-\infty}^{-r}\frac{\psi(y)}{(\xi-y)^{\alpha}}\,dy+\int_{-r}^{\xi}\frac{\psi(y)}{(\xi-y)^{\alpha}}\,dy.

The first one is treated as a known function of
\xi
as long as
\xi\geq-r
, while the second denotes the fractional derivative from
-r
and one needs the initial data to be given on
(-r,0)
. Without loss of generality, we consider the function
\phi(\xi-r)
to be given for
\xi\in(-\infty,0)
and split the fractional derivative in two parts as before and applying the change of variable

we get:

(A.2)

\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}[\phi]=d_{\alpha}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{\phi^{\prime}(y^{\prime}-r)}{(\xi+r-y^{\prime})^{\alpha}}\,dy^{\prime}+d_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\xi+r}\frac{\phi^{\prime}(y^{\prime}-r)}{(\xi+r-y^{\prime})^{\alpha}}\,dy^{\prime}=W(\xi)+\mathcal{D}_{-r}^{\alpha}[\phi](\xi),

where
W(\xi):=d_{\alpha}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{\phi^{\prime}(y^{\prime}-r)}{(\xi+r-y^{\prime})^{\alpha}}\,dy^{\prime}
is a given function.
Using (
A.2
), system (
A.1
) can be written as

(A.3)

\begin{cases}\phi^{\prime}&=\psi,\\
\psi^{\prime}&=\nu h(\phi)-\nu\ d_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\xi+r}\frac{\psi(y^{\prime}-r)}{(\xi+r-y^{\prime})^{\alpha}}\,dy^{\prime}-\nu W(\xi),\\
\nu^{\prime}&=0,\end{cases}

for all
\xi\geq-r
.
The first and the third equations are ordinary differential equations, whereas the second one is an integro-differential equation with finite delay in the integral term.
Following the notation of
[
15
, §2]
, equation (

) is a functional differential equation of the form

\dot{x}=F(\xi,x_{\xi})

In our particular case,

, and

\displaystyle F_{1}\left(\xi,(\phi,\psi,\nu)\right)

\displaystyle=\psi,

(A.6)

\displaystyle F_{2}\left(\xi,(\phi,\psi,\nu)\right)

\displaystyle=\nu h(\phi)-\nu\ d_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\xi+r}\frac{\psi(y^{\prime}-r)}{(\xi+r-y^{\prime})^{\alpha}}\,dy^{\prime}-\nu W(\xi),

(A.7)

\displaystyle F_{3}\left(\xi,(\phi,\psi,\nu)\right)

We recall

Finally, we consider the delay functional differential equation (

As it is mentioned previously, we only need the vector

In this appendix we consider the linear inhomogeneous equation:

(C.1)

\tau\psi^{\prime\prime}+\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{0}[\psi]+a\psi=Q(\eta)\,,\quad\mbox{with}\quad a>0,

(here
{}^{\prime}=\frac{d}{d\eta}

\psi(0^{+})=C_{0}\,,\quad\psi^{\prime}(0^{+})=C_{1}\,.

We recall the derivation of a solution via the Laplace transform see e.g.
[

]

Applying the Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}
to (
C.1
)–(
C.2

(C.3)

\mathcal{L}(\psi)(s)=\frac{1}{\tau s^{2}+s^{\alpha}+a}\left(\mathcal{L}(Q)(s)+(\tau s+s^{\alpha-1})\psi(0^{+})+\tau\psi^{\prime}(0^{+})\right)\,,

where
\mathcal{L}(f)(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s\eta}f(\eta)d\eta
.
Using
\mathcal{L}(f\ast g)(s)=\mathcal{L}(f)(s)\,\mathcal{L}(g)(s)
, we deduce

\begin{split}\psi=\psi(0^{+})\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left(\frac{\tau s+s^{\alpha-1}}{\tau s^{2}+s^{\alpha}+a}\right)&+\tau\psi^{\prime}(0^{+})\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\tau s^{2}+s^{\alpha}+a}\right)\\
&+\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\tau s^{2}+s^{\alpha}+a}\right)\ast Q\,.\end{split}

Define

(C.4)

Observing that
\lim_{\eta\to 0^{+}}v(\eta)=\lim_{s\to\infty}s\tilde{v}(s)=1
and

\frac{1}{\tau s^{2}+s^{\alpha}+a}=\frac{1}{a}(1-s\tilde{v}(s))=-\frac{1}{a}\big(s\mathcal{L}(v)(s)-v(0^{+})\big)

implies

\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\tau s^{2}+s^{\alpha}+a}\right)(\eta)=-\frac{1}{a}v^{\prime}(\eta)\,.

Consequently,

(C.5)

\lim_{\eta\to 0^{+}}v^{\prime}(\eta)=0\,.

Writing the expression for

(C.6)

\psi(\eta)=\psi(0^{+})v(\eta)-\frac{\tau}{a}\psi^{\prime}(0^{+})v^{\prime}(\eta)-\frac{1}{a}\int_{0}^{\eta}v^{\prime}(y)Q(\eta-y)\,\,dy\,.

(C.8)

Br:=\{s:\ \mbox{Re}(s)=\sigma\geq 1\ \&\ \mbox{Im}(s)\in(-\infty,\infty)\}.

Moreover, we restrict to the principal representation of

, namely, here
\mbox{arg}(s)\in(-\pi,\pi]

[
14
]
and
[
8
]

and denoting by

While the asymptotic behaviour at

Therefore, we get the desired asymptotic behaviour by the Taylor series expansion formula at the origin.
