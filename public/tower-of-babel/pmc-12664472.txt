Escherichia coli K1 meningitis concomitant with septic arthritis in an adult: a rare case and mini-review of the literature
Authors: Marie Tré-Hardy, Sophie Collignon, Andrea Della Vecchia, Mikael Leys, Roberto Cupaiolo, Ingrid Beukinga, Denis Piérard, Florence Crombé, Laurent Blairon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMeningitis caused by Escherichia coli K1 occurs only in specific cases: in neonates or in adults following trauma or in a nosocomial context resulting from procedures that create a direct entry point, such as neurosurgical procedures. Community-acquired cases in adults are rare, and those secondary to septic arthritis are even rarer.In this article, an exceptionally uncommon case of community-acquired meningitis due to Escherichia coli O25:K1:H4 (sequence type 95) with concomitant septic arthritis at the L4/L5 level is described, emphasizing the diagnostic process, which was aided by detailed microbiological documentation.Case presentationA 73-year-old man with a history of hypertension, alcohol use, and atrial fibrillation presented with acute lower back pain, confusion, persistent vomiting, headache, lethargy, and low-grade fever (37.9 °C). He also demonstrated a positive Lasegue's sign and had elevated inflammatory markers without an obvious infectious source. The patient experienced a favorable outcome with no neurological sequelae after receiving treatment with ceftriaxone 2g every 12 h, initiated from the first day of arrival at the emergency department.ConclusionsChallenging but achievable, the diagnosis was made by integrating clinical history, microbiological findings, and radiological results. In particular, multiplex PCR played a crucial role in the prompt diagnosis of Escherichia coli K1 meningitis, while also influencing the management and outcome of the patient.Keywords: Escherichia coli K1, Meningitidis, Adults, Septic arthritis

Introduction

Escherichia coli (E. coli) is not typically among the microorganisms commonly responsible for septic arthritis or meningitis. Staphylococcus aureus, followed by other Gram-positive bacteria such as streptococci, are the most common causes of septic arthritis [1]. Concerning meningitis, Streptococcus pneumoniae and Neisseria meningitidis are responsible for over 80 % of cases in immunocompetent children and adults [2,3]. However, in neonatal bacterial meningitis [4] or those occurring in adults in a nosocomial, traumatic, or iatrogenic context [5] E. coli represents one of the most common pathogens, particularly strains containing the K1 polysaccharides. In contrast, E. coli is an infrequent etiological agent in community-acquired adult meningitis, with an average incidence of one reported case of E. coli meningitis per year globally [6]. Finally, meningitis due to E. coli with concomitant septic arthritis is even rarer, with only four cases documented in prospective and retrospective reviews, but not fully described [[6], [7], [8]].

This article presents, for the first time, an unusual clinical case of community-acquired meningitis caused by E. coli K1 with concomitant septic arthritis at the L4/L5 level. It includes comprehensive microbiological documentation and a clinical description that outlines the diagnostic challenges posed by this case.

Case report

A 73-year-old patient brought in by the mobile emergency and resuscitation service presented with headaches, vomiting, acute lower back pain, significant fatigue, confusion, and a deterioration in general condition upon admission to the emergency department. Six days prior, during a musical concert, the patient reported a sudden onset of acute lower back pain with slight radiation into the right leg, without any other clear complaints. This lumbar pain was initially classified as mechanical in origin and was attributed by the patient to prolonged sitting in an uncomfortable position. There was no recent history of falls, head trauma, or recent travel. His medical history revealed hypertension, esophagitis, significant alcohol consumption, an ischemic stroke seven years ago treated with anticoagulants, atrial fibrillation, and otoliths with episodes of vertigo. The physical examination showed that the patient was normocolored and had no skin lesions. However, he was dehydrated, tachypneic, and appeared exhausted. The pulmonary and abdominal examinations revealed nothing noteworthy. Cardiac auscultation revealed an irregularly irregular heart rhythm consistent with atrial fibrillation; heart sounds (S1 and S2) were present without murmurs. Jugular veins were distended. The patient was in rapid atrial fibrillation. Neurologically, the Glasgow Coma Scale was assessed at E4V5M6. The patient did not exhibit neck stiffness, photophobia, Kernig's sign, or Brudzinski's sign. The pupils were isochoric and symmetrical, and the examination of cranial nerves was unremarkable. The Brudzinski maneuver was successful, there were no Babinski signs, but the patient was unable to perform the Mingazzini test, and the Lasegue sign was positive. Bilateral lower limb edema was also observed. The vital signs recorded upon admission showed a blood pressure of 134/86 mmHg, a heart rate of 116 beats per minute, oxygen saturation at 93 %, and a temperature of 37.9 °C. A lumbar puncture (LP) was performed, along with blood culture samples. The patient was then transferred to the intensive care unit. Laboratory test results revealed the following: white blood cell count 10,190/μL (normal range: 4000–10,000), with differential counts of neutrophils (80 %); hemoglobin level at 13.0 g/dL; platelet count 129,000/μL (normal range: 150,000–400,000/μL); c-reactive protein level at 352 mg/L; creatine phosphokinase at 239 UI/L (normal value < 190 UI/L); blood glucose level at 177 mg/dL and GFR at 43 mL/min/1.73 m2. On lumbar puncture, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) was hemorrhagic. There were 920 red blood cells/mm3 and 1100 nucleated cells/mm3 in the CSF, with a predominance of neutrophils (73 %), 8 % lymphocytes, and 19 % monocytes, a reduced glucose level at 43 mg/dL (normal range for normal blood glucose: 45–80 mg/dL), elevated lactates at 8.7 mmol/L (normal range: 1.1–2.4 mmol/L), and a high protein level at 3.034 g/L (normal range: 0.15–0.45 g/L). Isoelectric focusing of the CSF was performed and revealed a marked disruption of the blood–brain barrier with no evidence of intrathecal immunoglobulin synthesis. A multiplex test using the QIAstat-Dx® Meningitidis/Encephalitis panel (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) was conducted on the CSF, allowing for a comprehensive search for the most commonly encountered pathogens in meningitis: E. coli K1, Haemophilus influenzae, Listeria monocytogenes, Neisseria meningitidis, Streptococcus agalactiae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, HSV1, HSV2, HHV6, human parechovirus, varicella zoster virus and Cryptococcus neoformans/gattii. The E. coli K1 target was positive with a cycle threshold value of 29. While the microscopic examination of the cytospin® of the CSF after Gram staining and direct cultures of the CSF on standard culture media (Columbia with 5 % sheep blood, chocolate agar PolyViteX (BioMérieux), and Schaedler agar) turned out to be negative, only the culture of the CSF enrichment broth yielded, after 4 days of incubation, a single colony that was identified by MALDI-TOF MS (Bruker, Billerica, Massachusetts, USA) as E. coli. To validate these recent findings and to further strengthen the clinical implication of E. coli in the occurrence of meningitis, a new PCR analysis using another multiplex, the FilmArray Meningitis/Encephalitis® Panel (BioFire Diagnostics, Utah, USA, owned by bioMérieux), which detects the same bacterial targets as the QIAstat-Dx® panel, was performed on the cultured microorganism. The obtained result confirmed that it was indeed an E. coli K1. An antibiogram was generated by Vitek2 (bioMérieux, Nürtingen, Germany) and showed that the isolate was susceptible to ampicillin, cefotaxime, ceftazidime, cefepime, gentamicin, and ciprofloxacin.

Whole genome sequencing was performed, at the Brussels Interuƒniversity Genomics High Throughput core (www.brightcore.be), using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 Sequencing System to generate 2 x 150 bp paired-end reads. The raw reads were uploaded and de novo assembled in BioNumerics v.8.1 (Applied Maths, bioMérieux, Belgium). The isolated strain was characterized as E. coli O25:K1:H4 and classified as extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli (ExpEC)/uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC) based on the detection of specific genes (ExPEC: iutA and papC; UPEC: chuA, fyuA and yfcV) by means of the E. coli genotyping tool (BioNumerics v.8.1, Applied Maths, bioMérieux, Belgium). The list of virulence-associated genes, including the gene kpsMII_K1 encoding for the K1 capsule antigen characteristic for neonatal meningitis E. coli (NMEC), is presented in Table 1. As expected, no antimicrobial resistances genes were identified. The raw reads were also uploaded and automatically assembled in the public genome database EnteroBase. The isolate belonged to multilocus sequence type 95 (ST95). Using hierarchical clustering of core genome multilocus sequence typing (HierCC) [9], it was shown that the isolate genome belonged to HC50_1104, that includes genomes of isolates from humans and animals circulating worldwide. The studied isolate genome differed only by 10 alleles from an isolate genome from 2021 deposited in the EnteroBase database by the French National Reference Centre of E. coli.

The day after the patient's admission, thoracic, abdominal, and cerebral CT scans revealed nothing remarkable. However, the contrast-enhanced lumbar CT scan showed circumferential disc protrusion at L4-L5 and L3-L4, in contact with the dural sheath, as well as L5-S1 discopathy with osteophytosis and gas degeneration. On that day, two blood culture bottles tested positive, and Gram-negative bacilli were observed upon direct examination. At that point, the highlighted diagnosis was Gram-negative bacillus sepsis with meningitis and possible underlying spondylodiscitis. A lumbar MRI was performed two days after hospital arrival, confirming facet joint arthritis at the L4/L5 level bilaterally, involving the articular facets and predominantly affecting the right side with infection extension into the L3-L5 epidural space. The diagnosis of spondylodiscitis was then ruled out (Fig. 1). E. coli was identified in all blood culture bottles with an antibiogram profile identical to that of the bacterium isolated from the CSF.

He received antibiotic therapy with ceftriaxone 2g twice a day for 21 days. This was continued after obtaining the antibiogram results. Aciclovir was discontinued upon knowledge of the PCR meningitis panel result.

The patient's cognitive condition rapidly improved. After 3 days of intensive care hospitalization, he was transferred to a non-monitored care unit. Five days after the initiation of antibiotic therapy, the patient became afebrile, but lumbar pain remained prominent and took a few more days to go away. One month after arriving at the emergency department, the patient was discharged from the hospital without any neurological sequelae.

Discussion

Comprehensive microbiological documentation

The classical cytological and microbiological analysis of the CSF faced a series of difficulties. The direct examination of the CSF turned out negative, despite careful microscopic readings by 2 different operators. The bacterial culture was also challenging and took 4 days to yield positive results. In their study on E. coli meningitis, Pomar et al., [10] also highlight the low sensitivity of the direct examination, estimated at 38 % (n = 15), while the culture was positive in 97 % of cases (n = 39), but without specifying the time required to obtain a positive culture. Bacterial contamination must be considered, as multiple studies have shown that the time to CSF culture positivity is significantly shorter for true bacterial pathogens than for contaminants [11,12].

In our case, the CSF was hemorrhagic and therefore diluted, which may have reduced the sensitivity of bacterial culture. Additionally, growth inhibitors naturally present in blood could have interfered with culture yield. Furthermore, PCR results provide supportive evidence: the cycle threshold (Ct) value was 29, reflecting a significant bacterial DNA load and thus supporting the likelihood of infection. Cytology was also challenging to interpret as the sample was hemorrhagic. While cytological analysis of the CSF was of limited diagnostic value, biochemical studies revealed a significant hypoglycorrhachia at 43 mg/dL (given the patient's blood glucose level of 177 mg/dL), along with elevated protein and lactate levels consistent with bacterial meningitis. Moreover, despite being nonspecific, the marked disruption of the blood–brain barrier without evidence of intrathecal immunoglobulin synthesis suggested an acute process compatible with meningitis. Although classical microbiological analysis, including a direct examination associated with culture, remains the gold standard, the utility of multiplex PCR is now widely recognized. Multiplex PCR played a crucial role in the speed of diagnosing meningitis in this patient. The rapid identification of the causative agent of meningitis also plays a critical role in patient management and outcome [13,14] and the clinical (particularly neurological) improvement following appropriate antibiotic therapy further supports the diagnosis of a true infection rather than contamination. Finally, the LP was performed prior to any administration of antibiotics at L4–L5 on intact skin, with no signs of redness or swelling. There was no evidence that the LP was traumatic or that contamination of the CSF could have occurred.

The multidrug sensitivity of this bacterium is striking and contrasts with the increasingly described resistances to conventional antibiotics, which pose a growing challenge for the treatment of patients with septic arthritis [15]. In cases of E. coli meningitis described in adults, the emergence of multi-resistant profiles is also concerning [6].

Whole genome sequencing analysis enabled us to understand this striking result. Indeed, the studied E. coli, with predicted serotype O25:K1:H4 belonged to ST95, one of the major global ExPEC lineages causing bloodstream infection, urinary tract infection and neonatal meningitis in humans [16]. On basis of the presence of kpsMII_K1, coding for K1 capsule polysaccharide, it can be assigned to the NMEC pathotype. However, virulence factors of the three other pathotypes of ExPEC, i.e. UPEC, sepsis-associated E. coli (SEPEC), and avian pathogenic E. coli (APEC), were detected as well [17]. In contrast to other ExPEC lineages, ST95 strains are largely antibiotic susceptible [16].

Looking at the related genomes available in EnteroBase, the studied isolate genome was dispersed among other HC50_1104 isolate genomes from the rest of the world; however, only one additional isolate genome fell into HC20_232306. The latter was isolated from a case of neonatal meningitis in France in 2021 (Bonacorsi S., French National Reference Centre E. coli).

A challenging diagnosis

This challenging diagnosis is partly attributed to nonspecific symptoms that are present in various differential diagnoses. Additionally, the rarity of the case may contribute to it not being initially considered.

In line with previous reports [18,19], this clinical case confirms that diagnosing bacterial meningitis in patients over 65 may be challenging as the clinical presentation is often incomplete and classical meningeal signs (Kernig, Brudzinski) lack sensitivity in adults. Another study specifies that patients with gram-negative bacillary meningitis (GNBM) show lower frequencies of the classic meningeal triad, which includes fever, neck stiffness, and altered mental status, as well as lower frequencies of headache and rash, when compared to those with non-GNBM [10]. Another review showed that clinical presentations often included altered mental status in only 50 % of patients, and neck stiffness in 41.9 % [20]. The triggering factor that prompted a LP was the presence of fever, confusion, headaches, and vomiting persisting for 2 days.

As for the symptoms of septic arthritis, the sudden acute lower back pain experienced by the patient covers a broad spectrum of differential diagnoses, including other infectious causes (spondylodiscitis, vertebral osteomyelitis, etc.), mechanical or traumatic causes, neoplastic causes, and inflammatory or degenerative causes. Among these, septic arthritis remains a medical emergency associated with high morbidity and mortality. [21]. According to Newman et al. [22], a case definition of septic arthritis is based on one of the following four criteria documented in this clinical case: the isolation of E. coli clearly considered a pathogenic organism from a source other than the affected joint in the context of acute joint pain with sepsis. The MRI also played a significant role in the diagnosis. The described primary infection site in this clinical case at the L4/L5 level bilaterally with involvement of the articular facets is also unusual, as typically, large joints (especially in the legs) are affected in septic arthritis. Only one case of E. coli meningitis with cervical spondylodiscitis at C2-C3 down to C4-C5 has been reported [23]. Another recent case also reported E. coli meningitis but with involvement of T4/T5 vertebral bodies and coexistence of intervertebral disc involvement [24].

Although present discreetly in our patient, fever (37.9 °C) remains a sometimes-misleading symptom and is not a prerequisite for diagnosing septic arthritis or meningitis. In various retrospective studies, fever (>37.5 °C) at presentation is detected in only about 60 % of cases of septic arthritis [21] and in 37.8 % [8] or in 65.3 % [20] of cases of E. coli meningitis.

Finally, it is important to note that this clinical case remains exceptional, with a favorable outcome for the patient. Other studies have highlighted the severity of community-acquired E. coli K1 meningitis in adults, a severe illness associated with a high mortality rate estimated between 50 % and 90 %, linked to systemic complications [6,25,26].

Furthermore, following the changes in microbiological epidemiology observed since the COVID-19 pandemic, particular attention should be given to the possible cases of meningitis in adults, the incidence of which appears to be increasing [27,28].

To our knowledge, as of now, only 4 cases of E. coli meningitis with concomitant septic arthritis have been documented in prospective and retrospective studies[[6], [7], [8]], but without clinical and microbiological descriptions.

Predisposing factors

Among the known risk factors, previous joint pathology, especially rheumatoid arthritis, appears to predispose to the development of septic arthritis. Skin infection also represents a significant risk factor. These factors were not present in the patient. Although individuals of all ages can be affected, cases of septic arthritis typically manifest in the elderly and very young children. Despite the limited studies on E. coli meningitis in adults [6,8,20,[29], [30], [31], [32]], a well-established commonality, also present in our patient, is the association between advanced age (73 years) and the presence of comorbidities such as alcohol abuse and heart failure, which influence the characteristics and outcome of the disease.

Pathogenesis

Regarding the origin of the meningeal infection, several hypotheses must be considered: either by direct inoculation of the pathogenic organism following trauma or a medical procedure, or by hematogenous spread. 75 % of the documented cases of spontaneous E. coli meningitis in adults [10,31] occurred secondary to a distant or contiguous infection (e.g., urinary tract infection, gastrointestinal infection, or otitis media). Bacterial meningitis is caused either by direct extension of a contiguous focus or in consequence of bacteremia [33]. In our case, meningitis and septic arthritis are likely the result of hematogenous spread, with the exact entry point of the infection remaining undetermined. The absence of a skin lesion observed at the site of infection rules out the hypothesis of entry through cutaneous perforation. No other primary focus has been identified, even with PET/CT imaging.

Mini-review of the literature

To our knowledge, only four cases of E. coli meningitis concomitant with septic arthritis in adults have been reported in the literature[[6], [7], [8]]. These publications, which include both prospective and retrospective studies, offer valuable insights into statistical and epidemiological trends. However, they lack detailed clinical descriptions of the individual cases, such as age, gender, septic arthritis localization, associated comorbidities, and outcomes. Such data are essential to confirm that these rare cases predominantly occur in elderly, often immunocompromised patients with associated comorbidities and a likely high risk of mortality. Moreover, none of these studies provided whole-genome sequencing analyses of the implicated bacterial strains. This information is critical for identifying virulence genes and establishing connections with other cases recorded worldwide through EnteroBase.

Through this case, we illustrate a novel localization of community-acquired E. coli K1 meningitis with concomitant septic arthritis at the L4/L5 level, accompanied by genomic characterization. Our case highlights the diagnostic challenges posed by such unusual presentations, which require a multidisciplinary approach combining clinical, microbiological, and radiological data.
