Green and comparative route to 6-(pyrrolidin-1-yl)-thiazolo [4,5-d] pyrimidines using novel thiazole-based Pd(II) catalysts
Authors: Mahmoud Abd El Aleem Ali Ali El-Remaily, Thomas Nady A Eskander, Abdullah Yahya Abdullah Alzahrani, Mohammed H AL Mughram, Omar M Elhady, Ahmed M Abu-Dief
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThree innovative Pd(II) complexes were synthesized through the reaction of Pd(II)ions with thiazole ligands: BZU, BIU, and BTU. These complexes, along with their ligands, were characterized using a diverse set of analytical methods, including infrared spectroscopy, 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction, UV-visible spectroscopy, CHN elemental analysis, mass spectrometry, thermogravimetric analysis, and magnetic susceptibility tests. The results indicated that the structures of BZU-Pd, BIU-Pd, and BTU-Pd complexes are square planar. To explore their catalytic properties, the thiazole-based complexes were tested in a four-component condensation reaction under mild and eco-friendly conditions. The study systematically assessed the effects of various catalyst types, such asLewisacids, bases, and ionic liquids, as well as the influence of solvent choice and catalyst concentration. Optimal conditions revealed that all catalysts exhibited notable catalytic effectiveness, with high turnover frequency (TOF) values showcasing their ability to efficiently produce 6-(pyrrolidin-1-yl)-[1,5-a] thiazolo [4,5-d] pyrimidine derivatives. Moreover, the three catalysts retained their efficiency throughout the reaction, with the first catalyst maintaining its effectiveness for eight cycles, while the other two catalysts preserved their efficiency for six cycles each. This method, characterized by its simplicity, safety, stability of the catalysts, rapid reaction times, and high product yields, presents significant promise for future applications in industry.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25519-x.Keywords: Availability of components, Environmentally, High yields, Quick reaction times, Safety, StabilitySubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

Heterocyclic ligands containing both nitrogen and sulfur atoms, particularly those with multiple donor sites, exhibit a remarkable ability to coordinate with a wide range of transition metal ions. This property has attracted significant attention due to their potential applications in the design of biomimetic and bioactive coordination compounds1–4. Among these ligands, thiazoles are of particular importance, as they are electron-rich and possess multiple functional groups, making them especially valuable for constructing diverse metal complexes5,6. The structural features of thiazole-based ligands enable them to form stable and highly active catalysts by tightly binding to transition metals. In synthetic chemistry, the development of efficient and selective catalysts remains a central goal. Designing new catalytic methods to activate organic molecules is a fundamental challenge in organic synthesis. Catalysis is not only a core discipline of organic synthesis but also a key principle of sustainable chemistry, since it enables the production of large quantities of target compounds in shorter times and with reduced waste7–16. The advancement of catalytic systems plays an essential role in achieving green chemistry objectives and in promoting circular economy strategies. Meeting these challenges requires collaboration between chemists and engineers to develop catalytic processes that are both efficient and sustainable, thereby stimulating chemical innovation and providing competitive advantages. Transition metal complexes, due to their high catalytic activity and versatility, have been extensively employed in multicomponent reactions (MCRs). These reactions are powerful synthetic tools, as they allow multiple reactants to combine in a single step to form complex organic molecules17–20. Transition metal complexes can catalyze MCRs through various pathways, including oxidative addition, Lewis acid activation, and stereoselective control of reactants21–24. Such pathways contribute to improved yields, enhanced selectivity, and faster reaction rates. Their ability to function as both Lewis acids and bases makes transition metal complexes excellent catalysts in MCRs25,26. This dual functionality allows them to activate and coordinate several reactants simultaneously, thereby enabling the efficient synthesis of a wide variety of organic compounds. Among transition metal catalysts, palladium-based complexes are particularly significant. Palladium catalysts coordinated with nitrogen-donor ligands serve as excellent candidates due to their enhanced stability and reactivity. Well-designed N-donor ligands have been widely employed in palladium complexes, enabling their application in a broad spectrum of catalytic investigations. Palladium complexes are widely recognized for their excellent catalytic efficiency, stability, and versatility, along with their essential role in biological processes, making them more attractive frameworks compared to other transition metals. In the present study, we investigate in detail the design and application of novel Pd(II) complexes as catalysts for green one-pot multicomponent reactions (MCRs) involving aromatic aldehydes, rhodanine, pyrrolidine, and 5-aminotetrazole. These catalytic systems offer several advantages, including the use of a green solvent system (H2O/ethanol) that aligns with the principles of sustainable chemistry, faster reaction times, and high product yields27–32. Additionally, the recycling and reusability of the catalysts were explored, highlighting their potential for industrial applications. The ability to recover and reuse the catalysts not only enhances process efficiency and cost-effectiveness but also supports the principles of green chemistry by minimizing waste generation and promoting environmentally sustainable practices.

The IR spectra of these complexes typically feature distinctive absorption bands tied to metal-ligand vibrations, which stem from the stretching and bending movements of bonds linking the metal ion to its ligands. These bands’ frequencies and intensities are shaped by various factors, including the type of metal ion, the coordination environment, and the properties of the ligands involved. Magnetic moment

The determination of magnetic moments in metal complexes provides key insights into the electronic configuration and magnetic properties of the central metal ion and its surrounding ligands. This measurement acts as a valuable tool for interpreting electronic structure, spin states, and magnetic features, which enhances our understanding of these complexes’ behavior in various chemical and physical environments. In the current study, the experimentally obtained magnetic susceptibility values showed close agreement with theoretical predictions, calculated using the equation μeff=[4 S(S+l)]0.5, under the assumption of a non-interacting spin system. The recorded magnetic moment values for the BZU-Pd, BIU-Pd, and BTU-Pd complexes have no magnetic moment as it is close to zero (0.07-0.12) which indicates that the diamagnetism of the Pd(II) complex, as expected with its square planar geometry for dsp2 hybridization40.

Thermal gravimetric analysis

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) is a technique used to study the thermal stability and decomposition behavior of materials as a function of temperature. In the context of metal complexes, TGA can provide valuable information about their thermal properties, including decomposition pathways, decomposition temperatures, and the stoichiometry of decomposition products45. Metal complexes often undergo thermal decomposition in multiple steps, each associated with the degradation of specific components or ligands within the complex (Table 3 and Fig. S14). The thermogram for the BZU-Pd complex reveals a three-step decomposition process. The first step occurs within the temperature range of 52 to 312 °C, where a weight loss of 20.8% (calculated: 20.9%) is recorded. This loss is attributed to the release of two water molecules along with one acetate group (CH3COO). In the second degradation phase, which spans from 315 to 575 °C, the complex experiences a weight reduction of 40.1% (calculated: 40%). This phase is linked to the loss of a C6H5N4OS fragment. Finally, between 580 and 720 °C, the complex undergoes an additional weight loss of 12% (calculated: 11.93%), corresponding to the elimination of a C4H6 fragment. Ultimately, this decomposition process leaves a residue of PdO when the temperature exceeds 725 °C.The thermal analysis of the BIU-Pd complex shows that it undergoes decomposition in five distinct phases. The first phase occurs from 25 to 132 °C, corresponding to the loss of a single water molecule, with an observed weight loss of 3.80% (theoretical: 3.79%).In the second phase, an acetate molecule (C2H3O2) is lost, contributing to a weight reduction of 12.44% (theoretical: 12.43%). This is followed by three additional steps in the temperature range of 192 to 685 °C, which result in weight losses of 12.63, 16.69, and 28.60% (theoretical: 12.64, 16.64, and 28.66%, respectively). These reductions are attributed to the loss of C2H4O2, C4H3N2, and C6H4N2S fragments. Ultimately, when the temperature surpasses 690 °C, the decomposition leaves behind a residue of PdO. The degradation of the BTU-Pd complex occurs through a series of steps, beginning with the gradual loss of water molecules within the temperature range of 45 to 115 °C, which results in a mass loss of 3.45%. The next phase, occurring from 120 to 230 °C, involves the elimination of both H2O and C2H3O2 species, leading to a weight reduction of 15%.As the temperature rises to between 235 and 410 °C, the complex experiences the loss of C5H8N2OS molecules, accounting for a significant mass loss of 27.90%. The fourth degradation step corresponds to the removal of a C5H3N2 molecule, which results in an additional weight loss of 17.60%. Finally, during the last step, a C5H4 group is expelled, leaving behind a residue of PdO.

The coordination substances’ kinetic parameter for TGA

The degradation behavior of the examined complexes was analyzed using the Coats–Redfern equation, which facilitated the calculation of various kinetic and thermodynamic parameters, including activation energy (E*), entropy (ΔS*), Arrhenius factor (A), Gibbs free energy (ΔG*), and enthalpy (ΔH*), as presented in (Table 4). This analysis indicated that the thermal stability of these complexes exceeds the activation energy necessary for their degradation. Over time, the activation energy (E*) showed a gradual increase in later stages, suggesting a slower rate of decomposition for the complexes compared to the free ligands, indicating greater stability. This trend points to enhanced thermal resistance and reduced reaction rates, with the coordinated chelates demonstrating greater resilience than the initial ligands, as evidenced by the negative entropy values (ΔS*). The combination of negative entropy (ΔS) and positive Gibbs free energy (ΔG) suggests that the degradation process is non-spontaneous46,47. Furthermore, the negative enthalpy (ΔH*) reveals that the degradation reaction remains endothermic throughout the lifespan of the chelates, correlating stability with the observed non-spontaneous degradation behavior. The Arrhenius analysis via linear regression further reinforces a strong connection between thermal stability and decomposition stages. These thermodynamic insights, derived from TGA data, enhance understanding of thermal stability, decomposition kinetics, and reaction pathways, which is valuable for designing and optimizing synthesis processes as well as assessing the applicability of metal complexes in various fields.

The pH stability range of the complexes

The pH profile analysis of the tested complexes reveals a significant enhancement in stability due to the presence of BZU, BIU, and BTU ligands. This observation can be rationalized by considering the acid-base properties of the ligands and the nature of the metal-ligand interactions involved. The well-defined dissociation curves suggest that the complexation process is a reversible and equilibrium-driven phenomenon, with the ligands effectively coordinating to the metal centers and resisting protonation or deprotonation over the pH range of (3 to 11) (Fig. 5). The stability of metal complexes is typically influenced by several factors, including the electronegativity and steric hindrance of the ligands, the charge and size of the metal ions, and the overall electronic configuration of the resulting complexes. The BZU, BIU, and BTU ligands, being potentially bidentate, can form stable chelating structures with metal ions. Chelation increases the stability of the complex by reducing the enthalpy of the system and by precluding the ligands from binding to more than one metal center, which minimizes the formation of side products or precipitates. Moreover, the ability of these complexes to maintain a consistent structure across a broad pH range is indicative of their robustness towards hydrolysis, a common process that can degrade metal-ligand bonds and affect complex stability. This is crucial for the practical application of such complexes, as they are expected to encounter various pH conditions in biological, environmental, and industrial systems. The excellent stability observed across a wide pH range (3 to 11) suggests that these complexes are suitable for various applications such as catalysis, where pH control is essential for the selectivity and efficiency of chemical reactions, and in pharmaceutical formulations, where stability under acidic and basic conditions is required for drug delivery and efficacy. Additionally, these complexes may find use in environmental remediation and analytical chemistry, where they can be employed for the selective binding and transport of metal ions in different pH environments52.

The hot filtration test

The hot filtration test is a technique used to evaluate the heterogeneity of a catalyst in a reaction system. The process involves filtering the reaction mixture to remove the solid catalyst.The filtered liquid is then allowed to react further in the absence of the catalyst to determine if the reaction continues70.For the BIU-Pd catalysts, the test was performed under optimized reaction conditions to ensure accurate results (Fig. 13). After filtering out the catalyst, the reaction was monitored to see how it progressed. The observation that minimal reaction progress occurred without BIU-Pd indicates that the catalyst had indeed been active during the reaction. The lack of significant reaction after its removal suggests that the catalyst’s presence was essential for maintaining the reaction rate, which supports the conclusion that the BIU-Pd is heterogeneous. The advantage of heterogeneous catalysts is that they can be easily separated from the reaction mixture and often have high selectivity and stability. In this case, the BIU-Pd catalysts likely have active sites on its surface that promote the reaction without dissolving into the medium, making it suitable for use in various industrial and laboratory applications where efficient catalysis is desired without the need for complex separation procedures or the risk of catalyst loss.

Leaching test

The leaching test is a common method used to assess the stability of a heterogeneous catalyst, such as the BIU-Pd complex, during a catalytic reaction. The purpose of the test is to determine whether any of the active catalytic species are being released into the reaction medium, which can affect the reaction’s efficiency, selectivity, and overall performance. The catalytic reaction begins with the catalyst in contact with the reactants. The reaction proceeds for 5 min, allowing the catalysts to interact with the reactants and facilitate the transformation into products. After 5 min, the reaction is stopped, and the catalyst complexes are separated from the reaction mixture by filtration. This step is crucial as it isolates any potential leached species from the remaining active catalyst. The reaction is then continued for an additional 5 min using only the filtrate that has been separated from the catalyst in a new and clean flask. This filtrate contains any species that may have leached from the catalyst during the initial 5 min. After the total reaction time of 10 min, the amount of product formed is measured (Fig. 14). By comparing the product formation rate during the second 5-minute period (using only the filtrate) to the rate observed during the first 5 min with the catalyst present, we found no increase in product formation, indicating that no active species had leached out during the catalytic process71.

Expected mechanism in synthesis process

The synthesis initiates with the crucial activation of the aromatic aldehyde substrate. Within a water/ethanol solvent system, the aldehyde is treated with a catalytic quantity of the Palladium complex (BZU-Pd, BIU-Pd, or BTU-Pd) and heated to 70 °C.

In this step, the Palladium catalyst demonstrably functions as a Lewis acid, coordinating to the aldehyde’s carbonyl oxygen. This interaction polarizes the carbonyl bond, significantly enhancing the electrophilicity of the carbonyl carbon and priming it for the subsequent condensation reaction. Following aldehyde activation, rhodanine (2) is introduced. The Pd-activated aldehyde readily undergoes Knoevenagel condensation with the active methylene nucleophile derived from rhodanine, proceeding under reflux conditions to form the α, β-unsaturated Michael acceptor intermediate (A), as depicted in the provided reaction scheme (Scheme 2).

Suggested mechanism for synthesis of 6-(pyrrolidin-1-yl)-[1,5-a] thiazolo [4,5-d] pyrimidine (5a–l)and the catalytic role of the catalysts.

The explicit role of the Palladium catalyst in this initial phase is therefore to facilitate this key C-C bond formation via Lewis acid activation72. The reaction cascade continues from intermediate A with the addition of pyrrolidine (3). Based on the scheme, pyrrolidine attacks the thiocarbonyl carbon of the rhodanine moiety.

It is plausible that the Palladium catalyst, still present in the reaction mixture, could facilitate this step. By coordinating to the sulfur atom of the thiocarbonyl group in A, the Pd complex could further enhance the electrophilicity of the thiocarbonyl carbon, making it more susceptible to nucleophilic attack by pyrrolidine. This interaction could also potentially assist in the subsequent elimination of H2S, possibly by stabilizing the leaving group or facilitating proton transfers involved in the elimination process, leading to the formation of intermediate B73,74. Subsequently, 5-aminotetrazole (4) is added to the reaction mixture containing intermediate B. The scheme shows the nucleophilic amino group of 5-aminotetrazole attacking an electrophilic carbon within intermediate B.

Here too, the Palladium catalyst may play a role. Acting again as a Lewis acid, the Pd complex could coordinate to the C=C bond of the electrophilic center in B, thereby activating it towards nucleophilic attack by the aminotetrazole and facilitating the formation of intermediate C. The final stage involves the transformation of intermediate C into the target products (5a-l). This proceeds via an intramolecular cyclization, where an internal amino group attacks a carbonyl group within C, forming intermediate D. The Palladium catalyst could potentially promote this cyclization step by coordinating to the carbonyl oxygen, activating it towards the intramolecular nucleophilic attack, similar to its role in the initial aldehyde activation. Following cyclization, intermediate D undergoes dehydration, eliminating a water molecule to yield the final, likely stabilized heterocyclic product. The Lewis acidic nature of the Palladium catalyst might also assist in this dehydration step, potentially by coordinating to the oxygen of the hydroxyl group precursor, facilitating its departure as water.

Conclusions

This investigation focused on the innovative of three novel Pd(II) complexes. We utilized a comprehensive array of analytical and spectroscopic techniques to characterize these compounds and confirm their chemical formulas. The spectroscopic data indicated that the complexes exhibited a neutral bi-dentate coordination mode. The primary objective of this research was to evaluate the catalytic properties of these PdIIcomplexes. The BZU-Pd, BIU-Pd, and BTU-Pd complexes showed significant catalytic activity in a one-pot multi-component reaction (MCR) involving aromatic aldehydes, rhodanine, pyrrolidine, and 5-aminotetrazole, performed in a solvent mixture of water and ethanol. This method proved to be not only efficient but also environmentally friendly, yielding 6-(pyrrolidin-1-yl)-[1,5-a] thiazolo [4,5-d] pyrimidine derivatives with high yields while using minimal amounts of catalyst. The combination of ethanol and water provided a readily available and eco-conscious solvent system. In addition, the recyclability of these catalysts was examined over several reaction cycles. The BIU-Pd catalyst exhibited exceptional stability, effectively maintaining its catalytic performance over eight consecutive cycles without significant degradation. The BZU-Pd catalyst also demonstrated excellent performance, remaining effective across six cycles. Similarly, the BTU-Pd catalyst sustained its catalytic efficiency through six runs. This synthesis approach offers various advantages, such as the elimination of hazardous organic solvents, low catalyst loading, the utilization of non-toxic catalysts, swift reaction times, and high yields, all under mild conditions. The rapid execution of the green synthesis process, coupled with the favorable outcomes, underscores the effectiveness of the BZU-Pd, BIU-Pd, and BTU-Pd complexes. Our research represents a noteworthy advancement in the field of green chemistry, providing promising insights for the development of more sustainable and environmentally friendly chemical methodologies.
