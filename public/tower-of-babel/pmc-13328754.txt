Influences of sulfuric acid treatment on thermo-chemical properties of rice husk-pine sawdust heterogeneous briquettes
Authors: Ronald Walozi, George Samuel Onep, Yinka Sofihullahi Sanusi, Omer Eisa Babiker, Milon Selvam Dennison, Alphonse Candia, Samuel Okurut, Benard Wamubirigwe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeterogeneous briquettes made from rice husk-pine sawdust blend treated with sulfuric acid have the potential to satisfy the increasing global demand for sustainable energy. However, untreated blends are constrained by unfavorable thermochemical properties. In this study, a blend comprising 10 wt% rice husk and 90 wt% pine sawdust was subjected to H2SO4 treatment at 1%, 2%, 3%, 4%, and 5% (v/v) for 1-h at ambient temperature. Briquettes were subsequently formulated, and thermochemical properties were assessed to identify treatment conditions enhancing energy and combustion performance. The briquettes were analyzed for lignocellulose using acid detergent fiber and lignin, and neutral detergent fiber; higher heating value HHV using calorimetry; and thermal efficiency and emissions using a water boiling test (WBT) coupled with a portable emission monitoring system (PEMS) 4000-series sensor. The results demonstrated that sulfuric acid treatment increased briquettes’ HHV from 18.21 ± 0.09 MJ/kg (untreated) up to 18.82 ± 0.03 MJ/kg. The 1% concentration yielded the highest thermal efficiency (71.49 ± 2.05%), accompanied by the lowest CO emissions (0.88 ± 0.03 g/MJd), meeting the ISO 19,867–1:2018 Tier 5 limits, and the lowest PM2.5 emissions (100.67 ± 1.53 mg/MJd), within the Tier 3 limit. Overall, the optimal sulfuric acid treatment condition for effective combustion performance and energy delivery was 1%. However, cost–benefit analysis should be incorporated into future studies for practical real-world application.Keywords: Briquettes, Sulfuric acid, Treatment, Higher heating value, Thermal efficiency, EmissionsSubject terms: Chemistry, Engineering, Environmental sciences

Introduction

The rising global energy demand is one of the major challenges facing the World population. This energy demand is projected to grow by 34% between 2022 and 20501. This has exacerbated the already severe lack of access to reliable energy, especially in sub-Saharan Africa, where 730 million people depend on traditional biomass for household energy2. One promising solution to this challenge is to produce and utilize heterogeneous briquettes from the vast amounts of forest and crop wastes generated. Heterogeneous briquettes are solid fuels generated by compressing a mixture of loose biomass materials, such as rice husks and pine sawdust3. However, briquettes from a blend of rice husks and sawdust have inferior thermochemical properties. For example, briquettes made at a proportion of 50%:50% blending ratio exhibited a calorific value of 16.68 MJ/kg and ash contents of 14.60%, which limits their usefulness in energy applications4. This is due to the high ash and low energy content of the constituent materials. For instance, the calorific value of rice husk ranges from 12.8 MJ/kg to 14.5 MJ/kg, and the ash content ranges from 15.87%db to 25.56%db, respectively5. Sulfuric acid can be used to treat these residues, thereby reducing ash content and increasing calorific value. This treatment involves soaking biomass materials in sulfuric acid at a specific concentration, reaction time, and temperature. The material is then thoroughly washed with water and dried. The dried biomass is then compressed to produce briquettes. This treatment removes microelements such as zinc, copper, and iron, as well as macroelements such as magnesium, calcium, and potassium, which are responsible for ash6. This therefore reduces the ash content, thus improving interparticle bonding during densification and thermal properties7,8. Further, during this treatment, there is a hydrolytic breakdown of the polymer molecules, disintegrating the cellular matrix, accomplished by bond breakage in the lignin-carbohydrate complex, which removes the hemicellulose and condenses the lignin9,10.

Some studies have examined the effect of sulfuric acid treatment on ash content and calorific value. Key among these is by Wistara et al., 20217 where the ash content of oil palm biomass decreased from a range of 2.03% to 6.43% to a range of 0.62% to 4.27% and 0.4 2% to 2.15% at sulfuric acid concentrations of 1% and 5%, respectively. Another study was by Bamisaye et al., 202411, where the calorific value of watermelon peels was enhanced to 14.62 ± 0.21 MJ/kg and ash content reduced to 11.2 ± 0.13% after treatment with sulfuric acid. Similarly, the calorific value of watermelon waste increased by 31.83%, and the ash content decreased by 1.77% when treated with 0.5% sulfuric acid at 120 °C for 3 h, as reported by Adeleke et al., 202512. Further, the calorific value increased by 22.64% with sulfuric acid at 29.5% concentration at 120 °C for 3 h, as reported by Roy et al., 202213.

These studies have focused primarily on the sulfuric acid treatment of single biomass feedstock and subsequent briquettes. The present study introduces a novel heterogeneous blend of rice husks and pine sawdust that has been subjected to sulfuric acid treatment. The mixed feedstock approach leverages the balance between the structural and chemical characteristics of agricultural residues (rice husks) and lignocellulosic wood waste (pine sawdust), thereby addressing waste valorization and performance enhancement. Further, the study goes beyond conventional analysis by employing a multi-criteria optimization framework that combines thermochemical properties, thermal efficiency, and emission behavior. This not only differentiates the work from the stated sulfuric acid-treated studies that emphasize isolated parameters but also establishes a comprehensive pathway for designing high-performance, environmentally friendly, sustainable briquettes from heterogeneous biomass blends.

The choice of blending rice husks and pine sawdust is based on availability and complementary fuel properties. Rice husks are an abundant agricultural residue in the region, including Uganda, with an annual rice production of 350,000 metric tons, of which 20% is rice husk14. However, its use as a fuel is limited by its high ash content, low bulk density, and low HHV of 15.87–25.56%db, 88.82–124.26 kg/m3, and 12.77–14.54 MJ/kg, respectively5. Pine sawdust is widely generated as a by-product of timber processing, with a higher availability since it is 86% grown in Uganda compared to eucalyptus15. In addition, pine sawdust offers a lower ash content 1.72% and a higher HHV of 17.04 MJ/kg16. Combining these wastes contributes to their respective strengths17,18, like rice husks, which enhance resource utilization for energy recovery, while pine contributes to combustion enhancement. Blending these feedstock materials addresses practical considerations of feedstock supply and production volumes, thereby ensuring that the study’s findings are relevant to real-world applications where the residues are readily available but singularly constrained.

To address this gap, an experimental study was conducted to investigate the influence of varying sulfuric concentrations (1%, 2%, 3%, 4%, and 5% by volume) on the thermal-chemical properties of briquettes from a blend of rice husks and pine sawdust. The objective of this study was to formulate and determine the effect of sulfuric acid treatment at varying concentrations on the thermochemical properties, and to identify the treatment conditions that yield the best energy and combustion efficiency for briquettes made from a blend of rice husks and pine sawdust.

Briquettes were formulated from a treated blend of rice husks and pine sawdust at constant compression pressure and particle size. The effect of treatment on the briquettes’ thermochemical properties was studied using a thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA) for proximate analysis; acid detergent fiber (ADF), acid detergent lignin (ADL) and neutral detergent fiber (NDF) for lignocellulose; oxygen bomb calorimetry for higher heating value (HHV) and water boiling test (WBT) incorporated with a portable emission monitoring system (PEMS) 4000-series sensor box for thermal efficiency and emission analysis.

Methodology

Preparation of raw materials

Rice husks and pine sawdust samples were collected from Tirinyi Central Ward, Tirinyi Town Council, Kibuku District, Eastern Uganda, and from K.W Timber Center, a local timber processing facility, in Kawanda, Wakiso District. The husks were produced from the Kaiso rice variety, which is among the most widely cultivated in Eastern Uganda and is known for its heavy grains, high spikelet fertility, and plentiful panicles, according to Hong et al., 202119. The pine logs at the facility were processed using a locally fabricated circular blade power sawmill. The freshly collected materials were manually cleaned to remove dirt, stones, metals, and plastics, a method similar to that reported by Ajimotokan et al., 201920. The rice husk and pine sawdust materials were dried to a moisture content of 8.25 ± 0.16%db for standardization. These were then blended with 10 wt% rice husk and 90 wt% pine sawdust, for a total weight of 10 kg, five times. A solution of sulfuric acid was prepared using 98% sulfuric acid at concentrations of 1%, 2%, 3%, 4%, and 5% to achieve a total volume of 25 L. The solution volume of 25 L was prepared at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:0.0025 g/l (1:2.5 kg/l), which is lower than the 1:0.025 g/l ratio reported by Nwosu-Obieogu et al., 202221, thereby ensuring efficient utilization and minimizing acid consumption. Each of the five blends was independently introduced into acid solutions at 1%, 2%, 3%, 4%, and 5% concentrations at ambient temperature, and the mixtures were manually mixed for 5 min to ensure uniform distribution of the solid in the liquid. The mixtures were manually agitated at 10-min intervals throughout the one-hour reaction period to ensure uniform interaction of the material blend and the acid solution. The treated material blends were then thoroughly washed with water to remove any residual acid, thereby reducing the risk of corrosion in burner equipment during briquette use. Subsequently, the treated blends were dried in the sun for approximately 9 days (52 h) to a final moisture content of 8.34 ± 0.16%db. The moisture content before and after washing and drying was determined in accordance with the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC, 2000) standard method, using a drying oven (Oven Plus II OPL225.WD1.C, Gallenkamp, United Kingdom). The particle size distribution was determined in accordance with BS ISO 3310–1:2000 using standard sieves with mesh sizes of 2.36 mm, 1.18 mm, and 0.425 mm.

Production of treated heterogeneous briquette samples

Treated and dried blends of rice husks and pine sawdust were compacted separately using a briquetting press (BHP 3/7 A0402, CHEMA Anlagen-und Apparatebau GmbH, German) at the Agricultural Engineering and Appropriate Technology Research Center (AEATREC)-Namalere to produce cylindrical treated heterogeneous briquettes of diameter 70mm and height ranging from 50 to 60 mm. Due to their hygroscopic nature, the briquette samples were packed in airtight bags and labeled THBS1, THBS2, THBS3, THBS4, and THBS5 corresponding to blends treated with sulfuric acid at concentrations of 1%, 2%, 3%, 4%, and 5%, respectively. Similarly, an untreated, dried mixture of rice husk and pine sawdust was compacted, packed, and labeled THBS0 as a control. This provided a baseline reference for the experiment, ensuring accurate comparison and confirming that the observed effects in briquette properties are due to sulfuric acid treatment. The sample was prepared without subjecting the rice husk and pine sawdust blend to sulfuric acid treatment.

Determination of properties of the briquettes

The treated heterogeneous briquette samples were evaluated for proximate, chemical, thermal, emission and mechanical properties to determine how these properties are influenced by sulfuric acid concentration, as described in the following subsections.

Proximate properties

The proximate properties measured were moisture content, volatile matter, fixed carbon, and ash content. These were determined using a Thermogravimetric analyzer (ETRA THERMOSTEP, ELTRA GmbH, Germany) in accordance with the Deutsches Institut für Normung (DIN) 51,718–20 standard, similar to the method used by George et al., 202316, and Lubwama et al., 201822. The measurement process involved heating 1 g of each briquette sample at 103 °C for 6 h in a thermogravimetric analyzer to determine moisture content. This was followed by connecting a Nuffle furnace to an inert gas and further heating the sample for 5 s at 750 °C to determine volatile matter. Finally, the furnace containing the sample was connected to high-purity oxygen and burned to ash. Weight loss is the fixed carbon, and the remaining ash is the ash content.

Chemical properties

The chemical properties measured were only lignocellulose composition (cellulose, lignin, and hemicellulose). Lignocellulose was determined by measuring acid detergent fiber (ADF) and acid detergent lignin (ADL), and neutral detergent fiber (NDF), following the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 13,906:2008 and ISO 16,472:2006 standard methods, respectively23. To determine ADF, ADL, and NDF, 20g of each sample was treated with neutral detergent, acid detergent, and 72% sulfuric acid. The cellulose and hemicellulose were then determined by taking the difference between ADL and ADF, and NDF and ADF, respectively5.

Thermal properties and emissions

The thermal properties assessed were higher heating value (HHV), burning rate, cooking power, and thermal efficiency. HHV was measured using an oxygen bomb calorimeter (IKA C 2000, IKA®-Werke GmbH & Co.KG, Germany), which is integrated with software that automatically computes HHV, a method also used by Olupot et al. 20165. This was determined by burning 1g of treated heterogeneous briquettes under adiabatic conditions. Burning rate, cooking power, and thermal efficiency, as well as emissions, were measured using a water boiling test (WBT). These were determined in accordance with the ISO 19,867–1:2018 protocol for wood-based stove and fuel performance and emission testing, and following the Harmonized Laboratory Test Sequence24. This system comprises the Aprovecho Laboratory Emission Monitoring System (LEMS), a portable emission monitoring system (PEMS) 4000-series sensor box, and a mini moto TLUD gasifier. It uses the MusaPlotter software and the Aprovecho Excel Analysis Tool (ISO 19,867 module) to acquire real-time data and process standardized data, respectively. The PM2.5 was collected using 47 mm binderless borosilicate glass fiber filter (Part NO. FPAE-47, HI-Q Environmental Products Company, San Diego, CA, USA). These filters are first stabilized to meet the rigorous requirements of ISO 19,867, preventing moisture from affecting the weight. Calibration and initialization were performed using high-precision sensors for CO, CO2, and PM2.5 in the PEMS 4000 sensor box. This is followed by initialization of the MusaPlotter for data acquisition, and a dedicated CSV file was created for the test run. The sensors were given a 5-min warm-up, and the LEM blower was turned on for 10 min to stabilize the flow rate. To ensure a high-quality background filter was run before the test to measure the ambient laboratory emission, which was later subtracted from the experimental results. During the experiment, 200 g of treated heterogeneous briquettes were placed in the mini moto TLUD gasifier and ignited with 20 g of a briquette soaked in bioethanol for 2 min. A test pot containing seven liters of water was placed on the gasifier, and the experiment was run. This was followed by a standard test sequence involving cold-start high power to evaluate performance from a cold stove, hot-start high power to evaluate performance once the stove has reached thermal equilibrium, and low power to evaluate the ability to maintain a low boil with minimal emissions, each carried out for 30 min.

Finally, a post-test data processing was conducted by importing the recorded CSV data from the PEMS 4000 into the Aprovecho Excel analysis tool to compute thermal efficiency and specific emissions and assign ISO Tiers (0–5) for both emissions based on the ISO 19,867 framework. In addition, the analysis tool was used to compute the burning rate and cooking power. The tests were conducted at room temperature (25 °C), pressure (25.04 Hg), and humidity (60%) for 90 min. Mechanical properties

The mechanical properties determined were bulk density and breakage resistance. The bulk density was measured following the American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM) D7481-09 (2009), and density was calculated using Eq. (6), similar to that used by Orhadahwe et al., 202425. The height and diameter were measured with a Vernier caliper, and the mass was measured with a precision balance. The breakage resistance was determined using the breakage index, which was obtained by dropping a known mass M1 from each briquette sample from a height of 1 m onto a hard surface and measuring the mass M2 of the remaining briquette. Each briquette sample was dropped once. The breakage index was then calculated using Eq. (7), as used by Mulindwa et al.26.

The proximate, lignocellulosic, thermal, and mechanical properties were measured in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. The mean, standard deviation, and p-values at the 5% significance level (ANOVA single-factor) were calculated using the data analysis tools in Microsoft Excel. Additionally, the Pearson correlation coefficient was calculated using the data analysis tools in Microsoft Excel. Pearson offers greater statistical power and accuracy for assessing linearity and normality.

Cofunding variables included material heterogeneity in particle size, variability in acid distribution, sunshine exposure, distribution of rice husks and pine sawdust, moisture levels at biomass collection, moisture absorption by briquettes, biomass contaminants, and measurement errors in weight. These were addressed by sieving biomass through a 3 mm sieve to minimize heterogeneity in particle size, continuously mixing blends in acid solution at 10-min intervals during the 1-h experiment to ensure uniform acid distribution, measuring daily sunshine hours to reduce variability, thoroughly mixing rice husks and pine sawdust for five cycles during blending for even distribution, drying freshly collected biomass to a uniform moisture content before blending and treatment, carefully removing foreign materials such as metals, stones, and plastics to prevent contamination, weighing biomass three times with a high-precision scale to eliminate measurement errors in weights, and packing briquettes in airtight bags to prevent air absorption from the environment.

Results and discussions

Moisture content and particle size of raw materials during the process

The moisture content of the rice husk-pine sawdust blend before treatment was 8.25 ± 0.16%db, and after treatment with H2SO4 was 57.84 ± 0.29%db before drying and 8.34 ± 0.16%db after drying. The moisture contents of the untreated and treated blends after drying were below 13%, as reported by Lubwama et al. 202027 for successful briquetting. Particle size distribution of the untreated blend was 2.16%, 36.73%, 55.25%, and 5.86% for 2.36mm, 1.18mm, 0.425 mm, and less than 0.425 mm, respectively. For the treated blend, the values were 0%, 7.10%, 69.16%, and 23.74% for 2.36 mm, 1.18 mm, 0.425 mm, and less than 0.425 mm, respectively. The treated blends have a higher proportion of particles below 1.18 mm (92.9%) than the untreated blends (61.11%), indicating that treatment renders particles brittle and prone to shattering, thereby reducing particle size.

Effect of sulfuric acid concentration on proximate properties

The proximate properties investigated were moisture, ash content, volatile matter, and fixed carbon. The effects of H2SO4 at varying concentrations on these properties are presented in Fig. 2.

Effect of sulfuric acid concentration on (a) moisture, (b) ash content, (c) volatile matter, and (d) fixed carbon on briquettes.

In Fig. 2, the moisture content of treated rice husk-pine sawdust briquettes ranged from 7.79 ± 0.11% to 9.34 ± 0.15%. The briquettes were within the recommended range of less than 10% as defined by the German Institute for Standardization Deutsches Institut für Normung (DIN) 51,73118. This moisture content can reduce briquette decomposition and rotting28. In addition, the exhibited moisture content can improve the combustion efficiency of briquettes, as less energy is required to drive off moisture during combustion29. To maintain moisture at the recommended levels, the briquettes should be stored in an airtight bag to prevent moisture absorption from the environment, given their hygroscopic nature.

The ash content of briquettes from the treated blend ranged from 2.00 ± 0.64%db to 3.53 ± 0.29%db, with THBS1 the highest and THBS5 the lowest, all lower than 11.57 ± 0.53%db from the untreated blend, indicating a reduction with increasing concentration. This is similar to the reduction reported by Cuvilas et al., 201230 for spruce from 2.82%db to 1.45%db when treated with 0.1 M H2SO4 at 180 ± 2 °C, for 150 min. Also similar to a reduction from 12.97 ± 0.06% to 11.20 ± 0.13% for briquettes made from watermelon peels treated with 0.5% H2SO4 at 120 °C for three hours, as reported by Bamisaye et al., 202411. Furthermore, it is similar to the reduction from 2.1% to 0.02% for melon seed husks treated with 3.5 M H2SO4 at 90 °C for one hour, reported by Nwosu-Obieogu et al., 202421. The reduction is due to the removal of alkali and alkaline earth metals, which are responsible for ash, by H2SO46,7,31. Ash is transformed into inorganic oxides, such as alkali oxides, including K2O, Na2O, and alkaline earth oxides, including CaO, and MgO, and Si2O29. These oxides cause slagging due to ash diffusion as a result of the low deformation temperature of 900–1000 °C for alkali oxides32. The ash content should be kept low, as high ash content also downplays combustion rate and ignition due to limited transfer of heat during briquette combustion17,18. These briquettes’ ash content is < 4%, meeting the ISO 17,225 class B for standardized domestic heating and class I1-I3 for industrial application33. The correlation analysis between ash content and H2SO4 concentration yielded a coefficient of -0.984; increasing the concentration consistently reduced briquette ash content. In addition, there is a limited association between ash content and lignin (r = -0.338), cellulose (r = 0.137), and hemicellulose (r = 0.02).

The volatile matter ranged from 64.76 ± 4.61% to 78.25 ± 1.61%, with THBS4 the lowest and THBS5 the highest, versus 61.5 ± 0.57% for the untreated blend. Generally, it decreased with increasing acid concentration, except at 5%, where it increased. It is lower than for the untreated, which differs from findings by Cuvilas et al., 201230 where a decrease from 70.2% to 65.3% for spruce treated with 0.1 M H2SO4 at 180 ± 2 °C for 150 min and Bamisaye et al., 202411 where it decreased from 67.17 to 65.10 ± 0.04% for watermelon peels treated with 0.5% H2SO4 at 120 °C for three hours. The increase in volatile matter relative to the untreated blend is due to the hydrolysis of hemicellulose into arabinose and xylose, partial hydrolysis of cellulose into sugars, and partial modification of lignin into phenols9,10. These by-products generate more volatiles compared to hemicellulose, cellulose, and lignin during combustion. An increase in volatile matter is also an indicator of potassium removal, as observed in ash reduction, since potassium is one of the elements responsible for ash34. The general increase in volatile matter for the treated briquettes across all concentrations indicates their readiness to ignite with a proportionate flame and enhanced efficiency20,29. The correlation between acid concentration and volatile matter yielded a coefficient of -0.170, indicating that an increase in acid concentration has a marginal effect on reducing volatile matter. In contrast, a correlation between cellulose and volatile matter yields a coefficient of 0.867, suggesting that volatile matter is more strongly associated with increases in cellulose. In addition, there is a strong association of volatile matter and reducing lignin (r = -0.750), increasing cellulose (r = 0.867), and decreasing hemicellulose (r = -0.926).

The fixed carbon ranged from 10.62 ± 2.65% to 25.09 ± 4.55%, with THBS1 the lowest and THBS4 the highest. The values at THBS3 and THBS4 were higher than 17.50 ± 0.69% for the untreated blend. This increase is similar to that reported by Cuvilas et al., 201230 where it increased from 19.82 ± 0.11% to 22.70 ± 0.15%for spruce treated with 0.1 M H2SO4 treated at 180 ± 2 °C for 150 min, and Bamisaye et al., 202411 where it increased from 19.82 ± 0.11% to 22.70 ± 0.15% for watermelon peels treated with 0.5% H2SO4 at 120 °C for three hours. The fixed carbon increased with increasing H2SO4 concentration up to 4%, then decreased at 5%. This is because, at less than 5%, partial hydrolysis of hemicellulose and removal of inorganic elements occur, thereby increasing volatile matter and reducing ash content, respectively, thereby increasing fixed carbon. In addition, hemicellulose is more susceptible to acid hydrolysis than lignin, which has a condensed, aromatic structure that makes it resistant to acid attack; thus, lignin is retained, which substantially contributes to fixed carbon35. However, at 5% concentration, there is potential for excessive degradation of hemicellulose and cellulose and partial degradation of lignin, thereby reducing subsequent lignin and, consequently, fixed carbon. A correlation between H2SO4 acid concentration and fixed carbon produced a coefficient of 0.351, suggesting that concentration marginally increases fixed carbon. In contrast, that between lignin and fixed carbon was 0.727, which is much stronger, indicating that fixed carbon is strongly associated with increases in lignin. Further, there is a strong association of fixed carbon with increasing lignin (r = 0.727), decreasing cellulose (r = -0.815), and increasing hemicellulose (r = 0.855). Furthermore, a strong negative association between fixed carbon and volatile matter (r = -0.979) is observed, suggesting that an increase in fixed carbon is closely associated with a reduction in volatile matter. Similarly, a limited association occurs between ash content with volatile matter (r = 0.230), and a moderate negative correlation with fixed carbon (r = -0412). This relationship indicates a trade-off in treatment, in which lignin and carbon stabilization occur at the expense of volatile matter. The values of moisture content, ash content, volatile matter, and fixed carbon in the samples THBS1-THBS5 are presented in Fig. 2, along with their respective uncertainties as mean and standard deviation. The uncertainties propagate through comparative analysis of these properties, influencing the confidence with which samples are interpreted. The uncertainty in moisture content ranged from ± 0.03 to ± 0.38%, with the smallest indicating relatively high precision in distinguishing between the samples, while that of ash content ranged from ± 0.17 to ± 0.63%, resulting in greater overlap among the samples and reducing the statistical significance of small differences. In the same way, the large uncertainties in volatile matter (± 0.42–4.61%) and fixed carbon (± 0.61–4.55%) show that the exact magnitudes of variation must be interpreted within the bounds of these propagated errors, much as the general trend is robust. Overall, error margins indicate that the observed differences are reliable within the stated confidence intervals, though caution is warranted when comparing samples with overlapping uncertainty ranges.

Effect of sulfuric acid concentration on chemical properties

The lignocellulose composition was the only chemical property that was determined. Lignocellulose composition included cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin (true lignin). The effect of sulfuric acid 1% (THBS1), 2% (THBS2), 3% (THBS3), 4% (THBS4), and 5% (THBS5) sulfuric acid concentrations on lignocellulose composition of produced briquettes, as well as for untreated, is presented in Fig. 4.

Effect of sulfuric acid concentration on (a) Lignin, (b) Hemicellulose, and (c) Cellulose.

The true lignin ranged 8.36 ± 0.23% to 13.34 ± 0.65%, with THBS1 being the lowest and THBS3 the highest. The trend shows an increase between acid concentrations of 1–3%, followed by a drop between 3–5%. At a low acid concentration (1%), partial cleavage of lignin-carbohydrate linkage leads to solubilization of lignin fragments, thereby resulting in a reduction of measurable lignin content compared to untreated briquettes (10.14 ± 0.34%). At moderate concentrations (2–3%), lignin undergoes condensation and reprecipitates, leading to an apparent increase in its measured content in the solid fraction. This is due to the formation of Pseudo-lignin and the redeposition of lignin fragments on the biomass matrix36. At higher concentrations (4–5%), there is increased hydrolytic cleavage of lignin-carbohydrate linkages and possible depolymerization of lignin structures, resulting in partial solubilization and lignin removal, thus reducing the overall content. Lignin, though resistant, can undergo structural changes during acid treatment, including solubilization and condensation, thereby producing a non-linear relationship37.

Hemicellulose ranged from 11.61 ± 1.12% to 24.04 ± 0.08% at 4%, with THBS4 being the highest and THBS5 the lowest. It increased between 1–4% acid concentration, then decreased between 4–5%. At a lower acid concentration (1%), possible partial hydrolysis into xylose and arabinose, which are reducing sugars, as reported by Monlau et al., 201338, reduces hemicellulose compared to the untreated briquettes (16.21 ± 0.30%). At 1–4%, the initial increase in hemicellulose is attributed to relative enrichment rather than absolute formation, due to possible removal of extractives and ash, leaving hemicellulose largely intact because of insufficient hydrolytic severity at lower acid concentrations, enhancing fractional separation rather than complete solubilization of hemicellulose39. The continued increase in hemicellulose before reaching a peak at 4% acid concentration is due to a transitional phase between partial preservation and progressive depolymerization. The decline in hemicellulose after 4% acid concentration is due to increased hydrolytic severity, breaking down hemicellulose into xylose and arabinose, reducing, and possibly further degradation of the sugar into furfural and 5-hydroxymethyl furfural40.

Cellulose ranged from 34.75 ± 1.64% to 39.64 ± 1.28%, with THBS3 the lowest and THBS5 the highest. It decreased between 1–3% acid concentration and then increased between 3–5%. The higher cellulose content at 1% concentration compared to the untreated briquettes (37.43 ± 1.24%) is due to reduced lignin and hemicellulose. The decrease between 1–3% acid concentration is due to hydrolysis of the amorphous region, which is more accessible and susceptible to acid attack by increased hydrogen ions (H+)41. This results in partial polymerization by disrupting the bonding structural organization, reducing cellulose integrity, and hence loss of cellulose mass at mild acid treatment42. The observed increase in cellulose beyond 3% is due to relative enrichment resulting from possible extensive hemicellulose degradation and partial lignin removal. This leaves a solid fraction that is increasingly rich in cellulose. Further, the removal of amorphous cellulose leaves a more acid-resistant crystalline cellulose, with increased crystallinity as severity increases43.

Generally, the apparent increase in cellulose and a reduction in hemicellulose after 4% acid concentration is possibly due to the degradation of hemicellulose into reducing sugars and further into furfural and 5-hydroxymethylfurfural, while cellulose undergoes further depolymerization due to the attack of its amorphous region by the H+. This results in a relative increase in cellulose proportion as the loss of hemicellulose shifts to balance the solid composition, hence the observed trend in cellulose and hemicellulose.

Overall, the compositional trend shows that moderate acid treatment concentration (2–3%), yields optimal lignin levels (12.68 ± 0.28–13.34 ± 0.65%). This enriches the bonding of biomass during compaction44,45 through the exhibition of thermoplastic behavior at higher temperatures, and by acting as a lubricant45. The non-linear response of lignin, hemicellulose, and cellulose shows the competing processes of solubilization, condensation, hydrolysis, and pseudo-lignin formation. The drop in lignin and hemicellulose for THBS1 and THBS5 below the untreated blends is similar to the results reported by Nwosu-Obieogu et al., 202221 where the reductions were from 0.29% to 0.003% and from 23.0% to 5.0%, respectively, and the cellulose reduction for THBS1-THBS3 samples was similar to the reduction from 71.8% to 21.4% for melon seed husks treated with 3.5 M H2SO4 at 90 °C for 1 h. This results discussion points to an optimal treatment range of 2–3% where compositional and structural integrity balance can favour briquette performance.

Table 3 presents the Pearson correlation coefficient and ANOVA single-factor analysis of acid concentration and lignocellulosic composition. From Table 3, it is observed that there is no statistically significant difference in the lignocellulose composition between untreated and treated briquettes, as the P-values are > 0.05 and the F-values are < F crit. Regression analysis of concentration and lignocellulose compositions generated r values close to 0, indicating that there is little effect of lower acid concentration on lignocellulose composition of briquettes.

The apparent drop in lignin and hemicellulose in THBS5 is likely due to an analytical limitation inherent to sugar-based quantification rather than to complete structural degradation46. Lignin is resistant to sulfuric acid hydrolysis at increasing concentrations, partial solubilization, and redistribution of degraded products can influence the analytical balance, leading to an apparent increase in cellulose47. This shift complicates the direct interpretation of compositional changes, thereby underscoring the need to consider methodological constraints when evaluating lignocellulose fractions in biomass.

There was inherent variability of replicate measurements as shown by the means and standard deviation representation of the true lignin, hemicellulose, and cellulose in the samples THBS1-THBS5 in Fig. 3. These uncertainties propagate into any comparative interpretation of data. The uncertainty in true lignin ranged from ± 0.16–0.65%, the smallest, indicating a relatively high precision in distinguishing between samples, compared to that in hemicellulose (± 0.08–2.16%) and cellulose (± 0.13–2.15%), which were larger, indicating greater overlap among the samples and reducing the statistical significance of small differences. The overlap in the error margins for true lignin in samples THBS2 (12.68 ± 0.28%) and THBS3 (13.34 ± 0.65%) indicates that the observed difference may not be statistically significant when measurement uncertainty is considered, compared to hemicellulose and cellulose for the same samples. The propagated uncertainties influence the confidence intervals of regression analyses and trend interpretation when evaluating the relationships among lignin, hemicellulose, and cellulose compositions in the briquettes.

Effect of sulfuric acid concentration on thermal properties of the produced briquettes

The thermal properties studied were higher heating value (HHV), thermal efficiency, cooking power, and burning rate. The effect of H2SO4 concentration on these properties is presented in subsections 3.4.1, 3.4.2, and 3.4.3.

Higher heating value

The effect of H2SO4 at varying concentrations on the higher heating value (HHV) of rice husk-pine sawdust briquettes is shown in Fig. 5. The HHV of treated briquettes ranged from 18.31 ± 0.02 MJ/kg to 18.82 ± 0.03 MJ/kg, with THBS1 the lowest and THBS4 the highest, compared with 18.21 ± 0.09 MJ/kg for the untreated (THBS0).

Effect of sulfuric acid concentration on HHV.

The optimal treatment was achieved at an acid concentration of 4%, at which the enhanced HHV was attributed to selective hemicellulose hydrolysis and partial lignin modification. At low acid concentrations (1–2%), the increase in HHV was minimal due to limited hemicellulose hydrolysis and lignin modification. Between 3–4%, the increase was more pronounced, consistent with greater hemicellulose hydrolysis and lignin modification, which enriched cellulose (34.75 ± 1.64% to 35.03 ± 0.20%) and lignin (12.70 ± 0.16% to 13.34 ± 0.65%). Both cellulose and lignin are contributors to thermal properties39. Between 4–5%, however, the HHV decreased, suggesting over-hydrolysis of hemicellulose and cellulose degradation, leading to the formation of inhibitory compounds that reduce energy yield.

ANOVA single-factor test comparing HHV of untreated and treated briquettes yielded F-statistic (F), p-value (p), and critical F-value (F crit) of 6.36, 0.036, and 5.32, respectively. These results indicate that sulfuric acid treatment of the biomass significantly increases the HHV of resulting briquettes (F > Fcrit and p < 0.05).

The increase in HHV ranged from 0.51% to 3.3%, with THBS1 showing the smallest increase and THBS4 the largest. THBS4 is higher than the 1.72% (15.33MJ/kg) increment reported by Aslam et al.27 for briquettes made from rice husks treated with 5% H2SO4 at ambient temperature for 1 h. However, the increment is lower than the 22.64% recorded for rice stalks treated with sulfuric acid at 29.5% concentration, 120 °C temperature, and three hours reaction time reported by Roy et al., 202213. Furthermore, it is lower than the 31.83% increase reported for watermelon waste treated with H2SO4 at 0.5% concentration, 120 °C, and three hours of reaction time12. It is also lower than the 22.07% increase in calorific value observed for watermelon peels treated with H2SO4 at 0.5 concentration, 120 °C, and a reaction time of three hours, as reported by Bamisaye et al., 202411.

HHV generated correlation coefficient of 0.463 with concentration, 0.487 with fixed carbon, 0.488 with lignin, -0.876 with volatile matter, -0.510 with ash. Suggesting that an increase in HHV is more strongly associated with a reduction in volatile matter than with a reduction in ash, and with increases in H2SO4 concentration, fixed carbon, and lignin. In the analysis of HHV and moisture, low moisture content did not necessarily translate into a higher heating value. This is due to the intrinsic chemistry of the biomass, particularly as induced by H2SO4 hydrolysis of the lignocellulose matrix. This treatment promotes absorption of alkali and alkaline earth metals while facilitating solubilization of hemicellulose, thereby altering the fuel’s energy characteristics. Thermal efficiency, burning rate, and cooking power

The effects of H2SO4 at varying concentrations on thermal efficiency, burning rate, and cooking power in briquettes are presented in Fig. 6. The thermal efficiency used in this work is the char-based thermal efficiency. The thermal efficiency ranged from 50.28 ± 1.71% to 71.49 ± 2.05%, with THBS1 the highest and THBS5 the lowest, all exceeding the 49.95 ± 0.58% of the untreated blend. Generally, thermal efficiency decreased with increasing acid concentration. The highest efficiency is higher than the 61.04–64.00% range reported by Abdulkareem et al., 201848 and Walozi et al. 202618 for briquettes made from a blend of non-charred sawdust and cardboard waste. It is also close to the lower value of 75.06% reported by Elinge et al., 202149 and Walozi et al., 202618 for briquettes made from non-charred coconut and groundnut shells, with a starch slurry binder. At 1% acid concentration, the briquette thermal efficiency was higher due to minimal distortion of the cellulose structure and compaction, thereby enhancing heat transfer and minimizing energy loss, as evidenced by the high cellulose content. When the concentration increased to 2–3%, a pronounced reduction in thermal efficiency was observed due to moderate hydrolysis, resulting in cellulose partial degradation and hemicellulose degradation, which reduces combustion stability. Further increases in concentration beyond 3% generate a plateau, indicating thermal efficiency stability due to persistent degradation of cellulose and hemicellulose, resulting in incomplete energy conversion.

The briquettes burn slowly, making them suitable for slow-heating applications. As the concentration increased to 2–4%, the burning rate increased rapidly due to increased degradation of hemicellulose and amorphous cellulose, thereby creating more pores in the biomass blends. Further increases in concentration led to a slight decline due to over-degradation of cellulose and hemicellulose and to lignin modification. Further, the correlation between thermal efficiency and burning rate yielded a coefficient of -0.808, indicating that thermal efficiency increases as burning rate decreases. Effect of sulfuric acid concentration on emissions

The emissions studied were carbon dioxide (CO2), carbon monoxide (CO), and particulate matter (PM2.5). The effect of H2SO4 at varying concentrations on CO emissions, CO2 emissions, and PM2.5 emissions is presented in Fig. 7.

At 1%, these emissions are lower due to the preservation of cellulose integrity and the partial degradation of hemicellulose, which enhances cellulose and improves combustion. As the concentration increased to 2–3%, a sharp rise in CO emissions was observed, attributable to moderate acid hydrolysis that partially degrades cellulose and hemicellulose, thereby increasing volatiles and promoting incomplete combustion. When the acid concentration increased to 4%, CO emissions decreased due to structural modifications resulting from partial cellulose stabilization and lignin enrichment. Overall, a 1% H2SO4 concentration yields cleaner briquettes with lower CO and PM2.5 emissions; however, it also results in modest CO2 emissions. Furthermore, CO, CO2, and PM2.5 emissions were balanced at an acid concentration of 4%. This indicates that, when using sulfuric acid to treat biomass blends for briquetting, the acid concentration must be carefully controlled, as lower concentrations promote better combustion, whereas excessive acid concentration compromises air quality by increasing toxic emissions (CO and PM2.5). On the other hand, the focus on CO and PM2.5 emissions without measuring sulfur-related impacts, such as sulfur and associated SOx emissions, which also pose significant environmental and health concerns, limited the study. Therefore, future studies should incorporate the sulfur and associated emissions to provide a more comprehensive analysis of the sustainability of sulfuric acid-treated briquettes.

The measurement uncertainties for CO, CO2, and PM2.5 for the samples were presented as mean and standard deviation, as shown in Fig. 6. Therefore, the observed error margins for CO, CO2, and PM2.5 indicate that the observed differences are reliable within the stated confidence intervals, but caution is warranted when comparing samples with overlapping uncertainty ranges.

The briquettes under the 1% and 4% treatment conditions exhibit a clear trade-off between emissions and energy yield. At the 1% treatment condition, combustion is cleaner with CO of 0.88 ± 0.03 g/MJd and PM2.5 of 100.65 ± 1.53 mg/MJd, lower, accompanied by a higher thermal efficiency of 71.49 ± 2.05% and cooking power of 1001.67 ± 8.50 kW. On the other hand, the 4% treatment condition provides only an increase in the HHV of 18.82 ± 0.03 MJ/kg compared to 18.31 ± 0.02 MJ/kg at 1%, although this occurred at a cost of elevated emissions with CO of 2.56 ± 0.05 g/MJd and PM2.5 of 440 ± 6.08 mg/MJd, accompanied by reduced thermal efficiency of 50.70 ± 1.02% and cooking power of 612.00 ± 14.0 kW. For applications where reducing health-related pollutants and maximizing practical energy delivery are essential, the 1% treatment condition provides a more favorable balance; hence, it is recommended, since the increase in HHV at 4% does not surpass the decline in combustion quality for such applications. The 1% sulfuric acid concentration ensures minimal acid use, thereby reducing costs for acid acquisition, protective gear, and corrosion of burning equipment, thus increasing profitability. In addition, low-cost decentralized treatment systems, including buffering agents such as lime and ash, are recommended to reduce toxicity and corrosiveness, especially in rural settings, thereby enhancing profitability43. However, the economic implications of the costs associated with sulfuric acid treatment, including acid acquisition, protective gear, and washing, and their impact on profitability, were not incorporated into this study, limiting its practical real-world application. It is recommended that future studies incorporate economic analysis to ensure practical, real-world application.

The uncertainty in breakage index (± 0.15–2.23%) was the smallest, indicating relatively higher precision, thus clear differentiation compared to bulk density (± 6.89–65.85 kg/m3), which was larger. The overlapping error margin for bulk density for THBS3 (± 6.89 kg/m3) and THBS4 (32.37 kg/m3) suggests that the differences should be viewed with caution. The larger uncertainty for the breakage index of THBS1 (± 2.23%) broadens the reasonable range of values, reducing the strength of comparisons with a smaller error margin. These indicate that measurement uncertainties in bulk density and breakage index propagate into comparative analyses and trend evaluations, and that careful consideration of error ranges is essential for robust conclusions.

Conclusions

The study treated a blend of 10% rice husk and pine sawdust with H2SO4 at 1%, 2%, 3%, 4%, and 5% concentrations, and formulated and analyzed briquettes from the treated blends to identify the most suitable treatment condition. It can be concluded that H2SO4 treatment effectively reduced the ash content to 2.00 ± 0.64—3.53 ± 0.29%db, from 11.57 ± 0.53%db for the briquettes from a rice husk and pine sawdust blend, thereby improving fuel quality. The ash reduction increased with increasing acid concentration (r = -0.984). In addition, the treatment increased the heating value (HHV) to a range of 18.31 ± 0.02 MJ/kg to 18.82 ± 0.03 MJ/kg of briquettes from rice husk and pine sawdust blend, versus 18.21 ± 0.09 MJ/kg for briquettes from the untreated blend, with 4% concentration yielding the highest HHV. The HHV increased with increasing acid concentration (r = 0.463). Further, H2SO4 treatment enhanced cooking power and thermal efficiency with char of briquettes at 1% acid concentration to 1001.67 ± 8.50 kW and 71.49 ± 2.05%, versus 872.00 ± 0.00 kW and 49.95 ± 0.58% for briquettes from the untreated blend. However, as HHV increased with increasing acid concentration, both thermal efficiency (r = -0.893) and cooking power (r = -0.785) declined, suggesting that excessive acid treatment reduces the effective conversion of energy. Similarly, CO emissions reduced to 0.88 ± 0.03 g/MJd at 1% H2SO4 treatment versus 1.12 ± 0.01 g/MJd for briquettes from the untreated blend meeting the Tier 5 range (≤ 3 g/MJd) under the ISO 19,867–1:2018. While PM2.5 emissions reduced to 100.67 ± 1.53 mg/MJd, compared with 103.33 ± 1.53 mg/MJd for briquettes from the untreated blend, which is within the Tier 3 range (≤ 218 mg/MJd to > 62 mg/MJd). However, acid treatment at > 1% increased CO emissions (r = 0.688) and PM2.5 emissions (r = 0.906). Overall the 4% treatment condition resulted in a higher HHV (18.82 ± 0.03 MJ/kg versus 18.31 ± 0.02 MJ/kg) though associated with poorer combustion performance, including lower thermal efficiency (50.70 ± 1.02% versus 71.49 ± 2.05%), reduced cooking power (612.00 ± 14.00 kW versus 1001.67 ± 8.50 kW), higher CO emissions (2.56 ± 0.05 g/MJd versus 0.88 ± 0.03 g/MJd), and increased PM2.5 emissions (440 ± 6.08 mg/MJd versus 100.65 ± 1.53 mg/MJd) compared to the 1% treatment condition. The 1% treatment condition, on the other hand, provided a more balanced outcome, integrating lower pollutant emissions with higher thermal efficiency, a stable burning rate, and greater cooking power. Therefore, the 1% treatment condition is optimal, ensuring both effective combustion and practical energy delivery, and is recommended for adoption in briquette production.
