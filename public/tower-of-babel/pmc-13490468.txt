Integrative machine learning and multi-omics analysis reveals ATIC as a promoter of hepatocellular carcinoma progression
Authors: Longhui Xie, Tiantian Wang, Changbin Pan, Jianwei Lan, Yang Yang, Lijuan Lv, Cila Zhou, Shuang Qin, Yinkuan Ning
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutophagy plays a non-negligible role in the progression and immune regulation of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). An integrated analysis of the autophagy-related genes (ARGs) is of significance to deepen our mechanistic understanding about HCC pathology. In our present work, based on the expression patterns of 221 ARGs, we first identified 2 autophagy-related subtypes for TCGA-HCC patients using consensus clustering method. The two subtypes showed considerable distinctions in terms of molecular characteristics, immune landscapes and clinical outcomes. To augment the clinical utility of the subtyping system, a four-gene prognostic model including ATIC, RHEB, TMEM74 and PRKCD was developed and verified through LASSO and multivariate Cox regression analyses. ROC(AUC) analysis confirmed the predictive efficacy of the model across the training and validation cohorts. Notably, HCC patients in the high-risk group exhibited elevated tumor mutation burdens and higher expression of multiple immune checkpoint genes, suggesting distinct immune-related features between risk groups. Furthermore, single-cell RNA sequencing analysis revealed that the model’s marker gene–ATIC was predominantly expressed in tumor cells and proliferative T cells, with its expression showing strong and positive associations with autophagy activity. Finally, in vitro experiments were conducted to explore the potential role of ATIC in HCC. The results indicated that ATIC knockdown was associated with reduced proliferative and migratory capacities of HCC cells, along with alterations in autophagy-related phenotypes, including decreased autophagic flux. Taken together, our study provides a preliminary autophagy-related prognostic signature and identifies ATIC as a potential regulator of HCC progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54816-2.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Multi-omics, Machine learning, Biomarker, Tumor immune microenvironmentSubject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Oncology

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the sixth most common malignancy worldwide and ranks the third position in cancer-related deaths1,2. As a highly-heterogeneous types of malignancy, HCC patients shows varying degrees of alterations regarding the molecular characteristics and clinical outcomes. Despite recent advances in surgical resection, liver transplantation, targeted therapy, and immune checkpoint blockade, the overall prognosis of HCC remains unsatisfied3–9, which is largely attributed to the difficulties in early diagnosis, as well as high recurrence and metastasis rates. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify novel molecular biomarkers to improve prognostic prediction and guide individualized therapeutic decision-making9.

Autophagy is a highly-conserved cellular homeostatic process responsible for the degradation and recycling of damaged proteins and organelles10,11. Mounting evidence indicated autophagy’s dual role in tumorigenesis12,13. On one hand, it suppresses tumor initiation by preventing genomic instability and removing dysfunctional organelles12,14. On the other hand, once a tumor is established, autophagy can provide cancer cells with metabolic substrates to meet the high energy demands for rapid growth, thereby supporting their survival11,15. In the context of HCC, autophagy has reportedly been correlated with epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), drug resistance, and immune evasion16. In addition, current prognostic models and molecular classifications for HCC are predominantly constructed based on relatively limited gene signatures, and their capacity to comprehensively delineate autophagy-related heterogeneity and to reliably predict immunotherapy responsiveness remains suboptimal. In particular, systematic investigations into the integrative stratification of HCC patients according to autophagy-associated molecular programs and the translational relevance of such stratification to individualized therapeutic decision-making remain insufficient. Thus, an integrative analysis of autophagy-related genes (ARGs) could help us gain a deeper understanding about the underlying mechanism in HCC pathology.

In this study, we systematically analyzed the expression profiles and prognostic relevance of ARGs in HCC using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Based on 30 prognosis-associated ARGs, we identified two distinct HCC autophagy subtypes, with markedly different immune microenvironment landscapes, biological functions, and survival outcomes. Later, to enhance the clinical utility of the subtyping system, a prognostic risk model was developed and validated using LASSO and Cox regression methods. The model showed potential value in stratifying HCC patients by survival risk. In addition, through single-cell RNA sequencing analysis, we found that ATIC, a key gene in the model, was predominantly expressed in tumor cells and strongly associated with elevated autophagy activity. Finally, functional assays added to the credibility of identifying ATIC as a key regulator linking autophagy to HCC progression, with its silencing significantly suppressed autophagy, and impaired the proliferation and migration of HCC cells. Collectively, the present study established an autophagy-related prognostic signature with potential prognostic value and identified ATIC as a potential regulator of HCC progression.

Methods

Data collection

In this study, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) transcriptomic data and corresponding clinical information for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-LIHC cohort (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Data on somatic mutation counts and copy number variations (CNVs) were also retrieved from the TCGA database. Autophagy related genes (ARGs) were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) within the GSEA platform. Specifically, we used the GO Biological Process gene set GOBP_REGULATION_OF_AUTOPHAGY from the MSigDB human release v2024.1.Hs, and genes included in this gene set were defined as ARGs for downstream analyses17.

Characterization of autophagy-related genes

We analyzed the interaction network among the ARGs, including their somatic mutation frequencies, genomic loci, and CNV profiles. The expression patterns of ARGs across different HCC subtypes were systematically assessed. Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed using the “coxph” function in R to evaluate the prognostic significance of these genes, and the results were visualized using forest plots.

Unsupervised consensus clustering

Unsupervised consensus clustering was conducted using the “ConsensusClusterPlus” R package to stratify HCC patients into distinct autophagy-related molecular subtypes based on ARG expression profiles. The robustness of the clustering was validated through consensus matrix analysis.

Construction and validation of the prognostic risk model

TCGA-LIHC patients were randomly divided into internal training and validation cohorts in a 1:1 ratio using a computer-generated randomization procedure with a fixed random seed (set.seed = 2024) to ensure reproducibility. In the training cohort, univariate Cox regression analysis was first conducted using the “survival” R package to identify prognostically relevant hub genes. Subsequently, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression model was applied using the “glmnet” R package to reduce overfitting and identify the most robust prognostic markers. The risk score for each patient was calculated using the following formula: Risk score = ∑i = 1ⁿ (βi × Expi), where βi represents the regression coefficient of gene i derived from the LASSO Cox model, Expi is the normalized expression level of gene i, and n is the number of genes selected in the final model. Based on the median risk score calculated from the training cohort, patients in both the training and validation cohorts were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups.

Nomogram construction

A nomogram integrating the risk score and clinical parameters was constructed using the “rms” R package to predict survival probabilities at specific time points. Calibration curves were applied to assess the accuracy of the predicted survival, while receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC) values were used to quantify the discriminative ability. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to compare the net clinical benefits of the nomogram, the risk score alone, and individual clinical variables.

Functional enrichment analysis

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment was conducted using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)18–20. Pathways with normalized enrichment score (NES) > |1| and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Additionally, gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was performed using the “GSVA” R package to compare pathway activity differences between high- and low-risk groups, based on gene sets obtained from the MSigDB database.

Immune cell infiltration analysis

To estimate the infiltration levels of immune cells across different risk groups, multiple algorithms including TIMER, CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT-ABS, QUANTISEQ, MCPCOUNTER, XCELL, and EPIC were utilized. These methods provided a comprehensive and cross-validated assessment of immune infiltration characteristics. Furthermore, immune function-related signatures (e.g., antigen-presenting cell co-stimulation, T cell co-stimulation, cytolytic activity) were quantified and compared between risk groups. The expression levels of immune checkpoint molecules such as PDCD1, CTLA4, and TIGIT were also analyzed to evaluate differences in immune regulatory profiles.

scRNA-seq analysis

Single-cell RNA-seq preprocessing and batch effect correction were performed using Seurat v5.0. Cells with fewer than 500 detected genes or with mitochondrial gene percentages greater than 20% were excluded. Gene expression matrices were log-normalized using the NormalizeData function with default parameters. Highly variable genes were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function. Data were scaled using the ScaleData function, and principal component analysis (PCA) was performed based on the top 3,000 variable genes. The first 20 principal components were used for downstream analyses. Batch effects across samples were corrected using the Harmony algorithm with default parameters, and the corrected embeddings were used for clustering and visualization. Nearest-neighbor graphs were constructed using FindNeighbors (dims = 1:20), and cells were clustered using FindClusters (resolution = 0.6). t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) was performed using the RunTSNE function with the first 20 principal components for dimensionality reduction and visualization.

Clinical sample collection

18 pairs of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and matched adjacent non-tumor tissues were collected from patients undergoing surgical resection at the central Hospital of Yongzhou, with informed consent and approval from the institutional ethics committee. All methods involving human participants were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations and with the Declaration of Helsinki. Samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analyses.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells or tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and RNA quality was evaluated based on the A260/A280 ratio. Subsequently, equal amounts of RNA (typically 1 µg) were reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using a cDNA synthesis kit (Takara). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR system. Each reaction was carried out in a total volume of 20 µL, containing 10 µL of SYBR Green Master Mix, 0.4 µL of forward primer, 0.4 µL of reverse primer, 2 µL of cDNA template, and nuclease-free water to adjust the final volume. The amplification protocol was set as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2–5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. A melting curve analysis was performed at the end of the amplification to verify the specificity of the PCR products and exclude nonspecific amplification or primer-dimer formation.

All experiments were conducted with at least three independent replicates. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^–ΔΔCt method, with β-actin serving as the internal reference gene.

Western blot analysis

Total proteins were extracted from cells or tissues using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. During lysis, samples were incubated on ice for approximately 30 min with intermittent agitation to ensure complete protein extraction, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10–15 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected for subsequent analyses. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of protein (typically 20–40 µg) were mixed with loading buffer and denatured at 95 °C for 5 min. Proteins were then separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes, which were pre-activated with methanol. After transfer, membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1–2 h at room temperature to reduce nonspecific binding. Subsequently, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ATIC, LC3B, P62, and β-actin (used as a loading control). After incubation, membranes were washed three times with TBST (5–10 min each), followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were then washed again three times with TBST to remove unbound antibodies. Protein signals were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents and captured using a chemiluminescence imaging system. The expression levels of target proteins were quantified by normalization to β-actin.

Stable cell line construction

Huh7 and Hep3B cells were seeded in appropriate culture plates and allowed to reach approximately 60%–70% confluence before infection. Cells were then infected with lentiviruses carrying ATIC-targeting short hairpin RNA (sh-ATIC) or a non-targeting control (sh-NC). During infection, polybrene (typically 4–8 µg/mL) was added to enhance transduction efficiency, and cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24–48 h. After infection, the medium was replaced with fresh culture medium, and puromycin was added for selection (the concentration was determined by preliminary experiments, typically 1–3 µg/mL). The selection process was maintained for approximately 5–7 days, with regular replacement of puromycin-containing medium, until control cells were completely eliminated. Following the establishment of stable cell lines, total RNA and protein were extracted. The knockdown efficiency of ATIC was evaluated by qPCR and Western blot analyses. All subsequent experiments were performed using cell lines with confirmed stable knockdown efficiency.

Autophagy detection

Autophagy-related activity was evaluated by examining LC3B and P62 expression levels using Western blot analysis in control and ATIC-knockdown HCC cells. In addition, HCC cells were stably transduced with a lentiviral LC3-RFP-GFP tandem fluorescent reporter (GeneChem, China). After establishing stable expression, fluorescent puncta were observed and imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope under both control and ATIC knockdown conditions. Changes in red and yellow puncta were interpreted as indicative of alterations in autophagy-related processes.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded tissue sections were stained for ATIC, Ki67, and LC3B. Antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer, followed by incubation with primary antibodies and DAB chromogenic detection. Hematoxylin was used for counterstaining. Positive staining intensity and area were evaluated semi-quantitatively.

Cell proliferation assays

Cell viability was determined using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo). Absorbance at 450 nm was measured at various time points post-treatment. EdU incorporation assays (RiboBio) were conducted to assess DNA synthesis and proliferation according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded at low density in 6-well plates and cultured for 10–14 days. Formed colonies were fixed with methanol, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, photographed, and counted.

Transwell migration assay

Migration assays were performed using Transwell chambers (8 μm pore size; Corning). Cells suspended in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber, while medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After incubation, migrated cells on the lower membrane surface were fixed, stained, and quantified under a microscope.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). All results were derived from at least three independent experiments and are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Before performing parametric tests, data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. Parametric analyses were conducted using the chi-square test, Student’s t-test, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) when the corresponding assumptions were satisfied.

For multiple comparisons, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and adjusted P values (FDR < 0.05) were considered statistically significant. Statistical significance was defined as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.

Results

Construction of autophagy-related subtypes based on key genes

ARGs were retrieved from the MSigDB Database. Subsequently, RNA-seq expression profiles of these ARGs in liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) samples were obtained from the TCGA database. The differential expression of ARGs between normal and tumor tissues was visualized using heatmaps and volcano plots (Fig. 1A–B). In total, 40 differentially expressed ARGs were identified, including 4 downregulated and 36 upregulated genes. Univariate Cox regression analysis revealed that 30 of these genes were significantly associated with overall survival (OS) in LIHC patients (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1C).

Based on the expression profiles of these 30 prognostic ARGs, consensus clustering was performed on TCGA-LIHC samples using the “ConsensusClusterPlus” R package. The optimal number of clusters (k = 2) was determined according to the maximum clustering stability (Fig. 1D–E). As a result, 370 LIHC patients were stratified into two distinct molecular subtypes, designated as C1 and C2 (Fig. 1F). The expression patterns of the 30 ARGs were compared between subtypes, revealing that the majority of ARGs exhibited significantly higher expression in the C2 subtype compared to the C1 subtype.

Identification of autophagy-related subtypes

We further identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two autophagy-related subtypes, and visualized the results using a volcano plot (Fig. 2A-B). Functional enrichment analyses were performed to explore the biological significance of these DEGs. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that the DEGs were primarily enriched in pathways such as response to xenobiotic stimulus, nuclear division, chromosome segregation, arachidonic acid monooxygenase activity, and organic anion transmembrane transporter activity. In addition, KEGG pathway analysis indicated significant enrichment in cell cycle, drug metabolism – cytochrome P450, metabolism of xenobiotics by cytochrome P450, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and DNA replication (Fig. 2C-D). Furthermore, gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was conducted using GO and KEGG gene sets to evaluate pathway activity differences between subtypes. In the KEGG-based GSVA results, pathways such as endocytosis, Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis, basal transcription factors, and pyrimidine metabolism were more highly enriched in the C2 subtype. In contrast, the C1 subtype was associated with multiple metabolic pathways, suggesting that patients in the C2 subtype may exhibit dysregulated programmed cell death mechanisms contributing to tumor progression (Fig. 2E).

Notably, the significant enrichment of cell cycle–related processes in the C2 subtype suggests a proliferation-oriented transcriptional program, which may partly account for the inferior survival outcomes observed in this subtype. Meanwhile, the over-representation of glycolysis as well as xenobiotic and drug metabolism pathways indicates metabolic reprogramming and enhanced adaptive capacity, features that have been closely associated with therapeutic resistance in hepatocellular carcinoma. Given the established role of autophagy in sustaining cellular bioenergetic demands and modulating immune surveillance, these findings collectively suggest that autophagy-associated molecular programs may contribute to the distinct clinical outcomes between the two subtypes by coupling proliferative and metabolic advantages with immune microenvironment remodeling.

To investigate the heterogeneity of the tumor immune microenvironment between the subtypes, immune cell infiltration analysis was performed. Compared to the C1 subtype, the C2 subtype exhibited significantly higher infiltration of immune cells including activated CD4 T cells, central memory CD4 T cells, effector memory CD4 T cells, T follicular helper cells, and activated dendritic cells (Fig. 2F). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis further demonstrated that patients in the C1 subtype had significantly better overall survival than those in the C2 subtype P < 0.05)(Fig. 2G). These findings indicate that the HCC subtypes identified based on ARG expression display marked differences in immune microenvironment, biological functions, and clinical prognosis.

Construction of an autophagy-related prognostic signature

Given the complexity and heterogeneity of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) among individual patients, we developed an autophagy-related prognostic signature to evaluate patient outcomes. The TCGA-LIHC cohort was randomly divided into training and validation sets. ARGs identified by univariate Cox regression analysis were subjected to least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression to eliminate overfitting and refine candidate genes (Fig. 3A–B). After removing highly correlated genes via LASSO, eight candidate genes were retained. Subsequently, multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed to construct the prognostic model, and four ARGs were ultimately selected along with their corresponding regression coefficients (Table 1). The risk score for each HCC patient was calculated using the following formula: Risk score = (0.023 × ATIC) + (0.0427 × RHEB) − (0.5989 × TMEM74) + (0.0383 × PRKCD). Based on the median risk score derived from the training set, patients in the training, validation, and overall cohorts were classified into high-risk and low-risk groups (Fig. 3C–E). Analysis of survival status revealed that high-risk patients exhibited a greater likelihood of poor prognosis across all three cohorts (Fig. 3F–H). Kaplan–Meier survival curves confirmed that high-risk patients had significantly worse overall survival than low-risk patients in all cohorts (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3I–K). To assess the predictive power of the autophagy-related signature, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed. In the training cohort, the area under the curve (AUC) values were 0.733 at 1 year, 0.675 at 3 years, and 0.688 at 5 years. In the validation cohort, the AUC values were 0.770, 0.677, and 0.661, respectively. For the entire TCGA cohort, the AUCs were 0.749, 0.671, and 0.683 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively (Fig. 3L-N). Collectively, these results support the prognostic value of the autophagy-related signature for stratifying LIHC patients within the TCGA-LIHC cohort.

Immune heterogeneity associated with the autophagy-related prognostic signature

Given the pivotal role of autophagy in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment, we further investigated the immune heterogeneity between different risk groups. Immune cell infiltration was assessed using seven independent algorithms, including TIMER, CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT-ABS, QUANTISEQ, XCELL, MCPCOUNTER, and EPIC. Importantly, a high degree of concordance was observed across different immune deconvolution algorithms (Fig. 4A). Key immune populations, including CD4⁺ T cells, dendritic cells, and macrophage populations, consistently exhibited increased infiltration in the high-risk group. In contrast, NK cells and cytotoxic immune signatures were consistently enriched in the low-risk group. In addition, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) revealed marked differences in immune cell abundance between the two groups. Immune cells such as activated CD4 T cells, central memory CD4 T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and activated dendritic cells were significantly differentially enriched between high- and low-risk groups (Fig. 4B). In addition, there are significant differences in various HLA molecules between the high-risk and low-risk groups (Fig. 4C). We further explored immune functional differences between the two groups. The high-risk group demonstrated enhanced activity of major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I), whereas the low-risk group exhibited stronger cytolytic activity, as well as more robust type I and type II interferon (IFN) responses (Fig. 4D). These findings suggest that patients in the low-risk group may possess a more active immune phenotype and greater anti-tumor capacity, which may contribute to their improved clinical prognosis.

Immune related features associated with the autophagy-related prognostic signature

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting PD-1, PD-L1, and CTLA-4 have emerged as promising therapeutic strategies for the treatment of various cancer types21–23. To further investigate the relationship between autophagy-related features and immunotherapy response, we examined the expression levels of multiple immune checkpoints in high-risk and low-risk groups. The results showed that immune checkpoints such as PDCD1, CTLA4, VTCN1, CD276, and TIGIT were significantly upregulated in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group, suggesting distinct immune regulatory patterns that may be relevant to immunotherapy response. (Fig. 5A). Previous studies generally indicate that tumors with high tumor mutation burden (TMB) tend to respond more favorably to immunotherapy and have better prognoses24,25. Using somatic mutation data from the TCGA-LIHC cohort, we generated waterfall plots to visually represent the distribution of somatic mutations in the high-risk and low-risk groups. In the high-risk group, 146 out of 175 samples (83.43%) harbored somatic mutations (Fig. 5B), whereas 136 out of 176 samples (73.12%) carried mutations in the low-risk group (Fig. 5C). The top five mutated genes in the high-risk group were TTN, TP53, MUC16, CSMD, and LRP1B. Among these, TP53 exhibited the highest mutation frequency at 39%, followed by TTN (25%) and MUC16 (17%). In the low-risk group, TTN had the highest mutation frequency at 23%, followed by MUC16 at 16%. To assess the impact of somatic mutations on patient prognosis, Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were conducted. The results demonstrated that patients with high TMB (H-TMB) had significantly worse prognosis (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, patients in the high-risk and low-risk groups were stratified into H-TMB and low TMB (L-TMB) subgroups for survival analysis (Fig. 5D). The findings revealed that patients in the high-risk H-TMB subgroup exhibited the poorest prognosis, and significant survival differences were observed among the four subgroups. Collectively, these analyses suggest associations among somatic mutations, immune checkpoint expression, and immune-related risk stratification.

Evaluation of the prognostic model based on autophagy-related genes

To assess the clinical utility of the risk score model, we first performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses (Fig. 6A-B). The results demonstrated that the risk score was a significant prognostic factor in both univariate (HR = 1.387, p < 0.001) and multivariate (HR = 1.360, p < 0.001) analyses, indicating that the model has independent prognostic value. In contrast, other clinical parameters such as age, gender, and grade did not reach statistical significance in the multivariate analysis. Then, we compared the concordance index (C-index) differences between risk score model and traditional clinical variables in predicting patients’ survival at different time points (Fig. 6E). The results showed that the risk score model consistently maintained a higher C-index throughout the follow-up period and outperformed other clinical features such as age, gender, and tumor stage. Consistent with this, time-dependent ROC curves for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS (Fig. 6F-H) also revealed that the area under the curve (AUC) for the risk score was higher than that of other clinical characteristics. Next, to evaluate the applicability of the model in different clinical subgroups, we conducted stratified survival analyses (Fig. 6I). In all subgroups stratified by age, gender, tumor stage, or TNM classification, patients in the high-risk group showed significantly worse overall survival than those in the low-risk group (P < 0.05), further supporting the prognostic relevance of the model across different clinical subgroups. Finally, to further enhance the predictive efficacy, we integrated the risk score with other clinical features including age, gender, grade, and stage to generate a hybrid nomogram model (Fig. 6C). A calibration curve was used to evaluate the performance of the nomogram. The calibration curves showed good agreement between the predicted and observed OS probabilities, particularly at the 1- and 3-year time points. (Fig. 6D). In summary, the risk score model based on AGRs exhibits independent prognostic value and outperforms conventional clinical indicators.

Heterogeneity of ATIC in the tumor microenvironment of hepatocellular carcinoma

To further investigate the heterogeneity of ATIC within the tumor microenvironment of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and its potential association with autophagy, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis using the GSE210679 dataset26. After stringent quality control, a total of 31,525 cells were retained for downstream analyses. Dimensionality reduction and clustering were conducted using principal component analysis (PCA) and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE), resulting in the identification of 13 distinct cell clusters (Fig. 7A). We then assessed the expression distribution of ATIC across different cell types using density plots, revealing its significant enrichment in tumor cells (Fig. 7B). The accuracy of cell type annotation was validated using classical marker genes (Fig. 7C). Violin plots further illustrated the expression patterns of ATIC, showing that it is highly expressed in tumor cells and proliferative T cells, compared to other cell types (Fig. 7D). To explore the role of ATIC in tumor cells and its relationship with autophagy, we reclustered tumor cells into eight subpopulations. Notably, ATIC expression was predominantly enriched in clusters 0, 1, and 2 (Fig. 7E–F). Next, we evaluated autophagy-related activity in each tumor cell using the autophagy-related gene set defined above and the AddModuleScore algorithm (Fig. 7G). Violin plots were used to quantify autophagy scores across the tumor cell subclusters (Fig. 7H), revealing a strong overlap between high-autophagy-score clusters and those with high ATIC expression (Fig. 7I). Furthermore, we stratified tumor cells into high and low autophagy score groups (Fig. 7J), and found that ATIC was predominantly expressed in the high-score group. Collectively, these findings demonstrate a strong correlation between ATIC expression and autophagy, suggesting that ATIC may promote the progression of HCC by regulating autophagic processes.

ATIC knockdown is associated with reduced autophagy and impaired malignant phenotypes in HCC cells

To investigate the role of ATIC in the development of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), we designed and performed a series of cellular and molecular experiments. First, quantitative PCR (qPCR) was conducted on 18 pairs of tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissue samples from clinically diagnosed HCC patients at the central Hospital of Yongzhou (Fig. 8A). We observed that ATIC expression was elevated in tumor tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues based on Western blot analysis of 14 paired HCC clinical samples. (Fig. 8B). To further explore the functional role of ATIC in HCC progression, we established ATIC-knockdown Huh7 and Hep3B cell lines and confirmed knockdown efficiency by Western blot (Fig. 8C). These two cell lines were chosen for their distinct p53 status and differentiation levels, representing the molecular heterogeneity of HCC and enhancing the relevance of our findings. Based on preliminary findings suggesting a potential association between ATIC and autophagy, we further examined the expression levels of autophagy-related markers LC3B and P62 (Fig. 8D). The results showed that ATIC knockdown was associated with decreased LC3B expression and increased P62 levels, suggesting alterations in autophagy-related phenotypes. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis showed that ATIC expression was elevated in clinical HCC tissues, and increased expression levels of Ki67 and LC3B were also observed (Fig. 8E). In addition, LC3-RFP-GFP tandem fluorescence assays indicated that autophagy-related dynamic processes may be altered in HCC cells following ATIC knockdown. Consistently, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed a reduced number of autophagosome-like structures upon ATIC silencing (Fig. 8F–G), providing supportive morphological evidence of autophagy-related changes. Functional assays showed that ATIC knockdown suppressed HCC cell proliferation, as evidenced by CCK-8 assay results (Fig. 8H). Colony formation assays further demonstrated a reduced clonogenic capacity in ATIC-deficient cells (Fig. 8I). Transwell migration assays indicated impaired migratory ability following ATIC knockdown (Fig. 8J), and EdU assays suggested that ATIC knockdown was associated with reduced proliferative activity in HCC cells (Fig. 8K).

Discussion

This study systematically explored the potential functions and clinical value of autophagy-related genes in HCC. By integrating multi-omics data from the TCGA database, we identified 30 ARGs significantly associated with prognosis at the whole-genome level and, based on these, established two distinct autophagy-related molecular subtypes (C1 and C2). Notably, patients classified under the C2 subtype exhibited worse survival outcomes, upregulated expression of immune checkpoint molecules, and a more complex immune cell infiltration profile, indicating that autophagy may not only play a role in tumor progression but also actively participate in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment. These findings demonstrated the involvement of autophagy in immune evasion27. Our results provide new insights into molecular classification and pave the way for individualized therapeutic strategies in HCC.

Considering the limitations of molecular subtyping in clinical practice, we constructed a simplified prognostic risk model based on four pivotal ARGs. This model showed prognostic value in the TCGA-LIHC training set, internal validation set, and overall cohort, and remained independently associated with survival across different clinical subgroups, including TNM stage, age, and gender. Additionally, the incorporation of clinical parameters into a nomogram further enhanced the model’s interpretability and applicability in real-world settings.

Mechanistically, we focused on the core gene ATIC within the risk model, which was found to be highly expressed in tumor cells and significantly enriched in clusters with high autophagic activity, based on single-cell RNA-seq data. Functional assays showed that ATIC silencing altered LC3B and p62 expression and reduced autophagosome-like ultrastructural features, suggesting that ATIC may be involved in autophagy-related phenotypes in HCC cells. Together, these molecular and ultrastructural observations support a potential association between ATIC expression and autophagy-related alterations in HCC cells. Similar findings were reported by Yang et al. in gastric cancer, where modulation of LC3B expression and lysosome formation by autophagy-related genes was shown to impact tumor cell migration and survival, further supporting our data28.

Although ATIC was demonstrated to regulate autophagy in HCC, it is also known to participate in purine biosynthesis and cellular energy metabolism29,30. Therefore, its knockdown may exert broad, non-specific biological effects. To precisely determine whether ATIC promotes tumor progression via autophagy-dependent pathways, future studies should utilize CRISPR/Cas9-based gene editing, metabolic flux analyses, and rescue experiments31. Additionally, the autophagy network is inherently redundant; thus, potential compensatory activation of key regulators such as BECN1, ATG5, or ULK1following ATIC inhibition warrants further investigation32,33.

Immune profiling indicated that high-risk patients exhibited a paradoxical tumor immune microenvironment, characterized by simultaneous immune activation and suppression. These patients demonstrated increased infiltration of cytotoxic and antigen-presenting immune cells, along with heightened activity of immune-related signaling pathways such as T cell co-stimulation and antigen processing and presentation. However, this immune-active state coexisted with marked upregulation of multiple immune checkpoint molecules, including PDCD1, CTLA4, and TIGIT. This complex phenotype suggests a potential link between the risk score and immune regulatory status. Previous studies have demonstrated a strong association between autophagy and anti-tumor immunity. Notably, Galluzzi et al. identified autophagy as a critical modulator of antigen presentation and T cell activation. Based on this mechanistic link, the combined application of autophagy inhibitors—such as chloroquine or ULK1 inhibitors—with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has emerged as a potentially effective therapeutic approach for high-risk HCC patients. However, the efficacy and clinical relevance of this combination strategy remain to be confirmed in large-scale immunotherapy cohorts.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the risk model was developed and internally evaluated using the TCGA-LIHC cohort. Although the internal validation supported its prognostic relevance, further validation in independent and multicenter cohorts would help confirm its generalizability across broader HCC populations. Second, the causal link between ATIC and TIME remains to be fully elucidated and will require further investigation using animal models and spatial transcriptomic approaches. Future studies should integrate multi-omics and multi-dimensional experimental evidence to expand the applicability of this model in diverse patient populations and facilitate the clinical translation of our findings. Third, although our in vitro data supported the functional relevance of ATIC in HCC progression and autophagy-related phenotypes, further in vivo validation, rescue experiments, and pathway-specific analyses are needed to clarify the underlying mechanism.

Conclusions

In summary, this study established an autophagy-related prognostic model for HCC and identified ATIC as a potential regulator of HCC progression. These findings provide new insights into autophagy-associated risk stratification and immune-related heterogeneity in HCC.
