The impact of core stability training on jump serve speed in male volleyball players
Authors: Xizhe Li, Wenzi Wang, Chenglin Zhou, Bin Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study examined the impact of Core Stability Training (CST) on the jump serve speed of elite male volleyball athletes. A total of 20 athletes participated in a 10-week in-tervention, with participants assigned to either the Core Stability Training Group or the Conventional Core Strength Training Group. Core stability metrics, Jump serve arm-swing performance, and jump serve speed were assessed via pre-test, mid-test, and post-test measurements. Results indicated that all measured indicators showed significant improvements in both groups (P < 0.05). However, the Core Stability Training Group demonstrated significantly greater enhancements in side plank endurance, 1-minute sit-up performance, plank hold duration, and Jump serve arm-swing performance (assessed via medicine ball forward throw and badminton long throw) compared to the Conventional Group. Post-test data revealed that the jump serve speed of the Core Stability Training Group was significantly higher than that of the control group (P = 0.037). Correlation analysis confirmed positive associations between core stability, Jump serve arm-swing performance, and serve speed. The findings suggest that Core Stability Training effectively enhances the jump serve speed of male volleyball athletes. The un-derlying mechanism involves improvements in core stability and the efficiency of force transfer along the kinetic chain, which in turn increases the swing speed of the distal limbs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51991-0.

Introduction

The complexity of modern competitive sports has raised higher demands for athletes’ physical fitness, requiring more comprehensive and scientifically-based core training to enhance athletic performance. Core stability training is a functional training method that focuses on the core region, with functional human movement being based on biomechanical motions in real-life environments. These movements are achieved through multi-plane and multi-joint exercises that engage the core muscle groups and nervous system. According to the definition of functional movement by the National Academy of Sports Medicine (NASM), the changes in the kinetic chain control and influence acceleration, deceleration, and dynamic stability within the three planes of motion. Training should start with static low-load exercises to build a foundation, while also incorporating high-load dynamic training to enhance muscular strength1.

In recent years, researchers have continuously enriched the study of areas such as the neuromuscular system and kinetic chain theory, gradually introducing many innovative training methods for core stability training. Specific methods include various bridge exercises, balance pad training, suspension training, and Swiss ball training. These methods predominantly focus on exercises performed on unstable surfaces to strengthen the body’s ability to transition between stable and unstable positions. Research has shown that core stability training can significantly improve the throwing speed of handball players, the spiking speed and accuracy of badminton players, the vertical jump performance and trunk stability of volleyball players, all by optimizing force transfer efficiency2–5.

Serving is a crucial component of volleyball tactics and one of the primary methods of scoring. The service effectiveness and scoring rate play a decisive role in determining the outcome of a match6. The implementation of serving tactics in a match is demonstrated through the aggressiveness and placement variation of the serve, which disrupts the opponent’s first attack system, relying heavily on the athlete’s specialized techniques and fitness. In high-level indoor volleyball competitions, the use of jump serving has accounted for more than 80% of the serves7. Statistically, high-speed jump serves offer a technical advantage, creating significant psychological pressure on the opposing team’s receive-and-pass players and effectively disrupting their first attack system8.

The physical fitness characteristics of elite volleyball players can be summarized in three aspects: jumping ability, mobility, and arm swinging ability. Among these, the speed, power, endurance of the arm swing, and the corresponding physical functions together form the swinging ability, which is a key factor in the spiking and serving technical levels of volleyball players. This can be measured and assessed through tests such as medicine ball throws, Badminton Overhead Throw distances, and the number of consecutive swings in 30 s. High-level athletes’ arm swing hitting requires a balance of power and speed. The movement structure consists of multiple segments, including the torso, upper limbs, and hands, forming a “whip-like action.” The power originates from the waist and is transmitted sequentially through the upper limb joints to the end, reaching its maximum force and ultimately affecting the ball. The maximum ball speed after impact depends on the quality and speed of the arm swing, influenced by muscle strength and the coordination of various segments9. The interactive torque formed at the joints during the arm swing has a significant effect on the force generation and control of the distal segments, enabling the force from the torso and lower limbs to converge and be transmitted through the “kinetic chain” to the distal limbs, increasing the force generation speed at the distal end10. Ayşegül YaPıcı, through a 6-week bodyweight bridge-based core training program, explored the impact of core stability training on balance, strength, and serving performance in female volleyball players. The experimental group showed significant improvements in unilateral leg dynamic balance, core strength values, serving accuracy scores, and serving speed scores11.

Despite extensive research on core training, the mechanisms by which core stability influences sport-specific performance, particularly in volleyball jump serving, remain insufficiently understood. Most previous studies have focused on general performance outcomes rather than the underlying kinetic chain interactions. Therefore, this study aims to bridge this gap by examining not only performance improvements but also the relationships among core stability, Jump serve arm-swing performance, and serve speed. This integrative approach provides novel insights into the functional role of core stability in high-speed sport movements.

This study aims to conduct a 10-week core stability intervention program, further exploring the trend of changes in jump serve speed at different stages (pre-, mid-, and post-training) over time. Through correlation studies, the relationships between various indicators will be examined to discuss the impact of core stability training on improving core stability and serving performance in high-level male volleyball players. We hypothesized that Core stability training will lead to greater improvements in serve velocity than conventional training; Improvements in serve velocity are mediated by core stability and arm swing performance.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study selected 20 high-level male volleyball players from Shanghai Universi-ty of Sport as the experimental subjects. All participants were professional athletes with at least 4 years of training experience, and they all reached the level of first-class athletes or above. Inclusion criteria: having a good foundation in jump serve, and having never received systematic core stability training before. All 20 team members fully participated in the 12-week experimental process, and there were no withdrawals during the period. All participants were right-handed, had no psychological or neuro-logical health issues, and voluntarily took part in the experiment. Our study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Scientific Research of the Shanghai University of Physical Education (No. 102772025RT088) and complied with the principles of the revised Helsinki Declaration. All of the participants signed informed consent forms and received remuneration. This research experiment began on June 6, 2025 and ended on December 6, 2025.

Experimental design

Based on the pre-test results, the 20 participants were grouped and assigned to either the experimental group or the control group using a serpentine arrangement. The experimental group underwent core stability training, while the control group performed conventional core strength training. The entire experiment lasted 12 weeks. The first 2 weeks were allocated to preparation and action instruction, followed by a 10-week formal intervention phase. Testing time points were as follows: the first assessment (pre-test) was conducted at the end of Week 2; the second assessment (mid-test) was carried out in Week 7, after 5 weeks of intervention; and the third assessment (post-test) took place in Week 12, after 10 weeks of intervention. Both experimental groups received the intervention twice weekly, with each session lasting 30 minutes. Selection of experimental test indicators and training exercises

Selection of test indicators

Based on previous12 studies and expert opinions, a preliminary list of test indicators was compiled into a questionnaire and distributed to experts (see Appendix). After analyzing the results and summarizing the feedback from expert interviews, the final selection of five core stability test indicators for volleyball players was made: side bridge, side medicine ball throw, 1-minute sit-up, plank, and closed-eye single-leg stance. These indicators comprehensively assess the contraction and stabilization abilities of the athlete’s core muscles, and the reliability and validity of this method have been confirmed as effective13; According to expert-selected physical fitness indicators that impact serve speed, two tests had higher frequency of selection: forward medicine ball throw and Badminton Overhead Throw distance. Therefore, these two tests were ultimately chosen as reference standards for the study.

In this experiment, the ball speed test was conducted to measure the maximum serve speed of the athletes during the jump serve. The maximum ball speed for each serve was measured using a radar gun, positioned 2 m behind the baseline of the volleyball court, directly facing the net and the athlete. The experimenter held the Stedek sport radar gun. Video recordings were made during the jump serve test, and the footage was later reviewed by experts for evaluation (Fig. 2).

Training exercise content

Based on feedback from experts in physical fitness, sports training, and volleyball coaching, relevant materials were summarized and organized into an expert questionnaire for investigation (see Appendix). According to the results of the questionnaire survey, the training exercises for the experimental and control groups, as well as the specific training content for each stage. The training protocol in this study was divided into three phases: an adaptation phase (Weeks 1–3), an improvement phase (Weeks 4–6), and an intensification phase (Weeks 7–10) (see Tables 2, 3 and 4). Across all three phases, the experimental group combined core stability exercises with strength training. Apart from differences in the support surface, the experimental and control groups were matched in terms of training volume, intensity, duration, and rest intervals. Throughout the intervention, emphasis was placed on exercise quality, and all exercises were required to be performed strictly in accordance with the prescribed standards. Statistical analysis

The experimental test results were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 software. A two-way repeated measures ANOVA with a 2 (groups) × 3 (time points) design was conducted to examine the data collected at the pre-test, mid-test, and post-test. Mauchly’s test of sphericity was performed for the data, and the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied when the assumption of sphericity was violated. For post hoc analyses, the least significant difference (LSD) test was used for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and partial eta squared (η²p) was calculated as the effect size.

Results

Effects of core stability training on core stability test indices

In terms of the side bridge test indicators, both the time main effect (F = 157.945, P < 0.001, η²p = 0.898) and the group main effect (F = 5.064, P = 0.037, η²p = 0.22) were significantly different, and the interaction between time and group was significant (F (1.422, 25.597) = 23.121, P < 0.001, η²p = 0.562). For the Eyes closed Single Leg Stance test indicators, the time main effect was significant (F = 29.01, P < 0.001, η²p = 0.617), while the group main effect was not significant (F = 0.073, P = 0.79, η²p = 0.004), and the interaction was not significant (F = 2.261, P = 0.139, η²p = 0.112). Regarding the Side Medicine Ball Throw test indicators, the time main effect was significant (F = 69.155, P < 0.001, η²p = 0.793), but the group main effect was not significant (F = 0.79, P = 0.386, η²p = 0.042), and the interaction between time and group was significant (F (2, 36) = 8.284, P = 0.001, η²p = 0.315). In the 1-minute sit-up test indicators, the time main effect was significant (F = 132.861, P < 0.001, η²p = 0.881), while the group main effect was not significant (F = 1.854, P = 0.190, η²p = 0.093), and the interaction between time and group was significant (F (2, 36) = 8.325, P = 0.001, η²p = 0.316). For the plank test indicators, the time main effect was significant (F = 53.001, P < 0.001, η²p = 0.746), and the group main effect was also significant (F = 6.167, P = 0.023, η²p = 0.255), and the interaction between time and group was significant (F (1.283, 23.089) = 9.437, P = 0.003, η²p = 0.344). The results of simple effect analysis and post hoc multiple comparisons for all indicators are shown in Fig. 3.

The comparative analysis of the results of the core stability test after the experiment shows that the improvement rates of the test scores of the experimental group in the pre-test and mid-test, mid-test and post-test, and pre-test and post-test of the side bridge index test are 30.25%, 48.49%, and 93.41%, while those of the control group are 9.34%, 31.91%, and 44.23%. The improvement rates of the test scores of the experimental group in the pre-test and mid-test, mid-test and post-test, and pre-test and post-test were 43.21%, 43.84%, and 106%, while those of the control group were 25.09%, 23.63%, and 54.65%. The improvement rates of the test scores in the pre-test and mid-test, mid-test and post-test, and pre-test and post-test of the side-throwing solid ball experimental group were 19.68%, 12.28%, and 34.38%, while those in the control group were 10.07%, 5.63%, and 16.27%. In the 1-minute sit-up index test, the improvement amplitances of the test scores of the experimental group in the pre-test, mid-test, mid-test and post-test, and pre-test and post-test were 11.68%, 10.46% and 23.35%, while those of the control group were 7.61%, 6.26% and 14.35%. The improvement rates of the test scores of the side bridge experimental group in the pre-test and mid-test, mid-test and post-test, and pre-test and post-test were 37.54%, 34.48%, and 84.96%, while those of the control group were 11.01%, 24.61%, and 38.33%.

Intervention effects of core stability training on jump serve swing ability indicators

In the test indicators of Forward Medicine Ball Throw, both the time main effect (F = 40.217, P < 0.001, η²p = 0.691) and the group main effect (F = 5.526, P = 0.030, η²p = 0.235) were significantly different, and the interaction between time and group was also significantly different (F (1.404, 25.268) = 7.289, P = 0.007, η²p = 0.288). In the test indicators of Badminton Overhead Throw distance, both the time main effect (F = 36.58, P < 0.001, η²p = 0.67) and the group main effect (F = 4.66, P = 0.045, η²p = 0.206) were significantly different, and the interaction between time and group was also significantly different (F (2, 36) = 4.424, P = 0.019, η²p = 0.197). The results of simple effect analysis are shown in Fig. 4A and B.

The comparison and analysis of the test scores of jump serve swing ability after the experiment showed that the improvement rates of the experimental group in the test of throwing a solid ball forward from above the head before, during, and after the experiment were 10.17%, 10.37%, and 21.98%, respectively, while those of the control group were 4.15%, 5.28%, and 9.65%. In the test of throwing a badminton far, the improvement rates of the experimental group before, during, and after the experiment were 6.48%, 5.71%, and 12.57%, respectively, while those of the control group were 3.67%, 2.49%, and 6.25%, respectively. The improvement rates of the experimental group were greater than those of the control group.

Discussion

Effects of core stability training on core stability

In previous studies, some scholars have suggested that core stability training, as a form of functional training, helps maintain correct posture patterns and ensures efficiency and safety during static or dynamic movements13. Practical evidence has confirmed the significant role of the three planes of the human core muscle group in the serving and spiking techniques of athletes. The results of this study show that after 10 weeks of core stability training, all indicators of core stability, except for the one-legged standing with eyes closed, have significantly improved at different test time points. Among them, the test results of the experimental group in the plank test have significantly improved in the mid-test and post-test, and there are significant differences between the experimental group and the control group, indicating that the improvement effect of the core stability training method is more significant. Sommer’s research confirmed that the transversus abdominis muscle, which is located deep in the spine, is mobilized before the body accepts external loads and other muscle groups contract, and it is the first muscle to be mobilized before the limbs move, providing a stable foundation for functional movements; the multifidus muscle, which is also located deep in the spine, plays a key role in proprioception and neuromuscular control during movement, and unstable support training such as balance pads and Swiss balls in core stability training is an effective way to activate the multifidus muscle14. The results of the side throw solid ball test indicate that both the regular strength training of the current men’s volleyball team and the core stability training method in this experiment can stimulate the muscles in the horizontal plane of the core muscle group of athletes, but after 5 weeks of regular training, the body has adapted to the current training state, and the load and intensity should be increased according to the actual situation of the athletes to further improve the training effect. The results of the 1-minute sit-up test show that although both core stability training and regular core strength training significantly improved the test scores of the experimental group and the control group at different times, the training method of the experimental group had a more significant impact on the sagittal plane strength of the core muscle group in the post-test, and the improvement effect was better than that of the control group.

In core stability training, the training gradually transitions from the deep small muscle groups to the superficial muscle groups, with more emphasis on the combination of the two. The neural activity of the core muscles precedes the movement of the limbs, and while fully activating the muscles in the core area, it also stimulates the proprioceptors within the muscle chain, making the impact on movements more profound1. This further enhances muscle strength and posture maintenance ability, and also establishes channels for the convergence, transmission, and integration of upper and lower limb strength15, providing support points for movements and forming efficient and coordinated limb movements.

The impact of core stability training on the jump serve swing ability

In the human joint system, the hip, knee, and ankle constitute the lower limb joints; the thoracic and lumbar vertebrae form the core region; and the shoulder, elbow, and wrist comprise the upper limb joints. The joint chain is connected through the skeletal structure, while posture and balance are maintained via neuromuscular control. The interaction between the muscle chain and joint chain plays a pivotal role in energy transmission within the human body, and during the process of force transfer, the kinetic chain induces changes in the relative positions and movement patterns of each segment. The force application patterns for throwing a solid ball and a shuttlecock are similar to the force application patterns used in serving and spiking in tennis and volleyball, which resemble a ‘whipping’ motion. This requires the body to begin from a ‘reverse bow’ stance, applying force through the legs, flexing the hips and contracting the abdomen, and transmitting this power through the torso to the upper limbs to forcefully release the ball. The arm swing, which is above shoulder level, is classified as ‘open chain’ force application. Throughout this process, the contribution of core strength exceeds 54%16, effectively simulating the force application at the end of the serving arm movement. Following the intervention, both groups of athletes demonstrated significant improvements in swing ability indicators. Specifically, in the Forward Medicine Ball Throw, both the experimental and control groups showed progressive improvements from mid-test to post-test, with the experimental group demonstrating earlier and more pronounced gains. These findings suggest that core stability training enhances athletes’ swinging ability more effectively and at an earlier stage compared to conventional core training. The results further corroborate previous evidence on the kinetic chain, where the generation of force in sport-specific movements originates from the core region and is subsequently transferred to distal limb segments. Thus, core stability is critical for optimizing the efficiency of force transmission.

Correlation analysis further revealed that four of the five core stability indicators excluding the Single-Leg Stance with Eyes Closed were positively associated with jump serve swinging ability. Among them, Badminton Overhead Throw distance showed strong linear relationships with Side Bridge and Side Medicine Ball Throw, while Forward Medicine Ball Throw demonstrated moderate correlations with Side Bridge, 1-Minute Sit-Up, and Plank tests. These results indicate that core stability training influences swinging ability by improving frontal-plane and transverse-plane rotational control as well as sagittal-plane core strength, thereby enhancing the kinetic chain structure of force application. Manchado’s repeated measures ANOVA on handball players’ data reached similar conclusions. By increasing the strength and stability of the waist and pelvis, the power chain from proximal to distal in handball throwing was improved, thereby increasing the throwing speed. The core muscle group is the “power source” in the movement process. Core stability training enhances the stability of the core, reduces energy loss during force transmission, makes the arm swing and whip technique more fluid and powerful, and maximizes the force release effect4.

The impact of core stability training on jump serve speed

After 10 weeks of training, the jump serve speed test results of both groups of athletes significantly improved. In the post-test, the jump serve speed of the experimental group under core stability training intervention was significantly higher than that of the athletes under conventional core training intervention. The advantage observed in the experimental group may be attributed to the inclusion of jump resistance and sport-specific whipping training in the later stage, which more closely aligned with the movement structure of volleyball. The reason might be that core stability training is a progressive process from easy to difficult. In the early stage, it mainly focuses on dynamic and static exercises with stable support and static exercises with unstable support. There was no significant difference in ball speed between the two groups. However, in the later stage of the experiment, the experimental group combined the stabilizing strength of the deep core muscles with the strength of the superficial large muscles, and added core stability training movements that were in line with the volleyball jump serve technique in the improvement stage. The training transitioned from unstable support training such as sitting and lying down to training postures that were in line with volleyball, such as standing and jumping. By integrating core stability training with volleyball-specific technical movements in terms of the structure and force application pattern of the jump serve, the force transmission and movement efficiency were improved. In training, adopting body training methods similar to the characteristics of different technical movements is the key to applying specific physical fitness and mastering specific technical and tactical skills. Traditional training methods often ignore multi-planar and multi-joint movements, thus breaking the movement chain during the completion of movements, which is crucial for improving techniques17.

Correlation analysis shows that the speed of the jump serve has a strong correlation with the ability to throw a solid ball forward, which reflects the swing ability. This indicates that through experimental intervention, the subjects gradually mastered the effective serving method that forms the optimal joint mechanics, which is more conducive to the transmission of force from the movement chain to the upper limbs and then to the ball speed. The speed of the jump serve has a moderate positive correlation with the side bridge, 1-minute sit-ups, and plank support indicators. Combined with the correlation analysis results of swing ability and core stability, it is indicated that core stability can affect the speed of the jump serve through swing ability, by increasing the force application speed at the limb ends, thereby increasing the ball speed. In this study, there is no correlation between the one-legged stand with eyes closed and swing ability or the speed of the jump serve. The analysis suggests that the serving action involves dynamic shifts in the center of mass, and that visual feedback plays a dominant role in the jump serve. Consequently, static balance under visual deprivation (e.g., single-leg stance with eyes closed) is not directly associated with ball velocity during dynamic performance. The whip technique theory explains that the force generated by muscle contraction and the reaction force of the joint jointly contribute to the transmission of angular momentum, thereby affecting the acceleration and braking of each link of the limb. This is also the reason why the speed of the upper limb joints increases successively in the whip action. During the process of force transmission from the trunk to the upper limbs, the reasonable joint angles and muscle force application sequence will affect the height of the hitting point and the energy transmission of the swing. During the process from the run-up and jump to hitting the ball in the air and then landing, athletes need to maintain their posture in the air and hit the ball at the same time, which all rely on the important role of the “kinetic chain”. Core stability training consolidates the control of the athlete’s center of gravity and posture during high-speed movement, which helps the athlete adjust their movements in the air, making the serve more purposeful. It can also effectively avoid muscle compensation, deformation of the air movement, and sports injuries when losing balance. However, at this time, athletes also need to strengthen technical practice to consolidate the force application mode and form muscle memory, making the improved technical movements more “automated” to reduce the inevitable mistakes such as netting or out of bounds caused by the “forced serve” tactic.

Core stability training leads to superior improvements in jump serve speed compared with conventional training. Importantly, this effect appears to be indirect, mediated through improvements in swing ability and kinetic chain integration. The strong correlation between forward medicine ball throw and serve speed supports the notion that upper-body explosive power is a key determinant of performance. However, this study further demonstrates that such power output is contingent upon proximal stability. Without adequate core control, force transmission becomes inefficient, limiting per-formance gains. Additionally, the progressive nature of the training protocol transitioning from stable to unstable conditions and from general to sport-specific movements may explain the delayed but greater improvements observed in the experimental group. This aligns with motor learning principles, where specificity and task-relevance are critical for performance transfer.

Strengths and limitations

Both core stability training and conventional core strength training can effectively enhance the jump serve speed of high-level college volleyball players. During training, these two methods can be appropriately combined to comprehensively improve the athletes’ specialized physical fitness and technical proficiency. Core stability training is beneficial for efficiently enhancing the core stability and swing ability of jump serves of athletes. At the same time, basic technical training and refinement should also be taken into account, and specialized physical fitness should be consolidated in technical practice to meet higher requirements in actual competitions. Additionally, due to the relatively short intervention period of this study and the small sample size (N = 20), random allocation was not conducted, and there was no long-term effect tracking and there was no long-term effect tracking and the failure to separately discuss the effects of the two different types of jump serves, future research can further explore the different impacts of core stability training on powerful jump serves and jump float serves.

Conclusions

Core Stability Training effectively enhances the jump serve speed of male volleyball athletes. The underlying mechanism involves improvements in core stability and the ef-ficiency of force transfer along the kinetic chain, which in turn increases the swing speed of the distal limbs.
