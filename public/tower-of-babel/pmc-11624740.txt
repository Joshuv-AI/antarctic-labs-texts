Cost-effectiveness of human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination in Tunisia: a modelling study
Authors: Hyem Khiari, Karima Makni, Khedija Meddeb, Olfa Jaidane, Mohamed Hsairi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe objective of this study is to assess the cost utility of the implementation of the human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination programme in Tunisia in addition to the current cytology screening strategy. This study is very important to guide health policy decision-makers and to help them allocate their scarce healthcare resources.DesignWe used a previously described Markov model on the Online Cost Effectiveness Analysis tool programmed with R Software. The Markov cohort model was used to simulate the natural history of HPV infection and cervical cancer for a cohort of 100 000 females aged from 10 to 84 years. The model was calibrated with Tunisian parameters to calculate the long-term health benefits and costs of different cervical cancer preventive strategies in Tunisia.Outcome measuresThe primary outcome was the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of the implementation of HPV vaccination to the current cytology screening strategy to fight against cervical cancer. The ICER was calculated per life-years saved and per quality-adjusted life-year (QALY) gained, expressed in 2020 US$. Thresholds were fixed at one to three times the gross domestic product (GDP). One-way sensitivity analyses were carried out concerning the coverage rate of the cytology screening and vaccination as well as for the vaccination cost and doses.ResultsCompared with screening alone, the combined strategy predicted an ICER of US$1920.8 year of life saved and US$2239.3/QALY which is still lower than the GDP per capita of Tunisia in 2020 (US$3319). Sensitivity analysis showed that the cost-effectiveness of adding vaccination to the existing screening programme did not exceed the GDP per capita by varying the cost per dose upwards to US$45 (ICER would be US$2910/QALY) and vaccination coverage up to 100% (ICER of US$3245/QALY). However, the combined strategy exceeds the GDP per capita threshold when the cytological screening coverage rises to 70%, causing the ICER to rise to US$4787/QALY.ConclusionThis study showed that compared with screening alone, the implementation of HPV vaccination in addition to the current cytology screening programme in Tunisia would be considered cost-effective on the basis of the threshold GDP per capita. Improvement of the current screening programme itself also remains important and provides further potential to achieve optimal cervical cancer prevention strategies.

Introduction

Invasive cervical cancer (ICC) remains a significant global public health issue worldwide, ranking as the fourth-leading cause of cancer among women worldwide.1 According to the Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN), there were 604 127 new cases and approximately 341 831 deaths from ICC globally in 2020.2 The disease’s prevalence is disproportionately higher in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs), largely due to limited access to public health services and inadequate screening and treatment options.3 ICC is among the most preventable cancers, with the majority of cases linked to infections by high-risk human papillomaviruses (HPV). Embracing effective primary prevention measures, such as HPV vaccination, along with secondary prevention strategies like screening and treating precancerous lesions, could substantially reduce the incidence of cervical cancer.

Accordingly, WHO launched the Cervical Cancer Elimination Initiative in 2020, advocating for a comprehensive approach to prevention and control through HPV vaccination.4 However, the successful implementation of HPV vaccination requires sustained efforts, strong political commitment and adequate financial resources. Before deploying an HPV vaccination strategy, it is recommended to perform country-specific economic assessments that consider existing screening practices.5

In Tunisia, the current cervical cancer control strategy relies on opportunistic cytology screening for women aged 35–59 every 5 years, yet coverage remains alarmingly low at around 15%.6 Despite a relatively low incidence (4.1/100 000)7 and mortality (2.4/100 000)1 of ICC in Tunisia, a noticeable trend of decline, with an annual percentage change (APC) of −2.3% (p<0.05),7 there remains debate over the introduction of the HPV vaccine. Given the limited financial resources, conducting a cost-effectiveness analysis of ICC prevention strategies is crucial for guiding health policy decisions and optimising the allocation of scarce healthcare resources.8 This study aims to assess the cost-effectiveness of implementing an HPV vaccination programme in Tunisia alongside the existing cytology screening strategy.

Methods

The model

We used a previously described Markov model, complemented by the Online Cost Effectiveness ANalysis tool,3 which is based on a stochastic Markov model programmed in R Software (online supplemental appendix 1). This model was specifically calibrated with data sets from Tunisia to simulate the natural history of HPV infection, enabling the calculation of long-term health benefits and costs associated with different cervical cancer prevention strategies in the country. We employed a population-based Markov model that tracks a cohort of 100 000 females, aged 10–84 years, through 12 distinct health states. These states—ranging from healthy, through various stages of HPV infection and cervical intraepithelial lesions (CIN grades 1–3), to different stages of cervical cancer as classified by the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics, and including survival, cervical cancer mortality and mortality from other causes—reflect mutually exclusive conditions that the individuals transition between on an annual basis based on specific transition probabilities. Regarding the herd immunity effect, the question mainly arises for vaccination for boys and not for girls only. According to Fesenfeld et al,9 mathematical models have consistently predicted that vaccinating girls against HPV is highly cost-effective. However, due to herd immunity, the picture is less clear for vaccinating boys.10 On the other hand, Brisson et al11 demonstrated that, due to the important herd effects from girls-only vaccination, mathematical models predict that increasing HPV vaccination coverage among girls provides greater incremental benefit for both male and female individuals than adding boys to a vaccination programme.

The model’s adaptation involved integrating Tunisia-specific epidemiological data and cost assessments for screening and treatment practices to evaluate the cost utility of various prevention strategies.

Calibrating the model

The initial step involved calibrating a transition probability matrix using a previously validated model for Spain2, which was then adapted with specific Tunisian data.2 The model was manually calibrated to age-specific cervical cancer incidence for natural history, age-specific HPV prevalence and age-specific mortality rate observed in Tunisia (online supplemental appendix 2).

Disease burden

Incidence rates of ICC by age group were estimated using data from the Northern cancer registry of Tunisia (2010–2014)7 while performing a projection of incidence rate for the year 2020 using the age period cohort model APC12 (online supplemental appendix 3, table 1). Due to the lack of national causes of death statistics in Tunisia, mortality rates were generated from the GLOBOCAN study by applying the mortality/incidence ratio to our incidence data.1

Scenarios and sensitivity analysis

The model conducted a follow-up study of a cohort comprising 100 000 females, ranging from 10 to 84 years of age, to evaluate the cost utility of various cervical cancer prevention strategies in Tunisia across three defined scenarios:

Scenario of reference: Natural history (this scenario assumes no preventive measures are implemented)

Scenario A: Screening strategy alone which consists of a cytology test of women aged between 35 and 59 years every 5 years with an estimated coverage rate of 15%.

Scenario B: Association of screening and HPV vaccination (bivalent HPV 16/18 vaccination) targeting preadolescent girls at 10 years old. The vaccination strategy includes two doses with a 70% coverage rate, assuming 90% efficacy.

Outcomes and cost-effectiveness analysis

For each strategy, the model predicts the life expectancy and the lifetime cost per woman. Results were aggregated using year of life saved (YLS), quality-adjusted life-year (QALY)15 and the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER).16

All cost and outcome parameters were discounted at an annual rate of 3%, aligning with recent recommendations. To evaluate the robustness of the ICERs, one-way sensitivity analyses were conducted. These analyses assessed the impact of changes in the maximum and minimum values for each parameter, including screening and vaccine coverage rates, the cost of the HPV vaccine and the number of doses required.14 This approach helped determine the sensitivity of the ICERs to variations in key economic inputs.

Threshold

To identify strategies that appear to be cost-effective, usually, a cost-effectiveness threshold (CET) is defined. We used the approach of the Commission on Macroeconomics and Health since there is no specific local threshold defined for Tunisia. This heuristic suggests that an intervention should be considered highly cost-effective if the ICER is less than the country’s per capita gross domestic product (GDP) and cost-effective if the ICER is less than three times the per capita GDP. The estimated GDP per capita in Tunisia for the year 2020 was US$3 319.

Results

Cost-effectiveness analysis

The ICER for the combined strategy, when compared with no intervention, was US$966.5/QALY, three times the ICER for cytology screening alone, which was US$316.3/QALY. The discounted costs and QALYs per person for the strategy combining cytology screening with HPV vaccination (US$77.34 and US$29.85, respectively) were found to be higher than those for cytology screening alone (US$30.6 and US$29.83, respectively) (figure 1). Furthermore, compared with screening alone, the combined strategy yielded an ICER of US$1920.8/YLS and US$2239.3/QALY (table 3), figures that remain below the GDP per capita of Tunisia in 2020 (US$3319).

One-way sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis revealed that the cost-effectiveness of integrating vaccination into the existing screening programme remained within acceptable limits under various scenarios. When the vaccine cost per dose was increased to US$45, the ICER reached US$2910/QALY, and with vaccination coverage expanded to 100%, the ICER was US$3245/QALY. In both instances, the ICERs did not surpass the GDP per capita threshold. However, when cytological screening coverage was increased to 70%, the combined strategy’s ICER escalated to US$4787/QALY, exceeding the GDP per capita threshold, as demonstrated in figure 2.

Discussion

This study has examined the cost-effectiveness of the implementation of vaccination for girls 10 years of age assuming 70% coverage and efficacy, with HPV-16/18v in addition to the current screening strategy based on cytology (at 15% coverage) compared with screening alone. The study results indicated that the ICER for vaccination and cytology screening in Tunisia was US$1920.8/YLS and US$2239.3/QALY. These values are considered cost-effective when compared with Tunisia’s 2020 GDP per capita threshold of US$3319.

Despite the challenges in directly comparing our findings with other studies due to varying methodologies and screening strategies, our results are broadly consistent. The ICER for the combined strategy in Tunisia was slightly higher than that in a comparable Moroccan study, which reported an ICER of US$1295.0/YLS22 ; and a strategy in Indonesia which reported an ICER of US$1863/QALY with a similar vaccine coverage.14 In contrast, a study in the Philippines demonstrated a significantly lower ICER of US$783/QALY.23

Although the criticisms on the use of 1–3 times GDP per capita thresholds, they are currently still being used within global health.21 It is clear that, in all countries, the most likely CETs based on empirical evidence are well below the 1–3 times GDP CETs that have frequently been applied to date in LMICs. In fact, the issue with using these criteria is observed in countries with the lowest levels of per capita income to inform resource allocation that are above these levels will likely reduce overall population health and may well exacerbate health inequalities.24

The global strategy for the elimination of cervical cancer recommends vaccination coverage of 90% among girls under.4 This target seems to be difficult to reach, especially in LMICs. In sub-Saharan Africa, for example, where both ICC incidence and mortality are very high,1 the HPV vaccine was available for 15 years, and the vaccination coverage remains as low as 20%.25 Even in some developed countries, such as Australia and New Zealand, the coverage was around 77%.26 In Tunisia, the introduction of HPV vaccination in the national vaccination programme is compounded by its optional status requiring parental consent, suggesting a need for substantial public education to boost vaccine uptake. Thus, a study of acceptance and willingness to get the HPV vaccination among Tunisian girls and parents is recommended. Financial constraints also significantly influence vaccine adoption; the Global Alliance for Vaccines and Immunization (GAVI) initially proposed to fund a fraction of the first doses needed for Tunisian girls, but the country has recently been classified as Never GAVI eligible countries (as its gross national income per capita is over US$1810) which would have incentivised policymakers to introduce the vaccine.27 Moreover, the benefits of cancer prevention from HPV vaccines targeting young girls may not be evident for decades due to the slow progression from HPV infection to ICC.28 Hence, reducing the HPV vaccine price is highly recommended to ensure the continuity of the HPV vaccination programme in Tunisia. Policy-makers, donors and global partners should continue to consider ways to drive down the costs of vaccine procurement.

Vaccination, screening and treatment are the three essential pillars of the Global Strategy for the Elimination of Cervical Cancer.4 While this study highlights that HPV vaccination is promising, it does not diminish the importance of enhancing current screening strategies. ICC Screening in Tunisia is based on cytology for women aged 35 and 59 years every 5 years.17 This is in accordance with results of the review of Bruni et al, which reported that 35% of countries recommended HPV screening, but most of them were still transitioning from cytology to HPV. The same review reported that 40% of the 139 included countries recommended screening between the ages of 25 and 29 years, 65% recommended ending screening between the ages of 60 and 69 years and only 7% followed WHO recommendation to prioritise screening in women aged 30–49 years and women from LMICs.29 Like other LMICs, Tunisia suffers from low coverage and challenges in implementation due to inadequate infrastructure and a shortage of qualified personnel. Hence the coverage of the current cytology screening programme in Tunisia was 15%.30 This was a little higher than other Maghreb countries such as Morocco31 where the coverage of the screening programme was 10%. In addition, cytology screening modality was reported to face many difficulties in implementation in LMIC due to inadequate infrastructure and insufficient qualified human resources.32 Thus, reinforcing cytology screening is crucial, even as we explore more efficient alternatives like HPV DNA tests, which have proven to be more effective and less costly. According to a systematic review in LMICs, the HPV DNA test was the most efficient alternative for screening, and cytology-based screening was shown to be the least effective and most costly option.30

According to WHO, HPV-DNA detection is recommended in women 30 years and older with regular testing every 5–10 years. So, the current cytology screening in Tunisia needs to be revised and the age of starting this screening would also be discussed.

Ultimately, we must point out that even if this cost–utility analysis showed that the implementation of HPV vaccination in combination with the current screening strategy would be cost-effective on the basis of the GDP per capita, the decision to implement and finance these interventions rests with policy-makers, who must balance the cost-effectiveness of these strategies against other public health priorities and available resources.32

This study is the first cost-utility evaluation for HPV vaccination to prevent ICC in Tunisia. Another strength of our study was the use of local data on the cost of ICC screening and treatment. However, it is essential to note the limitations of our study, including the exclusive consideration of the bivalent HPV vaccine due to cost considerations and the assumption of lifelong HPV immunity, which remains uncertain as long-term vaccine efficacy studies are ongoing. Another limitation was the absence of Tunisian-specific utility values; we used data from the literature and health economic models.

Conclusion

This study showed that compared with screening alone, the implementation of HPV vaccination in addition to the current cytology screening programme in Tunisia could be considered cost-effective on the basis of the threshold GDP per capita. However, it is up to policy-makers to determine according to other public health priorities if this intervention is considered cost effective. Nevertheless, improvement of the screening programme itself also remains important and provides further potential to achieve optimal cervical cancer prevention strategies. An optimal strategy for unvaccinated women would be to implement organised screening in the target age group while using HPV DNA testing and to improve coverage of the screening.
