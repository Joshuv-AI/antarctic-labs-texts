Robot-assisted tracheal carina resection and reconstruction for adenoid cystic carcinoma: a case report

Background

Tracheal adenoid cystic carcinoma (TACC) is a slow-growing primary tracheal malignancy. Surgical resection is one of the essential treatments for primary tracheal tumors [1, 2]. Thoracotomy has traditionally been the standard approach for tracheal surgery, however, the utilization of robotic-assisted surgery has been shown to significantly reduce the technical complexity associated with minimally invasive carinal reconstruction. Another major challenge of this procedure is the airway management. Conventional intubation strategies, such as cross-field ventilation, may compromise the operative field and result in inadequate ventilation or airway injury. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) provides effective respiratory support and facilitates the performance of complex tracheal procedures. We report a case of robot-assisted carinal resection and reconstruction performed under support of ECMO.

Case presentation

A 48-year-old male patient who complained of cough for six months was admitted to the Thoracic Surgery Department of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. Cervical and thoracic computed tomography (CT) revealed a tracheal mass (Fig. 1a and b). Bronchoscopy demonstrated a tumor involving tracheal carina, extending 3.5 cm proximally into the lower trachea and 0.5 cm distally into the left main bronchus (Fig. 1c). Histopathological examination confirmed tracheal adenoid cystic carcinoma (TACC). The patient had a 30-year smoking history. Pulmonary function tests demonstrated mild ventilatory impairment (forced expiratory volume in 1 s [FEV1], 2.35 L; FEV1% predicted, 72%). Following multidisciplinary evaluation and after obtaining informed consent, the patient was scheduled to undergo robot-assisted carinal resection and reconstruction under veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO) support.

Anesthesiologic and ECMO management

Considering the patient’s pulmonary function and preference for a minimally invasive approach, VV-ECMO was selected instead of conventional ventilation strategies, such as cross-field ventilation or high-frequency ventilation. The patient was placed in a supine position and a single-lumen endotracheal tube was positioned under bronchoscopic guidance proximal to the tracheal lesion. A 5-Fr vascular sheath (Terumo, Japan) was inserted into the right femoral vein and the right internal jugular vein, respectively. Following intravenous administration of unfractionated heparin (1400 IU), a guidewire was advanced through the vascular sheath after 3 min. Given the anticipated short duration of ECMO support and to minimize bleeding risk, a single low-dose heparin regimen (20 IU/kg) was used for anticoagulation. After progressive dilation using a Maquet dilator, a 21-Fr heparin-coated cannula (Medtronic, USA) was inserted into the right femoral vein to a depth of 45 cm. A 17-Fr heparin-coated cannula was inserted into the right internal jugular vein to a depth of 13 cm. The cannulas were connected to a heparin-coated ECMO circuit (Maquet, Germany), and a femoro-jugular cannulation configuration was established. After secure placement of the cannulas, the patient was repositioned to the left lateral decubitus position, and ECMO support was initiated. The ECMO system was set at a centrifugal pump speed of 3500 rpm, with a blood flow of approximately 3.8 L/min. The sweep gas flow was maintained at 4 L/min with an oxygen fraction of 100%. Once adequate gas exchange was achieved with ECMO, mechanical ventilation was temporarily suspended.

After completion of the procedure, mechanical ventilation was resumed with standard settings, and the sweep gas to the ECMO circuit was discontinued. The patient remained hemodynamically stable. After 15 min of observation, arterial blood gas parameters remained satisfactory, and the ECMO cannulas were subsequently removed. Intraoperative arterial blood gas analysis showed a PaO₂ range of 82–310 mmHg, a PaCO₂ range of 36–44 mmHg, and oxygen saturation consistently above 95%.

Surgical procedures

The procedure involved carinal resection, followed by end-to-end anastomosis between the proximal trachea and left main bronchus, and subsequent end-to-side anastomosis of the right main bronchus to the trachea. The da Vinci Xi Surgical System (Intuitive Surgical, USA) was configured using a three-port approach, as previously described in our previous description of robot-assisted thoracic surgery [3]. One incision served as a combined camera port and assistant access. The robot was docked on the back side of the patient, with arms No. 2, 3 and 4 activated, while arm No. 1 remained inactive. A 3-cm incision was created in the fifth intercostal space (ICS) at the midaxillary line for the camera port (arm No. 3) and assistant access. And a wound protector was applied. Two additional ports were placed at the third ICS along the anterior axillary line for arm NO. 4 and at the ninth ICS along the subscapular line for arm NO. 2. The azygos vein was divided, and lymph node dissection was performed at stations 2, 4, 7, and 10. After taping both the trachea and carina, the trachea was sectioned at the right main bronchus near the carina. The tumor, along with a segment of the lower trachea and left main bronchus, was resected. Negative resection margins were confirmed by intraoperative frozen-section analysis. The left main bronchus was sutured to the lower trachea using two 3–0 barbed sutures placed in a semicontinuous fashion (Fig. 2a). An orifice was created on the lateral wall of the trachea. End-to-side anastomosis between the right main bronchus and trachea was then performed using 3–0 barbed sutures in a semicontinuous manner (Fig. 2b). No air leakage was observed after the underwater testing. The total operating time was 440 min, with an estimated blood loss of 100 mL. Two chin-to-chest sutures were placed at the end of the procedure to reduce anastomotic tension.

Postoperative management

The patient was extubated in the operating room. After a brief stay in the post-anesthesia care unit, the patient was transferred to the general ward without requiring intensive care unit admission. In the general ward, the patient was maintained in a neck-flexed position to reduce tension on the anastomosis. The chest tube was removed on postoperative day 4 after the following criteria were met: (1) adequate oxygen saturation; (2) clear drainage without blood or chyle; (3) no evidence of air leak; and (4) satisfactory lung expansion on chest radiography. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 5. Histopathological examination confirmed TACC, with negative resection margins and no lymph node metastasis at stations 2, 4, 7, and 10. At the 1-month follow-up, CT and bronchoscopy demonstrated a well-healed and patent anastomosis without evidence of stenosis or recurrence (Fig. 1d, e and f). During the subsequent 2-year follow-up period, no anastomotic stenosis or tumor recurrence was observed.

Discussions

Primary tracheal tumors are rare, accounting for approximately 0.1–0.4% of all malignancies [4]. TACC represents one of the most common histological subtypes of tracheal tumors. When the tumor involves the tracheal carina, carinal resection and reconstruction remains the primary surgical intervention. This procedure is technically demanding and requires precise airway management and advanced reconstructive techniques. Our institution is a regional thoracic surgery center, with an annual surgical volume of over 10,000 cases. Among these, more than 20 tracheal surgeries are performed annually. Extensive experience in managing complex tracheal tumors contributed to the safety and feasibility of this procedure. Robot-assisted carinal reconstruction remains technically challenging and has been infrequently reported in the literature [5–8]. The reconstruction strategy depends on tumor location and extent. In the present case, the tumor predominantly involved the carina and left main bronchus. Accordingly, end-to-end anastomosis was performed between the left main bronchus and distal trachea, followed by end-to-side anastomosis of the right main bronchus to the trachea.

VV-ECMO support improves intraoperative ventilation stability and enhances surgical flexibility during carinal resection and reconstruction. Conventional ventilation strategies for tracheal surgery primarily included cross-field ventilation and high-frequency ventilation [9]. However, these approaches may result in hypercapnia and airway injury, and the presence of an endotracheal tube in the operative field can hinder tracheal anastomosis and increase the risk of air leakage. Non-intubated spontaneous ventilation has also been explored in selected centers for tracheal resection [10]. However, its ventilation efficiency may be inadequate, particularly in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and cardiac insufficiency. Previous studies have suggested that ECMO may be considered in complex tracheal reconstruction [11]. Compared with these approaches, ECMO provides more stable gas exchange and minimizes interference from airway instrumentation [12]. Nevertheless, ECMO has inherent limitations, including risks of bleeding and thrombosis, as well as increased cost. In this case, the patient had impaired pulmonary function, and the economic burden was mitigated by comprehensive medical insurance coverage. ECMO support was discontinued immediately after completion of the procedure, and the short duration of use, combined with a low-dose heparinization strategy, was not associated with vascular injury, thrombosis, or hemorrhagic complications. Postoperatively, the patient was managed in the general ward without intensive care unit admission, which may further reduce healthcare costs. For elderly patients with multiple comorbidities (e.g., coronary artery disease or COPD), as well as those undergoing complex procedures such as extended carinal reconstruction, VV-ECMO represents a feasible and effective ventilation strategy during tracheal surgery.

The Da Vinci robotic system has significant advantages in complex reconstruction surgeries involving tracheal carina. Although thoracotomy remains the typical approach of tracheal resection and reconstruction, several experiences has proved the safety, flexibility and efficiency of robotic approach. The anastomosis during the surgery and the operations in the narrow space are very convenient, which shortens the learning curve for minimally invasive tracheal reconstruction. To our knowledge, reports of robot-assisted carinal reconstruction under ECMO support remain limited. Gonzalez reported an older female patient with tracheal squamous cell carcinoma underwent robot-assisted carina reconstruction under ECMO support [6]. The surgery was performed through a single surgical incision using the Da Vinci XI system. The Da Vinci XI system was not specifically designed for single-port robotic surgery, and there was interference between the robotic arms during the procedure, which increased the surgical difficulty. Spaggiari reported another case of a young female patient who underwent end-to-end tracheal anastomosis with ECMO assistance (without involving carinal reconstruction) [13]. This surgery utilized the conventional 4-arm approach. In this case, one incision was made for both the robot camera port and assistant access, further reducing the trauma. The anastomosis was not disturbed by the cross-field endobronchial tube. The successful completion of this case also confirms that the universal incision proposed by our center for robotic thoracic surgery is applicable to various surgical procedures including esophageal, pulmonary (including carinal reconstruction), and mediastinal tumors, and has certain promotional potential.

Based on our previous experience and findings presented in other reports, two 3–0 barbed sutures placed in a semicontinuous fashion were optimal for tracheal anastomosis in this case. Prolene sutures are widely used for airway anastomosis due to their strength and durability. However, due to the lack of haptic feedback in robotic systems, Prolene sutures may be prone to tearing during knot tying or traction, whereas barbed sutures may provide better handling in this context. Moreover, barbed sutures are absorbable, which may reduce foreign body reaction at the anastomotic site. The three-dimensional visualization and enhanced instrument precision provided by the robot system facilitate meticulous suturing. Robotic assistance allows for more precise suture placement with fewer adjustments during tracheal anastomosis [14]. In this case, the anastomosis was completed smoothly without technical difficulty.

This report has some limitations. This study is a single-center case report. The robotic surgical approach and ECMO management strategy described herein require further validation before broader clinical implementation. Furthermore, the absence of a control group limits the generalizability of the results. The heterogeneity of indications and the high cost of ECMO limit the feasibility of large comparative studies between ECMO-supported and non-ECMO procedures. Nevertheless, this report provides preliminary evidence supporting ECMO combined with robotic assistance for complex airway reconstruction in patients with impaired pulmonary function. Future prospective multicenter studies are needed to further define optimal indications and refine perioperative management strategies.

Conclusion

Our experience suggests that robot-assisted carinal reconstruction with ECMO support is a feasible and effective approach. Further validation in larger cohorts and across multiple centers is required to confirm its safety, feasibility, and broader applicability.
