The impact of GenAI on applicant behaviour, performance, and interview reliability during virtual interviews for medical school admissions
Authors: Kevin W Eva, Seanna Martin, Catherine Macala, Shahin Shirzad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs artificial intelligence becomes increasingly powerful and accessible, education programs must guard against risks during student selection. Chief among those is avoidance of rewarding applicants who use prohibited tools. The trend towards increased use of virtual interviews makes programs particularly susceptible. To empirically study the risk and mitigation strategies, we conducted a randomized experiment comparing preparatory behaviors, performance, reliability and acceptability for candidates encouraged to surreptitiously use ChatGPT relative to two control groups. No advantages were observed (ChatGPT group mean = 3.67 (sd = 0.69) vs 3.74 (sd = 0.61) for ‘usual practice’ controls and 3.73 (sd = 0.80) for participants who were foretold station content). Reducing the time interviewees had to engage with ChatGPT off camera did not harm performance (3.73 (sd = 0.58) vs 3.70 (sd = 0.81) for controls); precision of test scores (SEMeas = 0.34 vs 0.39); or acceptability ratings (mean = 4.0 vs 4.1). These findings suggest an easy way to increase confidence in the fairness of virtual interviews.Subject terms: Education; Information systems and information technology; Mathematics and computing; Psychology; Psychology; Science, technology and society

Introduction

The landscape of selection for admission to medical education programs has undergone substantial transformation in recent years1–3. Particularly prevalent has been a shift towards virtual formats to reduce the expenses, time pressures, infectious disease transmission and environmental impacts that accompany traveling for in-person interviews4–7. Technological advances enable greater capacity to transition in this direction, but may lessen confidence that the data gathered reflect the information the educational program seeks8–11. That is, as videoconferencing tools prevent interviewers from observing everything to which applicants have access and generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) becomes increasingly robust, we must increasingly worry about whether we are measuring what we intend to measure or rewarding misconduct12,13. To consider the validity of virtual interviews14, and enable programs to make evidence-informed policy, it is critical that we understand how access to GenAI changes the behavior and performance of individual applicants and of the interview process itself. Therefore, we conducted an experiment to (a) investigate the impact of applicants’ access to GenAI during virtual interviews; and (b) test strategy for mitigating against any benefits it enables.

We performed this exploration in the context of a Multiple Mini-Interview (MMI)15. Developed and becoming more prominent in the last two decades, MMIs offer an interview strategy that enables larger numbers of raters to contribute their perspective on applicants with feasible and finite resources16,17. What is focused upon depends on the priorities and practices of the institution responsible for implementation18,19, but MMIs are generally used to provide a thorough assessment of applicants’ non-academic skills (e.g., ethical reasoning, communication skills, or professionalism)20,21. The key design feature that has enabled better reliability and better prediction than traditional panel interviews is the sampling inherent in requiring as many people as possible to independently interact with and judge each applicant’s performance22–24. As a result, MMIs are typically run as a series of short (5- to 8-min) interactions between an applicant and an interviewer with a brief (2-min) break between each interaction to allow examiners to score the preceding candidate and candidates to prepare for their next interview25. Reports of MMIs being conducted prior to widespread access to GenAI indicate that conducting interviews in person or virtually has little impact on their utility26–28.

Since then, however, direct translation of the original, in-person, MMI structure to virtual formats has inadvertently created opportunity for misuse of GenAI. During the 2-min space between interactions, applicants have typically been presented with a scenario that would set the focus for their next interview (for examples, see ref. 15). In-person, it is easy to monitor that applicants are doing nothing more than reading the scenario and thinking about their next task. In virtual settings, it is near impossible to ensure that candidates do not enter (manually or by dictation) interview questions into generative AI tools for guidance to help pre-formulate responses, undermining the authenticity and spontaneity that MMIs are designed to assess. Not all applicants will do so, of course, but considered in terms of the elements of Cressey’s “fraud triangle”—Pressure, Rationalization, and Opportunity—the three factors that combine to determine whether or not people engage in cheating29–31, there is no question that medical school applicants feel great pressure and can easily rationalise that other applicants will take an unfair advantage from using GenAI. Combined, those factors create a situation that is ripe for leading candidates to avail themselves of the opportunity that time away from the eyes of others provides during typical MMI implementation.

From the perspective of candidate behavior, it is the perception of unfair advantage through GenAI use that may be a particularly powerful prompt towards cheating behavior32. From the perspective of educational programs though, we should not assume that GenAI provides an advantage given that there are as many reasons to believe surreptitious use of GenAI can hurt interview outcomes as there are reasons to believe it helps. Having rapid access to the world’s knowledge (however biased the large language model happens to be) seems, at face value, likely to be beneficial, but the gains offered could easily be offset by the cognitive and logistical challenges of incorporating the information derived into a real-time conversation without being caught or giving an unnatural element to the interview. Given that state of equipoise, knowing whether actual benefit accrues is critically important because efforts to police applicant behavior can be costly and inaccurate. Further, worry about incapacity to weed out individuals who use prohibited technologies can unnecessarily lead to abandonment of tools that otherwise improve selection decisions.

To address the issue empirically, we randomized participants in a virtual MMI (vMMI) to one of 3 conditions: a standard practice control group that was given typical instructions to not use any external resources during the interview; an intervention group that was instructed to familiarize themselves with ChatGPT and prepare to use it (surreptitiously) during the interview; and a second, test breach, control group that was given the scenarios and initial questions that would be asked during the interview one week in advance. All three groups were further divided, again randomly, to test whether any observed benefits of having access to ChatGPT or test questions can be effectively neutralized through changes to vMMI administration: half of all participants in each group participated in a vMMI using the standard procedure outlined above (2 min of preparation time, alone, followed by an 8 min interview); the other half completed a modified vMMI in which examiners had 1 min to score the preceding candidate while the next candidate awaited further instruction. Interviews in the modified condition began with an examiner reading the station prompt directly to the candidate rather than candidates having time to reflect on the prompt in isolation. Outcomes examined include participant self-report of the preparation undertaken; scores assigned to applicant performance, reliability and precision of the interview process as a means of consistently differentiating between applicants, and applicant/examiner opinions regarding the fairness and acceptability of the interview process. While most experiments of this type would specify primary vs secondary outcomes, we present our results in chronological sequence (i.e., before, during or after the vMMI) because validation is a matter of looking at the broad swath of evidence to inform decisions about whether scores resulting from the assessment are fit for the purpose to which they are put14.

Results

Sample characteristics

Due to 2 last minute cancellations and technical difficulty or interviewer cancellation preventing 2 individuals from completing at least 4 stations, N = 140 of the 144 enrolled participants were included in our analyses. N = 86 (61.4%) reported being female; 48 (34.3%) reported being male; 4 (2.9%) identified themselves as non-binary; and 2 (1.4%) did not disclose their gender. N = 37 (26.4%) reported their ethnicity as Caucasian; 28 (20.0%) reported Chinese ethnicity; 26 (18.6%) identified themselves as South Asian; 7 (5.0%) reported Persian ethnicity; and all other identifiers were used fewer than 5 times. N = 119 (85.0%) reported dominantly growing up in an urban or suburban setting; 11 (7.9%) grew up rurally; 8 (5.7%) lived in a mix of sub/urban and rural settings; and 2 (1.4%) did not disclose the location of their dominant residence. N = 107 (76.4%) declared their dominant academic background to be science; 13 (9.3%) did not disclose the focus of their formal training; 6 (4.3%) studied science and art; 6 (4.3%) applied from another health profession (nursing or pharmaceutical sciences); 4 (2.9%) studied engineering; 3 (2.1%) studied business; and 1 (0.7%) participant had a purely arts academic background.

Preparing for the vMMI

N = 125 participants (89.2% of the sample) completed a post-study survey. On it, four questions were asked regarding how much time they spent in preparatory activities for the vMMI (see Table 1). Outliers were identified and removed if responses were greater than 3 standard deviations from the mean to ensure a small number of participants did not have a disproportionate effect on the findings. Despite this resulting in removal of a few very large values and despite there being no real stakes involved for applicants, responses suggested that participants took the task seriously as they reported spending nearly a total of 2.5 h (143.6 min) preparing to be interviewed, on average. Performance during the vMMI

To encourage interviewers to identify if they suspected inappropriate behavior on the part of candidates without disclosing the experimental intervention, the rating form asked interviewers to indicate, with a check box and a short response, if they had any concerns about the candidate. That box was checked 6 times, mostly to note a candidate who was not present. Only once was a behavioral concern identified (“At the 2:00 [minute] mark I said I had no further questions; applicant had nothing to add, but started typing, which raised concerns about cheating”). That interviewee, however, was not in the ChatGPT condition, suggesting that applicants were able to successfully hide any use of ChatGPT.

3 × 2 ANOVA performed on the ratings assigned revealed no main effect of Instruction (F < 1, p > 0.8) no main effect of Timing (F < 1, p > 0.7), and a significant interaction between the two variables (F = 2.7, p < 0.1). Post-hoc tests to account for the interaction, performed separately on the standard 2 + 8 timing condition and the modified 1 + 9 condition revealed that the ChatGPT group performed significantly worse than both controls in the 1 + 9 condition (F = 3.2, p < 0.05), whereas no statistically significant differences arose in the 2 + 8 condition (F < 1, p > 0.4). Utility of the vMMI

With respect to the reliability of the vMMI (i.e., the tool’s capacity to consistently differentiate those who scored highly from those who were rated less so), the internal consistency, reflecting the correlation between questions on the rating form, was high (Cronbach’s alpha > 0.90) in each experimental condition.

The inter-station reliability, reflecting the consistency of ratings from one station to the next, was similarly consistent when the data were examined as a function of Instruction, but highly variable when the data were examined as a function of Timing (Table 4). Because the number of stations is always a determinant of an MMI’s overall reliability, we report not only the observed reliability, but what can be expected (using the Spearman-Brown prophecy formula) from these data had a 12-station vMMI been conducted (thereby allowing comparison to the psychometrics reported in the literature for the original MMI protocol). All three instructional conditions revealed comparable reliabilities, suggesting that the vMMI differentiated between applicants equally well regardless of experimental condition. All three reliabilities, in fact, are precisely within the range reported previously for 12-station MMIs. The modified timing (1 + 9), however, appeared to have a deleterious effect on the consistency of the ratings compared to that observed in the standard (2 + 8) timing condition (Table 4).

To examine the extent to which these patterns reflect differences in the variability of performances within experimental condition (i.e., the standard deviations illustrated in Table 3) as opposed to test precision, we calculated the Standard Error of Measurement (SEmeas). SEmeas describes “the likely accuracy of the score achieved by a randomly chosen candidate,” which is appropriate for our purposes because admissions decisions are normative rather than needing to test the adequacy of a particular pass score33,34. The lower the SEmeas the more precise the measure. Separating reliability from the variability in the sample in this way revealed much more consistency in the performance of the vMMI across experimental condition (Table 4).

In terms of acceptability to users, the post-MMI survey responses revealed high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.82), leading us to treat the average as a general marker of satisfaction with the experience. No differences were observed between groups, their average rating being 4.0 or 4.1 in all cases.

Additionally, applicants in the groups that had access to ChatGPT or the test questions were asked about the extent to which they agreed with two statements specific to the resource they were provided: (1) [The resource] was helpful; and (2) I was able hide my access. Those in the modified timing condition (1 + 9) were less positive about the use of ChatGPT relative to those in the standard timing condition (2 + 8) (F > 4, p < 0.05 for both statements) whereas no such differences were seen regarding access to the test questions (F < 1, p > 0.3 for both statements) (Table 5).

Similarly, interviewers were asked to complete a post-vMMI survey. N = 57 of the 70 (81.4%) who completed the study responded, outlining their satisfaction with the vMMI, their perceptions of applicant behavior, and their capacity to gather the information they needed to offer a judgment on candidate performance. Interviewers, unbeknownst to them, saw candidates from all three Instruction conditions, so only Timing comparisons are possible. As with applicants, the questions asked were highly correlated with one another (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.86), suggesting the average to be a Gestalt indication of their satisfaction with the vMMI. Both those who examined candidates in the standard 2 + 8 timing and those who examined in the modified 1 + 9 timing were equally satisfied as the mean ratings they provided were 4.1 (on a 5-point Likert-scale) in both groups.

Finally, in terms of predicting scores on an actual MMI, conducted for real selection decisions, our analyses were limited because only N = 43 participants were invited to interview for entry into the MD program. All but 2 of those consented to having their scores matched to study data, but N = 41 is too small a number to allow the correlation to be examined on a condition-by-condition basis. Nonetheless, the overall correlation provides a manipulation check, providing confirmation that the data collected for this study are reflective of what can be expected in an actual high-stakes admissions context. In this regard, it is reassuring that the observed correlation between our vMMI and actual MMI scores was r = 0.54, which translates to a disattenuated r = 0.66 had a full 12-station vMMI actually been conducted, according to the Spearman-Brown prophecy formula.

Discussion

As society and technology evolve, healthcare and healthcare provider selection processes must adapt35,36. Doing so is partly a matter of contemplating and revising the competencies we prioritize, but also requires continuous monitoring of our admissions practices and their utility37,38. Exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic, but precipitated by schools’ desires to offer a cost-effective, student-centered, and environmentally friendly process7,8, most medical education programs in Canada transitioned to virtual interviews prior to the emergence of widespread access to generative artificial intelligence. While unanticipated, the combination of virtual interviews and GenAI creates a validity threat that cannot be ignored.

One reasonable response to the perceptions of unfairness that arise when some applicants are suspected of using GenAI is to even the playing field by changing interview protocols such that every applicant is welcomed to use the technology. That might seem extreme and could have unintended consequences related to inequity given differential access to technology and the likelihood that some applicants resist engagement with AI for various reasons. However, one cannot deny that GenAI is becoming more fully embedded in our daily lives and healthcare alike. Already it is easily arguable that not incorporating technology well can have negative repercussions for patient-centered care39–41. Fluency with technology is, thus, an important competence that will determine the quality of care offered by future providers, as evidenced by criticisms expressed about some physicians focusing too much on their computer during patient interactions as they begin to incorporate electronic health records and AI into clinical practice42–44. Can we use GenAI during live-time conversations with interviewers as a means for candidates to demonstrate proficiency at integrating personal skills (e.g., empathy and teamwork) with technology usage? In psychometric terms, posing such questions amounts to considering if capacity with GenAI should be a defining feature health professional programs need to nurture, as early as student selection, rather than being a “construct irrelevant” factor that may only benefit those brazen and skilled enough to use it surreptitiously.

It is for each program to decide whether such fluency is high enough priority to warrant such a shift. The possibility, however, is a good reminder that one can never claim a tool (interview or otherwise) to be “valid” without specifying the purpose to which resulting scores are put14,38. For those programs ready to make such a change, the data presented in this report should be reassuring because encouraging a group of applicants to use ChatGPT did not have a discernible impact on the utility of a virtual MMI—its acceptability and precision of measurement were maintained. For those programs (which we suspect to be in the majority) that are not ready to make such a dramatic change, continued effort to guard against offering benefit (even if just perceived) to those who use GenAI surreptitiously is required. The research included in this report is, thus, crucial for maintaining the validity and fairness of medical school admissions processes in an increasingly digital and AI-enhanced world. In this regard, a variety of findings are noteworthy.

The benefits one might reasonably assume to be gained from having access to ChatGPT did not materialize. That is, those who were encouraged to use the technology in standard timing conditions were not statistically different from those in the other two instructional conditions. Insight into why may come from the observation that the test-breach condition similarly did not yield performance benefit. This finding is consistent with a series of studies demonstrating that pre-planning responses does not improve MMI scores45,46. That has, presumably, been a robust pattern because MMI stations are generally designed to be conversational, requiring the applicant to be responsive to the discussion rather than simply reciting a rehearsed answer. If anything less was required, programs could more simply ask candidates to write essays. In such discussion-based contexts, if pre-planning responses is insufficient, incorporating advice from genAI technology in live time is likely to be an even more difficult task. As such, worry over cheating with ChatGPT may be inflated.

That said, perceptions often matter more than reality. With that in mind, the findings reported in this article lead us to conclude it to be wise to eliminate time in vMMIs for candidates to review station content while alone. Unlike the perceived benefits of having access to interview questions in advance (Table 5), access to GenAI was deemed particularly unhelpful by applicants in the modified timing condition (i.e., when they did not have opportunity to review the content or interact with their computer separated from the eyes of their interviewer). Consistent with those perceptions, participants in the standard 2 + 8 condition more strongly agreed that they were able to hide their use of ChatGPT from their examiner (Table 5). With respect to practical implications, therefore, we use these findings to advocate for a change to typical MMI protocols when interviews are moved into a virtual space. While there does not appear to be major benefit derived from access to ChatGPT, convincing anxious candidates of that seems unlikely. It seems sensible, thus, to transition to a model more aligned with the modified 1 + 9 timing given the simplicity of removing awareness of a station’s focus until there is an examiner present and the lack of harm to the vMMI precision incurred upon doing so (see SEmeas in Table 4). We suggest, however, that the 2-min break between stations be maintained to allow examiners a comfortable amount of time to think about and complete their scoring task and to grant candidates a moment to catch their breath between interactions. Because previous research has shown 5-min interactions can yield scores that are as reliable as 8-min interactions47, a 2 min pause could be achieved by either taking the time required to present the station to candidates from the 8-min interaction period or by expanding the timing to a 2 min pause + 9 min interaction model. Movement towards having examiners present their station focus in live time would constrain station foci to tasks that can be conveyed fairly efficiently, but presenting clear, straightforward and unambiguous instruction should be considered a marker of an MMI’s validity in any case25.

More research is required to confirm these findings and interpretations because this study has limitations. Chief among them is that the stakes inherent in applicant performance in this study were non-existent and the study was dependent on volunteers, creating risk that participants did not perform or prepare as they would for a real MMI. That was necessary as we could not ethically mount a randomized experimental design in the context of a real admissions process. Concern, however, that candidates did not take the task seriously is offset by their claims to have spent 2.5 h readying for the exercise and anecdotal observations of how eager participants were to receive feedback on their performance (which was not given). It is also important to note that we have no way of knowing the extent to which candidates in the ChatGPT condition tried to incorporate the use of GenAI into their interviews. Again anecdotally, some expressed reservation to do so, presumably because they have been warned many times throughout their education against claiming work from ChatGPT as their own. In that regard, this trial should be considered an “intention-to-treat” study with plausible variability of engagement with GenAI being reflective of divergent comfort levels in the real applicant pool. And, as GenAI technology evolves rapidly it will be necessary to engage ongoing monitoring and the impact modern developments may have on these findings.

Strengths of the study include the large and representative sample, its randomized and blinded nature, close mimicking of real-world vMMI procedures, and the fact that this is the first project to move beyond speculation about the dangers of GenAI to report actual empirical findings. The results are fairly parsimonious and lead us to the conclusion that the widespread availability of GenAI need not be considered a fatal blow to the utility of virtual interviewing. Through careful management and procedural adaptations, admissions offices can raise confidence that their vMMI protocols are fair and trustworthy without fundamentally altering the cost or utility of completing such exercises.

Study design and setting

We conducted a randomized, controlled, 3 (Instructions) × 2 (Timing) factorial experiment to examine the effects of (a) having access to GenAI and (b) a procedural modification. Outcomes included candidate behavior, candidate performance, and the utility (reliability, Standard Error of Measurement, and acceptability) of a vMMI. The study took place in November 2024 using a secure videoconferencing platform owned and operated by Kira Talent, a commercial on-line platform for admissions interviews and assessments.

Eligibility criteria

Participants were medical school applicants to the University of British Columbia (UBC) Undergraduate MD Program’s 2025 admissions cycle. No other inclusion or exclusion criteria were applied. At the time of study, applicants had not yet been told whether they would be invited to interview for the program.

Recruitment and consent

All applicants were invited, via email sent by the MD Admissions Team, to participate in a research project being conducted at arm’s length from the admissions office. The email noted that the principal investigator (K.E.) has no role in admissions decision making at UBC and, hence, participation (or lack thereof) would have no bearing on applicants’ chance of admission. To reinforce that separation between research and selection, potential participants were offered a $10 gift certificate in appreciation of time spent completing the study. The email linked to an information sheet and a survey through which interested individuals were asked to provide contact information, to indicate dates and times they could participate, and to provide demographic information (gender, ethnicity, dominant residence, and academic background). Throughout the study, identifying information was only accessible to the PI.

The recruitment survey remained open for one week during which N = 551 responses were received. Removal of duplicates and those who did not indicate their availability reduced this number to N = 412 (13.2% of all 3111 applicants). An email was then sent by the PI to those who registered, containing a PDF copy of the consent form and a link to a second survey. That survey required candidates to consent to the study and to indicate they understood that any instructions given during the research should NOT be considered a guide to how the UBC MD program’s actual MMI would be implemented. It also gave a secondary option of consenting to participants’ research data being linked to their actual admissions file. The consent survey remained open for one week during which N = 392 responses were received. Removal of duplicates and incomplete responses reduced this number to N = 345 (83.7% of those who registered). N = 325 (94.2% of those who gave consent) agreed to having their study scores linked to their admissions file.

Interviewers were recruited from the database of examiners maintained by the MD Program’s Admissions Office. An email inviting participation was sent by the Admissions Team. The pool includes UBC faculty members, physicians working in communities throughout the province of British Columbia, current medical students and individuals from the general public who have interviewed for the MD program previously. No further inclusion or exclusion criteria were applied.

Randomization, enrollment, and blinding

The target sample size was calculated to detect a minimum effect size of d = 0.5, a moderate effect, assuming α = 0.05 and 80% power, using statskingdom.com for ANOVA with 3 groups (given that ‘Instructions’ was the primary variable of interest). The result was n = 42 (14 per group), which we tripled and then rounded up to n = 144 to account for drop-outs and create a total number of study slots that could be accommodated by running six sessions (twice a night, once a week, for three weeks) if 4 parallel circuits of a 6-station vMMI were completed each session.

To assign consenting participants to a session and experimental condition, a spreadsheet was created that listed study dates and start times chronologically, ensured two circuits were assigned to each of the Timing conditions (detailed below) every evening, and counterbalanced the order in which 2 participants from each of 3 GenAI Instruction conditions (detailed below) were encountered by examiners in every circuit.

Consenting participants were then assigned a random number using Excel and sorted based on that number. Working from top to bottom, participants were added into the first row of the assignment spreadsheet that aligned with their stated availability until all 144 slots were filled (see Fig. 1). An email was then sent to each individual participant three weeks in advance of the study, alerting them to their scheduled participation time. When cancellations occurred, the next available participant in the randomly sorted list was enrolled.

Flow diagram illustrating how applicants (read top to bottom) and interviewers (read bottom to top) were recruited and assigned to each experimental condition.

Applicants were enrolled in 1 of 6 conditions (3 Instructions × 2 Timings) whereas interviewers were enrolled in 1 of 2 timing conditions but saw applicants from all 3 instruction conditions.

To staff 4 tracks of 6-station MMIs, 24 examiners were required for each of the 3 evenings. The first 72 volunteer examiners who could be scheduled into one of the sessions were enrolled and randomly assigned to one of the two timing conditions with slight adjustments made in an effort to balance personal characteristics such as age, gender, profession and ethnic background across track (see Fig. 1). Doing so ensured a range of interviewers were encountered by each applicant, mimicking the interviewer assignment practices of the standard UBC Medical School MMI.

All participants were informed that the study was being conducted “to understand the advantages and disadvantages of different ways of administering an MMI to inform further development of MMI use in subsequent years.” Thus, the specific experimental conditions were not disclosed, leaving both applicants and examiners blinded to the study design and their allocation.

Interventions and procedure

One week prior to each applicant’s participation, they were sent an email that established the experimental condition to which they were assigned. Thus, 6 versions of the email were created to enable the 3 (GenAI Instructions) × 2 (Timing) structure of the study.

With respect to the Instructions variable, 1/3 of all participants received each of the following instructions:

Standard practice control: “It is important that you do everything as you would expect to do during an actual MMI. That is, no outside materials are allowed; you may not consult with anyone during your interview process; and, it is expected that you treat the process and content of the stations confidentially.”

ChatGPT Access: “Unlike an actual MMI, we encourage you to use https://chatgpt.com to help you perform to the best of your ability while interacting with the interviewer. If you have not used ChatGPT before, spend some time this week familiarizing yourself with the website and contemplating how you can use it effectively during your interview to help make your answers stronger. You should NOT, however, disclose to interviewers that you are using ChatGPT either directly or via nonverbal communication. It is important to make your interview and interactions as natural as possible because the interviewer is not aware that you will be using ChatGPT. Finally, please note that it is expected that you treat the process and content of the stations confidentially.”

Test breach control: “Unlike an actual MMI, we are providing you with the list of stations to help you perform to the best of your ability while interacting with the interviewer. Please spend some time this week familiarizing yourself with the focus of the interviews and contemplating how you can use the attached information effectively during your interview to help make your answers stronger. You should NOT, however, disclose to interviewers that you had access to the questions, so it is important to make your interview and interactions as natural as possible. Finally, please note that it is expected that you treat the process and content of the stations confidentially.”

With respect to the Timing variable, 1/2 of all participants (within each Instructions condition) received each of the following instructions:

Standard timing: “On <date and time>, your mock interview will begin. It will consist of 6 MMI stations, each comprised of a 2-min opportunity to review a scenario that will be presented to you followed by 8 min of interaction with an interviewer.”

Modified timing: “On <date and time>, your mock interview will begin. It will consist of 6 MMI stations, each comprised of a 9-min interaction with an interviewer that will begin with them reading the focus of the station to you.”

The next day, all participants were sent an email that explained how to register with Kira Talent, how to test their camera and microphone, and how to complete a practice interview (all per normal vMMI procedures at UBC).

Examiner training

A week prior to the interviews, examiners received a 6-page instructional manual and attended an hour-long training session offered via Zoom, aligned with UBC’s standard training protocols for MMI interviewers. Both the document and the live presentation detailed the logistics of the MMI, the role of the interviewer, and how the evaluation process was to take place. Questions were answered and examiners were encouraged to login to Kira Talent to ensure they were prepared for the interview to which they were assigned. Separate training sessions were held, facilitated by the Admissions Team (C.M. and S.M.), for examiners assigned to the standard timing condition vs those assigned to the modified timing condition to avoid confusion about the assigned protocol and disclosure of there being a timing manipulation embedded in the study.

vMMI stations and scoring

Each participant completed 6 stations, within their assigned condition, drawn from the UBC MD Program’s database of previously used cases. Selection considerations included that the station had not been used recently in an actual MMI; that it remained relevant to all applicants; that it was sufficiently short and straightforward that the instruction could be read verbally within 1 min; and that an array of competencies, as defined by the program database, were represented. In the end, 2 autobiographically focused stations were chosen along with 2 stations requiring quote interpretation, and 2 stations demanding discussion about a current issue.

As per the norm in MMI development, each station consisted of (a) instructions for the candidate, (b) one page of detailed instructions for the interviewer (including a list of questions that could be asked to facilitate conversation), and (c) 1–2 pages of background and theory to ensure the interviewer had some grounding in the content to be discussed.

The scoresheet asked interviewers to assess candidate performance in keeping with UBC’s standard medical school MMI procedures. Interviewers also assigned an overall performance score, using 6-point scales ranging from 1 = Weak to 6 = Exceptional. An evaluation criteria document was provided to examiners to detail what constituted strength of performance within domain, again adhering to UBC’s standard MMI protocol.

Outcomes

The outcomes for this study fall into three categories: Candidate behavior prior to the vMMI; candidate performance during the vMMI; and, the utility of the vMMI (i.e., its reliability, Standard Error of Measurement and acceptability).

To assess candidate behavior, a survey was administered to candidates immediately after their completion of the vMMI that asked them to indicate how much time they spent in 8 preparatory activities (measured in min), what resources they used to prepare (dichotomous yes/no), and how helpful they found those resources (using 7-point adjectival scales ranging from 1 = Very harmful to 7 = Very helpful).

To assess candidate performance, average scores were calculated for each participant across station using the examiner-completed 6-point scales described above.

To assess utility, the inter-station reliability and Standard Error of Measurement was calculated separately for each experimental condition using candidate performance scores; candidates and examiners were also asked, on post-vMMI surveys, to rate their satisfaction with the experience (using 5-point Likert scales ranging from 1 = Strongly disagree to 5 = Strongly agree); and, the correlation between scores on the study’s vMMI and the MD program’s actual vMMI, run 4 months later, was calculated for the portion of participants who were invited to interview and consented to this being done. More specifically, the post-vMMI survey asked candidates to rate the extent to which they agreed with 8 statements: (1) I was able to readily engage in the discussion; (2) I was comfortable responding to the questions asked; (3) The examiners treated me fairly; (4) The examiners treated me kindly; (5) I had enough time to gather my thoughts before each discussion began; (6) The discussion component of the interview was the right amount of time; (7) The interview questions were the right level of difficulty; (8) I was able to portray an accurate impression of my ability.

Statistical analysis

We summarized participant characteristics with descriptive statistics. Two-way ANOVAs were used to test main and interaction effects of survey and performance data with Tukey tests conducted for post-hoc comparisons. Given the stakes involved in erroneously concluding no difference, we used a liberal two-sided α = 0.1. Reliability estimates were computed separately for each experimental condition using Generalizability Theory with the Spearman-Brown formula applied48 to equate this study’s results to the standard MMI-protocol expressed in the literature49. In addition, Standard Error of Measurements were computed for each condition to separate the influence of reliability and sample variability when considering the precision of the performance scores generated33,34. All analyses were conducted using SPSS and Excel.
