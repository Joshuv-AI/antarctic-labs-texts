Barriers to integration of health and equity into urban design policies in Regina, Saskatchewan
Authors: Akram Mahani, Joonsoo Sean Lyeo, Agnes Fung, Kelly Husack, Nazeem Muhajarine, Tania Diener, Chelsea Brown
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough there is extensive literature on the impact of urban design on health, little is known about the barriers to integrating health into urban design policies. As cities increasingly lead efforts to improve health equity and population health, understanding the perspectives and experiences of municipal actors on health and equity is essential. To address this gap, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 30 stakeholders engaged with urban design policy- and decision-making at the City of Regina in Saskatchewan, Canada. We analysed our data using a qualitative thematic framework. Our research uncovered a lack of shared understanding of health among municipal actors. Interviewees identified several barriers to integrating health and equity in urban design policies, including inaccessibility of evidence; insufficient resourcing; fragmented governance structure; limited legal power of local governments in Canada; a deeply ingrained culture of individualism and lack of representation. Our findings underscore the importance of adopting an integrated and holistic approach for healthy and equitable urban design. As urbanization continues to bring a greater share of the world’s population into urban areas, it is crucial to understand how municipal governance can foster environments that promote residents’ well-being.Keywords: health equity, municipality, policy-making, urban design, urban planning, local government, built environment, social determinants of health, healthy cities

BACKGROUND

The link between health and urban design and planning has deep historical roots, dating back to the 19th century when initiatives such as segregated zoning policies were introduced to address public health concerns (Giles-Corti et al., 2016). Today, leading global organizations, such as the World Health Organization (WHO), emphasize the critical role of urban design and planning in shaping population health outcomes. The WHO advocates for embedding health and health equity into the core of urban governance and planning processes (Marmot et al., 2008). Similarly, the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goal 11 calls for the creation of inclusive, resilient, safe and sustainable cities (United Nations, 2015). In 1986, the WHO launched the Healthy Cities movement, a transformative initiative aimed at promoting population health by fostering community participation and integrating health considerations into all facets of urban planning and governance (de Leeuw, 2009; de Leeuw et al., 2015, 2020; de Leeuw and Simos, 2017a, b; Hancock, 2017, 2018; Hancock et al., 2017).

There is a growing body of evidence indicating that urban design can impact physical health (Fazeli Dehkordi et al., 2022), mental health (Galea, 2005) and social well-being (Barton et al., 2015; Lowe et al., 2019). Equally important is the emerging recognition of urban design’s role in promoting equity, as it is closely tied to the social determinants of health (SDOH) (Marmot and Health, 2007; Raphael et al., 2020). The built environment—comprising the physical spaces where people live, work and interact—(Amuda & Berkowitz, 2019), plays a complex and interdependent role in shaping population health outcomes and equity, underscoring the need for thoughtful, inclusive urban planning (Lowe et al., 2019; Pineo, 2022). However, strategies to effectively integrate health and equity considerations into urban design remain under-researched and underdeveloped (Jackson et al., 2013; Giles-Corti et al., 2015; Harris et al., 2016).

As urbanization accelerates globally, including in Canada where over 80% of the population resides in urban areas (Hancock, 2017, 2018; Hancock et al., 2017), cities are at the forefront of addressing complex health challenges such as climate change, and food insecurity (Giles-Corti et al., 2016). These challenges demand cross-sectoral collaboration, community engagement, nonpartisan political leadership (Giles-Corti et al., 2016) and a multidisciplinary approach (Burton et al., 2015; Prior et al., 2018). However, collaboration between public health and urban planning remains limited. Many city planners lack the knowledge or resources to incorporate health and equity into their policies (Lawton et al., 2017; Pineo, 2022). A case study from Vancouver illustrates this gap, where municipal officials demonstrated limited awareness of their role in reducing health inequities (Collins, 2014).

The disconnect between urban design and public health in Canadian cities is further compounded by political and economic constraints. Municipal governments often struggle to prioritize public health in their planning processes, particularly in the face of competing demands such as infrastructure development and economic growth (Barten et al., 2007; Collins, 2014; Pineo et al., 2018). This has led to urban sprawl and a reliance on private automobiles, which further exacerbates health inequities by limiting access to active transportation options (Frumkin, 2016; M. Young et al., 2016).

This study presents a case study from Regina, Saskatchewan to explore barriers to the integration of health and equity into urban design policies from the perspective of key municipal stakeholders.

Conceptual framework

This study was guided by two theoretical frameworks: policy integration and health equity. The policy integration framework (Candel & Biesbroek, 2016) provided a lens to examine how health considerations are integrated into urban design policies, identifying barriers like political, institutional and stakeholder dynamics. It also helped explore how different actors (e.g. city planners, policy-makers) navigate the complexities of urban planning and public health. The health equity lens (Whitehead, 1992; Braveman, 2014; Braveman et al., 2018) helped explore how urban design policies promote inclusive, equitable health outcomes. This framework allowed us to explore how certain urban design features (e.g. green spaces, public amenities) can either reduce or exacerbate health disparities, particularly for vulnerable populations. Together, these frameworks enabled a comprehensive analysis of both the governance and structural challenges in urban planning, and their broader implications for population health and social equity.

METHODS

We employed a qualitative case study (Yin, 2009) and followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies (COREQ) guidelines to improve the transparency and rigour of our qualitative research reporting (Tong et al., 2007; Walsh et al., 2020).

Ethical considerations

Ethics approval for this research was received from the University of Regina Research Ethics Board (project ID: 2023–109). All research participants received verbal and written information about the objectives and voluntary nature of the study. Written and verbal consent were obtained from all interviewees. The interviewer removed all identifying information from the transcripts.

Research setting

The City of Regina is the capital and second-largest city in the province of Saskatchewan, with an estimated population of 226 404 (Statistics Canada, 2023). Today, immigrants and Indigenous peoples account for 20.3% and 10.4% of the city’s residents, respectively (Statistics Canada, 2023) contrasting with figures from two decades ago in 2001, when these groups represented just 7.4% and 8.7% of the population (Statistics Canada., 2023).

The municipality of Regina is governed by the Regina City Council, consisting of 11 elected representatives, one Mayor and 10 City councillors (City of Regina., n.d.), who serve 4-year terms. The Mayor is elected city-wide, while City Councillors are elected by voters within their respective wards. In addition to their decision-making role, City Council appoints the City Manager, who is accountable to the City Council and oversees the four divisions of the City of Regina’s operations. The City Manager and the divisions, collectively form the City Administration, responsible for day-to-day municipal operations.

In this study, ‘decision-makers’ refer to elected officials responsible for choosing policy directions and priorities, deciding which policies will be translated into tangible action by the municipal government (Morestin, 2020). This responsibility primarily falls onto the City Council and the Mayor. The term ‘policy-makers’ refers to public servants responsible for developing policies in accordance with the mandate chosen by decision-makers (Morestin, 2020). In Regina, this work falls onto the City Administration. While policy-makers can make recommendations, submit proposals and be delegated some decision-making, they often have comparatively little influence over the overall direction of policy relative to decision-makers. The terms ‘stakeholder’ and ‘actor’ are used in reference to both policy-makers and decision-makers.

Sampling and recruitment

We used a combination of purposive, snowball and convenience sampling strategies to recruit 30 research participants. We purposefully recruited our research participants from the four City Administration divisions City of Regina., (n.d.) including: (i) Financial Strategy & Sustainability, (ii) City Planning & Community Development, (iii) Citizen Services and (iv) the Transformation Office (see Table 1). We also included City Councillors to have decision-makers’ perspectives. Participants were eligible if they were City staff engaged with policy- and decision-making regarding urban design and urban policies related to the SDOH. The City assisted with distributing our recruitment letter among the divisions, and those interested (n = 4) contacted the first author directly (convenience). One member of our research team Advisory Board is a City staff, and thus assisted with identifying potential participants. Participants were also identified through snowball sampling through their colleagues. We also collected a list of City Councillors from the City website.

Data collection

The findings presented in this article are part of a larger study that examined the integration of health and equity into urban design policies. The first author (A.M.), a trained qualitative researcher, conducted all semi-structured interviews (n = 30) for the larger study between May and July 2023 using the video conferencing platform, Zoom. With participants’ consent, all interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim using Otter.ai, a speech-to-text software. To ensure transcription accuracy, two student research assistants reviewed the anonymous verbatim transcriptions. Each interview lasted a minimum of 60 min and was guided by a protocol developed through the expertise of the research team and a review of existing literature. This article focuses on two key topics from the interview guide: (i) definition of the health, equity and SDOH, and (ii) barriers to integration of health and equity into urban design policies. Demographic information, such as participants’ educational backgrounds and years of experience in their current roles, was collected at the start of each interview. These data provided important context for interpreting the diverse perspectives shared.

Data analysis

The interview transcripts were analysed using a qualitative thematic framework (Corbin et al., 2014) to identify recurring patterns (Braun & Clarke, 2006). The differences and commonalities between these recurring patterns were used to categorize interviewee responses into distinct themes, with each theme grouping excerpts that communicated a shared implicit or explicit message. When appropriate, themes representing broad ideas were divided into smaller sub-themes focusing on specific elements of these higher-level ideas. Themes and sub-themes formed the fundamental units of our analysis and guided the development of our explanatory conclusions. The analysis employed a combination of inductive and deductive methods (Bryman, 2015).

Two master themes, derived from the interview guide, were identified a priori: (i) definition of health, equity and SDOH, and (ii) barriers to integration of health and equity into urban design policies. Initially, we organized transcript excerpts into these pre-defined themes using a deductive approach. Subsequently, we applied a constant comparative methodology inherent to a grounded theory approach (inductive coding) to establish new emergent themes a posteriori, as needed (Corbin et al., 2014). This approach served as the basis for our inductive analysis, which involved a reflexive and flexible approach (Braun & Clarke, 2019). For instance, under the master theme of ‘definition of health and equity’, sub-themes emerged, such as ‘paying lip service to equity’ and ‘a lack of shared understanding of health’.

To further ensure accuracy and reliability, all selected illustrative quotes were validated through a member-checking process with the research participants. During this process, each quotation was presented within the context of the corresponding theme or sub-theme, and participants were asked to confirm whether the selected theme or sub-theme accurately reflected the meaning of their quote. This ensured alignment between the participants’ intended message and our thematic interpretation. While a few participants made minor edits to their quotes, all approved the selection of themes and sub-themes.

Two members of the research team (J.S.L. and A.F.) individually reviewed the interview transcripts to organize relevant excerpts into corresponding themes and sub-themes. The analysis was an iterative process involving frequent team meetings to resolve conflicts and review, revise and refine the emergent themes and sub-themes. Throughout the analysis, regular peer debriefing took place to explore whether the initial findings correspond with their expertise (Connelly, 2016). Continuous dialogues and discussions within our multidisciplinary research team, which included knowledge users from both the public health system and the municipal government, encouraged and enhanced reflexivity, trustworthiness and credibility (Braun & Clarke, 2019). We used NVIVO 12 for data analysis.

RESULTS

Characteristics of participants

Out of 65 potential participants contacted, 30 agreed to participate in the study, resulting in a 46% response rate. Participants had different roles/positions and diverse educational backgrounds. Most participants (n = 18) were from the City Planning & Community Development division, given the direct role of this division in urban planning and urban design. UNDERSTANDING OF HEALTH AND EQUITY

The interviewees’ perceptions, definitions and understandings of the concepts of health, equity and SDOH influenced the development of their urban design policies and decisions. Interviewees shared examples of how gaps in their own and their peers’ comprehension of health and equity acted as barriers to meaningful action in policy- and decision-making. Two sub-themes emerged: paying lip service to equity and lack of a shared understanding of health.

Paying lip service to equity

A common belief existed that equity was superficially integrated into policy. All interviewees acknowledged the importance of equity, but not all interviewees believed they could effectively integrate equity into their policy- or decision-making. A significant barrier to the City Administration’s integration of equity was the lack of a shared understanding of the concept. Research participants had differing interpretations of equity, resulting in differing and sometimes contradictory approaches to pursuing equity. The lack of a standardized definition for equity, or a common language to facilitate dialogue around equity, was described by one participant as being a source of conflict and friction:

And I think we can all sit in an office and decide what we think is equitable, or what we think is equal, and what we think our definitions of them are, but every human is going to have their own definition of what is equitable or equal to them. And so, I’ve actually recently kind of started to not like having even the conversation of equitable versus equal. And instead, I like to more talk about what are the choices. [P20]

Another participant noted:

The way I would characterise the dominant model of equity is everybody at the City would be aghast if they found out a particular person was without a house, because they were a queer person. But nobody is upset that there are a lot of queer people without houses for some other reasons. And so, the principle isn’t queer people should have houses or everybody should have a house, it’s nobody should lack a house simply because they’re queer. But if they just happen to lack a house and be queer, well, so be it. And I think that highlights differences in our definitions of equity, whereas I would say it’s about having this base model, which includes a house, they would say, well, as long as you’re not out of a house because of discrimination, so be it. [P26]

Many interviewees noted the importance of integrating equity into their work, although this view was not held by all members of the City Administration. Interviewees recounted instances where peers dismissed the need for integrating equity into urban planning decisions. One interviewee described how their peers brushed aside concerns about equity under the pretence that such considerations were unnecessary:

There are some people I think, who have this idea, they say: ‘well, I don’t see colour, or gender, or whatever, I just support the best person for the job’; but they don’t look at, okay, there’s systemic structural issues that are maybe creating racism. [P25]

Several interviewees were concerned that efforts to integrate equity into municipal action were of tokenism nature. They criticized policies for giving the appearance of pursuing equity without meaningfully supporting its integration. Some even argued that decision-makers recognized increasing public interest in the concept of equity, and so sought to integrate equity-based messaging to appease voters, without ensuring that these policies had a tangible impact in practice.

It does feel at times, like we’re putting forward that equity term, just to check the box, right? That’s when we run into the problems when it comes to, okay, what are we doing tactically to move this forward? Well, now we don’t know, because we haven’t really defined that well in the first place, right? And so, what does that mean? That’s always a question. [P13]

Interviewees suggested that stronger leadership was needed to overcome these barriers. Participants argued that this responsibility ultimately falls on the decision-makers, including the Mayor and City Council. Given that decision-makers are typically seen as authoritative figures within the municipal government, they are well-positioned to provide clear and consistent messaging on what the integration of equity into urban planning should entail. They can outline specific objectives and benchmarks to guide policy-makers in incorporating equity into their work. One interviewee described the need for leadership as follows:

…if leadership was prioritising equity and health and committing the resources in order to provide, like staff training and staff positions, like to be able to kind of keep the work moving, I think that would help. I think just fostering a culture throughout the corporation of this needing to be a priority. I think, in some ways that would need to come from Council and executive leadership. [P7]

Lack of a shared understanding of health

There were discrepancies in how interviewees understood the concept of health. The term ‘well-being’ was more frequently used by interviewees compared to ‘health’ or ‘wellness’, but its meaning was not consistently agreed upon. Some participants used ‘well-being’ to describe health more holistically, while others emphasized only physical aspects. When interviewees were asked to define health, the physical dimension was consistently mentioned, whereas the social dimension (i.e. social well-being) was less frequently acknowledged. For example, when describing physical health, participants often referred to characteristics that could be visually identified or quantifiably measured.

First thing that comes to my mind is just the physical well-being of a person, obviously, so any type of ailments and whatever else would contribute to poor health, but then obviously, physical characteristics like…do you have good blood pressure? Or do you have the proper vitamins, minerals… [P18]

In the few instances where the City Administration had the opportunity to engage with public health professionals (i.e. Saskatchewan Health Authority), conversations around health were mainly limited to projects that interpreted health through the lens of physical health. It was also common for participants to consider mental health an important and integral dimension of health. This widespread consideration seems to be a relatively recent development reflecting broader societal shifts in how health is understood in the public consciousness.

I think now there’s been more of a focus on mental health. But I think probably growing up, and probably till five years ago, it was really about physical health. So, you know, exercise, diet, nutrition, all that kind of stuff. But there’s probably been a real focus now, especially with the pandemic on more mental health and mental well-being. [P5]

In contrast, fewer interviewees included social well-being in their definitions of health, at least until explicitly prompted with a question asking them to define SDOH.

I think typically people think of physical health, but I very much appreciate that health is really multifaceted. [It] involves physical, mental, spiritual. Our health and our mental health can be impacted by all sorts of things and communities from recreation to social inclusion to… [P28]

Interestingly, a few interviewees referenced the WHO’s definition from 1948, which describes health as ‘a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease and infirmity’ (World Health Organization, 2006). It indicates that these participants had a more holistic understanding of the concept. However, despite these references, there was still a general lack of consensus across participants on the full scope of health and well-being. Participants also highlighted how health was interpreted differently by various stakeholders. One interviewee noted:

I’m thinking about our Council members and the decision-makers. And I think the struggle with that is an understanding of what health is, because what health is to one Councillor may be different than what it is to another. So, I think what we don’t have at the city is a good collaborative understanding of what health is. [P6]

The absence of a consistent and shared understanding of health among municipal stakeholders was described as a significant barrier to the meaningful integration of health into urban planning. Opportunities for integration are limited by the lack of shared motivation, knowledge or interest among key stakeholders.

I don’t think our [design] standards have ever really been looked at from that health perspective. And I think a lot of what drives from the developer side is the market and what people are willing to buy. [P5]

Our interviewees described the current approach of the municipal government to health as reactive rather than proactive.

One of the things that I noticed as a professional in this area is that health promotion is not really discussed. …we don’t talk about holistic, how do we promote health so that we don’t have to get to this point where we’re addressing issues. [P6]

SYSTEMIC BARRIERS TO THE INTEGRATION OF HEALTH INTO URBAN DESIGN

Interviewees identified several systemic barriers to the integration of health in urban design policies including inaccessibility of evidence; insufficient resourcing; fragmented governance structure; limited legal power of local governments in Canada; a deeply ingrained culture of individualism and lack of representation. These barriers were often entrenched and perpetuated by institutions, systems of government or wider beliefs, making them challenging to overcome through the efforts of individual stakeholders acting alone. These barriers made certain policy actions infeasible or impractical within the existing status quo.

Inaccessibility of evidence

Evidence was considered an essential building block of policy- and decision-making. Interviewees acknowledged the value of using evidence to inform policies. However, in practice, many interviewees faced significant barriers to accessing evidence, which prevented them from incorporating the best available evidence into their policies. One interviewee described how administrative barriers, namely, the lack of coordination between provincial and municipal governments, hindered the uptake of evidence in policy-making:

We were seeking data from the province on the number of licensed facilities, and they weren’t very forthcoming with even that kind of basic information. So, to me, if we want to get into more nuanced data, if we want to apply that health lens, like in a meaningful way, we really do need adequate data to back that up. And I don’t think we’re there yet in terms of relationship with the province or their willingness to provide that. [P13]

Other interviewees identified barriers in accessing the scientific evidence produced by the academic community. In some cases, these barriers were associated with prohibitive financial costs, such as when access to scientific studies was locked behind paywalls. In other cases, the scientific evidence produced by academia was described as shrouded in a ‘black box’. Several interviewees described situations where their ability to read, interpret or understand academic studies was impeded by the use of jargon and technical language.

There’s a lot of academic acumen that’s used and terminology, and it can be overwhelming, and nobody wants to walk out of a room and feel stupid. [P6]

At times, the value of using data and evidence to inform decisions and policies is not thoroughly understood by the government. To address this, one interviewee emphasized the importance of knowledge translation, which involves translating research recommendations into accessible goals for policy- and decision-makers.

…we need individuals that can distil that [academic] information, and provide it in a more palatable, and more understandable format. [P6]

Insufficient resourcing

Many interviewees described instances where they believed their capacity to enact change was hampered by resource constraints. These constraints came in various forms, with one of the most frequently discussed limitations being a shortage of funds. In Canada, these financial challenges are exacerbated by the limited revenue streams available to municipalities. Apart from transfer payments from the federal or provincial governments, which are often tied to stipulations regarding spending, municipalities mainly rely on property taxes to finance most local services and expenditures. As a result, municipalities often have minimal flexibility to levy funds for municipal projects.

They’re being asked to do more with the same. I think it all comes down to resource allocation. We put our money where we are individually, and we put our money where we think things are important to us, where our values are, and the values are not necessarily aligning with the allocation. [P6]

Other frequently mentioned resource limitations involve time and staffing shortages. However, these limitations are often directly related to funding. While many interviewees expressed encountering time constraints in policy development, these constraints were often related to the inability to indefinitely fund policy projects. Similarly, while many interviewees noted that their teams often lacked the operational capacity to fully implement policies, these limitations were often related to the lack of funds needed to hire additional staff. One interview discussed the interrelated nature of these resource limitations as follows:

Change does not happen overnight, it takes a lot of time, sometimes it takes a cultural shift within City Hall. Sometimes it takes new positions to be allocated, people specifically devoted to advancing whatever goals there are. And a lot of times, it’s just not easy to hire staff, cities get into budget crunches, and decisions are made to collapse portfolios, and combine them into others and then things kind of get watered down or kind of lost, or they start to slow down. [P27]

Siloed or fragmented governance structure

A siloed or fragmented governance structure and lack of an integrated approach to urban design were significant barriers to meaningful collaboration and coordination among policy-makers from different branches and divisions of the municipality. While siloing is a common challenge across many policy issues, it poses particular barriers to integrating health and equity into urban design policies, where multidisciplinary collaboration is essential. Stakeholders often find themselves confined to their own areas of expertise, making it difficult to leverage resources from other sectors and missing opportunities for comprehensive, multidisciplinary approaches. Siloing can also lead to varying institutional logics, priorities and cultures within and between different divisions, hindering cross-sectoral work. One interviewee highlighted how differences in terminology across disciplines can cause misunderstanding, which further hinders collaboration and effective communication:

Often, it’s just language we’re using. So, when I was working with architects, they used the word program very differently than I do as a [X] planner. And I didn’t realise that until a few weeks in and I went, oh, you’re using a program as a different term. [P6]

Interviewees also described that siloing could lead to conflicts between the objectives of different branches and divisions. One such example was the opposing objectives of active transportation planners, urban designers and landscape architects and traffic engineers. An interviewee stated that while these professionals focus on the movement of people, traffic engineers prioritize the movement of vehicles. This discrepancy, while subtle, resulted in conflicts as described by the interviewee:

Often, the natural adversary of good urban design and opportunities for active transportation is, or has been, the traffic engineer. There were lots of policies that we seem to put in place that very much favour the movement of vehicles over the movement of pedestrians, cyclists. [P1]

Limited legal power of local governments in Canada

In Canada, municipalities are often seen as ‘creatures of the province’ or ‘a third order of government’, reflecting the differences in their legal powers compared to provinces. While provinces have constitutionally guaranteed powers, municipalities derive theirs from provincial law. This means municipalities can only exercise powers delegated by the province, which can be modified or revoked by provincial governments. The ambiguity regarding the roles and responsibilities of municipalities and provincial government was reported as a source of tension and incurred costs for the municipality.

There’s disagreement with how we interact with the province and we’ve kind of gone through a whole cycle in the last two to three years of whose responsibility is this? Like, does the city have a role? But then, around what issues? We’re seeing that political narrative draws back to what it was before, because we’re just seeing that the Council’s assessing that the levying of property taxes for social issues is too much of a burden on property owners. [P4]

Facing an entrenched culture of individualism

Some interviewees highlighted barriers arising from the tension between collectivism and individualism in Canada, where individuals are often reluctant to compromise personal benefits in favour of policies that benefit the broader community. Efforts to integrate health into urban design tend to align with collectivist ideals, which emphasize collective well-being over individual preferences. However, interviewees pointed out that urban design in Canada frequently reflects individualistic ideologies, such as the prominence of urban sprawl and car-centric infrastructure, which prioritize personal convenience and autonomy over communal health and equity. One interviewee illustrated how this individualism shapes urban transportation systems:

North American transportation systems mirror that libertarian individualism, like the very existence of having a car is intrinsic to feelings of identity and liberty and the ability to sort of, like freely move at any one point. And there’s a lot of wrapped up sort of suppositions and considerations within that which are sort of strange, like I am entitled to drive anywhere, I’m always entitled to have my car, I’m always entitled to have a parking space, there is a dedicated on- street parking space for my property in front of my house. [P9]

These cultural attitudes also influence public perception of policy issues, with proposals perceived as limiting individual liberties—particularly when framed as benefiting the collective good—often facing resistance. For instance, one interviewee recalled the public backlash against a policy proposal to restrict pesticide use, which emphasized the broader environmental and public health impacts of individual actions:

With the pesticide thing, I think the PhD in biology [Biologist] was persuasive and explicit that pesticides do not want to stick to property lines, and will get onto your neighbour’s line. And it was thought in the debate: ‘That’s fine. That happens. I don’t need to consider the impact on my neighbours when I do these things, because this is my property’. [P26]

Similarly, interviewees noted that policy- and decision-makers often faced opposition when advocating for policies that benefit marginalized groups or address equity issues. Policies seen as disadvantaging even a small group of individuals, despite broader community benefits, were met with resistance. For example, one interviewee described the opposition to policies ensuring safer conditions for sex workers. The interviewee described how marginalized groups, such as sex workers, are pushed to the periphery, both physically and socially, through policies that prioritize urban aesthetics (e.g. ‘flowerpots’) over safety and well-being.

I feel like our Council right now is really grappling with the same sorts of polarised cultural issues that are impacting everybody widely. Here we are in Saskatchewan, which is quite conservative. For example, I think sex workers are mostly relegated to the industrial areas of the city, where there’s fewer police and fewer bus, public transit, and fewer lights. And there was just a conservative element on Council that was more interested in flowerpots around the city than in keeping sex workers safe. [P17]

Lack of representation

Regina is a diverse city; however, not all communities have received equitable representation in municipal policy- and decision-making. Several interviewees acknowledged that the policy- and decision-making process was often influenced by a small group of voices, typically from communities that are more effectively represented in the City of Regina. These individuals could leverage their social, economic or political capital to promote their ideas. This imbalance raises concerns about why certain communities have more influence over policy direction, especially when the populations with the greatest need for change are often the least empowered to advocate for themselves. One interviewee highlighted that this may, in part, be due to the inaccessibility of public consultation processes:

It’s tricky, because I feel like it’s almost the voices you don’t hear from a public consultation perspective. I’m always thinking about, well you have a [public consultation] session and you have 15 people show up. Well, what about the other 1000 that didn’t come out, right? Is it that this isn’t a concern? Is it that the session wasn’t accessible? Sometimes I think that people have kids and lives and all that kind of stuff. [P5]

The overrepresentation of certain communities in decision-making can lead to an emphasis on their priorities at the expense of more marginalized groups. For instance, one interviewee noted that when predominantly male voices dominate Council discussions, other important issues, such as gender equity in sports, are often sidelined:

But when you look at our decision-makers, and those that have the ear of the Council, a lot of them are White men. Sports are predominantly male sports and women’s sports have been pushed to the backburner after the Second World War. [Women] had to fight for anything, any kind of equity within sport. And so, the argument that’s used is, well, women’s sports don’t bring in as much revenue, when I would argue that grassroots sports bring in more on an annual basis than the Roughriders [professional Canadian football team based in Regina] do. [P6]

The lack of representation in policy- and decision-making has significant implications for incorporating health and equity into urban planning. When marginalized voices are excluded, policies may not fully consider how SDOH, such as access to safe public spaces, affordable housing and public transportation, are experienced by individuals and communities. This gap can lead to urban designs that overlook or inadequately address the lived realities of these communities, potentially exacerbating health inequities. Addressing these barriers is essential to ensuring that urban planning promotes health and well-being for all residents.

DISCUSSION

Our interviews with municipal actors engaged with policy- and decision-making around urban design identified several barriers to the integration of health and equity in urban design policies and decisions. The absence of a shared framing of health and equity was a significant barrier to integrating health into urban design and planning. Our findings revealed variations in how different municipal stakeholders framed the concepts of health and equity. This study contributes to the limited literature examining how municipal actors conceptualize these critical ideas (Carmichael et al., 2013; Collins, 2014; Harris et al., 2016). The framing of ideas can influence policy- and decision-makers’ support for new initiatives and programs (Stone, 1989; Shiffman & Smith, 2007; Kingdon & Thurber, 2011; Harris et al., 2016). Many of the interviewed policy- and decision-makers defined health primarily as an individual’s physical state, which neglects more comprehensive and internationally recognized definitions, such as the WHO’s definition from 1948. This narrow framing has substantial consequences for urban planning policy. When policy- and decision-makers focus solely on physical health determinants, they often neglect critical non-physical determinants such as equitable access to services. This can lead to urban environments that promote physical activity but fail to address social inequities, ultimately undermining overall well-being (Barten et al., 2007; Corburn, 2015). For example, a city that invests in new sports facilities without considering surrounding socioeconomic factors may inadvertently exclude marginalized communities from participating, thereby exacerbating existing inequities (Shen et al., 2020).

There is a growing body of literature advocating for a holistic and intersectional framing of health to inform policy- and decision-making. Triguero-Mas and colleagues proposed a ‘just ecofeminist approach’ to build healthy cities and address social inequities, grounded in the understanding that an individual’s well-being extends beyond their physical state and is often influenced by gender identity and environment (Triguero-Mas et al., 2022). In our case study, this lack of a holistic framing of health has affected the integration of health into urban design policies. Many interviewees, when asked about health integration in their work, mainly referred to enablers of physical health. This may suggest that non-physical determinants of health are often undervalued by many policy- and decision-makers. For effective policy changes towards healthy urban design, embracing a holistic framing of health among policy actors operating within a policy domain is critical (Kingdon & Thurber, 2011). Advocating for a holistic approach means integrating diverse health considerations into urban design discussions (Barten et al., 2007; Corburn, 2015). For instance, incorporating community input on mental health services, affordable housing and social infrastructure alongside physical health initiatives can create more inclusive urban environments. This approach not only addresses physical health needs but also enhances mental health, social well-being and equity. Medellín, a city in Colombia, exemplifies this strategy through its ‘social urbanism’ approach, which aims to tackle both physical and SDOH (Maclean & Maclean, 2015; Sotomayor, 2018; Leite et al., 2020). By integrating health considerations into urban design, Medellín has improved social cohesion and access to essential services, showcasing how urban planning can contribute to healthier, more inclusive cities (Maclean, 2015; Sotomayor, 2018; Leite et al., 2020).

The siloing of municipal government institutions was another significant barrier to meaningfully integrating health into policy- and decision-making. This finding aligns with existing literature, including Williams et al. (2023), who not only argued for applying an intersectional lens in urban planning for health equity but also outlined four strategies for accomplishing this: challenging assumptions, fostering cross-sectoral collaboration, co-designing strategies and leveraging established tools and resources (Williams et al., 2023). Also, in a case study of a Norwegian city, Oldeide and colleagues identified siloing as a major obstacle to successful youth drug prevention. Siloing created confusion around jurisdictional ownership of drug prevention mandates, causing uncertainty over which departments were responsible for addressing youth drug prevention (Oldeide et al., 2019). This lack of meaningful opportunities for cross-sectoral collaboration further exacerbated these challenges preventing policy-makers from different departments from combining their knowledge and resources (Oldeide et al., 2019). Lowe et al. (2022) also acknowledged the limitations imposed by siloing in municipal policy- and decision-making. They stressed that creating healthy and sustainable cities requires integrated urban planning vertically, between different levels of government, and horizontally, between different organizations at the same level of government and also across different departments within the municipality (Lowe et al., 2022). This integration is vital to prevent fractured urban governance and foster the creation of coherent policy frameworks. We argue that horizontal integration should include local public health system (authorities) and community organizations, enabling municipal actors to work closely with public health professionals and community members to more effectively address the SDOH (Atkins et al., 2019; Fierlbeck et al., 2022). Recognizing the collaborative benefits of involving both the public health and planning workforce is an urgent priority (Azzopardi-Muscat et al., 2020), as highlighted by Giles-Cort et al. (2016). An integrated urban planning approach optimizes the use of existing resources and mitigates redundant jurisdictional overlap, while bringing together the insights of a range of stakeholders with unique perspectives (Giles-Corti et al., 2016). The WHO’s Healthy Cities initiative exemplifies this integrated approach by promoting the efficient use of resources across various sectors, including health, urban design and transportation (de Leeuw & Simos, 2017a, b). Copenhagen is a key participant in this initiative, showcasing how to effectively integrate public health into urban design through such collaborative efforts (Barton & Grant, 2013; Danielli et al., 2023).

The limited legal power of municipalities was another barrier to integrating health and equity into urban design. In contrast to American and European municipalities with constitutionally enshrined rights and powers that allow them to resist the actions of higher levels of government (Smith & Spicer, 2018), Canadian municipal governments largely operate within the mandates of provincial governments (Garcea J & K., 2006). Smith & Spicer found that none of Canada’s largest cities have significant fiscal, legal or political autonomy from their respective provincial governments (Smith & Spicer, 2018). This lack of autonomy is a major point of contention among many municipal policy- and decision-makers (Taylor & Dobson, 2020), exacerbated by the downloading or delegation of responsibilities from provincial to municipal governments (McMillan, 2006). Provincial governments justify that this delegation enhances municipal autonomy by expanding the scope of their jurisdictions (Jacob et al., 2008). However, some municipal governments argue that downloaded responsibilities are often associated with additional costs and expenditures (Jacob et al., 2008), and they are expected to cover these costs without receiving a commensurate increase in resource allocation. This effectively limits municipal autonomy by stretching their already limited resources thin (August & Walks, 2018). This untenable relationship between municipal and provincial governments impedes the effective capacities of municipal policy- and decision-makers.

Finally, inadequate representation was another significant barrier to integrating health, and particularly equity, into urban design policies and decisions. Heritz (2018) noted the underrepresentation of Indigenous peoples in Regina’s municipal government (Heritz, 2018), stemming from their historical experiences with systemic oppression and marginalization, which continue to create barriers to participating in the institutions that have played a leading role in dismantling their sovereignty, cultural identity and traditional ways of life (Carrière & Koop, 2023). A similar phenomenon has been observed among immigrants and visible minorities in Canadian local politics (Bird, 2005). For example, in Toronto, visible minorities formed only 11.1% of the City Council, despite making up 36.8% of the metropolitan population (Bird, 2005). These underrepresented populations face various structural barriers to political engagement, including language barriers, social exclusion and a lack of familiarity with Canada’s political systems (Lowe et al., 2022). Similar factors have also contributed to the longstanding underrepresentation of racial or ethnic minorities in cities in the United States (Ricca & Trebbi, 2022). Often, the populations experiencing the greatest barriers to engaging with local governments are the same ones encountering the greatest health inequities resulting from urban design.

Study limitations

One limitation of this study is the type and size of the sample we used to collect the data, which may limit the extent to which our findings can be extrapolated. We collected data from the City of Regina, a medium-sized city in Western Canada. Although the barriers to the integration of health and equity in urban design that we identified in this study are specific to the City of Regina, the findings may offer insights relevant to other jurisdictions, especially among cities of similar sizes in Canada. However, due to inconsistencies in policies and legislations across different jurisdictions, generalizing our findings to the legal or political perspectives may not be appropriate. Therefore, further research is warranted to validate and test the barriers in other jurisdictions and countries.

CONCLUSION

This study revealed some of the significant barriers and facilitators to integrating health into the urban design and planning of a medium-sized city in Canada. The findings of this study are invaluable to urban planners and public health professionals, especially as there has been growing recognition of the potential for cities to lead in enhancing urban population health and health equity. As urbanization continues to bring a greater portion of the world’s population into urban areas, it is imperative that we deepen our understanding of how municipal governance can be leveraged to create environments that promote the well-being of their residents.
