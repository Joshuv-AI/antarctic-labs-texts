Neuro-evolutionary computing approach for an epidemic model of ransomware detection using morlet wavelet neural network with meta-heuristic optimization
Authors: Zain Ul Abideen Khan, Muhammad Abid Mughal, Irfan Ul Haq, Muhammad Naeem Aslam, Bouthaina Dammak, Abdullatif Ghallab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper presents an algorithmic paradigm that will address a malware model in the form of a nonlinear set of differential equations. The neural networks are Morlet wavelet as estimate the state variables of the malware behavior. To maximize the network performance, we use a heuristic optimization algorithm that optimizes the network parameters to achieve rapid convergence and accurate performance. The learning stage is designed and constructed on the basis of a fitness criterion which is based on the residuals of the governing equations and the initial conditions. We discuss two various cases in order to test the strength of proposed approach. In both, the mean squared errors (MSE) of between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{10}^{-09}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{10}^{-07}$$\end{document}, which point to the accuracy of the scheme. Furthermore, the values of Theil inequality coefficient (TIC) are between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{10}^{-06}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{10}^{-04}$$\end{document} with strong predictive powers. The stability of optimization process is checked by the comparative behavior with the several runs. We find the results in perfect agreement with reference solutions, which suggests that our framework is an effective and useful substitute to traditional numerical solvers to malware dynamic systems.

Introduction

Wireless sensor networks (WSNs) are now an essential component of intelligent systems and are utilized as a building block in a broad range of applications, including environmental surveillance and industrial automation. The importance of WSNs escalated drastically due to the tremendous growth of the Internet of Things (IoT) and Industry 4.0 concepts, in which a great deal of sensor nodes are utilized to independently measure as well as broadcast data1–3. The attraction of WSNs is in their ability to work efficiently in terms of power consumption, cost, and ability to function in a decentralized way even in challenging environment settings. However, these very qualities make them particularly sensitive to reliability and security threats due to limitations in terms of battery power, memory, processing power, and the communication range4. One major problem of WSNs is their vulnerability to malware and hacking. Malware may be put in debt way through tampered nodes or be deliberate, the malware has a high chance of self-spreading without human foot print, which makes the malware dangerous. Worm attack can be fast and ruin update access applications through a large part of network. Since the WSN rely on wireless communication and their nature is work together, kids, not show in a man or integrated system. The later is more susceptible for malware attack compared to most current connected systems with wires5–8.

A bad malware hit can rob secrecy, fairness, and access to data, and take away sensor power. This can cause nodes to fail and the whole network to fall apart9,10. The way that malware jumps from one sensor node to another has been looked at through epidemic modeling. This kind of modeling comes from studies of how disease spreads in animals and humans. It has been used a lot for looking at malware spreading in many types of systems: computers, social media, WSNs, and IoT centers11–13. Studies have used classic and newer epidemic models to take a closer look at worm actions in WSNs. First, the SIR model was used by early studies to dig into infected nodal patterns14. After that, other models like SITR, SEIS, SIRS, and SEIRS helped to make models that are more close to the real spread of malware, and take better account of temporary immunity, delay, re-infection, and nodal loss15–20. Several pieces of research points out how the spatial spread of the nodes at hand, the distribution of those nodes, how fast the nodes move around, and the possibility of the channel to be used at any given moment all matter in how malware spreads. For example, simply changing the communication range or number of nodes can have a big effect on how close or how far apart nodes are that either aren’t infected or already infected, and that effect the expected spread again or spread at all (Ro)16,18. Other models want to look at items like how to watch for malware, the way the environment interacts with malware spread such as through regional findability, jamming, and the configuration of the area many nodes exist within18–20. There are more processes aside from spread into node depletion, delay, and virus mutation that tell us about the spread of malware in sensor webs21–34.

Solution methodology

The solution methodology is based on using Morlet Wavelet Neural Network (MWNN) and other neural networks methodologies36–39 to approximate the solution of the nonlinear models. The Morlet Wavelet neural network has not previously been designed for solving the WSN modeling. The Morlet Wavelet (MW) function is essentially the product of the cosine function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:cos\left(\frac{7}{4}t\right)$$\end{document} and a Gaussian function, and it is given as: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}{t}^{2}}cos\left(\frac{7}{4}t\right)$$\end{document}36.

First, the original five coupled nonlinear ODEs describing the system dynamics are written in standard first-order form with state variables \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:S\left(t\right)\:,\:{E}_{1}\left(t\right)\:,\:{E}_{2}\left(t\right)\:,\:I\left(t\right)\:\:\mathrm{a}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{d}\:R\left(t\right),$$\end{document} where the input layer represents time t and the output layer represents these five state trajectories. The neural approximation for each state is constructed using MW basis functions embedded in a feed-forward network, so that the network output is a weighted MW expansion that is smooth and differentiable. MWNN architecture is adopted in which 21 neurons are used in the input layer and 21 neurons in the output layer and 10 neurons in hidden layer. After defining the MWNN trial solutions, their time derivatives are obtained analytically through differentiation, allowing substitution directly into the ordinary differential equations ODEs system. Each ODE residual is then moved to the left-hand side, producing an error term for every equation at sampled collocation points over the time domain. These residual values are squared, to prevent cancellation of signs, and to encourage large deviations, and added together to constitute the ODE based component of the fitness function. Simultaneously, each initial condition is calculated with respect to every state variable, and one combined fitness function, describing a network the level of satisfaction to both the dynamics and initial constraints, is obtained. The optimization problem is then to obtain network weights and biases that would maximize this total fitness. The chaotic system would produce a highly nonconvex error space, so a metaheuristic optimization algorithm (flow direction algorithm) is used to globally optimize the weights and biases, rather than making use of gradient descent alone.

During training, the metaheuristic iteratively updates candidate weight/bias sets, evaluates the fitness, and drives the search toward lower residual error. Convergence is judged by how close the main fitness function approaches zero; the closer the minimum fitness is to zero, the more accurately the MWNN solution satisfies the five ODEs and initial conditions. Thus, accuracy is directly linked to minimizing the summed squared residuals, and a near-zero fitness indicates a highly reliable numerical solution for the 5D chaotic multi-malware model, equations Meta-heuristic optimization algorithm

The optimization algorithms based on metaheuristics40–45 are high-level, problem-independent methods that are intended to locate near-optimal solutions in cases when the exact optimization algorithms or the gradient-based algorithms only are too slow or stall. They particularly assist in complex search spaces (nonlinear), nonconvex search spaces, noisy search spaces, and high-dimensional search spaces. The majority of metaheuristics have the effect of balancing between exploration of the search space and exploration of promising regions, usually starting with random or heuristically selected initial solutions. They do not depend on derivatives and instead rely on stochastic rules to drive the search and, therefore, can be useful with discontinuous, non-differentiable, or black box objective functions. There are population-based methods, including Genetic Algorithms, Particle Swarm Optimization, or Differential Evolution, in which a large number of candidate solutions are evolved concurrently by means of operators like mutation, crossover, or velocity updates. Others, such as Simulated Annealing and Tabu Search, are trajectory based and evolve a single candidate by local steps and a bit of randomness.

One of the strong points of the metaheuristics lies in its ability to avoid the local minima. They do this through tricks such as random restarts, or playing less-than-perfect moves where necessary, or updating populations in various ways. Such strategies also are experienced in managing these constraints and employ strategies like penalty conditions, repair techniques, or operators that keep things viable. They may not always obtain the best solution, but when parameters such as population size, cooling schedules or learning rates are well tuned, then they generally give good and workable results46–57. Metaheuristics find application in the optimization of weights and biases in artificial neural networks since ANN loss surfaces exhibit a high degree of nonconvexity, are full of local minima, saddle points, and flat regions. These algorithms do not need gradients to be effective and therefore they are also useful when backpropagation fails because of vanishing or exploding gradients, noisy objectives, or non differentiable components. They can search over a wide variety of regions of the weight space throughout the globe, and hence are more likely to find improved configurations than local gradient descent. They are particularly useful when there is a small to medium network, when training by hybrid (metaheuristics and gradient fine-tuning) or when training weights with hyperparameters and architectures. In general, metaheuristic algorithms represent a versatile alternative or complement of the backpropagation, and they tend to enhance the reliability of convergence and the ultimate ANN performance.

In this study, we look at how rainfall losses happen due to infiltration, evapotranspiration, and interception. After accounting for these, the remaining rainfall turns into direct runoff. To track this movement, we split the drainage basin into several cells, where runoff from each cell flows into neighboring ones based on elevation and slope. To determine the water’s path, we compare the elevation and distance of each cell with its neighbors, calculating the slope for each. Water is directed towards the neighbor with the steepest slope. The FDA algorithm draws inspiration from the D8 method for determining flow direction within a drainage basin after rainfall is converted to runoff. Initially, the algorithm generates a starting population within the drainage basin or the problem’s search space. Performance

In the process of solving a 5D chaotic system using MWNN-FDA the evaluation of model performance is critical. Two key performance indicators used in this context are the mean squared error (MSE), Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE), Semi-Interquartile Range (SIR) and theil’s inequality coefficient (TIC). The MSE is calculated by averaging the squared error of every prediction of the system made to the actual one, and is a direct measure of how accurate the network in question is. The smaller the MSE is, the better approximates the neural network the dynamics of the uncertain chaotic system and the convergence of the neural network toward the correct dynamics of the chaotic system. TIC is again a normalized measure of performance, this time with respect to how well the solution the computer predicted adheres to the pattern of the solution used to reference. TIC values near zero readings would indicate their high predictive accuracy, and small variation with the true solution at various magnitudes of the variables. Combined, MSE and TIC provide adequate insight into both numerical accuracy and the structure straightness, which is needed to establish the integrity and usefulness of the proposed MWNN-FDA method in describing complicated chaos. Conclusion

In this study, we’ve developed a solid computational framework combining Morlet wavelet neural networks with a heuristic optimization algorithm to tackle a nonlinear dynamical system driven by malware. Our method adeptly approximates state variables while ensuring numerical stability in various trials. We explored two different scenarios to test the consistency and reliability of our approach. The neural network solutions align closely with reference solutions derived from traditional numerical schemes, highlighting their accuracy. Our optimization strategy is designed for rapid convergence with no early stagnation. Through a quantitative performance evaluation, we confirmed the high precision of this framework. Error analysis shows that our method accurately captures the underlying dynamics of malware. This approach overcomes the limitations of traditional solvers when dealing with nonlinear and complex systems. The computational results underscore the robustness and efficiency of the Morlet wavelet architecture. Overall, this method offers a strong alternative for tackling malware propagation models governed by differential equations.
