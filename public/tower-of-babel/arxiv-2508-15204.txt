R-ConstraintBench: Evaluating LLMs on NP-Complete Scheduling
Raj Jain rjain@labelbox.com Marc Wetter mwetter@labelbox.com Labelbox

Abstract
Effective scheduling under tight resource, timing, and operational constraints underpins large-scale planning across sectors such as capital projects, manufacturing, logistics, and IT fleet transitions. However, the reliability of large language models (LLMs) when reasoning under high-constraint regimes is insufficiently characterized. To address this gap, we present R-ConstraintBench, a scalable framework that evaluates models on Resource-Constrained Project Scheduling Problems (RCPSP), an NP-Complete feasibility class, while difficulty increases via linear growth in constraints. R-ConstraintBench incrementally increases non-redundant precedence constraints in Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) and then introduces downtime, temporal windows, and disjunctive constraints. As an illustrative example, we instantiate the benchmark in a data center migration setting and evaluate multiple LLMs using feasibility and error analysis, identifying degradation thresholds and constraint types most associated with failure. Empirically, strong models are near-ceiling on precedence-only DAGs, but feasibility performance collapses when downtime, temporal windows, and disjunctive constraints interact—implicating constraint interaction , not graph depth, as the principal bottleneck. Performance on clean synthetic ramps also does not guarantee transfer to domain-grounded scenarios, underscoring limited generalization.



1. INTRODUCTION

1.1. Motivation

Accurate scheduling under resource, timing, and operational constraints is critical across construction, manufacturing turnarounds, airline and crew planning, supply-chain coordination, clinical trial operations, satellite tasking, and large IT transitions.
As organizations explore LLMs for planning and coordination, small reliability lapses can propagate into cost, safety, and service risks. As a result, ensuring that LLM-generated schedules are feasible is therefore a prerequisite for deployment. Beyond feasibility, systematic evaluation is needed to understand the precise conditions under which LLMs remain reliable, where they break down, and when alternative solution strategies should be employed. To isolate models’ core latent reasoning ability on this task, our evaluation does not permit specialized tool use; tool-augmented pipelines are left to future work.

1.2. LLM Reliability on Complex RCPSPs

LLMs show competence on small scheduling puzzles and short dependency chains
[
1
]
, but generalization to regimes with interacting precedence, resource, and temporal rules is unclear. We adopt RCPSP feasibility as a focused testbed because it concentrates the reasoning burden on satisfying heterogeneous constraints.
We ask whether models scale their reasoning to deep, structured hierarchies with resource calendars and mutual-exclusion requirements, conditions common in multi-site data center migrations, capital builds, and airline crew pairing.

1.3. Our Contributions

In this work, we develop R-ConstraintBench (Resource-Constraint Bench), a controlled benchmarking framework that reveals when and why leading LLMs succeed or fail on RCPSP feasibility tasks. Our key contributions are:

•

Synthetic Constraint Progression
, via a layered DAG generator that incrementally increases non-redundant precedence constraints by one per level, followed by resource downtimes, temporal windows, and disjunctive exclusivity at controlled rates.

•

Domain Instantiation (Illustrative)
, mapping the same RCPSP structure to a data center migration scenario, preserving layered dependencies while placing tasks into representative operational phases and constraints.

•

Comprehensive Evaluation and Infeasibility Analysis
, assessing feasibility and failure modes while identifying breakpoints where reliability degrades.

3. PROBLEM FORMULATION

We formalize RCPSP feasibility and the axes of complexity we vary.

3.1. RCPSP as a Feasibility Problem

A Resource-Constrained Project Scheduling Problem (RCPSP) asks: “Given a set of tasks, resources, and constraints, can we assign start and end times such that the entire schedule is valid?” In our formulation, we do not minimize makespan; instead, we require
complete
constraint satisfaction. This choice isolates feasibility reasoning from optimization objectives, enabling binary verification and attributing errors to constraint handling rather than objective trade-offs, consistent with our focus on latent reasoning ability and comparable difficulty across instances as constraint density increases.
Formally, an RCPSP instance comprises a collection of tasks
T=\{1,\dots,n\}
, each with fixed duration
p_{i}
; a set of resources
R=\{1,\dots,m\}
, each with time-varying capacity
c_{r}(t)
; resource demands
q_{i,r}
denoting how much of resource
r
task
i
consumes; and precedence relations
i\to j
that forbid task
j
from starting before task
i
completes.

A schedule assigns start times
s_{i}\geq 0
so that:

Precedence:

s_{j}\geq s_{i}+p_{i},\quad\forall\,(i\to j).

(1)

Resource feasibility:

\sum_{i:\,s_{i}\leq t<s_{i}+p_{i}}q_{i,r}\leq c_{r}(t),\quad\forall\,r\in R,\ \forall\,t\geq 0.

(2)

3.2. Representing RCPSP as DAGs

Any set of precedence constraints prohibiting cycles forms a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG). Beyond tautology, using layered DAGs to represent RCPSPs gives three key benefits:

1.

Natural modeling of phases.
Large programs (e.g., construction sequences, release trains, data center relocations) decompose into stages; layers mirror these stages.

2.

Guaranteed acyclicity and clarity.
Tasks with no circular dependencies are fundamental to feasibility; DAG structure enforces this at origin. Layers prevent inadvertent back-edges and clearly separate “earlier” from “later” tasks.

3.

Controlled complexity progression.
We add one new cross-layer precedence edge at a time; at level
k
, the DAG has exactly
k
non-redundant constraints, isolating the effect of increasing dependency depth.

3.3. DAG Generation

To operationalize layered generation over
m
layers, we partition tasks into layers
L_{1},L_{2},\dots,L_{m}
and initialize an empty reachability matrix. Then, for each of
k
desired precedence-constraint levels, we sample a candidate edge
(i,j)
where
i\in L_{a}
and
j\in L_{b}
with
b>a
. We accept this edge only if no path
i\to j
already exists in the transitive closure, and subsequently update the reachability matrix so that later levels cannot re-introduce any implied edges. This guarantees acyclic, non-redundant structures. Parameters
m
and
k
allow fine-grained control over the DAG’s hierarchy and density.

3.4. Realism via Additional Constraint Axes

A pure-precedence DAG is the skeleton. We layer on three more constraint types:

•

Resource Downtime:
Certain resources become unavailable (
c_{r}(t)=0
).

•

Temporal Windows:
Release times
r_{i}
(
s_{i}\geq r_{i}
); deadlines
d_{i}
(
s_{i}+p_{i}\leq d_{i}
).

•

Disjunctive (No-Overlap):
For some
(i,j)
, enforce
s_{i}+p_{i}\leq s_{j}
or
s_{j}+p_{j}\leq s_{i}
.

Each axis is sampled at controlled probabilities (e.g., 75%). The result is a parameterized, multi-axis challenge suitable for systematic evaluation, forming the core of our R-ConstraintBench evaluation.

4. SYSTEM DESIGN AND EXPERIMENTAL METHODOLOGY

R-ConstraintBench’s evaluation pipeline consists of three integrated components:
(1) structured instance generation with guaranteed solvability,
(2) controlled LLM scheduling, and
(3) exact automated verification of each candidate schedule.
Separating generation, model interaction, and validation helps ensure measured performance reflects model reasoning rather than data noise or human intervention.

4.1. Structured Instance Generation

We synthesize RCPSP problem instances that are realistic and tunable in difficulty. Leveraging an RCPSP solver program (built on OR-Tools’ CP-SAT), we enforce a two-step process:

1.

Layered-DAG Construction.

Partition tasks into
m
layers (e.g., 2–5); initialize reachability. For each of
k
levels, add exactly one cross-layer edge if it is non-redundant, updating reachability immediately. The result is a DAG with precisely
k
non-redundant dependencies.

2.

Constraint Injection and Feasibility Check.

Randomly assign each task a duration drawn from
[1,D_{\max}]
, ensure every task demands at least one of three resource types, impose temporal windows on a subset of tasks, introduce downtime intervals during which certain resources are fully unavailable, and add disjunctive constraints forbidding simultaneous execution of specific task pairs.

Immediately after generation, we invoke the solver to check feasibility. Any instance that fails to admit a valid schedule is discarded and regenerated, guaranteeing that every instance entering evaluation admits a feasible schedule.

4.2. Controlled LLM Scheduling

Once instances are generated, we evaluate each LLM under identical conditions:

•

Prompt Design.
A concise natural-language description lists every task’s duration, resource needs, and all constraints, followed by a JSON-schema template defining the expected ‘schedule’ output format (Appendix). To keep evaluation focused on inherent model capability rather than prompt-engineering technique, we adopt a single-shot format that requires the model to integrate all constraints and produce a complete schedule in one pass
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
.

•

Response Capture.
We test a representative suite of models. Each instance is presented in a single-shot prompt, and model outputs are parsed into structured candidate schedules. This setup requires the model to interpret the entire scheduling problem holistically, integrating all constraints into a coherent plan without intermediate guidance or iterative feedback loops.

An overview of R-ConstraintBench system design

4.3. Exact Automated Verification

To measure correctness, every candidate schedule is fed into a verifier program, which checks:

•

Precedence Compliance.
No task starts before all its predecessors finish.

•

Resource and Downtime Adherence.
At each time step, resource usage never exceeds capacity, and resources remain idle during downtime windows.

•

Temporal Window Satisfaction.
All start times respect release dates and deadlines.

•

Disjunctive Enforcement.
For each forbidden pair
(i,j)
, tasks do not overlap in time.

Violations are automatically classified by type (precedence, resource/downtime, temporal, disjunctive), enabling a fine-grained infeasibility analysis. Together, this three-stage design—generation, scheduling, verification—forms the backbone of R-ConstraintBench, providing a controlled evaluation of LLM reliability on increasingly complex RCPSP feasibility tasks.

5. BENCHMARK CONSTRUCTION

To rigorously evaluate LLM scheduling reliability, we assembled a suite of synthetic RCPSP benchmarks whose structure and operational complexity can be dialed. Every instance is generated by our solver-driven pipeline (Section 5) and passed through a CP-SAT solver to guarantee feasibility before model evaluation. We organize our evaluation into two phases – first isolating pure precedence reasoning, then layering on full operational constraints – culminating in an illustrative data center migration scenario.

5.1. Phase I: Pure-Precedence DAGs

Phase I strips the problem to its dependency graph, so any model failure stems from mis-reasoning about precedence. Because Phase I is pure-precedence DAGs, the identification of any valid topological sort is admitted as a solution. Finding a topological sort of a precedence DAG can be done in linear time
O(V+E)
. Therefore, these are not NP-Complete problems.

5.2. Phase IIa: Multi-Constraint Interaction

Phase IIa fixes a 5-layer backbone and injects three additional constraint types at 75% probability to emulate the structure of complex, NP-Complete RCPSP problems. The 75% probability was selected to balance realism and difficulty — high enough to meaningfully stress models with frequent constraint interactions, yet low enough to avoid near-unsolvable instances at modest precedence levels. These cannot be solved in linear time because checking feasibility is no longer a pure ordering problem.

6. EVALUATION SETUP

6.1. Research Questions

We evaluate the performance of state-of-the-art (SOTA) LLMs on RCPSP problem solving with R-ConstraintBench. Specifically, we aim to address the following research questions (RQs):

1.

RQ1: Constraint Scalability.
At what level of added precedence constraints does each model’s feasibility rate drop below a usable threshold?

2.

RQ2: Constraint-Type Sensitivity.
How do resource downtime, temporal windows, and disjunctive constraints collectively impact model reliability when layered onto a fixed DAG?

3.

RQ3: Domain Transferability.
Can performance patterns observed on synthetic benchmarks predict reliability in an illustrative, business scenario?

6.2. Model Selection

Solving an RCPSP instance in R-ConstraintBench demands deep combinatorial reasoning, the ability to juggle long contextual descriptions, and careful handling of heterogeneous constraint types. To probe these capabilities, we select nine SOTA LLMs for evaluation. Two of them are open-source models (DeepSeek-R1-0528 and Qwen3-235B-A22B, where DeepSeek-R1 is a reasoning model and Qwen3-235B-A22B is used in “thinking” mode). The other seven are closed-source models (Claude-4-Sonnet, GPT-4o-2024-11-20, Gemini-2.5-Pro, o4-mini-2025-04-16, o3-pro-2025-06-10, GPT-5-2025-08-07, and Grok-4-0709). For brevity, version numbers will be omitted when referencing model names throughout the paper.

6.3. Evaluation Metrics

We measure model performance along three complementary dimensions—accuracy, robustness, and failure‐mode breakdown. All metrics are computed per model
M
and per dataset
D
, where each dataset spans
K

K{=}200
) with 10 instances per level (
k=1,\dots,K
).

1.

Feasibility Rate (Feasibility %)
:
For each level
k
,

F^{M}_{D}(k)=\frac{\#\{\text{instances at level }k\text{ solved correctly}\}}{10}\,,

(3)

i.e., the fraction of the 10 instances at level
k
that the model schedules feasibly.

2.

Weighted Area Under the Curve (WAUC)
:
Because difficulty increases monotonically with
k
(one additional constraint per level), later levels are weighted more heavily via linear weights

w_{k}\;=\;\frac{2k}{K(K{+}1)}\quad\text{with}\quad\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\;=\;1\,.

(4)

The dataset‐level robustness is then

\mathrm{WAUC}^{M}_{D}\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\,F^{M}_{D}(k)\,,

(5)

which rewards models that remain feasible at higher constraint levels.

3.

Breakpoint Level (BP‐Level)
:
The smallest level
k
at which

F^{M}_{D}(k)\;<\;\tau\,,

(6)

for a high‐accuracy threshold
\tau
(e.g.,
70\%
).

4.

Infeasibility Analysis
:
Every failed schedule is automatically classified by the verifier into one or more of four violation categories:
Precedence
,
Resource/Downtime
,
Temporal
, and/or
Disjunctive
.

Official R-ConstraintBench Score.

Benchmark’s headline score for model

averages WAUC over the Phase-II datasets:

\mathrm{Score}^{M}\;=\;\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{phase2}}\rvert}\sum_{D\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{phase2}}}\mathrm{WAUC}^{M}_{D}\,.

This definition aligns the score with practical difficulty, emphasizing high-constraint regimes while remaining comparable across datasets.

9. INFEASIBILITY ANALYSIS

To better understand the nature of model failures, we classify every invalid schedule into violation categories.

•

Precedence
: At least one task begins before all of its prerequisite tasks have completed.

•

Resource/Downtime
: Resource capacities are exceeded at one or more time steps, or tasks are scheduled during periods when the required resource is unavailable.

•

Temporal
: Violations of earliest-start or latest-finish requirements, where a task begins too early or finishes late.

•

Disjunctive
: Two tasks that must not overlap in time (due to exclusivity constraints) are scheduled concurrently.

•

Other
: Failures that do not fall neatly into the above categories, such as malformed outputs that cannot be parsed, conflicting minor violations, or combinations of multiple small errors.

10. DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS

The analysis reveals that even the strongest models—
o3
,
Grok 4
, and
GPT-5
—exhibit sharply divergent strengths depending on the constraint profile and domain.
o3
dominates on the synthetic MCI constraints track, sustaining feasibility deep into the difficulty ramp, yet it suffers the largest negative transfer in the
Data Center Migration
setting.
Grok 4
shows the reverse pattern: modest synthetic performance but substantially better real-world transfer.
GPT-5
lifts the real-world ceiling further (best observed WAUC and latest real-world BP), while remaining competitive synthetically; however, its real-world feasibility still declines in the tail, indicating that late-level constraint density remains a bottleneck even for the strongest model. Taken together, these outcomes show that success in purely synthetic RCPSP benchmarks is
necessary but not sufficient
for robustness in constraint-rich operations, and that domain grounding can materially alter the failure surface.

The infeasibility analysis further clarifies these differences. Models with high synthetic scores often fail predominantly on precedence violations, while those with stronger domain transfer suffer more from disjunctive or temporal window violations. These patterns indicate that constraint-specific reasoning weaknesses, rather than generalized “hardness,” drive many failures. This has implications for model selection: practitioners should match model strengths to the constraint types most prevalent in their operational context.

Interpreting these findings also requires recognizing the design boundaries of our study. First, we evaluate nine models under a single-shot prompting protocol; while this controls for prompt variance, it does not capture the effects of multi-step reasoning, tool integration, or self-verification strategies that could yield higher feasibility rates

. Second, the instance generator emphasizes five-layer DAGs with sampled temporal, downtime, and disjunctive constraints at fixed probabilities—ideal for controlled scaling but narrower than the correlated, irregular patterns found in real deployments. Third, we frame scheduling as a
feasibility
task, using CP-SAT for verification; this design isolates reasoning accuracy from optimization, yet leaves open the question of whether feasible outputs can also meet makespan or cost objectives without solver assistance. Future work could complement pass/fail feasibility with a
feasibility distance
metric that quantifies how far a schedule is from validity—e.g., minimal slack required to satisfy constraints, or minimal start-time adjustments—to enable graded scoring. Fourth, our real-world translation focuses on data center migration; while representative of a demonstrative scheduling domain, other areas such as manufacturing, construction, or satellite operations may exhibit different constraint interactions and failure distributions.

By integrating these limitations into the interpretation of results, we position R-ConstraintBench as both a reliable measure of current prompt-only LLM scheduling capability and a foundation for future exploration into constraint-aware and domain-adapted reasoning approaches.

11. CONCLUSION

Constraint-rich scheduling underpins operations across industries, yet prevailing evaluations rarely expose where LLM reasoning fails when heterogeneous rules must be satisfied simultaneously. R-ConstraintBench addresses this gap by isolating latent feasibility reasoning, ramping
constraint interaction
and density (MCI), and testing transfer in a domain-grounded data-center instantiation.

Two results are decisive. First,
constraint interaction—not graph depth—is the bottleneck
: strong models are near-ceiling on precedence-only DAGs, but feasibility collapses once downtime, temporal windows, and disjunctive exclusivity interact. Second,
synthetic success does not guarantee domain transfer
: models that excel on clean synthetic ramps can degrade when constraints are coupled as they are in practice.

Across nine models, we observe steep drops when constraint types interact. While
o3
leads on the synthetic MCI track,
GPT-5
attains the strongest data center score (and latest breakpoint), and all systems decline in the highest-constraint band. Failure profiles are diagnostic: precedence and disjunctive violations dominate many errors, with temporal and resource/downtime violations appearing less frequently.

We speculate that training and evaluation regimes emphasizing constraint interaction—rather than depth alone—will improve performance on RCPSP-style tasks and may support broader reasoning generalization; testing this explicitly is left to future work.

12. FUTURE WORK

Several directions can extend and deepen the impact of R-
ConstraintBench:

•

Finetuning for Scheduling Competence:
Explore whether finetuning models on RCPSP-style problems, with either synthetic or domain-specific schedules, improves constraint satisfaction rates and robustness under high-density conditions.

•

Ablations on Prompting and Instance Factors:
Systematically vary prompt formulation (instruction framing, schema strictness, verbosity, ordering, units), constraint mix (proportions and correlation of downtime/temporal/disjunctive), and structural scale (tasks per layer, number of layers, resource types, calendar granularity). Quantify sensitivity of WAUC, BP-level, feasibility-distance, and error taxonomy to these factors.

•

Expanding to Additional NP-Complete Problems:
Extend the benchmark to include other combinatorial optimization problems such as the Traveling Salesman Problem, Job-Shop Scheduling, and Vehicle Routing Problems, enabling broader assessment of reasoning and optimization capabilities.

•

Multi-Domain Evaluation:
Introduce new operational domains beyond data center migration (e.g., construction sequencing, supply chain coordination, satellite task scheduling) to evaluate transfer learning and domain adaptation.

•

Prompting Strategy Variations:
Systematically study the impact of chain-of-thought prompting, step-by-step reasoning scaffolds, verification prompts, and retrieval-augmented generation on schedule feasibility and constraint adherence.

•

Code-Generation for Solvers:
Given the growing ability of modern LLMs to produce and execute code, evaluate model performance in generating complete, correct CP-SAT solver programs for given scheduling problems, comparing direct-scheduling outputs with solver-assisted outputs.
