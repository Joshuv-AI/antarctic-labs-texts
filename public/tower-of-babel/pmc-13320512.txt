Lived experiences of young people with epilepsy in a resource-constraint setting: Contemporary findings from Cape Coast, Ghana
Authors: Akwasi Appiah, Kwasi Agyen-Mensah, Akua Afriyie Karikari, Daniel Lawer Egbenya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpilepsy is a chronic neurological condition that affects millions of young people globally, often leading to physical, cognitive, psychological, and social challenges. In resource-constraint settings, access to healthcare and medications, stigma, and psychosocial burdens can further exacerbate the impact of the condition. This study explored the lived experiences of young people with epilepsy (YPWE) who attend the Cape Coast Teaching Hospital, Ghana, focusing on emotional reactions to diagnosis, access to healthcare, coping strategies, support systems, and future aspirations. A qualitative study approach was employed. Twenty young people aged 12–25 years participated in in-depth, semi-structured interviews. Thematic data analysis was conducted using Braun and Clarke's six-step method to identify patterns and themes from the participants' narratives. Six themes emerged: (1) diagnosis and emotional reactions, where participants expressed shock, fear, sadness, and initial anxiety following diagnosis; (2) access to medications and healthcare services, highlighting challenges such as medication availability, affordability, long queues, and financial constraints; (3) psychosocial impact, encompassing disruption to education, social activities, work, and occasional feelings of low self-esteem; (4) coping strategies, including lifestyle modifications, faith-based practices, and engaging in recreational activities; (5) support systems like reliance on friends and family during aura, and (6) future aspirations illustrating the participants' hopes for education and career advancement. Impliedly, YPWE in resource-constrained settings face multifaceted challenges. Adaptive coping strategies, supportive relationships and faith-based resilience ameliorate these challenges. Interventions should focus on improving access to medications and healthcare services, reducing stigma, strengthening psychosocial support, and empowering young people to achieve their aspirations.Keywords: Epilepsy, lived experiences, resource-constraint setting, stigmatization, young people with epilepsy

Introduction

Due to its impact on cognitive, social, cultural, psychological, and economic facets of life, epilepsy remains a major health concern in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC), where it ranks fifth among all neurological causes of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [1]. Patients with epilepsy are three times more likely to die young than the general population, according to Thurman, Logroscino [2]. This risk could rise to seven times in environments with limited resources [3], [4]. How epilepsy affect young people in resource scarce regions needs to be examined for design of appropriate interventions.

Epilepsy is thought to be the most prevalent neurologic disorder in children worldwide, affecting 10.5 million of them under the age of 15 [5] with a higher prevalence in young people in lower- and middle-income countries than those in high-income countries [6]. Using the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) data of 2021, Xu et al. reported a global incidence number of childhood epilepsy of 1,227,191 cases with an incidence rate of 61 per 100, 000 [7]. Similarly, children and youth with epilepsy has an incidence rate of 61.8 per 100, 000, as shown in a Norwegian study [8]. In Africa, about one in every fifty children suffers from epilepsy with a pooled incidence of childhood epilepsy of 2.5 per 1000 children [6]. Although it can be diagnosed in individuals across all age groups, epilepsy lifetime prevalence shows two distinct peaks – one in youth and another in old age [9].

People with epilepsy (PWE) face numerous challenges in their daily lives. These include limited independence, adverse side-effects from anti-seizure medication, social stigma, and greater obstacles in achieving educational and career success [10]. Studies have shown that young adults with a history of epilepsy are more likely to experience higher rates of unemployment, binge drinking, unintended pregnancies, and lower high school graduation rates [10]. Moshe, Perucca [11] also reported that PWE are at a higher risk of psychiatric conditions than the general population. Young adults with epilepsy often suffer from emotional challenges such as stress, anger, loneliness, embarrassment, and depression, and tend to have poorer psychosocial outcomes compared to their peers without epilepsy [12]. For young people with epilepsy (YPWE), poor health outcomes may have lasting consequences.

Adolescence and young adulthood are critical developmental stages marked by age-specific developmental milestones noted for identity formation, increasing autonomy and social integration. To be diagnosed with epilepsy around this developmental stage may affect the physical, emotional, and social development of the affected young people [13]. Despite these, little or no effort is devoted to examining the lived experiences of YPWE in Ghana. By shedding light on the lived experiences of YPWE in a resource-limited setting, healthcare providers and policymakers may be equipped to better understand the challenges these individuals face hence designing appropriate interventions to influence the comprehensive care of YPWE. Therefore, the current study explored the lived experiences of YPWE in Cape Coast, Ghana.

Method

Study design

This study utilized a qualitative, cross-sectional research design to investigate the lived experiences of YPWE in the Cape Coast Metropolitan area of Ghana. This method enabled a deep exploration of personal perspectives and provided insight into how epilepsy affects the everyday lives of YPWE.

Study area

The present study was carried out at the Neurology Clinic of the Cape Coast Teaching Hospital (CCTH). Located in the northern part of Cape Coast in the Central region of Ghana, CCTH is a referral facility with a current capacity of 400 beds. It shares boundaries with Abura township to the north, Pedu Estate/4th Ridge to the south, Nkanfua to the east, and Abura/Pedu Estate to the west. In addition to offering diagnostic and rehabilitative services, the hospital provides both in-patient and out-patient care across general and specialized medical fields. Outpatient visits increased from 152,364 in 2021 to 170,441 in 2022, with an average of 467 patients seen daily. The Neurology Clinic, managed by the Internal Medicine Department of the hospital, operates on the first Tuesday of every month.

Study population

The study population consisted of YPWE, aged between 12 and 25 years, who were receiving treatment at the Cape Coast Teaching Hospital (CCTH). This age group was chosen because it represents a critical segment of people affected by epilepsy [5], [9], [14], especially in resource-limited settings where access to healthcare and support services is often inadequate. This age range forms part of an age-specific developmental stage during which significant life milestones, such as identity formation, take place. Both male and female participants were included to capture a broad range of experiences. Similarly, this reflected diverse perspectives by considering variations in socio-economic background, access to medical care, and other relevant factors among YPWE.

Sample and sampling procedure

This study used a purposive sampling method to select twenty (20) participants—thirteen (13) males and seven (7) females—who could offer detailed and meaningful insights into their lived experiences with epilepsy. The sample size was guided by the concept of data saturation, where recruitment continued until no new themes or information emerged during the interviews. Participants were identified and referred by healthcare workers at the Neurology Clinic of the hospital, ensuring that only individuals with a confirmed epilepsy diagnosis who met the inclusion criteria were selected. To be eligible, a prospective participant must be visiting the Neurology Clinic at CCTH, be between 12 and 25 years old, have a verified diagnosis of epilepsy, and be both willing and able to give informed consent either by themselves or their caregiver/guardian. Individuals were excluded if they fell outside this age range or had cognitive impairments that could hinder their ability to effectively participate in interviews or answer study questions.

Data collection instrument, data collection and analysis

A semi-structured interview guide was employed to gather data through in-depth interviews. The interview guide was developed based on the research objectives, which addressed issues including the participants' experiences with epilepsy, their access to healthcare, social support, coping mechanisms, and stigma. The interview was conducted by the researchers. To obtain informed consent from prospective participants, the study's aim was explained to the prospective participants as part of the recruitment process. They were told that participation in the study was voluntary and that one's decision to participate or not has no bearing on their continued access to healthcare services at the facility. Although the interview guide was designed in English, the preferred local language of prospective participants was used to facilitate communication and understanding in instances where participants did not speak English. This happened with four participants who were assisted with Fante (a local language) translations of the interview questions. The interviews were conducted at the Neurology Clinic while patients waited to be attended to. The interviews were audio-recorded (with prior notice to a prospective participant and consent obtained). After the study, these were transcribed verbatim. The transcripts were analyzed using thematic analysis, a method particularly effective for identifying, analyzing, and reporting recurring patterns within qualitative data. Using the Braun and Clarke's six-step framework, thematic data analysis was conducted to identify patterns and themes from the participants' narratives [15]. These steps are familiarization with data, generation of initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing of themes, defining and naming themes, and writing up (producing) the findings.

Data management

The data was de-identified using alphanumeric codes established for the coding process. It was securely saved and managed to ensure confidentiality and privacy of the participants through the use of a secure password-protected computer. The transcripts and any other data gathered are being kept securely for a specified period as demanded by the Ethics Committee.

Ethical issues

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Cape Coast Teaching Hospital Ethical Review Committee (CCTH ERC) prior to the start of data collection (CCTHERC/EC/2025/145 and dated 4th August 2025). Informed consent was sought and obtained from all participants, or from their caregivers/guardians in cases where participants were under 18 years of age. Participants were assured that their identities would be kept confidential by de-identifying them with codes. Also, they were informed of their right to withdraw from the study, at any time, if they so wished and that decision will not affect the care they receive at the clinic. They were also made aware that participation was entirely voluntary and that the information they provided would be used solely for research purposes, with the potential to contribute to improving the quality of life for YPWE in the future.

Results

A total of 20 participants took part in the study, with ages ranging from 12 to 25 years (Table 1). The majority of participants (60%) were between 18 and 21 years. Males represented 65% of the respondents while females accounted for 35%. Sixteen (80%) of the participants were Christians and four (20%) were Muslims. Most participants (90%) were single, while only two (10%) were married. With regard to educational status, 15% had no formal education, 25% had completed JHS, 30% had SHS education, and 30% were in or had completed tertiary education. Participants were almost evenly split between urban (55%) and rural (45%) residences.

Themes and sub-themes

The findings that emerged from the analysis of the in-depth interview conducted with 20 YPWE who attend the Cape Coast Teaching Hospital are presented and arranged as themes, sub-themes with quotes. Six broad themes consistent with the interview guide were revealed. These themes are: Diagnosis and emotional reactions, access to medication and healthcare services, psychosocial impact, coping strategies, support systems and future aspirations.

Diagnosis and emotional reactions

Participants' initial expressions following the epilepsy diagnosis revealed intense emotions including shock, fear, sadness, and eventual acceptance.

Shock and confusion

“I fainted one day and I was brought to the hospital and was told I have the condition. I didn't understand because I hadn't seen some before, so I was shocked.” (a 17-year-old male respondent).

“I woke up to see myself at the emergency and they told me I have the disease. I was not okay at all, because the disease isn't in my family.” (22 years old male respondent).

Sadness and distress

“I felt sad when it was made known to me.” (20 years-old male respondent).

“I was very worried and cried a lot when I first heard I had epilepsy.” (19 years-old female respondent).

Acceptance and hope

“I am not okay with it, but I know one day God will heal me.” (a 22-year-old male respondent).

“This experience has made me stronger and a better person.” (a 23-years-old female respondent).

Access to medications and healthcare services

Participants reported varied experiences in accessing medications and healthcare services. Challenges included cost, availability of facility, distance to facility, and long waiting times at facility.

Affordability of medications

“Sometimes, I don't get money to afford the medications.” (20-years-old male respondent).

“Though some of the medications are expensive, my parents help get them for me.” (23-years-old female respondent).

Availability of medications

“Sometimes, medications are readily available, but at other times, it's difficult to get some brands.” (23- years-old female respondent).

Experiences with healthcare facilities

“I usually waste much time in long queues and that makes me uncomfortable.” (23-years-old female respondent).

“Distance is far for me; lorry fare is a problem most times.” (20-years-old male respondent).

“They have a very good service. I am okay when it comes to their service.” (22- years-old male respondent).

Psychosocial impact

Epilepsy influenced participants' self-esteem, education, work, and social participation. Medication side effects like dizziness and drowsiness further affected the lives of YPWE.

Educational challenges

“It affects my studies because some of the medication makes me feel drowsy and sleepy most of the time.” (a 23-years-old female respondent).

“The condition has affected my studies because when I take the pills, it makes me dizzy and deters me from learning.” (a 17- year-old male respondent).

Limitations in work and activities

“Since it started, I have not been able to work again.” (a 22 -years-old male respondent).

“I can't do hard work; I can't go to the poolside unless I go with someone to look after me.” (17-years-old male respondent).

Emotional and social strain

“At times, I feel I lack confidence, and feel low.” (22-years-old male respondent).

“At times, life is stressful because I can't partake in some funny activities with friends and sometimes, they judge me.” (17- years-old male respondent).

Coping strategies

Coping with epilepsy require adjustments in lifestyle, reliance on therapy, and spiritual or self-driven encouragement.

Lifestyle adjustments

“I rely on friends when I sense/anticipate an aura; I have avoided carbonated drinks and stress, and changed my sleeping pattern.” (a 23- year-old female respondent).

Support from therapy and counseling

“Initially, it was difficult. But after contacting a therapist, I think I cope well now.” (23-years-old female respondent).

Faith and self-encouragement

“I always encourage myself with the word of God.” (20-years-old male respondent).

“I believe in God so I will be fine.” (22-years-old male respondent).

Support systems

Social and family support emerged as crucial in managing the condition. While some participants reported having strong support, others felt isolated.

Strong family and peer support

“My friends have been supportive to me in school; they bring me lecture notes whenever I am absent.” (23- years-old female respondent).

“My family supports me a lot, they remind me to take my medications and they make sure I have enough sleep.” (17-years, male respondent).

Stigma and lack of support

“I don't get any support from anyone.” (20-years-old male respondent).

“Sometimes, people find it difficult to play with me due to my condition.” (a 20-year-old male respondent).

Future aspirations

Despite the afore-stated challenges, participants expressed optimism for the future. Their goals reflected both personal ambition and a desire to support others with epilepsy.

Personal ambitions

“I want to become an accountant and I still have the hope.” (a 17- year-old male respondent).

“I hope to build my own house because I'm still living with my parents.” (22- years-old male respondent).

Desire to help others

“I want to encourage and help people, especially children, with this condition because I have had a good supportive system.” (23- years-old female respondent).

Discussion

This study explored the lived experiences of young people living with epilepsy who visit the Cape Coast Teaching Hospital in Ghana. Six major themes emerged from the thematic analysis: diagnosis and emotional reactions, access to medications and healthcare services, psychosocial impact, coping strategies, support systems, and future aspirations. These findings highlight the multifaceted impact of epilepsy on the daily lives and aspirations of young people and the crucial role of both medical and social support in managing epilepsy.

Participants' reactions to their diagnosis were predominantly characterized by shock, sadness, fear, and disbelief. For many, the experience was intensified when there was no family history of epilepsy, reflecting the uncertainty and anxiety associated with receiving an unfamiliar diagnosis. Some participants reported initial denial and emotional distress, while others eventually found comfort through faith and counseling. These experiences are consistent with previous research indicating that young people often experience significant emotional turmoil at the point of diagnosis, particularly in contexts where epilepsy is poorly understood or stigmatized [16]. Providing structured counseling at the time of diagnosis has been found to support acceptance of the condition and encourage adherence to treatment [17].

Access to medications and healthcare services emerged as a significant challenge. Participants reported difficulties in consistently obtaining anti-seizure medication due to financial constraints, occasional stockouts, and transportation barriers, particularly for those living in rural areas. Long waiting times at the hospital, CCTH, were also a common concern, although participants appreciated the professionalism and quality of care, once attended to. The literature confirms that limited availability and affordability of medications remain major barriers to effective epilepsy management in sub-Saharan Africa [18], [19]. These barriers contribute to poor adherence to medication, uncontrolled seizures, and increased psychosocial burden [20], emphasizing the need for interventions to improve access and reduce treatment gaps. To ease the financial burden on YPWE, the public healthcare funding institution in the country, the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS), must cover the cost of epilepsy treatment and management, particularly medication cost. Currently, the NHIS covers about 95% of diseases that affect the Ghanaian population. However, it does not cover the cost of management of neurological diseases including epilepsy despite the increasing prevalence of these conditions in the country and their contribution to disability and mortality. Recently, the NHIS levy has been uncapped making significant amount of money available for the management of various diseases hence an opportune moment to ensure the placement of neurological care on to the Scheme. Given that YPWE are generally either expected to be in school or unemployed, given their young age, the inclusion of the cost of managing epilepsy on the NHIS promises to significantly improve the condition of these patients as it would affect, among others, their ability to obtain anti-seizure medications.

Coping strategies adopted by participants included lifestyle modifications, such as regulating sleep, avoiding known seizure triggers, relying on friends during aura, and maintaining regular medication adherence. Participants also drew on personal resilience and fortitude and religious faith to manage the psychological burden of epilepsy reinforcing the religious nature of most Ghanaians. Recreational activities and social engagement were identified as mechanisms to reduce stress and maintain emotional wellbeing. These results are consistent with previous studies showing that effective coping mechanisms improve both treatment adherence and mental well-being in YPWE [21].

The psychosocial impact of epilepsy was evident in disruption to education, employment, and social interactions. Medication side effects, particularly drowsiness and cognitive difficulties, affected academic performance and productivity of some YPWE. Participants also reported experiences of stigma and social isolation, which affected confidence and peer relationships. Similar findings have been documented in Ghana and other sub-Sahara African countries, where stigma remains a significant barrier to social inclusion and can lead to marginalization and reduced self-esteem [22], [23], [24], [25]. These findings highlight the importance of community education and mass awareness initiatives to reduce misconceptions and promote social acceptance. In these days of social media, mass campaigns including use of online media may be a cost-efficient measure in reducing epilepsy-associated stigmatization.

Support systems like family support, with relatively less community support, were instrumental in helping participants manage their condition. Family members provided reminders for medication, emotional encouragement, and practical assistance, which facilitated adherence and reduced anxiety. Peer support and understanding/affable mentors also contributed positively, although variability in support indicated that not all participants had equal access. Aside support from immediate family to almost all the YPWE interviewed, comparatively, females received much community level support as well as emotional and financial assistance. This may reflect a wider sense of societal empathy towards females with epilepsy. This may be because females are reported to suffer more from psychosocial challenges associated with epilepsy including high anxiety and stigma [26], [27]. Social support networks are recognized as protective factors that enhance psychological well-being and functional outcomes among persons with epilepsy [28], suggesting that structured support programs and counseling services should complement medical/clinical management. Additionally, health literacy should be encouraged as this would offer an opportunity to share adequate information on epilepsy with not only the affected patients but also their caregivers, families and communities. This has the potential to result in improved quality of life of YPWE as they would then understand that living with the condition will not have disastrous effects on their lives as well as help in preventing stigmatization in the communities.

Despite the challenges of living with epilepsy, participants expressed hope for the future and maintained personal aspirations in education and career development. Many viewed their condition as a challenge rather than a limitation and demonstrated resilience in pursuing goals. Optimism and goal-oriented thinking have been identified as protective factors, enabling YPWE to navigate social and academic challenges effectively [28]. Interventions focusing on vocational guidance, educational support, and empowerment can help this population achieve their aspirations.

While this study provides valuable insights into the lived experiences of young people with epilepsy, some limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, the study was conducted in a single hospital, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other settings or populations. While we used a sufficient sample size in the present study, increasing the sample size may reveal experiences that may differ in larger or more diverse populations. In addition, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce recall bias or social desirability bias, as participants may have presented themselves in a favorable light. In future studies, these challenges would be catered for.

Conclusion

Epilepsy affects multiple dimensions of young people, including their emotional, cognitive, social, educational, and functional domains. Since this occurs during a significant developmental stage in their lives where they are forming their unique self-identities, epilepsy may have huge impact on their lives. Participants in this study reported initial feelings of fear, shock, and sadness upon diagnosis, as well as challenges in accessing medications and healthcare services. Despite these difficulties, coping strategies, family, peer and communal support, reliance on personal faith and resilience helped participants manage the condition and maintain hope for the future. These findings suggest that epilepsy is not only a medical condition but also a social and psychological experience that requires holistic interventions. Addressing both clinical needs and psychosocial challenges is critical in improving the quality of life for YPWE. Future interventions should emphasize integrated care that combines medical management, psychosocial support, community education, and empowerment strategies for YPWE.
