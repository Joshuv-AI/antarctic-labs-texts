Evolution of the transitional millisecond pulsar PSR J1023+0038 from Aqueye+ and NICER observations
S. Conforti ,, L. Zampieri, M. Fiori, A. Spolon, G. Naletto, A. Burtovoi

Abstract
Context. Transitional Millisecond Pulsars (tMSPs) are old, rapidly rotating neutron stars spun up by accretion from a low-mass companion star. These objects can switch between two emission regimes: rotation-powered radio pulsar and accreting X-ray pulsar. The mechanism responsible for these pulsations remains debated, with one prominent model suggesting that optical and X-ray pulsations arise from synchrotron emission produced by a shock resulting from the interaction between the pulsar wind and the accretion disk. This scenario is supported by the minimal phase lag between optical and X-ray pulses in PSR J1023+0038, and by their detection in the high-mode/flaring state of the system. Aims. We present a new measurement of the phase lag between optical and X-ray pulse profiles of PSR J1023+0038 and investigate the recent (till 2023) evolution of the binary system, monitoring the variation of the time of passage at the ascending node ( T a ​ s ​ c T_{asc} ). Methods. We performed a timing analysis of the optical observations of PSR J1023+0038 taken with Aqueye+ from 2021 through 2023, and of the X-ray observations of NICER taken in 2023. In particular, we used the optical and X-ray data to derive new measurements for the variation of T a ​ s ​ c T_{asc} , and employed simultaneous Aqueye+ and NICER observations to measure the phase lag between optical and X-ray pulses. Results. We find the shift of T a ​ s ​ c T_{asc} increases of ∼ \sim 20 s each year, and in January 2023, we measured a phase lag between optical and X-ray data of 0.067 ± 0.018 0.067\pm 0.018 , or 112.3 ± 30.7 ​ μ 112.3\pm 30.7\,\mu s. Conclusions. After 2017, the variation of the time of passage at the ascending node follows a well-defined parabolic increasing trend, suggesting a corresponding increment in the orbital period and orbital separation of the system. This evolution is consistent with a scenario in which the binary system is evolving through non-conservative Roche lobe overflow and the donor is interacting with the pulsar wind, losing mass at a rate much higher than the inferred accretion rate. The measurement of the phase lag between optical and X-ray data confirms the common origin of such pulsations.



1
Introduction

Millisecond pulsars (MSPs) are old and fast-spinning neutron stars, spun up to periods shorter than 30 ms by accretion of matter and angular momentum from a companion star
(
1
;
29
;
30
)
. Most of them are in low-mass binary systems (
<1M_{\odot}
), have weak magnetic fields (
\sim 10^{8}-10^{9}\,\rm{G}
), and are observed as accreting X-ray pulsars, or as rotation-powered radio pulsars. Recent works identified MSPs that can switch between these two regimes, called transition Millisecond Pulsars (tMPS). In particular, these sources experience a transition of state on timescales of a few weeks, passing from a rotation-powered regime, where they act like radio pulsars with winds that prevent accretion onto their surface, to a regime where they accrete matter lost by the companion star, emitting a high amount of X-ray luminosity like X-ray binary systems
(
26
)
. Only three tMPS are known so far, PSR J1023+0038
(
3
)
, PSR J1227-4853
(
11
)
and PSR J1824-2452
(
27
)
.

PSR J1023+0038 was initially classified as a Cataclysmic Variable
(
8
)
, but between 2000 and 2001 observations showed the existence of an accretion disk, which soon disappeared, leaving a radio pulsar
(
32
;
3
)
. In 2013, the source underwent a new transition in its emission regime, with the disappearance of the radio pulsations, the reappearance of the accretion disk, and the onset of a strong double-peaked
\rm{H_{\alpha}}
emission observed in the optical spectrum
(
16
;
28
;
31
)
. PSR J1023+0038 is also the only tMPS that shows detectable UV
(
23
;
20
)
and optical pulsations, the latest were first discovered with SiFAP2 at the Telescopio Nazionale Galileo
(
2
)
and then confirmed with Aqueye+ at the Copernicus telescope in Asiago
(
36
)
, and with the panoramic photometer-polarimeter mounted at BTA in Nizhniy Arkhyz
(
21
)
. The emission mechanism of these optical pulsations is still under debate. Several models have been proposed to explain it
(
10
;
25
;
33
, and reference therein)
. In particular, the shock-driven mini pulsar nebula scenario
(
33
;
25
)
predicts that optical and X-ray pulsations are produced by synchrotron emission caused by shocks that form when the pulsar wind meets the accretion disk. The fact that optical and X-ray pulses show a very short phase lag, between
0-0.15
, and the fact that both appear only when the source is in the high mode state, with X-ray pulsations in the high mode first discovered by
(
4
)
, support this model and a common emission mechanism for the optical and X-ray pulsations
(
18
)
.

In the present work, we aim to study the evolution of the binary system, monitoring the variation of the time of passage at the ascending node,
T_{\rm{asc}}
, and providing a more recent measurement of the phase lag between optical and X-ray pulse profiles. To achieve this, we performed a timing analysis of four
Aqueye+
observing runs from 2021 through 2023, as well as of
NICER
X-ray data from 2023. We derived the timing solution of the source for the 2021 data set, and we analyzed the evolution of
T_{\rm{asc}}
incorporating results from previous works
(
36
;
9
;
18
;
25
;
2
;
19
;
3
)
. Finally, we consider a model for the evolution of the Roche lobe radius of the donor as a function of the mass loss rate, to account for the observed long-term behavior of
T_{\rm{asc}}
.

In Section
2
we present the observations and data reduction for the
Aqueye+
and
NICER
observations. In Section
3
, we describe the correction for the orbital motion and the results of our timing analysis. We discuss our results in Section
4

4
Discussion

4.1
Evolution of
T_{\rm{asc}}

In Section
3.1
, we measured the variation of the time of passage at the ascending node from 2021 through 2023. We reported our results in Figure
1
, alongside those obtained in previous works. We fitted the
\Delta T_{\rm{asc}}
evolution with a second-order polynomial (see equation (
1
)), which is consistent with the observed trend of the data, in particular if we consider the
\Delta T_{\rm{asc}}
values after the observations performed by
2
. In the past, the variation of the
T_{\rm{asc}}
was consistent with an irregular evolutionary scenario for the binary system, supported by its initial oscillating behavior after the onset of the accreting phase. Recent results, however, seem to support a different scenario, where the increase of the
T_{\rm{asc}}
could be driven by a rise in the orbital period, and thus in the separation between the companion star and the neutron star. In this section, we discuss a possible general evolutionary scenario consistent with our recent results and the characteristics of this binary system.

Assuming accretion through Roche Lobe overflow (
34
,
15
), the exchange of mass leads to a variation in the distance between the two stars, due to the conservation of angular momentum, which translates into variations in the orbital period. Let us consider the general equation of the total angular momentum of the system,
J
, and its time derivative,
\dot{J}
, assuming a circular orbit:

J=M_{1}M_{2}\sqrt{\frac{Ga}{M_{1}+M_{2}}}M_{\odot}^{3/2},

(4)

where
G
is the gravitational constant,
a
is the orbital separation, and
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are the masses of the neutron star and of the donor star, respectively. The time derivative of equation (
4
) is:

2\frac{\dot{J}}{J}=\frac{\dot{a}}{a}+2\left(\frac{\dot{M_{1}}}{M_{1}}+\frac{\dot{M_{2}}}{M_{2}}\right)-\frac{\dot{M_{1}}+\dot{M_{2}}}{M_{1}+M_{2}},

(5)

and the total mass and mass ratio of the system are defined as

M=M_{1}+M_{2}\,\,\,,\,\,\,\,\,q=\frac{M_{2}}{M_{1}}\,\,.

(6)

We use the equation that approximates the evolution of the Roche Lobe radius of the donor star,
R_{2L}
, for
0.1\leq q\leq 0.8
, which is given by
(
14
)
:

\frac{\dot{R_{2L}}}{R_{2L}}=\frac{\dot{a}}{a}+\frac{\dot{M_{2}}}{3M_{2}}-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\dot{M}}{M},

(7)

The accretion luminosity in X-rays is
L_{X}=7\times 10^{33}\ \rm{erg\ s^{-1}}
in the high mode state, and
L_{X}=3\times 10^{33}\ \rm{erg\ s^{-1}}
in the low mode state
(
22
)
. Neglecting emission from the NS surface, we estimate the mass accretion rate of the source from:

L_{\rm{acc}}=\xi\frac{GM\dot{M}_{\rm{acc}}}{R_{\star}}\ \ \ \ ,

(8)

where
G
is the gravitational constant,
M
is the mass of the neutron star,
\dot{M}_{\rm{acc}}
the mass accretion rate,
R_{\star}
the radius of the neutron star, and
\xi
is the accretion efficiency, which depends in turn on the inner radius,
R_{\rm{in}}
, of the accretion disk (
\xi=R_{\rm{\star}}/R_{in}
). In the case of PSR J1023+0038, in the high mode state, material channeled along the magnetic field lines is likely not accreted onto the NS (as it would otherwise quench the pulsar wind), but expelled in a jet
(
25
;
6
)
. In the model of
6
, the inner radius of the disk, in the high mode state, is beyond the light cylinder radius, at
\simeq 80\,\rm{km}
, while when the source is in the low mode state the disk recedes up to a distance of
\simeq 1600\,\rm{km}
, and most of the X-ray emission produced by the compact jet along the pulsar rotational axis. Assuming
R\simeq 80\,\rm{km}
, and considering the X-ray accretion luminosity in the high mode, we obtain

\dot{M}_{\rm{acc}}=4.22\times 10^{14}\left(\frac{M}{M_{\odot}}\right)^{-1}\ \rm{g\cdot s^{-1}}

(9)

For a conservative mass transfer, the value of the mass accretion rate is between
\dot{M_{2}}=-1.1\times 10^{19}\,\rm{g\,s^{-1}}
, if
q=0.1
, and
\dot{M_{2}}=-2.5\times 10^{18}\,\rm{g\,s^{-1}}
, if
q=0.8

(
14
, see)
. These values, being at least four orders of magnitude higher than the value obtained from the observed X-ray luminosity, denote the inconsistency of a conservative accretion scenario.

The fact that the pulsar in PSR J1023+0038 is active implies that a powerful pulsar wind hits the donor surface, contributing to slowly evaporate the companion, as observed in other ”redback binary systems”.
We may then consider a non-conservative case, when the accretion rate onto the neutron star is negligible and the mass loss is dominated by the donor,
\dot{M_{1}}\simeq\dot{M}_{\rm{acc}}<<-\dot{M}_{2}
, and
\dot{M}\simeq\dot{M_{2}}
. Then, eq. (
5
) assumes the following form:

2\frac{\dot{J}}{J}=\frac{\dot{a}}{a}+2\left(\frac{\dot{M_{2}}}{M_{1}}\right)-\frac{\dot{M_{2}}}{M_{1}+M_{2}}\,\,.

(10)

Taking the time derivative of Kepler’s third law, under the same assumptions, we obtain:

\frac{\dot{a}}{a}=\frac{1}{3}\frac{\dot{M_{2}}}{M_{2}}\frac{M_{2}}{M}+\frac{2}{3}\frac{\dot{P}}{P}.

(11)

where
P
is the orbital period. Since we do not know exactly how the companion star evolves, interacting with the pulsar wind, we consider a general case, where the radius of the donor evolves as:

\frac{\dot{R_{2}}}{R_{2}}=\alpha\frac{\dot{M_{2}}}{M_{2}}\,\,.

(12)

When
\alpha=1
, this relation represents the evolution of a low mass donor that is losing mass in a quasi-steady state. Assuming that the system is in (or close to) Roche lobe overflow, and hence
R_{2}\simeq R_{\rm{2L}}
, we substitute this expression into equation (
7
) and then we combine it with equation (
11
). The resulting relation is:

\frac{\dot{P}}{P}=\frac{3}{2}\beta\frac{\dot{M_{2}}}{M_{2}}\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,,\,\,\,\,\,\,\rm{where}\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\beta=\left(\alpha-\frac{1}{3}\right)\,\,\,.

(13)

As mentioned above, the case
\alpha=1
represents the evolution of a low mass donor in a quasi-steady state. This is not a plausible scenario as
\beta=-2/3
and
{\dot{P}}/P={\dot{M}_{2}}/M_{2}<0
, at variance with the observed increase of the orbital period. The same conclusion holds in general for
\alpha>1/3
, while for
\alpha<1/3
,
{\dot{P}}/P\propto-{\dot{M}_{2}}/M_{2}>0
, which is consistent with the observations. We note that the case
\alpha=0
(
\beta=1/3
) would represent the evolution of a system with a constant donor and Roche lobe radius. In these conditions, the mass loss rate of the donor would be:
-\dot{M}_{2}/M_{2}=(1/2)(\dot{P}/P)=8.2\times 10^{18}\,\rm{gs^{-1}}
, higher by four orders of magnitude than the mass accretion rate. As we do not know the physics of the interaction between the pulsar wind and the donor, different values of
\alpha
would be possible, implying different evolutions for the donor and different values for the mass loss rate, but of the same order of magnitude as the previous estimate for
|\alpha|\approx 1
. At this pace, the donor would be completely consumed in
\sim 8
M yrs. Thus, a scenario with a binary system evolving through non-conservative Roche lobe overflow, similar to the one presented above, appears to be consistent with the observed steady parabolic increase of the orbital period with time.

4.2
Time Delay between Optical and X-rays

We analyzed
NICER
and
Aqueye+
data of January 2023 to perform new measurements of the time shift between optical and X-ray pulses of PSR J1023+0034.
We managed to measure the shift simultaneously between optical and X-rays only for the January 2023 data (January 21st), because for the December 2023 data set, the duration of the simultaneous X-ray observation (December 15th) was too short. The time lag between optical and X-ray pulses is
0.036\pm 0.016
, which corresponds to
60.7\pm 27.7\,\rm{\mu s}
. This result is consistent with that of
18
, in which they reported that, from the second harmonic of the pulsed profiles, the time lag between optical and X-ray pulses lies in the range of
(0-250)\,\rm{\mu s}
. This, along with the fact that both optical and X-ray pulses have similar morphology, confirms that they are produced in the same region and with the same emission mechanism, as proposed by
25
;
33
(see references therein). In this work, they present a possible scenario to explain such a simultaneous emission, the so-called striped wind model. In such a scenario, the source is in the disk state, but still able to emit a relativistic wind. In a region a few light cylinder radii from the star, the pulsar wind interacts with the accretion disk, producing two shocked symmetric regions, where the synchrotron radiation is produced, and pulsed emission in the X-ray and optical bands originates. They also estimated the synchrotron timescale for optical photons, getting a value compatible with the lag observed between optical and X-ray photons.

5
Summary

In the present work, we aim to study the evolution of the PSR J1023+0038 binary system, monitoring the long-term variation of the time of passage at the ascending node,
T_{\rm{asc}}
, and providing new measurements of the phase lag between the optical and X-ray pulse profiles. We performed a timing analysis of three runs of
Aqueye+
optical data from 2021 through 2023, as well as
NICER
X-ray data from 2023. We derived the timing solution of the source for January 2021, and we analyzed the evolution of the
T_{\rm{asc}}
, including also results from previous works.
We measured the shift between optical and X-ray pulses, using simultaneous
Aqueye+
optical data and
NICER
X-ray data, of January 2023.
The phase lag measured is
0.036\pm 0.016
, in agreement with
18
and extending the baseline of these measurements by one year. This result further confirms the common origin and emission mechanism of X-ray and optical pulses. Concerning the evolution of the
T_{\rm{asc}}
, starting from 2017, it follows a parabolic increasing trend, which translates into an increase of the orbital period and orbital separation of the system.
A scenario of a binary system evolving through non-conservative Roche lobe overflow in which the donor is interacting with the pulsar wind, losing mass at a rate much higher than the inferred accretion rate, appears to be consistent with the observed steady increase of the orbital period of the system. The large majority of the mass stripped from the donor is likely to be shocked by the pulsar wind itself and ejected from the system after crossing the Roche lobe. Further optical and X-ray timing observations of PSR J1023+0038 are definitely needed to continue to monitor the evolution of the parameters of the binary system and to determine the long-term rotational history of the pulsar.
