Comparison of Clinical Outcomes of Two Different Cardioplegic Solutions in Patients Undergoing Coronary Artery Bypass Graft Surgery; A randomize clinical trial
Authors: Alireza Hosseini, Mehran Shahzamani, Asieh Maghami-Mehr, Farzin Khosravi, Nafiseh Emadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Usually, manual cardioplegic solutions with low magnesium and lidocaine, and corporate solutions with high magnesium and procaine, are the only common solutions used to induce cardiac arrest during coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery. This study compares the clinical outcomes of these two solutions.Materials and Methods:This randomized, single-blind clinical trial was conducted on 70 patients undergoing CABG at Chamran Hospital in Isfahan. In the first group, a manual cardioplegic solution was used. In the second group, a corporate cardioplegic solution (Blood; Shahid Ghazi Company) was used. Subsequently, inflammatory factors, complications, and outcomes were evaluated in the two groups.Results:There were no significant differences between the two groups in the incidence of arrhythmia after declamping, the total inotropic support required within 24 h post-surgery, changes in electrolytes, or markers of myocardial injury (P > 0.05). However, the time to return to heart rhythm in the manual cardioplegic solution group, with a mean of 146.06 ± 118.39 s, was significantly longer than that in the corporate cardioplegic solution group, with a mean of 90.60 ± 59.72 s (P = 0.016).Conclusion:This study found no difference in patient outcomes between the corporate cardioplegic solution (high magnesium/procaine) and the manual solution (lower magnesium/lidocaine). Therefore, the manual solution can be used in some cases, as its lower magnesium level avoids hypermagnesemia and does not cause cardiac muscle weakness during mechanical activities.

INTRODUCTION

Cardioplegic solution plays a crucial role in minimizing cardiac damage and providing cellular protection during open-heart surgery, particularly during the ischemic phase following aortic clamping. Solutions with high potassium concentrations disrupt ion exchange and channel function, ultimately stopping cardiac activity. The elevated potassium levels in these solutions reduce the resting membrane potential from − 90 mV to − 65 mV, leading to sodium channel inactivation and resulting in diastolic cardiac arrest.[1] Cardioplegic solutions protect myocardial cells in two ways: by reducing cellular metabolism and oxygen demand during aortic clamping and ischemia, and by preventing cell damage caused by reperfusion after clamp removal.[2]

Administering high doses of potassium chloride to induce intraoperative cardiac arrest can result in abnormal blood potassium levels, depletion of cellular energy reserves during ischemia, impaired recovery of muscle fibers’ contractile function, arrhythmia, and irreversible cardiac arrest. Alternatively, using lower potassium concentrations combined with magnesium and sodium/calcium channel blockers such as lidocaine and adenosine has been shown to yield better clinical outcomes.[3]

Magnesium, a key component of cardioplegic solutions, acts as a negative inotropic agent. It helps counteract ischemia-induced hypomagnesemia and inhibits ion movement, playing a vital role in cardioplegia. When included in calcium-rich cardioplegic solutions, magnesium prevents calcium overload in muscle fibers, preserves high-energy phosphates, reduces reperfusion-related damage, and eases cardiac workload.[4]

Using higher magnesium concentrations (16 mmol/L) in cardioplegic solutions, compared to lower amounts (8 mmol/L), has been found to significantly reduce cardiac cell damage and enhance cellular protection. Studies also indicate that administering 80–100 mg/kg of magnesium during aortic clamping decreases the incidence of atrial fibrillation (AF) and shortens hospital stay duration.[4]

Using a higher concentration of magnesium (16 mmol/L) in cardioplegic solutions, compared to a lower amount (8 mmol/L), has been shown to significantly reduce cardiac cell damage and enhance cellular protection.[5]

Studies indicate that administering 80–100 mg/kg of magnesium during aortic clamping can lower the incidence of AF and reduce hospital stay duration.[6]

Lidocaine, a sodium channel blocker, inhibits the rapid sodium ion channels during the action potential phase, leading to cardiac arrest during the aortic clamp period. By decreasing cellular workload and metabolism, it exerts its protective effects. Additionally, lidocaine can induce genetic modifications at the genomic level of myocardial cells, affecting specific genes responsible for reducing inflammation, decreasing intracellular calcium binding, enhancing apoptotic cell protection, increasing oxygen availability via transferrin, and ultimately improving cell survival.[7]

Procaine, another sodium channel blocker, is incorporated into newer cardioplegic solutions. Comparative studies on the combination of adenosine with lidocaine versus adenosine with procaine as cardioplegic solutions have demonstrated distinct clinical effects.[8]

This study aims to evaluate and compare the effects of two manual and corporate cardioplegic solutions with different magnesium concentrations and the use of sodium channel blockers, lidocaine and procaine on both clinical and laboratory outcomes in coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) surgeries.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This single-blind, randomized clinical trial was conducted after obtaining ethical approval (IR.MUI.MED.REC.1403.177) from Isfahan University of Medical Sciences and the clinical trial code (Approval code: IRCT20200825048515N80). The trial was performed on 70 patients scheduled for CABG with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) at Chamran Hospital of Isfahan in 2024.

Inclusion criteria were age over 18 years, candidacy for CABG with CPB, absence of preoperative arrhythmia, ejection fraction (EF) above 30%, and willingness to participate in the study. Patients were excluded if they had abnormal preoperative potassium or magnesium levels, severe aortic valve insufficiency, significant left main coronary artery stenosis, or required additional cardiac surgeries. Participants who withdrew from the study, were transferred to another medical facility before intensive care unit (ICU) discharge, required reoperation, developed severe postoperative complications, or passed away were also excluded.

Eligible patients were randomly assigned to two groups of 35 by using the Random Allocation software. Baseline and clinical data, including gender, age, weight, height, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, EF, and history of substance use, were recorded at study entry.

All patients underwent CABG using the same surgical technique and identical anesthesia protocol, ensuring uniform general anesthesia.

The manual cardioplegic solution was prepared in the operating room by the perfusionist for each procedure. It was formulated by adding 40 mEq of potassium chloride (20 mL of 15% KCl), 1 g of magnesium sulfate, 50 mg of lidocaine, and 12.5 mEq of sodium bicarbonate to 500 mL of Ringer’s solution as the base. Following aortic clamping and the placement of a double-lumen needle in the aortic root by the surgeon, the solution—cooled to 4°C—was administered at a dose of 20 mL/kg by using two specialized small roller pumps in the cardiopulmonary bypass machine. The solution was delivered antegradely through a dedicated line, mixed with oxygenated blood in a 1:4 blood-to-crystalloid ratio, at a real-time pressure of 60–80 mmHg and a flow rate of 300–400 mL/min for 2 min into the aortic root.[2]

The corporate cardioplegic solution (Blood; Shahid Ghazi Company), containing a 50 mL vial containing 40 mEq of potassium chloride, 4.8 g of magnesium sulfate, 13.64 mg of procaine, and 12.5 mEq of sodium bicarbonate, was administered with a combination of 500 mL of Ringer’s solution as the base. Following aortic clamping and the placement of the double-lumen needle in the aortic root by the surgeon, the solution was administered similarly to the previous group.

It should be noted that in both groups, if additional cardioplegic doses were needed, 300–400 mL of the solution was administered under the same conditions.

It should be noted that to comply with the blinding conditions, given that both cardioplegic solutions were administered under general anesthesia during surgery, the investigator was aware of the type of intervention in each group. However, the patients, the data collector, and the statistician were unaware of the type of cardioplegic solution received in the two groups.

Time of onset of induced cardiac arrest, and time to return to heart rhythm after aortic clamp removal (based on ECG), occurrence of arrhythmias post-clamp removal; weaning from or return to CPB; total inotropic support required within 24 h post-surgery; ICU length of stay and patient outcomes; Serum potassium and magnesium levels measured at multiple time points: before surgery, 10 min after cardioplegia, 5 min post-clamp removal, and at 6, 12, and 24 h postoperatively; Serum levels of troponin I, C-reactive protein (CRP), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) measured preoperatively and at 12 and 24 h post-surgery were measured and recorded.

It is worth mentioning that a 5 mL blood sample was collected preoperatively by the anesthesia team. During surgery, two blood samples of 3 mL each were taken from the oxygenator. Within 24 h post-surgery, three additional blood samples of 5 mL each were drawn from the patient’s CV line. All samples were sent to Chamran Hospital’s laboratory within 5 min. Serum levels of potassium, magnesium, LDH, CRP, and troponin I were measured at each stage, all using the same device and operator in the hospital’s laboratory. The laboratory results were documented in the patient’s physical and electronic records, with laboratory confirmation.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were entered into SPSS (version 27;a SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) software. Quantitative and qualitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and n (%), respectively. Independent t-tests were used to compare the means of quantitative variables between the two groups at each follow-up time point. For comparing the mean of quantitative variables over time within each group, repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied. Additionally, the Chi-square test was used to compare the distribution of qualitative variables between the two groups. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses.

RESULTS

In the present study, out of 35 patients in the manual cardioplegic solution group, there were 26 (74.3%) male and nine (25.7%) female, with a mean age of 59.49 ± 6.44 years. In the corporate cardioplegic solution group, 32 patients (91.4%) were male and three (8.6%) were female, with a mean age of 60.17 ± 7.39 years (P > 0.05). The two groups did not show significant differences in other demographic and clinical characteristics such as weight, height, BMI, addiction, underlying diseases, and EF (P > 0.05) [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

The results of the present study investigating the laboratory and clinical outcomes of two different cardioplegic solutions, differentiated by the amount of magnesium and the type of sodium channel blocker (lidocaine instead of procaine), indicated that the levels of potassium and sodium did not show a significant difference between the two groups at any of the study time points. However, their changes over time were not significant in each of the two groups. In contrast, the magnesium level at 10 min after the first prescribed dose of cardioplegic solution, 5 min after declamping, and at 6, 12, and 24 h after surgery was significantly higher in the corporate cardioplegic solution group compared to the manual cardioplegic solution group. The higher magnesium level in the corporate cardioplegic solution compared to the manual may be considered an advantage for the corporate cardioplegic solution, as the greater amount of magnesium in this solution compared to the manual does not lead to an abnormal increase in magnesium and the occurrence of postoperative hypermagnesemia, nor to cardiac muscle weakness in performing mechanical activities.

Cardioplegia formulations and administration strategies evolved through research and experience, leading to varied solutions across centers.[9] Inadequate myocardial protection elevated lactate levels, indicating anaerobic metabolism, myocardial acidosis, and lactate production.[1011]

In addition, the different content in cardioplegic solutions and sodium bicarbonate in St. Thomas-II may affect the lactate levels after ischemic event initiation induced by aortic cross-clamping.[12]

A study states that serum lactate levels were significantly elevated in groups of Del-Nido (DN) cardioplegic solution and St. Thomas-II cardioplegia as Modified St. Thomas solution (MST).[13] Another study that compared two widely used crystalloid cardioplegics—Bret-Schneider (Custodiol®) versus St. Thomas-II—in patients who underwent right minithoracotomy mitral valve surgery concluded that using St. Thomas-II cardioplegia solution was associated with higher myocardial protection based on cardiac markers, such as troponin I, compared to other cardioplegia solutions.[14]

However, other studies showed different results in myocardial protection in patients undergoing cardiopulmonary bypass cardiac surgeries.[1516]

In this study, inflammatory factors such as CRP, LDH, and troponin I did not show a significant difference between the two groups before CPB and 12 and 24 h after surgery. In other words, it can be said that troponin I, as an important indicator of myocardial cell damage, did not differ between the two groups, and therefore, these two solutions did not differ in cellular protection and prevention of cardiac tissue damage and destruction.

Cardioplegic solutions protect the heart from myocardial injury during mitral valve surgery.[1718] Initially, the St. Thomas solution was invented as crystalloid cardioplegia, which provided reliable cardiac arrest and desirable levels of myocardial protection. However, the amount of cardioplegia used caused much hemodilution and many challenges in managing complications.[19]

In the study by Arki et al.,[20] it was revealed that the cardio-protective effect of the St. Thomas-II cardioplegia solution may be superior to the St. Thomas-I group.

Although these solutions have been studied extensively, evidence on the perfect preservation strategy during cardiac surgery is still disputed. This debatable evidence might in part be due to different patient cohorts, various solutions, and miscellaneous preservation strategies. Additionally, many different definitions of myocardial injury or low-output syndrome following cardiac procedures have been used to describe study endpoints. These definitions range from laboratory parameters in the form of elevated serum levels of CK-MB or troponin to mostly indirect clinical signs such as the occurrence of arrhythmias or ECG changes.[21]

The explanation for this lack of evidence in the face of a thorough clinical and laboratory investigation is complex. Complicating the comparison of various cardioplegic solutions is the great variety of commercially available cardioplegic solutions such as Custodiol or homemade blood cardioplegia as initially described by Calafiore, as well as the various options of application (warm vs. tepid vs. cold, antegrade vs. retrograde, single-shot or intermittent application).[22]

The clinical outcomes of patients between the two groups showed that the onset time of cardiac arrest in the manual cardioplegic solution group was slightly and insignificantly longer than that in the corporate cardioplegic solution group. In other words, the corporate cardioplegic solution led to cardiac arrest faster than the manual cardioplegic solution, which may be due to the quicker effect of the higher amount of magnesium and procaine compared to the lower amounts of magnesium and lidocaine in the manual cardioplegic solution group. All patients required inotropes, and there was no significant difference in the prescribed dose of inotropes between the two groups. In each group, six patients experienced arrhythmias after declamping, which were treated with medication, shock, or both methods, with no significant difference between them. In contrast, the time to return to heart rhythm in the manual cardioplegic solution group was significantly longer than that in the corporate cardioplegic solution group. Finally, the length of stay in the ICU did not show a significant difference between the two groups, and all patients were alive, with no reported deaths.

So far, few randomized clinical trials (RCTs) have been conducted to find the best cardioplegic solution. The results of the study by Øvrum et al.[23] showed that there was no superiority of retrograde cold blood cardioplegia over retrograde cold crystalloid cardioplegia in terms of spontaneous sinus rhythm after aortic valve opening, use of inotropic drugs, duration of ventilatory support, bleeding and allogeneic blood transfusion rate, intraoperative myocardial infarction, episodes of AF, stroke or minor neurological dysfunction, renal function, infections, physical rehabilitation, or mortality in patients undergoing aortic valve replacement, with or without CABG.

Sodium bicarbonate buffers excess hydrogen ions to maintain intracellular pH during myocardial arrest, preventing inhibition of anaerobic adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production. Potassium chloride induces rapid depolarized arrest to preserve ATP. Lidocaine blocks sodium channels, preventing intracellular sodium accumulation. Mannitol acts as an oxygen-free radical scavenger and has osmotic properties that can reduce cardiac myocyte edema. Magnesium functions as a calcium channel blocker and can reduce the buildup of intracellular calcium, which can lead to diastolic stiffness, resulting in poor filling and decreased cardiac function post cross-clamp removal.[24]

Finally, the composition of the various cardioplegia solutions used during cardiac surgery varies as much between institutions as it does between individual surgeons.

Therefore, one of the reasons for emphasizing this study on manual cardioplegic solution is that the preparation of cardioplegic solution cannot be limited to a specific company or brand. In situations where this solution is unavailable, such as sanctions, high costs, or lack of production by a company, it can be prepared quickly in the operating room. Furthermore, it may even be possible to adjust the amounts of certain substances in the cardioplegic solution for patients with specific conditions, such as abnormal electrolyte levels, to minimize myocardial damage and maximize protection. In other words, it may be better not to use a standardized cardioplegic solution with established contents for all patients, and ideally, we should be able to prepare a suitable cardioplegic solution tailored to each patient in the operating room.

One of the weaknesses of this study is the small sample size. In addition, a corporate cardioplegic solution with the brand name of “Blood” was used, which is very similar to St. Thomas, but none of the conventional cardioplegic solutions such as Del Nido or St Thomas were used; therefore, it is recommended that further studies be conducted on the other conventional solutions. In addition, the type of cardioplegic solution administration, which can be retrograde, antegrade, or a combination of both methods, should be considered, although in this study, all solutions were administered in the antegrade method; different methods of administering may have different clinical results.

CONCLUSION

According to the results of the present study, the effect of two different cardioplegic solutions with high amounts of magnesium and procaine (corporate cardioplegic solution) instead of lower amounts of magnesium and lidocaine (manual cardioplegic solution) did not show a significant change in the patients’ clinical conditions during and after surgery. The only difference was that the onset time of cardiac arrest in the corporate cardioplegic solution group was longer than that in the manual cardioplegic solution group; however, there were no differences in the time to return to heart rhythm and in the patients’ outcomes. Therefore, it may be said that both solutions can reliably protect against myocardial injury, although it is suggested that similar studies with larger sample sizes be conducted to generalize the results to the population.
