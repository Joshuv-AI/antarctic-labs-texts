Recurrent unexplained resting sinus tachycardia as a marker of adverse cardiovascular outcomes in cancer patients
Authors: Moied Al Sakan, Bahaa El Deen Wehbeh, Georgio Sayde, Yeva Fakih, Samia Mitri, Joe R Eid, Amal Jabali, Firas Kreidieh, Maurice Khoury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecurrent unexplained resting sinus tachycardia is common in cancer patients and is frequently attributed to reversible physiologic stressors. Whether unexplained resting sinus tachycardia is associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes (ACVO) and mortality in oncology populations remains poorly defined.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study enrolled 198 adults with confirmed cancer and ≥ 3 electrocardiogram (ECG) studies during 3 separate hospital encounters at a tertiary center (January 2019–January 2026). Patients with ≥3 ECG-confirmed episodes of unexplained resting sinus tachycardia (n = 38) were compared to patients with normal sinus rhythm (NSR) (n = 160). The primary outcome was ACVO; secondary outcomes included major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and all-cause mortality. Logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards models were used for multivariable analysis. A landmark sensitivity analysis was additionally performed to address potential immortal-time bias.ResultsPatients with recurrent resting sinus tachycardia had significantly lower hemoglobin (9.8 ± 1.4 vs. 11.1 ± 2.3 g/dL; p < 0.001) at baseline. Blood pressure at the index ECG was modestly lower but within the normal range in the exposed group (systolic 123.9 ± 10.9 vs. 129.5 ± 11.3 mmHg, p = 0.006; diastolic 64.6 ± 7.5 vs. 70.5 ± 9.9 mmHg, p < 0.001). The median interval between the first and third qualifying ECG was 2.5 months (IQR 1.4–5.5). ACVO occurred in 71.1% vs. 13.8% (adjusted OR 12.27, 95% CI 5.14–29.27; p < 0.001); MACE in 31.6% vs. 8.8% (adjusted OR 3.80, 95% CI 1.49–9.72; p = 0.005) of exposed vs. unexposed patients. Over a median follow-up of 3.97 years (IQR 1.59–7.93), Kaplan-Meier analysis showed lower survival in the exposed group (log-rank χ2 = 7.72; p = 0.006); however, after covariate adjustment, the mortality association was attenuated and no longer significant (adjusted HR 1.51, 95% CI 0.86–2.64; p = 0.15). Lower hemoglobin was the only independent predictor of mortality (adjusted HR 0.81 per 1 g/dL; 95% CI 0.69–0.95; p = 0.008).ConclusionRecurrent unexplained resting sinus tachycardia identifies patients with cancer at increased risk of both MACE and ACVO, although residual confounding may have inflated the observed associations. Sinus tachycardia was not independently associated with mortality after adjustment for illness burden. Recurrent sinus tachycardia may serve as an accessible marker of cardiovascular vulnerability that warrants prospective investigation in cardio-oncology.Keywords: Sinus tachycardia, Cardio-oncology, Cardiovascular outcomes, MACE, Mortality, cancer

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) and cancer represent the two leading causes of global mortality and share a complex bidirectional relationship mediated by common risk factors, systemic inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation [1], [2], [3]. Traditionally, the field of cardio-oncology has focused on identifying and mitigating the cardiotoxic effects of anticancer therapies [1], [4]. However, increasing evidence suggests that the malignant state itself can significantly influence cardiovascular physiology independent of treatment exposure [1], [3]. This evolving clinical intersection highlights the need to better understand cardiovascular signals in oncology populations, which may carry significant prognostic value (3).

Resting sinus tachycardia is one of the most frequently encountered cardiovascular findings in cancer patients (5). This represents a significant clinical dilemma, as an elevated heart rate is commonly attributed to transient or secondary causes, such as pain, anxiety, anemia, volume depletion, infection, or the immediate impact of chemotherapy and radiation (5). As a result, resting sinus tachycardia is often managed conservatively or overlooked, potentially masking its significance as a marker of underlying systemic or cardiovascular stress (5).

Emerging evidence suggests that persistent or unexplained resting sinus tachycardia may represent more than a benign physiologic reaction (5). In fact, malignancy is frequently associated with autonomic imbalance characterized by sympathetic activation, reduced vagal tone, systemic inflammation, and increased metabolic demand [5], [6]. These processes may lead to sustained elevations in heart rate even in the absence of an identifiable clinical trigger (5). In this context, persistent sinus tachycardia may reflect early cardiovascular stress, autonomic dysregulation, or evolving cardiac injury even before the development of overt structural or functional cardiac abnormalities (7).

The clinical significance of this phenomenon was recently highlighted in a comprehensive review by Fakih et al., which emphasized that resting sinus tachycardia in cancer patients remains an understudied entity (4). They proposed that sinus tachycardia may represent a clinically meaningful cardiovascular signal rather than a purely incidental symptom, potentially reflecting underlying physiologic stress or early cardiovascular dysfunction (4). This perspective supports the concept that heart rate may serve as an accessible and clinically informative marker in cardio-oncology (4).

Current evidence shows that elevated resting heart rate (RHR) has been identified as an independent predictor of adverse cardiovascular outcomes (ACVO) and mortality in cancer patients [5], [7]. In a landmark cohort study, Hemu et al. reported that cancer patients with electrocardiogram (ECG)-confirmed sinus tachycardia, defines as a heart rate ≥ 100 beats per minute (bpm) experienced a 2.8-fold higher risk of ACVO, including a 3.3-fold increased risk of new-onset heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and a 6.3-fold higher risk of hospitalization for acute heart failure (HF) exacerbation (7). Similarly, in a prospective cohort of treatment-naive cancer patients, Anker et al. demonstrated that every 5 bpm increase in RHR was associated with a 10% increase in all-cause mortality (1). Furthermore, elevated heart rate was found to correlate significantly with increased cardiovascular biomarkers, specifically high-sensitivity troponin T and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), suggesting a link between chronotropic dysregulation and subclinical myocardial injury (1). Earlier work by Anker et al. also showed that a RHR ≥75 bpm independently predicted increased mortality across several malignancies, including colorectal, pancreatic, and non-small cell lung cancer (8). Beyond mortality risk, elevated heart rate may also reflect underlying tumor biology. In a study of colorectal cancer survivors, Park et al. found that patients with an RHR ≥81 bpm had a six-fold higher risk of advanced adenoma recurrence, suggesting that persistent tachycardia may represent a hemodynamic marker of systemic stress or tumor progression (9).

Despite growing recognition of the prognostic relevance of elevated RHR in oncology populations, critical gaps in the literature remain. Prior studies have largely evaluated baseline RHR or isolated episodes of sinus tachycardia, without distinguishing between transient physiologic responses and recurrent unexplained sinus tachycardia (1). Most existing investigations have focused primarily on overall mortality, while the relationship between persistent tachycardia and specific cardiovascular complications remains poorly defined (1). Consequently, it remains unclear whether sinus tachycardia serves merely as a marker of disease severity or represents an independent contributor to cardiovascular risk (5).

The objective of this study is to evaluate whether persistent unexplained resting sinus tachycardia is associated with an all-cause mortality, increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), and ACVO in cancer patients. By examining these associations in a real-world oncology population, we aim to determine whether recurrent resting sinus tachycardia represents a clinically meaningful signal for cardiovascular risk stratification in the cardio-oncology setting.

Methodology

Study design

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the American University of Beirut Medical Center (AUBMC), a tertiary academic referral center in Lebanon, between January 2019 and January 2026. The study utilized electronic medical record (EMR) data to evaluate the association between recurrent unexplained resting sinus tachycardia and ACVO in adult cancer patients. Eligible participants included patients aged 18 years or older with an active or prior diagnosis of cancer who underwent electrocardiographic evaluation during emergency department visits, inpatient hospitalizations, or outpatient clinic encounters during the study period. A consecutive sampling strategy was implemented, enrolling all eligible adult cancer patients who presented to AUBMC between January 2019 and January 2026 and met the predefined inclusion criteria. No a priori sample size calculation was performed, as the study was designed to capture the full available population rather than test a pre-specified hypothesis, hence minimizing selection bias. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of AUBMC (IRB Protocol Number: BIO-2024-044), which waived the requirement for informed consent due to the retrospective design of this study.

Patient population

A total of 341 patients with confirmed cancer who had ≥3 available ECG studies recorded during ≥3separate hospital visits were initially screened for eligibility. Of these, 143 patients were excluded due to prior documented arrhythmias (including atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, or paced rhythm), pre-existing chronic heart failure, identifiable secondary causes of tachycardia (such as sepsis, anemia, dehydration, etc.), or incomplete clinical data. The remaining 198 patients were included in the final analysis and divided into two groups based on cardiac rhythm assessment. 38 patients with recurrent unexplained resting sinus tachycardia on ≥3 separate ECG studies obtained during three distinct hospital visits, without an identifiable secondary cause, comprised the exposed group. Whereas, 160 patients who maintained normal sinus rhythm on ≥3 available ECG studies during three unrelated hospital encounters throughout the study period served as the unexposed group. Exposure definition

The exposed group was defined as patients with confirmed cancer diagnosis and ≥ 3 ECG-confirmed resting sinus tachycardia episodes occurring during three separate clinical encounters on different dates, without an identifiable physiologic or clinical trigger. Sinus tachycardia was defined as sinus rhythm with a heart rate > 100 bpm confirmed on a standard 12‑lead ECG. For the exposed group, the index encounter corresponded to the ECG demonstrating the highest recorded heart rate among qualifying tachycardia episodes, from which clinical variables were extracted (10).

The unexposed group was defined as patients with normal sinus rhythm on the index ECG, with heart rates between 60 and 100 bpm and no documented supraventricular or ventricular arrhythmias during the encounter. The unexposed group was also required to have no atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, paced rhythm, or high-degree atrioventricular block at the index encounter. The index ECG for the unexposed group was the ECG used to classify the patient as unexposed, typically corresponding to the highest heart rate within the normal sinus range during that encounter. The unexposed group was required to have ≥3 ECG studies obtained during ≥3 separate hospital encounters, consistent with the inclusion criteria applied to the sinus tachycardia group/exposed group. The interval between the first and third qualifying tachycardia ECG was recorded to characterize the duration over which recurrence was documented.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they had documented atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, permanent pacemaker rhythm, or second- or third-degree atrioventricular block at the index encounter. Patients with pre-existing chronic HF diagnosed prior to the index encounter were also excluded. Additional exclusion criteria included documented sepsis or systemic infection at the index encounter and insufficient chart documentation to confirm ECG rhythm (<3 ECG studies available on separate hospital visits), heart rate (elevated heart rate documented on pulse oximeter only were excluded), or subsequent clinical outcomes. To avoid misclassification, patients whose cardiovascular events were documented to have occurred prior to the index ECG during the same encounter were excluded. Finally, patients with identifiable physiologic causes of sinus tachycardia at the time of ECG were excluded. Those included: pain, fever, anxiety, dehydration, anemia, infection, or tachycardia occurring during chemotherapy or radiotherapy administration. Regarding anemia specifically, patients with an acute hemoglobin drop exceeding 2 g/dL or severe anemia (hemoglobin <7 g/dL) at the index encounter were excluded, as these may provoke compensatory tachycardia. In contrast, patients with chronic, stable anemia of malignancy were retained, as this reflects baseline disease burden rather than an acute reversible trigger.

Data collection

Clinical data were extracted from the EMR and included demographic characteristics, cardiovascular comorbidities, laboratory parameters, medication use, cancer characteristics, and cardiac diagnostic findings. Baseline demographic variables included age, sex, and body mass index (BMI). Relevant cardiovascular comorbidities included diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and chronic kidney disease (CKD). Laboratory values obtained at the time of the index encounter were recorded and included hemoglobin, lipid profile parameters (total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and triglycerides), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), serum creatinine, serum sodium, N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), and C-reactive protein (CRP). Systolic and diastolic blood pressure recorded at the time of the index ECG were also collected for both groups.

Medication exposure at or preceding the index encounter was documented, including the use of antiarrhythmic agents (class I-III), beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, diuretics, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists.

Cancer-related variables included cancer type, categorized as solid or hematologic malignancy, and primary tumor site when available. Additional oncologic variables included cancer stage, tumor grade, the presence of metastatic disease, exposure to systemic chemotherapy, the number of chemotherapy cycles completed, and exposure to radiotherapy.

Cardiac diagnostic data included ECG parameters such as heart rate, rhythm, PR interval, QRS duration, QT interval, and electrical axis. Available echocardiographic data were also collected, including left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular dimensions, indices of diastolic function, and significant valvular abnormalities. A history of prior cardiovascular procedures, including coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), valve replacement, or cardioversion, was also recorded.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was ACVO defined as a composite MACE and HF. HF events included new-onset HFrEF (EF ≤40%), new-onset heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF; EF ≥50%), and admissions for acute HF exacerbation in patients with either HFrEF or HFpEF. MACE was defined as a composite of myocardial infarction, acute coronary syndrome (ACS), coronary revascularization (PCI or CABG), stroke, transient ischemic attack, and cardiovascular mortality during follow-up. Secondary outcomes included MACE and all-cause mortality during follow-up. Outcomes were identified through systematic review of hospital records, diagnostic codes, imaging reports, imaging studies, and physician documentation within the electronic medical record.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or median [interquartile range (IQR)] for skewed distributions, as assessed by visual inspection and the Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the Pearson chi-square test (or Fisher's exact test when expected cell counts were < 5) for categorical variables.

Time-to-event analyses for all-cause mortality was performed using the Kaplan-Meier method, with differences between groups evaluated using the log-rank test. Logistic regression was used to examine the association between recurrent unexplained resting sinus tachycardia (≥3 episodes) and binary outcomes, including MACE and ACVO. Univariable logistic regression was first performed for all candidate variables, and variables with p < 0.05 were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model. Final model selection was guided by clinical relevance and the Akaike information criterion (AIC) to minimize overfitting.

Cox proportional hazards regression was used to evaluate predictors of all-cause mortality. Covariates for the multivariable model were selected a priori based on clinical relevance or identified through univariable screening (p < 0.05). Associations are reported as odds ratios (ORs) or hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical significance was defined as a p-value <0.05. All analyses were performed using Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp).

To address the potential risk of immortal-time bias, secondary to the preset requirement that exposed patients must have three qualifying ECGs prior to classification, a landmark sensitivity analysis was performed. A landmark timepoint was set at the median interval from the first to the third qualifying ECG (2.5 months); patients surviving to the landmark were classified by exposure status as of that point and followed thereafter, with survival compared using Kaplan-Meier estimation and the log-rank test. This analysis was pre-specified as a sensitivity approach intended to mitigate immortal-time bias and is not intended to provide adjusted effect estimates.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 198 patients were included in the final analysis: 160 in the unexposed group (NSR) and 38 in the exposed group (≥3 episodes of sinus tachycardia). The majority of malignancies were solid tumors (71.2%), with breast and lung cancers being the most common, while hematologic malignancies accounted for 28.8% of the cohort and included leukemias, lymphomas, and multiple myeloma. Among exposed patients, the median interval between the first and third qualifying tachycardia ECG was 2.5 months (interquartile range 1.4–5.5 months; range 0.5–16.2 months), confirming that recurrence was documented over a sustained period rather than within a single acute episode. The mean age was 62.8 ± 16.7 years in the unexposed group and 65.4 ± 18.4 years in the exposed group (p = 0.39). There were no significant differences in age, sex, BMI, or most comorbidities between groups. Hemoglobin was significantly lower in the exposed group (9.8 ± 1.4 g/dL vs. 11.1 ± 2.3 g/dL; p < 0.001), and ECG heart rate was expectedly higher (116.8 ± 14.2 bpm vs. 81.2 ± 10.7 bpm; p < 0.001). Chemotherapy exposure was less common among patients in the exposed group (71.1% vs. 87.4%; p = 0.013), with a lower mean number of chemotherapy cycles (6.2 ± 12.9 vs. 8.9 ± 11.0; p = 0.001). Blood pressure at the index ECG was modestly lower in the exposed group but remained within the normal range (systolic 123.9 ± 10.9 vs. 129.5 ± 11.3 mmHg, p = 0.006; diastolic 64.6 ± 7.5 vs. 70.5 ± 9.9 mmHg, p < 0.001), arguing against hypotension-driven compensatory tachycardia as an explanation for the observed findings. All other demographic, laboratory, and echocardiographic parameters were comparable between groups (Table 1; more extensively in Supplemental Table 1; cancer type distribution among the study sample is detailed in Supplemental Table 2).

ACVO

A total of 49 patients (24.7%) experienced ACVO during follow-up. The incidence of ACVO was markedly higher in the sinus tachycardia group compared to the unexposed group (71.1% vs. 13.8%). In univariable analysis, recurrent resting sinus tachycardia was strongly associated with ACVO (OR 15.40, 95% CI 6.69–35.42; p < 0.001). Following multivariable adjustment for hemoglobin, chemotherapy exposure, and CKD, sinus tachycardia remained strongly and independently associated with ACVO (adjusted OR 12.27, 95% CI 5.14–29.27; p < 0.001). However, given the wide confidence interval, the composite nature of the endpoint, and the modest event count, this estimate should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and may overstate the true association. CKD showed a trend toward increased risk of ACVO but did not reach statistical significance (adjusted OR 2.2495% CI 0.89–5.62; p = 0.085). Hemoglobin and chemotherapy exposure were not independently associated with ACVO in the adjusted model. Table 2 shows the logistic regression analysis for ACVO. MACE

Overall, 26 patients (13.1%) experienced MACE during the study period. The incidence of MACE was substantially higher among patients with recurrent resting sinus tachycardia compared to the unexposed group (31.6% vs. 8.8%). In univariable logistic regression, recurrent resting sinus tachycardia was strongly associated with increased odds of MACE (OR 4.81, 95% CI 2.00–11.57; p < 0.001). After multivariable adjustment for hemoglobin and chemotherapy exposure, sinus tachycardia remained independently associated with MACE (adjusted OR 3.80, 95% CI 1.49–9.72; p = 0.005). Chemotherapy exposure was independently associated with lower odds of MACE (adjusted OR 0.36, 95% CI 0.14–0.96; p = 0.041), whereas hemoglobin was not significantly associated with MACE in the adjusted model. Mortality

During a median follow up of 3.97 years (IQR [1.59–7.93]), 61 deaths occurred. Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated significantly reduced survival among patients with recurrent resting sinus tachycardia compared with the unexposed groups (χ2 = 7.72; p = 0.006), reported in Fig. 2. In univariable Cox regression, recurrent sinus tachycardia was associated with an approximately two-fold increased risk of death (HR 2.04, 95% CI 1.22–3.40; p = 0.006). However, after multivariable adjustment for age, hemoglobin, CRP, and metastatic disease, the association between sinus tachycardia and mortality was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (adjusted HR 1.51, 95% CI 0.86–2.64; p = 0.15). Lower hemoglobin levels remained independently associated with increased mortality risk (adjusted HR 0.81 per 1 g/dL increase, 95% CI 0.69–0.95; p = 0.008), suggesting that anemia may partly mediate the observed association between sinus tachycardia and mortality. CRP demonstrated a borderline independent association with mortality (adjusted HR 1.003, 95% CI 1.001–1.005; p = 0.055), while metastatic disease was not independently associated with mortality in the adjusted model. Discussion

Highlights

In this retrospective cohort study of cancer patients at a tertiary academic center, we investigated the prognostic implications of recurring unexplained resting sinus tachycardia and its association with cardiovascular events and mortality.

Patients with recurrent resting sinus tachycardia experienced significantly higher rates of both MACE and ACVO compared with patients with NSR.

Importantly, these associations remained significant even after adjustment for clinically relevant and statistically significant covariates such as hemoglobin level, chemotherapy exposure, and baseline comorbidities.

However, residual confounding and the small sample size may have inflated the observed associations.

Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

In contrast, while recurrent resting tachycardia was associated with higher mortality in unadjusted analyses, the relationship was attenuated after adjusting for markers of systemic illness, including anemia, inflammation, and metastatic disease.

Taken together, these findings suggest that persistent sinus tachycardia may serve less as an independent driver of mortality and more as a clinical marker of physiologic stress and cardiovascular vulnerability in patients with malignancy.

An unexpected finding was that chemotherapy exposure was less common in the exposed group, which also received fewer chemotherapy cycles on average.

This argues against the possibility that the observed association between recurrent unexplained resting sinus tachycardia and ACVO was driven solely by greater treatment-related cardiotoxic exposure.

Instead, recurrent resting sinus tachycardia may reflect a broader physiologic vulnerability that is not fully captured by chemotherapy burden alone.

Because cycle count incompletely captures cardiotoxic risk and may also reflect treatment interruption in vulnerable patients, recurrent sinus tachycardia may represent a broader marker of cardiovascular susceptibility in cancer patients.

These findings contribute to a growing body of literature highlighting the prognostic importance of heart rate in oncology populations [11], [12], [13].

This study builds on prior literature with a primary focus on recurrent unexplained resting sinus tachycardia, rather than single measurements of RHR, thereby providing additional insight into the clinical significance of persistent chronotropic dysregulation in cancer patients.

ACVO

A key finding of this study was the strong association between recurrent resting sinus tachycardia and ACVO. After adjustment for relevant covariates, patients with recurrent tachycardia had more than twelve-fold higher risk of developing ACVO compared with patients with NSR. These outcomes included myocardial infarction, stroke, and HF, all of which represent clinically significant complications in cancer patients.

This observation is consistent with prior studies suggesting that sinus tachycardia may precede cardiovascular decompensation in oncology populations. Hemu et al. previously reported that cancer patients with ECG-documented sinus tachycardia had a significantly higher risk of ACVO, including new-onset HF and HF hospitalization (7). Moreover, elevated heart rate has been associated with increased circulating levels of cardiac biomarkers such as NT-proBNP and high-sensitivity troponin, suggesting a link between chronotropic dysregulation and subclinical myocardial injury (1).

The mechanisms underlying this association are likely multifactorial. Persistent sympathetic activation increases myocardial oxygen consumption and may exacerbate myocardial ischemia in susceptible individuals. Sustained tachycardia may also impair ventricular filling and promote diastolic dysfunction, particularly in patients with limited cardiovascular reserve (12). In the context of malignancy, these effects may be further amplified by systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and the hypercoagulable state frequently observed in cancer patients [2], [3], [14].

Beyond serving as a marker of systemic stress, tachycardia may also contribute directly to ACVO. Chronic elevations in heart rate reduce diastolic filling time and coronary perfusion while increasing myocardial oxygen demand. Over time, these hemodynamic effects can promote myocardial ischemia, ventricular remodeling, and progression of heart failure (12). In oncology populations, these mechanisms may interact with treatment-related cardiotoxicity and metabolic stress, further increasing the risk of cardiovascular events [13], [15].

MACE

Recurrent unexplained resting sinus tachycardia was strongly associated with the occurrence of MACE. After adjustment for hemoglobin levels and chemotherapy exposure, patients with recurrent sinus tachycardia had nearly four-fold increase in the risk of MACE.

In the multivariable analysis for MACE, hemoglobin was included as a covariate based on its clinical relevance and biologic plausibility as a potential confounder. Anemia is common in oncology populations and may contribute to compensatory sinus tachycardia as well as increased cardiovascular stress, potentially influencing both the exposure of interest and cardiovascular outcomes (16). Beta-blocker use was initially evaluated in our multivariable models; however, it was associated with an increased odds of MACE, a finding that is not biologically plausible and likely reflects confounding by indication, as beta-blockers are more frequently prescribed to patients with higher baseline cardiovascular risk, including those with coronary disease, arrhythmias, or hypertension [17], [18]. To minimize this bias and maintain a parsimonious model focused on baseline physiologic and disease-related factors rather than treatment variables, hemoglobin was retained while beta-blocker therapy was excluded from the final multivariable analysis (18). Multivariate analysis including beta blocker usage can be seen in Supplementary Table 6.

Elevated RHR has long been recognized as a marker of cardiovascular risk in the general population. Increased heart rate reflects heightened sympathetic activity and has been associated with endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and accelerated atherosclerosis [11], [12]. These pathophysiologic processes may be particularly pronounced in patients with cancer, where malignancy itself promotes a proinflammatory and prothrombotic environment [2], [3].

Mortality

Recurrent unexplained resting sinus tachycardia was associated with an approximately two-fold increase in all-cause mortality in unadjusted analyses. However, this association was no longer statistically significant after adjustment for hemoglobin levels, inflammatory markers (CRP), age, and metastatic disease. This finding suggests that tachycardia may reflect the overall severity of illness rather than independently contributing to mortality risk.

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated a relationship between elevated resting heart rate and mortality among patients with cancer. In a prospective cohort study of treatment-naïve oncology patients, Anker et al. found that each 5 bpm increase in resting heart rate was associated with a 10% increase in all-cause mortality (1). Similarly, earlier work from the same group showed that a resting heart rate ≥ 75 bpm independently predicted mortality across several malignancies, including colorectal, pancreatic, and non-small cell lung cancers (8). In addition, population-based analyses have shown that cardiovascular disease represents a substantial contributor to mortality among cancer patients, underscoring the complex interaction between cancer and cardiovascular risk (19).

Several mechanisms may explain the observed relationship between tachycardia and mortality in oncology populations. Cancer is frequently accompanied by systemic inflammation, metabolic alterations, and autonomic imbalance characterized by increased sympathetic activation and reduced parasympathetic tone [3], [5], [11]. These changes can lead to persistent elevations in RHR even in the absence of overt CVD. From a physiologic standpoint, sustained sinus tachycardia increases myocardial oxygen demand while simultaneously shortening diastolic perfusion time, thereby placing additional stress on the myocardium (12). Over time, these hemodynamic effects may contribute to myocardial injury, ventricular remodeling, and eventual cardiovascular decompensation (12).

The attenuation of mortality risk after multivariable adjustment likely reflects the close relationship between tachycardia and markers of systemic disease burden, such as anemia and inflammation. Nonetheless, the consistent signal observed in unadjusted analyses suggests that recurrent resting sinus tachycardia may still provide useful prognostic information, particularly as a readily measurable indicator of physiologic stress in patients with cancer.

Clinical implications

Sinus tachycardia is frequently encountered in patients with cancer and is often attributed to reversible physiologic factors such as anemia, pain, infection, or treatment effects (4). As a result, it may be overlooked or dismissed as a benign finding in routine clinical practice.

This study suggests that recurrent unexplained resting sinus tachycardia may instead represent an important clinical signal indicating increased cardiovascular vulnerability. In patients with cancer, repeated episodes of sinus tachycardia should therefore prompt careful clinical evaluation for potential underlying cardiovascular dysfunction, systemic inflammation, or early cardiotoxicity.

Because heart rate is easily measured and widely available in clinical settings, it may represent a candidate signal for identifying patients who could benefit from closer cardiovascular monitoring. Current cardio-oncology guidelines emphasize the importance of early detection and proactive management of cardiovascular complications in cancer patients [13], [15]. Within this framework, recognition of recurrent resting sinus tachycardia as a potential risk marker may facilitate earlier cardiology referral and more targeted surveillance strategies.

Future research should explore whether therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating heart rate, such as beta-blockade or other autonomic interventions, may have a role in selected oncology populations.

Strengths and limitations

Several limitations decrease the impact of our findings. First, the requirement that exposed patients accrue three qualifying tachycardia ECGs before classification introduces the potential for immortal-time bias, since these patients had to survive and remain event-free long enough to meet the exposure definition. This likely contributes to the large magnitude of the observed associations, and the effect estimates should therefore not be interpreted as causal or directly predictive. To partially address this, we performed a landmark sensitivity analysis, which continued to demonstrate a significant survival difference (Fig. 2), suggesting that while the absolute magnitude is likely overstated, the underlying association is unlikely to be entirely due to chance. Nonetheless, residual immortal-time and selection effects cannot be excluded, and the odds ratios reported here are best understood as hypothesis-generating rather than precise measures of risk.

Despite these limitations, this study addresses a genuinely understudied question. To our knowledge, it is among the first to examine recurrent, rather than single-timepoint, unexplained resting sinus tachycardia in a real-world oncology population, and to characterize its relationship with blood pressure, anemia, and a spectrum of cardiovascular outcomes. Even interpreted conservatively, the consistent directionality across ACVO, MACE, and unadjusted survival, together with the persistence of a survival difference in a bias-aware landmark analysis, supports recurrent sinus tachycardia as a clinically accessible signal that merits dedicated prospective study.

Additionally, the availability of detailed clinical data allowed adjustment for several important confounders, including anemia, chemotherapy exposure, inflammatory markers, and cardiovascular comorbidities. Moreover, the use of ECG-confirmed sinus tachycardia episodes across separate clinical encounters strengthens the validity of exposure classification and minimizes the risk of misclassification. However, this stringent inclusion criterion also reduced the overall sample size, as many patients did not have sufficient ECG documentation across multiple encounters within the study period.

The retrospective design introduces the possibility of residual confounding and selection bias. Although identifiable secondary causes of sinus tachycardia were excluded, unrecognized physiologic stressors such as pain or subclinical infection may still have contributed to elevated heart rates. In addition, patients receiving beta-blockers were retained in the cohort, even though beta-blocker use did not differ significantly between groups, residual pharmacologic effects on heart rate cannot be fully excluded. The inclusion of both actively treated and previously diagnosed cancer patients may also have introduced heterogeneity in cardiovascular risk that could not be fully accounted for. Furthermore, the study was conducted at a single tertiary center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Moreover, the relatively small number of patients with recurrent sinus tachycardia also limits statistical power, particularly for mortality analyses. In addition to that, the heterogeneity of cancer types, disease stages, and treatment regimens may influence cardiovascular risk and could not be fully accounted for in the present study. Finally, the large magnitude of the association between recurrent resting sinus tachycardia and ACVO should be interpreted with caution. The composite nature of the endpoint, combining MACE with HF and HF hospitalization, as well as a modest sample size and the risk of immortal-time bias may have inflated the observed association.

Prospective studies with larger cohorts and longitudinal heart rate monitoring will be necessary to better clarify the mechanistic relationship between tachycardia and cardiovascular risk in oncology populations.

Conclusion

Recurrent unexplained resting sinus tachycardia appears to be a clinically meaningful finding among patients with cancer. In our cohort, recurrent resting sinus tachycardia was strongly associated with both ACVO and MACE. Although its relationship with mortality was attenuated after adjustment for markers of systemic illness, recurrent sinus tachycardia was consistently associated with cardiovascular vulnerability. Although residual confounding and the small sample size may have inflated the observed associations, the consistency of the signal supports further investigation of recurrent sinus tachycardia as an accessible marker in cardio-oncology.

These findings suggest that resting sinus tachycardia should not be readily dismissed as a benign or incidental observation in oncology patients. Instead, repeated episodes of unexplained resting sinus tachycardia identify patients at increased risk of cardiovascular complications. Incorporating heart rate assessment into routine oncologic care may therefore represent a simple yet valuable strategy for improving cardiovascular risk stratification in the growing population of patients with cancer.
