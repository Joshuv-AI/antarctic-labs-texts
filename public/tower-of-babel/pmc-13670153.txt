Innovative Approach Reveals How Receptors Control G Protein State Equilibria
Authors: Alexandre Bouyssou, Mélanie Da Silva, Julien Soulé, Ludovic Fabre, Laurent Lamarque, Rémy Sounier, Thierry Durroux, Sébastien Granier, Thomas Roux, Elodie Dupuis, Eric Trinquet, Philippe Rondard, Jean‐Philippe Pin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTG protein‐coupled receptors (GPCRs) constitute the largest family of drug targets, acting through heterotrimeric G proteins. Agonist bound GPCRs promote GDP release from G proteins allowing their activation upon GTP binding. Here, we analyze this process with innovative tools that detect the main states of native Gi proteins: the GDP‐bound, the open nucleotide‐free and the active GTP‐bound states. We show that receptor activation increases the amount of open‐state Gi proteins, consistent with GDP release from inactive Gi proteins. We also observe that the active receptor can release GTP from the active Gi protein, to return to an inactive open state. Consistently, receptor activation also promotes the formation of the Gαβγ complex from the GTP‐bound state in cells, in agreement with the formation of the receptor‐G protein ternary complex. These data suggest that the amount of active GTP‐bound form produced upon GPCR activation can be controlled by the receptor‐induced GTP release. This control of G protein state equilibrium that depends on the receptor subtype may affect coupling efficacy. These data reveal receptor‐dependent G‐protein state equilibria and identify GTP release as an additional, context‐dependent control point that can shape apparent coupling efficacy.

Introduction

G protein‐coupled receptors (GPCRs) are the largest family of cell surface receptors and are involved in many important physiological processes. For these reasons, GPCRs are the most important drug targets with approximately one‐third of all approved drugs acting on such receptors [1]. GPCRs activate several signaling cascades through various effectors including arrestins and heterotrimeric G proteins [2]. The molecular basis of G protein activation by GPCRs has been studied for many years [3, 4]. G proteins adopt three main conformational states, the inactive GDP‐bound, a nucleotide‐free open state [3, 4, 5, 6] and the active GTP‐bound conformations (Figure 1A). In the active state, GTP is rapidly hydrolyzed into GDP by the GTPase activity of the Gα subunit, a reaction catalyzed by RGS proteins [7]. The open, nucleotide‐free state of the G protein results from the formation of the high affinity ternary complex between the agonist, the receptor (R) and the Gαβγ complex after GDP release [8, 9]. While this R‐Gαβγ open complex can be stable in vitro [10, 11], it is transient in cells because of the intracellular concentration of GTP [12]. Structures of such ternary complexes have been solved for many receptors and G protein subtypes [8, 10], and revealed the open conformation of the Gα subunit. The empty open Gα subunit (GαOPEN) has a high affinity for GTP, such that the complex R GαOPENβγ is destabilized by GTP binding, resulting in a large reorganization between Gα‐GTP and Gβγ.

G protein activation cycle and FRET sensors developed. In the GPCR resting state, G protein is in GDP‐bound conformation composed by three subunits, Gα bound to GDP, Gβ and Gγ. Upon stimulation by an extracellular agonist, the receptor adopts a conformational change that allows G protein binding and catalyzes GDP release from Gα. Then the G protein is found in an empty conformation that leads to a high‐affinity complex between the activated receptor and the G protein. This high‐affinity complex is destabilized by GTP binding to Gα, that allows the Gα‐GTP and Gβγ subunits to interact with downstream effector proteins. Signaling from G protein is terminated on hydrolysis of GTP to GDP by Gα that is favored by the regulator of G protein signaling (RGS), then leading to a reassociation of the G protein heterotrimer. (B) G protein conformations detected by the FRET sensors developed in this study. Scheme adapted from Rasmussen et al., 2011 [8].

Importantly, while GPCRs are classically described as guanine nucleotide exchange factors that promote GDP release and subsequent GTP binding, early biochemical studies in rhodopsin/transducin, adrenergic and muscarinic systems indicated that receptor‐catalyzed nucleotide exchange can be reversible and that activated receptors can promote release of bound non‐hydrolysable GTP analogues [13, 14, 15, 16]. Subsequently this behavior was demonstrated in native cardiac and leukocyte membranes [17, 18]. This phenomenon, sometimes referred to as ‘reverse mode’ coupling, has recently regained attention through radioligand‐based pulse–chase studies at opioid receptors, which reported agonist‐dependent GTPγS release and proposed the existence of ligand‐selective receptor active states favoring GTP release vs. binding [19, 20].

GPCRs can activate multiple G protein subtypes (Gs, Gi/o, Gq and G12/13) with most receptors able to couple to several of these [21, 22, 23, 24]. The GPCR ability to activate multiple G protein subtypes and the possibility of developing drugs that favor the activation of only some of them, in a concept named biased signaling [25], underline the necessity of better understanding the way each G protein subtype is activated. Multiple assays measuring the activation of endogenous G proteins [10, 26, 27] that avoid the influence of overexpression of G protein sensors [28, 29, 30, 31, 32] are being used. They are based on the quantification of the active GTP‐bound G proteins through the measurement of GTPγS or fluorescent GTP binding [26, 27, 33, 34, 35]. However, these assays are not specific for one G protein subtype, unless an immunoprecipitation step is added [36]. Recently, the detection of the GTP‐bound form of Gα subunits through their interaction using specific sensors has been reported [30]. The sensor was modified to recognize and be specific to different G protein families. While these assays successfully track the final step in the activation process, they do not enable the analysis of the equilibrium between the three main conformations of the G protein upon receptor activation, an essential aspect to understand the effect of various ligands and receptors.

Here, we aimed at examining the equilibrium between the G protein states upon receptor activation. For this, we developed and characterized pairs of binders that selectively recognize either the open form, or the GTP bound form of native Gαi (Figure 1B). After labeling with fluorophores compatible with time‐resolved Förster resonance energy transfer (trFRET) measurements, we could measure the appearance or disappearance of these different conformations of the endogenous Gαi proteins upon receptor activation. Using these tools, we reveal that activated GPCRs can not only release GDP from the inactive state of Gαi but also limit the amount of GTP‐bound form, by stabilizing the open state of the G protein. These findings reveal that the amount of active GTP‐bound form of the G protein can be regulated by the activated receptors, in both positive and negative ways. Such data suggest a more complex mode of G protein regulation by GPCRs and highlight the importance of considering the equilibrium between the different G protein states to understand the effects of various ligands and receptors on G protein signaling.

Results

Innovative Tools to Detect Gαi Conformational States

Using the purified Gαi bound to GDP (Gαi1‐GDP) and the heterotrimer Gαi1‐GDP β1γ2, we have developed and characterized three kinds of binders: (1) a peptide that recognizes selectively Gi proteins in their active state, based on previously reported peptides [35]; (2) a commercial anti‐Gαi monoclonal antibody; (3) finally, home‐made monoclonal antibodies developed by mice immunization with the recombinant monomeric Gαi protein.

We have generated a 19‐residue peptide biotinylated at the N‐terminal and coupled to streptavidin labeled with the fluorophore acceptor XL665 compatible with trFRET measurement. This peptide, called KB1753, was reported to bind specifically the GTPγS bound conformation of the Gαi protein family [37, 38]. To improve its apparent affinity, we have prepared a biotinylated version that can be detected with a multivalent and fluorescent streptavidin. This peptide, called KB1753DS, and referred as peptide‐XL665, binds selectively to the active conformation of Gαi1 proteins stabilized by GTPγS, but not to its GDP and empty conformations (Figure 2A). The trFRET signal measured, in a homogenous configuration (HTRF), between increased amounts of the XL665‐labelled peptide in presence of a constant concentration of the commercial monoclonal antibody Ab13 labelled with the fluorescent donor Lumi4‐Tb. Of note, Ab13 has a similar affinity for the different conformations of Gαi1, GDP, GTPγS and empty state, as measured by the FRET signal between d2‐labelled Ab13 and the anti‐Twin‐Strep‐tag (TST) antibody labeled with Lumi4‐Tb that recognizes a TST‐tag introduced at the N‐terminal part of the Gα subunit (Figure 2B).

We have also developed additional monoclonal antibodies, by immunizing mice with purified recombinant Gαi1 loaded with either GDP or GTPγS, because the nucleotide‐free or open state of Gα (GαOPEN) was proposed to be thermally unstable [37]. Among them, Ab26 is selective of both the GDP and GαOPEN conformations, and it did not bind to the GTP state (Figure 2C). The higher maximum FRET signal for GαOPEN than Gαi1‐GDP was most probably due to a larger distance between the two antibodies rather than from a difference of affinity. Indeed, Ab26 has a similar Kd value for GαOPEN and Gαi1‐GDP (Table S1). In contrast, Ab39 has no preference for the different Gαi protein conformations (Figure 2D), similarly to the Ab13 described above. Of note, the peptide has a nanomolar affinity for Gαi1‐GTPγS while these monoclonal antibodies have a sub‐nanomolar affinity for the different conformations of Gαi1 (Table S1).

FRET Sensors to Detect Conformational States of Endogenous Gi Proteins

We combined these binders in order to develop trFRET conformational sensors to detect the endogenous Gαi1‐GTPγS and GαOPEN states of the Gαi proteins. The pair peptide‐XL665 + Ab13 was specific of the GTPγS active, for both the purified Gαi1 and the heterotrimer Gαi1. β1γ2 (Figure 2E). It suggested that GTPγS was able to dissociate the Gβγ subunits from Gα thus removing its steric hindrance for the binding of the peptide known to bind to the Switch 2 region of Gα [37]. Most importantly, the peptide‐XL665 and Ab13 allowed the detection of endogenous Gi proteins in cell membrane preparations with high sensitivity (Figure 2E).

Detecting the GαOPEN could be a highly efficient approach to reveal the activation of G protein by the receptor [39]. Interestingly, the pair Ab26 + Ab39 recognized most preferentially the Gαi‐OPEN states both for the purified Gαi and Gαiβγ and the endogenous Gi proteins in membranes (Figure 2F). Of note, the low level of FRET signal obtained with the membranes might be due to a lower amount of Gi proteins in the assays compared to the recombinant Gi proteins, or alternatively to steric hindrance limiting the binding of at least one antibody, coming from the receptor or other components of the membranes. Nevertheless, the lower endogenous level of Gi proteins did not prevent the specific detection of GαOPEN (Figure 2F, inset), further indicating the high sensitivity of this FRET sensor. Finally, the detection of Gαi‐OPEN by this pair of antibodies was further confirmed by treatment with apyrase, an enzyme that hydrolyzes GDP into GMP which has a low affinity for the G protein [8, 9, 40]. Apyrase strongly increased the FRET signal of the pair Ab26 + Ab39 for both the recombinant Gαi and endogenous G proteins loaded with GDP, to a level similar to that obtained in the absence of added nucleotide (Extended Data Figure 1). In addition, a similar FRET signal for the monomeric recombinant Gαi in the absence or presence of apyrase indicated that this protein purified in absence of nucleotide was most probably in the nucleotide free open state.

Specific Gαi Subunits are Detected by the Biosensors

We have determined which G protein subtypes are detected by our conformational sensors. To do so, we have developed a cellular assay where the binding of the antibodies was measured for each transfected Gα subunit tagged with both a TST and a Flag epitope at their N‐terminus (Extended Data Figure 2A). After HEK293 cells permeabilization, trFRET signal was measured between the anti‐TST antibody labeled with Lumi4‐Tb, and the antibodies Ab13, Ab26 or Ab39 labeled with d2 for each transfected Gα subunit (Extended Data Figure 2B–D). As a control, we verified that all the Gα subunits were expressed at similar level, except Gα12 expressed at a lower level (Extended Data Figure 2E,F), by measuring the FRET signal between the anti‐TST‐Lumi4‐Tb and anti‐Flag‐d2 antibodies. A similar trFRET‐based method for the relative quantification of proteins, including G proteins and GPCRs, in biological samples has been reported [41, 42].

Interestingly, Ab13 is highly selective for Gαi1 and Gαi3 and Ab39 of Gαi1 in these cellular assays (Extended Data Figure 2B,C). Ab26 recognized preferentially both Gαi1 and Gαi3 subunits, but a low FRET signal was also observed with other Gi/o subunits such Gαi2, Gαo and Gαz (Extended Data Figure 2D), suggesting it binds these subunits with a lower affinity than Gαi1 and Gαi3. Of note, the binding of Ab13 and Ab39 was not sensitive to the conformation of the Gα subunits, since the addition of saturating concentrations of GDP or GTPγS did not modify the FRET signal (Extended Data Figure 2B,C). In contrast, Ab26 better recognized the “no nucleotide added” and GDP‐bound states than the GTPγS bound state (Extended Data Figure 2D), as shown above with the purified G protein (Figure 2C). Finally, the peptide KB7153DS was previously reported to be specific of the GTPγS active state of Gαi1, Gαi2 and Gαi3, but it did not recognize the Gαo subunit [43].

Altogether, we conclude that the FRET sensor made of Ab13 + peptide can detect specifically the GTP bound state of Gαi1 and Gαi3 in membranes (Figure 2E). The pair Ab26 + Ab39 is specific of the endogenous Gαi1, indicating this latter is well expressed in the HEK293 cell membranes used (Figure 2F). Of note, the stronger FRET signal of Ab13 + peptide‐XL665 compared to Ab26 + Ab39 in membranes (Figure 2E,F), might be due to the presence of Gαi3 subunits in the membrane fractions [43, 44]. But alternatively, this better detection of the endogenous Gαi1 by the pair Ab13 + peptide might be due to a lower steric hindrance of the tools with the membranes compared to the pair of antibodies. Although we cannot exclude a higher efficacy of FRET for the pair Ab13 + peptide when bound to the Gα protein.

Sensors Detect the Relative Reorientation of the α‐Helical and Ras‐Domains

In order to better understand the molecular basis of these sensors, we have performed an epitope mapping of the three antibodies based on their high selectivity for some Gi proteins. First, overlapping epitopes between Ab13 and Ab39 have been proposed based on competition assays (Extended Data Figure 3). Briefly, the FRET binding saturation experiments of the antibodies Ab13, Ab26 and Ab39 have been performed in presence of one of the unlabeled G protein binders (Ab13, Ab26, Ab39 or the peptide‐XL665). A strong competition was measured between Ab13 and Ab39, and not between the other binders, and it was consistent with a direct competition.

We then used bioinformatics analysis using the Gαi structure and Gα protein sequence alignment to build six different Gαi1 mutants made of single or multiple changes (Extended Data Figure 4). Among them, one mutant (V109A, G112C and T120P) in the α‐helical domain abolished Ab39 binding and decreased the affinity 20‐fold of Ab13, while Ab26 binds with a similar affinity to both the mutant and wild‐type Gαi1 subunit. It is in agreement with Ab39 and Ab13 being competitive for the binding to Gαi1 by sharing overlapping epitopes, and also to their absence of sensitivity to the nucleotide bound. Regarding Ab26, one mutation (A59N) located in the linker connecting the GTPase domain and the α‐helical domain, impairs its affinity by a factor of 20. It is consistent with Ab26 being sensitive to the nucleotide bound, with a higher affinity for the GDP and empty Gαi1. Altogether, our results enable to propose a rough model for the epitope of the three antibodies. Ab13 and Ab39 would bind to α‐helical domain then compatible with the antibodies being far apart from the membrane, whereas Ab26 would bind between the two structural domains of Gαi1. These results of epitope mapping indicate that our two FRET sensors detect the relative reorientation between the α‐helical and ras‐domains that is expected to occur during the nucleotide exchange, and consistent with the appearance of a nucleotide‐free open conformation [4].

Monitoring Agonist‐Induced Gi Open State

We used these two FRET sensors to measure the activation of endogenous Gi proteins by a GPCR in membrane preparations. The human δ opioid receptor (DOP) known to couple to Gi/o proteins was used as a model. We first performed G protein activation with the sensor Ab13 + peptide at high concentration of nucleotides (10 µM GDP in the presence of 100 nM GTPγS) similar to the conditions used for GTPγ35S binding (Figure 3A). Surprisingly, the agonist SNC162 induced a strong decrease of FRET signal, while the antagonist naltrindole, in presence of EC80 of agonist, had the opposite effect (Figure 3B). Although SNC162 and naltrindole produced the expected agonist and antagonist profiles in both formats, their relative apparent potency values slightly differ between the GTPγ35S and the FRET assays, reflecting assay‐specific operational readouts rather than changes in intrinsic ligand affinity. The GTPγ35S assay measures bulk nucleotide incorporation under a high GDP:GTPγ35S ratio, which favors GDP‐loaded heterotrimers and may limit the contribution of receptor‐associated nucleotide‐free intermediates. In contrast, the GDP‐free FRET assay selectively monitors loss of Gαi‐GTPγS state and may allow a greater contribution from pre‐associated or nucleotide‐free receptor‐G protein complexes, thereby favoring agonist occupancy and reducing the apparent potency of antagonist displacement. Differences in assay sensitivity, incubation conditions and state kinetics, together with possible effects of the Ab13 + peptide pair on GTPγS association or dissociation, may also contribute to the observed shifts. Since Ab13 + peptide is specific to the Gαi‐GTPγS conformation, it suggests the agonist induced the accumulation of a Gαi state different from the GTPγS conformation, most probably the GαOPEN conformation. Altogether, these data suggested that the agonist stabilized the nucleotide free state of the G protein, while the antagonist has an opposite effect by limiting the appearance of this open state (Figure 3B).

To further demonstrate this mechanism, we used the sensor Ab26 + Ab39 that detects specifically Gαi‐OPEN. Interestingly, a strong increase of FRET signal was measured in the presence of agonist and GDP but without GTPγS (Figure 4A). A similar effect of the agonist was obtained under conditions where GTPγS is present but not GDP (Figure 4B). It revealed that upon agonist stimulation, the receptor is able to stabilize the open state of the G protein, whether it was bound to GDP or GTPγS. As expected, the process was reversible since the antagonist, tested at EC80 agonist, reverses the high FRET signal (Figure 4A,B). It showed then that stabilizing the resting state of the receptor limits the accumulation of the G protein open state, in agreement with the ternary complex model where the agonist stabilized the receptor coupled to the nucleotide free G protein [9]. In the GDP condition, antagonist treatment did not fully return the trFRET signal to the baseline. This likely reflects both the narrower dynamic range of the Ab26 and Ab39 sensor with GDP because GDP‐bound Gαi retains measurable signal, whereas GTPγS‐bound Gαi is nearly undetected and the distinct equilibria imposed by the two nucleotides. GDP supports continued cycling between GDP‐bound and nucleotide‐free receptor‐coupled states, whereas GTPγS stabilizes a low‐signal GTP‐like state; thus, the residual signal cannot be assigned unambiguously to a single G‐protein population.

Altogether, these data demonstrated that agonists, by activating the receptor DOR, were able to favor the Gαi‐OPEN state whatever the G protein was loaded with GDP or GTPγS. The most possible mechanism is that the receptor was able to empty the Gα subunit by inducing nucleotide release whatever the guanine nucleotide bound (Figure 4C). Conversely, and as expected for a reversible process, the antagonist produced the opposite effect by reducing the population of G protein existing in the empty state even in the presence of saturating concentrations of GDP or GTPγS or both.

Pharmacological Characterization of Different G Protein‐Coupled Receptors

We then took advantage of our membrane‐based assay that relies on the detection of the endogenous GαOPEN, to analyze the molecular pharmacology of different Gi‐coupled GPCRs [22, 32, 45]. These receptors are in different cellular backgrounds that can potentially have different composition of Gi protein subtypes [22, 46, 47]. We thus verified that the sensor Ab13 + peptide‐XL665 produced similar results in membrane preparations of the recombinant human DOR from HEK293, Chinese hamster CHO‐K1, and rat CHEM‐1 cell lines (Extended Data Figure 5). Similar trFRET signal changes for the agonist (SNC162) and antagonist (naltrindole) were obtained indicating our tools are not selective of the human Gi/o proteins but can recognize other mammalian species.

We then used our sensor Ab13 + peptide to perform the pharmacological characterization of DOR agonists (Figure 6A) and antagonists (Figure 6B) with different efficacies. The results confirmed that we can precisely define the potency of either agonists or antagonists of a given Gi‐coupled receptor. Indeed, pEC50 and pIC50 obtained for these compounds with our FRET sensor strongly correlated with those reported previously by [35S]‐GTPγS binding assay (Figure 6C,D). Of note, while the pIC50 for the antagonists were the same in the two assays (Figure 6D), a shift to better potencies was measured with the agonists in our FRET sensor assay (Figure 6C). It might be due to the influence of the tools used to detect the Gi.GTPγS conformation, since Ab13 increased the kinetics of GTPγS‐Bodipy binding (kon) and the releasing kinetic (koff) of GTPγS by the Gαi subunit, alone or in presence of the peptide‐XL665 (Extended Data Figure 6A), while this effect was less observed with the other antibodies (Extended Data Figure 6B).

Interestingly, DOR partial agonists can be discriminated from full agonists (Figure 6A). In addition, antagonists such as BNTX and naltriben, that were described as inverse agonists [48] in contrast to the other antagonists (naltrindole, naltrexone and naloxone) showed a larger efficacy than the other antagonists (Figure 6B). It is consistent with our sensor being able to measure a larger proportion of the Gi.GTPγS conformation in the presence of inverse agonists, since these ligands are expected to induce less G protein emptying resulting from the constitutive activity of the receptor.

The analysis of the two other opioid receptors, MOR and KOR, reveals that our FRET sensor is also relevant to investigate these receptors (Figure 6E,F). Nalbuphine acts as a full agonist on KOR and a partial agonist on DOR as previously reported [49, 50]. But the dynorphin B reported to be a full agonist on these three opioid receptors, behaves as a partial agonist on µOPr with our sensor [51]. Further studies will be necessary to investigate if these unexpected differences are due to the fact that our sensor recognizes Gαi1 and Gαi3 only, and not Gαi2, or it is due to a biased agonist activity of dynorphin B on the Gi/o proteins [52].

Finally, we have applied our FRET sensor to other strictly Gi/o‐coupled receptors (Figure 6G) such as the nociceptin (Extended Data Figure 7), dopamine D2 (Figure 6H), serotonin 5HT1A and mGlu2 receptors. We also investigated the neurotensin receptor (Figure 6I) that has a primary coupling to Gq with a secondary coupling to Gi and Gs proteins [53], and GLP1R that do not activate Gi/o. For these different receptors, the maximal rate of agonist‐induced GαOPEN form was estimated between 20% and 40% of the detectable pool of Gi/o proteins measured by the tools mAb13 + peptide‐XL665 (Figure 7). As a negative control, no activation has been detected from GS‐coupled receptors such as GLP‐1 receptor (Figure 6G). These data nicely illustrate that GPCRs can have different effect on the GαOPEN—GαGTP equilibrium.

Visualizing G Protein State Equilibria and Ternary Complex Stabilization in Cellulo

To confirm that the receptor‐mediated nucleotide release and stabilization of the open state observed in membranes can occur in a more physiological environment, we adapted our trFRET sensors for use in a cellular context using a soft permeabilization approach (Figure 8A). Consistent with our membrane findings, the agonist DAMGO induced a concentration‐dependent decrease in trFRET signal between the GTP bound‐state peptide‐XL665 and the Ab13‐Tb in HEK293 cells expressing µOPr (Figure 8B). This decrease, observed in the presence of both 10 and 100 nM of GTPγS, directly correlates with the receptor‐stimulated release of GTPγS from the Gα subunit, confirming the “reverse” GEF activity in cells.

GPCR‐mediated stabilization of the G protein open‐state ternary complex in cells. (A) Schematic of cellular trFRET adaptation via soft permeabilization. (B) DAMGO‐induced GTPγS release in cells measured by the peptide‐XL665/Ab13‐Tb sensor. (C) Design of the heterotrimer sensor (Ab39‐d2/Ab31‐Tb) for tracking Gα/βγ reassociation. (D) Validation of heterotrimer dissociation in purified proteins and lysates. (E‐F) Agonist‐induced increase in trFRET signal in cells, indicating stabilization of the ternary complex at overexpressed (E) and endogenous (F) G protein levels. (G) Proposed model of GPCR‐mediated G protein state equilibria.

To investigate the structural consequences of this release, we developed a novel heterotrimer sensor consisting of Ab39‐d2 targeting Gαi/o and Ab31‐Tb, a new antibody targeting the Gβγ interface (Figure 8C). Characterization of Ab31 revealed that its binding is strictly dependent on the presence of the Gγ2 subunit and shows specificity for Gβ1γ2 and Gβ3γ2 complexes (Extended Data Figure 8A–E). Quantitative PCR confirmed that the HEK µOPr cell line lacks endogenous Gγ2 (Extended Data Figure 8G–I), necessitating its overexpression to facilitate heterotrimeric G protein complex detection by the sensor.

In purified proteins and cell lysates, the addition of GTPγS induced a robust decrease in trFRET between Gα and Gβγ, reflecting heterotrimer dissociation (Figure 8D). However, in the cellular context, DAMGO stimulation produced a paradoxical increase in trFRET ratio in cells pre‐loaded with 10 nM GTPγS (Figure 8E). This increase indicates that the activated receptor actively recruits dissociated Gβγ back to the Gα subunit by stabilizing the nucleotide‐free open conformation (GαOPEN). We observed a similar phenomenon when only Gγ2 was overexpressed, demonstrating that the sensor can track the stabilization of the high‐affinity ternary complex at endogenous Gαi/o and Gβ expression levels (Figure 8F).

Titration experiments revealed that this observation is critically dependent on the GTPγS concentration; the GEF‐mediated stabilization of the ternary complex is most apparent when GTPγS levels are sufficient to induce basal dissociation but low enough to allow the receptor to shift the equilibrium back to the open state (Extended Data Figure 8J,K). Altogether, these data support a model where the activated GPCR acts as a scaffold that not only promotes nucleotide exchange but actively stabilizes a high‐affinity A‐R*‐ GαOPEN‐Gβγ complex by promoting the release of bound GTP (Figure 8G).

Discussion

Our study shows that an active GPCR can not only favor the formation of the GTP bound state, but can also release GTP from the active G protein under certain conditions, to reform a GαOPEN state offering the possibility to precisely control the amount of GTP‐bound active form generated by the activated GPCR. Such observations were made possible thanks to the innovative tools we report here that allow the detection of either the open or the GTP bound state of endogenous Gi proteins. The validation of these tools is well illustrated by the determination of both the efficacy and potency of various ligands acting at different Gi coupled GPCRs.

Up to now, G protein activation is mainly being measured using over‐expressed biosensors that can possibly interfere with the coupling process [28, 29, 31, 32, 51]. To measure native G protein activation, most assays quantify the GTP bound form of the G protein using either radioactive [26, 27] or fluorescent GTP [33, 34, 35] analogs, or specific sensors responding to GTP bound G proteins [30]. These assays just estimate the quantity of active G proteins. However, the amount of GTP bound form of G proteins is not only controlled by the GTP binding step resulting from GPCR activation, but also by the GTPase activity [54] and its regulation by RGS proteins [7], a step that is not involved when non‐hydrolysable GTP analogs are used. Another process possibly involved in the final amount of GTP‐bound G proteins is the capacity of GPCRs to stabilize the open form of G proteins [4], likely able to release not only bound GDP, but also bound GTP, then possibly limiting the amount of active G proteins. Indeed, such a process has well been reported in the early days [17, 55], but is not yet fully characterized, and its possible influence of GPCR coupling efficacy not yet fully considered [56].

While this manuscript was under preparation, two independent studies from the Bohn's laboratory reported agonist‐dependent GTPγS release at opioid and other Gi/o‐coupled receptors using radiolabeled nucleotide pulse–chase paradigms [19, 20]. These studies provide strong pharmacological evidence that receptor activation can promote dissociation of GTPγS from Gi proteins and further propose that distinct agonists may differentially favor GTP binding vs. GTP release, consistent with multi‐active‐state models of GPCR signaling. Our findings are fully consistent with the existence of receptor‐mediated GTP release, but extend these observations by directly resolving the underlying conformational states of endogenous Gi proteins. By selectively detecting the nucleotide‐free open state and the GTP‐bound active state using independent trFRET sensors, we demonstrate that activated GPCRs actively shift the equilibrium between these states, rather than simply promoting a single unidirectional exchange reaction.

In the present work, we take a complementary approach by directly monitoring the conformational equilibrium of endogenous Gi proteins using trFRET sensors that selectively recognize defined Gαi subunits and discriminate between nucleotide‐free and GTP‐bound conformations. This enables direct assessment of receptor‐induced GTP release from specific G protein subtypes, rather than inference from bulk nucleotide turnover. Such subtype‐level resolution is particularly relevant given that GPCR coupling efficacy, apparent signaling bias, and downstream signaling profiles can depend on the precise Gα isoform engaged. We developed two pairs of binders that allow to selectively detect the open form and the GTP bound form of the native Gαi proteins. Using these tools labelled with trFRET compatible fluorophores, we could measure the effect of the activation of various GPCRs on the formation or disappearance of the three main states of the Gi protein subtypes, the GDP‐bound, the open, and the GTP bound states. As expected, we did detect the increase in open G proteins from pre‐loaded GDP‐bound G proteins.

We also observed the disappearance of the pre‐loaded GTP‐bound G proteins, as well as the increase in the open form, indicating that GPCRs can stabilize the open form of the G proteins, starting from either the GDP‐bound form known to be associated with Gβγ, or the GTP‐bound form, that may not be associated with Gβγ. The large signal to noise ratio of the trFRET changes observed allowed an easy determination of the potencies and efficacies of agonists, as well as the action of selective antagonists. As such, these pharmacological data, fully consistent with the literature, nicely validate our tools to detect native specific Gi protein activation. Because of its high selectivity, this approach can well be expanded to monitor the activation of any specific subtypes of native G proteins. Most importantly, these data also indirectly validate our observation that the active receptors are able to stabilize the open form of the Gi protein from either the GDP‐ or the GTP‐bound forms. This observation is consistent with previous studies revealing the release of GTPγS from the G protein by activated GPCRs [19, 20, 54, 57], and by FRET studies(50) showing that strong GPCR activation can lead to the recruitment of the GTP bound G protein on the receptor, and the reformation of the inactive Gαβγ complex in cells.

From a mechanistic standpoint, nucleotide binding and release assays alone cannot distinguish whether observed decreases in GTPγS occupancy arise from altered nucleotide binding kinetics, reassociation of Gα–GTP with the receptor, or stabilization of nucleotide‐free intermediates. By contrast, our conformational sensors reveal that receptor activation consistently increases the population of the open, nucleotide‐free Gi state, irrespective of whether the starting pool of G proteins is GDP‐ or GTP‐loaded. This is consistent with the receptor‐induced GTP release being mechanistically coupled to stabilization of the open G protein conformation. By directly tracking the reassociation of the heterotrimer in cells, we provide structural evidence that the ‘reverse mode’ is not merely a decrease in nucleotide occupancy but a physical stabilization of the high‐affinity A‐R‐GαOPEN/Gβγ ternary complex. Using our heterotrimer sensor, we observed a paradoxical increase in trFRET signal upon agonist stimulation in cells pre‐loaded with GTPγS, indicating that the activated receptor actively recruits dissociated Gβγ back to the Gα subunit. This stabilization of the ternary complex was found to be critically dependent on the nucleotide concentration, becoming most apparent when GTPγS levels permit basal dissociation but allow the receptor to shift the equilibrium back toward the open state. Notably, DAMGO displayed an approximately tenfold higher apparent potency in permeabilized cells than in isolated membrane preparations, highlighting the context dependence of operational receptor–G‐protein coupling. Differences in membrane organization, receptor/G‐protein stoichiometry, endogenous interacting proteins, nucleotide exposure, permeabilization, and sensor accessibility may contribute to this shift. This mechanism likely serves as a fundamental tuning knob for signaling output, where the receptor can quantitatively limit the pool of active, dissociated G proteins under conditions of high‐efficacy activation or restricted GTP availability.

Our observations then demonstrate that the amount of the GTP‐bound active Gi proteins is controlled by three reactions: i) GTP binding on the high affinity open state of the G protein associated with the active GPCR; ii) the intrinsic GTPase activity potentiated by RGS proteins, leading to the formation of the GDP‐bound form; and iii) the capacity of the active GPCR to bind the GTP‐bound state to re‐form the open state of the G protein, then inactivating the G protein. This finding nicely complements previous results by Hommers et al. showing that strong receptor activation can limit the amount of active Gi proteins [56]. Such findings are consistent with the observation that strong activation of the adenosine receptor did not lead to a full activation of GIRK channels, a phenomenon that can be observed in native atrial cells, and indeed led to a paradoxical decrease in pre‐activated GIRK channels. Our findings bring further evidence that the re‐formation of the RG complex from an activated GPCR and the GTP active G protein can occur under certain conditions, leading to a decrease in the maximal amount of active G proteins, then limiting the maximal cellular response. Although it is accepted that the high GTP concentration in the cell (from 50 to 1000 µM) [12] as well as the large amount of G proteins relative to a GPCR would largely favor the formation of the GTP bound state of the G protein, a strong and sustained GPCR activation, especially in a limited subcellular compartment, may be sufficient to decrease GTP concentration to a level allowing the formation of a significant amount of RG complex. This could then limit the maximal signal as observed with the GIRK channel activation [56]. However, we can speculate that this backward reaction may well depend on the receptor and the G protein subtype considered, and possibly on the ligand considered [58], as one can easily expect it will depend on the agonist‐bound receptor affinity for the open state of the G protein. The possibility that certain agonists may bias signaling toward GTP release has been proposed to confer therapeutic advantages in vivo, particularly in the context of opioid analgesia [20]. While these observations are intriguing, they should be interpreted in light of the strong dependence of nucleotide exchange equilibria on receptor density, G protein subtype availability, and intracellular GTP concentrations, parameters that can vary substantially across cellular contexts and experimental systems.

Our results indicate that receptor‐driven stabilization of the open G protein state is an intrinsic property of GPCR–Gi coupling and may therefore operate broadly across receptors, ligands, and G protein subtypes. Rather than representing a rare or ligand‐restricted signaling mode, GTP release appears to be a fundamental component of receptor control over G protein state equilibria, capable of quantitatively limiting signaling output under peculiar conditions of sustained or high‐efficacy receptor activation and limited amount of GTP. Although more work is needed to better understand such a process, our data obtained with native G proteins, and with two independent sets of tools, and consistent with previous studies [19, 20, 56], indicate that it can occur and may limit the maximal response of a GPCR under peculiar conditions that remain to be identified. In support of a likely physiological involvement of such a process, drugs with differential effect on this GPCR action display specific responses in animals [19, 20]. Whatever the possible physiological relevance of this process, our data and tools highlight its usefulness in characterizing the pharmacological properties of GPCRs.

Methods

Materials

Peptide KB 1753 (SSRGYYHGIWVGEEGRLSR) [43] were synthesized by PolyPeptide group. Fluorophores Lumi4‐Tb and d2 were provided by Revvity (#62TBSPEA, #62D2DPEA). Biotinylation and cysteine addition were performed by Revvity. Commercial antibody against Gαi proteins (SC‐13533) were from Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc. Home‐made antibodies were provided by Revvity from mice immunization program. Membrane preparations were purchased from Revvity (Membrane Target Systems #RBHODM400UA, #6110549400UA, #6110558400UA, #RBHS1AM400UA, #RBHD2CM400UA, #RBXNT1M400UA, #6110537400UA, #RBHORLM400UA) and Eurofins Pharma Discovery Services. BNTX, DAMGO, DPDPE, dynorphin B, endomorphin‐1, J113397, [Leu5]‐enkephalin, naloxone, naltrexone, naltrindole, neurotensin, nociceptin, risperidone, SNC162 and SR142849 were from Tocris Bioscience. Nalbuphine, naltriben, PPHT and 6’GNTI were from Sigma–Aldrich.

Production and Purification of the Recombinant Gαi1

The synthetic gene coding for the full‐length human Gαi1with a Twin Strep‐Tag (TST) in N‐terminus (SAWSHPQFEKGGGSGGGSGGSAWSHPQFEK) was cloned into baculovirus expression system (Bac‐to‐Bac Baculovirus expression system, Thermo Fischer Scientific). Sf9 insect cells were transfected with bacmid coding for TST‐Gαi1 with Cellfectin II reagent and cells were cultured in suspension and incubated at 28°C for 72 h. The supernatant of infected cells was collected as first baculovirus production batch (P1). Then Sf9 cells were infected with the baculovirus P1 and incubated at 28°C for 6 days in order to produce the second baculovirus batch (P2), that have been collected from the supernatant of the cell culture.

Sf9 cells were infected with baculovirus P2 at a multiplicity of infection of 5, at a cell density of 2 × 106 cells/mL and incubated at 28°C for 48 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min. All purification steps were carried out at 4°C. Pellets were resuspended with ice‐cold lysis buffer (LB) consisting of 20 mM HEPES (pH 8), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA, 23 µg/mL PMSF, 21 µg/mL TLCK, 21 µg/mL TPCK (protease inhibitors), 3.3 µg/mL leupeptin, 3.3 µg/mL Lima bean trypsin inhibitor. Cells were homogenized with Ultra Turrax three times for 15 sec, detergent Genapol 0.05% was added to solubilize membranes, lysed cells were incubated 30 min at 4°C under agitation. Proteins were collected in the supernatant by centrifugation at 38 420 x g at 4°C for 15 min. The clear supernatant was applied on a column containing Strep‐Tactin Superflow resin (IBA) which had been equilibrated with LB. The column was washed with wash buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA, 11 mM CHAPS), and protein were eluted with elution buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA, 11 mM CHAPS, 5 mM D‐desthiobiotin). The fractions containing proteins were pooled and the buffer solution was exchanged with 20 mM HEPES pH 8, 100 mM NaCl, 11 mM CHAPS with Sephadex G25 PD‐10 desalting columns (GE Healthcare Life Sciences). TST‐Gαi1 proteins were stored at −80°C.

Bodipy‐GTPγS Binding Assay

Recombinant TST‐Gαi1 proteins were tested in Bodipy‐GTPγS (Bodipy‐FL‐GTPγS, Thermo Fischer Scientific) binding assay. Proteins were diluted in HEM buffer (10 mM HEPES, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM MgCl2) and added (10 µL) in a 384‐well black plate (Greiner) at several range of concentrations. Bodipy‐GTPγS at 10 nM was added (5 µL), and kinetic binding of the fluorescent GTPγS was measured at PHERAstar (BMG Labtech) with the following parameters: excitation at 482 nm (10 flashes per well), emission at 520 nm and gain of 300. Nonspecific signal is obtained by adding non labeled GTPγS.

Antibodies and Peptides Binding Assay

The binding assay was performed in 384‐well white plate (Greiner). Recombinant TST‐Gαi1 proteins at 1 nM (5 µL) were incubated with 10 µM of each nucleotide (5 µL), GDP or GTPγS within 30 min at 25°C. Then antibodies or peptide labeled with d2 against proteins were added at different concentrations (5 µL). The antibody against the tag of the protein (anti‐TST), labeled with Lumi4‐Tb was added at 0.5 nM (5 µL). Reagents were incubated at 25°C for 3 h. trFRET measurements were measured with a PHERAstar microplate reader (BMG Labtech) with the following setup: excitation at 337 nm (40 flashes per well), donor emission at 620 nm, acceptor emission at 665 nm, integration delay of 60 µs and integration time of 400 µs.

Cell Culture and Transfection

Plasmids encoding for the different G‐proteins with a TST and a Flag‐tag added at the N‐terminus of the proteins (Gαi1, Gαi2, Gαi3, Gαo, Gαz, Gαs, Gαq, Gα12 and Gα13) were synthetized by Genecust. HEK293 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 in 96‐well black plates (Greiner). For each well, the mix ratio DNA (µg): Lipofectamine 2000 (µL) was 0.4. A total of 0.15 µg of DNA was incubated with 0.375 µL of Lipofectamine 2000 in 50 µL Opti‐MEM (1x) (Invitrogen) for 20 min at 25°C and then added in the well. 50 000 cells were added in each well on the mix and incubated at 37°C in a humified incubator containing 5% CO2.

Antibodies Selectivity Assay

The HEK293 cells were pre‐incubated for 30 min at 20°C in the presence or absence of nucleotides GDP or GTPγS (10 µM) diluted in permeabilized buffer containing 50 mM Tris HCl pH 7.4, 10 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.1% BSA and 0.02% Triton. Antibodies against the two tags were added to measure the expression levels of each recombinant G proteins (0.5 nM anti‐Twin‐strep‐tag labeled with Lumi4‐Tb (provided by Revvity), 10 nM HTRF Anti‐FLAG mAb d2‐Conjugate (Revvity, #61FGBDLF/A/B). The binding of each antibody anti‐Gα labeled with d2 (10 nM) was measured by the trFRET signal with the anti‐TST labeled with Lumi4‐Tb (0.5 nM) after 20 h incubation at 20°C. A PHERAstar microplate reader (BMG Labtech) was used with the following setup: excitation at 337 nm (40 flashes per well), donor emission at 620 nm, acceptor emission at 665 nm, integration delay of 60 µs and integration time of 400 µs.

[35S]‐GTPγS Binding Assay

[35S]‐GTPγS binding to membrane preparations were performed in 96‐well microplates (from Revvity). Membrane preparations (15 µg/well) were incubated with or without ligands dose response for 15 min at 30°C in 20 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, 3 µM GDP and 3 mM MgCl2. Then, 0.1 nM [35S]‐GTPγS (Revvity, #NEG030H250UC) was added to the membranes for 30 min at 30°C. The reaction was stopped by centrifugation (800 g for 10 min) at 4°C and removal of supernatants. Microplates were counted for 2 min per well with the MicroBeta TriLux.

trFRET Gi Protein Assay

The activation assay of δ opioid receptor (DOR, 9 pmol/mg), μ opioid receptor (MOR, 8 pmol/mg), κ opioid receptor (KOR, 3.6 pmol/mg), nociceptin receptor (OPRL1, 1.7 pmol/mg), dopamine D2 short receptor (D2sR, 34.4 pmol/mg) and neurotensin receptor (NTS1R, 3.45 pmol/mg) overexpressed in commercial membrane preparations was performed in 384‐well white microplates. All the reagents were diluted in 50 mM Tris HCl pH 7.4, 10 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2 and 0.1% BSA. The biotinylated peptide (50 nM biotine‐KB1753DS) was pre‐incubated with the 25 nM HTRF Streptavidin XL665‐Conjugate (Revvity, # 610SAXLF/A/B) for 30 min at 20°C. Then, ligands were diluted with the buffer supplemented with 10 nM GTPγS and incubated with the membrane preparations. At the same time, streptavidin‐peptide complex or d2 labeled antibodies and Lumi4‐Tb labeled antibodies were added to the membrane preparations and ligands for 1 h at 20°C. trFRET signal was measured with a PHERAstar microplate reader (BMG Labtech) with the following setup: excitation at 337 nm (40 flashes per well), donor emission at 620 nm, acceptor emission at 665 nm, integration delay of 60 µs and integration time of 400 µs.

trFRET Cellular Activation and Stabilization Assays

HEK293 cells stably expressing the human µ‐opioid receptor (µOPr) were transfected with Gαi1, Gβ1 and Gγ2 or Gγ2 alone using Lipofectamine 2000. For cellular trFRET, cells were washed with a phosphate‐free buffer and subjected to soft permeabilization using Revvity HTRF Permeabilization buffer#1 within the HTRF Cellular GTP/Gi protein binding kit (Revvity, #62CGTPPEG/C). For the heterotrimer stabilization assay, cells were pre‐incubated with indicated concentrations of GTPγS (10 nM) for 30 min to allow for spontaneous G protein dissociation. trFRET binders (Ab39‐d2 and Ab31‐Tb) were added simultaneously with dose‐responses of DAMGO. Plates were incubated for 1 h at 20°C before measurement of trFRET signal with a PHERAstar microplate reader (BMG Labtech) using the following setup: excitation at 337 nm (40 flashes per well), donor emission at 620 nm, acceptor emission at 665 nm, integration delay of 60 µs and integration time of 400 µs.

Quantitative Real‐Time PCR (qPCR)

To characterize the endogenous landscape of G protein subunits, total RNA was extracted from HEK µOPr cells using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). cDNA was synthesized and subsequent qPCR was performed using TaqMan technology (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's protocols. Specific TaqMan probes were utilized for the relative quantification of all human Gα, Gβ, and Gγ subtypes. The GAPDH gene was employed as the internal housekeeping gene for normalization. Data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method to determine the percentage expression relative to GAPDH.
