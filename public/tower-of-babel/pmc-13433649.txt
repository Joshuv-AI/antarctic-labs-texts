Cement Use in Bipolar Hemiarthroplasty and Its Association With Cancer Risk
Authors: Jung-Wee Park, Danbee Kang, Hyunsoo Kim, Eunjee Kang, Juhee Cho, Young-Kyun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies have been unable to confirm a direct relationship between polymethylmethacrylate use and cancer risk in hemiarthroplasty (HA) due to a lack of differentiation between cemented and cementless fixation in cancer risk assessment. This study aimed to clarify this by examining the incidence of cancer in patients who underwent cemented HA compared to those who received cementless HA.MethodsThis study was a retrospective nationwide cohort study using the Korean National Health Insurance Service database, which represents nearly the entire Republic of Korea (South Korea) population. We included adult patients aged over 19 years who underwent HA between January 1, 2003, and December 31, 2019 (N = 160,394). Patients with previous revision surgeries, bilateral procedures, or prior cancer history were excluded. Cemented and cementless groups were defined based on the type of femoral stem used. The primary outcome was the incidence of cancer, measured through cancer-specific insurance codes and cancer diagnosis codes. Kaplan-Meier curves and Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate cancer risk differences between cemented and cementless HA groups.ResultsAmong the patients, 37.6% underwent HA with cemented fixation. During a follow-up period of up to 12 years, no significant differences were observed between the cemented and cementless groups in the risk of developing any cancer, solid tumors, or hematologic cancers. However, the cemented group had a 1.8-fold (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.12–2.99) increased risk of multiple myeloma compared to the cementless group. This association was particularly pronounced among patients under 70 years of age (adjusted hazard ratio, 3.44; 95% CI, 1.10–10.82) relative to those aged 70 or older.ConclusionPatients who underwent cemented HA showed a higher risk of multiple myeloma than those who received cementless HA. The association between cement use and multiple myeloma was especially evident in younger patients. These findings highlight the importance of evaluating long-term risks associated with cemented HA to inform safer surgical approaches and guide treatment selection.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06307145Keywords: Hemiarthroplasty, Bone Cement, Cancer, Multiple Myeloma, National Registry, Korea

INTRODUCTION

Bipolar hemiarthroplasty (HA) has emerged as a widely employed and effective surgical procedure for hip fractures, involving the replacement of the femoral head with a prosthetic implant while preserving the natural acetabulum in elderly patients.1,2 The choice of fixation method, either cemented or cementless, plays a pivotal role in determining the surgery’s long-term success and clinical outcomes.3,4,5 Cemented fixation, which employs bone cement typically composed of polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) to secure the prosthesis, has been a traditional choice for total joint arthroplasty due to its proven benefits in enhancing implant stability, allowing early weight bearing, and reducing postoperative pain.3,4,5

Although bone cement is not classified as a carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer, there is an ongoing debate among researchers about its potential health risks. The application of PMMA-based bone cement in surgical procedures has been reported to induce chronic irritation and inflammation at the implant sites.6,7,8 This inflammatory response is of significant concern because it may contribute to an elevated risk of malignancy, considering the tissue and cellular toxicity attributed to PMMA.9,10,11 Intriguingly, several registry studies have suggested an increased incidence of certain malignancies following total joint arthroplasty, which frequently employs bone cement.12,13,14,15,16,17,18,19 However, insufficient evidence exists to establish a direct link between the use of PMMA and cancer risk in HA because previous studies did not distinguish between cemented and cementless fixation when identifying the risk of cancer.

In this regard, using a nationwide cohort which could offer a comprehensive unparalleled resource for investigating the long-term safety of medical devices,20,21,22,23,24 we sought to investigate the incidence of cancer in patients undergoing cemented HA compared to those undergoing cementless HA.

METHODS

Study population and design

We followed Strengthening the Reporting of Cohort Studies in Surgery criteria for the methods.25 For this study, we obtained the data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) database. The Korean NHIS covers approximately 97% of Koreans, and 3% of the remaining Koreans who cannot afford national insurance are covered by the Medical Aid Program.26 Therefore, it represents the entire population of Republic of Korea (South Korea) and contains national records of all covered inpatient and outpatient visits, procedures, and prescriptions.

We included all adult patients older than 19 years who were admitted and received HA (procedure code: N0715, N2710) between January 1, 2003, and December 31, 2019 (N = 192,149). This cohort was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06307145). For this specific study, we excluded patients who had undergone any previous revision surgery (procedure code: N1711, N3710, N3720, N1715, N4710, N1725, N1721, N4720; n = 772), bilateral surgery (n = 7,922), and those with a history of cancer (n = 15,087). We excluded patients with pathological fractures (n = 2,219) or insufficient patient information (n = 5,446). Taking into account that patients could meet multiple exclusion criteria, the final sample size of this study was 160,394 (Fig. 1). The official review committee of the NHIS approved this study and provided access to the data.

Cementation

To identify cemented and cementless groups, we reviewed all femoral stem codes registered in NHIS during the study period. We categorized patients as belonging to the cemented group if a cemented femoral stem was used in the HA; otherwise, they were categorized into the cementless group.

Incidence of cancers

Cancer was defined as the presence of a cancer-specific insurance claim code (V193 code) with cancer diagnosis code (C00–C97). Since the cancer-specific code became available in 2017, we also considered the presence of a cancer diagnosis code occurring more than three times within a year or an inpatient hospitalization with a cancer diagnosis code as indicative of the incidence of cancer.27 In this study, we included twenty-four different types of cancer following the International Association of Cancer Registries cancer classification.28 Vital status was obtained from death certificates collected by Statistics Korea, which operates under the Ministry of Strategy and Finance of Korea.29

Confounding factors

Comorbidities within the year prior to hospitalization for surgery were summarized using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI).30,31 Additionally, we included hypertension (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision [ICD-10] codes I10–I13 and I15), which is not included in the CCI. The reason for surgery was also defined using the ICD-10 code for the primary diagnosis at admission. These included femoral neck fractures (S720), intertrochanteric fractures (S721), and other conditions (osteonecrosis of the femoral head, femoral head fractures, or subchondral insufficiency fractures of the femoral head). Hospitals were classified according to their capacity based on the number of hospital beds and the number of specialties, as defined by the Korean Health Law. A hospital is defined as a healthcare institution having more than 30 inpatient beds. General hospitals have more than 100 beds and more than seven specialty departments, including Internal Medicine, Surgery, Pediatrics, Obstetrics and Gynecology, Anesthesiology, Pathology, and Laboratory Medicine. Tertiary hospitals are general hospitals with > 20 specialty departments that serve as teaching hospitals for medical students and nurses. The NHIS claims for inpatient and outpatient visits, procedures, and prescriptions were coded using the ICD-10 codes.32 As the NHIS routinely audits the claims; such data are considered reliable and used in numerous peer-reviewed publications.29,33

Statistical analysis

The primary endpoint was the incidence of cancer. The follow-up time was measured from the insertion of the initial prosthesis until the occurrence of cancer, death, or December 31st, 2019 (end of the study), whichever came first. The annual incidence percent per year was calculated as the number of events per 100 person-years (py) of follow-up. The cumulative incidence of each outcome was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier curves.34 Hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models. The proportionality of hazards was assessed by visual inspection of log-minus-log plots and Schoenfeld residuals. The models were adjusted for age, sex, hospital type, presence of hypertension, and CCI score. There were no missing variables due to the nature of the data.

In addition, to account for the competing risks of mortality, sub-distribution hazard ratios (subHRs) for the incidence of each cancer were calculated using the Fine and Gray method.35 We also performed a subgroup analysis to confirm that the association between cement and the incidence of multiple myeloma (MM) was consistent across age groups and sex.

All analyses were two-sided, and P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.2 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R software, version 4.0.3 (Free Software Foundation Inc., Boston, MA, USA).

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Samsung Medical Center (IRB number: SMC 2021-08-174), and the requirement for informed consent was waived because only de-identified data were used.

RESULTS

The cumulative incidence of MM was consistently higher in the cemented group than that in the non-cemented group throughout the follow-up period (Fig. 2). The cemented group had a 1.8 times (95% CI, 1.12–2.99) higher risk of MM than the cementless group. The association also persisted after competing risks due to mortality were considered (subHR, 1.93; 95% CI, 1.06–3.52). Furthermore, sensitivity analysis conducted on patients without any cancer within 6 months after surgery showed robust results (HR, 1.93; 95% CI, 1.06–3.52) (Table 2).

When MM risk was evaluated by age, the association was particularly strong for those < 70 (adjusted HR, 3.44; 95% CI, 1.10–10.82) than ≥ 70 years of age (adjusted HR, 1.43; 95% CI, 0.83–2.45) (Table 3). Similar association was observed between the cemented group and increased MM in male (adjusted HR, 2.15; 95% CI, 0.74–6.28) and female patients (adjusted HR, 1.76; 95% CI, 1.01–3.04) (Table 3).

On the other hand, pancreas (fully adjusted HR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.61–0.92) and breast cancer (fully adjusted HR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.54–0.89) risk was relatively lower in the cemented group than in the cementless group (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

In this nationwide cohort study of HA, the overall cancer risk did not differ between the cemented and cementless groups, except for MM. The cemented group had a higher risk of MM than the cementless group. The association between cement use and risk of MM development was stronger in younger patients.

Several studies have reported an increased risk of certain types of cancer, including stomach, thyroid, pancreas, prostate, melanoma, leukemia, and MM, after arthroplasty compared to the general population.12,13,14,15 However, these previous studies did not distinguish between cemented and cementless HA. They could not identify which part of the materials used for arthroplasty could be associated with cancer risk. Therefore, we directly compared the cemented group with the cementless group, controlling for confounding factors with a sufficient sample size and long-term follow-up. Cement use was not associated with an increased risk of cancer. A previous study in total hip arthroplasty found similar results, indicating that patients who received cemented implants had no additional risk of cancer compared with those who received cementless implants (relative risk [RR], 0.92; 95% CI, 0.81–1.06).36 However, the previous study found an increasing trend for hematolymphoid cancers (RR, 1.17; 95% CI, 0.68–2.02).36 In our study, while cemented fixation did not increase the risk of hematologic cancer, patients with cement fixation had higher incidence of MM than patients with cementless fixation. Furthermore, in the subgroup analysis, we found a stronger association between cement exposure and the risk of developing MM in people under the age of 70 years. Given that MM is typically diagnosed at an average age of 70 years, and only a small percentage of MM patients are younger,37 cement might be an additional risk to increase MM. Moreover, the stronger association observed among patients younger than 70 years may be partly explained by differences in exposure duration and bone marrow responsiveness. Younger patients generally have a longer remaining life expectancy, providing a longer time window during which chronic local inflammatory reactions induced by PMMA and alterations in the bone marrow microenvironment could contribute to clonal plasma cell expansion. In addition, bone marrow in younger individuals tends to retain higher cellularity and metabolic activity than that in older adults, which may make the marrow microenvironment more susceptible to inflammatory or oxidative stress-related signaling that facilitates malignant transformation.38 Taken together, these factors may partially account for the more pronounced association in younger patients.

In our study, the patients who underwent cement fixation had a higher incidence of MM than those who underwent cementless fixation alone. This might be due to the inflammation and chronic irritation caused by PMMA in the bone cement. PMMA can be released into the surrounding tissues after surgery, causing potential tissue and cell toxicity.9 This can lead to inflammation and chronic irritation at the implant site,10 which is associated with an increased risk of cancer.11 Another possible explanation is that the implantation of foreign materials, including cement, can provoke a foreign body reaction in the body. This response may involve the activation of immune cells and inflammatory processes. Prolonged foreign body reactions have been linked to an increased cancer risk in some theories.7,8 Additionally, the process of polymerizing bone cement would release free radicals that can induce oxidative stress in surrounding tissue.39 The oxidative stress can damage tissue and DNA, potentially increasing the risk of cancer development.40 These changes resulting from PMMA or free radicals can lead to alterations in the microenvironment of the bone marrow. Although bone disease is not included in the standard model of MM oncogenesis, recent studies have suggested that the bone marrow microenvironment attracts MM cell precursors and promotes their development within the bone marrow.41

Interestingly, in our study, the patients who underwent cement fixation had a lower risk of developing pancreatic or breast cancer. This may be due to the selection process for patients receiving HA. During the exclusion of patients with a history of cancer, women at high risk of breast cancer and hip fractures were likely to be excluded from this study. Although the exact reason for the lower risk of pancreatic cancer cannot be explained, it may be similar in men. Further research, including biological mechanisms and more comprehensive studies, is necessary to validate these findings and gain a more comprehensive understanding of this association.

This highlights the critical need for further research, particularly studies with larger patient populations and longer follow-up periods. Such studies will be vital for comprehensively understanding the long-term implications of cement use in orthopedic surgery. Additionally, future research should aim to elucidate the underlying mechanisms linking cement fixation to an increased risk of MM. Understanding these connections is essential for developing safer surgical approaches and for making informed decisions regarding the selection of HA techniques, balancing benefits against potential long-term risks.

Our study had several limitations. Firstly, the data on exposure and outcomes were extracted solely from the claims data. However, the identification of cancer cases based on claims is considered reliable in Korea because cancer codes are reviewed by the NHIS and have implications for additional patient benefits. Secondly, we had limited information on HA treatment and disease management available in the claims data. Thirdly, because this study was observational, causality cannot be definitively established. Although we adjusted for major demographic and clinical variables, the possibility of unmeasured or residual confounding cannot be excluded. Thus, the observed association should be interpreted cautiously and further mechanistic or prospective studies are warranted. Finally, although we had sufficient statistical power to observe the incidence of MM in patients who underwent HA, a longer follow-up duration may be needed, given the incidence of MM by various material combinations of HA.

Despite these limitations, the use of a national claims database offers several advantages. This enabled the inclusion of a diverse patient population and a wide range of clinical scenarios, thereby enhancing the generalizability of the findings. By analyzing data from this database, this study aimed to determine whether there is a significant difference in cancer incidence between patients who received cemented and cementless fixation during bipolar HA.

In conclusion, we found no increased risk of cancer in patients undergoing cemented HA compared to those undergoing cementless fixation. However, cemented HA in patients showed an increased risk of MM compared to cementless fixation.
