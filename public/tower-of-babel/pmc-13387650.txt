Modeling the networking between hospitals and follow-up care providers during discharge management – Insights from eight structured interviews
Authors: Christine Brütting, Thomas Frese, Alexander Bauer, Erwin Selg, Stefanie Andernach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurpose:Digital platforms for more efficient hospital discharge management already exist. However, current solutions primarily focus on care services, while many patients also require therapeutic treatment after discharge. This study models the integration of previously unconnected external healthcare providers into the hospital discharge management process.Methods:Eight structured face-to-face interviews were conducted with two general practitioners, two physiotherapists, two occupational therapists, and two speech therapists. Based on these interviews, an analysis of the current and target state, as well as user requirements for a digital platform, was developed.Results:Currently, networking between hospitals and therapy practices is largely analogue and inefficient. Appointment coordination is handled via phone, SMS, or email, often causing delays, and redundant efforts. Additional inefficiencies include unnecessary staff movement, redundant documentation, and delays in accessing patient information. Therapists require a digital platform to facilitate collaboration with general practitioners, specialists, medical care providers, nursing homes, hospitals, and rehabilitation clinics. Key requested features include electronic access to discharge letters, diagnoses, and findings. Email is the preferred communication method. A future digital discharge platform should automate patient referrals, enhance transparency, and improve efficiency through real-time tracking, automated documentation, and seamless integration with hospital information systems.Conclusion:A networked digital platform is essential for the efficient exchange of discharge letters, diagnoses, and findings, enabling timely communication with all relevant healthcare stakeholders. Developing a comprehensive digital discharge platform, or integrating general practitioners and healthcare providers into an existing solution is strongly recommended.

Introduction

In many countries, routine discharge management is in place for hospital patients.[1] Hospital social workers currently invest a lot of time in organizing all necessary arrangements for patients after discharge.[2] Digitalization could help save substantial resources in this process.

In Germany, several providers have already developed digital platforms to enhance discharge management efficiency.[3] However, these platforms primarily focus on service providers such as nursing homes or home care services. Yet, many patients also require therapeutic treatment after hospital discharge,[456] and the integration of general practitioners into post-hospital care is not always optimal.[7] As part of a project on cross-sectoral discharge management, general practitioners were integrated into a digital discharge management system alongside nursing homes and outpatient care services. However, this project mainly identified support needs, barriers, and facilitating factors, while a structured assessment of the current state or concrete requirements for a digital discharge platform has not yet been conducted.[8]

In the DigiPost project (https://inno-tdg.de/projekte/digipost/), a digital discharge platform is being developed that connects all relevant therapists (general practitioners, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, and speech therapists) with hospitals and provides the data necessary for patient treatment in digital form. As part of the platform development, eight therapists will be interviewed. The following research questions are to be addressed:

What is the current status quo of the networking between hospitals and therapy practices?What user requirements do therapists have for a digital platform within their practice, and in collaboration with other healthcare actors?How can the future networking between hospitals and therapy practices look through integration into a digital discharge platform?

In the present work, both an as-is and a to-be model of the networking between hospitals and therapists during discharge management are developed. Additionally, a list of user requirements is created. To our knowledge, our project is the only one aiming to integrate all relevant therapists into a digital discharge management system. The results of this study can contribute to improving patient care by ensuring faster and better-coordinated transitions from hospital to outpatient therapy. For healthcare professionals, the proposed digital platform offers relief from redundant administrative tasks and enhances communication efficiency, allowing them to focus more on patient-centered treatment.

Material and Methods

Interviews

To address the research questions, an exploratory approach was chosen in the form of structured interviews. The participating practice owners were asked about the number of employees in their practice, their level of digitalization, and their workflow when contacted by a patient, a patient’s relative, or hospital staff regarding an appointment after a hospital stay.

Additionally, the interviewees were asked to describe their ideal digital platform solution that would allow them to work efficiently and patient-centered—both within their practice and in collaboration with patients and other healthcare providers. Of particular interest was that data should be accessible on the platform, which stakeholders should be connected, and how communication should be structured.

The order of the questions was flexible, allowing for an “organic” interview flow based on the responses, but all questions were to be answered. The interview guideline, developed in collaboration with a software company to determine therapists’ needs for a digital platform, is included in Appendix 1. It was tested with two general practitioners and subsequently adjusted slightly.

Between November 2023 and April 2024, a total of eight face-to-face interviews were conducted in Halle (Saale), each lasting approximately 1 hour on average. Two general practitioners, two physiotherapists, two occupational therapists, and two speech therapists were interviewed [Table 1]. The general practitioners were recruited from the pool of teaching physicians at our general medicine institute. The therapists were recruited from the telephone directory. In both cases, the selection was made alphabetically and consecutively. Care was taken to ensure a balanced gender ratio. All participants have agreed to the interviews and the publication of the data.

Sample description

Most interviews took place at the participants’ workplaces. Since the interviews followed a structured questionnaire, no audio recordings were made. Instead, the interviewer took notes on the participants’ responses, which were later compiled into a written protocol. The final number of respondents was determined by reaching a theoretical saturation point in the interview statements.[9]

Ethics approval

No ethics approval is required for anonymized data collection and analysis in Halle. There is an email correspondence with the managing director of the ethics commission in Halle dated February 19, 2024.

Data analysis

What is the current status quo of the networking between hospitals and therapy practices?

The workflows in the individual practices were modeled based on the interview protocols using Business Process Model and Notation (BPMN), as BPMN is considered an industry standard and is also recognized as an ISO/IEC standard.[10] An excerpt from the corresponding interview protocol is provided using a speech therapy practice as an example, and the BPMN modeling is visually represented.

The integration of the usage context according to DIN standards into the current modeling is crucial for the development of user-centered systems.[11] The DIN standard ISO 9241-11:2020 describes the concept of usability for interactive systems, going beyond mere user-friendliness. It defines the extent to that a system can be used by the intended users to achieve the desired efficiency, effectiveness, and satisfaction—provided it is used as intended and in a defined usage context.[12] The usage context includes the users, the work tasks, the work tools (hardware, software, and materials), as well as the physical and social environment in that the product is used.[13] Based on the interview protocols, the usage context was derived for all the practice owners who participated in the interviews.

The TIMWOODS matrix is a tool for identifying and reducing waste in processes, particularly in areas with high complexity and many interfaces—such as discharge management between hospitals and follow-up care providers. The eight types of waste according to TIMWOODS (Transport, Inventory, Motion, Waiting, Overproduction, Overprocessing, Defects, Skills) help to uncover inefficient workflows, and improve them in a targeted manner.[14] Therefore, a TIMWOODS matrix for the current status quo of networking between hospitals and therapy practices is created based on the interview protocols.

What user requirements do therapists have for a digital platform within their practice and in collaboration with other healthcare actors?

The user requirements of the interviewees for a digital discharge platform are extracted from the eight interview protocols. The focus is on the desired contacts, the information to be digitally stored on the platform, and the type of communication between the various actors in the healthcare system.

How can the future networking between hospitals and therapy practices look through integration into a digital discharge platform?

Target modelling is a deliberate construction to focus on a specific goal.[15] The here presented target modeling for a digital discharge platform based on the current status quo and the user requirements derived from interviews. This modeling aims to optimize discharge processes, improve communication between healthcare providers, and ensure seamless transitions for patients from hospitals to follow-up care providers. The target modeling is also based on the BPMN. Additionally, the potential of digital process features in discharge management, such as location independence, transparency, change dynamics, speed, seamless transitions, automation, algorithmic processes, controllability, and validity,[16] will be listed in a table through the target modeling.

Results

What is the current status quo of the networking between hospitals and therapy practices?

As-is modeling using an excerpt from an interview protocol of a speech therapy practice

Interview Protocol: In this example, a speech therapist was interviewed, who works in a practice alongside five other speech therapists and a receptionist. The practice operates entirely analogue (appointment scheduling, documentation, etc.). When patients or hospital discharge managers want to schedule an appointment, the receptionist answers the call and individually informs all speech therapists via SMS or email about the patient’s request. The speech therapists then respond to the receptionist via SMS or email during a treatment break. The receptionist then calls the discharge manager or patient back to relay the response. In some cases, the discharge manager or patient has already found another practice by that time.

The current state of communication and appointment scheduling is modeled and visualized using BPMN [Figure 1]. The diagram clearly illustrates that contacting individual staff members within the speech therapy practice is logistically and time-consuming.

Context of use

Based on the conducted interviews, the context of use can be well described. The users include all employees of the respective general practitioner and therapy practices. These may be individual practitioners, but more often, they consist of larger teams of multiple general practitioners or therapists, along with medical assistants and receptionists.

Their tasks primarily include scheduling patient appointments, assessing the patient’s status, providing therapy, and documenting results. Additionally, they are responsible for sending and receiving documents, such as reports or prescriptions.

The tools used in these practices are diverse. Hardware includes computers, laptops, mobile and landline phones, fax machines, copiers, printers, and scanners. Regarding software, every practice has a practice management system. Some practices maintain their own websites, which integrate functions such as appointment requests or prescription uploads into daily operations. General practitioners are also connected to digital communication via a uniform standard for the electronic transmission of healthcare-related documents. Paper is still used in all practices, albeit to varying degrees. Some practices only scan documents brought in by patients, while others still maintain their own records in paper form.

The physical environment includes not only the practice itself but also patients’ homes when house visits are necessary. Within the practice, there are typically multiple rooms, such as the reception area and various treatment rooms. The social environment encompasses colleagues within the practice, other healthcare professionals from different practices, hospitals, or health insurance providers, as well as patients and their relatives.

Transport, inventory, motion, waiting, overproduction, overprocessing, defects, skills matrix

The practices of the surveyed practice owners vary significantly in their level of digitalization. However, it is evident that waste occurs across all listed dimensions [Figure 2]. For example, unnecessary material and personnel movements occur due to the retrieval and transport of patient records to treatment rooms. Patient information is sometimes stored redundantly, both in paper form and digitally.

TIMWOODS Matrix on current waste during discharge planning in general practitioner and therapy practices. TIMWOODS = Transport, Inventory, Motion, Waiting, Overproduction, Overprocessing, Defects, Skills

In some cases, there are delays in accessing patient data (e.g. waiting for hospital discharge letters), leading to missing information during treatment. General practitioners and therapy providers often need to actively request this data, which can be time-consuming. Frequently, multiple phone calls are required before reaching the correct contact person.

What user requirements do therapists have for a digital platform within their practice and in collaboration with other healthcare actors?

Figure 3 illustrates the frequency of mentions across all interviews, grouped into stakeholders, documents and information, and communication options. The figure shows that documents and information were discussed most frequently, while communication options were mentioned comparatively rarely. Table 2 summarizes the user requirements in detail. The focus was on the stakeholders to be involved, the information to be stored, and the type of contact options. Eleven different actors or institutions were mentioned with whom the interviewees would like to be connected. The most frequently mentioned were general practitioners and other specialist physicians, healthcare providers, nursing homes, hospitals, and rehabilitation clinics. In response to the question about the information to be stored, 24 suggestions were made. It was emphasized that, above all, the discharge letter, diagnoses, and findings of the patients should be accessible on the platform. How can the future networking between hospitals and therapy practices look through integration into a digital discharge platform?

The target modeling shows a digital discharge management system that automatically organizes all necessary requirements around discharge [Figure 4]. Based on the patient data from the hospital information system, a pseudonymized patient profile is created, and potential external stakeholders are automatically notified. They can view the pseudonymized patient profile and either accept or decline accordingly. The discharge manager can view the acceptances and rejections on the platform and select a therapeutic service provider. The discharge letter is then automatically made available to the therapeutic service provider.

Ideal target modeling of work processes during discharge planning in coordination with various speech therapy practices based on Business Process Model and Notation (BPMN)

The characteristics of digital processes [Table 3] are clearly recognizable in the target modeling. Since patient data, such as the discharge letter, is provided virtually, there is location independence. Transparency is ensured through the real-time tracking of platform usage. It shows when a request, for example for speech therapy, is made and who responds to the request and when. The speed is very high due to the entirely digital communication channels, and automation is provided, for example, through the automatic documentation of requests. If the discharge manager accepts a speech therapist based on the patient’s preference, the other applicants automatically receive a rejection, allowing for dynamic handling of changes. Seamless transitions occur especially at the interfaces between the platform and the Hospital Information System as well as between the platform and the information systems of external stakeholders. An algorithm signals a positive response from external stakeholders, while simultaneously analyzing the process data. Despite this, the requests remain controllable. The patient’s choice of a specific therapeutic service provider always takes precedence and can be adjusted. Validity is ensured through plausibility checks of inputs.

Discussion

Currently, networking between hospitals and therapy practices is largely analogue and inefficient. Communication for scheduling appointments is handled via phone calls, SMS, or email, often leading to delays and redundant effort. Various inefficiencies exist, such as unnecessary staff movement, redundant documentation, and delays in obtaining patient information. Therapists require a digital platform to streamline collaboration with general practitioners and other specialists, medical care providers, nursing homes, hospitals, and rehabilitation clinics, prioritizing features like electronic access to discharge letters, diagnoses, and findings. The most frequently requested form of communication is e-mail. A future digital discharge platform would automate patient referrals, improve transparency, and enhance efficiency through real-time tracking, automated documentation, and seamless integration with hospital information systems.

The number of conducted interviews appears to be sufficient.[9] The platform requirements regarding the stakeholders to be integrated and the contact options already show complete saturation from the fourth interview onward [Table 2]. The platform requirements for documents and information show a theoretical saturation of over 90% from the fourth interview onward. The fact that a relatively small number of interviewees already leads to a theoretical saturation effect is typical for relatively homogeneous study groups,[17] as is the case here.

Based on the interview protocols [Appendix 2], it is evident that the degree of digitalization in medical practices varies significantly. Digital maturity models assessing a company’s ability to use digital technologies effectively in various dimensions and to identify areas for improvement[18] are also used in healthcare, especially in hospitals.[19] In the outpatient sector, there are currently no standardized large-scale surveys to measure digital maturity. However, various research studies have addressed this topic, revealing that methodologies vary significantly, and the digital maturity level remains poorly defined.[20]

The TIMWOODS matrix [Figure 2] reflects current issues and inefficiencies within medical practices and in the networking of healthcare stakeholders, which have also been identified in other studies. These include missing discharge letters from the hospital on the day of discharge,[21] lack of approachability of the clinic staff,[22] or additional workload to access patient information.[22] In addition, digital and paper-based processes coexist in most practices.[23]

So far, it remains unclear whether digitalization in medical practices saves time. For example, issuing an electronic certificate of incapacity for work currently takes 50 sec longer than issuing a paper-based sick note.[24] After the introduction of a digital discharge platform in Mannheim, no significant changes were observed among external stakeholders regarding the time spent with patients or on the computer.[25] The introduction of a discharge platform at another hospital also showed no significant impact on the readmission rate.[8]

In contrast, an observational study on diabetes patients after hospitalization found that when hospital records were promptly made digitally accessible to external follow-up providers, the rate of outpatient follow-up examinations and the use of laboratory tests decreased.[26] Similarly, digital nursing documentation led to time savings.[27]

However, important differences separate our study from previous work. Observational and implementation studies typically evaluate system-level outcomes (readmissions, follow-up rates)[26] or report on platforms designed primarily to connect hospitals with nursing homes.[8] For example, Gonçalves-Bradley et al.[1] concluded that tailored discharge planning probably reduces length of stay and readmission risk. In contrast, our study is a qualitative, user-centered needs assessment that (a) explicitly focuses on therapeutic service providers (physiotherapists, occupational therapists, speech therapists) along with general practitioners, and (b) uses process-modelling to document concrete as-is inefficiencies and detailed to-be functionality requirements. This micro-level, practice-oriented perspective complements implementation studies by clarifying which specific workflow barriers must be removed for broader platforms to succeed in outpatient therapy settings—a stakeholder group that is under-represented in existing digital discharge research.[8]

Conclusion

The as-is modeling particularly highlights many inefficient workflows in the areas of communication and documentation. The introduction of digital systems could significantly reduce many of the current challenges. The cumbersome communication within practices as well as between different healthcare providers, which currently takes place through phone, fax, and email, demonstrates the need for a centralized platform.

In future research, the developed as-is and to-be models could serve as a blueprint for other digital discharge platforms. A subsequent evaluation in real-world healthcare settings should assess measurable outcomes such as time savings, and reduction of communication errors. Furthermore, interoperability with existing hospital information systems and electronic patient records should be systematically examined to ensure seamless data exchange across sectors. In the long term, integrating artificial intelligence could further support predictive discharge planning. Finally, involving patients more actively through patient-facing modules or mobile applications could improve continuity of care after hospital discharge.

Limitations

This work provides a comprehensive picture of the status quo and the requirements for a digital platform in general practitioner and therapy practices. The participation of eight individuals in the in-depth interviews and the inclusion of four different professional groups underline the significance of the results.

However, there are some limitations. For instance, interviewer effects or social desirability effects may have occurred during the interviews.[28] Additionally, it is possible that individuals, who are more interested in the topic of digitalization were more likely to participate in the interviews.[29] Therefore, the responses could be somewhat more positive or negative than those of the general population.[30]
