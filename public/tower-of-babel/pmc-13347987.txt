Early neurodevelopmental outcomes in a child with tuberous sclerosis complex treated with everolimus and vigabatrin
Authors: Sarah ML. Wilson, Megan Samuels, Luana Pimentel, Shelley Varnado, Claire Sartwell, Rosleen Mansour, Deborah Pearson, Hope Northrup, Mary Kay Koenig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo report early neurodevelopmental outcomes in a child with Tuberous Sclerosis Complex (TSC) treated with everolimus and vigabatrin.MethodsThe authors report a five-year-old girl with Tuberous Sclerosis Complex who was initiated on vigabatrin at seven weeks old to treat infantile spasms and on everolimus at two months of age to treat a subependymal giant cell astrocytoma. Neuropsychological assessments were conducted in this patient at 12, 24, and 36 months using the Bayley-III and Vineland-II.ResultsOn the Bayley-III, the patient had a high average cognitive composite score at 12 months and a high average language composite score at 36 months. Bayley-III assessments at 12, 24, and 36 months, yielded average cognitive, language, and motor composite scores. On the Vineland-II at 12 months, her standard score fell in the moderately low range on daily living skills and overall adaptive behavior. Vineland-II assessments at 12, 24, and 36 months yielded average range standard scores in the areas of communication, socialization, daily living skills, overall adaptive behavior, and motor skills.DiscussionThis case highlights early neurodevelopmental outcomes in one TSC child treated with everolimus and vigabatrin in infancy. Continued assessment and longitudinal follow-up is required to understand the broader implications of early treatment with mTOR inhibitors and vigabatrin in TSC patients in childhood.

Background

Tuberous Sclerosis Complex (TSC) is a genetic disorder that affects multiple organ systems, displaying neurological manifestations such as epilepsy, neurodevelopmental disorders, and subependymal giant cell astrocytomas (SEGAs). TSC is caused by variants in either the TSC1 or TSC2 genes, leading to hyperactivation of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway. Everolimus, an mTOR inhibitor, is FDA-approved for the treatment of SEGAs and as an adjuvant therapy for TSC-associated partial-onset seizures. Vigabatrin is FDA-approved for the treatment of partial seizures and infantile spasms in individuals with TSC. This case report examines the neurodevelopmental outcomes of a child with TSC who received early treatment with both everolimus and vigabatrin.

Methods

The child described in this case report was enrolled in a clinical study (Neuropsychological Testing in Tuberous Sclerosis Patients on Everolimus, not funded) approved by the local Institutional Review Board. Neuropsychological assessments were conducted in this patient at 12, 24, and 36 months using the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scale, Second Edition and Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, Third Edition [1, 2]. The Bayley-III is a valid and reliable developmental test for ages 1 month to 42 months. The Vineland-II is an adaptive skills measure that is valid and reliable from birth to 90 years of age. For both tests, the composite score is a mean of 100, and one standard deviation is defined as 15. Together, these tests evaluate the patient’s cognition, language and communication, daily living skills, motor skills, socialization, and overall adaptive behavior and have been used in TSC research trials.

Case presentation

The patient is a five-year-old girl diagnosed with TSC at birth due to prenatal detection of cardiac rhabdomyomas. Genetic testing confirmed a de novo pathogenic variant in the TSC2 gene. There is no known family history of TSC, epilepsy, developmental delay, or autism spectrum disorder.

Timeline

The child’s EEG was normal at two weeks of age. She was seen by ophthalmology at 1 month of age and found to have a normal dilated eye exam and mild hyperopia on exam, but considered to be normal for age. She presented for infantile spasms and was started on vigabatrin at 7 weeks of age, titrating to a dose of 150 mg/kg/day, with cessation of infantile spasms. Additionally, her brain MRI at 2 months of age revealed cortical tubers, subependymal nodules, and a large mass near the left foramen of Monro consistent with a sub-ependymal giant cell astrocytoma (SEGA). She initiated everolimus 2 mg every other day at 2 months of age. At 5 months of age, a brain MRI demonstrated findings of T2-weighted hyperintensities in the bilateral basal ganglia and thalamus suggestive of vigabatrin-associated brain abnormalities prompting withdrawal of vigabatrin. She was initiated on levetiracetam for seizure control. The child remained seizure-free and was successfully weaned from levetiracetam at 24 months. Serial follow-up neuroimaging has demonstrated decrease in the size of the SEGA. She continues to take 5 mg of everolimus for indefinite therapy for the SEGA and tolerates everolimus well.

Clinical findings

On clinical examination at five years of age, the child was alert, interactive, and spoke in full sentences. She had intact cranial nerves on exam including her pupils were reactive to light and her extraocular movements were intact. Her motor examination was normal without focal neurological deficits. She had independent ambulation.

Diagnostic assessment

Serial neuropsychological testing was performed in this child at 12 months, 24 months and 36 months to assess cognitive, language and communication, motor skills, daily living skills, socialization, and overall adaptive behavior through the Vineland-II [1] and Bayley-III scales [2].Specifically on the Bayley-III at 12 months, she had a high average cognitive composite score and at 36 months she had a high average language composite score. On all other Bayley-III assessments at 12 months, 24 months, and 36 months, she had average cognitive, language, and motor composite scores (Table 1). On the Vineland-II at 12 months, she had moderately low range standard scores on daily living skills and overall adaptive behavior. Although on the Vineland-II, the patient showed mild decrease in the standard score at 12 months (standard score of 90) compared to 24 months and 36 months (standard score of 88), the child’s standard score remained in the average range for motor development. On all other Vineland-II assessments at 12 months, 24 months, and 36 months she had average range standard scores on communication, socialization, daily living skills, overall adaptive behavior, and motor skills (Table 2).

Discussion

Neurodevelopmental testing

TSC-Associated Neuropsychiatric Disorders (TAND) encompasses a broad spectrum of neuropsychiatric manifestations, categorized into six clusters: scholastic difficulties, hyperactivity/impulsivity, mood/anxiety disorders, neuropsychological deficits, dysregulated behavior, and autism spectrum disorder (ASD)-like symptoms [3]. ASD is present in 40–50% of individuals with TSC [4]. TSC2 pathogenic variants have been described in the literature to have a more severe phenotypical presentation [4]. Additionally, individuals with TSC are at increased risk of intellectual disability. The TOSCA study, which assessed individuals with TSC, reported that 44.4% of individuals had IQs within the normal range, while 28.1% had mild intellectual disability and 15.1% had moderate intellectual disability [5].

Everolimus

Everolimus functions by inhibiting the overactive mTOR pathway in TSC, thereby reducing abnormal cellular growth. It received FDA approval in 2010 for the treatment of SEGAs in TSC and in 2018 for the treatment of epilepsy in TSC. The safety of everolimus in TSC patients has been assessed in multiple studies. Saffari et al. examined 17 children with a median starting age of five months and found that while adverse events occurred, SEGA size and seizure frequency decreased, suggesting a favorable safety profile in young TSC patients [6].

The EXIST-3 trial demonstrated a significant reduction in seizure frequency among TSC patients treated with everolimus compared to placebo. Despite its efficacy in reducing seizure burden, randomized controlled trials have not demonstrated improvements in cognitive function or ASD-related symptoms with everolimus treatment. Overwater et al. conducted a 12-month double-blind, placebo-controlled study in children aged 4–17 years and found no significant cognitive or behavioral benefits [7]. Similarly, Krueger et al. evaluated patients aged 6–21 years in a placebo-controlled trial and reported no neurocognitive improvements over six months [8, 9].

The TSC-STEPS trial, an ongoing Phase II randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study, is evaluating the use of sirolimus, another mTOR inhibitor, in infants younger than six months to determine its efficacy in seizure prevention and neurodevelopmental outcomes [10].

Vigabatrin

Epilepsy is highly prevalent in TSC, affecting approximately 83.6% of individuals. Infantile spasms, occurring in 38.9% of TSC patients, typically emerge between 3 and 8 months of age and are characterized by sudden flexor or extensor muscle contractions [11]. Patients with TSC that have infantile spasms may not always exhibit hypsarrhythmia on EEG. Early recognition and treatment of infantile spasms is crucial, as studies have demonstrated better cognitive outcomes in TSC patients whose spasms are effectively managed with vigabatrin [12].

Vigabatrin acts by irreversibly inhibiting the enzyme responsible for GABA breakdown and was FDA-approved for infantile spasms in 2009. It has been shown to be more effective than steroids in treating TSC-related spasms [13]. Despite its benefits, vigabatrin is associated with potential adverse effects, including visual field changes which continues to be researched. A study by Van der Prost Clement et al. evaluated 21 TSC patients on vigabatrin and found no retinal toxicity in 14 individuals assessed via ophthalmologic examination [14].

The PREVeNT trial, a multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled study, enrolled TSC infants younger than six months to evaluate the effects of vigabatrin treatment prior to onset of spasms. Those exhibiting abnormal epileptiform findings on serial EEGs were randomized to vigabatrin or placebo. The primary outcomes included epilepsy onset and neurodevelopmental performance at 24 months. Although no significant differences were found in cognitive outcomes between treatment groups, early intervention remains a key strategy in TSC management [15].

This single case report describes periodic neuropsychological testing in a child with TSC2 with infantile spasms and a SEGA treated in infancy. The patient developed infantile spasms and was successfully treated with vigabatrin. Her SEGA has been treated with everolimus. It is not possible to conclude from this single case study if early treatment of her SEGA with an mTOR inhibitor and use of vigabatrin for infantile spasms led to her typical development. However, it is interesting to note that this patient had multiple risk factors for poor neurological outcome including both the TSC2 genotype and early onset of infantile spasms. This case calls attention to the need for further evaluation of early use of mTOR inhibitors on neurodevelopmental outcomes in TSC individuals. Additionally, this case also highlights the role of evaluating early use of dual therapy (vigabatrin and mTOR inhibitors) on neurodevelopmental outcomes in TSC patients.

Conclusion

This case highlights the neurodevelopmental outcomes of one child with Tuberous Sclerosis Complex who received early treatment with a mTOR inhibitor (everolimus) for a SEGA and vigabatrin therapy for infantile spasms in infancy. This child had age-appropriate neurodevelopmental outcomes at 36 months. Further research is needed to assess the long-term neurodevelopmental impact of early use of vigabatrin and mTOR inhibitors in individuals with TSC.
