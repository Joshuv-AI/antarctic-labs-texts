Muscle weakness, greater fat mass, lower hematocrit levels, and advanced age in a diagnostic prediction model of periodontitis in adults with obesity: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Karina Sarno Paes Alves Dias, Virgílio Santana-Júnior, Luciana Mara Barbosa Pereira, Felipe Oliveira Bittencourt, Hérika Maria Silveira Ruas, Gefter Thiago Batista Correa, Stênio Fernando Pimentel Duarte, Juciane Fagundes Durães Benitez, Renato Sobral Monteiro Junior, Sérgio Henrique Sousa Santos, Desirée Sant’Ana Haikal, Alfredo Maurício Batista de Paula

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeriodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease that shares key biological pathways with obesity, particularly low-grade systemic inflammation and immune dysregulation. This study aimed to develop and internally validate a diagnostic prediction model for periodontitis in adults with obesity using routinely available clinical and laboratory parameters.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 115 Brazilian adults with obesity (body mass index ≥ 30 kg/m2) receiving care in public ambulatory health services. Data collection included demographic characteristics, anthropometric and body-composition measures, handgrip strength (HGS), hematological and biochemical parameters, oral-health behaviors and status, and a comprehensive periodontal examination. Periodontitis was diagnosed using clinical and radiographic criteria consistent with the 2017 World Workshop framework. Candidate independent predictors were pre-specified based on biological plausibility and feasibility in public primary health care settings. A pre-specified forced-entry multivariable logistic regression model was developed with a maximum of four predictors and internally validated using 1,000 bootstrap resamples.ResultsPeriodontitis was diagnosed in 71.3% of participants (82/115). The final model retained age (adjusted OR = 1.13 per year; 95%CI = 1.08–1.20), fat mass (adjusted OR = 1.04 per kg; 95%CI = 0.99–1.09), hematocrit (adjusted OR = 0.89 per percentage point; 95%CI = 0.76–1.02), and reduced HGS (adjusted OR = 5.22; 95%CI = 1.80–17.30) as independent predictors. Apparent discrimination was excellent (AUC = 0.873; 95%CI = 0.807–0.938), with an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.865. Calibration was acceptable (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.749), and overall accuracy was 80.0%, with sensitivity of 76.8% and specificity of 87.9%. A LASSO sensitivity analysis confirmed predictor robustness. A simplified exploratory screening score and a provisional nomogram were derived to improve interpretability.ConclusionsA parsimonious diagnostic prediction model identified age, fat mass, hematocrit, and HGS as independent predictors of prevalent periodontitis in adults with obesity. The model should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. It may inform future risk-stratification strategies in public primary health care settings, but external validation and recalibration are required before clinical implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08601-2.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a multifactorial, chronic, biofilm-associated inflammatory disease characterized by dysbiosis of the subgingival microbiota in susceptible individuals, progressive destruction of the tooth-supporting tissues, and, in advanced stages, tooth loss [1, 2]. Its pathogenesis involves interactions between periodontal pathogens and the host response, modulated by genetic and behavioral factors such as smoking and inadequate oral hygiene [3, 4]. Clinically, periodontitis is characterized by increased probing depth, bleeding on probing, and clinical attachment loss [2]. Beyond its local effects, periodontitis has systemic relevance because microbial products and inflammatory mediators may disseminate into the circulation and contribute to low-grade inflammation [5]. Observational studies and systematic reviews have reported associations between periodontitis and multiple comorbidities, particularly cardiometabolic disorders [6–8].

Obesity is a highly prevalent chronic disease associated with important metabolic, immunological, and inflammatory disturbances [9]. In addition to increasing the risk of multiple chronic diseases and being associated with higher all-cause mortality, obesity imposes a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems [10, 11]. Excess adiposity promotes chronic low-grade systemic inflammation through adipokines, cytokines, and immune-cell activation, thereby shaping susceptibility to several chronic non-communicable diseases [12].

A growing body of evidence suggests that obesity and periodontitis are biologically linked [13, 14]. Despite this background, clinically pragmatic diagnostic prediction tools capable of identifying adults with obesity who are more likely to have periodontitis remain scarce. Prediction models based on routinely obtainable variables may be useful to prioritize periodontal assessment in resource-constrained health care settings.

The present study therefore aimed to develop and internally validate a parsimonious diagnostic prediction model for prevalent periodontitis among adults with obesity using demographic, body-composition, oral health status and health behaviors, functional, and laboratory measures that are potentially available in public primary health care settings.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This single-center observational cross-sectional study was conducted between April 2022 and December 2024 in public ambulatory health care services with oral-health teams in Vitória da Conquista, Bahia, Brazil. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the TRIPOD statement for the development of a diagnostic prediction model [15] (Table 1S. Material Supplementary). All procedures were approved by the relevant ethics committees under CAAE No. 50251721.0.0000.5578 (Process No. 4.931.111), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Participants

Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older, of either sex, with obesity defined as BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2, who were physically independent and had preserved cognitive and functional abilities at enrolment. Recruitment was prospective and consecutive, using a pragmatic non-probability strategy that included all eligible adults who attended the participating services during the study period and agreed to participate. Exclusion criteria were acute local or systemic inflammatory conditions at assessment, untreated comorbidities likely to impair physical performance, medications with major effects on body composition, relevant orthopedic or locomotor limitations, recent oncological treatment, or diagnosed neurological disease.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was prevalent periodontitis, diagnosed using clinical and radiographic criteria aligned with the 2017 World Workshop case definition [16]. A case was defined as interdental clinical attachment loss at two or more non-adjacent teeth, or buccal/oral clinical attachment loss of at least 3 mm with probing depth greater than 3 mm at two or more teeth. Radiographic evidence of alveolar bone loss was used to support classification when appropriate. Examiners were trained and calibrated before fieldwork; inter-examiner agreement for key periodontal measures was high (kappa = 0.87). Variables used to define the outcome were not considered candidate predictors.

Predictors and data collection

Distinct trained teams collected demographic, anthropometric/body-composition, functional, periodontal diagnosis, oral health status and behaviors, and laboratory data to reduce measurement bias. Demographic and behavioral variables included age and sex, and tobacco and alcohol consumption. Anthropometric measurements were obtained using standardized and calibrated instruments [17]. Body composition was assessed using multi-frequency bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) (InBody120, ROK), estimating absolute fat mass—FM (kg), body fat percentage (%), and skeletal muscle mass—SMM (kg) [18]. Height was measured with a fixed stadiometer (Cescorf, BRA), and body weight with a calibrated electronic scale (HBF222‑T, Omron®, JPN). Obesity was defined as BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 according to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria. Handgrip strength (HGS) was measured with a hydraulic dynamometer, and reduced HGS was operationalized using a sample-based threshold (values below the 50th percentile), reflecting relative functional weakness within the adult study population rather than a clinical diagnosis of sarcopenia [19]. Hematological and biochemical variables included red blood cell count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, total leukocytes, fasting glucose, total cholesterol, triglycerides, albumin, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (CRP). hs‑CRP values ≥ 10 mg/L were interpreted as suggestive of acute inflammation and analyzed separately [20–22]. Oral-health behaviors included toothbrushing frequency and dental-floss use. The oral examination was performed by previously trained and calibrated dentists, following standard clinical procedures [23]. The number of remaining, decayed, and missing natural teeth was recorded to characterize participants’ dental status. Periodontal status was evaluated using comprehensive clinical and radiographic assessments. All remaining natural teeth were examined at six sites per tooth, with standardized recording of key periodontal parameters, including probing depth, clinical attachment level, bleeding on probing, visible biofilm, gingival recession, and tooth mobility. Periapical radiographs were obtained and independently interpreted by calibrated examiners blinded to predictor data, to support diagnostic classification and the evaluation of alveolar bone loss.

Statistical analysis and model development

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median and interquartile range, as appropriate, and categorical variables as absolute and relative frequencies. Candidate predictors were pre-specified according to biological plausibility, prior evidence, and routine feasibility in primary care rather than selected solely based on univariable statistical significance. Missingness was restricted exclusively to biological variables; anthropometric, body-composition, and demographic variables had no missing data (0%). Among laboratory parameters, most hematological and biochemical variables had one missing observation each (0.9%; n = 1/115), attributable to hemolyzed or insufficient samples. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein had two missing values (1.7%; n = 2/115) and fasting glucose had four missing values (3.5%; n = 4/115); the latter was not included in the final model. Little's MCAR test applied to biological variables indicated data were not missing completely at random (χ2 = 100.78, df = 73, p = 0.017); accordingly, a missing-at-random assumption was adopted, and expectation–maximization imputation was applied to affected laboratory variables prior to modelling. A multivariable logistic regression model was developed using a pre-specified forced-entry approach, with all four candidate predictors entered simultaneously into the model regardless of individual statistical significance. This strategy was adopted in alignment with current recommendations for diagnostic prediction modelling, which prioritize clinical and biological pre-specification over data-driven variable selection. Model complexity was constrained a priori to a maximum of four predictors based on events-per-variable considerations. Multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factors (all VIF < 1.4). Model performance was evaluated by discrimination (AUC-ROC with 95% CI via DeLong method), calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and calibration plot), Nagelkerke R2, and classification metrics at the optimal probability threshold identified by the Youden Index. Internal validation used 1,000 bootstrap resamples to estimate optimism and assess coefficient stability. As a pre-planned sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of the pre-specified predictor set, a LASSO-penalized logistic regression model was fitted using tenfold cross-validation optimizing discriminative performance, with the regularization parameter selected at the minimum cross-validated loss (λ = 0.0049). All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.5.1 [24–27].

As a pre-planned sensitivity analysis, a LASSO-penalized logistic regression model was fitted with tenfold cross-validation (λ = 0.0049). All four pre-specified predictors were retained without zero-shrinkage, with coefficients directionally consistent with the forced-entry model: age (β = 0.112), fat mass (β = 0.030), hematocrit (β = − 0.103), and reduced HGS (β = 1.439). The LASSO model achieved an AUC of 0.875, essentially identical to the forced-entry model (AUC = 0.873), confirming that the pre-specified predictor set is robust and not an artefact of the modelling procedure.

To facilitate individualized risk estimation, a nomogram was constructed based on the final multivariable logistic regression model (Fig. 4). The nomogram incorporates age, FM, hematocrit, and reduced HGS, assigning a weighted point value to each predictor according to its relative contribution to the model. The sum of these values yields a total score, which corresponds to the predicted probability of prevalent periodontitis. The graphical representation demonstrates coherent scaling across predictors, consistent with their relative regression coefficients: age and reduced handgrip strength dominate the nomogram score, reflecting their higher absolute contribution to the predicted log-odds, followed by hematocrit and fat mass. Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of adults with obesity receiving care in public ambulatory services, prevalent periodontitis was common and a parsimonious diagnostic prediction model identified age, FM, hematocrit, and reduced HGS as independent predictors of disease probability. Taken together, the findings reinforce the view that periodontitis in obesity may reflect multisystem dysregulation rather than an isolated oral condition. Collectively, these results support the concept that periodontal health is closely intertwined with systemic health and reinforce the need for integrated approaches to the management of obesity and related systemic chronic diseases [28–30].

The association between older age and periodontitis is consistent with epidemiological evidence showing that cumulative exposure to biofilm, chronic inflammation, and systemic or behavioral risk factors contributes to greater periodontal burden across adulthood [31–33]. Within a population composed entirely of adults with obesity, age likely acts as a proxy for cumulative biological and clinical susceptibility. Regarding periodontitis, this pattern has been attributed to cumulative periodontal damage over time, resulting from prolonged exposure to dental biofilm, chronic inflammation, and multiple systemic and behavioral risk factors [28, 34].

Greater FM remained independently associated with periodontitis after adjustment. Excess adiposity is widely recognized as a state of chronic low-grade systemic inflammation characterized by sustained secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and adipokines, all of which may intensify periodontal vulnerability [9, 12, 35, 36]. Adiposity-related inflammation and metabolic dysfunction have been implicated in increased susceptibility to periodontitis and worse periodontal outcomes [13, 14, 37–40]. Our findings suggest that the degree of adiposity may confer an incremental inflammatory burden that exacerbates periodontal vulnerability. Nevertheless, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Importantly, the use of FM rather than BMI alone provides a more specific representation of adiposity burden in this population.

Chronic low-grade systemic inflammation—commonly observed in both obesity and periodontitis—has been shown to disrupt erythropoiesis, impair iron homeostasis, and alter micronutrient metabolism, thus contributing to subclinical hematological changes consistent with stages preceding the clinical manifestation of anemia [41, 42]. In the present study, lower hematocrit was independently associated with prevalent periodontitis, but the present data do not support a diagnosis of anemia. Instead, the result should be interpreted as a subtle hematological difference that may reflect inflammation-related alterations in erythropoiesis or iron handling. Because average hematocrit values remained within broadly normal ranges, overinterpretation should be avoided.

Obesity has been consistently linked to impaired muscle quality, reduced relative strength, and the development of sarcopenia or sarcopenic obesity, particularly in older individuals [43, 44]. Reduced HGS was the strongest functional status predictor retained in the model. This finding should be interpreted cautiously. HGS is best viewed here as a marker of overall functional reserve and systemic health rather than as a direct causal determinant of periodontal disease. Both reduced SMS and periodontitis may arise from shared pathways involving chronic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation. The relatively small number of participants with reduced HGS in the non-periodontitis group resulted in a wide confidence interval, underscoring limited statistical precision. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted with caution and require confirmation in larger, adequately powered prospective studies. From a clinical perspective, the association between muscle weakness and periodontitis in adults with obesity suggests that functional indicators such as HGS may provide complementary information for risk stratification in this population [19, 44–46].

Several methodological considerations warrant emphasis. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal or temporal inference; the model estimates the probability of existing disease, not future incidence. Second, the modest sample size, despite the pre-specified inclusion of only four clinically grounded predictors, may have limited the precision of coefficient estimates [24, 25]. A formal sample size calculation was not performed prior to data collection. Post-hoc adequacy assessment was performed using the sample size framework [25] and, based on the observed C-statistic (0.873), four predictors, and outcome prevalence of 71.3%, indicated that criteria for overfitting control (minimum N = 84) and intercept precision (minimum N = 92) were both met by the analytical sample of 115 participants. However, the criterion for C-statistic precision estimation would require N = 315, reinforcing the need for external validation. Third, reduced HGS was defined with a sample-based threshold rather than consensus sarcopenia criteria, which limits comparability with other studies and may influence effect estimates. Fourth, external validation was not performed. Accordingly, model performance may be optimistic and generalizability to independent populations remains unknown. Fifth, participants were recruited from public primary health care settings and may differ from the broader population of adults with obesity in terms of socioeconomic background, comorbidity burden, and health-care utilization. These features may introduce selection bias and limit extrapolation to other settings. Finally, the simplified score and the provisional nomogram are exploratory implementation tools and should not be interpreted as ready-for-use clinical decision aids.

Even with these limitations, the model has practical value as an initial framework for integrated oral-systemic screening research. It relies on variables that are conceptually accessible in multidisciplinary obesity care and suggests that future implementation studies may be able to combine anthropometric, functional, and hematological information to prioritize referral for periodontal evaluation. Larger external datasets, graphical calibration assessment, and recalibration will be essential next steps.

Conclusion

This study developed a parsimonious diagnostic prediction model for prevalent periodontitis in adults with obesity using age, FM, hematocrit, and HGS parameters. The model showed encouraging apparent performance after internal bootstrap validation, but it should be regarded as exploratory. External validation, calibration refinement, and implementation studies are required before the model, its simplified score, or its nomogram can be recommended for routine clinical use.
