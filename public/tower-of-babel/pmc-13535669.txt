Physiologically Based Pharmacokinetic Modeling to Predict Human Starting Dose and Pharmacokinetics for Rocbrutinib
Authors: Lu Wang, Dandan Yang, Rong Shao, Jinliang Chen, Xiaodan Wang, Zheng Wang, Bo Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRocbrutinib is a fourth‐generation Bruton's tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitor that covalently binds wild‐type BTK and non‐covalently engages the C481S mutant. Its pharmacokinetic (PK) characteristics in healthy Chinese subjects remain unclear. This study aimed to support maximum recommended starting dose (MRSD) selection and predict exposure of rocbrutinib in healthy Chinese subjects via an integrated model‐informed strategy. We determined key extrapolation parameters and preclinical PK in CD‐1 mice and beagle dogs. Three approaches were employed to determine the MRSD: The no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) dose method based on body surface area, the NOAEL exposure method based on a physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) model, and the minimum effective dose method based on a PBPK model. The PBPK model was developed and validated using preclinical and clinical PK data. Predicted MRSD values by the NOAEL dose method, the NOAEL exposure method, and the minimum effective dose method were 48.7, 9.8, and 10.5 mg, respectively. Considering the lowest predicted value and available tablet strength, a starting dose of 12.5 mg was selected. Predicted plasma concentration‐time profiles were consistent with those observed in animals and humans. The fold error for Cmax and AUC fell within the 0.5–2.0 range. The model further predicted brain tissue exposure across species. Moreover, the predicted brain BTK occupancy at 12.5 mg was approximately 37% for wild‐type BTK and 6% for C481S mutant BTK. The PBPK model serves as a valuable tool for dose selection, PK prediction, and CNS target engagement evaluation, supporting the clinical development of rocbrutinib.Keywords: dose selection, human PK simulation, PBPK model, rocbrutinib

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) and neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders (NMOSD) are chronic inflammatory diseases of the CNS characterized by demyelination and neurological morbidity [1]. Current therapeutic approaches aim to reduce the frequency of relapses and delay disease progression. However, despite treatment with current medications, some MS patients still experience progressive neurologic deterioration, characterized most commonly by impaired ambulation, loss of bladder control, and slowed cognitive processing [1]. Furthermore, the treatment landscape for NMOSD has been challenging, with a longstanding absence of effective therapies specifically targeting its distinct pathophysiology. Bruton's tyrosine kinase (BTK), a member of the Tec family of kinases, mediates signaling through multiple cell surface receptors in B cells and myeloid cells. Accumulating evidence highlights the pivotal role of BTK in the pathological course of both MS and NMOSD. Notably, BTK inhibitors can cross the blood–brain barrier and exert modulatory effects on the innate immune system, thereby targeting compartmentalized inflammation within the CNS [2]. BTK inhibitors have demonstrated impressive efficacy in relapsing MS and NMOSD [3, 4]. Currently, several BTK inhibitors are in clinical trials for MS, though none have yet received regulatory approval [5].

Rocbrutinib (code name “LP‐168”), developed by Guangzhou Lupeng Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd. is a highly selective fourth‐generation BTK inhibitor. It can bind wild‐type BTK covalently and engage C481‐mutant variants through non‐covalent interactions [6]. Rocbrutinib demonstrated favorable tolerability following both single and repeated administration in mice and beagle dogs. Preclinical pharmacokinetic studies showed that rocbrutinib exhibits high plasma protein binding across species. Rocbrutinib was distributed rapidly in various tissues. The distribution of rocbrutinib was also observed in the brain tissue. Metabolism studies identified cytochrome P450 (CYP) 3A as the primary enzyme responsible for its clearance. Together, these preclinical studies suggested that rocbrutinib is a promising candidate for MS and NMOSD clinical therapy.

Establishing the maximum recommended starting dose (MRSD) for first‐in‐human (FIH) trials is a crucial step for a new drug to transition from preclinical studies to clinical development. Regulatory agencies including the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), European Medicines Agency (EMA), and China's National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) have issued relevant guidelines [7, 8, 9]. These guidelines recommending the use of the no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) and the minimal anticipated biological effect dose (MABEL) for MRSD estimation. However, the NOAEL‐based approach relies heavily on safety factor (SF) and may lead to overestimation due to its dependence on toxicological study endpoints. A physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) model is a mathematical model that integrates species‐specific physiologically parameters with drug‐dependent parameters [10]. The PBPK model in GastroPlus software consists of fourteen tissue compartments connected by venous and arterial blood circulation, representing major organs relevant to drug disposition. The PBPK model facilitates the translation of in vitro data into reliable predictions of drug exposure in both animals and humans [11]. It has been proven to be a powerful tool in early drug development [12, 13] and operates through an iterative “predict, learn, and confirm” cycle [14, 15]. Nevertheless, model development remains challenging, largely due to the substantial number of required parameters and the frequent scarcity of observed in vivo data.

This study integrates in vitro and in vivo data using traditional methods combined with PBPK modeling to determine the human starting dose of rocbrutinib and further applies the model to predict pharmacokinetic profiles across clinical dose ranges. These findings provide a scientific framework and innovative methodology for optimizing dose selection strategies and advancing clinical development of rocbrutinib.

Methods

Reagents and Materials

Rocbrutinib was synthesized by Guangzhou Lupeng Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd. The PBPK model was developed using GastroPlus software (Version 9.9, Simulations Plus Inc., USA). Drug physicochemical and biopharmaceutical parameters based on the chemical structure were predicted by ADMET predictor (Version 10, Simulations Plus Inc., USA).

MRSD for Rocbrutinib in Phase I Clinical Trial

The prediction of MRSD in Phase I clinical trial for healthy subjects was performed through the following methods:

NOAEL Dose Method Based on Body Surface Area

The NOAEL dose of tested animal species was first identified from repeated‐dose toxicity studies. In 28‐day repeated‐dose toxicity studies in mice, doses of 30 mg/kg, 100 mg/kg, and 300 mg/kg were evaluated. In 28‐day repeated‐dose toxicity studies in dogs, doses of 10 mg/kg, 30 mg/kg, and 100 mg/kg were evaluated. No death and no adverse reaction dose was determined to be 100 mg/kg for mice and NOAEL dose was estimated to be 30 mg/kg for dogs. We assumed that the dose‐toxicity relationship is similar among mice, beagle dogs, and humans. In addition, NOAEL doses were then converted to human equivalent doses (HED) based on body surface area. NOAEL Exposure Method Based on the PBPK Model

The area under the concentration‐time curve (AUC) under NOAEL dose was determined for each animal species. In 28‐day repeated‐dose toxicity studies in mice, systemic exposure AUC under the NOAEL dose was 81,400 ng·h/mL for males and 69,200 ng·h/mL for females. Therefore, mean AUC under the NOAEL dose was 75,300 ng·h/mL. In 28‐day repeated‐dose toxicity studies in dogs, AUC under the NOAEL dose was 38,300 ng·h/mL for males and 38,400 ng·h/mL for females. Therefore, mean AUC under the NOAEL dose was 38,350 ng·h/mL. We assumed that the dose‐exposure‐toxicity relationship of drug is similar among mice, beagle dogs, and humans. MRSD was extrapolated from animal exposure using the following steps: (1) animal NOAEI AUC was first corrected by the ratio of fraction unbound in plasma (fup) in animal versus human; (2) the human PBPK model was then used to predict the HED required to achieve this target exposure; (3) the MRSD was calculated by dividing the predicted human dose by a SF. A default SF of 10 was applied.

Minimum Effective Dose Method Based on the PBPK Model

In experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) mice models, oral administration of rocbrutinib at the minimum effective dose (MED) of 3 mg/kg (once daily for 19 days) improved EAE scores. We assumed that the pharmacokinetics (PK)/pharmacokinetics (PD) relationship related to efficacy is similar between mice and humans. The human minimum effective dose was determined using the following steps: (1) the exposure corresponding to the MED was first determined using the mouse PBPK model; (2) the AUC was then corrected by the ratio of fup in mouse versus human; (3) a human PBPK model was subsequently applied to predict the human dose required to achieve this target exposure.

Establishment and Validation of PBPK Model

For mouse and beagle dog PBPK models, parameters listed in Table S1 were applied. The observed intravenous CL for each species was input into the GastroPlus software as hepatic clearance (drug elimination was assumed to occur predominantly via the liver). For the model, Vss cannot be entered directly as a numerical value, and the in vivo Vss observed after intravenous administration was similar to the Vss calculated by the customized Kp method; therefore, the customized Kp‐derived Vss was used in the model. The developed mouse and dog PBPK models were further validated using in vivo PK data. The validation datasets included single intravenous bolus (1 mg/kg) and oral (30 mg/kg) administration in mice, and single intravenous bolus (1 mg/kg) and oral (10 mg/kg) administration in beagle dogs. For animal in vivo studies, plasma drug concentrations were quantified using a liquid chromatography/tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis method. The LC system was from Shimadzu, and the mass spectrometer was a triple quadrupole instrument from AB Sciex, which was equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operating in positive ion mode. Sample preparation was performed using protein precipitation. For beagle dogs, the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) was 20.0 ng/mL and the upper limit of quantification (ULOQ) was 6000 ng/mL. For mice, the LLOQ was 0.5 ng/mL and the ULOQ was 5000 ng/mL. All in vivo PK parameters were calculated using Phoenix WinNonlin software (version 8.4; Pharsight Corporation) and are presented in Table S6. For the PBPK modeling, the observed PK parameters were directly analyzed using the built‐in PK analysis module of GastroPlus.

The human PBPK model under fasting condition was extrapolated from the animal PBPK model by integrating species‐specific parameters while maintaining structural consistency. The parameters of Rbp and fup were changed from animal values to corresponding human values. The CL value of 2.44 L/h derived from the corrected IVIVE approach was input into the model, and the Vss value of 31.40 L was automatically computed by the software via the customized Rodgers & Rowland method. Moreover, the human PBPK model in software incorporates different ethnic populations, including Chinese, American, and Japanese. These populations can be stratified by demographic parameters including sex, health status, age, body weight, and body mass index (BMI). Each population group is equipped with corresponding physiological data, including organ volumes and blood flow rates. For our human model, we selected the Chinese healthy male population with an age of 30 years and a body weight of 60 kg (BMI = 20.52 kg/m2). The finalized Chinese PBPK model was validated using clinical PK data following oral administration of rocbrutinib at doses of 12.5 mg, 25 mg, 50 mg, and 75 mg.

Clinical Study

The clinical studies were performed at the Center of Clinical Pharmacology, the Second Affiliated Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Ethics No.: (2022), LSYD No. (002)).

In this study, male and female healthy subjects were both eligible for the studies. All subjects were willing to give written informed consent. Subjects were considered healthy by the principal investigator based on a detailed medical history, vital signs, full physical examination, and clinical laboratory test. Subjects were excluded for the following reasons: QTcF ≥ 450 ms or abnormal ECG or myocardial zymogram results; positive blood screen for hepatitis B virus, hepatitis C virus, human immunodeficiency virus, and syphilis tests; any other systemic medications within 2 weeks before administration; any diet that may affect the expression or function of enzymes within 1 week before administration; any food or juice containing caffeine, tea, alcohol, or rich in xanthines within 24 h before drug administration; history of alcoholism or drug abuse; donation or blood loss of more than 450 mL within 3 months before screening; surgery history within 6 months before screening; female subjects who have a positive pregnancy test result or are breastfeeding.

The study was a randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled Phase I study in healthy Chinese subjects. In the study, four dose cohorts were set up, including Cohort 1 (12.5 mg), Cohort 2 (25 mg), Cohort 3 (50 mg), and Cohort 4 (75 mg). In each cohort, drug dosing started with two sentinel subjects. After no serious adverse reactions or ≥Grade 2 adverse reactions occurred within 48 h of administration, the other eight subjects could be randomized in a 3:1 ratio to receive a single dose of rocbrutinib or placebo. The decision to proceed to each higher‐dose cohort was based on a review of safety and tolerability data collected for 4 days after administration. Each dose of rocbrutinib was taken orally with 240 mL water after a minimum 10‐h fast. Regular standardized meals were provided 4 h after drug administration. Water was allowed as desired, except for 1 h before and 2 h after drug administration. Blood samples (3 mL) were collected within 1 h before drug administration, and 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 24, 48, and 72 h after administration. The blood samples were centrifuged using a refrigerated centrifuge to separate the plasma. The plasma samples were quantified using LC–MS/MS analysis. The LC system was from shimadzu, and the mass spectrometer was a triple quadrupole instrument from AB Sciex, which was equipped with an ESI source operating in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. Sample preparation was performed using protein precipitation. The LLOQ was 5.0 ng/mL and the ULOQ was 5000 ng/mL. All PK parameters were calculated using Phoenix WinNonlin software (version 8.4; Pharsight Corporation) and are presented in Table S7. For the PBPK modeling, the observed PK parameters were directly analyzed using the built‐in PK analysis module of GastroPlus.

In this study, a total of 40 subjects were enrolled. The mean (range) age was 27 (19.43), body weight was 62.12 (8.67) kg, height was 166.07 (8.09) cm, and the body mass index (BMI) was 22.46 (1.93) kg/m2. All the adverse events (AEs) were presented in Table S8.

In parallel, a systemic exposure‐based PBPK model was also applied to calculate MRSD. As shown in Table 1, the human equivalent AUC was first estimated, and the PBPK model was subsequently used to simulate exposure levels across a range of different doses, thereby identifying the HED corresponding to the target AUC. Moreover, a minimum effective dose‐based PBPK model was used to calculate MRSD. Based on preclinical EAE studies, the minimum effective dose was 3 mg/kg. Using the mouse PBPK model, the AUC associated with this effective dose was calculated to be 5314 ng·h/mL. The corrected AUC by the ratio of fup was 4198 ng·h/mL. Simulation using the human PBPK model indicated that a dose of 10.5 mg would achieve this target exposure.

Integrating the findings from the three approaches, the lowest value (9.8 mg) among the starting doses was selected as the reference. Considering that the smallest available tablet strength is 12.5 mg per tablet, a starting dose of 12.5 mg was selected for the clinical trials in healthy Chinese subjects to ensure safety.

Optimization of the Human PBPK Model for Rocbrutinib

To improve the agreement between simulated and observed rocbrutinib PK parameters in humans, parameter sensitivity analysis (PSA) was conducted for key modeling parameters. These included Kp of various tissues, as well as other absorption‐related parameters such as intestinal permeability, reference solubility, particle radius, particle density, and precipitation time. As shown in Figure S1, the predicted Cmax of rocbrutinib was most sensitive to changes in the Kp value of adipose tissue. Among other model parameters, the predicted Cmax was most sensitive to changes in reference solubility and particle density, while the predicted Tmax was most sensitive to changes in reference solubility and intestinal permeability.

To increase the predicted Cmax and reduce the predicted Tmax, the Kp value of adipose tissue was reduced from 0.44 to 0.22, and the reference solubility was increased from 0.02 to 0.04 mg/mL with a change in Vss from 31.40 to 25.54 L. The optimized model was then simulated. As shown in Figure 3 and Table 3, simulations with the optimized model showed significantly improved agreement with observed plasma concentrations across the entire dose range. All FEs for Cmax, AUC0–t, and AUC0–∞ were within the range of 0.5–2.0, and all AFE values ranged from 0.77 to 0.94 (AFE < 2), confirming the predictive reliability of the optimized human PBPK model for rocbrutinib.

Prediction of Tissue Concentrations and Brain BTK Target Occupancy

Using the optimized PBPK model, the gastrointestinal absorption fraction of rocbrutinib in mice, dogs, and humans was simulated. As shown in Figure S2, the results indicated that rocbrutinib exhibited the highest drug absorption in the ascending colon for mice and dogs, and in the jejunum 1 and duodenum for humans, revealing interspecies differences in oral absorption.

In addition, brain tissue exposure (AUC) of rocbrutinib was predicted across mice, dogs, and humans. Results obtained from the PBPK model are shown in Figure 4. Based on the optimized human model, the predicted Cbrain of rocbrutinib at the 12.5 mg starting dose was 4.04 ng/mL. Using the plasma fup (0.012), Cu,brain was calculated as 0.0485 ng/mL, corresponding to a molar concentration of 0.064 nM. Applying the Hill equation with the in vitro wild‐type BTK IC50 of 0.11 nM, the estimated brain BTK occupancy at the 12.5 mg dose was approximately 37%. Using the C481S mutant BTK IC50 of 1.0 nM, the estimated occupancy was approximately 6%. These results indicate that the 12.5 mg starting dose of rocbrutinib achieves moderate BTK target occupancy in the brain.

Discussion

Rocbrutinib is a highly active and highly selective BTK inhibitor. Preclinical results have shown its therapeutic potential in relapsing autoimmune diseases. This study combined in vitro assays, animal experiments, and PBPK modeling to predict the MRSD and PK characteristics of rocbrutinib in healthy Chinese subjects. The developed PBPK models demonstrated predictive capability for rocbrutinib MRSD and exposure. The findings provide valuable insights for clinical trial design and dose selection, facilitating the clinical development of rocbrutinib.

Reliable estimation of MRSD is crucial and challenging in new drug development. A well‐estimated MRSD supports rational dose escalation and provides a reference for determining the initial escalation increment. Guidelines from the FDA, EMA, and NMPA recommend the use of NOAEL‐based and MABEL‐based approaches for first‐in‐human starting dose selection [7, 8, 9]. However, the conventional NOAEL approach relies entirely on the SF and involves uncertainty. Therefore, an integrated multi‐method strategy is warranted for robust prediction of the initial human dose. Previous studies have demonstrated the accuracy of modeling approaches for human dose prediction [19, 20, 21]. In our study, we compared three approaches: a NOAEL dose method based on body surface area, a NOAEL exposure method based on a PBPK model, and a MED method based on a PBPK model. Although the empirical NOAEL method requires relatively limited data and is relatively straightforward, it yielded substantially higher MRSD values than the two PBPK‐based strategies. In contrast, PBPK models integrated interspecies physiological differences, drug‐specific exposure parameters, and pharmacodynamic information, providing a more mechanism‐driven and accurate basis for dose prediction. In exposure‐based starting dose estimation, the choice between AUC and Cmax as the exposure metric for interspecies extrapolation depends on the toxicity and its relationship with the exposure parameter. Certain acute AEs, such as QTc interval prolongation, are typically driven by Cmax [22], whereas delayed or cumulative toxicities, such as hepatotoxicity, are more closely associated with AUC [23]. In the starting dose prediction stage of this study, we adopted the NOAEL systemic exposure‐based method recommended in the literature [24, 25], using AUC as the primary metric for interspecies extrapolation. During interspecies exposure extrapolation, AUC was corrected using the fup value. Furthermore, with reference to relevant literature on exposure‐based extrapolation [26], a human PBPK model was applied to predict the HED required to achieve the target exposure level in human. Notably, for highly protein‐bound drugs, minor deviations in fup can lead to large errors in predicted human exposure [27, 28]. In this study, we further applied a 10‐fold SF for dose reduction after estimating the human equivalent dose, which offset the errors in fup estimation to a certain extent. In future studies, multiple extrapolation methods will be used to further cross‐validate results.

Accurate prediction of Vss and CL is essential for interspecies translation. Allometric scaling, Øie‐Tozer, and modeling approaches are commonly used to predict human Vss [29, 30]. Kp values are key parameters for determining Vss in the PBPK model. To assess the robustness of the Vss values derived from our PBPK model, we compared the predicted Vss with that obtained by the AS method reported in a previous study [16]. The results showed good agreement between the two approaches, indirectly supporting the validity of our method. In our study, Kp values for several tissues, including lung, adipose, muscle, liver, spleen, heart, brain, and kidney, were measured in mice and assumed to be species‐independent. PSA results revealed that the predicted Cmax of rocbrutinib was most sensitive to adipose Kp. Rocbrutinib is a highly lipophilic compound. For such compounds, even small changes in adipose Kp can markedly alter Vss and systemic exposure. Traditional tissue‐composition‐based PBPK models may also overestimate tissue Kp values, particularly for adipose tissue, leading to inflated Vss predictions [31]. Moreover, human plasma contains a higher lipoprotein content than rodent plasma, resulting in lower adipose Kp values in humans [31]. Accordingly, applying mouse adipose Kp value may overestimate human Kp and Vss. Based on these considerations, we reduced the adipose Kp from 0.44 to 0.22, which decreased the estimated Vss from 31.40 L to 25.54 L. In addition to optimizing the adipose Kp, a minor adjustment to the reference solubility was also made. The optimized model showed excellent agreement with clinical observations.

Multiple methods have been established to predict CL based on the underlying elimination mechanisms [28, 29, 32, 33, 34]. For hepatically metabolized drugs, a microsome or hepatocyte‐based prediction method is preferred [12, 35, 36]. In our study, in vitro‐predicted CL in preclinical species was higher than that observed in vivo. The reason may involve several complex factors, such as lower protein concentration in the in vitro incubation system, restriction of hepatic clearance by excretion processes in vivo, and so on. We therefore corrected human CL using an animal‐derived IVIVE scaling factor, improving prediction accuracy.

Compared with traditional empirical PK models, the PBPK model offers several key advantages. First, it provides mechanism‐based predictions by integrating drug physicochemical properties with species‐specific physiology, enabling extrapolation from animal to human. Moreover, it allows quantitative tissue concentration prediction, which is difficult to achieve using conventional compartmental PK models. In the present study, we used the developed PBPK model to predict brain tissue concentrations, supporting the potential central nervous system exposure of rocbrutinib. These features establish PBPK modeling as a powerful tool in model‐informed drug development. Of note, some limitations still existed in this study. First, a data‐related limitation should be noted. Systematic AUC data were not collected in the EAE efficacy studies, which precluded formal PK/PD modeling to establish a quantitative exposure‐response relationship. Therefore, we relied on the PBPK modeling to estimate animal exposure and translate the minimum effective dose to human equivalent exposure. The MABEL calculation was based solely on the minimum effective dose (3 mg/kg) and its estimated exposure via PBPK modeling, which serves as a conservative input parameter rather than a fully quantitative exposure‐response relationship. Moreover, the model does not include detailed CNS sub‐compartments due to software limitations, although brain tissue distribution was predicted.

In summary, our study combined in vitro experiments, animal experiments, and PBPK modeling to predict the human dose and PK characteristics in healthy Chinese volunteers. The model also enabled quantitative prediction of intestinal absorption and CNS tissue exposure, supporting target engagement and clinical translation. These findings provide a rational and mechanistic framework for clinical trial design and dose optimization, facilitating the clinical development of rocbrutinib.
