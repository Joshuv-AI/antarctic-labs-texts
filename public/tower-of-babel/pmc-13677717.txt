Efficient Hamiltonian, structure and trace distance learning of Gaussian states
Authors: Marco Fanizza, Cambyse Rouzé, Daniel Stilck França
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
When learning descriptions of many-body quantum systems, assuming locality of interactions can dramatically reduce the required number of observations and computational time compared to unstructured approaches. In this work, we uncover an infinite-dimensional instance of this phenomenon, initiating the study of Hamiltonian learning for positive-temperature bosonic Gaussian states. We present sample- and computationally efficient protocols for inferring the parameters and graph structure of underlying quadratic Hamiltonians, provided that temperature, squeezing, displacement, and the interaction graph’s maximal degree are bounded. Notably, our protocol relies solely on heterodyne measurements, which are often experimentally feasible, and achieves a sample complexity that scales only logarithmically with the number of modes. Furthermore, our techniques allow for the learning of positive-temperature Gaussian states in trace distance with quadratic scaling in precision and polynomial scaling in the number of modes.

Introduction

Inferring parameters of quantum systems from measurement data is a fundamental task in quantum information science that has seen significant advancements in recent years1–15. Although it has been known for roughly a decade that complete tomography of general quantum states inevitably requires a sample complexity scaling exponentially in the system’s size16–18, recent works have shown that it is possible to obtain a lot of information of practical interest much more efficiently. This is exemplified by the framework of classical shadows19. Another fruitful approach to obtain efficient algorithms is to consider the learning of more restricted and physically motivated quantum states and evolutions, such as those generated by local Hamiltonians6,8–11 or their Gibbs states1–3,5,7,12, where the problem of inferring the parameters of the underlying Hamiltonian is broadly referred to as the Hamiltonian learning problem. This line of work culminated in sample- and computationally efficient algorithms under various assumptions. A sample-efficient algorithm for an arbitrary constant-temperature local quantum Hamiltonian was given by ref. 2; however, the sample complexity was still polynomial in system size and the postprocessing was only efficient under further assumptions on the model, as it required solving a maximum entropy optimization problem. This was followed by work of ref. 1, which focused on the high-temperature regime. Resorting to cluster expansion techniques, they were able to obtain optimal sample complexity bounds and efficient post-processing. More recently, ref. 3 gave Hamiltonian learning algorithms for general Hamiltonians with efficient sample and computational complexity based on sums-of-squares relaxations.

This program can be understood as a quantum version of the widely studied classical problem of learning a discrete graphical or Ising model20–26. For classical discrete graphical models it is known how to learn the graph with a number of samples that scales logarithmically in the system’s size22–24, and known algorithms are essentially optimal20. Substantial progress is still required to put the quantum protocols on an equal footing with their classical counterparts. At the time the first version of the present work was made publicly available, the best known general-purpose computationally efficient quantum protocols for Hamiltonian learning from a Gibbs state of spin systems required previous knowledge of the graph with respect to which the Hamiltonian is local and had a sample complexity that is polynomial in the system’s size3,12. Afterward, logarithmic sample complexity was proven by ref. 27. Furthermore, the computationally efficient quantum algorithms have a much worse scaling in precision3,12,27 compared to the classical case and, in a nutshell, the main technical barrier for quantum algorithms is the lack of (exact) conditional independence structures for the underlying states, which is central to most classical results.

In the classical case, there is also an extensive literature on learning Gaussian graphical models28–30, which can be understood as continuous-variable versions of discrete graphical models. In the last years, many fundamental questions related to this problem were settled, such as finding a sample-optimal algorithm for graph recovery in the bounded-degree regime31 matching information-theoretic bounds32. Related work has also studied Gaussian learning under differential privacy33 and estimation of Gaussian mixtures34. The natural quantum variation of learning a Gaussian model is that of learning bosonic Gaussian states35, which correspond to Gibbs and ground states of quadratic Hamiltonians. Their use as a learning resource is physically motivated by the equilibration of open quantum systems36–39. Bosonic Hamiltonians with a finite number of modes are used to describe a multitude of interacting many-body quantum systems, such as cold atoms in optical lattices, magnetization fields, optomechanical systems, trapped ions, and electromagnetic fields in cavities, interacting with matter40. In many cases of interest, the interaction strength decays with the distance with respect to a graph. Sparse Hamiltonians considered in this work are models where the interactions are assumed to be zero if vertices are not connected, for example, because the corresponding sites are further than some fixed distance on a lattice. This includes the approximation of considering non-zero interactions only between first neighbors. In Fig. 1, we show an example of an interaction graph and its corresponding structure of the Hamiltonian matrix. Experimentally realizing local (and thus sparse) Hamiltonians, including bosonic ones, is the goal of analog quantum simulation41, both for reproducing models of condensed matter physics and for creating and studying meta-materials that cannot be found in nature42–44. Current programmable simulators can moreover implement Hamiltonians with engineered graph structures in a variety of platforms, see Supplementary Note 2 for a short discussion. Here, we mention that the closest work to our idealized setting is ref. 45, which reports the engineering of various interaction graphs, including rings, chains, ladders, non-Archimedean geometries, in an array of atomic ensembles with 18 sites and 104 Rubidium atoms per site, interacting via spin-exchange. The collective magnetizations at each site can be treated as bosonic variables in the relevant regime, with a quadratic Hamiltonian. The paper also shows procedures to verify the interactions from two-point correlation functions of the magnetization, using certain approximations and assumptions. Other notable examples of platforms with programmable bosonic Hamiltonians are refs. 46–48. In all the above, quadratic hopping Hamiltonians already explore non-trivial physics and testing their correct implementation is paramount to go forward to the simulation of interacting models. Somewhat surprisingly, given the prevalence and importance of bosonic Gaussian Hamiltonians, to the best of our knowledge, previous works that considered bosonic Hamiltonian learning problems focused on learning parameters from dynamics6,9, not Gibbs states. In our work, we address this question by studying three related tasks for bosonic Gaussian states: Hamiltonian learning, graph learning and trace distance learning. We show that it is possible to learn all the parameters of a quadratic Hamiltonian on m modes on a known interaction graph of bounded degree with a number of samples scaling logarithmically in m, the natural measure of the size of the system, with nearly inverse-quadratic dependence on the target error, when all the other parameters are bounded. Furthermore, the classical postprocessing of the data is efficient and we resort only to heterodyne measurements, which are often simple to implement in the lab. We also show how to recover an unknown interaction graph, assuming bounded parameters and a lower bound on every nonzero interaction. The corresponding sample complexity is logarithmic in m and nearly inverse-quadratic in the interaction threshold parameter. Thus, our method only requires experimentally feasible measurements, is highly scalable, and it provides quantitative guarantees on the precise estimation of the interactions and the graph in bosonic simulators that hold at any positive temperature and that are not model-specific. In the context of sensing, our results imply that without trying to achieve the optimal information-theoretic limits, which are highly sensitive to the precise values of the parameters themselves, one can ensure that the estimate of any parameter encoded linearly in the Hamiltonian matrix is robust with respect to uncertainties in the other parameters. In addition, we obtain a protocol to learn a positive-temperature Gaussian state in trace distance with an inverse-quadratic scaling in error. Previous work53,54 obtained polynomial dependence on the number of modes but inverse-quartic scaling in error, while imposing fewer assumptions on the state compared to our result. Closely contemporaneous work also obtained an inverse-quadratic scaling by a different method that also applies to pure states, and a different concrete trace distance bound55.

From a technical perspective, our main innovations are various perturbation bounds for covariance and Hamiltonian matrices of Gaussian states that are of independent interest, combined with what we call the local inversion subroutine, a key part of our algorithms for graphical models. This allows us to estimate the Hamiltonian of Gaussian states by accessing only small blocks of the covariance matrix at a time, with the precision of the estimates only scaling with the size of the blocks and not the whole system. Relation to Gaussian graphical models

But the interpretation in the quantum case of the nonzero entries of H is similar to the classical case, as these represent the systems that “interact” with each other.

In the literature on learning of Gaussian graphical models, one is often interested in learning the conditional independence structure of the random variables, which corresponds to estimating the sparsity structure of Θ. From that, one is also usually interested in estimating the parameters of Θ, while having a scaling of the sample complexity that only scales logarithmically with the dimension of the random variables. Thus, we see that the problem of learning the sparsity structure of H is in direct correspondence with that of learning the conditional independence structure graph of a Gaussian graphical model. And that of estimating the entries of H in turn corresponds to that of estimating the parameters of the Gaussian graphical model.

Furthermore, the two problems also share some technical aspects; for instance, in contrast to the discrete case, the underlying objects we sample from typically take unbounded values and a finer understanding of concentration is required. But the quantum problem poses unique challenges that are not present in the classical case. One difference is that the uncertainty principle obstructs from sampling from a multivariate normal with covariance matrix V measuring one copy of the state: we resort to heterodyne measurement, whose output is a multivariate normal with covariance matrix V + I/2. More importantly, the more complicated functional dependence between the covariance matrix and H makes the analysis more challenging, since entries of V corresponding to the neighborhood of a vertex i are not sufficient by themselves to reconstruct exactly the interactions of i with its neighbors (matrix elements Hij). This fact can be understood as related to the lack of conditional independence in the quantum case: in general, one cannot reconstruct the state of the system by applying a channel on the complement of vertex i that acts nontrivially only on the vertices connected to i. Our local inversion method, instead, goes further in the graph to account for the lack of conditional independence.

The problem of estimating Gaussian graphical models with sparsity is a central theme of high-dimensional statistics, and we refer to books such as refs. 56–58 for a more in-depth history of the problem and we provide a more detailed review of technical results in the classical literature in Supplementary Note 2. Summary of results and comparison with relevant work

All the protocols require O(poly(m)) computation time to learn the relevant parametrization. In the case of graph learning, the polynomial degree depends on the other parameters, obeying the bounded parameters condition.

Moreover, all of our results only require heterodyne measurements, which are easy to implement experimentally, and have polynomial postprocessing in the number of modes. As is well-known and explained in detail in Supplementary Note 3, given a Gaussian state ρ with covariance matrix V, performing a heterodyne measurement corresponds to sampling from a Gaussian random vector with covariance matrix given by V + I/2. Thus, by resorting to standard concentration inequalities of Gaussian random variables67, we show in Supplementary Note 3 that to estimate the entries of the covariance matrix it is sufficient to compute the empirical estimator of the covariance matrix of the heterodyne output and subtract I/2. This procedure is the bread and butter of all our algorithms, as we always depart from some estimate of V. We will first present our result on learning in trace distance. Roughly speaking (see Supplementary Definition 4.1 for a formal definition), the trace distance learning problem requires to output a covariance matrix or Hamiltonian and mean vectors such that the corresponding Gaussian state is ϵ-close in trace distance to the true state with probability at least 1−δ, given access to measurement data. It is analogous to the classical problem of estimating a multivariate Gaussian in total variation distance. We then show in Supplementary Note 4:

The computational complexity of the above procedure is O(poly(m)) when the other parameters are fixed, dominated by the classical post-processing of LearnGraphGaussian in Box 2. Discussion

The present work establishes a foundation for studying the problem of learning quantum Gaussian graphical models. Several key challenges remain, which may require refinements of the techniques proposed or new approaches.

First of all, we make a remark on the (implicit) dependence of our result on the condition number of H. In the sample complexity for Hamiltonian and graph learning, we see a dependence on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${d}_{\min }$$\end{document}dmin, which can be interpreted as a “maximum temperature” parameter of the Gaussian state. Since \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${d}_{\min }$$\end{document}dmin gives a smaller contribution to the Hamiltonian matrix, it is natural to ask if this dependence can be removed. Although other quantum Hamiltonian learning results for discrete variable systems1–3,12 display polynomial dependence on the system size, they do not exhibit a dependence on a condition number. In fact, the dependence of our learning guarantees on the condition number can also be expressed in terms of potentially much smaller quantities, but we preferred to state our results as we did to avoid unnatural assumptions. Supplementary Remarks 4.1, 6.2, 7.2 clarify this point. In the analogous classical problem of learning the precision matrix (the term used in this context for the Hamiltonian matrix) or the graph, the dependence on the condition number was removed only by ref. 31, crucially using multiplicative error bounds on covariance estimation. In the graph learning problem, non-zero interactions respect a relative strength condition: two vertices (i, j) are connected if a Hamiltonian matrix element \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${H}_{2i-\delta,2j-{\delta }^{{\prime} }}$$\end{document}H2i−δ,2j−δ′ is sufficiently large compared with the diagonal elements \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${H}_{2i-\delta,2i-{\delta }^{{\prime} }}$$\end{document}H2i−δ,2i−δ′ and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${H}_{2j-\delta,2j-{\delta }^{{\prime} }}$$\end{document}H2j−δ,2j−δ′ (here \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta,\,{\delta }^{{\prime} }$$\end{document}δ,δ′ can be 0 or 1 and index the position and momentum quadratures). The sample complexity depends on the promise on the relative strength but not on the condition number. We leave the exploration of multiplicative error bounds in the quantum case for future work. See Supplementary Note 11 for an argument suggesting that with additive precision guarantees, one cannot avoid the condition number dependence.

Several natural developments of our work can be envisioned. The dependence of the sample complexity on ϵ and κ for Hamiltonian and graph learning, respectively, is not exactly the expected inverse quadratic one. While this can be remedied for graphs whose neighborhoods increase polynomially with distance, in the general case, this could be a limitation of our approach based on a Taylor expansion. It would be very interesting to understand if this can be overcome by other techniques. We also leave open the problem of finding lower bounds on the sample complexity of both Hamiltonian estimation and graph learning. It would be especially interesting to understand how these bounds differ from the ones that can be obtained in the classical case32. It would also be valuable to investigate whether our results can be extended to Gaussian fermionic states. Finally, we address the Hamiltonian learning problem by plugging-in estimates of the covariance matrix via a simple single-copy measurement procedure. It would be interesting to investigate if entangled measurements could be beneficial to the estimation of the covariance matrix and of the Hamiltonian matrix.

In this section, we discuss interpretations of our choice of parametrization appearing in our proofs, and connections with other possible choices. Operationally, the symplectic diagonalization (or Williamson’s normal form) corresponds to a preparation procedure for a Gaussian state: (1) prepare m thermal states of harmonic oscillators with inverse temperatures 2di. Trace distance learning

The main idea is to employ the symmetrized form of Pinsker’s inequality to upper-bound the trace distance between two Gaussian states in terms of their (symmetrized) relative entropies: 18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\parallel \rho -\hat{\rho }{\parallel }_{1}\le \sqrt{D(\rho \parallel \hat{\rho })+D(\hat{\rho }\parallel \rho )}\,.$$\end{document}∥ρ−ρ^∥1≤D(ρ∥ρ^)+D(ρ^∥ρ). We are then able to obtain a closed formula for the relative entropy, yielding the expression: 19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\parallel \rho -\hat{\rho }{\parallel }_{1}\le \sqrt{\mathop{\sum}_{i,j}\left[({\hat{H}}_{i,j}-{H}_{i,j})({V}_{i,j}-{\hat{V}}_{i,j})+({t}_{i}-{\hat{t}}_{i})({t}_{j}-{\hat{t}}_{j})({H}_{i,j}+{\hat{H}}_{i,j})\right]}$$\end{document}∥ρ−ρ^∥1≤∑i,j(H^i,j−Hi,j)(Vi,j−V^i,j)+(ti−t^i)(tj−t^j)(Hi,j+H^i,j) Then, using our estimators for the covariance matrix and the mean vector, combined with the continuity estimate in Eq. Hamiltonian learning

Recall that, given the covariance matrix V, the Hamiltonian of the Gaussian is given by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H=\frac{1}{2}\ln \left(I+\frac{2}{2i\Omega V-I}\right)i\Omega$$\end{document}H=12lnI+22iΩV−IiΩ. Thus, it is possible to infer the Hamiltonian if we have a sufficiently accurate approximation of the inverse of 2V−iΩ. These observations and the continuity estimates from Eqs. (10) and (11) already suffice to obtain a Hamiltonian learning protocol with polynomial sample complexity in the number of vertices. The main reason for the polynomial scaling is that this plug-in strategy requires us to estimate each entry of the covariance matrix to polynomial precision to ensure that the error in operator norm is of order ϵ once we perform the inversion.

However, based on classical results28,30, we expect to be able to solve the Hamiltonian learning problem with a number of samples that scales logarithmically with the number of modes. To achieve such a scaling, we pioneer what we call the local inversion technique. The intuition behind the technique is that, for local Gibbs states, the correlations with neighboring sites should determine the interactions. For classical Gaussian graphical models, this is true by conditional independence, so it is possible to completely determine the Hamiltonian matrix by just inverting blocks of the covariance matrix of constant-sized neighborhoods around each vertex. While this fails in the quantum case, we nevertheless show that for Gaussian quantum states it suffices to post-process an estimate of (2V−iΩ)−1, constructed such that matrix entries corresponding to a pair of vertices are obtained from inverting submatrices of the covariance matrix corresponding to vertices that are at most \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim \frac{\log ({\epsilon }^{-1})}{\log \log ({\epsilon }^{-1})}$$\end{document}~log(ϵ−1)loglog(ϵ−1) away from the target pair of vertices, to estimate the interactions with error up to ϵ. To show this, we resort to various bounds on the error in approximating inverses of approximately sparse matrices with local inversions, and the Taylor series representation of (2V−iΩ)−1 in terms of the Hamiltonian, namely 20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${(2V-i\Omega )}^{-1}=-\frac{I-{e}^{+2Hi\Omega }}{2}(i\Omega )=\frac{1}{2}{\sum }_{n=1}^{\infty }\frac{{\left(2Hi\Omega \right)}^{n}}{n!}(i\Omega ) \\=\frac{1}{2}{\sum }_{n=1}^{l}\frac{{\left(2Hi\Omega \right)}^{n}}{n!}(i\Omega )+E\,,$$\end{document}(2V−iΩ)−1=−I−e+2HiΩ2(iΩ)=12∑n=1∞2HiΩnn!(iΩ)=12∑n=1l2HiΩnn!(iΩ)+E, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\parallel E{\parallel }_{\infty }\le \frac{\parallel S{\parallel }_{\infty }^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{{(2{d}_{\max })}^{l+1}{e}^{2{d}_{\max }}}{(l+1)!}\right)$$\end{document}∥E∥∞≤∥S∥∞22(2dmax)l+1e2dmax(l+1)!, such that the parameter l controls both the sparsity of the approximation of (2V−iΩ)−1 and the approximation error. Graph learning

The arguments to obtain our graph learning result also mostly rely on our local inversion techniques. The graph learning algorithm takes as input an estimate of the covariance matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{V}$$\end{document}V^, a promised lower bound on the strength of interaction κ > 0, a promised upper bound on the size of neighborhoods of distance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l\, \sim \frac{\log ({\kappa }^{-1})}{\log \log ({\kappa }^{-1})}$$\end{document}l~log(κ−1)loglog(κ−1) of vertices, denoted by ξ(l) ≤ Δl, and a threshold parameter η, which depends on ∥S∥∞ and the symplectic eigenvalues. It then iterates over all vertices i and all possible neighborhoods of size ξ(l), denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\mathcal{N}}}}_{i}$$\end{document}Ni. For each of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\mathcal{N}}}}_{i}$$\end{document}Ni, we then consider all possible neighborhoods enlarged by ξ(l) additional vertices, defining a set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\overline{{{\mathcal{N}}}}}_{i}$$\end{document}N¯i, and perform our local inversion with the enlarged neighborhood. If we see that in the resulting matrix there are no large entries (as measured by the threshold η) on row i and vertices \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$j\in {\overline{{{\mathcal{N}}}}}_{i}$$\end{document}j∈N¯i, we then set our neighborhood as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\mathcal{N}}}}_{i}$$\end{document}Ni. The intuition behind the procedure is as follows: recall that we assume that we have a lower bound on the size of all interactions and that matrix elements of (2V−iΩ)−1 corresponding to vertices that are far apart decay rapidly. Furthermore, performing local inversions with entries at a distance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l \sim \frac{\log ({\eta }^{-1})}{\log \log ({\eta }^{-1})}$$\end{document}l~log(η−1)loglog(η−1) from a vertex suffices to obtain a good recovery of the entries up to error ~η, which scales roughly as κ. If the l-neighborhood of i is contained in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\mathcal{N}}}}_{i}$$\end{document}Ni, then all the estimates with the ξ(l)-enlarged neighborhood will be correct, and all the entries added will be small because of the decay. Thus, we will correctly accept in this case. On the other hand, if we have the incorrect or incomplete l-neighborhood, then there are ξ(l) vertices we can add that will make it complete. For that joint neighborhood, we see two possibilities: either this will “turn on” a large entry, which will show us that we were not operating with the right neighborhood. In contrast, if we see that for all possibilities we add this never occurs, then we can also conclude that the vertices we are missing will only add a small error and can be neglected. By iterating over all such enlarged neighborhoods, we are able to successfully find submatrices to locally invert the covariance matrix for each vertex. After that, we inspect the resulting Hamiltonian and discard the interactions we find to be below the threshold given to us. Here, too, we are able to obtain a logarithmic scaling in the number of modes because we only need to invert locally.
