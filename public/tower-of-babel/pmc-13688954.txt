Histology images in spatial transcriptomics: a critical evaluation of their effectiveness in spatial domain detection
Authors: Abdullah Al Noman, Asif Ajrof, Md Abul Hassan Samee, M Saifur Rahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
MotivationSpatial transcriptomics (ST) has become a powerful tool for studying gene expression at the spatial level, as the position of a given spot provides crucial information about its phenotype. One key application of downstream analysis on these ST datasets is identifying spatial domains within tissue regions. However, the accuracy of spatial domain detection can be hindered by technical noise and low resolution due to technological limitations. Some ST technologies now provide high-resolution histology images of the original tissue sample prior to sequencing, offering complementary information. Incorporating these histology images presents a promising approach to addressing these limitations. However, there is a scarcity of reviews on recent methods that use histology images for spatial domain detection tasks. Also, it remains unclear how much improvement is gained by these methods when integrating histology image with ST data over using ST data alone.ResultsThis article presents an analysis of various methodologies that integrate histology images for spatial domain detection. We describe the specific challenges these methods address in incorporating histology images alongside gene expression data, highlighting both their commonalities and distinctions. Furthermore, we design a statistical test to systematically determine the significance of domain quality improvement with the additional modality of histology images, if any, by comparing the performance of methods run both with and without the histology data. Furthermore, we conduct statistical tests to determine the significance of histology images in improving spatial domain detection tasks. Our findings indicate no notable enhancement in spatial domain detection when histology images are used as an additional source of information alongside gene expression data for the methods under study. Building on these results, we investigate the possible reasons why histology images may fail to improve performance and discuss future directions for their effective integration in spatial domain detection.Availability and implementationSource code is available at Github (https://github.com/asifajrof/StHistDDComp).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-12830-8.

Introduction

Identifying distinct tissue regions, known as spatial domains, is fundamental to understanding tissue architecture. These domains are defined by their unique cellular compositions and molecular states, and discovering them is critical in development, health, and diseases like cancer. Characterizing the molecular makeup of these domains requires tools that can profile cells with high precision. For this, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has been a pivotal technology, enabling researchers to classify cell types and states from their gene expression profiles [1–4]. However, they have a significant limitation: they require the dissociation of tissue, which causes all native spatial information to be lost. This is a critical drawback, as many biological systems, such as embryos, liver lobules, intestinal villi, and tumors depend on the spatial organization of their cells [5, 6]. Spatial transcriptomics (ST) bridges this gap by preserving spatial information by not requiring tissue dissociation for profiling transcriptome-wide gene expression. However, there are variations in ST technologies. For example, sequencing-based methods for spatially resolved transcriptomics (SRT), such as Visium from 10x Genomics [7], provide users with gene expression data, spatial locations, and a microscopy image stained with hematoxylin and eosin, called a histology image. Studies have shown a strong link between histology and gene expression data [8], highlighting the correlation between these two modalities. Conversely, imaging-based Fluorescence in situ Hybridization (FISH) technologies, such as MERFISH [9] and seqFISH [10], deliver high-resolution gene expression data within a spatial context but do not yield direct histology images. Many computational methods have been developed that use these ST data to perform this spatial domain detection task.

Clustering algorithms for detecting spatial domains in spatial transcriptomics (ST) data are primarily categorized into non-spatial and spatial clustering methods. Non-spatial algorithms like k-means [11] and Louvain [12] applied to gene expression data often fail to align well with tissue organization due to their inability to incorporate spatial information. To address this limitation, spatial clustering approaches have been developed, integrating both gene expression profiles and spatial coordinates. BayesSpace [13], SC-MEB [14], and Giotto [15] are examples of spatial clustering methods designed to enhance ST data analysis by leveraging spatial context. BayesSpace employs a fully Bayesian statistical model, while SC-MEB utilizes an empirical Bayes approach with hidden Markov random fields for spatial clustering. Despite advancements in ST technologies, challenges such as dropout noise from sequencing technology’s low capture rates and diffusion errors during mRNA permeabilization persist, significantly impacting the accuracy of spatial cluster identification.

To mitigate these challenges due to low resolution or high noise, recent methods have integrated matched histopathological images provided by sequencing technologies for a diverse range of computational task. A primary application involves leveraging histology to predict spatial gene expression profiles directly from histology slides [16, 17]. Furthermore, histological data is used in enhancing the quality of ST data by addressing common challenges such as sparsity, noise, and missing values. This enhancement, in turn, improves downstream analyses, including characterization of intercellular communication and the identification of ligand-receptor interactions [18]. Also other key applications including the identification of gene markers specific to the tumor microenvironments [18], where morphological features provide critical context to augment molecular data. Following the trend, recent methodologies use histology image to improve the spatial domain detection task [19–24] with the rationale that rich morphological data can ameliorate the inherent technical noise and limited resolution of ST data.

Despite the recent popularity, there is a notable gap in literature reviewing methods that integrate histology images with gene expression data in spatial domain detection. Understanding how these methods incorporate histology images and their general approach to spatial domain detection is crucial for users seeking to enhance spatial domain detection in gene expression data. It remains unclear whether the presence of a matched histology image is an advantage in downstream analysis. In this article, we have conducted a thorough review of various methods that incorporate histology images alongside gene expression data. Our goal is to understand how recent tools improve spatial domain detection tasks when histology images are integrated with gene expression data for spatial domain detection. To explore the workflow of recent methods that combine histology with gene expression data for spatial domain detection in Spatial Transcriptomics (SRT) datasets, we identified eight methodologies through searches on PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and the Museum of Spatial Transcriptomics (https://pachterlab.github.io/LP_2021/). These methods include stLearn [22], SpaGCN [19], conST [20], TIST [23], ConGI [25], DeepST [21], St2cell [24], and our previous work ScribbleDom [26].

Among these eight methods, we identified three that can function even in the absence of histology images. Using these three methods, we conducted a statistical test on the cluster labels they produced under two scenarios: one where both transcriptomics data and histology images were used, and another where only transcriptomics data was used to detect spatial domains in tissue samples. This test aimed to determine if incorporating histology images significantly improves spatial domain detection. Our analysis reveals that the direct integration of raw histology images does not consistently enhance spatial domain detection, as evidenced by the lack of significant improvement for the SpaGCN and conST models. This key finding underscores that simply combining imaging features with spatial transcriptomics is not a universally effective solution for improving the accuracy of tissue region identification. During our work in ScribbleDom, we observed that human-guided scribbles on the images did yield substantial performance gains in many cases. But its success was not absolute, as the effectiveness of this method varied across different tissue samples. The limited utility of histology images alongside the variable success of semi-supervised methods prompted our detailed discussion into the potential reasons for these challenges and an exploration of promising future directions to overcome these obstacles. To our knowledge, this perspective represents a novel contribution to the field.

Methods and materials

Dataset for benchmarking

In our analysis, we utilized four distinct datasets: The first dataset comprises 12 human brain samples from the human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC). These samples were analyzed using the 10x Genomics Visium platform. Maynard et al. [7] provided annotations for the DLPFC layers, considering cytoarchitecture and selecting gene markers. This dataset is considered a standard benchmark for performance evaluation by several methods, including SpaGCN, DeepST, ConGI, conST, and ScribbleDom. Our second dataset is the human breast cancer dataset, specifically “Block A, section 1”. This dataset was analyzed using the H&E staining technique and includes manual pathology labelling [27]. Notably, it contains similar tissue regions dispersed in spatially diverse areas, presenting a challenge for the methods in accurately capturing this spatial diversity. Our third dataset is a melanoma cancer sample processed using the Spatial Transcriptomics (ST) platform [28]. We specifically used the second replicate from biopsy 1, as it contains manual annotations where tissue regions are dispersed across different spatial areas. This sample contains a total of 293 spots covered by tissue. Our fourth dataset is a publicly available human breast cancer ductal carcinoma (BCDC) ffpe (Formalin-Fixed Paraffin-Embedded) sample from 10x Genomics, obtained using the Visium platform [1]. This dataset features ductal carcinoma tissue where tumors are spread across various regions, comprising a total of 2,518 spots. The manual annotation for this dataset, which classifies each spot as ’tumor’ or ’non-tumor’, was provided by Ni et al. [29].

We selected these specific datasets for several key reasons. Foremost, we have ground truth for these tissue samples in the form of manual annotations, which are essential for the quantitative evaluation of model performance. By adding different types of data, specifically, both healthy, layered tissue (DLPFC) and heterogeneous cancerous tissues (melanoma and breast cancer) we aim to test the models across diverse biological contexts.

Selection criteria for recent spatial domain detection methods

We compiled a comprehensive list of 10 spatial domain detection methods by conducting searches for ‘spatial domain’, ‘spatial cluster’ and ‘histology’ on PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and the Museum of Spatial Transcriptomics (https://pachterlab.github.io/LP_2021/). HisToGene [30] and His2ST [31] were excluded as they solely rely on the histology image for spatial domain detection without utilizing ST data. Ultimately, our comparison includes 8 methods: stLearn [22], SpaGCN [19], conST [20], TIST [23], ConGI [25], DeepST [21], St2cell [24], and ScribbleDom [26].

To provide a comprehensive performance benchmark, we conducted an experimental comparison of several state-of-the-art spatial domain identification methods: stLearn, SpaGCN, conST, DeepST, and ScribbleDom. This evaluation was performed on four manually annotated datasets to ensure a thorough assessment across different tissue architectures: the 12 human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) samples [7], a human breast cancer sample (Block A, section 1) [27], a melanoma cancer sample [28], and a breast cancer ductal carcinoma (BCDC) ffpe dataset [25, 29]. The performance of each method was evaluated using several key metrics. To measure concordance with ground-truth annotations, we primarily used the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI), which also served as our main evaluation metric when assessing the significance of histology image integration. For internal cluster validation, we assessed gene expression similarity within clusters using the silhouette score and measured spatial autocorrelation with Moran’s I and Geary’s C. Finally, we compared resource utilization by recording the running time, peak memory (RAM) usage, and GPU memory consumption.

The machine specifications were Ubuntu 20.04, RAM: 13 GB, and GPU: T4. In our analysis, we excluded ConGI from the comparison due to its extensive runtime of over 6.5 hours and high GPU memory consumption of 26 GB per sample, which proved to be impractical for running on all samples. TIST was not included in the comparison because we encountered dependency errors on multiple machine setups in the installation of the R package distribution of TIST. And St2cell authors have not published their codebase at the time of comparison.

Cross-method concordance and boundary analysis

To address the need for an analysis of label-level consistency and boundary precision, we implemented a cross-method concordance framework. For these comparisons, we selected the specific experimental run (seed) for each method that yielded the highest ARI with respect to the ground truth. This analysis focuses on three key aspects: pairwise label agreement, boundary stability, and spatially aligned visualization.

To compare the domain structures identified by different methods, we calculated the pairwise Adjusted Mutual Information (AMI) score. To facilitate direct visual comparison and the generation of aggregate confusion matrices, we aligned the predicted labels of all methods to the ground truth using the Hungarian algorithm [33]. This method constructs a contingency table between the reference and comparison labels and finds an one-to-one mapping that maximizes the total overlap, ensuring consistent color coding across domain maps. Also, we developed a specific protocol to evaluate how well different methods capture spatial boundaries. We calculated the boundary mask of the ST data using a k-nearest neighbor approach (k=6). A spot was classified as a boundary spot if the ratio of neighbors possessing a different label exceeded a threshold of 0.3. Using these binary boundary masks, we performed two analyses. First, we calculated the Intersection over Union (IoU) of the detected boundary barcodes between pairs of methods. This metric quantifies the physical agreement on where domain transitions occur. Secondly, we calculated Moran’s I on the generated boundary masks. A higher Moran’s I in this context indicates that the detected boundaries form spatially continuous, coherent structures rather than scattered, noisy artifacts.

To visualize agreements and disagreements simultaneously in the spatial context, we generated overlay maps. In these visualizations, each spatial spot is rendered as a multi-slice pie chart. Each slice represents the aligned label prediction from a different method. This visualization provides an intuitive, high-resolution view of method consensus, highlighting regions where methods converge on a single domain versus regions of high uncertainty or disagreement.

Rank-sum test for finding statistical significance

We employed the rank-sum test [34] to evaluate our hypothesis, which is a non-parametric and non-paired test. Given the uncertainty about whether the distribution of ARI values from the samples follows a normal distribution, we opted for a non-parametric approach. In this context, let us consider two sets of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d) samples: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{ X_1, X_2, \ldots , X_n \}$$\end{document} from the distribution of results with the use of histology images (X), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{ Y_1, Y_2, \ldots , Y_m \}$$\end{document}from the distribution of results without the use of histology images (Y). When we rank the combined sample (X||Y), the test statistic (TS) is calculated as the sum of the ranks of the first sample. When both n and m are greater than 7, the test statistic TS, under the assumption that the null hypothesis (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_0$$\end{document}) is true, is expected to have an approximately normal distribution. This distribution follows certain characteristics, including a mean and variance represented by E[TS] and Var(TS), respectively.\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} E[TS] & = \frac{n \times (n+m+1)}{2}\\ Var(TS) & = \frac{n \times m \times (n+m+1)}{12} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Our null hypothesis, represented as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_0$$\end{document}, suggests that X ≤ Y, indicating that incorporating histology images results in performance that is equal to or inferior to the performance without the use of histology images. In this context, the statistic, z, is derived under the large-sample approximation, where the rank-sum statistic approximates a normal distribution. The p-value for the sample is subsequently determined from the cumulative density function (Φ ) of the normal distribution up to the z value.\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} & z = \frac{TS - E[TS]}{\sqrt{Var(TS)}}\\ & p\text {-}value = \Phi (z) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Correction for multiple hypothesis testing

To ensure that our reported improvements are statistically robust and not artifacts of multiple comparisons, we applied the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure [35] to the set of calculated raw p-values. This correction controls the False Discovery Rate (FDR)—the expected proportion of false positives among all rejected null hypotheses. We aggregated the raw p-values from all independent tests into a single vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P = \{p_1, p_2, \dots , p_k\}$$\end{document} and computed the adjusted p-values (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{adj}$$\end{document}). Statistical significance was strictly determined at an FDR threshold of α = 0.05. A result was considered statistically significant only if its corresponding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{adj} < 0.05$$\end{document}. This correction ensures that our reported improvements are not artifacts of multiple statistical comparisons.

Histology image pre-processing and enhancement

To evaluate whether image pre-processing and quality control improve the performance of spatial domain detection models when integrating histology images, we incorporated a state-of-the-art image enhancement method into our workflow. The objective was to determine whether enhancing the visual features of the histology data leads to a more effective multi-modal analysis. Our choice of method was guided by a comparative study from Touhami et al. [36], which benchmarked several techniques for histopathology image enhancement. Their findings demonstrated that SwinIR [37] delivered the best quantitative performance. We applied the pre-trained SwinIR model to the raw histology images for each tissue sample, generating a corresponding set of enhanced images. Consequently, we adapted our statistical test (Algorithm 1) to systematically assess the significance of histology images by evaluating their integration with ST data under two separate scenarios: once with the raw image and again with the SwinIR-enhanced image. These two comparisons enable a direct assessment of whether image enhancement provides a tangible and significant advantage over using raw images for this task.

Results

In this section, we conducted a comparative analysis of methods that integrate histology images with gene expression data for spatial domain detection tasks, assessing their similarities and differences. Subsequently, we evaluated whether the inclusion of histology images significantly enhances the performance of spatial domain detection tasks.

Processing of gene expression data

In the processing of gene expression data, the raw gene expression count matrix is initially filtered to identify highly variable genes. Subsequently, the gene expression count matrix is narrowed down to include only these highly variable genes for further analysis. Traditionally, many methods proceed to reduce the dimensionality of this gene expression data using techniques such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA). Only after this enhancement, they proceed to apply dimension-reduction techniques. (See 1st row of Table 1)

Processing of histology image

The conventional method for extracting features from histology images typically involves applying deep learning techniques to extract features from image patches corresponding to specific spots (See Table 2). However, TIST deviates from this approach by utilizing a Markov Random Field-based method after clustering the image using k-means clustering. Meanwhile, SpaGCN simplifies the process by calculating the weighted average of pixel intensity as the feature. Additionally, ScribbleDom incorporates scribbles generated through human cognition as additional features from histology images.

Combined processing of the multi-modalities

For the combined processing step, stLearn uses histological similarity to augment gene expression data and calculate normalized gene expression, while TIST constructs TIST-net by integrating the histology and gene expression similarity network. St2cell, on the other hand, employs convex quadratic programming for the combined processing. The other methods use deep learning architectures, such as graph convolutional networks and auto-encoder, to learn the latent representation. ScribbleDom uses a semi-supervised approach originally used for segmenting image data with human scribble to incorporate human cognition with the ST data. (See 4th row of Table 1)

Clustering algorithm

For clustering, the methods run existing clustering solutions on the learned representations. stLearn uses Louvain or k-means clustering. SpaGCN employs an unsupervised clustering algorithm called Deep Embedded Clustering, with Louvain as the initialization algorithm for the cluster centroids. In conST, the Leiden algorithm is used for clustering. TIST detects clusters using Walktrap, a random walk-based algorithm. ConGI takes two different strategies. When the number of spatial domains is specific, they apply the Mclust [38] algorithm. Otherwise, they identify the domains through the Louvain algorithm. DeepST uses the Leiden algorithm. St2cell initializes their clustering with the k-means algorithm. ScribbleDom creates an automated scribble from the output of a non-spatial algorithm like Mclust.

Refinement on the clusters

After the main clustering algorithm, certain methods employ an additional step of running a refinement process on the identified spatial domains (Table 2). stLearn applies sub-clustering after the global clustering. SpaGCN and ConGI apply an optional refinement on the clustering to ensure the continuity of the clusters. The other methods do not apply such techniques.

Sensitivity to the corresponding histology image

Some methods like, SpaGCN, conST and ScribbleDom offer the capability of spatial domain detection even without using the histology image whereas some methods like stLearn and DeepST do not work without a histology image. Moreover, SpaGCN provides a hyperparameter that allows controlling the weight assigned to the histology information. Other methods rely on high-quality histology images for effective spatial domain detection (See 7th row of Table 1).

Performance measure across tissue samples

To evaluate the performance of spatial domain detection, we conducted an analysis of five methods: SpaGCN, conST, ScribbleDom, stLearn, and DeepST, using 12 human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and 3 cancer samples. The evaluation was based on four metrics: Adjusted Rand Index (ARI), silhouette score, Moran’s I, and Geary’s C. For methods that support both modes, we considered performance with and without histology data (SpaGCN, conST, ScribbleDom), whereas methods requiring histology (stLearn, DeepST) were evaluated only under that condition. To ensure robust results, each configuration was executed with 20 different random seeds to generate performance distributions. The results are shown in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figures S1 to S14. Our experiments revealed several key trends. Notably, for ScribbleDom and DeepST, higher ARI scores were consistently associated with improved spatial autocorrelation, specifically, higher Moran’s I and lower Geary’s C values. This suggests that for these methods, enhancements in spatial organization directly correlate with greater accuracy against the ground truth. In contrast, no consistent performance trend was observed for SpaGCN and conST when histology was integrated. For SpaGCN, using histology resulted in an equal or improved median ARI in only 2 of the 12 samples, 151510 and 151669 (Supplementary Table S1). Similarly, conST showed improved ARI in just 2 of 12 samples, 151674 and 151675, while ScribbleDom demonstrated improved or equal performance in 8 of the 12 DLPFC samples when using guided scribbles (Supplementary Table S1). In the cancer samples, we found a different trend: stLearn performed better then DeepST, showing better ARI and spatial autocorrelation. However, we saw similar trend for SpaGCN, conST and ScribbleDom. They performed better with histology in human breast cancer sample but failed to show any improvement with histology in melanoma and human BCDC samples. Furthermore, we observed a significant disconnect between gene expression similarity and accuracy with respect to ground truth. Across most samples, the silhouette score was not positively correlated with the ARI for ScribbleDom and DeepST. For instance, ScribbleDom often exhibited a higher ARI when using scribbles, even as its corresponding silhouette score decreased compared to when using only ST data. Conversely, SpaGCN and conST sometimes produced high silhouette scores in configurations that yielded lower ARI values. Cross method concordance and boundary precision

To evaluate the agreement among the spatial domain detection methods, we implemented cross-method concordance framework to analyze pairwise label agreement, boundary stability, and spatial consensus for each of the ST tissue samples. This analysis was performed using the experimental seed yielding the highest ARI for each method and histology usage and aligning the domain labels with ground truth using Hungarian algorithm [33].

We utilized Pairwise Adjusted Mutual Information (AMI) to quantify the informational overlap not just with the ground truth, but between the methods themselves (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Figure S15a - S28a). Our analysis revealed the existence of distinct methodological families, where algorithms often exhibited greater similarity to one another than to the ground truth. For instance, in DLPFC Sample 151507, while DeepST and ScribbleDom achieved moderate agreement with the ground truth (AMI ≈ 0.61 and 0.68, respectively), they demonstrated substantial pairwise concordance with each other (AMI ≈ 0.60), suggesting that these models capture analogous biological signals despite differences in architecture (Fig. 4a). The Intersection over Union (IoU) analysis of boundary spots highlighted a critical dichotomy between tissue types (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Figure S15b - S28b). Across the human DLPFC datasets, pairwise IoU values were universally low, rarely exceeding 0.20 for any pair of methods or between any method and the ground truth. For example, in Sample 151507 (Fig. 4b), the boundary overlap between DeepST and SpaGCN was only 0.26, and their overlap with the ground truth was even lower (≤ 0.16). This consistent trend indicates that boundary detection in the brain cortex is a highly uncertain task; while methods converge on the general spatial location of a domain, they fundamentally disagree on the precise pixel-level transition zones. In contrast, boundary detection proved significantly more robust showing higher IoU in melanoma cancer and human BCDC dataset tissues. For example, in the Melanoma sample (Supplementary Figure S28b), the boundary overlap between SpaGCN’s predictions with and without histology was ≈ 0.69, maintaining robust overlap with the ground truth (≈ 0.50). This confirms that boundary uncertainty is highly context-dependent, sometimes driven by biological distinctness than simply an algorithmic failure. Another observation emerged when analyzing the internal consistency of individual methods across both AMI and IoU metrics. We found a high degree of intra-method concordance, where variants of the same algorithm consistently produced label maps that were highly similar to each other. For instance, the pairwise AMI between SpaGCN’s variants (SpaGCN with histology vs without histology) frequently approached higher score (e.g., 0.67 in Sample 151507), indicating that the addition of histology resulted in negligible changes to the spatial domains (Fig. 4b). We further assessed boundary continuity using Moran’s I (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Figure S15c-S28c). A consistent trend emerged in the DLPFC datasets where computational methods produced boundaries with significantly higher spatial autocorrelation (0.54–0.60) compared to the ground truth (≈ 0.36). This quantitative difference reflects the nature of the detected transitions: the lower Moran’s I of the ground truth indicates that manual boundaries are sharp, abrupt, and defined by thin lines of separation. In contrast, the higher values observed in algorithmic predictions indicate that these methods tend to generate broader, spatially clustered transition zones instead of a sharp border. Also, the spatially resolved consensus maps (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Figure S15d-S28d) visually corroborates this dichotomy between domain certainty and boundary uncertainty. In these visualizations, the core regions of spatial domains appeared as solid, single-colored pies, indicating a high degree of certainty where all methods converge on the same label. Conversely, the region boundaries did not manifest as clean lines but as broad zones of multi-colored pies. This visualization directly reflected the low IoU scores observed in complex tissues. Both raw and enhanced histology images offer limited improvement for spatial domain detection

To evaluate the capability of methods to improve performance using histology images, we selected three methods out of the eight we studied: SpaGCN, conST, and ScribbleDom. These methods can run both with only the gene expression count matrix data (in the absence of histology images) and with the addition of histology images to the count matrix when available. We ran SpaGCN, conST, and ScribbleDom with and without the inclusion of feature extraction from histology images for 20 random seeds (note that stLearn and DeepST do not allow operation without histology images). Our goal was to find the significance of utilizing histology images in conjunction with spatial transcriptomics (ST) data for spatial domain detection in tissue samples. We employed the rank-sum test to examine our null hypothesis, which postulated that the ARI values achieved by combining histology images with ST data would outperform (or same) the use of ST data alone (See Algorithm 1). Through our experiments involving all 15 samples accross the datasets, an interesting pattern emerged. The null hypothesis consistently failed to be rejected, observing very low negative log of FDR corrected p-values for all the human DLPFC dataset samples in the cases of SpaGCN and conST (Fig. 5, Column 1 and 3). This indicates that the inclusion of histological images did not result in significant improvements in clustering when combined with ST data for these methods (Table 3, column 1 and 3). Particularly noteworthy was the case of SpaGCN, where the negative log of adjusted p-values was close to zero (Fig. 5, Column 1), suggesting an exceedingly low likelihood of benefiting from histology images within this dataset. Conversely, for the breast cancer sample, conST demonstrated a substantial improvement when histology images were integrated, signifying their utility in this specific scenario. However, SpaGCN did not exhibit significant enhancements in this dataset. ScribbleDom, which employs a different approach by using scribbles on histology images contributed by human annotators rather than using histology images directly, displayed significant potential in domain detection. It showcased the significance of histology images in 7 out of the 12 samples (Fig. 5, Column 5) and also demonstrated the significance of using these scribbles in the human breast cancer dataset. To evaluate if this failure to show significant improvement with raw histology image in spatial domain detection is due to the low signal-to-noise ratio of the histology images, we applied the pre-trained SwinIR model to enhance the raw histology images for the same 15 samples. We ran spaGCN and conST with this enhanced image, instead of using a raw histology image. These findings strongly suggest that while image enhancement models like SwinIR can improve quality, this improvement does not translate into more effective feature extraction for the spatial domain detection algorithms tested.

Domain detection methods fail to detect relevant information from histology data

In the previous subsection, we observed that SpaGCN and conST did not show significant improvement in any of the 12 samples of the human DLPFC dataset along with melanoma and bcdc ffpe samples, whereas conST showed improvement when using both raw and enhanced histology images only in the human breast cancer dataset. To investigate why using histology images didn’t show significant improvement, we evaluated the quality of the histology images in these datasets by filtering the pixels under the tissue spots and clustering them using k-means clustering (details in Supplementary Algorithm 1). Even with this naive non-spatial clustering method, we found that although samples 151508, 151673, 151674, 151675, and 151676 from the human DLPFC dataset, human breast cancer, and Melanoma cancer samples showed significant signals, as indicated by reasonable ARI (More than 0.18) values compared to ground truth (details in Supplementary Table S2). This indicates that for these samples, the histology image has relevant information to the ground truth data. However, SpaGCN did not show significant improvement with the use of any of these histology images. ConST only demonstrated significant improvement with the histology image from the human breast cancer dataset, but they also didn’t show any improvement in other cases which has good histology image. In contrast, ScribbleDom demonstrated significant improvement in seven out of twelve samples (Fig. 5). This difference can be attributed to the workflow of ScribbleDom, which does not use histology images for spatial domain detection directly. Instead, it utilizes expert scribbles on histology images as representations, using these scribbles to identify spatial domains. This suggests that the failure of conST and SpaGCN to show improvement in these samples could be due to their inability to extract and integrate relevant features from raw histology images.

Discussion

In this work, we analyzed a suite of recent methods designed to integrate histology and ST data for spatial domain detection. While each tool possesses a unique implementation, they generally adhere to a common computational workflow. This process typically involves parallel feature extraction and integration from the gene expression matrix and corresponding histology image patches, followed by the incorporation of spatial context via a graph or matrix structure. Finally, a clustering algorithm is applied to this integrated representation to delineate spatial domains. To systematically test the assumption that histology images should improve performance in spatial domain detection, we designed a statistical framework. This test evaluates methods like SpaGCN, conST, and ScribbleDom by comparing their Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) scores when run with ST data alone versus with both ST data and a histology image or scribble on the image. Each condition was run with 20 different seeds to test the null hypothesis that performance is not superior when histology is included with ST data relative to when they use ST data alone. Our analysis yielded a counterintuitive primary result: the inclusion of raw histology images does not guarantee improved performance. This was most evident in the unsupervised methods; for the 12 human DLPFC samples, we found no significant performance improvement for either SpaGCN or conST. In contrast, the semi-supervised, scribble-based method ScribbleDom demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in 7 of the 12 DLPFC samples. Moreover, for the human breast cancer sample, both conST and ScribbleDom showed statistically significant improvement. In contrast, none of the methods yielded a significant performance gain for the melanoma or bcdc ffpe samples. To test if this result was related to image quality, we used SwinIR, an image enhancement method, which improved the average PSNR value of the histology images by 29.74. However, repeating the statistical tests for SpaGCN and conST with these algorithmically enhanced images yielded the same results; significant improvement was still only observed for conST on the human breast cancer sample. This is particularly noteworthy because we found that a naïve k-means clustering on the histology images alone shows a significant correlation with ground truth annotations (ARI> 0.15) for samples 151508, 151673, 151674, 151675, and 151676 from the human DLPFC dataset, as well as the human breast cancer and melanoma cancer samples (Supplementary Table S2). This suggests that while the histology images contain relevant information for these samples, this signal did not result in significant improvement for domain detection by the integration strategies of SpaGCN and conST.

The frequent failure of histology integration to improve performance can be attributed to several fundamental challenges. A primary challenge is the foundational mismatch between quasi-continuous histology images and the discrete, pixelated sampling framework of ST technologies [39] that makes the integration between two different data types complex. Also, the physical preparation of tissues, including slicing, transferring, and staining, can introduce significant non-linear stretching, compression, folding, and tearing. These artifacts disrupt the true spatial relationships between cells, making a perfect alignment of histology image with the gene expression data difficult [40]. Furthermore, a model’s performance can be undermined by domain shift, where even small variations in image characteristics like color, brightness, and contrast caused by differences in slide preparation, staining protocols, or scanner properties can result in large, detrimental differences in the model’s learned representation, leading to poor generalization on new data [41].

Despite these challenges, our findings elucidate several promising future directions. The success of ScribbleDom strongly suggests that semi-supervised, human-in-the-loop approaches are a powerful alternative to relying on automated feature extraction from raw pixel data. Future methods could also focus on more robust features from histology images, such as nuclear morphology [42], to obtain a clearer biological signal. The rise of large-scale pathology foundation models, fueled by the growth of public ST datasets [43], offers another promising avenue for extracting high-information embeddings to explore novel biomarkers [44, 45]. However, their effectiveness when fine-tuned on smaller ST datasets remains to be determined. While a formal benchmark of these nascent strategies is beyond the scope of this study, our statistical framework provides a robust methodology for their future validation. Newer methods can use it to formally determine if their approach to histology integration provides a significant, quantifiable benefit. It can also be used for ablation studies, allowing researchers to test the effect of each model component and determine its specific contribution in improving performance after histology integration. Finally, given the significant challenges of histology integration, ST technologies that do not rely on a matched histology image, such as MERFISH and seqFISH, should not be considered disadvantaged. Since methods like SpaGCN and conST do not show significant improvement with the inclusion of histology images, they can use these data for high-resolution spatial analysis.

Conclusion

In this study, we reviewed several spatial domain detection algorithm that uses histology images along with ST data and introduced a statistical framework to evaluate the utility of incorporating histology images into this task. Our results demonstrate that, for methods such as SpaGCN and conST, histology images did not consistently improve performance compared to using ST data alone. ScribbleDom, particularly when provided with scribble annotations, achieved higher performance; however, its utility was not guaranteed across all samples. These findings suggest that the added value of histology integration is method- and dataset-dependent, and not always superior to transcriptomics-only approaches. Future work should therefore focus on designing robust histology integration strategies that clearly demonstrate significant gains over ST data alone across diverse datasets and tissues.
