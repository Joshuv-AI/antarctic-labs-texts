The Janus State: Strong Photon Antibunching from a Superposition of Squeezed Vacua
Arash Azizi

Abstract
Fundamental principles of quantum mechanics, such as superposition, have long been used to create nonclassical “cat states” from classical-like components. We push this concept into the fully quantum regime by introducing the Janus State —a superposition of two squeezed vacuum states. Here, we report the discovery that this state, born from two photon-bunched components, exhibits strong photon antibunching. Our exact analytical theory reveals this emergent nonclassicality is driven by pure quantum interference, which suppresses two-photon events and drives the second-order coherence to g ( 2 ) ​ ( 0 ) ≈ 0.567 g^{(2)}(0)\approx 0.567 . This result fundamentally challenges the paradigm that antibunching requires non-Gaussian resources or post-selection. The Janus State thus constitutes a new foundational tool for quantum engineering, providing a deterministic and experimentally accessible route to generating highly nonclassical light.



Introduction.
—The landscape of quantum optics is defined by foundational states that illuminate the boundary between the quantum and classical worlds. The most celebrated nonclassical superposition is the Schrödinger cat state, which demonstrates profound quantum principles by superposing two maximally distinct, classical-like coherent states
Yurke and Stoler 1986
;
Brune et al. 1996
;
Ourjoumtsev et al. 2006
. This paradigm, however, has been confined to the classical-quantum boundary. It begs a fundamental question: what new physics emerges if this principle of superposition is applied to the next tier of the quantum hierarchy—the canonical
nonclassical
Gaussian states?

In this Letter, we answer this question by creating and analyzing for the first time a superposition of two squeezed vacuum states. We christen this new class of states the
“Janus State,”
after the two-faced Roman god who represents duality. The name is physically motivated: the state is a superposition of two squeezed vacuum components with opposing phase-space orientations—like two faces looking in opposite directions. Such a state is conceptually distinct from the well-known “squeezed cat state.” The latter involves superposing classical displacements (
\pm\alpha
) on top of a common background squeezing
Etesse et al. 2015
. In fundamental contrast, the Janus State is formed by superposing the squeezing orientations themselves upon the vacuum.

By exploring this uncharted territory, we uncover a remarkable and unexpected property. We show that the Janus State, formed from components that are themselves photon-bunched (
g^{(2)}(0)>1
), can exhibit strong photon antibunching (
g^{(2)}(0)<1
). By deriving the exact second-order coherence function, we identify the optimal conditions for this effect, revealing that quantum interference can drive the photon statistics into a deeply nonclassical regime, reaching a minimum of
g^{(2)}(0)\approx 0.567
. This result is significant because antibunching is a key resource for quantum technologies
Knill et al. 2001
;
Flamini et al. 2018
, and its generation from purely Gaussian resources, without post-selection or strong nonlinearities, has remained a major challenge.

Our approach, based on the direct, unconditional superposition of squeezed vacua, is fundamentally different from other strategies that have sought to generate nonclassical light from Gaussian systems. Those methods rely on distinct physical mechanisms such as engineering interference in driven, nonlinear cavities
Lemonde et al. 2014
, applying conditional measurements and post-selection
Jiang and Wei 2021
, interfering dissimilar states like squeezed light and coherent light on a beam splitter
Boddeda et al. 2019
, or analyzing the specific properties of displaced and thermal mixtures
Alexanian 2018
. The Janus State, therefore, represents a new and foundational route to engineered nonclassicality.

Experimental Feasibility.
—The experimental generation of the Janus State is well within reach of current capabilities. Single-mode squeezed vacuum states are routinely generated using optical parametric oscillators (OPOs) below threshold, achieving squeezing levels exceeding 10 dB
Manceau et al. 2017
. To form the desired Janus State, two such squeezed states with identical squeezing parameters and tunable quadrature phases can be combined interferometrically.

A Mach-Zehnder or fiber-based interferometer with phase stabilization allows coherent superposition of the squeezed modes with controlled relative amplitude and phase
Yoshikawa et al. 2008
;
Cogan et al. 2023
. The required parameters—relative phase
\Delta
, superposition amplitude
|\eta|
, and global phase
\delta
—can be implemented via piezo-controlled mirrors and electro-optic modulators, as demonstrated in recent continuous-variable experiments
Asavanant et al. 2019
;
Larsen et al. 2019
.

The nonorthogonality of the squeezed components is the crucial ingredient that allows quantum interference to alter photon statistics. Measurement of these correlations can be performed using Hanbury Brown–Twiss interferometry or photon-number-resolving detectors, which are now available with high quantum efficiency
Esmaeil Zadeh et al. 2021
. While losses and mode mismatch may reduce the observable antibunching, our calculations show that significant effects persist for moderate squeezing values (
r\sim 0.3-0.4
), which are easily achievable. The robustness of this scheme makes it promising for fundamental tests of quantum interference and for integration into quantum optical platforms.

Conclusion.
—In summary, we have introduced a new foundational class of quantum states, the
Janus State
, formed by the coherent superposition of two squeezed vacua. Our investigation into its properties has led to the primary discovery that this state, born from bunched components, exhibits the remarkable property of strong photon antibunching. Our exact analytical framework, culminating in Eq. (
5
), demonstrates that quantum interference can drive
g^{(2)}(0)
significantly below unity to
\approx 0.567
(Fig.
5
). This finding fundamentally challenges the view that antibunched light requires non-Gaussian operations. By providing an experimentally feasible pathway to the Janus State, our work opens a new field of quantum state engineering based on the interference of nonclassical Gaussian states, with wide-ranging implications for quantum technologies and fundamental physics.

Appendix A
What is
g^{(2)}
of superposition?

The study of quantum superpositions of non-orthogonal states is a cornerstone of quantum optics, with profound implications for photon statistics and nonclassical light generation. In this section, we address the central question: what is the second-order coherence
g^{(2)}
for a superposition of two squeezed states? Let us consider two squeezed states,
\ket{\xi}=\ket{r\, e^{i\theta}}
and
\ket{\zeta}=\ket{s\, e^{i\phi}}
, with arbitrary squeezing parameters and phases. The general form of their superposition can be written as

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle=\chi\ket{\xi}+\eta\ket{\zeta}.

(S1)

Here,
\chi
and
\eta
are complex amplitudes controlling the relative weight and phase of each constituent state.

Before analyzing the superposition, it is instructive to recall the photon statistics of a single squeezed state. For such a state, the well-known result for the normalized second-order correlation function reads

\displaystyle g^{(2)}(\xi)=\frac{\bra{\xi}a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}aa\ket{\xi}}{\left(\bra{\xi}a^{\dagger}a\ket{\xi}\right)^{2}}=3+\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}r},

(S2)

which demonstrates the enhanced photon bunching (
g^{(2)}>1
) typical of squeezed light. The underlying moments can be made explicit as

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{a^2 \ket{\xi}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\sinh^{2}r\left(3\sinh^{2}r+1\right),\qquad\absolutevalue{a \ket{\xi}}^{2}=\sinh^{2}r

\displaystyle g^{(2)}=

\displaystyle\frac{\absolutevalue{a^2 \ket{\xi}}^{2}}{\absolutevalue{a \ket{\xi}}^{4}}=3+\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}r}.

(S3)

Turning now to the superposed state
\ket{\psi}
given by Eq. (
S1
), we seek the corresponding second-order coherence
g^{(2)}(\psi)
. Namely,

\displaystyle g^{(2)}(\psi)=\frac{\bra{\psi}a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}aa\ket{\psi}}{\bra{\psi}a^{\dagger}a\ket{\psi}^{2}}.

(S4)

This expression involves both diagonal and cross terms in the numerator and denominator, reflecting interference and non-orthogonality between
\ket{\xi}
and
\ket{\zeta}
.

Let us examine the denominator, which encodes the mean photon number squared for
\ket{\psi}
. It can be conveniently rewritten as

\displaystyle\bra{\psi}a^{\dagger}a\ket{\psi}=\absolutevalue{a \ket{\psi}}^{2}=\absolutevalue{\chi a \ket{\xi}+ \eta a \ket{\zeta}}^{2}=|\chi|^{2}\absolutevalue{a \ket{\xi}}^{2}+|\eta|^{2}\absolutevalue{a \ket{\zeta}}^{2}+2\mathscr{Re}\left[\chi\eta^{*}\bra{\zeta}a^{\dagger}a\ket{\xi}\right],

(S5)

where the third term represents interference between the two squeezed states, and can be constructive or destructive depending on the amplitudes and phases.

Next, consider the numerator of
g^{(2)}(\psi)
, which involves the normally-ordered four-operator moment:

\displaystyle\bra{\psi}a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}aa\ket{\psi}=

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{a^2 \ket{\psi}}^{2}=\absolutevalue{\chi a^2 \ket{\xi}
+ \eta a^2 \ket{\zeta}}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\chi|^{2}\absolutevalue{a^2 \ket{\xi}}^{2}+|\eta|^{2}\absolutevalue{a^2 \ket{\zeta}}^{2}+2\mathscr{Re}\left[\chi\eta^{*}\bra{\zeta}a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}aa\ket{\xi}\right],

(S6)

Equation (
S6
) explicitly captures the interplay between the photon-pair amplitudes in each squeezed state and their mutual coherence.

Taken together, Eqs. (
S4
), (
S5
), and (
S6
) provide a complete and general analytic framework for evaluating
g^{(2)}
in an arbitrary superposition of two squeezed. As will be discussed in the subsequent sections, the cross terms—arising from non-orthogonality and relative phase—enable quantum statistical behavior that is fundamentally inaccessible to either squeezed state alone. This interference-induced modification of photon correlations is the central physical novelty of this work.

A.1
Cross terms for
g^{(2)}
of superposition

A key technical challenge in evaluating the second-order coherence for superpositions of non-orthogonal states is the careful treatment of cross terms. In the context of the superposition state
\ket{\psi}
defined in Eq. (
S1
), these cross terms arise due to the overlap between the squeezed states
\ket{\xi}
and
\ket{\zeta}
. Specifically, the evaluation of
g^{(2)}(\psi)
requires us to compute expectation values such as
\langle\xi|a^{\dagger}a|\zeta\rangle
and
\langle\xi|a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}aa|\zeta\rangle
, which capture quantum interference between the two constituent states:

\displaystyle\langle\xi|a^{\dagger}a|\zeta\rangle\qquad\text{and}\qquad\langle\xi|a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}aa|\zeta\rangle.

(S7)

To proceed, we first recall the standard representations for squeezed states. These states can be written in the exponential form
Azizi 2025b
:

\displaystyle|\xi\rangle=(\cosh r)^{-1/2}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\tanh r\,e^{i\theta}a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}\right)|0\rangle

\displaystyle|\zeta\rangle=(\cosh s)^{-1/2}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\tanh s\,e^{i\phi}a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}\right)|0\rangle.

(S8)

Here,
r
and
s
are the squeezing parameters, while
\theta
and
\phi
are the squeezing phases for each state. To simplify notation, we define the parameters
\alpha=-\tanh r\,e^{i\theta}
and
\beta=-\tanh s\,e^{i\phi}
, encapsulating both the amplitude and phase information. With these definitions, both squeezed states can be expanded explicitly in the Fock basis. This gives:

\displaystyle|\xi\rangle

\displaystyle=(1-|\alpha|^{2})^{1/4}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{n}\sqrt{(2n)!}\,|2n\rangle

\displaystyle|\zeta\rangle

\displaystyle=(1-|\beta|^{2})^{1/4}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}\left(-\frac{\beta}{2}\right)^{m}\sqrt{(2m)!}\,|2m\rangle.

(S9)

Equation (
S9
) shows that squeezed states consist only of even photon number components, with coefficients determined by the parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
.

Normalization and Overlap.

The calculation of the cross terms, and in particular the normalization of
\ket{\psi}
, crucially depends on the overlap
\langle\zeta|\xi\rangle
between the two squeezed states. This overlap, given their non-orthogonality, does not vanish and can be computed by direct substitution from Eq. (
S9
):

\displaystyle\langle\zeta|\xi\rangle=

\displaystyle(1-x)^{1/4}(1-y)^{1/4}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}\left(-\frac{\beta^{*}}{2}\right)^{m}\sqrt{(2m)!}\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{n}\sqrt{(2n)!}\,\langle 2m|2n\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-x)^{1/4}(1-y)^{1/4}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2n)!}{(n!)^{2}}\left(\frac{\alpha\beta^{*}}{4}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-x)^{1/4}(1-y)^{1/4}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2n-1)!!}{(2n)!!}\left(\alpha\beta^{*}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-|\alpha|^{2})^{1/4}(1-|\beta|^{2})^{1/4}(1-\alpha\beta^{*})^{-1/2},

(S10)

where,

\displaystyle x=

\displaystyle|\alpha|^{2}=\tanh^{2}r,\qquad(1-x)^{1/4}=(\cosh r)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle y=

\displaystyle|\beta|^{2}=\tanh^{2}s,\qquad(1-y)^{1/4}=(\cosh s)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle z=

\displaystyle\alpha\beta^{*}=\tanh r\,\tanh s\,e^{i(\theta-\phi)},

(S11)

and we have made use of the combinatorial identity

(1-z)^{-1/2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2n-1)!!}{(2n)!!}z^{n}.

(S12)

Equation (
S10
) provides a closed-form expression for the overlap in terms of the squeezing parameters and phases. Moreover, note that if
\xi=\zeta
, then
\alpha=\beta
, and hence Equation (
S10
) gives
\langle\xi|\xi\rangle=1

Consequently, the normalization of the superposed state
\ket{\psi}
, which must satisfy
\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1
, becomes

\displaystyle\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=

\displaystyle|\chi|^{2}\langle\xi|\xi\rangle+|\eta|^{2}\langle\zeta|\zeta\rangle+2\mathscr{Re}\Big[\chi\eta^{*}\langle\zeta|\xi\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\chi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2}+2\mathscr{Re}\Big[\chi\eta^{*}(1-x)^{1/4}(1-y)^{1/4}(1-z)^{-1/2}\Big],

(S13)

since, by construction, each squeezed state is normalized individually. Summarizing, the final normalization constraint takes the compact form

\displaystyle\boxed{\quad|\chi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2}+2\mathscr{Re}\Big[\chi\eta^{*}(1-x)^{1/4}(1-y)^{1/4}(1-z)^{-1/2}\Big]=1\,.\quad}

(S14)

A.2
Finding
\langle\zeta|a^{\dagger}a|\xi\rangle

A central step in the evaluation of the second-order coherence function for a superposition of squeezed states is the determination of off-diagonal expectation values, in particular
\langle\zeta|a^{\dagger}a|\xi\rangle
. This term encodes the quantum interference between the two constituent squeezed states in the observable photon statistics.

We begin with the explicit expression for the matrix element in operator form:

\displaystyle\langle\zeta|a^{\dagger}a|\xi\rangle=(\cosh r)^{-1/2}(\cosh s)^{-1/2}\langle 0|\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{*}aa\right)a^{\dagger}a\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\alpha a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}\right)|0\rangle,

(S15)

where we have written both squeezed in their exponential disentangled forms, following Eq. (
S8
).

To simplify the calculation, we first act with the annihilation operator
a
on the squeezed
\ket{\xi}
. As shown by direct differentiation, this yields:

\displaystyle a\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\alpha a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}\right)|0\rangle=\frac{\partial}{\partial a^{\dagger}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\alpha a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}\right)|0\rangle=-\alpha a^{\dagger}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\alpha a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}\right)|0\rangle.

(S16)

Analogously, acting with
a^{\dagger}
to the left on
\langle 0|\exp(-\frac{1}{2} \beta^* a a)
results in:

\displaystyle\langle 0|\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{*}aa\right)a^{\dagger}=-\beta^{*}\langle 0|\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{*}aa\right)a.

(S17)

Combining these results, Eq. (
S15
) reduces to

\displaystyle\langle\zeta|a^{\dagger}a|\xi\rangle=(\cosh r)^{-1/2}(\cosh s)^{-1/2}\alpha\beta^{*}\langle 0|\exp(-\frac{1}{2} \beta^* a a)aa\exp(-\frac{1}{2} \alpha a^{\dagger} a^{\dagger})|0\rangle.

(S18)

At this stage, it is advantageous to adopt a Fock basis expansion for both squeezed states, as in Eq. (
S9
), and then evaluate the matrix elements by summing over photon number. To this end, we note that:

\displaystyle a\ket{\xi}

\displaystyle=(1-x)^{1/4}\left(-\alpha\right)a^{\dagger}\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}\right)|0\rangle

\displaystyle=-\alpha(1-x)^{1/4}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{n}\sqrt{(2n+1)!}\,|2n+1\rangle,

(S19)

and, for the bra state,

\displaystyle\langle\zeta|a^{\dagger}=(1-y)^{1/4}\left(-\beta^{*}\right)\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}\left(-\frac{\beta^{*}}{2}\right)^{m}\sqrt{(2m+1)!}\langle 2m+1|,

(S20)

The matrix element can now be explicitly evaluated by the orthonormality of the Fock basis:

\displaystyle\langle\zeta|a^{\dagger}a|\xi\rangle=

\displaystyle\alpha\beta^{*}(1-x)^{1/4}(1-y)^{1/4}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{m,n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!n!}\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{n}\left(-\frac{\beta^{*}}{2}\right)^{m}\sqrt{(2n+1)!}\sqrt{(2m+1)!}\langle 2m+1|2n+1\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\beta^{*}(1-x)^{1/4}(1-y)^{1/4}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}\left(\frac{\alpha\beta^{*}}{4}\right)^{n}(2n+1)!,

(S21)

where the last equality follows since
\langle 2m+1|2n+1\rangle=\delta_{mn}
, thus collapsing the double sum.

Next, recognizing the structure of the series, we introduce the variable
z=\alpha\beta^{*}
, and use the known result for the generating function of factorial moments. Thus, the summation simplifies to:

\displaystyle\langle\zeta|a^{\dagger}a|\xi\rangle=\frac{(1-x)^{1/4}(1-y)^{1/4}}{(1-z)^{1/2}}\,\underbrace{z(1-z)^{1/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(2n+1)\frac{(2n-1)!!}{(2n)!!}z^{n}}_{\displaystyle\frac{z}{1-z}},

(S22)

where we have used the closed-form expression for the sum, which will be derived in Eq. (
S27
), yielding
\displaystyle\frac{z}{1-z}
. Collecting all prefactors, we arrive at the following result:

\displaystyle\boxed{\quad\langle\zeta|a^{\dagger}a|\xi\rangle=\frac{(1-x)^{1/4}(1-y)^{1/4}}{(1-z)^{3/2}}\,z\,.\quad}

(S23)

A.2.1
Finding closed forms for series

Using Taylor series, one can show that

\displaystyle(1-z)^{-1/2}=\sum C_{n}z^{n},\qquad C_{n}=\frac{(2n-1)!!}{(2n)!!}.

(S24)

Then we have

\displaystyle z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}(1-z)^{-1/2}=\sum C_{n}nz^{n},\qquad z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}(1-z)^{-1/2}=\sum C_{n}n^{2}z^{n}.

(S25)

Moreover,

\displaystyle z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}(1-z)^{-1/2}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}z(1-z)^{-3/2},

\displaystyle z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\Big(z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}(1-z)^{-1/2}\Big)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\Big(z(1-z)^{-3/2}\Big)=\frac{1}{2}z\left((1-z)^{-3/2}+\frac{3}{2}z(1-z)^{-5/2}\right).

(S26)

Now we have all ingredients to find the closed form fo the expressions appear in our calculation.

Closed form for
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(2n+1)\bm{C}_{n}z^{n}
.

Thus, using Eqs. (
S25
,
S26
) we have

\displaystyle z(1-z)^{1/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{n}(2n+1)C_{n}=

\displaystyle z(1-z)^{1/2}\big(2z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+1\big)(1-z)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z(1-z)^{1/2}\big(\frac{z}{1-z}+1\big)(1-z)^{-1/2}=\frac{z}{1-z}.

(S27)

Closed form for
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(2n+1)^{2}\bm{C}_{n}z^{n}
.

Again, using Eqs. (
S25
,
S26
) we have

\displaystyle z(1-z)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(2n+1)^{2}C_{n}z^{n}

\displaystyle=z(1-z)^{1/2}\left(4z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+4z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+1\right)(1-z)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=z(1-z)^{1/2}\Bigg\{2z\left((1-z)^{-3/2}+\frac{3}{2}z(1-z)^{-5/2}\right)+2z(1-z)^{-3/2}+(1-z)^{-1/2}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle=\frac{z(2z+1)}{(1-z)^{2}}.

(S28)

A.3
Finding
\langle\zeta|a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}aa|\xi\rangle

Having determined the cross term involving a single pair of creation and annihilation operators, we now proceed to compute the more intricate expectation value
\langle\zeta|a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}aa|\xi\rangle
.

The approach is analogous to the previous subsection, but with an additional application of ladder operators. We begin by acting twice with the annihilation operator
a
on the squeezed
|\xi\rangle
. Utilizing the Fock state expansion Eq. (
S19
),
a^{2}\ket{\xi}
reads

\displaystyle a^{2}\ket{\xi}=

\displaystyle a\left(a\ket{\xi}\right)=-\alpha(1-x)^{1/4}a\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{n}\sqrt{(2n+1)!}\,|2n+1\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\alpha(1-x)^{1/4}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{n}\sqrt{(2n+1)!}\sqrt{2n+1}|2n\rangle,

(S29)

where, we have simply used
a|2n+1\rangle=\sqrt{2n+1}|2n\rangle
. For the bra state, we similarly apply
a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}
to the squeezed
\langle\zeta|
, yielding:

\displaystyle\langle\zeta|a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}=-\beta^{*}(1-y)^{1/4}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}\left(-\frac{\beta^{*}}{2}\right)^{m}\sqrt{(2m+1)!}\sqrt{2m+1}\langle 2m|.

(S30)

We are now in a position to assemble the full cross term. Taking the inner product, and invoking the orthonormality of the Fock basis, we find:

\displaystyle\langle\zeta|(a^{\dagger})^{2}a^{2}|\xi\rangle=

\displaystyle\alpha\beta^{*}(1-x)^{1/4}(1-y)^{1/4}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{n,m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!m!}\left(-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{n}\left(-\frac{\beta^{*}}{2}\right)^{m}\sqrt{(2n+1)!(2m+1)!}\sqrt{2n+1}\sqrt{2m+1}\langle 2m|2n\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\beta^{*}(1-x)^{1/4}(1-y)^{1/4}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}\left(\frac{\alpha\beta^{*}}{4}\right)^{n}(2n+1)!(2n+1),

(S31)

where, as before,
\langle 2m|2n\rangle=\delta_{mn}
reduces the sum to a single index. Since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4^{n}(n!)^{2}}(2n+1)!(2n+1)=

\displaystyle(2n+1)^{2}\frac{(2n)!!(2n-1)!!}{2^{2n}(n!)^{2}}=(2n+1)^{2}\frac{2^{n}n!(2n-1)!!}{2^{2n}(n!)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2n+1)^{2}\frac{(2n-1)!!}{(2n)!!},

(S32)

using the closed form found in Eq. (
S28
) for
z=\alpha\beta^{*}
, we have

\displaystyle z(1-z)^{1/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(2n+1)^{2}\frac{(2n-1)!!}{(2n)!!}z^{n}=\frac{z(2z+1)}{(1-z)^{2}}.

(S33)

Putting all factors together, the final closed-form expression is obtained:

\displaystyle\boxed{\quad\langle\zeta|a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}aa|\xi\rangle=\frac{(1-x)^{1/4}(1-y)^{1/4}z(2z+1)}{(1-z)^{5/2}}.\quad}

(S34)

where
x
,
y
, and
z
are defined in Eq. (
S11
).

A.4
Finding
g^{(2)}
of squeezed state

Finally, let us determine the well-known second-order coherence,
g^{(2)}
, for a single squeezed state. This is achieved by setting
\xi=\zeta
in Eq (
S23
). Consequently, Eq (
S11
) yields
x=y=z=\absolutevalue{\alpha}^{2}
, and we find

\displaystyle\langle\xi|a^{\dagger}a|\xi\rangle=\frac{x}{1-x}.

(S35)

Moreover, Eq (
S34
) reduces to the following simple form for
\xi=\zeta
(i.e.,
x=y=z
):

\displaystyle\langle\xi|a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}aa|\xi\rangle=\frac{x(2x+1)}{(1-x)^{2}}.

(S36)

Hence,
g^{(2)}
for a single squeezed state is then

\displaystyle g^{(2)}(\xi)=\frac{\langle\xi|a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}aa|\xi\rangle}{\langle\xi|a^{\dagger}a|\xi\rangle^{2}}=\frac{2x+1}{x}=2+\coth^{2}{r}=3+\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}{r}}.

(S37)

A.5
Plugging into the superposition formulas

To systematically analyze the photon statistics of a superposition of two squeezed states, it is essential to first compile the key expectation values for a single squeezed state. These quantities provide the building blocks for the more general calculations.

For a squeezed state
|\xi\rangle
(with squeezing parameter
r
and phase
\theta
), we have:

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{a\ket{\xi}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\sinh^{2}r=\frac{x}{1-x},\qquad\qquad\absolutevalue{a^2\ket{\xi}}^{2}=\frac{x(2x+1)}{(1-x)^{2}},

\displaystyle|a\ket{\zeta}|^{2}=

\displaystyle\sinh^{2}s=\frac{y}{1-y},\qquad\qquad|a^{2}\ket{\zeta}|^{2}=\frac{y(2y+1)}{(1-y)^{2}},

\displaystyle\langle\zeta|a^{\dagger}a|\xi\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-x)^{1/4}(1-y)^{1/4}z}{(1-z)^{3/2}},

\displaystyle\langle\zeta|a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger}aa|\xi\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-x)^{1/4}(1-y)^{1/4}z(2z+1)}{(1-z)^{5/2}},

(S38)

where
x,y,z
are given by Eq. (
S11
). These expressions capture both the diagonal and off-diagonal (cross) terms necessary for photon correlation functions.

These results allow us to efficiently plug the relevant quantities into the superposition formulas. In particular, the normalization and photon number moments for the superposed state
|\psi\rangle=\chi|\xi\rangle+\eta|\zeta\rangle
can be written in closed analytic form. The mean photon number and its higher moments for
|\psi\rangle
take the forms:

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{a \ket{\psi}}^{2}=

\displaystyle|\chi|^{2}\frac{x}{1-x}+|\eta|^{2}\frac{y}{1-y}+2\,\mathscr{Re}\left[\chi\eta^{*}\frac{(1-x)^{1/4}(1-y)^{1/4}z}{(1-z)^{3/2}}\right]

(S39)

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{a^2 \ket{\psi}}^{2}=

\displaystyle|\chi|^{2}\frac{x(2x+1)}{(1-x)^{2}}+|\eta|^{2}\frac{y(2y+1)}{(1-y)^{2}}+2\,\mathscr{Re}\left[\chi\eta^{*}\frac{(1-x)^{1/4}(1-y)^{1/4}z(2z+1)}{(1-z)^{5/2}}\right]

(S40)

These formulas generalize the photon statistics for superposed squeezed states, capturing quantum interference between the two components. Finally
g^{(2)}(\psi)
can be found as:

\displaystyle\boxed{\quad g^{(2)}=\frac{|\chi|^{2}\displaystyle\frac{x(2x+1)}{(1-x)^{2}}+|\eta|^{2}\frac{y(2y+1)}{(1-y)^{2}}+2\,\mathscr{Re}\left[\chi\eta^{*}\frac{(1-x)^{1/4}(1-y)^{1/4}z(2z+1)}{(1-z)^{5/2}}\right]}{\Bigg\{|\chi|^{2}\displaystyle\frac{x}{1-x}+|\eta|^{2}\frac{y}{1-y}+2\,\mathscr{Re}\left[\chi\eta^{*}\frac{(1-x)^{1/4}(1-y)^{1/4}z}{(1-z)^{3/2}}\right]\Bigg\}^{2}},\quad}

(S41)

with the normalization given in Eq (
S14
).

Appendix B
Simplification: equal squeezing, phase offset

An important special case arises when both squeezed states have the same squeezing strength but differ in phase:
r=s
,
\theta\neq\phi
. This regime is particularly relevant for experiments in which the phase between two identical squeezed modes is controlled. The simplifications are:

\displaystyle x=y=\tanh^{2}r,\quad(1-x)^{1/4}=(1-y)^{1/4}=(\cosh r)^{-1/2},\quad z=xe^{i\Delta},

(S42)

where
\Delta=\theta-\phi
is the relative phase.

Now, the mean photon number in the superposed state Eq (
S39
) reduces to

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{a \ket{\psi}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{x}{1-x}\Big(|\chi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2}\Big)+2\,(1-x)^{1/2}\mathscr{Re}\left[\chi\eta^{*}\frac{z}{(1-z)^{3/2}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{x}{1-x}\Bigg\{|\chi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2}+2\,(1-x)^{3/2}\mathscr{Re}\left[\frac{\chi\eta^{*}e^{i\Delta}}{(1-z)^{3/2}}\right]\Bigg\}

(S43)

where the curly bracket groups the direct terms and the interference/cross terms, which encode the effect of quantum superposition. The second moment, the expression Eq (
S40
) becomes:

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{a^2 \ket{\psi}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{x(2x+1)}{(1-x)^{2}}\Big(|\chi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2}\Big)+2\,x(1-x)^{1/2}\mathscr{Re}\left[\chi\eta^{*}\frac{e^{i\Delta}(2z+1)}{(1-z)^{5/2}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{x(2x+1)}{(1-x)^{2}}\Bigg\{|\chi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2}+2\,(1-x)^{5/2}\mathscr{Re}\left[\frac{\chi\eta^{*}e^{i\Delta}}{(1-z)^{5/2}}\displaystyle\frac{2z+1}{2x+1}\right]\Bigg\}.

(S44)

The normalization of the superposed state Eq (
S14
) also takes a compact form:

\displaystyle\boxed{\quad|\chi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2}+2\mathscr{Re}\Big[\chi\eta^{*}\Big(\frac{1-x}{1-xe^{i\Delta}}\Big)^{1/2}\Big]=1\,.\quad}

(S45)

This ensures that
|\psi\rangle
is a normalized quantum state for any choice of superposition coefficients. Consequently, the second-order coherence function for the superposed state simplifies to:

\displaystyle\boxed{\quad g^{(2)}(\psi)=\frac{2x+1}{x}\,\frac{\Bigg\{|\chi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2}+2\,\mathscr{Re}\left[\chi\eta^{*}e^{i\Delta}\Big(\displaystyle\frac{2z+1}{2x+1}\Big)\,\displaystyle\Big(\frac{1-x}{1-z}\Big)^{5/2}\right]\Bigg\}}{\Bigg\{|\chi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2}+2\,\mathscr{Re}\left[\chi\eta^{*}e^{i\Delta}\,\displaystyle\Big(\frac{1-x}{1-z}\Big)^{3/2}\right]\Bigg\}^{2}}\,.\quad}

(S46)

Elementary algebra.

Let’s perform some elementary algebraic manipulations in the following equations:

\displaystyle\frac{x(2x+1)}{(1-x)^{2}}=\frac{\tanh^{2}r\left(2\tanh^{2}r+1\right)}{(1-\tanh^{2}r)^{2}}=\cosh^{4}r\frac{\sinh^{2}r}{\cosh^{2}r}\left(\frac{2\sinh^{2}r+\cosh^{2}r}{\cosh^{2}r}\right)

(S47)

Hence

\frac{x(2x+1)}{(1-x)^{2}}=\sinh^{2}r\,(3\sinh^{2}r+1).

Furthermore,
\displaystyle\frac{1-x}{1-z}
, which appears in nearly all cross-terms between the two squeezed states in the superposition, can be simplified as follows. Since
x=\tanh^{2}r
and
z=xe^{i\Delta}
(for phase difference
\Delta
), we can write

\displaystyle\frac{1-x}{1-z}=

\displaystyle\frac{1-x}{1-x\,e^{i\Delta}}=\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}r-\sinh^{2}r\,e^{i\Delta}}

(S48)

The denominator can be written as:

\displaystyle\cosh^{2}r-\sinh^{2}r\,e^{i\Delta}=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\cosh^{2}r-\sinh^{2}r\cos\Delta)-i\sinh^{2}r\sin\Delta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big((\cosh^{2}r-\sinh^{2}r\cos\Delta)^{2}+(\sinh^{2}r\sin\Delta)^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\times\exp{-i \tan^{-1} \left( \frac{\sinh^2 r \sin\Delta}
{\cosh^2 r - \sinh^2 r \cos\Delta} \right) }

(S49)

Thus, the denominator can be expressed as a product of a modulus (a real positive factor) and a pure phase, which we label
f(r,\Delta)^{1/2}
and
e^{i\gamma}
, respectively:

\displaystyle\cosh^{2}r-\sinh^{2}r\,e^{i\Delta}=

\displaystyle f(r,\Delta)^{1/2}e^{i\gamma}.

(S50)

This leads to the identity,

\displaystyle\boxed{\quad\frac{1-x}{1-z}=f(r,\Delta)^{-1/2}e^{-i\gamma},\quad}

(S51)

where

\displaystyle\boxed{\quad f(r,\Delta)=\cosh^{4}r-2\sinh^{2}r\cosh^{2}r\cos\Delta+\sinh^{4}r,\quad\gamma=-\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\sin\Delta}{\coth^{2}r-\cos\Delta}\right).\quad}

(S52)

Implications for normalization and
g^{(2)}(\psi)
:

With these simplifications, every normalization and photon correlation term involving
\displaystyle\frac{1-x}{1-z}
can now be systematically recast in terms of
f(r,\Delta)
and
\gamma
, leading to substantial analytical and numerical advantages. The normalization condition Eq (
S45
) becomes

\displaystyle|\chi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2}+2f(r,\Delta)^{-1/4}\mathscr{Re}\Big[\chi\eta^{*}e^{-\frac{i\gamma}{2}}\Big]=1\,.

(S53)

Substituting these analytic expressions into the original formula for
g^{(2)}(\psi)
, we arrive at the following general and compact result:

\displaystyle g^{(2)}(\psi)=

\displaystyle\frac{2x+1}{x}\,\Bigg\{1-2f(r,\Delta)^{-1/4}\mathscr{Re}\Big[\chi\eta^{*}e^{-\frac{i\gamma}{2}}\Big]+2\,f(r,\Delta)^{-3/4}\mathscr{Re}\left[\chi\eta^{*}e^{i\big(\Delta-\frac{3}{2}\gamma\big)}\,\right]\Bigg\}^{-2}

\displaystyle\Bigg\{1-2f(r,\Delta)^{-1/4}\mathscr{Re}\Big[\chi\eta^{*}e^{-\frac{i\gamma}{2}}\Big]+2\,f(r,\Delta)^{-5/4}\mathscr{Re}\left[\chi\eta^{*}e^{i\big(\Delta-\frac{5}{2}\gamma\big)}\Big(\displaystyle\frac{2xe^{i\Delta}+1}{2x+1}\Big)\right]\Bigg\}\,.

(S54)

B.1
Finding
g^{(2)}
in terms of the phase of coefficients

To further simplify and parametrize the superposition state, it is advantageous to express the coefficients
\chi
and
\eta
in terms of their magnitudes and relative phase. Without loss of generality, one can choose

\displaystyle\chi\in\mathbb{R},\qquad\eta=|\eta|e^{i\delta}

(S55)

where the overall phase of the quantum state
\ket{\psi}
can always be chosen arbitrarily and does not affect measurable quantities such as
g^{(2)}(\psi)
.

With this convention, the normalization condition for the state, as previously derived in Eq. (
S53
), now becomes

\displaystyle\boxed{|\chi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2}+2f(r,\Delta)^{-1/4}|\chi|\,|\eta|\,\cos\big(\delta+\frac{\gamma}{2}\big)=1}

(S56)

where
f(r,\Delta)
and
\gamma
are given by Eq. (
S52
).

With this parametrization, all relevant real parts appearing in
g^{(2)}
can be written explicitly as cosine functions:

\displaystyle\mathscr{Re}\Big[\chi\eta^{*}e^{-i\frac{\gamma}{2}}\Big]

\displaystyle=|\chi|\,|\eta|\cos\big(\delta+\frac{\gamma}{2}\big),

\displaystyle\mathscr{Re}\Big[\chi\eta^{*}e^{i(\Delta-\frac{3}{2}\gamma)}\Big]

\displaystyle=|\chi|\,|\eta|\cos\big(\Delta- \frac{3}{2}\gamma- \delta\big),

(S57)

\displaystyle\mathscr{Re}\left[\chi\eta^{*}e^{i\big(\Delta-\frac{5}{2}\gamma\big)}(2xe^{i\Delta}+1)\right]

\displaystyle=|\chi|\,|\eta|\left[2\tanh^{2}r\cos\big(2\Delta- \frac{5}{2}\gamma- \delta\big)+\cos\big(\Delta- \frac{5}{2}\gamma- \delta\big)\right],

where
x=\tanh^{2}r
. Combining all, the closed-form result for
g^{(2)}
in terms of the phase
\delta
reads:

\displaystyle g^{(2)}(\psi)=

\displaystyle\Big(3+\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}r}\Big)\Bigg\{1-2f(r,\Delta)^{-1/4}|\chi|\,|\eta|\cos\big(\delta+ \frac{\gamma}{2}\big)

(S58)

\displaystyle+2\frac{f(r,\Delta)^{-5/4}|\chi|\,|\eta|}{2\tanh^{2}r+1}\Big[2\tanh^{2}r\cos\big(2\Delta- \frac{5}{2}\gamma- \delta\big)+\cos\big(\Delta- \frac{5}{2}\gamma- \delta\big)\Big]\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\times\Bigg\{1-2f(r,\Delta)^{-1/4}|\chi|\,|\eta|\cos\big(\delta+ \frac{\gamma}{2}\big)+2f(r,\Delta)^{-3/4}|\chi|\,|\eta|\cos\big(\Delta- \frac{3}{2}\gamma- \delta\big)\Bigg\}^{-2}.

If either
|\chi|=0
or
|\eta|=0
, then, as expected, one recovers the familiar result for a single squeezed state:

\displaystyle g^{(2)}(\psi)=3+\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}r}.

(S59)

indicating that the nonclassicality (sub-Poissonian statistics) arises exclusively due to interference in the superposition when both components are present.

B.2
Choosing a specific phase for the superposition

Based on the plots, it appears that
\delta=\pi
and
\Delta=\pm\pi
minimizes
g^{(2)}
. Let’s focus specifically on this case. For this case, we have:

\displaystyle\chi=|\chi|,\qquad\eta=-|\eta|.

(S60)

The normalization condition becomes:

\displaystyle|\chi|^{2}+|\eta|^{2}-2\big(2\sinh^{2}r+1\big)^{-1/2}|\chi|\,|\eta|=1.

(S61)

Also, for
\Delta=\pi
:

\displaystyle f(r,\pi)=\big(2\sinh^{2}r+1\big)^{2},\quad\gamma=0.

(S62)

The second-order coherence function
g^{(2)}(\psi)
is then given by:

\displaystyle g^{(2)}(\psi)=

\displaystyle\Big(3+\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}r}\Big)\Bigg\{1+2\big(2\sinh^{2}r+1\big)^{-1/2}|\chi|\,|\eta|+2\big(2\sinh^{2}r+1\big)^{-3/2}|\chi|\,|\eta|\Bigg\}^{-2}

\displaystyle\Bigg\{1+2\big(2\sinh^{2}r+1\big)^{-1/2}|\chi|\,|\eta|+2\big(2\sinh^{2}r+1\big)^{-5/2}\frac{1-2\tanh^{2}r}{1+2\tanh^{2}r}\,|\chi|\,|\eta|\Bigg\}.

(S63)

We now have
g^{(2)}
subject to a constraint. Its minimum can be found using the Lagrange multiplier method. This is a very straightforward problem. For simplicity, let
|\chi|=s
and
|\eta|=t
, where
s,t>0
. Thus, we are dealing with three positive variables
r,s,t
, and the expression for
g^{(2)}(\psi)
becomes:

\displaystyle g^{(2)}(\psi)=

\displaystyle\Big(3+\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}r}\Big)\Bigg\{1+2st\big(2\sinh^{2}r+1\big)^{-1/2}+2st\big(2\sinh^{2}r+1\big)^{-3/2}\Bigg\}^{-2}

\displaystyle\Bigg\{1+2st\big(2\sinh^{2}r+1\big)^{-1/2}+2st\big(2\sinh^{2}r+1\big)^{-5/2}\frac{1-2\tanh^{2}r}{1+2\tanh^{2}r}\Bigg\},

(S64)

subject to the constraint:

\displaystyle\boxed{s^{2}+t^{2}-2st\big(2\sinh^{2}r+1\big)^{-1/2}=1.}

(S65)
