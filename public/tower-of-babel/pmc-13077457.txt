Pulmonary toxicity of immune checkpoint inhibitors
Authors: Sean Garvey, Parthiban Nadarajan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have brought about major advances in the treatment of cancer over the past 15 years. With a growing armoury of these immunotherapies available and an ever-expanding list of indications for their use, ICIs have become a standard of care in the treatment of cancers including nonsmall cell lung cancer, melanoma and renal cell carcinoma. ICIs are generally well tolerated by patients; however, they bring with them a novel class of toxicities, termed immune-related adverse events. ICI-associated pneumonitis (CIP) is among the most clinically important immune-related adverse events due to both its high incidence and associated morbidity. CIP is associated with a nonspecific clinical presentation, a heterogeneous imaging appearance and an unpredictable clinical course. In this review, we discuss the biology of immune checkpoint inhibition and the epidemiology, clinical manifestations and management of CIP. Additionally, we discuss less well described manifestations of ICI-related pulmonary toxicity: immune-mediated pleural effusion and sarcoid-like reactions.

Introduction

In 2010, a new era of anticancer therapies began when ipilimumab, a novel immune checkpoint-directed antibody, was shown to improve survival in patients with metastatic melanoma [1]. In the years since then, a host of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have quickly become available and are now embedded in first-line therapy for a wide range of solid and haematological malignancies. Drugs that target specific immune checkpoints including cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated antigen (CTLA)-4, programmed cell death protein (PD)-1, programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) and more recently, lymphocyte activation gene (Lag)-3, have all shown antitumour efficacy to date, and a number of other targets show promise in pre-clinical settings [2–4].

In tandem with the rapid expansion of indications and widespread use of these antibodies must come a recognition of their unique toxicity profile. ICIs act by inhibition of negative regulation at immune checkpoints, allowing potentiation of the host's T-cell-mediated antitumour response [5]. However, this immune upregulation can ultimately bring about unpredictable and clinically significant consequences, known as immune-related adverse events (irAEs). Although not fully understood, it is thought that these adverse events are related to disruption of the role immune checkpoints play in maintaining immune homeostasis [6]. irAEs are a relatively new issue in clinical medicine, and understanding of how to manage these events is evolving rapidly. These immune-related toxicities are a heterogeneous group of autoimmune phenomena which can affect any organ system, at any time, after ICI administration [7]. In this review, we focus on pulmonary irAEs, manifesting most frequently as ICI-associated pneumonitis (CIP). As ICIs are increasingly used in oncological settings, CIP is now encountered with increasing frequency by oncologists and pulmonary physicians. This is a particularly important irAE, due to both its high incidence and its significant associated morbidity. CIP is a leading cause of irAE-associated mortality in patients receiving ICIs [8, 9].

Immune checkpoint biology

Immune checkpoints are a family of regulatory molecules expressed by immune cells that act as gatekeepers of the immune response, fine-tuning immune homeostasis. Most immune checkpoints have inhibitory, immunosuppressives roles, dampening the activation of T-lymphocytes [10]. They regulate a broad spectrum of T-cell activity from proliferation and motility, to the expression of cytokines and cytotoxic molecules [11]. In normal physiology, these actions allow immune checkpoints to modulate the magnitude of the physiological immune response, maintaining self-tolerance and preventing overaction and autoimmunity.

The T-cell mediated immune response begins with T-cell activation, which requires two distinct signals. The first signal is brought about by the interaction between the T-cell receptor (TCR) and an antigen, presented by antigen-presenting cells (APCs). The second signal is an interaction between a T-cell costimulatory receptor, such as CD28, and one of its ligand proteins on the APC (CD80 or CD86) [12]. TCR engagement with CD28 results in activation of intracellular signalling pathways leading to increased production of cytokines such as interleukin-2, further supporting T-cell activation. During T-cell activation, immune checkpoints such as CTLA-4, PD-1 and Lag-3 are induced on the T-cell surface to limit overstimulation of the immune response [8]. CTLA-4 exerts its inhibitory effects by competing for the same APC ligands as CD28, preventing CD28 co-stimulation and so inhibiting T-cell proliferation [13]. PD-1 is expressed widely on activated T-cells as well as B-cells and natural killer cells. It engages with its APC ligand, PD-L1, resulting in disruption of TCR signalling molecules through dephosphorylation of proteins and other poorly understood mechanisms [14] (figure 1).

Cancer immunoediting describes the dynamic interaction between the immune system and cancer cells. This process involves three stages: elimination, equilibrium and escape. In the elimination stage, the immune system has the ability to identify and destroy nascent tumour cells. If elimination is not completely successful, the tumour enters a state of immune-mediated equilibrium where its growth is kept under control. Over time, this results in selection of less immunogenic variants. These edited variants can ultimately escape immune surveillance, leading to cancer proliferation [15, 16]. This process encourages tumours to co-opt immune checkpoint pathways as an important immune resistance mechanism. They can achieve this via upregulation of PD-L1 expression on the cell surface through both innate and adaptive immune resistance mechanisms [17].

Pathophysiology of checkpoint inhibitor pneumonitis

ICIs have shown oncological efficacy through targeting upregulated immune inhibitory pathways, thus reigniting the T-cell antitumour response. However, the effects of ICIs are not specific to the tumour-immune system interaction and they exert their influence at checkpoints throughout the broader immune system. Disruption of these pathways incurs the risk of autoimmune dysfunction, manifesting as irAEs.

The precise pathophysiology of irAEs remains largely elusive. Several mechanisms for immune dysfunction have been suggested including T-cell, autoantibody and cytokine responses [18]. CIP is marked by elevated levels of pro-inflammatory T-cell subsets, with CD4+ T-cells, type 17 T-helper (Th17.1) cells and central memory T-cells found in abundance in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) studies [19]. Central memory T-cells serve to rapidly augment the immune response to familiar antigens. When found to be circulating in larger numbers pre-treatment, they have been associated with more severe irAEs [20]. Additionally, both central memory and Th17.1 lymphocytes have been linked with steroid-refractoriness [21], often a clinical feature of CIP.

Autoantibody upregulation has also been implicated in irAE development. For example, circulating autoantibodies have been detected in patients who have developed immune thyroiditis and bullous pemphigoid after ICI therapy [22, 23]. In addition, autoantibodies specific to the development of CIP have been flagged, with anti-CD74 antibodies identified in one CIP cohort [24]. Altered cytokine balance may also play a role in pathogenesis with abundant numbers of pro-inflammatory cytokines noted in studies of BAL and serum samples of CIP patients [25, 26]. Further work here may yield biomarkers predictive of irAEs or potentially therapeutic targets.

Incidence

Initially, CIP was considered a relatively rare event. Meta-analysis of the initial clinical efficacy trials of ICIs in solid organ tumours reported overall incidence of CIP at 2.7%, with just 0.8% developing severe disease. Higher incidence was demonstrated in the nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC) subgroup (4.1%) and in the combination ICI therapy subgroup (6.6%) [27]. However, more recently, retrospective analyses of real-world data suggest that the incidence of CIP may be higher than suggested in those original clinical trials. In one cohort, irAEs occurred in 30% of NSCLC patients receiving ICIs, with CIP occurring in 10% [28]. Another group demonstrated severe CIP in 9.5% of NSCLC patients on ICIs and a signal for higher mortality in these patients [29]. In a third real-world cohort, multidisciplinary team consensus diagnosis of CIP was reached in 19% of ICI-treated patients. This group included combination ICI treatment, which again was associated with a higher incidence compared to monotherapy [30]. CIP can be a difficult diagnosis to make and the increased incidence in more recent real-world cohorts may represent greater physician awareness, the use of ICIs in combination with each other and with other cancer therapies and underrepresentation of higher-risk patients in the original randomised trials [31].

Risk factors

As with other irAEs, it is difficult to predict with certainty which patients will develop CIP; however, a number of risk factors have been identified to date. Broadly, these include demographics, background comorbidities and tumour type, stage and treatment [32] (figure 2).

Demographics

Demographics including advanced age, male sex and smoking history each confer an increase in risk of CIP. Current or former smokers exhibit particularly increased risk compared to never-smokers in both lung cancer and extrapulmonary cancers treated with ICIs. One study demonstrated an increased risk for smokers for all-grade and symptomatic pneumonitis and also showed a significant correlation with the amount of smoking, with an increased risk of CIP per additional 20 pack-years smoked [33].

Lung disease

A history of pulmonary comorbidity confers a significantly increased risk of CIP. Subgroup analysis of the landmark KEYNOTE-001 study of pembrolizumab in NSCLC found that pneumonitis was more common in those with obstructive airways disease compared to those without (5.4% versus 3.1%) [34]. Subsequently, this increased risk has been demonstrated across other tumour types where patients with COPD, asthma and bronchiectasis demonstrate not only a higher incidence of CIP, but also a high likelihood of recurrent pneumonitis [35].

Those patients with interstitial lung disease (ILD) have perhaps an even higher risk of CIP. However, evidence here is largely observational as these patients were excluded from prospective studies of ICI therapies [36]. Meta-analyses show a markedly increased risk (OR 3.24) for severe pneumonitis in ILD patients trialled on ICI therapy [37]. Pooled incidences of any grade and severe CIP (27% and 15%, respectively) were demonstrated in this cohort. In patients with idiopathic interstitial pneumonias treated with atezolizumab, the total incidence of CIP was 29.4%, but for those with radiological honeycombing, incidence was as high as 57.1%. This suggests a higher risk associated with usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) than with other ILD patterns [38]. CIP was fatal in 25% of this small cohort and a further 25% developed severe respiratory failure. As such, prudence is required when considering ICI therapy in patients with ILD. This is an important consideration particularly in lung cancer patients, where a history of UIP is also linked to less favourable outcomes after thoracic radiation and cytotoxic chemotherapy, making this a particularly difficult group of patients to treat.

Autoimmune disease

Patients with underlying autoimmune disease have not been included in clinical trials for ICI treatments, which, given the proposed mechanisms of irAE development, is based on an assumption that these patients are at higher risk of toxicity. Indeed, this theoretical risk has been borne out subsequently through retrospective analyses. In a systematic review including >500 patients treated with ICIs on a background of autoimmune disease, 44% had a flare of the pre-existing autoimmune disease, 24% developed de novo irAEs and 18% needed to discontinue ICI permanently because of toxicity [39]. The risk of autoimmune disease flare on ICI is highest for patients with psoriatic arthritis followed by rheumatoid arthritis and polymyalgia rheumatica [40]. However, while irAEs are more prominent in this cohort, data are limited on whether autoimmune disease increases risk of CIP specifically.

Radiotherapy

While ICIs and radiotherapy have been shown to have a synergistic antitumour response, this combination also carries a higher risk of CIP [41]. This is thought to be due to the cellular damage and changes in tumour microenvironment caused by radiation, combined with the reactivation of T-cells by ICIs, which leads to the release of large amounts of cytokines, damaging lung tissue through downstream signalling pathways [42]. In this cohort, it can be difficult to differentiate CIP from radiation pneumonitis, both of which usually become apparent in the first months after treatment initiation. Radiation parameters including treatment duration and technique are probably important factors for pneumonitis development. Similarly, timing of ICI commencement in patients undergoing radiation is important with earlier use of ICIs increasing the risk of pneumonitis in combination with chemoradiotherapy [43].

Cancer and drug-related factors

Among cancers where ICI therapy is indicated, NSCLC patients have the highest risk of CIP compared to others including melanoma and renal cell carcinoma [42]. This may be due to pre-existing lung conditions or exposure to other pneumotoxic therapies. Within NSCLC, squamous cell cancer histology is most frequently associated with CIP [44].

There is a difference between ICI subclasses in rate of pneumonitis. Overall, PD-1 inhibitors are most strongly implicated, followed by PD-L1 blockers, with CTLA-4 inhibitors least implicated [45, 46]. In clinical trials, CTLA-4 inhibitors carried an ∼1% risk of CIP, compared to 3% for PD-1 blockers [47], although the risk associated with both drug classes is known to be higher in a real-world setting. Combination immunotherapy with blockade of both pathways confers the highest risk of CIP, ∼10%, and is linked with faster onset and more severe pneumonitis [48].

Clinical presentation

The clinical presentation of CIP is nonspecific and may be acute, insidious or delayed with a prolonged latent period [49]. Given the high prevalence of irAEs in the ICI-treated population, the American Society of Clinical Oncology suggests that a high level of suspicion should be maintained that any new or suggestive symptoms are treatment related [50]. The median time to onset of pneumonitis after initiation of therapy is 34 weeks, but ranges from 1.5 to 127 weeks, and so this suspicion needs to be maintained long after treatment induction or even completion [51]. New or worsening respiratory symptoms including dyspnoea or cough are the most common presenting features. Other less common symptoms include fever and chest pain. A significant proportion of patients may be asymptomatic at the onset of pneumonitis, and incidental discovery during surveillance imaging is a common presentation. Patients may also present with symptoms related to other irAEs, as more than half of patients with pneumonitis may experience additional involvement in other organ systems [48]. Hypoxaemia may be noted, depending on severity of pneumonitis at presentation. Chest auscultation may reveal crackles, but often there will be no particular clinical signs.

Diagnosis

CIP has no pathognomonic clinical, radiological or pathological characteristics. Diagnosis is based on newly identified parenchymal opacities in patients receiving ICI therapy [52]. Clinicians rely on the temporal relationship between the administration of ICI therapy and the onset of lung injury as well as the exclusion of other causes for these changes [53]. All patients with suspected pneumonitis should undergo imaging with chest computed tomography (CT), as chest radiography will fail to identify pneumonitis changes in almost a quarter of cases [48]. The radiological features on chest CT are both heterogeneous and nonspecific, often resembling other conditions commonly encountered in patients with cancer. This results in a broad radiological differential diagnosis that can include pulmonary infection, oedema, haemorrhage, radiation pneumonitis and progression of cancer. As such, investigations to exclude other diagnoses should be performed in parallel including basic blood work, sputum sample for culture and screening for viral or opportunistic infections. Additionally, depending on the severity of the presentation, bronchoscopy with BAL and pulmonary function testing including diffusion capacity may also be performed [54].

Imaging

The individual CT features of CIP include a constellation of ground-glass opacities (GGOs), airspace consolidation, reticular opacities, centrilobular nodules, interlobular septal thickening, honeycombing and traction bronchiectasis [55]. These features together form radiographic patterns, based on the American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society classifications of interstitial pneumonias [56]. The typical patterns encountered are organising pneumonia, acute interstitial pneumonia (AIP)/acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) and hypersensitivity pneumonitis [49, 52] (figure 3). In many cases, there will be more than a single radiological pattern. In a study of 20 patients treated with nivolumab who developed CIP, organising pneumonia was the most common pattern found in 13 patients, followed by NSIP in three, hypersensitivity pneumonitis in two and AIP/ARDS in two patients. Each of these patterns was associated with a particular severity, with AIP/ARDS corresponding with highest grade pneumonitis, followed by organising pneumonia and then NSIP and hypersensitivity pneumonitis representing less severe clinical severity by pneumonitis grade [57].

Bronchoscopy

Although not routinely performed, flexible bronchoscopy with BAL is a useful tool in some patients with symptomatic pulmonary infiltrates and has been recommended in some clinical practice guidelines [54]. The decision to proceed to bronchoscopy should involve careful patient selection as the procedure carries risks, particularly in patients with higher-grade pneumonitis, and in most cases a diagnosis of CIP can be made with reasonable certainty without the need for invasive testing. When undertaken, bronchoscopy is primarily used to exclude other differentials, primarily infection. This is of particular importance as systemic immunosuppression with corticosteroids remains the cornerstone of CIP management [58]. Culture and PCR analysis of BAL fluid should be performed to evaluate for infectious causes of pneumonia, bearing in mind that negative cultures do not exclude infection definitively. BAL lymphocyte counts tend to increase significantly in CIP [19]. A BAL lymphocyte count >15% has been demonstrated in >80% of patients with CIP, irrespective of the radiological pattern observed. Lymphocyte counts >25% are strongly associated with drug-related ILD, organising pneumonia, NSIP, hypersensitivity pneumonitis or pulmonary granulomatous disease, and so this can be helpful in narrowing the differential diagnosis to a degree [59, 60]. Furthermore, bronchoscopy offers the opportunity to perform transbronchial lung biopsy in selected individuals. Pathology findings do not necessarily correspond to the patient's radiological patterns, with organising pneumonia observed on biopsy samples of patients with radiological NSIP and hypersensitivity pneumonitis [61]. As there is no diagnostic or pathognomonic histological pattern for CIP, transbronchial biopsy is rarely justified, unless needed for an alternative diagnosis.

Initial management

The general approach to management of CIP depends on the severity of the pneumonitis as determined by the Common Terminology for Adverse Events grading system (CTCAE version 5.0). This tool has been developed by the National Cancer Institute to standardise adverse event reporting with severity ranging from grade 1 (mild disease) to grade 5 (death) (table 1). Grossly, management of irAEs involving any organ tends to range from close observation in mild, asymptomatic disease to withdrawal of ICI therapy and systemic immunosuppression in more severe, higher-grade cases [62].

There are a number of general principles that can be applied to the management of irAEs which include prevention, anticipation, detection, treatment and monitoring [63]. Paramount to all of this is patient education prior to initiation of therapy [64]. Patients should have an awareness of the possibility of irAEs emerging at any time during or after completion of ICI treatment, and should be encouraged to report any new or worsening symptoms immediately to their treating team. They should be made aware of any particular toxicities which they are at a particular risk of developing. Baseline screening should be undertaken prior to commencement of ICI therapy to identify conditions that place patients at higher risk, such as lung or autoimmune disease in the case of CIP. Pre-treatment baseline biomarkers, including lung function testing in patients with respiratory diseases, can serve as a reference point for any new abnormality that occurs during the treatment phase. Active surveillance for irAEs during treatment is undertaken on a routine basis. There are no evidence-based biomarkers or investigations in this space at present and so surveillance for CIP is anchored on symptoms with opportunistic clinical examination and pulse oximetry monitoring [65, 66]. These measures together allow early identification of toxicities and prompt initiation of management to prevent evolution of disease and lessen the need for aggressive immunosuppression strategies [54].

Grade 1 pneumonitis lacks symptoms by definition and usually is detected as an incidental imaging finding. A conservative approach to management is taken here with frequent clinical observation. Patients are managed on an ambulatory basis with self-monitoring and reporting of symptoms every 2–3 days. Oximetry monitoring can be carried out at time of clinical reviews and short interval reimaging with chest CT is performed to assess for resolution or worsening of parenchymal changes. The American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) guidelines advise that ICI drugs can be continued in grade 1 CIP [50]. However, in practice, ICI therapy is often held temporarily in this cohort and reinitiated after radiological resolution of pneumonitis [48].

Patients who develop mild to moderate symptoms have grade 2 pneumonitis. In the majority of cases, this group may also be managed in an outpatient setting, with close observation and daily symptom monitoring. Immunotherapy is withheld and corticosteroids are commenced, although drug choice and dosing varies according to local practice. The European Society for Medical Oncology and ASCO guidelines both suggest prednisolone 1 mg·kg−1·day−1 as a standard starting dose [50, 54]. During steroid therapy, patients must be monitored for adverse effects including heightened infection risk, psychiatric disturbance and hyperglycaemia. As standard, they should be commenced on gastrointestinal prophylaxis, calcium and vitamin D supplementation and antibiotic prophylaxis against opportunistic Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia infection should be considered if steroid treatment continues beyond 4 weeks. Clinical improvement is generally expected within the first days of treatment, and steroids are generally tapered over 6 weeks. This regimen leads to full resolution of CIP in >80% of patients [67].

Management of grade 3–4 CIP requires hospitalisation. At this point, ICIs are permanently discontinued. Intravenous methylprednisolone is used, typically 1–2 mg·kg−1·day−1, again tapered over 6 weeks [50, 54]. Higher doses of pulsed methylprednisolone (up to 1000 mg·day−1) are sometimes used in the most severe cases, albeit with limited evidence. Additionally, patients are often covered with broad-spectrum antimicrobials in the initial stage, until infection can be adequately outruled. These patients need close inpatient monitoring for deterioration. Respiratory failure necessitating high-flow oxygen therapy or even invasive mechanical ventilation may be required and early discussions with intensive care unit colleagues is recommended. Recurrence of symptoms during the steroid taper is possible. In this case, the steroid dose is increased, returning to the most recent dose at which symptoms were controlled and after stabilisation, a more gradual taper over 3 months can be instigated [68].

Steroid-refractory disease

CIP that does not improve clinically with corticosteroid treatment and requires additional immunosuppression is deemed steroid-refractory. This is a significant problem with reported incidence of ∼20%, but even higher in some studies [69, 70]. This cohort carries a high mortality and the decision to escalate therapy typically needs to be made if clinical improvement is not noted after 48–72 h of steroid therapy. This scenario poses a significant therapeutic challenge as there is a paucity of evidence for adjunctive immunosuppressant options. Our knowledge is limited to case reports, small series and expert opinion, inferred largely from autoimmune conditions with pulmonary involvement.

Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) is increasingly recommended for severe and refractory CIP. Its favourable safety profile, ability to neutralise antibodies and bolster resistance to infections make this a popular agent, particularly in this often frail, immunocompromised population [66]. One case report demonstrated an excellent response to IVIg in grade 3 CIP, using a dose of 2 g·kg−1 divided over 5 days with eventual clinical remission [71].

Infliximab dampens the inflammatory cascade by reducing levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and has proven to be efficacious in a host of immune-mediated conditions. In studies of infliximab for steroid-refractory irAEs, the drug has shown some efficacy in checkpoint inhibitor-associated colitis particularly. However, results have been more mixed in CIP, with high rates of chronic pneumonitis and mortality in that cohort, varying between studies [69, 72]. One series looked at IVIg, infliximab or a combination of both. In this small group, all five patients who received infliximab died of refractory CIP or a complication of treatment. For those that received IVIg alone, the outcomes were more mixed, with a majority of patients improving or stabilising, but a large minority (43%) progressed and ultimately died [70].

A wide range of other agents including mycophenolate mofetil [73], cyclophosphamide [74] and tocilizumab [75] have been studied to date in steroid-refractory CIP. These studies have had similarly mixed outcomes with no outstanding candidate therapy emerging. Ultimately, randomised trials are urgently required to inform clinical decisions. Prospective clinical studies are underway for a number of therapeutic options including ruxolitinib, pirfenidone, rituximab and itacitinib [66]. However, so far there is no guideline-based consensus approach for where to reach when corticosteroids fail.

Outcomes

Chronic CIP

Most patients with ICI pneumonitis demonstrate symptomatic and radiographic resolution after a steroid taper; however, the long-term clinical course can be unpredictable. Chronic CIP is a separate, but closely linked, phenomenon to steroid-refractory CIP. This has been defined as persisting clinical or radiological abnormalities requiring a treatment duration beyond 12 weeks, with 14% of cases meeting this criterion and often requiring much longer courses of immunosuppression [76]. This may evolve after incomplete resolution of acute pneumonitis or represent a smouldering immune process with repeated return of symptoms upon steroid taper. Radiologically, chronic CIP is most commonly associated with an organising pneumonia pattern. This organising pneumonia pattern, as well as longer courses of ICI treatment, are risk factors for relapse of CIP after initial treatment course of steroids [77]. Almost 20% of patients with chronic CIP will continue to have symptoms for over a year with persistent radiological changes ranging from ground-glass to chronic fibrotic remodelling at this interval [78].

Resuming ICI therapy

After CIP resolves, consideration must be given as to whether restarting of ICI treatment is appropriate. The main concern with reinitiation is that of recurrent pneumonitis. Therefore, it is important to understand the scale of this risk to inform decision-making. Rechallenge with ICI is associated with a recurrence rate between 20% and 43% and is associated with a more severe CIP than the initial episode. Furthermore, 16–25% of patients develop recurrence despite complete discontinuation of the drug, unprovoked by rechallenge. Severe-grade CIP, rapid steroid taper and poor initial performance status are associated with a higher recurrence rate [79, 80]. Recurrent CIP is managed in the same way as the initial presentation.

Guidelines suggest that after resolution of grade 1 or 2 pneumonitis, if ICI was withheld, it is typically deemed safe to resume treatment, with close surveillance for recurrence. Conversely, ICI therapy should be permanently discontinued after life-threatening grade 4 pneumonitis, regardless of clinical resolution [54]. More nuance is required after an episode of grade 3 pneumonitis, balancing the risks and benefits in a detailed discussion with the patient. Given the significant risk of recurrence, the decision to resume ICI therapy depends on the severity of the index episode, the degree of initial steroid-responsiveness, the fitness of the patient and the clinical need for ongoing ICI treatment. Special caution is advised in patients with early-onset initial irAEs [50]. Additionally, patients who achieved a treatment response to ICI therapy before developing CIP may not gain a survival advantage with resumption of treatment [81].

Prognosis

The emergence of irAEs is reflective of a more vigorous activation of the immune system against tumour antigens, as well as healthy tissues. It has been shown that development of an irAE mandating delay or discontinuation of ICI therapy is associated with better ICI efficacy and significantly improved overall survival. This holds true even in the context of need for hospitalisation and systemic immunosuppression [82]. Clearly, this benefit is contingent on adequate resolution of the irAE. However, compared to other irAEs, the prognostic benefits to developing CIP are not as clear, with inconsistent findings reported. Although some studies find overall prognostic benefits even in high-grade CIP, other studies show worse outcomes in NSCLC patients and those who develop early-onset pneumonitis [9, 83, 84]. CIP is the most common fatal irAE among patients on PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors, accounting for 35% of immune-related deaths in this group. Fatal CIP is less common with CTLA-4 inhibitors and accounts for 5% of drug-related deaths here [85]. Patients admitted to the hospital for CIP carry a higher overall mortality and are less likely to have long-term survival [86].

ICI-associated pleural effusion

Compared to CIP, ICI-mediated pleural effusion (IPE) is a much rarer and less well described phenomenon. Evidence limited to case reports and the epidemiology of IPE is not yet well characterised. Clinically, IPE is likely to present with dyspnoea, or as an incidental pick-up on imaging. Effusions are often bilateral and sometimes described alongside pericardial effusion, as part of an immune-mediated serositis [58] (figure 4). These effusions tend to reaccumulate after drainage and patients may develop chronic symptoms.

Of course, the development of pleural effusion in a patient with cancer raises immediate concern for progression of disease. To avoid mislabelling, physicians must have an initial index of suspicion for IPE in the right clinical context and a thorough diagnostic work-up must be pursued. IPE is a diagnosis of exclusion, and pleural malignancy, pseudoprogression, infection and other more common causes of pleural effusion among patients with cancer must be outruled. Thoracentesis is a necessary first step in evaluation. In IPE, pleural fluid analysis is nonspecific and may demonstrate lymphocytosis predominance, but eosinophilia, neutrophilia and even chyle are reported [87, 88]. Pleural biopsy may subsequently be required to exclude other diagnoses, particularly cancer, in order to make a diagnosis of IPE with a degree of confidence [89]. Optimal management of IPE is as yet unclear. Some effusions will resolve with withdrawal of ICI therapy and immunosuppression, while others will be refractory to treatment and reaccumulate recurrently after drainage [90, 91]. In refractory cases, procedural interventions such as indwelling pleural catheter insertion or thoracoscopy and/or pleurodesis may be required to maintain adequate symptom control.

Sarcoid-like reactions

Sarcoid-like reactions (SLRs) are another irAE manifestation which can affect the lungs. They are characterised by noncaseating granulomas, distributed in a manner typical for sarcoidosis. Intrathoracic lymph nodes and the lungs are commonly affected as well as the skin, eyes and other organs. This phenomenon has been observed in 5–7% of patients following both CTLA-4 and PD-1 blockers in a range of solid organ cancers [58].

Patients with sarcoid-like lymphadenopathy tend to be asymptomatic, but may present with nonspecific respiratory complaints (cough, chest pain or fatigue). Cutaneous or ocular SLRs and other irAEs may be noted simultaneously and both will offer important diagnostic clues. Hilar and/or mediastinal lymphadenopathy is the most common radiological presentation, while the lung parenchyma may also be involved with micronodular opacities or GGOs. Of course, differentiating SLR from progression of disease is critical, particularly as many in this cohort have malignant thoracic lymphadenopathy prior to ICI treatment [92]. Without reliable biomarkers for diagnosis, histology remains the gold standard, and these lymph nodes are usually readily accessible via endobronchial ultrasound.

Overall, ICI-mediated SLRs tend to be of mild severity. As described previously, irAEs tend to be associated with improved overall survival and that is also the case with SLRs [82, 93]. Management of SLR is similar to initial management of CIP. Asymptomatic patients are managed conservatively and followed-up closely. More severe cases with multisystem involvement may necessitate interruption of ICI treatment, with or without a course of corticosteroids [94]. Radiological resolution of SLR is typically seen at a median interval of 3 months [95].

Future directions: artificial intelligence and radiomics

Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly becoming integrated into medical practice with machine-learning models capable of enhancing diagnostic accuracy, personalising patient management and improving efficiency of administrative tasks now widely available. In respiratory medicine, AI can now interpret pulmonary function tests, analyse CT and ultrasound images, integrate imaging with clinical data, monitor adherence and empower AI-supported self-management in patients with chronic respiratory conditions [96, 97].

Radiomics is the process of extracting large numbers of quantitative features from medical images to detect patterns not visible to the human eye [98]. This approach could bear fruit in CIP which presents with heterogeneous radiographic features, often clinically indistinguishable from infection, radiation pneumonitis or tumour progression [99]. Early studies here demonstrate that radiomics can successfully diagnose CIP from CT imaging data and can differentiate it from other causes of pneumonitis, including cases where clinician-based radiological assessment is inconclusive [100–102]. Radiomics, when integrated with clinical risk factors or deep-learning models, can even predict the probability of developing CIP prior to ICI initiation [103, 104]. Evidence is mounting that AI and radiomics have the potential to improve diagnostic accuracy and augment current treatment algorithms with a personalised approach to patient selection, treatment duration and decisions around treatment rechallenge in the near future. However, this will require prospective, multicentre validation before mainstream roll-out.

Methodological challenges

Significant clinical and methodological limitations constrain progress within CIP literature. Firstly, CIP is difficult to characterise, with nonspecific clinical and radiological presentations. Furthermore, studies are confounded by the variety of ICI drug-classes, concomitant use of other anticancer treatments, and a broad set of potentially overlapping differential diagnoses. As a diagnosis of exclusion, there is no accepted diagnostic definition of CIP and there are wide-ranging thresholds for various diagnostic practices between centres. There is also inconsistent terminology applied when discussing CIP, further hampering clear definition of clinical characteristics [105].

This heterogeneity makes CIP a difficult disease to study, and leads to methodological limitations inherent to the current literature. There are wide variations in reported incidence and outcomes in CIP, depending on study design, methods, cohort and definitions used. The small amount of prospective data available comes from drug efficacy trials of the individual ICIs. These trials excluded high-risk groups and were not powered to accurately evaluate CIP specifically. Outside of these trials, the majority of studies directly evaluating CIP are retrospective, relying on nonstandardised clinical practices, definitions, documentation and follow-up intervals. Suboptimal methodologies lead to bias within the study population, less robust CIP diagnoses and chance findings in small, single-centre cohorts. This results in the inconsistent findings between studies that have been seen in the CIP literature [106]. Given this complexity, multidisciplinary collaboration, larger multicentre studies and accessible central databases are needed to improve the accuracy and impact of the literature [105].

Conclusion

Pulmonary toxicity related to ICIs represents a novel clinical challenge for both pulmonary physicians and oncologists. As indications for ICIs continue to expand across tumour types, lines of therapy and treatment regimens, CIP has become an increasingly well recognised and consequential irAE. Understanding of the pathophysiology of CIP continues to evolve and we now know that mechanisms including dysregulated T-cell activity, autoantibodies and cytokine imbalance all contribute to this heterogenous syndrome. Early diagnosis is crucial and hinges on a high clinical suspicion, CT imaging and careful exclusion of differentials. Management is centred on timely immunosuppression with corticosteroids and yet, steroid-refractory and chronic CIP continue to pose therapeutic dilemmas. A major limitation in this field is a lack of prospective trials and heavy reliance on expert consensus for management. High-quality research into predictive and diagnostic biomarkers, optimised immunosuppressive strategies and safer approaches to ICI rechallenge will be essential to improving outcomes for this growing patient population.
