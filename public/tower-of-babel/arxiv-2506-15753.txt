Quantum Fisher-Preconditioned Reinforcement Learning: From Single-Qubit Control to Rayleigh-Fading Link Adaptation
Oluwaseyi Giwa , Muhammad Ahmed Mohsin , Muhammad Ali Jamshed

Abstract
In this letter, we propose Quantum-Preconditioned Policy Gradient (QPPG), a natural-gradient–based algorithm for link adaptation that whitens policy updates using the full inverse quantum Fisher information with Tikhonov regularization. QPPG bridges classical and quantum geometry, achieving stable learning even under noise. Evaluated on classical and quantum environments, including noisy single-qubit Gym tasks and Rayleigh-fading channels, QPPG converges 4 × \times faster than REINFORCE and sustains a 1dB gain under uncertainty. It reaches 90% return in 100 ± 12 100\pm 12 episodes with high noise robustness, showcasing the advantages of full QFI-based preconditioning for scalable quantum reinforcement learning.

I
Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) has achieved remarkable success in domains ranging from games to robotics by directly optimizing policy parameters via gradient ascent on expected returns. However, classical policy gradients struggle in environments with high stochasticity—such as quantum control tasks subject to measurement collapse and environmental noise—because sampling variance leads to slow convergence and unstable updates. In quantum reinforcement learning (QRL), where an agent must steer a qubit from
\ket{0}
to
\ket{1}
under depolarizing and dephasing noise, this variance is exacerbated by projective measurements and decoherence. To address these challenges, we introduce Quantum–Preconditioned Policy Gradients (QPPG), a novel algorithm that incorporates the full inverse quantum Fisher information (QFI) with Tikhonov regularization into the policy gradient update. QPPG “whitens” gradient directions according to the information geometry of the quantum state manifold, yielding more stable, sample-efficient learning under noise collapse.

Policy-gradient foundations date back to Williams’ REINFORCE algorithm
(
williams1992
)
, which estimates the gradient of expected return via Monte Carlo sampling. Classical natural policy gradients (NPG) extend this by preconditioning with the inverse Fisher information matrix to respect the curvature of the policy distribution, improving convergence stability
(
amari1998
;
kakade2001
)
nd inspiring state-of-the-art algorithms such as TRPO and PPO
(
schulman2015
;
schulman2017
)
.

In the quantum domain, Stokes
et al.
proposed quantum natural gradients (QNG) for variational circuits by leveraging the Fubini–Study metric (QFI) to accelerate convergence in noiseless bandit settings
(
stokes2020
)
. Meyer
et al.
applied QNG to single-qubit bandits, demonstrating faster learning than vanilla policy gradients
(
Meyer2022
)
, but highlighted that naively using QFI in multi-step Markov decision processes can introduce bias and neglect measurement–induced variance. Alternative QRL approaches include quantum deep Q-networks (Q-DQN), which train value-based agents using parameterized quantum circuits as function approximators
(
skolik2023
;
zhihao2023
)
, offering a contrasting paradigm to policy-gradient methods.

Building on these insights, QPPG explicitly constructs and inverts the full QFI matrix for the agent’s parameterized circuit state, adding a small Tikhonov regularizer to mitigate singularity under noisy estimates. In the limit of vanishing regularization, QPPG recovers the ideal quantum natural policy gradient, while in practice the regularization stabilizes updates against sampling fluctuations. To benchmark our method, we implement three agents sharing identical neural-circuit architectures—CPG (REINFORCE), QNPG (block-diagonal QFI scaling), and QPPG—as well as a Quantum DQN baseline, and evaluate them across five fixed seeds in both a two-state classical environment and a custom single-qubit Gym environment with depolarizing, amplitude-damping, and dephasing noise. Training curves, sample efficiency metrics, and noise-robustness tests demonstrate that QPPG converges faster and maintains higher success rates under elevated noise compared to all baselines, laying the groundwork for scalable, noise-resilient QRL in larger Hilbert spaces.

Section

Methodology

II-A

Reinforcement Learning Formulation

We model the control task as an MDP
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,R,\gamma)
, where at each
t
the agent in state
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
selects
a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
under policy
\pi_{\theta}(a|s)
, transitions with probability

P(s_{t+1}|s_{t},a_{t})\,,

(1)

and receives reward
r_{t}=R(s_{t},a_{t})
. The objective is

J(\theta)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}}\Bigl[\sum_{t=0}^{H}\gamma^{t}r_{t}\Bigr],

(2)

with discount
\gamma\in(0,1)
. Using the likelihood-ratio trick, the policy gradient is

\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_{t=0}^{H}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}|s_{t})\,G_{t}\Bigr],\quad G_{t}\!=\!\sum_{k=t}^{H}\gamma^{k-t}r_{k}.

(3)

II-B

Natural Policy Gradient and Quantum Fisher Information

Classical natural PG premultiplies the gradient by the inverse Fisher information matrix

F(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}^{\top}\bigr],\quad\Delta\theta=\alpha F(\theta)^{-1}\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta).

(4)

For a parameterized pure quantum state
|\psi(\theta)\rangle
, the quantum Fisher information (QFI) is given by the Fubini–Study metric

[G_{Q}(\theta)]_{ij}=4\,\mathrm{Re}\Bigl[\langle\partial_{i}\psi|\partial_{j}\psi\rangle-\langle\partial_{i}\psi|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\partial_{j}\psi\rangle\Bigr].

(5)

This defines the steepest-ascent direction on the state manifold
stokes2020
.

For a general (possibly mixed) quantum state
\rho(\theta)
, the QFI is most generally defined via the symmetric logarithmic derivatives (SLDs)
L_{i}
satisfying

\partial_{i}\rho=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\rho\,L_{i}+L_{i}\,\rho\bigr),

(6)

and

\bigl[G_{Q}(\theta)\bigr]_{ij}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Tr\bigl[\rho\,\{L_{i},L_{j}\}\bigr],

(7)

where
\{A,B\}=AB+BA
. In the pure-state limit
\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
, this reduces to the Fubini–Study form

G_{Q}^{\rm pure}{}_{ij}=4\,\real\!\Bigl[\langle\partial_{i}\psi|\partial_{j}\psi\rangle-\langle\partial_{i}\psi|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\partial_{j}\psi\rangle\Bigr].

(8)

(9)

Here
\xi
prevents singularity in noisy estimates of
G_{Q}

These findings confirm that quantum-aware gradient shaping substantially outperforms both classical policy gradients and a straightforward quantum value-based approach in this minimal single-qubit task. QNPG already captures much of the benefit, but QPPG’s explicit inversion and Tikhonov regularization sharpen and stabilize updates, explaining its top performance on both speed and noise tolerance. Although Q-DQN offers an alternative paradigm, its slower convergence and poorer high-noise success underscore that value-based quantum function approximation may require more sophisticated circuit designs or larger replay buffers to match policy-gradient methods in quantum control. Finally, while classical REINFORCE remains competitive under low noise, its vulnerability to measurement-induced variance makes it less suitable for realistic quantum systems.

Overall, QPPG represents a compelling middle ground—combining fast, geometry-aware learning with strong noise resilience—pointing toward its application in larger multi-qubit control settings.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced Quantum–Preconditioned Policy Gradients (QPPG), a natural-gradient–inspired algorithm that leverages the full inverse quantum Fisher information (QFI) with Tikhonov regularization to stabilize policy updates in noisy single-qubit control tasks. By benchmarking QPPG against classical REINFORCE (CPG), a simpler Quantum Natural PG (QNPG) baseline, and a value-based Quantum DQN (Q-DQN), we have demonstrated that QPPG delivers the best overall performance in both sample efficiency and noise robustness.

Specifically, QPPG required only
100\pm 12
episodes to reach 90% of its asymptotic reward—over four times faster than CPG and roughly three times faster than Q-DQN—and maintained a 90% success rate under elevated (15%) noise, outperforming all other methods. QNPG captured much of the geometric benefit (
120\pm 20
episodes to convergence), while Q-DQN offered an alternative quantum-value approach but lagged in both speed and noise tolerance.

These results underscore that:

•

Geometry matters: full QFI preconditioning yields faster, more stable learning than classical or heuristic quantum gradients.

•

Policy gradients remain competitive: even under stochastic measurement collapse, geometry-aware updates outperform straightforward value-based quantum models.

•

Noise resilience is critical: QPPG’s regularized inversion confers strong robustness, an essential feature for near-term quantum devices.

Looking ahead, extending QPPG to multi-qubit environments and exploring adaptive regularization schedules will be vital steps toward scalable, noise-resilient quantum reinforcement learning.
