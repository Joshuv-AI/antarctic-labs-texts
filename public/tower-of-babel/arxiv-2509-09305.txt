Hans Lee, Sergei Nayakshin, Richard A. Booth

Abstract
It is often argued that gravitational instability of realistic protoplanetary discs is only possible at distances larger than ∼ 50 \sim 50 au from the central star, requiring high disc masses and accretion rates, and that therefore disc fragmentation results in the production of brown dwarfs rather than gas giant planets. However, the effects of dust growth on opacity can be very significant but have not been taken into account systematically in the models of fragmenting discs. We employ dust opacity that depends on both temperature and maximum grain size to evaluate analytically the properties of a critically fragmenting protoplanetary disc. We find that dust growth may promote disc fragmentation at disc radii as small as ∼ 30 \sim 30 au. As a result, the critical disc masses and accretion rates are smaller, and the initial fragment masses are in the gas giant planet mass regime. While this suggests that formation of gas giant planets by disc fragmentation may be more likely than usually believed, we caution that numerical models of the process are needed to evaluate the effects not taken into account here, e.g., dust grain mobility and fragment evolution after disc fragmentation.



1
Introduction

Planet formation by Gravitational Instability
(
Helled et al. 2014
;
Kratter & Lodato 2016
, GI; see reviews by)
posits that young massive protoplanetary discs fragment gravitationally into clumps
(
Kuiper 1951
;
Boss 1998
, e.g.)
, which then evolve further to form planets
(
Nayakshin 2017a
)
or brown dwarfs
(
Stamatellos & Whitworth 2009
)
.

It is universally accepted that to fragment, discs must be massive and cool
(
Toomre 1964
)
, and also be able to cool rapidly
(
Gammie 2001
;
Meru & Bate 2010
;
Deng et al. 2017
)
; otherwise, a self-regulated gravito-turbulent state develops in which disc density perturbations in the disc constantly appear and dissolve away
(
Rice et al. 2005
;
Cossins et al. 2009
;
Paardekooper 2012
)
. The disc fragmentation conditions are believed to be satisfied only at large distances, i.e.,
R\gtrsim 50
au
(
Rafikov 2005
;
Clarke & Lodato 2009
)
. Giant planets discovered orbiting their stars on wide orbits
(
Marois et al. 2008
;
Marois et al. 2010
;
Vigan et al. 2021
;
Blunt et al. 2023
, e.g.)
may have formed via GI.

However, the potential of GI to make planets rather than brown dwarfs is frequently questioned since both analytical estimates
(
Kratter et al. 2010
;
Forgan & Rice 2011
)
and most numerical simulations
(
Stamatellos & Whitworth 2008
;
Zhu et al. 2012
;
Xu et al. 2024
)
indicate initial fragment masses
M_{\rm frag}\gtrsim 10{\,{\rm M}_{\rm J}}
, although note that some numerical studies do suggest that sub-Jovian mass gaseous objects can be formed by GI
(
Boley et al. 2010
;
Kubli et al. 2023
, e.g.)
.

Turbulence in protoplanetary discs may result in very rapid grain growth
(
Weidenschilling 1984
;
Dullemond & Dominik 2005
)
. A number of authors
(
Rice et al. 2004
;
Boley & Durisen 2010
;
Booth & Clarke 2016
;
Vorobyov & Elbakyan 2018
;
Vorobyov & Elbakyan 2019
, e.g.)
pointed out that dust in self-gravitating discs can grow to significant sizes,
a_{\rm max}\sim 1-100
cm in the first
\sim 0.1
Myr
(
Molyarova et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
. Furthermore, focused in spiral density arms
(
Rice et al. 2004
)
, large grains can become self-gravitating and collapse into massive solid cores
(
Gibbons et al. 2012
;
Gibbons et al. 2014
;
Baehr 2023
;
Longarini et al. 2023a
;
Longarini et al. 2023b
;
Rowther et al. 2024
)
, which could later turn into gas giant planets via gas accretion as in the Core Accretion scenario
(
Helled et al. 2014
, CA,)
or the Pebble Accretion Scenario
(
Baehr 2023
)
.

In this Letter, we draw attention to the complementary and potentially stronger effects of dust growth on planet formation by GI due to opacity effects. Although dust makes up approximately 1% of the mass of protoplanetary discs, it contributes
\gtrsim 99.9
% of the total opacity
(
Malygin et al. 2014
, e.g. cf. Fig. 5 in)
.

Dust opacity depends significantly on the distribution of particle sizes
(
Draine & Lee 1984
;
Woitke et al. 2016
, e.g.)
. For a collection of spherical dust particles with uniform size
a
and fixed total mass, its total geometric cross-sectional area scales as
\propto a^{-1}
, and therefore, the Rosseland mean opacity usually decreases with grain growth
(
Rosotti et al. 2019
, although the behavior of frequency-dependent opacity is more nuanced, e.g., see the “opacity cliff” in Fig. 1 in)
.

1
shows several commonly used Rosseland mean opacities for dusty discs. Except for opacities of
Zhu et al. 2021
, all of these consider only relatively small dust grains, with maximum grain sizes
a_{\rm max}\lesssim 10\mu

a_{\rm max}
, and can drop by one-two orders of magnitude in an important range of disc temperatures if grains can grow to sizes larger than
\sim 1

Zhu et al. 2021

2.2
Sublimation

Since the DIANA standard opacity calculation does not account for dust sublimation, we estimate how much opacity remains above each sublimation point using the results from
Pollack et al. 1994

\bigl(f_{i}+(1-f_{i})\exp(\dfrac{T-T_{i}}{10\,{\rm K}})\bigr)

3
Disc fragmentation with dust growth

In this section, we use the opacity function derived in §
2
to calculate the properties of a critically fragmenting disc.

3.1
Critically fragmenting discs

Pseudo-viscous disc models is a convenient framework to study the disc fragmentation boundary analytically
(
Rafikov 2005
;
Levin 2007
;
Clarke 2009
, e.g.)
, with their results being consistent with numerical simulations within a factor of order unity
(
Zhu et al. 2012
, e.g. fig. 2 in)
. In this approach, a marginally stable self-gravitating protoplanetary disc has the
Toomre 1964
parameter
Q=\dfrac{c_{s}\Omega}{\pi G\Sigma}\simeq 1
, which gives the sound speed
c_{s}=\dfrac{\pi G\Sigma}{\Omega}
, where
\Omega
is the orbital angular velocity. The disc mid-plane temperature is then

T=\dfrac{2m_{p}c_{s}^{2}}{k_{B}}=\dfrac{2m_{p}}{k_{B}}\Bigl(\dfrac{\pi G\Sigma}{\Omega}\Bigr)^{2}.

(2)

Further, in marginally stable discs, heating generated by gravitoturbulence is balanced by radiative cooling
(
Gammie 2001
)
.
Xu et al. 2024
show that the disc’s radiative cooling rate is well approximated in both optically thick and optically thin regimes by

F_{\rm cool}=8\sigma_{\rm B}(T^{4}-T_{\text{irr}}^{4})\dfrac{\tau}{(1+(0.875\tau^{2})^{0.45})^{1/0.45}},

(3)

where the optical depth of the disc
\tau=\kappa\Sigma/2
, and the disc temperature due to stellar irradiation heating
T_{\text{irr}}=\Bigl(\dfrac{0.1L_{*}}{4\pi r^{2}\sigma_{B}}\Bigr)^{1/4}

(
Zhu et al. 2012
)
.
The constant factor of 0.1 is a simple estimate of non-local disc irradiation effects, which depend on the disc global
H/R
profile and properties of the dust at the height of a few
H
above the disc midplane
(
Chiang & Goldreich 1997
, e.g.,)
. These effects, including possible disc self-shadowing
(
Bell 1999
, e.g.,)
, cannot be computed in a local disc stability calculation. Nonetheless, our choice of 0.1 is consistent with the flaring angle in our calculations within a factor of order unity. Supplementary materials provide examples of how our results vary depending on the choice of
L_{*}
, which is equivalent to varying the irradiation angle.

On the other hand, the heating rate due to gravitoturbulence with the corresponding viscosity parameter
\alpha
is given by

F_{\rm heat}=\dfrac{3}{8\pi}\Omega^{2}\dot{M}=\dfrac{9}{8}\alpha\Omega c_{s}^{2}\Sigma=\dfrac{9}{8}\dfrac{\alpha(\pi G)^{2}\Sigma^{3}}{\Omega}.

(4)

Setting
F_{\rm cool}=F_{\rm heat}
for a self-regulating disc, one obtains an equation consisting only of
T
,
\Omega
,
\alpha
, and physical constants. This equation can be solved numerically to find the critical mid-plane temperature
T_{\text{crit}}
of the disc as a function of
\Omega
for a fixed
\alpha

(
Levin 2007
)
. Since we are interested in disc fragmentation, we set
\alpha=\alpha_{\text{crit}}\simeq 0.06
, above which the disc fragments
(
Rice et al. 2005
)
. Note that, in the language of self-gravitating discs in thermal balance between turbulent heating and radiative cooling, this choice of
\alpha_{\rm c}
corresponds to the “
\beta
” cooling parameter of
\beta=4/[9\gamma(\gamma-1)\alpha_{\rm c}]\approx 7

(
Gammie 2001
, cf. eq. 20 in)
for
\gamma=5/3

(
Boley et al. 2007
, as appropriate for the cold regions of the disc;)
.

From that, we calculate the corresponding disc scale height,
H_{\text{crit}}=0.69\dfrac{c_{s}}{\Omega}

(
Kratter & Lodato 2016
, eq. 11 with
Q=1
)
, surface density,
\Sigma_{\text{crit}}
(cf. equation
2
), and thus arrive at the critical accretion rate above which the disc should fragment
(
Clarke 2009
;
Zhu et al. 2012
, e.g.,)
, as well as the initial fragment mass, which we take to be
M_{\text{frag}}=57\Sigma_{\text{crit}}H_{\text{crit}}^{2}

(
Xu et al. 2024
)
. While the factor in front of
\Sigma_{\text{crit}}H_{\text{crit}}^{2}
differs between different studies by an order of magnitude
(
Kratter & Lodato 2016
, see)
, here we follow the larger value derived by
Xu et al. 2024
in their radiation hydrodynamics simulations, which leads to a more conservative estimate of the initial fragment masses.

The maximum grain size in a marginally stable disc depends on many parameters of the problem and is a non-local quantity due to dust dynamics being a function of grain size
(
Rice et al. 2004
;
Boley & Durisen 2010
;
Booth & Clarke 2016
, e.g.,)
. Below, we consider four representative scenarios to evaluate the effects of dust growth on disc fragmentation. Two of the cases assume a fixed
a_{\rm max}
independent of disc radius,
R
,
a_{\rm max}=10\,\mu
m and
a_{\rm max}=1
cm, respectively, roughly indicating the “no growth” and the significant grain growth limits.

For the third case, we estimate dust growth limited by the grain fragmentation velocity, which we take to be
v_{\rm frag}=10
m s
-1
. We calculate the maximum dust size as
a_{\rm max}=\dfrac{2\Sigma}{\pi\alpha\rho_{s}}\dfrac{v_{\rm frag}^{2}}{c_{s}^{2}}

(
Birnstiel et al. 2009
)
, where
\rho_{s}
is the material density of solid dust, which we set
3
g cm
-3
here. This model assumes that similarly sized grain collisions dominate both grain growth and fragmentation, and occur with relative velocity
\Delta v\sim\sqrt{\alpha\,\rm St}\,c_{s}
. Other mechanisms of grain growth such as sweep-up of small grains by larger grains
(
Xu & Armitage 2023
)
may allow for growth significantly beyond this barrier, but we do not consider these effects here.

Finally, we use
\text{St}=0.1
as an optimistic upper limit to the grain sizes for our fourth case because previous work has shown that self-gravity produces particle motions that are correlated when
\text{St}<1

(
Booth & Clarke 2016
, e.g.)
. As a result, collisions may occur at lower velocities than those estimated by the
Birnstiel et al. 2009
formulae, unless the large-scale motions associated with the spirals can be efficiently converted into small-scale turbulence. While small-scale motion has been identified in high-resolution 3D simulations
(
Riols et al. 2017
;
Booth & Clarke 2019
)
, its effect on the dust growth has not been quantified. Instead, we estimate the most optimistic case by assuming that the only source of collisions between dust grains is due to the differential motion created by the spiral-induced pressure gradients. Following the arguments laid out in
Booth & Clarke 2016
, this produces
\text{St}\lesssim 0.1
for our parameters. The maximum dust size is then found via
a_{\rm max}=2\Sigma\text{St}/(\pi\rho_{\rm s})
.

4
Summary and Discussion

We investigated the effects of dust growth on disc fragmentation conditions. We find that dust growth beyond
a_{\rm max}\sim 1
mm reduces the disc opacity sufficiently to allow fragmentation to occur in colder, less massive, discs, and at smaller radii, i.e.,
R\sim 30
versus
R\sim 60
au (cf. Fig.
4
) for the standard ISM dust opacity. Dust growth also reduces the initial fragment mass (Fig.
5
). As a result, disc fragmentation on planetary mass clumps,
M_{\rm frag}\leq 10{\,{\rm M}_{\rm J}}
, may be more likely than usually concluded, but we note that our analytical calculations rely on a number of assumptions; numerical simulations are needed to explore the problem further.

We assumed that dust properties depend only on the radial coordinate, and that the metallicity (which encompasses both solids and vaporised materials) is constant throughout the disc volume. Numerical simulations show large inhomogeneities in the distribution of both solids
(
Boley & Durisen 2010
;
Forgan & Rice 2011
;
Booth & Clarke 2016
;
Longarini et al. 2023b
;
Birnstiel 2024
, e.g.,)
and volatile materials
(
Molyarova et al. 2021
)
in self-gravitating discs. These effects are multi-faceted and future dedicated numerical studies are required. For example, vertical dust settling to the disc midplane may reduce disc column-averaged dust opacity even further compared to our estimations. However, preferential dust accumulation in spiral density arms may increase the opacity there, making these regions less likely to fragment onto clumps.

Furthermore, simulations show that after forming, the fragments evolve significantly within the disc. The fragments gain mass by merging
(
Hall et al. 2017
)
or by accreting material from the disc
(
Zhu et al. 2012
;
Stamatellos & Inutsuka 2018
)
, yet they can lose mass due to tidal effects
(
Nayakshin 2017b
;
Vorobyov & Elbakyan 2018
, e.g.)
. Therefore, simulations are required to establish the eventual outcome of disc fragmentation. We note in passing that grain growth will also affect gas accretion onto the fragments since gas can only accrete onto a planet if the cooling rate is sufficiently high
(
Pollack et al. 1996
;
Ayliffe & Bate 2009
)
.
