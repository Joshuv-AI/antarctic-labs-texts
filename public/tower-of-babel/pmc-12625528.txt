A missed opportunity for preventing CMMRD: is it time to include Lynch syndrome genes in prenatal genetic testing?
Authors: Geethu Nair, Nicola Cadenas, Aparajita Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis manuscript highlights the importance of incorporating Lynch syndrome-related genes into prenatal genetic testing to facilitate early detection of constitutional mismatch repair deficiency syndrome (CMMRD). CMMRD, linked to biallelic pathogenic variants in mismatch repair genes, is an aggressive hereditary cancer syndrome. A couple conceived through in vitro fertilisation (IVF), unaware they were carriers of Lynch syndrome. Their child, initially healthy, was diagnosed with acute bilineal leukaemia and later found to have CMMRD. His diagnosis highlighted gaps in prenatal testing. Advances in next-generation sequencing enable broader genetic screening, offering parents crucial risk assessment tools, especially those undergoing IVF, to make informed reproductive choices.

Background

Prenatal genetic testing plays a vital role in reproductive planning for many prospective parents. Traditionally, such testing has focused on identifying genes associated with significant risk for disease during pregnancy and childhood.

Currently, constitutional mismatch repair deficiency syndrome (CMMRD)-related genes are not included in prenatal genetic testing panels.

CMMRD arises from biallelic pathogenic variants/likely pathogenic variants (PV/LPV) in mismatch repair (MMR) genes (MLH1, MSH2/EPCAM, MSH6 or PMS2) and is recognised as one of the most aggressive hereditary cancer syndromes.1 Affected individuals face a very high incidence of malignancy and death in childhood. CMMRD is a complex condition and can rarely present with cancers in early or late adulthood as well.2

Brain cancer, leukaemia, lymphoma and gastrointestinal adenocarcinoma are some of the most common conditions associated with CMMRD.2 The incidence of CMMRD is estimated to be one in a million.3

Monoallelic PV/LPV in the same mismatch repair genes are associated with Lynch syndrome, a more common hereditary cancer syndrome with an estimated prevalence of 1:354 in the general population.4 Lynch syndrome primarily predisposes to adult-onset cancers, but it can occasionally lead to malignancies in adolescence as well.5

Case presentation

A couple in their 40s conceived their first child through in vitro fertilisation (IVF) after being unable to conceive naturally for several years. Standard prenatal genetic testing yielded negative results, and the implanted embryo resulted in the birth of an apparently healthy child. The child was diagnosed with acute bilineal leukaemia before he turned 1 year of age. Unfortunately, the disease progressed despite multiple cycles of chemotherapy. After exhausting treatment options, the child was transitioned to hospice care and passed away before his second birthday.

During the oncologic workup, the child was found to carry biallelic pathogenic variants in PMS2, consistent with a diagnosis of CMMRD. This triggered germline genetic testing for parents. The parents were non-consanguineous but were found to be undiagnosed carriers for Lynch syndrome, with each carrying a pathogenic variant in the PMS2 gene. Mother had a single pathogenic variant in the PMS2 gene: c.2500_2501delinsG (p.Met834Glyfs*17), while the father had a different pathogenic variant, PMS2 c.1859_1860insAT(p.Phe620Leufs*4).

Both parents had no known Lynch syndrome history but had multiple family cancers diagnosed after age 50. The mother’s father had bladder cancer, and her maternal grandmother had breast cancer. The father’s mother had breast cancer; his paternal grandmother had ovarian cancer, and both his paternal grandfather and paternal uncle had colon cancer.

The parents, devastated by the loss of their child, expressed frustration that standard prenatal testing did not include Lynch syndrome-related genes. Knowledge of these variants could have informed their selection of embryos and potentially altered the course of events. Given their advanced age, they chose not to pursue additional pregnancies. Currently, the parents are enrolled in Lynch syndrome surveillance programmes.

PMS2 and MSH6-associated Lynch syndrome have reduced penetrance, often lacking a strong family history of cancers, with over 60–77% of cases remaining undiagnosed4 6. In patients undergoing prenatal genetic testing, the detection of Lynch syndrome/CMMRD-related PV/LPV could help parents with risk assessment and help them make informed choices. Identification of the underlying pathogenic variants also provides critical information for parents, extended family members and at-risk siblings of the baby, who face a 25% likelihood of inheriting CMMRD if both parents carry a pathogenic MMR gene variant. Additionally, established guidelines exist for surveillance and immune-based therapies for CMMRD.7 This is particularly important in communities where consanguinity is more prevalent. A case report from Pakistan demonstrates the utility of prenatal genetic diagnosis of CMMRD in a family with a history of multiple cases of childhood brain tumours.8

Adding CMMRD-related genes to prenatal carrier testing in the USA can raise significant ethical concerns. Carrier testing primarily aims to identify couples at risk for having a child with CMMRD. However, as mentioned above, prenatal testing may inadvertently reveal parental Lynch Syndrome risk, may influence reproductive decision-making and can potentially increase anxiety. Testing for adult-onset conditions in a fetus raises questions about autonomy, psychological impact and parental decision-making. Professional guidelines (American Academy of Paediatrics,9 National Society of Genetic Counsellors) recommend deferring predictive testing for adult-onset conditions until adulthood to preserve child autonomy. Ethical testing involves obtaining informed consent, providing both pre- and post-test genetic counselling, and ensuring that privacy protections are in place, as mandated by the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA). Genetic counselling helps clarify inheritance patterns, potential outcomes and limitations, including variants of uncertain significance.

Adding CMMRD-related genes to prenatal testing also requires several practical considerations. Currently, most carrier panels and cancer panels are run on separate laboratory platforms; therefore, inclusion would require either ordering two separate tests or expanding carrier panels to incorporate Lynch syndrome genes, while also addressing technical challenges related to PMS2 sequencing. Advances in next-generation sequencing technology have revolutionised genetic testing, making large multigene panels (including >100 cancer-related genes) widely accessible and affordable.10 Many such panels are now available for less than $250. Additionally, a robust counselling infrastructure is necessary to ensure that families receive appropriate pre- and post-test guidance. Workflow integration should include incorporating results into electronic health records with decision-support tools, educating providers and patients and coordinating with payers and regulatory bodies to ensure reimbursement and compliance with Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments and Food and Drug Administration standards.

In summary, while Lynch syndrome predominantly predisposes to adult-onset cancers, the association of its genes with CMMRD highlights the need for including Lynch syndrome-related genes in prenatal testing panels. By doing so, we can reduce the burden of this devastating childhood cancer syndrome.
