Cross-species metabolic characterization of eutylone and mechanism-based inhibition of CYP2D6 and CYP2B6 with drug interaction implications
Authors: Min Seo Lee, Im-Sook Song, Yeonsu Jang, Yong-Yeon Cho, Joo Young Lee, Han Chang Kang, Ju-Hyun Kim, Hye Suk Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEutylone is a methylenedioxy-substituted synthetic cathinone increasingly detected in toxicological case analyses and commonly encountered under polydrug use conditions. To support metabolism-informed surveillance and risk assessment of emerging stimulants, this study comprehensively characterized eutylone disposition and interaction liability using plasma protein binding, metabolic stability in liver microsomes and hepatocytes across four species, untargeted feature-based molecular networking-based metabolite profiling, CYP phenotyping, and reversible and time-dependent inhibition assays. Eutylone exhibited moderate plasma protein binding (fu 0.38–0.51) and pronounced species differences in clearance, with high hepatic extraction in rat hepatocytes but only moderate extraction in human, mouse, and dog hepatocytes, indicating limited translatability of rodent data. Twenty metabolites were identified across phase I and phase II pathways, including O-demethylenation, N-deethylation, β-ketone reduction, hydroxylation, O-methylation, glucuronidation, and sulfation. While sixteen metabolites were conserved across species, four O-methylated and hydroxylated catechol metabolites were detected exclusively in rodents, and molecular networking revealed higher overall metabolite formation in rat hepatocytes. CYP2D6 was the primary enzyme mediating eutylone O-demethylenation, with secondary contributions from CYP2C19 and CYP2B6. Notably, eutylone caused time-dependent inhibition of CYP2D6 with Ki = 4.2 µM and kinact = 0.069 min−1, and of CYP2B6 with Ki = 32.9 µM and kinact = 0.080 min−1, without any measurable inhibition of UGT enzymes. This mechanism-based inhibition, comparable in efficiency to that reported for MDMA, suggests a clinically relevant potential to increase exposure to co-used CYP2D6 and CYP2B6 substrates. Together, these findings provide a translationally relevant metabolic framework for eutylone, highlight critical rat–human differences, and identify interaction-driven risks that are particularly relative in the current landscape of polydrug misuse.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48814-7.Keywords: Eutylone, Metabolism, Metabolite, Drug-drug interaction, CYPSubject terms: Biochemistry, Drug discovery

Introduction

The synthetic cathinone market has changed rapidly, complicating regulatory control and surveillance and increasing uncertainty in exposure and risk assessment1,2. The United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime reported record-high seizures of amphetamine-type stimulants in 2023, with synthetic cathinones comprising a substantial share2. The European Union Drugs Agency documented a 356% increase in synthetic cathinone problems from 2018 to 2023 and reported 37 tons seized in 20233. More than 200 distinct synthetic cathinones have been identified thus far, highlighting the rapid chemical diversification of this class and the growing challenge of anticipating metabolic behavior and interaction risk4.

Eutylone is a methylenedioxy-substituted synthetic cathinone that is frequently detected in seizures and toxicological case analyses and often co-occurs with related stimulants5. Because synthetic cathinones undergo rapid phase I and phase II metabolism, parent drug concentrations in biofluids are transient, and confirmation commonly relies on metabolite evidence in toxicological and clinical settings6,7. In the United States, 343 eutylone-involved deaths were recorded in 2020, most involving illicitly manufactured fentanyls, highlighting the predominance of polydrug exposure5. In such settings, metabolic variability and enzyme-mediated interactions become key determinants of systemic exposure and toxicity.

Eutylone shares the 3,4-methylenedioxy moiety with methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA), a feature known to generate metabolic-intermediate complexes and cause time-dependent inhibition of cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYP), particularly CYP2D68,9. In humans, co-administration of MDMA with the CYP2D6 probe substrate dextromethorphan increased systemic exposure of dextromethorphan 10-fold and elevated the dextromethorphan to dextrorphan ratio by two orders of magnitude10. These observations suggest that eutylone may similarly alter metabolic phenotypes and modify the disposition of co-used substances, emphasizing the need for mechanistic evaluation of clearance pathways, metabolite formation, and enzyme inhibition.

Analytically, untargeted high-resolution mass spectrometry coupled with feature-based molecular networking (FBMN) is well suited to capture such complexity by reducing missed metabolite detection in vitro11–13. FBMN clusters MS/MS spectra by fragment similarity, enabling the recognition of biotransformation families without reference standards and facilitating the discrimination of isobars or closely related analogues that challenge targeted analysis14. Implemented within a cross-species design using hepatic preparations, this strategy supports comparative pathway mapping that distinguishes conserved from species-specific metabolic routes and informs translational interpretation15.

Previous work on the eutylone metabolism is limited in scope, typically focusing on single-species, targeted metabolite lists, and lacking systematic enzyme confirmation or interaction assessment16–19. The phase I metabolism such as O-demethylenation, N-deethylation, and reduction of the β-keto group, has been described in human urine, plasma, and liver microsomes, as well as in rat liver microsomes, with phase II metabolites from O-methylation and glucuronidation, and a suggested sulfation pathway16–19. Nevertheless, systematic cross-species comparison, metabolic enzyme characterization, and quantitative assessment of the drug–drug interaction potential remain insufficiently explored.

To address these gaps, the present study combines cross-species microsome and hepatocyte systems with FBMN-guided metabolite profiling, CYP phenotyping, and quantitative reversible and time-dependent inhibition kinetics. This integrated approach provides a metabolically informed characterization of eutylone disposition and interaction potential that is directly relevant to current patterns of polydrug use.

Plasma protein binding

The plasma protein binding of eutylone was determined using a rapid equilibrium dialysis (RED) device (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) as described previously20. Briefly, 300 µL of plasma containing 1 µM eutylone and 550 µL of dialysis buffer (pH 7.4) were added to the red and white chambers, respectively. The plate was sealed and incubated at 37 °C on an orbital shaker at 250 rpm for 4 h. After incubation, 50 µL aliquots were collected from the plasma and buffer chambers, and matrix matching was performed by adding 50 µL of dialysis buffer to the plasma samples and 50 µL of blank plasma to the buffer samples. The protein was precipitated by adding 300 µL of acetonitrile containing 20 ng/mL hydroxybupropion (internal standard; IS), followed by vortex-mixing and centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 8 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were analyzed by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) to determine the eutylone concentration in the plasma and buffer compartments. Metabolic stability of eutylone in human, rat, mouse, and dog liver preparations

For hepatocyte incubations, 60 µL of hepatocyte suspension in Opti-Incubate hepatocyte medium (final density, 1.0 × 106 cells/mL) from each species was gently mixed with 60 µL of a 2 µM eutylone solution in the same medium and incubated at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator for 5, 10, 20, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min. At each predetermined time point, the metabolic reaction was quenched by adding 120 µL of ice-cold hydroxybupropion (IS, 200 ng/mL) in acetonitrile. The quenched mixtures were sonicated for 10 min and centrifuged at 13,500 rpm for 8 min at 4 °C. An aliquot of each supernatant was diluted 50-fold with a 15% aqueous methanol solution, and 5 µL of the diluted sample was injected into the LC–MS/MS system to quantify the eutylone concentration.

For liver microsomal incubations, 95 µL of potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.4) containing liver microsomes (final concentration, 0.2 mg/mL), 10 mM MgCl2, and 1 µM eutylone was preincubated at 37 °C for 3 min. The reaction was initiated by adding 5 µL of 20 mM NADPH solution to achieve a final concentration of 1 mM, followed by incubation at 37 °C in a shaking water bath for 5, 10, 20, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min. At each point, samples were quenched with an ice-cold hydroxybupropion solution as described above and processed in the same manner before LC–MS/MS analysis.

Metabolic profiling of eutylone in hepatocytes and liver S9 fractions

The eutylone metabolism was investigated in human, rat, mouse, and dog hepatocytes; human and rat liver S9 fractions; and enzymatic hydrolysis assays.

For hepatocyte incubations, 60 µL of cell suspension in Opti-Incubate medium (1.0 × 106 cells/mL) from each species was mixed with 60 µL of 20 µM eutylone and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h in a CO2 incubator. For catechol-O-methyltransferase-mediated reactions, the liver S9 fractions (0.5 mg/mL) were incubated in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) containing 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM NADPH, 20 µM SAM, and 10 µM eutylone at 37 °C for 1 h. After each incubation, the reactions were quenched with an equal volume of ice-cold acetonitrile, centrifuged (13,500 rpm, 8 min, and 4 °C), and the supernatants were evaporated to dryness under a nitrogen stream.

For the enzymatic hydrolysis of conjugated metabolites, the dried residues obtained from the eutylone-treated hepatocyte incubations were reconstituted in the corresponding enzyme buffer containing β-glucuronidase (2000 units/mL in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer, pH 6.8) or sulfatase (500 units/mL in 200 mM sodium acetate buffer, pH 5.0) and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The reactions were quenched by adding an equal volume of ice-cold acetonitrile, and the supernatants were processed as described above.

All dried residues were reconstituted in 10% methanol, and 5 µL aliquots were analyzed using liquid chromatography high–resolution mass spectrometry (LC–HRMS) for metabolite profiling.

Feature-based molecular networking

FBMN analysis was performed by converting raw mass spectrometry data to mzXML format using MSConvert (version 3.0.23312; https://proteowizard.sourceforge.io) and processed in MZmine (version 4.8.5; https://mzio.io) through the MZWizard workflow, which automated chromatographic feature detection, alignment, and data preprocessing for FBMN analysis24,25. Ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) parameters were configured with a maximum of 15 peaks per chromatogram, minimum consecutive scans of 4, an approximate feature FWHM of 0.1 min, and retention time tolerances of 0.08 min (intra-sample) and 0.2 min (inter-sample). The orbitrap parameters were a noise threshold of 5 × 103 for MS1 and 2.5 × 103 for MS2, a minimum feature height of 5 × 104, and m/z tolerances of 10 ppm (scan-to-scan), 5 ppm (intra-sample), and 5 ppm (inter-sample). Moreover, ion identity molecular networking (IIMN) was incorporated to resolve the disconnected sub-networks arising from distinct fragmentation behaviors of multiple ion species belonging to the same compound. As part of the IIMN configuration, the ion identity library was expanded by adding the neutral losses of glucose and sulfate, improving the ion-grouping performance and enhancing networking. The processed data were exported as a .csv file (aligned feature intensity table) and .mgf file (MS/MS spectra) for FBMN on the Global Natural Product Social Molecular Networking 2 (GNPS2) platform (https://gnps2.org, accessed on November 24 2025)26. All MS data are publicly available via MassIVE (https://massive.ucsd.edu) under the accession number MSV000101408. The molecular networking parameters included a precursor and fragment ion mass tolerance of 0.02 Da, a minimum of 3 matched peaks, and a cosine similarity score > 0.6. The resulting networks were visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.10.3; https://cytoscape.org)27.

Screening of CYP enzymes involved in the eutylone metabolism

The CYP enzymes responsible for the eutylone metabolism were investigated using human cDNA-expressed CYP enzymes (i.e., CYP 1A1, CYP1A2, CYP2A6, CYP2B6, CYP2C8, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, CYP2E1, CYP3A4, and CYP3A5). A 95 µL aliquot of potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.4) containing an individual CYP enzyme (4 pmol each), 10 mM MgCl2, and 5 µM eutylone was preincubated at 37 °C for 3 min. The reaction was initiated by adding 5 µL of 20 mM NADPH and further incubated at 37 °C in a shaking water bath for 30 min. After incubation, the reaction was quenched by adding 100 µL of hydroxybupropion (IS, 20 ng/mL) in acetonitrile. The mixtures were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 5 min. Subsequently, 50 µL of each supernatant was mixed with 50 µL of water. A 5 µL aliquot of the mixture was then injected into the LC–MS/MS system to quantify the eutylone metabolites.

Inhibitory effects of eutylone on the major CYP enzymes in human liver microsomes

The inhibitory effects of eutylone on the major CYP enzyme activities in pooled human liver microsomes were evaluated using reported LC-MS/MS methods28,29. Briefly, the incubation mixtures containing potassium phosphate buffer (100 mM, pH 7.4), pooled human liver microsomes (0.2 mg/mL), eutylone (0.1–100 µM), and the CYP enzyme-specific substrates of cocktail sets A [amodiaquine (2 µM, CYP2C8), bufuralol (5 µM, CYP2D6), coumarin (2.5 µM, CYP2A6), diclofenac (10 µM, CYP2C9), (S)-mephenytoin (100 µM, CYP2C19), midazolam (2.5 µM, CYP3A4), and phenacetin (50 µM, CYP1A2)] or set B [bupropion (50 µM, CYP2B6)] were preincubated for 3 min at 37 °C. The metabolic reaction was initiated by adding 5 µL of 20 mM NADPH, followed by incubation in a shaking water bath at 37 °C for 15 min, with or without 30 min preincubation with NADPH. The reactions were quenched by adding 50 µL ice-cold methanol, containing 13C2,15N-acetaminophen and 1′-hydroxybufuralol-d9 (IS). The incubation mixtures were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 8 min at 4 °C, and 50 µL aliquots of each supernatant from set A and set B were combined. After mixing, aliquots (5 µL) were analyzed using an LC–MS/MS system as described previously29.

Mechanism-based inhibition of CYP2B6 and CYP2D6 activity by eutylone

The potential mechanism-based inhibition of eutylone on CYP2B6 and CYP2D6 activities was evaluated using time- and concentration-dependent assays in human liver microsomes. Microsomes (1 mg/mL) were pre-incubated with various concentrations of eutylone in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) containing 1 mM NADPH at 37 °C. At the designated time points (5, 10, 20, and 30 min for CYP2B6; 2, 4, 8, and 15 min for CYP2D6), 10 µL aliquots of the preincubation mixture were transferred into secondary reaction tubes containing CYP-specific probe substrates (50 µM bupropion for CYP2B6 and 5 µM bufuralol for CYP2D6), 1 mM NADPH, 10 mM MgCl2, and 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) to a final volume of 100 µL. The secondary reaction was carried out for 10 min and quenched by adding 100 µL of ice-cold methanol containing 13C2,15N-acetaminophen and 1′-hydroxybufuralol-d9 (IS). The mixtures were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, and 50 µL of each supernatant was mixed with an equal volume of water. After mixing, aliquots (5 µL) were analyzed using an LC–MS/MS system to quantify 1′-hydroxybufuralol and hydroxybupropion. The observed inactivation rate constant (kobs) at each inhibitor concentration was calculated from the negative slopes of the linear regression lines obtained by plotting the natural logarithm of the remaining enzyme activity against preincubation time. Inhibitory effects of eutylone on the major UGT enzymes in human liver microsomes

The inhibitory effects of eutylone on the major uridine 5′-diphospho-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT) enzyme activities in pooled human liver microsomes were evaluated using the previous LC-MS/MS methods28,29. Briefly, the incubation mixtures containing potassium phosphate buffer (100 mM, pH 7.4), pooled human liver microsomes (0.2 mg/mL), eutylone (0.1–100 µM), and the UGT enzyme-specific substrates of cocktail sets A [SN-38 (0.5 µM, UGT1A1), chenodeoxycholic acid (2 µM, UGT1A3), trifluoperazine (0.5 µM, UGT1A4)] or set B [N-acetylserotonin (1 µM, UGT1A6), mycophenolic acid (0.2 µM, UGT1A9), naloxone (1 µM, UGT2B7)]. The mixtures were preincubated for 3 min at 37 °C, and the reactions were initiated by adding 5 µL of 100 mM UDPGA. The reactions were quenched by adding 50 µL ice-cold methanol, containing propofol glucuronide and verapamil (IS). The incubation mixtures were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 8 min at 4 °C, and 50 µL aliquots of each supernatant from set A and set B were combined. After mixing, aliquots (5 µL) were analyzed using the LC–MS/MS system as described previously29.

Metabolite identification using LC–HRMS

A Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) coupled with a Nexera X2 UPLC system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) was used to identify the eutylone metabolites. The samples were separated using a Kinetex Phenyl-Hexyl column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm; Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) with a gradient elution of 0.1% formic acid in water (mobile phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in methanol (mobile phase B) at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The gradient program was as follows: 5% B for 1 min, 5%–30% B over 9 min, 30%–90% B over 5 min, 90% B for 3 min, 90%–5% B over 0.1 min, and 5% B for 3.9 min. The column and autosampler were maintained at 40 °C and 4 °C, respectively. Positive electrospray ionization was applied under the following conditions: nitrogen sheath gas, 20 arbitrary units; auxiliary gas, 10 arbitrary units; spray voltage, 3.5 kV; capillary temperature, 350 °C; and auxiliary gas heater temperature, 350 °C. Full-scan MS1 in data-dependent MS2 (dd-MS2) mode was used to detect molecular ions and their corresponding fragment ions. The full MS scan was performed over an m/z range of 100–1000 with a 70,000 resolution. The dd-MS2 spectra were acquired for the ten most intense precursor ions per cycle at a resolution of 17,500, using a normalized collision energy of 25 eV. In addition, parallel reaction monitoring was conducted to confirm the results obtained from the full-scan MS analysis. Data processing was performed using Xcalibur software (version 2.2; Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The product ion structures were elucidated using Mass Frontier software (version 6.0; HighChem Ltd., Bratislava, Slovakia).

Analysis of eutylone and its metabolites using LC–MS/MS

An Agilent 6495 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer coupled to a 1290 Infinity LC system (Agilent Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA) was used to quantify eutylone and its metabolites.

The samples were separated under two LC conditions depending on the experimental purpose. (1) For metabolic stability and plasma protein binding, separation was achieved using an Atlantis dC18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 3 μm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA) with a gradient elution of 0.1% formic acid in water (mobile phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in methanol (mobile phase B) at a flow rate of 0.25 mL/min. The gradient program was as follows: 15% B for 0.5 min, 15%–50% B over 0.5 min, 50%–95% B over 2 min, 95% B for 2 min, 95%–15% B over 0.1 min, and 15% B for 2.9 min. (2) For CYP enzyme screening, separation was performed using a Kinetex Phenyl-Hexyl column with a gradient elution of 0.1% formic acid in water (mobile phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in methanol (mobile phase B) at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The gradient program was as follows: 5% B for 1 min, 5%–30% B over 9 min, 30%–90% B over 2 min, 90% B for 3 min, 90%–5% B over 0.1 min, and 5% B for 2.9 min.

The autosampler and column temperatures were set to 4 °C and 40 °C, respectively. The electrospray ionization source operated in positive ion mode under the following conditions: gas temperature, 290 °C; gas flow, 11 L/min; nebulizer pressure, 45 psi; sheath gas temperature, 250 °C; sheath gas flow, 11 L/min; capillary voltage, 3500 V; and nozzle voltage, 500 V. Multiple reaction monitoring transitions were used as follows: m/z 236.1 → 188.1 for eutylone, m/z 224.1 → 188.1 for M1, m/z 208.1 → 160.1 for M2, m/z 238.2 → 220.1 for M3, m/z 252.3 → 149.0 for M4, and m/z 256.1 → 238.0 for hydroxybupropion (IS) at collision energies of 20, 25, 12, 10, 20, and 5 eV, respectively. The metabolites were quantified using the calibration curve of eutylone in the absence of authentic standards.

Results

Plasma protein binding

Across species, eutylone showed moderate plasma protein binding. Specifically, the unbound fractions (fu) for humans, rats, mice, and dogs were 0.51 ± 0.03, 0.38 ± 0.02, 0.46 ± 0.03, and 0.42 ± 0.02, respectively (mean ± SD) (Supplementary Table 1). Accordingly, approximately 40%–50% of circulating eutylone remains unbound and available for central nervous system exposure and hepatic uptake. These fu values were incorporated into calculations for hepatic clearance scaling in hepatocyte systems.

Metabolic stability in liver microsomes and hepatocytes

Comparative analyses revealed marked interspecies differences in the apparent half-life and intrinsic clearance (Fig. 1). In liver microsomes, depletion in the human system was minimal over the 2 h incubation, resulting in a t1/2 of 972.5 min and CLint value of 4.1 mL/min/kg. In contrast, the microsomal metabolism was rapid in rats, with a t1/2 of 6.8 min and a CLint value of 921.0 mL/min/kg, and slow to intermediate in mice and dogs (t1/2 of 100.1 and 139.9 min, and CLint values of 136.3 and 35.7 mL/min/kg, respectively).

Comparative depletion profiles of eutylone in human, rat, mouse, and dog liver microsomes and hepatocytes. The data are presented as the means ± SD (n = 3).

In hepatocytes, eutylone again showed slow intrinsic turnover in humans (t1/2 = 375.5 min and CLint = 13.2 mL/min/kg), faster depletion in rats (t1/2 = 27.1 min and CLint = 239.6 mL/min/kg), and intermediate behavior in mice and dogs (t1/2 = 62.1 and 120.8 min, CLint = 329.8 and 78.9 mL/min/kg). When scaled with the measured plasma unbound fractions, the corresponding predicted CLhep were 8.1 mL/min/kg, 44.9 mL/min/kg, 56.5 mL/min/kg, and 16.0 mL/min/kg in humans, rats, mice, and dogs, respectively, yielding extraction ratios of 0.39, 0.81, 0.63, and 0.52, respectively. Thus, rats exhibited high hepatic extraction. The human data indicated low intrinsic microsomal turnover and moderate hepatocyte clearance.

Identification of metabolites and proposed metabolic pathways

Untargeted LC–HRMS/MS combined with FBMN provided a comprehensive map of the eutylone biotransformation in human, rat, mouse, and dog hepatocytes. The nodes were annotated as metabolites based on the accurate mass (within 5 ppm), isotope pattern, retention time, and diagnostic product ions. Molecular network analysis showed that eutylone and its metabolites formed a single extensive cluster. This cluster included the metabolites generated through O-demethylenation, N-deethylation, β-ketone reduction, O-methylation, and hydroxylation, as well as their corresponding glucuronide and sulfate conjugates, effectively illustrating the overall metabolic pathways. All phase Ⅰ metabolites characterized were consistent with previous studies. M1 was formed via O-demethylenation, and M2 via N-deethylation. M3 resulted from β-ketone reduction, and M4 corresponded to a hydroxylated metabolite. Two chromatographic peaks with identical MS/MS spectra were observed for M3, which may be due to the presence of two diastereomeric forms, as reported elsewhere19.

Seven O-methylation–related metabolites of M1 (M5–M11) were identified. O-methylation of M1 yielded M5 and M6; hydroxylation followed by O-methylation generated M7 and M8; and hydroxylation followed by di-O-methylation produced M9 and M10. In addition, O-methylation combined with N-deethylation formed M11. Cofactor-supplemented incubation experiments were performed to confirm their formation pathways because M3 and the O-methylated metabolites of M1 (M5 and M6) share the same nominal mass. Incubation of human and rat liver S9 fractions with NADPH produced M3 but not M5 or M6, whereas the addition of SAM resulted in the clear formation of M5 and M6 (Supplementary Fig. 4). Other O-methylation–related metabolites (M7–M11) were also detected in rat liver S9 incubations supplemented with NADPH and SAM (Supplementary Fig. 4).

M1 formed glucuronides (M12 and M13) and sulfates (M14 and M15). M5 and M6 were further conjugated to yield M16/M17 (glucuronides) and M18/M19 (sulfates), and the glucuronidation of M7 produced M20. Multiple glucuronides exhibited a characteristic neutral loss of 176 Da, whereas sulfates were identified by the 80 Da neutral loss. These assignments were further supported by decreases in conjugated metabolites and corresponding increases in their deconjugated forms following the β-glucuronidase or sulfatase treatment of the hepatocyte incubations (Supplementary Table 2).

Overall, 20 metabolites were identified. Figure 3 presents the in vitro metabolic pathway of eutylone in human, rat, mouse, and dog hepatocytes. Among them, M1–M6, M11–M18, and M20 were detected in all four species. M7 and M9 were detected in rat and mouse hepatocytes, M19 in rat and dog hepatocytes, and M8 and M10 exclusively in rat hepatocytes. Among the four species, human and dog hepatocytes showed the most similar metabolic profiles. (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Metabolic enzyme phenotyping

Eutylone was incubated with eleven recombinant human CYP isoforms (1A1, 1A2, 2A6, 2B6, 2C8, 2C9, 2C19, 2D6, 2E1, 3A4, and 3A5) in the presence of NADPH. Parent-drug depletion and targeted metabolite formation were monitored. Among the isoforms, CYP2D6 played the dominant role, with 42.2% of eutylone metabolized during 30 min of incubation, while CYP2C19 contributed to a lesser extent (Fig. 4A). Metabolite quantification supported this selectivity. M1 was formed predominantly by CYP2D6, with CYP2C19 as a secondary contributor. M2 was generated mainly by CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP2B6. M4 formation was mediated primarily by CYP2D6, with an additional contribution from CYP3A4/5. In particular, M3 was not detected in CYP incubation, suggesting that CYP does not mediate the β-ketone reduction of eutylone (Fig. 4B).

CYP and UGT inhibition

Eutylone showed clear time dependent inhibition for CYP2D6 and CYP2B6 after preincubation with NADPH (Fig. 5). Kinetic characterization gave Ki = 32.9 µM with kinact = 0.080 min−1 for CYP2B6 and Ki = 4.2 µM with kinact = 0.069 min−1 for CYP2D6, respectively (Fig. 6). The resulting inactivation efficiency (kinact to Ki) was 2.43 × 10−3 min−1 µM−1 for CYP2B6 and 1.64 × 10−2 min−1 µM−1 for CYP2D6, respectively, indicating greater inactivation efficiency toward CYP2D6. No measurable inhibition was observed for UGT isoforms (1A1, 1A3, 1A4, 1A6, 1A9, and 2B7) with up to 100 µM of eutylone (Fig. 7).

Discussion

This study provided an integrated and translational characterization of eutylone disposition by combining cross-species plasma protein binding across species, microsomal and hepatocyte stability with in vitro–in vivo extrapolation (IVIVE), untargeted LC–HRMS coupled to FBMN, CYP phenotyping, and enzyme inhibition kinetics. Beyond describing metabolic pathways, these data address critical gaps relevant to public health surveillance and risk assessment under contemporary polydrug use patterns.

Eutylone exhibited moderate plasma protein binding (fu ≈ 0.4–0.5), greater binding than methylone or mephedrone in rats (fu ≈ 0.70–0.78) but less than benzedrone or chlorinated analogues (fu ≈ 0.20–0.33 in humans)30–32. This pattern is consistent with its intermediate lipophilicity and methylenedioxyphenyl scaffold, which can increase the interactions with albumin and alpha-1-acid glycoprotein relative to simple para-substituted cathinones. Using fu-corrected IVIVE, hepatic extraction was predicted to be moderate in humans, mice, and dogs, but high in rats, indicating the efficient first-pass elimination of eutylone in rats compared to humans. Such differences are particularly relevant for regulatory decision-making, because rats are often used as preclinical models to infer human risk for emerging psychoactive substances. Characterization of the full metabolite profile across species is therefore essential to understanding these differences, and was pursued using an untargeted FBMN-guided approach.

Unlike targeted screening, which is inherently limited to metabolites predictable from a defined set of biotransformation rules, FBMN groups features by MS/MS spectral similarity irrespective of their biosynthetic origin. This enabled the recognition of M7–M10 as a structurally coherent cluster derived from sequential O-demethylenation, subsequent hydroxylation, and O-methylation of M1, metabolites that would likely have been missed in a standard targeted workflow due to the difficulty of anticipating multi-step biotransformation products a priori. Beyond metabolite discovery, the network topology also facilitated a cross-species comparison of relative metabolite formation. Fourteen of the eighteen connected nodes showed the highest signal intensities in rat hepatocyte incubations (Fig. 2), suggesting greater overall metabolite formation in rats and consistent with the faster metabolic turnover observed in rat liver microsomes and hepatocytes.

Comparison with the published literature confirmed that the major phase I metabolites (M1–M4) and O-methylated phase II metabolites (M5, M6, and M11) were consistent with previous reports in human and rat in vitro systems and biological samples16–18. Beyond these conserved pathways, the present study identified several metabolites not previously reported or fully characterized. The hydroxylated and O-methylated catechol-derived metabolites (M7–M10) were detected predominantly in rat and mouse hepatocytes and have not been described previously. Although glucuronide conjugates of eutylone have been reported17,18, M20 was newly identified as the glucuronide of M11. Similarly, while sulfation has been proposed as a metabolic pathway19, the present study provides the first structural characterization of four sulfate metabolites (M14, M15, M18, and M19). In contrast, the secondary β-ketone-reduced metabolites of M2 and M5/M6 reported in human urine17 were not detected in the present hepatocyte systems. Although methylenedioxyphenyl compounds can form glutathione conjugates via quinone intermediates, no such adducts were detected across any of the four species, likely reflecting effective detoxification of the catechol metabolite M1 through competing phase II pathways including O-methylation, sulfation, and glucuronidation33.

The involvement of CYP2D6 as the major enzyme and CYP2C19 as a secondary contributor in eutylone O-demethylenation is consistent with observations for other 3,4-methylenedioxy-substituted cathinones such as MDMA, 3,4-methylenedioxyethylamphetamine (MDEA), and methylone34–36. Similar to the observations made for other synthetic cathinones, eutylone N-deethylation was catalyzed mainly by CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP2B636–38.

The enzyme phenotyping and inhibition kinetics indicate a common mechanism in which the oxidative steps that generate eutylone metabolite also produce reactive intermediates capable of inactivating enzymes. Mechanistically, the methylenedioxy ring undergoes oxidative cleavage to a carbene that coordinates with CYP heme, forming a metabolic intermediate complex and causing the time-dependent loss of activity9. In the current assays, eutylone caused time-dependent inhibition of CYP2B6 with Ki = 32.9 µM and kinact = 0.080 min−1, and of CYP2D6 with Ki = 4.2 µM and kinact = 0.069 min−1 (Fig. 6). The resulting inactivation efficiencies were 2.43 × 10−3 min−1 µM−1 for CYP2B6 and 1.64 × 10−2 min−1 µM−1 for CYP2D6, indicating a greater propensity to inactivate CYP2D6. This metabolic-intermediate complex formation is well documented for MDMA, a close structural analogue of eutylone. The reported MDMA values at CYP2D6 were Ki = 8.8–45.3 µM and kinact = 0.12–0.26 min−1, which yields an inactivation efficiency of about 2.65 × 10−3–1.70 × 10−2 min−1 µM−18. Accordingly, eutylone presents a significant perpetrator risk comparable to that of MDMA, and under polydrug use it can raise exposure to CYP2D6 substrates to a clinically significant extent.

In the 2020 U.S. dataset of 343 eutylone-involved deaths, co-exposures were common, with illicitly manufactured fentanyls involved in approximately 77% of cases, cocaine or methamphetamine in approximately 53%, opioids (codeine, tramadol, or methadone) in approximately 83%, and benzodiazepines in approximately 14%5. In this polydrug context, the time-dependent inhibition of CYP2D6 by eutylone is expected to acutely increase the exposure to CYP2D6 substrates such as codeine, tramadol, tricyclic antidepressants, several selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, and MDMA. In contrast, inhibition of CYP2B6 can elevate the concentrations of substrates such as bupropion, methadone, and ketamine. These interaction mechanisms, superimposed on concurrent stimulant or opioid effects, may result in clinically severe or life-threatening outcomes and emphasize the need for regulatory control and targeted harm-reduction strategies.

Conclusion

By integrating cross-species metabolism, untargeted metabolite profiling, enzyme phenotyping, and inhibition kinetics, this study provides a metabolically informed assessment of eutylone relevant to toxicological detection, translational modeling, and interaction-based risk evaluation. The marked differences between rodent and human clearance caution against direct extrapolation from animal models, while the MDMA-comparable inhibition of CYP2D6 and CYP2B6 identifies eutylone as a meaningful perpetrator in polydrug use scenarios. These findings offer decision-relevant evidence to support continued regulatory attention, improved surveillance strategies, and harm-reduction efforts targeting emerging synthetic cathinones.
