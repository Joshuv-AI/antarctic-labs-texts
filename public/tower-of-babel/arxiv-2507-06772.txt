Abstract
. This paper studies sparse nonlinear least squares problems, where the Jacobian matrices are unavailable or expensive to compute, yet have some underlying sparse structures. We construct the Jacobian models by the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} minimization subject to a small number of interpolation constraints with interpolation points generated from some certain distributions, and propose a derivative-free Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm based on such Jacobian models. It is proved that the Jacobian models are probabilistically first-order accurate and the algorithm converges globally almost surely. Numerical experiments are presented to show the efficiency of the proposed algorithm for sparse nonlinear least squares problems.

1.
Introduction

Nonlinear least squares problems arise frequently in areas of engineering, economy, machine learning and so on.
In this paper, we consider the nonlinear least squares problems

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\ f(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|F(x)\right\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\big(F^{(i)}(x)\big)^{2},

(1.1)

where
F(x):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is continuously differentiable, yet its Jacobian
J(x):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is either unavailable or expensive to compute but has some underlying sparse structure. Thus, the nonlinear least squares methods that rely on exact Jacobian matrices are not applicable anymore.

For derivative-free nonlinear least squares problems,
Brown et al.
[
5
]
gave finite difference analogues of the Levenberg-Marquardt (LM) method and Gauss-Newton method.
Some model-based methods were also developed.
For example, Zhang et al.
[
26
]
built quadratic interpolation models for each residual function
F^{(i)}
and presented a framework of derivative-free algorithms within a trust region framework, while Cartis et al.
[
9
,
8
]
constructed linear interpolation/regression models for each
F^{(i)}
and presented a derivative-free version of the Gauss-Newton method.
Different from the above deterministic methods, probabilistic methods have attracted increasing attention in recent years.
Bergou et al.
[
4
]
presented a LM method based on probabilistic gradient models.
Zhao et al.
[
28
]
established linear interpolation approximation for each
F^{(i)}
with the interpolation points generated by the standard Gaussian distribution, and showed that the Jacobian models obtained are probabilistically first-order accurate and the LM method based on such Jacobian models converges to a first-order stationary point of the objective function almost surely.
Chen et al.
[
11
]
built the probabilistically first-order accurate Jacobian models via orthogonal spherical smoothing and proved that the LM method based on such Jacobian models also converges globally almost surely.
In
[
10
]
, Cartis et al. introduced a general framework for large-scale model-based derivative-free optimization based on iterative minimization within random subspaces.
The framework is then specialized to nonlinear least-squares problems, which is based on the Gauss-Newton method and constructs local linear interpolation models to approximate the Jacobian.

In some practical applications, each residual function
F^{(i)}
involves only a small fraction of variables, implying a sparse structure in the Jacobian matrix. Typical problems of this kind are large scale geodesic adjustment
[
15
]
, least squares network adjustment
[
23
]
, wireless sensor network localization
[
19
]
, etc.
Curtis et al.
[
12
]
showed how to reduce the work required to estimate the Jacobian matrix by finite differences when it is sparse with a known sparsity pattern.
An et al.
[
1
]
presented an inexact Newton methods with quasi-conjugate-gradient backtracking for large sparse systems of nonlinear equations.
Krejić et al.
[
17
]
proposed a decoupling procedure which splits the original problems into a sequence of independent problems of smaller sizes and developed a modification of the LM method with smaller computational costs. However, all these methods require either the sparsity pattern of the Jacobian matrices or the exact derivatives.

In this paper, we focus on nonlinear least squares problems, where the Jacobian matrices are unavailable and possess some underlying sparse structures but the sparsity patterns are not known.
We construct the Jacobian models based on compressed sensing and propose a derivative-free LM algorithm for solving it.

First, we show how to construct the sparse Jacobian models by linear interpolation and

\ell_{1}
minimization, then propose a derivative-free LM algorithm based on such Jacobian models in Section
2
.
Second, we introduce the probabilistically bounded RIP matrices (i.e., satisfying the restricted isometry property), and prove that the sparse Jacobian models constructed are first-order accurate if the interpolation points are generated appropriately, such as from Gaussian distribution, Bernoulli distribution and Bernoulli-like distribution.
These results are given in Section
3
.
Third, we study convergence properties of the algorithm and
show each accumulation point of the sequence generated by the algorithm is a stationary point of the objective function with probability one in Section
4
.
In Section
5
, we present numerical experiments for solving sparse nonlinear least squares problems. Numerical results indicate that the number of interpolation points can be much less than the problem dimension if the Jacobian sparsity level is high.
Some preliminaries in compressed sensing are given in Section
3
.

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\|x\|_{0}

2.
Derivative-free LM algorithm for sparse nonlinear least squares problems

In this section, we show how to construct the sparse Jacobian models by linear interpolation and
\ell_{1}
minimization, and then propose a LM algorithm for sparse nonlinear least squares problems based on such Jacobian models.

At the
k
-th iteration, we generate
p
random vectors
v^{1}_{k},\ldots,v^{p}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
p<n
and take the set of interpolation points

\{x_{k}+\sigma_{k}v^{1}_{k},\ldots,x_{k}+\sigma_{k}v^{p}_{k}\},

(2.1)

where
\sigma_{k}>0
is updated at each iteration and controls the distance from the interpolation points to
x_{k}
. Throughout the paper, we denote
v_{k}=\{v^{1}_{k},\ldots,v^{p}_{k}\}
.

For each residual function
F^{(i)}(x),i=1,\ldots,m
, construct its linear interpolation model
m_{k}^{(i)}(x)=c_{k}^{(i)}+(g_{k}^{(i)})^{T}(x-x_{k})
that satisfies

\displaystyle m_{k}^{(i)}(x_{k})

\displaystyle=F^{(i)}(x_{k}),

(2.2a)

\displaystyle m_{k}^{(i)}(x_{k}+\sigma_{k}v_{k}^{j})

\displaystyle=F^{(i)}(x_{k}+\sigma_{k}v_{k}^{j}),\quad j=1,\ldots,p.

(2.2b)

Substituting (
2.2a
) into (
2.2b
) gives

(g_{k}^{(i)})^{T}(\sigma_{k}v_{k}^{j})=F^{(i)}(x_{k}+\sigma_{k}v_{k}^{j})-F^{(i)}(x_{k}),\quad j=1,\ldots,p.

(2.3)

Denote

A(v_{k})=\begin{bmatrix}(v^{1}_{k})^{T}\\
\vdots\\
(v^{p}_{k})^{T}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n},\quad y_{k}^{(i)}=\begin{bmatrix}F^{(i)}(x_{k}+\sigma_{k}v_{k}^{1})-F^{(i)}(x_{k})\\
\vdots\\
F^{(i)}(x_{k}+\sigma_{k}v_{k}^{p})-F^{(i)}(x_{k})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

(2.4)

(
2.3
) can be written as

A(v_{k})g_{k}^{(i)}=\sigma_{k}^{-1}y_{k}^{(i)},\quad i=1,\ldots,m.

(2.5)

Note that the Jacobians of the problem (
1.1
) are sparse.
One can solve the
\ell_{1}
minimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{g\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle\left\|g\right\|_{1}

(2.6)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\left\|A(v_{k})g-\sigma_{k}^{-1}y_{k}^{(i)}\right\|\leq\xi_{k},

to obtain
g_{k}^{(i)}
, where
\xi_{k}
is a positive number, and take the Jacobian model
J_{m_{k}}
as

J_{m_{k}}=\begin{bmatrix}\big(g^{(1)}_{k}\big)^{T}\\
\vdots\\
\big(g^{(m)}_{k}\big)^{T}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}.

(2.7)

Then
J_{m_{k}}
can serve as an approximation of
J(x_{k})
. The choices of
p,~v_{k},~\sigma_{k}
and
\xi_{k}
will be discussed in Section
3
.

Denote
F_{k}=F(x_{k})
. We solve the system of linear equations

(J_{m_{k}}^{T}J_{m_{k}}+\lambda_{k}I)d=-J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\text{ with }\lambda_{k}=\theta_{k}\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|

(2.8)

to obtain the trial step
d_{k}
, where
\lambda_{k}
is the LM parameter with
\theta_{k}
being updated from iteration to iteration.
Define the ratio of the actual reduction to the predicted reduction of the objective function as

\rho_{k}=\frac{\mathrm{Ared}_{k}}{\mathrm{Pred}_{k}}=\frac{\left\|F_{k}\right\|^{2}-\left\|F(x_{k}+d_{k})\right\|^{2}}{\left\|F_{k}\right\|^{2}-\left\|F_{k}+J_{m_{k}}d_{k}\right\|^{2}}.

(2.9)

It plays an important role in deciding whether
d_{k}
is acceptable and how to update
\theta_{k}
.
If
\rho_{k}
is smaller than a small positive constant, we reject
d_{k}
and increase
\theta_{k}
. Otherwise we accept
d_{k}
and update
\theta_{k}
as in
[
27
]
: we decrease
\theta_{k}
if the product of
\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|
and
\theta_{k}
is larger than a positive constant, otherwise we keep it unchanged or increase it.

3.
First-order accurate Jacobian models

In this section, we first review some basic results in the field of compressed sensing, which are very helpful for the later analysis, then prove that the sparse Jacobian models constructed as described in Section
2
are first-order accurate if
v_{k}
,
\sigma_{k}
and
\xi_{k}
are taken appropriately.

The focus of noisy compressed sensing is to solve the underdetermined system

y=A\hat{x}+z,

(3.1)

where
\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the sparse signal to reconstruct,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the available measurement with
m\ll n
,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is the known sensing matrix, and
z
is the unknown noise.
The recovery of sparse signals relies on the restricted isometry property (RIP) of the sensing matrix (cf.
[
7
]
).

Definition 3.1
.

For each integer
s=1,2,\ldots
, define the
s
-RIP constant
\delta_{s}
of a matrix
A
as the smallest number such that

(1-\delta_{s})\left\|x\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|Ax\right\|^{2}\leq(1+\delta_{s})\left\|x\right\|^{2}

(3.2)

holds for all
s
-sparse vectors. A vector is said to be
s
-sparse if it has at most
s
nonzero entries.

The following theorem shows that the desired sparse vector
\hat{x}
can be recovered by solving an
\ell_{1}
minimization problem, provided that the RIP constant of the sensing matrix satisfies some certain condition.

Theorem 3.2

(
[
20
, Theorem 4.6]
)
.

Let
y=A\hat{x}+z
, where
\hat{x}
is
s
-sparse and
\left\|z\right\|\leq\xi
. If the
2s
-RIP constant of
A
satisfies
\delta_{2s}<0.4931
, then the solution
x^{*}
to

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle\left\|x\right\|_{1}

(3.3)

\displaystyle\rm{s.t.}

\displaystyle\left\|Ax-y\right\|\leq\xi

satisfies

\left\|x^{*}-\hat{x}\right\|\leq c_{1}\xi,

(3.4)

where
c_{1}
is a constant only depends on
\delta_{2s}
.

For simplicity, we use the RIP matrix as a shorthand for the matrix that satisfies the RIP of order
2s
with constant
\delta_{2s}<0.4931
, where
s
is the sparsity level.

Define the event

T_{k}=\{A(v_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}\ \mbox{is an RIP matrix and}\ \|v_{k}^{j}\|\leq\kappa_{\mathrm{bv}}\ \mbox{for all}\ j=1,\ldots,p\,\},

(3.5)

where
A(v_{k})
is defined by (
2.4
) and
\kappa_{\mathrm{bv}}
is a positive constant.

Definition 3.3
.

Given
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\kappa_{\mathrm{bv}}>0
.
\{A(V_{k})\}
is said a sequence of
\beta
-probabilistically
\kappa_{\mathrm{bv}}
-bounded RIP matrices if the events
T_{k}
satisfy the submartingale-like condition

\mathbb{P}\left(T_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right)\geq\beta,

(3.6)

where
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
V_{0},\ldots,V_{k-1}
.

To estimate the difference between the exact Jacobian matrix and its model, we introduce the definition of the first-order accurate Jacobian model as in
[
28
]
. Hereafter,
J_{k}
is used to denote
J(x_{k})
.

Definition 3.4
.

Given
\kappa_{\mathrm{ej}}>0
. A Jacobian model realization
J_{m_{k}}
is said to be
\kappa_{\mathrm{ej}}
-first-order accurate if

\left\|J_{m_{k}}-J_{k}\right\|\leq\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{ej}}}{\theta_{k}}.

(3.7)

Now we prove that
J_{m_{k}}
constructed by (
2.6
)–(

\kappa_{\mathrm{ej}}
-first-order accurate if

and
\xi_{k}
are chosen appropriately.
We make some assumptions.

Assumption 3.5
.

(i)
F(x):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is continuously differentiable and the gradients
\nabla F^{(1)}(x),\ldots,\nabla F^{(m)}(x)

\kappa_{\mathrm{lg}}>0
such that for all

\left\|\nabla F^{(i)}(x)-\nabla F^{(i)}(y)\right\|\leq\kappa_{\mathrm{lg}}\left\|x-y\right\|,\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(3.8)

(ii)
\nabla F^{(1)}(x),\ldots,\nabla F^{(m)}(x)
are
s
-sparse for all

.

(
3.8

\kappa_{\mathrm{lj}}>0
such that

\left\|J(x)-J(y)\right\|\leq\kappa_{\mathrm{lj}}\left\|x-y\right\|,\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(3.9)

Theorem 3.6
.

Suppose that Assumption
3.5
holds.
Let
\sigma_{k}=\left\|d_{k-1}\right\|

\xi_{k}=\sqrt{p}\kappa_{\mathrm{lg}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bv}}^{2}\sigma_{k}/2

If

constructed by (

2.7

\kappa_{\mathrm{ej}}
-first-order accurate,
where
\kappa_{\mathrm{ej}}=\sqrt{mp}c_{1}\gamma_{2}\kappa_{\mathrm{lg}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bv}}^{2}/2

with
c_{1}
given in Theorem
3.2

Proof.

Since
\nabla F^{(i)}(x)
is
\kappa_{\mathrm{lg}}
-Lipschitz continuous and
T_{k}
happens, for
j=1,\ldots,p,
we have

\displaystyle\hskip 13.00005pt{\left|F^{(i)}(x_{k}+\sigma_{k}v^{j}_{k})-F^{(i)}(x_{k})-\nabla F^{(i)}(x_{k})^{T}(\sigma_{k}v^{j}_{k})\right|}

\displaystyle={\left|\int_{0}^{1}\big[\nabla F^{(i)}(x_{k}+t\sigma_{k}v^{j}_{k})-\nabla F^{(i)}(x_{k})\big]^{T}(\sigma_{k}v^{j}_{k})dt\right|}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\kappa_{\mathrm{lg}}\big\|\sigma_{k}v^{j}_{k}\big\|^{2}\;t\;dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{\mathrm{lg}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bv}}^{2}\sigma_{k}^{2},

which yields

\left\|A(v_{k})\nabla F^{(i)}(x_{k})-\sigma_{k}^{-1}y_{k}^{(i)}\right\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{p}\kappa_{\mathrm{lg}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bv}}^{2}\sigma_{k}.

Then, by Theorem

\left\|g_{k}^{(i)}-\nabla F^{(i)}(x_{k})\right\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{p}c_{1}\kappa_{\mathrm{lg}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bv}}^{2}\sigma_{k}.

Hence

\left\|J_{m_{k}}-J_{k}\right\|_{F}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{mp}c_{1}\kappa_{\mathrm{lg}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bv}}^{2}\sigma_{k}.

(3.13)

By the definition of

and (

\sigma_{k}=\left\|d_{k-1}\right\|\leq\frac{\big\|J_{m_{k-1}}^{T}F_{k-1}\big\|}{\lambda_{k-1}}=\frac{1}{\theta_{k-1}}\leq\frac{\gamma_{2}}{\theta_{k}}.

\left\|J_{m_{k}}-J_{k}\right\|\leq\left\|J_{m_{k}}-J_{k}\right\|_{F}\leq\frac{\sqrt{mp}c_{1}\gamma_{2}\kappa_{\mathrm{lg}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bv}}^{2}}{2\theta_{k}}.

(3.15)

The proof is completed.

4.
Almost-sure global convergence

In this section, we study convergence properties of Algorithm
1
.
It is shown that Algorithm
1
converges to a stationary point of the sparse nonlinear least squares problem with probability one.

Define the level set

\mathcal{L}_{0}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\left\|F(x)\right\|\leq\left\|F(x_{0})\right\|\}.

By (
2.10
), the sequence
\{x_{k}\}
generated by Algorithm
1
lies in

\mathcal{L}_{0}
. Denote

\mathcal{L}:=\mathcal{L}_{0}\;\bigcup\;\textstyle\bigcup\limits_{x\in\mathcal{L}_{0}}\!B(x,\theta_{\min}^{-1}).

We make the following assumptions.

Assumption 4.1
.

(i)
\mathcal{L}
is compact.

(ii)
F(x)
is Lipschitz continuous in
\mathcal{L}
, i.e., there exist
\kappa_{\mathrm{lf}}>0
such that

\left\|F(y)-F(x)\right\|\leq\kappa_{\mathrm{lf}}\left\|y-x\right\|,\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{L}.

(4.1)

By Assumption
4.1
, there exist
\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}}
such that

\left\|F(x)\right\|\leq\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}},\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{L},

(4.2)

and

\left\|J(x)\right\|\leq\kappa_{\mathrm{lf}},\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{L}.

(4.3)

Assumption 4.2
.

For every realization of Algorithm
1

J_{m_{k}}
is bounded for all
k
, i.e., there exists
\kappa_{\mathrm{bmj}}>0

\left\|J_{m_{k}}\right\|\leq\kappa_{\mathrm{bmj}},\quad\forall k.

(4.4)

It can be verified that

\min_{d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\ \frac{1}{2}\left\|F_{k}+J_{m_{k}}d\right\|^{2}\quad\text{s.t.}\ \left\|d\right\|\leq\left\|d_{k}\right\|.

By Powell’s result
[
22

Lemma 4.3
.

The predicted reduction satisfies

\left\|F_{k}\right\|^{2}-\left\|F_{k}+J_{m_{k}}d_{k}\right\|^{2}\geq\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|\min\left\{\left\|d_{k}\right\|,\frac{\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|}{\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}J_{m_{k}}\right\|}\right\}

for all

We first prove that the
k

J_{m_{k}}
is first-order accurate and

\theta_{k}\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose that Assumptions
3.5
,
4.1
and
4.2
hold. Consider the
k

1
. If (
3.7
) is satisfied and

\theta_{k}\geq\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{lj}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}}+\kappa_{\mathrm{lf}}^{2}+2\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}}\kappa_{\mathrm{ej}}+\kappa_{\mathrm{bmj}}^{2}}{(1-\eta_{0})\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|}:=\frac{M}{\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|},

then
\rho_{k}\geq\eta_{0}

Proof.

By (
3.9
) and (
4.2
)–(
4.3
), for any
x,y\in\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle\left\|J(x)^{T}F(x)-J(y)^{T}F(y)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\left\|J(x)^{T}(F(x)-F(y))\right\|+\left\|(J(x)-J(y))^{T}F(y)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle(\kappa_{\mathrm{lf}}^{2}+\kappa_{\mathrm{lj}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}})\left\|x-y\right\|.

(4.8)

Hence the gradient of

is Lipschitz continuous.
So it holds

\displaystyle\big|\left\|F(y)\right\|^{2}\!-\!\left\|F(x)\right\|^{2}\!-\!(2J(x)^{T}F(x))^{T}(y-x)\big|\leq{}

\displaystyle(\kappa_{\mathrm{lf}}^{2}+\kappa_{\mathrm{lj}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}})\left\|x\!-\!y\right\|^{2}.

(4.9)

Combining (
3.7
), (
4.2
), (
4.4
) and (
4.9
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\left\|F_{k}+J_{m_{k}}d_{k}\right\|^{2}\!-\!\left\|F(x_{k}+d_{k})\right\|^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\left|\left\|F_{k}\right\|^{2}+2(J_{k}^{T}F_{k})^{T}d_{k}-\left\|F(x_{k}+d_{k})\right\|^{2}\right|+\left|2((J_{m_{k}}-J_{k})^{T}F_{k})^{T}d_{k}\right|+\left\|J_{m_{k}}d_{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle(\kappa_{\mathrm{lj}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}}+\kappa_{\mathrm{lf}}^{2})\left\|d_{k}\right\|^{2}+2\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}}\kappa_{\mathrm{ej}}\theta_{k}^{-1}\left\|d_{k}\right\|+\kappa_{\mathrm{bmj}}^{2}\left\|d_{k}\right\|^{2}.

(4.10)

By (
4.4
), (
4.7
) and the definition of
d_{k}
,

\displaystyle\left\|d_{k}\right\|\leq\frac{1}{\theta_{k}}\leq\frac{(1-\eta_{0})\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|}{\kappa_{\mathrm{bmj}}^{2}}\leq\frac{\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|}{\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}J_{m_{k}}\right\|}.

(4.11)

It then follows from Lemma
4.3
that

\mathrm{Pred}_{k}=\left\|F_{k}\right\|^{2}-\left\|F_{k}+J_{m_{k}}d_{k}\right\|^{2}\geq\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|\left\|d_{k}\right\|.

(4.12)

Hence, by (
4.7
), (
4
)–(
4.12
),

\displaystyle|\rho_{k}-1|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\left\|F_{k}+J_{m_{k}}d_{k}\right\|^{2}-\left\|F(x_{k}+d_{k})\right\|^{2}}{\left\|F_{k}\right\|^{2}-\left\|F_{k}+J_{m_{k}}d_{k}\right\|^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(\kappa_{\mathrm{lj}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}}+\kappa_{\mathrm{lf}}^{2})\left\|d_{k}\right\|^{2}+2\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}}\kappa_{\mathrm{ej}}\theta_{k}^{-1}\left\|d_{k}\right\|+\kappa_{\mathrm{bmj}}^{2}\left\|d_{k}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|\left\|d_{k}\right\|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{lj}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}}+\kappa_{\mathrm{lf}}^{2}+2\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}}\kappa_{\mathrm{ej}}+\kappa_{\mathrm{bmj}}^{2}}{\theta_{k}\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\eta_{0}.

(4.13)

This implies that
\rho_{k}\geq\eta_{0}
. The proof is completed.
∎

Next we prove that the sequence
\{\theta_{k}\}
goes to infinity.

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose that Assumptions
4.1
and
4.2
hold.
For any realization of Algorithm
1
,

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\theta_{k}=+\infty.

(4.14)

Proof.

We prove by contradiction.
Suppose that (
4.14
) does not hold.
Then there exists
\tilde{\theta}>0
such that the set
\Omega_{1}=\{k\mid\theta_{k}<\tilde{\theta}\}
is infinite. Hence the set
\Omega_{2}=\{k\mid\theta_{k}<\frac{\tilde{\theta}}{\gamma_{1}}\}

is also infinite because of
0<\gamma_{1}<1
.

Define the set

\displaystyle\Omega_{3}=\{k\in\Omega_{2}\mid\theta_{k+1}\leq\theta_{k}\}.

(4.15)

We show that
\Omega_{3}
is infinite too.
Otherwise, there exists
K_{0}\in\Omega_{2}
such that
\theta_{k+1}>\theta_{k}
for all
k\geq K_{0}
and
k\in\Omega_{2}
.
Then there exists
K_{1}>K_{0}
such that
\theta_{K_{1}}\geq\frac{\tilde{\theta}}{\gamma_{1}}
and
\theta_{k+1}>\theta_{k}
for all
K_{0}\leq k<K_{1}
.
Since
\Omega_{2}
is infinite, by (
2.11
),
there exists
K_{2}>K_{1}
such that
\tilde{\theta}\leq\theta_{K_{2}}<\frac{\tilde{\theta}}{\gamma_{1}}
and

\theta_{k}\geq\frac{\tilde{\theta}}{\gamma_{1}}
for all
K_{1}\leq k<K_{2}
.
By induction, we obtain
\theta_{k}\geq\tilde{\theta}
for all
k>K_{2}
.
This is a contradiction to the infiniteness of
\Omega_{1}
. So
\Omega_{3}
is infinite.

By (
2.11
), for all
k\in\Omega_{3}
,

\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|\geq\frac{\eta_{1}}{\theta_{k}}\geq\frac{\eta_{1}\gamma_{1}}{\tilde{\theta}}.

(4.16)

This, together with (
4.4
), implies

\left\|d_{k}\right\|\geq\frac{\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|}{\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}J_{m_{k}}\right\|+\theta_{k}\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|}\geq\frac{\eta_{1}\gamma_{1}}{\tilde{\theta}(\kappa_{\mathrm{bmj}}^{2}+\eta_{1})}.

(4.17)

It then follows from Lemma
4.3
and
\rho_{k}\geq\eta_{0}
that for any
k\in\Omega_{3}
,

\displaystyle\left\|F_{k}\right\|^{2}-\left\|F(x_{k}+d_{k})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\eta_{0}(\left\|F_{k}\right\|^{2}-\left\|F_{k}+J_{m_{k}}d_{k}\right\|^{2})

\displaystyle\geq\eta_{0}\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|\min\left\{\left\|d_{k}\right\|,\frac{\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|}{\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}J_{m_{k}}\right\|}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\eta_{0}\eta_{1}^{2}\gamma_{1}^{2}}{\tilde{\theta}^{2}(\kappa_{\mathrm{bmj}}^{2}+\eta_{1})}.

(4.18)

This contradicts the infiniteness of
\Omega_{3}
and
\left\|F_{k}\right\|\geq 0
. So (
4.14
) holds true. The proof is completed.
∎

We are now ready to prove that at least one accumulation point of the sequence generated by Algorithm
1
is a stationary point of the sparse nonlinear least squares problem (
1.1
) with probability one.

Theorem 4.6
.

Suppose that Assumptions
3.5
,
4.1
and
4.2
hold and
\beta\geq\frac{\log\gamma_{2}}{\log\gamma_{2}-\log\gamma_{1}}
.
Let
\sigma_{k}=\left\|d_{k-1}\right\|
and
\xi_{k}=\sqrt{p}\kappa_{\mathrm{lg}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bv}}^{2}\sigma_{k}/2
.
If
\{A(V_{k})\}
is a sequence of
\beta
-probabilistically
\kappa_{\mathrm{bv}}
-bounded RIP matrices,
then the random iterates
\{X_{k}\}
generated by Algorithm
1
satisfies

\mathbb{P}\left(\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}\left\|J^{T}(X_{k})F(X_{k})\right\|=0\right)=1.

(4.19)

Proof.

Consider the random walk

W_{j}=\sum_{k=0}^{j}\left(\mathbf{1}_{T_{k}}-\beta\right),

(4.20)

where
\mathbf{1}_{T_{k}}
is the indicator function of the event
T_{k}
. Then

W_{j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}W_{j-1}+1-\beta,&\text{if}\ \mathbf{1}_{T_{j}}=1,\\
W_{j-1}-\beta,&\text{if}\ \mathbf{1}_{T_{j}}=0.\\
\end{array}\right.

(4.21)

By (
3.6
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[W_{j}\mid\mathcal{F}_{j-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[W_{j-1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{j-1}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{T_{j}}-\beta\mid\mathcal{F}_{j-1}\right]

\displaystyle=W_{j-1}+\mathbb{P}\left(T_{j}\mid\mathcal{F}_{j-1}\right)-\beta

\displaystyle\geq W_{j-1}.

(4.22)

Hence
W_{j}
is a submartingale with bounded increments.
Thus, according to
[
2
, Theorem 4.1]
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(\Big\{\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}W_{j}\text{ exists and is finite}\Big\}\,{\textstyle\bigcup}\,\Big\{\limsup_{j\rightarrow\infty}W_{j}=+\infty\Big\}\right)=1.

(4.23)

Since
|W_{j+1}-W_{j}|\geq\min\{\beta,1-\beta\}
, the sequence
\{W_{j}\}
has no limit, so we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\limsup_{j\rightarrow\infty}W_{j}=+\infty\right)=1.

(4.24)

Now we prove (
4.19
) by contradiction. Without loss of generality, suppose that there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\left\|J^{T}(X_{k})F(X_{k})\right\|\geq\varepsilon

(4.25)

holds for all
k
with positive probability.
By Lemma
4.5
, for any realization of Algorithm
1
, there exists
K>1
such that for all
k\geq K
,

\theta_{k}\geq\max\left\{\frac{2\kappa_{\mathrm{ej}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}}}{\varepsilon},\frac{2M}{\varepsilon},\frac{2\eta_{2}}{\varepsilon},\frac{\theta_{\min}}{\gamma_{1}}\right\},

(4.26)

where
M
is a constant given in (
4.7
).
We discuss the case when
\left\|J^{T}(X_{k})F(X_{k})\right\|\geq\varepsilon
happens. Define the random variable
Z_{k}
with the realization

z_{k}=(1-\beta)\log_{\gamma_{1}}(\theta_{k}).

(4.27)

In the following, we consider two cases:
\mathbf{1}_{T_{k}}=1
and
\mathbf{1}_{T_{k}}=0
.

(i)
\mathbf{1}_{T_{k}}=1
. By Theorem
3.6
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|J_{m_{k}}-J_{k}\right\|\leq\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{ej}}}{\theta_{k}}
. This, together with (
4.26
), yields

\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|\geq\left\|J_{k}^{T}F_{k}\right\|-\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}-J_{k}^{T}F_{k}\right\|\geq\varepsilon-\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{ej}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}}}{\theta_{k}}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(4.28)

Combining (
4.26
) with (
4.28
), we obtain

\theta_{k}\geq\frac{2M}{\varepsilon}\geq\frac{M}{\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|}.

(4.29)

It then follows from Lemma
4.4
that
\rho_{k}\geq\eta_{0}
. Since

\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\geq\frac{\eta_{2}}{\theta_{k}},\ \gamma_{1}\theta_{k}\geq\theta_{\min},

(4.30)

by the update rule of
\theta_{k}
, we have
\theta_{k+1}=\gamma_{1}\theta_{k}
.
Thus

z_{k+1}-z_{k}=(1-\beta)\left(\log_{\gamma_{1}}(\gamma_{1}\theta_{k})-\log_{\gamma_{1}}(\theta_{k})\right)=1-\beta=w_{k}-w_{k-1},

(4.31)

where
w_{k}
is a realization of
W_{k}
.

(ii)
\mathbf{1}_{T_{k}}=0
. Note that
\theta_{k+1}\leq\gamma_{2}\theta_{k}
always holds. Hence

z_{k+1}-z_{k}\geq(1-\beta)\left(\log_{\gamma_{1}}(\gamma_{2}\theta_{k})-\log_{\gamma_{1}}(\theta_{k})\right)=(1-\beta)\log_{\gamma_{1}}(\gamma_{2}).

(4.32)

Since
\beta\geq\frac{\log\gamma_{2}}{\log\gamma_{2}-\log\gamma_{1}}
, we have

z_{k+1}-z_{k}\geq-\beta=w_{k}-w_{k-1}.

(4.33)

It then follows from (
4.31
) and (
4.33
) that

z_{k+1}-z_{K}\geq w_{k}-w_{K-1},\ \forall k>K.

(4.34)

Since
z_{K}
and
w_{K-1}
are finite, (
4.24
) and (
4.34
) imply that

\mathbb{P}\left(\limsup_{j\rightarrow\infty}Z_{j}=+\infty\right)=1,

(4.35)

which contradicts
\theta_{k}\rightarrow+\infty
and
\gamma_{1}<1
. So (
4.25
) cannot be true. The proof is completed.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Suppose that Assumptions
3.5
,
4.1
and
4.2
hold and
\beta\geq\frac{\log\gamma_{2}}{\log\gamma_{2}-\log\gamma_{1}}
.
Let
\sigma_{k}=\left\|d_{k-1}\right\|
and
\xi_{k}=\sqrt{p}\kappa_{\mathrm{lg}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bv}}^{2}\sigma_{k}/2
.
For any given
\varepsilon>0
, define

\Omega=\{k\mid\left\|J^{T}(X_{k})F(X_{k})\right\|>\varepsilon\}.

(4.36)

If
\{A(V_{k})\}
is a sequence of
\beta
-probabilistically
\kappa_{\mathrm{bv}}
-bounded RIP matrices, then the sequence
\{\Theta_{k}\}
generated by Algorithm
1
satisfies

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{k\in\Omega}\frac{1}{\Theta_{k}}<+\infty\right)=1.

(4.37)

Proof.

Define

\Omega_{1}=\{k\in\Omega\mid T_{k}\text{ happens}\}\text{ and }\Omega_{2}=\Omega\backslash\Omega_{1}.

(4.38)

It follows from Theorem
3.6
that

\displaystyle\left\|J_{m_{k}}-J_{k}\right\|\leq\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{ej}}}{\theta_{k}}
for all
k\in\Omega_{1}
. We first prove that

\sum_{k\in\Omega_{1}}\frac{1}{\theta_{k}}<+\infty.

(4.39)

Obviously, (
4.39
) holds true when
\Omega_{1}
is finite.
When
\Omega_{1}
is infinite, by a similar analysis as that in Theorem
4.6
, we have

\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\text{ and }\rho_{k}\geq\eta_{0}

(4.40)

for sufficiently large
k\in\Omega_{1}
.
It then follows from (
4.2
), (
4.4
), (
4.40
) and Lemma
4.3
that for sufficiently large
k\in\Omega_{1}
,

\displaystyle\left\|F_{k}\right\|^{2}-\left\|F(x_{k}+d_{k})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\eta_{0}(\left\|F_{k}\right\|^{2}-\left\|F_{k}+J_{m_{k}}d_{k}\right\|^{2})

\displaystyle\geq\eta_{0}\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|\min\left\{\left\|d_{k}\right\|,\frac{\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|}{\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}J_{m_{k}}\right\|}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\eta_{0}\frac{\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}J_{m_{k}}\right\|+\theta_{k}\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\eta_{0}\theta_{k}}{\kappa_{\mathrm{bmj}}^{2}+\theta_{k}\kappa_{\mathrm{bmj}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}}}\frac{\left\|J_{m_{k}}^{T}F_{k}\right\|^{2}}{\theta_{k}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\eta_{0}\theta_{\min}\varepsilon^{2}}{4(\kappa_{\mathrm{bmj}}^{2}+\theta_{\min}\kappa_{\mathrm{bmj}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}})}\frac{1}{\theta_{k}}.

(4.41)

Thus (
4.39
) holds true.

Define the random variable
G_{0}=0
and
G_{k}=G_{k-1}+(\mathbf{1}_{T_{k}}-\beta)\Theta_{k}^{-1}~(k\geq 1)
. Since
\{A(V_{k})\}
is a sequence of
\beta
-probabilistically
\kappa_{\mathrm{bv}}
-bounded RIP matrices,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[G_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[G_{k-1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbf{1}_{T_{k}}-\beta)\Theta_{k}^{-1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle=G_{k-1}+\Theta_{k}^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{T_{k}}-\beta\mid\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle\geq G_{k-1}.

(4.42)

Thus
G_{k}
is a submartingale with bounded increments. It then follows from
[
2
, Theorem 4.1]
that

\mathbb{P}\left(\Big\{\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}G_{k}\text{ exists and is finite}\Big\}\,{\textstyle\bigcup}\,\Big\{\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}G_{k}=+\infty\Big\}\right)=1.

(4.43)

Note that

G_{k}=(1-\beta)\sum_{k\in\Omega_{1}}\frac{1}{\Theta_{k}}-\beta\sum_{k\in\Omega_{2}}\frac{1}{\Theta_{k}}.

(4.44)

If the event

\left\{\sum_{k\in\Omega_{2}}\frac{1}{\Theta_{k}}=+\infty\right\}

happens with some positive probability, then by (
4.39
),
the event

\Big\{\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}G_{k}=-\infty\Big\}

(4.45)

also happens with some positive probability, which is a contradiction to (
4.43
).
Thus

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{k\in\Omega_{2}}\frac{1}{\Theta_{k}}<+\infty\right)=1

(4.46)

and

\sum_{k\in\Omega}\frac{1}{\Theta_{k}}=\sum_{k\in\Omega_{1}}\frac{1}{\Theta_{k}}+\sum_{k\in\Omega_{2}}\frac{1}{\Theta_{k}}<+\infty

(4.47)

holds almost surely.
∎

Now we prove that the sequence generated by Algorithm
1
converges to a first-order stationary point of the problem (
1.1
) almost surely.

Theorem 4.8
.

Suppose that Assumptions
3.5
,
4.1
and
4.2
hold and
\beta\geq\frac{\log\gamma_{2}}{\log\gamma_{2}-\log\gamma_{1}}
.
Let
\sigma_{k}=\left\|d_{k-1}\right\|
and
\xi_{k}=\sqrt{p}\kappa_{\mathrm{lg}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bv}}^{2}\sigma_{k}/2
.
If
\{A(V_{k})\}
is a sequence of
\beta
-probabilistically
\kappa_{\mathrm{bv}}
-bounded RIP matrices,
then the random iterates
\{X_{k}\}
generated by Algorithm
1
satisfies

\mathbb{P}\left(\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\left\|J^{T}(X_{k})F(X_{k})\right\|=0\right)=1.

(4.48)

Proof.

We prove by contradiction. Suppose that (
4.48
) is not true.
Then there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
\Omega_{1}=\{k\mid\left\|J^{T}_{k}F_{k}\right\|>2\varepsilon\}
is infinite holds with positive probability. Define

\Omega_{2}=\{k\mid\left\|J^{T}_{k}F_{k}\right\|>\varepsilon\}.

(4.49)

By Theorem
4.6
, there exist infinite integer pairs
(k^{\prime},k^{\prime\prime})
with
0<k^{\prime}<k^{\prime\prime}
such that

\left\|J^{T}_{k^{\prime}}F_{k^{\prime}}\right\|\leq\varepsilon,\ \left\|J^{T}_{k^{\prime}+1}F_{k^{\prime}+1}\right\|>\varepsilon,\ \left\|J^{T}_{k^{\prime\prime}}F_{k^{\prime\prime}}\right\|>2\varepsilon

(4.50)

and for any
k
such that
k^{\prime}<k<k^{\prime\prime}
,

\varepsilon<\left\|J^{T}_{k}F_{k}\right\|\leq 2\varepsilon

(4.51)

hold with positive probability.
Hence by (
4.8
),

\displaystyle\varepsilon<

\displaystyle\Big|\left\|J^{T}_{k^{\prime\prime}}F_{k^{\prime\prime}}\right\|-\left\|J^{T}_{k^{\prime}}F_{k^{\prime}}\right\|\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{l=k^{\prime}}^{k^{\prime\prime}-1}\Big|\left\|J^{T}_{l+1}F_{l}\right\|-\left\|J^{T}_{l}F_{l}\right\|\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{l=k^{\prime}}^{k^{\prime\prime}-1}(\kappa_{\mathrm{lf}}^{2}+\kappa_{\mathrm{lj}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}})\left\|d_{l}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\kappa_{\mathrm{lf}}^{2}+\kappa_{\mathrm{lj}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}})\sum_{l=k^{\prime}}^{k^{\prime\prime}-1}\frac{1}{\theta_{l}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\kappa_{\mathrm{lf}}^{2}+\kappa_{\mathrm{lj}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}})\left(\frac{1}{\theta_{k^{\prime}}}+\sum_{l=k^{\prime}+1}^{k^{\prime\prime}-1}\frac{1}{\theta_{l}}\right).

(4.52)

It then follows from Lemma
4.5
that
\frac{1}{\theta_{k^{\prime}}}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2(\kappa_{\mathrm{lf}}^{2}+\kappa_{\mathrm{lj}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}})}
holds for sufficiently large
k^{\prime}
.
Thus

\sum_{l=k^{\prime}+1}^{k^{\prime\prime}-1}\frac{1}{\theta_{l}}>\frac{\varepsilon}{2(\kappa_{\mathrm{lf}}^{2}+\kappa_{\mathrm{lj}}\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}})}>0
.
Therefore we have

\sum_{l\in\Omega_{2}}\frac{1}{\theta_{l}}=+\infty,

(4.53)

which contradicts Lemma
4.7
.
So (
4.48
) holds true.
The proof is completed.
∎

We present the numerical results by using the performance profile
[
13
]
.
Denote by
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{P}
the sets of algorithms and test problems, respectively.
If a solver
s\in\mathcal{S}
gives a point
x
that satisfies

f(x)\leq\tau f(x_{0})+(1-\tau)f_{p}^{*},

(5.2)

where
\tau\in(0,1)
is an accuracy level and
f_{p}^{*}
is the smallest value of the objective function of problem
p
obtained by any solver in
\mathcal{S}
,
we say that
s\in\mathcal{S}
solves
p\in\mathcal{P}
up to the convergence test.

Let
N_{s,p}
be the least number of function evaluations of
F(x)
required by the solver
s\in\mathcal{S}
to solve the problem
p\in\mathcal{P}
up to the convergence test.
If
s
fails to satisfy (
5.2
) for
p
within
1000(n+1)
function evaluations, we let
N_{s,p}=\infty
.
The performance profile of
s
is defined as

\pi_{s}(\alpha):=\frac{\mathrm{card}\left(\Big\{p\in\mathcal{P}\mid N_{s,p}\leq\alpha\cdot\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\{N_{s,p}\}\Big\}\right)}{\mathrm{card}(\mathcal{P})}

(5.3)

for
\alpha\geq 1
, where
\mathrm{card}(\cdot)
is the cardinality of a set.
Hence,
\pi_{s}(\alpha)
is the proportion of problems that are solved by
s
with function evaluations at most
\alpha
times of
\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\{N_{s,p}\}
.
In particular,
\pi_{s}(1)
is the proportion of problems that
s
performs better than any other solvers in
\mathcal{S}

and
\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow\infty}\pi_{s}(\alpha)
is the proportion of problems that can be solved by
s
.
