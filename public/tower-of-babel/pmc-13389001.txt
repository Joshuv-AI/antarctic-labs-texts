Very low-carbohydrate ketogenic diet in treatment-naïve women with endometrial cancer and overweight: a randomized feasibility study
Authors: Ezequiel Dantas, Katie C Hootman, Jenna Moyer, Morgan Tomberlin, Katherine Curran, Bhavani Ramesh, Hannah Price, Jeshua Kim, Alex C McPherson, Maurice A Hurd, Yuan-Shan Zhu, Andrew J Plodkowski, Erica Nagase, Anastasia Tsomides, Brandon M Cuevas, M Laura Martin, John Nguyen, Gabrielle Bennetti, Gissell Botero Rodriguez, Kaity Chang, Marissa Mezzancello, Ginger J Gardner, Vance Broach, Jennifer J Mueller, Yukio Sonoda, Oliver Zivanovic, Mario M Leitao, Alexia Iasonos, Krysten Soldan, Karen Carthew, Vania Hom, Francis N Villamater, Rema Rao Chaari, Kathryn Gorski, Michael Sigouros, Carol Aghajanian, Olivier Elemento, Lewis C Cantley, Benjamin D Hopkins, Britta Weigelt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis multicenter, prospective, randomized controlled trial (NCT03285152) evaluates the primary endpoint of feasibility of a very-low carbohydrate diet (VLCD) in 19 women with obesity/overweight and endometrial cancer, who are randomized 2:1 to either a VLCD or a standard diet for 21–28 days. Fifteen participants complete the study, with 91 ± 4% of VLCD meals consumed, 5.5 ± 0.8% weight lost, and no grade 3/4 adverse events. Secondary endpoints include assessments of tumor biology and circulating metabolic biomarkers. Fasting glucose and insulin fall 22 ± 5.9% and 60 ± 3.8%, while total cholesterol and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) rise 6 ± 2.7% and 17.8 ± 8.9%. In a pre-specified exploratory outcome, RNA-Seq shows enrichment of CD8 + T-cells (q < 0.034; NES > 1.75), confirmed by immunohistochemistry (IHC) showing CD8 + T-cells infiltration of the tumor margins (q = 0.034; NES = 1.75). We conclude that VLCD is feasible and well-tolerated.Subject terms: Endometrial cancer, Obesity

Introduction

Endometrial cancer is the most common gynecologic malignancy and the leading cause of death from gynecologic cancer1. One of the strongest risk factors for endometrial cancer is obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m2), which accounts for about 50% of cases in Europe and the US2. Women with obesity have a 3-fold increased risk of developing endometrial cancer, and women with obesity and endometrial cancer have an up to 6.25-fold higher risk of death compared with women of normal weight with this cancer2,3. Obesity induces a variety of systemic changes that favor tumor initiation and progression4. Some of these factors, such as hyperinsulinemia, increased sex hormones, and chronic inflammation, have been directly implicated in the pathogenesis of endometrial cancer, and may underlie the strong association of obesity with this cancer type5.

Endometrial cancer development is associated with clinical biomarkers of hyperinsulinemia such as high fasting insulin, c-peptide, fasting glucose, hemoglobin A1C, and dietary glycemic load5–10. Insulin stimulates the growth of the endometrial mucosa, especially in the proliferative phase of the endometrial cycle, during which insulin-like growth factor (IGF) signaling is essential11. IGF-1 and insulin bind to the same cell surface receptors to activate phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K), a lipid kinase that promotes several intracellular signaling pathways (e.g., AKT/mTOR) that mediate the production of macromolecules and drive proliferation12. PI3K activation is sufficient for the initiation of carcinoma in the adult uterine epithelia13, and enhancements in the PI3K signaling pathway dominate the genetics of human endometrial cancer14. For example, more than 90% of human endometrial tumors have alterations in the PI3K signaling pathway, most often activating mutations in PIK3CA (the gene encoding the catalytic subunit of PI3K), or loss of function mutations in PTEN, the gene encoding a phosphatase with activity that directly opposes that of PI3K15,16.

Diets with high glycemic load, those rich in refined carbohydrates and simple sugars, induce hyperinsulinemia and predispose to endometrial cancer. Glycemic load is the single best dietary predictor of postprandial blood glucose and insulin concentrations, explaining about 90% of the variance17,18. Several large prospective cohort studies support a link between glycemic load and endometrial cancer development8–10. For example, the risk for endometrial cancer is estimated to increase 15% for every 50 units of glycemic load per day, and 18% for every 100 g of carbohydrate per day. No studies have evaluated the effects of glycemic load or dietary carbohydrate on biomarkers of endometrial cancer growth, signaling, or immunity. Conversely, very-low-carbohydrate diets (VLCD) potently lower insulin levels and improve insulin sensitivity in humans and murine models19–22. VLCDs have proven to be safe in adults with advanced cancer, including women with gynecologic cancers, where this dietary pattern lowers insulin and promotes the selective loss of fat mass23,24.

In this study, we randomized eligible women with endometrial cancer to receive a VLCD or continue their standard diet (SD) during a pre-surgical window period between diagnosis and surgical staging. Both experimental arms received weekly nutritional counseling. We examine the systemic effects of this dietary pattern on glucose and lipid homeostasis, and interrogate tumor markers of PI3K signaling, proliferation, and immunity using a combination of immunohistochemistry (IHC) and RNA sequencing (RNASeq).

Results

Study design and patient disposition

We performed a multicentre, parallel, prospective, randomized, controlled, feasibility study in treatment naïve women with endometrial cancer during the presurgical window period between diagnosis and surgical staging. Participants were enrolled for 42 months between February 2018 and August 2021 at seven Memorial Sloan Kettering locations in New York and New Jersey (IRB no:17-396 A(14)). Of 775 people screened, 19 fulfilled the enrollment criteria and agreed to participate (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 1, and Supplementary Data 1). The most common reasons for screen failure were that the patient: never consented for surgery or the appointment was canceled (22%), declined participation without stating a reason (22%), had too low a BMI (12%), did not want to delay surgery (11%), was never approached by their physician (7.5%), had diabetes on active treatment (6.4%), or was limited by travel or their location (5.7%). After the biopsy results were reviewed, participants were randomized in a 2:1 fashion to receive a VLCD or to continue with their usual, standard diet (SD) until the day of surgery, 3-4 weeks later. Of the 19 participants randomized, 15 completed the study (Supp. Fig. 1).

The clinical and demographic characteristics of the participants are shown in Supplementary Data 1. Briefly

Participants were white (60%), non-Hispanic (66%), middle-aged (median age 57, range 37–74), with obesity (median BMI 37 with range 26–60 kg/m2), with the exception of 3, who would be considered with overweight (BMI: 26.1, 28.2, and 28.7). Tumor stages ranged from IA to IIIC2 based on the FIGO 2009 staging guidelines. The VLCD group had a higher average starting body weight due, primarily, to two subjects with class III obesity that were randomized to the VLCD group. Baseline and endpoint body composition characteristics were similar between groups (Supplementary Table 2). The baseline glucose, insulin, hemoglobin A1C (HbA1C), and lipid measures were comparable between groups and were consistent with a population with obesity/overweight, glucose intolerance or pre-diabetes (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2).

Using targeted tumor-sequencing (MSK-IMPACT), we identified many tumor driver mutations in the PI3K pathway and other mutations that allowed us to subtype the tumors into clinically relevant genomic classes encompassing the complete spectrum of the major TCGA genomic subtypes (1 POLE, 5 MSI, 7 copy-number low, 2 copy-number high)25.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome of the study was feasibility, which was assessed by measuring recruitment rate, overall attrition, meal completion rate, adherence to the study protocols, and dietary adherence. Since 19 participants enrolled over 42 months, the recruitment rate was 0.5 participants per month. While this value was below the anticipated recruitment of 2 participants per month, it was pre-specified in the protocol that lower-than-expected recruitment rates would not contribute to the decision to deem the study as unfeasible. Four of the 19 participants were randomized but did not complete the study, making the overall attrition 21%. This value was below the pre-specified cutoff value of 25% for feasibility. The 4 participants who were randomized but did not complete the study were either found to be ineligible (low kidney function and inconclusive histology) or withdrew because of pre-planned personal travel during the study period (Supp. Fig. 1). The remaining participants completed 100% of the scheduled visits, blood draws, meal pick-ups, imaging appointments, and quality of life (QOL) assessments. Participants in the VLCD arm reported consuming 91 ± 4% of the meals that were offered, which was above our pre-specified threshold of 90% for feasibility. Dietary adherence was reflected by a rapid rise in urine ketones by day 3 or 4 in all subjects (Supplementary Table 4) and high levels of plasma BHB by the end of the first week (1.9 ± 0.4 vs 0.17 ± 0.4 mM, p < 0.01) and throughout the intervention period (Fig. 1A). Based on these data, the approach met the pre-specified feasibility criteria, although the slower-than-anticipated recruitment rate highlighted a need to optimize recruitment in future studies.

The most notable diet-related adverse event (AE) was anorexia, which is a known effect of the VLCD26,27. All participants consuming the VLCD reported some degree of anorexia and experienced weight loss, as described below, without evidence of malnutrition. No patient left the study due to dissatisfaction with or inability to adhere to the VLCD diet. A quality of life (QOL) questionnaire showed no significant difference in perceived overall quality of life, activity, or energy levels (Supplementary Table 5). There were four cases of primary hyperuricemia (Grade 1) and one case of hypomagnesemia (Grade 1). There were no liver-related events; however, one subject on the VLCD started the study with fatty liver disease and a long-standing elevation in circulating transaminases (baseline AST 60, ALT 122), which resolved over the 4-week VLCD period (week 4 AST 18, ALT 24).

Secondary outcomes

Over the study period, the VLCD had significant effects on body weight and measures of systemic glucose and lipid metabolism (Fig. 1). Weight was reduced by an average of 5.5 ± 0.8% in subjects consuming the VLCD (Fig. 1B), and fasting plasma glucose and insulin levels were reduced by 22 ± 6% and 60 ± 4%, respectively, over the 4 weeks (Fig. 1C/D). Total cholesterol and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) were increased by 6 ± 3% and 18 ± 9%, respectively (Fig. 1E/F), and triglycerides tended to be lower over the study period (Fig. 1G). There were no changes in HDL (Fig. 1H). These data are consistent with the known metabolic effects of VLCDs in this population23, and highlight the potent suppression of insulin that can be achieved.

Glucose, insulin, insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF1), and leptin are thought to be the main factors supporting the link between obesity and cancer4. We measured the changes in these factors during the intervention period. The VLCD led to reductions in glycemia and insulin secretion, as indicated by drops in HbA1C and c-peptide (Supp. Fig. 2A/B). Furthermore, the levels of IGF1 and leptin were also suppressed by the VLCD and not the SD (Supplementary Fig. 2C/D). Other circulating hormones and cytokines known to modulate tumor growth and the tumor immune microenvironment, including adiponectin, cortisol, IGF2, C-Reactive Protein (CRP), Interleukin-6 (IL-6), Tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), and IGF-binding protein 3 (IGFBP3), were unchanged (Supp. Table 6).

We hypothesized that the suppression of circulating insulin and IGF1 would alter histologic markers of tumor PI3K signaling. Therefore, we performed IHC on tumor tissue obtained before (biopsy) and after (surgical resection) the diet intervention using a validated antibody against phosphorylated AKT (Ser473), a readout of PI3K activity. Although both the SD and the VLCD exhibited a decreasing trend in p-AKT tumor staining, a statistically significant reduction was observed only in participants consuming the VLCD (Supplementary Fig. 3A–C). We did not observe any histological changes in the apoptosis marker cleaved caspase-3 (CC3), or the cell proliferation marker Ki-67 (Supplementary Fig. 3D–I). These data suggest the VLCD intervention may limit the effects of obesity on tumor growth.

Exploratory outcomes

To gain insights into the effects of VLCD on the tumor, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) on tissue obtained at the time of biopsy and surgery, as part of the pre-specified exploratory objectives. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that sample distribution was primarily explained by TCGA molecular subtype28. Biopsy and resection pairs from the same patient clustered tightly and in proximity to other samples sharing that same molecular subtype (Fig. 2A). Next, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to gain a deeper understanding of which pathways were most significantly impacted by the VLCD. This analysis revealed that the VLCD upregulated gene expression related to oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid metabolism and interferon-alpha response (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Data 2). Of note, VLCD also enriched pathways related to hypoxia and estrogen response (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Data 2).

The suppression of insulin and IGF1 following intervals of fasting has been shown to reshape intratumor immunity in subjects with breast cancer29. Thus, to assess the effects of the VLCD diet on the tumor microenvironment, we performed GSEA using cancer-specific pathways30. We found that “major histocompatibility complex class II (MHCII)” and “Effector Cells” showed the highest enrichments, suggesting that VLCD facilitates an anti-tumor microenvironment (Fig.3A)30. GSEA with cell-specific signatures confirmed this by showing an enrichment in the CD8 + T-Cell signature (Fig. 3B)30. We investigated these unexpected results by adding as a non-pre-specified exploratory objective, the histological quantification of CD8 + T cells. In agreement with the RNA-seq results, immunohistochemistry showed that the VLCD increased the number of CD8⁺ T cells at the tumor myometrium interface (Fig. 3C–E). However, no significant differences in CD8⁺ T cells were observed within the tumor (Supp. Fig. 3J–L).

Discussion

Dietary changes can lead to profound physiologic, metabolic, and immune changes, which might aid in treating cancer. Preclinical evidence shows that a VLCD is an effective way to control hyperinsulinemia related to treatment with PI3K inhibitors and that it might facilitate antitumoral response31–33. However, several questions remain regarding the feasibility and efficacy of a VLCD in human subjects with cancer.

Here, we evaluate the feasibility of implementing VLCD in women with endometrial cancer while assessing its effects on whole-body metabolism, tumor PI3K signaling, and anti-tumor immunity. Overall, our data suggest that the VLCD is safe and feasible albeit with a notably slow rate of recruitment. As with many studies performed during this time, recruitment was likely impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic. Pandemic-related restrictions and participant concerns about in-person visits could all have contributed to a slow recruitment pace. Future studies would benefit from the use of targeted advertising platforms, hospital newsletters, and patient-facing materials to enhance awareness. Also, our approach could be optimized to reduce participation burden. For example, adding a meal delivery option and remote blood collection sites would have made participation more attractive.

This study finds that the VLCD is a highly effective, insulin-lowering intervention. These results agree with Cohen et al. who reported durable reductions in fasting insulin after 12 weeks of a VLCD in women with ovarian or endometrial cancer23. Similarly, our results show that a VLCD is effective in decreasing systemic IGF-1, and the overall reduction in these circulating mitogens correlated with reduced PI3K pathway activity in the tumor as indicated by lower p-AKT abundance. The reductions in insulin and tumor PI3K activity led to some expected and unexpected changes in tumor gene expression. For example, increased oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid metabolism were expected following high dietary lipid exposure, which can activate peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors34. Also, increases in estrogen and Myc signaling following the VLCD could have been predicted from prior data showing that these pathways are known escape mechanisms of PI3K inhibition35–38. We did not expect to find an increase in the interferon-alpha response in the tumors of participants; however, this pathway could have led to the infiltration of cytotoxic CD8 + T cells surrounding the tumors. The signals driving the local infiltration of these cells remains unknown and future studies should address why this infiltration of CD8 + T cells in the margin did not translate into increased tumor infiltration nor cytotoxicity.

This study provides valuable human data to support the use of a VLCD intervention in women with endometrial cancer; however, it has several limitations. First, the small sample size might have reduced the statistical power needed for elucidating changes in the histological and transcriptomic datasets. Endometrial cancer consists of multiple molecular subtypes, each with distinct genetic, epigenetic, and metabolic profiles39. These subtypes may respond differently to treatments, including dietary interventions and a small sample size with the presence of multiple subtypes can dilute the ability to detect statistically significant differences in outcomes. Furthermore, randomization cannot guarantee equal distribution when the small sample size is small, which can explain how the VLCD group had a higher average starting body weight, VAT, and SAT. Second, we are limited by the short study duration. This period may not be representative of the long-term effects and sustainability of VLCD in people with cancer. However, our study shows that a short period of VLCD is sufficient to induce significant systemic and tumor metabolic changes, which is encouraging for longer-term studies. Third, this study focused on women with endometrial cancer with planned surgical resection. This design element was required to compare tumor tissue before and after the dietary intervention; however, it may preclude the generalization of our results to those with relapsed and refractory disease. Another significant limitation is the histologic comparison of biopsy and surgical resection tissue. This approach is limited by sampling error inherent in the biopsy procedure using Pipelle curettes. Pipelle curettes are safe and highly sensitive but they are performed without visual guidance and are highly influenced by endometrial thickness. This contrasts with surgical resection performed in an operating room under anesthesia, which recovers more tissue and preserves histoarchitecture. Finally, the biopsy sample was limited by other sources of uncontrolled confounding, such as the time of day, the participants’ stress levels, the interval since their last meal, and concurrent medications. For instance, we found that the p-AKT levels showed a decreasing trend when comparing biopsy and resection even in subjects consuming the SD. This change could be attributed to the recommended interval of fasting before surgery, which itself suppresses insulin and may conceal some of the effects of the VLCD.

Despite these limitations, our study shows that a VLCD is safe and feasible in women with endometrial cancer and that even a short duration of VLCD is sufficient to alter CD8 + T cell infiltration. These potential therapeutic benefits warrant further investigation in larger clinical trials, and the data presented here should be viewed as hypothesis-generating.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study (NCT03285152) was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (IRB no. 17-396 A(14)). All participants provided informed consent.

Participants and randomization

Potential study participants (775) were screened by a member of the study team using the electronic medical record if they had a scheduled appointment with a member of the gynecologic oncology team. Only female sex participants were recruited, independent of gender identity. Patients self-reported their race and ethnicity using a prespecified list of categories. Ethnicity: Hispanic 7 (29.2%), Non-Hispanic 15 (62.5%), Unknown 2 (8.3%). Race: Asian 1 (4.2%), Black 3 (12.5%), Other 3 (12.5%), Refused 2 (8.3%), White 15 (62.5%).Study inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied (Supp. Table 1). Patients underwent an endometrial biopsy and, once a pathologic diagnosis was made, participants were randomized 2:1 (VLCD/SD) by the method of random permuted block within MSK’s electronic Clinical Research Database (CRDBi) system. The allocation sequence was generated by statisticians at the beginning of the trial. Participants were consented by gynecologic surgeons with aid from research assistants and the intervention was assigned following the randomization assignment. The sample size was not determined based on statistical considerations but rather based on sole feasibility and based on previous literature40. The protocol and its records can be accessed at https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03285152. Twelve additional ancillary cases were added to the standard diet arm for histology analysis, as detailed in the CONSORT diagram and Supp. Data 1. Nine were from Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center, IRB Number: 20-272 A(7), and three were sourced from www.tissuearray.com, (cat no. T094a).

Interventions

Participants randomized to the VLCD group received a 7-day cycle menu consisting of both frozen and fresh pre-prepared meals at the Clinical and Translational Science Center Metabolic Research Kitchen at Weill Cornell Medicine. For each day, subjects received a complete multivitamin (One A Day®, Bayer), 3 meals, and 2 snacks, that were formulated to meet individualized estimated energy needs. The composition of the VLCD was formulated with the daily ratio of 3:1 of dietary fat to the sum of dietary carbohydrate and protein (without dietary fiber). Each subject received a eucaloric ketogenic diet that was formulated to meet individualized estimated energy needs. Energy needs were estimated using the Mifflin St. Jeor and Harris-Benedict formulae. Minimum protein needs were individualized and based on 80-100% of the Recommended Dietary Allowance for adults, 0.8 gram protein per kilogram ideal body weight in accordance with clinical guidelines. On average, the VLCD provided 2,139 ± 110 kilocalories (kcal) per day, with 87.2% of energy from fat, 8.7% from protein, and 6.2% from carbohydrate (Supp. Table 3). If weight loss was noted in participants, the calorie prescription was increased to promote weight regain. The nutrient composition of the ketogenic diet was analyzed using The Food Processor, Esha Research, version 11.6.441, and nutritional data were compiled and summarized using Python version 3.11.4 and Microsoft Excel version 2302. Participants consumed this diet for 26.2 ± 1.9 days. Subjects on the standard diet arm were not provided food, yet received the same weekly contact with the study dietitian and completed all other study assessments. Of note, one participant missed one of the meal pickup visits but was able to reschedule it 4 days later and remained adherence to a VLCD in the interim with guidance from the study dietitian. Diet-related adverse events (AEs) were assessed using CTCAE v5.0.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint is feasibility assessment, with no formal decision rules or statistical considerations. Feasibility is assessed by recruitment rate, attrition, demographics, completed laboratory assessments, meal procurement and completion rates, and quality of life surveys. The anticipated recruitment rate was two per month; however, the protocol prespecified that any rate lower than this expected value will be investigated but will not lead to study investigators to consider the study a failure of feasibility. Pre-specified secondary outcomes in this manuscript include fasting insulin, C-peptide, glucose, lipid profiles, leptin, adipokines, cortisol, C-reactive protein, beta-hydroxybutyrate, non-esterified fatty acids, adiponectin, IGFBP-3, IGF-1, IGF-2, and circulating inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IFN-γ. Additional secondary outcomes evaluating the effects of the ketogenic diet on the tumor include immunohistochemical markers (including Ki-67 and p-AKT) and tumor size. Other reported secondary outcomes include body composition, blood pressure, BMI, and quality of life, as assessed by the EORTC QLQ-C30 and supplementary EORTC QLQ-EN24 questionnaires. Exploratory outcomes reported in this manuscript include IHC for cell apoptosis (CC3), CD8 + T-Cells, and RNA Sequencing with pathway analysis.

Secondary outcomes preplanned but not performed due to budgetary prioritization include glucagon and IL-1β measurements, proteomic and metabolomic testing of presurgical tumor biopsy and surgical pathology and measurements of estradiol and SHBG, which were deemed lower priority; most patients were in the post-menopausal range. Due to scant pathological material, IHC for p-STAT3, p-ERK, and GLUT-1 was not performed. IHC for p-INSR was initiated but not completed because the antibody was discontinued by the manufacturer (SAB1300309-100ug).

Relevant amendments to the protocol from the starting date (2017-08-11) include changes in the eligibility criteria, such as exclusion of those with dietary intolerances like allergies, the inclusion of those with carcinosarcoma and stage IVB endometrial cancer. Additionally, treatment length was modified to 3-4 weeks to allow for flexibility in the surgery date, and those in the SD arm were allowed to proceed to surgery sooner if deemed medically necessary. All these changes were made to facilitate patient enrollment. Recruitment ended on 2023-07 due to slow recruitment and having reached a number of participants deemed sufficient to evaluate feasibility. Complete protocol can be accessed at https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03285152.

Nutrition education and counseling

All participants met with the study dietitian weekly. Participants in the intervention group were provided nutritional education regarding the ketogenic diet and were monitored for anthropometric changes, dietary tolerance, and intervention compliance. Participants in the control group reviewed self-reported dietary intake with the dietitian at weekly appointments; the dietitian monitored anthropometry, dietary intake, and counseled participants as needed to prevent intervention contamination.

Quality of life questionnaire

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) was assessed at baseline and end of the intervention using the EORTC QLQ-C30 and the endometrial cancer-specific QLQ-EN24. Item responses were transformed to 0–100 subscale scores with the standard EORTC linear algorithm. Between-group differences were calculated by testing the change (endpoint minus baseline) with Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, with FDR correction with Benjamini–Hochberg. Results are reported as median change (IQR).

Biochemical markers

Serum determinations were performed at the Clinical and Translational Science Center (Weill Cornell Medicine) using the following kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions: c-peptide (ALPCO, #80-CPTHU-E01), IGF-1 (R&D, #DG100), IGF-2 (R&D, #DG200) and IGFBP3 (R&D, #DGB300). The cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, IFN-γ, TNF-α) and CRP were measured using MesoScale #K15052G and #K151STG respectively. Cortisol was measured using radioimmunoassay (RIA) from IBL America #KIRI28000. Leptin and adiponectin were measured using ELISA from IBL America #E07 and R&D #DRP300 respectively.

Histology

Immunohistochemistry was performed at the Center for Translational Pathology (Department of Pathology & Laboratory Medicine at Weill Cornell Medicine). Stainings were performed using Leica Bond refine kit DS9800 in a BOND- III autostainer with the following primary antibodies pAKT (CST, #4060, 1:100 dilution), CD8 (Leica, #PA0183, ready to use), Ki67(Leica, #PA0118, ready to use) and CC3 (CST, #9661, 1:200 dilution). Antigen retrieval was performed at pH9, for 20 minutes at 100 °C. Slides were scanned using an Aperio GT450, and slides were annotated and stainings quantified using QuPath41 on whole slides previously annotated by a pathologist (L.H.E.). Tumor margins were defined as the area surrounding the tumor, up to 1 mm. Slides with no sufficient tumor material in the biopsies or tumor were not included in the analysis.

RNA-sequencing

RNA was extracted from the biopsies and tumor resections using the Maxwell® RSC simplyRNA Tissue Kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The quality and quantity of extracted RNA were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Tumor RNA samples were selected for library preparation based on the RNA Integrity Number (RIN). Libraries were prepared using either TruSeq Stranded mRNA Sample Library Preparation Kit (PolyA method) or Illumina Stranded Total RNA Prep with Ribo-Zero Plus (rRNA depletion method) (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Reads from paired-end FASTQ files were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh37 (hg19) using STAR-Fusion, and quality controlled using FastQC. Raw counts for each gene were generated using HTSeq with gencode v19 annotation. RNA-Seq analysis was done in R Studio R Studio (2023-10-31), with R (4.3.2) and DESeq2 (v.1.42.1). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), and the resulting p-values and p.adjusted values and Normalized Enrichment Scores (NES) were obtained using the R package fGSEA(1.32.4) and the GSEA JAVA desktop application v4.3.2. Tumor purity was estimated using PUREE42 and ESTIMATE43 packages. Participants 7 and 14 were excluded from further RNASeq analysis due to low purity.

MSK-IMPACT targeted sequencing

Mutations were assessed using Integrated Mutation Profiling of Actionable Cancer Targets (MSK- IMPACT) at Memorial Sloan Kettering (MSK)44. The MSK-IMPACT assay interrogates 505 cancer-associated genes and reports somatic mutation count, POLE mutation status, TP53 mutation status, MSIsensor score, tumor purity, fraction of genome altered (FGA), and tumor mutational burden (TMB)44,45. POLE exonuclease-domain hotspot mutations were defined according to Leon-Castillo et al46,47. MSIsensor scores were interpreted as follows: ≥10 as MSI-H, ≥3 and <10 as MSI-indeterminate, and <3 as stable (MSS), as described48,49. Molecular subtypes were assigned hierarchically: I) POLE subtype was defined by the presence of a recognized hotspot mutation in the POLE exonuclease domain46,47. II) MSI-H subtype was assigned when the MSIsensor score was ≥1048–51. III) CN-H subtype was called based on a TP53 homozygous deletion or a pathogenic driver mutation curated by OncoKB, CIViC, and/or Cancer Hotspots in cBioPortal52–57. IV) CN-L subtype was assigned to samples lacking any defining features of the other categories45.

Statistics and reproducibility

This study was a multicenter, parallel, prospective, randomized, controlled feasibility study in 19 treatment naïve women with obesity/overweight and endometrial cancer. Participants were randomized in a 2:1 fashion to a very-low carbohydrate (VLCD) diet, or to continue with their standard diet (SD) by the method of random permuted block. Participants and investigators were not blinded. The sample size was not determined based on statistical considerations but rather based on sole feasibility and based on previous literature40. Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10 or RStudio (2023.03.0 + 386) with R (v. 4.2.3). Normality was tested using the Shapiro-Wilks test and comparisons were made using either two-sided Wilcoxon or T-Tests in prism or R using the package ggpubr (0.6.0) and rstatix (v.0.7.2). Plots were made in GraphPad Prism 10 or R using ggplot2 (v.3.4.4). False discovery rate was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg correction in R, unless stated otherwise. In the RNA-Seq analysis samples from participants 7 and 14 were excluded due to low tumor purity calculated using PUREE42 and ESTIMATE43 R packages. On the histological analysis, slides with no tumor in their sections or with technical artifacts were excluded from analysis. For boxplots horizontal bars within boxes represent medians. Tops and bottoms of boxes represent 25th and 75th per- centiles, and vertical lines extend to the 1.5× interquartile range.
