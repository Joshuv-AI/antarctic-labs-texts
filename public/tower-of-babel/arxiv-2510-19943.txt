Quantum geometry and impurity sensitivity of superconductors without time-reversal symmetry: application to rhombohedral graphene and altermagnets
Denis Sedov, Mathias S. Scheurer

Abstract
Analyzing the consequences of the quantum geometry induced by the momentum dependence of Bloch states has emerged as a very rich and active field in condensed matter physics. For instance, for the superfluid stiffness or the pairing mechanism, these geometric aspects can play an important role. We here demonstrate that quantum geometry can also be essential for the disorder sensitivity of a superconductor, in particular when time-reversal symmetry is broken in the normal-state Bloch Hamiltonian. We derive a general expression for the behavior of the critical temperature T c T_{c} involving weighted (anti-)commutators of the superconducting order parameter and impurity matrix elements, which includes both wave-function effects and kinetic pair breaking due to broken time-reversal symmetry in the dispersion. We analyze how the former effects lead to “quantum geometric pair breaking”, where any superconductor becomes susceptible to microscopically non-magnetic impurities, and formally relate it to the maximum possible localization of two-particle Wannier states. Further, in the presence of kinetic pair breaking, impurities can also enhance pairing, leading to an overall more complex, non-monotonic behavior of T c T_{c} with impurity concentration. We also analyze the fate of finite-momentum pairing. Our results are directly relevant to rhombohedral graphene, twisted MoTe 2 , and superconducting altermagnets.

A second reason why TRS is central to the theoretical description of pairing is its role in gauge fixing. Consider a superconducting order parameter
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}
in a single (possibly spin-degenerate) band with Bloch states
\ket{\phi_{\mathbf{k}}}

\ket{\phi_{\mathbf{k}}}\rightarrow e^{i\alpha_{\mathbf{k}}}\ket{\phi_{\mathbf{k}}},

(1)

Our general results are illustrated using concrete models, relevant to the aforementioned systems, as examples.

II
Results

In this work, we are interested in the stability of superconductivity in the presence of impurities modeled by

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{dis}}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\int d\mathbf{r}\,V(\mathbf{r})\psi^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})w_{\alpha\beta}\psi_{\beta}^{\phantom{\dagger}}(\mathbf{r}),

(2)

where
\psi^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
are the field operators creating an electron with internal index
\alpha
(e.g., sublattice or layer quantum numbers) at position
\mathbf{r}
(either in the continuum or on a lattice);
V(\mathbf{r})
is the local quenched disorder potential, which we assume to be
\delta
-correlated,
\braket{V(\mathbf{r})V(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}_{\mathrm{dis}}=\gamma^{2}\mathcal{V}\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
or
\braket{V_{\mathbf{q}}V_{-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}}_{\mathrm{dis}}=\gamma^{2}\delta_{\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}
in Fourier space, with system volume
\mathcal{V}
and disorder strength
\gamma
. Furthermore,
w
is a matrix describing the internal structure of the impurities, in general only constrained by Hermiticity to obey
w^{\dagger}=w
.

As superconductivity is typically a low-energy phenomenon, it is natural to go to the eigenbasis of the normal-state Bloch Hamiltonian
h_{\mathbf{k}}
and further project onto the “active band” at the Fermi level with energies
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}
and wave functions
\ket{\phi_{\mathbf{k}}}
, respectively. This is a common approximation in this context as superconducting matrix elements to higher-energy bands away from the Fermi level are often negligible, and since scattering processes involving other bands are suppressed by a factor of the squared inverse bandgap. On the level of field operators
\psi(\mathbf{r})\to 1/\mathcal{V}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\phi_{\mathbf{k}}e^{i\mathbf{kr}}d_{\mathbf{k}}
and the impurity Hamiltonian becomes

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{dis}}\to\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}V_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}d^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}d^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}},\,W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}=\braket{\phi_{\mathbf{k}}|w|\phi_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}.

(3)

Our general analysis will capture three different cases concerning the spin degree of freedom: (1) if the normal state is already spin polarized (e.g., by a Zeeman field or the spontaneous formation of a “quarter metal” in rhombohedral graphene
Han et al. 2025
or, locally in
\mathbf{k}
space, by the simultaneous presence of altermagnetic order), the aforementioned bands are generically non-degenerate and
d_{\mathbf{k}}
have no additional spin quantum number. This is also true (2) in the limit of strong spin-orbit coupling and broken inversion symmetry where the bands are also non-degenerate and
d_{\mathbf{k}}
carry no additional index. Finally, (3) if the bands are spin degenerate, one should replace
d_{\mathbf{k}}\rightarrow d_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}
with an additional spin quantum number
\sigma
. Our analysis still applies to this case as well, but we assume for notational simplicity here that the impurities do not carry spin such that
W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
is not a matrix in spin space.

In this description, the superconducting order parameter is a
\mathbf{k}
-dependent complex function
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}
coupling to the electrons as

\sum_{\mathbf{k}}d^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}d^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k}+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2}}\,\text{ and }\,\sum_{\mathbf{k}}d^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2},\uparrow}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}d^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k}+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2},\downarrow}

(4)

for cases (1,2) and (3), respectively. For later reference, we here also allowed for finite momentum,
\mathbf{q}\neq 0
, Cooper pairs. Note that
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=-\Delta_{-\mathbf{k}}
for the first, spinless case in Eq. (
4
) due to Fermi statistics.

As already mentioned above, the gauge transformation (
1
) of the Bloch states affects
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}
. In the presence of TRS in the normal state,
\Theta h_{\mathbf{k}}\Theta^{\dagger}=h_{-\mathbf{k}}
, however, we have
\ket{\phi_{-\mathbf{k}}}=e^{i\rho_{\mathbf{k}}}\Theta\ket{\phi_{\mathbf{k}}}
which we can use to fix the gauge. Furthermore, TRS favors pairing of Kramers’ partners and thus
\mathbf{q}=0
. For cases (1) and (3), we effectively have
\Theta^{2}=\mathbbm{1}
and can thus choose
e^{i\rho_{\mathbf{k}}}=1
[see SI for the discussion of case (2)]. In this gauge, it holds

w=t\,\Theta w\Theta^{\dagger}\,\Rightarrow\,W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}=t(W_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})^{*},\quad t=\pm,

(5)

and it is natural to split a generic disorder matrix element into a non-magnetic (
t=+
) and magnetic (
t=-
) component,

W=W^{+}+W^{-}
with
W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{t}=t\left(W_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{t}\right)^{*}
.
The celebrated Anderson theorem
Anderson 1959
;
Abrikosov and Gor’kov 1959a
;
Abrikosov and Gor’kov 1959b
states that non-magnetic disorder matrix elements,
W=W^{+}
, do not affect the critical temperature of a BCS-like superconductor with
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\text{const.}
,
\mathbf{q}=0
, and assuming
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}=\xi_{-\mathbf{k}}
as also follows from TRS.

In the absence of TRS in the normal-state Hamiltonian, which is our main interest here, Eq. (
5
) is in general not valid anymore, such that already intrinsically nonmagnetic impurities (
w=\Theta w\Theta^{\dagger}
) lead to an effectively magnetic part
W^{-}\neq 0
, indicating that even a BCS-like state is suppressed by impurities. What is more, it is not even clear how to fix the gauge anymore, giving
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\text{const.}
a well-defined meaning. On top of this, the Anderson theorem heavily relies on the parent band dispersion being time-reversal symmetric and we will also analyze how
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}\neq\xi_{-\mathbf{k}}
affects the disorder sensitivity and interferes with the above-mentioned wave function effects.

We now briefly discuss the main approximations we will use to obtain analytical results. We first split the energy into the symmetric and antisymmetric parts,
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}=\tilde{\xi}_{\mathbf{k}}+a_{\mathbf{k}},\tilde{\xi}_{\mathbf{k}}=(\xi_{\mathbf{k}}+\xi_{-\mathbf{k}})/2,a_{\mathbf{k}}=(\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\xi_{-\mathbf{k}})/2

\mathbf{k}

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\ldots\to\int d\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{\rho}(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})\int d\tilde{\xi}\ldots,

(6)

where
\tilde{\rho}(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})
is the angular-resolved density of states of the symmetrized energies

We begin our analysis by expanding the particle-particle bubble to leading order of
\gamma^{2}
to derive a general expression for the dimensionless disorder sensitivity
\zeta
which captures the deviation of the critical temperature

\frac{T_{c}(\tau)-T_{c,0}}{T_{c,0}}=-\frac{\pi}{4T_{c,0}\tau}\zeta+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{-2}),

(7)

Allowing for an arbitrary order parameter

\zeta=\frac{\sum_{t=\pm}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left(\bigl|C_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},f_{1}}^{t}\bigr|^{2}-t\bigl|C_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},f_{2}}^{t}\bigr|^{2}\right)}{4\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left|W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\right|^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{FS}}{|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|}^{2}f_{3}(\mathbf{k})},

(8)

where
\sum_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\ldots=\tilde{\nu}^{-1}\int d\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{\rho}(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})\ldots
,
\tilde{\nu}=\int d\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{\rho}(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle C^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},f_{1}}

\displaystyle=W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}f_{1}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\pm f_{1}(\mathbf{k})\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}W_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{*},

(9a)

\displaystyle C^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},f_{2}}

\displaystyle=f_{2}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\left[W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\pm\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}W_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{*}\right].

(9b)

Writing
W=W^{+}+W^{-}
as before, one can think of
C^{\pm}_{f_{1,2}}
as the sum of the commutator of
\Delta
with
W^{\mp}
and of the anti-commutator of
\Delta
with
W^{\pm}
, with
\Delta
and
W
dressed by

\displaystyle f_{1}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\cosh(a_{\mathbf{k}}/T_{c,0})},

(10a)

\displaystyle f_{2}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}\tanh[\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}}{2T_{c,0}}]-a_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\tanh[\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}{2T_{c,0}}]}{(a_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}-a_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{2})/T_{c,0}}\right]^{1/2},

(10b)

respectively.
These factors are the spectral weights entering the perturbative expansion of the particle-particle bubble—
f_{1}
comes from the self-energy correction and
f_{2}
from the diagram with one disorder rung. Finally, the function
f_{3}
in Eq. (
8
) is proportional to the temperature derivative of the bare particle-particle bubble; for small

, it behaves as
f_{3}(\mathbf{k})\sim 1+\mathcal{O}(a_{\mathbf{k}}^{2})
and its exact form can be found in the SI.

Note that Eq. (
8
) readily reproduces the Anderson theorem: for
a_{\mathbf{k}}\rightarrow 0
, we see that
f_{1}=f_{2}=1/\sqrt{2}
such that, for non-magnetic impurities,
\zeta
is proportional to the commutator of
\Delta
and
W
, which vanishes for constant
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}
. Beyond this simple limit, Eq. (
8
) encodes wave function effects and the consequences of
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}\neq\xi_{-\mathbf{k}}
as well as their complex interplay. Equation (
8

\zeta<0
is possible, i.e., an enhancement of
T_{c}
with disorder. In the remainder of the paper, we will scrutinize these effects, different limits, and applications, both based on Eq. (

II.1
Quantum geometric pair breaking

We begin by isolating the effects of the wave functions and set
a_{\mathbf{k}}\rightarrow 0
. From the general expression for the suppression factor in Eq. (
8
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left|W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}-\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}W^{*}_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\right|^{2}}{4\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left|W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\right|^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\right|^{2}},

(11)

which is consistent with the expression derived in
Timmons et al. 2020
and agrees with
Andersen et al. 2020
for
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\text{const.}
, in the range of applicability of the results of
Andersen et al. 2020
.
Before applying Eq. (
11
) to some concrete examples, we will discuss how it can be used to define a natural gauge for a disordered superconductor without TRS. We formulate it as the gauge which, for
fixed

\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}
and
w
, yields minimal
\zeta
. Since the denominator of Eq. (
11
) is gauge invariant, we can focus on the numerator,

\displaystyle I=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left|W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}-\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}W^{*}_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\right|^{2}\geq 0.

(12)

This functional can be further split into two contributions,
I=2\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}|W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|^{2}-2\tilde{I}
; the first term is gauge-invariant, while the second part acquires momentum-dependent phase factors under Eq. (
1
). This allows us to write it explicitly as a function of
\bar{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}=\alpha_{\mathbf{k}}+\alpha_{-\mathbf{k}}
as

\tilde{I}\to\tilde{I}[\bar{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}]=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}W_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}e^{i(\bar{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}-\bar{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}})}.

(13)

To simplify the presentation, we will first study the spin degenerate case (3), before discussing (1,2) below. While one can in principle use any
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}
, the most natural approach for case (3) is to define the optimal gauge as the one that maximizes
\tilde{I}[\bar{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}]
for
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\Delta_{0}
, since it most directly connects to the case with TRS. In fact, in the presence of TRS in the normal state, there are phase factors
e^{i\rho_{\mathbf{k}}}=e^{i\rho_{-\mathbf{k}}}
such that
\ket{\phi_{-\mathbf{k}}}=e^{i\rho_{\mathbf{k}}}\Theta\ket{\phi_{\mathbf{k}}}
and it is straightforward to see that Eq. (
13
) is maximized by
e^{i\bar{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}}=e^{-i\rho_{\mathbf{k}}}
(thus, effectively,
e^{i\rho_{\mathbf{k}}}\rightarrow 1
) for any non-magnetic impurity with maximum value given by
\tilde{I}[\bar{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{opt}}]=|\Delta_{0}|^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}|W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|^{2}
. As such, our optimization procedure recovers the conventional phase choice for superconductors with TRS discussed above, with
I
further reaching its lower bound,
I_{\text{opt}}=0
, in line with Anderson’s theorem (
\zeta_{\text{opt}}=0
).

Without TRS, however, the optimization problem is in general “frustrated” in the sense that
I_{\text{opt}}>0
and, thus,
\zeta_{\text{opt}}>0
. Loosely speaking, this is expected since for
N_{k}
values of
\mathbf{k}
in Eq. (
12
), there are
N_{k}^{2}
values of
W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
but only
N_{k}
phases to optimize. Physically,
\zeta_{\text{opt}}>0
means that, irrespective of the phases of the superconducting order parameter, the underlying TRS-breaking part of the quantum geometry induces a pair breaking effect, which we dub “quantum geometric pair breaking”.

To provide a real-space interpretation of this effect, let us rewrite the pairing part of the Hamiltonian (
4
) in the original real-space basis,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}d^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}d^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}=\sum_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{x},\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r}-\frac{\mathbf{x}}{2},\alpha,\uparrow}D_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r}+\frac{\mathbf{x}}{2},\alpha^{\prime},\downarrow}.

(14)

Here and in the following, we absorbed the projection onto the Fermi surface into the redefinition of the order parameter,
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\to\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\delta(\xi)/\nu
, with weight located around the Fermi surface. The real-space form of the order parameter in Eq. (
14

\displaystyle D_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\phi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}\phi_{-\mathbf{k},\alpha^{\prime}}e^{i\mathbf{kx}}

(15)

and can be thought of as the analogue of a Wannier state for Cooper pairs, but constructed from the (for
|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|\neq\text{const.}

(U_{\mathbf{k}})_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}=\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\phi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}\phi_{-\mathbf{k},\alpha^{\prime}}
.
As
D
is complex and matrix-valued, we probe its spatial behavior using a real, scalar measure of the form

\displaystyle C_{v}(\mathbf{x}):=\mathrm{Tr}\left[D^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x})v^{*}D(\mathbf{x})v\right],\quad v^{\dagger}=v,

(16)

which picks up different components of
D
depending on the choice of

and becomes the Frobenius norm for
v=\mathbbm{1}
.
Remarkably, choosing
v=w
, we find
C_{w}(\mathbf{x}=0)=\tilde{I}
, meaning that the minimization of the superconducting suppression rate
\zeta
is equivalent to finding the gauge in which the Cooper pair wavefunction is maximally localized in the sense of having largest intra-unit-cell weight
C_{w}(\mathbf{x}=0)
or, equivalently, smallest
\sum_{\mathbf{x}\neq 0}C_{w}(\mathbf{x})

It also provides another perspective on the role of the quantum geometry for the stability of superconductivity in TRS-broken systems: similar to how quantum geometry constrains the localization of Wannier states in Bloch bands, non-trivial

II.2
Non-degenerate Fermi surfaces

For the cases (1,2) with non-degenerate Fermi surfaces, the superconducting order parameter in Eq. (
4
) obeys
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=-\Delta_{-\mathbf{k}}
. As this is inconsistent with
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\Delta_{0}
, one has to choose another
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}
to fix the gauge. While other possibilities are conceivable, we propose
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\Delta_{0}e^{i\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}}

\ket{\phi_{-\mathbf{k}}}=e^{i\rho_{\mathbf{k}}}\Theta\ket{\phi_{\mathbf{k}}}

\tilde{I}[\bar{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}]
in Eq. (
13
) is maximized for
e^{i\bar{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}}=e^{-i(\rho_{\mathbf{k}}+\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})}
yielding
I_{\text{opt}}=0
(Anderson theorem); note that this solution is only possible for case (2), where
e^{i\rho_{\mathbf{k}}}
is odd in
\mathbf{k}
. For case (1), the gauge choice optimization problem is already frustrated with TRS in the normal state and there is generically no Anderson theorem (
I_{\text{opt}}>0
), for any gauge choice or superconductor.

This, however, does
not
mean that the superconductor always effectively behaves like an unconventional state with
\zeta=1/2
. To illustrate this, let us again consider the matrix elements in Eq. (
17
) for
n=3
, i.e., R
3
G. For
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\Delta_{0}e^{i\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}}

\bar{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}=-4\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}
for all

only reaches

for
\eta=0,1
and is smaller in between, with an emergent Anderson theorem for
\eta=1/2
.
Similar to our discussion above, we can also reinterpret the gauge optimization as a search for the most stable superconductor, which in the current case turns out to be
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\Delta_{0}e^{-3i\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}}

\eta
.
We emphasize that
\zeta_{\text{opt}}<1/2

\eta\neq 0,1

As anticipated above, the physics and concepts discussed here also find a very natural application in altermagnets. To illustrate this with a concrete example, we use a two-sublattice model on the checkerboard lattice (see SI) and study it as a function of the altermagnetic order parameter
\Phi

\mathbf{k}

In Fig.

While the model and state are different, we note that our non-zero value of

Concrete pairing states

II.4
Energy effect

Having discussed the wave-function effects in detail while assuming
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}=\xi_{-\mathbf{k}}
, we next focus on the consequences of the TRS-breaking part of the dispersion,
a_{\mathbf{k}}=(\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\xi_{-\mathbf{k}})/2
, for the disorder sensitivity. To keep the discussion focused, let us assume that
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\Delta_{0}
for
\mathbf{k}
around the Fermi surface by properly adjusting the gauge.

We find it instructive to first identify the effect of
a_{\mathbf{k}}
on the clean superconductor. At zero temperature, the particle-particle bubble is proportional to
\sum_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\log(1+\omega_{D}^{2}/a^{2}_{\mathbf{k}})
, where
\omega_{D}
is the cutoff frequency introduced to regularize the usual logarithmic divergence at the upper limit.
For fixed
\omega_{D}
, this integral is finite as long as
a_{\mathbf{k}}
vanishes at most at isolated points on the Fermi surface; therefore, the emergence of superconductivity requires finite interaction strength, making the SC a strong-coupling instability, and
a_{\mathbf{k}}
can be thought of as “kinetic pair-breaking”.

Moving to the disordered SC, we first note that the disorder sensitivity factor
\zeta
in Eq. (
11
) is always non-negative when
a_{\mathbf{k}}=0
, and in the chosen gauge determined only by the magnetic part of the disorder
W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{-}
which is a hallmark of Anderson’s theorem. However, the general form in Eq. (
8
) can lead to either sign. To make this apparent, and since wave-function-related pair breaking was already discussed above, let us assume fully non-magnetic (
W=W^{+}
) disorder. Expanding Eq. (
11
) to leading order in
a_{\mathbf{k}}
, we get

\displaystyle\zeta=-\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left|W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\right|^{2}(a_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+a_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{2})}{48T_{c,0}^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left|W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\right|^{2}}\leq 0,

(19)

i.e., superconductivity is enhanced by disorder. Intuitively, this enhancement can be understood as follows: while preserving the coherence between electrons with
\mathbf{k}
and
-\mathbf{k}
, scattering off non-magnetic disorder (similar to scattering by phonons
Iorsh 2025
) also averages
a_{\mathbf{k}}
and suppresses its pair-breaking effect.
A related, but not identical enhancement with disorder was recently discussed in a model of altermagnetism
Vasiakin and Mel’nikov 2025
: as opposed to our discussion here, they studied a superconductor that is incompatible with the altermagnetic order parameter and, thus, suppressed by it (and enhanced by averaging it to zero). Note that our enhancement is also distinct from that discussed in Ref.
Zhitomirsky and Walker 1998
.

Our intuitive picture is further consistent with the behavior in the opposite limit of strong disorder (while still keeping
k_{F}l\gg 1
), where we find

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{T}_{c,0}-T_{c}(\tau)}{\tilde{T}_{c,0}}=\frac{\pi\tau}{2\tilde{T}_{c,0}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{FS}}a_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{1}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left|W_{\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{1}^{\prime}}\right|^{2}}{\sum_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left|W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\right|^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2}).

(20)

Here,
\tilde{T}_{c,0}
is the critical temperature of the clean superconductor when replacing
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}
with the symmetrized dispersion
\tilde{\xi}_{\mathbf{k}}
. Thus, the large-disorder limit is independent of the exact behavior of the antisymmetric part of the energies and is determined purely by
\tilde{\xi}_{\mathbf{k}}
. Since
T_{c,0}<\tilde{T}_{c,0}
, non-magnetic disorder enhances the critical temperature also for
\tau\rightarrow 0
(as long as localization corrections can be neglected).

Composite effect

We finally consider the interplay between the two discussed effects—quantum geometric and kinetic pair breaking. For concreteness, we apply it to the full 8-band continuum model

II.6
Finite-momentum pairing

Because of the energy mismatch

In Fig.

III
Discussion

In this work, we have analyzed the impact of impurity scattering on the stability of superconductivity emerging from a normal-state with broken time-reversal symmetry, i.e., without any anti-unitary relation between the Bloch states
\ket{\phi_{\mathbf{k}}}
and
\ket{\phi_{-\mathbf{k}}}
. Both wave-function effects as well as kinetic pair breaking have been taken into account and are shown to be essential for a complete description. These two effects, their consequences, and interplay can be most compactly seen in our general relation of the dimensionless disorder sensitivity
\zeta
in Eq. (
8
), where they enter in the form of the disorder matrix elements
W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}=\braket{\phi_{\mathbf{k}}|w|\phi_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}
and the antisymmetric part
a_{\mathbf{k}}=(\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\xi_{-\mathbf{k}})/2
of the normal-state dispersion, respectively.

First, our findings connect quantum geometry, which has previously been very actively studied in the context of different aspects of superconductivity, and the disorder sensitivity of the pairing state. We found that quantum geometry can lead to pair breaking in any superconducting state which we connect back to its obstruction in maximizing the weight of the Cooper-pair wavefunction in Eq. (
15
) in a single unit cell with respect to the measure in Eq. (
16
).
As this relation is very general and there are many systems with non-trivial quantum geometry and a rich set of impurities to study, this directly opens up a very broad set of possible follow-up studies. It will also be interesting to see whether other impurity-related features in superconductors, such as local density of states effects, can also be directly related to quantum geometry.

Second, we have seen that significant chirality in the normal state Bloch states can lead to a chiral topological or triplet superconductor being most stable against certain types of nominally non-magnetic impurities. Within the localization picture above, this can be seen as a result of the
\mathbf{k}
-dependent phases in the Bloch states (normal-state chirality) leading to maximal localization of
D(\mathbf{x})
in Eq. (
15
) for a non-trivial winding of the superconducting order parameter. This also leads to the counterintuitive result that if multiple pairing states, including a trivial (or spin singlet) superconductor and a topological chiral (triplet) state, are energetically close, impurities can be beneficial for realizing a chiral topological state (triplet pairing).

Third, by explicit computations for rhombohedral graphene, we showed that these quantum geometric effects lead to a significant dependence of the disorder-sensitivity of the critical temperature on the layer and type of pairing state. As such, using controlled disorder in this and other related systems, such as twisted MoTe
2
, could be used as a versatile tool to pinpoint the microscopic form of the pairing state. This is further corroborated by the fact that we showed that the kinetic pair breaking effects can lead to a non-monotonic dependence of
T_{c}
on the disorder concentration, including an initial increase of it, see Fig.
3
. If observed in these systems, this could be seen as direct evidence that superconductivity and valley polarization coexist microscopically.

Finally, we showed that these aspects are also directly relevant to superconductivity coexisting with or proximitized by altermagnetism, where quantum geometry plays a dual role: it leads to pair breaking for weak altermagnetism but allows for superconducting states with
\zeta<1/2
in Eq. (
7
). Therefore, quantum geometric pair breaking also plays an important role in the emerging field of superconducting altermagnets.

Derivation of the general results for the particle-particle bubble

Figure 5:
Blocks entering the disordered particle-particle bubble.

As discussed in the main text, in our calculations, we split the energy into the symmetric and antisymmetric parts,
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}=\tilde{\xi}_{\mathbf{k}}+a_{\mathbf{k}},\tilde{\xi}_{\mathbf{k}}=(\xi_{\mathbf{k}}+\xi_{-\mathbf{k}})/2,a_{\mathbf{k}}=(\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\xi_{-\mathbf{k}})/2
, and rewrite all summations over
\mathbf{k}
using the following generalized constant-DOS approximation,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\ldots\to\int d\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{\rho}(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})\int d\tilde{\xi}\ldots,

(21)

where
\tilde{\rho}(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})
is the angular-resolved density of states of the symmetrized energies
\tilde{\xi}_{\mathbf{k}}
at the associated symmetrized Fermi surface
\tilde{\xi}_{\mathbf{k}}=0
, the integral over
\tilde{\xi}
corresponds to summation over the momentum perpendicular to the above-mentioned Fermi surface. We will also assume that
a_{\mathbf{k}}
and the disorder matrix elements
W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
depend only on the angle
\varphi_{\mathbf{k}/\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
and not on
\tilde{\xi}
.

We first calculate the perturbative self-energy,

\displaystyle\Sigma(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})=\gamma^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|W_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|^{2}G_{0}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})=-i\mathrm{sign}(\omega_{n})\pi\gamma^{2}\int d\varphi_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\tilde{\rho}(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})|W_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|^{2}=-i\mathrm{sign}(\omega_{n})\Gamma_{\mathbf{k}},

(22)

where we used the fact that
\int d\tilde{\xi}(i\omega_{n}-\tilde{\xi}-a_{\mathbf{k}})^{-1}=-i\pi\mathrm{sign}(\omega_{n})
. Because of the trivial identity

\displaystyle\int d\xi G_{0}(i\omega_{n}\to i\omega_{n}+i\mathrm{sign}(\omega_{n})\Gamma,\xi)=\int d\xi G_{0}(i\omega_{n},\xi),

(23)

the obtained self-energy also corresponds to the full self-consistent non-crossing result. Thus, the disordered Green’s function reads

\displaystyle G^{-1}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})=G^{-1}_{0}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})-\Sigma(\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})=i\omega_{n}+i\mathrm{sign}(\omega_{n})\Gamma_{\mathbf{k}}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}.

(24)

Next we focus on calculating the building block of the vertex corrections which is diagramatically presented in Fig.
5
a (grey lines do not enter the definition of the block, and they are presented for convenience),

\displaystyle B(i\omega_{n})=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}G(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})G(-i\omega_{n},-\mathbf{k})\ket{\phi_{\mathbf{k}}}\bra{\phi_{\mathbf{k}}}\otimes\ket{\phi_{-\mathbf{k}}}\bra{\phi_{-\mathbf{k}}},

(25)

where the direct product is taken between the spaces associated with upper and lower fermionic lines. The integral over
\tilde{\xi}
yields

\displaystyle\int d\tilde{\xi}\frac{1}{i\omega_{n}+i\mathrm{sign}(\omega_{n})\Gamma_{k}-\tilde{\xi}-a_{\mathbf{k}}}\frac{1}{-i\omega_{n}-i\mathrm{sign}(\omega_{n})\Gamma_{k}-\tilde{\xi}+a_{\mathbf{k}}}=\pi\frac{|\omega_{n}|+(\Gamma_{\mathbf{k}}+\Gamma_{-\mathbf{k}})/2}{(|\omega_{n}|+(\Gamma_{\mathbf{k}}+\Gamma_{-\mathbf{k}})/2)^{2}+a^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}},

(26)

hence,

\displaystyle B(i\omega_{n})=\pi\int d\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{\rho}(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})\frac{|\omega_{n}|+(\Gamma_{\mathbf{k}}+\Gamma_{-\mathbf{k}})/2}{(|\omega_{n}|+(\Gamma_{\mathbf{k}}+\Gamma_{-\mathbf{k}})/2)^{2}+a^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}}\ket{\phi_{\mathbf{k}}}\bra{\phi_{\mathbf{k}}}\otimes\ket{\phi_{-\mathbf{k}}}\bra{\phi_{-\mathbf{k}}}.

(27)

It is convenient to also define the edge block of the particle-particle bubble shown in Fig.
5
b,

\displaystyle B_{e}^{\dagger}(i\omega_{n})=\pi\int d\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{\rho}(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})\frac{|\omega_{n}|+(\Gamma_{\mathbf{k}}+\Gamma_{-\mathbf{k}})/2}{(|\omega_{n}|+(\Gamma_{\mathbf{k}}+\Gamma_{-\mathbf{k}})/2)^{2}+a^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}}\bra{\phi_{\mathbf{k}}}\otimes\bra{\phi_{-\mathbf{k}}}.

(28)

Thus, full disordered Cooperon ladder can be written in terms of defined blocks as follows

\displaystyle\Pi^{C}(i\omega_{n})=\pi\int d\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{\rho}(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})\frac{|\omega_{n}|+(\Gamma_{\mathbf{k}}+\Gamma_{-\mathbf{k}})/2}{(|\omega_{n}|+(\Gamma_{\mathbf{k}}+\Gamma_{-\mathbf{k}})/2)^{2}+a^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}}+B_{e}^{\dagger}\gamma^{2}w\otimes w\left(\mathds{1}-B\gamma^{2}w\otimes w\right)^{-1}B_{e},

(29)

where the first part correspond to a particle-particle bubble constructed with dressed Green’s function and bare vertex, and the second term is the resummed series of ladder diagrams which include at least one disorder rung.

Thus, for the constant SC order parameter
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\Delta_{0}
, the quadratic part of the Ginzburg-Landau theory reads

\displaystyle\braket{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{dis}}\sim\Delta_{0}^{*}\left(-T\sum_{\omega_{n}}\Pi^{C}(i\omega_{n})+\frac{1}{g}\right)\Delta_{0},

(30)

where
g
is an effective interaction constant.

It is straightforward to derive the generalization of this expression for an arbitrary
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}
. Let us first define the
\mathbf{k}
-dependent version of the edge block,

\displaystyle B_{e}^{\dagger}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})=\pi\frac{|\omega_{n}|+(\Gamma_{\mathbf{k}}+\Gamma_{-\mathbf{k}})/2}{(|\omega_{n}|+(\Gamma_{\mathbf{k}}+\Gamma_{-\mathbf{k}})/2)^{2}+a^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}}\bra{\phi_{\mathbf{k}}}\otimes\bra{\phi_{-\mathbf{k}}},

(31)

and for convenience, the summation over the symmetrized Fermi surface as follows,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{FS}}=\frac{1}{\tilde{\nu}}\int d\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{\rho}(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})

(32)

with
\tilde{\nu}=\int d\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{\rho}(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})
. Then the quadratic part is given by the following expression

\displaystyle\braket{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{dis}}\sim\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}\left[-\chi^{C}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(T)+(g^{-1})_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\right]\Delta_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}

(33)

where
g
is now the kernel of the interaction mediating the SC, and the particle-particle bubble is given by

\displaystyle\chi^{C}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(T)=T\sum_{\omega_{n}}\left(\pi\frac{|\omega_{n}|+(\Gamma_{\mathbf{k}}+\Gamma_{-\mathbf{k}})/2}{(|\omega_{n}|+(\Gamma_{\mathbf{k}}+\Gamma_{-\mathbf{k}})/2)^{2}+a^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}}\delta_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}+B_{e}^{\dagger}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})\gamma^{2}w\otimes w\left\{\mathds{1}-B(i\omega_{n})\gamma^{2}w\otimes w\right\}^{-1}B_{e}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\right).

(34)

Although the particle-particle bubble can be written in the more compact form, we find this expression to be particularly useful for numerical calculations as it only involves the inversion of the matrix
\mathds{1}-B(i\omega_{n})\gamma^{2}w\otimes w
, whose dimensionality is determined by the number of internal degrees of freedom.

Appendix B
Weak disorder expansion

Figure 6:
Diagrams corresponding to the first order disorder corrections to the particle-particle bubble.

We now derive the generalized version of the Abrokosov-Gorkov law in the SC emerging from a TRS-broken band. To do this, we first expand
\chi^{C}
to the leading order of
\gamma^{2}
,

\displaystyle\chi^{C}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(T)=\pi T\sum_{\omega_{n}}\left(\frac{|\omega_{n}|}{\omega_{n}^{2}+a^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}}\delta_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}+\frac{\Gamma_{\mathbf{k}}+\Gamma_{-\mathbf{k}}}{2}\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}-\omega_{n}^{2}}{(\omega_{n}^{2}+a^{2}_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}}\delta_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}+\pi\gamma^{2}\frac{|\omega_{n}|}{\omega_{n}^{2}+a^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}}\frac{|\omega_{n}|}{\omega_{n}^{2}+a^{2}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}}W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}W_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\right)+o(\gamma^{2}).

(35)

The summation over Matsubara frequency yields

\displaystyle\pi T\sum_{\omega_{n}}\frac{|\omega_{n}|}{\omega_{n}^{2}+a_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{n+1/2}{(n+1/2)^{2}+(a_{\mathbf{k}}/(2\pi T))^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\geq 0}\left[\frac{1}{n+1/2+ia_{\mathbf{k}}/(2\pi T)}+\frac{1}{n+1/2-ia_{\mathbf{k}}/(2\pi T)}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\geq 0}\left[\frac{1}{n+1}-\frac{1}{n+1/2+ia_{\mathbf{k}}/(2\pi T)}+\frac{1}{n+1}-\frac{1}{n+1/2-ia_{\mathbf{k}}/(2\pi T)}\right]+\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{n+1}

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{\omega_{D}}{2\pi T}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\psi\left(\frac{1}{2}+i\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}}{2\pi T}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\psi\left(\frac{1}{2}-i\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}}{2\pi T}\right)\eqcolon f_{0}(\mathbf{k},T),

(36)

\displaystyle T\sum_{\omega_{n}}\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}-\omega_{n}^{2}}{(\omega_{n}^{2}+a_{\mathbf{k}}^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4T}\cosh^{-2}\left(\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}}{2T}\right)\eqcolon-\frac{1}{2T}f_{1}^{2}(\mathbf{k},T),

(37)

\displaystyle T\sum_{\omega_{n}}\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}}{(\omega_{n}^{2}+a_{\mathbf{k}}^{2})(\omega_{n}^{2}+a_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}\tanh[a_{\mathbf{k}}/(2T)]-a_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\tanh[a_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}/(2T)]}{2(a_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}-a_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{2})}\eqcolon\frac{1}{2T}f_{2}^{2}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},T),

(38)

where
\psi(z)
is the digamma function and
\omega_{D}
is the cutoff frequency regularizing logarithmic a divergence of the bare particle-particle bubble. One can think of the introduced functions
f_{1}(\mathbf{k},T),f_{2}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},T)
as spectral weights of the diagrams corresponding to the first order correction to the particle-particle bubble presented in Fig.
6
, respectively.

To find the disordered critical temperature
T_{c}
we should also expand bare particle-particle bubble near the clean
T_{c,0}
,

\displaystyle f_{0}(\mathbf{k},T_{c,0}+(T_{c}-T_{c,0}))

\displaystyle=f_{0}(\mathbf{k},T_{c,0})-f_{3}(\mathbf{k},T_{c,0})\frac{T_{c}-T_{c,0}}{T_{c,0}}+o\left(\frac{T_{c}-T_{c,0}}{T_{c,0}}\right),

(39)

\displaystyle f_{3}(\mathbf{k},T_{c,0})

\displaystyle\coloneq 1+\frac{ia_{\mathbf{k}}}{2\pi T_{c,0}}\left[\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{2}+i\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}}{2\pi T_{c,0}}\right)-\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{2}-i\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}}{2\pi T_{c,0}}\right)\right].

(40)

We now rewrite the summation over momenta in terms of the following weighted commutators,

\displaystyle C^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},f_{1}}(T)

\displaystyle=W_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}f_{1}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},T)\pm f_{1}(\mathbf{k},T)\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}W_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{*},

(41)

\displaystyle C^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},f_{2}}(T)

\displaystyle=W_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}f_{2}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},T)\Delta_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\pm f_{2}(-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime},T)\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}W_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{*},

(42)

\displaystyle C^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=W_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\pm\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}W_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{*}.

(43)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{FS}}|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}f_{1}^{2}(\mathbf{k},T)(\Gamma_{\mathbf{k}}+\Gamma_{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi\gamma^{2}\tilde{\nu}}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}f_{1}^{2}(\mathbf{k},T)\left(|W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|^{2}+|W_{-\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi\gamma^{2}\tilde{\nu}}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}f_{1}^{2}(\mathbf{k},T)\left(|W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|^{2}+|W_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi\gamma^{2}\tilde{\nu}}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left(|W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|^{2}|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|^{2}f_{1}^{2}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},T)+f_{1}^{2}(\mathbf{k},T)|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}|W_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi\gamma^{2}\tilde{\nu}}{4}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left(|C_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},f_{1}}^{+}|^{2}+|C_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},f_{1}}^{-}|^{2}\right),

(44)

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}f_{2}^{2}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},T)W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}W_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}f_{2}^{2}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},T)\left(\Delta^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}W_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}W_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}+\Delta^{*}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}W_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{k}}W_{-\mathbf{k}^{\prime},-\mathbf{k}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}f_{2}^{2}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},T)\left(\Delta^{*}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}W_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}W_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}+\Delta^{*}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}W_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{*}W_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{*}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left(|C_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},f_{2}}^{+}(T)|^{2}-|C_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},f_{2}}^{-}(T)|^{2}\right).

(45)

Finally, we define the average lifetime as follows

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\tau}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\Gamma_{\mathbf{k}}=\pi\gamma^{2}\tilde{\nu}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}|W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|^{2},

(46)

and obtain the generalization of the Abrikosov-Gorkov law,

\displaystyle\frac{T_{c}-T_{c,0}}{T_{c,0}}=-\frac{\pi}{4T_{c,0}}\tau^{-1}\zeta+o(\tau^{-1})

(47)

where

\displaystyle\zeta=\left(\sum_{t=\pm}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left|C_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},f_{1}}^{t}(T_{c,0})\right|^{2}-t\left|C_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},f_{2}}^{t}(T_{c,0})\right|^{2}\right)\left(4\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left|W_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\right|^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{FS}}f_{3}(\mathbf{k},T_{c,0})|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}\right)^{-1}.

(48)

Since the behavior of this expression is hard to analyze analytically, we simplify it by considering the small
a_{\mathbf{k}}
limit,
\|a_{\mathbf{k}}\|/T_{c,0}\ll 1
. We first note that when
a_{\mathbf{k}}=0
,
C_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},f_{1/2}}^{\pm}(T_{c,0})\to 1/2C_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\pm}
and we recover the result from
Timmons et al. 2020
,

\displaystyle\zeta=\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|C_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{-}|^{2}\right)\left(4\sum_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{FS}}|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left|W_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\right|^{2}\right)^{-1}.

(49)

Including the leading order of
a_{\mathbf{k}}/T_{c,0}
, we get

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \zeta=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}&\left(|C_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{-}|^{2}\left[1-\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+a_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{2}}{24T_{c,0}^{2}}+\frac{7\zeta_{\mathrm{R}}(3)}{2\pi^{2}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}^{2}}{T_{c,0}^{2}}\frac{|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}|^{2}}{\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{1}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}_{1}^{\prime}}|^{2}}\right]\right.\\
&\left.-|C_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{+}|^{2}\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+a_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{2}}{T_{c,0}^{2}}-\frac{1}{8}\sum_{t=\pm}\left|W_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\frac{|a_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}|}{T_{c,0}}+t\frac{|a_{\mathbf{k}}|}{T_{c,0}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}W_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{*}\right|^{2}\right)\left(4\sum_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{FS}}|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left|W_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\right|^{2}\right)^{-1}\end{aligned}

(50)

where
\zeta_{\mathrm{R}}(z)
is the Riemann
\zeta
-function.

It is instructive to consider the case of the SC order parameter with constant amplitude
|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|=\Delta_{0}
, and choose a gauge in which it is constant, then the disorder sensitivity factor
\zeta
can be split into two terms,
\zeta=\zeta^{+}+\zeta^{-}
,

\displaystyle\zeta^{+}

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{1}{48}\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left|W_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{+}\right|^{2}\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+a_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{2}}{T_{c,0}^{2}}\right)\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left|W_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\right|^{2}\right)^{-1}+o(\|a_{\mathbf{k}}\|^{2}),

(51)

\displaystyle\zeta^{-}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left|W_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{-}\right|^{2}\left[1-\frac{5}{48}\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+a_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{2}}{T_{c,0}^{2}}+\frac{7\zeta_{\mathrm{R}}(3)}{2\pi^{2}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\frac{a_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}^{2}}{T_{c,0}^{2}}\right]\right)\left(\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left|W_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\right|^{2}\right)^{-1}+o(\|a_{\mathbf{k}}\|^{2}).

(52)

where
W^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
is time-reversal even and odd part of the disorder
W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
. Here, we can clearly see that
\zeta^{+}<0
meaning that the nonmagnetic part of disorder can increase the critical temperature.

Appendix C
Some comments on finite momentum pairing calculations

Let us first highlight an artifact of the constant DOS approximation applied to the finite-
\mathbf{q}
pairing. To simplify our calculations we only consider the simplest pairing,
\Delta_{0}d^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2,\uparrow}d^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2,\downarrow}
, and nonmagnetic structureless disorder,
W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\to 1
. It is tempting to introduce the generalized symmetric and antisymmetric part of the shifted energies,
\tilde{\xi}_{\mathbf{k},{\mathbf{q}}}=\left(\xi_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2}+\xi_{-\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2}\right)/2,a_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}}=\left(\xi_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2}-\xi_{-\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2}\right)/2
, and then use a similar approximation as in the zero-
\mathbf{q}
case,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\to\int_{\text{F.S.}(\tilde{\xi}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}})}d\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{\rho}_{\mathbf{q}}(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})\int d\tilde{\xi},

(53)

where
\tilde{\rho}_{\mathbf{q}}(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})
is the DOS of the symmetrized energy
\left(\xi_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2}+\xi_{-\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2}\right)/2
. We then can use this approximation to calculate the block of the ladder diagram,

\displaystyle B(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}G(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2)G(-i\omega_{n},-\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2)

(54)

For the self-energy calculation, however, it might no longer be justified to use the same approximation of the constant DOS (
53
), since the scattering rate should not depend on the momentum of the Cooper pair. Arguably, one should use the approximation that assumes that the DOS of
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}
(denoted by
\rho(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})
) is constant,

\displaystyle\Sigma(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})=\gamma^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}G_{0}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})\to\gamma^{2}\int d\xi\frac{1}{i\omega-\xi}\int d\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}\rho(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})=-i\mathrm{sign}(\omega_{n})\pi\nu\gamma^{2},

(55)

where
\nu=\int d\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}\rho(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})
. From Eq. (
54
), the block of the ladder can now be explicitly written as follows

\displaystyle B(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{q})=\pi\int d\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{\rho}_{\mathbf{q}}(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})\frac{|\omega_{n}|+\pi\nu\gamma^{2}}{(|\omega_{n}|+\pi\nu\gamma^{2})^{2}+a^{2}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q}}}.

(56)

In the limit
\gamma\to\infty
, we get
\gamma^{2}B(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{q})\to\tilde{\nu}_{\mathbf{q}}/\nu
with
\tilde{\nu}_{\mathbf{q}}=\int d\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{\rho}_{\mathbf{q}}(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})
. In our numerical calculations for R
n
G, we find that
\tilde{\nu}_{\mathbf{q}}/\nu
can be greater than one, and therefore, the particle-particle bubble, given by

\displaystyle\Pi^{C}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{q})=B(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{q})\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}[\gamma^{2}B(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{q})]^{m},

(57)

formally diverges when
\gamma\to\infty
. This is an artifact of the approximation suggesting that there is no straightforward way to generalize our analytical calculations to the disordered finite-momentum particle-particle bubble capturing both kinetic and geometric effects in all orders of
\gamma^{2}
. However, the isolated pair-breaking effect of quantum geometry in the weak disorder limit is still well-defined as shown in Appendix
G
.

Thus, we focus on the numerical calculation of the finite-
\mathbf{q}
bubble. We have found that its quantitative behavior depends a lot on the specific value of the cutoff frequency used to calculate the summation over
\mathbf{k}
. To make our numerical calculations consistent, we construct them using the Anderson theorem as an anchor.

Let us consider the isolated TRS-broken band of interest and artificially create its full time-reversal copy. We then consider
\mathbf{k}
-independent pairing of full TRS partners. In this case, the clean particle-particle bubble reads

\displaystyle\Pi_{0,\text{TRS}}^{C}(i\omega_{n})=\sum_{\mathbf{k},|\xi_{\mathbf{k}}|<\omega_{D}}\left|G_{0}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})\right|^{2}=\sum_{\mathbf{k},|\xi_{\mathbf{k}}|<\omega_{D}}\frac{1}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}.

(58)

Disregarding the real part of the self-energy, the disordered Green’s function is given by
G(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})=\left(i\omega_{n}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}+i\mathrm{sign}(\omega_{n})\Gamma(\omega_{n})\right)^{-1}
(the dependence of
\Gamma(\omega_{n})
on
\omega_{n}
is kept), the one block of the ladder determining the vertex correction is given by

\displaystyle B_{\text{TRS}}(i\omega_{n})=\sum_{\mathbf{k},|\xi_{\mathbf{k}}|<\omega_{D}}\left|G(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})\right|^{2}.

(59)

The Anderson theorem is fulfilled if the disordered particle-particle bubble is equal to the bare one, hence, after summing up all the ladder diagrams, we get the following condition

\displaystyle\Pi_{0,\text{TRS}}^{C}(i\omega_{n})=\Pi_{\text{TRS}}^{C}(i\omega_{n})=\frac{B_{\text{TRS}}(i\omega_{n})}{1-\gamma^{2}B_{\text{TRS}}(i\omega_{n})}.

(60)

We solve this nonlinear equation to find the scattering rate
\Gamma(i\omega_{n})
. This is an important step, since the naive computation of the self energy with some cutoff frequency does not necessarily guarantee the fulfillment of the Anderson theorem. We then use the obtained scattering rate to compute the finite-momentum particle-particle bubble for the isolated TRS broken band that we started with,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{k},|\xi_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2}|<\omega_{D},|\xi_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2}|<\omega_{D}}G(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2)G(-i\omega_{n},-\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2).

(61)

Note that here one should replace
\Gamma(i\omega_{n})\to\Gamma(i\omega_{n})/2
as we reduce the number of bands back to one.

Appendix D
Explicit form of the Cooper pair wave function

Let us explicitly calculate the real space Cooper pair wavefunction for the minimal model of the rhombohedral n-layer graphene. Starting from the Hamiltonian

h_{\mathbf{k}}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{0}-\mu&t_{0}(k_{x}-ik_{y})^{n}\\
t_{0}(k_{x}+ik_{y})^{n}&-u_{0}-\mu\end{pmatrix},

(62)

we parametrize the Bloch wavefunction of the upper band at the Fermi surface as follows
\phi_{\mathbf{k}}=(\phi_{A},\phi_{B}e^{in\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}})^{T}
with
\phi_{A},\phi_{B}\in\mathds{R}
. The Cooper pair wavefunction is given by

\displaystyle D_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\phi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}\phi_{-\mathbf{k},\alpha^{\prime}}e^{i\mathbf{kx}},

(63)

where
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}
includes projection onto the Fermi surface. By limiting our analysis to the special cases discusses in the main text, we replace
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\to\Delta_{0}e^{im\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}}\delta(\tilde{\xi})/\tilde{\nu},m\in\mathds{Z}
, which yields

\displaystyle D(\mathbf{x})=2\pi\Delta_{0}i^{m}\begin{pmatrix}J_{m}(k_{F}x)\phi_{A}^{2}&i^{n}J_{n+m}(k_{F}x)\phi_{A}\phi_{B}\\
i^{n}J_{n+m}(k_{F}x)\phi_{A}\phi_{B}&i^{2n}J_{2n+m}(k_{F}x)\phi_{B}^{2}\end{pmatrix},

(64)

where
J_{l}(z)
is the Bessel function of the first kind.

Let us also calculate the disorder-induced measure of
D(\mathbf{x})
for specific impurity matrix,

\displaystyle C_{w}(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{Tr}\left[D^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x})w^{*}D(\mathbf{x})w\right],\quad w=\begin{pmatrix}w_{A}&0\\
0&w_{B}\end{pmatrix},

(65)

\displaystyle C_{w}(x)=(2\pi\Delta_{0})^{2}\left[w_{A}^{2}\phi_{A}^{4}J_{m}^{2}(k_{F}x)+2w_{A}w_{B}\phi_{A}^{2}\phi_{B}^{2}J_{n+m}^{2}(k_{F}x)+w_{B}^{2}\phi_{B}^{4}J_{2n+m}^{2}(k_{F}x)\right]

(66)

Considering for simplicity only positive
w_{A},w_{B}
, we now rewrite this expression in the parametrization introduced in the main text, in which the matrix element of the impurity reads

\displaystyle W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}=\phi_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}w\phi_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}=W_{0}\left[\eta^{2}+(1-\eta^{2})e^{in(\varphi_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}-\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})}\right],

(67)

\displaystyle W_{0}=w_{A}\phi_{A}^{2}+w_{B}\phi_{B}^{2},\quad\eta^{2}=\frac{w_{A}\phi_{A}^{2}}{w_{A}\phi_{A}^{2}+w_{B}\phi_{B}^{2}}.

(68)

In our calculation related to the sensitivty of the SC,
W_{0}
naturally drops out, therefore, we just set it to
1
and arrive at the following expression for
C_{w}(x)
,

\displaystyle C_{w}(x)=(2\pi\Delta_{0})^{2}\left[\eta^{4}J_{m}^{2}(k_{F}x)+2\eta^{2}(1-\eta^{2})J_{n+m}^{2}(k_{F}x)+(1-\eta^{2})^{2}J_{2n+m}^{2}(k_{F}x)\right].

(69)

Appendix E
Phase conventions for strong spin-orbit coupling

As the connection to the normal-state degrees of freedom is the most complex in case (2)—the limit of strong spin-orbit coupling—and to properly define it, we elaborate on it in this appendix. Just like in the main text, we start from a microscopic Hamiltonian, which we now directly state in momentum space,

H=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\alpha,\beta}\psi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}^{\dagger}(h_{\mathbf{k}})_{\alpha,\beta}\psi_{\mathbf{k},\beta}^{\phantom{\dagger}}+\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\left[\psi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}^{\dagger}(\hat{\Delta}_{\mathbf{k}})_{\alpha,\beta}\psi_{-\mathbf{k},\beta}^{\dagger}+\text{H.c.}\right]+H_{\mathrm{dis}}.

(70)

Here
\psi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}
are related to
\psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
by Fourier transform, and
\hat{\Delta}_{\mathbf{k}}
denotes the superconducting order parameter matrix in the microscopic basis;
\alpha
also includes spin as a quantum number, however, as opposed to case (3), we assume that there is strong, asymmetric spin-orbit coupling, such that, for the purpose of studying pairing, we can project onto the single, non-degenerate active band closest to the Fermi level for each momentum
\mathbf{k}
—this is referred to as the “weak pairing limit” in Ref.
Scheurer et al. 2017
. Formally, the band projection corresponds to
\psi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}\rightarrow(\phi_{\mathbf{k}})_{\alpha}d_{\mathbf{k}}
and leads to

\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\phi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}^{*}(\hat{\Delta}_{\mathbf{k}})_{\alpha,\beta}\phi_{-\mathbf{k},\beta}^{*}

(71)

in the first term in Eq. (
4
). Naturally,
\hat{\Delta}_{\mathbf{k}}=-\hat{\Delta}_{\mathbf{k}}^{T}
as imposed by Fermi Dirac statistics in Eq. (
70
), automatically implying
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=-\Delta_{-\mathbf{k}}
.

In the special case with TRS in the normal state, we have
\ket{\phi_{-\mathbf{k}}}=e^{i\rho_{\mathbf{k}}}\Theta\ket{\phi_{\mathbf{k}}}
where
e^{i\rho_{\mathbf{k}}}=-e^{i\rho_{-\mathbf{k}}}
as a result of
\Theta^{2}=-\mathbbm{1}
. Using this in Eq. (
71
), we can write

\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\widetilde{\Delta}_{\mathbf{k}}e^{-i\rho_{-\mathbf{k}}},\quad\text{with}\quad\widetilde{\Delta}_{\mathbf{k}}=\braket{\phi_{\mathbf{k}}|\hat{\Delta}_{\mathbf{k}}T^{\dagger}|\phi_{\mathbf{k}}},

(72)

where
T
is the unitary part of the time-reversal operator
\Theta=T\mathcal{K}
(
\mathcal{K}
is complex conjugation). As shown in Ref.
Scheurer et al. 2017
,
\widetilde{\Delta}_{\mathbf{k}}=\widetilde{\Delta}_{-\mathbf{k}}
is a very natural representation of the superconductor order parameter as it transforms explicitly under the same irreducible representation as
\hat{\Delta}_{\mathbf{k}}
. For instance,
\widetilde{\Delta}_{\mathbf{k}}=\Delta_{0}
transforms trivially under all point-group symmetries.

We finally recall that we found
e^{i\bar{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}}=e^{-i(\rho_{\mathbf{k}}+\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})}
in the main text as the general solution to the gauge-optimization problem starting with
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\Delta_{0}e^{i\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}}
. From the perspective of the gauge choice, this corresponds to choosing Bloch states,
\ket{\phi^{\prime}_{\mathbf{k}}}
which now obey
\ket{\phi^{\prime}_{-\mathbf{k}}}=e^{-i\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}}\Theta\ket{\phi^{\prime}_{\mathbf{k}}}
, i.e., effectively
e^{i\rho_{\mathbf{k}}}\rightarrow e^{-i\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}}
.

Alternatively, one can this of this as changing the superconducting order parameter as follows:

\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\rightarrow\Delta_{0}e^{i\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}}e^{-i(\rho_{\mathbf{k}}+\varphi_{\mathbf{k}})}=\Delta_{0}e^{-i\rho_{\mathbf{k}}},\quad\text{or, equivalently,}\quad\widetilde{\Delta}_{\mathbf{k}}\rightarrow-\Delta_{0}.

(73)

This means that, in the presence of TRS, our gauge choice just corresponds to the superconductor with
|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|=\text{const.}
that transforms trivially under all point group symmetries, naturally explaining why we find
\zeta_{\text{opt}}=0
(Anderson theorem). Our analysis of the main text generalizes this phase choice to the case without TRS.

Appendix F
Altermagnetic model

To illustrate quantum geometric pair breaking in altermagnets, we use the same checkerboard-lattice model of itinerant electrons as in Ref.
Sobral et al. 2025
, supplemented by spin-orbit coupling, see Fig.
7
a. Denoting Pauli matrices in sublattice and spin space by
\tau_{j}
and
s_{j}
, respectively, the normal-state Bloch Hamiltonian reads as

\displaystyle\begin{split}h_{\mathbf{k}}&=-2t_{2}s_{0}\left(P_{+}\cos{k_{x}}+P_{-}\cos{k_{y}})\right)+t_{1}(\tau_{+}f_{\mathbf{k}}+\tau_{-}f^{*}_{\mathbf{k}})+\Phi s_{3}\tau_{3}\\
&\qquad+\alpha_{1}\left(i\sum_{p_{1},p_{2}=\pm}\mathbf{v}_{p_{1},p_{2}}\cdot\mathbf{s}\,\tau_{+}e^{\frac{i}{2}(p_{1}k_{x}+p_{2}k_{x})}+\text{H.c.}\right)+\alpha_{2}\left(s_{2}P_{+}\sin k_{x}-s_{1}P_{-}\sin k_{y}\right),\end{split}

(74)

where
\tau_{\pm}=(\tau_{x}\pm i\tau_{y})/2
,
P_{\pm}=(\tau_{0}\pm\tau_{3})/2
,
f_{\mathbf{k}}=\sum_{p_{1,2}=\pm}e^{\frac{i}{2}(p_{1}k_{x}+p_{2}k_{x})}=4\cos\frac{k_{x}}{2}\cos\frac{k_{y}}{2}
, and
\mathbf{v}_{+,+}=(-1,1)
,
\mathbf{v}_{+,-}=(1,1)
,
\mathbf{v}_{-,+}=(-1,-1)
,
\mathbf{v}_{-,-}=(1,-1)
. Physically,
t_{j}
corresponds to
j
th-nearest neighbor hopping,
\Phi
is the altermagnetic order parameter, and
\alpha_{1}
(
\alpha_{2}
) is the strength of nearest-neighbor (next-nearest-neighbor) spin-orbit coupling. For concreteness, we set
\alpha_{1}=0
,
\alpha_{2}=\alpha
in the main text. All energies are measured in units of
t_{1}
and we set
t_{2}=0.3
.

Importantly, when
\alpha_{j}=0
and
\Phi\neq 0
, the system exhibits an “orbital” TRS,
\Theta=\mathcal{K}
with
\Theta^{2}=\mathbbm{1}
, while the usual spinful TRS (
\Theta=is_{2}\mathcal{K}
) is broken by the altermagnetic order parameter. The opposite is true in the limit
\alpha\neq 0
and
\Phi=0
, where the preserved TRS is spinful,
\Theta=is_{2}\mathcal{K}
and obeys
\Theta^{2}=-\mathbbm{1}
.

Naturally, there is no TRS when both
\alpha
and
\Phi
are non-zero. Nonetheless, it holds
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}=\xi_{-\mathbf{k}}
which follows from spinful
C_{2z}
rotational symmetry, with representation
\hat{C}_{2z}=is_{3}\tau_{0}
within the conventions of Eq. (
74
). As such, for zero-momentum pairing, there is no kinetic pair breaking effect. The magnetic point group of the model is generated by the product of four-rotation and time-reversal,
\Theta C_{4z}
, as well as the combination of reflection at the
xz
plane and time-reversal,
\Theta\sigma
; their spinor representations read as
is_{2}e^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}s_{3}}\tau_{1}\mathcal{K}
and
s_{0}\tau_{0}\mathcal{K}
, respectively.

In the parameter regime studied in the main text (see Fig.
2
g-h), only two out of the four bands give rise to Fermi surfaces. If, furthermore, both
\alpha
and
\Phi
are non-zero, the two sheets do not touch, and, in the weak pairing limit
Scheurer et al. 2017
, we can describe superconductivity by a single complex number
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}
as in the first term in Eq. (
4
) for
\mathbf{k}
in the vicinity of the respective Fermi surface.

Concerning quantum geometric pair breaking based on Eq. (
11
), the limit
\alpha\gg\Phi
corresponds to strong spin-orbit coupling, as discussed in Appendix
E
, where the spinful TRS is only weakly broken by
\Phi
leading to a small but finite
\zeta_{\text{opt}}
. In the limit
\Phi\rightarrow 0
, the optimization problem is unfrustrated and we obtain an Anderson theorem (
\zeta_{\text{opt}}\rightarrow 0
). In the opposite limit,
\alpha\ll\Phi
, this is rather different as the problem remains frustrated: to see this, let us use the spinless TRS
\Theta
for
\alpha\rightarrow 0
and choose a gauge with
\ket{\phi_{-\mathbf{k}}}=\Theta\ket{\phi_{\mathbf{k}}}
. We can therefore conclude

\tilde{I}=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}|W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|^{2}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}<\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}|W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|^{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\zeta_{\text{opt}}>0,

(75)

where the first inequality follows from the Fermi-Dirac constraint
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=-\Delta_{-\mathbf{k}}
(and continuity of
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}
). In fact, Eq. (
75
) shows that quantum geometry,
|W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|^{2}\neq\text{const.}
, here enhances the stability of superconductivity: for
|W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|=W_{0}
, we directly see that
\tilde{I}=0
and
\zeta_{\text{opt}}=1/2
(irrespective of the form of the pairing state). This is also related to the reason why we used a full two-sublattice (with spin, four-band) model, such as Eq. (
74
), instead of an effective two-band model (only with spin). A simple and natural example of the latter would be
h_{\mathbf{k}}=s_{0}\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}+\Phi s_{3}(k_{x}^{2}-k_{y}^{2})
, leading to
\ket{\phi_{\mathbf{k}}}=\ket{\phi_{-\mathbf{k}}}
; this in turn yields
W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}=W_{-\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
and hence
\tilde{I}=0
, without any quantum geometric effects.

We finally note that
\zeta_{\text{opt}}=0
in antiferromagnets since there is a spinful TRS. Of course, this does not mean that any given pairing state will be stable, see, e.g., Ref.
Fyhn et al. 2023
, but that there is a superconductor with an Anderson theorem. We can also explicitly demonstrate this using the model in Eq. (
74
), which reduces to an antiferromagnet as long as no next-nearest-neighbor terms are present (
t_{2}=\alpha_{2}=0
). Indeed, one can see in Fig.
7
b, where we set
\alpha_{2}=0
that
\zeta_{\text{opt}}\rightarrow 0
when
t_{2}\rightarrow 0
, irrespective of the value of
\Phi
.

Figure 7:
(a) Lattice representation of the Hamiltonian defined in momentum space in Eq. (
74
). (b) Optimal
\zeta
in the limit
\alpha_{2}=0
at fixed
\alpha_{1}=0.05
, shown as a function of the altermagnetic order-parameter strength
\Phi
and next-nearest-neighbor hopping
t_{2}
. Note that the system becomes an antiferromagnet in the limit
t_{2}\rightarrow 0
, where
\zeta_{\text{opt}}\rightarrow 0
.

Appendix G
Quantum geometric pair breaking and gauge fixing for finite
\mathbf{q}

As anticipated in the main text, the discussion of quantum geometric pair breaking can be readily generalized to finite momentum (
\mathbf{q}\neq 0
) pairing. Again assuming that
\xi_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2}=\xi_{-\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2}
(perfect nesting) to suppress the kinetic pair breaking effect, Eq. (
11
) is now to be substituted by

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\left|\mathcal{W}^{\mathbf{q}}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}-\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathcal{W}^{\mathbf{q}}_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}})^{*}\right|^{2}}{4\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}|\mathcal{W}^{\mathbf{q}}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}|^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{FS}}|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}},

(76)

where we defined
\mathcal{W}^{\mathbf{q}}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}:=W_{\mathbf{k}+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2}}
for notational simplicity. Consequently, the functional in Eq. (
13
) yielding the optimal gauge is to be replaced by

\tilde{I}[\bar{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}]=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{FS}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}^{*}\mathcal{W}^{\mathbf{q}}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\mathcal{W}^{\mathbf{q}}_{-\mathbf{k},-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}e^{i(\bar{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}-\bar{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}})},

(77)

where
\bar{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}
are still constrained to be even in
\mathbf{k}
. For
\mathbf{q}\neq 0
, the real-space superconducting order parameter now depends on the center-of-mass coordinate
\mathbf{r}
of the electrons,

\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}d^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2}}d^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k}+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2}}=\sum_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{x},\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r}-\frac{\mathbf{x}}{2},\alpha}D_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r}+\frac{\mathbf{x}}{2},\alpha^{\prime}}e^{-i\mathbf{q}\mathbf{r}}.

(78)

Meanwhile, the form of

\displaystyle D_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\phi_{\mathbf{k}+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2},\alpha}\phi_{-\mathbf{k}+\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2},\alpha^{\prime}}e^{i\mathbf{kx}}

(79)

still closely parallels that of Eq. (
15
). Importantly, it also still holds
C_{w}(\mathbf{x}=0)=\tilde{I}
with
C_{w}
given in Eq. (
16
) and
\tilde{I}
in Eq. (
77
). Consequently, the gauge optimization according to
\zeta
is equivalent to finding the most localized Cooper pair wavefunction, even when
\mathbf{q}\neq 0
.
