Solving Poisson’s equation for Wasserstein contractive Markov chains
Julian Hofstadler

Abstract
We study Poisson’s equation in the context of general state space Markov chains. For chains satisfying a contraction assumption w.r.t. a Wasserstein distance, we show that a solution exists for Lipschitz functions and investigate its regularity properties. If the kernel is additionally reversible we are also able to show that solutions for L p L^{p} functions exist. Combining our findings with Doob’s inequalities for martingales we derive maximal inequalities for contractive Markov chains. A number of examples is provided to demonstrate the applicability of our results, in particular in the context of Markov chain Monte Carlo methods.

2
Wasserstein contractive Markov chains

In this section we review concepts from the Markov chain literature and related fields which are required later.
In particular, we provide a definition of
Wasserstein contractive Markov chains
, cf. Definition
2.1
, which are our main object to study Poisson’s equation.
At the end of this section we include a number of examples, which fit into our framework.

Throughout all sections
(\mathcal{X},\rho)
is a (non-trivial) Polish space and
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
is the corresponding Borel
\sigma
-algebra. Moreover,
d\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a generic lower semi-continuous (w.r.t. the product topology of
\rho

2.1
Definitions and notation

Let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a Markov chain evolving in
\mathcal{X}
with transition kernel
P\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\to[0,1]
.
Unless specified otherwise,
\nu
denotes the initial distribution, that is,
\nu
is a probability measure on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
and we have
X_{1}\sim\nu
.
We follow the usual convention and write
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}_{\nu})
and
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}
for the underlying probability space and expectation, respectively, cf.
[
DMPS18
]
for details.

Given a probability measure
\mu
on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
, we define
\mu P
as

\mu P(\cdot)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}P(x,\cdot)\mu({\rm d}x),

which is again a probability measure. Hence
\mu\mapsto\mu P
is a well-defined mapping from the set of probability measures on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
, write
\mathcal{P(\mathcal{X})}
, into itself.
If
\mu
and
P
satisfy

\int_{A}P(x,B)\mu({\rm d}x)=\int_{B}P(x,A)\mu({\rm d}x),

for any measurable sets
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
, then
P
is called
reversible
w.r.t.
\mu
. In this case
\mu
is an
invariant distribution
, i.e.
\mu P=\mu
, see
[
DMPS18
]
.

For a sufficiently regular function
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
we define

Pf(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x^{\prime})P(x,{\rm d}x^{\prime}).

Typically,
P
is called
Markov operator
when we work with
Pf
. For more details about
P
acting on either probability measures or functions, the reader may consult e.g. the books
[
MT09
,
DMPS18
]
.

Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{P(\mathcal{X})}
and denote by
\mathcal{C}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
the set of couplings between
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
. The 1-Wasserstein distance, sometimes also called Kantorovich-Rubinstein metric, is defined as

\mathcal{W}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=\inf_{\eta\in\mathcal{C}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}d(x,y)\eta({\rm d}x,{\rm d}y).

If
\mathcal{W}
is finite, then it indeed satisfies all axioms of a metric, and hence allows to measure the distance between
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
. It is possible to generalise the above definition to so-called
p
-Wasserstein distances, with
p\geq 1
, which are fundamental objects in optimal transport, we refer to
[
Vil09
]
.
However, we only consider the 1-Wasserstein distance in this article, whence we shall simply call it
Wasserstein distance
.

Define the
Kantorovich norm
of a Markov kernel
P\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\to[0,1]
as

\tau(P)=\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{\mathcal{W}(P(x,\cdot),P(y,\cdot))}{d(x,y)}.

This quantity was used e.g. by Dobrushin
[
Dob56
]
, see Equation 1.5 there, who later called it Kantorovich norm in
[
Dob96
]
.
In
[
DMPS18
]
the terminology
Dobrushin coefficient
is used and in
[
RS18
]
it was called
generalised ergodicity coefficient
.
From
[
Oll09
]
, see also
[
JO10
,
Pau16
]
, it is known that
\tau(P)
is connected to the Ricci curvature of the corresponding Markov chain.

Definition 2.1
.

A Markov chain
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with transition kernel
P
is called Wasserstein contractive, if
\tau(P)<\infty
and
\tau(P^{m})<1
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Remark 2.2
.

From this point onwards, we always assume that
m\in\mathbb{N}
in Definition
2.1
is the minimal
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\tau(P^{m})<1
.

Remark 2.3
.

The assumption
\tau(P^{m})<1
is closely related to the so-called multistep coarse curvature of
[
Pau16
]
, and whence can be seen as generalisation of the assumptions in
[
Oll09
,
JO10
]
.

Let us briefly explain the terminology. Propositions 14.3 and 14.4 of
[
Dob96
]
, see also
[
RS18
, Proposition 2.1]
;
[
DMPS18
, Proposition 20.3.3]
, yield

(i)

\tau(PP^{\prime})\leq\tau(P)\tau(P^{\prime})
, and

(ii)

\mathcal{W}(\mu_{1}P,\mu_{2}P)\leq\tau(P)\mathcal{W}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
,

where
P,P^{\prime}
are Markov kernels and
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{P(\mathcal{X})}
.
By (ii), if
\tau(P^{m})<1
, then
\mu\mapsto\mu P^{m}
(considered as mapping from
\mathcal{P(\mathcal{X})}
to
\mathcal{P(\mathcal{X})}
) is a contraction w.r.t. the Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}
.
Therefore, one might expect that there is a fixed point (aka invariant distribution), say
\pi
.
Yet,
\mathcal{W}
might be infinite in general.
However, if
\int_{\mathcal{X}}d(x_{0},x)P(x_{0},{\rm d}x)<\infty
for some
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
, then we can prove the existence of
\pi
, c.f.
[
DMPS18
, Theorem 20.3.4]
, see also
[
Pau16
]
.

Remark 2.4
.

From property (i) above it follows that for a contractive chain

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tau(P^{n})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\tau(P^{m\ell+j})\leq\frac{m\tau(P)^{m}}{1-\tau(P^{m})}<\infty.

Hence, for any contractive chain we may define
\Lambda:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tau(P^{n})\in[0,\infty)
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let

be a uniformly ergodic Markov chain, that is

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|P^{k}(x,\cdot)-\pi\right\|_{tv}\leq C\kappa^{k},

Uniformly ergodic Markov chains are of particular interest in the Markov chain Monte Carlo literature, as the upcoming three examples demonstrate.

3
Solving Poission’s equation

This section contains our main results about existence and regularity of Poisson’s equation for Wasserstein contractive chains.
First, we consider the case where the forcing function
f
is Lipschitz w.r.t.
d
.
Then, we study the
L^{p}
setting, where
p\geq 1
, additionally assuming reversibility.

Throughout, let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a Markov chain evolving in
\mathcal{X}
with transition kernel
P\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\to[0,1]
and unique invariant distribution
\pi
.

3.1
Solutions for Lipschitz functions

In this section we solve Poisson’s equation (
1
) for forcing functions
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
which are Lipschitz w.r.t.
d
.
Recall that
f
is Lipschitz w.r.t.
d
if there exists a constant
L\geq 0
such that

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq L\cdot d(x,y),

for any
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
.
Throughout this section, we always refer to the metric
d
when speaking of Lipschitz functions.
We denote the space of all measurable Lipschitz functions on
\mathcal{X}
by
\mathscr{L}
.
The mapping
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{d}\colon\mathscr{L}\to[0,\infty)
, given by

\left\|f\right\|_{d}=\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{d(x,y)},

is a semi-norm
8
8

8

That is,
\left\|f\right\|_{d}\geq 0
,
\left\|cf\right\|_{d}=|c|\cdot\left\|f\right\|_{d}
and
\left\|f+g\right\|_{d}\leq\left\|f\right\|_{d}+\left\|g\right\|_{d}
for any
f,g\in\mathscr{L}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
.
on
\mathscr{L}
, cf.
[
CMN19
]
.
Note that
\left\|f\right\|_{d}=0
if

is constant.
Hence,
(\mathscr{L},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{d})
is not a normed space in general.

The Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality formula, see
[
Vil09
, Chapter 5]
, connects
(\mathscr{L},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{d})
and

\mathcal{W}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=\sup_{f\in B_{1}(\mathscr{L})}\left|\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x)\mu_{1}({\rm d}x)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x)\mu_{2}({\rm d}x)\right|,

with
B_{1}(\mathscr{L})=\{f\in\mathscr{L}\colon\left\|f\right\|_{d}\leq 1\}
being the unit ball in
\mathscr{L}
.

Poisson’s equation (
1
) for forcing function
f
only makes sense if
\pi(f)
exists.
Hence, to study Poisson’s equation in the context of Lipschitz functions it is vital that
\pi(f)
is well-defined for any
f\in\mathscr{L}
.
Following
[
JO10
]
we define the

, with
p\in[1,\infty)
, by

E_{p}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}d(x,y)^{p}\pi({\rm d}y).

Note that
E_{p}(x)
is well-defined for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
, but it may be infinite.
However, if
E(x_{0})<\infty
for some
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
, then
E(x)<\infty
for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
by triangle inequality; compare also to
[
JO10
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that
E_{p}(x_{0})<\infty
for some
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
and
p\in[1,\infty)
. Then, for any
f\in\mathscr{L}
we have
\pi(|f|^{p})<\infty
, and consequently also
\pi(f)
is well-defined and finite.

Proof.

For any
f\in\mathscr{L}
we note that
|f|^{p}
is non-negative and measurable. Observe that
|f(x)|^{p}\leq(|f(x)-f(x_{0})|+|f(x_{0})|)^{p}\leq(d(x,x_{0})+|f(x_{0})|)^{p}
.
It follows that

\int_{\mathcal{X}}|f(x)|^{p}\pi({\rm d}x)\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}(d(x,x_{0})+|f(x_{0})|)^{p}\pi({\rm d}x)\leq 2^{p}\left(E_{p}(x_{0})+|f(x_{0})|^{p}\right),

where in the last step we used the inequality
(\alpha+\beta)^{p}\leq 2^{p}(\alpha^{p}+\beta^{p})
, which is valid for any
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
.
The statement about
\pi(f)
is now a simple consequence of Hoelder’s inequality (consider
\pi(f\cdot\mathds{1})
).
∎

Under the conditions of Lemma
3.1
the right hand side of Poisson’s equation (
1
), i.e.
f(x)-\pi(f)
, is well-defined for any
f\in\mathscr{L}
.
In this case it is no loss of generality to assume
f\in\mathscr{L}_{0}=\{f\in\mathscr{L}\colon\pi(f)=0\}
which allows to work in a Banach space.
Although results in this flavour are standard in theory of Lipschitz functions

CMN19
]

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume that
E_{p}(x_{0})<\infty
for some
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
and
p\in[1,\infty)
. Then,
(\mathscr{L}_{0},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{d})
is a Banach space.

Proof.

Let
\xi\in\mathcal{X}
be fixed and set
\mathscr{L}_{\xi}=\{f\in\mathscr{L}\colon f(\xi)=0\}
.
First we show that
(\mathscr{L}_{\xi},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{d})
is a Banach space.
Namely, let
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a Cauchy sequence in
(\mathscr{L}_{\xi},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{d})
. Then, there exists a Lipschitz function
f
with
f(\xi)=0
such that
\left\|f_{n}-f\right\|_{d}\to 0
, see
[
CMN19
, Theorem 8.1.3]
.
Additionally,

|f_{n}(x)-f(x)|=|f_{n}(x)-f(x)-(f_{n}(\xi)-f(\xi))|\leq\left\|f_{n}-f\right\|_{d}d(x,\xi),

for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
Hence,
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x)=f(x)
for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Thus

is measurable and therefore

.

Next we show that
(\mathscr{L}_{\xi},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{d})
and
(\mathscr{L}_{0},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{d})
are isometrically isomorph.
To this end, observe that by Lemma
3.1

is
\pi

We define the mappings
T\colon\mathscr{L}_{0}\to\mathscr{L}_{\xi}

T(f)=f(\cdot)-f(\xi)\qquad\text{and}\qquad R(h)=h(\cdot)-\pi(h).

Note that both,
T
and
R

\left\|g\right\|_{d}

\left\|Tf\right\|_{d}=\left\|f\right\|_{d}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\|Rh\right\|_{d}=\left\|h\right\|_{d}.

Finally,
T(R(h))=h

R(T(f))=f

∎

In what follows we always equip
\mathscr{L}_{0}
with the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{d}

\mathscr{L}_{0}
for the (Banach) space
(\mathscr{L}_{0},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{d})
.
The upcoming lemma is one of our key ingredients to study existence and regularity of solutions to Poisson’s equation.
For a Markov kernel

to the operator norm of (the Markov operator)

\mathscr{L}_{0}
.
In particular, this shows that besides

being connected to the ergodic properties of a Markov chain

[

, there is another interpretation

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume that
E_{p}(x_{0})<\infty
for some
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
;
p\in[1,\infty)
, and that the Markov kernel
K\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\to[0,1]
satisfies

Then, the mapping
K\colon\mathscr{L}_{0}\to\mathscr{L}_{0}
given by

defines a linear operator and we have

\left\|K\right\|_{\mathscr{L}_{0}\to\mathscr{L}_{0}}=\tau(K).

Proof.

First, we note that if
\tau(K)<\infty
, then
K\colon\mathscr{L}\to\mathscr{L}
indeed defines a linear (and bounded) operator.
Since
\pi
is the invariant measure, the same is true for

.

Observe that the semi-norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{d}
(on

and

\left\|f\right\|_{d}=\left\|f+c\right\|_{d}

Particularly, by setting

\sup_{f\in B_{1}(\mathscr{L}_{0})}\left\|Kf\right\|_{d}=\sup_{f\in B_{1}(\mathscr{L})}\left\|Kf\right\|_{d},

where
B_{1}(\mathscr{L})
and

are the unit balls in

and

Finally, by using the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality formula

10

We refer to

Equipped with Lemma

Theorem 3.4
.

Assume that
E_{p}(x_{0})<\infty
for some
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
and
p\in[1,\infty)
and let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a Wasserstein contractive Markov chain.
Then, for any

the space

contains exactly one solution

of Poisson’s equation (

f

\left\|u\right\|_{d}\leq\Lambda\left\|f\right\|_{d}

\int_{\mathcal{X}}|u(x)|^{p}\pi({\rm d}x)\leq 2^{p}\inf_{y\in\mathcal{X}}(|u(y)|^{p}+\left\|u\right\|_{d}^{p}E_{p}(y))<\infty
.

Proof.

Note that all assumptions to apply Lemmas
3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3
are satisfied, such that

\left\|P^{m}\right\|_{\mathscr{L}_{0}\to\mathscr{L}_{0}}=\tau(P^{m})<1.

Since

is a Banach space, the Neumann series corresponding to

, i.e.
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}P^{k}
, converges. Thus
(\Id-P)\colon\mathscr{L}_{0}\to\mathscr{L}_{0}
is invertible and

(\Id-P)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}P^{k}.

Additionally,
\left\|(\Id-P)^{-1}\right\|_{\mathscr{L}_{0}\to\mathscr{L}_{0}}\leq\Lambda
, with
\Lambda<\infty

Given

we observe that for any

Whence, in the space

) is given by

.

For the rest of the proof fix
f\in\mathscr{L}_{0}
and
u=(\Id-P)^{-1}f
.
To see the bound on

claimed in i), we note that

\left\|u\right\|_{d}\leq\left\|(\Id-P)^{-1}\right\|_{\mathscr{L}_{0}\to\mathscr{L}_{0}}\left\|f\right\|_{d}\leq\Lambda\left\|f\right\|_{d}.

Observing

\int_{\mathcal{X}}|u(x)|^{p}\pi({\rm d}x)\leq 2^{p}(|u(y)|^{p}+\left\|u\right\|_{d}^{p}E_{p}(y))

for arbitrary

3.2
Solutions for
L^{p}
functions

In this section we study Poisson’s equation in the
L^{p}
setting.
Again, w.l.o.g. we consider centred forcing functions.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
.
Recall, that the space
L^{p}(\pi)
contains all (equivalence classes
11
11

11

Two functions are considered as equivalent if they are equal
\pi
-almost everywhere.
of) functions
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
for which

\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\pi)}^{p}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}|f(x)|^{p}\pi({\rm d}x)<\infty.

It is well-known that
(L^{p}(\pi),\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L^{p}(\pi)})
is a Banach space for any
p\in[1,\infty)
, and that
(L^{2}(\pi),\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L^{2}(\pi)})
is a Hilbert space with inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\pi)}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x)g(x)\pi({\rm d}x).

The linear subspace
L_{0}^{2}(\pi)=\{f\in L^{2}\colon\pi(f)=0\}
of centred
L^{2}
functions is again a Hilbert space, also with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L^{2}(\pi)}
and induced norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}
.
Similarly, for
p\in[1,\infty)
, we define the centred
L^{p}
spaces, write
L^{p}_{0}(\pi)
, as
L_{0}^{p}(\pi)=\{f\in L^{p}(\pi)\colon\pi(f)=0\}
.

Any Markov kernel
K
with invariant distribution
\pi
defines a linear operator on the

Indeed, if
f\in L^{p}(\pi)
, then
Kf\in L^{p}(\pi)
and for the corresponding operator norm we know

\left\|K\right\|_{L^{p}(\pi)\to L^{p}(\pi)}:=\sup_{\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\pi)\leq 1}}\left\|Kf\right\|_{L^{p}(\pi)}=1,

where equality is obtained for the constant function
\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{X}}
.
We refer e.g. to
[

]
for a proof.
However, on the

\left\|K\right\|_{L^{p}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{p}_{0}(\pi)}:=\sup_{\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}_{0}(\pi)\leq 1}}\left\|Kf\right\|_{L^{p}(\pi)}<1.

In particular, if
\left\|K\right\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{2}_{0}(\pi)}<1

-)

In the MCMC literature chains with a spectral gap are of interest since for those explicit error bounds in the context of numerical integration are known, cf.
[
Rud12
,

]
.

The rest of this section is organised as follows. First, we show a general result about solutions of Poisson’s equation for chains with a spectral gap, see Section

. Then we provide two results with sufficient conditions for Wasserstein contractive chains to admit a spectral gap, see Section

.

3.2.1
General chains with a spectral gap

In this section we consider a generic Markov kernel

We begin with the following auxiliary result, which is a simple combination of

Lemma 3.7

For any

Let

\left\|K^{\ell}\right\|_{L^{p}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{p}_{0}(\pi)}\leq\left\|K^{\ell}-\Pi\right\|_{L^{p}(\pi)\to L^{p}(\pi)}
as well as

\left\|K^{\ell}-\Pi\right\|_{L^{p}(\pi)\to L^{p}(\pi)}\leq\begin{cases}2^{2/p}\left\|K\right\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{2}_{0}(\pi)}^{2\ell\frac{p-1}{p}}\qquad&\text{ if }p\in(1,2),\\
2^{2\frac{p-1}{p}}\left\|K\right\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{2}_{0}(\pi)}^{2\ell/p}&\text{ if }p\in(2,\infty),\end{cases}

which yields the desired result.
∎

The following theorem is our main result concerning solutions of Poisson’s equation in the
L^{p}_{0}
setting for kernels
K
which admit a spectral gap.
That is, for given
f\in L^{p}_{0}(\pi)
we want to find
u\in L^{p}_{0}(\pi)
such that
Ku-u=f
, understood in the
L^{p}(\pi)
-sense.
In
[
DMPS18
, Proposition 21.3.8]
the existence of
u
is shown assuming that
\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\left\|K^{\ell}f\right\|_{L^{p}(\pi)}
converges, which is closely related to a converging Neumann series.
Below we extend the mentioned result by a), showing that a spectral gap is a sufficient condition, and b), providing
L^{p}(\pi)
bounds for
u
in terms of the spectral gap.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and
f\in L^{p}_{0}(\pi)
.
If the Markov kernel
K
admits a spectral gap, then, the space
L^{p}_{0}(\pi)
contains exactly one solution of Poisson’s equation
u\in L^{p}_{0}(\pi)
(with forcing function
f
) and we have

\left\|u\right\|_{L^{p}(\pi)}\leq\begin{cases}\frac{2^{2/p}}{1-\kappa^{2\frac{p-1}{p}}}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\pi)}\qquad&\text{ if }p\in(1,2),\\[6.45831pt]
\frac{1}{1-\kappa}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}&\text{ if }p=2,\\[5.16663pt]
\frac{2^{2\frac{p-1}{p}}}{1-\kappa^{2/p}}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\pi)}&\text{ if }p\in(2,\infty),\end{cases}

where
\kappa=\left\|K\right\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{2}_{0}(\pi)}<1
.

Proof.

We split the proof into three cases, where we deal with different values of
p\in(1,\infty)
.

Case 1:
Let
p\in(1,2)
. Since
\kappa=\left\|K\right\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{2}_{0}(\pi)}<1
we have
\kappa^{2\frac{p-1}{p}}<1
.
By Lemma
3.7
,

\left\|K^{\ell}\right\|_{L^{p}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{p}_{0}(\pi)}\leq 2^{2/p}\left(\kappa^{2\frac{p-1}{p}}\right)^{\ell}.

Hence the corresponding Neumann series converges and on
L^{p}_{0}(\pi)
,

(\Id-K)^{-1}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}K^{\ell}.

Additionally we have the following bound for the operator norm,

\left\|(\Id-K)^{-1}\right\|_{L^{p}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{p}_{0}(\pi)}\leq\frac{2^{2/p}}{1-\kappa^{2\frac{p-1}{p}}}.

Now,
u=(\Id-K)^{-1}f\Leftrightarrow u-Ku=f
, such that Poisson’s equation (with forcing function
f
), admits the unique solution
u
in
L^{p}_{0}(\pi)
, and

\left\|u\right\|_{L^{p}(\pi)}\leq\left\|(\Id-K)^{-1}\right\|_{L^{p}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{p}_{0}(\pi)}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\pi)}\leq\frac{2^{2/p}}{1-\kappa^{2\frac{p-1}{p}}}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\pi)}.

Case 2:
Let
p=2
. Since
\left\|K\right\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{2}_{0}(\pi)}<1
, we again know that the Neumann series corresponding to
K
converges and

\left\|(\Id-K)^{-1}\right\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{2}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{2}_{0}(\pi)}\leq\frac{1}{1-\left\|K\right\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{2}_{0}(\pi)}}.

Setting
u=(\Id-K)^{-1}f
we obtain the desired result for
p=2
.

Case 3:
Let
p>2
. Here we can use the same arguments as in case 1, but with the corresponding bounds for
p>2
from Lemma
3.7
.
∎

3.2.2
Contractive chains with spectral gaps

Here we provide two results which connect contractive chains and spectral gaps.
These give sufficient conditions which ensure a contractive chain also admits a spectral gap, and whence provide access to Theorem
3.8
.
However, before we start we require some additional definitions.

Recall that
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Markov chain with transition kernel
P
and invariant distribution
\pi
.
In the spirit of
[
Oll09
]
we define

\sigma(x)=\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}d(y,y^{\prime})^{2}P(x,{\rm d}y)P(x,{\rm d}y^{\prime})\right)^{1/2},

called the
coarse diffusion
coefficient of
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, and its
L^{2}
norm,

\sigma=\left\|\sigma(\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}=\left(\int_{\mathcal{X}}\sigma(x)^{2}\pi({\rm d}x)\right)^{1/2}.

Using
[
Oll09
, Proposition 30]
we deduce the following result for the case where
d=\rho
, i.e. the metric rendering
\mathcal{X}
Polish is the same as in
\mathcal{W}
.

Lemma 3.9

(
[
Oll09
, Proposition 30]
)
.

Let
d=\rho
.
Assume
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Wasserstein contractive Markov chain being reversible w.r.t.
\pi
.
If
\sigma<\infty
, then

\left\|P\right\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{2}_{0}(\pi)}\leq\sqrt[m]{\tau(P^{m})}<1.

Proof.

Proposition 30 of
[
Oll09
]
implies that the spectral radius of the Markov operator
P^{m}\colon L^{2}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{2}_{0}(\pi)
, say
r(P^{m})
, is bounded by
\tau(P^{m})<1
.
Since
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is reversible the operator
P^{m}
is self adjoint on
L^{2}_{0}(\pi)
implying

\left\|P^{m}\right\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{2}_{0}(\pi)}=r(P^{m})\leq\tau(P^{m})<1.

Using the formula for the spectral radius of
P
acting on
L^{2}_{0}(\pi)
, say
r(P)
, it follows

\left\|P\right\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{2}_{0}(\pi)}=r(P)=\inf_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\left\|P^{\ell}\right\|^{1/\ell}\leq\tau(P^{m})^{1/m}<1.

∎

The above lemma provides a criterion for contractive chains to have a spectral gap.
However, the requirement that
d=\rho
may not always be true.

The next result, which is
[
HSV14
, Proposition 2.8]
, see also
[
RW01
]
, shows that
d=\rho
is not needed if the space of Lipschitz functions (w.r.t.
d
), say
\mathscr{L}
, is dense in
L^{2}(\pi)
.

Lemma 3.10

(
[
HSV14
, Proposition 2.8]
)
.

Assume
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Wasserstein contractive Markov chain, which is reversible w.r.t.
\pi
.
If
\mathscr{L}\cap L^{\infty}(\pi)
is dense in
L^{2}(\pi)
, then

\left\|P\right\|_{L^{2}(\pi)\to L^{2}(\pi)}=\sqrt[m]{\tau(P^{m})}<1.

Proof.

Define the (reversible/self-adjoint) Markov kernel/operator
K=P^{m}
, and note that
\tau(K)=\tau(P^{m})<1
.
By
[
HSV14
, Proposition 2.8]
it follows

\left\|Kf\right\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}\leq\tau(K)\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}

for any
f\in L^{2}_{0}(\pi)
, which clearly implies
\left\|K\right\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{2}_{0}(\pi)}\leq\tau(K)<1
.
By the well-known formula for the spectral radius of
P
, say
\rho
, and since
P
is self-adjoint on
L^{2}_{0}(\pi)
, we arrive at

\left\|P\right\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{2}_{0}(\pi)}=\rho=\inf_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\left\|P^{\ell}\right\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{2}_{0}(\pi)}^{1/\ell}\leq\left\|P^{m}\right\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\pi)\to L^{2}_{0}(\pi)}^{1/m}\leq\sqrt[m]{\tau(P^{m})},

which shows the claimed result.
∎

4
Maximal inequalities

In this section we apply our results to derive maximal inequalities for Wasserstein contractive Markov chains.

Given some integrable
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\pi(f)=0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
we define

S_{n}^{*}f:=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|S_{k}f|=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{k}f(X_{j})\right|.

Estimates for
S_{n}^{*}f
are called
maximal inequalities
.
For example, Kolmogoroff’s inequality bounds
\mathbb{P}[S_{n}^{*}f\geq\eps]
for
f\in L^{2}(\pi)
if
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is an iid sequence with
X_{1}\sim\pi
, cf.
[
Dud02
, Chapter 9]
.
Additionally, there are different inequalities due to Doob bounding
\mathbb{P}[S_{n}^{*}f\geq\eps]
or
\mathbb{E}[(S_{n}^{*}f)^{p}]
provided
(S_{n}f)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sufficiently regular martingale, see e.g.
[
RY99
]
.

Here we want to derive analogues for Wasserstein contractive Markov chains.
As before, such a chain is denoted by
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, its transition kernel by
P
, and its invariant and initial distribution are
\pi
and
\nu
, respectively.
Recall that the space of measurable Lipschitz functions (w.r.t.
d
) is
\mathscr{L}
, and
\mathscr{L}_{0}\subseteq\mathscr{L}
contains those
f\in\mathscr{L}
satisfying
\pi(f)=0
.
Finally, we remind ourselves about the eccentricities
E_{p}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}d(x,y)^{p}\pi({\rm d}y)
.

The main tool in this section is decomposition (
2
), which shows that

S_{n}f=M_{n}+u(X_{1})-u(X_{n+1}),

where
u
is a solution of Poisson’s equation with forcing function
f
.
Using this identity for any
1\leq k\leq n
we obtain

S_{n}^{*}f\leq M_{n}^{*}+R_{n}^{*},

(4)

with
M_{n}^{*}=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|M_{k}|
and
R_{n}^{*}=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|u(X_{1})-u(X_{k+1})|
.

The remainder of this section is organised as follows. First we prove some preliminary results which provide estimates for
M_{n}
under general integrability conditions on
u
.
Then, we prove maximal inequalities for
f\in\mathscr{L}_{0}
.

4.1
Regularity of the martingale
M_{n}

Here we investigate the integrability conditions of
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
This is done under general conditions on
u
and the Markov chain
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Recall that the underlying probability space is
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}_{\nu})
and that
the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is defined as
\mathcal{F}_{n}=\sigma(X_{k}\colon 1\leq k\leq n+1)
.

Let
\nu\ll\pi
with Radon-Nikodým derivative
\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\pi}\in L^{q}(\pi)
, for some
q\in(1,\infty]
.
For some generic integrable
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
assume
u
is the solution of Poisson’s equation with forcing function
f
.
Throughout this section
M_{n},R_{n}
(see (
2
)) correspond to this
f
and
u
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
s
be the Hoelder conjugate
12
12

12

That is,
s\in(1,\infty)
is the unique number such that
s^{-1}+q^{-1}=1
.
of
q\in(1,\infty)
.
Let
p\geq 1
and let
r\geq sp
. If
u\in L^{r}(\pi)
, then
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a martingale w.r.t.
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and for its differences we have

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[|M_{n+1}-M_{n}|^{p}]^{1/p}\leq 2\left\|\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\pi}\right\|_{L^{q}(\pi)}^{1/p}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{r}(\pi)}

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
In particular, this implies that
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
L^{p}
martingale.

Proof.

We begin by showing the claimed bound on the differences.
For the sake of brevity write
\Delta_{j}=u(X_{j+1})-Pu(X_{j})
such that
M_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Delta_{j}
.

Note that
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}
only differ by the choice of the initial distribution.
By Hoelder’s inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[|\Delta_{j}|^{p}\right]^{1/p}=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\pi}(X_{1})|\Delta_{j}|^{p}\right]^{1/p}\leq\left\|\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\pi}\right\|_{L^{q}(\pi)}^{1/p}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[|\Delta_{j}|^{ps}\right]^{1/(sp)}.

By assumption
r\geq sp
, such that by definition of
\Delta_{j}
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[|\Delta_{j}|^{ps}\right]^{1/(sp)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[|\Delta_{j}|^{r}\right]^{1/r}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[|u(X_{j+1})|^{r}\right]^{1/r}+\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[|Pu(X_{j})|^{r}\right]^{1/r}\leq 2\left\|u\right\|_{L^{r}(\pi)},

where in the last step we used that
\left\|P\right\|_{L^{r}(\pi)\to L^{r}(\pi)}=1
.

It follows that
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[|M_{n}|]<\infty
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, by definition of
M_{n}
, we also have

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{n}|\mathcal{F}_{n-1}]=0

showing that
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a martingale as claimed.
∎

The proof of the previous lemma also transfers to the case
q=\infty
.
We summarise this in the following lemma, whose proof is left to the reader.

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume
q=\infty
and let
p\geq 1
.
If
u\in L^{p}(\pi)
, then
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a martingale w.r.t.
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and for its differences we have

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[|M_{n+1}-M_{n}|^{p}]^{1/p}\leq 2\left\|\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\pi}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\pi)}^{1/p}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{p}(\pi)}

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
In particular, this implies that
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
L^{p}
martingale.

4.2
Maximal inequalities for Lipschitz functions

In this section we show bounds for
S_{n}^{*}f
, with
f\in\mathscr{L}_{0}
.
Throughout, we assume that
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is contractive, cf. Definition
2.1
.
We define

\delta:=\text{diam}(\mathcal{X})=\sup_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}d(x,y).

We begin by proving results for the case where
\delta<\infty
, and then turn to more general settings.

4.2.1
Bounded diameter

Here we consider the case
\delta<\infty
, implying
E_{p}(x_{0})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}d(x_{0},x)^{p}\pi({\rm d}x)\leq\delta^{p}<\infty
for any
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
and
p\geq 1
.
Examples include uniform ergodic chains, see Lemma
2.8
, or compact state spaces
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{s}
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
f\in\mathscr{L}_{0}
. Assume
\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\pi}\in L^{q}(\pi)
for some
q\in(1,\infty)
, then, for any
p\in[1,\infty)
and any
t\in(0,\infty)
we have

\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left[S_{n}^{*}f>t\right]\leq\left(\frac{2}{t}\right)^{p}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[|M_{n}|^{p}\right]+C^{p}\left\|f\right\|_{d}^{p}\right),

with
C=\delta\cdot\Lambda
and
M_{n}
as in (
2
).

Proof.

Observe that
E_{r}(x)\leq\delta^{r}
for any
r\in[1,\infty)
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

By virtue of Theorem
3.5
we find that there exists a solution of Poisson’s equation (with forcing function
f
), say
u
, and that
\left\|u\right\|_{L^{r}(\pi)}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{d}
for any
r\in[1,\infty)
, with
C=\delta\cdot\Lambda
.

Hence Lemma
4.1
may be applied with arbitrary
p\in[1,\infty)
and yields that
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
L^{p}
martingale (on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}_{\nu})
and w.r.t. filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
). For arbitrary
\eps>0
Doob’s maximal inequality, see
[
RY99
]
- Corollary 1.6 of Chapter II, yields

\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left[M_{n}^{*}>\eps\right]\leq\left(\frac{1}{\eps^{p}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[|M_{n}|^{p}]\right).

By the Lipschitz continuity of
u
it follows

|u(X_{k})-u(X_{1})|\leq\left\|u\right\|_{d}d(X_{k},X_{1})\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{d}.

Hence
R^{*}_{n}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{d}
which implies that
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}[R_{n}^{*}>\eps]\leq\left(\frac{C\left\|f\right\|_{d}}{\eps}\right)^{p}
, for any
p\in[1,\infty)
.
Now, the result follows by observing that

\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left[S_{n}^{*}f>t\right]\leq\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left[M_{n}^{*}f>t/2\right]+\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left[R_{n}^{*}f>t/2\right],

which is a consequence of (
4
).
∎

The upcoming result can be seen as a version of Doob’s maximal inequality, see Corollary 1.6 of Chapter II in
[
RY99
]
, for contractive chains.

Theorem 4.4
.

Assume
\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\pi}\in L^{q}(\pi)
for some
q\in(1,\infty)
with Hoelder conjugate
s>1
.
Then, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\sup_{f\in\mathscr{L}_{0};\left\|f\right\|_{d}\leq 1}\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left[S_{n}^{*}f>t\right]\leq\frac{4}{t^{2}}\left(2n\left\|\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\pi}\right\|_{L^{q}(\pi)}C^{2r}+C^{2}\right),

for any
t>0
,
where
C=\delta\cdot\Lambda
and
r=2s
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathscr{L}_{0}
with
\left\|f\right\|_{d}\leq 1
.
Note that all assumptions to apply Proposition
4.3
with
p=2
are met.

Employing Lemma
4.1
we know that
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
L^{2}
martingale (on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}_{\nu})
w.r.t. filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
).
Set
\Delta_{j}=u(X_{j+1})-Pu(X_{j})
such that
M_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Delta_{j}
.
By the martingale property and Lemma
4.1
,

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[M_{n}^{2}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\Delta_{j}^{2}\right]\leq 2n\left\|\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\pi}\right\|_{L^{q}(\pi)}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{r}(\pi)}^{2}\leq 2n\left\|\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\pi}\right\|_{L^{q}(\pi)}C^{2r},

where im the last step we used Theorem
3.5
to bound
\left\|u\right\|_{L^{r}(\pi)}
.
The desired result is now a consequence of Proposition
4.3
.
∎

Next we present an
L^{2}
maximal inequality for contractive chains.

Theorem 4.5
.

Assume
\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\pi}\in L^{q}(\pi)
for some
q\in(1,\infty)
with Hoelder conjugate
s>1
.
Then, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\sup_{f\in\mathscr{L}_{0};\left\|f\right\|_{d}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[(S_{n}^{*}f)^{2}\right]\leq\left(32C^{2r}n\left\|\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\pi}\right\|_{L^{q}(\pi)}+2C^{2}\right),

where
C=\delta\cdot\Lambda
and
r=2s
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathscr{L}_{0}
with
\left\|f\right\|_{d}\leq 1
.
As in the proof of Theorem
4.4
we deduce that
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
L^{2}
martingale and

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[M_{n}^{2}\right]\leq 2n\left\|\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\pi}\right\|_{L^{q}(\pi)}C^{2r},

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Additionally, we know that
R_{n}^{*}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{d}=C
(see proof of Proposition
4.3
).
Using (
4
) and then Doob’s
L^{2}
inequality, see
[
RY99
]
, yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[(S_{n}^{*}f)^{2}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[(M_{n}^{*})^{2}\right]^{1/2}+\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[(R_{n}^{*})^{2}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[M_{n}^{2}\right]^{1/2}+\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[(R_{n}^{*})^{2}\right]^{1/2}\leq 4\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[M_{n}^{2}\right]^{1/2}+C.

Combining the inequality
(\alpha+\beta)^{2}\leq 2(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})
with the above estimates, the desired result follows.
∎

4.2.2
Distributions with finite moments

In the previous section we studied the case where
\mathcal{X}
has bounded diameter which might not always be the case in applications. Here we prove maximal inequalities under suitable moment assumptions on

, where one does not necessarily require

To this end, for

, define

Throughout this section we assume that

In order prove our main result we shall make use of the following condition about

.

Assumption 4.6
.

, with

as in (

Remark 4.7
.

The uniformity in Assumption

Theorem 4.8
.

Let

and
\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\pi}\in L^{q}(\pi)
for some

, with Hoelder conjugate

.
Assume that
\eps_{r}<\infty
for

.
If Assumption

is satisfied, then, for any

we have

\sup_{f\in\mathscr{L}_{0};\left\|f\right\|_{d}\leq 1}\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left[S_{n}^{*}f>t\right]\leq\frac{4n}{t^{2}}\left(4\left\|\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\pi}\right\|_{L^{q}(\pi)}\left(\Lambda\eps_{r}\right)^{2}+\widehat{C}\right),

for any
t>0
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathscr{L}_{0}
with
\left\|f\right\|_{d}\leq 1
be fixed.
Due to Theorem
3.5
the corresponding solution of Poisson’s equation
u
is in
L^{r}_{0}(\pi)
.
Lemma
4.1
implies that the martingale
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, as specified in (
2
), has increments, say
(\Delta_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, with
\Delta_{j}=u(X_{j+1})-Pu(X_{j})
, which are uniformly bounded in
L^{2}
, that is

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\Delta_{j}^{2}\right]\leq 4\left\|\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\pi}\right\|_{L^{q}(\pi)}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{r}(\pi)}^{2}.

Doob’s maximal inequality (see Corollary 1.6 of Chapter II in
[
RY99
]
) yields

\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left[M_{n}^{*}>\delta\right]\leq\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[M_{n}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Delta_{j}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{4n\left\|\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\pi}\right\|_{L^{q}(\pi)}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{r}(\pi)}^{2}}{\delta^{2}},

for any
\delta>0
.

Combining Assumption
4.6
and
\left\|f\right\|_{d}\leq 1
we deduce

\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left[R_{n}^{*}>\delta\right]\leq\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[(R_{n}^{*})^{2}\right]\leq\frac{\widehat{C}\cdot n}{\delta^{2}}.

Hence the statement follows by observing that

\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left[S_{n}^{*}f>t\right]\leq\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left[M_{n}^{*}>t/2\right]+\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left[R_{n}^{*}>t/2\right],

and that
\left\|u\right\|_{L^{r}(\pi)}\leq\Lambda\eps_{r}
(see Theorem
3.5
).
∎

Combining the bounds for
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[(M_{n})^{2}\right]
and
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[(R_{n}^{*})^{2}\right]
from the proof of Theorem
4.8
with Doob’s
L^{2}
-inequality, cf.
[
RY99
]
, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 4.9
.

Under the same assumptions as in Theorem
4.8
, and with
q,r,\widehat{C}
as specified there, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\sup_{f\in\mathscr{L}_{0};\left\|f\right\|_{d}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[(S_{n}^{*}f)^{2}]\leq{4n}\left(8\left\|\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\pi}\right\|_{L^{q}(\pi)}\left(\Lambda\eps_{r}\right)^{2}+\widehat{C}\right).

We conclude this section by showing that Assumption
4.6
is satisfied for the setting considered in Example
2.13
.

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
\alpha<1/2
and
d_{\alpha}
,
\mathcal{W}_{\alpha}
,
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be as in Example
2.13
.
Then, Assumption
4.6
is true if
\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\pi}\in L^{\beta}(\pi)
where
\beta
is the Hoelder conjugate of
1/(2\alpha)>1
.

Proof.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed and for
1\leq j\leq n
chose
A_{j}^{(n)}\in\mathcal{F}
such that on each
A_{j}^{(n)}
we have
R_{n}^{*}=|u(X_{1})-u(X_{j+1})|
.
Note that such a collection of (measurable) sets always exists, and moreover, that it is possible to chose the
A_{j}^{(n)}
’s disjoint. This implies that
(A_{j}^{(n)})_{j=1}^{n}
is a disjoint partition of
\Omega
.

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[(R_{n}^{*})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\mathds{1}_{A_{j}^{(n)}}|u(X_{1})-u(X_{j+1})|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[|u(X_{1})-u(X_{j+1})|^{2}\right].

By Theorem
3.4
the function
u
is Lipschitz with constant
C(\tau)\left\|f\right\|_{d}={C^{\prime}}
, where
C(\tau)
does not depend on
f\in\mathscr{L}_{0}
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[|u(X_{1})-u(X_{j+1})|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(C^{\prime})^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[d_{\alpha}(X_{1},X_{j+1})^{2}]

\displaystyle=(C^{\prime})^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[(V^{\alpha}(X_{1})+V^{\alpha}(X_{j+1}))^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq 4(C^{\prime})^{2}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[V^{2\alpha}(X_{1})]+\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[V^{2\alpha}(X_{j+1})]\right).

Setting
\theta=\left\|\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\pi}\right\|_{L^{\beta}(\pi)}
we obtain for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
by Hoelder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[V^{2\alpha}(X_{k})]

\displaystyle\leq\theta\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[V(X_{k})]^{2\alpha}.

Now,
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[V(X_{k})]=\pi(V)\leq\frac{b}{1-\lambda}
, which follows e.g. by
[
Hai18
, Proposition 4.24]
.
Combining all the above equations we arrive at

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[(R_{n}^{*})^{2}\right]\leq\widehat{C}\left\|f\right\|_{d}^{2}n,

where
\widehat{C}\in(0,\infty)
is a constant independent of
f
,
u
and
n
as required.
∎

Remark 4.11
.

Proposition
4.10
can be extended to chains for which

\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[d(X_{j+1},X_{1})^{2}]<\infty.

4.3
Path-wise stability of No-U-Turn-Samplers

In this section we demonstrate how maximal inequalities can be used to investigate path-wise convergence of MCMC estimators for
\pi(f)
.
We focus on the NUTS algorithm, compare Example
2.15
and
[
HG14
,
DGK+25
]
.
Recently, similar results were obtained in
[
HŁRR26
]
for adaptive MCMC methods, however, the No-U-Turn-Sampler was not studied there.

Recall that in the setting of Example
2.15
we work in the Euclidean space
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{s}
.
Writing
\left\|\cdot\right\|
for the Euclidean norm, define
V\colon\mathcal{X}\to[1,\infty)
by

V(x)=\exp(\eta\left\|x\right\|),

where
\eta>0
.
We assume that all conditions to apply
[
DGK+25
, Theorem 17]
are satisfied such that NUTS algorithm induces a
V
-uniformly ergodic chain
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Note that the proof of
[
DGK+25
, Theorem 17]
relies on showing
drift and minorisation conditions
, as in Example
2.13
.
Hence, as argued in Example
2.13
,
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is also
V^{\alpha}
-uniformly ergodic for any
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
.

For the remainder of this section let
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
be fixed.
By Lemma
2.12
we obtain that
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is contractive w.r.t.
\mathcal{W}_{\alpha}
based on the metric

d_{\alpha}(x,y)=\mathds{1}_{\{x\neq y\}}(V^{\alpha}(x)+V^{\alpha}(y)).

The following result characterises the speed of convergence for a single realisation of the NUTS estimator for
\pi(f)
.
For the sake of exposition we assume that
\frac{{\rm d}\nu}{{\rm d}\pi}
exists and is bounded, where
\nu
is the initial distribution.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be measurable with
|f|\leq V^{\alpha}
.
Then, there exists
C\colon\Omega\to(0,\infty)
such that for
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
-almost all
\omega\in\Omega
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(X_{j}(\omega))-\pi(f)\right|\leq C(\omega)\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}.

Proof.

Note that
\pi(V)<\infty
implying that
f\in L^{2}(\pi)
, hence it is no restriction to assume
\pi(f)=0
.
Additionally,
|f|\leq V^{\alpha}
implies that
f
is Lipschitz w.r.t.
d_{\alpha}
, and Assumption
4.6
is true as a consequence of Proposition
4.10
.

Set
n_{k}=2^{k}
for and
r_{k}=\sqrt{k}\log(k)
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Theorem
4.8
implies that

\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left[S_{n_{k}}^{*}f>r_{n_{k}}t\right]\leq\frac{c}{(tk)^{2}},

for any
t>0
, with some absolute constant
c>0
.
Employing the lemma of Borel-Cantelli we deduce that
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
-almost surely

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{r_{n_{k}}}S_{n_{k}}^{*}f=0.

Now, given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, chose
k
such that
n_{k-1}<n\leq n_{k}
.
We observe

0\leq\left|\frac{1}{r_{n}}S_{n}f\right|\leq\frac{1}{r_{n}}S_{n_{k}}^{*}f\leq\frac{4}{r_{n_{k}}}S_{n_{k}}^{*}f.

Hence

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{1}{r_{n}}S_{n}f\right|=0,

\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
-almost surely implying the desired result.
∎
