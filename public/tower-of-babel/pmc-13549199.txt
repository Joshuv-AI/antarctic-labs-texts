Acute Invasive Fungal Rhino-Orbital Sinusitis: Current Concepts and Controversies
Authors: Alise Aucoin, Evan Kalin-Hajdu, Tarjani V Dave, M Reza Vagefi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute invasive fungal rhinosinusitis (AIFRS) is a fulminant angioinvasive infection that primarily affects immunocompromised patients and carries substantial morbidity and mortality. Orbital involvement presents particular diagnostic and therapeutic challenges and remains an area of controversy. This review summarizes current concepts in the classification, microbiology, diagnosis, and management of AIFRS, with emphasis on orbital involvement, termed acute invasive fungal rhino-orbital sinusitis (AIFROS). Early recognition, urgent imaging, histopathologic diagnosis, systemic antifungal therapy, debridement of necrotic tissue, and reversal of underlying immunosuppression remain the cornerstones of treating AIFRS. In contrast, management of AIFROS remains less standardized; globe-sparing approaches are increasingly employed in selected patients, as the literature has not demonstrated a clear survival benefit from orbital exenteration.

Introduction

Acute invasive fungal rhinosinusitis (AIFRS) is a fulminant and frequently fatal infection of the paranasal sinuses, with reported mortality rates ranging from 50% to 80%.1,2 It is defined by fungal hyphal invasion of mucosa, submucosa, bone, or vasculature that develops over a time course of four weeks or less.1,2 Infection arises almost exclusively in immunocompromised hosts, with diabetes mellitus, hematologic malignancy, and solid organ transplantation accounting for the majority of cases.3 The disease frequently spreads into adjacent structures, including the orbit. Once present in the orbit, the disease may be termed acute invasive fungal rhino-orbital sinusitis (AIFROS). Orbital involvement occurs in about 40% to 65% of AIFRS cases.3–5

Because early detection and intervention are essential to limit morbidity and mortality, investigators have sought to identify characteristic radiologic findings and rapid diagnostic tools that facilitate prompt recognition.6 The standard of care for sinonasal involvement is relatively well established, whereas management of AIFROS remains less well defined, with current evidence largely limited to small- to medium-sized retrospective case series. The COVID-19 pandemic led to a marked rise in AIFROS cases, expanding the literature on orbital management and accelerating interest in globe-sparing strategies such as transcutaneous retrobulbar amphotericin B (TRAMB).7,8

This review summarizes current concepts in AIFRS and focuses on unresolved controversies in diagnosis and management, particularly the roles of advanced imaging, rapid diagnostics, local orbital therapy, and orbital exenteration. A practical treatment framework is also proposed.

Methods and Materials

This article is a narrative review of AIFRS and AIFROS, with emphasis on orbital involvement, imaging, diagnostics, and management controversies. The aim was to synthesize contemporary evidence across multiple specialties and to identify areas of ongoing controversy.

A comprehensive search of PubMed/MEDLINE database covered publications from January 2020 through May 2026 to capture contemporary evidence, post-COVID-19 epidemiological shifts, the emergence of globe-sparing orbital therapies, and novel diagnostics. Foundational publications predating 2020 were included when they established diagnostic classifications, defined management principles, or provided historical context for trends in outcomes.

The search strategy utilized a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms: “acute invasive fungal rhinosinusitis”, “invasive fungal sinusitis”, “mucormycosis”, “rhino-orbital-cerebral mucormycosis”, “ROCM”, “retrobulbar amphotericin B”, “orbital exenteration”, “globe-sparing”, and “COVID-19-associated mucormycosis”.

Eligible publications were English-language and peer-reviewed, reported on human subjects with acute invasive fungal rhinosinusitis with or without orbital involvement, and addressed epidemiology, risk factors, diagnosis, imaging, medical or surgical management, or outcomes. Priority was given to systematic reviews, meta-analyses, multicenter studies, consensus guidance, and clinically influential case series relevant to ophthalmology, otolaryngology, infectious disease, radiology, and pathology.

Exclusion criteria comprised studies of chronic invasive, granulomatous invasive, and noninvasive fungal rhinosinusitis; non-English publications; conference abstracts without full-text publication. Animal studies, in vitro studies, and case reports were excluded, except where they provided the sole mechanistic or clinical basis for a diagnostic or treatment strategy. The level of evidence is identified explicitly in the text for those instances.

Classification and Pathophysiology

Fungal sinusitis is classified based on two primary characteristics. Chronologically, it is defined as acute if symptom duration is less than one month and chronic if symptoms persist for more than three months.9 Histologically, the disease is categorized as either non-invasive or invasive.9

Non-Invasive Fungal Sinusitis

Non-invasive fungal sinusitis commonly presents in immunocompetent hosts and is limited to the nasal cavity and sinuses. It is subdivided into three histopathologic categories: fungus ball, saprophytic fungal sinusitis, and allergic fungal rhinosinusitis.10 These types of fungal sinusitis will not be a focus of this review.

Invasive Fungal Rhinosinusitis

Invasive fungal rhinosinusitis (IFRS) defines any fungal infection of the sinuses that breaches the mucosal barrier to invade underlying tissues. There are three subtypes of IFRS: acute invasive fungal rhinosinusitis (AIFRS), chronic invasive fungal sinusitis (CIFS), and chronic granulomatous invasive fungal sinusitis (CGIFS).1,11 Although the invasive subtypes share overlapping diagnostic principles, they differ in tempo, host factors, histopathology, and management considerations.

AIFRS is the most common and aggressive subtype; it has a symptom onset less than a month and a mortality rate typically ranging from 50% to 80%.9,12 This disease almost exclusively affects immunocompromised patients.9,12 Histologically, the disease is defined by fungal angioinvasion leading to thrombosis and infarction.9,12 Orbital involvement occurs in about 40% to 65% of AIFRS cases.3–5

CIFS and CGIFS share a clinical course that exceeds three months and differ in their epidemiologic and histopathologic profiles. CIFS affects both immunocompetent and immunosuppressed individuals without geographic preference.13 Histologically, CIFS is defined by hyphal sinonasal invasion with non-granulomatous inflammation, and unlike AIFRS, there is minimal angioinvasion.1

CGIFS primarily occurs in healthy, immunocompetent individuals and is endemic to India, Sudan, and the Middle East.13 Histopathology reveals submucosal granulomatous inflammation with diffuse fibrosis and sparse fungal elements.1

Organisms

Fungi primarily involved in AIFRS include species within the genera Mucor, Rhizopus, and Aspergillus.14 Mucor and Rhizopus belong to the Mucorales order.15 Infections caused by this group are often termed “mucormycosis”, which has replaced the term “zygomycotes”.15 Mucor and Rhizopus species have aseptate hyphae with wide-angle (90°) branching.14 Aspergillus species have narrow septate hyphae with acute-angle (45°) dichotomous branching.14

Organisms within the order Mucorales, specifically the Rhizopus species, are the most frequently isolated pathogens in AIFROS.16,17 Aspergillus species are the primary cause of AIFRS in neutropenic patients, including those with leukemia or bone marrow transplant recipients.18,19 While Aspergillus species can infect diabetics, Mucorales species are more commonly associated with this population.17

Risk Factors

Immunosuppression, particularly neutrophil dysfunction, is the primary risk factor for AIFRS.2,3 Diabetes mellitus is the most common predisposing condition for AIFRS, including in COVID associated mucormycosis (CAM).3,20 This is especially true for patients with diabetic ketoacidosis.3 The combination of hyperglycemia and low pH in diabetes mellitus impairs the ability of neutrophils to detect, phagocytose, and kill pathogens, increasing susceptibility for invasive fungal infections.21,22

Severe neutropenia (defined as counts less than 500 neutrophils/mm3) poses a significant risk for AIFRS.3 Conditions associated with severe neutropenia include hematologic dyscrasias and malignancies, chemotherapy, immunosuppressive therapy, solid organ transplantation, bone marrow and stem cell transplantation.3,19,23

Other risk factors include systemic steroid use, HIV, iron overloaded, renal insufficiency, and advanced age.3,5,20,24–28 More recently, COVID-19 was identified as a risk factor, particularly in the Middle East and India.20,25,29–31

COVID-19 and AIFRS

The COVID-19 pandemic led to a rise in cases of AIFRS.20,25,29–31 This surge was most pronounced in India, with a prevalence nearly 80 times higher than developed countries.32–34 Three factors are thought to contribute to the increased susceptibility to AIFRS in patients with COVID-19: immune dysregulation secondary to the virus, exacerbation of comorbidities (especially diabetes), and iatrogenic immune suppression with corticosteroid treatment for respiratory failure.28,35 A meta-analysis of 958 COVID-19-associated mucormycosis infections identified concurrent diabetes in 77.9% of patients and steroid use in 78.5%.31

The vast majority of AIFRS cases associated with COVID-19 are caused by organisms in the order Mucorales, leading to the term “COVID-19 associated mucormycosis” (CAM).29,36,37 Within this order, Rhizopus species were the most commonly isolated organisms.29,36,37 AIFRS caused by coinfection with Mucorales and Aspergillus species has been reported more frequently in COVID-19 patients (8.5–18.9%) than in those without COVID (6–10%) and has been associated with higher mortality.31,38

Diagnostic Evaluation

Clinical Exam

AIFRS can be a challenging clinical diagnosis to establish due to nonspecific findings on presentation. Patients typically report indistinct sinonasal complaints such as facial swelling, fever, nasal congestion, nasal discharge, and facial pain.39 Because early signs are also often subtle, a high index of suspicion is important in immunocompromised patients presenting with rapidly progressive sinusitis or facial pain. When suspected, nasal endoscopy and oral examination of the hard palate are indicated.

Imaging

Radiologic imaging is an essential tool for the detection of AIFRS. In practice, computed tomography (CT) is often used for rapid initial assessment, whereas magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is more informative for early extrasinus spread, orbital soft-tissue involvement, vascular compromise, and prognostication.40,41 CT can quickly evaluate disruption of soft tissues and bone; however CT findings are often nonspecific, with mucosal thickening of the paranasal sinuses and nasal cavity that may mimic bacterial or viral sinusitis.42,43 More characteristic signs on CT like bony erosion usually appear late in the disease course, when the window for successful intervention has narrowed.42,43

John et al constructed an imaging-based screening tool for AIFRS using nine CT markers.41 These markers include pterygopalatine fossa involvement, premaxillary thickening, retroantral fat stranding, sphenopalatine foramen involvement, infratemporal muscle edema, inferior orbital fissure involvement, extraconal fat stranding, intraconal fat stranding, and lacrimal sac and nasolacrimal duct involvement.41 The presence of three or more markers yielded a 91.5% sensitivity and 95.9% specificity.41 The authors noted a mortality rate of 26%, lower than the 50–80% typically associated with AIFRS.41,44–47

MRI is the imaging modality of choice as it is more sensitive for detecting early invasion.42,48,49 In addition, MRI correlates more accurately with intraoperative findings.42,48,49 Early MRI findings suggestive of AIFROS include stranding of orbital fat and loss of contrast enhancement (LoCE).50–52 LoCE reflects infarcted or necrotic tissue and can be assessed on contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging (Figure 2).50–52 T2-weighted MRI may identify hypointense or isointense regions in areas that would otherwise be expected to enhance normally, a finding believed to reflect high iron and magnesium content that supports fungal growth.50,52–54 Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) may also help identify infarcted or necrotic tissue by demonstrating restricted diffusion related to cytotoxic edema.50,51 The initial MRI may have prognostic value for both visual acuity and mortality.50 The risk of poor visual outcome increases significantly when orbital apex or cerebral arterial involvement is present. Histology and Culture

Biopsy and culture are essential for selecting the appropriate antifungal therapy. Intranasal biopsies are often obtained from the middle turbinate because it is frequently the first structure to show signs of necrosis and offers a high diagnostic yield. Among the paranasal sinuses, the maxillary sinus is the most frequently involved site.23,40,56 Biopsies should be obtained from highly suspicious areas, such as pale, darkened, crusted, or ulcerated mucosa.57

The gold standard for definitive diagnosis of AIFRS involves histopathological confirmation of fungal hyphae invading tissue (Figure 3). The process of preparing formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded sections requires about 4 to 5 days or more if decalcification of bony tissue is indicated.58 Because AIFRS progresses rapidly and carries high morbidity and mortality, this slower diagnostic tool is often less practical in urgent clinical decision-making. Intraoperative frozen section biopsies are used because they offer rapid turnaround. A 2021 meta-analysis found that using frozen sections to diagnose AIFRS had a sensitivity, specificity, and positive predictive value (PPV) of 0.833, 0.985, and 0.982, respectively.59 However, the study also identified a 17% false negative rate.59 A high false negative rate may delay treatment or leave residual disease untreated. A retrospective analysis by Alkhateb et al found that examining multiple specimens yielded a sensitivity of 90% per patient.56 Surgeons should therefore consider obtaining multiple biopsies when initial results are negative despite a high index of suspicion.

Molecular and Biomarker Testing

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) is an important diagnostic tool for detecting fungal species. A large 2021 retrospective study evaluating broad-range PCR for the diagnosis of AIFRS found that PCR sensitivity (85%) was significantly higher than culture alone (67.5%), and that combining PCR with culture increased sensitivity to 90%.60 In addition, PCR provided shorter turnaround times for both preliminary positive results (94.5 hours vs 108.8 hours) and final positive results (137.9 hours vs 278.5 hours).60 Accordingly, broad-range fungal PCR may serve as a useful adjunct to standard culture by increasing sensitivity and shorten time to organism identification.

Serological tests offer an additional means of identifying fungal species in AIFRS. Galactomannan (GM) and 1,3-β-d-glucan (BDG) are polysaccharides found in the cell wall of Aspergillus species and can be measured via serum immunoassays to support the diagnosis invasive Aspergillosis. A 2024 meta-analysis found that the pooled sensitivity and specificity of combined serum GM and BDG testing for invasive Aspergillosis were 84% and 76%, respectively.61

Although serologic assays are primarily used to diagnose Aspergillus infection, serologic targets have also been identified for Mucorales species. Burnham-Marusich et al found that the monoclonal antibody 2DA6, which detects α-1.6-mannan backbone on the fungal cell wall, has a high analytical sensitivity for Mucorales species.62 Thornton et al developed a monoclonal antibody, TG11, which binds to extracellular polysaccharide (EPS) antigens during Mucorales hyphal growth.63 This assay yields results within 30 minutes and demonstrates no cross-reactivity with other fungi.63 Although these tests are not yet commercially available, they represent promising approaches for serological detection of Mucorales.

Emerging Diagnostics

Forthcoming diagnostic techniques may further improve the diagnosis of invasive fungal infections. Point-of-care (POC) clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-based tools, such as CRISPR-chip platforms, offer sensitivity and specificity comparable to or greater than PCR and may detect genomic DNA in as little as 15 minutes.64 Most currently available CRISPR-based POC tests target viral pathogens.65–67 Fungal assays are now being investigated.61,68,69 Huang et al developed a quantitative CRISPR-based POC assay to detect invasive fungal infections by Candida, Aspergillus, and Cryptococcus and reported high sensitivity, high specificity, and low production costs.70 While promising, CRISPR-based diagnostic tests remain investigational in fungal disease and require broader clinical validation, particularly for AIFRS.61,71

Other methodologies such as recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) are also being investigated. Xu et al developed a real-time RPA and quantitative PCR (qPCR) assay targeting the mitochondrial rnl gene for rapid detection of Mucorales. Although the assay demonstrated promising results in experimental strains across several Mucorales species, studies using clinical samples are needed to better define its clinical utility.72

Multidisciplinary Management

The management of AIFRS and AIFROS requires a multidisciplinary approach. Optimal care typically requires close coordination among otolaryngology, ophthalmology, neurosurgery, infectious disease, radiology, pathology, and the teams managing the patient’s underlying immunocompromised state, such as hematology-oncology, transplant medicine, or critical care. Early communication among these services can shorten time to biopsy, accelerate interpretation of imaging and frozen section findings, guide antifungal selection, and support timely decisions regarding local orbital intervention.

The standard of care treatment for all patients with AIFRS (with or without orbital involvement) involves a three-pronged approach with: (1)Systemic antifungal therapy.(2)Surgical debridement of necrotic sinonasal tissue.(3)Reversal of immunocompromised status.

Systemic Antifungals

Antifungal treatment is organism dependent. Amphotericin B is the standard antifungal for AIFRS caused by Mucorales species.71,73,74 Due to the significant toxicity of Amphotericin B deoxycholate (D-AmB), more tolerable lipid formulations (ie, Liposomal Amphotericin B (L-AmB) and Amphotericin B Lipid Complex (ABLC)) were developed. Current guidelines from the European Confederation of Medical Mycology and European Conference on Infections in Leukemia endorse liposomal amphotericin B (L-AmB) as first-line systemic antifungal for mucormycosis.71,73,74 Standard dosing is 5 mg/kg per day.71,73,74 The AMBIZYGO trial evaluated the safety and efficacy of high dose (10 mg/kg/day) L-AmB monotherapy but found no statistical improvement in response rates compared to standard dosing.75 Additionally, 40% of patients experienced doubling of their baseline serum creatinine, requiring a reduction of dose.75 High dose (10 mg/kg/day) is usually reserved for those with intracranial extension.71,73,74

Patients who achieve a clinical and radiographic response to an initial parenteral course of L-AmB (typically lasting at least three weeks) are usually step-downed to oral posaconazole or isavuconazole.76–80 These agents are also effective options for patients with renal failure or those refractory to or intolerant of amphotericin B.76–81

Studies have evaluated combination therapy with amphotericin B. Echinocandins like caspofungin show no activity against Mucorales fungi in standard in vitro susceptibility tests. However, R. oryzae expresses the target enzyme for this drug class.82,83 Studies treating mucormycosis in murine models found a synergistic response with caspofungin and ABLC.82,83 Reed et al supported this clinically; patients treated with caspofungin plus ABLC had a 100% response rate compared with a 45% response rate among patients treated with ABLC alone.84 The favorable safety profile of caspofungin make it an attractive option for combination therapy.

Voriconazole remains the preferred first-line agent for invasive aspergillosis (IA).71,73,74 However, isavuconazole is increasingly recommended as a first-line alternative due to its comparable efficacy and more favorable safety profile, especially in patients over 80 years old.85 The SECURE trial compared isavuconazole to voriconazole and found no significant difference in all-cause mortality (19% vs 20%).86 L-AmB is recommended for initial therapy when triazoles are contraindicated.87 In those who fail therapy, develop triazole resistance, or have adverse effects, L-AmB or echinocandins can be used.87

Sinonasal Debridement

Surgical debridement of the infected sinonasal tissue is consistently identified in the literature as a positive prognostic factor for survival in AIFRS.28,56,88,89 Mechanistically, debridement of infected tissue reduces fungal load and allows for better antifungal delivery to the site of infection.90 The timing of intervention is important; multiple studies report that early debridement limits disease extension and significantly improves survival rates.91–94

Endoscopic sinus surgery is generally preferred over the open approaches due to its lower morbidity and comparable survival outcomes.44,95 Open surgeries, such as craniofacial resection or maxillectomy, are typically reserved for extensive disease that cannot be adequately addressed endoscopically and for cases with osteomyelitis.3,90,96

Reversal of Immunocompromised Status

Reversal of immunocompromised status is essential in the management of AIFRS. Diabetic patients generally demonstrate superior survival outcomes compared to other etiologies of immunosuppression, as hyperglycemia is more readily reversible.19,45,97,98 For patients with neutropenia, such as those with hematologic conditions or drug-induced immunosuppression, survival is strongly correlated with the recovery of ANC to normal levels.99,100 Reversal of immunocompromised status is often more difficult in the setting of underlying malignancy or solid-organ transplantation and should be undertaken in coordination with the relevant clinical team, such as oncology or transplant medicine. Granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor may be an option to boost in the innate immune response and restore neutrophil presence in some patients.101,102

Treatment of the Orbit

Orbital involvement occurs in about 40% to 65% of AIFRS cases.3–5 Management depends on disease extent and ranges from conservative debridement, with or without antifungal irrigation, to local antifungal delivery (eg, transcutaneous retrobulbar injection of amphotericin B, orbital catheter placement, etc), and, in advanced cases, orbital exenteration.103,104 Although exenteration was historically favored because it was thought to reduce the risk of cerebral extension, supporting evidence has been limited. More recently, practice has shifted toward globe-sparing management when possible.

Orbital Exenteration

Until recently, no standard protocol existed to guide management of the orbit in AIFROS, particularly in balancing the delicate trade-off between globe-sparing therapies and disfiguring orbital exenteration. In 2019, Kalin-Hajdu et al proposed a stepwise treatment paradigm, subsequently refined by Ashraf et al, incorporating the extent of LoCE on orbital imaging to guide whether to pursue orbital exenteration, TRAMB, and/or local orbital debridement.103,104

The literature provides limited evidence supporting the impact of exenteration on mortality. While a 2006 study by Hargrove et al suggested that orbital exenteration provided a survival benefit in patients presenting with fever, the validity of these findings was compromised by selection bias and the inclusion of historical cohorts treated prior to the adoption of L-AmB.105 A meta-analysis evaluating survival in AIFRS found that approximately 20% of patients with orbital involvement underwent exenteration, but exenteration was not associated with improved survival.3 Multiple, single-institution studies and a meta-analysis have also found no significant difference in mortality between exenterated and non-exenterated patients.3,5,106,107

Advanced orbital disease as reflected by diffuse LoCE consistent with devitalized, non-salvageable tissue and a high infectious burden, should prompt consideration of exenteration.103,104 Patients should be counseled, however, that exenteration is intended for local disease control and has not been shown to improve survival. Modified approaches to exenteration based on the localization of infection and the patient’s immune status can be employed to minimize disfiguring surgery.

Conservative Debridement with Irrigation

Conservative debridement with irrigation (CDI) aims to remove only affected, necrotic orbital tissue while delivering high local concentrations of antifungal therapy and preserving the globe. Methods of delivering antifungals following debridement include soaked gauze, catheters, and irrigation cannulas.108–115 Standard protocols for orbital irrigation with amphotericin B typically utilize concentrations between 0.25 mg/mL and 1.25 mg/mL administered in volumes of 1 to 15 mL.109,110 When an indwelling delivery method is employed, treatment is delivered one to four times daily.109,110 Reported durations vary widely, ranging from 5 days to 4 weeks.109,110

The clinical utility of CDI remains uncertain, as current literature is limited to case series and reports. Several reports describe improved vision and recovery of ocular motility following treatment, while others report cases of disease progression requiring orbital exenteration.109–114 Guimaraes et al reported successful infection control in a case of mucormycosis with CNS involvement using a combination of surgical debridement and orbital CDI.116 A 2025 case series of 14 patients with late-stage or refractory orbital involvement were treated with endoscopic orbital debridement combined with intraoperative amphotericin B-soaked gelfoam to achieve 100% globe preservation.117 Although vision was not recovered, the globe-sparing protocol halted disease progression in 79% of patients and resulted in a mortality rate of 14.2%.117

The favorable safety profile of CDI makes it a desirable treatment option. The most common side effects of CDI are chemosis and orbital pain which resolve once the treatment is complete.110,116

Transcutaneous Retrobulbar Injection of Amphotericin B

Transcutaneous retrobulbar injection of amphotericin B (TRAMB) has increasingly been implemented as adjunctive therapy to surgical debridement and offers a potential orbit-sparing treatment for AIFROS.2,104,105 It may be performed at bedside after administration of a retrobulbar block of anesthetic, obviating the need for the operating room setting and allowing for expedited intervention within the orbit.

Because TRAMB targets local spread rather than the sinonasal source, it serves as an adjunct to standard of care (ie, systemic therapy, surgical debridement, and immunosuppression reversal) for addressing orbital disease and can be considered as the primary approach to orbital disease when appropriate. Since amphotericin B must be reconstituted in sterile water, local injection of the hypotonic formulation results in significant osmotic disruption to cells and inflammation.118–120 To mitigate some of this response, the liposomal formulation is preferred over the deoxycholate formulation due to its superior safety profile as studies have indicated that cell signaling is diverted away from the characteristic proinflammatory response with the less toxic preparation.118–120 Reported protocols vary, employing concentrations ranging from 1 to 5 mg/mL and volumes of 1.0 to 1.5 mL per injection, administered on a daily or weekly basis.2,121 Subsequent dosing is guided by clinical response and local disease control assessed on radiologic imaging. Notably, subsequent orbital imaging after TRAMB will demonstrate increased contrast enhancement and caution must be taken to not mistaken this for disease progression.

Several reports have associated TRAMB with higher rates of globe preservation. Ashraf et al demonstrated that exenteration rates declined from 36.4% to 9.1% when TRAMB was introduced to their treatment algorithm for AIFROS.122 A recent review by Abdulbaki et al analyzing 411 eyes found that the addition of TRAMB to standard care significantly reduced orbital exenteration rates from 21–57% to approximately 9–12%.103,123 This finding was supported by a 2025 study, which reported an exenteration rate of 8.6% in patients receiving at least one TRAMB injection.124 Many studies have reported improvement in visual acuity (VA) with TRAMB at various follow up intervals.103,123,125,126 Others reported higher rates of VA stability in those treated with TRAMB.127,128 Dallalzadeh et al reported that VA improved with TRAMB in those with limited orbital disease, while VA progressively declined in those with significant orbital disease both with and without TRAMB.123

Limitations of TRAMB include reduced efficacy in advanced orbital disease, treatment-related adverse effects, and uncertainty regarding its impact on survival. Dallalzadeh et al found that the reduction in exenteration was primarily observed in patients with localized orbital involvement; among patients with extensive disease involving the orbital apex, cavernous sinus, or intracranial compartment, there was no significant difference in exenteration (4/15 vs 6/14, p=0.38), or mortality, and orbital congestion at final examination was significantly worse in treated eyes (p=0.047).123 Fischer et al similarly reported no difference in survival between patients treated with TRAMB, orbital exenteration, or no orbital intervention after adjustment for diabetes severity, causative organism, and transfer status.124 Importantly, the absence of survival difference between TRAMB and exenteration should not necessarily be interpreted as a lack of therapeutic benefit; rather, it suggests that globe-sparing treatment may avoid the morbidity and disfigurement of exenteration without adversely affecting survival in appropriately selected patients. Treatment-related adverse effects, although generally transient, have also been reported. In the multicenter series by Dallalzadeh et al, worsening chemosis occurred in 21.7% of eyes treated with TRAMB, and one eye required orbitotomy to drain an admixture of amphotericin B and debris attributed to drug precipitation after ten consecutive injections.123 Ashraf et al reported a lower complication rate of 4.3%, consisting primarily of orbital inflammation; one patient developed orbital compartment syndrome requiring emergent intervention.103 Collectively, these findings highlight the limitations of TRAMB in advanced disease and underscore the importance of appropriate patient selection and close clinical surveillance following each injection.

Local cutaneous injection of L-AmB is also emerging as a potential treatment for fungal involvement of the soft tissues, although current evidence is limited to case reports. Smith et al reported two cases of cutaneous mucormycosis of the eyelid treated with subcutaneous L-AmB (3.5 mg/mL), preventing extension of the disease into the orbit.129 Das et al documented three cases of invasive fungal infections where intralesional L-AmB (2.5 mg/mL) was used to manage cutaneous extension.130 All cases achieved complete resolution of the cutaneous fungal lesions.130 While promising, further research is needed to further define the role of L-AmB for soft tissue involvement.

Although the COVID-related surge in AIFROS substantially expanded the literature on TRAMB, larger and better powered interventional studies are still needed to further define its role. A 2023 multidisciplinary consensus statement assigned TRAMB an aggregate grade C level of evidence and recommended considering it for patients with limited orbital disease.2 Nevertheless, it must be acknowledged that this is a rare infection, and real-world experience underscores the importance of delivering antifungal therapy directly to the affected orbital tissue bed.

Orbital Treatment Algorithm

Orbital management in AIFROS should be guided by the overarching goal of achieving local disease control while avoiding unnecessary procedural morbidity in mild cases and preventing delayed escalation in advanced disease. Decision-making should be based on both clinical examination and orbital imaging. In general, the least invasive intervention considered appropriate for the stage of disease should be prioritized, with readiness to escalate if deterioration occurs.

A practical orbital management algorithm is anchored in early recognition, urgent imaging and endoscopic evaluation, prompt systemic antifungal therapy, and timely sinonasal debridement. Orbital intervention should be tailored to disease severity, visual function, orbital apex involvement, and radiologic evidence of devitalized tissue.

Building on the orbital treatment algorithm proposed by Kalin-Hajdu et al and further refined by Ashraf et al, orbital management is best approached as a staged escalation pathway in which MRI findings, particularly the presence, extent, and pattern of LoCE, are interpreted alongside the orbital exam (Figure 4).103,104 In this framework, mild disease is characterized by subtle enhancement adjacent to the affected sinus and may be observed when the examination is normal, whereas moderate disease includes moderate, diffuse, or apical enhancement, or milder enhancement accompanied by decreased vision or dysmotility, and is often managed with TRAMB. Focal LoCE within a small- to moderate-sized, surgically accessible extraconal or anterior compartment favors conservative debridement with adjunctive local antifungal delivery. In contrast, diffuse or apical LoCE, particularly when accompanied by progressive cranial neuropathy or vision loss, should raise concern for severe disease and prompt consideration of escalation, including exenteration in carefully selected cases. Given this approach, frequent monitoring is essential to assess for progression and to identify disease that is refractory to initial treatment. Monitoring should include serial ophthalmic examinations with assessment of visual acuity, pupillary function, ocular motility, proptosis, and anterior and posterior segment findings, in addition to repeat endoscopic sinonasal evaluation when indicated. Interval MRI may be useful for patients with equivocal clinical response, worsening clinical exam, or known orbital apex disease. Worsening vision, expanding areas of LoCE, progressive cranial neuropathy, or persistent necrotic tissue despite treatment should prompt reassessment of the therapeutic plan and consideration of escalation.

Prognosis

Reported mortality rates for AIFRS in the literature range from 16.7% to 68.4%.9,12,19,28,39,103 According to a 2025 meta-analysis, outcomes have improved in recent years; mortality rates fell from 41.9% (pre-2013) to 28.2% (post-2013).122 Advances in diagnostics and management may have contributed to this decline.

Various factors can affect survival in AIFRS, including patient demographics, etiology of immunosuppression, causative fungal organism, time until treatment, and management. Advanced age and intracranial extension consistently confer a poor prognosis.3,31,107,131 Diabetes mellitus has been associated with better survival outcomes compared to other etiologies of immunocompromise largely credited to the relative reversibility of hyperglycemia.19,45,97,98 Recovery of ANC to normal levels is a positive prognostic indicator for survival.99,100 However, in transplant recipients and those with hematologic malignancies, fungal infection-related immune reconstitution syndrome may paradoxically contribute to mortality.132–134

Orbital involvement occurs in about 40% to 65% of AIFRS cases.3–5 The literature remains mixed on whether orbital invasion in AIFRS is associated with worse prognosis.2,3,17,106,131,135 Notably, Ashraf and colleagues evaluated whether orbital involvement on initial MRI differed between survivors and non-survivors of AIFROS and found that 75% of survivors compared to 50% of non-survivors, had orbital invasion on initial MRI.103 One possible explanation is that earlier orbital involvement increases the likelihood of prompt recognition and treatment, rather than reflecting a true biologic survival advantage. This interpretation is further supported by the observation that orbital involvement on the initial MRI was protective, possibly because it facilitated earlier clinical recognition of the infection.50

Reported mortality for CAM ranges between 29% and 49% in the literature.136–140 A meta-analysis of 958 cases of CAM identified several independent risk factors for mortality, including, advanced age, obesity, diabetic ketoacidosis, a history of malignancy, hypertension, underlying renal or pulmonary diseases, Aspergillus coinfection, and tocilizumab use during COVID-19.31 In addition, some studies suggest that CAM is associated with greater ocular morbidity. For example, Adlakha et al found significantly higher rates of orbital involvement (56% vs 41%) and visual loss (28% vs 18%) in CAM cohorts compared with non-COVID groups.4

Key Controversies and Unresolved Questions

Several issues remain unresolved despite the expanding literature. These include early disease identification including how artificial intelligence may allow timelier radiographic identification of infection and how emerging molecular diagnostics can be incorporated into routine practice. In addition, opportunity lies in determining how LoCE on MRI should be integrated into orbital treatment algorithms, as well as which patients are most likely to benefit from globe-sparing interventions such as TRAMB or CDI. The role of orbital exenteration also remains incompletely defined. Retrospective studies have not shown a survival benefit, suggesting that exenteration is best viewed as a measure for local disease control in carefully selected patients when globe-sparing treatment is unlikely to succeed. Its effect on outcomes in patients with intracranial extension remains unclear. Future work should refine orbital staging systems, standardize treatment thresholds, and compare globe-sparing versus more aggressive strategies in prospective multicenter studies.

Limitations of the Evidence Base

The available literature on AIFRS and especially AIFROS remains limited by several factors. Much of the evidence is derived from retrospective single-center studies, heterogeneous case series, and post-COVID observational cohorts with variable inclusion criteria and treatment thresholds. Definitions of orbital involvement, radiologic severity, and indications for exenteration are not standardized across studies, which complicates direct comparison of outcomes. Referral bias toward severe disease and evolving diagnostic and treatment practices over time further limit the strength of broad management conclusions.

Conclusion

AIFRS is a rapidly progressive and potentially fatal infection that requires early recognition, prompt imaging, tissue diagnosis, systemic antifungal therapy, surgical debridement, and reversal of underlying immunosuppression whenever possible. Although the core principles of management are well established, important controversies remain in AIFROS. MRI is increasingly valuable for early detection, assessment of extrasinus spread, and prognostication, while frozen section and fungal PCR may shorten diagnostic delay. Management of AIFROS remains less standardized, with growing use of globe-sparing strategies such as TRAMB or CDI in selected patients. Orbital exenteration may still be considered for local disease control in carefully selected cases, but current retrospective evidence does not support a survival benefit. Future studies should prioritize multicenter prospective data, standardized orbital staging, and comparative evaluation of globe-sparing vs more aggressive strategies.
