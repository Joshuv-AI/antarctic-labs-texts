Comparative effects of Capsicum annuum selenium nanoparticles and sodium selenite on growth, antioxidant activity, bioaccumulation, and DNA damage in quails
Authors: Muhammad Usama, Shabana Naz, Sania Satti, Anqash Ayyub, Hira Khalid Khan, Ibrahim A. Alhidary, İsmail Ülger, Ala Abudabos, Luciana Rossi, Sohail Ahmad, Rifat Ullah Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Selenium nanoparticles (Se-NPs), a nanoscale form of selenium, and sodium selenite (SS), an inorganic selenium source, were included in the diet of quails to evaluate their effects on growth performance, selenium accumulation, antioxidant status, and DNA damage. A total of 420 quails (14-day-old) were distributed into 5 groups. Each group consisted of 84 birds, arranged into 3 replicates (28 quails per replicate). The first group served as the control and received a basal diet, while the second and third groups were supplemented with Se-NPs at 0.2 and 0.4 mg/kg, respectively. The fourth and fifth groups received SS at 0.2 and 0.4 mg/kg, respectively. The feeding trial was conducted for 65 days. The results demonstrated that quails supplemented with Se-NPs (0.4 mg/kg) exhibited the highest body weight gain and performance index, as well as the lowest feed conversion ratio. Selenium bioaccumulation increased significantly in all examined organs and was greatest in the liver. Analytical selenium recovery from tissue samples ranged from 90.0 to 95.0%, supporting the reliability of tissue selenium quantification. Antioxidant enzyme activities, including catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx), were significantly enhanced in all selenium-supplemented groups, with the highest activities observed in birds receiving Se-NPs (0.4 mg/kg). Comet assay analysis revealed significant differences in DNA damage parameters among the treatment groups, with the highest level of DNA strand breaks observed in quails supplemented with SS (0.4 mg/kg). Correlation analysis and heatmap visualization demonstrated a strong positive association between selenium bioaccumulation and antioxidant enzyme activities, whereas DNA damage parameters showed predominantly negative associations with selenium accumulation. The findings indicate that Se-NPs enhance growth performance, selenium retention, and antioxidant defense and are associated with lower DNA strand breaks than SS. Therefore, Se-NPs may serve as an effective and promising selenium source for poultry nutrition, exhibiting a favorable biological response compared with sodium selenite.

Introduction

The poultry industry is one of the fastest-growing agricultural sectors and plays a vital role in providing protein-rich meat and eggs to the rapidly increasing human population (Magnoli et al., 2024; Berberoğlu et al., 2025). However, modern poultry production faces several challenges, including rising feed costs, environmental stress, infectious diseases, and antimicrobial resistance, all of which negatively affect bird health, immunity, growth performance, and productivity (Khallaf et al., 2025; Oubouyahia et al., 2025). Poultry is frequently exposed to stressors such as high temperature, overcrowding, poor housing conditions, and pathogenic infections, which disturb metabolic balance and reduce production efficiency. Therefore, improving poultry health and performance through nutritional interventions has become an important research focus (Dablool et al., 2024; Chhetri et al., 2025).

Among various nutritional strategies, trace mineral supplementation has gained considerable attention due to its essential roles in metabolism, immunity, antioxidant defense, and physiological regulation. Selenium (Se) is one of the most important trace elements required for normal growth and health in poultry (Al-Khalaifah et al., 2025a). It is incorporated into approximately 25 selenoproteins involved in several biological processes, including antioxidant defense, thyroid hormone metabolism, DNA synthesis, immune regulation, and maintenance of cellular redox balance (Khan et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025).

Traditionally, selenium is supplemented in poultry diets in inorganic forms such as sodium selenite and sodium selenate, or in organic forms (Islam et al., 2024). However, conventional selenium sources often show limited bioavailability and may induce toxicity at higher concentrations. In particular, inorganic selenium such as sodium selenite can interact with cellular thiols to form reactive selenium intermediates, leading to oxidative imbalance and cellular damage when excessively administered (Khalid et al., 2025).

In recent years, nanotechnology has emerged as a promising approach in animal nutrition due to its potential to enhance nutrient utilization and biological efficiency (Ahsan et al., 2024; Albohiri et al., 2025). Nanoparticles possess unique physicochemical characteristics, including small particle size, large surface area, high reactivity, and improved absorption capacity. These properties have increased interest in nano-minerals as dietary supplements to improve growth performance, immunity, antioxidant status, and nutrient bioavailability in poultry (Almuzaini, 2024).

Among nano-minerals, selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs) have attracted considerable attention due to their enhanced bioavailability, biological activity, and potentially lower toxicity compared with conventional selenium sources (Satti et al., 2024). Their nanoscale properties facilitate efficient selenium utilization and have been associated with improvements in growth performance, feed efficiency, antioxidant status, and immune responses in poultry (Satti et al., 2025). Selenium also contributes to genomic stability by reducing oxidative stress-induced DNA damage. Excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) can induce DNA strand breaks, chromosomal instability, and apoptosis, whereas selenium-dependent antioxidant systems help minimize oxidative DNA damage and maintain cellular integrity (Lee et al., 2025).

Although several studies have investigated the effects of selenium nanoparticles on poultry growth performance and antioxidant status, limited information is available regarding their combined effects on selenium bioaccumulation, antioxidant defense, and DNA damage across different organs in Japanese quails. The present study hypothesized that dietary supplementation with selenium nanoparticles would enhance selenium utilization, improve growth performance and antioxidant defense, and reduce DNA damage compared with sodium selenite in Japanese quails. Therefore, the present study was conducted to evaluate the effects of graded levels of selenium nanoparticles and sodium selenite on selenium bioaccumulation, antioxidant enzyme activities, and DNA damage, as assessed by the comet assay, in Japanese quails. Additionally, this study aimed to compare the efficacy of nano-selenium and inorganic selenium in improving antioxidant status and reducing DNA strand breaks in birds.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

All chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) and were of analytical grade purity.

Synthesis and characterization of selenium nanoparticles

Selenium nanoparticles were prepared as described previously by Satti et al. (2024), Khan et al. (2025). Briefly, about 15 kg of Capsicum annuum was thoroughly washed with double-distilled water to remove all surface impurities. The material was then cut into small pieces and dried at a controlled temperature of 60°C until a constant weight was achieved, ensuring complete moisture removal without degradation of bioactive compounds. The dried sample was ground into a fine powder. For extract preparation, 500 g of the powdered sample was soaked in 1000 mL of ethanol and kept at room temperature for seven days to facilitate efficient extraction of phytochemicals. The mixture was then filtered using Whatman filter paper No. 41. The filtrate was concentrated using a rotary evaporator for 15 min and subsequently subjected to vacuum evaporation until a constant weight was obtained. For Se-NPs synthesis, 50 mL of C. annuum extract was mixed with 0.263 g of selenious acid (H₂SeO₃) under controlled laboratory conditions and proper safety precautions. The reaction mixture was maintained for 15 h at pH 5.4. Formation of selenium nanoparticles was confirmed by the appearance of a characteristic red coloration, indicating successful reduction of selenium ions. The nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 4 min. The obtained precipitate was washed several times with acetone, absolute ethanol, and double-distilled water to remove residual impurities. Finally, the product was dried under vacuum at ambient temperature until a constant weight was achieved.

Characterization of the synthesized Se-NPs was carried out using UV–Visible spectrophotometry in the range of 200–800 nm to confirm optical properties. Functional groups and chemical interactions were analyzed using Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy over 400–4000 cm⁻¹ with a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹. The crystalline structure and phase composition were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis with a Bruker D8 Advance system, and scanning was performed at a rate of 10°/min. The zeta potential of the synthesized nanoparticles was −28 mV, indicating good colloidal stability and a low tendency for particle aggregation.

Quantification of selenium concentration in Se-NPs

The selenium content of the synthesized Se-NPs was determined using inductively coupled plasma–optical emission spectrometry (ICP–OES; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Approximately 10 mg of dried Se-NPs was digested with 5 mL of concentrated nitric acid (HNO₃) and 2 mL of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) at 120°C until a clear solution was obtained. After digestion, the samples were diluted to a final volume of 25 mL with deionized water and analyzed using selenium calibration standards ranging from 0.1 to 10 mg/L. The measured selenium concentration was used to verify the consistency and reproducibility of the synthesized nanoparticle batches.

Experimental birds and design

All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals approved by Government College University Faisalabad (GCUF). A total of 420 healthy, unsexed Japanese quail chicks, aged 14 days, were used in this study. The birds were housed in steel cages (75 × 50 × 65 cm) under standard management conditions and acclimatized for 10 days before the commencement of the experiment. Following the acclimatization period, birds (24 days of age) were individually weighed and randomly allocated into five experimental groups to ensure a uniform distribution of initial body weight among treatments. Each treatment group comprised three replicate cages, with 28 birds per cage (84 birds per treatment group), providing a consistent cage-based experimental structure across all treatments. Each replicate (cage) was considered the experimental unit. The first group served as the control and received the basal diet without selenium supplementation. The second and third groups were supplemented with selenium nanoparticles (Se-NPs) at doses of 0.2 and 0.4 mg/kg body weight, respectively, whereas the fourth and fifth groups received inorganic selenium in the form of sodium selenite (SS) at equivalent doses of 0.2 and 0.4 mg/kg body weight, respectively. The respective treatments were administered daily by oral gavage to ensure accurate dosing according to body weight (mg/kg). The selenium concentrations of the experimental diets were calculated based on the supplemented levels of Se-NPs and SS (Table 1). The feeding trial was conducted for 65 days. Throughout the experimental period, birds were maintained under controlled environmental conditions, with a temperature range of 20–25°C and a relative humidity of approximately 70%. A lighting schedule of 16 h light and 8 h darkness (16L:8D) was maintained throughout the experimental period using artificial lighting. Feed and water were provided ad libitum. The basal diet was formulated according to the nutrient requirements recommended by National Research Council (1994), and its ingredient composition and calculated nutrient profile are presented in Table 2. Growth performance evaluation

Feed intake and body weight were recorded weekly for all Japanese quails in both control and treatment groups throughout the experimental period, starting from day one and continuing on a weekly basis. Feed intake was measured on a replicate cage basis by calculating the difference between the amount of feed offered and the residual feed remaining at the end of each week. Body weight was recorded individually, and the mean body weight gain for each replicate cage was calculated for growth performance evaluation. The cumulative feed intake and cumulative body weight gain per bird were used to determine feed conversion ratio (FCR) and performance index (PI) (Chand et al., 2020; Mayasari et al., 2025).

Feed intake (g): Feed intake was calculated weekly using the following formula:Feedintake=Feedoffered(g)−Feedremaining(g)

Body weight gain (g): Body weight gain was recorded on a weekly basis using the difference between initial and subsequent body weights.

Feed conversion ratio (FCR): FCR was calculated using the following formula:FCR=Feedintake(g)/Bodyweightgain(g)

Performance index (PI): The overall growth performance was assessed using the performance index, calculated as:PI=(Bodyweightgain/FCR)×100

Selenium bioaccumulation in organs

At the end of the experimental period, five birds per replicate were humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation following ethical guidelines. Cervical dislocation was selected because it provides rapid and humane euthanasia while avoiding chemical contamination that could interfere with subsequent selenium quantification and biochemical analyses. Following euthanasia, the liver, kidney, heart, and breast muscle were carefully excised, thoroughly rinsed with deionized water to remove residual blood and surface contaminants, cut into small pieces, and stored on ice until further processing.

For sample digestion, approximately 0.5 g of dried tissue was accurately weighed and transferred into Teflon digestion tubes. A freshly prepared mixture of nitric acid (HNO₃) and perchloric acid (HClO₄) in a 1:1 ratio (6 mL total volume) was added to each sample. The tubes were kept under a fume hood and gently agitated for 12–16 h to allow initial digestion. After this, samples were heated in a water bath at 70°C for 30 min, followed by further digestion on a hot plate until a clear solution was obtained. The digests were then cooled to room temperature, filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper, and quantitatively transferred into volumetric flasks. The final volume was adjusted using deionized distilled water. Selenium concentrations were determined using an atomic absorption spectrophotometer (Aurora AI 1200) at a wavelength of 196.0 nm under standard analytical conditions, with a detection limit of 1 mg/L. The selenium concentration in tissues was calculated using the following formula:Seleniumconcentration=(Instrumentreading×Dilutionfactor)/Sampleweight

In this study, the dilution factor was 25 mL per gram of dry tissue sample.

Antioxidant enzyme analysis in organs

For antioxidant enzyme analysis, five birds per replicate (15 birds per treatment group) were randomly selected and humanely euthanized according to ethical guidelines. Liver, kidney, breast muscle and heart tissues were collected immediately after dissection and rinsed with ice-cold physiological saline. The tissues were homogenized in chilled phosphate buffer and centrifuged. The clear supernatant was collected for biochemical analysis. Superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activities were measured using standard commercial assay kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was recorded using a UV–Visible spectrophotometer (Aurora Al 1200).

Analytical recovery of selenium

The accuracy of the selenium determination was evaluated using a standard-addition recovery procedure. Representative liver, kidney, heart, and breast muscle samples were analyzed before and after selenium spiking. The selenium concentration measured in each unspiked sample was used as the baseline concentration. Separate portions of each tissue sample were spiked with known amounts of selenium standard at concentrations of 0.20 and 0.40 µg/g before digestion. Both spiked and unspiked samples were subjected to the same digestion, dilution, and AAS procedures described above. The expected selenium concentration was calculated by adding the selenium concentration of the unspiked sample to the known amount of selenium added. The measured selenium concentration was obtained from the AAS analysis of the spiked samples. Selenium recovery was calculated as follows:Recovery(%)=Cspiked−Cunspiked×100Caddedwhere Cspiked is the selenium concentration measured in the spiked sample, Cunspiked is the selenium concentration measured in the corresponding unspiked sample, and Cadded is the known concentration of selenium added to the sample.

DNA damage examination

DNA damage was assessed using the comet assay (single-cell gel electrophoresis) to evaluate DNA strand breaks following selenium supplementation. The assay was performed according to the protocol described by Singh et al. (1988) (Fig. 1). Peripheral blood samples were collected from 9 randomly selected birds per treatment group at the end of the experiment, with 3 birds per replicate. In total, 45 blood samples across all experimental groups were obtained for analysis. Fresh blood samples were processed immediately to maintain cell integrity and to prevent any artificial DNA damage. Blood cells were embedded in low-melting-point agarose on clean microscope slides. The cells were then lysed to remove cellular membranes and nuclear proteins, leaving nucleoids containing supercoiled DNA. The slides were subjected to alkaline electrophoresis, allowing fragmented DNA to migrate and form comet-like structures. DNA damage was quantified using Casp_1.2.3b1 software by analyzing comet parameters, including head length (L head), tail length (L tail), comet length (L comet), percentage of DNA in head, percentage of DNA in tail, tail moment (TM), and olive tail moment (OTM). These parameters were used as indicators of DNA strand breaks and genomic instability.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (Version 25). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Prior to analysis, all datasets were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For growth performance parameters (feed intake, body weight gain, feed conversion ratio, and performance index), the replicate cage was considered the experimental unit because feed intake was measured at the cage level and feed conversion ratio was calculated accordingly. The mean value of each replicate cage (n = 3 per treatment) was used for statistical analysis. Growth performance, selenium bioaccumulation, and comet assay parameters were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test for multiple comparisons. Analytical recovery of selenium was assessed using standard-addition tests at spike levels of 0.20 and 0.40 µg/g, and recovery percentages were calculated accordingly. Antioxidant enzyme activities (CAT, SOD, and GPx) were analyzed using a linear mixed model (LMM) to account for repeated measurements obtained from multiple organs of the same bird. The model included treatment and organ as fixed effects, bird ID as a random effect, and the treatment × organ interaction. The model was expressed as:Yijk=μ+Ti+Oj+(T×O)ij+Bk+εijkwhere Yijk​ represents the observed antioxidant enzyme activity, μ is the overall mean, Ti is the fixed effect of treatment, Oj is the fixed effect of organ, (T × O)ij​ is the interaction between treatment and organ, Bk is the random effect of bird, and εijk ​ is the residual error. Model assumptions were evaluated by assessing the normality of residuals and examining residual diagnostic plots. No substantial deviations from model assumptions were observed. Pearson's correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the associations among selenium bioaccumulation, antioxidant enzyme activities, and DNA damage parameters. Correlation coefficients were calculated using the mean values of the five experimental groups (n = 5) and visualized using a heatmap. P-values from multiple correlation tests were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, and an adjusted P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Effects of Se-NPs and SS supplementation on antioxidant enzyme activities in Japanese quails

Table 6 presents the effects of different doses of Se-NPs and SS on antioxidant enzyme activities (CAT, SOD, and GPx) in different organs of Japanese Quails. Overall, a significant (P < 0.001) increase in antioxidant enzyme activities was observed across all treated groups compared to the control, indicating a clear dose-dependent response to selenium supplementation. The group receiving the highest dose of Se-NPs (0.4 mg/kg) showed the greatest antioxidant enzyme activities in all parameters, including CAT, SOD, and GPx, particularly in liver tissue (P < 0.001). Discussion

The present study demonstrated that dietary supplementation with selenium nanoparticles (Se-NPs) improved growth performance and feed utilization efficiency in Japanese quails. The superior response observed with Se-NPs compared with sodium selenite (SS) suggests that the nano-form of selenium may provide enhanced biological availability and utilization. Unlike inorganic selenium sources, Se-NPs possess unique physicochemical properties, including small particle size, large surface area, and improved cellular interactions, which may facilitate selenium absorption and efficient incorporation into biological pathways involved in growth and metabolism (Zambonino et al., 2023). The beneficial effects of Se-NPs supplementation may be associated with improved nutrient utilization and metabolic efficiency. Selenium is an essential component of selenoproteins, which play critical roles in antioxidant defense, cellular protection, and regulation of redox homeostasis (Hossain, 2025). Selenium-dependent enzymes, particularly glutathione peroxidase (GPx), reduce reactive oxygen species and protect cellular components from oxidative damage, thereby supporting normal metabolic activity and growth (Ferroudj et al., 2026). In addition, selenium contributes to thyroid hormone metabolism by regulating the conversion of thyroxine (T4) into the biologically active triiodothyronine (T3), which may influence energy metabolism, protein synthesis, and tissue development (Piray et al., 2025). These physiological functions may collectively contribute to improved growth responses by maintaining cellular homeostasis and enhancing metabolic efficiency. The improved feed utilization associated with Se-NPs may also be linked to enhanced intestinal nutrient absorption. Due to their nanoscale properties, Se-NPs may interact more efficiently with intestinal epithelial surfaces and facilitate selenium uptake through cellular transport pathways (Hosseintabar-Ghasemabad et al., 2025). Previous studies have suggested that Se-NPs may improve intestinal integrity, digestive enzyme activity, and nutrient absorption; however, these mechanisms were not directly evaluated in the present study and should therefore be considered potential explanations (Yuan et al., 2024). Similarly, the possible influence of Se-NPs on gut microbial composition may contribute to improved nutrient availability and energy utilization, although gut microbiota was not investigated in this study. The involvement of growth-regulatory pathways may also contribute to the beneficial effects of Se-NPs. Previous studies have suggested that selenium supplementation may modulate insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1)-associated pathways, which are involved in muscle development and cellular proliferation (Zahran et al., 2025). The greater effectiveness of Se-NPs compared with SS may be attributed to differences in selenium bioavailability, absorption, and cellular utilization. Inorganic selenium sources are rapidly absorbed and metabolized, which may increase the possibility of excessive selenium accumulation under higher supplementation conditions (Lv et al., 2021). In contrast, Se-NPs may provide a more controlled selenium release, allowing efficient utilization while reducing the risk of selenium overload. Previous studies have reported that nano-selenium exhibits greater bioavailability and biological activity than conventional selenium sources (Xu et al., 2025), which may explain the improved physiological responses observed following Se-NPs supplementation. Although selenium is an essential trace element required for normal physiological functions, it has a relatively narrow margin between beneficial and toxic effects. Insufficient selenium intake may impair antioxidant defense, whereas excessive selenium exposure can induce oxidative stress, reduced productivity, tissue damage, and other toxic effects (Bai et al., 2024). Selenium toxicity depends on several factors, including dose, chemical form, bioavailability, and duration of exposure (Bodnar et al., 2012). The absence of adverse effects and the beneficial responses observed in the present study suggest that the applied Se-NPs concentrations were within an effective and tolerable range for Japanese quails. Further investigations involving long-term supplementation, histopathological evaluation, and molecular biomarkers are required to define the safe upper limits of Se-NPs supplementation (Malyugina et al., 2021). From a practical perspective, the improvements observed in body weight gain and feed conversion ratio may also be biologically and economically meaningful for commercial quail production. The increased body weight gain and improved feed conversion ratio achieved with Se-NPs supplementation indicate more efficient feed utilization, which may reduce feed costs and enhance production efficiency (Oke et al., 2025). As feed constitutes a major proportion of poultry production costs, even modest improvements in feed efficiency may offer economic advantages under commercial production conditions (Fayyaz et al., 2025). Similar improvements in the growth performance of Japanese quails supplemented with Se-NPs have also been reported by Satti et al. (2024) and Khan et al. (2025).

The present study demonstrated that selenium bioaccumulation in Japanese quails was influenced by both the chemical form and supplementation level of selenium. The greater tissue selenium retention observed following Se-NPs supplementation compared with sodium selenite (SS) suggests enhanced bioavailability and metabolic utilization of nano-selenium. This improved availability may be attributed to the unique physicochemical characteristics of Se-NPs, including their small particle size, large surface area, and increased surface reactivity, which may facilitate intestinal absorption and cellular uptake (Ferroudj et al., 2026; Piray et al., 2025). Among the examined organs, the liver exhibited the highest selenium accumulation, which may be explained by its central role in selenium metabolism, storage, and homeostatic regulation. The liver is considered the primary organ responsible for selenium transformation into biologically active metabolites and incorporation into selenoproteins, particularly glutathione peroxidase, which plays a critical role in maintaining cellular antioxidant defense (Satti et al., 2025). Following intestinal absorption, selenium is transported to the liver, where it undergoes metabolic processing before being distributed to peripheral tissues. The relatively high selenium deposition in the kidney may be associated with its involvement in selenium excretion and regulation of systemic selenium balance (Satti et al., 2025). In contrast, the comparatively lower selenium accumulation in breast muscle may reflect the lower metabolic activity of muscle tissue in selenium turnover compared with organs actively involved in selenium metabolism. The differences in selenium deposition between Se-NPs and SS groups may be attributed to variations in absorption efficiency, metabolic processing, and tissue distribution. Nano-selenium particles may enhance intestinal selenium uptake through improved interaction with epithelial surfaces and cellular transport mechanisms, resulting in greater systemic availability and tissue incorporation (Piray et al., 2025). In contrast, inorganic selenium sources such as sodium selenite undergo rapid absorption and metabolic conversion, which may increase urinary excretion and reduce tissue retention efficiency (Al-Khalaifah et al., 2025b; Ferroudj et al., 2026). Similar findings have been reported in poultry, where Se-NPs supplementation resulted in greater selenium deposition in tissues compared with inorganic selenium sources, indicating improved bioavailability and biological utilization (Khan et al., 2025; Piray et al., 2025). The tissue retention efficiency of Se-NPs can also be compared with organic selenium sources, such as selenomethionine and selenium yeast, which are recognized for their high bioavailability and efficient incorporation into body proteins through the methionine pathway. Organic selenium compounds are generally retained more effectively because they can be incorporated into protein pools and mobilized during periods of increased selenium demand (Surai and Cardoso, 2024). However, Se-NPs may provide an alternative approach by combining improved bioavailability with controlled selenium release, potentially reducing the rapid metabolic conversion associated with inorganic selenium sources (Khalid et al., 2025). Further comparative studies involving organic selenium compounds are required to determine the relative retention efficiency, metabolic fate, and long-term biological availability of different selenium formulations. Selenium has a narrow safety margin between beneficial and toxic levels, and excessive exposure may disrupt redox homeostasis, enhance reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and induce oxidative damage to cellular components, including lipids, proteins, and DNA (Naz et al., 2024). Furthermore, excessive selenium or Se-NP exposure may interfere with normal selenoprotein regulation, potentially weakening antioxidant defense mechanisms and increasing cellular stress (Naz et al., 2024; Khalid et al., 2025). The potential persistence and accumulation of nanomaterials in biological systems further emphasize the importance of evaluating long-term exposure effects and tissue responses (Al-Khalaifah et al., 2025c).

The analytical recovery assessment provides additional methodological support for interpreting the tissue selenium bioaccumulation observed in the present study. The recovery range of 90.0–95.0% across the examined tissues indicates that the analytical procedure was capable of consistently recovering selenium from complex biological matrices. This is important because selenium may be present in tissues in association with proteins and other organic constituents, and incomplete digestion or matrix interference can lead to an underestimation of its actual concentration (Kleckner et al., 2017). The relatively consistent recovery across liver, kidney, heart, and breast muscle therefore suggests that the tissue-dependent differences in selenium concentrations were unlikely to be driven primarily by differences in analytical recovery. Instead, these differences may reflect the distinct physiological roles of these organs in selenium metabolism and distribution (Burk and Hill, 2015). The greater deposition observed following Se-NP supplementation may be related to the enhanced biological availability of nanoscale selenium, potentially facilitating its interaction with intestinal epithelial cells and subsequent absorption (Karunakar et al., 2025). Following uptake, selenium is metabolized and incorporated into selenoproteins or redistributed to tissues according to their metabolic requirements. The liver, as a major site of selenium metabolism and selenoprotein synthesis, may consequently retain greater amounts of absorbed selenium (Goraya, et al., 2025), whereas the kidney contributes to selenium homeostasis and excretion (Kurokawa and Berry, 2013). Thus, the satisfactory recovery obtained across tissue matrices strengthens confidence that the observed treatment-related differences primarily reflect biological variation in selenium absorption and tissue utilization rather than analytical artifacts (Sauer et al., 2025).

The present study demonstrated that selenium supplementation modulated the antioxidant defense system of Japanese quails, as indicated by enhanced activities of key antioxidant enzymes, including catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx). The antioxidant response was influenced by selenium source, supplementation level, and tissue type, suggesting that selenium metabolism and oxidative regulation are highly dependent on organ-specific physiological functions (Surai and Kochish, 2020). The greater antioxidant response observed following Se-NPs supplementation compared with sodium selenite indicates that nano-selenium may provide improved selenium availability for biological processes involved in redox regulation. The enhanced antioxidant capacity associated with selenium supplementation may be attributed to its essential role in selenoprotein biosynthesis (Zoidis et al., 2018). Selenium acts as a structural component of several antioxidant enzymes, particularly GPx, where it is incorporated as selenocysteine at the catalytic site and contributes to the removal of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Marković et al., 2018). Increased selenium availability may therefore promote selenoprotein synthesis and strengthen endogenous antioxidant defense mechanisms. In addition, CAT, SOD, and GPx function synergistically to neutralize ROS and protect cellular components, including lipids, proteins, and DNA, from oxidative damage. Although these molecular pathways were not directly investigated in the present study, they provide plausible explanations for the improved antioxidant enzyme activities observed following selenium supplementation (Debata et al., 2023; Hamid et al., 2026). The variation in antioxidant enzyme activities among organs may reflect differences in metabolic activity, oxygen consumption, and oxidative stress exposure. The higher antioxidant activity observed in the liver may be associated with its central role in detoxification, xenobiotic metabolism, and regulation of oxidative balance, as the liver continuously encounters metabolic challenges and ROS generation (Ferroudj et al., 2026). Similarly, the kidney requires efficient antioxidant protection due to its involvement in filtration, metabolism, and elimination of metabolic waste products. In contrast, the relatively lower antioxidant response in muscle tissue may be related to its comparatively lower metabolic activity and reduced exposure to oxidative challenges. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) in initiating oxidative stress and metabolic disturbances. Impaired mitochondrial respiratory function can increase mtROS production, leading to redox imbalance, NADPH depletion, and disruption of cellular metabolism (Liu et al., 2025). Although mitochondrial function and ROS production were not measured in the present study, improved antioxidant enzyme activity following selenium supplementation may represent an adaptive response that helps maintain cellular redox homeostasis. The superior antioxidant response associated with Se-NPs may also be explained by their gradual selenium release and improved cellular availability. Previous studies have suggested that Se-NPs can be metabolized into biologically active selenium forms that participate in selenoprotein synthesis, providing a sustained selenium supply for antioxidant defense mechanisms (Puri et al., 2024). In contrast, sodium selenite undergoes rapid reduction and metabolic conversion, which may limit its efficient incorporation into selenoproteins. Furthermore, inorganic selenium may interact with dietary components, forming less bioavailable complexes that reduce selenium utilization (Kieliszek, 2019). According to previous studies, selenium may exert its antioxidant effects through the modulation of the redox-sensitive transcription factor Nrf2 (nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2) (Bityutsky et al., 2020; Rusetskaya et al., 2019) . Activation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway has been reported to enhance the expression of antioxidant genes, including those encoding SOD, CAT, and GPx, thereby strengthening the cellular defense against oxidative stress. In addition to Nrf2 signaling, recent evidence indicates that selenium-dependent proteins, particularly selenoprotein S (SelS), may contribute to maintaining redox homeostasis by regulating macrophage polarization and protecting epithelial cells from oxidative stress-induced injury (Yao et al., 2024). Collectively, these findings provide mechanistic evidence supporting the antioxidant properties of selenium (Surai and Kochish, 2020). Increased SOD and catalase activity in the erythrocytes of broiler birds has been reported to indicate improved tolerance to oxidative stress, as these enzymes are important scavengers of reactive oxygen metabolites (Khan et al., 2025). Similarly, dietary selenium supplementation has been shown to increase catalase and SOD activities in the erythrocytes and tissues of chickens, possibly by reducing oxidative stress and enhancing the scavenging of superoxide radicals (Debata et al., 2023).

The comet assay findings suggest that the genotoxic response to selenium supplementation depends strongly on the chemical form and biological availability of selenium. The greater DNA damage associated with sodium selenite (SS) exposure compared with Se-NPs may be related to differences in selenium metabolism and intracellular processing. Inorganic selenium sources, particularly sodium selenite, are rapidly absorbed and can undergo redox cycling, generating reactive selenium intermediates and reactive oxygen species (ROS) under excessive exposure conditions (An et al., 2023). Elevated ROS production may overwhelm cellular antioxidant defenses, resulting in oxidative damage to nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids, ultimately compromising genomic stability (Khalid et al., 2025; Al-Khalaifah et al., 2025c). Oxidative stress is considered one of the major mechanisms underlying selenium-induced genotoxicity when selenium exposure exceeds physiological requirements. Excessive ROS generation can promote DNA strand breaks through direct oxidative modification of DNA bases and indirect mechanisms involving mitochondrial dysfunction and lipid peroxidation (Naz et al., 2024). Therefore, the genotoxic effects of selenium are likely associated not only with selenium concentration but also with its chemical form, metabolic fate, and the ability of biological systems to maintain redox balance. In contrast, the comparatively lower DNA damage associated with Se-NPs supplementation may be linked to their controlled selenium release and improved biological utilization. Due to their nanoscale properties, Se-NPs may provide a more gradual supply of selenium for incorporation into selenoproteins, thereby supporting antioxidant defense mechanisms while minimizing excessive selenium accumulation in reactive forms (Hosnedlova et al., 2018). Previous studies have reported that Se-NPs can enhance antioxidant enzyme activity, including superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx), which contribute to ROS neutralization and protection against oxidative stress (Surai and Kochish, 2020). Furthermore, improved selenium absorption and utilization associated with Se-NPs may support cellular protection and physiological functions in poultry (Hamid et al., 2026). However, the antioxidant and protective effects of selenium are dose-dependent, and excessive selenium exposure may shift its biological role from antioxidant protection toward pro-oxidant activity. Selenium has a narrow safety margin, and concentrations exceeding the physiological requirement may impair antioxidant systems, induce oxidative stress, and increase the risk of cellular damage (Barchielli et al., 2022).

Conclusions

The study revealed that Se-NPs supplementation significantly improved growth performance, selenium bioaccumulation, and antioxidant enzyme activities in quails, particularly in the liver, and resulted in lower DNA damage compared to SS. In contrast, SS, especially at higher doses, exhibited greater DNA strand breaks. Therefore, Se-NPs demonstrated superior biological efficacy under the present experimental conditions and may serve as an effective alternative to SS for enhancing selenium status, antioxidant defense, and overall physiological performance in poultry.
