Synergized Effects of Amino Acids and NaCl to Enhance Silicate Mineral Dissolution in Aqueous Environments for Efficient Atmospheric CO2 Removal
Authors: Jiajie Wang, Sena Kikuchi, Noriyoshi Tsuchiya, Yoshinori Sato, Mei-Fang Chien, Noriaki Watanabe
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Enhanced weathering of silicate minerals is a promising approach for reducing atmospheric CO2 levels by increasing the aquatic pH and facilitating CO2 dissolution. However, the slow and unsustainable dissolution of silicate minerals in natural environments remains a challenge. This study proposed a new CO2 capture system that uses the combined effect of amino acids and NaCl to promote mineral dissolution, and its characteristics were investigated experimentally. The results showed that amino acids are promising for enhancing the near-congruent dissolution of silicate minerals, specifically at weakly alkaline pHs (i.e., 8), implying the long-term effectiveness of the system. Comprehensive findings revealed a 13-fold increase in the level of Ca extraction from wollastonite (CaSiO3) in the presence of 0.1 mol/L glutamic acid (Glu) over 72 h at 35 °C and a 22-fold increase in the level of CO2 capture efficiency. However, Fe-bearing minerals, such as olivine ((Mg,Fe)2SiO4), are unsuitable for application, because the enhanced Fe extraction results in the generation of Fe hydroxide, which lowers pH and consequently reduces CO2 capture efficiency. Moreover, NaCl facilitates the release of the Ca–Glu complex from mineral surfaces into the solution, synergizing amino acids to promote mineral dissolution. A semiclosed application system is proposed, with future studies needed to assess ecological impacts and ensure long-term sustainability.

Introduction

The chemical weathering of minerals is one of the most important processes regulating atmospheric CO2 levels.1,2 Mineral dissolution associated with enhanced weathering increases the alkalinity of aquatic systems, thereby enhancing the absorption of atmospheric CO2, which has significant implications for mitigating global warming. Consumption of atmospheric/soil CO2 by weathering is estimated at 258–288 Mt C a–1. Notably, the weathering of silicate minerals accounts for approximately 60% of carbon consumption.2,3

A simple approach to apply enhanced weathering for CO2 reduction involves spreading grains of suitable minerals, such as olivine ((Mg,Fe)2SiO4), over the ocean for dissolution, as suggested by the VESTA project.4 The associated reactions are shown in eqs 1 and 2, using forsterite (Mg2SiO4) as an example.12

However, the effectiveness of CO2 removal is primarily constrained by the sluggish mineral dissolution kinetics at ambient temperatures and pressures.5,6 Through calculations, Hangx and Spiers (2009)7 concluded that the coastal spreading of olivine is not a viable method of CO2 reduction on the required scale when olivine dissolution kinetics are considered. Furthermore, the dissolution of silicate minerals is typically incongruent, with elements being selectively leached out and the remaining elements gradually forming a passive layer on the mineral surface such as silica-rich layers. This phenomenon is undesirable for long-term applications of enhanced mineral dissolution because it can seriously inhibit further dissolution process.8 Consequently, atmospheric CO2 capture through mineral dissolution cannot be sustained over long periods of time.5 Therefore, advanced technologies for enhancing congruent mineral dissolution are urgently required.

Existing approaches to enhance mineral dissolution include using acidic environments,9 hydrothermal conditions,10 and grinding of minerals into fine powders to amplify the mineral specific surface area for the reaction. Nonetheless, these approaches are marked by their energy-intensive characteristics and environmental concerns, including wastewater generation, increasing carbon, and environmental footprint, making them unsuitable for implementation in natural environments.11 In recent decades, interest in the potential of chelating agents to promote mineral dissolution has been growing.12−15 Chelating agents can bond with metal ions in solution across a wide pH range, reducing the concentration of free metal ions and thereby enhancing the leaching of additional metal ions. Additionally, direct binding of metal ions on the mineral surface is also possible.16,17 The dissolution of minerals in the presence of chelating agents may become more congruent,18 enhancing the extraction of both metal ions and Si. Further, this more congruent dissolution potentially inhibits the formation of passive reaction layers, enabling the minerals to dissolve over extended periods. Chelating agents have been applied in diverse fields, including oil field stimulation, geothermal field stimulation, and the dissolution of industrial byproducts for CO2 mineralization.19−23

Many chelating agents are synthetic derivatives of amino acids,24 and amino acids are also attractive candidates for use as potential chelating agents to enhance mineral weathering.25 In natural systems, mineral weathering rates have been noticed to be accelerated at the zone with fungi, which releases ligands, including amino acids. Zheng et al. (2022) reported the effectiveness of glycine in enhancing the extraction of Ca from anhydrite (CaSO4) and calcite (CaCO3) in coal fly ash.26 Kawano and Obokata (2007)27 highlighted the promotion effect of amino acids on the dissolution of amorphous silica at a near-neutral pH, and Bag et al. (2020)28 noted the interactions between amino acids and the Si–O–Si surface groups of silicate minerals. However, studies with a focus on directly examining the potential of amino acids to enhance the dissolution of silicate minerals or clarifying their role in the capture of CO2 via enhanced weathering are limited.

Although amino acids have the potential to enhance mineral dissolution, concerns remain regarding their persistent adsorption onto mineral surfaces, which may in turn inhibit dissolution. Hamdona et al. (1995)29 reported that the dissolution of calcite is significantly hindered by the adsorption of amino acids at active dissolution sites. To mitigate this issue, NaCl was introduced in the system. It has been shown that high NaCl concentrations can influence the adsorption and desorption behavior of amino acids on mineral surfaces,30 potentially establishing a synergistic effect with amino acids that promotes mineral dissolution.

Recently, amino acid salts have also been regarded as a promising class of green materials for CO2 capture because of their extremely low toxicity, negligible volatility, and high absorption capacity. Amino acids can act as bases to form the deprotonated amines in neutral to alkaline solutions, thereby facilitating CO2 absorption through eqs 3–5.31,32 For instance, the deprotonation of –COOH and –NH3 in glutamic acid occurs at pH values of approximately 2.17 and 9.10, respectively.33 Therefore, the addition of basic or alkaline compounds is often required to raise the pH to a sufficiently high level (above 9) to activate amino groups.34,35 Conversely, the dissolution of minerals consumes protons, leading to an increase in the pH of the water bodies,36 which is expected to continuously activate amino groups for CO2 capture.345

The aim of this study was to investigate silicate mineral dissolution in the presence of amino acids and NaCl, assess its effectiveness in enhancing CO2 capture, and reveal the mechanism of the amino acid–NaCl–mineral interaction in aqueous environments via laboratory experiments. Based on the experimental results, possible application systems for enhanced weathering using amino acid and NaCl-containing waters were provided.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Wollastonite (CaSiO3), olivine ((Mg0.91, Fe0.09)2SiO4), and basalt were selected for the dissolution experiments. Wollastonite, a Ca-silicate, was sourced from Aichi Prefecture, Japan. Olivine, a Mg- and Fe-silicate, was obtained from Damaping, China. Basalt, a Ca-, Mg-, and Fe-bearing rock, was sourced from Iceland and consisted of plagioclase (Ca0.7Na0.3Al1.7Si2.3O8), pyroxene (Ca00.8Mg00.7Fe0.5Si2O6), glass, and minor amounts of olivine (Mg1.6Fe0.4SiO4), Fe–Ti oxides, and Cr oxides, as determined using an electron probe microanalyzer (EPMA, JEOL JXA-8200). The major elemental composition of these minerals and rocks was determined using X-ray fluorescence analysis (XRF, RIGAKU, ZSX PrimusIV), with results presented in Table 1. Subsequently, these minerals and rock were crushed and ground to obtain a size range of 63–100 μm for batch dissolution experiments.

Three types of amino acids commonly found in natural environments with potential metal chelation abilities were selected for the experiments: glycine (Gly, H2NCH2COOH), L-serine (Ser, HOCH2CH(NH2)COOH), and L-glutamic acid (Glu, HOOCC2H4CH(NH2)COOH).25,37,38 The powders of these amino acids were obtained from Kanto Chemical, Japan, with a purity exceeding 97%. Amino acid solutions were prepared using Milli-Q water. The pH was adjusted using sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and nitric acid (HNO3) solutions to the desired values. Sodium chloride (NaCl) with 99.9% purity, procured from Kanto Chemical (Japan), was used in the experiments to assess the feasibility of using saltwater for dissolution.

Experiments and Measurements

In a typical batch dissolution experiment, 2.0 g of mineral powders was mixed with 100 mL of the prepared amino acid solution in a 250 mL plastic sample bottle, resulting in a mineral-to-solution ratio of 20 g/L. The bottle was then covered with a breathable film to allow for the continuous absorption of CO2 from the surrounding atmosphere into the solution. The atmospheric CO2 concentration in the room was approximately 400 ppm throughout the experimental period. Subsequently, this suspension was subjected to continuous shaking at 150 rpm on a thermostatic shaker (AS ONE, MSI-60) maintained at 30 °C for 72 h. Notably, water evaporation during the dissolution experiments was negligible. At specified reaction intervals of 0, 5, 24, and 72 h, a small amount of solution sample (1 mL) was extracted by using a syringe and filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter. Upon completion of the 72 h reaction period, the fluid and solid components were separately collected using a 0.45 μm membrane filter, and the solid was dried in an oven at 60 °C for 24 h for further measurement.

To evaluate the effectiveness of amino acids in enhanced mineral dissolution, solutions containing different amino acids at a concentration of 0.05 mol/L and pH of 8 (similar to marine systems) were individually prepared for the dissolution of wollastonite powders. Subsequently, solutions containing 0.1 mol/L Glu, which demonstrated a relatively moderate enhancement ability in wollastonite dissolution, were adjusted to different pH values within the range typical of natural environments (i.e., 5–10) to elucidate the effects of pH on mineral dissolution and CO2 absorption. For comparison, dissolution experiments were also conducted using Milli-Q water with the pH adjusted to the same value. Subsequently, Glu solutions (0.1 M) and the pH yielding the highest CO2 absorption were used to dissolve olivine and basalt powders to investigate the functionality of the amino acids for Mg- and Fe-bearing mineral dissolution. To further explore the potential synergistic effect of NaCl and amino acids, 0.1 mol/L Glu solutions with 1.7 and 3.5 wt % NaCl (3.5 wt % NaCl is similar to marine conditions) with pH adjusted to 8 were prepared and employed for wollastonite dissolution.

The pH and elemental concentrations of the collected aqueous solutions were analyzed by using inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, Agilent 5110). Additionally, the solutions before and after the reaction were assessed for total dissolved inorganic carbon (TIC), indicative of CO2 absorption, using a portable carbon dioxide meter (DKK-TOA, CGP-31). The initial TIC in Milli-Q water was approximately 1 mg/L. A dissolution factor (α) was introduced and calculated using the equations provided in eqs 6 and 7 to elucidate the characteristics of silicate dissolution67where [M] refers to the sum of the molar concentrations of the primary metal ions (e.g., Ca in the case of wollastonite) and [Si] refers to the molar concentrations of Si, either in the extraction solution or in the initial mineral. α > 1 suggests a faster leaching of metal ions than Si, while α < 1 suggests a faster leaching of Si than metal ions, both of which are incongruent dissolution. While when α approaches 1, the dissolution is considered more congruent.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses were conducted on the mineral samples before and after exposure to Glu and NaCl–Glu solutions. This analysis, performed using a K-Alpha system (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) equipped with a monochromatic Al-Kα source, aimed to identify the chemical compositions of the mineral surface within a depth of 10 nm. Before the XPS measurement, the reacted sample was washed multiple times with Milli-Q water in an ultrasonic cleaning tank to remove any potential chelating agents adhering to the mineral surface. Subsequently, the sample was dried in an oven at 60 °C for 24 h.

Results and Discussion

Characteristic of Mineral Dissolution in Solutions Containing Amino Acids

All amino acids used in this study demonstrated the ability to enhance wollastonite dissolution under weak alkaline conditions, with Ser exhibiting the most pronounced effect in leaching Ca, increasing its leaching rate by 29-fold compared to that in Milli-Q water, followed by Glu and Gly, enhanced by 22- and 21-fold, respectively (Figure 1a). Furthermore, the leaching of Ca from wollastonite persisted for the entire experimental duration (72 h) in solutions containing amino acids. In contrast, leaching from water almost stopped after 5 h. Simultaneously, the leaching in Si also lasted longer and was enhanced by 4–6-fold at the reaction time of 72 h in the presence of amino acids (Figure 1b).

Temporal changes in (a) Ca concentration, (b) Si concentration, and (c) dissolution factor (α) during the dissolution of wollastonite with Milli-Q water and solutions containing 0.05 mol/L Ser, Gluf, and Gly, respectively, at initial pH 8 throughout 72 h. An α value > 1 suggests that the dissolution is Ca dominated, α < 1 suggests that the dissolution is Si dominated, a decreased α suggests that the leaching of Si is faster than Ca, while an α value approaching 1 indicates a more congruent dissolution.

The dissolution factor (α) for each sampling point in each experiment was calculated and is summarized in Figure 1c. During the first 5 h of dissolution, the α values for the experiment without amino acids were lower than 1, indicating that the wollastonite dissolution was Si dominated. However, in the presence of amino acids, the α value increased to greater than 1, indicating that the dissolution was Ca dominated. As the reaction time increased, the α value gradually decreased for both solutions with and without amino acids, indicating that from 5 to 72 h, the leaching rate of Si was higher than that of Ca. These changes may be related to the difference in kinetics between the formation of Ca complexes with amino acids and the breakdown of the silica framework. At the beginning of the reaction, the rate of silicate dissolution was lower than that of the chelation process, resulting in more rapid Ca leaching in the early stages of the reaction. As dissolution progressed, the mineral surface became dominated by Si, leading to a shift toward faster Si leaching relative to Ca leaching. Regardless, both elements exhibited sustained leaching behavior over time. At a reaction time of 72 h, experiments with amino acids exhibited α values closer to 1, suggesting that the dissolution was generally more congruent than those without amino acids. Consequently, wollastonite dissolution continued for a much longer time. In contrast, wollastonite dissolution in Milli-Q water ceased after 5 h. This phenomenon could be attributed to the significantly faster leaching rate of Si compared to that of Ca ions, as indicated by the low dissolution factor, α, which was approximately 0.2 (Figure 1c). This observation further indicated that under alkaline conditions, a Ca-rich layer was formed.9 Additionally, the analysis of solid samples obtained after the reaction via XRD revealed the absence of secondary minerals.

Effects of pH and Mineral Type on CO2 Absorption

Wollastonite was dissolved in solutions containing 0.1 mol/L of Glu with the pH adjusted to 5, 7, 8, and 10, respectively. The concentrations of Ca leached from wollastonite at a reaction time of 72 h are summarized in Figure 2a. A notable increase in the Ca concentration was observed with a decrease in the initial pH of the Glu solutions from 10 to 5, as expected from a more intensive attack from protons at lower pH levels. The Ca concentration in the experiment at pH 10 was even lower than that without Glu at the same pH, suggesting a potential negative effect of Glu in promoting mineral dissolution at alkaline pH. This observation may be attributed to the adsorption of amino acids at the active dissolution site of the mineral, as suggested by Hamdona et al. (1995).29 Notably, under both acidic and alkaline conditions, the dissolution of wollastonite was more incongruent. Specifically, dissolution was Ca dominated at an acidic pH (e.g., pH 5), whereas it was Si dominated under alkaline conditions (e.g., pH 10). This indicates that amino acid-assisted mineral dissolution is more favorable under near-neutral conditions, considering long-term applications.

(a) Concentration of Ca extracted from wollastonite and changes in pH, and (b) increase in TIC concentration in experiments involving the dissolution of wollastonite in Glu solutions at a concentration of 0.1 mol/L, initial pHs of 5, 7, 8, and 10, at 35 °C for 72 h. “Initial pH” refers to the pH before adding wollastonite powders, whereas “pH” refers to the pH after the experiments.

The increase in TIC concentration at varying pH values, which was used to evaluate the CO2 absorption efficiency, is summarized in Figure 2b. First, a significantly higher TIC concentration was observed in experiments with the addition of Glu at all pH values compared with those without Glu, indicating the promise of a substantial reduction in atmospheric CO2 levels using amino acids. Notably, the presence of Glu itself also significantly enhanced CO2 absorption, as evidenced by an increase in the TIC by a factor of 9 at pH 10, at which the Ca leaching concentration was low.

The highest TIC was achieved in the experiment with Glu at a weakly alkaline pH of 8, reaching 185 mg/L, 22-fold higher than that of Milli-Q water at the same pH. The decrease in TIC concentration with decreasing pH may be attributed to the reduced solubility of CO2. This suggests that while a greater amount of minerals was dissolved under acidic conditions, this did not always result in more CO2 being captured. Thus, a weakly alkaline pH is suggested to maximize the CO2 absorption.

The presence of Glu also facilitated the extraction of Ca, Mg, and Si from olivine and basalt (Figure 3a–c). However, the dissolution enhancement of wollastonite remained the most significant, suggesting that the reactivity of wollastonite was more significantly improved than those of olivine and basalt. This may be because the crystal structure of wollastonite is more susceptible to amino acid attacks. Although many chelating agents are strong chelators of Fe, in the present study, the concentration of Fe was negligible in the experiments with both olivine and basalt.

Changes in pH and concentrations of Si, Ca, and Mg extracted from (a) wollastonite, (b) olivine, and (c) basalt, as well as (d) increase in TIC concentration in experiments using Milli-Q water and Glu solution at a concentration of 0.1 mol/L, initial pH of 8, and 35 °C for 72 h.

Although the dissolution of silicate minerals typically leads to an increase in pH, the enhanced dissolution of olivine and basalt with Glu resulted in an unexpected decrease in pH.20,36 The decrease in pH along with the negligible presence of Fe in solution could be attributed to the precipitation of Fe(OH)2. Fe(OH)2 has a low solubility; its solubility product is approximately 8 × 10–16. Further, the binding between Glu and Fe(II) is relatively weak (stability constant, logK = 3.5)39 and, thus, facilitates the reaction between dissolved Fe(II) and OH–, leading to Fe(OH)2 precipitation. Calculation using the solubility products of Fe(OH)2 and the Fe–Glu stability constant indicated that under the experimental conditions, the total concentration of free Fe(II) and Fe–GLDA complexes was less than 1 ppm. The more pronounced pH decrease observed in the experiments with olivine compared to those with basalt is attributed to the more extensive extraction and hydroxylation of Fe. This decrease in pH is undesirable, as it reduces the CO2 solubility and CO2 capture efficiency by smaller increases in the TIC observed in experiments using olivine with Glu compared to those without Glu (Figure 3d). Therefore, even though amino acids can enhance the dissolution of Fe-bearing minerals, they are not suitable for CO2 capture, particularly under neutral to weakly alkaline conditions.

Synergistic Effect of NaCl and Amino Acids for Enhanced Mineral Dissolution

Wollastonite dissolution experiments were also conducted in the presence and absence of NaCl with or without Glu. The temporal changes in the Ca concentrations are summarized in Figure 4. NaCl alone also enhanced wollastonite dissolution, which is related to the higher ionic strength of the solution, as suggested by Wang et al. (2013).40 Moreover, a strong synergistic effect of NaCl and Glu on mineral dissolution was observed, with the synergistic effect being more intense at higher NaCl concentrations (Figure 4a). The Ca leaching concentration significantly increased by 1.5-fold in the Glu solution with 1.7 wt % NaCl. Further, it increased to 1.9-fold in the Glu solution with 3.5 wt % NaCl compared with that in the Glu solution without NaCl. Changes in pH remained insignificant for solutions containing Glu. The dissolution of wollastonite in solutions containing NaCl and Glu is more Ca-leaching dominated, with α > 1, specifically at the beginning of the dissolution in Glu solutions with a higher NaCl concentration (Figure 4b). The dissolution gradually became Si dominant, with the value of α decreasing to approaching 1.

(b) Dissolution factor (α) during the dissolution of wollastonite with Milli-Q water, solutions containing 3.5 wt % NaCl, 0.1 mol/L Glu, 1.7 wt % NaCl + 0.1 mol/L Glu, and 3.5 wt % NaCl + 0.1 mol/L Glu, at an initial pH of 8 and a temperature of 30 °C over 168 h.

To investigate the role of NaCl during Glu chelation, XPS measurements were conducted on wollastonite particles collected after reacting with solutions containing 0.1 mol/L Glu and 0.1 mol/L Glu + 3.5 wt % NaCl and compared with those collected before the reaction. Figure 5a presents a full-range survey of the XPS spectra of the wollastonite particle surface, revealing the presence of Na, O, Ca, C, and Si in both unreacted and reacted wollastonite particles. The presence of C on the surface of the unreacted wollastonite particles was attributed to a small amount of organic matter.

(a) XPS survey spectra and XPS narrow-range spectra of the (b) N 1s, (c) Cl 2p, (d) Na 1s, and (e) Ca 2p of wollastonite before (Wol (BR)) and after reaction with solution containing 0.1 mol/L Glu (Wol + Glu (AR1)) and with solution containing 0.1 mol/L Glu + 3.5 wt % NaCl (Wol + Glu + NaCl (AR2)).

After reacting wollastonite with Glu (C5H9NO4) and subsequently washing with water, a small peak for N 1s was observed, indicating the adsorption of Glu onto the mineral surface (Figure 5a). However, such a peak was not observed for wollastonite after reaction with the Glu + NaCl solution (Figure 5b), implying the facilitated desorption of Glu from the surface of wollastonite due to the presence of NaCl. This finding is consistent with the observations of Sebben and Pendleton (2015),30 who noted that the adsorption of Glu on solid surfaces may decrease in the presence of NaCl.

The surface of the wollastonite particles after reacting in the Glu solution (Wol + Glu (AR1)) contained considerable Na+, as evidenced by the prominent Na 1s peak at 1071.3 eV (Figure 5d), which may exist in the form of RCOO-Na. The Na ions were introduced in solution via the addition of NaOH during the adjustment of the pH of the initial solution prior to the experiment. However, upon the inclusion of NaCl in the solution and the desorption of Glu from the surface of the wollastonite, the Na 1s peak decreased. Moreover, the binding energy increased slightly to 1072.1 eV, which was assigned to O–Na rather than RCOO-Na, according to Hammond et al. (1981),41 where O is within the structure of wollastonite. Although Cl also possesses a metal complexing ability, the Cl 2p peak was minimal after reacting wollastonite with the Glu + NaCl solution (Figure 5c), suggesting that Cl did not adsorb onto the mineral surface. The decrease in the binding energy of Ca after reaction with the Glu solution shown in Figure 5e suggests chemical binding between Ca in wollastonite and the amino group (−NH3+) in the Glu molecules. This binding increases the electron density around Ca, thus facilitating its extraction. This is reasonable because at a neutral pH, wollastonite is negatively charged (the zero point of charge of wollastonite is approximately pH 2),42 which can combine with the NH3+ group in amino acids. Under weakly alkaline conditions, the combination of Glu and Ca may primarily occur near mineral dissolution sites, which consume protons and increase the pH near the dissolution site, resulting in the generation of –NH2 as –NH3+ forms under highly alkaline conditions (e.g., above pH 9.6). The desorption of Glu from wollastonite in the Glu + NaCl solution may also be related to a preferred combination of –NH3+ with Cl–.

A possible mechanism for the enhanced mineral dissolution by natural chelating agents and the amplification of this enhancement effect by NaCl at neutral pH is illustrated in Figure 6. Previous studies have shown that in solutions containing chelating agents, one of the primary mechanisms for promoting solid dissolution is the binding of chelating agents to free metal ions, which reduces the concentration of free metal ions in solution. In contrast, this study revealed the adsorption of Glu molecules onto the surface of wollastonite via binding with Ca in the wollastonite structure through both R–COO– and NH3+ groups43 (Figure 6a). This adsorption may enhance the extraction of Ca from wollastonite if the Ca–O bonds in the wollastonite structure are broken, leading to the release of the Ca–Glu complex into solution. However, if the Ca–O remains intact, Glu molecules adsorbed on the surface of wollastonite may hinder the interaction between free Glu molecules and the mineral surface.29 Therefore, it appears that the presence of Na ions facilitated the breaking of Ca–O bonds via interaction with O in the wollastonite structure, thereby enhancing Ca release from wollastonite. Additionally, with the removal of Glu and the adsorption of Na+ on the surface of wollastonite, the mineral surface became positively charged, attracting more free Glu2–, which allowed for further interaction with Ca in wollastonite (Figure 6b).

Environmental Implementations

This study suggests that amino acids are effective in enhancing the capture of CO2 from the atmosphere through enhanced mineral dissolution and the direct reaction of CO2 with amino acids. The system is potentially effective in natural environments, such as wetlands, fishing harbors, or farms for shellfish and kelp, which are rich in both amino acids and water. The concentration of amino acids in natural environments is lower than those used in the experiments described in this study. For instance, the concentration of amino acids in surface water typically ranges between 10 and 100 nM. Thus, the promotion of the dissolution of minerals in such amino acid-enriched natural environments over short time periods may not be significant. However, the presence of amino acids is expected to enhance mineral dissolution over longer durations owing to the observed near-congruent dissolution, while the dissolution of minerals increases pH, hence, the number of active amino groups for more CO2 capture. This extended interaction may greatly improve the system’s sustainability as well as its overall capacity for CO2 capture.

To improve CO2 capture efficiency, we propose a semiclosed system, as depicted in Figure 7. In this system, plants or microbes capable of releasing specific amino acids are artificially introduced. Alternatively, amino acids can be directly supplemented to achieve the desired concentration. Freshwater, seawater, or brine, as well as various minerals or rocks, can also be used in the system. Regardless, the system showed more efficient CO2 absorption when saltwater and Fe-free minerals are used. The use of Fe-bearing minerals, such as olivine and basalt, may lead to the formation of Fe hydroxides, which reduces pH and diminishes the system’s CO2 capture capacity. Given the challenge of finding Fe-free minerals because Fe is one of the most abundant elements in the Earth’s crust, certain industrial byproducts, such as slags and waste concrete, have emerged as promising materials for enhanced weathering systems. When minerals are dissolved in the presence of amino acids, the pH increases, activating the CO2 capture ability of amino acids. In a closed system, this process may quickly reach a stable state, resulting in a slowdown of dissolution, as observed in the experiments. However, semiclosed systems consider the mineral dissolution and CO2 capture processes sustainable. First, the alkaline and CO2-rich solution seeps through the soil into the subsurface, eventually transporting it to the ocean. Simultaneously, rainwater or artificially added water compensates for water loss in the system, lowering the pH and the CO2 concentration of the water body, thereby reinitiating mineral dissolution and CO2 capture. A near-congruent dissolution of minerals in the presence of amino acids under weakly alkaline conditions ensures a more efficient dissolution. Finally, the loss of amino acids is expected to be minimal because microbes continuously release amino acids into the system. Implementing this semiclosed CO2 capture system is expected to significantly mitigate ocean acidification and increase total CO2 storage capacity of marine systems. However, there remains a significant gap between laboratory-scale experiments and field applications, particularly with respect to CO2 transport, and addressing these challenges will be the focus of future research.

Schematic representation of the semiclosed system based on the use of amino acids to enhance near-congruent mineral dissolution for efficient atmospheric CO2 removal.

The effect of enhanced weathering on microbes was not experimentally illustrated in this study. However, integrating enhanced mineral dissolution is expected to contribute to biodiversity conservation by providing nutrient (e.g., Fe, Ca, K, and P) for plants and microbes.44,45 Yan et al. (2023) noted that by adding wollastonite, the limiting effects of soil acidity on microbial metabolism and nutrient shortage can be overcome by increasing soil pH and adding Si and P, resulting in a faster rate of C utilization by microbes.46 Further, Ren et al. (2022) observed that olivine supplementation brings about an increase in the relative abundances of biofilm-forming microbes in particle-attached fractions.47 Therefore, wetlands, as vital ecosystems hosting a rich variety of species, are expected to benefit from amino acid-enhanced mineral dissolution systems that foster habitat resilience. This integration ensures that wetlands serve as critical environmental buffers and water purification reservoirs, promoting sustainable land-use practices. Finally, although the utilization of amino acids holds promise, carefully considering their potential ecological impacts and ensuring the long-term sustainability of such interventions is imperative. Continued research and comprehensive monitoring efforts are essential to thoroughly understand the intricate interactions within wetland ecosystems and optimize the application of amino acid-enhanced mineral weathering systems.
