Every Bit Counts: A Theoretical Study of Precision–Expressivity Tradeoffs in Quantized Transformers
Sayak Chakrabarti, Toniann Pitassi, Josh Alman

Abstract
Quantization reduces the numerical precision of Transformer computations and is widely used to accelerate inference, yet its effect on expressivity remains poorly characterized. We demonstrate a fine-grained theoretical tradeoff between expressivity and precision: For every p p we exhibit a function Γ \Gamma , inspired by the equality function, and prove that a one-layer softmax Transformer can compute Γ \Gamma , with p p bits of precision, but not with p − 1 p\!-\!1 bits of precision. This result concretely explains the widely observed phenomenon of empirical loss of expressivity when quantization is used. Practically, it suggests that tasks requiring equality-like comparisons (exact match, membership, etc.) are especially sensitive to quantization. Dropping even one bit can cross a threshold where the model cannot represent the needed comparison reliably. Thus, it paves the way for developing heuristics that will help practitioners choose how much quantization is possible: the precision should be chosen as a function of the length of equality to be checked for the specific task. Our proofs combine explicit finite-precision Transformer constructions with communication-complexity lower bounds, yielding a tight “one-bit” threshold.

Equality.

Our new fine-grained precision results concern the famous equality function,

Equality is a basic task that is ubiquitous across a wide variety of applications, and therefore important for Transformers to be able to quickly solve.

Main Results

We first describe what we mean by a Transformer solving a task. The Transformer can be viewed as a function, which takes

In this paper, we study the ability of Transformers to compute functions of interest. Let us assume that we want to compute a Boolean function

(n+1)

We prove our tradeoff on a function similar to the equality function. For integers
m>0
and

, define
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{EQ}_{m}}^{n}:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, where, given

for all

As we described before, computing an equality check is a natural and important component of many natural language processing tasks. Our main result shows exactly how many bits of precision are needed to compute it.

Theorem 1.1

(Informal)
.

For every integer
p>0
and
n>4p
, there exists an input representation such that

1.

no one-layer Transformer that uses
\leq p-1
bits of fixed-point precision can solve
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{EQ}_{2p-1}}^{n}
,

2.

there exists a one-layer Transformer that can solve
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{EQ}_{2p-1}}^{n}
using
p
bits of fixed-point precision.

This is the tightest tradeoff possible: reducing the precision by just one bit degrades the capability of the Transformer to solve equality. Note that contrary to prior works where precision has been considered as a coarse function of
n
(either constant, logarithmic in
n
, or polynomial in

p
.

We also prove similar tradeoff results for the floating-point precision format, where we obtain a tight tradeoff for linear Transformers, and an almost tight tradeoff for soft-max Transformers. The function on which this tradeoff is performed is again very similar to the equality function. However, owing to the mantissa and exponent structure of this precision format, constructing an upper bound (a Transformer that solves this variant of equality task) is more difficult. We discuss the challenges here in more detail in
Section

2.3
below and, due to space constraints, the formal results have been moved to
Appendix

C
.

These results are primarily theoretical and intended to provide a rigorous framework to study the disadvantages of quantization. This is important as an understanding of expressivity loss can guide the design of quantization schemes that incur as much expressivity loss as the task of interest can tolerate.
Indeed, it suggests that tasks requiring equality-like comparisons (exact match, membership, etc.) are especially sensitive to quantization. Dropping even one bit can cross a threshold where the model cannot represent the needed comparison reliably. Thus, it paves the way for developing heuristics that will help practitioners choose how much quantization is possible: the precision should be chosen as a function of the length of equality to be checked for the specific task.

Moreover, this result concretely explains the widely observed phenomenon of empirical loss of expressivity when quantization is used. As described above, the qualitative difficulty of equality-style tasks under limited precision has been observed in prior works
(

In

Proof overview.

The main idea of this construction is inspired by the proof of the lower bound, where both the numerator and denominator of self-attention are used to transfer information. The construction uses the numerator to check equality in the first half (whether

are small constants irrespective of

, and summation
\sum_{i={\lceil m/2\rceil}+1}^{m}e^{\langle Q_{n+1},K_{j}\rangle}+\sum_{i=m+{\lceil m/2\rceil}+1}^{2m}e^{\langle Q_{n+1},K_{j}\rangle}
will be
\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{{\lceil m/2\rceil}+1:m}+\mathbf{z}_{{\lceil m/2\rceil}+1:m}+2^{-1}
. This is a fixed value if
\mathbf{y}_{{\lceil m/2\rceil}+1:m}=\mathbf{z}_{{\lceil m/2\rceil}+1:m}
, otherwise a larger value.

Summing up, if
\mathbf{y}_{1:m}=\mathbf{z}_{1:m}
, then the numerator will be
(2^{{\lceil m/2\rceil}}-1)
and the denominator will be a fixed value
2^{({\lceil m/2\rceil}-1)}-1+2^{-1}
, otherwise either the numerator make the output infinity (
float(‘inf’)
), or the denominator will a larger value (therefore the output different from
\frac{2^{{\lceil m/2\rceil}}-1}{2^{{\lceil m/2\rceil}-1}-2^{-1}}=2
), either of which can be detected with a simple MLP layer after the self-attention computation.
∎

2.2
Tradeoff with Fixed-Point Precision: Theorem
1.1

In this section, we prove a tight one-bit tradeoff. We use the same function
\mathsf{EQ}_{m}
as before, along with the same promise that
m
is odd and
\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{z}
.

For ease of notation, we consider indexing from
-1
up
n-2
. The input representation
\mathcal{T}_{m}^{1}:\{0,1\}^{2m}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
is defined as follows:

1.

\mathcal{T}_{m}^{1}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})_{-1}=0
.

2.

\mathcal{T}_{m}^{1}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})_{0}=y_{1}
.

3.

\mathcal{T}_{m}^{1}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})_{i}=y_{i}
for
i\in[m]
.

4.

\mathcal{T}_{m}^{1}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})_{i}=z_{1}
for

.

5.

\mathcal{T}_{m}^{1}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})_{m+3}=0
.

6.

\mathcal{T}_{m}^{1}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})_{i}=z_{i-m-2}
for

7.

\mathcal{T}_{m}^{1}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})_{i}=0
for all

Formally, Theorem

can be stated as follows.

Theorem 2.4

(Tight Tradeoff with Fixed-Point Precision)
.

For every integer
p>0
and every integer

, choosing
m=2p-1

1.

and one head, can compute

with the input representation

2.

there exists a one-layer Transformer with
n
tokens having value dimension

and one head, that can compute
{\mathsf{EQ}_{m}}
with the input representation
\mathcal{T}_{m}^{1}
using
p
bits of precision in the fixed-point precision format.

As we will see in the proof, padding tokens do increase the expressivity of the Transformer and helps us achieve this exact trade off for this function. On a theoretical standpoint, using padding tokens do indeed improve the expressivity of Transformers was also proved by
(

)
. In our case, we use this representation to prevent overflow as we are dealing with the exact number of bits that can be used to compute the function. We first state the lower bound present in
Section

B.2
.

Lemma 2.5

(Lower Bound)
.

Given the input representation
\mathcal{T}_{m}^{1}
, any one-layer Transformer with
d_{v}=1
and
H=1
that computes
\mathsf{EQ}_{m}
requires precision
\geq{\lceil m/2\rceil}
bits.

Now, we prove the existence of a Transformer with precision
{\lceil m/2\rceil}
bits which actually computes
T_{m}
.

Lemma 2.6

(Upper Bound)
.

There exists a one-layer Transformer with
d_{v}=1
and
H=1
, that uses
{\lceil m/2\rceil}
bits of fixed-point precision to compute
\mathsf{EQ}_{m}
in the input representation
\mathcal{T}_{m}^{1}
.

Proof overview.

The construction is similar to the proof of
Lemma

2.3
, where the numerator of the self-attention checks equality in the first half (whether
\mathbf{y}_{1:{\lceil m/2\rceil}}=\mathbf{z}_{1:{\lceil m/2\rceil}}
), and then the denominator checks the rest.

For the numerator part, we will choose the embedding such that the final numerator value is equal to one if they are equal. We make the summation value
\sum_{i=-1}^{n+1}e^{\langle Q_{n+1},K_{i}\rangle}V_{i}=\mathbf{y}_{1:{\lceil m/2\rceil}}-\mathbf{z}_{1:{\lceil m/2\rceil}}+1
, by ensuring
\sum_{i=-1}^{{\lceil m/2\rceil}-1}e^{\langle Q_{n+1},K_{i}\rangle}V_{i}
gives
\mathbf{y}_{1:{\lceil m/2\rceil}}
, and
\sum_{i=m}^{m+{\lceil m/2\rceil}}e^{\langle Q_{n+1},K_{i}\rangle}V_{i}
gives
\mathbf{z}_{1:{\lceil m/2\rceil}}
. However, this is not directly possible as before, since
\mathbf{y}_{1:{\lceil m/2\rceil}},\mathbf{z}_{1:{\lceil m/2\rceil}}
use
{\lceil m/2\rceil}
bits, but in
{\lceil m/2\rceil}
bits of fixed-point precision, one bit is allocated to the sign bit, giving us
{\lceil m/2\rceil}-1
positions.
To circumvent this difficulty, we use a dummy token in the
(-1)
-th position, such that
e^{\langle Q_{n+1},K_{-1}\rangle}V_{-1}=-(2^{{\lceil m/2\rceil}-1}-1)
, and is used to add a negative shift to the value of
\mathbf{y}_{1:{\lceil m/2\rceil}}
in order to bring it to the permissible range of
{\lceil m/2\rceil}
precision bits.

There is one more difficulty, the value
\mathbf{y}_{1}2^{{\lceil m/2\rceil}-1}
might be infinity while computing
\mathbf{y}_{1:{\lceil m/2\rceil}}
. In order to prevent this, we split this value amongst two tokens corresponding to
0
and
1
positions, where each of them has the value
e^{\langle Q_{n+1},K_{i}\rangle}V_{i}=\mathbf{y}_{1}2^{{\lceil m/2\rceil}-2}
. This will give the first part of the summation of the numerator as

\sum_{i\in[-1:m]}e^{\langle Q_{n+1},K_{i}\rangle}V_{i}=\mathbf{y}_{1,{\lceil m/2\rceil}}-\underbrace{1\ldots 1}_{{\lceil m/2\rceil}-1}.

A similar shift is added to
-\mathbf{z}_{1:{\lceil m/2\rceil}}
, to obtain:

\sum_{i\in[m+1:2m+2]}e^{\langle Q_{n+1},K_{i}\rangle}V_{i}=-\mathbf{z}_{1,{\lceil m/2\rceil}}+\underbrace{1\ldots 1}_{{\lceil m/2\rceil}-1}.

Finally, we choose the embedding such that
e^{\langle Q_{n+1},K_{2m+3}\rangle}V_{2m+3}=1
, such that the value of the numerator will be exactly
1
if
\mathbf{y}_{1:{\lceil m/2\rceil}}=\mathbf{z}_{1:{\lceil m/2\rceil}}
, otherwise
\leq 0
from the promise on
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{z}
.

For the denominator, again we ensure that
\sum_{i=1}^{{\lceil m/2\rceil}}e^{\langle Q_{n+1},K_{j}\rangle}
and
\sum_{i=m+1}^{m+{\lceil m/2\rceil}}e^{\langle Q_{n+1},K_{j}\rangle}
are small constants irrespective of
\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}
, and ignored with respect to the summation
\sum_{i={\lceil m/2\rceil}+1}^{m}e^{\langle Q_{n+1},K_{j}\rangle}+\sum_{i=m+{\lceil m/2\rceil}+3}^{2m+2}e^{\langle Q_{n+1},K_{j}\rangle}
. This gives the value
\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{{\lceil m/2\rceil}+1:m}+\mathbf{z}_{{\lceil m/2\rceil}+1:m}
, which is a fixed value for
\phi_{{\lceil m/2\rceil}+1:m}=\phi_{m+{\lceil m/2\rceil}+1:2m}
, otherwise a larger than that due to the promise.

Summing up, if
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{z}
, then the numerator will be
1
and the denominator will be a fixed value
2^{({\lceil m/2\rceil}-1)}-1
, otherwise either the numerator will be non-positive or the denominator a larger value (i.e.,
float(‘inf’)
), either of which can be detected by the final MLP layer.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We choose
m:=2p-1
. From Lemma
2.5
,
\mathsf{EQ}_{m}
cannot be computed by a Transformer using
\leq{\lceil m/2\rceil}-1=p-1
bits for input representation
\mathcal{T}_{m}^{1}
, but can be computed by a Transformer using
{\lceil m/2\rceil}=p
bits (Lemma
2.6
).
∎

2.3
Overview of Floating-Point Results

We prove similar bounds for floating-point precision format with the same function
\mathsf{EQ}_{m}
. However, the input representation will be different– instead of each token containing a bit of
\mathbf{y}
or
\mathbf{z}
, we will allow each token to contain a tuples, where one-element is a tuple of
e-1
bits from either
\mathbf{y}
or
\mathbf{z}
(which will be used to directly create the exponent in the floating-point precision format for the Transformer construction in the upper bound), and one more bit (which will contribute to the mantissa).

This difficulty arises because, in the floating-point representation, the exponent will be exponentiated twice for computing
e^{\langle Q_{i},K_{j}\rangle}
, and summation over this to yield another floating-point number is difficult as it might lead to overflow in bounded precision format.

This choice of input tokens containing tuples is a fairly known technique, and have been used in other Transformer based problems, including function evaluation where the indices and values are given as tuples
(
76
)
, graph problems where the edges are given as tuples
(
68
)
, higher-order graph Transformers where
k
-tuples of vertices are used
(
94
)
etc. Indeed, reformatting of inputs is an important technique that can completely alter what a Transformer can learn or represent
(
95
)
. More details and the complete proofs can be found in
Appendix

C
.

4
Conclusion

This paper theoretically justifies that expressivity loss is inevitable with quantization. It further aims to develop a class of tasks on which quantization is difficult– practitioners need to be careful while choosing quantization schemes on these.

The precision-expressivity trade-offs proved here can be extended beyond
H,d_{v}=1
, by dividing the strings
\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}
into
H(d_{v}+1)
parts, and computing
\mathsf{EQ}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})
in parallel. However, finding such a trade-off for the multi-layer Transformer remains elusive due to the lack of lower-bound techniques. Overcoming this challenge will help design quantization schemes where accuracy will be maintained.

For integers

\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
,
[m:n]
to denote the set of integers
\{m,m+1,\ldots,n\}
, where
m\leq n
, and
(m,n)
(resp.
[m,n]
) to denote the set of real numbers between
m
and
n
, end points excluded (resp. end points included). For a matrix
{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and for
i\in[n]
,
{A}_{i}
denotes the
i
-th row vector
({A}_{i,1},\ldots,{A}_{i,m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Similarly, for
j\in[m]

{A}_{:,j}
denotes the column vector
({A}_{1,j},\ldots,{A}_{n,j})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

Strings are emboldened, and for a string
\mathbf{a}\in\{0,1\}^{n}

\mathbf{a}
will denote the value of the number whose binary expression is
a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{n}

\mathbf{a}_{p:q}
represents the substring
a_{p}a_{p+1}\ldots a_{q}
, for

A.1
Transformers

We define the multi-layer Transformer architecture as follows.

Definition A.1
.

An
L
-layer Transformer is a function
\Sigma^{n}\rightarrow\Sigma^{n}
, where
\Sigma
is an alphabet set and
n
is the number of tokens, also regarded as the sequence length. It consists of:

1.

an embedding function
\mathbb{E}:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d
is the embedding dimension,

2.

several Transformer units for each layer that are functions
\mathsf{Tf}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
for
i\in[L]
,

3.

and a final output projection function, also known as language modeling head,
f_{LM}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,1]^{|\Sigma|}
, which give the probabilities of every alphabet from the alphabet set
\Sigma
.

Each Transformer layer consists of two components, a self-attention function
f^{i}_{SA}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{v}}
and an MLP function
f^{i}_{MLP}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d_{v}
is the value dimension. Now, an
L
-layer Transformer is the composition of the functions:

f_{LM}\circ f_{MLP}^{L}\circ f^{L}_{SA}\circ\ldots\circ f_{MLP}^{1}\circ f_{SA}^{1}\circ\mathbb{E}:\rightarrow\Sigma^{n}\rightarrow[0,1]^{|\Sigma|}.

The core operation in a Transformer layer is the self-attention function, which is used to compute cross-token interactions. Soft-max self-attention is the most commonly used form of self-attention and is defined as follows.

Definition A.2

(Softmax Self-Attention)
.

Given parameters
W^{Q},W^{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{q}}
, and
W^{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{v}}
, where
d_{q}
is the query dimension and
d_{v}
is the value dimension, the soft-max self-attention is a function
\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, where the
i
-th row of the output is computed as

SA_{i}=\frac{\sum_{j\in[n]}e^{(X_{i}W^{Q}(W^{K})^{T}X_{j}^{T})}X_{j}W^{V}}{\sum_{j\in[n]}e^{(X_{i}W^{Q}(W^{K})^{T}X_{j}^{T})}},

for all
i\in[n]
.

We will commonly refer to
XW^{Q}
as the query matrix
Q
,
XW^{K}
as the key matrix
K
and
XW^{V}
as the value matrix
V
. Also,
\sum_{j\in[n]}e^{(X_{i}W^{Q}(W^{K})^{T}X_{j}^{T})}X_{j}W^{V}
will be called the numerator term and
\sum_{j\in[n]}e^{(X_{i}W^{Q}(W^{K})^{T}X_{j}^{T})}
the denominator term.

We also define the linear self-attention mechanism, which is widely used as a more computationally efficient counterpart of the soft-max self-attention mechanism.

Definition A.3

(Linear Self-Attention)
.

Given parameters
W^{Q},W^{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{q}}
, and
W^{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{v}}
, the linear self-attention is a function
\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, where the
i
-th row of the output is computed as

SA_{i}=\sum_{j\in[n]}X_{i}W^{Q}(W^{K})^{T}X_{j}^{T}X_{j}W^{V},

for
i\in[n]
.

In our constructions, given tokens
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
we will consider them to contain positional encodings, and the embedding as a function
[n]\times\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where the inputs to the function are the index of the token and the value in
\Sigma
, and the output
f(i,X_{i})
is a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Definition A.4

(Final MLP Layer)
.

The final MLP layer is a function
f_{MLP}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which has
d_{v}
input nodes,
d
output nodes, and
d_{ff}
nodes in one hidden layer in between them (usually
d_{ff}=4d
). Each edge from nodes one layer to the next is associated with weights and biases.

The weights of the
i
-th input node to the
j
-th hidden node is denoted as
w_{i,j}^{1}
, and the bias of the
j
-th hidden node as
b^{1}_{j}
, for
i\in[d_{v}],j\in[d_{ff}]
. Subsequently, the weight of the
j
-th hidden node to the
k
-th output node is denoted as
w_{j,k}^{2}
, and the bias of the
k
-th output node as
b^{2}_{k}
, for
j\in[d_{ff}],k\in[d]
.

Given the values of the hidden nodes as
v_{1},\ldots,v_{d_{v}}
, the
j
-th hidden node will have the value

h_{j}=\max\{0,\sum_{i\in[d_{v}]}w^{1}_{i,j}v_{i}+b_{j}\},

for
j\in[d_{ff}]
, and the
k
-th output node will have the value

\max\{0,\sum_{j\in[d_{ff}]}w^{2}_{j,k}h_{j}+b_{k}\},

for
k\in[d]
.

A.2
Communication Complexity

We use a model of communication with two players Alice and Bob, each of whom have unbounded computational power. In order to solve the problem, Alice has some bits, Bob has some bits and they want to communicate to compute a function known to all. Furthermore, we only allow one-way communication which is communication only from Alice to Bob. Interested readers can see
(
42
)
for more background on communication complexity.

Definition A.5

(Fooling sets)
.

Given a function
f
, a set
F=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\,|\,i\in[\ell]\}
is called a fooling set for
f
of size
\ell
if:

•

for all
i\in\ell
,
f(x_{i},y_{i})
has the same value,

•

for all
i\neq j
,
i,j\in[\ell]
, either
f(x_{i},y_{j})
or
f(x_{j},y_{i})
has a value different from
f(x_{i},u_{i})
.

Lemma A.6

(Folklore)
.

If the function
f
has a fooling set of size
\ell
, then the communication complexity of
f
is at least
\lceil\log_{2}\ell\rceil
.

A.3
Computations with Bounded Precision

In this article, we use a certain kind of precision defined as follows.

Definition A.7

(Fixed-point precision)
.

We define the
fixed-point precision
by a triple
\langle s,a,b\rangle
, where
a,b
are binary expression of integers and
s
is the sign bit. The value is given by

\langle 0,a,b\rangle=a+2^{-|b|}b=a\,\cdot\,b\;,

and

\langle 1,a,b\rangle=-(a+2^{-|b|}b)=-a\,\cdot\,b\;.

We say that this requires
p
bits of precision if
1+|a|+|b|=p
.

Typically, fixed-point precision formats also have a scaling factor, say
\lambda
, associated with them, which brings the final value in the range
[-\lambda(2^{p-1}-1):\lambda(2^{p-1}-1)]

For addition or multiplication of two numbers, we first find the exact value of the two numbers, and then round off the answer to bring it back to the given precision bounds. Approximation occurs by rounding-off to the nearest number within the permissible set of numbers. Ties can be broken by truncation.

Example A.7
.

To compute the sum of
1\cdot 1101
and
10\cdot 111
using
p=5
, the answer becomes

1\cdot 1101+10\cdot 111=100\cdot 0011=100\cdot 01.

Similarly, for multiplying
1\cdot 101
with
10\cdot 11
using
p=4
, we get

Note that in this model,

do not need to be fixed separately, which gives it more representational power. When we perform iterated addition or multiplication, we use

(resp.

p

p
most significant bits. For summing

a_{1}+a_{2}+\ldots+a_{n}=((((a_{1}+a_{2})+a_{3})+\ldots)+a_{n}),

and multiplication is also

a_{1}\times a_{2}\times\ldots\times a_{n}=((((a_{1}\times a_{2})\times a_{3})\times\ldots)\times a_{n}).

Example A.7
.

We show why left associativity is important, as consider
10\cdot 0
,
1\cdot 01
,
1\cdot 11
with
p=3
. Now,
((10\cdot 0+1\cdot 01)+1\cdot 11)=(11+1\cdot 11)=100

(10\cdot 0+(1\cdot 01+1\cdot 11))=(10\cdot 0+11\cdot 0)=101
.

Similarly, for multiplication, given
a,b
, in order to compute
a\times b
, we first compute the exact value, and then consider the
p
significant bits in this precision format.

This precision format is directly inspired from communication complexity. Our lower bounds are in terms of Alice sending bounded precision numbers to Bob. For this fixed-point precision format, Alice sending each number to Bob is equivalent to her sending 3 consecutive messages to Bob, the first being the sign bit, then the number before the decimal, and finally the number after the decimal.

We also define the floating-point precision format as follows.

Definition A.8

(Floating-point precision)
.

We define the
floating-point precision
by a quadruple
\langle s_{a},a,s_{b},b\rangle
, where
a,b
are binary expression of integers and
s_{a},s_{b}
are the corresponding sign bits. The value represented by this is given by

\langle s_{a},a,s_{b},b\rangle=(-1)^{s_{a}}\times 1\cdot a\times 2^{(-1)^{s_{b}}\times b}.

We say that this requires
p

.

Here,
1\cdot a
is called the

and
b
is called the

Again, addition and multiplication of two numbers are first computed exactly, and then rounded off to the desired format of the floating point precision. Both addition and multiplication of more than two numbers follow left-associativity.

A.4
Self-attention Computation with Bounded Precision

In the bounded precision, we consider self-attention to be computed using left-associativity for both addition and multiplication. Denoting
Q:=XW^{Q}
as the query matrix and
K:=XW^{K}
as the key matrix, the output of self-attention can be seen as the value

D^{-1}AXW^{V},

where
A=e^{QK^{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
D
is a diagonal matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that
D_{i,i}=A_{i}\mathbf{1}_{n}
. The computation is performed as:

1.

First compute
Q:=XW^{Q}
,
K=XW^{K}
in bounded precision regime. For
i\in[n],j\in[d_{q}]
, this is performed as:

Q_{i,j}=\sum_{k\in[d]}X_{i,k}W^{Q}_{k,j},

where each of
X_{i,k}W^{Q}_{k,j}
is first computed in bounded precision regime, and then the summation is performed using left associativity. A similar thing is done to compute
K
.

2.

Similarly, we compute
QK^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

3.

Subsequently, we compute
A:=e^{QK^{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

4.

After this, we compute the normalizing term
D
from
A
.

5.

We then matrix multiply as:

D^{-1}AXW^{V}=D^{-1}((AX)W^{V}),

i.e., first compute
A
, store it and then compute
AX
, followed by computing
(AX)W^{V}
which gives the numerator of the softmax, followed by computing
D
from
A
to give the final output matrix. Here,
((AX)W^{V})
is the numerator term and
D
is the denominator term.

Lemma C.8

(Upper Bound)

There exists a Transformer which can compute the function

Proof.

The idea of the proof is similar to the previous construction, but with a slight modification where each token
\mathcal{T}_{m}^{3}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})_{i}
is a tuple
(\mathcal{T}_{m}^{3}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})^{1}_{i},\mathcal{T}_{m}^{3}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})^{2}_{i})

\mathcal{T}_{m}^{3}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})^{1}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{e-1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{m}^{3}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})_{i}^{2}\in\{0,1\}

Overview.

We again prove this upper bound on the function
T_{m}
by checking equality of the first half of
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{z}

\mathbf{y}_{1:{\lceil m/2\rceil}+\log{\lceil m/2\rceil}}

\mathbf{z}_{1:{\lceil m/2\rceil}+\log{\lceil m/2\rceil}}
(slightly more than half) in the numerator, and that of
y_{{\lceil m/2\rceil}+\log{\lceil m/2\rceil}:m}
and
\mathbf{z}_{{\lceil m/2\rceil}+\log{\lceil m/2\rceil}:m}

The idea is again similar to the previous constructions, where the numerator computes

1\cdot y_{e}\ldots y_{e+t-1}\times 2^{e^{1}_{y}}-1\cdot z_{e}\ldots z_{e+t-1}\times 2^{e^{1}_{z}},

along with a normalizing term to indicate when the first half if similar. The denominator will have terms from the first half which will be ignored, and the second half will be a fixed number
(1-2^{(t-1)})\times 2^{e^{2}_{y}}
if and only if the second half is also equal.

Numerator.

For computing the numerator, as before we compute each of
e^{\langle Q_{n+1}K_{i}\rangle}X_{i}W^{V}
by left-associative multiplication, after which we exponentiate.

From the embeddings, Step 1 gives
1\cdot 0\times 2^{e^{1}_{y}}
, after which Step 2 adds
y_{e+i-1}2^{-i}\times 2^{e^{1}_{y}}=y_{e+i-1}\times 1\cdot 0\times 2^{e^{1}_{y}-i}
, for
i\in[t-1]
, which is also in the permissible range. Therefore, Step 1 and 2 combined give
1\cdot y_{e}\ldots y_{e+t-2}\times 2^{e^{1}_{y}}
.

Steps 3-5 are zeros. Step 6 subtracts
1\cdot 0\times 2^{e^{1}_{z}}
from this. If
\mathbf{y}_{1:e-1}<\mathbf{z}_{1:e-1}
, then after Step 6, the value becomes negative (since
1\cdot 0\times 2^{e^{1}_{z}}>1\cdot y_{e}\ldots y_{e+t-2}\times 2^{2^{1}_{y}}
), and continues to stay negative as Step 7 essentially generates the number
-0\cdot z_{e}\ldots z_{e+t-2}\times 2^{e^{1}_{z}}
, after which everything else is zero. There is no loss due to rounding-off since
t-1<2^{e-1}
(since
e>\log{\lceil m/2\rceil}
and
t+e={\lceil m/2\rceil}+\log{\lceil m/2\rceil}
).

Now, assuming
e^{1}_{y}=e^{1}_{z}
, after Step 6, the summation becomes
0\cdot y_{e}\ldots y_{e+t-2}\times 2^{e^{1}_{y}}
, which is still within the range of the allowed precision bits because
t-1<2^{e-1}
. After this, in Step 7,
-z_{e+j}\times 2^{-j}\times 2^{e^{1}_{z}}
is added from this summation, for
j\in[t-1]
, which gives the number
(1\cdot y_{e}\ldots y_{e+t-2}-1\cdot z_{e}\ldots z_{e+t-2})\times 2^{e^{1}_{z}}
. If
\mathbf{y}_{e+t-2}=\mathbf{z}_{e+t-2}
, then this value is zero, otherwise it is some negative number (since
\mathbf{y}<\mathbf{z}
), with however a positive exponent (due to
t-1<2^{e-2}
).

Steps 8, 9, 11 add 0 to this, and Step 10 makes the summation equal to
1\cdot 0\times 2^{-e^{2}_{z}}
if
\mathbf{y}_{1:e+t-2}=\mathbf{z}_{e+t-2}
, otherwise some negative number (the exponent of
(1\cdot y_{e}\ldots y_{e+t-2}-1\cdot z_{e}\ldots z_{e+t-2})\times 2^{e^{1}_{z}}
is positive).

Denominator.

For denominator, we compute each of
e^{X_{n+1}W^{Q}(W^{K})^{T}X_{i}}
. Steps 1, 2 yield the summation as
t\times 1\cdot 0\times 2^{-(2^{e-1}-1)}=1\cdot 0\times 2^{\log t-(2^{e-1}-1)}
, where
1\cdot 0\times 2^{-(2^{e-1}-1)}
is the smallest possible positive number. To this, Step 3 adds
\bar{y}_{e+2t-2-\log{\lceil m/2\rceil}+j}2^{-(j)}\times 2^{-e^{2}_{y}}
, and along with Step 4, gives the number
0\cdot\bar{y}_{e+2t-\log{\lceil m/2\rceil}-1}\ldots\bar{y}_{m}\times 2^{-e^{2}_{y}}+1\cdot 0\times 2^{\log t-(2^{e-1}-1)}
. Now,
e^{2}_{y}
consists of
e-1-\log{\lceil m/2\rceil}
bits, which implies

\begin{split}&2^{e-1}-1-\log t-e^{2}_{y}>2^{e-1}-1-\log t-2^{e-1-\log{\lceil m/2\rceil}}-1\\
&=2^{e-2}+((1-\frac{1}{{\lceil m/2\rceil}})2^{e-2}-\log t-1)>t.\end{split}

This implies that
1\cdot 0\times 2^{\log t-(2^{e-1}-1)}
will be ignored while rounding off, giving the final value as
0\cdot\bar{y}_{e+2t-\log{\lceil m/2\rceil}-1}\ldots\bar{y}_{m}\times 2^{-e^{2}_{y}}
.

Step 5 simply adds zero and have been used as dummy tokens for notational ease.

Step 6-7 again forms
1\cdot 0\times 2^{\log t-(2^{e-1}-1)}
, which will be rounded off, and Step 8 adds
z_{e+2t-2-\log{\lceil m/2\rceil}+j}2^{-(j)}\times 2^{-e^{2}_{z}}
, which gives us
0\cdot\bar{y}_{e+2t-\log{\lceil m/2\rceil}-1}\ldots\bar{y}_{m}\times 2^{-e^{2}_{y}}+0\cdot z_{e+2t-\log{\lceil m/2\rceil}-1}\ldots z_{m}\times 2^{-e^{2}_{z}}
as the final answer.

Wrap up.

We study the following cases, by first computing the value of the output of the self-attention for the equality case. When
y_{1}\ldots,y_{m}=z_{1}\ldots z_{m}
:

•

The numerator will become
1\cdot y_{e}\ldots y_{t+e-2}\times 2^{e^{1}_{y}}-1\cdot z_{e}\ldots z_{t+e-2}\times 2^{e^{1}_{z}}+1\cdot 0\times 2^{-e^{2}_{z}}
, which is equal to just
1\cdot 0\times 2^{-e^{2}_{y}}
.

•

For the denominator, the value will essentially become
0\cdot\bar{y}_{t+2e-1-\log{\lceil m/2\rceil}}\ldots\bar{y}_{m}\times 2^{-e^{2}_{y}}+0\cdot z_{t+2e-1-\log{\lceil m/2\rceil}}\ldots z_{m}\times 2^{-e^{2}_{z}}
, which is equal to
1\cdot\underbrace{1\ldots 1}_{t-2}\times 2^{-(e^{2}_{z}+1)}
.

•

This gives the softmax as
\frac{1}{1-2^{-(t-1)}}
.

We now show that if equality does not hold, the self-attention’s output will always be different, and can be checked using the given bits of precision.

Case 1,
y_{1}\ldots y_{\lceil m/2\rceil}\neq z_{1}\ldots z_{\lceil m/2\rceil}
:

Case 1.1,
e^{1}_{y}<e^{1}_{z}
:

In this case, the value of the numerator will be negative as discussed before.

Case 1.2,
e^{1}_{y}=e^{1}_{z}
but
y_{e}\ldots y_{e+t-1}<z_{e}\ldots z_{e+t-1}
:

Again in this case, we the numerator will be negative.

Case 2,
y_{1}\ldots y_{\lceil m/2\rceil}=z_{1}\ldots z_{\lceil m/2\rceil}
but
y_{1}\ldots y_{m}\neq z_{1}\ldots z_{m}
:

In this case, the numerator will always be
1\cdot 0\times 2^{-e^{2}_{z}}
. Let us now analyze the denominator.

Case 2.1,
e^{2}_{y}<e^{2}_{z}
:

The denominator will be
0\cdot\bar{y}_{t+2e-1-\log{\lceil m/2\rceil}}\ldots\bar{y}_{m}\times 2^{-e^{2}_{y}}+0\cdot z_{t+2e-1-\log{\lceil m/2\rceil}}\ldots z_{m}\times 2^{-e^{2}_{z}}<1\cdot 1\times 2^{-e^{2}_{y}}
, and the final output as
\geq\frac{2^{e^{2}_{y}-e^{2}_{z}}}{1+2^{-1}}
, which is strictly greater than the equality case where the value is
\frac{1}{1-2^{-(t-1)}}
for
t>2
.

Case 2.2,
e^{2}_{y}=e^{2}_{z}
but
y_{t+2e-1-\log{\lceil m/2\rceil}}\ldots y_{m}
:

In this case, there will be a maximum
i_{0}
such that for all
i\in[t+2e-1-\log{\lceil m/2\rceil},i_{0}-1]
,
y_{i}=z_{i}
. Due to the condition that
\mathbf{y}<\mathbf{z}
, we must have
y_{i_{0}}=0
and
z_{i_{0}}=1
. Therefore, from the value of the denominator, the exponent
2^{-e^{2}_{y}}
, and for the mantissa, the summation will be
0\cdot\underbrace{1\ldots 1}_{i_{0}-1}\ldots\times 2^{-e^{2}_{y}}
, while for the
i_{0}
-th place after decimal, Step 3 and Step 8 will both give
2^{-i_{0}}\times 2^{-e^{2}_{y}}
. Therefore, the value of the denominator will be strictly greater than
1\cdot 1\ldots 1\times 2^{-e^{2}_{y}}
, and , the output of the self-attention will also be
<\frac{1}{1-2^{-(t-1)}}
. This will differ from the expected output at the
i_{0}
-th position after decimal. Since
i_{0}\leq t
, this can be checked.

Finally, we can again construct an MLP similar to the one used in the proof of Lemma
2.6
, containing only one hidden layer which has only 2 nodes, that returns 0 if the output of self-attention is not equal to
\frac{1}{2-2^{-(t-1)}}
, and
1
otherwise.
∎

Appendix D
Details of Experiments

In this section, we complete the details of the experiments. A one-layer Transformer was trained to learn the equality task, and then quantized to both fixed-point and floating-point precisions using PTQ. After this, each model was further fine-tuned using QAT to report the final accuracies. The experiments were performed over 10 seeds randomly generated.

The experiments were repeated for
4
values of
m
with
m=100,50,30,15
.

Input Generation.

For each
m
, the tokens are of length
n=2m+1
, where the first
m
tokens contain the first string
\mathbf{y}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
, the next
m
tokens contain the second string
\mathbf{z}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
, and the final token contains zero. The output is expected in the final token.

The strings are chosen such that:

1.

With probability
1/2
, the strings will be equal. In this case,
\mathbf{y}
is chosen uniformly at random and
\mathbf{z}
is set to
\mathbf{y}
.

2.

With the remaining
1/2
probability, the strings will be unequal. In this case,
\mathbf{y}
is chosen uniformly at random, and a set
P\subset[m]
of size
|P|=\lfloor 0.75m\rfloor
is also chosen uniformly at random. Then,
\mathbf{z}
is set of the string whose bits are equal to that of
\mathbf{y}
at indices
[m]\backslash P
, and are flipped at indices in
P
.

A one-layer Transformer was trained on this dataset, defined as exactly similar to Definition
A.2
. The Transformer had heads
H=2
, embedding dimension
