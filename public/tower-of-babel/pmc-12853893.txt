Predictors of VV-ECMO weaning safety: longitudinal ventilatory ratio assessment during sweep gas off trials, a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Elliott Worku, Feibi Yang, Kiran Shekar, Timothy Southwood, Richard Totaro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background Separation from venovenous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO) is a pivotal transition for patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). Impaired alveolar ventilation more so than oxygenation may be implicated in weaning failure. The ventilatory ratio (VR), a simple marker of patient ventilatory efficiency, correlates with physiological dead space and ARDS outcomes. We hypothesised that elevated VR measured during sweep gas off trials (SGOT) predicts unsafe liberation from VV-ECMO. Methods In this monocentric retrospective study, adult patients undergoing VV-ECMO for ARDS between July 2019 and August 2023 were recruited. Patients who died on ECMO or were palliatively separated were excluded. Physiological, ventilatory and ECMO data were extracted from the electronic medical record. VR was calculated as VE [minute ventilation] × PaCO₂/(predicted body weight × 100 × 37.5). Safe wean was defined as ECMO free survival without predefined “unsafe criteria” (hypoxia, hypercapnia with acidosis, escalations in mechanical ventilation, or haemodynamic deterioration) occurring during SGOT (sweep gas off trial) or within 48 h of ECMO separation.ResultsThirty-nine patients (median age 47 years; 76.9% male) were included. Twenty-four patients separated safely from ECMO following a single SGOT, the remaining 15 patients underwent 36 unsafe trials before decannulation, 69.4% of which failed due to hypercapnia. Safe wean trials were longer (19 [5.7–25.0]hr vs. 5 [0.3–13.3]hr; p = 0.01) and preceded by lower fractional delivered oxygen (FdO2) (65 [40–85]% vs. 100 [60–100]%; p = 0.03). During unsafe SGOT, VR was higher at both early (1.7 vs. 1.4; p = 0.02), and late time points (2.6 vs. 1.7; p = 0.01) in unsafe trials. Hypercapnia following separation was associated with prolonged post-ECMO ventilation (β = 1.4 [0.21–2.59]; p = 0.02). No deaths or re-initiations occurred within 48 h of ECMO removal.ConclusionsElevated ventilatory ratio during SGOT was associated with unsafe weaning from VV-ECMO in this retrospective cohort of patients with ARDS. Prospective validation is warranted to inform future weaning practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-025-03558-1.

Introduction

Venovenous Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV ECMO) supports respiratory gas exchange in patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), permitting reductions in ventilator induced and self-inflicted lung injury. Despite growing global experience in extracorporeal support, ARDS mortality and morbidity remain significant [1–4]. VV-ECMO initiation criteria typically pertain to impaired oxygenation, however derangements in physiological dead space may more closely align with ARDS mortality and ECMO outcomes [5–10].

Initially, high extracorporeal blood flow (ECBF) is often required to overcome venous admixture and facilitate ultraprotective ventilation [9, 11–16]. As improvements in lung compliance, gas exchange or radiological appearances are demonstrated, weaning is undertaken culminating in a sweep gas off trial (SGOT). This represents a critical juncture where respiratory work and gas exchange must be assumed solely and safely by the native lung. In a scoping review of VV ECMO liberation significant heterogeneity in both wean strategy and duration of SGOT was reported, reflecting the dearth of high-quality evidence to guide practice [17].

In a retrospective cohort of patients, 42% of sweep gas off trials (SGOT) were observed to fail [18]. This was observed to be primarily due to excessive respiratory frequency and effort, with higher end tidal to arterial carbon dioxide ratio indicating greater dead space in patients who failed [18]. Gerhardinger et al. reaffirmed the relevance of impaired alveolar ventilation with both higher PaCO2 and respiratory rate before failed wean trials [19]. The ventilatory ratio (VR) [20], has emerged as a dynamic bedside correlate of physiological dead space (Vd/VtPhys) [21–25] derived from routinely collected data [10, 26]. Values approximating 1 indicate normal ventilatory efficiency, with increasing values corresponding to greater derangements [20, 22, 24, 27]. This metric demonstrates independent association with ARDS mortality [25, 28] after adjusting for PEEP and PaO2:FiO2 (PF) ratio [29]. The evolution of VR following intubation is negatively prognostic [23, 25, 29]. Specifically addressing ECMO cohorts, Al Fares reported significantly higher VR values in patients who developed unsafe criteria during SGOT [30], which corresponded to greater respiratory effort as measured by oesophageal manometry [31–33].

We hypothesised that higher levels of ECMO support prior to SGOT, and higher VR evaluated during SGOT may each predict unsafe wean from VV-ECMO in ARDS patients.

Methods

Patients and study design

Study approval was granted by the Sydney Local Health District Ethics Review Committee (2023/ETH0192). This retrospective, non-randomised study was conducted in a tertiary intensive care unit of a university-affiliated hospital that provides a high volume ECMO service. All consecutive adult patients receiving VV ECMO for ARDS between July 2019 and August 2023 were evaluated, (supplement Fig. 1) including patients cannulated elsewhere and subsequently retrieved. Hybrid configurations involving arterial return (e.g. V-AV ECMO) were removed, as were patients receiving ECMO primarily for hypercapnic respiratory failure without evidence of ARDS. Patients who died during ECMO support or those separated with palliative intent were not analysed.

VV-ECMO initiation was according to published criteria [34], and consensus from multidisciplinary consultation (respiratory physician, anaesthetist/perfusionist, admitting medical officer, ECMO intensivist, general intensivist) taking into account trajectory of illness, anticipated recovery, comorbidities and functional status at baseline.

Cannulation was undertaken by a locally accredited ECMO intensivist [35], with femoro-jugular configuration favoured. Timing and conduct of wean was not explicitly protocolised, however, patients were reviewed daily by the ECMO team to guide this process (supplement).

Data collection

Data was anonymised and registered in an Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft Corporation, Albuquerque, NM, USA). Baseline characteristics, illness severity scores, ECMO parameters, ventilation parameters, vasoactive support, and arterial blood gas analysis were extracted from the electronic medical record (Philips IntelliSpace Critical Care and Anaesthesia [ICCA]). Outcome data was extracted from the hospital wide electronic medical record. The ventilatory ratio is automatically calculated and reported in ICCA following each arterial blood gas using the formula VE [minute ventilation] × PaCO₂/(predicted body weight × 100 × 37.5) [20].

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the description of risk factors associated with unsafe SGOT or unsafe decannulation. We additionally evaluated whether unsafe liberation from VV ECMO influenced patient outcomes following decannulation.

Safe wean was defined as successful liberation from VV-ECMO, without the emergence of ‘unsafe criteria’ during SGOT or following decannulation.

Unsafe criteria during the SGOT broadly aligned with published criteria [30]; hypoxia (documented or desaturation < 90%), hypercapnia > 45mmHg associated with change to ventilation or acidosis (pH < 7.3), transition from spontaneous to assisted or mandatory ventilation, increase in delivered airway pressure, and haemodynamic instability, defined as new vasopressor requirement or increasing vasoactive inotropic score [36]. Unsafe criteria in the post-decannulation period were as above, with the addition of need for rescue prone positioning, neuromuscular blockade, or death/re-initiation within 48 h.

Patient characteristics

A total of 231 patients received ECMO support during the study period, 67 of whom underwent VV ECMO for ARDS. After excluding 28 patients (41.8%) who died on ECMO, or were palliatively separated, 39 remained for analysis. Patients either separated after an initial ‘safe’ SGOT (n = 24) and continued to remain safe following decannulation, or breached safety criteria during SGOT +/- the post decannulation period. Thus, all unsafe trials occurred in the unsafe group of patients (n = 15). Ten patients breached safety criteria during wean trials and following separation, whilst 3 of the remaining patients underwent a single unsafe SGOT then separated with resolution of unsafe criteria and 2 patients underwent multiple unsafe SGOT which resolved following decannulation.(Figure S1.) All 36 unsafe SGOT in these 15 patients were analysed collectively, as subgroups were of insufficient size for meaningful inference. Median age of the study population was 47 (39.5–59) and 76.9% were male (Table 1).

There were no differences in pre ECMO duration of mechanical ventilation; 1(1–4.8.8) days, baseline SOFA score; 9 [7–10.5.5]), ECMO duration 9(6–17) days, nor rates of immunosuppression and COVID-19 infection (Table 1) between safe and unsafe wean patient groups. Bacterial pneumonia was significantly more common in patients in the unsafe group, OR 0.09 (0.002–0.772) p = 0.01 (Table 2). Neuromuscular blockade was employed in 33% of the overall cohort, and pre-ECMO cardiac arrest occurred in 5 patients (Table 2). No patients died within 48 h of decannulation, nor did any undergo a second run of ECMO during index admission. Neither length of stay nor mortality differed between groups (Table 1). Analysis by sweep gas off trial

Sixty SGOT were analysed, 24 in the safe patient group, and 36 in the unsafe patient group. Median time to first wean trial was 8 (5–16.25.25) days (Table S1), and similar between groups. Fifty SGOT (83.3%) were undertaken during ventilation via endotracheal tube, and 10 trials (16.7%) during tracheostomy ventilation. (Table S2)

The most common ventilatory mode was pressure control – assist control (38.5%), whilst 23% of SGOT were conducted during pressure support ventilation. No patients were extubated on ECMO.

Patients in the safe group (n = 24) were separated following a single SGOT after 8 (5–13.5.5) ECMO days (Table 1). Fifteen patients in the unsafe patient group (n = 15) underwent a median 2 (2–3.5.5) SGOT to liberate at 10 (6.5–18.5) ECMO days p = 0.25. Three of these 15 patients decannulated after a single unsafe SGOT, with the remaining 12 patients undergoing 33 unsafe trials (figure S1). Prolonged weaning (≥ 3 SGOT prior to separation) [37] was observed in one third of patients in the ‘unsafe’ group; 5 patients (12.8% of the total cohort) (Fig. 2). Median SGOT duration was longer in the safe wean group, 19 (5.7–25.0) Vs 5 (0.3–13.3) hours p = 0.01.(Table S3).Fig. 2Frequency of SGOT undertaken by number of patients. 1 trial = 27 pts (69.2%), 2 trials = 7 pts (17.9%), 3 trials = 1 pt (2.6%), 4 trials = 4 pts (10.3%)

Hypercapnia was the prevailing ‘unsafe’ criterion during SGOT (figure S1,Table S4), occurring in 69.4% of SGOT. Hypoxia was less commonly encountered (16.7%). Safety breaches occurring in the post decannulation epoch were also predominantly due to hypercapnia (70%), or a requirement for increased ventilation intensity (higher driving pressure, pressure support/change from spontaneous to mandatory mode).

Oxygenation did not differ before or during SGOT (Table S1). Higher PaCO2 (OR 1.16, 95% CI 1.04–1.33; p = 0.02), and lower pH were associated with unsafe wean (OR 6.8 × 10− 10, 95% CI 2.09 × 10⁻¹⁷ – 6.1 × 10⁻⁴; p = 0.01). (Table 2). Age, SOFA score, and pre ECMO duration of mechanical ventilation were not predictive of wean safety in multivariate analysis, neither were fluid balance, nor vasoactive inotropic scores. When adjusted for age and SOFA score before the start of weaning trial, higher BMI was protective (OR 0.9116, 95% CI: 0.8269–0.9956; p = 0.047).

Ventilator ratio – optimal thresholds

We examined early (VRT1) and late (VRT2) VR values and calculated optimal cutoffs for predicting unsafe wean under the receiver-operating characteristic curves. Early VRT1 >1.8 (sensitivity 83%, specificity 63%, AUC 0.72) was sensitive for identifying patients at risk of unsafe wean, and late VRT2 (1.6, sensitivity 47%, specificity 92%, AUC 0.7) was more specific, suggesting safe ECMO separation if values did not exceed 1.6. Secondary outcomes

Ventilator days post separation, ICU and hospital length of stay did not differ by wean safety. ECMO free survival at 48 h was 100%, and in hospital mortality was 10.3% (8.3% Vs 13.3% p = 0.63) (Table 1). In univariate analysis, hypercapnia post decannulation (> 46mmHg) was associated with increased ICU LOS, but not duration of mechanical ventilation following ECMO. (table S5)

In multivariate linear regression adjusted for age, BMI, SOFA score, pH, PaO2, PaCO2, cumulative fluid balance and ECMO duration, elevated PaCO2 after separation was associated with increased ventilator days following ECMO removal; ß coefficient 1.4 (0.21–2.59) p = 0.02. (table S6) Higher dynamic driving pressure at time of SGOT (Paw-PEEP) [30] was associated with fewer ventilator days post ECMO decannulation ß−3.6(−6.43 to −0.78) p = 0.01, however no significant effect of VR on secondary outcomes was observed.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of 39 patients with ARDS receiving, and subsequently separated from VV-ECMO, a majority achieved safe liberation following initial SGOT. However, safety breaches for the remaining patients during SGOT largely reflected impaired native lung ventilation, and continued post separation from ECMO. Patients in the unsafe group underwent a greater number of SGOT, with 13% patients overall undergoing prolonged weaning trials (≥ 3 SGOT) [19]. Elevated VR at both the start and end of SGOT associated with unsafe wean, reflecting the predominance of impairments in ventilation rather than oxygenation in this context. Hypercapnia following decannulation, the corollary of impaired ventilation, was associated with longer mechanical ventilation and increased ICU length of stay [38]. Fischbach reported similar increases in resource utilisation following unsafe weaning [39], and both ECMO duration and mortality was significantly higher in other patient series [30, 40]. Whilst patients in our unsafe group did not suffer excess mortality, weaning before normalisation of physiological dead space may still prematurely and deleteriously burden patients. Safe wean trials were observed exclusively in the ‘safe’ patient group and were both longer in duration and preceded by lower fractional delivered oxygen (FdO2). Bacterial pneumonia was a more common aetiology amongst the unsafe cohort, but higher BMI was protective. Higher respiratory rate and dynamic driving pressures were observed prior to SGOT in those patients who weaned safely. No patients died in the immediate post decannulation period, and in hospital mortality was 10.3%, comparable to rates in a recent ELSO registry [2].

The association of bacterial pneumonia and unsafe weaning may reflect the underlying ARDS phenotype. Pneumonic, or direct ARDS can produce diffuse rather than focal patterns of radiological involvement and high elastance disease [41, 42]. Elevated VR is found in such cases and may thus be a marker of a latent class of particular radiological and histopathological characteristics [43, 44].

The findings regarding oxygenator blend fraction before SGOT are interesting. ELSO guidance recommends consideration of blending sweep gas prior to undertaking a 2–3 h long SGOT [45], however this is neither backed by high quality evidence nor universally practiced [17].

Blending may reduce the total membrane transfer of oxygen and pulmonary arterial saturations [46, 47] (lumb physiology), permitting some reemergence of hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (LUMB), which is rapidly attenuated upon VV-ECMO initiation [48]. Assessing patient tolerance to reductions in FdO2 might also identify patients susceptible to early SGOT failure due to hypoxaemia, avoiding inappropriately protracted wean trials and potentially precipitous decompensations [49]. Of course, inferences that lower FdO2 facilitated dissociation of extracorporeal oxygenation from CO2 removal must be reconciled in the context of ECBF, native cardiac output, membrane lung functioning, and any recirculation present, none of which are accounted for in this study [50, 51]. Lastly, blending may offer adjunctive benefits, such as permitting higher circuit flows to be maintained during the weaning phase [47], potentially mitigating the high rates of patient thrombosis reported by Gerhardinger et al. in the context of ECBF weans to 1.5LPM [19].

Higher BMI associated with greater odds of safe wean, supporting the ‘obesity paradox’ [52]. Higher driving pressure reduced ventilator days in multivariate analysis, but absolute values did not exceed ‘unsafe’ thresholds in either patient group [15, 53]. The mediation of driving pressure on outcomes elucidated by Amato et al. [15], may be less relevant in patients with obese habitus [54, 55]. This may be accounted for by an increased partitioning of pressures by chest wall elastance, such that higher driving pressure may be required to avert cyclical atelectasis [56].

Observational data supports limiting mechanical power delivery under ECMO, yet practice varies substantially with few centres adhering to explicit protocols [57, 58]. Aggressive reductions in driving pressure and ultraprotective tidal volumes ≤ 4 ml/Kg [59, 60], may initially be targeted under ECMO but risk engendering atelectasis [61, 62]. Thus, increasing ventilation intensity prior to SGOT may be critical to facilitate the transition from membrane to native lung [47]. Higher respiratory frequency before SGOT was also associated with safe liberation, possibly due to less abrupt transition of CO2 elimination between the membrane and native lung [63], or by mitigation of atelectasis and pulmonary shunt that lower respiratory frequency and mean airway pressure may incur [64, 65].

In a previous study by Al-Fares, despite median SGFR (unblended, FdO2 1.0) of 1LPM prior to SGOT, and a protracted SGOT lasting 25 h; the resulting wean rate was 70%, with 25% of patients developing unsafe criteria following decannulation [30]. In contrast, 65% of patients in our cohort safely weaned safely at first SGOT, also encountering no unsafe criteria in the intervening period following separation. While our ECBF was comparable, our SGFR was higher (median 3.5 L safe group) albeit blended (65%; 40–85%). We observed a shorter time to decannulation. However, inferences are potentially confounded by our lower baseline severity of illness (SOFA 9 Vs 11), and shorter pre ECMO duration of mechanical ventilation (1 day Vs 3 days).

Protocolised daily assessments for SGOT [37, 66], and iterative weaning practices have been proposed to expedite ECMO liberation [40, 67, 68]. With this approach, Gannon reported a 52% rate of wean trial failure (median 4 min), with hypoxia cited frequently [37]. This suggests premature wean trials occurred in patients who were demonstrably dependent on ECMO for oxygenation. In contrast to protocolised extubation, where an ‘optimal’ failure rate has been described, ECMO is unarguably more invasive, and separation is an ‘all or nothing transition’ not readily reinstated [26, 69]. Premature decannulation may therefore carry substantial morbidity and resource implications [70, 71].

A recent scoping review exposes substantial heterogeneity in SGOT conduct, with trials ranging from 30 min to over 6 h across 39 studies [17]. No second runs were reported in studies with longer SGOT, whilst recannulation rate was as high as 7.1% following the shortest trials, with post ECMO mortality circa. 20% [37, 72, 73]. Our zero reinitiation rate and low post decannulation mortality (10.3%) is reported in the context of a median SGOT duration of 6.5 h.

In our study, unsafe SGOT were shorter than safe trials; 5 (0.3–13.3) Vs 19 (5.7–25.0) hours p = 0.01 (Table S3). Al Fares conversely observed the converse (24 h safe Vs 20 h unsafe) [30]. In both cases a unified account is that prolonged SGOT with longitudinal observations enrich clinician confidence and decision making.

VR measured approximately 1 h into SGOT > 1.8 demonstrated sensitivity for unsafe wean, whilst a later VR (approximately 6 h) above 1.6 was highly specific (> 90%). However, these cutoff values were derived within the same population and dataset risking overfitting of the data. Fischbasch observed a similar threshold value during a 30-minute SGOT (VR > 1.75) [39]. Al Fares reported a higher cutoff, VR > 2.3 at approximately 4 h with 100% sensitivity, but lower (81%) specificity for unsafe liberation [30]. (Table S7) The early VR value is well aligned to ours despite differing populations, institutional practices and weaning conduct. Prospective validation of these results is required to help inform future weaning protocols.

Strengths

Our results support the utility of monitoring ventilator ratio longitudinally during SGOT and suggests that blending may decrease the burden of hypoxia during wean trials.

Even early VR elevations may signal that unsafe criteria are likely following ECMO removal and thus provide grounds to abort the current SGOT and optimise the patient prior to further wean trials. This effect was found despite differences in patient and ECMO management between practitioners locally, and over time, and support those reported by others.

Limitations

While the ventilator ratio is a strong correlate of Vd/Vtphys [20], it is also influenced by V’CO2 and admixture [28], the relative significance of which cannot be determined retrospectively.

We excluded patients who undertook SGOT yet died on VV-ECMO, as these patients often succumb to extrapulmonary organ injuries or from decisions to withdraw life sustaining therapy, however a mortality association for VR may have been found had these patients been analysed. There is a risk of survivorship bias, such that we cannot extend our findings to outcomes in all VV-ECMO patients. Unfortunately, we are also unable to comment on the importance of ventilatory efficiency in patients who did not undergo SGOT, as this relies upon the membrane contribution to carbon dioxide being negated. We treated each SGOT as an independent test, this may inflate the potential for type 1 errors. Whilst we attempted to limit population heterogeneity by limiting the study to patients with ARDS, there are inherent differences in disease course and treatment responses between disease sub-phenotypes which future study should aim to address. Lastly, understanding the relevance of VR and respiratory effort in awake, ambulatory patients is critical as ECMO practices evolve.

Conclusions

Higher ventilator ratio measured longitudinally predicted unsafe weaning from VV-ECMO. This metric should be assessed in diverse patient cohorts and its prognostic relevance understood in the context of different ECMO services.

Optimal ECMO weaning strategies and patient management, including anticipatory changes in mechanical ventilation prior to sweep gas off trials need to be explored in high quality prospective studies.
