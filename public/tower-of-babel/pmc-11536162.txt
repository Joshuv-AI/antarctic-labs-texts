Reply to Masters: Recombinant coronavirus balances biosafety and benefit
Authors: Jonathan W. Yewdell
Published: 2024

We recently described a replication-competent recombinant OC43 human coronavirus (CoV) with the natural spike (S) glycoprotein replaced with severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) S (rOC43-CoV2 S) and demonstrated its advantages for measuring SARS-CoV2 neutralizing antibodies and potential as an attenuated vaccine (1). Based on the low pathogenicity of OC43 in humans and our documented attenuation of rOC43-CoV2 S in cultured cell lines, human airway epithelial cells, mice, and monkeys, the NIH biosafety committee designated rOC43-CoV2 S as a Biosafety Level 2 (BSL-2) agent. While fully acknowledging our compliance with Center for Disease Control/NIH/Office of Science Policy biosafety policies (2, 3), Masters expresses “significant concerns” with the possible evolution of rOC43-CoV2 S as a human pathogen (4).

Masters is concerned about rOC43-CoV2 S as a “totally novel human CoV.” While rOC43-CoV2 S is undisputedly novel, we noted (1) that SARS-CoV-2, as a typical CoV, exhibits robust genome recombination and that individuals are frequently coinfected with SARS-CoV-2 and OC43 (5–7) and are likely to generate unfit recombinants unable to successfully compete with parent viruses. The risks of working with rOC43-CoV2 S are further reduced by widespread robust human T cell immunity to OC43 and neutralizing antibodies to SARS-CoV-2 (8).

Masters argues that the attenuation of rOC43-CoV2 S is related to our failure to appropriately modify the spike endodomain, resulting in the selection of mutations that improve viral fitness upon passaging. While this is a valid point in isolation, our minimally changing SARS-CoV-2 spike is advantageous in generating a highly attenuated virus with low potential for pathogenic adaptation. The natural evolution of OC43 as an upper respiratory virus with corresponding optimal growth at 35 °C further reduces the risk of rOC43 evolving into a lower respiratory pathogen, particularly since rOC43 is optimally propagated at 35 °C in vitro. Further, we have no intention of serially passaging rOC43-CoV2 S in animals or human airway epithelial cultures, which theoretically could result in the selection of pathogenic variants.

Masters points out that OC43 possesses gene products absent from SARS-CoV-2 that “could augment in vivo infection by rOC43-CoV2 S or its evolved variants.” While possible, viruses evolve to maximize replication in cell types that enable interhost transmission. Receptor-based redirection of a virus to a different cell type will likely entail high fitness costs that require multiple mutations to overcome.

Myriad replicating recombinant viruses expressing exotic viral receptor proteins have been generated since their introduction 40 y ago (9) and used safely under BSL-2 practices. A replicating recombinant-receptor replacement vesicular stomatitis virus is a safe and effective Ebola vaccine administered to over 350,000 individuals. Generating recombinant viruses has risks, but these have been effectively mitigated by established biosafety practices and mandated reviews of proposed projects by institute review entities (2, 3, 10, 11). The COVID-19 pandemic demonstrates the dangers of emerging viruses and the tangible benefits of decades of research advances in understanding viruses and antiviral immunity, many of which relied on recombinant virus research. While there are potential risks of recombinant virus research, these risks must, and in this case are, balanced against the value of recombinant viruses as reagents, vaccines, therapeutics, and essential tools for understanding, preventing, and treating viral diseases.
