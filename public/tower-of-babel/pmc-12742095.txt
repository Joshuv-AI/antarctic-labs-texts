Evaluating the impact of the risk-mitigation guidance for opioid prescribing in British Columbia, Canada using a cross-model comparison approach: study protocol
Authors: Mallory J Flynn, Hareem Mustafa, Benjamin Enns, Morgan Karugaba, Addie Carter, Brenda Carolina Guerra-Alejos, Amanda Slaunwhite, Bohdan Nosyk, Michael A Irvine
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction Drug poisoning, caused predominantly by fentanyl in the unregulated drug supply, is the leading cause of death among persons 10–59 years in British Columbia (BC), Canada. In March 2020, in response to the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic, the province of BC released the Risk Mitigation Guidance (RMG) as a clinical tool for physicians and nurse practitioners, allowing prescribers to provide selective withdrawal management medications, such as hydromorphone, dextroamphetamine, diazepam and others, as a legal and regulated supply of pharmaceutical alternatives to individuals who were at-risk of COVID-19 and overdose. In July 2021, the government of BC released the prescribed safer supply (PSS) policy, extending the scope beyond the COVID-19 pandemic and initial medications offered under the RMG. Recent studies have shown clear benefits among people with a diagnosed opioid use disorder who were prescribed PSS, in reducing mortality, as well as improving retention on opioid agonist treatment for those who were coprescribed PSS medications. The objective of the analysis detailed in this protocol is to use a cross-model comparison approach, comparing two independently developed models which are currently used in public health institutions in BC, to estimate the impact of this policy on opioid overdose-related mortality, while also considering the potential negative impacts of PSS medication diversion to those who are opioid naïve. This project will add to the limited evidence-base on the population-level impact of pharmaceutical alternatives interventions to date.Methods and analysisWe have identified two appropriate mathematical models to evaluate the impact of PSS on the number of opioid overdose-related deaths within BC from the inception of the programme (March 2020) until December 2022. We will use recently established guidelines on conducting a cross-model comparison to identify structural and parameter differences between the models and perform adaptation steps to generate the counterfactual scenarios. These will include creating additional health states for the population representing individuals receiving PSS, and parameterising the overdose risk, mortality and retention in the new compartments from a comprehensive population-level data set. Harmonisation will be conducted to ensure that both models evaluate the same scenarios with the same data. Further sensitivity analyses will be conducted to consider alternative counterfactual scenarios and changes to the population following the implementation of the intervention.Ethics and disseminationThis study is exempt from research ethics board review, as outlined in the Tri-Council Policy Statement, because it relies on data that is available in the public domain and there is no possibility of identifying individual persons. Results of the model validation analysis will be distributed through peer-reviewed journals and knowledge translation materials posted on the websites of the BC Centre for Disease Control and Centre for Advancing Health Outcomes.Registrationhttps://osf.io/kju2p/overview.

Introduction

Officially declared a public health emergency in 2016,1 the overdose crisis in British Columbia (BC), Canada has been driven by an increasingly toxic drug supply, resulting in over 2500 opioid-related overdose deaths in 2023.2 Since 2017, over 80% of unregulated drug deaths have involved fentanyl or other synthetic opioids, and fentanyl was detected in 85% of deaths in 2023.2 In response to the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic, the province of BC released the ‘Risk Mitigation in the Context of Dual Health Emergencies—Interim Clinical Guidance’ (RMG) as a clinical tool for physicians and nurse practitioners,3 which allowed prescribers to provide select medications for non-indicated purposes, such as hydromorphone, amphetamines, benzodiazepines and other classes, as a legal and regulated supply of pharmaceutical alternatives in tablet form, to individuals who were at-risk of COVID-19 and overdose, experiencing withdrawal symptoms from opioids, stimulants, benzodiazepines or alcohol.2 4 RMG medications could be dispensed for up to 30 days and were available with or without both supervised ingestion or concurrent opioid agonist treatment (OAT).2 A notable decrease in both all-cause and overdose-related mortality in the week following RMG opioid dispensations for one or more days has previously been observed.5 Among individuals with a diagnosed opioid use disorder (OUD), 7.6% received opioid prescriptions through RMG between March 2020 and August 2021, with a substantial surge in dispensations noted from March to May 2020.5 Individuals receiving RMG opioid dispensations for four or more days experienced lower mortality,5 and were 46% more likely to continue OAT engagement in the subsequent week.6 In July 2021, the government of BC released the prescribed safer supply (PSS) policy, extending the scope beyond the COVID-19 pandemic and initial medications offered under the RMG. Despite the limited uptake, currently available evidence shows lower rates of overdose for people dispensed opioid RMG, with clinical and client experiences and advocacy groups finding that RMG used as prescribed alternatives averted mortality risks from the unregulated drug supply.5 7

Research objective and questions: The objective of this analysis is to estimate the population-level impact of PSS policy changes within BC from March 2020 to December 2022, using a cross-model comparison between two independently developed simulation models. The period of the study was selected to include the start of the programme, as well as the expansion as PSS to the end of the most recently available data. The main outcomes will include the number of deaths averted by PSS compared with a counterfactual scenario with no intervention. We aim to quantify the population-level benefits of PSS, which include reduced mortality rates among people with OUD and improved retention on OAT with concurrent PSS prescription, while also estimating the potential for population-level harms such as increased OUD incidence resulting from the diversion of PSS medication to individuals who are opioid naïve. This study leverages estimates produced by these two independently validated models through an extended temporal scope of previous work in BC,5 encompassing later phases of the RMG programme and subsequent adaptations of safer-supply prescribing through PSS, then comparing model estimates and policy effects. Key methodological insights into how model design influences population-level impact estimates will also be gained, enabling an analysis of robustness and consistency of these frameworks.

Methods and analysis

Current mathematical models used to inform overdose response activities in BC

This study will focus on adapting two existing mathematical models that have been used to estimate the impact of several different interventions for OUD, such as naloxone distribution and OAT to estimate the impact of PSS policy changes in BC.8 The study period extends from March 2020 to December 2022, covering the inception of the RMG through the most recent available provincial data. Data extraction and analysis will occur from January to February 2026.

The Overdose Response Model (British Columbia Response to Overdose Model (BCROM)) is a Bayesian hierarchical model designed to incorporate a latent time-dependent rate of fentanyl adulteration in the unregulated drug supply.9 It provides monthly estimates for each health region, focusing on critical metrics such as the overdose rate, fatality risk from untreated overdoses, naloxone usage and the use of overdose prevention services.9 Counterfactual analyses are performed to evaluate the impact of different interventions on overdose-related outcomes. This involves sampling the posterior values of the overdose rate, fatality risk and naloxone use probability, and modifying the probability model to exclude each intervention individually and in combination.10 This model has proven instrumental in several key areas; it has been used to evaluate the impact of the BC Take Home Naloxone programme on reducing overdose-related deaths11 and has been used to assess the combined impact of the BC Take Home Naloxone programme and overdose prevention sites (OPS).9 Furthermore, the model has been used by the BC Centre for Disease Control to generate quarterly reports detailing the impact of these combined interventions across BC.9 The model is able to provide detailed, region-specific insights into the effectiveness of overdose prevention strategies, making it a valuable tool for policymakers and public health officials.

The Opioid Treatment Evaluation Model (OTEM) is a semi-Markov cohort model designed to assess the cost-effectiveness of various treatment scenarios for individuals with OUD. Unlike a traditional Markov model, it incorporates time-dependent transition probabilities based on health state residence or patient history and calendar time (eg, changing overdose risk due to an increase in the prevalence of fentanyl and other contaminants). The model population can be stratified by key subgroups of interest, such as injection and non-injection opioid users, HIV/HCV/coinfection status and individual health phases, such as initial diagnosis, intermediate complications and advanced health states, each with different degrees of severity and associated healthcare needs. This model has been used to estimate the cost-effectiveness of diacetylmorphine compared with methadone for individuals with chronic opioid dependence who are refractory to treatment,12 OAT programmes in California,13 and take-home buprenorphine vs methadone for treating prescription-type OUD in Canada.14

For the proposed application, we will adapt both the BCROM and the OTEM to integrate PSS, allowing the models to evaluate the impact of this intervention. We will then conduct a cross-model comparison of results to provide structural model insights, and an intermodel range of effect estimates for the PSS intervention. Our analysis plan is organised into four implementation stages, and will adhere to best-practice guidelines for conducting multi-model comparisons, as outlined by ISPOR and den Boon et al.10 15 As these guidelines are based on immunisation-based models and policy questions involving mechanistic disease models, we have adapted them to this study by translating the policy question into a research question through stakeholder engagement, and have identified the appropriate models to address this research question. We divide the rest of the analytic plan into: (1) adaptation of models to the research question, (2) harmonisation of parameter inputs and outputs, (3) sensitivity analyses and (4) presentation of results.

Implementation Phase 1: adapting and harmonising steps for cross-model comparison

The models used in this analysis will be adapted from their original applications to assess the impact of PSS policy changes. The objective of this analysis is not to determine which model performs better, but rather to assess robustness and consistency of population-level results across two validated models currently used in BC public-health decision-making for distinct purposes. Comparing outcomes through a cross-model comparison framework therefore provides an immediate, policy-relevant opportunity to understand model-specific biases and strengths. Both OTEM and BCROM track individuals currently receiving OAT through an OAT engagement state, incorporating both initiation and retention rates. New health states added for this analysis will include PSS alone, as well as PSS coprescribed with OAT. We will also include health states that track individuals using diverted PSS medication, including both individuals with a diagnosed OUD who could not legally access PSS prescriptions, as well as individuals who were opioid naïve and initiated use through diverted PSS. Models will be calibrated to reflect the epidemiological and healthcare scenarios in BC during the relevant period. Intervention parameters related to PSS, including dosage, frequency and adherence rates, will be estimated from linked population-level data on all individuals receiving PSS. Key intervention effectiveness parameters will be derived from recent studies estimating reductions in risk of mortality,5 as well as improvements in OAT retention with PSS coprescription.6 Each model will be validated independently using established procedures; however, the main validation approach is the comparison between the model outcomes in the two rounds. In the first round, we also explore model structure of the two models and will update with a second round of harmonisation if outcomes produced are inconsistent with each other.

Implementation Phase 2: harmonisation of parameter inputs and outputs

Broadly, the study period (March 2020–Dec 2022), geographical location (ie, BC) and population at risk size estimates will be standardised across models to maintain consistency. Harmonising model outcomes is also critical to performing a cross-model comparison. Both the BCROM and OTEM are designed to produce fatal overdoses as an outcome metric, enabling direct comparability between both models’ outputs.

Both models will be calibrated to data from the same population, ensuring consistency in demographic and clinical characteristics. Baseline mortality rates, overdose event rates and treatment efficacy data will be harmonised, as well as parameters related to the setting, disease characteristics, population demographics and interventions. Efforts will also be made to ensure consistency between both models by aligning time periods and estimates of the at-risk population size.16 Table 1 lists parameters of interest and outlines their specific incorporation into each model. The modelling of impacts of interventions (eg, naloxone) are left to the discretion of each team, and will form the necessary heterogeneity in model structure. The harmonisation process will be iterative; an initial round of harmonisation will align the primary elements of the models, followed by an optional, subsequent round, allowing for adjustments based on findings from the initial comparison. This iterative approach ensures that any discrepancies are addressed, and the models remain aligned in terms. Results of both stages of the harmonisation process will be included in the study results.

Implementation Phase 3: sensitivity analyses

General sensitivity analysis

This phase explores types of uncertainty to ensure robust and reliable model outcomes. Both models will account for randomness and variability inherent in real-world events, by estimating a distribution of outcomes rather than a single deterministic result. BCROM is a Bayesian model and the outcome uncertainty is represented using the posterior predictive distribution, and outcomes from the OTEM are derived from the distribution of outcomes estimated from a large number of Monte Carlo simulations, where parameter values are drawn from distributions rather than point estimates. OTEM parameter uncertainty will be explored through a deterministic, one-way sensitivity analysis of all model parameters. This analysis varies each parameter individually to observe the influence of individual parameters on model outcomes, thereby highlighting the most influential parameters. BCROM is a fully Bayesian model and the resulting posterior distribution incorporates uncertainty from both parameters and data observations. Parameter uncertainty will be explored through comparison between prior and posterior marginal distributions for each parameter. Informed and weakly informed priors will be varied by increasing the width of their distributions and comparing the impact on the model outcomes.17 Where Poisson-distributed outcomes are used, we will also explore the suitability of negative-binomial specifications as an alternative in the case of overdispersion (eg, Vuong test), and through use of the posterior predictive checks; the most appropriate distribution will then be used for analysis. Through sensitivity analysis of certain key assumptions, we can evaluate the robustness of these models, ensuring that their results are not unduly influenced by specific parameter values.18 19

Structural sensitivity analysis on the estimated benefits of PSS, and potential harms resulting from diversion

The first PSS-specific sensitivity analysis will focus on input parameters for the primary effects estimated for individuals receiving PSS prescriptions, including the estimated benefit on mortality outcomes, improvement in OAT treatment retention and diversion-related incident OUD. We will vary model parameters within empirically estimated ranges from the source studies, as well as using subgroup and sensitivity analysis estimates from these studies as model inputs, to explore the full range of potential effect estimates.

The second sensitivity analysis will focus on parameters related to diversion of PSS medications. This involves varying key parameters such as the proportion of medication diverted, the likelihood of non-medical use and the impact of diversion on treatment outcomes. The models assume that diversion influences OUD incidence rates based on the extent and type of diversion. A proportion of diverted PSS medications are assumed to be redirected to the population with OUD who could not legally access PSS via prescription, while others are assumed to be diverted to individuals who are opioid naïve. Some subset of the latter population is then assumed to develop OUD over time, so that the overall proportion of the population developing OUD as a result of diverted PSS medications is multiplicative in these parameters. By contrast, PSS medications diverted to the existing OUD population are assumed to have no effect, with potential protective effects if the diverted PSS medication is replacing some or all unregulated opioid use.

Ranges for parameters related to diversion will be informed by self-reported survey data collected prospectively from individuals receiving PSS prescriptions, and/or literature estimates. We will report model outcomes when varying parameters within these ranges, and explore outcomes in threshold sensitivity analyses, where we increase or decrease parameters until outcomes from each scenario are equivalent (or the alternative scenario is favoured). Estimates for the amount of diversion resulting in incident OUD cases required to mitigate the benefits of PSS prescriptions in reducing mortality for individuals with OUD will be explored, and compared against available data on the extent of diversion. This will be an important empirical finding in and of itself, given the lack of quality data on diversion. In addition, longer-term temporal effects of the combined effects of diverted PSS medications on the size of the OUD population will be estimated. Identifying which diversion-related parameters have the most significant impact on key outcomes may help inform meaningful programme change, intervention strategy and/or policy.20 A summary of the proposed sensitivity analyses and the impacted model parameters is outlined in table 2.

Implementation of Phase 4: presentation of results

We will present the estimated number of deaths averted between scenarios for each model across all of BC for the study period, by month and by quarter. We will also present the estimated number of incident OUD cases resulting from different diversion scenarios. These estimates will also be presented for key subgroups and population strata for each model. We will not provide an aggregated estimate of impact across models, as we want to preserve any differences in estimated impact that result from structural differences in models. Furthermore, there are no external data available for which to validate model outputs and weight each of their contributions.

To enhance the reproducibility and transparency of the models and analysis, code will be published within an open access repository alongside publication of the study. Some input parameters and sensitivity analyses presented here may change based on feedback on the models and their results. Any deviations from this protocol will be noted in future studies.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this study, as it is based on data that is available in the public domain. However, the findings are highly relevant to policymakers, healthcare providers and substance users, as they provide insights that may inform harm reduction strategies, healthcare interventions and policy decisions aimed at addressing substance-related outcomes.

Ethics and dissemination

This study is exempt from research ethics board review, as outlined in the Tri-Council Policy Statement, because it relies on data that is available in the public domain and there is no possibility of identifying individual persons. Results of the model validation analysis will be distributed through peer-reviewed journals and knowledge translation materials posted on the websites of the BC Centre for Disease Control and Centre for Advancing Health Outcomes.

Statistical code and model datasets will be available from the Dryad repository, and preregistration has been completed for The Open Science Framework.
