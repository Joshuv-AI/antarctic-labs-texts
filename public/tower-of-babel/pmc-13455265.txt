Rising trend among young adults: analysis of suicide-related deaths in Türkiye 2007–2022
Authors: Hasan Durmuş, Arda Borlu, Özlem Olguner Eker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuicide is a leading cause of death among young adults and is influenced by economic crises and the COVID-19 pandemic. This study aims to examine trends in suicide mortality rates in Türkiye between 2007 and 2022 using Joinpoint regression analysis.MethodsThis retrospective ecological time-trend study utilized publicly available data from the Turkish Statistical Institute. Suicide mortality rates per 100,000 population were calculated by age and gender. Joinpoint regression analysis was applied to identify significant changes in trends over time. Annual Percent Changes (APCs) and Average Annual Percent Changes (AAPCs) were computed.ResultsThe overall suicide rate increased annually by 5.86% (95% CI: 1.24–12.50) between 2019 and 2022. For females, the APC was 8.11% (95% CI: 0.96–18.43) in the same period. For females, a decreasing trend was observed from 2007 to 2019 (APC: -2.27%), followed by a significant increase between 2019 and 2022 (APC: +8.11%). In contrast, the suicide rate among males exhibited a consistent upward trend from 2007 to 2022 (APC: +2.28%).ConclusionsIt has been shown that suicide-related deaths in young adults in Türkiye are increasing. The fact that the increase especially in women is significantly high requires health authorities to take steps in this field.Keywords: Suicide, Joinpoint, Pandemic, Trends, Young adults

Background

Suicide is a critical public health problem that manifests when an individual ends their own life, profoundly affecting not only the individual but also the entire society and especially families [1, 2]. Each year, over 700,000 people die by suicide. Every 40 s, someone in the world takes their own life, and approximately one in every 11 individuals contemplates suicide at some point in their lifetime [1]. Suicide is the second most prevalent cause of death within the 15–29 age group, posing a serious threat to young adults. The heightened vulnerability of this age group is compounded by a unique convergence of developmental, social, and digital pressures. In recent years, researchers have highlighted the profound impact of the digital landscape, where factors such as cyberbullying, prolonged social media use, and exposure to online content related to self-harm can significantly increase suicide risk [3]. Furthermore, the unprecedented mental health challenges brought on by the COVID-19 pandemic—including social isolation, disruptions to education, and economic uncertainty—have disproportionately affected young people, leading to a documented rise in mental distress and suicidal ideation globally [4, 5]. Remarkably, suicide rates are at their peak in middle- and low-income countries, although higher-income nations also experience alarming rates of suicide [6]. Although women are more inclined to attempt suicide, the rate of suicide-related deaths in men is approximately two times higher across all countries [6]. In international comparisons, suicide rates exhibit significant variation across countries. This disparity can be attributed to a multitude of factors, including economic, religious, and social influences, as well as the reliability of statistical data records. Suicide rates appear to be lower, especially in the Middle East [7].

Suicide-related fatalities are preventable occurrences. International organizations such as the World Health Organization (WHO) and initiatives like the Global Sustainable Development Goals have established the goal of reducing suicide deaths by one third by the year 2030. Suicide prevention involves the implementation of various measures at population, sub-population, and individual levels. The World Health Organization (WHO) advocates the “LIVE LIFE” approach, which emphasizes interventions aimed at limiting access to suicide means (such as pesticides, firearms, and specific medications), responsible reporting of suicide in the media, development of socio-emotional life skills in adolescents, and early identification, assessment, and management of individuals affected by suicidal behaviors. WHO underscores the significance of conducting a comprehensive “situation analysis” as a fundamental step in effectively implementing these interventions [6].

Several factors, including genetic, social, cultural, environmental, and psychological elements, may influence the pathway to suicide. Distinguishing their interconnections and their individual impacts on suicide is highly challenging, resulting in a diverse and intricate array of risk factors. Despite this complexity, economic recessions, wartime periods, and media coverage of celebrity suicides generally have notable effects on suicide rates [7, 8]. It is widely recognized in the historical context of pandemics and epidemics that increased suicide rates are associated with the loss of relatives, overwhelming despair, and economic collapse caused by these health crises [9]. With the COVID-19 pandemic, human beings have been affected psychologically, and it has been observed that depression and anxiety are higher than before the pandemic [10]. Furthermore, research has shown that infectious diseases (e.g. COVID-19) negatively affect mental health [11]. According to a comprehensive study that compiled analyses of suicide rates during the pandemic period, it was found that suicide rates exhibited variation across different countries. Some countries experienced an increase in suicides, while others witnessed a decrease, and in certain countries, the rates remained unchanged [2, 12, 13]. In another meta-analysis, it was demonstrated that there was a significant increase in suicidal ideation. However, the incidence of suicide-related deaths tended to decrease, although not significantly, during the period under study [14]. Against this analytical background, it is pertinent to consider how major external stressors influence suicide trends. Previous research suggests that significant socio-economic disruptions, such as economic crises, can lead to an increase in suicide rates, particularly affecting vulnerable populations like young adults who face heightened unemployment or future uncertainty [15, 16]. The global COVID-19 pandemic also introduced unprecedented challenges, with studies indicating varying impacts on mental health and suicidal behavior across different demographic groups. For instance, some evidence points to increased distress among young females, while economic downturns might disproportionately affect young males [17].

Suicide poses a significant global public health concern for adolescents and young adults. Globally, it ranks as the second leading cause of mortality in this demographic [6, 7]. While advancements in preventive measures have led to a decrease in youth suicide rates in some high-income nations, a concerning upward trajectory is evident in numerous low- and middle-income countries [6]. In Türkiye, despite a generally stable suicide rate across the broader population, a notable increase has been observed among young adults, particularly females, especially post-2020 [14, 18]. Consequently, a thorough understanding of the underlying dynamics and specific risk factors associated with youth suicide is imperative for the development of effective, targeted interventions. Understanding dynamic shifts in suicide trends over time is paramount for effective public health planning. Analytical methodologies, such as joinpoint regression analysis, are crucial as they identify specific time points where a trend significantly changes direction or magnitude [19, 20]. This offers insights into the impact of major societal or economic events that simple annual rates might obscure. This approach allows for a more nuanced examination of long-term patterns, essential for developing timely and targeted prevention strategies.

Türkiye started to experience an economic crisis in 2018, which was exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic [21]. It is hypothesized that the escalation of stressors arising from both the pandemic and the economic crisis might contribute to a rise in suicide cases. The objective of this study is to analyze the fluctuations in suicide-related mortality rates in Türkiye from 2007 to 2022.

Methods

This study is a retrospective ecological time-trend analysis based on publicly available national mortality statistics related to suicide in Türkiye between 2007 and 2022. An evaluation was conducted utilizing openly accessible data on suicide-related fatalities and population statistics provided by the Turkish Statistical Institute (TUIK). Suicide rates (per 100,000) were computed by considering the yearly occurrences of suicide-related deaths categorized by gender and age group, spanning from 2007 when address-based population registrations were initiated, up to 2022. The study included all suicide-related deaths reported by the Turkish Statistical Institute (TUIK) between 2007 and 2022, categorized by age group, gender, and year. Suicide was identified according to the officially reported cause of death statistics. Individuals aged 5 years and above were included, with age groups stratified into 5–14, 15–29, 30–49, 50–64, and 65+. Deaths for which the cause was not explicitly coded as suicide or where the age was under 5 years were excluded from the analysis due to data ambiguity or lack of reliability in classification. The data on suicide deaths were obtained from the Turkish Statistical Institute (TUIK), based on official cause-of-death statistics. Suicide deaths were identified using ICD-10 codes X60–X84, which cover intentional self-harm. While TUIK data are the official source of national mortality, underreporting or misclassification may occur, particularly in cases of ambiguous or socially stigmatized deaths. This represents a limitation of the study. However, it is noteworthy that the number of suicides in the age group below fifteen years is presented collectively; hence, the suicides in the under-fifteen age group were calculated in proportion to the population falling within the 5–14 years age bracket. Crude suicide mortality rates were calculated as the number of suicide deaths per 100,000 population annually. Standardized mortality rates were not calculated in this study due to the unavailability of detailed age-specific population data for all years. However, future research may benefit from direct standardization using reference populations to account for age structure differences.

Statistical analysis

For the statistical evaluation, Joinpoint regression analysis was employed to assess the variation in the calculated suicide rates over time. Joinpoint regression analysis was employed to identify statistically significant changes in long-term trends of age-standardized suicide rates. Joinpoint regression analysis is a statistical test utilized to identify specific years in which there is a significant change in trends over time. The Annual Percent Change (APC) indicates the yearly rate of change in suicide rates for a given time segment, showing whether the trend is increasing or decreasing. The Average Annual Percent Change (AAPC) summarizes the overall trend across multiple segments as a single value. Both APC and AAPC values are presented with 95% confidence intervals, and a p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Joinpoint regression analysis was used to calculate the Annual Percent Change (APC) in suicide rates over time. In our analysis, the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.0.2) was used with the standard settings recommended by the National Cancer Institute (NCI). The software uses a grid search method and applies permutation tests to determine significant joinpoints. Heteroscedasticity was handled by applying a log-transformation of the suicide rate values before modeling, ensuring variance stabilization across years. The statistical analyses were conducted using the Joinpoint Regression 5.0.2 software and SPSS version 28.0 software.

Results

In Türkiye, a total of 2,793 suicide deaths (1,808 males and 985 females) were recorded in 2007, while this number increased to 4,146 (3,111 males and 1,035 females) in 2022, indicating a notable rise over the 16-year period. Figure 1 illustrates the observed suicide rates per 100,000 population by gender (female, male, and total) in Türkiye between 2007 and 2022. Joinpoint regression analysis was used to identify significant changes in trend over time. For females, a decreasing trend was observed from 2007 to 2019 (APC: -2.27%), followed by a significant increase between 2019 and 2022 (APC: +8.11%). In contrast, the suicide rate among males exhibited a consistent upward trend from 2007 to 2022 (APC: +2.28%). The total suicide rate showed a mild increase from 2007 to 2019 (APC: +0.74%), which accelerated significantly between 2019 and 2022 (APC: +5.86%). These segmented trends are visualized with regression lines in the figure. In Table 1, data on the joinpoint regression analysis are presented, according to which it is seen that the average annual increase in all suicides in 2019–2022 was 5.86% (1.24–12.50) and this increase was 8.11% (0.96–18.43) for women. In men, a general upward trend continues between 2007 and 2022, with an annual increase of 2.28% (1.40–3.14). The average annual percent change (AAPC) in total suicide rates between 2007 and 2022 was 1.35% (95% CI: 0.39–2.31), which was statistically significant (p = 0.01).

Discussion

This study, which examined suicide-related fatalities in Türkiye between 2007 and 2022, revealed a consistent increase in rates among males and the overall population. Conversely, female suicide rates, which had previously declined from 2007 to 2020, reversed this trajectory and began to rise after 2020. This divergence underscores gender- and age-specific vulnerabilities that are likely influenced by broader societal, economic, and health-related factors. A critical aspect of suicide epidemiology is the quality and reliability of mortality data. While TUIK employs robust mechanisms for death registration, including verification by medical practitioners and forensic authorities, the potential for misclassification of cause-of-death, commonly referred to as ‘garbage codes,’ or intentional underreporting due to cultural stigma remains a recognized challenge globally. For instance, some intentional self-harm deaths might be erroneously classified as accidental deaths, particularly in cases where the intent is ambiguous or where there is a strong societal desire to avoid the stigma associated with suicide [22]. Although the extent of such misclassification in Türkiye is difficult to quantify precisely, it is a common limitation inherent in all retrospective mortality studies and should be considered when interpreting the absolute rates. However, for trend analysis, consistent biases over time are less likely to distort the identified patterns of change.

In its global suicide evaluation report, the WHO indicates that between 2000 and 2019, the age-standardized global suicide rate witnessed a significant decline of 36%. However, when analyzed regionally, an increase was noted solely in the American Region [6]. Despite a general decline, the COVID-19 pandemic significantly intensified global mental health challenges, leading to increased anxiety, depression, and suicidal ideation [10, 14, 15]. A recent meta-analysis indicated that while suicidal ideation and attempts rose during the pandemic, overall suicide mortality did not exhibit a statistically significant change [14]. These observations highlight the intricate nature of suicide, where suicidal thoughts do not consistently result in fatal outcomes. Nevertheless, diverse nations have demonstrated varied responses to the pandemic and its associated stressors.

In the United States, suicide rates increased by 37% between 2000 and 2018, then temporarily declined by 5% between 2018 and 2020 [23]. However, rates have almost returned to their peak in 2021, and preliminary data for 2022 show that the number of suicide deaths in 2022 was the highest on record, exceeding the next closest year (2018) by more than 1,000 deaths. Looking further back to 1999, there is a significant increase of 37% from a rate of 10.57 per 100,000. Specifically, 2022 marked the highest recorded number of suicide deaths, similar to the rates observed in 2018 (14.5 per 100,000 in 2022 compared to 14.2 per 100,000 in 2018) [23]. This rate was higher than that in 2020, the year preceding the resurgence in suicide deaths [23]. Certain studies have posited that suicides could potentially be misclassified as deaths resulting from drug overdoses. This misclassification arises from the challenge of distinguishing intentional drug overdoses from unintentional cases. Additionally, there is an ongoing argument suggesting that the overall number of suicides might be underestimated [24].

The CDC report also emphasises that increases in the number of suicide deaths in America follow high levels of mental health symptoms during COVID, increased financial stressors, and long-standing challenges in accessing needed mental health services, particularly for some populations. According to data from the CDC, the suicide rate among men in the United States was approximately four times higher than that among women in 2021. When evaluating the rate of increase in suicide mortality rates between 2021 and 2022, it becomes apparent that there was a higher increase among women (3.8%) compared to men (2.3%), contributing to an overall increase of 2.6% [23].

According to the UK Office for National Statistics, 5,583 suicides were recorded in England and Wales in 2021, equivalent to a death rate of 10.7 deaths per 100,000 people, which is statistically significantly higher than the 2020 rate of 10.0 deaths per 100,000 people, but consistent with pre-coronavirus (COVID-19) pandemic rates in 2019 and 2018 [25. The Office for National Statistics in the United Kingdom interpreted the decline in the suicide rate in 2020 as a result of a decrease in male suicides at the start of the coronavirus pandemic and delays in death registration due to the pandemic [25]. Despite approximately three-quarters of suicides being male, the age-specific suicide rate among women is highest in the 45–49 age group, while among men, it peaks in the 50–54 age group. Notably, since 1981, when records began to be analyzed, the most significant increase in the suicide rate has been observed among women aged 24 years and younger [25].

The total number of suicides in Japan increased in 2022, with the highest number of suicides by age group occurring among people in their fifties [26]. A contributing factor to this increase is thought to be that during the COVID-19 pandemic, more children faced problems due to the inability to communicate properly with their friends and teachers [26].

The “Health at a Glance: Europe 2022” report highlighted that the COVID-19 pandemic and the subsequent economic crisis have imposed a growing burden on citizens’ mental health, leading to elevated levels of stress, anxiety, and depression [27]. Young individuals and those in lower income groups are identified as being at a higher risk. The report underscores that disruptions in health services for individuals with pre-existing mental health issues constitute a significant aspect of the adverse impact of the pandemic on mental health [27].

The majority of suicide-related deaths in Türkiye involve men, and there is a consistent upward trend observed between 2007 and 2022, with an annual increase of 2.28% (1.40–3.14). Upon analyzing all suicide-related deaths, it was noted that the average annual increase during the period 2019–2022 was 5.86% (1.24–12.50). Furthermore, this increase was even higher among women, standing at 8.11% (0.96–18.43). The most notable increase is observed among women aged 15–29, showing a rise of 20.19% (3.20–29.16) between 2020 and 2022. The trend in this age group, which had been decreasing between 2007 and 2020, changed direction and began to show an increasing tendency after 2020 (Fig. 2). Similarly, the trend in the 30–49 age range for women, which had previously been decreasing, started to exhibit an annual increase of 13.56% (0.16–21.76) after 2020 (Table 1). The observed gender-specific divergences in suicide trends in Türkiye warrant careful consideration. The sustained increase in male suicide rates throughout the study period, with a particularly sharp rise in the most recent segment, may be linked to evolving socio-economic pressures. For young adult males, traditional gender roles often emphasize provider status, making them particularly vulnerable to economic downturns, unemployment, and associated feelings of failure or loss of self-worth [28]. This vulnerability could have been exacerbated by recent economic volatilities in Türkiye and the broader disruptions from the pandemic.

Conversely, the notable increase in female suicide rates, especially in the 2019–2022 period, suggests a different set of contributing factors. While females historically exhibit higher rates of non-fatal self-harm, their completed suicide rates have shown diverse patterns globally. The recent increase in Türkiye could reflect the cumulative impact of increased domestic stress, heightened mental health burden due to social isolation during the pandemic, limited access to mental health support, or societal pressures impacting female autonomy and well-being [15, 29]. These factors may have interacted to create a challenging environment for young adult females, leading to an upward shift in suicide mortality trends.

In the 15–29 age group, there was a significant annual increase of 10.58% (3.07–21.08) between 2019 and 2022, while in the 30–49 age group, the upward trend was 6.81% (1.43–15.09) per annum, as illustrated in Fig. 2. According to CDC data, when the suicide mortality rates for 2021–2022 are analyzed; it is seen that there is the highest increase with 0.7% between the ages of 25–44, 6.6% between the ages of 45–64, and 8.1% at the age of 65 and over [23].

Suicide statistics vary widely across countries, and in a meta-analysis of suicide ideation, attempts and deaths during the COVID-19 pandemic, it was observed that suicide ideation and suicide attempts increased, but there was no significant change in deaths [14]. The noticeable rise in suicide mortality rates among women and the 15–29 age group in Türkiye in recent years is noteworthy. While the effects related to COVID-19 are acknowledged in this increase, it is believed that the most significant impact might stem from economic changes.

Indeed, deaths related to suicide represent one of the most crucial causes of mortality in young adults, exerting a significant impact on burden of disease studies [30]. Hence, public health interventions in the realm of suicide prevention hold paramount importance.

Strengths and limitations

This research benefits from several significant advantages, including its utilization of national mortality statistics over period, the employment of Joinpoint regression analysis to identify critical shifts in trends, and a methodology stratified by gender and age to elucidate demographic variations.

Nevertheless, the study is subject to certain constraints. The reported suicide mortality rates are derived from officially recorded causes of death, which may potentially suffer from underreporting or misclassification. Furthermore, the absence of consistent age-specific population data for the entire study duration precluded the calculation of standardized mortality rates. The study also lacks information on psychosocial or socioeconomic determinants, thereby limiting the scope for inferring causality.

Conclusion

This joinpoint regression analysis offers critical insights into the evolving landscape of suicide mortality in Türkiye between 2007 and 2022. It reveals distinct gender-specific trends and identifies significant inflection points. Our findings confirm a sustained increase in male suicide rates throughout the study period. In contrast, female suicide rates, after an initial period of decline, showed a concerning upward shift in the most recent years. These patterns align with our hypotheses regarding gender-specific divergences and highlight the dynamic impact of societal stressors.

The observed trends underscore the critical need for differentiated public health interventions. Specifically, interventions targeting young adult males should address issues related to economic stability, mental health stigma, and coping mechanisms in the face of career and financial pressures. For young adult females, strategies should focus on enhancing the accessibility of mental health support, addressing social isolation, and mitigating the impacts of gender-specific societal stressors, particularly those exacerbated by recent global events such as the COVID-19 pandemic. These findings emphasize that suicide prevention strategies must be data-driven, tailored to specific demographic vulnerabilities, and continuously adapted to respond to the rapidly changing socio-economic and public health contexts in Türkiye.
