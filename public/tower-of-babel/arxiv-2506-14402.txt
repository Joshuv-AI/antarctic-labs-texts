Distinguishing finite and infinite trees of arbitrary cardinality
Wilfried Imrich, Rafał Kalinowski, Florian Lehner, Monika Pilśniak, Marcin Stawiski

Abstract
Let G G be a finite or infinite graph and m ⁡ ( G ) m(G) the minimum number of vertices moved by the non-identity automorphisms of G G . We are interested in bounds on the supremum Δ ⁡ ( G ) \Delta(G) of the degrees of the vertices of G G that assure the existence of vertex colorings of G G with two colors that are preserved only by the identity automorphism, and, in particular, in the number a ⁡ ( G ) a(G) of such colorings that are mutually inequivalent. For trees T T with finite m ⁡ ( T ) m(T) we obtain the bound Δ ⁡ ( T ) ≤ 2 m ⁡ ( T ) / 2 \Delta(T)\leq 2^{m(T)/2} for the existence of such a coloring, and show that a ⁡ ( T ) = 2 | T | a(T)=2^{|T|} if T T is infinite. Similarly, we prove that a ⁡ ( G ) = 2 | G | a(G)=2^{|G|} for all tree-like graphs G G with Δ ⁡ ( G ) ≤ 2 ℵ 0 \Delta(G)\leq 2^{\aleph_{0}} . For rayless or one-ended trees T T with arbitrarily large infinite m ⁡ ( T ) m(T) , we prove directly that a ⁡ ( T ) = 2 | T | a(T)=2^{|T|} if Δ ⁡ ( T ) ≤ 2 m ⁡ ( T ) \Delta(T)\leq 2^{m(T)} .



Keywords: Automorphisms, finite and infinite trees, distinguishing and asymmetrizing number.

Introduction

Given a graph
G
, a set
S\subseteq V(G)
is
distinguishing
if the identity is the only element of
\operatorname{Aut}(G)
that fixes
S
setwise. A graph with a distinguishing set is called
2
-distinguishable
, and the number
a(G)
of inequivalent distinguishing sets of
G
is its
asymmetrizing number
.
We investigate the dependence of
a(G)
on bounds on the supremum
\Delta(G)
of the degrees of the vertices of
G
, and in terms of the
motion

m(G)
of
G
, which is the minimum number of vertices moved by the non-identity automorphisms of
G
.

Our main results are Theorems
6
and
12
. In Theorem
6
we show that a tree
T
with finite motion
m(T)
and
\Delta(T)\leq 2^{m/2}
is 2-distinguishable, and that its asymmetrizing number
a(T)
is
2^{|T|}
if
T
is infinite.

In Theorem
12
we consider tree-like graphs
G
, and show that
a(G)=2^{|G|}
if
\Delta(G)\leq 2^{\aleph_{0}}
.
For its proof we invoke Theorem
9
, which is a consequence of a theorem of Polat
[
11
, Theorem 3.3]
. The latter theorem is the main result in a series of three papers by Polat and Sabidussi
[
12
]
, and Polat
[
10
,
11
]
.

Since the proof of
[
11
, Theorem 3.3]
is somewhat difficult to read, it would be good to have simpler proofs both for this theorem, and for Theorem
9
. In the last section we provide such a proof for the special case of Theorem
9
when
T
is rayless or one-ended. The proof uses properties of the Schmidt rank of rayless graphs.

A curious minor result is Lemma
7
, which establishes a relationship between the order of a connected graph
G
, the size of
\operatorname{Aut}(G)
, and
a(G)
. In Sections
4
and
5
we also pose a conjecture and a problem.

Papers on 2-distinguishability rarely explicitly use the number of distinguishing sets, although it can help in proving 2-distinguishability. An exception is Babai’s groundbraking 1977 paper
[
2
]
, in which he showed that each tree
T
, all vertices of which have the same degree
\alpha\geq 2
of arbitrarily large cardinality, is “asymmetrizable by
2^{|T|}
inequivalent asymmetric 2-colorings”. In the current terminology, this means that
T
is 2-distinguishable by
2^{|T|}
inequivalent distinguishing sets.

Later Babai’s result was reproved and generalised by entirely different means by Polat and Sabidussi
[
12
]
, and Polat
[
10
,
11
]
. They also introduced the notation
a(T)
for the number of inequivalent distinguishing sets.

Apart from the concept of motion and the asymmetrizing number, our terminology is that of Albertson and Collins
[
1
]
. They called a graph
d
-distinguishable
if there is a vertex coloring with
d
colors, such that no non-identity automorphism preserves the coloring. Hence, when a graph is
2
-distinguishable, then the sets of either color are distinguishing.

We focus on the connection between 2-distinguishability and the motion of a graph. Such a relationship was already noted by Cameron, Neumann and Saxl
[
4
]
in 1984, where they studied the interdependence between the existence of distinguishing sets of permutation groups and their degrees, where the degree of a permutation group corresponds to our concept of motion. Another result of this type is from
Russel and Sundaram
[
14
]
, who showed in 1998 that
G
is
d
-distinguishable if
d^{m(G)/2}>|\operatorname{Aut}(G)|
.
For further results on motion and distinguishabiliy we refer to the paper of Conder and Tucker
[
6
]
, which also contains an extensive bibliography.

The most substantial contribution linking motion to asymmetrization is due to Babai
[
3
]
. In 2022 he proved that each connected, locally finite graph with infinite motion is 2-distinguishable, thereby verifying the Infinite Motion Conjecture of Tucker
[
16
]
.

Preliminaries

We call a graph
G
2-distinguishable if it has a distinguishing set of vertices, that is, a set
S\subseteq V(G)
that is preserved only by the identity automorphism. If
S
is such a set, then its complement
V(G)\setminus S
is also distinguishing.
The definition allows that
S
or
V(G)\setminus S
are empty. In this case the identity is the only automorphism of
G
, and we call
G

asymmetric
. Note that pincking a subset
S
of
V(G)
is equivalent to coloring the vertices in
S
and in
V(G)\setminus S
with two distinct colors. If
S
is a distinguishing set, then we call the resulting coloring a
distinguishing coloring.

Subsets
S
of
G
and
S^{\prime}
of
G^{\prime}
are called
equivalent
if there exists an isomorphism
\varphi
from
G
to
G^{\prime}
such that
\varphi(S)=S^{\prime}
. Recall that we defined the asymmetrizing number
a(G)
of
G
, as the number of pairwise inequivalent distinguishing sets of
G
. Clearly
a(G)\leq 2^{|G|}
for all graphs,
a(G)=2^{|G|}
for asymmetric graphs, and
a(G)=0
when
G
is not 2-distinguishable. For example,
a(K_{3})=0
,
a(K_{2})=1
, and
a(K_{1})=2
; the inequivalent distinguishing sets of
K_{1}
being
V(K_{1})
and the empty set.

We shall write
(T,w)
for a tree
T
with root
w\in V(T)
, and
\operatorname{Aut}(T,w)
for the subgroup of
\operatorname{Aut}(T)
that fixes
w
. We say a subset
S\subseteq V(T)

distinguishes

(T,w)
if the identity is the only element of
\operatorname{Aut}(T,w)
that fixes
S
setwise. Two subsets
S
and
S^{\prime}
are called
equivalent with respect to
\operatorname{Aut}(T,w)
, if there is an element of
\operatorname{Aut}(T,w)
which maps
S
to
S^{\prime}
, and we define
a(T,w)
as the number of inequivalent distinguishing sets of
(T,w)
. Note that
a(T)\leq a(T,w)
, because
\operatorname{Aut}(T)\supseteq\operatorname{Aut}(T,w)
.

For vertices
x
,
y
of a rooted tree
(T,w)
,
x
is a
descendant
of
y

, if

lies on the unique path from

to

. Vertex

is the

of

if

and

is an edge. In that case,

is a
child
of
y
. We say

and

are

if they have the same parent, and that

and
x^{\prime}
are
twins
if they are siblings, and if there is an automorphism that moves

to
x^{\prime}
(and fixes their parent). We call the set of twins of

the
similarity class
of
x
, denote it by
\bar{x}
, and call its size the multiplicity
\mu(x)
of
x
. We always have
\mu(x)\geq 1
, because
x
is a twin of itself. By
R_{w}
we denote a set of representatives for the similarity classes of the children of the root
w
.

For a vertex
x
of
(T,w)
with parent
y
, let
T^{x}
denote the component of
T-xy
which contains
x
. For
x=w
, we set
T^{w}=T
. We consider
T^{x}
as a rooted tree with root
x
and write
a(T,w;x)
for the number of inequivalent distinguishing sets of
(T^{x},x)
. If the rooted tree
(T,w)
is clear from the context, we write
a(x)
instead of
a(T,w;x)
. Let
y
be the parent of
x
and
x^{\prime}
. Then
x
and
x^{\prime}
are twins if and only if
T^{x}
and
T^{x^{\prime}}
are isomorphic. By slight abuse of notation we also call
\mu(x)
the multiplicity
\mu(T^{x})
of
T^{x}
.

We also consider a slightly generalized version of
T^{x}
. Let
F
be a set of edges, say the edge set of a one-sided infinite path, or some other subgraph of
T
. Then
T^{x}_{F}
denotes the component of
T-F
containing
x
. We write
T^{x}
for short if the set

follows from the context. Such a
T^{x}
is a

with respect to
F
.

Recall that
\Delta(G)
is the least upper bound on the degrees of the vertices of a graph
G
. If
G
is finite, then
\Delta(G)
is the maximum degree, and if
\Delta(G)
is infinite and connected, then
\Delta(G)=|G|
.

We shall frequently refer to the following theorem of Polat and Sabidussi
[
12
]
.

Theorem 1

(
[
12
, Theorem 2.3]
)
.

The asymmetrizing number of a rooted tree
(T,w)
is

a(T,w)=2\prod_{{x\in R_{w}}}{a(x)\choose\mu(x)},

(1)

where
{a\choose\mu}
denotes the usual binomial coefficient if
a
and
\mu
are finite. If
a
is infinite, then
{a\choose\mu}=a^{\mu}
if
\mu\leq a
, and
{a\choose\mu}=0
if
a<\mu
.

Proof.

Let
x\in R_{w}
, where
R_{w}
is a set of representatives for the similarity classes
\bar{y}
of the children
y
of the root
w
, and
v
be a twin of
x
. By
definition, the number of inequivalent distinguishing sets of the
rooted tree
T^{v}
is
a(x)
, and the number of inequivalent distinguishing sets of
\bigcup_{v\in\bar{x}}T^{v}
in
(T,w)
clearly is
{a(x)\choose\mu(x)}
.

Then
\prod_{{x\in R_{w}}}{a(x)\choose\mu(x)}
is the number of inequivalent distinguishing sets of
X=\bigcup_{v\in N(w)}T^{v}
in
(T,w)
, where
N(w)
is the set of all neighbors of
w
.
The observation that for each
S\subseteq V(X)
that distinguishes
X
, both
S
and
\{w\}\cup S
distinguish
(T,w)
, completes the proof.
∎

An immediate consequence of Theorem
1
is the following corollary.

Corollary 2

(Polat, Sabidussi
[
12
, page 282]
)
.

A rooted tree
(T,w)
is
2
-distinguishable if and only if
\mu(x)\leq a(x)
for all

.

We end this section with a lemma that holds for all trees.

Lemma 3

(Polat and Sabidussi
[

]
, Proposition 2.5 (ii))
.

Let

be a tree with a central edge

, and
T^{u}
be the component of

that contains

. If each non-identity automorphism of

swaps
u
and
v
, then

a(T)={{a(T^{u})}\choose{2}}.

(2)

Proof.

Subdivide the edge

by vertex
w
, apply Theorem

to the new tree
(T,w)
, and remove

again.
∎

3
Trees with finite motion

By Kőnig’s Infinity Lemma each infinite tree, all of whose vertices have finite degrees, has a one-sided infinite path. Such paths are called
rays
. An immediate consequence of the lemma is that infinite rayless trees have vertices of infinite degrees. Clearly, finite trees are also rayless. All rayless trees, finite or infinite, have a center consisting of a single vertex or a pair of adjacent vertices that is invariant under all automorphisms of
T
. For finite trees this is well known, for infinite trees this was shown by Polat and Sabidussi in
[
13
]
, but also immediately follows from a result of Schmidt in
[
15
]
. Note that the center is uniquely defined also for asymmetric trees.

Graphs that contain rays, but no double rays, are called
one-ended
. Each one ended tree has at least one vertex of degree 1, otherwise it would contain a double ray. It is also easy to see that each vertex of a tree with rays, is the origin of a ray. Hence, each one-ended tree contains a ray whose origin has degree 1. The subrays of a ray are called
tails
, and any two of them differ at most by a finite initial segment.

For one-ended trees we need the following result of Carmesin, Lehner and Möller
[
5
]
.

Lemma 4
.

(
[
5
]
, Lemma 4.1)
Let
T
be a one-ended tree and
R
a ray of
T
. Then each automorphism of
T
fixes some tail of
R
pointwise.

Recall that motion was not defined for asymmetric graphs
G
. For this case, we extend the definition by the convention that
m(G)>\alpha
for any asymmetric graph, and any cardinal
\alpha
. In particular, a graph
G
with fewer than
\alpha
vertices has, by this convention, motion
m(G)\geq\alpha
if and only if it is asymmetric.

Recall that the motion
m(T,w)
of
(T,w)
cannot be smaller than the motion
m
of
T
, because
\operatorname{Aut}(T,w)\subseteq\operatorname{Aut}(T)
. This means, if the degrees of
T
are bounded by
2^{m}
, then they are also bounded by
2^{m(T,w)}
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
(T,w)
be a finite rooted tree, where
T
has motion
m
, and where
\Delta(T)\leq 2^{m/2}
. Then
(T,w)
is
2
-distinguishable. Furthermore, if
\deg(w)<2^{m/2}
, then
a(T,w)\geq 2\cdot 2^{m/2}
, and if
(T,w)
is asymmetric, then
a(T,w)=2^{|T|}
.

Proof.

If
(T,w)
is asymmetric the lemma is true. Otherwise, suppose that the lemma is false, and that
(T,w)
is a minimal counterexample. By the minimality of
(T,w)
, the lemma must be true for all
T^{x}
, where
x
is a child of
w
, and all
T^{x}
must be pairwise isomorphic and 2-distinguishable.

If
\mu(x)=1
, then
(T,w)
is 2-distinguishable, because
T^{x}
is. Hence,
\mu(x)\geq 2
, and
|T^{x}|\geq m/2
, otherwise the motion of
(T,w)
would be smaller than

.

T^{x}
cannot be asymmetric, otherwise
a(T^{x})\geq 2^{m/2}>\mu(x)
, and
(T,w)
is 2-distinguishable by Theorem
1
.

Hence
T^{x}
, for which the lemma holds, is not asymmetric, has motion at least
m
,
\Delta(T^{x})\leq 2^{m/2}
, and the degree of
x
in
T^{x}
is less than
2^{m/2}
. We infer that
a(x)=a(T^{x})\geq 2\cdot 2^{m/2}>{\rm deg}(w)=\tau(x)
.
If
{\rm deg}(w)=2^{m/2}
, then

a(T,w)=2{a(x)\choose\tau(x)}\geq 2{2\cdot 2^{m/2}\choose 2^{m/2}}\geq 2,

and if
{\tau(x)=\rm deg}(w)<2^{m/2}
, then

a(T,w)=2{a(x)\choose\tau(x)}\geq 2{2\cdot 2^{m/2}\choose\tau(x)}\geq 2\cdot 2^{m/2},

because
\tau(x)<2^{m/2}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 6
.

Let
T
be a finite or infinite tree with finite motion
m
and maximum degree
\Delta(T)\leq 2^{m/2}
. Then:

(i)

T
is
2
-distinguishable.

(ii)

a(T)=2^{|T|}
if
T
is infinite.

Item (i) improves a bound of Imrich and Tucker
[
9
, Theorem 1]
, and item (ii) is new.

For finite trees we have no analogue to (ii), because it is possible that
a(T)=2
for finite trees
T
with arbitrarily large motion

, even when
\Delta(T)=2^{m/2}
.
Just consider a tree
(T,w)
with
2^{m/2}
lobes, all of which are paths of order
m/2
.
Then
a(T)=2
by Theorem

Proof.

As
T
has bounded finite degree, and because each infinite rayless tree has a vertex of infinite degree,
T
must be finite if it is rayless. We thus consider the cases when
T
is finite, infinite with a ray but without a double ray, and infinite with double rays.

Case 1.

T
is finite. Suppose that its center consists of a single vertex

. Then
a(T)=a(T,w)
, and

is 2-distinguishable by Lemma

.

If the center of

consists of an edge

, then we define

as in Lemma

. By Lemma

,
T^{u}
is 2-distinguishable and

is 2-distinguishable by Lemma

.

Case 2.

T
contains a ray, but no double ray. Let

be a vertex of degree 1 in

and
R

v_{0}
. Then all lobes
T^{x}
, with respect to

, are rayless, because

contains no double ray. By Kőnig’s Infinity Lemma they are finite. Hence, the

are finite,

, and we have to show that

Let

, and

. The number of these colorings is

, and each of them is a distinguishing coloring of

. For the proof of Case 2 it thus suffices to show that each

.

For

consist of the automorphisms of

, and fix the tail

of

. Clearly, the

, and

.

For

in

, namely the lobe containing

, denoted

, and the one containing

. Furthermore, let

be the subtree of

, whose lobes are the lobes of

, and

, and the degree of their roots is

.

, and, for

, and at least one non-identity automorphism. Interestingly, this is not the case. In fact, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 7

Let

|\operatorname{Aut}(G)|\cdot a(G)\leq 2^{|G|}.

Proof.

The claim trivially holds if
G
is an asymmetric graph since then
|\operatorname{Aut}(G)|=1
and
a(G)=2^{|G|}
. Otherwise, the proof follows from a remark in
[
4
]
that the automorphism group of any 2-distinguishable finite graph
G
acts regularly on the orbit of each distinguishing set of
G
in the power set
2^{V(G)}
of
V(G)
. Obviously, this is also true for infinite graphs. Recall that the orbit of a subset
S
of
V(G)
is the set of all
\varphi(S)
for
\varphi\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
, and that an action of a permutation group
A
on a set
\Omega
is regular if its action is transitive and fixed point free. By definition, the action of
\operatorname{Aut}(G)
on the orbit of any
S\subset V(G)
is transitive, and if
S
is distinguishing, then each element of the orbit of
S
is distinguishing, and thus it can be fixed only by the identity automorphism. As each distinguishing set has
|\operatorname{Aut}(G)|
equivalent sets, we infer that
a(G)|\operatorname{Aut}(G)|\leq 2^{|G|}
.
∎

Note that this result is independent of
m(G)
and
\Delta(G)
.

Trees with infinite motion

In
[
11
]
Polat defines a tree
T
to be
a-maximum
(resp.
s-maximum
) if for any
root
w
and any neighbor
x
of
w
,
a(T^{x})=2^{|T^{x}|}
(resp.
s(T^{x})=2^{|T^{x}|}
, where
s(T)
denotes the number of inequivalent subsets of a tree
T
). He then proves
[
11
, Theorem 3.3]
about
a
-maximum and s-maximum trees, of which the part about
a
-maximum trees reads as follows:

Theorem 8
.

A tree
T
is
a
-maximum if and only if, for any
vertex
w
and any neighbor
x
of
w
, if
T^{x}
is finite then
\mu(x)=1
, and if

is infinite,
then
\mu(x)=2^{|T^{x}|}
.

Polat also shows that

for
a
-maximum trees if and only if they are infinite or have a fixed point
[
11
, Theorem 3.16]
. Because trees with infinite motion
m
and
\Delta(T)\leq 2^{m}
satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
8
, this clearly implies the validity of the following theorem:

Theorem 9
.

Let
T
be a tree of infinite motion
m
and
\Delta(T)\leq 2^{m}
. Then

.

We conclude this section with a conjecture:

.

A tree

and any neighbor

of

\mu(x)\leq a(T^{x})

5
Tree-like graphs

We now apply Theorem
9
to
tree-like graphs
. Imrich, Klavžar and Trofimov defined them in
[
8
]
as rooted graphs
(G,w)
, in which each vertex
y
has a neighbor
x
such that
y
is on all shortest paths from
x
to
w
. In other words, tree-like graphs are rooted graphs
(G,w)
in which each vertex has a child of which it is the only parent.
Note that
w
is the only vertex of
(G,w)
that may have degree 1.

By
[
8
, Theorem 4.2]
,
each tree-like graph
G
with
\Delta(G)\leq 2^{\aleph_{0}}
is 2-distinguishable as an unrooted graph. We show that
a(G)=2^{|G|}.

Lemma 11
.

Let
T
be a tree of infinite motion
m
and
\Delta(T)\leq 2^{m}
. If each vertex of
T
lies on a double ray, then
T
has
2^{|T|}
distinguishing sets
S
in which each vertex of
S
is adjacent to a vertex of
V(T)\setminus S
, and for any
w\in V(T)
there are
2^{|T|}
distinguishing sets
S_{w}
of
T
where
w
is the only vertex of
S_{w}
with no neighbor in
V(T)\setminus S_{w}
.

Proof.

Form a new tree
T^{\prime}
from
T
by choosing an arbitrary vertex
w\in V(T)
and by subsequently contracting all edges
ab
to single vertices if
d_{T}(w,a)
is even and
d_{T}(w,b)=d_{T}(w,a)+1
.

T^{\prime}
has motion
m
,
\Delta(T^{\prime})\leq 2^{m}
, and
|T^{\prime}|=|T|
. By Theorem
9
,
a(T^{\prime})=2^{|T^{\prime}|}=2^{|T|}
. From
a(T^{\prime},w)\geq a(T^{\prime})
and
a(T,w)\geq a(T)
we also infer that
a(T^{\prime},w)=a(T,w)=2^{|T|}
.

Let
\varphi\in\operatorname{Aut}(T,w)
and let
\varphi^{\prime}
denote its restriction to
V(T^{\prime})
. Then
\varphi^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Aut}(T^{\prime},w)
, and
\varphi
is uniquely determined by its action on
(T^{\prime},w)
, because each vertex of
(T^{\prime},w)
different from
w
has only one parent. This implies that a set
S\subseteq V(T)
distinguishes
(T,w)
if
S^{\prime}=S\cap V(T^{\prime})
distinguishes
(T^{\prime},w)
.

Given a distinguishing set
S^{\prime}
of
(T^{\prime},w)
we extend it in two ways to a distinguishing set
S
of
(T,w)
. The first is to set

S=S^{\prime}
. Clearly this implies that each vertex of
S
has a neighbor in
V(T)\setminus S
and, because
a(T^{\prime},w)=2^{|T|}
, the number of these sets is
2^{|T|}
.

The second way is to form a set
S
by adding all children of
w
to
S^{\prime}
, unless there is a child
u
of
w
all of whose children are in
S^{\prime}
. Then we only add
u
. Clearly we obtain
2^{|T|}
distinguishing sets in this way, and either
w
or
u
are the only vertices in
S
all of whose neighbors are in
S
.
∎

Theorem 12
.

Let
G
be a tree-like graph with
\Delta(G)\leq 2^{\aleph_{0}}
. Then
a(G)=2^{|G|}
.

Proof.

Let
G
be a tree-like graph
(G,w)
. Then the set of edges
yx
where
y
is on all shortest
x,w
-paths are the edges of a spanning subgraph, say
F
. Clearly
F
is a forest with no finite components. Each component
U
has motion at most
2^{\aleph_{0}}
, unless it is asymmetric. Because
\Delta(U)\leq\Delta(G)\leq 2^{\aleph_{0}}
we infer by Theorem
9
that
a(U)=2^{|U|}\geq 2^{\aleph_{0}}
.

Observe that, by the definition of
F
, any automorphism
\varphi
of
G
that fixes
w
preserves the components of
F
. This is the case when
w
has degree 1.

We now distinguish the components of
F
under the following restrictions: Let
U_{w}
be the component of
F
that contains
w
. If
w
has degree 1 we admit any distinguishing set
S
of
U_{w}
, otherwise we only admit distinguishing sets
S
in which there is only vertex that has no neighbors in
V(U_{w})\setminus S
.
For all other components
U
of
F
we only admit distinguishing sets
S
where each vertex has a neighbor in
V(U)\setminus S
.

Because
F
has at most
2^{\aleph_{0}}
components we can distinguish them pairwise inequivalently with admitted distinguishing sets
S_{U}
. That is, we distinguish by sets
S_{U}
that obey the above restrictions, and if
U,U^{\prime}
are isomorphic components of
F
, then there is no isomorphism from
U
to
U^{\prime}
that maps
S_{U}
into
S_{U^{\prime}}
.

Let
S
be the union of all
S_{U}
in such a selection. If
w
has degree 1, then each
\varphi\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
fixes
w
. Otherwise each
\varphi\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
that preserves
S
fixes
w
because
w
is the only vertex of
S
that has no neighbors in
V(G)\setminus S
. Hence, in either case each
\varphi\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
that preserves
S
also preserves
F
. As each
S_{U}
distinguishes
U
, the set
S
distinguishes
F
, and thus also
G
, because
F
is a spanning subgraph of
G
.

Let
\alpha
be the cardinality of the set of components of
F
. By transfinite induction with respect to the well ordering of the components of
F
induced by
\alpha
, it is easily seen that there are
2^{|G|}
distinguishing sets
S
of
F
.
∎

6
Rayless and one-ended trees with infinite motion

We now prove, without invoking Theorem
8
, that rayless and one-ended trees of infinite motion satisfy Theorem
9
.

Our proof uses the rank of rayless graphs. It was introduced by Schmidt in
[
15
]
, but we define it as Halin in
[
7
]
.

For all ordinals
\lambda
define the following class
A(\lambda)
of graphs by transfinite induction:

1.

A(0)
consists of all finite graphs.

2.

If
\lambda>0
and
A(\mu)
has been defined for each
\mu<\lambda
, then a graph
G
is in
A(\lambda)
if there exists a finite set
S
of vertices such that each component of
G-S
is in an
A(\mu)
with
\mu<\lambda
.

Let
A
be the union of the classes
A(\lambda)
. Then for every
G\in A
there exists a smallest
\lambda
with
G\in A(\lambda)
; this
\lambda
is called the
rank
of
G
and denoted by
\rho(G)
.

One can then show, see Halin
[
7
]
, that the class of graphs that is assigned a rank coincides with the class of rayless graphs, and that there is a canonical choice for the set
S
in the definition above, by choosing
S
minimally among all sets that work. This minimal set is called the
core
of
G
. Furthermore, the core is unique, setwise fixed by each automorphism of
G
, and if
S^{\prime}\subseteq V(G)
contains the core, then every component of
G-S^{\prime}
has rank less than
\rho
.
The proofs are neither difficult nor long, but subtle.

Note that the existence of a unique finite core implies that each rayless tree
T
has a center. Just consider the minimal subtree
T_{S}
of
T
that contains the core
S
. This tree
T_{S}
is finite, and is preserved by all automorphisms of
T
, and thus so is its center.

Direct proof of Theorem
9
for rayless and one-ended trees
.
For rayless trees we use transfinite induction on the rank of
T
. Trees of rank 0 are finite, so they have infinite motion only if they are asymmetric, and therefore
a(T)=2^{|T|}
for every tree of rank 0 that satisfies the conditions of the theorem.

For the induction step, let
\rho>0
be any ordinal, let
T
be a tree of rank
\rho
, and assume that
the statement of the theorem holds for any tree with rank
\sigma<\rho
.
Let
T_{S}
be the minimal subtree containing the core of
T
. If
T
has a central vertex, then let
w
be this central vertex. Otherwise, let
w
be one endpoint of the central edge. Consider the rooted tree
(T,w)
. Clearly, the motion of
(T,w)
is at least
m
.

We claim that
a(x)=2^{|T^{x}|}
for every vertex
x
of
(T,w)
. For vertices not contained in
T_{S}
this is true by the induction hypothesis. If there are vertices that do not satisfy the claim, let
x
be such a vertex at maximal distance from
w
; note that the maximal distance is finite because
T_{S}
is finite. All children of
x
satisfy the claim, and by Theorem
1
the claim is satisfied for
x
as well. This yields a contradiction.

If there is a central edge we invoke Lemma
3
.

This leaves the case when
T
is one-ended. As in the proof of Case 2 of Theorem
6
we choose a ray
R=v_{0}v_{1}\cdots
, where
v_{0}
has degree 1, and note that the lobes
T^{v_{i}}
of
T
with respect to
R
are rayless, because
T
has no double rays.

We first show that
T
is asymmetric if
T^{v_{i}}
is finite for all
i\geq 0
. Clearly all
T^{v_{i}}
are asymmetric. Let
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(T)
. By Lemma
4
,
\alpha
fixes a tail of
R
, say
R_{v_{k}}
. It thus fixes all
v_{j}
with
j\geq k
, and all lobes
T^{v_{j}}
for
j\geq k
.
The remaining part of
T
is a finite subtree, and thus asymmetric. Hence,
\alpha=\text{\rm id}
,
T
is asymmetric, and
a(T)=2^{|T|}
.

Therefore at least one
T^{v_{i}}
has infinite order. In this case we color all vertices of
R
black, and all vertices that are adjacent to a vertex of
R
white. Let
c
be this coloring. Then all color preserving automorphisms of
T
fix
R
pointwise, and each
T^{v_{i}}
setwise. We have to extend
c
to
2^{|T|}
distinguishing 2-colorings of
T
.

This means that we have to find a distinguishing 2-coloring for each
T^{v_{i}}
, in which
v_{i}
is black and all its neighbors white. If
T^{v_{i}}
is finite we color all vertices but
v_{i}
white.

If
T^{v_{i}}
is infinite, then it is rayless, of motion at least
m
, and maximum degree at most
2^{m}
. Therefore it has
2^{|T^{v_{i}}|}
distinguishing colorings.
Let
I
be the set of indices
i
, for which
T^{v_{i}}
is infinite. Because the total order of the finite
T^{v_{i}}
is at most countable, we infer that
|T|=\sum_{i\geq 0}|T^{v_{i}}|=\sum_{i\in I}|T^{v_{i}}|
. Thus the number of distinguishing 2-colorings we thus obtain is

\prod_{i\in I}2^{|T^{v_{i}}|}=2^{\sum_{i\in I}|T^{v_{i}}|}=2^{|T|}.

∎
