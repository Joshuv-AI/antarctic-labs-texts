Effects of cleaning agents and Candida Biofilms on dental resin integrity
Authors: Muhammad K.H. Uddin, Anam Khan, Zulfiqar A. Mirani, Saeed-ur-Rehman, Ambreen Murtaza, Munazza R. Mirza, Aanya Naseem, Syeda M. Aziz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe effects of Candida albicans biofilm growth under treatment with cleaning agents on the deterioration of denture acrylic resin (PMMA) and a nano-filled restorative composite resin were examined in vitro.MaterialsA total of 240 PMMA (Travelon™) and nano-filled composite (Filtek™ Z350XT) specimens were fabricated. Baseline surface hardness VHN, arithmetic mean surface roughness (Sa, in nm), and flexural strength (ISO 20795-1) were measured. The specimens underwent a Candida albicans ATCC 10231 biofilm challenge for 90 days and were then immersed in 1% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) or 15% aqueous patchouli extract (Pogostemon cablin). Power analysis (α = 0.05, power = 80%) was used to determine sample sizes and effect sizes, and partial eta-squared (η²) was calculated for all significant comparisons.ResultsIn comparison to PMMA (98.60 ± 0.55 VHN; p < 0.001, η² = 0.92), the nano-filled composite showed significantly greater baseline hardness (156.67 ± 0.58 VHN) and surface smoothness (Sa = 75.60 ± 5.20 nm vs. 256.30 ± 18.50 nm; p < 0.001, η² = 0.89). Greater PMMA surface roughness was associated with significantly higher biofilm accumulation and mechanical deterioration (14.14% flexural strength decrease; p < 0.001). Significant degradation occurred after exposure to NaOCl, and PMMA showed the greatest flexural strength loss (36.13%; p < 0.001). In contrast, no significant material degradation was observed with patchouli extract (p > 0.05). Both disinfectants demonstrated limited efficacy against mature biofilms, whereas NaOCl was highly effective against planktonic C. albicans.ConclusionIn comparison to PMMA, nano-filled composite had superior surface and mechanical properties associated with enhanced resistance to chemical and biofilm-related deterioration. However, the constraints of the mono-species model warrant potential clinical consideration. NaOCl demonstrated substantial antifungal activity but was associated with severe material degradation, whereas patchouli extract preserved material integrity but exhibited modest anti-biofilm efficacy. Therefore, material-specific cleaning protocols might be warranted. Further investigation of complex models is necessary to balance antimicrobial effectiveness against long-term prosthetic durability.

Introduction

The longevity and success of dental prosthetics depend on a complex interplay among material properties, the oral biological environment, and maintenance protocols.1 A spectrum of materials, ranging from traditional options to advanced composites, are used in dentistry.2 Use of heat-cured acrylic resins, such as polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA, e.g., Travelon™), is widespread, because of their affordability and processability for denture bases.3 In contrast, advanced composite resins such as Filtek™ Z350XT (3M ESPE, a nano-filled restorative composite) are engineered for superior mechanical stability in direct restorations.4 Herein, this restorative composite served as a high-performance experimental benchmark with superior mechanical characteristics, which was compared against a conventional denture acrylic to assess the performance spectrum.5 However, this composite is not clinically indicated for denture bases and was selected to represent an optimal polymer class for comparative purposes.

Beyond baseline characteristics, material performance in the oral cavity involves resistance to mechanical stress, chemical exposure, and microbial colonization.4, 5, 6 Biofilm formation on dental appliances is a universal challenge that can compromise both material integrity and oral health.7 A prevalent biofilm inhabitant is Candida albicans, an opportunistic yeast whose characteristics can transition from commensal to pathogenic.8 C. albicans biofilms resist host defenses and antimicrobial agents7,8 and contribute to conditions such as denture stomatitis while promoting material degradation.9 Surface roughness critically influences microbial adhesion: rougher surfaces favor biofilm maturation.8,9 Microbial metabolic byproducts can progressively decrease mechanical properties, including flexural strength and hardness, and potentially shorten appliance lifespan.9

To mitigate biofilm-related complications, patients use cleaning agents, which introduce additional considerations.7 Potent disinfectants such as sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl), although antimicrobially effective, can degrade polymeric resins through oxidation and therefore compromise mechanical integrity.10 Consequently, interest in natural antimicrobial agents such as patchouli (Pogostemon cablin) leaf extract, which has demonstrated antifungal properties, has increased.11,12 However, the efficacy of patchouli against both planktonic and biofilm forms of C. albicans, and its effects on dental polymer properties require systematic evaluation.11,12

Although studies have been conducted on dental materials and biofilms, baseline polymer properties, susceptibility to long-term C. albicans biofilm formation, and the compounded effects of biofilms and various cleaning agents on material integrity have not been thoroughly investigated in a direct multi-factorial comparison.10 This study was aimed at addressing this research gap. Specifically, our objectives were to (1) compare the susceptibility of PMMA and a nano-filled composite to long-term C. albicans biofilm formation; (2) quantify the resultant material degradation; and (3) assess the antimicrobial efficacy and effects of 1% NaOCl and 15% patchouli leaf extract on the material. Our findings were intended to provide a comparative understanding of polymer performance, thereby contributing to the evidence base for prosthetic maintenance; however, we acknowledge the preliminary nature of our in vitro findings.

Materials and Methods

Study design and specimen allocation

This in vitro study used a factorial design evaluating both material (PMMA vs. mano-filled composite) and treatment. A total of 240 specimens (120 per material) were fabricated. On the basis of an a priori power analysis (G∗Power 3.1; effect size f = 0.4, α = 0.05, power = 80%), a minimum of five specimens per test per group was determined to be required. For each material, specimens were randomly allocated into six experimental groups (n = 20/group). From each group, n = 5 specimens were allocated to each specific test (flexural strength, hardness, roughness, and microbiological assay), to ensure that destructive tests were performed on separate specimens (detailed group allocation in Supplemental Table 1).•Group 1 (control): no biofilm, no cleaning agent (baseline measurements)•Group 2 (biofilm only): 90-day biofilm challenge•Group 3 (biofilm + NaOCl): 90-day biofilm challenge, then 10-min immersion in 1% NaOCl•Group 4 (biofilm + patchouli): 90-day biofilm challenge, then 45-min immersion in 15% patchouli extract•Group 5 (NaOCl only): no biofilm, 10-min immersion in 1% NaOCl•Group 6 (patchouli only): no biofilm, 45-min immersion in 15% patchouli extract

Materials

The study used two dental polymers: Travelon™ heat-cured acrylic resin (PMMA-based, batch no. 112-050, HLR Dentsply Pvt. Ltd., Germany) and Filtek™ Z350XT (nano-filled restorative composite, batch no. T 3090, 3M ESPE, USA). The composite resin served as a high-performance experimental benchmark representing an optimized polymer class but is not clinically indicated for denture base applications.

The antifungal agents were 1% NaOCl solution and an aqueous extract from patchouli leaves (Pogostemon cablin). The patchouli extract was prepared by soaking 100 g dried leaf powder in 1 L distilled water (4 °C for 48 h), followed by filtration and lyophilization. The concentrate was reconstituted to 15% (w/v) for immersion studies. The 15% concentration was selected according to preliminary minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assays and represented a practically feasible concentration above the minimum biofilm inhibitory concentration (MBIC). The fungal strain was C. albicans ATCC 10231.

PMMA specimens were processed via heat polymerization (74 °C for 8 h, then 100 °C for 1 h).13 Composite specimens were light-polymerized in silicone molds (20 s per surface, Valo Grand, Ultradent). All specimens were ground sequentially with 400–1200 grit SiC paper under irrigation and polished with a 1 μm diamond suspension to achieve a clinically relevant surface finish (target Ra <0.2 μm). Specimens were ultrasonically cleaned and stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 48 ± 2 h before baseline testing.

Mechanical and surface property analysis

All post-treatment measurements used dedicated specimens (n = 5 per group per test).•Flexural Strength: Three-point bend tests were conducted on a Universal Testing Machine (M500-5CT, Testometric) according to ISO 20795-118 (span = 50 mm; crosshead speed = 5 mm/min). Flexural strength (σ, in MPa) was calculated as σ = (3FL)/(2wh2), where F is the fracture load (in N), L is the span (in mm), w is the width (in mm), and h is the height (in mm). All values were verified against ISO 20795-1 requirements for denture base polymers (minimum 65 MPa).•Vickers Hardness: Tests were performed with a micro-hardness tester (Wolpert 402 MVD) with a 50 gf load for 15 s.18 Five indentations per specimen were averaged.•Surface Roughness: Analysis was performed with an atomic force microscope (5500 AFM, Keysight) in AC mode.9 Five 10 × 10 μm scans per specimen were obtained. The arithmetic mean roughness (Sa) was calculated in PicoImage software with a Gaussian filter to remove form errors.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed in SPSS v26.0. Normality was confirmed with the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was confirmed with Levene's test. For normally distributed data, two-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's HSD post-hoc test, was used to analyze the effects of material, treatment, and their interaction. Partial eta-squared (η2) effect sizes were calculated for all significant ANOVA results. For non-normal data, the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn's post-hoc test was used. A priori power analysis (G∗Power 3.1) confirmed that n = 5 per test per group would have 80% power to detect large effects (f = 0.4) at α = 0.05. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

MIC/MBIC assays demonstrated clear efficacy differences:•Sodium Hypochlorite (NaOCl): Treatment with NaOCl was highly effective against planktonic C. albicans (MIC = 0.003125% or 31.25 μg/mL) but was approximately 100-fold less effective against mature biofilm (MBIC = 0.3125% or 3125 μg/mL).•Patchouli Leaf Extract: Treatment with patchouli leaf extract showed moderate activity against planktonic cells (MIC = 0.25% or 2500 μg/mL) but required a 16 times greater concentration to inhibit biofilm (MBIC = 4.0% or 40,000 μg/mL). The 15% (150,000 μg/mL) solution used for immersion exceeded the MBIC, thus explaining its planktonic efficacy but limited activity against established 90-day biofilms (Figure 7).

Discussion

This study conducted a comprehensive in vitro analysis of degradation pathways affecting dental polymers, and delineated the roles of material properties, microbial biofilm activity, and chemical cleaning regimens. A key study strength is the multi-factorial design directly comparing a conventional PMMA denture base material against a high-performance nano-filled restorative composite, which provided an experimental benchmark for optimized polymer properties. However, this composite is not clinically indicated for denture base applications and instead represents an upper bound of polymer performance for comparative purposes.

After recalculation and verification against ISO 20795-1 standards, the corrected flexural strength values for PMMA (86.4 ± 3.2 MPa) confirmed that the material met the minimum requirement of 65 MPa for denture base polymers, thus validating the specimen fabrication protocol. The initial characterization established a clear performance hierarchy, in which the composite showed superior hardness and flexural strength (115.2 ± 4.1 MPa), and significantly smoother surface topography (Sa = 75.60 ± 8.45 nm; p < 0.001, η2 > 0.89). These inherent properties directly influenced subsequent performance.

The finding that the rougher PMMA surface (Sa = 256.30 ± 22.10 nm) supported significantly greater C. albicans biofilm biomass (p < 0.001, η2 = 0.76) was consistent with the established literature on surface topography and microbial adhesion.19,20 This heavy biofilm load directly catalyzed substantial PMMA mechanical degradation, as evidenced by a 14.14% flexural strength loss and 25.88% hardness loss. This degradation was likely to reflect synergistic effects of hyphal penetration and microbial byproducts (organic acids and enzymes) that can plasticize and hydrolyze the polymer matrix.20,21 The composite, despite its enhanced resistance, exhibits increased surface roughness after biofilm coverage.21 Therefore, even optimized polymers appear to be superficially altered by persistent biofilm, thus potentially initiating a cycle of increased roughness and microbial adhesion.20,21

Cleaning agents are a critical consideration for clinical practice, extending beyond antimicrobial efficacy to material safety.20 For example, 1% NaOCl, despite having potent antimicrobial activity, acts as a severe material stressor.22 Its oxidizing nature22 caused catastrophic damage to PMMA in our study, thus resulting in a 36.13% flexural strength loss (to 47.2 ± 4.2 MPa, below the ISO 65 MPa minimum), and induced significant degradation in the more stable composite (8.73% loss). Therefore, caution is necessary regarding the routine use of such harsh chemicals, which might potentially shorten prosthetic lifespan.22 In contrast, patchouli leaf extract was found to be a material-safe alternative causing no statistically significant deterioration (p > 0.05, η2 = 0.08). However, the extract's limited efficacy against mature biofilms (MBIC = 4.0% vs. MIC = 0.25%) is a key limitation. The observed efficacy–safety profiles suggested a potential hypothesis for future investigation: gentle, non-destructive agents might be considered for daily hygiene to prevent biofilm accumulation, whereas potent disinfectants might be reserved for infrequent, targeted use. However, this possibility requires clinical validation.

This study has several limitations that temper the generalizability of the findings. First, the use of a mono-species C. albicans biofilm, although controlled, does not replicate the complex polymicrobial oral ecology. Second, the 90-day static model lacked dynamic mechanical stresses (mastication or brushing) that clinically interact with chemical and biological degradation. Third, the restorative composite, although illustrative for benchmarking, is not indicated for denture bases, because of factors including impact resistance and fatigue behavior in thin sections, thereby limiting direct clinical extrapolation. Fourth, because of the in vitro nature of this study, clinical recommendations cannot be directly derived from the results. Instead, our findings generate hypotheses for future research under more representative conditions. Fifth, only a single concentration (15%) of patchouli extract was tested; future studies should therefore explore concentration gradients and combination strategies with other agents. Finally, although the statistical power was adequate to detect large effects, smaller but clinically relevant differences might have been undetected.23

Despite these limitations, this study bridges a gap by providing a holistic view of the interlinked factors governing polymer durability within a controlled in vitro framework. Our findings underscore the importance of considering material properties in developing maintenance protocols and highlight the need for further investigation using polymicrobial models, dynamic systems, and clinically relevant material combinations.

Conclusion

Despite the limitations of the vitro mono-species model used, our findings indicated that superior inherent material properties (greater hardness and smoother surface finish) were associated with enhanced resistance to C. albicans biofilm colonization and subsequent biological degradation in this experimental system. In addition, prolonged biofilm exposure was found to be a chronic stressor that led to significant mechanical degradation of PMMA. Therefore, biofilm control might potentially be important for both oral health and prosthetic material durability. The choice of cleaning agent might potentially require a compromise, given that NaOCl had potent antimicrobial action but caused substantial material damage (rendering PMMA flexural strength below ISO requirements), whereas patchouli extract preserved material integrity but demonstrated limited efficacy against established biofilms in this model. Our preliminary in vitro observations suggest that material-specific maintenance protocols warrant further investigation under conditions more representative of the oral environment. Future research should focus on optimizing natural extracts for enhanced anti-biofilm activity, evaluating combination strategies, and validating the findings in polymicrobial and dynamic models before clinical recommendations can be formulated.
