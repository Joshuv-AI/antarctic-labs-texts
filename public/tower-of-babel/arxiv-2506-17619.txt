A C 0 C^{0} weak Galerkin method with preconditioning for constrained optimal control problems with general tracking Thanks: Submitted to a journal in 2025.
SeongHee Jeong, Seulip Lee, Kening Wang

Abstract
This paper presents a C 0 C^{0} weak Galerkin ( C 0 C^{0} -WG) method combined with an additive Schwarz preconditioner for solving optimal control problems (OCPs) governed by partial differential equations with general tracking cost functionals and pointwise state constraints. These problems pose significant analytical and numerical challenges due to the presence of fourth-order variational inequalities and the reduced regularity of solutions. Our first contribution is the design of a C 0 C^{0} -WG method based on globally continuous quadratic Lagrange elements, enabling efficient elementwise stiffness matrix assembly and parameter-free implementation while maintaining accuracy, as supported by a rigorous error analysis. As a second contribution, we develop an additive Schwarz preconditioner tailored to the C 0 C^{0} -WG method to improve solver performance for the resulting ill-conditioned linear systems. Numerical experiments confirm the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed method and preconditioner for both biharmonic and optimal control problems. Keywords: Weak Galerkin; optimal control; general tracking; pointwise state constraints; fourth-order variational formulation; preconditioners



1
Introduction

Optimal control problems (OCPs) governed by partial differential equations (PDEs) arise naturally in dynamical systems where the goal is to control spatial-temporal processes described by physical laws.
These problems involve determining control inputs that optimize a performance criterion, such as minimizing energy usage or achieving a desired state distribution, while satisfying the underlying PDE constraints (see e.g.,
[
44
,
33
,
23
]
).
For instance, in heat conduction, the control may represent boundary heating to achieve uniform temperature distribution; in diffusion processes, it may involve regulating sources to maintain concentration levels; and in incompressible fluid flow, optimal control can be used to minimize drag or match velocity profiles by manipulating boundary inflow or body forces.

Among such OCPs, those involving
a general tracking cost functional
and
pointwise state constraints
are widely used in real-world applications, but often present significant analytical and numerical challenges.
General tracking refers to the objective of matching the system state to a desired target over various spatial features, such as
points
[
3
,
17
,
4
]
, curves, or subregions in the domain
[
10
,
28
]
.
Tracking over specific features inherently reduces the regularity of the optimal state and the associated Lagrange multipliers, potentially resulting in measure-valued multipliers. Moreover, pointwise state constraints, which impose lower and upper bounds on the state variable almost everywhere in the domain, introduce additional complexity.
The state constraints also degrade the regularity of the optimal solution, making it more challenging to enforce constraints in a numerically stable way and to achieve optimal convergence orders.
Therefore, addressing these difficulties remains a central challenge in the analysis and simulation of the constrained OCPs.

To this end, recent studies
[
10
,
28
]
propose reformulating the constrained OCPs by eliminating the control variable via the PDE constraint, leading to a reduced formulation with only the state variable.
When pointwise state constraints are applied, the reduced problem becomes a fourth-order variational inequality in the state variable. Once the optimal state is computed, the optimal control can be recovered using the PDE constraint.
For the numerical treatment of such fourth-order formulations, the
C^{0}
interior penalty (
C^{0}
-IP) methods, originally introduced in
[
12
]
, utilize globally continuous Lagrange elements with penalty terms to weakly enforce the continuity of first derivatives using a sufficiently large penalty parameter. Compared to conforming
C^{1}
finite elements, the
C^{0}
-IP methods are appealing for fourth-order problems such as biharmonic problems, as they employ standard Lagrange polynomial spaces, minimizing the degrees of freedom. These methods were recently adopted in
[
10
,
28
]
to solve the constrained OCPs.
However, the
C^{0}
-IP method in the symmetric case relies on careful tuning of penalty parameters to ensure stability and convergence, and it includes several trace terms that interfere with elementwise (or parallel) assembly of the stiffness matrix.

In this paper, we propose a weak Galerkin (WG) approach for the constrained OCPs using globally continuous quadratic Lagrange elements, aiming to enhance the efficiency of both numerical discretizations and linear solvers.
WG methods, based on weakly defined differential operators, offer several advantages across a variety of PDEs, including flexibility for complex geometries through the use of polygonal meshes, elementwise local formulations, and the ability to vary polynomial degrees across elements (see, e.g.,
[
45
,
31
,
34
,
36
,
38
,
46
]
).
Especially for biharmonic problems, WG methods based on the weak Laplacian have been developed in
[
37
,
35
,
47
,
49
]
. A recent study
[
30
]
further demonstrated that a WG formulation using the weak gradient can significantly improve the efficiency of stiffness matrix assembly by replacing symmetric interior penalty formulations in the Stokes equations
[
48
,
25
]
.
Motivated by these advances, we adopt a WG framework to address the constrained OCPs governed by a fourth-order variational inequality and subject to pointwise state constraints, where efficient and robust biharmonic discretizations are essential.

Our first contribution is the development of a
C^{0}
weak Galerkin (
C^{0}
-WG) method that employs globally continuous quadratic Lagrange elements to simplify stiffness matrix assembly, eliminating the trace terms and the penalty parameter required in the symmetric
C^{0}
-IP method. The discrete weak Laplacian is locally computed as a piecewise constant function in each element, using function values at the barycenters of interfaces shared by two neighboring elements, along with basic geometric data such as lengths of edges and areas of elements in the two-dimensional setting (details will be provided in Section
3
).
In the
C^{0}
-WG method, the bilinear form is assembled from the
L^{2}
inner product of the weak Laplacians and a parameter-free penalty (or stabilization) term.
As a result, the proposed method offers efficient matrix assembly and stable performance independent of tuning parameters.
Furthermore, we present a rigorous error analysis within the WG framework, establishing the well-posedness and accuracy of the method for the constrained OCPs by drawing a connection to the existing
C^{0}
-IP method
[
10
]
.

As a second contribution, we develop an additive Schwarz preconditioner tailored to the
C^{0}
-WG method to address the computational challenges posed by ill-conditioned systems arising from fourth-order PDEs. In particular, when the cost functional involves general tracking terms, the resulting system matrix often exhibits a large condition number, which can severely degrade solver performance. To mitigate this issue, we adopt a one-level additive Schwarz preconditioner within the framework of
[
13
]
. This domain decomposition technique partitions the global problem into overlapping subdomain problems, each solved independently, and combines their solutions additively to form a global approximation. This approach is particularly effective for parallel computing and has been successfully applied in large-scale simulations
[
11
,
39
]
. For the proposed WG formulation, the preconditioner significantly improves the boundedness of the condition number, enabling efficient and scalable iterative solvers for the constrained OCPs.

The
C^{0}
-WG method and preconditioner are developed for both two- and three-dimensional settings. For the constrained OCPs, the discussion focuses on two dimensions due to the complexity introduced by the general tracking terms; however, the approach can be extended to three dimensions without significant difficulty. Accordingly, the numerical experiments are also presented in two-dimensional settings.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
introduces key notation and definitions, presents the constrained OCPs, and reviews existing approaches.
In Section
3
, we recall the definition of the weak Laplacian and introduce a WG discretization for the constrained OCPs.
Section
4
provides a convergence analysis for the
C^{0}
-WG method applied to the OCPs.
An additive Schwarz preconditioner for the resulting discrete system is discussed in Section
5
.
Finally, Section
6
presents numerical experiments for the proposed numerical method applied to both biharmonic problems and the constrained OCPs, including a comparison of condition numbers with and without the proposed preconditioner. We summarize our contributions and discuss related research directions in Section
7
.

Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce key notation and definitions, present the optimal control problems with general tracking and state constraints, and review existing numerical approaches.

2.1
Notation and Definitions

We introduce the notation and definitions used throughout this paper.
Let
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain, where
d=2
or 3. For any real number
s\geq 0
, we denote the Sobolev space on
\mathcal{D}
by
H^{s}(\mathcal{D})
, with associated norm
\|\cdot\|_{s,\mathcal{D}}
and seminorm
|\cdot|_{s,\mathcal{D}}
. In particular,
H^{0}(\mathcal{D})=L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
, and the
L^{2}
inner product is denoted by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathcal{D}}
. When
\mathcal{D}=\Omega
, where
\Omega
is the domain associated with the optimal control problems (see Section
2.2
), the subscript
\mathcal{D}
will be omitted for brevity. This notation naturally extends to vector- and tensor-valued Sobolev spaces.
We denote by
H_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{D})
the space of functions in
H^{1}(\mathcal{D})
with vanishing trace on
\partial\mathcal{D}
, and by
H^{2}_{0}(\mathcal{D})
the space of functions in
H^{2}(\mathcal{D})
with vanishing trace and vanishing normal flux on
\partial\mathcal{D}
.
The space of polynomials of degree less than or equal to
k
on
\mathcal{D}
is denoted by
P_{k}(\mathcal{D})
.
We also define the Hilbert space

H(\mathrm{div},\mathcal{D}):=\left\{\mathbf{v}\in[L^{2}(\mathcal{D})]^{2}\,:\,\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{v}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})\right\}

equipped with the norm

\|\mathbf{v}\|_{H(\mathrm{div},\mathcal{D})}^{2}:=\|\mathbf{v}\|_{0,\mathcal{D}}^{2}+\|\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{v}\|_{0,\mathcal{D}}^{2}.

For the discrete setting, let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a shape-regular triangulation of
\Omega
, where each element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
is either a triangle or a tetrahedron. Let
\mathcal{E}_{h}
denote the set of all edges or faces, which is partitioned into interior edges or faces
\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}
and boundary edges or faces
\mathcal{E}_{h}^{b}
.
For any element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, we denote its diameter by
h_{T}
and its outward unit normal vector on
\partial T
by
\mathbf{n}_{T}
. For each interior interface
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}
shared by neighboring elements
T^{+}
and
T^{-}
, we define
\mathbf{n}_{e}
to be the unit normal vector pointing from
T^{-}
to
T^{+}
. For a boundary edge or face
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{b}
,
\mathbf{n}_{e}
denotes the outward unit normal vector on
\partial\Omega
.

The broken Sobolev space associated with
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is defined as

H^{s}(\mathcal{T}_{h}):=\left\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega)\,:\,v|_{T}\in H^{s}(T),\ \forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\},

with the norm

\|v\|_{s,\mathcal{T}_{h}}:=\left(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\|v\|_{s,T}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

For
s=0
, the
L^{2}
inner product over
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is denoted by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
. Similarly, the
L^{2}
inner product and norm on
\mathcal{E}_{h}
are denoted by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}}
and

\|v\|_{0,\mathcal{E}_{h}}:=\left(\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}}\|v\|_{0,e}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

The piecewise polynomial space over
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is defined as

P_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h}):=\left\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega)\,:\,v|_{T}\in P_{k}(T),\ \forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\}.

For any function
v
, the jump and average across
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}
are defined as

[v]=\begin{cases}v^{+}-v^{-}&\text{on }e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o},\\
v&\text{on }e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{b},\end{cases}\qquad\{v\}=\begin{cases}(v^{+}+v^{-})/2&\text{on }e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o},\\
v&\text{on }e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{b},\end{cases}

where
v^{\pm}
denotes the trace of
v|_{T^{\pm}}
on
e=\partial T^{+}\cap\partial T^{-}
.
These definitions extend naturally to vector- and tensor-valued functions.

We also recall the following identity and trace inequality used in this paper. For any vector-valued function
\mathbf{v}
and scalar function
q
, we have

\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\langle\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{T},q\rangle_{\partial T}=\langle[\mathbf{v}]\cdot\mathbf{n}_{e},\{q\}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}}+\langle\{\mathbf{v}\}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{e},[q]\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}.

(2.1)

Moreover, for any
v\in H^{1}(T)
and
e\subset\partial T
, the following trace inequality holds:

\|v\|_{0,e}^{2}\leq C\left(h_{T}^{-1}\|v\|_{0,T}^{2}+h_{T}\|\nabla v\|_{0,T}^{2}\right),

(2.2)

where
C>0
is a constant depending only on the shape regularity of the mesh.

2.2
Optimal Control Problems

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a convex, bounded, and connected Lipschitz domain with boundary
\partial\Omega
.
The optimal control problems (OCPs) considered in this work aim to find

\left(\bar{y},\bar{u}\right)=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{(y,u)\in\mathbb{U}}\frac{1}{2}\left[\int_{\Omega}|y-y_{d}|^{2}d\chi+\beta\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2}d{\bf x}\right],

(2.3)

where
\mathbb{U}\subset H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
,
y_{d}
is a desired state (to be defined in (
2.7
)), and
\beta>0
. The optimization is subject to the elliptic PDE:

\displaystyle-\Delta y=u\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(2.4a)

\displaystyle y=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega,

(2.4b)

and the pointwise state constraints:

\psi_{-}({\bf x})\leq y({\bf x})\leq\psi_{+}({\bf x})\quad\text{a.e. }\;{\bf x}=(x,y)\in\Omega.

(2.5)

In (
2.3
), we recall the definition of the Radon measure
\chi
on
\bar{\Omega}
in
[
10
,
28
]
:

\int_{\Omega}f\,d\chi=\sum_{j=1}^{J}f(\mathscr{P}_{j})w^{j}_{\mathscr{P}}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\int_{\mathscr{C}_{l}}fw^{l}_{\mathscr{C}}\,ds+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\int_{\mathscr{E}_{m}}fw^{m}_{\mathscr{E}}\,d{\bf x},

(2.6)

where:

•

\mathscr{P}=\{\mathscr{P}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{P}_{J}\}
is a finite set of points in
\Omega
,

•

\mathscr{C}=\{\mathscr{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{C}_{L}\}

is a collection of curves where
\mathscr{C}_{l}\subset\Omega
,

•

\mathscr{E}=\{\mathscr{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{E}_{M}\}
is a collection of subdomains
\mathscr{E}_{m}\subset\Omega
.

The weight functions
w_{\mathscr{P}}^{j}
,
w_{\mathscr{C}}^{l}
and
w^{m}_{\mathscr{E}}
are bounded, nonnegative Borel-measurable functions defined on
\mathscr{P}
,
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{E}
, respectively.
The desired state
y_{d}
is a target function for the state function
y
, defined by

y_{d}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}y_{\mathscr{P}}&\text{on }\mathscr{P},\\
y_{\mathscr{C}}&\text{on }\mathscr{C}\setminus\mathscr{P},\\
y_{\mathscr{E}}&\text{on }\mathscr{E}\setminus(\mathscr{C}\cup\mathscr{P}),\end{array}\right.

(2.7)

such that

\|y_{d}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega;\chi)}:=\int_{\Omega}|y_{d}|^{2}d\chi<\infty.

We also assume that the functions
\psi_{\pm}
in (
2.5
) satisfy the following conditions
[
10
]
:

\displaystyle\psi_{\pm}\in W^{3,q}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\text{\quad for }q>2,

(2.8a)

\displaystyle\psi_{-}<\psi_{+}

\displaystyle\text{\quad on }\bar{\Omega},

(2.8b)

\displaystyle\psi_{-}<0<\psi_{+}

\displaystyle\text{\quad on }\partial\Omega.

(2.8c)

In this work, instead of solving simultaneously for both the state
y
and the control
u
, we reformulate the OCPs (
2.3
) by substituting the elliptic PDE (
2.4a
) directly into the cost functional.

Reduced Optimal Control Problems

Find
\bar{y}\in U
such that

\bar{y}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{y\in U}\frac{1}{2}\left[\int_{\Omega}|y-y_{d}|^{2}d\chi+\beta\int_{\Omega}|\Delta y|^{2}d{\bf x}\right],

(2.9)

where the admissible set is given by

U=\{y\in H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\,:\,\psi_{-}\leq y\leq\psi_{+}\text{ in }\Omega\}.

(2.10)

Note that, according to (
2.8b
)-(
2.8c
),
the admissible set
U
is nonempty.

Remark 2.1
.

As a result of the convexity of
\Omega
, the constraint (
2.4a
) implies
that
y\in H^{2}(\Omega)
for any

(y,u)\in\mathbb{U}
, by elliptic regularity.
Therefore, the cost functional in (
2.3
)
is well-defined by the Sobolev embedding theorem
[
1
]
(
H^{2}(\Omega)\subset C(\bar{\Omega})
).

The reduced OCPs (
2.9
) admits a unique solution
\bar{y}\in U
, which is characterized by the following fourth-order variational inequality
[
29
]
:

\beta\int_{\Omega}(\Delta\bar{y})(\Delta(y-\bar{y}))\,d{\bf x}+\int_{\Omega}(\bar{y}-y_{d})(y-\bar{y})\,d\chi\geq 0,\quad\forall y\in U.

(2.11)

This variational inequality is equivalent to the following generalized Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions
[
26
,
40
]
:

\beta\int_{\Omega}(\Delta\bar{y})(\Delta z)\,d{\bf x}+\int_{\Omega}(\bar{y}-y_{d})z\,d\chi=\int_{\Omega}z\,d\xi,\quad\forall z\in H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

(2.12)

where
\xi
is a bounded regular Borel measure satisfying the conditions

\displaystyle\xi\geq 0

\displaystyle\quad\text{if }\bar{y}=\psi_{-},

(2.13a)

\displaystyle\xi\leq 0

\displaystyle\quad\text{if }\bar{y}=\psi_{+},

(2.13b)

\displaystyle\xi=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{otherwise}.

(2.13c)

We refer the reader to
[
27
]
for a detailed derivation of the KKT conditions.

We conclude this section by presenting the regularity results for the optimal state
\bar{y}
of the reduced problem (
2.9
).
It is known
[
2
,
24
,
10
]
that the solution satisfies the local regularity:

\bar{y}\in W^{3,s}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega),\quad\forall s\in(1,2).

Furthermore, by global regularity theory
[
22
,
20
,
10
]
, we have

\bar{y}\in H^{2+\alpha}(\Omega),

(2.14)

for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, where
\alpha
depends on the geometric regularity of
\Omega
. In the case where
\Omega
is a rectangle, it is known
that
\alpha=1-\epsilon
for any
\epsilon>0
; see
[
20
,
18
]
for more details.

2.3
Existing Approaches to the Optimal Control Problems

We begin by introducing the bilinear form
(see, e.g.,
[
22
]
):

a(v,w)=\int_{\Omega}(\Delta v)(\Delta w)\,d{\bf x}=\int_{\Omega}D^{2}v:D^{2}w\,d{\bf x},\quad\forall v,w\in H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

(2.15)

where
D^{2}v
denotes the Hessian matrix of
v
, and
D^{2}v:D^{2}w
represents the Frobenius inner product of
D^{2}v
and
D^{2}w
.

Let
V_{h}\subset H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
be the Lagrange finite element space of degree
k\geq 2
associated with

\mathcal{T}_{h}
, consisting of globally continuous, piecewise polynomials from
P_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
.
The discrete bilinear form in the
C^{0}
interior penalty (
C^{0}
-IP) method
[
12
]
is defined for all
v,w\in V_{h}
as

\displaystyle a_{h}(v,w)

\displaystyle=(D^{2}v,D^{2}w)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\langle\{\nabla(\nabla v\cdot{\bf n}_{e})\}\cdot{\bf n}_{e},[\nabla w]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\langle\{\nabla(\nabla w\cdot{\bf n}_{e})\}\cdot{\bf n}_{e},[\nabla v]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}+\rho\langle h_{e}^{-1}[\nabla v]\cdot{\bf n}_{e},[\nabla w]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}.

(2.16)

Here,
\rho>0
is a penalty parameter, and
h_{e}=|e|^{1/(d-1)}
, where
|e|
denotes the measure of the interfaces
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}
.

In the discrete bilinear form, although the finite element functions in
V_{h}
are globally continuous, their normal derivatives are discontinuous across interfaces
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}
because the functions do not belong to
H^{2}(\Omega)
. This discontinuity in the normal derivatives gives rise to the jump and average terms across interfaces in the discrete bilinear form.
Moreover, thanks to the symmetric structure of the discrete bilinear form and the inclusion of a penalty term, the bilinear form
a_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
becomes symmetric positive-definite (SPD) with a sufficiently large penalty parameter
\rho
, ensuring that the discrete problem preserves the SPD property of the continuous bilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
in (
2.15
).

Using the
C^{0}
-IP method, we discretize the reduced OCPs (
2.9
) (see
[
10
,
28
]
for futher details), formulated as

\bar{y}_{h}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{y_{h}\in U_{h}}\frac{1}{2}\left[\beta a_{h}(y_{h},y_{h})+\|y_{h}-y_{d}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega;\chi)}\right],

(2.17)

where
a_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined in (
2.16
), and
U_{h}
is a discrete admissible set corresponding to
U
in (
2.10
) (details will be provided in Section
3.4
).

Remark 2.2
.

The
C^{0}
-IP method offers a significant advantage over
C^{1}
-conforming and other higher-order finite element methods by requiring a minimal number of degrees of freedom.
Whereas
C^{1}
-conforming elements necessitate complex shape functions and a substantial increase in degrees of freedom to achieve higher continuity, the
C^{0}
-IP method overcomes these challenges by employing standard Lagrange elements and enforcing higher continuity weakly through interior penalty terms.
As a result, it enables a much simpler implementation and more efficient use of computational resources, particularly for fourth-order problems.

Remark 2.3
.

The
C^{0}
-IP method
[
12
]
for the biharmonic problem in
H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
,

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}u

\displaystyle=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,

(2.18a)

\displaystyle u=\Delta u

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

(2.18b)

seeks
u_{h}\in V_{h}
such that

a_{h}(u_{h},v_{h})=(f,v_{h}),\quad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}.

(2.19)

With the mesh-dependent norm

\lVert v\rVert_{h}^{2}=(D^{2}v,D^{2}v)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\langle h_{e}^{-1}[\nabla v]\cdot{\bf n}_{e},[\nabla v]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}},

(2.20)

there exist positive constants
\eta_{*}
and
\eta^{*}
such that

\displaystyle a_{h}(v,v)\geq\eta_{*}\lVert v\rVert_{h}^{2},

(2.21)

\displaystyle a_{h}(v,w)\leq\eta^{*}\lVert v\rVert_{h}\lVert w\rVert_{h}.

(2.22)

Therefore, the discrete problem (
2.19
) is well-posed; see
[
12
]
for further details.

3
A
C^{0}
Weak Galerkin Method for the Optimal Control Problems

In this section, we propose a
C^{0}
weak Galerkin (
C^{0}
-WG) finite element method for the optimal control problems described in (
2.9
), using globally continuous quadratic Lagrange elements together with the weak Laplacian
[
35
,
37
]
.
In contrast to the WG methods in
[
35
,
37
]
, which are formulated for the space
H_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
, our method addresses biharmonic and optimal control problems in the space
H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.
When applied to biharmonic problems using the same quadratic Lagrange elements, the proposed
C^{0}
-WG method results in a bilinear form that is simpler than that of the
C^{0}
-IP method (
2.16
).

3.1
Weak Laplacian

We introduce the space of weak functions
[
35
,
37
]
on each element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
:

\mathcal{W}(T)=\left\{\nu=\left\{\nu_{0},\nu_{b},\bm{\nu}_{g}\right\}\;:\;\nu_{0}\in L^{2}(T),\ \nu_{b}\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial T),\ \bm{\nu}_{g}\cdot{\bf n}_{T}\in H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\partial T)\right\}

(3.1)

Each component of
\nu
corresponds to a term arising from the integration by parts formula for the Laplacian:

\displaystyle(\Delta v,\varphi)_{T}=(v,\Delta\varphi)_{T}-\langle v,\nabla\varphi\cdot{\bf n}_{T}\rangle_{\partial T}+\langle\nabla v\cdot{\bf n}_{T},\varphi\rangle_{\partial T}.

More precisely, the correspondences are given by

v|_{T}\ \leftrightarrow\ \nu_{0},\quad v|_{\partial T}\ \leftrightarrow\ \nu_{b},\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla v|_{\partial T}\ \leftrightarrow\ \bm{\nu}_{g}.

We also introduce the test function space

\mathcal{G}_{2}(T)=\left\{\varphi\;:\;\varphi\in H^{1}(T),\ \Delta\varphi\in L^{2}(T)\right\},

highlighting that

\displaystyle\nabla\varphi\in H(\text{div},T)
, so

\displaystyle\nabla\varphi\cdot{\bf n}_{T}\in H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\partial T)
for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{G}_{2}(T)
.

Definition 3.1

(Weak Laplacian
[
35
,
37
]
)
.

The weak Laplacian of
\nu=\{\nu_{0},\nu_{b},\bm{\nu_{g}}\}\in\mathcal{W}(T)
is defined as a linear functional
\Delta_{w}\nu
in the dual space of
\mathcal{G}_{2}(T)
, whose action is given by

(\Delta_{w}\nu,\varphi)_{T}=(\nu_{0},\Delta\varphi)_{T}-\langle\nu_{b},\nabla\varphi\cdot{\bf n}_{T}\rangle_{\partial T}+\langle\bm{\nu}_{g}\cdot{\bf n}_{T},\varphi\rangle_{\partial T},\quad\forall\varphi\in\mathcal{G}_{2}(T).

(3.2)

In a finite-dimensional setting, we introduce the discrete weak function space
[
35
]
,

\mathcal{W}_{k}(T)=\left\{\nu=\left\{\nu_{0},\nu_{b},\bm{\nu}_{g}\right\}\;:\ \nu_{0}\in P_{k}(T),\ \nu_{b}\in P_{k}(e),\ \bm{\nu}_{g}\in[P_{k-1}(e)]^{d},\ e\subset\partial T\right\}

for any given integer
k\geq 2
.

Definition 3.2

(Discrete weak Laplacian operator
[
35
]
)
.

For
\nu=\{\nu_{0},\nu_{b},\bm{\nu}_{g}\}\in\mathcal{W}_{k}(T)
, the discrete weak Laplacian operator is defined as the unique polynomial

\Delta_{w}\nu\in P_{r}(T)
for
r\geq 0
satisfying

(\Delta_{w}\nu,p_{r})_{T}=(\nu_{0},\Delta p_{r})_{T}-\langle\nu_{b},\nabla p_{r}\cdot{\bf n}_{T}\rangle_{\partial T}+\langle\bm{\nu}_{g}\cdot{\bf n}_{T},p_{r}\rangle_{\partial T},\quad\forall p_{r}\in P_{r}(T).

(3.3)

We emphasize that a continuous (or
C^{0}
) WG method, first introduced in
[
37
]
, considers discrete weak functions
\nu=\{\nu_{0},\nu_{0},\bm{\nu}_{g}\}
, where
\nu_{b}
is simply the trace of
\nu_{0}
on
\partial T
.
Moreover, a global WG finite element space
[
35
]
is defined by patching
\mathcal{W}_{k}(T)
over all the elements
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
while enforcing common values on interior interfaces
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}
:

\mathcal{W}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})=\left\{\nu=\{\nu_{0},\nu_{b},\bm{\nu}_{g}\}\,:\,\nu|_{T}=\{\nu_{0}|_{T},\nu_{b}|_{\partial T},\bm{\nu}_{g}|_{\partial T}\}\in\mathcal{W}_{k}(T),\ \forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\}.

The corresponding subspace incorporating homogeneous boundary conditions is defined as

\mathcal{W}_{k}^{0}(\mathcal{T}_{h})=\left\{\nu=\{\nu_{0},\nu_{b},\bm{\nu}_{g}\}\in\mathcal{W}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\,:\,\nu_{b}|_{e}=0,\ \forall e\subset\partial\Omega\right\}.

Therefore, the general WG finite element method
[
35
]
for biharmonic problems seeks
\mu=\{\mu_{0},\mu_{b},\bm{\mu}_{g}\}\in\mathcal{W}_{k}^{0}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
such that

(\Delta_{w}\mu,\Delta_{w}\nu)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}+s(\mu,\nu)=(f,\nu_{0}),\quad\forall\nu=\{\nu_{0},\nu_{b},\bm{\nu}_{g}\}\in\mathcal{W}_{k}^{0}(\mathcal{T}_{h}),

(3.4)

where the stabilizer
s(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined by

s(\mu,\nu)=\langle h_{e}^{-1}[\nabla\mu_{0}-\bm{\mu}_{g}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e},[\nabla\nu_{0}-\bm{\nu}_{g}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}}+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\langle h_{T}^{-3}(\mu_{0}-\mu_{b}),\nu_{0}-\nu_{b}\rangle_{\partial T}.

(3.5)

We observe that, in the continuous setting (i.e.,
\nu_{b}=\nu_{0}
on
\partial T
), the second term of the stabilizer vanishes, resulting in a simpler stabilizer.
Furthermore, selecting
r>k
in (
3.3
) allows the formulation in (
3.4
) to eliminate the need for the stabilizer, as demonstrated in stabilizer-free WG methods
[
47
,
49
]
.
However, using such high-order discrete weak Laplacian operators significantly increases the computational complexity, especially for
r>k\geq 2
.

3.2
A Weak Galerkin Interpretation of the
C^{0}
Interior Penalty Method

As discussed in Section
2.3
, the
C^{0}
-IP method
[
12
]
uses a finite-dimensional space based on globally continuous, piecewise
P_{k}
Lagrange polynomials, which reduces the degrees of freedom for biharmonic problems while utilizing interior penalty methods.
In this context, we interpret the
C^{0}
-IP finite element space incorporating the discrete weak Laplacian operator (
3.3
) and demonstrate that this approach results in a simplified formulation for biharmonic problems.

We recall that
V_{h}\subset H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
is the set of globally continuous, piecewise
P_{k}
Lagrange polynomials used in the
C^{0}
-IP method. While
k\geq 2
in general, we fix
k=2
to minimize the degrees of freedom and to derive an efficient formula for the discrete weak Laplacian.
Then, any function
v_{h}\in V_{h}
can be interpreted as a discrete weak function in
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
.
Specifically,

\nu_{0}=v_{h},\quad\nu_{b}=v_{h},\quad\bm{\nu}_{g}=\{\nabla v_{h}\}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\{v_{h},v_{h},\{\nabla v_{h}\}\}\in\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mathcal{T}_{h}),

since
v_{h}
is continuous, but its flux is discontinuous.
Moreover, the discrete weak Laplacian operator (
3.3
) for
v_{h}\in V_{h}
is defined as
\Delta_{w}v_{h}|_{T}\in P_{r}(T)
for
r\geq 0
, satisfying

(\Delta_{w}v_{h},p_{r})_{T}=(v_{h},\Delta p_{r})_{T}-\langle v_{h},\nabla p_{r}\cdot{\bf n}_{T}\rangle_{\partial T}+\langle\{\nabla v_{h}\}\cdot{\bf n}_{T},p_{r}\rangle_{\partial T},\quad\forall p_{r}\in P_{r}(T).

While various options exist for choosing the polynomial degree
r\geq 0
, we primarily focus on the lowest-order discrete weak Laplacian, where
\Delta_{w}v_{h}|_{T}\in P_{0}(T)
, and compare it with
\Delta v_{h}|_{T}\in P_{0}(T)
in the
C^{0}
-IP method.
In the case where
p_{0}\in P_{0}(T)
, the formula for the discrete weak Laplacian for
v_{h}\in V_{h}
simplifies to

(\Delta_{w}v_{h},p_{0})_{T}=\langle\{\nabla v_{h}\}\cdot{\bf n}_{T},p_{0}\rangle_{\partial T},\quad\forall p_{0}\in P_{0}(T).

(3.6)

Remark 3.3
.

For any
v_{h}\in V_{h}
, the difference between the discrete weak Laplacian and the standard Laplacian is expressed as

\left(\Delta_{w}v_{h}-\Delta v_{h},p_{0}\right)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}=\langle[\nabla v_{h}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e},\{p_{0}\}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}},\quad\forall p_{0}\in P_{0}(\mathcal{T}_{h}).

(3.7)

This identity follows directly from the definition of the discrete weak Laplacian, integration by parts, and the trace property (
2.1
).

Since both
\Delta_{w}v_{h}
and
p_{0}
are constant in each
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, we set
p_{0}=1
and divide by
|T|
on both sides to derive an explicit expression for the weak Laplacian on
T
,

\left.\Delta_{w}v_{h}\right|_{T}=\frac{1}{|T|}\int_{\partial T}\{\nabla v_{h}\}\cdot{\bf n}_{T}\,ds.

We note that
\{\nabla v_{h}\}\cdot{\bf n}_{T}
is a linear function on each edge or face
e\subset\partial T
, which ensures that the one-point quadrature rule yields exact integration.
Consequently, the final expression becomes

\left.\Delta_{w}v_{h}\right|_{T}=\frac{1}{|T|}\sum_{e\subset\partial T}\frac{|e|}{2}\big(\nabla v_{h}^{+}(\mathbf{m}_{e})+\nabla v_{h}^{-}(\mathbf{m}_{e})\big)\cdot({\bf n}_{T}|_{e}),

(3.8)

where
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}
is an interior interface shared by neighboring elements
T^{+}
and
T^{-}
, and
\mathbf{m}_{e}
denotes the barycenter of
e
.
When
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{b}
, the formula (
3.8
) is adjusted by using
\{\nabla v_{h}\}=\nabla v_{h}
.

Additionally, for the stabilizer
s(\cdot,\cdot)
in (
3.5
), we observe the following:

\nu_{0}-\nu_{b}=v_{h}-v_{h}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla\nu_{0}-\bm{\nu}_{g}=\nabla v_{h}-\{\nabla v_{h}\}=\pm\frac{1}{2}[\nabla v_{h}],

where the sign of the jump depends on the orientation of the elements sharing an interface.
On a boundary edge or face
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{b}
, it is straightforward to verify that
\nu_{0}-\nu_{b}=v_{h}-v_{h}=0
and
\nabla\nu_{0}-\bm{\nu}_{g}=\nabla v_{h}-\{\nabla v_{h}\}=\nabla v_{h}-\nabla v_{h}=\mathbf{0}
.
Therefore, the stabilizer
s(u_{h},v_{h})
for
u_{h},v_{h}\in V_{h}
is defined as

s(u_{h},v_{h})=\frac{1}{4}\langle h_{e}^{-1}[\nabla u_{h}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e},[\nabla v_{h}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}.

(3.9)

Remark 3.4
.

In contrast to the
C^{0}
-IP method, which requires a sufficiently large penalty parameter
\rho
in the penalty term
\rho\langle h_{e}^{-1}[\nabla u_{h}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e},[\nabla v_{h}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}
, our proposed method is parameter-free.
Moreover, the formula (
3.8
) enables elementwise assembly of the stiffness matrix without the need to compute certain trace terms in (
2.16
).
See
[
30
]
for a detailed comparison.

3.3
A
C^{0}
Weak Galerkin Method for Biharmonic Problems

Therefore, we propose a
C^{0}
weak Galerkin (
C^{0}
-WG) method, formulated using the discrete weak Laplacian and the stabilizer that are compatible with the finite-element functions
v_{h}\in V_{h}
.
In the following, we first apply the
C^{0}
-WG method to the biharmonic problem (
2.18
).

C^{0}
Weak Galerkin Method for Biharmonic Problems

Find
u_{h}\in V_{h}
such that

a_{w}(u_{h},v_{h})=(f,v_{h}),\quad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h},

(3.10)

where

\displaystyle a_{w}(v,w)=(\Delta_{w}v,\Delta_{w}w)_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\frac{1}{4}\langle h_{e}^{-1}[\nabla v]\cdot{\bf n}_{e},[\nabla w]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}.

(3.11)

The weak Laplacian
\Delta_{w}v|_{T}\in P_{0}(T)
is computed using the explicit formula (
3.8
), which depends solely on
\nabla v
.
The stabilizer, as defined in (
3.9
), is analogous to the penalty term in the
C^{0}
-IP bilinear form (
2.16
), but it does not include a penalty parameter.

Following
[
37
,
35
]
, we define the mesh-dependent norm associated with the bilinear form
a_{w}(\cdot,\cdot)
by

\lVert v\rVert_{w}^{2}:=a_{w}(v,v),\quad\forall v\in V_{h}.

(3.12)

Then, the following coercivity and continuity properties hold with respect to this norm.

Lemma 3.5
.

For any
v,w\in V_{h}
, the bilinear form
a_{w}(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies

\displaystyle a_{w}(v,v)=\lVert v\rVert_{w}^{2},

(3.13)

\displaystyle|a_{w}(v,w)|\leq\lVert v\rVert_{w}\lVert w\rVert_{w}.

(3.14)

Therefore, the discrete biharmonic problem (
3.10
) is well-posed.

Remark 3.6
.

Our proposed method (
3.10
) achieves the optimal order of convergence (first order with respect to
h=\max_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{T}
in the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{w}
), following the convergence analysis developed in
[
37
,
35
]
.
This convergence order is consistent with the optimal convergence order obtained by the
C^{0}
-IP method
[
12
]
.
Moreover, the norm equivalence that we will establish in Lemma
4.1
further supports this observation.
Corresponding numerical results will be presented in Section
6.1
.

Remark 3.7
.

The
C^{0}
-WG method (
3.10
) does not require a penalty parameter, whereas the
C^{0}
-IP method (
2.19
) relies on a sufficiently large penalty parameter
\rho
.
Furthermore, as shown in (
3.8
) and (
3.9
), the bilinear form
a_{w}(\cdot,\cdot)
is computed solely using
\nabla v|_{T}
for all
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, in contrast to the
C^{0}
-IP bilinear form (
2.16
), which involves second-order derivatives. The proposed bilinear form is assembled using the
L^{2}
inner product of weak Laplacians and a parameter-free penalty term, whereas the
C^{0}
-IP method includes additional trace terms.
As a result, the proposed method is penalty-parameter-free and offers reduced computational complexity (see
[
30
]
for a detailed comparison).

-WG method to the reduced optimal control problems (

) to derive its discrete formulation.

Discrete Optimal Control Problems

Find
\bar{y}_{h}\in U_{h}
such that

\bar{y}_{h}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{y_{h}\in U_{h}}\frac{1}{2}\left[\beta a_{w}(y_{h},y_{h})+\|y_{h}-y_{d}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega;\chi)}\right],

(3.15)

where

U_{h}=\{y_{h}\in V_{h}\,:\,I_{h}\psi_{-}\leq I_{h}y_{h}\leq I_{h}\psi_{+}\},

(3.16)

and
I_{h}
is the
P_{1}
nodal interpolation operator associated with
\mathcal{T}_{h}
.

Let

By (

maps

into

, and
U_{h}
is nonempty.
Therefore, the discrete problem (

\mathcal{A}_{w}(\bar{y}_{h},z_{h}-\bar{y}_{h})-\int_{\Omega}y_{d}(z_{h}-\bar{y}_{h})\,d\chi\geq 0,\quad\forall z_{h}\in U_{h},

(3.17)

(3.19)

Proof.

For any
v_{h}\in V_{h}
, choosing

4.1
Key Estimates

To proceed with the convergence analysis, we first establish an estimate for a key term.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\bar{y}\in H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)

):

\displaystyle(\Delta_{w}(\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\Theta_{h}\bar{y}),\Delta_{w}z_{h})_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\langle\{\nabla\Pi_{h}\bar{y}\}\cdot{\bf n}_{T}-Q_{{\bf n}}(\nabla\bar{y}\cdot{\bf n}_{T}),\Delta_{w}z_{h}\rangle_{\partial T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\langle(\{\nabla\Pi_{h}\bar{y}\}-\nabla\bar{y})\cdot{\bf n}_{T},\Delta_{w}z_{h}\rangle_{\partial T}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\lVert h_{T}^{-1/2}\{\nabla\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\nabla\bar{y}\}\rVert_{0,\partial T}\lVert h_{T}^{1/2}\Delta_{w}z_{h}\rVert_{0,\partial T}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{\tau}\lVert\Delta_{w}z_{h}\rVert_{0,\mathcal{T}_{h}}.

For the stabilizer defined in (
3.5
) (with
\bm{\nu}_{g}=\{\nabla v_{h}\}
for
v_{h}\in V_{h}
), by applying the regularity of
\bar{y}
, the trace inequality (
2.2
), and the interpolation estimates (
4.1
), we obtain:

\displaystyle s(\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\Theta_{h}\bar{y},z_{h})

\displaystyle\quad\quad=s(\Pi_{h}\bar{y},z_{h})-s(\Theta_{h}\bar{y},z_{h})

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\frac{1}{4}\langle h_{e}^{-1}[\nabla\Pi_{h}\bar{y}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e},[\nabla z_{h}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}-\frac{1}{2}\langle h_{e}^{-1}[\nabla\Pi_{h}\bar{y}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e},[\nabla z_{h}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad=-\frac{1}{4}\langle h_{e}^{-1}[\nabla\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\nabla\bar{y}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e},[\nabla z_{h}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq C\lVert h_{e}^{-1/2}[\nabla\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\nabla\bar{y}]\rVert_{0,\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}\lVert h_{e}^{-1/2}[\nabla z_{h}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rVert_{0,\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq Ch^{\tau}(s(z_{h},z_{h}))^{1/2}.

The second identity holds because
[Q_{\bf n}(\nabla\bar{y}\cdot{\bf n}_{e}){\bf n}_{e}]=\mathbf{0}
on
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}
.
Thus, we conclude:

(\mathrm{I})\leq Ch^{\tau}\lVert z_{h}\rVert_{w}\leq Ch^{\tau}\lVert z_{h}\rVert_{h},

using the norm equivalence (
4.5
).

Estimate of
(\mathrm{II})
:
Using the identity (
3.7
) and the commutative property (
4.3
), we obtain:

\displaystyle(\Delta_{w}\Theta_{h}\bar{y},\Delta_{w}z_{h})_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}

\displaystyle=(\Delta_{w}\Theta_{h}\bar{y},\Delta z_{h})_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\langle\{\Delta_{w}\Theta_{h}\bar{y}\},[\nabla z_{h}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{Q}_{h}\Delta\bar{y},\Delta z_{h})_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\langle\{\mathbb{Q}_{h}\Delta\bar{y}\},[\nabla z_{h}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}

\displaystyle=(\Delta\bar{y},\Delta z_{h})_{\mathcal{T}_{h}}+\langle\{\mathbb{Q}_{h}\Delta\bar{y}\},[\nabla z_{h}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}

\displaystyle=a_{h}(\bar{y},z_{h})+\langle\{\mathbb{Q}_{h}\Delta\bar{y}-\Delta\bar{y}\},[\nabla z_{h}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}.

Using this result, along with the trace inequality (
2.2
) and the estimates (
4.1
), we estimate the term
(\mathrm{II})
:

\displaystyle(\mathrm{II})

\displaystyle=a_{w}(\Theta_{h}\bar{y},z_{h})-a_{h}(\bar{y},z_{h})

\displaystyle=s(\Theta_{h}\bar{y},z_{h})+\langle\{\mathbb{Q}_{h}\Delta\bar{y}-\Delta\bar{y}\},[\nabla z_{h}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\langle h_{e}^{-1}[\nabla\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\nabla\bar{y}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e},[\nabla z_{h}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}+\langle\{\mathbb{Q}_{h}\Delta\bar{y}-\Delta\bar{y}\},[\nabla z_{h}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\lVert h_{e}^{-1/2}[\nabla\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\nabla\bar{y}]\rVert_{0,\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}\lVert h_{e}^{-1/2}[\nabla z_{h}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rVert_{0,\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+\lVert h_{e}^{1/2}\{\mathbb{Q}_{h}\Delta\bar{y}-\Delta\bar{y}\}\rVert_{0,\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}\lVert h_{e}^{-1/2}[\nabla z_{h}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rVert_{0,\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{\tau}\lVert z_{h}\rVert_{h}.

The term involving
\mathbb{Q}_{h}\Delta\bar{y}-\Delta\bar{y}
is bounded using the Bramble-Hilbert lemma
[
6
,
21
]
, combined with the regularity of
\bar{y}
, following the approach presented in
[
14
, Lemma 2.5]
:

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}\lVert h_{e}^{1/2}\{\mathbb{Q}_{h}\Delta\bar{y}-\Delta\bar{y}\}\rVert_{0,e}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{e}}|\mathbb{Q}_{h}\Delta\bar{y}-\Delta\bar{y}|_{0,T}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2\tau}\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{e}}|\bar{y}|_{H^{2+\tau}(Q_{e})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2\tau}|\bar{y}|_{H^{2+\tau}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Here,
\mathcal{T}_{e}
denotes the collection of elements sharing
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}
, and
Q_{e}
represents the union of the two adjacent elements in
\mathcal{T}_{e}
.

Estimate of
(\mathrm{III})
:
Using the continuity result (
2.22
) and the interpolation estimate (
4.1e
), we obtain

\displaystyle(\mathrm{III})=a_{h}(\bar{y}-\Pi_{h}\bar{y},z_{h})\leq C\lVert\bar{y}-\Pi_{h}\bar{y}\rVert_{h}\lVert z_{h}\rVert_{h}\leq Ch^{\tau}\lVert z_{h}\rVert_{h}.

Estimate of
(\mathrm{IV})
:
This term quantifies the discrepancy between the bilinear forms
a_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
and

. We invoke the estimate provided in
[

]
, which yields:

(\mathrm{IV})=a_{h}(\Pi_{h}\bar{y},z_{h})-a(\bar{y},E_{h}z_{h})\leq Ch^{\tau}\lVert z_{h}\rVert_{h}.\hfill

Combining the above bounds completes the proof.
∎

Based on the estimate (
4.9
) in Lemma

, we establish the following estimate for the bilinear forms associated with the optimal control problems.

Lemma 4.4
.

There exists a positive constant C, independent of

, such that

\mathcal{A}_{w}(\Pi_{h}\bar{y},z_{h})-\mathcal{A}(\bar{y},E_{h}z_{h})\leq Ch^{\tau}{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|z_{h}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{\mathcal{H}},\quad\forall z_{h}\in V_{h},

(4.10)

where the bilinear form
\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined in (
3.19
).

Proof.

We begin by expanding the bilinear forms using their definitions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{w}(\Pi_{h}\bar{y},z_{h})-\mathcal{A}(\bar{y},E_{h}z_{h})

\displaystyle=\beta\left[a_{w}(\Pi_{h}\bar{y},z_{h})-a(\bar{y},E_{h}z_{h})\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int_{\Omega}(\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y})z_{h}\,d\chi+\int_{\Omega}\bar{y}(z_{h}-E_{h}z_{h})\,d\chi

The fourth-order term is bounded by
Ch^{\tau}{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|z_{h}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{\mathcal{H}}
using estimate (
4.9
). For the remaining two terms, we apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the estimate (
4.1d
):

\int_{\Omega}(\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y})z_{h}\,d\chi\leq\lVert\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega,\chi)}\lVert z_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega,\chi)}\leq Ch^{\tau}{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|z_{h}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{\mathcal{H}}.

Since
\|\bar{y}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\chi)}\leq C_{\bar{y}}<\infty
, by its regularity, and using the estimate (

) for the operator

, we obtain

\int_{\Omega}\bar{y}(z_{h}-E_{h}z_{h})\,d\chi\leq\lVert\bar{y}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega,\chi)}\lVert z_{h}-E_{h}z_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega,\chi)}\leq Ch^{\tau}{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|z_{h}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{\mathcal{H}}.

Combining the above bounds completes the proof.
∎

4.2
Convergence Results

Consequently, we establish the following error estimate, which supports the convergence of our proposed numerical method.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\bar{y}
be the optimal state of the continuous optimal control problems (
2.9
), and let
\bar{y}_{h}
be the discrete optimal state of the discrete optimal control problems (

{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{\mathcal{W}}\leq Ch^{\tau}.

(4.11)

Proof.

From the coercivity (
4.6
) of
\mathcal{A}_{w}(\cdot,\cdot)
, the definition of the bilinear form, and the discrete variational inequality (
3.17
), we have

\displaystyle C{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{A}_{w}(\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h},\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h})

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\mathcal{A}_{w}(\Pi_{h}\bar{y},\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h})}_{(\mathrm{I})}+\underbrace{\left[-\int_{\Omega}y_{d}(\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h})\,d\chi\right]}_{(\mathrm{II})}.

We estimate the terms
(\mathrm{I})
and
(\mathrm{II})
separately.

Estimate of
(\mathrm{I})
:

Choosing

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{w}(\Pi_{h}\bar{y},\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h})\leq\mathcal{A}(\bar{y},E_{h}(\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h}))+Ch^{\tau}{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{\mathcal{H}}.

Estimate of
(\mathrm{II})
:

Since
\|y_{d}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\chi)}\leq C_{y_{d}}<\infty
, and using the estimate (
4.8d
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}y_{d}(E_{h}z_{h}-z_{h})\,d\chi\leq\lVert y_{d}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega,\chi)}\lVert E_{h}z_{h}-z_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega,\chi)}\leq Ch^{\tau}{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|z_{h}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{\mathcal{H}}.

Combining the two estimates, we obtain

\displaystyle(\mathrm{I})+(\mathrm{II})

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{\tau}{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{\mathcal{H}}+\underbrace{\mathcal{A}(\bar{y},E_{h}(\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h}))-\int_{\Omega}y_{d}(E_{h}(\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h}))\,d\chi}_{(\mathrm{III})}.

(\mathrm{III})
:

By choosing

in the KKT conditions (
2.12
) with (
3.19

\displaystyle\mathrm{(III)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(\bar{y},E_{h}(\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h}))-\int_{\Omega}y_{d}(E_{h}(\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h}))\,d\chi

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}E_{h}(\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h})\,d\xi_{-}+\int_{\Omega}E_{h}(\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h})\,d\xi_{+}

\displaystyle{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}

{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\Pi_{h}\bar{y}-\bar{y}_{h}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{\mathcal{W}}\leq Ch^{\tau}.\hfill\qed

5
An Additive Schwarz Preconditioner

To improve the efficiency of our method, we develop and analyze a one-level additive Schwarz preconditioner for the discrete system in this section, following the framework of
[
13
]
.
We use the notation
\lesssim
to denote an inequality up to a constant factor. Specifically, for two quantities
A
and
B
, the expression
A\lesssim B
means that there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of discretization parameters (e.g., mesh size
h
, overlap size
\delta
, or problem data), such that
A\leq CB
.

We define an operator
\mathsf{A}_{h}
on
V_{h}\to V_{h}^{\prime}
by

\langle\mathsf{A}_{h}u,v\rangle=a_{w}(u,v),\quad\forall u,v\in V_{h}.

Our goal is to develop a one-level additive Schwarz preconditioner for
\mathsf{A}_{h}
.
By the coercivity property (
3.13
), we have the following identity:

\langle\mathsf{A}_{h}v,v\rangle=\lVert v\rVert_{w}^{2},\quad\forall v\in V_{h}.

(5.1)

We assume that the domain
\Omega
is partitioned into overlapping subdomains
\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{J}
such that
\Omega=\cup_{j=1}^{J}\Omega_{j}
, each with diameter
\text{diam}(\Omega_{j})\approx H
, and that the boundaries of
\Omega_{j}
align with the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
. The overlap among the subdomains is characterized by a parameter
\delta
, and we assume the existence of nonnegative functions
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{J}\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})
satisfying

i.

\theta_{j}=0
on
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{j}
,

ii.

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\theta_{j}=1
on
\bar{\Omega}
,

iii.

\|\nabla\theta_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lesssim\delta^{-1}
and
\|D^{2}\theta_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lesssim\delta^{-2}
,

where
D^{2}\theta_{j}
denotes the Hessian of
\theta_{j}
. Moreover, we assume

Overlap assumption:
any point in
\Omega
belongs to at most
N_{c}
subdomains.

(5.2)

Remark 5.1
.

The construction of
\theta_{j}
is standard
[
41
]
. Given a coarse partition of
\Omega
consisting of convex elements, such as quadrilaterals in two dimensions or hexaherdra in three dimensions, we define
\Omega_{j}
, by enlarging each coarse element by an amount of
\delta
, such that each
\Omega_{j}
becomes a union of fine mesh elements in
\mathcal{T}_{h}
.

For each subdomain
\Omega_{j}
, define the local finite element space

V_{j}=\left\{v\in V_{h}\,:\,v=0\text{ on }\Omega\setminus\Omega_{j}\right\},

and define the local operator
\mathsf{A}_{j}:V_{j}\rightarrow V_{j}^{\prime}
by

\langle\mathsf{A}_{j}u_{j},v_{j}\rangle=a_{w,j}(u_{j},v_{j}),\quad\forall u_{j},v_{j}\in V_{j},

where

a_{w,j}(u_{j},v_{j})=(\Delta_{w}u_{j},\Delta_{w}v_{j})_{\mathcal{T}_{h,j}}+\frac{1}{4}\langle h_{e}^{-1}[\nabla u_{j}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e},[\nabla v_{j}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}_{h,j}},

with

\mathcal{T}_{h,j}=\left\{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\,:\,T\subset\Omega_{j}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{E}_{h,j}=\left\{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h}\,:\,e\subset\bar{\Omega}_{j}\setminus\partial\Omega\right\}.

We define the local energy norm on
V_{j}
by

\lVert v_{j}\rVert_{w,j}^{2}:=a_{w,j}(v_{j},v_{j}),\quad\forall v_{j}\in V_{j},

(5.3)

which induces a norm on
V_{j}
.

The one-level additive Schwarz preconditioner
\mathsf{B}_{h}:V_{h}^{\prime}\rightarrow V_{h}
is defined by

\mathsf{B}_{h}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathsf{I}_{j}\mathsf{A}_{j}^{-1}\mathsf{I}_{j}^{\top},

where
\mathsf{I}_{j}:V_{j}\rightarrow V_{h}
, for
1\leq j\leq J
, denotes the natural injection operator, and
\mathsf{I}_{j}^{\top}:V_{h}^{\prime}\rightarrow V_{j}^{\prime}
is its transpose (i.e., the adjoint operator with respect to the duality pairing).
We now turn to estimating the condition number of the preconditioned system
\mathsf{B}_{h}\mathsf{A}_{h}
. The following theorem provides the condition number estimate.

Theorem 5.2
.

It holds that

\kappa(\mathsf{B}_{h}\mathsf{A}_{h}):=\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\mathsf{B}_{h}\mathsf{A}_{h})}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathsf{B}_{h}\mathsf{A}_{h})}\lesssim\delta^{-4},

(5.4)

where
\lambda_{\max}(\mathsf{B}_{h}\mathsf{A}_{h})
and
\lambda_{\min}(\mathsf{B}_{h}\mathsf{A}_{h})
denote the largest and smallest eigenvalues of
\mathsf{B}_{h}\mathsf{A}_{h}
, respectively.

Proof.

First, let
v\in V_{h}
be arbitrary. For any decomposition
v=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathsf{I}_{j}v_{j}
with
v_{j}\in V_{j}
, we apply
(
5.1
), (
5.2
), Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and (
5.3
) to obtain

\langle\mathsf{A}_{h}v,v\rangle=\lVert v\rVert_{w}^{2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lVert\mathsf{I}_{j}v_{j}\rVert_{w}^{2}\lesssim\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lVert v_{j}\rVert_{w,j}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\langle\mathsf{A}_{j}v_{j},v_{j}\rangle,

which implies

\langle\mathsf{A}_{h}v,v\rangle\lesssim\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathsf{I}_{j}v_{j}\\
v_{j}\in V_{j}\end{subarray}}\langle\mathsf{A}_{j}v_{j},v_{j}\rangle.

Therefore, by the standard theory of the additive Schwarz preconditioners
[
7
,
32
,
5
,
43
,
42
]
, we conclude that

\lambda_{\max}(\mathsf{B}_{h}\mathsf{A}_{h})\lesssim 1.

(5.5)

Next, we estimate
\lambda_{\min}(\mathsf{B}_{h}\mathsf{A}_{h})
.
Let
v\in V_{h}
be arbitrary, and define

v_{j}=\Pi_{h}(\theta_{j}v),\quad 1\leq j\leq J,

(5.6)

where
\Pi_{h}
is the Lagrange nodal interpolation operator onto
V_{h}
.
Then, by the properties of
\theta_{j}
, we have
v_{j}\in V_{j}
, and

\sum_{j=1}^{J}v_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\Pi_{h}(\theta_{j}v)=\Pi_{h}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{J}\theta_{j}\right)v=\Pi_{h}v=v.

The interpolation operator also has the following property in each
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}

[
15
]
:

|\Pi_{h}(\theta_{j}v)|_{2,T}\lesssim|\theta_{j}v|_{2,T}.

(5.7)

Then, using the same argument in the proof of Lemma
4.1
, we have

\langle\mathsf{A}_{j}v_{j},v_{j}\rangle=\lVert v_{j}\rVert_{w}^{2}\lesssim\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h,j}}|v_{j}|_{2,T}^{2}+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{h,j}}\lVert h_{e}^{-1/2}[\nabla v_{j}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\rVert_{0,e}^{2}.

(5.8)

For any element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h,j}
in (
5.8
), by the definition (
5.6
), the interpolation property (
5.7
), and the bounds on
\theta_{j}
, we obtain

\displaystyle|v_{j}|_{2,T}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\Pi_{h}(\theta_{j}v)|_{2,T}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle|\theta_{j}v|_{2,T}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|\theta_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}(T)}^{2}|v|_{2,T}^{2}+\|\nabla\theta_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}(T)}^{2}|v|_{1,T}^{2}+\|D^{2}\theta_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}(T)}^{2}\|v\|_{0,T}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle|v|_{2,T}^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}|v|_{1,T}^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta^{4}}\|v\|_{0,T}^{2}.

For any interface
e\in\mathcal{E}_{h,j}
in (
5.8
), we estimate using the definition of
v_{j}
(
5.6
), the trace inequality (
2.2
), the interpolation estimates (
4.1
), and the bounds on
\theta_{j}
,

\displaystyle\|h_{e}^{-1/2}[\nabla v_{j}]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\|_{0,e}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|h_{e}^{-1/2}[\nabla(\Pi_{h}(\theta_{j}v))]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\|_{0,e}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|h_{e}^{-1/2}[\nabla(\Pi_{h}(\theta_{j}v)-\theta_{j}v)]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\|_{0,e}^{2}+\|h_{e}^{-1/2}[\nabla(\theta_{j}v)]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\|_{0,e}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{e}}\left[h_{T}^{-2}|\Pi_{h}(\theta_{j}v)-\theta_{j}v|_{1,T}^{2}+|\Pi_{h}(\theta_{j}v)-\theta_{j}v|_{2,T}^{2}\right]+\|h_{e}^{-1/2}[\nabla v]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\|_{0,e}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{e}}|\theta_{j}v|_{2,T}^{2}+\|h_{e}^{-1/2}[\nabla v]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\|_{0,e}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{e}}\left[|v|_{2,T}^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}|v|_{1,T}^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta^{4}}\|v\|_{0,T}^{2}\right]+\|h_{e}^{-1/2}[\nabla v]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\|_{0,e}^{2},

where
\mathcal{T}_{e}
denotes the set of elements in
\mathcal{T}_{h}
sharing the interface
e
.
Combining the above results for (
5.8
),
summing over
j
, and applying the overlap assumption (
5.2
) along with the Poincaré-Friedrichs inequalities
[
16
]
and the norm equivalence (
4.5
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\langle\mathsf{A}_{j}v_{j},v_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left[|v|_{2,T}^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}|v|_{1,T}^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta^{4}}\|v\|_{0,T}^{2}\right]+\|h_{e}^{-1/2}[\nabla v]\cdot{\bf n}_{e}\|_{0,\mathcal{E}_{h}^{o}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\delta^{4}}\langle\mathsf{A}_{h}v,v\rangle.

Hence, the standard theory of additive Schwarz preconditioners yields

\lambda_{\min}(\mathsf{B}_{h}\mathsf{A}_{h})\gtrsim\delta^{4}.

(5.9)

Therefore, combining (
5.5
) and (
5.9
), the condition number estimate (
5.4
) follows.
∎

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we developed a
C^{0}
weak Galerkin (
C^{0}
-WG) method combined with an additive Schwarz preconditioner to efficiently and accurately solve optimal control problems governed by elliptic partial differential equations, featuring general tracking cost functionals and pointwise state constraints.
By eliminating the control variable via the PDE constraint, we reformulated the OCP as a fourth-order variational inequality that characterizes the optimal solution.
These problems pose significant numerical challenges due to reduced regularity and the need to address ill-conditioned sparse systems arising from discretization.

Our first contribution introduced a weak Galerkin (WG) discretization based on globally continuous quadratic Lagrange elements, eliminating the need for penalty parameters and trace terms required in the symmetric
C^{0}
interior penalty method. This WG formulation enables efficient stiffness matrix assembly and robust performance via a parameter-free stabilization.
Our second contribution addressed the ill-conditioning of the resulting linear systems by designing a one-level additive Schwarz preconditioner. This preconditioner significantly improves the condition number of the system, enabling efficient and scalable iterative solvers. Numerical experiments confirmed the accuracy and effectiveness of the proposed
C^{0}
-WG method and the associated preconditioner for both biharmonic problems and constrained OCPs.

Overall, this work presents a robust and efficient computational framework for high-order constrained OCPs and lays a foundation for future extensions to more complex problems, including time-dependent and nonlinear settings.
