The Value of NLR, PLR, MLR, and SII Combined with Lower Respiratory Tract Symptoms in Predicting Pulmonary Complications in Non-Risk-Factor Influenza A Patients
Authors: Shanmu Ai, Haojie Wu, Rui Gu, Sen Zhang, Jie Luo, Fang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo analyze the predictive value of blood cell count results, derived indicators, and patient symptoms for pulmonary complications in non-risk-factor influenza A patients.MethodsA retrospective cohort study analyzed 54 non-risk-factor influenza A patients. Those with pulmonary complications were assigned to the PC group, while the others were placed in the NPC group. Blood cell count and its derived indices, including neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), were compared between the two groups. Four prediction models with different symptoms were established, and the ROC curve was used to verify their predictive value for pulmonary complications in non-risk-factor influenza A patients. Multivariate logistic regression was applied to the optimal prediction model.ResultsLower respiratory tract symptoms were present in 63.6% of patients in the PC group, which was significantly higher than the 25.6% in the NPC group (P < 0.05). The PC group exhibited significantly higher levels of white blood cell (WBC) count (9.38 ± 3.98×109/L vs. 6.54 ± 2.37×109/L), neutrophil count (8.05 ± 3.94×109/L vs. 5.16 ± 2.25×109/L), NLR (13.36 ± 7.26 vs. 7.42 ± 4.04), and SII (2548.87 ± 1967.89 vs. 1437.40 ± 892.69) compared with the NPC group. In ROC, WBC, neutrophils, and NLR were valid predictors of pulmonary complications. Among them, NLR showed the best individual predictive performance (AUC = 0.746). All four combined models achieved higher AUC values than NLR alone, and Model 4 (lower respiratory tract symptoms + NLR) presented the highest AUC of 0.829. Multivariate logistic regression identified that an elevated NLR (OR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.10–1.63, P = 0.004) and lower respiratory tract symptoms (OR = 15.26, 95% CI: 1.95–119.31, P = 0.009) were independent risk factors for pulmonary complications.ConclusionElevated NLR and lower respiratory tract symptoms are independent predictors of pulmonary complications in non-risk-factor influenza A patients. Their combination offers more accurate and effective predictive value for early risk stratification.Keywords: influenza A, NLR, prediction model, non-risk-factor influenza A patients

Introduction

Influenza causes seasonal outbreaks globally each year, with influenza A being the most prevalent. According to the latest 2024–2025 influenza season report from the CDC, the cumulative hospitalization rate of patients with related diseases reached 127.1 per 100,000 population, the highest since the 2010–2011 influenza season, and the in-hospital mortality rate was 3%.1,2

Pathologically, the influenza virus infects respiratory epithelial cells, induces apoptosis and necroptosis of alveolar epithelial cells, destroys alveolar integrity, and triggers a cytokine storm and excessive inflammatory response.3 This process leads to a high incidence of pulmonary complications, including primary influenza viral pneumonia, secondary bacterial pneumonia, and viral–bacterial coinfection, which typically determine the severity and mortality of influenza patients.4–6

People aged over 65, pregnant individuals, those within 4 weeks postpartum, individuals with chronic underlying diseases, and obese individuals are recognized as high-risk populations for influenza complications.7–9 However, influenza pulmonary complications can also occur in non-risk-factor influenza A patients, who are defined as individuals with influenza A without conventional risk factors for influenza complications. These patients are therefore prone to being overlooked. One study reported that 33% of influenza-associated respiratory deaths occurred in patients younger than 65,10 and 10.9% of hospitalized patients had no underlying diseases in the CDC’s report.2 Early identification of pulmonary complications is critical to optimizing clinical treatment and improving patient prognosis.11

Blood cell count is a low-cost and readily available blood test for screening outpatients and emergency patients.12 Acute inflammation is typically characterized by elevated neutrophils, platelets, and monocytes, and reduced lymphocytes.13 Studies have shown that hematological ratio indicators such as NLR, PLR, MLR, and SII can indicate a subclinical inflammatory state.14–16 Although many studies have verified the diagnostic value of NLR, MLR, and PLR in influenza and COVID-19,17–19 research on the diagnostic significance of SII in influenza is still limited. Furthermore, studies that combine inflammatory indices, including NLR, MLR, PLR, and SII, with clinical symptoms to predict the risk of complicated pneumonia are particularly scarce among non-risk-factor patients with influenza A.

This study aimed to retrospectively compare differences in early blood cell count results and derived indicators between the pulmonary complication and non-pulmonary complication groups among influenza A patients without conventional risk factors and provide evidence for early risk stratification of pulmonary infection in this specific population.

Methods

Study Subjects

We conducted a retrospective observational study of 104 patients with influenza A admitted to our hospital between October 1 and December 31, 2023. Nasopharyngeal swab and blood samples were collected at the patients’ first visit, and a unified sampling and testing protocol was applied to all participants. Influenza A infection was confirmed by real-time fluorescent quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) of nasopharyngeal swabs. All patients with positive influenza A nucleic acid results were followed up by telephone throughout the entire disease course. During this period, serial clinical assessments were performed according to clinical indications, and relevant data were documented.

Inclusion Criteria

① Patients with positive influenza A nucleic acid test results in our hospital from October 1 to December 31, 2023; ② Patients who visited the hospital within 72 hours after influenza symptom onset and completed a blood count; ③ Chest CT was documented during illness.

Exclusion Criteria

① Patients with influenza A and complication risk factors (those with underlying diseases such as chronic respiratory, cardiovascular, kidney, liver, hematological, nervous-system and neuromuscular, metabolic and endocrine diseases, cancer, immunodeficiency diseases, and obese patients (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2); ② Patients with incomplete treatment collection.

Grouping and Data Collection

A total of 54 patients were enrolled in this study. Radiologists at our institution, blinded to the patients’ clinical data, independently reviewed the chest CT images of all participants to evaluate pulmonary complications, including primary influenza viral pneumonia and bacterial or fungal superinfection. The CT diagnosis of pneumonia mainly relies on new pulmonary infiltrates (consolidation, ground-glass opacity).

Eleven patients were diagnosed with pulmonary complications of influenza A infection through chest CT (PC Group), while 43 patients were not infected (NPC Group). A single clinician, unaware of the patient group allocation, gathered patient data, encompassing clinical characteristics such as age, sex, symptoms, and maximum body temperature.

The results of blood cell counts, including white blood cell, lymphocyte, red blood cell, and platelet counts, were computed, and indices derived from blood cell counts, such as the NLR, PLR, MLR, and SII, were calculated. NLR was defined as the ratio of neutrophil counts to lymphocyte counts; PLR was the ratio of platelet counts to lymphocyte counts; MLR was the ratio of monocyte counts to lymphocyte counts; SII was calculated as the product of neutrophil and platelet counts divided by lymphocyte counts.

Statistical Analysis

This study used SPSS Statistics software (version 26.0) to analyze data on quantitative and categorical variables, and GraphPad Prism (version 8.3.0) for mapping. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared between groups using the t-test, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and compared via the chi-square test. The effect size in t-tests was represented by Cohen’s d values, with higher values indicating larger differences. P-values < 0.05 and Cohen’s d values > 0.8 were considered statistically significant.

The multivariate logistic regression method was used to establish four clinical prediction models for pulmonary complications in 54 influenza A patients, and the prediction probability of each case under different models was calculated. The prediction probabilities of two patient groups under different models were compared by the t-test.

The predictive value of blood cell count results and risk prediction models for pulmonary complications of influenza A infection were determined using the receiver-operating curve (ROC). The optimal combination of variables identified by ROC analysis was then entered into a multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent predictors.

Results

Baseline Characteristics and Clinical Symptoms of 54 Patients

A total of 54 patients with influenza A without risk factors were included in the study. The mean age was 32.46 ± 12.96 years, and 24 (44.44%) were male.

Eleven (20.37%) patients with pulmonary complications of influenza A infection diagnosed by CT were in the PC group, and the rest were in the NPC group. The PC group had 63.6% male patients (7/11) with a median age of 42.18 ± 14.40 years, while the NPC group had 39.5% male patients (17/43) and a median age of 33.47 ± 12.68 years. There was no significant difference in patient characteristics between the groups (p > 0.05).

The highest pre-admission body temperature was 38.67 ± 0.82°C in the PC group and 38.23 ± 0.85°C in the NPC group. In the PC group, 72.7% (8/11) had upper respiratory symptoms, compared to 60.5% (26/43) in the NPC group. Overall, 81.8% (9/11) in the PC group had extra-respiratory symptoms, while 76.7% (33/43) in the NPC group did. There was no significant difference between the groups (p > 0.05).

However, lower respiratory tract symptoms occurred in 63.6% (7/11) of the PC group, significantly higher than the 25.6% (11/43) in the NPC group (p < 0.05) (Table 1).

Prediction Probabilities of Different Models for 54 Patients

Multivariate logistic regression was used to build a clinical prediction model for pulmonary complications in 54 influenza A patients, based on differential blood cell count results and lower respiratory symptoms.

Using lower respiratory tract symptoms combined with WBC to construct Prediction Model 1, the probability of the PC group was 0.432 ± 0.317, significantly higher than the NPC group’s 0.145 ± 0.131. Using lower respiratory tract symptoms combined with Neutrophil to construct Prediction Model 2, the PC group’s probability was 0.448 ± 0.329, significantly higher than the NPC group’s 0.141 ± 0.131. Using lower respiratory tract symptoms combined with SII to construct Prediction Model 3, the PC group’s probability was 0.444 ± 0.331, significantly higher than the NPC group’s 0.142 ± 0.106. Using lower respiratory tract symptoms combined with NLR to construct Prediction Model 4, the PC group’s probability was 0.534 ± 0.379, significantly higher than the NPC group’s 0.119 ± 0.111 (p < 0.01 and Cohen’s d > 0.8) (Table 3 and Figure 1).

The Predictive Capacity of Main Blood Cell Count Results, Derived Indices, and Prediction Models

ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the performance of blood cell parameters, derived indices, and prediction models for predicting pulmonary complications in influenza A patients (Table 4 and Figure 2). The AUC for WBC was 0.733 (95% CI: 0.566–0.899; P = 0.018), with an optimal cutoff of 11.265 (×109/L, Youden’s index: 0.341), yielding a sensitivity of 0.364 and specificity of 0.977. The AUC for Neutrophils was 0.746 (95% CI: 0.582–0.911; P = 0.012), with an optimal cutoff of 5.980 (×109/L, Youden’s index: 0.450), yielding a sensitivity of 0.636 and specificity of 0.814. The AUC for NLR was 0.746 (95% CI: 0.558–0.935; P = 0.012), with an optimal cutoff of 10.228 (Youden’s index: 0.541), yielding a sensitivity of 0.727 and specificity of 0.814. For the prediction models, the AUC for Model 1 was 0.805 (95% CI: 0.646–0.965; P = 0.002), Model 2 was 0.820 (95% CI: 0.671–0.970; P = 0.001), Model 3 was 0.770 (95% CI: 0.572–0.967; P = 0.006), and Model 4 was 0.829 (95% CI: 0.672–0.986; P = 0.001), with corresponding optimal cutoffs, Youden indices, sensitivities, and specificities listed in Table 4. Conversely, SII showed poor predictive value, with an AUC of 0.651 (95% CI: 0.456–0.846; P = 0.125), indicating fair predictive performance.

Validation of Prediction Model via Multivariate Logistic Regression

To further validate the predictive model identified through ROC curve analysis, multivariate logistic regression was performed using lower respiratory tract symptoms and NLR. The results indicated that both variables were independent predictors of pulmonary complications. The presence of lower respiratory tract symptoms was an independent risk factor (OR = 15.26, 95% CI: 1.95–119.31, P = 0.009). Each 1-unit increase in NLR was also independently associated with an increased risk of pulmonary complications (OR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.10–1.63, P = 0.004) (Table 5).

Discussion

Our research initially found no significant age or gender disparities between groups, suggesting these factors do not predict pulmonary complications in non-risk-factor influenza A patients. Lower respiratory tract symptoms were more common among patients with complications, which is same with previous findings.20 In contrast to previous studies,20 body temperature did not reflect the risk of complications, presumably due to differences in the study populations; our study concentrated on specific influenza A patients, while body temperature is correlated with autoimmune factors, virus subtype, and viral load.21,22 Second, compared with the non-pulmonary complication (NPC) group, the pulmonary complication (PC) group exhibited higher levels of WBC count, neutrophil count, NLR, and SII. No significant differences were observed in lymphocyte count, monocyte count, or hemoglobin level between the two groups. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis showed that WBC count, neutrophil count, and NLR were effective predictors of pulmonary complications. Among them, NLR yielded the best predictive performance: AUC = 0.746, Youden’s index = 0.541, with an optimal cut-off value of 10.228, corresponding to a sensitivity of 0.727 and a specificity of 0.814. Consistent with the predictive performance demonstrated in the ROC analysis, the multivariate logistic regression confirmed that lower respiratory tract symptoms and elevated NLR are independent risk factors for pulmonary infection.

With secondary pulmonary infection, chemokines and complement activation products drive phagocytes into the bloodstream and lungs. There, neutrophils aggregate to fight bacteria, increasing white blood cells (especially neutrophils), which is consistent with our study. Respiratory viral infections often deplete peripheral lymphocytes, possibly due to virus-induced T-cell destruction (eg, influenza A causes thymocyte apoptosis and atrophy).23,24 Early lymphocyte (T/NK cell) decline predicts influenza B pulmonary complications.25 In our study, both groups had reduced lymphocytes, and the PC group had lower mean counts (non-significant, p>0.05), likely due to unclassified lymphocytes or a small sample size. Previous studies have also confirmed that NLR is the optimal predictive indicator for pulmonary infection in patients with COVID-19 or influenza. However, the optimal cutoff value of NLR in previous studies was 7.30, which differs from our result. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the study population and the relatively small sample size in this study.26–28 The SII was initially applied to predict the prognosis of tumors and immune diseases.29,30 At present, it has also been used to evaluate acute and chronic inflammation as well as COVID-19 outcomes, albeit with inferior predictive efficacy relative to NLR.29 The present study revealed that the predictive value of SII remained limited.

As an innovative approach, we developed four predictive models by, respectively, integrating symptoms with WBC count, neutrophil count, NLR, and SII. Logistic regression was employed to estimate the probability of complications. All models were effective in predicting risks among non-high-risk patients (as indicated by ROC analysis), with the combination of NLR and lower respiratory tract symptoms demonstrating the optimal predictive value (superior to NLR alone). Specifically, Chest computed tomography (CT) is the gold standard for diagnosing pulmonary diseases and for screening influenza complications. However, excessive CT scans expose patients to radiation, waste resources, and are unavailable in many primary healthcare institutions.31,32 Blood cell counts, more accessible than procalcitonin (PCT), serum amyloid A (SAA), and C-reactive protein (CRP), are suitable for outpatient and emergency screening. Our findings help identify high-risk influenza A patients without risk factors, in line with precision medicine.

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size and limited cases of pulmonary complications may reduce statistical robustness and increase the risk of model overfitting. Larger samples are needed for further validation. Second, chest CT was not routinely performed in influenza A patients without pulmonary complications when their symptoms gradually subsided, resulting in insufficient negative samples. Expanding negative samples barely affects the AUC but may markedly change the optimal cut-off value. Such obvious fluctuation of cut-off value limits its general clinical applicability. Third, the prediction models established in this study lack external validation in an independent patient cohort, which restricts the generalizability and clinical translation of our findings. Additionally, physical examination results are affected by differences in operators’ proficiency and experience. Standardized physical examination criteria can effectively improve the accuracy of study results.

Conclusions

Elevated NLR and lower respiratory tract symptoms are independent predictors of pulmonary complications in non-risk-factor influenza A patients. Their combination provides more accurate and effective predictive value for early risk stratification. Subsequent to further validation, this indicator can offer guidance for subsequent examinations and clinical management.
