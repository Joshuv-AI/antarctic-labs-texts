Decoding myoepithelioma: Highlighting diagnostic dilemmas through a series of rare cases
Authors: Mangesh Londhe, Neil Abhay Shah, Sushama Gurwale, Charusheela Gore
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Myoepitheliomas are exceedingly rare neoplasms of the salivary glands. Minor salivary gland neoplasms most frequently occur in the palate (hard palate), followed by the lips, oral mucosa, and tongue/floor of the mouth. Additionally, less prevalent locations encompass the retromolar area, pharynx, nasal cavity, paranasal sinuses, and larynx. The lack of distinctive cytologic features and bland morphology in both benign and malignant myoepitheliomas necessitates the use of histopathology and immunohistochemistry for accurate diagnosis. Hence, here, we report a series of three cases, including two benign and one malignant myoepithelioma, stressing the significance of the above-mentioned techniques for efficient diagnosis and management. This rare case series comprises many salivary gland myoepithelial neoplasms. Examples include the cystic plasmacytoid and clear cell myoepithelioma and low-grade myoepithelial carcinoma with parenchymal invasion. Immunohistochemical myoepithelial markers include vimentin, smooth muscle antigen, S 100, and p63. All of our patients had a positive p63. Myoepithelioma is diagnosed by biopsy because many tumours have similar clinical and radiological characteristics. They rarely reoccur, improving prognosis. Malignant myoepitheliomas may require wide local excision with clean margins. Regular follow-up is recommended. Myoepitheliomas are rare salivary gland tumours. Myoepithelioma should be suspected in slow-growing, well-defined salivary gland masses with variable post-contrast enhancement on CT images. Histopathology and immunohistochemistry are the best myoepithelioma diagnostic methods. Malignant myoepithelioma must be ruled out by thorough invasion testing.

INTRODUCTION

Myoepitheliomas are exceedingly uncommon neoplasms of the salivary glands, consisting of myoepithelial cells that are devoid of ductal development.[1] They mostly impact the major salivary glands, with the parotid gland being the most frequently affected (approximately 40%), followed by the minor salivary glands (approximately 21%).[2] Minor salivary gland myoepitheliomas predominantly arise in the hard palate, followed by the lips, oral mucosa, and the tongue or floor of the mouth. Other, less common sites include the retromolar area, pharynx, nasal cavity, paranasal sinuses, and larynx. Their prevalence in the oral cavity is exceedingly uncommon, comprising 1.5% of all salivary gland neoplasms.[3] The histological subtypes comprise spindle, plasmacytoid or hyaline, epithelioid, clear, and oncocytic (a form of the spindle type).[1] The clear cell subtype is infrequent, with a limited number of documented cases to date.[124] The histomorphology may appear similar in both benign and malignant myoepitheliomas; however, the identification of invasion in histopathology supports the diagnosis of myoepithelial carcinoma rather than myoepithelioma. Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) is the primary and most frequently conducted examination for salivary gland neoplasms; nevertheless, the absence of distinguishing cytomorphology complicates the diagnosis of myoepithelioma, necessitating histopathology plus immunohistochemistry for precise diagnosis.

CASE REPORT

Case 01

A 67-year-old male presented with complaints of right submandibular swelling since 8-9 years, not associated with pain. Ultrasonography (USG) was suggestive of enlarged hypoechoic heterogenous conglomerated lymph nodes forming an abscess showing internal echoes. USG-guided FNAC was indicative of salivary gland neoplasm of unknown malignant potential. The specimen once received for HPE, externally appeared greyish white, capsulated, and nodular. On the cut surface, a fairly circumscribed lesion with heterogenous areas was identified measuring 2.5 × 2.1 cm and forming multiple nodules [Figure 1a]. HPE revealed a partially encapsulated tumour displaying a canalicular pattern of growth. The tumour cells showed plasmacytoid morphology with monomorphic bland nuclei and hyaline eosinophilic cytoplasm, showing minimal atypia along with occasional mitosis. The adjacent parenchyma showed infiltration by tumour [Figure 2a-c]. For further differentiation of the tumour, immunohistochemistry (IHC) workup was done, which revealed positive expression for p63, Smooth Muscle Actin (SMA), and vimentin with focal positivity for CD117 and a low Ki67 index (2%), thus confirming the diagnosis of low-grade myoepithelial carcinoma [Figure 2d]. The patient is on regular follow-up with radiotherapy being advised due to the invasive component.

Case 02

A 42-year-old female presented with complaints of swelling below the right ear since 2 months. USG was suggestive of a well-defined heterogeneously hypoechoic cystic lesion with solid component within and multiple free-floating internal echoes and thick septations within the superficial lobe of the right parotid gland. Parotid cyst fluid was sent for cytology, which was paucicellular and inadequate to opine. Subsequently excision was done, and the specimen was sent for histopathology. Grossly, it appeared as a circumscribed cystic lesion with heterogenous areas identified forming multiple nodules [Figure 1b]. HPE showed a fairly circumscribed lesion composed of round to polygonal cells with central to eccentric nuclei, inconspicuous nucleoli, and a moderate amount of eosinophilic granular cytoplasm arranged in sheets. Stroma showed eosinophilic acellular areas. The cyst wall showed lining epithelium of oncocytic cells [Figure 3a and b]. IHC revealed positive expression for cytokeratin 7 and p63, with the Ki67 index being less than 1% thus confirming the diagnosis of myoepithelioma, plasmacytoid type [Figure 3c]. The six-monthly follow-up shows no signs of recurrence.

Case 03

A 28-year-old female had slow, painless soft palate swelling for two years. Examination revealed a 3 × 3 cm swelling at the soft-hard palate junction. From the midline to the right, it covered the soft palate. Well-defined, non-pulsatile, and smooth pinkish. The lesion did not blanch on palpation, indicating inadequate superficial vascularity. On a contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CE-CT) scan, a 17 × 19 × 16 mm lesion was discovered on the right side of the hard palate, in the right palatoglossal arch. After contrast, the image showed mild heterogeneous enhancement with central non-enhancing regions, indicating necrosis [Figure 4]. FNAC was performed, and blood-tinged smears were obtained and stained with H&E. and Leishman’s stain. The smears revealed monolayered sheets of spherical, monomorphic cells. The individual cells were round to oval with scanty to moderate cytoplasm and fine, uniformly distributed chromatin with indistinct nucleoli. In some clusters, fibrillary material without chondromyxoid stroma was seen. Mitosis, nuclear atypia, and necrosis were absent. As per the Milan system for reporting salivary gland cytopathology, the lesion was found as a salivary gland tumour with uncertain malignancy. She had the palatal mass surgically removed and sent for HPE. A single 2 × 2 × 1 cm soft-to-firm nodular mass was observed in the gross results. The sliced surface was solid and bright white [Figure 1c and d]. Encapsulated tumours from a tiny salivary gland had monomorphic plasmacytoid cells in loose clusters under the microscope. The cells had a moderate amount of clear cytoplasm, homogenous nuclei, and inconspicuous nucleoli. Localized basement membrane-like material was discovered in collagenized stroma. Epithelial cells, pleomorphism, atypia, adjacent tissue invasion, and mitotic figures were absent [Figure 3d and e]. Tumour IHC showed p63 positivity. The Ki67 index was less than 1%. Histological, and immunohistochemical studies confirmed the diagnosis of clear cell myoepithelioma [Figure 3f]. After surgery, the patient is doing well with no recurrence and getting regular follow-up.

DISCUSSION

This report presents a rare case series featuring a wide spectrum of myoepithelial neoplasms of the salivary gland. This includes low-grade myoepithelial carcinoma identified through parenchymal invasion, predominantly cystic plasmacytoid myoepithelioma, and clear cell myoepithelioma [Table 1].

Minor salivary gland neoplasms primarily occur in the hard palate, followed by the lips, oral mucosa, and the tongue or floor of the mouth. Additionally, less prevalent locations encompass the retromolar region, pharynx, nasal cavity, paranasal sinuses, and the larynx. Myoepithelioma is a benign neoplasm that arises in all exocrine glands.[5] They consist of contractile smooth muscle cells originating from ectoderm and exhibit an absence of ductal differentiation. These neoplasms have been documented in salivary glands, breast tissue, larynx, and cutaneous sweat glands, exhibiting myoepithelial cells.[3] Approximately 50% of myoepitheliomas originate in the parotid gland, followed by the sublingual gland at 33% and the submandibular gland at 13%.[6] Intraoral myoepitheliomas are uncommon, representing 1.5% of all salivary gland neoplasms.[7] Of all salivary gland myoepitheliomas, 26% arise in the small salivary glands of the oral cavity, with the palate being the predominant location.[3] Myoepithelioma exhibits no notable gender preference, occurring with almost equal frequency in both males and females. This applies to both benign and malignant myoepithelial tumours, encompassing myoepithelioma of soft tissues and salivary glands. The highest frequency is noted during the third decade of life.[8] Myoepitheliomas lack distinctive clinical characteristics. Similar to other salivary gland neoplasms, they manifest as asymptomatic, gradually enlarging masses over a period of months to years, typically in individuals in the fourth decade of life.[3] Ultrasound findings in myoepithelioma generally manifest as a well-circumscribed, solid mass with either a lobulated or smooth margin. The echogenicity may vary from hypoechoic to hyperechoic or isoechoic. Certain accounts characterize myoepitheliomas as exhibiting a heterogeneous appearance with internal echoes, but others depict them as homogeneous.[9] Myoepitheliomas generally manifest as well-defined, enhancing masses on CT imaging, frequently exhibiting smooth or lobulated borders. On MRI, they may appear as heterogeneous isointense to the pharyngeal muscle on T1-weighted imaging, heterogeneously hyperintense on T2-weighted images, and heterogeneously enhancing on contrast-enhanced T1-weighted images.[10] The elements influencing the enhancement include histological components, cell type, stromal fibrosis, and vascularity. Significant enhancement is observed in myoepitheliomas with fibrous stroma, although it is subtle in those with predominantly myxoid stroma. The tumour exhibits subtle heterogeneous post-contrast enhancement. Enhancement patterns are crucial in the differential diagnosis of slow-growing, well-circumscribed tumours in the soft palate.[3] Kumar P V et al. have delineated the cytomorphology of plasmacytoid, spindle, and mixed variants of myoepitheliomas in seven patients. The plasmacytoid type had clusters and isolated cells that were round to oval, featuring eccentrically positioned nuclei and mild to copious granular cytoplasm. Ductal cells were observed; however, myxoid tissue was absent. The spindle cell variant exhibited many clusters of spindle cells characterized by little cytoplasm and localized myxoid tissue. The mixed type exhibited characteristics of both plasmacytoid and spindle cell types, together with a myxoid background. Due to insufficient specificity, none of the seven cases were identified as myoepithelioma using aspiration cytology.[11] Gayathri et al. documented a case in which FNAC smears had cells organized in distinct formations, weakly cohesive clusters, and infrequent acinar, papillary, and cylindromatous patterns.[12] The cells had an epithelioid morphology with ovoid nuclei displaying mild anisokaryosis, fine chromatin, normal nuclear contours, indistinct nucleoli, and a modest cytoplasmic volume. Fibrillary material was observed; however, chondromyxoid stroma was absent in the Giemsa stain. No indication of tubule development, mitosis, necrosis, or cytological atypia was observed. A diagnosis of a low-grade salivary gland tumour was made. In our instance, analogous cytomorphology results in a diagnosis of salivary gland neoplasm with indeterminate malignant potential. Myoepitheliomas represent under 5% of ductal differentiation, lacking chondromyxoid matrix or osteoid development in histopathological examination. The clear cell type exhibits cells characterized by transparent to pale eosinophilic cytoplasm, moderate to abundant in quantity, as observed in our case. Immunohistochemically, the expressed myoepithelial markers include vimentin, smooth muscle antigen, S 100, and p63.[3] All our cases had widespread positivity for p63. The malignant variant of myoepithelioma must be distinguished due to its increased aggressiveness and propensity for recurrence despite sufficient resection. The infiltrative growth pattern supports the diagnosis of malignant myoepithelioma, while cellular atypia and pleomorphism, necrosis, and heightened mitotic activity favours high-grade features. The differential diagnosis of myoepitheliomas of the soft palate encompasses benign tumours such as pleomorphic adenoma, nerve sheath tumours, haemangiomas, fibrous histiocytoma, solitary fibrous tumour, paraganglioma, and leiomyoma, as well as malignant tumours including lymphoma, metastatic tumours, leiomyosarcoma, and various inflammatory diseases.[13] Due to the resemblance of many of these lesions in clinical and radiological characteristics, a biopsy is necessary for a definitive diagnosis of myoepithelioma. The absence of specific characteristics in FNAC results in misinterpretation, with pleomorphic adenoma being the nearest differential diagnosis.[3] The lack of chondromyxoid stroma and minimal or absent ductal cells in cytology should indicate a diagnosis of myoepithelioma. The preferred treatment for benign myoepithelioma is straightforward with total surgical resection. They infrequently reappear, resulting in a favourable prognosis. In malignant myoepitheliomas, wide local excision with clear margins is the treatment of choice. Our cases are recommended for regular follow-up and currently exhibits no signs of recurrence.

CONCLUSION

Myoepitheliomas are uncommon neoplasms of the salivary glands. Slow-growing, well-defined salivary gland masses exhibiting diverse patterns of post-contrast enhancement on CT scans should arouse suspicion of myoepithelioma. FNAC serves as an effective diagnostic tool, exhibiting distinctive characteristics such as the absence of chondromyxoid stroma and a minimal presence of ductal cells. Histopathology, in conjunction with immunohistochemistry, constitutes the gold standard for diagnosing myoepitheliomas. Thorough examination for invasion is mandatory to rule out malignant myoepithelioma.
