Comparative health risk assessment of occupational dust exposure in industrial sectors using multiple models and monte carlo simulation
Authors: Zahra Ordudari, Farzaneh Mohammadi, Reza Esmaeili
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dust, a common pollutant resulting from industrial activities, poses significant risks to workers across various sectors, including agriculture, mining, and manufacturing. Dust risk assessments are vital for implementing preventive measures and improving air quality in workplaces. This study aims to identify the most suitable technique for evaluating dust exposure risk among workers in the wood, textile, food, and livestock industries. A cross-sectional descriptive-analytical study was conducted to assess dust exposure risks in the wood, textile, food, and livestock industries using three risk assessment approaches: qualitative, quantitative, and semi-quantitative methods. These methods were compared, and the Monte Carlo Simulation was used to model non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic risks. The highest dust concentration was in the food industry, while the textile sector had the lowest. Dust levels in the textile and food industries exceeded safe limits. US-EPA assessment showed adverse health effects in all industries, with significant carcinogenic risks in the wood, food, and livestock sectors. The SQRA method categorized risks as very high in textile, low in food, and negligible in wood and livestock, while the ICMM method classified risks as intolerable in all industries. A significant correlation was found between risk scores and dust concentrations (P < 0.05). Chemical exposure risks in the studied industries were found to be intolerable, with adverse health effects. The results highlight the critical need for interventions to control dust exposure and mitigate health risks in these sectors.

Introduction

Industrial development enhances employment and socio-economic conditions but also increases air pollution, with dust being a major concern for workers1. Dust refers to solid particles of various sizes, shapes, and densities2. generated through natural or mechanical processes such as processing, crushing, grinding, or explosive activities involving organic and inorganic materials. Its health effects depend on particle type, size, and deposition in the respiratory system3. Occupational exposure to dust is common in industries like agriculture4, mining (miners, team leaders, drill operators, and locomotive drivers)5, ferrous metal foundries6, welding, livestock and poultry farming, chemical and food industries, stonemasonry, textile production3, brick and tile manufacturing4, and woodworking. Dust may be mineral, metallic, or organic2. Airborne dust forms during activities such as rock movement, crushing, and shaping. Both short- and long-term exposure can cause acute or chronic respiratory illnesses7,8.

If dust contains toxic metals like lead, mercury, nickel, chromium, or cadmium, it can cause pulmonary fibrosis and respiratory distress9. Crystalline silica dust can deeply penetrate the lungs, leading to coughing, phlegm, shortness of breath, asthma, lung damage, tuberculosis, kidney disease, arthritis, lung cancer, COPD, bronchitis, pneumoconiosis, silicosis10, cardiovascular diseases, and even death11,12.

In addition, wheat stands as the cornerstone cereal grain in the food and bakery industries13. However, it’s important to note that wheat flour harbors at least 40 allergens, which can provoke adverse health effects in workers and trigger allergic cross-reactivity14. In the UK and Norway, exposure to grain and flour dust ranks as the second most frequently reported cause of occupational asthma13. Within the baking and milling sectors, measurements of total flour dust have shown remarkable variability, ranging from just a few milligrams to over 400 mg/m³ during peak exposure13. Also, wood dust, a complex mixture of organic and inorganic particles, encompasses wood fragments, toxic chemicals, minerals and metals, and microorganisms such as bacterial and fungal spores15,16. In 1995, the International Agency for Research on Cancer classified hardwood dust as a Group 1 human carcinogen. Seven years later, dust from certain softwood species also fell into this category17. Beyond its potential link to lung cancer, wood dust exposure can lead to headaches, lung function impairments, itchy eyes, runny nose, genotoxicity, and hepatotoxicity18. On the one hand, raw cotton dust particles with an aerodynamic diameter less than 15 microns in the textile industry penetrate nasal passages and are deposited in the tracheobronchial tract or lung. Inhalable cotton dust primarily triggers lower airway reactions, while larger particles (40 microns or more) predominantly affect the upper airways19. These reactions can manifest as respiratory symptoms, including irritation of the upper and lower respiratory tracts, characterized by a runny nose, chronic cough, shortness of breath or wheezing, and chest tightness20. Exposure to organic dust may also lead to various allergic or immunotoxic diseases, such as hypersensitivity pneumonitis(HP), organic dust toxic syndrome(ODTS), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease(COPD), and asthma21,22. Also, respirable dust is also generated throughout various poultry production and breeding processes, exposing industry operators to the aforementioned pollutants and putting their health at risk23.

It is estimated that several hundred million people worldwide may be exposed to organic dust during their work. In the United States alone, in 2000, approximately 0.5 million workers were occupationally exposed to grain dust, and 0.8 million to cotton dust24. In India, recent estimates suggest that more than 20 million people employed in intensive livestock production may be exposed to organic dust25.

Based on aerodynamic diameter, dust is categorized as inhalable, thoracic, and respirable9. Inhalable particles (< 100 μm) enter through the nose or mouth, thoracic particles (< 10 μm) pass through the larynx, and respirable particles (< 4 μm) reach deep into the airways. “Total dust” includes particles between inhalable and thoracic sizes2. Total suspended particulates (TSP) consist of particles up to 100 μm, PM10 (< 10 μm), and PM2.5 (< 2.5 μm)3. These fine particles can enter the bloodstream10, affecting the lungs and heart, potentially causing irregular heartbeat, asthma, and reduced lung function11.

According to the International Labour Organization, work-related deaths are attributed to respiratory diseases (21%), cancer (34%), accidents (25%), cardiovascular diseases (15%), and other factors (5%). The World Health Organization estimates that particulate matter exposure causes around three million deaths annually Due to the high risk of occupational respiratory diseases, the National Commission for Occupational Safety and Health reduced the permissible exposure limit for respirable crystalline silica from 0.2 mg/m³ to 0.1 mg/m³ in 2004. However, evidence indicates that even lower concentrations can lead to silicosis with prolonged exposure11.

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) has established a permissible exposure limit (PEL) of 5 mg/m³ for inhalable dust (8-hour TWA). In contrast, The American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists (ACGIH) recommends lower exposure limits: <3 mg/m³ for inhalable dust, < 10 mg/m³ for respirable dust, and < 0.025 mg/m³ for crystalline silica26,27. A study on the textile industry’s spinning process found that the average total dust concentration over one hour was 233 µg/Nm³ (max: 478.1 µg/Nm³, min: 0.005 µg/Nm³), exceeding Indonesia’s regulatory limit of 230 µg/Nm³3. Similarly, Golbabaei et al. measured an average respirable dust concentration of 4.9 mg/m³ among poultry industry workers23.

Given the adverse effects of dust on workers across various industries, the implementation of management programs, including health risk assessments, is crucial in industrial settings. Health risk assessments for chemicals and dust help organizations choose and apply appropriate control measures to eliminate or reduce pollutant emissions28. These assessments describe the adverse health effects of chemical exposure, a process practiced globally by organizations29. Risk assessments can be carried out qualitatively, using risk matrices, or quantitatively, using mathematical models30. One semi-quantitative method that has recently gained attention is the Semi-Quantitative Risk Assessment (SQRA) method31. Several studies have introduced quantitative health risk assessment techniques, such as the US-EPA-based methods28,32. Assessing and controlling dust and chemical hazards are vital for monitoring and implementing preventive measures to improve air quality in dust-laden work environments33. Monte Carlo simulation is the most widely recommended probabilistic risk analysis method, as per the National Academy of Sciences and the United States Environmental Protection Agency, for quantifying variability, uncertainty and finally achieving a quantitative risk assessment34.

For instance, Mahmoudian et al. assessed the health risks of wood dust exposure using the Singapore Risk Assessment (SORA) method, finding a moderate risk that could lead to long-term harm35. Similarly, Duan et al. (2023) reported the time-weighted average concentrations of silica dust in a ferrous metal foundry during various processes, ranging from 0.8 to 83.52 mg/m³. Their risk assessment using the International Council on Mining and Metals (ICMM) method revealed that 100% of jobs involved silica dust exposure, with “unacceptable” risk levels in 59 foundries, leading to pneumoconiosis6.

Despite extensive research on dust exposure, no study has comprehensively assessed risks across multiple industries (especially industries whose dust potentially leads to illness) or identified the most suitable assessment method for specific occupations.

The selected industries were intentionally chosen to represent distinct categories of occupational dust exposure with differing physicochemical and toxicological characteristics. Flour dust in the food industry is primarily biological and allergenic in nature; wood dust in carpentry workshops consists of organic particulate matter with established carcinogenic potential; and mineral dust exposure in stone-related activities is predominantly siliceous, associated with fibrotic and carcinogenic respiratory outcomes4,20. By including these heterogeneous exposure scenarios, the study aimed to evaluate the performance and consistency of different health risk assessment models across dust types with varying intrinsic toxicities. The applied models (US-EPA, SQRA, and ICMM) were not chemically calibrated for specific dust composition but were implemented according to their standardized methodological frameworks, allowing comparative evaluation of their structural sensitivity to exposure magnitude and hazard classification across diverse industrial contexts. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate and simulation health risks associated with respirable dust exposure in the wood, textile, food, and livestock industries. The findings provide managers with a better understanding of industry-specific dust-related health hazards, facilitating informed decision-making and improved risk management.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional descriptive-analytical study was conducted on 80 workers across various industries in Isfahan, including the food industry (grain mill and packaging workers), textile industry (weavers), wood industry (sawmill workers), and poultry industry (egg collectors). The study aimed to assess the chemical risks associated with respirable dust exposure in different occupations and industries and to compare the outcomes of three chemical risk assessment methods based on the following steps:

i.Collection and analysis of dust samples from the air in the industries and occupations under study.ii.Evaluation of chemical risk associated with dust exposure using the quantitative method based on the US-EPA model in the studied industries and occupations.iii.Assessment of chemical risk using the semi-quantitative SQRA method in the studied industries and occupations.iv.Evaluation of chemical risk using the qualitative method of the International Council on Mining and Metals (ICMM) for the air in the industries and occupations under study.v.Comparison of the findings from the four methods with the measured dust concentrations to determine the most suitable approach for dust risk assessment in the industries and occupations under study.vi.Modeling the risk of dust exposure in the studied industries using the Monte Carlo simulation.

Sampling and analysis of respirable dust

Respirable dust measurement and analysis followed the NIOSH 0600 method36. Sampling was conducted at a height of 1.5 m using an SKC Universal 44XR air sampling pump (USA) at a flow rate of 2.5 L/min. Polyvinyl chloride filters (37 mm, 5-µm pore size, SKC, USA) were mounted on a backup pad within a three-piece cassette (Whatman Grade QM-A, 37 mm, UK) (Whatman Grade QM-A, 37 mm; Whatman, Midstone, UK) equipped with a respirable cyclone.

Cotton dust concentration was measured using a vertical elutriator (Sierra Instruments Inc., Carmel Valley, CA) following OSHA standards. This device collects particles with an aerodynamic equivalent diameter cutoff of ~ 15 μm at a flow rate of 7.4 ± 0.2 L/min in the breathing zone (1.6 m above ground). Dust samples were collected on 37-mm polyvinyl chloride filters with a 5-µm pore size37.

Filters were weighed before and after sampling using an electronic balance (Mettler Toledo, AG245, Switzerland) with a precision of 0.01 mg. The pump flow rate was calibrated before and after sampling using an air flow calibrator (Drycal, Mesa Labs Bios Defender 510-H, USA).

Mass concentration was calculated by dividing the measured weight by the sampled air volume, determined from the flow rate and sampling duration38. The collected samples from various industries were then sent to the laboratory for further analysis.

A total of 160 personal respirable dust samples were collected across the four industries (40 per industry). Each sample was obtained over a full-shift duration to reflect time-weighted average exposure conditions. Sampling was conducted under normal operational circumstances without modification of production processes. Environmental conditions, including workshop ventilation status and seasonal climate characteristics, were documented during monitoring to ensure representativeness of routine exposure scenarios. All samples were collected within the breathing zone of workers using calibrated personal sampling pumps, in accordance with the NIOSH 0600 protocol. All exposure measurements and subsequent risk calculations in this study were based exclusively on respirable dust fraction (PM₄), as determined using the NIOSH 0600 method. Inhalable dust fraction was not considered in this study due to differences in sampling methodology and health impact pathways.

Monte carlo simulation

In this study, Monte Carlo simulation (MCS) was used for health risk assessment modeling. MCS is a mathematical technique that estimates the probable outcomes of uncertain events by generating random numbers based on the probability distribution of each variable with inherent uncertainty45. The results are then recalculated across the distribution of the variables. For this study, 10,000 data points were generated to explore potential outcomes. RStudio software (version 2022.02.2) and the Monte Carlo package (version 1.06) were used for MCS calculations46. The first step in this method was to determine the statistical distributions of the input parameters, for which the fitdistrplus package (version 1.1-8) in RStudio was used. Table 6 presents the statistical distributions for the input variables. Based on these distributions, 10,000 data points were generated using R software. The generated data were then used to calculate the carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic risks of the respirable dust.

The selection of probability distributions for Monte Carlo simulation inputs (Table 6) was based on the statistical characteristics of the variables and established practices in exposure assessment studies. Lognormal distributions were assigned to exposure-related variables such as contaminant concentration, as environmental and occupational exposure data typically exhibit right-skewed behavior. Parameters with approximately symmetric variability were modeled using normal distributions. In cases where limited data were available, uniform or triangular distributions were applied based on minimum, maximum, and most likely values. These assumptions are consistent with previous risk assessment studies and widely accepted modeling approaches.

The probability distributions assigned to input variables were selected based on their statistical properties and common practices in occupational exposure modeling. Lognormal distributions were used for exposure-related variables such as respirable dust concentration, reflecting their typically right-skewed distribution. Other parameters, including physiological and exposure factors, were modeled using normal, uniform, or triangular distributions depending on data availability. Monte Carlo simulations were performed using 10,000 iterations to ensure stability and convergence of the output estimates.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS software (version 25). For the evaluation of quantitative variables, the mean and standard deviation were calculated using descriptive statistical tests. In this study, a one-sample t-test was applied to determine whether the mean measured concentrations and corresponding risk scores significantly differed from established reference values, including occupational exposure limits (OELs) and method-specific acceptable risk thresholds.

The results of the toxicological risk assessment for respirable dust exposure across different industries are presented in Table 9. It should be noted that the statistical analysis presented in Table 9 is based on comparison with predefined benchmark values rather than direct comparison between variables. The one-sample t-test was used to evaluate whether the estimated risk scores and measured concentrations significantly deviate from acceptable or permissible limits. This approach aligns with the risk assessment framework, where deviations from regulatory thresholds are of primary importance. The rationale for using this test is based on the fact that risk interpretation in occupational health is inherently benchmark-driven, where both exposure levels and risk scores are evaluated against predefined regulatory or guideline values rather than compared between independent groups. Therefore, the one-sample t-test was considered appropriate for assessing whether observed values deviate significantly from these reference standards.

According to the findings, the average carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic risk scores assessed using Method 1 indicate potential adverse health effects in all industries. Specifically, the carcinogenic risk in the textile industry requires attention, while in the wood, food, and livestock & poultry industries, it is a significant concern.

Based on the toxicological risk assessment using Method 2, the average risk level was negligible in the wood and livestock & poultry industries, indicating no need for further risk evaluation, with measurements required only once every five years. In the food industry, the risk level was low, suggesting no need for continued sampling, with periodic evaluations every five years. However, in the textile industry, the risk level was classified as very high, requiring immediate sampling, engineering controls, personal protective equipment, and urgent corrective actions.

The results of the toxicological risk assessment using Method 3 indicated that the measured risk levels in all studied industries were intolerable.

The time-weighted average (TWA) concentrations of respirable dust in the food and textile industries exceeded the permissible limits, while in the wood and livestock & poultry industries, the concentrations were below the permissible limits. As shown in Table 9, respirable dust was evaluated and ranked using all three methods.

Further analysis compared the risk scores obtained from the three toxicological risk assessment methods with the TWA concentrations of respirable dust in the studied industries. A one-sample t-test revealed a significant difference between the mean risk scores from all methods and the TWA concentrations in the studied industries (P-value < 0.05). However, the significance of the difference in non-carcinogenic risk scores between Method 1 and Method 3 was lower in the livestock & poultry industry compared to the other industries and methods.

Discussion

This study was conducted in the wood, food, textile, and poultry industries, all of which are exposed to respirable dust particles. The objective was to evaluate toxicological risks using three different methods and compare the results with the actual measured values. Worker exposure to respirable dust was assessed using the NIOSH 0600 method36. The selection of wood, food, textile, and poultry industries allowed comparison across dust types with fundamentally different physicochemical and toxicological characteristics. Wood dust consists predominantly of organic particulates and has been classified as carcinogenic in relation to sinonasal cancers. Flour and poultry dust are largely biological and allergenic in nature, frequently associated with occupational asthma and chronic inflammatory airway responses. In contrast, textile dust contains mixed organic fibers and endotoxins, which may trigger both obstructive and restrictive respiratory patterns. These toxicological differences are critical when interpreting inter-industry risk variation, as concentration-equivalent exposures may not correspond to equivalent biological impact47,48.

It is important to emphasize that the present study specifically focuses on respirable dust particles, which are capable of penetrating into the alveolar region of the lungs. Unlike inhalable dust, which primarily deposits in the upper respiratory tract, respirable particles are associated with more severe chronic health outcomes, including obstructive and restrictive lung diseases. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted within the context of respirable dust exposure and should not be generalized to inhalable dust fractions.

The findings indicated that exposure levels in the food and textile industries exceeded the permissible limit, while in the wood and livestock & poultry industries, exposure remained below the permissible limit.

In the current study, the minimum, average, and maximum respirable dust concentrations in the wood industry were estimated at 1.3, 4.09, and 7.1 milligrams per cubic meter, respectively, with the average concentration being below Iran’s permissible limit. In contrast, Mahdoyan’s study reported minimum, average, and maximum exposure concentrations of 34.4, 22.3, and 6 mg/m³, respectively, which were several times higher than Iran’s OEL35. These findings are inconsistent with the present study. According to Scarselli et al., factors such as job classification, industrial sector, workshop size, environmental conditions, and geographic location can influence exposure levels49. Therefore, the differences in results can be attributed to variations in the sampling environment, including climate and the nature of the work. Mahdoyan’s study took place in a humid city in Iran with a furniture workshop, whereas the current study was conducted in a dry city with a carpentry workshop. Similarly, Schipper et al. examined worker exposure to respirable wood dust in carpentry and furniture production factories, finding significantly lower exposure in carpentry workshops. This disparity was attributed to the more frequent use of sanding and woodworking tools in furniture production50.

In the present study, the minimum, average, and maximum respirable dust concentrations in the food industry were estimated at 2.4, 10.83, and 18.6 milligrams per cubic meter, respectively, with the average concentration exceeding Iran’s permissible limit. Dust concentrations in South African mill workers ranged from 1.3 to 17.6 milligrams per cubic meter51. Similarly, Kakooei et al. reported an average concentration of 11.1 milligrams per cubic meter in Iranian flour mill workers52. which closely aligns with the results of this study. Additionally, the study by Khodadadi et al. on exposure to respirable dust in a flour mill found that the time-weighted average respirable dust concentration exceeded the permissible limit53, which is consistent with the findings of the present study.

In this study, the minimum, average, and maximum respirable dust concentrations in the textile industry were estimated at 0.1, 1.36, and 4.1 milligrams per cubic meter, respectively, with the average exceeding Iran’s permissible limit. A study on yarn spinning processes in textile mills reported an average respirable dust concentration of 233 micrograms per newton cubic meter over 1 h, with a maximum of 1.478 micrograms and a minimum of 0.005 micrograms, which exceeded the threshold set by Indonesia’s government regulations (230 micrograms per newton cubic meter)3. This result is consistent with the findings of the present study.

In the present study, the minimum, average, and maximum respirable dust concentrations in the poultry industry were estimated at 0.3, 2.91, and 6.6 milligrams per cubic meter, respectively, with the average being below Iran’s permissible limit. Golbabaei et al. measured respirable dust concentrations in poultry workers, which averaged 4.9 milligrams per cubic meter23, a value higher than the current study and exceeding the permissible limit. This discrepancy could be attributed to variations in sampling locations and the specific occupations of the individuals involved. Beyond sampling variability, the chemical composition and metal content of particulate matter may significantly influence toxicological risk. Recent meta-analyses have demonstrated that particulate matter enriched with heavy metals such as lead, cadmium, chromium, and nickel exhibits amplified oxidative stress and inflammatory potential compared to organic dust of similar mass concentration. Seasonal variability and emission source have also been shown to modify ecological and human health risk indices54,55. Therefore, differences in industrial dust toxicity may not be fully captured when evaluation is based solely on mass concentration values.

To assess toxicological risks using the three methods, exposure risk levels for 80 workers across four industries were calculated. Table 9 showed that the risk calculated using Method 1 indicated adverse health effects in the non-carcinogenic category for all four industries. For carcinogenic risk, the exposure to carcinogenic substances should strongly be considered in the wood, food, and livestock & poultry industries, and should also be considered in the textile industry. In Method 2, the risk in the food industry was low, negligible in the wood and livestock & poultry industries, and very high in the textile industry. In Method 3, the risk for all industries was calculated as intolerable.

The time-weighted average respirable dust concentrations were higher than the permissible limit in the food and textile industries, and lower in the wood and livestock & poultry industries. These findings align with the expectation that the exposure risk should be intolerable in industries where the concentration exceeds the permissible limit, and acceptable in those below the limit, thus confirming the effectiveness of the methods. It is important to note that, while the time-weighted average respirable dust concentrations in the wood and livestock & poultry industries were slightly below the permissible limit, Methods 1 and 3 provided accurate estimates for all industries. As observed in the risk assessment formulas, there is a direct relationship between exposure concentration and risk score. As the concentration increases, so does the risk score, indicating a strong and meaningful correlation between chemical concentrations and the final risk score in these methods, despite other influencing factors. However, risk estimation should also be interpreted in light of existing exposure control measures within each sector. The presence or absence of local exhaust ventilation (LEV), process enclosure, mechanical air circulation, and worker compliance with respiratory protective equipment can substantially modify effective inhalation exposure. For example, textile facilities may operate enclosed spinning systems with partial dust extraction, while small-scale woodworking workshops may lack standardized ventilation infrastructure. Similarly, poultry facilities often involve intermittent high-dust tasks combined with inconsistent PPE usage. The current risk models did not explicitly incorporate these engineering and administrative modifiers as independent variables, which may partly explain discrepancies between concentration values and final risk categorization.

In the SQRA method, despite dust concentrations exceeding the permissible limit in the food and textile industries, the estimated risk levels were classified as low and very high, respectively, while in the wood and livestock & poultry industries, risks were considered negligible even when concentrations approached regulatory limits. This apparent inconsistency reflects the structural characteristics of the SQRA approach rather than a direct linear relationship between concentration and risk level.

Within this semi-quantitative framework, the Hazard Rating (HR) is primarily determined based on acute toxicity indices such as LD₅₀ and LC₅₀ values. Grain dust, although strongly associated with chronic respiratory disorders and allergic conditions, does not exhibit high acute toxicity and therefore receives a relatively low HR score, which reduces the overall calculated risk. In addition, the Exposure Rating (ER) is derived from standardized weekly exposure assumptions based on a 40-hour workweek, potentially moderating the exposure category even when measured concentrations exceed occupational exposure limits. Moreover, the SQRA method does not differentiate between carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic effects and does not incorporate individual physiological parameters. These methodological characteristics may limit its sensitivity to chronic health outcomes and contribute to potential underestimation of health risks in environments with elevated dust exposure. These findings are consistent with previous comparative studies. For instance, Karimi Zeverdegani et al.56, reported that the SQRA method yielded lower or moderate risk levels for metal fume exposure even when measured concentrations exceeded permissible limits, whereas the US-EPA quantitative approach demonstrated clearer alignment between exposure concentration and calculated health risk. Similarly, a scoping review by Zhou et al.43 highlighted that semi-quantitative occupational risk assessment models may underestimate chronic health risks when hazard classification relies primarily on acute toxicity indices. Together, these studies support the interpretation that the lower risk classification observed in the present study is attributable to inherent methodological constraints of SQRA rather than an absence of potential health concern. Importantly, occupational dust exposure should not be interpreted solely through the lens of regulatory thresholds. Recent epidemiological investigations in Southwest Iran have demonstrated that both short-term and long-term exposure to fine particulate matter are associated with increased hospital admissions, cardiovascular morbidity, and systemic inflammatory responses57. These findings highlight that even exposure levels near occupational limits may contribute to cumulative health burden over time, particularly when particle sources include crustal materials and combustion-derived components. From a methodological perspective, the use of the one-sample t-test in this study reflects the benchmark-oriented nature of occupational risk assessment. Unlike comparative statistical designs that evaluate differences between groups, the primary objective here was to determine whether exposure levels and risk scores exceed or fall below established regulatory and health-based thresholds. Therefore, statistical inference was centered on deviation from these reference values rather than inter-variable comparison. However, it is acknowledged that complementary analyses such as correlation assessment may provide additional insights into the relationship between concentration and risk estimates.

Methods 1 and 3 demonstrated greater sensitivity to concentration-driven exposure variability under the conditions of the present study. Between the two, Method 1 provides a more comprehensive framework due to its ability to distinguish between carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic risk. However, Method 3 offers a simpler formula, making the estimation of the parameters involved easier by relying on guideline tables. Additionally, Method 3 provides a clearer classification of risks and outlines necessary actions, making it more user-friendly.

Nonetheless, Method 3 does not account for individual factors such as body weight, which means the calculated risk level may only be generalized to the industry or occupational group under study. In a study by Mahmoudian et al., the SORA method was used to assess the health risk from exposure to wood respirable dust in a furniture workshop. Although the average estimated concentration exceeded the permissible limit, the risk was classified as moderate35. Similarly, Karami et al. assessed the risk level of metallic fumes in furnace operators in a casting industry, and while some fumes exceeded the permissible limit, the estimated risk using the SQRA method was moderate. However, when applying the USEPA method, the concentration of fumes in the work environment aligned with the estimated risk level56. Furthermore, Karami-Mosafer et al. evaluated formaldehyde exposure in histopathology lab operators using three methods based on the US-EPA. The results of this study showed that both the concentration of formaldehyde and its estimated carcinogenic risk were high58, aligning with the findings of the current study.

From a practical risk management perspective, the simulation results suggest that priority should be given to engineering controls aimed at reducing airborne dust concentration at the source. Investments in local exhaust ventilation systems, process enclosure, wet suppression techniques, and automated material handling are likely to yield greater risk reduction compared to reliance on exposure time reduction or personal protective equipment alone. While administrative controls and PPE remain essential supplementary strategies, concentration-driven parameters exerted the strongest influence on quantitative risk scores in the present analysis, underscoring the importance of source control interventions.

Sensitivity analysis is an important component of Monte Carlo-based risk assessment, as it helps identify the most influential parameters affecting model outputs. Although a formal sensitivity analysis using Tornado diagrams was not conducted in the present study, the structure of the applied models suggests that variables such as exposure concentration and exposure duration have the greatest influence on the estimated risk values. Future studies are recommended to incorporate quantitative sensitivity analyses to further improve model robustness and parameter prioritization.

Conclusion

Workers in the food and textile industries were exposed to respirable dust levels exceeding permissible limits, while the average exposure in the wood and livestock & poultry industries was slightly below the permissible limit. As a result, technical and engineering controls should be prioritized in the control programs. In terms of risk classification, methods 1 and 3 indicated undesirable and intolerable chemical exposure risk levels for all four industries, which aligned with the measured concentrations. However, despite the high exposure concentrations, method 2 estimated the risk level as moderate or low. Therefore, the findings suggest that Methods 1 and 3 demonstrated stronger alignment with measured exposure concentrations in the studied industries. Method 1 provides a more comprehensive quantitative framework by incorporating individual exposure parameters and distinguishing between carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic risks. In contrast, Method 3 offers a simpler and more practical structure, facilitating risk classification and management decision-making through guideline-based scoring tables. However, the selection of an appropriate risk assessment method should not be considered universally fixed. The choice of method may depend on the availability and quality of exposure data, the regulatory context governing occupational health practices, and whether a detailed distinction between carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic risks is required. Semi-quantitative approaches such as SQRA may remain useful in settings with limited monitoring data or for preliminary screening purposes, whereas quantitative models may be more appropriate when precise risk estimation and regulatory compliance evaluation are necessary.
