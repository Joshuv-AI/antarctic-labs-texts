Development and validation of a diagnostic model for malignant pleural effusion based on random forest
Authors: Xu Guo, Xiao-Lei Wei, Shu-Min Yin, Yu-Peng Li, Kan Zhai, Huan-Zhong Shi, Zheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe timely and accurate identification of malignant pleural effusion (MPE) is crucial for prompt treatment. The current diagnostic approaches based on cytology, pathology, or biomarkers have inherent limitations; notably, the drawbacks of single indicators can be overcome by combining various indicators. Accordingly, the present study was designed to construct a machine learning (ML) model integrating pleural effusion (PE) and peripheral blood indicators to identify MPE.MethodsThis retrospective study enrolled 1,239 adult participants with PE who underwent diagnostic thoracentesis and then eligible participants were randomly assigned to training (70%) and test (30%) sets. Demographic data, laboratory variables, and systemic inflammatory indicators were analyzed. After feature selection by Boruta and the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator, we constructed five ML models, including multivariable logistic regression, support vector machine, random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting, and k-nearest neighbors. The predictive performance of each model was comprehensively evaluated using multiple metrics, calibration curve, and decision curve analysis. In addition, Shapley additive explanations and Gini importance were applied to interpret each model.ResultsAmong the employed ML models, the RF model achieved optimal performance. Following further importance analysis, the eight-feature RF model achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.940, with sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, accuracy, and F1 score of 0.875, 0.835, 0.882, 0.826, 0.858, and 0.878, respectively. These key features included PE-adenosine deaminase, carcinoembryonic antigen, PE-cell count, PE-total protein, PE-percentage of mononuclear cells, age, PE-lactate dehydrogenase, and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio.ConclusionsWe developed and validated an interpretable and accurate eight-feature RF model for MPE identification with an AUC of 0.940. This model provides a valuable approach for MPE differential diagnosis.Keywords: Malignant pleural effusion (MPE), machine learning (ML), Shapley additive explanations (SHAP)

Key findings

• Random forest (RF) model achieved the optimal performance in distinguishing malignant pleural effusion (MPE) from benign pleural effusion (PE).

• PE-adenosine deaminase, carcinoembryonic antigen, PE-cell count, PE-total protein, PE-percentage of mononuclear cells, age, PE-lactate dehydrogenase, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio were identified as the top key indicators for the diagnosis of MPE.

What is known and what is new?

• The combination of multiple indicators derived from peripheral blood and PE has been shown to yield superior diagnostic efficacy compared to single indicators.

• The RF model developed in this study achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.940, highlighting the clinical value of multi-indicator diagnostic models for MPE.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The multi-indicator diagnostic RF model demonstrated potential for application in the differential diagnosis of MPE, aiding in timely clinical management and improving patient prognosis.

Introduction

Background

Malignant pleural effusion (MPE), a frequent complication of primary or metastatic malignancies involving the pleura, occurs in 15% of patients with cancer, with lung cancer accounting for the largest proportion among all cancers (37.8%) (1,2). Given that MPE severely impairs patients’ quality of life (3) and delayed treatment caused by misdiagnosis adversely affects patient prognosis, accurate and prompt differentiation of MPE is critical for guiding appropriate and timely therapeutic intervention.

Rationale and knowledge gap

Nevertheless, the current diagnostic techniques for MPE have inherent limitations. The cytological or histological identification of malignant cells from pleural effusion (PE) or tissue, obtained via thoracentesis or pleural biopsy, respectively, is the gold diagnostic standard for MPE. The cytological examination of PE is simple and widely available; however, its diagnostic sensitivity for metastatic pleural malignancy is only 46–59%. Primary pleural biopsy techniques include image-guided biopsy and medical thoracoscopy. The sensitivity of computed tomography (CT)-guided and ultrasound-guided biopsy for metastatic pleural malignancy is 61–90% and 77–87%, respectively, whereas medical thoracoscopy achieves a diagnostic sensitivity of 89–95% (4). Despite their superior diagnostic performance, these invasive procedures have a higher risk of complications, substantial costs, and heavy reliance on the operator’s expertise. Relevant biomarkers have also been applied to the diagnosis of MPE. Notably, PE carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) demonstrates promising diagnostic performance. However, no consensus has been reached regarding the cutoff value of this diagnostic indicator, which also exhibits low sensitivity (5). Thus, there is a clear clinical need for a more accurate and less invasive alternative for identifying MPE.

Over the past few years, machine learning (ML) has been increasingly employed for the diagnosis, prediction, monitoring, and prognosis of various diseases. Compared with traditional statistical approaches, ML demonstrates superior performance in the analysis of high-dimensional clinical data with complex collinearity (6). Several studies have developed and validated ML-based models for differentiating MPE from benign pleural effusion (BPE) using tumor markers (7) and laboratory parameters (8). However, these studies have limitations in terms of feature selection or model evaluation. In this study, we developed an ML diagnostic model incorporating a selection strategy that intersects results from the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) (9) and Boruta (10) to prioritize impactful features based on demographic and laboratory characteristics and systemic inflammatory indicators. This feature selection framework not only reduces dimensionality but also selects the truly useful features and has been validated in diverse clinical contexts, including diabetes mellitus and hemodialysis-related outcomes (11,12).

Objective

In this study, we aimed to identify key features for MPE diagnosis using the LASSO-Boruta integrated method and to compare the performances of the constructed models. Additionally, the optimal model was interpreted by Shapley additive explanations (SHAP). We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0233/rc).

Methods

Study population

A total of 1,239 adult participants diagnosed with PE from January 2014 to June 2025 at the Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital, Capital Medical University, Beijing were enrolled in this retrospective study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) patients with confirmed PE diagnosis by chest X-ray, CT, or ultrasound; and (II) patients who underwent diagnostic thoracentesis. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) patients with indeterminable pathological diagnosis of PE; or (II) patients with empyema, chylothorax, or hemothorax. MPE was diagnosed if malignant cells were found on pleural fluid cytology or pleural biopsy, or if imaging showed malignant pleural nodules or thickening with a confirmed extrapleural primary malignancy (13,14). BPE was characterized by the absence of malignant disease and included tuberculous pleural effusion (TPE), parapneumonic pleural effusion, and other PE with established etiology (13,14). Detailed diagnostic criteria for BPE were shown in Table S1. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the ethics committee of Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital (No. 2025-ke-1085) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Data collection and processing

The potential diagnostic variables analyzed in this study included: (I) demographic data (sex and age); (II) biochemical and cytological variables of PE [total protein (TP), chloride, glucose, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), adenosine deaminase (ADA), cell count, and percentage of mononuclear and multinuclear cells]; (III) biochemical and cytological variables of peripheral blood [white blood cell (WBC) count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, eosinophil count, platelet count, TP, albumin, LDH, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein, and CEA]; and (IV) coagulation profile [activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), prothrombin time (PT), and fibrinogen]. Based on these primary variables, a series of systemic inflammatory indicators were calculated. Table S2 provides detailed calculation methods and formulas for these derived indicators.

Among patients diagnosed with MPE and BPE, those with missing clinical data exceeding 15% were excluded. The rate of missing variables is presented in Figure S1. Before conducting imputation, we evaluated the missing data mechanism using Little’s Missing Completely At Random (MCAR) test and Chi-squared test.

Random forest (RF) imputation was performed to generate a complete dataset using the “missRanger” package in R. This method predicts missing values by capturing nonlinear relationships and interactions among clinical laboratory variables. Compared with simple imputation, it is more suitable for non-normal distribution and can reduce bias. In this study, the imputation model trained on the training set was applied to the test set.

Feature selection

The data set was randomly divided into training (70%) and test (30%) sets, and LASSO and Boruta were employed for feature selection using the training set and implemented via the “glmnet” and “Boruta” packages in R, respectively. For LASSO, the core parameter settings were as follows: family = “binomial” and alpha =1. Through 10-fold cross-validation, two lambda values were determined: lambda.min (lambda corresponding to the minimum cross-validation deviance) and lambda.1se (lambda within one standard error of lambda. min). In this study, lambda.min was selected as the final parameter to achieve a balance between feature retention and model simplification. The Boruta algorithm compares the importance of original features with that of “shadow features” to determine the true importance of each feature. The core parameter settings were as follows: maxRuns =500 and ntree =500.

The features identified by these two methods were defined as the preliminary candidate features. Additionally, the variance inflation factor (VIF) was employed to assess the multicollinearity among these features, and those variables with a value <2 were retained (15).

Development and evaluation of ML models

Five classifiers, including multivariable logistic regression (MLR), support vector machine (SVM), RF, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and k-nearest neighbors (KNN), were employed to construct models. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) derived from 10-fold cross-validation was selected as the primary optimization metric in the training set. All modeling processes were implemented using the “mlr” package in R (details in Appendix 1). Synthetic minority oversampling technique was employed to address class imbalance in the training set. Besides, this process was performed only on the training subset in each fold. To eliminate the influence of different variable scales on model accuracy, the data was standardized in different ways according to the characteristics of the respective models during the hyperparameter tuning in the training set.

The performance of the models was comprehensively evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic curve, calibration curve, decision curve analysis, and key quantitative metrics, including specificity, sensitivity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), accuracy, and F1 score. These metrics were calculated at a fixed threshold of 0.5. This threshold provides a balanced performance between sensitivity and specificity and is easy to understand and apply in clinical practice.

Model interpretation and feature reduction

SHAP and Gini importance were used to assess the features’ importance. SHAP is a unified framework for interpreting ML models, which is rooted in Shapley values in game theory (16). Gini importance is specifically applicable to decision trees and ensemble models. Its core principle lies in quantifying the attribution of each feature to reducing the “impurity” of decision trees through the process of “node splitting” (17). The optimal model was further simplified based on the contribution ranking of features to enhance interpretability. The hyperparameters for the final RF model are as follows: ntree =588, mtry =2, nodesize =50.

Sample size estimation

To determine the proper sample size of the training set, we adopted the widely accepted “10 events per variable” and the 4-step procedure established by Riley et al. (18). For the test set, the estimation was conducted following the method proposed by Hanley and McNeil (19). Detailed calculation processes and results are presented in Table S3.

Statistical analysis

The normality of continuous numerical variables was examined using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Student’s t-test was used to compare normally distributed data between two groups, and results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. The Mann-Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed data, and the results are expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, and group comparisons were conducted using the Chi-squared test. The DeLong’s test was performed to assess the differences among different models. Furthermore, a complete-case analysis was performed based on the final model to examine the robustness of study results. These analyses were implemented using R (version 4.4.2). A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Feature selection

Eligible participants were randomly assigned to the training (70%) and test (30%) sets (Figure 1). Specifically, the training set consisted of 516 participants (301 participants with MPE, 215 participants with BPE) and the test set included 219 participants (128 participants with MPE, 91 participants with BPE). No statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics were observed between the training and test sets (Table S6), indicating successful randomization and comparability of the two sets.

Boruta, which quantitatively estimates the importance of every feature, was used to screen the candidate features. This algorithm identified 37 key features, including PE-ADA, CEA, PE-LDH, PE-TP, age, PE-cell count, C-reactive protein-to-lymphocyte ratio, PE-percentage of mononuclear and multinuclear cells, and other indicators (Figure 2A). Meanwhile, LASSO, a compression estimation method, identified 14 features, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte and platelet ratio, WBC count, PE-ADA, PE-TP, APTT, PT, age, TP, ESR, PE-percentage of mononuclear cells, PE-LDH, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), CEA, and PE-cell count (Figure 2B). The features identified by these two methods were defined as the preliminary candidate features consisting of neutrophil-to-lymphocyte-and-platelet ratio, WBC count, PE-ADA, PE-TP, APTT, PT, ESR, age, PE-percentage of mononuclear cells, PE-LDH, PLR, CEA, and PE-cell count (Figure 2C). Subsequently, the multicollinearity among these features was assessed using the VIF method. The results revealed minimal multicollinearity among these variables, as all VIF values were below 2 (Table S7). Therefore, all 13 features were retained for model construction.

Model development and performance comparison

Five classifiers, including MLR, SVM, RF, XGBoost, and KNN, were employed to construct models. The hyperparameters of these classifiers are presented in Table S8. Table S9 and Table 2 detail the performance metrics of these models in the training and test sets, respectively. Among these models, the RF model achieved highest AUC (0.940) in the test set with 0.871 for F1 score. The AUC values for KNN, XGBoost, MLR, and SVM were 0.936, 0.935, 0.935, and 0.930, respectively (Figure 3A). There was no statistically significant difference in AUC between the RF model and other models (Table S10). However, the standard error of AUC of RF was smallest (0.0139) (Table S11), indicating the more stable and reliable performance among all models. Additionally, the RF model exhibited balanced sensitivity (0.867) and specificity (0.824), reflecting robust diagnostic consistency. The calibration curve demonstrated that the model’s predictions were closely aligned with the actual occurrence of MPE (Figure 3B). In addition, the decision curve analysis demonstrated that this model yielded a net clinical benefit when used to distinguish MPE from BPE across a wide range of decision thresholds (Figure 3C). Collectively, these results support the selection of the RF model as the final diagnostic model for further analysis among these five models.

Model interpretability and final model identification based on feature contributions

At the global level, feature importance was ranked by Gini importance (left panel) and SHAP values (right panel) (Figure 4A). This analysis revealed that PE-ADA had the strongest MPE predictive power. The top eight key predictors were PE-ADA, CEA, PE-cell count, PE-LDH, PE-percentage of mononuclear cells, age, PE-TP and PLR, highlighting their critical roles in MPE prediction (Figure 4B). We assessed the performance of RF models across different feature subsets guided by SHAP value-based feature reduction (Table 3). The eight-feature RF model was identified as the final model, with 0.940, 0.875, 0.835, 0.882, 0.826, 0.858, and 0.878 for AUC, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, accuracy, and F1 score in the test set, respectively (Table 3). The new model included PE-ADA, CEA, PE cell count, PE-TP, PE percentage of mononuclear cells, age, PE-LDH, and PLR (Figure 5A) and the details was showed in the Table S12. Besides the sample size was sufficient to meet the statistical requirements, thereby ensuring the model’s reliability. The AUC of this eight-feature RF model in the complete-case analysis was 0.934 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.900–0.968] with corresponding sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, accuracy, and F1 score of 0.862 (95% CI: 0.785–0.923), 0.809 (95% CI: 0.708–0.892), 0.862 (95% CI: 0.785–0.923), 0.809 (95% CI: 0.708–0.892), 0.840 (95% CI: 0.779–0.892), and 0.862 (95% CI: 0.804–0.912), respectively. These results were consistent with those of the primary model, confirming that missing value imputation did not introduce significant bias.

The SHAP dependence plot visually explained the relationships between individual features and the predicted probability of MPE, where positive SHAP values indicated a higher likelihood of MPE (Figure 5B). It can be observed from the figure that lower PE-ADA, PE-percentage of mononuclear cells and PLR, higher CEA, PE-LDH, PE-cell count and PE-TP, and older age were associated with higher predicted probability of MPE.

To compare with traditional methods, we used the conventional clinical approaches including PE cytology examination, PE-ADA, CEA and CR [cancer ratio (serum LDH-to-pleural ADA ratio)] for the diagnosis of MPE. Among all MPE patients (n=429), 203 patients were confirmed positive by PE cytology corresponding to a sensitivity of 47.3%. The AUC of PE-ADA for diagnosing MPE was 0.755 with a cut-off value of 22.5 U/L in the test set. And the AUC of CEA for diagnosing MPE was 0.798, with a cut-off value of 3.59 ng/mL (Table S13). These results indicated that our RF model performed better. We further applied this RF model to distinguish cytology-non-positive MPE (n=60) from BPE (n=91) in the test set. This RF model achieved an AUC of 0.850 (95% CI: 0.788–0.911), which had a good diagnostic performance. The corresponding sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, accuracy, and F1 score were 0.667 (95% CI: 0.585–0.957), 0.967 (95% CI: 0.803–1.000), 0.930 (95% CI: 0.787–1.000), 0.815 (95% CI: 0.798–0.963), 0.848 (95% CI: 0.828–0.934), and 0.777 (95% CI: 0.723–0.916), respectively.

Discussion

In this study, we developed an ML model to distinguish MPE. Our study enrolled 1,239 participants with PE, and the RF model, which had the highest AUC of 0.940, was identified as the best model in comparison with MLR, SVM, XGBoost, and KNN. Additionally, this RF model exhibited good calibration performance and positive clinical net benefit.

Artificial intelligence and ML have been increasingly applied to the diagnosis of MPE (7,8,20). However, previous studies have limitations in terms of feature selection or model evaluation. One study developed a deep learning tool using CT images, achieving an AUC of 0.791. However, its reliance on advanced imaging limits its clinical applicability (20). Another study focused on tumor markers reported that the AUC of the XGBoost model combining CEA and carbohydrate antigen 153 was 0.921, but other clinical and laboratory information that could enhance accuracy was overlooked (7). In a recent study utilizing comprehensive laboratory information, the AUC of XGBoost reached 0.918; however, the lack of systematic feature selection led to the inclusion of redundant or non-specific features (e.g., hematocrit, calcium), and the pathological relevance of these variables to MPE was not clarified (8). These limitations impede the translation of the models into clinical practice. Compared with previous studies, our model not only contained PE indicators but also integrated peripheral indicators to achieve the combination of local and systemic indicators, significantly improving the model’s applicability. Moreover, to ensure the clinical interpretability of the diagnostic model, we employed a feature selection strategy by intersecting results from LASSO and Boruta. LASSO prioritizes features according to their contribution to model performance through shrinkage estimation (9), while Boruta evaluates the statistical relevance of each feature against random noise via shadow features (10). This targeted approach not only mitigated collinearity and overfitting, but also enhanced the interpretability and generalizability of the model by focusing on truly impactful predictors. In conclusion, this selection strategy facilitated a superior diagnostic model.

Although ML models often outperform human and traditional methods in diagnostic tasks, their “black-box” characteristics make it challenging to fully comprehend how decisions are generated. SHAP value provides a unified framework for both global and local features in ML models. This approach enables consistent and accurate quantification of each feature’s attribution to the prediction, while also providing the interpretation of patient-specific predictions (16). Thus, SHAP enhances clinicians’ understanding and trust in the predictive model and facilitates its practical application. Combining this method and the LASSO-Boruta feature selection strategy, we constructed an RF model incorporating eight discriminative features, including PE-ADA, CEA, PE-cell count, PE-TP, PE-percentage of mononuclear cells, age, PE-LDH, and PLR.

In our study, SHAP identified PE-ADA and serum CEA as core discriminative indicators for MPE. This is consistent with the widespread use of these two indicators in MPE diagnosis; however, their utility in MPE diagnosis is quite limited when used alone (21-23). ADA is a key purine metabolism enzyme, and its activity reflects T lymphocyte activation (24). Previous studies have demonstrated that the ADA level was frequently lower than 40 U/L in MPE (25). This is consistent with our study, which revealed that lower PE-ADA indicated a higher probability of MPE, in which the function of T cells is impaired (26). Additionally, CEA in serum and PE are both widely used for the diagnosis of MPE (25,27). PE-CEA exhibits high specificity (mostly >90%), but its sensitivity varies significantly (ranging from 35% to 89%) depending on the cutoff value applied. The level of serum CEA is influenced by systemic physiological and pathological conditions, resulting in relatively low sensitivity in diagnosing MPE (27). During data collection, it was found that more than 50% of participants lacked information on PE-CEA; thus, serum CEA was included in this study owing to its lower missing rate, which may be associated with its greater accessibility in clinical practice. Our model not only incorporates PE-ADA and serum CEA, but it also employs other readily accessible indicators, thereby addressing these limitations and significantly enhancing the diagnostic performance for MPE. Specifically, our RF model outperformed the single indicator, with an AUC of 0.940, sensitivity of 0.875, and specificity of 0.835. Notably, the other indicators are also associated with the occurrence and progression of the disease and have been used for the diagnosis of MPE in multiple studies (28-30). Elevated PE-TP was associated with tumor-induced vascular permeability, which promotes pleural space protein leakage (31). Similarly, higher PE cell counts were correlated with the accumulation of malignant and associated inflammatory cells (26). In this study, MPE risk increased with advancing age, consistent with the increase in the incidence of malignant tumors with age (32). Higher levels of LDH (a key glycolytic enzyme released upon cell damage) are attributed to tumor-induced tissue destruction and enhanced anaerobic glycolysis in cancerous tissues (33). Additionally, although a previous study demonstrated that PLR acted as an independent prognostic predictor for shortened overall survival in patients with stage IV non-small cell lung cancer (34), our study highlights its diagnostic value for MPE. Thus, our RF model integrates multiple clinical indicators, including demographics, PE and serum features, and systemic inflammatory indicators, and thus can assist clinicians in identifying MPE.

In our study, the RF algorithm exhibited optimal performance. This ensemble ML method employs Bootstrap sampling and random feature selection to construct multiple decision trees, and yields integrated classification results via voting. Furthermore, this method inherits many advantages of decision trees while effectively avoiding overfitting (17), and is highly suitable for our dataset, because it can capture non-linear relationships within our data without complex preprocessing. Additionally, the sample size of this study aligns with the RF’s merit of anti-overfitting in small-to-medium-sized samples (35).

However, this study also has several limitations. First, this study was retrospective, single-centric lacking external validation. Resultantly, selection bias is likely to impede the application of our findings to diverse patient populations. In particular, TPE accounted for more than half of the BPE cases, which may further restrict the model’s applicability in regions with a low prevalence of tuberculosis. Second, there was a discrepancy between the expected MPE prevalence (23.7%, Table S3) and the prevalence in our data (58.4%). This difference was attributed to referral bias, temporal changes, and inclusion criteria in our study. Third, only demographic variables, laboratory variables, and relevant derived features were included in the analysis. Diagnostic performance may be further enhanced by incorporating other variables, including medical history, symptoms, physical signs, and imaging data. Fourth, RF imputation was performed to generate a complete dataset; this method may induce data bias. Multiple imputation is needed to produce more high-quality data in future studies. Therefore, a multicenter and prospective study with external validation cohorts needs to be conducted to further validate the reliability and robustness of this model, particularly in patients with diagnostically challenging PE.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed an interpretable eight-feature RF model with excellent performance for MPE diagnosis. This model incorporated readily available clinical laboratory and demographic parameters, which may confer potential advantages for its application in the settings with limited access to advanced diagnostic tools. SHAP analysis further enhanced the model’s interpretability by elucidating its clinical significance. This model demonstrated potential for application in the differential diagnosis of MPE, aiding in timely clinical management and improving patient prognosis.
