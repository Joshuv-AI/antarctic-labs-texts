Streptomyces-induced Glycerol-3-Phosphate enriches beneficial microbiota to enhance resistance against banana Fusarium wilt
Authors: Bingyu Cai, Lu Zhang, Zekai Wu, Jie Wang, Yongzan Wei, Raza Waseem, Miaoyi Zhang, Dengfeng Qi, Dengbo Zhou, Yankun Zhao, Kai Li, Huigang Hu, Jianghui Xie, Wei Wang

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense Tropical Race 4 (Foc TR4) is the causal agent of banana Fusarium wilt, a destructive soil-borne disease. Using antagonistic microorganisms, such as Streptomyces species, offers a promising strategy for controlling fungal diseases. However, their field application is limited by an incomplete understanding of microbe-plant-pathogen interactions.ResultsThis study shows that the marine-derived Streptomyces malaysiensis WHL7 exhibits strong antagonistic activity against multiple phytopathogenic fungi in vitro, particularly Foc TR4. In pot experiments using natural soil, WHL7 treatment significantly reduced the incidence of Fusarium wilt from 55% in the Foc TR4-inoculated control to 15%. No protective effect was observed in sterilized soil, indicating that its biocontrol efficacy depends on the indigenous microbial community. Integrated metabolomic analysis reveals that WHL7 stimulates the key root secretion of glycerol-3-phosphate (G3P). The compound enriches beneficial Bacillus and Pseudomonas species in the rhizosphere. Compared to the Foc TR4-treated group, exogenous application of G3P reduced Foc TR4 abundance by 73%, inhibited pathogenic infection, and consistently increased the populations of Bacillus and Pseudomonas. Introduction

Bananas and plantains are vital global staple foods and fruit crops, widely cultivated in tropical and subtropical regions. In 2024, global production exceeded 184 million tons from 13.11 million hectares, supporting millions of livelihoods (FAOSTAT, 2025). However, most cultivated bananas are triploid and propagate asexually, leading to a narrow genetic background that increases susceptibility to pests and diseases [1]. Especially, banana Fusarium wilt caused by Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense tropical race 4 (Foc TR4) is a devastating fungal disease. This pathogen infects over 80% of banana cultivars, resulting in severe yield losses. Its chlamydospores can survive in soil for over 30 years without a host, making disease management exceptionally challenging [2, 3]. Although chemical fungicides and cultural practices are employed for control, their efficacy remains limited. These fungicides also pose significant risks of environmental pollution. So, as an alternative, biological control emerges as a promising and sustainable tool to manage the destructive soil-borne disease.

Biocontrol microorganisms represent valuable biological resources for managing plant diseases and reducing reliance on chemical pesticides in agriculture. Commonly utilized strains include Bacillus, Pseudomonas, Trichoderma, and Streptomyces [4–6]. Among those, Streptomyces spp. are particularly noteworthy due to their production of diverse bioactive compounds, demonstrating significant potential in the biocontrol of plant diseases [7]. The previous studies have identified several Streptomyces strains with antifungal activity against Foc TR4, such as Streptomyces sp. CB-75, SCA3-4, and YYS-7 [8–10]. Despite extensive research, only a few biocontrol agents have achieved large-scale agricultural application [11]. Key limitations include poor environmental stability, inconsistent field efficacy, narrow host specificity, and inadequate soil persistence [12–14]. This highlights a critical knowledge gap in understanding interactions of biocontrol agents with soil biotic and abiotic characteristics, key factors determining their efficacy in sustainable agriculture. The rhizosphere is a critical interface for plant health and soil ecosystem function, where complex signaling and metabolic exchanges occur between plant roots and diverse microbial communities [15–17]. These microbes enhance plant disease resistance both directly through niche competition and antibiotic production, and indirectly by inducing systemic resistance in the host [18–21]. A stable and diverse rhizosphere microbiome can further serve as a protective barrier against pathogens. Nevertheless, the mechanisms governing the interactions among biocontrol agents, host plants, and the rhizosphere microbiota are still not well understood.

The assembly of rhizosphere microbial communities is governed by a dynamic interplay of plant genotype, soil properties, and environmental factors [22]. Root exudates such as organic acids, sugars, amino acids, flavonoids, and plant hormones serve as the primary mediators of plant–microbe communication. They function as nutritional substrates and molecular signals to enrich microbes [23–26]. Notably, the plant–microbe dialogue operates bidirectionally. Beneficial microbes like Pseudomonas spp. can alter the recycling pathway of host amino acids to modify exudate profiles [27], while Streptomyces spp. significantly regulate plant root metabolism patterns and promote the accumulation of immune-related metabolites such as flavonoids, organic acids, and phenylalanine [28, 29]. However, current understanding of the Streptomyces-plant metabolites-microbial community interactions remains incomplete, particularly regarding metabolite functions and molecular mechanisms.

A previously isolated Streptomyces sp. WHL7 from marine soft coral shows antagonistic potency against Foc TR4 in vitro and in vivo [30]. This study systematically deciphers the biocontrol mechanism of WHL7 through an integrated multi-omics approach. The genome sequencing identified key biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) responsible for antifungal compound production. The combined microbiome-metabolome analysis uncovered that the key bioactive metabolites enhanced WHL7’s biocontrol efficacy and significantly altered Foc TR4 colonization patterns in the rhizosphere of banana. Pot experiments further demonstrated critical metabolites enriching beneficial microbiota to induce plant systemic resistance. These findings provide a mechanistic framework for Streptomyces-based Foc TR4 inhibition and establish a foundation for developing precision biocontrol strategies in banana cultivation.

Results

Marine Streptomyces sp. WHL7 possesses the potential to produce numerous bioactive compounds

Previous studies demonstrated that WHL7 extracts exhibit strong antifungal activity against Foc TR4 [30]. Here, we further showed that volatile organic compounds (VOCs) produced by this strain also displayed antifungal activity, effectively inhibiting mycelial growth of Foc TR4 in vitro (Fig. 1a). This suggests that WHL7 employed a multi-faceted antagonistic strategy involving both metabolites and VOCs. We hypothesized that the genome of WHL7 would harbor diverse BGCs responsible for metabolite production. To investigate this, its draft genome was sequenced and contained 11,820,727 bp and 69.98% of GC content, which is typical for the Streptomyces genus (Fig. S1). The longest contig is 832,367 bases, and the contig N50 value is 280.552 Kb (Table S1). A total of 9,310 protein-coding genes were predicted. Among these, 2,813 genes were annotated using the KEGG database (Table S2). The most prevalent category was metabolism (1728 genes, 61.45% of the annotated genes), followed by genetic and environmental information processing (424 genes, 15.08%), and cellular processes (269 genes, 9.57%) (Fig. 1b and Table S2). A phylogenetic tree was constructed using 16 Streptomyces genomes to further characterize the taxonomic position (Table S3). Phylogenetic analysis of 1,471 single-copy orthologous genes revealed that WHL7 formed a robust cluster with Streptomyces malaysiensis, supported by a 100% bootstrap value and an average nucleotide identity (ANI) of 98.71% (Fig. 1c, Table S4). To assess its potential as an antifungal agent, BGCs in the WHL7 genome were predicted using antiSMASH. The analysis identified multiple BGCs encoding polyketides and other secondary metabolites with putative antifungal properties (Fig. 1d, Table S5), including geldanamycin (100% similarity), curamycin (100% similarity), nigericin (94% similarity), hygrocins (96% similarity), and azalomycin F3a (91% similarity). Additionally, two siderophore-synthesizing gene clusters were detected. The abundance and diversity of BGCs in the WHL7 genome underscore its significant potential for producing antifungal compounds.

WHL7 exhibits antifungal activities against various phytopathogenic fungi

The genomic evidence revealing an extensive repertoire of BGCs in WHL7 prompted us to further evaluate its antifungal activity against ten phytopathogenic fungi (Fig. 1e). The antagonistic assay in vitro showed that WHL7 possesses potent broad-spectrum antifungal activity. The inhibition rates ranged from 45.40% to 77.67%. The strongest suppression was observed against Foc TR4, with a remarkable inhibition rate of 77.67 ± 0.55%. Similarly, strong activity was exhibited against Alternaria tenuissima (76.09 ± 1.16%), Colletotrichum gloeosporioides (74.66 ± 1.13%), and Curvularia lunata (72.28 ± 2.03%). In contrast, the weakest antagonistic effect was recorded against Curvularia fallax (45.40 ± 1.18%). This implies that the strain has broad-spectrum inhibitory activity, mediated by diverse mechanisms.

WHL7 improves the plant resistance against Foc TR4 by enriching beneficial rhizosphere microbiota

In pot experiments, WHL7 application effectively inhibited the infection of Foc TR4 in banana plants. Under Foc TR4 infection, WHL7 treatment significantly increased chlorophyll content, though no significant differences were observed in plant height or stem diameter (Fig. 2a). By contrast, in sterilized soil, there were no significant differences in disease severity (Fig. S2) or detected physiological indicators between Foc TR4 and WHL7 + Foc TR4 treatment groups. Further analysis showed no correlation (R = −0.34, p = 0.15) between the in vitro antifungal activity of WHL7 against Foc TR4 and its biocontrol efficacy in natural soil. This result indicated that direct pathogen suppression is not the primary mechanism responsible for disease control in vivo (Fig. S3). Instead, the protective effect of WHL7 appears to be is mediated through modulation of the rhizosphere microbial community. To investigate this hypothesis, we analyzed the bacterial community composition in the rhizosphere soil across four treatment groups, including control (CK), Foc TR4 (F), WHL7 (W), and WHL7 + Foc TR4 (WF). In the presence of Foc TR4, WHL7 significantly altered the relative abundance of major bacterial phyla, notably increasing Proteobacteria and reducing Acidobacteria (Fig. 2b). In contrast, the relative abundance of Streptomyces showed no significant difference was observed among treatments (Fig. S4). Alpha diversity indices, including Chao1 richness (Fig. 2c) and Shannon diversity (Fig. 2d), demonstrated that WHL7 mitigated the decline of microbial diversity caused by Foc TR4 infection. Beta diversity analysis revealed clear separation among the microbial communities under four treatments (p = 0.004), with WHL7 + Foc TR4 and Foc TR4 samples forming distinct clusters (Fig. 2e). Further analysis identified 302 significantly enriched and 222 depleted taxa in the WHL7 + Foc TR4 group compare to the Foc TR4-treated group based on differentially abundant amplicon sequence variants (DAAs) (Fig. 2f). Most DAAs belonged to the class Gammaproteobacteria, with Pseudomonas being the most prominently enriched genus (Fig. 2g).

Bacillus and Pseudomonas are core beneficial bacteria inhibiting Foc TR4 infection

In order to explore core functional bacteria in the rhizosphere soil, we performed a weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) on amplicon sequence variant (ASV) abundance data from the rhizosphere microbial community. This analysis revealed a specific module strongly associated with disease suppression. WHL7 application significantly weakened the correlation between microbial community composition and disease incidence (Fig. S5a), indicating its potential to modulate microbiome structure and enhancing resistance. The ASV-based co-expression network was divided into 12 distinct modules (Fig. 3a). Among these, the MEgrey module was identified as the most biologically significant. Correlation analysis between MEgrey module and disease incidence showed a strong significant negative correlation (r = −0.76, p = 0.004) (Fig. 3b). In addition, a highly significant relationship in the MEgrey module was observed between module membership (MM) and gene significance (GS) (r = 0.58, p < 1 × 10–200) (Fig. S5b). This indicates that the centrally connected ASVs in the network are closely associated with the suppression of Foc TR4, highlighting their potential keystone roles in the microbiome's antifungal function. Taxonomic dissection of the hub ASVs in the MEgrey module revealed a dominance of Proteobacteria (41.22%), followed by Acidobacteria (19.52%) and Bacteroidetes (12.41%) (Fig. S5c). A detailed examination of the co-expression network further identified specific key genera that function as major hubs, including the well-known beneficial taxa Bacillus, Pseudomonas, and Streptomyces. These genera formed densely interconnected sub-networks (Fig. 3c), implying strong cooperative interactions to underpin the stability and efficacy of the disease-inhibitory microbiome.

Integrating WGCNA with DAAs identified 143 overlapping ASVs from the highly preserved MEgrey module, all of which were hub taxonomic units with significant module membership (Table S6). This convergence of network centrality and differential abundance underscores their functional importance and strong association with WHL7-induced suppression of Foc TR4. To further investigate their functional roles, representative culturable strains corresponding to these hub ASVs were selected and evaluated for in vitro antagonistic activity against Foc TR4. Through selective isolation from the rhizosphere soil of WHL7-treated banana plants, 115 bacterial strains were obtained, primarily belonging to the genera Bacillus (40.87%) and Pseudomonas (59.13%). A phylogenetic tree constructed from all isolates divided into four clades. From each clade, the strain with the best antifungal effect were chosen as a representative to analyze anti-Foc TR4 activity (Fig. 3d). In plate confrontation assays on PDA medium, three Bacillus strains (5–36, 5–88, and 5–136) and one Pseudomonas strain (6–34) exhibited pronounced inhibition of Foc TR4, significantly reducing mycelial growth (Fig. 3d). These in vitro antagonistic activities align with their putative role in suppressing disease incidence in vivo. Together, the findings provide strong evidence that WHL7-associated disease suppression is mediated through the enrichment of specific beneficial taxa within the rhizosphere microbiome.

WHL7-induced root metabolites improve plant resistance against Foc TR4

To further investigate the potential mechanism of WHL7-induced resistance, we characterized the root metabolic profiles of banana plants under different treatment conditions at 0, 1, and 3 days post-inoculation (dpi) using non-targeted metabolomics. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed clear separation among the metabolic profiles of the four treatment groups. The most pronounced difference was observed at 1 dpi (Fig. 4a). Specifically, plants treated with WHL7 + Foc TR4 exhibited distinct metabolic reprogramming compared to those inoculated with Foc TR4 alone or the control. Comparative analysis identified 249 differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) between the WHL7 + Foc TR4 and Foc TR4-only treatments. Of these, 151 were up-regulated and 98 were down-regulated at 1 dpi. At 3 dpi, 132 were up-regulated and 156 were down-regulated in the WHL7 + Foc TR4 treatment group relative to the Foc TR4-only group (Fig. 4b). A total of 42 DAMs were consistently altered at two time points (Fig. S6a), suggesting their potential roles in sustained defense modulation. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that these DAMs were significantly associated with key metabolic pathways, including flavonoid biosynthesis, phenylalanine/tyrosine metabolism, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, and tryptophan biosynthesis (Fig. S6b). Similar to the microbial community analysis, WHL7 application significantly attenuated the correlation of metabolites with disease incidence (Fig. 4c). To identify key disease-suppressive metabolites, WGCNA of the metabolome revealed nine distinct co-expression modules (Fig. S7a). The MEsalmon module showed a strong negative correlation with disease incidence (r = −0.77, p = 0.003) (Fig. 4d). This module contained 158 metabolites. WHL7 treatment caused significant upregulation of 50, downregulations of 83, and no significant change of 25 (Fig. S7b). Importantly, metabolite significance (GS) for disease incidence was highly correlated with MM (r = 0.82, p = 1.2 × 10–39), indicating that hub metabolites within this network are central to disease suppression (Fig. 4e).

To identify these pivotal metabolites, we extracted and analyzed the hub components in the MEsalmon module. Using the cytoHubba plugin with 12 topological algorithms, central metabolites such as glycerol-3-phosphate (G3P), 17-phenyltriene prostaglandin, and F2a serine amide were identified as potential regulatory hubs (Fig. 4f). By integrating WGCNA and DAMs results, we found 30 overlapping metabolites present in both the MEsalmon module and the DAM set (Fig. 4g and Table S7). Furthermore, these hub metabolites from the WHL7-related microbiome showed a strong correlation with beneficial ASVs. Notably, G3P exhibited significant positive correlations with multiple beneficial bacterial taxa. The strength and number were higher than those of other metabolites (Fig. 4h). WHL7 treatment also led to marked accumulation of G3P in banana roots relative to the control (Fig. S8), indicating that WHL7 induces G3P biosynthesis. Thus, G3P may serve as a key metabolite mediating the plant–microbe interaction and potentially enriches beneficial microorganisms under WHL7 treatment.

Glycerol-3-phosphate enriches beneficial rhizosphere microbiota

To investigate whether host-derived metabolites mediate the enrichment of beneficial rhizosphere microbiota, an experiment applying exogenous G3P was performed. Banana seedlings were treated with different concentrations of G3P prior to inoculation with Foc TR4. Control plants were either inoculated with Foc TR4 alone or treated with water. To monitor fungal colonization and infection, a green fluorescent protein (GFP)-labeled Foc TR4 was used. At 14 dpi, confocal microscopy revealed extensive hyphal colonization in the corm and root tissues of seedlings inoculated with Foc TR4 alone. In contrast, G3P pretreatment markedly reduced pathogen infection (Fig. 5a). We also carried out a regression analysis to assess the relationship between G3P accumulation and disease suppression in all four treatment groups (CK, Foc TR4, WHL7, and WHL7 + Foc TR4) (Fig. 5b). G3P abundance exhibited a strong negative correlation with disease incidence (R2 = 0.96, p = 0.0082). The elevated G3P levels are tightly associated with reduced disease severity at the system level rather than within a single treatment (Fig. 5b). We further evaluated the effect of G3P on Foc TR4 dynamics in the soil. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was used to quantify fungal biomass in the rhizosphere. A standard curve (R2 = 0.9906) was generated using serial dilutions of Foc TR4 spores (Fig. S9). G3P application significantly reduced Foc TR4 populations in the soil, with the strongest suppressive effect observed at 0.75 nM (Fig. 5c). In vitro assays confirmed that G3P did not directly inhibit Foc TR4 growth (Fig. S10), indicating that the observed reduction in pathogen colonization and infection is not due to direct antifungal activity. We hypothesize that G3P enhances banana resistance by modulating the rhizosphere microbial community, thereby indirectly limiting pathogen proliferation.

Therefore, microbiome analysis was used to further characterize the rhizosphere microbial composition following G3P treatment. Taxonomic profiling at the phylum level revealed overall shifts in bacterial community composition, consistent with the results from β-diversity and genus-level analyses (Fig. S11). Alpha diversity showed that G3P application partially component maintains microbial richness, as reflected by the Chao1 index, which was reduced following Foc TR4 infection. However, at higher G3P concentrations (1.5 nM and 3.0 nM), the Shannon index decreased compared to the control, suggesting that a reduction in community evenness was likely due to the selective enrichment of specific bacterial taxa (Fig. 5d-e). Beta diversity analysis based on principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) showed clear separation among all treatment groups, indicating pronounced shifts in overall microbial community structure (Fig. 5f). In particular, microbial communities in G3P-treated soils were distinctly separated from those of the Foc TR4-only and control groups (Fig. 5f). Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEFSE) identified several bacterial taxa significantly enriched in G3P-treated rhizosphere. These included Bacillus and Pseudomonas, genera also enriched in the WHL7-treated rhizosphere. Additionally, beneficial plant growth promotion bacteria such as Rhodomicrobia and Mesorhizobium were also significantly more abundant (Fig. 5g). an overlap analysis revealed that WHL7 and G3P treatments shared 75 beneficial taxa, representing 52.45% of those enriched by WHL7. This indicates that both agents modulate a common core of rhizosphere microbia (Fig. 5h). So, the inhibition of Foc TR4 infection by exogenous G3P is likely mediated through this reshaping of the rhizosphere microbial community.

G3P promotes the growth of beneficial bacteria

In order to clarify the role of G3P in medicating the enrichment of beneficial bacteria in the rhizosphere, growth promotion and taxis experiments were conducted using Bacillus or Pseudomonas as representative strains. Growth dynamics showed that the addition of G3P to Luria–Bertani (LB) medium significantly enchanced the bacterial proliferation compared to the control (Fig. 6a). After 24 h of culture, the optical density at 600 nm of the tested bacteria was markedly higher in G3P-amended media. Therefore, G3P could serve as a readily metabolizable substrate that supports the growth of these bacteria. Discussion

As significant biocontrol agents, Streptomyces can produce diverse secondary metabolites with antimicrobial and antiviral properties [31]. The metabolite diversity enables their survive across diverse environmental niches. Especially, marine-derived Streptomyces represent a high valuable and underexplored resource for bioactive compounds. Their adaptation to extreme marine conditions drives the biosynthesis of structurally unique secondary metabolites with potent biological activities [32]. In this study, the marine-derived strain Streptomyces malaysiensis WHL7 exhibited strong antagonistic activity against several phytopathogenic fungi, particularly Foc TR4. Genomic analysis revealed that WHL7 harbors numerous BGCs linked to secondary metabolism, reflecting its metabolic versatility. These genomic features are likely responsible for the strain’s ability to inhibit pathogens and mediate ecological interactions.

Although the metabolic potential of Streptomyces species is well-established, conventional cultivation-based approaches often fail to detect bioactive compounds due to the prevalence of transcriptionally silent cryptic BGCs in their genomes [30]. This limitation has propelled the adoption of genome mining for the systematic identification of putative BGCs encoding bioactive metabolites. Notably, the genome of WHL7 revealed remarkable metabolic diversity, harboring multiple putative BGCs for polyketides, nonribosomal peptides, and siderophores. The previous study reported that certain Streptomyces-derived compounds, including Nigericin and Geldanamycin, contribute to plant pathogenicity [33]. Although BGCs for both compounds are present in the WHL7 genome, their transcription and expression appear to be contingent upon specific inductive conditions [30]. This inference is corroborated by no visible disease symptom or growth inhibition assessed following WHL7 application. Conversely, it significantly reduced Fusarium wilt incidence. These findings supported that metabolic robustness might serve as a critical adaptation strategy for microorganisms in harsh environments [34].

Extensive research reveals that Streptomyces species employ diverse strategies to inhibit fungal pathogens, such as secreting antimicrobial metabolites, hydrolytic enzymes, and VOCs [35]. Consistent with these findings, WHL7 extracts were shown to induce severe morphological abnormalities in hyphae and spores of Foc TR4 [30], suggesting a synergistic disruption of cell wall synthesis and membrane integrity. Despite their antagonistic properties in vitro, the practical application faces significant challenges. Their inherently slow growth may limit competitiveness against native soil microbes, and large-scale cultivation is difficult due to the complex nutritional and environmental requirements for metabolite production. Moreover, bioactive compounds may exhibit reduced stability when introduced into non-native environments. VOC-mediated suppression frequently depends on sustained emission thresholds that are hard to maintain in open-field systems. These constraints might explain that the potent in vitro antagonism of S. malaysiensis WHL7 against Foc TR4 did not fully translate to equivalent efficacy in experimental performance. This discrepancy underscores the necessity to evaluate biocontrol agents not only for direct inhibition but also for their ecological resilience, including their stability, microbial community, and metabolic functionality under environmental stressors.

Beyond direct antagonism, indirect mechanisms, such as induced plant resistance and modulation of the rhizosphere microenvironment, are also critical to biocontrol efficacy [36]. Intriguingly, the direct antagonistic effect of WHL7 against Foc TR4 fully lost in sterilized soil, indicating that its protective capacity depends on biotic interactions. Instead, WHL7 treatment markedly enriched beneficial taxa such as Bacillus and Pseudomonas, both known for their antagonistic traits and ability to induce systemic resistance in plants [37]. These beneficial microbes are reported to enhance plant defense by stimulating the production of plant-specific metabolites involved in salicylic acid, jasmonic acid, and ethylene signaling pathways [38]. Thus, the biocontrol function of WHL7 appears to relay heavily on its ability to reshape the rhizosphere microbiome. For instance, introduction of Streptomyces albus S4 was shown to increase the abundance of Bacillus subtilis, which correlated with suppression of Foc TR4 [39]. Field trials in Foc TR4-infected banana plantations further demonstrated that Streptomyces inoculants (e.g., S. lydicus, S. morookaensis) can reduce disease incidence by modifying microbial community structure [40, 41]. Therefore, a more comprehensive assessment of biocontrol agents should consider their interactions with both the host plant and the resident soil microbial community.

However, the mechanism by which WHL7 enriches beneficial microbes to enhance banana plant immunity remains unclear. Beyond their well-known antibiotic and plant growth-promoting properties [38], Streptomyces are emerging as microbial ‘signalers’ that trigger systemic metabolic changes in plants [18]. In this study, multi-omics analysis identified G3P as a critical root metabolite induced by WHL7. Whether G3P induction is a general feature of Streptomyces or unique to strain WHL7 still need to be elucidated. Previous studies have demonstrated that G3P functions as an important mobile signal involved in the regulation of plant systemic acquired resistance (SAR) [42, 43]. However, the potential role of G3P-induced sar in plant roots is poorly understood. We found that these elevated G3P levels correlated strongly with reduced Foc TR4 disease incidence. Key ASVs showed strong co-occurrence with G3P accumulation during Foc TR4 challenge. A selective increase in beneficial rhizosphere bacteria was observed such as Pseudomonas and Bacillus. Our results suggest that the root-exported G3P contributes to the restructuring of rhizosphere microbiota (Fig. 7). The enriched functional microbes activated key metabolic pathways in banana roots, including the biosynthesis of flavonoids, phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan, and α-linolenic acid, all of which are associated with plant defense response [44]. Our study extends this concept by demonstrating that microbially induced G3P enriches specific rhizosphere microorganisms. This link reveals how plant metabolic signals are translated into community-level disease suppression.

While the mixed-gel assay showed no direct inhibitory of G3P on Foc TR4 in vitro, pot experiments verified that G3P significantly reduced pathogen colonization in the soil and root infection. Interestingly, The G3P-mediated disease inhibition followed a non-dose-dependent pattern, further supporting an indirect mechanism of action. The indirect regulation likely depends on various environmental factors, such as soil heterogeneity and microbial community structure. High-throughput sequencing revealed that G3P markedly increased the relative abundance of beneficial rhizosphere microbes, particularly Bacillus and Pseudomonas. A concomitant decrease in shannon diversity supported a selective enrichment of specific bacterial taxa. A similar pattern of microbial enrichment was observed in WHL7-treated plants, suggesting that WHL7 may shape the banana rhizosphere microbiome by inducing G3P production. G3P addition was also found to enhance bacterial taxis and growth rates. However, empirical evidence still needs to determine whether these bacteria directly ingest or utilize G3P. Moreover, we detected a notable increase in plant growth-promoting taxa such as Rhizobium and Sphingobium, indicating that G3P may not only suppress pathogens but also promote the proliferation of beneficial microbes. Thus, the disease-suppressive effects of G3P is driven primarily by indirect modulation of the rhizosphere microbiome. These findings support a new G3P-based strategy for managing Foc TR4 through targeted microbiome manipulation. While this study demonstrated consistent microbiome alterations and disease suppression in pot experiments, such conditions do not fully replicate the environmental complexity of field systems. Therefore, field validation remains essential to evaluate the practical biocontrol potential of G3P.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that WHL7 has broad-spectrum antifungal activity in vitro, especially against Foc TR4. Pot experiments showed that WHL7 employs antifungal mechanisms In natural soil that differs from its antagonistic mode in plate assays. metabolomic profiling revealed that WHL7 inoculation stimulates plant roots to secrete G3P, Which enriches rhizosphere beneficial microbes, particularly Bacillus and Pseudomonas. although G3P did not directly inhibit Foc TR4 growth, its exogenous application recapitulates the WHL7-induced microbiota shift and reduced pathogen colonization. these enriched microbes activated systemic acquired resistance in plants through key metabolic pathways. Our findings indicated that WHL7 suppresses Foc TR4 indirectly by eliciting G3P-mediated enrichment of beneficial bacteria, rather than direct antagonism.

Materials and methods

Genome annotation and taxonomic classification of Streptomyces sp. WHL7

Genomic DNA of Streptomyces sp. WHL7 was extracted using the TIANGEN Bacterial DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA integrity was assessed by electrophoresis on 1% (w/v) of agarose gel. The purity was evaluated using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), with OD260/280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0. Genome sequencing was conducted on the Illumina HiSeq × 10 platform (Majorbio, Shanghai, China). Raw reads were processed with fastp v1.99.2 to remove adapter sequences, trim low-quality bases (quality score < 20), and discard reads shorter than 50 bp. Clean reads were assembled using SPAdes v4.0.0 with default parameters [45]. Assembly quality was evaluated using QUAST v5.2.0, which provided metrics such as N50, L50, GC content, and total assembly length [46]. Protein-coding sequences (CDSs) were predicted using Prodigal v2.6.3 in the single-genome mode. Sequences lacking a complete gene structure were removed [47]. Functional annotation of CDSs was performed against the EggNOG database (v5.0) using emapper v2.1.12 with default parameters (e-value cut off: 1e−5) [48]. Functional enrichment analysis was carried out in R v4.2.1 with the clusterProfiler v4.12.6 package, including KEGG pathway enrichment [49]. BGCs of secondary metabolites were predicted via antiSMASH v7.1 [50].

The 16S rRNA gene sequence was amplified following the protocol described by Wang et al. [30]. The product was sequenced by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd.. The obtained 16S rRNA gene sequence was aligned against the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) to identify closely related species. The genome sequences of 15 closely related species were downloaded (Table S3). Single-copy orthologous genes among these strains were identified using OrthoFinder2 [51]. These gene sequences were aligned using ParaAT, and combined in a conserved order using the module of seqkit [52, 53]. A species phylogenetic tree was constructed with IQtree2 under the maximum likelihood framework [54]. The ANI values between WHL7 and the selected strains were calculated using FastANI v1.33 [55].

Antifungal activity assay of volatile organic compounds

The antifungal activity of VOCs produced by WHL7 against Foc TR4 was evaluated using a dual-plate assay with a minor modification [56]. Briefly, WHL7 was inoculated onto yeast extract (YE) medium agar plates (10 g/L yeast extract, 20 g/L agar) and incubated at 28℃ for 48 h. A 5-mm mycelial disc was taken from actively growing margins of 5-day-old Foc TR4 colonies on potato dextrose agar (PDA; 200 g/L potato extract, 20 g/L dextrose, 20 g/L agar). The selected disc was transferred to the center of fresh PDA plates. For the dual culture, a WHL7-YE plate and a Foc TR4-PDA plate were sealed together face-to-face using three layers of Parafilm® to facilitate VOC exchange without physical contact. These plates were incubated at 28℃ for 6 days. A YE plate without WHL7 was used as a control. Each treatment included three biological replicates. All experiments were independently repeated twice.

Antifungal activity of WHL7 against multiple phytopathogenic fungi

Ten phytopathogenic fungi were selected to evaluate the antifungal activity of WHL7. They were Alternaria tenuissima (ATCC 26513) from wheat, Botrytis cinerea (ATCC 11542) from grape, Curvularia fallax (ATCC 38579) from banana, Curvularia lunata (ATCC 42011) from maize, Colletotrichum gloeosporioides (ATCC 58222) from mango, Colletotrichum fragariae (ATCC 58718) from strawberry, Colletotrichum acutatum (ATCC 56815) from loquat, Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense tropical race 1 (ACCC 31271) from banana, Fusarium graminearum (ATCC 46779) from wheat and Fusarium oxysporum f. sp cucumerinum (ATCC 204378) from cucumber. All fungal strains were kept in the Institute of Tropical Biotechnology, Chinese Academy of Tropical Agricultural Sciences. The antagonistic activity was determined using a plate confrontation assay [30]. A 5-mm mycelial disc was placed at the center of the PDA plate. WHL7 was inoculated at four equidistant points (2.5 cm) around the fungal disc. Control plates contained only the pathogenic fungal disc. All plates were incubated at 28℃ in darkness for 7 days. Antifungal activity was assessed by measuring the radial growth of fungal colonies, and inhibition rate was calculated [57]. Each treatment included three biological replicates. All experiments were independently repeated twice.

Collection of banana rhizosphere soils inoculated with WHL7

To investigate the effect of WHL7 on the rhizosphere microbial community structure, four experimental treatments were established: (i) Foc TR4 alone (106 CFU g−1 soil; Foc TR4), (ii) WHL7 alone (106 CFU g−1 soil; WHL7), (iii) co-inoculation of WHL7 and Foc TR4 (106 CFU g−1 soil each; WHL7 + Foc TR4), and (iv) water only (CK). For each treatment, forty healthy and uniform banana (Musa acuminata Cavendish cv. ‘Brazil’) seedlings were selected. In the Foc TR4 group, Foc TR4 spore suspension was thoroughly mixed with soils at a final spore concentration of 106 CFU g−1 soil. After 24 h of incubation at room temperature, WHL7 fermentation broth was added to soils with a final density of 106 CFU g−1 soil to achieve WHL7 + Foc TR4. All plants were maintained in a greenhouse at 28℃, 70% relative humidity, and under natural light conditions for 40 days. For each treatment, rhizosphere soils were collected from ten individual banana plants and thoroughly homogenized to account for plant-to-plant variability. Banana seedlings were carefully uprooted. Loosely adhering bulk soil was removed by gentle shaking. The tightly attached soil on roots was brushed off and collected into sterile 50 mL tubes. The pooled soil was then divided into three subsamples, which were used as replicates for DNA extraction, 16S rRNA gene sequencing, and qPCR analyses. The samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80℃. Disease index, plant height, stem diameter, and chlorophyll content (SPAD) were recorded for each treatment group. Each treatment included twelve biological replicates and all experiments were independently repeated twice.

Effect of Streptomyces sp. WHL7 on banana rhizosphere microbial community

The bacterial community composition of banana rhizosphere soils was assessed by high-throughput sequencing of the hypervariable V3-V4 region of 16S rRNA gene. Total genomic DNA was extracted from rhizosphere soil samples using the FastDNA Spin Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The V3-V4 region was amplified using the universal primers (341F: CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG and 806R: GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT). PCR products were subjected to paired-end sequencing on the Illumina HiSeq platform (Magigene, Guangzhou, China). Raw reads were processed with fastp v1.99.2, which including adapter removal, elimination of reads shorter than 50 bp, and trimming of low-quality bases (Q-score < 20) [58]. Chimeric sequences were removed using VSEARCH v2.29.2 [59]. Clean paired-end reads were merged using FLASH v1.2.11 (minimum overlap 10 bp) [60]. ASVs were generated at 100% similarity using the deblur plugin in QIIME2 v2023.5 [61]. Taxonomic assignment of representative ASV was performed against the SILVA database (release 138.1) with a confidence threshold of 80% [62]. Alpha diversity indices, including Chao1 (species richness) and Shannon (community diversity), were calculated in the diversity module of QIIME2. Group difference in alpha diversity was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test in R (v4.2.1). Beta diversity was measured using weighted UniFrac distance matrices and visualized via PCoA. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was applied to determine significant differences in community structure among groups. DAAs between groups were identified using the ANCOM method within the QIIME2 composition module, with a significance threshold of |log₂(fold change)|≥ 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.01.

Metabolomic analysis of banana roots

Banana root samples from each treatment group were harvested at 0, 1, and 3 days post-inoculation (dpi) for non-targeted metabolomic analysis. At each time point, three independent biological replicates were collected. Each consists of 10 g of fresh root tissue. The samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80℃. Sample preparation and metabolite extraction were performed according to the standard protocols provided by BioMarker Technologies Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Briefly, the frozen samples were ground into fine powder under liquid nitrogen and extracted with 1 mL of pre-chilled 70% aqueous methanol (v/v, −20℃) spiked with internal standards for quality control. After vortexing for 3 min, the extracts were incubated on ice for 10 min and subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4℃. The supernatants were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and transferred to LC–MS vials for subsequent analysis.

Metabolite analysis was performed using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC system coupled to a Thermo Fisher Q Exactive™ Orbitrap mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was carried out on an acquity UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) maintained at 40℃. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B), with a gradient elution program optimized for a total run time of 20 min. The flow rate was set at 0.3 mL/min, and the injection volume was 2 μL. Mass spectrometric detection was performed in both positive and negative ionization modes under the following parameters: capillary voltage 3.5 kV, capillary temperature 320℃, sheath gas flow rate 35 arb, auxiliary gas flow rate 10 arb, and scan range m/z 100–1500. Data processing was conducted using the vendor’s software (Compound Discoverer 3.1, Thermo Fisher) for peak extraction, alignment, and normalization. We removed metabolites with missing values in more than 50% of samples. The remaining features were normalized to the total ion intensity of each sample and subsequently log2-transformed to improve a normal distribution. PCA was applied to visualize global metabolic differences among treatments and time points. Differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were identified using the limma package (version 3.60.6, R Bioconductor), applying thresholds of |log₂fold change|≥ 1 and a false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p ≤ 0.05. Pathway enrichment analysis of DAMs was conducted via the MetaboAnalyst 6.0 online platform under default settings [63].

Identification of key ASVs and metabolites associated with disease incidence

To identify DAAs and DAMs associated with Foc TR4 infection, we conducted an integrative network analysis using the Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) package (v1.73) [64]. Before network construction, both amplicon sequencing and metabolomic datasets were preprocessed to ensure comparability. The amplicon sequence variant (ASV) table was filtered to remove low-abundance ASVs (lower than 20% of samples) and normalized via centered log-ratio transformation to mitigate compositional bias. Metabolite intensity data were normalized by total ion current and log₂-transformed to approximate a normal distribution.

For each dataset, a signed weighted correlation network was constructed based on a topological overlap matrix (TOM). The soft-thresholding power was determined by assessing the scale-free topology fit index (R2 ≥ 0.85) using the pickSoftThreshold function in WGCNA. Hierarchical clustering was then applied to the TOM-based dissimilarity matrix (1-TOM), and network modules were identified via the dynamic tree cut algorithm with the following parameters: minModuleSize = 30, deepSplit = 2, and mergeCutHeight = 0.25. Modules containing lower than 30 members were excluded from subsequent analyses. Representative ASVs and metabolites from each module were correlated with disease incidence. Hub features identified through this process were further visualized using Cytoscape (v3.8.2) to elucidate their potential roles in disease progression. Additionally, the Cytohubba plugin (v0.1) was employed to identify hub nodes within each module.

Beneficial microorganism isolation

Fresh soil samples (5 g) were suspended in 45 mL of sterile distilled water and agitated at 180 rpm for one hour at 28℃ to generate a homogeneous soil suspension. The suspension was serially diluted to 10–1, 10–2, and 10–3 by transferring 100 μL into 900 μL of sterile water at each step. Aliquots of 100 μL from each dilution were spread evenly onto isolation agar plates. The plates were inverted and incubated at 28℃for 7 days. Three independent biological replicates were performed. After incubation, colonies displaying distinct morphological characteristics were carefully picked, streaked onto fresh LB agar plates, and subcultured repeatedly until pure isolates were obtained. For molecular identification, genomic DNA was extracted from each isolate, and the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using universal primers (27F and 1493R). PCR products were verified by gel electrophoresis and subsequently sequenced by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The sequences were aligned against the EZBioCloud database (https://www.ezbiocloud.net/) to determine taxonomic affiliation at the genus and species levels. The Foc TR4 was used as the target strain, and the isolated strains with antagonistic activity were detected using a plate confrontation assay [30].

Integrated multi-omics analysis of metabolites and microbial communities

Pearson correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the association between rhizosphere microbial communities and banana root metabolites after treatment with strain WHL7. Significant ASVs and metabolites were identified based on correlation coefficients and p-values. Key metabolites contributing to the structural changes of the rhizosphere microbial community were further highlighted.

Biocontrol efficiency evaluation of key metabolites against Foc TR4

The biocontrol efficacy of G3P was evaluated using both in vitro and in plate assays (Fig. S12). For the in vitro assay, G3P was added to PDA medium at final concentrations of 0.75 nM, 1.5 nM, and 3 nM, respectively. The metabolite-free PDA served as the control. A 5-mm hyphal disc of Foc TR4 was placed at the center of each plate. After incubation at 28℃ for 7 days, colony diameters were measured, and inhibition rates were calculated [65]. Each treatment included three biological replicates. All experiments were independently repeated twice. A schematic overview of the G3P pot experiment, including treatment groups, application timing, and sampling strategy, was shown in Fig. S12. In the pot experiment, banana seedlings were divided into three treatment groups: (i) water-treated control (CK), (ii) Foc TR4 inoculation, and (iii) G3P pretreatment followed by Foc TR4 challenge. In the G3P treatment group, banana seedlings were pretreated with different concentrations of G3P for two weeks, and then, the roots were inoculated with GFP-tagged Foc TR4 (GFP-Foc TR4, 106 CFU g−1 soil). The CK group received only water, while the Foc TR4 group was inoculated with the pathogen without G3P pretreatment. All plants were maintained in a greenhouse at 28℃ and 60% relative humidity under natural light. Root colonization by GFP-Foc TR4 was observed using confocal microscopy (FV3000, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Rhizosphere soil samples were collected for further analysis. The GFP-Foc TR4 strain was kept in the Institute of Tropical Bioscience and Biotechnology, Chinese Academy of Tropical Agricultural Sciences (Haikou, China).

Effects of key metabolites on rhizosphere microbial community

Rhizosphere soil samples were collected from banana plants treated with different concentrations of G3P. Foc TR4-only or water treatment (CK) was used as the control. For each treatment, rhizosphere soils were collected from ten individual banana plants and thoroughly homogenized to minimize plant-to-plant variation. The pooled soil sample was then equally divided into three subsamples, which were treated as biological replicates for downstream DNA extraction, 16S rRNA gene sequencing, and qPCR analyses. The bacterial 16S rRNA gene (V3-V4 region) was amplified and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq platform (Magigene, Guangzhou, China). Microbial community composition and diversity were analyzed with standardized bioinformatic pipelines. The results were subsequently compared with those from WHL7 treatment to evaluate relative effects on the rhizosphere microbiome.

Detection of Foc TR4 abundance in banana rhizosphere soil

Rhizosphere soil samples were collected from banana plants subjected to different treatments. The fungal abundance of Foc TR4 was quantified by qPCR (Table S8) [66]. A standard curve was generated using tenfold serial dilutions of Foc TR4 genomic DNA, ranging from 108 to 104 copies per reaction. Soil DNA was extracted with the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek Inc., Norcross, GA, USA). qPCR was performed on a LightCycler® 96 System (Roche, Basel, Switzerland), with per sample amplified in five technical replicates. The abundance of Foc TR4 was expressed as the log10-transformed copy numbers per gram of soil.

Key metabolites driving beneficial microbial enrichment in banana rhizosphere

To investigate how banana root metabolites recruit beneficial microorganisms in the rhizosphere, we performed both bacterial growth and taxis assays. For the growth assay, G3P was supplemented into the LB liquid medium at the indicated concentrations. Beneficial bacterial strains were inoculated and cultured at 28℃ for 24 h. Bacterial growth was assessed by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) using a spectrophotometer. For the taxis assay, a syringe containing LB medium supplemented with G3P served as the attractant chamber. The syringe was immersed in a suspension of beneficial bacteria and incubated at 28℃ for 30 min without agitation. After incubation, residual liquid in the syringe needle was carefully removed, and the syringe was rinsed three times with sterile water to remove external contaminants. The remaining solution inside the syringe barrel was then spread onto LB agar plates and incubated in the dark at 28℃ for 24 h. Colony-forming units were subsequently counted to quantify the taxis responses of beneficial bacteria toward the tested metabolites. Each treatment included twelve biological replicates. All experiments were independently repeated twice.

Statistical analysis

Data were statistically analyzed using one-way ANOVA in SPSS 22.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). The Duncan’s multiple range test was carried out to determine the statistical significance (P < 0.05).
