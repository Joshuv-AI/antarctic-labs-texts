Ethereal AI: Infrared Spectra of Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons with Machine Learning DFT Scaling Factors
Authors: Reese Bos, Matthew King, Anthony J. Calangian, Kylie A. Davin, Sabrina McCraley, Riley A. Putnam, José Manjarrés, Samuel J. Stoneburner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are of interest in astrochemistry and are studied through infrared (IR) signals from space. Deconvoluting the observed IR of PAHs often relies on computational chemistry for references. Current practice typically uses IR predicted by density functional theory adjusted with scaling factors to account for anharmonicity and basis set limitations. We present a machine learning approach to scaling factors that adjusts each predicted IR frequency individually based on computed frequencies, intensities, reduced masses, and force constants. Our machine learning model is able to obtain a mean absolute error (MAE) and maximum error of 5 and 13 cm–1, respectively, a drastic improvement over a current-practice scaling factor approach with an MAE and maximum error of 10 and 23 cm–1, respectively. Using machine learning to determine scaling of DFT-generated IR spectra shows promise for enabling higher quality predictions on larger systems than those that have previously been possible.

Introduction

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) have been of significant interest in astrochemistry since they were determined to be responsible for many of the prominent infrared (IR) emission features observed in the IR spectra of interstellar space. − PAHs are now believed to account for about 10% of the carbon in the universe, second only to carbon dioxide. Understanding whether PAHs play a role in the formation of other organic molecules, especially prebiotic compounds, is an ongoing discussion in the astrochemistry community. IR signatures from PAHs are also used in analyzing star-forming regions of space, , the interstellar medium, and galactic evolution. − PAHs have even been proposed as possible technosignatures, i.e., observable signs of extraterrestrial technology.

Understanding PAHs in the universe requires interpreting IR data from telescopes and deconvoluting overlapping spectra from multiple chemical species, which motivated the development of the NASA Ames PAH Infrared Spectroscopic Database (PAHdb). − The PAHdb includes 84 experimental IR spectra taken in argon matrices designed to imitate the low temperatures and pressures of the interstellar medium, but there are many additional PAH species that are not readily accessible on Earth. The PAHdb therefore also includes over 4000 theoretical IR spectra predicted with density functional theory (DFT). The PAHdb has been employed in many astrochemical studies, including to identify PAHs in a comet in our solar system’s Oort cloud, in the atmosphere of Titan, and to interpret data from many other regions in space obtained with the Spitzer Space Telescope , and the James Webb Space Telescope. ,,,

IR spectra are challenging to simulate with “spectroscopic accuracy” (errors below 1 cm–1). A more complete accounting of the difficulties than we can address here can be found in a recent review by Fortenberry, but there are several challenges worth noting here: (1) Basis set incompleteness, in which errors arise from using truncations of a hypothetical infinite basis set, is typically addressed by extrapolation to the complete-basis-set (CBS) limit, but this is practical only for small systems. (2) Practical quantum chemical approaches require approximations to the full solution of the Schrodinger equation, which has been solved analytically only for the hydrogen atom. In wave function theory, the computational “gold standard” of the coupled cluster with singles, doubles, and perturbative triples [CCSD­(T)] is the least severe approximation in wide use, but it is computationally intensive and is not tractable for large systems. In the popular DFT, all functionals in use can be viewed as approximations to the unknown “exact” functional. There are hundreds of approximate functionals available, and selection of the appropriate functional is a matter of ongoing discussion − that is beyond the scope of this work. (3) More specific to vibrational spectroscopy, DFT and many wave function methods usually rely on the harmonic oscillator approximation for vibrational calculations, but real chemical bonds have an anharmonic potential with an increasingly shallow slope as the bond is stretched toward breaking. For molecules with only a few atoms, it is possible to apply advanced methods like VPT2 that explicitly include anharmonicity and can be extrapolated to the complete basis set limit, but that approach is not computationally feasible with any but the smallest of PAHs. For larger systems, such as those contained in the PAHdb, the common practice is to use DFT with a finite basis set, which systematically overestimates the IR frequencies. The computed frequencies are then adjusted by multiplication by a “scaling factor”, which is typically a single value for a given combination of the DFT functional and the basis set determined by benchmarking known systems. Scaling factors are primarily intended to correct for anharmonicity, but because they are derived for specific combinations of the method and basis set, they also mitigate the other errors noted here. This is of particular importance in the context of PAHs because most astrochemical work and most methodological benchmarking are in consideration of much smaller systems, but with PAHs, the scale of the systems in question often precludes many best- or preferred-practice methods or basis sets. For example, the 2007 study by Merrick, Moran, and Radom studied systems up to 10 atoms, and the recent VIBFREQ1295 database contains systems up to 14 atoms. Conversely, naphthalene, the simplest PAH, contains 18 atoms, and there are many systems in PAHdb with hundreds of atoms. Therefore, the only feasible option is to derive scaling factors for a specific functional and basis set that can be applied to all systems under consideration, which is what Bauschlicher et al. have done.

The most common practice with scaling factors is to find a single scalar value that minimizes disagreement between scaled computed frequencies for a large number of reference compounds, , sometimes supplemented by high-quality theory such as some variant of CCSD­(T). The derived scaling factor can then be used by the community to correct calculations of IR spectra using the same computational method and the same basis set as was used in the original minimization. The well-known 1996 article by Scott and Radom provides a detailed mathematical description of the minimization. The most common approach has focused on minimizing root-mean-square error (RMSE), , although Zapata Trujillo and McKemmish have called for using the median value instead. , We present both within this work without making judgments about which statistical measures will be most relevant for the reader. Bauschlicher et al. used a variation on this scheme in the most recent version of the PAHdb because of their specific interest in reducing the maximum error, even at the expense of increasing small errors, and the maximum error is similarly highlighted in this work. The PAHdb adds a level of nuance to the traditional single scaling factor approach by applying three scaling factors rather than one, with the scaling factors being assigned to specific frequency ranges. Bauschlicher and Langhoff have been experimenting with multiple scaling factors for PAHs as early as 1997 in efforts to minimize error cations as well as neutral molecules, both of which are present in the PAHdb, but more recently, multiple scaling factors have also been proposed by Zapata Trujullo and McKemmish in a general scheme for the vibrational spectroscopy of other compounds. , The three scale factors currently in use in the PAHdb were derived for several different basis sets for use with the B3LYP functional − based on a gas-phase spectrum of naphthalene, the simplest and one of the best-understood PAHs. (The authors note that this using gas-phase data as their reference is a departure from their previous work using argon-matrix data but indicate the effects are limited.) With the 4-31G basis set, − which is used for much of the PAHdb, the scaling factors are 0.956 for 0 to 1111.1 cm–1, 0.952 for 1111.1 to 2500 cm–1, and 0.960 for frequencies above 2500 cm–1. This three-factor scheme has been noted to significantly improve accuracy for PAHs and has qualitative similarities to recommendations from more recent benchmarking work on smaller systems. With their three-factor scheme, Bauschlicher et al. reported a mean average error (MAE) of 7.8 cm–1 and a maximum error of 26.9 cm–1 when using B3LYP/4-31G. They also reported using the BP86 functional , and/or the 6-31G* basis set ,,, in cases where their preferred method of B3LYP/4-31G gave large errors.

As is often the case in computational chemistry, there is a tension between the need for more accurate calculations and the need to include larger systems. Recent work toward advancing the computational description of PAHs has generally addressed one side or the other of this tension: either proposing inexpensive methods to expand the scope to larger molecules − or emphasizing anharmonicity more explicitly, , which requires computationally expensive approaches. , Attempting to follow modern recommendations ,,, to replace B3LYP with more advanced double-hybrid functionals or the 4-31G basis set with an augmented-polarized-split-valence basis set would increase computational cost without resolving the underlying tension. Such a strategy would work against the stated goal of PAHdb authors Bauschlicher et al. of including larger and more varied PAHs. Furthermore, changing the functional or basis set would entail a significant reimagining of PAHdb, which is beyond the scope of this work. Our hypothesis was that we could instead find a “middle way” and improve the accuracy of the relatively B3LYP/4-31G already in use by the PAHdb by using machine learning to derive case-specific scaling factors for each frequency individually.

Machine learning (sometimes referred to as “AI”) has been attempted in IR spectra as an alternative to performing any calculations, , including for the IR spectra of PAHs. − Machine learning has also been employed to predict new hypothetical species in astrochemistry. In this work, we apply machine learning to obtain uniquely tailored scaling factors as corrections to DFT results. Rather than replacing the quantum chemical calculations outright, we propose using machine learning to make the existing scaling factor approach more effective. The traditional scaling factor has one input: the computed frequencies. The approach presented here instead uses several inputs: computed frequencies, intensities, force constants, and reduced masses. This work is distinct from the efforts of Kerkeni et al., in which a general “Frequency Scaling Function” was developed for semiempirical methods, but in which DFT calculations were scaled using the same three scaling factors previously derived by Bauschlicher et al. for the PAHdb. Prior efforts by Bauschlicher et al. in ref (and citations within) to improve accuracy included going from one scaling factor to two, and then three, with still more scaling factors being needed in the future noted as a strong possibility. Here, that concept is extended to its ultimate limit by employing machine learning to identify unique scaling factors for each computational band. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first use of machine learning for the computation of IR scaling factors.

Rather than minimizing error with one-three scalar parameters that are applied to large regions of the IR spectrum, as has been common practice, here we are proposing a system in which each calculated frequency is adjusted based on several input parameters that are readily available after a vibrational calculation. The goal of this study was to improve the quality of IR predictions for PAHs in the context of PAHdb with minimal disruption to the existing framework, but any questions of broader application or generalizability are beyond the scope of this work. With our individualized scaling factor approach, we hope to enable the study of larger PAHs with quantum mechanical methods, mitigate the need for untenably expensive anharmonicity-explicit methods, and simplify future work by reducing the need for multiple DFT functionals and basis sets on comparable systems.

However, relative intensities are useful as an input value because they can favorably skew the model toward the most intense lines, which naturally are what will be most readily observed in astronomical data.

The “NIST scaling” was calculated based on the NIST Computational Chemistry Comparison and Benchmark DataBase (CCCBDB). The CCCBDB does not include a recommendation for B3LYP/4-31G, so the 0.962 scaling factor for B3LYP/6-31G was used. Of particular note in Table is that all error assessments are smallest for the SVR except for the median, which is only slightly smaller for the NIST scaling factor than for the SVR. Especially important is that the maximum error for the SVR is approximately 44 and 56% of the maximum error obtained for the same data using NIST scaling and PAHdb scaling, respectively. Reducing the maximum error in this way can reduce uncertainty in assigning astronomically observed bands and has been a stated priority of past work on the PAHdb. We also note that the percentage of outliers, calculated as the percentage of entries with absolute errors above 10 cm–1, is a particular strength of SVR, with less than half of the outliers of NIST and less than one-sixth of the outliers of the PAHdb scaling. This implies that the reduced maximum error of SVR reflects the distribution of error as a whole.

It is worth noting that the primary limitation of this study was the availability of experimental reference data under the appropriate conditions. These data are biased toward small PAHs, and therefore, our confidence is stronger for small PAHs than for larger systems. The experimental data are also dominated by planar systems, although there were a small number of slightly nonplanar systems, as well. Additional detail is in the Supporting Information. Should significant additional experimental work become available, it might be possible to retrain the SVR model in pursuit of increased confidence in the extension of our model to larger PAHs and PAHs of more diverse geometries.

Conclusion

The novel use of machine learning to determine individualized scaling for DFT-predicted IR is shown to be effective in reducing error for PAHs calculated with B3LYP/4-31G. The mean absolute error using the current PAHdb scaling factor scheme is 10.41 cm–1, while the SVR model results presented here yield an MAE of 5.07 cm–1. The maximum error, which is of particular importance, is reduced from 23.49 to 13.17 cm–1 in the current PAHdb scheme vs the SVR scheme, respectively. Comparisons within specific wavenumber ranges show that the machine learning prediction is especially advantageous for the C–H stretching region. This work could serve to enable consideration of larger compounds than those currently in the PAHdb by making B3LYP/4-31G feasible for a wider range of systems. Almost all of the 4000+ compounds in the theoretical section of PAHdb have now been recalculated using this machine learning protocol, and these data are provided in the Supporting Information.

The work presented here includes several innovations, including the application of machine learning to deriving scaling factors, the individualization of scaling factors, and the inclusion of reduced masses and force constants in the derivation of the scaling factors. In our highly specific application and context, we achieved a maximum error (13.17 cm–1) well below a generalized limit of traditional single scaling factor approaches (25 cm–1). We make no claims based on the present data as to whether our approach can be extended to systems beyond PAHs or to methodological approaches beyond B3LYP/4-31G. However, the generalizability of this model is an open question for future exploration. Future work includes exploring this approach with a more general set of chemical systems and considering the functional and basis set as input variables for a more robust machine learning model. We anticipate the recent work of Zapata Trujillo and McKemmish on model chemistries in harmonic frequency calculations to be a useful guidepost in such further development. Work in this area would need to be done to determine if machine learning could be successfully applied to scaling factors for DFT predictions of IR spectra in a wide range of chemical contexts beyond PAHs, or even beyond astrochemistry.

Methods

Experimental bands were obtained from the NASA Ames Research Center PAH IR Spectral Database (PAHdb). ,,− More details about the processing and specific identities of the PAHs are provided in the Supporting Information. All DFT calculations were performed in Gaussian 16. GaussView6 was used for visualizations. In keeping with the vast majority of the work in the PAHdb, the B3LYP functional − and the 4-31G basis set − were used. Computational work began with B3LYP/4-31G geometry optimizations and frequency calculations using initial geometries from the computational entries in the PAHdb. Experimental and computational bands were matched automatically using a custom Python script up to 1590 cm–1, above which bands were matched manually. Further details are provided in the Supporting Information.

Several machine learning regression models were trained by using 465 correlated instances of computed features and their corresponding experimental bands from over 80 PAHs. The set of computed features comprises the frequency, intensity, relative intensity, reduced mass, and force constant. We divided the data set by setting 80% (372 instances) for training the models and the remaining 20% (93 instances) to test them. Each of these partitions comes from random sampling all portions of the spectrum and from all molecules in our data set. Before training our models, we standardized the features in both the training and testing partitions. This standardization process guarantees that each feature has zero mean and unit variance, reducing the effects of model bias toward features with larger values.

All machine learning work was conducted with NumPy, Pandas, and scikit-learn using Google Colab. ChatGPT and Google Gemini were used to assist coding, but no AI tool was used in the writing of this manuscript. A variety of machine learning models were trained and tested, including SVR, Elastic Net regression, Lasso regression, Ridge regression, Gradient Boosting, MLP regression, k-NN, Random Forest regression, and XGBoost, all of which are implemented within scikit-learn.
