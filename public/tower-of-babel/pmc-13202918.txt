Mitochondrial dysfunction in neonatal brain injury: from molecular mechanisms to therapeutic interventions
Authors: Chengqian Teng, Jianjie Wei, Yixiao Zhu, Andi Chen, Xiaohui Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeonatal brain injury, including hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy, preterm brain injury, and neonatal infectious brain injury, remains a major cause of death and long-term neurodevelopmental disability worldwide. The immature brain is highly dependent on oxidative metabolism yet particularly vulnerable to energy failure and oxidative stress, placing mitochondria at the core of injury cascades. By integrating disturbances in energy production, redox balance, calcium homeostasis, and cell death signaling, mitochondrial dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a unifying driver of diverse neonatal brain injury phenotypes.Main bodyThis narrative review synthesizes current knowledge on the main clinical forms of neonatal brain injury and their developmental context, alongside an overview of mitochondrial physiology in neural cells, including the regulation of bioenergetics, reactive oxygen species, calcium signaling, mitochondrial dynamics, and inter‑organelle communication. It critically examines how mitochondrial dysfunction contributes to injury across hypoxic–ischemic, preterm, and infectious or inflammatory insults, emphasizing links between impaired oxidative phosphorylation, excessive oxidative and nitrosative stress, calcium overload with pathological opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, activation of apoptosis and regulated necrosis, disrupted mitochondrial fusion–fission balance and biogenesis, and defective mitophagy and mitochondrial quality control. These mitochondrial disturbances precipitate acute neuronal and oligodendroglial injury and hinder the long-term maturation and connectivity of neural circuits. Finally, we review emerging mitochondria‑targeted neuroprotective strategies, focusing on approaches that enhance mitochondrial biogenesis, reduce mitochondrial oxidative stress, and target mitochondrial dynamics to restore mitochondrial homeostasis and improve cellular resilience in the immature brain.ConclusionBy linking specific patterns of mitochondrial dysfunction to distinct forms and stages of neonatal brain injury, this review provides a mechanistic framework for identifying high‑risk infants, refining pathophysiological understanding, and guiding the rational development of mitochondria‑targeted interventions aimed at improving neurological outcomes in vulnerable newborns.

Introduction

Neonatal brain injury refers to neurological dysfunction arising in the early postnatal period and is caused by diverse prenatal, perinatal, and postnatal pathological factors, including hypoxia, ischemia, infection, and metabolic disorders [1–3]. Globally, moderate to severe neonatal brain injury occurs in approximately 2–3 per 1,000 live births and is associated with a high risk of cerebral palsy, intellectual disability, epilepsy, and other severe long‑term sequelae [4, 5]. These adverse outcomes substantially impair the quality of life of affected children and impose a considerable burden on their families and society [6]. However, current treatments for neonatal brain injury are largely limited to symptomatic management, predominantly therapeutic hypothermia and rehabilitation‑based interventions [7–10]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop novel, mechanism‑based strategies for the treatment of neonatal brain injury.

Mitochondria are multifunctional organelles that support a wide range of cellular processes and are widely recognized as the energy powerhouses of the cell [11, 12]. They primarily generate adenosine triphosphate(ATP) through oxidative phosphorylation(OXPHOS), which is essential for cellular energy homeostasis and survival [13, 14]. The functional state of mitochondria therefore has a profound impact on cell survival and death and is fundamental to the maintenance of cellular health [15]. In addition, mitochondria play key roles in regulating the cell cycle, controlling apoptosis, and maintaining Ca2 + homeostasis, among other critical physiological processes [16, 17].

Recent studies have established a close association between mitochondrial dysfunction and neonatal brain injury [18–20]. The high energy demands of the developing brain require a continuous and adequate mitochondrial energy supply, and mitochondrial dysfunction may result in inadequate energy delivery to neurons, thereby compromising their function [20]. Furthermore, mitochondrial dysfunction promotes oxidative stress, leading to the accumulation of reactive oxygen species(ROS) that damage cellular membranes, proteins, and DNA, and thereby aggravate neuronal injury [21]. Throughout the pathological evolution of neonatal brain injury, mitochondrial dysfunction interacts with inflammatory responses, apoptosis, neurodegeneration, and other interrelated processes, collectively contributing to the progression of brain damage (see Fig. 1) [22, 23]. This review provides an overview of the fundamental pathophysiological mechanisms underlying neonatal brain injury, with a particular focus on the central role of mitochondrial dysfunction in its initiation and progression. The review also discusses mitochondria targeted therapeutic interventions as promising strategies for the treatment of neonatal brain injury and for improving long term neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Common types and pathological mechanisms of neonatal brain injury

Neonatal brain injury encompasses various entities, including white matter injury (WMI), hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE), intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), traumatic brain injury (TBI), periventricular leukomalacia (PVL), perinatal stroke, perinatal asphyxia, neonatal infectious brain injury (NIBI), and metabolic or toxic brain injuries [25, 26]. HIE, preterm brain injury (PBI), and NIBI are among the most prevalent and clinically significant forms of neonatal brain injury [3, 27–31] (Table 1). HIE

Neonatal HIE is a leading cause of neurological injury and mortality among infants worldwide [32, 33]. The incidence of HIE varies markedly by region. In high income countries with advanced perinatal care, the incidence of moderate to severe HIE among term live births is approximately 1–3 cases per 1000 live births [34, 35], whereas in resource limited settings the incidence is considerably higher [33]. The mortality rate for infants with moderate to severe HIE ranges from 25% to 60%, and approximately 60% of survivors develop persistent neurological sequelae, including cerebral palsy, cognitive impairment, and epilepsy, which constitute a major cause of long term childhood disability [36–38].

The pathophysiology of HIE involves a cascade of tightly interconnected events initiated most commonly by perinatal asphyxia, which in clinical practice is frequently related to umbilical cord compression, such as a nuchal cord, or placental abruption [39–41]. The primary insult leads to a reduction in cerebral blood flow and a consequent decrease in the delivery of substrates such as oxygen and glucose to neural tissue, together with mitochondrial dysfunction that severely impairs mitochondrial ATP production [38]. The resulting energy crisis, combined with direct ischemic injury, activates multiple cell death pathways that culminate in apoptosis and necrosis of vulnerable neurons and glial cells [42]. At the molecular level, these processes are characterized by profound energy depletion and a marked reduction in intracellular ATP levels [43]. During reperfusion, and even under ongoing hypoxic conditions, dysfunctional mitochondria generate excessive ROS, leading to severe oxidative stress [44]. Oxidative damage, in combination with bioenergetic failure, activates caspase dependent apoptotic pathways, thereby committing cells to programmed death [45]. In parallel, ATP depletion impairs the activity of ion pumps, particularly the Na+/K+ ATPase, resulting in intracellular sodium accumulation, osmotic water influx, and cytotoxic cell swelling [46].

PBI

PBI is characterized by structural and functional impairments in the developing brain of infants born before 37 weeks of gestation and represents a major global health burden, accounting for a substantial proportion of neonatal deaths [47, 48]. This heterogeneous condition arises from the interaction between the intrinsic vulnerabilities associated with preterm birth and extrinsic factors such as perinatal hypoxia ischemia, infection, and intracranial hemorrhage [49, 50]. PBI is traditionally classified into classical and non classical types. The classical type mainly includes WMI and IVH, whereas non classical types predominantly involve injuries affecting the cerebral cortex and cerebellum. Clinically, PBI may initially manifest as acute neurological dysfunction, such as seizures, respiratory instability, or altered tone, and frequently evolves into long term neurodevelopmental disabilities, including cerebral palsy, cognitive deficits, and autism spectrum disorders [51, 52]. Of particular concern, even mild metabolic disturbances during this critical window of brain development may induce lasting epigenetic modifications with profound effects on neuroplasticity and behavior [53].

The pathogenesis of PBI is largely driven by the interplay between inflammation and hypoxia, which together disrupt normal developmental processes in the immature brain [54]. Proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF‑α, IL‑1β, and IL‑6 are released by activated microglia and contribute directly to the injury and loss of oligodendrocyte precursor cells(OPCs) [68]. These cytokines also promote mitochondrial fission through activation of dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1), thereby impairing OXPHOS and further compromising cellular energy metabolism [69, 70]. In addition, hypoxic conditions upregulate cyclooxygenase 2 (COX‑2), resulting in increased production of prostaglandin E2 and amplification of glutamate mediated excitotoxicity [71]. This vicious cycle depletes ATP stores and forces cells to rely on anaerobic glycolysis, leading to lactate accumulation and creating a microenvironment that favors oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis [72].

NIBI

NIBI is an inflammatory condition caused by invasion of the central nervous system by bacteria, viruses, fungi, or other pathogens, and typically occurs during the perinatal period and within the first 28 days after birth [55]. Common pathogens include bacteria such as Escherichia coli, group B Streptococcus (GBS), and Listeria monocytogenes, as well as viruses such as cytomegalovirus (CMV). These pathogens may be transmitted vertically from mother to fetus, acquired during delivery, or contracted as hospital acquired infections [56, 57]. Clinically, NIBI most often presents as encephalitis, meningitis, or brain abscess, with manifestations including fever, seizures, altered consciousness, and signs of raised intracranial pressure [57, 58]. Severe cases are frequently complicated by long term neurological sequelae such as cerebral palsy, intellectual disability, and epilepsy [59, 60].

The precise mechanisms underlying NIBI remain incompletely understood. However, the primary pathological mechanisms include increased production of inflammatory mediators such as cytokines, prostaglandins, and ROS, as well as damage to immature oligodendrocyte progenitors [61, 62]. Blood-brain barrier(BBB) dysfunction is characterized by increased permeability associated with disruption of tight junction proteins and upregulation of inflammatory cytokines, and is closely linked to worse clinical outcomes [63–65]. Once pathogens or their components breach the BBB, they activate microglia and astrocytes, leading to the release of proinflammatory mediators including TNF‑α, IL‑6, and IL‑1β [66]. These inflammatory factors exacerbate neuroinflammation and exert deleterious effects on oligodendrocytes and neurons [61]. In addition, excessive production of ROS and Ca2 + overload can trigger neuronal apoptosis and further aggravate tissue injury [67]. Infection induced immune dysregulation may thereby amplify secondary brain damage. Postmortem studies have identified characteristic lesions in the cerebral tissue of infants with infectious neuroinflammatory brain injury, including subcortical white matter necrosis, neuronal loss in the hippocampus, and gliosis, which are strongly associated with persistent neurodevelopmental impairment [73].

The role of mitochondria in neural development

Mitochondria are essential organelles in neurons and play a central role in neurodevelopment. They provide the energy required by cells, maintain Ca2 + homeostasis, and regulate ROS levels to support normal neuronal function [74]. In addition, mitochondria participate in cell signaling and metabolic regulation during neurodevelopment, thereby sustaining normal development and function of the nervous system and supporting neuronal survival and functional maintenance [75]. Mitochondria exert multiple critical functions in neural development, including energy metabolism, Ca2 + homeostasis, regulation of ROS, control of apoptosis, mitochondrial dynamics and quality control, and intercellular signaling (see Fig. 3). Energy supply

Neurodevelopment is a complex and highly energy demanding process that encompasses the proliferation, differentiation, migration, and synapse formation of neurons. As the primary intracellular source of ATP, mitochondria generate large amounts of ATP through OXPHOS to meet the high metabolic demand of developing neurons [76]. During critical stages of neurodevelopment, including the differentiation of neural stem cells into mature neurons, the growth of axons and dendrites, the establishment and maintenance of synapses, and activity dependent plasticity that underlies learning and memory, efficient mitochondrial energy provision is indispensable [77]. Mitochondria are not uniformly distributed within neurons but exhibit pronounced enrichment in metabolically active compartments, such as presynaptic terminals and dendritic spines, to satisfy local energy requirements [78, 79].

The generation of mitochondrial ATP depends on the tight coupling of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and the electron transport chain (ETC) during OXPHOS [80]. Pyruvate, mainly derived from glucose, is transported into mitochondria and converted to acetyl coenzyme A, which enters the TCA cycle. This cycle produces reducing equivalents, primarily NADH and FADH2, that donate electrons to the ETC [81]. Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10), an endogenously synthesized lipid cofactor located in the inner mitochondrial membrane, functions as a mobile electron carrier within the ETC and is essential for establishing the proton gradient that drives ATP synthesis [82]. Beyond its role in ATP production, CoQ10 contributes to cellular redox balance and antioxidant defense, functions that are particularly important in the metabolically active and highly oxidative environment of the developing neonatal brain [83–85].

Ca2 + ion homeostasis

Ca2 + ions act as critical second messengers in neurons and participate in diverse physiological processes, including neurotransmitter release and the regulation of gene expression [86]. Precise control of Ca2 + signaling is essential for neuronal differentiation, the regulation of synaptic plasticity, and the formation of neural circuits [87]. Mitochondria play a key role in maintaining Ca2 + homeostasis during neural development through the coordinated action of ion channels, metabolic sensors, and signaling networks [88]. Mitochondrial Ca2 + homeostasis depends on channels located on both the inner and outer mitochondrial membranes, such as the mitochondrial Ca2 + uniporter (MCU) complex and the voltage dependent anion channel (VDAC), which together mediate Ca2 + uptake and efflux [89].

The MCU complex consists of MCU pore forming subunits and regulatory proteins including MICU1, MICU2, and EMRE, and constitutes the principal pathway for Ca2 + entry into the mitochondrial matrix [90]. MICU1 acts as a Ca2 + sensitive gatekeeper that inhibits MCU opening at resting cytosolic Ca2 + concentrations, thereby preventing Ca2 + leakage when Ca2 + levels are below approximately 300 nM [91]. When cytosolic Ca2 + concentrations exceed about 1 μM, conformational changes in MICU1 relieve this inhibition and permit rapid mitochondrial Ca2 + uptake [92]. In the neonatal brain, mitochondrial Ca2 + buffering capacity is not fully mature. Developmental differences in MCU and MICU expression, together with relatively reduced Na+/Ca2+ exchanger (NCLX) mediated efflux, may lower the threshold for mitochondrial Ca2 + overload under excitotoxic stress and render neonatal neurons more vulnerable to Ca2 + dysregulation after hypoxic ischemic injury [93–95].

Ca2 + efflux from mitochondria occurs primarily through NCLX on the inner mitochondrial membrane, which exports Ca2 + in exchange for sodium ions and is driven by the mitochondrial membrane potential. Impaired NCLX function can lead to mitochondrial Ca2 + overload, membrane depolarization, and a reduction in Ca2 + transient amplitude, thereby compromising synaptic transmission [96, 97]. In developing neurons, the relative immaturity of NCLX regulation may further limit mitochondrial Ca2 + extrusion and amplify excitotoxic injury cascades [98, 99]. VDAC, located in the outer mitochondrial membrane, regulates Ca2 + flux across the outer membrane by switching between open and closed states. Mitochondria ER interactions are also crucial for Ca2 + regulation. VDAC interacts with inositol 1,4,5 trisphosphate receptors (IP3Rs) on the ER via the chaperone glucose regulated protein 75 (Grp75), facilitating direct Ca2 + transfer from the ER to mitochondria and forming local Ca2 + microdomains that enhance the efficiency of mitochondrial Ca2 + signaling [100, 101]. During early brain development, mitochondria associated membranes (MAMs) undergo dynamic structural remodeling, which may strengthen ER–mitochondria Ca2 + coupling and increase susceptibility to microdomain mediated mitochondrial Ca2 + overload [102, 103].

Among voltage-gated Ca2 + channels(VGCCs), L type calcium channels (LTCCs) activate the calmodulin–cAMP response element‑binding protein (CaM–CREB) signaling pathway and promote the expression of neurotrophic factors [104]. The developmentally predominant NR2B containing NMDA receptor subtype exhibits prolonged open times and higher Ca2 + permeability compared with NR2A containing receptors and activates TrkB–PI3K–Akt survival signaling [105, 106]. In the neonatal brain, the predominance of NR2B containing NMDA receptors facilitates activity dependent synaptic refinement but also increases the susceptibility of immature neurons to excessive Ca2 + influx under hypoxic ischemic conditions [107, 108]. The transient receptor potential melastatin (TRPM) family further contributes to Ca2 + regulation. TRPM7 participates in intracellular Ca2 + homeostasis by modulating store operated Ca2 + entry and providing Ca2 + influx required to maintain and refill ER Ca2 + stores, processes that are closely linked to ER dependent Ca2 + release [109]. TRPM2 senses ADP ribose dependent Ca2 + signals and can activate CaMKII/eNOS pathways, thereby promoting ROS production and apoptosis. Relatively low TRPM2 expression during early development may serve as a compensatory protective mechanism that limits ROS associated Ca2 + overload and prevents premature apoptosis of neural progenitor cells [110, 111].

ROS regulation

ROS are metabolic byproducts that also function as important signaling molecules in many physiological processes [112]. Excessive accumulation of ROS, however, causes oxidative stress and damages cellular structures and functions [113]. Mitochondria are both a major source of ROS and a key regulator of their levels [114]. Moderate ROS levels support normal neural development, as these molecules participate in signal transduction pathways that regulate neuronal growth, differentiation, and synaptic plasticity. Mitochondria modulate ROS production by regulating electron transport chain activity and the expression and activity of antioxidant enzymes [115]. Manganese dependent superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2) converts superoxide anions generated by complexes I and III of the electron transport chain into hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) [116, 117]. SOD2 activity increases during neural precursor differentiation and supports proper brain development [118]. H₂O₂ is subsequently detoxified by catalase (CAT) or reduced by glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPx4) using reduced glutathione (GSH), producing oxidized glutathione (GSSG) [119]. Maintenance of the GSH/GSSG ratio above a critical threshold is essential for redox homeostasis and is supported by glutathione reductase and NADPH [120].

The thioredoxin 2 (Trx2) and peroxiredoxin 3 (Prx3) system cooperates to remove H₂O₂ and preserve mitochondrial function [121, 122]. Iron homeostasis is tightly controlled by ferritin heavy chain, which sequesters iron and limits Fenton chemistry. GPx4 suppresses ferroptosis by repairing lipid peroxides; its deficiency leads to profound loss of mitochondrial membrane potential and cell death [123]. Abnormal overactivation of electron transport chain complexes markedly enhances ROS production, whereas uncoupling protein 2 (UCP2) can attenuate ROS generation by modulating mitochondrial substrate oxidation, glycolysis, and Ca2 + handling [124, 125]. Furthermore, mitochondria regulate ROS production and clearance through dynamic changes in morphology, such as fission and fusion [126].

Regulation of apoptosis

During neural development, mitochondria play a crucial role in regulating apoptosis, thereby eliminating excess neurons and synapses and optimizing the structure and function of neural networks [76]. The Bcl-2 family of proteins modulates mitochondrial outer membrane permeability and exerts a decisive influence on cell fate. Disruption of this regulatory system may lead to neurodevelopmental disorders. Excessive neuronal apoptosis contributes to neurodegenerative diseases, whereas insufficient apoptosis can favor tumorigenesis [127]. Regulation of mitochondrial apoptosis is also closely linked to synaptic plasticity and thus affects learning and memory [87].

Two principal pathways control apoptosis: the intrinsic pathway (mitochondrial pathway) and the extrinsic pathway (death receptor pathway) [42]. The mitochondrial pathway is a central component of programmed cell death. It is governed by the balance between pro apoptotic and anti apoptotic Bcl-2 family proteins. Pro apoptotic members such as Bax and Bak oligomerize in the outer mitochondrial membrane and form pores that permit the release of cytochrome c into the cytosol. Cytochrome c then binds to apoptotic protease activating factor 1 (Apaf 1) and ATP or dATP to form the apoptosome, which activates caspase 9 and initiates a caspase cascade culminating in caspase 3 activation [128–130]. Collapse of the mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) and opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) further disrupt energy metabolism and aggravate oxidative stress [131, 132]. The mitochondrial release of second mitochondria derived activator of caspases (Smac/DIABLO) antagonizes inhibitors of apoptosis proteins (IAPs) and thereby accelerates apoptosis [133].

In the nervous system, the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway functions not only as an execution mechanism for cell death but also as an important hub for neural development and homeostatic regulation. During embryogenesis, Bax/Bak dependent apoptosis removes excess neurons and helps maintain an appropriate neuron to glia ratio [134]. Local activation of caspase 3 can mediate non apoptotic functions that promote synaptic pruning. Mitochondria derived ROS gradients facilitate complement C1q deposition at low activity synapses and enhance microglial phagocytosis, thereby refining neural circuit connectivity [135]. With respect to synaptic plasticity, phosphorylation dependent modulation of Bcl-xL regulates mitochondrial ATP supply and supports efficient synaptic vesicle cycling [136]. During tissue repair and immune regulation, sublethal cytochrome c release activates the ataxia‑telangiectasia mutated (ATM)–checkpoint kinase 2 (Chk2) pathway and enhances DNA repair capacity [137, 138]. Thus, mitochondrial apoptosis pathways play multifaceted roles in neural development, functional regulation, and immune defense and provide potential targets for the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases.

Mitochondrial dynamics and renewal

Mitochondrial dynamics denotes the balance between mitochondrial fission and fusion, whereas mitochondrial quality control encompasses the integrated processes of fission–fusion remodeling, mitophagy, and mitochondrial biogenesis. Together, these processes constitute a core regulatory network that maintains cellular energy homeostasis and critically influences cell fate during neural development. Mitochria continuously remodel their morphology and spatial distribution through coordinated fission and fusion, whereas mitophagy selectively eliminates dysfunctional organelles. The coordinated activity of this quality‑control machinery is essential for neural stem‑cell proliferation, neuronal migration, and synaptic plasticity [87, 139, 140].

Mitochondrial fission is primarily mediated by Drp1, which is recruited to the outer mitochondrial membrane by receptors such as Fis1, mitochondrial fission factor (MFF), and MiD49/50. Drp1 assembles into constriction rings that drive mitochondrial division and facilitate the segregation of damaged mitochondrial segments [141]. In neural precursor cells, increased Drp1 activity promotes mitochondrial fragmentation, supports cell division, and preserves stem cell properties. Neural stem cells lacking Dnm1l, a key regulator of mitochondrial fission, display elongated mitochondrial morphology, reduced respiratory capacity, and a metabolic shift toward glycolysis for ATP production, resulting in decreased proliferation and increased cell death [142, 143]. In contrast, mitochondrial fusion is mediated by mitofusin 1/2 (MFN1/2) on the outer membrane and optic atrophy 1 (OPA1) on the inner membrane. MFN1/2 promote outer membrane fusion, whereas OPA1 maintains inner membrane integrity and efficient respiratory chain function.

Mitophagy is mainly executed via the PTEN‑induced kinase 1(PINK1)–Parkin RBR E3 ubiquitin ligase (Parkin) pathway and the BCL2 interacting protein 3(BNIP3) and BCL2 interacting protein 3‑like(NIX/BNIP3L) receptor mediated pathway [144]. When mitochondrial membrane potential is lost, PINK1 accumulates on the outer membrane and phosphorylates Parkin, which in turn ubiquitinates outer membrane proteins such as VDAC1 and recruits autophagy receptors including p62 and optineurin (OPTN), leading to formation of autophagosomes that selectively remove damaged mitochondria [145–147]. Under hypoxic or ischemic conditions, BNIP3 binds to LC3 and can trigger excessive mitophagy, which enhances caspase 3 activity and increases neuronal apoptosis [148, 149]. Overall, dynamic regulation of mitochondrial fission, fusion, and mitophagy preserves mitochondrial morphology, distribution, and function and is critical for the proper progression of neural stem cell proliferation, neuronal migration, and synaptic plasticity.

Cell communication

Mitochria contribute to neural development not only by regulating intracellular metabolism but also by mediating communication between cells. Mitochondria modulate the metabolism and function of neighboring cells through the release of extracellular vesicles enriched in mitochondrial DNA(mtDNA), proteins, and lipids, including exosomes [150]. These vesicles can be internalized by adjacent cells, transferring mitochondrial components and signaling molecules and thereby promoting metabolic and informational exchange. This mechanism is particularly important in complex tissues such as the brain, where intercellular communication is essential for the formation and maintenance of neural networks [151].

Intracellularly, the AMP activated protein kinase (AMPK)/mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway continuously senses cellular energy status, coordinates mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy, and helps maintain energy homeostasis [152]. Concurrently, activation of nuclear factor erythroid 2 related factor 2 (Nrf2) by ROS induces the expression of antioxidant enzymes including SOD2 and confers neuroprotection against oxidative stress [153]. Nrf2 activation also modulates brain derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) expression through mTOR dependent signaling and thereby enhances synaptic function [154–156].

At the level of Ca2 + signaling, Ca2 + transfer through ER–mitochondria contact sites activates key dehydrogenases in the TCA cycle, supports ATP synthesis, and directly contributes to synaptic plasticity. Ca2 + gradients also regulate Drp1 activity, determine sites of mitochondrial division, and ensure the appropriate spatial distribution of mitochondria along growing neurites [157–159].

At the intercellular level, mitochondria can be transferred between cells via tunneling nanotubes, helping to restore metabolic function in injured or metabolically compromised cells. Gap junctions mediate the transcellular movement of ATP, NAD+, and GSH and synchronize the metabolic state of neuronal networks [160, 161]. Exosomes transport mtDNA, heat shock protein 60 (HSP60), and cytochrome c oxidase subunit IV (COXIV) and can influence synaptic gene expression through epigenetic mechanisms [162–164]. Additionally, mtDNA contained in mitochondria derived vesicles can activate Toll like receptor 9 (TLR9) and cyclic GMP–AMP synthase (cGAS) pathways, induce anti-inflammatory responses, and cooperate with exosomal GSH to regulate oxidative stress [165, 166]. In recipient cells, transferred mtDNA may integrate into the nuclear genome, modulate signaling networks, and influence cell differentiation and developmental trajectories [167, 168]. Nuclear respiratory factor 1 (NRF1), a nuclear encoded transcription factor, and peroxisome proliferator‑activated receptor gamma coactivator‑1α (PGC-1α) cooperatively drive transcriptional activation of mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) and promote mitochondrial biogenesis in neural disease models [169, 170]. Additionally, extracellular ATP directly supports high energy processes such as microtubule assembly, whereas NAD+ activates sirtuin deacetylases and optimizes axonal transport, together forming a multilayered regulatory network that precisely guides neural development [171–173].

Collectively, mitochondria exert multiple beneficial roles in neurodevelopment. They provide essential energy to support neuronal proliferation and function and participate in neuronal signal transduction and physiological regulation by maintaining Ca2 + and ROS homeostasis [77]. These mitochondrial functions are crucial for the normal maturation of developing neurons and the establishment of synaptic connectivity. Conversely, mitochondrial dysfunction can precipitate neurodevelopmental disorders and contribute to neurodegenerative diseases [174]. A comprehensive understanding of mitochondrial mechanisms in neurodevelopment is therefore fundamental for the design of effective neuroprotective strategies and the development of therapies targeting neurodegenerative conditions. Future research should focus on elucidating the fine regulation of mitochondrial function and exploiting these pathways to promote healthy neurodevelopment.

Pathological mechanisms of mitochondrial dysfunction in neonatal brain injury

In neonatal brain injury, mitochondria act as a central integrator of multiple pathological pathways. Mitochondrial dysfunction manifests through several interconnected mechanisms, including energy metabolism disorders such as impaired activity of electron transport chain complexes, reduced OXPHOS efficiency, and inadequate ATP synthesis, which together cause failure of energy dependent ion pumps and neuronal membrane depolarization [103, 175]. Oxidative stress is markedly increased because neonatal brain tissue is rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids while endogenous antioxidant defenses are immature [176]. Compromised mitochondria therefore generate excessive reactive oxygen and nitrogen species(ROS/RNS), leading to lipid peroxidation, protein and nucleic acid damage, and mtDNA mutations [177, 178]. Disruption of Ca2 + homeostasis is also prominent, with mitochondrial Ca2 + overload promoting pathological opening of the mPTP, collapse of the mitochondrial membrane potential, and further energy depletion [179–182].

In addition, mitochondrial dynamics and turnover are frequently disturbed, with imbalanced fission and fusion, reduced mitochondrial biogenesis, and impaired autophagy leading to long term accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria [183, 184]. Mitochondria also orchestrate multiple regulated cell death pathways: release of cytochrome c and other pro apoptotic factors activates caspase dependent apoptosis and contributes to necroptosis and pyroptosis, thereby exacerbating neuronal and glial loss [185–188]. Mitochondria further interact with inflammatory signaling, as damage associated molecular patterns(DAMPs) such as mtDNA activate microglia and other innate immune pathways and promote chronic neuroinflammation [189, 190]. These mechanisms are highly interdependent and form cascading injury networks. Different types of neonatal brain injury reshape these mitochondrial pathways according to their specific triggers and temporal dynamics and produce characteristic, yet substantially overlapping, spectra of functional impairment. Pathological mechanisms of mitochondrial dysfunction in different pathological stages of HIBD

Neonatal hypoxic ischemic brain damage is defined by acute brain injury resulting from perinatal hypoxia ischemia. Its pathological evolution shows distinct stage specific features and is commonly divided into four phases: primary energy failure, the latent phase, secondary energy failure, and a long term remodeling phase [20, 44]. Mitochondria, as the core of brain energy metabolism, are impaired throughout the course of HIBD and display characteristic alterations at each phase. The mechanisms active in each stage are closely interrelated and gradually intensify, thereby promoting progression and irreversibility of brain injury [19, 103]. Primary energy failure phase: acute collapse of energy metabolism

The initial phase of HIBD is defined by an acute response to the onset of hypoxia ischemia. This phase is characterized by a reduction in cerebral blood flow and oxygen delivery, resulting in primary energy failure that manifests within minutes as a rapid decline in high energy phosphate levels and an accumulation of cerebral lactate [205]. The fundamental pathological event is acute mitochondrial dysfunction, primarily reflected by a collapse of OXPHOS and a profound disruption of energy metabolism in brain tissue [258]. Hypoxia ischemia causes a marked reduction in cerebral perfusion and in the availability of substrates such as oxygen and glucose. The lack of oxygen as the terminal electron acceptor markedly inhibits electron transport chain activity at its distal components and becomes a critical bottleneck for ATP synthesis [259]. Impaired OXPHOS leads to a rapid fall in ATP production and the failure of energy dependent ion transporters, including Na+/K+ ATPase, resulting in membrane potential instability and disturbance of the intracellular milieu [260].

Failure of ion pumps induces neuronal membrane depolarization, which promotes excessive glutamate release. This, in turn, activates voltage gated Ca2 + channels and NMDA/AMPA receptors, causing a rapid influx of Ca2 + ions and triggering an excitotoxic cascade [261]. Under oxygen deprivation, cells transiently rely on anaerobic glycolysis to maintain basal ATP production. However, this pathway is inefficient and associated with lactate accumulation and proton generation, leading to intracellular acidosis that further suppresses mitochondrial enzyme activity and exacerbates the energy crisis [262]. Electron transport chain dysfunction at this early stage also promotes electron leakage and the formation of small amounts of ROS, initiating oxidative stress [261]. In parallel, ATP depletion and impaired Ca2 + pump activity reduce intracellular Ca2 + clearance, and early Ca2 + influx disrupts mitochondrial Ca2 + homeostasis, laying the foundation for subsequent Ca2 + overload, mPTP opening, and later secondary injuries [192].

The severity and duration of this primary injury phase are critical determinants of long term neurodevelopmental outcomes [263, 264]. Prospective cohort studies indicate that early clinical severity, quantified by modified Sarnat scores and electroencephalography(EEG) within the first six hours after birth, is strongly predictive of mortality and neurodevelopmental impairment at two years of age, whereas infants with mild initial signs often achieve near normal development [265, 266]. Timely correction of hypoxia and partial restoration of mitochondrial function during this phase can limit brain injury to mild or reversible dysfunction, and, with the aid of neural plasticity and compensatory mechanisms, some infants may attain relatively favorable outcomes [267]. By contrast, prolonged energy failure, delayed reoxygenation, or marked reperfusion injury tend to produce extensive, irreversible neuronal necrosis and pronounced glial responses, substantially increasing the risk of long term sequelae such as cerebral palsy, epilepsy, and cognitive and behavioral disorders [268]. Early recognition of HIBD and proactive intervention during this primary injury window are therefore essential for improving prognosis [206].

Latent phase: fragile recovery and silent injury accumulation

The second phase of HIBD, often termed the latent or partial energy recovery phase, typically occurs within 1–6 hours after HI event [269]. This phase represents a critical turning point characterized by a transient and unstable partial recovery of mitochondrial function, during which multiple potentially lethal processes continue to accumulate. Clinical and magnetic resonance spectroscopy(MRS) studies show that resuscitation and reperfusion can induce a temporary restoration of cerebral ATP and phosphocreatine levels within a few hours, yet a secondary decline in high energy phosphates commonly arises 8–24 hours later [207]. Mechanistically, although blood flow and oxygen delivery are reestablished, the electron transport chain (ETC), particularly complexes I and III, fails to fully recover normal activity. Ischemia–reperfusion models reveal persistent uncoupling of the electron transport chain and increased mitochondrial reactive oxygen species(mtROS) generation during reperfusion, indicating that electron leakage produces superoxide and drives significant oxidative stress [270].

Meanwhile, factors such as preexisting Ca2 + influx, accumulated ROS, and the “pH paradox”, in which rapid normalization or mild overshoot of brain pH occurs after reperfusion [271], act together to increase the sensitivity and likelihood of mPTP opening [272]. Thus, although ATP levels partially recover, mitochondria remain functionally unstable organelles. Persistent oxidative stress and heightened mPTP sensitivity create a pathological substrate for subsequent secondary energy failure and structural damage [207]. In parallel, animal studies demonstrate that Drp1 mediated mitochondrial fission is upregulated in the neonatal brain after hypoxia ischemia. This is initially considered an adaptive response that promotes mitochondrial network quality control by fission, segregation, and clearance of damaged segments. However, when the injury burden exceeds autophagic and repair capacity, this remodeling becomes maladaptive and contributes to excessive mitochondrial fragmentation and cell death [184, 196].

The latent phase is widely recognized as a key window for neuroprotection, and infant outcomes are closely related to the implementation of effective interventions during this period [208, 273]. Evidence from clinical trials and cohort studies indicates that initiating therapeutic hypothermia within six hours of birth significantly reduces brain injury in infants with moderate to severe HIE and improves neurodevelopmental outcomes at 18–24 months [209, 274]. Prompt correction of hypoxia in this phase, combined with neuroprotective strategies such as mild hypothermia to stabilize mitochondrial function, may limit damage to mild or reversible deficits, allowing some infants to achieve favorable long term outcomes through cerebral plasticity and compensatory processes [275, 276]. In the absence of effective intervention, accumulated risk factors, including oxidative stress and vulnerability to mPTP opening, are more likely to fully manifest. This can lead to failure of the temporarily restored energy metabolism and irreversible progression into the secondary energy failure phase, characterized by progressive mitochondrial dysfunction and delayed neuronal death [103, 193, 210].

Secondary energy failure phase: comprehensive mitochondrial collapse

The third phase of HIBD, the secondary injury or secondary energy failure phase, usually emerges within approximately 6–72 hours after the initial insult. This phase is defined by secondary energy failure driven by widespread mitochondrial dysfunction, including further reductions in energy metabolism, pronounced oxidative and nitrosative stress, severe disruption of Ca2 + homeostasis, and synergistic activation of multiple regulated cell death pathways. After a transient period of clinical stability, this phase steers brain injury toward irreversibility [44, 275]. During this phase, intracellular Ca2 + loads and ROS levels that accumulated in earlier phases are further amplified. Massive Ca2 + influx into the mitochondrial matrix occurs and interacts synergistically with ROS, maintaining the mPTP in a highly sensitive or persistently open state and causing collapse of the mitochondrial membrane potential with severe impairment of OXPHOS. This precipitates secondary energy failure, characterized by rapid ATP depletion and a transition from compensated dysfunction to irreversible structural damage [194, 205]. Concomitantly, electron transport chain complexes, particularly I and IV, are significantly impaired and become major sources of superoxide. In the context of increased inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) activity, nitric oxide (NO) production is elevated; the reaction of superoxide with NO generates peroxynitrite(ONOO−) and related radicals that promote lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation and nitration, and nuclear and mtDNA damage, thereby contributing to oxidative and nitrosative stress [195]. Mitochondrial Ca2 + overload also promotes mPTP opening and activates Ca2 + dependent proteases, lipases, and nucleases, further aggravating injury [213].

Loss of mitochondrial barrier function and increased outer membrane permeability activate multiple cell death programs. Enhanfced outer membrane permeabilization, together with conformational changes in Bcl-2 family proteins after hypoxia ischemia, facilitates the release of pro apoptotic factors such as cytochrome c into the cytosol. Cytochrome c then associates with Apaf-1 and caspase 9 to form the apoptosome and initiates a caspase 9–caspase 3 cascade. Clinical studies in infants with HIE and experimental neonatal HIBD models report elevated cerebrospinal fluid levels of cytochrome c, caspase 9, and caspase 3, consistent with activation of a classic mitochondria dependent apoptotic pathway [214, 215]. In addition, under conditions of sustained ROS and Ca2 + signaling, necroptosis mediated by the RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL axis and pyroptosis mediated by the gasdermin family are pathologically activated in HIE. Both necroptosis and pyroptosis contribute to early and delayed injury by forming membrane pores, disrupting membrane integrity, and releasing large amounts of pro inflammatory mediators and DAMPs, thereby amplifying mitochondrial damage and inflammatory tissue injury [188, 216].

Mitochondrial dynamics and quality control are also profoundly disturbed during this phase. DRP1 mediated mitochondrial fission is excessively activated, and highly fragmented mitochondria are frequently observed in neonatal hypoxia ischemia and other ischemia models. DRP1 hyperactivation and pro fission phosphorylation are closely associated with increased cytochrome c release and aggravated brain damage, whereas DRP1 inhibition mitigates cell death and improves neurological function [184, 197]. In parallel, impaired or downregulated MFN1/2 and OPA1 decrease mitochondrial network connectivity and promote fragmentation. In neonatal hypoxia ischemia models, OPA1 cleavage, accumulation of short OPA1 isoforms, and abnormal mitochondrial morphology are observed together with reduced ATP production and elevated ROS [19, 198]. Although early mitophagy can aid in the removal of damaged organelles, accumulating evidence suggests that the autophagy lysosome pathway is relatively insufficient or functionally compromised under sustained ischemia–reperfusion stress, leading to intracellular accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria and perpetuating the vicious cycle of energy failure and oxidative stress [183, 199].

The severity and duration of secondary energy failure have a major impact on long term neurodevelopment [264]. Brain MRI studies show that the extent and severity of injury in key regions such as the cortex and basal ganglia after hypoxia ischemia are strongly associated with adverse outcomes, including cerebral palsy, cognitive impairment, and childhood mortality, whereas infants with limited or near normal imaging findings are more likely to attain normal or only mildly to moderately impaired cognitive and motor development [276, 277]. During this phase, standardized mild hypothermia, careful physiological management, and multimodal neuroprotective strategies can help shorten secondary energy failure and attenuate apoptotic cascades, limiting structural injury and allowing some infants to achieve acceptable cognitive and motor outcomes [275, 278]. In contrast, inadequate seizure control, suboptimal circulatory and respiratory management, or concurrent systemic insults such as severe infection often result in extensive cystic encephalomalacia and necrosis of cortical and deep nuclear structures, with a corresponding increase in mortality and in the incidence of cerebral palsy, refractory epilepsy, and severe cognitive and behavioral disorders [279]. Consequently, the secondary injury phase is both a critical period for further deterioration of mitochondrial function and irreversible tissue damage, and a key window for prognostic stratification through imaging and neurophysiological assessment [275, 277].

Tertiary phase: persistent dysfunction and developmental impairment

The fourth phase of HIBD, the tertiary or long-term remodeling phase, typically unfolds over weeks to months and may extend into years after the initial insult. This phase is characterized by persistent mitochondrial dysfunction that shapes structural remodeling and functional plasticity in the developing brain, manifesting as chronic energy insufficiency, abnormal neural circuit development, and progressive neurological deficits [211, 212]. Mitochondrial dynamics remain disturbed beyond the acute phase, with long standing imbalances between fission and fusion. Excessive fission and impaired fusion lead to fragmented, small, and mislocalized mitochondria in neuronal somata, presynaptic terminals, and axons. These dysfunctional mitochondria fail to provide adequate ATP and local Ca2 + buffering at sites of high demand, compromising synaptic plasticity, long term potentiation (LTP), axonal transport, and ultimately the precise wiring of neural networks and higher cognitive functions [18, 200]. Concurrently, Adaptive activation of mitochondrial biogenesis is critical for long term recovery. Experimental studies indicate that PGC 1α mediated mitochondrial biogenesis is essential for restoring mitochondrial morphology and function, promoting neurite regeneration, and supporting cell survival. Inhibition of PGC-1α significantly hinders these reparative processes [191]. Although direct evidence in infants with HIBD is limited, these data suggest that chronically low activity of mitochondrial biogenesis and upstream regulators may reduce the production of new mitochondria and limit functional reserve. In such a “low energy margin” state, brain tissue may struggle to meet metabolic demands during key developmental periods such as learning, motor skill acquisition, or environmental stress, thereby restricting repair capacity and functional maturation [191, 212].

It has also been proposed that persistently damaged mitochondria act as continuous sources of DAMPs, including mtDNA, that activate innate immune pathways such as cGAS–STING and maintain a chronic, low grade pro inflammatory environment [217]. Work in ischemia–reperfusion and neuroinflammatory models demonstrates that leaked mtDNA can function as a danger signal, activating cGAS–STING signaling and eliciting inflammatory responses in neurons and microglia. In neonatal HIE models, cGAS–STING activation correlates with microglial NLRP3/gasdermin mediated pyroptosis and neuroinflammation, whereas pharmacological cGAS inhibition attenuates cGAS–STING signaling, suppresses inflammasome dependent pyroptosis, reduces brain injury, and improves neurobehavior [218, 219, 280]. Persistent mitochondria driven inflammation is thought to impair oligodendrocyte differentiation and myelination, inhibit neurogenesis, and disrupt precise synaptic pruning, thereby contributing to white matter underdevelopment and aberrant cortical network activity that manifest as learning difficulties and altered behavioral phenotypes [230, 231].

The neonatal innate immune system is at a unique developmental stage and exhibits age dependent expression and function of DNA sensing pathways, including cGAS–STING and TLR9, which differ from those of adults [281]. Consequently, a similar degree of mtDNA leakage may elicit a more robust or qualitatively different inflammatory response in the developing brain, providing a plausible mechanism for the heightened susceptibility of the neonatal brain to hypoxia ischemia [220]. In terms of mitochondrial quality control, long lasting disturbances in mitochondrial dynamics combined with reduced mitophagy efficiency result in inadequate clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria. These organelles accumulate in neurons and serve as chronic sources of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, sustaining oxidative stress and further mtDNA damage [221]. Studies in brain injury and neurodegenerative diseases show that appropriate activation of mitophagy promotes removal of damaged mitochondria, reduces ROS production, and maintains intracellular homeostasis, whereas insufficient autophagy is closely associated with mitochondrial accumulation, increased oxidative stress, and progressive neurodegeneration [201, 202]. In neonatal HIBD models, impaired mitophagy has been linked to accumulation of damaged mitochondria and aggravated neuronal death, while enhancement of mitophagy through pathways such as RORα activation attenuates mitochondrial ultrastructural damage, suppresses inflammasome activation, reduces brain injury, and improves functional outcomes [203, 204]. Taken together, insufficient mitochondrial turnover in the long-term phase is likely to impair mitochondrial energy supply and Ca2 + buffering at synapses, leading to reduced synaptic transmission efficiency, decreased dendritic spine density, and delayed apoptosis of some neurons. These changes can gradually consolidate the initial acute insult into persistent neurodevelopmental impairments characterized by learning difficulties, motor coordination problems, attention deficits, and emotional and behavioral disturbances [201, 204].

Longitudinal studies indicate that neurological evolution in the tertiary phase is closely linked to the degree and pace of mitochondrial and metabolic recovery after the initial injury. Cohort data show that even in children with HIE who do not develop cerebral palsy, approximately 25–63% experience general or specific cognitive problems at school age, including mild to moderate difficulties in attention, executive function, memory, or language, along with increased behavioral issues that often necessitate additional educational and social support [212, 264, 282]. By contrast, infants with severe early lesions, especially extensive cortical or deep nuclear injury, have a higher prevalence of intellectual disability, cerebral palsy with cognitive impairment, and emotional and behavioral problems, often displaying clinical features reminiscent of attention deficit and autism spectrum disorders, underscoring the long term impact of structural and functional abnormalities [212, 283]. Mechanistic work in neurodevelopment and neurodegeneration further shows that chronic mitochondrial dysfunction depletes neural stem cells, impairs adult neurogenesis, and produces lasting synaptic deficits and hippocampus dependent learning and memory impairments, implicating mitochondrial defects as shared drivers of diverse neurodevelopmental and cognitive disorders [140, 284, 285]. Thus, the tertiary phase represents a period in which the sequelae of early mitochondrial injury and energetic imbalance are expressed at cognitive, motor, and behavioral levels and a critical window for optimizing development through systematic follow up, targeted screening, and sustained rehabilitation [282, 283].

Collectively, the four pathological phases of HIBD delineate the progressive trajectory of mitochondrial dysfunction. During the primary phase, acute hypoxia ischemia directly suppresses mitochondrial OXPHOS and precipitates a rapid fall in ATP production and primary energy failure [20]. In the latent phase, cerebral high energy phosphate levels partially recover after reoxygenation, yet mitochondria remain functionally fragile due to persistent electron transport chain defects, accumulated oxidative stress, and heightened mPTP sensitivity, creating the substrate for subsequent secondary energy failure and structural injury [192]. In the secondary energy failure phase, disturbances in energy metabolism, surges in ROS, severe Ca2 + imbalance, and aberrant activation of multiple regulated cell death pathways generate synergistic injury cascades that culminate in delayed neuronal death and tissue damage [188, 192]. During the extended tertiary phase, mitochondria in surviving cells frequently exhibit persistent functional defects and dynamic imbalances that limit their capacity to sustain the developing brain’s demand for stable energy, homeostatic regulation, and signal transduction, thereby contributing to long term cognitive, motor, and behavioral sequelae [211, 212, 284].

This phase based framework integrates mitochondrial mechanisms across the course of HIBD and provides a rationale for stage specific therapeutic strategies. In the latent phase, interventions can focus on attenuating oxidative stress and modulating mPTP opening to stabilize mitochondrial function and prevent secondary energy failure. During the secondary injury phase, therapeutic priorities shift toward blocking apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis to reduce delayed neuronal loss. In later stages, it becomes important to promote partial repair and remodeling of mitochondrial function by modulating mitochondrial dynamics, enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis, and improving chronic inflammation control and mitochondrial quality management [191, 192, 211]. These concepts align with emerging mitochondria targeted therapies, including mPTP inhibitors, antioxidants, modulators of mitochondrial dynamics, and agents that boost biogenesis and mitophagy, and thus provide a theoretical basis for the development and optimization of future treatment strategies [24, 204].

Mechanisms of mitochondrial dysfunction in PBI

In preterm related brain injury, the core pathological mechanism of mitochondrial dysfunction can be summarized as developmental immaturity combined with multiple perinatal insults. This mechanism is closely related to the unique biological milieu of the immature central nervous system and underlies the heightened vulnerability of preterm brain tissue [103]. Compared with term infants, the white matter of preterm infants is predominantly composed of OPCs and immature neurons that are in critical stages of differentiation and myelination. These cells have high metabolic demands but incomplete antioxidant defenses, as reflected by relatively low activity of endogenous antioxidant systems, including glutathione dependent enzymes, superoxide dismutase (SOD), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx). As a result, they are highly susceptible to perturbations in energy metabolism and cellular damage even under relatively mild oxidative stress [222–224]. Recurrent perinatal challenges such as hypoxia, hypoperfusion, infection, hemodynamic instability, and nutritional imbalance place additional stress on mitochondrial respiratory chains and OXPHOS. This promotes excessive mtROS production and oxidative damage to mitochondrial membrane lipids and mtDNA, which particularly disrupts OPC differentiation and maturation and impairs white matter development [224, 225] At the same time, mitochondrial Ca2 + buffering and ER mitochondria contact sites in developing neurons and glia are functionally immature. This immaturity increases vulnerability to intracellular Ca2 + accumulation and mitochondrial Ca2 + overload in the setting of excitotoxicity and inflammatory signaling and represents a key determinant of mitochondrial susceptibility in PBI [226, 227]. Experimental models of PBI and hypoxia ischemia show that disturbed Ca2 + homeostasis can trigger abnormal opening of cyclophilin D-dependent mPTP, leading to loss of membrane potential, further ATP decline, and mitochondrial structural damage, thereby aggravating energy failure and amplifying neuronal and OPC injury [103, 224].

During critical windows of brain development, balanced mitochondrial fission–fusion dynamics and adequate autophagy are essential for OPC survival, differentiation, and myelination. An imbalance characterized by excessive fission, impaired fusion, and reduced autophagic flux promotes the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria that cannot be efficiently removed. This condition predisposes OPCs to cell death or differentiation arrest as a result of combined energy deficiency and oxidative stress and directly compromises white matter growth [232, 233]. Inflammatory states such as intrauterine infection, chorioamnionitis, and postnatal sepsis further exacerbate mitochondrial injury by suppressing respiratory chain activity and increasing the production of ROS and RNS. Damaged mitochondria release mtDNA and other DAMPs that amplify microglial and astrocytic inflammation through innate immune pathways, including cGAS–STING, thereby creating a positive feedback loop of inflammation and mitochondrial damage. This vicious cycle accelerates dysfunction of neurons and glia and increases the risk of diffuse white matter underdevelopment and long term disturbances in the refinement of neural circuits [218, 286–288].

Long term neurodevelopmental outcomes following PBI are tightly linked to the initial burden of injury and to the extent and persistence of subsequent oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction [228]. Longitudinal studies demonstrate that the severity and distribution of non hemorrhagic WMI correlate strongly with the risk of cerebral palsy and other neurodevelopmental disorders. Infants with mild WMI often show only minor motor delay or subtle cognitive deficits, whereas moderate to severe injury is associated with moderate to severe cerebral palsy and widespread neurodevelopmental impairment [234, 235]. These findings support the view that oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction are key pathological drivers of PBI. Excessive mtROS and inadequate ATP production disrupt essential developmental processes such as neurogenesis, synaptogenesis, and myelination, and, through activation of microglia and inflammatory pathways, perpetuate a deleterious cycle of energy deficiency, inflammation, and mitochondrial damage that substantially increases the risk of persistent developmental and cognitive impairments [224, 229].

Clinical cohort studies and randomized trials indicate that when early brain injury in preterm infants is relatively mild and optimal oxygenation, nutrition, pain and stress control, and antioxidant management are provided during the perinatal and follow up periods, many infants exhibit only mild to moderate motor or cognitive delays and can achieve near normal or only slightly restricted neurodevelopmental levels due to cerebral plasticity and rehabilitation [236, 237]. In contrast, severe early injury combined with prolonged mitochondrial energy deficiency and sustained inflammatory stress is associated with a higher likelihood of adverse outcomes, including cerebral palsy, cognitive impairment, learning difficulties, attention deficits, and emotional and behavioral problems [238, 239] Consequently, strategies aimed at reducing early oxidative stress, optimizing oxygen delivery and nutritional support, and developing antioxidant and mitochondria supportive pharmacotherapies should be regarded as integral components of neuroprotection in preterm infants and hold promise for improving long term neurodevelopment in addition to survival [224, 240, 241].

Mechanisms of mitochondrial dysfunction in NIBI

In neonatal infectious or sepsis related brain injury, mitochondrial dysfunction often results from the interaction between systemic inflammation and the developing central nervous system [251, 289]. The neonatal immune system is immature, and innate immune receptor responses differ substantially from those in adults. Experimental data show that neonatal monocytes and dendritic cells can produce interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) at levels comparable to or higher than adults after Toll like receptor(TLR) stimulation, indicating that neonatal innate immunity is biased toward pro inflammatory and Th17/Th2 profiles and is prone to dysregulated responses under specific stimuli [290]. Animal studies further suggest that TLR9 expression in neonatal monocytes is relatively high and that dysregulated TLR9 signaling can cause severe systemic inflammatory injury early after birth, highlighting the heightened sensitivity of this pathway in early life [291].

Pathogens and their components, such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), initially activate peripheral innate immune responses and induce the release of pro inflammatory mediators including tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), IL-1β, and IL-6 [252]. In neonates, where blood–brain barrier function is not fully mature, these mediators can more readily penetrate the central nervous system or initiate intracerebral inflammatory cascades via signals transmitted within the neurovascular unit [253]. Within the brain parenchyma, mitochondria in microglia and astrocytes serve as key integration hubs for inflammatory signaling. Mitochondria derived ROS play a central role in activating the NLRP3 inflammasome, which promotes the maturation of interleukin 1β and establishes a self amplifying inflammatory loop [242]. In addition, mtDNA released from damaged mitochondria functions as a prototypical damage associated molecular pattern and is sensed by TLR9 and cGAS, driving the expression of type I interferons and other pro inflammatory mediators through MyD88 dependent and cGAS–STING pathways. This cascade has been described as an mtDNA–TLR9/cGAS–STING–inflammation axis [219] (see Fig. 5). Animal models suggest that inhibition of cGAS–STING signaling alleviates inflammation induced WMI and improves neurological outcome, reflecting the high sensitivity and signal amplification capacity of this pathway in the developing brain [288].

Sustained inflammation exerts multiple deleterious effects on mitochondrial function in neurons and oligodendrocytes. Inflammatory signaling upregulates inducible nitric oxide synthase, increasing nitric oxide production, which reacts with superoxide to form ONOO−. ONOO− directly impairs energy metabolism by inhibiting respiratory chain complexes and ATP synthase through oxidative damage [243, 244]. In parallel, inflammatory mediators activate DRP1, promoting excessive mitochondrial fission, fragmentation, and loss of membrane potential and facilitating cytochrome c release and apoptotic pathway activation [70, 292]. Under infection induced stress, neurons undergo metabolic reprogramming similar to that in immune cells, shifting from OXPHOS toward glycolysis to adapt to hypoxic and inflammatory conditions. In the immature brain, however, this shift is more prone to imbalance, leading to insufficient ATP supply and increased ROS and RNS production due to mismatches between glycolytic flux and mitochondrial capacity [222]. Pathogen derived toxins can also directly disrupt mitochondrial membranes and inhibit respiratory complexes, while systemic hypoperfusion, hypotension, and hypoxemia associated with sepsis secondarily induce ischemia like mitochondrial hypoxia, creating a combined pattern of inflammatory and ischemic mitochondrial injury [244].

Mitochondrial dysfunction in brain endothelial cells is another critical component of this process. Inflammatory activation of endothelial cells disrupts tight junctions and increases blood–brain barrier permeability, facilitating the entry of inflammatory mediators and immune cells into the brain. Aberrant cGAS–STING activation in endothelial cells can aggravate barrier breakdown [245, 246]. Pharmacological inhibition of this pathway in sepsis associated encephalopathy models has been shown to mitigate blood–brain barrier disruption, neuroinflammation, and cognitive deficits [247]. Persistent inflammation additionally impairs mitophagy, reduces clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria, and leads to their accumulation and enhanced release of mtROS and mtDNA, establishing a self-reinforcing cycle of mitochondrial injury, neuroinflammation, and barrier disruption.

Existing longitudinal studies demonstrate that long term neurodevelopmental outcomes after NIBI are strongly influenced by infection severity, the intensity and duration of inflammatory responses, and the presence of multiple organ dysfunction. Cohort studies and meta analyses consistently show that both preterm and term infants with neonatal sepsis have a higher risk of cognitive delay, motor impairment, sensory deficits, and cerebral palsy than noninfected infants, with the highest risk observed in those with severe sepsis, recurrent or late onset sepsis, or complications such as meningitis and intraventricular hemorrhage [31, 250, 254, 256]. By contrast, infants with mild to moderate infections and effectively controlled inflammation mainly exhibit mild or transient neurological abnormalities and tend to achieve relatively favorable neurodevelopmental outcomes on follow up [255, 257]. These observations highlight the importance of early identification and active management of neonatal infections and timely control of systemic inflammation. Similarities and differences in mitochondrial dysfunction across three types of brain injury

Taken together, the mitochondrial mechanisms underlying these three major forms of neonatal brain injury share fundamental common features but also display distinct patterns related to their differing etiologies and developmental contexts. Ultimately, all converge on mitochondrial dysfunction as a critical pathological hub that drives brain injury and contributes to long term neurodevelopmental impairment (see Fig. 6).

Across hypoxic ischemic, preterm, and infectious brain injury, several core mitochondrial pathways are closely interconnected. These include disturbances in energy metabolism, excessive generation of ROS and RNS, disruption of Ca2 + homeostasis, pathological opening of the mPTP, abnormalities in mitochondrial dynamics and mitophagy, and mitochondria mediated cell death and inflammatory amplification. These processes are not independent but form a self-reinforcing cycle characterized by persistent ROS production and accumulation of damaged mitochondria throughout the course of injury. Mitochondrial dysfunction thus emerges as both a central target and a key integrator of injury signals. Regardless of the primary insult, the downstream result is impaired neuronal and glial function through mitochondria dependent pathways such as apoptosis and necroptosis, which in turn adversely affect long term neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Despite these shared features, each condition exhibits specific mitochondrial signatures. Hypoxic ischemic brain damage is dominated by ischemia–reperfusion injury and distinct temporal staging. It is characterized by a rapid onset with primary energy failure, followed by secondary energy failure driven by sustained mPTP opening, leading to phase specific peaks of acute injury and delayed cell death that predominantly affect gray matter neurons in regions such as the hippocampus and basal ganglia [20, 44, 192]. In contrast, PBI is rooted in intrinsic developmental immaturity of mitochondria, including structural, functional, and antioxidant reserve deficits. When combined with perinatal stressors, these vulnerabilities intensify energy insufficiency and developmental disturbance, causing pronounced injury to OPCs and cerebral white matter and manifesting as mitochondrial maladaptation and impaired developmental remodeling under chronic stress [222–224]. Infectious brain injury is characterized primarily by inflammation and immune mediated mitochondrial stress, in which inflammatory signaling precedes overt energy failure and amplifies damage through a positive feedback loop between inflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction. This process simultaneously affects neurons, glia, and cerebrovascular endothelial cells and is accompanied by marked inflammatory infiltration and blood–brain barrier disruption [251, 289].

Within this framework of shared yet distinct mitochondrial pathology, rational development of mitochondria targeted therapies should proceed along two complementary axes. First, broadly applicable interventions should focus on common targets such as enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis, limiting oxidative stress, and restoring dynamic balance in mitochondrial fusion, fission, and mitophagy. Enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis

Mitochondrial biogenesis refers to the process by which mitochondrial content and function are increased within cells and encompasses mitochondrial biosynthesis, expansion of the mitochondrial network, and functional optimization. It represents an adaptive response to elevated energy demands or stressors such as exercise, cold exposure, and hypoxia and is essential for maintaining cellular energy metabolism and overall health [294, 295, 312]. Administration of NAD+ precursors, including nicotinamide riboside (NR) and nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN), increases intracellular NAD+ levels, activates sirtuins such as SIRT1, and promotes PGC‑1α mediated mitochondrial biogenesis [296, 297]. Resveratrol similarly activates the SIRT1–PGC‑1α pathway, thereby augmenting mitochondrial content and improving insulin sensitivity [298, 313]. In addition, supplementation with mitochondrial cofactors can support mitochondrial function and biogenesis. For example, alpha lipoic acid has been reported to enhance mitochondrial function and biogenesis and to exert neuroprotective effects in experimental models of ischemic stroke and neurodegenerative disease [314, 315]. IACS‑010759 selectively targets mitochondrial metabolism in cancer cells by inhibiting complex I of the electron transport chain and is currently under clinical evaluation [299, 300]. Supplementation with coenzymes and metabolites also represents an important approach; for instance, creatine supports the phosphocreatine buffering system and ameliorates energy deficits and has been explored as a therapy for mitochondrial encephalomyopathies [301]. Collectively, these strategies aim to optimize mitochondrial energy metabolism and provide novel options for mitochondria related diseases. In clinical practice, however, most of these approaches have been investigated in adults with age related or metabolic disorders or in women with high risk pregnancies rather than in neonates, and neonatal pharmacokinetics, optimal dosing, and interactions with standard neonatal intensive care unit therapies remain poorly defined and require dedicated trials before routine use can be recommended [316].

Reducing mitochondrial oxidative stress

Mitochondrial dysfunction is frequently associated with elevated oxidative stress. As outlined above, mitochondria are a major source of ROS. Endogenous enzymatic systems, including transmembrane NADPH oxidases (NOXs) and the mitochondrial electron transport chain, are principally responsible for the generation of superoxide and H₂O₂ [317]. Antioxidants and free radical scavengers therefore represent important therapeutic approaches for mitochondrial dysfunction, as they can neutralize excessive ROS and protect cells from oxidative injury [318]. MitoSNO is a mitochondria targeted S nitrosothiol that reduces excessive superoxide and H₂O₂ production during the early reperfusion period after ischemia [319]. By transiently inhibiting complex I reactivation at the onset of reperfusion, MitoSNO limits oxidative damage and improves tissue outcomes in adult models of myocardial ischemia–reperfusion. In a neonatal mouse model, administration of MitoSNO at reperfusion reduced oxidative stress and improved neurological recovery [302]. Protopine (Pro) is an isoquinoline alkaloid derived from various plants. Experimental work has shown that protopine significantly decreases infarct volume, alleviates brain edema, and attenuates neuronal loss and ROS accumulation after HI injury by activating the AMPK/PGC‑1α pathway, thereby exerting neuroprotective effects [303]. Coenzyme Q10 plays a central role as a cofactor in the mitochondrial electron transport chain, participating in ATP synthesis and acting as a lipid soluble antioxidant. Studies in pregnant women with and without preeclampsia indicate that CoQ10 supplementation increases maternal plasma and placental levels, whereas umbilical cord blood levels remain substantially lower, suggesting only partial placental transfer and important developmental pharmacokinetic differences [307]. CoQ10 is also present in human breast milk and contributes to postnatal intake; observational data indicate that breastfed infants exhibit higher increases in circulating CoQ10 than formula fed infants, consistent with additional intake from human milk [308, 309]. Translational and clinical studies suggest that combining mild hypothermia with CoQ10 may improve cerebral energy metabolism under hypoxic–ischemic conditions [84, 304]. Vitamin antioxidants such as vitamin E, vitamin C, alpha lipoic acid, and N-acetylcysteine (NAC) can scavenge free radicals and attenuate oxidative injury and have been used to mitigate excessive ROS production in various metabolic settings [320]. In immature rodent models of hypoxic–ischemic brain injury, NAC administered in combination with systemic hypothermia provided greater and more sustained neuroprotection than either treatment alone, supporting the biological plausibility of its adjunctive use in neonatal encephalopathy [321]. However, clinical experience with NAC in human neonates is currently limited to small or early phase studies, and potential interactions with therapeutic hypothermia and other standard neonatal intensive care (NICUs) medications have not yet been systematically evaluated [322]. The development and rigorous evaluation of safer and more effective antioxidants for neonatal brain injury remain important challenges for future research.

Targeting mitochondrial dynamics

A key aspect of mitochondrial dynamics is the fission and subsequent clearance of damaged mitochondria [323]. Recent studies indicate that physiological mitochondrial fission during exercise is an adaptive mechanism to meet increased energy demands, whereas selective clearance of damaged mitochondria is essential for maintaining mitochondrial quality [324]. In PINK1/Parkin mutant Drosophila models of Parkinson disease, increased expression of the mitochondrial fusion protein MFN2 has been associated with mitochondrial membrane rupture, activation of mitophagy, and mitochondrial dysfunction [305]. Ubiquitin specific protease 14(USP14) is a deubiquitinating enzyme that negatively regulates proteasome activity and inhibits mitophagy by preventing proteasomal degradation of mitochondrial proteins; modulation of this pathway has been shown to influence hypoxic–ischemic brain injury in experimental models [203]. Pleckstrin homology like domain family A member 1 (PHLDA1) has been implicated in disease processes related to oxidative stress, immune responses, and apoptosis. In neonatal hypoxic–ischemic brain injury, PHLDA1 exacerbates neuronal damage by suppressing FUNDC1 mediated mitophagy, whereas genetic deletion of PHLDA1 in neonatal rats attenuates hippocampal neuronal injury and improves long term cognitive outcomes [183]. Melatonin has also been shown to restore mitochondrial dynamics and inhibit excessive mitochondrial fission in experimental models, in part through regulation of mitophagy, thereby providing important mechanistic insight for novel neuroprotective strategies [306]. In a randomized pilot trial in term infants with hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy, enteral melatonin administered with therapeutic hypothermia reduced circulating oxidative stress markers, seizure burden, and white matter abnormalities on MRI and increased the proportion of survivors without major neurodevelopmental impairment at 6 months [310]. More recently, the Acute High Dose Melatonin for Encephalopathy of the Newborn (ACUMEN) phase 1 dose escalation study has begun to characterize the pharmacokinetics, tolerability, and feasibility of intravenous melatonin in cooled neonates, although definitive efficacy and long term safety data are still awaited [311].

Summary and outlook

The importance of mitochondrial dysfunction in neonatal brain injury is increasingly recognized. As the central organelles of cellular energy metabolism, mitochondria exert a direct influence on neuronal survival and development. Future research should therefore prioritize strategies that protect and repair mitochondrial function and integrate principles of precision medicine into the management of neonatal brain injury. Detailed characterization of genetic background, metabolic profiles, and mitochondrial function in affected infants may enable more tailored therapeutic approaches. In parallel, interdisciplinary collaboration spanning neuroscience, molecular biology, bioinformatics, and engineering will be essential to accelerate the development of innovative mitochondrial targeted therapies. Through these efforts, it may be possible to improve treatment efficacy, reduce long term disability, and enhance quality of life for children with neonatal brain injury.

Early diagnosis of mitochondrial dysfunction is also crucial, and the development of sensitive biomarkers and imaging methods to detect mitochondrial impairment at an early stage should be a priority. Existing treatment strategies need to be refined to improve both safety and efficacy in neonatal populations. By focusing on mitochondrial protection and repair, advancing personalized approaches, exploiting interdisciplinary collaborations, and developing early diagnostic tools while optimizing current interventions, we can obtain a more comprehensive understanding of mitochondrial roles in neonatal brain injury. Ultimately, translation of these advances into routine practice will require neonatal specific clinical trials that rigorously evaluate efficacy, safety, and compatibility with standard neonatal intensive care therapies.
