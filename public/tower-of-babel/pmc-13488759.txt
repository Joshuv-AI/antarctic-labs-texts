Complicated grief following pregnancy loss in women with autoimmune rheumatic diseases: a comparative cross-sectional study
Authors: Maria Eugenia Corral-Trujillo, Rodrigo Cerda-González, Anahí Carrazco-Chapa, Rodrigo Cantu-Barreda, Luis Carlos Berlanga-Muñoz, Brayan Uriel Montes-Rodriguez, Jesus Alberto Cardenas-de la Garza, Lorena Perez-Barbosa, Cassandra Michele Skinner-Taylor, Andrea Lizbeth Guajardo-Aldaco, Janett Riega-Torres, Dionicio Angel Galarza-Delgado
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWomen with autoimmune rheumatic diseases (ARDs) present a higher rate of obstetric adverse events including perinatal losses (PL). The aim of this study is to determine the frequency of both complicated grief (CG) following PL and resilience in women with ARDs and compare it to a control group.MethodsWe performed an analytical, comparative, cross-sectional study from November 2022 to July 2024. Women with and without an ARD diagnosis with a history of PL who completed a self-report survey including the Perinatal Grief Scale (PGS) and the Wagnild and Young Resilience Scale (RS) were selected. We analyzed sociodemographic data, clinical characteristics, and scale scores with SPSS Version 25.ResultsNinety participants were enrolled in this study. Thirty-three women met criteria for CG following PL, 22 (66.7%) belonged to the control group and 11 (33.3%) to the ARD group (p = 0.016). The RS median score was 138.00 (IQR 117–163). A logistic regression revealed that having an ARD is independently associated with not presenting CG (OR = 0.338; p = 0.018, 95% CI = 0.138–0.829), suggesting that women with ARDs are 2.95 times less likely to develop CG.ConclusionsWomen with ARDs had a lower frequency of CG following PL than the control group. We found no significant difference between groups regarding resilience capacity. The logistic regression suggests that ARDs serve as a protective factor for developing CG following PL. Screening for CG following PL in women with and without ARDs may help optimize maternal and infant health.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Women with autoimmune rheumatic diseases (ARDs) have a higher rate of obstetric adverse events such as preterm delivery, abortions, and perinatal losses (PL) than the general population [1]. Stillbirth, miscarriage, and neonatal death are considered traumatic life events [2]. According to international studies, the prevalence of PL in women with ARDs ranges from 15.15 to 26% [3–5]. Grieving is considered a common physiological and instinctive response to the feeling of loss, such as PL [6]. Despite the high prevalence of PL, research on the socio cultural aspects of grief, particularly in Latin American contexts, remains limited [7]. In Western societies, miscarriage or stillbirth is often not recognized as a significant loss [8]. The social pressure on parents to suppress their grief, compounded by a lack of culturally appropriate rituals and insufficient support from healthcare professionals, significantly exacerbates emotional distress [9].

The pathological form of grief is complicated grief (CG), characterized by prolonged periods of intense bereavement that impair daily functioning [10]. CG It is also known as Prolonged Grief Disorder in the International Classification of Disorders 11th Edition (ICD-11) and the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders Fifth Edition (DSM-5) [7]. It is known that CG following PL is associated with a myriad of health problems, including sleep disturbances, substance abuse, suicidal behavior, activation of systemic inflammation, cardiovascular disease, and cancer [10], highlighting the importance of its assessment in every woman who goes through this process. Resilience refers to the individual’s ability to recover their homeostasis and avoid developing a psychopathological outcome after acute or chronic stress exposure [11]. Both psychological processes can be assessed with different screening tools, such as the Perinatal Grief Scale (PGS), considered the best screening tool to evaluate complicated grief [12], and the Wagnild and Young Resilience Scale (RS), which the American Psychological Association recommends to measuring the resilience capacity [13].

Even though CG may interfere with therapeutic adherence [10] and worsen pregnancy outcomes [14], there is a knowledge gap regarding CG following PL in women with ARDs in Latin America and its consequences on both mother and overall health. In this study, we aim to describe the frequency of CG and the resilience capacity among women with ARDs with a history of PL and compare them with women without ARDs.

Methods

A descriptive, comparative, cross-sectional study was conducted in the “Dr. Jose E. Gonzalez” University Hospital in northeast Mexico from November 2022 to July 2024. Inclusion criteria were women with ARDs (rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, antiphospholipid syndrome, Sjögren syndrome, and others) with a history of PL. Women who met the inclusion criteria were invited to participate from the Reproduction and Pregnancy Clinic in Rheumatic Diseases (CREER for its initials in Spanish). For the control group, we invited women without ARDs with a history of PL from the Obstetrics and Gynecology outpatient clinic waiting room. Both groups completed online surveys, however were differed in content. For the ARD group, they were asked to complete both scales (PGS and RS) since the sociodemographic, ARD and PL data were available in the CREER clinical records; whereas in the control group, the online survey included sociodemographic, clinical and PL information as well as both scales. For both groups, the inclusion criterion was a one month minimum after the most recent PL.

Exclusion criteria included a previous diagnosis of psychiatric disorders, cognitive impairment, or dropout while filling out the questionnaires. The only cause of exclusion after screening was incomplete survey responses. The PGS is a 27-item Likert-type instrument validated in Mexico in Spanish-speaking women with a history of PL [15]. It was originally developed in English, and the questions are categorized into four subscales: active grief (10 items), guilt (8 items), depression (6 items), and acceptance (3 items); scores higher than 50 points suggest the presence of CG comorbidity [16]. The RS is a 25-item Likert-type instrument validated in Spanish-speaking women of Mexican descent [13]. Originally developed in English, the scale’s items are rated on a 7-point scale and summed to yield a total score ranging from 25 to 175, with higher scores indicating greater resilience capacity [17]. Both the PGS and RS have demonstrated high validity and reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients greater than 0.80 [16].

For statistical analysis, the sample size was calculated using the formula for comparing two independent proportions. Prevalence of perinatal losses was estimated at 20% [18] for the control group and 15% [3] for the ARD group. A significance level of α = 0.05 (two-tailed) and power of 95% were applied. Based on Z₁–α/₂ = 1.96 and Z₁–β = 1.64, the calculated sample size was 33 participants per group, yielding a total sample of 66 women. The Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to determine normality. Demographic characteristics were presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and for continuous variables, we provided median, interquartile range (IQR). Chi-square and Mann–Whitney U tests were employed to analyze the differences between groups. Spearman’s correlations were performed to assess the association between the PGS and RS scores. A binary logistic regression (with odds ratios, [OR]) was used to evaluate the relationship between CG (dependent variable) and participant characteristics (number of pregnancies and having a rheumatic disease). Reported p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The statistical analysis was performed with the statistical program SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

After receiving an explanation about the purpose of the study, the scales, and the clinical and demographic details, both groups agreed to sign an informed consent form to participate and complete the survey according to their group (with or without ARD). Patients identified with CG following PL were provided with psychological support to prevent the development of other psychiatric conditions, such as PTSD and major depressive disorder. The protocol was approved by the research and ethics committee of the “Dr. José Eleuterio González” University Hospital (November 12, 2018; RE18-00008).

Results

A total of 90 women were enrolled: 45 women with ARDs and 45 without ARDs (controls). The median age of the ARD group was 42 (38.50–51.50) and the control group was 28 (24-35.50), with significant differences between groups (p = 0.001). The main diagnosis for the ARD group was rheumatoid arthritis (n = 20; 44.4%), followed by systemic lupus erythematosus (n = 10; 22.2%) and other diagnoses (n = 8; 17.8%). The sociodemographic characteristics are described in Table 1. We found significant differences between the ARD group and the control group in the number of pregnancies (3 [IQR 3–4] vs. 3 [IQR 2–4]; p = 0.033), the PGS depression subscale (11 [IQR 10–13] vs. 14 [IQR 12–20]; p = 0.001) and the PGS total score (43 [IQR 39-49.50] vs. 49 [IQR 40.50–60]; p = 0.013).

In the ARD group, a significant negative correlation between the PGS total score and the RS score was found (p = 0.041, r = -0.306) while in the control group, no significant correlation was found. The logistic regression analysis reported that having a rheumatic disease ([OR] = 0.338; p = 0.018, 95% CI = 0.138–0.829) was independently associated with lower chance of CG not having CG, indicating that a woman with an ARD is 2.95 times less likely to develop CG following PL.

Discussion

Women with ARDs may experience recurrent PL during their reproductive age [19], and CG is one of the most common comorbidities reported worldwide [20]. The aim of our study was to assess the frequency of CG following PL among women with ARDs and compare them to women without ARDs. Our total sample showed a frequency of 36.70% for CG following PL, similar to the prevalence reported for the general population, which ranged between 12 and 35% [21] to 46.1% [22].

We found statistically significant differences in the CG following PL frequency between groups, with a lower frequency of this condition in women with ARDs. A cross-sectional study conducted in Mexican women aged 15–45 years found that 71.3% of them developed CG following PL, similar to the frequency observed in the control group of our study [23]. In a comparative study between Brazilian and Canadian women, a low education level (< 12 years of formal education) was associated with CG following PL, particularly among the Brazilian participants [21]. This is consistent with the educational level observed in our control group. Both Mexico and Brazil are middle-income countries, where social inequality, limited access to specialized mental health services, and the absence of culturally appropriate bereavement support may contribute to the higher prevalence of CG. We infer that these shared socioeconomic and cultural factors play a significant role in the development of CG following PL in this population.

Cultural expectations around motherhood, including beliefs that motherhood provides meaning and aligns with societal norms, may protect women from external pressures and shape their emotional reactions to pregnancy loss [24]. Societal attitudes toward pregnancy loss, such as viewing miscarriage as a woman’s failure to fulfill her maternal role, can intensify guilt and hinder grief expression [25]. Although sociodemographic factors, such as age, religious beliefs, education level, and work have been shown to impact the development of CG following PL [7], in our population, we did not identify an association between CG and years of education, having a current partner, or receiving economic remuneration. Factors that may influence the substantially lower frequency of CG following PL among women with ARDs include the expectations and knowledge of potential adverse pregnancy outcomes and the specialized multidisciplinary healthcare provided by the pregnancy and rheumatic disease clinic, where psychological support is provided [26].

Our findings suggest that patients with ARDs are more capable of coping with the emotional stress of experiencing a PL than the control group. This supports our logistic regression analysis results, which suggest that women with ARDs are less likely to develop CG following PL than controls. We also found significant differences in the number of pregnancies, the depression subscale and the total PGS scores, with the control group scoring higher. Some evidence suggests that women with ARDs facing adverse clinical outcomes have an improved resilience capacity that helps them to be able to deal better with stressful experiences [11]. This psychological capacity could alleviate the grieving process in women with ARDs. However, no significant difference between groups in the RS was found, suggesting that having an ARD doesn’t enhance the resilience capacity in women with PL. Our findings are consistent with a study in women with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), which reported similar resilience levels between patients and healthy controls, and found no association between sociodemographic variables and resilience [27]. In contrast, a study in Colombian women with ARDs found that resilience was influenced by age and socioeconomic status, with younger women and those from more vulnerable contexts showing lower resilience levels [11]. These mixed results highlight the complex and multi factorial nature of resilience in women with ARDs and suggest that its role in the grieving process requires further exploration.

The support system of the women who suffer a PL is crucial to prevent psychiatric comorbidities. It is known that the whole nuclear family suffers the consequences of the perinatal death individually. After suffering a PL, the women return to their social environment, and they must work through their grief and adapt to life without the lost baby. In this environment, the pain of the PL can become invisible and minimized. Underestimating grief could hamper the mother’s ability to take actions toward a healthy grief [8].

Screening scales have been used as part of the early detection and management of CG following PL. Considering that our population has shown to be more vulnerable to developing psychiatric disorders [28], systematic screening is crucial as part of healthcare management to follow up on women who suffer from PL by offering counseling and psychological support [29]. Applying screening tests for perinatal depression may help to identify and treat undiagnosed patients and develop tailored programs to reduce morbidity and stigmatization [30]. Screening for CG is recommended following PL and prior to or during the planning of a subsequent pregnancy to optimize maternal and infant health [28].

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths. This is one of the first studies to investigate CG following PL in women with ARDs. Additionally, we employed validated instruments to measure these variables. The limitations include memory biases for past pregnancies in patients of older age, age-unmatched groups, sample size, and diagnostic heterogeneity. The small sample size limits the generalizability of our findings to other populations and cultural contexts. Studies with a larger sample size and examining other factors such as treatments, social support, and psychological influences are necessary.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study showed that women with ARDs had a lower frequency of CG following PL compared to controls. Furthermore, it seems that having an ARD was a protective factor against the development of CG, with no significant differences in resilience capacity between groups. Additional studies are needed to confirm these findings. Recognizing and screening for CG following PL in women with and without ARDs may contribute to optimizing maternal and infant health outcomes.
