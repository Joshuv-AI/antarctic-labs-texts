Health-related physical fitness in children and adolescents and the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals: Signals of convergence
Authors: Diego Augusto Santos Silva, Mark S Tremblay
Published: 2025

Background

The United Nations (UN) 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, adopted in 2015, established the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) as a comprehensive framework to address global challenges through interconnected social, economic, and environmental targets.1,2 Among the 17 SDGs,1 several carry direct and indirect implications for the health and development of children and adolescents, including via their relationship with physical activity behaviors.3, 4, 5, 6 Recognizing that health outcomes are shaped by multisectoral determinants has, in turn, spurred growing interest in understanding how measurable health indicators in youth populations align with and contribute to broader development objectives.7

Health-related physical fitness in children and adolescents functions both as a health outcome and as a determinant of populations’ future well-being,8,9 positioning this construct as a potentially powerful indicator for monitoring national progress across multiple sectors. The social, environmental, and institutional factors that shape the development of physical fitness in youth10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 are intrinsically linked to the systemic transformations targeted by the SDGs, creating an interdependent web that warrants systematic investigation. Conceptually, the relationship between the SDGs and youth fitness indicators is grounded in frameworks that emphasize the multisectoral nature of health determinants and development outcomes. The ecological model of health behavior16 provides a foundation for understanding how physical fitness is shaped by nested influences, including interpersonal relationships, community environments, institutional policies, and broader social structures, a perspective that aligns with the SDG logic that sustainable development requires coordinated action across sectors and levels of society. Within the framing of these systems, health indicators (here we highlight physical activity and physical fitness) are positioned at the nexus of social and environmental goals, legitimizing the study of fitness both as an outcome of, and a contributor to, multiple development objectives.

In this opinion piece, we argue that the SDGs’ systems perspective is directly applicable to health-related physical fitness. Because improvements in fitness among children and adolescents can simultaneously function as a determinant and an outcome, contributing to better health,8,9 enhanced educational outcomes,17 gender equity (through inclusive sport and physical education),5,6 environmental sustainability (via active transport and nature-based recreation),4, 5, 6 and stronger institutions,11,13 it is reasonable to hypothesize that physical fitness also operates as a policy-relevant marker aligned with multiple SDGs.

Accordingly, this brief exploratory report pursues 2 aims: (a) to outline the theoretical rationale linking selected SDGs to health-related physical fitness in children and adolescents; and (b) to examine whether countries performing better on SDG targets also exhibited better youth fitness performance (e.g., smaller pandemic-related declines) during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) period.

Rationale linking different SDGs to health-related physical fitness in children and adolescents

There is consistent convergence of evidence reflected in statements from scientific societies, normative documents from the World Health Organization (WHO), and peer-reviewed studies indicating that promoting physical activity is a strategic, population-level intervention whose co-benefits extend beyond health to multiple SDGs.3, 4, 5, 6,18 Accordingly, there is an emerging consensus that countries should adopt physical activity promotion as an intersectoral, cost-effective, high-impact public strategy to improve population health while simultaneously catalyzing progress across several SDGs. 3, 4, 5, 6,18

Health-related physical fitness in children and adolescents is an important product of physical activity behaviors19,20 and these behaviors constitute the principal mediating pathway through which social, environmental, and institutional determinants translate into fitness outcomes. Mechanistically, it is through the volume, intensity, frequency, and type of physical activity that contextual conditions are converted into physiological (e.g., cardiorespiratory, neuromuscular) adaptations.19,20 However, mediation by physical activity is not exclusive; sedentary behavior, nutrition, sleep, biological maturation, clinical conditions, and air quality can also influence fitness directly.19,20 Illustrating the influence of factors beyond physical activity, Ludwig-Walz et al.21 analyzed 32 studies representing 30 unique populations, comprising 1,519,386 fitness measurements (552,622 pre-pandemic and 966,764 during or after the pandemic) from 270,179 children and adolescents. The authors reported declines in fitness indicators including endurance, speed, and lower-limb muscular strength when comparing the pre-COVID-19 period with the pandemic period, with more pronounced reductions in some components among adolescents and girls. According to the authors,21 reductions in physical activity during the pandemic period and school closures partly explain the observed declines. However, additional contextual factors should also be considered. Notably, downward trends in youth fitness indicators were already being reported prior to the pandemic, likely reflecting a constellation of influences, including high volumes of sedentary behavior (especially screen time), rising urban violence, deficits in urban planning, diminishing opportunities for structured and unstructured practice, and discrimination affecting specific subgroups of children and adolescents.21

Building on prior research linking physical activity and the SDGs,3, 4, 5, 6 this opinion piece argues that there is also empirical support for relating health-related physical fitness to SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being), SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation, and Infrastructure), SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities), SDG 13 (Climate Action), and SDG 16 (Peace, Justice, and Strong Institutions). In addition, following the arguments advanced by Spence et al.,6 namely, the potential of sport to promote gender equity and the role of natural/green environments in enabling recreational physical activity among children and adolescents, we further posit that these pathways plausibly strengthen the links between SDG 5 (Gender Equality) and SDG 15 (Life on Land) and youth physical fitness.

Do countries with better SDGs performance show smaller reductions in children and adolescents’ physical fitness from the pre-pandemic to the COVID-19 pandemic period?

To address this question, we conducted an ecological analysis using previously published data. The first dataset referred to estimates of physical fitness (endurance, speed, coordination, flexibility, and strength) comparing the pre-pandemic period with the period during or after the COVID-19 pandemic among children and adolescents. These estimates were derived from standardized mean differences (SMDs) for each fitness component reported by Ludwig-Walz et al.,21 who analyzed 32 studies representing 30 unique populations from 17 countries in the WHO European Region (Austria, Croatia, Czech Republic, Estonia, France, Germany, Greece, Hungary, the Netherlands, Poland, Portugal, Serbia, Slovenia, Spain, Switzerland, Turkey, and the United Kingdom). SMDs were reported for multiple subgroups; we used the values for boys, girls, children, and adolescents.

The second dataset concerned the UN SDGs. Data were obtained from the SDG Transformation Center, which annually reports the performance of each of the 195 United Nations Member States in achieving the SDGs.22,23 The SDG Index provides a standardized assessment of each country’s performance across the 17 UN SDGs, assigning equal weight to each SDG. Scores range from 0, representing the worst possible outcome, to 100, representing full achievement of the target.22,23 In the present analysis, we employed the 2025 SDG Index scores23 for each of the countries with physical fitness data from the study by Ludwig-Walz et al.21

In addition to the overall SDG Index, which aggregates performance across all 17 UN SDGs, this opinion piece also specifically focused on SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being), SDG 5 (Gender Equality), SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation, and Infrastructure), SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities), SDG 13 (Climate Action), SDG 15 (Life on Land), and SDG 16 (Peace, Justice, and Strong Institutions) using the 2025 SDG Index scores for each country.22,23 These SDGs were selected because, in the preceding section, we outlined the theoretical rationale linking each of them to health-related physical fitness in children and adolescents.

Muscular strength showed positive, moderate correlations with the overall SDG achievement index (ρ = 0.44; p = 0.014 for males; ρ = 0.45; p = 0.020 for females; ρ = 0.38; p = 0.041 for children) and, specifically, with SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being) (ρ = 0.41; p = 0.024 for males), SDG 5 (Gender Equality) (ρ = 0.46; p = 0.011 for males), SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation, and Infrastructure) (ρ = 0.55; p < 0.001 for males; ρ = 0.45; p = 0.019 for females; ρ = 0.53; p = 0.003 for children), SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities) (ρ = 0.42; p = 0.022 for males), SDG 15 (Life on Land) (ρ = 0.57; p = 0.001 for children), and SDG 16 (Peace, Justice, and Strong Institutions) (ρ = 0.59; p = 0.001 for males; ρ = 0.45; p = 0.018 for females; ρ = 0.53; p = 0.002 for children). The coordination component of fitness exhibited very strong, positive correlations with SDG 5, SDG 11, and SDG 16 (ρ = 0.85; p = 0.034 for both males and females across all tested relations). Speed showed very strong, positive correlations with the overall SDG index, SDG 3, SDG 5, SDG 9, SDG 11, and SDG 16 (ρ = 0.83; p = 0.042 for both males and females across all tested relations). Endurance demonstrated a weak, positive correlation with SDG 3 (ρ = 0.56; p = 0.047 for males). Flexibility component was not significantly correlated with any SDG examined (Tables 2 and 3).

Our ecological analyses suggest that the fitness components strength, coordination, and speed were positively associated with countries’ SDG achievement scores across sex and age strata, indicating that children and adolescents in contexts further advanced on health (SDG 3), gender equity (SDG 5), infrastructure (SDG 9), urban planning (SDG 11), and governance (SDG 16) experienced smaller declines in physical fitness during the COVID-19 period. These findings align with the WHO Global Action Plan on Physical Activity,3 which articulated a whole-of-society approach across four strategic pillars (active societies, active environments, active people, and active systems), to expand everyday opportunities for movement through intersectoral action in transport, urban design, education, sport, and health.

In line with prior literature, strength in childhood and adolescence is responsive to structured sport and physical activity routines and to access to enabling facilities and resources.9,11,12 Consistent with these relationships, our findings suggest that countries performing better on health (SDG 3), gender equity (SDG 5), infrastructure (SDG 9), urban planning (SDG 11), life on land (SDG 15), and strong institutions (SDG 16), likely offered more routine opportunities for children and adolescents to be active during or immediately after the pandemic period. Speed is a fitness component sensitive to the volume and intensity of practice and responds to structured, school-based interventions13 and to contexts that facilitate active play and recreation, such as safe walking/cycling routes and the availability of nearby parks, playgrounds, fields, and gymnasia, which have been associated with higher adolescent fitness.12 This evidence coheres with the very strong, positive correlations we observed between SMDs for Speed and SDGs 3, 5, 9, 11, and 16, suggesting that stronger performance on these goals creates conditions that maintain or accelerate recovery of speed performance among youth under adverse circumstances. The coordination component in childhood tends to benefit from a broad diversity of movement experiences,14 which is supported by natural environments, safe public spaces, and equitable social climates that expand opportunities and autonomy for outdoor play for both boys and girls.15,25 This pattern aligns with the very strong, positive correlations we observed between SMDs for Coordination and SDGs 5 and 16, indicating that equity, favorable urban design, and safe, trustworthy institutions help generate the everyday conditions required for the development of coordinative skills.

The modest association between endurance and SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being) and the absence of relationships for flexibility with the SDGs are plausible. Cardiorespiratory capacity depends on regular aerobic routines,19,20 yet it was likely more vulnerable to the prolonged disruption of school- and sport-based activities during the pandemic period. In contrast, flexibility tends to be more strongly determined by biological characteristics and specific practice modalities,19,20 which were less available or prioritized during the pandemic, limiting opportunities for measurable gains.

In our analyses, we observed a moderate negative correlation between muscular strength among children and SDG 13 (Climate Action), as well as very strong negative correlations between speed among boys and girls and SDG 13 (Climate Action). At first glance, these findings may appear paradoxical, yet they are consistent with recent evidence examining the relationship between SDG 13 (Climate Action) targets and physical activity indicators in children and adolescents.5 Data from the Global Matrix of the Active Healthy Kids Global Alliance (AHKGA) showed that the best physical activity indicators among youth in 57 countries/jurisdictions were recorded in localities with poorer performance on SDG 13 (Climate Action).5 Moreover, those countries/jurisdictions with higher grades for physical activity were predominantly high-income and followed a neoliberal economic orientation which, while potentially boosting infrastructure and other dimensions aligned with various SDGs, also tends to stimulate consumption and increase atmospheric pollutant emissions, reflecting lower climate responsibility.5 In sum, the inverse pattern between physical fitness performance and SDG 13 (Climate Action) may reflect a structural trade-off whereby contexts that strongly facilitate youth practice and performance do not necessarily prioritize climate mitigation and adaptation policies to the same extent.

Final thoughts

This opinion piece proposes a theoretical rationale situating youth health-related physical fitness within the SDG systems framing. We outlined SDG-specific mechanisms such as health governance, gender equity and inclusive physical education and sport, quality infrastructure and innovation, safe/green urban design, climate integration and adaptation, ecosystem services, and safe, just institutions, through which policies and environments can translate into physical fitness adaptations and richer movement repertoires in childhood and adolescence. This theoretical scaffold justifies treating health-related physical fitness as both an outcome and a near-term determinant of population well-being, and as a policy-sensitive marker aligned with multiple SDGs.

Building on that framework, our ecological analyses showed positive associations of strength, coordination, and speed with performance on SDGs 3, 5, 9, 11, and 16, and inverse associations with SDG 13 (Climate Action). These findings should be interpreted with the usual caveats (ecological design, reliance on national indices, and the concentration of fitness data in the WHO European Region), yet they yield actionable signals: (a) Countries should integrate Global Action Plan on Physical Activity (GAPPA)-aligned physical-activity promotion into SDG implementation with explicit health-related physical fitness targets, such as age- and sex-specific benchmarks for cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness embedded in routine health surveillance; (b) They should close gender and access gaps by ensuring gender-inclusive physical education and sport, safe and green active-mobility infrastructure, opportunities for outdoor active play, and institutional safeguards that raise the dose and quality of physical activity and, in turn, improve fitness components; and (c) They should advance climate policies that simultaneously improve both the behavioral and non-behavioral determinants of health-related physical fitness.
