Assessment of Noninvasive Ear Molding: A Classification‐Guided Approach
Authors: Lanzhen Cui, Xiaoming Li, Yongqing Zhou, Cong Xu, Kun Chen, Jing Xu, Sumin Li, Li Li, Baojiao Lu, Yingfen Wei, Qi Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo systematically evaluate the clinical application and long‐term efficacy of ear molding devices and to explore future treatment strategies for auricular morphological deformity (AMD).MethodsClinical data of patients with AMDs who visited our department between 2017 and 2022 were retrospectively collected. This study included 102 cases who underwent noninvasive ear molding correction and were followed up for more than 3 years. The correction of different types of AMDs—such as prominent ear, cup ear, cryptotia, and lop ear—was analyzed in detail. Correction outcomes were recorded.ResultsThe overall success rate of correction was 85.29%. No significant differences were found in total wear time among different deformity types. Similarly, no significant differences were observed in correction outcomes across different morphological types of AMDs. The age at treatment initiation was positively correlated with total wear time. Both the age at treatment initiation and total wear time influenced the correction outcomes. Age ≤ 7 days and total correction time ≤ 28 days were independently associated with better treatment outcomes, each exerting a significant and non‐confounding effect on prognosis. Notably, only one case developed relatively severe eczema, which interrupted device wear for approximately 1 week; this complication was not associated with the use of domestic or imitation correction devices.ConclusionWhile different correction approaches may vary in their application across deformity types, the long‐term correction outcomes are comparable among different morphological classifications. The final outcome is influenced by the age at which correction is initiated and the total wear time. Only a small proportion of patients experienced complications that affected device wear. Noninvasive ear molding is an effective treatment modality with favorable long‐term outcomes.Level of Evidence4.

Introduction

Auricular morphological deformity (AMD), encompassing conditions such as lop ear, prominent ear, cup ear, cryptotia, and Stahl's ear, is more prevalent than structural malformations. These deformities not only disrupt the natural aesthetic harmony of the auricle but may also exert varying degrees of negative psychological impact on affected children. While some AMD may self‐correct [1], most require early intervention. Untreated AMD may necessitate surgical correction, typically performed at age 6–7 years in childhood. Auricular cartilage is fully mature, rendering cartilage molding unfeasible beyond infancy. The preexisting deformity can also cause significant and irreversible psychological distress. If not addressed through early intervention, subsequent auricular surgery represents one of the most complex challenges in facial plastic surgery [2]. Ear molding devices can mitigate the psychological burden on both children and their families [3]. They eliminate surgical trauma and associated distress, reduce the procedural and financial burdens inherent in multistage surgical interventions, and often achieve superior aesthetic outcomes compared to surgery.

Materials and Methods

Study Subjects

A retrospective review was conducted on pediatric patients diagnosed with AMD at the Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery outpatient clinic of Bethune International Peace Hospital between January 2017 and December 2022. Clinical data were analyzed based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Inclusion criteria were (1) age ≤ 12 weeks at presentation; (2) presence of an auricular shape anomaly or minor structural deformity; (3) intact skin over the auricle and surrounding area, without eczema or lesions; (4) absence of external auditory canal or middle ear pathologies; (5) availability of follow‐up data for more than 3 years post‐correction.

Exclusion criteria were (1) age > 12 weeks at presentation; (2) microtia Grades II or III; and (3) poor compliance with the follow‐up protocol.

A total of 102 infants who underwent noninvasive ear molding correction were included. Among them, 24 cases presented with bilateral involvement, while the remaining 54 cases were unilateral (28 left ears and 26 right ears). The spectrum of deformities included prominent ear, cryptotia, lop ear, Stahl's ear, cup ear, conchal hypertrophy, helical deformities (including cutaneous‐cartilaginous adhesion of the helical rim), combined/complex deformities, and other minor variants. Complete demographic and follow‐up information was available for all subjects. Two cases of Stahl's ear and three of conchal crus were excluded due to loss to long‐term follow‐up after the study period; all other types were included. The cohort comprised 40 males and 38 females. The age at treatment initiation ranged from 2 to 77 days, with a mean of 24.28 days. The total treatment duration ranged from 20 to 57 days, averaging 33.34 days.

Written informed consent was obtained from the guardians of all participants. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Bethune International Peace Hospital (Ethics approval no.: 2021‐KY‐69).

Experimental Methodology

The auricular orthosis system (e.g., Earwell, USA; Liang'er, China; or other comparable devices) was employed, comprising a base support, a cover, a helical retractor, a conchal cavity shaping sponge, and a conchal cavity corrector. The device was affixed to the periauricular skin using hypoallergenic adhesive tape.

Following hair removal from the skin surrounding the affected ear, the non‐invasive corrective ear model scaffold was positioned according to the classification‐based treatment protocol. Subsequently, the helical retractor, conchal cavity sponge, or conchal cavity corrector was sequentially installed (Figure 1). Post‐application, weekly outpatient follow‐ups were conducted. The observation window was opened to assess corrective progress, document outcomes photographically, and record therapeutic efficacy. The skin was examined for complications such as dermatitis or eczema. After cleansing the skin surface, the orthosis was re‐secured, with adjustments made to the traction vector and force as necessary. Throughout the treatment period, parents were instructed to monitor the orthosis closely. Immediate medical consultation was mandated upon detection of device detachment, eczema, erythema, skin breakdown, or abnormal discharge. The orthosis was to be removed for appropriate management if necessary, with resumption of wear after complete skin recovery.

Efficacy Evaluation Criteria

Treatment duration: The period from initial orthosis application to treatment conclusion was recorded. For cryptotia, lop ear, and cutaneous‐cartilaginous adhesion of the helical rim, treatment was discontinued after 2 weeks of continued wear following the achievement of a satisfactory outcome for the family, followed by a 1‐week observation period. Treatment was deemed complete if no recoil occurred; recoil warranted a 1–2 week extension. For cupped ear and prominent ears, treatment ensured continuous wear for at least 4–8 weeks. Treatment concluded if no recoil was observed at a 1‐week post‐discontinuation follow‐up or if no improvement was evident after 4 weeks of correction. Therapeutic outcome: Pretreatment and posttreatment photographs were evaluated independently by one to two experienced attending otorhinolaryngologists. Outcomes were classified into three grades: marked improvement or cure (essentially normal appearance), effective (improvement compared to pretreatment but not achieving normal appearance), and ineffective (no improvement compared to pretreatment). Efficacy rate = (marked improvement/cure + effective)/total cases × 100%. Recoil rate = recoil cases/total cases × 100%. Long‐term efficacy: Patients with over 3 years since orthosis wear were followed up. Two experienced attending otorhinolaryngologists compared pretreatment auricular morphology with the current state, using the same three‐grade classification system for immediate outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS Statistics 20. Categorical data were compared using the chi‐square test, with Fisher's exact test applied when theoretical frequencies were less than 1. Correlations between continuous variables were assessed using Pearson correlation analysis. Comparison of total treatment duration across different groups was performed using one‐way analysis of variance (one‐way ANOVA). A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Classification of AMDs

A total of 78 patients (102 ears) with AMDs were included. The distribution of deformity types was as follows: prominent ear (6 cases), cryptotia (3 cases), lop ear (6 cases), cup ear (12 cases), Stahl's ear (0 cases), helical deformities (54 cases), conchal crus (0 cases), and combined deformities (21 cases). Combined deformities presented as various combinations, such as helical deformity with cup ear and cryptotia with lop ear (Figure 2). All infants received ear correctors from various manufacturers, all of which replicated the design of the EarWell system. The choice of brand did not affect treatment outcomes, and the domestically produced devices were not associated with any severe complications.

Comparable Short‐Term and Long‐Term Correction Efficacy Across Deformity Types

All 102 patients completed the treatment course and follow‐up. At the 1‐week posttreatment assessment, based on the improvement in auricular morphology, 75 ears (73.53%) achieved marked improvement or cure, 17 ears (16.67%) were graded as effective, and 10 ears (9.80%) were ineffective, yielding an overall efficacy rate of 90.20% (Table 1). The follow‐up after 3 years showed 74 ears (72.55%) were assessed as marked improvement/cure, 13 ears (12.75%) as effective, and 15 ears (14.71%) as ineffective, resulting in a long‐term efficacy rate of 85.29% (Table 2). Comparative analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in therapeutic outcomes among the different deformity types. Only two patients transitioned from excellent/effective to ineffective outcomes over the 3‐year follow‐up period. Notably, only two patients (1.96%) showed deterioration from excellent or effective outcomes immediately posttreatment to ineffective outcomes at the 3‐year follow‐up, indicating that the vast majority of initial treatment results were maintained long‐term.

Long‐Term Correction Efficacy Varied With Initiation Age and Total Wearing Duration

Patients were stratified into three groups based on age at treatment initiation: Group 1 (≤ 7 days), Group 2 (≤ 30 days), and Group 3 (30–80 days). Statistical analysis of treatment outcomes indicated that initiation of treatment within 30 days after birth yielded significantly superior results compared to initiation beyond 30 days. However, no significant difference was observed between initiation within 7 days and initiation between 7 and 30 days (Table 3). Furthermore, a significant difference in therapeutic efficacy was noted between patients with a total wearing duration of ≤ 28 days and those with > 28 days (Table 4).

Tables 3 and 4 analyzed age at treatment onset and total correction time in isolation, and we acknowledge the potential interdependence between these two variables. Older infants often require longer treatment durations, which could introduce confounding bias in our initial univariate analysis. To address this concern and to assess the independent associations of these factors with the outcomes, we have now performed a multivariable logistic regression analysis. After adjusting for total correction time, age ≤ 7 days remained significantly associated with better outcomes (OR = 7.62, 95% CI: 1.35–43.10; p = 0.022), while the association for age 7–30 days was not statistically significant (OR = 2.44, 95% CI: 0.93–6.36; p = 0.069). Low Incidence of Complications During Follow‐Up

Complications occurred in 37 ears, with an overall incidence of 36.27%. These included pressure ulcers (21 cases), skin furuncles (5 cases), eczema (3 cases), device detachment (rarely), and recoil (14 cases). Among eczema cases, one was moderate‐to‐severe and two were mild (Figure 4). Recoil was observed in 14 ears; improvement was achieved in 7 of these ears after resuming orthosis wear for consolidation therapy. Device detachment is infrequent. In the rare cases where it occurs, prompt reapplication—either later the same day or by the following day—does not compromise the overall treatment outcome (Table 7).

Discussion

The incidence of neonatal AMD is reported to be 55.2%–57.5% [4]. While some studies report a high incidence of AMD [5, 6], this may reflect increased awareness and diagnostic capabilities. Research indicates a rising global incidence of AMD, which adversely affects the quality of life of affected infants. The precise etiology remains unclear. The external ear typically possesses sufficient skin and cartilage; deformities often arise from abnormal muscular development or external forces, such as intrauterine or birth canal pressure. Deformities with a genetic component may also result from aberrant attachment or absence of intrinsic auricular muscles, leading to insufficient or abnormal folding of the cartilage and consequent auricular distortion.

This study summarizes classification‐based treatment methods utilizing orthotic devices. Prominent ear: The core anatomical issues are a flattened antihelix and an excessively deep conchal cavity. The correction focuses on posteriorly repositioning the helix to form the antihelix. Early detection and intervention are crucial, and the treatment duration is typically prolonged. In our practice, one 3‐year‐old child underwent orthotic treatment at the family's strong request. Despite good cartilage pliability, the deformity recurred within 1 week after 2 months of treatment and device removal. Cryptotia: The key problem is the superior pole of the auricle being embedded in the scalp [7]. Correction involves outward and upward traction on the helix to fully exteriorize the embedded cartilage. A traction hook is usually applied for 1–2 weeks, followed by an orthosis to prevent recurrence. The direction and force of traction are critical. Lop ear: This involves the collapse of the superior portion of the helical rim. Correction aims to lift the helix upward to open the superior auriculocephalic sulcus. The traction must fully elevate the drooping helical rim. Cup ear: Characterized by a constricted helical rim, shortened vertical height, and upturned lobule. Correction requires superolateral traction to elongate the helix and posterolateral expansion of the concha. Sustained traction force is essential, and correction is often challenging. It is frequently associated with other deformities like a lop ear or cutaneous‐cartilaginous adhesion of the helical rim. Stahl's ear: Features an abnormal third crus (superior crus of the antihelix). Correction applies pressure over the aberrant ridge to create a normal fold. Flattening the abnormal cartilaginous ridge is the focus. Helical deformities (including folding and cutaneous‐cartilaginous adhesion of the helical rim): Caused by cartilaginous or cutaneous folds. Correction involves continuous fixation to “unfold” and reshape the curled or constricted helix using physical force. Mild cases may be corrected using a traction hook alone without the base scaffold. Conchal crus: Involves abnormal fullness on the posterior aspect of the conchal cartilage. A conchal corrector is used to depress the fullness and expand the conchal cavity. Combined deformities: These are common, presenting with various combinations of the above issues. Treatment requires a combination of techniques and individualized approaches, demanding significant clinical experience.

Based on the authors' clinical experience, no obvious differences in efficacy or complication rates were observed between patients treated with domestic devices and those treated with the imported EarWell system. Proper application during treatment and management of adverse reactions appeared more critical for outcomes. A meta‐analysis [8] reported an 89.1% efficacy rate and an 11.4% complication rate (9.0% skin lesions, 3.4% dermatitis) for the EarWell system. Complications observed in our cohort included pressure ulcers, skin furuncles, eczema, device detachment, and recoil. Pressure ulcers: The most common adverse reaction, usually mild, related to application technique or prolonged local pressure. Management involves thorough disinfection, air‐drying, and reapplication while avoiding excessive pressure on the affected area. Rash/skin furuncles: A rash may occur due to improper care or high ambient temperature. Furuncles were infrequent and treated with topical erythromycin. Eczema: Occasional cases were observed. Severe eczema necessitating treatment discontinuation was rare (one case in this study) and showed no clear association with device origin. Etiology may involve the infant's immune system and feeding posture, as milk flowing into the periauricular area during feeding can contribute. Proper feeding instruction is important. Odor noted during follow‐up was often linked to residual milk. Device detachment: Uncommon. Parents were advised to reinforce loose tape temporarily and seek professional reapplication the next day. Recoil: Possible in all deformity types. We recommend continuing orthosis wear for 2 weeks after achieving normal morphology, followed by a 1‐week observation period. Recurrence warrants resumed wear. This protocol significantly reduced late recoil rates. Prominent ears showed a higher long‐term recoil rate, possibly related to cartilage composition [9]. Prolonged treatment did not affect the final outcome.

Current orthotic materials are effective for correcting most auricular deformities. Some scholars [10] suggest further research is needed to determine the optimal wearing duration. Our study confirmed a positive correlation between total wearing time and age at treatment initiation, with both factors influencing efficacy. Initiation within the first week of life yielded better outcomes. The findings demonstrate that age ≤ 7 days and total correction time ≤ 28 days are independent prognostic factors for superior treatment outcomes. Notably, the beneficial effects of early treatment and short duration were independent of one another, underscoring their respective contributions to clinical efficacy. Later initiation required appropriately extended wearing time, but total duration was not associated with deformity type. Overall, auricular orthoses proved effective and safe for correcting AMD.

Importantly, while our multivariable analysis identified age under 7 days and treatment duration under 28 days as independent predictors of favorable outcomes, this association should be interpreted with caution. Infants requiring prolonged molding courses are more likely to present with more severe or complex deformities that respond less completely to noninvasive treatment. Therefore, shorter treatment duration is likely a marker of milder initial deformity rather than a direct driver of success. This distinction is clinically important: our findings should not be misinterpreted as evidence that truncating treatment improves outcomes. Rather, treatment duration should be tailored to individual response, especially in more severe cases where longer molding may still be beneficial. Clinicians should avoid shortening therapy based solely on this association and should instead guide treatment duration according to the infant's individualized response to molding, particularly in more complex deformities.
