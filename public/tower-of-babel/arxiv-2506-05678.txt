Numerical Investigation of Sequence Modeling Theory using Controllable Memory Functions
Haotian Jiang, Zeyu Bao, Shida Wang, Qianxiao Li

Abstract
The evolution of sequence modeling architectures, from recurrent neural networks and convolutional models to Transformers and structured state-space models, reflects ongoing efforts to address the diverse temporal dependencies inherent in sequential data. Despite this progress, systematically characterizing the strengths and limitations of these architectures remains a fundamental challenge. In this work, we propose a synthetic benchmarking framework to evaluate how effectively different sequence models capture distinct temporal structures. The core of this approach is to generate synthetic targets, each characterized by a memory function and a parameter that determines the strength of temporal dependence. This setup allows us to produce a continuum of tasks that vary in temporal complexity, enabling fine-grained analysis of model behavior concerning specific memory properties. We focus on four representative memory functions, each corresponding to a distinct class of temporal structures. Experiments on several sequence modeling architectures confirm existing theoretical insights and reveal new findings. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method in advancing theoretical understanding and highlight the importance of using controllable targets with clearly defined structures for evaluating sequence modeling architectures.



1
Introduction

Sequence modeling is a fundamental task in machine learning,
with applications in natural language processing (NLP),
time-series forecasting,
and the modeling of dynamical systems.
These tasks often involve capturing diverse temporal structures,
requiring specialized architectures to model the underlying patterns effectively.
To address this, a wide range of sequence modeling architectures have been developed,
including recurrent neural networks (RNNs)
(
Rumelhart et al. 1986
)
,
convolutional models like WaveNet
(
van den Oord et al. 2016
)
,
Transformers
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
,
and structured state-space models (SSMs)
(
Gu et al. 2022a
;
Orvieto et al. 2023
)
.
However, systematically characterizing the strengths and limitations of these architectures remains challenging.
Without clear theoretical guidance,
model improvement often relies on scaling up architectures with more data and computing,
which can be both costly and inefficient.
A deeper theoretical understanding can offer more principled approaches,
providing insights into how different architectures handle temporal dependencies
and potentially leading to more efficient model designs.

The current approach for evaluating sequence models often involves training them on large, real-world datasets.
For example, benchmarks like Long Range Arena (LRA)
(
Tay et al. 2020
)
and
LongBench
(
Bai et al. 2024
)
are widely used to assess model performance
(see
Appendix

A
for further discussion).
However, these datasets are typically complex and lack clearly defined temporal structures,
making it difficult to draw meaningful conclusions that can improve our understanding
of the implicit biases of different architectures, or how their performance
can generalize to new applications.
For instance, if Model A outperforms Model B on a particular task,
it is often unclear what specific temporal properties contributed to this result.
Without a precise understanding of the temporal structures present in the data,
it is difficult to identify the strengths and limitations of different architectures
in a precise way.

To address this,
we propose a systematic method for evaluating sequence modeling architectures.
Our approach generates families of synthetic sequence targets,
each characterized by a mathematically well-defined memory function
and controlled by a tunable parameter that adjusts the strength of temporal dependence.
This setup creates a continuum of tasks with varying temporal complexity,
allowing for fine-grained analysis of model behavior concerning specific temporal structures.
By varying the strength parameter,
we can test how different architectures respond to increasingly challenging temporal dependencies,
providing deeper insights into their learning capabilities.
This approach offers a flexible framework for studying various theoretical properties,
including approximation, optimization, and generalization.

Experiments using this approach confirm several existing theoretical insights,
while also revealing new behaviors related to approximation bounds, optimization performance, and architectural trade-offs.
These findings highlight the value of using controllable, well-defined targets for evaluating sequence models,
and suggest that this method could serve as a valuable tool for gaining a deeper theoretical understanding of different sequential architectures.

2
Sequence-to-sequence Targets with Controllable Memory Functions

To systematically evaluate the ability of sequence modeling architectures to capture different temporal structures,
we construct synthetic targets based on a family of parametric memory functions.
Each memory function is designed to represent a specific aspect of temporal dependency,
such as decay rate, long-range dependency, or sparsity,
allowing us to study these effects in a controlled setting.

We first define the input-output relationship,
which specifies how past inputs influence current output:

\displaystyle y(t)=H_{t}^{(\alpha)}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\sigma_{1}\left(\sum_{s=0}^{t}\rho(s,\alpha)\,\sigma_{2}\big(x(t-s)\big)\right),\quad t\in\{0,\dots,T\},

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{x}
is the input sequence,

T
is the maximum sequence length,
and
\sigma_{1}
,
\sigma_{2}
are activation functions.
The function
\rho(s,\alpha)
is referred to as the
memory function
.
In the linear case, where
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
are identity maps,
this formulation reduces to a convolution operation,
and
\rho
corresponds to the impulse response in linear time-invariant (LTI) systems,
which is well-established in control theory (see, e.g.,
Oppenheim et al. 1996
).

We define a memory function
\rho
as a function that maps a discrete time index
s
and a controllable parameter
\alpha
to a real-valued weight:

\rho:\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}\times[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.

The parameter
\alpha
controls the strength of the temporal dependency,
with larger values corresponding to stronger memory effects.
To facilitate controlled experiments, we focus on a scalar input-output setting,
where both the memory function
\rho
and the input sequence are scalar-valued.
Further theoretical details regarding memory functions are provided in
Appendix

B
.

In this work, we consider four representative memory functions with distinct temporal characteristics:

exponential
and
polynomial
decay (
\rho_{\text{exp}}
and
\rho_{\text{poly}}
, capturing memory decay speed),

impulse
(
\rho_{\delta}
, capturing dependency range),
and
Airy
(
\rho_{\text{Ai}}
, capturing sparsity).
Each memory function is paired with a strictly increasing
scaling function

\mu
that maps the parameter
\alpha
to an appropriate range,
ensuring that larger values of
\alpha
correspond to stronger memory effects.
The form of the scaling function is chosen based on the specific temporal properties being captured,
and further details are provided in
Section

3.2
.

Exponential and Polynomial Decay

We begin by considering memory functions with decaying behavior over time, specifically the exponential and polynomial memory functions.
Both capture the idea that recent inputs have a greater influence on the output than earlier ones,
but differ in the rate at which this influence decays.

The exponential memory function is defined as:

\rho_{\text{exp}}(s,\alpha)=\exp\left(-\frac{s}{\mu_{\text{exp}}(\alpha)}\right),\quad\mu_{\text{exp}}(\alpha):[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}.

(2)

The polynomial memory function is defined as:

\rho_{\text{poly}}(s,\alpha)=(1+s)^{-\frac{1}{\mu_{\text{poly}}(\alpha)}},\quad\mu_{\text{poly}}(\alpha):[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}.

(3)

As
\alpha
increases, the decay becomes slower,
resulting in stronger dependencies on past inputs.

Impulse

In contrast to decay-based functions,
the impulse memory function captures long-range dependencies,
where the output depends on a specific distant input.
The impulse memory function is defined as:

\displaystyle\rho_{\delta}(s,\alpha)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }s=\mu_{\delta}(\alpha),\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},\quad\mu_{\delta}(\alpha):[0,1]\to{\mathbb{N}}_{\geq 0}.

(4)

In this case, the output at time
t
depends solely on a single input value at a specific offset:

y(t)=\sigma_{1}\circ\sigma_{2}\Big(x\big(t-\mu_{\delta}(\alpha)\big)\Big).

As
\alpha
increases, the output is determined by inputs further in the past,
capturing the effect of long-range memory.

Airy

In addition to decay speed and dependency range,
we also consider sparsity as a critical factor in temporal structure.
The notion of sparsity for memory functions, as discussed in
Jiang et al. 2021
,
is typically defined using a binary criterion,
where any nonzero value is considered non-sparse.
While the exponential, polynomial, and impulse memory functions exhibit fixed sparsity regardless of
\alpha
,
we consider a truncated version of the Airy function of the first kind,
where the degree of sparsity is directly controlled by the parameter
\alpha
.
We define the truncated Airy function as

\displaystyle\text{Ai}_{\text{tr}}(s)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos\left(\frac{v^{3}}{3}+vs\right)\,dv&s<20\\
0&s\geq 20\end{cases}\,,

(5)

where the truncation at
s=20
is applied because the Airy function decays exponentially fast for positive
s
,
and its magnitude becomes negligibly small beyond this point.
Using this, we define the memory function as

\rho_{\text{Ai}}(s,\alpha)=\text{Ai}_{\text{tr}}\big(\mu_{\text{Ai}}\left(s-c\cdot\alpha\right)\big),\quad\mu_{\text{Ai}}:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0},

(6)

where
c>0
is a scaling factor.
As
\alpha
increases, the nonzero region of the function shifts outward,
reducing the effective sparsity of the memory structure.

Figure

5
provides an overview of the four representative memory functions, illustrating their distinct temporal structures.

3
Systematic Evaluation of Sequential Architectures

In this section, we introduce a systematic approach to evaluate how different types of temporal structures affect the theoretical properties of sequential architectures.
We then present experimental results on various important classes of sequential models to demonstrate this method.

3.1
Methodology

Approximation, optimization, and generalization are three key aspects in machine learning theory.

Approximation
refers to a model’s ability to accurately capture a target’s underlying structure.
In practice, we use the final training loss to estimate the approximation error bound,
under the condition that the model does not overfit (More details can be found in
Section

B.1
).

Optimization
focuses on the model’s ability to minimize the loss function during training effectively.
This can be evaluated by measuring the number of training steps required to reach a given loss threshold,
and by examining the distribution of final losses across different random seeds to assess sensitivity to initialization.

Generalization
, which refers to the model’s ability to perform well on unseen data,
can be studied by varying the size of the training dataset and measuring how the test loss changes as a function of data availability.

In this work, we primarily focus on the approximation aspect due to the
availability of existing theoretical studies, but
we also present some interesting phenomena observed in optimization.
A detailed analysis of generalization is deferred to future work.

Evaluating the Impact of Temporal Structure

The core idea of our approach is to analyze how the performance of a model is influenced by different target temporal structures.
To do this, we fix the model size and train on targets generated with different values of
\alpha
for each memory function.
This allows us to systematically assess how the model’s properties,
such as its approximation error and its sensitivity to initializations,
change as the temporal strength increases.

For example, to evaluate approximation properties,
we can plot the loss as a function of
\alpha
,
after first ensuring that no overfitting occurs,
so that the loss accurately reflects the model’s approximation error.
Comparing these curves across different memory functions reveals cases where a model is more influenced by one type of temporal structure than another.
This approach also allows for qualitative comparisons between models,
revealing which architectures are better suited for particular temporal structures and which struggle under certain conditions.

This approach provides a framework for evaluating sequence models,
allowing for both quantitative and qualitative comparisons of their theoretical properties.
This not only helps validate existing theoretical insights,
but also reveals new empirical phenomena that may guide future theoretical developments.

3.2
Scaling Methodology for Proper Comparison

In this section, we discuss several practical considerations for implementing the proposed methodology,
including scaling function definitions and loss scaling.
These are critical to ensuring consistent and meaningful evaluation across different memory functions,
sequence lengths, and the memory strength parameters
\alpha
.
More detailed experimental settings can be found in
Appendix

D
.

Scaling Function Definitions

Recall from
Section

2
that each type of memory function is paired with a scaling function
\mu

that maps the parameter
\alpha
to an appropriate range, controlling the temporal strength.
These scaling functions are not arbitrary and must be carefully chosen to ensure that the target retains the intended temporal structure,
even for different sequence lengths
T
used in practice.

Since both the exponential memory function
\rho_{\text{exp}}
and the polynomial memory function
\rho_{\text{poly}}
consider decaying properties over time,
it is important to compare them on a consistent scale.
To achieve this, we define the scaling function
\mu_{\text{exp}}(\alpha)
such that for all
\alpha
, both functions have the same total area under the curve over
[0,T]
,
ensuring that their relative decay rates can be directly compared:

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{T}\rho_{\text{exp}}(s,\mu_{\text{exp}}(\alpha))=\sum_{s=0}^{T}\rho_{\text{poly}}(s,\mu_{\text{poly}}(\alpha)).

(7)

This normalization removes the effect of the overall scale,
allowing us to isolate and compare the impact of different decay speeds accurately.

For the polynomial memory function
\rho_{\text{poly}}
, the scaling function is defined as

\displaystyle\mu_{\text{poly}}(\alpha)=\alpha\cdot\alpha_{\text{max}},

where
\alpha_{\text{max}}
is chosen such that the memory contribution from the tail is negligible for finite input lengths
T
,
satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{s=T+1}^{\infty}\rho_{\text{poly}}(s,\alpha_{\text{max}})=\epsilon,

where
\epsilon
is a small threshold controlling the allowed influence from the tail.

For the impulse memory function
\rho_{\delta}
,
we define the scaling function as

\displaystyle\mu_{\delta}(\alpha)=\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha T}{2}\right\rfloor,

which ensures that the maximum shifted distance
\mu_{\delta}(1)
does not exceed half of the input sequence length.
This constraint is necessary because the output is truncated at
T
,
and we must retain enough information within the output sequence for the model to effectively learn the target dependencies.

For the Airy memory function,
we define
\mu_{\text{Ai}}(\alpha)
such that

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{Ai}}(s,\alpha)=\text{Ai}_{\text{tr}}\left(40\left(\frac{s}{T}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\right),

ensuring that as
\alpha
increases, the high-magnitude region of the function shifts outward,
reducing the sparsity of the memory function,
as illustrated in
Figure

5(d)
.

Loss Scaling

One important consideration when comparing models on the same memory function across different
\alpha
values is the variation in output scale.
For a given memory function
\rho(s,\alpha)
, the output scale can vary significantly with
\alpha
.
For example, for the impulse memory function, the output for a large
\alpha
will have a much smaller scale than the output for a smaller
\alpha
,
because the sequence is truncated and zero-padded, reducing its overall magnitude.
To account for this, we use a relative mean squared error (RMSE), defined as

\text{RMSE}=\frac{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}|\hat{y}(t)-y(t)|^{2}}{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}|y(t)|^{2}},

(8)

where
T
is the sequence length.
This approach normalizes the loss by the average energy of the true output sequence,
making the loss comparable across different memory functions and values of
\alpha
.
Note that based on the definition of the target generation, the denominator is always positive,
ensuring that the RMSE is well-defined.

Observation 1
.

Consistent with existing theoretical understanding,
the approximation behavior of the recurrent architectures is strongly influenced by the decay properties of
\rho_{\text{exp}}
and
\rho_{\text{poly}}
,
as well as the dependency range of the impulse memory function
\rho_{\delta}
.

As shown in
Figure

,
both LSTM and S4D maintain low and stable loss across varying values of
\alpha

\rho_{\text{exp}}
.
This indicates that these architectures are well-suited for approximating sequences with rapidly decaying temporal dependencies.
This behavior is supported by the theoretical analysis in
Miller & Hardt 2019
;
Li et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2023
,
which demonstrates that recurrent architectures can efficiently approximate targets with exponential memory decay.
Additionally,
Wang & Xue 2023
shows that state-space models with layer-wise nonlinearity also adapt naturally to exponential memory structures.

However, under the polynomial memory function
\rho_{\text{poly}}
,
both models exhibit a noticeable increase in loss as
\alpha
increases, as shown in
Figures

1(b)
and
1(e)
.
This suggests a significant limitation when approximating slowly decaying temporal structures,
where past inputs retain a non-negligible influence over long timescales.
This observation is consistent with the findings of
Zhao et al. 2020
,
which shows that RNNs and LSTMs struggle to capture polynomial decaying dependencies without explicit architectural modifications.
Similarly,
Li et al. 2022
demonstrates that linear recurrent models face fundamental approximation limitations under slowly decaying memory.

Wang & Li 2024
further argues that standard state-space models also suffer from this limitation by default,
and proposes a stable reparameterization to improve their long-range dependency handling.

Finally, when evaluated on the impulse memory function
\rho_{\delta}
,
both LSTM and S4D show a rapid increase in loss as
\alpha
grows, as shown in
Figures

1(c)
and
1(f)
.
This highlights a severe limitation in modeling sparse, long-range dependencies,
where the target depends on a single distant input.
This difficulty is investigated in

Bengio et al. 1994
;
Li et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2023
showing that recurrent architectures struggle to approximate sparse, isolated dependencies.
Empirical evidence supporting this limitation is also provided in
Bai et al. 2018
.

Observation 3
.

The approximation behavior of TCNs cannot be solely characterized by sparsity.

As shown in
Figure

2(c)
,
TCNs exhibit increased approximation error under the Airy memory function
\rho_{\text{Ai}}
.
In this case,
larger values of
\alpha
reduce the sparsity and
making the target function more challenging to approximate.
In contrast, the impulse memory function
\rho_{\delta}
remains maximally sparse regardless of
\alpha
,
leading to stable approximation performance as shown in
Figure

2(d)
.
This aligns with theoretical insights from
Jiang et al. 2021
,
which shows that TCNs are more effective at approximating targets with higher sparsity,
where each channel can be utilized to represent a single nonzero value of the memory function.

However, as shown in
Figures

2(a)
and
2(b)
,
the performance of TCNs also degrades as
\alpha
increases under both exponential and polynomial memory functions.
This behavior cannot be fully explained by conventional binary sparsity measures,
since both
\rho_{\text{exp}}
and
\rho_{\text{poly}}
maintain fixed sparsity regardless of
\alpha
,
which would predict constant error across different
\alpha
values.
This suggests that a more precise notion of complexity is required to capture the observed trend.

To address this,
we propose a more flexible notion of
tail energy
that accounts for the magnitude decay of the memory function,
rather than relying solely on a strict binary sparsity criterion.
We define this tail energy as

\displaystyle C(\rho,s):=\sum_{t=s}^{T}\left\lvert\rho(\pi(t),\alpha)\right\lvert^{2},

(9)

where the function
\pi:\{0,\ldots,T\}\rightarrow\{0,\ldots,T\}

is a permutation that reorders the values of the memory function
so that the composed function
\rho\circ\pi
is monotone non-increasing.
This measure captures the tail of the memory function after accounting for the largest values.

In
Figures

6
and
7
,
we plot this complexity measure for each memory function.
The results show a clear positive correlation between the complexity measure and the observed loss for
\rho_{\text{exp}}
,
\rho_{\text{poly}}
, and
\rho_{\text{Ai}}
,
indicating that this measure can effectively capture the increasing approximation difficulty for these memory structures.
In contrast, the complexity measure for
\rho_{\delta}
is constant zero,
reflecting the inherently sparse nature of the impulse function,
which aligns with the stable loss observed in this case.
This suggests that the proposed complexity measure provides a meaningful estimate of the approximation bound for TCNs.
While this measure may not yield bounds as tight as the rank-based approach in
Jiang et al. 2021
,
it has the advantage of being straightforward to compute,
making it a practical alternative for characterizing the approximation power of TCNs.

Observation 5
.

The approximation behavior of Transformers on the target form defined in
Equation

1
exhibits a trend similar to that of TCNs.

Figure

3
shows that the loss curves for Transformers across different memory functions exhibit trends that are qualitatively similar to those observed for TCNs.
Specifically, the loss increases with
\alpha
for
\rho_{\text{exp}},\rho_{\text{poly}},
and
\rho_{\text{Ai}}
,
while remaining nearly constant for
\rho_{\delta}
.
This similarity is notable given the fundamental architectural differences between convolutional networks and attention-based models.

Although the observed trends suggest that Transformers exhibit behavior similar to convolutional models,
it is not accurate to conclude that they directly perform convolution.
Several studies, including
Cordonnier et al. 2020
and
Li et al. 2021
,
demonstrate that Transformers equipped with relative position encoding can approximate convolutional kernels,
but this requires a large number of attention heads,
with the required head count scaling with the kernel size.
In contrast, our experiments in
Figure

3
use a fixed number of attention heads (
n_{h}=4
),
suggesting that the observed trends may arise from mechanisms other than directly performing convolution.

From an approximation theory perspective,

Jiang & Li 2024
provides a more consistent interpretation.
It shows that the approximation difficulty of Transformers is closely related to the effective rank of the target function.
When applied to our setting, this implies that the effective rank of the attention matrix increases with
\alpha

for memory functions like
\rho_{\text{exp}}
,
\rho_{\text{poly}}
, and
\rho_{\text{Ai}}
,
while remaining constant for
\rho_{\delta}
.
This is supported by the spectrum plots in
Figure

11
,
which show that the spectrum decays more slowly as
\alpha
increases for the first three functions,
but remains largely unchanged for
\rho_{\delta}
.
This trend aligns with our empirical results, where higher
\alpha
leads to greater approximation difficulty for the first three cases,
but not for
\rho_{\delta}
, which maintains stable performance.

Furthermore,
we observe that different position encodings lead to distinct intrinsic temporal structures in Transformers.
In our main experiments, we use absolute position encoding as described in
Vaswani et al. 2017
.
However, we observe that different position encoding schemes can lead to significantly different model behaviors,
affecting the ability to capture long-range dependencies.
Further analysis of these effects is provided in
Section

E.3
.

Observation 6
.

The trade-off between the number of heads
n_{h}
and the hidden dimension
m
in Transformers
is influenced by both the memory function type and the strength of the temporal dependency.

The trade-off between the number of heads
n_{h}
and the hidden dimension
m

is a fundamental consideration in Transformer architecture.
In common implementations like PyTorch and x-transformers,
the total hidden dimension
m
is typically split evenly across
n_{h}
heads,
resulting in a per-head dimension of
m/n_{h}
.
As the number of heads increases,
the dimension of each head decreases,
potentially reducing the expressive power of each individual head.
This relationship has been explored in the theoretical literature.
For example,
Amsel et al. 2024
investigates a special target function (the nearest neighbor function),
demonstrating that a single head with dimension
m
can efficiently approximate this target,
while a smaller per-head dimension requires exponentially more heads to achieve high approximation quality.

In this work, we aim to investigate how the temporal structure of the target influences this trade-off.
To do this, we fix the total hidden dimension
m
and vary the number of heads
n_{h}
,
resulting in a corresponding decrease in the dimension of each attention head.

Figure

4
shows that this trade-off is strongly influenced by both the memory function type and the strength parameter
\alpha
.
Specifically, for memory functions
\rho_{\text{exp}}
and
\rho_{\text{poly}}
,
we observe that at smaller values of
\alpha
, the loss decreases consistently as
n_{h}
increases,
indicating that a larger number of heads improves approximation.
However, at larger values of
\alpha
, this trend becomes non-monotonic,
with a distinct turning point where further increasing
n_{h}
results in degraded performance.
For the impulse function
\rho_{\delta}
, the loss is non-monotonic across the entire range of
\alpha

suggesting a more complex relationship that is not purely determined by the number of heads.
In contrast, for the Airy function
\rho_{\text{Ai}}
, the loss remains nearly monotonic across all tested values of
\alpha
,
indicating that this memory structure is less sensitive to the trade-off between head count and per-head dimension.

These results indicate that the trade-off between head count and hidden dimension is not solely determined by the memory function type,
but also by the strength of the temporal dependencies.
Investigating how this trade-off depends on the temporal structure of the target is a meaningful direction for future research,
with the potential to provide deeper insights into the expressivity of multi-head attention.

5
Conclusion

This work introduces a synthetic framework for systematically analyzing the theoretical properties of sequence models.
By designing targets with controlled memory patterns,
we isolate key factors that influence model performance.
Within a unified numerical setting,
we evaluate LSTM, S4D, TCN, and Transformer architectures.
Where applicable, our findings align with existing theoretical results,
validating known behaviors in sequence modeling.
In other cases, the observed behaviors, such as the impact of sparsity, position encoding, or the non-monotonic relationship between attention heads and performance, suggest new directions for theoretical investigation.

While much of the existing theoretical literature focuses on linear models and targets,
we find that many of the observed phenomena remain consistent in the nonlinear settings considered here.
This reinforces the value of theoretical investigations as meaningful guides to understanding real-world architectures.

Promising theoretical directions identified through our approach.

•

Novel complexity measure in TCN Approximation.

We propose a complexity measure in
Equation

9

that offers a practical alternative to rank-based approaches for characterizing TCN approximation error bounds.
Further exploration of this measure could provide a deeper understanding of temporal convolutional architectures.

•

Trade-off between the number of heads
n_{h}
and the hidden dimension
m
in Transformers.

We observe a non-monotonic relationship between the number of heads and approximation performance when the hidden dimension of the Transformer is fixed.
Understanding this phenomenon and its dependence on memory structure remains an open theoretical challenge.

•

Effect of Positional Encoding.

The choice of positional encoding significantly impacts performance, particularly under sparse, long-range dependencies.
Clarifying the inductive biases introduced by different encoding schemes is a promising direction for future work.

•

Training Dynamics.

Understanding how target memory structure influences the training dynamics of sequence models remains an important open question.
This includes the impact of optimizer choice and initialization sensitivity.

Each of these directions is directly motivated by the specific memory structures considered in this study,
including
\rho_{\text{exp}}
,
\rho_{\text{poly}}
,
\rho_{\delta}
, and
\rho_{\text{Ai}}
,
and reflect insights that cannot be obtained without a systematic evaluation of how different temporal structures impact model performance.

This systematic framework provides a valuable foundation for evaluating the fundamental theoretical properties of sequence models.
The empirical findings, combined with their theoretical insights,
offer a foundation for future work aimed at bridging the gap between theoretical understanding and practical model design,
potentially leading to more principled and effective sequence architectures.

Appendix A
Related Work on Sequential Model Benchmarks

Long Range Arena
(
Tay et al. 2020
)
was introduced to evaluate the ability of transformers to handle long-context tasks.
It encompasses tasks from various modalities, including CIFAR-10
(
Krizhevsky 2009
)
and IMDb
(
Maas et al. 2011
)
, alongside synthetic tasks like Pathfinder and Pathfinder-X, which are particularly challenging for transformers to solve.
While these tasks are valuable for testing long-term memory, the benchmark poses challenges in interpreting model performance across tasks.
This is primarily due to its lack of flexibility in adjusting task configurations, making it difficult to draw consistent conclusions about a model’s long-context capabilities.
The needle-in-a-haystack (NIAH) test is a widely used task to demonstrate the model’s long-term memory capability.
RULER
(
Hsieh et al. 2024
)
expands the vanilla NIAH test and introduce diverse configurations to test the models’ memory capacity.
Traditional language modeling datasets have long been a natural benchmark for evaluating long-term memory in sequence models.
Datasets such as WikiText
(
Merity et al. 2017
)
are specifically curated to include long passages of text , offering models the opportunity to learn dependencies across sentences and paragraphs.
Similarly, The Pile
(
Gao et al. 2020
)
is a large-scale dataset comprising diverse domains such as books, scientific articles, and code, making it particularly useful for assessing a model’s ability to capture long-range dependencies in realistic and varied contexts.
Another noteworthy dataset is Project Gutenberg (PG-19) (CITE PG-19), which is a collection of books over 100 years old, specifically designed to evaluate models on extremely long contexts. PG-19 provides long passages spanning entire books, making it a valuable resource for testing the ability of models to retain and utilize information across thousands of tokens.
BAMBOO
(
Dong et al. 2024
)
consists of five different tasks and the assessment of long-context capability is mainly focused on downstream tasks.
Efficiently utilizing context is a critical requirement for sequence models, and the LAMBADA benchmark
Paperno et al. 2016
is specifically designed with this goal.
From a human perspective, the task is intentionally challenging when limited to a single-sentence context but becomes relatively straightforward with access to the full-paragraph context.
This design is rooted in an interesting motivation, as it emphasizes the importance of learning long-range dependencies for language understanding.
LongBench
Bai et al. 2024
is an open-source bilingual dataset with the focus on the comparison of different open source models.
The average length of the data is 6,711 words (English) and 13,386 characters (Chinese).

Appendix B
Theoretical Preliminaries

In this section,
we discuss the theoretical preliminaries underlying the definition of the proposed datasets,
building upon the theoretical framework from
Li et al. 2022
;
Jiang et al. 2021
.
Sequential targets are formulated as mappings between function spaces,
enabling a precise mathematical representation of the input-output relationships in sequence modeling.
Specifically,
we define the input space
{\mathcal{X}}
and the output space
{\mathcal{Y}}
as:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{x}:x(t)\in R^{d}\text{ for }t\in{\mathcal{I}}\},

(10)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{y}:y(t)\in R\text{ for }t\in{\mathcal{I}}\}.

(11)

Here,
{\mathcal{I}}
is the index set,
which can be either discrete or continuous depending on specific conditions. Both
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
are functions defined over the index set
{\mathcal{I}}
, with
\boldsymbol{x}
representing the input sequence and
\boldsymbol{y}
the corresponding output sequence.
A sequential target is defined as a mapping
\boldsymbol{H}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}
, where
\boldsymbol{H}
can be considered as an operator between
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
.
For each time point
t
, the input-output relationship can be expressed as:

\displaystyle y(t)=H_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(12)

where
H_{t}:{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
is treated as a functional. Importantly, the output
y(t)
may depend on the entire input sequence
\boldsymbol{x}
, capturing the temporal dependencies inherent in the target.

Below are some properties usually implied on the target relationship.

Properties of target relationships

In order to facilitate meaningful analysis,
it is essential to establish specific properties of the target relationship.
The following four properties are defined,
and their selection will be based on the particular application in later chapters.

•

Linearity & Continuity:

H_{t}\in\boldsymbol{H}
is a continuous linear functional if for any
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\in\mathcal{X}

and
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\begin{gathered}H_{t}(\lambda_{1}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\lambda_{2}\boldsymbol{x}_{2})=\lambda_{1}H_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})+\lambda_{2}H_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}),\\
\left\lVert H_{t}\right\lVert:=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X},\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\right\lVert_{\mathcal{X}}\leq 1}\left\lvert H_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})\right\lvert<\infty,\end{gathered}

(13)

where
\left\lVert H_{t}\right\lVert
denotes the induced functional norm.

•

Causality:

\boldsymbol{H}
is
causal
if it only depend on past inputs:
for any
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
and any
t\in{\mathcal{I}}
such that

x_{1}(s)=x_{2}(s)\ \text{ for all }s\leq t
,
the output satisfies
H_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})=H_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})
.

•

Time-homogeneity:

For a sequence
\boldsymbol{x}
, a shift of
\boldsymbol{x}
by
\tau
is denoted as

x^{(\tau)}(s):=x(s-\tau)
for all
s\in{\mathcal{I}}
.

\boldsymbol{H}
is
time-homogeneous
if
for any
t,\tau\in{\mathcal{I}}
, we have

H_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})=H_{t+\tau}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(\tau)}).

This is a similar idea to a time-invariant system in the context of dynamical system,
where the target relationship does not explicitly depends on time.

Causality and time-homogeneity are fundamental temporal structures inherent to many sequential architectures.
Examples include RNNs, GRUs, SSMs, TCNs, and Transformers equipped with relative position encoding and causal masking,
all of which adhere to these principles.
To construct the dataset,
we assume a linear,
continuous, causal,
and time-homogeneous temporal relationship.
While this assumption simplifies the model,
prior theoretical work has demonstrated that such linear assumptions are sufficient to distinguish sequential relationships.
This allows for clear performance differentiation across various architectures when learning such targets.

Based on Lemma 15 in
Li et al. 2022
,
under appropriate assumptions (See Appendix), a linear,
continuous, causal,
and time-homogeneous temporal relationship have the following representation:

\displaystyle H_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{0}^{t}\rho(s)^{\top}x(t-s)ds,

(14)

where
\rho:[0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a integrable function.

This representation shows that any target
\boldsymbol{H}
satisfying these conditions can be uniquely characterized by a function
\rho
.
This result is utilized to quantify the approximation capabilities of various sequence modeling architectures.
We refer to
\rho
as the
memory function
in this context,
as it encapsulates the temporal structure of the target.

The specific structure of
\rho
generates targets with distinct temporal properties.
For example, it has been shown in
Li et al. 2022
that the performance of RNNs is closely tied to the decay rate and smoothness of
\rho
.
Meanwhile, other works
Jiang et al. 2021
demonstrate that the performance of temporal convolutional networks (TCNs) is influenced by the sparsity of
\rho
.
These findings highlight how the memory function plays a central role in determining the effectiveness of architectures in capturing different temporal structures.

To better reflect the nonlinearities present in practical applications,
we introduce discretization and add nonlinear layers at both the input and output stages,
resulting in the data generation process defined in
Equation

1
.

B.1
Numerical Estimation of Approximation Error Bound

In supervised learning, the approximation error for a target
y
within a hypothesis space
{\mathcal{H}}
is defined as

\displaystyle e=\inf_{\hat{y}\in{\mathcal{H}}}\left\lVert y-\hat{y}\right\lVert,

(15)

which represents the minimum achievable distance between the target function and the hypothesis space.

To numerically estimate this approximation error bound,
we use the final training loss as an upper bound,
under the condition that no overfitting occurs.
This ensures that the model is not simply interpolating the target,
which would invalidate the use of training loss as an approximation error estimate.

In our experiments, we evaluate multiple values of
\alpha
,
and it is important to ensure that the training loss accurately reflects the approximation error for each setting.
This is confirmed by checking that the test loss remains consistent with the training loss,
indicating that the model is not overfitting.
For the parameter settings used in this study, we have verified that no significant overfitting is observed.

Additionally, to account for the stochastic nature of the training process,
we train each model on the same target using multiple random seeds.
We then take the minimum training loss across these runs as an estimate of the approximation error bound,
providing a more robust measure of model performance.

Appendix C
Approximation of the TCN

Based on the complexity measure defined in
Equation

9
,
we provide the following approximation theorem,
it can be directly fit into the setting of
Jiang et al. 2021
,
and the notions in this sections a also adatps there.

Theorem C.1
.

Fix the filter size
l\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
.
Let

M:=\sum_{k=2}^{K}M_{k}M_{k-1}-K
be the effective number of filters.
Then for any set of parameters

(K,\left\{M_{k}\right\})
,
and any
\boldsymbol{H}\in\mathcal{C}^{(l,g)}
, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\boldsymbol{\hat{H}}\in{{\mathcal{H}}}_{\text{CNN}}^{(l,K,\left\{M_{k}\right\})}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{H}-\boldsymbol{\hat{H}}\right\lVert\leq d\,C(\rho,M+1)+{d\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\rho}^{(\boldsymbol{H})}_{[l^{K},\infty]}\right\lVert_{2}},

(16)

where

\displaystyle C(\rho,s):=\sum_{t=s}^{l^{K}-1}\left\lvert\rho(\pi(t))\right\lvert^{2}.

(17)

Proof:
this is a direct consequence of Equation (18) from
Jiang et al. 2021

\displaystyle\left\lvert H_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})-\hat{H}_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})\right\lvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\Big\rvert\boldsymbol{\rho}^{(\boldsymbol{H})}(s)-\boldsymbol{\rho}^{(\boldsymbol{\hat{H}})}(s)\Big\rvert

(18)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{l^{K}-1}\Big\rvert\boldsymbol{\rho}^{(\boldsymbol{H})}(s)-\boldsymbol{\rho}^{(\boldsymbol{\hat{H}})}(s)\Big\rvert+\sum_{s=l^{K}}^{\infty}\Big\rvert\boldsymbol{\rho}^{(\boldsymbol{H})}(s)\Big\rvert.

For the first term we have

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{l^{K}-1}\Big\rvert\boldsymbol{\rho}^{(\boldsymbol{H})}(s)-\boldsymbol{\rho}^{(\boldsymbol{\hat{H}})}(s)\Big\rvert

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{l^{K}-1}\Big\rvert\boldsymbol{\rho}^{(\boldsymbol{H})}(\pi(s))-\boldsymbol{\rho}^{(\boldsymbol{\hat{H}})}(\pi(s))\Big\rvert

(19)

\displaystyle=C(\rho,M+1).

(20)

We use the
M
channels to cover the largest
M
values of
\rho
, and the remainder error is characterized by
C(\rho,M+1)
.

Appendix D
Experiment Settings

We divide the discussion of the experiment settings into three parts: data generation, model parameters, and training settings.
For each memory function and model pair, multiple experimental runs are conducted,
depending on the number of sampled
\alpha
values,
the range of model sizes
m
tested,
and the number of random seeds used.
This approach ensures comprehensive coverage of the parameter space,
capturing both model sensitivity and robustness across different memory structures.

D.1
Data Generation

The most critical parameter in the data generation process is the sequence length
T
.
This choice is influenced by several factors.
The sequence must be long enough to capture the full range of temporal dependencies,
especially for larger values of
\alpha
,
to avoid limitation of the model’s ability to learn long-term patterns.
However, increasing the sequence length also significantly impacts training speed and memory consumption.

To balance these considerations, we set the sequence length to 1024 for LSTM, S4, and TCN,
and to 128 for Transformer models,
reflecting the typically higher memory requirements of attention-based architectures.
These lengths were selected based on preliminary experiments to ensure that models can adequately capture the temporal structures without excessive computational overhead.

Some extra settings:

•

We use double precision for all the experiments.

•

The nonlinear activation function
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
are set to
tanh
.

•

We choose
\alpha_{\text{max}}=0.3
for
\mu_{\text{poly}}
to ensure that the tail sum of
\rho_{\text{poly}}
is sufficiently small. (
10^{-8}
scale)
The corresponding values for
\mu_{\text{exp}}
are solved numerically.

•

The input sequence
x(t)
are i.i.d. random number for all
t
, such that
x(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

D.2
Model Parameters

For each model, we vary the model size to capture the impact of capacity on approximation behavior.
The chosen sizes are designed to be neither too small (where the model cannot learn even for small
\alpha
)
nor too large (where the model can easily fit the target regardless of
\alpha
).
This balance ensures that our experiments can effectively measure the influence of model size on approximation performance.

For TCN, we set the number of layers to
10
to ensure that the receptive field can cover the entire sequence range.
We focus on investigating how the number of channels affects the model’s behavior.

For Transformers, we vary both the hidden dimension and the number of attention heads,
as these two parameters together determine the per-head dimensionality,
which plays a critical role in the observed trade-off behaviors.
This dual-parameter variation allows us to investigate how these architectural choices interact with the memory structure and the target’s temporal properties.

D.3
Training Settings

To ensure a fair comparison, we use the same optimizer and learning rate across all
\alpha
values and memory functions.
Each experiment is also repeated across multiple random seeds to reduce the impact of initialization variance and stochastic optimization dynamics.

An important consideration is the choice of batch size.
Since our training data is generated from simple random distributions,
even small batch sizes can provide accurate gradient estimates with low noise.
This is confirmed in
Figure

15
,
which shows that gradients computed with smaller batches have high cosine similarity with those computed using a full dataset,
indicating that smaller batches are sufficient for stable training.

D.4
Computational Resources

The proposed framework involves a large number of experiments,
given the need to evaluate different model architectures, memory functions, and hyperparameter settings.
For a typical setup, this includes 4 model types, 4 memory functions, 8 model sizes (
m
), 4 random seeds, and 8 values of
\alpha
,
resulting in approximately 4,000 individual experiments.
To manage this computational load efficiently, it is recommended to run these experiments in parallel.

In our implementation, we use the Ray Tune package to coordinate and parallelize these runs.
For our current setup, the full set of experiments was conducted on 40 A100 GPUs,
with 2 to 4 experiments running concurrently on each GPU.
This configuration allows the entire benchmark to be completed within 24 hours.

The framework is also designed to be flexible,
allowing users to adjust the computational requirements based on available resources.
For example, reducing the number of model sizes, random seeds, or
\alpha
values can significantly decrease the total runtime.
Additionally, reducing the sequence length
T
can further speed up training,
though this may limit the ability to capture long-range dependencies.

D.5
Limitations

While our experiments provide valuable insights into the approximation properties of various sequence models,
several limitations should be noted:

•

Single Layer Models.

All models in this study are single-layer architectures.
Understanding how multi-layer models interact with different memory structures remains an open question and presents a promising direction for future work.

•

Single-Filter Temporal Structures.

The datasets are generated using a single temporal filter.
In practice, real-world sequences often involve multiple interacting temporal patterns.
Extending this framework to multiple simultaneous filters could provide a more comprehensive assessment of model capabilities.

•

Restricted Memory Function Forms.

The memory functions considered here are defined according to
Equation

1
,
which captures a specific form of temporal structure.
This excludes more complex interactions, such as those involving token-to-token dependencies,
which are known to be critical in many natural language processing tasks.
Exploring these richer structures is an important direction for future research.

Despite these limitations,
the synthetic benchmarking approach presented here provides a controlled and flexible framework for evaluating the approximation capabilities of sequence models,
laying the groundwork for more comprehensive studies in the future.

Additional Discussions

E.1
TCN is sensitive to initialization

Figures
8
and
9
present the loss as a function of
\alpha
for TCNs trained with the Adam and AdamW optimizers.
For a fixed
\alpha

each point represents a different random seed, revealing the impact of initialization on training process.

From the plots, it is clear that TCNs exhibit significant variability across random seeds,
with the extent of this variability depending on both the optimizer and the memory function.
For the Adam optimizer, the spread in loss values is generally tighter, indicating more consistent training across different seeds.
This is especially evident for the exponential and polynomial memory functions.
In contrast, the impulse show greater variability,
with some seeds achieving significantly lower loss than others, suggesting less stable training dynamics.

With the AdamW optimizer, this variability becomes more pronounced.
The impulse and airy functions in particular exhibit a much wider spread in loss values,
with some runs diverging substantially from the majority of seeds.
For the exponential and polynomial functions, the loss values are still relatively clustered,
but the overall spread is noticeably larger than with Adam, indicating a stronger dependence on initialization.

Overall, these results indicate that TCNs are more sensitive to initialization than other architectures (
Figure

10
is plot for LSTM),
and that this sensitivity depends on both the choice of optimizer and the structure of the target memory function.
While the exponential and polynomial functions tend to produce more stable training outcomes,
impulse structures lead to significantly more variable performance across different initializations.
This suggests that the relationship between model stability and memory structure is an important consideration for TCN training.

E.2
Memory Effects on Training Speed

We also investigate the impact of memory structure on training speed.
This is done by plotting the number of training steps required to reach a specified error threshold as a function of
\alpha

This analysis helps us understand how different memory structures affect convergence rates.

These results indicate that both the memory structure and the model capacity significantly influence the training speed of TCNs.

NeurIPS Paper Checklist

1.

Claims

Question: Do the main claims made in the abstract and introduction accurately reflect the paper’s contributions and scope?

Answer:
[Yes]

Justification: The contributions and scope are clearly stated in the abstract and introduction.

Guidelines:

•

The answer NA means that the abstract and introduction do not include the claims made in the paper.

•

The abstract and/or introduction should clearly state the claims made, including the contributions made in the paper and important assumptions and limitations. A No or NA answer to this question will not be perceived well by the reviewers.

•

The claims made should match theoretical and experimental results, and reflect how much the results can be expected to generalize to other settings.

•

It is fine to include aspirational goals as motivation as long as it is clear that these goals are not attained by the paper.

2.

Limitations

Question: Does the paper discuss the limitations of the work performed by the authors?

Answer:
[Yes]

Justification: The limitations is discussed in
Section

D.5

Guidelines:

•

The answer NA means that the paper has no limitation while the answer No means that the paper has limitations, but those are not discussed in the paper.

•

The authors are encouraged to create a separate "Limitations" section in their paper.

•

The paper should point out any strong assumptions and how robust the results are to violations of these assumptions (e.g., independence assumptions, noiseless settings, model well-specification, asymptotic approximations only holding locally). The authors should reflect on how these assumptions might be violated in practice and what the implications would be.

•

The authors should reflect on the scope of the claims made, e.g., if the approach was only tested on a few datasets or with a few runs. In general, empirical results often depend on implicit assumptions, which should be articulated.

•

The authors should reflect on the factors that influence the performance of the approach. For example, a facial recognition algorithm may perform poorly when image resolution is low or images are taken in low lighting. Or a speech-to-text system might not be used reliably to provide closed captions for online lectures because it fails to handle technical jargon.

•

The authors should discuss the computational efficiency of the proposed algorithms and how they scale with dataset size.

•

If applicable, the authors should discuss possible limitations of their approach to address problems of privacy and fairness.

•

While the authors might fear that complete honesty about limitations might be used by reviewers as grounds for rejection, a worse outcome might be that reviewers discover limitations that aren’t acknowledged in the paper. The authors should use their best judgment and recognize that individual actions in favor of transparency play an important role in developing norms that preserve the integrity of the community. Reviewers will be specifically instructed to not penalize honesty concerning limitations.

Experimental result reproducibility

Question: Does the paper fully disclose all the information needed to reproduce the main experimental results of the paper to the extent that it affects the main claims and/or conclusions of the paper (regardless of whether the code and data are provided or not)?

Answer:
[Yes]

Justification: We discuss the experiment settings in

D

Guidelines:

•

The answer NA means that the paper does not include experiments.

•

If the paper includes experiments, a No answer to this question will not be perceived well by the reviewers: Making the paper reproducible is important, regardless of whether the code and data are provided or not.

•

If the contribution is a dataset and/or model, the authors should describe the steps taken to make their results reproducible or verifiable.

•

Depending on the contribution, reproducibility can be accomplished in various ways. For example, if the contribution is a novel architecture, describing the architecture fully might suffice, or if the contribution is a specific model and empirical evaluation, it may be necessary to either make it possible for others to replicate the model with the same dataset, or provide access to the model. In general. releasing code and data is often one good way to accomplish this, but reproducibility can also be provided via detailed instructions for how to replicate the results, access to a hosted model (e.g., in the case of a large language model), releasing of a model checkpoint, or other means that are appropriate to the research performed.

•

While NeurIPS does not require releasing code, the conference does require all submissions to provide some reasonable avenue for reproducibility, which may depend on the nature of the contribution. For example

(a)

If the contribution is primarily a new algorithm, the paper should make it clear how to reproduce that algorithm.

(b)

If the contribution is primarily a new model architecture, the paper should describe the architecture clearly and fully.

(c)

If the contribution is a new model (e.g., a large language model), then there should either be a way to access this model for reproducing the results or a way to reproduce the model (e.g., with an open-source dataset or instructions for how to construct the dataset).

(d)

We recognize that reproducibility may be tricky in some cases, in which case authors are welcome to describe the particular way they provide for reproducibility. In the case of closed-source models, it may be that access to the model is limited in some way (e.g., to registered users), but it should be possible for other researchers to have some path to reproducing or verifying the results.

Experiment statistical significance

Question: Does the paper report error bars suitably and correctly defined or other appropriate information about the statistical significance of the experiments?

Answer:
[Yes]

Guidelines:

•

The answer NA means that the paper does not include experiments.

•

The authors should answer "Yes" if the results are accompanied by error bars, confidence intervals, or statistical significance tests, at least for the experiments that support the main claims of the paper.

•

The factors of variability that the error bars are capturing should be clearly stated (for example, train/test split, initialization, random drawing of some parameter, or overall run with given experimental conditions).

If error bars are reported in tables or plots, The authors should explain in the text how they were calculated and reference the corresponding figures or tables in the text.
