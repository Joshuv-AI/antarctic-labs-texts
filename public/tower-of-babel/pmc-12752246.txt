Is colonic lavage an effective tool for left-sided colonic oncological emergencies?
Authors: Silvia Strambi, Francesco Giudice, Camilla Cremonini, Francesco Arces, Filippo Vagelli, Federico Coccolini, Massimo Chiarugi, Dario Tartaglia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe management of left-sided colonic oncological emergencies remains challenging, especially in choosing the optimal approach to control fecal contamination while minimizing surgical risks. This retrospective study evaluates intraoperative colonic lavage (IOCL) versus manual decompression (MD) in emergency left-sided colonic resections with primary anastomosis. Data from 70 patients who underwent emergency colorectal surgery at Pisa University Hospital between 2010 and 2022 were divided into two groups: IOCL (Group A, n = 23) and MD (Group B, n = 47). Primary endpoint was the rate of anastomotic leakage while secondary endpoints included incisional surgical site infections (SSI), overall morbidity, mortality, and length of hospital stay (LOS). Although IOCL significantly increased operative time (p < 0.0001), it was associated with a shorter ICU stay (p < 0.0001). No significant differences were observed between groups regarding anastomotic leakage, incisional SSI rates, overall morbidity, or LOS. These results align with previous studies suggesting that while IOCL may offer benefits in reducing ICU stays, it does not significantly impact overall morbidity or infection risk compared to MD. Our findings suggest that IOCL could be advantageous in selected, hemodynamically stable patients; however, further research is needed to confirm its efficacy. Given the similar morbidity and infection rates, MD remains a viable alternative. Future multicenter randomized studies could further clarify IOCL’s clinical benefits in left-sided colonic emergencies, helping to establish guidelines for its use in routine practice.Keywords: Oncologic emergencies, Colon, Intraoperative lavage, Anastomotic leak

Introduction

The human gastrointestinal tract contains over 10^12 bacteria per gram of content [1]. Increasing evidence from animal models suggests that bacterial proliferation may play a role in the etiology of anastomotic leaks and wound infections, leading to increased morbidity and mortality in large bowel surgery [2]. This has renewed interest in preoperative bowel preparation to reduce post-operative complications in colorectal surgeries. However, in emergency cases involving bowel obstruction, perforation, or peritonitis, preoperative bowel preparation is not feasible.

Right-sided oncological emergencies are generally manageable with a single surgical intervention, such as right colectomy with primary anastomosis. For left-sided emergencies, however, multiple treatment options exist, which often depend on the surgeon’s experience and the patient’s overall condition [3]. Hartmann’s procedure, while relatively low-risk, requires an additional surgery to restore bowel continuity and may result in up to 50% of permanent stomas [4, 5].

Intraoperative colonic lavage (IOCL) is one approach used to cleanse the bowel when primary resection and anastomosis are planned in left-sided colonic emergencies [6]. The purpose of IOCL is to mechanically remove fecal material, decreasing contamination risk, reducing colonic distention, improving blood supply, minimizing anastomotic tension, and facilitating abdominal closure [7]. However, the efficacy of IOCL is debated, with some studies indicating associations with extended operative time, hypothermia, and electrolyte imbalances. Alternative methods, such as manual decompression, have been proposed as effective alternatives for managing fecal content. Studies report that manual decompression (MD) is comparable to colonic irrigation in left-sided colorectal emergencies in terms of anastomotic leakage, wound infections, and 30-day mortality [8–10].

The current study aims to compare surgical outcomes following resection and primary anastomosis for left-sided colonic oncological emergencies, performed with or without IOCL.

Materials and methods

Study design

We retrospectively analyzed data from patients undergoing emergency colorectal surgery for left-sided neoplastic disease at the Emergency Department of Pisa University Hospital from 2010 to 2022.

Inclusion criteria were single-staged left-sided colonic resection with primary anastomosis either with IOCL or intraoperative manual colonic decompression (MD) for malignant causes.

Exclusion criteria included diverticular disease or other non-oncological etiologies, right colon/transverse sided resections, partial resections (e.g., left flexure), subtotal colectomy, Hartmann’s procedures, palliative lateral ostomies, colonic perforation with diffuse peritonitis and peritoneal contamination, and fully laparoscopic interventions.

Preoperative abdominal CT scans assessed neoplastic disease, with histological confirmation in all cases.

Patients were divided into two groups: Group A (resection and primary anastomosis with IOCL) and Group B (resection and primary anastomosis with MD).

All patients received prophylactic antibiotics with cefazolin (2 g) or clarithromycin (500 mg for penicillin-allergic patients) and metronidazole (500 mg) at induction, with continued dosing as per institutional guidelines [11].

The study was conducted according to the STROBE guidelines [12]. Data collection and analysis were performed according to the institutional guidelines and to the ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. Informed consent for this study was waived because of its retrospective nature.

Data collection and endpoints

Data collected included patient demographics, clinical features, night time surgery, operative procedures, anastomosis technique (mechanical or manual), American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status (ASA), Age-adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index, intensive care unit (ICU) requirements, ICU stay length, hospital length of stay (LOS), tumor staging according to the TNM classification of AJCC/UICC [13] and 30-day postoperative follow-up. Surgical site infections (SSIs) were defined according to the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [14] and categorized as incisional SSIs or organ/space infections, including anastomotic leakage and intra-abdominal abscess. Anastomotic leakage was diagnosed clinically and by imaging.

Primary endpoint included anastomotic leakage. Secondary endpoints were incisional (superficial and deep) SSI, overall morbidity, mortality, and length of stay (LOS).

Intraoperative colonic lavage (IOCL) procedure

Following resection, a 14 Fr Foley catheter was inserted into the appendix, if present; otherwise, the Foley was directly introduced into the cecum through a millimetric enterotomy. Irrigation was performed with at least three liters of saline at 37 °C until the effluent was clear. The cecal opening was then closed with a running absorbable suture or a purse-string suture.

Manual decompression (MD) procedure

After resection, the distal colon was milked out of its contents into a sterile container until no further fecal material, fluids, or gas were present.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages, while continuous variables are shown as means with standard deviations (if normally distributed) or as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables were compared with Student’s t-test for independent variables (homoscedastic and heteroscedastic versions), and the Mann–Whitney U test as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-squared test. Statistical analysis was performed using XLStat software (Addinsoft Corporation, US), with a p-value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 464 patients underwent surgery for colorectal emergencies during the study period, with 200 excluded due to right colon or transverse colon involvement. Among the 264 eligible patients, further exclusions based on reported clinical and surgical criteria resulted in a final sample of 70 patients. Group A (IOCL) included 23 patients (33%), and Group B (MD) included 47 patients (67%) (Fig. 1).

Intraoperative findings

Laparoscopic procedures were attempted in 8 patients (11%), with 2 cases in Group A and 6 in Group B (p = 0.615). Conversions to open surgery were due to adhesions, small bowel distention, advanced disease, ureter involvement, or diffuse peritoneal contamination. Mechanical anastomosis was performed in 59% of patients (52% in Group A, 62% in Group B; p = 0.256). The IOCL procedure was associated with a significant increase in mean operative time (262.5 vs. 216.4 min, p < 0.0001) (Table 2).

Outcomes

No statistically significant differences were observed in ICU needs (p = 0.565), overall morbidity (p = 0.407), or postoperative LOS (p = 0.964) between groups. ICU length of stay was significantly shorter in IOCL patients (p < 0.0001). Postoperative complications primarily included incisional surgical site infections (SSI) (14%) and anastomotic leaks (3%), with no statistically significant differences between groups. Re-interventions were required in three cases, all in Group B, due to anastomotic leakage, hemorrhagic shock and fascial dehiscence, respectively (p = 0.525). One patient in group B died due to heart failure following fascial dehiscence (p = 0.484) (Table 3).

Histopatology

Intestinal adenocarcinoma was found in 61 (87%) patients, followed by mucinous adenocarcinoma in 8 cases (11%) and in one (1%) case by a neuroendocrine tumor. Margins were free from residual tumor in 100% of the resected specimens. The disease was metastatic at diagnosis in 16% of cases.

Discussion

The management of left-sided colorectal emergencies due to cancer has evolved significantly. Intraoperative colonic irrigation, first introduced in the 1980 s, allows for a one-stage resection with primary anastomosis by creating a clean and decompressed colonic environment [15]. Despite concerns regarding operative time, recent studies indicate that primary anastomosis with IOCL is feasible and safe, even in cases with peritonitis [16, 17]. An appendiceal incannulation with a Foley catheter should be the first choice to perform IOCL, otherwise a millimetric enterotomy directly into the cecum might allow the catheter insertion. Moreover, increasing importance is given to the effect of other factors, like immunocompromising and malnutrition to the anastomotic seal [18–21].

Different methods for colonic irrigation with saline have been evaluated [22, 23]. Using large volumes of saline can extend operative time and may also lead to hypothermia [9, 20]. Alternatively, colonic manual decompression (MD) has been used to reduce fecal load while avoiding the potential drawbacks of irrigation. In a prospective randomized trial, Lim et al. compared MD directly with colonic washout in 53 patients with obstructed left-sided colorectal cancer [19]. They found a significantly shorter median procedural time for MD (13 min vs. 31 min for IOCL); however, this difference did not translate into a significant difference in the median total operative time. There were also no significant differences between the groups regarding postoperative length of stay, wound infection rates, or anastomotic leaks [20]. A systematic review by Kam et al. [9], which analyzed seven trials, confirmed that in obstructed left-sided colorectal emergencies, MD was comparable to colonic washout, with no significant differences in anastomotic leak rates, wound infection rates, or 30-day mortality. Tham et al. [24] recently conducted a comprehensive meta-analysis of 28 studies on emergency left-sided large bowel pathologies, comparing outcomes of resection with primary anastomosis using either IOCL, manual decompression, or no IOCL against Hartmann’s procedure and subtotal colectomy. Their analysis confirmed a statistically significant increase in operative time when performing IOCL, with similar rates of postoperative complications. However, they found that primary anastomosis with IOCL was associated with a significantly shorter hospital stay compared to Hartmann’s procedure.

Our findings align with previous research, suggesting that while IOCL may extend operative time, it is associated with a significantly shorter ICU stay. This data, in the absence of significant differences between the two groups in terms of comorbidities, may suggest that it might be an advantage in favor of IOCL in selected patients. Notably, patients who underwent IOCL had a lower Charlson Comorbidity Index, as well as lower rates of immunosuppression and metastatic disease at presentation. These factors might partially explain the observed differences. However, due to the limited sample size, this trend did not reach statistical significance. A larger patient cohort may help confirm these preliminary findings and clarify whether this association is truly clinically relevant. In fact, this benefit could make IOCL a suitable option mainly in hemodynamically stable patients, where the advantages of a cleaner operative field may offset the longer surgical duration. Interestingly, despite the theoretical benefits of reducing fecal load and contamination, we did not observe significant differences in anastomotic leak or wound infection rates between IOCL and manual decompression (MD). This observation may reflect the effectiveness of MD as a simpler alternative, capable of achieving similar postoperative outcomes without the procedural demands of IOCL.

Some authors suggest adding an intraoperative colonoscopy to detect any synchronous lesions before colon resection. In a retrospective comparative study of 715 patients with left-sided colorectal cancer, Sasaki et al. [25] reported that intraoperative colonoscopy identified a synchronous adenomatous polyp in 27 of 101 cases (26.8%) and carcinoma in 4 patients (4%), leading to a change in the surgical procedure in 9 cases (8.9%). In their study, colonoscopy extended the operative time by an average of 28 min without increasing patient morbidity. We do not routinely perform on-table colonoscopy in emergency settings, as patients undergo a preoperative CT scan, which allows us to rule out significant lesions (any questionable areas can be palpated during surgery) and to identify active sources of bleeding.

Our study has several limitations, primarily due to its retrospective design and the limited sample size. Additionally, as a single-center study, institutional practices could introduce bias in both the surgical technique and patient management. This homogeneity, while beneficial for consistency, limits the generalizability of our results to broader settings. Another important limitation is related to the challenges of comparing small incidence events, which could make comparison statistics underpowered. Further research, ideally through multicenter randomized controlled trials, is needed to confirm these findings and to refine the criteria for selecting patients who would benefit most from IOCL. Such studies could also explore combining IOCL with intraoperative colonoscopy to detect synchronous lesions, which could further enhance surgical outcomes in emergency settings.

Conclusions

Colonic lavage is a valuable tool in the management of left-sided oncological colonic emergencies, facilitating primary anastomosis in a cleaner surgical field. However, it does not significantly reduce anastomotic leak or wound infection rates and adds to operative time. Reduced ICU suggests IOCL may be beneficial for specific patient profiles. Future prospective studies are essential to confirm these findings and clarify its role in clinical practice.
