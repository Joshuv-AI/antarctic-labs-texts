FEASTS: Radial Distribution of HI surface densities down to 0.01 ​ M ⊙ ​ pc − 2 0.01\,\rm{M_{\odot}}~{\rm pc}^{-2} of 35 Nearby Galaxies Facilities: FAST: 500 m, VLA
Jing Wang (王菁), Dong Yang (杨冬), Xuchen Lin (林旭辰), Qifeng Huang (黄齐丰), Zhijie Qu (屈稚杰), Hsiao-wen Chen (陳曉雯), Hong Guo (郭宏), Luis C. Ho (何子山), Peng Jiang (姜鹏), Zezhong Liang (梁泽众), Céline Péroux, Lister Staveley-Smith, Simon Weng

Abstract
We present the H ​ i {\rm H}{\textsc{i}} surface density ( Σ HI \Sigma_{\rm HI} ) radial distributions based on total-power H ​ i {\rm H}{\textsc{i}} images obtained by FAST in the FEASTS program, for 35 galaxies with inclinations lower than 72 ° . We derive the H ​ i {\rm H}{\textsc{i}} radius R 001 R_{001} , which is the radius for the 0.01 M ⊙ ​ pc − 2 \,\rm{M_{\odot}}~{\rm pc}^{-2} ( ∼ 10 18.1 ​ cm − 2 \sim 10^{18.1}\,\rm{cm^{-2}} ) iso-density level, 100 times deeper than the 1 M ⊙ ​ pc − 2 \,\rm{M_{\odot}}~{\rm pc}^{-2} level previously commonly used to measure R 1 R_{1} . The profile shapes show a large diversity at a given radius in units of kpc, group virial radius, and R 1 R_{1} , but align more tightly with radius normalized by R 001 R_{001} . The universal H ​ i {\rm H}{\textsc{i}} profile has a scatter of ∼ 0.2 \sim 0.2 dex, and a scale-length of ∼ 0.11 ​ R 001 \sim 0.11R_{001} in the outer region. We derive a new R 001 R_{001} - M HI M_{\rm HI} relation, which has a scatter of 0.02 dex, and similar slope of ∼ \sim 0.5 as the previously known R 1 R_{1} - M HI M_{\rm HI} relation. Excluding strongly tidal-interacting galaxies, the ratio R 001 / R 1 R_{001}/R_{1} (anti-)correlate strongly and significantly with the H ​ i {\rm H}{\textsc{i}} -to-stellar mass ratio and sSFR, but not with the stellar mass, M HI M_{\rm HI} , dark matter mass, or SFR. The strongly tidal-interacting galaxies tend to show deviations from these trends, and have the most flattened profiles. These results imply that in absence of major tidal interactions, physical processes must cooperate so that Σ HI \Sigma_{\rm HI} distributes in a self-similar way in the outer region down to the 0.01 M ⊙ ​ pc − 2 \,\rm{M_{\odot}}~{\rm pc}^{-2} level. Moreover, they may drive gas flows in such a way, that H ​ i {\rm H}{\textsc{i}} -richer galaxies have H ​ i {\rm H}{\textsc{i}} disks not only extend further, but also transport H ​ i {\rm H}{\textsc{i}} inward more efficiently from R 001 R_{001} to R 1 R_{1} .

I
Introduction

Correctly characterizing the inflow and outflow of gas has become a major bottleneck in developing galaxy evolution theory
[
10
]
.
The neutral atomic hydrogen (
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
) is a significant gas component fueling star formation, and promising tracer of the cool gas in and around galaxies.
Observing and combining it with multi-wavelength data is expected to provide key clues on gas flow processes.

{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
images are conventionally obtained with interferometric observation of the 21-cm emission lines, typically reaching a depth of
\sim 10^{20}\,\rm{cm^{-2}}
.
Based on them, it was revealed that
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disks show the following self-similar structures
[
48
]
.
The relation between the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
mass and the semi-major axes of
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
surface density (
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
) contour at the
1\,\rm{M_{\odot}}~{\rm pc}^{-2}
(
\sim 1.25\times 10^{20}\,\rm{cm^{-2}}
) level (
R_{1}
), is remarkably tight with a scatter of 0.06 dex.
The
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
radial profiles in the outer region show homogeneous shapes when the radius is normalized by
R_{1}
.
These features are useful constraints to baryonic models in cosmological simulations
[
21
, e.g.,]
.
One question is whether the self-similar behavior extends to lower surface densities.

Deeper than typical interferometric observations, detections of gas with
N_{\rm HI}\sim 10^{18}\,\rm{cm^{-2}}
is of significant importance to understanding baryonic flow and galaxy evolution.
It is the possible interface of disk confronting the circum-galactic medium (CGM;
Faucher-Giguère & Oh 18
), and frequently reveals optically dark interactions between galaxies (
Wang et al. 52
, hereafter W24), bearing important implications on the CGM structure and dynamics.
Its coverage sets the mean free path of ionizing photons, regulating the photoionization states in the intergalactic medium (IGM).
For decades, uncovering the faint
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
regime has been relying on ultraviolet absorption observations, where large samples are required to overcome the incidental sampling limitation
[
4
]
.
Yet compiling a statistical sample in absorption for the
\sim 10^{18}\,\rm{cm^{-2}}
regime, typically through Lyman Limit Systems (LLS), has also been challenging.
Latest developments consistently conclude on the complex environment, and therefore a statistically representative sample is required to characterize the connections between LLS properties and host galaxies
[
55
,
9
,
34
,
54
]
.

Directly mapping
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
with 21-cm emission line down to
10^{18}\,\rm{cm^{-2}}
for statistical samples is still difficult but has become possible in the nearby universe (
de Blok et al. 13
,
52
).
It is exciting, not only for studies based on those data, but also because it provides reference to understand the behavior of LLSs at higher redshift.
It can be combined with LLSs to extract multi-resolution properties like the clumpiness or power spectrum.
It also bridges the disks to the even lower column density regime that further links the IGM, the Ly
\alpha
absorption line detected regime of
\lesssim 10^{15}\,\rm{cm^{-2}}
.

In this Letter, we use observations from FEASTS (FAST Extended Atlas of Selected Targets Survey,
Wang et al. 51
, hereafter W23) that directly maps
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
down to a 3-
\sigma
limit of
10^{17.7}\,\rm{cm^{-2}}
.
Previous studies of FEASTS reveal that the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
of relatively isolated galaxies distributes in surprisingly flat disks at the data depth (Yang et al. in prep,
52
).
This study thus focuses on the disk aspect of
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
distribution: radial profiles of projection-corrected surface densities, and characteristic sizes based on them, for moderately inclined galaxies.
Directly depicting
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disks at this new surface density limit, not only constrains disk formation, but also provides benchmark for further characterizing the column density distribution or coverage fraction, with random inclinations and environments for central galaxies.
We assume a Kroupa IMF
[
28
]
, and do not include helium in calculations of the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
mass.

II
Data and Method

FEASTS is a long-term effort to obtain
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
images for external galaxies selected by
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
fluxes (
>
50 Jy km
\,{\rm s}^{-1}
), with the Five-hundred-meter Aperture Spherical radio Telescope (FAST,
Jiang et al. 25
) in the observable sky of FAST.
The data reduction is conducted with a customized pipeline, the details of which can be found in
51
.
The data cubes have spatial resolution of 3.24
′
, and velocity resolution of 1.61
\rm{km~s^{-1}}
.
The source finding is performed with the software SoFiA
[
38
]
, with parameters listed in
52
.
The parent sample used here is 55 galaxies already observed in the FEASTS program, during the period 2021.09-2024.06.
The RFI (radio frequency interference) contamination incidence is less than 6.6% for 95% of the observations.
Assuming a line width of 20
\rm{km~s^{-1}}
, the median 3-
\sigma
depth in column density is 10
{}^{17.7}\,\rm{cm^{-2}}
, corresponding to a face-on surface density of 0.004
\rm{M_{\odot}}~{\rm pc}^{-2}
.

The majority of the galaxies are within a distance of 20 Mpc, and have an integral
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
flux above 100 Jy
\rm{km~s^{-1}}
.
We take isophotal axis ratios measured in the Spitzer IRAC 1 (3.6
\textmu
m) band from the S
4
G catalog (The Spitzer Survey of Stellar Structure in Galaxies,
Sheth et al. 39
).
In the following analysis, we select from the starting sample the 35 not-too-inclined galaxies, which have IRAC-1 axis ratios larger than 0.3, roughly corresponding to inclinations lower than 72
°
.

The association of galaxies to galaxy groups, and group masses and virial radius (
r_{\rm vir}
) are taken from the group catalog of
Kourkchi & Tully [27]
.
Other properties including stellar masses (
M_{*}
), star formation rates (SFR), and distances are taken from the catalog of
z=0
Multiwavelength Galaxy Synthesis (z0MGS,
Leroy et al. 30
).
Figure
1
shows the distributions of analysis sample (and FEASTS-observed) galaxies in the 2D spaces of
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
mass (
M_{\rm HI}
) versus
M_{*}
, and SFR versus
M_{*}
.
There are 5 dwarf galaxies in the analysis sample having
M_{*}<10^{9}\,{\rm M}_{\odot}
, and others are MW-like.
Compared to the general galaxy population at
z=0
, the sample is biased toward
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
-rich and star-forming galaxies, a compromise for observational convenience at this stage.

In a deep
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
view, many galaxies experience some level of tidal interactions.
For galaxies at a relatively early stage of merger, we perform 3D de-blending using a pipeline developed in
Huang et al. [24]
to separate the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
fluxes into individual galaxies (example atlas in Appendix A).
This process is done on a best-effort basis, and biased against the galaxies with very close and small companions, or in late stage of merger.
Additionally, we specifically tag 11 most intensively interacting galaxies in the following analysis: IC 1727, NGC 672, NGC 3169, NGC 3368, NGC 4254, NGC 4449, NGC 4490, NGC 4532, NGC 4725, NGC 5033, NGC 5194.
Readers can easily find numerous publications on each of these systems.
The remaining are less interacting galaxies.

We derive the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
surface density (
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
) radial profiles for each galaxy using elliptical annulus around galaxy center with geometry set by the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
contour shape at
10^{20}\,\rm{cm^{-2}}
.
The axis ratios of the less interacting subset have 10, 50, and 90 percentiles of 0.51, 0.80, and 0.92.
We do not use the optical geometry, because
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disks typically warp outside the optical disks
[
20
,
26
,
50
]
, reflecting recent interactions with the external environment including the CGM.
Meanwhile, kinematical modeling for the outlying
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
of the whole sample is being conducted and will be presented in a future paper.

The
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
values are deprojected by multiplying with the axis ratio (
\cos i
) of annulus, with a thin disk assumption.
Such a thin disk assumption may be less appropriate for dwarf irregular galaxies that are less confined by the disk gravity and more susceptible to perturbations
[
35
]
. We leave such a possible caveat to future quantification with high-resolution observations (e.g., with MeerKAT).
We derive characteristic radius at the
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
levels of 1 and 0.01
\,\rm{M_{\odot}}~{\rm pc}^{-2}
, and refer to them as
R_{1}
and
R_{001}

1
1

1

For three galaxies (all in the strongly interacting subset) that the radial profiles don’t reach 0.01
\,\rm{M_{\odot}}~{\rm pc}^{-2}
, localized linear extrapolation is conducted in the logarithm space to derive
R_{001}
.
.
The procedure is the same as the one used in
[
48
]
.

We justify in Appendix
B
that these radius are robustly measured at the FAST resolution.
The typical deviations from true values due to beam smoothing are smaller than 0.1 dex for the range of
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disks angular sizes in this sample.
There, we also describe procedures to correct for this small-extent bias, and estimate uncertainties for
R_{1}
and
R_{001}
after taking into account both photometric error and beam smoothing effects.
The measurements of
R_{1}
and
R_{001}
together with errors are presented in Table
1
in Appendix
D
.

III
Results

III.1
The
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
Radial Profiles

We show all the
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
radial profiles in Figure
2
, with radius normalized to different units.

In unit of kpc (Figure
2
-a), most MW-like galaxies have profiles extending beyond 50 kpc down to the data depth of
\log\,(\Sigma_{\rm HI}/\rm{M_{\odot}}~{\rm pc}^{-2})=-2.4
.
The most extended of them go near 100 kpc.
Possibly due to selection bias, these profiles are systematically higher than the median distribution predicted by simulations in
Péroux & Howk [33]
.
The dwarf galaxies on average have the least extended profiles here, but do not differ significantly from the MW-like galaxies in other panels.

Normalizing by the group virial radius
r_{\rm vir}
reflects connection to the dark matter halo mass and CGM properties.
When normalized by
r_{\rm vir}
(Figure
2
-b), the scatter of profiles is the largest among the first four panels.
Galaxies have
r/r_{\rm vir}
ranging between 0.05 to 0.5 when reaching the data depth, and the
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
at
r/r_{\rm vir}=0.2
span
\gtrsim
3 index.
These conclusions do not significant change, if we only select central galaxies (
20/24
and
4/11
for the less and strongly interacting subsets, respectively).

The
R_{1}
and
R_{001}
are characteristic radius for
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disks.
When normalized by
R_{1}
(Figure
2
-c), all
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
profiles align around the median profile derived with interferometric data
[
48
]
(W16 hereafter) in the inner region (within
R_{1}
).
Except for the strongly interacting galaxies, they are also close to the W16 median profile in the outer region, with offset
\lesssim
1.5 dex.
On the other hand, almost all profiles exceed the extrapolated W16 median profile in the outer region, reflecting the need to directly observe deep.
The strongly interacting galaxies show much more flattened shapes than less interacting galaxies in this normalization scheme.

Finally, when normalized by
R_{001}
(Figure
2
-d), the profiles align in the tightest way throughout the radius.
For less interacting galaxies, the scatters of
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
are
\lesssim
1 dex (or
\sigma\lesssim 0.2
dex) at most radius.
Even the profiles of the strongly interacting galaxies do not deviate far in this normalization scheme.
The
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
distribution thus looks self-organized: it is most strongly determined by its own largest extension, rather than by plain unit (e.g., kpc) scales or the dark matter halo size.
Such a self-organization is remarkable, when one considers that galaxies are ecosystems, CGM are multiphase, and
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
gas is susceptible to perturbations.
We are not inferring that effects from other baryonic components, dark matter or radiation field do not influence the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
distribution.
Instead, their net effect produces the self-similar
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
distribution all the way down to the 0.01
\rm{M_{\odot}}~{\rm pc}^{-2}
level, which is a strong constraint to galaxy formation models.

III.1.1
Analytical Approximations of the Median
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
Profile

For future comparison convenience, the median profile of less interacting galaxies is provided in Table
2
, and plotted again in Figure
3
.
The median profile has an exponential shape on the outer region with scale-length
r_{s}=0.11R_{001}
(purple line in Figure
3

R_{001}/R_{1}
ratio in Section
III.2

We use the analytical equational form from
Wang et al. [49]
to fit the shape of the median profile, and obtain the following (blue curve in Figure
3
):

y=\log\frac{I\,e^{-x/r_{s1}}}{1+(I/J-1)e^{-x/r_{s2}}}

(1)

where
y=\log\,(\Sigma_{\rm HI}/\rm{M_{\odot}}~{\rm pc}^{-2})
,
x=r/R_{001}
,
I=62.95^{+14.78}_{-11.13}
,
J=5.96^{+0.34}_{-0.32}
,
r_{s1}=0.114^{+0.004}_{-0.004}
, and
r_{s2}=0.106^{+0.007}_{-0.006}
.
This equation form is based on the previous observational findings that both total neutral gas surface densities (
I
) and the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
-to-H
2
conversion efficiencies (
{\rm H}_{2}/{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}=(I-J)/J
) decrease with radius roughly following exponential functions
[
29
,
3
,
2
]
.
It has been found to well fit the individual
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
profiles of MW-type galaxies at
\Sigma_{\rm HI}\gtrsim 0.5\rm{M_{\odot}}~{\rm pc}^{-2}
levels
[
49
]
.
Here it also well describes the median
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
profile down to the
\lesssim 0.01\,\rm{M_{\odot}}~{\rm pc}^{-2}
level.

The overall shape can also be roughly described by the following cored power law equation with two less parameters (green curve in Figure
3
):

y=\log\frac{(1+(x/r_{c})^{2})^{-\beta}}{100(1+(1/r_{c})^{2})^{-\beta}}

(2)

where
r_{c}=0.94^{+0.09}_{-0.07}
, and
\beta=8.43^{+1.15}_{-0.87}
.

III.2
A New
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
Size-mass Relation at the 0.01
\,\rm{M_{\odot}}~{\rm pc}^{-2}
level

The previously reported
R_{1}
-
M_{\rm HI}
relation was based on interferometry data
[
43
,
48
]
, suffering from the possible problem of short-spacing or missing-flux.
In Figure
4
, the
R_{1}
and
M_{\rm HI}
measured here, which do not have missing fluxes problem, follow well the previous
R_{1}
-
M_{\rm HI}
relation.
It suggests that either the levels of underestimate in previous studies due to missing-flux are low for most interferometric observations, or just move galaxies along the relation.

Figure
4
shows that
R_{001}
and
M_{\rm HI}
also have a strong correlation with Pearson R value of 0.92.
It is consistent with the homogeneous
R_{001}
-normalized profiles in Figure
2
.
In contrast, the correlations of
R_{001}
with
M_{*}
or
M_{\rm halo}
are weaker, with R values of 0.80 and 0.56, respectively.
The strongly interacting galaxies have 1.4 times systematically higher
R_{001}
at a given
M_{\rm HI}
than the less interacting ones, in contrast to their
R_{1}
being scattered around the
R_{1}
-
M_{\rm HI}
relation.
If we shift the
R_{1}
-
M_{\rm HI}
relation upward by 0.31 (0.34) dex, most
R_{001}
versus
M_{\rm HI}
data points excluding (including) strongly interacting galaxies lie on it.
So at a given
M_{\rm HI}
,
R_{001}
is around 2 (2.2) times
R_{1}
.
For less interacting systems, the ratio
R_{001}/R_{1}=2
indicates an outer profile scale-length
r_{s}=0.22R_{1}=0.11R_{001}
(Figure
2
-d).

We conduct a linear regression for the less interacting subset, and derive the following relation:

\log R_{001}=0.49(\pm 0.02)\log M_{\rm HI}-3.11(\pm 0.21)

(3)

The
emcee
method
[
19
]
is used to derive the slope and intercept uncertainties, as well as a fractional intrinsic scatter
<0.001
of the relation.
The standard deviation of data points around the relation is 0.02 dex, slightly higher than the 0.01 dex around the
R_{1}
-
M_{\rm HI}
relation with the same sample.
Such a tight relation can be used to estimate
R_{001}
based on the
M_{\rm HI}
, which will be useful for studies where deep
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
images are not always available.

Like the
R_{1}
-
M_{\rm HI}
relation, the new
R_{001}
-
M_{\rm HI}
relation has a slope close to 0.5.
It suggests an almost constant average surface density of
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
within
R_{001}
, determined by the intercept
b
of the
R_{001}
-
M_{\rm HI}
relation with a value
\Sigma_{\rm HI,avg}=10^{-2b}/\pi=0.35\,\rm{M_{\odot}}~{\rm pc}^{-2}
.
It also suggests that distance uncertainties only shift galaxies along the relation and have a minimal effect on the scatter.

For the less interacting galaxies, while the area between
R_{1}
and
R_{001}
is 3 times larger than that within
R_{1}
, most (80%)
M_{\rm HI}
are enclosed within
R_{1}
(Figure
2
-e), and the majority (90%) of them are within 0.5
R_{001}
(Figure
2
-b).
It is thus the structure instead of
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
mass budget that makes the

relation and

-normalized profiles interesting.
This small amount of
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
mass beyond
R_{1}
(or between 0.5 and 1

) could have distributed and ended anywhere, yet it seems to almost always reach 0.01
\,\rm{M_{\odot}}~{\rm pc}^{-2}
at
\sim 2R_{1}
(with scatter
<

We test the relation of
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
mass between
R_{1}
and
R_{001}

\pi(R_{001}^{2}-R_{1}^{2})
and volume
\frac{4}{3}\pi(R_{001}^{3}-R_{1}^{3})

The former has smaller scatter (0.12 dex) than the latter (0.18 dex), supporting a disky instead of spherical
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
distribution.

III.3
The Dependence of
R_{001}/R_{1}

As both
R_{001}
and
R_{1}
have tight relations with
M_{\rm HI}
, it is useful to ask whether any new clues are revealed by
R_{001}
on building the
M_{\rm HI}
.
Because the
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
profiles are well aligned locally when normalized by either
R_{1}
or
R_{001}
, we ask, whether the profile slope between these two radius carry information on regulation from properties other than
M_{\rm HI}
.
Both questions can be studied by looking into how the size ratios,
R_{001}/R_{1}
, relate to other galaxy properties.

An investigation of these relations is presented in Figure
5
.
The Pearson
R
and
p
values are used to quantify the correlation strength and significance of each relation, as labeled in each panel.
We confirm that switching to the Spearman correlation coefficients does not significantly change the following results.
As a further robustness check, for correlations identified to be strong and significant, we also derive the partial correlation coefficients, removing possible mutual dependence of
R_{001}/R_{1}
and parameters on galaxy distances.

Figure
5
(panels a and b) shows that the
R_{001}/R_{1}
ratios depend most strongly and significantly on the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
mass fraction (
M_{\rm HI}/M_{*}
) and secondly on specific SFR (SFR
/M_{*}
).
As the slopes of the two size-mass relations are close,
R_{001}/R_{1}
have a rather small dynamic range (
\lesssim 0.2
dex), yet they show clear trends with these two parameters.
The partial correlation coefficients controlling for distances suggest that the
R_{001}/R_{1}
-
M_{\rm HI}/M_{*}
correlation is highly robust (Pearson
R=-0.55
,
p=0.01
), while the
R_{001}/R_{1}
-sSFR correlation becomes considerably weaker and insignificant (Pearson
R=-0.23
,
p=0.30
).
We reach a similar conclusion when testing the partial correlation between
R_{001}/R_{1}
and
M_{\rm HI}/M_{*}
(sSFR) with the effect of sSFR (
M_{\rm HI}/M_{*}
) being controlled for.
Thus, the
R_{001}/R_{1}
-
M_{\rm HI}/M_{*}
relation seems to be the most important and possibly most intrinsic one among the relations studied here.

Figure
5
(panels c to f) also shows that
R_{001}/R_{1}
do not exhibit strong or significant dependence (reflected in either low R or high p values) on the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
mass, stellar mass, dark matter halo mass, or SFR, although these parameters are considered important in determining galaxy evolution.

We note that removing the five dwarf galaxies does not significantly change the results presented above.
Therefore, the steepness of
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
profiles in outer regions indeed point to effects other than self-organization of
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disk.
Such that, gas-richer galaxies tend to have steeper
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
profiles with respect to
R_{1}
in the outer regions.

Finally, while the highest values of
R_{001}/R_{1}
value are contributed by the strongly interacting galaxies, only 4 out of 11 strongly interacting galaxies stand out as clear outliers in the
R_{001}/R_{1}
-
M_{\rm HI}/M_{*}
relation.
Together with the result of strongly interacting galaxies fluctuating around the
R_{1}-M_{\rm HI}
relation, it implies some global and regular response of
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
outer disks to external perturbations, possibly also linking to self-regulation via interacting with the CGM.

IV
Summary and Discussion

It was found that galaxies with higher SFR at a given
M_{*}
have a higher
M_{\rm HI}

[
36
]
, higher fraction of
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
mass beyond the optical disk over the total
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
mass
[
47
]
, and higher incidence and strength of cool gas absorbers in the CGM
[
15
]
.
These relations are direct constraints to the quasi-equilibrium states of baryonic flow through galaxies, in which the relative rates of gas accretion, gas inflow, star formation, and gas outflow determine the baryonic mass reservoir in each step, the so-called gas-regulator or bathtub model
[
6
,
11
,
31
]
.
One possible interpretation is that for star-forming galaxies gas accretion is highly efficient on large scales, whereas inward flows within the galaxy disk are comparatively less effective.
In other words, while the specific rates of both gas accretion (onto the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disk) and inflow (radially through the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disk and into the optical radius) increase with sSFR, the former possibly increases more quickly than the latter.
So that, a large reservoir of
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
can be built up beyond the optical disks of actively star-forming galaxies.

Our results provide new details to the aforementioned picture, specifically on the gas flow between
R_{1}
and
R_{001}
.
The tight relation between
M_{\rm HI}
and
R_{1}
has been taken as an important characterization of the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disk structure, and strong constraint on the built-up and shrinking of
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disks as a reservoir of star-forming material in galaxy formation models (e.g.,
Gensior et al. 21
).
The relation between
M_{\rm HI}
and
R_{001}
confirms that this reservoir in the form of
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disks structurally extends to the
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
level of
0.01\,\rm{M_{\odot}}~{\rm pc}^{-2}
.
Importantly, these
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
radius being most tightly related with
M_{\rm HI}
infers that the balance of inflow and outflow organizes the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
distribution in a self-similar way out to to
R_{001}
.
It is consistent with recent finding that Ly
\alpha
absorber strengths within the virial radius correlate more strongly with
M_{\rm HI}
than with
M_{*}
, SFR, or
M_{\rm vir}

[
5
]
.
But our results for the first time directly quantify
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
as a function of
r
, and uniquely indicate that at
\Sigma_{\rm HI}>0.01\,\rm{M_{\odot}}~{\rm pc}^{-2}
this reservoir tends to take the format of a flat disk, instead of halo gas, because the disk structure is assumed and preferred here to obtain the tight relations (also see
52
and D. Yang in prep for direct images of face-on and edge-on disks).
The trend of
R_{001}/R_{1}
decreasing with
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
-richness (sSFR) for less interacting galaxies implies that the gas flow from
R_{001}
to
R_{1}
is efficient, possibly because the newly condensed gas at large radius is not yet fully supported by rotational velocity
[
42
]
.
Hence, the aforementioned bottleneck of gas inflowing to the optical disk possibly happens inward
R_{1}
.
The possibly relatively fast and slow inflows near
R_{001}
and optical radius respectively naturally lead to cumulation of gas between these two radius, which may explain why higher fractions of
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
mass beyond optical disks tend to be found around more star-forming galaxies
[
47
]
.

We use a sketch in Figure
6
to help explain the scenario discussed above.
In the sketch, the two example galaxies have the same optical disk size.
While the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
-rich galaxy has larger
R_{1}
and
R_{001}
, it has smaller
R_{001}/R_{1}
compared to the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
-normal one.
The purple arrows represent the net
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
inflow: the rightmost arrow indicates inflow from the CGM onto the extended
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disk (possibly from a different gas phase and different structure from the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disk), the middle arrow shows the flow of gas from
R_{001}
to
R_{1}
, and the leftmost arrow represents the flow from
R_{1}
to within the galaxy optical disk.
Within each galaxy, the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
is accreted onto the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disk near
R_{001}
in a faster way than it enters the optical disk, so that
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
piles up beyond
R_{\rm opt}
and the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disk is larger than the optical disk.
Compared to the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
-normal galaxy, the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
-rich galaxy has enhanced inflows at all radii, but the inflow into the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disk at
R_{001}
is enhanced more than that into the optical disk at
R_{\rm opt}
, so that its
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disk grows in size more significantly, resulting in higher
R_{001}/R_{\rm opt}
and higher
R_{1}/R_{\rm opt}
(i.e.,
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
-richer galaxies have larger optical-to-
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
size ratios and higher fraction of
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
beyond the optical disks,
Wang et al. 49
,
Wang et al. 47
).
Within the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
-rich galaxy, the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
flows from
R_{001}
to
R_{1}
in a faster way than it from
R_{1}
into the optical disk, so as a result, the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disk grows faster near
R_{1}
than it does near
R_{001}
, leading to a down-bending
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disk and smaller value of
R_{001}/R_{1}
compared to the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
-normal disk (i.e.,
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
-richer galaxies have lower
R_{001}/R_{1}
values).

Because the localized
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
is the result of balance between cooling, heating, ionization, radial inflow, outflow, and stripping, an alternative interpretation for the trend of
R_{001}/R_{1}
decreasing with
M_{\rm HI}/M_{*}
can be truncation in the outer disks due to ionization or dynamic effects.
However, the
R_{001}/R_{1}
-
R_{001}/r_{\rm vir}
correlation is considerably weaker (Pearson
R=-0.13
and
p=0.55
) than the
R_{001}/R_{1}
-
M_{\rm HI}/M_{*}
correlation, which seems to disfavor this picture.
Velocity field modeling and multiphase-gas observation in the outer disks will be essential to directly differentiate the possible scenarios.
Existing (e.g., MHONGOOSE) and future high-resolution deep
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
observations may be efficient at directly detecting signatures of gas accretion near
R_{001}
.

These observational details provide useful clues on CGM dynamics and gas accretion in galaxy formation models.
Below we give an example of qualitatively comparing with the cooling flow model proposed with the FIRE-2 simulation
[
23
]
.
This model suggests subsonic cooling flows conserving angular momentum to be a feasible way of gas accretion in MW-type halos
[
41
]
.
One key prediction is that hot gas only condenses near the “circular radius” determined by the virial radius and average halo spin
[
42
]
, and thus forming a flat, well-aligned, rotating cool gas disk
[
22
]
.
The related simulations also predict that the accreted gas tends to pile around
R_{1}
, which effectively defines the disk edge
[
44
]
.
The
R_{001}
-
M_{\rm HI}
relation qualitatively supports this model on the aspect of gas accretion directly building an
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disk, instead of precipitating into a halo of cool clouds.
But the relation and homogeneously
R_{001}
-normalized profiles also indicate that the newly accrete gas does not pile up around the same circular radius or
R_{1}
, but seems to smoothly “move” outward.
The moving out is in a way corresponding only to the existing total
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
mass, but not to the virial radius or halo spin.
The anti-correlation between
R_{001}/R_{1}
and gas richness implies that the edge of the disk may be beyond
R_{001}
, rather than around
R_{1}
.
We expect the results to be also useful to constraining other models and simulations.

Finally, we comment briefly on the strongly interacting galaxies, which tend to have much more extended
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
distribution than other galaxies.
Such distribution increases the interface of
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
with CGM, and thus possibly localized CGM cooling rate through turbulent mixing
[
40
]
.
Previous observations did find statistical evidence for post-mergers to show enhanced
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
richness
[
17
]
, and localized cooling enhancement is consistent with
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
distribution and kinematics in some individual interacting systems (
52
, X. Lin in prep).
However, the universality and exact stage of that happening remains to be quantified.

The results of our work can be re-examined using the up-coming high-resolution and deep observations of moderately-large nearby galaxies at MeerKAT, ngVLA, and SKA, in the future.

Appendix A
Deblending HI disks Undergoing Interactions

We follow similar procedures outlined in
Huang et al. [24]
to perform 3D source de-blending and separate the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
flux in the interacting systems.
It uses optical information as initial guess for positions of galaxies, and utilizes the
watershed
algorithm in the 3D to segment a data cube into individual galaxies.

In brief, we perform a two-pass
watershed
on the FEASTS data cubes.
During the first pass, initial markers are set at voxels contained in the optical disk for each galaxy (i.e., the sky coordinates of the markers are the same in all channels).
The
watershed
algorithm is then executed based on these markers to de-blend the data cubes.
In the second pass, we identify the local maxima of
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
flux density within the data cube region of each galaxy that has been allocated in the first pass, and utilize them to update the markers.
The
watershed
algorithm is run again to achieve the final segmentation of the data cubes.
In
Huang et al. [24]
, we use mock test to show that this procedure successfully segments HI data cubes of merging pairs that have projected distances larger than
0.5(R_{1,1}+R_{1,2})
, or radial velocity differences larger than
0.5(W_{1}+W_{2})
.
The
R_{1,1}
and
R_{1,2}
are the
R_{1}
, and the
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are the integral
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
line width of the two galaxies in each pair.
The uncertainties in
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
flux for the deblended primary galaxies are less than 10%.
And the procedure works significantly better than deblending galaxies on the 2D moment maps.

In Figure
7
, we show two examples of early-stage mergers observed by FEASTS. After de-blending, the
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
flux of the target galaxies is clearly separated from its neighbors in the overlapping region.

Appendix B
The Reliability of HI Size Measurements from FEASTS images

We have developed procedures in previous studies to cross calibrate single-dish and interferometry data
[
51
,
52
]
, and combine them into coherent moment-0 images
[
53
]
.
Because these combined images have the same high spatial resolution as but do not miss flux as the input interferometric images, we can accurately measure
R_{1}
and
R_{001}
from them (see justification in Appendix
C
).
We can downgrade the combined images and simulate FAST observations with them.
Using the measurements from the input combined images as true values, we can test how well
R_{1}
and
R_{001}
can be measured at the resolution of FEASTS, for different true
R_{1}
values.

Among the analysis sample in the main text part, there are 10 galaxies with interferometric data from the surveys HALOGAS (4) or THINGS (6), allowing us to combine them with the single-dish
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
images of FEASTS.
We take two additional single dish+interferometry combined
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
images published in previous studies, NGC 2403
[
12
]
and NGC 5236
[
16
]
.
These 12 combined images are used as the input of simulation.

Here is the procedure of simulating the FAST observation with them.
We shift the input images to different further distances, to mimic disks with smaller apparent sizes.
Expectedly, a wide distance range is allowed for initially larger disks, if we set a minimal
R_{1}
for the simulated disks.
The corresponding true
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
radius are scaled accordingly and recorded.
We then convolved the shifted images with the FAST average beam
[
52
]
, to produce simulated images of FAST.
The FAST beam has a FWHM of 3.24
′
.

We measure
R_{1}
and
R_{001}
from the simulated images (
R_{1,{\rm m}}
and
R_{001,{\rm m}}
), and compare with the true values (
R_{1,{\rm t}}
and
R_{001,{\rm t}}
).
The results are presented in the top row of Figure
8
.
Most data points concentrate into a coherent trend in each panel, but there are a few outlying branches with larger
R_{\rm m}
versus
R_{\rm t}
differences than the other data points.
Each outlying branch corresponds to one original input galaxy, which happen to have significant small-scale structure on the radial
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
profile close to the characteristic radius.
We will need a larger sample in the future, in order to robustly quantify how the coupling of such structures with poor spatial resolution lead to systematic uncertainties in radius measurements.
So far, galaxies with uncertainties of this type in our test have an incidence rate of
3/12
for
R_{1}
, but the highest systematic uncertainty does not exceed 0.065 dex.
The largest systematic uncertainty of this type is higher for
R_{001}
(0.16 dex), but the incidence rate of corresponding galaxies is only
1/12
.
Considering that either the uncertainties or the incidence rates are low for this type of outliers, we focus on the behavior of major trend in the following.

For the best-resolved images in this simulation, the scatter and median values of
R_{\rm m}/R_{\rm t}
are small (
<
0.02 dex) for both
R_{1}
and
R_{001}
measurements.
The median values and scatter of
R_{\rm m}/R_{\rm t}
for both radius increase when disks are smaller.
When the measured
R_{1}=250^{\prime\prime}
(1.28 times the FAST FWHM), the median value of
R_{1,{\rm m}}/R_{1,{\rm t}}
and
R_{001,{\rm m}}/R_{001,{\rm t}}
increase to 0.03 and 0.08 dex, while the corresponding scatters both increase to
\sim
0.02 dex.

All except for two galaxies in the analysis sample have
R_{1}>250^{\prime\prime}
(bottom panel of Figure
8
).
None of the conclusions change if we exclude those two smallest disks from the analysis.
On the other hand, the derived size-mass relations in Section
III.2
have scatters comparable to the offsets (and scatter) found here.
Consistently, in section
III.2
, the mcmc analysis indicates the intrinsic scatter of the
R_{001}
-
M_{\rm HI}
relation to be lower than 0.001.
We thus conclude that the FEASTS images allowed reasonably accurate measure of
R_{1}
and
R_{001}
, but the scatter in each size-mass relation should be significantly contributed by these measurement uncertainties.

B.1
Correcting for Biases and Estimating Uncertainties for
R_{1}
and
R_{001}
Measurements

The median curves in the top panels of Figure
8
are used to correct for biases due to beam smoothing.
Based on the directly measured
R_{1}
, we interpolate the median curves to obtain the smoothing-related biases of
R_{1}
and
R_{001}
with respect to true values for each galaxy.
We estimate the error of this correction as 0.74 times the difference between the 25 and 75 percentiles (equivalent to 1-
\sigma
) at a given directly measured
R_{1}
.

The uncertainties of
R_{1}
and
R_{001}
are estimated as the square root of the quadratic sum of systematic uncertainties due to smoothing correction (described above) and uncertainties due to image errors.

The image errors include pointing errors (
\sim 10^{\prime\prime}
,
Jiang et al. 25
) and noise of pixel values (propagated from the cube noise).
We estimate the image-error-related uncertainties by simulating 100 “perturbed” images for each observed moment-0 image.
We randomly shift the image with extents following a gaussian distribution with
\sigma=10^{\prime\prime}
.
We also add random noise to the pixel values following a gaussian distribution with
\sigma
equivalent to the 1-
\sigma
depth of image.
We run the same
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
and size measuring procedure on these perturbed images as for the real images.
We calculate the standard deviation of
R_{1}
(
R_{001}
) distribution from the 100 perturbed images, and view it as the image-error-related size uncertainty.

Appendix C
The Reliability of HI size Measurements from Combined Images

To optimize the procedures that combine single-dish and interferometry images, we have developed procedures to generate mock galaxies, and simulate single-dish and interferometric observations
[
52
]
.
We tested using different types of interferometric data reduction products as input in the combination.
The data types include the clean model convolved with the clean beam (
I_{\rm conv}
),
I_{\rm conv}
plus rescaled clean residual (
I_{\rm rescaled}
), and
I_{\rm conv}
plus full residual (
I_{\rm standard}
).
Please refer to
[
52
]
for a detailed discussion on pron and cons of these different types as interferometric data products alone.
They are combined with single-dish images with the standard Fourier-space linear combination method, but using improved procedure of
Wang et al. [53]
, which produced the combined images
C_{\rm conv}
,
C_{\rm rescaled}
, and
C_{\rm standard}
, respectively.
By comparing measurements to true values, we were able to find the optimized way of cross-calibrating the single-dish and interferometric data is to use
I_{\rm rescaled}

[
52
]
, while the combined image that most accurately recover
\Sigma_{\rm HI}
near
R_{1}
is
C_{\rm conv}

[
53
]
.

Here we add upon the previous tests presented in
Wang et al. [53]
, and ask which type of combined image best recovers
R_{1}
and
R_{001}
.
We generate 18 sets of mock data.
The first 13 sets have a fixed
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disk size
R_{1}=9^{\prime}
, which are the same mocks used in
Wang et al. [53]
.
Sets 1 to 6 have a fixed integral signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) of 2.8, but integral increasingly larger
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disk power spectral slopes from 1.8 to 3.2.
Sets 7 to 13 have a fixed power spectral slope of 2.2, but decreasing SNR from 4.4 to 0.9.
Sets 14 to 18 have a fixed power spectral slope of 2.2, and a fixed integral SNR of 2.8, but different
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disk sizes, with
R_{1}
decreasing from 9
′
to 5
′
with a step of 1
′
.
The result is present in Figure
9
.
It is clear that from
C_{\rm conv}
, we can obtain most accurate
R_{1}
and
R_{001}
measurements, with offsets from true values less than 0.01 dex in most cases.
There is no strong dependence on power spectral slopes of
{\rm H}{\textsc{i}}
disks or SNRs of data for these results.

We also check and find that the measurements from FAST images are also reasonably good for these mock disks, with offset less than 0.1 dex when the
R_{1,\rm{t}}<5^{\prime}
.
Because the statistics is small, we conduct a more careful investigation on the possibility of accurately measuring sizes from FAST images in Appendix
B
.

The conclusion here is that we can accurately measure
R_{1}
and
R_{001}
from the combined images when the true
R_{1}>5^{\prime}
.
