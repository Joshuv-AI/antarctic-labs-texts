GPU-Accelerated Energy-Conserving Methods for the Hyperbolized Serre–Green–Naghdi Equations in 2D
Collin Wittenstein, Vincent Marks, Mario Ricchiuto, Hendrik Ranocha

Abstract
We develop energy-conserving numerical methods for a two-dimensional hyperbolic approximation of the Serre–Green–Naghdi equations with variable bathymetry for both periodic and reflecting boundary conditions. The hyperbolic formulation avoids the costly inversion of an elliptic operator present in the classical model. Our schemes combine split forms with summation-by-parts (SBP) operators to construct semidiscretizations that conserve the total water mass and the total energy. We provide analytical proofs of these conservation properties and also verify them numerically. While the framework is general, our implementation focuses on second-order finite-difference SBP operators. The methods are implemented in Julia for CPU and GPU architectures (AMD and NVIDIA) and achieve substantial speedups on modern accelerators. We validate the approach through convergence studies based on solitary-wave and manufactured-solution tests, and by comparisons to analytical, experimental, and existing numerical results. All source code to reproduce our results is available online.

2
Review of Split Forms and Summation-by-Parts Operators

In this section, we review the techniques employed for the spatial
discretization of the SGN equations. We utilize split forms combined with
SBP operators to guarantee conservation of a discrete
energy. Following the method of lines approach, we first discretize in space
and subsequently perform the time integration.

2.1
Split Forms

In general, discrete derivative operators do not satisfy discrete analogs of
the product and chain rules
[
56
]
. To address this limitation, we
employ split forms
[
23
]
that mimic those, thereby enabling us
to prove energy conservation solely through integration by parts.

For example, consider the classical 1D shallow water equations with constant
bathymetry

\displaystyle h_{t}+(hu)_{x}=0,

(2.1)

\displaystyle(hu)_{t}+\left(hu^{2}+\frac{1}{2}gh^{2}\right)_{x}=0.

For smooth solutions, these equations satisfy the energy conservation law

\biggl(\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}gh^{2}+\frac{1}{2}hu^{2}}_{=E}\biggr)_{t}+\biggl(\underbrace{gh^{2}u+\frac{1}{2}hu^{3}}_{=F}\biggr)_{x}=0.

(2.2)

A split form of equation (
2.1
) is given by
[
49
,
54
]

\displaystyle h_{t}+h_{x}u+hu_{x}=0,

(2.3)

\displaystyle hu_{t}+g(h^{2})_{x}-ghh_{x}+\frac{1}{2}h(u^{2})_{x}-\frac{1}{2}h_{x}u^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(hu)_{x}u-\frac{1}{2}huu_{x}=0.

Using integration by parts, we can verify that the energy is conserved

\displaystyle\frac{\dif}{\dif t}\int E\dif x

\displaystyle=\frac{\dif}{\dif t}\int\left(\frac{1}{2}gh^{2}+\frac{1}{2}hu^{2}\right)\dif x

(2.4)

\displaystyle=\int\left(ghh_{t}+\frac{1}{2}h_{t}u^{2}+huu_{t}\right)\dif x

\displaystyle=\int\left[gh\left(-h_{x}u-hu_{x}\right)+\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\left(-h_{x}u-hu_{x}\right)\right]\dif x

\displaystyle-\int u\left[g(h^{2})_{x}-ghh_{x}+\frac{1}{2}h(u^{2})_{x}-\frac{1}{2}h_{x}u^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(hu)_{x}u-\frac{1}{2}huu_{x}\right]\dif x

\displaystyle=\int\Bigl(-ghh_{x}u+ghh_{x}u-gh^{2}u_{x}-gu(h^{2})_{x}-\frac{1}{2}u^{3}h_{x}+\frac{1}{2}h_{x}u^{3}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}hu_{x}+\frac{1}{2}hu^{2}u_{x}-\frac{1}{2}hu(u^{2})_{x}-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}(hu)_{x}\Bigr)\dif x=0.

2.2
Summation-by-Parts Operators

SBP operators are discrete derivative operators that mimic
integration by parts and thereby enable the transfer of analytical results from
the continuous setting to discrete settings. An SBP operator consists of a
derivative operator and a mass matrix
M
that defines a quadrature rule.
Originally proposed in the finite difference context
[
39
,
58
,
7
]
, SBP
operators have a broad range of applications, including finite volume

[
46
]
, continuous finite element
[
32
,
33
,
1
]
,
discontinuous Galerkin
[
25
,
6
]
,
flux reconstruction
[
35
,
64
,
52
]
,
active flux methods
[
19
,
3
]
,
and meshfree schemes
[
34
,
40
]
.

The definitions and notations described in this section are standard
in the SBP literature, e.g.,
[
60
,
22
,
51
]
.
Here, we focus on one-dimensional SBP operators and extend them to multiple
dimensions via tensor products as usual for finite difference methods.

We discretize the spatial domain
[x_{\mathrm{min}},x_{\mathrm{max}}]
using a nodal collocation method.
Thus, we first introduce the grid
\boldsymbol{x}=(\boldsymbol{x_{1}},...,\boldsymbol{x_{\mathcal{N}}})^{T}
, where
x_{\mathrm{min}}=\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\leq\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\leq...\leq\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathcal{N}}=x_{\mathrm{max}}
.
The discrete representation
\boldsymbol{u}
of a function

u
consists of its values at the grid points, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}=u(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
.
Nonlinear operations such as
(\boldsymbol{u}^{2})_{i}=\boldsymbol{u}_{i}^{2}
are
evaluated componentwise.
Moreover,
\mathbf{1}=(1,\ldots,1)^{T}
denotes the vector of ones.
Finally, we use the notation

\boldsymbol{e}_{L}=(1,0,\ldots,0)^{T}
,
\boldsymbol{e}_{R}=(0,\ldots,0,1)^{T}
.

2.1
.

Given a grid
\boldsymbol{x}
, a
p
-th order accurate
i
-th derivative matrix
D^{(i)}
is a matrix that satisfies

\forall k\in\{0,\ldots,p\}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\quad D^{(i)}\boldsymbol{x}^{k}=k(k-1)\ldots(k-i+1)\boldsymbol{x}^{k-i},

(2.5)

with the convention
\boldsymbol{x}^{0}=\mathbf{1}
and
0\boldsymbol{x}^{k}=\mathbf{0}
. We say
D^{(i)}
is consistent if
p\geq 0
.

2.2
.

A first-derivative SBP operator consists of a grid
\boldsymbol{x}
, a consistent first-derivative matrix
D
, and a symmetric and positive-definite matrix
M
such that

MD+D^{T}M=\boldsymbol{e}_{R}\boldsymbol{e}_{R}^{T}-\boldsymbol{e}_{L}\boldsymbol{e}_{L}^{T}.

(2.6)

We refer to
M
as a mass matrix or norm matrix. In the periodic case, we define
\boldsymbol{e}_{L/R}=\boldsymbol{0}
such that

MD+D^{T}M=0.

(2.7)

This definition yields a discrete version of integration by parts as

\displaystyle\underbrace{\boldsymbol{u}^{T}MD\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{u}^{T}D^{T}M\boldsymbol{v}}_{\rotatebox{90.0}{$\approx$}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{R}\boldsymbol{e}_{R}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{L}\boldsymbol{e}_{L}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}}_{\rotatebox{90.0}{$\approx$}}

(2.8)

\displaystyle\overbrace{\int_{x_{\min}}^{x_{\max}}u\,(\partial_{x}v)\,dx+\int_{x_{\min}}^{x_{\max}}(\partial_{x}u)\,v\,dx}

\displaystyle=\overbrace{u(x_{\max})\,v(x_{\max})-u(x_{\min})\,v(x_{\min})}.

Since we are dealing with a two-dimensional problem, we also need to consider
SBP operators in multiple dimensions. Following
[
55
]
,
we provide the following definition:

2.3
Multidimensional SBP operator.

An SBP operator on a
d
-dimensional element
\Omega
with order of accuracy
p\in\mathbb{N}
consists of the following components:

•

Derivative operators

D_{j}
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, approximating the partial derivative in the
j
-th coordinate direction. These are required to be exact for polynomials of degree
\leq p
.

•

Mass matrix

M
, approximating the
L_{2}
scalar product on
\Omega
via

\boldsymbol{u}^{T}M\boldsymbol{v}=\langle\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{M}\approx\langle u,v\rangle_{L_{2}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}uv\,dx,

(2.9)

where
u,v
are functions on
\Omega
and
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}
their approximations in the SBP basis (i.e., their projections onto the grid).

•

Restriction operator

R
, performing interpolation of functions from the volume
\Omega
to the boundary
\partial\Omega
.

•

Boundary mass matrix

B
, approximating the
L_{2}
scalar product on
\partial\Omega
via

\boldsymbol{u}_{B}^{T}B\boldsymbol{v}_{B}=\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{B},\boldsymbol{v}_{B}\rangle_{B}\approx\langle u_{B},v_{B}\rangle_{L_{2}(\partial\Omega)}=\int_{\partial\Omega}u_{B}v_{B}\,ds,

(2.10)

where
u_{B},v_{B}
are functions on
\partial\Omega
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{B},\boldsymbol{v}_{B}
their approximations in the SBP basis.

•

Multiplication operators

N_{j}
,
j\in\{x,y,z,\ldots\}
, performing multiplication of functions on the boundary
\partial\Omega
with the
j
-th component
n_{j}
of the outer unit normal vector. Thus, if
\boldsymbol{u}
is the approximation of a function
u|_{\Omega}
in the SBP basis, then
R\boldsymbol{u}
is the approximation of
u|_{\partial\Omega}
on the boundary, and
N_{j}R\boldsymbol{u}
is the approximation of
n_{j}u|_{\partial\Omega}
, where
n_{j}
is the
j
-th component of the outer unit normal at
\partial\Omega
.

•

The restriction and boundary operators satisfy

\boldsymbol{u}^{T}R^{T}BN_{j}R\boldsymbol{v}\approx\int_{\partial\Omega}uvn_{j}\,ds,

(2.11)

where
n_{j}
is the
j
-th component of the outer unit normal vector
n
, and this approximation has to be exact for polynomials of degree
\leq p
.

•

Finally, the
SBP property

MD_{j}+D_{j}^{T}M=R^{T}BN_{j}R

(2.12)

has to be fulfilled, mimicking the divergence theorem on a discrete level

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u\,(\partial_{j}v)\,dx+\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{j}u)\,v\,dx

(2.13)

\displaystyle\approx\boldsymbol{u}^{T}MD_{j}\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{u}^{T}D_{j}^{T}M\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{u}^{T}R^{T}BN_{j}R\boldsymbol{v}\approx\int_{\partial\Omega}uvn_{j}\,ds.

In one space dimension, the indices of the derivative and multiplication operators
D_{x}
,
N_{x}
are typically dropped. Furthermore, the boundary matrix is the
2\times 2
identity matrix
B=\operatorname{diag}(1,1)
, and multiplication with the outer normal is given by
N=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1)
, i.e.,

BN=\boldsymbol{e}_{R}\boldsymbol{e}_{R}^{T}-\boldsymbol{e}_{L}\boldsymbol{e}_{L}^{T}
.

Further information about SBP operators in multidimensional settings can be
found in
[
32
,
53
]
. We use tensor
products of one-dimensional SBP operators as usual for finite difference
methods. The concrete operators employed in this work are specified in Remark
4.4
.

3
Review of the Serre–Green–Naghdi Equations

In this section, we review the two-dimensional SGN
equations in both their classical form and their hyperbolic approximation
with variable bathymetry. For each formulation, we present the corresponding
energy conservation law.

3.1
Classical Form with Variable Bathymetry

The classical two-dimensional SGN equations with variable bathymetry
[
27
]
read

\displaystyle h_{t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}(hv_{j})=0,

(3.1)

\displaystyle(hv_{i})_{t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left(hv_{i}v_{j}+\left(\frac{gh^{2}}{2}+\frac{h^{2}}{3}\left(\ddot{h}+\frac{3}{2}\ddot{b}\right)\right)\delta_{ij}\right)+\left(gh+h\left(\ddot{b}+\frac{1}{2}\ddot{h}\right)\right)\frac{\partial b}{\partial x_{i}}=0.

Here, the dot notation denotes the material derivative along the velocity

\dot{f}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+u\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}

(3.2)

for any scalar function
f(t,x,y)
, and two dots denote the
corresponding second material derivative.
For
b_{t}=0
, the system satisfies the energy conservation law

(h\mathcal{E})_{t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\left(h\mathcal{E}+\frac{gh^{2}}{2}+\frac{h^{2}}{3}\left(\ddot{h}+\frac{3}{2}\ddot{b}\right)\right)v_{j}\right)=0

(3.3)

with

\mathcal{E}=\frac{u^{2}+v^{2}}{2}+g\left(\frac{h}{2}+b\right)+\frac{1}{6}\left(\dot{h}+\frac{3}{2}\dot{b}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{8}\dot{b}^{2}.

(3.4)

3.2
Hyperbolic Approximation with Variable Bathymetry

The classical SGN equations require solving an elliptic system to obtain
the time derivatives of the velocity at each time step, which is
computationally expensive
[
27
]
.
An alternative approach is
to use a hyperbolic approximation of the SGN equations
[
21
]
. The
key idea is as follows: Starting from the master Lagrangian of the SGN
equations
[
21
]
, we introduce additional potentials and a constant

\lambda
, adding them to the master Lagrangian to create an augmented
Lagrangian. The Euler–Lagrange equations of this augmented Lagrangian converge
to the original SGN equations as
\lambda\to\infty
. If these Euler–Lagrange
equations possess additional properties (such as unconditional hyperbolicity;
see
[
21
]
for details), they are referred to as a hyperbolic
approximation.
Previous work has derived a two-dimensional hyperbolic approximation of the SGN
equations
[
5
]
and rigorously established its
properties
[
14
]
.

The two-dimensional hyperbolic SGN equations with variable bathymetry are given
by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}(hv_{k})

\displaystyle=0,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial hv_{i}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\left(hv_{i}v_{k}+\left(\frac{1}{2}gh^{2}+hp\right)\delta_{ik}\right)+\left(gh+\frac{3}{2}\frac{h}{\eta}p\right)\frac{\partial b}{\partial x_{i}}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial h\eta}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}(h\eta v_{k})+\frac{3}{2}hv_{k}\frac{\partial b}{\partial x_{k}}

\displaystyle=hw,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial hw}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}(hwv_{k})

\displaystyle=\lambda\left(1-\frac{\eta}{h}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial b}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=0,

where

p(h,\eta)=\frac{\lambda}{3}\frac{\eta}{h}\left(1-\frac{\eta}{h}\right).

(3.6)

This system also satisfies an energy conservation law

\Biggl(\underbrace{h\left(\frac{u^{2}+v^{2}}{2}+e\right)}_{E}\Biggr)_{t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\Biggl(\underbrace{\left(h\left(\frac{u^{2}+v^{2}}{2}+e\right)+P\right)v_{k}}_{F_{k}}\Biggr)=0

(3.7)

with

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}w^{2}+\frac{g}{2}(h+2b)+\frac{\lambda}{6}\left(\frac{\eta}{h}-1\right)^{2},

(3.8)

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\frac{gh^{2}}{2}+\frac{\lambda}{3}\eta\left(1-\frac{\eta}{h}\right).

In the limit
\lambda\to\infty
, we (formally) obtain

\eta=h,\quad w=-h\cdot(\partial_{x}u+\partial_{y}v)+\frac{3}{2}\cdot(u\partial_{x}b+v\partial_{y}b).

(3.9)

3.1
.

Note that the hyperbolic approximation presented here is based on
the mild-slope approximation. In
[
5
]
, the augmented
Lagrangian used to derive the hyperbolic formulation is simplified
by assuming time-independent bathymetry (
b_{t}=0
) and small gradients of
b
.

4
Energy-Conserving Methods for the 2D Hyperbolization

In this section, we extend existing energy-conserving methods for the SGN
equations in one space dimension
[
49
]
to the two-dimensional
hyperbolic SGN equations with variable bathymetry for periodic and reflecting
boundary conditions.

4.1
Periodic Boundary Conditions

For periodic boundary conditions, we propose the semidiscretization

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{h}_{t}+\boldsymbol{u}D_{x}\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{h}D_{x}\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{v}D_{y}\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{h}D_{y}\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0},

(4.1)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{t}+gD_{x}\left(\boldsymbol{h}(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{b})\right)-g(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{b})D_{x}\boldsymbol{h}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}D_{x}(\boldsymbol{u}^{2})-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{u}^{2}D_{x}\boldsymbol{h}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{u}D_{x}(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u})-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u}D_{x}\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}D_{y}(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v})-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v}D_{y}\boldsymbol{h}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{v}D_{y}\boldsymbol{u}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u}D_{y}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda}{6}\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{h}^{2}}D_{x}\boldsymbol{h}+\frac{\lambda}{3}D_{x}\boldsymbol{\eta}-\frac{\lambda}{3}\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}}{\boldsymbol{h}}D_{x}\boldsymbol{\eta}-\frac{\lambda}{6}D_{x}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{h}}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(1-\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}}{\boldsymbol{h}}\right)D_{x}\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{0},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{v}_{t}+gD_{y}\left(\boldsymbol{h}(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{b})\right)-g(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{b})D_{y}\boldsymbol{h}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}D_{y}(\boldsymbol{v}^{2})-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{v}^{2}D_{y}\boldsymbol{h}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{v}D_{y}(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{v})-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{v}D_{y}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}D_{x}(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v})-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v}D_{x}\boldsymbol{h}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u}D_{x}\boldsymbol{v}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{v}D_{x}\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda}{6}\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{h}^{2}}D_{y}\boldsymbol{h}+\frac{\lambda}{3}D_{y}\boldsymbol{\eta}-\frac{\lambda}{3}\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}}{\boldsymbol{h}}D_{y}\boldsymbol{\eta}-\frac{\lambda}{6}D_{y}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{h}}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(1-\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}}{\boldsymbol{h}}\right)D_{y}\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{0},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}D_{x}(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{w})+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u}D_{x}\boldsymbol{w}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{w}D_{x}\boldsymbol{h}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{w}D_{x}\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}D_{y}(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{w})+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{v}D_{y}\boldsymbol{w}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{w}D_{y}\boldsymbol{h}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{w}D_{y}\boldsymbol{v}=\lambda\left(1-\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}}{\boldsymbol{h}}\right),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}+\boldsymbol{u}D_{x}\boldsymbol{\eta}+\boldsymbol{v}D_{y}\boldsymbol{\eta}+\frac{3}{2}\boldsymbol{u}D_{x}\boldsymbol{b}+\frac{3}{2}\boldsymbol{v}D_{y}\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{w}.

4.1
.

Consider the semidiscretization (
4.1
) of the two-dimensional hyperbolic approximation of the
SGN equations (
3.5
) with periodic boundary conditions.
If
D_{x},D_{y}
are periodic first-derivative SBP operators
with diagonal mass/norm matrix
M
,

1.

the total water mass
\mathbf{1}^{T}M\boldsymbol{h}
is conserved.

2.

the total energy
\mathbf{1}^{T}M\boldsymbol{E}
is conserved.

Proof.

Part 1:
We get

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\boldsymbol{h})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{h}

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\boldsymbol{u}D_{x}\boldsymbol{h}-\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\boldsymbol{h}D_{x}\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\boldsymbol{v}D_{y}\boldsymbol{h}-\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\boldsymbol{h}D_{y}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=-(\boldsymbol{u}^{T}MD_{x}\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{h}^{T}MD_{x}\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{v}^{T}MD_{y}\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{h}^{T}MD_{y}\boldsymbol{v})=0

using the periodic SBP property
MD_{d}+D_{d}^{T}M=0
for
d\in\{1,2\}
.

Part 2:
To show energy conservation, we have to show that

-\partial_{\boldsymbol{q}}\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{q}=\partial_{\boldsymbol{h}}\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{h}+\partial_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}+\partial_{\boldsymbol{v}}\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}+\partial_{\boldsymbol{w}}\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{w}+\partial_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\eta}=0\\

(4.2)

is satisfied, with the energy

\boldsymbol{E}=\boldsymbol{h}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{u}^{2}+\boldsymbol{v}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{6}\boldsymbol{w}^{2}+\frac{g}{2}(\boldsymbol{h}+2\boldsymbol{b})+\frac{\lambda}{6}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}}{\boldsymbol{h}}-1\right)^{2}\right)

and its derivatives

\displaystyle\partial_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{E}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u},

(4.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{\boldsymbol{v}}\boldsymbol{E}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{v},

\displaystyle\partial_{\boldsymbol{w}}\boldsymbol{E}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{3}\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{w},

\displaystyle\partial_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\boldsymbol{E}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{\lambda}{3}\!\left(1-\tfrac{\boldsymbol{\eta}}{\boldsymbol{h}}\right),

\displaystyle\partial_{\boldsymbol{h}}\boldsymbol{E}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{u}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{v}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{6}\boldsymbol{w}^{2}+g\boldsymbol{h}+g\boldsymbol{b}+\tfrac{\lambda}{6}\!\left(1-\tfrac{\boldsymbol{\eta}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{h}^{2}}\right).

We compute

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\boldsymbol{E})

\displaystyle=-\left(\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\partial_{\boldsymbol{h}}\boldsymbol{E}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\partial_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{E}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\partial_{\boldsymbol{v}}\boldsymbol{E}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\partial_{\boldsymbol{w}}\boldsymbol{E}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\partial_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\boldsymbol{E}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\eta}\right)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\left(g\boldsymbol{h}+g\boldsymbol{b}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{u}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{v}^{2}+\frac{1}{6}\boldsymbol{w}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{6}\left(1-\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{h}^{2}}\right)\right)\left(\boldsymbol{u}D_{x}\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{h}D_{x}\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{v}D_{y}\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{h}D_{y}\boldsymbol{v}\right)

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\boldsymbol{u}\left(gD_{x}(\boldsymbol{h}(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{b}))-g(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{b})D_{x}\boldsymbol{h}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}D_{x}\boldsymbol{u}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{u}^{2}D_{x}\boldsymbol{h}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{u}D_{x}(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u})\right.

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u}D_{x}\boldsymbol{u}+\frac{1}{2}D_{y}(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v})+\frac{1}{2}\left(-\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v}D_{y}\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{v}D_{y}\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u}D_{y}\boldsymbol{v}\right)

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{\lambda}{6}\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{h}^{2}}D_{x}\boldsymbol{h}+\frac{\lambda}{3}D_{x}\boldsymbol{\eta}-\frac{\lambda}{3}\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}}{\boldsymbol{h}}D_{x}\boldsymbol{\eta}-\frac{\lambda}{6}D_{x}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{h}}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(1-\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}}{\boldsymbol{h}}\right)D_{x}\boldsymbol{b}\right)

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\boldsymbol{v}\Bigl(gD_{y}(\boldsymbol{h}(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{b}))-g(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{b})D_{y}\boldsymbol{h}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}D_{y}\left(\boldsymbol{v}^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{v}^{2}D_{y}\boldsymbol{h}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{v}D_{y}(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{v}D_{y}\boldsymbol{v}+\frac{1}{2}D_{x}(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v})+\frac{1}{2}\left(-\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v}D_{x}\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u}D_{x}\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{v}D_{x}\boldsymbol{u}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{6}\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{h}^{2}}D_{y}\boldsymbol{h}+\frac{\lambda}{3}D_{y}\boldsymbol{\eta}

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda}{3}\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}}{\boldsymbol{h}}D_{y}\boldsymbol{\eta}-\frac{\lambda}{6}D_{y}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{h}}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(1-\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}}{\boldsymbol{h}}\right)D_{y}\boldsymbol{b}\Bigr)

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\left(\frac{1}{3}\boldsymbol{w}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}D_{x}(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{w})+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u}D_{x}\boldsymbol{w}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{w}D_{x}\boldsymbol{h}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{w}D_{x}\boldsymbol{u}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{1}{2}D_{y}(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{w})+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{v}D_{y}\boldsymbol{w}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{w}D_{y}\boldsymbol{h}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{w}D_{y}\boldsymbol{v}-\lambda\left(1-\frac{\boldsymbol{\eta}}{\boldsymbol{h}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle+g(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{b})^{T}MD_{x}\boldsymbol{u}+g(\boldsymbol{b}\boldsymbol{v})^{T}MD_{y}\boldsymbol{h}+g(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{b})^{T}MD_{y}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=g(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u})^{T}MD_{x}\boldsymbol{h}+g(\boldsymbol{b}\boldsymbol{u})^{T}MD_{x}\boldsymbol{h}-g\left(\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{b})\right)^{T}MD_{x}\boldsymbol{h}

The NHS part is

\displaystyle=0.

Thus, the semidiscretization conserves the energy.

4.3
.

Consider the semidiscretization (
4.5
) of the two-dimensional hyperbolic approximation of the
SGN equations (
3.5
) with reflecting boundary conditions.
If
D_{x},D_{y}
are non-periodic first-derivative SBP operators
with diagonal mass/norm matrix
M
,

1.

the total water mass
\mathbf{1}^{T}M\boldsymbol{h}
is conserved.

2.

the total energy
\mathbf{1}^{T}M\boldsymbol{E}
is conserved.

Proof.

Part 1:
We get

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\boldsymbol{h})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{h}

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\boldsymbol{u}D_{x}\boldsymbol{h}-\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\boldsymbol{v}D_{y}\boldsymbol{h}-\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\boldsymbol{h}D_{x}\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\boldsymbol{h}D_{y}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{1}^{T}R^{T}BN_{x}R(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u})+\boldsymbol{1}^{T}R^{T}BN_{y}R(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{u}^{T}MD_{x}\boldsymbol{h}-\boldsymbol{h}^{T}MD_{x}\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}^{T}MD_{y}\boldsymbol{h}-\boldsymbol{h}^{T}MD_{y}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{1}^{T}R^{T}BN_{x}R(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u})+\boldsymbol{1}^{T}R^{T}BN_{y}R(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{u}^{T}(R^{T}BN_{x}R-D_{x}^{T}M)\boldsymbol{h}-\boldsymbol{h}^{T}MD_{x}\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{h}^{T}(R^{T}BN_{x}R)\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{v}^{T}(R^{T}BN_{y}R-D_{y}^{T}M)\boldsymbol{h}-\boldsymbol{h}^{T}MD_{y}\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{h}^{T}(R^{T}BN_{y}R)\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{u}^{T}(R^{T}BN_{x}R)\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{h}^{T}(R^{T}BN_{x}R)\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}^{T}(R^{T}BN_{y}R)\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{h}^{T}(R^{T}BN_{y}R)\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{u}^{T}D_{x}^{T}M\boldsymbol{h}-\boldsymbol{h}^{T}MD_{x}\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{v}^{T}D_{y}^{T}M\boldsymbol{h}-\boldsymbol{h}^{T}MD_{y}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=0

using the SBP property.

Part 2: We compute

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}(\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\boldsymbol{E})

\displaystyle=-\left(\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\partial_{\boldsymbol{h}}\boldsymbol{E}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{h}+\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\partial_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{E}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\partial_{\boldsymbol{v}}\boldsymbol{E}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\partial_{\boldsymbol{w}}\boldsymbol{E}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{1}^{T}M\partial_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\boldsymbol{E}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\eta}\right)

4.4
Concrete SBP operators and SATs.

We provide explicit examples of one-dimensional second-order accurate SBP operators and their associated mass matrices for both periodic and reflecting boundary conditions. These operators are constructed on uniform grids with spacing
\Delta x
and are employed in all numerical experiments in Section
5
.

For
periodic boundary conditions
, the derivative operator and mass matrix are given by
[
24
]

\displaystyle D_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\Delta x}\begin{pmatrix}0&1&&&-1\\
-1&0&1&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&-1&0&1\\
1&&&-1&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{N}_{x}\times\mathcal{N}_{x}},\quad M=\Delta x\operatorname{diag}(1,1,\ldots,1,1).

(4.6)

One can verify that
MD_{x}+D_{x}^{T}M=0
, as required for periodic SBP operators.

For
reflecting boundary conditions
, the derivative operator employs the standard second-order accurate central finite difference stencil in the interior and first-order accurate one-sided finite differences at the boundaries
[
42
]
, yielding

\displaystyle D_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\Delta x}\begin{pmatrix}-2&2&&&\\
-1&0&1&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&-1&0&1\\
&&&-2&2\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{N}_{x}\times\mathcal{N}_{x}},\quad M=\Delta x\operatorname{diag}(\tfrac{1}{2},1,\ldots,1,\tfrac{1}{2}).

(4.7)

For these operators, the SBP property
MD_{x}+D_{x}^{T}M=\boldsymbol{e}_{R}\boldsymbol{e}_{R}^{T}-\boldsymbol{e}_{L}\boldsymbol{e}_{L}^{T}
holds, where
\boldsymbol{e}_{L}=(1,0,\ldots,0)^{T}
and
\boldsymbol{e}_{R}=(0,\ldots,0,1)^{T}
. Note that the reduced boundary weights of
1/2
in the mass matrix arise from the trapezoidal quadrature rule.

The
SATs
in the semidiscretization (
4.5
) act only at boundary nodes. The term
M^{-1}R^{T}BN_{x}R(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u})
evaluates to

\bigl(M^{-1}R^{T}BN_{x}R(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u})\bigr)_{i,j}=\begin{cases}-\dfrac{2}{\Delta x}(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u})_{1,j}&\text{if }i=1,\\[8.00003pt]
+\dfrac{2}{\Delta x}(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{u})_{\mathcal{N}_{x},j}&\text{if }i=\mathcal{N}_{x},\\[5.0pt]
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(4.8)

where the signs arise from the outer unit normal
-1
at the left boundary and
+1
at the right boundary, and the factor
2/\Delta x
comes from the boundary weight of the inverted mass matrix. Analogously, the term
M^{-1}R^{T}BN_{y}R(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{v})
evaluates to

\bigl(M^{-1}R^{T}BN_{y}R(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{v})\bigr)_{i,j}=\begin{cases}-\dfrac{2}{\Delta y}(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{v})_{i,1}&\text{if }j=1,\\[8.00003pt]
+\dfrac{2}{\Delta y}(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{v})_{i,\mathcal{N}_{y}}&\text{if }j=\mathcal{N}_{y},\\[5.0pt]
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(4.9)

At corner nodes, both contributions are added.

5.1.1
One-Dimensional Solitary Wave

The one-dimensional Serre–Green–Naghdi equations possess an exact solitary wave

[

\begin{split}h=h_{\infty}\left(1+\varepsilon\operatorname{sech}^{2}\bigl(\kappa(x-Ct)\bigr)\right),\quad u=C\left(1-\dfrac{h_{\infty}}{h}\right),\end{split}

\kappa^{2}=\dfrac{3\varepsilon}{4h_{\infty}^{2}(1+\varepsilon)}\;,\quad C^{2}=gh_{\infty}(1+\varepsilon).

This solution serves as a useful test case for our two-dimensional

solution of the original equations for validation of the hyperbolic

[-30,30]
and is uniformly repeated in the other
direction to ensure a two-dimensional setup, using periodic boundary conditions
in both directions. The time interval is chosen such that the wave traverses
the domain exactly once.
Figure 2
shows the convergence

x

The convergence results for propagation in the
y

4.3
, the spatial
semidiscretization preserves the discrete energy exactly. While standard
explicit Runge-Kutta methods do not conserve nonlinear invariants such as
energy in the fully discrete scheme, a semidiscretization that conserves energy
exactly ensures that energy errors arise solely from time integration. This
typically leads to improved long-term qualitative behavior and numerical
stability. To verify the correctness of our implementation, we numerically
evaluate the time rate of change of energy directly from the semidiscretization
and confirm that it vanishes to machine precision.

We perform this verification using the setup from
Section
5.8
, in which a solitary wave travels over a
Gaussian-shaped obstacle, providing a nontrivial fully two-dimensional flow
with wave-bathymetry interactions. Figure
4
shows
the computed values of
\langle\partial_{\mathbf{q}}E,\partial_{t}\mathbf{q}\rangle_{M}
for both periodic and reflecting boundary conditions over an
extended simulation time. The energy time derivative remains at the level of
floating-point errors, confirming that the spatial semidiscretization conserves
energy to within machine precision. This validates both the theoretical
analysis and the correctness of our numerical implementation.

:

Motivated by the setup in

h(x,0)=h_{R}+\frac{h_{L}-h_{R}}{2}\bigl(1-\tanh(x/2)\bigr),\qquad u(x,0)=0.

(5.5)

Using the Riemann invariants of the shallow-water equations together with the
Whitham modulation theory for the SGN system
[
17

26

61

approximate expressions for the mean state
\left(h^{*},u^{*}\right)
separating
the rarefaction region from the dispersive shock zone

h^{*}=\frac{\bigl(\sqrt{h_{L}}+\sqrt{h_{R}}\bigr)^{2}}{4},\qquad u^{*}=2\bigl(\sqrt{gh^{*}}-\sqrt{gh_{R}}\bigr),\qquad a^{+}=\delta_{0}-\frac{1}{12}\delta_{0}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\delta_{0}^{3}).

(5.6)

Here
a^{+}
denotes a second-order asymptotic approximation of the leading soliton amplitude with
\delta_{0}=|h_{L}-h_{R}|
. In the following, we choose
h_{L}=1.8
and
h_{R}=1.0
and solve up to time
t=47.434
. The spatial domain in the
x
-direction is
[-600,600]
with
\Delta x=0.3
.
As before, the setup is uniformly repeated in the
y
-direction.
For these values of
h_{L}
and
h_{R}
, we theoretically expect
h^{*}\approx 1.37
and
a^{+}\approx 0.747
.
Note that
a^{+}
is the amplitude above the background height of
1
.
Hence, the maximum water height is
h^{m}=1+a^{+}\approx 1.74
.
Figure
8
displays the solution in the interval
[-300,300]
. Both

and

are in good agreement with the theoretical predictions.

5.6
Favre Waves

We also tested our semidiscretization using Favre waves, following the setups in
[
8
,
36
]
. A
bore represents a transition between two streams with different water depths.
The intensity of such a bore can be characterized by the Froude number

\text{Fr}=\sqrt{(1+\varepsilon)(1+\varepsilon/2)},

(5.7)

where
\varepsilon
denotes the
nonlinearity parameter. Larger values of
\varepsilon
correspond to greater
height differences between the water streams. For small Froude numbers
(
\text{Fr}\leq 1.3
), the bore exhibits a smooth profile followed by undular
wave trains, whereas larger Froude numbers lead to increasingly steep bores
that eventually form breaking waves. In this work, we focus on bores with small
Froude numbers, commonly referred to as Favre waves.

The initial condition for this setup is

\displaystyle h(x,t=0)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=h_{0}+\frac{\llbracket h\rrbracket}{2}\left\{1-\tanh\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\alpha}\right)\right\},

(5.8)

\displaystyle u(x,t=0)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=u_{0}+\frac{\llbracket u\rrbracket}{2}\left\{1-\tanh\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\alpha}\right)\right\},

where

\llbracket h\rrbracket\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\varepsilon h_{0},

(5.9)

with
\varepsilon
being the nonlinearity parameter, and with
\llbracket u\rrbracket
satisfying the shallow-water Rankine–Hugoniot relations, and in
particular

\llbracket u\rrbracket=\sqrt{g\frac{h_{1}+h_{0}}{2h_{0}h_{1}}}\llbracket h\rrbracket.

(5.10)

5.9
Performance Comparison

To provide an assessment of computational performance across different hardware
platforms, we benchmark the total runtime of the wave over Gaussian obstacle
test case from Section
5.8
to final time
t=12
s
with varying grid resolutions
\mathcal{N}_{x}=2^{n}
and
\mathcal{N}_{y}=\mathcal{N}_{x}/2
.

We emphasize that this is not a comprehensive performance study, but rather a
single-run benchmark on each platform to obtain rough estimates of
computational efficiency and scaling behavior.
The three hardware platforms tested are:

•

CPU: Intel Core i7-1185G7

•

AMD GPU: Instinct MI210

•

Nvidia GPU: H100

Figure
15
presents the wallclock time as a function of
grid resolution. The GPU implementations demonstrate substantial performance
advantages over the CPU, particularly at higher resolutions when the overhead
becomes negligible. The Nvidia H100 shows the best performance across all
problem sizes tested, followed by the AMD MI210, with the CPU implementation
being significantly slower for larger grids, as expected.

Extrapolating the CPU time for
\mathcal{N}_{x}=8192
yields an estimated runtime
exceeding one day, whereas the H100 completes the same computation in less than
15 minutes. These results demonstrate that GPU acceleration is essential
for large problem sizes or long-term simulations.

An interesting observation is that the runtime scales approximately as

\mathcal{N}_{x}^{2.7}
(or equivalently as
\mathcal{N}_{\text{total}}^{1.35}
where

\mathcal{N}_{\text{total}}
is the total number of grid points). This super-linear
scaling is primarily attributable to the adaptive time stepping: smaller
spatial grid spacing
\Delta x
imposes more restrictive Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) conditions,
requiring additional time steps to reach the final time.

Figure 15
:

\mathcal{N}_{x}
for CPU (Intel Core i7-1185G7), AMD GPU (Instinct MI210), and Nvidia GPU (H100). The problem size scales as
\mathcal{N}_{x}^{2}/2
. Note the log-log scale. These results are from single runs on each platform, so no error bars are shown.

To account for the influence of adaptive time stepping on the observed
scaling, we normalize the runtime by the number of right-hand-side evaluations
performed during the simulation. Figure
16

shows the average time per right-hand-side evaluation as a function of the
problem size
\mathcal{N}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{N}_{x}\times\mathcal{N}_{y}
. As expected, the scaling

calculated by dividing the total wallclock time by the number of RHS

large problem sizes, this overhead becomes negligible compared to the total

Note that in all benchmarks, as in all other simulations presented in this

This choice was made because we encountered unexpected

switching to 32-bit precision on an H100 GPU typically yields an additional speedup of
about a factor of 2.

6
Summary and Conclusions

In this work, we developed structure-preserving numerical schemes for the
two-dimensional hyperbolic approximation of the SGN equations with variable
bathymetry. Using the framework of summation-by-parts operators and split
forms, we derived semidiscretizations that conserve both total mass and total
energy at the discrete level for both periodic and reflecting boundary
conditions (Section
4
). The implementation in Julia
enabled efficient simulations on both CPU and GPU platforms and is
available in our reproducibility repository
[
67
]
.

We tested our numerical methods using a comprehensive set of cases. Convergence
studies using solitary wave solutions of the SGN equations
(Section
5.1.1
) and manufactured solutions
(Section
5.1.2
) confirmed second-order spatial
accuracy for all variables. We numerically verified that the spatial
semidiscretization conserves energy to machine precision, as predicted by the
theoretical analysis. Comparisons with experimental data and
numerical reference solutions from the literature demonstrated good agreement
(Sections
5.3
,
5.4
,
5.5
,
5.6
,
5.7
,
5.8
).

The present study also revealed the inherent limitations of the SGN framework.
In the Favre wave simulations (Section
5.6
) with large Froude
numbers (
\text{Fr}\gtrsim 1.25
), the numerical solution crashed due to wave
steepening that led to breaking, a physical regime not captured by the SGN
equations without wave breaking modeling.

Several avenues exist for extending this work. Systematic investigations of
long-time soliton propagation errors using relaxation Runge-Kutta (RRK) schemes
would be valuable. Additionally, split-form discretizations that conserve the
total momentum for flat bathymetry and periodic boundary conditions could be investigated.
