A high-dimensional steady-state structural framework for regional transmission interface capacity planning using physics-embedded graph representation learning
Authors: Dongliang Zhang, Ying Mu, Dashun Guan, Wanlei Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The increasing deployment of heterogeneous flexibility resources, including energy storage, pumped-hydro units, and demand response, has substantially reshaped steady-state power-flow patterns in multi-area transmission networks. Conventional transmission planning approaches based on fixed dispatch assumptions or a limited set of representative snapshots are often insufficient to capture the structural effects induced by coordinated flexibility across regions. This paper proposes a physics-informed learning framework for planning-level analysis of steady-state transmission interfaces, with the objective of characterizing the structural behavior of power flows over a broad ensemble of plausible future operating conditions. A large set of representative steady-state scenarios is constructed to reflect long-horizon variations in renewable generation, load profiles, and flexibility activation. These scenarios are embedded into a graph-based representation learning architecture that integrates nodal injections, PTDF-guided propagation, and nonlinear structural correction mechanisms. The learned representation enables the extraction of planning-oriented metrics, including interface flow envelopes, structural sensitivity gradients, stress persistence indicators, and weak-corridor identification scores. Case studies on a realistic multi-area test system show that several critical transmission interfaces exhibit persistent proximity to their structural limits, with sensitivity levels exceeding 90 MW per 100 MW of regional injection shift and stress persistence ratios above 80% across the scenario ensemble. The results further indicate that flexibility deployment does not uniformly alleviate congestion: while some corridors experience a reduction in structural stress, others exhibit amplified interface loading under coordinated storage and demand-response actions. By explicitly capturing these interaction-driven structural behaviors and their associated percentages, the proposed framework provides a complementary analytical tool for transmission planning, enabling interface screening and reinforcement prioritization beyond snapshot-based or purely linear analyses.

Introduction

The structural behavior of transmission networks is undergoing profound changes as modern power systems evolve into landscapes shaped by geographically concentrated renewable resources, increasingly versatile energy storage technologies, and rapidly expanding demand-side flexibility1. Traditional planning practices, which have long relied on deterministic snapshots and fixed dispatch patterns to evaluate steady-state flows, are finding themselves increasingly mismatched with the realities of flexibility-rich grids2. The spatial distribution of injections has become more volatile, the interactions among resource types more interdependent, and the influence of region-to-region balancing more nonlinear than in any previous generation of electric systems3. These developments have intensified the need for analytical tools capable of capturing the high-dimensional manifold of possible steady-state outcomes that arise when multiple heterogeneous resources co-exist and participate in shaping long-term power-flow patterns4.

Historically, much of the transmission planning literature centered on simplified representations of steady-state operation5. Deterministic power-flow calculations, sensitivity-based transfer capability studies, N-1 security screening, and linearized optimal power flow approximations formed the methodological backbone of planning decisions6. These were serviceable approaches in systems dominated by conventional generation units whose behavior was predictable and whose operating envelopes were bounded by relatively stable load-growth patterns7. With the steady emergence of wind, solar, distributed generation, large-scale storage, and digitalized demand response, the underlying assumptions that made classical methods reliable have weakened considerably8. Renewable injections no longer behave as perturbations around a stable base case, and demand flexibility can now reshape the location and magnitude of effective loads in ways that traditional peak-valley models are unable to foresee9. The structural implications of these transformations are not adequately captured through manually curated scenario sets or low-dimensional approximations of operating conditions10.

In response to renewable uncertainty, several research strands extended classical planning with stochastic or scenario-based techniques, generating numerous renewable profiles to better represent variability11. Yet these advancements mostly preserved the linear modeling core and did not deeply examine the nonlinear structural distortions induced by coordinated flexibility actions12. Probabilistic methods attempted to quantify variability and provide reliability metrics, but their focus remained on uncertainty rather than on the emerging interaction patterns that truly reshape long-term structural flow distributions13. Other contributions explored the operational coordination of energy storage, demand response, and hybrid resource portfolios, usually within short-term economic dispatch or security-constrained OPF formulations14. While this operational research offered valuable insights into flexibility scheduling, it largely sidestepped the deeper planning question: how do these resources collectively alter the geometry of steady-state power flows across regional boundaries and transmission interfaces15?

At the same time, machine-learning research in power systems developed rapidly, producing advances in renewable forecasting, OPF surrogates, and topology-aware neural architectures16. Novel applications of graph neural networks, physics-informed neural networks, and differentiable power-flow solvers demonstrated the potential of data-driven tools to approximate complex physical relationships17. However, the overwhelming majority of these works were targeted toward operational or real-time tasks rather than toward transmission planning18. Learning-based OPF models aimed to approximate specific solutions quickly; neural forecasting tools predicted renewable or load profiles; graph embeddings attempted to reconstruct flows under fixed or lightly varying conditions19. Very little attention was directed toward understanding how these methods might be adapted to discover broader structural relationships across a full ensemble of plausible future steady-state operating conditions20. The needs of long-term planning–including interface stress characterization, estimation of reinforcement requirements, and identification of persistent weak links–remained mostly unaddressed within the learning literature21.

Recent advances in uncertainty-aware OPF and network structural modeling have significantly improved the capability of power systems to operate under high levels of renewable uncertainty and flexible resource participation. In particular, chance-constrained and robust OPF formulations have been widely adopted to explicitly account for stochastic and worst-case variations in nodal injections. For instance, chance-constrained robust dynamic OPF models enable probabilistic guarantees on constraint satisfaction while capturing temporal coupling effects in distribution networks with uncertain injections, thereby enhancing operational reliability under dynamic conditions22. In parallel, robust network topology optimization approaches have been developed to identify resilient configurations of active distribution networks, where uncertain injections and network asymmetry are jointly considered, allowing the system to maintain feasible operation under a wide range of structural and injection uncertainties23s. Furthermore, convex relaxation and continuous-variable-based formulations have been proposed to address the computational intractability of network reconfiguration problems, especially in unbalanced and mutually coupled distribution systems, providing scalable and physically consistent approximations for large-scale uncertainty-aware optimization24.

Despite these important developments, existing methods primarily focus on ensuring feasibility and optimality of individual operating points or short-term decision-making under uncertainty. They do not explicitly characterize how uncertainty and coordinated flexibility reshape the structural distribution of power flows across transmission interfaces over a large ensemble of steady-state scenarios. In particular, interaction-driven phenomena such as sensitivity amplification, stress persistence, and structural flow redistribution remain insufficiently explored within conventional robust or chance-constrained OPF frameworks. This limitation motivates the proposed framework, which shifts the analytical focus from pointwise optimization toward structural characterization of steady-state power flows, enabling planning-oriented insights into interface behavior under high-dimensional uncertainty and coordinated flexibility deployment.

This gap suggests an opportunity to create a new class of planning-oriented tools that can integrate the physical rigor of steady-state models with the expressive capabilities of modern representation learning. Instead of treating injections and flows as simple inputs and outputs, such frameworks would view the network as a spatial manifold governed by physics yet shaped by complex, multi-resource interactions. They would aim not merely to compute flows for isolated cases but to approximate the underlying structure linking regional injections, flexibility deployment, and interface stress propagation. Such an approach becomes particularly crucial in multi-area systems, where region-to-region transfers are constrained by a small number of key corridors whose behavior is sensitive to subtle shifts in generation patterns, storage operation, or demand-response activation.

Stimulated by these insights, the present work proposes a fully integrated steady-state learning framework specifically crafted for long-term transmission planning in flexibility-rich multi-area systems. The approach begins by generating a broad representative set of future steady-state operating conditions, reflecting plausible renewable output patterns, flexible resource deployment strategies, and evolving load shapes. These operating points serve as the training foundation for a learning system that aims to approximate the manifold of feasible steady-state flows. Unlike approaches that treat neural networks as black-box predictors, the proposed model embeds physics directly into its architecture. Nodal injections, flexibility adjustments, and structural deviations are first organized into high-dimensional nodal representations, which then propagate through a graph-based learning mechanism leveraging PTDF-informed message passing, nonlinear structural corrections, and multi-order embedding interactions. In doing so, the learning system acquires the capacity to reflect not only immediate power-flow sensitivities but also the deeper structural dependencies linking spatial correlations, mixed-resource adjustments, and region-level balancing signatures.

Despite extensive prior work on transmission planning, robust and uncertainty-aware OPF, and learning-based power system analysis, several important gaps remain insufficiently addressed. First, most existing studies focus on operational feasibility or economic optimality under uncertainty, rather than on planning-level structural behavior of steady-state power flows across large scenario ensembles. Second, flexibility resources such as energy storage and demand response are often modeled as localized or short-term control actions, while their long-horizon, systemwide structural impacts on transmission interfaces are rarely examined. Third, learning-based approaches in power systems have primarily targeted fast approximation or prediction of power-flow solutions, with limited emphasis on physical interpretability or sensitivity structure relevant for transmission reinforcement decisions. As a result, the interaction-driven amplification, stress persistence, and redistribution effects that emerge under coordinated multi-resource deployment remain largely unexplored. These exception areas motivate the present work, which explicitly targets structural characterization of transmission interfaces at the planning horizon, bridging physics-based sensitivity analysis and data-driven representation learning.

The methodology extends beyond flow prediction by constructing a suite of planning-oriented metrics directly from the learned latent space. A predicted interface flow envelope quantifies how different operating conditions shape individual corridor loadings across future horizons. A capacity-demand estimator leverages learned structural embeddings to infer how global and regional conditions map to corridor reinforcement needs. A stress concentration index measures how frequently and persistently each interface approaches its structural limits across the scenario ensemble, offering a planning-level perspective on chronic congestion risk. Complementing these analytic tools is a flexibility-imprint decomposition that attributes interface stress to resource categories such as storage, demand response, or renewable generation, making the learned model interpretable rather than opaque. A final weak-link measure detects corridors whose structural sensitivities magnify small injection shifts into large interface distortions, revealing locations where transmission expansion may be most urgently necessary.

The broader landscape of prior literature hints at the necessity of such an integrated approach. Despite substantial progress in operational flexibility modeling, many existing studies still treat storage or demand response as relatively simple adjustments rather than as agents capable of inducing systemwide structural change. Similarly, while scenario-based planning frameworks incorporate renewable variability, they rarely characterize how renewable spatial distribution interacts with network topology over the long term. Machine-learning tools have shown that power-flow structures contain embedded geometric regularities that can be learned, but they have not yet been harnessed in a way that speaks directly to long-term planning questions. What remains missing is a framework capable of learning from the full diversity of future steady-state patterns, interpreting how mixed-resource portfolios reshape backbone flows, and presenting planning insights that remain faithful to physical realities.

The novelty of the proposed framework lies in its explicit focus on planning-level structural characterization of steady-state power flows under flexibility-rich conditions, rather than on operational optimization or pointwise flow prediction. Unlike conventional robust or uncertainty-aware OPF approaches that aim to ensure feasibility or optimality for individual scenarios, this work targets the geometric and sensitivity structure of transmission interfaces across a broad ensemble of plausible steady-state conditions. By embedding physics-guided sensitivity information into a graph-based representation learning architecture, the framework enables planners to identify persistent interface stress patterns, sensitivity amplification mechanisms, and weak-link corridors that are not apparent from snapshot-based or purely linear analyses. From an applicability perspective, the proposed method is intended as a complementary planning analysis tool that operates on top of standard power-flow and scenario-generation pipelines, providing actionable insights for interface screening and reinforcement prioritization in systems with high renewable penetration and coordinated flexibility deployment.

By combining physics-informed learning with multi-resolution structural analysis, the proposed approach aims to fill this methodological gap in a manner that aligns naturally with contemporary planning challenges. It treats steady-state flows not as isolated numerical outcomes but as manifestations of a deeper structural manifold shaped by resource diversity, regional balancing, and system topology. It captures the nonlinear, often subtle ways in which flexible resources influence interface behavior. It enables planners to understand not only where future stress may accumulate but why certain corridors become structural chokepoints. And it brings interpretability, scalability, and physical consistency into a unified planning-oriented analytical tool, offering a new paradigm for assessing transmission interface capacity in the era of heterogeneous flexibility.

Problem formulation

Before presenting the detailed mathematical formulation, we first provide an intuitive overview of the modeling structure to improve readability. The proposed formulation is designed to capture how nodal injections, flexibility resources, and structural interactions jointly shape steady-state power flows across transmission interfaces. At a high level, the model consists of three interconnected components: (i) a set of injection and flexibility variables that describe scenario-dependent operating conditions, (ii) physics-consistent mapping relationships that translate these variables into interface flows based on PTDF and structural sensitivity, and (iii) a group of structural enhancement terms that capture higher-order interaction effects arising from coordinated resource deployment. By organizing the formulation in this manner, the subsequent equations can be interpreted not as isolated mathematical expressions, but as a structured representation of how local adjustments propagate through the network and influence interface-level stress patterns.

In parallel, we position the proposed framework within the broader literature on uncertainty-aware power system optimization. Existing works on chance-constrained OPF provide probabilistic guarantees on constraint satisfaction under uncertain injections, while robust topology optimization approaches explicitly account for worst-case variations in network structure and operating conditions. In addition, convex relaxation-based formulations have been developed to ensure computational tractability for reconfiguration and optimization of unbalanced distribution networks under uncertainty. These methods have significantly advanced uncertainty-aware operation and network modeling. However, they are primarily focused on feasibility and optimality of individual operating points. In contrast, the proposed formulation aims to characterize the structural behavior of power flows across a large ensemble of steady-state scenarios, with particular emphasis on sensitivity amplification, stress persistence, and flow redistribution effects induced by coordinated flexibility. The mathematical formulation developed in this section provides a rigorous quantitative foundation for characterizing how heterogeneous flexibility resources reshape the steady-state power-flow distribution across regional interfaces. Because the planning objective of this study is not short-term operation but the structural behavior of multi-area transmission corridors under large ensembles of plausible future injections, the model must simultaneously preserve full AC power-flow physics, encode the differentiated operational envelopes of renewable generation, storage, demand-side modulation, and conventional dispatch, and represent the spatiotemporal regularities that emerge when these resources interact. To achieve this, the formulation begins by establishing a parametric representation of nodal injections and adjustments across a wide spectrum of steady-state operating points, treating each operating configuration as an element embedded within a higher-dimensional structural manifold. These variables are then linked through Kirchhoff-consistent flow equations, regionally defined balancing relationships, and interface-level transfer definitions that capture how local nodes collectively contribute to cross-boundary stress. Each constraint is constructed not only to guarantee feasibility but also to reflect the deeper behavioral patterns that the learning system will ultimately distill. The resulting set of coupled, nonlinear expressions serves as the analytical backbone for the manifold that the learning architecture later approximates, ensuring coherence between the physical system and the high-dimensional representations extracted during training.

Before presenting the detailed mathematical formulation, we clarify the structural roles of different constraint components used in this study. To improve transparency and interpretability for planning applications, the formulation is explicitly organized into two categories: (i) core planning and physics-consistent constraints, which are strictly required to ensure steady-state feasibility and physical validity of power flows; and (ii) auxiliary or enhancement terms, which are introduced to enrich structural expressiveness, capture nonlinear interaction effects, and improve the representation of flexibility-driven distortions, but are not strictly required for feasibility. Case studies

The case study is built upon a multi-area transmission system constructed by aggregating three interconnected subregions, each representing a distinct resource composition and spatial demand distribution. Region A contains 62 buses, 78 branches, and a renewable portfolio formed by 1.3 GW of utility-scale PV and 0.9 GW of onshore wind. Region B contains 47 buses and 55 branches, with a higher proportion of flexible assets including 420 MW/1.68 GWh of lithium-ion storage and a 600 MW pumped-hydro station capable of operating in either pumping or generation mode within a 5-minute steady-state transition tolerance. Region C includes 53 buses and 61 branches with a mixture of 720 MW of thermal units and a strongly responsive demand-side portfolio covering 180 MW of industrial DR and 95 MW of commercial DR. Across the three regions, the combined system has 162 buses, 194 transmission branches, and four major inter-regional transmission interfaces whose nominal thermal ratings range between 600 and 1,200 MW. To construct a sufficiently broad ensemble of steady-state operating points, the study generates 1,500 representative scenarios covering renewable variability, storage dispatch postures, DR activation levels, and structural deviation samples; these scenarios span both typical and stress-heavy operating patterns relevant for long-term planning.

Conclusion

This paper proposes a physics-informed learning framework for planning-level analysis of steady-state transmission interfaces in multi-area power systems with high penetrations of heterogeneous flexibility resources. In contrast to conventional transmission planning methods that rely on fixed dispatch assumptions, limited operating snapshots, or purely linear sensitivity analysis, the proposed approach focuses on characterizing the structural behavior of interface flows across a broad ensemble of plausible steady-state operating conditions. The central objective is to understand how coordinated renewable generation, energy storage, and demand response reshape interface sensitivity and congestion patterns at the planning horizon, rather than to optimize individual operating points. By embedding PTDF-guided physical consistency into a graph-based representation learning architecture with structural enhancement mechanisms, the framework reconstructs the manifold of feasible steady-state power-flow patterns. This learned structure enables the extraction of planning-oriented metrics, including interface flow envelopes, structural sensitivity gradients, stress persistence ratios, and weak-corridor identification scores. Case studies on a realistic multi-area transmission system demonstrate that several critical transmission interfaces exhibit persistent proximity to their structural limits. In particular, the results show that sensitivity levels exceed 90 MW per 100 MW of regional injection shift for multiple corridors, and that stress persistence ratios surpass 80% across the scenario ensemble, indicating chronic structural congestion rather than rare extreme events. The analysis further reveals that flexibility resources do not uniformly mitigate transmission stress. While certain interfaces experience reduced average loading due to storage dispatch or demand-response activation, other corridors exhibit amplified interface stress as a result of nonlinear interaction and flow redistribution effects under coordinated flexibility deployment. These amplification phenomena are reflected in the widened interface flow envelopes and elevated weak-link scores, and cannot be reliably identified using snapshot-based planning studies or linear PTDF analysis alone. The inclusion of higher-order structural terms is therefore essential for capturing these interaction-driven effects and for distinguishing between structurally resilient corridors and those prone to persistent congestion. From an applicability perspective, the proposed framework is intended as a complementary planning analysis tool that builds upon standard power-flow modeling and scenario-generation pipelines. The formulation admits simplified variants when interaction effects are weak, enabling a balance between model complexity and insight for different planning contexts. Overall, by explicitly quantifying structural sensitivity amplification and stress persistence through physically interpretable metrics and core percentages, the proposed approach provides transmission planners with a robust analytical lens for interface screening and reinforcement prioritization in flexibility-rich power systems.
