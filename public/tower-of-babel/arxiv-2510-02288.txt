Optimal Lieb–Thirring type inequalities for Schrödinger and Jacobi operators with complex potentials
Sabine Bögli, Sukrid Petpradittha

Abstract
. We prove optimal Lieb–Thirring type inequalities for Schrödinger and Jacobi operators with complex potentials. Our results bound eigenvalue power sums (Riesz means) by the L p L^{p} norm of the potential, where in contrast to the self-adjoint case, each term needs to be weighted by a function of the ratio of the distance of the eigenvalue to the essential spectrum and the distance to the endpoint(s) thereof. Our Lieb–Thirring type bounds only hold for integrable weight functions. To prove optimality, we establish divergence estimates for non-integrable weight functions. The divergence rates exhibit a logarithmic or even polynomial gain compared to semiclassical methods (Weyl asymptotics) for real potentials.

1.
Introduction

The
d
-dimensional Schrödinger operator in the Hilbert space
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is defined by

H_{V}:=-\Delta+V

with a potential
V
. In the following, let
p
depend on the dimension
d
as follows:

p\geq 1,\;\mbox{ if }d=1;\qquad p>1,\;\mbox{ if }d=2;\qquad p\geq d/2,\;\mbox{ if }d\geq 3.

(1)

If a real-valued potential
V
is sufficiently regular, namely
V\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, then the essential spectrum
\sigma_{{\rm e}}(H_{V})
is
[0,\infty)
and the discrete spectrum
\sigma_{{\rm d}}(H_{V})
(isolated eigenvalues of finite algebraic multiplicities) consists of negative eigenvalues which can accumulate only at the point
0
, the bottom of the essential spectrum. The classical Lieb-Thirring inequality (after Lieb and Thirring
[
28
,
29
]
) states that there exists a constant
C_{p,d}>0
depending on
p
and
d
such that for all real-valued potentials
V\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(H)}|\lambda|^{p-d/2}\leq C_{p,d}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|V(x)|^{p}\;{\rm d}x,

(2)

where in the sum we repeat each eigenvalue according to its (finite) algebraic multiplicity.
For more background material on self-adjoint Lieb–Thirring inqualities, see e.g.
[
17
,
19
,
20
,
27
]
.

For a complex-valued potential
V\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
we still have
\sigma_{{\rm e}}(H_{V})=[0,\infty)
but the behaviour of the discrete spectrum can be much more wild. For example, there can be non-zero accumulation points of the discrete spectrum
[
2
,
4
]
.
This immediately implies that the inequality (
2
) is false for general complex potentials
V\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

Recent years have seen a significant interest in Lieb–Thirring type inequalities for the complex-potential case, see e.g.
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
12
,
15
,
16
,
18
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
31
]
.
Frank, Laptev, Lieb, and Seiringer
[
18
]
proved that for given
p\geq d/2+1
and
\tau>0
there exists a constant
C_{p,d,\tau}>0
such that for all (complex-valued)
V\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,
there exists a bound for all eigenvalues outside of a cone around the essential spectrum,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(H_{V})\\
|\operatorname{Im}\lambda|\geq\tau\operatorname{Re}\lambda\end{subarray}}|\lambda|^{p-d/2}\leq C_{p,d,\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|V(x)|^{p}\;{\rm d}x,

(3)

where
C_{p,d,\tau}=C_{p,d}\left(1+\frac{2}{\tau}\right)^{p}
for a constant
C_{p,d}>0
.

By averaging the bound (
3
) with respect to the parameter
\tau
, Demuth, Hansmann and Katriel
[
11
]
obtained a bound involving
all
eigenvalues, namely, for any
0<\kappa<1
,

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{\rm d}(H_{V})}\dfrac{\text{dist}(\lambda,[0,\infty))^{p+\kappa}}{|\lambda|^{d/2+\kappa}}\leq C_{p,d,\kappa}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|V(x)|^{p}\;{\rm d}x,

(4)

where
C_{p,d,\kappa}>0
is a constant depending on
p,d
and
\kappa
. In
[
3
]
, Bögli improved the latter Lieb-Thirring type inequalities. More precisely, given a continuous, non-increasing function
f:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
, if

\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\;{\rm d}t<\infty,

(5)

then there exists a constant
C_{p,d,f}>0
such that for all
V\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{\rm d}(H_{V})}\dfrac{\text{dist}(\lambda,[0,\infty))^{p}}{|\lambda|^{d/2}}f\left(-\log\left(\frac{\text{dist}(\lambda,[0,\infty))}{|\lambda|}\right)\right)\leq C_{p,d,f}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|V(x)|^{p}\;{\rm d}x

(6)

where
C_{p,d,f}=C_{p,d}\,\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\;{\rm d}t+f(0)\right)
for an
f
-independent constant
C_{p,d}>0
.
Note that the inequality (
4
) can be recovered by inserting the exponential function
f(t)={{\rm e}}^{-\kappa t}
into the formula (
6
). We remark that the inequalities (
4
) and (
6
) are generalisations of the classical Lieb-Thirring inequalities for self-adjoint Schrödinger operators as they reduce to (
2
) for a real-valued potential because, in that case,
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[0,\infty))=|\lambda|
for every discrete (negative) eigenvalue.

Define the ratio of the left- and right-hand sides of (
6
) by

{\rm Ratio}(V,f):=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|V(x)|^{p}\;{\rm d}x\right)^{-1}\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{\rm d}(H_{V})}\dfrac{\text{dist}(\lambda,[0,\infty))^{p}}{|\lambda|^{d/2}}f\left(-\log\left(\frac{\text{dist}(\lambda,[0,\infty))}{|\lambda|}\right)\right).

In dimension
d=1
, Bögli
[
3
]
proved that the assumption (
5
) cannot be removed. Indeed, if the integral (
5
) is infinite, then
\sup_{0\neq V\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}})}{\rm Ratio}(V,f)=\infty
for any
p\geq 1
.
More precisely, taking
V_{h}={\rm i}h\chi_{[-1,1]}
with
\chi_{[-1,1]}
the characteristic function of the interval
[-1,1]
, in the large-coupling limit
0<h\to\infty

we have the divergence rate

{\rm Ratio}(V_{h},f)\gtrsim F(\varepsilon\log h)

(7)

for any
0<\varepsilon<1
where
F(x):=\int_{0}^{x}f(t)\,{\rm d}t
(see the proof of
[
3
, Thm. 2.2]
).

In the first main result of this paper, we prove that the bound (
6
) is optimal in dimensions
d\geq 2
as well. More precisely, if the integral (
5
) is infinite, taking the potential
V_{h}={\rm i}h\chi_{B_{1}(0)}
with
\chi_{B_{1}(0)}
the characteristic function of the open unit ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, in the large-coupling limit
0<h\to\infty

we prove the divergence rate (
7
) for any
0<\varepsilon<1
(see Theorem
2.1
for the precise statement with uniformity in
f
and see also Corollary
2.3
).
In the second main result (Theorem
2.4
), we prove similar divergence rates for
non-decreasing
functions
f
that satisfy a certain monotonicity assumption.
As an application, for
f(t)={\rm e}^{\xi t}
with
\xi\geq 0
, we obtain the divergence rate

\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|V_{h}(x)|^{p}\;{\rm d}x\right)^{-1}\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{\rm d}(H_{V_{h}})}\dfrac{{\rm{dist}}(\lambda,[0,\infty))^{p-\xi}}{|\lambda|^{d/2-\xi}}\gtrsim\begin{cases}h^{\varepsilon\xi}&\text{ if }\xi>0,\\
\log h&\text{ if }\xi=0\end{cases}

(see Corollaries
2.5
,
2.7
for the precise statement and also Remarks
2.6
,
2.8
). This answers a question posed by Cuenin and Frank
[
10
, Question 2]
; note that their (equivalent) formulation is the rescaled version of our strong-coupling limit (
h\to\infty
) to study the operators
-\hbar^{2}\Delta+V
in the semiclassical limit (
\hbar=1/\sqrt{h}\to 0
).
Our answer proves logarithmic (for
\xi=0
) and polynomial (for
\xi>0
) gain compared to the ideas from semiclassical analysis (or Weyl’s law).
Note that we restrict the parameter to
\xi\geq 0
since for
\xi<0
the function
f
is integrable and we recover the Lieb–Thirring type inequality (
4
) (with
\kappa=-\xi
).

As a further development, if
p>d/2
, for each
f
for which the integral (
5
) is infinite, by taking the sum of potentials
V_{h}
with supports sufficently far away from each other, we can construct a potential
V\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
such that
{\rm Ratio}(V,f)=\infty
(see Theorem
2.9
). This is a more direct proof of the optimality of the Lieb–Thirring type inequality (
6
).

Finally, we also show that the
\tau
-dependence of the constant
C_{p,d,\tau}
in (
3
) is sharp (see Theorem
2.11
).

In Section
3
we prove analogous results for (one-dimensional) Jacobi operators.
Let
J
be a Jacobi operator in the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
acting on a complex sequence
u=\{u_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
as

(Ju)_{n}=a_{n-1}u_{n-1}+b_{n}u_{n}+c_{n}u_{n+1},\quad n\in{\mathbb{Z}},

where
\{a_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}},\{b_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
and
\{c_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
are given bounded complex sequences. Then
J
is a bounded operator and can be represented by the doubly-infinite tridiagonal matrix

J=\begin{pmatrix}\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&&&&\\
&a_{-1}&b_{0}&c_{0}&&&\\
&&a_{0}&b_{1}&c_{1}&&\\
&&&a_{1}&b_{2}&c_{2}&\\
&&&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

The free Jacobi operator
J_{0}
is defined via the particular case
a_{n}\equiv 1,c_{n}\equiv 1
and
b_{n}\equiv 0
, i.e. its action on
u
is given by

(J_{0}u)_{n}=u_{n-1}+u_{n+1},\quad n\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

In this circumstance, it is well-known that
\sigma(J_{0})=\sigma_{{\rm e}}(J_{0})=[-2,2]
.
Let
v=\{v_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
be a sequence defined by setting

v_{n}:=\max\{|a_{n-1}-1|,|a_{n}-1|,|b_{n}|,|c_{n-1}-1|,|c_{n}-1|\},\quad n\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

If
\lim_{|n|\to\infty}v_{n}=0
, then
J
is a compact perturbation of
J_{0}
and hence
\sigma_{{\rm e}}(J)=[-2,2]
. Now, the discrete spectrum
\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)\subset{\mathbb{C}}\backslash[-2,2]
consists of isolated eigenvalues of finite algebraic multiplicities that can possibly accumulate anywhere in
[-2,2]
.

For the special case
a_{n}=c_{n}>0
and
b_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the Jacobi operator
J
is self-adjoint and the Lieb-Thirring inequalities due to Hundertmark and Simon
[
27
]
read that if
v\in\ell^{p}({\mathbb{Z}})
for some
p\geq 1
, then

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J),\;\lambda>2}|\lambda-2|^{p-1/2}+\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J),\;\lambda<-2}|\lambda+2|^{p-1/2}\leq C_{p}\,\|v\|^{p}_{\ell^{p}},

(8)

where
C_{p}>0
is a constant depending on
p
only.

For non-self-adjoint Jacobi operators, there exist Lieb-Thirring type inequalities outside a diamond-shaped sector in the complex plane. These results are due to Golinskii and Kupin
[
21
, Thm. 1.5]
but we use
the formulation of Hansmann and Katriel
[
25
, Eq. (8)]
.
For
0\leq\omega<\pi/2
let us define two sectors

\Phi^{\pm}_{\omega}:=\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}:2\mp\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda<\tan(\omega)|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda|\}.

Then, by
[
25
, Eq. (8)]
, for
p\geq 3/2
there exists a constant
C_{p,\omega}>0
such that for all
v\in\ell^{p}({\mathbb{Z}})

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)\cap\Phi^{+}_{\omega}}|\lambda-2|^{p-1/2}+\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)\cap\Phi^{-}_{\omega}}|\lambda+2|^{p-1/2}\leq C_{p,\omega}\|v\|^{p}_{\ell^{p}},

(9)

where
C_{p,\omega}=C_{p}\,(1+2\tan(\omega))^{p}
for a constant
C_{p}>0
.

Hansmann and Katriel
[
25
, Thm. 2]
used this inequality to prove a bound for
all
eigenvalues. Namely,
for
p\geq 3/2
and
0<\kappa<1
, there exists a constant
C_{p,\kappa}>0
such that for all
v\in\ell^{p}({\mathbb{Z}})

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])^{p+\kappa}}{|\lambda^{2}-4|^{1/2+\kappa}}\leq C_{p,\tau}\|v\|^{p}_{\ell^{p}}.

(10)

Note that this inequality reduces to (
8
) in the self-adjoint case.

As main results on Jacobi operators, we prove a stronger version of the estimate (
10
) involving an integrable function
f
(the analogue of (
6
) for Jacobi operators, see Theorem
3.1
) and we prove optimality in the sense that if
f
is not integrable, then no such bound holds and we establish explicit divergence rates
(see Theorem
3.3
for non-increasing
f
and Theorem
3.5
for non-decreasing
f
satisfying a monotonicity assumption).
Finally, we also show that the
\omega
-dependence of the constant
C_{p,\omega}
in (
9
) is sharp (see Theorem
3.8
).

Notation

The notation
\gtrsim
(
\lesssim
) means that the inequality
\geq
(
\leq
) holds up to a multiplicative constant.
The notation
\ll
(
\gg
) means that the ratio of the left-hand side to the right-hand side (the right-hand side to the left-hand side) converges to
0
in the limit.
In most instances we display the involved constants and indicate their dependencies by subscripts (unless stated otherwise, for ease of notation).

2.
Schrödinger operators

2.1.
Main results

In this section we study multidimensional Schrödinger operators.
The proofs of the following main results will be given in Section
2.3
.

First we show
that the estimate (
6
) is optimal in the sense that if the function
f
is not integrable, then
\sup_{0\neq V\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}{\rm Ratio}(V,f)
is infinity. To this end, we consider potentials of the form
V_{h}={\rm i}h\chi_{B_{1}(0)}
for
h>0
and are interested in the strong-coupling limit
h\to\infty
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
p
satisfy (
1
) and let
0<\varepsilon<1
. Take a function
w:[0,\infty)\to[1,\infty)
with
w(h)\to\infty
as
h\to\infty
(arbitrarily slowly). Then there exist
C_{p,d}>0
and
h_{*}\geq 1
such that for all continuous, non-increasing functions
f:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
with
\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\;{\rm d}t=\infty
and all
h\geq h_{*}

{\rm Ratio}(V_{h},f)\geq C_{p,d}\left(\frac{F(\varepsilon\log h)}{w(h)}-f(0)w(h)\right),

(11)

where
F(x):=\int_{0}^{x}f(t)\;{\rm d}t
.

Remark 2.2
.

We remark that even though (
6
) requires
p\geq d/2+1
, here Theorem
2.1
does not.
Note that the right-hand side of (
11
) is divergent whenever
w(h)
diverges sufficiently slowly, for example when
(w(h))^{2}\ll F(\varepsilon\log h)
as
h\to\infty
.

We use the function
w
to show the explicit (uniform) dependence on
f
. However, for a fixed function
f
, we can apply the estimate for a function
w
that diverges arbitrarily slowly and thus obtain an improvement of (
11
).
The next result concerns this improvement.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
p
satisfy (
1
) and
0<\varepsilon<1
. Given a continuous, non-increasing function
f:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
with
\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\;{\rm d}t=\infty
, there exist
C>0
and
h_{*}\geq 1
(both possibly
f
-dependent) such that for all
h\geq h_{*}

{\rm Ratio}(V_{h},f)\geq CF(\varepsilon\log h).

(12)

Next we broaden the study of divergence rates of the ratios to get lower bounds for the class of (positive, continuous)
non-decreasing
functions. In exchange, we require the monotonicity of the
tail
of the function
f(\log t^{2})/t
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
p
satisfy (
1
) and let
0<\varepsilon<1\leq x_{0}
. Take
w:[0,\infty)\to[1,\infty)
with
w(h)\to\infty
as
h\to\infty
(arbitrarily slowly). Then there exist
C_{p,d}>0
and
h_{*}\geq 1
such that for all
h\geq h_{*}
and all continuous, non-decreasing functions
f:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
such that
f(\log t^{2})/t
is monotonic for
t\geq x_{0}
one has

{\rm Ratio}(V_{h},f)\geq\frac{C_{p,d}}{w(h)}\left(F(\varepsilon\log h)-F\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\log h\right)\right)\geq C_{p,d}\,\frac{\varepsilon f(0)}{2w(h)}\log h.

(13)

Again we get an improvement for a fixed
f
.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
p
satisfy (
1
) and let
0<\varepsilon<1\leq x_{0}
. Given a continuous, non-decreasing function
f:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
such that
f(\log t^{2})/t
is monotonic for
t\geq x_{0}
, there exist
C>0
and
h_{*}\geq 1
(both possibly
f
-dependent) such that for all
h\geq h_{*}

{\rm Ratio}(V_{h},f)\geq C\left(F(\varepsilon\log h)-F\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\log h\right)\right).

(14)

Remark 2.6
.

For the special case of a constant weight function
f\equiv 1
in (
6
), the validity of a Lieb–Thirring type estimate was published as an open question by Demuth, Hansmann, and Katriel in
[
13
]
. The construction in
[
6
]
answered the question to the negative in dimension
d=1
, and in
[
5
]
the construction was generalised to higher dimensions, with the same class of potentials
V_{h}
as studied in the present paper. Note that in
[
5
]
, a lower bound of the form

{\rm Ratio}(V_{h},f)\geq C_{p,d}(\log h)^{\varepsilon}
was found where
0<\varepsilon<1
.
The new results presented here are an improvement over these results because Corollary
2.5
for
f\equiv 1
yields a divergence order of at least
\log h
.

To answer
[
10
, Question 2]
, we apply Theorem
2.4
to the exponential function
f(t)={\rm e}^{\xi t}
for
\xi>0
(for
\xi=0
we have
f\equiv 1
which was discussed in the latter Remark).

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
p
satisfy (
1
) and let
0<\varepsilon<1
. Take a function
w:[0,\infty)\to[1,\infty)
with
w(h)\to\infty
as
h\to\infty
(arbitrarily slowly). Then there exist
C_{p,d}>0
and
h_{*}\geq 1
such that for all
h\geq h_{*}
and all
\xi>0

\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|V_{h}(x)|^{p}\;{\rm d}x\right)^{-1}\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{\rm d}(H_{V_{h}})}\dfrac{{\rm{dist}}(\lambda,[0,\infty))^{p-\xi}}{|\lambda|^{d/2-\xi}}\geq C_{p,d}\frac{h^{\varepsilon\xi}}{\xi w(h)}(1-h^{-\varepsilon\xi/2}).

(15)

Remark 2.8
.

With aid of the equation (
14
) for
f(t)={{\rm e}}^{\xi t}
, we get a divergence rate of at least
Ch^{\varepsilon\xi}
for a (possibly
\xi
-dependent) constant
C>0
.

The following result proves optimality of the Lieb–Thirring type inequality (
6
) in a more direct way.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and let
p
satisfy (
1
) with
p>d/2
. Then, for every continuous, non-increasing function
f:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
with
\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\;{\rm d}t=\infty
there exists
V\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
such that

{\rm Ratio}(V,f)=\infty.

Remark 2.10
.

It would be interesting to know whether the result continues to hold for
d\geq 3
and
p=d/2
. The scaling argument that is used in the proof breaks down at this point.

In dimension
d=1
, Bögli
[
3
, Thm 2.4]
proved that the
\tau
-dependence of the constant
C_{p,d,\tau}
in (
3
), i.e. the order
\tau^{-p}
as
\tau\to 0
, is sharp. Here we prove sharpness in dimensions
d\geq 2
.

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
p
satisfy (
1
) and let
\varphi:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
be a continuous function such that
\varphi(\tau)\ll\tau^{-p}
as
\tau\to 0
. Then

\limsup_{\tau\to 0^{+}}\sup_{0\neq V\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\left(\varphi(\tau)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|V(x)|^{p}\;{\rm d}x\right)^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(H_{V})\\
|\operatorname{Im}\lambda|\geq\tau\operatorname{Re}\lambda\end{subarray}}|\lambda|^{p-d/2}=\infty.

(16)

The proof relies again on eigenvalue estimates for the class of potentials
V_{h}
for
h>0
.

2.2.
Preliminaries

We devote this section to preparations for the proofs of our main results and use this opportunity to introduce our notation and terminology. The key ingredient is the asymptotics in
[
5
]
on eigenvalues
\lambda_{\ell,j}
, with error bounds that were shown to be uniform in two parameters (integers)
j,\ell
in certain
h
-dependent index sets. The stronger results in the present paper require to work with larger index sets that depend on the function
w(h)
used in Theorems
2.1
and
2.4
.
Hence, in the following we summarise the approach from
[
5
]
and show that the asymptotics continue to hold for the new index sets depending on
w(h)
, with adapted error bounds.

First of all,
we note that since the potential
V_{h}
is purely imaginary with non-negative imaginary part, a numerical range argument
[
5
, Lem. 2]
shows that all eigenvalues
\lambda
belong to the first quadrant of the complex plane (
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda\geq 0
and
\operatorname{Im}\lambda\geq 0
) and hence
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[0,\infty))=\operatorname{Im}\lambda
.

Since the potential
V_{h}
is spherically symmetric, we find solutions of the eigenvalue problem

-\Delta f+V_{h}f=\lambda f

by using spherical coordinates and solving a corresponding radial eigenvalue problem.
To this end, we use complex parameters
k,m
as follows: let
m\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits m>0
and set
k:=\sqrt{{\rm i}h+m^{2}}
where we take the principal branch of the square root function. We assume that
\operatorname{Im}k=\operatorname{Im}\sqrt{{\rm i}h+m^{2}}>0
.
For
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we make the ansatz
f(x)=\psi(|x|)Y^{(\ell)}(x/|x|)
where
Y^{(\ell)}
is the spherical harmonic of degree
\ell
, defined on the
d
-dimensional unit sphere.
Then, by
[
5
, Sect. 2.2]
,
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is an eigenfunction corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda:=k^{2}={\rm i}h+m^{2}
if
\psi\in L^{2}((0,\infty),r^{d-1}\,{\rm d}r)
is the radial (
r=|x|
) function defined by

\psi(r)=\begin{cases}H^{(1)}_{\nu}(k)r^{1-d/2}J_{\nu}(mr)\quad&\textrm{if\;\;}0<r<1,\\
J_{\nu}(m)r^{1-d/2}H^{(1)}_{\nu}(kr)\quad&\textrm{if\;\;}r\geq 1,\end{cases}

and

satisfy the characteristic equation

\frac{k}{m}=\dfrac{J^{\prime}_{\nu}(m)H^{(1)}_{\nu}(k)}{J_{\nu}(m)(H^{(1)}_{\nu})^{\prime}(k)};

(17)

here

are respectively the Bessel and Hankel functions of the first kind of order

(18)

For the theory of Bessel functions and their classical asymptotics we refer to, for instance,

]
.
Standard results on the Laplacian in spherical coordinates (see e.g.

(19)

for all

converges to

as

as

for

Let

,

It was shown therein that there exists

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
\nu=\ell+\frac{d}{2}-1
. Then there exists
h_{*}\geq 1
such that for all
h\geq h_{*}
, all
\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{h}
and all
j\in\mathcal{J}_{h,\ell}
, the following claims hold true:

(1)

The function
f_{\nu,j}
is analytic in the ball
B_{\nu}(m_{\nu,j}^{(0)})
with a unique simple zero
m_{\nu,j}^{(1)}
therein;

(2)

|m_{\nu,j}^{(1)}-m_{\nu,j}^{(0)}|<\nu/2
;

and, in addition, for any two indices
j_{1},j_{2}\in\mathcal{J}_{h,\ell}
,
j_{1}\neq j_{2}
, we have

(iii)

|m_{\nu,j_{1}}^{(1)}-m_{\nu,j_{2}}^{(1)}|>4
.

Proof.

First we show that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\sup\left\{\frac{\nu}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits m}:|m-m_{\nu,j}^{(0)}|\leq\nu,\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{h},j\in\mathcal{J}_{h,\ell}\right\}\leq Cg(h)

(24)

for all
h
sufficiently large.
To this end, we use (
22
) to estimate

\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits m_{\nu,j}^{(0)}}{\nu}=\frac{2\pi j}{\nu}+\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{\pi}{2\nu}\geq\frac{j}{\nu}\gtrsim\frac{j}{\ell}\geq\frac{1}{g(h)}

for all
h
sufficiently large, where the non-displayed constant is independent of the choices of
\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{h}
and
j\in\mathcal{J}_{h,\ell}
. Hence, for any
m
in the closure of the ball
B_{\nu}(m_{\nu,j}^{(0)})
, we get

\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits m}{\nu}\geq\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits m_{\nu,j}^{(0)}}{\nu}-1\gtrsim\frac{1}{g(h)},

which implies
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits m>0
and (
24
).

Next we show that, for all
h
sufficienly large,
B_{\nu}(m_{\nu,j}^{(0)})\subset\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
for all
\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{h}
and all
j\in\mathcal{J}_{h,\ell}
, where
\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
is as in (
23
).
First, it follows readily from (
24
) that, for all
h
sufficiently large, we have
A\nu<\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits m
for all
m\in B_{\nu}(m_{\nu,j}^{(0)})
. Second, we estimate with (
22
),

|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits m_{\nu,j}^{(0)}|=\log\frac{4\pi j}{\sqrt{h}}\lesssim\log h,

and taking also (
21
) into account, we get

\frac{|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits m_{\nu,j}^{(0)}|}{\nu}\lesssim\frac{\log h}{h^{\alpha(h)+1/2}}\lesssim 1.

As a result, for
m\in B_{\nu}(m_{\nu,j}^{(0)})
, we deduce that

\frac{|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits m|}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits m}\leq\frac{1+|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits(m_{\nu,j}^{(0)})|/\nu}{-1+\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits(m_{\nu,j}^{(0)})/\nu}\lesssim g(h),

which implies that
|m|<2\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits m
for all
h
sufficiently large.
Thus
m\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
and
B_{\nu}(m_{\nu,j}^{(0)})\subset\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
.

The rest of the proof is analogous to the one of
[
5
, Lem. 10]
, with (
24
) used instead of
[
5
, Eq. (49)]
.
∎

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
\nu=\ell+\frac{d}{2}-1
. Then there exist constants
C>0
,
\tilde{C}>0
and
h_{*}\geq 1
such that for all
h\geq h_{*}
the following formulas hold:

\sup\left\{\left|\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits m}{2\pi j}-1\right|:|m-m_{\nu,j}^{(1)}|\leq 2,\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{h},j\in\mathcal{J}_{h,\ell}\right\}\leq Cg(h),

(27)

and

\displaystyle\sup\left\{\left|\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits m}{\log\left(\sqrt{h}/(4\pi j)\right)}-1\right|:|m-m_{\nu,j}^{(1)}|\leq 2,\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{h},j\in\mathcal{J}_{h,\ell}\right\}\leq\tilde{C}\;\frac{1}{|\log g(h)|}.

Proof.

From (
21
), (
22

\frac{1}{j}\leq g(h)\to 0\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\left|\frac{1}{\log\frac{\sqrt{h}}{4\pi j}}\right|=\frac{1}{\log\frac{4\pi j}{\sqrt{h}}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\log h^{\alpha(h)}+|\log g(h)|}\to 0,

where the hidden constant is uniform in

and
\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{h}
, therefore, bearing the triangle inequality in mind, it is sufficient to prove the uniform estimates

\sup\left\{\left|\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits m_{\nu,j}^{(1)}}{2\pi j}-1\right|:\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{h},j\in\mathcal{J}_{h,\ell}\right\}\lesssim g(h),

and

\sup\left\{\left|\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits m_{\nu,j}^{(1)}}{\log\left(\sqrt{h}/(4\pi j)\right)}-1\right|:\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{h},j\in\mathcal{J}_{h,\ell}\right\}\lesssim g^{2}(h).

To this end, we proceed analogously as in the proof of
[
5
, Lem. 11]
.
Indeed, the first estimate follows from
[
5
, Eq. (57)]
and using that, by (
18
) and (

\frac{\nu}{j}\lesssim\frac{\ell}{j}\leq g(h).

To prove the second estimate, we use
[
5
, Eq. (58), (38)]
to obtain

\left|\frac{\log\left(\sqrt{h}/(4\pi j)\right)}{\operatorname{Im}m_{\nu,j}^{(1)}}-1\right|\leq 4A^{2}\frac{\nu^{2}}{|m_{\nu,j}^{(1)}|^{2}}\leq 4A^{2}\frac{\nu^{2}}{(2\pi j)^{2}}\cdot\frac{(2\pi j)^{2}}{(\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})^{2}}.

We estimate the right-hand side by using (
22
) and (

) to obtain an upper bound of order
g^{2}(h)
. Since
g^{2}(h)\lesssim 1/|\log g(h)|
as

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
\nu=\ell+\frac{d}{2}-1
. Then there exists
h_{*}\geq 1
such that for all
h\geq h_{*}
,
\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{h}
, and
j\in\mathcal{J}_{h,\ell}
, the following claims hold:

(1)

The function

\operatorname{err}_{\nu,j}(m):=-1+\frac{m}{4\pi j}\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta_{\nu}(m)}}{\cos\theta_{\nu}(m)}\sqrt{1-\xi_{\nu}(m)}

is analytic in
B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
and there is a constant
C>0
independent of
j
,
\ell
and
m
such that

|\operatorname{err}_{\nu,j}(m)|\leq C(h^{-2\alpha(h)}+g(h))

(32)

for any
m\in B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})

(2)

If
m\in B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
satisfies

{\rm i}\left(\theta_{\nu}(m)-\frac{\pi}{4}-2\pi j\right)=\log\frac{4\pi j}{\sqrt{h}}+\log\left(1+\operatorname{err}_{\nu,j}(m)\right),

then
m
is a solution of the characteristic equation (

) with the corresponding
k=k(m)=\sqrt{{\rm i}h+m^{2}}
.

Proof.

First we prove (
32
); then the remaining claims follow in exactly the same way as in the proof of
[
5
, Lem. 17]
.
We estimate the three factors in

\operatorname{err}_{\nu,j}(m)=-1+\frac{m}{2\pi j}\frac{1}{1+{\rm e}^{-2{\rm i}\theta_{\nu}(m)}}\sqrt{1-\xi_{\nu}(m)}.

(34)

First, with the aid of (
29
), we deduce that

\sqrt{1-\xi_{\nu}(m)}=1+\mathcal{O}{\left(h^{\gamma-1/2}+g^{2}(h)\right)}.

Second, it follows from (
30

\frac{1}{1+{\rm e}^{-2{\rm i}\theta_{\nu}(m)}}=\frac{1}{1+\mathcal{O}{\left(h^{-2\alpha(h)}\right)}}=1+\mathcal{O}{\left(h^{-2\alpha(h)}\right)}.

Third, we use

\left|\frac{m}{2\pi j}-1\right|\leq\left|\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits m}{2\pi j}-1\right|+\left|\frac{\operatorname{Im}m}{\log\left(\sqrt{h}/(4\pi j)\right)}\cdot\frac{\log\left(\sqrt{h}/(4\pi j)\right)}{2\pi j}\right|.

Note that (
31
), (
22
), (

) yield

\left|\frac{\log\left(\sqrt{h}/(4\pi j)\right)}{2\pi j}\right|=\frac{\log\left(4\pi j/\sqrt{h}\right)}{2\pi j}\leq\frac{\log(4\pi h^{\gamma})g(h)}{2\pi h^{\alpha(h)+1/2}}\lesssim g(h)h^{\beta-\gamma};

(35)

where in the last estimate we have used

Therefore, together with Lemma

\frac{m}{2\pi j}=1+\mathcal{O}{\left(g(h)\right)}.

Inserting the estimates into (

Proposition 2.18
.

Suppose that
d\geq 2
and let
\nu=\ell+\frac{d}{2}-1
. Then there exists
h_{*}\geq 1

\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{h}

(3)

Proof of the claim
(i)
:

[

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits m_{\nu,j}>0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{Im}\sqrt{{\rm i}h+m_{\nu,j}^{2}}>0.

Consequently, we restrict ourselves to solutions

\operatorname{Im}({\rm i}h+m_{\nu,j}^{2})=h+2\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits m_{\nu,j}\operatorname{Im}m_{\nu,j}\geq h-2|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits m_{\nu,j}\operatorname{Im}m_{\nu,j}|>0;

m_{\nu,j}

\lambda_{\ell,j}

of the form
\lambda_{\ell,j}:={\rm i}h+m_{\nu,j}^{2}

(39)

Recall that

Proof of Theorem

.

Take an arbitrary function

Let

We claim that we can choose

and

Indeed, once we impose the restrictions (

as

By Proposition

, there exists

such that for all

, all

(42)

Since

and with (

) and (

\displaystyle{\rm Ratio}(V_{h},f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu_{d}}\;h^{-p}\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{\rm d}(H_{V_{h}})}\dfrac{(\operatorname{Im}\lambda)^{p}}{|\lambda|^{d/2}}f\left(\log\left(\frac{|\lambda|}{\operatorname{Im}\lambda}\right)\right)

where the hidden constant depends on
p
and

only.

Next, we determine a lower bound of

. Observe that for any continuous, monotonic function

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{N},\;u\leq x\leq v}k(x)\;{\rm d}x\geq\int_{\lceil u\rceil}^{\lfloor v\rfloor}k(x)\;{\rm d}x\geq\int_{u+1}^{v-1}k(x)\;{\rm d}x\geq\int_{2u}^{v/2}k(x)\;{\rm d}x

(43)

and

\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{\tilde{L}}_{h,j}}\ell^{d-2}\geq\int_{2h^{\alpha(h)+1/2}}^{jg(h)/2}\ell^{d-2}\;{\rm d}\ell=\frac{1}{d-1}\left[\left(\frac{jg(h)}{2}\right)^{d-1}-\left(2h^{\alpha(h)+1/2}\right)^{d-1}\right].

Applying the lower bound of

from (

\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{\tilde{L}}_{h,j}}\ell^{d-2}\gtrsim(jg(h))^{d-1};

here the non-displayed constant depends on the dimension

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{\tilde{J}}_{h}}\frac{1}{j^{d}}f\left(\log\left(\frac{2^{5}\pi^{2}j^{2}}{h}\right)\right)\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{\tilde{L}}_{h,j}}\ell^{d-2}\gtrsim g^{d-1}(h)\sum_{j\in\mathcal{\tilde{J}}_{h}}\frac{1}{j}f\left(\log\left(\frac{2^{5}\pi^{2}j^{2}}{h}\right)\right).

(44)

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{\tilde{J}}_{h}}\frac{1}{j}f\left(\log\left(\frac{2^{5}\pi^{2}j^{2}}{h}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{2^{4}h^{\alpha(h)+1/2}/g(h)}^{h^{\beta+1/2}/(2g(h))}\frac{1}{j}f\left(\log\left(\frac{2^{5}\pi^{2}j^{2}}{h}\right)\right)\;{\rm d}j

\displaystyle=\int_{\log(ch^{2\alpha(h)}/g^{2}(h))}^{\log(2^{3}\pi^{2}h^{2\beta}/g^{2}(h))}\frac{f(s)}{2}\;{\rm d}s,

\int_{\log(ch^{2\alpha(h)}/g^{2}(h))}^{\log(2^{3}\pi^{2}h^{2\beta}/g^{2}(h))}f(s)\;{\rm d}s\geq\int_{\log(ch^{2\alpha(h)}/g^{2}(h))}^{\varepsilon\log h}f(s)\;{\rm d}s;

\beta=\varepsilon/2

\int_{\log(ch^{2\alpha(h)}/g^{2}(h))}^{\varepsilon\log h}f(s)\;{\rm d}s=F(\varepsilon\log h)-F\left(\log\left(\frac{ch^{2\alpha(h)}}{g^{2}(h)}\right)\right).

Combining this with (

in Claim 1).
Note that the constants
\tilde{\delta}_{n}
have to be chosen so small that

\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\tilde{\delta_{n}}\leq\delta_{N}
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
; take for example
\tilde{\delta}_{n}=\delta_{n}-\delta_{n+1}
and recall that the sequence
\{\delta_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is assumed to be strictly decreasing and converging to
0
.
It is easy to see that the shifts and scaling factors can be chosen such that the potentials
r_{n}^{2}Q_{n}(r_{n}(\cdot-x_{n}))
have disjoint supports and

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|V(x)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|r_{n}^{2}Q_{n}(r_{n}(x-x_{n}))|^{p}\,{\rm d}x\leq 2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|Q_{n}(x)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x.

The right-hande side is finite by the assumptions.
This implies that

\|V-\tilde{V}_{N}\|_{L^{p}}^{p}=\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|r_{n}^{2}Q_{n}(r_{n}(x-x_{n}))|^{p}\,{\rm d}x\to 0

as
N\to\infty
.
Now, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
j=1,\dots,j_{n}
, for
N\geq n
there exist
\mu_{N;n,j}\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(-\Delta+\tilde{V}_{N})
with
|\mu_{N;n,j}-\lambda_{n,j}|<\delta_{n}
, i.e. the
\mu_{N;n,j}
are in an
N
-independent disc. Note that the assumption
\delta_{n}<|\operatorname{Im}\lambda_{n,j}|
implies that this disc does not touch the essential spectrum
[0,\infty)
. Now
[
24
, Lem. 5.4]
and its proof implies convergence of the discrete spectrum (including preservation of multiplicities) in each disc as
N\to\infty
. This proves Claim 2.

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
2.9
. We choose the potentials
Q_{n}
in such a way that
V\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
but
{\rm Ratio}(V,f)=\infty
.
To this end, let
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
. For
n<n_{0}
we take
Q_{n}\equiv 0
, and for
n\geq n_{0}
we take
Q_{n}(x)=c_{n}^{2}V_{n}(c_{n}x)
with
V_{n}(x)={\rm i}n\chi_{B_{1}(0)}(x)
and constants
c_{n}>0
that will be determined later on; they will also ensure that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|Q_{n}\|_{L^{p}}^{p}<\infty
.
Note that a scaling argument yields that
c_{n}^{2}\lambda\in\sigma_{d}(H_{Q_{n}})
if and only if
\lambda\in\sigma_{d}(H_{V_{n}})
.

Let
0<\varepsilon<1
be given. Then Claim 2 (applied with suitably small
\delta_{n}>0
) and
the bound (
7
) (for
d=1
) and Corollary
2.3
(for
d\geq 2
) imply that, for
n_{0}>1
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{\rm d}(H_{V})}\dfrac{\text{dist}(\lambda,[0,\infty))^{p}}{|\lambda|^{d/2}}f\left(-\log\left(\frac{\text{dist}(\lambda,[0,\infty))}{|\lambda|}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{n,j}\in\sigma_{\rm d}(H_{Q_{n}})\\
j=1,2,\ldots,j_{n}\end{subarray}}\dfrac{\text{dist}(\lambda_{n,j},[0,\infty))^{p}}{|\lambda_{n,j}|^{d/2}}f\left(-\log\left(\frac{\text{dist}(\lambda_{n,j},[0,\infty))}{|\lambda_{n,j}|}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{\infty}c_{n}^{2p-d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{n,j}\in\sigma_{\rm d}(H_{V_{n}})\\
j=1,2,\ldots,j_{n}\end{subarray}}\dfrac{\text{dist}(\lambda_{n,j},[0,\infty))^{p}}{|\lambda_{n,j}|^{d/2}}f\left(-\log\left(\frac{\text{dist}(\lambda_{n,j},[0,\infty))}{|\lambda_{n,j}|}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{\infty}c_{n}^{2p-d}\,F(\varepsilon\log n)\,\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|V_{n}(x)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x;

here we have used that the proofs of (
7
) and Corollary
2.3
only take finitely many discrete eigenvalues of
H_{V_{n}}
into consideration.

Claim 2 and a change of variables implies that

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|V(x)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x\leq 2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|Q_{n}(x)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x=2\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{\infty}c_{n}^{2p-d}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|V_{n}(x)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x.

By the assumptions on
p
, we have
2p-d>0
.
Define

c_{n}:=\left(\frac{k(\log n)}{n\,\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|V_{n}(x)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x}\right)^{1/(2p-d)}

for a continuous, non-increasing function
k:[\log n_{0},\infty)\to[0,\infty)
with
\int_{\log n_{0}}^{\infty}k(x)\,{\rm d}x<\infty
; we will explicitly choose this function later on.
Then the above estimates imply

{\rm Ratio}(V,f)\gtrsim\frac{\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{\infty}F(\varepsilon\log n)k(\log n)n^{-1}}{\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{\infty}k(\log n)n^{-1}}.

Note that
\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{\infty}k(\log n)n^{-1}
is finite by the integral test for convergence, since

\int_{n_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{k(\log n)}{n}\,{\rm d}n=\int_{\log n_{0}}^{\infty}k(x)\,{\rm d}x<\infty.

Again using the integral test, in order that
{\rm Ratio}(V,f)=\infty
, it remains to choose the function
k
such that

\int_{n_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}F(\varepsilon\log n)k(\log n)\;{\rm d}n=\int_{\log n_{0}}^{\infty}F(\varepsilon x)k(x)\,{\rm d}x=\infty.

Define
K(x):=(F(\varepsilon x))^{-1/2}
and
k(x):=-K^{\prime}(x)>0
for
x\geq\log n_{0}>0
. Now, the assertion that
k
is non-increasing for
x\geq\log n_{0}
can be verified by a direct calculation

k(x)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(F(\varepsilon x))^{-3/2}f(\varepsilon x),

where we have used that
f
is non-increasing.

Since
\lim_{x\to\infty}K(x)=0
, we see that the assumption
\int_{\log n_{0}}^{\infty}k(x)\,{\rm d}x<\infty
is satisfied.
In addition, for any
x_{0}\geq\log n_{0}
, because
F
is non-decreasing,

\int_{\log n_{0}}^{\infty}F(\varepsilon x)k(x)\,{\rm d}x\geq F(\varepsilon x_{0})\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}k(x)\,{\rm d}x=F(\varepsilon x_{0})K(x_{0})=(F(\varepsilon x_{0}))^{1/2}\to\infty

as
x_{0}\to\infty
.
Since the left-hand side is independent of
x_{0}
, we arrive at
\int_{\log n_{0}}^{\infty}F(\varepsilon x)k(x)\,{\rm d}x=\infty
which concludes the proof.
∎

Finally, we prove (
16
), i.e. the
\tau
-dependence in (
3
) is sharp.

Proof of Theorem
2.11
.

We take a function with
\varphi(\tau)\ll\tau^{-p}
as
\tau\to 0
, i.e.
\tau^{p}\varphi(\tau)\to 0
as
\tau\to 0
. We set the parameters similarly as in Section
2.2
with a modified condition as follows:

0<\alpha(h)<\frac{\gamma}{2}<\frac{3\gamma}{4}<\beta<\gamma<\frac{1}{2}.

Then one can see that
\beta-\gamma>\alpha(h)-\beta
for
h>0
. Hence,
with a function
g
as in Section
2.2
, in particular satisfying (
20
),

g(h)\geq 2h^{\beta-\gamma}\gg\,2h^{\alpha(h)-\beta}

as
h\to\infty
. Here note that we need neither (
41
) nor (
45
).

Take
\tau>0
to be
h
-dependent as
\tau(h):=h^{-2\beta}/32\pi^{2}
, which tends to
0
as
h\to\infty
.
We further choose
g
to decay so slowly that

g^{d-1}(h)\gg\frac{\varphi(\tau(h))}{h^{2p\beta}}=(32\pi^{2})^{p}\tau^{p}(h)\varphi(\tau(h)),\quad h\to\infty.

(47)

For
h\geq 1
we consider the Schrödinger operator
V_{h}={\rm i}h\chi_{B_{1}(0)}
and define the following index sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{h}:=\left\{\ell\in\mathbb{N}:h^{\alpha(h)+1/2}\leq\ell\leq\frac{g(h)}{2}\;h^{\beta+1/2}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{J}^{\prime}_{h,\ell}:=\left\{j\in\mathbb{N}:\frac{\ell}{g(h)}\leq j\leq h^{\beta+1/2}\right\}.

Notice that
\emptyset\neq\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{h}\times\mathcal{J}^{\prime}_{h,\ell}\subset\mathcal{L}_{h}\times\mathcal{J}_{h,\ell}
for all
h
sufficiently large.

In view of Proposition
2.18
, there exists
h_{*}\geq 1
such that for all
h\geq h_{*}
,
\ell\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{h}
and
j\in\mathcal{J}^{\prime}_{h,\ell}
the number
\lambda_{\ell,j}={\rm i}h+m_{\nu,j}^{2}
is an eigenvalue of
H_{V_{h}}
with the multiplicity
m(\lambda_{\ell,j})\gtrsim\ell^{d-2}
. Due to (
36
),

\frac{|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda_{\ell,j}|}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda_{\ell,j}}\geq\frac{h}{32\pi^{2}j^{2}}\geq\frac{h^{-2\beta}}{32\pi^{2}}=\tau(h).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varphi(\tau(h))\|V_{h}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(H_{V_{h}})\\
|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda|\geq\tau(h)\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda\end{subarray}}|\lambda|^{p-d/2}\gtrsim\frac{1}{\varphi(\tau(h))h^{p}}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{h}}\ell^{d-2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{\prime}_{h,\ell}}j^{2p-d}.

We estimate with (
43
)

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{\prime}_{h,\ell}}j^{2p-d}\geq\int_{(\ell/g(h))+1}^{(h^{\beta+1/2})-1}j^{2p-d}\;{\rm d}j\gtrsim\;h^{2p\beta+p-(d-1)(\beta+1/2)},

and

\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{h}}\ell^{d-2}\geq\int_{(h^{\alpha(h)+1/2})+1}^{(g(h)h^{\beta+1/2}/2)-1}\ell^{d-2}\;{\rm d}\ell\gtrsim\;g^{d-1}(h)h^{(d-1)(\beta+1/2)},

where the above hidden constants depend on
p
and
d
only.
In conclusion, via (
47
),

\frac{1}{\varphi(\tau(h))\|V_{h}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(H_{V_{h}})\\
|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda|\geq\tau(h)\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda\end{subarray}}|\lambda|^{p-d/2}\gtrsim\;g^{d-1}(h)\frac{h^{2p\beta}}{\varphi(\tau(h))}\to\infty,

for
h\to\infty
. This proves (
16
).
∎

3.
Jacobi operators

In this section we establish optimal Lieb–Thirring type inequalities for Jacobi operators.

3.1.
Main results

The proofs of the following main results will be presented in Section
3.2
.

The first result is an improvement of (
10
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
p\geq 3/2
and let
f:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
be a continuous, non-increasing function. If
\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\;{\rm d}x<\infty
, then there exists a constant
C_{p,f}>0
such that for all
v\in\ell^{p}({\mathbb{Z}})

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])^{p}}{|\lambda^{2}-4|^{1/2}}f\left(-\log\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,\{-2,2\})}\right)\right)\leq C_{p,f}\,\|v\|^{p}_{\ell^{p}},

(48)

where
C_{p,f}=C_{p}\,\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\;{\rm d}x+f(0)\right)
for an
f
-independent constant
C_{p}>0
.

Remark 3.2
.

The bound (
48
) reduces to the classical Lieb-Thirring inequality (
8
) in the self-adjoint case. In addition, this is a generalisation of the Hansmann-Katriel bound (
10
) which is recovered by setting
f(x)={\rm e}^{-\kappa x}
.

Next we show that Theorem
3.1
is optimal in the sense that if the integrability condition is removed, then the inequality (
48
) cannot be true by proving explicit divergence rates.
To this end, we consider the Jacobi operator
J
with
a_{k}=1,c_{k}=1
for all
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, which implies
v_{k}=|b_{k}|
. This is a discrete Schrödinger operator with a potential
b
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
b=b(n)
be defined by

b_{k}:=\begin{cases}{\rm i}n^{-2/3}\quad&\textrm{if\;\;}k\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\},\\
0\quad&\textrm{if\;\;}k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\backslash\{1,2,\ldots,n\}.\end{cases}

(49)

For ease of notation, we will not explicitly denote the dependence on
n
by a further index.
Then
b\in\ell^{p}({\mathbb{Z}})
and an easy calculation shows that

\|v\|^{p}_{\ell^{p}}=\|b\|^{p}_{\ell^{p}}=n^{1-2p/3}.

(50)

A numerical range argument
[
6
, Lem. 4]
implies the inclusion
\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)\subset[-2,2]+{\rm i}(0,n^{-2/3}]
for all
n\geq 2
.

The divergence rates, Theorems
3.3
and
3.5
, that will be formulated below rely on eigenvalue estimates of this type of discrete Schrödinger operators above.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
p\geq 1
and
\gamma\in(2/3,1)
. Take a function
g:[1,\infty)\to[1,\infty)
with
g(n)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
so slowly such that

\frac{g(n)}{n^{\gamma-2/3}}\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

Then there exist
C_{p}>0
and
n_{*}\geq 2
such that for all continuous, non-increasing functions
f:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
with
\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\;{\rm d}x=\infty
and all integers
n\geq n_{*}

\displaystyle\sup_{0\neq v\in\ell^{p}({\mathbb{Z}})}\frac{1}{\|v\|^{p}_{\ell^{p}}}\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])^{p}}{|\lambda^{2}-4|^{1/2}}

\displaystyle f\left(-\log\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,\{-2,2\})}\right)\right)

(51)

\displaystyle\geq C_{p}\left(F(\log n^{2/3})-3f(0)\log g(n)\right),

where
F(t):=\int_{0}^{t}f(x)\;{\rm d}x
for
t\geq 0
.

Remark 3.4
.

Clearly, the lower bound on the right-hand side of (
51
) diverges provided that
F(\log n^{2/3})
is divergent faster than
\log g(n)
as
n\to\infty
. In addition, we note that even though Theorem
3.1
requires
p\geq 3/2
, here Theorem
3.3
does not.

The following result concerns the divergence rates of the left-hand side of (
51
) when a function
f
is non-decreasing. Obviously, in this case,
\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\;{\rm d}x=\infty
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
p\geq 1
,
0<\varepsilon<2/3<\gamma<1
and let
x_{0}\geq 1
. Take a function
g:[1,\infty)\to[1,\infty)
with
g(n)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
so slowly that

\frac{g(n)}{n^{\gamma-2/3}}\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

Then there exist
C_{p}>0
and
n_{*}\geq 2
such that for all integers
n\geq n_{*}
and all continuous, non-decreasing functions
f:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
such that
f(\log t^{2})/t
is monotonic for
t\geq x_{0}
one has

\displaystyle\sup_{0\neq v\in\ell^{p}({\mathbb{Z}})}\frac{1}{\|v\|^{p}_{\ell^{p}}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])^{p}}{|\lambda^{2}-4|^{1/2}}f\left(-\log\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,\{-2,2\})}\right)\right)

(52)

\displaystyle\geq C_{p}\left[F\left(\log(\pi^{2}n^{\varepsilon})\right)-F\left(\log(\pi^{2}g^{2}(n))\right)\right]\geq C_{p}f(0)\;\log\frac{n^{\varepsilon}}{g^{2}(n)}.

Remark 3.6
.

We notice that if
g(n)\ll n^{\varepsilon/2}
, then the right-hand side of (
52
) diverges as
n\to\infty
.

Remark 3.7
.

It would be interesting to investigate whether or not an analogue of Theorem
2.9
for Jacobi operators is true. Unfortunately, the scaling argument that was used in the proof for Schrödinger operators is no longer available in the Jacobi case.

The last main result proves that the
\omega
-dependence of the constant
C_{p,\omega}
in (
9
), i.e. the order
\tan^{p}(\omega)
as
\omega\to\frac{\pi}{2}^{-}
, is optimal.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
p\geq 1
and let
\varphi:(0,\pi/2)\to(0,\infty)
be a continuous function such that
\varphi(\omega)\ll\tan^{p}(\omega)
as
\omega\to\frac{\pi}{2}^{-}
. Then

\limsup_{\omega\to\frac{\pi}{2}^{-}}\sup_{0\neq v\in\ell^{p}({\mathbb{Z}})}\frac{1}{\varphi(\omega)\|v\|^{p}_{\ell^{p}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)\\
2-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda<\tan(\omega)|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda|\end{subarray}}|\lambda-2|^{p-1/2}=\infty.

(53)

The proof relies on eigenvalue estimates for the same class of potentials
b
as in the previous results.

3.2.
Proofs of main results

First we prove the new Lieb–Thirring type inequalities.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Assume that the given function
f
satisfies

\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\;{\rm d}x<\infty.

Let
d>0
. Then, following the construction from
[
3
, Thm. 2.1]
, we can always find a continuous, non-increasing, integrable, piecewise
C^{1}
-function
g:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
such that

f\leq g\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{0}^{\infty}g(x)\;{\rm d}x\leq 2\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\;{\rm d}x+\frac{2f(0)}{d}<\infty,

(54)

and it then follows that, for
a>0
,

\int_{a}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-px}g(x)\;{\rm d}x\geq\frac{1}{p+d}\;{\rm e}^{-pa}g(a).

(55)

Now, we define the following sector in the complex plane

\Sigma_{1}:=\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}:\,\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda\geq 0,\;2-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda<|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda|\}=\{\lambda\in\Phi^{+}_{\pi/4}:\,\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda\geq 0\}.

It can be seen that for
\lambda\in\Sigma_{1}
, we have
|\lambda+2|\geq 2
and hence

|\lambda-2|^{p-1/2}\geq\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])^{p}\frac{|\lambda+2|^{1/2}}{|\lambda^{2}-4|^{1/2}}\geq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{f(0)}\;\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])^{p}}{|\lambda^{2}-4|^{1/2}}f\left(-\log\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,\{-2,2\})}\right)\right)

where we have used that
f(x)\leq f(0)
for all
x\geq 0
.
Applying this inequality to (
9
) yields

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)\cap\Sigma_{1}}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])^{p}}{|\lambda^{2}-4|^{1/2}}f\left(-\log\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,\{-2,2\})}\right)\right)\leq C_{p}\,f(0)\|v\|^{p}_{\ell^{p}}.

(56)

Here and in the following,
C_{p}>0
denotes a generic constant.

Next, we define another sector
\Sigma_{2}:=\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}:\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda\geq 0\}\backslash\Sigma_{1}.

Note that for
\lambda\in\sigma_{d}(J)\cap\Sigma_{2}
we have

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda\in[0,2),\quad|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda|=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2]),\quad\frac{2-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda}{|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda|}\geq 1.

Due to the inequality (
9
) with
x=\tan(\omega)\in[0,\infty)
, we have the estimate

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)\cap\Sigma_{2},\;\frac{2-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda}{|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda|}<x}|\lambda-2|^{p-1/2}\leq C_{p}\,(1+2x)^{p}\|v\|^{p}_{\ell^{p}}.

(57)

We, then, multiply both sides of (
57
) by
x^{-p-1}g(\log x)
and integrate over
x\in[1,\infty)
. For the left-hand side one has

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-p-1}g(\log x)\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)\cap\Sigma_{2},\;\frac{2-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda}{|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda|}<x}|\lambda-2|^{p-1/2}\;{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)\cap\Sigma_{2}}|\lambda-2|^{p-1/2}\int_{(2-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda)/|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda|}^{\infty}x^{-p-1}g(\log x)\;{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)\cap\Sigma_{2}}|\lambda-2|^{p-1/2}\int_{\log((2-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda)/|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda|)}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-px}g(x)\;{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\geq C_{p}\,\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)\cap\Sigma_{2}}|\lambda-2|^{p-1/2}\left(\frac{|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda|}{2-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda}\right)^{p}g\left(-\log\left(\frac{|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda|}{2-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda}\right)\right)\qquad\qquad(\textrm{by \eqref{eq:integral lower bd S}})

\displaystyle=C_{p}\,\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)\cap\Sigma_{2}}|\lambda-2|^{p-1/2}\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])}{2-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda}\right)^{p}g\left(-\log\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])}{2-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq C_{p}\,\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)\cap\Sigma_{2}}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])^{p}}{|\lambda^{2}-4|^{1/2}}g\left(-\log\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,\{-2,2\})}\right)\right),

where we have used
|\lambda-2|^{-1/2}\geq\sqrt{2}\,|\lambda^{2}-4|^{-1/2}
and
2-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda\leq|\lambda-2|=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,\{-2,2\})
in the last step.

For the right-hand side of (
57
) one proceeds similarly.
With
(1+2x)^{p}\leq 3^{p}\,x^{p}
for
x\geq 1
we obtain

\int_{1}^{\infty}C_{p}\,(1+2x)^{p}x^{-p-1}g(\log x)\;{\rm d}x\leq 3^{p}C_{p}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}g(x)\;{\rm d}x.

Together with the bounds (
54
), we finally arrive at

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)\cap\Sigma_{2}}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])^{p}}{|\lambda^{2}-4|^{1/2}}f\left(-\log\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,\{-2,2\})}\right)\right)\leq C_{p,f}\,\|v\|^{p}_{\ell^{p}}.

(58)

Noting that
\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}=\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}:\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda\geq 0\}
we have proven the inequality (
48
) for all discrete eigenvalues in the right half-plane by means of (
56
) and (
58
).

The proof for the left half-plane
\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}:\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda\leq 0\}
is analogous. Namely, we redefine the sectors
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}
appropriately,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}

\displaystyle:=\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}:\,\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda\leq 0,\;2+\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda<|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda|\}=\{\lambda\in\Phi^{-}_{\pi/4}:\,\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda\leq 0\},

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}

\displaystyle:=\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}:\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda\leq 0\}\backslash\Sigma_{1},

and we use

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)\cap\Phi^{-}_{\omega}}|\lambda+2|^{p-1/2}\leq C_{p,\omega}\,\|v\|^{p}_{\ell^{p}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

In order to prove the optimality and divergence rates, we use the following result.
In the following,
J
always denotes the Jacobi operator with the potential
b
as in (
49
).

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
\gamma\in(2/3,1)
and let
g
be a function as in Theorems
3.3
and
3.5
.
Define

\mathcal{J}(n):=\left\{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}:\frac{1}{2}n^{2/3}g(n)+\frac{3}{4}\leq j\leq\frac{n}{8}-\frac{1}{4}\right\}.

For
j\in\mathcal{J}(n)
define
x_{j}:=\frac{(4j-1)\pi}{2n}
and

D_{j}:=\left\{z=r{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\phi}:\,x_{j}-\frac{\pi}{n}\leq\phi\leq x_{j}+\frac{\pi}{n},\;R_{1}:=1-n^{-\gamma}\leq r\leq 1-\frac{\log g(n)}{n}=:R_{2}\right\}.

(59)

Then there exists
n_{*}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all integers
n\geq n_{*}
and all
j\in\mathcal{J}(n)
, there exists
z_{j}=r_{j}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\phi_{j}}\in D_{j}
such that the operator
J
has an eigenvalue

\lambda_{j}={\rm i}n^{-2/3}+z_{j}+z^{-1}_{j}=2\cos\phi_{j}+{\rm i}n^{-2/3}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-\gamma}),

with
\lambda_{j_{1}}\neq\lambda_{j_{2}}
for
j_{1}\neq j_{2}
, and

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda_{j},[-2,2])

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda_{j}=n^{-2/3}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-\gamma}),

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda_{j},\{-2,2\})

\displaystyle=|\lambda_{j}-2|=2(1-\cos\phi_{j})\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{g^{2}(n)}\right)\right),

\displaystyle|\lambda^{2}_{j}-4|

\displaystyle=4\sin^{2}\phi_{j}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{g^{2}(n)}\right)\right),

as
n\to\infty
.
The involved constants in the error terms are all independent of
j
.

Proof.

Due to the eigenvalue construction in
[
6
, Sect. 2.1]
, the complex solutions
z
of the polynomial equation

{\rm i}n^{-2/3}(z^{n+1}-1)(z^{n-1}-1)=z^{n-2}(z^{2}-1)^{2},

(60)

with
|z|<1,\;\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits z>0
and
|z^{n+1}-1|<|z^{n}-z|
, correspond to eigenvalues
\lambda
of
J
outside the closed interval
[-2,2]
. In fact, these eigenvalues
\lambda
are explicitly given by

\lambda={\rm i}n^{-2/3}+z+z^{-1},

see
[
6
, Prop. 6]
.

To solve (
60
) for
z
, we proceed analogously as in
[
6
, Prop. 8]
with the following modified restrictions. We seek solutions
z=r{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\phi}
of (
60
) in the closed region determined by

n^{-1/3}g(n)\pi\leq\phi\leq\frac{\pi}{4}\quad\text{and}\quad 1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\leq r\leq 1-\frac{\log g(n)}{n}.

(61)

Note that the assumption on
g
guarantees that for
n\to\infty
,
n^{-1/3}g(n)\ll n^{\gamma-1}
, therefore the set determined by (
61
) is non-empty for all
n
sufficiently large.
First, we prove the existence of solutions of the polynomial equation in this closed region.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
notice that
j\in\mathcal{J}(n)
if and only if

\left[x_{j}-\frac{\pi}{n},x_{j}+\frac{\pi}{n}\right]\subset\left[n^{-1/3}g(n)\pi,\frac{\pi}{4}\right].

We thus observe that for
j\in\mathcal{J}(n)
,
D_{j}
is a subset of that corresponding to (
61
).
For each
n
sufficiently large we will employ Rouché’s theorem to show that (
60
) has a solution
z
in the interior of
D_{j}
.

Due to (
61
),

r^{n}\leq\left(1-\frac{\log g(n)}{n}\right)^{n}=\frac{1}{g(n)}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log^{2}g(n)}{n}\right)\right),\quad n\to\infty.

Note that
\frac{\log^{2}g(n)}{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
by the assumptions on
g(n)
.
Thus

r^{n}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{g(n)}\right),\quad n\to\infty.

(62)

Moreover, again by (
61
),

r=1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right),\quad n\to\infty.

(63)

We rearrange (
60
):

\displaystyle{\rm i}n^{-2/3}(z^{n+1}-1)(z^{n-1}-1)=z^{n-2}(z^{2}-1)^{2}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;\;

\displaystyle{\rm i}n^{-2/3}+{\rm i}n^{-2/3}(z^{2n}-z^{n+1}-z^{n-1})=z^{n}(z-z^{-1})^{2}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;\;

\displaystyle{\rm i}n^{-2/3}+4z^{n}\sin^{2}x_{j}=z^{n}[(z-z^{-1})^{2}+4\sin^{2}x_{j}]-{\rm i}n^{-2/3}(z^{2n}-z^{n+1}-z^{n-1}).

Now, define

\displaystyle f(z):={\rm i}n^{-2/3}+4z^{n}\sin^{2}x_{j}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle h(z):={\rm i}n^{-2/3}(z^{2n}-z^{n+1}-z^{n-1})-z^{n}[(z-z^{-1})^{2}+4\sin^{2}x_{j}].

Then both functions
f
and
h
are analytic at every
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{0\}\supset D_{j}
.

For each
n
sufficiently large it can be verified that

\tilde{z}_{j}:=\left(\frac{n^{-2/3}}{4\sin^{2}x_{j}}\right)^{1/n}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}x_{j}}

is the unique, simple zero of
f(z)
inside
D_{j}
.
Let us check that indeed
\tilde{z}_{j}\in D_{j}
. The condition on the angle is obviously satisfied, so we check the condition on the radius.
Notice that
\sin x\geq x/2
for
x\in(0,\pi/2]
, which implies

\text{for }n^{-1/3}g(n)\pi\leq\phi\leq\frac{\pi}{4}:\quad\frac{1}{2\sin\phi}\leq\frac{1}{\phi}\leq\frac{n^{1/3}}{g(n)\pi}.

(64)

This yields, for
\phi=x_{j}
,

\frac{n^{-2/3}}{4\sin^{2}x_{j}}\leq\frac{n^{-2/3}}{x^{2}_{j}}\leq\frac{1}{g^{2}(n)\pi^{2}},

(65)

which converges to
0
as
n\to\infty
. Thus, for all sufficiently large
n
,
\log(n^{-2/3}/4\sin^{2}x_{j})<0
and

\left|\log\frac{n^{-2/3}}{4\sin^{2}x_{j}}\right|=\log(4n^{2/3}\sin^{2}x_{j})\leq\log(4n^{2/3}).

Now, we may write

|\tilde{z}_{j}|=\exp\left(\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{n^{-2/3}}{4\sin^{2}x_{j}}\right)=1+\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{n^{-2/3}}{4\sin^{2}x_{j}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{n^{2}}\right),\quad n\to\infty.

Using (
65
) we obtain the two-sided estimate

\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{n^{-2/3}}{4}\leq\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{n^{-2/3}}{4\sin^{2}x_{j}}\leq-\frac{2}{n}(\log g(n)+\log\pi).

As a result, the radial condition of (
59
) is satisfied, so
\tilde{z}_{j}\in D_{j}
. Now Rouché’s theorem guarantees that
f(z)+h(z)
has a unique zero in the interior of
D_{j}
provided that
|f(z)|>|h(z)|
for all
z
on the boundary
\partial D_{j}
.

To prove this, we begin by considering the asymptotic behaviour of
h(z)
in
D_{j}
as
n\to\infty
. Since
D_{j}
is a subregion of that determined by (
61
), one may write

{\rm i}n^{-2/3}(z^{2n}-z^{n+1}-z^{n-1})=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n^{-2/3}}{g(n)}\right),\quad n\to\infty,

where we have used (
62
) and (
63
).
Due to the definition of
D_{j}
, every
z=r{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\phi}\in D_{j}
satisfies

r=1+\mathcal{O}(n^{-\gamma}),\quad n\to\infty,

which implies

z-z^{-1}=r{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\phi}-r^{-1}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\phi}=2{\rm i}\sin\phi+\mathcal{O}(n^{-\gamma}),\quad n\to\infty.

(66)

In particular,

(z-z^{-1})^{2}=-4\sin^{2}\phi+\mathcal{O}(n^{-\gamma}),\quad n\to\infty.

(67)

Since
\phi\in[x_{j}-\pi/n,x_{j}+\pi/n]
, one has
|\phi-x_{j}|\leq\pi/n
. Furthermore,

\sin\phi=\sin(\phi-x_{j})\cos x_{j}+\cos(\phi-x_{j})\sin x_{j}=\sin x_{j}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right),\quad n\to\infty.

(68)

Now, combining (
62
), (
67
) and (
68
) yields

z^{n}[(z-z^{-1})^{2}+4\sin^{2}x_{j}]=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n^{-\gamma}}{g(n)}\right),\quad n\to\infty.

In total, we can infer from
\gamma>2/3
that, uniformly in
z\in D_{j}
,

h(z)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n^{-2/3}}{g(n)}\right),\quad n\to\infty.

(69)

Next, we investigate the asymptotic behaviour of
f(z)
on the boundary
\partial D_{j}
as
n\to\infty
.
We proceed in three steps. First, using the definition of
D_{j}
,
we consider
z=r{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\phi}
with

\phi=x_{j}\pm\frac{\pi}{n}\quad\text{and}\quad R_{1}\leq r\leq R_{2}.

Here we recall that
x_{j}=(4j-1)\pi/2n
. Consequently,

z^{n}=r^{n}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}n(x_{j}\pm\pi/n)}={\rm i}r^{n}.

Then

|f(z)|=|{\rm i}n^{-2/3}+4{\rm i}r^{n}\sin^{2}x_{j}|\geq n^{-2/3}>|h(z)|

for all sufficiently large
n
,
where we have taken (
69
) into account.

As a second step, consider
z=r{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\phi}
with
r=R_{1}
and
\phi\in[x_{j}-\pi/n,x_{j}+\pi/n]
. It follows that

r^{n}=R^{n}_{1}=(1-n^{-\gamma})^{n}={\rm e}^{-n^{1-\gamma}}(1+\mathcal{O}(n^{1-2\gamma})),\quad n\to\infty.

Notice that
1-\gamma>0
while
1-2\gamma<0
. Thus, by the reverse triangle inequality,

|f(z)|\geq|n^{-2/3}-4r^{n}\sin^{2}x_{j}|=n^{-2/3}-4R^{n}_{1}\sin^{2}x_{j}>|h(z)|

for all sufficiently large
n
.

As a third and last step, let
z=r{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\phi}
with
r=R_{2}
and
\phi\in[x_{j}-\pi/n,x_{j}+\pi/n]
. One readily has

r^{n}=R^{n}_{2}=\left(1-\frac{\log g(n)}{n}\right)^{n}=\frac{1}{g(n)}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log^{2}g(n)}{n}\right)\right),\quad n\to\infty.

Hence, with (
64
) for
\phi=x_{j}
,

|f(z)|\geq 4R^{n}_{2}\sin^{2}x_{j}-n^{-2/3}\geq R^{n}_{2}x^{2}_{j}-n^{-2/3}\geq n^{-2/3}g(n)-n^{-2/3}>|h(z)|,

for all
n
sufficiently large.

Now we can apply Rouché’s theorem which proves the existence of a unique solution
z_{j}
of (
60
) inside
D_{j}
.

Next, for each
n
sufficiently large we prove that the found solutions
z_{j}=r_{j}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\phi_{j}}
,
j\in\mathcal{J}(n)
, satisfy the condition
|z^{n+1}_{j}-1|<|z^{n}_{j}-z_{j}|
. Similarly as in
[
6
, Prop. 7]
, we do this by considering

k_{j}:=\frac{1-z^{n+1}_{j}}{z_{j}-z^{n}_{j}},

and show that
|k_{j}|<1
. In fact,

k_{j}=\frac{1}{z_{j}}\left(1-\frac{z^{n}_{j}(z_{j}-z^{-1}_{j})}{1-z^{n-1}_{j}}\right).

Recalling the uniform asymptotic formula (
66
) for
z\in D_{j}
, one can deduce with (
64
) for
\phi=\phi_{j}
,

z_{j}-z^{-1}_{j}=2{\rm i}\sin\phi_{j}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n^{1/3-\gamma}}{g(n)}\right)\right),\quad n\to\infty.

Then, using that
z_{j}
is a solution of (
60
) and with (
62
),

\frac{z^{n}_{j}(z_{j}-z^{-1}_{j})}{1-z^{n-1}_{j}}={\rm i}n^{-2/3}\frac{1-z^{n+1}_{j}}{z_{j}-z^{-1}_{j}}=\frac{n^{-2/3}}{2\sin\phi_{j}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{g(n)}\right)\right),\quad n\to\infty.

Employing
r_{j}^{-1}=1+\mathcal{O}(n^{-\gamma})
, one arrives at the asymptotic behaviour

k_{j}={\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\phi_{j}}\left(1-\frac{n^{-2/3}}{2\sin\phi_{j}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n^{-2/3}}{g(n)\sin\phi_{j}}\right)\right),\quad n\to\infty.

Note that, by the assumption on
g
and
\gamma>2/3
, one obtains
0<g(n)\sin\phi_{j}\ll n^{\gamma-2/3}
, which implies that
n^{-\gamma}\ll\frac{n^{-2/3}}{g(n)\sin\phi_{j}}
as
n\to\infty
.
Thus we can conclude that
|k_{j}|<1
,
j\in\mathcal{J}(n)
, when
n
is sufficiently large.

In total, for
j\in\mathcal{J}(n)
the found solution
z_{j}\in D_{j}
gives rise to the eigenvalue

\lambda=\lambda_{j}={\rm i}n^{-2/3}+z_{j}+z^{-1}_{j}.

Bearing
\gamma>2/3
and the definition of
D_{j}
in mind, we can write

\lambda_{j}=2\cos\phi_{j}+{\rm i}n^{-2/3}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-\gamma}),\quad n\to\infty.

(70)

Taking the imaginary parts on both sides of (
70
) yields

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda_{j},[-2,2])=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda_{j}=n^{-2/3}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-\gamma}),\quad n\to\infty.

Notice that
\cos\phi_{j}\in[1/\sqrt{2},1)
by (
61
). Consequently,
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda_{j}>0
for
n
sufficiently large. This readily implies that
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda_{j},\{-2,2\})=|\lambda_{j}-2|
. Since
1-\cos x\geq x^{2}/4
for
x\in(0,\pi/2]
,

\frac{1}{1-\cos\phi_{j}}\leq\frac{4}{\phi^{2}_{j}}\leq\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}\frac{n^{2/3}}{g^{2}(n)},

where we have used the first restriction of (
61
). Applying this estimate to (
70
) yields

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda_{j},\{-2,2\})=|\lambda_{j}-2|=2(1-\cos\phi_{j})\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{g^{2}(n)}\right)\right),\quad n\to\infty.

In addition,

\lambda^{2}_{j}=4\cos^{2}\phi_{j}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-2/3}),\quad n\to\infty,

which results in the asymptotic formula

4-\lambda^{2}_{j}=4\sin^{2}\phi_{j}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-2/3}),\quad n\to\infty.

Together with (
64
) for
\phi=\phi_{j}
, we arrive at

|\lambda^{2}_{j}-4|=4\sin^{2}\phi_{j}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{g^{2}(n)}\right)\right),\quad n\to\infty.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Now, everything is in place for deriving (
51
).

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Suppose that
f:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is a continuous, non-increasing function such that

\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\;{\rm d}x=\infty.

Then it is obvious that
F(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
.

From (
50
), Proposition
3.9
and the fact that
f
is non-increasing, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|v\|^{p}_{\ell^{p}}}\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])^{p}}{|\lambda^{2}-4|^{1/2}}f\left(-\log\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,\{-2,2\})}\right)\right)

(71)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{n^{1-2p/3}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}(n)}\frac{n^{-2p/3}}{2^{p}}\frac{1}{4\sin\phi_{j}}f\left(\log(8(1-\cos\phi_{j})n^{2/3})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2^{p+2}n}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}(n)}\frac{1}{\phi_{j}}f\left(\log(4\phi^{2}_{j}n^{2/3})\right)

where we have used
\sin x\leq x
and
1-\cos x\leq x^{2}/2
for
x\in(0,\pi/2]
in the last step.

From the definition of
D_{j}
, it can be seen that
|\phi_{j}-x_{j}|\leq\pi/n
, hence

\phi_{j}\leq 2x_{j}=\frac{(4j-1)\pi}{n}=:\theta_{j},

for
n
sufficiently large. Now, applying this to (
71
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{p+2}n}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}(n)}f\left(\log(4\phi^{2}_{j}n^{2/3})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2^{p+2}n}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}(n)}\frac{1}{\theta_{j}}f\left(\log(4\theta^{2}_{j}n^{2/3})\right)\geq\frac{1}{2^{p+2}n}\int_{(2n^{2/3}g(n)+7)/4}^{(n-2)/8}\frac{1}{\theta_{j}}f\left(\log(4\theta^{2}_{j}n^{2/3})\right)\;{\rm d}j

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2^{p+5}\pi}\int_{4n^{-1/3}g(n)\pi}^{\pi/4}\frac{2}{\theta_{j}}f\left(\log(4\theta^{2}_{j}n^{2/3})\right)\;{\rm d}\theta_{j}=\frac{1}{2^{p+5}\pi}\int_{\log(64\pi^{2}g^{2}(n))}^{\log(\pi^{2}n^{2/3}/4)}f(x)\;{\rm d}x,

where we have made the change of variables
x=\log(4\theta^{2}_{j}n^{2/3})
.

At this point, we put
C_{p}:=1/2^{p+5}\pi
and
n_{*}\geq 2
can be chosen so large that the above uniform asymptotics hold. Besides, for all
n\geq n_{*}
we may assume
64\pi^{2}g^{2}(n)\leq g^{3}(n)
.
Together with
F(x)\leq f(0)x
, we obtain

\int_{\log(64\pi^{2}g^{2}(n))}^{\log(\pi^{2}n^{2/3}/4)}f(x)\;{\rm d}x\geq F(\log n^{2/3})-F(\log g^{3}(n))\geq F(\log n^{2/3})-3f(0)\log g(n).

Combining this with (
71
) proves (
51
) as desired.
∎

Next, we prove the divergence rate for non-decreasing functions
f
.

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

Let
f:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
be a continuous, non-decreasing function such that
f(\log t^{2})/t
is monotonic for
t\geq x_{0}
. We consider the Jacobi operator
J
from Proposition
3.9
.
There exists
n_{0}\geq 2
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
we have
g(n)\geq 2x_{0}/\pi
. In particular, for all
t\geq n^{-1/3}g(n)\pi

\frac{t^{2}n^{2/3}}{4}\geq x^{2}_{0}.

Then the function

t\mapsto\frac{1}{t}f\left(\log\left(\frac{t^{2}n^{2/3}}{4}\right)\right)

is also monotonic for
t\geq n^{-1/3}g(n)\pi
.

Using (
50
), Proposition
3.9
and the fact that
1-\cos x\geq x^{2}/4
for
x\in(0,\pi/2]
results in

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|v\|^{p}_{\ell^{p}}}\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])^{p}}{|\lambda^{2}-4|^{1/2}}f\left(-\log\left(\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,[-2,2])}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{dist}\nolimits(\lambda,\{-2,2\})}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{n^{1-2p/3}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}(n)}\frac{n^{-2p/3}}{2^{p}}\frac{1}{4\sin\phi_{j}}f\left(\log((1-\cos\phi_{j})n^{2/3})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2^{p+2}n}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}(n)}\frac{1}{\phi_{j}}f\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{4}\phi^{2}_{j}n^{2/3}\right)\right).

Recalling
x_{j}=(4j-1)\pi/2n
, one may deduce from the definition of
D_{j}
that

\frac{x_{j}}{2}\leq\phi_{j}\leq 2x_{j}

for
n
sufficiently large. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{p+2}n}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}(n)}\frac{1}{\phi_{j}}f\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{4}\phi^{2}_{j}n^{2/3}\right)\right)\geq\frac{1}{2^{p+3}n}\int_{(2n^{2/3}g(n)+7)/4}^{(n-10)/8}\frac{1}{x_{j}}f\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{16}x^{2}_{j}n^{2/3}\right)\right)\;{\rm d}j

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2^{p+5}\pi}\int_{4n^{-1/3}g(n)\pi}^{\pi/8}\frac{2}{x_{j}}f\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{16}x^{2}_{j}n^{2/3}\right)\right)\;{\rm d}x_{j}=C_{p}\int_{\log(\pi^{2}g^{2}(n))}^{\log(2^{-10}\pi^{2}n^{2/3})}f(x)\;{\rm d}x,

where we have set
C_{p}=1/2^{p+5}\pi>0
.

Recall that
0<\varepsilon<2/3
.
Now, we choose
n_{*}\geq n_{0}
such that for all
n\geq n_{*}
we get
2^{-10}n^{2/3-\varepsilon}\geq 1
. Hence, one can conclude that

\int_{\log(\pi^{2}g^{2}(n))}^{\log(2^{-10}\pi^{2}n^{2/3})}f(x)\;{\rm d}x\geq F\left(\log(\pi^{2}n^{\varepsilon})\right)-F\left(\log(\pi^{2}g^{2}(n))\right)\geq f(0)\log\frac{n^{\varepsilon}}{g^{2}(n)},

which gives rise to (
52
).
∎

Finally we prove (
53
).

Proof of Theorem
3.8
.

First, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
we let
\omega
depend on
n
as

\omega(n):=\arctan\left(4(2-\sqrt{2})n^{2/3}\right)\in(0,\pi/2).

Due to spectral analysis in Proposition
3.9
, there exists
n_{*}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq n_{*}
and all
j\in\mathcal{J}(n)
the operator
J
has the eigenvalue
\lambda_{j}
inside
[0,2]+{\rm i}(0,n^{-2/3}]
with

|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda_{j}|\geq\frac{n^{-2/3}}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad 1-\cos\phi_{j}\leq|\lambda_{j}-2|\leq 4(1-\cos\phi_{j}).

Besides, recalling
x_{j}=\frac{(4j-1)\pi}{2n}
,

\frac{x_{j}}{2}\leq\phi_{j}\leq x_{j}+\frac{\pi}{n}\leq\frac{\pi}{4}.

One can see that

\frac{2-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda_{j}}{|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda_{j}|}<\frac{|\lambda_{j}-2|}{|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda_{j}|}\leq 8(1-\cos\phi_{j})n^{2/3}\leq 4(2-\sqrt{2})n^{2/3}=\tan(\omega(n)),

therefore, bearing (
50
) in mind,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varphi(\omega(n))\|v\|^{p}_{\ell^{p}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)\\
2-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda<\tan(\omega(n))|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda|\end{subarray}}|\lambda-2|^{p-1/2}\geq\frac{1}{\varphi(\omega(n))n^{1-2p/3}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}(n)}(1-\cos\phi_{j})^{p-1/2}.

Noticing that
1-\cos x\geq x^{2}/4
for
x\in(0,\pi/2]
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}(n)}(1-\cos\phi_{j})^{p-1/2}\gtrsim\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}(n)}\phi^{2p-1}_{j}\geq\frac{1}{2^{2p-1}}\int_{n^{2/3}g(n)+3/2}^{(n-2)/16}x^{2p-1}_{j}\;{\rm d}j,

where we have used (
43
) and
\phi_{j}\geq x_{j}/2
in the last step.

By change of variable,

\int_{n^{2/3}g(n)+3/2}^{(n-2)/16}x^{2p-1}_{j}\;{\rm d}j\geq\frac{n}{2\pi}\int_{2n^{-1/3}g(n)\pi+(5\pi/2n)}^{\pi/8-(3\pi/4n)}x^{2p-1}_{j}\;{\rm d}x_{j}\gtrsim n

which yields, using the assumption
\varphi(\omega)\ll\tan^{p}(\omega)
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varphi(\omega(n))\|v\|^{p}_{\ell^{p}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(J)\\
2-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits\lambda<\tan(\omega(n))|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda|\end{subarray}}|\lambda-2|^{p-1/2}\gtrsim\frac{n^{2p/3}}{\varphi(\omega(n))}=\frac{1}{4^{p}(2-\sqrt{2})^{p}}\frac{\tan^{p}(\omega(n))}{\varphi(\omega(n))}\to\infty,

as
n\to\infty
. This proves (
53
). Note that the non-displayed constants depend only on
p
.
∎
