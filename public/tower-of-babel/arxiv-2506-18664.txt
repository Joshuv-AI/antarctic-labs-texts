Shaping Boundaries to Control and Transport Topological Defects in Colloidal Nematic Liquid Crystals
Gerardo Campos-Villalobos, André F. V. Matias, Ethan I. L. Jull, Lisa Tran, Marjolein Dijkstra

Abstract
Anisotropic rod-like particles form liquid crystalline phases with varying degrees of orientational and translational order. When confined geometrically, these phases can give rise to topological defects, which can be selected and controlled by tuning how the rods align near boundaries, known as anchoring. While anchoring in molecular liquid crystals can be controlled through surface functionalization, this approach is not easily applicable to microscale colloidal systems, which have so far been limited to planar anchoring. Here, using particle-based simulations, Landau-de Gennes theory, and experiments on colloidal rods, we demonstrate that topographical patterning of the boundary can effectively control the anchoring type and, in turn, the defect state in two-dimensional confined nematics. Building on this, we show that dynamically shape-shifting the boundaries can transform and transport topological defects, enabling the design of liquid crystal analogs for binary information storage.



In the 1940s, Onsager
Onsager 1949
predicted that sufficiently long rod-like particles exhibit a first-order phase transition from an isotropic phase, lacking both positional and orientational order, to a nematic phase, which exhibits long-range orientational order but no positional order. In the bulk nematic phase, the long axes of the particles spontaneously align along a common direction described by the nematic director
\mathbf{\hat{n}}
. When nematic materials are confined, such as twisted nematic display cells
Castellano 2005
, physical boundaries disrupt the uniform nematic field, inducing elastic deformations of the director and increasing the free energy. Depending on the confinement geometry and the alignment of particles at the boundaries (anchoring), this frustration in
\mathbf{\hat{n}}(\mathbf{r})
can lead to the spontaneous formation and stabilization of topological defects (TDs)
Mermin 1979
. Formally, a TD corresponds to a configuration of the nematic field that cannot be continuously transformed into a uniform state. These defects may appear as points (hedgehogs), lines (disclinations), or walls (domain boundaries)
Alexander et al. 2012
. In 2D space, TDs reduce to point-like singularities where the orientation of the nematic director becomes ill-defined
Harth and Stannarius 2020
. TDs are characterized by their topological charge
q
, which quantifies how the nematic director rotates around a closed loop encircling the defect
Kleman and Lavrentovich 2003
. This charge determines the nature and stability of the defect, with different defect types corresponding to different values of
q
. Although TDs in 2D nematics are conventionally regarded as “points” interacting via elastic forces formally analogous to the Coulomb force in electrostatics
De Gennes and Prost 1993
, recent evidence indicates that they also possess intrinsic orientational properties that strongly influence their energetics and motion
Vromans and Giomi 2016
;
Tang and Selinger 2017
. One effective way to control the type and distribution of TDs is by manipulating the anchoring conditions of the liquid crystal under confinement
Lavrentovich 2003
;
Shojaei-Zadeh and Anna 2006
. By adjusting the boundary conditions, such as
planar
anchoring, where the director aligns tangent to the surface, or
homeotropic
anchoring, where it aligns perpendicular, it is possible to dictate the symmetry, arrangement, and stability of defects. This approach provides a versatile method for tailoring defect configurations, which is particularly valuable in applications such as photonic devices, where defect manipulation can control light propagation and enhance optical focusing
Hernandez et al. 2012
. In materials design, engineered defect patterns can be leveraged to fine-tune mechanical or optical properties
Lu et al. 2013
;
Khoo and Wu 1993
. Furthermore, in active matter systems, dynamic manipulation of defects can direct the collective behavior of self-organizing particles, enabling more predictable and controllable motion
Muševič et al. 2006
.

In recent years, significant progress has been made in controlling TDs through anchoring manipulation in molecular liquid crystals
Shin et al. 2018
. In these systems, simply altering the surface chemistry and composition allows for a fine control of anchoring conditions
Uline et al. 2010
;
Bryan-Brown et al. 1999
;
Patel and Yokoyama 1993
;
Drawhorn and Abbott 1995
. However, much less has been achieved for larger-sized colloidal particles, where due to entropic arguments, planar anchoring is generally favored in the vicinity of flat and structured walls
van Roij et al. 2000
;
Harnau et al. 2004
;
Dijkstra et al. 2001
;
Lewis et al. 2014
. Recently, compelling evidence has been put forward that purely geometric control of boundaries in confined colloidal liquid crystals can be leveraged to drive the anchoring state and, consequently, the resulting defect structures. Specifically, in 2D smectics of colloidal hard rods confined within elliptical boundaries, the local radius of curvature,
R_{\text{curv}}
, determines the anchoring type. A robust transition from homeotropic to planar anchoring occurs at a
critical
value of curvature that is approximately twice the length of the colloidal particles
Jull et al. 2024
. While these findings demonstrate how boundary curvature controls the alignment of anisotropic colloidal particles near curved regions, the influence of overall confinement anisotropy remains unclear, as does the applicability of these principles to geometries beyond simple elliptical shapes.

Inspired by surface functionalization in molecular liquid crystals, where anchoring is controlled by both energetic and entropic effects, we speculate that the anchoring of colloidal hard rods can be controlled purely by geometric manipulations of the boundary, driven solely by entropy. In this Letter, using Monte Carlo (MC) simulations, Landau-de Gennes (LdG) theory, and experiments, we demonstrate that homeotropic anchoring of hard colloidal rods can indeed be achieved by
patterning
the confining boundaries. We start by showing that a patterned boundary induces homeotropic anchoring of colloidal hard rods both experimentally and numerically. We then prove analytically that the anchoring transition occurs due to geometrical constraints. Next, we show how different anchoring conditions impact the defect structure within circular confinements. Finally, we manipulate nematics under circular confinement with multiple anchoring conditions and apply this to create analog devices that store and process binary information.

We now turn to circular confinement to explore how anchoring alone can be leveraged to manipulate TDs. We consider circular cavities with radii ranging from
R=2.5L
to
10.5L
. To facilitate TD identification we consider longer rods,
L/D=25
. We examine two boundary conditions, purely planar anchoring and a patterned boundary with
a/D=10
and
p/D=14
, which yield homeotropic anchoring. In Fig.

Finally, we explore the active control of nematic defect structures, demonstrating that they can be reversibly reconfigured by modifying the boundary conditions. This capability opens new possibilities for leveraging soft-matter systems for novel computing paradigms
Kos and Dunkel 2022
. We focus on circular confinement where anchoring can be selectively patterned in
60\degree
slices, Fig.
4

60\degree

4

+1/2
defect emerges opposite to the homeotropic boundary. The remaining TD is now distributed along the boundary discontinuities, causing frustration in the nematic order. Each boundary discontinuity contributes a winding number of
+1/4
, ensuring topological charge conservation
Yao et al. 2022

60\degree
slices alternate between planar and homeotropic anchoring, shown in Fig.
4
(b,d), six boundary discontinuities are present, totaling a winding number of
+3/2
. Thus, to ensure charge conservation, a
-1/2
defect emerges in the center of the circle. By implementing such anchoring patterns, we can precisely control defect configurations in nematics confined within multiple slightly overlapping circular cavities. This enables a form of defect-based information storage, where the presence of a
-1/2
defect encodes a bit
1

0

R=5L

-1/2

111

4

4

-1/2

We emphasize that the spacing between circle centers is crucial: at smaller distances, TDs are not fully isolated, while at larger distances, the system behaves as multiple independent units. While we illustrate this concept using three overlapping circles, the approach can be readily extended to an arbitrary number of circles, each encoding a distinct topological state (Supplementary Fig. S3).

So far, we have focused on equilibrium states, but minimizing the LdG free energy reveals an intrinsic free-energy path that connects different defect locations. For example, a defect can transition from the circle center, Fig.
4

4

In conclusion, we have demonstrated, by simulations, LdG theory, and experiments, that the anchoring type, and consequently the defect state, of confined colloidal hard rods can be precisely controlled by employing topographically patterned, undulated boundaries. We have mapped out the geometric parameters of patterned boundaries that govern the entropy-driven transition between planar and homeotropic anchoring. Interestingly, we identified an intermediate bistable region in which rod-like particles exhibit no clear preference for either anchoring type. Our analysis, based on intuitive geometric and entropic considerations, reveals that anchoring strength depends not only on these boundary parameters but also on particle dimensions such as rod length and diameter. Furthermore, we have shown using MC simulations and LdG theory that shape-shifting boundaries facilitate dynamic transformations between TDs, allowing precise control over their positions. We find that the creation and motion of TDs is facilitated under conditions of low chemical potential and/or high elastic constants. We hope this work motivates experimental realizations of shape-shifting boundaries, potentially through the use of liquid crystal elastomers
Warner and Terentjev 2003
, thereby opening new avenues for defect transport and information storage.
