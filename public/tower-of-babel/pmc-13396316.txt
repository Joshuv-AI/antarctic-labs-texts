A network toxicology and molecular docking study predicting putative molecular targets and pathways linking bisphenol a exposure to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma
Authors: Yue Zhang, Li Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bisphenol A (BPA) is a pervasive environmental endocrine disruptor implicated in hormone-related cancers.However, its association with non-hormone-dependent malignancies such as Diffuse Large B-cell Lymphoma (DLBCL) remains poorly understood.This study aimed to predict the potential molecular mechanisms linking BPA exposure to DLBCL pathogenesis through an integrated approach combining network toxicology and molecular docking.We systematically identified 46 overlapping genes shared between BPA-associated targets and DLBCL-related genes. Protein–protein interaction network analysis indicate six hub genes(HSP90AB1, HSPA8, CCNA2, CDK1, LDHA, and HSPA14),with HSP90AB1 exhibiting the highest topological centrality.Functional enrichment analysis demonstrated that these genes are significantly enriched in key oncogenic signaling pathways, including the PI3K-Akt and MAPK pathways, as well as critical biological processes such as protein folding and cell cycle regulation. Molecular docking simulations further predicted the stable binding affinity between BPA and each of the six hub proteins, with binding energies below − 6.0 kcal/mol. Collectively, our computational findings suggest a putative mechanistic framework in which BPA may contribute to DLBCL development by directly interacting with and potentially dysregulating central regulatory nodes in cellular networks, particularly the molecular chaperone HSP90AB1. These predictions require experimental validation but provide a basis for future mechanistic studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05274-9.

Introduction

Diffuse Large B-cell Lymphoma (DLBCL), the most prevalent and aggressive subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma in adults [1], has achieved notable clinical efficacy with R-CHOP-based immunochemotherapy. However, approximately 30%–40% of patients still experience treatment failure or disease recurrence, reflecting the profound biological heterogeneity inherent in DLBCL and underscoring the urgent need to elucidate its underlying mechanisms of drug resistance [2].

The heterogeneity of DLBCL is co-regulated by multiple factors, including cellular origin, gene mutation profiles, and the tumor microenvironment [3–5]. In recent years, beyond traditional risk factors such as viral infections and autoimmune diseases, growing research has focused on the potential role of environmental exposures in DLBCL initiation and progression [6]. For example, long-term occupational pesticide exposure has been identified as an independent risk factor for reduced complete remission rates and poor survival outcomes in DLBCL patients receiving first-line immunochemotherapy [7]. Collectively, these findings suggest that certain environmental pollutants may modulate DLBCL initiation, progression, and treatment responses by inducing genomic instability, interfering with key signaling pathways, or regulating immune responses.

Among various environmental chemicals, Bisphenol A (BPA) has garnered extensive attention due to its widespread and persistent human exposure, well-documented endocrine-disrupting activity, and potential carcinogenicity. As a common additive in daily consumer products, BPA can enter the human body via multiple routes (such as diet, inhalation, dermal contact) and accumulate chronically [8–10]. Previous studies on BPA’s carcinogenic risk have primarily focused on its estrogenic properties—binding to and activating estrogen receptors to drive the development of hormone-dependent tumors such as breast and prostate cancer [11, 12]. However, accumulating evidence indicates that BPA’s biological effects extend beyond estrogenic activity. For instance, BPA exposure has been shown to disrupt T and B lymphocyte functions and impair the homeostasis of the bone marrow hematopoietic microenvironment [13]. Thus, as an environmental pollutant with multi-target interference capabilities, BPA’s health hazards may not be restricted to hormone-sensitive tissues but could affect multiple systems, including the immune system. Given that DLBCL is a B cell-derived malignancy closely linked to immune dysregulation and chronic inflammation, the potential association between BPA and DLBCL remains an unaddressed research gap.

Therefore, this study employs network toxicology and molecular docking strategies to predict core targets and pathways that may underlie BPA-DLBCL interactions. By integrating these computational approaches, we generate a testable hypothesis that BPA could potentially contribute to DLBCL initiation and progression, providing a theoretical basis for future experimental validation.

Methods

Preliminary analysis of BPA toxicity

We obtained the three-dimensional structure and SMILES notation of BPA from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), a public repository of chemical information. To preliminarily assess the toxicological profile of BPA, we used two web-based platforms: ProTox-3.0 (https://tox.charite.de/protox3), which predicts acute toxicity and organ-specific hazards based on molecular structure, and SwissADME (http://www.swissadme.ch/), which estimates pharmacokinetic properties such as absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion [14, 15].

Screening BPA target

Using the SMILES notation of BPA retrieved from PubChem, we searched for known and predicted protein targets of BPA in three complementary databases: ChEMBL (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/), a database of bioactive drug-like compounds and their targets; CTD(http://ctdbase.org), which curates chemical–gene–disease interactions; and SwissADME(http://swissadme.ch), which also provides target prediction based on molecular similarity. The results from these three databases were combined to obtain a comprehensive set of BPA-associated targets.The screening criteria are as follows: (1) Only keep the targets annotated as Homo sapiens. (2) Remove duplicate entries of the same gene from different databases. (3) For CTD, only retain interactions with an inferred score of ≥ 0.5 (moderate to strong evidence); for ChEMBL, only keep targets with a confidence score of ≥ 6 (on a scale of 1 to 9, indicating direct binding evidence); for SwissADME, only include prediction results with a probability of ≥ 0.6.Finally, the target genes obtained from the three databases were merged through a Venn diagram.

Screening targets of DLBCL

To obtain DLBCL-associated genes, we searched the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/), a comprehensive compendium of human gene information that integrates data from over 150 sources. The search term “Diffuse Large B-cell Lymphoma” was used to retrieve genes with documented relevance to DLBCL. Additionally, we downloaded the gene expression dataset GSE10846 from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), which contains 22 DLBCL samples and 7 normal control samples.To mitigate the potential impact of this imbalance on the differential expression analysis, we employed the limma package with empirical Bayes moderation, a scheme specifically designed for small-sample microarray studies, which enhances statistical power by borrowing information across genes. Additionally, we applied strict filtering criteria (adjusted P value < 0.05, |log2FoldChange| > 1) to reduce false positives [16].

Construction of the protein-protein network and screening of hub targets

The overlapping genes among the BPA target set, GeneCards-derived DLBCL genes, and GSE10846 differential expression genes were identified using a Venn diagram, yielding 46 common genes. To explore the functional interactions among these 46 proteins, we constructed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network using the STRING database (https://cn.string db.org/), which aggregates known and predicted physical and functional associations from multiple sources. Only interactions with a combined confidence score > 0.7 (medium confidence) were retained.

The PPI network was visualized and analyzed using Cytoscape v3.10.4. To quantitatively assess node importance, we employed the NetworkAnalyzer tool (built into Cytoscape) to calculate three topological parameters: Degree (the number of direct connections), Betweenness Centrality (measuring how often a node lies on the shortest paths between other nodes), and Closeness Centrality (measuring how quickly a node can reach all others). These three metrics were selected because they capture complementary aspects of node importance: Degree identifies locally connected hubs (“party hubs”), Betweenness identifies globally bridging nodes (“bottlenecks”) that are critical for network communication, and Closeness identifies nodes that can efficiently disseminate information across the network. The combined use of multiple centrality metrics avoids the limitations of any single measure and is widely adopted in PPI-based hub gene identification studies to improve robustness.

These metrics are deterministic graph-theoretic calculations based on the network topology; no inferential statistics (P-values) were applied. For each parameter, we ranked all 46 nodes and selected the top 15 genes with the highest values. This range ensures sufficient candidate genes for downstream biological interpretation while excluding nodes with low topological importance. The intersection of these three top-15 sets was then determined using a Venn diagram to obtain the final set of hub genes. Taking the intersection of multiple centrality metrics ensures that the identified hub genes are consistently ranked high across different topological dimensions, thereby improving the robustness of hub gene selection and reducing false positives that may arise from relying on a single metric.

Gene function and pathway enrichment

To elucidate the potential biological functions and signaling pathways associated with the 46 common genes, we performed functional enrichment analyses using R. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis categorizes genes into three domains: Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC), and Molecular Function (MF), thereby providing a structured description of gene function. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment identifies signaling pathways that are significantly overrepresented among the input genes, helping to infer the biological processes in which these genes may be involved. Enrichment results were considered significant at an adjusted P-value < 0.05.

Molecular docking interaction for hub targets and DLBCL

To computationally predict whether BPA can physically bind to the identified hub proteins, we performed molecular docking simulations. The three-dimensional structure of BPA was downloaded from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The crystal structures of the six hub proteins (HSP90AB1, HSPA8, CCNA2, CDK1, LDHA, and HSPA14) were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB; https://www.rcsb.org/). Protein structures were prepared using PyMOL software by removing water molecules and original ligands.

Determination of the docking pocket

The binding pocket for each protein was defined using the GetBox-PyMOL-Plugin. Since all six hub proteins have experimentally resolved structures with co-crystallized ligands available in the PDB, we employed the ligand-guided mode (getbox). For each protein, the co-crystallized ligand was selected, and the box center was automatically positioned at its geometric center. The box dimensions were set by extending 6.5 Å in all three dimensions (x, y, z), which is appropriate for a small molecule like BPA (molecular weight ~ 228 Da). This extension radius ensures sufficient conformational space for BPA while maintaining computational efficiency. For completeness, the pocket was visually inspected to confirm that it corresponded to the known active or ligand-binding site.

Molecular docking protocol

Prepared protein and ligand files were converted to the required PDBQT format using AutoDock Tools v1.5.6, with Gasteiger charges assigned and non-polar hydrogens merged. Molecular docking was executed with AutoDock Vina 1.1.2, using an exhaustiveness parameter of 8 and a maximum of 10 binding poses per docking run (num_modes = 10). The energy_range parameter was set to the default value of 2 kcal/mol, which retains all poses within 2 kcal/mol of the best scoring conformation. The binding affinity (binding energy, ΔG, in kcal/mol) between BPA and each protein was calculated by AutoDock Vina’s scoring function, which integrates van der Waals, hydrogen bonding, electrostatic, and desolvation terms. More negative binding energies indicate stronger and more favorable spontaneous binding; a threshold of ΔG ≤ -5.0 kcal/mol was considered indicative of stable binding based on established criteria in molecular docking studies. For each protein, the docking pose with the most negative binding energy (i.e., the best model) was selected for subsequent interaction analysis.

Analysis of ligand-protein interactions

After docking, the lowest-energy binding pose (with the most negative ΔG) for each hub protein was selected for detailed interaction analysis using AutoDock Tools v1.5.6 and PyMOL 2.6.0. The following interaction types were identified according to standard geometric criteria:

Hydrogen bonds: identified when the distance between a hydrogen bond donor (e.g., –OH, –NH) and an acceptor (e.g., carbonyl oxygen, hydroxyl oxygen) was ≤ 3.5 Å, and the donor-hydrogen-acceptor angle was > 120°.

Hydrophobic interactions: identified based on the spatial proximity (≤ 4.0 Å) between BPA’s aromatic rings and hydrophobic amino acid residues (Leu, Val, Ile, Met, Phe, Tyr, Trp, Ala, Pro).

π-π stacking interactions: identified when the distance between the centroids of BPA’s aromatic rings and the aromatic rings of Phe, Tyr, Trp, or His residues was ≤ 5.5 Å, with an angle between the ring planes ≤ 30°.For each interaction, the specific amino acid residue (with its three-letter code and position number) and the corresponding bond distance (in Å) were recorded.

Results

Preliminary assessment of BPA toxicity

By integrating the results from ProTox-3.0 and SwissADME databases, we conducted a preliminary analysis of the toxicity profile of BPA. BPA has the molecular formula C15H16O2 and a molecular weight of 228.29. It possesses a certain degree of polarity and liposolubility, which facilitates its dissolution, penetration, and permeation through cell membranes in the body. Additionally, it has a high GI absorption rate, indicating that BPA is absorbed extensively in the gastrointestinal tract and has significant affinity for the blood-brain barrier. The toxicity assessment classifies it as a Class V toxicant.This grade indicates relatively low toxicity in standard acute toxicity tests, however, the potential risks of long-term and low-dose exposure still cannot be ignored(Fig. 1).

Identification of potential BPA targets in DLBCL

We integrated the target information of bisphenol A (BPA) from the ChEMBL, CTD and SwissADME databases, obtaining 26,564 original merged outputs. After completing species screening (retaining only human genes), duplicate removal, and confidence-based screening, we merged the target genes from the three databases through Venn diagrams and ultimately identified 739 core bisphenol A (BPA) action targets (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Table 1). Subsequently, we obtained 22 DLBCL samples and 7 control samples from the GEO database (GSE10846), and through differential analysis, we identified 1,530 DLBCL differential genes (Fig. 2B). Meanwhile, we obtained 7,164 related genes from GeneCard. Finally, we took the intersection of the 7,164 related genes from GeneCard, the 1,530 differentially expressed genes from GSE10846, and the 739 core BPA targets, obtaining 46 overlapping genes, which are the potential key genes affected by BPA in DLBCL (Fig. 2C and Supplement Table 2).

Construction of PPI network and identification of hub genes connection

Through the String database, we constructed a protein interaction network containing 46 cross-genes, with a minimum interaction score threshold of “medium confidence > 0.7” applied (Fig. 3A). We imported the results into Cytoscape and focused on analyzing parameters such as Betweenness Centrality, Closeness Centrality, and Degree of the cross-genes. By ranking each node, we selected the top 15 genes for analysis. Finally, we took the intersection of the genes in the three parameters and obtained 6 hub genes(HSP90AB1, HSPA8, CCNA2, CDK1, LDHA, and HSPA14). Among the three parameters, we found that HSP90AB1 had high Betweenness Centrality, Closeness Centrality, and Degree, indicating its significant role in mediating the impact of BPA on DLBCL (Fig. 3B-E).

Molecular docking for BPA and core target proteins of DLBCL

To predict the binding potential between BPA and the six hub proteins (HSP90AB1, HSPA8, CCNA2, CDK1, LDHA, and HSPA14), we performed molecular docking simulations.For each hub protein, AutoDock Vina generated 10 distinct binding poses (the default num_modes setting). The binding energies of these poses ranged from − 8.8 to -5.4 kcal/mol across all proteins, with the most negative binding energy selected as the best docking model for each protein (Supplementary Table 3). Below we describe the detailed interaction modes for the best model of each protein. The binding energies (ΔG) for BPA with each hub protein are summarized in Supplement Table 4 and visualized in Fig. 5. All six hub proteins exhibited negative binding energies ranging from − 6.1 to -8.8 kcal/mol, which are below the conventional threshold of -5.0 kcal/mol for stable binding. Below we describe the detailed interaction modes for each protein based on the docking poses.

CDK1 (PDB ID: 4Y72, ΔG = -8.8 kcal/mol) showed the strongest binding affinity among all hub proteins. BPA occupied the ATP-binding cleft of CDK1, stabilized by 4 hydrogen bonds and 5 hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 5A, Supplement Table 4). Hydrogen bonds analysis revealed that BPA (O3 atom) donated to the side chain oxygen of TYR223B (distance H-A: 2.95 Å, angle: 151.9°). BPA (O3) donated to the backbone oxygen of ARG298B (2.29 Å, 104.8°). SER227B (side chain O3) donated to BPA (O3) (3.38 Å, 127.7°). LEU300B (backbone NH) donated to BPA (O3) (2.88 Å, 125.5°). Hydrophobic interactions were observed with TYR223B (3.55 Å), PRO301B (3.55 Å), PRO340B (3.55 Å), and ILE343B (3.99 and 3.70 Å). The extensive hydrogen bond network and hydrophobic packing explain the exceptionally low binding energy.

CCNA2 (PDB ID: 4EOJ, ΔG = -7.7 kcal/mol) bound to the cyclin box domain with 4 hydrogen bonds and 9 hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 5B, Supplement Table 4). BPA (O3) donated to the backbone oxygen of CYS193B (2.50 Å, 175.8°). ARG211B (side chain Ng+) donated to BPA (O3) (3.25 Å, 119.2°). ASN237B (side chain Nam) donated to BPA (O3) (3.48 Å, 111.0°). BPA (O3) donated to the side chain oxygen of SER340B (2.24 Å, 119.9°). Hydrophobic contacts included VAL236B (3.66 Å), ASN237B (3.82 Å), VAL311B (3.31 Å), LEU341B (3.75 Å), TYR347B (3.91 Å), LEU348B (3.59 Å), PRO352B (3.30 and 3.76 Å), and ILE355B (3.70 Å).

HSPA8 (PDB ID: 4H5V, ΔG = -7.4 kcal/mol) docked into the substrate-binding domain via 1 hydrogen bond and 7 hydrophobic contacts (Fig. 5C, Supplement Table 4). A weak hydrogen bond formed between BPA (O3) and the side chain of SER275A (3.74 Å, 101.6°). Hydrophobic interactions involved TYR15A (four contacts: 3.59, 3.79, 3.63, 3.62 Å), THR37A (3.60 Å), ARG272A (3.96 Å), and ASP366A (3.62 Å).

LDHA (PDB ID: 5W8J, ΔG = -6.7 kcal/mol) bound to the substrate-binding pocket with 7 hydrophobic interactions and no classical hydrogen bonds (Fig. 5D, Supplement Table 4). Key hydrophobic residues included VAL52A (three contacts: 3.73, 3.49, 3.74 Å), ALA95A (3.83 Å), ILE115A (3.60 Å), PHE118A (3.58 Å), and ILE119A (3.96 Å).

HSP90AB1 (PDB ID: 8QMO, ΔG = -6.2 kcal/mol) bound to the ATP-binding domain with 5 hydrogen bonds and 4 hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 5E, Supplement Table 4). BPA accepted hydrogen bonds from GLN23B (2.73 Å, 110.6°), SER26B (2.86 Å, 101.5°), and LYS111A (2.53 Å, 144.1°), and donated hydrogen bonds to SER26B (2.27 Å, 147.8°) and the backbone oxygen of LEU386A (2.35 Å, 125.5°). Hydrophobic contacts were formed with LEU27B (3.80 Å), ASN30B (3.64 Å), PHE129A (3.62 Å), and PRO387A (3.70 Å). The proximity of PHE129A (3.62 Å) also suggests a potential π-π stacking interaction.

HSPA14 (UniProt ID: Q0VDF9, ΔG = -6.1 kcal/mol), a homology model, interacted with BPA primarily through 5 hydrophobic contacts without any classical hydrogen bonds (Fig. 5F, Supplement Table 4). Hydrophobic interactions involved THR11A (3.25 Å), ALA229A (3.73 Å), THR232A (3.87 Å), GLU233A (3.82 Å), and THR262A (3.54 Å).

The SwissADME analysis (Fig. 1B) predicted that BPA has high gastrointestinal absorption and good membrane permeability (log Po/w = 2.5). These properties suggest that BPA can reach systemic circulation and potentially accumulate in lymphoid tissues. The favorable binding energies (all ≤ -6.0 kcal/mol) and the presence of multiple stabilizing interactions (hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts) across all six hub proteins indicate that BPA is thermodynamically capable of forming stable complexes with these targets if sufficient tissue concentrations are achieved. However, it should be noted that these are in silico predictions, and the actual binding affinity and functional consequences require experimental validation.

Discussion

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common and aggressive subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma, accounting for approximately 40% of cases worldwide [1]. Despite advances in immunochemotherapy, a substantial proportion of patients develop relapsed or refractory disease, highlighting the profound clinical and biological heterogeneity of this malignancy [17]. The etiology of DLBCL remains incompletely understood, with known risk factors including immune dysfunction, specific infections, and genetic predispositions. Notably, epidemiological patterns—such as the consistently higher incidence in males and rising trends among certain ethnic populations suggest that non-genetic, environmental factors may contribute to disease pathogenesis [18]. Among these, exposure to environmental pollutants has emerged as a plausible risk modifier.This context underscores an urgent need to identify specific environmental chemicals and elucidate their molecular mechanisms in lymphomagenesis, which could inform both preventive strategies and a deeper understanding of disease heterogeneity.

Bisphenol A (BPA), a high-production-volume chemical widely present in everyday environments, is a well-characterized endocrine disruptor [9]. Beyond its classical estrogenic activity in hormone-dependent cancers such as breast and prostate cancer, accumulating evidence implicates BPA in the pathogenesis and progression of diverse malignancies through receptor-independent mechanisms. Notably, its potential impact on the immune system and hematologic malignancies is increasingly recognized. A study demonstrated that BPA exposure promotes lymphoma progression in human lymphoblastoid cells by inducing DNA damage and cell cycle disruption through aberrant activation of the CTNNB1 signaling pathway [19]. Furthermore, studies in acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) cells have shown that nanomolar concentrations of BPA reduce the cytotoxic efficacy of vincristine potentially via activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway [20]. Additional evidence indicates that BPA induces chemoresistance in both estrogen receptor-positive and -negative breast cancer cells [21]. Collectively, these findings position BPA not merely as an endocrine disruptor, but as a multi-modal oncogenic agent capable of compromising genomic integrity, disrupting programmed cell death, and promoting therapy resistance. It is a key hallmarks of aggressive cancers such as DLBCL.

Our network toxicology approach identified six hub genes (HSP90AB1, HSPA8, CCNA2, CDK1, LDHA, and HSPA14) at the intersection of BPA exposure and DLBCL biology, which can be broadly categorized into molecular chaperones (HSP90AB1, HSPA8, HSPA14) and cell cycle regulators (CCNA2, CDK1, LDHA). The biological relevance of these predictions is supported by existing evidence. For instance, CDK1 has been identified as an independent predictor of poor prognosis in DLBCL, with elevated protein levels observed in DLBCL samples compared to normal controls [22]. Similarly, LDHA plays an oncogenic role in DLBCL and is considered a pivotal prognostic biomarker and a potential therapeutic target [23]. While direct functional studies for HSPA8 and HSPA14 in DLBCL are less extensive, their expression is documented in DLBCL datasets, and as members of the HSP70 family, they are broadly implicated in protein homeostasis and cellular stress responses. CCNA2, a key regulator of the G1/S and G2/M cell cycle transitions, has also been linked to DLBCL pathogenesis [24].

Notably, HSP90AB1 is of particular biological interest. The heat shock protein 90β encoded by HSP90AB1 is a major molecular chaperone within cells. It stabilizes multiple oncogenic proteins and drives malignant signaling networks [25–27]. Studies have shown that in aggressive activated B-cell-like (ABC) DLBCL, HSP90 is crucial for maintaining the activity of the B-cell receptor (BCR) signaling pathway. It directly promotes the phosphorylation of key signaling molecules such as SYK and BTK, thereby activating downstream pro-survival signals such as NF-κB. Inhibiting HSP90 can effectively block this pathway and produce a synergistic killing effect with targeted drugs such as ibrutinib [28]. Furthermore, HSP90 is a key stabilizing factor for the core oncogenic transcription factor BCL6 in DLBCL. Studies have shown that the HSP90 inhibitor PU-H71 can directly lead to the degradation of BCL6 protein, thereby specifically inducing apoptosis in BCL6-dependent DLBCL cells and significantly inhibiting tumor growth in animal models [29].

It is worth noting that our molecular docking results predict that BPA capable of stably binding to the above-mentioned six hub proteins under the simulated conditions. This suggests that BPA may function as a multi-modal environmental risk factor, simultaneously interfering with the function of multiple key regulators. For HSP90AB1, the predicted stable complex formation with BPA may interfere with its chaperone function on key oncogenic clients (such as BCL6 and BCR signalosome components), potentially disrupting the signaling network and transcriptional program of DLBCL cells. Similarly, the predicted interactions with CDK1 and CCNA2 could directly impact cell cycle progression, while binding to LDHA might affect the metabolic reprogramming crucial for lymphoma cell survival. Collectively, these findings paint a picture of BPA as a potential “network disruptor,” capable of engaging multiple interconnected oncogenic nodes in DLBCL, a hypothesis that warrants further experimental investigation.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of our core genes revealed significant involvement in several cancer-critical pathways, most notably the PI3K-Akt and MAPK signaling pathways. This finding offers a crucial mechanistic bridge between BPA exposure and DLBCL pathogenesis.

The PI3K-Akt pathway is constitutively activated in a substantial subset of DLBCL and plays distinct roles depending on the cell-of-origin subtype [30]. In germinal center B-cell-like (GCB) DLBCL, loss of the tumor suppressor PTEN detectable in approximately 55% of cases leads to PIP3 accumulation and persistent activation of the PI3K-Akt-mTOR axis, promoting cell survival, proliferation, and MYC-driven transcription. Pharmacologic inhibition of PI3K has been shown to selectively induce cytotoxicity in PTEN-deficient GCB DLBCL cells, highlighting a potential therapeutic vulnerability in this subtype. Conversely, in activated B-cell-like (ABC) DLBCL, the PI3K-Akt axis is often upregulated as a compensatory mechanism in response to targeted therapy. For instance, ibrutinib-resistant ABC-DLBCL cells exhibit enhanced PI3K-Akt signaling that contributes to their survival, and miR-155-mediated downregulation of DEPTOR has been shown to activate PI3K-Akt signaling specifically in ABC-type DLBCLs [31, 32]. Notably, the KEGG enrichment of PI3K-Akt signaling in our study aligns with the identification of CCNA2 and CDK1 as hub genes, both are downstream effectors of PI3K-Akt that regulate G1/S and G2/M cell cycle transitions, suggesting that BPA-mediated dysregulation of this pathway could directly impact cell cycle progression in DLBCL.

The MAPK pathway is also critically involved in DLBCL pathogenesis, with distinct contributions across COO subtypes. In ABC DLBCL, tumor progression locus 2 (TPL2), encoded by MAP3K8, is activated downstream of the TLR/MYD88 pathway and drives both MAPK signaling and NF-κB activation [33]. TPL2 promotes cell proliferation and survival in TLR4/ MYD88-activated B-cells, the putative cell-of-origin of ABC DLBCL. In GCB DLBCL, MAPK signaling has been implicated in germinal center B-cell selection and differentiation through B-cell receptor and CXCR4 signaling. Furthermore, dysregulated MAPK1 activation has been shown to contribute to rituximab resistance in DLBCL by enhancing the anti-apoptotic capacity of tumor cells [34]. Our enrichment of the MAPK pathway, combined with the identification of HSP90AB1 as a hub gene, is mechanistically relevant because HSP90 has been shown to stabilize multiple kinases within the MAPK cascade (e.g., RAF, MEK, ERK). BPA-mediated interference with HSP90AB1 function could therefore destabilize MAPK signaling components, disrupting both proliferative and survival signals in DLBCL cells.

Importantly, the PI3K-Akt and MAPK pathways do not operate in isolation but exhibit extensive crosstalk. Upon B-cell receptor (BCR) activation, both pathways are engaged through a cascade of phosphorylation events, and crosstalk between PI3K-Akt and MAPK-ERK pathways has been implicated in driving drug resistance. Our finding that BPA is predicted to bind simultaneously to HSP90AB1 which stabilizes MAPK components, CDK1/CCNA2 downstream of PI3K-Akt, and LDHA involved in metabolic reprogramming supports a multi-node disruption model. We propose that BPA may function as a network disruptor, simultaneously engaging multiple interconnected hubs and pathways, such as stress response via HSP90, survival via PI3K-Akt, proliferation via MAPK and cell cycle genes, and metabolism via LDHA to orchestrate a coordinated pro-tumorigenic program in susceptible B-cells. This multi-target model provides a plausible mechanistic explanation for how a non-hormone-dependent cancer like DLBCL could be vulnerable to an environmental pollutant like BPA. These hypotheses require experimental validation in DLBCL cell lines and animal models.

To assess whether our hub gene identification was sensitive to the STRING interaction confidence threshold, we repeated the PPI network construction and hub selection using thresholds of 0.4 (low confidence) and 0.9 (high confidence), while keeping the top 15 cutoff constant. At threshold 0.4, the network contained 37 nodes and 172 edges, and the six hub genes were HSP90AB1, HSPA8, RAD51, CCNA2, LDHA, and HSPA14. At the original threshold 0.7 (37 nodes, 86 edges), the hub genes were HSP90AB1, HSPA8, CCNA2, CDK1, LDHA, and HSPA14. At the strictest threshold 0.9 (19 nodes, 46 edges), the hub set expanded to eight genes: HSPA8, CDK1, CCNA2, TPX2, ALDOA, LDHA, EEF1A1, and HSPA4L, while HSP90AB1 and HSPA14 dropped out. Notably, CDK1, CCNA2, and LDHA consistently appeared across all three thresholds, indicating they are the most robustly identified hubs(Supplementary Table 5). Overall, the core finding that BPA may influence DLBCL through cell cycle and metabolic regulators remains robust to parameter choices.

The principal strength of this study is its integrative, multi-database computational strategy, which provides a systematic and unbiased screening of potential BPA-DLBCL interactions. However, several critical limitations must be emphasized.First, the integration of heterogeneous databases (ChEMBL, CTD, SwissADME, GeneCards, and GEO) may introduce bias due to differences in curation standards and evidence levels. ChEMBL primarily relies on manually curated bioactivity data from peer-reviewed literature, CTD curates chemical–gene–disease interactions through expert review, while SwissADME provides computationally predicted targets based on molecular similarity. To mitigate this bias, we employed a conservative intersection strategy: only genes present in all three BPA target databases were retained as core BPA targets, prioritizing consensus across independent sources. Furthermore, the subsequent intersections with DLBCL-related gene sets (GeneCards and GEO) and PPI-based hub gene filtering provided additional biological validation layers. However, when interpreting the results, the inherent limitations of database integration should be taken into account, such as the potential overrepresentation of well-studied genes and the underrepresentation of genes with less characterized features.Second, all results are derived from in silico predictions without any in vitro, in vivo, or clinical validation. Therefore, the identified hub genes and pathways should be viewed as hypothetical targets rather than confirmed mediators.Thirdly, molecular docking simulations predict binding affinities and interaction modes based on scoring functions, but they cannot prove functional consequences (such as agonistic effects, antagonistic effects, or regulation of chaperone activity). The reported binding energies (≤ -6.0 kcal/mol) suggest the thermodynamic favorability of complex formation under idealized computer simulation conditions, but they do not take into account the cellular environment, endogenous competing ligands, or post-translational modifications. Therefore, the predictions we obtain are hypothetical binding potentials rather than evidence of biological activity.In the future, specific experiments need to be carried out to verify our prediction results.Fourth, the differential expression analysis relied on the GSE10846 dataset, which contained only 7 normal control samples, while there were 22 samples of DLBCL. Although we used the limma package with empirical Bayes moderation, which performs well with small sample sizes, and we set strict corrected P-value thresholds (< 0.05) and fold change cutoffs (|log2FC| > 1), this imbalance in sample size may reduce statistical power and potentially increase the risk of false negatives or false positives. We plan to validate our findings in larger, more balanced cohorts in the future.

Despite this, our research still indicates the significant influence of BPA in DLBCL and screens out six hub genes that stably bind with BPA. In future studies, we will further explore the specific mechanism of BPA in DLBCL in depth, providing certain theoretical basis for the prevention and treatment of DLBCL.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this network toxicology investigation predicts a putative link between BPA exposure and specific molecular networks operative in DLBCL, generating a testable hypothesis. We propose that BPA could potentially promote lymphomagenesis not only through indirect immune modulation but also via direct interference with critical cellular machinery, exemplified by its predicted interaction with the master chaperone HSP90AB1 and key cell cycle regulators. These in silico findings suggest BPA as a candidate multi-modal risk factor for DLBCL, with HSP90AB1 and associated pathways representing potential focal points for future mechanistic research into environmental lymphomagenesis and the development of targeted intervention strategies. However, experimental validation in DLBCL cell lines, animal models, and population-based studies is required to confirm these predictions.
