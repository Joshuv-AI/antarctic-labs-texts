Decoding Blaschkoid Pattern Disorders: Genetic Insights from Incontinentia Pigmenti and a Phenotypic Mimic – A Case Series
Authors: Sushma Muralidharan, Susmitha Divya Kethireddi, Guruprasad Patnala, Sudhir Babu Karri
Published: 2026

Introduction

Incontinentia pigmenti (IP) is a rare X-linked dominant disorder that affects primarily females, as it is lethal in males. It is caused by mutations in the IKBKG (NEMO) gene located on chromosome Xq28, which is needed for nuclear factor (NF)-κB signalling [1]. The condition presents with skin changes that has four stages: vesicular, verrucous, hyperpigmented, and atrophic following Blaschko’s lines. The disorder also presents with extracutaneous manifestations, including dental, ocular, and neurological abnormalities [2].

IP is primarily diagnosed based on clinical and histopathological criteria, with genetic confirmation serving as a gold standard. However, phenotypic mimics, particularly in male infants, pose diagnostic challenges. This case series highlights the significance of genetic analysis while illustrating the limitations of relying solely on molecular testing.

IP follows a well-defined sequence of cutaneous changes, often diagnosed based on clinical findings and histopathology. The revised Landy and Donnai diagnostic criteria (2014) emphasize the role of genetic confirmation, particularly in ambiguous cases [3].

Case Presentation

In this case series, all four children presented with vesiculobullous lesions in a striking Blaschkoid pattern at birth and later developed hyperpigmented plaques. On dermoscopic examination, the vesicular stage showed vesicles set against an erythematous background, and the pigmented lesions revealed a characteristic patchy distribution of brownish reticular, curved, linear, and branching lines, interspersed with clods and dots, findings strongly suggestive of IP. Histopathology of all cases showed remarkable consistency, with intraepidermal vesicles filled with eosinophils, eosinophilic spongiosis, and dyskeratotic keratinocytes. Genetic analysis targeting the IKBKG (NEMO) gene on chromosome Xq28 was performed in three of the four children. Two female patients tested positive for the pathogenic mutation. In one female child, genetic testing could not be performed due to financial constraints. The neurological involvement in the child, despite the absence of genetic confirmation, aligns with known associations between IP and seizures. Interestingly, the only male child in this series fulfilled the clinical, No new lesions No new lesions dermoscopic, and histopathological criteria for incontinentia pigmenti yet tested negative for the IKBKG gene mutation. Male cases of IP are exceedingly rare, often attributed to somatic mosaicism or hypomorphic mutations [4,5]. Two of the cases had amaternal history of rubella, which hints at the role of environmental factors in disease manifestation.

Conclusion

This case series highlights the diagnostic complexities of Blaschkoid pigmentary disorders. The findings show the importance of genetic testing while acknowledging its limitations in cases with overlapping clinical features. Future studies exploring alternative genetic mutations in clinically suggestive but IKBKG-negative cases are warranted. A multidisciplinary follow-up approach is essential to addressing the multisystem involvement of IP.
