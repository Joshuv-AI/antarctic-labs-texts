Imaging Findings of Thoracic Extranodal Lymphoma: A Pictorial Essay
Authors: Songa Seo, Soo-yeon Jeong, Mi Sook Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLymphoma is characterized by the neoplastic proliferation of lymphoid cells within the lymphatic system. When it infiltrates organs beyond the lymph nodes, it is called extranodal lymphoma. Extranodal lymphoma can affect various thoracic organs, including the lungs, pleura, chest wall, bony thorax, and breasts. This disease often presents with diverse imaging manifestations such as consolidation, nodules, masses, pleural or pericardial effusion, pleural thickening, pleural masses, large sternal masses, and breast masses. These findings may overlap with those of other thoracic conditions, posing challenges in the differential diagnosis. Given the critical importance of timely diagnosis and treatment in patients with lymphoma, distinguishing extranodal lymphoma from other diseases and evaluating the exact extent and distribution of lymphoma are crucial. This pictorial essay reviews the diverse thoracic involvement of extranodal lymphoma to facilitate diagnosis and appropriate treatment planning.Keywords: Lymphoma, Thorax, Extranodal, Computed Tomography

INTRODUCTION

Lymphoma is characterized by the neoplastic proliferation of lymphoid cells, pri marily within lymph nodes and lymphatic tissues (1). When lymphomatous involvement of non-nodal tissue occurs, it is referred to as extranodal lymphoma (2,3). Lymphomas are broadly classified into non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) and Hodgkin lymphoma (HL), with extranodal involvement occurring more frequently in NHL (up to 33%) than in HL (up to 5%) (3).

Extranodal lymphomas can occur in two forms: primary and secondary. Primary extranodal lymphomas refer to a group of disorders originating from non-lymph node tissues, whereas secondary extranodal lymphomas may arise through direct extension or by hematogenous or lymphatic dissemination (4,5). Secondary lymphoma is more common than primary lymphoma (6).

Among the extranodal sites, the gastrointestinal tract is most commonly involved (43%), followed by the head and neck (14%), skin (7%), brain (6%–7%), bone (5%), and lungs (2%) (1,4). Lymphomas can also involve thoracic structures other than the lungs, such as the pleura, mediastinum, chest wall, bony thorax, and breasts (Table 1).

Thoracic extranodal lymphoma presents with various imaging findings, such as a solitary or multiple nodules, masses, consolidation with air bronchograms, pleural or pericardial effusion, pleural thickening, and pleural masses (3,7,8).

Thoracic extranodal lymphoma can pose challenges in diagnosis owing to the overlapping imaging features with other thoracic conditions. Given the critical importance of timely diagnosis and treatment of patients with lymphoma, distinguishing extranodal lymphoma from other diseases is crucial.

The diagnosis of extranodal lymphoma necessitates a comprehensive assessment that includes the clinical history, physical examination, laboratory tests, imaging techniques such as CT, MRI, and PET/CT, as well as needle biopsy and surgical intervention (9)

Among the aforementioned imaging modalities, CT is a cost-effective and useful method for evaluating thoracic structures including the lungs, and accurately delineates the anatomy (10,11). MRI, with its high soft tissue resolution, is excellent for the detection and characterization of lymphomatous involvement in areas such as the heart, chest wall, and thoracic spine (3). However, it remains less effective than CT to evaluate the lungs (3). Therefore, CT is the preferred cross-sectional imaging modality for early detection and assessment of thoracic extranodal lymphoma and is the main focus of our study (1,6).

In this pictorial essay, we aim to explore various presentations of thoracic involvement in extranodal lymphoma to facilitate diagnosis and appropriate treatment planning.

LUNG

Both primary and secondary extranodal lymphomas may involve the lungs. In cases where the lungs are the primary site, the condition is termed primary pulmonary lymphoma (PPL). PPL is characterized by monoclonal lymphoid proliferation in the lungs in the absence of any identifiable extrathoracic lymphoma for a minimum duration of 3 months after the initial diagnosis (12).

PPL accounts for less than 1% of all lymphomas, while secondary pulmonary involvement can occur in up to 38% of HL and 24% of NHL (13,14).

The most frequent subtype within this category is extranodal marginal zone lymphoma of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT), also known as MALT lymphoma, followed by diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) (3,6). PPL also includes lymphomatoid granulomatosis, a rare Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)-positive subtype that manifests in patients with compromised immunity or autoimmune disorders (3,6,15).

The incidence of secondary pulmonary lymphoma is higher than that of PPL (6). The nature of this disease is largely determined by the primary subtype from which it originates (6).

The symptoms of primary and secondary pulmonary lymphomas are similar. Patients with pulmonary MALT lymphoma are mostly asymptomatic, whereas those with DLBCL typically exhibit symptoms, such as cough, dyspnea, fever, and weight loss (6). DLBCL is aggressive and extensive, with a poor prognosis (16,17).

The imaging features of primary involvement include consolidation with air bronchograms (Fig. 1), large masses (Fig. 2), and endobronchial nodules (Fig. 3). Meanwhile, the imaging features of secondary involvement include ground-glass opacities (Fig. 4), multiple masses (Fig. 5), and a central mass causing bronchial obstruction (Fig. 6). Endobronchial nodules or central masses obstructing the bronchus can mimic bronchogenic carcinoma.

In cases where findings such as consolidation or ground-glass opacities initially suggest pneumonia, secondary lymphoma should be considered in patients previously diagnosed with lymphoma at other body sites if lung lesions persist or worsen despite appropriate antibiotic treatment (6).

PLEURA

Primary and secondary involvement of the pleura occur in both NHL and HL, and accounts for approximately 16% of NHL (3,5). In cases where thoracic extranodal lymphoma affects the pleura, clinical symptoms are generally nonspecific and include chest pain, cough, and dyspnea (6). Extranodal lymphomas primarily involving the pleura are rare (6). When it does occur, it can manifest as two distinct subtypes: pyothorax-associated lymphoma (PAL) and primary effusion lymphoma (PEL) (3,6,18).

PAL is a rare EBV-positive DLBCL (6) that primarily presents as a solid pleural mass on CT (6,19). PAL should be considered when a pleural mass is observed on CT in patients with past or present chronic pyothorax (6).

PEL is an uncommon type of large B-cell NHL that manifests as effusion without an identifiable tumor in body cavities such as the pleural space, pericardium, or peritoneum (20). The diagnosis of PEL depends on the detection of human herpesvirus-8 infection in B cells, which is essential for its pathogenesis (20,21). Imaging findings such as pleural effusion (Fig. 7), pericardial effusion (Fig. 7), and diffuse pleural thickening (>1 cm) have been reported in PEL (3,20,22). Pleural effusion is predominantly unilateral in PEL, although there have been occasional reports of bilateral manifestations (Fig. 7) (23).

Secondary involvement accounts for most cases of pleural NHL (6). Imaging findings may include a solitary nodule, multiple masses with a broad pleural base, and pleural effusion, with pleural effusion as the most common finding in pleural NHL (5,6). Although solid pleural disease is less frequently observed than effusion, it can occur in patients with disseminated NHL (6). CT may reveal soft tissue attenuated nodules, masses, and extensive pleural thickening (Fig. 8). The differential diagnosis of pleural NHL includes metastatic adenocarcinoma and other solid pleural diseases (6). Although pleural NHL exhibits high uptake of 18F fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) on PET/CT, this finding is not specific to lymphoma; therefore, a biopsy is necessary for definitive diagnosis (6).

MEDIASTINUM

Mediastinal extranodal lymphoma is typically secondary but can occasionally present as primary lymphoma (24). Only 10% of mediastinal lymphomas are primary, and the majority of these (up to 60%) are HL (24).

Primary mediastinal lymphoma has three histological subtypes: primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma (PMBCL), lymphoblastic lymphoma, and nodular sclerosing HL (25). PMBCL arises from the thymic medullary B cells and accounts for approximately 2% of all NHL cases (14). Most lymphoblastic lymphomas are of T cell lymphoblastic origin (70%–80%), whereas a smaller proportion (20%–25%) are of B cell lymphoblastic origin (26). Nodular sclerosing HL arises from B lymphocytes (27). PMBCL often presents as a large anterior mediastinal mass, with accompanying signs of superior vena cava obstruction in approximately half of the cases, which is a rare finding in HL (14). Low-density masses with varying degrees of hemorrhage, necrosis, and cystic changes can be observed on CT. Pleural and pericardial effusions may also be present (28). Differentiating PMBCL from thymomas, thymic carcinomas, and DLBCL is challenging. Morphological and immunophenotypic tests are essential for definitive diagnosis.

Mediastinal organs include the heart and pericardium, and thoracic extranodal lymphomas can involve these organs either primarily or secondarily (3,14,28). Both primary and secondary diseases most commonly present as DLBCL (6). Primary cardiac lymphoma, a type of extranodal lymphoma confined to the heart or pericardium, accounts for 0.5% of all extranodal lymphomas (29). In contrast, secondary cardiac involvement in lymphoma has been reported in up to 25% of patients with nodal disease (29). Secondary involvement of the heart and pericardium is rare and typically occurs in cases of disseminated lymphoma (6). It can result from hematogenous or lymphatic spread, or direct extension from peripheral nodal tumors (3). Nonspecific symptoms such as dyspnea and chest pain, as well as congestive heart failure, may occur (6). Both primary and secondary cardiac lymphomas often manifest as bulky, ill-defined, infiltrative masses (6,30). Imaging findings, such as pericardial thickening, nodules, and pericardial effusion have been reported (3,6). Large amounts of pericardial effusion can induce tamponade (6). The differential diagnosis of cardiac lymphoma includes angiosarcoma, which is more aggressive than cardiac lymphoma (6,30). Cardiac angiosarcoma tends to penetrate the valves and great vessels and exhibits central necrosis more frequently than lymphomas (30).

Percutaneous needle biopsy is a commonly used diagnostic method when lymphoma is suspected (31,32). However, particularly in cases of mediastinal lymphoma, the diagnostic yield of percutaneous transthoracic biopsy is relatively low compared with that of other neoplasms, such as thymic tumors (33). In some cases, confirmation can be achieved through surgery or lymph node resection (33).

CHEST WALL AND BONY THORAX

Chest wall involvement has been reported in >9.6% of patients with lymphoma (34,35). Secondary involvement of the chest wall, which is more prevalent than primary involvement, occurs frequently in aggressive NHL subtypes such as DLBCL (6)

Most cases of secondary chest wall involvement are associated with intrathoracic disease (5,36), particularly anterior (most common) or posterior mediastinal lymphadenopathy affecting the parasternal area or thoracic spine, respectively. It may also present as an isolated chest wall mass (5). Pleural masses can extend to the chest wall along the pleural surface (5,6,14).

Thoracic skeletal involvement can occur due to direct extension of a tumor from the lungs or mediastinum (5). Localized bone pain is the most frequently reported symptom, although a palpable mass or pathological fracture may be the initial symptom in some cases (6).

CT findings of extranodal lymphoma of the chest wall and bony thorax include extensive involvement of the vertebral body, associated paraspinal soft tissue masses, nodularity in the extrapleural fat, or soft tissue reticulation (3,6,37). In cases of muscle invasion, various findings such as subtle loss of the fat plane between the muscles and focal or multiple soft tissue masses can be observed (Fig. 9) (3). Focal cortical bone destruction has also been previously reported (3,5). However, these imaging findings are not typical of lymphoma and must be differentiated from conditions such as chest wall tumors or osteomyelitis (3,6). Additionally, presence of enlarged bulky lymph nodes indicates a higher probability of lymphoma (3). A biopsy is required to confirm primary lymphoma or secondary involvement of the bones or muscles (3,6).

BREAST

Breast lymphomas account for approximately 0.15% of all malignant breast tumors (5,38). Primary breast lymphoma accounts for 0.1%–0.5% of all breast tumors, whereas secondary breast involvement occurs in 2% of extranodal lymphomas (5,6).

Breast lymphoma is diagnosed as primary when systemic lymphoma or leukemia is not concurrently present (5,38). The histological types of primary breast lymphoma include DLBCL, follicular lymphoma, MALT lymphoma, Burkitt lymphoma, and small lymphocytic lymphoma, with DLBCL being the most common type (39). Breast implant-associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma, a T-cell primary breast lymphoma, is another type that is associated with textured breast implants (40). It primarily presents as a fluid collection due to peri-implant effusion and less commonly as a mass (40).

Secondary breast lymphoma with extramammary involvement is more common and can present as multiple breast lesions or simultaneous lesions in the breast and other non-mammary organs (38).

Breast lymphoma can present with various nonspecific symptoms that may be mistaken for breast carcinoma (41). The most common symptom is a palpable mass (42,43). Other local signs such as discharge, nipple retraction, and skin changes rarely occur (41,42). Although rare, systemic symptoms such as weight loss, sweating, and fever have also been reported (41).

The imaging findings of breast lymphomas closely mimic those of typical breast carcinoma (6), and include an irregular or lobular solid mass on US (Figs. 10, 11), a circumscribed or indistinctly marginated, round or oval mass with various degrees of enhancement on CT (Figs. 10, 11), heterogeneous enhancement with rapid initial enhancement and plateau or delayed washout kinetics on MRI, and high FDG uptake on PET/CT (6,38,42).

Differentiating breast lymphoma from breast carcinoma and inflammatory diseases such as breast abscess is challenging but essential (6,42). Features such as spiculations, calcifications, and architectural distortion are more commonly associated with breast carcinoma, whereas such findings are typically absent in lymphomas (6,44). Additionally, some studies have suggested that the presence of multiple or bilateral lesions may indicate a higher probability of breast lymphoma than of breast carcinoma (6). A complex cystic mass observed on US suggests a breast abscess rather than a lymphoma (6,42).

CONCLUSIONS

While thoracic lymphomas frequently involve the lymph nodes, extranodal thoracic involvement is also commonly observed. Diagnosing extranodal lymphoma can be challenging because of the similarity of its imaging findings to those of other malignant and benign diseases. By demonstrating the diverse patterns of thoracic involvement in extranodal lymphomas, this review can assist in timely diagnosis and appropriate treatment planning.
