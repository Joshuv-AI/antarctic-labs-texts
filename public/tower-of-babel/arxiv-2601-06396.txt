Performance Analysis for Wireless Localization with Random Sensor Network PubID: pubid: 0000–0000 © 2023 IEEE
Mengqi Ma, Aihua Xia

Abstract
Accurate wireless localization underpins applications from autonomous systems to smart infrastructure. We study the mean-squared error (MSE) and conditional MSE (CMSE) of a practical fusion-based estimator in d d -dimensional, stationary isotropic (translation- and rotation-invariant) random sensor networks, where a central processor combines received-signal-strength (RSS) and angle-of-arrival (AOA) measurements to infer a target’s position. Our contributions are twofold. First, we establish an approximation theorem: when measurement noise is sufficiently large, the joint law of RSS and AOA observations under a broad class of stationary isotropic deployments is, in distribution, indistinguishable from that induced by a homogeneous Poisson point process (PPP). Second, leveraging this equivalence, we investigate a homogeneous PPP–based sensor network. We propose a fusion-based estimator in which a central processor aggregates RSS and AOA measurements from a set of spatially distributed sensors to infer the target position. For this PPP deployment within a finite observation region, we derive tractable analytical upper bounds for both the MSE and CMSE, establishing explicit scaling laws with respect to sensor density, observation radius, and noise variance. The approximation theorem then certifies these PPP-based bounds as reasonable proxies for non-Poisson deployments in noisy regimes. Overall, the results translate deployment and sensing parameters into achievable accuracy targets and provide robust, cost-aware guidance for the design of next-generation location-aware wireless networks.



I
Introduction

Accurate wireless localization is a fundamental enabler for a wide variety of emerging applications, ranging from autonomous vehicles and asset tracking to industrial automation, smart cities, and defense operations. While satellite-based systems such as GPS have transformed global positioning capabilities, their performance deteriorates in obstructed or indoor environments where signal blockage and multipath propagation become severe. To address these challenges, network-based wireless localization techniques have gained increasing attention.

In a typical wireless localization setup, multiple spatially distributed sensor nodes collect measurements of received signal waveforms from an unknown target source. Commonly used features include time-of-arrival (TOA), angle-of-arrival (AOA), and received signal strength (RSS). The TOA is obtained by measuring the signal propagation time between the target and the sensor; ideally, the distance estimate is the product of the known propagation speed and the measured travel time
[
46
]
. The AOA represents the angle at which a signal arrives at the sensor, and it can be measured either directly using a directional antenna or indirectly by using an antenna array to compare the relative TOA across elements
[
18
,
14
]
. The RSS refers to the power of the received signal, which can be used to estimate the distance to the target, although its accuracy is often limited by the difficulty of precisely modeling the relationship between signal strength and propagation distance. These measurements are then transmitted to a central processor or
fusion center
that jointly estimates the target’s position.

In this work, we focus on the problem of localizing a moving target in wireless sensor networks under realistic signal propagation effects. In practice, although sensor nodes may be deterministically placed or randomly deployed, the localization algorithm typically uses only the sensors that are sufficiently close to the target, since distant sensors provide measurements that are too weak or too noisy to be informative. As the target moves through space, the subset of nearby sensors continually changes. This evolving set of “usable” sensors, illustrated in Figs.
1a
–
1f
, appears from the target’s perspective as a random point pattern—even if the underlying sensor deployment is fixed and deterministic. This observation motivates our modeling approach: rather than studying a specific geographic network realization, we adopt an equivalent model in which the target is represented as an emitting source fixed at the origin, while the sensor nodes are modeled as points drawn from a spatial point process. What matters for localization performance is the relative spatial geometry between the target and the surrounding sensors, not their absolute geographical coordinates. By treating the effective neighboring sensors as realizations of a random point process, we preserve the inherent spatial uncertainty encountered by the moving target and obtain a mathematically tractable framework for characterizing average localization performance. This stochastic-geometry-based modeling paradigm—widely used in the literature
[
3
,
4
,
24
,
23
]
—enables rigorous analysis of the expected localization performance under varying network densities and measurement uncertainties.

In contrast, we adopt a complementary perspective by directly quantifying the MSE and conditional MSE (CMSE) of a concrete and practically implementable localization strategy (see Algorithm
1
). Specifically, we consider a fusion-based estimator wherein a central processor aggregates RSS and AOA measurements from spatially distributed sensor nodes to jointly infer the target position. Analytical and approximate upper bounds are then derived for the MSE and CMSE across a general class of stationary isotropic sensor networks.

As the exact MSE and CMSE for stationary isotropic sensor networks are generally intractable, we begin our analysis by establishing an approximation theorem: when the signal propagation effect is sufficiently significant, the joint law of RSS and AOA observations collected from a broad class of stationary isotropic sensor networks (satisfying the Short Range Dependency condition (
5
)) is, in distribution, indistinguishable from that collected from a homogeneous PPP network. Leveraging this equivalence, we investigate a homogeneous PPP–based sensor network. We propose a fusion-based estimator in which a central processor aggregates RSS and AOA measurements from a set of spatially PPP distributed sensors to infer the target position. For this PPP deployment within a finite observation region, we derive tractable analytical bounds for both the MSE and CMSE of the position estimator, establishing explicit scaling laws with respect to sensor density, observation radius, and noise variance. The approximation theorem then certifies these PPP-based bounds as reasonable approximation bounds for stationary isotropic deployments that satisfy the SRD condition (
5
) in noisy regimes. Extensive simulation studies are conducted to evaluate the tightness of the proposed upper bounds in homogeneous PPP, repulsive, and clustered sensor networks under realistic propagation environments.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the system model and presents the convergence result, which establishes the asymptotic equivalence between observations collected from a broad class of stationary isotropic sensor networks and those from a homogeneous PPP network. Examples based on repulsive and clustered point processes are provided to illustrate this convergence behavior. Section 3 revisits the Poisson model and introduces the proposed fusion-based estimator, deriving analytical upper bounds on the MSE and CMSE. In Section 4, simulation studies are conducted to evaluate the quality of these bounds when sensor nodes are drawn from homogeneous PPP, repulsive, and clustered point processes. Section 5 concludes with a summary of our findings and outlines potential directions for future research, while Section 6 contains the proofs of the main theoretical results.

Remark 2
.

By Theorem
1
, all stationary isotropic point processes obeying (
5
) have the same limiting law, although the theorem is phrased to highlight their connection to the homogeneous PPP.

The convergence result shows that, when the random propagation noise
\sigma
is large, the observable process generated by an arbitrary stationary isotropic point process satisfying (
5
) becomes statistically indistinguishable from that generated by a homogeneous PPP with the same intensity. This justifies the use of the PPP network model in noisy environments, even when the underlying sensor configuration does not strictly follow a PPP.

The proof of Theroem
1
follows the spirit of Theorem 2.10 in
[
29
]
, where the authors proved that the inverse RSS process
\{N_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{\Xi}}
(also referred to as the propagation process) generated by a
d
-dimensional random network, under some regularity conditions
[
29
, (2.6)]
, converges in distribution to a Poisson process on
\mathbb{R}^{\circ}_{+}
. This limiting Poisson process can be interpreted as the signal strength process induced by an underlying Poisson network in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which implies that, from the perspective of an observer interested only in the RSS or its related statistics, the underlying network appears indistinguishable from a Poisson network when the noise is sufficiently significant.

Our proof follows a similar line of reasoning, but extends the inverse RSS process to the observable process
\mathcal{N}^{(\sigma)}_{\Xi}
, in which we are concerned not only with the signal strength but also with the directions of the incoming signals.

It is worth mentioning that in
[
29
]
, the context of study is based on a setting where a receiver is located at the origin and receives signals from transmitters that form a random point process. In contrast, in our framework, the target is located at the origin and is modeled as an emitting source that transmits signals to sensors, which themselves form a random point process. In our setting, a fusion center collects the observable information gathered by the sensors, analogous to the receiver in
[
29
]
. Therefore, our result can be interpreted as a dual result, highlighting that the respective roles of transmitters and receivers can be interchanged; what ultimately matters is the information collected from the network.

It is also worth noting that the result in
[
29
]
applies to a broader class of random networks in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, including inhomogeneous models and deterministic models, whereas in our case we restrict our analysis to stationary and isotropic random networks.

Sensors placed according to a Matérn hardcore process

The assumption that sensors are distributed according to a homogeneous PPP is widely used

In this section, we focus on the Matérn Type I and Type II processes (see
[
3
, Chapter 2.1.3]
), which introduce repulsion through dependent thinning of an initial Poisson process. Specifically, we begin by generating sensor locations according to a homogeneous PPP in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

\Phi^{p}=\{X_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{\Phi^{p}}}

with intensity
\lambda_{p}
. The points in
\Phi^{p}
are then selectively retained based on a specified thinning rule, resulting in a new point configuration.

For the Matérn Type I process, each point
X_{i}\in\Phi^{p}
in the initial configuration is retained if there are no other points within a distance of
\rho_{c}
from
X_{i}
, where
\rho_{c}
denotes the hardcore radius. If there are points within this distance, then

and all points within
\rho_{c}

are removed. The remaining points constitute a Matérn Type I process, denoted by
\Xi_{1}
, which ensures that any two retained points are at least
\rho_{c}

In contrast, the Matérn Type II thinning rule is more conservative. For each point
X_{i}\in\Phi^{p}
in the initial configuration, we assign a random mark
\text{Age}(X_{i})
sampled uniformly from
[0,1]

X_{i}
, we examine all other points within a distance
\rho_{c}
. If

has the smallest age among its neighbors within
\rho_{c}

\Xi_{2}

\rho_{c}

III

Theorem
1

\bf 0

III-B

Main Result

We are interested in both the conditional and unconditional MSE between the overall estimate
\mathcal{X}
and the true target location at the origin. These are defined respectively as:

\text{CMSE}_{\mathcal{R}}(n):=\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathcal{X}|^{2}\;\middle|\;|\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}|=n\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{X}_{j}^{2}\;\middle|\;|\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}|=n\right],

\text{MSE}_{\mathcal{R}}:=\mathbb{E}[\text{CMSE}(|\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}|)]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{X}_{j}^{2}\right].

Theorem 5
.

Under the setup described above, the conditional and unconditional MSE satisfy the following bounds:

\text{CMSE}_{\mathcal{R}}(n)\leq\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{d}{d+2}\mathcal{R}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}^{2}\right]-\frac{2d\mathcal{R}}{d+1}e^{-\frac{d-1}{2}\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R})}\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\right]\right),

\displaystyle\text{MSE}_{\mathcal{R}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\lambda V_{d}(\mathcal{R})}\left(\frac{d}{d+2}\mathcal{R}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}^{2}\right]-\frac{2d\mathcal{R}}{d+1}e^{-\frac{d-1}{2}\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R})}\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\right]\right),

where
V_{d}(\mathcal{R})
is the volume of a
d
dimensional ball
B_{\bf 0}(\mathcal{R})
of radius
\mathcal{R}
.

Proof.

The argument further exploits that, conditional on the number of sensors, the sensor locations are i.i.d. uniformly distributed in
B_{\bf 0}(\mathcal{R})
. Under this conditioning, the estimator becomes a (symmetric) function of i.i.d. uniform samples, and the stated bounds follow by invoking the calculations developed in Chapter 5; for readability, we defer the full derivation to that chapter.
∎

Theorem
5
provides an explicit upper bound for the CMSE and MSE between the estimated and true location of the target, expressed in terms of the number of sensors within the ball
B_{\bf 0}(\mathcal{R})
, the density of the sensor placements, and the estimation range
\mathcal{R}
. From this result, we observe that for a given estimation range
\mathcal{R}
, the CMSE decreases as the number of sensors increases. Similarly, the MSE decreases as the sensor density increases. However, for the CMSE, increasing the estimation range
\mathcal{R}
while keeping the number of sensors fixed leads to a larger localization error. These monotonicity properties are consistent with intuition: increasing information—via additional sensors or higher spatial density—improves accuracy, whereas enlarging the estimation region with a fixed sensor count degrades it.

To the best of our knowledge, the closest result to Theorem
5
is due to
[
6
]
, who consider an

on the variance (or equivalently MSE) of any unbiased estimator. In their asymptotic regimes, the bound scales with density as

for wideband localization and
\lambda^{-\beta/2-1}

\mathcal{R}

First, our bound is stated for a finite observation window, while their analysis targets the infinite PPP networks. Second, their CRB framework provides bounds that hold for
any unbiased
estimator of the target position without prescribing a specific algorithm. By contrast, we analyze a concrete localization procedure (Algorithm

R_{i}
from RSS measurements

\mathcal{X}
.

Theorem
1
considers stationary isotropic sensor networks in the entire space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and states that, as the noise level
\sigma\to\infty
, the fusion center cannot distinguish—from the observed measurements—whether the underlying sensor deployment follows a homogeneous PPP or a more general stationary isotropic point process. The result can be interpreted as a joint asymptotic statement for a large observation radius
\mathcal{R}
and a large noise parameter
\sigma
. In particular, when the fusion center collects measurements from a sufficiently large region and those measurements are strongly affected by random propagation effects, the influence of short-range spatial dependence among sensor locations becomes negligible. Consequently, one may employ a finite Poisson model with the same intensity even when the actual sensor configuration is not exactly Poisson. The performance bounds in Theorem
5

5

V
Discussion and Future Work

This paper has presented a comprehensive analysis of wireless target localization in
d
-dimensional sensor networks, with particular emphasis on the role of spatial point process models. By combining analytical derivations with simulation studies, we have characterized the MSE and CMSE under a broad range of network configurations. In particular, we established an approximation theorem showing that when the observation region is sufficiently large and the propagation noise is sufficiently significant, the fusion center—which aggregates measurements from all sensors within this finite observation region—cannot statistically distinguish whether the underlying sensor placements follow a PPP or a more general stationary isotropic point process satisfying the SRD condition (
5
). Consequently, the MSE and CMSE of this broader class of networks are expected to be close to those of the Poisson case.

Focusing on the finite Poisson model, we proposed an unbiased estimator (
11
) of the target position and then derived explicit upper bounds for the MSE and CMSE in terms of node density, observation radius, and measurement noise. These bounds can serve as approximate performance bounds for a wide range of non-Poisson deployment models satisfying the SRD condition (
5
). From a practical perspective, if network designers believe that the underlying sensor configuration falls within this class, the derived bounds offer quantitative guidance for deployment decisions: for example, determining how many sensors are required to achieve a desired level of accuracy, or evaluating how different spatial deployment strategies (uniform, repulsive, or clustered) affect localization performance.

As emphasized in Section 1, the objective of this work is not to analyze the performance of a particular realization of a sensor network at a specific geographic location. Such an approach would require repeating the analysis for each distinct configuration. Instead, our goal has been to characterize the
average performance across all possible realizations
, thereby providing general guidelines and insights for the design and deployment of wireless sensor networks. By interpreting different geographical networks as realizations of the same spatial point process, we leveraged tools from stochastic geometry and point process theory to analyze the performance of the localization algorithm (Algorithm
1
) that utilizes RSS and AOA as inputs.

The findings of this paper open several promising directions for future research:

•

CMSE and MSE bounds for stationary isotropic sensor networks:

The more relevant CMSE and MSE bounds for a stationary isotropic sensor network should be expressed in terms of the network’s characteristic properties. To this end, there are two aspects to be studied. Firstly, finding the limiting distribution for the second-order Wasserstein distance between the estimator and the target point, with proper scaling, as such distribution is the basis for corresponding statistical inference. The most likely candidate is the chi-squared distribution, as it represents the sum of squares of independent standard normal random variables. Secondly, establishing the bound for the second-order Wasserstein distance between the estimator and the limiting distribution. Developing suitable tools for estimating such errors presents an intriguing research challenge, with potential implications for improving the accuracy and reliability of sensor network-based estimations.

•

Extension to inhomogeneous models:

This work focused on homogeneous PPP models. A natural extension is to analyze MSE and CMSE in inhomogeneous PPPs, where sensor density varies spatially. Building on this, an intertesting open question is whether one can formulate general conditions under which the observable process
\mathcal{N}^{(\sigma)}_{\Xi}
generated by a possibly non-stationary or non-isotropic point process
\Xi
converges to that of an inhomogeneous PPP with the same intensity measure.

•

Spatially correlated propagation effects:

Our convergence results assumed i.i.d. signal propagation effects for each sensor. This assumption, while analytically convenient, may be unrealistic in practice
[
21
,
12
]
. For example, signals arriving from similar directions are expected to exhibit spatially correlated fading and angular errors. In this context,
[
42
]
extended the convergence results of
[
29
]
for the inverse signal strength process by modeling fading variables as a log-Gaussian random field with a spatial correlation function, rather than as i.i.d. positive random variables. A similar extension of our results to account for spatial correlation in propagation effects would be of both theoretical and practical interest.

•

Alternative localization strategies:

This work focused exclusively on localization strategies that use RSS and AOA as inputs.
An interesting future direction is to study other strategies that rely on different
measurement modalities, such as TOA, time difference of arrival,
or combinations of multiple signal features. Analyzing their performance within the
stochastic geometry framework could provide deeper insights into the trade-offs between
different sensing modalities.

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{\sigma\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[|\Lambda|\left(g^{-1}\left(S{(\sigma)}b\right)\right)\mathbb{I}(S{(\sigma)}\leq T_{\epsilon})\right]

\displaystyle=0.

Therefore, we have

\limsup_{\sigma\to\infty}v_{\sigma}\leq\left\|\mathcal{D}_{d}f\right\|^{2}V(\mathcal{S})^{2}\;\epsilon\;\lim_{\sigma\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[|\Lambda|_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\left(g^{-1}\left(S{(\sigma)}b\right),\left\{e_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{d-1}\right)\right]^{2}.

Since the choice of
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary, we have

\lim_{\sigma\to\infty}v_{\sigma}=0.

Now
(\mathrm{I})_{\Xi}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\rightarrow}0
follows easily from applying the Markov inequality.

(c) Use the same reasoning as that for (
27
), we have

\displaystyle|(\mathrm{III})_{\Xi}|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{s_{0}}\int_{\mathcal{S}}|\Lambda|_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\left(g^{-1}\left(st\right),\boldsymbol{\nu}\right)\left|\mathcal{D}_{d}f\left(t,\boldsymbol{\nu}\right)\right|\;\text{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}\;\text{d}t\;\text{d}F_{\sigma}(s)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathcal{D}_{d}f\right\|V(\mathcal{S})\mathbb{E}\left[|\Lambda|_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\left(g^{-1}\left(S{(\sigma)}b\right),\left\{e_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{d-1}\right)\mathbb{I}(S{(\sigma)}\leq s_{0})\right]

\displaystyle\to 0

as
{\sigma\to\infty}
, which proves the claim.

(d) Following the arguments in (c),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|(\mathrm{IV})_{\Xi}|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{s_{0}}\int_{\mathcal{S}}|\Xi|_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\left(g^{-1}\left(st\right),\boldsymbol{\nu}\right)\left|\mathcal{D}_{d}f\left(t,\boldsymbol{\nu}\right)\right|\;\text{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}\;\text{d}t\;\text{d}F_{\sigma}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{s_{0}}\int_{\mathcal{S}}|\Lambda|_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\left(g^{-1}\left(st\right),\boldsymbol{\nu}\right)\left|\mathcal{D}_{d}f\left(t,\boldsymbol{\nu}\right)\right|\;\text{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}\;\text{d}t\;\text{d}F_{\sigma}(s)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathcal{D}_{d}f\right\|V(\mathcal{S})\mathbb{E}\left[|\Lambda|_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\left(g^{-1}\left(S{(\sigma)}b\right),\left\{e_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{d-1}\right)\mathbb{I}(S{(\sigma)}\leq s_{0})\right]

\displaystyle\to 0

as
{\sigma\to\infty}
, which proves the claim.

(e) Let
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}[c_{j},e_{j}]
. We show that under the SRD condition (
5
),

\frac{\operatorname{Var}\!|\Xi|_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\left(\rho,\boldsymbol{\nu}\right)}{\;|\Lambda|_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\left(\rho,\boldsymbol{\nu}\right)^{2}}\;\to\;0

uniformly in
\boldsymbol{\nu}
as
\rho\to\infty
. By defining

\mathcal{P}(X_{i},\boldsymbol{\nu}):=\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{i}\leq\nu^{(j)}\,\middle|\,X_{i}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{i}\leq\nu^{(j)}\,\middle|\,R_{i},\Psi^{(j)}_{i}\right),

we can write

|\Xi|_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\left(\rho,\boldsymbol{\nu}\right)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{\Xi}}\mathbb{I}\!\left(0<|X_{i}|\leq\rho\right)\,\mathcal{P}(X_{i},\boldsymbol{\nu}).

We can show that
\mathcal{P}(X_{i},\boldsymbol{\nu})
is bounded away from zero and infinity. Notice that

\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{i}\leq c_{j}\,\middle|\,R_{i},\Psi^{(j)}_{i}\right)\;\leq\;\mathcal{P}(X_{i},\boldsymbol{\nu})\;\leq\;1.

Recall that for
1\leq j\leq d-2
,
0\leq\Psi^{(j)}_{i}\leq\pi
and

\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{i}\,\middle|\,R_{i},\Psi^{(j)}_{i}\right)\ \overset{d}{=}\ \mathrm{N}\left(\pi-\Psi^{(j)}_{i},\,\mathcal{E}(R_{i})\right),

it is easy to see that the function

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{i}\leq c_{j}\,\middle|\,R_{i},\Psi^{(j)}_{i}=\psi\right)

is a strictly increasing function of
\psi\in[0,\pi]
almost surely. Similarly, since
0\leq\Psi^{(d-1)}_{i}\leq 2\pi
and

\left(\Theta^{(d-1)}_{i}\,\middle|\,R_{i},\Psi^{(d-1)}_{i}\right)\ \overset{d}{=}\ \mathrm{N}\left(\Psi^{(d-1)}_{i}-\pi,\,\mathcal{E}(R_{i})\right),

the function

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{i}\leq c_{j}\,\middle|\,R_{i},\Psi^{(j)}_{i}=\psi\right)

is a strictly decreasing function of
\psi\in[0,2\pi]
almost surely. From this observation, we have

\mathcal{P}(X_{i},\boldsymbol{\nu})\;\geq\;\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{i}\leq c_{j}\,\middle|\,R_{i},\Psi^{(j)}_{i}=0\right)\;\mathbb{P}\!\left(\Theta^{(d-1)}_{i}\leq c_{d-1}\,\middle|\,R_{i},\Psi^{(d-1)}_{i}=2\pi\right)

almost surely.

For
1\leq j\leq d-2
,

\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{i}\,\middle|\,R_{i},\Psi^{(j)}_{i}=0\right)\ \overset{d}{=}\ \mathrm{N}\left(\pi,\,\mathcal{E}(R_{i})\right),

so when
c_{j}<\pi
, using the assumption that
\mathcal{E}
is bounded away from 0, there exists
\varepsilon_{L}>0
such that
\varepsilon_{L}\leq\mathcal{E}(R_{i})
almost surely, and thus

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{i}\leq c_{j}\,\middle|\,R_{i},\Psi^{(j)}_{i}=0\right)\;\geq\;\mathbb{P}\!\left(Z^{(j)}\leq c_{j}\right)\;>\;0

almost surely, where
Z^{(j)}\overset{d}{=}\mathrm{N}\left(\pi,\,\varepsilon_{L}\right)
; when
c_{j}\geq\pi
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{i}\leq c_{j}\,\middle|\,R_{i},\Psi^{(j)}_{i}=0\right)\ \geq\ \tfrac{1}{2}.

Combining, we get for
1\leq j\leq d-2

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{i}\leq c_{j}\,\middle|\,R_{i},\Psi^{(j)}_{i}=0\right)\;\geq\;\min\!\Big\{\tfrac{1}{2},\ \mathbb{P}\!\left(Z^{(j)}\leq c_{j}\right)\Big\}\;>\;0

almost surely.

Similarly,

\left(\Theta^{(d-1)}_{i}\,\middle|\,R_{i},\Psi^{(d-1)}_{i}=2\pi\right)\ \overset{d}{=}\ \mathrm{N}\!\left(\pi,\mathcal{E}(R_{i})\right),

we can show

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\Theta^{(d-1)}_{i}\leq c_{d-1}\,\middle|\,R_{i},\Psi^{(d-1)}_{i}=2\pi\right)\;\geq\;\min\!\Big\{\tfrac{1}{2},\ \mathbb{P}\!\left(Z^{(d-1)}\leq c_{d-1}\right)\Big\}\;>\;0,

where
Z^{(d-1)}\overset{d}{=}\mathrm{N}\!\left(\pi,\varepsilon_{L}\right)
.
Therefore, by defining

\kappa\;:=\;\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}\min\!\Big\{\tfrac{1}{2},\ \mathbb{P}\!\left(Z^{(j)}\leq c_{j}\right)\Big\}\;>\;0,

we have
\ \kappa\ \leq\ \mathcal{P}(X_{i},\boldsymbol{\nu})\ \leq\ 1
, which yields the boundedness.

Now, by Campbell’s formula (see, for example, Theorem 4.1 in
[
24
]
),

|\Lambda|_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\left(\rho,\boldsymbol{\nu}\right)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{\Xi}}\mathbb{I}\!\left(0<|X_{i}|\leq\rho\right)\,\mathcal{P}(X_{i},\boldsymbol{\nu})\right]=\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{I}\!\left(0<|x|\leq\rho\right)\,\mathcal{P}(x)\,\text{d}x.

Since
\mathcal{P}(x)
is bounded, we have

\lambda\,\kappa\,V_{d}(\rho)=\lambda\,\kappa\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{I}\!\left(0<|x|\leq\rho\right)\text{d}x\ \leq\ |\Lambda|_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\left(\rho,\boldsymbol{\nu}\right)\ \leq\ \lambda\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{I}\!\left(0<|x|\leq\rho\right)\text{d}x=\lambda\,V_{d}(\rho).

Equivalently,
|\Lambda|_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\left(\rho,\boldsymbol{\chi}\right)=\mathrm{O}\left(V_{d}(\rho)\right)=\mathrm{O}(\rho^{d})
as
\rho\to\infty
.
Similarly, using Campbell’s formula,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}|\Xi|_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\left(\rho,\boldsymbol{\nu}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{\Xi}}\mathbb{I}(0<|X_{i}|\leq\rho)\,\mathcal{P}(X_{i},\boldsymbol{\nu})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{\Xi}}\mathbb{I}(0<|X_{i}|\leq\rho)\,\mathcal{P}(X_{i},\boldsymbol{\nu})\right)^{\!2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{\Xi}}\mathbb{I}(0<|X_{i}|\leq\rho)\,\mathcal{P}(X_{i},\boldsymbol{\nu})\right]^{\!2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{\Xi}}\mathbb{I}(0<|X_{i}|\leq\rho)\,\mathcal{P}(X_{i},\boldsymbol{\nu})^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{I}^{\Xi}\ :\ i\neq j}\mathbb{I}(0<|X_{i}|\leq\rho)\mathbb{I}(0<|X_{j}|\leq\rho)\,\mathcal{P}(X_{i},\boldsymbol{\nu})\mathcal{P}(X_{j})\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\lambda^{2}\!\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{I}(0<|x|\leq\rho)\,\mathcal{P}(x)\,\text{d}x\right)^{\!2}

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{I}(0<|x|\leq\rho)\,\mathcal{P}(x)^{2}\,\text{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{I}(0<|x|\leq\rho)\mathbb{I}(0<|y|\leq\rho)\,\mathcal{P}(x)\mathcal{P}(y)\,\varrho^{(2)}(x,y)\,\text{d}y\,\text{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad-\lambda^{2}\!\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{I}(0<|x|\leq\rho)\mathbb{I}(0<|y|\leq\rho)\,\mathcal{P}(x)\mathcal{P}(y)\,\text{d}y\,\text{d}x

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{I}(0<|x|\leq\rho)\,\mathcal{P}(x)^{2}\,\text{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad+\lambda^{2}\!\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{I}(0<|x|\leq\rho)\mathbb{I}(0<|y|\leq\rho)\,\mathcal{P}(x)\mathcal{P}(y)\,\left[\frac{\varrho^{(2)}(x,y)}{\lambda^{2}}-1\right]\,\text{d}y\,\text{d}x

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{I}(0<|x|\leq\rho)\,\mathcal{P}(x)^{2}\,\text{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad+\lambda^{2}\!\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{I}(0<|x|\leq\rho)\mathbb{I}(0<|y|\leq\rho)\,\mathcal{P}(x)\mathcal{P}(y)\,\left(h_{SI}(|x-y|)-1\right)\,\text{d}y\,\text{d}x.

Since
\kappa\leq\mathcal{P}(x)\leq 1
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{I}(0<|x|\leq\rho)\,\mathcal{P}(x)^{2}\,dx=\mathrm{O}\left(V_{d}(\rho)\right)=\mathrm{O}\left(\rho^{d}\right)

as
\rho\to\infty
.
Using a change of variables
u=y-x
and
\mathcal{P}(x)\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{I}(0<|x|\leq\rho)\mathbb{I}(0<|y|\leq\rho)\,\mathcal{P}(x)\mathcal{P}(y)\,\left(h_{SI}(|x-y|)-1\right)\,\text{d}y\,\text{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\quad=\left|\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{I}(0<|x|\leq\rho)\mathbb{I}(0<|x+u|\leq\rho)\,\mathcal{P}(x)\mathcal{P}(x+u)\,\left(h_{SI}(|u|)-1\right)\,\text{d}u\,\text{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{I}(0<|x|\leq\rho)\mathbb{I}(0<|x+u|\leq\rho)\,\big|h_{SI}(|u|)-1\big|\,\text{d}u\,\text{d}x

\displaystyle\quad\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|h_{SI}(|u|)-1\big|\,\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{I}(0<|x|\leq\rho)\,\text{d}x\right)\,\text{d}u

\displaystyle\quad=V_{d}(\rho)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|h_{SI}(|u|)-1\big|\,\text{d}u

\displaystyle\quad=V_{d}(\rho)\,\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}\!\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl|h_{SI}(r)-1\bigr|\,r^{d-1}\,\text{d}r}_{<\infty\ \text{ by SRD}}

\displaystyle\quad=\mathrm{O}\left(\rho^{d}\right),

where we used hyperspherical coordinates
[
7
]
in the second last equality, and
\Gamma
is the Gamma function. Therefore, we can conclude that

\operatorname{Var}|\Xi|_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\left(\rho,\boldsymbol{\nu}\right)=\mathrm{O}\left(\rho^{d}\right)

as
\rho\to\infty
, and hence

\frac{\operatorname{Var}|\Xi|_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\left(\rho,\boldsymbol{\nu}\right)}{\,|\Lambda|_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\left(\rho,\boldsymbol{\nu}\right)^{2}}=\mathrm{O}\left(\rho^{-d}\right)\;\longrightarrow\;0

uniformly in
\boldsymbol{\nu}
as
\rho\to\infty
, which yields the claim.

∎

Proof of Lemma
4
.

To show
\mathcal{X}
is an unbiased estimator of the target position (the origin), we need to show that for
1\leq j\leq d

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{X}_{j}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i,j}\right]=0.

(28)

Given
|\Xi_{\mathcal{R}}|=n
, the sensor positions
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}=\{(X_{i,1},...,X_{i,d})\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. uniformly distributed over
B_{\bf 0}(\mathcal{R})
(see
[
28
]
, Chapter 1.2), which implies that their hyperspherical coordinates
\left(R_{i},\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{i}\right)
are also i.i.d. The estimated locations
\left\{\tilde{X}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
depend only on the observable quantities
\left(P_{i},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{i}\right)
, which themselves are functions of
\left(R_{i},\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{i}\right)
, that are conditionally i.i.d., and i.i.d. random variables
S_{i}(\sigma)
. As a result, the estimates
\left\{\tilde{X}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i,j}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{X}_{1,j}\right],

and hence, it suffices to show that for
1\leq j\leq d

\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{X}_{1,j}\right]=0.

(29)

Recall that

\begin{cases}\tilde{X}_{1,k}=X_{1,k}+\hat{R}_{1}\cos\left(\Theta^{(k)}_{1}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\sin\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{1}\right),1\leq k\leq d-1,\\[6.0pt]
\tilde{X}_{1,d}=X_{1,d}+\hat{R}_{1}\sin\left(\Theta^{(d-1)}_{1}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\sin\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{1}\right).\end{cases}

Since
(X_{1,1},...,X_{1,d})
is the Cartesian coordinates of a uniformly chosen point in
B_{\bf 0}(\mathcal{R})
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{1,k}\right]=0

for
1\leq k\leq d
by symmetry.

Therefore, using the tower property, we have for
1\leq k\leq d-2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{X}_{1,k}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[X_{1,k}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\cos\left(\Theta^{(k)}_{1}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\sin\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\cos\left(\Theta^{(k)}_{1}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\sin\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{1}\right)\;\middle|\;R_{1},\left\{\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right\}_{j=1}^{k}\right]\right]

(30)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\;\middle|\;R_{1}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(\Theta^{(k)}_{1}\right)\;\middle|\;R_{1},\Psi^{(k)}_{1}\right]\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sin\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{1}\right)\;\middle|\;R_{1},\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right]\right],

(31)

as
\hat{R}_{1}
and
\left\{\Theta^{(j)}_{1}\right\}_{j=1}^{k}
are conditionally independent given
R_{1}
and
\left\{\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right\}_{j=1}^{k}
. Using the assumption that
\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{1}\;\middle|\;\Psi^{(j)}_{1},R_{1}\right)\overset{d}{=}\mathrm{N}\left(\pi-\Psi^{(j)}_{1},\mathcal{E}(R_{1})\right)
for
1\leq j\leq d-2
, we can compute
\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\Theta^{(j)}_{1}\;\middle|\;R_{1},\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right]
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\sin\Theta^{(j)}_{1}\;\middle|\;R_{1},\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right]
via the characteristic function of normal random variables:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\Theta^{(j)}_{1}\;\middle|\;R_{1},\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\sqrt{-1}\Theta^{(j)}_{1}}\;\middle|\;R_{1},\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\sqrt{-1}\Theta^{(j)}_{1}}\;\middle|\;R_{1},\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right]

(32)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}e^{\sqrt{-1}\left(\pi-\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}(R_{1})}+\frac{1}{2}e^{-\sqrt{-1}\left(\pi-\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}(R_{1})}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}(R_{1})}\cos\left(\pi-\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=-e^{-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}(R_{1})}\cos\left(\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right),

and similarly

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sin\Theta^{(j)}_{1}\;\middle|\;R_{1},\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}(R_{1})}\sin\left(\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right).

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{X}_{1,k}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[-\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\;\middle|\;R_{1}\right]\;e^{-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}(R_{1})}\cos\left(\Psi^{(k)}_{1}\right)\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}(R_{1})}\sin\left(\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[-\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\;\middle|\;R_{1}\right]e^{-\frac{k}{2}\mathcal{E}(R_{1})}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(\Psi^{(k)}_{1}\right)\right]\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sin\left(\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right)\right],

(33)

where we have used the fact that the hyperspherical coordinates
\left(R_{i},\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{i}\right)
of the points of a PPP are independent of each other, as their joint density (computed via change of variables) factorizes:

\displaystyle p_{\left(R_{i},\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{i}\right)}\left(r,\left\{\psi^{(j)}\right\}_{j=1}^{d-1}\right)

(34)

\displaystyle\propto r^{d-1}\mathbb{I}(0\leq r\leq\mathcal{R})\;\mathbb{I}(0\leq\psi^{(d-1)}<2\pi)\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\sin^{d-1-j}\left(\psi^{(j)}\right)\mathbb{I}\left(0\leq\psi^{(j)}\leq\pi\right)\right),

where
\propto
means that they differ by a constant scaling factor. The marginal densities of the hyperspherical coordinates are given by

p_{R_{1}}(r)=\frac{dr^{d-1}}{\mathcal{R}^{d}}\mathbb{I}(0\leq r\leq\mathcal{R}),

(35)

p_{\Psi^{(d-1)}_{1}}\left(\psi^{(d-1)}\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathbb{I}\left(0\leq\psi^{(d-1)}<2\pi\right),

(36)

and for
1\leq j\leq d-2
,

p_{\Psi^{(j)}_{1}}\left(\psi^{(j)}\right)\propto\sin^{d-1-j}\left(\psi^{(j)}\right)\mathbb{I}\left(0\leq\psi^{(j)}\leq\pi\right).

(37)

From (
37
), we can compute for
1\leq k\leq d-2
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(\Psi^{(k)}_{1}\right)\right]\propto\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos\left(\psi^{(k)}\right)\sin^{d-1-k}\left(\psi^{(k)}\right)\text{d}\psi^{(k)}=0,

and thus it follows from (
33
) that

\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{X}_{1,k}\right]=0.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{X}_{1,d-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\;\middle|\;R_{1}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(\Theta^{(d-1)}_{1}\right)\;\middle|\;R_{1},\Psi^{(d-1)}_{1}\right]\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sin\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{1}\right)\;\middle|\;R_{1},\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[-\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\;\middle|\;R_{1}\right]e^{-\frac{d-1}{2}\mathcal{E}(R_{1})}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(\Psi^{(d-1)}_{1}\right)\right]\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sin\left(\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=0,

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{X}_{1,d}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\;\middle|\;R_{1}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\sin\left(\Theta^{(d-1)}_{1}\right)\;\middle|\;R_{1},\Psi^{(d-1)}_{1}\right]\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sin\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{1}\right)\;\middle|\;R_{1},\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[-\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\;\middle|\;R_{1}\right]e^{-\frac{d-1}{2}\mathcal{E}(R_{1})}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\sin\left(\Psi^{(d-1)}_{1}\right)\right]\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sin\left(\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=0,

where

\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(\Theta^{(d-1)}_{1}\right)\;\middle|\;R_{1},\Psi^{(d-1)}_{1}\right]=-e^{-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}(R_{1})}\cos\left(\Psi^{(d-1)}_{1}\right),

\mathbb{E}\left[\sin\left(\Theta^{(d-1)}_{1}\right)\;\middle|\;R_{1},\Psi^{(d-1)}_{1}\right]=-e^{-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}(R_{1})}\sin\left(\Psi^{(d-1)}_{1}\right),

(38)

are computed using the characteristic function of
\left(\Theta^{(d-1)}_{1}\;\middle|\;\Psi^{(d-1)}_{1},r_{1}\right)\overset{d}{=}\mathrm{N}\left(\Psi^{(d-1)}_{1}-\pi,\mathcal{E}(R_{1})\right)
, and

\mathbb{E}\left[\sin\left(\Psi^{(d-1)}_{1}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(\Psi^{(d-1)}_{1}\right)\right]=0

are computed using the marginal density in (
36
).

∎

Proof of Theorem
5
.

As observed in the proof of Lemma
4
, the individual estimates
\left\{\tilde{X}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. Therefore,

\displaystyle\text{CMSE}_{\mathcal{R}}(n)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i,j}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{d}\tilde{X}_{i,j}^{2}\right]+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq m}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{X}_{i,j}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{X}_{m,j}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{d}\tilde{X}_{1,j}^{2}\right]+\frac{n-1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{X}_{1,j}\right]^{2}.

Since we have shown in (
29
) that

\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{X}_{1,j}\right]=0

for
1\leq j\leq d
, we have

\displaystyle\text{CMSE}_{\mathcal{R}}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{d}\tilde{X}_{1,j}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\left(X_{1,k}+\hat{R}_{1}\cos\left(\Theta^{(k)}_{1}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\sin\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{1}\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left(X_{1,d}+\hat{R}_{1}\sin\left(\Theta^{(d-1)}_{1}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\sin\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{1}\right)\right)^{2}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\Bigg(\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{d}X_{1,j}^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}^{2}\right]+2\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{1,k}\hat{R}_{1}\cos\left(\Theta^{(k)}_{1}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\sin\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+2\mathbb{E}\left[X_{1,d}\hat{R}_{1}\sin\left(\Theta^{(d-1)}_{1}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\sin\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{1}\right)\right]\Bigg).

Proceeding as in (
30
), (
31
), and (
32
), and invoking the tower property, the independence of the hyperspherical coordinates, and the characteristic function of a normal random variable, we obtain, for
1\leq k\leq d-1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X_{1,k}\hat{R}_{1}\cos\left(\Theta^{(k)}_{1}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\sin\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[X_{1,k}\hat{R}_{1}\cos\left(\Theta^{(k)}_{1}\right)\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\sin\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{1}\right)\right)\;\middle|\;R_{1},\left\{\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right\}_{j=1}^{k}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[X_{1,k}\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\;\middle|\;R_{1}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(\Theta^{(k)}_{1}\right)\;\middle|\;R_{1},\Psi^{(k)}_{1}\right]\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sin\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{1}\right)\;\middle|\;R_{1},\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[R_{1}\cos\left(\Psi^{(k)}_{1}\right)\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\sin\left(\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right)\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\;\middle|\;R_{1}\right]\;

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\left(-e^{-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}(R_{1})}\cos\left(\Psi^{(k)}_{1}\right)\right)\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}(R_{1})}\sin\left(\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right)\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[-R_{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\;\middle|\;R_{1}\right]e^{-\frac{k}{2}\mathcal{E}(R_{1})}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\cos^{2}\left(\Psi^{(k)}_{1}\right)\right]\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sin^{2}\left(\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq-\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\;\middle|\;R_{1}\right]e^{-\frac{d-1}{2}\mathcal{E}(R_{1})}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\cos^{2}\left(\Psi^{(k)}_{1}\right)\right]\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sin^{2}\left(\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right)\right],

(39)

and similarly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X_{1,d}\hat{R}_{1}\sin\left(\Theta^{(d-1)}_{1}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\sin\left(\Theta^{(j)}_{1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\;\middle|\;R_{1}\right]e^{-\frac{d-1}{2}\mathcal{E}(R_{1})}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\sin^{2}\left(\Psi^{(d-1)}_{1}\right)\right]\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sin^{2}\left(\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right)\right],

(40)

where the negative sign is due to (
38
).

By applying the identity

\mathbb{E}\left[\sin^{2}\left(\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\cos^{2}\left(\Psi^{(j)}_{1}\right)\right]=1

iteratively to the sum of (
39
) for
1\leq k\leq d-1
and (
40
), it follows that

\displaystyle\text{CMSE}_{\mathcal{R}}(n)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1}^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}^{2}\right]-2\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\;\middle|\;R_{1}\right]e^{-\frac{d-1}{2}\mathcal{E}(R_{1})}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{d}{d+2}\mathcal{R}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}^{2}\right]-2\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1}\;\hat{R}_{1}\;e^{-\frac{d-1}{2}\mathcal{E}(R_{1})}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{d}{d+2}\mathcal{R}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}^{2}\right]-2e^{-\frac{d-1}{2}\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R})}\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1}\;\hat{R}_{1}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{d}{d+2}\mathcal{R}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}^{2}\right]-2e^{-\frac{d-1}{2}\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R})}\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{d}{d+2}\mathcal{R}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}^{2}\right]-\frac{2d\mathcal{R}}{d+1}e^{-\frac{d-1}{2}\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R})}\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\right]\right),

(41)

where
\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1}^{2}\right]=\frac{d}{d+2}\mathcal{R}^{2}
and
\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1}\right]=\frac{d}{d+1}\mathcal{R}
are computed using the marginal density (
35
), and the last inequality is because

\operatorname{Cov}(R_{1},\hat{R}_{1})=\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1}\;\hat{R}_{1}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\right]\geq 0.

(42)

To justify (
42
), we use the assumption that
\hat{R}_{i}
is a non-increasing function of the RSS
P_{i}
. Suppose that
\hat{R}_{i}=l(P_{i})=l\left(\ell(R_{i})S_{i}{(\sigma)}\right)
, where
l:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is non-increasing. Since
\ell
is also non-increasing,
\hat{R}_{i}
is a non-decreasing function of
R_{i}
, so they are non-negatively correlated. More precisely, we let
F_{\sigma}
be the distribution function of
S_{1}{(\sigma)}
. Then it follows that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}(R_{1},\hat{R}_{1})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1}\;\hat{R}_{1}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1}\;l\left(\ell(R_{1})S_{1}{(\sigma)}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[l\left(\ell(R_{1})S_{1}{(\sigma)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{0}}\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1}\;l\left(\ell(R_{1})s\right)\right]\text{d}F_{\sigma}(s)-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{0}}\mathbb{E}\left[R_{1}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[l\left(\ell(R_{1})s\right)\right]\text{d}F_{\sigma}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{0}}\operatorname{Cov}\left(R_{1},l\left(\ell(R_{1})s\right)\right)\text{d}F_{\sigma}(s)

\displaystyle\geq 0.

We now derive the bound for
\text{MSE}_{\mathcal{R}}
. Recall that

\text{MSE}_{\mathcal{R}}=\mathbb{E}[\text{CMSE}_{R}(|\Xi_{\mathcal{R}}|)]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(|\Xi_{\mathcal{R}}|=n)\cdot\text{CMSE}_{\mathcal{R}}(n).

By convention, we define
\text{CMSE}(0)_{\mathcal{R}}:=0
to reflect the case where no sensors are present in the region. Hence, the summation begins effectively at
n=1
:

\text{MSE}_{\mathcal{R}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(|\Xi_{\mathcal{R}}|=n)\cdot\text{CMSE}_{\mathcal{R}}(n).

(43)

From the bound established earlier, we have from (
41
) that

\text{MSE}_{\mathcal{R}}\leq\left(\frac{d}{d+2}\mathcal{R}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}^{2}\right]-\frac{2d\mathcal{R}}{d+1}e^{-\frac{d-1}{2}\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R})}\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\right]\right)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{P}(|\Xi_{\mathcal{R}}|=n).

Note that
|\Xi_{\mathcal{R}}|\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda V_{d}(\mathcal{R}))
, so

\mathbb{P}(|\Xi_{\mathcal{R}}|=n)=\frac{(\lambda V_{d}(\mathcal{R}))^{n}}{n!}e^{-\lambda V_{d}(\mathcal{R})},\quad n=0,1,2,\dots

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{P}(|\Xi_{\mathcal{R}}|=n)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2}{n+1}\cdot\frac{(\lambda V_{d}(\mathcal{R}))^{n}}{n!}e^{-\lambda V_{d}(\mathcal{R})}\leq\frac{2}{\lambda V_{d}(\mathcal{R})}.

Therefore, we obtain:

\text{MSE}_{\mathcal{R}}\leq\frac{2}{\lambda V_{d}(\mathcal{R})}\left(\frac{d}{d+2}\mathcal{R}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}^{2}\right]-\frac{2d\mathcal{R}}{d+1}e^{-\frac{d-1}{2}\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{R})}\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{R}_{1}\right]\right).

∎
