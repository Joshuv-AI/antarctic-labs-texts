On framed rook algebras
Diego Arcis Jorge Espinoza Marcelo Flores

Abstract
We introduce and study the framed rook algebra, a structure that unifies two significant generalizations of the Iwahori–Hecke algebra. The first one, introduced by Solomon, extends the Hecke algebra to the full matrix monoid, yielding the rook monoid algebra. The second one, developed by Yokonuma, replaces the Borel subgroup with the unipotent subgroup, resulting in the Yokonuma–Hecke algebra. Our concrete algebra is constructed from the double cosets of the unipotent subgroup within the full matrix monoid. We show that this double coset decomposition is indexed by the framed symmetric inverse monoid. We also define the Rook Yokonuma–Hecke algebra as an abstract structure using generators and relations. We then prove the main isomorphism theorem, which establishes that this abstract algebra is isomorphic to the framed rook algebra under a specific parameter specialization. To complete our characterization, we provide a faithful representation on a tensor space and establish a linear basis for the Rook Yokonuma–Hecke algebra.

Introduction

The Iwahori–Hecke algebra of type
A_{n-1}
, denoted by
\mathcal{H}_{n}(u)
, is a central object in algebraic combinatorics, representation theory, and knot theory. Originally, it arose from the study of finite Chevalley groups
[
23
]
. Classically, let
G=\mathbf{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
be the general linear group over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and let
B
be the standard Borel subgroup of upper triangular matrices. The algebra
\mathcal{H}_{n}(u)
can be realized as the endomorphism algebra
\mathsf{End}_{G}(\mathsf{Ind}_{B}^{G}(1))
of the permutation representation of
G
on the cosets of
B

[
11
]
. A fundamental result, based on the Bruhat decomposition of
G
, establishes that the double cosets of
B
in
G
are indexed by the Weyl group
W\simeq\mathcal{S}_{n}
, the symmetric group on
n
letters
[
4
,
5
]
.

This classical construction has been generalized in two distinct but significant directions over the last decades.

The first direction involves extending the underlying algebraic structure from a group to a monoid
[
38
]
. In his seminal works, Solomon investigated the Iwahori algebra associated with the full monoid of
n\times n
matrices,
M:=\mathbf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, rather than the group of units
G

[
40
]
. He considered the double cosets of the Borel subgroup
B
within
M
and demonstrated that the decomposition
M=\bigsqcup_{\sigma}B\sigma B
is indexed by the
rook monoid

\mathcal{R}
, also known as the symmetric inverse monoid
\mathcal{I}_{n}

[
37
]
. The resulting algebra, often referred to as the rook monoid algebra, serves as a semigroup analogue to the Hecke algebra and has been extensively studied for its representation theory and combinatorial properties
[
40
,
41
,
19
,
2
,
18
]
. In
[
42
]
, Solomon gives a presentation for the rook monoid algebra and provides a tensor representation for such algebra. Subsequently, in
[
17
]
, Halverson provides an alternative presentation for this algebra and studies its representation theory.

The second direction involves modifying the observing subgroup. In 1967, Yokonuma introduced a generalization of the Hecke algebra by replacing the Borel subgroup
B
with its unipotent radical
U
, where
B=UT
and
T
is the maximal torus of diagonal matrices
[
47
]
. The resulting algebra, known as the
Yokonuma–Hecke algebra

\mathcal{Y}_{d,n}(u)
, arises from the double cosets in
G

[
44
]
. Unlike the Iwahori–Hecke algebra, the Yokonuma–Hecke algebra supports a modular structure related to the ”framing” of the underlying braid groups, which has led to significant applications in representation theory
[
27
,
6
,
12
,
14
]
and knot theory. More precisely, the Iwahori–Hecke algebra is used to derive the well known HOMFLY–PT polynomial
[
25
]
. On the other hand, the Yokonuma–Hecke algebra can also be used to derive invariants of classical and framed links
[
28
,
8
,
9
,
16
,
7
]
. It is worth noting that the invariant obtained from
\mathcal{Y}_{d,n}(u)
distinguishes pairs of links that are not distinguished by the HOMFLY–PT polynomial.

Motivated for this, Juyumaya and Lambropoulou introduce the notion of
framization of an algebra
in
[
29
]
, which consists in adding framing generators to the algebra with the aim of finding new and powerful invariants of classical links by using the algebra obtained from this procedure. The Yokonuma–Hecke algebra is the prototype of a framization; indeed this algebra can be thought of as a framization of the Iwahori–Hecke algebra
\mathcal{H}_{n}(u)
. A more formal definition of this procedure was given recently in
[
1
]
.

In this paper, we propose a synthesis of these two generalizations. We construct and study the algebra arising from the double cosets of the unipotent subgroup
U
within the full matrix monoid
M
. We refer to this concrete structure as the
framed rook algebra
, denoted by
H(M,U)
. Furthermore, we introduce an abstract algebra version, defined by generators and relations, which we call the
Rook Yokonuma–Hecke algebra
, denoted by
\mathcal{RY}_{d,n}(u)
. This new algebra can be thought as a framization of the rook monoid algebra in the sense of
[
1
]
. In particular, the algebra
\mathcal{RY}_{d,n}(u)
generalizes both the rook monoid algebra and the Yokonuma–Hecke algebra.

Our approach is motivated by the observation that the transition from the Borel subgroup
B
to the unipotent subgroup
U
introduces an action of the torus
T\simeq(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})^{n}
on the double cosets
U\sigma U
. This action effectively ”colors” or ”frames” the combinatorial objects that index them. While Solomon’s construction relies on the rook monoid
\mathcal{R}
, we show that the decomposition of the full matrix monoid
M
into double cosets is naturally indexed by a generalized version of this monoid, which we call the
generalized rook monoid

\mathcal{Q}
(Subsection
3.2
). As we establish,
\mathcal{Q}
is isomorphic to a combinatorial structure given by coloured set partitions, which we call the
framed symmetric inverse monoid

\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathcal{I}_{n})
(Subsection
2.5
). Geometrically, elements of this monoid can be visualized as partial permutations labelled by elements of the cyclic group
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

1.

We explicitly define the framed rook algebra
H(M,U)
as a convolution algebra of
U
-invariant functions on
M
, providing a basis indexed by the generalized rook monoid
\mathcal{Q}
.

2.

We introduce an abstract algebra by generators and relations, which we call the
Rook Yokonuma–Hecke algebra
, denoted by
\mathcal{RY}_{d,n}(u)
. This presentation unifies the defining relations of the classical Yokonuma–Hecke algebra with the idempotent and projection relations characteristic of rook monoid algebras.

3.

We prove a main isomorphism theorem (Theorem
4.7
), establishing that the concrete algebra
H(M,U)
is isomorphic to the abstract algebra
\mathcal{RY}_{d,n}(u)
under the specific parameter specialization
d=q-1
and
u=q
. This result parallels Solomon’s characterization of the Iwahori algebra of
M
.

4.

Finally, we construct a faithful tensor space representation for the Rook Yokonuma–Hecke algebra (Theorem
4.12
), extending the classical tensor representations of the rook monoid algebra. Furthermore, we establish a linear basis for the Rook Yokonuma–Hecke algebra (Theorem
4.14
).

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
establishes the combinatorial framework necessary for our construction. We review partition monoids (Subsection
2
) and formally introduce the concept of framed monoids (Subsection
2.4
). Specifically, we define the framed symmetric inverse monoid (Subsection
2.5
), denoted by
\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathcal{I}_{n})
, and provide a presentation by generators and relations (Proposition
2.5
).

Section
3
focuses on the matrix interpretation of these combinatorial structures. We recall the classic rook monoid (Subsection
3.1
) and define the generalized rook monoid
\mathcal{Q}
as a submonoid of the full matrix monoid
M
(Subsection
3.1
). A key observation in this section is the establishment of an explicit isomorphism between
\mathcal{Q}
and the framed symmetric inverse monoid
\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathcal{I}_{n})
, which allows us to translate geometric properties of diagrams into matrix operations. Furthermore, we analyze the double coset decomposition of
M
with respect to the unipotent subgroup
U
(Subsection
3.4
), showing that these double cosets are naturally indexed by elements of
\mathcal{Q}
. We also study the length function and the structure of double classes (Subsection
3.3
). We extend the classical length function of the rook monoid to the generalized setting and analyze the convolution product of double cosets, providing explicit multiplication formulas that are crucial for the algebra structure.

Section
4
contains the main results of the paper. First, we define the concrete framed rook algebra
H(M,U)
as the algebra of
U
-bi-invariant functions on
M
. Then, we introduce the Rook Yokonuma–Hecke algebra
\mathcal{RY}_{d,n}
as an abstract algebra defined by generators and relations (Subsection
4.1
). We prove our main isomorphism theorem, demonstrating that
H(M,U)
is isomorphic to the specialization of
\mathcal{RY}_{d,n}(u)
at
d=q-1
and
u=q
. We construct a faithful tensor space representation for
\mathcal{RY}_{d,n}
and use it to establish a standard basis for the algebra, proving that its dimension is equal to the cardinality of the generalized rook monoid (Subsection
4.2
). Finally we give some other presentations for the algebra
\mathcal{RY}_{d,n}
(Subsection
4.3
).

, we define

. For integers

is denoted by

. Furthermore, given a binary relation

.

2
Framed monoids

In this section, we establish the combinatorial framework necessary to construct the framed rook algebra. Our approach begins with a review of set partitions and the partition monoid
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
emphasizing their diagrammatic representation via strand diagrams. We recall the concept of coloured set partitions and introduce framed monoids by attaching weights from the cyclic group
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
to the arcs of these diagrams. This construction leads to the definition of the framed symmetric inverse monoid, denoted by
\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathcal{I}_{n})
. We conclude the section by providing a presentation of
\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathcal{I}_{n})
via generators and relations, a result that is crucial for establishing the isomorphism theorem in Section
4
.

Throughout this section, let
A
and
B
denote nonempty sets of integers and
n
a positive integer. We refer to singletons and to sets containing exactly two integers as
points
and
arcs
, respectively. See Figure
1
.

2.1
Set partitions

A
set partition
of
A
is a collection of pairwise disjoint nonempty subsets of
A
, called
blocks
, whose union is
A
. The collection of all set partitions of
A
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}(A)
, and we write
\mathcal{P}([n])
as
\mathcal{P}_{n}
. It is well-known that the cardinality is
|\mathcal{P}_{n}|=\mathfrak{b}_{n}
, the
n
th
Bell number

[
22
,
A000110
]
.

A set partition
I=\{I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}\}
of
[n]
is often represented in its
canonical form
as
I=(I_{a_{1}},\ldots,I_{a_{k}})
, ordered such that
\min(I_{a_{1}})<\cdots<\min(I_{a_{k}})
. Such partitions are commonly depicted by
arc diagrams
, in which elements within the same block are transitively connected by arcs. See Figure
2
. For
i,j\in[n]
, we write
i\sim_{I}j
to indicate that
i
and
j
belong to the same block of
I
. Following
[
13
, Section 2]
, we denote by
[i]_{I}
the unique block of
I
containing
i
. Thus,
i\sim_{I}j
if and only if
[i]_{I}=[j]_{I}
.

Figure 2
:
Arc diagram of a set partition of
\mathcal{P}_{7}
.

For
I,J\in\mathcal{P}(A)
, we say that
I
is
finer
than
J
, or equivalently that
J
is
coarser
than
I
, denoted by
I\prec J
, if and only if every block of
J
is a union of blocks of
I
. This relation defines a partial order that endows
\mathcal{P}(A)
with the structure of a lattice, whose
join
operation is denoted by
\vee
. The pair
(\mathcal{P}(A),\vee)
forms a semigroup with identity element
(\{a\}\mid a\in A)
, referred to as the

of
A
.

As established in
[
15
, Theorem 2]
, the monoid
(\mathcal{P}_{n},\vee)
admits a presentation by generators
e_{i,j}
for
i,j\in[n]
with
i<j
, subject to the relations:

(2.1)

e_{i,j}^{2}=e_{i,j};\qquad e_{i,j}e_{r,s}=e_{r,s}e_{i,j};\qquad e_{i,j}e_{i,k}=e_{i,j}e_{j,k}=e_{i,k}e_{j,k}.

Here,
e_{i,j}
denotes the set partition of
[n]
whose only non-singleton block is
\{i,j\}
. See Figure
3
.

Figure 3
:
Generators of
\mathcal{P}_{4}
.

Every set partition of a subset
X\subset A
can be viewed as a set partition of
A
by adjoining singleton blocks for each element of
A\setminus X
. This construction defines a monomorphism from
\mathcal{P}(X)
into
\mathcal{P}(A)
. Accordingly, given a set
B
and a pair
(I,J)\in\mathcal{P}(A)\times\mathcal{P}(B)
, we write
I\vee J
to denote the join of
I
and
J
, regarded as set partitions of
A\cup B
. Conversely, for any
I\in\mathcal{P}(A)
, we denote by

the
restriction
of

to
X
, defined as the set partition of

whose blocks are the nonempty intersections of the blocks of
I
with
X
.

Partition monoids

The collection of set partitions of

It is also known that

corresponds to the simple transposition exchanging

is the set partition where

2.2.2
Partial permutations

The collection of set partitions of

\displaystyle r_{i}^{2}=r_{i};\qquad r_{i}r_{j}=r_{j}r_{i};

\displaystyle s_{i}r_{i}=r_{i+1}s_{i};\qquad r_{i}s_{i}r_{i}=r_{i}r_{i+1};\qquad s_{i}r_{j}=r_{j}s_{i},\quad j\not\in\{i,i+1\}.

Remark 2.1

3
Rook monoids

In this section, we focus on the matrix interpretation of the combinatorial structures established in Section
2
. We begin by recalling the classical rook monoid and introducing the generalized rook monoid
\mathcal{Q}
as a submonoid of the full matrix monoid
M
.
A central result of this section is the establishment of an explicit isomorphism between
\mathcal{Q}
and the framed symmetric inverse monoid
\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathcal{I}_{n})
. Furthermore, we analyze the double coset decomposition of
M
with respect to the unipotent subgroup
U
, demonstrating that these double cosets are naturally indexed by elements of
\mathcal{Q}
. Finally, we extend the classical length function to this generalized setting and study the convolution product of double cosets, providing the necessary multiplication formulas for the algebra structure defined in the sequel.

Let
M=\mathbf{M}_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
be the monoid of
n\times n
matrices over the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. For each
r\in[n]_{0}
, we denote by
M^{r}
the subset of

consisting of matrices of rank
r
. If
A\subset M
, we set
A^{r}:=A\cap M^{r}
.

For
i,j\in[n]
, let
E_{i,j}
be the
n\times n
matrix with all entries equal to zero, except for a
1
in position
(i,j)
. Every matrix
\sigma=(\sigma_{i,\,j})\in M
can be written uniquely as a linear combination of these elementary matrices:

(3.1)

\sigma=\sum_{i,\,j\in[n]}\sigma_{i,\,j}E_{i,j}.

The matrix product is given by the following rule for the elementary matrices:

(3.2)

E_{i,j}E_{h,k}=\begin{cases}E_{i,k}&\text{if }j=h\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

3.1

The

consisting of

3.2
The generalized rook monoid

The
n
th
generalized rook monoid

[
26
, Definición 4.0.1]
is the submonoid
\mathcal{Q}\subset M
consisting of those
n\times n
matrices having at most one nonzero entry in each row and column.

For
\sigma\in\mathcal{Q}
as in (
3.1
), we define the following subsets of indices:
I(\sigma)\subset[n]
is the set of indices
i
such that the
i
th row of
\sigma
is nonzero, and
J(\sigma)\subset[n]
is the set of indices
j
such that the
j
th column of
\sigma
is nonzero. We denote by
\sigma^{\star}
the
|I(\sigma)|\times|J(\sigma)|
matrix obtained from
\sigma
by removing all zero rows and zero columns. Crucially, we define the
n\times n
matrix
\tilde{\sigma}=(\tilde{\sigma}_{i,j})
by setting
\tilde{\sigma}_{i,j}=1
if
\sigma_{i,j}\neq 0
and
\tilde{\sigma}_{i,j}=0
otherwise. This matrix essentially records the positions of the nonzero entries of
\sigma
. For instance,

Observe that

is the preimage of
\mathcal{R}
under the map
\sigma\mapsto\tilde{\sigma}

\mathcal{Q}^{r}

\mathcal{R}^{r}

[

\sigma=(\sigma_{i,j})\in\mathcal{Q}
and
i\in I(\sigma)

i\sigma
the unique element in
J(\sigma)
such that
\sigma_{i,i\sigma}
is nonzero. Similarly, for each
j\in J(\sigma)
we denote by
\sigma j
the unique element in
I(\sigma)
such that
\sigma_{\sigma j,j}
is nonzero. With this notation,
\sigma
admits the decompositions:

\sigma=\sum_{i\in I(\sigma)}\sigma_{i,i\sigma}E_{i,i\sigma}=\sum_{j\in J(\sigma)}\sigma_{\sigma j,j}E_{\sigma j,j}.

Therefore, analogously to
[
40
, Equation (3.9)]
, we obtain:

(3.4)

E_{i,j}\sigma=\begin{cases}\sigma_{j,\,j\sigma}E_{i,j\sigma}&\text{if }j\in I(\sigma)\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad\sigma E_{i,j}=\begin{cases}\sigma_{\sigma i,i}E_{\sigma i,j}&\text{if }i\in J(\sigma)\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

\sigma_{i,j}

\sigma_{i,j}
is nonzero. This correspondence yields an isomorphism between
\mathcal{Q}
and the
n

3.3
Length function

The
length function
of the rook monoid extends naturally to the generalized rook monoid by setting the length of
\sigma\in\mathcal{Q}
to be the length of its support matrix
\tilde{\sigma}
, that is,
\ell(\sigma):=\ell(\tilde{\sigma})
.

Via the isomorphism
\mathcal{Q}\simeq\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathcal{I}_{n})
described above, the notion of inversion for partial permutations carries over naturally to matrices. Specifically, an
inversion
of
\sigma\in\mathcal{Q}
is a pair
(i,j)
with
i<j
such that
i,j\in I(\sigma)
and
i\sigma>j\sigma
. The set of inversions of
\sigma
is denoted by
{\mathsf{inv}}(\sigma)
, and we write
n(\sigma):=|{\mathsf{inv}}(\sigma)|
. The number of inversions is preserved under transposition, that is,
n(\sigma_{*})=n(\sigma)
.

A
left-inversion
(resp.
right-inversion
) of
\sigma
is a pair
(i,j)
with
i<j
such that
i\not\in I(\sigma)
and
j\in I(\sigma)
(resp.
i\in J(\sigma)
and
j\not\in J(\sigma)
). We denote by
{\mathsf{inv}}_{L}(\sigma)
and
{\mathsf{inv}}_{R}(\sigma)
the sets of left- and right-inversions, respectively, and define

m_{L}(\sigma)=|{\mathsf{inv}}_{L}(\sigma)|,\qquad m_{R}(\sigma)=|{\mathsf{inv}}_{R}(\sigma)|,\qquad m(\sigma)=m_{L}(\sigma)+m_{R}(\sigma).

Since the length function satisfies
\ell(\sigma)=\ell(\tilde{\sigma})
, the following result is an immediate consequence of
[
40
, Proposition 2.43 and Lemma 2.25]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

For every
\sigma\in\mathcal{Q}
, we have
\ell(\sigma)=n(\sigma)+m(\sigma)
. Furthermore, if
\sigma\in\mathcal{Q}^{r}
, then

m_{L}(\sigma)=\sum_{i\in I(\sigma)}(i-1)-\frac{r(r-1)}{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad m_{R}(\sigma)=\sum_{j\in J(\sigma)}(n-j)-\frac{r(r-1)}{2}.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
p_{1},\ldots,p_{h+k}
and
q_{1},\ldots,q_{h+k}
be indices in
[n]
, and let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{h+k}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
. Define the matrix

, where

\sigma=\alpha_{1}E_{p_{1},q_{1}}+\cdots+\alpha_{h}E_{p_{h},q_{h}}\quad\text{and}\quad\varsigma=\alpha_{h+1}E_{p_{h+1},q_{h+1}}+\cdots+\alpha_{h+k}E_{p_{h+k},{q_{h+k}}}.

If the pairs
(p_{i},q_{i})
are all distinct for

, then
\tilde{\tau}=\tilde{\sigma}+\tilde{\varsigma}
, where

\tilde{\sigma}=E_{p_{1},q_{1}}+\cdots+E_{p_{h},q_{h}}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{\varsigma}=E_{p_{h+1},q_{h+1}}+\cdots+E_{p_{h+k},{q_{h+k}}}.

As each

occurs only once in
\sigma
, then
\alpha_{i}=\sigma_{p_{i},q_{i}}
for all

\tilde{\varsigma}=E_{p_{h+1},q_{h+1}}+\cdots+E_{p_{h+k},{q_{h+k}}}

.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

\sigma\in\mathcal{R}
and
\varsigma\in M

1.

\tilde{\tau}=\sigma\tilde{\varsigma}

4.1
The Rook Yokonuma–Hecke algebra

In what follows,
\mathbb{K}
denotes a field of characteristic zero and
u
denotes an indeterminate.

Given integers
n,d\geq 1
, the
Rook Yokonuma–Hecke algebra
is the
\mathbb{K}(u)
-algebra
\mathcal{RY}_{d,n}(u)
presented by generators
\mathsf{T}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{T}_{n-1}
,
\mathsf{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{F}_{n}
, and
\mathsf{N}
, subject to the relations:

(4.12)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{i}\mathsf{T}_{j}\mathsf{T}_{i}=\mathsf{T}_{j}\mathsf{T}_{i}\mathsf{T}_{j},\quad|i-j|=1;\qquad\mathsf{T}_{i}\mathsf{T}_{j}=\mathsf{T}_{j}\mathsf{T}_{i},\quad|i-j|>1;

(4.13)

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{i}^{d}=1;\qquad\mathsf{F}_{i}\mathsf{F}_{j}=\mathsf{F}_{j}\mathsf{F}_{i};

(4.14)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{i}^{2}=u+(u-1)\mathsf{F}_{i}^{\frac{d^{2}-d}{2}}\mathsf{E}_{i}\mathsf{T}_{i};\qquad\mathsf{T}_{i}\mathsf{F}_{j}=\mathsf{F}_{js_{i}}\mathsf{T}_{i};

(4.15)

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{i}\mathsf{N}=\mathsf{N}\mathsf{F}_{i+1},\quad i<n;\qquad\mathsf{N}\mathsf{F}_{1}=\mathsf{F}_{n}\mathsf{N}=\mathsf{N};

(4.16)

\displaystyle\mathsf{N}^{i+1}\mathsf{T}_{i}=u\mathsf{N}^{i+1};\qquad\mathsf{T}_{i}\mathsf{N}^{n-i+1}=u\mathsf{N}^{n-i+1};

(4.17)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{i}\mathsf{N}=\mathsf{N}\mathsf{T}_{i+1};\qquad\mathsf{N}\mathsf{T}_{1}\cdots\mathsf{T}_{n-1}\mathsf{N}=u^{n-1}\mathsf{N};

where

(4.18)

\mathsf{E}_{i}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\mathsf{F}_{i}^{k}\mathsf{F}_{i+1}^{-k}.

By (
3.9
), we note that
\mathsf{F}_{i}^{\frac{d^{2}-d}{2}}=\mathsf{F}_{i}^{\,\frac{d}{2}}
when
d
is even, and
\mathsf{F}_{i}^{\frac{d^{2}-d}{2}}=1
otherwise. Moreover, each
\mathsf{T}_{i}
is invertible; more precisely,

(4.19)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{i}^{-1}=u^{-1}\mathsf{T}_{i}-\left(1-u^{-1}\right)\mathsf{F}_{i}^{\frac{d^{2}-d}{2}}\mathsf{E}_{i}.

Applying (
4.17
), most relations in (
4.16
) become superfluous and can be reduced to the specialized relations:

(4.20)

\mathsf{N}^{2}\mathsf{T}_{1}=u\mathsf{N}^{2};\qquad\mathsf{T}_{n-1}\mathsf{N}^{2}=u\mathsf{N}^{2}.

Indeed, we have

\mathsf{N}^{i+1}\mathsf{T}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{092}}}{{=}}\mathsf{N}^{2}\mathsf{T}_{1}\mathsf{N}^{i-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{107}}}{{=}}u\mathsf{N}^{i+1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathsf{T}_{i}\mathsf{N}^{n-i+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{092}}}{{=}}\mathsf{N}^{n-i-1}\mathsf{T}_{n-1}\mathsf{N}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{091}}}{{=}}u\mathsf{N}^{n-i+1}.

Remark 4.5
.

The quadratic relation in (
4.14
) differs from the original relation in
[
47
]
. Our formulation is instead inspired by the generic presentation developed in
[

]
. Specifically, one can recover the original Yokonuma-type quadratic structure by defining a new set of generators:

\mathsf{T}_{i}^{\prime}:=\mathsf{F}_{i+1}^{\frac{d^{2}-d}{2}}\mathsf{T}_{i}.

More precisely, with these generators we obtain the following relation:

\mathsf{T}_{i}^{\prime}\,{}^{2}=u\left(\mathsf{F}_{i}\mathsf{F}_{i+1}\right)^{\frac{d^{2}-d}{2}}+(u-1)\mathsf{E}_{i}\mathsf{T}_{i}^{\prime}.

Our next goal is to construct a spanning set for the algebra
\mathcal{RY}_{d,n}(u)
. See Theorem
4.6
. Before defining our proposed spanning set, we first introduce some notation and preliminary definitions.

Given indexes
j,k\in[n]
with
j\leq k
, and a sequence

, we define

\mathsf{T}_{k,\,j}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }k=j\\
\mathsf{T}_{k-1}\cdots\mathsf{T}_{j}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathsf{F}_{\mathbf{m}}=\mathsf{F}_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots\mathsf{F}_{r}^{m_{r}}.

Additionally, for
\omega\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
and
s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{l}}
a reduced expression for
\omega
, we set
\mathsf{T}_{\omega}=\mathsf{T}_{i_{1}}\cdots\mathsf{T}_{i_{l}}
. Matsumoto’s theorem
[
34
]
ensures that this definition does not depend on the choice of the reduced expression.

For a subset
A=\{a_{1}<\cdots<a_{k}\}\subseteq[n]
, we define

\mathsf{T}_{A}=\mathsf{T}_{a_{1,1}}\ldots\mathsf{T}_{a_{k,k}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{\mathsf{T}}_{A}=\mathsf{T}_{n-k+1,a_{1}}\ldots\mathsf{T}_{n,a_{k}}.

Observe that
\mathsf{T}_{A}=\overline{\mathsf{T}}_{A}=1
whenever
|A|=n
.

Set
\mathsf{C}_{n,0}=\{\mathsf{N}^{n}\}
, and for
r\in[n]
we define

(4.21)

\mathsf{C}_{n,r}=\left\{\mathsf{T}_{A}\mathsf{F}_{\mathbf{m}}\mathsf{T}_{\omega}\mathsf{N}^{n-r}\overline{\mathsf{T}}_{B}\mid|A|=|B|=r,\,\mathbf{m}\in(\Z/d\Z)^{r},\,\omega\in\mathcal{S}_{r}\right\}.

We then define our proposed spanning set as
{\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{n}=\bigsqcup_{r=0}^{n}\mathsf{C}_{n,r}}
. Observe that

(4.22)

|\mathsf{C}_{n,r}|=d^{r}\binom{n}{r}^{2}r!,\quad\text{and hence}\quad|\mathsf{C}_{n}|=\sum_{r=0}^{n}d^{r}\binom{n}{r}^{2}r!.

The following theorem will be proved in Subsubsection

.

The set

Representation on tensors

In the sequel, we set
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{K}(u^{\frac{1}{2}})
. For each
s\in[d]
, let
V_{s}
be the free
\mathbb{S}
-module spanned by
\{v_{i}^{s}\mid i\in[n]\}
. Let
V=\bigoplus_{s\in[d]}V_{s}
and
U=\mathbb{S}\oplus V
. In particular, if
v_{0}
denotes the identity of
\mathbb{S}
, then
\{v_{i}^{s}\}\cup\{v_{0}\}
is a
\mathbb{S}
-basis of
U
.

Now, we define three operators
\mathbf{F}\in\mathsf{End}_{\mathbb{S}}(U)
,
\mathbf{E}\in\mathsf{End}_{\mathbb{S}}(U^{\otimes 2})
and
\mathbf{T}\in\mathsf{End}_{\mathbb{S}}(U^{\otimes 2})
, as follows:

(4.25)

\mathbf{F}(v_{i}^{s})=\xi^{s}v_{i}^{s},\qquad\mathbf{F}(v_{0})=v_{0},

(4.26)

\mathbf{E}(x\otimes y)=\begin{cases}v_{i}^{t}\otimes v_{j}^{s}&\text{if }x=v_{i}^{s},\;y=v_{j}^{t}\text{ and }s=t\\
v_{0}\otimes v_{0}&\text{if }x=v_{0}\text{ and }y=v_{0}\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and

(4.27)

\mathbf{T}(x\otimes y)=\begin{cases}u^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot v_{j}^{t}\otimes v_{i}^{s}&\text{if }x=v_{i}^{s},\;y=v_{j}^{t}\text{ and }s\neq t\\
(-1)^{(d-1)s}u\cdot v_{i}^{s}\otimes v_{j}^{t}&\text{if }x=v_{i}^{s},\;y=v_{j}^{t},\;s=t,\;i=j\\
(-1)^{(d-1)s}u^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot v_{j}^{t}\otimes v_{i}^{s}&\text{if }x=v_{i}^{s},\;y=v_{j}^{t},\;s=t,\;i>j\\
(-1)^{(d-1)s}(u-1)\cdot v_{i}^{s}\otimes v_{j}^{t}+(-1)^{(d-1)s}u^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot v_{j}^{t}\otimes v_{i}^{s}&\text{if }x=v_{i}^{s},\;y=v_{j}^{s},\;s=t,\;i<j\\
u\cdot v_{0}\otimes v_{0}&\text{if }x=v_{0},\;y=v_{0}\\
u^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot y\otimes x&\text{if }x=v_{0},\;y\in V\text{ or }x\in V,\;y=v_{0}\end{cases}

where
\xi
is a primitive
d
th root of unity.

We extend them to operators
\mathbf{F}_{i}
,
\mathbf{E}_{i}
and
\mathbf{T}_{i}
acting on the tensor space
U^{\otimes n}
by letting
\mathbf{F}
act in the
i
th factor,
\mathbf{E}
in the
i
th and
(i+1)
st factors and
\mathbf{T}
in the
i
th and
(i+1)
st factors, respectively.

It was shown in
[
14
, Lemma 6]
that

\mathbf{E}_{i}=\dfrac{1}{d}\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\mathbf{F}_{i}^{k}\mathbf{F}_{i+1}^{-k}.

In order to define the operator
\mathbf{N}
associated with the generator
\mathsf{N}
of
\mathcal{RY}_{d,n}(u)
, for
x=x_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{n}\in U^{\otimes n}
, we set
\varepsilon(x)=|\{k\mid x_{k}\in V\}|
, which counts the number of non-scalar components in the tensor.

The operator
\mathbf{N}
on
U^{\otimes n}
is then defined by

\mathbf{N}(x_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{n})=u^{\frac{\varepsilon(x)}{2}}x_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{n}\otimes x_{1}^{0},

where
x_{1}^{0}=v_{0}
whenever
x_{1}\in\mathbb{S}
, and
x_{1}^{0}=0
whenever
x_{1}\in V
.

Theorem 4.12
.

There is a representation
\rho
of
\mathcal{RY}_{d,n}(u)
in
U^{\otimes n}
given by
\mathsf{F}_{i}\mapsto\mathbf{F}_{i}
,
\mathsf{T}_{i}\mapsto\mathbf{T}_{i}
and
\mathsf{N}\mapsto\mathbf{N}
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that the operators defined above satisfy the defining relations in (
4.12
) to (
4.17
). We first observe that relations in (
4.13
) follow immediately from the commutativity of the operators
\mathbf{F}_{i}
. On the other hand, the second relation in (
4.14
) is verified exactly as in the proof of
[
14
, Theorem 8]
, upon identifying our operators
\mathbf{T}
and
\mathbf{F}
with
\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{T}
, respectively.

Similarly for relations (
4.15
) to (
4.17
), involving
\mathbf{T}
and
\mathbf{N}
, the proof is the same as in
[
42
, Lemma 3.8]
. Indeed, the argument depends only on the structure of
\mathbf{N}
, which is the same as the operator
N
in
[
42
, Equation (3.6)]
, and on the property that
\mathbf{T}
maps
v_{i}^{s}\otimes v_{j}^{t}
into the
\mathbb{S}
-submodule spanned by
v_{i}^{s}\otimes v_{j}^{t}
and
v_{j}^{t}\otimes v_{i}^{s}
, that is,
\mathbf{T}(v_{i}^{s}\otimes v_{j}^{t})=\alpha\,v_{i}^{s}\otimes v_{j}^{t}+\beta\,v_{j}^{t}\otimes v_{i}^{s}
for suitable scalars
\alpha,\beta
. This is exactly the case for our
\mathbf{T}
, so the proofs of relations (
4.15
) to (
4.17
) go through unchanged.

To verify the quadratic relation in (
4.12
) it suffices to consider a tensor
x\in U\otimes U
. We split into cases:

If
x=v_{0}\otimes v_{0}
, it follows by

\mathbf{T}^{2}(v_{0}\otimes v_{0})=u^{2}(v_{0}\otimes v_{0})=(v_{0}\otimes v_{0})u+(u^{2}-u)(v_{0}\otimes v_{0})=(v_{0}\otimes v_{0})u+(u-1)\mathbf{F}^{\frac{d^{2}-d}{2}}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{T}(v_{0}\otimes v_{0}).

If
x=v_{i}^{s}\otimes v_{j}^{s}
and
d
even. Whenever
s
is even, the quadratic relation reduces to the classical rook case, and our operator
\mathbf{T}
coincides with the operator
T
in
[
42
, Equation (3.2)]
. Hence it holds. Whenever
s
is odd, the quadratic relation becomes
\mathbf{T}^{2}(v_{i}^{s}\otimes v_{j}^{s})=(v_{i}^{s}\otimes v_{j}^{s})u-(u-1)\mathbf{T}(v_{i}^{s}\otimes v_{j}^{s})
. Furthermore, in this case the operator
\mathbf{T}
coincides with
-T
. On the other hand, if
d
is odd, then
\mathbf{T}
again coincides with the operator
T
, so the equality holds in this case as well.

If
x=v_{i}^{s}\otimes v_{j}^{t}
with
s\neq t
, or
x=v_{0}\otimes v_{i}^{s}
, or
x=v_{i}^{s}\otimes v_{0}
, then
\mathbf{T}^{2}(x)=ux
, and
\mathbf{E}
vanishes on them, since
\mathbf{T}
acts as
u^{\frac{1}{2}}
times the flip. Consequently
\mathbf{T}^{2}(x)=ux=ux+(u-1)\mathbf{F}^{\frac{d^{2}-d}{2}}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{T}(x)
.

We now verify the relations in (
4.12
).

First, we verify the commutation relation for distant generators; it suffices to work on
U^{\otimes 4}
. In this setting
\mathbf{T}_{i}
and
\mathbf{T}_{j}
act on disjoint tensor factors, hence they commute, and the identity
\mathbf{T}_{i}\mathbf{T}_{j}=\mathbf{T}_{j}\mathbf{T}_{i}
follows immediately.

To verify the relation
\mathbf{T}_{i}\mathbf{T}_{i+1}\mathbf{T}_{i}=\mathbf{T}_{i+1}\mathbf{T}_{i}\mathbf{T}_{i+1}
, it suffices to work on
U^{\otimes 3}
. We split into cases:

If the upper indices are all distinct, more generally, whenever a pair has distinct upper indices or involves a scalar, each
\mathbf{T}
on its adjacent pair acts as
q^{\frac{1}{2}}
times the flip. Both sides then implement the same permutation of the three tensor factors, multiplied by the same overall scalar, so the relation holds immediately.

If the upper indices are all equal, say
s
, then on
V_{s}^{\otimes 3}
we have
\mathbf{T}_{k}=(-1)^{(d-1)s}T_{k}
for
k=i,i+1
, where
T
is the classical rook operator. Hence both sides are
(-1)^{3(d-1)s}
times the classical rook relation and the sign cancels on both sides. Similarly, for the case
v_{0}\otimes v_{0}\otimes v_{0}
the relation follows from the classical rook case.

For the mixed pattern with two equal upper indices and one different, by symmetry it suffices to consider a representative vector
x=u_{i}\otimes u_{j}\otimes v
, where each
u_{\ell}
denotes either
v_{\ell}^{\,s}
(for a fixed
s
) or
v_{0}
, and
v
denotes either
v_{k}^{\,t}
with
t\neq s
or
v_{0}
, as appropriate. In all these cases the operator
\mathbf{T}
acts on the first two factors by
\mathbf{T}(w_{i}\otimes w_{j})=\alpha w_{i}\otimes w_{j}+\beta w_{j}\otimes w_{i}
with
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{S}
. A direct use of the defining rules of
\mathbf{T}
then gives

\mathbf{T}_{1}\mathbf{T}_{2}\mathbf{T}_{1}(x)=u\alpha v\otimes w_{i}\otimes w_{j}+u\beta v\otimes w_{j}\otimes w_{i}=\mathbf{T}_{2}\mathbf{T}_{1}\mathbf{T}_{2}(x).

Therefore the relation holds in this case as well. By symmetry, the other placements of the distinct upper index are analogous, so the result follows.
∎

We now use this tensor representation to prove that the spanning set

\mathsf{C}_{n}
of
\mathcal{RY}_{d,n}(u)
in Theorem
4.6
is in fact a basis. For this purpose we introduce the notion of support for tensors in
U^{\otimes n}
and establish a few elementary properties.

We define the
support
of a tensor
x=x_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{n}\in U^{\otimes n}
by
\mathsf{supp}(x)=\{k\in[n]\mid x_{k}\in V\}
. If
{\mathsf{supp}}(x)=A
, we say that
x

has support

A
. For instance,
v_{0}\otimes v_{2}^{1}\otimes v_{0}\otimes v_{3}^{0}\otimes v_{0}\otimes v_{1}^{2}\otimes v_{0}
has support
\{2,4,6\}
.

From now on, for
A,B\subseteq[n]
and
\omega\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, we write
\mathbf{T}_{A}
,
\overline{\mathbf{T}}_{B}
and
\mathbf{T}_{\omega}
to denote the respective images of
\mathsf{T}_{A}
,
\overline{\mathsf{T}}_{B}
and
\mathsf{T}_{\omega}
in Subsection
4.1
, under the tensor representation in (
4.27
).

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
r\in[n]
, and let
B=\{b_{1}<\cdots<b_{r}\}\subseteq[n]
. Then:

1.

If
x\in U^{\otimes n}
has support
B
, then
\overline{\mathbf{T}}_{B}(x)
is nonzero and has support
[n-r+1,n]
.

2.

If
y\in U^{\otimes n}
has support of size
r
, then
\mathbf{N}^{n-r}(y)\neq 0
if and only if
{\mathsf{supp}}(y)=[n-r+1,n]
. Moreover, in this case
\mathbf{N}^{n-r}(y)
has support
[r]
.

3.

If
z\in U^{\otimes n}
has support
[r]
and its non scalar
factors have strictly decreasing lower indices, then for every

\omega\in\mathcal{S}_{r}
the tensor
\mathbf{T}_{\omega}(z)
is nonzero, and

\mathbf{T}_{\omega}
acts on
z
exactly as the permutation
\omega
acts
on tensors: the
r
non scalar factors of
z
are permuted according to
\omega
,
up to nonzero scalar multiples.

Proof.

(
1
)
Let
x\in U^{\otimes n}
with support
B:=\{b_{1}<\cdots<b_{r}\}
. Recall that

\overline{\mathbf{T}}_{B}=\mathbf{T}_{n-r+1,b_{1}}\mathbf{T}_{n-r+2,b_{2}}\cdots\mathbf{T}_{n,b_{r}},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{T}_{k,j}=\mathbf{T}_{k-1}\cdots\mathbf{T}_{j}.

Each operator
\mathbf{T}_{k,j}
moves the non scalar factor sitting in position
j
successively across adjacent scalar factors until it reaches
position
k
, never creating nor removing non scalar entries. Thus each position
b_{j}
is moved to position
n-r+j
, and no other positions become non scalar. Hence
\overline{\mathbf{T}}_{B}(x)
is nonzero and has support
[n-r+1,n]
.

(
2
) Let
y\in U^{\otimes n}
having support
B^{\prime}
with
|B^{\prime}|=r
. If
B^{\prime}=[n-r+1,n]
, then during the first
n-r
applications of
\mathbf{N}
the first tensor factor is always scalar, and
\mathbf{N}
simply rotates the tensor left while preserving the number of non scalar entries. After
n-r
steps, the
r
non scalar factors occupy the positions
[r]
, proving that
\mathbf{N}^{n-r}(y)\neq 0
and has support
[r]
.

Conversely, if
B^{\prime}\neq[n-r+1,n]
, then some non scalar factor of
y
lies among the first
n-r
positions. Since
\mathbf{N}
rotates the tensor left and annihilates it as soon as the first factor becomes non scalar, one of the first
n-r
applications of
\mathbf{N}
sends
y
to
0
.
Therefore
\mathbf{N}^{n-r}(y)=0
in this case.

(
3
) Let
z\in U^{\otimes n}
having support
[r]
and strictly decreasing
lower indices on these
r
non scalar tensor factors. For each simple transposition
s_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{r}
, the operator
\mathbf{T}_{i}
acts only on positions
i
and
i+1
, where the two non scalar factors have distinct lower indices. By the explicit formula for
\mathbf{T}
, this action is a nonzero scalar multiple of the flip, and it does not interact with the remaining components. Hence
\mathbf{T}_{i}(z)
is nonzero and its non scalar factors are obtained from
those of
z
by exchanging the
i
th and
(i+1)
st positions.

Since every
\omega\in\mathcal{S}_{r}
is a product of simple transpositions,
\mathbf{T}_{\omega}
acts on
z
by composing the corresponding flips, and therefore coincides with the natural permutation action of
\omega
on tensors, up to nonzero scalar multiples.
∎

Theorem 4.14
.

The set
\mathsf{C}_{n}
introduced in (
4.21
) is linearly independent over
\mathbb{K}(u)
. Consequently,

\dim_{\mathbb{K}(u)}\!\bigl(\mathcal{RY}_{d,n}(u)\bigr)=|\mathsf{C}_{n}|=\sum_{r=0}^{n}d^{r}\binom{n}{r}^{\!2}r!.

In particular, the tensor representation
\rho:\mathcal{RY}_{d,n}(u)\to\mathsf{End}_{\mathbb{K}(u)}\bigl(U^{\otimes n}\bigr)
is faithful whenever
d\geq n
.

Proof.

Using the defining relations, each element of
\mathsf{C}_{n}
can be rewritten by moving the generators
\mathsf{F}_{i}
to the left. More precisely, for every
r\in[n]_{0}
,
A,B\subseteq[n]
with
|A|=|B|=r
,
\omega\in\mathcal{S}_{r}
and

\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\in(\Z/d\Z)^{r}
, we write

\mathsf{T}_{A}\mathsf{F}_{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}}\mathsf{T}_{\omega}\mathsf{N}^{n-r}\overline{\mathsf{T}}_{B}=\mathsf{F}_{\mathbf{m}}X_{A,\omega,B},\quad\text{where}\quad X_{A,\omega,B}=\mathsf{T}_{A}\mathsf{T}_{\omega}\mathsf{N}^{n-r}\overline{\mathsf{T}}_{B},

for some
\mathbf{m}\in(\Z/d\Z)^{r}
depending on
(A,B,\omega,\mathbf{m}^{\prime})
. Thus, it suffices to show that any relation

(4.28)

\lambda_{0}\mathsf{N}^{n}+\sum_{r=1}^{n}\mathop{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|A|=|B|=r\\
\omega\in\mathcal{S}_{r},\ \mathbf{m}\in(\Z/d\Z)^{r}\end{subarray}}}\lambda_{A,B,\omega,\mathbf{m}}\mathsf{F}_{\mathbf{m}}X_{A,\omega,B}=0

with coefficients
\lambda_{A,B,\omega,\mathbf{m}}\in\mathbb{K}(u)
is trivial.

We first deal with the case
r=0
. Evaluating (
4.28
) on the
n
tensor
x=v_{0}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{0}
, all terms in the double sum vanish, whereas
\mathsf{N}^{n}(x)=u^{\frac{n}{2}}x
. Hence the equality forces
\lambda_{0}u^{\frac{n}{2}}x=0
, which shows that
\lambda_{0}=0
. Thus, no nontrivial relation can occur at level
r=0
.

We now assume, for a contradiction, that the sum in (
4.28
) is nontrivial. Let
r_{0}\geq 1
be maximal such that
\lambda_{A,B,\omega,\mathbf{m}}\neq 0
for some
A,B\subseteq[n]
with
|A|=|B|=r_{0}
,
\omega\in\mathcal{S}_{r_{0}}
and
\mathbf{m}\in(\Z/d\Z)^{r_{0}}
. Fix such a subset
B_{0}=\{b_{1}<\cdots<b_{r_{0}}\}
with
\lambda_{A,B_{0},\omega,\mathbf{m}}\neq 0
for at least one
(A,\omega,\mathbf{m})
. Passing to the tensor representation
\rho
gives a nontrivial identity:

(4.29)

\sum_{r=1}^{n}\mathop{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|A|=|B|=r\\
\omega\in\mathcal{S}_{r},\ \mathbf{m}\in(\Z/d\Z)^{r}\end{subarray}}}\lambda_{A,B,\omega,\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{T}_{A}\mathbf{T}_{\omega}\mathbf{N}^{n-r}\overline{\mathbf{T}}_{B}=0.

Choose a basis tensor
x_{B_{0}}\in U^{\otimes n}
with support
B_{0}
and strictly decreasing lower indices on those positions. Evaluating (
4.29
) at
x_{B_{0}}
yields the following:

(4.30)

\sum_{r=1}^{n}\mathop{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|A|=|B|=r\\
\omega\in\mathcal{S}_{r},\ \mathbf{m}\end{subarray}}}\lambda_{A,B,\omega,\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{T}_{A}\mathbf{T}_{\omega}\mathbf{N}^{n-r}\overline{\mathbf{T}}_{B}(x_{B_{0}})=0.

Since
x_{B_{0}}
has exactly
r_{0}
non scalar components, Lemma
4.13
(
2
) implies that
\mathbf{N}^{n-r}\overline{\mathbf{T}}_{B}(x_{B_{0}})=0
for all
r<r_{0}
. All coefficients with
r>r_{0}
vanish by maximality of
r_{0}
. Thus, (
4.30
) reduces to

(4.31)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|A|=|B|=r_{0}\\
\omega\in\mathcal{S}_{r_{0}},\ \mathbf{m}\end{subarray}}\lambda_{A,B,\omega,\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{T}_{A}\mathbf{T}_{\omega}\mathbf{N}^{n-r_{0}}\overline{\mathbf{T}}_{B}(x_{B_{0}})=0.

Lemma
4.13
(
1
)–(
2
) gives
\mathbf{N}^{n-r_{0}}\overline{\mathbf{T}}_{B}(x_{B_{0}})=z_{0}
if
B=B_{0}
, and
\mathbf{N}^{n-r_{0}}\overline{\mathbf{T}}_{B}(x_{B_{0}})=0
otherwise, where
z_{0}
is a nonzero basis tensor supported on
[r_{0}]
with strictly decreasing lower indices, rescaling
x_{B_{0}}
if necessary. Hence, (
4.31
) becomes

(4.32)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|A|=r_{0}\\
\omega\in\mathcal{S}_{r_{0}},\ \mathbf{m}\end{subarray}}\lambda_{A,B_{0},\omega,\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{T}_{A}\mathbf{T}_{\omega}(z_{0})=0.

By Lemma
4.13
(
3
), the tensors
\mathbf{T}_{\omega}(z_{0})
are nonzero and obtained by permuting the
r_{0}
non scalar factors of
z_{0}
. For a fixed subset
A=\{a_{1}<\dots<a_{r_{0}}\}\subseteq[n]
, Lemma
4.13
(
1
) shows that
\mathbf{T}_{A}
moves these
r_{0}
factors to the positions in
A
without mixing them with scalar components. Thus
\mathbf{T}_{A}\mathbf{T}_{\omega}(z_{0})
has support exactly
A
, and different choices of
A
give disjoint supports. Therefore (
4.32
) splits into independent relations, one for each fixed
A
:

(4.33)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega\in\mathcal{S}_{r_{0}},\ \mathbf{m}\end{subarray}}\lambda_{A,B_{0},\omega,\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{T}_{A}\mathbf{T}_{\omega}(z_{0})=0.

Multiplying on the left by
\mathbf{T}_{A}^{-1}
and using that
\mathbf{T}_{A}^{-1}\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{T}_{A}=\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{m}^{\prime}}
for a permutation
\mathbf{m}^{\prime}
of the entries of
\mathbf{m}
(together with re-indexing of
\mathbf{m}
), we obtain

(4.34)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega\in\mathcal{S}_{r_{0}}\\
\mathbf{m}\end{subarray}}\lambda_{A,B_{0},\omega,\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{T}_{\omega}(z_{0})=0.

To conclude, we analyse (
4.34
) inside the
\langle\mathbf{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{F}_{n}\rangle
-submodule generated by
\mathbf{T}_{\omega}(z_{0})
, for a fixed
\omega\in\mathcal{S}_{r_{0}}
.

Write
\mathbf{T}_{\omega}(z_{0})
in the standard cyclic basis
\{w_{i_{\ell}}^{(a)}\}_{a\in\Z/d\Z}
, where

w_{i_{\ell}}^{(a)}=\sum_{t\in\Z/d\Z}\xi^{-at}v_{i_{\ell}}^{t},\qquad\mathbf{F}_{\ell}\bigl(w_{i_{\ell}}^{(a)}\bigr)=w_{i_{\ell}}^{(a+1)}.

For a fixed
\omega\in\mathcal{S}_{r_{0}}
, the operator
\mathbf{T}_{\omega}
acts on
z_{0}
by permuting the lower indices of the tensor, up to multiplication by a power of
u
. In particular,
\mathbf{T}_{\omega}(z_{0})
still generates a
\langle\mathbf{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{F}_{n}\rangle
-submodule spanned by tensors written in the cyclic basis
\{w_{i_{\ell}}^{(a)}\}
.

Since
\mathbf{T}_{\omega}
does not affect the upper indices, we may assume without loss of generality that all upper indices of
z_{0}
are equal to zero. With this convention, the situation reduces exactly to that considered in the proof of
[
14
, Theorem 10]
. Hence the tensors

\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{T}_{\omega}(z_{0})
, with
\omega\in\mathcal{S}_{r_{0}}
and
\mathbf{m}\in(\Z/d\Z)^{r_{0}}
, are pairwise distinct basis elements of this submodule.

Therefore (
4.34
) forces
\lambda_{A,B_{0},\omega,\mathbf{m}}=0
for all
\omega\in\mathcal{S}_{r_{0}}
and
\mathbf{m}\in(\Z/d\Z)^{r_{0}}
. Since
A
was arbitrary, this contradicts the assumption that some coefficient
\lambda_{A,B_{0},\omega,\mathbf{m}}
was nonzero. Thus no nontrivial relation (
4.28
) exists, and
\mathsf{C}_{n}
is linearly independent.
∎

4.3
Other presentations

The Rook Yokonuma–Hecke algebra
\mathcal{RY}_{d,n}(u)
can also presented by generators
\mathsf{T}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{T}_{n-1}
and
\mathsf{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{F}_{n}
satisfying (
4.12
) to (
4.14
), and the generator
\mathsf{R}:=\mathsf{T}_{1}\cdots\mathsf{T}_{n-1}\mathsf{N}
, subject to the following relations:

(4.35)

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}^{2}=u^{n-1}\mathsf{R};

(4.36)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{i}\mathsf{R}=\mathsf{R}\mathsf{T}_{i},\quad i>1;\qquad\mathsf{R}\mathsf{T}_{1}^{-1}\mathsf{R}\mathsf{T}_{1}=u\mathsf{R}\mathsf{T}_{1}^{-1}\mathsf{R}=\mathsf{T}_{1}\mathsf{R}\mathsf{T}_{1}^{-1}\mathsf{R};

(4.37)

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{i}\mathsf{R}=\mathsf{R}\mathsf{F}_{i};\qquad\mathsf{R}\mathsf{F}_{1}=\mathsf{R}.

Relations (
4.35
) to (
4.37
) are derived by substituting
\mathsf{N}=\mathsf{T}_{n-1}^{-1}\cdots\mathsf{T}_{1}^{-1}\mathsf{R}
into relations (
4.15
), (
4.17
) and (
4.20
). Specifically, relation (
4.37
) follows from (
4.15
), the first relation in (
4.36
) corresponds to the first relation in (
4.17
), relation (
4.35
) is obtained from the second relation in (
