Overcoming barriers to neurosurgical care with technology and innovation
Authors: Jay J. Park, Michael Lee, Evelyn Wong, Priya Bhanot, Audrey H. Limb, Omar Ceesay, Alexandra Sakamoto, Andrew Lin, Vivek P. Buch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Technological advancements show great potential in transforming and advancing global surgery. Compared to general medicine, technology in neurosurgery has seen an explosive growth in recent years in ways that directly address accessibility. New, rapidly developing innovations pave the way for the expansion of neurosurgical care worldwide, particularly in areas with historically limited access to such facilities, personnel, clinical expertise, or financial means. Here, we present promising innovations like telemedicine, online collaborative platforms (OCPs), immersive technology (IT), and clinical artificial intelligence (AI) and their implementation in mitigating barriers to neurosurgical care. We argue how these tools enhance communications between providers and patients, streamline intraoperative workflows, and democratise access to neurosurgical education and training by reducing resource demands such as time, travel, and human labour. It is necessary, though, to identify how these technologies can be most successful in the context of global neurosurgery. In addition to reviewing these various technological avenues, we explore the nuances associated with the implementation and reported outcomes for each tool—highlighting challenges and opportunities for further innovation.

Background

An estimated 5 billion people globally lack access to essential surgical care [1]. This global disparity is reflected in the provision of neurosurgical services: despite 22.6 million cases requiring intervention annually, less than half of the global demand was met in 2019 [2].

As a highly resource-intensive specialty, neurosurgery presents a particular public health concern, demanding advanced technological support, specialized training, and extensive health-system infrastructure for timely access to quality care. Globally, these inequities are especially prevalent between high-income countries (HICs) and low-to-middle income countries (LMICs), as LMICs often lack the necessary infrastructure, workforce, and resources to support sufficient neurosurgical coverage [3]. Globally, in 2019 the median density of neurosurgeons across 198 countries was 0.36 per 100,000 population, far lower than the density of neurosurgeons in the Americas (0.97 per 100,000) or Europe (1.18 per 100,000) [4]. More recently, a cross-sectional study conducted in 2024 found a median distribution of 0.44 neurosurgeons per 100,000 people, with heavier disparities in LMICs at only 0.12 per 100.000 [5]. Despite the moderate growth from older estimates, the regional disparities and the absolute gap between HICs and LMICs still remain. Barriers such as the geographic workforce distribution of surgical subspecialties—including neurosurgery—drives inequitable access to timely care [1, 3]. Additionally, unequal access to specialists or medical infrastructure substantially limits training opportunities for providers in resource-constrained regions, further increasing existing inequities in neurosurgical care. A lack of such resources can furthermore stall access to timely care, posing a threat to patient outcomes. For example, in two systematic reviews studying the barriers to detection and care for brain tumors in LMICs, these resource-constrained environments were found to face delayed patient presentation in 61% of studies, further exacerbated by additional disparities in the necessary diagnostic tools, intraoperative equipment, and lack of sufficiently trained professionals to recognize to treat these tumors [6, 7]. For neurosurgery, lack of access occurs systematically at the many stages of care. Such widespread lack of access can delay detection and treatment, resulting in disease progression that complicates future management and contributes to worse clinical outcomes.

However, while over 80% of the surgical burden arises from LMICs, barriers to access are also evident in medically underserved populations within HICs [8]. Significant disparities within HICs arise due to geographic maldistribution of specialists and disproportionate distances to healthcare facilities, limiting access to timely treatment for neurological conditions in rural and low-income communities. Within the U.S. for instance, neurosurgical access is significantly correlated with median household income, level of education, and total population density, with high-access areas more likely to be metropolitan [5]. These factors contribute to disparate mortality rates from conditions requiring immediate neurosurgical intervention, such as stroke, subarachnoid hemorrhage, or traumatic brain injury [8]. The many facets of neurosurgical access reflect broad disparities occurring at multiple levels. Addressing such gaps could help drive improvements in patient outcomes and mortality rates, as well as advance public health goals related to health equity and the global burden of neurological disease.

While inequities in neurosurgical care remain a worldwide issue, with severe workforce shortages, geographic maldistribution, and limited training infrastructure for future providers, the growth of technology in neurosurgery has offered valuable potential solutions in overcoming these barriers. These technology categories consist of telemedicine, online collaborative platforms (OCPs), immersive technology, and artificial intelligence (AI), and were selected on the basis that they can offer a cost-effective, highly comprehensive solution to address multiple different stages of neurosurgical care as a systemic issue. The highly recent growth of these particular domains also makes it necessary to understand how they can be best implemented to serve those who need neurosurgical care the most. Telemedicine: a convenient and scalable alternative for addressing geographic disparities

Telemedicine, the use of communicative technology to deliver healthcare, has been increasingly explored as a means of delivering timely and effective patient consultations, transfers, follow-ups, and remote neurological examinations [9]. The scarcity of neurosurgeons and relevant facilities geographically makes it difficult to connect patients and providers. Electronic exchange of health information, video appointments, and remote communication among healthcare staff and patients enable this communication over geographic distances. Certain manufacturers, such as KHealth and Medtronic, manufacturers based in New York and Minnesota, respectively, for instance, also use mobile technology such as a wearable vitals monitor (BioButton by Medtronic) and smartphone apps (e.g., KHealth, MyCareLink Heart) to allow for continual patient monitoring. These are simply examples, as numerous other apps and devices exist. While not directly replacing in-person visits, these avenues offer highly scalable opportunities to reach a greater patient population. Given their convenience and widespread mobile technology use, it is worth further investigating how steps of the neurosurgical care process can be streamlined with this approach.

The rise of global internet use and COVID-19 pandemic in particular popularized the concept of telemedicine, but even prior, rural areas and LMICs with greatly limited access to neurosurgical services have demonstrated its efficacy [10]. Past studies have shown that telemedicine reduces travel time, costs, and wait times for specialised care [11, 12]. Notably, a paediatric neurosurgical clinic reported saving an average of $48.50 on transportation and work costs, along with 2.5 h of travel time per visit for each family [12]. Importantly, telehealth has yielded high patient satisfaction, comfort, and globally reported treatment success rates [9].

Telemedicine can be effective in facilitating the transfer of neuroimaging data, allowing for efficient screening, fewer repeat imaging procedures, and –as some studies suggest– even lowered mortality rates [13, 14]. But despite these potential benefits, telemedicine can be limited by the lack of direct physical evaluation and dependence on audio or video quality. One study, for example, found that 30.1% of video consults experienced technical failures [14]. The specific neuropathology and types of evaluation needed for a patient may vary the effectiveness of telemedicine. Neurosurgical subspecialties in a more outpatient setting (i.e., spine as opposed to cranial) may serve to benefit more from telemedicine when appointments have been made and imaging has been completed in advance [9]. Also in the outpatient setting, telemedicine can be an accessible means of follow-up care. Discontinuous treatment can exacerbate mortality rates and is of particular concern for LMICs that lack the financial or infrastructural means for consistent, comprehensive care. A systematic review by Shakir et al.[15] found that telemedicine was a leading strategy in LMIC settings to improve service delivery for follow-up care of brain tumors.

Ensuring telemedicine alleviates rather than exacerbates disparities also depends on digital equity. To this end, investments in related services such as mobile clinics, health information systems for patient record management, and patient education initiatives have been proposed solutions that may enhance the effectiveness of telemedicine in LMICs and improve patient-provider communication for better clinical outcomes [15]. Barriers to neurosurgical care is a multifaceted issue, but one that telemedicine can assist with in some aspects. Its increasingly widespread use, especially since the COVID-19 pandemic, has shown numerous ways telemedicine can be appropriately weaved into the patient care process, particularly in areas lacking in proximity to experienced physicians or capable hospitals.

Online collaborative platforms: enhancing global networking, clinical knowledge base, and future workforce mentorship

Neurosurgery as a highly specialized field requires immense training and expertise for a properly qualified workforce; disparities in workforce and educational facilities can greatly limit access to training facilities and mentorship [54]. Efforts such as cross-border collaboration, resource sharing, and training, especially between LMICs and HICs, are promising strategies to help mitigate such issues [55]. OCPs—a term we will use to describe general technology enabling remote mass communication outside of telehealth—are one way to bridge geographical gaps in neurosurgical training and information exchange. OCPs encompass many forms of virtual media that provide similar benefits to telemedicine as shown in Table 1, but contribute outside of direct patient care. These platforms can increase educational opportunities and cultivate connections between subspecialty providers in remote areas, allowing trainees to learn in areas where neurosurgical education remains sparse [56]. In one study mapping neurosurgical education in Pakistan, undergraduate and postgraduate students reported insufficient mentorship or limited exposure to surgical specialties; this opens up opportunities for virtual classrooms, webinars, or international conferences as a low-cost method of training and education where such nearby facilities are lacking [16]. Open-access video conferencing platforms, including popular apps such as Zoom and YouTube, have proven capable of delivering educational content to thousands of participants across countries [17]. In addition to facilitating remote networking and mentorship, Zoom has been used to offer international medical students exposure to otherwise inaccessible training opportunities [18]. Online platforms have also fostered collaboration among providers, researchers, and organisations working in global neurosurgery [19]. One particularly interactive platform is Intersurgeon, a website established in 2017 for global collaboration among surgeons, which has been shown to facilitate partnerships between individuals and organisations interested in improving surgical care globally, with 77.8% of these collaborations occurring between HICs and LMICs [20]. Additionally, the overall receptiveness to these online avenues could be important to note. In one example with a survey of students across 23 countries, an online neurosurgical conference platform was positively received as a useful avenue for improvements in neurosurgical knowledge and clinical skills, [57] a sign that increasing acceptance could help improve the expansion of convenient, virtual learning opportunities.

Social media additionally plays a large role in fostering an academic connection in the global neurosurgical community. Neurosurgery Cocktail, an Instagram and Facebook group with over 29,500 members from over 100 countries, enables sharing of educational articles, clinical experiences, and research opportunities [58]. Other social media platforms have shown to be a cost-effective avenue in LMICs for purposes such as neurosurgical training, easier access to expertise, educational webinars, and global networking [59].

While OCPs have significantly expanded access to neurosurgical education, they may not fully replicate human interactions between providers and trainees. Building personal relationships and developing hands-on clinical skills through direct patient encounters remains essential [60]. Rather than supplanting traditional curricula, virtual platforms should supplement in-person education whenever possible to ensure more equitable access globally.

Immersive technology: a lower-cost, lower-risk alternative to surgical training

A significant constraint to accessible neurosurgical care also lies within the immense amount of expertise and training needed for a practicing neurosurgeon. We therefore present IT as a more accessible avenue for the training of novice neurosurgeons. IT encompasses tools such as virtual reality (VR), augmented reality (AR), and mixed reality (MR). AR overlays computer-generated visuals onto a lens of the real world, while MR enables the interaction between the virtual and real world, and VR creates entirely virtual environments. 3D constructions of anatomical views and simulation of surgical environments can facilitate hands-on training for neurosurgeons and improve intraoperative performance where training and education is scarce [61].

Notably, IT presents a highly robust and scalable avenue for surgical training through “tele-proctoring,” where an experienced proctor remotely observes and assists a surgeon during a procedure, providing real-time guidance [21]. One of the earliest uses of tele-proctoring was reported in a 2005 study in Canada, where a robotic telecollaboration system allowed for audio communication and transmission of neuronavigation data to allow feedback from the mentor to the operating surgeon [22]. Especially in combination with newer mixed-reality systems, this approach could further enhance remote intraoperative communications between neurosurgeons, helping overcome geographic barriers and expand access to expert mentorship. Further developments in IT for tele-proctoring could greatly improve the learning experience for neurosurgeons in more resource-constrained settings.

While IT incorporation into tele-proctoring is a highly recent field, some studies have shown how IT can enhance remote training or surgical guidance beyond verbal communication. For example, in a recent study involving training resident neurosurgeons from the Philippines, surgical mentees could observe live annotations made by an overseas neurosurgeon observer overlaid onto cadaver anatomy during a cadaver dorsal rhizotomy dissection. This allowed for real-time surgical guidance and evaluation via the Objective Structured Assessment of Technical Skills (OSATS) tool and improved reported confidence by training residents [23]. This study also used relatively simpler devices and internet-based communications, such as smartphones and Zoom, compared to complex robotic systems employed early on in the field. Further advancements in more commercially available hardware could make remote neurosurgical training through teleproctoring a more feasible strategy to address mentorship and learning disparities.

Neurosurgery-specific applications of IT in tele-proctoring remain in early translational stages, but some feasibility has been reported in case and pilot studies. For example, a systematic review by Bocanegra-Becerra et al.[24] in 2024 found AR to be useful in trainee education, with smaller exploratory use in preoperative planning and intraoperative support. However, bandwidth-related problems such as audio and video latency, as well as image resolution, were among the commonly reported problems. For tele-proctoring, guidelines issued by the Society of American Gastrointestinal and Endoscopic Surgeons recommend a speed of 40 mbps with latency no longer than 450 ms [62]. While studies vary in their internet quality, multiple studies in Bocanegra-Becerra et al.’s review reported reliance on 3G internet, which typically lies below this speed. This study also reported a variety of hardware used, including phones and laptops. For safe implementation of AR in tele-proctoring with internet-based systems, it is necessary to further establish guidelines on sufficient internet speeds to determine if they can be a reliable tool for intraoperative assistance.

Other challenges remain with IT, particularly in training requirements, maintenance costs, and overall limited access to these tools [63, 64]. Further efforts have been made to create more user-friendly tools that lower barriers to implementation and training in underserved areas, [65, 66] showing feasibility for more widespread application. Partnerships and collaborations, particularly with HICs can help procure necessary equipment for LMICs [67], and newer technology such as Zoom can offer lower-cost solutions. International surgical boot camps additionally for instance have been effective at introducing new technologies to LMICs, [65] indicating the importance of global collaborations in bridging the digital divide and democratising training resources.

Artificial intelligence: supplementing the neurosurgical workforce and streamlining decision making

A relatively recent innovation in neurosurgery and at the forefront of modern technology, AI mimics “human” intelligence, assisting with various tasks throughout pre-, intra-, and postoperative care. AI offers solutions to access limitations both by reducing the need for human resources and by augmenting the clinical decision-making process [25, 26].

Computer vision architectures such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been used in neuroradiology for detecting and segmenting neuropathologies, improving the efficacy and accuracy of MRI and CT scan analyses [27]. By automating these processes, AI helps address workforce shortages, especially within subspecialties such as neuroradiology or neurosurgery, by lightening their workload. In addition to its utility in imaging-based diagnostics, AI can aid in surgical training by evaluating performance and providing feedback. By analysing surgical videos and quantifying tool-handling motion metrics, CNN models have been shown to accurately differentiate surgeon skill levels [28]. This method offers a more systematic approach to neurosurgical training by eliminating the need for direct expert surgeon observation and evaluation.

While implementation, training, and maintenance costs of AI are valid concerns, more user-friendly, human-facing tools have been developed, and AI continues to reduce long-term costs and improve access to neurosurgical care [68]. Large language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT have been used not only to analyse medical records, generate administrative reports, and aid in clinical diagnostics, [29] but also serve in patient-facing roles such as translation of medical documents, explaining medical jargon, and potentially offering clinical guidance based on reported signs and symptoms [30]. LLMs as they currently stand though, are far from infallible, especially when considering biases that may be present in training models due to limited data from underrepresented groups, negatively affecting the quality of AI-generated outputs [69].

While AI is not intended to replace human medical expertise, its potential to enhance neurosurgical care is significant and continually advancing. AI can provide rapid, accurate analyses in time-sensitive cases, [27] offering a more systematic framework for tasks that traditionally rely on human judgement and that may be subject to provider bias [28]. As AI models advance and datasets become more comprehensive, their role in improving patient care and neurosurgical access will continue to grow.

Perspective and outlook

Compared to other specialties, neurosurgery presents unique challenges to care access because of the relative scarcity of practitioners and training opportunities. The extensive training required and limited accredited programs help explain the disproportionate disparities evident in neurosurgical care for LMICs [2]. Telemedicine, OCPs, IT, and AI can be methods of revolutionizing access to neurosurgical care in different ways as evidenced in Table 1. Telemedicine connects a scarcity of neurosurgery practitioners to greater patient populations and promotes preventive, timely medical care – especially critical in this field. OCPs connect neurosurgeons across the world to optimize clinical idea sharing and training opportunities. IT promotes quality of care in underserved communities by providing real-time anatomic labeling and feedback intraoperatively. AI mitigates costs in underserved areas by reducing human reliance, automating time-intensive processes, and increasing quality of care through flexible, broadscale computing. Each of these technologies also presents limitations and risks like an inability to replace in-person demands, a need for effective regulation, limited access, high upfront costs, and implementation challenges that must be appraised critically.

Telemedicine allows physicians and patients to interact remotely, which increases convenience, compliance, and preventative care. However, much of neurosurgery depends on in-person intervention and it can be difficult to adequately assess a patient through video and audio alone. Telemedicine can never replace direct patient care, but many intake, consult, and follow-up visits could be done remotely; reduced patient costs and time demands may also encourage more patients to seek care. Therefore, developing more intuitive, standardized, and capable models of telemedicine is critical to encouraging more physicians and patients to utilize it. Infrastructure like instructing patients on how to take clear photos/videos of their postoperative surgical sites before telehealth visits or standardized guidelines physicians can use to determine when a televisit would be appropriate could help expand the capacity of telemedicine and strike a balance between virtual and traditional medicine.

The portability, scalability, and quality of OCP technology promotes widespread access to care and training globally. Webinars, social media, and video conferencing represent ways of implementing a worldwide network of collaboration and the results from several studies substantiate the promise of OCPs in increasing access to care [17–19, 58, 59]. Though this type of unlimited reach promotes access and inclusivity, it also risks the release of personal health information, lack of quality control, and misinformation. Therefore, in order to ensure that accessibility does not compromise standards of care, regulation and safeguards must be integrated within these networks. Existing third party OCPs like X, Facebook, or Reddit provide opportunities for community guidelines, moderators, and ongoing feedback. However, these mechanisms are not specific to medicine and are not overseen by a centralized regulating body.

In order for an OCP to be most effective, future efforts should focus on developing a singular network specific to neurosurgery that provides regulation that meets the standards of clinical practice like patient confidentiality and iterative peer-review. An OCP of this nature would allow neurosurgeons globally to instantly share questions, feedback, or techniques with one another and their respective patient populations. This would allow surgeons practicing in underserved or remote areas to learn from cases and research not available to them directly.

Clinical validation of digital technologies also relies largely on regulatory approval. In 2022, the World Health Organization and the International Telecommunication Union jointly published a “Global standard for accessibility of telehealth services” to list technical requirements for telehealth platforms [70]. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has established an open interest and concern in regulating digital health, creating the Digital Health Center for Excellence. More recently in April 2026, this center launched the new “Technology-Enabled Meaningful Patient Outcomes (TEMPO) for Digital Health Devices Pilot” to promote access to digital health while protecting patient safety [71].

In the European Union, all commercial medical devices require a Conformité Européene (CE) marking to indicate that they meet safety and performance requirements. Receiving the CE mark is a significant step toward the practical installation and incorporation of innovative technologies into patient care. For instance, TytoCare—a user-friendly handheld device for remote patient examination—has been shown to be of use in remote physical examinations [72] following its receipt of a CE mark in 2018.

While the U.S. FDA does not regulate devices like smartphones or tablets, it does regulate mobile medical apps and encourages developers to consult the Digital Health Policy Navigator, an online tool for determining whether a software is subject to oversight [73]. Software as medical devices is under special control by the FDA. To be approved for use, the FDA requires submission of clinical data and detailed reports demonstrating the safety and effectiveness of the proposed device [74].

The quality of neurosurgical care is also largely dependent on the experience of the physician. Those trained at well funded institutions with abundant educational resources or practicing in urban centers with high patient volume and diverse cases have more experience than those operating in underserved or remote communities. IT provides automated intraoperative labeling and guidance that can supplement the care of all neurosurgeons, particularly those with less experience. One limitation is that VR and AR systems can present high upfront and maintenance costs and demands that underserved and/or remote institutions may be unable to meet. New innovations, however, depend only on inexpensive mobile smartphones or low cost headsets [21].

IT also enables long-distance mentorship through telementoring. Outreach efforts from high resource areas to LMICs using virtual technology have shown success [65, 75, 76] through tele-proctoring and exposure to new technology in training curricula. While this model worked well in a study conducted in the U.S., [75] a study in Mozambique was limited by the need for high bandwidth which created video feed disruptions [76]. In countries with limited internet access, bandwidth, and familiarity, more user-friendly solutions with lower computational power could be more effective. For example, a mixed reality boot camp was successfully deployed in Vietnam, using an AR headset with a 3D model to teach novice participants with no AR experience the proper surgical approach to a cranial tumour [65]. Simulative approaches to training like these, especially in conjunction with AI, can offer complete assessments of neurosurgical skill without a need for a proctor [61]. Currently, the FDA maintains a public list of over 100 approved medical devices featuring AR and VR. Medivis’ NeuroAlign software, Claronav’s Navient Image Guided Navigation System for ENT, and Surgalign Spine Technologies’ ARAI Surgical Navigation System are just a few examples of AR/VR systems primed for clinical implementation in neurosurgery [77]. Highly advanced systems such as these, however, can also demand a high cost despite potential reductions as the market grows in the future. In this regard, outreach to lower access areas can be greatly beneficial for initial exposure.

AI and machine learning (ML) algorithms are a particularly promising type of software as medical devices. Since approving its first AI/ML tool, PAPNET, in 1995, the FDA has approved over 1000 more as of May 2025 [78]. The FDA has also created its own AI systems for internal use, including the forthcoming Harmonized AI and Lifecycle Operation (HALO) program [79]. Generative AI algorithms such as ChatGPT pose a more nuanced challenge to oversight due to their broad applications, many of which lie beyond healthcare [78]. That said, the FDA recently launched Elsa, a secure generative AI tool designed for agency-wide optimization, [80] suggesting that clearer guidelines for generative AI in healthcare are not far.

However, a perhaps overlooked yet key barrier to the widespread adoption of new technologies is cultural acceptance within clinical settings. AI-powered tools may raise concerns about replacing human judgement. Particularly in global neurosurgery, an algorithm trained on a non-representative dataset may underperform in underrepresented populations - those who may need it the most. A substantial issue with computer vision implementation in surgery for example lies in the training data. Successful computer vision models often use a great deal of training data to be able to recognize novel scenarios. Using more diverse surgical data with different procedures has shown improvement in model performance [81]. However, in addition to the time-consuming nature of obtaining large amounts of human-annotated datasets, this matter is further complicated by the fact that research papers may not elaborate on their strategies for annotation, putting into question the reliability and replicability of annotations [82]. Differences in disease burden and treatment approaches across regions require culturally and demographically diverse datasets [69]. As more training data is generated, discriminatory bias in the models can be an issue if the data is not diverse enough to include underrepresented and/or minority populations, potentially diminishing the quality for underserved communities who need care the most [69]. The disproportionate, low numbers of neurosurgeons globally make this a particularly relevant concern, as highly asymmetrically trained models have been shown to lead to discrimination and inaccuracies [83]. Developers must ensure global diversity in AI training datasets and annotation, and promote the use of transparent and explainable AI models so that providers may understand and validate the factors used in model decision-making. Regulatory frameworks must also be established to safeguard against data privacy risks, especially when implementing technologies where PHI is shared or analysed. Conclusions

Access to neurosurgical care faces multiple barriers that make achieving equity a complex challenge. However, technological advancements are continuously evolving, offering increased versatility and improved health outcomes for medically underserved communities. The technologies reviewed—telemedicine, OCPs, IT, and AI—demonstrate promising potential to overcome or circumvent barriers to equitable access. Yet, the effective and responsible implementation of these technologies varies in resource-constrained settings. Considerations such as internet access, implementation and maintenance costs, and user acceptability are critical for successful adoption. Despite these challenges, these continually evolving technologies present significant opportunities to expand and improve neurosurgical care worldwide.
