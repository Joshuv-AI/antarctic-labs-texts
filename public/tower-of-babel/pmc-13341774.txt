Time series of environment and plankton during Raphidiopsis raciborskii blooms in two subtropical Chinese reservoirs
Authors: Shuzhen Li, Huihuang Chen, Lei Jin, Peng Xiao, Jun R. Yang, Zijie Xu, Lemian Liu, Jun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The increasing frequency of freshwater cyanobacterial blooms has emerged as a critical ecological and environmental concern, yet long-term time series data documenting such blooms remain scarce. This study presents a 13-year dataset (2010–2022) from two adjacent subtropical reservoirs (Shidou and Bantou) in Xiamen, Fujian Province, Southeast China. It provides a monthly and quarterly overview of 20 physicochemical parameters (348 samples), microscope-based phytoplankton (348 samples), and DNA sequence-based data for bacteria (342 samples) and microeukaryotes (348 samples). The dataset highlights recurrent cyanobacterial blooms dominated by Raphidiopsis raciborskii (basionym Cylindrospermopsis raciborskii). This long-term dataset serves as a valuable resource for investigating, predicting, and controlling cyanobacterial blooms, and will support efforts in biodiversity forecasting, ecological restoration, and targeted management of freshwater ecosystems.

Background & Summary

Freshwater environments are increasingly threatened by the triple planetary crisis, including climate change, biodiversity loss, and pollution1. One consequence of these changes is the increasing frequency of cyanobacterial blooms, which profoundly alter the physicochemical factors as well as the biological communities in the lake or reservoir ecosystems, thereby influencing the water environment and ecosystem services2,3. Accurate assessment of freshwater ecosystem variations caused by cyanobacterial blooms critically depends on high-quality data. However, detailed quantification of physicochemical parameters and biological community composition in inland waters remains constrained by insufficient high-resolution, long-term spatiotemporal datasets, particularly for the reservoir environments. Securing long-term time-series data faces significant challenges due to the substantial costs of sustained monitoring and the necessity for stabilized measurement protocols throughout the observation period to prevent acquisition biases. Consequently, planktonic community records, particularly those documenting freshwater phytoplankton communities, remain scarce and underrepresented in scientific literature, reflecting critical data gaps in aquatic ecological research4.

The cyanobacterial species Raphidiopsis raciborskii has emerged as a globally invasive microorganism, expanding its range from tropical origins to temperate and even subpolar freshwater ecosystems worldwide5. Its blooms pose significant ecological and environmental concerns, including the production of potent toxins (i.e., cylindrospermopsin) that threaten aquatic biota and human health, disrupting food webs, altering nutrient cycling, and contributing to the degradation of water quality6. Despite its expanding distribution and ecological impacts, R. raciborskii has received comparatively less research attention compared to other well-studied bloom-forming cyanobacteria such as Microcystis7. Critical gaps remain in understanding its physiological adaptability, bloom-driving mechanisms under changing environmental conditions, and interactions with other organisms within complex aquatic ecosystems. Given its invasive potential and multifaceted risks, in-depth investigations into R. raciborskii are urgently needed to inform effective management strategies and predict its ecological footprint in a warming world8. To date, research on plankton, microbial communities, and environmental drivers during bloom events has predominantly focused on single or intra-annual recurrent bloom events within individual aquatic ecosystems9,10. In contrast, studies examining cyanobacterial bloom dynamics across multiple waterbodies over extended temporal scales, particularly those spanning more than a decade or longer, remain notably scarce. This scarcity is even more pronounced for R. raciborskii, for which long-term datasets are extremely limited. Long-term spatiotemporal datasets are essential baselines for ecological research, enabling the detection of warning signals for natural and anthropogenic disturbances, tracking of cyanobacterial bloom dynamics, identification of environmental drivers, predictive modeling of ecosystem trajectories, and formulation of targeted mitigation policies11. Consequently, developing high-resolution, long-term monitoring frameworks enhances predictive accuracy and intervention efficacy, thereby supporting scientifically robust assessments of cyanobacterial impacts on freshwater ecosystems.

Here, we present a multi-parameter database documenting phytoplankton communities (microscope-based quantification), prokaryotic and microeukaryotic communities (16S and 18S rRNA gene-based sequencing), and concurrent physicochemical parameters over 13 years (2010–2022) of monthly and quarterly sampling from two subtropical reservoirs in Southeast China experiencing periodic cyanobacterial blooms12. This compendium constitutes one of the longest and most robust spatiotemporal records of aquatic biodiversity in the region. The dataset provides fundamental knowledge for systematically investigating cyanobacterial bloom proliferation and dissipation, while establishing a critical framework for analyzing structure-function relationships in freshwater ecosystems. The dataset serves as a decision-support tool for environmental management, enables predictive modeling of natural and anthropogenic impact scenarios, and offers foundational insights for local mitigation strategies and advancing mechanistic understanding of reservoir ecosystem dynamics.

Methods

Study area and sampling sites

Field sampling was conducted in both Shidou (24°42′ N, 118°00′ E) and Bantou (24°40′ N, 118°01′ E) reservoirs from 2010 to 2022 (Fig. 1). The Shidou Reservoir, with a total storage capacity of 61.4 million m³, is situated directly upstream of the Bantou Reservoir (4.4 million m³). Sampling was conducted monthly from May 2010 to April 2011, followed by sampling in July and October 2011. From 2012 to 2022, sampling was normally carried out in January, April, July, and October each year. During each sampling event, surface water (0.5 m depth) was collected from riverine (upstream), transitional (midstream), and lacustrine (downstream) zones in each reservoir with a 5 L polymethyl methacrylate sampler13. After being transported to the laboratory within 4 h, water samples were divided into three portions: one (0.5 L) for physicochemical measurements, another (2.5 L) for phytoplankton identification, and the remaining water was retained for microbial plankton community analysis. Physicochemical measurements

Water transparency was measured with a Secchi disk. Water transparency was generally higher in Shidou Reservoir than Bantou Reservoir (Fig. 2). Water temperature (WT), dissolved oxygen (DO), pH, turbidity, electrical conductivity (EC), and oxidation-reduction potential (ORP) were measured in situ with a water quality analyzer (Hydrolab DS5, Hach Company, Loveland, CO, USA). Total carbon (TC), total organic carbon (TOC), total nitrogen (TN), ammonium nitrogen (NH4-N), nitrate nitrogen (NO3-N), nitrite nitrogen (NO2-N), total phosphorus (TP), and phosphate phosphorus (PO4-P) were measured according to standard methods14. Total chlorophyll-a (Chl-a) concentrations were analyzed using a PHYTO-PAM Phytoplankton Analyzer (Heinz Walz GmbH, Effeltrich, Germany) from April 2012. Minimal differences were observed between the reservoirs for water temperature, pH, salinity, dissolved oxygen, electrical conductivity, and nutrient concentrations (i.e., nitrogen and phosphorus). TN concentrations exhibited a marked decline after 2012 in both reservoirs, a pattern that is visible in the long-term dataset. Water level data were provided by the local reservoir management agency. Shidou Reservoir maintained consistently deeper water than Bantou Reservoir throughout the dataset period, with significantly higher interannual depth variability over the 13 years. Phytoplankton and cyanobacterial bloom identification

Lugol’s iodine solution (1.5%) was used for fixation and preservation of each phytoplankton sample. For each sample, a minimum of 500 individuals were identified and quantified using an inverted microscope (Motic, Xiamen, China) following established taxonomic guides16–18. Phytoplankton species abundance (individual/L) was converted to biomass (mg/L) using standardized geometric models19. To identify cyanobacterial blooms, both absolute biomass and relative biomass (%) (the proportion of cyanobacteria or R. raciborskii biomass relative to total phytoplankton biomass) were calculated. Cyanobacterial blooms were defined as conditions where cyanobacterial biomass exceeded 10 mg/L and constituted over 50% of total phytoplankton biomass8. In our dataset, cyanobacterial biomass and its proportional contribution to total phytoplankton exhibited periodic fluctuations in both reservoirs, indicating recurrent cyanobacterial blooms (Fig. 3). During bloom periods, R. raciborskii was identified as the dominant species, comprising >50% of total phytoplankton biomass (Fig. 4).Fig. 3Absolute biomass and relative biomass (%) of Cyanobacteria in phytoplankton. Bacterial and microeukaryotic communities

Water samples were pre-filtered through a 200 μm mesh to remove larger particles and macro- to mesoplankton, then ca. 500–1000 mL of water was filtered through 0.22 μm pore-size polycarbonate filters (47 mm diameter, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) using a vacuum pump to collect plankton. Membranes were stored at −80 °C for subsequent processing.

DNA was extracted from the membrane using the FastDNA SPIN Kit and the instrument (MP Biomedicals, Solon, OH, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration was quantified through a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) based on absorbance ratios at 260/280 nm and 260/230 nm. The hypervariable V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene which targets bacteria, as well as the V9 region of the 18S rRNA gene which targets microeukaryotes, were amplified using universal primer pairs, specifically 341 F/806 R20 and 1380 F/1510 R21, respectively. All polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplifications were conducted in triplicate with 20 μL reaction mixtures containing 10 μL of Takara Premix Taq (Ex Taq version 2.0 with dye; Takara Bio, Japan), 0.25 μM of each primer, and 10 ng of microbial DNA. For bacterial communities, the thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation (95 °C, 30 s), annealing (55 °C, 30 s), and elongation (72 °C, 30 s), with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min22. For microeukaryotic communities, the thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation (95 °C, 30 s), annealing (55 °C, 30 s), and elongation (72 °C, 30 s), with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min23. Amplification products from triplicate reactions for each sample were pooled, purified via gel electrophoresis, and quantified using the Qubit assay with a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies). High-throughput sequencing (HTS) was conducted on the Illumina platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) through a paired-end (2 × 250 bp for bacteria; 2 × 150 bp for microeukaryotes) sequencing strategy.

Amplification of bacterial DNA failed for samples collected in July 2010, resulting in the loss of six samples. Ultimately, 342 bacterial samples and 348 microeukaryotic samples were retained for analysis. Raw sequencing data were processed using USEARCH (v11)24. Chimeras were identified and discarded using default settings through UCHIME25. Quality-filtered reads were assigned to zero-radius operational taxonomic units (zOTUs) using the UNOISE3 algorithm26. Representative sequences from zOTUs were aligned against the SILVA database (v138.1)27 for bacteria and the PR2 database (v5.0.0)28 for microeukaryotes to obtain taxonomic assignments. zOTUs with fewer than 10 reads were removed to minimize sequencing errors29. All archaea, chloroplasts, mitochondria and unknown sequences were discarded. After subsampling to an equal number of reads per sample, the bacterial dataset comprised 13621 zOTUs, corresponding to 70459 reads per sample. The microeukaryotic dataset comprised 21922 zOTUs, corresponding to 29051 reads per sample. The rarefaction curves of most samples almost flattened, indicating that sequencing depth was sufficient to capture most of the microbial diversity (Fig. 5a, b). Species richness of both bacterial and microeukaryotic communities in the two reservoirs consistently fluctuated between 1000 and 3000 zOTUs (Fig. 5c, d).Fig. 5Rarefaction curves of (a) bacterial and (b) microeukaryotic communities in Shidou and Bantou reservoirs. The raw high-throughput sequencing data have been uploaded to the public NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) (16S rRNA gene V3–V4 region, PRJNA689332, SRP121028; 18S rRNA gene V9 region, PRJNA415265, SRP121028)30,31 and NODE database (16S rRNA gene V3–V4 region, OEP00001430; 18S rRNA gene V9 region, OEP00002057). These raw FASTQ files contain all sequenced reads, including those from chloroplasts, and are deposited without any filtering. This allows for complete reprocessing or reanalysis of the data using alternative bioinformatics pipelines.

Technical Validation

To ensure data accuracy, measurements of physicochemical factors were performed in triplicate. For microscopic analysis, a minimum of 500 individuals were enumerated to guarantee counting precision. For bacterial and microeukaryotic data, strictly consistent PCR cycling conditions were maintained across all long-term time-series samples to ensure experimental consistency, with each sample subjected to three technical replicates to enhance robustness. Raw sequencing data were uniformly processed using USEARCH (v11) for quality control, which included removal of low-quality bases and chimeric sequences. Following initial zOTU construction, sequences with abundances below 10 were discarded as potential sequencing errors. All samples were subsequently resampled to an equal sequencing depth to ensure comparability in downstream statistical analyses. These procedures collectively constitute our technical validation.
