Trends, disparities, and multilevel interventions: A global review of HPV vaccination among university students
Authors: Yunjun Yan, Shuang Guan, Wei Chen, Junli Shi, Ruoyu Chao, Jing Fang, Chunhui Zheng, Zhen Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuman papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination is a critical yet underutilized strategy for preventing HPV-related cancers in young adults. University students represent a key demographic for catch-up vaccination owing to their risk profile and transitional healthcare access. This review synthesized data from January 2015 to February 2026 to examine the trends, disparities, and determinants of HPV vaccine uptake in this population. Vaccination rates remain suboptimal worldwide, with pronounced disparities across sex, race/ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and geographic regions. Key barriers operate at multiple levels: individual (knowledge gaps, low perceived risk, hesitancy), interpersonal (lack of provider recommendations), and structural (cost, healthcare access). Promising evidence-based interventions include proactive campus health services, digital campaigns tailored to Generation Z, healthcare provider recommendations, and supportive policies. An integrated socioecological approach coordinating strategies across these levels is essential. This review highlights the urgent need for equity-focused multichannel interventions and policies to improve HPV vaccination coverage among university students worldwide.

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) remains the leading cause of preventable cancers worldwide, with the vaccine offering over 90% protection against the high-risk HPV types responsible for cervical, anal, and oropharyngeal malignancies.1 Despite strong scientific consensus on its efficacy and safety, HPV vaccination rates among young adults, particularly those in college, remain suboptimal, creating a significant public health challenge.2–7 College-aged individuals represent a critical demographic owing to their heightened risk of sexual transmission, transitional healthcare access, and potential for catch-up vaccination. As global efforts to eliminate HPV-related cancers intensify, understanding the evolving trends and determinants of vaccine uptake in this population is essential.8–10 This report synthesizes recent evidence from diverse academic sources to examine the dynamic landscape of HPV vaccination among college students across multiple countries, highlighting persistent disparities and emerging opportunities for intervention.

Recent longitudinal and cross-sectional studies have revealed a complex picture of the fluctuating vaccination trends. In the United States, while female vaccination rates have consistently outpaced those of males, both groups have shown gradual increases over time, particularly among younger cohorts aged 18–21 y.11 Interventions, such as campus-based clinics and provider recommendations, have demonstrated measurable success, with one study reporting a 76% increase in HPV doses administered at a university health center following a targeted campaign.12 Similarly, in Japan, a 3-y survey documented a dramatic rise in vaccination uptake among new female college students, from 5.4% in 2011 to 48.7% in 2013, which was attributed to financial support programs and public awareness initiatives.13

However, significant disparities persist across sex, race, geography, and socioeconomic status. In China, HPV vaccination rates among college students remain alarmingly low, with only 3.1% vaccinated overall (including five males and 27 females).14 Limited knowledge, cost barriers, and safety concerns are frequently cited as obstacles. Among U.S. college men, vaccination rates hover around 11.8%, with knowledge levels often inadequate despite positive attitudes toward the vaccine.15 Religious beliefs, cultural perceptions, and misconceptions – particularly regarding sexual morality – are key drivers of hesitancy, especially among Christian students at private religious universities.2,16 Furthermore, international students, who may lack prior access to vaccines in their home countries, face unique challenges related to language, information accessibility, and healthcare navigation.5,7 These findings underscore the role of policy and institutional support in driving uptake, particularly when aligned with accessible delivery models.

Despite these challenges, actionable strategies for improvement have been identified. Studies grounded in behavioral theories such as the Health Belief Model and Theory of Planned Behavior have consistently highlighted that healthcare provider recommendations, perceived susceptibility, and social norms are powerful predictors of vaccination intent and behavior.5,17,18 Institutional interventions – including proactive invitations, campus-wide communication campaigns, and multilingual outreach – have proven effective in increasing both awareness and uptake.19,20 The integration of digital tools, such as conversational agents and electronic medical record prompts, further demonstrates innovation in overcoming provider workload and patient hesitation.21,22

This review aimed to provide a comprehensive synthesis of the current trends and influencing factors of HPV vaccination among college students by integrating findings from high-impact, peer-reviewed studies conducted between January 2015 and February 2026. It analyzes data across sex, regional, and sociocultural contexts to identify consistent patterns, gaps in service delivery, and promising strategies for scalable implementation. Materials and methods

Study design

This study is a comprehensive narrative review that systematically synthesizes existing evidence on HPV vaccination among university students, with an emphasis on trends, disparities, determinants, and multilevel intervention strategies. The review is based exclusively on previously published studies and did not involve the collection of new primary data.

Literature identification

Relevant studies were identified through a structured review of the peer-reviewed literature published between January 2015 and February 2026. Studies focusing on HPV vaccination in college or university populations were included. Representative studies are summarized in Table 1 to illustrate the range of research designs and key findings.

Rather than relying on a single database or a predefined search pipeline, this review integrates evidence from a broad range of published studies across multiple countries and research contexts. The body of evidence includes a diverse range of study types, including cross-sectional, cohort, and randomized controlled trials, and systematic reviews. Table 1 presents the selected representative studies and illustrates the diversity of study designs, populations, and key research themes included in this review.

Data extraction and thematic categorization

The key information from the selected studies was systematically extracted and organized into a structured evidence framework. The following elements were recorded for each study: (1) study design and methodological approach; (2) sample characteristics and population; (3) geographic setting; and (4) key findings related to HPV vaccination.

To facilitate a structured synthesis, the included studies were categorized into 12 thematic domains, as presented in Table 1. These domains encompass determinants, disparities, health literacy, vaccine hesitancy, healthcare provider influence, digital interventions, and policy-related factors. This thematic classification served as an analytical foundation for the synthesis and interpretation of the findings.

Data synthesis

A qualitative synthetic approach was used to integrate the findings of included studies. Evidence was comparatively analyzed to identify (1) consistent patterns in vaccination uptake and disparities, (2) recurring barriers and facilitators across populations, and (3) effective intervention strategies at the individual, interpersonal, organizational, and policy levels.

The synthesis was further guided by a socioecological perspective, enabling the interpretation of findings across multiple levels of influence, including individual, interpersonal, organizational, and structural factors. Specifically, studies were compared and grouped based on thematic similarities and key characteristics, and findings were synthesized across studies to generate higher-level conceptual insights. This approach enhanced the transparency and structure of the synthesis process.

Human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination trends among college students: epidemiological burden, disparities by gender, race/ethnicity, and international regions

Epidemiological burden and risk profile in college populations

The epidemiological burden of HPV infection among college students is substantial, particularly given the high prevalence of sexually transmitted infections and significant cancer risk associated with persistent HPV types. In the United States, HPV remains the most prevalent sexually transmitted infection among young adults aged 15–25 y, with a disproportionately high disease burden observed in college-age populations.39 Despite the proven efficacy of HPV vaccines in preventing six major cancers – cervical, anal, oropharyngeal, penile, vulvar, and vaginal – vaccination coverage among college students remains suboptimal.40 A significant gap exists between public health recommendations and actual vaccination rates, as HPV vaccination coverage remains low despite this elevated risk.17,40–43 This disparity is further underscored by findings from national surveillance data and large-scale college health surveys, which reveal that while the overall initiation rate of HPV vaccination among young adults has increased significantly, stark variations persist across demographic strata, reflecting underlying inequities.5,44

In Ghana, cervical cancer is the second most common cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths among female university students. Despite plans to integrate HPV vaccination into the national immunization schedule by 2023, a baseline survey prior to its implementation found that only 4.5% of female senior high school students had received the vaccine.45 This exceptionally low uptake rate highlights a critical gap between policy intentions and initial population coverage before the program’s formal rollout. The situation in China presents another dimension of the global burden: approximately 47,739 deaths result from HPV-related cervical cancer annually; however, vaccination rates among Chinese college students remain extremely low despite the high awareness of HPV.26 These data collectively illustrate that, while the cancer burden attributable to HPV is significant and rising in various regions, college students represent a vulnerable population with inadequate protection due to insufficient vaccination.

The severity of this issue is further emphasized by the low completion rates of the full HPV vaccine series. In a US-based study, only 28.9% of female college students and 11.5% of male college students completed the recommended three-dose regimen.25 Another study found that among medical students in southern Italy, vaccination uptake was 55.5%, with marked sex disparities: 78.5% among females versus only 16.5% among males.41 Even among highly educated groups such as medical students who possess advanced biological knowledge about infectious diseases, vaccination rates remain far below optimal levels, suggesting that knowledge alone does not drive behavior. These findings highlight a persistent disconnect between understanding risks and taking preventive action, particularly during the critical transition period from adolescence to early adulthood, when college enrollment typically occurs.

Disparities by gender identity and sexual orientation

Intersectional analyses reveal profound disparities in HPV vaccination initiation among college students based on gender identity and sexual orientation, challenging assumptions of uniform risk and access.17,21,46,47 Using Bayesian multilevel logistic regression models nested within 36 social strata defined by gender identity, sexual orientation, and race/ethnicity, a large-scale study of 65,047 U.S. college students identified significant inequities.46 Strata including cisgender men, transgender women, and non-binary individuals assigned male at birth exhibited a significantly lower likelihood of HPV vaccination initiation than did cisgender women and non-Hispanic White individuals.46 Conversely, strata comprising lesbian, gay, bisexual, pansexual, and queer individuals demonstrated higher vaccination rates than their heterosexual counterparts.46 This suggests that sexual minority identities may be associated with greater health literacy or proactive engagement with preventive care, possibly due to increased exposure to lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer (LGBTQ+) health advocacy and community-based education.47

The predicted prevalence of HPV vaccination initiation varies dramatically across these intersectional strata, ranging from 59.2% among heterosexual, racial/ethnic minority, and cisgender men to 87.1% among bisexual/pansexual/queer, racial/ethnic minority, and non-binary individuals assigned females at birth.46 Despite these differences, the discriminatory accuracy of intersectional stratification in predicting vaccination status was low, meaning that while disparities exist, they cannot be reliably predicted using the current models. However, the presence of pronounced variations underscore the need for tailored interventions beyond one-size-fits-all messaging. Notably, the majority of the variance in vaccination rates was driven by additive rather than interaction effects, indicating that individual factors, such as race, sex, and sexuality, contribute independently to disparities, with no significant synergistic interaction among them.

These findings are consistent with those of other studies showing that male college students consistently report lower vaccination rates than do their female peers. For example, in a systematic review of 39 studies, male students showed significantly lower completion rates with only 11.5% completing a vaccine series.42 The reasons for this include a perceived lack of personal risk, limited provider recommendations, and concerns regarding side effects. Moreover, male students often perceive themselves as being at a lower risk of HPV-related cancers despite evidence that HPV causes a significant proportion of oropharyngeal and penile cancers in men. This phenomenon – termed “unrealistic optimism” – was identified as a key barrier in the theoretical domain framework (TDF) analysis, where it emerged in 50% of the included studies. Such cognitive biases must be addressed through targeted educational campaigns that emphasize the broad spectrum of HPV-related cancers affecting all sexes.

Racial and ethnic disparities across global contexts

Racial and ethnic disparities in HPV vaccination rates are evident across multiple international contexts, though the nature and drivers of these inequities vary by region and healthcare system.35,48 In the United States, studies have revealed that racial/ethnic minority students, particularly those identified as Black Americans, have lower compliance with HPV vaccination, especially when they do not speak English at home.6,25,49 This suggests that language barriers, cultural mistrust of medical institutions, and systemic inequities in healthcare access play a role in shaping vaccination behaviors. Similarly, in Ghana, although no direct racial disparities were reported due to the homogeneity of the Ashanti Region sample,45 the broader national context indicates that rural-urban divides and socioeconomic status likely influence vaccine access, particularly in low-resource settings.

In contrast, foreign-born college students in the U.S. exhibited a moderate vaccination rate of 63%, with moderate knowledge levels about HPV and the vaccine.7 However, neither acculturation level nor knowledge of HPV significantly predicted vaccination behavior, suggesting that structural and attitudinal factors may outweigh informational factors.7 Perceived susceptibility to HPV infection and cues to action – including recommendations from parents, religious leaders, and healthcare providers – were significantly associated with vaccination, indicating that social and environmental prompts are more influential than personal knowledge alone.21,43 This implies that public health strategies should focus on mobilizing trusted community figures rather than solely increasing information dissemination.

China presents a unique case in which awareness of HPV is high (71% of students have heard of it); however, the actual vaccination rates remain extremely low.26 This paradox reflects deep-rooted cultural and systemic barriers, including the stigma surrounding sexually transmitted infections, parental hesitancy, and a lack of government-supported free vaccination programs. Although students expressed a high intention to vaccinate if the costs were covered, the absence of policy-level support prevented widespread uptake. Mathematical modeling studies suggest that even with first-generation vaccines, racial/ethnic disparities in cancer burden persist, with certain groups facing higher lifetime risks of developing HPV-associated cancers owing to differential HPV-type distribution and delayed diagnosis.48 Second-generation vaccines may reduce these disparities but only if equitable access is ensured.48 In southern Italy, while the overall vaccination rate among medical students was relatively high at 55.5%, the sex gap was extreme (78.5% among females versus 16.5% among males), highlighting how regional norms and sex-specific perceptions of risk can shape behavior.41 These findings reinforce the idea that disparities are not merely individual but are embedded in societal structures, including sex roles, healthcare delivery systems, and institutional policies.

Influencing factors across multiple levels: knowledge, perception, and structural barriers

A comprehensive synthesis of evidence reveals that HPV vaccination uptake among college students is shaped by a complex interplay of individual, interpersonal, and structural factors.17,25,30,40–42 At the individual level, knowledge gaps persisted despite high awareness. Many students are aware of HPV and its vaccine but lack an accurate understanding of transmission modes, symptoms, risk factors, and vaccine mechanisms. This knowledge deficit contributes to misconceptions and hesitation. For instance, concerns about safety and side effects were frequently cited as barriers, particularly among male students. In a qualitative study of Chinese college students, participants acknowledged the potential benefits of vaccination, but remained hesitant owing to fear of adverse reactions and long-term effects, despite expressing a willingness to receive the vaccine if subsidized.26 This finding suggests that removing financial barriers alone may be insufficient to achieve high uptake, unless safety concerns are concurrently addressed through targeted education and transparent communication.

Perceived susceptibility and disease severity are critical determinants. Studies have indicated that many college students, especially males, underestimate their personal risk of contracting HPV or developing HPV-related cancers. This “unrealistic optimism” was confirmed in a TDF analysis, which was conducted in half of the reviewed studies. Individuals who do not believe they are personally vulnerable are less likely to take preventive measures. Conversely, those who perceived higher benefits from vaccination, such as reduced cancer risk, were more likely to be vaccinated. In Ghana, students with stronger beliefs in vaccine effectiveness showed significantly higher intention scores, underscoring the importance of reinforcing the perceived benefits.45

Social influence plays a pivotal role at the interpersonal level. Recommendations from parents, religious leaders, service providers, and vaccinated peers significantly increased the likelihood of vaccination. In Ghana, students whose parents or religious leaders recommended the vaccine were more likely to have positive intentions.45 Similarly, in the U.S., the receipt of any recommendation from a healthcare provider was strongly linked to higher vaccination compliance.25 Peer influence is also powerful: students are more inclined to vaccinate if their friends have done so. However, social stigma and negative peer attitudes can act as deterrents, particularly in cultures where sexual health topics are taboos.

However, the structural and environmental barriers remain pervasive. The lack of access to healthcare, absence of health insurance, and financial costs are major obstacles. In the U.S., cost and lack of provider recommendations are the most commonly reported barriers.17,50 In Italy, active outreach from healthcare institutions significantly increased vaccination likelihood (Odds ratio [OR] 1.70, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.09–2.65),41 demonstrating that logistical support matters. Additionally, the absence of a centralized up-to-date call strategy limits catch-up vaccination opportunities. In China, the lack of government-funded programs prevents widespread adoption despite strong interest in reducing costs.26

International variations in vaccination strategies and policy implications

International approaches to HPV vaccination reflect diverse policy environments and cultural contexts, which result in markedly different outcomes. Examining countries’ experiences along three key dimensions – policy approach, implementation strategy, and vaccination outcomes – revealed common challenges and context-specific solutions.

In the U.S., efforts have focused on integrating school-based programs and leveraging the recommendations of healthcare providers. However, a study evaluating the impact of school nurse recommendations found no statistically significant difference in vaccination rates between intervention and control schools, suggesting that passive recommendations may be insufficient.28,43 This highlights the need for more robust proactive strategies such as automated reminders, mobile clinics, and digital tools.

In Ghana, the 2025 integration of HPV vaccination into the national immunization schedule represents a transformative opportunity.51,52 However, the success of this initiative depends on addressing vaccine hesitance through culturally appropriate messages. Key stakeholders – parents, religious leaders, educators, and peers – must be engaged in building trust and normalizing vaccination. Free access is crucial because students explicitly indicated that cost is a major barrier. The model proposed in Ghana emphasizes a multi-stakeholder approach that combines mass education with community-level endorsements.

China’s experience illustrates the power of policy change. Although awareness is high, vaccination remains low because of a lack of state support.53 The introduction of government-subsidized or free vaccination programs could dramatically improve vaccination uptake, as evidenced by students’ willingness to vaccinate if costs were covered.54,55 Tailored communication strategies that address cultural taboos and promote vaccines as a preventive measure for both men and women are essential.

Italy’s findings emphasize the importance of institutional engagement. Despite having advanced knowledge, male medical students showed low vaccination rates, indicating that even highly educated groups require targeted outreach. Active, ongoing calls from healthcare institutions improved uptake, suggesting that continuous engagement is more effective than one-time interventions.41,56

The United States and Italy both leveraged healthcare institutions, yet their strategies yielded different outcomes: passive school nurse recommendations proved ineffective in the U.S., whereas proactive institutional outreach significantly increased uptake in Italy. Ghana and China face cost barriers, with Ghana additionally confronting challenges in culturally appropriate communication and China lacking government-funded support. Collectively, these international examples demonstrate that successful HPV vaccination programs require more than education, and demand systemic changes, including universal access, provider advocacy, community mobilization, and policy-level support. A consistent theme across all regions is that structural and social factors outweigh individual knowledge, necessitating a shift from information-centric to ecosystem-based strategies.

Knowledge gaps and misconceptions among university students

A consistent theme across multiple studies is the presence of significant knowledge gaps and persistent misconceptions about HPV and its vaccine among university students, despite high levels of general awareness.27,31,57–59 In a study conducted at a Southeastern U.S. university, only 40 out of 360 participants were familiar with the HPV virus itself, even though they had heard of the vaccine, highlighting a critical disconnect between recognition of the vaccine and understanding of the disease it prevents.57 This lack of foundational knowledge has contributed to widespread misinformation, such as the belief that the vaccine is only relevant for women or that it promotes promiscuity, which persists even among educated young adults.

Further evidence from Greece revealed that while 86.6% of undergraduate students were aware of HPV, this awareness did not uniformly translate into an accurate understanding.58 The study found that vaccination status was significantly associated with knowledge of national immunization program (NIP) recommendations, suggesting that structured public health messaging plays a crucial role in shaping informed decisions.27,60,61 Similarly, a Thai study found that although approximately one-third of female secondary school students had fair overall health literacy, those with low knowledge and understanding of HPV were more likely to be vaccinated, a counterintuitive finding that may reflect reliance on external cues rather than personal comprehension.62 Conversely, students with poor communication skills were significantly more likely to remain unvaccinated, indicating that the ability to process and articulate health information was a key determinant.62

In Japan, where HPV vaccination rates have historically been low due to policy suspension, healthcare students show higher awareness and uptake than do the general population, yet many still cite concerns about adverse reactions and scheduling difficulties as barriers.27 Notably, over half of unvaccinated but interested female students reported basing their decisions on parental advice, underscoring the influence of informal, non-clinical sources of information.27 These findings collectively indicate that awareness alone is insufficient and that accurate, accessible, and culturally appropriate education is required to bridge the gap between awareness and action.

Vaccine hesitancy and the role of information ecosystems

Vaccine hesitancy among young adults is increasingly shaped by complex information ecosystems, particularly online platforms, where misinformation spreads rapidly.34 A 2025 study analyzing YouTube content revealed that videos opposing HPV vaccination were more likely to reference politicians, media outlets, podcasts, and news analyses, indicating a strategic alignment with political narratives rather than scientific discourse.34 By contrast, provaccine videos have predominantly focused on specific diseases and medical facts.

This dichotomy suggests that anti-vaccine content leverages emotional and political appeal to gain traction, whereas pro-vaccine messages rely on factual delivery, often resulting in lower engagement despite greater accuracy.34

The impact of online misinformation extends beyond HPV to broader vaccine hesitancy.63 A study on early COVID-19 vaccination patterns found that counties with higher levels of online misinformation exhibited significantly lower vaccination uptake and higher hesitancy, even after adjusting for political affiliation, demographics, and socioeconomic factors.63 Notably, the effect of misinformation was strongest in democratic-leaning areas, challenging the assumption that political ideology alone drives hesitancy. Granger causality analysis confirmed a directional relationship between online misinformation and vaccine hesitancy, reinforcing the need for proactive digital interventions.63

This dynamic underscores a growing challenge: Traditional public health campaigns may struggle to compete with emotionally charged and politically framed content on social media.64 For instance, a study on skin cancer prevention via Twitter demonstrated that shock and humorous messages generated higher engagement, whereas information-based content was more likely to be shared, suggesting that effective messaging must balance emotional resonance with factual integrity.64 Applying this insight to HPV vaccination, future strategies should incorporate shareable formats that maintain scientific accuracy without sacrificing reach.64

Health literacy and its variable impact on vaccination behavior

Health literacy has emerged as a multifaceted construct with differential impacts on HPV vaccine uptake, depending on the context and population. While some studies have reported a positive association between health literacy and HPV knowledge, others have found no direct link to vaccination status. For example, a study on Greek university students found that higher health literacy was correlated with increased awareness of HPV and vaccination, yet it did not independently predict actual vaccine receipt.58 Similarly, a U.S. study of Asian American and Pacific Islander (AAPI) undergraduates found that non-Latina White (NLW) women scored higher on all five measures of HPV vaccine literacy and had significantly higher completion rates (60.7% vs. 38.6%); however, the authors emphasized that sociocultural factors beyond literacy, such as family norms and trust in institutions, also play a major role.24

In Thailand, a nuanced analysis revealed that while overall health literacy was not significantly associated with vaccination, specific dimensions mattered. Students with low knowledge and understanding were more likely to be vaccinated, possibly because of their reliance on trusted recommendations, whereas those with poor communication skills were more likely to be unvaccinated.62 This suggests that health literacy functions not as a monolithic trait but as a constellation of competencies, each influencing behavior differently. Furthermore, a systematic review concluded that while vaccine literacy is linked to reduced hesitancy, the causal relationship remains unclear owing to methodological limitations in existing studies, including cross-sectional designs and self-reported data.38

These findings highlight a critical paradox: individuals may possess sufficient knowledge to make informed decisions yet fail to act owing to structural or psychological barriers. Therefore, interventions must move beyond simply increasing knowledge and focus on improving the practical application of health information, such as navigating healthcare systems, communicating with providers, and interpreting risk assessments.

Social determinants and structural barriers in diverse populations

Social determinants – including sex, race/ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and institutional trust – play a decisive role in shaping HPV vaccination disparities among university populations.24,49,59 In the United States, male college students consistently show lower vaccination rates than that of female college students, with completion rates as low as 11.5% in some studies.25 This disparity is driven by perceived low personal risk, limited provider recommendations, and concerns about side effects, despite evidence that HPV causes oropharyngeal and penile cancers in men.59 Even among medical students in southern Italy, vaccination uptake was 78.5% among females versus just 16.5% among males,41 illustrating how deeply entrenched sex-based perceptions of risk persist even within highly educated groups.

Racial and ethnic disparities were also evident. In the U.S., AAPI women had significantly lower HPV vaccine completion rates (38.6%) than that of non-Latina white women (60.7%), despite similar levels of awareness.24 This gap is attributed not only to lower health literacy but also to cultural stigma, mistrust in medical institutions, and language barriers, particularly among non-English-speaking families.24 In China, despite high awareness (71% of students have heard of HPV), vaccination rates remain extremely low owing to cost, lack of government support, and parental hesitation, demonstrating that systemic access issues can override individual willingness.26

Moreover, institutional trust and access to care are also important. A cluster randomized trial in Australia found no increase in HPV vaccine uptake despite a complex intervention involving education and logistical support, largely due to inadequate resourcing and advisory board resistance to financially impactful strategies.65 This highlights that even well-designed programs can fail when structural constraints such as funding, staffing, and administrative flexibility are not addressed.65 In contrast, a recent trial in China demonstrated that chatbot intervention significantly increased HPV vaccination uptake among middle school girls’ parents, with 7.1% of the intervention group receiving the vaccine compared to 1.8% in the control group, underscoring the potential of scalable, low-cost digital tools to overcome access barriers.36

Healthcare provider influence and innovative intervention strategies

Healthcare provider recommendations remain one of the most powerful predictors of HPV vaccine acceptance, even amid pandemic-related disruptions.32,35 A study conducted during the coronavirus disease-19 (COVID-19) pandemic confirmed that provider recommendations significantly influenced vaccination decisions, mitigating the negative impact of the pandemic on routine immunization schedules.32 This finding aligns with qualitative research showing that parents who received personalized, transparent discussions from trusted providers were more likely to vaccinate their children, especially when concerns regarding autonomy and safety were addressed.35

Innovative digital interventions are emerging as effective tools to amplify provider influence and improve access. The Chinese chatbot trial exemplified this trend: over a 2-week period, the intervention led to a statistically significant increase in both vaccine uptake and parental consultations with healthcare professionals.36 Participants using the chatbot also demonstrated enhanced vaccine literacy and an improved ability to discern rumors, suggesting that artificial intelligence (AI) tools can serve as virtual advocates and guide users through complex health decisions.36 Similarly, network modeling studies have suggested that targeted vaccination strategies based on social network centrality, such as degree, betweenness, and percolation centrality, can maximize herd immunity by focusing on individuals with the highest number of sexual contacts.66 These models indicate that ring-based vaccination strategies are particularly effective in reducing cumulative incidence among females and offer a data-driven approach for optimizing resource allocation.66 Together, these findings point to a future where HPV vaccination programs integrate behavioral science, digital innovation, and network analytics to achieve equitable and sustainable coverage.36,66,67

Campus-based health services as catalysts for increased access and uptake

Campus-based health services play a pivotal role in improving HPV vaccination rates among college students by reducing structural barriers and increasing accessibility.11,19,33,62,68 A case study from Purdue University demonstrated that the implementation of on-campus vaccination clinics significantly elevated HPV vaccine uptake, with 1,827 individuals receiving the vaccine across two clinical events in late 2022.19 The success of this model was attributed to the provision of vaccines at no out-of-pocket cost, which directly addressed financial concerns – a barrier identified in multiple studies. Notably, participants reported that informed awareness and the absence of financial burden were key facilitators of vaccination, underscoring the importance of cost-free access for promoting vaccine acceptance.

Further evidence from Maryland colleges and universities confirms the feasibility and impact of these models. Of the 20 responding institutions, 10 offered HPV vaccination services, covering approximately 39% of the state’s college student population.68 These programs are inclusive and offer immunization to both men and women at no cost. Importantly, four of these institutions utilized periodic campus health clinics or provided prescriptions filled at nearby pharmacies, indicating a scalable, low-infrastructure alternative to permanent on-site clinics. This hybrid approach allows institutions with limited resources to maintain consistent access, without requiring full-time staff or dedicated medical facilities.

The effectiveness of these services is enhanced when combined with strategic communication. At Purdue, e-mail outreach and visually appealing posters proved instrumental in disseminating information on clinic availability,19 demonstrating that even simple and low-cost promotional tools can drive engagement. Similarly, a Maryland study emphasized that while only a minority of campuses currently offer vaccines, those that do serve as critical access points – particularly in regions where healthcare access is limited.68 These findings suggest that expanding campus-based clinics through partnerships with local pharmacies or public health agencies could dramatically increase the reach and equity of vaccine deliveries.

Moreover, institutional engagement extends beyond service provision. A cross-sectional study among health sciences students at the University of Naples Federico II found that proactive institutional vaccination invitations increased the likelihood of vaccination nearly threefold (adjusted OR [aOR]: 2.81; 95% CI: 1.48–5.33).56 This highlights the power of structured institution-led initiatives in shaping behavior. When universities play an active role in scheduling appointments, sending reminders, and integrating vaccination into routine health checkups, they create a culture of prevention rather than reaction. Such interventions are especially effective in highly mobile populations such as university students, who may otherwise fall through the cracks of traditional healthcare systems.

Digital health campaigns: tailoring communication to Generation Z (Gen Z) preferences

Digital health campaigns represent a promising avenue to reach college students, particularly given their strong engagement with social media and digital platforms.22 Research from Arizona State University revealed that college students prefer specific digital media channels for future HPV vaccine communication campaigns, including Instagram, Twitter, and printable flyers.69 These preferences align with broader trends in Gen Z communication habits, in which visual content, short-form messaging, and peer-driven platforms dominate information consumption. The American College Health Association (ACHA)’s “Gen Z Can Beat HPV” campaign exemplifies this strategy by providing a ready-to-use social media toolkit featuring images tailored for Twitter and Instagram, along with printable flyers for physical distribution.69 This toolkit enables campus health professionals to deliver consistent, youth-appropriate messaging without developing content from scratch.69 The campaign’s focus on empowering students as agents of change – using slogans like “Can Beaten HPV” – resonates with the self-identity and activism often seen in younger generations.

Furthermore, qualitative research indicates that digital tools must not only be accessible but also culturally relevant and interactive. For instance, a chatbot intervention in China successfully increased HPV vaccination uptake among parents of middle school girls by leveraging automated personalized messaging.36 Although targeted at parents, this finding suggests that similar digital nudges – such as short message service (SMS) reminders, app-based tracking, or gamified educational modules – could be adapted for college students. These tools can overcome time constraints and perceived lack of necessity, which were cited as barriers in Purdue’s study.19

Digital campaigns should be designed based on empirical data on student preferences. The ASU study used small-group discussions to identify preferred formats, ensuring that messages resonated with actual user behavior.69 This participatory design process ensures that campaigns are not only technically feasible, but also socially acceptable. Future efforts should prioritize multimodal strategies combining social media posts, influencer collaborations, and interactive webinars to maximize reach and retention.

In addition, digital platforms allow real-time feedback and adaptive messaging. By monitoring engagement metrics, such as click-through rates, shares, and comments, health promotion teams can dynamically refine content. This iterative approach enables campaigns to respond to emerging misconceptions and shifts in students’ sentiments, making them more responsive and effective over time.

Healthcare provider recommendations: the most consistent predictor of vaccination behavior

Among all the factors influencing HPV vaccination, healthcare provider recommendations have emerged as the single most powerful predictor of vaccination uptake. Multiple studies have consistently reported that receiving recommendations from healthcare providers significantly increase the likelihood of vaccination. In a Tennessee university study, the aOR for HPV vaccination among students who received a provider recommendation was 12.90 (95% CI: 7.08–23.51), indicating a near 13-fold increase in vaccination probability.70,71 This finding was corroborated by OSU Center for Health Sciences research, which highlighted the influence of health advice on college students’ decision-making.28

This effect was not limited to the GPs. The same trend holds true in specialized settings. Among health science students in Italy, positive attitudes toward vaccination strongly predicted uptake; however, institutional communication, including provider recommendations, was a critical enabler.56 Even in populations with advanced biomedical knowledge, formal recommendations are a decisive trigger for action.

The mechanisms underlying this influence are trust and authority. College students often view healthcare providers as credible sources of health information, particularly during transitional periods, such as the transition from high school to university. A recommendation serves as both a cue to action and validation of the vaccine’s importance. When a provider explicitly states, “You should get this vaccine,” it transforms the personal choice into a medically endorsed standard-of-care.

However, the quality of recommendations is important. Passive or generic advice is less effective than direct personalized encouragement. Reportedly, brief and focused conversations during routine visits, especially those addressing risk perception and vaccine benefits, can significantly boost intention and completion rates.21,36 Therefore, training providers is essential. Many clinicians remain hesitant or uninformed about the HPV vaccination guidelines, particularly for males and older adolescents. Ensuring that campus healthcare providers are well informed and equipped to deliver strong and consistent recommendations is a foundational step in any intervention strategy.

Given the strength of this factor, policy-level integration should mandate provider education and incentivize recommendation practices. For example, similar to other preventive services, electronic health record (EHR) systems could include prompts to recommend HPV vaccination during visits. Such system-level changes ensure that recommendations are not left to individual initiatives but become embedded in clinical workflows.

Policy-level strategies: enabling systemic change through institutional and statewide frameworks

Policy-level strategies are essential to create sustainable and equitable improvements in HPV vaccination rates across college populations. Evidence suggests that isolated interventions are insufficient without systemic support, regardless of how well they are designed. A national survey of five U.S. states with low adolescent coverage rates found that parental vaccine hesitancy was a major determinant, with children of hesitant parents being 83% less likely to receive any doses (aOR: 0.17, 95% CI: 0.11–0.27).37 This underscores the need for policies that address not only individual beliefs, but also the broader ecosystem of care.

At the institutional level, policies that mandate or incentivize campus-based vaccination services can drive their widespread adoption. The Maryland study concluded that cooperation between universities and state cancer control coalitions – such as the Maryland Cancer Collaborative – creates a framework for scalable, coordinated action.68 Institutions can access funding, technical assistance, and shared resources by aligning campus health initiatives with state-wide public health goals. This model demonstrates how policies can bridge the gaps between academic institutions and the public health infrastructure.

On a broader scale, the German experience illustrates the transformative potential of organized vaccination programs. Despite the initial low uptake, only 17.4% of girls vaccinated within the first-year countries with structured government-led programs achieved significantly higher coverage.70 This suggests that the absence of a national program in Germany contributed to suboptimal outcomes. In contrast, the U.S. has made progress through school-based programs and provider recommendations, but the uptake remains uneven.32,35,68,71 A shift toward universal, publicly funded vaccination programs, even if initially limited to college-aged populations, can dramatically improve access and equity.

Policies to eliminate financial barriers are particularly important. The Purdue University model, which offers free vaccines on campus, led to increased uptake and reduced perceptions of vaccine ineligibility.19 This aligns with findings from the Tennessee study, where perceived barriers were negatively associated with vaccination (aOR: 0.73; 95% CI: 0.66–0.81).71 Eliminating cost-related concerns through state-funded or insurance-covered programs addresses one of the most persistent structural barriers.

Additionally, policies that support digital innovation can amplify this effect. For example, allowing universities to partner with telehealth platforms or digital health start-ups could enable remote consultations, virtual consent forms, and automated appointment scheduling. These innovations reduce logistical burdens and make vaccination more convenient, which are key factors for young adults juggling academics, work, and social life.

Finally, the policy must extend beyond the campus. The inclusion of HPV vaccination in comprehensive college health mandates, similar to the requirements for meningococcal or influenza vaccines, would normalize practice and reduce stigma. A U.S. study showed that the receipt of other vaccines (e.g., meningococcal conjugate) was positively associated with HPV vaccination.37 These findings suggest that integrating HPV into existing vaccination protocols can leverage established pathways and increase compliance.

Integrating multi-channel strategies for maximum impact

The most effective interventions are integrated multichannel approaches that combine campus services, digital campaigns, provider recommendations and supportive policies. Evidence from multiple studies supports a synergistic model, in which each component reinforces the others. For example, a campus health clinic is more successful when paired with targeted digital outreach via social media and e-mail, which is supported by provider recommendations during routine visits.19 The Purdue University case study provides a blueprint for such an integration: cost-free vaccines delivered through scheduled clinics, promoted via e-mail and posters, and facilitated by healthcare providers who actively recommend the vaccine.19 This combination addresses individual, interpersonal, and structural levels simultaneously by targeting knowledge, perceptions, and access.

Similarly, the Tennessee study found that higher HPV knowledge and perceived risk were associated with vaccination, but only when combined with provider recommendations.71 This indicates that information alone is insufficient; it must be contextualized and validated by trusted figures. Digital campaigns can raise awareness; however, students may hesitate without provider endorsement.

Institutional policies that support all three elements: funding clinics, training providers, and investment in digital infrastructure, create sustainable ecosystems. For instance, a university could establish a central HPV vaccination task force responsible for coordinating clinic schedules, managing digital content, and ensuring provider compliance with recommended protocols.

Ultimately, the goal is to embed HPV vaccination into the fabric of campus life, not as an optional add-on but as a routine, expected part of preventive healthcare. By combining accessible services, communication, authoritative endorsements, and enabling policies, colleges can transform HPV vaccination from a low-priority issue to a cornerstone of student health.

Discussion

This review provides a comprehensive synthesis of HPV vaccination among university students, focusing on the current trends, disparities, and influencing factors. Overall, HPV vaccination uptake remains suboptimal despite some regional improvements, with persistent disparities across sex, race/ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and geographic settings. Vaccination behaviors are influenced by multiple interacting factors, including knowledge and health literacy, vaccine hesitancy, healthcare provider recommendations, and structural barriers, highlighting the need for multilevel intervention strategies.

Existing evidence reveals a persistent gap between the proven efficacy of the HPV vaccine and its global uptake among college students. From a global perspective, current trends indicate that HPV vaccination uptake among university students remains uneven and suboptimal. Despite being recommended for individuals up to age 26 y and capable of preventing six major cancers, vaccination rates remain suboptimal, particularly among male students, racial/ethnic minorities, and those with marginalized gender identities.41,46,49 The epidemiological burden is substantial, with college populations facing elevated risks owing to high rates of sexually transmitted infections and a critical lack of protection against preventable cancers. Importantly, substantial disparities exist in HPV vaccination uptake across populations. Key findings underscore that low completion rates – such as 28.9% among female and 11.5% among male U.S. college students25 – reflect deep-seated disparities rooted in knowledge gaps, perceived low personal risk (especially “unrealistic optimism”), vaccine hesitancy, and structural barriers like cost, lack of healthcare access, and insufficient provider recommendations.17,25,40,42 Notably, even highly educated groups, such as medical students, show significant sex disparities, with only 16.5% of males vaccinated compared to 78.5% of females, highlighting that knowledge alone does not drive behavior.41

Multiple factors have been identified as influencing HPV vaccination behavior among university students. The most powerful predictor of vaccination is a recommendation from a healthcare provider, which can increase vaccination uptake by nearly 13-fold.71 This underscores the need to strengthen provider engagement through training and system-level prompts in EHRs. Complementing this, digital health campaigns tailored to Gen Z preferences – such as Instagram, Twitter, and interactive chatbots – show promise in increasing awareness and intention, especially when combined with cost-free on-campus clinics and multi-channel outreach.19,22,36,69 The success of such integrated models, exemplified by Purdue University’s clinic events and China’s AI-driven parental intervention, demonstrates that addressing individual, interpersonal, and structural barriers simultaneously yields the greatest impact.19,36 These findings point to a critical shift: effective interventions must move beyond information dissemination to create accessible, trusted, and convenient vaccination pathways.

Policy-level actions are essential for achieving equitable and sustainable improvements. Governments and institutions must prioritize universal access through publicly funded or insurance-covered programs, mandate provider recommendations, and support campus-based vaccination services and digital innovation.37,68,71 The integration of HPV into broader student health mandates – similar to requirements for meningococcal or influenza vaccines – could normalize prevention and reduce stigma.17,37

Future efforts should also adopt an intersectional lens to address the compounded inequities faced by multiple marginalized students,46 while leveraging network analytics to optimize catch-up strategies based on social connectivity.66 Ultimately, transforming HPV vaccination into a routine component of campus health infrastructure requires coordinated, multi-sectoral collaboration grounded in evidence, equity, and a youth-centered design. The evidence is clear: bridging the gap between vaccine efficacy and uptake demands a fundamental reorientation of campus health systems toward accessibility, equity, and sustained engagement.
