ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTotal mesorectal excision (TME) is highly effective for early-stage rectal cancer, but is associated with considerable morbidity, which can substantially impair the quality of life (QoL) of patients. For very early tumours (low-risk cT1), local excision (LE) offers the possibility of organ preservation (OP) with reduced morbidity; however, its application is limited to a selected group. For early tumours where upfront LE is not feasible, primary OP with (chemo)radiotherapy as an alternative to TME surgery has been evaluated in the STARTREC phase II/III studies, which reported promising 1-year OP rates.ObjectiveThe STARTREC-3 trial aims to increase the 2-year OP rate from 60% to 80% in early rectal cancer (cT1–3abN0) and from 30% to 60% in early-intermediate rectal cancer (cT1–3abN1, ≤3 mesorectal nodes measuring ≤8 mm) by intensifying neoadjuvant treatment in different study arms.MethodsSTARTREC-3 is embedded in the STARTREC master trial protocol, which uses an adaptive platform study design allowing early termination of inferior treatment arms and the addition of novel arms. The multicentre STARTREC-3 trial investigates three parallel, non-comparative treatment strategies for patients with early and early-intermediate rectal adenocarcinoma who prefer OP over primary TME surgery. All arms start with 5×5 Gy radiotherapy, followed by: an endoluminal boost via contact X-ray brachytherapy (arm 1), an external beam radiotherapy (EBRT) boost by MR-guided EBRT (arm 2) or three cycles of capecitabine oxaliplatin systemic treatment chemotherapy (arm 3). Treatment allocation is predefined and centre-dependent. Response evaluations (MRI and endoscopy) are planned at 14–16 weeks and 26 weeks after onset of radiotherapy. The primary endpoint is the proportion of patients with successful OP at 24 months from onset of therapy. Secondary endpoints include toxicity, QoL, functional and oncological outcomes. Data will be analysed separately for early (cN0) and early-intermediate (cN1) disease. The total planned sample size is 210 patients across the three arms. Interim analyses will be performed for each study arm to determine early failures and discontinue ineffective arms.Ethics and disseminationThe trial was approved by the medical ethics committee NedMec of the Netherlands and is registered in the EU Clinical Trials Information System (CTIS). Introduction

Background and rationale

Colorectal cancer screening and improved diagnostics have increased early-stage rectal cancer diagnoses.1,3 While total mesorectal excision (TME) remains the standard of care due to its proven oncological efficacy, it is associated with significant perioperative risks, including surgical complications and long-term morbidity, which negatively impact quality of life (QoL), caused by low anterior resection syndrome (LARS), genitourinary complaints and the potential need for a temporary or permanent stoma.4,15 Local excision (LE) techniques have become established alternatives to TME and offer organ preservation (OP) with less morbidity for very early tumours (eg, low-risk cT1), but their application is limited to a selected group of patients.16 17 This underscores the need for organ-preserving strategies for early-stage rectal cancer (cT1–3abN0–1).

Neoadjuvant chemoradiation (CRT) and short-course radiotherapy (SCRT) effectively downstage tumours and reduce local recurrence rates in locally advanced rectal cancer.818,21 In patients achieving a clinical complete response (cCR) with CRT, a watch-and-wait (W&W) strategy can be offered safely by omitting TME surgery without compromising oncological outcomes.22,24 This method represents opportunistic or secondary OP. Since radiotherapy appears even more effective in smaller, early-stage tumours,25 the concept of primary OP has emerged: using (C)RT to achieve a cCR and avoid TME surgery in patients who would not otherwise require neoadjuvant treatment. Various prospective studies have reported promising OP rates exceeding 50% in early-stage patients treated with (C)RT, with or without LE.26,29

The previous STARTREC trials further demonstrated the feasibility of this approach. In phase II, patients were randomised between TME surgery, CRT or SCRT.30 The subsequent phase III study was adapted to a patient-preference design, allowing patients to choose between standard TME surgery or OP, with randomisation between CRT and SCRT within the organ-preserving arm.31 Preliminary 12-month results (presented at ESTRO 2025)32 reported OP rates of 80% with CRT and 61% with SCRT, with low toxicity and no grade 4–5 events.32 Although these results are promising, OP rates are expected to decline during longer follow-up due to regrowth or recurrence requiring salvage surgery. This suggests room for further improving response and thereby OP rates, aiming to avoid overtreatment with TME surgery after neoadjuvant treatment for this early-stage group.

Different strategies may increase response rates. Contact X-ray brachytherapy (CXB) delivers a high-dose intraluminal radiation boost directly to the tumour with minimal damage to surrounding tissues.33 CXB has achieved cCR rates of 64%–72% in combination with CRT,34 35 and a 5-year OP rate of 79% in the OPERA trial, rising to 93% for tumours <3 cm treated with CXB first.36 37 Furthermore, MR-guided external beam radiotherapy (EBRT) allows adaptive, high-precision dose escalation. A novel approach using online guidance on an MR-linac was applied by Gani et al to increase cCR rates with good organ function and limited toxicity.38 39 Their pilot study combined conventionally delivered CRT (45 Gy/25 fractions) with a simultaneous integrated boost (5 Gy/25 fractions) and an additional weekly, response-adaptive boost (3 Gy) delivered on an MR-linac. Ongoing trials, including the Danish Watchful Waiting 3 and APHRODITE, are also investigating different external beam dose-escalation techniques to increase cCR and OP rates.40 41 In the Dutch pre-RADAR study (International Clinical Trials Registry Platform #NL8997), the maximum tolerated dose of dose-escalated SCRT using MR-guided radiotherapy was investigated,42 and preliminary data suggest that four fractions of 5 Gy following SCRT are safe in intermediate-risk rectal cancer patients. Another promising route is through total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT), which combines (C)RT with induction or consolidation chemotherapy. TNT, especially with consolidation chemotherapy, improves pathological complete response rates in locally advanced rectal cancer and is increasingly investigated in the context of OP,43,46 with the OPRA trial showing long-term OP in half of the patients with locally advanced disease treated with TNT.47 48

All of these approaches indicate that superior outcomes may be achievable in smaller tumours or in early-stage disease. However, the optimal OP strategy remains undefined, and not all techniques are widely available. The STARTREC-3 trial addresses this gap by prospectively evaluating three parallel intensification strategies with CXB, EBRT and capecitabine oxaliplatin systemic treatment (CAPOX) following SCRT in patients with early-stage rectal cancer who prefer OP over primary TME surgery. The trial aims to improve OP rates with minimal additional toxicity.

Methods and analysis

Study design

STARTREC-3 is a prospective, non-randomised, non-comparative, multicentre trial evaluating three parallel intensification strategies for early-stage rectal cancer. This study is part of the STARTREC platform trial, which allows for adaptive modifications, including early discontinuation of inferior arms and the addition of new study arms when required.

All arms start with standard SCRT (5 fractions of 5 Gy EBRT), followed by CXB boost (arm 1), MR-guided EBRT boost (arm 2) or systemic chemotherapy in terms of 3 cycles of CAPOX (arm 3). Response evaluations are performed at fixed-time intervals to assess the degree of response and determine OP feasibility (figure 1). Participating hospitals are allocated to one treatment arm based on available (radiotherapy) resources. In this Dutch multicentre study, centres with access to CXB or MR-guided EBRT will include patients in arm 1 or arm 2, respectively; all other centres will, in principle, include patients in arm 3. A full version of the study protocol is provided in the supplementary material (online supplemental STARTREC-3 Subprotocol).

Objective

The primary aim of the STARTREC-3 study is to improve OP rates at 24 months after the onset of radiotherapy in early-stage rectal cancer: from 60% to 80% for early (cN0) and from 30% to 60% for early-intermediate (cN1) rectal cancer patients.

Study population

Patients with early to early-intermediate adenocarcinoma of the rectum, defined as cT1–3abN0/N1 (≤3 mesorectal nodes measuring ≤8 mm) M0, with a tumour ≤40 mm in size and located in the mid or distal rectum, and with a wish for OP are eligible for participation in the current study.

Interventions

Short-course radiotherapy

All patients receive a dose of 25 Gy (EBRT) applied to the primary tumour and surrounding mesorectum in 5 daily fractions of 5 Gy. The target volume is tailored to early risk rectal cancer based on the publication of Peters et al,49 similar to the previous STARTREC 1 and 2 trials. For patients with cN1, the same delineation protocol is used as for patients with cN0, that is, no radiation of the elective lateral lymph node regions. SCRT will be followed by one of the three following treatments:

Arm 1: contact X-ray brachytherapy

A dose of 90 Gy is applied as an internal boost in an outpatient setting by means of CXB in 3 fractions of 30 Gy. CXB will start 2–3 weeks after SCRT, with an interval of 2 weeks between each fraction. If the tumour exceeds 3 cm and CXB is initially unfeasible, treatment may be delayed (maximum 10 weeks post SCRT) to allow for more tumour regression.

Arm 2: MR-guided external beam radiotherapy boost

A dose of 20 Gy as an external boost is delivered in four daily fractions of 5 Gy, targeted to the primary tumour and involved lymph nodes. Treatment is performed on an MR-linac using daily online adaptive planning, based on the PRERADAR protocol42 where 4 fractions were feasible (outcomes not published yet). The EBRT boost will be applied in the week adjacent to the SCRT.

Arm 3: chemotherapy

Three courses of CAPOX are administered starting 2–3 weeks after SCRT. Each cycle consists of capecitabine 1000 mg/m2 twice daily on days 1–14 and oxaliplatin 130 mg/m2 on day 1, every 3 weeks.

Response assessments

Tumour response assessment will be performed at 14–16 weeks (first response assessment) and at 26 weeks (second response assessment) after the start of SCRT. The assessment consists of a clinical evaluation with digital rectal examination (DRE), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), endoscopy and MRI. A cCR is defined as: DRE without palpable tumour, endoscopy reveals a flat scar without residual ulcer or tumour, and MRI demonstrates possible fibrosis or oedema but no residual tumour and no suspicious lymph nodes on T2-weighted and diffusion-weighted sequences. All response assessments are evaluated in the MDT of each study centre. When required, a central review of specific cases will be performed during a monthly nationwide expert meeting. The main purpose of the first response assessment (14–16 weeks after SCRT) is to identify those cases in which little to no tumour response is observed. Such cases are deemed unsuitable for OP and these patients will be strongly advised to proceed to TME surgery. In the case of a good clinical response, patients will proceed to the second response assessment. The second response assessment (at 26 weeks after SCRT) determines whether patients qualify for a W&W strategy in case of a cCR, or require TME surgery in case of an incomplete response. An extra response assessment can be considered when a near-complete response is observed at 26 weeks and a cCR is still likely to occur, instead of proceeding to TME surgery directly (figure 2). In the CAPOX arm, a LE by transanal minimally invasive surgery may be considered for a small tumour remnant (<3 cm), if technically feasible. The histopathological results will determine whether W&W (in the case of a low-risk tumour) can be started or whether patients will be advised to undergo completion TME (in the case of a high-risk tumour). LE is not offered in the CXB and EBRT boost arms due to expected increased morbidity following the local radiotherapy boost.

Follow-up

Patients with a cCR at 26 weeks after the onset of treatment, or those with a pathological low-risk tumour (ypT0–1) after a radical LE, are offered W&W surveillance. This follow-up consists of an intensified schedule with DRE, sigmoidoscopy, MRI, CEA and CT scans for up to 5 years. Study-specific follow-up continues for 36 months. Patients who underwent completion or salvage TME revert to standard postoperative follow-up according to the national guidelines for colorectal cancer (table 1 and table 2).

Statistical design and analysis

This non-randomised phase II trial is conducted with three non-comparative parallel arms for intensified neoadjuvant treatment following SCRT. Outcomes will be analysed separately for each treatment arm, and stratified for both early (cN0) and early-intermediate (cN1) rectal cancer patients, resulting in six study arms. Sample sizes were calculated to provide 80% power with a one-sided alpha of 0.05, accounting for an expected 5% dropout rate. For early (cN0) rectal cancer, a total of 135 patients (45 per arm) will be included. The null hypothesis, which assumes an OP rate <80%, will be rejected if at least 32 of 43 patients achieve OP at 24 months. For early-intermediate (cN1) rectal cancer, 75 patients (25 per arm) will be included. Here, the null hypothesis, an OP rate of <60%, will be rejected if at least 12 of 24 patients achieve OP at 24 months.

An interim analysis will be performed for each study arm to identify early failures, defined as the need for TME surgery due to an incomplete response at the first or second response assessment after SCRT. Early failures will be distinguished from failures at a later stage due to regrowth or recurrent disease. The interim analysis specifically focuses on early failures, while the final analysis will include all failures. Since all treatments were considered safe, study arms will not be temporarily stopped during the interim analysis. For early (cN0) rectal cancer, the interim analysis will be performed after the first 11 patients have been included and both response assessments have been completed. Based on an acceptable early failure rate of 14%, a study arm will, in principle, be stopped if four or more failures occur, with an estimated 6% risk of unjust stop (false negative) and 37% risk of unjust continuation (false positive). For early-intermediate (cN1), the interim analysis is performed after eight patients. Based on a 28% early failure rate, a study arm will, in principle, be stopped if five or more failures occur, with a calculated 4% unjust stop and 34% unjust continuation risk. These analyses will help to determine whether the observed failure rate in one arm is acceptable or unacceptable and will be reviewed within the Trial Steering Committee, which could result in early closure of ineffective treatment arms prior to the end of the study period.

Safety

The study acknowledges the potential risks of treatment intensification, primarily related to increased toxicity. All adverse events, including serious adverse events (SAE), will be graded according to the CTCAE V5.0. SAEs occurring during treatment or within 90 days of a study intervention will be reported within 24 hours.

An independent Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB), consisting of a surgical oncologist, a radiation oncologist and a statistician, has been installed in order to monitor quality and patient safety in this multicentre trial. The DSMB will evaluate interim outcomes and will advise on continuation of the study.

Radiotherapy quality assurance

To ensure protocol adherence and treatment consistency, a radiotherapy quality assurance (QA) programme will be implemented. Centres previously involved in the STARTREC trial are exempted from pre-trial QA for SCRT. Other centres must complete pre-trial QA by delineating and submitting a predefined case for review by the study team. For arm 3 (CAPOX), no additional pre-treatment QA is planned. For arm 1 (CXB boost), pre-treatment QA is not feasible because no delineation or planning is involved. Instead, all centres adhere to the same protocol and all patients are treated with the four-eyes principle. For arm 2 (EBRT boost), all centres will implement the arm-specific delineation, planning and adaptation protocol. The QA consists of reviewing and discussing the gross tumour volume (GTV) delineation (primary tumour and lymph nodes) prior to the start of SCRT and boost with a radiation oncologist from the Trial Steering Committee. Post-treatment QA will be performed centrally at the Netherlands Cancer Institute, where treatment plans and scans will be collected and reviewed for all patients enrolled in STARTREC-3. This will assess the quality of target delineations and radiotherapy plans to ensure adherence to protocol-defined standards.

Data management

Clinical data will be collected from medical records and validated questionnaires. Assigned and authorised data managers will enter pseudo-anonymised data into electronic Case Report Forms (eCRFs) using the Castor Clinical Data Management System (Castor CDMS, Castor EDC), developed by Castor (Ciwit B.V., Amsterdam, the Netherlands, and Castor Research Inc., Dover, Delaware, USA). Data handling complies with good clinical practice (GCP) and data protection regulations.

Discussion

The STARTREC-3 study aims to improve OP rates in patients with early-stage rectal cancer. According to current guidelines, these patients are not offered neoadjuvant treatment for oncological reasons. To enable OP in this early-stage group, neoadjuvant therapy can be applied with the specific aim of achieving a cCR and avoiding TME surgery, in the context of so-called planned or primary OP. As the optimal strategy for this subgroup remains unclear, prospective trials are warranted to evaluate safe and effective strategies for primary OP. STARTREC-3 addresses this gap by investigating three different treatment strategies. As the trial investigates radiotherapy-based strategies (CXB and MR-guided EBRT boost) as well as the more widely accessible CAPOX regimen, hospitals can implement a treatment based on their available (radiotherapy) resources and expertise. This strategy enables broader access to OP for all early-stage rectal cancer patients in the Netherlands.

STARTREC-3 is part of the STARTREC master protocol, which uses an adaptive design to evaluate multiple, parallel and non-comparative OP strategies for early-stage rectal cancer patients. This innovative design offers methodological and practical advantages.50 For example, it enables the efficient evaluation of multiple (novel) strategies within one protocol. It also increases accessibility, as potential candidates will likely have access to at least one of the study arms. Another key feature is the flexibility to discontinue underperforming arms based on interim analyses, while new treatment arms can be added through subsequent substudies as evidence evolves. However, this design also has limitations. Treatment allocation is centre-dependent, as not all hospitals have access to each investigational modality, which may limit generalisability to centres without this expertise. Moreover, the non-randomised and non-comparative design may introduce selection bias and prevent direct evaluation between treatment arms. However, promising strategies that demonstrate effectiveness may be further evaluated in a successive phase III trial.

Given that STARTREC-3 specifically targets patients seeking OP, that is, those wishing to avoid radical TME surgery, no standard TME control arm was incorporated in this study design as it was deemed unethical and likely unfeasible in the context of this OP study. Randomisation against TME could limit trial accrual due to strong patient preference, while also raising ethical concerns, which were also seen in the previous STARTREC trial, where the design was modified from randomisation (phase II) to a patient preference study (phase III).31 51 52 Nowadays, randomisation between two such different treatment regimens (ie, OP vs TME surgery) does not reflect real-world decision-making. Therefore, patients can opt for standard-of-care TME surgery outside the study or the OP approach offered through the trial.

SCRT (rather than CRT) serves as the basis for all three study arms in this trial primarily due to practical advantages, including shorter treatment duration, lower costs, reduced staff requirements and a shorter duration of acute toxicity compared with CRT.53 54 The TREC study demonstrated that SCRT followed by LE achieved 70% OP, with low morbidity and indications of improved QoL in patients with early-stage rectal cancer.29 Preliminary results from the previous STARTREC trials suggest higher (1 year) OP with CRT compared with SCRT (80% vs 61%). However, unlike the previous studies, our trial adds a radiotherapy boost or systemic treatment to SCRT, which is expected to further enhance response rates. Given these considerations and the pending long-term (30 months) outcomes of the previous trial, also including functional and QoL outcomes, SCRT remains a rational backbone for organ-preserving approaches in this trial.

The goal of intensifying treatment is to increase OP while limiting additional toxicity. This balance influenced the decision to limit CAPOX to three cycles, rather than the more commonly applied four to six cycles. Supporting this approach, the LARCT-US trial demonstrated that a TNT regimen with two chemotherapy cycles less (four cycles of CAPOX or six cycles of FOLFOX) than RAPIDO maintained a high clinical response rate with some favourable toxicity outcomes.55 56 Therefore, we hypothesise that three cycles of CAPOX will retain therapeutic efficacy in this early-stage patient population, while minimising toxicity.

One of the main challenges in STARTREC-3 is balancing the benefits of treatment intensification against the risk of overtreatment, particularly in the context of primary OP where patients otherwise would not have received neoadjuvant treatment. Additionally, the treatment course may involve an extended period of uncertainty, often exceeding 6 months, before it becomes clear whether OP remains a realistic scenario. Nevertheless, prior research and clinical experience suggest that many patients are willing to accept a prolonged treatment course to try to avoid TME surgery with its accompanying morbidity.57 58 Shared decision-making about the benefits and risks of the additional treatments is essential.

In conclusion, the STARTREC-3 trial will provide critical insights into the role and safety of SCRT followed by a CXB boost, an online adaptive MR-guided EBRT boost or three cycles of CAPOX for primary OP in early-stage rectal cancer patients. By exploring these novel strategies for early rectal cancer, STARTREC-3 is expected to further gain a foothold in daily clinical practice. The trial will enhance nationwide clinical experience with primary OP and its outcomes may pave the way for OP as a safe and viable alternative to radical surgery for patients with early-stage rectal cancer.
